Joint distribution of primes in multiple short intervals
Sun-Kai Leung

Abstract
. Assuming the Riemann hypothesis (RH) and the linear independence conjecture (LI), we show that the weighted count of primes in multiple disjoint short intervals has a multivariate Gaussian logarithmic limiting distribution with weak negative correlation. As a consequence, we derive short-interval counterparts for many important works in the literature of the Shanks–Rényi prime number race, including a sharp phase transition from all races being asymptotically unbiased to the existence of biased races. Our result remains novel, even for primes in a single moving interval, especially under a quantitative formulation of the linear independence conjecture (QLI).



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
11M26, 11N05

1.
Introduction

The study of primes in short intervals can be traced back to the late 18th century when the young prodigy Gauss examined tables of primes in search of patterns. In 1792, at the age of 15, he made a guess that despite its fluctuations, roughly one in every
\log x
integers is prime at around
x
, as observed from counting primes in intervals of length
1000
(chiliads). However, this prediction took more than a century to justify.
Eventually, in 1896, Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin independently resolved this conjecture, which is now known as the Prime Number Theorem. It is stated that as
x\to\infty,
we have

\displaystyle\pi(x):=\#\{p\leqslant x\}\sim\mathrm{Li}(x):=\int_{2}^{x}\frac{dt}{\log t},

or equivalently, the weighted count of primes (and prime powers) is

\displaystyle\psi(x):=\sum_{p^{k}\leqslant x}\log p\sim x,

which turns out to be easier to handle.
Consequently, one can show that the mean
of
\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)
for
n\in[1,N]
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\leqslant N}\left(\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)\right)\sim H,

provided that
H=o(N)
as
N\to\infty.

In his famous letter to Encke,
Gauss counted primes in intervals of length
100
from 1 million to 3 million, and also
compared
\pi(n+H)-\pi(n)
and
\mathrm{Li}(n+H)-\mathrm{Li}(n)
with
H=10^{5}
but did not explore the matter beyond comparing it with Legendre’s calculation. Given that these deviations exhibit wild fluctuations, a natural question arises: What is their statistical behaviour? In 1973, Goldston and Montgomery
[
GM87
]
showed that the variance of
\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)
for
n\in[1,N]
is
\sim H\log\frac{N}{H}
in the range of
H\in\left[N^{\epsilon},N^{1-\epsilon}\right]
under the Riemann hypothesis (RH) and the strong pair correlation conjecture
[
Mon73
]
, which respectively concern the horizontal and vertical distribution or correlation of zeros.

Relatively recently, in 2004, Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
MS04
]
showed that the distribution of
\psi(n+H)-\psi(n)
for
n\in[1,N]
is approximately normal with mean

\sim H
and variance
\sim H\log\frac{N}{H},
provided that

\frac{H}{\log N}\to\infty
and
\frac{\log H}{\log N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty,
by computing higher moments
under a uniform Hardy–Littlewood prime
k
-tuple conjecture
[
HL23
]
, which concerns the correlation of primes. They further conjectured that in the range of

H\in\left[(\log N)^{1+\delta},N^{1-\delta}\right],
the normality persists.

In this paper, we revisit the “Fourier side”. However, rather than relying on the analytic nature of the pair correlation conjecture, we adapt the method of Rubinstein and Sarnak
[
RS94
]
, and assume the linear independence over
\mathbb{Q}
of the positive ordinates of nontrivial zeros (LI), which is an algebraic assumption. This is the first instance where the emphasis is not placed on the distribution of primes or irreducible polynomials among congruence classes.

Given a large
X
and
x\in[2,X].

As we will see, for
h=h(x)=\delta x,
where
\delta>0
is small but independent of
X,
which is beyond the conjectural range of Montgomery and Soundararajan above, the distribution of
\psi(x+h)-\psi(x)
for
x\in[2,X]
(in logarithmic scale) remains to be Gaussian under RH and LI (see
Theorem
3.1
or
Theorem
3.2
with
r=1
).

Furthermore, one may ask: What is the joint distribution of the weighted count of primes in two neighbouring intervals? Are they independent? If not, how are they correlated?
In fact, we show that assuming RH and LI, the weighted prime count

\left(\psi(x)-\psi(x-h),\psi(x+h)-\psi(x)\right)

for
x\in[2,X]
(in logarithmic scale) is approximately bivariate Gaussian with weak negative correlation.

Corollary 1.1
.

Assume RH and LI. Given a Borel subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2},
define

\displaystyle S_{X,\delta;B}:=\left\{x\in[2,X]\,:\,\frac{\left(\psi(x)-\psi(x-\delta x)-\delta x,\psi(x+\delta x)-\psi(x)-\delta x\right)}{\sqrt{\left(\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}+(1-\gamma-\log 2\pi)\delta\right)x}}\in B\right\}.

Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log X}\int_{S_{X,\delta;B}}\frac{dx}{x}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\mathcal{C}}}\int_{B}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\right)d\bm{x}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)

with the covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\frac{\log 2}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\\
-\frac{\log 2}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}&1\end{pmatrix}.

In particular, it may come as a surprise that primes in two neighboring intervals are aware of and avoid each other. More generally, we show that the weighted count of primes in
multiple disjoint short intervals has a multivariate Gaussian (logarithmic) limiting
distribution with weak negative correlation under RH and LI (see Theorem
3.1
or Theorem
3.2
).

Remark 1.1
.

To derive Corollary
1.1
from Theorem
3.1
, one can simply adapt the proof of Corollary
3.1
(see Section
8
for more details).

In section
9
, further assuming a quantitative formulation of the LI hypothesis, we demonstrate that for primes in a single moving interval, the normality persists as long as
\delta>(\log X)^{-\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
(see Theorem
9.1
).

2.
Preliminaries

Let us introduce several quantities that will appear throughout the paper.

Given an integer
r\geqslant 1,
real numbers
x\geqslant 2,\delta>0
and a vector
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},
we denote by

\bm{E}(x;\delta,\bm{t})
the
r
-tuple

(E(x;\delta,t_{1}),\ldots,E(x;\delta,t_{r})),
where

\displaystyle E(x;\delta,t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\left[\psi\left((1+t\delta)x+\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\psi\left((1+t\delta)x-\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\delta x\right],

i.e. the normalized deviation of the weighted prime count in the short interval of length
\delta x
centred at
(1+t\delta)x.

Here, we always assume
\delta
is small so that

2\leqslant(1+t\delta)x-\delta x/2\leqslant(1+t\delta)x+\delta x/2\leqslant 2x.

To simplify our discussion, we also require that
|t_{j}-t_{k}|\geqslant 1
whenever
j\neq k,
i.e. the intervals are disjoint.

Remark 2.1
.

In logarithmic scale, it is not unnatural to study primes in “multiplicative intervals”
[\exp((t-\frac{1}{2})\delta)x,\exp(t+\frac{1}{2})\delta)x]
instead of “additive intervals”
[(1+t\delta)x-\frac{1}{2}\delta x,(1+t\delta)x+\frac{1}{2}\delta x].
In particular, some calculations in the proof of
2.2
can be simplified. Nevertheless, the difference is minimal, since
\exp(\delta)=1+\delta+O(\delta^{2})
as
\delta\to 0^{+}.

Given a subset
S
of
[r]:=\{1,\ldots,r\},
we define

\displaystyle T_{S}:=1+\max_{j\in S}|t_{j}|.

For
s\in\mathbb{C},
we define

\displaystyle w(s)=w(s;\delta,t):=\frac{1}{s}\left[\left(1+\left(t+\frac{1}{2}\right)\delta\right)^{s}-\left(1+\left(t-\frac{1}{2}\right)\delta\right)^{s}\right]

and write
w_{j}(s):=w(s;\delta,t_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,r.

Inspired by the work of Rubinstein–Sarnak
[
RS94
]
on primes in multiple arithmetic progressions to a large modulus, we study the joint distribution of primes in multiple short intervals with respect to the logarithmic density under RH and LI.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\bm{Y}(x)
be a
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-valued function. We say that
\bm{Y}(x)
has a logarithmic limiting distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}^{\log}\left[f\left(\bm{Y}(x)\right)\right]:=

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log X}\int_{2}^{X}f(\bm{Y}(x))\frac{dx}{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{U\to\infty}\frac{1}{U}\int_{1}^{U}f(\bm{Y}(e^{u}))du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{r}}f(\bm{y})d\mu(\bm{y})

for all bounded continuous functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{r},

i.e. (logarithmic) time average equals space average.

If such a measure
\mu
exists, then given a Borel subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r},
we shall represent
\mu(B)
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\bm{Y}(x)\in B\right):=\mathbb{E}_{x}^{\log}\left[1_{B}\left(\bm{Y}(x)\right)\right],

where
1_{B}(\bm{x})
is the indicator function of the Borel subset
B.

In preparation for our main result Theorem
3.1
below, we establish the following propositions.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
r\geqslant 1,\delta>0
and
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
be fixed. Assume RH. Then

\bm{E}(x;\delta,\bm{t})
has a logarithmic limiting distribution
\mu_{\delta,\bm{t}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{r}.
Furthermore, if LI is also assumed in addition to RH, then
\mu_{\delta,\bm{t}}
is an absolutely continuous probability measure corresponding to the
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-valued random vector
\bm{X}_{\delta,\bm{t}}=(X_{\delta,t_{1}},\ldots,X_{\delta,t_{r}}),
where

\displaystyle X_{\delta,t}:=\mathrm{Re}\left(2\sum_{\gamma>0}w(\rho)U_{\gamma}\right)

with
\{U_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma>0}
being a sequence of independent random variables uniformly distributed on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}.

Moreover, the covariance matrix of
\bm{X}_{\delta,\bm{t}}
is real symmetric with the
(j,k)
-entry being

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{jk}=\mathrm{Cov}_{jk}(\delta,\bm{t}):=\sum_{\gamma}w_{j}(\rho)\overline{w_{k}(\rho)}.

To lighten the notation, for
j=1,\ldots,r,
we denote the variance
\mathrm{Cov}_{jj}
by
V_{j}.

Proposition 2.2
.

Assume RH and LI. Let
\delta>0
and
T=T_{j,k}\leqslant\delta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}.
Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}_{jk}=\begin{cases}\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}+(1-\gamma-\log 2\pi)\delta+O\left(\left(T\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)&\mbox{{if $j=k,$ } }\\
-\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)\delta+O\left(\left(T\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)&\mbox{{if $j\neq k,$ } }\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\Delta(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left((t+1)\log(t+1)-2t\log t+(t-1)\log(t-1)\right),

i.e. the second order central difference of the function
f(t)=\frac{1}{2}t\log t.

Note that
\Delta(1)=\log 2
and

\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)>0
in general. Also,
as
|t_{j}-t_{k}|\to\infty,
we have
\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)\to 0^{+}
monotonically and more precisely,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)\sim\frac{1}{2|t_{j}-t_{k}|},

which can be interpreted as “Coulomb’s law”. Therefore, primes in disjoint short intervals repel each other, albeit very weakly. Moreover, the larger the gap between the intervals, the weaker the repulsion.

Remark 2.2
.

The appearance of the secondary term
(1-\gamma-\log 2\pi)\delta
is expected as the variance computed by Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
MS02
]
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{X}\int_{1}^{X}\left(\psi(x+H)-\psi(x)-H\right)^{2}dx\sim H\log\frac{X}{H}-(\gamma+\log 2\pi)H

for
X^{\epsilon}\leqslant H\leqslant X^{1-\epsilon}
under a uniform Hardy–Littlewood prime
k
-tuple conjecture. Besides, Chan
[
Cha02
]
derived the same expression under a refined strong pair correlation conjecture.

3.
Main results

In view of Proposition
2.1
, we shall state our main theorems in terms of the renormalized deviation

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t}):=\left(\frac{E(x;\delta,t_{1})}{\sqrt{V_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{E(x;\delta,t_{r})}{\sqrt{V_{r}}}\right).

Making a suitable change of variables, one can show that

\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})
also has a logarithmic limiting distribution. In particular, for any Borel subset
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r},
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})\in B\right)=\mu_{\delta,\bm{t}}(\widetilde{B}),

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\,:\,\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{V_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{x_{r}}{\sqrt{V_{r}}}\right)\in B\right\}.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume RH and LI. Given a small
\delta>0
and an integer
r\geqslant 1
satisfying
r=o\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Let
T=T_{[r]}\leqslant\delta^{-\frac{1}{10}}
and
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
be a Borel subset.
Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})\in B\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}(\det\mathcal{C})^{1/2}}\int_{B}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\right)d\bm{x}

\displaystyle+O\left(r^{4}T^{3}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+O\left(\frac{\log 2r}{\log 1/\delta}\right)\right)^{r}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}\mathrm{meas}(B)\right),

where

\mathcal{C}=(c_{jk})_{r\times r}
is the “correlation matrix” with
entries

\displaystyle c_{jk}=c_{jk}(\delta,\bm{t}):=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}_{jk}}{\sqrt{V_{j}V_{k}}},

i.e. the renormalized deviation of the weighted prime count in multiple short intervals of length
\delta x
is approximately normal with mean
\bm{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathcal{C}
as
\delta\to 0^{+}.

While our error term here depends on the measure, the estimate can be made uniform for any Borel subset if there are not too many intervals.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume RH and LI. Given a small
\delta>0
and an
integer
1\leqslant r\leqslant\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\log\log 1/\delta}.
Let
T=T_{[r]}\leqslant\delta^{-\frac{1}{10}}
and
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}
be a Borel subset.
Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})\in B\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}(\det\mathcal{C})^{1/2}}\int_{B}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\right)d\bm{x}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{r^{4}T^{3}}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}\min\left\{\mathrm{meas}(B),\left(20\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{r/2}\right\}\right).

In particular, the total variation distance

\displaystyle\sup_{B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}\,:\,B\,\text{Borel}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})\in B\right)-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathcal{C})\in B)\right|\ll r^{4}T^{3}\delta\left(\frac{10}{\pi}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{r/2-1},

where
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\mathcal{C})
is an
r
-dimensional Gaussian random variable with mean
\bm{0}
and covariance matrix
\mathcal{C}.

As mentioned after Proposition
2.2
, the (negative) correlation between primes in two intervals is asymptotically inversely proportional to their gap. Therefore, informally speaking, weighted prime counts in multiple short intervals resemble jointly normally distributed point charges.

Remark 3.1
.

On the “Fourier side”, the logarithm of the modulus of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line
\log|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)|
for
t\in[T,2T]
resembles a log-correlated Gaussian field (see
[
Bou10
, Theorem 1.1]
). In particular, if the gap between shifts
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
satisfies
\frac{1}{\log T}\ll|h_{1}-h_{2}|\ll 1
, then the (positive) correlation between
\log|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it+ih_{1})|
and
\log|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it+ih_{2})|
is asymptotically proportional to
\log\left(|h_{2}-h_{2}|^{-1}\right).
It would then be natural to explore races among
\log|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it+ih_{j})|
for many
j
’s.

Many interesting consequences can be derived from Theorem
3.1
or Theorem
3.2
, in particular Corollaries
3.4
,
3.5
and
3.6
, which concern a sharp phase transition from all races being asymptotically unbiased to the existence of biased races.

First of all, when the number of intervals does not grow with
\delta
as
\delta\to 0^{+},
one can detect the negative correlation explicitly.

Corollary 3.1
.

Assume RH and LI.
Suppose both
r
and
T=T_{[r]}
are uniformly bounded with respect to
\delta
. Let

R_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]
be a box (not necessarily bounded) in
\mathbb{R}^{r}.

Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})\in R_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}\right)=\Phi(R_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}})-\frac{1}{2\pi\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)

\displaystyle\times\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{j}^{2}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}}-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{k}^{2}}\right)\Phi\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j,k\end{subarray}}^{r}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]\right)+O_{r,T}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

where

\displaystyle\Phi(R_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{R_{\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}}d\bm{x}.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(E(x;\delta,t_{1}),E(x;\delta,t_{2}),\ldots,E(x;\delta,t_{r})>0\right)=\frac{1}{2^{r}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2^{r-2}}\times\frac{1}{2\pi\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)

\displaystyle+O_{r,T}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

and similarly

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(E(x;\delta,t_{1}),E(x;\delta,t_{2}),\ldots,E(x;\delta,t_{r})<0\right)=\frac{1}{2^{r}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2^{r-2}}\times\frac{1}{2\pi\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)

\displaystyle+O_{r,T}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

i.e. it is less likely that the normalized deviation of weighted prime counts in short intervals are either all positive or all negative, as one might expect.

Moreover, we have the short-interval counterpart of the Shanks–Rényi prime number race with several arithmetic progressions to a large modulus (see
[
Lam13
, Theorem 2.1]
). To simply notation, let us denote

\displaystyle\rho(\delta;\bm{t}):=\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{1})>\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{2})>\cdots>\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{r})\right).

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume RH and LI.
Suppose both
r
and
T=T_{[r]}
are uniformly bounded with respect to
\delta
. Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},

we have

\displaystyle\rho(\delta;\bm{t})=\frac{1}{r!}-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}\frac{\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{x_{1}>\cdots>x_{r}}x_{j}x_{k}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}}d\bm{x}+O_{r,T}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

In practice, for each
j
, one can replace
\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{j})
above by

\displaystyle\psi(x;\delta,t_{j}):=\psi\left((1+t_{j}\delta)x+\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\psi\left((1+t_{j}\delta)x-\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right).

For instance, in the case of three consecutive intervals, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\psi(x;\delta,\mp 1)>\psi(x;\delta,0)>\psi(x;\delta,\pm 1)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}-\frac{(\log 4-\log 3)\sqrt{3}}{4\pi\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{6}-\frac{0.039652}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}.

Meanwhile, the probability for each of the remaining four orderings is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}+\frac{(\log 4-\log 3)\sqrt{3}}{8\pi\log\frac{1}{\delta}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\approx\frac{1}{6}+\frac{0.019826}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}},

i.e. it is less likely that the weighted counts of primes in three consecutive intervals (from left to right) respectively are in either ascending or descending order.

On the other hand, we also establish a large deviation result for the
L^{2}
-norm of the deviation of prime count (see
[
Lam12
, Theorem 1]
in the context of the Shanks–Rényi prime number race).

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume RH and LI.
Suppose both
r
and
T=T_{[r]}
are uniformly bounded with respect to
\delta
. Then as
\delta\to 0^{+},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\|\widetilde{\bm{E}}(x;\delta,\bm{t})\|>V\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{\|\bm{x}\|>V}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\right)d\bm{x}+O_{r,T}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

When the number of intervals
r
is not necessarily bounded as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have the following short-interval analog of the prime number race with many arithmetic progressions (see
[
HL18
, Theorem 1.2]
).

Corollary 3.4
.

Assume RH and LI. Given a small
\delta>0
and
an integer
1\leqslant r\leqslant\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\log\log 1/\delta}.

If
T_{[r]}\leqslant\delta^{-\frac{1}{10}},
then as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\rho(\delta;\bm{t})=\frac{1}{r!}\left(1+O\left(\frac{r\log 2r}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right).

Therefore, all
r
-way prime number races remain asymptotically unbiased as long as
r=o\left(\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\log\log 1/\delta}\right)
. When
r\asymp\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\log\log 1/\delta}
, however, it turns out there exist
r
intervals such that the corresponding prime number race is noticeably biased (see
[
HL18
, Theorem 3]
and
[
FHL19
, Theorem 1.2]
in the context of the Shanks–Rényi prime number race). As in
[
HL18
]
and
[
FHL19
]
, we first introduce the density

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(\delta;\bm{t}):=\mathbb{P}_{x}^{\log}\left(\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{1})>\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{2})>\cdots>\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{s})>\max_{s<j\leqslant r}\widetilde{E}(x;\delta,t_{j})\right),

which concerns the ordering of the first
s
contestants in a prime number race with
r
competitors.

Corollary 3.5
.

Assume RH and LI.
Let
\epsilon>0.
Then there exists
\eta=\eta(\epsilon)>0
such that the following holds. Given a small
\delta>0
and
integers
1\leqslant r\leqslant\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\log\log 1/\delta},1\leqslant s\leqslant\eta r.

If
T_{[r]}\leqslant\delta^{-\frac{1}{10}},

then as soon as
s\to\infty
as
\delta\to 0^{+},
we have

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(\delta;\bm{t})\leqslant\exp\left(o(1)-(2-\epsilon)\times\frac{\log(r/s)}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j<k\leqslant s\end{subarray}}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)\right)\frac{(r-s)!}{r!},

provided that
|t_{j}-t_{k}|\geqslant\log\frac{1}{\delta}
whenever
\max\{j,k\}>s
for

\displaystyle=\prod_{\gamma>0}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left(2i\mathrm{Re}\left(-\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right)e^{2\pi i\theta}\right)\right)d\theta.

Making a suitable change of variables, this becomes

\displaystyle\prod_{\gamma>0}\int_{0}^{1}\exp\left(2i\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|\cos\left(2\pi\theta\right)\right)d\theta=\prod_{\gamma>0}J_{0}\left(2\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|\right),

and hence the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

We define the large spectrum as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi}):=\left\{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\,:\,\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|>\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}\|\bm{\xi}\|\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})\right|>\frac{1}{250rT^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Proof.

We define

\displaystyle S_{\delta}(\bm{\xi}):=\sum_{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|^{2}.

Opening the square, this becomes

(6.1)

\begin{gathered}\sum_{1\leqslant j,k,\leqslant r}\left(\sum_{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}w_{j}(\rho)\overline{w_{k}(\rho)}\right)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\sum_{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}|w_{j}(\rho)|^{2}\right)|\xi_{j}|^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant j\neq k\leqslant r}\left(\sum_{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\mathrm{Re}\left(w_{j}(\rho)\overline{w_{k}(\rho)}\right)\right)\xi_{j}\xi_{k}.\end{gathered}

By the proof of Proposition
2.2
, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma>\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}|w_{j}(\rho)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin\left(\delta t/2\right)}{t}\right)^{2}\log\frac{t}{2\pi}dt+O(\delta).

Making the change of variables
x=\delta t/2,
the first term of the right-hand side becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\delta\int_{\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right)^{2}\log\left(\frac{x}{\pi\delta}\right)dx\ll\delta.

Combining (
6.2
) with Proposition
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}|w_{j}(\rho)|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}+O(\delta).

Similarly, one can show that

\displaystyle\sum_{0<\gamma\leqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\mathrm{Re}\left(w_{j}(\rho)\overline{w_{k}(\rho)}\right)\ll\delta.

Therefore, the expression (
6.1
) becomes

(6.3)

\displaystyle S_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})=\left(\frac{1}{2}\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}+O\left(r\delta\right)\right)\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}.

On the other hand, we split
S_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})
into

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|^{2}+\sum_{\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\setminus\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|^{2}.

Using (
4.4
) followed by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the sum over the large spectrum is

(6.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|^{2}\leqslant(10T\delta)^{2}r\left|\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})\right|\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2}.

By the definition of the large spectrum, the remaining sum is

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\setminus\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant N\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}\|\xi\|\right)^{2}

(6.5)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{4\pi}\delta\|\xi\|^{2}.

Then, the lemma follows from comparing (
6.4
), (
6.2
) with (
6.3
).
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Proof.

Suppose
\|\bm{\xi}\|>2.
Since
|J_{0}(x)|\leqslant 1
for

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\mu}_{\delta,\bm{t}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\bm{\xi}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\prod_{\gamma>0}\left|J_{0}\left(2\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|\right)\right|

On the other hand, suppose
\epsilon<\|\bm{\xi}\|\leqslant 2.
Since

is a positive decreasing function on

we have

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\mu}_{\delta,\bm{t}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\bm{\xi}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\prod_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\delta}(\bm{\xi})}\left|J_{0}\left(2\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}(\rho)\xi_{j}\right|\right)\right|

Then, it follows immediately from Lemma

∎

Lemma 6.4
.

\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
6.1

\displaystyle\log\hat{\mu}_{\delta,\bm{t}}\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\sqrt{V_{j}}},\ldots,\frac{\xi_{r}}{\sqrt{V_{r}}}\right)=\sum_{\gamma>0}\log J_{0}\left(2\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{w_{j}(\rho)}{\sqrt{V_{j}}}\xi_{j}\right|\right).

\displaystyle\log J_{0}(x)=-\frac{1}{4}x^{2}+O(x^{4})

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma>0}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{w_{j}(\rho)}{\sqrt{V_{j}}}\xi_{j}\right|^{2}+O\left(\sum_{\gamma>0}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{w_{j}(\rho)}{\sqrt{V_{j}}}\xi_{j}\right|^{4}\right).

On the other hand, by Lemma

(6.15)

7.
Proof of Theorem
3.2

Let us begin with a lemma about the determinant and inverse of
almost identity matrices.

Lemma 7.1
.

Define
M_{r}(\epsilon)
as the set of all
r\times r

symmetric matrices whose diagonal entries are
1
, and whose off-diagonal entries have
absolute value at most
\epsilon\leqslant 1/2r.

Let
A=(a_{jk})
be a matrix in
M_{r}(\epsilon).
Then

(7.1)

\displaystyle\det(A)=1+O\left(\sum_{1\leqslant j\neq k\leqslant r}|a_{jk}|^{2}\right).

Moreover, the matrix
A
is invertible and if
\tilde{a}_{jk}
’s denote the entries of
the inverse matrix
A^{-1},
then

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{jk}=\begin{cases}1+O\left(\sum_{1\leqslant l\neq m\leqslant r}|a_{lm}|^{2}\right)&\mbox{{if $j=k,$} }\\
-a_{jk}+O\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
i\neq j,k\end{subarray}}|a_{ji}a_{ik}|+\epsilon\sum_{1\leqslant l\neq m\leqslant r}|a_{lm}|^{2}\right)&\mbox{{if $j\neq k.$} }\end{cases}

To apply the lemma, let
1\leqslant r\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}
and
\epsilon=\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}.
Then, by Proposition
2.2
, the matrix

\mathcal{C}
is in
M_{r}(\epsilon)
as the entries

\displaystyle|c_{jk}|=\frac{\left|\mathrm{Cov}_{jk}\right|}{\sqrt{V_{j}V_{k}}}\leqslant\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}

for
1\leqslant j\neq k\leqslant r.

Proof.

This is a more precise version of a lemma of Harper and Lamzouri
[
HL18
, Lemma 3.3]
. Let us estimate the determinant first. We have

(7.2)

\displaystyle\det(A)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{r}\\
\sigma\neq e\end{subarray}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)a_{1,\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{r,\sigma(r)},

where
S_{r}
denotes the symmetric group on
r
elements. We divide the sum according to the number of points
t
that are not fixed by
\sigma.
Note that the only term
with
t=0
is the identity permutation
e
, which has been isolated from the sum. There are no
terms with
t=1,
and the contribution from
t=2
is at most

(7.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}|a_{jk}|^{2}.

For each
3\leqslant t\leqslant r
, by averaging the total contribution is at most

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{r},\sigma(j)\neq j\\
\text{$\sigma$ has $t$ non-fixed points}\end{subarray}}|a_{j,\sigma(j)}||a_{\sigma^{-1}(j),j}|\epsilon^{t-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k,l\leqslant r\\
j\neq k,l\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}a_{lj}|\epsilon^{t-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{r},\sigma(j)=k,\sigma^{-1}(j)=l\\
\text{$\sigma$ has $t$ non-fixed points}\end{subarray}}1.

We split the sum into the diagonal sum

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k=l}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k\leqslant r\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}|^{2}\epsilon^{t-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{r},\sigma(j)=k,\sigma^{-1}(j)=k\\
\text{$\sigma$ has $t$ non-fixed points}\end{subarray}}1

and the off-diagonal sum

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k\neq l}:=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k,l\leqslant r\\
j,k,l\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}a_{lj}|\epsilon^{t-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{r},\sigma(j)=k,\sigma^{-1}(j)=l\\
\text{$\sigma$ has $t$ non-fixed points}\end{subarray}}1.

Then by simple combinatorics, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k=l}\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k\leqslant r\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}|^{2}\epsilon^{t-2}{r-2\choose t-2}(t-1)!

(7.4)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(\epsilon r)^{t-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k\leqslant r\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}|^{2}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k\neq l}\leqslant\epsilon^{t-2}r^{t-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k,l\leqslant r\\
j,k,l\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}a_{lj}|,

which is by the AM-GM inequality

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\epsilon^{t-2}r^{t-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k,l\leqslant r\\
j,k,l\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}(|a_{jk}|^{2}+|a_{lj}|^{2})

(7.5)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(\epsilon r)^{t-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k\leqslant r\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}|^{2}.

Therefore, the expression (
7.1
) follows from combining (
7.2
), (
7.3
), (
7
) and (
7
). Also, since by assumption
|a_{jk}|\leqslant\epsilon\leqslant 1/2r
, the determinant

\displaystyle\det(A)\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k\leqslant r\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}|^{2}-2\sum_{3\leqslant t\leqslant r}(\epsilon r)^{t-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j,k\leqslant r\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}|a_{jk}|^{2}\geqslant\frac{3}{8}.

In particular, the matrix
A
is invertible.

It remains to estimate the entries of the inverse matrix
A^{-1}.

Recall that

(7.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{jk}=(-1)^{j+k}\frac{\det A_{kj}}{\det A},

where
A_{kj}
denotes the matrix
A
with the
k
-th row and the
j
-th column removed. If
j=k,
then

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{jj}=\frac{\det A_{jj}}{\det A}.

Since
A_{jj}\in M_{r-1}(\epsilon),
it follows from (
7.1
) that

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{jj}=1+O\left(\sum_{1\leqslant l\neq m\leqslant r}|a_{lm}|^{2}\right).

Without loss of generality, suppose
j<k.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{kj}
denote the set of bijections from
[r]\setminus\{k\}
to
[r]\setminus\{j\}.
Then

(7.7)

\displaystyle\det A_{kj}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{kj}}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i\neq k}a_{i,\sigma(i)},

where
\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma):=\mathrm{sgn}(\tilde{\sigma})
with
\tilde{\sigma}\in S_{[r]\setminus\{k\}}
denoting the permutation

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(i):=\begin{cases}\sigma(i)-1&\mbox{{if $j<\sigma(i)\leqslant k,$ } }\\
\sigma(i)&\mbox{{otherwise } }\end{cases}

for
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{kj}.
Again, we divide the sum (
7.7
) according to the number of points
t
that are not fixed by
\sigma.
Note that there are no terms with
t=0,
and the only term with
t=1
is
a_{jk},
whose contribution to (
7.6
) is

\displaystyle(-1)^{j+k}\frac{(-1)^{k-j+1}a_{jk}}{\det A}=-a_{jk}+O\left(\epsilon\sum_{1\leqslant l\neq m\leqslant r}|a_{lm}|^{2}\right)

using (
7.1
).
Arguing similarly as above, one can show that the remaining contribution from
t\geqslant 2

is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
i\neq j,k\end{subarray}}|a_{ji}a_{ik}|+\epsilon\sum_{1\leqslant l\neq m\leqslant r}|a_{lm}|^{2},

and hence the lemma follows.
∎

We can now estimate the probability density function of the
r

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}(\det\mathcal{C})^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\|\bm{x}\|^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}c_{jk}x_{j}x_{k}\right).

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant k\leqslant r\\

\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{C})=1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det(\mathcal{C})^{1/2}}=1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right).

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle

Lemma 7.3

\displaystyle\left(1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{x_{1}>\cdots>x_{r}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log 2r}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\right)d\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\frac{1}{r!}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log 2r}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\right)dx\right)^{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r!}\left(1+O\left(\frac{r\log 2r}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right).

Using Stirling’s formula, one can show that the error term in (

∎

Proof of Corollary
3.5
.

Let

We define

\displaystyle H_{r,s;R}:=\{x_{1}>\cdots>x_{s}>\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j},\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leqslant R\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{r,s;R}:=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\,:\,\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{V_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{x_{r}}{\sqrt{V_{r}}}\right)\in H_{r,s;R}\right\}.

Applying

with Proposition

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(\delta;\bm{t})=\mu_{\delta;\bm{t}}(\widetilde{H}_{r,s;R})+O\left(r\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{4}\right)\right).

Then, it follows from Theorem

that

\displaystyle\mu_{\delta;\bm{t}}(\widetilde{H}_{r,s;R})=

(8.10)

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{r^{4}T^{3}}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}\mathrm{meas}(\widetilde{H}_{r,s;R})\right).

Note that by symmetry, we have

so that the error term here is

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(r-s)!}{r!}\times\frac{r^{4}T^{3}}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{-1}(2R)^{r}.

On the other hand, since

\displaystyle H_{r,s}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}=H_{r,s;R}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}.

Therefore, by Lemma
7.4
, the main term of (

(8.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}(\det\mathcal{C})^{1/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{C}^{-1}\bm{x},\bm{x}\rangle\right)d\bm{x}+O\left(\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{4}\right)\right).

Appealing to Lemma
7.2
, the main term here is

(8.13)

\displaystyle\frac{1+o(1)}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\|\bm{x}\|^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}c_{jk}x_{j}x_{k}\right)d\bm{x}

Using the assumption that

whenever

for

it follows from Proposition
2.2

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant r}c_{jk}x_{j}x_{k}=-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant s}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)x_{j}x_{k}+O\left(\frac{r}{\log^{2}\frac{1}{\delta}}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\right).

Since

we conclude that (

\displaystyle\frac{1+o(1)}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant s}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)x_{j}x_{k}\right)d\bm{x}.

To generate a strong negative bias, let
M=\sqrt{(2-\epsilon)\log(r/s)}.
Then, again by Proposition
7.2
, the contribution from those
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
for which
\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\leqslant M
to (

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\leqslant M\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\log 2r}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\right)d\bm{x}

\displaystyle\ll\left(1+O\left(\frac{r\log 2r}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)\right)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\leqslant M\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\right)d\bm{x},

which is simply

(8.15)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\leqslant M\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\right)d\bm{x}.

We further decompose the domain of integration as

\displaystyle H_{r,s}\cap\{\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\leqslant M\}=H_{0}\sqcup H_{1}\cdots\sqcup H_{s},

where

\displaystyle H_{i}:=\{x_{1}>\cdots>x_{i}>M\geqslant x_{i+1}>\cdots>x_{s}>\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\}.

Then, the integral over each

is computable and is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{i}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i!}(1-\Phi(M))^{i}\times\frac{(r-s)!}{(r-i)!}\Phi(M)^{r-i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r-s)!}{r!}\times{r\choose i}(1-\Phi(M))^{i}\Phi(M)^{r-i},

where

Therefore, the expression (

\displaystyle\ll\frac{(r-s)!}{r!}\sum_{0\leqslant i\leqslant s}{r\choose i}(1-\Phi(M))^{i}\Phi(M)^{r-i}.

To bound the sum, let
X\sim b(n,p)
be a binomial random variable with parameters

and

If

then Chernoff bound gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant nq)=\sum_{0\leqslant k\leqslant nq}{n\choose k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}\leqslant e^{-nD(q\|p)},

where the divergence

\displaystyle D(q\|p):=q\log\frac{q}{p}+(1-q)\log\frac{1-q}{1-p}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leqslant i\leqslant s}{r\choose i}(1-\Phi(M))^{i}\Phi(M)^{r-i}\leqslant\exp\left(-s\log\frac{s/r}{1-\Phi(M)}-(r-s)\log\frac{1-(s/r)}{\Phi(M)}\right).

Let
\eta=\eta(\epsilon)>0
such that

Then, one can show that this is

\leqslant\exp(-10s)
so that (

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(r-s)!}{r!}\times e^{-10s}=o\left(\frac{(r-s)!}{r!}\right)

as soon as

as
\delta\to 0^{+}.
Finally, we are left with the integral

\displaystyle\frac{1+o(1)}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}\cap\{\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}>M\}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant s}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)x_{j}x_{k}\right)d\bm{x},

which is

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(o(1)-\frac{M^{2}}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant s}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)\right)\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{r/2}}\int_{H_{r,s}\cap\{\|\bm{x}\|\leqslant R\}\cap\{\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}>M\}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{x}\|^{2}}d\bm{x}.

Since one can argue analogously that the contribution from those
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
for which
\|\bm{x}\|>R
or
\max_{s<j\leqslant r}x_{j}\leqslant M
is again negligible, by substituting

this is

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(o(1)-(2-\epsilon)\times\frac{\log(r/s)}{\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\sum_{1\leqslant j<k\leqslant s}\Delta(|t_{j}-t_{k}|)\right)\frac{(r-s)!}{r!}.

Using Stirling’s formula, one can show that the error terms in (

), (

) and (

) are negligible, and hence the corollary follows.
∎

9.
Primes in shorter intervals

One unfortunate drawback of the Rubinstein–Sarnak approach is the qualitative nature of the LI hypothesis, which prevents achieving uniformity of
\delta
in
X
for
x\in[X,2X].
Therefore, to address shorter intervals, i.e.
\delta=\delta(X)\to 0
as
X\to\infty
, we shall propose the following conjecture.

Conjecture 9.1

(Quantitative linear independence conjecture (QLI))
.

Let
k\geqslant 2.
Then there exists a constant
c_{k}>k
such that
for any
\bm{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{k},
we have

(9.1)

\displaystyle\#\{\bm{\gamma}\in[0,T]^{k}\,:\,0<|\langle\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\gamma}\rangle|\leqslant T^{-c_{k}}\}=o_{k}(N(T)^{k/2})

as
T\to\infty,
where

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\gamma}\rangle:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\epsilon_{j}\gamma_{j}.

The heuristic is as follows. To justify our choice of
T^{-c_{k}}
with
c_{k}>k,
note that the average gap of

\langle\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\gamma}\rangle\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 1)
is
(2N(T))^{-k}\asymp_{k}(T\log T)^{-k}.
On the other hand,
assuming LI, the alternating sum
\langle\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\gamma}\rangle

vanishes if and only if
k
is even and
[k]
can be partitioned into
k/2
pairs
\{j,j^{\prime}\}

of which
\epsilon_{j^{\prime}}=-\epsilon_{j}
and
\gamma_{j^{\prime}}=\gamma_{j}.
We call these
\bm{\gamma}
’s diagonal. As there are
\asymp_{k}N(T)^{k/2}
such vectors, the conjecture can be reinterpreted as
100\%
of the
\bm{\gamma}
’s satisfying

|\langle\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\gamma}\rangle|\leqslant T^{-c_{k}}

are diagonal.

For future reference, let
g(T)\leqslant\log T
be a function such that
g(T)\to\infty
as
T\to\infty
so that the left-hand side of (
9.1
) is

(9.2)

\displaystyle\ll_{k}N(T)^{k/2}/g(T).

Remark 9.1
.

Interestingly, Lamzouri
[
Lam23
]
very recently formulated an effective linear independence conjecture (ELI), which is stronger than our QLI to obtain omega results for the error term in the prime number theorem. They are conjectured to be best possible by Montgomery.

With QLI, we can now show that primes in a short moving interval of length
h=h(x)=\delta x
is approximately normal for
x\in[1,X]
(in logarithmic scale) as long as
\delta=\delta(X)>(\log X)^{-\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0.

Theorem 9.1
.

Assume RH, LI and QLI. Given real numbers
U,\delta>0.
Suppose
\delta=o(1)
but
\log(1/\delta)=o(\log U).

If
u\in[U,2U]
is chosen uniformly at random, then
we have the convergence in distribution to a standard Gaussian

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\quad\text{as $U\to\infty$,}

where we recall that

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}\left(x;\delta,0\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V(\delta,0)\times x}}\left(\psi\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\psi\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\delta x\right)

with
V(\delta,0)=\sum_{\gamma}|w(\rho)|^{2},

i.e. for any fixed real numbers
\alpha<\beta,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{U}\mathop{\mathrm{meas}}\left\{x\in[U,2U]\,:\,\widetilde{E}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\in(\alpha,\beta]\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+o(1).

Remark 9.2
.

Invoking Proposition
2.2
, in the above theorem
\widetilde{E}\left(x;\delta,0\right)

can be replaced by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}+(1-\gamma-2\pi)\delta\right)x}}\left(\psi\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\psi\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\delta x\right)-\delta x\right).

Before we embark on the proof of the theorem, we compute the moments of the finite approximation

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right):=\frac{E^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)}{\sqrt{V^{(T)}(\delta,0)}},

where we recall that

\displaystyle E^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)=

\displaystyle-\sum_{|\gamma|\leqslant T}w(\rho)e^{i\gamma u}

and

\displaystyle V^{(T)}(\delta,0):=

\displaystyle\sum_{|\gamma|\leqslant T}\left|w(\rho)\right|^{2}

for some
T\geqslant 2
such that
\delta T\to\infty
as
U\to\infty.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
\delta>0,T\geqslant 2.
Then

\displaystyle V^{(T)}(\delta,0)=V(\delta,0)+O\left(\frac{\log T}{T}\right).

Proof.

Since
w(\rho)\ll|\gamma|^{-1},
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{|\gamma|>T}|w(\rho)|^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{|\gamma|>T}\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{\log t}{t^{2}}dt

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\log T}{T},

and hence the lemma follows.

∎

Instead of the Lebesgue measure, the moments are computed with respect to a nonnegative Schwartz function
W
supported on
\left(1/2,5/2\right)
of unit mass (see

]
for instance). In particular, both
W
and its Fourier transform
\widehat{W}
are rapidly decaying in the sense that for any

we have

(9.3)

\displaystyle W(u),\,\widehat{W}(u)\ll_{A}(1+|u|)^{-A},

where the Fourier transform
\widehat{W}
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{W}(v):=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}W(u)e^{-iuv}du.

For convenience, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(f(u)\right):=\frac{1}{U}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)W\left(\frac{u}{U}\right)du.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 1
be an integer and
\delta\to 0
as
U\to\infty.
If
T\geqslant 2
satisfies

but

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left({\widetilde{E}^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)}^{k}\right)=\mu_{k}+o_{k}(1),

where

\displaystyle\mu_{k}:=\begin{cases}\frac{k!}{2^{k/2}(k/2)!}&\mbox{{if $k$ is even,} }\\
0&\mbox{{otherwise. }}\end{cases}

Remark 9.3
.

Without assuming LI but only RH, La Bretèche and Fiorilli

Proof.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{\bm{\epsilon}\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}}\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in[0,T]^{k}}w^{\bm{\epsilon}}(\bm{\rho})\times\widehat{W}\left(-U\langle\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\gamma}\rangle\right),

As
\mu_{k}=0
when
k
is odd, this holds for any integer
k\geqslant 1.
Since

\displaystyle V^{(T)}(\delta;0)=\delta\log\frac{1}{\delta}+O\left(\delta+\frac{\log T}{T}\right)

by Proposition
2.2
and Lemma
9.1
, the proposition follows from
combining (

9.5
), (
9
) and (

∎

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
T,U\geqslant 2
with
T\leqslant e^{U}.
Suppose
\delta\to 0
as
T\to\infty.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(\left(E\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)-E^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\right)^{2}\right)\ll\frac{\log^{2}T}{TU}+U^{3}e^{-U/2}.

Proof.

This is similar to Lemma
4.3
, except we are integrating with respect to a smooth weight
W
for extra savings. Let
u\in[U,2U].
Invoking
Lemma
4.1
gives

\displaystyle E\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)-E^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)=-\sum_{T<|\gamma|\leqslant e^{U}}w(\rho)e^{i\gamma u}+O\left(U^{2}e^{-u/2}\right).

On one hand, since
W
is supported on
(1/2,5/2),
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{U}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|U^{2}e^{-u/2}\right|^{2}W\left(\frac{u}{U}\right)du\ll U^{3}e^{-U/2}.

On the other, opening the square gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{U}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|-\sum_{T<|\gamma|\leqslant e^{U}}w(\rho)e^{i\gamma u}\right|^{2}W\left(\frac{u}{U}\right)du=\sum_{T<|\gamma_{1}|,|\gamma_{2}|\leqslant e^{U}}w(\rho_{1})\overline{w(\rho_{2})}\times\widehat{W}\left(-U(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2})\right).

Since
w(\rho)\ll|\gamma|^{-1}
and
\widehat{W}(x)\ll(1+|x|)^{-2},
this is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma_{1}|,|\gamma_{2}|>T\\
|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|\leqslant U^{-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}|}+\frac{1}{U^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma_{1}|,|\gamma_{2}|>T\\
|\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|>U^{-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}||\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}|^{2}}.

By partial summation, the first sum is

\displaystyle\ll\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\in(T,\infty)^{2}\\
|x-y|\leqslant U^{-1}\end{subarray}}{\int\int}\frac{\log x\log y}{xy}dxdy

and the second sum is

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{U^{2}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,y)\in(T,\infty)^{2}\\
|x-y|>U^{-1}\end{subarray}}{\int\int}\frac{\log x\log y}{xy}\frac{dxdy}{(x-y)^{2}}.

Making suitable change of variables, one can show that both sums are

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{U}\int_{T}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\log x}{x}\right)^{2}dx\ll\frac{\log^{2}T}{TU},

and hence the lemma follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
9.1
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a Borel subset. We define

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(B\right):=\frac{1}{U}\int_{B}W\left(\frac{u}{U}\right)du.

Let
T\geqslant 2
be a real number satisfying
\delta T\to\infty
but
\log\left(\delta T\right)=o\left(\log g(T)\right),
and
\log T=o\left(\log U\right)
as
U\to\infty
so that Proposition
9.1
is applicable for any integer
k\geqslant 1.

Using the method of moments (see
[
Bil12
, Theorem 30.1]
), the proposition implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(\widetilde{E}^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\in(\alpha,\beta]\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+o(1)

for any fixed real numbers
\alpha<\beta.
Applying Proposition
2.2
and Lemma
9.1
, we have

\displaystyle V^{(T)}(\delta,0)=(1+o(1))V(\delta,0)

so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(\frac{E^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)}{\sqrt{V(\delta,0)}}\in(\alpha,\beta]\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+o(1).

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(\left(E\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)-E^{(T)}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\right)^{2}\right)=o\left(V(\delta,0)\right)

by Proposition
2.2
and Lemma
9.2
, it follows from Chebyshev’s inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(\widetilde{E}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\in(\alpha,\beta]\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt+o(1).

Finally, let
0<\epsilon<1.
We choose
W
such that
W\equiv 1
on
(1+\epsilon,2-\epsilon).
Since
W
is nonnegative and of unit mass, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{U}\mathop{\mathrm{meas}}\left\{x\in[U,2U]\,:\,\widetilde{E}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\in(\alpha,\beta]\right\}-\mathbb{P}_{u\sim U}^{W}\left(\widetilde{E}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\in(\alpha,\beta]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{U}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}1_{(\alpha,\beta]}\left(\widetilde{E}\left(e^{u};\delta,0\right)\right)\left(1_{[1,2]}\left(\frac{u}{U}\right)-W\left(\frac{u}{U}\right)\right)du

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(1+\epsilon,2-\epsilon)}\left(1_{[1,2]}(x)+W(x)\right)dx=4\epsilon.

Letting
\epsilon\to 0,
the proof is completed.
∎

10.
Open questions and future work

As discussed in the introduction, Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
MS04
]
established the central limit theorem conditionally for primes in a (very) short moving interval, provided that
\frac{H}{\log N}\to\infty
and
\frac{\log H}{\log N}\to 0
as
N\to\infty.
Our Theorem
9.1
, on the other hand, establishes the central limit theorem conditionally, provided that
h=h(x)=\delta x
with
\delta=\delta(X)>(\log X)^{-\epsilon}
for any
\epsilon>0
in logarithmic scale, however. Given the state of affairs, one may ask how the primes in a short moving interval behave in the missing intermediate range. In particular, how does the phase transition from natural density to logarithmic density arise?

In our forthcoming work, we will shift our focus to the “physical side” and compute the mixed moments of primes in multiple (very) short intervals
under
a uniform Hardy–Littlewood
prime
k
-tuple conjecture. Unsurprisingly, the mixed moments coincide with those of a multivariate Gaussian random variable with weak negative correlation.
