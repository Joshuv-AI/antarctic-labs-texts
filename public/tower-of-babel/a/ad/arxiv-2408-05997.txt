Formalizing the Cryptographic Migration Problem
Daniel Loebenberger, Stefan-Lukas Gazdag, Daniel Herzinger, Eduard Hirsch, Christian Näther, Jan-Philipp Steghöfer

Abstract
With the advancements in quantum computing, transitioning to post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is becoming increasingly critical to maintain the security of modern systems. This paper introduces a formal definition of the cryptographic migration problem and explores its complexities using a suitable directed graph model. Characteristics of the resulting migration graphs are analyzed and trade-offs discussed. By using classical mathematical results from combinatorics, probability theory and combinatorial analysis, we assess the challenges of migrating “random” large cryptographic IT-infrastructures. We show that any sufficiently large migration project that follows our model has an intrinsic complexity, either due to many dependent (comparatively easy) migration steps or due to at least one complicated migration step. This proves that in a suitable sense cryptographic migration is hard in general. Furthermore, we analyze the proposed model with respect to practical applicability and explain the difficulties that emerge when we try to model real-world migration projects.



1
Introduction

Cryptography is fundamental ensuring information security in today’s digital world. Despite advances in cryptographic technologies, the process of migrating existing systems and IT-infrastructures to new standards presents significant challenges
[
5
]
. Examples include the transition from IPv4 to IPv6, which took more than 20 years due to the complexity, and technologies such as gateways and NAT became popular migration tools to circumvent the need to actually migrate from IPv4 to IPv6. A cryptographic example is the update of key sizes (RSA keys got bigger and so did the integration problems) or the transition between different hash functions (SHA-1 to the SHA-2 family to the SHA-3 family) with very slow adoption of the SHA-3 family as it is seen as an add-on or fallback rather than a replacement. For a rapid transition, it seems that there needs to be a strong incentive in terms of the benefits of adopting the new technology. One notable example of quicker migration, at least in the broad public, is the transition from the Data Encryption Standard (DES) to the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), which was driven by the need for stronger security measures due to DES’s vulnerability to brute force attacks
[
22
]
.

This issue is particularly prominent in the current transition towards PQC, which aims to protect data against future quantum computing threats. The German Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) uses the work-hypothesis for the high-security sector that with significant probability there is a cryptographically relevant quantum computer in the early 2030s
[
11
]
. Thus, there are numerous practical post-quantum migrations going on world-wide. The reason is that we are already running-out of time: Sensitive (e. g. classified) data has long confidentiality requirements (e. g. 25 years). In addition migrating the IT-infrastructure correspondingly takes a considerable amount of time. This runs against estimates on how long it will take to build a cryptanalytically relevant quantum-computer, c. f. Mosca’s famous inequality
[
16
]
.
To understand the structure of cryptographic migration and to potentially improve the practicability of real-world migrations, we will look at the cryptographic migration problem, i. e. the task of migrating a given IT-infrastructure from one cryptographic algorithm to another one, from a mathematical point of view. Despite many common-sense approaches being applied to tackle PQC migration, there remains a significant gap in scientifically grounded methodologies. We address that gap by providing a formal model that lays the foundation for a more rigorous understanding and systematic approach to the complexities of cryptographic migration.

This paper is structured as follows: An overview of related work can be found in Section
2
. Our first contribution is to provide a unified and consistent notation by introducing a formal model of cryptographic migration in Section
3
. In Section
4
we prove that migration is generally hard in a suitable sense: Namely, we show that any “random”, reasonably large migration project has an inherent complexity. This is our second contribution. Sections
5
and
6
further describe our work in terms of its usage and its potential applications, which shall serve as a foundation for upcoming real-world migrations – our third contribution.

A Formal Model of the Migration Problem

Consider a finite set
V
of components in a large IT-infrastructure. If the migration of a component
v\in V
depends on the migration of a component
w\in V
, we write
v\to w
. In other words: We cannot migrate
v
before migrating
w
(but possibly at the same time). Collecting all such dependencies in a set

(without loops
v\to v
) gives us the

we have in mind. Canonically, we extend the relation

to sets

M_{1}\to_{G}M_{2}:=\left\{v\to w\;\mathchoice{\vrule width=0.8pt}{\vrule width=0.8pt}{\vrule width=0.6pt}{\vrule width=0.6pt}\;v\in M_{1},w\in M_{2}\right\}\subseteq E

\mathop{\text{source}}\nolimits(e):=v
and
\mathop{\text{target}}\nolimits(e):=w

for the

G|_{M}=(M,M\to M)

The

in the graph

. We can extend the relation ’
\to

\to^{*}

then

are connected by the empty path. We define the

We state the important

Definition 1

(Migration Cluster)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a migration graph and
v\in V
. The
migration cluster
c(v)
of
v
is the set of all components
w\in V
such that
w\in\mathop{\text{dep}}\nolimits(v)
and
v\in\mathop{\text{dep}}\nolimits(w)
.

In the literature, the migration clusters are classically called the
strongly connected components
of
G
. For sake of exposition we stick to the term
migration cluster
in the context of cryptographic migration. Intuitively, the elements of
c(v)
are precisely those components that have to be migrated at the same time as
v
due to mutual dependencies. As it will turn out, the migration clusters specify how complex single
migration steps
of a migration project will be. Additionally, the properties of the migration clusters inside a migration graph
G
will shed light on the complexity of the resulting
migration problem
which we will describe below.
In fact, we have the following important characteristics:

1.

The
size

s
of the cluster
s(v):=\#c(v)
.

2.

The
degree
of the cluster
\mathop{\text{cdeg}}\nolimits(v):=\mathop{\text{argmax}}\nolimits_{w\in G|_{c(v)}}\mathop{\text{deg}}\nolimits(w)
.

Clearly, in the case
\mathop{\text{cdeg}}\nolimits(v)=0
we have
s(v)=1
since otherwise there would be two components
v_{1}\neq v_{2}\in c(v)
with
v_{1}\in\mathop{\text{dep}}\nolimits(v_{2})
and
v_{2}\in\mathop{\text{dep}}\nolimits(v_{1})
. But then
\mathop{\text{dep}}\nolimits(v_{1})=\mathop{\text{target}}\nolimits(v_{1}\to^{*}c(v_{2}))
would be non-empty and thus
v_{1}\to^{*}c(v_{2})
would also be non-empty. Therefore, we have
\mathop{\text{deg}}\nolimits_{G|_{c(v_{1})}}(v_{2})>0
, contradicting our assumption. Conversely, if
s(v)=1
then
\mathop{\text{cdeg}}\nolimits(v)=0
since
G|_{c(v)}
consists of a single component only and has no edges. In practice this case occurs in the situation where we have a single component that simply can be migrated without any dependencies on other components. This is the easiest case in a migration process.

In the case
\mathop{\text{cdeg}}\nolimits(v)=1
the migration cluster
c(v)
is a cycle. In practice this case often occurs for
s(v)=2
when there are two components that communicate using the same protocol. A migration of the protocol can only be done successfully, if both components are migrated at the same time. Cycles of larger sizes are uncommon in practice, as we will later see in Section
5
.
The set
m(v)
of
migratable components around
v
is given by

m(v)=\begin{cases}c(v)&\text{, if $c(v)\to\bar{c}(v)=\emptyset$}\\
\emptyset&\text{, otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

where we write
\bar{c}(v)=V\setminus c(v)
. The set
m(v)
specifies exactly which components can be migrated in
G
when we migrate
v\in V
. If
v
has dependencies outside its migration cluster, i. e.
v
cannot be migrated, then
m(v)=\emptyset
. If there are no direct dependencies of
v
, then
m(v)=\left\{v\right\}
. Also, in all other cases
m(v)=c(v)
, i. e. the whole migration cluster. If
m(v)\neq\emptyset
, we call
v

migratable
(in
G
).

Now, in a migration project, there might be several subsequent steps to fully migrate all involved components, i. e. solve the migration problem the migration graph poses. We write
G^{(v)}=(V\setminus m(v),E\setminus(V\to m(v)))
for the graph after a migration attempt of
v
. If
v
is not migratable, then
G^{(v)}=G
. Generally,
G^{(v)}
is exactly the graph restricted to the components outside the migratable components around
v
. Using similar to above the notation
\bar{m}(v)=V\setminus m(v)
, we can thus alternatively write
G^{(v)}=G|_{\bar{m}(v)}=(\bar{m}(v),\bar{m}(v)\to\bar{m}(v))
. We now show

Lemma 1

In any (finite and non-empty) migration graph
G=(V,E)
there is at least one component
v\in V
that is migratable.

Proof.

Suppose there is no migratable component, i. e.
m(v)=\emptyset
for all
v\in V
. By definition,
c(v)\to\bar{c}(v)
is always non-empty, i. e. there is always a dependency to a component outside the migration cluster of
v
. Call such a dependency
w\in\bar{c}(v)
. Since
w
is outside the migration cluster of
v
,
w
cannot depend on elements of
c(v)
and all dependencies of
w
reside in the (strict) sub-graph
G|_{\bar{c}(v)}
. Repeating this process, after a finite number of steps, we end-up in a graph which only contains a single migration cluster
c(u)
and
u\in V
is migratable in
G
contradicting our assumption.
∎

We will now define
migration strategies of
G
. Formally, a strategy
S=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})\in V^{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
is a sequence of components that are migrated one by one (of course together with their respective migration clusters).
\ell
is the
length
of the strategy. For
1\leq i\leq\ell
denote by
G_{i}
the graph after successively migrating
v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}
, i. e.
G_{i}:=G_{i-1}^{(v_{i})}
, where
G_{0}=G
. The strategy
S
is
reasonable
if for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
, we have
m(v_{i})\neq\emptyset
in the graph
G_{i-1}
. That means that the component
v_{i}
is in fact migratable in the graph
G_{i-1}
and, therefore
G_{i}\subsetneq G_{i-1}
, i. e. the migration of
v_{i}
yields a smaller graph.
S
is
successful
, if
G_{\ell}
is the empty graph. By Lemma
1
there is a reasonable, successful strategy for any migration graph
G
.

Lemma 2

All reasonable, successful strategies
S=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})
for a migration graph
G=(V,E)
have the same length
\ell
.

Proof.

Consider two reasonable, successful strategies
S_{1}=(v_{1}^{1},\ldots,v_{\ell_{1}}^{1})
and
S_{2}=(v_{1}^{2},\ldots,v_{\ell_{2}}^{2})
with
\ell_{2}\geq\ell_{1}
. Since
S_{1}
is successful,
V=\biguplus_{1\leq i\leq\ell_{1}}m(v_{i})
partitions into the migration clusters given by
S_{1}
. Note that all
m(v_{i})=c(v_{i})
are non-empty since
S_{1}
is reasonable. Now, consider the (sub-)strategy
S_{3}=(v_{1}^{2},\ldots,v_{\ell_{1}}^{2})
of the strategy
S_{2}
. By the partition property, there is for each
1\leq j\leq\ell_{1}
an
1\leq i\leq\ell_{1}
such that
v_{j}^{2}\in c(v_{i}^{1})
. Furthermore, for each
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq\ell_{1}
and corresponding
1\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq\ell_{1}
, we have
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
since otherwise
v_{j_{2}}^{2}\in m(v_{j_{1}}^{2})\neq\emptyset
. The non-emptiness of
m(v_{j_{1}}^{2})
follows from the assumption that
S_{2}
is reasonable. But then
\sum_{1\leq j\leq\ell_{1}}s(v_{j}^{2})=\#V
and the strategy
S_{3}
is already successful.
∎

4
Migration is Hard in General

We now show that, expectedly, in any migration graph of certain size there is at least one point in the migration process which is hard to perform. As it turns out there are also relevant trade-offs in any graphs.

4.1
Simple Trade-Offs

First of all, there is a simple trade-off between the number of migration clusters in a graph
G
and the size of the clusters:

Theorem 4.1

Assume that for a migration graph
G=(V,E)
the migration length is bounded by a constant
k
, i. e.
\ell(G)\leq k
. Then, there is a component
v\in V
with
\#c(v)\geq n/k
.

Proof.

By definition
\ell(G)=\#(V/c)
and
n=\sum_{c(u)\in V/c}\#c(u)
. Let
v
be a component with a maximally large migration cluster, i. e.
v=\mathop{\text{argmax}}\nolimits_{u\in V}\#c(u)
. In general
v
is not uniquely defined, but for our existential statement, this does not trouble us: Namely, for such
v\in V
, we have
n=\sum_{c(u)\in V/c}\#c(u)\leq\sum_{c(u)\in V/c}\#c(v)=\ell(G)\cdot\#c(v)\leq k\cdot\#c(v)
and the claim follows.
∎

Informally, we have

Fact 2
.

In any sufficiently large migration project, we either have many different migration clusters or at least one large one.

Also locally, there is a similar trade-off between the size of clusters and the number of independent components:

Theorem 4.2

Consider a component
v
in a migration graph
G
with
m\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
direct dependencies. Assume there are at most
k
independent clusters within those
m
components. Then there is a migration cluster of size
s
with
s\geq m/k
. Conversely, if each migration cluster has size at most
s
then we have
k\geq m/s
migration clusters.

Proof.

Assume each migration cluster has size
s<m/k
. Since there are at most
k
independent clusters, we have at most
k\cdot s<m
components. Contradiction.
∎

Thus, we see that we have to expect either many independent components or at least one large migration cluster.
Another simple trade-off emerges, when we bound the number of descendants of any component in the graph suitably. Then, we get a large migration length as seen from

Theorem 4.3

Let
\mathchoice{f\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}}{f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}{f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}{f\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
be a smooth function in a real variable with
f\in o(n)
, e. g.
f(n)=\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
. Assume for any component
v
in a migration graph
G=(V,E)
with
n
components, we have for each
k\geq 0
the bound
\#(v\to^{k}V)\leq f(n)
. Then
\ell(G)\geq n/f(n)
. In other words, the number of elements in the set of components with a common ancestor of distance
k
has a trade-off with the length of the graph.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
G
has a single source
v
. Otherwise take the set of sources and consider the one with the longest path in
v\to^{*}V
. This path has length
\ell(G)
. For
0\leq k<\ell(G)
define
W_{k}:=v\to^{k}V
. Since
G
has only a single source,
\biguplus_{0\leq k<\ell(G)}W_{k}=V
, e. g.
n=\#V=\#\biguplus_{0\leq k<\ell(G)}W_{k}=\sum_{0\leq k<\ell(G)}\#W_{k}
. By assumption
\#W_{k}\leq f(n)
for all
0\leq k<\ell(G)
. Thus
n\leq\ell(G)\cdot f(n)
and the claim follows.
∎

Note that the same trade-off exists for the condensed graph
G/c
bounding the migration depth
d(G)
of the graph
G
from below when assuming for all
k
bounds
\#T(k)\leq f(n)
on the number of migration clusters with canonical migration time

as defined on page

Fact 3
.

In any sufficiently large migration project, if at all points in time the number of migratable migration clusters is small, then migration will take long. Phrased the other way round: If migration can be done in few successive steps, then at least one of the steps must be difficult.

From classical results in combinatorial analysis we know the following about random decompositions of the migration graph in its clusters.

4.2
Random Migration Clusters

Since every migration graph decomposes disjointly into its migration clusters, we now study the expected properties of these migration clusters. Let
n
be the number of components. There are
B_{n}
different possible partitions of
n

B_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\choose k}B_{k}=\frac{1}{e}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{k^{n}}{k!}

is the

partitions of our components in
k
distinct non-empty subsets, where
n\brace k
are called the
Stirling numbers of the second kind
. Obviously, we have
B_{n}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}{n\brace k}
.

Sampling Migration Clusters.

Consider now a migration project with
m
different sub-projects and suppose each of our
n
components is assigned to one of the
m
sub-projects uniformly at random. Let
\Pi_{nm}
be the random variable defining the resulting partition
\pi
of our components. We have:

\mathop{\text{prob}}\nolimits(\Pi_{nm}=\pi)=\frac{m^{\underline{k}}}{m^{n}}

if
\#\pi=k
, where
m^{\underline{k}}=m\cdot(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)
is the falling factorial function. In other words: if there are
k
different sub-projects with at least one component, then the probability of creating one such partition is given by the formula above. For the distribution of the number of non-empty different sub-projects we have

\mathop{\text{prob}}\nolimits(\#\pi=k)={n\brace k}\frac{m^{\underline{k}}}{m^{n}}.

Note that for arbitrary migration graphs, we do not know the distribution of the different partitions. We have the following

Theorem 4.4

(Proposition 2 in
[
23
]
)

Let
M
be the random variable with values in
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and suppose given
M=m
that
n

are thrown independently and uniformly at random into

1.

M

for
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, where

whose first

2.

\Pi_{nM}
has uniform distribution over the set of all partitions of
n

The theorem gives a constructive way of uniformly generating a migration cluster: First sample
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
following the above distribution, then distribute the
n
components uniformly in
m
bins. The resulting partition

will be a uniformly selected partition of our
n
components.
We will now study the expected properties of the outcome.

Distribution of Random Migration Clusters.

Studying the distribution of random partitions of sets with
n
elements is a classical problem. Set into the context of migration problems, we state

Theorem 4.5

(Summary of

The number of migration clusters
M

\mathsf{E}(M)\sim n/\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
and the expected size of each migration cluster is
\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
. Also, almost all partitions have approximately
e\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
distinct cluster sizes.

Furthermore, the expected maximum size of a migration cluster is, for growing

er-\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits\sqrt{r}
, where
r\cdot\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(r)=n
and

[

. This means that we typically expect lots of small clusters and we do not expect exceptionally large ones. Asymptotically, we have

Theorem 4.6

(Chapter 4, Theorem 1.1 in

The distribution of the number of migration clusters

Results on the speed of convergence are beyond the scope of this article. We now compute an upper bound on the expected number of migration clusters when looking independently at
s
randomly selected migration projects.

Lemma 4

The expected maximal number of migration clusters when looking independently at
s
randomly selected migration projects is bounded above by

\frac{n}{\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n}+\frac{\sqrt{2n\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits s}}{\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n}.

(1)

Proof.

Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{s}
be the resulting numbers of migration clusters and define
X=\mathop{\text{max}}\nolimits_{1\leq i\leq s}M_{i}-\mu
. Then conveniently
\mathsf{E}(X)=0
.

Since for a fixed real constant
t
the function
x\mapsto\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(tx)
is convex, by Jensen’s inequality we have:

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(t\mathsf{E}(X))

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{E}(\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(tX))=\mathsf{E}(\max_{1\leq i\leq s}(\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(tM_{i})))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mathsf{E}(\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(tM_{i}))

\displaystyle=s\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(t^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)

Here, the last equality follows from the fact that the moment generating function
\mathsf{E}(\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(tM_{i}))
for a normal distribution with mean zero is equal to
\mathop{\text{exp}}\nolimits(t^{2}\sigma^{2}/2)
and in particular independent of the index-variable
i
.
Rewriting this,
\mathsf{E}(X)\leq\frac{\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits s}{t}+\frac{t\sigma^{2}}{2}
. This bound is minimal at
\frac{\sqrt{2\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits s}}{\sigma}
since its derivative in direction

is
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}-\frac{\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits s}{t^{2}}
. Thus,
\mathsf{E}(X)\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits s}
and the result follows.
∎

In practice, the Lemma implies that we would have to sample exponentially many migration graphs (in the number of components
n

n/\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
migration clusters. Thus, the statement from Theorem

applies in practice all the time, at least for sufficiently large

It remains to study the dependencies between the migration clusters, i. e. the structure of the condensation modulo the migration clusters.

4.3
Properties of the Condensation Graph

According to Theorem
4.6
, we consider a condensation graph, i. e. the directed acyclic graph resulting from the migration graph on
n
components modulo its strongly connected components, with expectedly
n^{\prime}=n/\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
vertices. Since we are considering a single migration project, we can assume that this graph is connected.
How can we model this graph?

A first idea would be to use the standard Erdős–Rényi model
[
8
]
, where the graph is constructed by connecting the components independently at random with a fixed probability
p
. One can ensure that the result is acyclic by defining any linear order on the vertices and include a directed edge only if it obeys the selected ordering. Each such edge is included in the graph with probability
p
, independently from every other edge.
For such graphs, we have

Theorem 4.7

(Theorem 4.1 in
[
13
]
, a variant of Theorem 1 in
[
7
]
)

Let
G_{n,p_{n}}
be a random
n
-vertex graph, where each arc is present with probability
p_{n}=\frac{\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n+c_{n}}{n}
. Then, for growing
n
, the probability that
G
is connected is given by

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathop{\text{prob}}\nolimits(G_{n,p_{n}}\text{ is connected})=\begin{cases}0&\text{, if $c_{n}\to-\infty$}\\
e^{-e^{-c}}&\text{, if $c_{n}\to c$ constant}\\
1&\text{, if $c_{n}\to\infty$}\\
\end{cases}

Note that we can directly apply this Theorem to our directed acyclic graph situation, since such a graph is connected if and only if the undirected variant of it is connected.

As our goal is to prove a lower bound on the number of edges in the condensation graph (with
n^{\prime}
vertices!) in our model, we assume now the sparsest case
c_{n^{\prime}}\to c
constant for which the graph is connected with high probability. Note that the doubly-exponential term in Theorem
4.7
approaches
1
already for comparatively small
c
very quickly. For example, already for
c=10
, the value of
e^{-e^{-c}}\geq 0.99995
. But also for any
c>0
, we obtain for the number of edges in the condensation graph expectedly asymptotically

\frac{\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n^{\prime}+c_{n^{\prime}}}{n^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{n^{\prime}\cdot(n^{\prime}-1)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}((n^{\prime}-1)(\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n^{\prime}+c_{n^{\prime}}))\sim\frac{1}{2}n^{\prime}\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n^{\prime}.

That means that even in the sparsest case, we expect a considerable number of dependencies between the migration clusters.

Also, if we bound the number of components with a common ancestor of distance
k
, i. e.
\#(v\to^{k}V)
by a small function
f(n)=o(n)
, e. g.
f(n)=\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
, we obtain by Theorem
4.3
for the condensation graph expectedly
d(G)\geq\frac{n}{f(n)\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n}
, that is, large chains of dependencies of the migration clusters. Of course one can trade depth
d(G)
vs. the bound
f(n)
, but then the complexity of at least one migration step will increase.

4.4
Discussion

In the previous sections we analyzed how different graph structures affect the
properties of our migration problem. To do so, we looked at random migration graphs, arguing that a random migration project should exhibit random clustering and the remaining directed acyclic condensation should also have a random structure. Thereby, we found several mathematical arguments that underline the fact that migration is hard in general.
Specifically, for a random (large) migration graph with
n
components, we showed that expectedly we have:

1.

If for each node the number of dependencies is suitably bounded, the
condensation graph has large depth, i. e. many migration clusters that successively
depend on each other. Stated oppositely, we have at least one migration
step with many dependencies if we assume small depth
(Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
).

2.

Even after observing many migration projects, we do not
expect to observe any with considerably more than
n/\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
migration clusters (Lemma
4
).

3.

The migration graph decomposes in
n/\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
migration clusters of size
\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
with standard deviation
\sqrt{n}/\mathop{\text{log}}\nolimits n
(Theorem
4.5
).

4.

The condensation graph is connected and thus not sparse (Theorem
4.7
).

We thus showed

Fact 4
.

Any sufficiently large migration project has intrinsic complexity due to many dependent (comparatively small) migration clusters. The migration of these clusters either takes many successive steps or includes at least one particularly difficult one.

The question remains how reasonable our assumptions on the randomness of the migration graphs are in practice. The first assumption is that in any practical migration project, partitions (i. e. clustering) of components appears uniformly from the set of all partitions. This clearly does not have to be the case. Another assumption is that the structure of the condensation graph follows the Erdős–Rényi model, i. e. that dependencies appear with a fixed probability independently of each other. This also does not have to be the case. Finally, our third assumption is that the two modeling steps are structurally independent which we are unable to prove.

5
Real-World Dependencies

We provided the theoretical foundation for working with migration graphs in general.
Now we take a first step towards its application in the real world.
One has to consider that a migration graph’s structure will vary significantly
depending on the concrete IT-infrastructure. But also the abstraction layer and the type of migration lead to many design choices of the model.
The resulting graphs will show parallels to those that already exist in several disciplines of computer science. Those comprise flowcharts,
call graphs and finite-state machines in programming, network diagrams, or software
dependency graphs, just to name a few. Additionally, current efforts in the creation
of Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs) or Cryptography Bills of Materials (CBOMs) and
their respective tooling add to that collection of suitable graphs.

5.1
Post-Quantum Migration

We expect that post-quantum migration is the most significant type of cryptographic migration
of IT-infrastructures in the near future
[
17
]
. Its specific properties introduce
aspects that were less prevalent in the classical cryptography world. One
example are hybrid cryptographic schemes. Depending on the specific model, a hybrid
solution may just be seen as a single cryptographic primitive or cipher suite. Yet,
by modeling detailed software dependencies or taking dependencies on space/throughput
requirements into account, this might have a significant effect.

Post-quantum migration also emphasizes the importance of cryptographic agility (see for example
[
20
]
). While many communication protocols used to be optimized for a limited set of somewhat similar algorithms, the introduction of
crypto-agile mechanisms that support various kinds of cryptographic primitives and,
sometimes arbitrary, combinations of these, will lead to an increase in complexity. In a graph which only models systems and their protocol-related communication relations in
between, one may only treat this implicitly. Yet again, graphs that take other dependencies into account become harder to model depending on the level of detail but, on the other hand, sometimes better reflect the complicated circumstances of the real world.

Also, regulatory requirements of post-quantum solutions and different views on current and future algorithm recommendations may become way more complicated, especially in a multinational context.

All in all, post-quantum migration is the scenario that motivated our formal
migration model. At the same time, it implicates several special properties
which make modeling even more complex as we demonstrate in
the following section.

5.2
Real-World Migration Graphs

Today’s large scale IT-infrastructures are both complex and highly diverse and examining models to create a suitable graph from a
real-world IT-infrastructure is an open research topic.

The aggregation of the
necessary data that is used to derive the graph is highly individual and specific to the given migration goal. We state a few concrete examples of such modeling attempts:

Network migration:

When talking about network migration it seems intuitive to think of
the components in
V
to represent computers, servers and similar devices. Much
thought has to be taken into the dependencies depicted by the edges
E
. These could,
e. g. model communication paths for specific (cryptographic) protocols. For the same network all connections between machines may be interesting regardless of the specific
protocol, illustrating all the relations between machines in the network. Depending
on the objective one may study several
graphs representing different kinds of communication relations each – for example
individual graphs for protocols like TLS/HTTPS, SSH, IPsec/IKEv2).

System migration:

Different graphs may be modeling a specific system, e. g.
V
being a set of
components within a firmware or operating system. It is unclear at which
granularity such modeling may be done. We expect that relations may be handled
similarly to SBOMs, showing dependencies between software, libraries, and so on. This
may even go into the details of a specific software project showing relations between
different modules, library calls, etc.

Full-scale migration:

For a system administrator, a tool focusing on the high-level network view may be
enough, while programmers may profit from call-graph-like models. A full view on all
abstraction levels may be provided by a combination of different graphs, e. g. a
component
v
being linked to another graph depicting the same component in a
different context. This leads us to having differing graph models of the same
infrastructure, just depending on our perspective.

Note that when we consider classical, naturally grown enterprise networks, the
graphs will probably be both: large and complex. Also they will have many interdependent
systems. It is beyond our current model to capture time dependent changes in the
migration graph.
But even when we look at a snap-shot of a migration graph, the design choices for modeling real-world IT-infrastructure graphs are vast and we discovered a variety of possible ways to model such IT-infrastructures.

A reasonable assumption might be that the best strategy is to model only what is vital for the given migration and leave out all other information. Examining the exact choices, however, deserves its own research track.

API-compatible change of algorithms:

When migrating an isolated (generic)
technological component
v

Operating System dependencies:

Certain components

When regulatory requirements
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},\dots

impose restrictions on the migratability of certain technical components
v

Communication protocols:

When migrating communication protocols from one
version to another, it is vital that all communicating parties are migrated at the
same time. This is in particular a problem when there are many parties involved and
only a part of the own IT-infrastructure may be updated conveniently, e. g. by

often leads to backwards compatibility as a feature, but likely also leads to the
possibility of downgrading attacks. This corresponds to case Fig.

Occasionally, there are

This corresponds to case Fig.

all assets are migrated at the same time. In the context of post-quantum migration
this might happen for example when we observe circularly cross-signed certificates.
Such circular cases are rare in practice, because they create dependencies that are
avoided in software architecture and engineering. Also, implementers can address
those kinds of dependencies by introducing adapter nodes in one direction which
allow to break the dependency cycle.

We encourage the inclined reader to find more naturally occurring real-world examples. Using these base-patterns, a wide range of possible security scenarios can not only be modeled, but also described and simplified, by breaking them down into smaller, well-understood structures. Thus, once identified, the above patterns might serve as fundamental steps when deriving migration strategies in practical settings.

6
Practical Implications

As stated in Fact
4
and further outlined in Section
5
, big migration projects are intrinsically complex. These projects require many successive steps and in-depth strategic planning. Migration graphs can help in understanding this complexity involved and in considering the time needed to migrate, but also specify the number of components and the various dependencies. As we have seen, these dependencies can exist between single components, between interdependent clusters of components, and between external factors such as regulatory requirements, legal issues, or third-party components. The complexity of migrations requires expert knowledge and sufficient dedicated resources, resulting in significant costs. Economic implications and the risks of both, migrating and not migrating to PQC, must be evaluated. It is also crucial for stakeholders, policy makers, and non-technical decision makers to grasp this complexity and to develop appropriate organizational measures to address it.

Ott et al. define the term
cryptographic agility
as “the ability to make a transition from one cryptographic algorithm or implementation to another”
[
20
]
. Such an approach is in line with making security solutions sustainable by allowing to adapt to changing threats and technologies. We have shown that it is necessary to approach a migration process step by step and reevaluate the changed system once the first migrations have been performed. The model we propose can serve as a starting point to identify the highest priority parts of the system, e. g., by identifying the parts which are at the beginning of a migration chain, or which have the highest impact. We believe that our approach works well in an iterative migration approach and we will investigate this further in future work.

Standardizing migration projects through guidelines, rules, and best practices can optimize the process, provide practical examples for guidance, and reduce cost and effort. First steps in these directions are approaches like CARAF, a risk management approach for post-quantum migration
[
15
]
. CARAF defines a threat assessment and a risk assessment stage in which migration graphs as we define them can be helpful. During threat assessment, migration clusters identified using our approach can be used to identify vulnerable subsystems. If one of the systems in the cluster is vulnerable, so is the cluster in its entirety. Likewise, during risk assessment, information about which systems need to be migrated together is helpful to establish cost and effort estimates. Therefore, we believe our approach supports existing methods in the area
[
17
]
.

Currently, our approach is not supported by tools. We believe that the development and refinement of open-source tools and frameworks, along with ensuring easy access to these resources, can significantly lower the barriers for migration, especially for smaller organizations. These tools can streamline migration processes and offer cost-effective solutions for managing complex projects. We envision tooling to support the creation and maintenance of migration graphs as well as their analysis. Similar tools can be found in related areas such as network analysis and visualization, SBOMs and CBOMs, and graph-based databases. The formalization approach may facilitate the use and adaption of such tools by providing a structured understanding of the underlying complexities. Our future work will also address costs and effort associated with migrations and how these can be captured in the graphs.

In summary, managing the intrinsic complexity of large migration projects involves
understanding the dependencies and strategically planning of the different migration
steps, while keeping the economic implications in mind. Supporting companies with
standardized guidelines and open-source tools is crucial, as is fostering an agile
approach to sustainable security solutions. By addressing these key areas,
organizations can overcome migration challenges and maintain robust security within their IT-infrastructures.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we used classical mathematical results from graph theory, probability
theory, and combinatorial analysis to assess the difficulty of a very practical
problem: Cryptographic migration of large IT-infrastructures. We formulated a formal
model of cryptographic migratability in terms of directed graphs and showed several
expected properties of these graphs. This approach not only elevates the discussion to a scientific level but also establishes a foundation for further research on more efficient migration strategies. From these formal results, we derived four “facts” that informally state that migration is difficult in general.
In summary, we showed:

1.

Migration attempts of independent parts of the IT-infrastructure do not interfere with each other.

2.

In sufficiently large migration projects we either have many different migration clusters or at least one large one.

3.

In sufficiently large migration projects, if at all points in time the number of migratable migration clusters is small, then migration will take long.

4.

Such migration projects always have an intrinsic complexity due to many dependent (comparatively small) migration clusters which (successively) depend on each other.

Still, there is a lot of work to be done, when it comes to practical migration projects: It is not clear how to obtain useful migration graphs in practice, such that they are neither trivial nor too vast. Analyzing how to model real-world IT-infrastructures or relevant subsets will clearly be a challenging task.
Also, there is clearly room for improvements: As migration problems typically come with some kind of measurable cost per migration step, a refinement of our model would result in migration graphs where optimal migration strategies are not as easy to obtain as here. One further important open question is also the development of migration graphs and the changes in such graphs over time.

Our work sets the stage for future research aimed at refining these models, integrating them with real-world data, and ultimately creating more adaptive, user-friendly tools that support real-world migration. The paper therefore aims to scientifically substantiate migration approaches. By emphasizing the relevance of this formal approach, we hope to support the development of more robust migration strategies and (open-source) tooling that can keep pace with the evolving landscape of cryptographic standards.

Future work should apply our model to real-world migration projects. The hope is to
get a better insight of the complexity of migration projects not just in general, as discussed here, but for specific practically occurring cases. This might help to better understand and categorize real-world migration steps and processes, specifically in the context of PQC migration.
