Predictive performance of power posteriors \blankfootnote Code to replicate the results is freely available at https://github.com/yannmclatchie/power-posterior-prediction .
Yann McLatchie, Edwin Fong, David T. Frazier, Jeremias Knoblauch

Abstract
We analyse the impact of using tempered likelihoods in the production of posterior predictions. While the choice of temperature has an impact on predictive performance in small samples, we formally show that in moderate-to-large samples, tempering does not impact posterior predictions.



keywords
generalised Bayes; power posteriors; learning rate; posterior predictive distribution.

1
Introduction

Bayesian inference has become a popular framework for inference due to the incorporation of prior beliefs and automatic uncertainty quantification.
Traditional Bayesian analysis, however, is only optimal under the strong assumption that the model is well-specified
(
Bernardo and Smith,, 1994
;
Aitchison,, 1975
)
.
When this assumption is violated and the true data-generating distribution is outside the model class, the standard Bayes posterior can deliver unreliable inference
(
Owhadi et al.,, 2015
;
Bissiri et al.,, 2016
;
Jewson et al.,, 2018
;
Knoblauch et al.,, 2022
)
.
A popular remedy are
power posteriors

(
Holmes and Walker,, 2017
;
Grünwald and van Ommen,, 2017
)
, also known as
fractional

(
Bhattacharya et al.,, 2019
)
or
\alpha
-posteriors

(
Yang et al.,, 2020
)
.
These temper the likelihood
f_{\theta}
by raising it to a power
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a constant referred to as the
temperature
or
learning rate
.
Denoting the prior density over
\theta
by
\pi
, and
y_{1:n}
as the observed data, the power posterior is

\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})\propto\pi(\theta)f_{\theta}(y_{1:n})^{\tau}.

(1)

For
\tau=1
, this recovers the Bayes posterior, which optimally processes information if the model is correctly specified
(
Zellner,, 1988
)
, while
\tau<1
can improve robustness to model misspecification in small-to-moderate sample sizes
(
Grünwald and van Ommen,, 2017
)
.
Further,
Miller and Dunson, (2019, Equation 3.5)
argue that
Equation

1
is an approximation of Bayes’ Theorem when conditioning on neighbourhoods of the observed data and show asymptotically that taking
\tau<1
can provide inferential benefits.
Similarly,
Bhattacharya et al., (2019)
use asymptotic arguments to argue that power posteriors have preferable concentration and generalisation properties.

To find an appropriate value for
\tau
, various contributions have focused on frequentist calibration
(
Syring and Martin,, 2019
;
Lyddon et al.,, 2019
;
Altamirano et al.,, 2023
;
Matsubara et al.,, 2023
, see e.g.)
, expected information matching
(
Holmes and Walker,, 2017
)
, and the so-called
SafeBayes
approach
(
Grünwald,, 2012
;
Grünwald and van Ommen,, 2017
)
.
For a comparison of such methods, see
Wu and Martin, (2023)
.
Choosing
\tau
for optimal predictive performance, however, is under-studied.
This is surprising given the growing interest in Bayesian prediction
(
Fortini and Petrone,, 2012
;
Fortini and Petrone,, 2016
;
Fortini and Petrone,, 2024
;
Fong et al.,, 2023
)
.
In the remainder, we study the effect of
\tau
on predictive performance in settings where the sample size is large enough for posterior concentration to be in evidence.
The results are intriguing: while varying
\tau
may yield predictive benefit in small samples, in moderate-to-large samples the choice of
\tau
has no meaningful impact on predictive performance. Indeed, trying to choose the value of
\tau
that provides optimal predictive performance leads to an ill-defined optimisation problem.

2
A predictive view on power posteriors

2.1
Choosing the temperature predictively

With the rise of
algorithmic modelling

(
Breiman,, 2001
)
, mainstream research has increasingly emphasised the prediction of observables.
For a Bayesian statistician, this amounts to focusing on the
posterior predictive
, which integrates out parameter uncertainty via

p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})=\int f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n}).

For large but finite values of
\tau
, the prior is discounted and the posterior
\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})
places most of its mass in a small neighbourhood around the point estimator
\hat{\theta}_{n}=\argmax_{\theta}\log f_{\theta}(y_{1:n})
, so that the resulting posterior predictive resembles the
plug-in predictive

f_{\hat{\theta}_{n}}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
.
Conversely, for small values of
\tau
, the posterior predictive resembles the
prior predictive

\int f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n}){\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)}
.
A naïve approach would be to assume that there exists a unique choice for
\tau
that interpolates the two in a predictively optimal manner, and in turn optimise for it.
For instance, in the idealised case where one knows the true predictive distribution
q^{\star}_{n}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
, one could attempt to choose
\tau
so that its distance from the posterior predictive
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
is minimised.
In this paper we show that this is not possible:
both theoretically and empirically, once
n
is even just moderately large, all values of
\tau
taken over any positive and compact interval deliver a
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
with virtually identical predictive performance.
Our findings are two-fold:
first, we show that there is little theoretical benefit to choosing
\tau
predictively.
Second, if, regardless, we choose tau by optimising predictive performance then we demonstrate that this results in an ill-defined optimisation problem.
Before formally stating this result, we demonstrate this behaviour on a simple numerical example.

Normal location example

q^{\star}_{n}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})

Indeed, our theoretical findings identify the key condition for these results to be posterior concentration.

3
The temperature is eventually inconsequential to predictive accuracy

3.1
Technical results

In this section, we show that as
n
increases,
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
and
{f_{\hat{\theta}_{n}}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})}
become uniformly close over
\tau
.
Hence, in large samples, varying
\tau
will not improve predictive performance.

For simplicity, assume that
\tau
lies in a positive and compact interval,
\tau\in[\underline{\tau},\overline{\tau}]
for constants
0<\underline{\tau}<\overline{\tau}<\infty
.
To state our assumptions, we take
d:\Theta\times\Theta\to[0,\infty)
to be a divergence so that
d(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\geq 0
for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and
d(\theta,\theta)=0
implies that
\theta=\theta^{\prime}
.
This divergence can be taken to be the Euclidean distance when
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but can take other forms for more general parameter spaces. Further, define
\mathsf{L}(\theta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}\log f_{\theta}(y_{1:n})
,
\theta^{\star}=\argmax_{\theta}\mathsf{L}(\theta)
as the population-optimal value for
\theta
, and
\mathbb{P}
as the distribution from which the observations
y_{1:n}
are drawn.
Throughout, we do not assume that the model is well-specified, so that there need not exist a
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
for which
f_{\theta^{\star}}=q^{\star}_{n}
.
Instead, we posit two generic assumptions:
the first imposes a mild technical condition satisfied for a wide range of models, including all regular parametric statistical models, as well as a range of non-parametric models.
The second is a posterior concentration condition, and is likewise satisfied in a very large class of settings.

Assumption 1
.

For
\lambda
denoting the Lebesgue measure, for some
\varepsilon>0
, any
\theta
such that
d(\theta,\theta^{\star})\leq\varepsilon
, and any
y_{1:n}
, there exists a constant
0<M_{\varepsilon}<\infty
, independent of
\theta
and
y_{1:n}
, so that

\displaystyle\int\left\{f_{\theta}(x\mid y_{1:n})^{1/2}-f_{\theta^{\star}}(x\mid y_{1:n})^{1/2}\right\}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda(x)\leq M_{\varepsilon}d(\theta,\theta^{\star})^{2}.

(2)

This assumption is similar to differentiability in quadratic mean, which is satisfied by statistical models with positive Fisher information at
\theta^{\star}

(
Vaart,, 1998
, Lemma 7.6)
.
While the assumption is given in its most general form, the dependence of
M_{\varepsilon}
on
\varepsilon
can be suppressed entirely if a sufficiently large constant can be found that satisfies the condition uniformly over shrinking values of
\varepsilon
: a situation common in both parametric and non-parametric settings.

Assumption 2
.

Take
\varepsilon>0
,
K>0
,
C>0
, and
A_{\varepsilon}=\{\theta\in\Theta:d(\theta,\theta^{\star})\leq K\varepsilon\}
.
There exists a sequence
\varepsilon_{n}>0
,
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
,
n\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/M_{\varepsilon_{n}}\rightarrow\infty
, such that, for
K
sufficiently large,

\int\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}\right\}}\,{\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})}\leq\exp(-Cn\tau\varepsilon_{n}^{2}K^{2})

with
\mathbb{P}
-probability at least
1-\exp(-Cn\tau\varepsilon_{n}^{2}K^{2})
.
Further, there is a sequence
\nu_{n}\rightarrow 0,
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, so that
d(\hat{\theta}_{n},\theta^{\star})\leq\nu_{n}/M^{1/2}_{\nu_{n}}
, with
\mathbb{P}
-probability at least
1-\nu_{n}
, with
M_{\nu_{n}}
as in
Assumption

1
and such that
M_{\nu_{n}}\ll\nu_{n}
.

Assumption

2
says that as
n
increases, and in high probability,
\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})
allocates an increasing amount of its probability mass onto a ball containing
\theta^{\star}
, and that
\hat{\theta}_{n}
approaches
\theta^{\star}
at rate
\nu_{n}
.
Rather than directly assume posterior concentration as we have done, one may instead derive this assumption, and subsequent results, from sufficient conditions similar to those of
Martin and Syring, (2022, Theorems 1 and 2)
.
In
Section

A.3
of the supplementary material, we demonstrate that
Assumption

2
is satisfied under some well-understood regularity conditions.
While we will later empirically investigate only finite-dimensional models, our maintained assumptions do not limit us to this setting.
Indeed, our results apply to models of growing dimension and non-parametric models.

Lemma 1
.

Under
Assumptions

1
and
2
, for any
0<\underline{\tau}<\overline{\tau}<\infty
and
\tau\in[\underline{\tau},\overline{\tau}]
,

d_{\mathrm{TV}}\left\{{f_{\hat{\theta}_{n}}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})},\,p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})\right\}\leq 2\max\left\{\varepsilon_{n}+\exp(-Cn\tau\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/M_{\varepsilon_{n}}),\nu_{n}\right\}

(3)

with
\mathbb{P}
-probability at least
1-2\max\left\{\varepsilon_{n}+\exp(-Cn\tau\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/M_{\varepsilon_{n}}),\nu_{n}\right\}
.

Lemma

1
shows that the difference between
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
and the plug-in predictive
{f_{\hat{\theta}_{n}}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})}
vanishes uniformly over
\tau\in[\underline{\tau},\overline{\tau}]
, where the rapidity of this convergence depends on the rate of the plug-in estimator,
\nu_{n}
, and the rate of posterior concentration,
\varepsilon_{n}
.

In
Section

A.3
of the supplementary material,
we derive a similar result in expectation over
y_{1:n}
using the same conditions, (see
Lemma

3
).
Furthermore, we also demonstrate that these results extend to the case where
\tau=\tau_{n}
with
\tau_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and
n\tau_{n}\rightarrow\infty
(see Lemmas
4
and
5
).
As it is not empirically relevant, the limiting case
\tau\rightarrow\infty
is not considered.

3.2
Interpretation

Lemma

1
shows that the choice of
\tau
has vanishing impact on prediction as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
As a result, attempting to optimise
\tau
for predictive performance produces a range of values of
\tau
that have indistinguishable predictive accuracy.
This finding is robust: it holds in high probability (
Lemma

1
), in expectation (
Lemma

3
in the supplement), for sequences
\tau_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
(
Section

A.3
in the supplement), and for distances other than total variation (see Section
4
).

Lemma

1
provides a rigorous explanation for the behaviour in
Figure

1
:
as the posterior concentrates,
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
with
\tau\in[\underline{\tau},\overline{\tau}]
, becomes indistinguishable from the oracle predictive
f_{\theta^{\star}}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
, which is itself indistinguishable from the plug-in predictive
{f_{\hat{\theta}_{n}}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})}
, neither of which depend on
\tau
.
As the normal location model in
Section

2.2
suggests, this behaviour occurs even for moderate sample sizes.
Of course, our results are asymptotic in nature, and thus not necessarily indicative of the behaviours observed in small sample sizes.
Indeed, for small
n
,
Grünwald and van Ommen, (2017)
demonstrate that tempering can have predictive benefits.

3.3
Applicability to generalised Bayes

Power posteriors are a special case of generalised Bayes posteriors
(
Bissiri et al.,, 2016
;
Knoblauch et al.,, 2022
)
.
Indeed for any loss
\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta,y_{1:n})
whose parameters
\theta
index a statistical model
f_{\theta}
satisfying
Assumption

1
,
Lemma

1
applies equally to the predictive
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})=\int f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n}){\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})}
constructed from a Gibbs measure
\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})\propto\pi(\theta)\exp\{-\mathsf{L}(\theta,y_{1:n})\}
that satisfies
Assumption

2
.
In settings like this, the choice of
\tau
still matters greatly, as it calibrates the weight of a data-dependent loss relative to the prior
(
Knoblauch et al.,, 2018
;
Altamirano et al.,, 2024
;
Matsubara et al.,, 2022
, see e.g.)
.
Consequently, predictive performance for generalised Bayes posteriors is also largely independent of
\tau
, provided the sample size is sufficiently large.
While this was suggested by the experiments in
Loaiza-Maya et al., (2021)
and
Frazier et al., (2025)
,
Lemma

1
provides the first rigorous and general proof of this fact.

5
Additional numerical experiments

We further explore our findings in two additional examples.
First, we show that contrary to the conclusions one might draw from
Figure

1
, the plug-in predictive is unsafe, and tempering can improve predictions in small samples.
Second, we demonstrate that our results remain valid under model misspecification.

Casual reading of our results may suggest the plug-in predictive (
\tau=\infty
) as a sensible default choice for prediction.
This is not the case: our results only bound the difference between the posterior predictive and the plug-in predictive for
n
large enough, and in high-probability.
For example, given
n
observations sampled according to a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
0.5<\theta^{\star}\leq 1
, the plug-in predictive will predict all future observations to be failures with
\mathbb{P}
-probability
(1-\theta^{\star})^{n}
.
This corresponds to the worst possible predictive distribution, and
any

\tau<\infty
would have performed better.
To illustrate this, we fit a conjugate beta-Bernoulli model with a weakly-informative prior to
n
samples from a Bernoulli distribution and plot the predictive performance over
0.01\leq\tau\leq 100
in
Figure

3(a)
.
Even for
n=100
, there are predictively optimal values of
\tau
across some data replicates, and the choice of temperature does influence the predictive performance in small data samples.
Further, this is not a pathology of discrete data, and it is easy to construct similar examples for the continuous case.

6
Discussion

Our results constitute formal evidence of the common folklore that, in terms of predictive accuracy, parameter uncertainty is of second-order importance relative to data and model uncertainty.
The dominant paradigm in statistical forecasting is to produce probabilistic forecasts that maximise the
sharpness
of the predictions, subject to them being
calibrated

(
Gneiting et al.,, 2007
)
.
Sharpness measures the concentration of the predictive around likely values of the future random variable, and calibration is a frequentist notion of coverage regarding future predictions.
Our study formally demonstrates that, at least in moderate sample sizes, neither sharpness nor calibration of the posterior predictive obtained via the power posterior
\pi^{(\tau)}_{n}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})
depend on the temperature
\tau
in any meaningful way.

This is important since, under model misspecification, the oracle model
f_{\theta^{\star}}
is not calibrated, and thus nor is the tempered posterior predictive.
As such, likelihood tempering is not sufficient to remedy the lack of calibration due to model misspecification.
This was already noted by
Syring and Martin, (2019)
, and later investigated by
Wu and Martin, (2021)
who proposed the twice-tempered predictive
\int f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})^{\tau}{\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})}
to resolve this.

Future work will explore which other settings our results extend to.
For instance, the coarsened posteriors introduced by
Miller and Dunson, (2019)
explicitly prohibit posterior concentration, and thus aren’t covered by our assumptions.
It is also not clear for which hierarchical models our results hold, or how our results map to statistically non-identifiable models like Bayesian neural networks.
This is a particularly poignant question since the phenomenon summarised in
Figure

1
was previously noted empirically in this setting and called the
cold posterior effect

(
Wenzel et al.,, 2020
;
Aitchison,, 2021
)
.
While the cold posterior effect was thought to be a pathology of Bayesian neural networks, we have rigorously proven it to be a much more universal phenomenon.
Importantly, the assumptions we imposed to do so are never met in Bayesian neural networks, suggesting that the effect may be recoverable under far weaker assumptions.

Appendix A
Technical results

A.1
Notation

As mentioned in
Section

3.3
, power posteriors are a special case of generalised Bayes posteriors where we update our prior
\pi(\theta)
through an arbitrary loss function
\mathsf{L}_{n}
, which can be different to the negative log-likelihood.
Our results hold for so-called generalised Bayes posteriors beyond just power posteriors.
Indeed, we can replace any mention of a power posterior
\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})\propto\pi(\theta)f_{\theta}(y_{1:n})^{\tau}
with a generalised Bayesian posterior
\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})\propto\pi(\theta)\exp\{-\tau\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta,y_{1:n})\}
in our assumptions, and proceed by studying the generalised posterior predictive
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})=\int f_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})
.
In doing so, we show that our results hold for a wide range of posteriors.

A.2
Main results

In
Assumption

2
, we assumed that the risk-minimiser
\hat{\theta}_{n}
converges to the population-optimal
\theta^{\star}
, and that the Gibbs posterior under that same risk concentrates around a neighbourhood of this
\theta^{\star}
.

Martin and Syring, (2022, Theorem 2)
derived this assumption from two sufficient conditions:
that the risk converges uniformly in
\theta
in
\mathbb{P}
-probability, for any
\delta>0
and for some small
\beta>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\theta:\,d(\theta,\theta^{\star})>\delta}\left|n^{-1/2}\{\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta)-\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta^{\star})-\mathsf{L}(\theta)+\mathsf{L}(\theta^{\star})\}\right|\right]\lesssim\delta^{\beta},

(5)

and, an identifiability condition on
\theta
such that for a small
\alpha>0
,

\inf_{\theta:\,d(\theta,\theta^{\star})>\delta}\{\mathsf{L}(\theta)-\mathsf{L}(\theta^{\star})\}\gtrsim\delta^{\alpha}.

(6)

Under these conditions,
Vaart, (1998, Theorem 5.7, Lemma 19.24)
shows that
\hat{\theta}_{n}
converges to
\theta^{\star}
.
And
Equations

5
and
6
taken together closely resembles
Assumption

3
with
\tau_{n}
fixed.
Under an additional weak prior mass assumption, which resembles our
Assumption

4
,
Martin and Syring, (2022, Theorem 2)
show that the Gibbs posterior
\propto\exp\{-\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta,y_{1:n})\}\pi(\theta)
concentrates around a neighbourhood of
\theta^{\star}
.
Their result is similar to our
Lemma

5
with a fixed
\tau_{n}>0
.
In a word, rather than begin with our
Assumption

2
, one can start from a set of sufficient conditions which are common to the M-estimation literature and arrive at our result and
Assumption

2
that way.

By viewing
\theta
as risk-minimising parameters more generally than the parameters of a statistical model, we can extend the M-estimation literature
(
Vaart,, 1998
, Section 5)
to include prior information
(
Martin and Syring,, 2022
)

\displaystyle Z_{n}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-2\tau_{n}nt_{n}}\int_{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)

\displaystyle=e^{-2\tau_{n}nt_{n}}\left[\Pi(G_{n})-\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right],

where
\Pi(A)=\int_{A}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)
and
\pi(\theta)
is the prior.
So we now have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{Z_{n}\leq\Pi(G_{n})e^{-2n\tau_{n}t_{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(e^{-2\tau_{n}nt_{n}}\left[\Pi(G_{n})-\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]\leq\Pi(G_{n})e^{-2n\tau_{n}t_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[e^{-2\tau_{n}nt_{n}}\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(G_{n})e^{-2n\tau_{n}t_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(G_{n})\right].

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(G_{n})\right]\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]}{\Pi(G_{n})}

(22)

and we must therefore control
\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]
.
By Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}^{n}}\int_{\Theta}\mathds{1}_{\{\theta\in G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})\}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(y_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}^{n}}\int_{\Theta}\mathds{1}_{\{\theta\in G_{n}\}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})\right\}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(y_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{Y}^{n}}\int_{G_{n}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})\right\}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(y_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\int_{G_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}^{n}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})\right\}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(y_{1:n})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)

If
\theta,y_{1:n}\notin\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, then
\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})\right\}}=0
.
As such, for
y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}
, the integrand only takes non-zero values on the joint event
(\theta,y_{1:n})\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.
Therefore, over
(\theta,y_{1:n})\in G_{n}\times\mathcal{Y}^{n}
, the integrand is non-zero only on the event
(\theta,y_{1:n})\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{G_{n}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}^{n}}\mathds{1}_{\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})\right\}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(y_{1:n})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{G_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}:y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\theta)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)

Now, by Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}:y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\theta)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}:|Q_{n}(\theta^{\star},\theta)|\geq t_{n}^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}:\frac{|K_{n}(\theta^{\star},\theta)-K(\theta^{\star},\theta)|}{V(\theta^{\star},\theta)^{1/2}}\geq t_{n}^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}:\frac{n^{1/2}|K_{n}(\theta^{\star},\theta)-K(\theta^{\star},\theta)|}{V(\theta^{\star},\theta)^{1/2}}\geq(nt_{n})^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Y}^{n}:\frac{[n^{1/2}\{K_{n}(\theta^{\star},\theta)-K(\theta^{\star},\theta)\}]^{2}}{V(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\geq nt_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{nt_{n}}\frac{1}{V(\theta^{\star},\theta)}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left([n^{1/2}\{K_{n}(\theta^{\star},\theta)-K(\theta^{\star},\theta)\}]^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1/(nt_{n})

where the expectation in the inequality comes from the definitions of
K(\theta^{\star},\theta)
and
V(\theta^{\star},\theta)
.
Hence, we can bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{G_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left\{y_{1:n}:y_{1:n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\theta)\right\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{nt_{n}}\int_{G_{n}}\pi(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pi(G_{n})}{nt_{n}}.

(23)

Applying
Equation

23
to
Equation

22
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{Z_{n}\leq\Pi(G_{n})e^{-2n\tau_{n}\ t_{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Pi(G_{n})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\Pi\{{G_{n}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{n}(y_{1:n})}\}\right]}{\Pi(G_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\Pi(G_{n})}\frac{\Pi(G_{n})}{nt_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{nt_{n}}

as stated.
∎

Define the set
A_{\varepsilon}=\{\theta\in\Theta:d(\theta,\theta^{\star})\leq K\varepsilon\}
for
K
arbitrarily large.
Using
Lemmas

4
and
3
we achieve a version of
Assumption

2
in the main text where
\tau_{n}
is allowed to shrink to zero.
This result is similar to Theorem 2 in
Shen and Wasserman, (2001)
and Theorem 3 in
Syring and Martin, (2023)
.

Lemma 5
.

Under
3
and
4
, for
\varepsilon_{n}=\max(s_{n},t_{n}^{1/2})
, if
n\tau_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\rightarrow\infty
, for
K>0
and sufficiently large,

, and

sufficiently large,

\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{(\tau_{n})}_{n}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})\lesssim\exp(-nK^{2}c\tau_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/2)

with
\mathbb{P}
-probability converging to one.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon_{n}
as in the statement of the proof,

\displaystyle\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{(\tau_{n})}_{n}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{n}}\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\exp[n\tau_{n}\{\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta)-\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta^{\star})\}]\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta).

(24)

By
Lemma

4
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{Z_{n}\leq\Pi(G_{n})e^{-2n\tau_{n}t_{n}}\right\}\leq\frac{2}{nt_{n}}.

(25)

Now, by
Assumption

3
, with probability converging to one,

\displaystyle\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\exp[n\tau_{n}\{\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta)-\mathsf{L}_{n}(\theta^{\star})\}]\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta)\leq\exp\{-cn\tau_{n}(K\varepsilon_{n})^{2}\},

(26)

since
K
is large and
\varepsilon_{n}\geq s_{n}
.
Hence, applying
Equation

25
and
Equation

26
to
Equation

24
, with
\mathbb{P}
-probability tending to one,

\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{(\tau_{n})}_{n}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})\leq\frac{e^{-cn\tau_{n}K^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}{\Pi(G_{n})e^{-2n\tau_{n}t_{n}}}.

Finally, by
Assumption

4
,
\Pi(G_{n})\gtrsim\exp(-2n\tau_{n}t_{n})
, so that
1/\Pi(G_{n})\lesssim 1/\exp(-2n\tau_{n}t_{n})
, and thus

\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{(\tau_{n})}_{n}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})\leq\frac{e^{-cn\tau_{n}K^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}{e^{-4n\tau_{n}t_{n}}}.

For

large enough so that

, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{A_{\varepsilon_{n}}^{c}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{(\tau_{n})}_{n}(\theta\mid y_{1:n},\mathsf{L}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-cn\tau_{n}K^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}{e^{-4n\tau_{n}t_{n}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-cn\tau_{n}K^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}{e^{-4n\tau_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-cn\tau_{n}K^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}}{e^{-4n\tau_{n}cK^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/8}}

\displaystyle=e^{-cn\tau_{n}K^{2}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}/2}

with
\mathbb{P}

∎

Lemma

4
shows that if
\tau_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then the posterior concentration rate is determined by the condition
n\tau_{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{2}\rightarrow\infty
.
The rate of posterior concentration can then be expressed as
\varepsilon_{\tau,n}=(n\tau_{n})^{-1/2}\log(n)
.
Hence, if we take
\tau_{n}=n^{-1/2}
, the rate of posterior concentration, as determined by
\varepsilon_{\tau,n}
, cannot exceed
n^{-1/4}
.
This illustrates that choosing a learning rate sequence
\tau_{n}\rightarrow 0
essentially reduces your sample size from
n
to
n\tau_{n}
, and reduces the rate of posterior concentration accordingly.

A consequence of
Lemma

4
is that the results on the accuracy of the posterior predictive given in
Lemmas

1
and
2
in the main text remain valid when we replace
\varepsilon_{n}
in those results with
\varepsilon_{\tau,n}
.
Hence, even if we allow
\tau_{n}\rightarrow 0
, a version of the stated results in the main text will remain valid so long as
\tau_{n}
does not go to zero faster than
\log(n)/n
.

\mathsf{L}_{n}

B.1
Normal location example

In order to demonstrate the flatness proven above and referred to in
Section

2.2
of the main text, we empirically show the distance of the posterior predictive distribution,
p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
, to the true predictive,
q^{\star}_{n}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})
.
We do this for a range of
\tau
, two different prior distributions, and two distances: the total variation distance, and the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

We simulate data from a
\mathcal{N}(\theta^{\star}=0,{\sigma^{\star}}^{2}=1)
distribution and consider the likelihood
\mathcal{N}(y_{1:n};\,\theta,\sigma=1)
, so that the model is well-specified.
We define a weakly-informative prior
\pi(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\theta;\,0,\sigma_{0}^{2})
.
Letting
\sigma_{0}\to\infty
results in a flat prior over the parameter space.
In this case, the posterior predictive density is
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\,\mu_{n},\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2})
where

\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n\tau/\sigma^{2}+1/\sigma_{0}^{2}},\quad\mu_{n}=\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{n\tau\bar{y}_{1:n}}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

Here and throughout,
\bar{y}_{1:n}
denotes the sample mean over
n
observations.
We simulate
1,000
data replicates from the true distribution and compute the total variation distance by quadrature integration for the experiments in the main text.

Now for the Kullback-Leibler divergence, we have that

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left\{q^{\star}_{n}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n});\,p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\sigma^{2}_{n})+\frac{1+\mu_{n}^{2}}{2(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})}-\frac{1}{2}.

We can analytically compute the expectation under
y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}
to achieve the so-called
risk
as a function of
\tau
,

\displaystyle\textsc{risk}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left\{q^{\star}_{n}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n});\,p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\cdot\mid y_{1:n})\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\sigma^{2}_{n})+\frac{1+n\tau\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{y_{1:n}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left\{(\bar{y}_{1:n})^{2}\right\}}{2(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})}-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\sigma^{2}_{n})+\frac{1+\tau\sigma_{n}^{2}}{2(1+\sigma_{n}^{2})}-\frac{1}{2}\cdot

If we take
\sigma_{0}^{2}\to\infty
to simplify this (which is equivalent to the prior disappearing with
n
), we get

\textsc{risk}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{n\tau}\right)+\frac{1+n^{-1}}{2\{1+(n\tau)^{-1}\}}-\frac{1}{2}.

(27)

The derivative of which with respect to
\tau
is

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\textsc{risk}(\tau)=\frac{1}{2n}\left\{\frac{\tau(1+n^{-1})-(\tau+n^{-1})}{\tau(\tau+n^{-1})^{2}}\right\}=\frac{1}{2n}\frac{(\tau-1)n^{-1}}{\tau(\tau+n^{-1})^{2}},

which is negative for
\tau<1
, 0 at
\tau=1
and increasing for
\tau>1
.
This means a theoretical optimum is attained at
\tau=1
.

Figure B.1
:
Normal location example. The grey curves correspond to individual dataset replicates, dotted black lines to
5\%
and
95\%
quantiles, and solid black curves to expectation.

In Figure
B.1
we plot the Kullback-Leibler divergence and the risk across two priors: a weakly-informative
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}=1)
prior, and a flat prior (letting
\sigma_{0}^{2}\to\infty
).

As previously mentioned, in practice, we can estimate the Kullback-Leibler divergence with the expected negative log-likelihood, and approximate the inner expectation with cross-validation.
We call this approximation the leave-one-out cross-validation expected log-predictive density
(
Vehtari et al.,, 2017
)
, or just the cross-validation score in short.
In this case, the cross-validation score is

\textsc{elpd}(\tau)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{n}^{(\tau)}(y_{i}\mid y_{-i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\mathcal{N}(y_{i};\,\mu_{-i},\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{-i}^{2}),

(28)

where now

\sigma_{-i}^{2}=\frac{1}{(n-1)\tau/\sigma^{2}+1/\sigma_{0}^{2}},\quad\mu_{-i}=\sigma_{-i}^{2}\left\{\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{(n-1)\tau\bar{y}_{-i}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

It is worth emphasising that our results are meant only to explain the impact of the temperature parameter on predictive inferences when
n
is large enough so that posterior concentration is in evidence, and they are not necessarily indicative of the behaviour of the posterior predictive in small samples.
Indeed, as suggested by the experiments in
(
Grünwald and van Ommen,, 2017
, Figure 3)
, the role of the temperature parameter can have meaningful impacts on predictive performance in small-to-moderate samples, but its impact abates as the sample size increases.
It is relatively straightforward to illustrate this behaviour in our simple Gaussian example.

Figure B.2
:
Normal location example. Lines correspond to the scaled risk of
Equation

27
across different values of
\tau
.

Consider a prior centred on the true parameter value
\pi(\theta)=\mathcal{N}(\theta;\theta^{\star},\surd 0.25^{2})
.
In
Figure

B.2
we see how, once
n
is large enough, the risk induced by different values of
\tau
converge.
While
n
is small, however, tempering can offer a significant predictive advantage.
In this case, the prior is very good and so assigning it more weight than the data results in better predictive distributions when the data are small.
This is seen in the figure inset, where small values of
\tau
significantly out-perform the Bayes predictive (
\tau=1
).
What our theoretical results (e.g.
Lemma

2
) show is that, at a rate which depends on the posterior concentration, this predictive difference vanishes in
n
.
And eventually, all choices of
\tau
result in indistinguishable predictive distributions, although for small
\tau
this phenomenon only occurs at larger
n
.
As such, the predictive benefits of power posteriors are in finite-samples, and vanish with
n
.
Crucially, this behaviour is independent of whether or not the model is well-specified.

B.4
Linear regression

Let
\epsilon
denote the ‘outlier’ rate (which we set to
\epsilon=0.5
) and
\delta
the standard deviation of the outlier distribution (
\delta=\surd 0.01
) in a linear regression experiment.
We then simulate the predictors by
X_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{p},I_{p})
, and the target according to

y_{i}\sim(1-\epsilon)\mathcal{N}(X_{i}^{\top}\beta^{\star},{\sigma^{\star}}^{2})+\epsilon\mathcal{N}(0,\delta^{2}).

We suppress the dependence on the fixed and known constants
\epsilon,\delta
, and
\sigma^{\star}
going forward (fixing
\sigma^{\star}=1
).
The true parameters are
\beta^{\star}=(0.1,0.1,0.1,0.1,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

p=5
and only the first four are relevant.

Consider the regression coefficients
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with weakly-informative prior
\mathcal{N}(0_{p},\Sigma_{0})
.
The likelihood is
f_{\beta}(y_{i}\mid X_{i})=\mathcal{N}(y_{i};\,\beta^{\top}X_{i},{\sigma^{\star}}^{2})
, and is thus misspecified.
Now for data
\mathsf{D}=\{X,y\}
with
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
,

\displaystyle\log\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\beta\mid\mathsf{D})

\displaystyle=\log\pi(\beta)+\tau\log f_{\beta}(y\mid X)+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\beta-\frac{\tau}{2{\sigma^{\star}}^{2}}\lVert X\beta-y\rVert^{2}+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{\top}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\beta-\frac{\tau}{2{\sigma^{\star}}^{2}}\left(\beta^{\top}X^{\top}X\beta-2\beta^{\top}X^{\top}y+y^{\top}y\right)+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\bigg\{\beta^{\top}\underbrace{\left(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}+\tau{\sigma^{\star}}^{-2}X^{\top}X\right)}_{M}\beta-2\beta^{\top}\underbrace{\left(\tau{\sigma^{\star}}^{-2}X^{\top}y\right)}_{b}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta^{\top}M\beta-2\beta^{\top}b\right)+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left\{(\beta-M^{-1}b)^{\top}M(\beta-M^{-1}b)\right\}+C

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(\beta-\beta_{n})^{\top}\Sigma_{n}^{-1}(\beta-\beta_{n})+C

C
which does not depend on
\beta
, and where

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=\tau{\sigma^{\star}}^{-2}\Sigma_{n}X^{\top}y,\quad\Sigma_{n}^{-1}=\tau{\sigma^{\star}}^{-2}X^{\top}X+\Sigma_{0}^{-1},

so that the posterior is
\mathcal{N}(\beta;\,\beta_{n},\Sigma_{n})

Considering the posterior predictive evaluated at new datum

\displaystyle p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\tilde{y}\mid\tilde{X},\mathsf{D})

\displaystyle=\int f_{\beta}(\tilde{y}\mid\tilde{X})\pi^{(\tau)}_{n}(\beta\mid\mathsf{D})\,\,\mathrm{d}\beta

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{y};\,\beta_{n}^{\top}\tilde{X},\tilde{X}^{\top}\Sigma_{n}\tilde{X}+{\sigma^{\star}}^{2}).

(29)

In the leave-one-out case, we have

\textsc{elpd}(\tau)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\mathcal{N}(y_{i};\;\beta_{-i}^{\top}X_{i},X_{i}^{\top}\Sigma_{-i}X_{i}+{\sigma^{\star}}^{2}),

where

\displaystyle\beta_{-i}=\tau{\sigma^{\star}}^{-2}\Sigma_{-i}X_{-i}^{\top}y_{-i},\quad\Sigma_{-i}^{-1}=\tau{\sigma^{\star}}^{-2}X_{-i}^{\top}X_{-i}+\Sigma_{0}^{-1}.

Beta-binomial example

Consider data sampled according to
y_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\theta^{\star})
.
Suppose of these
n
observations we observe
x
successes and
z=n-x
failures, then we model the data as coming from a beta-binomial model with prior
\mathrm{beta}(\alpha,\beta)
.
1
1

1

We use the shape-scale parameterisation of the beta distribution throughout.

The posterior distribution under Bayesian inference with the likelihood scaled by
\tau
following the
n
observations is then
\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})\propto\mathrm{beta}(\theta;\,\tau x+\alpha,\tau z+\beta)
.
In turn we have the posterior predictive

\displaystyle p_{n}^{(\tau)}(\tilde{y}\mid y_{1:n})\,

\displaystyle=\int f_{\theta}(\tilde{y})\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{n}^{(\tau)}(\theta\mid y_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\text{beta-binomial}(1,\tau x+\alpha,\tau z+\beta)

evaluated on the hitherto unseen observation
\tilde{y}
.
Considering the leave-one-out case, denoting
x_{-i}
the number of successes with the
i
-th datum deleted, and likewise for
z_{-i}
the number of failures.
Then the cross-validation score is

\displaystyle\textsc{elpd}(\tau)\,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_{n}^{(\tau)}(y_{i}\mid y_{-i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\{\text{beta-binomial}(y_{i};\,1,\tau x_{-i}+\alpha,\tau z_{-i}+\beta)\}.
