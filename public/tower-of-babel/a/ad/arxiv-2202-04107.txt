1.
Preliminaries

We use standard conventions.
Nethertheless we recall some notation. For more details see
[
1
]
.

Let
A
be a finite non-empty set and
A^{*}
be
the free monoid generated by
A
. The set
A
is also called an
alphabet
, its elements are called
letters
and
those of
A^{*}
are called
finite words
. The identity element of
A^{*}
is called
an
empty word
and denoted by
\lambda
. We set

A^{+}=A^{*}\backslash\{\lambda\}
.

The length of a word
w
, denoted by
|w|
, is the number of occurrences of the letters
in
w
. In other words, if
w=w_{0}w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}
with
w_{i}\in A
,
0\leq i<n
, then
|w|=n
. In
particular, the length of the empty word is zero. The set of words of length
k
over
A
is denoted by
A^{k}
.

Definition 1.1

A 2–sorted algebra
\mathcal{M}=\langle Q,A,\circ,\ast\rangle
is called a
Mealy machine if
Q,A
are finite, nonempty sets, the mappings

Q\times A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\longrightarrow}}Q
,

Q\times A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\longrightarrow}}A

are total functions.

•

Q
is called a set of states;

•

A
— an alphabet;

•

\circ
— the transition function;

•

*
— the output function.

One draws

\displaystyle\bigcirc

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{a/b}

\displaystyle\bigcirc

(1)

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle q_{2}

to mean
q_{1}\circ a=q_{2},q_{1}*a=b
. That is, in the state
q_{1}
with the input
a
, the machine
outputs
b
and goes to the state
q_{2}
.

The machine is said to be
invertible
if for each state
q\in Q
the output function is a permutation.
The inverse machine is the
machine
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
obtained by switching the input
a
and the output
b
on each
arrow (
1
). The resulting transition in the inverse machine is
(
2
).

\displaystyle\bigcirc

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{b/a}

\displaystyle\bigcirc

(2)

\displaystyle q_{1}

\displaystyle q_{2}

The mappings
\circ
and
\ast
may be extended to
Q\times A^{*}
by defining

\begin{array}[]{lr}q\circ\lambda=q,&q\circ(ua)=(q\circ u)\circ a,\\
q\ast\lambda=\lambda,&q\ast(ua)=(q\ast u)\#((q\circ u)\ast a)\,,\end{array}

for each
q\in Q
,
(u,a)\in A^{*}\times A
. Here
\#
means the concatenation of words. In other words, if

u=u_{0}u_{1}\cdots u_{n}
,
v=v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{k}
, then
u\#v=uv=u_{0}u_{1}\cdots u_{n}v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{k}
.

2.
An Ultrametric

An (indexed) infinite word
x
on the alphabet
A
is any total mapping

x\,:\,\mathbb{N}\rightarrow A
. We shall set for any
i\geq 0
,
x_{i}=x(i)

and write

x=(x_{i})=x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n}\cdots\;.

The set of all the infinite words over
A
is denoted by
A^{\omega}
.

Let
A^{\infty}=A^{*}\cup A^{\omega}
. A word
u\in A^{*}
is a
prefix
of

x\in A^{\infty}
if there exist
u\in A^{*}
,
y\in A^{\infty}
such that
x=uy
.
The word
y
is called the
suffix
in this situation.
The set of
prefixes of
x
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(x)
.

Definition 2.1

A mapping
d:A^{\infty}\times A^{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
is called the prefix metric, if

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}0,&{\rm if}\;x=y;\\
2^{-n},&{\rm if}\;x\neq y\;{\rm and}\;n=\max\{|u|\,|\,u\in\mathcal{P}(x)\cap\mathcal{P}(y)\}.\end{cases}

An
ultrametric
is a metric which satisfies the following strengthened version of the triangle inequality:

d(x,z)\leq\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))\quad{\rm for\;all}\quad x,y,z.

Proposition 2.2

The prefix metric is an ultrametric.

\Box
(i) If
x=z
, then
d(x,z)=0\leq\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))
.

(ii) If
x\neq z
, then
d(x,z)=2^{-n}
, where
\forall i<n\;(x_{i}=z_{i})
but
x_{n}\neq z_{n}
.
Hence,
x_{n}\neq y_{n}
or
y_{n}\neq z_{n}
. Therefore
d(x,y)\geq 2^{-n}
or
d(y,z)\geq 2^{-n}
. Thus,

d(x,z)=2^{-n}\leq\max(d(x,y),d(y,z))
.

If
\mathcal{M}=\langle Q,A,\circ,\ast\rangle
is a
Mealy machine, then the mapping
\ast
may be extended to
Q\times A^{\infty}
by defining

q*x=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}q*x[0,n],

where
x[0,n]=x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
.

We like to be more flexible therefore we sometimes write
uq
instead of
q*u
and state
q
identify with map
q*u
. In other words we identify map
\bar{q}:A^{*}\to A^{*}:u\mapsto q*u
with
q
.
For an invertible Mealy machine
\mathcal{M}
, the group generated by
\{\bar{q}\,|\,q\in Q\}

under the operation of composition is called the machine (automaton) group

\Gamma(\mathcal{M})
.

3.
Sequential functions

Now we recall standard results about sequential functions. Proofs see in
[
1
]
.

Definition 3.1

A total mapping
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
is called a sequential function if

(i)

\forall u\in A^{*}\;|u|=|f(u)|
;

(ii)

u\in\mathcal{P}(v)\Rightarrow f(u)\in\mathcal{P}(f(v))
.

Corollary 3.2

For all sequential functions, we have that if

u\in\mathcal{P}(v)\cap\mathcal{P}(w),

then

f(u)\in\mathcal{P}(f(v))\cap\mathcal{P}(f(w)).

Definition 3.3

Let
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
be a sequential function and
u\in A^{*}
, then
f_{u}(v)
defines a suffix of mapping
f(uv)
with length
|v|
.
The mapping
f_{u}
is called a remainder of sequential function
f
.

Corollary 3.4

f(uv)=f(u)f_{u}(v)
.

Lemma 3.5

uw\in\mathcal{P}(uv)\Rightarrow w\in\mathcal{P}(v)
.

Proposition 3.6

The remainder
f_{u}
is a sequential function.

Lemma 3.7

If mapping
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
is a sequential function,
then

f_{u}(vw)=f_{u}(v)f_{uv}(w).

Proposition 3.8

Let a mapping
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
be a sequential
function. If
f_{u}=f_{u^{\prime}}
, then

\forall v\in A^{*}\;f_{uv}=f_{u^{\prime}v}.

Lemma 3.9

If
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
is a a sequential
function, then

f_{uv}=(f_{u})_{v}.

\Box
Let
w\in A^{*}
, then

\displaystyle f(uvw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(uv)f_{uv}(w)=f(u)f_{u}(v)f_{uv}(w),

\displaystyle f(uvw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(u)f_{u}(vw)=f(u)f_{u}(v)(f_{u})_{v}(w).

Thus,
f_{uv}(w)=(f_{u})_{v}(w)
.

Proposition 3.10

Let mappings
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
and
g:B^{*}\to C^{*}

be sequential functions, then

\forall u\in A^{*}\;(g\circ f)_{u}=g_{f(u)}\circ f_{u}.

Here
(g\circ f)(x)=f(g(x))
.

Definition 3.11

Let a mapping
f:A^{*}\to B^{*}
be a sequential function. The function
f
defines the set

Q_{f}=\{f_{u}\,|\,u\in A^{*}\},

where
f_{u}
is a remainder of
f
. The function
f
is called a restricted
sequential function (or a sequential function on a restricted domain) if
the set
Q_{f}
is finite.

Definition 3.12

A 2-sorted algebra
\mathcal{M}=\langle Q,A,q_{0},\circ,\ast\rangle
is called an
initial Mealy machine if

•

q_{0}\in Q
;

•

\langle Q,A,\circ,\ast\rangle
is a Mealy machine.

Theorem 3.13

A function
f:A^{*}\to A^{*}
is the restricted
sequential function if and only if there exists an
initial Mealy machine
\mathcal{M}=\langle Q,A,q_{0},\circ,\ast\rangle
such that

\forall v\in A^{*}\quad f(v)=q_{0}*v.

4.
\omega
–sequential functions

Definition 4.1

A total mapping
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to B^{\omega}
is called an
\omega
–sequential function if
it satisfies such condition:

u\in\mathcal{P}(x)\cap\mathcal{P}(y)\Rightarrow\zeta(x)[0,|u|)=\zeta(y)[0,|u|).

Here, if
\zeta(x)=z_{0}z_{1}\cdots z_{n}\cdots
then
\zeta(x)[0,n)=z_{0}z_{1}\cdots z_{n-1}
.

Proposition 4.2

If
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to B^{\omega}
,
\xi:B^{\omega}\to C^{\omega}
are
\omega
–sequential functions then

\zeta\xi=\xi\circ\zeta:A^{\omega}\to C^{\omega}
is an
\omega
–sequential function.

\Box
Let
u\in\mathcal{P}(x)\cap\mathcal{P}(y)
then
\zeta(x)[0,|u|)=\zeta(y)[0,|u|)
.
Hence

\xi(\zeta(x))[0,|u|)=\xi(\zeta(y))[0,|u|).\quad\rule{5.69054pt}{5.69054pt}

If
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to B^{\omega}
is an
\omega
–sequential function then

\forall x\in A^{\omega}\;\forall y\in A^{\omega}\;\forall u\in A^{*}\quad\zeta(ux)[0,|u|)=\zeta(uy)[0,|u|).

Hence
\zeta(ux)=\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(x)
, where
\epsilon
and
\zeta_{u}
are mappings

\epsilon:A^{|u|}\to B^{|u|},\quad\zeta_{u}:A^{\omega}\to B^{\omega}.

The mapping
\zeta_{u}
is called the
remainder
of
\zeta
for the word
u
.

Proposition 4.3

\zeta_{u}
is an
\omega
–sequential function.

\Box
Let
v\in\mathcal{P}(x)\cap\mathcal{P}(y)
then
uv\in\mathcal{P}(ux)\cap\mathcal{P}(uy)
.
Since
\zeta
is an
\omega
–sequential function then

\displaystyle\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(vx)[0,|v|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta(uvx)[0,|uv|)=\zeta(uvy)[0,|uv|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(vy)[0,|v|)\quad\rule{5.69054pt}{5.69054pt}

Lemma 4.4

If
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to B^{\omega}
is an
\omega
–sequential function then

\zeta_{uv}=(\zeta_{u})_{v}
.

\displaystyle\Box\quad\zeta(uvx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}(u)\#\zeta_{u}(vx),\quad{\rm where}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta(uvx)[0,|u|);

\displaystyle\zeta_{u}(vx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}(v)\#(\zeta_{u})_{v}(x),\quad{\rm where}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{u}(vx)[0,|v|);

\displaystyle\zeta(uvx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}(uv)\#\zeta_{uv}(x),\quad{\rm where}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{3}(uv)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta(uvx)[0.|uv|).

Since
|\epsilon_{1}(u)|+|\epsilon_{2}(v)|=|u|+|v|=|uv|=\epsilon_{3}(uv)
then

(\zeta_{u})_{v}=\zeta_{uv}
.

Lemma 4.5

If
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to A^{\omega}
is a bijective
\omega
–sequential function then

\epsilon:A^{|u|}\to A^{|u|}
is a bijection.

\Box
Let
u\neq v
. If
\epsilon(u)=\epsilon(v)
then there exists
w^{\prime}\in A^{|u|}
such that

\forall w\in A^{|u|}\quad\epsilon(w)\neq w^{\prime}

because the set
A^{|u|}
is finite.

Let
w^{\prime}y\in A^{\omega}
then there exists
x\in A^{\omega}
such that
\zeta(x)=w^{\prime}y
because
\zeta
is a surjection.
Let
w\in\mathcal{P}(x)
and
|w|=|w^{\prime}|
then
x=wx^{\prime}
for some
x^{\prime}\in A^{\omega}
and

w^{\prime}y=\zeta(x)=\zeta(wx^{\prime})=\epsilon(w)\#\zeta_{w}(x^{\prime}).

Therefore
\epsilon(w)=w^{\prime}
. Contradiction!

This means that
\epsilon:A^{|u|}\to A^{|u|}
is an injection.
Now we can conclude that
\epsilon:A^{|u|}\to A^{|u|}
is a bijection because the set
A^{|u|}
is finite.

Lemma 4.6

If
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to A^{\omega}
is a bijective
\omega
–sequential function then
\zeta_{u}
is a bijection.

\Box
We know
\zeta(ux)=\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(x)
. Let
\epsilon(u)=v
. Since
\epsilon:A^{|u|}\to A^{|u|}
is a bijection then there exists only one word
u^{\prime}
for which
\epsilon:u^{\prime}\mapsto v
, namely
u
is such the word.
Therefore the words
vy
can be obtained only from words
ux
, namely,

\forall y\in A^{\omega}\;\exists x\in A^{\omega}\quad vy=\zeta(ux)=\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(x)=v\zeta_{u}(x)

Hence
y=\zeta_{u}(x)
. This means that
\zeta_{u}
is a surjection.

Since
\zeta
is an injection then

\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(x)=\zeta(ux)\neq\zeta(ux^{\prime})=\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(x^{\prime})

if
x\neq x^{\prime}
. Hence
\zeta_{u}(x)\neq\zeta_{u}(x^{\prime})
. This means that
\zeta_{u}
is an injection.

Now we can conclude that
\zeta_{u}
is a bijection because
\zeta_{u}
is both a surjection and an injection.

Lemma 4.7

If
\zeta:A^{\omega}\to A^{\omega}
is a bijective
\omega
–sequential function then
\zeta^{-1}
is a bijective
\omega
–sequential function.

\Box
Since
\zeta
is a bijection then
\zeta^{-1}
exists and it is a bijection. We must prove that

\zeta^{-1}(wx)[0,|w|)=\zeta^{-1}(wy)[0,|w|)
for all
w\in A^{*}
and all infinite words
x,y
over alphabet
A
.

Let
ux^{\prime}=\zeta^{-1}(wx)
,
vy^{\prime}=\zeta^{-1}(wy)
and
|u|=|v|=|w|

then

\zeta(ux^{\prime})=\zeta\zeta^{-1}(wx)=wx,\quad\zeta(vy^{\prime})=\zeta\zeta^{-1}(wy)=wy

and

\zeta^{-1}(wx)[0,|w|)=u
,
\zeta^{-1}(wy)[0,|w|)=v
.

wx=\zeta(ux^{\prime})=\epsilon(u)\#\zeta_{u}(x^{\prime}),\quad wy=\zeta(vy^{\prime})=\epsilon(v)\#\zeta_{v}(y^{\prime}).

Hence
\epsilon(u)=w=\epsilon(v)
and thus
u=v
because
\epsilon:A^{|u|}\to A^{|u|}
is a bijection.
Therefore
\zeta^{-1}(wx)[0,|w|)=u=v=\zeta^{-1}(wy)[0,|w|)
.

6.
Formal power series

Let
R
be an associative commutative ring with a multiplicative identity element and
X\not\in R
. We shall identify
R^{\omega}
with
R[[X]]
via

(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n},\ldots)\mapsto\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}X^{k},

where on the left hand side we write sometimes elements of
R^{\omega}
as tuples to avoid
confusion between concatenation of words and the multiplication in
R
.
If
f=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}X^{k}
then we write
f(n)=a_{n}
.

Definition 6.1

Algebra
\langle R[[X]],+,\cdot\rangle
is called a formal power series if

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}X^{k}+\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}X^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}(a_{k}+b_{k})X^{k},

\displaystyle\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}X^{k}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}X^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}b_{k-i}\right)X^{k}

9.
A Semidirect product

Let

\bullet

\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}
be groups;

\bullet

\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N})
— a group homomorphism, where

\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N})
— the automorphism group of the group
\mathcal{N}
.

We shall write
\varphi^{h}\in\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N})
insted of
\varphi(h)
.
Then define
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{N}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{N}\rtimes\mathcal{H}
to be the set

\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{H}
with the multiplication defined by

(n_{1},h_{1})(n_{2},h_{2})=(n_{1}\varphi^{h_{1}}(n_{2}),h_{1}h_{2}).

Definition 9.1

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group with subgroups
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}
such that

\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{H}=\{e\}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{N}\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}.

If
\mathcal{N}
is normal (but not necessarily
\mathcal{H}
), then we say that
\mathcal{G}
is the semidirect product of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}
. Here
e
is the neutral element of
\mathcal{G}
.

We recall some standard results (see for example in
[
2
]
).

Lemma 9.2

Let
h\in\mathcal{G}
. If
\mathcal{N}\trianglelefteq\mathcal{G}
then
\varphi^{h}\in\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N})
where

\varphi^{h}:x\mapsto hxh^{-1}
.

\Box
(i)
\varphi^{h}(xy)=hxyh^{-1}=hxh^{-1}hyh^{-1}=\varphi^{h}(x)\varphi^{h}(y)
. Thus
\varphi^{h}
is a homomorphism.

(ii) If
\varphi^{h}(x)=\varphi^{h}(y)
then
hxh^{-1}=hyh^{-1}
. Hence
x=y
, therefore
\varphi^{h}
is an injection.

(iii) Since
\mathcal{N}\trianglelefteq\mathcal{G}
then
h^{-1}yh\in\mathcal{N}
for every
y\in\mathcal{N}
.
Hence
\varphi^{h}(h^{-1}yh)=hh^{-1}yhh^{-1}=y
. Thus
\varphi^{h}
is a surjection.

Theorem 9.3

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the semidirect product of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}
. If

\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N}):h\mapsto\varphi^{h}

is defined by

\varphi^{h}:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{N}:n\mapsto hnh^{-1}

then
\varphi
is a homomorphism and

f:\mathcal{N}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}:(n,h)\mapsto nh

is an isomorphism.

\displaystyle\Box\;{\rm(i)}\quad\varphi^{kh}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle khn(kh)^{-1}=khnh^{-1}k^{-1},

\displaystyle\varphi^{k}\circ\varphi^{h}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi^{k}(\varphi^{h}(n))=\varphi^{k}(hnh^{-1})=khnh^{-1}k^{-1}.

Thus
\varphi^{kh}=\varphi^{k}\circ\varphi^{h}
or
\varphi(kh)=\varphi(k)\circ\varphi(h)
. Hence

\varphi:\mathcal{H}\to\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N}):h\mapsto\varphi(h)

is a homomorphism.

(ii) Since
\mathcal{G}
is the semidirect product of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}
then
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{N}\mathcal{H}
. Therefore the map

f:\mathcal{N}\rtimes_{\varphi}\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{G}:(n,h)\mapsto nh

is surjective.

(iii) Let
f(n_{1},h_{1})=f(n_{2},h_{2})
. This means that
n_{1}h_{1}=n_{2}h_{2}
or
n_{2}^{-1}n_{1}=h_{2}h_{1}^{-1}
.
Since
\mathcal{G}
is the semidirect product of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{H}
then
\mathcal{N}\cap\mathcal{H}=\{e\}
.
Hence
n_{2}^{-1}n_{1}=e=h_{2}h_{1}^{-1}
. Thus
n_{1}=n_{2}
and
h_{1}=h_{2}
. Therefore
f
is injective.

\displaystyle{\rm(iv)}\quad f((n_{1},h_{1})(n_{2},h_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(n_{1}\varphi^{h_{1}}(n_{2}),h_{1}h_{2})=n_{1}\varphi^{h_{1}}(n_{2})h_{1}h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{1}(h_{1}n_{2}h_{1}^{-1})h_{1}h_{2}=n_{1}h_{1}n_{2}h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(n_{1},h_{1})f(n_{2},h_{2})

Hence
f
is a homomorphism.

Now we can conclude that

is an isomorphism.

Lemma 9.4

If
\phi\in\mathfrak{A}ut(\mathcal{N}_{1})
and
