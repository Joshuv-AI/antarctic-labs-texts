Abstract
In this study we propose a unified model of optimal retirement, consumption and portfolio choice of an individual agent, which encompasses a large class of the models in the literature and provide a general methodology to solve the model. Different from the traditional approach, we consider the problems before and after retirement simultaneously and identify the difference in the dual value functions as the utility value of lifetime labor. The utility value has an option nature, namely, it is the maximized value of choosing the retirement time optimally and we discover it by solving a variational inequality. Then, we discover the dual value functions by using the utility value. We discover the value function and optimal policies by establishing a duality between the value function and the dual value function.

Introduction

In this study, we propose and investigate a general model of optimal retirement, consumption and portfolio choice of an individual agent. The optimal retirement decision is an important determinant of labor supply and human capital. The option of voluntary retirement makes the beta of human capital negative, since one can work longer after poor realizations of investment outcomes and has an important effect on risk taking attitudes (
Dybvig and Liu, 2010
). Researchers have developed economic models of the retirement decision in conjunction with the consumption and portfolio choice. In particular, they have shown that the retirement option induces people to increase savings and to make aggressive investments before retirement and to increase risky positions in a stock market boom even after making large profits (
Choi and Shim, 2006
,
Fahri and Panageas, 2007
,
Choi et al., 2008
, and
Yang and Koo, 2018
).
The models, thus, have provided a profound insight into the interaction between the retiement decision and the life cycle choice of consumption and investment.

The models in the literature, however, are highly specialized and often make contradicting predictions. For example, the models of
Fahri and Panageas, 2007
and
Dybvig and Liu, 2010
predict jumps of consumption at retirement, whereas those of
Choi and Shim, 2006
and
Yang and Koo, 2018
do not. The difference in the models originates from the special utility functions they employ, and hence, the predictions are not robust to changes in the utility functions.

Our objective is (i) to propose a unified model, which encompasses a large class of the models in the literature and (ii) to provide a general methodology to solve the model. For the first objective, we propose a model with minimum assumptions among those employing time separable expected utility functions with a constant subjective discount rate and stationary felicity functions before and after retirement in a single-good economy. The minimum assumptions consist of two; Assumption
1
is the condition for the problem to be well-defined, which is much less restrictive than those in the literature, and Assumption
2
is the necessary and sufficient condition for the retirement option to have a positive value. We next solve the problem by the dual martingale approach. However, we do not take the traditional approach of taking sequential steps; solving the problem after retirement in the first step and then solving the problem before retirement in the second step, taking the solution in the second step as an essential ingredient.

Most of the papers in the literature take this two-step approach. See
Yang and Koo, 2018
for a systematic treatment.
Instead we consider the problems before and after retirement simultaneously and identify the difference in the dual value functions as the utility value of lifetime labor. The utility value has an option nature, namely, it is the maximized value of choosing the retirement time optimally, and can be obtained as a solution to a variational inequality. Then, we find the dual value functions by using the utility value. We discover the value function and optimal policies by establishing a duality between the value function and the dual value function. In this way we show the total wealth of the agent is composed of financial wealth and human wealth and human wealth can be derived as a part of the derivative of utility value of lifetime labor.

As an illustrative example we provide a model which subsumes major models in the literature as special cases. We obtain optimal policies in closed form when the felicity function has constant relative risk aversion.

Model

We consider an agent who lives in a single good economy. The agent is currently working and has an option to retire voluntarily. The agent cannot come back to work after retirement, and hence, retirement is an irreversible decision. The agent’s preference over consumption is represented by the following expected utility function:

U\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}u_{B}(c_{t})dt+\int_{\tau}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}u_{A}(c_{t})dt\right].

(1)

Optimization

We derive a solution to the agent’s optimization problem by the standard dual martingale approach developed by
Karatzas et al., 1987

Cox and Huang, 1989

\displaystyle{\xi}_{t}=e^{-rt-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}t-\theta B_{t}},\quad\theta\equiv\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}.

(9)

Note that the constant
\theta
is equal to the Sharpe ratio of the risky asset.

In
the following proposition, we transform the wealth dynamics (
6

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
x>-\frac{\epsilon}{r}.
Suppose
(c,\pi,\tau)\in{\cal A}(x).
Then
c=(c_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
satisfies the following static budget constraint

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi\left(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}}\right)dt\right]=x.

(10)

Conversely, suppose that
c=(c_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
satisfies the first condition in (
8
) and
\tau\in{\cal S}
such that (
10
) is satisfied. Then there exists a portfolio process
\pi_{t}
such that
(c,\pi,\tau)\in{\cal A}(x)
. The corresponding wealth process

dX_{t}^{x,c,\pi}=[rX_{t}^{x,c,\pi}+(\mu-r)\pi_{t}-c_{t}+\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}}]dt+\sigma\pi_{t}dB_{t}\;\;\;t\geq 0

and

X_{t}^{x,c,\pi}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{\infty}\dfrac{\xi_{s}}{\xi_{t}}(c_{s}-\epsilon 1_{\{s<\tau\}})ds\right],

(11)

where
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\mid{\cal F}_{t}\right]
is the conditional expectation at time

{\cal F}_{t}

3.2
Dual formulation

We formulate the following Lagrangian:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathfrak{L}=&\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}u_{B}(c_{t})dt+\int_{\tau}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}u_{A}(c_{t})dt\right]+y\left(x-\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}{\xi}_{t}(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}})dt\right]\right)\\

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{A}(y)\equiv

\displaystyle\sup_{c\geq 0}\left(u_{A}(c)-yc\right)=u_{A}(I_{u_{A}}(y))-yI_{u_{B}}(y),

respectively. The dual variable
{\cal Y}_{t}
is the
marginal value
of wealth, as will be shown in Theorem
4.1
.

Thus, for
y>0
the candidate of optimal consumption
(\hat{c}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y}))_{t=0}^{\infty}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{c}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})=\begin{cases}I_{u_{B}}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})\;\;\;&\mbox{for}\;\;0\leq t<\tau,\\
I_{u_{A}}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})\;\;\;&\mbox{for}\;\;t\geq\tau.\end{cases}\end{split}

(20)

Considering the Lagrangian (
18
), we formulate the dual problem which chooses the optimal time of retirement by observing the changes of the marginal value of wealth
{\cal Y}_{t}
.

Problem 2
.

J(y)=J_{A}(y)+\sup_{\tau\in{\cal S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})+{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon\right)dt\right],

(21)

where
{\cal S}
denotes the set of
{\mathcal{F}}-
stopping times. We call
J(y)
the dual value function.

Problem
2
states that the dual value function consists of
J_{A}
and the optimized value of the optimal stopping problem. If
\tau=0
, i.e., the agent retires immediately, then the dual value function is equal to
J_{A}
, and hence,
J_{A}
can be regarded as the
dual value function after retirement
. The quantity

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})+{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon

(22)

inside the integral in the optimal stopping problem is composed of two components: the first is the difference in the conjugate felicity functions,

\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y}),
which can be interpreted as the difference in the utility values before and after retirement, and the second is
{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon
, labor income adjusted by the marginal utility of wealth and can be interpreted as the utility value of labor income. Accordingly, quantity (
22
) is the
marginal benefit of work
relative to retirement. The agent chooses the retirement time
\tau
that maximizes the present value of the marginal benefit. In this sense we will call the optimized value of the optimal stopping problem as the
utility value of lifetime labor.

Let us denote the utility value of lifetime labor by
{\cal P}(y),
i.e.,

{\cal P}(y)=\sup_{\tau\in{\cal S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})+{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon\right)dt\right].

(23)

Let us denote the current normalized marginal benefit of work by
\Psi(y),
i.e.,

\Psi(y)=\dfrac{1}{y}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)\right)+\epsilon.

We will now investigate properties of the normalized marginal benefit.

Lemma 3.3
.

\Psi(y)
is a strictly increasing function of
y>0
and

\lim_{y\to\infty}\Psi(y)=\epsilon.

Proof.

It follows from Assumption
1
that

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}(y)=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{y^{2}}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)\right)+\dfrac{1}{y}(-I_{u_{B}}(y)+I_{u_{A}}(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{y^{2}}\left((\tilde{u}_{B}(y)+yI_{u_{B}}(y))-(\tilde{u}_{A}(y)+yI_{u_{A}}(y))\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(u(I_{u_{B}}(y))-u(I_{u_{A}}(y))\right)>0.

Hence,
\Psi(y)
is a strictly increasing function of
y>0
.

(i)

If
\lim_{y\to\infty}|\left(\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)\right)|=\infty
,

it follows form L’Hospital’s rule that

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to\infty}\dfrac{\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)}{y}=\lim_{y\to\infty}\dfrac{-I_{u_{B}}(y)+I_{u_{A}}(y)}{1}=0.

(ii)

If
\lim_{y\to\infty}|\left(\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)\right)|<\infty
,

it is clear that

\displaystyle\lim_{y\to\infty}\dfrac{\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)}{y}=0.

By (i) and (ii), we deduce that

\lim_{y\to\infty}\Psi(y)=\epsilon.

∎

If
\lim_{y\to 0+}\Psi(y)\geq 0
, Lemma
3.3
implies that
\Psi(y)>0
for all
y>0
. Thus, we deduce that for any
\tau\in{\cal S}

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})+{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon\right)dt\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})+{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon\right)dt\right].

That is, the agent does not choose the option to retire in the case when
\lim_{y\to 0+}\Psi(y)\geq 0
, that is, the marginal benefit of work is always positive. Consequently, the necessary condition for the agent to retire at a finite time
\tau
is the following assumption:

Assumption 2
.

\lim_{y\to 0+}\Psi(y)<0.

Under Assumption
2
, Lemma
3.3
implies that there exists a unique
\bar{z}>0
such that

\Psi(\bar{z})=0.

By the standard theory of the optimal stopping problem (
Peskir and Shiryaev, 2006

{\cal P}(y)
satisfies the following variational inequality:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbb{L}{\cal P}+h(y)\leq 0\;\;\;&\mbox{if}\;\;{\cal P}(y)=0,\\
\mathbb{L}{\cal P}+h(y)=0\;\;\;&\mbox{if}\;\;{\cal P}(y)>0,\\
\end{cases}

(24)

where
h(y)\equiv y\Psi(y)=(\tilde{u}_{B}(y)-\tilde{u}_{A}(y)+\epsilon y)
and the differential operator

is given by

\mathbb{L}\equiv\dfrac{\theta^{2}}{2}y^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}}{dy^{2}}+(\rho-r)y\dfrac{d}{dy}-\rho.

Theorem 3.1

(Verification theorem)
.

Suppose that variational inequality (
24
) has a solution
{\cal Q}(y)
which is continuously differentiable in
y>0
and twice continuously differentiable in
(0,\infty)\backslash\{b\}
for some point

and

Then,

(a)

{\cal P}(y)\leq{\cal Q}(y)
.

(b)

If
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[{\cal Q}({\cal Y}_{t})\right]=0
, then

and the optimal solution to the problem in (

\tau_{R}(y)=\inf\{t\geq 0\mid{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\in\{y>0\mid{\cal P}(y)=0\}\}.

Proof.

Even though

is only continuously differentiable, we can still apply Itô’s lemma (see Exercise 6.24 in
Karatzas and Shreve, 1991

Knudsen et al., 1998
and we omit the proof.
∎

From Theorem

\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[{\cal P}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})\right]=0.

Proposition 3.3
.

The utility value of lifetime labor,

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal P}(y)=\sup_{\tau\in{\cal S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\tilde{u}_{B}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})-\tilde{u}_{A}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})+{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}\epsilon\right)dt\right]=\begin{cases}Dy^{n_{2}}+\Xi_{h}(y)\;\;\;&\mbox{for}\;\;y\geq z_{R},\\
0\;\;\;&\mbox{for}\;\;\;0<y\leq z_{R},\end{cases}\end{split}

We first derive the utility value of lifetime labor,

in a heuristic and intuitive manner. Suppose that there exists a boundary

\displaystyle{\cal P}(z_{R})={\cal P}^{\prime}(z_{R})=0,\quad{\cal P}(y)=0\;\;\;\mbox{for}\;\;0<y\leq z_{R}.

\displaystyle\Xi_{h}(y)=\dfrac{2}{\theta^{2}(n_{1}-n_{2})}\left[y^{n_{2}}\int_{0}^{y}\nu^{-n_{2}-1}{h}(\nu)d\nu+y^{n_{1}}\int_{y}^{\infty}\nu^{-n_{1}-1}{h}(\nu)d\nu\right]

Proof.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let us denote
{\cal X}(y)
and

\dfrac{\theta^{2}}{2}y^{2}J_{A}^{\prime\prime}(y)+(\rho-r)yJ_{A}^{\prime}(y)-\rho J_{A}+\tilde{u}_{A}(y)=0.

(31)

For

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\cal X}(y)=\begin{cases}-Dn_{2}y^{n_{2}-1}-\Xi_{\tilde{u}_{B}}^{\prime}(y)-\dfrac{\epsilon}{r}\;\;\;&\mbox{for}\;\;y\geq z_{R},\\
-\Xi_{\tilde{u}_{A}}^{\prime}(y)\;\;\;&\mbox{for}\;\;\;0<y\leq z_{R},\end{cases}\end{split}

it is easy to show that

\|\,V\,\|\,_{\mathcal{W}^{2,p}(0,\infty)}\triangleq\left[\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\left(\,|\,V\,|\,^{p}+|\,\partial_{y}V\,|\,^{p}+|\,\partial_{yy}V\,|\,^{p}\;\right)\,dy\,\right]^{1\over p}.

\displaystyle\begin{split}d{\cal X}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})=&{\cal X}^{\prime}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})d{\cal Y}_{t}^{y}+\dfrac{1}{2}{\cal X}^{\prime\prime}({\cal Y}_{t}^{y})(d{\cal Y}_{t}^{y})^{2}\\

6
Concluding Remarks

We have studied a retirement and consumption/portfolio choice of an individual economic agent. We have identified minimum assumptions for the problem well-defined and the retirement option valuable. We have solved the problem by formulating a Lagrangian which allows us to consider the difference between the dual value functions before retirement and after retirement as the option value of lifetime labor. We have shown that the option value is the present value of the marginal benefit of work compared to retirement. We have shown human wealth is a component in the derivative of the option value with respect to the marginal value of wealth.

In this paper we have assumed a constant investment opportunity facing the agent. Consideration of the model to allow general market environment as in
Yang et al., 2020
would be a useful topic for future research. We have not considered borrowing constraints, which can play an important role for individuals’ choice. The extension incorporating borrowing constraints would also be an interesting topic for future research.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We derive budget constraint (
10
).
For a given
T>0
, we define the equivalent martingale measure
\mathbb{Q}
by

\dfrac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}T-\theta B_{T}}.

(53)

Then,
B_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}}=B_{t}+\theta t,t\in[0,T]
is a standard Brownian motion under measure
\mathbb{Q}
.
Let us consider the process
N_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}(c_{s}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{s<\tau\}})ds+e^{-rt}X_{t}
.

Then, Itô’s lemma implies
N_{t}
is a continuous
\mathbb{Q}
-local martingale, i.e.,

dN_{t}=e^{-rt}\sigma\pi_{t}dB_{t}^{\mathbb{Q}}.

(54)

By constraint (
7
) we know

N_{t}\geq-\epsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{-rs}ds-e^{-rt}\frac{\epsilon}{r}=-\dfrac{\epsilon}{r}.

Since
N_{t}
is bounded below,
N_{t}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-supermartingale. It follows that

x=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[N_{0}]\geq\mathbb{E}^{Q}[N_{T}]

(55)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle x=X_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-rt}(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{-rT}X_{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\xi_{t}(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}})ds\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{T}X_{T}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\xi_{t}(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}})ds\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{T}\frac{\epsilon}{r}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\xi_{t}(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}})ds\right]-e^{-rT}\frac{\epsilon}{r},

(56)

where the second inequality follows from constraint (
7
). By the dominated convergence theorem the first expectation in the right-hand side of (
56
) converges to
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\xi_{t}(c_{t}-\epsilon{\bf 1}_{\{t<\tau\}})dt\right]
. Now budget constraint (
10
) follows from (
56
) by taking the limit as

.

The converse can be obtained by slightly modifying the proof of Theorem 9.4 in
Karatzas and Shreve, 1998
.
