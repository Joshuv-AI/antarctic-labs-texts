Abstract
. For an arbitrary normed space 𝒳 \mathcal{X} over a field 𝔽 ∈ { ℝ , ℂ } \mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\} , we define the directed graph Γ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \Gamma(\mathcal{X}) induced by Birkhoff–James orthogonality on the projective space ℙ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}) , and also its nonprojective counterpart Γ 0 ​ ( 𝒳 ) \Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X}) . We show that, in finite-dimensional normed spaces, Γ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) \Gamma(\mathcal{X}) carries all the information about the dimension, smooth points, and norm’s maximal faces. It also allows to determine whether the norm is a supremum norm or not, and thus classifies finite-dimensional abelian C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebras among other normed spaces. We further establish the necessary and sufficient conditions under which the graph Γ 0 ​ ( ℛ ) \Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{R}) of a (real or complex) Radon plane ℛ \mathcal{R} is isomorphic to the graph Γ 0 ( 𝔽 2 , ∥ ⋅ ∥ 2 ) \Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2}) of the two-dimensional Hilbert space and construct examples of such nonsmooth Radon planes.

1.
Introduction and Preliminaries

How important is a given relation?
The question is intentionally vague and its answer depends very much on the context, i.e, on the choice of objects of study. One can measure the importance of the relation by using graphs: The vertices, typically (though not always), are all the elements of a given object and two vertices are connected by a directed edge if they are related. This approach concentrates on the relation alone and leaves aside how the relation interacts with other structures/operations inside the object. Having built the graphs on two objects within the same category (like the category
Mat
of matrix algebras), one can then examine how close the two objects are if the relation on them behaves in exactly the same way. Technically, if the two graphs are isomorphic, does it imply that the two objects are isomorphic? We call this
an isomorphism problem
and if the answer is yes on objects within some category, then the relation is clearly very important there. For example, it was shown in
[
17
]
that commutativity has exactly this property in the category
{\bf Mat}(\mathbb{C})
of complex matrix algebras. The same relation alone can tell if a given nonabelian finite group is simple and can also distinguish among them
[
1
,
11
,
23
]
, although it is less powerful on general groups
[
7
,
9
]
.

Another measure of the importance of the relation is whether it can classify elements with a particular property inside an object. We call this
property recognition problem
.
Presently we investigate these questions in the category
{\bf Ban}
of finite-dimensional Banach spaces. In this paper we focus our attention on the relation of Birkhoff–James orthogonality.

Given a normed vector space
\mathcal{X}
over the field
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, there are several nonequivalent possibilities to equip it with orthogonality relation. One of the most known and investigated among them is Birkhoff–James orthogonality (BJ-orthogonality for short), denoted in the present paper by
x\perp y
. In the words of Birkhoff
[
4
]
(choosing the origin to be a point which he denoted by
p
, since Birkhoff considered vectors of the form
\overrightarrow{pq}
), it is defined as a condition that no point on the (extended) line containing
0
and
y
is nearer to
x
than
0
. Equivalently,
x\perp y
if

\|x+\lambda y\|\geq\|x\|;\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{F}.

Birkhoff then proved in
[
4
]
that if any given line has at most one perpendicular to any given point outside of this line, then the above relation on a three-dimensional (real) space is symmetric, i.e.,

x\perp y\Longleftrightarrow y\perp x,

if and only if the norm is induced by an inner product. The first condition here is interpreted as follows: let
\ell
be a line passing through some distinct points
x
and
y
, and let
z
be an arbitrary point which does not belong to
\ell
. Then there exists at most one point
w\in\ell
such that
\overrightarrow{wz}=z-w\perp y-x=\overrightarrow{xy}
(this is equivalent to the fact that the norm on
\mathcal{X}
is rotund, i.e., strictly convex).

This orthogonality relation was further developed (again in real spaces) by James in a series of papers
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
; in particular in
[
14
, Theorem 1]
he managed to remove the rotundness condition from Birkhoff’s result by a nice application of Kakutani’s
[
16
, Theorem 3]
classification of inner-product spaces using the norm of a projection. Note that Kakutani’s proof works only in real spaces, however it can be extended without altering its statement to complex normed spaces as well (see
[
6
]
). Thus, by
[
14
, Theorem 1]
, the symmetricity of BJ-orthogonality is equivalent to the fact that the norm is induced by an inner product, for any normed space of dimension at least three and over any field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
.
The interesting part of this result is that it classifies inner-product spaces purely in terms of the BJ-orthogonality relation without using additional operations available in every vector space (additivity and multiplicativity by scalars).

A natural tool to investigate this further (i.e., the information on a normed space encoded by the properties of BJ-orthogonality) is via a graph of the relation. More precisely, to every normed space
\mathcal{X}
we associate a directed graph (i.e, a digraph)
\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
whose vertices are all the elements of
\mathcal{X}
and vertices
x,y
form a directed edge
x\rightarrow y
if
x\perp y
. Observe that
x\perp x
if and only if
x=0
. Therefore,
x=0
is the only vertex with a loop in the graph
\Gamma_{0}
.

Since Birkhoff–James relation is clearly homogeneous in a sense that
x\perp y
if and only if
(\lambda x)\perp(\mu y)
for every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}
, there is another natural digraph,
\Gamma=\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
, associated with
\mathcal{X}
.
Its vertex set equals
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{X}:=\{[x]=\mathbb{F}x;\;\;x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}\}
(i.e., a vertex in
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
is a one-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{X}
), and two vertices
[x],[y]
form a directed edge (denoted
[x]\rightarrow[y]
) if some, hence any, of their representatives
x\in[x]
and
y\in[y]
satisfy
x\perp y
. Observe that
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
has no loops. Observe also that
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
is a quotient graph of the induced subgraph of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
obtained by removing its only loop vertex, under the relation
\mathbb{F}x=\mathbb{F}y
on
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\setminus\{0\}
. We call
\Gamma=\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
an
orthodigraph
in the sequel.

Because an orthodigraph was defined over a projectivization rather than a space itself, it encodes one additional piece of information besides the BJ-orthogonality, namely, linear dependence between two vectors. This subtlety is by no means automatic (see

[
2
, Example 3.9]
) and may on the one hand appear rather restrictive, while, on the other hand, makes our key results valid for general norms.

For a subset
\Omega
we denote by
|\Omega|
its cardinality. If
S
is a subset of a (general) digraph
\hat{\Gamma}
then
x\rightarrow S
will be a shorthand for the statement that
x\rightarrow s
for every
s\in S
, i.e.,
x
forms a directed edge with every element in
S
. Recall that a clique in digraph
\hat{\Gamma}
is a subset of pairwise connected vertices. Given a vertex
x
in a (general) digraph
\hat{\Gamma}
, we let

x^{\bot}:=\{y\in\hat{\Gamma};\;\;x\rightarrow y\}

be its
outgoing neighborhood
, i.e., the set of vertices that have an edge from
x
. Also,

{}^{\bot}x:=\{y\in\hat{\Gamma};\;\;y\rightarrow x\}

denotes its
incoming neighborhood
, i.e., the set of vertices that have an edge to
x
. Notice that for
[x]\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
we have

[x]^{\bot}=\{[y]\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X});\;\;[x]\perp[y]\};

similarly for
x^{\bot}
with
x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
. Actually, since BJ-orthogonality is homogeneous,

(1.1)

\displaystyle[z]\in[x]^{\bot}

\displaystyle\hbox{ if and only if }\quad[z]\subseteq x^{\bot}

\displaystyle(x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\});

\displaystyle z\in x^{\bot}\setminus\{0\}

\displaystyle\hbox{ if and only if }\quad[z]\in[x]^{\bot}

\displaystyle(x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}).

Similar description/connections hold for/between
{}^{\bot}x
and
{}^{\bot}[x]
.

Given a subset of vertices
S\subseteq\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, its span,
\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}S
, is the set of vertices obtained by taking the linear span of
S
in the vector space
\mathcal{X}
.
With our first result we show that the operation of taking the spans is graphological in smooth normed spaces (that is, the span can be computed by studying the connection in the graph alone and, consequently,
\Phi(\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}S)=\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}\Phi(S)
for every bijection
\Phi
which preserves BJ-orthogonality in both directions). We furthermore show that the dimension is also a graphological property in a general normed space.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a normed space over
\mathbb{F}
. Then
\dim\mathcal{X}<\infty
if and only if the clique number of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is finite. If the clique number of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is finite, then

(1.2)

\dim\mathcal{X}=\min\bigg\{|\Omega|;\;\Omega\text{ a finite subset of }\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\hbox{ with }\bigg|\bigcap_{x\in\Omega}x^{\bot}\bigg|=1\bigg\}.

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{X}
is a smooth finite-dimensional space and
S
is a subset of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{span}(S)=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\\
x\rightarrow S\end{subarray}}x^{\bot}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{span}(S))=\dim\mathcal{X}-\min\bigg\{|\Omega|;\;\Omega\subseteq\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\text{ with }\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\Omega\\
x\rightarrow S\end{subarray}}x^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}(S)\bigg\}.

Remark 1.2
.

The proof will show that both formulas for the dimension inside Theorem
1.1
also hold if
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
(which contains exactly one loop vertex) is replaced by
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
(which contains no loop vertices), the only difference is that in (
1.2
) we minimize the cardinality of subsets
\Omega\subseteq\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
with
\left|\bigcap_{x\in\Omega}x^{\bot}\right|=0
. In this light we can define the dimension,
\dim\hat{\Gamma}
, of any digraph
\hat{\Gamma}
, as follows:

(i)

\dim\hat{\Gamma}=\infty
if
\hat{\Gamma}
contains an infinite clique,

(ii)

\dim\hat{\Gamma}
is given by (
1.2
) if
\hat{\Gamma}
has a finite clique number and a loop vertex,

(iii)

\dim\hat{\Gamma}=\min\left\{|\Omega|;\;\Omega\text{ is a finite subset of }\hat{\Gamma}(\mathcal{X})\hbox{ with }\left|\bigcap_{x\in\Omega}x^{\bot}\right|=0\right\}
if
\hat{\Gamma}
is loopless with a finite clique number.

(We follow the convention that
\min\varnothing=\infty
.)

Recall that a nonzero point
x
in a vector space
\mathcal{X}
is smooth if it has a unique supporting functional (a normalized functional
f
is supporting for
x
if
f(x)=\|x\|
). It is easy to see that
x
is smooth if and only if
\lambda x
is smooth for every nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. With this in mind, we call a point
[x]
in a projective space
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{X}
to be smooth if some, hence any, of its representatives is a smooth point in
\mathcal{X}
.

A face of the norm’s unit ball is a convex subset
F
on the norm’s unit sphere such that if
x,y
are different normalized vectors whose open line segment
\{\lambda x+(1-\lambda y);\;0<\lambda<1\}
intersects
F
, then already
x,y\in F
.
We define
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})
to be a face if we can choose a representative for each
[x]\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\{x;\;[x]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X})\}
is a face of
\mathcal{X}
. A face is maximal if it is not properly contained in a bigger face.

Given a (nonzero) point in a normed space, can we determine if it is smooth from BJ-orthogonality relation alone? Such a point corresponds to some vertex in orthodigraph and we can equivalently ask: Can we determine whether this vertex comes from a smooth point by studying only its connections within the digraph? We give an affirmative answer in Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
below but the answer is rather technical (likewise we show in Corollary
2.11
below how to collect all vertices that correspond to a maximal face). We temporarily content ourselves with a slightly less precise corollary of this classification: Every bijection between the projectivizations of normed spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
which strongly preserves BJ-orthogonality (that is, every isomorphism between orthodigraphs) must preserve smooth points and maximal faces. Recall that the dimension of orthodigraph was defined within Remark
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be normed spaces over
\mathbb{F}
. If orthodigraph
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
is finite-dimensional then an orthodigraph isomorphism
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma(\mathcal{Y})
maps smooth points onto smooth points and maps maximal faces onto maximal faces.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{Y}
is finite-dimensional and
\dim\mathcal{X}=\dim\mathcal{Y}
.

Our final main result allows us to distinguish the supremum norm on a finite-dimensional space from any other norm in terms of
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
alone.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
be a finite-dimensional normed space over
\mathbb{F}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a linear bijection
A\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that
\|x\|=\|Ax\|_{\infty}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(ii)

The cardinality of
\{x^{\bot};\;\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X}),\hbox{ and $x$ is smooth}\}
is minimal possible among all the norms on
\mathcal{X}
.

(iii)

|\{x^{\bot};\;\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X}),\hbox{ and $x$ is smooth}\}|=\dim\mathcal{X}
.

This has the following immediate application to orthodigraphs of finite-dimensional commutative
C^{*}
-algebras. It is well-known (
[
5
, II.2.2.4 and II.1.1.3.(2)]
) that any commutative
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
is
*
-isomorphic to
C_{0}(\Omega)
, the
C^{*}
-algebra of all continuous complex functions on a locally compact Hausdorff space
\Omega
vanishing at ‘infinity’, with the norm
\|g\|=\max\{|g(t)|;\;g\in\Omega\}
. If
\mathcal{A}
is finite-dimensional, then
\Omega
is finite, so
\mathcal{A}
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{\infty})
with componentwise multiplication for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, Theorem
1.4
solves the problem of classification of commutative
C^{\ast}
-algebras among general finite-dimensional normed spaces.

We remark that Ryotaro Tanaka
[
26
, Proposition 4.5, Theorem 4.6, and Corollary 4.7]
has recently obtained a similar classification of supremum norm based on topological properties of ultrafilters associated with outgoing neighborhoods of
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
. Moreover, he has obtained
[
27
]
another formula for computing the dimension, which does not directly use (ortho)digraphs as does our formula in (
1.2
), but is again based on topological properties of ultrafilters associated with
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
. We are indebted to Professor Tanaka for sending us his preprint. A different approach to compute the dimension, based on BJ-orthogonality in a projective space, can be found in
[
2
, Lemma 2.3 and Remark 2.4]
.

The proofs of our main results will be given in Section
2
. We now summarize the rest of the results of our paper where we study how far Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
can be extended from the projective orthodigraph,
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
, to its nonprojective counterpart,
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
. For this purpose, in Section
3
, we introduce the class of BJ-normed spaces. In particular, if the norm on
\mathcal{X}
is rotund or smooth, then
\mathcal{X}
is BJ-normed. However, as Example
4.4
shows, the converse is not true. By Corollaries
3.5
and
3.6
, in BJ-normed spaces smooth points can be distinguished from the nonsmooth ones even in the nonprojective setting, so Theorem
1.3
remains valid for an isomorphism
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
. Besides, as shown in Lemma
3.7
, if
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are BJ-normed spaces, then any isomorphism between
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
induces an isomorphism between
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma(\mathcal{Y})
.

However, these results are no longer valid for arbitrary normed spaces. Recall that a two-dimensional normed space where BJ-othogonality is symmetric is called a Radon plane. In Lemmas
3.11
and
3.12
, we establish the necessary and sufficient conditions under which the graph
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{R})
of a (real or complex) Radon plane
\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to the graph
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
of the two-dimensional Hilbert space. In Examples
3.13
and
3.17
, we construct a nonsmooth Radon plane
\mathcal{R}
over
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
such that
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{R})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
are isomorphic. Notice, in contrast, that for a nonsmooth Radon plane
\mathcal{R}
, Theorem
1.3
implies that
\Gamma(\mathcal{R})
and
\Gamma(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
are not isomorphic, since the norm on
(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
is smooth. Next, in Example
3.19
, we show that the assumption about a BJ-normed space is essential in Corollary
3.6
. In Section
4
, we end with some related results and concluding remarks.

2.
Proofs of main results

It will be beneficial to utilize the norm’s subdifferential at a vector
x
, denoted by
\partial\|x\|
(see
[
19
, Definition 1.9]
and
[
10
, Definition 1.2.4]
), which is defined as

\partial\|x\|=\{f\in\mathcal{X}^{*};\;\|y\|-\|x\|\geq\mathrm{Re}\left(f(y-x)\right)\text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X}\}.

We note that the subdifferential set at a nonzero vector
x
coincides with the set of all supporting functionals at
x
(see
[
19
, Lemma 5.10]
and
[
10
, Example 1.2.16]
). By
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
(which is stated for real spaces but the proof works for complex ones as well), we have
x\perp y
if and only if there exists a supporting functional
f
at
x
which annihilates
y
. Hence

(2.1)

x^{\bot}=\bigcup_{f\in\partial\|x\|}\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits f.

Recall that, if
\mathcal{X}
is a complex normed space with
\dim\mathcal{X}=n
, by restriction of scalars we may regard it as a
2n
-dimensional real normed space, which, to avoid the confusion, we denote as
(\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathbb{R}})
. If
\mathcal{X}
is a real normed space, then we set
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathcal{X}
. Then a face
F
of the norm’s unit ball in
\mathcal{X}
is called
exposed
if

F=\{x\in\mathcal{X};\;\;\|x\|=1\hbox{ and }f(x)=\|f\|\}

for some
\mathbb{R}
-linear functional
f
on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
f\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
, the dual space of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a normed space over
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
. The face
F
of the norm’s unit ball in
\mathcal{X}
is exposed if and only if

(2.2)

F=\{x\in\mathcal{X};\;\;\|x\|=1\hbox{ and }f(x)=\|f\|\}

for some
\mathbb{F}
-linear functional
f
on
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,
f\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, then the statement is immediate from the definition of an exposed face, so let
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. Assume first that
F
is an exposed face, and let
f
be the corresponding
\mathbb{R}
-linear functional. Then it is a real part of a
\mathbb{C}
-linear functional
\widetilde{f}(x):=f(x)-if(ix)
which has the same norm as
f
. For any
x\in F
, we have
\|f\|=\|\widetilde{f}\|\geq|\widetilde{f}(x)|=|f(x)-if(ix)|=\sqrt{|f(x)|^{2}+|f(ix)|^{2}}\geq|f(x)|=\|f\|
, so
f(ix)=0
and
\widetilde{f}(x)=f(x)=\|\widetilde{f}\|
. Thus
F=\{x\in\mathcal{X};\;\|x\|=1\hbox{ and }\widetilde{f}(x)=\|\widetilde{f}\|\}.

Conversely, let
F=\{x\in\mathcal{X};\;\|x\|=1\hbox{ and }f(x)=\|f\|\}
for some
\mathbb{C}
-linear functional
f
on
\mathcal{X}
. By
[
22
, p. 58]
, its real part
(\mathrm{Re}\,f)
is an
\mathbb{R}
-linear functional which satisfies
\|(\mathrm{Re}\,f)\|=\|f\|
. Therefore,
F=\{x\in\mathcal{X};\;\|x\|=1\hbox{ and }(\mathrm{Re}\,f)(x)=\|(\mathrm{Re}\,f)\|\}
, so
F
is an exposed face.
∎

Recall that an
exposed point
of the norm’s unit ball is a singleton exposed face. Note that, for an exposed point
x
, the functional
f
in (
2.2
) may not be unique, so there might exist two distinct functionals
f
and
g
of unit norm such that

\{x\}=\{x\in\mathcal{X};\;\;\|x\|=1\hbox{ and }f(x)=g(x)=1\}.

The smooth points of a normed space are related to the exposed points of the dual space as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
be a finite-dimensional normed space over
\mathbb{F}
. The set of supporting functionals of smooth points in
\mathcal{X}
coincides with the set
\mathop{\mathrm{Exp}}\nolimits(\mathcal{X}^{\ast})
of exposed points in the dual norm.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
be a smooth point and
f
be its unique supporting functional. Let us consider the evaluation functional
\varphi_{x}
defined on
\mathcal{X}^{*}
by
\varphi_{x}(g)=g(x)
. Note that, for
g
of norm one, we have
\varphi_{x}(g)=1
if and only
g
is a supporting functional of
x
. Since
f
is the unique supporting functional of
x
, we have

\{f\}=\{g\in\mathcal{X}^{*};\;\|g\|=\varphi_{x}(g)=1\}.

So,
f
is an exposed point of
\mathcal{X}^{*}
.

Conversely, let
f
be an exposed point of
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. Then there exists a functional
\varphi
on
\mathcal{X}^{*}
of norm one such that
\{f\}=\{g\in\mathcal{X}^{*};\;\|g\|=\varphi(g)=1\}.
Since
\mathcal{X}
is reflexive, there exists
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\varphi=\varphi_{x}
. It means

\{f\}=\{g\in\mathcal{X}^{*};\;\|g\|=\varphi_{x}(g)=g(x)=1\}.

So,
f
is the unique supporting functional for
x
. Hence,
x
is a smooth point whose supporting functional is
f
.
∎

We note that, in any finite-dimensional normed space
\mathcal{X}
over
\mathbb{F}
, the convex span of its exposed points is dense in its closed unit ball. For
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, the proof can be found in
[
20
, Theorem 18.7]
. For the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, we note that the set of exposed points in
\mathcal{X}
coincides with the set of exposed points in
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{R}}
, and then the real case can be applied.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
be a finite-dimensional normed space over
\mathbb{F}
. Then
\mathcal{X}
has at least
n=\dim\mathcal{X}
smooth points whose supporting functionals are linearly independent.

Proof.

As shown above, the convex span of
\mathop{\mathrm{Exp}}\nolimits(\mathcal{X}^{\ast})
, the set of all exposed points in a dual space, is dense in the closed unit ball. So
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
has a basis consisting of exposed points
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Exp}}\nolimits(\mathcal{X}^{\ast})
. By Lemma
2.2
, these are the supporting functionals of some smooth points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Note that this has no counterpart in infinite-dimensional spaces. In fact, the Banach space
\ell_{1}(\Lambda)

Corollary 2.5

Let

Corollary 2.6

If

contains infinitely many pairwise BJ-orthogonal vectors, then

However, as we show at the end of this section,
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
being isomorphic does not always imply that
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma(\mathcal{Y})
are isomorphic. We now introduce a class of norms, where the converse of the above lemma is true. Within the introduced class we could easily adapt Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
to the setup of orthodigraph
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
of the original normed space
\mathcal{X}
rather than its projectivization,
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that the elements
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
are
BJ-equivalent
(denoted by
x\mathbin{\sim_{BJ}}y
) if
x^{\bot}=y^{\bot}
and
{}^{\bot}x={}^{\bot}y
.

Since BJ-orthogonality is homogeneous, every two nonzero points in
\mathbb{F}x
are BJ-equivalent. However, the converse is not always true: in
(\mathbb{R}^{3},{\|\cdot\|}_{\infty})
the points
(1,1/2,0)
and
(1,1/3,0)
are clearly linearly independent, but still are BJ-equivalent (see
[
2
, Example 3.9]
).

Definition 3.3
.

A norm is called a
BJ-norm
if BJ-equivalent elements are always linearly dependent.

Observe that, in a BJ-normed space
\mathcal{X}
, the equivalence classes induced by BJ-equivalency relation are
\{0\}
and points in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If a norm is rotund or smooth, then it is a BJ-norm.

Proof.

Assume first that
\mathcal{X}
has a rotund norm. Take any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
with
x^{\bot}=y^{\bot}
. From (
1.1
), it is straightforward that this is equivalent to
[x]^{\bot}=[y]^{\bot}
, which, by
[
2
, Lemma 2.6]
, implies
[x]=[y]
, as claimed.

Assume now that
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
is a smooth normed space. Take two linearly independent
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
and choose a norm-attaining functional
f
with
f(x)\neq 0
and
f(y)=0
(it exists by Hahn-Banach theorem). Without loss of generality we may assume that
\|f\|=1
.
Choose
z\in\mathcal{X}
such that
f
is a supporting functional at
z
. Then
z\in{}^{\bot}y
. But since
{\|\cdot\|}
is smooth norm,
f
is the unique supporting functional at
z
and
f(x)\neq 0
, so
z\notin{}^{\bot}x
. Hence,
{}^{\bot}x\neq{}^{\bot}y
.
∎

As we will show in the last section, the class of BJ-norms is broader and contains not only smooth or strictly convex norms. However, as we have already mentioned, not every norm is a BJ-norm.

For the class of BJ-normed spaces
\mathcal{X}
, we can easily see that smooth points can be described not only in terms of the projective orthodigraph
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
but also from the digraph
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
whose vertex set consists of all vectors in
\mathcal{X}
.

Recall that
0
is the only vertex in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
with a loop.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a two-dimensional BJ-normed space over
\mathbb{F}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent for
x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
:

(i)

x
corresponds to a smooth point in
\mathcal{X}
.

(ii)

x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
has no loops and every two elements of
x^{\bot}
are BJ-equivalent.

Proof.

Immediately follows from Lemma
2.7
and Definition
3.3
of a BJ-norm.
∎

We call a subset
\Omega\subseteq\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
to be a
BJ-set
if any two vertices in
\Omega
are BJ-equivalent. In other words,
\Omega
is completely contained in a single BJ-equivalence class.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite-dimensional BJ-normed space over
\mathbb{F}
with
n=\dim\mathcal{X}\geq 3
. Then the following conditions are equivalent for
x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
:

(i)

x
corresponds to a smooth point in
\mathcal{X}
.

(ii)

x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
has no loop and there exist
(n-2)
loopless vertices
x_{3},\dots,x_{n}
in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
such that

x^{\bot}\cap x_{3}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap x_{n}^{\bot}
is a BJ-set.
Moreover, for every reordering of this
(n-2)
-tuple of vertices we can define

\Omega:=\{y\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X});\;\;x^{\bot}\cap y^{\bot}\cap x_{4}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap x_{n}^{\bot}\hbox{ is a BJ-set}\},

and then
x_{4}^{\bot}\cap\dots\cap x_{n}^{\bot}\cap x^{\bot}\cap\bigcap\limits_{y\in\Omega}y^{\bot}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Follows from Lemma
2.8
and Definition
3.3
of a BJ-norm.
∎

The following statement is immediate from the above two corollaries: if
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are BJ-normed spaces over
\mathbb{F}
with
\dim\mathcal{X}<\infty
, then every digraph isomorphism
\Phi\colon\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\to\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
maps smooth points onto smooth points.

Next, we prove that the converse of Lemma
3.1
holds for BJ-normed spaces.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be BJ-normed spaces over
\mathbb{F}
. If
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
are isomorphic, then
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma(\mathcal{Y})
are also isomorphic.

Proof.

Let
\Phi\colon\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
be an isomorphism. For every projective point
\ell\in\mathbb{P}\mathcal{X}
choose its representative
\ell^{\prime}\in\ell
. Since
0\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is the only vertex with a loop, we clearly have
\Phi^{-1}(0)=\{0\}
. Therefore,

\Psi(\ell):=[\Phi(\ell^{\prime})]

is a well-defined map from
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
to
\Gamma(\mathcal{Y})
. Clearly,
\Psi
is a strong graph homomorphism. Since
\mathcal{X}
is a BJ-normed space, for all
x\neq 0
it holds that

\mathbb{F}x=\{y\in\mathcal{X};\;y^{\bot}=x^{\bot}\text{ and }{}^{\bot}y={}^{\bot}x\}.

Since
\mathcal{Y}
is also a BJ-normed space and
\Phi
is an isomorphism, we have

\Phi(\mathbb{F}x)=\mathbb{F}\Phi(x).

Then injectivity and surjectivity of the induced map
\Psi
are straightforward.
∎

Furthermore, in Examples
3.13
and

we construct a nonsmooth two-dimensional normed space
\mathcal{X}
over

,
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
and

.

By
[
14
, Theorem 1]
, if
\dim\mathcal{X}\geq 3

is symmetric if and only if the norm on
\mathcal{X}
is induced by the inner product. However, this result does not hold for
\dim\mathcal{X}=2

. Their first examples are due to Birkhoff
[
4
]
and James
[
14
, p. 561]

Radon planes which was obtained by Day
[
8
]
. We denote
x\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}y

and

Remark 3.8

(
[
8
, pp. 330–333]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a two-dimensional real normed space. Then BJ-orthogonality in
\mathcal{X}
is symmetric if and only if modulo a linear transformation its unit sphere
{\bf S}_{\mathcal{X}}
can be obtained by the following procedure:

(i)

Choose (auxiliary) any two-dimensional real normed space
\mathcal{Y}
.

(ii)

Find any two normalized vectors
x,y\in\mathcal{Y}
with
x\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}y
(they always exist by
[
28
, Theorem 2]
).

(iii)

Find supporting functionals
f_{x},f_{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
with
f_{x}(x)=f_{y}(y)=1
and
f_{x}(y)=f_{y}(x)=0
.

(iv)

Choose a coordinate system in
\mathcal{Y}
with
x
and
y
at
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
, correspondingly. Similarly, choose a coordinate system in
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
with
f_{x}
and
f_{y}
at
(0,1)
and
(-1,0)
.

(v)

Set the first and the third quadrants of
{\bf S}_{\mathcal{X}}
equal to the first and the third quadrants of
{\bf S}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, set the second and the fourth quadrants of
{\bf S}_{\mathcal{X}}
equal to the second and the fourth quadrants of
{\bf S}_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}
.

Tanaka showed in
[
24
, Theorem 4.7]
that orthodigraphs of real smooth Radon planes are isomorphic to the orthodigraph of the Euclidean plane. We now extend his result. We will rely on the following simple lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a nonconstant continuous function and let
g\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a function whose image is at most countable. Then the image of
f+g
contains continuum many points.

Proof.

The image of the continuous function
f
is a nondegenerate interval. Let
(x_{t})_{t}\in[a,b]
be continuum many points which
f
maps bijectively onto
\Xi:=(f(x_{t}))_{t}
. Let
\Omega=g([a,b])
be at most countable set. Then the same is true for

\Omega^{\prime}:=\Omega-\Omega=\bigcup_{y\in\Omega}(\Omega-y),

and the set
\Xi
is partitioned into continuum many equivalence classes under the equivalence relation
f(x_{t})\simeq f(x_{s})
if
f(x_{t})-f(x_{s})\in\Omega^{\prime}
. The
f
-preimages of these equivalence classes partition the set
(x_{t})_{t}
into continuum many disjoint sets. And
f+g
is injective on the representatives because if
(f+g)(x^{\prime}_{t})=(f+g)(x^{\prime}_{s})
for two representatives of distinct preimages, then
f(x^{\prime}_{t})-f(x^{\prime}_{s})=g(x^{\prime}_{s})-g(x^{\prime}_{t})\in\Omega-\Omega=\Omega^{\prime}
, which contradicts the fact that
f(x^{\prime}_{t})
and
f(x^{\prime}_{s})
are not equivalent.
∎

For brevity, we will refer to the real affine dimension of a norm’s face
F
as simply
the dimension of
F
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}:=(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
be a Radon plane over
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, and
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
be a point in the norm’s unit sphere. Then the following statements hold.

(i)

There exists a norm’s face
F_{x}
such that

(3.1)

x^{\bot}=\mathbb{F}F_{x}.

The dimension of
F_{x}
equals
0
if and only if
x
is a smooth point.

(ii)

If, moreover, the boundary points of each nonzero-dimensional face in
{\mathcal{R}}
are smooth, then:

(a)

If
x
is nonsmooth, then
z^{\bot}=\mathbb{F}x
for any
z\in F_{x}
. Consequently, each point in
F_{x}
is BJ-orthogonal only to
\mathbb{F}x
, and
x
is BJ-orthogonal only to
\mathbb{F}F_{x}
.

(b)

Given an arbitrary nonzero-dimensional norm’s face
F
in
{\mathcal{R}}
, we have
\mathbb{F}F\subseteq\mathbb{F}F_{x}
for some nonsmooth normalized point
x\in{\mathcal{R}}
.

Proof.

(i). Assume first that
x
is nonsmooth. Then it has at least two
\mathbb{F}
-linear supporting functionals, and their kernels are distinct one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{2}
. So, by
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
, there exist at least two normalized linearly independent vectors

y_{1},y_{2}\in x^{\bot},

and since
{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}}
is a Radon norm, we also have
x\in y_{1}^{\bot}\cap y_{2}^{\bot}
.
Then, again by
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
,
there exist supporting functionals
f_{1},f_{2}\colon\mathbb{F}^{2}\to\mathbb{F}
of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, respectively, such that

(3.2)

f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(x)=0.

Then their kernels are the same, and hence
f_{1},f_{2}
must be linearly dependent. Since they are normalized,
f_{2}=\mu f_{1}
for some unimodular number
\mu
. By replacing
y_{2}
with
\mu y_{2}\in x^{\bot}
, we achieve that
y_{1},(\mu y_{2})
both share the same supporting functional
f:=f_{1}=f_{2}
. Following along the same lines shows that for any normalized
z\in x^{\bot}
there exists a unimodular number
\mu_{z}
such that
f
is a supporting functional of
\mu_{z}z
.
It is straightforward that then there exists a convex set

(3.3)

F_{x}:=f^{-1}(1)\cap{\bf B}_{\mathcal{R}}

(where
{\bf B}_{\mathcal{R}}
is the norm’s closed unit ball) such that
x^{\bot}\subseteq\mathbb{F}F_{x}
.
Conversely, if
z\in F_{x}
, then
f
is its supporting functional which, by (
3.2
), annihilates
x
, so
z\perp x
, and since
\mathcal{R}
is a Radon plane, this implies
x\perp z
, that is,
z\in x^{\bot}
. Thus
x^{\bot}=\mathbb{F}F_{x}
. Note that, by (
3.3
) and Lemma
2.1
, the set
F_{x}
is actually a norm’s (exposed) face.

Clearly, (
3.1
) holds also for a smooth point
x
, if we let
F_{x}
to be the norm-one representative in the one-dimensional kernel of the supporting functional at
x
.

(ii). We first prove item (b). It is not hard to see that each point
z
in the relative interior of
F
is smooth: namely,
z
is the middle point of some nontrivial line segment

[y_{1},y_{2}]\subseteq F,

and then, if
f
is any
\mathbb{F}
-linear supporting functional for
z=\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}
, it follows that

1=f(z)=\frac{f(y_{1})+f(y_{2})}{2}.

Since
\|f\|=\|y_{1}\|=\|y_{2}\|=1
, we have
|f(y_{1})|,|f(y_{2})|\leq 1
, and then the triangle inequality implies that

(3.4)

f(y_{1})=f(y_{2})=1.

Notice also that
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
must be
\mathbb{F}
-linearly independent because otherwise
y_{2}=\mu y_{1}
for some unimodular
\mu\neq 1
, and then
\|z\|=\left\|\frac{y_{1}+y_{2}}{2}\right\|=\frac{|1+\mu|}{2}<1
, a contradiction. Therefore, they form a basis for
\mathbb{F}^{2}
. It then follows from (
3.4
) that the supporting functional at
z
is unique, so
z
is smooth.
By the hypothesis, the boundary points of
F
are also smooth. Then, by (ii)
\Longrightarrow
(i) of Lemma
2.9
(whose proof can be easily adapted to work also in nonprojective setting), for every
z,w\in F
we have
z^{\bot}=w^{\bot}
, and it is a one-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{2}
(spanned by, say,
x
), so

(3.5)

\bigcup_{z\in F}z^{\bot}=\mathbb{F}x.

Since
{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}}
is a Radon norm, together with (
3.1
) this gives us that
\mathbb{F}F\subseteq\mathbb{F}F_{x}
.

To prove (a), note that, for a nonsmooth point
x
, the dimension of
F_{x}
is greater than
0
, so we can apply (
3.5
) to
F=F_{x}
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}:=(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
be a real Radon plane and let
\|(x,y)\|_{2}:=\sqrt{|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}}
be the Euclidean norm. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
.

(ii)

Both boundary points of each one-dimensional face in
{\mathcal{R}}
are smooth.

Proof.

(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(i).
If
{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}}
is smooth, then this follows by Tanaka’s
[
24
, Theorem 4.7]
. Otherwise, by Lemma
3.10
, for each nonsmooth point
x
there exists a one-dimensional norm’s face
F_{x}
such that each point in
F_{x}
is BJ-orthogonal only to
\mathbb{R}x
, and
x
is BJ-orthogonal only to
\mathbb{R}F_{x}
. Conversely, each point of a one-dimensional norm’s face
F
is BJ-orthogonal to a unique one-dimensional subspace spanned by a nonsmooth point. There are at most countably many nonsmooth points, and hence also the same cardinality of maximal one-dimensional faces; they can be paired into mutually BJ-orthogonal pairs
(x,F_{x})
, so that no other nonzero point in
\mathcal{R}
is in BJ-orthogonality relation to any nonzero point in
\mathbb{R}x\cup\mathbb{R}F_{x}
.

We next show that there are continuum many smooth points in the norm’s unit sphere, which do not belong to a one-dimensional face. To see this, note that the norm’s unit sphere is not a square, so its portion can be parametrized as
(x,f(x))

for some nonconstant convex function

f\colon(\alpha,\beta)\to\mathbb{R}
.
It is a classical result
[
20
, Theorems 23.1 and 24.1]
that
f(x)
has left,
f^{\prime}_{-}(x)
, and right,
f^{\prime}_{+}(x)
, derivative at every point of its domain, they monotonically increase and coincide except possibly at countably many points. The graph of its subdifferential,

{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}:=\{(x,x^{\ast})\in\mathbb{R}^{2};\;x^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R},\;f^{\prime}_{-}(x)\leq x^{\ast}\leq f^{\prime}_{+}(x)\},

is a continuous curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which may contain horizontal and vertical line segments. Moreover,

(x,x^{\ast})\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}\quad\hbox{ if and only if }\quad(x,f(x))\perp(1,x^{\ast}).

Indeed, if
(x,f(x))
is a smooth point, then its supporting functional is given by norm’s gradient at this point, which is perpendicular to the tangent vector of the level curve parametrized by
x\mapsto(x,f(x))
. If
(x,f(x))
is a nonsmooth point, then, by using
[
20
, Theorem 25.6]
, we obtain that its subdifferential is the convex hull of all possible limits of gradients at smooth points on
{\bf S}_{\mathcal{R}}
converging to
(x,f(x))
.
It follows that each vertical segment in
{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}
corresponds to a point in
(x,f(x))^{\bot}
for a fixed nonsmooth point

x\in(\alpha,\beta)
, and it also follows that two points
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\ast}),(x_{2},x_{2}^{\ast})\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}
do not belong to the same horizontal/vertical line segment if and only if
(x_{1},f(x_{1}))^{\bot}\neq(x_{2},f(x_{2}))^{\bot}
.

We may clearly assume that
f^{\prime}
is defined also at points where
f
is not differentiable, by requiring that
x\mapsto f^{\prime}(x)
is right continuous. Since it is increasing, a quick application of Stieltjes integral shows that there exists a finite positive measure
\mu
, supported on the interval
(a,b)
, such that
f^{\prime}(x)=\mu((a,x])
is its cumulative distribution function. Consider a purely atomic measure
\mu_{p}
, supported on the at most countable set of nonsmooth points

x_{1},x_{2},\dots\in(a,b)
and defined by

\mu_{s}(\{x_{i}\}):=f^{\prime}_{+}(x_{i})-f^{\prime}_{-}(x_{i}),

i.e., the length of the vertical line segment of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}
at
X_{i}
.

Notice that
\mu-\mu_{p}
is a continuous measure. It cannot be zero, because then
f^{\prime}
would be a cumulative distribution function of a purely atomic measure
\mu_{p}
, and as such
{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}
would consist only of horizontal and vertical line segments, so that the end points of one-dimensional norm’s faces (which correspond to horizontal line segments of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\partial f}
) would be nonsmooth, a contradiction. Then
f^{\prime}
is a sum of a nonconstant continuous increasing function
g\colon x\mapsto(\mu-\mu_{p})((a,x])
and an increasing function which is constant except for jumps at
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
. Thus, by Lemma
3.9
,
f^{\prime}(x)
attains continuum many different values. By the above, this translates to the fact that the norm’s unit sphere contain continuum many smooth points
x
with
x^{\bot}
pairwise distinct.

We can now construct the graph isomorphism
\Phi\colon\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})\to\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
as follows: Let
{\mathcal{S}F}
be a collection of mutual BJ-orthogonal pairs
(\mathbb{R}{x_{i}},\mathbb{R}F_{x_{i}})
, where
x_{i}
is a nonsmooth point on the norm’s unit sphere, and
F_{x_{i}}
is the corresponding face. It contains at most countably many pairs. With each such pair we associate a pair of orthogonal points
((c_{i},s_{i}),(-s_{i},c_{i}))\in(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
. By the last paragraph,
(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
still contains continuum many mutual BJ-orthogonal pairs
(\mathbb{F}x,\mathbb{F}x^{\prime})
consisting of smooth points. Then there exist bijections
\varphi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}F_{x_{i}}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, and also a bijection
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i}(\mathbb{R}x_{i}\cup\mathbb{R}F_{x_{i}})
onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\bigcup_{i}(\mathbb{R}(c_{i},s_{i})\cup\mathbb{R}(-s_{i},c_{i}))
which maps smooth mutual BJ-orthogonal pairs onto orthogonal pairs. Hence the mapping
\Phi:(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})\to(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
defined by

(i)

\Phi(0)=0
,

(ii)

\Phi(\lambda x_{i})=\lambda(c_{i},s_{i})
and
\Phi(\lambda z)=\varphi_{i}(\lambda z)(-s_{i},c_{i})
for
z\in F_{x_{i}}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

(iii)

\Phi(x)=\varphi(x)
if
x\notin\bigcup_{i}(\mathbb{R}x_{i}\cup\mathbb{R}F_{x_{i}})

is a bijection which preserves BJ-orthogonality in both directions, i.e., it is a graph isomorphism between
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
.

\neg
(ii)
\Longrightarrow

\neg
(i). Suppose that
F=[x_{1},x_{2}]
is a one-dimensional norm’s face with a nonsmooth end point
x_{1}
. Then
x_{1}^{\bot}
properly contains
y^{\bot}
, where
y=\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}}{2}
is the midpoint of
F
. Notice that such property cannot take place in Hilbert spaces, so there can be no graph isomorphism between
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
.
∎

We have a similar classification for complex Radon planes:

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{R}}:=(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
is a complex Radon plane and let
(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
be a Hilbert space with the norm

(i)

\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
.

(ii)

(a) The boundary points of each nonzero-dimensional face in
{\mathcal{R}}
are smooth, and

(b) the set
\{x^{\bot};\;\;x\in\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})\}
has continuum many points.

Proof.

(ii)
\Longrightarrow

By Lemma

, for any normalized point

there exists a norm’s face

such that

is zero-dimensional if and only if

is a smooth point. Besides, if

is BJ-orthogonal only to
\mathbb{C}x
, and

is BJ-orthogonal only to the points in
\mathbb{C}F_{x}
. Moreover, if

is a norm’s face of dimension greater than

\mathbb{C}F\subseteq\mathbb{C}F_{x}
for some nonsmooth normalized point

decomposes into a union

\mathbb{C}^{2}=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{x}(\mathbb{C}^{\ast}x\cup\mathbb{C}^{\ast}F_{x});\qquad\mathbb{C}^{\ast}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}

where the union runs over all
{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}}

\mathbb{C}^{\ast}x
and in
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}F_{x}
are mutually BJ-orthogonal, but are BJ-orthogonal to no other nonzero point and no other nonzero point is BJ-orthogonal to a point in

(\mathbb{C}^{\ast}x\cup\mathbb{C}^{\ast}F_{x})
and
(\mathbb{C}^{\ast}y\cup\mathbb{C}^{\ast}F_{y})
are equal or they are disjoint.

Also, by part (b) of assumption (ii)
there are continuum many such pairs.
We can now construct a graph isomorphism
\Phi\colon\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})\to\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
as follows. We partition the two-dimensional Hilbert space into continuum many pairwise disjoint pairs, consisting of mutually orthogonal vectors, by the formula

\mathbb{C}^{2}=\{0\}\cup\bigcup_{0\leq t,\theta<\pi/2}\Bigl(\mathbb{C}^{\ast}(\cos t,e^{4\theta i}\sin t)\cup\mathbb{C}^{\ast}(-e^{-4\theta i}\sin t,\cos t)\Bigr).

With each of the continuum many pairwise distinct pairs

that constitute the union in (

\Phi:(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})

(i)

\Phi(0)=0

(ii)

\Phi(\lambda x)=\lambda(\cos t_{x},e^{4\theta_{x}i}\sin t_{x})
and
\Phi(\lambda z)=\varphi_{x}(\lambda z)(-e^{-4\theta_{x}i}\sin t_{x},\cos t_{x})

\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{C}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})

\neg
(ii)
\Longrightarrow

\neg
(i). Clearly, the orthogonality graph of the two-dimensional Hilbert space contains continuum many different outgoing neighborhoods. Namely, if
c,s
are real numbers with
c>0

are pairwise distinct. In addition, every isomorphism

An example of a real Radon plane
\mathcal{R}
whose orthodigraph,

Example 3.13
.

Consider an arbitrary normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|})

(ii)

(iii)

it passes through
\pm(1,0)

(iv)

, and thus

(even when it is a smooth point of

the rightmost point of the horizontal line segment

in the first quadrant of the unit sphere (if

is also extended to the second quadrant).

Assume that there is a nonsmooth point

and

and

and

of the unit sphere;

We denote

(ii)

(iii)

\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{*}I)=\mathbb{R}^{*}(0,1)

.

Therefore,

is a graph isomorphism between
\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathcal{R}})
and

Example 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an absolute nonsmooth two-dimensional real normed space whose unit sphere is given by Figure

for some unknown decreasing smooth function
\eta(\xi)
and unknown value
0<\xi_{0}<1
. We now write down a system of equations which guarantees that
\mathcal{X}
is a Radon plane and present one of its solutions.

Consider any vector on the unit sphere
v=(\xi,\eta(\xi))
,
\xi_{0}<\xi<1
. Recall that the supporting functional at
v
equals the norm’s gradient at
v
which is always perpendicular to the tangent vector of the norm’s unit sphere. Therefore,
v^{\bot}=\mathbb{R}(1,\eta^{\prime}(\xi))
, so
v
is BJ-orthogonal to a unit vector
u=(\alpha(\xi),\alpha(\xi)\eta^{\prime}(\xi))
,
\xi_{0}<\alpha(\xi)<1
, which belongs to the fourth quadrant. Since BJ-orthogonality on any Radon plane is symmetric and
u
is a smooth point of the norm, we have
u^{\bot}=\mathbb{R}v
, so the symmetry of our Radon plane with respect to the
x
-axis implies that
\alpha(\alpha(\xi))=\xi
. By the continuity,
\alpha
satisfies the boundary conditions
\alpha(\xi_{0})=1
and
\alpha(1)=\xi_{0}
. For instance, we can take
\alpha(\xi)=\xi_{0}/\xi
.

Since
u
is a unit vector from the fourth quadrant, we have

(3.8)

\eta(\alpha(\xi))=-\alpha(\xi)\eta^{\prime}(\xi).

Then
\alpha(\alpha(\xi))=\xi
implies that

(3.9)

\eta(\xi)=-\xi\eta^{\prime}(\alpha(\xi)).

We now differentiate (
3.8
), multiply it by
\xi
and substitute (
3.9
) to obtain

(3.10)

\xi\alpha(\xi)\eta^{\prime\prime}(\xi)+\alpha^{\prime}(\xi)(\xi\eta^{\prime}(\xi)-\eta(\xi))=0.

By substituting
\alpha(\xi)=\xi_{0}/\xi
and multiplying the resulting equation by
\xi^{2}/\xi_{0}
, we get

(3.11)

\xi^{2}\eta^{\prime\prime}(\xi)-\xi\eta^{\prime}(\xi)+\eta(\xi)=0.

Its general solution is
\eta(\xi)=c_{1}\xi+c_{2}\xi\ln\xi
,
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. But
\eta(1)=0
, so
c_{1}=0
. Since
\eta^{\prime}(\xi_{0})=0
, we then have
\xi_{0}=1/e
, and
\eta(\xi_{0})=1
implies that
c_{2}=-e
. Thus
\eta(\xi)=-e\,\xi\ln\xi
. One can verify that
\eta(\xi)
indeed satisfies (
3.8
).

A two-dimensional normed space

(
[

Let

Example 3.17
.

Let

By

\displaystyle|\widehat{(-d,c)}(a,b)|

\mathop{\mathrm{Arg}}\nolimits(-da)=\mathop{\mathrm{Arg}}\nolimits(cb)

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be normed spaces over
\mathbb{F}
, and
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\oplus_{2}\mathcal{Y}
with
\|(x,y)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}=\sqrt{\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}
. Then for any nonzero
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have

\displaystyle(x,0)^{\bot}

\displaystyle=x^{\bot}\oplus\mathcal{Y},

\displaystyle{}^{\bot}(x,0)

\displaystyle={}^{\bot}x\oplus\mathcal{Y}.

Proof.

Let us denote the norm’s directional derivatives at
u
in direction of
v
by

D_{-}(u;v):=\lim_{t\nearrow 0}\frac{\|u+tv\|-\|u\|}{t}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad D_{+}(u;v):=\lim_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\|u+tv\|-\|u\|}{t}.

By
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
, we have
u\perp_{\mathbb{R}}v
if and only if
D_{-}(u;v)\leq 0\leq D_{+}(u;v)
. Now note that, by continuity of the norm,

\displaystyle D_{\pm}((x,0);(y,z))

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\pm 0}\frac{\|(x+ty,tz)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}-\|(x,0)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\pm 0}\frac{\sqrt{\|x+ty\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\|tz\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}-\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\pm 0}\frac{\|x+ty\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+t^{2}\|z\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}{t\left(\sqrt{\|x+ty\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\|tz\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}+\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\pm 0}\frac{(\|x+ty\|_{\mathcal{X}}-\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}})(\|x+ty\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}})}{2t\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\pm 0}\frac{\|x+ty\|_{\mathcal{X}}-\|x\|_{\mathcal{X}}}{t}=D_{\pm}(x;y).

Hence
(x,0)\perp_{\mathbb{R}}(y,z)
if and only if
x\perp_{\mathbb{R}}y
. In the complex case, we use a general result that
u\perp v
if and only if
u\perp_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda v
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
(x,0)\perp(y,z)
if and only if
(x,0)\perp_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda(y,z)=(\lambda y,\lambda z)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
which, by above, is equivalent to
x\perp_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda y
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, and then to
x\perp y
.

For the second part of the statement, note that
(y,z)\perp(x,0)
if and only if
\sqrt{\|y\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\|z\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}=\|(y,z)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}\leq\|(y,z)+\lambda(x,0)\|_{\mathcal{Z}}=\sqrt{\|y+\lambda x\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\|z\|_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. Clearly, this is equivalent to
\|y\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\|y+\lambda x\|_{\mathcal{X}}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
, that is, to
y\perp x
.

(i)

The subdifferential set is also related to the right hand derivative of the norm as follows (see

(4.1)

\displaystyle\partial\|x\|

\displaystyle{}=\{f\in\mathcal{X}^{*};\;\text{Re}f(y)\leq D_{+}(x,y)\text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X}\},

(4.2)

\displaystyle D_{+}(x,y)

\displaystyle{}=\max\{\text{Re}f(y);\;f\in\partial\|x\|\}.

(ii)

The subdifferential set
\partial\|x\|
is a convex subset of
\mathcal{X}^{*}
(because convex sum of two supporting functionals is a supporting functional).

(iii)

The point
x\in\mathcal{X}
is smooth if and only if
\partial\|x\|
is a singleton set and only contains the functional
f
defined as
f(y)=D_{+}(x,y)=D_{-}(x,y)
(see
[
10
, Corollary 1.2.10]
).

Now we give a characterization for the condition
x^{\bot}\subseteq y^{\bot}
, which complements Lemma
2.9
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a normed space over
\mathbb{F}
and let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. Then

\displaystyle x^{\bot}\subseteq y^{\bot}

\displaystyle\iff\big(\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }\partial\|\alpha x\|\subseteq\partial\|y\|\big)

\displaystyle\iff\big(\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }D_{+}(\alpha x,z)\leq D_{+}(y,z)\;\;\forall z\in\mathcal{X}\big).

Proof.

We first prove the equivalence:

x^{\bot}\subseteq y^{\bot}\iff\big(\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }\partial\|\alpha x\|\subseteq\partial\|y\|\big).

The converse implication follows from (
2.1
) and the fact that
y^{\bot}=(\alpha y)^{\bot}
.

Now, let
x^{\bot}\subseteq y^{\bot}
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\|x\|=\|y\|=1
. Using (
2.1
), we obtain
x^{\bot}=\bigcup\{\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits f;\;f\in\partial\|x\|\}
. Let
f
be a supporting functional at
x
. Then
x^{\bot}\subseteq y^{\bot}
implies
\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits f\subseteq y^{\bot}
. It follows from
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
that
|f(y)|=1
, i.e., there exists unimodular
\alpha
such that
f(y)=\alpha
.

Hence for
f,g\in\partial\|x\|
there exist unimodular
\alpha,\beta
such that
f(y)=\alpha
and
g(y)=\beta
. Since
(f+g)/2
also belongs to
\partial\|x\|
, there exists unimodular
\gamma
such that

\tfrac{\alpha+\beta}{2}=\left(\tfrac{f+g}{2}\right)(y)=\gamma.

Thus
|\alpha+\beta|=2
, so it follows from
|\alpha|=|\beta|=1
that
\alpha=\beta
, and hence
\partial\|\alpha x\|\subseteq\partial\|y\|
. The last equivalence follows directly from Eqs. (
4.1
) and (
4.2
).
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a normed space and let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. We have

\displaystyle x^{\bot}=y^{\bot}

\displaystyle\iff\big(\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }\partial\|\alpha x\|=\partial\|y\|\big)

\displaystyle\iff\big(\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}\text{ such that }D_{+}(\alpha x,z)=D_{+}(y,z)\;\;\forall z\in\mathcal{X}\big).

By Lemma
2.9
, two smooth vectors belong to the same face if and only if they have the same supporting functional, which, in finite-dimensional spaces, coincides with their gradients. This yields the next observation.

Corollary 4.3
.

Two smooth normalized vectors in a finite-dimensional normed space lie on the same face of the norm’s unit ball if and only if their gradients are equal.

We show next that there do exist nonsmooth and nonrotund real BJ-norms. The following example proves the existence of such a norm in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but it can be easily generalized to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
.

Example 4.4
.

Consider

\|(x,y,z)\|:=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}},&|z|\leq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}};\\
\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}+|z|,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

To see that this is indeed a norm, we only need to prove the triangle inequality. This will be done indirectly by studying the set of points
C
where
{\|\cdot\|}\leq 1
. Notice first that, by Cauchy-Schwarz-Bunyakowsky inequality, applied to
(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},|z|)
and
(1,1)
, we immediately have

(4.3)

\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}+|z|\leq\sqrt{2}\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}},

and the equality holds if and only if
|z|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
. Therefore, the set
C
coincides with a cone
\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3};\;\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}+|z|\leq 1\}
, when
|z|\geq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
, and a Euclidean ball of radius
1/\sqrt{2}
, otherwise. By (
4.3
), this ball is inscribed into the cone, so it touches it tangentially, and as such the function
{\|\cdot\|}
is smooth at nonzero points of intersection
|z|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
. Also,
C
is invariant under rotation around the
z
-axis, more precisely, under the action of a matrix
U\oplus 1
where
U
is an orthogonal
(2\times 2)
matrix. Hence
C
coincides with a rotational body obtained by rotating

C\cap(\{0\}\times(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R})
around the
z
-axis. One can compute that the boundary points of this intersection lie on a convex curve

y(z)=\begin{cases}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}-z^{2}},&\phantom{\frac{1}{2}<{}}|z|\leq\frac{1}{2};\\
|z|-1,&\frac{1}{2}<{}|z|\leq 1.\end{cases}

As such,

C=\left\{\left(y\cos\theta,y\sin\theta,z\right);\;\;0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi,\;y\leq 0,\;\|(0,y,z)\|\leq 1\right\},

being a rotation of a convex planar region, is also convex. Moreover,
C
is clearly a compact, absorbing, and balanced set whose boundary points lie outside the open ball of radius
1/\sqrt{2}
. So, by
[
22
, Theorem 1.35]
, its Minkowsky functional is a norm whose closed unit ball coincides with
C
. Then Minkowsky functional must be equal to
{\|\cdot\|}
, and the triangle inequality follows.

We proceed to show that
{\|\cdot\|}
is a BJ-norm. It suffices to verify that there exist no linearly independent normalized vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
u^{\bot}=v^{\bot}
and
{}^{\bot}u={}^{\bot}v
. Assume otherwise. By Lemma
2.9
, after replacing, if needed,
v
with
-v
we achieve that
u
and
v
belong to the same norm’s face, i.e., the same face of norm’s unit ball
C
.
Clearly, neither
u
nor
v
can coincide with the only two nonsmooth points
(0,0,\pm 1)
of
C
because then one among
u^{\bot},v^{\bot}
would be a hyperplane, while the other would be a union of several distinct hyperplanes, contradicting
u^{\bot}=v^{\bot}
.
Hence both
u,v
are smooth and belong to the same face. Since
u^{\bot}=\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits f_{u}
, where
f_{u}
is a supporting functional at
u
which can be identified with the norm’s gradient at
u
, we see that
\partial\|u\|
and
\partial\|v\|
are parallel. Now, the norm’s gradient at a smooth point
(x,y,z)
is easily seen to be

\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sqrt{2}(x,y,z)}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}},&|z|\leq\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}};\\
\left(\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}},\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}},\mathrm{sign}\,z\right),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore, under our assumption that
u,v
are two distinct points which belong to the same face, the two gradients at
u=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
and
v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
are parallel if and only if
|u_{3}|\geq\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}}
and
|v_{3}|\geq\sqrt{v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}}
and
(u_{1},u_{2})
is parallel to
(v_{1},v_{2})
. By a suitable rotation around the
z
-axis and multiplication by
\pm 1
(which are both isometries of our norm, hence preserve the in-coming and out-going neighbourhoods), we can achieve that normalized and smooth points
u,v
satisfy
u=(0,u_{2},1-u_{2})
and
v=(0,v_{2},1-v_{2})
with
0<u_{2},v_{2}\leq 1/2
. The only smooth points in
{}^{\bot}u
which lie on the hyperplane
\Pi=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the ones whose supporting functional annihilates
u
. Since supporting functional is given by the norm’s gradient, we hence see that the only such points are
(0,\lambda(1-1/u_{2}),\lambda)
where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. They clearly do not belong to
{}^{\bot}v\cap\Pi
, therefore,
{}^{\bot}u\neq{}^{\bot}v
. Thus we have shown that
{}^{\bot}u={}^{\bot}v
and
u^{\bot}=v^{\bot}
for some nonzero
u,v
if and only if
\mathbb{R}u=\mathbb{R}v
.

A norm on a finite-dimensional real vector space is called polyhedral if its closed unit ball is the intersection of finitely many closed halfspaces. This is equivalent to having only finitely many faces (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 19.1]
for an even more general result).

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
be a real finite-dimensional normed space. Then the norm
{\|\cdot\|}
is polyhedral if and only if
\big|\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\}\big|<\infty
.

Proof.

If the norm
{\|\cdot\|}
is polyhedral, then it has finitely many faces. Using Remark
2.10
, we obtain
x^{\bot}=y^{\bot}
for all
x,y
in the relative interior of any particular face. By
[
20
, Theorem 18.2]
, the norm’s unit sphere is a disjoint union of relative interiors of its faces, and hence
\big|\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\}\big|<\infty
.

Conversely, assume that
|\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\}|=m
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
be points of
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
such that
x_{i}^{\bot}\neq x_{j}^{\bot}
for all
i\neq j
. Since for any
y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})
we have
y^{\bot}=x_{i}^{\bot}
for some
1\leq i\leq m
, Lemma
2.9
implies that
y
and
x_{i}
belong to the same face. Therefore, the number of faces of the unit ball is less than equal to
m
. Hence the norm
{\|\cdot\|}
is a polyhedral norm.∎

Finally, let us conclude with some observations.

(A)

Recall that a complexification of a real normed space
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
is a complex vector space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{X}
equipped with a complex norm
{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathbb{C}}
that agrees with
{\|\cdot\|}
on the real subspace
1\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{X}
. Our next example shows that there might exist different complexifications
(\mathcal{Y}_{1},{\|\cdot\|}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{Y}_{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
of
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
such that projective orthodigraphs
\Gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{1})
and
\Gamma(\mathcal{Y}_{2})
are not isomorphic. For example, consider two complexifications of the real Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
: the complex Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
and the Taylor minimal complexification
(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathfrak{T}})
. These two complexifications of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
are not isometric because their isometry groups are not isomorphic (see
[
12
, Example 6.1]
). By James’ result, BJ-orthogonality in
(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathfrak{T}})
is not symmetric, so projective orthodigraphs
\Gamma(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
and
\Gamma(\mathbb{C}^{n},{\|\cdot\|}_{\mathfrak{T}})
are not isomorphic.

(B)

Corollary
2.11
holds even if we replace the word ‘maximal face’ by ‘maximal exposed face’. This follows from the fact that, in a finite-dimensional normed space, a maximal face is always exposed. To see this, assume that
F
is the norm’s maximal face. Let
A=\mathop{\mathrm{Span}}_{\mathrm{aff}}F
be its (real) affine span. If
F
is zero-dimensional, then let
z\in F
be its unique point. Otherwise, let
z
be an arbitrary point from the (nonempty) relative interior of
F
. Consider a linear subspace
\mathcal{V}=A-z
, so that
A=\mathcal{V}+z
. Since either
\mathcal{V}=0
or
z
belongs the relative interior of
F
, for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
we have
z+\lambda v\in F
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with small enough
|\lambda|
, see
[
20
, Corollary 6.4.1]
. But
\|x\|=1
for all
x\in F
, so for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
it holds that
D_{\pm}(z;v)=0
, and thus, by
[
15
, Theorem 3.2]
,
z\perp v
. It follows from
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
that there exists an
\mathbb{R}
-linear functional
f
with
\|f\|=1
such that
f(z)=1
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Ker}}\nolimits f
. Then
f(F)\subseteq f(A)=f(\mathcal{V}+z)=\{1\}
, so
F\subseteq f^{-1}(1)\cap\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
, where
\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
is the norm’s closed unit ball of
\mathcal{X}
. Since this intersection is also a face, and
F
, being a maximal face, cannot be properly contained in another face, it follows that
F=f^{-1}(1)\cap\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
. Thus
F
is an exposed face.

(C)

In particular, each maximal face in a complex Radon plane
\mathcal{R}
takes the form
f^{-1}(1)\cap\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathcal{R}}
for some normalized
\mathbb{C}
-linear functional
f
, so its dimension is either
0
or
2
. Thus, condition (ii) of Lemma
3.12
is also equivalent to:
(a
′
) The boundary points of each two-dimensional face in
{\mathcal{R}}
are smooth, and (b
′
) the set
\{x^{\bot};\;\;x\in\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})\}
has continuum many points.

(D)

Note that in the proof of Lemma
3.4
we showed that for a smooth norm the condition
{}^{\bot}x={}^{\bot}y
implies
\mathbb{F}x=\mathbb{F}y
. With the out-going neighborhoods we can say more: in
[
2
, Lemma 2.6]
it was proved that a norm on
\mathcal{X}
is strictly convex if and only if, given
x,y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{0\}
, the condition
x^{\bot}=y^{\bot}
implies
\mathbb{F}x=\mathbb{F}y
.

(E)

One way to interpret the existence of an isomorphism in item (i) of Lemmas
3.11
and
3.12
(and the existence of appropriate nonsmooth Radon planes as guaranteed by Examples
3.13
and
3.17
) is that orthodigraph
\Gamma_{0}
cannot detect nonsmooth norms on two-dimensional spaces. In this regard, its projective counterpart, orthodigraph
\Gamma
is again more restrictive: by Lemma
2.7
, there can be no isomorphism between
\Gamma({\mathcal{R}})
for a (real or complex) nonsmooth Radon plane and
\Gamma(\mathbb{F}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
for a (real or complex) two-dimensional Hilbert space.

(F)

Let
{\mathcal{R}}
be a smooth real Radon plane which is not isometrically isomorphic to a two-dimensional Hilbert space
\ell_{2}^{2}
, and, for a nonempty index set
I
, let
c_{0}(I)
denote the Banach space of all systems
(a_{n})_{n\in I}
such
that
\{n\in I;\;|a_{n}|\geq\varepsilon\}
is finite for each
\varepsilon>0
. In a recent paper, Tanaka
[
25
, Theorem 3.13]
showed that Banach spaces
{\mathcal{R}}\oplus_{\infty}c_{0}(I)
and
\ell_{2}^{2}\oplus_{\infty}c_{0}(I)
are not isometrically isomorphic, but still there does exist a homogeneous bijection
\Phi\colon{\mathcal{R}}\oplus_{\infty}c_{0}(I)\to\ell^{2}_{2}\oplus_{\infty}c_{0}(I)
, which preservers BJ-orthogonality in both directions. Because it is homogeneous, it hence induces a graph isomorphism between projective orthodigraphs
\Gamma({\mathcal{R}}\oplus_{\infty}c_{0}(I))
and
\Gamma(\ell_{2}^{2}\oplus_{\infty}c_{0}(I))
. This shows that, in nonsmooth normed spaces, orthodigraphs cannot completely classify the norms up to isometry.

(G)

Shortly after the submission of this paper we were informed that Saikat Roy and Ryotaro Tanaka just recently obtained yet another classification, parallel to our Lemma
3.11
, of real Radon planes with
\Gamma_{0}({\mathcal{R}})\simeq\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2},{\|\cdot\|}_{2})
(see
[
21
, Corollary 2.14 and Theorems 3.10 and 3.13]
). Moreover,
[
21
, Example 3.16]
supplements our Example
3.14

which satisfies this condition. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, our Lemma
3.12
and the accompanying Example
3.17
, for the complex Radon planes are completely new.

(H)

For a complex normed space
\mathcal{X}
, if we define two norm’s faces
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
to be equivalent if there exists a unimodular scalar
\mu
such that
F_{1}=\mu F_{2}
, then the result for complex normed space corresponding to Proposition
4.5
states as follows. There exists finitely many nonequivalent norm’s faces if and only if
\big|\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\}\big|<\infty.
We also remark that this characterization is valid even in nonprojective case. More precisely, there exists finitely many nonequivalent norm’s faces in a finite-dimensional normed space
(\mathcal{X},{\|\cdot\|})
if and only if
\big|\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\}\big|<\infty
. This follows directly because from (
1.1
), we see that there is bijective correspondence between
\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\}
and
\{x^{\bot};\;x\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})\setminus\{0\}\}
given by
[x]^{\bot}\longleftrightarrow x^{\bot}.
