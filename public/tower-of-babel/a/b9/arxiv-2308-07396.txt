Extremal solutions for Network Flow with Differential Constraints – A Generalization of Spanning Trees
René Brandenberg, Paul Stursberg Technische Universität München, Germany {rene.brandenberg, paul.stursberg}@tum.de

Abstract
In network flow problems, there is a well-known one-to-one relationship between extreme points of the feasibility region and trees in the associated undirected graph. The same is true for the dual differential problem. In this paper, we study problems where the constraints of both problems appear simultaneously, a variant which is motivated by an application in the expansion planning of energy networks. We show that all extreme points still directly correspond to graph-theoretical structures in the underlying network. The reverse is generally also true in all but certain exceptional cases. We furthermore characterize graphs in which these exceptional cases never occur and present additional criteria for when those cases do not occur due to parameter values.

1
Introduction

The
minimum cost flow
problem is a classical optimization problem on a graph: In a network with cost and capacity bound assigned to its edges, what is the lowest-cost configuration to transfer a given amount of
“flow”
from a dedicated
source
to a
sink
?
This problem has been studied extensively and is one of the cornerstones of
network flow theory
(see, e. g.
[
Roc84
]
). It is well-known in particular for a very elegant combinatorial characterization of extremal solutions in terms of
spanning trees
in the underlying graph.

One area of application where network flows commonly play a role is
Energy System Optimization
, the study of optimal dispatch and expansion in electrical power systems. Especially in modern systems with a high share of renewables, transmission networks may play an important role since supply from renewable energy sources ( e. g. offshore or coastal wind turbines
) may be located far from demand centers.

These transmission systems typically operate under
alternating current
and power flows across the system are governed by physical laws, whose non-convex equations do not lend themselves very well to mathematical optimization methods. As a consequence, different approximations have been developed to represent the most important aspects of an alternating current transmission grid. The simplest such approximation is the so-called
Transport Model
. Here, the flow on each individual transmission line is restricted only by a capacity constraint, like in the classical network flow problem mentioned above (see, e.g.,
[
Tuo+09
,
SSH12
]
).

While flows on different lines are independent under the Transport Model, the Linearized Load Flow Model (or DC model) approximates the interaction of flows on different transmission lines in the following way (see
[
SJA09
]
): A
potential value
is assigned to each vertex in the network and the power flow on each line is required to be proportional to the potential difference between its two endpoints. The constant of variation may be different for each individual line and depends on its technical parameters (the line’s
susceptance
), influenced by the length of the line and the material used (see, e.g.,
[
Hew+16
]
).

To enable a more targeted analysis, one needs a better understanding of the solution structures. These structures have long been known for the classical network flow theory which, as mentioned above, corresponds to the Transport model. The DC model, in a similar way, can be captured mathematically by
differential flows
in a network. In this paper, we generalize the theory of classical network flows to the setting of differential flows, providing a useful characterization of its extremal solutions. This work is based on results from
[
Stu19
]
.

1.1
Network graph and differential flow

Starting from an undirected graph, we assign arbitrary directions to each edge in order to distinguish the direction of flow on that edge (such that a negative flow represents a flow in the reverse direction). The result is an
anti-symmetric
directed graph ( i. e. between every pair of vertices there exists at most one directed edge). Adding upper and lower bounds for flows on each edge and for flow conservation deficit/surplus in each vertex, as well as an elasticity value for each edge, we obtain the following definition of a
network
in the context of this article:

Definition 1
(Network)

A
network

(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
consists of a weakly connected, anti-symmetric directed graph
(V,E)
together with an
elasticity vector

b\in\mathds{R}^{E}
with
b>0
and pairs of upper and lower bounds
{\bar{p}}=(p^{-},p^{+})\in(\mathds{R}\cup\{-\infty\})^{V}\times(\mathds{R}\cup\{\infty\})^{V}
and
{\bar{f}}=(f^{-},f^{+})\in(\mathds{R}\cup\{-\infty\})^{E}\times(\mathds{R}\cup\{\infty\})^{E}
for vertices and edges, respectively, such that
p^{-}\leq p^{+}
and
f^{-}\leq f^{+}
.

\Box

We write, e. g.,
p^{+}=\infty
to denote that
p^{+}_{e}=\infty
for all
e\in E
.

Based on our motivation that positive and negative flows on an edge merely represent the same flow rate in different directions, it seems most natural to have
f^{-}_{e}=-f^{+}_{e}
for all
e\in E
. However, all results in this paper hold for the more general case without this requirement, as well. The elasticity vector
b
corresponds to the
line susceptance
in an alternating current power transmission network.

Definition 2
(Differential Flow)

Let
G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
be a network. We call
f\in\mathds{R}^{E}
a
differential flow
in
G
if there exists a potential
\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}
such that for
(v,w)\in E:f_{vw}=b_{vw}\cdot(\varphi_{w}-\varphi_{v})
. In this case, we say that
\varphi

induces

f
.
Furthermore, we say that
f
is
feasible
(for
G
) if

a)

f^{-}_{e}\leq f_{e}\leq f^{+}_{e}
for all
e\in E
, and

b)

p^{-}_{v}\leq\sum_{e\in\delta^{\text{out}}(v)}f_{e}-\sum_{e\in\delta^{\text{in}}(v)}f_{e}\leq p^{+}_{v}
for all
v\in V
,

where
\delta^{\text{in}}(v)
denotes the set of all edges ending in
v
, while
\delta^{\text{out}}(v)
denotes the set of all edges starting in
v
.

\Box

A differential flow is thus an ordinary flow on the edges of the graph
(V,E)
, which satisfies capacity
constraints on the edges and relaxed flow conservation constraints on the vertices, as well as the additional
differential constraints

f_{vw}=b_{vw}\cdot(\varphi_{w}-\varphi_{v})
for a suitable choice of the potential
\varphi
. Note that the vertex constraints represent bounds on the flow that
is created
in the respective vertex, the upper bound
p^{+}_{v}
thus represents a
lower
bound on the
excess
in the vertex
v
as defined, e. g., in
[
AMO93
, Ch. 7.6]
.

In the case of
p^{-}=p^{+}=0
the conditions in b) would be ordinary flow conservation constraints (which are well-known from the literature on network flows, see, e. g.,
[
PS98
, Chs. 3.4 and 7.3]
or
[
Sch03
, Ch. 13.2]
) for all vertices. In that case all flows would be sums of flows along cycles. However, the differential constraints require that no cycle can carry a nonzero flow (since otherwise
\varphi
would strictly increase along the cycle). Consequently,
f\equiv 0
would be the only feasible differential flow in
G
.

1.2
The Differential Flow Polytope

Given a
network

G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
,
we define the
elasticity matrix

B
of
G
by

B_{ve}:=\begin{cases}b_{e}&e\in\delta^{\text{in}}(v)\\
-b_{e}&e\in\delta^{\text{out}}(v)\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(1)

Thus we have
B=A\cdot\diag(b)
with
A
being the incidence matrix of the graph
(V,E)
.

We can now write the set of feasible differential flows as

Q_{f}(G):=\left\{f\in\mathds{R}^{E}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\exists\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}:\begin{gathered}B^{\top}\varphi=f\\
p^{-}\leq-Af\leq p^{+}\\
f^{-}\leq f\leq f^{+}\end{gathered}\right\}.

(2)

As mentioned above, we refer to the first set of constraints
B^{\top}\varphi=f
as
differential constraints
and to the other two sets of constraints
p^{-}\leq-Af\leq p^{+}
and
f^{-}\leq f\leq f^{+}
as
relaxed flow conservation constraints
(or
vertex constraints
) and
capacity constraints
(or
edge constraints
), respectively. We also see, as mentioned above, that inverting the sign of the vector
b
does not change the resulting set
Q_{f}(G)
, it merely inverts the sign of the corresponding
\varphi
, as well.

Note that
\dim(Q_{f}(G))\leq|V|
, since the polyhedron is contained in the
|V|
-dimensional linear subspace defined by
B^{\top}\varphi=f
. If we identify this linear subspace with
\mathds{R}^{V}
, we obtain the following alternative representation of
Q_{f}(G)
:

Q_{\varphi}(G):=\left\{\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\begin{gathered}f^{-}\leq B^{\top}\varphi\leq f^{+}\\
p^{-}\leq-AB^{\top}\varphi\leq p^{+}\end{gathered}\right\}

(3)

The matrix
AB^{\top}
is known in electrical engineering as the
nodal admittance matrix
(see e.g.,
[
SJA09
]
) and if
b_{e}=1
for all
e\in E
then
AB^{\top}=AA^{\top}
is known as the
Laplacian matrix
of
(V,E)
, which captures many interesting properties of the graph (see, e. g.,
[
Chu97
]
about its eigenvalues).

Before we proceed, we observe that all three matrices
A
,
B
, and
AB^{\top}
have rank
|V|-1
.

Proposition 3

Let
(V,E)
be a weakly connected directed graph ( i. e. one where the underlying undirected graph is connected) with incidence matrix
A
. Let
b\in\mathds{R}^{E}
,
b>0
and let
B
be the elasticity matrix as defined in
Eq.

1
.
Then,

A^{\top}v=0\Longleftrightarrow B^{\top}v=0\Longleftrightarrow AB^{\top}v=0\Longleftrightarrow v=\lambda\cdot\mathbbm{1}\text{ for }\lambda\in\mathds{R},

(4)

where
\mathbbm{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}
.

\Box

1.3
Optimal Flows and Differentials

The polyhedron
Q_{f}(G)
bears a close resemblance to the
feasible flow
and
feasible differential
polyhedra, which are both well-known from the theory of network flows.
[
Roc84
]
gives an extensive account of the related theory, based on which we present the short overview given in this section.

Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with incidence matrix
A
, the (uncapacitated)
feasible flow
polyhedron (for some right-hand-side vector
d
satisfying
\mathbbm{1}^{\top}d=0
) can be written as

Q_{F}(G):=\left\{x\in\mathds{R}^{E}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}Ax=d\right\}.

Note that, while
Q_{F}(G)
does not restrict the flow by any edge capacities nor sign constraints, we could add capacity constraints without changing the properties that we will discuss below, albeit at the cost of a somewhat more cumbersome notation.

Now, suppose that we want to treat positive and negative flows on an edge separately, say by assigning a (possibly different) positive cost
f^{+}_{e}\geq 0
and
-f^{-}_{e}\geq 0
to positive and negative flows, respectively.
To achieve this, we can split up the vector
x
into a positive part
x^{+}
and a negative part
x^{-}
to obtain

\min\left\{{f^{+}}^{\top}x^{+}-{f^{-}}^{\top}x^{-}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}x^{+},x^{-}\geq 0,A(x^{+}-x^{-})=d\right\}.

(5)

Observe that since
f^{+}_{e},-f^{-}_{e}\geq 0
, we can assume w. l. o. g. that for each edge
e
, at most one of
x^{+}_{e}
and
x^{-}_{e}
is strictly positive.

The feasible flow polyhedron (written in the form of
Eq.

5
) is famous for a very neat characterization of its combinatorial structure:
Any of its extremal solutions can be characterized by the set of components of
x^{+}
and
x^{-}
for which the sign constraints are binding. It is convenient now to differentiate between the set
\mathcal{N}
of edges
e
for which both
x^{+}_{e}
and
x^{-}_{e}
are fixed to
0
and its complement
\mathcal{B}:=E\setminus\mathcal{N}
,
the set of edges
e
for which one of
x^{+}_{e}
and
x^{-}_{e}
may assume a value strictly greater than
0
.

Assuming that
G
is weakly connected, we have
\rank(A)=|V|-1
by
Proposition

3
.
For the pair
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N})
to uniquely describe an extremal solution, it is therefore necessary that
\mathcal{N}
contains
|E|-(|V|-1)
edges corresponding to linearly independent columns of
A
.
We call
\mathcal{B}
a
basis
if
\mathcal{B}
consists of exactly
|V|-1
such edges.

The following characterization now holds with respect to the bases of the above polyhedron:
\mathcal{B}
is a basis if and only if the edges of
G
which correspond to those columns of
A
indexed by
\mathcal{B}
form an undirected spanning tree in
G
. This can be seen as follows: The columns for any cycle in
G
are always linearly dependent (as the column of any edge can be obtained as the sum of positive/negative columns of the other edges), hence the sets of
|V|-1
linearly independent columns are exactly those corresponding to spanning trees.

A similar characterization holds for the optimization problem dual to
Eq.

5
, the optimal differential problem. It asks for a potential
\varphi
maximizing the linear function
d^{\top}\varphi
over the polyhedron
Q_{D}(G)
of
feasible differentials

[
Roc84
]
defined as follows:

Q_{D}(G):=\left\{\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}f^{-}\leq A^{\top}\varphi\leq f^{+}\right\}

The variables in this new polyhedron do not have to satisfy any (individual) bounds or sign restrictions.
An extreme point of this polyhedron is hence characterized by the set of components of the vector
A^{\top}\varphi
that are fixed to one of their respective capacity bounds. Dual to the notation above, we can call the set of edges that index these rows
\mathcal{B}
and analogously
\mathcal{N}:=E\setminus\mathcal{B}
. Again, by
Proposition

3
,
\rank(A)=|V|-1
and hence in order to uniquely identify (up to translation along
\mathbbm{1}
) the vector
\varphi
conforming the specifications of
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N})
, we need that
\mathcal{B}
indexes
|V|-1
linearly independent rows of
A^{\top}
(or equivalently columns of
A
). As argued above, a set
\mathcal{B}
of exactly
|V|-1
such rows satisfies this requirement if and only if the corresponding edges of
G
form a spanning tree. Furthermore, the potential
\varphi
uniquely determined by
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{N})
(up to translation along
\mathbbm{1}
) is feasible if and only if
f^{-}_{\mathcal{N}}\leq A_{\mathcal{N}}^{\top}\varphi\leq f^{+}_{\mathcal{N}}
.

Note that the roles of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{N}
in both cases are reversed: While for the feasible flow polyhedron, the entries of
x
indexed by
\mathcal{B}
are the ones that are free to take values between the respective bounds, the inequalities indexed by
\mathcal{B}
in the feasible differential polyhedron are the ones that are forced to be binding. A summary of the characterizations outlined above is provided in

The connections of the above theory of basic flows and differentials to the polyhedron
Q_{f}(G)
defined in
Eq.

2
above are easy to make:
The feasible flow polyhedron
Q_{F}(G)
(possibly with added edge capacities) can be seen as a variant of the polyhedron
Q_{f}(G)
with
p^{-}=p^{+}=-d
and without the differential constraint
B^{\top}\varphi=f
.
On the other hand,
Q_{D}(G)
is (up to a full-rank linear transformation) a special case of the polyhedron
Q_{f}(G)
with
{\bar{p}}=(-\infty,\infty)
.

In this sense,
differential flows
jointly generalize the notions of flows and differentials. This also explains our choice of terminology (see
Definition

2
): A differential flow combines the defining properties of both flows and differentials, placing it somehow
in between
the two concepts.
This raises the following question, which we answer in the following: Can a combinatorial characterization of extremal points along the lines of
Table

1
be recovered for
Q_{f}(G)
, given that
Q_{f}(G)
imposes constraints of both types,
B^{\top}\varphi=f
and
p^{-}\leq-Af\leq p^{+}
?

2
\alpha
-Forests and
\alpha
-Trees

In the following we prove that, in almost all cases, a combinatorial characterization of the extremal points of
Q_{f}(G)
is indeed possible: Using a suitable notion of acyclicity, extremal points of
Q_{f}(G)
can be associated
with maximal acyclic collections of edges
and vertices
which, again, correspond to the set of active inequalities. Furthermore, every such collection also corresponds to an extremal point of
Q_{f}(G)
in all but some exceptional cases.

To motivate the following definition, observe the following (non-exhaustive) list of sufficient criteria:

The point
f
is extremal in
Q_{f}(G)
if

•

the edge capacity constraints are active for every edge in an undirected spanning tree (this is the case of the original feasible differential problem (see
Table

1
),

•

the edge capacity constraints are active in all edges from a spanning tree
except one
, and additionally the relaxed flow conservation constraint is binding in one of the endpoints of that missing edge,

•

the relaxed flow conservation constraints are active in exactly
|V|-1
vertices (this follows from
Proposition

3
),

•

an edge capacity constraint is active on an edge
(v,w)
and any
|V|-2
relaxed flow conservation constraints are active that do not contain both
v
and
w
.

These observations (which will later be proved formally in a more general context) motivate the following definition of a structure that can be used to characterize extremal solutions (see
Fig.

).

(
\alpha
-Forest)

Let

be a network. A pair

and a set of vertices
V_{F}\subset V
in
G
is called an
\alpha
-forest
in
G
if there exists an injective function
\alpha_{F}:V_{F}\rightarrow E\setminus E_{F}
, mapping each vertex from

to a neighboring edge, which is not already in

, such that the set
E_{F}\cup\alpha_{F}(V_{F})
does not contain an undirected cycle. We call any such
\alpha_{F}
a

for

and any edge
e\in E

active
in
F
if
e\in E_{F}
. Analogously, for a vertex
v\in V
, we say that
v
is
active
in
F
if
v\in V_{F}
.
The
size
of an
\alpha
-forest
F
is defined by
|F|:=|E_{F}|+|V_{F}|
.
If there is no
\alpha
-forest
F^{\prime}
in
G
with
|F^{\prime}|>|F|
, then we say that
F
is

.

Definition 5
(Conforming

Given a feasible differential flow

, we say that the

.

Remember that for the special case of

We prove that for every extremal solution

.

Lemma 6

Let
G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
be a network. Let
V^{*}:=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{k}\}\subset V
denote a set of
k

denote a set of

\rank\begin{pmatrix}(B^{\top})_{E^{*}}\\
(AB^{\top})_{V^{*}}\end{pmatrix}=|V|-1.

(6)

Then,
G^{*}:=(V,E^{*})
has
k+1

, there exists a
k\times(k+1)

Lemma 7

Let
H:=(W\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}S,R\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}U)
be a bipartite graph with
|S|=|W|+1
satisfying the following conditions:

a)

\delta(v)\cap R=1
for all
v\in W

b)

|N_{H}(W^{\prime})|\geq|W^{\prime}|+1
for every
W^{\prime}\subset W
.

Then, there exists a selection of edges
U^{*}\subset U
such that

i)

\delta(v)\cap U^{*}=1
for all
v\in W
and

ii)

the graph
(W\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}S,R\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}U^{*})
is connected (and therefore a tree).

\Box

Proof
.

We first observe that
Item

b)
, together with
Item

a)
, implies in particular that
\delta(v)\cap U\geq 1
for all
v\in W
. Thus,
|U|\geq|W|
and since
H
is bipartite, a selection
U^{*}\subset U
of edges with
\delta(v)\cap U^{*}=1
always exists.

We prove the statement by induction over
|U|
. If
|U|=|W|
, then every vertex
v\in W
is incident with exactly one edge from
U
. The selection
U^{*}:=U
thus satisfies
Item

i)
. If we suppose that
Item

ii)
does not hold, i. e.
(W\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}S,R\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}U)
is not connected, then we can choose a connected component
C
and denote by
W_{C}
the subset of vertices from
W
that is covered by
C
. Since
C
is a connected component,
N_{H}(W_{C})\cap N_{H}(W\setminus W_{C})=\emptyset
and by
Item

b)
we obtain
|N_{H}(W_{C})|>|W_{C}|
. Hence,

|N_{H}(W\setminus W_{C})|\leq|S\setminus N_{H}(W_{C})|=|S|-|N_{H}(W_{C})|<|W|+1-|W_{C}|=|W\setminus W_{C}|+1,

a contradiction to
Item

b)
.

Now, let us assume that
|U|>|W|
, which means that there exists
v\in W
with
\delta(v)\cap U\geq 2
. Choose two distinct edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in\delta(v)\cap U
and denote the corresponding neighbors of
v
in
S
by
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, respectively.

We claim that at least one of the two graphs
H_{1}:=(W\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}S,R\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}U\setminus\{e_{1}\})
and
H_{2}:=(W\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}S,R\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}U\setminus\left\{e_{2}\right\})
must satisfy the condition
Item

b)
. By induction, we then obtain a selection
U^{*}
of edges which satisfies
Items

i)
and
ii)
with respect to the graph
H_{1}
(or
H_{2}
) and thus also for
H
, proving the statement.

For a contradiction, suppose otherwise. Then, there exists a set
W_{1}\subset W
which violates
Item

b)
in the graph
H_{1}
and a set
W_{2}\subset W
which violates
Item

b)
within
H_{2}
. Since neither
W_{1}
nor
W_{2}
violate
Item

b)
within
H
, it must hold that
v\in W_{1}\cap W_{2}
.

Now, let
W^{\prime}_{1}:=W_{1}\setminus W_{2}
,
W^{\prime}_{2}:=W_{2}\setminus W_{1}
, and
W_{12}:=W_{1}\cap W_{2}\setminus\{v\}
. We use the following statements, which we successively derive from each other below:

I)

|N_{H_{1}}(W_{1})|=|W_{1}|=|N_{H}(W_{1})|-1
and

|N_{H_{2}}(W_{2})|=|W_{2}|=|N_{H}(W_{2})|-1

II)

|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{1})\cap N_{H}(W_{12})|\geq|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{1})|+|N_{H}(W_{12})|-|W^{\prime}_{1}|-|W_{12}|-1
and

|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{2})\cap N_{H}(W_{12})|\geq|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{2})|+|N_{H}(W_{12})|-|W^{\prime}_{2}|-|W_{12}|-1

III)

|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{1}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}W_{12}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}W^{\prime}_{2})|\leq|W^{\prime}_{1}|+|W_{12}|+|W^{\prime}_{2}|+1

IV)

N_{H}(v)\setminus\{s_{1}\}\subset N_{H}(W_{1}\setminus\{v\})
and

N_{H}(v)\setminus\{s_{2}\}\subset N_{H}(W_{2}\setminus\{v\})

Statements
Item

III)
and
Item

IV)
together will then prove that the original graph
H
would already violate
Item

b)
, a contradiction.

To prove
Item

I)
and
Item

II)
, let
i\in\{1,2\}
. From the assumption that
W_{i}
violates
Item

b)
in the graph
H_{i}
, but satisfies
Item

b)
in the graph
H
, we can immediately follow that
|N_{H_{i}}(W_{i})|\leq|W_{i}|\leq|N_{H}(W_{i})|-1
. On the other hand,

lacks only a single edge compared with

and thus
|N_{H_{i}}(W_{i})|\geq|N_{H}(W_{i})|-1
. This proves

I)
.

We can now conclude that

\displaystyle|W^{\prime}_{i}|+|W_{12}|+1

\displaystyle=|W_{i}|\overset{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{it:atg-h1v1}}{\geq}|N_{H_{i}}(W_{i})|\geq|N_{H_{i}}(W^{\prime}_{i}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}W_{12})|=|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{i}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}W_{12})|

.

Using the inclusion/exclusion principle, it follows

\displaystyle|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{1}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}W_{12}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}W^{\prime}_{2})|

\displaystyle=|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{1})|+|N_{H}(W_{12})|+|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{2})|

\displaystyle\qquad-|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{1})\cap N_{H}(W_{12})|-|N_{H}(W^{\prime}_{2})\cap N_{H}(W_{12})|

\displaystyle|N_{H}(W_{i})\setminus N_{H}(W_{i}\setminus\{v\})|

\displaystyle=|N_{H}(W_{i})|-|N_{H}(W_{i}\setminus\{v\})|

\displaystyle\overset{\mathclap{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{it:atg-h1v1},\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{it:at-subset}}}{\leq}\,(|N_{H_{i}}(W_{i})|+1)-(|W_{i}\setminus\{v\}|+1)

v^{\prime}\in V\setminus\{v,w\}
. Now, let
f_{e}=0
for all
e\in E
. Then,
(W\cup S\cup T,\left\{v,w\right\})
is an
\alpha
-tree in the resulting network that conforms with
f
.
At the same time, we can choose
b
such that

\frac{\sum_{e\in E_{wt}}b_{e}}{\sum_{e\in E_{ws}}b_{e}}=\frac{\sum_{e\in E_{vt}}b_{e}}{\sum_{e\in E_{vs}}b_{e}}=1.

Defining

\displaystyle\varphi_{v^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $v^{\prime}$ is covered by $W$}\\
1&\text{if $v^{\prime}$ is covered by $S$}\\
-1&\text{if $v^{\prime}$ is covered by $T$}\end{cases}

we have that
0\neq\pm B^{\top}\varphi\in Q_{f}(G)
since
(B^{\top}\varphi)_{e}=0
for every edge
e\in W\cup S\cup T
. The only other bounds that are not
\pm\infty
are the vertex constraints in
v
and
w
.

Let
N^{\text{in}}(v):=\{w\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}(w,v)\in E\}
and
N^{\text{out}}(v):=\{w\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}(v,w)\in E\}
for every
v\in V
.

Then, for these constraints

\displaystyle(AB^{\top}\varphi)_{v}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\sum_{\mathclap{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{in}}(v)\cap W}}b_{v^{\prime}v}(\varphi_{v}-\varphi_{v^{\prime}})+\sum_{\mathclap{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{in}}(v)\cap T}}b_{v^{\prime}v}(\varphi_{v}-\varphi_{v^{\prime}})+\sum_{\mathclap{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{in}}(v)\cap S}}b_{v^{\prime}v}(\varphi_{v}-\varphi_{v^{\prime}})\\
-\sum_{\mathclap{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{out}}(v)\cap W}}b_{vv^{\prime}}(\varphi_{v^{\prime}}-\varphi_{v})-\sum_{\mathclap{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{out}}(v)\cap T}}b_{vv^{\prime}}(\varphi_{v^{\prime}}-\varphi_{v})-\sum_{\mathclap{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{out}}(v)\cap S}}b_{vv^{\prime}}(\varphi_{v^{\prime}}-\varphi_{v})\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\sum_{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{in}}(v)\cap T}b_{v^{\prime}v}\cdot 1+\sum_{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{in}}(v)\cap S}b_{v^{\prime}v}\cdot(-1)\\
-\sum_{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{out}}(v)\cap T}b_{vv^{\prime}}\cdot(-1)-\sum_{v^{\prime}\in N^{\text{out}}(v)\cap S}b_{vv^{\prime}}\cdot 1\end{multlined}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E_{vt}}b_{e}-\sum_{e\in E_{vs}}b_{e}=0

and analogously
(AB^{\top}\varphi)_{w}=0
.
Thus,
f=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(B^{\top}\varphi+(-B^{\top}\varphi))
is not extremal, which proves that the network
G
is degenerate.

\Box

The following fact is generally well-known. Since we could not find a suitable reference, however, we have added a short proof of our own, which provides us with a simple algorithm to determine wether non-degenerateness can be guaranteed for a given graph.

Proposition 19

It can be decided in linear time whether a graph
G=(V,E)
is a cactus.

\Box

Proof
.

Perform a depth-first search (DFS) in time
\mathcal{O}(|E|+|V|)
. Choose a back-edge
\{v,w\}
, i. e. an edge that connects
v
to some previously-visited vertex
w
. Then,
w
must be a predecessor of
v
, otherwise we would have visited
v
from
w
and the edge
\{v,w\}
would be in the DFS tree. Re-trace the path from
v
back to
w
, marking edges as we go. If we reach a vertex
v^{\prime}
incident with another back-edge
\{v^{\prime},w^{\prime}\}
, then recursively re-trace its cycle before continuing on the original path. If we reach the vertex
w
, then chose a new back-edge and repeat until all back-edges have been marked.

If at some point we reach an edge that has already been marked, then
G
was not a cactus graph (the edge belonged to two distinct simple cycles). Otherwise,
G
is a cactus graph: Every simple cycle in
G
produces a back-edge, we have marked every back-edge together with the cycle that it belongs to and no edge has been marked twice. Hence, no edge belongs to two simple cycles and thus by
Proposition

17
, the graph
G
is a cactus.

\Box

We have already encountered the diamond graph, the forbidden minor which characterizes cacti, in
Example

9
: It was our first example of a network that is degenerate.
Theorem

18
now shows that this is in fact the characterizing structure for non-degenerateness, at least from a topological point of view. Cactus graphs, for which we can guarantee non-degenerateness under all circumstances, are closely related to several results from the context of electrical circuits and related areas:

They represent exactly those graphs that, if we add a single edge, remain
of series parallel type
(or
confluent
) in the sense of
[
Duf65
]
. A graph is of series parallel type if it can be constructed by iteratively replacing an edge by two sequential or two parallel edges, starting from an edge connecting a single vertex with itself.
Such networks are those, for which the equivalent resistance can be computed by iteratively applying Ohm’s laws for resistors in series and parallel.
Note that the diamond graph itself is also of series parallel type, but by adding a single edge we can obtain
K_{4}
, which is no longer of series parallel type.

4
Beyond Cactus Graphs

We can conclude that the well-known (but limited) family of cactus graphs completely characterizes those networks on which optimization problems over electrical power flows (as represented by the differential flow polyhedron) are
“well-behaved”
from a
purely topological perspective
. However, network topology is only one aspect of non-degenerateness, the exact values of the vector
b
of edge weights highly matter, as well: Indeed, all networks are non-degenerate unless the weight vector
b
is chosen from the union of finitely many lower-dimensional subspaces of the parameter space (one subspace for every diamond minor in the network). The network from
Example

9
, for instance, is almost always non-degenerate unless
\frac{b_{wt}}{b_{ws}}=\frac{b_{vt}}{b_{vs}}
. In the theory of electrical circuits, this case is known as the one where the Wheatstone bridge is
balanced

[
Duf65
]
.
Furthermore, note that by
Theorem

8
, even in this case, every extremal point corresponds to an
\alpha
-tree (only the reverse might not always hold).

This implies that we can in practice expect non-degenerateness to hold not only for the (quite limited) family of networks with cactus topology and every network can be made non-degenerate by slightly disturbing the edge weights.

For a specific network, however, it is generally
\mathbb{NP}
-complete to determine whether or not it is non-degenerate, as we will see in Section
4.2
.
Still, we can identify
sufficient conditions
to identify
\alpha
-trees for which every conforming differential flow is always extremal (regardless of whether or not the underlying network is non-degenerate).

4.1
Sufficient Conditions

In this subsection, we present some conditions for a given
\alpha
-tree under which a solution
f\in Q_{f}(G)
that conforms to it is guaranteed to be extremal. We start by observing the following:

Remark 20

Let
G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
be a network and
f\in Q_{f}(G)
. Then
f
is
not
extremal if and only if there exists a differential flow
g\neq 0
(which itself does not need to be feasible) such that
f\pm g\in Q_{f}(G)
. In particular, for every vertex
v
with
-(Af)_{v}\in\{p^{-},p^{+}\}
, we must have
A_{v}g=0
and for every edge
(v,w)\in E
with
f_{vw}\in\{f^{-}_{vw},f^{+}_{vw}\}
, we must have
g_{vw}=0
.

\Box

In light of the remark above, it makes sense to investigate the restrictions which an
\alpha
-forest
F=(E_{F},V_{F})
that conforms with
f
imposes on a differential flow
g
with
f\pm g\in Q_{f}(G)
. For a potential
\varphi
that induces
g
, the above observation implies for every connected component
S
of the graph
(V,E_{F})
and
v,w\in S
that
\varphi_{v}=\varphi_{w}
(since
f_{vw}\in\{f^{-}_{vw},f^{+}_{vw}\}
for every
(v,w)\in E_{F}
), i. e.
\varphi
is constant within any connected component of
(V,E_{F})
.

In order to represent restrictions imposed by
F
on
\varphi
between different connected components of
(V,E_{F})
, we need the following definition of a
generalized differential flow
which is induced by a potential
\varphi
via a
generalized elasticity vector

b
.

Definition 21
(Generalized Differential Flow)

Let
V
be a finite set and
b\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}^{V\times V}
. A potential
\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}
induces a
generalized differential flow

f\in\mathds{R}^{V\times V}
on
V
with respect to
b
by
f_{vw}=b_{vw}(\varphi_{w}-\varphi_{v})
. We say that
f
is feasible if

\sum_{w\in V}f_{vw}=0

(10)

for all vertices
v\in V
.

\Box

Note that in contrast to the elasticity vector in a network, a generalized elasticity vector is defined for all pairs
(v,w)\in V\times V
(even if
(v,w)\notin E
). In particular, there may be pairs
(v,w)
with
b_{vw}\neq b_{wv}
and, as a consequence,
f_{vw}\neq-f_{wv}
(one could even be 0).

Furthermore, equation
Eq.

10
is similar to a common flow conservation constraint, but not identical: For every
v\in V
, we only sum over the
“outgoing”
entries
f_{vw}
for
w\in V
(which may be positive or negative). In particular, the
“incoming”
flow value
f_{wv}
does not appear in the constraint
Eq.

10
for vertex
v
but only in the corresponding constraint for vertex
w
.

Note further that for any
\varphi_{0}\in\mathds{R}
, the trivial potential
\varphi\equiv\varphi_{0}
induces the generalized differential flow
f\equiv 0
(independently of
b
), which is always feasible. Analogously to
Definition

4
, the following tree structure can be used to characterize the cases where
f\equiv 0
is the unique feasible generalized flow (as we will see in
Lemma

23
):

Definition 22

Let
T=(V,E)
be a weakly connected directed graph that does not contain an undirected cycle. We then call
T
a
directed tree
. If furthermore
|\delta^{\text{out}}(v)|\leq 1
for all
v\in V
, then
T
is an
anti-arborescence
.

\Box

In particular, if
T
is an anti-arborescence, then there is a unique vertex
v_{0}\in T
such that
|\delta^{\text{out}}(v)|=0
(the
sink
of the anti-arborescence).

Lemma 23

Let
V
be a finite set,
b\in\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}^{V\times V}
, and
E:=\{(v,w)\in V\times V\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}v\neq w\text{ and }b_{vw}>0\}
such that
(V,E)
contains a spanning anti-arborescence as a subgraph. If
\varphi
induces a feasible generalized differential flow
f
on
V
with respect to
b
, then
\varphi\equiv\varphi_{0}
for some
\varphi_{0}\in\mathds{R}
and as a consequence
f\equiv 0
.

\Box

Proof
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}
be a potential that induces a feasible generalized differential flow. Let
v_{0}\in V
be the sink of the anti-arborescence
F
. We prove that
\max_{v\in V}\varphi_{v}=\varphi_{v_{0}}
, the argument to see that
\min_{v\in V}\varphi_{v}=\varphi_{v_{0}}
is identical. Let
v_{1}\in\operatorname{argmax}_{v\in V}\varphi_{v}
, i. e.
\varphi_{v_{1}}\geq\varphi_{w}
for all
w\in V
. If
\varphi_{v_{1}}=\varphi_{v_{0}}
, then we are done. Otherwise, in particular
v_{1}\neq v_{0}
and, since
v_{0}
is the unique sink, there exists
v_{2}\in V
with an edge
(v_{1},v_{2})\in T
. By the definition of a feasible generalized differential flow and by maximality of
\varphi_{v_{1}}
, it follows

0=\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{v_{1}\}}\underbrace{\underbrace{b_{v_{1}w}}_{\geq 0}\underbrace{(\varphi_{w}-\varphi_{v_{1}})}_{\leq 0}}_{\leq 0}.

Hence, in particular
b_{v_{1}v_{2}}(\varphi_{v_{2}}-\varphi_{v_{1}})=0
. Since furthermore
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E
, we have that
b_{v_{1}v_{2}}>0
which implies that
\varphi_{v_{2}}=\varphi_{v_{1}}>\varphi_{v_{0}}
and thus in particular
v_{2}\neq v_{0}
. Hence,
v_{2}\in\operatorname{argmax}_{v\in V}\varphi_{v}
and we can apply the same argument to find a vertex
v_{3}
with
\varphi_{v_{2}}=\varphi_{v_{3}}
. Since
T
is an anti-arborescence and we only traverse edges in
T
in the direction of the sink, we never reach the same vertex twice. By iterating this argument, we therefore finally obtain that
\varphi_{v_{1}}=\varphi_{v_{2}}=\varphi_{v_{3}}=\dotsb=\varphi_{v_{0}}
, a contradiction.

\Box

The above lemma can be used to derive a sufficient condition for extremality of a differential flow
f\in Q_{f}(G)
based on the existence of an
\alpha
-tree
F
with one additional property (which in particular excludes the situation encountered in
Example

9
).

Theorem 24

Let
G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
be a network,
f\in Q_{f}(G)
and
F=(E_{F},V_{F})
an
\alpha
-tree in
G
that conforms with
f
. Furthermore, let
F
be such that every connected component of the graph
(V,E_{F})
contains at most one vertex which is active in
F
. Then,
f
is extremal.

\Box

Proof
.

In order to show that
f
is extremal, we need to prove that no non-zero differential flow
g
exists such that
f\pm g\in Q_{f}(G)
(see
Remark

20
). Suppose otherwise and let
\varphi\in\mathds{R}^{V}
be a potential that induces
g
. Denote by
\mathcal{S}
the set of connected components of the graph
(V,E_{F})
.

By our assumption, every connected component
S
contains at most one vertex which is active in
F
. Denote this vertex (if it exists) by
v_{S}
. For two connected components
S,T\in\mathcal{S}
, we define the value
b^{\prime}_{ST}
as follows (we write
\delta(S):=\bigcup_{v\in S}\delta(v)
for any
S\subset V
):

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{ST}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\sum_{e\in\delta(v_{S})\cap\delta(T)}b_{e}&\text{if $S$ contains a vertex $v_{S}$ active in $F$}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Note that it may be the case that
b^{\prime}_{ST}\neq b^{\prime}_{TS}
, in particular one of the two may be
0
(or both), but in any case
b^{\prime}\geq 0
since
b>0
.

Let
\alpha_{F}
be a vertex-orientation map for
F
and denote by
F^{\prime}
the set of pairs of connected components for which there exists an active vertex in the first component which is mapped by
\alpha_{F}
to an edge connecting it with the second component:

\displaystyle F^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\left\{(S,T)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\nonscript\>\middle|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}\exists(v,w)\in\alpha_{F}(V_{F}\cap S):(v,w)\in(S\times T)\cup(T\times S)\right\}

By the definition of
\alpha
-trees, the set
E_{F}\cup\alpha_{F}(V_{F})
does not contain an undirected cycle and since
F
is maximal, it spans
V
. This implies that the graph
(\mathcal{S},F^{\prime})
is weakly connected and does not contain a cycle, either. Furthermore, since every connected component
S\in\mathcal{S}
contains at most one active vertex, there is at most one
T\in\mathcal{S}
such that
(S,T)\in F^{\prime}
for every
S\in\mathcal{S}
. The graph
(\mathcal{S},F^{\prime})
is thus an anti-arborescence.

Returning to the differential flow
g
,
Remark

20
implies for any
v,w\in V
with
(v,w)\in E_{F}
that
g_{vw}=0
and hence
\varphi_{v}=\varphi_{w}
.
Let
\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
be defined by
\varphi^{\prime}_{S}:=\varphi_{v}
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
v\in S
(note that this is well-defined, since
\varphi_{v}=\varphi_{w}
for any
S\in\mathcal{S}
and
v,w\in S
).
Now, observe that
\varphi^{\prime}
induces a generalized differential flow on the set
\mathcal{S}
with respect to
b^{\prime}
. Thus, for all
S\in\mathcal{S}
, one of the following two statements holds:

•

Either
S
contains no active vertex and then
b^{\prime}_{ST}=0
for all
T\in\mathcal{S}
and hence
\sum_{T\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{S\}}b^{\prime}_{ST}(\varphi_{T}-\varphi_{S})=0
, or

•

S
contains exactly one active vertex
v_{S}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{S\}}b^{\prime}_{ST}(\varphi_{T}-\varphi_{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{S\}}\sum_{e\in\delta(v_{S})\cap\delta(T)}b_{e}(\varphi_{T}-\varphi_{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{S\}}\left(\ \quad\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\\
N^{\text{out}}(v_{S})\cap T\end{subarray}}}\ b_{v_{S}w}(\varphi_{w}-\varphi_{v_{S}})-\ \sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\\
N^{\text{in}}(v_{S})\cap T\end{subarray}}}\ b_{wv_{S}}(\varphi_{v_{S}}-\varphi_{w})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in N^{\text{out}}(v_{S})}b_{v_{S}w}(\varphi_{w}-\varphi_{v_{S}})-\sum_{w\in N^{\text{in}}(v_{S})}b_{wv_{S}}(\varphi_{v_{S}}-\varphi_{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in N^{\text{out}}(v_{S})}g_{v_{S}w}-\sum_{w\in N^{\text{in}}(v_{S})}g_{wv_{S}}=0

where the last equality follows from
Remark

20
.

Hence, the generalized differential flow induced by
\varphi^{\prime}
is feasible.

Let
E^{\prime}:=\{(S,T)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\nonscript\>|\allowbreak\nonscript\>\mathopen{}S\neq T\text{ and }b^{\prime}_{ST}>0\}
and observe that
F^{\prime}\subset E^{\prime}
, since the definition of
F^{\prime}
captures exactly those pairs
(S,T)
for which the first case in the definition of
b^{\prime}
applies.
Hence, the anti-arborescence
(\mathcal{S},F^{\prime})
is a subgraph of
(\mathcal{S},E^{\prime})
and we now obtain from
Lemma

23
that there exists
\varphi_{0}\in\mathds{R}
such that
\varphi^{\prime}\equiv\varphi_{0}
and thus
\varphi\equiv\varphi_{0}
which implies that
g\equiv 0
.

\Box

Using
Theorem

24
, we can prove another sufficient condition, which excludes a different aspect of
Example

. In particular, this also implies that every network with maximal degree
\leq 2
is non-degenerate, providing an alternative proof for

,

, and

.

Theorem 25

Let
G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
be a network,
f\in Q_{f}(G)
and
F=(E_{F},V_{F})
an
\alpha
-tree in
G
that conforms with
f
. Furthermore, let
F
be such that there is at most one
v\in V_{F}
with
deg(v)\geq 3
. Then,
f
is extremal.

\Box

Proof
.

We prove the statement by induction over the number
|V_{F}|
of active vertices in
F
. If
|V_{F}|=0
, i. e. there is no vertex active in
F
, then
(V,E_{F})
is in fact a spanning tree and, since any potential
\varphi
that induces a differential flow
g
with
f\pm g\in Q_{f}(G)
has to be constant within connected components of
(V,E_{F})
,
f
is extremal.

Now, let
|V_{F}|=k
. We distinguish two cases:
First, assume that
V_{F}
contains no vertex of degree at most
2
that is incident with an active edge. Then, for every active vertex
v
, one of the following holds with respect to the connected component
S_{v}
of the graph
(V,E_{F})
that contains
v
:

a)

v
is not incident with an active edge and hence
S_{v}
is a singleton, or

b)

v
is incident with an active edge, which implies that
deg(v)\geq 3
and, as there can be only one such vertex,
v
is the only active vertex in
S_{v}
.

In both cases,
v
is the only active vertex in
S_{v}
and hence every connected component of
(V,E_{F})
contains at most one active vertex. Thus,
F
satisfies the conditions of
Theorem

24
and
f
is extremal.

Now, assume that
V_{F}
does contain a vertex
v
of degree at most
2
that is incident with an active edge
e^{*}\in E_{F}
and let
\alpha_{F}:V_{F}\rightarrow E\setminus E_{F}
be a vertex-orientation map for
F
.

Then
e^{*}\in E_{F}
and
\alpha_{F}(v)\in E\setminus E_{F}
are the only edges incident with
v
.
Assume w. l. o. g. that both are oriented away from
v
and let
s,t\in V
be such that
\alpha_{F}(v)=(v,t)
and
e^{*}=(v,s)
.
Let
p^{*}_{v}:=\sum_{e\in\delta^{\text{out}}(v)}f_{e}-\sum_{e\in\delta^{\text{in}}(v)}f_{e}=f_{vt}+f_{vs}
.

Since
(v,s)
and
v
are active, it follows
f_{vs}\in\{f^{+}_{vs},f^{-}_{vs}\}
and
p^{*}_{v}\in\{p^{+}_{v},p^{-}_{v}\}
.
Thus, for every
f^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}\in Q_{f}(G)
such that
f
is a convex combination of
f^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime\prime}
, it must hold that
f^{\prime}_{vs}=f^{\prime\prime}_{vs}=f_{vs}
and
f^{\prime}_{vs}+f^{\prime}_{vt}=f^{\prime\prime}_{vs}+f^{\prime\prime}_{vt}=f_{vs}+f_{vt}
. Together, this also implies
f^{\prime}_{vt}=f^{\prime\prime}_{vt}=p^{*}_{v}-f_{vs}
.

Let
{\bar{f}}^{\prime}
be given by

{\bar{f}}^{\prime}_{e}=\begin{cases}(f_{vt},f_{vt})&\text{if }e=(v,t)\\
{\bar{f}}_{e}&\text{else}\end{cases}

and define
H:=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}}^{\prime})
. Then, we can conclude that

is extremal in
Q_{f}(G)
if and only if

is extremal in
Q_{f}(H)
.

But in
H
,

is an
\alpha
-tree that conforms to

. Since
|V_{F^{\prime}}|<|V_{F}|
, we can conclude by induction that

is extremal in
Q_{f}(H)
and therefore also in
Q_{f}(G)
.

\Box

4.2
Non-degenerateness is
\mathbb{NP}
-hard

In Section
3
, we have identified all graphs that are non-degenerate, regardless of the values
b
,
{\bar{p}}
, and
{\bar{f}}
in a network derived from those graphs. Section
4.1
collects some sufficent conditions for when a given
\alpha
-tree definitely identifies an extremal point.

We conclude the paper by noting that in general it is
\mathbb{NP}
-hard (see
[
GJ79
]
) to determine whether in a given network every
\alpha
-tree identifies an extremal point.

Theorem 26

Given a network
G=(V,E,b,{\bar{p}},{\bar{f}})
, it is
\mathbb{NP}
-complete to decide whether there exists and
\alpha
-tree
F
and a non-extremal point
f\in Q_{f}(G)
that conforms with
F
.

\Box

Proof
.

We first settle membership in
\mathbb{NP}
: Suppose that there exists a non-extremal solution
f\in Q_{f}(G)
that conforms to the
\alpha
-tree
F=(E_{F},V_{F})
. Let
P
denote the set of vertices of
Q_{f}(G)
that are contained in the minimal face of
Q_{f}(G)
which contains
f
and let
f^{\prime}:=\nicefrac{{1}}{{|P|}}\sum_{p\in P}p
. Then,
f^{\prime}
conforms to
F
as well and is not extremal (since
|P|\geq 2
). Given a certificate in the form of
f^{\prime}
,
F
and a corresponding vertex-orientation map
\alpha_{F}:V_{F}\rightarrow E\setminus E_{F}
(which are all polynomial in size), we can easily check that
F
is indeed an
\alpha
-tree and that
f^{\prime}
conforms with
F

5
Conclusion and Outlook

We have investigated the polytope of
differential flows
in a network, which captures a common way of representing electrical power flows in
Energy System Optimization
. A characterization of extremal points in terms of so-called
\alpha
-trees has been derived, similar to the characterization of extremal points in classical network flow problems by spanning trees of edges with non-zero flow. It is proven that every extremal solution can be represented by a corresponding
\alpha
-tree (
Theorem

8
) and that the reverse is also true in almost all cases. In particular, it is true regardless of the choice of network parameters for networks on cactus graphs (
Theorem

18
). Finally, for a specific given network, we showed that it is
\mathbb{NP}
-hard to decide whether every feasible
\alpha
-tree indeed corresponds to an extremal solution (
Theorem

26
).

Our work may raise new theoretical questions, most notably in the area between networks on cactus graphs, which are always non-degenerate, and our hardness result: Can other classes of networks be identified (e.g., based on the elasticity vector
b
) for which non-degenerateness can always be guaranteed?

From a practical perspective in the context of power networks, our work might open two interesting directions of future work:

a)

Algorithmically, one famous application of the characterization of extremal points in network flow problems is the
Network Simplex Algorithm
, which allows for an extremely efficient solution of associated optimization problems. Since
Theorem

8
guarantees that every extremal differential flow can be represented by an
\alpha
-tree, a similar approach might be possible for optimization problems over differential flows, as they widely appear in Energy System Optimization.

b)

Structurally, differential flows which might not be feasible (see
Definition

2
) can be interpreted as power flows which are electrically feasible, but violate edge and/or vertex capacity constraints. This might be a useful concept in the study of
Capacity Expansion
problems, which investigate the optimal increase in generation and network capacities to make given energy systems feasible.
