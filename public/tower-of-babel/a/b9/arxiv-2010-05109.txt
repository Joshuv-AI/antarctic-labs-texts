AEGD: Adaptive Gradient Descent with Energy Thanks: Submitted to the editors 5/31/2021.
Hailiang Liu, Xuping Tian

Abstract
We propose AEGD, a new algorithm for first-order gradient-based optimization of non-convex objective functions, based on a dynamically updated ‘energy’ variable. The method is shown to be unconditionally energy stable, irrespective of the step size. We prove energy-dependent convergence rates of AEGD for both non-convex and convex objectives, which for a suitably small step size recovers desired convergence rates for the batch gradient descent. We also provide an energy-dependent bound on the stationary convergence of AEGD in the stochastic non-convex setting. The method is straightforward to implement and requires little tuning of hyper-parameters. Experimental results demonstrate that AEGD works well for a large variety of optimization problems: it is robust with respect to initial data, capable of making rapid initial progress. The stochastic AEGD shows comparable and often better generalization performance than SGD with momentum for deep neural networks. The code is available at https://github.com/txping/AEGD .

Introduction

From a mathematical perspective, training neural networks is a high-dimensional non-convex optimization problem, and the dynamics of the training process is incredibly complicated. Despite this, stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
45
]
and its variants have proven to be extremely effective for training neural networks in practice (see, for example,
[
9
,
15
]
). Such stochastic training often reduces to solving the following unconstrained minimization problem

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\theta)

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

is a finite-sum function, defined as

f(\theta):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(\theta)
,
where
f_{i}(\theta):=L(F(x_{i};\theta);y_{i})
is the loss of a given machine learning (ML) model
F(\cdot;\theta)
on the training data
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
, parametrized by
\theta
. For many practical applications,
f(\theta)
is highly non-convex, and
F(\cdot;\theta)
is chosen among deep neural networks (DNNs), known for their superior performance across various tasks. These deep models are heavily over-parametrized and require large amounts of training data. Thus, both
m
and the dimension
n
of
\theta

can scale up to millions or even billions. These complications pose serious computational challenges.

Gradient descent (GD) or its variants
are the method of choice for
solving (
1
). There are two variations of gradient decent, which differ in how much data we use to compute the gradient of the objective function. They are fully gradient decent, aka batch GD, and SGD. For large dataset, SGD is usually much faster by performing a parameter update for each training example.
The GD method dates back to Cauchy (1847), and it updates
\theta
according to:

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\nabla f(\theta_{k}),

(2)

where
\eta>0
is the step size (called the learning rate in ML). GD has advantages of easy implementation and being fast for well-conditioned and strongly convex objectives, independent of the dimension of the underlying problem
[
37
]
. However, GD typically limits the step size to ensure numerical stability.
The step limitation issue can be largely resolved by implicit updates
[
54
]
,

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\nabla f(\theta_{k+1}).

(3)

This can be rewritten in the form of the celebrated proximal point algorithm (PPA)
[
46
]
:

\theta_{k+1}={\rm argmin}_{\theta}\left\{f(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|\theta-\theta_{k}\|^{2}\right\}.

(4)

Such method has the advantage of being monotonically decreasing, which is guaranteed for any step size
\eta>0
. However, for non-quadratic function
f
, one would have to solve it by iteration, for example by the backward Euler method
[
59
]
. A natural question is:

Can we adapt the GD (
2
) and improve stability with arbitrary step size as in (
3
) but still easy to implement?

We answer this question by proposing AEGD, a new method for efficient optimization that only requires first-order gradients. At its core, an auxiliary energy variable is used to adjust the step size at each update. The name AEGD is derived from the adaptive GD method with energy. This method is motivated by the energy dissipation structure hidden in the GD method with momentum (GDM)
[
41
]
. We would like to emphasize that our AEGD features both a global and an element-wise formulation, while by empirical evidence, the latter appears to perform better in most cases.

1.1
Main Contributions

To our best knowledge, AEGD is the first algorithm that uses the energy update to stabilize GD in the optimization of non-convex objective functions. Meanwhile we show that AEGD still feathers the expected convergence rates for first-order gradient-based methods including GD.
Some major benefits of AEGD are summarized below:

•

It is shown to be unconditionally energy stable, irrespective of the step size.

•

It allows a larger base step size than GD.

•

It converges fast even for objective functions that have a large condition number.

•

It is robust with respect to initial data.

•

It has visible advantages over the GD algorithm with momentum.

Most of the above advantages are kept for the stochastic AEGD, which will be discussed in Section 4. In particular, our numerical results also show that the stochastic AEGD has the potential for training neural networks with better generalization, at least on some benchmark machine learning tasks.

2
AEGD and Algorithm

It is known one can accelerate gradients for certain objective functions by momentum, say by the HB scheme
[
41
]
of form

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\nabla f(\theta_{k})+\mu(\theta_{k}-\theta^{k-1}),

where
0<\mu<1
is a constant. This upon passing limit
\eta\to 0
and
\mu\to 1
with
\alpha=\frac{\eta}{(1-\mu)^{2}}
kept fixed leads to

\displaystyle\alpha\ddot{\theta}+\dot{\theta}=-\nabla f,\quad\alpha>0.

(5)

The faster convergence and reduced oscillation may be expected from the perspective that the system is driven by the energy dissipation law
\dot{E}=-|\dot{\theta}|^{2}\leq 0
with the energy
E(t)=f(\theta(t))+\frac{\alpha}{2}|\dot{\theta}(t)|^{2}
. Motivated by this energy dissipation structure, we define
g(\theta)=\sqrt{f(\theta)+c}
with
c
chosen so that
f(\theta)+c>0
. Set
r=g(\theta)
, then
r^{2}
plays the role of energy and

\nabla f=2r\nabla g,\quad r=g(\theta).

This transformation leads to an augmented system

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}=-2r\nabla g(\theta),

(6a)

\displaystyle\dot{r}=\nabla g\cdot\dot{\theta}.

(6b)

It allows the dissipation of the quadratic energy

\frac{d}{dt}(r^{2})=-|\dot{\theta}|^{2}

in place of the dissipation of nonlinear loss function:
\frac{d}{dt}f(\theta)=-|\dot{\theta}|^{2}
. Note also that the above transformation with
c
taken as constant is linked back to the first order gradient flow
\dot{\theta}=-\nabla f
, instead of the inertial equation (
5
). Based on (
6
) we introduce the following update rule:

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-2\eta r_{k+1}\nabla g(\theta_{k}),\quad k=0,1,2,\cdots,

(7a)

\displaystyle r_{k+1}-r_{k}=\nabla g(\theta_{k})\cdot(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}).

(7b)

This is actually a linear algorithm
since

\displaystyle r_{k+1}=\frac{r_{k}}{1+2\eta|\nabla g(\theta_{k})|^{2}},\quad r_{0}=\sqrt{f(\theta_{0})+c},

(8a)

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-2\eta r_{k+1}\nabla g(\theta_{k}),\quad k=0,1,2,\cdots,

(8b)

which are easy to implement. In order to allow the use of different learning rate in each coordinate, we also propose an element-wise AEGD, set forth as follows:

\displaystyle r_{k+1,i}=\frac{r_{k,i}}{1+2\eta(\partial_{i}g(\theta_{k}))^{2}},\quad i\in[n],\quad r_{0,i}=\sqrt{f(\theta_{0})+c},

(9a)

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1,i}=\theta_{k,i}-2\eta r_{k+1,i}\partial_{i}g(\theta_{k}).\quad k=0,1,2,\cdots

(9b)

This also implies

\displaystyle r_{k+1,i}-r_{k,i}=\partial_{i}g(\theta_{k})(\theta_{k+1,i}-\theta_{k,i}),\quad i\in[n].

(10)

Remark 1
.

The essential idea behind (
6
) is the so called invariant energy quadratizaton (IEQ) strategy, originally introduced for developing linear and unconditionally energy stable schemes for gradient flows in the form of partial differential equations
[
58
,
63
]
. One may wonder whether
r=(f+c)^{\alpha}
is admissible for building similar algorithms for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
. To see this, we write the corresponding augmented system as

\dot{\theta}=-\alpha^{-1}r^{1/\alpha-1}\nabla g(\theta),\quad\dot{r}=\nabla g\cdot\dot{\theta}.

A similar implicit- explicit discretization can yield a linear update for
r
if and only if

\frac{1}{\alpha}-1=1\Leftrightarrow\alpha=\frac{1}{2}.

This explains why energy quadratization has been used in the PDE community to name such strategy.

3
Theoretical Results

In this section, we will show AEGD is unconditionally energy stable, irrespective of the step size
\eta
, and obtain different convergence rates for non-convex, convex, and strongly convex functions. We say
f
is
\alpha
-strongly convex if for all
u
and
v
we have
f(u)\geq f(v)+\langle\nabla f(v),u-v\rangle+\alpha\|u-v\|^{2}/2
;
f
is
L
-smooth if
\|\nabla^{2}f(u)\|\leq L
for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For convex (
\alpha=0
) and
L
-smooth functions, it is known that the convergence rate
O(1/k)
for GD of form (
2
) is guaranteed if
\eta\leq\frac{1}{L}

[
36
]
.
This may suggest very small step-sizes in practice, which is a condition that may be violated in more complicated scenarios. In contrast, AEGD is energy stable for any
\eta>0
(see Theorem
2
), and converges for a large range of
\eta
(see Theorem
4
).

3.1
Unconditional energy stability

Theorem 2
.

(Energy stability and convergence) Consider

\min\{f(\theta),\;\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

where
f(\theta)
is differentiable and bounded from below so that
f(\theta)+c>0
for some
c>0
.
Then

(i) AEGD (
8
) is unconditionally energy stable in the sense that for any step size
\eta>0
,

r_{k+1}^{2}=r^{2}_{k}-(r_{k+1}-r_{k})^{2}-\eta^{-1}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2},

(11)

r_{k}
is strictly decreasing and convergent with
r_{k}\to r^{*}
as
k\to\infty
, and also

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|=0,\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j}\|^{2}\leq\eta(r^{2}_{0}-(r^{*})^{2}).

(12)

(ii) AEGD (
9
) is unconditionally energy stable in the sense that for any step size
\eta>0
,

r^{2}_{k+1,i}=r^{2}_{k,i}-(r_{k+1,i}-r_{k,i})^{2}-\eta^{-1}(\theta_{k+1,i}-\theta_{k,i})^{2},\quad i\in[n],

(13)

r_{k,i}
is strictly decreasing and convergent with
r_{k,i}\to r_{i}^{*}
as
k\to\infty
, and also

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|=0,\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j}\|^{2}\leq\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r^{2}_{0,i}-(r^{*}_{i})^{2}).

(14)

Proof.

(i) We use the two equations in (
7
) to derive

2r_{k+1}(r_{k+1}-r_{k})=2r_{k+1}\nabla g(\theta_{k})\cdot(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k})=-4\eta r_{k+1}\|\nabla g(\theta_{k})\|^{2}=-\frac{1}{\eta}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}.

Upon rewriting with
2b(b-a)=b^{2}-a^{2}+(b-a)^{2}
we obtain equality (
11
). From this we see that
r^{2}_{k}
is monotonically decreasing (also bounded below), therefore convergent; so does
r_{k}
since
r_{k}\geq 0
. Summation of (
11
) over
k
from
0,1,\cdots
yields (
12
). The proof of (ii) is entirely similar.

Remark 3
.

It is worth pointing out that this theorem does not require the function
f
satisfy L-smoothness or convexity assumption. The result asserts that the unconditional energy stability featured by AEGD also implies convergence of
\{r_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
for any
\eta>0
, and the sequence
\{\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|\}_{k\geq 0}
converges to zero at a rate of at least
1/\sqrt{k}
. But this is not sufficient—at least in general—to prove convergence of the sequence
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, when no further information is available about this sequence.

3.2
Convergence and convergence rates

To understand the convergence behavior of AEGD (
8
), we reformulate it as

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f(\theta_{k}),\quad\eta_{k}:=\eta\frac{r_{k+1}}{g(\theta_{k})}.

(15)

Note that using
L
-smoothness of
f
and (
15
) we have

\displaystyle f(\theta_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(\theta_{k})-\left(\frac{1}{\eta_{k}}-\frac{L}{2}\right)\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}<f(\theta_{k}),

when
\eta_{k}
gets smaller so that
\eta_{k}<2/L
. Since
r_{k}
is decreasing, after finite number of iterations
\eta_{j}
can be ensured (by choosing
\eta
to be suitably small if necessary) to fall below
2/L
under which
f(\theta_{j})
turns into a strictly decreasing sequence, hence convergent. To ensure the convergence of
\{\theta_{k}\}
, one needs further info on the geometrical property of the objective function, such as convexity, or the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property. The KL property at a point describes how the objective function can be made sharp through a concave mapping near that point. This property characterizes a rich function class, and often considered as a structural assumption in general nonconvex optimization (see
[
3
]
for a detailed account on this property and its applications). In the present work we restrict to a special case of KL, called Polyak-Lojasiewics (PL) property:
f
is PL if there exists
\mu>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(\theta)\|^{2}\geq\mu(f(\theta)-f^{*}),

(16)

where we assume that
\{\theta,\min f(\theta)=f^{*}\}
is not empty. One such example
f(x)=x^{2}+3sin^{2}(x)
is non-convex, yet satisfying the PL inequality with
f^{*}=0
and
\mu=1/32
.

In Theorem
4
below, we present convergence rates of AEGD in three different cases: nonconvex with the PL property, convex and strongly convex. For general
\eta
, convergence rates can depend on the behavior of
r_{k}
, which is part of the solution to the AEGD algorithm. Therefore, we present a hybrid result of convergence rates combining both
k
and
r_{k}
(a posterior estimates).

Theorem 4
.

(Convergence rates)
Suppose
f
is differentiable and bounded from below.
Let
\theta_{k}
be the
k
-th iterate generated by AEGD (
15
), then

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla g(\theta_{j})\|^{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{f(\theta_{0})+c}}{2\eta kr_{k}}.

We have convergence rates in three distinct cases:

(i)
f
is PL and
L
-smooth with a minimizer
\theta^{*}
. If
\max_{k_{0}\leq j<k}\eta_{j}\leq 1/L
for some
k_{0}\geq 0
, then
\{\theta_{k}\}
is convergent. Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{2\mu}}\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})-f(\theta^{*})},

(17)

\displaystyle f(\theta_{k})-f(\theta^{*})\leq e^{-c_{0}(k-k_{0})r_{k}}(f(\theta_{k_{0}})-f(\theta^{*})),\quad c_{0}:=\frac{\mu\eta}{\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})+c}}.

(ii)
f
is convex and
L
-smooth with a global minimizer
\theta^{*}
. If
\max_{k_{0}\leq j<k}\eta_{j}\leq 1/L
for some
k_{0}\geq 0
, then

f(\theta_{k})-f(\theta^{*})\leq\frac{c_{1}\|\theta_{k_{0}}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{2(k-k_{0})r_{k}},\;c_{1}:=\frac{\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})+c}}{\eta}.

(iii)
f
is
\alpha
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth with the global minimizer
\theta^{*}
. Assume that
\max_{k_{0}\leq j<k}\eta_{j}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha+L}
for some
k_{0}\geq 0
, then

\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\|\leq e^{-c_{2}(k-k_{0})r_{k}}\|\theta_{k_{0}}-\theta^{*}\|,\quad c_{2}:=\frac{\alpha\eta}{\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})+c}}.

See Appendix A.1 for the proof.

Remark 5
.

The gradient estimate in Theorem
4
when using
g(\theta)=\sqrt{f(\theta)+c}
gives

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla f(\theta_{j})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2(f(\theta_{0})+c)^{\frac{1}{2}}(F_{k}+c)}{\eta kr_{k}},\quad F_{k}=max_{j<k}f(\theta_{j}).

For this estimate, neither
L
-smoothness nor step size restriction is needed. This is in sharp contrast to the classical result for GD (see pages 29-31 in
[
36
]
).

Remark 6
.

Regarding the convergence rates, a series of remarks are in order.

1.

The convergence rate in (i) may be extended to the case when
f
features the more general KL property; see
[
3
,
2
]
for relevant techniques in establishing convergence rates for proximal and other gradient methods.

2.

If
r^{*}>0
, which is the case to be shown in Lemma
8
for
\eta<\tau
,
r_{k}
on the right of the three estimates in Theorem
4
can all be replaced by
r^{*}(<r_{k})
, hence (i) guarantees the linear convergence rate when
f
is PL;
and (ii) recovers the usual sublinear rate
O(\frac{1}{k})
; while with strict convexity, (iii) gives the usual linear convergence rate.

3.

It is clear that all results are valid as long as
r_{k}\to 0
slower than
1/k
. Our numerical tests indicate that for each objective function
f
there exists a sharp threshold
\tilde{\eta}
in the sense that convergence is ensured if
\eta\leq\tilde{\eta}
. However, identifying
\tilde{\eta}
appears to be a challenging task in theory.

Remark 7
.

Regarding the base step size
\eta
, we make further remarks.

1.

Empirical evidence (see Figure
2
(c)) shows that there exists a threshold index
J
so that
\eta_{j}
turns to decrease for
j>J
. Hence the assumptions in (i)-(iii) are reasonable and readily met.

2.

Our results suggest that decaying
\eta
at a later stage (say
k\geq k_{0}
for some
k_{0}
) to ensure the sufficient conditions in Theorem
4
can help to achieve all-time good performance of the AEGD algorithm. This comment applies to the element-wise AEGD as well (see Theorem
10
).

3.3
Behavior of the energy

Since
r_{k}
is strictly decreasing and
g(\theta_{k})
is positive and bounded from below and above, their relative ratio
\eta_{k}
essentially depends on the behavior of
r_{k}
. On the other hand, as
\eta\to 0
, the numerical solution
(\theta_{k},r_{k})
may be shown to converge to the solution of the ODE system

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}=-2r\nabla g,\quad\dot{r}=\nabla g\cdot\dot{\theta}

(18)

at
t_{k}=k\eta
, subject to initial data
\theta(0)=\theta_{0},r(0)=g(\theta_{0})
.
For this system the level set
r(t)-g(\theta(t))=0
is invariant for all time. Hence for a fixed but suitably small
\eta
, starting from
(\theta_{0},g(\theta_{0}))
, the limit of
(\theta_{k},r_{k})
as
k\to\infty
must be approaching to
(\theta^{*},g(\theta^{*}))
, that is

r^{*}=g(\theta^{*})>0.

A natural question is whether a threshold for the base step size
\eta
can be identified so that we will still have
r^{*}>0
for any
\eta<\tau
. Indeed, we are able to obtain a sufficient condition to ensure this.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose
f
is
L
-smooth, bounded from below by
f(\theta^{*})
and we have
\max\|\nabla f(\theta)\|\leq G_{\infty}
, then
g
is
L_{g}
-smooth with

g\geq g(\theta^{*})=\sqrt{f(\theta^{*})+c},\quad L_{g}=\frac{1}{2g(\theta^{*})}\left(L+\frac{G_{\infty}^{2}}{2g^{2}(\theta^{*})}\right).

Consider AEGD (
8
), then

r_{k}>r^{*}>g(\theta^{*})(1-\eta/\tau),\quad\tau:=\frac{2g(\theta^{*})}{L_{g}(g(\theta_{0}))^{2}}.

This implies that
r^{*}>0
if
\eta\leq\tau
.

Proof.

For any
x,y\in\{\theta_{k}\}_{j=0}^{T}
we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla g(x)-\nabla g(y)\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigg\|\frac{\nabla f(x)(g(y)-g(x))}{g(x)g(y)}+\frac{\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)}{g(y)}\bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{G_{\infty}}{2(g(\theta^{*}))^{2}}|g(y)-g(x)|+\frac{1}{2g(\theta^{*})}\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq L_{g}\|x-y\|.

Such
L_{g}
-smoothness of
g
implies that

\displaystyle g(\theta_{j+1})

\displaystyle\leq g(\theta_{j})+\nabla g(\theta_{j})\cdot(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})+\frac{L_{g}}{2}\|\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=g(\theta_{j})+r_{j+1}-r_{j}+\frac{L_{g}}{2}\|\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j}\|^{2}.

Take a summation over
j
from
0
to
k-1
so that

\displaystyle g(\theta_{k})-g(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle\leq r_{k}-r_{0}+\frac{L_{g}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=r_{k}-r_{0}+\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(r^{2}_{j}-r^{2}_{j+1}-(r_{j+1}-r_{j})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=r_{k}-r_{0}+\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}\left(r^{2}_{0}-r^{2}_{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(r_{j+1}-r_{j})^{2}\right),

where (
11
) was used. Using
r_{0}=g(\theta_{0})
and
g(\theta_{k})\geq g(\theta^{*})
, we have for any
k
,

\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(r_{j+1}-r_{j})^{2}+\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}r^{2}_{k}-r_{k}+g(\theta^{*})-\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}(g(\theta_{0}))^{2}\leq 0.

Passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
, we also have

\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(r_{j+1}-r_{j})^{2}+\frac{L_{g}\eta}{2}(r^{*})^{2}-r^{*}+g(\theta^{*})(1-\eta/\tau)\leq 0.

Hence

r^{*}>g(\theta^{*})(1-\eta/\tau).

Remark 9
.

1. Note that
\eta<\tau
is only a sufficient condition, not necessary for
r^{*}>0
to surely happen.

2. One may take a suitably large
\eta
to gain initial rapid progress, and adjust
\eta
at a later stage at
k=k_{0}
. A similar argument shows that
r^{*}>0
is ensured by
\eta<\tau_{1}
for
k\geq k_{0}
,

\eta<\tau_{1}:=\frac{2(g(\theta^{*})+r_{k_{0}}-g(\theta_{k_{0}}))}{L_{g}r^{2}_{k_{0}}}.

(19)

As we expected, for small
\eta
, AEGD features same convergence rates as GD does, since the rates are essentially depending on the local geometry of
f
near
\theta^{*}
.

3.4
Convergence results for the element-wise AEGD

Similar results also hold for the element-wise AEGD (
9
), although analysis is more involved. With the notation

\eta_{ij}:=\eta r_{j+1,i}/g(\theta_{j}),\;i\in[n],\quad j=0,1,2,\cdots,

we now present the main result for (
9
) in the following.

Theorem 10
.

(Convergence rates)
Suppose
f
is differentiable and bounded from below.
Let
\theta_{k}
be the
k
-th iterate generated by the AEGD (
9
), then

\min_{j<k}(\partial_{i}g(\theta_{j}))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(\partial_{i}g(\theta_{j}))^{2}\leq\cfrac{\sqrt{f(\theta_{0})+c}}{2\eta kr_{k,i}},\quad i\in[n].

We have convergence rates in three distinct cases:

(i)
f
is PL and
L
-smooth with a minimizer
\theta^{*}
. If
\max_{k_{0}\leq j\leq k}\eta_{ij}\leq\frac{1}{L}
for
i\in[n]
and some
k_{0}\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle f(\theta_{k})-f(\theta^{*})\leq e^{-c_{0}(k-k_{0})\min_{i}r_{k,i}}(f(\theta_{k_{0}})-f(\theta^{*})),\quad c_{0}:=\frac{\mu\eta}{\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})+c}}.

(ii)
f
is convex and
L
-smooth with a global minimizer
\theta^{*}
. If
\max_{k_{0}\leq j<k}\eta_{ij}\leq\frac{1}{L}
for
i\in[n]
and some

f(\theta_{k})-f(\theta^{*})\leq\frac{c_{1}\max_{k_{0}\leq j<k}\|\theta_{j}-\theta^{*}\|^{2}}{(k-k_{0})\min_{i}r_{k,i}},\;c_{1}:=\frac{\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})+c}}{\eta}.

(iii)
f
is
\alpha-
strongly convex and
L
-smooth with the global minimizer
\theta^{*}
. Assume that

\max_{k_{0}\leq j<k}\eta_{ij}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha+L}
for
i\in[n]
and some
k_{0}\geq 0
,
then

\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\|\leq e^{-c_{2}(k-k_{0})\min_{i}r_{k,i}}\|\theta_{k_{0}}-\theta^{*}\|,\quad c_{2}:=\frac{\alpha\eta}{\sqrt{f(\theta_{k_{0}})+c})}.

See Appendix A.3 for the proof.

Remark 11
.

The above gradient estimate when using
g(\theta)=\sqrt{f(\theta)+c}

leads to

\min_{j<k}(\partial_{i}f(\theta_{j}))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(\partial_{i}f(\theta_{j}))^{2}\leq\cfrac{2(f(\theta_{0})+c)^{\frac{1}{2}}(F_{k}+c)}{\eta kr_{k,i}},\quad F_{k}=max_{j<k}f(\theta_{j}).

As argued above, our convergence rates in Theorem
10
depend also on the behavior of
r_{k,i}
, about which we have the following result.

Lemma 12
.

Under the same assumptions as in Lemma
8
, for AEGD (
9
) we have for
i\in[n]
,

r_{k,i}>r_{i}^{*}>g(\theta^{*})(1-\eta/\tilde{\tau}),\quad\tilde{\tau}:=\frac{2g(\theta^{*})}{nL_{g}(g(\theta_{0}))^{2}}.

This implies that

if
\eta<\tilde{\tau}
.

We include a proof in Appendix A.2.

Stochastic AEGD

This section presents a stochastic AEGD algorithm for the unconstrained finite-sum optimization problem:

\min_{\theta}\left\{f(\theta)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(\theta)\right\}.

(20)

We use
\theta^{*}
to denote a minimizer of
f(\theta)
, and assume that
f_{i}
is differentiable and lower bounded so that

f_{i}(\theta)>-c
, for
i\in[m]
,
for some
c>0
. This problem is prevalent in machine learning tasks where
\theta
corresponds to the model parameters,
f_{i}(\theta)
represents the loss on the training point
i
and the aim is to minimize the average loss
f(\theta)
across points. When
m
is large, SGD or its variants are preferred for solving (
20
) mainly because of their cheap per iteration cost. To present a stochastic algorithm, we
use
v_{k}
to denote a random search direction at
k
-th step.
Here we give only the element-wise version of our stochastic AEGD algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Stochastic AEGD. Good default setting for parameters are
c=1
and
\eta=0.1
.

1:

\{f_{i}({\theta})\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
\eta
: the step size,
\theta_{0}
: initial guess of
\theta
, and
T
: the total number of iterations.

2:

c
: a parameter such that for any
i\in[m]
,
f_{i}({\theta})+c>0
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, initial energy:
r_{0}=\sqrt{f_{i_{0}}(\theta_{0})+c}{\bf 1}

3:

for

k=0
to
k-1

do

4:

v_{k}:=\nabla f_{i_{k}}(\theta_{k})/\big(2\sqrt{f_{i_{k}}(\theta_{k})+c}\big)

(
i_{k}
is a random sample from
[m]
at step
k
)

5:

r_{k+1}=r_{k}/(1+2\eta v_{k}\odot v_{k})
(update energy)

6:

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-2\eta r_{k+1}\odot v_{k}

end

for

return

\theta_{k}

Remark 13
.

If at each iteration step, a mini-batch of training data were selected, Algorithm
1
still applies if
f_{i_{k}}(\theta_{k})
is replaced by

\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in B_{k}}f_{i}(\theta_{k}),

where
B_{k}
denotes a randomly selected subset of
[m]
of size
b
at step
k
with
b\ll m
.

To allow for any form of minibatching we use the arbitrary sampling notation

f_{\xi}(\theta)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{j}f_{j}(\theta),

where
\xi=(\xi^{1},\cdots,\xi^{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
is a random sampling vector (drawn from some distribution) such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{j}]=1
for
j\in[m].
The element-wise update rule for the stochastic AEGD with arbitrary sampling can be reformulated as

\displaystyle v_{k}=\nabla f_{\xi_{k}}(\theta_{k})/(2\sqrt{f_{\xi_{k}}(\theta_{k})+c}),

(21a)

\displaystyle r_{k+1,i}-r_{k,i}=v_{k,i}(\theta_{k+1,i}-\theta_{k,i}),\quad r_{0,i}=\sqrt{f_{\xi_{0}}(\theta_{0})+c}

(21b)

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1,i}=\theta_{k,i}-2\eta r_{k+1,i}v_{k,i}\quad k=0,1,2,\cdots.

(21c)

It follows immediately from the definition of sampling vector
\xi
that

\mathbb{E}[f_{\xi}(\theta)]=f(\theta),\quad\mathbb{E}[\nabla f_{\xi}(\theta)]=\nabla f(\theta),

which means that we still have access to unbiased estimates of
f
and its gradient. Of particular interest is the minibatch sampling:
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
is a b-minibatch sampling if for every subset
B\in[m]
with
|B|=b
we have that

\mathbb{P}\left[\xi=\frac{m}{b}\sum_{i\in B}e_{i}\right]=\frac{b!(m-b)!}{m!}.

One can show by a double counting argument that if
\xi
is a
b-
minibatch sampling, it is indeed a valid sampling with
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{j}]=1
(see
[
16
]
) and
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\xi^{j}=1
.

No matter how
v_{k}
is defined, we have the following result.

Theorem 14
.

(Unconditional energy stability)
The stochastic AEGD of form (
21
)
is unconditionally energy stable in the sense that for any step size
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{E}[r^{2}_{k+1,i}]=\mathbb{E}[r^{2}_{k,i}]-\mathbb{E}[(r_{k+1,i}-r_{k,i})^{2}]-\eta^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\theta_{k+1,i}-\theta_{k,i})^{2}],\quad i\in[n],

(22)

that is
\mathbb{E}[r_{k,i}]
is strictly decreasing and convergent with
\mathbb{E}[r_{k,i}]\to r_{i}^{*}
as
k\to\infty

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(\theta_{k+1,i}-\theta_{k,i})^{2}]=0,\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[(\theta_{j+1,i}-\theta_{j,i})^{2}]\leq\eta(f(\theta_{0})+c),\quad\forall i\in[n].

(23)

The proof is entirely similar to that for Theorem
2
, details are omitted.

Due to the nonlinearity of
v_{k}
in terms of the random variables, we only have

2\mathbb{E}\left[v_{k}\sqrt{f_{\xi_{k}}(\theta_{k})+c}\right]=\nabla f(\theta_{k}),

the estimate of convergence rates can be quite subtle. Nevertheless, with mild assumptions on
f_{l}
we are able to establish the following.

Theorem 15
.

Suppose
\|\nabla f_{l}\|_{\infty}\leq G_{\infty}
, and
f_{l}+c\geq a>0
for all

. Then stochastic AEGD of form (
21
)
admits the following direction-wise estimate, for
i\in[n]

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}[(v_{j,i})^{2}]\leq\frac{C_{i}}{k\mathbb{E}[r_{k,i}]},\quad C_{i}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[r_{0,i}](2a+\eta G^{2}_{\infty})}{4a\eta}.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.4
.

We evaluate the deterministic AEGD (

In all experiments, we fine tune the base step size for each algorithm to obtain its best performance. The base step size is given in respective plots, denoted as “lr”, the momentum in GDM and stochastic GDM (SGDM) is set to

6
Conclusions

Inspired by the IEQ approach for gradient flows in the form of time-dependent partial differential equations, we proposed AEGD in both global and element-wise form, as a new algorithm for first-order gradient-based optimization of non-convex objective functions. This simple update with an auxiliary energy variable is easy to implement, proven to be unconditionally energy stable irrespective of the step size, and features energy-dependent convergence rates with mild conditions on the step size. It is suitable for general objective functions, as long as they are bounded from below. Numerical examples ranging from performance test problems, K-means clustering, to CNNs, all demonstrate the advantages of the proposed AEGD: it enjoys rapid initial progress and faster convergence than GD, is robust with respect to initial data, and generalizes better for deep learning problems. Overall, the results presented in this paper suggest that further research of AEGD could prove useful.

In this section, we prove Theorem
4
and Theorem

from Section
3
.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
4

We rewrite AEGD (
8
) as

\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f(\theta_{k}),\quad\eta_{k}:=\eta\frac{r_{k+1}}{g(\theta_{k})}.

(27)

This is in the form of the usual GD with a variable step size
\eta_{k}
.

(i) Using scheme (
7
) we have

r_{j+1}-r_{j}=\nabla g(\theta_{j})\cdot(\theta_{j+1}-\theta_{j})=-2\eta r_{j+1}\|\nabla g(\theta_{j})\|^{2}.

Take a summation over
j
from
0
to
k-1
gives

r_{0}-r_{k}=2\eta\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}r_{j+1}\|\nabla g(\theta_{j})\|^{2}\geq 2\eta r_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla g(\theta_{j})\|^{2}

Use
r_{0}=g(\theta_{0})
and
r_{k}
is strictly decreasing to get

k\min_{j<k}\|\nabla g(\theta_{j})\|^{2}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\|\nabla g(\theta_{j})\|^{2}\leq\frac{g(\theta_{0})}{2\eta r_{k}}.

Next we prove (i), (ii) and (iii), taking
k_{0}=0
, for simplicity in presentation.

(i) By L-smoothness of
f
and scheme (
27
), we have

\displaystyle f(\theta_{k+1})-f(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\nabla f(\theta_{k})\cdot(\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k})+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}(2-L\eta_{k})\|\nabla f(\theta_{k})\|^{2}\leq-\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}\|\nabla f(\theta_{k})\|^{2}

as long as
\eta_{k}\leq 1/L
. Here and in what follows, we denote
w_{k}=f(\theta_{k})-f^{*}
, then the PL property reads

\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(\theta_{k})\|^{2}\geq\mu(f(\theta_{k})-f^{*})=\mu w_{k}.

With this property, (
28
) can be written as

w_{k+1}-w_{k}\leq-\mu\eta_{k}w_{k}.

This implies
w_{k+1}\leq(1-\mu\eta_{k})w_{k}
. By induction,

\displaystyle w_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-\mu\eta_{j})w_{0}=\exp\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{\rm log}(1-\mu\eta_{j})\right)w_{0}\leq\exp\left(-\mu\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\eta_{j}\right)w_{0}.

Noticing that

\eta_{j}=\eta\frac{r_{j+1}}{g(\theta_{j})}\geq\eta\frac{r_{k}}{g(\theta_{0})},

we further get

w_{k}\leq\exp(-c_{0}kr_{k})w_{0},\quad c_{0}=\frac{\mu\eta}{g(\theta_{0})}.

To obtain the convergence of
\theta_{k}
, we use scheme (
27
) to rewrite (
28
) as

f(\theta_{k})-f(\theta_{k+1})\geq\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad w_{k}-w_{k+1}\geq\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}.

The PL property when combined with (
27
) gives

\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}\geq 2\mu\eta^{2}_{k}w_{k}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{w_{k}}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\mu}\eta_{k}}{\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|}.

Using the above two inequalities and noting that
w_{k}\geq w_{k+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{w_{k}}-\sqrt{w_{k+1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{w_{k}}}(w_{k}-w_{k+1})\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\mu}}{4}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|.

Taking a summation over
k
from
0
to
\infty
gives

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{2\mu}}\sqrt{w_{0}}.

This yields (
17
), which ensures the convergence of
\{\theta_{k}\}
.

(ii)
By the
L
-smoothness assumption, scheme (
27
)
and
\eta_{k}\leq 1/L
, as in (i) we obtain

f(\theta_{k+1})\leq f(\theta_{k})-\frac{1}{2\eta_{k}}\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\|^{2}.

Denoting
d_{k}:=\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\|
, we proceed to obtain the convergence rate. By convexity of
f
,

f(\theta_{k})\leq f(\theta^{*})+\nabla f(\theta_{k})\cdot(\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}).

\displaystyle w_{k+1}
