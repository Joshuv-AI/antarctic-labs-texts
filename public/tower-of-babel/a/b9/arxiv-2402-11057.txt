Are you Struggling? Dataset and Baselines for Struggle Determination in Assembly Videos
Shijia Feng, Michael Wray, Brian Sullivan, Youngkyoon Jang, Casimir Ludwig, Iain Gilchrist, Walterio Mayol-Cuevas

Abstract
Determining when people are struggling from video enables a finer-grained understanding of actions and opens opportunities for building intelligent support visual interfaces. In this paper, we present a new dataset with three assembly activities and corresponding performance baselines for the determination of struggle from video. Three real-world problem-solving activities including assembling plumbing pipes (Pipes-Struggle), pitching camping tents (Tent-Struggle) and solving the Tower of Hanoi puzzle (Tower-Struggle) are introduced. Video segments were scored w.r.t. the level of struggle as perceived by annotators using a forced choice 4-point scale. Each video segment was annotated by a single expert annotator in addition to crowd-sourced annotations. The dataset is the first struggle annotation dataset and contains 5.1 hours of video and 725,100 frames from 73 participants in total. We evaluate three decision-making tasks: struggle classification, struggle level regression, and struggle label distribution learning. We provide baseline results for each of the tasks utilising several mainstream deep neural networks, along with an ablation study and visualisation of results. Our work is motivated toward assistive systems that analyze struggle, support users during manual activities and encourage learning, as well as other video understanding competencies.

Understanding when someone is struggling is an important cognitive competence and a key component of skill acquisition
(
Newell \APACyear1991
)
. This capability could enable the development of systems that understand how effectively people perform activities and assist with achieving goals or supporting learning outcomes. The ability to detect struggle and subsequently offer help, is a core developmental ability and exists already in pre-verbal children and even other primates
(
Warneken \BBA Tomasello \APACyear2006\APACexlab\BCnt1
)
.

Motivated by the larger goal of finer-grained visual understanding and opportunities on eye-wear computing to provide real-time assistance, we collected a dataset for struggle determination using indoor and outdoor real-world activities, including three domains: assembling plumbing pipes; pitching a camping tent; and solving the ‘Tower of Hanoi’ puzzle. These three domains were chosen specifically to demonstrate the viability of struggle detection, due to their task structures and assembly requirements. The Tower of Hanoi puzzle presented a strict rule-based framework requiring participants to solve a spatial puzzle. Pipe plumbing uses rigid objects and printed instructions for participants to follow, showcasing their ability to perform an unknown task with guidance. The tent assembly task introduced a free-form assembly process with deformable objects, demanding greater improvisation and manual skill. Compared to other general real daily-living tasks (eg cooking or crafts), our chosen tasks are examples of real-world problem-solving and have well-defined goals (completion of the structures). Our tasks allow participants to demonstrate a variety of complex visuomotor behaviours with multiple, albeit constrained, opportunities for struggle.

Our struggle-determination approach is based on two main assumptions. First, we believe there is sufficient information for a large number of activities to determine if a person is struggling from video, based on low-level motion cues of hand-object interaction and without requiring specific task knowledge. This is supported by behavioural evidence e.g.
Warneken \BBA Tomasello \APACyear2006\APACexlab\BCnt1
;
Warneken \BBA Tomasello \APACyear2006\APACexlab\BCnt2
in people.
Second, the same deep learning models and train/test strategies can be used for different tasks as they share common visual cues that help to determine struggle, such as the smoothness of hand motion.

Figure
1
shows sample video frames from our data. We used two levels for annotation: MTurk workers and a domain expert. Annotations used a forced choice, four-level struggle scale so that we can determine if a person is struggling and to what degree. We evaluated several video understanding methods trained on our dataset and provided a variety of baseline results across our proposed struggle determination activities and machine learning tasks.

Our primary contributions are:
1) Surfacing the problem of struggle determination for finer-grained assembly understanding in egocentric videos.
2) Introducing a public dataset for struggle determination, which is annotated via an expert annotator and crowdsourcing.
3) Presenting three benchmark tasks for struggle determination: classification, regression, and label-distributed learning, all using weakly supervised learning with only video-level labels.
4) We provide initial benchmark results of video understanding models on struggle determination thus providing baseline results and demonstrating the essential role of motion information.

Our three activities were chosen to require complex visuomotor coordination and problem-solving while remaining goal-driven. This promotes similar yet varied occurrences of struggle behaviours and provides enough diversity within and across activities.

3
Struggle Determination Dataset

Here we detail the methodology to record our egocentric videos and collect annotations for the struggle determination dataset. Our struggle dataset is presented under the Non-Commercial Government Licence for public sector information
(
The National Archives \APACyear\bibnodate
)
.

We initially recorded the behaviour of the participants in three scenarios that require complex visuomotor coordination and problem-solving and where participants may occasionally make errors or otherwise struggle.
These scenarios were intended to be similar to what one might encounter in daily life (both indoors and outdoors) and where one might want to receive instructional advice.
Finding suitable scenarios for struggle detection presents a balancing act. The scenarios shouldn’t be too easy, as participants might not exhibit struggle behaviours, but they also shouldn’t be overly complex or open-ended to prevent unclear
goals and excessive task completion times (we favour activities contained within a few minutes).
One alternative option would have been to leverage existing visuomotor datasets, such as EPIC-Kitchen
(
Damen \BOthers. \APACyear2018
)
, by selecting and annotating videos related to struggle. We have investigated this option and generated Table
6
(see in appendix) summarizing potential datasets. Unfortunately, we encountered two key limitations on most previous datasets:
(1) Misaligned annotations: None of the existing datasets offered annotations that specifically targeted or purposefully quantified struggle;
(2) Scarcity of repeated goal-directed tasks: existing datasets mostly lack repeated instances of goal-directed activities (e.g., not every participant is cracking eggs or flipping pancakes).
These limitations led us to consider collecting new data rather than just relying on the existing ones.
An exception is
Jang \BOthers. \APACyear2019
;
Sullivan \BOthers. \APACyear2021
, where several subjects repeat the same activity for a complex visuomotor assembly of a camping tent. We annotate this data in our work.
Finally, we want real scenarios that are ordinary enough so that naive observers can estimate if a participant is struggling without prior knowledge. Across the three activities, we recorded first-person videos and later supplemented the videos with struggle-determination annotations from human observers.

The scenarios included: assembling plastic plumbing pipes by following one of two diagrams (easy or difficult) while seated indoors; solving a version of the Tower of Hanoi puzzle while seated indoors; and finally assembling a camping tent moving freely outdoors. We respectively name the scenarios as Pipes, Tower, and Tent and the corresponding activities/subsets for struggle determination as Pipes-Struggle, Tower-Struggle and Tent-Struggle. Each scenario includes fine-grained actions that people perform routinely (e.g. arranging, picking up and placing objects, attaching them, etc.). Equipment needed, difficulty and goals vary between scenarios, as does participant performance, with some struggling more or less in completing a scenario.

Table 1
:
Struggle level descriptions.

Definitely non-struggling

The actions are executed with complete ease and confidence, with no indication of any challenge or difficulty encountered throughout the task.

Slightly non-struggling

The actions are generally executed smoothly and with confidence, with only occasional minor errors or corrections that indicate some level of challenge or difficulty.

Slightly struggling

There are some signs of challenge or difficulty including occasional errors, pauses, dropping off items, or wobbly hands that indicate the participants may not be very experienced in the task at hand but are still making an effort to complete it.

Definitely struggling

There are clear, consistent signs of difficulty, such as repeated attempts, unsmooth motions, and significant pauses during the task. These actions indicate that the participants don’t really know what to do and may lack the knowledge or skill necessary to complete the task successfully.

3.1
Dataset Construction

3.1.1
Data Collection

For all three scenarios, university undergraduate and graduate student populations were recruited. All participants gave informed consent and approval for data sharing. We recruited 24 individuals to perform the plumbing task (80% female, mean age 22.6 years), and 20 participants from this group also performed the Tower of Hanoi task. For Struggle-Tent, as stated in
Jang \BOthers. \APACyear2019
, 29 individuals (71% female, mean age 23.3 years) performed the tent-pitching scenario. In all activities, participants wore a head-mounted GoPro Hero 5 (1080p at 60Hz).

In the plumbing task, participants assembled home plumbing pipes following a diagram of a double sink layout (see supplementary material). Participants were evenly split between those with an easier and more difficult diagram to follow. To simplify dexterity requirements, the activity was reduced to assembling the layout of the pipes on a table rather than in a real sink.

Videos were captured at
1920\times 1080
resolution, at 30Hz for Tent-Struggle, and 60Hz for Pipes-Struggle and Tower-Struggle. Struggle determination annotation was accomplished by MTurkers rating 10s video clips from the tasks. We segment videos into 10s blocks. Given the struggle’s inherent ambiguity, we discovered during the early annotation process that it can be difficult for annotators to locate the beginning and end of the struggle in untrimmed video recordings. Therefore we chose to use a weakly labelling approach instead. Inspired by Experimental Psychology research (e.g.
Warneken \BBA Tomasello \APACyear2006\APACexlab\BCnt1
,
Warneken \BBA Tomasello \APACyear2006\APACexlab\BCnt2
) that shows pre-verbal children and even chimpanzees can determine struggle actions within 10s, our clips were created by uniformly splitting the videos into 10-second segments from beginning to end (e.g. 0-9s, 10-19s, etc.). We also resized videos to
456\times 256
at 30Hz. This ensured segments had a sufficient resolution for human observers while maintaining a resolution suitable for deep learning models. Our annotation process thus focuses on evaluating struggle within isolated 10-second clips. This means that we assess and label the presence of struggle solely within each clip, without considering the broader context of the full video recording.
Using the EPIC-Tent action label annotations
(
Jang \BOthers. \APACyear2019
)
, we chose to focus on video segments from five actions (assemble support, insert stake, insert support, insert support tab, and place guyline) where our expert annotator judged they had the most obvious visual cues of struggle in a series of pilot studies.

3.1.2
Annotating Struggle

Since the original videos are trimmed uniformly into 10-second segments we focus on ‘low-level’ struggling cues which we believe to be the most transferable between participants and scenarios. Struggle markers may include shaky hands, pausing actions, dropping items, or action repetition.
While there is a variety of ways one might ask an observer to estimate struggle, we chose to ask observers to rate video segments using a four-point scale varying from definitely non-struggle to definitely struggle. Table
1
lists the descriptions for each struggle level.
The value of a finer, four-point gradation of struggle supports the longer-term goal of assistive systems. For example, the support provided to a person who is ‘slightly’ struggling may consist of a few basic instructions or reminders. However, a person determined to be definitely struggling may require more detailed guidance or even recommendations to stop the task to prevent injury.

This scoring approach allows naive observers to indicate the degree of struggle/non-struggle. Note, we forced annotators to choose between struggle and non-struggle, instead of allowing a neutral option. This avoided centring strategies on ambiguous judgements and allowed easy binarisation of annotations during early pilot approaches that classed only struggle presence/absence. Throughout our research, we found that indicating the degree of struggle is necessary to address significant challenges in classifying fine-grained features.

As self-reporting levels of struggle can be unreliable and provide only a single judgement, we focus on a balance between expert and naive observers. We collected two distinct types of struggle determination annotations: naive ‘voter labels’ obtained from Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) participants, and ‘expert annotations’ (or ‘golden annotation’ (GA)) obtained from the author who collected the video dataset, who repeatedly viewed them during analysis, and annotated action and error labels in the EPIC-Tent dataset.
For each 10s video clip, there is only one expert annotation label, and there are multiple voters’ labels that form a frequency distribution. This distribution consists of 15 or 20 voter labels. To extract a single struggle label from this distribution, we take the mode. Since voter labels and expert annotation come from distinct groups of individuals with differing prior knowledge, label distributions can vary across all video segments for each dataset. Consistency between the voters’ modes and expert annotations indicates that the struggle-level annotations of the videos are more reliable.
In addition, the standard deviation of the voters’ distributions provides a measure of the voter consistency or label ambiguity for a segment (smaller values indicate greater consistency, i.e. less label ambiguity).
We explore these annotations in Section
3.2
.

3.1.3
Expert Annotator Qualifications and Training

Unlike the MTurk workers who only viewed 10-second clips for each task, the expert, as a paper author, had previously reviewed the full videos (multiple times) during data annotation and quality control. This exposure provided them with a deeper understanding of the task context and its specific challenges. Additionally, the expert had prior experience coding actions, objects, and errors within the ‘tent’ dataset, further honing their observation and annotation skills relevant to the 10-second clips.

We acknowledge the inherent ambiguity in struggle determination, similar to skill assessment. Much like Olympic judges offering slightly different scores for a performance of exceptional skill, we anticipate slight disagreement in struggle determination. In our dataset of short clips, struggle typically manifests as motor errors, such as abrupt stops, repetitive attempts without success, object drops, or awkward manipulation. We believe that basic motor struggle is readily detectable by most human observers based on the fluidity of movement. However, the expert’s advantage lies in their extensive experience making such judgments, coupled with their unique knowledge of the full videos and specific task goals.

3.1.4
MTurk Annotator Validation

We recognize that struggle determination and its annotation is an ambiguous task, which is part of the challenge and research value for this problem. However, we still want to ensure annotation by crowd-sourcing is as valid as possible and that the votes and MTurkers can be trusted. We use the following assumptions in this ambiguous undertaking: 1) we assume the expert annotator is trustworthy, 2) ambiguity between sides of struggle (e.g. definitely struggling and slightly struggling and similarly for non-struggling) is acceptable and expected, 3) If a Mturker is untrustworthy in one of the assignments, all results by that MTurker is questionable and best to remove. Based on these assumptions we implemented the following validation checks:

•

If all votes for all videos assigned to the MTurker on an assignment are the same, all results from all assignments by that MTurker are rejected.

•

We also identified 6 validation videos using three
definitely struggling
and three
definitely non-struggling
videos as labelled by the expert annotator. These videos were further checked for unanimous agreement by simple inspection by the authors.

•

These 6 validation videos were included as part of the MTurk assignments and for every MTurker we calculate the level of disagreement w.r.t. the validation videos and the expert’s votes. If the MTurker’s votes are
on the same side
of struggle w.r.t the expert’s vote, i.e. (struggling (3,4) or non-struggling (1,2)) this does not count as a disagreement. The MTurker is rejected if the ratio of disagreement w.r.t. the validation videos is
>0.33
, i.e. three or more out of 6 disagreements for the validation videos.

3.2
Struggle Statistics

The three subsets of Our struggle determination dataset, Tent-Struggle, Pipes-Struggle, and Tower-Struggle, contain 585, 1011, and 236 annotated 10s videos, respectively, totalling 5.09 hours recorded with 725,100 frames. We further divide each of the subsets into four splits for four-fold cross-validation such that the video samples from the same participants are within the same split and the struggle label distributions are similar across the four splits (see Figure
2
for more details). The final split of participants and the corresponding number of video segments they contributed are shown in Figure

. We note that some participants have contributed a significantly higher number of video segments than the other participants.

When considering struggle labels for video samples, there are three types of labels to take into account: a) Expert annotation—the struggling label given by the expert annotator; b) Voters’ mode—the statistical mode’s struggle value the voters give; and c) Voters’ label distribution—composed of the frequencies of voters’ labels on each of the struggle levels for each video sample.

We introduce three tasks for assessing struggle determination. First,
struggle classification
includes binary and four-way classifications using expert labels. In the four-way classification, we use the original struggle labels ranging from 1 to 4 corresponding to definitely non-struggle to definitely struggle. In binary classification, we binarize the label of struggling versus non-struggling by grouping 1&2 votes and 3&4 votes. Secondly,
regression
fits to the degree of struggle. Here we treat the struggle level labels from the experts as continuous real numbers ranging from 1 to 4. The third task is
label distribution learning
. Given the video samples, we train a deep model that can predict the frequency distribution over the four struggle levels while using the frequency distributions of the voters’ annotations for each video segment as the target. In the rest of this section, we present our baseline methods for end-to-end training, followed by the baseline experiment implementation details and results. We train and test our deep models using two Titan X (Pascal) 12GB GPUs or two GTX 1080Ti 11GB GPUs across all the experiments.

4.1
Baseline Methods

We implement three different deep architectures in our experiments: (1) 2D-ConvNet represented by the Temporal Segment Networks (TSN)
(
Wang \BOthers. \APACyear2016
)
, using the implementation from
yjxiong \BBA Line290 \APACyear2019
. (2) 3D-ConvNet represented by the SlowFast Networks
(
Feichtenhofer \BOthers. \APACyear2019
)
based on ResNet50 (SlowFast-R50)
(
Fan \BOthers. \APACyear2020
)
, and (3) hybrid architectures of 3D-ConvNets and Vision Transformers
(
Dosovitskiy \BOthers. \APACyear2020
)

4.2
Classification Task

4.2.1
Implementation Details

We conducted two classification experiments: binary classification, in which we classify only struggle and non-struggle, and four-class classification, in which we predict the level of struggle. In either case, the model predicts the probability for each struggle level and is supervised by the Cross-Entropy (CE) loss. We use top-1 accuracy as an evaluation metric.

During training, we uniformly sample 128 frames from each video. The input frames are randomly cropped from
256\times 456
to
256\times 256
. We also implement a horizontal flip at a probability of 0.5 during the training stage. In the test stage, we perform 5 random crops and ensemble the predictions. For TSN
(
Wang \BOthers. \APACyear2016
)
, we implement the BN-Inception base architecture with the average consensus for the output from each of the segments. We use ImageNet
(
Russakovsky \BOthers. \APACyear2015
)
pre-trained features and only train the fully-connected layer for classification. To overcome overfitting, we set the dropout rate to 0.5. We train for 65 epochs using mini-batch stochastic gradient descent with a momentum of 0.9 and weight decay 5e-4. The learning rate starts from 0.0001 and decreases
10\times
in epochs 30 and 60. For the SlowFast model and its variants we use the SlowFast-R50 and load the parameters pre-trained on Kinetics-400
(
Kay \BOthers. \APACyear2017
)
. During training, we freeze the parameters in the backbone and train the rest of the MLP layers. We use the same optimizer but the weight decay is set to 1e-5. We train these deep models for 70 epochs and implement a cyclic cosine decay learning rate scheduler with fixed cycle restart intervals
(
Athiwaratkun \BOthers. \APACyear2019
)
in which the learning rate starts from 0.001 and decreases 100 times in 50 epochs and followed by two restart intervals for 10 epochs each with the restart learning rate increase 10 times on the minimum value and decrease back to the minimum in every 10 epochs.

4.3
Regression Task

4.3.1
Implementation Details

In the regression task, the labels for struggle are treated as continuous scores indicating the degree of struggle, ranging from 1 (definitely non-struggle) to 4 (definitely struggle). We add a fully connected layer that takes the feature vectors as input and outputs a regression score. The predicted regression score is supervised by Mean Squared Error (MSE) together with target regression labels. The evaluation metrics include Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for regression and top-1 binary or four-way classification accuracy for classification. The hyperparameter settings of the optimization process are the same as those in the classification task.

4.3.2
Regression Results

Regression results are shown in Table
4
. To obtain the classification results, we quantize the regression score into binary and four-way classification labels using evenly divided thresholds and then calculate classification accuracy. VTN-SlowFast-ViT generally has the lowest MSE and MAE and the highest classification accuracy rate on activities pipes and towers, followed by SlowFast-R50 and then TSN. We find that there is a greater variability of model performance when using continuous labels compared to the classification task. However, this trend does not hold on the Tent-Struggle dataset where the model VTN-SlowFast-ViT does not outperform the SlowFast-R50, either on regression metrics or on the converted classification metrics. This may be because the dataset tent is the most complex for struggle determination—it involves outdoor tent assembly scenes with five different sub-actions, so it is hard to optimize the extra ViT layer in the model VTN-SlowFast-ViT by only supervising a regression output.

4.4
Label Distribution Learning Task

4.4.1
Implementation Details

In label distribution learning, we train a deep model to predict voters’ struggle label frequency distribution given video samples. In our implementation, the final layer output of the model has four nodes corresponding to each of the four struggle levels ranging from 1 (definitely non-struggle) to 4 (definitely struggle) followed by a SoftMax layer so that each node’s output value ranges from 0 to 1 with the summation equaling to one. We optimize the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence loss function to align the predicted distribution with the frequency distribution of the voters’ struggle labels for each video sample. For evaluation, we calculate Spearmans’ Rank Correlation (Spearmans’ Rho) between the predicted frequency distribution and the voters’ struggle label frequency distribution so that the value ranges from -1 to 1 with a higher score indicating that the predicted distribution better aligns with its target. We also calculate the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between the predicted distribution and the target distribution. For converted classification, we calculate the accuracy rate between the predicted highest frequency label and the voters’ mode label. The hyper-parameter settings are the same as those in the previous tasks.

5
Ablation Study

In this section, we investigate the importance of temporal modelling and motion understanding for the struggle determination task.
Specifically, we conduct two experiments: Firstly, how much information can be gleaned from a single frame, and secondly, how important is the temporal ordering of frames.

5.1
Multiple Frames

To show the necessity of using multiple frames, we conducted experiments using one frame and made a comparison with the original multi-frame setting. We first loaded the model parameters trained using 128 frames which is the standard setting for our experiments and then ran only the test stage by loading one frame from each of the video sequences. The one frame is selected from proportions of 25%, 50%, and 75% respectively from the beginning of each video segment. Note that we duplicated the sampled one frame to meet the minimum number of input frames required by 3D-ConvNets, which is 8 for SlowFast, 128 for SlowFast-gMLP and VTN-SlowFast-ViT. To maintain consistency between the training and testing stages, we conducted additional experiments by randomly selecting one frame from each video during the training stage and one frame from the same proportions during the testing stage. The results for the two cases described above are shown in Figure
5
. We report the one-frame test accuracy using average accuracy from proportions of 25%, 50%, and 75% respectively. There is a significant decrease in accuracy across all of the models we tested on all three activities. And because the video samples in the dataset are randomly trimmed into 10-second segments, the accuracy rates for the frame sampled from 25%, 50%, and 75% are expected to be similar, which means a small standard deviation (StdDev) of accuracy close to 0 in the bar plots. Even though we trained the model using a single frame, the test results still showed a notable decrease in accuracy, despite a slight increase compared to testing the models with only one frame. These results indicate that no sufficient visual information is provided for the deep learning models to rely on just one frame to determine whether a person is struggling. Therefore, effectively capturing the useful temporal motion features is crucial for our struggle determination task. A sequence of frames can typically reveal anomalous hand motions such as ‘unsmooth’ motions, stopping, and repeating that indicate the person did not have things under control. A single frame cannot represent these features. To test this idea more rigorously, however, it is important to present the models with a sequence of frames that do not contain useful temporal motion features.

5.2
Temporal Ordering

We ran a shuffle-frame test to show the importance of temporal ordering. We did not consider TSN because it only considers 2D spatial features and calculates the average of the output logits of all the input frames, making it insensitive to the order of the frames. We first loaded the model parameters pre-trained using 128 frames in the right order. Then we loaded the same number of 128 frames from each of the video samples and shuffled them randomly so that we disrupted the correct order of the video frames. We ran the model in inference mode ten times and calculated the average accuracy. As shown in Figure
6
, the accuracy rate decreased significantly to the random level, which means the models are very sensitive to the frame orders. To make a fair comparison, we conducted another series of experiments in which we also trained the models using shuffled frames and then reported the test shuffle-frame accuracy. Figure
6
shows that after training using shuffled frames, the models can perform much better than when solely conducting the test stage, but still lower than the results when using the frames in the correct order. Our illustration suggests that the deep models learned key temporal dependencies of video sequences when they were trained using the correct order of frame sequences; as a result, when we test the models using shuffled frames, their accuracy approaches random performance.

7
Discussion and Conclusions

In this paper, we have shown and discussed how struggle determination is an important component of human video understanding and a key element for building assistive systems.
We have introduced a dataset including three activities for the struggle determination task, with a total of 5.09 hours of video.

Our results reveal opportunities for improvement in the four-way classification task aimed at finer-grained assessment of struggle. The similar accuracy rates between four-way to binary conversion and binary classification indicate that the main challenge lies in distinguishing the degree of struggle. This is key for useful and dependable assistive systems. The tent assembly task has the lowest accuracy (
40\%
), for the four-way task, supporting our hypothesis that the ambiguity on task judgement for the tent pitching activity is harder than for the other activities that have more structure. The regression and label distribution learning tasks have encouraging results, demonstrating their efficacy in distinguishing model differences and determining struggle in different ways. In particular, the VTN-SlowFast-ViT model overall performs well in the label distribution learning tasks, yet albeit better than random, it still achieves low performance (
34\%
) for the four-way Tent-Struggle.

Several limitations and challenges remain:
(1) Limited background scene variations across the dataset, consisting mostly of two indoor scenes and one outdoor scene for tent assembly.
(2) We have only video-level ‘weak’ labels for struggle, finer temporal localisation and further task knowledge could help the model to improve performance.
(3) Current pre-trained model parameters based on coarse-grained video action recognition may be sub-optimal for fine-grained struggle determination.
(4) By using randomly trimmed 10-second video segments, we may overlook certain types of struggling that are more obvious in the entire video and can only focus on ‘low-level’ struggling detection.
(5) A better understanding and validation of what is an appropriate human (or statistical) baseline is likely needed to validate model performance.

In conclusion, this paper aims to motivate and support further work on determining the level of struggle in video. A new annotated dataset for struggling with three real daily living activities is introduced. This data is with video-level annotations from both crowd voters and human experts. Three research challenges, namely classification, regression, and label distribution learning, are presented along with definitions and baseline results using various deep architectures.
We hope this research provides valuable insights for additional work on fine-grained egocentric video understanding and supports new methods and directions to benchmark assistive systems.

Action recognition datasets encompass both coarse-grained and fine-grained human actions. Deep models trained on large-scale action recognition datasets can be transferred to various downstream tasks due to the diverse scenarios and learned features. However, these datasets do not focus on scenes depicting struggling, particularly in the case of coarse-grained AR, which mainly includes common human actions and lacks scenarios for skills.

Action quality assessment datasets primarily annotate action quality scores for videos showcasing sports or surgical skills. However, lower action quality scores do not necessarily indicate struggling, and well-performed actions can still receive relatively low scores.

Skill determination datasets are more relevant to our struggle determination task as they often showcase different skills at different levels with hand-object interactions. However, the annotations in these datasets are in the form of pairwise rankings, indicating which skill is better than the other. Lower-ranked videos in these pairs may not guarantee that a person is struggling.

Video anomaly detection datasets mainly consist of surveillance videos capturing unusual events, such as fights or unintentional falls. These datasets are typically used for binary classification tasks to distinguish between anomaly and normal videos. While struggle determination also involves binary classification, it falls within the context of determining skills. Moreover, our goal is not only to differentiate struggle from non-struggle but also to determine the degree of struggle, which is crucial for developing a wearable intelligent system to provide appropriate assistance to the user.

In comparison, our struggle determination datasets encompass three tasks that demonstrate different types of skills in assembling pipes, pitching tents, and playing the Tower of Hanoi game. These datasets cover indoor and outdoor scenes and include both rigid and non-rigid objects. Additionally, we specifically use a struggle scoring scale, and thus, the annotations in our dataset are not limited to binary labels but are further fine-grained to indicate the degree of struggle. Aside from the focus on struggle, this distinguishes them from the class labels in AR, action quality scores in AQA, ranking labels in skill determination, and even the binary contrastive labels in VAD. Hence, it is essential to create a dataset with a focus on struggle annotations to train deep models for determining struggle and quantifying its degree.

is the frequency of the voters label for struggle level 3 (slightly struggle), noted as
Dist_{i}(f_{3})
;
f_{4}
is the frequency of the voters label for struggle level 4 (definitely struggle), noted as
Dist_{i}(f_{4})
, while
f_{1}+f_{2}+f_{3}+f_{4}=1
. Let the mode of the voters’ label for video
V_{i}
be
y^{l}_{i}
.
y^{l}_{i}
is the struggle label index (from 1 to 4 ) of
Mode(Dist_{i})
. Note that in the case of binary classification, we combine 1 and 2 to 0 (non-struggle), and 3 and 4 to 1 (struggle) as discussed in the main paper. Moreover, the label distributions over a certain split are calculated by averaging the expert annotation
y^{e}_{i}
or the voters’ label distributions
Dist_{i}=\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\}
across all the videos
V_{i}
within the split, denoted as
Dist(y_{0})
and
Dist(y_{1})
respectively.

In this way, for the random baseline, we assume the random model only has the ability to predict each of the struggle levels in a probability that corresponds to the proportions of the expert annotations in the test split
Dist(y_{0})
, so that we can calculate the top-1 accuracy rate each time we run the random model. When the number of runs tends to be a large number close to infinity, the averaged top-1 accuracy rate over the number of runs is approaching a set percentage which is the random baseline accuracy. The majority class baseline is the percentage of the maximum entity in
Dist(y_{0})
for a split. Finally, we illustrate the voter’s baseline. Given a video
V_{i}
, we define the Kronecker delta function:

\delta_{y_{i}^{e}y_{i}^{l}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }y_{i}^{e}=y_{i}^{l}\\
0&\text{if }y_{i}^{e}\neq y_{i}^{l}\end{cases}

(1)

where
y_{i}^{e}
is the expert annotation and
y_{i}^{l}
is the mode of the voters’ labels. So the voters’ baseline accuracy is calculated as:

\frac{1}{N_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j}}\delta_{y_{i}^{e}y_{i}^{l}}\times 100\%

(2)

where the
N_{j}
is the number of videos in a split
j
.
