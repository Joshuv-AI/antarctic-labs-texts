The landscape function on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d}
G. David, A. Gloria, and S. Mayboroda

Abstract
. Consider the Schrödinger operator − △ + λ ​ V -\triangle+\lambda V with non-negative iid random potential V V of strength λ > 0 \lambda>0 . We prove existence and uniqueness of the associated landscape function on the whole space, and show that its correlations decay exponentially. As a main ingredient we establish the (annealed and quenched) exponential decay of the Green function of − △ + λ ​ V -\triangle+\lambda V using Agmon’s positivity method, rank-one perturbation in dimensions d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , and first-passage percolation in dimensions d = 1 , 2 d=1,2 .



1.
Motivation and the main result

1.1.
The landscape

In the recent years the landscape function introduced in
[
9
]
proved to be an indispensable tool both for the theoretical study of the localization of waves in disordered media and in many physical and engineering applications where the localization phenomena play a decisive role. Roughly speaking, the emerging principle is that in order to understand the behavior of waves governed by the Schrödinger Hamiltonian
H=-\Delta+V
with some disordered potential
V
, one should first compute the landscape function, a solution to
Hu=1
, and then use its reciprocal,
1/u
, as an
effective potential
. The latter is much more illustrative than the original
V
, accurately accounting for both quantum and classical effects. For instance, the corresponding Agmon distance predicts exponential decay in disordered potentials
[
4
]
and the corresponding counting function gives a non-asymptotic estimate on the integrated density of states across the entire spectrum
[
8
]
.

However, virtually all of the existing work addresses the passage from the properties of the landscape to those of the spectrum or the eigenfunctions of the corresponding Hamiltonian, and this is only a half of the puzzle. One naturally arrives at the question about the properties of the landscape as a probabilistic object, e.g., in standard disordered potentials inducing Anderson localization. This is exactly the goal of this paper.

To this end, let us
introduce some
classical Anderson-type potentials. Let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{1/10}(0))

be a nontrivial bump function supported in the
ball centered at 0 of radius
1/10
, with
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
, and set

(1)

V=V_{\omega}(x)=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\omega_{j}\varphi(x-j)\quad\hbox{for }x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where the
\omega_{j}
are i.i.d. variables taking values in
[0,1]
,
whose probability distribution

F(\delta)=\mathbb{P}[\omega\leq\delta],\quad 0\leq\delta\leq 1,

is not trivial, i.e., not concentrated at one point, and such that
0
is
the infimum of its support. Note that we do not require any smoothness of the probability distribution, and in particular, we treat atomic (e.g., Bernoulli) potentials under the same hat. The Hamiltonian in the center of our attention will be
H=-\Delta+\lambda V
, and we will carefully track the dependence on the strength of the disorder, the real parameter
\lambda>0
.

Consider the landscape function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is, the solution to the equation

(2)

-\triangle u+\lambda Vu=1.

As mentioned above, it was originally introduced in
[
9
]
, on bounded domains with homogeneous boundary conditions, in the setting where, e.g., its existence is deterministic and standard. However, to relate the landscape function to spectral properties of random operators on the whole space, and to properly describe its probabilistic properties, it is important to define it on the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as well.

Recall that a random field
v
(that is, a measurable map of both the space variable
x

and the randomness
\omega
), is called stationary iff for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

it satisfies

v(x+z,\omega)=v(x,\tau_{z}\omega),\text{ where for all }j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},(\tau_{z}\omega)_{j}=\omega_{j+z}.

Informally one expects that
u(x)=\int G(x,y)dy
, where
G
would denote the Green function of the Schrödinger operator
-\triangle+\lambda V
.
The latter is however not easily defined (especially in dimensions 1 and 2), and uniform deterministic bounds do not ensure that it is integrable.
It is therefore not clear a priori that (
2
) admits a stationary solution in the sense above. Proving the existence of the landscape, along with the sharp bounds on its moments and correlations, is one of the main objectives of this note.

In what follows, we adopt Hardy’s notation:
C\geq 1\geq c>0
denote positive constants (that might change from line to line) depending only on
d
,
\varphi
, and
F
– and not on
\lambda
. When the constants in question, in addition, depend on
\lambda
and/or the forthcoming integrability parameter
p
, we make it explicit in notation by writing, e.g.,
C_{\lambda,p}.
We also write
A\lesssim B
to signify
A\leq CB
for some constant
C>0
with the same dependence, and add subscripts to specify a dependence on further parameters.

Our first result ensures the well-posedness of (
2
).

Theorem 1
.

There exists a
unique stationary solution of the landscape equation (
2
) with finite second moment.
That is, for almost all
\omega
,
u
lies in the Sobolev space
H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and is a weak solution
of (
2
), and
\mathbb{E}\big[\int_{Q}u^{2}\big]<+\infty
.
In addition, it is positive and satisfies

(3)

\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{Q}u^{p}\big]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C_{\lambda,p},

for all
p\geq 1
.
Here again

Q:=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{d}
.

Next we investigate the correlations of the landscape function, and establish the following.

Theorem 2
.

The stationary solution
u
of (
2
) satisfies for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{u(x)};{u(y)}\right]|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}\exp(-c\,(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|),

\displaystyle\Bigl|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{\frac{1}{u}(x)};{\frac{1}{u}(y)}\right]\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}\exp(-c\,(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|),

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{\nabla\log u(x)};{\nabla\log u(y)}\right]|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}\exp(-c\,(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|),

where
\operatorname{Cov}\left[{};{}\right]
denotes the covariance, and where the multiplicative positive constant
c
depends on the dimension and the distribution
F
of the random variable, and
C_{\lambda}
depends additionally on
\lambda
.

To establish these results, we first consider an infra-red regularization of the landscape function
u_{\eta}
,
\eta>0
, such that

(4)

-\triangle u_{\eta}+(\lambda V+\eta)u_{\eta}=1,

which is well-posed on a deterministic level.
We shall prove Theorem
1
by establishing moment bounds on
u_{\eta}

which are uniform with respect to
\eta
and by passing to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
in (
4
).
Likewise, Theorem
2
is obtained by passing to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
in the corresponding estimates for
u_{\eta}
.
These uniform estimates rely on uniform decay estimates for the infra-red regularization of the Green function, which are the object of Section
1.2
below.
Theorems
1
and
2
are then obtained by a post-processing in
Section
3
.

1.2.
Exponential decay of the Green function

The present section is devoted to the exponential decay estimates on the Green function which we hope will be of independent interest.

For all
\eta>0
, denote by
G_{\eta}
the Green function of
-\triangle+(\lambda V+\eta)
, defined
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the
unique decaying solution of

(5)

(-\triangle_{y}+(\lambda V+\eta))G_{\eta}(x,y)=\delta(x-y).

The main result of this section is the following exponential decay of
G_{\eta}
, uniformly with respect to
\eta>0
.

Theorem 3
.

There exists
c>0
such that for all
p\geq 1
,
\eta>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

(6)

0\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\int_{Q}G_{\eta,\lambda}(x,y+y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}\big)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\,C_{\lambda,p}\exp\big(-\tfrac{c}{p}(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|\big),

where the constant
C_{\lambda,p}
, which only depends on
\lambda
,
p
, and
d
, is made explicit in the proofs.

Remark 1
.

Since the estimate (
6
) is uniform in
\eta>0
, one can pass to the limit as
\eta\downarrow 0
and uniquely define the Green function
G_{\lambda}
of the random operator
-\triangle+\lambda V
as an almost surely exponentially decaying function (that satisfies the estimate (
6
)). We refer the reader to Section
3.1
for a similar argument at the level of the landscape function.

Let us provide a few comments on Theorem
3
.

These estimates capture an effect of randomness as opposed to simple spectral gap considerations. Indeed, the spectrum of
-\triangle+(\eta+\lambda V)
starts at
\eta
.
If
\omega
were
treated deterministically (or in other words, without taking an expectation on the left-hand side of (
6
)), one would not be able to assure the exponential decay without dependence on
\eta
1
1

1

If we do not take the expectation on the left-hand side of (
6
), one needs
C_{\lambda,p}
to be a random field depending on
x
– this would correspond to the quenched version of (
6
), which we also prove in this note.
.
Theorem
3
is however not surprising: it is certainly well-known to the experts, and should essentially follow from a post-processing of subtle localization results, such as
[
3
,
10
]
in the continuous setting. It should also follow from
[
16
,
14
]
for the discrete Laplacian on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see also
[
15
]
when the obstacles on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are replaced by Poisson obstacles on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
– see also the early references
[
6
,
5
]
on these questions. It would however take
some
re-proving, such a result is not stated explicitly in any of the aforementioned works (nor would it be possible inside the spectrum), and we do not want to use either of these routes, in part because known treatments of Anderson localization still do not include all potentials covered in this note, and in part because that would make the methodology unnecessarily complicated.

Here we present a somewhat more streamlined and self-contained argument, which also yields stronger results in our setting. Indeed, contrary to the aforementioned estimates on the Green function in papers devoted to the Anderson localization, we control high moments. The reason is that we only focus on the bottom of the spectrum, and in particular, we have positivity methods (such as Harnack’s inequality) at our disposal. The proof we display below is short, has a PDE flavor, and seems to be new.
It is based on Agmon’s positivity method
[
1
]
, which we combine with rank-one perturbation for
d\geq 3
and with first-passage percolation in dimensions
d\leq 2
.

The scaling
\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1
of the exponential decay with respect to
\lambda
is optimal, that is, that the rate of the exponential decay does not depend on
\lambda
when
\lambda\uparrow+\infty
and that it is of order
\sqrt{\lambda}
when
\lambda\leq 1
.
This has to do with the percolation assured in our setting. Indeed, given that
\varphi
is supported in
B_{1/10}
, the set where
V
is zero percolates. For every finite
\lambda
we have
G_{\lambda}\geq G_{\infty}
by the maximum principle, where we denote by
G_{\infty}
the limit of the Green function as
\lambda\uparrow+\infty
. It vanishes identically where
V
is positive and it can be viewed as a Dirichlet Green function of the set
\{V=0\}
. Since
G^{\infty}
itself does not carry a dependence on
\lambda
and does not decay faster than exponentially
by Harnack’s inequality,
the fact that
G_{\lambda}\geq G^{\infty}
assures that the rate of decay of
G_{\lambda}
cannot get faster as
\lambda\to\infty
. For the optimality of the scaling for
0\leq\lambda\ll 1
, we refer to

[
15
,
14
]
, which treat models to which our approach also applies.

2.
Proof of the exponential decay of the Green function

To start, let us recall perturbative deterministic estimates on
G_{\eta}
.
By the maximum principle,

(7)

0\leq G_{\eta}(x,y)\leq\tilde{G}_{\eta}(x-y),

where
\tilde{G}_{\eta}
denotes the
Green function of the massive Laplacian
-\triangle+\eta
.
The latter satisfies the two-sided bound (with different constants
c_{d}
)

(8)

\tilde{G}_{\eta}(y)\,\simeq\,\exp(-c_{d}\sqrt{\eta}|y|)\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\sqrt{\eta}^{-1}&:&d=1,\\
\log(2+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}|y|})&:&d=2,\\
|y|^{2-d}&:&d>2,\end{array}\right.

and its gradient satisfies

(9)

|\nabla\tilde{G}_{\eta}(y)|\,\lesssim\,\exp(-\sqrt{\eta}|y|)|y|^{1-d}.

Since
V
is positive,
we can apply Caccioppoli’s estimate to
\nabla G_{\eta}
as usual (say, on a ball
B_{|y|/2}(y)
) – see, e.g., Lemma 4.1 in
[
7
]
, and hence we also have a bound on
\nabla G_{\eta}
, however with
a dependence on
\eta
in dimensions
d=1,2
:

(10)

|\nabla G_{\eta}(y)|\,\lesssim\,\exp(-c_{d}\sqrt{\eta}|y|)|y|^{1-d}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\sqrt{\eta}^{-1}&:&d=1,\\
1+|\log\eta|&:&d=2,\\
1&:&d>2,\end{array}\right.

with a slightly different constant
c_{d}
still depending on dimension only.
Note that the exponential decay of
G_{\eta}
cannot be uniform with respect to
\eta>0
as there could be arbitrarily large regions where
V
is simply zero and
G_{\eta}
should more or less look like
G
. As we will show below, it is however uniform after taking a statistical average (and, in fact, any finite moment), which is an effect of randomness.

2.1.
Agmon’s positivity method

The upcoming lemma is the starting point of Agmon’s positivity method, cf.
[
1
]
.
It is a variant of Caccioppoli’s inequality when the test-function is chosen as the solution times an exponential weight.

Lemma 1
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a non-negative differentiable function, and
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a smooth function with compact support that vanishes on

Q:=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})^{d}
. Then

(11)

\tfrac{1}{2}\int\chi^{2}e^{2h}|\nabla G_{\eta}|^{2}+\int\chi^{2}e^{2h}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)(\lambda V-4|\nabla h|^{2})\,\leq\,4\int|\nabla\chi|^{2}e^{2h}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0),

where
\int
denotes
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

Proof.

Testing (
5
) with
\chi^{2}e^{2h}G_{\eta}(\cdot,0)
yields the identity

\int\nabla(\chi^{2}e^{2h}G_{\eta}(\cdot,0))\cdot\nabla G_{\eta}(\cdot,0)+\int(\eta+\lambda V)\chi^{2}e^{2h}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)=0.

Expanding
\nabla(\chi^{2}e^{2h}G_{\eta})=\chi^{2}e^{2h}\nabla G_{\eta}+2G_{\eta}\chi^{2}e^{2h}\nabla h+2G_{\eta}\chi e^{2h}\nabla\chi
, we obtain the claim by discarding the term with
\eta
, and using
ab\leq\frac{1}{8}a^{2}+2b^{2}

on each term to get

|(2G_{\eta}\chi^{2}e^{2h}\nabla h+2G_{\eta}\chi e^{2h}\nabla\chi)\cdot\nabla G_{\eta}|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\chi^{2}e^{2h}|\nabla G_{\eta}|^{2}+4G_{\eta}^{2}e^{2h}|\nabla\chi|^{2}+4G_{\eta}^{2}\chi^{2}e^{2h}|\nabla h|^{2}.

∎

In order to infer exponential decay of
G_{\eta}
from (
11
), one would need
h
to be linear.
However,
V
is not uniformly bounded from below, and if
h
is linear, the integrand on the left-hand side of (
11
) cannot be positive.
To circumvent this issue, in what follows, we shall combine (
11
) with Harnack’s inequality and two probabilistic ingredients, depending on
the
dimension:

•

For
d>2
, by taking the expectation of (
11
) and using a rank-one perturbation argument,
we may
choose
h(x)=c|x|

and conclude directly;

•

For
d\leq 2
, this rank-one perturbation argument does not work (since we have no deterministic a priori bound on
G_{\eta}
which is uniform in
\eta
). In this case (this holds in any dimension) up to technical details, we essentially choose for the Agmon function
h
the chemical distance of first passage percolation, which we then control using the classical results
[
13
]
by Kesten.

By the maximum principle, for all
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\prime}
we have
G_{\eta,\lambda^{\prime}}\leq G_{\eta,\lambda}
, so that
in the rest of this section we may assume without loss of generality that
\lambda\leq 1
. This will make the technicalities simpler as in many instances the constants in Harnack’s inequality will be uniform with respect to
\lambda
.

2.2.
Rank-one perturbation for
d\geq 3

We split the proof of (
6
) into two steps.

Step
1. Proof of (
6
), modulo the rank-one perturbation estimate (
14
).

In what follows, we denote by
Q(z)
the unit cube centered at
z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Since
\lambda\leq 1
and the size of
Q(z)
is 1, by Harnack’s inequality (see for instance
[
11
, Theorem 8.20]
) for non-negative solutions (
(-\triangle+(\lambda V+\eta))G_{\eta}=0
outside
Q
), there exists
C<\infty
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
z\neq 0
,

\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\leq C\inf_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)
.
Hence, (
11
) entails

(12)

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\int_{Q(z)}\chi^{2}e^{2h}(\tfrac{1}{C}\lambda V-|\nabla h|^{2})\,\lesssim\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\int_{Q(z)}|\nabla\chi|^{2}e^{2h}.

Set now
G_{*}(z):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Assume that
h
and
\chi
are deterministic.
By taking the expectation on both sides of the above, we obtain

(13)

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\int_{Q(z)}\chi^{2}e^{2h}(\tfrac{1}{C}\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda V\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\big]-G_{*}^{2}(z)|\nabla h|^{2})\,\leq\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}G_{*}^{2}(z)\int_{Q(z)}|\nabla\chi|^{2}e^{2h}.

Assume now that there exists
C<\infty
such that

(14)

\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda V\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\big]\geq\tfrac{1}{C}\lambda G_{*}^{2}(z)\varphi(\cdot-z).

Combined with (
14
), (
13
) then takes the form

(15)

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}G_{*}^{2}(z)\int_{Q(z)}\chi^{2}e^{2h}(\tfrac{1}{C^{2}}\lambda\varphi(\cdot-z)-|\nabla h|^{2})\,\leq\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}G_{*}^{2}(z)\int_{Q(z)}|\nabla\chi|^{2}e^{2h}.

At this point we basically want to choose
h
as a multiple of
\sqrt{\lambda}|x|
and
\chi
equal to 0 near zero and 1 away from a ball of radius 2 so that the integral on the left-hand side is roughly
e^{c\sqrt{\lambda}|z|}
and the right hand side is confined to a ball of radius 2 and bounded by a constant. This would give the desired exponential decay of the Green function. However, the formalities are a bit more technical than that, and we follow
[
12
, Appendix B]
.

For all
n,m
, define
\chi_{n}
and
h_{m}
via

\displaystyle\chi_{n}|_{Q_{1}}\equiv 0,\quad\chi_{n}|_{Q_{n}\setminus Q_{2}}\equiv 1,\quad\chi_{n}|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus Q_{n+1}}\equiv 0,\quad|\nabla\chi_{n}|\leq 2,

\displaystyle h_{m}(x)=\mu|x|_{\infty}\text{ for }x\in Q_{2m},\ h_{m}(x)=\mu m\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus Q_{2m},

where
Q_{n}
is a cube centered at zero of the sidelength
n
and

\mu=c\sqrt{\lambda}
with
c
to be chosen later. With these choices, for any finite
n,m
the inequality (
15
) is satisfied with
h=h_{m}
and
\chi=\chi_{n}
. Fixing
m
and using the decay of the Green function given by
G_{\eta}\leq\tilde{G}_{\eta}
, and (
8
) and boundedness of
h_{m}
, we can pass to the limit
n\uparrow+\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem. Combining all the terms with
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|z|_{\infty}\leq 2
, on the right-hand side of the inequality, this gives

(16)

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,|z|_{\infty}>2}G_{*}^{2}(z)\int_{Q(z)}e^{2h_{m}}(\tfrac{1}{C^{2}}\lambda\varphi(\cdot-z)-\mu^{2})\,\\
\leq\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\},\,|z|_{\infty}\leq 2}G_{*}^{2}(z)\int_{Q(z)}(\tfrac{1}{C^{2}}\lambda\varphi(\cdot-z)+\mu^{2}+4)e^{2h_{m}}.

We now choose
\mu
. By definition of
h_{m}
we have (up to redefining
C
)

\int_{Q(z)}e^{2h_{m}}\tfrac{1}{C^{2}}\varphi(\cdot-z)\geq\Big(\int_{Q}\varphi\Big)\int_{Q(z)}e^{2h_{m}}e^{-2\mu}\tfrac{1}{C^{2}}=\int_{Q(z)}e^{2h_{m}}e^{-2\mu}\tfrac{1}{C},

so that for the choice
\mu=c\sqrt{\lambda}
with a suitable
c
and using the deterministic bound

G_{\eta}(x,0)\leq C|x|^{2-d}

(recall that
d\geq 3
), (
16
) turns into

\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,|z|_{\infty}>2}G_{*}^{2}(z)\tfrac{1}{C}{\lambda}\int_{Q(z)}e^{2h_{m}}\,\leq\,C

(we only care about small
\lambda
).
By the monotone convergence theorem in the limit
m\uparrow+\infty
, this entails for some constants
C\geq 1\geq c>0
independent of
\lambda

G_{*}(z)\,\leq\,C\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\exp(-c\sqrt{\lambda}|z|)\quad\mbox{for all $z\neq 0$}.

Combining this with Harnack’s inequality and the deterministic bound
G_{\eta}(x,y)\leq C|x-y|^{2-d}
, then yields

\mathbb{E}\left[(\int_{Q(x)}G_{\eta}(\cdot,0))^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\exp(-c\sqrt{\lambda}|x|)\Big)\wedge 1,\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,}

and once again using Harnack’s inequality, the same deterministic bound, and interpolation, we establish

0\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,y+y^{\prime})dy^{\prime})^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{2p}}\leq\,C\Big(\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2p}}\exp(-\tfrac{c}{p}\sqrt{\lambda}|x-y|)\Big)\wedge 1,\,\,\mbox{for all $p\geq 1$, $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$,}

and ultimately, (
6
).

Step
2. Proof of (
14
) by rank-one perturbation.

For all
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, denote by
G_{\eta}^{z,-}
the Green function
for the potential
V^{z,-}
associated with
\omega^{z,-}

defined by
\omega^{z,-}_{j}=\omega_{j}
if
j\neq z
and
\omega^{z,-}_{z}=1
. By the maximum principle we have
0\leq G_{\eta}^{z,-}\leq G_{\eta}
, so that

\mathbb{E}\big[V\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\big]\geq\mathbb{E}\big[V\sup_{Q(z)}(G_{\eta}^{z,-}(\cdot,0))^{2}\big].

On
Q(z)
,
V
only depends on
\omega_{z}
whereas
G_{\eta}^{z,-}(\cdot,0)
is independent on
\omega_{z}
by construction, so that by independence,

\mathbb{E}\big[V\sup_{Q(z)}(G_{\eta}^{z,-}(\cdot,0))^{2}\big]=\varphi(\cdot-z)\mathbb{E}\left[\omega\right]\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{Q(z)}(G_{\eta}^{z,-}(\cdot,0))^{2}\big].

To prove (
14
), it remains to argue that
\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{Q(z)}(G_{\eta}^{z,-}(\cdot,0))^{2}\big]\gtrsim\mathbb{E}\big[\sup_{Q(z)}(G_{\eta}(\cdot,0))^{2}\big]
. To this aim, we
notice
that the function
G_{\eta}-G_{\eta}^{z,-}
satisfies the equation

(17)

-\triangle(G_{\eta}-G_{\eta}^{z,-})+(\lambda V+\eta)(G_{\eta}-G_{\eta}^{z,-})=\lambda(1-\omega_{z})\varphi(\cdot-z)G^{z,-}_{\eta},

so that we have the Green representation formula

(18)

(G_{\eta}-G_{\eta}^{z,-})(x,0)=\lambda\int_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}(x,x^{\prime})(1-\omega_{z})\varphi(x^{\prime}-z)G_{\eta}^{z,-}(x^{\prime},0)dx^{\prime}.

We combine (
18
) with the deterministic bound
0\leq G_{\eta}(y,y^{\prime})\leq C|y^{\prime}-y|^{2-d}
to the effect that for all
x\in Q(z)
,

0\leq(G_{\eta}-G_{\eta}^{z,-})(x)\leq C\lambda\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{z,-},

from which we deduce (using
\lambda\leq 1
)

\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{z,-}\geq\tfrac{1}{C}\sup_{Q(z)}G_{\eta},

and therefore (
14
).

2.3.
Agmon function and first-passage percolation for
d\geq 1

In low dimensions
d=1,2
, we do not have the deterministic and uniform bound
G_{\eta}\lesssim 1
.
By a percolation argument,
we will first check
that for all
p\geq 1
,
\sup_{z}\mathbb{E}\left[(\int_{Q(z)}G_{\eta}^{p}(\cdot,0))^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim_{p}1
, which we refer to as anchoring, cf. Lemma
2
below. This is however not sufficient to run (optimally) the rank-one perturbation argument displayed above for
d\geq 3
.
Instead, we use a quenched approach, and use as a function
h
in Lemma
1
the chemical distance in first passage percolation. Using the classical bounds
[
13
]
by Kesten on first passage percolation, we then post-process the quenched bounds into annealed bounds on the Green function.

2.3.1.
Anchoring by percolation

Lemma 2

(Anchoring)
.

There exists a stationary random field
r_{*}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
\eta>0
and all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

we have

0\leq\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime})dy^{\prime}\leq r_{*}(x,y)+\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}d=1&:&C\lambda^{-1},\\
d=2&:&C\lambda^{-1},\\
d>2&:&1,\end{array}\right.

and for all
p\geq 1
,

(19)

\sup_{x,y}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{*}^{p}(x,y)\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}d=1&:&p,\\
d=2&:&\log(p+1),\\
d>2&:&1.\end{array}\right.

In dimensions
d>2
, (
19
) follows from the deterministic bound
G_{\eta}(x,y)\lesssim|x-y|^{2-d}
on the Green function. In dimensions
d\leq 2
, (
19
)
will follow
from integrating the defining equation for
G_{\eta}
in combination with the maximum principle and a percolation argument (only needed for
d=2
).

Proof of Lemma
2
.

It remains to treat
d=1,2
.
We integrate the equation for
G_{\eta}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which yields using exponential decay

(20)

\int(\lambda V+\eta)(y)G_{\eta}(0,y)dy=1.

We start with
d=1
, and then turn to
d=2
.

Step
1. Dimension
d=1
.

Let
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
y_{1}>y^{\prime}
(respectively,
y_{-1}<y^{\prime}
) be the closest integer to
y^{\prime}
such that
\omega_{y_{1}}=1
(respectively,
\omega_{y_{-1}}=1
). Then, by (
20
) and Harnack’s inequality (say, on
Q(y_{1})
),
0\leq G_{\eta}(0,y_{1})\leq C\lambda^{-1}
(respectively,
0\leq G_{\eta}(0,y_{-1})\leq C\lambda^{-1}
). By the maximum principle, we thus have
0\leq G_{\eta}(0,y)\leq g(y)
, where
g
solves

-g^{\prime\prime}(y)=\delta(y)\text{ on }(y_{-1},y_{1}),\quad g(y_{-1})=G_{\eta}(0,y_{-1}),\quad g(y_{1})=G_{\eta}(0,y_{1}).

The function
g
can be decomposed as a sum of the Laplace’s Dirichlet Green function on
(y_{-1},y_{1})
and a harmonic function with data
G_{\eta}(0,y_{-1})
,
G_{\eta}(0,y_{1})
at the endpoints. The latter is a linear function maximized
for
G_{\eta}(0,y_{-1})
or
G_{\eta}(0,y_{1})
,
and the former is
at most
y_{1}-y_{-1}
,
so that all in all we have

0\leq g(y^{\prime})\leq G_{\eta}(0,y_{-1})+G_{\eta}(0,y_{1})+y_{1}-y_{-1},

so that

(21)

G_{\eta}(0,y^{\prime})\,\leq\,C\lambda^{-1}+(y_{1}-y_{-1}).

We then set
r_{*}(0,y^{\prime}):=y_{1}-y_{-1}
, and it remains to notice that for all
n>0
,
\mathbb{P}[y_{1}-y^{\prime}=n]=\mathbb{P}[y^{\prime}-y_{-1}=n]\leq C\exp(-\frac{1}{C}n)
, to the effect that
\mathbb{E}\left[r_{*}(0,y^{\prime})^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq Cp
for all
p\geq 1
.
To pass from integers
y^{\prime}
to real numbers, we use Harnack’s inequality.
Since
0
plays no role in the argument, this concludes the proof for
d=1
.

Step
2. Dimension
d=2
.
The idea is the same as for
d=1
.
Set

g_{\eta}(y)=\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,y)dx
, so that, by (
20
),

(22)

\int(\lambda V+\eta)(y)g_{\eta}(y)dy=1

and, by (
5
),

(23)

(-\triangle_{y}+(\lambda V+\eta))g_{\eta}(y)=\mathds{1}_{Q}(y).

Much as for
d=1
, given
y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we want to construct a set
C(y^{\prime})
containing
y^{\prime}
, and such that on its boundary
\partial C(y^{\prime})
we control the
function
g
. Because of (
22
),
we know that on any cube where
V
is larger than a constant
c_{0}
the function
g\lesssim 1/(\lambda c_{0})
. The problem is that these cubes might fail to
percolate and form connected sets, much less the sets at a controlled distance from
y^{\prime}
. The remedy is to enlarge these cubes by a fixed multiple depending on the probability law only to ensure percolation and preserve the control of the Green function using the Harnack inequality.

For future reference, we consider general dimension
d
. To this end, consider the partition
\mathcal{Q}_{k}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into cubes
Q_{k}+(2^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{d}
, where
Q_{k}=[-2^{k-1},2^{k-1})^{d}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
will be chosen below (depending on the probability law of
V
and independently of
\lambda
). Ultimately,
2^{k}
will be the multiple alluded to before.
Call
\mathcal{T}_{k}
the graph associated with
(2^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{d}
via
e\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
iff
e=(z,z^{\prime})
with
z,z^{\prime}\in(2^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{d}
,

|z-z^{\prime}|=2^{k}
(the Euclidean distance).
For each edge
e=(z,z^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
we associate the open cube
Q^{\prime}_{e}
centered at
\frac{z+z^{\prime}}{2}
and of sidelength
2^{k-1}
(to the effect that those cubes are disjoint).
We now define conductivities
(\xi_{e})_{e\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}
as follows. Let
\gamma>0
be such that
\mathbb{P}[\omega\geq\gamma]>0
.
Set
\xi_{e}=1

if
\max_{j\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap Q^{\prime}_{e}}\omega_{j}\geq\gamma
, and
\xi_{e}=0
otherwise.
The sequence
\{\xi_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}
has iid Bernoulli entries with the probability that
\xi_{e}=0
equal to

p=\mathbb{P}[\omega<\gamma]^{\sharp(Q^{\prime}_{e}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq\mathbb{P}[\omega<\gamma]^{\frac{1}{C}2^{k}-1}

as
\sharp(Q^{\prime}_{e}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})\geq\frac{1}{C}2^{k}-1
. We then choose
k
large enough (and depending only on the probability law
F
) such that
p<\frac{1}{2}
, so that the edges
e
such that
\xi_{e}=1
percolate
almost surely.

Denote by
\mathcal{E}
the unique percolation cluster (a set of edges) given by the procedure above and by
\mathcal{Z}
the set of vertices of
2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
forming the endpoints of these edges.
Notice that Harnack’s inequality yields on each cube
Q_{k}(z)=z+Q_{k}
with
z\in\mathcal{Z}

(24)

\sup_{Q_{k}(z)}g_{\eta}\,\leq\,C_{k}\Bigl(\inf_{Q_{k}(z)}g_{\eta}+1\Bigr)

(with a constant depending on
k
but independent of
\lambda
, which is possible due to our assumption
\lambda\leq 1
). If
y^{\prime}\in\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}Q_{k}(z)
, then by (
22
) and (
24
),
g_{\eta}(y^{\prime})\leq C\lambda^{-1}
.
If
y^{\prime}\notin\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}Q_{k}(z)
, hence
y^{\prime}\in Q_{k}(z_{y})
for some
z_{y}\in 2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{Z}
,
we call
\mathcal{C}_{d}(y^{\prime})
the connected component of
2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\mathcal{Z}

containing
z_{y}
– where connectedness is understood in the discrete sense,
that is, two points
x^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime\prime}
of
2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are connected iff
|x^{\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}|=2^{k}
(again, the Euclidean distance).
We then set
\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime})=\cup_{z\in\mathcal{C}_{d}(y^{\prime})}Q_{k}(z)
, which is our analogue of the set
(y_{-1},y_{1})\ni y^{\prime}
from the
d=1
case.

On
\partial\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime})
, (
22
) and (
24
) imply that
g_{\eta}\leq C\lambda^{-1}
. Similarly to the case
d=1
, we then control
g_{\eta}
inside
\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime})
by
g
, the function defined by

-\triangle g=\mathds{1}_{Q}\text{ in }C(y^{\prime}),\quad g=g_{\eta}\text{ on }\partial\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime}),

where
Q
is again the unit cube centered at
0
that was used to define
g_{\eta}
in (
23
).
By the maximum principle and the fact that
\log(|x|)
is the fundamental solution of the Laplacian,

0\leq\sup_{\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime})}g\leq\sup_{\partial\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime})}g_{\eta}+C\log(\operatorname{diam}\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime}))
, so that

g_{\eta}(y^{\prime})\leq C\lambda^{-1}+C\log(\operatorname{diam}\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime}))

(analogously to (
21
) above).
The conclusion follows from subcritical Bernoulli percolation estimates in the form
\mathbb{P}[\operatorname{diam}\mathcal{C}(y^{\prime})\geq n]\lesssim\exp(-\frac{1}{C}n)
(see
[
2
]
) and the fact that
G_{\eta}(x,y)=G_{\eta}(y,x)
.
∎

2.3.2.
Agmon distance, exponential decay, and first-passage percolation

We now prove Theorem
3
for
d=1,2
. The argument is a probabilistic upgrade of the Agmon exponential decay estimates that we used for the case
d>2
.
When
d>2
we were able to
provide deterministic anchoring of the Green function and this furnished a more straightforward argument. For low dimensions, the proof is resonating but more delicate as the probabilistic argument has to be intertwined with the elliptic considerations from §
2.2
.
The upcoming proof holds in any dimension
d\geq 1
, which we all treat at once.
The strategy relies on the exponential decay measured in the chemical distance to the origin using Agmon’s positivity method, followed by a “change of variables” using first passage percolation estimates to revert back to the Euclidean distance.

Step
1. The chemical distance.

We use the notation of Step 2 of the proof of Lemma
2
.
In this case,
\mathcal{E}
is the set of edges of
(2^{k})\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\{\xi_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
denotes a set of weights on edges.
Recall that if
\xi_{[x,x^{\prime}]}=1
, then there exists
z\in Q_{k+1}(x)\cap Q_{k+1}(x^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
w_{z}>\gamma>0
.
We say that
\pi=(x_{0},\dots,x_{N+1})
is a path of length
N+1
in
(2^{k})\mathbb{Z}^{d}
between
x_{0}
and
x_{N+1}
if for all
0\leq j\leq N
we have
|x_{j+1}-x_{j}|=2^{k}
. The path is self-avoiding if
x_{j}\neq x_{i}
for all
i\neq j
.
To any path
\pi
we assign the weight

w(\pi)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\xi_{[x_{j},x_{j+1}]}.

We then define the chemical distance
d_{\chi}:2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times 2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
via

d_{\chi}(x,x^{\prime})=\inf\Big\{w(\pi)\,:\,\pi\text{ path between $x$ and $x^{\prime}$}\Big\}.

Finally, we define the function
h:2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},x\mapsto d_{\chi}(x,0)
, which we extend on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by using a continuous piecewise affine interpolation (say, on a tetrahedral tessellation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
associated with
2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
).
Since for all
x,x^{\prime}\in 2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
|x-x^{\prime}|=2^{k}
,
|d_{\chi}(x,0)-d_{\chi}(x^{\prime},0)|\in\{0,1\}
, this extension is
2^{-k}
-Lipschitz.

Step
2. Chemical exponential decay via Agmon positivity method.

Set
(2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{*}:=\{x\in 2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,|\,|x|\geq 2^{k+2}\}
, and define
G_{\eta,*}=\sum_{x\in(2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{*}}(\sup_{Q_{k+1}(x)}G_{\eta})\mathds{1}_{Q_{k}(x)}
.
By Harnack’s inequality on each cube
Q_{k+1}(x)
(which do not contain the origin), we have

\sup_{Q_{k+1}(x)}G_{\eta}\,\leq\,C_{k}\inf_{Q_{k+1}(x)}G_{\eta}

(with a constant depending on
k
but independent of
\lambda\leq 1
).
Proceeding as in Step 1 in Subsection
2.2

and keeping the gradient term in (
11
), we obtain for all
\mu>0
and
m\geq 1
by monotone and dominated convergence

\int e^{2(h\wedge m)\mu}|\nabla G_{\eta}|^{2}+\sum_{x\in(2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{*}}e^{2(h(x)\wedge m)\mu}\Big(\int_{Q_{k+1}(x)}G_{\eta,*}^{2}\tfrac{e^{-2\mu}}{C^{2}}\lambda V-\int_{Q_{k}(x)}G_{\eta,*}^{2}Ce^{\mu}\mu^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\Big)\\
\leq\,C\int_{Q_{k+1}(0)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0).

If for some
x\in 2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
\nabla h|_{Q_{k}(x)}\not\equiv 0
, then there exists
z\in Q_{k+1}(x)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\omega_{z}=1
. Combined with the
2^{-k}
-Lipschitz property of
h
, this entails with the choice
\mu=c\sqrt{\lambda}

\int_{Q_{k+1}(x)}G_{\eta,*}^{2}\tfrac{e^{-2\mu}}{C^{2}}\lambda V-\int_{Q_{k}(x)}G_{\eta,*}^{2}Ce^{\mu}\mu^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\geq 0,

so that the above takes the form

(25)

\int e^{2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}|\nabla G_{\eta}|^{2}\,\leq\,C\int_{Q_{k+1}(0)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0).

Step
3. Control of the chemical distance.

Since
p
is chosen small enough, we are in the regime of super-critical percolation, and
[
13
, Proposition (5.8)]
reads: There exists
0\leq c\leq 1\leq C
such that for all
R\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}[\exists\text{ a self-avoiding path }\pi\\
\text{ starting at 0 of length at least $2^{k}R$ such that }w(\pi)\leq cR]\leq C\exp(-cR).

This implies in particular that for all
R\geq 1

\mathbb{P}[d_{\chi}(0,2^{k}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus[-2^{k-1}R,2^{k-1}R)^{d})\leq cR]\leq C\exp(-cR),

so that there exist
c,C
such that for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
,

(26)

\mathbb{P}[\inf_{S_{l}^{+}}h\leq c2^{l}]\leq C\exp(-c2^{l}).

Step
4. Conclusion.

It remains to post-process (
25
) and (
26
).
Set
S_{l}:=\{2^{l}<|x|\leq 2^{l+1}\}
and
S_{l}^{+}:=\{2^{l}<|x|\leq 2^{l+2}\}
.
On the one hand, since
G_{\eta}
decays exponentially fast by (
7
) & (
8
), we have the telescopic identity

\fint_{S_{j}}G_{\eta}=\sum_{l=j}^{\infty}\Big(\fint_{S_{l}}G_{\eta}-\fint_{S_{l+1}}G_{\eta}\Big).

On the other hand, by the fundamental theorem of calculus and Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality, and (
25
),

\displaystyle\Big|\fint_{S_{l}}G_{\eta}-\fint_{S_{l+1}}G_{\eta}\Big|\,\leq

\displaystyle\,C2^{l}\Big(\fint_{S_{l}^{+}}e^{2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}|\nabla G_{\eta}|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\fint_{S_{l}^{+}}e^{-2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,C\Big(\int_{Q_{k+1}(0)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(2^{l})^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\fint_{S_{l}^{+}}e^{-2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, by Harnack’s inequality,

\sup_{S_{j}}G_{\eta}\,\leq\,C\Big(\int_{Q_{k+1}(0)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{l=j}^{\infty}(2^{l})^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\Big(\fint_{S_{l}^{+}}e^{-2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Combined with the following consequence of (
26
)

\mathbb{E}\left[\fint_{S_{l}^{+}}e^{-2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\Big(\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-c\sqrt{\lambda}2^{l}}\mathds{1}_{\inf_{S_{l}^{+}}h>c2^{l}}\right]+\mathbb{P}[\inf_{S_{l}^{+}}h\leq c2^{l}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\leq\,Ce^{-c\sqrt{\lambda}2^{l}},

and with Lemma
2
,
this implies by Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality in probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{S_{j}}G_{\eta}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\,C\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{Q_{k+1}(0)}G_{\eta}^{2}(\cdot,0)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{l=j}^{\infty}(2^{l})^{1-\frac{d}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\fint_{S_{l}^{+}}e^{-2c\sqrt{\lambda}h}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,C\sum_{l=j}^{\infty}(2^{l})^{1-\frac{d}{2}}e^{-c\sqrt{\lambda}2^{l}}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}d=1&:&\lambda^{-1},\\
d=2&:&\lambda^{-1},\\
d>2&:&1,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,Ce^{-c\sqrt{\lambda}2^{j}}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}d=1&:&\lambda^{-1},\\
d=2&:&\lambda^{-1},\\
d>2&:&1,\end{array}\right.

and (
6
) follows by interpolation with Lemma
2
again.

3.
Application to the landscape function

3.1.
Existence and control of the moments: proof of Theorem
1

Let
0<\eta<1
and consider the modified landscape equation

(27)

-\triangle u_{\eta}+\lambda(V+\eta)u_{\eta}=1.

By the exponentially weighted energy estimate

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-c\sqrt{\eta}|x|)(|\nabla u_{\eta}|^{2}+(\lambda V+\eta)u_{\eta}^{2})(x)dx\lesssim\frac{1}{\eta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-c\sqrt{\eta}|x|)dx

(which we obtain by testing the equation with
x\mapsto\exp(-c\sqrt{\eta}|x|)u_{\eta}
for
c>0
small enough), this equation is well-posed in the space
H^{1}_{{\operatorname{uloc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{v\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{B(x)}v^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2}<+\infty\}
. In addition we have the
representation formula for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(28)

0<u_{\eta}(x)\,=\,\int G_{\eta}(x,y)dy\,\leq\,\int\tilde{G}_{\eta}(y)dy,

and
u_{\eta}
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with the bound depending on
\eta
).
By uniqueness in the space above,

u_{\eta}
is stationary, and
we now check that
the energy estimate for (
27
),
in combination with the ergodic theorem,
yields

(29)

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{Q}|\nabla u_{\eta}|^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{Q}u_{\eta}\right].

Indeed, let
\chi
be a smooth non-negative, compactly supported function of mass unity.
For all
R\geq 1
, set
\chi_{R}:=R^{-d}\chi(\cdot/R)
.
Then for all
R
we have by testing (
27
) with
\chi_{R}u_{\eta}
and discarding the non-negative term in the right hand side

\displaystyle\int\chi_{R}u_{\eta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\nabla(\chi_{R}u_{\eta})\cdot\nabla u_{\eta}+(\lambda V+\eta)\chi_{R}u_{\eta}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int\chi_{R}|\nabla u_{\eta}|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{R}\int R^{-d}u_{\eta}|\nabla\chi|(\frac{\cdot}{R})|\nabla u_{\eta}|.

Since
u_{\eta}\in H^{1}_{{\operatorname{uloc}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the second right-hand side term vanishes in the limit
R\uparrow+\infty
, and (
29
) follows by the ergodic theorem in the form

\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\int\chi_{R}u_{\eta}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{Q}u_{\eta}\right],\quad\lim_{R\uparrow+\infty}\int R^{-d}\chi(\frac{\cdot}{R})|\nabla u_{\eta}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{Q}|\nabla u_{\eta}|^{2}\right].

Since
u_{\eta}
is non-negative and uniformly bounded,
for all
p\geq 1
we have
\sup_{x}\mathbb{E}\left[u_{\eta}(x)^{p}\right]<\infty
.
We wish to pass to the limit in this bound as
\eta\downarrow 0
, and therefore need uniformity with respect to
\eta
.
To this end, we appeal to Theorem
3
.
Taking the
p
-th power and the expectation of (
28
), we have by Minkowski’s inequality
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u_{\eta}(x)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\int G_{\eta}(x,y)dy\Big)^{p}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,z+z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\Big)^{p}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,z+z^{\prime})dz^{\prime}\Big)^{p}\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

from which we conclude that
\mathbb{E}\left[u_{\eta}(x)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\lesssim_{p,\lambda}\,1
.
By elliptic regularity (Moser estimates - cf., e.g.,
[
11
, Theorem 8.22]
),

\sup_{Q}u_{\eta}\lesssim 1+\int_{2Q}u_{\eta},

so that the above yields for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(30)

\mathbb{E}\left[(\sup_{Q}u_{\eta})^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\lesssim_{p,\lambda}\,1.

This furnishes the desired moment bounds (
3
) for
u_{\eta}
in place of
u
, and it remains to pass to the limit.
In particular, used for
p=1
and
p=2
, and combined with (
29
), this entails that
u_{\eta}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}(Q))

(where
\Omega
is our probability set of events
\omega
) independently of
\eta>0
.
Hence, by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, there is a subsequence converging weakly towards a function
u\in L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}(Q))
(which we may extend by stationarity as a function of
L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
) that satisfies the same moments bounds as
u_{\eta}
.
It remains to show that
u
solves (
2
).
Let
\zeta\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By testing
(
27
) with
\chi
, multiplying by
\zeta
, and taking the expectation,
we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta\Big(\int\nabla\chi\cdot\nabla u_{\eta}+(\lambda V+\eta)\chi u_{\eta}-\chi\Big)\right]=0.

Taking the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
(along the subsequence) yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta\Big(\int\nabla\chi\cdot\nabla u+\lambda V\chi u-\chi\Big)\right]=0.

Since
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is separable and
\zeta
is arbitrary, this implies that almost surely
we have for all
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\int\nabla\chi\cdot\nabla u+\lambda V\chi u=\int\chi,

and hence
u
is a distributional solution of (
2
) almost surely.
Uniqueness is standard and follows the proof of (
29
) (we simply
use
u\in L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}(Q))
rather than
u_{\eta}\in H^{1}_{\operatorname{uloc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
almost surely).
Indeed let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
be two stationary solutions in
L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}(Q))
, and let
\chi
be a smooth non-negative, compactly supported function of mass unity.
For all
R\geq 1
, set
\chi_{R}:=R^{-d}\chi(\cdot/R)
.
Then for all
R
we have by testing (
2
) with
\chi_{R}(u_{1}-u_{2})

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\nabla(\chi_{R}(u_{1}-u_{2}))\cdot\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})+\lambda V\chi_{R}(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int\chi_{R}|\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})|^{2}-\frac{1}{R}\int R^{-d}|u_{1}-u_{2}||\nabla\chi|(\frac{\cdot}{R})|\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})|.

Since
u_{1},u_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega,H^{1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
are stationary, taking the limit
R\uparrow+\infty
yields by the ergodic theorem

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{Q}|\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})|^{2}\right]=0,

so that
\nabla u_{1}=\nabla u_{2}
. This implies in turn that
u_{1}=u_{2}+K
for some random constant
K
. Using the zero order term in the above in form of
0=\int\lambda V\chi_{R}K^{2}
for
R\gg 1
large enough so that
V|_{\operatorname{supp}\chi_{R}}\not\equiv 0
(which holds true almost surely), we deduce that
K=0
, and therefore
u_{1}=u_{2}
.

We conclude this subsection by proving that
u_{\eta}
converges strongly to
u
in
L^{2}(\Omega,W^{1,\infty}(Q))
(this will be needed to control covariances).
The difference
u_{\eta}-u
satisfies the equation

-\triangle(u_{\eta}-u)+(\lambda V+\eta)(u_{\eta}-u)=\eta u,

so that

(u_{\eta}-u)(x)=-\eta\int G_{\eta}(x,y)u(y)dy.

Hence, by the triangle inequality followed by Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[(u_{\eta}-u)(x)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\leq\,\eta\int\mathbb{E}\left[(\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,y+y^{\prime})dy^{\prime})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{Q}u(y+\cdot)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}dy.

By Theorems
1
and
3
, the right-hand side is of order
\eta
.
Combined with elliptic regularity in the form

(31)

\sup_{Q}|\nabla(u-u_{\eta})|^{2}+\sup_{Q}|u-u_{\eta}|^{2}\,\lesssim\,\int_{2Q}(u-u_{\eta})^{2}+\eta\sup_{2Q}u^{2},

and with Theorem
1
, this entails the claimed convergence in
L^{2}(\Omega,W^{1,\infty}(Q))

(32)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{Q}\Big(|u_{\eta}-u|^{2}+|\nabla(u_{\eta}-u)|^{2}\Big)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\lesssim\,\eta.

3.2.
Control of covariances: proof of Theorem
2

Since
\omega=\{\omega_{z}\}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is iid, we have the covariance inequality
for any measurable maps
X
and
Y
of
\omega

(33)

|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{X};{Y}\right]|\,\leq\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[(X-X_{z})^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-Y_{z})^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
X_{z}:=X(\omega^{z})
with
\omega^{z}_{z^{\prime}}=\omega_{z^{\prime}}
for all
z^{\prime}\neq z
and
\omega^{z}_{z}

distributed according to
F
independently of
\omega
.
This is the main ingredient in the proof Theorem
2
.

We start with the covariance of the function
u
itself.
By (
32
), it is enough to prove the claim for
u_{\eta}
and pass to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
in the estimates.
We shall apply (
33
) to
X=u_{\eta}(x)
and
Y=u_{\eta}(y)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denote by
u_{\eta}^{z}
the modifed landscape function with
\omega
replaced by
\omega^{z}
(which exists on a deterministic basis). The function
\delta_{z}u_{\eta}:=u_{\eta}-u_{\eta}^{z}
solves

-\triangle\delta_{z}u_{\eta}+(\lambda V+\eta)\delta_{z}u_{\eta}=\lambda(\omega_{z}-\omega)\varphi(\cdot-z)u^{z}_{\eta}.

By the Green representation formula,
we obtain (recall that
\varphi
is supported in
Q
)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[|\delta_{z}u_{\eta}(x)|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\int G_{\eta}(x,y)(\omega_{z}-\omega)(y)\varphi(y-z)u^{z}(y)dy\Big)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[(\omega_{z}-\omega)^{2}\Big(\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)\varphi(y^{\prime})u^{z}_{\eta}(y^{\prime}+z)dy^{\prime}\Big)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Since
\varphi
is bounded and
|\omega_{z}-\omega|\leq 1
, this yields by Theorem
3
and (
30
) (recall that
u^{z}
has the same law as
u
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[|\delta_{z}u_{\eta}(x)|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\int_{Q}G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)dy^{\prime}\Big)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\sup_{Q}u^{z}_{\eta}(z+\cdot)\Big)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\exp(-c(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|z-x|).

Inserting this bound into (
33
) applied to
X=u_{\eta}(x)
and
Y=u_{\eta}(y)
,
we obtain

(34)

|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{u_{\eta}(x)};{u_{\eta}(y)}\right]|\,\lesssim\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\exp(-c(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-z|)\exp(-c(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|z-y|)\\
\lesssim\,\exp(-c(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|),

which yields Theorem
2
for
u
by passing to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
.

We now turn to
\frac{1}{u}
.
Since for all
\eta\leq\lambda
,
u\geq u_{\eta}\geq\int\tilde{G}_{\lambda}=:\tau_{\lambda}>0
(a deterministic positive lower bound) by the maximum principle,
(
32
) also entails

(35)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{Q}\Big|\frac{1}{u_{\eta}}-\frac{1}{u}\Big|^{2}\right]\lesssim\eta,

so that it is enough to bound the covariance of
\frac{1}{u_{\eta}}
and pass to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
. We will consider a slightly more general case.

Let
g
be a twice-differentiable function.
By the Taylor formula

\delta_{z}g(u_{\eta}):=g(u^{z}_{\eta})-g(u_{\eta})=g(u_{\eta}-\delta_{z}u_{\eta})-g(u_{\eta})=-g^{\prime}(u_{\eta})\delta_{z}u_{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{z})(\delta_{z}u_{\eta})^{2},

with
\alpha_{z}:=tu_{\eta}+(1-t)u^{z}_{\eta}
for some
t\in[0,1]
.
Applied to
g(s)=1/s^{2}
,
s>0
, this yields

\delta_{z}g(u_{\eta})=\frac{1}{u^{2}_{\eta}}\delta_{z}u_{\eta}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{z}^{3}}(\delta_{z}u_{\eta})^{2},

since
\frac{1}{u_{\eta}}<\frac{1}{\tau_{\lambda}}<\infty
and the same is true for
u^{z}_{\eta}
.
Hence, as before, (
34
) holds for
\frac{1}{u_{\eta}}
in the form

(36)

\left|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{\frac{1}{u_{\eta}(x)}};{\frac{1}{u_{\eta}(y)}}\right]\right|\,\lesssim\,\exp(-c(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|),

which yields Theorem
2
for
\frac{1}{u}
by passing to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
.

Since
\nabla\log u=\frac{\nabla u}{u}
, by (
35
),
it is enough to prove bounds on the covariance of
\nabla\log u_{\eta}
and pass to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
.
By the “discrete” Leibniz rule,

(37)

\delta_{z}\nabla\log u_{\eta}\,=\,(\nabla\delta_{z}u_{\eta})\frac{1}{u_{\eta}}-(\delta_{z}u_{\eta})\frac{\nabla u_{\eta}^{z}}{u_{\eta}u_{\eta}^{z}}.

By (
31
) and Theorem
1
and since
\frac{1}{u_{\eta}}<\frac{1}{\tau_{\lambda}}<\infty
, we have for all
p\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{Q}\Big|\frac{\nabla u_{\eta}^{z}}{u_{\eta}u_{\eta}^{z}}\Big|^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}}\lesssim 1,

which allows us to treat the second right-hand side of (
37
) as we did before.
For the first right-hand side term, we distinguish two regimes.
If
|x-z|\leq 2
, we use the triangle inequality and obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[|\delta_{z}\nabla u_{\eta}(x)|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[|\nabla u_{\eta}(x)|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\lesssim\,1.

If
|x-z|>2
, we differentiate the Green representation formula
and obtain as for
u_{\eta}
itself

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\left[|\delta_{z}\nabla u_{\eta}(x)|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\int_{Q}|\nabla_{x}G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)|dy^{\prime}\Big)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\sup_{Q}u_{\eta}^{z}(z+\cdot)\Big)^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Since
G_{\eta}(x,\cdot)
satisfies
-\triangle G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)+(\lambda V+\eta)G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)=0

for
y^{\prime}+z
away from
x
, we may differentiate the equation with respect to
x
and use elliptic regularity to the effect that

\int_{Q}|\nabla_{x}G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)|dy^{\prime}\,\lesssim\,\int_{(-2,2)^{d}}G_{\eta}(x,y^{\prime}+z)dy^{\prime}dx.

At this point, we may conclude as for
u_{\eta}
itself, and finally obtain the desired
variant of (
34
) for
\nabla\log u_{\eta}

(38)

|\operatorname{Cov}\left[{\nabla\log u_{\eta}(x)};{\nabla\log u_{\eta}(y)}\right]|\,\lesssim\,\exp(-c(\sqrt{\lambda}\wedge 1)|x-y|),

which yields Theorem
2
for
\nabla\log u
by passing to the limit
\eta\downarrow 0
.
