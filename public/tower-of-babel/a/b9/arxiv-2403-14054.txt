Adaptive finite element interpolated neural networks
Santiago Badia 1,2,∗, Wei Li 1, Alberto F. Martín 3

Abstract
. The use of neural networks to approximate partial differential equations (PDEs) has gained significant attention in recent years. However, the approximation of PDEs with localised phenomena, e.g., sharp gradients and singularities, remains a challenge, due to ill-defined cost functions in terms of pointwise residual sampling or poor numerical integration. In this work, we introduce h h -adaptive finite element interpolated neural networks. The method relies on the interpolation of a neural network onto a finite element space that is gradually adapted to the solution during the training process to equidistribute a posteriori error indicator. The use of adaptive interpolation is essential in preserving the non-linear approximation capabilities of the neural networks to effectively tackle problems with localised features. The training relies on a gradient-based optimisation of a loss function based on the (dual) norm of the finite element residual of the interpolated neural network. Automatic mesh adaptation (i.e., refinement and coarsening) is performed based on a posteriori error indicators till a certain level of accuracy is reached. The proposed methodology can be applied to indefinite and nonsymmetric problems. We carry out a detailed numerical analysis of the scheme and prove several a priori error estimates, depending on the expressiveness of the neural network compared to the interpolation mesh. Our numerical experiments confirm the effectiveness of the method in capturing sharp gradients and singularities for forward PDE problems, both in 2D and 3D scenarios. We also show that the proposed preconditioning strategy (i.e., using a dual residual norm of the residual as a cost function) enhances training robustness and accelerates convergence.



1.
Introduction

\Acp

pde are an effective mathematical tool for modelling a variety of physical phenomena in science and engineering, such as heat conduction, fluid dynamics and electromagnetism.
Since analytical solutions of
pde
are only available in few special cases, numerical methods, such as the
fem
(
fem
), become the primary approach for approximating
pde
solutions using computers. The
fem
has solid mathematical foundations
[
1
]
and it can readily handle a broad range of
pde
problems. Over the years, advanced discretisation techniques have been proposed
[
2
]
, and highly efficient (non)linear solvers that can exploit large-scale supercomputers have been designed and polished
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

The
fem
is a linear method that provides the best approximation in some specific measure on a finite-dimensional vector space. However, this space is not adapted to local features (e.g., sharp gradients or singularities) and the convergence is slow for problems that exhibit multiple scales. Adaptive
fe
, e.g., the
h
-adaptive
fem
, adapt the space to the solution by gradually increasing resolution in regions with larger
a posteriori
error estimations. It adds an external loop, known as the solve, estimate, mark and refine loop, to the standard
fem
workflow. Robust
a posteriori
error estimators have been designed for diverse problems (see e.g.,
[
6
,
7
]
), making the adaptive
fem
a widely employed technique in scientific disciplines.

Recently, deep learning, especially
nn
, has demonstrated outstanding proficiency in many challenging tasks, including biomedical image segmentation
[
8
]
, natural language processing
[
9
]
and speech recognition
[
10
]
, just to mention a few. The striking success achieved by deep learning has encouraged researchers to explore the application of
nn
in solving
pde
.
In contrast to standard discretisation methods,
nn
are parametrised manifolds that enjoy non-linear approximation power, i.e.,
nn
have built-in adaptation capabilities essential in multi-scale problems.
Notably,
pinn
have been proposed in
[
11
]
as a novel approach to tackle
pde
-constrained problems. The
pinn
method integrates the
pde
into the loss function used to train the
nn
. This loss evaluates the strong
pde
residual on collocation points, and the derivatives within this loss can be computed using automatic differentiation.

\Acp
pinn are easy to implement and have been examined in different types of
pde
, such as fractional
[
12
]
and stochastic
pde

[
13
]
.

Many recent works have tried to address the limitations of
pinn
, aiming to improve their robustness, efficiency and accuracy. The authors in
[
14
]
introduce
vpinn
, which make use of a polynomial test space and take the norm of the corresponding residual vector as the loss function. To overcome the integration challenge in
vpinn
, the authors in
[
15
]
propose the
ivpinn
(
ivpinn
) method. This method interpolates a
nn
onto a
fe

In
pinn
, the imposition of Dirichlet boundary conditions is a challenging task; as opposed to
fe
spaces,
nn
are not interpolatory objects. The addition of a penalty term to weakly impose the Dirichlet boundary conditions has a very negative effect on the training. In
[
15
]
, the authors compose the
nn
with a polynomial function to design a
nn
that vanishes on the boundary. This approach is applicable to simple geometries but its extension to general geometries is unclear
[
16
]
. Besides, while better than the penalty method, such composition negatively impacts the generalisation of
nn
themselves
[
17
]
.

Our previous work
[
17
]
introduces the
feinn
(
feinn
) method. This method interpolates the
nn
onto a
fe
space that strongly satisfies the boundary condition, avoiding the issues discussed above. Besides, we propose to use the dual norm of the
pde
residual as the loss function, which can be re-stated as a preconditioned loss function that is well-posed at the continuous level; see also
[
18
]
for the application of dual norms in
vpinn
. The preconditioning requires the application of the inverse of the inner product matrix (e.g., a Laplacian in
H^{1}(\Omega)
-conforming cases), which we efficiently approximate using geometric multigrid techniques. The
feinn
method has been successfully applied to, e.g., forward
pde
with smooth or non-smooth solutions and inverse heat conduction design problems. We show that the trained
nn
can outperform the
fem
solution by several orders of magnitude when the exact solution is smooth.

Despite these efforts, the training of
pinn
and related methods often fail when dealing with target solutions containing high-frequency modes
[
19
]
, e.g., solutions with sharp gradients or discontinuities.
Some works aim to improve the expressivity of the
nn
to capture these solutions, adding more neurons/layers
[
20
]
, or adapting the non-linear activation functions
[
21
]
.
However, none of these works solve the integration issue; a quadrature on a uniform mesh is used in
[
20
]
and random collocation points in
[
21
]
.
To solve this issue, several approaches propose an adaptive distribution of points in collocation-based
pinn
.
The authors in
[
22
]
design a residual-based adaptive refinement process to add more collocation points in the locations with the largest
pde
residual, while a deep generative model that guides the sampling of collocation points is proposed in
[
23
]
. The adaptive collocation point movement method in
[
24
]
resamples the collocation points and uses an adaptive loss weighting strategy. In
[
25
]
, in addition to the strong residual of the

, the gradient information of the

is also used to guide the sampling procedure. All these adaptive methods have demonstrated enhanced performance and robustness compared to standard
pinn
when solving

with steep gradients. Still, they suffer the applicability and robustness issues of collocated
pinn
mentioned above. The provably accurate adaptive quadratures in
[
26
]
address the integration issue of

by automatically creating an accurate quadrature rule for loss functions that involve the integration of
pde

A posteriori error estimations are crucial for adaptive methods. Since the
nn
in our method is interpolated onto a

[
6
,
27
]
. In recent years,
nn
-based a posteriori error estimators have been developed. In
[
28
]
, the authors propose a

pinn
. Another related work is
[
29
]
, where an error estimator for
vpinn
consisting of a residual-type term, a loss-function term, and data oscillation terms is introduced.
Functional error control techniques have been derived in
[
30
]
to estimate the error in deep Galerkin simulations.

Remarkably, the state-of-the-art contains few investigations into the application of
nn
for problems with singular solutions. In
[
15
,
17
]
, the authors use the
fe
interpolation of the trained
nn
as the final solution of a 2D

with singularity. The
dfr
(

) method
[
31
]
tackles problems with singular solutions using

via adaptive domain decomposition. However, the method is still prototypical in the sense that automatic refinement is currently limited to 1D scenarios and relies on tensor products of 1D orthonormal basis functions and an overkill integration procedure is used.

In our previous study
[
17
]
, we have demonstrated
feinn
outstanding performance in solving various forward and inverse problems. In this work, we carry out a detailed numerical analysis of the method for the
fe
residual minimisation in a dual norm. Since the training of
feinn
on a fixed underlying mesh limits their ability to leverage the full potential of
nn
non-linear capabilities, we propose the
h
-adaptive

method to overcome this limitation by introducing an
automatic
adaptation of the interpolation space (mesh) during training, driven by
a posteriori
error estimation. We propose a
nn
error estimator based on the strong
pde
residual (which is straightforward to implement and can be accurately computed via Gaussian quadratures) and a standard
fe
Kelly indicator. These advances allow for a more comprehensive exploitation of the non-linear nature of
nn
, thus enhancing the capability of the method to tackle problems with localised features. The minimisation of the loss function relies on a train, estimate, mark, adapt procedure, which is repeated till a certain level of accuracy is reached. We leverage hierarchically adapted non-conforming octree-based meshes and adaptive
fe
spaces with hanging node constraints to keep the conformity of the interpolation.

We conduct a comprehensive set of numerical experiments, focusing on forward problems featuring steep gradients in 2D and singularities in both 2D and 3D.
We verify that expressive enough

can replicate the rate of convergence of the

-adaptive
fem
solutions. In addressing the sharp-gradient problem, the generalisation capabilities of the trained
nn
yield remarkable results. The non-interpolated

solutions can be orders of magnitude more accurate than their interpolation counterparts on the same mesh. This observation is consistent with the findings in our previous work
[
17
]
for smooth solutions. Our findings reveal that the performance of the
nn
error indicator is on par with, if not surpassing, the traditional Kelly
fe
error estimator in various situations. Moreover, we confirm that a well-posed residual norm in the loss function has a tremendously positive impact on the convergence of the
h
-adaptive training process.

2.
Methodology

2.1.
Continuous problem

Let us consider a Hilbert space
\tilde{U}
on a Lipschitz polyhedral domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can consider a partition of the boundary
\partial\Omega
into a Dirichlet
\Gamma_{D}
and Neumann
\Gamma_{N}
component. Let
U
be the subspace of
\tilde{U}
with zero trace on
\Gamma_{D}
. We denote by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{U}:U\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
an inner product in
U
and by
\|\cdot\|_{U}
the norm induced by the inner product. We can define an abstract weak formulation of a
pde
problem: find

(1)

u\in U\ :\ a(u,v)=\ell(v)-a(\bar{u},v),\quad\forall v\in U,

where
a:U\times U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a bilinear form and
\ell\in U^{\prime}
is a functional in the dual space of
U
. Non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are handled by a lifting
\bar{u}\in\tilde{U}
while Neumann boundary conditions and body forces are included in
\ell
. The final solution of the problem is
u+\bar{u}\in\tilde{U}
. In order for this problem to be well-posed, the following inf-sup condition must be satisfied: there exists a constant
\beta_{0}>0
such that

(2)

\inf_{w\in U}\sup_{v\in U}\frac{a(w,v)}{\|w\|_{U}\|v\|_{U}}\geq\beta_{0}.

Besides, the bilinear form must be continuous, i.e., there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that

(3)

a(u,v)\leq\gamma\|u\|_{U}\|v\|_{U},\quad\forall u,v\in U.

The solution of (
1
) vanishes the
pde
residual

(4)

\mathcal{R}({u})\doteq\ell(\cdot)-a({u}+\bar{u},\cdot)\in{U}^{\prime},

In this work, we propose an
h
-adaptive
feinn
formulation to discretise (
1
), which can be applied to any
pde
that fits in the previous framework. It covers not only coercive problems but also indefinite and nonsymmetric problems. We consider linear
pde
in the analysis, but the method can readily be applied to non-linear problems. We stress, however, that we focus on
low-dimensional

pde
, e.g.,
d\in\{2,3\}
. The numerical approximation of high-dimensional
pde
face other problems, due to the curse of dimensionality, and the requirements are different.

2.2.
Finite element method

We consider a shape-regular partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega
.
On
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we can define a trial
fe
space
\tilde{U}_{h}\subset\tilde{U}
and the subspace
U_{h}\subset U
with zero trace. We define a
fe
interpolant
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{C}^{0}\rightarrow U_{h}
obtained by evaluation of the
dof
of
U_{h}
.
Similarly, we can define the interpolant
\tilde{\pi}_{h}
onto
\tilde{U}_{h}
.
We can readily define a
fe
lifting
\bar{u}_{h}\in\tilde{U}_{h}
by interpolation on
\Gamma_{D}
.

Let us consider a Petrov-Galerkin discretisation of (
1
) with a test space
V_{h}\subset U
. The
fe
problem reads: find
\tilde{u}_{h}=\bar{u}_{h}+u_{h}
where

(5)

u_{h}\in U_{h}\ :\ a(u_{h},v_{h})=\ell(v_{h})-a(\bar{u}_{h},v_{h})\doteq\bar{\ell}(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

Problem (
5
) is well-posed under the assumption that the trial and test
fe
spaces
U_{h}
,
V_{h}
have the same dimension and satisfy the following inf-sup condition
[
32
]
: there exists a constant
\beta>0
independent of the mesh size
h
such that

(6)

\inf_{w_{h}\in U_{h}}\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{a(w_{h},v_{h})}{\|w_{h}\|_{U}\|v_{h}\|_{U}}\geq\beta,\qquad\beta>0.

We represent with
\mathcal{R}_{h}
the restriction
\mathcal{R}|_{{U}_{h}\times V_{h}}
.

2.3.
Neural networks

In this work, we use fully-connected feed-forward
nn
. These kind of networks are constructed through a series of affine transformations and non-linear activation functions. However, the only requirement in our analysis is that the output can be written as a linear combination of the neurons at the previous layer, allowing for the application of other architectures as well. We represent the
nn
architecture by a tuple
(n_{0},\ldots n_{L})\in\mathbb{N}^{(L+1)}
, where
L
denotes the total number of layers and
n_{k}
means the number of neurons in layer
k
with
0\leq k\leq L
. We take
n_{0}=d
and, for the scalar-valued
pde
we consider, we have
n_{L}=1
.

We denote by
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
the affine map from layer
k-1
to
k
(
1\leq k\leq L
), defined as
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{b}_{k}
, where
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times n_{k-1}}
represents the weight matrix and
\boldsymbol{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
the bias vector.
The activation function
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is applied element-wise after each affine map, excluding the final one. With these definitions in place, the
nn
can be conceptualised as a parametrisable function
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, expressed as:

(7)

\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{L}\circ\rho\circ\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{L-1}\circ\ldots\circ\rho\circ\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1},

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
denotes the collection of all trainable parameters
\boldsymbol{W}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{k}
. Besides, we apply the same activation function across all layers.
In this work, we represent the
nn
architecture as
\mathcal{N}
and a specific instance of the
nn
with parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We extend the notation to also use
\mathcal{N}
to represent the manifold of all possible realisations of the
nn
, i.e.,
\left\{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ :\ \boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{N}}}\right\}
,
n_{\mathcal{N}}
being the total number of parameters.

The
nn
structure in (
7
) can be split into two parts: the last-layer operation,
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{L}
, defined by parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ll}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L}}
, and the hidden-layer operations,

\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{L-1}\circ\ldots\circ\rho\circ\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1}
, defined by parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{hl}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{hl}}
, with
n_{hl}=n_{\mathcal{N}}-n_{L}
. We refer to the input of the last-layer operation as the
last-layer functions
, consisting of
n_{L-1}
output functions from the hidden-layer operations.
The output of a
nn
realisation can be expressed as a linear combination of the last-layer functions, with coefficients
\boldsymbol{W}_{L}
, plus a constant, the bias
\boldsymbol{b}_{L}
. Thus, the hidden-layer parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{hl}
define a linear space, i.e., the one spanned by the last-layer functions. We denote this linear space as
S(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{hl})
, and refer to it as to the last-layer space.
With this notation, the manifold of
nn
realisations can alternatively be expressed as the union of all possible last-layer vector spaces:

(8)

\mathcal{N}=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{hl}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{hl}}}S(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{hl}).

In the
feinn
method, we apply the
U_{h}
interpolation operator
\pi_{h}(\cdot)
to the
nn
realisations. Since the interpolation operator is linear, we can define the last-layer interpolated space
S_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{hl})\subset U_{h}
as the span of the interpolation of the last-layer functions. The interpolated
nn
manifold
\mathcal{N}_{h}\doteq\left\{\pi_{h}(v)\ :\ v\in\mathcal{N}\right\}\subset U_{h}
can be represented as the union of all last-layer interpolated spaces. These definitions are used in Sec.
3
.

2.4.
Finite element interpolated neural networks

The
feinn
method combines the
nn
in (
7
) and the
fe
problem in (
5
). The goal is to find

(9)

u_{\mathcal{N}}\in\underset{w_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\,\mathscr{L}(w_{\mathcal{N}}),\qquad\mathscr{L}(w_{\mathcal{N}})\doteq\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}({\pi}_{h}(w_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}},

where
u_{\mathcal{N}}
is the
nn
approximation of the solution, and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}
is some suitable dual norm. Different norms will be considered in the numerical experiments in Sec.
5
.
However, we only consider
X=U
in the numerical analysis.

In general, it is unclear whether
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{N}_{h}
are closed spaces. If
\mathcal{N}_{h}
is not closed, we cannot attain the minimum in the optimisation problem (
9
), since we work on
\mathcal{N}_{h}
, not its closure. To be mathematically precise, the
feinn
problem must be stated as: find

(10)

u_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}\ :\ \pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\in\underset{w_{\mathcal{N},h}\in\overline{\mathcal{N}_{h}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\,\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{\mathcal{N},h})\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}.

However, for any tolerance
\epsilon>0
, we can find

(11)

u_{\mathcal{N},\epsilon}\in\mathcal{N}\ :\ \left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}(\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N},\epsilon}))\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}\leq\underset{w_{\mathcal{N},h}\in\overline{\mathcal{N}_{h}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\,\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{\mathcal{N},h})\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}+\epsilon.

Remark 2.1
.

We refer to the solution of (
11
) as an
\epsilon
-
quasiminimiser
of the
feinn
problem. The concept of quasiminimisers in the frame of
pinn
has already been previously discussed
[
33
,
34
]
. This is of relative practical relevance since the problem is solved iteratively up to a certain level of accuracy and one can produce a sequence of
\epsilon
-
quasiminimisers
that converges to one of the solutions of the
feinn
problem (
10
).

Remark 2.2
.

It has been proven in
[
35
]
that the space of functions that can be generated by a
nn
is not closed in
L^{p}
-norms for
0<p<\infty
for most activation functions. For the
L^{\infty}
-norm, and (parameterised) ReLU activation functions, the
nn
space is proven to be closed in some cases. ReLU activation functions are not smooth enough for
pinn
or Deep Galerkin methods
[
26
]
. However, in
feinn
,
\mathcal{N}_{h}
only depends on the pointwise values of the neural network on the nodes of
U_{h}
and ReLU activation functions are suitable, since the method computes spatial derivatives of
fe
functions. Thus, using the results in
[
35
, Sec. 3.2]
, one can check that
\mathcal{N}_{h}
is closed for ReLU activation functions and the minimum in (
10
) is attained. This is not the case for other activation functions (see Ex.
3.11
).

As discussed in
[
17
]
, we can define a discrete Riesz projector
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}:V_{h}^{\prime}\to V_{h}
such that

\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h})\in V_{h}\ :\left(\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h}),v_{h}\right)_{U}=\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h})(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

and
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}
is (an approximation of) the inverse of the corresponding Gramm matrix in
V_{h}
. Then, we can rewrite the loss function in primal norm as follows:

(12)

\mathscr{L}(u_{\mathcal{N}})=\left\lVert\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}({\pi}_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{X}.

Unlike loss functions that rely on discrete norms of the residual vector, this loss function,
preconditioned
by
\mathcal{B}_{h}
, is well-defined in the limit
h\downarrow 0
. In practice,
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}
can be replaced by any spectrally equivalent approximation to reduce computational costs; e.g. one
gmg
(
gmg
) cycle has been proposed in
[
17
]
.

Remark 2.3
.

We note that the
fe
residual
\mathcal{R}_{h}
in (
9
) is isomorphic to the vector
[\mathbf{r}_{h}(w_{h})]_{i}=\left<\mathcal{R}(w_{h}),\varphi^{i}\right>\doteq\mathcal{R}(w_{h})(\varphi^{i})
, where
\{\varphi^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the
fe
shape functions that span the test space
V_{h}
. As a result, we can use the following loss function:

(13)

\mathscr{L}(u_{\mathcal{N}})=\left\lVert\mathbf{r}_{h}(\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}}))\right\rVert_{\chi},

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\chi}
is an algebraic norm of a vector. The algebraic norm is ill-posed at the continuous level and, as a consequence, the convergence of the minimisation process is expected to deteriorate as
h\downarrow 0

[
36
]
.

2.5.
h
-Adaptive finite element interpolated neural networks

The
h
-adaptive
feinn
method is essentially the
feinn
method equipped with a dynamically/automatically adapting mesh. The method is sketched in Alg.
1
. It consists of a loop of the form:

\text{TRAIN}\rightarrow\text{ESTIMATE}\rightarrow\text{MARK}\rightarrow\text{ADAPT}.

At the beginning of the
i
-th loop iteration, the
nn
realisation
u_{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}
has initial parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
. After training of
u_{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})}
using the weak
pde
loss built upon the partition
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, the parameters of the
nn
become
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i+1}
.
Then, we estimate the local error for each element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{i}
using an error indicator
\zeta_{K}
. Next, we mark a set of elements
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\rm r}
for refinement and another set
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\rm c}
for coarsening. We denote the refinement ratio as
\delta^{\rm r}\in(0,1)
, and the coarsening ratio as
\delta^{\rm c}\in(0,1)
.
To determine
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\rm r}
and
\mathcal{M}_{i}^{\rm c}
, we use Dörfler marking. Essentially, we select the top
\delta^{\rm r}
elements with the largest estimator for refinement and the bottom
\delta^{\rm c}
with the smallest estimator for coarsening. We then adapt the marked elements to construct a new partition
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
. Finally, we start a new iteration of the loop by interpolating
u_{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i+1})}
onto
\mathcal{T}_{i+1}
. This iterative procedure continues until a stopping criterion is met.

Requirements:

k_{U}
, stopping criterion,
\delta^{\rm r}\in(0,1)
,
\delta^{\rm c}\in(0,1)

Input:

u_{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})}
,
\mathcal{T}_{0}

Output:

u_{\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I+1})}
,
\mathcal{T}_{I}

for

2.6.
Gradient-conforming discretisation

As a model problem in this work, we consider the Poisson equation. The problem reads as (
1

\tilde{U}=H^{1}(\Omega)
,

and the bilinear form and functional defined as follows:

a(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\nabla}(u)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}(v),\quad\ell(v)=\int_{\Omega}fv,

We consider an
exotic
Petrov-Galerkin
fe
method. The well-posedness of the discretisation is guaranteed by the discrete inf-sup condition (
6
) and the same dimension for trial and test spaces (see
[
32
]
). We use a grad-conforming nodal Lagrangian
fe
space
U_{h}
of order
k_{U}
built upon a (possibly non-conforming) partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of
\Omega
. The
fe
space
U_{h}
is built upon the partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. We use a
linearised
test

space

(
k_{V}=1
), built upon the partition
\mathcal{T}_{h}
obtained after
k_{U}
levels of uniform refinement applied to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
. It is easy to check that the dimension of
{U}_{h}
and

are identical.
The reason behind this choice of the test space is the dramatic improvement in the convergence of the optimisation process compared to a Galerkin discretisation, as observed, e.g., in
[

]

2.7.
Error indicators

We introduce three types of error indicators to estimate the local errors of each element. In this section, we restrict the exposition to the Laplacian problem for the sake of simplicity. However, the error indicators can be extended to other
pde
.

The first error indicator is the classic Kelly error estimator
[
27
,
6
]
, a well-established indicator in
h
-adaptive
fem
, particularly for the Poisson equation. This error indicator estimates the error per element by integrating the gradient jump of the
nn
interpolation along the faces of each element. Specifically, for the element
K
, the Kelly error indicator is defined as

(14)

\zeta_{K}^{k}=\sqrt{\sum_{F\in\partial K}c_{F}\int_{F}\left\llbracket\frac{\partial({\pi}_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})+\bar{u}_{h})}{\partial n}\right\rrbracket^{2}},

where
c_{F}
is a scale factor,
\partial K
is the boundary of
K
, and
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
denotes the jump across the face
F
.
The
fe
offset
\bar{u}_{h}
term in
\zeta_{K}^{k}
can be excluded if the Dirichlet boundary is exactly approximated by the employed finite elements, i.e.
\bar{u}_{h}=g
.
In the notation
\zeta_{K}^{k}
, the superscript
k
stands for “Kelly”, and the subscript
K
denotes an element of the mesh. We use
c_{F}=h_{K}/24
as proposed in
[
6
]
, where
h_{K}
is the characteristic size of element
K
.

Then we introduce another error indicator that leverages the
nn
and the strong form of the
pde
. The error estimator at element
K
is defined as

(15)

\zeta_{K}^{n}=\sqrt{\int_{K}|\boldsymbol{\Delta}u_{\mathcal{N}}+f|^{2}}.

The superscript
n
in
\zeta_{K}^{n}
stands for “network”.
We note that
\zeta_{K}^{n}
is similar to the mean
pde
residual
\varepsilon_{r}
defined in
[
22
]
.
The main difference is that
\zeta_{K}^{n}
is evaluated with a high-degree Gauss quadrature, while
\varepsilon_{r}
is computed by Monte Carlo integration. At each mesh adaptation step, the strategy in
[
22
]
adds more collocation points only on the region with the highest
\varepsilon_{r}
. Instead, our approach can refine multiple elements with the largest errors and coarsen those with the smallest errors simultaneously.

Finally, we include the real error of the interpolated
nn
as the third indicator, which is defined for element
K
as:

\zeta_{K}^{r}=\sqrt{\int_{K}|{\pi}_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})+\bar{u}_{h}-u|^{2}},

where
u
is the exact solution. The superscript
r
in
\zeta_{K}^{r}
stands for “real”. In practice, the real error is unavailable. In this study, we use it as a reference to assess and compare the performance of the other two indicators.

3.
Numerical analysis

In this section, we extend the analysis in
[
17
]
in multiple ways.
In particular, in Sec.
3.1
, we bound the error of the interpolated
nn

under the general scenario in which the

, i.e.,

(or, equivalently, the interpolation operator
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{N}\mapsto U_{h}
is not surjective).
Remarkably, we prove that the error of the interpolated

3.1.
Error analysis

The error analysis in this section relies on the discrete inf-sup condition (
6
), and can readily be applied to indefinite systems. In the following theorem, we state the well-posedness of the residual minimisation problem in a
fe
setting.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let us consider a pair of
fe
spaces
U_{h}
and
V_{h}
that satisfy the inf-sup condition (
6
).
The discrete quadratic minimisation problem

(16)

u_{h}=\underset{w_{h}\in U_{h}}{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}\|r_{h}(w_{h})\|_{U},\qquad r_{h}(w_{h})\doteq\mathcal{B}_{h}^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{h}),

is well-posed. The problem can be re-stated as: find
u_{h}\in U_{h}
and
r_{h}=r_{h}(u_{h})\in V_{h}
such that

(17)

\displaystyle(r_{h},v_{h})_{U}+a(u_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle=\bar{\ell}(v_{h}),\quad

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

(18)

\displaystyle a(w_{h},r_{h})

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\forall w_{h}\in U_{h}.

The solution satisfies the following a priori estimates:

(19)

\|r_{h}\|_{U}\leq\underset{w_{h}\in U_{h}}{\mathrm{inf}}4\gamma\|u-w_{h}\|_{U},\qquad\|u-u_{h}\|_{U}\leq\left(1+\frac{4\gamma}{\beta}\right)\underset{w_{h}\in U_{h}}{\mathrm{inf}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}.

Proof.

First, we note that the loss function is differentiable (see
[
17
, Prop. 3.1]
). To obtain (
17
)-(
18
), we compute the variational derivative of (
16
) to variations of
u_{h}
and recall the definition of the discrete Riesz projector. Under the assumption of the discrete inf-sup condition (
6
), we can prove the well-posedness of the problem using the Babuska-Brezzi theory. Namely, using (
6
), we can readily find
k_{h}\in V_{h}
such that
\|k_{h}\|_{U}=\|u_{h}\|_{U}
and
a(u_{h},k_{h})\geq\beta\|u_{h}\|_{U}^{2}
. We can take
v_{h}=r_{h}+\alpha k_{h}

for an arbitrary
\alpha>0
and
w_{h}=-u_{h}
, to get

(20)

\displaystyle\|r_{h}\|^{2}_{U}+\alpha a(u_{h},k_{h})+\alpha(r_{h},k_{h})_{U}

\displaystyle\geq\|r_{h}\|^{2}_{U}+\alpha\beta\|u_{h}\|^{2}_{U}-\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\|u_{h}\|_{U}^{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2\beta}\|r_{h}\|^{2}_{U}

(21)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2\beta}\right)\|r_{h}\|^{2}_{U}+\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}\|u_{h}\|^{2}_{U}.

Taking
\alpha=\beta
, we get, using (
17
):

(22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\|r_{h}\|_{U}+\beta\|u_{h}\|_{U})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|r_{h}\|^{2}_{U}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\|u_{h}\|^{2}_{U}\leq\|r_{h}\|^{2}_{U}+\beta a(u_{h},k_{h})+\beta(r_{h},k_{h})_{U}

(23)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\ell}\|_{U^{\prime}}(\|r_{h}\|_{U}+\beta\|u_{h}\|_{U}).

Using the fact that
\bar{\ell}(v_{h})=a(u,v_{h})
, and subtracting
a(w_{h},v_{h})
on both sides of (
17
) for an arbitrary
w_{h}\in U_{h}
and using the continuity of the bilinear form
a
, we get the following error estimate from (
22
):

(24)

\|r_{h}\|_{U}+\beta\|u_{h}-w_{h}\|_{U}\leq 4\gamma\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}.

Using the triangle inequality, we readily get the second estimate in (
19
). It proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

We note that
dim(U_{h})\neq dim(V_{h})
in general, and the trial space can be of lower dimension than the test space. In the case in which
dim(U_{h})=dim(V_{h})
, the solution
u_{h}
of (
17
)-(
18

1

r_{h}=0

The previous theorem readily applies to any subspace

, since the inf-sup condition readily holds for
W_{h}\times V_{h}
. We use the notation
u(W_{h})
and
r(W_{h})
to denote the unique solution of (
17
)-(
18
) on
W_{h}\times V_{h}
. We can prove the following result.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let us consider
u_{h,1},\,u_{h,2}\in U_{h}
.
The following error estimate holds:

(25)

\frac{1}{\beta}\|u_{h,1}-u_{h,2}\|_{U}\leq\|r(u_{h,1})-r(u_{h,2})\|_{U}\leq\gamma\|u_{h,1}-u_{h,2}\|_{U}.

Proof.

Let us denote for conciseness
u_{h}\doteq u_{h,1}
,
u_{h}^{\prime}\doteq u_{h,2}
,
r_{h}\doteq r(u_{h,1})
,
r_{h}^{\prime}\doteq r(u_{h,2})
. Using the definition of
r_{h}(\cdot)
in (
16

\displaystyle(r_{h},v_{h})_{U}

\displaystyle=-a(u_{h},v_{h})+\bar{\ell}(v_{h}),

\displaystyle(r_{h}^{\prime},v_{h})_{U}

\displaystyle=-a(u_{h}^{\prime},v_{h})+\bar{\ell}(v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

Subtracting the previous equations, we get

(26)

(r_{h}-r_{h}^{\prime},v_{h})_{U}=-a(u_{h}-u_{h}^{\prime},v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

The upper bound can readily be obtained using the continuity of
a
in (
3

6

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let us consider
y_{h}\in U_{h}
and
W_{h}\subseteq U_{h}
such that
\|r(y_{h})\|_{U}\leq\|r_{h}(W_{h})\|_{U}+\epsilon

\|u-y_{h}\|_{U}\leq\rho\inf_{w_{h}\in W_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}+\beta\epsilon,\qquad\rho\doteq 1+\frac{4\gamma}{\beta}+{8\gamma}{\beta}.

Proof.

Using Prop.
3.3
, the triangle inequality, the statement of the proposition and the error estimate in (

\frac{1}{\beta}\|y_{h}-u(W_{h})\|_{U}\leq\|r(y_{h})-r(W_{h})\|_{U}\leq 2\|r(W_{h})\|_{U}+\epsilon\leq 8\gamma\inf_{w_{h}\in W_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}+\epsilon.

Then, using the triangle inequality and (

\|u-y_{h}\|_{U}\leq\|_{U}u-u(W_{h})\|_{U}+\|y_{h}-u(W_{h})\|_{U}\leq\rho\inf_{w_{h}\in W_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}+\epsilon.

∎

Next, we prove that the infimum (

.

The following equalities hold:

(28)

\underset{w_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}}{\mathrm{inf}}\|r_{h}(\pi_{h}(w_{\mathcal{N}}))\|_{U}=\underset{w_{h}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}{\mathrm{inf}}\|r_{h}(w_{h})\|_{U}=\underset{u_{h}\in\mathcal{M}_{h}}{\mathrm{inf}}\|r_{h}(u_{h})\|_{U}.

The first equality is an obvious result of the definition for

Remark 3.6
.

To consider the minimisation on

is practically possible. It involves to solve a linear system for the last-layer weights of the

Let us consider the closure
\overline{\mathcal{N}_{h}}

For any

\|u-\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N},\epsilon})\|_{U}\leq\rho\inf_{w_{h}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}+\beta\epsilon.

Let us consider the minimisation problem on

Thanks to Prop.

3.2.
Quasi-emulation

In the previous section, we have considered the case in which the
nn
space cannot emulate the
fe
space, i.e.,
\mathcal{N}_{h}\subsetneq U_{h}
. If the
nn
is expressive enough, we can prove that
\mathcal{N}_{h}=U_{h}
following the construction in
[
17
, Prop. 3.2]
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let us consider an adaptive Lagrangian
fe
space
U_{h}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
N
nodes, which can be defined on a non-conforming mesh and include hanging nodes. Let
\mathcal{N}
be a neural network architecture with ReLU activation function, 3 hidden layers and
(3dN,dN,N)
neurons per layer. The interpolation operator
\pi_{h}:\mathcal{N}\mapsto U_{h}
is surjective.

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as in
[
17
, Prop. 3.2]
. The neural network above can represent in the last layer all the shape functions in the adaptive
fe
space
U_{h}
, both the free ones as the ones that are constrained and associated to hanging nodes. The linear combination of these last layer shape functions can represent any function in
U_{h}
. As a result, the interpolation operator
\pi_{h}
is surjective.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

The previous result is overly pessimistic and can be improved when using meshes that are obtained after adaptive mesh refinement of a regular mesh. In this case, the
fe
space
U_{h}
can be represented by a
nn
with a much smaller number of neurons, since one can exploit a tensor product structure in the proof (see
[
17
]
).

Exact emulation is not attainable for some activation functions, like
\tanh
, regardless of the expressivity of the
nn
. In this case, we introduce the concept of quasi-emulation.

Definition 3.10
.

A
nn
is a
\delta
-emulator of a vector space
U_{h}
if for all
w_{h}\in U_{h}
, there exists a
w\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\|\pi_{h}(w)-w_{h}\|_{U}\leq\delta
.

Example 3.11
.

Let us consider a 1D problem for simplicity and a fully-connected
nn
as
in Sec.
2.3

with
\rho=\tanh
activation,
m\in\mathbb{N}
and the weights and biases:

\boldsymbol{W}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}m,\ -m\end{bmatrix}^{T},\ \boldsymbol{b}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{m}{4},\ \frac{m}{4}\end{bmatrix}^{T},\quad\boldsymbol{W}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}m,\ m\end{bmatrix},\ \boldsymbol{b}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0\end{bmatrix}.

One can check that the corresponding realisation
f_{m}({x})=\rho\circ(\boldsymbol{W}_{2}(\rho\circ(\boldsymbol{W}_{1}{x}+\boldsymbol{b}_{1})+\boldsymbol{b}_{2}))
satisfies
f_{m}({x}=0)=1-\delta
,
f_{m}({x}=-1)=f_{m}({x}=1)=c\delta
for some constant
c
, and monotonically decreases as

goes to
\pm\infty
. Thus,
\lim_{m\to\infty}f_{m}(x)=h(x)
, where
h(x)
is the hat function with support in
[-1,1]
and value 1 at
x=0
. We can readily obtain a hat function in any interval by applying translation and/or scaling.
Thus, we can construct a
nn
with a set of last-layer functions that emulates a
fe

\delta>0
.
Similar constructions are possible in higher dimensions.

Due to the well-posedness of the

, there is a unique minimum

of (

)-(

. Thus, we can attain minimisers

u_{\mathcal{N}}\in\mathcal{N}\ :\ \pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\in\underset{w_{\mathcal{N},h}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{h}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\,\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{h}(w_{\mathcal{N},h})\right\rVert_{X^{\prime}}.

In fact, the minimisers are all the

such that
\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})=u_{h}
and the a priori error estimate (

) holds. For quasi-emulation, the following result is a direct consequence of Th.

and the previous definition.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let us consider a

\mathcal{N}
that is a
\delta
-emulator of the

U_{h}
. For any
\epsilon>0
, an
\epsilon

of (

(30)

\|u-\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\|_{U}\leq\rho\inf_{w_{h}\in U_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{U}+\beta\epsilon.

3.3.
Generalisation error and equivalence classes

In the discussions above, we have only bounded the error of the interpolated
nn
. Analogously, we have discussed the well-posedness in the space of interpolations of the
nn
. The total error of the
nn
is harder to bound, since it is
transparent
to the loss.

In order to better bound the total error of the
nn
, we can make use of quotient spaces (see a related approach in
[
18
]
for
vpinn
). Let us define the following equivalence relation
\sim
: two
nn
realisations
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})
are equivalent if their interpolations on the adaptive
fe
space
U_{h}
are identical, i.e.,
\pi_{h}(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))=\pi_{h}(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}))
. The equivalence class of
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is denoted by
[\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
. We can define the quotient space
\mathcal{N}/\sim
as the set of all equivalence classes
[\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
.

Let us consider the case of exact emulation for simplicity. In this situation, the problem (
10
) admits a unique solution
u_{h}
in
U_{h}
. Then, the problem is also well-posed in the quotient space
\mathcal{N}/\sim
; the unique solution is the equivalence class
[u_{h}]\in\mathcal{N}/\sim
. Analogously, in the case of quasi-emulation, quasi-minimisers in the quotient space will be arbitrarily close to
[u_{h,\epsilon}]
.

Assuming that the training process converges to the global minimiser, we can prove the following result.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
u_{h}\in U_{h}
be the solution of (
5
). Let us assume that the training process has attained an
\epsilon
-quasiminimiser
u_{h,\epsilon}\in\mathcal{N}_{h}
, or analogously, a
[u_{h,\epsilon}]\in\mathcal{N}/\sim
. Any possible realisation
u_{\mathcal{N}}\in[u_{h,\epsilon}]\subset\mathcal{N}
obtained after training satisfies the following a priori error result:

(31)

\left\lVert u-u_{\mathcal{N}}\right\rVert_{U}\leq\left\lVert u-u_{h}\right\rVert_{U}+\sup_{v\in[\pi_{h}(u_{\mathcal{N}})]}\left\lVert\pi_{h}^{\perp}(v)\right\rVert_{U}+\epsilon,

where
\pi_{h}^{\perp}(u)=u-\pi_{h}(u)
.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality and taking the supremum of the error between the
nn
and the
fe
solution in the
nn
architecture, we prove the result.
∎

The first term in the error estimate is the
fe
error, the second term is the generalisation error, and the third term is a training error. The generalisation error describes the maximum distance between the
fe
solution and the
nn
realisations in the corresponding equivalence class. Since the loss function does not
feel

\|\pi_{h}^{\perp}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\|
, there is no explicit control on it. This error is related to the expressiveness of the
nn
with respect to the
fe
mesh. The more expressive the
nn
, the larger the generalisation error can be, as it is well-known in the machine learning literature (over-fitting). Besides, if the
nn
is too coarse, the emulation assumption will not hold and the
feinn
will not be able to capture the
fe
solution.

1
1

1

This discussion motivates the usage of a regularisation term that controls the distance between the
nn
and the
fe
solution. For example, one could consider a term of the order
h^{-2}\|\pi^{\perp}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
or
\|\pi^{\perp}(u_{\mathcal{N}})\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, integrated with a high order quadrature. Such a term is weakly consistent, i.e., it does not affect the convergence of
feinn
compared to
fem
. This stabilisation resembles the so-called orthogonal subscale stabilisation common in the
fe
literature to circumvent the inf-sup condition (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
38
]
). We have not considered stabilisation in this work.

The previous analysis is
fe
-centric, in the sense that we consider the
nn
error by comparing it with the
fe
solution and use
fe
theory to quantify the error. Even though the
feinn
can capture the
fe
solution with a much smaller number of parameters than the number of
dof
in the
fe
space
[
17
]
, it can be argued that if the only aim is to attain the
fe
error, it is more effective to solve the
fe
problem. However, the
feinn
approach can naturally deal with inverse problems by simply adding data misfit terms to the loss function and the
nn
parametrisation regularises the inverse problem and can readily be used in a one-shot fashion, avoiding the need of adjoint problems
[
17
]
. It has also been observed experimentally in
[
17
]
that the
fe
-centric analysis of
feinn
is overly pessimistic in general. The trained
nn
using the
feinn
approach can be up to two orders of magnitude more accurate than the
fe
solution for forward problems. In Sec.
5
, we analyse this fact in the case of singular solution and sharp gradients combined with
h
-adaptivity and a posteriori error estimation. A mathematical justification of the superior accuracy of the
feinn
trained
nn
compared to the
fem
solution is still open.

The two error estimates in Sec.
2.7
are related to these two different perspectives. The a posteriori error estimate (
14
) aims to reduce the error of the interpolated
nn
, following the
fe
-centric perspective. Instead, the estimate (
15
) aims to directly reduce the error between the
nn
and the exact solution, considering the interpolation just as a way to enforce the Dirichlet condition, define a trial space to compute the weak residual and perform an approximate numerical integration.

4.
Implementation

4.1.
Automatic mesh adaptation using forest-of-octrees

Automatic mesh adaptation is a key component of
h
-adaptive
feinn
.
In this work, we leverage a particular kind of hierarchically-adapted meshes known as
forest-of-octrees (see, e.g.,
[
39
,
40
]
).
Forest-of-octrees meshes can be seen as a two-level
decomposition of
\Omega
, referred to as macro and micro level, respectively. The macro level is a suitable
conforming
partition
\mathcal{C}
of
\Omega
into quadrilateral (
d=2
) or hexahedral cells (
d=3
). This mesh, which may be generated, e.g., using an unstructured mesh generator, is referred to as the coarse mesh. At the micro level, each of the cells of
\mathcal{C}
becomes the root of an adaptive octree with cells that can be recursively and dynamically refined or coarsened using the so-called
1:2^{d}
uniform partition rule. If a cell is marked for refinement, then it is split into
2^{d}
children cells by subdividing all parent cell edges. If all children cells of a parent cell are marked for coarsening, then they are collapsed into the parent cell. The union of all leaf cells in this hierarchy forms the decomposition of the domain at the micro level, i.e.,
\mathcal{T}
.

In this work, we utilise the
GridapP4est.jl

[
41
]
Julia package in order to handle such kind of meshes.
This package, built upon the
p4est
meshing engine
[
39
]
, is endowed with the so-called Morton space-filling curves, and it provides high-performance and low-memory footprint algorithms to handle forest-of-octrees.

4.2.
Non-conforming interpolation of neural networks

Unlike our previous work
[
17
]
, which relies on (uniformly-refined) conforming meshes, in this work
we interpolate the
nn
onto (grad-conforming)
fe
spaces built out of non-conforming forest-of-octrees meshes.
With this kind of meshes, generating a cell-conforming global enumeration of
dof
is not sufficient to impose the required continuity at the interface of cells with different refinement levels. In order to remedy this, it is standard practice to equip the
fe
space with additional linear multi-point constraints at the
dof
lying on hanging vertices, edges and faces. The structure and set up of such constraints is well-established knowledge, and thus not covered here; see, e.g.,
[
40
,
42
]
for further details. In any case, the presence of these linear multi-point constraints in the
fe
space implies that the interpolation of the
nn
at hanging
dof
cannot by given by its evaluation at the corresponding nodes, but rather by a linear combination of the
nn
evaluations at the nodes of the adjacent coarser cell. To this end,
GridapP4est.jl
provides the required functionality to build and apply these linear multi-point constraints, and to resolve these constraints during
fe
assembly.

4.3.
Finite element interpolated neural networks

In this section we briefly overview the main building blocks of our Julia implementation of
h
-adaptive
feinn
.
For a more complete coverage of these (including the inverse problem case), we refer to
[
17
]
.
At a purely algebraic level, we can rewrite the preconditioned loss (
12
) as:

\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left\lVert r_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{X},

where
r_{h}\in V_{h}
is the discrete Riesz projection of the
fe
residual. The vector of
dof
of
r_{h}
is
\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{h}-\mathbf{f})
, where
\mathbf{B}
is the discretisation in the test space
V_{h}
of the Gram matrix associated to the inner product in
U
, e.g., the Laplacian matrix for the Poisson problem,
\mathbf{A}
is the coefficient matrix resulting from discretisation,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is the parameters for the
nn

u_{\mathcal{N}}
,
\mathbf{u}_{h}
is the vector of
dof
of
U_{h}
, and
\mathbf{f}
is the
rhs
(
rhs
) vector.
We use the abstract data structures in
Gridap.jl

[
43
,
44
]
as implemented by
GridapP4est.jl
to compute
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{f}
, and the
fe
nodal coordinates
\mathbf{X}
of those

dof
which are not subject to linear multi-point constraints. We utilise
Flux.jl

[
45
,
46
]
to build
u_{\mathcal{N}}
and evaluate
u_{\mathcal{N}}
at
\mathbf{X}
to compute
\mathbf{u}_{h}
.
Without preconditioning, i.e.,
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}
, we can minimise the loss
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{f}\right\rVert_{\chi}
directly.
During training, we rely on
Zygote.jl

[
47
]
to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(see
[
17
]
for details). We note that in the context of
h
-adaptive
feinn
, we need to recompute
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{f}
, and
\mathbf{u}_{h}
at each adaptation step. Furthermore, depending on the specific problem, these arrays can be stored cell-wise to improve efficiency.

If a preconditioner is employed, i.e.,
\mathbf{B}\neq\mathbf{I}
, we need to perform these two additional steps at each iteration:

(1)

Compute
r_{h}
by solving
\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{f})
on
V_{h}
.

(2)

Compute the preconditioned loss
\mathscr{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left\lVert r_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert_{X}
.

The forward and backward passes of the above steps are both handled by
Gridap.jl
.
It is noteworthy that in step (1), we do not explicitly compute the inverse of
\mathbf{B}
, but rather solve the linear system
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{r}_{h}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}_{h}-\mathbf{f}
to compute the vector of
dof

\mathbf{r}_{h}
that uniquely determine
r_{h}\in V_{h}
.
Considering the problem linear, we can reuse the factorisation of
\mathbf{B}
, leading to linear complexity computational cost in the application of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}
per iteration. Alternatively, as explored in
[
17
]
, we can use a cheaper preconditioner
\mathbf{B}
, such as the
gmg
preconditioner, which has linear complexity in the number of
dof
of the
fe
space.
Besides, given that
r_{h}\in V_{h}
and
V_{h}
is a linearised
fe
space, and the gradient of
r_{h}
is piecewise constant, the computation of
\left\lVert r_{h}\right\rVert_{X}
is very cheap.

5.1.2.
The effect of preconditioning

In the experiments so far, we have observed a notable variance with respect to parameter initialisation in the errors corresponding to non-interpolated
nn
, particularly when using Kelly and real error indicators.
In this experiment, we investigate whether preconditioning can effectively enhance robustness (i.e., reduce this variance) and accelerate the training process. We concentrate on the impact of the preconditioner on the results using the Kelly error indicator, as accessing to the true error is often impossible in practice. Besides, we also note that
the results obtained with the network error indicator are very similar to those obtained with the Kelly error indicator, thus we omit from this section the detailed results obtained with the former indicator for brevity.

We consider the preconditioner
\mathbf{B}_{\rm inv\_lin}
, which is defined as the discrete Laplacian resulting from discretisation with the linearised space
V_{h}
used both as trial and test FE spaces. Thus,
\mathbf{B}_{\rm inv\_lin}
is a sparse

(

) matrix with reduced computational cost compared to a regular sparse matrix. Note that we apply

levels of uniform refinement for the partition associated to

to obtain the mesh for the

V_{h}
.
Besides, in
[
17
]
, we have observed the effectiveness of a

\mathbf{B}_{\rm inv\_lin}
, suggesting its potential benefits for this problem as well. Nonetheless, for the purpose of this study, we just exactly invert
\mathbf{B}_{\rm inv\_lin}
(by solving linear systems), as it is sufficient to demonstrate the impact of preconditioning.
In these experiments, we cut the number of training iterations by reducing those used in the unpreconditioned experiments by a factor of 4, except for the final adaptation step, where these are divided by a factor of 2. This adjustment accommodates for the increased demand for training on the finest meshes.

In addition to evaluating to what extent preconditioners can expedite convergence, we also investigate the impact of norm choice on the convergence.
We train the same
nn
(i.e., the same parameter initialisation) using the preconditioned loss (
12
) equipped with 4 different norms: the
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
norm of the discrete Riesz map of the residual and its gradient; we denote the
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
norms on the gradient as
W^{1,1}
and
W^{1,2}
norms, since

represents the Sobolev norm with

-th power.
In Fig.

The first thing to note is that all the preconditioned curves are much steeper than the unpreconditioned ones, indicating that the application of the preconditioner significantly accelerates the convergence of both

Moreover, the

In contrast, the preconditioned error curves are all steadily decreasing, suggesting that the preconditioner effectively stabilises the training process.
This also reinforces the conclusion from our previous work
[

that using the
L^{2}
norm in the preconditioned loss can accelerate the convergence of training in terms of

Furthermore, the

, all the four norms in (

6.
Conclusions

In this work, we propose the
h
-adaptive
feinn
method, an extension of the
feinn
method
[
17
]
that incorporates adaptive meshes. The method inherits the desired properties of
feinn
, namely the applicability to general geometries, simple imposition of Dirichlet boundary conditions and accurate integration of the interpolated weak residual. Furthermore, since the interpolation of the
nn
is performed on an adaptive
fe
space, the method preserves the non-linear capabilities of the
nn
to capture sharp gradients and singularities. The dual norm of the finite element residual in the loss function enhances training robustness and accelerates convergence. We propose a train, estimate, mark and adapt strategy to train the
feinn
and adapt the mesh simultaneously. We have carried out a detailed numerical analysis of the method, proving a priori error estimates depending on the expressiveness of the neural network compared to the interpolation mesh.

Our numerical findings confirm the capability of
h
-adaptive
feinn
in capturing sharp gradients and singularities in 2D and 3D cases.
Remarkably, in the 2D arc wavefront experiments,
nn
exhibit excellent generalisation, surpassing
fem
solutions by two orders of magnitude in terms of
H^{1}
error at the final mesh adaptation step.
Additionally, our results verify the substantial improvements in training robustness and convergence with the introduction of preconditioning techniques.
The 2D L-shaped singularity experiments showcase the superior error reduction capabilities of
h
-adaptive
feinn
compared to uniform refinement
fem
for various interpolation orders.
Furthermore, we demonstrate the method’s capability to capture multiple singularities in the 3D numerical experiments. Alongside the development, we introduce a
nn
error indicator and conduct a thorough comparison with the standard Kelly error indicator in our experiments. The results show that the
nn
error indicator is competitive with the Kelly error indicator in terms of mesh adaptation efficiency and accuracy.

There are several promising directions for future exploration. One research avenue is to examine the effectiveness of the adaptive training strategy in solving inverse problems. In addition, we aim to investigate the applicability of the proposed approach in attacking problems featuring singularities defined in
H(\mathrm{curl})
and
H(\mathrm{div})
spaces
[
51
]
. Towards increasing applicability, we would also like to explore the combination of
h
-adaptive
feinn
with unfitted
fe
spaces
[
52
]
, which can be used to tackle problems with complex interfaces and geometries. Future work will also focus on extending the framework to
p
and
r
-refinement strategies, as well as to transient
pde
.
Finally, a mathematical analysis of generalisation error bounds for the
feinn
-trained
nn
is a promising direction for future research.
