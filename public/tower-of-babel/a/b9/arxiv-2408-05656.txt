Applications of the Modified Hulthén–Kohn Method for Bound and Scattering States
Mamoon A. Sharaf, Andrey M. Shirokov, Weijie Du, James P. Vary

Abstract
We adapt the Hulthén–Kohn method suggested by Efros [Phys. Rev. C 99 , 034620 (2019)] for calculating various observables in the continuum and discrete spectrum using two-body interactions in single- and coupled-channel systems. We explore the convergence of phase shifts and wave functions as well as the location of S S -matrix poles which enables obtaining both resonance and bound state parameters. We find that employing a harmonic oscillator basis, together with an interaction smoothing scheme introduced by Gyarmati et al. [Nucl. Phys. A 326 , 119 (1979)], and adopting approximate bound-state solutions for the short-range components of basis wave functions lead to good convergence even with restricted oscillator quanta accessible for modern no-core shell model (NCSM) codes. The adapted Efros method will facilitate ab initio many-body nuclear structure applications.

II.1
Ansatz wave function

The scattering wave function at the energy
E
in the center-of-mass system corresponding to the entrance channel
i
is parameterized as

u_{i}(E;\vec{r})=\eta^{-}_{i}(\vec{r})-\sum_{j=1}^{w}\eta^{+}_{j}(\vec{r})\,\mathbb{S}_{ji}(E)+\sum_{q=0}^{v-1}b_{q}^{i}(E)\,\beta_{q}(\vec{r}).

(1)

Here
\vec{r}
is the coordinate of relative separation of colliding fragments, the channel multi-indices
i
and
j
include all quantum numbers distinguishing the channels and those common for all coupled channels
like the orbital momentum of relative motion of colliding particles
l_{j}
in the channel
j

S_{j}

J
and its projection
M_{J}
.
\beta_{q}(\vec{r})
are
v
linearly independent short-range functions (SRFs) vanishing as
{r\rightarrow\infty}
which are introduced to describe the wave function at short distances. The set of
v
energy-dependent coefficients
b_{q}^{i}(E)
,

q=0,...\>,v-1
, together with
w

S
-matrix elements
\mathbb{S}_{ji}(E)
,
j=1,...\>,w
, comprise a set of
N=v+w
unknowns in each given entrance channel
i
that should be determined within this approach.

For nuclear physics applications, it is natural and convenient to use the
expansions in terms of harmonic oscillator functions. The harmonic oscillator basis has been used in many
ab initio
nuclear physics applications, for example,
in the NCSM and coupled cluster applications mentioned above as well as to describe clustering from first principles in

,
{}^{10}\textrm{Be}
, and
{}^{12}\textrm{C}

[

]

to be equal to finite sums of harmonic oscillator functions,

\beta_{q}(\vec{r})=\sum_{j=1}^{w}\sum_{n=0}^{M_{j}-1}a_{qn}^{j}\phi_{nj}(\vec{r}),

(2)

or just to single oscillator functions
\phi_{nj}(\vec{r})
in different channels

supposing that all
coefficients

in Eq. (
2
) with an
exception of one of them for each value of

are equal to zero. The oscillator functions
\phi_{nj}(\vec{r})
are defined as

\displaystyle\phi_{nj}(\vec{r})\equiv\phi_{nl_{j}}\!(\vec{r})=R_{nl_{j}}\!(r)\,\mathscr{Y}_{JM_{J}}^{l_{j}S_{j}}(\Omega_{\hat{r}}),

(3)

where

are generalized spherical harmonics which describe the
spin-angular degrees of freedom in the channel

\mathscr{Y}_{JM_{J}}^{lS}(\Omega_{\hat{r}})=\sum_{m_{S},m_{l}}(lm_{l}Sm_{S}|JM_{J})Y_{lm_{l}}(\Omega_{\hat{r}})\chi_{m_{S}}^{S},

(4)

(lm_{l}Sm_{S}|JM_{J})

69

(5)

[
70

(6)

is the oscillator length parameter,

The long-range behavior of the wave

\displaystyle\eta^{\pm}_{i}(\vec{r})\equiv\eta^{\pm}_{i}(k,\vec{r})=\tilde{h}^{\pm}_{l_{i}}(kr)\mathscr{Y}_{JM_{J}}^{l_{i}S_{i}}(\Omega_{\hat{r}}).

(7)

Here

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{\pm}_{l}(kr)=\tilde{n}_{l}(kr)\pm ij_{l}(kr),

(8)

\displaystyle k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}

(9)

is the relative momentum of colliding particles,

(10)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}^{\pm}_{l}(z)

\displaystyle\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{z\to\infty}{\pm ih^{(1,2)}_{l}(z)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{z\to\infty}\frac{i^{\mp l}}{z}e^{\pm iz}.

The singularity at the origin of the spherical Neumann

and

It is convenient sometimes to employ the standing-wave approach where the continuum wave functions are real-valued:

{u}_{i}(E;\vec{r})=j_{l_{i}}(kr)\,\mathscr{Y}_{JM_{J}}^{l_{i}S_{i}}(\Omega_{\hat{r}})\\

As for the functions
\bar{\beta}_{q}(\vec{r})
, we suggest to use
{N-1}
functions
\beta_{q}(\vec{r})
plus an additional function
\bar{\beta}_{N-1}(\vec{r})

\phi_{\mathcal{N}}(\vec{r}),&q=N-1.\end{cases}

(57)

A natural option is to use
\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N-1\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\leq\mathcal{N}
eigenfunctions

of the truncated Hamiltonian (

which solve the Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle H^{tr}\beta_{q}^{\textrm{Efn}}(\vec{r})=E_{q}\beta_{q}^{\textrm{Efn}}(\vec{r}),

\beta_{q}^{\textrm{Efn}}(\vec{r})=\sum_{n=0}^{\mathcal{N}-1}a^{\textrm{Efn}}_{qn}\phi_{n}(\vec{r}).

In this case,

coefficients

{v=N-1}
unknown energy-dependent coefficients

in the wave function expansion (

The use of the eigenfunctions simplifies the calculation of matrix
A=A^{1}
:

A_{qq^{\prime}}=(E_{q}-E)\delta_{qq^{\prime}},\quad q=0,...\,,N-1;\quad q^{\prime}=0,...\,,N-2.

(60)

The choice of eigenfunctions
\beta_{q}^{\textrm{Efn}}(\vec{r})
as SRFs is also convenient from the point of view of many-body applications in combination with the NCSM or other
ab initio
approaches. These approaches conventionally utilize the
M-scheme
and thus operate with a huge number of many-body basis functions (Slater determinants) that do not have a definite angular momentum
J
. Therefore, to simplify applications of the adapted Efros method, it is preferable to organize superpositions of these basis functions with a definite
J
value. The available NCSM codes like MFDn
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20

and

[

]
provide the NCSM eigenenergies and eigenfunctions with definite

J
superpositions of
M-scheme
Slater determinants is to use the NCSM eigenfunctions corresponding to eigenenergies in the energy range of interest.

We note that in the case of
N=\mathcal{N}
(and, generally speaking, with
N>\mathcal{N}
)
the version of the Efros formalism under discussion is equivalent to the HORSE formalism
[

of SRFs
\bar{\beta}_{q}(\vec{r})

\mathcal{N}
. The HORSE formalism provides exact solutions for the Hamiltonian Eq. (
56

{\cal N}
. The adapted Efros formalism with

suggests an approximation of the HORSE and generates solutions which converge to the HORSE solutions with increasing

and even with

in some cases. This feature is especially important because the HORSE method is impractical for many-body problems because it requires the calculation of all eigenstates with a definite
J

0s
center-of-mass motion. The number of such eigenstates is very large and is unknown. See Ref.
[

for a discussion of the number of

VI
Conclusions

We adapted the Efros method
[
60
]
for calculating various observables in the continuum and discrete spectrum using two-body interactions in single- and coupled-channel systems. Additional features are introduced to the adapted method with the goal of utilizing it in future many-body applications. These features include using achievements developed
within the HORSE formalism
[
40
]
, constructing basis wave functions by using different choices of linearly independent superpositions of oscillator functions as SRFs, and utilizing a smoothing scheme suggested in Refs.
[
64
,
65
]
.

We demonstrated the utility of these features by investigating in detail possibilities of the adapted Efros method in describing scattering parameters, wave functions, and in locating
S
-matrix poles to obtain resonant energies and widths or accurate bound state energies and wave functions. To study the convergence and accuracy of the adapted Efros method, we utilized various two-body systems where the exact results may be obtained by other
approaches. In particular, we studied the description of
np
scattering in the
{{}^{1}S}_{0}

partial wave in the single-channel version of the Efros approach and in the
{{}^{3}SD}_{1}

coupled partial waves in its two-channel version using the realistic Daejeon16
NN

interaction. To investigate the location of
S
-matrix poles, we explored the
\frac{3}{2}^{-}

resonance in
n\alpha
scattering described by the Woods–Saxon potential. We located also the

S
-matrix poles in the two-channel systems by calculating the
deuteron bound state using the Daejeon16
NN
interaction. In SM
[
67
]
, we also calculated the resonance parameters
in the Noro–Taylor two-channel model
[
81
]
. In each of those cases, we have demonstrated that the adapted Efros method combined with the smoothing of potential energy matrix elements suggested in Refs.
[
64
,
65
]
significantly improve the convergence of all calculations and lead to accurate results using a limited number of basis functions even when restricted to oscillator quanta accessible for modern NCSM computations.

We note that obtaining accurate results with limited oscillator quanta is essential for making our method applicable for many-body problems. Having in mind future applications in many-body systems in combination
with the NCSM
[
15
]
, we exploited the harmonic oscillator basis and introduced the
Hamiltonian
H^{tr}
truncated in the oscillator basis at some maximal oscillator
quanta
N_{\max}
as is done in the NCSM. We demonstrated how this truncated
Hamiltonian
H^{tr}
can be utilized within the Efros method. We suggested some
modifications of the formalism of the Efros method using achievements developed
within the HORSE formalism
[
40
]
in scattering theory. The Efros method
is equivalent to the HORSE approach if the set of SRFs includes all oscillator functions
with oscillator quanta up to
N_{\max}
. Calculations within the HORSE method however, are impractical in many-body applications since it requires the calculation of all eigenstates with definite
J
and
0s
center-of-mass motion which is extremely large and is unknown. The great advantage of the adapted Efros method is that it provides a reasonable convergence with significantly reduced SRF sets. One can use as SRFs a limited number of
the oscillator functions describing
the relative motion in each channel, a number of the eigenfunctions of
H^{tr\!}
, or
their mixture. The use of the
H^{tr}
eigenfunctions seems to be most convenient for future
many-body applications of the Efros method in combination with the NCSM. Therefore, most of our studies were performed with the eigenfunction SRF sets.

Generally, the adapted Efros method provides accelerated convergence and good accuracy in the description of various observables with a number of eigenfunction SRFs that are
accessible in modern NCSM calculations.
We did not find problems in calculations of
single-channel scattering but in calculations of two-channel scattering, we obtained
spikes and discontinuities of some
S
-matrix elements discussed in the SM Ref.
[
67
]
. These shortcomings,
however, can be eliminated by choosing a suitable SRF selection.

We conclude that the adapted
Efros method in combination with the NCSM is promising for
ab initio
studies of
elastic scattering and reactions with light nuclei. By locating the
S
-matrix poles
within this approach, one can investigate resonant states in light nuclei and derive
resonant energies and widths. The
S
-matrix poles associated with bound states which
can be calculated within the Efros extension of the NCSM, will improve the results of calculations of bound state energies and wave functions. In particular, it will be possible
to describe the asymptotically decreasing tails of the wave functions.
These improvements will result in converged and accurate predictions of various nuclear
observables like the rms radii, quadrupole moments, asymptotic normalization constants,
electromagnetic transition rates, etc. The most important improvements are expected in
weakly bound systems, e. g., in drip-line nuclei which are a focus of modern
research in nuclear physics.

This set of SRFs
\{\beta_{q}\}
may be arbitrary: it is only important that the
functions
\beta_{q}
are linearly independent. In this case, one can use the
over-complete Eq. (
27
) which suggest additional relations for independent
calculations of the unknowns that should be equal
such as
\mathbb{S}_{12}(E)=\mathbb{S}_{21}(E)
in the case of two-channel scattering. As an
example we consider the
S
-matrix
calculations at the energy of

MeV in the coupled
{}^{3}SD_{1}

np

H^{tr}
eigenfunction SRFs

\{\beta_{q}\}

68

-0.0022-0.0035i&1-0.0002i\\[3.00003pt]
\end{pmatrix}\!,

which is unitary and
\mathbb{S}_{12}(E)=\mathbb{S}_{21}(E)
. Since
we can switch from the “complete” set of eigenfunction SRFs to an equivalent set of harmonic oscillator SRFs via a unitary transformation, both sets of SRFs
yield the same solutions.
Thus the “complete” set of SRFs
\{\beta_{q}\}
guarantees the consistency of relations
between unknowns following from Eq. (
27
).

As it was already noted, in the above case of the “complete” set of SRFs
\{\beta_{q}\}
,
the adapted Efros and HORSE methods give the same results. Since the “complete” set of SRFs in
current
ab initio
nuclear structure applications is extremely large, it
is more interesting to investigate the essential simplification suggested within the adapted
Efros method as compared to the HORSE method which makes it possible to use a reduced
set of SRFs. Let us
consider the same case as above, but for
N=12
. In this case, Eq. (
68
)
results in the
S

-0.0016-0.0025i&1-0.0002i\\[3.00003pt]
\end{pmatrix}

with broken unitarity and

\mathbb{S}_{12}(E)\neq\mathbb{S}_{21}(E)
. In the case of a reduced set of
SRFs
\{\beta_{q}\}
, one is pushed to use Eq. (
33
) and impose the
S
-matrix
symmetry either by calculating
\mathbb{S}_{21}(E)
and
setting
\mathbb{S}_{12}(E)
equal to it or vice versa.
