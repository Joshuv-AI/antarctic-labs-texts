Abstract
. Place one active particle at the root of a graph and a Poisson-distributed number of dormant particles at the other vertices. Active particles perform simple random walk. Once the number of visits to a site reaches a random threshold, any dormant particles there become active. For this process on infinite d d -ary trees, we show that the total number of root visits undergoes a phase transition.



1.
Introduction

We study an interacting particle system in which active particles diffuse across a graph interspersed with dormant particles which become active once a visit threshold is met. There is initially one active particle at the root and
X_{v}
dormant particles at each nonroot site
v
. The
X_{v}
are independent with common probability measure
\xi
supported on the nonnegative integers. Each site is also independently assigned a threshold
T_{v}
with common probability measure
\tau
supported on the positive integers with
\tau(\infty)<1
. We will write
X
and
T
for generic random variables corresponding to
\xi
and
\tau
, respectively. Active particles perform simple random walk in discrete time steps while dormant particles remain in place. Once the total number of visits to
v
by active particles reaches
T_{v}
, any dormant particles at
v
are converted to active particles.

These dynamics may be written as a reaction equation by viewing
A
-particles as active and
B_{n}
-particles as dormant at a site that has received
n
total visits from
A
-particles. The reaction rules at
v\in G
are then

(1)

\displaystyle A+B_{n}\to\begin{cases}A+A,&n=T-1\\
A+B_{n+1},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Note that if multiple
A
-particles arrive simultaneously, each visit contributes towards meeting the threshold.

The dynamics at (
1
) with
\tau=\delta_{1}
have been interpreted as a model for combustion as well as rumor/infection spread
[
RS04
,
AMP02b
]
. It is natural to introduce thresholds, since in these applications multiple interactions may be required to spark a reaction in the such systems. Other recent work used similar dynamics with thresholds to model viral phages cooperating to overcome immunity in host bacteria
[
BP22
,
LGM+18
]
.
From a mathematical perspective, it is interesting to measure the effect different thresholds have on the propagation of
A
-particles.

Because of the chaotic manner in which
B
-particles are converted to
A
-particles, the process with reactions at (
1
) is often colloquially referred to as the
frog model
;
A
-particles are
active frogs
and
B
-particles are
sleeping frogs
. The vivid imagery, though removed from applications, is useful when describing the process. For this reason, we will refer to particles as frogs and call the process the
threshold frog model on
G
with threshold
\tau
and initial configuration
\xi
which we abbreviate with
\TFM(G,\tau,\xi)
. If
\tau
or
\xi
are point masses
\delta_{k}
or have a named distribution we will often replace them with the simpler representation. For example,
\TFM(G,\delta_{1},\Poi(\mu))
has
\tau=\delta_{1}
and
\xi\sim\Poi(\mu)
where
\Poi(\mu)
denotes a Poisson distribution with mean
\mu
.

There is a significant body of work devoted to studying frog models on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The first published result about the frog model came from Telcs and Wormald
[
TW99
]
and concerned the number of visits to the root for
\TFM(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\delta_{1},\delta_{1})
. Later, Alves, Machado, and Popov in
[

proved that the set of visited sites scaled linearly by time converges to a deterministic limiting shape. A similar result in continuous time was proven by Ramírez and Sidoravicius in
[
RS04
]

Subsequent work has studied variations at the front
[
CQR09
,
BR10
,
KRS12
]
. Additionally, some variants have attracted recent attention including: the frog model with death
[

,

]
; competitive dynamics with two species of

[

,

]
; a version in which

[

; versions in which the random walk paths have drift

,

,

To our knowledge no previous work has considered the threshold variant described at (

1.1.
Result

Our focus is the threshold frog model on the infinite complete
d
-ary tree
\mathbb{T}_{d}
i.e., the rooted tree in which each vertex has
d
child vertices. Denote the root by
\varnothing
. We assume that
\xi\sim\Poi(\mu)
.
To lighten notation we will write

\TFM_{d}(\tau,\mu):=\TFM(\mathbb{T}_{d},\tau,\Poi(\mu)).

A fundamental statistic is the total number of visits by active frogs to the root up to time
t
, which we label
V_{t}
. It is convenient to not count the presence of the initially awake frog at the root as a visit. So, formally, if
A_{s}
is the number of active frogs at
\varnothing
at time
s
, then
V_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}A_{s}
. Let
V=\lim_{t\to\infty}V_{t}
. We call a realization of the process
recurrent
if
V=\infty
, and otherwise call it
transient
. Explicitly, we are interested in the quantity

\mu_{c}(\tau,d):=\inf\{\mu\colon\TFM_{d}(\tau,\mu)\text{ is recurrent almost surely}\}.

A simple adaptation of the last paragraph of the proof of
[
BFJ+19
, Proposition 1.4]
gives that recurrence of
\TFM
satisfies a
0
-
1
law. Thus, it is equivalent to define
\mu_{c}(\tau,d)
for recurrence occurring with positive probability.

Although trees are often simpler settings to study statistical physics models, this is not the case for the frog model. The process on the integer lattice was proven by Popov to be recurrent for all
\mu>0

[
Pop01
]
. It appears to us that one could easily extend this result to prove that
\TFM(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\tau,\xi)
is recurrent for all
d,\tau,
and
\xi
.
On the other hand, it was unknown for over a decade whether or not there was a recurrent phase for the frog model with
\tau\sim\delta_{1}
on a
d
-ary tree
[
Pop03
,
GS09
]
.
Hoffman, Johnson, and Junge resolved that question by proving that the frog model with one particle per site and threshold one is recurrent on the binary tree but transient on the
d
-ary tree with
d\geq 5
[
HJJ17
]
. The same authors showed that
0<\mu_{c}(\delta_{1},d)<\infty

[
HJJ16
]
. So the frog model with threshold one and
\xi\sim\Poi(\mu)
undergoes a phase transition from transience to recurrence as
\mu
is increased.

Transience and recurrence behavior for the frog model on trees is subtle.
Random walk paths inherit a drift from the tree structure. Individual particles visit each level of the tree a small number of times.
Widespread activation is required to overcome the drift and persistently send active particles to the root.
Thresholds introduce a new difficulty since active frogs will often silently pass through sites with sleeping frogs. It is not immediately clear whether or not recurrence can occur with thresholds.
Indeed, we were unable to find any argument that directly used the known recurrence of the frog model on trees without thresholds. Our main result establishes that an analogous phase transition occurs for any threshold.

Theorem 1
.

For all
d\geq 2
it holds that
0<\mu_{c}(\tau,d)<\infty.

A corollary of ?THM?
is that this transition holds for
\TFM(\mathbb{T}_{d},\tau,\xi)
with more general
\xi
. Establishing transience for small initial densities only uses a branching process comparison. So
\TFM(\mathbb{T}_{d},\tau,\xi)
can always be made transient by taking the mean of
\xi
sufficiently small. As for recurrence, the comparison result from
[
JJ18
]
implies that
\TFM(\mathbb{T}_{d},\tau,\xi)
is recurrent so long as
\xi
dominates a Poisson distribution with mean
m>\mu_{c}(\tau,d)
in the increasing concave (icv) order. Roughly speaking, the icv order rewards distributions for being concentrated. For example, a frog model with a fixed number of particles
m>\mu_{c}(\tau,d)
is more concentrated, and thus recurrent on
\mathbb{T}_{d}
. See
[
JJ18
]
for more details.

1.2.
Proof overview

Our main contribution is the upper bound on
\mu_{c}(\tau,d)
. It is obtained by considering a modified version of
\TFM_{d}(\tau,\mu)
that trims from the ranges of the random walk paths to produce an embedded sub-process with less root visits inspired by the
self-similar frog model
introduced in
[
HJJ17
]
. We restrict active particles to follow a lazy non-backtracking portion of their full random walk path. To accommodate thresholds, we only allow the threshold
T_{v}
at a site
v
to be met if the first active particle that visits
v
accrues at least
T_{v}-1
lazy steps (plus its initial step) there.

In ?THM?
, we prove that the number of visits to the root in our restricted model
V^{\prime}
satisfies a recursive distributional equation (RDE). The RDE
involves
(d-1)N
independent thinned copies of
V^{\prime}
with
N
a geometric random variable.
Our approach to deriving and analyzing the RDE follows the same general strategy as introduced in
[
HJJ16
]
. Namely, we use it to prove that if
V^{\prime}\succeq\Poi(\lambda)
, then in fact
V^{\prime}\succeq\Poi(\lambda+1)
. See ?THM?
. Bootstrapping implies that
V^{\prime}=\infty
almost surely. As
V\succeq V^{\prime}
, this gives ?THM?
.

The recursive equation satisfied by
V^{\prime}
is less homogeneous than past analogues. In particular, the number of i.i.d. copies of
V^{\prime}
is random and these variables are thinned by different (random) amounts. The most novel aspect of the proof of ?THM?
is the derivation of an exact formula for the probability a given subset of variables are “activated” in the Poisson version of our RDE. See ?THM?
. The formula is a basic consequence of Poisson thinning (see ?THM?
). However, past works
[
HJJ16
,
JJ16
]
missed this characterization, and instead relied on simpler approximations.
The approaches from
[
HJJ16
,
JJ16
]
are too crude to prove ?THM?
.
?THM?
allows us to balance the Poisson quantity of root visits with proportional probability bounds that penalize few visits. This balancing act can be seen in the formula (
14
) and tractable bound at (
15
). These, and ?THM?
, are the most novel technical aspects of our work.

1.3.
Discussion and a further question

?THM?
shows that, like the usual frog model, the threshold frog model on trees has a transience to recurrence phase transition. This confirms that the models are qualitatively similar. However, we expect that the quantitative behavior of the threshold is rather different. It would be interesting to describe the asymptotic growth of
\mu_{c}(\tau,d)
as
d\to\infty
. It is proven in
[
JJ16
]
that
\mu_{c}(\delta_{1},d)=O(d)
. Our approach can be refined to give a super-exponential bound
\log_{d}\mu_{c}(\tau,d)=O(d^{C}/\mathbf{E}[(d+1)^{-\tau}])
. We believe that the true growth rate is much smaller. A concrete open question, for which we have no conjecture, is determining whether the growth of
\mu_{c}(\delta_{2},d)
is polynomial, exponential, or super-exponential in
d
.

Lastly, we apply ?THM?

Lemma 4

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(Y=0)

2.2.
Construction

Concisely, in the self-similar frog model active frogs follow lazy non-backtracking random walk paths and at most one active frog is allowed to move away from the root to each vertex, that same frog is the only frog able to activate the frogs there. We now make this more precise.

We say that a walk or frog is

\varnothing

The only-one rule does not apply for non-backtracking steps towards the root.

Now we explain how the active frogs present at this stage move and wake new frogs. Each follows its lazy non-backtracking random walk and is possibly removed at each step due to the only-one rule. Suppose that a frog, say

2.3.
Properties

Now we turn to proving some useful properties of the self-similar threshold frog model.
The first step is relating

We rely on the intuitive, often cited monotonicity of the frog model that ignoring parts of the random walk paths and killing frogs decreases the total number of root visits in a given realization of the frog model (see the formal constructions of the frog model in

Lemma 12

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(A=\mathbf{a}\mid N=n)

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigl(-\sum_{x,j}(1-a_{x,j})\Bigl[\tfrac{\mu}{d+1}d^{x-n}+\lambda d^{x-n-1}+\lambda\sum_{y,i}a_{y,i}d^{-|y-x|-1}\Bigr]\Bigr)

(6)

\displaystyle\hskip 206.28247pt\times\mathbf{P}(A^{\mathbf{a}}\mid N=n).

The sum is over all
(x,j)
and
(y,i)
in
\{1,\ldots N\}\times\{1,\ldots,d-1\}

Conditional on

Proposition 13
.

There exists
\lambda_{0},\mu_{0}\geq 0
such that for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
and
\mu\geq\mu_{0}
it holds that

V^{\prime}\succeq\Poi(\lambda)
implies
V^{\prime}\succeq\Poi(\lambda+1)
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda\geq 0
and assume that
V^{\prime}\succeq\Poi(\lambda)
. It follows from ?THM?
, ?THM?
, and ?THM?
that

(7)

\displaystyle V^{\prime}

\displaystyle\succeq\Poi\left(\textstyle\frac{\mu}{d+1}d^{-N+1}+\lambda d^{-N}+\lambda\sum_{y,i}A_{y,i}d^{-y}\right).

Let
\Theta
to be the random Poisson parameter in (
7
). ?THM?
gives that
\Poi(\Theta)\succeq\Poi(\lambda+1)
if and only if
\mathbf{E}[e^{-\Theta}]\leq e^{-\lambda-1}
. Proving this inequality for
\mu
and
\lambda
large enough is our goal.

To this end, we start by writing

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{-\Theta}]

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\mu}{d+1}d^{-n+1}}\mathbf{P}(N=n)s_{n}

with

(9)

\displaystyle s_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\exp\left(-\lambda({d^{-n}+\textstyle\sum_{y,i}a_{y,i}d^{y-n}})\right)\mathbf{P}(A=\mathbf{a}\mid N=n).

Note that the sum in
s_{n}
is over all
\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{(d-1)n}
.
We first require a bound on
s_{n}
. Let

(10)

\displaystyle h_{n}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle:=d^{-n}+\sum_{y,i}a_{y,i}d^{y-n}+\sum_{x,j}(1-a_{x,j})\left[d^{x-n-1}+\sum_{y,i}a_{y,i}d^{-|y-x|-1}\right],

and
g_{n}(\mathbf{a}):=\frac{\mu}{d+1}d^{x-n}.

Applying ?THM?
lets us write

(11)

\displaystyle s_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{a}}e^{-\lambda h_{n}(\mathbf{a})-g_{n}(\mathbf{a})}\mathbf{P}(A^{\mathbf{a}}\mid N=n).

The trivial bounds

(12)

\displaystyle s_{n}\leq\sum_{\mathbf{a}}e^{-\lambda h_{n}(\mathbf{a})}=2^{(d-1)n}\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda h_{n}(\mathbf{a})}].

In the last equality, the expectation is taken with respect to the uniform measure on
\{0,1\}^{(d-1)n}

We have reduced bounding
s_{n}
to estimating the moment generating function of

evaluated at a uniformly random binary string
\mathbf{a}

Lemma 14
.

If
\mathbf{a}
is a uniformly random binary string from
\{0,1\}^{(d-1)n}
and

is as defined at (
10

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda h_{n}(\mathbf{a})}]\leq e^{-\lambda}2^{-(d-1)n+1}(1+e^{-\lambda/d^{2}})^{(d-1)n-1}.

Proof.

Rearranging and simplifying

gives

(14)

\displaystyle h_{n}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{x,j}^{n}\sum_{y,i}^{n}(1-a_{x,j})a_{y,i}d^{-|x-y|-1}+\sum_{x,i}a_{x,i}\left[d^{-x}-d^{x-n-1}\right].

Let
f_{n}(\mathbf{a}):=|\{(y,i)\colon a_{y,i}\neq a_{y,i+1}\}|
with the convention that
a_{y,d}=a_{y+1,1}

counts the number of “bit flips” reading

Each flip contributes

(15)

\displaystyle h_{n}(\mathbf{a})\geq 1+f_{n}(\mathbf{a})/d^{2}.

If
\mathbf{a}
is sampled uniformly from

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda f_{n}(\mathbf{a})/d^{2}}]

\displaystyle=(2^{-1}+2^{-1}e^{-\lambda/d^{2}})^{(d-1)n-1}

This and (

Continuing towards the goal of bounding

Applying (
