Multigraded Sylvester forms, Duality and Elimination Matrices
Laurent Busé, Marc Chardin, Navid Nemati

Abstract
. In this paper we study the equations of the elimination ideal associated with n + 1 n+1 generic multihomogeneous polynomials defined over a product of projective spaces of dimension n n . We first prove a duality property and then make this duality explicit by introducing multigraded Sylvester forms. These results provide a partial generalization of similar properties that are known in the setting of homogeneous polynomial systems defined over a single projective space. As an important consequence, we derive a new family of elimination matrices that can be used for solving zero-dimensional multiprojective polynomial systems by means of linear algebra methods.



1.
Introduction

The elimination of several variables from a system of homogeneous polynomial equations is a fundamental operation that is involved in many computational methods in algebraic geometry. It has received a lot of interest in the existing literature, from the very beginning of elimination theory with contributions by Cayley, Sylvester, Macaulay, and many others, to its more recent developments deeply rooted in modern algebraic geometry (e.g.
[
GKZ94
]
). The geometric interpretation of elimination is in terms of projection maps, as illustrated by the famous Main Theorem of elimination theory
[
EH00
, Chapter V]
. On the algebraic side, the elimination of variables from homogeneous equations relies on the saturation of the ideal generated by these equations with respect to the ideal generated by the variables. The importance of this saturated ideal was already noticed from the classical literature on elimination theory; polynomials in this ideal are called
inertia forms
after Hurwitz. The case of generic complete intersections has been extensively studied and structural results are known, especially in the case of
n+1
generic homogeneous polynomials in
n+1
variables, as it corresponds to the setting of the Macaulay resultant, which is of particular interest. To be more precise, let us introduce some notation.

Let
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
be the
n+1
homogeneous polynomials in the variables
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
with indeterminate coefficients. We denote by
d_{i}
the degree of
F_{i}
which is a homogeneous polynomial in the graded polynomial ring
C=A[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
, where
A
stands for the universal ring of coefficients, i.e. the polynomial ring over the integers whose variables are all the coefficients of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
(see Section
2.1
for more details). The saturation of the ideal
I=(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})
with respect to the ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
is the ideal
I^{\sat}:=(I:{\mathfrak{m}}^{\infty})
. It is well known that the graded component of degree 0 of
I^{\sat}
,
(I^{\sat})_{0}=I^{\sat}\cap A
, is a principal and prime ideal of
A
generated by the Macaulay resultant of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
. Setting
\delta:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(d_{i}-1)
, we also have that
I^{\sat}_{\nu}=I_{\nu}
for all
\nu>\delta
, a property that is often summarized by saying that all inertia forms of degree
\nu>\delta
are trivial inertia forms, as they can be obtained as polynomial combinations of the
F_{i}
’s. Actually, there exists a duality that provides many structural properties of the quotient
I^{\sat}/I
. This duality can be made explicit by means of Bezoutian determinants, also called Morley forms; see
[
Jou91
,
Jou97
,
SS01
]
(where this duality is actually proved in the more general context of anisotropic graded rings). In particular, one gets that the graded components
(I^{\sat}/I)_{\nu}
are free
A
-modules for all
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
and explicit bases are known. More precisely, the graded component of degree
\delta
is an
A
-module of rank one which is generated by the (twisted) Jacobian determinant of the polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
. Basis for the other graded components are obtained by means of Sylvester forms associated with
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
, as proved in
[
Jou97
]
(see Section
2.10
for a review of this construction). For lower degree
\nu
, to the best of our knowledge, their is no known explicit sets of generators in general; except for very particular settings as the case
n=1

[
Jou97
,
Bus09
]
.

An important application of the above results is solving zero-dimensional polynomial systems, with coefficients in a field
{\mathbb{K}}
, by means of linear algebra techniques. The main ingredient is to build matrices whose columns are filled with the coefficients of some inertia forms of a given degree
\nu
with respect to a given basis of homogeneous polynomials of degree
\nu
(e.g. the canonical monomial basis). Typical examples, in the generic setting, are matrices of the maps (of free
A
-modules)

(1.1)

\displaystyle\oplus_{i=0}^{n}C_{\nu-d_{i}}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})}

\displaystyle C_{\nu}

\displaystyle(G_{0},\ldots,G_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}G_{i}F_{i},

which correspond to the graded components of the presentation matrix of
I
. The columns of these matrices are filled with the coefficients of trivial inertia forms of degree
\nu
. When the coefficients of the
F_{i}
’s are specialized to a field
{\mathbb{K}}
, which corresponds to a polynomial system over
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}
that we denote by
f_{0}=\cdots=f_{n}=0
, it turns out that for all
\nu>\delta
the matrices (
1.1
) are not surjective after specialization if and only if the
f_{i}
’s have a common root in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}}
,
\overline{{\mathbb{K}}}
denoting the algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{K}}
. More importantly, if the number of common roots of the
f_{i}
’s is finite, then the corank of these matrices is precisely this number; we will quote this property as the
drop-of-rank property
. This was first noticed and proved by Lazard in his foundational paper
[
Laz81
]
. He also showed that linear algebra techniques, such as Gaussian elimination, can be performed on the cokernel of these matrices to extract (approximate values of) the roots of the polynomial system considered. Another close approach relies on the computation of eigenvalues and eigenvectors from the cokernel of these matrices. It is based on the famous Eigenvalue Theorem that goes back to Stickelberger (see
[
Cox20
]
for more details). In this paper we will focus on the construction of elimination matrices and we refer the readers to
[
CLO98
]
and
[
Tel20
]
for more details on the solving of polynomial systems from elimination matrices having the drop-of-rank property. We retain that from a computational efficiency perspective it is useful to build as small as possible such matrices. Sylvester forms can be used for that purpose, by completing the matrices (
1.1
) in degree
\nu
such that
\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}\leq\nu<\delta
, so that the drop-of-rank property is preserved. We will review this construction and its related results with more details in Section
2
.

The main objective of this paper is the extension of the above results from the single graded case to the multigraded case. Thus, instead of considering homogeneous polynomials in a single set of variables, we consider multihomogeneous polynomials. In geometric terms, such multihomogeneous equations define hypersurfaces in a product of projective spaces
{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{1}}\times\ldots\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{r}}
. We will consider multiprojective polynomial systems of
n+1
hypersurfaces where
n=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}
. Let
F_{0},\dots,F_{n}\in C
be the
n+1
generic multihomogeneous polynomials of degrees
{\bm{d}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{d}}_{n}
, where
C=A[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]
with
A
the universal ring of coefficients. Such systems arise in many contexts and applications and there is a rich literature on their study. In particular, the theory of multiprojective resultant has been extensively covered, for instance by Gelfand, Kapranov and Zelevinsky in
[
GKZ94
]
from a geometric perspective, and by Rémond in
[
Rém01
]
and Jouanolou and his students in
[
Chk88
,
Cha88
]
from more algebraic and computational points of view. However, to the best of our knowledge, structural results on the corresponding saturated ideal are not available, as well as compact elimination matrices built from non trivial inertia forms and having the drop-of-rank property. The main contribution of this paper is to provide new results in this direction.

In the multigraded setting, the saturation of ideals is done with respect to the product
{\mathfrak{b}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}
where
{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}
is the irrelevant ideal associated with the projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}}
. Similarly to the single graded case, one can define a critical degree
{\bm{\delta}}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bm{d}}_{i}-(n_{1}+1,\dots,n_{r}+1)
.
In Section
3
, we prove a duality property for the ideal
I
generated by
n+1
generic multihomogeneous polynomials (Theorem
3.10
) and derive some consequences:

Theorem A
.

Let
F_{0},\dots,F_{n}
be the
n+1
generic multihomogeneous polynomials. There exists
\Theta\subset{\bm{\delta}}-{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
, such that

(I^{\sat}/I)_{\bm{\mu}}={\mathrm{Hom}}_{A}((C/I)_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}},A)

for every
{\bm{\mu}}\in\Theta
.

The region
\Theta
is explicitly described in Theorem
3.10
. In particular, we recover the known fact that
(I^{\sat}/I)_{{\bm{\delta}}}
is a free
A
-module of rank
1
(Corollary
3.11
) and more generally we show that there is a region of degrees for which graded components of the quotient
I^{\sat}/I
are free
A
-modules.
In Section
4
we provide some explicit basis for these graded components. The graded component of degree
{\bm{\delta}}
is generated by a multigraded twisted Jacobian; this result already appeared in the existing literature, for instance in
[
CCD97
]
and
[
CDS98
]
(in the more general context of toric geometry) and in
[
Chk88
]
. After reviewing this construction in Section
4.1
, we introduce multigraded Sylvester forms (Definition
4.6
) and prove that they provide the expected basis for some multigraded components of
I^{\sat}/I
(Theorem
4.9
):

Theorem B
.

Let
F_{0},\dots,F_{n}
be the
n+1
generic multihomogeneous polynomials of degrees
{\bm{d}}_{i}:=(d_{i,1},\dots,d_{i,r})
for
0\leq i\leq n
. For every
{\bm{\mu}}:=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
such that
0\leq\mu_{j}<\min_{i}d_{i,j}
, the set of
multigraded Sylvester forms yields an
A
-basis of the free

A
-module
(I^{\sat}/I)_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}}
.

From all this, in Section
5
we construct new elimination matrices that are built using non trivial inertia forms and having the drop-of-rank property. We conclude with some illustrative examples, including some matrices due to Dixon
[
Dix1909
]
that we recover and that are associated with the resultant of three generic bihomogeneous polynomials of the same bidegree.

2.
Preliminaries: the single graded case

In this section we review some results on elimination matrices associated with zero-dimensional polynomial systems defined by
n+1
equations over a single projective
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}
, where
{\mathbb{K}}
is field. More precisely, we consider
n+1
homogeneous polynomials
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
in the polynomial ring
R={\mathbb{K}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
of positive degrees
d_{0},\ldots,d_{n}
. We describe a family of matrices that only depend on the coefficients of the
f_{i}
’s and that can be used to solve this polynomial system by means of linear algebra techniques.

Our strategy to study elimination ideals of homogeneous polynomial systems has two steps. We first consider the generic setting, i.e. the coefficients of the
f_{i}
’s are seen as variables. Subsequently, we proceed by analyzing specializations of these coefficients to a field
{\mathbb{K}}
. We will denote by
\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}
the algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{K}}
.

2.1.
Macaulay-type elimination matrices

We consider the generic polynomial system of
n+1
homogeneous polynomials in
n+1
variables of positive degrees
d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
, over a commutative ring
k
. Thus, for all
i=0,\ldots,n
we will denote by

F_{i}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{|{\bm{\alpha}}|=d_{i}}U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}}{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\alpha}}}

the generic polynomial of degree
d_{i}\geq 1
, where
{\bm{\alpha}}
is a multi-index
(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n+1}
,
{\bm{x}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is the monomial
x_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\ldots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
and
|{\bm{\alpha}}|:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}
. The universal ring of coefficients of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
over
k
is the ring

A_{k}:=k[U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}}:i=0,\ldots,n,\ |{\bm{\alpha}}|=d_{i}]

and we set
C_{k}=A_{k}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
. To not overload the notation, we will only display the base ring
k
when it plays an important role.

The polynomial ring
C
is canonically graded by setting
{\mathrm{deg}}(x_{i})=1
. We denote by
I
, respectively
{\mathfrak{m}}
, the homogeneous ideal of
C
generated by
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
, respectively
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
. We also define the graded quotient ring
B=C/I
and set
\delta:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(d_{i}-1)
.

From a geometric point of view, the ideal
I
defines a subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{A}
whose canonical projection on the affine (coefficient) space
{\mathrm{Spec}}(A)
is defined by the elimination ideal
{\mathfrak{A}}=(I:{\mathfrak{m}}^{\infty})\cap A
. Although this property is not needed for what follows, we notice that in our setting the ideal
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a prime and principal ideal which is generated by the Macaulay resultant of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}

[
Jou91
]
. The following classical result is important as it allows to compute
{\mathfrak{A}}
as the annihilator of some graded components of
B
(see e.g.
[
Jou97
]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

For any integer
\nu>\delta
,
{\mathfrak{A}}={\mathrm{ann}}_{A}(B_{\nu})
.

By classical properties of Fitting ideals, both ideals
{\mathrm{Fitt}}^{0}_{A}(B_{\nu})
and
{\mathrm{ann}}_{A}(B_{\nu})
of
A
have the same radical (for all
\nu\in{\mathbb{N}}
) and hence Lemma
2.2
suggests to consider presentation matrices of
A
-modules
B_{\nu}
. Consider the graded map

\displaystyle\Phi:\oplus_{i=0}^{n}C(-d_{i})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})}

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle(G_{0},\ldots,G_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle G_{0}F_{0}+G_{1}F_{1}+\cdots+G_{n}F_{n}.

For any integer
\nu
we denote by
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
the matrix of the graded component map
\Phi_{\nu}
of
\Phi
. It is a map of
A
-modules which provides a presentation of
B_{\nu}
. Thus, as a consequence of Lemma
2.2
, the ideal generated by the maximal minors of
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
has the same radical as
{\mathfrak{A}}
for all
\nu>\delta
.

Now, let us consider a zero-dimensional polynomial system with coefficients in a field
{\mathbb{K}}
:
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
are
n+1
homogeneous polynomials of degrees
d_{0},\ldots,d_{n}
in
R={\mathbb{K}}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
. This polynomial system can be seen as a specialization of the generic polynomial system
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
via a ring map
\rho:A_{\mathbb{Z}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{K}}
. In other words, setting
{\bm{f}}:=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
,
{\bm{f}}
can be seen as a point in
\mathrm{Spec}(A_{\mathbb{K}})
. Thus, we use the notation
I({\bm{f}})
for the ideal generated by
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
in
R
and similarly we will use the notation
B({\bm{f}})
and
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
.

The graded components of
B({\bm{f}})
are
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector spaces whose dimensions are defining the Hilbert function of
B({\bm{f}})
,
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})}(\nu):=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}B({\bm{f}})_{\nu}
. It is known since Hilbert that for sufficiently high values of
\nu
, the Hilbert function is a polynomial function which is called the Hilbert polynomial and denoted by
{\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})}(\nu)
. In the case where
I({\bm{f}})
defines finitely many points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}
, the Hilbert polynomial is a constant polynomial which is equal to the number of points defined by
I({\bm{f}})
, counted with multiplicity.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any integer
\nu>\delta
we have
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})}(\nu)={\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})}(\nu)
.

Proof.

This is a classical result; see for instance
[
Laz81
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
Jou96
, §1.5]
.
∎

All the above considerations lead to the following result that we will refer to as the
drop-of-rank property
in the rest of this paper.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that the polynomial system
{\bm{f}}=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
defines a finite subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}}
and let
\kappa
be its degree. Then, for all
\nu>\delta
, the dimension of the cokernel of
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
is equal to
\kappa
.

Proof.

This is a immediate consequence of the right exactness of the tensor product and Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Recall that the degree of a finite subscheme is the sum of the length of the local rings of the points. In particular, it is equal to zero if the subscheme is empty.

This proposition is the key property to use the matrix
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
to solve the polynomial system
{\bm{f}}=0
by means of linear algebra techniques, in particular singular value decompositions and eigenvalue computations. We notice that in general the matrices
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
are not square, except for rare exceptions, as the following one.

Example 2.5
.

If
n=1
, then the matrix of
{\mathbb{M}}_{\delta+1}({\bm{f}})
is nothing but the classical Sylvester matrix of the two polynomials
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
. Thus, Proposition
2.4
is the well known property that the corank of
{\mathbb{M}}_{\delta+1}({\bm{f}})
is equal to the degree of the greatest common divisor of
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
.

2.6.
Saturation and duality

To construct smaller matrices having the drop-of-rank property, one possibility is to add new equations to our polynomial system. As we do not want to change its geometry, we consider the ideal
I^{\sat}
which is obtained by saturation of the ideal
I
with respect to the irrelevant ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}
in
C
, i.e.

I^{\sat}=(I:{\mathfrak{m}}^{\infty})=\{p\in C:\exists k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\ {\mathfrak{m}}^{k}p\subset I\}\subset C.

We set
B^{\sat}=C/I^{\sat}
and we adopt the following notation for specialized polynomial systems over a field
{\mathbb{K}}
. Let
{\bm{f}}
be a polynomial system with coefficients in
{\mathbb{K}}
, we denote by
B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}
the quotient ring
R/I({\bm{f}})^{\sat}
, where the saturation is taken after specialization, and by
B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})
the quotient ring
R/I^{\sat}({\bm{f}})
, where the saturation is taken in the generic setting and then specialized. The following result is an improvement of Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that
I({\bm{f}})
defines finitely many points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\bar{k}}
, say
\kappa\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then for any integer
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
,
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}(\nu)={\mathrm{HF}}_{B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})}(\nu)={\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})}(\nu)=\kappa
.

Proof.

A first observation is that the three ideals
I({\bm{f}})\subset I^{\sat}({\bm{f}})\subset I({\bm{f}})^{\sat}
of
R
have the same saturation so
B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}
,
B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})
and
B({\bm{f}})
have the same Hilbert polynomial.

By the Grothendieck-Serre formula
[
BH93
, Theorem 4.3.5]
, since
H^{i}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B({\bm{f}})^{\sat})=0
for all
i>1
by our assumption, we have
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}(\nu)={\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}(\nu)
for all
\nu
such that
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B({\bm{f}})^{\sat})_{\nu}=0
(observe that
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B({\bm{f}})^{\sat})=0
). The fact that this latter condition is satisfied for all
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
follows classically from the analysis of the two Čech-Koszul spectral sequences associated with
I({\bm{f}})
; see for instance
[
Jou80
, §2.11]
.

Similarly, we have
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})}(\nu)={\mathrm{HP}}_{B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})}(\nu)
for all
\nu
such that
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat}({\bm{f}}))_{\nu}=0
and
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat}({\bm{f}}))_{\nu}=0
. The vanishing of these two local cohomology modules can be controlled as fibers of projective morphisms. More precisely, by
[
Cha13
, Proposition 6.3]
we deduce that
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat}({\bm{f}}))_{\nu}=0
and
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat}({\bm{f}}))_{\nu}=0
for all
\nu
such that
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat})_{\nu}=0
and
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat})_{\nu}=0
. But
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat})=0
and
H^{1}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B^{\sat})_{\nu}=0
for all
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
by the analysis of the two Čech-Koszul spectral sequences associated with
I
, as we already mentioned, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

As a consequence of Lemma
2.7
, we notice that the canonical map from
I^{\sat}_{\nu}
to
I({\bm{f}})^{\sat}_{\nu}
, which is induced by the specialization
C\rightarrow R
sending
F_{i}
to
f_{i}
, is surjective for all
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
.

To take advantage of Lemma
2.7
, we need to understand the graded components of
{B^{\sat}}
, equivalently
{I^{\sat}}
, for all degree
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
. Since
I\subset I^{\sat}
, it is sufficient to analyze the quotient
I^{\sat}/I
. The following classical duality property is a key point
[
Jou96
,
SS01
]
.

Proposition 2.9
.

For any integer
\nu>\delta
,
\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{\nu}=0
. In addition, for any integer
0\leq\nu\leq\delta
there is a duality of
A_{k}
-modules

{\mathrm{Hom}}_{A_{k}}(B_{\nu},A_{k})\xrightarrow{\sim}\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{\delta-\nu}.

The duality maps in this proposition can described in terms of Morley forms, as proved by Jouanolou in
[
Jou97
, §3.11]
. In particular, for all
0\leq\nu<\min_{i}d_{i}
the graded components
\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{\delta-\nu}
are isomorphic to
C_{\nu}
and
A
-bases of these graded components are provided by Sylvester forms.

2.10.
Sylvester forms

We suppose given a multi-index
{\bm{\gamma}}:=(\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{n})
,
\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|{\bm{\gamma}}|=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\gamma_{i}<\min_{j}d_{j}
. Under this assumption, one can decompose any polynomial
F_{i}
under the form

(2.1)

F_{i}=x_{0}^{\gamma_{0}+1}F_{i,0}+x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}+1}F_{i,1}+\cdots+x_{n}^{\gamma_{n}+1}F_{i,n},

where the polynomials
F_{i,k}
are homogeneous of degree
d_{i}-\gamma_{k}-1
in
C
. Following
[
Jou97
, §3.10]
, we define the Sylvester form of the polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
in degree
{\bm{\gamma}}
as the determinant

{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{\bm{\gamma}}:=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}F_{0,0}&\cdots&F_{n,0}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
F_{0,n}&\cdots&F_{n,n}\end{array}\right).

By construction, the Sylvester form
{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{\bm{\gamma}}
belongs to the ideal
I^{\sat}
and is of degree
\delta-|{\bm{\gamma}}|
. It depends on the choice of decompositions (
2.1
), but its class in
I^{\sat}/I=H^{0}_{\mathfrak{m}}(B)
, which we denote by
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{\bm{\gamma}}
, does not depend on these choices; see
[
Jou97
, §3.10.1]
. We have the following property which is of particular interest for our purposes.

Proposition 2.11
.

For any multi-indexes
{\bm{\gamma}},{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}
such that
|{\bm{\gamma}}|=|{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}|<\min_{i}d_{i}
we have

(2.2)

{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}=\begin{cases}{\mathrm{sylv}}_{(0,\ldots,0)}&\mathrm{\ if\ }{\bm{\gamma}}={\bm{\gamma}}^{\prime}\\
0&\mathrm{\ otherwise.\ }\end{cases}

In addition, the set of Sylvester forms
\{{\mathrm{sylv}}_{\bm{\gamma}}\ :|{\bm{\gamma}}|=\delta-\nu\}
yields an
A_{k}
-basis of the graded component
\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{\nu}
for all
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
.

The form
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{(0,\ldots,0)}
plays a particular role: it is a generator of
\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{\delta}
, which is a free
A
-module of rank one, and it is actually proportional to the more classical Jacobian determinant of the polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
.

We notice that Sylvester forms are, by construction, universal in the coefficients of each polynomial
F_{i}
and are actually linear in each of these sets of coefficients. For any polynomial system
{\bm{f}}
with coefficients over a field
{\mathbb{K}}
, we denote by
{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{f}})
and
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{\bm{\gamma}}({\bm{f}})
the corresponding specialized Sylvester forms.

2.12.
Hybrid elimination matrices

From the previous results one can extend the family of elimination matrices
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
and get some more compact ones. For that purpose, for any integer
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
consider the
A
-modules morphism

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\nu}:\oplus_{i=0}^{n}C_{\nu-d_{i}}\oplus_{{\bm{\gamma}}:|{\bm{\gamma}}|=\delta-\nu}A

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle C_{\nu}

\displaystyle(G_{0},\ldots,G_{n},\ldots,\ell_{\bm{\gamma}},\ldots)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}G_{i}F_{i}+\sum_{|{\bm{\gamma}}|=\delta-\nu}\ell_{\bm{\gamma}}{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{{\bm{\gamma}}}.

We denote by
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
the matrix of (
2.3
) in canonical bases. This matrix is made of two column blocks; the matrix
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
defined in Section (
2.1
) and the coefficient matrix of the Sylvester forms of degree
\delta-\nu
. In particular, if
\nu>\delta
then the second block vanishes and
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}={\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
. Therefore, the family of matrices
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
can be seen as an extension of the family of matrices
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
that is valid for integers
\nu
such that
\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}<\nu\leq\delta
.

As a consequence of Proposition
2.11
,
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
is a presentation matrix of
B^{\sat}_{\nu}=C_{\nu}/I^{\sat}_{\nu}
. Hence, the ideal generated by the maximal minors of
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
has the same radical as the elimination ideal
{\mathfrak{A}}
. Moreover, the matrices
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
have the expected drop-of-rank property. To be more precise, observe that, by the construction, the matrices
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
are universal in the coefficients of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
. Hence, given a polynomial system
{\bm{f}}=\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
with coefficients in a field
{\mathbb{K}}
we denote by
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
the matrix
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
specialized to the system
{\bm{f}}
. We did not find the following result in the existing literature, although all the necessary ingredients to prove it are known (we notice that the matrix
{\mathbb{H}}_{\delta}
already appeared in
[
CDS98
, Proposition 2.1]
).

Proposition 2.13
.

Assume that the polynomial system
{\bm{f}}
defines a finite subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}_{\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}}
of degree
\kappa
. Then, for all
\nu>\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}
, the corank of
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
is equal to
\kappa
.

Proof.

By construction, the cokernel of
{\mathbb{H}}({\bm{f}})_{\nu}
is isomorphic to
B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})_{\nu}
and hence the claimed result follows from Lemma
2.7
.
∎

We notice that the matrix
{\mathbb{H}}_{\delta-\min_{i}d_{i}+1}
is of smaller size than the matrix
{\mathbb{M}}_{\delta+1}={\mathbb{H}}_{\delta+1}
and it can be used in a similar way to solve the polynomial system
f_{0}=\cdots=f_{n}=0
without changing its geometric structure. More precisely, assuming
d_{0}\geq d_{1}\geq\ldots\geq d_{n}
, the number of rows of
\psi_{\delta+1-\min_{i}d_{i}}
is equal to

\binom{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d_{i}}{n}

whereas the number of rows of the more commonly used matrix
\phi_{\delta+1}
is equal to

\binom{\sum_{i=0}^{n}d_{i}}{n}.
We also mention that for
\nu\leq\delta
we call the matrices
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
“hybrid elimination matrices” because of the following example.

Example 2.14
.

If
n=1
we have
\delta=d_{0}+d_{1}-2
and the matrix
{\mathbb{M}}_{d_{1}+d_{2}-1}
is the Sylvester matrix whose determinant is the Sylvester resultant of
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
. The matrices
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
, with
\max_{i}d_{i}\leq\nu\leq\delta
, corresponds to the well known hybrid Bézout matrices; see for instance
[
SGD97
,
DTGV02
]
.

In the rest of this paper, we will generalize the above results to the multigraded case, i.e. in the case where the polynomials
F_{i}
are multihomogeneous polynomials. For that purpose we will prove a (partial) duality property and introduce multigraded Sylvester forms.

3.
Multigraded saturation and duality

In the previous section we considered homogeneous polynomial equations defining hypersurfaces in the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. From now on we will consider multihomogeneous polynomial equations in a product of projective spaces. Our main goal in this section is to provide generalizations of Lemma
2.7
and Proposition
2.9
to this context. We will use the following notation for the rest of the article.

Notation 3.1
.

Fix positive integers
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}
and set
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
. For all
j=1,\ldots,r
we denote by
{\bm{x}}_{j}
the set of variables
x_{j,0},\ldots,x_{j,n_{j}}
and for all
i=0,\ldots,n
, we consider the generic multihomogeneous polynomial of degree
{\bm{d}}_{i}=(d_{i,1},\dots,d_{i,r})

F_{i}({\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{r}):=\sum_{|{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}|=d_{i,1},\ldots,|{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}|=d_{i,r}}U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}.

We define the universal ring of coefficients over the commutative ring
k
as

A_{k}=k[U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}:i=0,\ldots,n,|{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}|=d_{i,1},\ldots,|{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}|=d_{i,r}]

which is multigraded by setting

{\mathrm{deg}}(U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}})=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)=:\textbf{e}_{i}

(the “1” is at the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
place). Thus, the polynomials
F_{i}
are multihomogeneous polynomials in the polynomial ring
C:=A_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]
:

{\mathrm{deg}}_{{\bm{x}}_{j}}F_{i}=d_{i,j}\geq 1\textrm{ and }{\mathrm{deg}}_{{\bm{U}}}F_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i},

where
U
denotes the set of all coefficients of all the polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
.
We define the ideals
{\mathfrak{b}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}
where
{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}:=({\bm{x}}_{i})
for all
i
,
{\mathfrak{m}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\mathfrak{m}}_{i}\subset C
and
I:=(F_{0},\dots,F_{n})\subset C
, as well as the quotient ring
B:=C/I
. For all
j=1,\ldots,r
we define
\delta_{j}:=(\sum_{i=0}^{n}d_{i,j})-(n_{j}+1)
and we set

(3.1)

{\bm{\delta}}=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{r})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\bm{d}}_{i}-(n_{1}+1,n_{2}+1,\dots,n_{r}+1).

Geometrically, the multigraded ring
C
can be interpreted as the coordinate ring of the product of projective schemes
{\mathbb{P}}_{A_{k}}={\mathbb{P}}^{n_{1}}_{A_{k}}\times\ldots\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{r}}_{A_{k}}
. Thus,
{\mathfrak{b}}
is the irrelevant ideal of
{\mathbb{P}}_{A_{k}}
. The ideal
I
defines a subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}_{A_{k}}
whose canonical projection on the affine space
{\mathrm{Spec}}(A_{k})
is defined by the elimination ideal
{\mathfrak{A}}=(I:{\mathfrak{b}}^{\infty})\cap A_{k}
. Notice that, as expected, this elimination ideal is equal to
0
if the number of generators of
I
is less than or equal
n
(see Corollary
3.13
). Therefore, having
n+1
polynomials as the generators of
I
is the first interesting case of study for
{\mathfrak{A}}
.

We begin with two technical lemmas that are taken from
[
Chk88
, Chapter I, Proposition 3.1.2]
. We reproduce their proofs for the sake of completeness and accessibility. These lemmas are extensions of well-known results in the single graded case, see for instance
[
Jou91
, §4.2 and §4.7]
, to the multigraded case.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any sequence of
r
integers
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
such that
0\leq i_{j}\leq n_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,r
, there exists an isomorphism of
A^{\prime}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]
-algebras

B_{x_{1,i_{1}}\ldots x_{r,i_{r}}}\xrightarrow{\sim}A^{\prime}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]_{x_{1,i_{1}}\cdots x_{r,i_{r}}}

where
A^{\prime}_{k}=k[U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}|\,U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\neq U_{i,i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\,\,0\leq i\leq n]
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we consider the case
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
and we set
\sigma:=x_{1,n_{1}}\ldots x_{r,n_{r}}
. For all
i=0,\ldots,n
we also set

\epsilon_{i}:=U_{i,n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}
and we define the monomial
\tau_{i}=x_{1,n_{1}}^{d_{i,1}}\cdots x_{r,n_{r}}^{d_{i,r}}
and the polynomial
H_{i}:=F_{i}-\epsilon_{i}\tau_{i}
. Notice that
A_{k}=A^{\prime}_{k}[\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{r}]
. Now, consider the morphism
\phi
of
A^{\prime}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]
- algebras

\displaystyle\phi:A_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]

\displaystyle\rightarrow A^{\prime}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto-{H_{i}}/{\tau_{i}}

which leaves invariant all variables and all coefficients except the
\epsilon_{i}
’s. Since

\phi(F_{i})=0
for all
i
,
\phi
induces the claimed isomorphism of
A^{\prime}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]
- algebras.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

A consequence of the above lemma is that for any choice of sequences of integers
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
and
(i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{r}^{\prime})
, the monomial
x_{1,i_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots x_{r,i_{r}^{\prime}}
is not a zero divisor in
B_{x_{1,i_{1}}\ldots x_{r,i_{r}}}
. In particular, if the class of
P\in A_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]
is equal to 0 in
B_{x_{1,i_{1}}\ldots x_{r,i_{r}}}
then it is also equal to 0 in
B_{x_{1,i_{1}^{\prime}}\ldots x_{r,i_{r}^{\prime}}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The generic multihomogeneous polynomials
F_{0}
,
\ldots
,
F_{n}
form a regular sequence in
C_{x_{1,i_{1}}\ldots x_{r,i_{r}}}
for any sequence
(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})
of
r
integers such that
0\leq i_{j}\leq n_{j}
for all
j=1,\ldots,r
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we treat the case
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
for simplicity and we set
\sigma:=x_{1,n_{1}}\ldots x_{r,n_{r}}
.

To begin with, we claim that
F_{0}
is a nonzero divisor in
C
. To see it we use the following corollary of Dedekind-Mertens Lemma (see
[
BJ14
, Corollary 2.8]
): a polynomial
F
is a nonzero divisor in
C
if and only if its content ideal (i.e. the ideal in
A_{k}
generated by the coefficients of
F
) does not divide zero in
A_{k}
. Thus, as any coefficient
U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
is a nonzero divisor in
A_{k}
, we deduce that
F_{0}
is a nonzero divisor in
C
, hence in
C_{\sigma}
.

Now, set

\epsilon_{i}:=U_{i,n_{1},\dots,n_{r}}
for all
i=0,\ldots,n
, let
t
be an integer such that
0<t<n
and define

A^{(t)}_{k}:=k[U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\,|\,U_{i,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\neq\epsilon_{i},\,0\leq i\leq t],

so that
A_{k}=A^{(t)}_{k}[\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{t}]
. According to Lemma
3.2
(applied with
t+1
polynomials instead of
n+1
),

\left(A_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]/(F_{0},\dots F_{t})\right)_{\sigma}\simeq A^{(t)}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]_{\sigma}

and since
F_{t+1}
is a nonzero divisor in
A^{(t)}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]
by the above corollary of Dedekind-Mertens Lemma, we deduce that
F_{t+1}
is a nonzero divisor in
\left(A_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]/(F_{0},\dots F_{t})\right)_{\sigma}\simeq A^{(t)}_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots{\bm{x}}_{r}]_{\sigma}
.
∎

After these preliminaries, our next task is to provide the precise statement and proof of Theorem A. As our strategy relies on the analysis of some local cohomology modules, we first introduce additional notation in order to describe the local cohomologies of the polynomial ring

with respect to

Definition 3.5

For all

Example 3.7

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
F_{0},\dots,F_{n}
be the
n+1
generic multihomogeneous polynomials
of degree
{\bm{d}}_{0},\ldots,{\bm{d}}_{n}
, respectively. If
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
, then

\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}\simeq{\mathrm{Hom}}_{A_{k}}((C/I)_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}},A_{k}).

Proof.

We proceed by analyzing the spectral sequences associated with the Čech-Koszul double complex
{C}^{\bullet}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}({K}_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C))
. If we
start taking homologies vertically, in the second page we get

{}^{v}E^{2}_{p,q}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H_{q}(H^{p}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}({K}_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C)))&\mbox{if $0\leq p\leq n+1\textrm{ and }0\leq q\leq n+1,$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

If
p=n+1
and
q=n+1
, then by Mayer-Vietoris exact sequence,

{}^{v}E^{2}_{n+1,n+1}

\displaystyle\cong H_{n+1}(H^{n+1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(K_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C)))

\displaystyle\cong H_{n+1}\left(H^{n+r}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}\left(K_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\cong H_{n+1}(K_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C)\left({\bm{\delta}}\right)^{\star})

\displaystyle\cong B^{\star}({\bm{\delta}}).

The notation
(-)^{\star}
stands, classically, for the graded dual (see
[
BH93
, §1.5, p. 33]
for more details). For instance,
B^{\star}=\oplus_{{\bm{\nu}}}{\mathrm{Hom}}_{A}(B_{-{\bm{\nu}}},A)
.

By definition, if
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\Gamma_{0}
then for
0\leq p\leq n
,
\left(H^{p+1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(K_{p}({\bm{F}},C))\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}=0
, which means
\left({}^{v}E^{2}_{p+1,p}\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}=0
. The maps from
{}^{v}E^{2}_{n+1,n+1}
in the next pages are to
{}^{v}E^{2}_{p+1,p}
for
0\leq p\leq n
and no nonzero map points to
{}^{v}E^{\ell}_{n+1,n+1}
for
\ell\geq 2
. It follows that

\left({}^{v}E^{2}_{n+1,n+1}\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}\cong\left({}^{v}E^{\infty}_{n+1,n+1}\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}.

If
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\Gamma_{1}
then
\left(H^{p}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(K_{p}({\bm{F}},C))\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}=0
for
0\leq p\leq n
. Hence,
\left({}^{v}E^{\infty}_{p,p}\right)_{{\bm{\mu}}}=0
for
p\neq n+1
and
(^{v}E^{\infty}_{n+1,n+1})_{{\bm{\mu}}}\cong B^{\star}_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}}={\mathrm{Hom}}_{A}(B_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}},A)
.

If we start taking homology horizontally, the second page of the spectral sequence is:

\begin{matrix}\ast&\cdots&\ast&H_{1}(K_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C))&I^{\sat}/I\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\ast\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\ast.\\
\end{matrix}

Notice that vanishing of
H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(H_{1}(K_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C)))
follows from Lemma
3.4
. Finally, the claimed assertion follows from comparing the two spectral sequences.
∎

This is a direct consequence of Theorem

for any

.

Corollary 3.12

We follow the same lines of the proof of Theorem

. As the length of the Koszul complex is equal to

H_{n+2}(H^{n+1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}({K}_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C)))
implies that

\Supp\left(H_{1}({K}_{\bullet}({\bm{F}},C))\right)\subseteq\bigcup_{-1\leq p\leq n-1}\Supp\left(H^{p+1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}\left(K_{p+2}\left({\bm{F}},C\right)\right)\right)=\Gamma_{2}.

∎

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
0\leq m\leq n-1
,
G_{0},\dots,G_{m}
be the
m+1
generic multihomogeneous polynomials and

be the ideal generated by the

then

. In particular, the elimination ideal

is equal to

.

Proof.

Let
\textbf{G}=\{G_{0},\dots,G_{m}\}
and consider the two spectral sequences associated with the Čech-Koszul double complex

. Since

From here, the conclusion follows as

,

, since

for all

and one coordinate of a nonzero element in any

’s.
∎

In the case

Proposition 3.14
.

With the same assumption as in Theorem

∎

To conclude this section, we focus on multigraded zero-dimensional polynomial systems with coefficients in a field
{\mathbb{K}}
, our goal being to generalize Lemma
2.7
to the multigraded setting. Let
f_{0},\dots f_{n}
be
n+1
multihomogeneous polynomials of degree

in

. These polynomials can be considered as a specialization of the
n+1
generic polynomials

. Thus, following what we did in §
2.6
, we define
I({\bm{f}})
as the ideal generated by
{\bm{f}}:=\{f_{0},\dots,f_{n}\}
,
B({\bm{f}}):=R/I({\bm{f}})
,

and

. Similarly to the single graded case,
the graded component

,

, is a
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space and the Hilbert function of

is the function
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})}({\bm{\nu}})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}B({\bm{f}})_{\bm{\nu}}
. For
{\bm{\nu}}
sufficient large component-wise, the Hilbert function becomes a polynomial function which is called the Hilbert polynomial and that is denoted by
{\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})}({\bm{\nu}})
(see e.g.
[
BC17
, Proposition 4.26]
).

Proposition 3.15
.

Assume that
I({\bm{f}})
defines a finite subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{1}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}\times\ldots\times{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{r}}_{{\mathbb{K}}}
of degree
\kappa
. Then,
for any
{\bm{\mu}}\in{\bm{\delta}}-(\min_{i}d_{i,1}-1,\dots,\min_{i}d_{i,r}-1)+{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
,

{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}({\bm{\mu}})={\mathrm{HF}}_{B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})}({\bm{\mu}})={\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})}({\bm{\mu}})=\kappa.

Lemma 3.16
.

if
{\bm{\mu}}\in{\bm{\delta}}-(\min_{i}d_{i,1}-1,\dots,\min_{i}d_{i,r}-1)+{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
, then
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\left(\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}\right)
.

Proof.

We show that
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\Gamma_{1}
, the proof for
\Gamma_{0}
is the same. By the definition, one can rewrite
\Gamma_{1}
as

\Gamma_{1}=\cup_{\alpha,\lambda}\Gamma_{1}^{\alpha,\lambda}
,
where
\Gamma_{1}^{\alpha,\lambda}=\sum_{j\in\lambda}{\bm{d}}_{j}+Q_{\alpha}
. Fix
\alpha,\lambda

and assume
i\in\alpha
. As
\sharp\lambda=n(\alpha)+1\leq n-1
, the
i
-th entry of every element in
\Gamma_{1}^{\alpha,\lambda}
is at most
{\bm{\delta}}-d_{j,i}

for all
j\notin\lambda
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.15
.

This proof follows along the same lines as the one of Lemma
2.7
.
We first prove that
{\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}({\bm{\mu}})={\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})}({\bm{\mu}})
in the claimed region. For that purpose, consider the spectral sequences associated with the Čech-Koszul double complex
{C}^{\bullet}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}({K}_{\bullet}({\bm{f}},R))
. As
I({\bm{f}})
defines finitely many points, if we start taking homologies horizontally the second page of the spectral sequence is of the form

\begin{matrix}{\ast}&\cdots\ast&H_{2}(K_{\bullet})&H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(H_{1}(K_{\bullet}))&I({\bm{f}})^{\sat}/I({\bm{f}})\\
0&\cdots 0&0&H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(H_{1}(K_{\bullet}))&H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(B({\bm{f}}))\\
0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
\end{matrix}

where
K_{\bullet}
stands for the Koszul complex
K_{\bullet}({\bm{f}},R)
; recall that
H_{0}(K_{\bullet})=B({\bm{f}})
,
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B({\bm{f}}))=I({\bm{f}})^{\sat}/I({\bm{f}})
and

. On the other hand, the other spectral sequence is the same as in the proof of Theorem

. Now, by definition, if
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\Gamma_{0}
then
H^{1}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(B({\bm{f}})^{\sat})_{{\bm{\mu}}}\simeq H^{n+1}_{\mathfrak{b}}(K_{n}({\bm{f}},R))_{{\bm{\mu}}}
and setting

\displaystyle\Supp(H^{n+1}_{\mathfrak{b}}(K_{n}({\bm{f}},R)))

\displaystyle=\Supp(H^{n+1}_{\mathfrak{b}}(\oplus_{i=0}^{n}R(-\bar{{\bm{d}}}+{\bm{d}}_{i})))

\displaystyle=\Supp(\oplus_{i=0}^{n}H^{n+1}_{\mathfrak{b}}(R)(-\bar{{\bm{d}}}+{\bm{d}}_{i}))

\displaystyle=\cup_{i=0}^{n}({\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{d}}_{i}-{\mathbb{N}}^{r}),

where the last equality follows from Example
3.7
. By Lemma
3.16
,
{\bm{\mu}}\notin\Gamma_{0}
and

. By the multigraded Grothendieck-Serre formula

, Proposition 4.27]

{\mathrm{HP}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}({\bm{\mu}})={\mathrm{HF}}_{B({\bm{f}})^{\sat}}({\bm{\mu}})+\sum_{i\geq 1}(-1)^{i}\dim H^{i}_{{\mathfrak{b}}}(R/I({\bm{f}}))_{\bm{\mu}}.

Since

and

for

Now, we turn to the proof of

for all

in the claimed region. As a consequence of the Grothendieck-Serre formula again, this latter equality holds for all

and

and

∎

4.
Multigraded Sylvester forms

In this section, we introduce elements in
I^{\sat}
that we call multigraded Sylvester forms. In the generic setting, these forms yield an explicit duality similar to the one described in Section
2.10
. We begin with the construction of the
twisted Jacobian determinant
. We use Notation
3.1
and we emphasize that in the generic setting the base ring
k
is an arbitrary commutative ring.

4.1.
Multigraded twisted Jacobian

As proved in Section
3
,
\left(I^{\sat}/I\right)_{\bm{\delta}}
is a free
A_{k}
-module of rank one. A first natural task is to get an explicit generator of this module. Such a generator already appeared in the literature, notably in
[
CCD97
]
and
[
CDS98
]
under the name of
toric Jacobian
in the more general setting of toric geometry, and in
[
Chk88
]
, where a more algebraic treatment is proposed in the multiprojective setting. What follows in this section is strongly inspired by the construction given in
[
Chk88
, Chapter III]
; we provide proofs, with slight modifications, for the sake of accessibility and completeness.

For all
i=0,\ldots,n
, we first decompose the generic polynomials
F_{i}
with respect to the variables
{\bm{x}}_{1}
as follows

(4.1)

F_{i}=x_{1,0}F_{i,0}^{(1)}+x_{1,1}F_{i,1}^{(1)}+\cdots+x_{1,n_{1}}F_{i,n_{1}}^{(1)}.

There are many choices for such a decomposition, and we take one of them. Notice that a possible constraint to uniquely determine this decomposition is, for instance, to impose the conditions

(4.2)

F_{i,l}^{(1)}\in A[x_{1,l},\ldots,x_{1,n_{1}}][{\bm{x}}_{2},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}],\ l=0,\ldots,n_{1}.

Then, in a similar way, we decompose
F_{i,n_{1}}^{(1)}
with respect to
{\bm{x}}_{2}
:

(4.3)

F_{i,n_{1}}^{(1)}=x_{2,0}F_{i,0}^{(2)}+x_{2,1}F_{i,1}^{(2)}+\cdots+x_{2,n_{2}}F_{i,n_{2}}^{(2)}.

Again, there are many choices for this decomposition and we take one of them, but we can also impose, for instance, the conditions

(4.4)

F_{i,l}^{(2)}\in A[x_{1,n_{1}}][x_{2,l},\ldots,x_{2,n_{2}}][{\bm{x}}_{3},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}],\ l=0,\ldots,n_{2}.

We continue this process similarly until we decompose
F_{i,n_{r-1}}^{(n_{r-1})}
with respect to
{\bm{x}}_{r}
. In the end, each polynomial
F_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,n
, is decomposed as follows

(4.5)

F_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{1}-1}x_{1,j}F_{i,j}^{(1)}+x_{1,n_{1}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{2}-1}x_{2,j}F_{i,j}^{(2)}+x_{2,n_{2}}\left(\cdots+x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{r}}x_{r,j}F_{i,j}^{(r)}\right)\right)\right).

Now, we define
{\mathcal{D}}
as the determinant of the following
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix:

(4.6)

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}F_{0,0}^{(1)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{1}-1}^{(1)}&F_{0,0}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{2}-1}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{r-1}-1}^{(r-1)}&F_{0,0}^{(r)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{r}}^{(r)}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
F_{n,0}^{(1)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{1}-1}^{(1)}&F_{n,0}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{2}-1}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{r-1}-1}^{(r-1)}&F_{n,0}^{(r)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{r}}^{(r)}\end{array}\right).

From its definition,
{\mathcal{D}}
is multihomogeneous in the sets of variables
{\bm{x}}_{i}
. More precisely, a straightforward counting shows that

(4.7)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{deg}}_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}-\sum_{j=i+1}^{r}n_{j},\ i=1,\ldots,r,

where we recall that
{\bm{\delta}}
is defined by (
3.1
). As a matter of fact,
{\mathcal{D}}
is a linear form with respect to the coefficients of each polynomial
F_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,n
. In addition, another property that follows directly by definition is that
{\mathcal{D}}
belongs to the ideal
I^{\sat}\subset C
. Notice that the order of variables and polynomials plays an important role in the above construction, as well as the choice of decompositions.

Definition 4.2
.

After a choice of ordering for polynomials and variables, and a choice of decompositions, one defines a
twisted Jacobian

\Lambda
of the
F_{i}
’s by

\Lambda=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}x_{i,n_{i}}^{v_{i}}\right){\mathcal{D}},

where
v_{i}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{r}n_{j}
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
.
The one uniquely determined by the choices as detailed by (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), is denoted by
\Lambda_{0}
and is called the
twisted Jacobian
.

From the above definition, we deduce that
\Lambda
is a multihomogeneous polynomial of degree
{\bm{\delta}}
and that it is a linear form with respect to the coefficients of each polynomial
F_{i}
. Although
\Lambda
is not unique, the following result shows that it is essentially unique modulo the ideal
I
.

Proposition 4.3

(
[
Chk88
, Theorem III.1.5]
)
.

The class of
\Lambda
in
B=C/I
, denoted
\Delta
, is a generator of
(I^{\sat}/I)_{\bm{\delta}}
which is a free
A_{k}
-module of rank 1. In particular,
\Delta
is independent of the order of variables and polynomials and of the choice of the decompositions (
4.5
) used for
\Lambda
, up to multiplication by an invertible element in
k
.

This implies the following. Choose any order of variables and polynomials. Make the unique decomposition as in the definition of
\Lambda_{0}
, but following the corresponding orders in place of the one above. Then
\Delta=\pm\Delta_{0}
(the class of
\Lambda_{0}
). Indeed, any of these decompositions is defined for
k={\mathbb{Z}}
, in which case only
\pm 1
are invertible.

To prove Proposition
4.3
we will need the following property.

Lemma 4.4

(
[
Chk88
, Lemma III.1.6]
)
.

Let
P
be a multihomogeneous polynomial in
I^{\sat}
of degree
{\bm{\delta}}
such that the class of
P
in
B
is nonzero. Then,
P
depends on all the coefficients of all the polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
.

Proof.

As
I
is independent on the order of polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
, it is sufficient to prove the claim for the coefficients of
F_{0}
. Suppose that there exist
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}
, with
|{\bm{\alpha}}_{j}|=d_{0,j}
, such that
P
does not depend on the coefficient
U_{0,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
of the polynomial
F_{0}
. In order to emphasize this coefficient we rewrite
F_{0}
as

F_{0}=U_{0,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}+\tilde{F_{0}}.

As
P\in I^{\sat}
, there exist
{\bm{\beta}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\beta}}_{r}
and polynomials
G_{0},\ldots,G_{n}
such that

(4.8)

{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{1}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{2}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{r}}P=G_{0}F_{0}+G_{1}F_{1}+\cdots+G_{n}F_{n}\in I.

Now, in the localized ring
C_{{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}}
we substitute the coefficient
U_{0,{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
by the element
-{\bm{x}}_{1}^{-{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{-{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{-{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\tilde{F_{0}}
in (
4.8
) and we deduce that

{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{1}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{2}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{r}}P\in(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})\subset C_{{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}}.

It follows that there exist
{\bm{\gamma}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\gamma}}_{r}
such that

{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\gamma}}_{1}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{{\bm{\gamma}}_{2}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\gamma}}_{r}}P\in(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})\subset C.

From here, using Lemma
3.2
and Remark
3.3
, we deduce that
P
belongs to
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})^{\sat}
. In addition,
P
is of degree
{\bm{\delta}}
and since

\delta_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}d_{i,j}-(n_{j}+1)>\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i,j}-(n_{j}+1)

for all
j=1,\ldots,r
, Corollary
3.13
implies that
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})^{\sat}_{\bm{\delta}}=(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})_{\bm{\delta}}
. Therefore, we get a contradiction with the fact that the class of
P
in
B
is nonzero.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

We first prove that
\Delta\neq 0
. We proceed by induction on
n=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}
(recall that
n_{j}\geq 1
for all
j
).

If
n=1
then
r=1
,
n_{1}=1
and hence we are dealing with two single graded homogeneous polynomials in two variables. The claimed result hence follows from the properties of Sylvester forms in the single graded setting; see Section
2.10
.

Assume
n>1
. If
\Delta=0
then there exist multihomogeneous polynomials
G_{0},\ldots,G_{n}
in
C
such that

(4.9)

\Lambda=x_{1,n_{1}}^{v_{1}}x_{2,n_{2}}^{v_{2}}\ldots x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{v_{r-1}}{\mathcal{D}}=G_{0}F_{0}+G_{1}F_{1}+\cdots+G_{n}F_{n}.

By the construction of the determinant
{\mathcal{D}}
, there exist multihomogeneous polynomials
D_{0},\ldots,D_{n}
in
C
such that

(4.10)

x_{1,n_{1}}x_{2,n_{2}}\ldots,x_{r,n_{r}}{\mathcal{D}}=D_{0}F_{0}+D_{1}F_{1}+\cdots+D_{n}F_{n}.

Indeed, multiplying the last column of (
4.6
) by
x_{1,n_{1}}x_{2,n_{2}}\ldots,x_{r,n_{r}}
gives a matrix whose determinant is equal to (
4.10
). But by definition, one can add suitable multiples of the other columns of this matrix to the last one so that this last column is composed, from top to bottom, of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
. Thus, by developing this determinant with respect to the last column we get the claimed formula; for instance
D_{n}
is nothing but the determinant of the top left
(n\times n)
-minor of (
4.6
).

Combining (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) we get that
\sum_{i=0}^{n}H_{i}F_{i}=0
where for all
i=0,\ldots,n,

H_{i}=x_{1,n_{1}}^{v_{1}-1}x_{2,n_{2}}^{v_{2}-1}\ldots x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{v_{r-1}-1}D_{i}-x_{r,n_{r}}G_{i}\in C.

The polynomial
H_{i}
is of degree

(\delta_{1}-d_{i,1},\delta_{2}-d_{i,2},\ldots,\delta_{r-1}-d_{i,r-1},\delta_{r}-d_{i,r}+1)={\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{d}}_{i}+(0,\ldots,0,1).

We deduce that
(H_{0},\ldots,H_{n})
belongs to the first syzygy module
{\mathrm{Syz}}(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})
of the polynomials
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
; taking grading into account,
{\mathrm{Syz}}(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})\subset\oplus_{i=0}^{n}C(-d_{i})
and
(H_{0},\ldots,H_{n})
is a syzygy of degree
{\bm{\delta}}+(0,\ldots,0,1)
. By Corollary

H_{n}=x_{1,n_{1}}^{v_{1}-1}x_{2,n_{2}}^{v_{2}-1}\ldots x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{v_{r-1}-1}D_{n}-x_{r,n_{r}}G_{n}\in(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n-1})

Consider the specialization that sends

to
0
. Introducing the notation

for all

On the other hand,

is equal to the determinant

in the set of variables

and

Therefore, if

If

\bar{F}_{i}=x_{r,0}^{d_{i,r}}F^{\flat}_{i}({\bm{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{r-1})

\bar{D}_{n}=x_{r,0}^{(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d_{i,r})-1}{\mathcal{D}}(F^{\flat}_{0},\ldots,F^{\flat}_{n-1}),

(4.13)

x_{1,n_{1}}^{n_{2}+\ldots+n_{r-1}}\ldots x_{r-2,n_{r-2}}^{n_{r-1}}x_{r,0}^{(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}d_{i,r})-1}{\mathcal{D}}(F^{\flat}_{0},\ldots,F^{\flat}_{n-1})\in(x_{r,0}^{d_{0,r}}F^{\flat}_{0},\ldots,x_{r,0}^{d_{n-1,r}}F^{\flat}_{n-1}).

But by definition

.

Now that we have proved that

, we aim to show that it is a generator of

. As the commutative ring

Let

. By its definition from the determinant (

. Since

, but in view of Lemma

. Therefore, we deduce that

.

Let

by means of a determinant, the class of

Before closing this section, we explain why the determinant
\Lambda
is called a twisted Jacobian determinant. Partial derivatives and the Euler formula can be used to get decompositions similar to the ones used to define the determinant
{\mathcal{D}}
. Indeed, pick an integer
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, by the Euler formula

d_{i,1}F_{i}=x_{1,0}\frac{\partial{F_{i}}}{\partial x_{1,0}}+x_{1,1}\frac{\partial{F_{i}}}{\partial x_{1,1}}+\cdots+x_{1,n_{1}}\frac{\partial{F_{i}}}{\partial x_{1,n_{1}}},

which provides a decomposition similar to (
4.1
) with the difference that we need to multiply
F_{i}
by
d_{i,1}
. Applying Euler formula to
\frac{\partial{F_{i}}}{\partial x_{1,n_{1}}}
with respect to the variables
{\bm{x}}_{2}
implies

d_{i,2}\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{1,n_{1}}}=x_{2,0}\frac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial x_{1,n_{1}}\partial x_{2,0}}+x_{2,1}\frac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial x_{1,n_{1}}\partial x_{2,1}}+\cdots+x_{2,n_{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}F_{i}}{\partial x_{1,n_{1}}\partial x_{2,n_{2}}},

which is very similar to (
4.3
). Continuing this way one can build a determinant similar to
{\mathcal{D}}
where the entries are replaced by partial derivatives. We denote by
{\mathcal{J}}(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})
this Jacobian determinant. Comparing the degrees, we see that
{\mathcal{J}}
and
{\mathcal{D}}
have the same degree with respect to each set of variables
{\bm{x}}_{i}
and each set of coefficients of any polynomial
F_{j}
. Thus, denoting by
J(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})
the class of

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}x_{i,n_{i}}^{v_{i}}\right){\mathcal{J}}(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})

in
(B_{k})_{\bm{\delta}}
,
J
and
\Delta
are expected to differ by a multiplicative element in
k
. Actually, one can show that
[
Chk88
, Proposition III.2.6]

J(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})=\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq n\\
1\leq k\leq r\end{subarray}}d_{i,j}\right)\Delta_{0}(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})

in
(B_{\mathbb{Z}})_{\bm{\delta}}\simeq A_{\mathbb{Z}}
. So, the twisted Jacobian
\Delta_{0}
provides a generator of
H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B_{k})_{\bm{\delta}}
for any commutative ring
k
, whereas the Jacobian
J
is sensitive to finite characteristic settings.

4.5.
Multigraded Sylvester forms

In this section, we introduce multigraded Sylvester forms which are generalizations of the twisted Jacobian determinant. These forms provide additional nonzero elements in
I^{\sat}/I
of degree lower than
{\bm{\delta}}
(component-wise) and generate some graded components under suitable assumptions.

For all
j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, choose a multi-index of non negative integers
{\bm{\alpha}}_{j}=(\alpha^{(j)}_{0},\ldots,\alpha^{(j)}_{n_{j}})
such that

|{\bm{\alpha}}_{j}|=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha^{(j)}_{i}<\min_{i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}}d_{i,j}.

Under these assumptions one can always decompose each polynomial
F_{i}
as follows:

(4.14)

F_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{1}-1}x_{1,j}^{\alpha^{(1)}_{j}+1}F_{i,j}^{(1)}+x_{1,n_{1}}^{\alpha^{(1)}_{n_{1}}+1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{2}-1}x_{2,j}^{\alpha^{(2)}_{j}+1}F_{i,j}^{(2)}\right.\\
\left.+x_{2,n_{2}}^{\alpha^{(2)}_{n_{2}}+1}\left(\cdots+x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{\alpha^{(r-1)}_{n_{r-1}}+1}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n_{r}}x_{r,j}^{\alpha^{(r)}_{j}+1}F_{i,j}^{(r)}\right)\right)\right)

where
F_{i,j}^{(l)}
are multihomogeneous polynomials.
Define
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
as the determinant of the following matrix:

(4.15)

\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}F_{0,0}^{(1)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{1}-1}^{(1)}&F_{0,0}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{2}-1}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{r-1}-1}^{(r-1)}&F_{0,0}^{(r)}&\cdots&F_{0,n_{r}}^{(r)}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
F_{n,0}^{(1)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{1}-1}^{(1)}&F_{n,0}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{2}-1}^{(2)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{r-1}-1}^{(r-1)}&F_{n,0}^{(r)}&\cdots&F_{n,n_{r}}^{(r)}\end{array}\right).

From its definition, it is straightforward to check that
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
is a multihomogeneous polynomial. For all
i=0,\ldots,r-1
,

{\mathrm{deg}}_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}({\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}})=\delta_{i}-|{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}|-(\alpha^{(i)}_{n_{i}}+1)(n_{i+1}+\cdots+n_{r})=\delta_{i}-|{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}|-(\alpha^{(i)}_{n_{i}}+1)v_{i},

and

{\mathrm{deg}}_{{\bm{x}}_{r}}({\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}})=\delta_{r}-|{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}|.

Observe that
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
is a linear form in the coefficients of each polynomial
F_{i}
. In addition, it is immediate to verify that
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
belongs to
I^{\sat}
(as a consequence of Lemma
3.2
, it is sufficient to check that
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
multiplied by a certain monomial is in
I
).

Definition 4.6
.

After a choice of ordering for polynomials and variables, and a choice of decomposition, the Sylvester form of degree
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}
is defined as

{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}:=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}x_{i,n_{i}}^{(\alpha^{(i)}_{n_{i}}+1)v_{i}}\right){\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\in A_{k}[{\bm{x}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}].

The class of
{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
in
B=C/I
is denoted by
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
and we have

{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\in(I^{\sat}/I)_{(\delta_{1}-|{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}|,\dots,\delta_{r}-|{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}|)}.

Observe that this definition generalizes Definition
4.2
since
{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}
, respectively
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}
, is nothing but the twisted Jacobian
\Lambda
, respectively
\Delta
. The next results aim to generalize Proposition
4.3
that shows that
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}
is a generator of the free
A_{k}
-module
(I^{\sat}/I)_{\bm{\delta}}
of rank one.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}
, and
{\bm{\beta}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\beta}}_{r}
be multi-indices of non negative integers such that
|{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}|=|{\bm{\beta}}_{i}|
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
. Then

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{r}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{{\bm{\beta}}_{i}}\right){\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}=\begin{cases}0\textrm{ if }({\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r})\neq({\bm{\beta}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\beta}}_{r}),\\
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}}}\ \ \mathrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We begin with some observations about the determinant
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
defined by (
4.15
). Multiply the first column of (
4.15
) by

and add suitable multiples of the other columns according to the decomposition (
4.14
), the first column would become the column vector of

. Therefore

x_{1,0}^{\alpha_{0}^{(1)+1}}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\in I.

With the same argument,

\displaystyle x_{1,j}^{\alpha_{j}^{(1)}+1}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}

\displaystyle\in I,\ j=0,\ldots,n_{1}-1,

\displaystyle x_{1,n_{1}}^{\alpha_{n_{1}}^{(1)}+1}x_{2,j}^{\alpha_{j}^{(2)}+1}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}

\displaystyle\in I,\ j=0,\ldots,n_{2}-1,

\displaystyle x_{1,n_{1}}^{\alpha_{n_{1}}^{(1)}+1}\ldots x_{r-2,n_{r-2}}^{\alpha_{n_{r-2}}^{(r-2)}+1}x_{r-1,j}^{\alpha_{j}^{(r-1)}+1}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}

\displaystyle\in I,\ j=0,\ldots,n_{r-1}-1,

(4.16)

\displaystyle x_{1,n_{1}}^{\alpha_{n_{1}}^{(1)}+1}\ldots x_{r-2,n_{r-2}}^{\alpha_{n_{r-2}}^{(r-2)}+1}x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{\alpha_{n_{r-1}}^{(r-1)}+1}x_{r,j}^{\alpha_{j}^{(r)}+1}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}

By Definition

where

is a positive integer for all

. Using (

), we deduce that if

from some

In addition, since the class

To conclude the proof, it remains to show that

{\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}={\mathrm{sylv}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}.

{\mathcal{D}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}={\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}x_{1,n_{1}}^{v_{1}\alpha_{n_{1}}^{(1)}}{\bm{x}}_{2}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{2}}x_{2,n_{2}}^{v_{2}\alpha_{n_{2}}^{(2)}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r-1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r-1}}x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{v_{r-1}\alpha_{n_{r-1}}^{(r-1)}}{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}.

By Definition (

\displaystyle{\mathrm{sylv}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}

\displaystyle=x_{1,n_{1}}^{v_{1}}\ldots x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{v_{r-1}}{\mathcal{D}}_{({\bm{0}},\ldots,{\bm{0}})}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}x_{1,n_{1}}^{v_{1}\alpha_{n_{1}}^{(1)}+v_{1}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r-1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r-1}}x_{r-1,n_{r-1}}^{v_{r-1}\alpha_{n_{r-1}}^{(r-1)}+v_{r-1}}{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}},

as claimed.
∎

Therefore, by Theorem
3.10

{\check{C}}_{{\bm{\mu}}}\simeq\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(C_{{\bm{\mu}}},H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B)_{{\bm{\delta}}})

and an
A
-basis of
\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(C_{{\bm{\mu}}},H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B)_{{\bm{\delta}}})
is given by
\phi\circ({\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}})^{\check{}}
,
|{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}|=\mu_{1},\ldots,|{\bm{\alpha}}|=\mu_{r}
. By using Proposition
4.7
, it turns out that the map
\phi\circ({\bm{x}}_{1}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1}}\ldots{\bm{x}}_{r}^{{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}})^{\check{}}
is nothing but the multiplication map by
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
.

Now, by the argument using spectral sequences, we have an isomorphism of
A
-modules:

H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B)_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(C_{{\bm{\mu}}},H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B)_{{\bm{\delta}}}).

The canonical map that sends
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}\in H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B)_{{\bm{\delta}}-{\bm{\mu}}}
to the multiplication map by
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
in
\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(C_{{\bm{\mu}}},H^{0}_{\mathfrak{b}}(B)_{{\bm{\delta}}})
realizes the above isomorphism. ∎

Remark 4.10
.

As a consequence of the above theorem, the classes of Sylvester forms
{\mathrm{sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
are independent of the choice of decompositions (
4.14
), up to multiplication by an invertible element in
k
, that is independent of
{{\bm{\alpha}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\alpha}}_{r}}
. This element is
\pm 1
if one restricts to the unique decompositions as in the beginning of this section, varying orders of variables and forms (more generally to any decomposition that lifts to the base ring
{\mathbb{Z}}
).

5.
Application to Multigraded Elimination Matrices

In this section, the results obtained in Section
3
and Section
4
are applied to build a family of elimination matrices for multihomogeneous polynomial systems.

5.1.
Hybrid elimination matrices

Adopt Notation
3.1
and define a family of matrices indexed by
{\bm{\nu}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
as follows. First, for all
{\bm{\nu}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
such that

(5.1)

{\bm{\nu}}\notin({\bm{\delta}}-{\mathbb{N}}^{r})\cup\Gamma_{0}\cup\Gamma_{1}

we define the matrix
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}
as the matrix of the
A
-linear map

(5.2)

\displaystyle\oplus_{i=0}^{n}C_{{\bm{\nu}}-{\bm{d}}_{i}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\nu}}

\displaystyle(G_{0},\ldots,G_{n})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}G_{i}F_{i}

in canonical bases. This matrix is the classical multigraded Macaulay-type matrix of
F_{0},\ldots,F_{n}
in degree
{\bm{\nu}}
. Second, for all

{\bm{\nu}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
such that

(5.3)

{\bm{\nu}}={\bm{\delta}}-(\mu_{1},\ldots\mu_{r})\textrm{ such that }0\leq\mu_{j}<\min_{i}d_{i,j}\textrm{ for all }j=1,\ldots,r,

we define the matrix
{\mathbb{H}}_{\bm{\nu}}
as the matrix of the
A
-linear map

(5.4)

\displaystyle\oplus_{i=0}^{n}C_{{\bm{\nu}}-d_{i}}\oplus_{{\bm{\alpha}}:|\alpha_{j}|=\delta_{j}-\nu_{j}}A

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle C_{\bm{\nu}}

\displaystyle(G_{0},\ldots,G_{n},\ldots,\ell_{\bm{\alpha}},\ldots)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}G_{i}F_{i}+\sum_{{\bm{\alpha}}:|\alpha_{j}|=\delta_{j}-\nu_{j}}\ell_{\bm{\alpha}}{\mathrm{Sylv}}_{{\bm{\alpha}}}

in canonical bases. This matrix in an hybrid matrix: it has a Macaulay-type block and another block built from multigraded Sylvester forms of degree
{\bm{\nu}}
.

We notice that the smallest matrix in the entire family made of the matrices
{\mathbb{H}}_{\bm{\nu}}
and
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}
we have just defined is obtained for
{\bm{\nu}}={\bm{\delta}}-(\min_{i}d_{i,1}-1,\ldots,\min_{i}d_{i,r}-1)
.

Given a polynomial system
{\bm{f}}:=\{f_{0},\dots f_{n}\}
of
n+1
multihomogeneous polynomials of degree
{\bm{d}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{d}}_{n+1}
in
R=\mathbb{K}[{\bm{x}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]
, we recall that the notation
I({\bm{f}})
,
B({\bm{f}})
,
B^{\sat}({\bm{f}})
and
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}({\bm{f}})
respectively, stand for the specialization of
I,B,B^{\sat}
and
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}
, respectively.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
{\bm{\nu}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{r}
satisfies (
5.1
), respectively (
5.3
), then
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}
, respectively
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}
, is a presentation matrix of the
A
-module
(B^{\sat})_{\bm{\nu}}
.
In particular,
{\mathbb{M}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
, respectively
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
, is surjective if and only if
{\bm{f}}
has no common zeroes in
{\mathbb{P}}_{\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{P}}_{\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}}^{n_{r}}

Proof.

If
{\bm{\nu}}
satisfies (
5.1
) this result follows from Theorem
3.10
and if
{\bm{\nu}}
satisfies (
5.3
) it follows from Theorem
4.9
.
∎

5.3.
The drop-of-rank property

As already mentioned, a key feature of elimination matrices for solving polynomial systems with coefficients over a field is to have the drop-of-rank property. It turns out that the family of matrices
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}
defined above has this property.

To be more precise, consider a multigraded zero-dimensional polynomial system with coefficients in a field
\mathbb{K}
:
{\bm{f}}:=\{f_{0},\dots,f_{n}\}
are
n+1
multihomogeneous polynomials of degree
{\bm{d}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{d}}_{n+1}
in
R=\mathbb{K}[{\bm{x}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{r}]
. These polynomials are specialization of the
n+1
generic polynomials
F_{0},\dots,F_{n}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Assume
{\bm{f}}
defines a finite subscheme in
{\mathbb{P}}_{\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{P}}_{\bar{{\mathbb{K}}}}^{n_{r}}
of degree
\kappa
. If

(5.5)

{\bm{\nu}}\in{\bm{\delta}}-(\min_{i}d_{i,1}-1,\ldots,\min_{i}d_{i,r}-1)+{\mathbb{N}}^{r},

the corank of
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}({\bm{f}})
, or
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
depending on
{\bm{\nu}}
, is equal to
\kappa
.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{\nu}}
be a multi-index satisfying (
5.5
), by Lemma
3.16
,
{\bm{\nu}}
satisfies (
5.1
) or (
5.3
). Therefore, the claimed result follows straightforwardly from Proposition
3.15
as
{\mathbb{M}}_{\bm{\nu}}({\bm{f}})
, or
{\mathbb{H}}_{\nu}({\bm{f}})
depending on
{\bm{\nu}}
, yields a presentation matrix of the
{\mathbb{K}}
-vector space
(B^{\sat}({\bm{f}}))_{\bm{\nu}}
.
∎
