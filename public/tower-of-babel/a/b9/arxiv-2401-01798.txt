Micro-macro Parareal, from ordinary differential equations to stochastic differential equations and back again
Ignace Bossuyt, Stefan Vandewalle, Giovanni Samaey

Abstract
We are concerned with the micro-macro Parareal algorithm for the simulation of initial-value problems. In this algorithm, a coarse (fast) solver is applied sequentially over the time domain, and a fine (time-consuming) solver is applied as a corrector in parallel over smaller chunks of the time interval. Moreover, the coarse solver acts on a reduced state variable, which is coupled to the fine state variable through appropriate coupling operators. We first provide a contribution to the convergence analysis of the micro-macro Parareal method for multiscale linear ordinary differential equations (ODEs). Then, we extend a variant of the micro-macro Parareal algorithm for scalar stochastic differential equations (SDEs) to higher-dimensional SDEs. 2020 MSC codes: 65L11, 34E13, 65C30, 68Q10, 65C35, 60H35; Keywords: Parallel-in-time; Parareal; multiscale; McKean-Vlasov SDE; micro-macro; moment model; reduced model.



1
Introduction

Parallel-in-time methods aim to pararallelise the time-simulation of initial-value problems over the time domain.
In the Parareal algorithm, first proposed by
Lions et al. 2001

[
9
]
, a fine time-propagator (accurate but with long wall-clock time) is applied on small chuncks of the time domain in parallel. A correction is provided by a coarse time-propagator (fast but with reduced accuracy), that is applied sequentially over the complete time domain.

More specifically, let
u(t):\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a function of time
t
, let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a function, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a constant vector, then the Paraeal method applies to ODE problems of the form

du/dt=f(u)\qquad u(0)=\alpha.

(1)

on a time interval
[0,\,T]
.
The time domain is divided in
N
subintervals of equal length
\Delta t=T/N
, and the elements of the time grid are denoted with
t_{n}=n\Delta t
, for
0\leq n\leq N
.
Let
\mathcal{F}
be a fine solver
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, numerically evolving an approximate solution to equation (
1
) over a time chunk of time
\Delta t
, and similarly let
\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a coarse solver.

The Parareal iterate
u^{k}_{n}
with index
k
for iteration number
k\geq 0
and
n
for time index
0\leq n\leq N-1
, obeys this double recursion:

\begin{cases}u^{k}_{n+1}=\mathcal{C}(u^{k}_{n}),&\qquad k=0,\\
u^{k+1}_{n+1}=\mathcal{F}(u^{k}_{n})+\mathcal{C}(u^{k+1}_{n})-\mathcal{C}(u^{k}_{n}),&\qquad k>0.\end{cases}

(2)

Typically, a reference solution
u_{n}
is defined as the result of the sequential application of the fine solver:
u_{0}=\alpha
and
u_{n+1}=\mathcal{F}(u_{n})
for
0\leq n\leq N-1
.
The approximations by the Parareal algorithm then possesses a finite-termination property, namely
u^{k}_{n}=u_{n}
for
k\geq n
(see Theorem 3.1 and Remark 4.7 in
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
).
In practice, Parareal is also applicable for nonautonomous problems and nonuniform time grids.

The convergence of Parareal for linear scalar differential equations is given by the following theorem by
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
, corrolary 4.6 and theorem 4.9]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}(u)=fu
be a scalar fine propagator and
\mathcal{C}(u)=gu
be a scalar coarse propagator with
f,g\in\mathbb{R}
and
|g|<1
.
Then, the maximum error
E^{k}_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|u^{k}_{n}-u_{n}|
,
satisfies a superlinear bound

E^{k}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\left|f-g\right|^{k}\binom{N-1}{k}E^{0}_{\text{max}},

(3)

and a linear bound:

E^{k}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\left(\frac{\left|f-g\right|}{1-\left|g\right|}\right)^{k}E^{0}_{\text{max}}.

(4)

In micro-macro Parareal, which is a generalisation of Parareal, the coarse propagator does not act on the original state variable
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(micro state), but instead on a reduced version
U\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
(macro state).
These micro and macro states are coupled.
A restriction operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{r}
extracts macro information from a micro state.
A lifting operator
\mathcal{L}
provides a unique micro state
u
, consistent with a given macro state
U
. That is,
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}:u=\mathcal{L}(U)
such that
\mathcal{R}(u)=U
.

Blouza et al. 2010

[
2
]
proposed one possible micro-macro Parareal algorithm (see also
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
, Equation 3.21]
).
The micro initial condition for all iterations
k
is
u^{k}_{0}=u_{0}
and the macro initial condition equals
U^{k}_{0}=\mathcal{R}(u_{0})
.
In the zeroth iteration, one computes
U^{0}_{n}
and
u^{0}_{n}
sequentially for
n=1\ldots N
:

\displaystyle U_{n+1}^{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(U_{n}^{0}),

(5)

\displaystyle u_{n+1}^{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}(U_{n+1}^{0}),

In the subsequent iterations:

\displaystyle u_{n+1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(u_{n}^{k})+\mathcal{L}\left[\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}(u_{n}^{k+1}))-\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{R}(u_{n}^{k}))\right]

(6)

\displaystyle U^{k+1}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(u^{k+1}_{n+1})

Depending on the context, however, a lifting operator may involve costly computations.
In that case, a matching operator can be used instead.
A matching operator
\mathcal{M}
outputs a micro state
u
consistent with a given macro state
U
, based on prior information about the micro state
\hat{u}
, that is,
\mathcal{M}:(\mathbb{R}^{r},\,\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}:\mathcal{M}(U,\hat{u})=u
such that
\mathcal{R}(u)=U
.

Another micro-macro Parareal algorithm, using a matching operator, was proposed by
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
, Algorithm 2]
.
As in the previous algorithm, the micro initial condition for all iterations
k
is
u^{k}_{0}=u_{0}
and the macro initial condition equals
U^{k}_{0}=\mathcal{R}(u_{0})
.
It starts with (
5
), and replaces (
6
) by

\displaystyle U_{n+1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(U_{n}^{k+1})+\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{F}(u_{n}^{k}))-\mathcal{C}(U_{n}^{k})

(7)

\displaystyle u_{n+1}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(U_{n+1}^{k+1},\mathcal{F}(u_{n}^{k})).

When we refer to micro-macro Parareal in the rest of this text, we refer to this variant.

Remark 1

(Micro and macro error)
.

In the study of convergence properties of the micro-macro Parareal algorithm, one may be interested in the error on the micro variable
u_{n}^{k}
or in the error on the macro variable
U_{n}^{k}
.
We denote these errors
e^{u,k}=u_{n}^{k}-u_{n}
and
e^{U,k}:=U_{n}^{k}-U_{n}=U^{k}_{n}-\mathcal{R}(u^{k}_{n})
, respectively.
In practice, this choice is influenced by the context.
See also remark
2
.

Property 1
.

If
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{M}
are chosen such that, for any
U
and
u
,
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{M}(U,u))=U
, then the following properties of micro-macro Parareal (
5
)-(
7
) hold:

(i)

A micro-macro consistency property, that is
U^{k}_{n}=\mathcal{R}(u^{k}_{n})
,
\forall n,k\geq 0
(see
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
, last paragraph of section 3.2]
).

(ii)

If, in addition,
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{R}(u),u)=u
, then, a finite termination property holds:
u^{k}_{n}=u_{n}
for all
k\geq n
(see
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
, Theorem 12]
). From (i), it also holds that
U^{k}_{n}=U_{n}=\mathcal{R}(u_{n})
.

Under the same assumptions of property
1
, it holds that

(i)

e^{U,k}_{n}=\mathcal{R}(e^{u,k}_{n})
,
\forall n,k\geq 0
,

(ii)

e^{u,k}_{n}=0
and
e^{U,k}_{n}=0
for all
k\geq n
.

]
.

2
Convergence theory of Micro-macro Parareal for linear multiscale ODEs

In this section, we consider a two-dimensional
linear multiscale ODE

Test system and assumptions.

The ODE that we consider models the evolution of a state vector
u=(x,y)
where
x\in\mathbb{R}
is a slow variable and
y\in\mathbb{R}
is a fast variable.
It contains the parameters
\alpha
,
\beta,\delta\in\mathbb{R}
.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\begin{matrix}x\\
y\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&\delta\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}x\\
y\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}x(0)\\
y(0)\end{matrix}\right],=\left[\begin{matrix}x_{0}\\
y_{0}\end{matrix}\right].

(8)

Here, we assume that
\delta
is large compared to
\alpha
:
|\delta|\gg\alpha
such that the fast variable reaches its equilibrium much faster in time than the slow variable.
We assume that
\delta<0
and also that
\delta\neq\alpha
. This ensures that the fast variable decays to zero.
If one increases the absolute value of
\delta
, the numerical simulation of this system gets more expensive due to the stiffness in the
y
variable.

The (exact) solution to equation (
8
) equals
u(t+\Delta t)=\mathcal{A}(\Delta t)u(t)

where

\mathcal{A}(\Delta t)=\left[\begin{matrix}e^{\alpha\Delta t}&(e^{\delta\Delta t}-e^{\alpha\Delta t})\frac{\beta}{\delta-\alpha}\\
0&e^{\delta\Delta t}\end{matrix}\right].

(9)

This is a special case of the linear ODE considered in
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
]
, namely a two-dimensional case where the leftmost entry in the matrix
\mathcal{A}
equals zero.

Reduced model.

Now we build a one-dimensional reduced model, describing only the dynamics of a slow variable
U
(as an approximation to
x
).
To this aim, we introduce a modified decay rate
\bar{\alpha}\approx\alpha
.
The evolution of the reduced variable
U
is given as follows:

\frac{dU}{dt}=\bar{\alpha}U,\qquad\qquad U(0)=\bar{x}_{0}.

(10)

where
\bar{x}_{0}
is the initial condition of the reduced model. We denote the effect of a numerical time-stepper on equation (
10
) with a scalar
G\in\mathbb{R}
as
G\approx e^{\alpha\Delta t}
.

Definition 1

(Operators for micro-macro Parareal on linear multiscale ODEs)
.

Following
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
]
, we choose the micro variable
u=(x,\,y)
(corresponding to the multiscale ODE (
8
)) and the macro variable
U
(corresponding to the reduced model (
10
)). Further, we choose

•

a fine propagator
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{F}(u):=\mathcal{A}u
(see the stiff model (
8
)) and

•

a coarse propagator
\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{C}(U):=GU
(see the reduced model (
10
)),

•

a restriction operator

\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:\mathcal{R}((x,\,y)):=x
,

•

a matching operator

\mathcal{M}:(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{M}(U,(x,\,y)):=(U,\,y)

•

a lifting operator

\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{L}(U):=(U,\,0)
.

We divide the time-interval into
N
subintervals of equal length.

The coarse and fine solver are linear, as the ODE problem and its reduced model are linear.
The above choices suffer from the issue of initial slip, for more information see for instance
Van Kampen 1985

[
14
, Appendix A]
.
We postpone a discussion of this issue to section
2.3
, where we also give some numerical experiments.

Relation to existing work.

Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
]
study the convergence of micro-macro Parareal with respect to the time-scale separation between the fast and slow variable for a more general multiscale linear ODE than the ODE (
8
), i.e., with a bottom left term of the matrix
\mathcal{A}
possibly different from zero.
Their bound contains a constant which depends on the iteration number. That makes it difficult to predict the error of micro-macro Parareal
a priori
as a function of the iteration number.
In this work, we wish to obtain a convergence bound directly with respect to the iteration number.

2.1
A non-tight error bound

In this section, we first interpret micro-macro Parareal for the multiscale ODE as a special case of classical Parareal.
We then explain why it is not possible to readily apply the convergence theory from
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
, because the coarse and fine propagators do no commute.
Then we derive a bound on the error of micro-macro Parareal based on the generating functions methodology developed by
Gander et al. 2023

[
6
]
.

Preparatory considerations

Definition 2

(Introduction of symbols)
.

In order to make notation shorter, we introduce
F=e^{\alpha\Delta t}

and
b=(e^{\delta\Delta t}-e^{\alpha\Delta t})\frac{\beta}{\delta-\alpha}
.
We also define the matrix
\mathcal{B}
as follows:

\mathcal{B}=\left[\begin{matrix}G&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right].

(11)

We first present a lemma about the interpretation of the micro-macro Parareal iterates as iterates of classical Parareal, with proof in appendix
A
.

Lemma 2

(Interpretation of micro-macro Parareal as classical Parareal)
.

The use of micro-macro Parareal (
5
)-(
7
) with the operators from definition
1

is equivalent to the application of classical Parareal with a fine propagator
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{F}(u)=\mathcal{A}u
and a coarse propagator
\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{C}(u)=\mathcal{B}u
:

\displaystyle u^{0}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}u^{0}_{n}

(12)

\displaystyle u^{k+1}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})u^{k}_{n}+\mathcal{B}u^{k+1}_{n}.

Lemma 3

(Error equation for micro-macro Parareal iterates)
.

Consider micro-macro Parareal, defined in
equations (
5
)-(
7
), with coarse and fine time-stepping operators and coupling operators from definition
1
.
The error on the micro state
u^{k}_{n}
in iteration
k
and at time point
t_{n}
, namely
e^{u,k}_{n}=u^{k}_{n}-u_{n}
, satisfies for
0\leq n\leq N-1

\displaystyle e^{u,0}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=Bu^{0}_{n}-Au_{n}=B^{n}u_{0}-A^{n}u_{0}

(13)

\displaystyle e^{u,k+1}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})e^{u,k}_{n}+\mathcal{B}e^{u,k+1}_{n}.

Proof.

Substracting the reference solution
u_{n}=\mathcal{A}^{n}u_{0}
on both sides of equation (
12
) leads to (
13
).
∎

We denote the error on the slow variable with
e^{x,k}_{n}=x^{k}_{n}-x_{n}
and the error on the fast variable with
e^{y,k}_{n}=y^{k}_{n}-y_{n}
.

Remark 2
.

It is, in general, not possible to derive a closed form solution to equation for the evolution of the macro error.
Indeed, using the linearity of
\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle e^{U,k+1}_{n+1}=\mathcal{R}\left(e^{u,k+1}_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\left((\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})e^{u,k}_{n}+\mathcal{B}e^{u,k+1}_{n}\right)

(14)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\left((\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})e^{u,k}_{n}\right)+\mathcal{R}\left(\mathcal{B}e^{u,k+1}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}\left((\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})e^{u,k}_{n}\right)+Ge^{U,k+1}_{n}.

For our model problem,
\mathcal{R}\left((\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})e^{u,k}_{n}\right)\neq(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B})\mathcal{R}\left(e^{u,k}_{n}\right)
, thus it is not possible to write an expression for the evolution of the macro error in terms of the macro error alone.

Summary of derivation of convergence bounds from
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
.

A first approach to obtain an upper bound on the error
e^{u,k}_{n}
could be to generalise the analysis for a linear scalar test ODE used by
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
to multidimensional linear ODEs.
In this approach, we define the vector

\mathbf{e}^{u,k}:=\left[\begin{matrix}e^{u,k}_{1}&\ldots&e^{u,k}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]
.

Let us now define the matrices
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
as

M=\left[\begin{matrix}I&0&\ldots&0&0\\
-\mathcal{B}&I&\ldots&0&0\\
\ldots\\
0&-\mathcal{B}&\ldots&I&0\\
0&0&\ldots&-\mathcal{B}&I\\
\end{matrix}\right];\quad C=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\mathcal{A-B}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&\mathcal{A-B}&\ldots&0&0\\
\ldots&&&&\ldots\\
0&0&\ldots&\mathcal{A-B}&0\\
\end{matrix}\right]

(15)

Then,
\mathbf{e}^{u,k+1}
is related to
\mathbf{e}^{u,k}
through this equation:

\displaystyle M\mathbf{e}^{u,k+1}

\displaystyle=C\mathbf{e}^{u,k},\mathrm{thus}

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}^{u,k+1}

\displaystyle=M^{-1}C\mathbf{e}^{u,k}.

Lemma 4
.

If
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}
commute, then
the matrices
M^{-1}
and
C
commute.
Furthermore, let us define the matrices
H\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
:

H=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
I&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\mathcal{B}&I&\ldots&0&0\\
\ldots\\
\mathcal{B}^{N-2}&\mathcal{B}^{N-1}&\ldots&I&0\\
\end{matrix}\right];\quad D=\left[\begin{matrix}\mathcal{A-B}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&\mathcal{A-B}&\ldots&0&0\\
0&&\ldots&0&0\\
\ldots&&&&\ldots\\
0&0&\ldots&0&\mathcal{A-B}\\
\end{matrix}\right].

(17)

Then,
M^{-1}C=HD
.
If the matrices
M^{-1}
and
C
commute, then the matrices
H
and
D
also commute.

Proof.

See appendix
C
.
∎

If the matrices
M^{-1}
and
C
commute, then it is possible to write

e^{k}=(M^{-1}C)^{k}e^{0}=(HD)^{k}e^{0}=H^{k}D^{k}e^{0},

(18)

and thus

\left\|e^{k}\right\|\leq\left\|H^{k}\right\|\left\|D^{k}\right\|\left\|e^{0}\right\|.

(19)

The superlinear upper bound from
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
, given in lemma
1
, is obtained through bounding
\left\|H^{k}\right\|
and
\left\|C^{k}\right\|
.

If the matrices
M^{-1}
and
C
do not commute, it holds that

\left\|e^{k}\right\|\leq\left\|H\right\|^{k}\left\|D\right\|^{k}.

(20)

The linear bound in equation (
27
), as obtained by
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
, follows from bounding
\left\|H\right\|
and
\left\|D\right\|
.
In our test problem, the matrices
H
and
C
indeed do not commute, since the matrices
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
that appear as blocks in
M
and
C
, do not commute.

In conclusion, the superlinear bound requires commutativity of
H
and
D
, while a linear bound can be derived without this assumption. It is our aim, however, to derive an effective superlinear bound for our multiscale test problem.

Using generating functions.

An alternative idea is to use the generating function methodology used by
Gander et al. 2023

[
6
]
. One then proceeds as follows. From equation (
13
) it can be written that

\|e^{u,k+1}_{n+1}\|\leq\|\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}\|\|e^{u,k}_{n}\|+\|\mathcal{B}\|\|e^{u,k+1}_{n}\|

(21)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes a vector norm or the induced matrix norm. A proper matrix norm satisfies the submultiplicativity property
\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
for arbitrary matrices
A
,
B
.
Starting from equation (
21
), the generating function method delivers this bound on
\left\|e^{u,k}_{n}\right\|
, taken from
Gander et al. 2023

[
6
, equation 3.12]
:

\|e^{u,k}_{n}\|\leq\frac{\|\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}\|}{(k-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}(i+l)\left\|\mathcal{B}\right\|^{i}\max_{1\leq n\leq N}\left\|e^{u,0}_{n}\right\|.

(22)

A bound for
e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|x^{k}_{n}-x_{n}|
can be constructed by writing

e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|e^{x,k}_{n}|\leq\left\|e^{u,k}_{\mathrm{max}}\right\|
.
This bound is not tight, however, as is illustrated in Section
2.3
.

2.2
Exploiting the structure of the multiscale ODE to obtain tighter covergence bounds

In this section, we derive an upper bound for
e_{\mathrm{max}}^{x,k}
, which exploits the special structure of the linear multiscale ODE (
8
).
In this analysis, we first reformulate micro-macro Parareal for the multiscale ODE (
8
) as classical Parareal for a scalar nonhomogeneous ODE.
This allows us to extend the convergence analysis originally presented by
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
.
First we provide a lemma on the convergence of the (autonomous) fast variable.

Lemma 5

(Error of the fast variable)
.

Let
e^{y,k}_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|y^{k}_{n}-y_{n}|
be the maximum error (over all
0\leq n\leq N
) in the micro-macro Parareal approximation (equations (
5
)-(
7
)) of the fast variable, with the operators defined in definition
1
.
Then,

e^{y,k}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq e^{k\delta\Delta t}e^{y,0}_{\mathrm{max}}.

(23)

Proof.

From equation (
13
) it holds that

\displaystyle e^{y,k+1}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=y^{k+1}_{n}-y_{n+1}

(24)

\displaystyle=e^{\delta\Delta t}y^{k}_{n}-e^{\delta\Delta t}y_{n}

\displaystyle=e^{\delta\Delta t}e^{y,k}_{n}.

Algebraic proof.

The solution of the recursion (
24
) equals

e^{y,k}_{n}=\begin{cases}e^{k(\delta\Delta t)}e^{y,0}_{n-k}&\qquad n\geq k\\
0&\qquad n<k.\\
\end{cases}

(25)

Then taking the maximum over
n
leads to equation (
23
).

Equivalent proof in matrix notation.

Let
L\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be a matrix and let

\mathbf{e}^{y,k}=\left[\begin{matrix}e_{1}^{y,k}&\ldots&e_{N}^{y,k}\end{matrix}\right]^{T}
, then we can write

\mathbf{e}^{y,k+1}=\underbrace{\left[\begin{matrix}0&\ldots&\\
e^{\delta\Delta t}&0&&\\
\vdots&&&\\
&&&e^{\delta\Delta t}&0&\\
0&\ldots&&&e^{\delta\Delta t}&0\end{matrix}\right]}_{L}\mathbf{e}^{y,k}.

(26)

The solution of this recursion equals
e^{y,k}=L^{k}e^{y,0}
.
Taking the maximum norm on both sides leads to equation (
23
).
∎

The linear bound (
23
) for the error on the fast variable does not capture the property that after
N
iterations, it is equal to zero.

Lemma 6

(Linear and superlinear bound for the error in the slow variable)
.

Assume that
\alpha<0
and
\delta<0
in the multiscale ODE (
8
) and
|G|<1
in the reduced model (
11
).
Let
e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|x^{k}_{n}-x_{n}|
be the error in the micro-macro Parareal approximation (equations (
5
)-(
7
)) of the slow variable, with the operators defined in definition
1
.

Then,
e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}
satisfies a linear bound:

e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\left(\frac{\left|F-G\right|}{1-\left|G\right|}\right)^{k}e^{x,0}_{\mathrm{max}}+\frac{b}{1-\left|G\right|}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{|F-G|}{1-|G|}\right)^{i}e^{\delta(k-1-i)\Delta t}e^{y,0}_{\mathrm{max}}.

(27)

It also satisfies a superlinear bound

\displaystyle e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}

\displaystyle\leq\left|F-G\right|^{k}\binom{N-1}{k}e^{x,0}_{\mathrm{max}}

(28)

\displaystyle+b\frac{1-|G|^{N-1}}{1-|G|}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}|F-G|^{i}\binom{N-1}{i}e^{\delta(k-1-i)\Delta t}e^{y,0}_{\mathrm{max}}.

Proof.

Equation (
13
) can be expanded as

\left[\begin{matrix}e_{n+1}^{x,k+1}\\
e_{n+1}^{y,k+1}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}F&b\\
0&d\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}e_{n}^{x,k}\\
e_{n}^{y,k}\end{matrix}\right]-\left[\begin{matrix}G&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}e_{x,n}^{k}\\
e_{n}^{y,k}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}G&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}e_{n}^{x,k+1}\\
e_{n}^{y,k+1}\end{matrix}\right],

(29)

where
d=e^{\delta\Delta t}
.
Thus the error on the slow variable, defined as
e^{x,k}_{n}=x^{k}_{n}-x_{n}
, satisfies

e_{n+1}^{x,k+1}=(e^{\alpha\Delta t}-e^{\bar{\alpha}\Delta t})e_{n}^{x,k}+e^{\bar{\alpha}\Delta t}e_{n}^{x,k+1}+be_{n}^{y,k}.

(30)

Let us define the matrices
M_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
C_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

M_{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}I&\ldots&\\
-G&I&&\\
\vdots&&&\\
&&&-G&I&\\
0&\ldots&&&-G&I\end{matrix}\right];\qquad C_{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&\ldots&&0\\
(F-G)&0&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&\\
&&0&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&(F-G)&0\end{matrix}\right].

(31)

The matrices
M_{x}
and
C_{x}
in equation (
32
) are not the same as the matrices
M
and
C
in equation (
15
): the latter are block matrices containing the full state space, while the former only describe the slow variable (the evolution of the fast variable is manually added in equation (
32
)).
Here, the matrices
M_{x}
(or
M_{x}^{-1}
) and
C_{x}
commute.

We now collect all errors at iteration
k
at different time points
n=1\ldots N
in a vector, i.e.

\mathbf{e}^{x,(k)}=\left[\begin{matrix}e_{1}^{x,k}&\ldots&e_{N}^{x,k}\end{matrix}\right]^{T}

and

\mathbf{e}^{y,(k)}=\left[\begin{matrix}e_{1}^{y,k}&\ldots&e_{N}^{y,k}\end{matrix}\right]^{T}
.
Equation (
30
) can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle M_{x}\mathbf{e}^{x,(k+1)}

\displaystyle=C_{x}\mathbf{e}^{x,(k)}+be^{y,(k)},\,\mathrm{thus}

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}^{x,(k+1)}

\displaystyle=M_{x}^{-1}C_{x}\mathbf{\mathbf{}}e^{x,(k)}+bM^{-1}_{x}\mathbf{e}^{y,(k)}

We now define the matrices
H_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
D_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}

H_{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
I&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\ldots\\
G^{N-1}&\ldots&I&0&0\\
G^{N-2}&G^{N-1}&\ldots&I&0\\
\end{matrix}\right];\quad D_{x}=\left[\begin{matrix}F-G&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&F-G&\ldots&0&0\\
0&&\ldots&0&0\\
\ldots&&&&\ldots\\
0&0&\ldots&0&F-G\\
\end{matrix}\right].

(33)

Then, equation (
32
) can be rewritten as

\mathbf{e}^{x,(k+1)}=H_{x}D_{x}\mathbf{e}^{x,(k)}+bM^{-1}_{x}\mathbf{e}^{y,(k)}.

(34)

From lemma
5
(equation (
26
)), it holds that
e^{y,(k)}=L^{k}e^{y,(0)}
.
The solution to the recursion in equation (
32
) equals, according to lemma
8
(see appendix
B
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}^{x,(k)}=

\displaystyle[H_{x}D_{x}]^{k}\mathbf{e}^{(0)}+b\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}[H_{x}D_{x}]^{i}M^{-1}_{x}L^{k-1-i}\mathbf{e}^{y,(0)}.

(35)

This equation is an exact formula for
e^{x,(k)}
. Next, we derive an upper bound for
\max_{1\leq n\leq N}|e^{x,k}_{n}|=\left\|e^{x,(k)}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Since
F
and
G
are scalars,
M^{-1}_{x}
and
C_{x}
commute (and, from lemma
4
, so do
H_{x}
and
D_{x}
), we can proceed using a similar technique as the one used by
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
.

•

The linear bound in equation (
27
) is obtained by writing

\|H_{x}^{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{(1-|G|)^{k}}
and

\|C_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq(F-G)
and

\|M^{-1}_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{1-|G|}
(see
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
, proof of theorem 4.9]
).

•

The superlinear bound in equation (
28
) is obtained by writing

\|H^{k}_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\binom{N-1}{k}
and

\|C_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq(F-G)
and

\|M^{-1}_{x}\|_{\infty}=\frac{1-|G|^{N}}{1-|G|}

(see
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
, lemma 4.4]
).

∎

The linear and superlinear bounds (
27
) and (
28
) capture two effects.
For both of them, the first term equals the convergence bound from
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
, given in lemma
1
. It arises from a difference in
F
and
G
, for instance due to different time-steppers for the multiscale and averaged ODEs.
The second term captures the effect of the convergence of the fast variable on the convergence of the slow variable.
Each of the summands in the second term is a product of a dampening factor (determined by
F
and
G
) and the error on the fast variable at previous iterations.

In practice, the quantities
e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}
and
e^{y,k}_{\mathrm{max}}
are unknown
a priori
.

e^{x,k}_{\mathrm{max}}
is also unknown when the classical bounds from
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
are used.
For the linear ODE that we consider here, one can estimate

e^{y,k}_{\mathrm{max}}\leq|y_{0}|
.

Avoiding initial slip.

A proper choice of the initial condition

in equation (

[

is

\bar{x}_{0}=x_{0}-y_{0}\frac{\beta}{\delta-\alpha}.

This choice does affect the operators from definition

then the following classical Parareal iteration describes the effect the modified initial condition from equation (

):

\displaystyle u^{0}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{B}}u^{0}_{n}

\displaystyle u^{k+1}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{A}-\hat{\mathcal{B}})u^{k}_{n}+\hat{\mathcal{B}}u^{k+1}_{n}

.
The proof of this equality is similar to the proof of lemma
2
.

•

•

a coarse propagator

,

•

a restriction operator

\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{R}(u):=u
,

•

a matching operator

\mathcal{M}:(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{M}((X,Y),(x,y)):=(X,y)

a lifting operator

\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}:\mathcal{L}((x,y)):=(x,\,0)

3
Monte Carlo-moments Parareal: extension to multidimensional SDEs

In
[
4
]
, we proposed the Monte Carlo-moments (MC-moments) Parareal method, a micro-macro Parareal algorithm for the simulation of scalar McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equations.
In this section we extend that method to multidimensional SDEs.

3.1
McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equations

McKean-Vlasov SDEs model the dynamics over time
t
of an ensemble of
P
(coupled) particles
x^{(p)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p=1\ldots P
.
The system is defined for some initial distribution of particles
p_{0}
.
Each particle
x^{(p)}
is subject to noise from the Wiener process
W(t,\omega^{(p)}
) for noise realisations parametrised by
\omega^{(p)}
.
For notation, these particles are collected in the state variable

\bar{x}=\left(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(P)}\right)
.

We consider a special class of Mckean-Vlasov SDEs, where the mean-field interaction is the expected value of a function of the particles (for a more general treatment, see, e.g.,
Sznitman 1991

[
13
]
).
Let
\psi
be some function of the collective state variable, and
\Lambda(t)=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi(\bar{x}(t))]
, in which the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{P}
is taken over the particle ensemble
\bar{x}
, where each particle is one realisation of the stochastic process.
For instance, if
\psi
is the identity, then one is interested in just the mean of the particle ensemble.
Later we also use the operator
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}]
, which computes the covariance matrix of the ensemble
\bar{x}
.

This is the McKean-Vlasov SDE that we consider:

\displaystyle dx^{(p)}

\displaystyle=a\left(x^{(p)},\Lambda(t),t\right)dt+b\left(x^{(p)},\Lambda(t),t\right)dW^{(p)},

(38)

\displaystyle\bar{x}(0)

\displaystyle\sim p_{0},

in which the coefficient
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a drift coefficient, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}
is a diffusion coefficient.
We use the Itô interpretation of the SDE (
38
).

Assumptions.

We only consider SDEs whose particle distribution is unimodal at all times
t\geq 0
.
For more information about loosening these assumptions, see
Bossuyt et al. 2023

[
4
]
.

Simulation.

The Monte-Carlo simulation of these equations is computationally expensive because (i) for accurate results, a lot of particles (samples) are required, and (ii) in each time-step, the mean of the particles in
\Lambda(t)
needs to be computed.

3.2
Moment ODEs as approximation for multidimensional SDEs

In this section, we aim to obtain a cheap approximation to the McKean-Vlasov SDE in equation (
38
).

We first write a general multivariate Taylor series of the drift and diffusion coefficients for a particle
x^{(p)}
around the mean value
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}]
:

a(x^{(p)},\Lambda,t)\approx a(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}],\Lambda,t)+A_{1}(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}],\Lambda,t)(x^{(p)}-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}])

(39)

b(x^{(p)},\Lambda,t)\approx b(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}],\Lambda,t)+B_{1}(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}],\Lambda,t)(x^{(p)}-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}]).

(40)

Here,
A_{1}
and
B_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
are the Jacobian matrices of the drift and diffusion coefficients
a
and
b
.

This expansion, however, is not practically useful, because it assumes that
\Lambda
is available. A more practical expansion is constructed by approximating
\Lambda=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\psi(\bar{x})]
with
\Lambda_{\mathbb{E}_{P}}=\psi(\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}])
(the approximation consists in changing the position of the expected value operator).

We now define ODEs for describing the evolution the variables
M
and
\Sigma
, which aim to closely approximate the mean and the covariance matrix of the particle ensemble.
For background theory on these approximations for classical SDEs, see for instance
Rodriguez and Tuckwell 1996

[
12
]
and
Arnold 1974

[
1
]
.
The variable
M
approximates the mean
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle\frac{dM}{dt}

\displaystyle=a(M,\psi(M))+\frac{1}{2}Q\operatorname{Vec}(\Sigma),\qquad M(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}(0)],

(41)

where
Q
is a matrix whose
j
-th row contains a flattened Hessian matrix of the
j
-the component of
a
and where
\operatorname{Vec}
is the vectorisation (flattening) operator.
The evolution of an approximation
\Sigma
to the covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}[\bar{x}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
can be described with a matrix differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d\Sigma}{dt}

\displaystyle=A_{1}(M,\psi(M),t)\Sigma+\Sigma A_{1}(M,\psi(M),t)^{T}+B_{1}\Sigma B_{1}^{T}

(42)

\displaystyle+b(M,\psi(M),t)b(M,\psi(M),t)^{T}

\displaystyle\Sigma(0)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}(0)].

3.3
Coupling operators for multidmensional MC-moments Parareal

Definition 3

(MC-moments Parareal for SDEs of any dimension)
.

The micro variable
u
is an ensemble of particles
u=\bar{x}
, and the macro variable
U
contains its first moments (mean and covariance matrix)
U=[\mathbb{E}_{P}[u],\,\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[u]]
. We choose

•

a fine propagator
\mathcal{F}
: a Monte Carlo simulation of the SDE (
38
)

•

a coarse propagator
\mathcal{C}
: (a numerical simulation of) the moment model (
41
)-(
42
).

•

a restriction operator

\mathcal{R}(\bar{x}):=[\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}],\,\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}]]
.

•

a matching operator
\mathcal{M}
that takes as input a particle ensemble
\bar{x}
and a pair of desired mean
M
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
Let us assume that both
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}]
and
\Sigma
are positive semidefinite (PSD).
Now define the matrices
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
via the Cholesky decompositions
\Sigma=VV^{T}
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}]=QQ^{T}
.

–

If
Q
is invertible, then we define
A=VQ^{-1}
, and

\mathcal{M}([M,\,\Sigma],\bar{x}):=A(\bar{x}-\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}])+M

(43)

–

If
Q
is not invertible, then the particles
\bar{x}
are resampled from a standard normal distribution.

•

a lifting operator
\mathcal{L}
, used in the zeroth micro-macro Parareal iteration is choosen to be matching with respect to the initial condition of the SDE
\mathcal{L}(U):=\mathcal{M}(U,\bar{x}(0))
.

If the drift and diffusion coefficients are affine functions in their first two arguments
M
and
\Lambda=\phi(M)
, and if
\phi
is a linear function as well, then the moment equations (
41
)-(
42
) are an exact description of
\mathbb{E}_{P}[\bar{x}]
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}]
.
This fact indicates that the choice of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{C}
is sensible.

We now present a lemma that is important in the light of the exactness property of micro-macro Parareal (see property
1
).
This lemma is proven in appendix
D
.

Lemma 7

(Micro-macro consistency of the choosen matching operator)
.

Let
\bar{x}
be an ensemble of particles such that
\mathrm{Cov}_{P}[\bar{x}]
is symmetric positive definite.
Let
U=\left[\begin{matrix}M,\Sigma\end{matrix}\right]
be a macro variable, with
\Sigma
PSD.
Then the matching operator (
43
) satisfies
\mathcal{R}\left(\mathcal{M}(U,u)\right)=U
and
\mathcal{M}\left(\mathcal{R}(u),u\right)=u
.

4
Conclusion

In section
2
, we presented a new convergence bound for micro-macro Parareal for a special linear multiscale ODE.
While our work focuses on a specific case of the multiscale ODEs considered by
Legoll et al. 2013

[
8
]
, our bound is a generalisation of an existing bound for scalar ODEs from
Gander and Vandewalle 2007

[
5
]
.
It contains an extra term that decreases as the decay rate of the fast component in the ODE system increases.
In the future, it would be interesting te develop a convergence bound for a more general class of linear multiscale ODEs, where the bottom left element of
\mathcal{A}
does not need to be zero.
Also, as we considered only
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, generalizations to higher dimensions and to more general nonlinear ODEs would be interesting.

In section
3
, we extended a Parareal algorithm in which the fine solver is a Monte Carlo simulation of the SDE, and the coarse solver is an approximate ODE description of the first two statistical moments of the SDE.
In the future, it would be interesting to perform tests with the algorithm on more examples, such as true McKean-Vlasov SDEs, and to study the influence of the form of different SDEs on the convergence.
Another path would be to augment the MC-moments Parareal method with a way to exploit other reduced models, such as the normal forms described in
Roberts 2014

[
11
]
.

In summary, we first studied micro-macro Parareal for ODEs, and then we visited a Parareal method for SDEs that exploits ODE descriptions.
This journey brought us first to ODEs, and via SDEs we returned to ODEs.
