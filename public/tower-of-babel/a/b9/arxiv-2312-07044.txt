Large Foundation Models for Power Systems
Chenghao Huang 1 § , Siyang Li 2 § , Ruohong Liu 2 § , Hao Wang 1 , and Yize Chen 2

Abstract
Foundation models, such as Large Language Models (LLMs), can respond to a wide range of format-free queries without any task-specific data collection or model training, creating various research and application opportunities for the modeling and operation of large-scale power systems. In this paper, we outline how such large foundation model such as GPT-4 are developed, and discuss how they can be leveraged in challenging power and energy system tasks. We first investigate the potential of existing foundation models by validating their performance on four representative tasks across power system domains, including the optimal power flow (OPF), electric vehicle (EV) scheduling, knowledge retrieval for power engineering technical reports, and situation awareness. Our results indicate strong capabilities of such foundation models on boosting the efficiency and reliability of power system operational pipelines. We also provide suggestions and projections on future deployment of foundation models in power system applications.

Introduction

Recent advancements on large foundation models such as large language models (LLM) and large multi-modal models have greatly transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence and machine learning (ML)
[
1
,
2
]
. The conversational variant of Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4) developed by OpenAI, ChatGPT, has been widely perceived as a milestone toward large foundation models. While standard machine learning models are often-wise trained on single-source, domain-specific dataset, which is largely limited by the data collection and application scope. Large foundation models are based on the transformer neural network architectures
[
3
]
, and are trained on unprecedented scale of compute and data. Once trained, they exhibit impressive, generalizable capabilities across a variety of domains and tasks including reasoning, abstraction, understanding and projection. Such models indeed revolutionize the development and implementation of machine learning algorithms and reveal many novel opportunities.

The equivalent- or even beyond-human-level abilities provided by foundation models motivate us to explore the emerging opportunities in boosting the performance of power system modeling and operations.
[
4
]
explores the pre-training of large transformer model for power system time-series forecasting and imputation, while in
[
5

the authors show early success on applying ChatGPT for generating test circuits in an interactive way. However, many abilities provided by foundation models are yet to be utilized for improving the reliability and efficiency of power networks. For instance, such large foundation models have been shown to be able to output understanding, reasoning, and using tools for a wide variety of domains to some degree
[
6
]
. Such capabilities can potentially help or even change the operational procedures for large-scale power networks.

In this paper, we systematically develop application pipelines and examine the capabilities of large foundation models on a set of power system modeling and operation tasks. We find using off-the-shelf pre-trained foundation models such as GPT-4 and GPT-4 Vision (GPT-4V), it is indeed efficient to create multiple novel pathways in helping engineers, policymakers, and energy users for solving challenging problems in the power and energy domain. These problems often involve high-dimensional unstructured data, such as interacting with plain-language technical documents, system measurements or image data. In addition, by leveraging the power of large foundation models, it effectively removes the barriers for users to interact with the power systems. This differentiates from other machine learning implementation for power grid applications. For instance, previous research identified machine learning algorithms can successfully predict locational marginal price and power dispatch signals
[
7

. But this needs trained engineers to carefully craft labeled operational data and specific machine learning models. On the contrary, large foundation models can potentially ease the data collection and model training burden, while providing instant feedback for system operators and energy users

II-A

LLM as Optimizer

Optimization have been regarded as one of the foundational tools for solving various decision-making problems in power system, such as the OPF, EV charging scheduling, and building energy system management
[
17
]
. Although researchers have provided numerous solutions, there still exist challenges from both computation cost and algorithm performance perspectives. In particular, for a variety of users lacking domain knowledge in optimization or power systems, it is impractical for them to learn to model and solve such problems
[
18
]
.

Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) demonstrates the ability of LLMs to solve optimization problem by describing the problem with natural language, and then iteratively generates new solutions based on task description and previously generated solutions
[
19
]
. We further develop a framework named LLM for the OPF (
LLM4OPF
) to solve constrained optimization problems as shown in Fig.
1
(a). In this framework, we generate prompt with historical solution-cost pairs and task description. In each optimization step, several solution-cost pairs with minimal cost values are retrieved from solution-cost pair buffer, and LLM optimizer generates new solution based on the well-designed prompt. The new solution which satisfies physical constraints will be stored in solution-cost pair buffer. The detailed prompt and response example is described in our online repository
[
20
]
. We additionally explore the ability of
LLM4OPF
to quickly adapt to new problems with different constraints, given new task description and solutions of previous problem.

Though OPRO can find good quality solutions in some optimization problems, the inefficiency in searching solutions space limits its application in optimization problems with larger scale.
[
18
]
empirically shows the ability of LLMs to generate code, while occasionally triggering errors during execution. Hence, instead of relying on LLMs to generate the entire code, we introduce function templates for the optimization problem in the novel framework named LLM for EV (
LLM4EV
). LLMs interacts with end users, comprehends inquiries, writes code to invoke the functions, and explains the results of code execution to end users. Fig.
1
(b) illustrates our interactive process between users and LLMs. Complete interaction process is detailed in
[
20
]
.

II-B

LLM for Knowledge Retrieval

In this subsection, we introduce our approach of adopting LLMs to achieve summarization and question answering (QA) on energy field-based long-textual documents, named
LLM4Doc
. The framework of our approach is depicted in Fig.
1
(c), where we adopt retrieval augmented generation (RAG) and prompt engineering (PE) as workhorses. RAG is a hybrid mechanism based on natural language generation and information retrieval. It is capable of enriching LLMs’ knowledge by pulling in information from a corpus of useful data, much like fetching a book from a library
[
10
]
. Integrated with RAG, LLMs can generate not only contextually accurate, but also information-rich outputs.
Retrieval
involves searching for relevant information from a large corpus of data, such as textual files and database. Only using retrieval can probably generate inaccurate or unrelated outputs.

Augmentation
involves incorporating the retrieved information into LLM’s internal representation of knowledge to significantly improve the output quality of LLMs.

Generation
involves generating text from LLMs.

Fig. 2:
The format of proposed engineered prompts for processing technical documents in power systems.

We firstly process a document with long text into vector embeddings. Then, we use integrate these vectors into an external knowledge base. Based on this base, we ask GPT-4 to do summarization and multiple QAs on the processed document. On the other hand, prompt engineering is a crucial step in LLM development, which focuses on refining the language prompts to help LLMs understand human’s requirements better, and produce fairly accurate, relevant, and information-rich outputs
[
21
]
. There are various purposes of prompt engineering. Specifically, in this paper, we design a format for our prompts, where humans can customize different parts to get diversified answers, detailed in Fig.
2
.

II-C

Large Multimodal Models for Situation Awareness

Multi-modal models are able to connect learned representations across diverse data types, e.g. language, vision, and etc, to gain a more comprehensive understanding and generate appropriate responses. Current state-of-the-art multi-modal models are built upon LLMs with incremental pre-training with image-text pairs
[
22
]
, so the resulting models can achieve a variety of tasks with multiple types of model input and output data. Such capabilities can provide valuable tools for power system applications, as system measurements and logging data are naturally large scale and multi-modal.

In particular, we look into the situation awareness problem which can be tackled by large multi-modal models
[
13
]
. To guarantee the operation safety, system operators need to make timely and efficient measurements processing, operating states comprehension, and anomaly detection. For instance, imaging data have been used in
[
23
]
to identify the insulator surface status. However, there are
limited sample availability of target situations and limited diversity of operating situations
, such as the sub-station usually stays in normal stage with scare fire disaster occurrence, which render it hard to foster a dedicated fire detector. Hence, a zero/few-shot situation monitor with reliable and aligned vision-text representations is highly favored. GPT-4V is a competent candidate
[
24
]
, which owns potent image-text retrieval ability to produce accurate scene descriptions for measured images, as well as outstanding zero/few-shot generalization inference and scenarios transferability to discern rare situations. In this work, we propose a framework named
LLM4SA
by adopting in-context learning plus prompt engineering
[
25
]
which circumvents cumbersome post-hoc fine-tuning. In this regard, proper prompt design plays a key role which should provide multi-modal QA exemplars of high correspondence and concrete detection task or situation category information
[
26
]
.

There are two orthogonal ways to achieve desirable situation understanding. One way is instruction tuning coupled with prompt learning
[
27
]
, which needs to handcraft domain-specific input-label pairs and use them to tailor an ad-hoc prompt embedding and refine original parameters for LLMs. But this method is hard to execute due to formidable data collection and computational efforts for large parameter fine-tuning. The other way is in-context learning plus prompt engineering
[
25
]
which circumvents cumbersome post-hoc fine-tuning. In this regard, proper prompt design plays a key role which should provide multi-modal query-answer exemplars of high correspondence and concrete detection task or situation category information
[
26
]
. In this work, we majorly focus on the in-context learning capabilities to attain the zero/few-shot situation awareness by curating detailed in-context prompts.

III-C

Situation Awareness via Multimodal Capabilities

We utilize multimodal capabilities of GPT-4V to achieve situation awareness based on scenario images. Situation awareness usually involves classification tasks such as anomaly detection. In this part, we demonstrate the results of satellite image-based wildfire detection
[
31
]
, a binary classification task. We design 4 approaches: (1). One image query and directly asking if wildfire exists in the image;
(2). One image query and applying prompt engineering;
(3). Multiple image examples along with ground true labels as the engineered prompts;
(4). Based on (3), adding language explanations associated with true labels.

To get average accuracy of each approach, we take multiple-round experiments, where 5 positive images and 5 negative images are involved in each round. An example of Approach (3) is demonstrated in Fig.
5
and see
[
20
]
for complete results. Among all approaches, the Approach 4) achieves the highest accuracy 91%, proving that GPT-4V is capable of utilizing multimodal information to improve performance, while the Approach (3) achieves 86%, also indicating the effectiveness of few-shot prompting. However, it is worth mentioning GPT-4V’s performance on specific image classification tasks is still inferior to standard ML models such as convolutional neural network with 94% accuracy, and random forest with 96% accuracy. Even so, GPT-4V holds remarkable potential in saving the training time and data collection processes, which are needed for typical ML methods. Furthermore, GPT-4V have more flexibility on accepting and generating multimodal outputs, such as generating explanations of current states.

IV
Limitations, Summary and Future Work

In this paper, we explore a set of promising applications, promises and challenges of developing foundation models for power systems. The potential of such models are validated by a range of comprehension, modeling, and operation tasks. We show the noteworthy performance provided by LLMs and multi-modal models without fine-tuning or further training. We conclude that such strong capabilities of foundation models can provide system operators, policymakers, and end users efficient tools.

While we note that large foundation models are still at the initial stage for industrial deployments, since there still exists limited understanding to the model’s behaviors, and needs thorough evaluation and testing on real-world scenarios. We also notice a set of limitations on using the current foundation models, such as model validity, efficiency and applicable scenarios, as detailed in Table
II
