Hydrodynamics of the t t -PNG model via a colored t t -PNG model
Hindy Drillick, Yier Lin

Abstract
In this paper, we prove the hydrodynamic limit of the t t -PNG model using soft techniques. One key element of the proof is the construction of a colored version of the t t -PNG model, which allows us to apply the superadditive ergodic theorem and obtain the hydrodynamic limit, albeit without identifying the limiting constant. We then find this constant by proving a law of large numbers for the α \alpha -points. Along the way, we construct the stationary t t -PNG model and prove a version of Burke’s theorem for it.

1.1
Background

The distribution theory of the length of the longest increasing subsequence
\ell_{n}
of a random permutation of the numbers
1,\dots,n
with uniform measure has been under intense study in the past few decades. Now there are surveys and books
[
5
,
26
,
43
]
on this topic.

One object of particular interest is the
n\to\infty
asymptotic behavior of
\ell_{n}
.
[
28
]
proved a law of large numbers
\frac{\ell_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\overset{p}{\to}\gamma
(without identifying the constant
\gamma
) by considering a Poissonized version of
\ell_{n}
and relating it to a last passage percolation model called the polynuclear growth (PNG) model or Hammersley’s process. Then the superadditive ergodic theorem implies the law of large numbers. There have been various approaches to determining the constant
\gamma
, see
[
39
,
52
,
45
,
5
,
25
,
16
]
. By detailed analysis of the exact expression of the distribution function,
[
6
]
(see also
[
10
,
31
]
)
proved a Tracy-Widom fluctuation limit theorem for
\ell_{n}
.

The PNG model lies in the so-called Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality (KPZ) class. It is natural to wonder whether there is a way to deform the PNG model so that its deformation also belongs to the KPZ universality class.
[
3
]
recently introduced a one-parameter deformation of the PNG model called the
t
-PNG model. The
t
-PNG model is also related to the length of the longest increasing subsequence from the perspective of patience sorting
[
5
]
.
Using the method of integrable probability, the authors of
[
3
]
proved a Tracy-Widom fluctuation limit theorem for the
t
-PNG model that substantially generalizes the result of
[
6
]
. This shows that the
t
-PNG model also belongs to the KPZ universality class.

Inspired by
[
28
,
5
,
25
,
16
]
, the purpose of our paper is to study the
t
-PNG model using soft arguments.
The main result is a shape theorem for the
t
-PNG model. We will view the
t
-PNG model from two different perspectives:
as a single-colored projection of the colored
t
-PNG model and as an interacting particle system. We will see how a combination of these perspectives leads us to the main result.

1.2
The
t
-PNG model

Let us proceed to define the
t
-PNG model. Fix
t\in[0,1]
. First, we place a Poisson point process with intensity
1
on the upper-right quadrant representing
nucleations
.
We draw lines emanating from each of these nucleations in both the upward and rightward directions until they collide with one another. We call this collision point an

- and

and

Taking

as the deformation parameter (following

1.3
Statement of main result

We present our main result, which is a shape theorem for the
t
-PNG model.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
t\in[0,1)
. Let
N(x,y)

be the height function of the
t
-PNG model as defined above. Then the following shape theorem holds: With probability
1
, we have for all fixed
x,y>0
,

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{N(sx,sy)}{s}=\frac{2\sqrt{xy}}{\sqrt{1-t}}.

As a consequence, we have
\frac{N(x,y)}{\sqrt{xy}}\overset{p}{\to}\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-t}}
as
xy\to\infty
.

Remark 1.2
.

When
t=0
, our result recovers the shape theorem for the PNG model that was proved in
[
39
,
52
,
4
,
45
,
25
,
16
]
. If we take
t=1
, the right-hand side blows up. In that case,
N(x,y)
equals the number of Poisson nucleations in the rectangle
[0,x]\times[0,y]
. The asymptotic behavior of
N(sx,sy)
then follows from the central limit theorem for a Poisson random variable.

Remark 1.3
.

Using the methods of integrable probability,
[
3
]
showed that as
s\to\infty
,

\frac{N(sx,sy)-\frac{2s\sqrt{xy}}{\sqrt{1-t}}}{(1-t)^{-\frac{1}{6}}s^{\frac{1}{3}}(xy)^{\frac{1}{6}}}\Rightarrow F_{2}

where
F_{2}
is the Tracy-Widom distribution. Our theorem uses a softer technique to extract the first-order asymptotic of
N(sx,sy)
at the almost sure level.

Remark 1.4
.

A natural question to ask is whether one can prove anything about the local convergence of the model. When
t=0
,
[
4
]
showed that

\{N(as+x,s)-N(as,s),x\in(-\infty,\infty)\}

converges in distribution to a Poisson point process with intensity
\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}
.
The proof in
[
4
]
is via identifying the PNG model as an interacting particle system on the real line and classifying its stationary distributions as convex combinations of Poisson point processes.

We believe that for general
t\in[0,1)
, the same process
converges in distribution to a Poisson point process with intensity
\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-t)a}}
. The value
\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-t)}a}
comes from taking the spatial partial derivative of the hydrodynamic limit at
x=a
. The difficulty is that although the
t
-PNG model can still be viewed as an interacting particle system on any finite interval (see Section
4
), it is not clear how to extend the definition of the particle system to the entire real line due to its intricate dynamics.

A natural first step to proving the hydrodynamic limit theorem of the PNG model and other last passage percolation models is to apply the superadditive ergodic theorem. To apply the superadditive ergodic theorem (see Theorem
3.1
), one needs to construct a family of superadditive random variables
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
where
\{X_{0,n},n\geq 0\}
records the height function. A subfamily of the random variables also needs to be ergodic. For the PNG model, one can define
X_{m,n}
as the length of the longest up-right path from
(m,m)
to
(n,n)
(we allow segments that go straight up or to the right), where the length of a path is defined as the number of nucleation points that it collects along its trajectory.

Let us introduce a few notions for the
t
-PNG model so that we can try to modify the approach above for the case where
t>0
. We define two sets of points called
\alpha
-points and
\beta
-points, which generalize the definitions in
[
25
]
. We define the set of
\alpha
-points as the union of the Poisson nucleations and crossing points. We define the set of
\beta
-points as the union of corner points and crossing points. In particular, the intersection of the set of
\alpha
-points and the set of
\beta
-points is the set of crossing points.

We redefine the length of an up-right path to be the number of
\alpha
-points it collects. Then the height function of the
t
-PNG model is equal to the length of the longest up-right path. Hence, one can naively try to define the random variables
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
in a similar way as above, but with our new definition of length. With this definition, the
X_{m,n}
are superadditive as desired. However, they are no longer stationary since the number of crossing points in a box no longer just depends on the number of Poisson nucleations in that box—it also depends on the lines entering that box from the left and bottom boundaries. Therefore, we no longer even have stationarity and thus no ergodicity.

It turns out that there is indeed a way construct a family of random variables
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
that satisfies the conditions required by the superadditive ergodic theorem. The family
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
comes from a colored version of the
t
-PNG model that we are going to introduce.

1.4
The colored
t
-PNG model

As we see from above, for the
t
-PNG model, two lines emanate rightward and upward from each Poisson nucleation point. The key rule for sampling the
t
-PNG model after fixing the Poisson nucleation points is that when two lines meet, they cross with probability
t
and annihilate each other with probability
1-t
. This rule can be encoded into a stochastic matrix.
We associate each intersection point with a 4-tuple
i,j,k,l\in\{0,1\}

1.4.1
Connection to the stochastic six vertex model

The stochastic six vertex (S6V) model is a classical model in two-dimensional statistical physics. The model was introduced in
[

[

,
9
]

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}

1.5
A Burke’s theorem for the
t
-PNG model

The original Burke’s theorem
[
14
]
states that the departure process
of an M/M/1 queue with a Poisson arrival process is a Poisson process. Generalizations of Burke’s theorem have been proved for last passage models
[
16
,
7
,
50
]
, polymers
[
41
,
49
,
19
]
, and stochastic vertex models
[
1
,
29
,
37
]
. Note that these models all admit a stationary version and that Burke’s theorem is a stronger property than stationarity.

The
t
-PNG model has a stationary version, which will be an important tool in the proof of our main theorem and is constructed in Section
4
. As a natural extension, we also prove a Burke’s theorem for the
t
-PNG model. Our result extends Theorem 3.1 in
[
16
]
from
t=0
to general

.

Theorem 1.8

(Burke’s Theorem)
.

Fix
T_{1},T_{2}>0
. Consider the
t
-PNG model on
[0,T_{1}]\times[0,T_{2}]
with a Poisson process of sources on of intensity
\lambda
on the bottom boundary, a Poisson process of sinks of intensity
\frac{1}{\lambda(1-t)}
on the left boundary, and Poisson process of nucleations of intensity
1
in the interior of the box. We choose all three Poisson processes to be independent. Denote this process by
L_{\lambda}
and let
L_{\lambda}^{\text{corner}}
denote the set of corner points, let
L_{\lambda}^{\text{in}}
denote the entry points of paths on the right boundary, and let
L_{\lambda}^{\text{out}}
denote the exit points of paths on the top boundary. Then

(i)

L_{\lambda}^{\text{corner}}
is a Poisson point process with intensity
1
in
[0,T_{1}]\times[0,T_{2}]
,
L_{\lambda}^{\text{in}}
is a Poisson point process of intensity
\frac{1}{\lambda(1-t)}
, and
L_{\lambda}^{\text{out}}
is a Poisson point process of intensity
\lambda
.

(ii)

All three Poisson processes are independent.

The proof of Theorem

is given in Section

1.6
Proof idea

As explained after Remark
1.4
, unlike in the case of the PNG model, we can no longer directly apply the superadditive ergodic theorem to the
t
-PNG model using attractivity. Instead, we need to define a colored version of the
t
-PNG model which will allow us to construct a family of random variables
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
that satisfies the conditions of the superadditive ergodic theorem.

Definition
1.7
for the matrices
\{\mathsf{L}^{n}:n\geq 1\}
follows from a sophisticated guess based on Properties
1
–
3
;
however, it is not immediately clear that these matrices are stochastic, and it also takes some work to show that all of the desired Properties
1
–
3
are indeed satisfied. The proof of stochasticity follows from an induction argument. Properties
1
–
3
then follow from a case-by-case study using the stochasticity and the fact that the entries of
\{\mathsf{L}^{n}:n\geq 1\}
belong to
\{0,t,1-t,1\}
.

Having defined the stochastic matrices
\{\mathsf{L}^{n}:n\geq 1\}
, we proceed to prove the hydrodynamic limit theorem. The idea is to consider a colored
t
-PNG model and define
X_{m,n}
to be a color-dependent version of the height function restricted to
[m,n]\times[m,n]
. We assign the Poisson nucleations with different colors so that points with smaller
x
-coordinates or
y
-coordinates have lower priority. We can now define
X_{m,n}
in such a way so that it only depends on nucleations inside the box
[m,n]\times[m,n]
and ignores all lines entering from the bottom or the left since those lines will have lower priority. Therefore, the random variables
X_{m,n}
will be stationary and independent on disjoint boxes and hence ergodic. The superadditivity intrinsically follows from a coupling argument that says that allowing more lines to enter the boundary only increases the height function. We can now apply the superadditive ergodic theorem
[
35
]
to the family of random variables
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
. By the property of mod 2 erasure, the colored
t
-PNG model reduces to the single-colored
t
-PNG model after color projection. Hence, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{N(n,n)}{n}=\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{E}[N(n,n)]}{n}=\gamma,\qquad\text{ a.s.},

(1.2)

where
\gamma
is some constant in
[0,\infty].

To determine the value of this constant, we adopt an idea from
[
25
]
. Before carrying out that idea in the next paragraph, we need to first show that
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
. We do this by deriving an upper bound and lower bound for
\gamma
. The lower bound can be simply obtained via a coupling with the PNG model. To obtain the upper bound, we generalize
[
4
]
and view the
t
-PNG model as an interacting particle system where the vertical lines play the role of particle trajectories. We add sources and sinks on the left and bottom boundary and obtain a stationary version of the
t
-PNG model. A coupling with the stationary model together with the attractive property provides an upper bound for
\gamma
.

In
[
25
]
, the author proposed a soft argument for computing the constant
\gamma
in the case of
t=0
. The key idea is to relate the computation of
\gamma
to the law of large numbers (LLN) for the number of
\alpha
-points. When
t=0
, the
\alpha
-points are exactly the Poisson nucleations and the LLN follows immediately. For
t>0
, the set of
\alpha
-points is the union of Poisson nucleations and crossing points. We prove an LLN for the number of
\alpha
-points using the fact that the number of
\alpha
-points on the horizontal line emanating from a Poisson nucleation is asymptotically a geometric random variable.

(\mathbf{k}^{1}_{[1,m]},\mathbf{l}^{1}_{[1,m]})=(\mathfrak{k}^{1},\mathfrak{l}^{1})
and

\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1})=t
. For

(\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]},\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]})=(\mathfrak{k}^{1},\mathfrak{l}^{1})
and
(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})\neq(\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1})
, we have
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=0

For a similar reason, there exists a unique pair
(\mathbf{k}^{2},\mathbf{l}^{2})
such that
(\mathbf{k}^{2}_{[1,m]},\mathbf{l}^{2}_{[1,m]})=(\mathfrak{k}^{2},\mathfrak{l}^{2})

\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k}^{2},\mathbf{l}^{2})=1-t

\mathsf{L}^{n}
, for
(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})\notin\{(\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1}),(\mathbf{k}^{2},\mathbf{l}^{2})\}
, we have
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=0
.
This concludes the lemma.
∎

Let us prepare some notation for the next lemma. We call

\pi
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix positive integers
m\leq n
. Fix a partition
\pi

Assume that for fixed
\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}\in\{0,1\}^{m}

\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

, there exist
\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1}
,
\mathbf{k}^{2},\mathbf{l}^{2}\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1})=t,\qquad\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k}^{2},\mathbf{l}^{2})=1-t.

For
(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})

or

, we have
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=0
.

The lemma is clearly true when

m<n
.

By stochasticity of
\mathsf{L}^{m}
, there exists
\mathfrak{k}^{1},\mathfrak{l}^{1},\mathfrak{k}^{2},\mathfrak{l}^{2}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
satisfying
\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k}^{1},\mathfrak{l}^{1})=t

\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k}^{2},\mathfrak{l}^{2})=1-t
.
We let

\pi=\big\{\{1,\dots,r_{1}\},\{r_{1}+1,\dots,r_{2}\},\dots,\{r_{m-1}+1,\dots,r_{m}\}\big\}

where
r_{m}=n
. Let
A_{\pi}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\}
and
B_{\pi}=\{1,\dots,n\}\backslash A_{\pi}
.
Using (
1.1
), we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{min}_{r=1}^{n}\big(\mathsf{L}^{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{i}),\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{j});\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{k}),\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{l}))\big)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{min}\Big(\mathfrak{min}_{r\in A_{\pi}}\big(\mathsf{L}^{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{i}),\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{j});\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{k}),\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{l}))\big),\mathfrak{min}_{r\in B_{\pi}}\big(\mathsf{L}^{1}(\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{i}),\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{j});\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{k}),\mathfrak{s}_{r}(\mathbf{l}))\big)\Big)

(g_{\pi}(\mathbf{k}),g_{\pi}(\mathbf{l}))=(\mathfrak{k}^{1},\mathfrak{l}^{1})

Proposition 2.5

(Color Ignorance)
.

Fix
m\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and

\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. For all
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{i}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{i}
and
\mathbf{j}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{j}
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k},\\
\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{l}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}).

(2.3)

We have
\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})\in\{0,t,1-t,1\}
. We prove the equality (
2.3
) for each possible value of
\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})
.

Case 1:

\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=0
. By (
2.2
), when
\mathbf{i}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{i}
,
\mathbf{j}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{j}
,
\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k}
,
\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{l}
, we have
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=0
. Hence, (
2.3
) holds.

Case 2:

\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=1
. By stochasticity of
\mathsf{L}^{m}
and (
2.2
), if we have
\mathbf{i}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{i}
,
\mathbf{j}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{j}
and
(\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]},\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]})\neq(\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})
, we have
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=0
. Using this and the stochasticity of
\mathsf{L}^{n}
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k}\\
\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{l}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=1=\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}).

2.3
) holds.

Case 3:

\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=t
. Since
\mathbf{i}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{i}
and
\mathbf{j}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{j}
, by Lemma
2.3
, we know that there exist unique
\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying
\mathbf{k}^{1}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k}
,
\mathbf{l}^{1}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{l}
and
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1})=t
. For all
(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying
\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k}
,
\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k}
and
(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})\neq(\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1})
,
\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=0
. Hence,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{k}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{k}\\
\mathbf{l}_{[1,m]}=\mathfrak{l}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k}^{1},\mathbf{l}^{1})=t=\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}).

Case 4:
\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=1-t
. The proof is similar to Case 3, and we omit it. This concludes the proof of the proposition. ∎

Proposition 2.6

(Mod 2 Erasure)
.

Fix a partition
\pi
of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
\ell(\pi)=m
. Fix
\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. For all
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfying
g_{\pi}(\mathbf{i})=\mathfrak{i}
and
g_{\pi}(\mathbf{j})=\mathfrak{j}
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})=\mathfrak{k}\\
g_{\pi}(\mathbf{l})=\mathfrak{l}\end{subarray}}\mathsf{L}^{n}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j};\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l})=\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j};\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l}).

(2.4)

Let
\pi=\big\{\{1,\dots,r_{1}\},\{r_{1}+1,\dots,r_{2}\},\dots,\{r_{m-1}+1,\dots,r_{m}\}\big\}
, where
1\leq r_{1}<\dots<r_{m}=n
. We again divide our proof into four cases.

Case 1:
\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=0

1.1
), for
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k},\mathbf{l}

. Hence,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})=\mathfrak{k}\\

\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=1

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})=\mathfrak{k},\\

Case 3:
\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=t

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{\pi}(\mathbf{k})=\mathfrak{k},\\

\mathsf{L}^{m}(\mathfrak{i},\mathfrak{j},\mathfrak{k},\mathfrak{l})=1-t

Remark 2.7
.

One can also use real numbers to label colors, not just positive integers. The rule is that for two colors with labels
a<b
, the color
b
has less priority than
a
. Hence, we can still sample the output of an intersection given finite input lines labeled by real numbers, using
\{\mathsf{L}^{n}:n\geq 1\}
. For our application of the colored model in the next section, we use negative integers to label the colors (see also Remark
3.3
).

3
The colored model and superadditivity

We are going to construct
\{X_{m,n}:0\leq m\leq n\}
as discussed in the introduction using the colored
t
-PNG model. Before doing that, let us recall Liggett’s superadditive ergodic theorem from
[
35
]
. Note that the theorem was originally stated in the subadditive setting, but for our purposes, we formulate it in the superadditive setting by placing a negative sign where necessary.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
\{X_{m,n}\}
is a collection of random variables that is indexed by integers
0\leq m\leq n
and satisfies:

(i)

Almost surely
X_{0,0}=0
and
X_{0,n}\geq X_{0,m}+X_{m,n}
for
0\leq m\leq n
.

(ii)

For each
k\geq 1
,
\{X_{(n-1)k,nk}:n\geq 1\}
is an ergodic process.

(iii)

\{X_{m,m+k}:k\geq 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{X_{m+1,m+k+1}:k\geq 0\}
for each
m\geq 0
.

(iv)

\mathbb{E}[X_{0,1}^{-}]<\infty
where
x^{-}=\max(-x,0)
.

Then there exists a constant
\gamma=\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{0,n}]}{n}\in(-\infty,\infty]
satisfying

\displaystyle\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{X_{0,n}}{n}\text{ a.s.}

Definition 3.2

(Step colored
t
-PNG model)
.

We consider a Poisson point process of nucleations on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with density
1
. We can assume that there are no nucleations with an integer
x
- or
y
-coordinate and that no two nucleations have the same
x
- or
y
-coordinates, since these events have probability zero.
Fix arbitrary integers
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We color the nucleations inside the unit square
[m,m+1]\times[n,n+1]
with the color
-\min(m+1,n+1)
. In other words, we assign the color
-m-1
to nucleations lying in the L-shape area
[m,\infty)\times[m,m+1]\cup[m,m+1]\times[m,\infty)
.
For each nucleation with a given color
k
, the lines emanating from it in the upward and rightward directions also have color
k
. When horizontal and vertical lines intersect, the output lines emanate from the intersection following the stochastic matrix
\{\mathsf{L}^{n}:n\geq 1\}
defined in Definition
1.7
(see also Remark
2.7
).
The model is referred to as the
step colored
t
-PNG model
. See Figure
13
.

, we want to construct a model with infinite colors such that nucleations closer to the axes have lower priority.

We have

Proposition 3.5
.

The stochastic process
\{X_{m,n}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},m\leq n\}
satisfies conditions
(ii)
-
(iv)
of Theorem
3.1
.

We first prove
(ii)
. Consider the square
[m,n]\times[m,n]
. There are lines flowing inside through the left boundary
\{m\}\times[m,n]
and the bottom boundary
[m,n]\times\{m\}
. These lines have colors that belong to
\{-m,\dots,-1\}

[m,n]\times[m,n]
have colors in
\{-n,\dots,-m-1\}
. Note that the color
i
takes priority over
j

i<j
, so the lines that emanate from the Poisson nucleations in
[m,n]\times[m,n]
have higher priority than the lines entering through the left and bottom boundaries. Therefore, by Proposition
2.5
, we can sample the colored
t
-PNG model in the square
[m,n]\times[m,n]
just using the colors
\{-n,\dots,-m-1\}
and ignore the lower priority lines entering from the left and bottom. Hence, the distribution of
X_{m,n}
is independent of the number and location of the lines entering the bottom and left boundaries of
[m,n]\times[m,n]
. This implies that for each
k\geq 1
, the random variables
\{X_{(n-1)k,nk},n\geq 1\}
are independent. It is straightforward to see that
X_{(n-1)k,nk}
has the same distribution as
N(k,k)
for all
n\geq 1

We proceed to prove
(iii)
. It suffices to show that for arbitrary

\{X_{m,m+k},k\geq 0\}\overset{d}{=}\{X_{0,k},k\geq 0\}.

(3.2)

t

[m,\infty)\times[m,\infty)
. By Proposition
2.5
, the behavior of lines in
[m,\infty)\times[m,\infty)
with colors less than
-m
is unaffected by the lower priority lines entering from the boundary.
This implies that after a diagonal shift by
(m,m)

[m,\infty)\times[m,\infty)
behave the same (in distribution) as the lines with colors
i_{1}+m,\dots,i_{k}+m
in
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)
. Hence, we conclude (
3.2
).

Finally, since
X_{0,1}
is non-negative,
X_{0,1}^{-}=0
. Hence,

R=[x_{1},x_{2}]\times[y_{1},y_{2}]
. Define

h_{R}=\min\Big\{\text{number of intersections between }[x_{1},x_{2}]\times\{y\}\text{ and the vertical lines in $t$-PNG model}:y\in[y_{1},y_{2}]\Big\}.

In other words, as we move the horizontal segment vertically from
[x_{1},x_{2}]\times\{y_{1}\}
to
[x_{1},x_{2}]\times\{y_{2}\}
,
h_{R}
is the minimum number of intersections between the vertical lines and this horizontal segment.

Lemma 5.2
.

Fix any rectangle
R=[x_{1},x_{2}]\times[y_{1},y_{2}]
. Let
\ell
be the number of horizontal lines that enter the rectangle from the left. Let
b
be the number of vertical lines that enter the rectangle
R
from the bottom. Then
h_{R}\geq(b-\ell)^{+}
.

In the
t
-PNG model, a vertical line will go upward until it meets a horizontal line. Moreover, each horizontal line can annihilate at most one vertical line. This concludes the lemma.
∎

Fix
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
. Let
R(s)=[(1-\frac{1}{M})s,s]\times[\frac{s}{M},s]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

We have almost surely,

\lim_{s\to\infty}h_{R(s)}\to\infty.

(5.1)

For each
i\in\{0,1,\dots,M^{2}-1\}
, let
b_{i}(s)
be the number of lines entering the rectangle
R_{i}=[(1-\frac{1}{M})s,s]\times[\frac{is}{M^{2}},\frac{(i+1)s}{M^{2}}]
from the bottom boundary. Let
\ell_{i}(s)
be the number of lines entering the same rectangle from the left boundary. It is straightforward that

\displaystyle b_{i}(s)=N\Big(s,\frac{is}{M^{2}}\Big)-N\Big((1-\frac{1}{M})s,\frac{is}{M^{2}}\Big),

\displaystyle\ell_{i}(s)=N\Big((1-\frac{1}{M})s,\frac{(i+1)s}{M^{2}}\Big)-N\Big((1-\frac{1}{M})s,\frac{is}{M^{2}}\Big).

By Lemma
5.2
, we know that
h_{R_{i}(s)}\geq b_{i}(s)-\ell_{i}(s)
.
Since
R=\cup_{i=M}^{M^{2}-1}R_{i}
, we have
h_{R(s)}\geq\min_{i=M}^{M^{2}-1}\big(b_{i}(s)-\ell_{i}(s)\big)
.

By the law of large numbers in Proposition
3.10
, we know that almost surely,

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{b_{i}(s)}{s}=\gamma\frac{\sqrt{i}}{M}\Big(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M}}\Big),

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\ell_{i}(s)}{s}=\gamma\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M}}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{i+1}}{M}-\frac{\sqrt{i}}{M}\Big).

Subtracting the second equation from the first above yields

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{b_{i}(s)-\ell_{i}(s)}{s}=\gamma\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{i}}{M}\Big(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M}}\Big)-\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M}}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{i+1}}{M}-\frac{\sqrt{i}}{M}\Big)\bigg).

When
i\geq M
, it is straightforward to check that

\frac{\sqrt{i}}{M}\Big(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M}}\Big)-\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{M}}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{i+1}}{M}-\frac{\sqrt{i}}{M}\Big)>0.

This together with
\gamma>0
(see Lemma
3.11
) implies that for any
i\geq M
, we have
\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{b_{i}(s)-\ell_{i}(s)}{s}>0
. Since

h_{R(s)}\geq\min_{i=M}^{M^{2}-1}\big(b_{i}(s)-\ell_{i}(s)\big)
, we conclude that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{h_{R(s)}}{s}>0
. This concludes the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

We have

\lim_{s\to\infty}s^{-2}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)\big]=\frac{1}{1-t}.

We prove the lemma by showing that

\displaystyle\limsup_{s\to\infty}s^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)]\leq\frac{1}{1-t},

(
upper bound
)

\displaystyle\liminf_{s\to\infty}s^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)]\geq\frac{1}{1-t}.

(
lower bound
)

Proof of (
upper bound
):

Let
K(s)
be the total number of Poisson nucleations in
[0,s]\times[0,s]
. We label these points by
1,\dots,K(s)
. Let
\alpha_{i}(s)
be the number of vertical lines in the
t
-PNG model that cross the the horizontal line emanating from the nucleation point
i
(including the vertical line that emanates from the point
i
). For every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha_{n}(s)\,|\,K(s)\geq n\big]\leq\frac{1}{1-t},

(5.2)

This is because if we condition on the event
\{K(s)\geq n\}
,
\alpha_{n}(s)
is stochastically dominated by a geometric random variable with parameter
1-t
.
As a result, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)\big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}\alpha_{i}(s)\Big]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(K(s)\geq n)\mathbb{E}\big[\alpha_{n}(s)\,|\,K(s)\geq n\big]\leq\frac{1}{1-t}\mathbb{E}\big[K(s)\big]=\frac{s^{2}}{1-t}.

For the first equality, note that there are
\alpha_{i}(s)
number of
\alpha
-points that have the same
y
-coordinate as that of the nucleation point
i
, hence
\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}\alpha_{i}(s)
.
The first inequality above is due to (
5.2
). The third equality holds because
K(s)
is a Poisson random variable with parameter
s^{2}
.
Hence, we have shown that

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{s^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)\big]\leq\frac{1}{1-t}.

(5.3)

Proof of (
lower bound
):

Fix
M,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
. Define
c_{M}(\epsilon)=(1-\frac{1}{M})^{2}-\epsilon
. We can choose
\epsilon>0
small enough such that
c_{M}(\epsilon)>0
. Let
K_{M}(s)
be the total number of Poisson nucleations in the square
[0,(1-\frac{1}{M})s)]\times[\frac{s}{M},s]
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)\big]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{K_{M}(s)}\alpha_{i}(s)\Big]\geq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2}}\alpha_{i}(s)\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\{K_{M}(s)\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2},h_{R}(s)\geq m\}}\Big].

For a line that emanates rightward from a Poisson nucleation in
[0,(1-\frac{1}{M})s)]\times[(1-\frac{1}{M})s,s]
, there are potentially at least
h_{R}(s)
number of vertical lines it can cross.
Hence, there exist i.i.d. geometric random variables
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}}\sim\text{Geo}(1-t)
that are independent of the
t
-PNG model, and such that
\alpha_{i}(s)\geq X_{i}\wedge h_{R}(s)
. Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2}}\alpha_{i}\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\{K_{M}(s)\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2},h_{R}(s)\geq m\}}\Big]\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[X_{1}\wedge m\big]\mathbb{P}\Big(K_{M}(s)\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2},h_{R}(s)\geq m\Big).

We divide both sides by
s^{2}
and let
s\to\infty
. Note that
K_{M}(s)
is a Poisson random variable with mean
(1-\frac{1}{M})^{2}s^{2}
. By a standard large deviation bound,
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(K_{M}(s)\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2}\big)=1
. Furthermore, by Lemma
5.1
, we have
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(h_{R}(s)\geq m)=1
. Hence,

\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(K_{M}(s)\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)s^{2},h_{R}(s)\geq m\Big)=1.

This implies that

\liminf_{s\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)\big]}{s^{2}}\geq c_{M}(\epsilon)\mathbb{E}[X_{1}\wedge m].

Letting
M,m\to\infty
and
\epsilon\to 0
, we get

\liminf_{s\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)]}{s^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{1-t}.\qed

To prove Proposition
5.1
, it remains to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5.5
.

We have
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)-\frac{1}{1-t}\big)^{+}\Big]=0
where
x^{+}:=\max(x,0)
.

Since each
\alpha_{i}(s)
is stochastically dominated by a geometric random variable, it follows that the random variable
\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)
is stochastically dominated by
\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}X_{i}
, where
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are i.i.d. geometric random variables that are independent of
K(s)
. Hence, it suffices to show that

\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}X_{i}-\frac{1}{1-t}\Big)^{+}\bigg]=0.

(5.4)

We compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}X_{i}-\frac{1}{1-t}\Big)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}(X_{i}-\frac{1}{1-t})+\frac{1}{1-t}(\frac{K(s)}{s^{2}}-1)\Big)^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\bigg[\Big(\frac{1}{s^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K(s)}(X_{i}-\frac{1}{1-t})\Big)^{2}\bigg]+\frac{2}{(1-t)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[(\frac{K(s)}{s^{2}}-1)^{2}\Big]=\mathcal{O}(s^{-2}).

For the inequality, we use
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
. The last equality follows from a direct computation since
K(s)
is a Poisson random variable with mean
s^{2}
. This concludes (
5.4
).
∎

Using Lemma
5.4
and
5.5
, we have
\lim_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[|\frac{1}{s^{2}}\mathsf{A}_{1}(s)-\frac{1}{1-t}|]=0
. This implies the convergence in probability.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Recall
\gamma
from Proposition

. We have
\gamma=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-t}}
.

Note that

, and
sV_{s}^{+}(x,y)=\mathsf{A}_{1}(sx,sy)
. Using this together with Lemma

, we have

V_{s}(x,y)^{2}=2s^{-2}\mathsf{A}_{1}(sx,sy)-s^{-1}V_{s}(x,y)+2\int_{[0,x]\times[0,t]}\widetilde{V}_{s}(u,v)dV_{s}(u,v).

(5.7)

By Proposition
3.10
, we have almost surely
\lim_{s\to\infty}V_{s}(u,v)=\gamma\sqrt{uv}
. Hence, we have as

(see
[
25
, page 688]
for detail)

\int_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\widetilde{V}_{s}(u,v)dV_{s}(u,v)\to\int_{[0,x]\times[0,y]}\gamma\sqrt{uv}\cdot d(\gamma\sqrt{uv})=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}xy.

By Proposition
5.1
and scaling, we have
\frac{1}{s^{2}}\mathsf{A}_{1}(sx,sy)\overset{p}{\to}\frac{xy}{1-t}
, as

. Hence, there exists a sequence

\gamma^{2}xy=\frac{2}{1-t}xy+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}xy.
Using this together with Lemma

(which shows that

is finite),
we have
\gamma=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-t}}
.
∎

We have

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{x\in E_{n},z\in[0,T_{1}],i\leq j(z)\right\}}f\left(\mathcal{R}^{i}_{z}x\right)g(x)dxdz

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{y\in E_{n},s\in[0,T_{1}],i\leq k(s)\right\}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{L}_{s}^{i}y)dyds.

Second term:
We have

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{x\in E_{n},z\in[0,T_{1}],i>j(z)\right\}}f\left(\mathcal{R}^{i}_{z}x\right)g(x)dxdz

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{x\in E_{n},z\in[0,T_{1}],j(z)=j\right\}}f\left(\mathcal{R}^{i}_{z}x\right)g(x)dxdz

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{y\in E_{n+1}\right\}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{R}^{j+1}y)dy.

We sum over
i
and shift the index
j
by one to get

\lambda^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}t^{j-1}(1-t)\int_{\{y\in E_{n+1}\}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{R}^{j}y)dy.

Third term:
We have

\displaystyle\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}t^{i-1}\int_{E_{n}}f(\mathcal{L}^{i}x)g(x)dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}t^{j-1}(1-t)\int_{E_{n}}f(\mathcal{L}^{i}x)g(x)dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}t^{j-1}(1-t)\int_{\{y\in E_{n-1},s\in[0,T_{1}],y_{i-1}\leq s\leq y_{i}\}}f(y)g(\mathcal{L}^{j}_{s}y)dyds

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}t^{j-1}(1-t)\int_{\{y\in E_{n-1},s\in[0,T_{1}],j>k(s)\}}f(y)g(\mathcal{L}^{j}_{s}y)dyds.

Fourth term:
We have

\displaystyle\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}\int_{E_{n}}f(\mathcal{L}^{i}x)g(x)dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}\int_{E_{n}}f(y)g(\mathcal{R}^{i}y)dy.

We collect the four terms and relabel the index
j
by
i
if necessary to obtain

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{y\in E_{n},s\in[0,T_{1}],i\leq k(s)\right\}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{L}_{s}^{i}y)dyds

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{E_{n+1}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{R}^{i}y)dy

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\{y\in E_{n-1},s\in[0,T_{1}],i>k(s)\}}f(y)g(\mathcal{L}^{i}_{s}y)dyds

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}\int_{E_{n}}f(y)g(\mathcal{R}^{i}y)dy.

Finally, we re-index each term so that all the integrals are over
E_{n}
instead of
E_{n-1}
or
E_{n+1}
:

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\left\{y\in E_{n},s\in[0,T_{1}],i\leq k(s)\right\}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{L}_{s}^{i}y)dyds

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{E_{n}}f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{R}^{i}y)dy

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)\int_{\{y\in E_{n},s\in[0,T_{1}],i>k(s)\}}f(y)g(\mathcal{L}^{i}_{s}y)dyds

\displaystyle\quad+\lambda^{n-1}\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}\int_{E_{n}}f(y)g(\mathcal{R}^{i}y)dy.

Multiplying everything back by
e^{-\lambda T_{1}}
and summing over
n
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{E}G_{+}f(x)g(x)\mu(dx)=e^{-\lambda T_{1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda T_{1}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\lambda^{n}\int_{E_{n}}\int_{0}^{T_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}(1-t)f\left(y\right)g(\mathcal{L}^{i}_{s}y)dsdy+\lambda^{n-1}\int_{E_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}t^{i-1}f(y)g(\mathcal{R}^{i}y)dy\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{E}f(y)G_{+}^{*}g(y)\mu(dy),

where
G_{+}^{*}
is the dual of
G_{+}
.
By (
B.1
), we have

G^{*}g=G_{+}^{*}g-\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda(1-t)}+T_{1}\Big)g,

which concludes the proof.

∎
