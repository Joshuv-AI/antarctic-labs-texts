Unlocking the Resonant Structure of the New Poisson Equation Preprint: APS/1-DFIST

Abstract
This paper presents a modified form of the Poisson equation derived from a variational method. The equation can model resonant structures and various physical systems and can be solved using numerical techniques like finite element, boundary element, and spectral methods. Modifications to the Poisson equation can improve predictions and safety protocols. Related works on efficient solvers for electrostatic potential and nonlocal generalization of Einstein’s theory of gravitation are also discussed.



I
Introduction

The Poisson equation is a fundamental partial differential equation in mathematical physics
Poisson
;
Maxwell
that finds broad applications in the modeling of various physical systems. The equation is a second-order partial differential equation that can be solved using a range of analytical and numerical techniques.

This new form of the Poisson equation is derived from a variational method that balances the tendency of energy to be minimized and entropy to tend towards a maximum. The resulting set of differential equations offers novel opportunities for understanding and interacting with the physical world around us. It provides a more generalized description of resonant structures. It can be a useful tool for modeling a range of physical systems, including acoustic, heat transfer, and electromagnetic systems. Additionally, it can model resonant structures such as those found in nuclear models
Myers
, inter-galactic gravitational fields
Wright
, spintronics
Zutic
, and the emergence of longitudinal electromagnetic waves
Serdyukov
. The solution of the equation allows for the determination of the frequencies at which these physical systems will vibrate and the effect that will be produced.

Finite element methods, boundary element methods, and spectral methods are among the numerical techniques available for solving the new form of the Poisson equation. The selection of a particular method depends on the specific problem at hand. For instance, finite element methods are more suitable for problems involving complex geometries, whereas boundary element methods are more efficient for simple geometries. Spectral methods are ideal for problems with periodic boundary conditions and can solve the new Poisson equation quickly and accurately (see, e.g., Refs.
Gilbarg
;
Reddy
;
Mammoli
;
Trefethen
).

A modification of the Poisson equation can benefit the potential applications of this method because it allows for a more accurate and precise description of physical systems. By considering nonlocal effects, the modified Poisson equation can provide a better understanding of complex systems and enable more efficient and reliable energy management systems. It can also lead to improved safety protocols for hazardous environments by providing more accurate predictions of the behavior of these systems.

III.5
Generation of electron beams carrying orbital angular momentum

The generation of electron beams carrying orbital angular momentum (OAM) refers to the creation of electron beams that possess a well-defined helical phase front, similar to a spiral staircase. This helical structure of the electron beam arises due to the presence of OAM, which is a property of a wave that describes the rotation of the wave’s phase around its axis of propagation.

One way to generate electron beams carrying OAM is by using electron holography, which is a technique that allows for the measurement and manipulation of electron wavefronts. In electron holography, a beam of electrons is split into two paths, with one path being used as a reference wave and the other passing through a sample. The two beams are then recombined, resulting in an interference pattern that contains information about the electron wavefront. By manipulating the electron wavefront using holography, it is possible to introduce a helical phase shift into the electron beam, thereby generating an electron beam carrying OAM. Such electron beams have been used in a variety of applications, such as electron microscopy and nanofabrication, and have the potential to revolutionize fields such as quantum computing and communication. We will demonstrate how this formalism can be used to address this inquiry.
