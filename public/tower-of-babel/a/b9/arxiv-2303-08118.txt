Abstract
The multi-type Moran process is an evolutionary process on a connected graph G G in which each vertex has one of k k types and, in each step, a vertex v v is chosen to reproduce its type to one of its neighbours. The probability of a vertex v v being chosen for reproduction is proportional to the fitness of the type of v v . So far, the literature was almost solely concerned with the 2 2 -type Moran process in which each vertex is either healthy (type 0 0 ) or a mutant (type 1 1 ), and the main problem of interest has been the (approximate) computation of the so-called fixation probability , i.e., the probability that eventually all vertices are mutants. In this work we initiate the study of approximating fixation probabilities in the multi-type Moran process on general graphs. Our main result is an FPTRAS (fixed-parameter tractable randomised approximation scheme) for computing the fixation probability of the dominant mutation; the parameter is the number of types and their fitnesses. In the course of our studies we also provide novel upper bounds on the expected absorption time , i.e., the time that it takes the multi-type Moran process to reach a state in which each vertex has the same type.



1
Introduction

The study of the (
2
-type) Moran process dates back to the late 1950s
[
16
]
with the goal of modelling and understanding how an advantageous mutation spreads through a finite community: In its initial form, the process starts with
n
individuals,
n-1
of which are healthy (fitness
1
), and one of which is a “mutant” with an advantage (fitness
r>1
). In each step of the process, an individual is chosen with probability proportional to its fitness and replaces the type of another individual, which is chosen uniformly at random, with its own type (healthy or mutant). This process becomes stable if either all individuals are mutants, or if the mutation is extinct. It is well-known that the probability that the mutation takes over, which is called the
fixation probability
, is
(1-r)/(1-r^{n})
(see e.g. Lieberman, Hauert, and Nowak
[
14
]
).

The Moran process was later generalised to communities with a spatial structure
[
14
,
17
]
. In the generalisation, individuals are vertices of an
n
-vertex graph
G
and each vertex can only replace the type of its neighbours. Moreover, the single mutant at the start of the process is chosen uniformly at random from all vertices. For this
spatial
Moran process, the fixation probability crucially depends on the structure of the graph. This quantity can be calculated explicitly, but the number of equations for doing so would grow exponentially in the graph’s size. Hence, Díaz, Goldberg, Mertzios, Richerby, Serna and Spirakis
[
5
]
constructed a
fully polynomial randomised approximation scheme (FPRAS)
for the associated computational problem.
This means that they gave an algorithm that takes as input a graph
G
, a mutation fitness
r\geq 1
, and real numbers
\varepsilon
and
\delta
in the interval
(0,1)
that guide the the accuracy of the approximation. With probability at least
1-\delta
,
the algorithm outputs an
\varepsilon
-approximation of the fixation probability, which means a number which is within
1\pm\varepsilon
of the fixation probability. According to the definition of FPRAS, the running time of this algorithm is bounded from above by a polynomial in
|G|
,
1/\varepsilon
and
\log(1/\delta)
. For more related work on the
2
-type Moran process including improved FPRASes, see Section
1.4
.

Much less is known about the Moran process
when there is more than one type of mutation. Although the multi-type Moran process has been considered for the special case of complete graphs by Ferreira and Neves
[
8
]
, there is, to the best of our knowledge, no work on the multi-type Moran process on general graphs.

In the setting of multiple types, mutated individuals will compete not only against healthy individuals, but different mutation lineages will also fight for dominance against each other. For example, this is the case in the formation of cancer in which typically a tumour contains two to eight “driver” mutations and hundreds of “passenger” mutations (see e.g. Vogelstein, Papadopoulos, Velculescu, Zhou, Díaz, and Kinzler
[
18
]
).
For this reason, we initiate in this work the study of the multi-type Moran process on general graphs. The goal is to compute the fixation probability of the dominant mutant, i.e., the type with maximum fitness: If this probability is small enough, then we can hope for the healthy individuals/cells to eventually take over.

Our main result is an efficient algorithm for approximating the fixation probability of the dominant mutant.
For the formal statement of our results, we will next introduce the process in detail.

1.1
The Multi-Type Moran Process

The graphs that we consider in this work are simple, undirected, and without loops. Given a vertex
v
of a graph
G=(V,E)
, we write
N(v):=\{u\mid\{u,v\}\in E\}
for the (open) neighbourhood of
v
, and we write
d(v):=|N(v)|
for the degree of
v
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex graph. Let
\tau
be a finite set of types
and let
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}
be an element of
\tau
.
Intuitively,
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}
is the “ordinary” type — all other types in
\tau
are mutations.
Let
f
be a function from
\tau
to
\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
.
We refer to
f
as a “fitness function” because we use it to
measure the “fitness” of the types in
\tau
.
Let
f^{+}=\max_{i\in\tau}f(i)
and
{\tau_{f^{+}}}=\{i\in\tau\mid f(i)=f^{+}\}
.
Similarly, let

f^{-}=\min_{i\in\tau}f(i)
and
{\tau_{f^{-}}}=\{i\in\tau\mid f(i)=f^{-}\}
.
In this work, we will
study the case where mutations are advantageous, so
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}\in{\tau_{f^{-}}}
.
We say that
f
is an
advantageous fitness function
for
\tau
and
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}
in this case.

Let
\Omega
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau
.
The elements of
\Omega
will be the states that we study — each state assigns a type to every vertex of
G
.
Given a state
M_{0}\in\Omega
, the

Moran process

corresponding to
G
,
\tau
,
f
and
M_{0}
is a Markov Chain
M=M_{0},M_{1},\ldots
with state space
\Omega

and start state
M_{0}
.

Before defining the transitions of
M
, we give some notation. If
S
is a state in
\Omega

and
v
and
w
are vertices in
V
then
S|_{v\rightarrow w}
is the state
S^{\prime}

such that
S^{\prime}(w)=S(v)
and, for all
u\in V\setminus\{w\}
,
S^{\prime}(u)=S(u)
.
Informally,
S^{\prime}
is derived from
S
by reproducing the type of
v
onto the type of
w
. For example, if
v
is a mutation type, then this mutation spreads to its neighbour
w
in
S|_{v\rightarrow w}
.

We refer to
M_{t}
as the state of the Markov Chain
M
at (discrete) time
t
.
For any type
j\in\tau
, we write
V_{j}(t)
for the set of vertices that are assigned type
j
at time
t
, that is,
V_{j}(t):=\{v\in V\mid M_{t}(v)=j\}
. To simplify the notation, we set
f(v,t):=f(M_{t}(v))
for the fitness of
the type of
v
at time
t
. Given a subset
U\subseteq V
, we write

f(U,t):=\sum_{v\in U}f(v,t)
to denote
the total fitness of the vertices in
U
at time
t
. The
total fitness

F(t)
at time
t
is the total fitness of all vertices, that is,
F(t):=f(V,t)
.

The transition from
M_{t}
to
M_{t+1}
is defined as follows.
A random vertex
v\in V
is chosen with probability
f(v,t)/F(t)
.
A vertex
w\in N(v)
is chosen with probability
1/d(v)
.
Then
M_{t+1}:=M_{t}|_{v\rightarrow w}
.
Intuitively, in each transition, a vertex
v
is chosen with probability proportional to the fitness of its type at time
t
and a vertex
w
is chosen uniformly at random from the neighbours of
v
. Then the type of
w
is replaced with the type of
v
.

If
M_{t}
assigns the same type to all vertices then the state of the Markov chain
M
does not change after time
t
.
We say that type
j\in\tau

fixates
by time
t
if

M_{t}
assigns type
j
to all vertices.
If
G
is connected, then with probability
1
, some type in
\tau
fixates.
We use
\pi_{j}
to denote the probability that
j
fixates.

If we want to clarify
G
,
\tau
,
f
, and
M_{0}
,
then we refer to
M
as
M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
and we refer to
\pi_{j}
as

\pi_{j}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.

Sometimes it will be useful to consider a distribution on the start state
M_{0}
.
If
D
is a distribution on
\Omega
then

\pi_{j}(G,\tau,f,D)=\sum_{M_{0}\in\Omega}\Pr_{D}(M_{0})\,\pi_{j}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.

For our purposes, not every distribution
D
on
\Omega
will be important.
In the well-studied case where

|\tau|=2
and the fitness function is advantageous (so

\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}\in{\tau_{f^{-}}}
)
the idea is that the type
\alpha\in\tau\setminus\{\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}\}

is a “mutant” type with a high fitness that arises as a result
of a single mutation. It is therefore appropriate
to consider
the
case where the start state
M_{0}
is drawn from the
distribution
D_{\mathrm{mut}}=D_{\mathrm{mut}}(G,\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{ord}})

which is uniform on states
M_{0}\in\Omega
in which
exactly one vertex has the type in
\tau\setminus\{\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}\}
.
This is the distribution that has been studied in previous works.

There is a natural generalisation of
D_{\mathrm{mut}}
to the multi-type Moran process
where
|\tau|=k>2
. In this case,
D_{\mathrm{mut}}
is the uniform distribution on states
M_{0}\in\Omega
which have exactly one vertex assigned to every type
i\in\tau\setminus\{\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}\}
.

Our results will apply to
D_{\mathrm{mut}}
and also to several other distributions.
Let
V[k]
be the set containing all
k
-tuples of distinct vertices
from
V
and
let
\tau[k]
be the set containing all
k
-tuples of distinct types in
\tau
.
For every
\mathbf{u}\in V[k]
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\tau[k]
,
let
\Omega(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
be the set of states in
\Omega

that map vertices in
\mathbf{u}
respectively to types in
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
When a state
S\in\Omega
is drawn from
D_{\mathrm{mut}}
, there is a clear lower bound on the probability that
S\in\Omega(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
. Our results will apply to any distribution with this property.
For concreteness, we identify a set of suitable distributions as follows.
The set of distributions
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(G,\tau)
is
the set containing every distribution
D
on
\Omega

that meets the following criteria.

•

For every pair
(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\in V[k]\times\tau[k]
,

D_{\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is a distribution on
\Omega(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
.

•

For any
S\in\Omega
,

\Pr_{D}(S)=\frac{1}{|V[k]\times\tau[k]|}\sum_{(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\in V[k]\times\tau[k]}\Pr_{D_{\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(S)
.

It is easy to see that
D_{\mathrm{mut}}(G,\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{ord}})\in\mathcal{D}(G,\tau)
since, in this case,
D_{\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
can be taken to be the distribution containing the single state in

\Omega(\mathbf{u},\boldsymbol{\gamma})

such that every vertex outside of
\mathbf{u}
is assigned type
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}
.

Let
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
be the set of all states
S\in\Omega

with range
\tau
. By construction, any sample
S
drawn from a distribution
D\in\mathcal{D}(G,\tau)
is in
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
.

1.2
Parameterised and Approximation Algorithms

We will study the following problems concerned with the fixation probability of a type with maximum fitness.

Definition 1

(
\textsc{MaxFixProb}_{\mathrm{mut}}
)
.

The problem
\textsc{MaxFixProb}_{\mathrm{mut}}
takes as input a connected graph
G
, a set of types
\tau
with ordinary type
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}\in\tau
, an advantageous fitness function
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
, and a type
\alpha\in{\tau_{f^{+}}}
.
The goal is to compute
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D_{\mathrm{mut}}(G,\tau,\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}))
.

Definition 2

(
MaxFixProb
)
.

The problem
MaxFixProb
takes as input a connected graph
G
, a set of types
\tau
, a fitness function
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
, a type
\alpha\in{\tau_{f^{+}}}
and a distribution
D\in\mathcal{D}(G,\tau)
(via a black-box oracle which provides samples from
D
).
The goal is to compute
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D)
.

As explained in the Introduction, we are interested in efficient approximation algorithms for
MaxFixProb
and
\textsc{MaxFixProb}_{\mathrm{mut}}
.

As is usual in the setting of parameterised algorithms
(and is natural in the case of this application),
we will assume that the number of types (and the encoding of their fitnesses) is significantly smaller than the size of the
input graph
G
, which we denote by
|G|
. Thus, we are happy to accept a factor
in the running time that depends on
\tau
and
f
, say
g(\tau,f)
— but we would not be happy with an algorithm whose running time is as high as
|G|^{g(\tau,f)}
for any function
g
.

Formally, we let
||f||
be the description length of the fitness function
f
. The exact details about how
f
is encoded will not be important here. We
parameterise
our problems in terms of the parameter
\kappa:=|\tau|+||f||
and we aim to construct a
fixed-parameter tractable approximation scheme (FPTRAS)
, which is the standard notion for efficient parameterised approximation algorithms
(see Arvind and Raman
[
1
]
).

Definition 3

(FPTRAS)
.

A
fixed-parameter tractable randomised approximation scheme
(FPTRAS) for a problem
P
is a randomised algorithm
\mathbb{A}
that takes as input
a problem
input
x
and reals
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
, and outputs
\hat{P}
such that

\Pr((1-\varepsilon)P(x)\leq\hat{P}\leq(1+\varepsilon)P(x))\geq 1-\delta
.
Moreover, there must be a computable function
g
such that the running time of
\mathbb{A}
is bounded from above by

g(\kappa)\cdot\mathsf{poly}(|x|,1/\varepsilon,\log(1/\delta))
, where
\kappa
is the parameter of
x
.

1.3
Main Results

We are now able to state our main results.

Theorem 4
.

MaxFixProb
has an FPTRAS when parameterised by
\kappa:=|\tau|+||f||
.

Theorem 5
.

\textsc{MaxFixProb}_{\mathrm{mut}}
has an FPTRAS when parameterised by
\kappa:=|\tau|+||f||
.

In the course of proving Theorems
4
and
5
we also obtain upper bounds on the absorption time, i.e. the time that the process runs until a type fixates.
For the
multi-type Moran process, this upper bound is of the form
g(|\tau|+||f||)\cdot\mathsf{poly}(|G|)
(see Theorem
18
).

1.4
Related Work

Approximation algorithms for the 2-type spatial Moran process

Work on approximating the fixation probability in the spatial Moran process has so far focused on the 2-type process, for which Díaz et al.
[
5
]
developed the first FPRAS. Denoting the types by
\tau=\{1,2\}
with
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}=1
being the ordinary type, the idea to approximate the fixation probability
\pi_{2}
of the mutation is to perform sufficiently many Markov chain Monte Carlo simulations of the process, where the number of steps and simulations can be bounded by proving (a) an upper bound on the expected absorption time and (b) a lower bound on the fixation probability
\pi_{2}
.
Their work applies to the case where the mutation is advantageous, so
{\tau_{f^{-}}}=\{1\}
and
f(2)>f(1)
. Note that when the mutation is disadvantageous, its fixation probability
\pi_{2}
can be exponentially small as a function of the size of the graph, even when the graph is the complete graph, so this approach to approximation is infeasible.

In a first improvement, Chatterjee, Ibsen-Jensen, and Nowak
[
4
]
exploit the observation that a vertex reproducing to a neighbour of the same type does not change the overall state. By simulating only those steps that change the underlying Markov chain’s state, they speed the FPRAS up.

The effect of
f(2)/f(1)
on the fixation probability
\pi_{2}
in
every
connected, undirected graph was characterised by Goldberg, Lapinskas, and Richerby
[
10
]
by demonstrating a phase transition in
\pi_{2}
as a function of
f(2)/f(1)
. This phase transition also entails an improved lower bound on
\pi_{2}
in the advantageous case. Combining this bound with early termination of the Monte Carlo simulation once fixation is sufficiently likely, they provide a faster FPRAS.

Multiple types in related models

The idea of modelling multiple mutation types has been explored for both the Wright-Fisher model
[
2
]
and, more recently, the non-spatial Moran process
[
7
,
8
,
3
]
.
The Wright-Fisher model
[
9
,
19
]
is a non-spatial model with a coarse-grained time scale where all individuals reproduce simultaneously, resulting in non-overlapping generations. This model served as the predecessor of the more fine-grained Moran process; see Lanchier
[
13
]
for a detailed definition and comparison to the non-spatial Moran process.

For the Wright-Fisher model, Beerenwinkel, Antal, Dingli, Traulsen, Kinzler, Velculescu, Vogelstein and Nowak
[
2
]
provide a generalisation to account for the evolution of multiple types: They consider a sequentially ordered set
\tau
of
infinitely many types, where higher types have higher fitness. Initially, all individuals are of some ordinary type
\tau_{\mathrm{ord}}
. Besides reproduction happening generation by generation, individuals can progress to a higher type at some rate
which depends on how close the initial type is to the new one in the ordering of
\tau
.
Beerenwinkel et al. perform computer simulations of this process which models the evolution of cancer cells. Their goal is to approximate the expected
time until a cancer phenotype appear, which they define as the waiting time
until some fixed type occurs as a mutation. The focus of their model and simulations is thus on the progression of types.

Etheridge and Griffiths
[
7
]
define for the non-spatial Moran process a finite set of types
\tau
where, in addition to the reproduction dynamics,
mutation happens between different types at given rates. Chao and Schweinsberg
[
3
]
extend the study to a population on a continuous torus, where the focus is on the progression between types (similar to Beerenwinkel et al.
[
2
]
).

For the Moran process without spatial dynamics and without progression between types, Ferreira and Neves
[
8
]
introduce in recent work the concept of multiple types.
Their model is the same as ours except that they focus on the special case where the graph
G
is the complete graph.
They provide bounds on the fixation probabilities in the 3-type case. In the context of our work on the
spatial
Moran process, these bounds can be derived by applying our coupling in the special case
where
G
is a complete graph and
|\tau|=3
, as we show in Lemma
22
.

2
The Continuous Moran Process

Although our main results are about the discrete-time Moran process, it is useful in proofs to consider a continuous-time version of the Moran process.

We use
\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)
to denote an exponential distribution with parameter
\lambda
.
Let
M
be the (discrete-time) Moran process
corresponding to
G=(V,E)
,
\tau
,
f
, and
M_{0}
.
Following the work of Diaz, Goldberg, Richerby, and Serna
[
6
]

in the case where
|\tau|=2
, we define
a continuous-time Moran process
\tilde{M}=\tilde{M}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
corresponding to
M
.
The process
\tilde{M}
has the same state space as
M
,
namely the set of function
\Omega
from
V
to
\tau
.
and it starts with
\tilde{M}_{0}=M_{0}
.
The evolution of
\tilde{M}
is guided by a sequence
T_{0},T_{1},\ldots
of non-negative real numbers with
T_{0}=0
.
For any non-negative integer
i
, the
evolution from
\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}
proceeds as follows.
Each vertex
v\in V
is equipped with a “clock”
c^{i}_{v}
which is an exponentially distributed random variable with parameter
\tilde{f}(v,T_{i}):=f(\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}(v))
. The distributions of the clocks
c^{i}_{v}

are mutually independent. Let
\Delta_{i}:=\min_{w\in V}c^{i}_{w}
and
T_{i+1}=T_{i}+\Delta_{i}
.
For
t\in[T_{i},T_{i+1})
, we define
\tilde{M}_{t}=\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}
.
We define
\tilde{M}_{T_{i+1}}

as follows. Let
v:=\arg\min\{c^{i}_{u}\mid u\in V\}
.
Choose
w\in N(v)
with probability
1/d(v)
.
Then

\tilde{M}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}|_{v\rightarrow w}
.

The reason that Díaz et al.
[
6
]
chose the exponential distribution for the clocks
is that it is memoryless, so it is convenient for couplings.
Moreover, the minimum of a set
S
of exponential distributions is
exponentially distributed with a new parameter that is the sum of the parameters in
S
. Therefore, for all
v
and
T_{i}
,

\Pr(\Delta_{i}=c^{i}_{v})={\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})}/{\tilde{F}(T_{i})}
where
\tilde{F}(T_{i})=\sum_{v\in V}\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})
. This is the same as the probability that
v
is chosen for reproduction
in a discrete step starting from
\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}
.
Thus, the process
\tilde{M}_{T_{0}},\tilde{M}_{T_{1}},\ldots
is a faithful copy of
M
.
It follows that
\tilde{\pi}_{j}(G,\tau,f,\tilde{M}_{0})
, the probability that
j
fixates in
\tilde{M}
, is equal to
\pi_{j}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.

3
Coupling Continuous Multi-Type Moran Processes

The following lemma is a generalisation of
[
6
, Lemma 5]
, which was for the case
k=2
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, let
\tau
be a set of types, and let
f,f^{\prime}\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be fitness functions. Suppose that
there is a type
\alpha\in\tau
with
f(\alpha)=f^{\prime}(\alpha)
and that, for any types
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}
,

f(\beta)\leq f^{\prime}(\beta^{\prime})
.
Let
\tilde{M}_{0}
and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{0}
be states in the set
\Omega
of functions from
V
to
\tau
.
Let
\tilde{M}=\tilde{M}(G,\tau,f,\tilde{M}_{0})
and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}=\tilde{M}^{\prime}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{0})
be continuous-time Moran processes. For all
j\in\tau
, let
\tilde{V}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid\tilde{M}_{t}(v)=j\}
and

\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{t}(v)=j\}
.
Suppose that
V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(0)\subseteq V_{\alpha}(0)
.
Then
there is a coupling between
\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}
such that, for any time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\subseteq V_{\alpha}(t)
.

Proof.

For convenience, let

\tilde{f}(v,T_{i}):=f(\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}(v))
and

\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i}):=f(\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i}}(v))
.
The joint process is guided by a sequence
T_{0},T_{1},\dots
of non-negative real numbers with
T_{0}=0
. At time
T_{0}
, both processes are in their respective initial states
\tilde{M}_{0}
and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{0}
. For any non-negative integer
i
, the evolution of the joint process from time
T_{i}
is defined as follows. First, we define some exponentially-distributed random variables.

\displaystyle\forall v\in V:\quad C^{0}_{v}(i)

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\min\left(\tilde{f}(v,T_{i}),\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})\right)).

\displaystyle\forall v\in V\text{ with }\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})>\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i}):\quad C_{v}^{1}(i)

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})-\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})).

\displaystyle\forall v\in V\text{ with }\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})<\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i}):\quad C_{v}^{2}(i)

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})-\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})).

The earliest of these random variables is the random variable
\hat{\Delta}(i)
defined by

\hat{\Delta}(i):=\min\left\{C^{0}_{v}(i)\mid v\in V\right\}\cup\left\{C_{v}^{1}(i)\mid\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})>\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})\right\}\cup\left\{C_{v}^{2}(i)\mid\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})<\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})\right\}.

Let
T_{i+1}=T_{i}+\hat{\Delta}(i)
. The two chains evolve from
T_{i}
as follows.
Both
\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}
stay in their respective states over the interval
[T_{i},T_{i+1})
.
At time
T_{i+1}
the state changes as follows.

Case 0.

If
\hat{\Delta}(i)=C^{0}_{v}(i)
for some
v\in V
,
select
w\in N(v)
u.a.r. and set
\tilde{M}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}|_{v\rightarrow w}

and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i}}|_{v\rightarrow w}

Case 1.

If
\hat{\Delta}(i)=C_{v}^{1}(i)
for some
v\in V
, select
w\in N(v)
u.a.r and set
\tilde{M}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}|_{v\rightarrow w}

and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i}}
.

Case 2.

If
\hat{\Delta}(i)=C_{v}^{2}(i)
for some
v\in V
, select
w\in N(v)
u.a.r and
set
\tilde{M}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}_{T_{i}}

and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i+1}}:=\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{T_{i}}|_{v\rightarrow w}
.

It is easy to see that this joint evolution is a correct evolution for the individual chains
\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{M}^{\prime}
. For example, consider
\tilde{M}
(the argument for
\tilde{M}^{\prime}
is symmetric). We will show that for every
v\in V
, the time until
v
is chosen for reproduction is exponentially distributed with parameter
\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})
.
If
\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})\leq\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})
this follows since
C^{0}_{v}(i)

is exponentially distributed with parameter
\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})
and
\tilde{M}
does not evolve in Case 2.
Otherwise, it follows since

\min\{C_{v}^{0}(i),C_{v}^{1}(i)\}

is exponentially distributed with parameter
\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})
.
Now assume
V_{{\alpha}}^{\prime}(T_{i})\subseteq V_{{\alpha}}(T_{i})
. To finish the proof, we will show
V_{{\alpha}}^{\prime}(T_{i+1})\subseteq V_{{\alpha}}(T_{i+1})
. Specifically, we
will rule out the possibility that
w\in V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i+1})\setminus V_{\alpha}(T_{i+1})
.

In Case 0 the only way that
w\in V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i+1})
is that

v\in V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i})
. This implies

v\in V_{\alpha}(T_{i})
so

w\in V_{\alpha}(T_{i+1})
, as required.

In Case 1 the only way that
w\in V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i+1})\setminus V_{\alpha}(T_{i+1})

is that

v\notin V_{\alpha}(T_{i})
so
v\notin V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i})
. But then Case 1 requires

\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})>\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})
and this is not possible since
v\notin V_{\alpha}(T_{i})

and
v\notin V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i})

(given the condition about types
\beta,\beta^{\prime}
in the lemma statement).

In Case 2 the only way that
w\in V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i+1})
is that

v\in V_{\alpha}^{\prime}(T_{i})
so
v\in V_{\alpha}(T_{i})
. But then we do not have
\tilde{f}(v,T_{i})<\tilde{f}^{\prime}(v,T_{i})
as required for Case 2.
∎

Corollary 7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, let
\tau
be a set of types, and let
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function. Let
\alpha
be a type in
\tau
.
Let
\beta=\arg\max\{f(j)\mid j\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}\}
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}=\{\alpha,\beta\}
.
Let
\Omega
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau
and let
\Omega^{\prime}
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau^{\prime}
. Let
g\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be defined as follows.
For every state
S\in\Omega
,
g(S)
is the state
such that
g(S)(v)=\alpha
if
S(v)=\alpha
and
g(S)(v)=\beta
, otherwise.
Then, for every
state
M_{0}\in\Omega
,
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\geq\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau^{\prime},f,g(M_{0}))
.

Proof.

There are three steps to the argument. First let
f^{\prime}\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}

be the fitness function with
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=\alpha
and, for any
\beta^{\prime}\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}
,

f^{\prime}(\beta^{\prime})=f(\beta)
.
Note that for any
M_{0}\in\Omega
,

\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau^{\prime},f,g(M_{0}))=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
, so the goal is to
prove
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\geq\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.

Second, let
\tilde{M}=\tilde{M}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
and let
\tilde{M}^{\prime}=\tilde{M}^{\prime}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.
For all
j\in\tau
, let
\tilde{V}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid\tilde{M}_{t}(v)=j\}
and

\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{t}(v)=j\}
.
Applying Lemma
6

we find that
for any time

,
\tilde{V}_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\subseteq\tilde{V}_{\alpha}(t)
.
so

\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\geq\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.

Finally, recall
(from the construction of the continuous Moran process) that

\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})

and

\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.
Putting these together proves the corollary.
∎

.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, let

\Omega

\tau

\Omega^{\prime}

4
Bounding the Absorption Time

The goal of this section is to bound the absorption time of the multi-type Moran process.
It will be convenient to work with connected graphs in this section. We will also start from initial states

M_{0}\in\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
since all distributions in
\mathcal{D}(G,\tau)
are distributions on the state space
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
.

Definition 10

(Absorption time)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, let
\tau
be a set of types, let
f:\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function and let
M_{0}\in\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
be an initial state.
For the Moran process
M=M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})

and for any type
j\in\tau

we define the stopping time

A_{j}(G,\tau,f,M_{0}):=\min\{\mbox{$t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid V_{j}(t)=V$ or $V_{j}(t)=\emptyset$}\}.

We refer to
A_{j}
as the
absorption time
of type
j
in
M
. The
total absorption time
of
M
is defined as

A(G,\tau,f,M_{0}):=\min\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\mbox{ for some
$j\in\tau$, $V_{j}(t)=V$}\}.

If
G,\tau,f
, and
M_{0}
are clear from the context, we will just write
A_{j}
and
A
.

We will upper bound the absorption time for each type, which will then easily translate to an upper bound on the total absorption time. To this end, we start with the most fit type.

4.1
Expected absorption time of the most fit mutant

To upper bound the expected absorption time, we adopt the potential function for the
2
-type process from the work of Díaz et al.
[
5
, Section 3]

adapted for a maximally-fit type in the multi-type process.

Definition 11
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
\tau
be a set of types.
Let
\Omega
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau
.
For any type
j\in\tau
,
the potential function
\Psi_{j}
is the function that maps every state
S\in\Omega

to the value
\Psi_{j}(S)=\sum_{v:S(V)=j}1/d(v)
.

Lemma
12
applies in situations where the most fit type is unique.
The proof is almost identical to the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 5]
, but we include the details for completeness.

Lemma 12
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, let
\tau
be a set of types with
|\tau|=k>1
, and let
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function. Let
\alpha

be a type in
{\tau_{f^{+}}}
.
Let
f^{*}=\max\{f(j)\mid j\in\tau\setminus\alpha\}
, and suppose that
f^{*}<f(\alpha)=f^{+}
.
Suppose that
V_{\alpha}(t)\notin\{\emptyset,V\}
for a non-negative integer
t
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}({\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t+1})-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t})})>(1-{f^{\ast}}/{f^{+}})/n^{3}.

Proof.

First note that
\mathbb{E}(\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t+1})-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t}))
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{u,v\}\in E\\
u\in V_{\alpha}(t)\\
v\in V_{j}(t),j\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{+}}{F(t)}\frac{1}{d(u)}\left(\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t}|_{u\rightarrow v})-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t})\right)+\frac{f(j)}{F(t)}\frac{1}{d(v)}\left(\Psi_{\alpha}\left(M_{t}|_{v\rightarrow u}\right)-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{u,v\}\in E\\
u\in V_{\alpha}(t)\\
v\in V_{j}(t),j\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{+}}{F(t)}\frac{1}{d(u)}\frac{1}{d(v)}-\frac{f(j)}{F(t)}\frac{1}{d(v)}\frac{1}{d(u)}

Next observe that
F(t)<nf^{+}
and
f(j)\leq f^{\ast}
for all
j\in\tau\setminus\alpha
. Thus

\mathbb{E}(\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t+1})-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t}))>\frac{f^{+}-f^{\ast}}{nf^{+}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{u,v\}\in E\\
u\in V_{\alpha}(t)\\
v\in V_{j}(t),j\neq\alpha\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d(u)d(v)}>\left(1-\frac{f^{\ast}}{f^{+}}\right)/n^{3},

concluding the proof.
∎

The technique that we use to upper bound
\mathbb{E}(A_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0}))
relies on
martingale techniques which are made explicit by Hajek
[
11
]
.
Diaz et. al
[
5
, Theorem 6]
used this technique to study the
2
-type process, following the approach of He and Yao
[
12
]
.
For convenience, we use here a multi-type generalisation of the statement from Goldberg et al.
[
10
]
.

Lemma 13

(
[
10
]
Lemma 45)
.

Let
Y
be a Markov Chain with a finite state space
\Omega_{Y}
. Let
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, let
\Psi:\Omega_{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a function, and let
\mathsf{A}\geq 0
be a stopping time with

\mathsf{A}\leq\min\{t\mid\mbox{$\Psi(Y_{t})=0$ or $\Psi(Y_{t})\geq c_{1}$}\}
. Suppose that

(i)

from every state
S_{1}\in\Omega_{Y}
with
0<\Psi(S_{1})<c_{1}
, there is a path in
Y
from
S_{1}
to some state
S_{2}
with
\Psi(S_{2})=0
or
\Psi(S_{2})\geq c_{1}
;

(ii)

for all
t\geq 0
, if
\Psi(Y_{t})<c_{1}
then
\Psi(Y_{t+1})\leq c_{1}+1
; and

(iii)

for all
t\geq 0
and all
S\in\Omega_{Y}
such that the events
\mathsf{A}>t
and
Y_{t}=S
are consistent,
\mathbb{E}(\Psi(Y_{t+1})-\Psi(Y_{t})\mid Y_{t}=S)\geq c_{2}
.

Then we have
\mathbb{E}(\mathsf{A})\leq(c_{1}-\Psi(Y_{0})+1)/c_{2}
.

Corollary 14
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, let
\tau
be a set of types with
|\tau|=k>1
, and let
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function. Let
\alpha

be a type in
{\tau_{f^{+}}}
.
Let
f^{*}=\max\{f(j)\mid j\in\tau\setminus\alpha\}
, and suppose that
f^{*}<f(\alpha)=f^{+}
.
Consider a state
M_{0}\in\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(A_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0}))\leq\frac{f^{+}}{f^{+}-f^{\ast}}\left(n+1\right)n^{3}.

Proof.

Set
c_{1}:=\sum_{v\in V}1/d(v)
and

c_{2}:=(1-{f^{\ast}}/{f^{+}})/n^{3}
.
Let
A_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
and let
\Psi_{\alpha}
be the potential function from Definition
11
.
Let
M=M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.
By construction

A_{\alpha}=\min\{t\mid\mbox{$\Psi(M_{t})=0$ or $\Psi(M_{t})=c_{1}$}\}.

If
V_{\alpha}(0)\in\{V,\emptyset\}
then
A_{\alpha}=0
and we are done.
Otherwise, note that the state space
\Omega
of
M
is finite and that
\Psi_{\alpha}
is non-negative for every state. We now show that all three requirements
(i)-(iii)
of Lemma
13
are satisfied.

For
(i)
, suppose
0<\Psi(M_{t})<c_{1}
for some non-negative integer
t
.
From the definition of
A_{\alpha}
,
V_{\alpha}\not\in\{\emptyset,V\}
. Since
G
is connected,
there is a positive probability that
M
moves from
M_{t}
to a state
S
in which
V_{\alpha}=V
so
\Psi(S)=c_{1}
. For
(ii)
, note that the maximum potential is
c_{1}
.
Finally, for
(iii)
, Lemma
12
ensures that
\mathbb{E}(\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t+1})-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{t}))>c_{2}>0
.

Lemma
13
thus
implies

\mathbb{E}(A_{\alpha})\leq(c_{1}-\Psi_{\alpha}(M_{0})+1)/c_{2}\leq(c_{1}+1)/c_{2}
.
Since
c_{1}\leq n
, the corollary follows.
∎

4.2
Expected total absorption time

Now that we have an upper bound on the expectation of
A_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})

for the most-fit type
\alpha

we use it to derive an upper bound on the expectation of
A_{j}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
for any

j\in\tau
. Our strategy is as follows.
If
j=\alpha
, we are finished. Otherwise after time
A_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})

there are two possibilities. If type
\alpha
has fixated, then
A_{j}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
occurs.
Otherwise, type
\alpha
has become extinct, so we repeat with the new set of types
\tau\setminus\alpha
.
This inductive approach applies to the setting where the fitnesses of the types
are pairwise distinct, which we will assume in the first part of this section. The general result (without this assumption) will be derived afterwards.

When we assume that the fitnesses are distinct we will typically assume that
\tau=\{1,\ldots,k\}
and that
f(1)<f(2)<\dots<f(k)
. This simplifies the notation with no loss of generality. Note that
{\tau_{f^{+}}}=\{k\}
in this case.

Definition 15

(
A_{\geq j}
)
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Let
k
be a positive integer.
Let
\tau=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Let

f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function such that

f(1)<f(2)<\dots<f(k)
.
Let
M_{0}
be a state in
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
and let
M=M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.
For any type
j\in\{2,\dots,k\}
, define

\displaystyle A_{\geq j}:=\min\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\mbox{
$\cup_{i\geq j}V_{i}(t)=\emptyset$ or, for some $i\geq j$, $V_{i}(t)=V$
}\}.

A_{\geq j}
is thus the first point in time when either all types
j,\ldots,k
are extinct or one of them has fixated.

Observation 16
.

Let
G
be a connected graph. Let
k
be a positive integer.
Let
\tau=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Let

f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function such that

f(1)<f(2)<\dots<f(k)
.
Let
M_{0}
be a state in
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
and let
M=M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.
Then

A_{\geq j}
is monotonically decreasing in
j
. That is, for all
j\in\{2,\dots,k\}
,

A_{\geq j}\geq A_{\geq j+1}
.

Proof.

At time
t=A_{\geq j}
, either

\cup_{i\geq j}V_{i}(t)=\emptyset
or, for some
i\geq j
,
V_{i}(t)=V
.
The only case that doesn’t directly imply

A_{\geq j+1}\leq t
(according to the definition)
is
V_{j}(t)=V
but this case implies

\cup_{i\geq j+1}V_{i}(t)=\emptyset
, hence

A_{\geq j+1}\leq t
.
∎

Lemma 17
.

Let
G
be a connected graph with
n
vertices. Let
k
be a positive integer.
Let
\tau=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Let

f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function such that

f(1)<f(2)<\dots<f(k)
.
Let
M_{0}
be a state in
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
and let
M=M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
.
Let
j
be a type in
\{2,\ldots,k\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j})\leq\sum_{i=j}^{k}\frac{f(i)}{f(i)-f(i-1)}(n+1)n^{3}.

Proof.

The lemma is proved by induction on
k-j
.
For the base case
j=k
note that
A_{\geq k}={A_{k}}
. Thus the claim holds
for
j=k
by Corollary
14
.

For the inductive step, assume that the claim holds for
j+1
; we will show that it holds for
j
.

To this end, let
\Omega^{j}
be the set of all states
with no vertices assigned to types
\{j+1,\ldots,k\}
. Using Corollary
14
on initial states in
\Omega^{j}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j}-A_{\geq j+1})\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\Omega^{j}}\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j}-A_{\geq j+1}\mid M_{A_{\geq j+1}}=S)\cdot\Pr(M_{A_{\geq j+1}}=S)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{f(j)}{f(j)-f(j-1)}(n+1)n^{3}\cdot\underbrace{\sum_{S\in\Omega^{j}}\Pr(M_{A_{\geq j+1}}=S)}_{\leq 1}.

Consequently, in combination with the induction hypothesis for
j+1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j}-A_{\geq j+1})+\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f(j)}{f(j)-f(j-1)}(n+1)n^{3}+\mathbb{E}(A_{\geq j+1})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f(j)}{f(j)-f(j-1)}(n+1)n^{3}+\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}\frac{f(i)}{f(i)-f(i-1)}(n+1)n^{3},

concluding the proof.
∎

In the final step, we extend our bounds on the absorption times to processes in which distinct types may have the same fitness.

Theorem 18
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph with
n
vertices, let
\tau
be a set of types with
|\tau|>1
, let

f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function, and let
M_{0}
be a state in
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
.
Suppose that the types in
\tau
have
k
distinct fitnesses and let
f^{-}=f_{1}<f_{2}<\dots<f_{k}=f^{+}
be these fitnesses.
For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, let

\ell_{i}=|f^{-1}(f_{i})|
be the number of types with fitness
f_{i}
and let
\ell=\max\{\ell_{i}\mid i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\}
be the maximum number of types that share the same fitness.
Then

(1)

The total absorption time is bounded by

\mathbb{E}(A(G,\tau,f,M_{0}))\leq(\ell-1)n^{6}+\sum_{i=2}^{k}\frac{f_{i}}{f_{i}-f_{i-1}}(n+1)n^{3}.

(2)

For any
j\in\{2,\dots,k\}
the absorption time of each type
\beta
with
f(\beta)=f_{j}
is bounded by

\mathbb{E}(A_{\beta}(G,\tau,f,M_{0}))\leq(\ell_{j}-1)n^{6}+\sum_{i=j}^{k}\frac{f_{i}}{f_{i}-f_{i-1}}(n+1)n^{3}.

(3)

The absorption time of each type
\beta
with
f(\beta)=f_{1}
is bounded by

\mathbb{E}(A_{\beta}(G,\tau,f,M_{0}))\leq(\ell_{1}-1)n^{6}+\sum_{i=2}^{k}\frac{f_{i}}{f_{i}-f_{i-1}}(n+1)n^{3}.

Proof.

We first treat types with equal fitnesses as the same type.
Let
\tau^{\prime}=\{1,\dots,k\}
and let

f^{\prime}\colon\tau^{\prime}\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}

be the fitness function such that, for each
i\in\tau^{\prime}
,
f^{\prime}(i)=f_{i}
.
Let
\Omega
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau
and
let
\Omega^{\prime}
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau^{\prime}
.
As usual, let
\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
be the set of functions in
\Omega
with range
\tau
and let

\Omega_{0}^{\prime}(G,\tau^{\prime})
be the set of functions in
\Omega^{\prime}
with range
\tau^{\prime}
.

We will define a function
g
from
\Omega
to
\Omega^{\prime}
.
Given
S\in\Omega
, let
g(S)
be the state in
\Omega^{\prime}
defined as follows.
For each
v\in V
, identify the value
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
f(v)=f_{i}
.
Then set
g(S)(v)=i
.
Note that if
S\in\Omega_{0}(G,\tau)
then
S^{\prime}\in\Omega_{0}^{\prime}(G,\tau^{\prime})
.
Let
M^{\prime}_{0}=g(M_{0})
.

Let
M=M(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
and
M^{\prime}=M^{\prime}(G,\tau^{\prime},f^{\prime},M^{\prime}_{0})
.
For any
j\in\tau^{\prime}
, let

V^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid M^{\prime}_{t}(v)=j\}
.
Following Definition
10
and
15
,
define

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle=\min\{\mbox{$t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid V^{\prime}_{j}(t)=V$ or $V^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\emptyset$}\},

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\min\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\mbox{ for some
$j\in\tau$, $V^{\prime}_{j}(t)=V$}\},\text{and}

\displaystyle A^{\prime}_{\geq j}

\displaystyle=\min\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\mid\mbox{
$\cup_{i\geq j}V^{\prime}_{i}(t)=\emptyset$ or, for some $i\geq j$, $V^{\prime}_{i}(t)=V$
}\}.

Since
A^{\prime}_{\geq 2}=A^{\prime}
,
Lemma
17
applied to
M^{\prime}
implies

\mathbb{E}(A^{\prime})\leq\sum_{i=2}^{k}\frac{f_{i}}{f_{i}-f_{i-1}}(n+1)n^{3}.

(1)

Note that
g(M_{0}),g(M_{1}),\ldots
is a faithful copy of the chain
M^{\prime}
.
If
M
and
M^{\prime}
are coupled using this coupling, then at time
A^{\prime}
, all vertices
are assigned types with the same fitness in
M_{A^{\prime}}
.

Consider any state
S\in\Omega
in which vertices are assigned
q
different types, all with the same fitness.
Theorem 11 of the work of Díaz et al.
[
5
]
shows that in the 2-type Moran process,
from any state with 2 types, the expected time until there is at most one type is at most
n^{6}
.
Applying this argument to
S
by distinguishing the first type from the
q-1
others, the expected time until there are at most
q-1
types is at most
n^{6}
.
Since there are at most
\ell
distinct types with any given fitness,

\mathbb{E}(A(G,\tau,f,S))\leq(\ell-1)n^{6}
.
The upper bound (1) on
\mathbb{E}(A(G,\tau,f,M_{0}))
given in the statement of the theorem follows.

In order to prove (2), note that
A^{\prime}_{j}\leq{A^{\prime}}_{\geq j}
.
Lemma

applied to

implies

\mathbb{E}(A^{\prime}_{\geq j})\leq\sum_{i=j}^{k}\frac{f_{i}}{f_{i}-f_{i-1}}(n+1)n^{3}.

The rest of the argument is analogous to the argument for (1).

Finally, for the proof of (3) observe that after time
A^{\prime}
, either type
\beta
is extinct, or all
remaining types have fitness

. The argument is then the same as the other cases. This concludes the proof.
∎

5
Construction of the FPTRAS

Theorem
4
(restated)
.

MaxFixProb
has an FPTRAS when parameterised by
\kappa:=|\tau|+||f||
.

Proof.

Consider first the following algorithm
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
that takes as input a connected graph
G
,
a set of types
\tau
with
|\tau|>1
,
a fitness function
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
,
a type
\alpha\in{\tau_{f^{+}}}
, accuracy parameters
\varepsilon
and
\delta^{\prime}
,
and (via oracle access) a distribution
D\in\mathcal{D}(G,\tau)
.
By Corollary
9
,
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D)\geq 1/n
.

Set
t=\lceil 3\ln(2/\delta^{\prime})n/\varepsilon^{2}\rceil
.
The algorithm
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
performs

t
independent simulations of
M(G,\tau,f,D)
,
stopping the simulation if either
(i) all vertices have type
\alpha
, or (ii) type
\alpha
is extinct.
In each simulation, the initial state is sampled using the oracle for
D
.

Let
f^{\ast}=\max\{f(j)\mid j\in\tau\setminus{\tau_{f^{+}}}\}
.
Corollary
20

implies that the expected time of each simulation is at most

\mathsf{poly}(n)\cdot|\tau|\cdot f(\alpha)/(f(\alpha)-f^{\ast})
.
Let
w
be the number of simulations in which type
\alpha
fixates. The output of algorithm
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
is
w/t
.

For
i\in\{1,\dots,t\}
let
X_{i}
be the indicator for the event that
\alpha
fixates in the
i
’th simulation. Since
\mathbb{E}(X_{i})=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D)\geq 1/n
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}(w/t)=\mathbb{E}(\tfrac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}X_{i})=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D)
. By a Chernoff bound (see e.g. the standard textbook of Mitzenmacher and Upfal
[
15
, Theorem 11.1]
),

\Pr(|w/t-\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D)|<\varepsilon\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D))\geq 1-\delta^{\prime}.

The expected total running time of
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
is bounded from above by
t\cdot\mathsf{poly}(n)\cdot|\tau|\cdot f(\alpha)/(f(\alpha)-f^{\ast})
.
We next obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximation algorithm
\mathbb{A}
from
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
using the following standard construction.
The algorithm
\mathbb{A}
takes as input
G
,
\tau
,
f
,
\alpha
,
\varepsilon
, and
(via oracle access) the distribution
D
.
The algorithm
\mathbb{A}
simulates
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}

with these inputs and with
\delta^{\prime}=1/8
for at most
8t\cdot\mathsf{poly}(n)\cdot|\tau|\cdot f(\alpha)/(f(\alpha)-f^{\ast})
steps. If
\mathbb{A}^{\prime}
terminates within this number of steps,
then algorithm
\mathbb{A}
returns its output.
Otherwise, algorithm
\mathbb{A}
stops the simulation and returns
0
.

By Markov’s inequality, the probability that
\mathbb{A}
stops the simulation is at most
1/8
. Thus, the probability that
\mathbb{A}
returns an
\varepsilon
-approximation to
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,D)
is at least
1-\delta^{\prime}-1/8\geq 3/4
.

The desired FPTRAS takes the median of the outputs after repeating
algorithm
\mathbb{A}

O(\log(1/\delta))
times.
∎

6
Upper-bounding fixation probabilities in terms of the 2-type Moran process

Corollary
7
gives a lower bound on the fixation probability of a type
\alpha
in the multi-type Moran process, based on the fixation probability of
\alpha
in a related two-type Moran process.
It is just as easy to get upper bounds with this idea, as the following corollary shows.

Corollary 21
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, let
\tau
be a set of types, and let
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function. Let
\alpha
be a type in
\tau
.
Let
\beta=\arg\min\{f(j)\mid j\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}\}
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}=\{\alpha,\beta\}
.
Let
\Omega
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau
and let
\Omega^{\prime}
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau^{\prime}
. Let
g\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be defined as follows.
For every state
S\in\Omega
,
g(S)
is the state
such that
g(S)(v)=\alpha
if
S(v)=\alpha
and
g(S)(v)=\beta
, otherwise.
Then, for every
state
M_{0}\in\Omega
,
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\leq\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau^{\prime},f,g(M_{0}))
.

Proof.

Let
f^{\prime}\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}

be the fitness function with
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=\alpha
and, for any
\beta^{\prime}\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}
,

f^{\prime}(\beta^{\prime})=f(\beta)
.
Note that

\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau^{\prime},f,g(M_{0}))=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
, so the goal is to
prove
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\leq\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.

Let
\tilde{M}=\tilde{M}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})
and let
\tilde{M}^{\prime}=\tilde{M}^{\prime}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.
For all
j\in\tau
, let
\tilde{V}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid\tilde{M}_{t}(v)=j\}
and

\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\{v\in V\mid\tilde{M}^{\prime}_{t}(v)=j\}
.
Applying Lemma
6
(with the roles of
f
and
f^{\prime}
reversed)
we find that
for any time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\tilde{V}_{\alpha}^{\prime}(t)\supseteq\tilde{V}_{\alpha}(t)
.
so

\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\leq\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.
As in the proof of Corollary
7
the final step is to observe that

\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})

and

\tilde{\pi}_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})=\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f^{\prime},M_{0})
.
∎

We conclude the paper by using Corollaries
7
and
21

to re-derive the recent bounds of Ferreira and Neves
on fixation probabilities of the 3-type Moran process in the very special case where
G
is a complete graph. Their main result on the Moran process
[
8
, Theorem 4]

corresponds to the special case of Lemma
22
with
\tau=\{A,B,C\}
and
f(A)>f(B)>f(C)
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
n
be a positive integer and let
G=(V,E)
be a complete graph with
n
vertices.
Let
\tau
be a set of types and let
f\colon\tau\to\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 1}
be a fitness function.
Let
\alpha
be a type in
\tau
. Let
\beta^{+}=\arg\max\{f(\beta)\mid\beta\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}\}
and

\beta^{-}=\arg\min\{f(\beta)\mid\beta\in\tau\setminus\{\alpha\}\}
.
Suppose that
f(\alpha)\neq f(\beta^{+})
and
f(\alpha)\neq f(\beta^{-})
.
Let
M_{0}
be a state in the set
\Omega
of functions from
V
to
\tau
.
Let
i
be the number of vertices in
V

which are mapped to type
\alpha
by
M_{0}
. Then

\frac{1-\left(\frac{f(\beta^{+})}{f(\alpha)}\right)^{i}}{1-\left(\frac{f(\beta^{+})}{f(\alpha)}\right)^{n}}\leq\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\leq\frac{1-\left(\frac{f(\beta^{-})}{f(\alpha)}\right)^{i}}{1-\left(\frac{f(\beta^{-})}{f(\alpha)}\right)^{n}}.

Proof.

We first give the lower bound.
Let
\beta=\beta^{+}
.
Let
\tau^{\prime}=\{\alpha,\beta\}
.
Let
\Omega^{\prime}
be the set of functions from
V
to
\tau^{\prime}
. Let
g\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be defined as follows.
For every state
S\in\Omega
,
g(S)
is the state
such that
g(S)(v)=\alpha
if
S(v)=\alpha
and
g(S)(v)=\beta
, otherwise.
Corollary
7
shows that
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau,f,M_{0})\geq\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau^{\prime},f,g(M_{0}))
.
To conclude the proof, we note that
\pi_{\alpha}(G,\tau^{\prime},f,g(M_{0}))
is
equal to the lower bound given in the lemma statement. This well-known fact follows because

is
the probability of
hitting the lower boundary in a biased one-dimensional random walk.
The walk is biased since

. See Lieberman et al.
[
14
]
for details.
The upper bound is the same, except
\beta=\beta^{-}
and Corollary
21
is
used instead of Corollary
7
. ∎
