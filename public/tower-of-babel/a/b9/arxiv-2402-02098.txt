Self-attention Networks Localize When QK-eigenspectrum Concentrates
Han Bao, Ryuichiro Hataya, Ryo Karakida

Abstract
The self-attention mechanism prevails in modern machine learning. It has an interesting functionality of adaptively selecting tokens from an input sequence by modulating the degree of attention localization, which many researchers speculate is the basis of the powerful model performance but complicates the underlying mechanism of the learning dynamics. In recent years, mainly two arguments have connected attention localization to the model performances. One is the rank collapse, where the embedded tokens by a self-attention block become very similar across different tokens, leading to a less expressive network. The other is the entropy collapse, where the attention probability approaches non-uniform and entails low entropy, making the learning dynamics more likely to be trapped in plateaus. These two failure modes may apparently contradict each other because the rank and entropy collapses are relevant to uniform and non-uniform attention, respectively. To this end, we characterize the notion of attention localization by the eigenspectrum of query-key parameter matrices and reveal that a small eigenspectrum variance leads attention to be localized. Interestingly, the small eigenspectrum variance prevents both rank and entropy collapse, leading to better model expressivity and trainability.

Introduction

Transformers have been widely adopted in language modeling
[
28
]
, vision tasks
[
7
,
27
]
, and speech recognition
[
13
]
.
A crucial building block in transformers is the attention mechanism, dating back to
Graves 2013
, which was initially designed to capture long-range signals in sequential inputs by mixing individual tokens but has also been leveraged to capture general structures of input data.
After the fully-attention-based language model has appeared
[
5
]
, the research community gets interested in the functionality and benefits of the attention.
To mention a few, transformers implicitly prefer hierarchical interpretations of input sequences
[
10
]
; store relational knowledge in MLP layers as an associative memory
[
15
]
; its computational graphs tend to be tree-structured
[
18
]
; suddenly capture tree structures of inputs after long training epochs
[
17
]
.
Theoretically, training dynamics analysis explains how to learn spatially correlated patches by vision transformers (ViT)
[
9
]
, select dominant tokens
[
25
]
, store information as an associative memory
[
3
]
, and select max-margin tokens
[
24
]
, whereas
Xie et al. 2022
explains the in-context learning as a process of concept learning in Bayesian inference.

Among many aspects of attention, we specifically focus on
localization
—for a query token, self-attention can select a few relevant tokens only (which we call
localized
attention) or select many tokens
uniformly
.
As attention can be regarded as a token mixer, it plays a pivotal role in studying how it selects tokens to reveal the characteristics of the token embeddings.
To this end, we have the following research questions:
(Q1)
When
is self-attention localized or uniform?
(Q2)
How
does localization affect model performances?

Along this line, previous studies mainly investigated from the model expressivity and training stability perspectives.
On the one hand,
Dong et al. 2021
and
Noci et al. 2022
initiated the discussion of attention localization and theoretically showed that a network with self-attention layers without skip connections exponentially loses the rank of hidden layers; the fact indicates that
the model expressivity shall be immediately lost with more self-attention layers stacked
.
On the other hand,
Zhai et al. 2023
empirically found that attention entropy—averaged Shannon entropy of an attention probability matrix—correlates with training stability.
Specifically, a training loss curve tends to fall into a plateau when attention entropy is low.
Since higher entropy indicates near-uniform attention weights, their finding apparently suggests that
localized attention may lead the learning dynamics to a plateau
.
Up until now, these two failure modes have been discussed independently with slightly different notions of attention localization, and hence, our understanding of the blessing and curse of attention localization remains elusive.

To better comprehend, we characterize self-attention patterns by attention parameter matrices to reconcile the two collapse modes.
We formulate the concept of localization by
signal propagation probability
(
Section

3
), which describes how likely the signal of a specific input token propagates to the gradient of a training objective.
If the signal propagation probability is high for a few numbers of tokens only, attention is regarded to be localized.
We show that the localization mode can be characterized by the eigenspectrum of attention weight matrices (
Section

4
).
Specifically, attention is localized in the above sense when the eigenspectrum of the query-key parameter matrix has a non-zero mean and a small variance.
Furthermore, the small eigenspectrum variance is relevant to both the rank collapse and entropy collapse (
Section

5
), and thus, we give a unified perspective of the two notions of attention collapse.
For this reason, we argue that attention collapse and its performance can be viewed more transparently based on the eigenspectrum variance.
Lastly, we verified the correlation of the eigenspectrum and the model performance in the experiments with the WikiText dataset
[
16
]
by introducing a regularization scheme called
LocAteR
.

Transformer.

Let
\mathbf{X}\coloneqq[\mathbf{x}_{1}\;\mathbf{x}_{2}\;\dots\;\mathbf{x}_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}
be an input with
T
tokens, defined later shortly.
We suppose that all input sequences have the same length
T
, and
T
is occasionally taken sufficiently large.
The
\ell
-th (single-head) self-attention layer is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{S}\bigg(\frac{(\mathbf{X}^{\ell-1})^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{X}^{\ell-1}}{\lambda}\bigg),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}\mathbf{X}^{\ell-1}\mathbf{A}^{\ell},

(2)

where
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the value parameters,
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}(\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the query-key parameters (with joint parametrization),
\lambda>0
is temperature, commonly

, and
\mathbf{S}:\mathbb{R}^{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{T}
is the softmax applied for each row.
In this way, each input token in
\mathbf{X}^{\ell-1}
(embedded by
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}
) is mixed by
\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
.
Then, the transformer block (without layer normalization
[
1
]
) is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbf{U}^{\ell}+\mathbf{X}^{\ell-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{2}}\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{1}}\mathbf{Z}^{\ell}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbf{H}^{\ell}+\mathbf{Z}^{\ell},

where
\mathbf{H}^{\ell}
is a feed-forward net with parameters
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{1}},\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and an (element-wise) activation
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We omit the token embedding layer and set
\mathbf{X}^{0}\coloneqq\mathbf{X}
.
There are two common variants of layer normalization positions, Post-LN
[
28
]
and Pre-LN
[
30
]
, which are applied token-wise after the residual connections (
\mathbf{Z}^{\ell}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\ell+1}
) and before the inputs (
\mathbf{X}^{\ell}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\ell}
), respectively.
Then, the transformer block
\mathbf{X}^{\ell}
is stacked
L
times and
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X})\coloneqq\mathbf{X}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}
is the output.

Learning task.

We focus on causal language modeling, where a model predicts the next token given contextual tokens.
Formally, given
T
contextual tokens
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times T}
, the prediction target is the
(T+1)
-th token
\mathbf{y}\coloneqq\mathbf{x}_{T+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
With the squared loss, the objective is written as follows:

J(\bm{\Theta})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\E\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X})_{T}\|^{2},

where
\bm{\Theta}\coloneqq(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}},\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}},\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{1}},\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{2}})
denotes the model parameter set, and the expectation is taken over input sequences
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
.
Here, our decoding procedure in consideration is to simply choose the embedded last token
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X})_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
The parameters
\bm{\Theta}
are learned by minimizing
J
.
Note that our analysis considers optimizing the query-key parameters jointly.
Although such joint parameterization is less common in practice, it is convenient for the theoretical derivation of the gradients and has been used in several previous studies
[
9
,
25
]
.
Interested readers may refer to a recent work revealing that the joint and separate QK-parametrization lead to different implicit regularizations
[
23
]
.

Picewise linear approximation of softmax.

In this article, we choose to approximate the softmax function
\mathbf{S}
by linearization.
For a
T
-dimensional input
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, the softmax function is defined as

S(\bm{\omega})_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\exp(\omega_{i})}{\sum_{j\in[T]}\exp(\omega_{j})}\text{~~~for all }i\in[T].

For linearization, the Taylor expansion of
S(\bm{\omega})_{i}
around the origin
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\omega}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}
is used, where

\bm{\gamma}^{i}\coloneqq\nabla_{i}\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{0})=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{e}^{i}-\frac{1}{T^{2}}\mathbf{1},\quad\gamma_{0}^{i}\coloneqq S(\mathbf{0})_{i}=\frac{1}{T}.

Then, we approximate
\mathbf{S}
by the piecewise linear function such that
S(\bm{\omega})_{i}\approx\max\{0,\min\{1,\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\}\}=\left\langle{\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{i}
, where

(\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i},\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{i})=\begin{cases}(\mathbf{0},0)&\text{if }\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}<0,\\
(\bm{\gamma}^{i},\gamma_{0}^{i})&\text{if }\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\in[0,1],\\
(\mathbf{0},1)&\text{if }\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}>1.\\
\end{cases}

In the vector form, the piecewise approximation
\mathbf{S}(\bm{\omega})\approx\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}(\bm{\omega})
is given by

\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}(\bm{\omega})=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\bm{\omega}+\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{0},\text{~~where~}\begin{cases}\bm{\Gamma}\coloneqq[\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{1}\;\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{2}\;\dots\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}],\\
\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}=[\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{1},\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{2},\dots,\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{T}]^{\top}.\end{cases}

For notational simplicity, the column vectors of
\bm{\Gamma}
are exceptionally denoted by
\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i}
with superscripts, for which the
\alpha
-th element is written by
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}
.
The difference between
\mathbf{S}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
is illustrated in
Fig.

1
.
Note that a popular alternative to softmax, sparsemax
[
14
]
, is also a piecewise linear function, although the functional form is slightly different from
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
.

Remark 1
.

Each
(\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i},\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{i})
depends on the softmax input
\bm{\omega}
, unlike the Taylor coefficient
(\bm{\gamma}^{i},\gamma_{0}^{i})
being independent from
\bm{\omega}
.
This point matters particularly when we take expectations of terms involving
(\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i},\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{i})
.

Remark 2
.

When
T
is sufficiently large, the coefficient vector
\bm{\gamma}^{i}=T^{-1}\mathbf{e}^{i}+o(1)
.
In this regime,
\gamma^{i}_{\alpha}=o(1)
for any
\alpha\in[T]\setminus\left\{{i}\right\}
, so
\bm{\gamma}^{i}
behaves like a selector of the
i
-th input.
Hence,
\gamma^{i}_{i}=\gamma_{0}^{i}=T^{-1}+o(1)
.

3
Signal propagation probability

We analyze how much each token contributes to the learning dynamics.
To this end, we formalize how much the signal of a specific input token
\mathbf{x}_{i}
propagates to the gradient
\nabla J
.
Remark that this notion is slightly different from the contribution of an input token
\mathbf{x}_{i}
to the model output
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X})_{j}
analyzed by
Kobayashi et al. 2023
recently.

Uniform vs. localized softmax.

The piecewise linear approximation implies that the
i
-th input signal is propagated to the subsequent blocks when
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\in[0,1]
;
otherwise,
\widetilde{S}(\bm{\omega})_{i}=\left\langle{\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{i}=\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{i}
, which hinders the input token
\mathbf{x}_{i}
from contributing to the self-attention layer (
2
).
Thus, we will focus on the following quantity.

Definition 1

(Signal propagation probability)
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
is independent from
\mathbf{X}
.
For
i\in[T]
, the
signal propagation probability
of the
i
-th token is defined as follows:

\rho_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\in[0,1]\right\},

where
\bm{\omega}\coloneqq\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T}/\lambda
and the randomness originates solely from the input tokens
\mathbf{X}
.

When only a few
\rho_{i}
are significantly larger than zero, we can interpret it as localized softmax; in this case, the self-attention (
2
) is dominated by a small number of tokens.
By contrast, most of the tokens contribute to self-attention almost equally if
\rho_{i}
takes a similar value across different
i
; this situation is interpreted as uniform softmax.

Through the lens of gradient.

The signal propagation probability naturally arises in the gradient.
Since the learning dynamics of causal language modeling is governed by the gradient flow of
J
, we can benefit from deriving the gradient of
J
to see how attention affects the learning dynamics.
To keep the derivation concise, we consider a
1
-layer transformer (where we drop the superscripts
\ell
) without layer normalization and simplify the feed-forward net
\mathbf{H}
by supposing the identity activation.
With the approximated softmax
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
, the transformer can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X})_{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}\mathbf{X}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}(\bm{\omega})+\mathbf{x}_{T}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}\mathbf{X}\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\bm{\omega}+\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}\mathbf{X}\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}+\mathbf{x}_{T}\},

where
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{2}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}_{1}}+\mathbf{I}
and
\bm{\omega}\coloneqq\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T}/\lambda
.
For this architecture, the QK-gradient is computed:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}}J

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}\E[\mathbf{X}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{P}\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}]

(3)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{-1}\E[\mathbf{X}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}_{0}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}]+\lambda^{-1}\E[\mathbf{X}\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{q}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}],

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}\mathbf{X},

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\mathbf{x}_{T}-\mathbf{y}).

When
T
is sufficiently large, we can drop asymptotically negligible terms with respect to
T
as detailed in
Appendix

B
, and the QK-gradient (
3
) is simplified as follows:

\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\!\!\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta\in[T]}\!\!\E\left[\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}\widetilde{\gamma}^{j}_{\beta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{j})(\mathbf{x}_{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T})\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}\right],

(4)

where
\check{\mathbf{P}}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}
.

Now, in the gradient term (
4
), the summands with
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{i}
are asymptotically dominant over those with
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}
(with
\alpha\neq i
) because
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{i}=T^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}=o(T^{-1})
.
Additionally, the
i
-th signal propagates when
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{i}>0
, which holds iff
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\in[0,1]
by definition.
Therefore, we are motivated to check the condition
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\in[0,1]
to see whether
\mathbf{x}_{i}
contributes to the gradient (
3
).
The signal propagation probability
\rho_{i}
characterizes its strength.

Summary.

In this section, we introduced the signal propagation probability
\rho_{i}
, which characterizes how likely a given token
\mathbf{x}_{i}
contributes to the learning dynamics.
Specifically,
\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{i}\neq\mathbf{0}
holds more likely with larger
\rho_{i}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
contributes to the QK-gradient (
3
).
Subsequently, we will analyze the quantity
\rho_{i}
to see the behavior of the probability vector
\bm{\rho}\in[0,1]^{T}
.
When does the mass of
\bm{\rho}
concentrate to only a few tokens or scatter across most of the tokens?

4
When does attention localize?

We derive the signal propagation probability
\rho_{i}
based on the following synthetic data model for the sake of clarity.

Assumption 1

(Random walk)
.

The tokens
(\mathbf{x}_{t})_{t\geq 1}
are generated by the following Gaussian random walk:

\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}),\quad\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\bm{\Sigma}).

To derive
\rho_{i}
, we resort to the Gaussian approximation of
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}
.
Define

\mu^{i}\coloneqq\E[\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}]\text{~~and~~}v^{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{V}[\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}].

We approximately suppose that
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{i},v^{i})
.
Then, the signal propagation probability is approximated:

\rho_{i}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left\{{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{erf}}\left(\frac{1-\mu^{i}}{\sqrt{2v^{i}}}\right)+{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{erf}}\left(\frac{\mu^{i}}{\sqrt{2v^{i}}}\right)\right\}.

To leverage this formula, we derive
\mu^{i}
and
v^{i}
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
is symmetric and independent from
\mathbf{X}
, and let
\mathbf{W}\coloneqq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\bm{\Sigma}
.
Under
1
,
for
i\in[T]
, the mean
\mu^{i}
and variance
v^{i}
of
\left\langle{\bm{\gamma}^{i}},{\bm{\omega}}\right\rangle+\gamma_{0}^{i}
with the input
\bm{\omega}\coloneqq\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T}/\lambda
are given as follows:

\displaystyle\mu^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{i}{T}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\tr(\mathbf{W})}{\lambda}+o(1),

\displaystyle v^{i}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2i^{2}}{T^{2}}+\frac{7}{12}\right)\frac{\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})}{\lambda^{2}}+o(1).

The proof is given in
Appendix

C
.
The symmetry of
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
is assumed for convenience.
In the case of asymmetric
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
, we can redefine the signal propagation probability with the symmetrized matrix
(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}+\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{\top})/2
.

Recall that
\tr(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\in[d]}w_{i}
if we write the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{W}
by
(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{d})
, and that
\tr(\mathbf{W})^{2}\leq d\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
holds due to Jensen’s inequality.
This implies that

-\sqrt{d\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})}\leq\tr(\mathbf{W})\leq\sqrt{d\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})}.

(5)

Moreover,
\mu^{i}
and
v^{i}
are determined by the relative token location
i/T
.
By continuously extending
i/T
to
\theta\in[0,1]
, the signal propagation probability
\rho_{i}
can be extended to
\rho:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, defined over relative token locations:

\rho(\theta)\coloneqq\Phi\bigg(\Big(\theta-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\xi;\theta\bigg)-\Phi\bigg(\Big(\theta-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\xi-\frac{1}{\eta};\theta\bigg),

(6)

where

\displaystyle\xi\coloneqq\frac{\tr(\mathbf{W})}{\sqrt{\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})}},\quad\eta\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})}}{\lambda},

\displaystyle\Phi(z;\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{erf}}\bigg(\frac{z}{\sqrt{\smash[b]{2(2\theta^{2}+\frac{7}{12})}}}\bigg),

and the parameter ranges are

(due to the bound (

\eta\in(0,\infty)
.
Here,
\xi
and
\eta
can be regarded independent (when
\mathbf{W}
is independent from
\mathbf{X}
) because the eigenspectrum scale

can be modulated within the bound (

How

Lemma 2
.

\rho(\theta)
satisfies the following properties.

1.

(Late-/middle-site)
As

\xi\eta\to r
,

\rho(\theta)\to\begin{cases}\mathds{1}_{\{\theta\geq\frac{1}{2}\}}&\text{if }0\leq r\leq 2\\
\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{1}{2}\leq\theta\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{r}\}}&\text{if }r>2\end{cases}.

2.

(Early-/middle-site)
As
(\xi,\eta)\to(-\infty,0)
with
\xi\eta\to r
,

\rho(\theta)\to\begin{cases}\mathds{1}_{\{\theta\leq\frac{1}{2}\}}&\text{if }-2\leq r<0\\
\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{r}\leq\theta\leq\frac{1}{2}\}}&\text{if }r<-2\end{cases}.

3.

(Uniformity)
Fix
\eta
as a finite value.
As
|\xi|\to 0
,
|\rho^{\prime}(\theta)|\to 0
for any
\theta\in[0,1]
.

4.

(Vanishing signal)
Fix
\xi
as a finite value.
As
\eta\to\infty
,
\rho(\theta)\to 0
for any
\theta\in[0,1]
.

From late-/middle-/early-site focus in
Lemma

2
, we see interestingly that
\rho(\theta)
localizes when
\xi\eta=\tr(\mathbf{W})/\lambda
asymptotically deviates from zero significantly so that
|r|\gg 2
.
At this limit,
\rho(\theta)
concentrates on
\theta=0.5
, inducing the middle-site focus.
Conversely, attention becomes relatively uniform when
\xi\eta=\tr(\mathbf{W})/\lambda
is kept close to zero.
In
Fig.

3
, we numerically illustrate this regime:
\rho
localizes at the middle site when
\eta=0.02
(i.e.,
\xi\eta=5.12
).

Let us investigate the limiting condition of
(\xi,\eta)
for localization:
When is
(\xi,\eta)
close to the limit
(\infty,0)
while
\xi\eta\to r\gg 2
?
Here, we focus on the
eigenspectrum
of
\mathbf{W}
by regarding its eigenvalues
(w_{i})_{i\in[d]}
as being sampled from a distribution with the mean
\tr(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i\in[d]}w_{i}
and scale
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})=\sum_{i\in[d]}w_{i}^{2}
.
First,
\eta\to 0
indicates that the scale
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
should be close to zero.
Next,
\xi\eta(=\tr(\mathbf{W})/\lambda)\to r\gg 2
means that
\tr(\mathbf{W})\gg 2\lambda
at the limit, i.e.,
\tr(\mathbf{W})
should be significantly away from zero.
By combining them, we tell that
\rho
localizes when the eigenspectrum concentrates around a non-zero mean.
This happens more likely when the embedding dimension
d
is excessively large to make the eigenvalue sum
\tr(\mathbf{W})
bounded away from zero while keeping the scale
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
close to zero (i.e., keeping every eigenvalue close to zero).
Thus, a larger embedding dimension
d
is beneficial to drive attention to localization.

Next, from the claim of uniformity in
Lemma

2
, we tell that
\rho
fluctuates less and less with
\xi
closer to zero.
Hence,
\rho(\theta)
takes a similar value across different token positions
\theta
in this limit.
When
\tr(\mathbf{W})\to 0
,
\rho
attains this limit.

Summary.

Wrapping up this section, we obtain answers to (Q1) posed in
Section

1
under the random walk model.

•

\rho

localizes
when
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
is close to zero while
|\tr(\mathbf{W})|
is significantly bounded away from zero, i.e.,
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum
concentrates to a non-zero mean
.

•

\rho
is

when
\tr(\mathbf{W})
is close to zero while
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
remains finite, i.e.,
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum has
the zero mean and a finite variance
.

•

\rho

degenerates
to zero uniformly when
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
is sufficiently large, i.e.,
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum has
an infinitely large variance
.

Connection to rank collapse.

Dong et al. 2021
showed that self-attention blocks
\mathbf{U}^{\ell}
(see
Eq.

2
) converges to a rank-
1
matrix
\mathbf{z}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
(for some
\mathbf{z}
) with
L\to\infty
without skip connections or feed-forward blocks, which is called
rank collapse
.
1
1

1

Note that our matrix notation is different from the one used in
Dong et al. 2021
so that we chose to let each column of
\mathbf{X}
store a token, whereas they let each row of
\mathbf{X}
store a token.

They argued the importance of avoiding rank collapse for better expressivity because each token embedding in a rank-
1
self-attention block degenerates to the same.
Hence, the rank collapse is related to the failure mode attributed to the uniformity
after
mixing key tokens by attention, which is slightly different from what we are concerned about—how each token contributes
during
mixing by attention (through the gradient, as discussed in
Section

3
).

Dong et al. 2021
proved that the convergence rate to a rank-
1
matrix slows down when the matrix
\ell_{1}
-norm
\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{1}
is large.
Because we can draw the following connection between
\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{1}
and
|\tr(\mathbf{W})|
:

\frac{|\tr(\mathbf{W})|}{\sqrt{d}\|\bm{\Sigma}\|_{2}}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{1},

(7)

where the first inequality is due to the bound (
5
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,
it is advisable to increase
|\tr(\mathbf{W})|
under fixed
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
to mitigate the rank collapse.
This is equivalent to reducing the eigenspectrum variance

d^{2}\mathbb{V}[w_{i}]=d^{2}(\E[w_{i}^{2}]-\E[w_{i}]^{2})=d\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})-|\tr(\mathbf{W})|^{2}.

(8)

Hence,
minimizing the
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum variance leads to better expressivity
.

Connection to entropy collapse.

Zhai et al. 2023
introduced a concept called
entropy collapse
, in which the average Shannon entropy of the columns of the attention matrix
\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
(see
Eq.

1
) shrinks.
Intuitively speaking, low attention entropy induces localized attention.
This notion of localization is akin to ours because the attention entropy measures the uniformity the attention is applied to input tokens
during
mixing.
They empirically observed that the training loss falls into a plateau with low attention entropy, which causes training instability of transformers, and hence advocate for keeping attention less peaked during training.

In
Zhai et al. 2023
, the attention entropy is asymptotically lower-bounded for large
T
by

\ln(1+T\exp(-\nu))+\frac{\nu\exp(-\nu/2)}{T^{-1}+\exp(-\nu)},

(9)

where
\nu\coloneqq\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{2}
.
This lower bound is unimodal in
\nu
and vanishes at
\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{2}\to\infty
(see
Fig.

4
),
so the attention entropy tends to be higher when
\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{2}
is small.
If
|\tr(\mathbf{W})|
is not too small,
\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{2}
is lower-bounded (see
Eq.

7
) and the attention entropy may be kept close to the peak of the lower bound (
9
).
To mitigate the entropy collapse, it is natural to decrease
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
under fixed
\tr(\mathbf{W})
(which is equivalent to minimizing the eigenspectrum variance by
Eq.

8
) because of the bound

\|\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\sqrt{\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})}\geq|\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})|\geq\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\|_{2},

(10)

where the first inequality is due to the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
Hence,
minimizing the
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum variance helps the model to avoid the entropy collapse
.

Rank collapse vs. entropy collapse.

At first sight, the two notions of collapse seem to contradict each other because avoiding the rank collapse leads to diverse token embeddings, whereas avoiding the entropy collapse leads to a uniform token mixer.
Indeed, the matrix
\ell_{1}
-norm (that decreases under the rank collapse) and the spectral norm (that increases under the entropy collapse) are equivalent norms, and the two modes appear to be incompatible.

However, as we discussed above, this trade-off is reconcilable from the viewpoint of the
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum.
Setting the eigenspectrum mean to be bounded away from zero, we can avoid the rank collapse owing to the bound (
7
).
Under a fixed eigenspectrum mean, minimizing the eigenspectrum scale (equivalently, minimizing its variance (
8
)) leads to high attention entropy due to the bound (
10
) and the unimodal shape of the entropy lower bound (
9
).
This variance minimization is nothing else but the condition of attention localization.
Eventually,
\rho(\theta)
localizes and attends to specific sites of tokens, as we showed in
Lemma

2
.
Hence, the signal propagation probability offers us a better view of localization.
Let us summarize our second take-home.

•

Better expressivity
:
If
|\tr(\mathbf{W})|
is maximized for a fixed
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
, the convergence to the rank collapse becomes slow.

•

High attention entropy
:
If
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
is minimized for a fixed
|\tr(\mathbf{W})|
bounded away from zero, the attention entropy is increased.

Both of the above are attributed to minimizing the
\mathbf{W}
-eigenspectrum variance (
8
).

6
Intervening attention localization

To empirically see the impact of localization on the model performance, we propose a method to control the degree of attention localization.
We focus on the eigenspectrum of
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
instead of
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\bm{\Sigma}
because
\bm{\Sigma}
does not change during training, and the numerical simulation showed that
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
behaves as a reasonable proxy to
\mathbf{W}
(see
Section

5
).

We minimize the loss function
J
while minimizing the eigenspectrum scale and maintaining the mean to a fixed level:

\min_{\bm{\Theta}}\Big\{J(\bm{\Theta})+\kappa_{1}\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})+\kappa_{2}(\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})-1)^{2}\Big\},

where
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
are the regularization strengths.
Here, we allow
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}
to be asymmetric, and the eigenspectrum scale is represented by
\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})
.
The regularization terms can be optimized fairly easily thanks to the following derivative formulae (for
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}}
):

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}}\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})=2\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}}}\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})=2\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}}[(\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})-1)^{2}]\!=\![\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})-1]\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{K}}}[(\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})-1)^{2}]\!=\![\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})-1]\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{Q}}.

Since this whole objective drives the eigenspectrum scale to a small value, the signal vanishing can be avoided automatically.
We call this regularization scheme
LocAteR
(LOCalized ATtEntion Regularization).

8
Conclusion and limitation

We revealed that attention localizes when the eigenspectrum of
\mathbf{W}
concentrates to a non-zero mean, or equivalently, with larger eigenspectrum mean
\tr(\mathbf{W})
and smaller scale
\tr(\mathbf{W}^{2})
.
Based on it,
LocAteR
was proposed to shrink the scale
\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{2})
while maintaining the mean
\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})
.
Interestingly, maximizing the scale is related to mitigating both rank collapse and entropy collapse, and hence, the two apparently contradictory failure modes can be reconciled.
The experiments on a real-world dataset corroborate it, though the random walk model is not perfectly satisfied.

We recognize three limitations of this work.
First, we rely on the strong random walk model.
Although the Gaussianity may be reasonable because of usual initialization schemes of transformer embedding layers, it is interesting to consider an alternative model to capture token correlations better.
Second, the formal analysis is mainly restricted to
1
-layer transformers.
Recent studies often consider gradient explosion in the large-depth limit from the viewpoint of layer normalization
[
30
,
22
]
and initialization
[
2
,
21
]
.
It must be fruitful to integrate these perspectives to our gradient analysis through
Eq.

3
.
Lastly, why attention localization leads to better model performance still remains elusive.
Whereas localization is related to avoiding rank collapse (and hence higher model expressivity), we need additional effort to fully understand the mechanism.

B.2
Order evaluation of QK-gradient

The orders of the QK-gradient terms (
19
), (
20
), and (
21
) are evaluated.
In this subsection, we assume that the covariance matrix in
1
is
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathbf{I}
for simplicity.
The following evaluation still applies with minor modifications for a general
\bm{\Sigma}
.
For
Eq.

19
, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies that

\displaystyle\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_1_}}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta}\E[\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}\widetilde{\gamma}^{j}_{\beta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{j})(\mathbf{x}_{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T})\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}]\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\bigg\{\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}\widetilde{\gamma}^{j}_{\beta})^{2}]\bigg\}}_{\text{(A)}}\underbrace{\bigg\{\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{j})^{2}]\bigg\}}_{\text{(B)}}\underbrace{\bigg\{\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T})^{2}]\bigg\}}_{\text{(C)}}\underbrace{\bigg\{\sum_{i,j,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top})^{\odot 2}]\bigg\}}_{\text{(D)}}.

For (A), we have
\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha},\widetilde{\gamma}^{j}_{\beta}\leq T^{-1}
by definition, and hence
\text{(A)}=O(1)
.
For (B), by using
Eq.

17
,

\displaystyle\text{(B)}=T^{2}\sum_{i,j}\E[\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{i}]=T^{2}\sum_{i,j}\{(i^{2}+ij-3i-j+4)\tr(\check{\mathbf{P}}^{2})+(i^{2}-2i+2)\tr(\check{\mathbf{P}})^{2}\}=O(T^{6}).

For (C), by following the same computation as
Eq.

22
,

\displaystyle\text{(C)}

\displaystyle=T^{3}\sum_{\beta}\E[\mathbf{x}_{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{T}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}\mathbf{x}_{\beta}]

\displaystyle=T^{3}\sum_{\beta}\{(\beta^{2}+(T-3)\beta-(T-4))\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}}^{2})+(\beta^{2}-2\beta+2)\tr(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{QK}})^{2}\}=O(T^{6}).

For (D), its
(i,j)
-element can be evaluated as follows (no matter whether
i=j
or not):

\displaystyle\text{(D)}_{ij}=T^{3}\sum_{\alpha}\E[x_{\alpha,i}^{2}x_{T,j}^{2}]=T^{3}\sum_{\alpha}\E[x_{\alpha,i}^{2}\{(T-\alpha)+x_{\alpha,j}^{2}\}]=O(T^{5})\sum_{\alpha}\E[x_{\alpha,i}^{2}]+T^{3}\sum_{\alpha}\E[x_{\alpha,i}^{2}x_{\alpha,j}^{2}]=O(T^{6}).

By plugging them back, we now confirmed that
|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_1_}}|=O(T^{8})
.

The orders of
Eqs.

20
and
21
can be evaluated similarly and the detailed evaluations are omitted.

\displaystyle|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_2}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta}\E[\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{\beta}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{\beta})\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}]\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\{\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}\widetilde{\gamma}_{0}^{\beta})^{2}]\bigg\}\bigg\{\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\check{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{x}_{\beta})^{2}]\bigg\}\bigg\{\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top})^{\odot 2}]\bigg\}

\displaystyle=O(T)\cdot O(T^{4})\cdot O(T^{5})

\displaystyle=O(T^{10})

\displaystyle\implies|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_2}}|

\displaystyle=O(T^{5}).

\displaystyle|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_3}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\sum_{i,\alpha}\E[\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\mathbf{x}_{T}-\mathbf{y})\}\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top}]\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg\{\sum_{i,\alpha}\E[(\widetilde{\gamma}^{i}_{\alpha})^{2}]\bigg\}\bigg\{\sum_{i,\alpha}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{V}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\top}(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{F}}\mathbf{x}_{T}-\mathbf{y}))^{2}]\bigg\}\bigg\{\sum_{i,\alpha}\E[(\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}\mathbf{x}_{T}^{\top})^{\odot 2}]\bigg\}

\displaystyle=O(1)\cdot O(T^{4})\cdot O(T^{4})

\displaystyle=O(T^{8})

\displaystyle\implies|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_3}}|

\displaystyle=O(T^{4}).

Hence, we have
|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_1_}}|=O(T^{8})
,
|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_2}}|=O(T^{5})
, and
|\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{equation:qk_grad_3}}|=O(T^{4})
, which implies that the QK-gradient (
3
) is asymptotically dominated by
Eq.

19
.
