Large Deviations Principle for the tagged empirical field of a general interacting gas
David Padilla-Garza

Abstract
This paper deals with rare events in a general interacting gas at high temperature, by means of Large Deviations Principles. The main result is an LDP for the tagged empirical field, which features the competition of an energy term and an entropy term. The approach to proving this Large Deviations Principle is to first deduce one for the tagged empirical field of non-interacting particles at high temperature, and upgrade that result to interacting particle systems.

Introduction and motivation

Consider
N
particles in a manifold
M

M=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or
M=\mathbb{T}^{d}

\mathbb{T}^{d}
is the
d-

This is modeled by the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})=\sum_{i\neq j}g(x_{i}-x_{j})+N\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i}),

(1)

where
X_{N}\in M^{N},
with
X_{N}=(x_{1},...x_{N})
,
V:M\to\mathbb{R}
is the confining potential, and

is the pair-wise interaction.

Consider the Gibbs measure
\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}
associated to
\mathcal{H}_{N}:

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}(X_{N})=\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})\right)\,\mathrm{d}X_{N},

(2)

where
\beta=\beta(N)
is the inverse temperature, and

Z_{N,\beta}=\int_{M^{N}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})\right)\,\mathrm{d}X_{N}

(3)

is the partition function. A system governed by (
2

A very frequent form of the pair-wise interaction

\begin{cases}g(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{d-2}}\text{ if }d\geq 3\\
g(x)=-\log(|x|)\text{ if }d=1,2\end{cases}

(4)

We will refer to this setting as the Coulomb case for

, and log case for

. Another frequent form of

is given by

g(x)=\frac{1}{|x|^{d-2s}},

(5)

with

Given that there are
N
particles in space, and that typically they are confined to a compact set by the potential, the distance between the particles is of order
N^{-\frac{1}{d}}
. This means that the scale
N^{\frac{1}{d}}
is special and will be called the
microscopic scale
. We will call the original length scale
macroscopic
. Any length scale which is between these two will be called
mesoscopic
.

At a macroscopic scale, the system is well-described by the empirical measure. Given
X_{N}=(x_{1},...x_{N}),
the empirical measure is defined as

{\rm emp}_{N}(X_{N}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}.

(6)

The behavior of systems governed by (
2
) can be significantly different depending on the order of magnitude of
\beta
. We will call the regime
\beta=\frac{\theta}{N}
(with
\theta
constant) the
high-temperature
regime. In the regime
\frac{1}{N}\ll\beta
, the empirical measure converges (a.s. under the Gibbs measure) to a measure that typically has compact support, and is characterized by minimizing the mean-field limit of the Hamiltonian. In the high-temperature regime, however, this does not happen. Instead, the empirical measure converges (a.s. under the Gibbs measure) to a measure that is everywhere positive. In the Coulomb or Riesz setting, we will call the regime
\beta=\theta N^{\frac{2s-d}{d}}
the
low temperature
regime. The reason for identifying this temperature scaling is that in the low-temperature regime, we observe structure at the microscopic level, whereas at higher temperatures we don’t (see
[
26
]
), in the sense that the particles resemble a Poisson process.

At a microscopic scale, the system is well-described by the empirical field, which is defined (given a fixed
x\in M
) as

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{N^{\frac{1}{d}}(x_{i}-x)}.

(7)

This object is a positive, discrete measure of mass
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Alternatively, the empirical field may be thought of as an element of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
, defined as
N^{\frac{1}{d}}\theta_{x}X_{N}
, where
\theta_{x}
denotes the element-wise translation by the vector
x
. This object is often hard to study analytically (even in the non-interacting case), so we may define a less fine observable by averaging the empirical field over a compact subset
\Omega\subset M
:

\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\left(N^{\frac{1}{d}}\theta_{x}X_{N}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(8)

This last object is a probability measure on the space of point configurations. A more precise observable, which is still not as precise as the empirical field, is the tagged empirical field. It is given by averaging the empirical field while keeping track of the blow-up point. The tagged empirical field is defined as

\int_{\Omega}\delta_{\left(x,N^{\frac{1}{d}}\theta_{x}X_{N}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(9)

The tagged empirical field yields a measure on the cross product of
\Omega
and the space of point configurations. The first marginal of this measure is the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
, and the second marginal is the averaged non-tagged empirical field, given by equation (
8
).

A natural question is then to quantify this convergence by means of an LDP. Ideally, this would be quantified by an LDP for the empirical field. Still, as mentioned before, this is a very complicated problem, so we can quantify this convergence with an LDP for the tagged empirical field.

\infty\quad\text{ o.w.}\end{cases}

Lastly, we recall the definitions of rate functions and of Large Deviations Principle.

Definition 2.16

(Rate function)
.

Let
X
be a metric space (or a topological space). A rate function is an l.s.c. function
I:X\to[0,\infty]
, it is called a good rate function if its sublevel sets are compact.

Definition 2.17

(LDP)
.

Let
P_{N}
be a sequence of Borel probability measures on
X
and
a_{N}
a sequence of positive reals such that
a_{N}\to\infty.
Let
I
be a good rate function on
X.
The sequence
P_{N}
is said to satisfy a Large Deviations Principle (LDP) at speed
a_{N}
with (good) rate function
I
if for every Borel set
E\subset X
the following inequalities hold:

-\inf_{E^{\mathrm{o}}}I\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{a_{N}}\log\left(P_{N}(E)\right)\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{a_{N}}\log\left(P_{N}(E)\right)\leq-\inf_{\overline{E}}I,

(33)

where
E^{\mathrm{o}}
and
\overline{E}
denote respectively the interior and the closure of a set
E.

Formally, this means that
P_{N}(E)\simeq\exp(-a_{N}\inf_{{E}}I).

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\beta=\frac{\theta}{N}
and
g:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies:

Symmetry.

g(x)=g(-x).

(34)

2.

Integrability.

g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

(35)

3.

Uniform continuity. For any
\epsilon>0,
we have that

is uniformly continuous on

4.

Weak positive definiteness. If
\mu\in TV(\mathbb{T}^{d})

(36)

then

\mathcal{E}(\mu)\geq 0,

(37)

where
TV(\mathbb{T}^{d})
denotes the space of signed measures of bounded variation.

5.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\mathcal{E}(\mu)>-\infty.

(38)

Assume that the confining potential
V
satisfies:

1.

V
is lower-semi-continuous.

2.

V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Define
\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}
by Definition
2.4
with
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\mu_{\theta}
by (

\infty\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \textrm{if}\ \ \ \overline{\mathbf{P}}\notin\mathcal{P}_{s,1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times{\rm Config}),\end{cases}

(39)

where

4
Literature review

Rare events at the macroscopic scale were treated in
[
14
]
and
[
11
]
in the context of general interactions. The observable that allows us to analyze the macroscopic scale is the empirical measure (equation (
6
)). In our setting, their main results are that the pushforward of the Gibbs measure (equation (
2
)) by the empirical measure (equation (
6
)) satisfies an LDP in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
at speed
N
with a rate function given by

\mathcal{E}_{V}(\cdot)-\min_{\mu}\mathcal{E}_{V}(\mu)

(45)

if
\frac{1}{N}\ll\beta
, and

\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\cdot)-\min_{\mu}\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu)

(46)

if
\beta=\frac{\theta}{N}
. The reference
[
14
]
is even more general since it treats many particle systems in general compact manifolds, and with an interaction that is given by a many body formula. This paper continues the investigation of
[
14
]
by analyzing rare events in the high-temperature regime for general interactions at the microscopic scale.

The microscopic behavior of a general interacting gas in the high-temperature regime is also the subject of
[
23
]
. In this case, the author deals with the observable

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{N^{\frac{1}{d}}(x_{i}-x_{0})},

(47)

and shows that it converges to a Poisson Point Process, with density given by the thermal equilibrium measure at the point
x_{0}
. Even though the subject of this paper is also the microscopic behavior of a general interacting gas in the high-temperature regime, our results are to a large extent independent. Neither result implies the other, and the techniques used are quite different. Indeed, even though the LDP proved in Theorem
3.1
implies that the tagged empirical field converges to a tagged Poisson Point Process, it does not imply that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{N^{\frac{1}{d}}(x_{i}-x_{0})}
converges to a Poisson process. Conversely, one cannot derive an LDP from the convergence result proved in
[
23
]
.

As mentioned in the introduction, in
[
26
]
the authors treat the Riesz and Coulomb cases at low temperature (
\beta=\theta N^{\frac{2s-d}{d}}
). The main result of
[
26
]
was later extended to hyper-singular Riesz gases
[
18
]
, two-component plasmas
[
27
]
, and the local empirical field of a one-component plasma
[
25
,
3
]
. The main result in
[
26
]
has a similar flavor to ours because the rate function involves the competition of two terms: one derived from the energy and one derived from the entropy. In contrast, in the case of a Riesz gas at an intermediate temperature regime, there is no competition between the terms: the energy imposes a constraint at the leading order, and the entropy appears at the next order. Unlike
[
26
]
, the energy-derived term that appears in the rate function of Theorem
3.1
is not the renormalized energy, but rather a mean-field jellium-type energy. Indeed, it is not even clear what “renormalized energy” means in the context of general interactions. The object of our LDP is basically the tagged empirical field as defined in
[
26
]
. The main difference (in the definition of the observable) is that in our case, the domain of averaging is the entire space and not the support of the equilibrium measure. This is due to the fact that, unlike the equilibrium measure, the
thermal
equilibrium measure does not have compact support and is everywhere positive (even in Euclidean space). It is for this reason that we chose to work on the torus, and not in Euclidean space. The definition of the tagged empirical field would be trivial if the domain of averaging were the whole Euclidean space. Extending quantities to infinite space is a classical problem in statistical mechanics (see, for example, chapter 6 of
[
13
]
for a discussion of the “energy density”), but the intrinsically long-range range nature of the interaction makes it non-trivial to adapt this general setting to our problem. A feature that our LDP has in common with
[
26
]
is the presence of the tagged specific relative entropy in the rate function. Unlike
[
26
]
, however, in our case, the entropy is taken with respect to an inhomogeneous Poisson Point Process.

Apart from the microscopic and macroscopic scales, it is also possible to analyze rare events at a mesoscopic scale. In the Coulomb setting, this is the subject of
[
29
]
. In this case, the observable to analyze is the local empirical field, defined as

\frac{1}{N^{1-\lambda d}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{N^{\lambda}x_{i}}|_{\square_{R}},

(48)

for
\lambda\in\left(0,\frac{1}{d}\right)
. In this case, the typical event is that the local empirical field approximates a uniform measure of density
\mu_{V}(0)
. The rare events are governed by an LDP in which the rate function contains either an entropy-derived term or an energy-derived term, depending on the magnitude of the temperature.

5.1
Additional notation and definitions

We start by giving a few additional definitions and introducing additional notation.

Definition 5.1
.

We introduce the notation

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}(\mu,\nu)&=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}g(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{y}\\
\mathcal{G}^{\neq}(\mu,\nu)&=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus\Delta}g(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{y},\end{split}

(49)

where
\Delta=\{x,x\}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Given a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, we define

h^{\mu}=g\ast\mu.

(50)

Given a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and
X_{N}\in\mathbb{T}^{d\times N}
, we define

{\rm F}_{N}(X_{N},\mu)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}g(x_{i}-x_{j})+\mathcal{E}(\mu)-2\mathcal{G}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu).

(51)

Definition 5.2
.

Given an open subset
A\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and a positive measurable function
\mu:A\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we define the inhomogeneous Poisson Point Process of intensity
\mu
on
A
as
\mathbf{\Pi}^{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}({\rm Config}(A))
characterized by the requirement that for any Borel set
B\subset A
,

\mathbf{\Pi}^{\mu}\left\{|C|(B)=n\right\}=\frac{(m(B))^{n}}{n!}\exp\left(-\lambda|B|\right),

(52)

where

m(B)=\int_{B}\mu(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(53)

Definition 5.3
.

Given two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we define the relative entropy of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
as

\begin{cases}{\rm ent}[\mu|\nu]=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\log\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu\ {\rm if}\ \mu\ll\nu\\
{\rm ent}[\mu|\nu]=\infty\ {\rm if}\ {\rm not,}\end{cases}

(54)

where
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative.

5.2
The thermal equilibrium measure

In this section, we prove the existence, uniqueness, and some other basic properties of the thermal equilibrium measure. We also use the thermal equilibrium measure to derive a splitting formula for the energy.

Proposition 5.4

(Existence, uniqueness, characterization)
.

Assume that the confining potential
V
satisfies:

1.

V
is lower-semi-continuous.

2.

V\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Assume also that
g
satisfies:

1.

Integrability.

g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

(55)

2.

Weak positive definiteness. If
\mu\in TV(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is such that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0,

(56)

then

\mathcal{E}(\mu)\geq 0,

(57)

where
TV(\mathbb{T}^{d})
denotes the space of measures of bounded variation.

3.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

\mathcal{E}(\mu)>-\infty.

(58)

Then the functional (
14
) has a unique minimizer in the set
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, which we denote
\mu_{\theta}.
Additionally,
\mu_{\theta}
satisfies
\mu_{\theta}>0
a.e. and the Euler-Lagrange equation

2h^{\mu_{\theta}}+V+\frac{1}{\theta}\log\mu_{\theta}=c,

(59)

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

Step 1
[Existence and uniqueness]

Existence is a simple consequence of the direct method: Let
\mu_{n}
be a sequence such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}(\mu_{n})=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}(\mu).

(60)

Then, modulo a subsequence,
\mu_{n}
converges weakly to a measure
\mu
. By properties
1
and
2
of
V
, and
1
and
2
of
g
, we have that
\mathcal{E}_{V}
is lower semi-continuous with respect to weak convergence and therefore

\mathcal{E}_{V}(\mu)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{V}(\mu_{n}).

(61)

It is well-known that the entropy functional is lower semi-continuous, and hence

{\rm ent}[\mu]\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\rm ent}[\mu_{n}].

(62)

This implies that
\mu
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Uniqueness is a consequence of the strict convexity of

\mu\to{\rm ent}[\mu],

(63)

and property
3
of
g
: Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be minimizers of
\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. If
\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}
, then

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}\left(\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{2}\right)&<\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}(\mu_{1})+\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}(\mu_{2})\right)\\
&=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\mathcal{E}^{\theta}_{V}(\mu).\end{split}

(64)

This is a contradiction and therefore the minimizer is unique.

Step 2
[Positivity]

The proof is standard, see for example
[
4
,
28
,
32
]
.

We proceed by contrapositive, and assume that there is a bounded set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mu_{\theta}(x)=0
for all
x\in X
. Now consider

{\mu}_{\theta}^{\epsilon}=\frac{\mu_{\theta}+\epsilon\mathbf{1}_{X}}{1+\epsilon|X|}.

(65)

Doing a Taylor expansion of
\mathcal{E}_{\theta}({\mu}_{\theta}^{\epsilon})
, we get that

\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}({\mu}_{\theta}^{\epsilon})=\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})-\epsilon|X|\left(\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})\right)+\epsilon\int_{X}h^{\mu_{\theta}}(x)+V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{\theta}|X|\epsilon\log\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(66)

Note that

\int_{X}h^{\mu_{\theta}}(x)+V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

(67)

since
X
is bounded,
g
satisfies item
1
,
V
satisfies item
2
, and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mu_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}x=1
. We then get that

\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}({\mu}_{\theta}^{\epsilon})=\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})+\epsilon C+\frac{1}{\theta}|X|\epsilon\log\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(68)

where
C
depends on
X
.

If
|X|\neq 0
, this would imply that

\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}({\mu}_{\theta}^{\epsilon})<\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})

(69)

for
\epsilon>0
small enough.

This would be a contradiction and therefore
\mu_{\theta}
is positive a.e.

Step 3
[Euler-Lagrange equation]

Let
f
be a smooth, compactly supported function such that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\mu_{\theta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0
. Note that
(1+tf)\mu_{\theta}
is a probability measure for small enough
|t|
. Since
\mu_{\theta}
is a minimizer, we obtain that

\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})\leq\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}((1+tf)\mu_{\theta}),

(70)

which implies, taking the derivative at
t=0
, that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(2h^{\mu_{\theta}}+V+\frac{1}{\theta}\log\mu_{\theta})f\mu_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(71)

Since
\mu_{\theta}\neq 0
a.e. we infer that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(2h^{\mu_{\theta}}+V+\frac{1}{\theta}\log\mu_{\theta})g\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

(72)

for all
g
such that
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0
, which implies

2h^{\mu_{\theta}}+V+\frac{1}{\theta}\log\mu_{\theta}=c

(73)

for some
c
.

∎

Now that we have proved the existence, uniqueness, and some basic properties of the thermal equilibrium measure; we will use it to derive a splitting formula for the Hamiltonian. This formula appeared in the Coulomb case in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 5.5

(Thermal splitting formula)
.

Let
\theta>0
. We introduce the notation

\zeta_{\theta}=-\frac{1}{\theta}\log(\mu_{\theta}).

(74)

Then for any point configuration
X_{N}
the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}_{N}
can be rewritten (split) as

\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})=N^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})+{\rm F}_{N}(X_{N},\mu_{\theta})+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\zeta_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}\right).

(75)

Proof.

It suffices to write

\begin{split}\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})&=N^{2}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}g(x_{i}-x_{j})+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}V\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}\right)\\
&=N^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\theta})+2\mathcal{G}(\mu_{\theta},{\rm emp}_{N}-\mu_{\theta})+{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}V\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}\right)\end{split}

(76)

and then use the Euler-Langrange equation for
\mu_{\theta}
.
∎

5.3
Next order partition function

In analogy with previous work in the field
[
3
,
26
]
, we define a next-order partition function; which will in practice be a negligible error term in the rest of the paper.

Definition 5.6
.

We define the next order partition function
K_{N,\beta}
as

K_{N,\beta}=\frac{Z_{N,\beta}}{\exp\left(-N^{2}\beta\mathcal{E}_{V}^{\theta}(\mu_{\theta})\right)},

(77)

with
\theta=N\beta
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Assume that
\beta=\frac{\theta}{N}
for a fixed
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(K_{N,\beta}\right)}{N}=0.

(78)

Proof.

The strategy of the proof will be to use the Laplace Principle proved in
[
14
]
.

Consider the probability measure
\pi,
defined as

\mathrm{d}\pi(y)=\frac{1}{{z}^{*}}\exp\left(-V(y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(79)

where

{z}^{*}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\exp\left(-V(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(80)

Consider also the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}^{*}_{N}(X_{N})=\sum_{i\neq j}g(x_{i}-x_{j}),

(81)

with mean field limit

\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mu)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}g(x-y)\mathrm{d}\mu_{x}\mathrm{d}\mu_{y}.

(82)

Then by
[
14
]
, we have that the following Laplace principle holds: for every bounded and continuous function
f:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\theta}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d\times N}}\exp\left(-N\theta f({\rm emp}_{N})\right)\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{N}\right)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\{f(\mu)+F(\mu)\},

(83)

where the probability measure
\gamma_{N}
is defined as

\mathrm{d}\gamma_{N}=\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}^{*}_{N}\right)\mathrm{d}\pi^{\otimes N},

(84)

and
F
is defined as

F(\mu)=\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\theta}{\rm ent}[\mu|\pi].

(85)

In particular, taking
f=0,
we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\theta}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d\times N}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}^{*}_{N}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{\otimes N}\right)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\{\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\theta}{\rm ent}[\mu|\pi]\}.

(86)

Note that for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we have that

\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\theta}{\rm ent}[\mu|\pi]=\mathcal{E}_{V}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\theta}\left({\rm ent}[\mu]-\log z^{*}\right).

(87)

On the other hand, for any
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we have that

\frac{1}{N\theta}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d\times N}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}^{*}_{N}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\pi^{\otimes N}\right)=\frac{1}{N\theta}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d\times N}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N}\right)\,\mathrm{d}X_{N}\right)-\frac{\log z^{*}}{\theta}.

(88)

Therefore

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N\theta}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d\times N}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N}\right)\,\mathrm{d}X_{N}\right)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\{\mathcal{E}_{V}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\theta}\left({\rm ent}[\mu]\right)\},

(89)

which implies that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(K_{N,\beta}\right)}{N}=0.

(90)

∎

\mu_{\theta}

\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}
be a Poisson process on

(135)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}{\rm int}\left[\overline{\mathbf{P}}^{x}\right]\mathrm{d}x=1.

such that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|C_{N,\delta}|}{N}=1.

(137)

We then have that

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)&=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=j\right)\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(|C|=j\right)\\
&=\sum_{j=(1-\epsilon)N}^{(1+\epsilon)N}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=j\right)\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(|C|=j\right),\end{split}

(138)

for some function
\epsilon
such that
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\epsilon=0
.

Therefore

\begin{split}&\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{\mathbf{P}}},\delta)\right)\\
\leq&\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\frac{|C|}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\right)\max_{\frac{j}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=j\right),\end{split}

(139)

and similarly,

\begin{split}&\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)\\
\geq&\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\frac{|C|}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\right)\min_{\frac{j}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=j\right).\end{split}

(140)

\begin{split}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}(|C|=N)&\leq\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\frac{|C|}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\right)\\
&\leq N\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}(|C|=N).\end{split}

(141)

We recall Stirling’s approximation:

\log(N!)=N\log(N)-N+O(\log(N)).

(142)

Using equation (
142

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}(|C|=N)\right)=0,

(143)

which implies that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\frac{|C|}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\right)\right)=0.

(144)

On the other hand,

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\max_{\frac{j}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=j\right)\\
=&\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\min_{\frac{j}{N}\in(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)}\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=j\right)\\
=&\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\Big||C|=N\right).\end{split}

(145)

Therefore

\begin{split}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}\left(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}(X_{N})\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)\right)&=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{\Pi}_{N}\left(\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{N}(C)\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)\right)\\
&=-\overline{\rm Ent}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}].\end{split}

(146)

Proof of Theorem
3.1
, upper bound.

We will prove the upper bound of a weak LDP. It is standard (see, for example,
[
26
]
) that this result, along with a lower bound (and exponential tightness, which is easy consequence of the fact that the total number of particles is bounded by
N
), implies the full LDP. We thus need to show that for any
\overline{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}\times{\rm Config})
we have

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}\left(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)\right)\right)\leq-\theta\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})-\overline{\rm Ent}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}],

(147)

where
\rho(x)={\rm int}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}^{x}]
.

Here comes the argument: using the thermal splitting formula (equation (
75
)) and bounding the integral by its maximum value, we have that

\begin{split}&\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}\left(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)\\
=&\int_{\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)}\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta}}\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N}(X_{N})\right)\,\mathrm{d}X_{N}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{K_{N,\beta}}\inf_{\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)}\left\{\exp\left(-N\theta{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})\right)\right\}\int_{\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)}\Pi_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{\theta}(x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}.\end{split}

(148)

We recall that, by Lemma
5.7
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\left(K_{N,\beta}\right)}{N}=0.

(149)

Also, by Proposition
3.2

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\int_{\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)}\Pi_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{\theta}(x_{i})\,\mathrm{d}x_{i}\right)\leq-\overline{{\rm Ent}}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}].

(150)

For the remaining term, let
X_{N}^{\delta}
be defined as

X_{N}^{\delta}={\rm argmin}_{\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)}{\rm F}_{N}(X_{N},\mu_{\theta}).

(151)

We assume for simplicity that the minimum is achieved. Otherwise, we would repeat the argument up to an arbitrarily small error. We also define

{\rm emp}_{N}^{\delta}={\rm emp}_{N}(X_{N}^{\delta}).

(152)

Then, as
N\to\infty,
we have that

{\rm emp}_{N}^{\delta}\to\mu_{\delta}

(153)

weakly in the sense of probability measures for some
\mu_{\delta}
. By Lemma
5.10
, we have that

\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\delta}-\mu_{\theta})\leq\liminf_{N\to\infty}{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta}).

(154)

Furthermore, as
\delta\to 0,
we have by Lemma
5.9
that
\mu_{\delta}\to\rho
weakly in the sense of probability measures. Note that weak positive definiteness implies that
\mathcal{E}(\mu)
is l.s.c. Therefore

\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})\leq\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\mathcal{E}(\mu_{\delta}-\mu_{\theta}).

(155)

Putting everything together, we have that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}\left(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta)\right)\right)\right)\leq-\theta\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})-\overline{\rm Ent}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}].

(156)

∎

(161)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mu_{\theta}^{\otimes N}(\Lambda_{N})\right)\geq-\overline{\rm Ent}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}]-{\rm err}.

(162)

Then

\begin{split}\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta))&\geq\frac{1}{K_{N,\beta}}\int_{\Lambda_{N}}\exp\left(-N^{2}\beta\left[{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})\right]\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{\theta}^{\otimes N}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{K_{N,\beta}}\mu_{\theta}^{\otimes N}(\Lambda_{N})\exp\left(-N^{2}\beta\sup_{X_{N}\in\Lambda_{N}}\left[{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})\right]\right).\end{split}

(163)

Therefore for any
{\rm err},\delta>0
, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta))\right)\geq-\theta\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})-\overline{\rm Ent}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}]-{\rm err}.

(164)

Since
{\rm err}
is arbitrary, we can conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\mathbf{P}_{N,\beta}(\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{N}\in B(\overline{\mathbf{P}},\delta))\right)\geq-\theta\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})-\overline{\rm Ent}[\overline{\mathbf{P}}|\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}^{\mu_{\theta}}],

(165)

which implies the desired result.

∎

Step 3
[Energy estimate]

Throughout the proof, we will use the notation

Substep 3.1

Let
{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}={\rm emp}_{N}\ast\delta_{\epsilon}
, for
\epsilon>0
to be determined later. We will first derive an estimate for

\left|{\rm F}_{N}\left({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta}\right)-\mathcal{E}\left({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|.

(178)

For this, we write

\begin{split}&\left|{\rm F}_{N}\left({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta}\right)-\mathcal{E}\left({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|\\
=&\left|{\rm F}_{N}\left({\rm emp}_{N}\right),\mathcal{E}\left({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}\right)+2\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|\\
\leq&2\left|\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|+\left|\mathcal{G}^{\neq}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},{\rm emp}_{N}\right)\right|+\left|\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}\right)\right|\\
&\ \ \ \ \ +2\left|\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|+\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\int h^{\delta_{x_{i}}-\delta_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon}}\delta_{x_{j}}\right|+\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\int h^{\delta_{x_{i}}-\delta_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon}}\delta_{x_{j}}^{\epsilon}\right|+\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{E}(\delta^{\epsilon})\end{split}

(179)

First we deal with the term
\left|\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|
. Note that in order to show that

\left|\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|\to 0
, it suffices to show that
\left|\mathcal{G}\left(\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|\to 0
uniformly in
x
. To this end, take any
r>0
and write

\mathcal{G}\left(\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)=\int_{|y-x|\leq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\mu_{\theta}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{|y-x|\geq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\mu_{\theta}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(180)

Note that

\left|\int_{|y-x|\leq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\mu_{\theta}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq 2\|\mu_{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{|y-x|\leq r+\epsilon}g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

(181)

For the remainder of the proof, we will use the notation

I(\epsilon)=\int_{\square_{\epsilon}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

(182)

and also the function

\Psi(\alpha,\beta)=\sup_{|x-y|<\beta,|x|>\alpha,|y|>\alpha}\left|g(x)-g(y)\right|.

(183)

Note that
\Psi(\alpha,\beta)
is decreasing in
\alpha
, increasing in
\beta
, and for every
\alpha
,
\Psi(\alpha,\beta)\to 0
as
\beta\to 0
by uniform continuity (Property
3
of
g
).
We then have that

\left|\int_{|y-x|\geq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\mu_{\theta}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\|\mu_{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon),

(184)

since for any
y
such that
|x-y|>r
, we have that

\left|h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}(y)\right|\leq\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon).

(185)

Putting together equations (
181
) and (
184
), we have that

\left|\mathcal{G}\left(\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon},\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|\leq\|\mu_{\theta}\|_{L^{1}}\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon)+\|\mu_{\theta}\|_{L^{\infty}}I(r+\epsilon).

(186)

We will now deal with the term
\mathcal{G}^{\neq}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},{\rm emp}_{N}\right)
. The procedure is similar. Note that it suffices to show that
\mathcal{G}^{\neq}\left({\rm emp}_{N}-{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon},x\right)\to 0
uniformly for any
x
. For arbitrary
r>0
, we write

\mathcal{G}^{\neq}\left(\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon},{\rm emp}_{N}\right)=\int_{y:0<|y-x|\leq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}(y)+\int_{y:|y-x|\geq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}(y),

(187)

and we have

\int_{y:|y-x|\geq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}(y)\leq\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon).

(188)

On the other hand, it is not hard to prove that our construction (as a consequence of the regularization procedure) satisfies that for any
x
and any
r>\tau N^{-\frac{1}{d}}

\int_{B(x,\overline{r})}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}\leq C_{S,R,\tau}r^{d},

(189)

where
C_{S,R,\tau}
is a constant that depends only on
S,R,\tau
. In other words, the local density of points is uniformly bounded.

Therefore

\int_{y:0<|y-x|\leq r}h^{\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}{\rm emp}_{N}(y)\leq C_{S,R,\tau}I(r+\epsilon).

(190)

Putting everything together, we have that

\mathcal{G}^{\neq}\left(\delta_{x}-\delta_{x}^{\epsilon},{\rm emp}_{N}\right)\leq C_{S,R,\tau}I(r+\epsilon)+\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon).

(191)

Similarly, we have that

\left|\sum_{i\neq j}\int h^{\delta_{x_{i}}-\delta_{x_{i}}^{\epsilon}}\delta_{x_{j}}^{\epsilon}\right|\leq C_{S,R,\tau}I(r+\epsilon)+\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon),

(192)

where
C_{S,R,\tau}
depends on
S,R,\tau
.

Adding the errors we have that for any
r>0
,

\left|{\rm F}_{N}\left({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta}\right)-\mathcal{E}\left({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta}\right)\right|\leq C_{S,R,\tau}I(r+\epsilon)+C\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon)+\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{E}(\delta^{\epsilon}),

(193)

where
C
is a constant that depends on
\mu_{\theta}
and
C_{S,R,\tau}
is a constant that depends, additionally. on
S,R,
and
\tau
.

Substep 3.2

We now get an estimate for

\mathcal{E}\left({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho^{\epsilon}\right),

(194)

where

\rho^{\epsilon}=\rho\ast\delta^{\epsilon}.

(195)

For this, let
\eta>0
to be determined later, and let
K_{i}
be hypercubes of length
\eta
which cover
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and which are pairwise disjoint except for a set of measure
0
. Let
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, with
x\in K_{j}
. Note that we can write

h^{{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho^{\epsilon}}=\int_{K_{j}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}({\rm emp}_{N}-\rho)(y)+\sum_{i\neq j}\int_{K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}({\rm emp}_{N}-\rho)(y),

(196)

where

g^{\epsilon}=g\ast\delta^{\epsilon}.

(197)

We will now get an estimate for each of the terms in the RHS of equation (
196
). Assume W.L.O.G. that

{\rm emp}_{N}(K_{i})\leq\rho(K_{i}).

(198)

Then we have that

\begin{split}&\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\int_{K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}({\rm emp}_{N}-\rho)(y)\right|\\
\leq&\sum_{i\neq j}\left|{\rm emp}_{N}(K_{i})\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\rho(K_{i})\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right|\\
\leq&\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\left[{\rm emp}_{N}(K_{i})-\rho(K_{i})\right]\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right|+\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\rho(K_{i})\left[\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right]\right|.\end{split}

(199)

We now introduce the function
{\rm Disc}(\eta,\delta,N)
, defined as

{\rm Disc}(\eta,\delta,N)=\max_{X_{N}\in\Lambda_{N}}\max_{i}\left|{\rm emp}_{N}(K_{i})-\rho(K_{i})\right|.

(200)

Note that for any fixed
\eta>0
, we have that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Disc}(\eta,\delta,N)=0,

(201)

since
{\rm emp}_{N}
converges to
\rho
weakly in the sense of probability measures. On the other hand,

\begin{split}\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\left[{\rm emp}_{N}(K_{i})-\rho(K_{i})\right]\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right|&\leq{\rm Disc}(\eta,\delta,N)\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right|\\
&\leq\frac{c_{g,\epsilon}}{\eta^{d}}{\rm Disc}(\eta,\delta,N),\end{split}

(202)

where
c_{g,\epsilon}
is defined as

c_{g,\epsilon}=\max_{y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|g^{\epsilon}(y)\right|<\infty.

(203)

We now turn to the second term in the last line of (
199
). For this, let
r_{1}>0
be big enough compared to
\eta
. Then we have that

\begin{split}&\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\rho(K_{i})\left[\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right]\right|\\
\leq&\sum_{i\neq j}\left|\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\eta^{d}\left[\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right]\right|\\
=&\sum_{i\neq j,K_{i}\subset B(x,r_{1})}\left|\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\eta^{d}\left[\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right]\right|\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\sum_{i\neq j,K_{i}\cap(\mathbb{T}^{d}\setminus B(x,r_{1}))\neq\phi}\left|\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\eta^{d}\left[\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right]\right|.\end{split}

(204)

Proceeding as in step 3.1, we have that

\begin{split}\sum_{i\neq j,K_{i}\subset B(x,r_{1})}\left|\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\eta^{d}\left[\max_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)-\min_{y\in K_{i}}g^{\epsilon}(x-y)\right]\right|\leq 2\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}I(r_{1}+\epsilon),\\

\int_{K_{j}}g(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho)(y).

(206)

Proceeding again as in step 3.1, we have that

\left|\int_{K_{j}}g(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho)(y)\right|\leq(1+C_{S,R,\tau})I(\eta).

(207)

Putting everything together, we have that

h^{{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho^{\epsilon}}\leq\frac{c_{g,\epsilon}}{\eta^{d}}{\rm Disc}(\eta,\delta,N)+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\Psi(r_{1}-\eta,\eta)+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}I(r_{1}+\epsilon)+(1+C_{S,R,\tau})I(\eta).

(208)

Substep 3.3
[Conclusion of the energy estimate]

By adding and subtracting terms, we have that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\begin{split}&\left|{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})\right|\\
\leq&\left|{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})\right|+\left|\mathcal{E}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})\right|\\
\leq&\left|{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})\right|+\left|\mathcal{E}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})\right|\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\left|\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})\right|.\end{split}

(209)

Using polar factorization for the quadratic form
\mu\to\mathcal{E}(\mu)
, we have

\begin{split}\left|\mathcal{E}({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})\right|&=\left|\mathcal{G}\left({\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}+\rho^{\epsilon}-2\mu_{\theta},{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho^{\epsilon}\right)\right|\\
&\leq 4\|h^{{\rm emp}_{N}^{\epsilon}-\rho^{\epsilon}}\|_{L^{\infty}}.\end{split}

(210)

Putting together all the previous estimates, we have that, for any
\epsilon,\eta,r,r_{1}>0
,

\begin{split}&\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})\right|\\
\leq&C_{S,R,\tau}I(r+\epsilon)+C\Psi(r-\epsilon,\epsilon)+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}\Psi(r_{1}-\eta,\eta)+\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}}I(r_{1}+\epsilon)+\\
&\ \ \ \ \ \ (1+C_{S,R,\tau})I(\eta)+\left|\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho^{\epsilon}-\mu_{\theta})\right|.\end{split}

(211)

Taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, then
\eta\to 0
, then
r,r_{1}\to 0
, we have that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|{\rm F}_{N}({\rm emp}_{N},\mu_{\theta})-\mathcal{E}(\rho-\mu_{\theta})\right|=0.

(212)

Since the estimates are valid for any
X_{N}\in\Lambda_{N}
, we have that convergence is uniform.

This concludes the proof of Proposition
8.1
.
