A general maximal projection approach to uniformity testing on the hypersphere
Jaroslav Borodavka, Bruno Ebner

Abstract
We propose a novel approach to uniformity testing on the d d -dimensional unit hypersphere 𝒮 d − 1 \mathcal{S}^{d-1} based on maximal projections. This approach gives a unifying view on the classical uniformity tests of Rayleigh and Bingham, and it links to measures of multivariate skewness and kurtosis. We derive the limiting distribution under the null hypothesis using limit theorems for Banach space valued stochastic processes and we present strategies to simulate the limiting processes by applying results on the theory of spherical harmonics. We examine the behavior under contiguous and fixed alternatives and show the consistency of the testing procedure for some classes of alternatives. For the first time in uniformity testing on the sphere, we derive local Bahadur efficiency statements. We evaluate the theoretical findings and empirical powers of the procedures in a broad competitive Monte Carlo simulation study and, finally, apply the new tests to a data set on midpoints of large craters on the moon.

Introduction

Testing uniformity on the circle, the sphere and the hypersphere
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|=1\}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
, of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, endowed with the Euclidean norm
\|x\|=\sqrt{x^{\top}x}
, are classical and still up-to-date research fields in directional statistics. Here and in the following,
⊤
stands for the transpose of a matrix or a vector. We numerate just a small subset of fields, where data on the surface of the unit hypersphere
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is applied: meteorology, geology, paleomagnetism, political sciences, text mining and wildfire orientation, for examples of such datasets, see
[
30
]
and the contributions therein. The first step to serious statistical inference on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is to check whether or not a sample of unit vectors stems from the uniform law, since this distribution characterizes the absence of structure in directional data. To be specific, we model the observed data by independent identically distributed (iid.) column random vectors
U,U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
taking values in
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. The testing problem of interest is whether or not the hypothesis

H_{0}:\mathbb{P}^{U}=\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)

holds, against general alternatives. Here,
\mathbb{P}^{U}
stands for the distribution of
U
and
\mathcal{U}(\cdot)
denotes the uniform distribution. This problem has been extensively studied in the literature: Lord Rayleigh presented the first test of uniformity in
[
38
]
based on the norm of the arithmetic mean. Rayleigh’s test was followed by circular tests based on the classical goodness-of-fit measures of Kolmogorov-Smirnov type in
[
27
]
and of Cramér-von Mises type in
[
43
]
. Later, Bingham developed a test of uniformity in
[
9
]
based on the sample scatter matrix and Giné, see
[
21
]
, introduced the so-called Sobolev-tests. We refer to
[
25
,
33
]
for more details on these tests and to
[
24
]
for some new developments. More recently,
[
10
]
proposed a Kolmogorov-Smirnov type test based on random projections,
[
16
]
suggest a procedure using powers of volumes of nearest-neighbor spheres, and
[
19
]
consider the Cramér-von Mises counterpart to
[
10
]
. For details on this approach as well as more recent developments in uniformity testing of axial data see
[
29
]
, chapter 6. The authors of the review article
[
20
]
give an overview of uniformity tests on the hypersphere. Comparative Monte Carlo simulation studies are found in
[
14
]
for
d=3
and for higher dimensions in
[
17
]
.

A well-known characterizing property of
\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
is invariance with respect to rotations about the origin. Any test (say)
T_{n}
of uniformity should therefore inherit this structure and as such be invariant under rotations, i.e.

T_{n}(AU_{1},\ldots,AU_{n})=T_{n}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})\quad\mbox{holds for all}\;A\in\mbox{SO}(d),

(1.1)

where
\mbox{SO}(d)
is the
d
-dimensional rotation group, i.e., for
d\times d
-matrices
A\in\mbox{SO}(d)
we have
AA^{\top}=A^{\top}A=I_{d}
and
\mbox{det}(A)=1
. We denote the identity matrix by
I_{d}
, and
\mbox{det}(\cdot)
is the notation for the determinant of a matrix. In the following, we call the property (
1.1
) rotational invariance of the test statistic
T_{n}
.

We propose a novel class of statistics
T_{n,\,\beta}
based on powers of maximal projections. In this spirit assume
U\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
and by
[
7
]
, we have using the rotational invariance of the uniform distribution and symmetry arguments for every
b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}

\psi_{d}(\beta)=E(b^{\top}U)^{\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left((\beta+1)/2\right)\Gamma\left(d/2\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left((\beta+d)/2\right)},&\mbox{if}\,\beta\,\mbox{is even},\\
0,&\mbox{if}\,\beta\,\mbox{is odd},\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function. Hence
\psi_{d}(\beta)
is independent of the choice of
b
, a property that likewise follows by the rotation invariance of the uniform law on the sphere. Next, we define the family of statistics

T_{n,\,\beta}=T_{n,\,\beta}(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b^{\top}U_{j})^{\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right)^{2},\quad\beta\in\mathbb{N}.

(1.3)

It is obvious that
T_{n,\,\beta}
is rotational invariant for every
\beta
due to the rotational invariance of the maximum functional.

Interestingly,
T_{n,\,\beta}
has close connections to well-known classical tests such as the Rayleigh test, the Bingham test and to measures of multivariate skewness and kurtosis by Malkovich and Afifi.
First, notice that with the sample mean of the observations
\overline{U}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}
we have

T_{n,1}=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}b^{\top}U_{j}\right)^{2}=n\|\overline{U}_{n}\|^{2}\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(b^{\top}\frac{\overline{U}_{n}}{\|\overline{U}_{n}\|}\right)^{2}=n\|\overline{U}_{n}\|^{2},

since the scalar product in the maximum is the cosine of the angle between the two unit vectors, which takes its maximum for
b=\frac{\overline{U}_{n}}{\|\overline{U}_{n}\|}
. Hence we have an equivalent test as the classical Rayleigh test, see
[
38
]
, given by
R_{n}=2nd\|\overline{U}_{n}\|^{2}
.

Second, with the sample scatter matrix
S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}U_{j}^{\top}
we have

T_{n,2}=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b^{\top}U_{j})^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{2}=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(b^{\top}Sb-\frac{1}{d}\right)^{2}=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(b^{\top}\left(S-\frac{1}{d}I_{d}\right)b\right)^{2},

Notice that
T_{n,2}
is the squared spectral norm of
S-E(UU^{\top})
for
U\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
, hence it compares the scatter matrix to the covariance matrix of
U
, which is in the same spirit as the Bingham test, see
[
9
]
. Note that by the Courant–Fischer–Weyl min-max principle from linear algebra, we have

T_{n,2}=n(\max(|\lambda_{min}|,|\lambda_{max}|))^{2},

where
\lambda_{min}
and
\lambda_{max}
are the minimal and maximal eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
S-\frac{1}{d}I_{d}
.

Third, we have

T_{n,3}=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b^{\top}U_{j})^{3}\right)^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{n,4}=n\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b^{\top}U_{j})^{4}-\frac{3}{d(d+2)}\right)^{2},

(1.4)

which can be interpreted as analogs to the multivariate sample skewness and sample kurtosis by Malkovich and Afifi, for a definition see
[
31
]
. For
T_{n,\,\beta}
,
\beta>2
, no explicit closed form and easy to calculate formula is known. The authors of
[
31
]
suggest using the Newton-Raphson method to obtain a good approximation of the maximal value in (
1.3
). Since for such a numerical routine, the choice of some good start values is not straightforward, we suggest to use a random approach, see Section
6
, which is related to the idea of random projections as suggested in
[
10
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: We present asymptotic theory under the null hypothesis in Section

2
Asymptotic null distribution of
T_{n,\,\beta}

Let
C(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R})
be the Banach space of continuous functions
f:\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}|f(b)|
. We introduce the stochastic process

Z_{n,\beta}(b)=\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b^{\top}U_{j})^{\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right),\quad b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

For the covariance structure in the following theorem, we write

\displaystyle\eta_{\beta}(b,c)=\mathbb{E}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU)^{\,\beta}({c}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU)^{\,\beta}=\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}\frac{(c_{j,\,d}(\beta))^{2}}{\nu_{d}(j)}P_{j}^{\,d}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptc),\quad b,c\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

(2.1)

Here,
P_{j}^{\,d}(\cdot)
is the
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomial of order
j
, for a definition see (
A.4
),
\nu_{d}(j)
is the dimension of the space of
d
-dimensional spherical harmonics of order
j
, see (
A.1
), and
c_{j,d}(\beta)
are constants only depending on
j,d
and
\beta
, compare with (
A.5
) and Proposition
A.8
. An explicit way of calculation can be found in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
be iid. with
U_{1}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
. For fixed
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a centred Gaussian process
Z_{\beta}(b)
,
b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
with continuous sample paths and covariance kernel

\rho_{\beta}(b,c)=\eta_{\beta}(b,c)-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta),\quad b,c\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

(2.2)

Regarding
Z_{\beta}(\cdot)
as a random element of
C(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R})
, we have

Z_{n,\beta}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z_{\beta}(\cdot).

Remark 2.2
.

For the special cases of Section
1
and some higher powers
\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(b,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}(b^{\top}c),

\displaystyle\rho_{2}(b,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d(d+2)}\left(2(b^{\top}c)^{2}+1\right)-\frac{1}{d^{2}},

\displaystyle\rho_{3}(b,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d(d+2)(d+4)}\left(6(b^{\top}c)^{3}+9(b^{\top}c)\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{4}(b,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(24(b^{\top}c)^{4}+72(b^{\top}c)^{2}+9\right)\prod_{j=0}^{3}(d+2j)^{-1}-\frac{9}{d^{2}(d+2)^{2}},

\displaystyle\rho_{5}(b,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(120(b^{\top}c)^{5}+600(b^{\top}c)^{3}+225(b^{\top}c)\right)\prod_{j=0}^{4}(d+2j)^{-1},

\displaystyle\rho_{6}(b,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(720(b^{\top}c)^{6}+5400(b^{\top}c)^{4}+4050(b^{\top}c)^{2}+225\right)\prod_{j=0}^{5}(d+2j)^{-1}-\frac{225}{d^{2}(d+2)^{2}(d+4)^{2}},

and thus explicit formulas for the covariance kernel in Theorem

.

Note that the covariance kernel
\rho_{\beta}(b,c)
solely depends on the scalar product
b^{\top}c
and hence can be written as a function (say)
\rho_{\beta}(b,c)=Q(b^{\top}c)
, where

is a polynomial of degree
\beta
. Kernels of this particular structure are called zonal kernels, for an application of Gaussian processes with zonal covariance kernel in machine learning see

for all

follows by the inequalities of Cauchy–Schwarz and Popoviciu, since the projections are bounded random variables.

Define the integral operators
K_{\beta}
for
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
given by

K_{\beta}f(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\rho_{\beta}(\omega,x)f(\omega)\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega),\quad x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\;f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma),

(2.3)

where integration is with respect to the unique spherical Lebesgue measure
\sigma
on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. Since
\rho_{\beta}
is continuous on a compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the operator

is compact from
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
to
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
. Due to the zonal covariance structure we can even show that

is a finite-rank operator, i.e., an operator whose range is finite-dimensional. The latter, and other properties, are presented and proved in the next proposition. In the following, we denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and

be defined as in (

).

i)

For any spherical harmonic

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\hskip 14.22636pt\left(\frac{c_{k,\,d}(\beta)}{\nu_{d}(k)}\right)^{2}&,\quad&\textrm{for}\;0<k\leq\beta,\\[7.11317pt]
&\hskip 35.56593pt0&,\quad&\textrm{for}\;k=0\textrm{ or }k>\beta,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(2.4)

with constants

, depending only on

and

, compare with Proposition

ii)

3
Contiguous alternatives

In this section, we consider a triangular array
U_{n1},\ldots,U_{nn}
of rowwise identically independent distributed random vectors on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
having the density function
f_{n}(x)=\mu(x)\left(1+h(x)/\sqrt{n}\right),

x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1},
where
\mu(\cdot)
denotes the density of the uniform distribution with respect to the spherical Lebesgue measure
\sigma
, and

is a bounded measurable function satisfying
\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}h(x)\mu(x)\,\mbox{d}\sigma(x)=0
. We consider

.

First, define

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(n)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(\mu\;\sigma)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{(n)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(f_{n}\;\sigma)

on the measurable space
(\mathfrak{X}_{n},\mathfrak{B}_{n})=\displaystyle{\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\mathcal{M})}
, where
\mathcal{M}
denotes the class of subsets of
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
that are measurable with respect to
\sigma
. Further, denote the likelihood ratio with
L_{n}=\frac{d\mathbb{A}^{(n)}}{d\mathbb{P}^{(n)}}
and write

S_{\beta}:\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\times\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad(a,b)\mapsto S_{\beta}(a,b)=(a^{\top}b)^{\,\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta),

where
\psi_{d}(\cdot)
is defined in (
1.2
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Under the standing assumptions we have for the triangular array
U_{n1},\ldots,U_{nn}

Z_{n,\beta}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z_{\beta}(\cdot)+S^{*}_{\beta}(\cdot)\quad\text{under}\;\;\mathbb{A}^{(n)}

in
C(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R})
, where
Z_{\beta}(\cdot)
is a centred Gaussian process in
C(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R})
having covariance kernel
\rho_{\beta}
from Theorem
2.1
. The shift function
S^{*}_{\beta}(\cdot)
is given by

S^{*}_{\beta}(b)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}S_{\beta}(u,b)h(u)\;\mbox{d}\sigma(u),\;\;b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

(3.1)

As a direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
and the continuous mapping theorem we have the following Corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3.1
, we have

T_{n,\,\beta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left(Z_{\beta}(b)+S^{*}_{\beta}(b)\right)^{2}\quad\text{under}\;\;\mathbb{A}^{(n)}.

Example 3.3
.

As an example we consider the alternatives where

h(x)=h_{m,\theta}(x)=P_{m}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}x),\quad x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\;m\geq 1,

is the Legendre polynomial of degree
m
and
\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is fixed. Note that
x\mapsto P_{m}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}x)
is a spherical harmonic function of degree
m
such that the orthogonality property of spherical harmonics, see
[
22
]
, Section 3.2, implies

\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}h(x)\mbox{d}\sigma(x)=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}P_{m}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}x)P_{0}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}x)\;\mbox{d}\sigma(x)=0,

since
P_{0}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}\cdot)=1
is the spherical harmonic of degree 0. If
X
follows the law given by the density
f_{n}
we have for any orthogonal
d\times d
-matrix
A
with
A\theta=\theta
that the distribution of
AX
is the same as the distribution of
X
, hence these types of alternatives are rotationally symmetric about
\theta
. An application of the Funk/Hecke-Theorem
A.3
shows for
b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}

\displaystyle S^{*}_{\beta}(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}S_{\beta}(u,b)h(u)\;\mbox{d}\sigma(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left((b^{\top}u)^{\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right)P_{m}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}u)\;\mbox{d}\sigma(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{d}(\beta,m)P_{m}^{\,d}(\theta^{\top}b),

where

\lambda_{d}(\beta,m)=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{m}^{\,d}(t)\left(t^{\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\mbox{d}t.

It follows with (
A.5
) and Proposition
A.5

\displaystyle\lambda_{d}(\beta,m)=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}c_{j,\,d}(\beta)\langle P_{m}^{\,d},P_{j}^{\,d}\rangle-\psi_{d}(\beta)\langle P_{m}^{\,d},P_{0}^{\,d}\rangle\right]=\frac{c_{m,\,d}(\beta)}{\nu_{d}(m)},

so that

S_{\beta}^{*}(b)=\frac{c_{m,\,d}(\beta)}{\nu_{d}(m)}P_{m}^{\,d}({\theta}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptb),\quad b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

(3.2)

Note that
\lambda_{d}(\beta,m)=0
, if
\beta+m
is odd or
m>\beta
, because then the coefficients
c_{m,\,d}(\beta)
in (
A.5
) equal zero. Hence, in these cases we have the same asymptotic behaviour under contiguous alternatives as under the null hypothesis. We can conclude that the tests
T_{n,\,\beta}
are not able to detect the alternatives for such a combination of
\beta
and
m
. For the shift function we have
S_{\beta}^{*}(\theta)=c_{m,\,d}(\beta)/\nu_{d}(m)
, so that there is a non-negative shift in the limiting distribution under contiguous alternatives as long as we can show that the coefficients
c_{m,\,d}(\beta)
are non-negative. We conjecture that this is indeed the case, as all examples after Proposition fulfill this property. That in turn means that, under the assumption of this conjecture, there is a positive shift if
\beta+m
is even and
m\leq\beta
. Thus,
T_{n,\,\beta}
is a family of testing procedures which is able to detect the contiguous alternatives. As indicated in
[
11
]
, Section 2, the famous von Mises–Fisher distribution (see
[
33
]
, Section 9.3, for a definition) with mean direction
\theta
and concentration parameter
\kappa\geq 0
falls into a comparable class of contiguous alternatives. We expect to see matchable power performances of
T_{n,\,\beta}
in the simulation study, see Section
6
.

4
Consistency

In this short section we consider spherical random vectors
U,U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
with a distribution having a continuous density
f
w.r.t. the spherical Lebesgue measure
\sigma
. We adopt the reasoning in
[
8
]
to argue that the considered tests are consistent against a large class of alternatives. If for
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
there is a unit vector (say)
b_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
such that

\zeta(b_{0})=\left(\mathbb{E}(b_{0}^{\top}U)^{\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right)^{2}>0,

the strong law of large numbers shows

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\zeta_{n}(b_{0})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(b_{0}^{\top}U_{j})^{\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right)^{2}=\zeta(b_{0})\quad\mbox{a.s.}

and since
T_{n,\,\beta}/n\geq\zeta_{n}(b_{0})
we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}T_{n,\,\beta}=\infty\quad\mbox{a.s.}

This reasoning shows that the tests
T_{n,\,\beta}
are consistent against each such alternative. Nonetheless, as we have already seen in the last section,
T_{n,\,\beta}
is not consistent against any arbitrary alternative class. For certain combinations of
\beta
and
m
, the order of the Legendre polynomial,
T_{n,\,\beta}
exhibits the same asymptotic behaviour under the alternatives as under the null hypothesis. Another indication for the inconsistency of
T_{n,\,\beta}
can be seen in the case
\beta=1
, which essentially concerns the Rayleigh test. The authors of
[
18
]
have shown that, in the rather general context of rotationally symmetric alternatives with a location and concentration parameter and a defining angular function, the Rayleigh test is blind against certain local alternatives. These local alternatives show polynomial decrease of the concentration parameter towards zero (hence yielding the null hypothesis) and the odd-order derivatives of their angular function vanish at zero. An example of such an alternative is the well-known Watson distribution.

5
Bahadur efficiencies

In this section we present some interesting insights into the Bahadur asymptotic relative efficiencies (ARE) of the statistics
(T_{n,\,\beta})_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}}
. For an elaborate and comprehensive introduction to the concept of Bahadur efficiency we refer the reader to
[
5
]
and
[
35
]
.

We consider alternative classes whose defining density
f(\cdot\,|\,\kappa)
w.r.t.
\sigma
is parameterized through a non-negative number
\kappa\geq 0
, where the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is only obtained for the limit case
\kappa\to 0^{+}
in
L^{1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
, i.e.

\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0^{+}}||f(\cdot\,|\,\kappa)-f(\cdot\,|\,0)||_{L^{1}}=\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left|f(x\,|\,\kappa)-f(x\,|\,0)\right|\,\text{d}\sigma(x)=0.

(5.1)

Hence, the testing problem can be reformulated as

H_{0}:\kappa=0\text{ against }H_{1}:\kappa>0.

(5.2)

In order to properly apply the Bahadur theory, we consider the family of equivalent test statistics
\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}=\sqrt{T_{n,\,\beta}}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
. In the following we mainly focus our attention to the local approximate and the local exact Bahadur ARE. For two statistics
T_{n}^{(1)}
and
T_{n}^{(2)}
these are defined as follows.

The local approximate Bahadur ARE is given by

\Lambda_{T^{(1)},T^{(2)}}^{\text{a}}=\underset{\kappa\to 0^{+}}{\lim}\frac{c_{T^{(1)}}^{\;a}(\kappa)}{c_{T^{(2)}}^{\;a}(\kappa)},

where
c_{T}^{\;a}
denotes the approximate Bahadur slope of a statistic
T_{n}
, see
[
35
]
, page 10.

The local exact Bahadur ARE is defined by

\Lambda_{T^{(1)},T^{(2)}}^{\text{ex}}=\underset{\kappa\to 0^{+}}{\lim}\frac{c_{T^{(1)}}(\kappa)}{c_{T^{(2)}}(\kappa)},

where
c_{T}
denotes the exact Bahadur slope of a statistic
T_{n}
, see
[
35
]
, Section 1.2. In many cases the approximate and exact Bahadur slopes coincide in the proximity of the null hypothesis, i.e. in the limit case. This can also be observed for
(\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta})_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}}
in the next proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
. The approximate Bahadur slope of
\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}
is given by

c_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta}}^{\;a}(\kappa)=\frac{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\rho_{\beta}(b,b)}=\frac{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{j}\nu_{d}(j)},\quad\kappa>0,

with the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
from Proposition
2.3
,
\nu_{d}(j)
as in (
A.1
) and

\gamma_{\kappa}(b)=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU)^{\,\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta),\quad b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1},

where
U\sim f(\cdot\,|\,\kappa)
,
\kappa>0
. Furthermore, the exact Bahadur slope of
\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}
is for sufficiently small
\kappa>0
given by

c_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta}}(\kappa)=\frac{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{j}\nu_{d}(j)}+o\left(\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)\right).

The Bahadur slopes of
\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}
apparently coincide locally, so that we do not distinguish between them anymore. In the following, we consider some explicit alternative classes and determine the local Bahadur ARE of
\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}
w.r.t.
M_{n}=-2\log(\Lambda_{n})
, where
\Lambda_{n}
is the likelihood-ratio test. It is well-known that the exact and approximate Bahadur slope of
M_{n}
are the same and are given by

c_{M}^{\;a}(\kappa)=c_{M}(\kappa)=2\text{KL}(\kappa,0),\quad\kappa>0,

where

\text{KL}(\kappa,\kappa_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa}\left[\log\left(\frac{f(U\,|\,\kappa)}{f(U\,|\,\kappa_{0}}\right)\right],\quad\kappa,\kappa_{0}\geq 0,

is the Kullback–Leibler information number for
U\sim f(\cdot\,|\,\kappa)
.
The proof for the following quite technical calculations can be found in Appendix
B
.

Example 5.2
.

A random vector
U
with values in
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
has a von Mises–Fisher distribution

\text{vMF}(\theta,\kappa)
with mean direction
\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and concentration parameter
\kappa\geq 0
if the density w.r.t.
\sigma
is given by

f(x\,|\,\kappa)=\frac{\left(\kappa/2\right)^{d/2-1}}{2\pi^{d/2}I_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\kappa)}\exp(\kappa{x}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271pt\theta),\quad x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1},

(5.3)

where
I_{\frac{d}{2}-1}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind and order
d/2-1
, see (
B.2
). In case of the von Mises–Fisher alternative class
\text{vMF}(\theta,\kappa)
with a fixed mean direction
\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and
\kappa>0
we have

\lim_{\kappa\to 0^{+}}\frac{\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{2\textsc{KL}(\kappa,0)}=\lambda_{1}\nu_{d}(1),

whereby the local Bahadur ARE is

\Lambda_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta},M}^{\text{ex}}=\Lambda_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta},M}^{\text{a}}=\underset{\kappa\to 0^{+}}{\lim}\frac{c_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta}}^{\;a}(\kappa)}{c_{M}^{\;a}(\kappa)}=\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{j}\nu_{d}(j)}\;\underset{\kappa\to 0^{+}}{\lim}\frac{\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{2\text{KL}(\kappa,0)}=\frac{\lambda_{1}\nu_{d}(1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{j}\nu_{d}(j)}.

The special case of
\beta=1
yields the local asymptotic optimality of
\widetilde{T}_{1}
in the Bahadur sense (see
[
35
]
, page 9, for this concept)

\Lambda_{\widetilde{T}_{1},\text{M}}^{\text{ex}}=\Lambda_{\widetilde{T}_{1},\text{M}}^{\text{a}}=1.

This is not surprising since the Rayleigh test is exactly the likelihood-ratio test in the von Mises–Fisher model. On the contrary, if
\beta
is even, then

\Lambda_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta},\text{M}}^{\text{ex}}=\Lambda_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta},\text{M}}^{\text{a}}=0,

since the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
equals zero in this case.

6
Simulations

We present a competitive Monte-Carlo simulation study, that was implemented and performed in the statistical computing environment
R
, see
[
36
]
. The maximum on the hypersphere in (
1.3
) cannot be calculated analytically, and therefore one has to approximate it with a computationally fast method. We suggest to use a uniform random cover of the hypersphere: Simulate a large number
m
of uniformly distributed points on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
,
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
(say), evaluate the so chosen centered and squared projections
\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(B_{k}^{\top}U_{j})^{\,\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right)^{2}
,
k=1,\ldots,m
, and approximate the maximum value in (
1.3
) by the discrete maximum over all
k
. Critical values for
T_{n,\,\beta}
under
H_{0}
have been simulated with 20000 replications and a random cover of
m=5000
points for
d=2,3
and with 20000 replications and
m=20000
points for
d=5,10
, see Table
2
.

The critical values in the rows in Table
2
denoted by ”
\infty
” and ”
\infty^{*}
” represent approximations of the limit random element
\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}Z^{2}_{\beta}(b)
in Corollary
2.6
via two methods. The first method, which corresponds to the rows with ”
\infty
”, simulates the same random cover of the sphere as above, and it considers a large number (say)
\ell
of random variables
Z_{j}=\max(X_{j}^{2})
,
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, with iid.
X_{j}\sim\mbox{N}_{m}(0,\Sigma_{\beta})
, where
\mbox{N}_{m}
is the
m
-variate normal distribution and
\Sigma_{\beta}=\left(\rho_{\beta}(B_{k_{1}},B_{k_{2}})\right)_{k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}
is a singular
m\times m
-covariance matrix for
m\geq d
and
\rho_{\beta}
is the covariance kernel in (
2.2
) for which we have already summarized explicit formulas in Remark
2.2
. Here
x^{2}
is shorthand for the vector of squared components of
x
. Next, we calculate the empirical
95\%
quantile of
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{\ell}
, where each approximation was simulated with
\ell=100000
and
m=1000
for

as well as
\ell=10000
and

for

.
The second method utilizes the alternative representation of the Gaussian process from Proposition
2.4
. For this purpose, we need orthonormal bases of the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
for
k=0,\ldots,\beta
and the corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
. We have already presented an explicit list of these eigenvalues for
\beta=1,\ldots,6
at the end of section
2
. In each replication step of the Monte-Carlo simulation we generate an array
N_{k,j}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,\nu_{d}(k)\}
of independent unit normal random variables, cover
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, once again, with

uniformly distributed points
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
and calculate
Y_{i}=\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\sum_{k=0}^{\beta}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}\phi_{k,j}(b)N_{k,j},i=1,\ldots,m
.
Repeating this step for the number of set replications
\ell
yields an approximation of the limit distribution of
T_{n,\,\beta}
in the same fashion as before. However, so far there is no library with a stable implementation of orthonormal spherical harmonics in higher dimensions and orders, which is why we restricted the simulation with this method to the case of
d=2
. We used the package
HFT.m
in
Mathematica
in order to implement an orthonormal basis in
R
. Each approximation was performed with
\ell=20000
and
m=2500
.

We consider testing for uniformity on the unit circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}
, on the unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{2}
and on the hypersphere
\mathcal{S}^{5}
, and we divide the presentation of the simulation study into two parts, since different competing tests are considered in these cases.

Generating uniformly distributed random numbers on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
can be done efficiently, since for a random vector
N\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(0,I_{d})
, where
\mathcal{N}_{d}
stands for the
d
-variate normal distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\frac{N}{||N||}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right).

This property is merely a consequence of the rotational invariance of
N
and the fact that the uniform distribution is the only rotationally invariant distribution on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. We consider the following alternatives to the uniform distribution:

•

von Mises–Fisher distribution:

This alternative class was already introduced in Example
5.2
. The density is given by

\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\ni x\mapsto\frac{\left(\kappa/2\right)^{d/2-1}}{2\pi^{d/2}I_{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\kappa)}\exp(\kappa x^{\top}\theta),

where
I_{\frac{d}{2}-1}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind and order
d/2-1
. This class is denoted with
\text{vMF}(\theta,\kappa)
.

•

Mix of von Mises–Fisher distributions with two centers:

Let
U
be uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
,
p\in(0,1)
and
Y_{i}\sim\text{vMF}(\theta_{i},\kappa_{i})
with corresponding location and concentration parameters for
i=1,2
. Let
U
,
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be stochastically independent. Then we generate a random sample
X
according to

X=Y_{1}\mathds{1}_{\{U<p\}}+Y_{2}\mathds{1}_{\{U\geq p\}}.

We denote this alternative class with
\text{Mix-vMF}(p,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
.

•

Mix of von Mises–Fisher distributions with three centers:

Let
U
be uniformly distributed on
(0,1)
,
p\in(0,1/2)
and
Y_{i}\sim\text{vMF}(\theta_{i},\kappa_{i})
with corresponding location and concentration parameters for
i=1,2,3
. Let
U
,
Y_{1}
,
Y_{2}
and
Y_{3}
be stochastically independent. Then we generate a random sample
X
according to

X=Y_{1}\mathds{1}_{\{U<p\}}+Y_{2}\mathds{1}_{\{p\leq U<2p\}}+Y_{3}\mathds{1}_{\{U\geq 2p\}}.

We denote this alternative class with
\text{Mix-vMF}(p,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})
.

•

Bingham distribution:

The density

\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\ni x\mapsto\frac{1}{c(d,A)}\exp(x^{\top}Ax)

with a symmetric
d\times d
-matrix A and a normalizing constant
c(d,A)
yields the Bingham model. In the following this alternative class is denoted with
Bing(A)
.

•

Legendre polynomial distribution:

We defined the Legendre polynomial alternative class
\text{LP}_{m}(\theta,\kappa)
with order
m\in\mathbb{N}
, direction
\theta\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and
\kappa\in[0,1]
in Example
5.4
. This class is given by the density

f(x\,|\,\kappa)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\left(1+\kappa P_{m}^{\,d}({\theta}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptx)\right),\quad x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

Due to
f(x\,|\,\kappa)\leq(1+\kappa)|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|^{-1}
,
x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
,
the acceptance rejection algorithm gives us a simple method to generate random numbers of this alternative class, see
[
26
]
for a description of this algorithm. Simulations on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
with
\kappa=1
revealed that the order
m
dictates the formation of exactly
m
clusters, which spread out equidistantly from the direction
\theta
over the unit circle. So, numerically, this class generalizes uni- and multipolar distributions, among them the von Mises–Fisher and Watson distribution.

Further details and properties of the presented distributions may be found in
[
33
]
, Section 9.3 and 10.3. Throughout the whole chapter the nominal level of significance is set to
0.05
. Empirical critical values for the competing statistics have been computed with a replication number of
l=20000
as well. The empirical powers in all tables are based on
5000
replications of a testing decision and are, in reality, rejection frequencies. Next, we specify the considered alternatives further due to limited space in the tables.
Let

\displaystyle\theta_{1}=(1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top},\quad\theta_{2}=(-1,\ldots,-1)^{\top},\quad\theta_{3}=(-1,1,\ldots,1)^{\top},

\displaystyle A_{1}=\text{diag}(1,2,\ldots,d),\quad A_{2}=\text{diag}(-d,0,\ldots,0,d).

If directions do not lie on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, then the functions in
R
use the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
in order to generate random numbers of that alternative. Then let

•

vMF
{}_{1}(\kappa)=\text{vMF}(\theta_{1},\kappa)
,

•

Mix-vMF
{}_{1}(p)=\text{Mix-vMF}(p,-\theta_{1},\theta_{1},1,1)
and

Mix-vMF
{}_{2}(p)=\text{Mix-vMF}(p,-\theta_{1},\theta_{1},1,4)
,

•

Mix-vMF
{}_{3}(p)=\text{Mix-vMF}(p,\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{1},2,3,3)
and

Mix-vMF
{}_{4}(p)=\text{Mix-vMF}(p,\theta_{2},\theta_{3},\theta_{1},2,3,4)

6.1
Unit circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}

In this subsection the sample of random vectors on
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is expressed in polar coordinates, i.e. for
U_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, we write
U_{i}=(\cos(\vartheta_{i}),\sin(\vartheta_{i}))^{\top}
with a random angle
\vartheta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
. We consider the subsequent competing test procedures on the unit sphere. The references next to them refer to further information about the respective statistic. Note that large values are significant for all presented test statistics except for the statistic by Cuesta-Albertos et al. in
[
10
]
.

•

Kuiper test,
[
27
]
:

Let
F_{n}(\vartheta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{\vartheta_{i}\leq\vartheta\}}
be the empirical distribution function based on the angles
\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{n}
and
F(\vartheta)=\frac{\vartheta}{2\pi}\mathds{1}_{[0,2\pi)}(\vartheta)+\mathds{1}_{[2\pi,\infty)}(\vartheta)
the distribution function of the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
for
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}
. The Kuiper test considers the quantities

\displaystyle D_{n}^{+}=\sqrt{n}\underset{\vartheta\in[0,2\pi)}{\sup}\left(F_{n}(\vartheta)-F(\vartheta)\right)=\sqrt{n}\underset{i=1,\ldots,n}{\max}\left(\frac{i}{n}-X_{i}\right),

\displaystyle D_{n}^{-}=\sqrt{n}\underset{\vartheta\in[0,2\pi)}{\sup}\left(F(\vartheta)-F_{n}(\vartheta)\right)=\sqrt{n}\underset{i=1,\ldots,n}{\max}\left(X_{i}-\frac{i-1}{n}\right),

with
X_{i}=\frac{\vartheta_{(i)}}{2\pi}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
\vartheta_{(1)},\ldots,\vartheta_{(n)}
is the ordered sample of the random angles. Then the test utilizes the statistic

V_{n}=D_{n}^{+}+D_{n}^{-}.

•

Watson test,
[
43
]
:

According to the previous definitions, the Watson test is given by

\displaystyle U_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[F_{n}(\vartheta)-F(\vartheta)-\int_{0}^{2\pi}F_{n}(\omega)-F(\omega)\;\text{d}F(\omega)\right]^{2}\;\text{d}F(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\left(X_{i}-\frac{i-1/2}{n}\right)-\left(\bar{X}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right]^{2}+\frac{1}{12n},

where
\bar{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
.

•

Ajne test,
[
1
]
:

The Ajne test on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
is based on the statistic

A_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[N(\alpha)-\frac{n}{2}\right]^{2}\;\text{d}\alpha=\frac{n}{4}-\frac{1}{n\pi}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}d_{c}(\vartheta_{i},\vartheta_{j}).

Here,

N(\alpha)=\#\{\vartheta_{1},\ldots,\vartheta_{n}\,|\,d_{c}(\alpha,\vartheta_{i})<\pi/2,\;i=1,\ldots,n\},\quad\alpha\in[0,2\pi),

with
d_{c}(\alpha,\vartheta)=\min(|\alpha-\vartheta|,\,2\pi-|\alpha-\vartheta|)
,
\vartheta\in[0,2\pi)
.

•

Rayleigh test,
[
38
]
:

The classical Rayleigh test on
\mathcal{S}^{1}
is given by

R_{n}=2n\|\bar{U}\|^{2}=\frac{2}{n}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sin(\vartheta_{i})\right)^{2}\right].

However, for the simulation we will use the modification as in
[
33
]
, Section 6.3.1, which permits an improved
\chi_{2}^{2}
approximation, i.e., we use the statistic

R_{n}^{\text{ mod}}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2n}\right)R_{n}+\frac{n\|\bar{U}\|^{4}}{2}.

•

test of Cuesta-Albertos et al.,
[
10
]
:

The test by Cuesta-Albertos et al. is based on random projections of the sample
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
. For this, one chooses, independently of
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
, a direction
H\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
and considers the projected random variables
Y_{1}={U_{1}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH,\ldots,Y_{n}={U_{n}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH
. In
[
10
]
, Theorem 2.2, it has been proved that the distribution of
Y_{1}
characterizes, with probability 1, the distribution of
U_{1}
. To that effect,
H_{0}
is almost surely equivalent to

H_{0}^{*}:{U}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH\sim F_{1},

where
U
is a random vector with values in
\mathcal{S}^{1}
and
F_{1}
is the distribution of the random projection
Y_{1}={U_{1}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH
. In the case
d=2

F_{1}(y)=\left(1-\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos(y)\right)\mathds{1}_{[-1,1]}(y)+\mathds{1}_{(1,\infty)}(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}.

The test proceeds as follows:

i)

Choose
q\in\mathbb{N}
random projections
H_{1},\ldots,H_{q}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
.

ii)

For
m=1,\ldots,q
calculate the p-values
p_{m}
of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics

K_{n,m}=\underset{y\in[-1,1]}{\sup}|F_{n,m}(y)-F_{1}(y)|,

where
F_{n,m}
is the empirical distribution function based on
{U_{1}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH_{m},\ldots,{U_{n}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH_{m}
.

iii)

Reject
H_{0}^{*}
, and thus
H_{0}
, for small values of the aggregated test statistic

CA_{n}^{q}=\min\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{q}\}.

This test procedure is a modification of the test that only chooses a single random direction in order to mitigate poor power due to a possibly unfavorable choice of said random direction.

6.2
Unit sphere
\mathcal{S}^{2}
and unit hypersphere
\mathcal{S}^{5}

The Ajne and Rayleigh test statistic can be extended to
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
,
d\geq 2
, in a straightforward way, since they are special cases of the fruitful Sobolev test class, see
[

[

•

Ajne test:

The higher-dimensional extension of the Ajne test happens via

A_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2-1)}{2n\pi^{d/2}}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\left[N(\omega)-\frac{n}{2}\right]^{2}\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega)=\frac{n}{4}-\frac{1}{n\pi}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\arccos({U_{i}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU_{j}),

The Rayleigh test on

R_{n}=dn\|\bar{U}\|^{2}

[

]
:

The Bingham test uses the empirical covariance matrix of the sample
S=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}U_{j}^{\top}
and is based on the quantity

B_{n}=\frac{nd(d+2)}{2}\left(\text{trace}(S^{2})-\frac{1}{d}\right).

[

We consider Giné’s

test, which is given by the statistic

G_{n}=\frac{n}{2}-\frac{(d-1)\Gamma(d/2-1)^{2}}{2n\Gamma(d/2)^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\sin(\arccos({U_{i}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU_{j})).

[
10
]
:

The main idea and test procedure is the same as in the case
d=2
. The only quantity that changes in higher dimensions is the distribution function
F_{1}
of the random projection. For the general case, this distribution function may be obtained from the density of the projection as in
[

]
, Section 9.3.1, according to

\displaystyle F_{d-1}(y)

\displaystyle=B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{-1}\int_{-1}^{y}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,\text{d}t\,\mathds{1}_{[-1,1]}(y)+\mathds{1}_{(1,\infty)}(y)

(6.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\text{sign}(y)B_{y^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\right]\mathds{1}_{[-1,1]}(y)+\mathds{1}_{(1,\infty)}(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R},

where
B_{x}(a,b)=B(a,b)^{-1}\int_{0}^{x}t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,\text{d}t
,
x\geq 0,a,b>0
is the regularized, incomplete Beta function and sign
(\cdot)
the usual sign function on
\mathbb{R}
.

Cramér-von Mises type test,
[

]
:

Lastly, we present another test that is based on a projection approach and comes from García-Portugués et al., see
[

]
. It is, to a certain extent, the Cramér-von Mises counterpart to the test by Cuesta-Albertos et al. and, thus, considers the expected value

\text{CvM}_{n}=n\,\mathbb{E}_{H}\left[\int_{-1}^{1}|F_{n,H}(y)-F_{d-1}(y)|^{2}\;\text{d}F_{d-1}(y)\right].

Here,
H\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\right)
,
F_{n,H}
is the empirical distribution function based on
{U_{1}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH,\ldots,\linebreak{U_{n}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptH
, and
F_{d-1}
is the distribution function in (
6.1
). This test statistic can be written as a U-statistic for a practical implementation in
R
according to

\text{CvM}_{n}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\zeta_{d-1}(\arccos({U_{i}}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU_{j}))+\frac{3n-2}{6},

where for
\vartheta\in[0,\pi]

\displaystyle\zeta_{d-1}(\vartheta)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\vartheta}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\vartheta}{2\pi}-1\right)&,\quad&\textrm{for}\;d=2,\\[7.11317pt]
&\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\sin\left(\frac{\vartheta}{2}\right)&,\quad&\textrm{for}\;d=3,\\[7.11317pt]
&\zeta_{1}(\vartheta)+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\left((\pi-\vartheta)\tan\left(\frac{\vartheta}{2}\right)-2\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\vartheta}{2}\right)\right)&,\quad&\textrm{for}\;d=4,\\[7.11317pt]
\hfil\displaystyle\begin{split}&-4\int_{0}^{\,\cos(\vartheta/2)}F_{d-1}(y)F_{d-2}\left(\frac{y\tan(\vartheta/2)}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\right)\;\text{d}F_{d-1}(y)\\
&-\frac{3}{4}+\frac{\vartheta}{2\pi}+2F_{d-1}^{2}\left(\cos\left(\frac{\vartheta}{2}\right)\right)\end{split}&,\quad&\textrm{for}\;d\geq 5.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

8
Conclusion and outlook

We introduced a family of tests of uniformity on the
d
-dimensional hypersphere depending on powers of maximal projections, which include classical uniformity tests of Rayleigh and Bingham as well as measures of multivariate skewness and kurtosis in the sense of Malkovich and Afifi. We proved the weak convergence of the tests to maxima of a squared centred Gaussian process with zonal covariance structure in the space of continuous functions. We derived the eigenvalues of the connected integral operator and applied the largest eigenvalue to derive local Bahadur efficiencies. We proved consistency, as well as the behaviour under contiguous alternatives of the tests in dependence of the parity of the power parameter. Simulations illustrate that the new tests are serious competitors to classical procedures.

We finish the article by pointing out open questions and new directions for further research. The work at hand leaves the topic about the limit distribution of
(T_{n,\,\beta})_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}}
under familiar alternatives, like the von Mises–Fisher distribution or the Watson distribution, untouched. In this context only local Bahadur efficiencies have been derived, which have the major drawback that trivial local Bahadur efficiencies, as in the case of
\widetilde{T}_{n,\,2}
and a von Mises–Fisher distribution, do not suggest that the statistic is blind against the respective alternative. Essentially, this is because the local Bahadur efficiency does not make a statement about rates of consistency under which a statistic might nevertheless recognize the alternative. For example, the authors of
[
18
]
prove that the Bingham test, which has structural resemblance with
T_{n,\,2}
, reliably recognizes a von Mises–Fisher distribution under certain rates of consistency. Hence, it might be interesting to explore the problem of rates of consistency, possibly even for very general alternatives like rotationally symmetric distributions. Another direction for future research could be the problem of local asymptotic normality in the sense of Le Cam. Statistical models with this property allow the construction of optimal tests of a particular kind. This has already been done for the Rayleigh test for certain rotationally symmetric distributions, see
[
11
]
. Since we establish some results of the Le Cam theory for the statistical model in
3
, one might continue from there. In Section
6
we propose a new parametric family of spherical distributions, namely the Legendre polynomial distribution, which has shown some interesting properties in the numerical simulations. Due to its straightforward parameterization it is relatively easy to work with and to simulate. Nonetheless, at the given moment there is not enough substantial knowledge about the properties and practicability of this distribution, especially in higher dimensions, so that this is another option for further research. High-dimensional results for testing uniformity against monotone rotationally symmetric alternatives in
[
11
]
give first results for
T_{n,\,1}
, since this test is equivalent to the Rayleigh test. Similar results for
\beta=2
may be related to
[
12
]
, and the question for larger
\beta
is still open.

Appendix A
Some facts on
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomials and spherical harmonics

A
d
-dimensional spherical harmonic function of order
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the restriction of a
d
-dimensional harmonic polynomial of order
k
to
\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
be the space of
d
-dimensional spherical harmonics of order
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and let
\nu_{d}(k)=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}))
. We have

\nu_{d}(k)=\binom{d+k-1}{k}-\binom{d+k-3}{k-2}=\frac{d+2k-2}{d+k-2}\binom{d+k-2}{d-2},

(A.1)

where the second binomial coefficient after the first the equation is
0
, if
k-2<0
, and the fraction after the second equation is
1
, if
d=2
and
k=0
.
A couple of noteworthy special cases of
\nu_{d}(k)
are

\nu_{d}(0)=1,\quad\nu_{d}(1)=d,\quad\nu_{d}(2)=\frac{(d+2)(d-1)}{2},\quad\nu_{2}(k)=2\,(k>0),\quad\nu_{3}(k)=2k+1,

(A.2)

see
[
22
]
, Section 3, for details on spherical harmonics. Two important theoretical results in the theory of spherical harmonics are the density of finite linear combinations of spherical harmonics in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
and the orthogonality property of spherical harmonics of different order, which can be phrased as follows.

Theorem A.1

(
[
42
]
, Chapter 4, Corollary 2.3)
.

The set of finite linear combinations of elements of
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
.

Proposition A.2

(
[
42
]
, Chapter 4, Corollary 2.4)
.

For
\Phi\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}),\Psi\in\mathcal{H}_{l}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
with
k\neq l
, we have

\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\Phi(\omega)\Psi(\omega)\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega)=0.

With these statements, it is possible to represent a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
uniquely as a series of spherical harmonics. For this purpose, we consider an orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{k,1},\ldots,\phi_{k,\nu_{d}(k)}\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\{\phi_{k,1},\ldots,\phi_{k,\nu_{d}(k)}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
, and we can write

f\overset{L^{2}}{=}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_{k}

(A.3)

with
\Psi_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}\langle f,\phi_{k,j}\rangle_{L^{2}}\phi_{k,j}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
. The following Theorem is called the Funk–Hecke-Theorem and is used frequently.

Theorem A.3

(Funk–Hecke-Theorem,
[
22
]
, Theorem 3.4.1)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
u\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. If
\Lambda
is a bounded, integrable function on
[-1,1]
and
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, then the function
\Lambda({u}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271pt\cdot)\colon\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R};\;x\mapsto\Lambda({u}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptx)
is integrable and

\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\Lambda({u}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptx)\,\phi(x)\,\text{d}\sigma(x)=\lambda_{k}\phi(u)

with

\lambda_{k}=|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{\,d}(t)\Lambda(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,\text{d}t,

where
P_{k}^{\,d}
is the
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomial of order
k
.

The existence and uniqueness of higher dimensional Legendre polynomials are stated in the following Theorem.

Theorem A.4

(
[
22
]
, Theorem 3.3.3)
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there is exactly one polynomial
P_{k}^{\,d}
on
[-1,1]
with the property: If
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{\nu_{d}(k)}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}\phi_{i}(u)\phi_{i}(v)=\frac{\nu_{d}(k)}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}P_{k}^{\,d}({u}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptv),\quad u,v\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

The degree of
P_{k}^{\,d}
is
k
, and the function
P_{k}^{\,d}({u}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271pt\cdot)\colon\mathcal{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R};\;v\mapsto P_{k}^{\,d}({u}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptv)
is for fixed
u\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
a
d
-dimensional spherical harmonic of order
k
. Furthermore,
P_{k}^{\,d}
is an even function, whenever
k
is even, and an odd function, whenever
k
is odd.

The polynomial
P_{k}^{\,d}
is called the
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomial of order
k
. Legendre polynomials of different orders fulfill certain orthogonality properties. To state these properties, we introduce the weighted scalar product

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)g(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,\text{d}t

(A.4)

for bounded and integrable functions
f,g
on
[-1,1]
. The following proposition shows that Legendre polynomials are orthogonal w.r.t. this scalar product.

Proposition A.5

(
[
22
]
, Proposition 3.3.6)
.

Let
k,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. If
P_{k}^{\,d}
and
P_{l}^{\,d}
are
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomials of order
k
and
l
, respectively, then

\left\langle P_{k}^{\,d},P_{l}^{\,d}\right\rangle=\delta_{kl}\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}{\nu_{d}(k)\,|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}=\delta_{kl}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma((d-1)/2)}{\nu_{d}(k)\,\Gamma(d/2)}.

Remark A.6

(
[
22
]
, Lemma 3.3.5)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For the
d
-dimensional Legendre polynomial of order
k
, we have
|P_{k}^{\,d}(t)|\leq 1
for all
t\in[-1,1]
and
P_{k}^{\,d}(1)=1
.

The next result gives two explicit formulas for the calculation of Legendre polynomials of arbitrary dimension and order.

Proposition A.7

(
[
34
]
, Section 1.2, S.16 and Lemma 1.6.1)
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

P_{k}^{\,d}(t)=k!\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1}{2}\right)\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{l}\frac{(1-t^{2})^{l}t^{\,k-2l}}{l!\,(k-2l)!\,\Gamma(l+\frac{d-1}{2})}=\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}a_{2l,k}t^{k-2l},\quad t\in[-1,1],

with

\displaystyle a_{0,0}=1,

\displaystyle a_{2l,k}=\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right)^{l}\frac{\Gamma(d-1)}{\Gamma(d/2)}\frac{2^{k-1}k!}{(k+d-3)!}\frac{\Gamma(k-l+(d-2)/2)}{l!(k-2l)!},\quad l\in\{0,\ldots,\lfloor k/2\rfloor\},\;k>0,

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the lower Gauss bracket.

With these formulas, it is straightforward to obtain the first seven Legendre polynomials, which are given by

\displaystyle P_{0}^{\,d}(t)=1,\quad P_{1}^{\,d}(t)=t,

\displaystyle P_{2}^{\,d}(t)=\frac{1}{d-1}[dt^{2}-1],

\displaystyle P_{3}^{\,d}(t)=\frac{1}{d-1}[(d+2)t^{3}-3t],

\displaystyle P_{4}^{\,d}(t)=\frac{1}{(d-1)(d+1)}[(d+2)(d+4)t^{4}-6(d+2)t^{2}+3],

\displaystyle P_{5}^{\,d}(t)=\frac{1}{(d-1)(d+1)}[(d+4)(d+6)t^{5}-10(d+4)t^{3}+15t],

\displaystyle P_{6}^{\,d}(t)=\frac{1}{(d-1)(d+1)(d+3)}[(d+4)(d+6)(d+8)t^{6}-15(d+4)(d+6)t^{4}+45(d+4)t^{2}-15].

In a reverse conclusion, we can write any
m
th power,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, of a number
t\in[-1,1]
as a linear combination of Legendre polynomials

t^{m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j,\,d}(m)P_{j}^{\,d}(t),\quad t\in[-1,1],

(A.5)

with coefficients
c_{j,\,d}(m)
, which only depend on

), respectively.
Hence,

\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)=\kappa^{2}\lambda_{m}\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}|\,P_{m}^{d}({\theta}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptb)\,|^{2}=\kappa^{2}\lambda_{m}.

In view of these results and the fact, that
\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0^{+}}\xi_{\kappa}=0
, we conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{2\textsc{KL}(\kappa,0)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\kappa^{2}\lambda_{m}}{\cfrac{2\kappa^{2}}{\nu_{d}(m)}\left(1-\cfrac{1}{2(1+\xi_{\kappa})^{2}}\right)+O(\kappa^{3})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow 0^{+}}\cfrac{\lambda_{m}}{\cfrac{2}{\nu_{d}(m)}\left(1-\cfrac{1}{2(1+\xi_{\kappa})^{2}}\right)+O(\kappa)}=\frac{\lambda_{m}}{\cfrac{2}{\nu_{d}(m)}\left(1-\cfrac{1}{2}\right)}=\lambda_{m}\nu_{d}(m).

∎

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Proof.

i)

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Since
\rho_{\beta}(b,c)=Q({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptc)
for all
b,c\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, it follows by the Funk–Hecke-Theorem
A.3
for
x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and
\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})

\displaystyle K_{\beta}\phi(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\rho_{\beta}(\omega,x)\phi(\omega)\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}Q({\omega}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptx)\phi(\omega)\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega)=\lambda_{k}\phi(x)

with

\lambda_{k}=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{\,d}(t)Q(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}dt,

where the expression for
\lambda_{k}
is derived from the Funk–Hecke-Theorem. By Proposition
A.5
we have the following orthogonality property of the Legendre polynomials w.r.t the scalar product in (
A.4
)

\left\langle P_{k}^{d},P_{j}^{d}\right\rangle=\delta_{kj}\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}{\nu_{d}(k)\,|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|},\quad\forall k,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

In view of (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) we have

Q(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}\frac{(c_{j,\,d}(\beta))^{2}}{\nu_{d}(j)}P_{j}^{\,d}(t)-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta),\quad t\in[-1,1].

We calculate for
k\leq\beta
with
P_{0}^{d}\equiv 1

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{\,d}(t)Q(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}\frac{(c_{j,\,d}(\beta))^{2}}{\nu_{d}(j)}\left\langle P_{k}^{d},P_{j}^{d}\right\rangle-\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{\,d}(t)\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}\left(\frac{c_{j,\,d}(\beta)}{\nu_{d}(j)}\right)^{2}\delta_{kj}-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)\left\langle P_{k}^{d},P_{0}^{d}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}\left[\left(\frac{c_{k,\,d}(\beta)}{\nu_{d}(k)}\right)^{2}-\delta_{k0}\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)\right].

Thereby we obtain

\lambda_{k}=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{\,d}(t)Q(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\text{d}t=\left(\frac{c_{k,\,d}(\beta)}{\nu_{d}(k)}\right)^{2}-\delta_{k0}\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta),\quad k\leq\beta.

Evidently is
\lambda_{k}=0
for
k>\beta
by the orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials. Consider the case
k=0
. With
\nu_{d}(0)=1
we already know that

\lambda_{k}=(c_{0,\,d}(\beta))^{2}-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)=(c_{0,\,d}(\beta)-\psi_{d}(\beta))(c_{0,\,d}(\beta)+\psi_{d}(\beta)).

If
\beta
is odd, then
\psi_{d}(\beta)=c_{0,\,d}(\beta)=0
due to (
2.1
) and Proposition
A.8
. Thus let
\beta
be even. Then the Funk–Hecke-Theorem for
1\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, equation (
A.5
), and the orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials yield

\displaystyle\psi_{d}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU)^{\,\beta}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271pt\omega)^{\,\beta}\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega)=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{-1}^{1}t^{\,\beta}(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,\text{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}c_{j,\,d}(\beta)\int_{-1}^{1}P_{j}^{\,d}(t)(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,\text{d}t=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}c_{j,\,d}(\beta)\left\langle P_{j}^{d},P_{0}^{d}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}c_{j,\,d}(\beta)\frac{\delta_{j\,0}|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}{\nu_{d}(0)\,|\mathcal{S}^{d-2}|}=c_{0,\,d}(\beta).

ii)

Let
\{\phi_{k,1},\ldots,\phi_{k,\nu_{d}(k)}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
. Then
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\{\phi_{k,1},\ldots,\phi_{k,\nu_{d}(k)}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
and we get the unique series expansion for
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)

f\overset{L^{2}}{=}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Psi_{k},

(C.1)

with
\Psi_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}\left\langle f,\phi_{k,j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\phi_{k,j}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})
, compare with (
A.3
).
Set
a_{k,j}=\left\langle f,\phi_{k,j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,\nu_{d}(k)\}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It follows for
x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}

\displaystyle K_{\beta}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\rho_{\beta}(\omega,x)f(\omega)\,\text{d}\sigma(\omega)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\left\langle\,\rho_{\beta}(\cdot,x),f\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{d-1}|}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\left\langle\,\rho_{\beta}(\cdot,x),\phi_{k,j}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}\overset{i)}{=}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\lambda_{k}\phi_{k,j}(x)\mathds{1}_{\{k\,\leq\,\beta\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\phi_{k,j}(x).

Hence,
K_{\beta}
is a finite-rank operator, and it has the representation

K_{\beta}f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\phi_{k,j}(x),\quad x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

(C.2)

iii)

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\lambda\notin\{0\}\cup\{\lambda_{k}\;|\;k=1,\ldots,\beta\}
, and consider the equation
\lambda f-K_{\beta}f=0
für
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
as in (
C.1
). Observe for
i\in\{1,\ldots,\nu_{d}(m)\}
,
m\in\{1,\ldots,\beta\}

\displaystyle\lambda\left\langle f,\phi_{m,i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle K_{\beta}f,\phi_{m,i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\left\langle\phi_{k,j},\phi_{m,i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\delta_{mk}\delta_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\delta_{mk}a_{k,i}=\lambda_{m}a_{m,i}=\lambda_{m}\left\langle f,\phi_{m,i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Since
\lambda\neq\lambda_{m}
, it immediately follows that
\left\langle f,\phi_{m,i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=0
. Hence,
\left\langle f,\phi_{m,i}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\nu_{d}(m)\}
,
m\in\{1,\ldots,\beta\}
and therewith
K_{\beta}f=0
. We conclude
\lambda f=0
and, since
\lambda\neq 0
, it follows that
f=0
. This, in turn, means that
\lambda
is a resolvent point.

iv)

With
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\text{d}\sigma)
like in (
C.1
) and by (
C.2
) we conclude

\langle K_{\beta}f,f\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}\langle\phi_{k,j},f\rangle_{L^{2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\beta}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu_{d}(k)}a_{k,j}^{2}\geq 0,

since all eigenvalues are non-negative.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Proof.

For the first statement, we use
[
6
]
, Theorem 7.2, and verify the two conditions therein for the case of the approximate Bahadur slope, compare with
[
5
]
, Section 6. First of all, due to the strong law of large numbers we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU_{j})^{\,\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)\overset{\mathcal{\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}}\textrm{-f.s.}}{\rightarrow}\gamma_{\kappa}(b),\quad b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}.

Hence we immediately obtain

\frac{\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}}{\sqrt{n}}\overset{\mathcal{\mathbb{P}_{\kappa}}}{\rightarrow}\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b),\quad\kappa>0.

Under
H_{0}
, Corollary
2.6
yields

\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}=\sqrt{T_{n,\,\beta}}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{\longrightarrow}\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}|\,Z_{\beta}(b)\,|.

With
F(t)=\mathbb{P}(\,\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}|\,Z_{\beta}(b)\,|<t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, an application of
[
28
]
, Corollary 3.2, gives

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log(1-F(t))}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left(\,\max_{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}|\,Z_{\beta}(b)\,|\geq t\right)}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}\left(\,\|\,Z_{\beta}(\cdot)\,\|_{\infty}\geq t\right)}{t^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\rho_{\beta}(b,b)}.

Thus, the approximate Bahadur slope is

c_{\widetilde{T}_{\beta}}^{\;a}(\kappa)=\frac{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\gamma_{\kappa}^{2}(b)}{\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\rho_{\beta}(b,b)},\quad\kappa>0.

For the second statement, we utilize again Theorem 7.2 in
[
6
]
and prove the second condition for sufficiently small
\kappa>0
with the help of
[
37
]
, Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 2.2. Let us consider, once again, the
C(\mathcal{S}^{d-1},\mathbb{R})
-valued random elements
W_{j}(b)=({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptU_{j})^{\,\beta}-\psi_{d}(\beta)
,
b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, which are centered under
H_{0}
and due to their compact support fulfill the integrability condition of
[
37
]
, Lemma 2.1. Hence, with
F_{n}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}<t\right)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(1-F_{n}(\sqrt{n}\epsilon))}{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\widetilde{T}_{n,\,\beta}\geq\sqrt{n}\epsilon\right)}{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\,\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{j}(\cdot)\,\right\|_{\infty}\geq\epsilon\right)}{n}=-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\sup}\mathbb{E}W_{1}^{2}(b)}+o(\epsilon^{2}).

According to the proof of Theorem
2.1
,
W_{1}(\cdot)
defines the covariance kernel
\rho_{\beta}
of the Gaussian process
Z_{\beta}(\cdot)
, so that indeed

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(1-F_{n}(\sqrt{n}\epsilon))}{n}=-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2\underset{b\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}{\max}\rho_{\beta}(b,b)}+o(\epsilon^{2})

for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Due to the assumed condition (
5.1
), it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\gamma_{\kappa}(b)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptx)^{\,\beta}f(x\,|\,\kappa)\,d\sigma(x)-\psi_{d}(\beta)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptx)^{\,\beta}\left(f(x\,|\,\kappa)-f(x\,|\,0)\right)\,d\sigma(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\eta_{\beta}(b,b)-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}\frac{(c_{j,\,d}(\beta))^{2}}{\nu_{d}(j)}P_{j}^{\,d}({b}^{\top}\vskip-1.42271ptb)-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)=\sum_{j=0}^{\beta}\frac{(c_{j,\,d}(\beta))^{2}}{\nu_{d}(j)}-\psi_{d}^{2}(\beta)
