The Lattice Problem for Models of 𝖯𝖠 \mathsf{PA}
Athar Abdul-Quader and Roman Kossak

Abstract
. The lattice problem for models of Peano Arithmetic ( 𝖯𝖠 \mathsf{PA} ) is to determine which lattices can be represented as lattices of elementary submodels of a model of 𝖯𝖠 \mathsf{PA} , or, in greater generality, for a given model ℳ \mathcal{M} , which lattices can be represented as interstructure lattices of elementary submodels 𝒦 \mathcal{K} of an elementary extension 𝒩 \mathcal{N} such that ℳ ≼ 𝒦 ≼ 𝒩 \mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{K}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{N} . The problem has been studied for the last 60 years and the results and their proofs show an interesting interplay between the model theory of PA, Ramsey style combinatorics, lattice representation theory, and elementary number theory. We present a survey of the most important results together with a detailed analysis of some special cases to explain and motivate a technique developed by James Schmerl for constructing elementary extensions with prescribed interstructure lattices. The last section is devoted to a discussion of lesser-known results about lattices of elementary submodels of countable recursively saturated models of PA.



1.
Introduction

This paper concerns the study of substructure and interstructure lattices of models of
\mathsf{PA}
(Peano Arithmetic). All models will be models of
\mathsf{PA}
. We use
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}
,
\mathcal{K}
…for models, and
M
,
N
,
K
, …for their domains. We write
\mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{N}
if
\mathcal{M}
is an elementary submodel of
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{N}
if
\mathcal{M}
is a proper elementary submodel of
\mathcal{N}
(i.e., if
\mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}\not=\mathcal{N}
).

For a subset
X
of the domain of a model
\mathcal{N}
,
\Scl^{\mathcal{N}}(X)
denotes the Skolem closure of
X
in
\mathcal{N}
. Thanks to the provability of the least number principle in
\mathsf{PA}
, the
Skolem closure
of
X
in
\mathcal{N}
is the same as its definable closure, i.e., the closure of
X
under all parameter-free definable functions.
For
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{N}
and
a\in N\setminus M
, we will denote
\Scl^{\mathcal{N}}(M\cup\{a\})
by
\mathcal{M}(a)
and, for emphasis, we will denote
\Scl^{\mathcal{N}}(0)
by
\mathcal{N}_{\rm min}
.
\mathcal{N}_{\rm min}
is the prime model of the complete theory of
\mathcal{N}
, which we denote by
\Th(\mathcal{N})
.

We will discuss two types of lattices, namely substructure lattices and interstructure lattices.
Given
\mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{N}
, the

interstructure lattice
, denoted
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
, consists of all models
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{K}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{N}
ordered by inclusion. Clearly
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are the minimum and the maximum elements of
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
, respectively.

\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is a complete lattice. The meet of any set of elementary submodels of
\mathcal{N}
is the intersection of all the models in the set, and the join is the Skolem closure of their union.

The
substructure lattice
of
\mathcal{N}
, denoted
\Lt(\mathcal{N})
, is
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{N}_{\rm min})
, i.e., it is the lattice of all elementary submodels of
\mathcal{N}
.

The systematic study of substructure and interstructure lattices of models of
\mathsf{PA}
begins with Haim Gaifman’s seminal paper
[
6
]
. Gaifman introduced a powerful technique of end-extensional and minimal types, which he applied to construct models with some specific substructure and interstructure lattices. In particular, he proved that for every set
I
, every model
\mathcal{M}
has an elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to the Boolean algebra of all subsets of
I
.
Soon after
[
6
]
appeared, Schmerl
[
20
]
confirmed Gaifman’s conjecture that for every finite distributive lattice
D
, every model
\mathcal{M}

has an elementary end extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to
D
. Recall that a lattice
D
is
distributive
if the operations of join and meet in
D
distribute over each other, i.e.,
x\land(y\lor z)=(x\land y)\lor(x\land z)
and
x\lor(y\land z)=(x\lor y)\land(x\lor z)
. By well-known representation theorems of Stone and Priestley, a lattice
D
is distributive if for some set
X
,
D
is isomorphic to a sublattice of the Boolean algebra of all subsets of
X
.

George Mills
[
17
]
extended Gaifman’s technique to types with arbitrary sets of variables and completely characterized all distributive lattices that can be represented as
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
. Almost at the same time, Paris
[
19
]
and Alex Wilkie
[
32
]
applied different techniques to give examples of finite nondistributive substructure and interstructure lattices. We will give precise statements of all these results in Section
4
.

The work on substructure and interstructure lattices of models of
\mathsf{PA}
has turned out to be intimately connected with problems in lattice theory, Ramsey style combinatorics, and even some elementary number theory. Chapter 4 of
[
13
]
gives a comprehensive account of the main results obtained prior to 2005. Our goal is to give a brief survey of the area, to review the main lattice representation technique introduced by James Schmerl in
[
21
]
, and to report on some more recent results. The paper ends with a list of open problems. For now, let us just mention the most outstanding one.

Problem 1
.

Can every finite lattice be represented as
\Lt(\mathcal{N})
for some model
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathsf{PA}
?

The paper is organized as follows. Preliminaries in Section 2, along with introduction of the terminology and notation, contains a more detailed discussion of two particular examples that are included to illustrate basic concepts and to motivate more general results that follow.

The study of substructure and interstructure lattices of models of PA splits into two distinct areas: the distributive and nondistributive cases. This is reflected by splitting the brief overview of some major results into two sections 3 and 4 in which each case is discussed separately.

The next two sections are devoted to an introduction of Schmerl’s method of CPP-representations. In Section 5 we introduce basic definitions and illustrate them with a simple example. The purpose is not only to show how the method works, but also why it might be necessary. Section 6 proceeds in a similar fashion; a variant of the CPP-representations method is applied to lattices enriched by a rank function.

Section 7 is about substructure and interstructure lattices that are enriched further by adding the isomorphism relation between substructures. The idea leads to the concept of diverse models and diverse extensions that are illustrated by examples.

Section 8 is motivated by the interesting fact that the pentagon lattice can be realized by interstructure lattices of end extensions of countable models, but none of those extensions is conservative. This leads to the problem: what are possible sets of subsets of a model that are coded in elementary end extensions with prescribed interstructure lattices?

Section 9 is devoted to lattices of elementary submodels of countable recursively saturated models of
\mathsf{PA}
, and to one particular theorem that shows that for a large class of such models the isomorphism type of each model in the class is determined by its complete theory and its lattice of elementary substructures.

We end in Section 10 with some open questions.

2.
Preliminaries

This section presents all basic notions and results that will be needed for the discussion that follows.
Some concepts are illustrated by simple examples and some preliminary results. A full introduction to the model theory of
\mathsf{PA}
is Richard Kaye’s
[
9
]
and all details directly related to the lattice problem can be found in
[
13
, Chapter 4]
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the language of
\mathsf{PA}
.
Given an expansion
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathsf{PA}^{*}
is the theory consisting of the axioms of
\mathsf{PA}
along with the induction schema for all formulas in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. That is,
\mathsf{PA}^{*}
is not a single theory, but many, one for each such expansion. With one notable exception
[
30
, Theorems 3 and 4]
, all results about models of
\mathsf{PA}
in this article also hold for
\mathsf{PA}^{*}
in any countable language. This also applies to the last section in which we discuss recursively saturated models, but there we need to add the assumption that the language is computable.

A model
\mathcal{K}
in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is
finitely generated
if
\mathcal{K}=\Scl^{\mathcal{N}}(M\cup A)
, for some finite
A\subseteq N
.
Each finite tuple in a model of
\mathsf{PA}
is coded by a single element; hence, if
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{N}
, for every finitely generated over
\mathcal{M}
model
\mathcal{K}
in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
there is an
a\in N
such that
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{M}(a)
.

Let
L
be a lattice. An element
x\in L
is called
compact
if whenever
x\leq\bigvee X
for
X\subseteq L
, then
x\leq\bigvee X^{\prime}
for some finite
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
. It is easy to verify that the compact elements of
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
are the finitely generated over
\mathcal{M}
elementary submodels.

The set of compact elements of
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
forms a join-semilattice, which we denote as
\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
and
\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N})
will denote
\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{N}_{\rm{min}})
. For
a,b\in N
,
\mathcal{M}(a)\vee\mathcal{M}(b)=\mathcal{M}(\langle a,b\rangle)
, where
\langle x,y\rangle
is Cantor’s pairing function. It is not obvious that the intersection of two finitely generated models may not be finitely generated. Various examples can be shown, one is given shortly below.

A complete lattice
L
is
algebraic
if each element of
L
is the supremum of a set of compact elements. A lattice is
\kappa
-
algebraic
if it is algebraic and each compact element has less than
\kappa
many compact elements below it.
The domain of any
\mathcal{K}\in\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is the union of the domains of all models
\mathcal{M}(a)
, for
a\in K
; thus
\mathcal{K}
is the supremum of the set of compact elements below it in the lattice.

It follows from the remarks above that for any
\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{PA}
,
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
is
\aleph_{1}
-algebraic, and for any
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, if
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{N}
then
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is
|M|^{+}
-algebraic. Moreover, if
\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})\cong\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N}_{1}/\mathcal{M}_{1})
, then
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})\cong\Lt(\mathcal{N}_{1}/\mathcal{M}_{1})
. Thus, in order to realize a lattice as an interstructure lattice, we need only to ensure that we have control over the compact elements. Of course, if
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is finite, all elements of it are compact.

Extensions

If

, then we say that

\mathcal{M}
-

-formula. If

-definable, we just say that it is definable.

If

Let us note that

may be uncountable even if

is finitely generated, i.e., it is finitely generated over

. To see this, let

be a countable model with an uncountable substructure lattice (as an example, take

to be a countable, recursively saturated model). By

, every countable model

has a

such that
\mathcal{N}=\scl(b)
for every

.

For any completion
T
of
\mathsf{PA}
, starting with a prime model of

, we can build an

-chain of superminimal elementary end extensions. The union of this chain
\mathcal{N}
has no proper elementary uncountable submodels and
\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N})
is isomorphic to
(\omega_{1},\leq)
. The existence of such models was proved independently by Julia Knight
[
10
]
and Jeff Paris
[
18
]
.

Example 1
.

We can use the technique of superminimal extension to provide an example showing that the intersection of two finitely generated submodels might not be finitely generated.

As mentioned above, every countable model has a superminimal elementary end extension. Moreover, if
\mathcal{M}
is countable, nonstandard, and is generated by a bounded set of generators, then it has a superminimal cofinal extension (
[
13
, Exercise 2.5.2]
). Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is countable, nonstandard, and has a bounded, but not finite, set of generators. Such a model can be obtained as the union of an
\omega
-chain of elementary cofinal extensions of a nonstandard finitely generated model
\mathcal{M}
, such that, for some fixed
a
in
M
, each next model in the chain is a cofinal extension of the previous one generated over the previous model by a new element added below

. Let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
be a superminimal elementary end extension of
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{N}_{2}
a superminimal cofinal extension of
\mathcal{M}
, and let
\mathcal{N}=\Scl(a,b)
, where

and

(by superminimality, any such

will work). Then, in

and

are compact (finitely generated), but their intersection is

, not finitely generated.

2.2.
Ranked lattices

The following result is known as Gaifman’s Splitting Theorem.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{N}
, then there is a unique
{\mathcal{K}}
such that
\mathcal{M}\preccurlyeq_{\sf cof}{\mathcal{K}}\preccurlyeq_{\sf end}\mathcal{N}
. The domain of
{\mathcal{K}}
is
\{x\in N:\exists y\in M\ \mathcal{N}\models(x\leq y)\}
.

Thanks to Gaifman’s Splitting Theorem, for each
\mathcal{K}
in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
, we can define the
rank
of
\mathcal{K}
,
\rho(\mathcal{K})
, as the unique
\overline{\mathcal{K}}
such that
\mathcal{K}\preccurlyeq_{\sf cof}\overline{\mathcal{K}}\preccurlyeq_{\sf end}\mathcal{N}
. The set of ranks of all models in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
, called the
rankset
, is linearly ordered by inclusion. It is easy to see that for
\mathcal{K}_{0}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}
in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
, if
\mathcal{K}_{0}\prec\mathcal{K}_{1}
, then

•

\mathcal{K}_{0}\preccurlyeq_{\sf cof}\mathcal{K}_{1}
if and only if
\rho(\mathcal{K}_{0})=\rho(\mathcal{K}_{1})
, and

•

\mathcal{K}_{0}\preccurlyeq_{\sf end}\mathcal{K}_{1}
if and only if
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\rho(K_{0})\cap\mathcal{K}_{1}
.

Ranked lattices were introduced by Schmerl in
[
21
]
. Here is a modified definition from
[
13
]
: A
ranked lattice

(L,\rho)
is a lattice
L
equipped with a function
\rho:L\longrightarrow L
such that for all
x
and
y
in
L
we have

(1)

x\leq\rho(x)
;

(2)

\rho(\rho(x))=\rho(x)
;

(3)

\rho(x)\leq\rho(y)\textup{ or }\rho(y)\leq\rho(x)
;

(4)

\rho(x\lor y)=\rho(x)\lor\rho(y)
.

The
rankset
of a ranked lattice
(L,\rho)
is
\{\rho(x):x\in L\}
.

\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
equipped with the rank function defined above is a ranked lattice, denoted by
\Ltr(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
.

A lattice can have many expansions to a ranked lattice. To be represented as rank functions in interstructure lattices of models of arithmetic those expansion have to satisfy certain additional conditions.

Andreas Blass
[
1
]
showed that the intersection of two finitely generated cofinal submodels of a model
\mathcal{M}
must be cofinal in
\mathcal{M}
.

Thus, if

has a minimal cofinal extension.

The example of minimal extensions shows that a lattice—in this example the two element lattice
L=\{{\mathbf{0}_{L}},{\mathbf{1}_{L}}\}
—can be realized as
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
in two much different ways. For a countable nonstandard
\mathcal{M}

can be either an elementary end extension or elementary cofinal extension (by Gaifman’s splitting theorem, it cannot be a mixed extension).

3.
Distributive lattices

Two of the main results about the lattice problem from Gaifman’s
[
6
]
are:

(1)

For any set
I
, every model
\mathcal{M}
has a (conservative) elementary end extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to the Boolean algebra of all subsets of

[
6

(2)

For every countable linearly ordered set

, every model
\mathcal{M}
has a (conservative) elementary end extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt_{0}(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to

[
6

The proof of (1) uses an iteration of elementary end extensions generated by a minimal type, as we showed in Example

above. The result in (2) is a corollary of a theorem about the existence of a particular end-extensional type that is used to get an extension of
\mathcal{N}
in one step. The proof of that theorem is difficult and it takes 15 pages. Gaifman stresses that in both results one gets a uniform operator that produces the required extension
\mathcal{N}
in a uniform way. Notice that in both cases the interstructure lattice is distributive.

Soon after
[
6
]
appeared, Mills managed to generalize Gaifman’s results by extending the notions of end-extensional and minimal types to types with infinitely many variables. His main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 6

(
[
17
]
)
.

Let

be a distributive lattice. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

There exists a model
\mathcal{M}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to
D

(2)

Every model
\mathcal{M}
has an elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to
D

(3)

\aleph_{1}
-algebraic.

The equivalence of (1) and (3) was proved independently by Paris
[
18
]
. The proof of Mills’ theorem is difficult and it involves many technical details.

In
[
17
]
, Mills follows the statement of Theorem
6
with an instructive example. Let
L
be the ordered unit interval

.
L
is a complete distributive lattice, but it is not
\aleph_{1}

be the closely related lattice of initial segments in the set of rational numbers in [0,1] ordered by inclusion. In

is isomorphic to a substructure lattice.

All results about end-extensional types and their applications to the lattice problem in
[

hold for
\mathsf{PA}^{*}
in a countable language. Gaifman asked if they hold for uncountable languages as well. In response, Mills gave a construction of a model of

In terminology of Mills

Gaifman conjectured that for every completion

Let

4.2.
Infinite nondistributive lattices

In
[

Theorem 9

(

)

Let

\cong L

The theorems are formulated in lattice theoretic terms that take a good few pages to introduce.
We will only quote the following from

[

The proofs heavily depend on major results in lattice representation theory that we will introduce in the next section.

5.
Representations

Over the last 40 years, in several papers, Schmerl developed a special technique of constructing elementary extensions with prescribed interstructure lattices. It is based on particular representations of lattices as
lattices of equivalence relations
. In this section, we give basic definitions and motivate them with examples.

Definition 10
.

Let
A
be any set and
L
a finite lattice.

(1)

The set
\Eq(A)
is the set of all equivalence relations on
A
. This set forms a lattice under inclusion, with
\mathbf{0}_{A}
being the discrete relation
\{(a,a):a\in A\}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{A}
the trivial relation
A\times A
.

(2)

Let
L
be a finite lattice and
A
a set. Then
\alpha:L\longrightarrow\Eq(A)
is a
pseudo-representation
of
L
if:

•

\alpha(0_{L})=\mathbf{1}_{A}
, (
\alpha(0_{L})
is trivial)

•

\alpha(1_{L})=\mathbf{0}_{A}
, (
\alpha(1_{L})
is discrete) and

•

\alpha(x\vee y)=\alpha(x)\wedge\alpha(y)
.

(3)

\alpha
is a
representation
if it is a pseudo-representation and is one-to-one.

Oftentimes, realizing a particular finite lattice as an interstructure lattice requires choosing an appropriate representation of the lattice and proving some combinatorial lemmas about this representation. Before describing important properties of representations that are involved in these kinds of constructions, let us examine a motivating example.

Example 11
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{PA}
and
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})\cong\mathbf{B}_{2}
. Then there are
a,b\in N
such that
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}(\langle a,b\rangle)
and
\mathcal{M}(a)\not=\mathcal{M}(b)
(see Figure 1). Let
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
be the projection functions, so that

\mathsf{PA}\vdash\forall x\forall y(\pi_{1}(\langle x,y\rangle)=x\wedge\pi_{2}(\langle x,y\rangle)=y).

Then for any
c\in N
, there is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable
f:N\longrightarrow N
such that
\mathcal{N}\models f(\langle a,b\rangle)=c
. Let us fix such
c
and
f
and define a subset of
M
as follows. There are four possibilities:

•

c\in M
. In this case, let
X=\{\langle x,y\rangle:\mathcal{M}\models f(\langle x,y\rangle)=c\}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{M}\models\forall n,m\in X(f(n)=f(m))
.

•

c\in\mathcal{M}(a)\setminus\mathcal{M}
. In this case, there are
\mathcal{M}
-definable
g_{1},g_{2}
such that

\mathcal{N}\models g_{1}(a)=c\wedge g_{2}(c)=a.

Let
X=\{\langle x,y\rangle:\mathcal{M}\models f(\langle x,y\rangle)=g_{1}(x)\wedge g_{2}(f(\langle x,y\rangle))=x\}
. Notice that
\mathcal{M}\models\forall n,m\in X(f(n)=f(m)\Longleftrightarrow\pi_{1}(n)=\pi_{1}(m))
.

•

c\in\mathcal{M}(b)\setminus\mathcal{M}
. Similarly, there are
\mathcal{M}
-definable
g_{1},g_{2}
such that

\mathcal{N}\models g_{1}(b)=c\wedge g_{2}(c)=b.

Let
X=\{\langle x,y\rangle:\mathcal{M}\models f(\langle x,y\rangle)=g_{1}(y)\wedge g_{2}(f(\langle x,y\rangle))=y\}
. Again, one observes that
\mathcal{M}\models\forall n,m\in X(f(n)=f(m)\Longleftrightarrow\pi_{2}(n)=\pi_{2}(m))
.

•

c\in\mathcal{N}\setminus(\mathcal{M}(a)\cup\mathcal{M}(b))
. In this case, there is
\mathcal{M}
-definable
g
such that

\mathcal{N}\models g(c)=\langle a,b\rangle.

Let
X=\{\langle x,y\rangle:\mathcal{M}\models g(f(\langle x,y\rangle))=\langle x,y\rangle\}
. Here we observe that
\mathcal{M}\models\forall n,m\in X(f(n)=f(m)\Longleftrightarrow n=m)
; that is,

is one to one on
X
.

In each of these cases,
X
is an infinite,
\mathcal{M}
-definable, set and
\mathcal{N}\models\langle a,b\rangle\in X
. Let us refer to an infinite,
\mathcal{M}
-definable
X
such that
\langle a,b\rangle\in X^{\mathcal{N}}
as
large
.

Given any set
X
, we define the pseudo-representation
\alpha_{X}:\mathbf{B}_{2}\longrightarrow\Eq(X)
:

•

\alpha_{X}(0)
is trivial,

•

(n,m)\in\alpha_{X}(a)
iff
\pi_{1}(n)=\pi_{1}(m)
,

•

(n,m)\in\alpha_{X}(b)
iff
\pi_{2}(n)=\pi_{2}(m)
, and

•

\alpha_{X}(1)
is discrete.

Notice that whenever
f
is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable function, we can find
r\in\mathbf{B}_{2}
and a large set
X
such that

\mathcal{M}\models\forall n,m\in X(f(n)=f(m)\Longleftrightarrow(n,m)\in\alpha_{X}(r)).

We summarize the above as follows. Suppose
p(x)=\tp(\langle a,b\rangle/\mathcal{M})
and let
f
be an
\mathcal{M}
-definable function. Then there is
\phi(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})
defining a “large” set

and
r\in\mathbf{B}_{2}
such that
\phi(x)\in p(x)
and

\mathcal{M}\models\forall x,y[(\phi(x)\wedge\phi(y))\Longrightarrow(f(x)=f(y)\Longleftrightarrow(x,y)\in\alpha_{X}(r))].

Examples like this one provide the motivation for the following definitions. They were first introduced by Schmerl in
[

]
, and they have been refined over the years.

Definition 12
.

Let
L
be a finite lattice,
X
a set, and
\alpha:L\longrightarrow\Eq(X)
a representation.

(1)

Let
Y\subseteq X
. Then
\alpha|Y:L\longrightarrow\Eq(Y)
is the pseudo-representation given by
(\alpha|Y)(r)=\alpha(r)\cap Y^{2}
for each
r\in L
.

(2)

Let
\beta:L\longrightarrow\Eq(Y)
be a pseudo-representation. Then
\alpha\cong\beta
(
\alpha
is
isomorphic
to
\beta
) if there is a bijection
f:X\longrightarrow Y
such that for each
r\in L
,
(x,y)\in\alpha(r)
if and only if
(f(x),f(y))\in\beta(r)
.

(3)

Let
\Theta\in\Eq(X)
.
\Theta
is
canonical
for
\alpha
if there is
r\in L
such that for all
x,y\in X
,
(x,y)\in\Theta
if and only if
(x,y)\in\alpha(r)
.

(4)

\alpha
has the
0
-canonical partition property
, or is
0
-CPP
, if for each
r\in L
,
\alpha(r)
does not have exactly two classes.

(5)

\alpha
is
(n+1)
-CPP
if, for each
\Theta\in\Eq(X)
there is
Y\subseteq X
such that
\alpha|Y
is an
n
-CPP representation and
\Theta\cap Y^{2}
is canonical for
\alpha|Y
.

Using these definitions, let us examine Example
11
once more from the other direction. Let
\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{PA}
and
X=[M]^{2}=\{\langle x,y\rangle:x<y\}
. Then the representation
\alpha:\mathbf{B}_{2}\longrightarrow\Eq(X)
given in Example
11
is
n
-CPP for each
n\in\omega
. To see this, first recall the Canonical Ramsey Theorem for pairs (
\mathsf{CRT}^{2}
): for every
f:[\omega]^{2}\longrightarrow\omega
, there is an infinite
X\subseteq\omega
such that
f
is canonical on
[X]^{2}
. That is, one of the following holds:

•

f
is one to one on
[X]^{2}
,

•

f
is constant on
[X]^{2}
,

•

for all
\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle,\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle\in[X]^{2}
,
f(x_{1},y_{1})=f(x_{2},y_{2})
if and only if
x_{1}=x_{2}
, or

•

for all
\langle x_{1},y_{1}\rangle,\langle x_{2},y_{2}\rangle\in[X]^{2}

.

This result is due to Erdős and Rado

6.1.
Congruence Lattices

An

(A,\langle f_{i}:i\in I\rangle)

is a set,

is an index set, and for each

If

is an equivalence relation

In the definition below, for a lattice

7.
Diversity

The early results of Gaifman and Mills mentioned in Section 3 relied heavily on applications of minimal and end-extensional types. For a given
\mathcal{M}
, if
\mathcal{N}
is generated over
\mathcal{M}
by a set
A
of elements realizing the same minimal
{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{M})
-type, then
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is isomorphic to the Boolean algebra of subsets of
A
and the isomorphism type over
\mathcal{M}
of each model in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
is determined by the cardinality of its set of generators. In particular, if
A
is finite of cardinality
n
, then there are exactly
n+1
isomorphism types of models in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
. If
\mathcal{N}
is generated over
\mathcal{M}
by a finite set of elements realizing mutually independent minimal types, then no pair of distinct models in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
are isomorphic.

In
[
23
]
Schmerl asked if there is more that can be said about the diversity of isomorphism types in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
. He called a model
diverse
if no two of its elementary submodels are isomorphic, and called an extension
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{M}

diverse
if no two models in
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
are isomorphic over
\mathcal{M}
.

In
[
23
]
, Schmerl observes that
\mathcal{M}
is diverse if and only if no two distinct elements have the same type. For such structures (for any language) Ali Enayat coined the name
Leibnizian
. His article
[
4
]
is devoted to Leibnizian models of set theory.

All results in the rest of this section are from
[
23
]
. Schmerl notes that the first theorem suggests that constructing models that are not diverse is more difficult than constructing diverse ones.

Theorem 19
.

(1)

If
\mathcal{M}
is not an elementary extension of the standard model and

is finite, then there is a diverse
\mathcal{N}
such that

and
\Lt(\mathcal{M})\cong\Lt(\mathcal{N})
.

(2)

is nonstandard and has an elementary extension

such that

\cong L
, then

has a cofinal diverse extension
\mathcal{N}
such that
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{M})
\cong L
.

Because a model is diverse just in case it is a diverse extension of its prime elementary submodel, (1) above is an easy consequence of the second. The proof of (2) involves the full power of the CPP-representations theory, invoking at one point a canonical partition theorem of Prömel and Voigt (see
[

for the statement of the theorem).

9.
Countable recursively saturated models

Up to this point, this survey was about the the problem of finding, for a given lattice
L
, a substructure or interstructure lattice representation of
L
with or without some additional properties. In this section we will briefly discuss a dual problem for a particular class of models. Given a countable recursively saturated model, what can we say about its substructure lattice?

A model
\mathcal{M}
is recursively saturated if it is saturated with the respect to computable types with finite numbers of parameters. The
standard system
of a model
\mathcal{M}
,
\SSy(\mathcal{M})
, is the set of standard parts of the definable subsets of
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.,
\SSy(\mathcal{M})=\Cod(\mathcal{M}/\mathbb{N})
, where
\mathbb{N}
is the standard model. It is not difficult to prove that any two countable recursively saturated models are isomorphic if and only if they are elementarily equivalent and they have the same standard system.

For the rest of this section, let
\mathcal{M}
be countable and recursively saturated.

The lattice
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
is immense. If the domain of a model
\mathcal{K}
in
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
is an initial segment of
M
we call
\mathcal{K}
an
elementary cut
. Henryk Kotlarski proved that the set of elementary cuts of
\mathcal{M}
ordered by inclusion is isomorphic to
2^{\omega}
with the lexicographic ordering. Moreover, he proved that the set of elementary cuts that are recursively saturated is uncountable, dense, and is closed under infinite unions but not under infinite intersections
[
15
]
. All recursively saturated elementary cuts have the same standard system; hence, they are isomorphic to one another. Yet, there are continuum many first-order theories of pairs
(\mathcal{M},K)
, where
\mathcal{K}
is a recursively saturated elementary cut
[
31
]
.

An elementary cut of
\mathcal{M}
is not recursively saturated if and only if it is a closure under initial segment of the Skolem closure of single element of
M
. Every recursively saturated model realizes countably many mutually independent minimal types. It follows that there are countably many isomorphism types of elementary cuts that are not recursively saturated. Moreover, each cut that is not recursively saturated, except for the closure under initial segment of
\scl(0)
, has countably many automorphic images; hence the isomorphism relation of
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
restricted to such cuts has countably many countable equivalence classes.

Elementary cuts are linearly ordered by inclusion; hence they form a distributive sublattice of
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
. To show that
\Lt(\mathcal{M})
is not distributive we will use some facts about cofinal extensions.

In
[
24
]
, Schmerl calls the extension
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{N}

almost minimal
if
\mathcal{N}
is an end extension and for every
\mathcal{K}
, if
\mathcal{M}\prec\mathcal{K}\prec\mathcal{N}
, then
\mathcal{K}\cof\mathcal{N}
. For a lattice
L
,
{\mathbf{2}}\oplus L
is the extension of
L
obtained by adding with a new 0 element appended below
{\mathbf{0}}_{L}
. By the Blass condition, if
\mathcal{N}
is an almost minimal extension of
\mathcal{K}
and

is finite, then
\Lt(\mathcal{N}/\mathcal{L})\cong{\mathbf{2}}\oplus L
for some finite lattice

. The following is an abridged version of

Let
