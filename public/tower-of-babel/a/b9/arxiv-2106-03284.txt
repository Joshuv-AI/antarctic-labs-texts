Exactly solvable discrete time Birth and Death processes


Abstract
We present 15 explicit examples of discrete time Birth and Death processes which are exactly solvable . They are related to the hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme having discrete orthogonality measures. Namely, they are the Krawtchouk, three different kinds of q q -Krawtchouk, (dual, q q )-Hahn, ( q q )-Racah, Al-Salam-Carlitz II , q q -Meixner, q q -Charlier, dual big q q -Jacobi and dual big q q -Laguerre polynomials. The birth and death rates are determined by the difference equations governing the polynomials. The stationary distributions are the normalised orthogonality measures of the polynomials. The transition probabilities are neatly expressed by the normalised polynomials and the corresponding eigenvalues. This paper is simply the discrete time versions of the known solutions of the continuous time birth and death processes.

2
Markov chain

The subject of the present paper belongs to the simplest category of stationary Markov chain
on a one-dimensional integer lattice
\mathcal{X}
, either finite or semi-infinite:

\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,\ldots,N\}:\quad\text{finite},\qquad\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:\quad\text{semi-infinite}.

For analytic treatment we use
x,y,..
as representing the lattice points in

\mathcal{X}
,
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.
The general problem setting is as follows.
Suppose a non-negative matrix
L
of transition probability is given. Its element

L_{x\,y}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}L_{x\,y}=1,

(2.1)

is the transition probability
from
y
to
x
.
When the system has the probability distribution

\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)=1,

at
\ell
-th step, the next step distribution is given by

\mathcal{P}(x;\ell+1)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}L_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}(y;\ell).

(2.2)

The above condition (
2.1
) ensures the conservation of probability,

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(x;\ell+1)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}L_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}(y;\ell)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(y;\ell)=1.

Another immediate consequence of (
2.1
) and Perron-Frobenius theorem
applied to
L
is that its spectrum is bounded by
1
and
-1
:

-1\leq\text{Eigenvalues}(L)\leq 1.

(2.3)

This can be easily seen by considering the normalised eigenvector

of
L
corresponding
to the maximal eigenvalue
\kappa_{M}
,

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}L_{x\,y}v_{M}(y)=\kappa_{M}v_{M}(x),\quad\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}|v_{M}(y)|=1.

Since we can always choose
v_{M}(y)\geq 0
by Perron-Frobenius theorem,
v_{M}(y)
is a probability distribution,

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}v_{M}(y)=1
.
This means that
\kappa_{M}=1
since
\kappa_{M}v_{M}(x)
is also a probability distribution, as shown above.
The lower bound
-1
in (
2.3
) is derived by applying Perron-Frobenius theorem to
L^{2}
.
Other eigenvectors of
L
, having sign changes, can never constitute a probability distribution on their own.

\mathcal{P}(x;0)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(x;0)=1,

\bullet

\displaystyle 0<\bar{B}(x)<1,\quad 0<\bar{D}(x)<1,\quad 0<\bar{B}(x)+\bar{D}(x)<1,

\bar{D}(0)=0,\quad\bar{B}(N)=0:\ (\text{only for finite cases}).

(2.9)

The matrix

\displaystyle L=

\displaystyle{\small\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1-\bar{B}(0)&\bar{D}(1)&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\

\!\!0&\bar{B}(1)&1-\bar{B}(2)-\bar{D}(2)&\bar{D}(3)&\cdots&\vdots\\

Discrete time Birth and Death process

Probably it is now clear that the above generalised random walk (
2.7
)–(
2.12
)
can also be called
discrete time
Birth and Death (BD) process.
They are very closely related to the
continuous time
Birth and Death process.
For comparison, let us review it here.
Let
\mathcal{P}(x;t)
be the probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}
at time
t
.
Let us denote the birth rate at population
x
by
B(x)>0
and the death rate by
D(x)>0

The time evolution of the probability distribution is governed by the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{P}(x;t)

\displaystyle=(L_{BD}\mathcal{P})(x;t)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}{L_{BD}}_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}(y;t),\quad\mathcal{P}(x;t)\geq 0,\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=1,

\displaystyle=-(B(x)+D(x))\mathcal{P}(x;t)+B(x-1)\mathcal{P}(x-1;t)+D(x+1)\mathcal{P}(x+1;t),

(3.2)

with the boundary condition(s)

D(0)=0,\quad B(N)=0:\ (\text{only for a finite case}),

(3.3)

which is called reflecting boundary condition.
Here the matrix
L_{BD}
is also tri-diagonal

\displaystyle{L_{BD}}_{x+1\,x}=B(x),\ \ {L_{BD}}_{x-1\,x}=D(x),\ {L_{BD}}_{x\,x}=-B(x)-D(x),

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt{L_{BD}}_{x\,y}=0,\quad|x-y|\geq 2,

(3.4)

\displaystyle L_{BD}=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}-B(0)&D(1)&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
B(0)&-B(1)-D(1)&D(2)&0&\cdots&\vdots\\
\!\!0&B(1)&-B(2)-D(2)&D(3)&\cdots&\vdots\\
\!\!\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\!\!\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\!\!0&\cdots&\cdots&B(N\!\!-\!\!2)&-B(N\!\!-\!\!1)\!-\!D(N\!\!-\!\!1)&D(N)\\
\!\!0&\cdots&\cdots&0&B(N\!-\!1)&-D(N)\end{array}\right),

satisfying the condition

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{L_{BD}}_{x\,y}=0.

(3.12)

This ensures the conservation of probability, that is, the condition

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=1

3.1
).

(3.13)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow L=I_{d}+t_{S}\cdot L_{BD},\qquad I_{d}:\ \text{Identity matrix}.

(3.14)

If the continuous time BD is solved, the corresponding discrete time BD is solved, and vice versa,
as the eigenvalues are related as

\text{Eigenvalue of}\ L=1+t_{S}\cdot\left(\text{Eigenvalue of}\ (L_{BD})\right),

and the corresponding eigenvectors are common.

The bigger

D(N)&-B(N\!-\!1)-D(N\!-\!1)&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\\
-D(N)&B(N\!-\!1)&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right),

which is obtained from
L_{BD}
(
3.4
) by mirror reflection.
That is

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826ptL_{BD}^{M}=L_{BD}J,

(6.12)

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\text{anti-diagonal}\{1,1,\ldots,1\},\ \text{or}\ J_{x\,y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\delta_{x,\,N-y},\quad\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}J_{x\,y}\check{P}_{n}(y)=(-1)^{n}\check{P}_{n}(x).

(6.13)

From the general relation (
3.49
)

(L_{BD}\hat{\phi}_{0}\hat{\phi}_{n})(x)=-\mathcal{E}(n)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),\quad n\in\mathcal{X},

we obtain

(L_{BD}^{M}\hat{\phi}_{0}\hat{\phi}_{n})(x)=-(-1)^{n}\mathcal{E}(n)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),\quad n\in\mathcal{X}.

(6.14)

Since the spectrum of
L_{BD}^{M}
is not negative semi-definite,
L_{BD}^{M}
does not define
a stochastic process.
However, it is interesting to consider its discrete time version.

Let us introduce the mirror image of
L
(
2.7
),

\displaystyle L^{M}_{x+1\,N-x}=\bar{B}(x),\ \ L^{M}_{x-1\,N-x}=\bar{D}(x),\quad\ L^{M}_{x\,N-x}=1-\bar{B}(x)-\bar{D}(x),

\displaystyle\qquad L^{M}_{x\,y}=0\quad\text{for}\quad x+y<N-1,\quad x+y>N+1,

(6.15)

\displaystyle L^{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}LJ=

\displaystyle{\footnotesize\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}0&0&\cdots&\cdots&\bar{D}(1)&1-\bar{B}(0)\\
0&0&\cdots&\bar{D}(2)&1-\bar{B}(1)-\bar{D}(1)&\bar{B}(0)\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&1-\bar{B}(2)-\bar{D}(2)&\bar{B}(1)&0\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\bar{D}(N\!-\!1)&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\bar{D}(N)&1-\bar{B}(N\!-\!1)-\bar{D}(N\!-\!1)&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\\
1-\bar{D}(N)&\bar{B}(N\!-\!1)&\cdots&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right).}

This defines another exactly solvable stochastic process, as
L^{M}
is a non-negative tri-anti-diagonal
matrix with

\left(L^{M}\hat{\phi}_{0}\hat{\phi}_{n}\right)(x)=\kappa_{M}(n)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),\quad 1\geq\kappa_{M}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(-1)^{n}\left(1-t_{S}\cdot\mathcal{E}(n)\right)\geq-1,\quad n\in\mathcal{X}.

(6.16)

However, this is simply the mirror image of the original process.
Let us introduce the process governed by the sum of
L
and
L^{M}
,

L^{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}(L+L^{M})=\frac{1}{2}L(I_{d}+J),

(6.17)

which is exactly solvable having interesting properties,

\displaystyle\left(L^{S}\hat{\phi}_{0}\hat{\phi}_{n}\right)(x)=\kappa_{S}(n)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),

(6.18)

\displaystyle 1\geq\kappa_{S}(n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(1+(-1)^{n}\right)\bigl(1-t_{S}\cdot\mathcal{E}(n)\bigr)\geq-1,\quad n\in\mathcal{X}.

(6.19)

All odd eigenvalues vanish

\kappa_{S}(2n+1)=0,\quad n=0,1,\ldots,[\tfrac{N-1}{2}],

and the convergence to the stationary distribution is accelerated.
By expanding the initial distribution as (
3.51
), the distribution after
\ell
step is
described by the even eigenvectors only

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x;\ell)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\sum_{n=0}^{[\frac{N}{2}]}c_{2n}\bigl(\kappa_{S}(2n)\bigr)^{\ell}\hat{\phi}_{2n}(x),\quad\ell=1,2,\ldots.

(6.20)

Likewise, the transition matrix from
y
at
\ell=0
(
\mathcal{P}(x;0)=\delta_{x,y}
) to
x
after
\ell

\mathcal{P}(x,y;\ell)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\sum_{n=0}^{[\frac{N}{2}]}\bigl(\kappa_{S}(2n)\bigr)^{\ell}\hat{\phi}_{2n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{2n}(y)\hat{\phi}_{0}(y)^{-1}.

(6.21)

In other words, the asymmetric part of the initial distribution
\mathcal{P}^{AS}(x;0)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{AS}(x;0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{P}(x;0)-\mathcal{P}(N-x;0)\right)=\left(\frac{1}{2}(I_{d}-J)\mathcal{P}\right)(x;0),

is erased by one action of
L^{S}
(
6.17
)

\left(L^{S}\mathcal{P}^{AS}\right)(x;0)=\frac{1}{4}\left(L(I_{d}+J)(I_{d}-J)\right)\mathcal{P}(x;0)=0.

6.1
Dual systems

For finite systems, an apparently different looking exactly solvable system can be constructed from a
known exactly solvable one by the similarity transformation by the anti-diagonal matrix
J
(
6.13
),

L^{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}JLJ,\qquad L^{d}_{BD}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}JL_{BD}J.

That is,

\bar{B}^{d}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bar{D}(N-x),\quad\bar{D}^{d}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\bar{B}(N-x);\qquad B^{d}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{D}(N-x),\quad{D}^{d}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{B}(N-x),

and it is called a ‘dual system’
[
17
]
§7.
The original and its dual system have common eigenvalues and the eigenvectors are mapped by
J
.
For most of examples listed in §
4
dual polynomials are the same as the original one with
parameter cahnge (involution).

7
Comments

It should be stressed that the same solution procedures apply to BD processes related to
various new orthogonal polynomials
[
18
]
.
They are obtained from the classical orthogonal polynomials,
e.g.
the Racah and
q
-Racah, etc
by multiple applications of the discrete analogue of the Darboux transformations or
the Krein-Adler transformations. Since there are many different ways to deform
the classical orthogonal polynomials, these new polynomials offer virtually infinite examples of
exactly solvable BD processes.
