Rapidly Rotating Wall-Mode Convection
Geoffrey M. Vasil 1, Keaton J. Burns 2,3, Daniel Lecoanet 4,5, Jeffrey S. Oishi 6, Benjamin P. Brown 7 & Keith Julien 8

Abstract
In the rapidly rotating limit, we derive a balanced set of reduced equations governing the strongly nonlinear development of the convective wall-mode instability in the interior of a general container. The model illustrates that wall-mode convection is a multiscale phenomenon where the dynamics of the bulk interior diagnostically determine the small-scale dynamics within Stewartson boundary layers at the sidewalls. The sidewall boundary layers feedback on the interior via a nonlinear lateral heat-flux boundary condition, providing a closed system. Outside the asymptotically thin boundary layer, the convective modes connect to a dynamical interior that maintains scales set by the domain geometry. In many ways, the final system of equations resembles boundary-forced planetary geostrophic baroclinic dynamics coupled with barotropic quasi-geostrophic vorticity. The reduced system contains the results from previous linear instability theory but captured in an elementary fashion, providing a new avenue for investigating wall-mode convection in the strongly nonlinear regime. We also derive the dominant Ekman-flux correction to the onset Rayleigh number for large Taylor number, Ra ∼ 31.8 ​ Ta 1 / 2 − 4.43 ​ Ta 5 / 12 \textit{Ra}\sim 31.8\,\textit{Ta}^{1/2}-4.43\,\textit{Ta}^{5/12} for no-slip boundaries. However, we find that the linear onset in a finite cylinder differs noticeably compared to a Cartesian channel. We demonstrate some of the reduced model’s nonlinear dynamics with numerical simulations in a cylindrical container.

Introduction

After more than a century of vigorous investigation, Rayleigh-Bénard convection remains a quintessential distilled model for convective instability, pattern formation, and turbulence in buoyancy-driven flows
(
Chassignet et al. 2012
)
.
Likewise, rotationally influenced Rayleigh-Bénard convection serves a similar classic role in modelling many natural environments such as oceanic, stellar, and planetary systems
(
Ecke & Shishkina 2023
)
.

Four non-dimensional control parameters characterise Rayleigh-Bénard convection in a closed rotating vessel.
Historically, the Rayleigh number, Taylor number, Prandtl number, and aspect ratio provide the most common choice of dimensionless parameters,

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}\equiv\frac{g\alpha\,\Delta TH^{3}}{\nu\kappa},\quad\textit{Ta}\equiv\frac{4\Omega^{2}H^{4}}{\nu^{2}},\quad\sigma\equiv\frac{\nu}{\kappa},\quad\Gamma\equiv\frac{L}{H}.

(1)

These measure the strength of the characteristic buoyancy force, the strength of rotation, the fluid’s dissipative properties, and the geometry.
Here,
g
gives the gravitational acceleration,
\alpha=-\rho^{-1}(\partial\rho/\partial T)|_{T_{0},P_{0}}
is the thermal expansion coefficient,
\Delta T
is the vertical temperature contrast,
\nu
represents the kinematic viscosity,
\kappa
represents the thermal diffusivity,
\Omega
gives the background rotation rate,
H
represents the container depth, and
L
gives a characteristic lateral extent (or diameter) of the container.
The Ekman and convective Rossby numbers also provide useful equivalents to
Ta
and
Ra
.
That is,

\displaystyle\textit{E}\equiv\textit{Ta}^{-1/2}=\frac{\nu}{2\Omega H^{2}},\quad\textit{Ro}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\textit{Ra}}{\sigma\textit{Ta}}}=\frac{\sqrt{g\alpha\Delta T/H}}{2\Omega},

(2)

where
E
measures the size of viscous boundary layers, and
Ro
measures the relative influence of buoyancy and rotation.
In rapidly rotating systems, both
\textit{E}\ll 1
and
\textit{Ro}\ll 1
.

A significant partition in Rayleigh-Bénard convection modelling exists between
large-aspect-ratio
and
finite-aspect-ratio
systems.
In large-aspect-ratio systems, sidewall boundaries play no significant role in the dynamics. Theoretical efforts in this area focus on linear stability analyses and numerical simulations with periodic boundary conditions, a useful simplification in many instances.

Chandrasekhar 1953
first solved the linear stability theory for large-aspect-ratio rotating Rayleigh-Bénard convection;
Chandrasekhar 1961
discusses the subject in thorough detail.

For moderate Prandtl number,
Niiler & Bisshopp 1965
further clarified the influence of flow boundary conditions on the top and bottom plates in the rapidly rotating limit.
For the present discussion, the most relevant result of these efforts is that in the rapidly rotating limit (large
Ta
) with no-slip boundary conditions, the critical onset Rayleigh number scales as

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}_{\,\text{c},\text{bulk}}\approx 8.70\times\textit{Ta}^{\,2/3}-9.63\times\textit{Ta}^{\,7/12}+\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{\,1/2\,})\quad\text{as}\quad\textit{Ta}\to\infty,

(3)

assuming
\sigma=\mathcal{O}(1)

(
Clune & Knobloch 1993
;
Dawes 2001
, see)
. The
\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{7/12})
term in (
3
) arises because of Ekman pumping effects correlating with leading-order convective modes
(
Zhang & Roberts 1998
)
.
Buell & Catton 1983
first addressed the sidewall issue theoretically with linear stability analysis but found only modest corrections to critical
Ra
values. However, their analysis focused on the finite-aspect-ratio corrections to the types of modes considered in
Chandrasekhar 1953
.
The laboratory work of
Nakagawa & Frenzen 1955
gave the first qualitative stability agreement and flow visualisation for rotating convection.

Pioneering experimental studies, with an approximately 2:1 aspect ratio, found interesting discrepancies from the large-aspect ratio theory
(
Rossby 1969
)
.
Without a means to visualise the interior flow, heat transport data was the primary quantitative outcome of these experiments.
In discussing the convective onset in water, Rossby noted

We find excellent agreement between theory and experiment for the critical Rayleigh number at all Taylor numbers less than

5\times 10^{4}
;
beyond this the fluid becomes unstable at lower Rayleigh numbers. At a Taylor number
=10^{8}
, for example, the measured critical Rayleigh number is about one-third the expected value. We do not understand why this should be. It is quite reproducible; i.e., if one changes the depth of the fluid, the instability will occur at the same Rayleigh number for a given Taylor number
.
(
Rossby 1969
,
p.
322–323)

Motivated by the theory of
Veronis 1968
for subcritical phenomena, Rossby conjectured at the possibility of finite-amplitude effects and speculated about the possible modes for the “subcritical” instability.
In the early 1990s, several almost simultaneous efforts helped resolve Rossby’s paradox: We now understand that sidewalls lead to linear instability at lower Rayleigh numbers than in the large-aspect-ratio system.

Goldstein et al. 1993
;
Goldstein et al. 1994
conducted the first comprehensive linear stability analysis for a cylindrical vessel with a range of
\textit{Ra},\,\textit{Ta},\,\sigma,\,\Gamma
.
This work demonstrated that in a rapidly rotating finite-aspect ratio system, the onset of convection occurs via two distinct mode sets.
The first type comprises non-propagating modes supported within the bulk and corresponds to the periodic modes in Chandrasekhar’s large-aspect-ratio analyses and Buell’s & Catton’s finite-domain modes.
These solutions remain stationary in the rotating coordinate frame.

Goldstein et al. 1993
also found a second type of instability that proceeds via a set of faster precessing modes (retrograde with respect to system rotation).
In contrast to the bulk dynamics, these waves exist in a thin boundary layer attached to the container’s sidewall.
For large enough background rotation rates, the wall-localised modes emerge as the preferred pattern of instability in a regime subcritical to the bulk-mode instability. In the rapidly rotating limit,

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}_{\,\text{c},\text{wall}}\approx 31.8\times\textit{Ta}^{\,1/2}-4.43\times\textit{Ta}^{\,5/12}+\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{\,1/3\,})\quad\text{as}\quad\textit{Ta}\to\infty.

(4)

Herrmann & Busse 1993
derived the leading-order term in this expression. Assuming stress-free top and bottom boundary conditions,
Liao et al. 2006
computed an
\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{\,1/3})
correction distinguishing between different sidewall velocity boundary conditions. The
\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{\,5/12})
correction term also arises from no-slip top and bottom boundary conditions. As far as we know, this term is absent from the literature. In §
5.2
, we account for Ekman pumping
(
Zhang & Roberts 1998
, similar to)
to calculate this term, along with the other associated corrections.

Along with the linear stability theory, the advent of practical optical shadowgraph techniques allowed much more detailed studies of the flow structures contained in experiments
(
Boubnov & Golitsyn 1986
;
Kuo & Cross 1993
;
Zhong et al. 1993
)
.
A large number of studies — for example,
Rossby 1969
;
Boubnov & Golitsyn 1990

Alternatively, we focus on the parameter range below onset for bulk convection, where wall-mode convection exists in isolation. Fewer experimental results exist in this regime
(

With the rich interplay of wall-localised and interior dynamics, many questions remain. What is genuinely wall-generated behaviour versus what is wall-catalysed bulk dynamics? Without getting into a regime of
Ta
far exceeding current simulations and experiments, there is not enough dynamic range to disentangle various phenomena.

Within the wall-dominated cross-hatch region, the convective Rossby number satisfies

\textit{Ro}=\mathcal{O}(\textit{E}^{\,1/2})
indicating strong rotational influence.

Sprague et al. 2006
;
Julien & Knobloch 2007
show that regimes of low
\textit{E},\textit{Ro}
exhibit balanced dynamics for bulk convection and are prime candidates for dynamical reductions capturing nonlinear evolution through the application of multiple-scale perturbation theory.
In this paper, we derive a systematic dynamical reduction for wall-mode convection.

Model Setup

We start our multiscale asymptotic analysis from the Boussinesq equations
(
Spiegel & Veronis 1960
)
. We pose and analyse the system in a local Cartesian coordinate system
(x,y,z)
along a flat vertical wall, with
x
increasing into the wall at
x=\Gamma
, the coordinate
y
tangential along the wall, and
z
vertical. We choose this coordinate system to easily translate to more general situations, e.g. an upright cylinder,
(x,y,z)\to(r,\theta,z)
.

We pick our non-dimensionalisation to agree mostly with the standard thermal diffusion scaling. For box depth,
H
, and thermal diffusivity,
\kappa
, we scale
\text{length}\sim H
,
\text{velocity}\sim\kappa/H
, and
\text{time}\sim H^{2}/\kappa
.

For the temperature and pressure, the known properties of wall-mode dynamical balances
(
Herrmann & Busse 1993
)
imply a slightly non-standard normalisation. We define a linear reference temperature profile,

\displaystyle T_{\text{ref}}(z)=T_{\text{top}}+\Delta T\,(1-z/H),

(5)

where the domain comprises
0\leq z/H\leq 1
and
T_{\text{top}}
and
T_{\text{bot}}=T_{\text{top}}+\Delta T
are the fixed temperatures at the top and bottom boundary. We use the temperature perturbation,
\Theta=T-T_{\text{ref}}(z)
as a dynamical variable. Similarly, we evolve the pressure perturbation
p
relative to a reference hydrostatic profile balancing
T_{\text{ref}}(z)
.

It is common to scale
\text{pressure}\sim\nu\kappa/H^{2}
and
\text{temperature}\sim\Delta T
. However, we pick

\displaystyle p\sim 2\Omega\,\kappa,\qquad\Theta\sim\frac{2\Omega\,\kappa}{g\,\alpha\,H}.

(6)

These scalings allow the pressure to balance buoyancy and rotation; the temperature maintains thermal wind balance.

These scalings naturally introduce a “reduced” Rayleigh number as the buoyancy control parameter,

\displaystyle R\ \equiv\ \textit{Ra}\,\textit{E}=\sigma\frac{\textit{Ro}^{2}}{\textit{E}}=\frac{g\alpha\Delta TH}{2\Omega\kappa}=\mathcal{O}(1)\quad\text{as}\quad\textit{E}\to 0.

(7)

Our non-dimensional system takes the form,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{Du}{Dt}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{x}p-v}{\textit{E}}=\nabla^{2}u,

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{Dv}{Dt}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{y}p+u}{\textit{E}}=\nabla^{2}v,

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\frac{Dw}{Dt}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{z}p-\Theta}{\textit{E}}=\nabla^{2}w,

(10)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u+\partial_{y}v+\partial_{z}w=0,

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{D\Theta}{Dt}-R\,w=\nabla^{2}\Theta,

(12)

where

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}=\partial_{t}+u\,\partial_{x}+v\,\partial_{y}+w\,\partial_{z}\,,\qquad\nabla^{2}=\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}.

(13)

The system models the dynamics of a weakly compressible fluid in an upright container with uniform height
0\leq z\leq 1
and arbitrary horizontal geometry. The system rotates at a fixed rate about an axis anti-aligned with gravity; see, e.g., fig.
2
. The triplet
(u,v,w)
represents the fluid velocities in the
(x,y,z)
directions respectively. The quantity
\Theta
represents the temperature departure from a constant reference value, and
p
gives the dynamic pressure.

Figure 2:
Schematic diagram of the basic domain. Each region, I–III, comprises different length, time, and amplitude scalings in terms of powers of
\varepsilon=\textit{E}^{\,1/3}
. The asymptotic analysis considers each region separately and connects each through a series of matching conditions, eventually being left entirely with
\mathcal{O}(1)
dynamics in the bulk (Region I). We refer to Region II as the Stewartson sidewall layer and Region III as the Ekman layer (at both the top and bottom of the domain). We conduct the asymptotic analysis in Cartesian coordinates
(x,\,y,\,z)
without loss of generality. After obtaining the final results, it is straightforward to use general coordinates and re-pose the system in cylindrical polar coordinates with
x\to r
,
y\to\phi
and
z\to z
.

In this simplified geometry, we address both no-slip (
NS
) and stress-free (
SF
) boundary conditions on all walls:

\displaystyle\mbox{({z-NS}): }u=v=w=0,\mbox{ at }z=0,1

(14)

\displaystyle\mbox{({z-SF}): }\partial_{z}u=\partial_{z}v=w=0,\mbox{ at }z=0,1

(15)

\displaystyle\mbox{({x-NS}): }u=v=w=0,\mbox{ at }x=\Gamma

(16)

\displaystyle\mbox{({x-SF}): }u=\partial_{x}v=\partial_{x}w=0,\mbox{ at }x=\Gamma

(17)

Differences between no-slip and stress-free conditions on the sidewalls produce no leading-order effect on the final result, while the top/bottom boundaries show important differences.

Given that
\Theta
is the perturbation from the reference profile,
T_{\text{ref}}(z)
, the thermal boundary conditions on both plates are

\displaystyle\Theta|_{z=0}=\Theta|_{z=1}=0.

(18)

The thermal sidewall condition influences the dynamics more than any other. We use perfectly insulating sidewalls,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Theta|_{x=\Gamma}=0.

(19)

Relaxing the insulating condition to include finite conduction stabilises the wall mode because of heat losses. In the infinite conductivity limit, the container walls short-circuit the instability entirely.

3
Multiscale Asymptotic Analysis

We aim to find reduced equations capturing the leading-order nonlinear behaviour of the wall-mode instability in the limit of an infinitesimal Ekman number,
\textit{E}\ll 1
. We have already discussed the relevant pressure amplitude and Rayleigh number scaling. We now describe the
a priori
known properties of the boundary layer thicknesses and flow amplitudes.

(
i
): A thermally forced side-wall Stewartson boundary layer
(
Stewartson 1957
;
Barcilon & Pedlosky 1967a
;
Barcilon & Pedlosky 1967b
;
Hashimoto 1976
;
Fein 1978
;
Albaiz & Hart 1990
;
Pedlosky 2009
;
Kunnen et al. 2013
)
drives the dynamics from within a characteristic layer thickness

\displaystyle\,\text{d}{x}\sim\textit{E}^{\,1/3}

(20)

(
ii
) For no-slip boundary conditions, top and bottom Ekman layers
(
Greenspan 1969
;
Pedlosky 1987
)
influence the dynamics from within a characteristic layer thickness

\displaystyle\,\text{d}{z}\sim\textit{E}^{\,1/2}

(21)

(
iii
) Relative to the thermal diffusion velocity normalisation
\kappa/H
, the fluid velocities near the wall carry a large amplitude

\displaystyle\left(u,v,w\right)\sim\textit{E}^{\,-1/3},

(22)

while the interior velocities remain
\mathcal{O}(1)
in these units.

Throughout this paper, the Prandtl number remains a fixed order-unity number and does not scale with
E
in any way. Allowing
\sigma
to scale with
E
can lead to several interesting possibilities
(
Dawes 2001
;
Zhang et al. 2007
;
Zhang & Liao 2009
)
, but this remains beyond our current scope.

Assumptions (
i
) & (
ii
) introduce multiple scales, where all variables depend on both small and large length scales. For example, with the pressure

\displaystyle p=p\!\left(\frac{x}{\textit{E}^{\,1/3}},\,x,\,y,\,\frac{z}{\textit{E}^{\,1/2}},\,z,\,t\right).

(23)

Multiple-scale theory dictates
(
Kevorkian & Cole 1981
)
that each argument varies independently of the others. This assumption allows redefining the derivative operators

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\to\frac{1}{\textit{E}^{\,1/3}}\partial_{x}+\partial_{X},\quad\partial_{y}\to\partial_{Y},\quad\partial_{z}\to\frac{1}{\textit{E}^{\,1/2}}\partial_{z}+\partial_{Z}.

(24)

The capital-letter coordinates
(X,Y,Z)
now label position within the “large-scale” interior (outside boundary layers). The small-letter coordinates
(x,z)
label position within the Stewartson and Ekman boundary layers, respectively. We exclude asymptotically small-scale dynamics in the
Y
-direction along the sidewall. In the bulk-convection regime (
i.e.
,
R\sim\textit{E}^{\,-1/3}
) both horizontal directions adopt
\mathcal{O}(\textit{E}^{\,1/3})
length scales
(
Julien & Knobloch 2007
)
.

The
\mathcal{O}(\textit{E}^{\,1/3})
length scale controls the overall system, suggesting the expansion parameter,

\displaystyle\varepsilon\equiv\textit{E}^{\,1/3}.

(25)

The Ekman layers on the top and bottom thus require half-integer powers
\textit{E}^{\,1/2}=\varepsilon^{3/2}
.

Eq. (
25
) and the above scalings of pressure and velocity with
E
imply the following asymptotic series for the dynamical variables

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{u_{-1}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{u_{-1/2}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}+u_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1/2})

(26)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{-1}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{v_{-1/2}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}+v_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1/2})

(27)

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{w_{-1}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{w_{-1/2}}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}+w_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1/2})

(28)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{0}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\,p_{1/2}+\varepsilon\,p_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3/2})

(29)

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{0}+\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\Theta_{1/2}+\varepsilon\,\Theta_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3/2}).

(30)

Defining pure interior variables aids in the following analysis. Capitol-letter variables (e.g.,
U,V,W,P
) denote quantities depending only on large-scale interior coordinates
(X,Y,Z)
. These connect to the boundary layer variables via a matching principle. For example, with the leading-order pressure,

\displaystyle P_{0}(X,Y,Z)\equiv\lim_{|x|,|z|\to\infty}p_{0}(x,X,Y,z,Z),

(31)

and so on. The analysis in §
3.1
–
3.3
shows that the temperature is an interior variable, removing the need to start with a lowercase variable.

The three following subsections determine the leading-order dynamics. We examine each region shown in fig.
2
and connect each through appropriate matching conditions.

3.1
Region-I (Interior)

Given the scaling of (
8
)–(
11
), we can read off the leading-order interior dynamical equations by inspection.
The bulk dynamics only contains
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})
amplitudes. It is possible to derive this explicitly by considering the leading-order balances of the full asymptotic expansion.
From the momentum equations and continuity,

\displaystyle U_{0}=-\partial_{Y}P_{0},\quad V_{0}=\partial_{X}P_{0},\quad W_{0}=0,\quad\Theta_{0}=\partial_{Z}P_{0}.

(32)

The pressure gives the horizontal velocities and temperature in a
thermal-wind
balance (
i.e.
, a joint hydrostatic and geostrophic balance).

The temperature equation retains its original nonlinear form (except for vertical advection),

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+U_{0}\partial_{X}+V_{0}\partial_{Y})\,\Theta_{0}=(\partial_{X}^{2}+\partial_{Y}^{2}+\partial_{Z}^{2})\,\Theta_{0}.

(33)

The combined equations (
32
) & (
33
) almost form a closed system, with two important caveats.

The first major caveat is that the temperature evolution equation contains no obvious sources. Defining the horizontal velocity vector,
U_{\bot}=(U_{0},V_{0})
, the available thermal energy evolution,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\!\mathcal{A}}\frac{\Theta_{0}^{2}}{2}\,\text{d}{X}\!\,\text{d}{Y}\!\,\text{d}{Z}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\partial\mathcal{A}}\left[\frac{\Theta_{0}^{2}}{2}\,U_{\bot}-\Theta_{0}\nabla_{\bot}\Theta_{0}\right]\cdot\hat{n}\,\,\text{d}{\ell}\,\text{d}{Z}=

(34)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{\!\mathcal{A}}\left[|\nabla_{\bot}\Theta_{0}|^{2}+|\partial_{Z}\Theta_{0}|^{2}\right]\,\text{d}{X}\!\,\text{d}{Y}\!\,\text{d}{Z}\ \leq\ 0,

where
\,\text{d}{\ell}
is the integration measure along the boundary,
\partial\mathcal{A}
and
\hat{n}
is the outward unit normal vector. Eq. (
34
) shows that the boundary terms must force the system strongly enough to overcome persistent dissipation. Without the boundary terms (like in conventional bulk convection), the available thermal energy would monotonically decrease. Driving (
33
) requires forcing from the sidewall boundary layers, which we explain in §
3.2
.

The second caveat is that (
33
)
determines the evolution only for the temperature. Obtaining the velocities requires the pressure, and obtaining the pressure requires solving hydrostatic balance (
32
). The difficulty comes from not knowing the depth-independent, or
barotropic
, component of the pressure,

\displaystyle\!\left<P_{0}\right>\equiv\int_{0}^{1}P_{0}\,\text{d}Z.

(35)

We derive an equation for
\!\left<P_{0}\right>
by considering the top and bottom boundaries together with their velocity boundary conditions in §
3.3
.

3.2
Region-II (Stewartson Layer)

In the Stewartson sidewall layers, focusing on
X=\Gamma
and
-\infty<x\leq 0
, the physical container boundary corresponds to
x=0
and
X=\Gamma
, while the boundary of the interior corresponds to
X=\Gamma
and
x\to-\infty
.
The leading-order momentum equation (
8
) in the
X
-direction gives

\displaystyle u_{-1}=u_{-1/2}=0.

(36)

The leading-order diffusion of temperature from (
12
) gives

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}\Theta_{0}=0,

(37)

which implies a constant leading-order temperature across the boundary layer. Strictly speaking, (
37
) contains a linearly growing solution in
x
. The possibility of unbounded amplitudes as
|x|\to\infty

preclude this solution. Therefore, the leading-order temperature exists as a pure interior dynamical variable,

\displaystyle\Theta_{0}=\Theta_{0}(X,Y,Z).

(38)

While (
38
) implies that
\Theta_{0}
does not vary
across
the boundary layer, there exist temperature fluctuations in the directions along the wall both in
Y
and
Z
. The interior thermally forces the boundary layer via tangential gradients.

Given a temperature field in the interior, the leading-order momentum and continuity equations follow

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p_{0}-v_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(39)

\displaystyle\partial_{Y}p_{0}+u_{0}-\partial_{x}^{2}v_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(40)

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}p_{0}-\partial_{x}^{2}w_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{0}

(41)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u_{0}+\partial_{Y}v_{-1}+\partial_{Z}w_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(42)

The system is similar to the Stewartson layer balances derived in other work
(
Stewartson 1957
;
van Heijst 1983
;
Kunnen et al. 2013
)
. The main difference is that (
39
)–(
42
) have enough derivatives to satisfy all velocity boundary conditions at the physical wall, and thermal anomalies force the whole boundary layer from the interior. There is no need for the classical
\textit{E}^{\,1/4}
exterior layer needed typically needed to satisfy all velocity boundary conditions.

The interior forces the vertical velocity via eq. (
41
). Leading-order balances also imply
\partial_{x}^{2}\Theta_{1/2}=0
. To see how the boundary layer feeds back to the interior, the next-order temperature equation for
\Theta_{1}
,

\displaystyle v_{-1}\,\partial_{Y}\Theta_{0}+w_{-1}\,(\partial_{Z}\Theta_{0}-R)=\partial_{x}^{2}\Theta_{1}.

(43)

The crucial connection between the interior and boundary layer comes from the full form of the insulating thermal boundary condition at the physical sidewall,

\displaystyle\partial_{X}\Theta_{0}+\partial_{x}\Theta_{1}=0\quad\text{at}\quad x=0,\ X=\Gamma.

(44)

This couples
\Theta_{0}
, from the interior, with
\Theta_{1}
, from (
43
).
Integrating (
43
) over boundary layer coordinate,

\displaystyle x\in(-\infty,0\,]=\mathbb{R}^{-},

(45)

gives

\displaystyle q_{Y}\,\partial_{Y}\Theta_{0}+q_{Z}\,(\partial_{Z}\Theta_{0}-R)=\partial_{x}\Theta_{1}\big|_{x=0}=-\partial_{X}\Theta_{0}\big|_{X=\Gamma}

(46)

where the
\partial_{x}\Theta_{1}\big|_{x\to-\infty}
term vanishes because
v_{-1}
and
w_{-1}
decay exponentially away from
x=0
(see below), and there is no linearly growing homogeneous solution in
x
with bounded amplitude as
x\to-\infty
. Eq (
46
) expresses energy conservation between the boundary layer and the interior.

The total momentum fluxes within the sidewall layers are

\displaystyle q_{Y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!v_{-1}\,\text{d}{x},\qquad q_{Z}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!w_{-1}\,\text{d}{x}.

(47)

These purely surface quantities depend on the tangent directions,
(Y,Z)
. They couple to the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})
dynamics because while
v_{-1},\,w_{-1}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
, the boundary layer width,
\,\text{d}{x}\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. The tangential velocities act exactly like Dirac-
\delta
distributions at the boundary of the bulk,
X=\Gamma
.

To further constrain
q_{Y}
and
q_{Z}
, we integrate the continuity equation

\displaystyle\partial_{Y}q_{Y}+\partial_{Z}q_{Z}=u_{0}\big|_{x=-\infty}=U_{0}\big|_{X=\Gamma}.

(48)

The integration limit
u_{0}\big|_{x=0}=0
because of impenetrability at the physical container wall. Eq (
48
) expresses mass conservation between the boundary layer and the interior.

To close the system, we need another relation between
q_{Y}
and
q_{Z}
, which comes from directly solving (
39
)–(
42
).
We represent the horizontal velocities strictly in terms of the pressure

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\partial_{Y}p_{0}+\partial_{x}^{3}p_{0}

(49)

\displaystyle v_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p_{0}

(50)

The following coupled system relates the pressure and vertical velocity:

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}w_{-1}+\partial_{x}^{4}p_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(51)

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}^{2}w_{-1}+\partial_{Z}p_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{0},

(52)

which collapses into a single system for the vertical velocity,

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{Z}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{6}\right)w_{-1}=0.

(53)

3.2.1
Bulk onset comparison

Eq. (
53
) should look familiar; it is half of the traditional asymptotic stability condition in the rapidly rotating regime when
\textit{Ra}\sim\textit{E}^{\,4/3}
. In terms of the
R
and
\varepsilon
parameters, the full bulk leading-order balance,

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{Z}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{6}\right)w_{-1}=\varepsilon\,R\,\partial_{x}^{2}w_{-1}.

(54)

If
\varepsilon\,R\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, the left-hand side balances with
\partial_{x}^{2}\to-k^{2}
,
\partial_{Z}^{2}\to-\pi^{2}
and we have the traditional stability condition
(
Chandrasekhar 1961
)
,

\displaystyle\text{Bulk onset:}\qquad\varepsilon\,R\sim\frac{\pi^{2}+k^{6}}{k^{2}}\geq\frac{3\pi^{4/3}}{2^{2/3}}\qquad\text{for}\qquad k=\frac{\pi^{1/3}}{2^{1/6}}.

(55)

The equation represents a triple balance between vortex stretching (
i.e.
,
\partial_{Z}^{2}
) and diffusion (
i.e.
,
\partial_{x}^{6}
) on the left, with buoyancy on the right. However, if
R\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, the right-hand side of (
54
) drops out, and the bulk no longer supports harmonic
x
dependence. In that case, the only possible solutions happen with exponential
x
dependence, which can only remain bounded near a wall.

3.2.2
Boundary layer solutions

We solve the system for no-slip physical sidewall boundary conditions,

\displaystyle u_{0}=v_{-1}=w_{-1}=0\qquad\text{at}\qquad x=0.

(56)

We could alternatively use stress-free conditions; however, doing so gives exactly the same final relationship between
q_{Y}
and
q_{Z}
. We also impose
w_{-1}=0
at
Z=0,1
.

Solving (
51
) & (
52
) requires Fourier decomposing the velocities, pressure, and temperature in the
Z
direction.

\displaystyle\Theta_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\widehat{\Theta}_{n}(X,Y)\,\sin(n\pi Z),

(57)

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\hat{p}_{n}(x,X,Y)\,\cos(n\pi Z),

(58)

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\hat{u}_{n}(x,X,Y)\cos(n\pi Z),

(59)

\displaystyle v_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\hat{v}_{n}(x,X,Y)\,\cos(n\pi Z),

(60)

\displaystyle w_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}\hat{w}_{n}(x,X,Y)\,\sin(n\pi Z).

(61)

Solving for the Fourier coefficients, which decay as
x\to-\infty
and satisfy either no-slip or stress-free boundary conditions at
x=0
, gives

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{P}_{n}+\widehat{Q}_{n}\,\mathcal{X}(\alpha_{n}x),

(62)

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{n}\left(1-\mathcal{X}(\alpha_{n}x)\right),

(63)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\,\widehat{Q}_{n}\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n}x),

(64)

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}\,\widehat{Q}_{n}\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n}x),

(65)

where
\alpha_{n}=(n\pi)^{1/3}
. The boundary layer profile function,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}(x)=\frac{e^{\frac{x}{2}}\cos(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}\,x}{2}+\delta)}{\cos(\delta)},

(66)

where the phase
\delta=\pm\pi/6
depends on the tangential boundary conditions.
In both cases,

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}(x)-\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)+\mathcal{X}(x)=0,\qquad\mathcal{X}(0)=1,

(67)

which satisfies the normal velocity,
\hat{u}_{n}=0
at
x=0
. For the tangential velocities,

No-slip:

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{n}=\hat{w}_{n}=0

\displaystyle\quad\iff\quad\delta=+\frac{\pi}{6},\qquad\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(0)=0,\quad

(68)

Stress-free:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\hat{v}_{n}=\partial_{x}\hat{w}_{n}=0

\displaystyle\quad\iff\quad\delta=-\frac{\pi}{6},\qquad\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}(0)=0.

(69)

Fig.
3
shows the velocity amplitudes for the no-slip case.

The capitol-letter variables,
\widehat{U}_{n}
,
\widehat{P}_{n}
and
\widehat{\Theta}_{n}
are the Fourier transforms of the interior bulk quantities that satisfy thermal wind balance at
X=\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{n}=-\partial_{Y}\widehat{P}_{n},\qquad-n\pi\widehat{P}_{n}=\widehat{\Theta}_{n}.

(70)

The overall amplitude satisfies,

\displaystyle(\partial_{Y}+n\pi)\,\widehat{Q}_{n}=\widehat{U}_{n},

(71)

which is the Fourier-space version of mass conservation at the bulk boundary (
48
).

Figure 3:
Thermal Stewartson boundary layer solutions for no-slip tangential velocities: The blue line shows
1-\mathcal{X}(x)
. The orange line shows
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)
.

There are a couple of notable comments regarding (
62
)–(
65
). The first is that both
\hat{v}_{n}
and
\hat{w}_{n}
exist strictly within the boundary layer and do not continue into the bulk interior. The second comment is that
\hat{p}_{n}
and
\hat{u}_{n}
both contain components that are independent of
x
. These terms take precisely the same form as the interior geostrophic and hydrostatic balance found in §
3.1
.

The most important comment regarding (
64
) & (
65
) is that the coefficients of
\hat{v}_{k,n}
and
\hat{w}_{k,n}
take identical forms.

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{n}=\hat{w}_{n}

(72)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\hat{v}_{n}\,\text{d}{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\hat{w}_{n}\,\text{d}{x}.

(73)

That is, the
\widehat{Q}_{n}
amplitude gives the Fourier coefficients for
both

q_{Y}
and
q_{Z}
, closing the system of equations.
However, we still need to collect all the relevant information and pose the system in a local abstract form, not in Fourier space.

Finally, regarding the bulk, all relevant quantities do not depend on the value of
\delta=\pm\pi/6
. This observation accords with the findings from
Liao et al. 2006
that the leading-order stability is independent of the sidewall velocity boundary conditions.

3.2.3
Hilbert transform

While the Fourier amplitudes of
v_{-1}
,
q_{Y}
,
w_{-1}
and
q_{Z}
are all identical, the local functions are not equal because
v_{-1}
and
q_{Y}
are Fourier cosine series in
Z
, but
w_{-1}
and
q_{Z}
are Fourier sine series. We need an operator to transform between the different parities, which naturally leads to the Hilbert transform,
\mathcal{H}
, in the
Z
direction. For the present analysis, the utility of the Hilbert transform lies in its action on a Fourier sine and cosine basis. For
n\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\!\left[\,\sin(n\pi Z)\,\right]=-\cos(n\pi Z),\qquad\mathcal{H}\!\left[\cos(n\pi Z)\right]=+\sin(n\pi Z),

(74)

which alters the parity of the basis elements in the same manner as a derivative but without the multiplication by the wavenumber,
n\pi
. Additionally, the Hilbert transform possesses the following useful properties

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{2}[f]=-f+\left<f\right>,\quad\partial_{Z}\mathcal{H}\!\left[f\right]=\mathcal{H}\!\left[\partial_{Z}f\right]

(75)

The Benjamin-Ono equation
(
Benjamin 1967
;
Ono 1975
)
for small-amplitude surface gravity waves over an infinitely deep interior employs the Hilbert transform for reasons similar to the current context. In particular, (
75
) implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\partial_{Z}\equiv|\partial_{Z}|,

(76)

for trigonometric functions.
Therefore, we may factor the Laplacian-like operator in (
53
)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{Z}^{2}+\partial_{x}^{6}\right)=\left(|\partial_{Z}|+\partial_{x}^{3}\right)\!\left(-|\partial_{Z}|+\partial_{x}^{3}\right),

(77)

which separates modes that decay as
x\to\infty
versus
x\to-\infty
.

Our final closure for the boundary momentum fluxes is

\displaystyle q_{Y}=-\mathcal{H}\!\left[q_{Z}\right],

(78)

which means they share the same Fourier coefficients but with different vertical parity.

3.2.4
Barotropic mode

Finally, we derive properties of the barotropic mode in the Stewartson layer. We first take the vertical average of the horizontal velocities

\displaystyle\!\left<v_{-1}\right>=\partial_{x}\!\left<p_{0}\right>

(79)

\displaystyle\!\left<u_{0}\right>=-\partial_{Y}\!\left<p_{0}\right>+\partial_{x}^{3}\!\left<p_{0}\right>

(80)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\!\left<u_{0}\right>+\partial_{Y}\!\left<v_{-1}\right>=\partial_{x}^{4}\!\left<p_{0}\right>=0.

(81)

Therefore,
\!\left<p_{0}\right>=\!\left<P_{0}\right>
within the sidewall layer, with
\!\left<u_{0}\right>=-\partial_{Y}\!\left<P_{0}\right>
. Impenetrability at the physical container boundary, along with fixing the overall pressure gauge condition, implies

\displaystyle\!\left<P_{0}\right>=0\qquad\text{at}\qquad x=0,\ X=\Gamma,

(82)

which is the only sidewall boundary condition needed for no-slip top and bottom boundary conditions.

Free-slip top and bottom boundaries also require the next-order barotropic momentum balances and continuity in the sidewall layer,

\displaystyle\!\left<f\right>+\partial_{Y}\!\left<p_{1}\right>+\!\left<u_{1}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}\!\left<v_{0}\right>

(83)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\!\left<p_{1}\right>+\partial_{X}\!\left<P_{0}\right>-\!\left<v_{0}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(84)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\!\left<u_{1}\right>+\partial_{X}\!\left<U_{0}\right>+\partial_{Y}\!\left<v_{0}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(85)

where

\displaystyle\!\left<f\right>=\frac{\partial_{x}\!\left<u_{0}\,v_{-1}\right>+\partial_{Y}\!\left<v_{-1}\,v_{-1}\right>}{\sigma}.

(86)

The tangential solution in the sidewall layer,

\displaystyle\!\left<v_{0}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{X}\!\left<P_{0}\right>-\int_{-\infty}^{x}(x^{\prime}-x)\,\!\left<\,f(x^{\prime})\,\right>\,\text{d}{x^{\prime}}.

(87)

A no-slip boundary condition,
\!\left<v_{0}\right>=0
, at
x=0
,
X=\Gamma
implies

\displaystyle\sigma\,\partial_{X}\!\left<P_{0}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!x\,\left[\,\partial_{x}\!\left<u_{0}\,v_{-1}\right>+\partial_{Y}\!\left<v_{-1}\,v_{-1}\right>\,\right]\,\text{d}{x}

(89)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \!\left<\,U_{0}\,q_{Y}\right>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!x\,\partial_{x}\left[(1-\mathcal{X}(x))\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)\right]\,\text{d}{x}+

\displaystyle 2\,\!\left<\,q_{Y}\,\partial_{Y}q_{Y}\right>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!x\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)^{2}\,\text{d}{x}.

Computing the coefficients,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!x\,\partial_{x}\left[(1-\mathcal{X}(x))\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)\right]\,\text{d}{x}=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{-}}\!\!x\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)^{2}\,\text{d}{x}=-\frac{3}{4}.

(90)

Both coefficients are independent of
\delta=\pm\pi/6
.
Putting everything together,

\displaystyle\partial_{X}\!\left<P_{0}\right>=-\frac{3\!\left<\,q_{Y}\,\partial_{Y}q_{Y}\right>-\!\left<\,q_{Y}\,\partial_{Y}P_{0}\right>}{2\sigma}\qquad\text{at}\qquad X=\Gamma.

(91)

3.3
Region-III (Ekman Layers)

We still need to fix the barotropic pressure,
\!\left<P_{0}\right>
, in the interior, which requires considering the vertical vorticity along with the top and bottom boundary conditions.
Geostrophic balance relates the vertical vorticity and the pressure,

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}\equiv\partial_{X}V_{0}-\partial_{Y}U_{0}=\left(\partial_{X}^{2}+\partial_{Y}^{2}\right)P_{0}.

(92)

Taking the horizontal curl of the momentum equations and using the continuity equation,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\partial_{t}+U_{0}\partial_{X}+V_{0}\partial_{Y}\right)\zeta_{0}-\left(\partial_{X}^{2}+\partial_{Y}^{2}\right)\zeta_{0}=\partial_{Z}W_{3}+\partial_{Z}^{2}\zeta_{0}.

(93)

Notably, the leading-order rotational terms enforce

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}W_{i}=0\quad\text{for}\quad i=0,\,\tfrac{1}{2},\,1,\,\tfrac{3}{2},\,2,\,\tfrac{5}{2}.

(94)

The first non-constant vertical velocity is
W_{3}
.

3.3.1
Stress-free boundaries

For stress-free boundaries (
15
), applying the averaging operator to (
93
) gives

\displaystyle\mbox{({z-SF}): }\quad\partial_{t}\!\left<\zeta_{0}\right>+\partial_{X}\!\left<U_{0}\,\zeta_{0}\right>+\partial_{Y}\!\left<V_{0}\,\zeta_{0}\right>=\sigma\left(\partial_{X}^{2}+\partial_{Y}^{2}\right)\!\left<\zeta_{0}\right>.

(95)

The
\partial_{Z}
terms on the right-hand side vanish because of impenetrability and vanishing stress,

\displaystyle W_{3}(Z=0)=W_{3}(Z=1)=0

(96)

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}\zeta_{0}(Z=0)=\partial_{Z}\zeta_{0}(Z=1)=0.

(97)

Eq. (
95
) determines the missing depth-independent pressure component needed to obtain the velocities in (
33
). The depth-dependent pressure (hence temperature) forces (
95
) through the nonlinear baroclinic interactions, e.g.,

\!\left<U_{0}\zeta_{0}\right>-\!\left<U_{0}\right>\!\left<\zeta_{0}\right>
, and the two systems are coupled.
We solve (
95
) together with boundary conditions
\!\left<P_{0}\right>=0
and (
91
).

3.3.2
No-slip boundaries

For no-slip top and bottom boundaries, one expects an Ekman-pumping effect of the order
(
Zhang & Roberts 1998
)

\displaystyle W_{\text{Ekman}}\sim\textit{E}^{\,1/2}\,\zeta_{0}=\varepsilon^{3/2}\,\zeta_{0}.

(98)

Together, (
94
) & (
98
) imply a depth-independent Ekman velocity,

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}W_{3/2}=0.

(99)

The Ekman layers determine the barotropic pressure.

Given an interior flow, the Ekman boundary layer response follows as a well-known exercise
(
Greenspan 1969
;
Pedlosky 1987
)
. Region-III lies beyond the sidewall Stewartson layer, hence
\partial_{x}\to 0
. Within the top and bottom boundary layers,

\displaystyle\partial_{z}P_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(100)

\displaystyle-v_{0}+\partial_{X}P_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}u_{0},

(101)

\displaystyle u_{0}+\partial_{Y}P_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}v_{0}

(102)

\displaystyle\partial_{X}u_{0}+\partial_{Y}v_{0}+\partial_{z}w_{3/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(103)

The solutions to (
100
)–(
103
) are well known. For
Z=0
and the boundary layer coordinate
0\leq z<\infty
. For
Z=1
and the boundary layer coordinate
-\infty<z\leq 0
. In both cases,

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}u_{0}\\
v_{0}\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}\text{se}(z)&\text{ce}(z)\\
-\text{ce}(z)&\text{se}(z)\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{X}P_{0}\\
\partial_{Y}P_{0}\\
\end{array}\right],

where
P_{0}
is evaluated at
Z=0
or
Z=1
depending on the boundary layer and the functions,

\displaystyle\text{se}(z)=e^{-\frac{|z|}{\sqrt{2}}}\sin\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\sqrt{2}}\right),\qquad\text{ce}(z)=1-e^{-\frac{|z|}{\sqrt{2}}}\cos\left(\tfrac{|z|}{\sqrt{2}}\right).

(111)

Both
\text{se}(0)=\text{ce}(0)=0
.

For the vertical velocity at either boundary,

\displaystyle w_{3/2}=\text{sign}(z)\,\frac{\zeta_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\text{ce}(z)-\text{se}(z)\right).

(112)

The Ekman pumping velocity must connect to the interior as
|z|\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\!w_{3/2}=\text{sign}(z)\frac{\zeta_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}

(113)

However, in the interior
\partial_{Z}W_{3/2}=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}(X,Y,Z=0)+\zeta_{0}(X,Y,Z=1)=0,

(114)

which indirectly fixes the barotropic pressure. In terms of the local pressure,

\displaystyle P_{0}|_{Z=0}+P_{0}|_{Z=1}=2\,\Phi(X,Y),

(115)

where
\Phi(X,Y)
represents a two-dimensional harmonic function (the factor of two provides a later simplification). The Stewartson layer supports no barotropic pressure, and
\Phi
ensures
\left<P_{0}\right>
vanishes at the sidewall.

Given hydrostatic balance,
\partial_{Z}P_{0}(Z)=\Theta_{0}(Z)
, the pressure decomposes into barotropic and baroclinic components,

\displaystyle P_{0}(Z)=\!\left<P_{0}\right>+\int_{0}^{Z}\!Z_{0}\,\Theta_{0}(Z_{0})\,\text{d}{Z_{0}}-\int_{Z}^{1}\!(1-Z_{0})\,\Theta_{0}(Z_{0})\,\text{d}{Z_{0}}.

(116)

Applying (
115
), the barotropic pressure is

\displaystyle\!\left<P_{0}\right>=\Phi(X,Y)-\int_{0}^{1}\!\left(Z-\frac{1}{2}\right)\Theta_{0}(X,Y,Z)\,\text{d}{Z}.

(117)

The Stewartson layer equations imply
\!\left<P_{0}\right>=0
within the side wall layer, thus fixing the harmonic function,
\Phi(X,Y)
when applying (
117
) at
X=\Gamma
.

While the Ekman layers in the bulk dictate leading-order dynamics of the barotropic mode, the Ekman layers within the sidewall Stewartson layers generate a lower-order passive response. There is, however, one notable aspect. The balances in the sidewall naturally produce velocities
u_{0},v_{-1},w_{-1}
. That is, the tangential velocities are
\mathcal{O}(\textit{E}^{\,-1/3})
compared to
\mathcal{O}(1)
for the normal component. However, the normal component achieves its largest amplitudes in the form of an Ekman response.

Like before,
\partial_{z}p_{0}=0
. Now, the horizontal velocities are isotropic,

\displaystyle-v_{-1}+\partial_{x}p_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}\,u_{-1},

(118)

\displaystyle u_{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}\,v_{-1}.

(119)

The Ekman flux is now larger than within the bulk, but is still subdominant compared to the
w_{-1}
convection;
i.e.
,
\partial_{x}u_{-1}+\partial_{z}w_{-1/2}=0
,

\displaystyle w_{-1/2}=\text{sign}(z)\,\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}p_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\text{ce}(z)-\text{se}(z)\right).

(120)

The
w_{-1/2}
does not automatically satisfy the no-slip condition at
x=0
, which requires an
\mathcal{O}(\textit{E}^{\,1/2})
corner layer
(
Kerswell & Barenghi 1995
)
that remains passive with respect to the leading-order dynamics. Solving the corner layer requires a somewhat technical direct numerical calculation
(
Burns et al. 2022
)
. While the sidewall Ekman response remains passive in the fully asymptotic regime, we should expect noticeable corrections to heat transport for transitional values of the Rayleigh number
(
Stellmach et al. 2014
;
Julien et al. 2016
)
.

4
System summary

To summarise our multiscale asymptotic analysis, we here report the final equations in dimensional form, with all explicit parameter values restored. All equations and variables are either purely bulk quantities or supported on the bulk boundary. All boundary layers have been eliminated and act only backstage. We, therefore, drop the notation of small-case and capital letters distinguishing between different scales. All coordinates are lowercase, and all variables are in their dimensional physical form. The relevant physical variables are velocity,
u
, temperature,
T
, vertical vorticity,
\zeta=\hat{z}\cdot\nabla\times u
, and wall fluxes,
q_{\ell},\,q_{z}
.

4.1
Geometry and operators

The container is now a general upright direct product of a
z
direction with a smooth but otherwise arbitrary horizontal area,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{A}\times[\,0,H\,],

(121)

with (e.g.) Cartesian horizontal coordinates
(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}
, and vertical coordinate
z\in[0,H]
, with local unit vector,
\hat{z}
. The boundary of the horizontal area is
\partial\mathcal{A}
with the local outward normal vector,
\hat{n}
. In the case of a general smooth
\partial\mathcal{A}
, the analysis in §
3.2
would follow the same reasoning with
x
representing a local signed-distance coordinate
(
Hester & Vasil 2023
)
.
We represent the coordinate along the tangential direction to
\partial\mathcal{A}
as
\ell
, with local unit vector,
\hat{\ell}=\hat{z}\times\hat{n}
assuming a right-handed coordinate system. For short-hand, we denote derivatives,
\partial_{z}=\hat{z}\cdot\nabla
,
\partial_{n}=\hat{n}\cdot\nabla
, and
\partial_{\ell}=\hat{\ell}\cdot\nabla
. In the two most relevant cases,

Cartesian:

\displaystyle\partial_{n}=\partial_{x},\quad\partial_{\ell}=\partial_{y},

(122)

Cylindrical:

\displaystyle\partial_{n}=\partial_{r},\quad\partial_{\ell}=\frac{1}{r}\,\partial_{\phi}.

(123)

We define the respective barotropic average and Hilbert transform,

\displaystyle\!\left<p\right>=\frac{1}{H}\int_{0}^{H}\!\!p(z^{\prime})\,\text{d}{z^{\prime}},\qquad\mathcal{H}\!\left[q\right](z)=\text{p.v.}\!\left<q(z^{\prime})\,\cot(\tfrac{\pi(z^{\prime}-z)}{H})\right>,

(124)

where p.v. denotes the principle value in the Hilbert transform integral.

4.2
Dynamics

For
(x,y)\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle 2\Omega\,\partial_{z}u=g\alpha\,\hat{z}\times\nabla T,\qquad\partial_{t}T+u\cdot\nabla T=\kappa\nabla^{2}T,

(125)

\displaystyle T\big|_{z=0}=T_{\text{bot}},\qquad T\big|_{z=H}=T_{\text{top}}.

(126)

For
(x,y)\in\partial\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle q_{\ell}=-\mathcal{H}\!\left[q_{z}\right],\qquad\partial_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\partial_{z}q_{z}=u_{n},\qquad(q_{\ell}\,\partial_{\ell}+q_{z}\,\partial_{z})\,T=-\kappa\,\partial_{n}T,

(127)

\displaystyle q_{z}=0\quad\text{for}\quad z=0,H.

(128)

For vertical no-slip,

\displaystyle\zeta\big|_{z=0}+\zeta\big|_{z=H}=0.

(129)

For vertical stress-free,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\!\left<\zeta\right>+\nabla\cdot\,\!\left<u\,\zeta\right>=\nu\,\nabla^{2}\!\left<\zeta\right>,\qquad-2\nu\,\!\left<u_{\ell}\right>\!\big|_{\partial\mathcal{A}}=\!\left<\,q_{\ell}\left(4\,\partial_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\partial_{z}q_{z}\right)\,\right>.

(130)

4.3
Heat transport

Heat transport is an essential diagnostic in convection-dominated systems, with much work for laboratory and natural systems still ongoing
(
Doering et al. 2019
;
Schumacher & Sreenivasan 2020
;
Vasil et al. 2021
;
Lohse & Shishkina 2024
)
.
Rapid rotation adds many additional nuances
(
Julien et al. 2012
;
Stellmach et al. 2014
;
Julien et al. 2016
)
.
In studies of bulk convection, heat transport from wall modes tends to confound measurements, and efforts have attempted to quantify their influence
(
Terrien et al. 2023
)
.

At first glance, the reduced wall-mode equations do not appear to contain any means for vertical heat transport; there is no bulk vertical velocity. The resolution occurs when taking into account the sidewall heat fluxes. All the vertical heat transport happens in the sidewall layer, which manifests within the bulk via
q_{z}
.

Defining the horizontal average,
\overline{T}
, the mean thermal equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{T}+\partial_{z}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{A}}\!q_{z}T\,\text{d}{\ell}-\kappa\,\partial_{z}\overline{T}\right]=0.

(131)

The integrated boundary term follows straightforwardly from integrating the bulk equation and applying (
127
). When the system is in thermal equilibrium, the Nusselt number is a global constant with

\displaystyle\textit{Nu}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{A}}\!q_{z}\Theta\,\text{d}{\ell}-\kappa\,\partial_{z}\overline{T},

(132)

where in this case
\Theta=T-\overline{T}
.

When considering the boundary terms in (
34
), we also now find the nontrivial wall-modes “power integral”
(
Howard 1963
)
that regulates overall input and dissipation,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\!\left<\overline{\Theta^{2}}\right>=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{A}}\!\left<q_{z}\,\partial_{z}\overline{T}\,\Theta\right>\,\text{d}{\ell}-\kappa\!\left<\overline{|\nabla\Theta|^{2}}\right>.

(133)

In both cases, it is clear the vertical velocity behaves precisely as a Dirac-
\delta
distribution supported on the boundary; e.g., in local Cartesian coordinates with the wall at
x=0
,

\displaystyle w(x,y,z)=q_{z}(y,z)\,\delta(x),

(134)

where the
\delta
-function replaces the detailed behaviour within the sidewall layer.

5
Linear Instability

From now on, we revert to the same non-dimensionalisation used throughout the asymptotic analysis. However, we do not use mixtures of lowercase and capital letters to distinguish between boundary layer and bulk quantities. All variables and coordinates are bulk-only; hence, we use their standard nomenclature.

For the temperature perturbations linearised around a vertical gradient,
T=R\,(1-z)+\theta(x,y,z)
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta=\nabla^{2}\theta,\qquad\theta=\partial_{z}p

(135)

\displaystyle R\,q_{z}=\partial_{n}\theta,\qquad\partial_{\ell}q_{\ell}+\partial_{z}q_{z}=-\partial_{\ell}p\qquad q_{\ell}=-\mathcal{H}\!\left[q_{z}\right]

(136)

The linearised equations collapse into a single equation and set of boundary conditions for the temperature perturbations.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta=\nabla^{2}\theta\qquad\text{in}\qquad\mathcal{A}\times[\,0,1\,]

(137)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\ell}+|\partial_{z}|\right)|\partial_{z}|\,\partial_{n}\theta=R\,\partial_{\ell}\theta\qquad\text{on}\qquad\partial\mathcal{A}\times[\,0,1\,],

(138)

where
|\partial_{z}|=\mathcal{H}\,\partial_{z}
. For the top and bottom boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\theta\big|_{z=0}=\theta\big|_{z=1}=0.

(139)

5.1
Semi-infinite channel

This section reproduces the final results in
Herrmann & Busse 1993
with the advantage that now all aspects are physically apparent. We assume a semi-infinite domain with the wall-normal coordinate,
-\infty<x\leq 0
and harmonic dependence along the wall,
\sim e^{iky}
, with wavenumber
k
.

The following exponential profile satisfies the boundary condition at
x=0
,

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\,\beta\,x+i\,(ky+\omega t)}\sin(\pi z)+\text{c.c.}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\beta=\frac{k(k+i\pi)R}{\pi\left(k^{2}+\pi^{2}\right)}.

(140)

Assuming
R>0
, the temperature perturbations decays as
x\to-\infty
. The bulk evolution equation gives the dispersion relation for the complex-valued frequency

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2k^{3}R^{2}}{\pi(\pi^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}-i\,\frac{k^{2}(k^{2}-\pi^{2})R^{2}-\pi^{2}(\pi^{2}+k^{2})^{3}}{\pi^{2}(\pi^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}

(141)

The real-valued growth rate,

\displaystyle\gamma=-\mathfrak{Im}(\omega)=\frac{k^{2}(k^{2}-\pi^{2})R^{2}-\pi^{2}(\pi^{2}+k^{2})^{3}}{\pi^{2}(\pi^{2}+k^{2})^{2}}.

(142)

The growth rate is positive for

\displaystyle R\ \geq\ R_{\text{c}}(k)=\frac{\pi(k^{2}+\pi^{2})^{3/2}}{k(k^{2}-\pi^{2})^{1/2}}\geq R_{\text{c}}(k_{\text{c}})=\pi^{2}\sqrt{6\sqrt{3}}\ \approx\ 31.8167

(143)

The respective critical wavenumber and frequency are

\displaystyle k_{\text{c}}=\pi\sqrt{2+\sqrt{3}}\approx 6.0690,\qquad\omega_{\text{c}}=2\pi^{2}\sqrt{3(2+\sqrt{3})}\approx 66.0487.

(144)

The sign convention for frequency means that
\omega>0
moves retrograde to the background rotation,
\Omega
. The respective phase and group speeds,

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{\text{c}}}{k_{\text{c}}}=2\sqrt{3}\,\pi\approx 10.8828,\qquad\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k}\Big|_{k_{\text{c}},R_{\text{c}}}=2(\sqrt{3}-2)\,\pi\approx-1.68357.

(145)

Notably, the group speed is always prograde above onset.

Even though we remove the explicit Stewartson boundary layer, at onset, the temperature perturbations remain quite localised near the wall (on the bulk scale,
x
), where

\displaystyle\beta_{\text{c}}=\pi\sqrt{3+2\sqrt{3}}+i\,\pi\,3^{1/4}\ \approx\ 7.9873+4.1345\,i.

(146)

In physical units, the critical wavelength along the wall is approximately
\lambda_{y}\approx 1.04\,H
and the
e
-folding length away from the wall is
\lambda_{x}\approx 0.13\,H
.

For large
R
the growth rate,

\displaystyle\gamma\sim\frac{R^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-\frac{k^{4}+3R^{2}}{k^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(1)\qquad\text{as}\qquad R\to\infty.

(147)

The fastest-growing wavenumber,
k
maximises the 2nd term with
k\sim 3^{1/4}\sqrt{R}
leaving

\displaystyle\gamma\sim\frac{R^{2}}{\pi^{2}}-2\sqrt{3}R+\mathcal{O}(1)\qquad\text{as}\qquad R\to\infty.

(148)

The severe dependence of
\gamma
on
R
makes nonlinear simulations very stiff at high values of criticality. For example, when
R=5\,R_{\text{c}}(k_{\text{c}})
,
\gamma\approx 2500
. Fast timescales exist for the propagation as well. As
R\to\infty
the phase and group speeds are exactly opposite,

\displaystyle c_{p}\sim\frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}\pi}-\frac{4\pi}{3},\qquad c_{g}\sim-\frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}\pi}-4\pi.

(149)

Reintroducing physical parameters implies the dimensional growth rate in the large-
R
limit,

\displaystyle\gamma^{*}\sim\frac{(g\alpha\Delta T)^{2}}{(2\Omega)^{2}\kappa},

(150)

which is notably independent of the container size.

5.2
Ekman-pumping correction

In §
1
, we report the critical Rayleigh numbers, including the leading-order corrections due to Ekman pumping effects in (
3
) & (
4
). The
\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{7/12})
correction to the bulk
Ra
has an interesting history. The
\mathcal{O}(\textit{Ta}^{5/12})
correction to the wall
Ra
has not, to our knowledge, been calculated before.

In his famous book,
Chandrasekhar 1961
calculated the leading-order contribution for bulk modes (unaware of wall modes at the time) using sound asymptotic methods,

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}\sim(27\pi^{4}/4)^{1/3}\,\textit{Ta}^{\,2/3},\qquad\text{as}\qquad\textit{Ta}\to\infty.

(151)

However, when comparing to numerical calculations up to
\textit{Ta}\approx 10^{12}
he noticed a discrepancy for no-slip boundary conditions that appeared like an offset in the numerical proportionality factor, commenting
“… the discussion is not ‘fine’ enough to account for the differences in the constants of proportionality …”

(
Chandrasekhar 1961
, §27
d
“The origin of the
T^{\frac{2}{3}}
-law”)
. The resolution of the issue is that the relative leading-order correction is
\propto\textit{Ta}^{-1/12}
, which produces a noticeable change for realistic numerical and laboratory parameter values. Ekman pumping manifests especially clearly in heat-transport enhancement
(
King et al. 2009
;
Stellmach et al. 2014
;
Julien et al. 2016
)
. The upshot is that Chandrasekhar’s discussion was ‘fine’ enough; it simply did not go far enough.

The issue became fully resolved when
Zhang & Roberts 1998
showed the leading-order correction results from considering the Ekman pumping boundary conditions of the form we use in §
3.3
(
113
), together with a leading-order interior equation of the form as (
54
). The critical bulk Rayleigh number and wavenumber,

Ra

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle(27\pi^{4}/4)^{1/3}\,\textit{Ta}^{\,2/3}-(8192\,\pi^{4})^{1/6}\textit{Ta}^{\,7/12}

(152)

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle(\pi^{2}/2)^{1/6}\textit{Ta}^{1/6}-(27\pi^{4}/4)^{-1/3}\textit{Ta}^{1/12},

(153)

as
\textit{Ta}\to\infty
.
We use analogous techniques to find the equivalent correction for wall modes.

The Ekman flux in the bulk is much too small to produce a significant correction. However, in the sidewall,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}p_{1/2}-v_{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(154)

\displaystyle\partial_{Y}p_{1/2}+u_{1/2}-\partial_{x}^{2}v_{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(155)

\displaystyle\partial_{Z}p_{1/2}-\partial_{x}^{2}w_{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta_{1/2}

(156)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}u_{1/2}+\partial_{Y}v_{-1/2}+\partial_{Z}w_{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(157)

The boundary condition is the key difference from the leading order,

\displaystyle w_{-1/2}=\mp\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}p_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\qquad\text{at}\qquad Z=0,1.

(158)

Projecting the system on to the relevant
2\cos(n\pi Z)
and
2\sin(n\pi Z)
functions,

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\hat{p}_{n,1/2}-\hat{v}_{n,-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(159)

\displaystyle\partial_{Y}\hat{p}_{n,1/2}+\hat{u}_{n,1/2}-\partial_{x}^{2}\hat{v}_{n,-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(160)

\displaystyle-\alpha_{n}^{3}\,\hat{p}_{n,1/2}-\partial_{x}^{2}\hat{w}_{n,-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\Theta}_{n,1/2}

(161)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\hat{u}_{n,1/2}+\partial_{Y}\hat{v}_{n,-1/2}+\alpha_{n}^{3}\hat{w}_{n,-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\sum_{m+n=\text{even}}\!\!\!\!\sqrt{2}\,\alpha_{m}^{2}\widehat{Q}_{m}\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{m}x),

(162)

where recalling
\alpha_{n}=(n\pi)^{1/3}
.
The right-hand side results from integrating by parts in
Z
and using the Ekman boundary conditions,

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\cos(n\pi Z)\,\partial_{Z}w(Z)\,\text{d}{Z}=n\pi\,\hat{w}_{n}+2\left[(-1)^{n}w(1)-w(0)\right],

(163)

where by definition

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{n}=2\int_{0}^{1}\sin(n\pi Z)\,w(Z)\,\text{d}{Z}.

(164)

The solution to the system is straightforward but messy. However, to close the bulk dynamics, we only need the relationship between
q_{Y}
and
q_{Z}
.

The final result in general form (dropping the
X,Y,Z
bulk notation),

\displaystyle q_{\ell}+\mathcal{H}\!\left[q_{z}\right]=-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\mathcal{E}[q_{\ell}]\qquad\text{where}\qquad\sqrt{\varepsilon}=\textit{E}^{\,1/6}.

(165)

The right-hand side contains the highly nonlocal operator

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}[q]}_{n}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2/3}}\sum_{m+n=\text{even}}\frac{m}{(nm)^{1/3}(m+n)}\,\widehat{q}_{m}.

(166)

We can now continue to find the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1/2})
perturbation to
R,\omega,k
from the semi-infinite stability problem:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta_{1/2}+\partial_{\tau}\theta_{0}=\nabla^{2}\theta_{1/2},

(167)

\displaystyle\theta_{1/2}=\partial_{z}p_{1/2}

(168)

\displaystyle R_{0}\,q_{z,1/2}+R_{1/2}\,q_{z,0}=\partial_{n}\theta_{1/2},

(169)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}q_{y,1/2}+\partial_{z}q_{z,1/2}=-\partial_{y}p_{1/2},

(170)

\displaystyle q_{y,1/2}+\mathcal{H}\!\left[q_{z,1/2}\right]=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi^{2/3}}q_{y,0}.

(171)

The system has an extra
R_{1/2}
term along with an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1/2})
timescale,
\partial_{\tau}
. For a given wavenumber along the wall,
k
,

\displaystyle R_{1/2}=-\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi^{1/3}k\left(k^{2}-3\pi^{2}\right)\sqrt{k^{2}+\pi^{2}}}{\left(k^{2}-\pi^{2}\right)^{3/2}},\qquad\omega_{1/2}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\,\pi^{1/3}k\left(k^{2}+\pi^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(k^{2}-\pi^{2}\right)^{2}}.

(172)

Optimising the combined reduced Rayleigh number,
R\sim R_{0}(k)+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,R_{1/2}(k)
, over
k
yields

\displaystyle R\sim\sqrt{6\sqrt{3}}\,\pi^{2}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sqrt{4\sqrt{3}-6}\,\pi^{4/3}\ \approx\ 31.8167-4.4329\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(173)

\displaystyle k\sim\sqrt{2+\sqrt{3}}\,\pi+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\frac{5\,\left(1+\sqrt{3}\right)}{6}\,\pi^{1/3}\ \approx\ 6.06909+3.33445\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(174)

\displaystyle\omega\sim 2\sqrt{3(2+\sqrt{3})}\,\pi^{2}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\left(23+13\sqrt{3}\right)}{3}\pi^{4/3}\ \approx\ 66.0487+69.8097\sqrt{\varepsilon},

(175)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The next section computes the critical onset parameters directly.

5.3
Direct numerical calculations

We validate the asymptotic correction from §
5.2
with high-precision direct numerical calculations of the critical Rayleigh number, wave number and precession frequency over a range of very small but finite Ekman numbers. We solve a linearised version of (
8
)–(
12
) in a finite Cartesian channel assuming
y
-direction Fourier dependence with a complex-valued growth rate,
i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}(\gamma+i\,\omega)\,u

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{x}p-v}{\textit{E}}=(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}-k^{2})\,u,

(176)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}(\gamma+i\,\omega)\,v

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{ik\,p+u}{\textit{E}}=(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}-k^{2})\,v,

(177)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}(\gamma+i\,\omega)\,w

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{z}p-\theta}{\textit{E}}=(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}-k^{2})\,w,

\displaystyle(\gamma+i\,\omega)\,\theta-R\,w=(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2}-k^{2})\,\theta,

\displaystyle u\ =\ v\ =\ w\ =\ 0\qquad\text{at}\qquad z=1,\quad\text{and}\quad x=0,\,\Gamma.

(181)

However, vertical reflection symmetry,
z\to 1-z

\displaystyle u\ =\ v\ =\ p\ =\ \partial_{z}\theta\ =\ 0\qquad\text{at}\qquad z=\frac{1}{2}.

(182)

The perfectly insulating thermal boundary condition at
x=\Gamma
implies
\partial_{x}\theta=0
. We impose perfectly conducting conditions at all other walls,
\theta=0
at
x=0
and
z=1
. The conducting condition at
x=0

x=\Gamma
boundary with quasi-exponential decay into the interior.

We use Dedalus to implement a continuous Galerkin spectral-element solver for (
176
)–(
182

. Each subdomain uses a tensor-product discretisation with between 20-120 polynomial modes in each direction, chosen so that the solutions in each element are resolved to a truncation error of

.

We find the minimum onset value for

as a function of

\displaystyle\gamma(k,R)=\partial_{k}\gamma(k,R)=0.

We update the approximate solution to (

\displaystyle k\approx-\frac{\gamma_{1,0}}{2\gamma_{2,0}},\qquad R\approx\frac{\gamma_{1,0}^{2}}{4\gamma_{0,1}\gamma_{2,0}}-\frac{\gamma_{0,0}}{\gamma_{0,1}}.

\sigma

and azimuth angle
0\leq\phi<2\pi
, where
\Gamma
denotes the non-dimensional diameter or aspect ratio.

In this case, we solve the bulk equation first and substitute the result into the boundary condition. The radial dependence uses modified Bessel functions of the 1st kind,
I_{m}
, with integer wavenumber,
m\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\theta(r,\phi,z)=I_{m}(\beta\,r)\,e^{i(m\phi+\omega t)}\sin(\pi z)+\text{c.c.},

(189)

where
\beta=\sqrt{\pi^{2}+i\,\omega}
.

The dispersion relation follows from the boundary condition applied at the outer radius

\displaystyle R=\pi\beta\left(1+\frac{\pi\Gamma}{2im}\right)\frac{I_{m}^{\prime}(\beta\,\Gamma/2)}{I_{m}(\beta\,\Gamma/2)},

(190)

where
\mathfrak{Im}(R)=0
fixes the critical frequency,
\omega
, given
m
and
\Gamma
. We define
R_{\text{c}}
and
\omega_{\text{c}}
as their respective values when minimising

over integer

5.5
Local baroclinic instability

When assembled in one place, it becomes apparent that the wall-mode system is closely akin to the well-known
planetary geostrophy
(PG) equations, which typically model global-scale stratified ocean dynamics away from coastlines
(
Vallis 2017
, Ch. 5)
. The PG system has rich dynamical structure. However, a common puzzle is the lack of self-consistent forcing and eventual rundown
(
Schonbek & Vallis 1999
)
. Several works have considered coupling to smaller-scale
quasi-geostrophic
(QG) dynamics as an intriguing alternative
(
Grooms et al. 2011
, see)
.

This context clarifies that the wall-mode convection equations are a sideways forced PG-QG system that relies on non-hydrostatic QG
(
Sprague et al. 2006
;
Julien & Knobloch 2007
, similar to)
near lateral boundaries and interacts with a barotropic QG system in the stress-free case. However, we can also study the wall-mode equations without the lateral boundary interactions.

In a highly reduced setting, the combined baroclinic temperature and barotropic vorticity equations have a rudimentary form of baroclinic instability. To demonstrate, we neglect all diffusion and boundary conditions. Hence,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial_{z}\psi+J(\psi,\partial_{z}\psi)=0\qquad\partial_{t}\!\left<\nabla_{\!\bot}^{2}\psi\right>+\!\left<J(\psi,\nabla_{\!\bot}^{2}\psi)\right>=0.

(191)

The baroclinic equation has a family of exact nonlinear solutions,

\displaystyle\psi=P_{0}(z)+x\,V_{0}(z)+(c+V_{0}(z))\,A(y+c\,t)+\text{c.c.},

(192)

where
V_{0}(z)
is an arbitrary
y
-direction velocity profile (without loss of generality,
\!\left<V_{0}\right>=0
),
P_{0}(z)
is and arbitrary hydrostatic pressure,
A(y+c\,t)
is an arbitrary complex-valued amplitude function, and and
c
, is a complex-valued phase speed.

The barotropic equation requires the solvability condition,

\displaystyle\!\left<(c+V_{0})^{2}\right>=0\qquad\implies\qquad c=\pm\,i\,\|V_{0}\|.

(193)

For example,
V_{0}=z-1/2
,
c=\pm i/\sqrt{12}
, or
V_{0}=\cos(\pi z)
,
c=\pm i/\sqrt{2}
. When
A(y)=A_{0}\,e^{iky}
for wavenumebr
k
, then the growth rate is
k\|V_{0}\|
.

6
Nonlinear simulations

Using Dedalus
(
Burns et al. 2020
)
, we simulate the full nonlinear system of reduced wall-mode equations summarised in §
4
. We use a full cylinder with radius
0\leq r\leq\Gamma/2
, azimuth
0\leq\phi<2\pi
and height
0\leq z\leq 1
. We use a spectral basis of generalised Zernike polynomials that naturally represent scalars, vectors and tensors in the full unit disk, including
r=0
with no singularities; see
Vasil et al. 2016
for details.

6.1
Setup

We use the same non-dimensional form as the asymptotic analysis with control parameters
R
,
\sigma
and
\Gamma
. We use
p(r,\phi,z)
as the primary dynamical variable for the bulk. The respective thermal wind relations follow,

\displaystyle\Theta=\partial_{z}p,\qquad u_{\bot}=\mathcal{J}\cdot\nabla_{\!\bot\,}p\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathcal{J}=\hat{\phi}\otimes\hat{r}-\hat{r}\otimes\hat{\phi}.

(194)

We represent
p(r,\phi,z)
in a vertical cosine series,
\cos(n\pi z)
. The temperature perturbations,
\Theta(r,\phi,z)
, are naturally a sine series
\sin(n\pi z)
. For
n\geq 1
, the baroclinic evolution equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Theta-(\nabla_{\!\bot\,}^{2}+\partial_{z}^{2})\Theta=-u_{\bot}\cdot\nabla_{\!\bot\,}\Theta.

(195)

We use a second-order accurate multi-stage, mixed implicit-explicit time-stepping scheme
(
Wang & Ruuth 2008
, SBDF scheme from)
, with linear terms on the left-hand side treated implicitly and the nonlinear right-hand side treated explicitly. The time-step size follows a CFL criterion with a 0.2 safety factor. Because of the fine radial grid spacing near the walls, normal flow through the outer boundary principally determines the time-step size.

For the outer boundary conditions at
r=\Gamma/2
, we use
q_{z}(\phi,z)
as an additional primary variable and define,

\displaystyle q_{\phi}=\mathcal{H}\!\left[q_{z}\right].

(196)

At
r=\Gamma/2
, and for
n\geq 1
, the boundary conditions,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\phi\,}q_{\phi}+\partial_{z}q_{z}-\hat{r}\cdot u_{\bot}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

(197)

\displaystyle R\,q_{z}-\hat{r}\cdot\nabla_{\!\bot\,}\Theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q_{\phi}\nabla_{\!\phi\,}+q_{z}\partial_{z})\Theta,

(198)

where
\nabla_{\!\phi\,}=\hat{\phi}\cdot\nabla_{\!\bot\,}=r^{-1}\partial_{\phi}
. As before, all linear terms are time-implicit and nonlinear terms are explicit.

The
n=0
barotropic pressure mode has different dynamics depending on the velocity boundary conditions on the top and bottom. In both cases, we define the vorticity,
\zeta=\nabla_{\!\bot\,}^{2}p
.

For stress-free, we evolve the
n=0
vorticity equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\!\left<\zeta\right>-\sigma\nabla_{\!\bot\,}^{2}\!\left<\zeta\right>=-\nabla_{\!\bot\,}\cdot\!\left<u_{\bot}\zeta\right>,

(199)

with, again, the usual linear-nonlinear implicit-explicit time splitting. For boundary conditions at
r=\Gamma/2
,

\displaystyle\!\left<p\right>=0,\qquad-2\sigma\,\hat{r}\cdot\nabla_{\!\bot\,}\!\left<p\right>=3\!\left<q_{\phi}\nabla_{\!\phi\,}q_{\phi}\right>-\!\left<q_{\phi}\nabla_{\!\phi\,}p\right>.

(200)

For no-slip, the
n=0
mode involves a bit of subtlety. The Ekman boundary condition directly mixes barotropic and baroclinic variables, and parity mixes sine and cosine series.
Vasil et al. 2008a
;
Vasil et al. 2008b
discusses issues of this kind in detail. The problem is that we need to represent expressions like (
117
) spectrally using sine/cosine series. We fix the barotropic pressure via

\displaystyle\nabla_{\!\bot\,}^{2}\!\left<p\right>=\nabla_{\!\bot\,}^{2}\!\left<K_{\!N}\Theta\right>,

(201)

with the boundary condition
\!\left<p\right>=0
at
r=\Gamma/2
.
We use the finite spectral representation of
1/2-z
,

\displaystyle K_{\!N}(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{N/2}\frac{\sin(2\pi nz)}{n\pi}\quad\to\quad\frac{1}{2}-z\qquad\text{as}\qquad N\to\infty.

(202)

A sufficiently large resolution parameter,
N
, captures the integral exactly. That is,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\!K_{N}(z)\,\sin(n\pi z)\,\text{d}{z}=\int_{0}^{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}-z\right)\,\sin(n\pi z)\,\text{d}{z}\quad\textit{(exactly)}

(203)

for all
n\leq N
. The advantage is that
K_{N}
is a natural sine series having a natural inner product with
\Theta
.

7
Discussion and Conclusions

This work derives a balanced set of reduced equations governing the nonlinear development of convective wall-mode instabilities in rapidly rotating systems. Focusing on the essential dynamics within the bulk interior and the Stewartson boundary layers at the sidewalls captures the multiscale nature of wall-mode convection. The dynamics of the bulk interior diagnostically determine the small-scale behaviour within the boundary layers. In contrast, the sidewall layers feedback onto the interior through a nonlinear lateral heat-flux boundary condition, providing a closed and self-consistent system. The final reduced dynamics occupies the bulk interior and comprises geometrically prescribed scales. All boundary layers exist behind the scenes, their presence felt via a series of nontrivial boundary conditions that include nonlinear transport and non-local integral transforms.

The bulk-boundary connection offers a clearer understanding of the interplay between multiscale dynamics, often obscured in full models due to their complexity. Systematically eliminating lower-order effects isolates the dominant physical processes, enhancing explanatory power and providing avenues for investigating wall-mode convection in the strongly nonlinear regime. A key difference between our studies and previous high-resolution wall-mode simulations is that we do not find any evidence for secondary instability of the sharp propagating thermal structures. We do not know if this is because we have not reached high enough supercriticality or because the equations filter baroclinic inertial dynamics. We imagine future work methodically reintroducing dynamic ingredients that model various aspects of vortex separation, which can populate the interior with pseudo-convective structures. The first additional effect worth including is the
\mathcal{O}(\textit{E}^{\,1/6})
Ekman flux correction in (
165
).

We also reiterate that the final system of equations resembles boundary-forced planetary geostrophic baroclinic dynamics coupled with barotropic quasi-geostrophic vorticity. These connections point to ways systems can include more general effects, such as container topography. We also expect to include centrifugal buoyancy effects in future studies. We have also not considered the effect of a low Prandtl number, which would much more strongly emphasise inertia. Furthermore, these equations can incorporate additional physical effects, as described below.

7.1
Double diffusion

Given the simple physical principles involved, we can, in one way, generalise the thermal wall-mode instability almost by inspection. In the case of thermal-solutal buoyancy, we simply add an equation for the “salt” concentration,
s(t,x,y,z)
. The updated hydrostatic balance,

\displaystyle\theta-s=\partial_{z}p.

(205)

The relevant linear bulk equation and boundary condition,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}s=\textit{Le}^{-1}\nabla^{2}s,\qquad\text{with}\qquad-q_{z}R_{S}=\textit{Le}^{-1}\partial_{x}s\big|_{x=0}.

(206)

The total set of control parameters includes the solutal scaled Rayleigh number and the Lewis number,

\displaystyle R_{T}=\frac{g\,\alpha_{T}\,\Delta T\,H}{\kappa},\qquad R_{S}=-\frac{g\,\alpha_{S}\,\Delta S\,H}{\kappa},\qquad\textit{Le}=\frac{\kappa}{\lambda},

(207)

where
\lambda
is the solute diffusivity,
\alpha_{S}
is solutal the expansion coefficient, and
\Delta S
is the total imposed concentration jump. As defined,
R_{T}>0
and
R_{S}>0
both typically imply destabilising configurations. For a neutral-density background state,
R_{T}+R_{S}=0
. In many common situations,
\textit{Le}\gg 1
; e.g. for salt and water
\textit{Le}\approx 10^{3}
.

Carrying out a nearly identical analysis to the purely thermal case, the condition for marginal stability is

\displaystyle\frac{R_{T}}{\sqrt{k^{2}+\pi^{2}+i\omega}}+\frac{R_{S}\textit{Le}}{\sqrt{k^{2}+\pi^{2}+i\omega\textit{Le}}}=\frac{\pi(ik+\pi)}{ik},

(208)

where
k
is the
y
-direction Fourier wavenumber and
\omega
is the complex-valued frequency. In this case, the dispersion relation shows similarities and differences with both the fingering and oscillatory regimes of double-diffusive convection
(
Garaud 2018
)
.
We leave the exploration of the critical
R_{T},R_{S}
curve for later work.

7.2
Magnetism

Finally, we point out that magnetic wall modes have attracted significant recent attention
(
Busse 2008
;
Liu et al. 2018
;
Grannan et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
McCormack et al. 2023
;
Teimurazov et al. 2024
)
. There are many ways these dynamics will interact with rapid rotational effects.

In magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), the Chandrasekhar number
(
Chandrasekhar 1961
)
measures the influence of an imposed background magnetic field,

\displaystyle Q=\frac{B_{0}^{2}H^{2}}{\mu_{0}\rho_{0}\nu\eta},

(209)

where
B_{0}
is the vertical field strength,
\mu_{0}
is the permeability,
\rho_{0}
is the average density, and
\eta
is the magnetic diffusivity.
In the large-
Q
limit, several interesting overlaps occur with rapidly rotating parameters that point to extremely rich unexplored dynamics.

In particular, the equivalent MHD sidewall interactions will couple with the rotating version, provided the boundary layers have the same scaling in the same Rayleigh number regime. Indeed,
Busse 2008
shows wall-mode convection exists when

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}\sim Q^{3/4},

\displaystyle\,\text{d}{x}\sim Q^{-1/4},

(210)

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}\sim\textit{Ta}^{-1/2},

\displaystyle\,\text{d}{x}\sim\textit{Ta}^{-1/6}.

(211)

In both cases, the velocity amplitudes in the boundary layers scale
v,w\sim\,\text{d}{x}^{-1}
.
Therefore, when
Q\sim\textit{Ta}^{2/3}
, we expect interesting nontrivial interactions with
\mathcal{O}(1)
bulk dynamics coupled to the sidewall via nonlinear and non-local boundary conditions.

However, even more fascinating complications can occur in the “magnetostrophic” regime
(
Horn & Aurnou 2022
)
. In this case, convection can arise purely in the bulk on
\mathcal{O}(1)
length scales without reference to sidewalls. This regime requires a joint balance

\displaystyle\textit{Ra}\sim Q\sim\textit{Ta}^{1/2}.

(212)

Convection sets in when
Q^{2}\sim 3\textit{Ta}
and
\textit{Ra}\textit{Ta}^{-1/2}\sim 3\sqrt{3}\,\pi^{2}\approx 51.284
, or
R/R_{\text{c}}\approx 1.6
in terms of the wall modes.
The implication is that magnetostrophic convection will interact with the sidewall layers with a lower magnetic field strength than purely sidewall-catalysed magnetoconvection. If the wall modes can drive a kinematic dynamo, this is likely the regime the system would end up in.
