Transferred Q Q -learning
Elynn Y. Chen, Michael I. Jordan, Sai Li

Abstract
We consider Q Q -learning with knowledge transfer, using samples from a target reinforcement learning (RL) task as well as source samples from different but related RL tasks. We propose transfer learning algorithms for both batch and online Q Q -learning with offline source studies. The proposed transferred Q Q -learning algorithm contains a novel re-targeting step which enables vertical information-cascading along multiple steps in an RL task, besides the usual horizontal information-gathering as transfer learning (TL) for supervised learning. We establish first theoretical justifications of TL in RL tasks by showing a faster rate of convergence of the Q Q function estimation in the offline RL transfer, and a lower regret bound in the offline-to-online RL transfer under certain similarity assumptions. Empirical evidences from both synthetic and real datasets are presented to backup the proposed algorithm and our theoretical results.

Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) refers to a large group of statistical learning problems related to data-driven decision making
(
Sutton and
Barto 2018
)
.
RL theory and practice have advanced significantly in recent years and RL has found applications in a wide variety of domains, including robotics, business, healthcare, and education
(
Levine
et al. 2020
;
Schulte
et al. 2014
;
Singla et al. 2021
;
Kolm and
Ritter 2020
)
.
Theoretical guarantees have been established to guide the design of practical algorithms
(
Murphy 2005
;
Azar
et al. 2013
;
Song
et al. 2015
;
Jin
et al. 2018
;
Sidford
et al. 2018
;
Yang and
Wang 2019
;
Shi
et al. 2020
)
.
However, the theory also makes clear that there are serious limitations to existing RL algorithms; in particular the number of samples needed to learn a near-optimal policy with classical algorithms can be prohibitive
(
Azar
et al. 2013
;
Yang and
Wang 2019
)
.
This is problematic, for example, in observational studies, such as the dynamic treatment of a disease, where there are often a limited number of samples available
(
Ertefaie and
Strawderman 2018
;
Luckett et al. 2019
;
Shi
et al. 2020
)
.
One way out of this conundrum is to note that diverse datasets may be collected from different but similar observational studies, simulated environments, or existing offline repositories.
The promises of leveraging related studies from observational, simulated, or offline datasets to accelerate learning of a target RL task motivates us to investigate knowledge transfer in RL.

1.1
Related work

Our work is in the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL) and transfer learning (TL).
The literature on both topics is vast.
Here we review the most relevant work to ours: batch and online
Q
-learning with linear function approximation and TL in RL.

Batch
Q
-learning is an RL algorithm designed for learning a non-stationary policy with one training set of finite-horizon trajectories.
It is widely used in real-world applications such as estimating an optimal dynamic treatment strategy in medical care
(
Murphy 2003
;
Nahum-Shani et al. 2012
;
Chakraborty and
Murphy 2014
;
Schulte
et al. 2014
)
.
The term “batch” is used in this context to emphasize that learning occurs only after the collection of a full training set.
Given a batch of transition data,
Q
-learning estimates the state-value function and finds the optimal action at each stage, with information flowing backward from the final stage
(
Chakraborty
et al. 2010
;
Nahum-Shani et al. 2012
;
Song
et al. 2015
;
Ertefaie et al. 2021
)
.
The one-step
Q
-learning algorithms
(
Sutton and
Barto 2018
)
are closely related to batch
Q
-learning in the sense that they can be viewed as incremental implementations of batch
Q
-learning
(
Boyan 2002
;
Lagoudakis and
Parr 2003
;
Ernst
et al. 2005
;
Antos
et al. 2007
)
.
See more details in
Murphy 2005
and references therein.

A central question in online RL is to address the dilemma between “exploration”
(to generate information about the unknown parameters needed for efficient decision making)
and “exploitation”
(to select the action in an attempt to maximize the expected reward)
(
Lai and
Robbins 1985
)
.
This trade-off is measured quantitatively by the cumulative regret.
Recently, a great amount of research focuses on establishing regret guarantees for different online RL algorithms.
In the setting of episodic tabular MDPs,
Azar
et al. 2017
,
Jin
et al. 2018
, and
Yang and
Wang 2019
establish regret bounds for different
Q
-learning algorithms.
More detailed comparisons with some of these contributions will be
given after presenting the main results as more technical background is introduced.

Transfer for RL tasks has recently been a topic of interest in the machine learning community.

Taylor and
Stone 2009
provides a thorough survey of transfer in reinforcement learning with analysis of each transfer algorithm.

Lazaric 2012
introduces a formalization of the general transfer and proposed a taxonomy to classify transfer approaches among three main dimensions, namely, the setting, the transferred knowledge, and the objective.

Taylor and
Stone 2007
discusses the
representation
transfer problems in which agents have different representations.

Mousavi et al. 2014
considers the
context
transfer problem in which agents have the same environment with the same reward function but different contexts (i.e., different state or action space).
Recent works mainly focus on the empirical applications, including
Mousavi et al. 2014
;
Ammar
et al. 2014
;
Parisotto
et al. 2016
;
Gupta
et al. 2017
;
Barreto et al. 2017
and more.
Theoretical guarantees are lacking even in linear value function settings.

1.2
Main contributions

In this paper, we focus on finite-horizon (or episodic) non-stationary RL tasks with linear function approximation. The state and action spaces are assumed to be the same for the source and target tasks but the reward and
Q
-functions can be different.
A general similarity characterization is defined based on the reward functions for different tasks at each stage.

We propose methods for both batch
Q
-learning with offline transfer and online
Q
-learning with source offline data under such similarity structure. Our approach operates in a backward fashion and can accommodate high-dimensional covariates.
The proposal has two main ingredients.
First, we define pseudo-outcomes for source tasks by “re-targeting” the future states of source samples to the corresponding states of the target model.
Second, we leverage supervised transfer learning methods to estimate the target optimal
Q
-function at each stage based on the target data and pseudo source data.
Our methods achieve information aggregation in two ways.
The first one is called “information gathering”, where at each stage we borrow information across tasks.
The second one is called “information cascading,” which means that, in this backward algorithm, the information from the future stages in the related tasks are leveraged in the estimation of the current stage.
We demonstrate theoretically and empirically that our proposals can improve the estimation accuracy of the optimal
Q
-functions for offline RL tasks under mild assumptions and hence more efficient policies can be derived with knowledge transfer.
For online RL tasks, TL can improve the accumulated expected reward by shortening the exploration phases.
To our best of our knowledge, this is the first theoretical analysis of transferred reinforcement learning.
Significant advantages are further demonstrated through empirical studies.

Model

2.1
Preliminaries

The mathematical model of an episodic RL task is a finite-horizon Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined as a tuple
{\cal M}=\left\{{\cal X},{\cal A},\Pr,r,\gamma,T\right\}
where
{\cal X}
is the state space,
{\cal A}
is the action space,
r
is the reward function,
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor, and
T
is the finite horizon.
At time
t
, for the
i
-th individual, an agent observes the current system state
\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}\in{\cal X}
, chooses a decision
a_{t,i}\in{\cal A}
, and transit to the next state
\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1,i}
according to the system transition probability
\Pr\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1,i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i}\right)
.
At the same time, she receives an immediate reward
r_{t,i}
, which serves as a partial signal of the goodness of her action
a_{t,i}
.
The reward function
r(\cdot)
is the expected reward at stage
t
for the observation
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i})
, that is,
r(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i})=\mathbb{E}[r_{t,i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i}]
.

Considering a finite action space
{\cal A}
, an agent’s decision-making rule is characterized by a policy function
\pi\left(a_{t,i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}\right)
that maps the covariate space
{\cal X}
to probability mass functions on the action space
{\cal A}
.
For each step
t\in[T]
and a policy
\pi
, the
state-value function
is the expectation of the cumulative discounted reward starting from a state
\boldsymbol{x}
at
t
-th step:

V^{\pi}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{s=t}^{T}\gamma^{s-t}r(\boldsymbol{x}_{s,i},a_{s,i})\bigg|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}=\boldsymbol{x}\right].

(1)

Accordingly, the
action-value function
or
Q
-function
of a given policy
\pi
at step
t
is the expectation of the accumulated discounted reward starting from a state
\boldsymbol{x}
and taking action
a
:

Q^{\pi}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},a)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum_{s=t}^{T}\gamma^{s-t}r(\boldsymbol{x}_{s,i},a_{s,i})\bigg|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}=\boldsymbol{x},a_{t,i}=a\right].

(2)

The expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}
is taken under the trajectory distribution, assuming that the dynamic system follows the given policy
\pi
afterwards.
For any given action-value function
Q_{t}^{\pi}:{\cal X}\times{\cal A}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, the
greedy policy

\pi^{Q}_{t}
is defined as,

\pi^{Q}_{t}\left(a|\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a=\arg\underset{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{\max}\;Q^{\pi}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{x},a^{\prime}\right),\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(3)

The overall goal of RL is to learn an optimal policy,
\pi^{*}_{t}
,
t\in[T]
, that maximizes the discounted accumulative reward.
To characterize optimality, we define the optimal action value function
Q^{*}_{t}
as

Q^{*}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{x},a\right)=\underset{\pi\in\Pi}{\sup}\;Q^{\pi}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{x},a\right),\quad\forall\left(\boldsymbol{x},a\right)\in{\cal X}\times{\cal A},

(4)

where the supremum is taken over a support
\Pi
.
The optimal policy
\pi^{*}_{t}
can be derived as any policy that is greedy with respect to
Q^{*}_{t}
.
The
Bellman optimal equation
holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[r_{t,i}+\gamma\;\underset{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{\max}Q^{*}_{t+1}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1,i},a^{\prime}\right)-Q^{*}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i}\right)\bigg|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i}\right]=0

(5)

The
Q
-learning algorithms estimate optimal
Q^{*}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},a)
directly and then obtain optimal
\pi^{*}
as the greedy policy (
3
) according to
Q^{*}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},a)
.

To fix ideas, we consider a finite-horizon MDP with a finite action set
{\cal A}=\{-1,1\}
and assume a linear optimal
Q
-function for the target RL task:

Q^{*}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},a):=Q(\boldsymbol{x},a;\;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}&a\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},~~\text{for}~~t\in[T],

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\equiv\left\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{t}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}^{\top}\right\}^{\top}
,
with the convention that
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T+1}=0
and hence
Q(\boldsymbol{x},a;\;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T+1})=0
.
Linear
Q
-functions have been widely used in literature of dynamic treatment regime
(
Song
et al. 2015
;
Luckett et al. 2019
)
and machine learning
(
Yang and
Wang 2019
;
Jin
et al. 2020
)
.
For example, the
Q
-function of a linear MDP is a linear function of features defined on
\boldsymbol{x}
and
a

(
Yang and
Wang 2019
;
Jin
et al. 2020
;
Hao
et al. 2021
)
.
In different contexts,
\boldsymbol{x}
can be high-dimensional, such as the feature vector constructed by basis functions
(
Sutton and
Barto 2018
;
Luckett et al. 2019
;
Jin
et al. 2020
)
as an approximation to nonparametric
Q
-functions.

The target RL task of interest is referred to as the
0
-th task and written with a superscript “
{(0)}
”.
We denote the underlying response of the
Q
-function at step
t
is

y^{(0)}_{t,i}:=r^{(0)}_{t,i}+\gamma\cdot\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1,i}^{(0)},\;a;\;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}).

(7)

According to (
5
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[y^{(0)}_{t,i}-Q\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}_{t,i},a^{(0)}_{t,i};\;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\right)\bigg|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(0)},a_{t,i}^{(0)}\right]=0,

(8)

which provides a moment condition for the estimation of
the target parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
.
If
y^{(0)}_{t,i}
is directly observable, then
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
can be solved simply by regression.
However, what we observe is a “partial outcome”
r^{(0)}_{t,i}
.
The other component of
y^{(0)}_{t,i}
, as shown in the second term on the RHS of (
7
), depends on the unknown
Q
-function and future observations.
For a single RL task, the batch
Q
-learning calculates “pseudo responses”
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{y}^{(0)}_{t}
by

\widehat{y}^{(0)}_{t,i}:=r^{(0)}_{t,i}+\gamma\cdot\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}_{t+1,i},a;\;\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}),

(9)

where
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
is estimated backwardly by regression with
“pseudo samples”

\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(0)},a_{t,i}^{(0)},\widehat{y}_{t+1,i}^{(0)}\}
from
t=T
to
1
since the last-step response
y_{T,i}^{(0)}
is directly observed.

2.2
Similarity characterization on reward funcitons

Our goal is to leverage data from similar source RL tasks to accelerate learning of a target RL task.
The source data can be offline observational data or simulated data.
The target task can be offline or online RL task.
We consider
K
source tasks. The random trajectories for the
k
-th task are generated from the MDP
{\cal M}^{(k)}=\left\{{\cal X},{\cal A},\Pr^{(k)},r^{(k)},\gamma,T\right\}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume the horizon length of all tasks are the same
T
for a clear representation.
For each task
0\leq k\leq K
, we collect
n_{k}
trajectories of length
T
, that is
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)},a_{t,i}^{(k)},r_{t,i}^{(k)}\}_{t\in[T],i\in[n_{k}]}
.
We also assume that the trajectories in different tasks are independent and
n_{k}
does not depend on stage
t
, i.e., none of the tasks have missing data.

For transferred
Q
-learning, we leverage the similarity of the rewards for the target and source tasks at each stage.
Specifically, we parameterize the difference between the reward functions of the target and the
k
-th source task via

\mathbb{E}\left[r_{t}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i})-r_{t}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i},a_{t,i})\big|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}=\boldsymbol{x},a_{t,i}=a\right]=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}&a\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}^{(k)},

(10)

for
t\in[T]
and
k\in[K]
.
The task similarity is characterized by the
\ell_{1}
-sparsity of
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}_{t}
, that is

\max_{t\in[T],k\in[K]}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}^{(k)}\|_{1}\leq h\ll p.

(11)

The similarity metric is measured by the discrepancy between reward functions
(
Mehta
et al. 2008
;
Taylor and
Stone 2009
)
, which leads to a difference between Q functions.

Lazaric 2012
and
Mousavi et al. 2014
define settings when the optimal
Q
-functions are the same between different MDP’s and develop transfer algorithms accordingly.
Our definition of similarity between RL tasks is a generalization in that it allows similar but different
Q
-functions.
It is widely applicable to health care, education, and marketing scenarios where people may have slightly different responses (rewards) to the same treatment.

As the rewards for all the stages are directly observable, it is easy to verify when expression (
10
) or (
11
) is violated.

Remark 1

(Connection with the potential outcome framework)
.

The similarity quantification (
10
) has a potential outcome point of view.
Specifically, for a realized state-action pair
(\boldsymbol{x},a)
, the difference of reward values when switching its participation from the
k
-th task to the target task is
\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}~a\cdot\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}_{t}
.
Because one individual only participates one study in the current setting, the “switching” describes unobserved counterfactual facts.
Nevertheless, empowered by the potential outcome framework, we are able to generate some counterfactual estimates using samples from
k
-th study and estimated coefficients of the target study.
A similar concept of re-targeting by changing the population through weighting has been considered in
Kallus 2020
under the setting of counterfactual inference.

3.1

3.1
Method overview

In contrast to the single-task
Q
-learning setting, we construct the pseudo-outcomes for the source tasks and target tasks jointly.
Our idea is to construct pseudo-outcomes for the source tasks by
re-targeting
them to the target model, in order to leverage the similarity between reward functions.
Formally, at any step
t
,
if we know the true value of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
,
then we can design a
re-targeted response

y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}
, using source samples, as

y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}:=r^{(k)}_{t,i}+\gamma\cdot\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1,i}^{(k)},a;\;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}),

(12)

where notably
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
is not the true parameter of the
k
-th task but that of the target task. Indeed,
y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}
is generated by targeting its future state to the future state of the target model. As a consequence, the model discrepancy between the pseudo-outcomes
\widehat{y}^{(0)}_{t,i}
and
y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}
only comes from the discrepancy between their reward functions at stage
t
.

Combining equations (
6
), (
10
), and (
12
), we have the following moment equations

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[y^{(0)}_{t,i}~\big|~\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}=\boldsymbol{x},a_{t,i}=a\right]=\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\;\;a\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right]\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[y^{(tl-k)}_{t}~\big|~\boldsymbol{x}^{(k)}_{t,i}=\boldsymbol{x},a^{(k)}_{t,i}=a\right]=\left[\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\;\;a\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\right](\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k)}_{t}).

(14)

This motivates a backward fitting algorithm based on the
re-targeted pseudo response

\widehat{y}^{(tl-k)}_{t}
, which is

\widehat{y}^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}:=r^{(k)}_{t,i}+\gamma\cdot\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1,i}^{(k)},a;\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}),

(15)

where
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
is an estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
for the purpose of re-targeting samples from
k
-th RL task using estimated
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}
,
t<s\leq T
.

In Figure
1
, we illustrate the pipeline of our proposed transferred
Q
-learning in comparison to the conventional single-task
Q
-learning. We see that our proposal achieves information aggregation in two ways.
First, when estimating
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
at stage
t
, we borrow information from the
t
-th stage of source tasks, i.e., the information in
\{\boldsymbol{r}_{t,i}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.
This notion is referred to “information gathering” in the setting of transfer learning for supervised regression.
Second, the estimated response for the target model,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t,i}^{(0)}
, is constructed based on the transfer learning estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}
. In this way, we have a positive cascading effect: more accurate estimation of
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{s}
, for
t<s\leq T
, will give a more accurate estimate of the second term in (
7
), and further benefits the estimation of
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
in later steps.
As a result, the current-step estimator
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
benefits as well since the current response estimation
\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t,i}^{(0)}
has smaller error than its single task counterpart.
We call this “information cascading” which is unique to the RL transfer learning.

Illustration of single-task
Q
-learning (top) and transferred
Q
-learning (bottom).
Single-task methods only use data in blue blocks and do not contain the
k
-th source data in orange blocks. The re-targeting step is highlighted in red.

3.2
Offline stage-to-stage transfer

To leverage the source data for offline RL tasks, we propose a transferred
Q
-learning algorithm, which is based on a commonly-used backward recursive approach in both machine learning and dynamic treatment regimes.
The algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
, where
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times p}
denote the design matrix for the
k
-th task at stage
t
whose
i
-th row is
(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top}

and
\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^{(k)}
is a
{n_{k}\times(2p)}
matrix whose
i
-th row is
((\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top},a_{t,i}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top})
.

Now we give a more detailed explanation of the algorithm (see Algorithm 1). First, starting from the final stage
T
, we have full observations of
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{T,i}^{(k)},a_{T,i}^{(k)},y_{T,i}^{(k)}\}_{i\in[n_{k}]}
for each task and hence supervised TL algorithms can be directly applied to obtain the target parameter
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}
.
Then, moving backwardly to stage
t=T-1,\cdots,1
, we calculate the pseudo response
y_{t,i}^{(0)}
in (
9
) using
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}
, which estimates the full observations for the
Q
-function at stage
t
for the target task.
To borrow information from the source data, we calculate the re-targeted pseudo response
y_{t,i}^{(tl-k)}
in (
15
) using
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}
, which estimates the full observations for each source task.
Hence any chosen supervised TL algorithm can be applied to the target pseudo data
y_{t,i}^{(0)}
, together with the source pseudo data
y_{t,i}^{(tl-k)}
,

, to obtain the target parameter
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
.
Here, we employ the Trans-Lasso algorithm
(

)
– a representation of the transferred linear regression method – for its simplicity and rate optimal properties.

\{\boldsymbol{X}^{(0)}_{t},\boldsymbol{a}^{(0)}_{t},\boldsymbol{r}_{t}^{(0)}\}_{t\in[T]}
, source data
\{\{\boldsymbol{X}_{t}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{a}^{(k)}_{t},\boldsymbol{r}_{t}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}\}_{t\in[T]}
, and discount factor
\gamma
.

Output :

\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
.

1

Initialization:
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T+1}=0
.

2

for

t=T,\dots,1

do

3

4

Create pseudo-outcome which promotes similarity:
\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(tl-k)}_{t}=\boldsymbol{r}_{t}^{(k)}+\gamma(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{t+1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}+|\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{t+1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t+1}|)
,
k=1,\dots,K
;

\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(0)}_{t}=\boldsymbol{r}_{t}^{(0)}+\gamma(\boldsymbol{X}^{(0)}_{t+1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{t+1}+|\boldsymbol{X}^{(0)}_{t+1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t+1}|)
.

5

Apply the transfer learning algorithm for supervised regression:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t}^{(tl-k)}-\boldsymbol{W}_{t}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\mathrm{src}}\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{1}\right\}.

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t}^{(0)}-\boldsymbol{W}^{(0)}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\delta}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{0}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{1}\right\}.

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{t}
such that

.

\widehat{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}
such that

.

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}=\check{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}+\check{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{t}
.

\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
benefits as well since the current response estimation
\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t,i}^{(0)}
has smaller error than its single task counterpart.
Second, when estimating
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}
, we apply a transfer learning algorithm for supervised regression, which also borrows information from
t
-th stage of source studies, i.e., the information in
\{\boldsymbol{r}_{t,i}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
.

The second step of Algorithm
1
is closely related to transfer learning in supervised regressions. For example,
Lounici et al. 2009
considered transfers across multiple high-dimensional sparse models under an assumption that the support of all the regression coefficients are the same.
Bastani 2021
studied estimation and prediction in high-dimensional linear models with one informative source study where the sample size of the source study is larger than the number of covariates.
Li et al. 2020a
proposes a method for high-dimensional linear regression based on transfer learning with minimax optimality. Our proposal originates from
Li et al. 2020a
and further conquers the unique challenges in
Q
-learning. In fact, other supervised TL methods can be leveraged here.
For example,
Tripuraneni
et al. 2020
proposes a transfer learning method assuming a low-rank structure of the coefficient vectors for different tasks. And this method can also be used in Algorithm
1
at each stage with estimated data
(\boldsymbol{X}^{(k)}_{t},\boldsymbol{a}_{t}^{(k)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{t}^{(tl-k)})
. The last thresholding step in line 6 of the Algorithm
1
produces sparse estimators without inflating estimation errors.
The sparse estimators,
\check{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}
and
\check{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{t}
have desirable prediction errors for general sub-Gaussian designs, which lead to desired convergence rate. The multi-stage
Q
-learning has complex dependencies across stages and they make the restricted eigenvalue conditions suspicious without thresholding.

As a summary, transferred
Q
-learning differs from transferred supervised learning in the following two aspects.
First,
Q
-learning is a multi-stage model and for the stage
t
for
t<T
, we only have partial observations
\boldsymbol{r}^{(k)}_{t,i}
and the estimated full responses
\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}
contain measurement errors.
The proposed transferred
Q
-learning enables the benefit of transfer to be shared across stages.
Second, the estimated full responses
\widehat{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}
is nonlinear in the estimated parameters
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}
. Our proposal generalizes the existing Trans-Lasso to deal with proximal responses rather than the direct observations of responses.

In this subsection, we introduce the offline-to-online transferred

In this context, transfer learning can provide an efficient way to leverage the offline data, including demonstrations for the target task, suboptimal policies and demonstrations for the source tasks.

Algorithm 2

Algorithm

Lastly in the exploitation phase, it conducts greedy action to maximize the estimated

4
Theoretical results

In this section, we provide rigorous error and regret analyses of both offline and online transferred
Q
-learning under the similarity characterization (
11
).
Under such similarity, the parameter space we consider is

\Omega(s,h)=\left\{(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t},\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(1)}_{t},\dots,\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(K)}_{t}),t\in[T]:~\max_{t\in[T]}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|_{0}\leq s,\max_{t\in[T],k\in[K]}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{t}^{(k)}\|_{1}\leq h\right\}.

We write
N_{\mathrm{src}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}
as the total number of trajectories from the source tasks.

Assumption 1

(Conditions on the design)
.

For
k=0,\dots,K
and
t=1,\dots,T
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)}
are independent sub-Gaussian with mean zero.
For

(\boldsymbol{w}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top}=[(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top},a_{t,i}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top}]
,
the covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{t}=\mathbbm{E}[\boldsymbol{w}_{t,i}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{w}_{t,i}^{(k)})^{\top}]
is positive definite with bounded eigenvalues,
t=1,\dots,T
,
k=1,\dots,K
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[T]}\max_{k\in[K]}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(0)}_{t})^{-1}-I_{2p}\right\rVert_{\infty,1}\leq C_{\Sigma}<1.

(16)

Assumption 2

(Conditions on the noises)
.

For
y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}
defined in (
12
), the residuals
y^{(0)}_{t,i}-\mathbb{E}[y^{(0)}_{t,i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(0)},a_{t,i}^{(0)}]
and
y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}-\mathbb{E}[y^{(tl-k)}_{t,i}|\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)},a_{t,i}^{(k)}]
,
k\in[K]
, are independent sub-Gaussian random variables.

Assumption
1
requires sub-Gaussian designs for all the tasks. The positive definiteness of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{t}^{(k)}
assumes regularity of the covariance of
\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(k)}
but also requires
a_{t,i}^{(k)}
has constant variance across samples.
Moreover, we require in (
16
) that the covariance matrices for the source and target tasks are sufficiently similar. Assumption
2
requires sub-Gaussian noises in the observed reward at each stage.

4.1
Convergence rate of Algorithm
1

We provide theoretical guarantees for the offline stage-to-stage transfer in this subsection.
Theorem
1
establishes the convergence rate for
\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
in this setting.

Theorem 1

(Convergence rate of Algorithm
1
)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold and
\log p\leq c_{0}n_{0}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
.
We take the tuning parameters to be

\lambda_{\mathrm{src}}=c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}}}+c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{h}{s}}\left(\frac{\log p}{n_{0}}\right)^{1/4}~\text{and}~\lambda_{0}=c_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n_{0}}}.

Under the sample size condition that
s\sqrt{\log p/N_{\mathrm{src}}}+h+sh\sqrt{\log p/n_{0}}\leq C
, for any
t=1,\dots,T
, we have

\frac{1}{n_{0}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}^{(0)}_{t}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\vee\left\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{s\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}}+h\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n_{0}}}.

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\log p)
.

Theorem
1
establishes the estimation accuracy of the target
Q
-function at each stage.
The rate for the final stage (
t=T
) is the minimax optimal rate in supervised regression with transfer learning
(
Li
et al. 2020b
)
since the final-stage reward equals the value of the state-action pair.
For the
t
-th stage (
t<T
), the estimation error from
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}
accumulates, as a consequence of using pseudo-outcomes.
Thus, empirically one may expect that the estimation errors become larger in the earlier stages, even though the convergence rate of each stage are the same.
In the upper bound, the sparsity
s
of the target parameter is weighted with
1/N_{\mathrm{src}}
and the maximum sparsity
h
of the contrast vectors is weighted with
1/\sqrt{n_{0}}
.
This shows the improvement of transfer learning, where the
s
-sparse component is learned based on all the studies but the task-specific contrast vectors can only be identified based on the target study with
n_{0}
samples. When the similarity is sufficiently high, then
h\ll s
and transfer learning can lead to improvements in this case. In the theorem, we our choice of
\lambda_{\mathrm{src}}
depends on the unknown
h/s
, which is for a convenient proof of desirable convergence rate. When
h/s\ll\sqrt{n_{0}\log p}/N_{\mathrm{src}}
,
\lambda_{\mathrm{src}}\asymp\sqrt{\log p/N_{\mathrm{src}}}
. In practice,
\lambda_{\mathrm{src}}
can be chosen by cross-validation.

Remark 2

(Convergence rate of single-task
Q
-learning)
.

Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{(st)}
,
t\in[T]
, be the single-task
Q
-learning estimator studied in Song et al. (2015). For any
t=1,\dots,T
,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}^{(st)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(st)}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{s\log p}{n_{0}}.

For the final stage, the transfer learning estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}
has faster convergence rate than the single-task estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}^{(st)}
as long as
N_{\mathrm{src}}\gg n_{0}
and
h\ll s\sqrt{\log p/n_{0}}
. Furthermore, the convergence rate of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{T}
is minimax optimal in the parameter space of interest.
For the
t
-th stage for
t<T
, the gain of transfer learning has two aspects. The first is the error inherited from the next stage,
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}
, which is smaller than its single-task counterpart
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}^{(st)}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}
. The second gain comes from aggregating the data at the current stage.

4.2
Regret bound of offline-to-online transferred
Q
-learning

In this subsection, we provide theoretical guarantees for the online Algorithm
2
with knowledge transferred from offline data.
In the online setting, the learner aims to minimize the cumulative regret that measures the expected loss of following the estimated optimal policy instead of the oracle optimal policy.
Mathematically, the cumulative regret over
T
episodes is defined as

{\rm Regret}_{NT}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma^{t}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{E}[r_{t,i}^{(0)}|\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}_{t,i},a^{*}_{t,i}]-\mathbb{E}[r_{t,i}^{(0)}|\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}_{t,i},\widehat{a}^{(0)}_{t,i}]\right),

(17)

where the estimated optimal policy is
\widehat{a}_{t,i}^{(0)}=\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}}Q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(0)},a^{\prime};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})

and the oracle optimal policy is
a^{*}_{t,i}=\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}}Q(\boldsymbol{x}_{t,i}^{(0)},a^{\prime};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t})
.
Under the linear
Q
-function (
6
), they can be further simplified to

\widehat{a}_{t,i}^{(0)}=sgn((\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}_{t,i})^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{t}),\quad\text{and}\quad a^{*}_{t,i}=sgn((\boldsymbol{x}^{(0)}_{t,i})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{t}),

where
sgn(\cdot)
is the sign function.
The regret bound of online learning with offline transfer is given in Theorem
2
.

Theorem 2

(Cumulative regret of Algorithm
2
after
T
rounds)
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
-
2
hold and
s\sqrt{\log p/N_{\mathrm{src}}}+h\leq C
,
n_{e}\gtrsim s^{2}h^{2}\log p+\log p
.
We take tuning parameters to be

\lambda_{\mathrm{src}}=\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{h}{s}}\left(\frac{\log p}{n_{e}}\right)^{1/4}~~\text{and}~~\lambda_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n_{e}}}.

For any
N>n_{e}
,

\displaystyle{\rm Regret}_{NT}\lesssim\frac{n_{e}\gamma}{1-\gamma}+(N-n_{e})\left(\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}}}+h^{1/2}\left(\frac{\log p}{n_{e}}\right)^{1/4}\right)

(18)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log p)
.

We now find the optimal choice of
n_{e}
, which minimizes the RHS of (
18
). To simplify the analysis, we parameterize
h=N_{\mathrm{src}}^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\geq 0
.
In the supplementary files, we show that if we take

\displaystyle n_{e}\asymp\max\left\{\frac{N^{4/5}(\log p)^{1/5}}{N_{\mathrm{src}}^{2\alpha/5}},\frac{s^{2}\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}^{2\alpha}}\right\},

then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log p)

{\rm Regret}_{NT}\lesssim\max\left\{\frac{N^{4/5}(\log p)^{1/5}}{N_{\mathrm{src}}^{2\alpha/5}},\frac{s^{2}\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}^{2\alpha}}\right\}+N\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{N_{\mathrm{src}}}}.

(19)

We see that larger the
N_{\mathrm{src}}
, i.e., more source data, smaller the cumulative regret; and the larger the value of
\alpha
, i.e., higher the similarity, smaller the cumulative regret.

Remark 3

(Cumulative regret of single-task
Q
-learning)
.

Without offline data, we denote the estimated optimal policy by
\widehat{a}^{(st)}
. It is easy to show that for
n_{e}\gg(s\log p)^{2}
, then with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log p)
,

\displaystyle{\rm Regret}_{NT}^{(st)}\lesssim\frac{n_{e}\gamma}{1-\gamma}+(N-n_{e})\sqrt{\frac{s\log p}{n_{e}}}.

(20)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log p)
. If we take
n_{e}\asymp N^{2/3}(s\log p)^{1/3}\vee(s\log p)^{2}
, then

\displaystyle{\rm Regret}_{NT}^{(st)}\lesssim N^{2/3}(s\log p)^{1/3}+(s\log p)^{2}

(21)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log p)
.

Comparing (
18
) with (
20
), we see that the cumulative regret of transferred
Q
-learning is always smaller when
h=o(s\sqrt{\log p/n_{e}})
.
Comparing (
19
) with (
21
), we see that as long as
N_{\mathrm{src}}>N^{1/(3\alpha)}+N^{2/3}
, the regret of transfer learning policy is always no larger than the single-task policy. This comparison further implies that if
N
is very large, then the optimal choice of
n_{e}
can be larger than
N_{\mathrm{src}}
. In this case, transfer learning may not have significant improvement and it suffices to consider single-task
Q
-learning.

\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}
and
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}
are updated during the exploitation stage.
This phase-based ETC online

At the first phase, vanilla

-learning initializes

and
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}

\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}

Using the extra generated trajectories at the current phase, the values
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}

\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}

Each patient in the cohort is characterized by a set of 47 variables, including demographics, Elixhauser premorbid status, vital signs, and laboratory values.

In our dataset, the mortality rate is 24.21% for female and 22.71% for male.
For the intermediate rewards, we follow
Prasad et al. 2017

6
Conclusions

In this work, we consider
Q
-learning with knowledge transfer in the form of exploiting more samples from different but similar RL tasks.
The
similarity
between target and source RL tasks is characterized by their corresponding reward functions.
We note a salient feature of RL being its multi-stage learning and, accordingly, design a novel
re-targeting
step to enable vertical information-cascading along multiple steps in a RL task, in addition to the usual horizontal information-gathering in TL for supervised learning.
For both offline and online RL tasks, our proposal demonstrates improvements over single-task
Q
-learning theoretically and empirically.

We conclude this paper by pointing out some interesting future directions.
As the similarity level is always unknown, it is likely that we include some source tasks which are far away from the target task.
To address this concern, one can perform a model selection aggregation step and it guarantees that the aggregated estimator is not much worse than the best performance of the candidate estimators. Aggregation in multi-stage models, especially the online setting, is largely an open problem and requires further investigations.
