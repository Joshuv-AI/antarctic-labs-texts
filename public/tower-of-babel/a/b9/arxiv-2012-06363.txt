Cyclopean Geometry of Binocular Vision


Abstract
The geometry of binocular projection is analyzed, with reference to the primate visual system. In particular, the effects of coordinated eye movements on the retinal images are investigated. An appropriate oculomotor parameterization is defined, and is shown to complement the classical version and vergence angles. The midline horopter is identified, and subsequently used to construct the epipolar geometry of the system. It is shown that the Essential matrix can be obtained by combining the epipoles with the projection of the midline horopter. A local model of the scene is adopted, in which depth is measured relative to a plane containing the fixation point. The binocular disparity field is given a symmetric parameterization, in which the unknown scene-depths determine the location of corresponding image-features. The resulting Cyclopean depth-map can be combined with the estimated oculomotor parameters, to produce a local representation of the scene. The recovery of visual direction and depth from retinal images is discussed, with reference to the relevant psychophysical and neurophysiological literature.

1
Introduction

Information about the
3-d
structure of a scene can be recovered from binocular
image-pairs, owing to the spatial separation of the two viewpoints.
If the projections of corresponding points can be identified in the left and right
images, then in principle, the scene can be reconstructed from the resulting
binocular disparity field. In addition to the image information, the reconstruction
process requires knowledge (or estimation) of the projection parameters; in particular,
the relative orientation of the eyes must be known. If some, or all, of these calibration
parameters remain unknown, then reconstruction process is limited to affine or projective
properties of the scene
[
11
,
23
]
. Hence, in order to facilitate the recovery of
as much information as possible from the disparity field, it would be desirable to keep the
orientation of the eyes fixed in the head. Despite this fact, human vision involves
frequent eye movements, which serve a variety of purposes
[
6
]
.
For example, the eyes may be moved in order to direct the field of view, or to
foveate an object of interest. Eye movements are also used to stabilize the retinal
image with respect to head movements
[
38
]
, and to track moving visual
targets. It would be undesirable to suspend these functions, which are essentially
monocular, during the binocular analysis of a scene.

The geometry of binocular stereopsis is complicated by movements of the eyes, as
described above. However, the two eyes typically move in a coordinated fashion, such
that a single point in the scene is fixated. This can be achieved, in particular,
by vergence eye-movements, which are
driven
by binocular disparity.
[
34
]

These coordinated eye movements benefit steropsis, as they
align the two retinal images at the respective foveas. It follows that the amount
of disparity around the fixation point tends to be reduced, assuming that the scene is
locally smooth. This is important, given the relatively short range of biological
disparity detectors.
[
12
,
33
]

There may also be ethological reasons for the existence of binocular eye-movements,
despite the resulting complication of stereopsis. It has been suggested that the evolution
of binocular vision was motivated by the ability to detect camouflaged prey, by segmentation
in depth, with respect to the background
[
19
]
. Another impetus may have
been the improvement in image quality that can be achieved by combining two views, especially
in nocturnal conditions
[
31
]
. Both of these applications would
benefit from binocular eye movements, which allow the scene to be scanned without moving the
head, and help to register the two images. Because neither application requires geometric
reconstruction of the scene, the disadvantages of moving the eyes are negligible. It
should, however, be noted that stereopsis also exists in animals that do not move their
eyes significantly, such as owls.
[
30
]

It is clear that the binocular vision of humans (and other primates) has evolved beyond
simple tasks such as camouflage-breaking. Psychophysical evidence shows that at least
some geometric properties of a
3-d
layout can be estimated by stereopsis, and that
these estimates can be combined, as the eyes fixate successive points in the visible
scene
[
13
]
. Furthermore, it is clear that most types of eye movement are
binocularly coordinated
[
6
]
.

The combination of eye movements and stereopsis raises important questions about
the oculomotor parameterization, the processing of the binocular disparity field, and the
representation of the scene. These three questions are developed in more
detail below, in sections
1.A
,
1.B

and
1.C
, respectively.

1.A
Oculomotor Parameterization

Firstly, what is the appropriate parameterization of binocular eye movements? This
an important issue, because it determines the complexity of the control problem
that the oculomotor system must solve. In particular, it is desirable to establish
the minimal number of parameters that are compatible with the observed range of
oculomotor behaviour. The combination of two sensors, each of which can rotate in space,
results in a system which has six angular degrees of freedom.
However, if Donder’s Law is obeyed
[
6
]
, then the rotation of each
eye
around
the corresponding line of sight is determined by the
direction
of that
line of sight. This removes one degree of freedom from each eye. Furthermore, binocular fixation
implies co-planarity of the visual axes, which removes one elevation angle from the
parameterization. This leaves three degrees of freedom, which can be conveniently
assigned to the elevation, azimuth and distance of the fixation point. These variables
are most naturally specified in relation to the ‘Cyclopean point’
[
16
]
, which,
in the present work, is defined as the midpoint of the inter-ocular axis.
The trigonometry of this Cyclopean parameterization is defined in section

3
, and its relationship to the classical
version/vergence model
[
18
]
is stated.

1.B
Disparity Processing

The second question that is addressed here concerns the relationship between the orientation
of the eyes, and the structure of the binocular disparity field. The difference of position
between the left and right projections of a given scene point is, by convention, called the
‘absolute disparity’ of the point
[
4
]
. This is the quantity that can be
measured most directly, by disparity-sensitive mechanisms
[
9
]
. It is important
to note that the response of such a mechanism must depend on the orientation of the eyes. Indeed,
for a typical scene, and a typical fixation point, it may be hypothesized that the relative
orientation of the eyes will be the dominant source of absolute disparity.

It is important, for the reasons given above, to establish exactly how the disparity field is
affected by eye movements. The question is approached in section
4
, in which
the horopter of the fixating system is defined; this is the set of scene points that project to
the same location in each image.
[
16
]
The horopter is used in
section
5
to construct the epipolar geometry
[
22
]

of the system, which is effectively parametrized by the version and vergence angles. If a projected
point is identified in one image, then the epipolar constraint restricts the location of the
corresponding point to a
line
in the other image. This important relationship can be
expressed for any configuration of the eyes. In principle, the epipolar geometry could be used
to ‘rectify’ the retinal images, thereby removing the effect of eye movements on the disparity
field. However, this would not be consistent with the observed dependence of early binocular
processing on absolute retinal disparity
[
9
]
. Hence it is desirable to
parameterize the disparity field, with respect to the orientation of the eyes. The epipolar
geometry is the basis of such a parameterization.

1.C
Scene Representation

The two questions described above are part of a third, more general question: How can the
geometric structure of the scene be represented by the visual system? This issue is
complicated by the fact that the early mechanisms of primate binocular vision are sensitive to
a quite limited range of disparities
[
12
,
33
]
. The region of space that can be
resolved in depth depends, consequently, on the relative orientation of the eyes.
Specifically, only those points in Panum’s area (which is centred on the fixation point)
can be fused
[
28
,
4
]
. It follows that any global representation of the
scene must be assembled piecewise, over a series of fixations. It is natural to formulate this
process as the measurement of scene-structure with respect to a reference surface, followed
by an integration of the resulting local models
[
3
]
. The
plane that passes through the fixation point, and that is orthogonal to the Cyclopean visual
direction, is a convenient local model for binocular scene representation, as will be shown
in section
6
.

1.D
Scope and Assumptions

The word ‘Cyclopean’ has, in the present context, several possible meanings. As described in
section
1.A
, the ‘Cyclopean point’ is a notional centre of projection, located
at the midpoint of the inter-ocular axis (cf. Helmholtz
[
16
]
). It is convenient to
use this point as the origin of the binocular coordinate system, although there is a useful alternative,
as will be shown in section
3
. The word ‘Cyclopean’ is used elsewhere in a
more general sense, with reference to visual information that is intrinsically binocular, such
as the ‘edges’ that can be perceived in a random-dot stereogram (cf. Julesz
[
19
]
). The
phrase ‘Cyclopean geometry’, as used here, refers to the fact that the binocular configuration of a
fixating visual system can be parameterized by the state of a
single
eye
(cf. Hering
[
18
]
), as will be explained in section
3
.

It will be assumed, in this paper, that the retinal projections can be described by the usual
pin-hole camera equations, and that these projections are ‘internally calibrated’. This means that the
visual system is able to relate the monocular retinal separation of any two points to the angle between
the corresponding optical rays. It is unlikely that the visual system actually achieves such a Euclidean
representation
[
36
]
. However, the present work is primarily a
description
of binocular
geometry, which need not necessarily correspond to the encoding that is used by the visual system. The
main constructions of this paper, including the horopter and the epipolar geometry, can be obtained
(more naturally) in the projective setting, which does not require retinal calibration
[
11
,
23
]
.
However, the present work is much concerned with the effects of the oculomotor configuration on binocular
vision, and these effects are more readily studied by Euclidean methods.

A distinction should be made between
descriptions
and
models
of binocular vision.
The present work aims to describe binocular geometry in the most convenient way. This
leads to Cyclopean parameterizations of visual direction and binocular disparity. Whether these
parameterizations are actually used by the visual system is a further question
[
20
]
.
In particular, it is not
assumed
here that the Cyclopean representation has any biological
reality. Discussion of the psychophysical and physiological evidence that can be used to make such
claims is confined to section
7
.

It will be assumed here that the left and right visual axes intersect at a point in space. This is
approximately true in practice. In the absence of an intersection, it would be possible to define an
appropriate chord between the left and right visual axes, and to choose a notional fixation point on
this segment. Finally, it is assumed that each eye rotates in accordance with Donders’ law, meaning
that the cyclotorsion of the eyes can be estimated from the gaze direction.
[
6
]

The results that are presented here are related to established approaches in computer vision

[
11
,
23
,
2
,

]
. The derivations however,

2
Projection Model

The notation and coordinate systems used in this paper are described here. Points and vectors will be
shown in bold type, for example
\mathitbf{q}
,
\mathitbf{v}
. The transpose of
\mathitbf{v}
is a row-vector

\mathitbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, and the Euclidean length is
|\mathitbf{v}|
. The notation
(\mathitbf{v})_{3}
will be used to
indicate the third component of the vector
\mathitbf{v}
. Matrices are represented by upper-case letters,
for example
\mathitbf{M}
.

The
3-d
Euclidean coordinates of a point will be distinguished by a bar, e.g.
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
.
Note that the difference of two Euclidean points results in a vector,
e.g.
\mathitbf{v}=\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{p}}
. The homogeneous image-coordinates of points and lines
are written without a bar; for example, a point at retinal location

(x,y)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
is represented by

\mathitbf{q}=(\mu x,\mu y,\mu)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, with
\mu\neq 0
. Note that
the inhomogeneous coordinates can be recovered from
\mathitbf{q}/\mu
. Scalar multiples
of the homogeneous coordinates represent the same image point. For example, if

\mathitbf{p}=(\lambda x,\lambda y,\lambda)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
then
\mathitbf{q}/\mu=\mathitbf{p}/\lambda
; this relationship
will be written as
\mathitbf{p}\sim\mathitbf{q}
.

A line in the image plane has homogeneous coordinates

\mathitbf{n}=(a,b,c)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, such that
\mathitbf{q}
is on
\mathitbf{n}
if
\mathitbf{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{q}=0
. Scalar
multiples represent the same line; if
\mathitbf{m}=(\kappa a,\kappa b,\kappa c)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
, then

\mathitbf{m}\sim\mathitbf{n}
, with
\mathitbf{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{q}=0
, as before. If
\mathitbf{n}
is defined as

\mathitbf{n}=\mathitbf{p}\times\mathitbf{q}
, then
\mathitbf{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{p}=\mathitbf{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{q}=0
; hence

\mathitbf{n}
is the line through the two points. Similarly, if
\mathitbf{q}=\mathitbf{m}\times\mathitbf{n}

then
\mathitbf{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{q}=\mathitbf{n}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{q}=0
; hence
\mathitbf{q}
is the intersection point of the
two lines.

The left and right optical centres are labeled
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
and
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}
, respectively.
The Cyclopean point
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{b}
is fixed halfway between, and it will also be useful to define the
‘baseline’ vector
\mathitbf{b}
;

\displaystyle\mathitbf{b}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}-\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}

(1)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{b}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}+\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}).

(2)

Only the
ratio
of the scene size to the baseline length can be recovered from the images, in the
absence of other information. For this reason it is helpful to define the distance between the two optical
centres as
|\mathitbf{b}|=1
, so that Euclidean coordinates are measured units of interocular separation.
The location of the scene coordinate-system is immaterial, so it will be convenient to put the origin
at the Cyclopean point
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{b}
. The coordinates of the optical centres, with reference to
figure
1
, will be

\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}=-\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2},0,0\bigr)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}=\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2},0,0\bigr)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(3)

The baseline vector (
1
) is therefore parallel to the
\mathitbf{x}
axis, and a perpendicular axis

\mathitbf{z}=(0,0,1)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
will be taken as the head-centric outward direction. These two vectors define

\mathitbf{y}=(0,1,0)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}

so that the axes
\mathitbf{x}

The orientations of the left, right and Cylcopean eyes are expressed by

\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}

\mathitbf{R}_{r}
and

, respectively. A view of the scene is obtained by expressing
each point

relative to an optical centre, and applying the corresponding rotation.
Hence the homogeneous perspective projection into the left image is

\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}\sim\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}(\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}).

(4)

If both eyes are looking straight ahead (with zero cyclo-rotation),

\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}=\mathitbf{R}_{r}=\mathitbf{I}
, then the
projections of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}=(x,y,z)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
can be computed easily; they are

\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}\sim\bigl(x+\tfrac{1}{2},y,z\bigr)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}

and

\mathitbf{q}_{r}\sim\bigl(x-\tfrac{1}{2},y,z\bigr)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
.
The inhomogeneous coordinates of these points are
x_{\ell}=(x+\tfrac{1}{2})\big/z
,

x_{r}=(x-\tfrac{1}{2})\big/z
, and
y_{\ell}=y_{r}=y/z
, corresponding
to the ordinary perspective equations. It is straightforward, in this case, to evaluate the

binocular disparity

(x_{\ell},y_{\ell})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}-(x_{r},y_{r})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}=(1/z,0)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
. Note that,
in this case of parallel visual axes, the disparity vector is confined to the horizontal direction.
For general orientations of the eyes, disparity equations are more complicated, as will be seen in
section
6
.

3
Binocular Orientation

An angular parameterization of binocular eye movements is introduced in this section.
As was noted in section
1.A
, the degrees of freedom of the
binocular system can be reduced from six, to three. The reduction is achieved by imposing
the fixation constraint, together with Donders’ Law. An appropriate parameterization will
be developed, based on the Cyclopean azimuth, elevation and distance of the fixation point.
It will be shown that this representation complements the classical version/vergence
coordinates
[
18
]
.

Suppose that the point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
is to be fixated. The scene coordinates of this
point can be specified by a head-fixed direction
\mathitbf{v}
from the Cyclopean origin,
in conjunction with a distance,
\rho
, along the corresponding ray;

\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}=\rho\mathitbf{v}.

(5)

The direction
\mathitbf{v}
is a unit-vector, and the positive scalar
\rho
will be called the

range
of the fixation point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
. The Cyclopean direction may be written in
terms of the elevation and azimuth angles
\alpha
and
\beta
respectively;

\mathitbf{v}=(\sin\beta,\,-\sin\alpha\,\cos\beta,\,\cos\alpha\,\cos\beta)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}

(6)

where
\cos\beta
is the projected length of
\mathitbf{v}
in the mid-sagittal plane
x=0
, which divides
one side of the head from the other. Note that the
elevation
\alpha
is positive for points above the horizontal plane
(y<0)
, and that the azimuth

\beta
is positive for points to the right of the mid-sagittal plane
(x>0)
. These visual angles will
each be in the range
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, so that any point with
z\geq 0
can be identified, as shown
in figure
1
. If the fixation point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}=(x,y,z)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
is given in
Cartesian coordinates, then the corresponding range and direction are

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=|\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}|

(7)

\displaystyle\mathitbf{v}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}/\rho

(8)

respectively. The elevation and Cyclopean azimuth angles can be obtained from the equations

\tan\alpha=-y/z
and
\sin\beta=x/\rho
respectively. The vector

contains the
Helmholtz coordinates
of the point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}

Although the Helmholtz coordinates are convenient for specifying visual directions, the eyes do not, in
general, rotate around the corresponding axes. One characteristic of actual eye movements is that, for
general fixation points, each eye will be cyclo-rotated around the corresponding visual
direction. Although the cyclo-rotation angles
\gamma_{\ell}
and
\gamma_{r}
are non-zero, Donders’ law
states that they are completely determined by the corresponding visual directions; hence there
exist functions
\gamma_{\ell}(\alpha,\beta_{\ell})

\gamma_{r}(\alpha,\beta_{r})

of these functions can be obtained from, for example, Listing’s law and its extensions.
[

The effects of cyclo-rotation are relevant to the distribution of physiological
disparity mechanisms across the visual field
[
21
]
. However, cyclo-rotation
is of limited interest with respect to the analysis of binocular
geometry

this is because the parameters
\gamma_{\ell}
and
\gamma_{r}
simply determine
rotations of the respective
images
, independently of any

For this reason, the effects of cyclo-rotation will not be incorporated into
the following derivations, meaning that Donders’ law is obeyed in the trivial sense;

\gamma_{\ell}(\alpha,\beta_{\ell})=\gamma_{r}(\alpha,\beta_{r})=0.

(11)

It is, at the end of all derivations, easy to substitute any other example of Donders’
law, as will be explained in sections
4

6

the restriction (
11

\alpha

is removed from the analysis. This makes it possible to study the binocular geometry with
respect to fixation points in a
single
visual plane, without loss of generality.
Furthermore, Listing’s law agrees with (
11
) when
\alpha=0
; hence it is
natural to choose the horizontal plane
\mathcal{V}_{0}
for further investigation.

The properties of the vergence and version parameters can be understood with reference

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\cot\delta

(15)

4
Fixed Points

In this section it will be shown that, for a given vergence angle, certain points in the
scene project to the same location in each image. These points constitute the geometric

horopter
of the fixating system. The constructions that are given here will be used
to construct the epipolar geometry of the two images, in section
5
.
For this purpose, it will be convenient to study the horopter in the absence of any
cyclo-rotation.

It was shown in section
3
that the Vieth-Müller circle is
defined by the optical centres
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
and
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}
, together with
the fixation point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
. Consider another scene-point point,

\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle VM}}
, that lies on the forward section of the
Vieth-Müller circle. This point is in the visual plane
\mathcal{V}_{0}
, and therefore
satisfies the equation
y=0
, as well as the conditions

\begin{split}\begin{gathered}x^{2}+(z-\zeta)^{2}=\eta^{2}\\
z\geq 0.\end{gathered}\end{split}

(16)

The two points
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
and
\mathitbf{\bar{q}}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle VM}}
,
both of which are on the forward section of the Vieth-Müller circle, must inscribe
equal angles at the optical centres. The point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
is being
fixated, and therefore appears in the fovea of each image. Hence the projected
point
\mathitbf{q}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle VM}}
is fixed in the images;
it appears on the horizontal meridian of each retina, at a fixed angular
offset from the corresponding fovea.

The preceding argument has established that all scene points
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle VM}}
are fixed in the images. This means, in camera coordinates, that the equations

x_{\ell}/z_{\ell}=x_{r}/z_{r}
and
y_{\ell}/z_{\ell}=y_{r}/z_{r}
are satisfied. The
existence of points that are fixed in the scene, as well as in the images, will
now be investigated.
Recall that the Euclidean coordinates of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}=(x,y,z)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
in the left and right camera
frames are

\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{\ell}=\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}(\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell})
and

\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{r}=\mathitbf{R}_{r}(\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r})
, respectively.
The point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
is fixed in the scene if
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{\ell}=\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{r}
,
which in turn implies that
|\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{\ell}|^{2}=|\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{r}|^{2}
.
The squared-lengths are preserved by the rotation matrices
\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}
and
\mathitbf{R}_{r}
,
and so
|\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}|^{2}=|\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}|^{2}
.
From the definition of
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
and
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}
in (
3
), this is
equivalent, in scene-coordinates, to
the condition
(x+\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}=(x-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}
. Hence it can be seen
that any fixed point in the scene must lie in the mid-sagittal plane
x=0
, that
divides one side of the head from the other. Substituting
x=0
into
(
16
) leads immediately to
z=\zeta+\eta
, leaving
y
free to vary.
In general,
y_{\ell}=y_{r}
, because the axis of the vergence rotation
is perpendicular to the visual plane
\mathcal{V}_{\alpha}
. This argument has
established that there is an axis of fixed points
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{a}
in the
scene. This axis intersects the Vieth-Müller circle at a point

\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}
, and is perpendicular to the visual plane. If, without
loss of generality,
\alpha=0
, then the coordinates of these points are

\displaystyle\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}

\displaystyle=(0,0,\zeta+\eta)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}

(17)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{a}

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}+(0,y,0)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(18)

This axis of points,
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{a}
, which has been identified elsewhere
[
37
,
8
]
,
is the geometric
midline horopter
. The point
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}
is the
pole

of the planar transformation induced by the translation
\mathitbf{b}
and vergence
rotation
\mathitbf{R}_{r}\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
. The points
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{a}
lie on
the associated screw-axis
[
2
]
.

It will be useful to compute the
image
coordinates of the axis, which are common
to both eyes, as shown in fig.
4
. The points
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
and
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}
inscribe equal angles
at
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
,
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}
and

\bar{\mathitbf{c}}
; moreover, the angle at

\bar{\mathitbf{c}}
is, by definition, the binocular version,

\epsilon
. Having established that the angular direction of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{a}
from
either optical centre is
\epsilon
, the common distance of this point will also be
computed. The points
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
,
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{r}
and

\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}
form an isosceles triangle, from which it
can be seen that
|\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}|\sin(\delta/2)=\frac{1}{2}
.
It follows that, in the coordinates of either eye, the axis is specified by

\displaystyle\mathitbf{c}_{a}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\csc(\delta/2)\,(-\sin\epsilon,0,\cos\epsilon)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}

(19)

\displaystyle\mathitbf{q}_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathitbf{c}_{a}+(0,y,0)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(20)

These image points lie on a vertical line
\mathitbf{a}
, which has the same coordinates in
each eye. The equation of the line is
\mathitbf{q}_{a}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{a}=0
, and so it follows from
equation (
19
) that the coordinates of the line are determined by the
version angle
\epsilon
;

\mathitbf{a}\sim(\cos\epsilon,0,-\sin\epsilon)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(21)

The results of this section can be summarized as follows.
The geometric horopter, in the absence of cyclo-rotation, consists of the forward
part of the Vieth-Müller circle, together with the midline component.
If a scene point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
is on the horopter, then the image
coordinates of the corresponding points are equal,
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}\sim\mathitbf{q}_{r}
.
Furthermore, if the point
is on the midline component, then it is also true that the eye-coordinates of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}

are equal,
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{\ell}=\bar{\mathitbf{q}}_{r}
. Finally, the image coordinates
(
21
) of the midline are determined by the binocular version angle.
It should be noted that, in the presence of cyclo-rotation, the geometric horopter
takes the form of a twisted cubic curve
[
16
]
. This curve coincides
with the Vieth-Müller circle as it passes through the optical centres, and has
asymptotes at
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{a}\pm(0,y,0)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
.

5
Epipolar Geometry

It was established, in the preceding section, that certain scene points have the
same coordinates in both images. The related
epipolar constraint
is weaker,
but much more useful, as it applies to
all
scene points. The epipolar geometry
of the fixating system will now be described; in particular, the image of the midline horopter
(
21
), will be used to construct the appropriate ‘Essential matrix’.
[
22
]

The epipolar constraint is as follows: Given an image point
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
in

\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
, the corresponding point
\mathitbf{q}_{r}
in
\mathcal{I}_{r}

must lie on a known epipolar line
\mathitbf{u}_{r}
, such that

\mathitbf{u}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\!\mathitbf{q}_{r}=0.

The geometric interpretation of this is that the scene point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
must be
located on the ray defined by the optical centre
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
and the image point

\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
; the ray projects to a line
\mathitbf{u}_{r}
in the other view, and so

\mathitbf{q}_{r}
, being another image of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
, must lie on the line.
Furthermore, note that the optical centre
\bar{\mathitbf{c}}_{\ell}
is common to all such
rays, and so the resulting lines
\mathitbf{u}_{r}
must intersect at a single point in

\mathcal{I}_{r}
. This point is the right
epipole
,
\mathitbf{e}_{r}
.
Similar arguments can be used to introduce the left epipole
\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}
, as well
as the associated lines
\mathitbf{u}_{\ell}
in
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
.

Suppose that the point
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
is given; then, with reference to fig.
5
,

\mathitbf{u}_{\ell}=\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}\times\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
. Furthermore, this line intersects
the projection
\mathitbf{a}
of the midline horopter (
21
) at the image point

\mathitbf{q}_{a}=\mathitbf{a}\times\mathitbf{u}_{\ell}
. Any point on
\mathitbf{u}_{\ell}
must also be on

\mathitbf{u}_{r}
, as the two lines are images of the same ray. Hence
\mathitbf{u}_{r}
can be constructed from
\mathitbf{e}_{r}
and the point
in
\mathcal{I}_{r}
that corresponds to
\mathitbf{q}_{a}
. Furthermore,
\mathitbf{q}_{a}
is a
fixed point (being on
\mathitbf{a}
), and so its coordinates are unchanged in
\mathcal{I}_{r}
.
It follows that

\mathitbf{u}_{r}=\mathitbf{e}_{r}\times\mathitbf{q}_{a}
. The preceding construction may be summarized as

\mathitbf{u}_{r}\sim\mathitbf{e}_{r}\times\bigl(\mathitbf{a}\times(\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}\times\mathitbf{q}_{\ell})\bigr).

(22)

This equation will now be put into a more useful form.
Suppose that
\mathitbf{w}=(x,y,z)^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
; then the cross product
\mathitbf{w}\times\mathitbf{p}
can be
expressed as a matrix-vector multiplication,
(\mathitbf{w}\times)\mathitbf{p}
, where

\bigl(\mathitbf{w}\times\bigr)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-z&y\\
z&0&-x\\
-y&x&0\end{pmatrix}

(23)

is a
3\times 3
antisymmetric matrix, constructed from the components of
\mathitbf{w}
.
Consider the part of equation (
22
) that does not depend on the particular
choice of point
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
; the equivalence (
23
) can be used
to express this as a transformation

\mathitbf{E}\sim\bigl(\mathitbf{e}_{r}\times\bigr)\bigl(\mathitbf{a}\times\bigr)\bigl(\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}\times\bigr)

(24)

which is the
3\times 3

Essential matrix
[
22
]
. Given a point
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
, the corresponding point

\mathitbf{q}_{r}
must be on a certain epipolar line
\mathitbf{u}_{r}
, as described above. This constraint is
expressed via the Essential matrix as

\mathitbf{q}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{E}\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}=0

(25)

where

\mathitbf{u}_{r}\sim\mathitbf{E}\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
.
The analogous constraint,

\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{q}_{r}=0

applies in the opposite direction, the epipolar line being

\mathitbf{u}_{\ell}\sim\mathitbf{E}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\!\mathitbf{q}_{r}

in this case.
The epipoles, as described above, are each the image of the ‘other’ camera centre. This means that

\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}\sim\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}(\mathitbf{b})
, and
\mathitbf{e}_{r}\sim\mathitbf{R}_{r}(-\mathitbf{b})
, where

\mathitbf{b}
is the vector between the optical centres. Equations (
1
), (
3
) and (
4
)
can be used to show that the epipoles are simply

\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}\sim(\cos\beta_{\ell},\,0,\,\sin\beta_{\ell})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathitbf{e}_{r}\sim(-\cos\beta_{r},\,0,\,-\sin\beta_{r})^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}.

(26)

These equations can be combined with the definition of the geometric midline horopter
(
21
), to give a parametric structure to the Essential matrix.
The non-zero terms in the matrix product (
24
) are found to be

E_{12}=-E_{r}\sin\beta_{r}
,

E_{21}=E_{\ell}\sin\beta_{\ell}
,

E_{23}=-E_{\ell}\cos\beta_{\ell}
and

E_{22}=E_{r}\cos\beta_{r}
,
where

E_{\ell}=\cos\beta_{r}\cos\epsilon+\sin\beta_{r}\sin\epsilon

and

E_{r}=\cos\beta_{\ell}\cos\epsilon+\sin\beta_{\ell}\sin\epsilon
. The factors
E_{\ell}
and
E_{r}
are seen to be the angle-difference expansions of
\cos(\beta_{\ell}-\epsilon)

and
\cos(\beta_{r}-\epsilon)
, respectively. Furthermore, by reference to (
12
,
13
) the arguments

\beta_{\ell}-\epsilon
and
\beta_{r}-\epsilon
are equal to
\pm\delta/2
, and so it follows
from the even-symmetry of the cosine function that
E_{\ell}=E_{r}=\cos(\delta/2)
.
The Essential matrix is defined here as as a homogeneous transformation
(cf.
25
), and so this common scale-factor can be disregarded, which
leaves

\mathitbf{E}\sim\begin{pmatrix}0&-\sin\beta_{r}&0\\
\sin\beta_{\ell}&0&-\cos\beta_{\ell}\\
0&\cos\beta_{r}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

It is straightforward to verify that
\mathitbf{E}
is indeed an Essential matrix, having one
singular-value equal to zero, and two identical non-zero singular-values (here equal to unity).

. The location of

The same result can be obtained from a variant of the more traditional definition of the essential
matrix,
[

,

\mathitbf{E}\sim\mathitbf{R}_{r}(\mathitbf{b}\times)\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}
. This definition is, however, not specific to the case of visual fixation, and offers correspondingly less
insight into the present configuration.

6
Binocular Disparity

It was established in the preceding section that projected points in the left and right images must obey the epipolar constraint. In particular it was shown that, given a point
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
in
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
, the corresponding point
\mathitbf{q}_{r}
must lie on a line
\mathitbf{u}_{r}\sim\mathitbf{E}\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
in the other image,
\mathcal{I}_{r}
. The structure of the scene displaces the left and right image points along the corresponding lines, which pass through the left and right epipoles, respectively. These image displacements are quantified in this section.
In particular, it is shown that a scene-point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
which is in Cyclopean direction
\mathitbf{p}_{c}
will be projected to corresponding image-points
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
and
\mathitbf{q}_{r}
, where

\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}=\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}+t_{\ell}(s)\,\mathitbf{d}_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathitbf{q}_{r}=\mathitbf{p}_{r}+t_{r}(s)\,\mathitbf{d}_{r}.

(28)

The unit-vectors
\mathitbf{d}_{\ell}
and
\mathitbf{d}_{r}
point towards the respective epipoles
\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}
and

\mathitbf{e}_{r}
. The relation to the epipolar geometry developed in section
5
is that

\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
and
\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}
are on the same epipolar line; hence, in addition to

\mathitbf{q}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{E}\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}=0
, as in
(
25
),
it is true that
\mathitbf{q}_{r}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\mathitbf{E}\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}=0
.
The formulation (
28
) makes an approximate correspondence between points
\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}
and

\mathitbf{p}_{r}
, which is corrected by parallax functions
t_{\ell}(s)
and
t_{r}(s)
. The common parameter

s
is the signed orthogonal distance of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
from the plane
\mathcal{P}
which passes through
the fixation point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
. This representation makes it possible, in principle, to estimate a
‘local’ Cyclopean depth map
S(\mathitbf{p}_{c};\beta,\rho)
at each fixation point, where
\mathitbf{p}_{c}

ranges over the Cyclopean field of view.

The decomposition (
28
) has three important properties. Firstly, the unknown parallax
variables are scalars; there is no need to consider horizontal and vertical disparities separately. Secondly,
each image correspondence is parameterized by a single variable
s
, which has a direct interpretation as a
Euclidean distance in the scene. Thirdly, for points close to the fixation plane, the predictions
\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}

and
\mathitbf{p}_{r}
will be close to
\mathitbf{q}_{\ell}
and
\mathitbf{q}_{r}
respectively. In particular, the predicted
correspondence will be exact if the point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
lies in the fixation plane;
t_{\ell}(0)=t_{r}(0)=0
.
The decomposition (
28
) will now be described in detail.

The fixation plane
\mathcal{P}
, by definition, passes through the fixation point
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}
, and is
perpendicular to the Cyclopean gaze direction. Hence the plane has an outward normal vector
\mathitbf{v}
, as
defined in (
8
). The plane consists of scene points in the set

\mathcal{P}=\Bigl\{\bar{\mathitbf{p}}:\mathitbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(\bar{\mathitbf{p}}-\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0})=0\Bigr\}.

(29)

The orthogonal distance from
\mathcal{P}
to the Cyclopean origin is equal to the range,
\rho
,
of the fixation point, as defined in (
7
). The orthogonal distance from
\mathcal{P}
to
the scene point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
will be
s
; hence

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\mathitbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}

(30)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\mathitbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}(\bar{\mathitbf{q}}-\bar{\mathitbf{p}}_{0}).

(31)

The range,
\rho
, is strictly positive, whereas
s
is negative, positive or zero, according
to whether
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
is closer than, further than, or in the plane
\mathcal{P}
, respectively.
Note that
s
represents the
structure
of the scene with respect to
\mathcal{P}
.
Equations (
30
) and (
31
) can now be used to decompose the Cyclopean depth
z_{c}
of
the point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
as follows;

z_{c}=\mathitbf{v}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\bar{\mathitbf{q}}=\rho+s.

(32)

The Cyclopean ray through
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
intersects the fixation plane at
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}
.
Hence the Cyclopean coordinates of
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
can be expressed as
z_{c}\mathitbf{p}_{c}=\mathitbf{R}\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
,
where
\mathitbf{p}_{c}
has been normalized such that
(\mathitbf{p}_{c})_{3}=1
, and
\mathitbf{R}
encodes the orientation
of the Cyclopean eye (defined by the angle
\beta
, cf. equation
10
). The scene-coordinates of points on the corresponding visual ray, parameterized by
z_{c}
,
can be obtained by inverting this equation. In particular, the intersection
\bar{\mathitbf{p}}
of the ray with
the fixation plane
\mathcal{P}
can be obtained, as can the original scene-point
\bar{\mathitbf{q}}
;

\displaystyle\bar{\mathitbf{p}}

\displaystyle=\rho\mathitbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\!\mathitbf{p}_{c}

(33)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathitbf{q}}

\displaystyle=z_{c}\mathitbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\!\mathitbf{p}_{c}.

(34)

These two points will now be projected into the left image,
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
, and the difference

t_{\ell}(s)\mathitbf{d}_{\ell}
between the two projections will be evaluated. The analogous derivation applies to the other image,
\mathcal{I}_{r}
, with subscripts ‘
\ell
’ and ‘
r
’ exchanged.
The left coordinates are

\displaystyle\rho_{\ell}\mathitbf{p}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\rho\mathitbf{R}_{\ell}\mathitbf{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle\top}\!\mathitbf{p}_{c}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathitbf{e}_{\ell}

7
Discussion

Three problems of binocular vision were identified in section
1
,
concerning oculomotor parameterization, disparity processing, and scene representation.
A unified geometric treatment of these problems has been given in
sections
3
–
6
. The main psychophysical
and physiological findings that relate to each of the proposed geometric solutions will
now be reviewed.

7.A
Oculomotor Parameterization

As described in section
3
, the binocular orientation of the
eyes can be specified, in the visual plane, by the vergence and version angles,
\delta
and

\epsilon
. Furthermore, these variables are convenient for the specification
of a coordinated eye movements from one fixation point to another. It was suggested
by Hering that the oculomotor system actually encodes binocular eye movements in
terms of vergence and version
[
18
,
6
]
. Specifically, Hering’s law
of
equal innervation
states that the eyes are oriented by
signals
(\delta,\epsilon)
where, according to (
12
,
13
),

\beta_{\ell}=\epsilon+\delta/2
and
\beta_{r}=\epsilon-\delta/2
.
Each eye moves according to the sum of the
appropriate vergence and version components (which may cancel; e.g. if

\epsilon+\delta/2=0
, then both the initial and final fixation points
lie along the visual direction
\mathitbf{v}_{\ell}
).
The existence of physiological mechanisms that encode pure vergence has
been demonstrated in the midbrain
[
26
]
, and it has been
suggested that the vergence/version decomposition is used to represent
the difference between the current and target fixation points.
However, the actual trajectories of large binocular eye movements are not consistent
with the simple vergence/version decomposition
[
7
]
. Furthermore,
it has been found that the visual axes can be widely mis-aligned during
rem

sleep
[
40
]
, which seems more consistent with the independent
parameterization of each eye.

It may be that the application of Hering’s law is limited to those situations
in which the existence of a
3-d
fixation point can be ensured by the
foveal correspondence of the images.
This condition would, for example, distinguish the vergence response
from the disconjugate component of a binocular saccade. This is because
vergence is driven by visual feedback
[
34
]
(alignment of the retinal images),
which is not generally available during the course of a binocular saccade.
Likewise, when the eyes are closed, there is no visual information to ensure
the existence of a
3-d
fixation point. In summary, the evidence for Hering’s
law of equal innervation is mixed, but it seems clear that there are situations
in which it is not a satisfactory model.

7.B
Disparity Processing

It is possible to separate the initial estimation of image correspondences from
the
interpretation
of the resulting disparity field; this distinction is
made in several computational models of stereopsis
[
24
,
13
,
25
]
.
It is supposed, in these models, that the correspondence problem is first solved,
independently of the gaze parameters. The latter are then recovered from the
estimated disparity field
[
25
]
, and the two types of information are
combined, leading to a
3-d
(though not necessarily Euclidean) interpretation
of the scene
[
14
]
. This scheme is compatible with the physiological
basis of stereopsis; for example, it has been demonstrated that the initial
binocular mechanisms in area V1 are tuned to absolute disparity
[
9
]
,
as described in section
1.B
. This finding indicates that the
low-level mechanisms of stereopsis do not ‘compensate’ for any disparity that is
imposed by the relative orientation of the eyes.

The biological feasibility of a general solution to the binocular correspondence
problem will now be considered. Individual disparity-tuned cells, in area V1,
typically respond to a small range of absolute disparities, centred on some preferred value.
The distribution of preferred disparities, over the population of cells, can
also be inferred from the experimental data
[
1
]
. This arrangement
suggests the following difficulty: It seems likely that, for any particular scene,
and any particular fixation point, a large proportion of the V1 disparity response
may be spurious. This is because the occurence of the preferred absolute disparity,
for a given detector, effectively depends on the orientation of the eyes, as well
as on the scene structure. Hence the possibility of detecting a ‘false match’ is
exacerbated by the fact that, owing to the variable orientation of the eyes, the
true match may not even be in the range of a given detector.

One way to address this problem is by imposing some prior knowledge about the
typical structure of the scene. For, example, it might be assumed that the
scene is approximately planar in the neighbourhood of the fixation point.
Such a model could then be used to define
sets
of disparity detectors
that are effectively tuned to the same surface in depth. This is done by
computing the image-to-image mapping induced by the surface model, and comparing
it to the disparity response. Note that this process is entirely consistent with
detectors that respond to absolute disparity, as the scene model does not influence
the output of the individual mechanisms. Rather, the local scene model is

imposed
on the collective response.

If this approach is to be effective, then the induced image-to-image mapping
must be appropriately parameterized. It is important to note that an appropriate
parameterization would allow prior knowledge to be imposed on the gaze parameters,
as well as on the scene structure. For example, image-to-image mappings
associated with extreme configurations of the eyes might be penalized, and
some mappings might be excluded altogether (e.g. those associated with
non-intersecting visual axes). It is emphasized that this approach does
not require oculomotor information about the current gaze parameters; rather,
it assumes that a model of gaze-variation can be learned from the image data.
The geometric constraints described in sections
3

and
5
would seem to make this a biologically
feasible task. For example, if the scene is assumed to be approximately
perpendicular to the Cyclopean gaze direction, then the appropriate
scene/gaze model has just three parameters;
(\alpha,\beta,\rho)
.
Furthermore, the form of the induced image-to-image mapping has been
exactly described in section
6
.

7.C
Scene Representation

It was shown in section
6
that binocular disparity can
conveniently be measured with respect to the fixation plane,
\mathcal{P}
. This plane passes
through the fixation point, and is orthogonal to the Cyclopean viewing direction.
This construction is commonly used in physiological studies of stereopsis. In particular,
the shape of the disparity tuning profile, with respect to the depth of the fixation
plane, has been used to classify binocular neurons
[
32
]
. Subsequent
experiments have suggested that there exists a continuum of different disparity
tuning curves
[
33
]
, rather than a number of distinct types.
Nonetheless, the continuum of disparity tuning is clearly organized around the fixation
plane; for example, the majority of cells are tuned to disparities close to that of the
plane
[
10
]
.

The importance of the fixation plane is also reflected in psychophysical studies
of stereopsis. As noted in section
1.C
, only those points
in space that are in Panum’s area can be binocularly fused
[
28
]
. It
has further been demonstrated, using simple stimuli, that stereo acuity is highest
for targets located close to the fixation plane
[
28
,
4
]
.
However, the representation of more complex binocular stimuli raises further
questions. In particular, it has been shown that judgment of the
relative

depth between nearby targets is much more accurate than judgment of the deviation
of a singe target from the fixation plane
[
39
]
. Furthermore, the
surface that is perceived in depth is, in some cases, an
interpolation

of the point-wise disparity stimulus
[
27
]
. These observations
suggest that the representation of binocular disparity may depend on the
local scene-structure, as well as on the gaze parameters
[
15
]
.
The present model is compatible with this approach, and indeed, the fixation
plane
\mathcal{P}(\rho,\mathitbf{v})
can be interpreted as the
zeroth-order approximation of the local scene structure. It is straightforward to
substitute the first-order model
\mathcal{P}(\rho,\mathitbf{v};\theta,\phi)
,
in which the angles
\theta
and
\phi
represent the local surface orientation,
and to repeat the derivation of binocular disparity in section
6
.

The integration of the visual information across the larger scene will now be
considered. The representation described in section
6

allows the estimated viewing distance to be combined directly with the
Cyclopean depth map, because both are measured along the Cyclopean gaze direction,
as in equations (
30
) and (
31
). The global structure of
the scene could therefore be encoded in a collection of local depth-maps, of the type
described above. Although each depth-map would be associated with a different fixation
point, it would be geometrically straightforward to combine the encodings, based on
the corresponding gaze parameters.

Finally, it can be argued that the Cyclopean representation is consistent
with the perception of visual space
[
18
]
. Human binocular
vision results, at least subjectively, in a single view of the scene. If this
synthetic view has a meaningful centre of projection, then it may be hypothesized
that it is located at the Cyclopean point
[
29
]
.

7.D
Conclusions

A Cyclopean parameterization of binocular vision has been developed in detail.
The parameterization has been used to construct the horopter and the epipolar
geometry of a fixating visual system. Furthermore, the effect of the oculomotor
parameters on the binocular disparity field has been described. It is clear that
the interpretation of the disparity field is complicated by the variable
orientation of the eyes. However, it has been argued here that this complication
is minimized by the binocular coordination of the eyes. The geometric and
computational appeal of the Cyclopean representation has been emphasized,
and the biological relevance of the model has been indicated.
