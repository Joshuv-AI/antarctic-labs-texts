The liberation set in the inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph
Jephian C.-H. Lin, Polona Oblak, Helena Šmigoc

Abstract
The inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph G G is the problem of characterizing all lists of eigenvalues of real symmetric matrices whose off-diagonal pattern is prescribed by the adjacencies of G G . The strong spectral property is a powerful tool in this problem, which identifies matrices whose entries can be perturbed while controlling the pattern and preserving the eigenvalues. The Matrix Liberation Lemma introduced by Barrett et al. in 2020 advances the notion to a more general setting. In this paper we revisit the Matrix Liberation Lemma and prove an equivalent statement, that reduces some of the technical difficulties in applying the result. We test our method on matrices of the form M = A ⊕ B M=A\oplus B and show how this new approach supplements the results that can be obtained from the strong spectral property only. While extending this notion to the direct sums of graphs, we discover a surprising connection with the zero forcing game on Cartesian products of graphs. Throughout the paper we apply our results to resolve a selection of open cases for the inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph on six vertices.



Keywords:
Symmetric matrix; Inverse eigenvalue problem; Strong spectral property; Matrix Liberation Lemma; Zero forcing.

05C50, 15A18, 15B57, 65F18.

Introduction

Let
G
be a simple graph on
n
vertices. Let the set
\mathcal{S}(G)
denote the set of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices whose
(i,j)
-entry,
i\neq j
, is nonzero if and only if
\{i,j\}
is an edge of
G
, there are no constrains on diagonal entries. The
inverse eigenvalue problem of a graph
(the IEP-
G
, for short) is to find all possible spectra
\operatorname{spec}(A)
among matrices
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)

Despite huge interest and extensive literature on the problem, the IEP-
G

[
14

12

[

[

[

[

[

The study of the IEP-

\mathcal{S}(G)
with prescribed eigenvalues, or proving that such matrices do not exist. Since those tasks are hard once matrices get large and patterns more complicated, the research focuses on finding ways of extracting information for more difficult cases from simpler or smaller examples. An important advance in this direction was made in
[
5
,
4
]
with the introduction of the strong properties. In particular, if a matrix

has the strong spectral property defined below, then we can, for any spanning supergraph
H

that is cospectral with
A
.
The Matrix Liberation Lemma
[
4
]
(see also Lemma

1.2
.

Applying the Matrix Liberation Lemma directly involves first constructing a verification matrix
\Psi
, and then finding a vector
\bf x
with certain properties in the column space of
\Psi
. Both tasks can be technically demanding, and make it hard to develop an intuition into the process. With the aim of untangling some of the technical difficulties of applying the lemma, we introduce the liberation set of a matrix in Section
2
. With this notion we are able to form a result that is equivalent to the Matrix Liberation Lemma, but easier to apply.

With this new insight we are able to offer a series of examples that highlight how our result can be used to advance the IEP-
G
. In particular, in Section
3
we apply the method to direct sums of matrices. The eigenvalues of
M=A\oplus B
are straightforward to determine from the eigenvalues of
A
and
B
. Our method allows us to perturb the matrix
M
so that the new matrix corresponds to different connected graphs, while preserving the spectrum; see Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
. The strong spectral property already allows this, but only in the case when
A
and
B
have no eigenvalues in common.

Surprisingly, the zero forcing process introduced in
[
2
]
plays an important role in determining the liberation set of the directed sum of matrices with the strong spectral property; see Section
4
.
In Section
5
we continue the direction of research initiated in
[
19
]
and define the liberation set of a graph
G
, which is independent of the choice of
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
.

1.1
Notation and terminology

As the topic of this work spans matrix and graph theory, we depend on some standard notation from both areas.

For spaces of matrices,
\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of all
m\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{R}
(where
m=n
case is abbreviated as
\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
),
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the space of all symmetric matrices of order
n
over
\mathbb{R}
, and
\operatorname{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of all skew-symmetric matrices of order
n
over
\mathbb{R}
.

While
\mathcal{S}(G)
is not a subspace of
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the topological closure of
\mathcal{S}(G)
denoted by
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(G)
is. That is,
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(G)
is the set of matrices whose
(i,j)
-entry is nonzero only when
i=j
or
\{i,j\}
is an edge of
G
, and it is a subspace of
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
of dimension
|V(G)|+|E(G)|
. The set
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(G)
of matrices in
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(G)
with zero diagonal is another related subspace, this one of dimension
|E(G)|
.

For any positive integers
n
and
k
, let
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and
k+[n]:=\{k+1,\ldots,k+n\}
. For any given vector
{\bf v}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{i}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define its support by
\operatorname{supp}({\bf v}):=\left\{i\colon v_{i}\neq 0\right\}
, which is a subset of
[n]
.
The column space of an
m\times n
matrix
M
is denoted by

\mathrm{Col}(M):=\{M{\bf x}\colon{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
and its kernel (or null space) by
\ker(M):=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon M{\bf x}=\bf{0}\}
. For
\alpha\subseteq[m]
and
\beta\subseteq[n]
, let
M[\alpha,\beta]
be the submatrix of
M
induced on rows in
\alpha
and columns in
\beta
. Either
\alpha
or
\beta
can be replaced by the symbol
:
as an indication of the whole index set of rows or columns. When we write
\|M\|
, this can represent any matrix norm; however for concreteness we can take
\|M\|:=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(M^{\top}M)}
throughout.

Let
A
and
B
be matrices. Their direct sum is denoted by
A\oplus B
,
A\circ B
is the Hadamard (entrywise) product of
A
and
B
, and
[A,B]:=AB-BA
is the commutator, where in each case we assume
A
and
B
are of sizes compatible with the relevant operation.

Let
E^{i,j}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})
be the matrix with one at position
(i,j)
and zeros elsewhere,
I_{n}
the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
O_{m,n}
the
m\times n
zero matrix. In all cases the indices will be omitted if they are clear from the context. Moreover, let
K^{i,j}:=E^{i,j}-E^{j,i}
and
X^{i,j}:=E^{i,j}+E^{j,i}
.

The
lexicographical order
on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
is defined by
(i,j)\preceq(k,\ell)
if and only if
i<k
or (
i=k
and
j\leq\ell
). For
A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})
let
\operatorname{vec}_{\triangle}(A)
be the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
that records the entries in the upper triangular part of
A
under the lexicographical order. Similarly,
\operatorname{vec}_{\wedge}(A)
is the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}
that records the strictly upper triangular part of
A
, excluding the diagonal entries. Finally,
\operatorname{vec}_{\square}(A)
is the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
that records all entries of
A
under the lexicographical order.

For a symmetric matrix
A
we denote the multiplicity of an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
as
\mathrm{mult}_{A}(\lambda)
. Suppose that
A\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
has distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{q}
with multiplicities
m_{i}=\mathrm{mult}_{A}(\lambda_{i})
for
i\in[q]
. The spectrum of
A
will be denoted by
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{\lambda_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,\lambda_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}
, where
\lambda^{(k)}
denotes
k
copies of
\lambda
. The
unordered multiplicity list
of
A
is defined to be the list of multiplicities
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}\}
, in no particular order. We say that the unordered multiplicity list
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}\}
is
spectrally arbitrary
for
G
if for any distinct
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{q}
, the spectrum
\{\lambda_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,\lambda_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}
is realizable by a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
.
If the eigenvalues of
A
are ordered in an increasing order
\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{q}
, then the
ordered multiplicity list
of
A
is defined as an ordered list
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{q})
. We say that the ordered multiplicity list
(m_{1},\ldots,m_{q})
is
spectrally arbitrary
for
G
if for any
\lambda_{1}<\cdots<\lambda_{q}
, the spectrum
\{\lambda_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,\lambda_{q}^{(m_{q})}\}
is realizable by a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
.

The
disjoint union
of two graphs
G
and
H
will be denoted by
G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H
, and the complement of graph
G
by
\overline{G}
. If
G
and
H
are graphs with
V(G)=V(H)
and
E(H)=E(G)\cup\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
, we say that
H
is a
spanning supergraph
of
G
and write
H=G+\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
.

1.2
Preliminaries

A symmetric matrix
A
has the
strong spectral property
(the SSP, for short) if
X=O
is the only symmetric matrix satisfying
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
[A,X]=O
. The SSP was first introduced in
[
5
]
and was motivated by the strong Arnold property introduced in
[
9
,
10
]
. A symmetric matrix
A
has the
strong Arnold property
(the SAP, for short) if
X=O
is the only symmetric matrix satisfying
A\circ X=I\circ X=O
and
AX=O
. Having a matrix with a strong property in hand, we can infer the existence of other matrices that share the relevant spectral property, as detailed in the two results below.

Theorem 1.1

(Supergraph Lemma
[
5
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph and
H
a spanning supergraph of
G
.
If
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively), then for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(H)
with
\|A-A^{\prime}\|<\epsilon
and the SSP (the SAP, respectively) such that
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})
(
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})
, respectively).

Theorem 1.2

(Direct Sum Lemma
[
5
]
)
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs, and let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
both have the SSP (the SAP, respectively). Then
A\oplus B
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) if and only if
\operatorname{spec}(A)\cap\operatorname{spec}(B)=\emptyset
(
0\notin\operatorname{spec}(A)\cap\operatorname{spec}(B)
, respectively).

Example 1.3
.

Let
G=K_{n}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}K_{1}
and label the vertices
V(G)=[n+1]
of
G
so that
n+1
is the isolated vertex of
G
. Moreover, let

A=\begin{pmatrix}M&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\lambda\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}(G),

where
M
is the
n\times n
all-ones matrix. To check if
A
has the SSP, let
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

X=\begin{pmatrix}O_{n}&{\bf b}\\
{\bf b}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
X\circ A=X\circ I=O
and the condition
[A,X]=O
is equivalent to

M{\bf b}=\lambda{\bf b}
.
Therefore,
A
has the SSP if and only if
\lambda\notin\operatorname{spec}(M)=\{0^{(n-1)},n\}
. By Theorem
1.1
there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(H)
with the SSP and spectrum
\{0^{(n-1)},n,\lambda\}
for any spanning supergraph
H
of
G
and
\lambda\notin\{0,n\}
.

From the example we see that some matrices might not have the SSP. However, if we are willing to provide some more restrictions, e.g., assuming
{\bf b}
in Example
1.3
has only one nonzero entry, then the matrix is not so far from having the SSP. This leads to a generalization of the Supergraph Lemma.

Definition 1.4
.

[
20
, Definition 3.2]

Let
G
be a spanning subgraph of
H
. A matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has
the SSP with respect to
H
if
X=O
is the only symmetric matrix that satisfies
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{H})
and
[A,X]=O
.

A matrix
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has
the SAP with respect to
H
if
X=O
is the only symmetric matrix that satisfies
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{H})
and
AX=O
.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has the SSP with respect to
G
itself if and only if
A
has the SSP, because

\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G})=\{X\in\operatorname{Sym}_{n}(\mathbb{R}):A\circ X=I\circ X=O\}.

Let
G
,
H
,
H^{\prime}
be graphs on the same set of vertices such that
E(G)\subseteq E(H)\subseteq E(H^{\prime})
. By Definition
1.4
and the fact
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{H})\supseteq\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{H^{\prime}})
,
A
has the SSP with respect to
H
implies
A
has the SSP with respect to
H^{\prime}
. In particular,
A
has the SSP implies
A
has the SSP with respect to any spanning supergraph
H
of
G
. The statements in this remark also hold for the SAP.

Theorem 1.6
.

[
20
, Theorem 3.4]

Let
G
,
H
, and
H^{\prime}
be graphs such that
V(G)=V(H)=V(H^{\prime})
and
E(G)\subseteq E(H)\subseteq E(H^{\prime})
.
If
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) with respect to
H
, then for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(H^{\prime})
with
\|A-A^{\prime}\|<\epsilon
,
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})
(
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})
, respectively),
A
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) with respect to
H^{\prime}
and every entry of
A^{\prime}
that corresponds to an edge in
E(H^{\prime})\setminus E(H)
is nonzero.

Example 1.7
.

We revisit
G
,
A
, and
X
given in Example
1.3
.
Recall, that
A
does not have the SSP for
\lambda\in\{0,n\}
, so let us choose
\lambda=n
. Let
H:=G+\{n+1,i\}
for some
i\in[n]
, and
H^{\prime}
any supergraph of
H
with
V(H^{\prime})=[n+1]
.

Assuming the
i
-th entry of
{\bf b}
is zero, it is an easy computation to observe that the equality
M{\bf b}=n{\bf b}
implies
{\bf b}={\bf 0}
. This implies
A
has the SSP with respect to
H
. By Theorem
1.6
, there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}(H^{\prime})
with the SSP with respect to
H^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\{0^{(n-1)},n^{(2)}\}
. Moreover,
A^{\prime}
has nonzero entries corresponding to
E(H^{\prime})\setminus E(H)
, and since
A^{\prime}
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
A
, we may also assume the nonzero entries of
A
stay nonzero in
A^{\prime}
. However, Theorem
1.6
does not tell us if the entry of
A^{\prime}
corresponding to
\{n+1,i\}
is zero or not.

As we have seen in the example above, the drawback of applying Theorem
1.6
is that there are entries in the matrix
A^{\prime}
that can take any real value, so the graph of
A^{\prime}
is not completely defined. To attend to this drawback, we depend on work from
[
4
,
5
]
. In particular, we use the notion of the verification matrix and related results as summarized below.

Note that the subspace
\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G})

has a basis
{\mathcal{X}}=\{X^{i,j}:\{i,j\}\in E(\overline{G})\}
, and the subspace
\operatorname{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
has a basis
{\mathcal{K}}=\{K^{i,j}:i,j\in[n],\ i<j\}
.
Define a linear map

f\colon\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G})\rightarrow\operatorname{Skew}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\text{ with }f(X)=[A,X],

and let
\Psi
be the matrix representation of
f
from basis
{\mathcal{X}}
to basis
{\mathcal{K}}
so that
[X]_{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\Psi=[f(X)]_{\mathcal{E}}^{\top}
. (The ordering of rows and columns of
\Psi
respects lexicographic ordering.) Here
[X]_{\mathcal{X}}
and
[f(X)]_{\mathcal{K}}
denote the corresponding vector representations of
X
and
f(X)
in bases
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}
, respectively, and we use the left multiplication to follow the convention in
[
4
]
. By definition, one may verify whether
A
has the SSP by checking if the left kernel of
\Psi
is trivial. The matrix
\Psi
is called the SSP verification matrix and is formally defined in Definition
1.8
. Note that
[K]_{\mathcal{K}}=\operatorname{vec}_{\wedge}(K)
for any skew-symmetric matrix
K
.

Definition 1.8
.

[
5
, Theorem 31]

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices,
\overline{m}=|E(\overline{G})|
, and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. The
SSP verification matrix

\Psi_{\rm SSP}(A)
is the
\overline{m}\times\binom{n}{2}
matrix whose rows are
\operatorname{vec}_{\wedge}([A,X^{i,j}])
for all pairs
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
\{i,j\}\in E(\overline{G})
under the lexicographical order.

The
SAP verification matrix

\Psi_{\rm SAP}(A)
is the
\overline{m}\times n^{2}
matrix whose rows are
\operatorname{vec}_{\square}(AX^{i,j})
for all pairs
(i,j)
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
\{i,j\}\in E(\overline{G})
under the lexicographical order.

Remark 1.9
.

It is not hard to see (see also
[
4
]
) that
A
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) if and only if the left kernel of the verification matrix
\Psi_{\rm SSP}(A)
(
\Psi_{\rm SAP}(A)
, respectively) is trivial. This is true if and only if the set of rows in
\Psi_{\rm SSP}(A)
(
\Psi_{\rm SAP}(A)
, respectively) is linearly independent.

Indeed, similar arguments work for the extended strong properties. That is,
A
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) with respect to
H
if and only if the set of rows in
\Psi_{\rm SSP}(A)
(
\Psi_{\rm SAP}(A)
, respectively) corresponding to
E(\overline{H})
is linearly independent.

With the verification matrix, we are able to state the Matrix Liberation Lemma introduced in
[
4
]
. Here we rephrase the statements in terms of the extended strong properties in Definition
1.4
.

Lemma 1.10

(Matrix Liberation Lemma — vector version
[
4
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph,
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
, and
\Psi=\Psi_{\rm SSP}(A)
(
\Psi=\Psi_{\rm SAP}(A)
, respectively). Suppose there is a vector
{\bf x}\in\mathrm{Col}(\Psi)
such that
A
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) with respect to
G+\operatorname{supp}({\bf x})
. Then there exists a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G+\operatorname{supp}({\bf x}))
with the SSP (the SAP, respectively) and
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)
(
\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}(A)
, respectively).

Example 1.11
.

G=K_{4}\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}K_{1}

. Then the SSP verification matrix of

where the rows of
\Psi
are indexed by the nonedges
\{1,5\}
,
\{2,5\}
,
\{3,5\}
, and
\{4,5\}

\alpha=\{\{1,5\},\{2,5\}\}

{\bf x}=(0,0,1,-1)^{\top}

A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G+\beta)
with the SSP and
\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})=\operatorname{spec}(A)=\{0^{(3)},4^{(2)}\}
.

As we can see in Example
1.11
, the Matrix Liberation Lemma (Lemma
1.10
) relies on a vector
{\bf x}
in its assumption while the conclusion only uses the support of
{\bf x}
. That is, the exact values of entries of
{\bf x}
do not play a role. Since the support of
{\bf x}
is all we need in practice, we aim to better understand, how to determine all possible supports of vectors
{\bf x}
that meet the requirements in the Matrix Liberation Lemma in the next section. In particular, Example
2.7
will explain how the vector
{\bf x}
in the example above can be found.

2
Liberation set of a matrix

To motivate the work in this section, we start by stating the main definition and the main result.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. A nonempty set of edges
\beta\subseteq E(\overline{G})
is called an
SSP liberation set of
A
(or an
SAP liberation set of
A
, respectively) if and only if
A
has the SSP (the SAP, respectively) with respect to
G+\beta^{\prime}
for all
\beta^{\prime}\subset\beta
with
|\beta^{\prime}|=|\beta|-1
.

Lemma 2.2

(Matrix Liberation Lemma — set version)
.

Let
A
be a matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)
and
\beta
an SSP (an SAP, respectively) liberation set of
A
. Then there is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}(G+\beta)
with the SSP (the SAP, respectively) such that
\operatorname{spec}(A)=\operatorname{spec}(A^{\prime})
(or
\operatorname{rank}(A)=\operatorname{rank}(A^{\prime})
, respectively).

Example 2.3
.

Let
G
be a graph and
A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}(G)
. If
A
has the SSP (with respect to
G
), then any nonempty subset
\beta\subseteq E(\overline{G})
is an SSP liberation set of
A
since
A
has the SSP with respect to any spanning supergraph of
G
. On the other extreme,
\beta=E(\overline{G})
is an SSP liberation set of
A
unless there exist
\{i,j\}\in E(\overline{G})
such that
[A,X^{i,j}]=O
. This happens if and only if both of
i
and
j
are isolated vertices in
G
, and
a_{i,i}=a_{j,j}
.

In the remark below we give an outline of a proof of Lemma
2.2
that depends on Theorem
1.6
and highlights how the SSP with respect to a graph is used in the definition of the liberation set. Later we will develop a more detailed proof that relies on Lemma
1.10
and makes the equivalence between the two results transparent.

Remark 2.4
.

One may understand the liberation set as a set where every entry can be perturbed into a nonzero entry individually. Suppose
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
has a liberation set
\beta
. Let
\beta=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}
and
\beta_{i}:=\beta\setminus\{e_{i}\}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
. Then by definition
A
has the SSP with respect to
G+\beta_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
. According to the extended version of the Supergraph Lemma (Theorem
1.6
), one may perturb
A
to obtain
A_{1}
that satisfies:

•

the entries in
A_{1}
corresponding to
e_{1}
become nonzero,

•

every nonzero entry in
A
stays nonzero also in
A_{1}
,

•

off-diagonal entries of
A_{1}
outside
E(G)\cup\beta
remain zero,

•

A_{1}
has the same spectrum as
A
,

•

the new matrix still has the SSP with respect to
G+\beta_{i}
for each
i
where
A_{1}
is zero on the entry of
e_{i}
.

If
A_{1}
is nonzero on the entry of
e_{2}
, then set
A_{2}=A_{1}
.
Otherwise, since
A_{1}
has the SSP with respect to
G+\beta_{2}
, we may perturb
A_{1}
into
A_{2}
in the same way; in particular, the entry of
e_{2}
becomes nonzero. We may continue this process inductively to obtain
A_{3},\ldots,A_{k}
. Note that once an entry turns nonzero, it stays nonzero in this process. Therefore, in the end we obtain a matrix
A^{\prime}=A_{k}
as desired in the Matrix Liberation Lemma (Lemma
2.2
).

We will show in Proposition
2.6
that Lemma
2.2
is in the correct setting equivalent to Lemma
1.10
. As we try to understand all possible supports of vectors in the column space of a given matrix, we depend on basic linear algebra methods recalled in the next lemma.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})
and
\alpha\subsetneq[m]
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

M[\alpha\cup\{k\},:]
has full row-rank for all
k\in[m]\setminus\alpha
.

(2)

There exists a nonzero vector
{\bf v}\in\mathrm{Col}(M)
such that
\operatorname{supp}({\bf v})=[m]\setminus\alpha
and
M[\alpha,:]
has full row-rank.

(3)

The matrix obtained from
M
by permuting the the rows labeled by
\alpha
to the top has the column reduced echelon form

\begin{pmatrix}I_{|\alpha|}&O\\
?&B\end{pmatrix},

where each row of
B
is a nonzero vector.

Proof.

To prove that
(1)
implies
(2)
, suppose that for some set
\alpha\subsetneq[m]
, the matrix
M[\alpha\cup\{k\},:]
has full row-rank for all
k\in[m]\setminus\alpha
. This implies
\dim(\mathrm{Col}(M[\alpha\cup\{k\},:]))=|\alpha|+1
, and hence
\mathrm{Col}(M[\alpha\cup\{k\},:])=\mathbb{R}^{|\alpha|+1}.
In particular,
\mathrm{Col}(M[\alpha\cup\{k\},:])
contains the vector that vanishes on
\alpha
and has the entry corresponding to
k
equal to
1
. Therefore,
for each
k\in[m]\setminus\alpha
, we can find a vector
{\bf v}_{k}\in\mathrm{Col}(M)
satisfying
{\bf v}_{k}[\alpha]={\bf 0}
and
k
-th entry of
{\bf v}_{k}
is nonzero. Hence, there exists a linear combination of such vectors

{\bf v}=\sum_{k\in[m]\setminus\alpha}c_{k}{\bf v}_{k}\in\mathrm{Col}(M),

so that
\operatorname{supp}({\bf v})=[m]\setminus\alpha
, which proves our claim.

Conversely, we suppose
{\bf v}\in\mathrm{Col}(M)
is a nonzero vector such that
[m]\setminus\operatorname{supp}({\bf v})=\alpha
and the matrix
M[\alpha,:]
has full row-rank. Let
k\in[m]\setminus\alpha=\operatorname{supp}({\bf v})
and
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha\cup\{k\}
. We will show
M^{\prime}=M[\alpha^{\prime},:]
has full row-rank. To see this, let
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a vector that vanishes outside
\alpha^{\prime}
and
{\bf x}^{\top}M={\bf 0}
. Since
{\bf x}^{\top}M={\bf 0}
and
{\bf v}\in\mathrm{Col}(M)
, we know
{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf v}=0
. Moreover,
\operatorname{supp}({\bf x})\cap\operatorname{supp}({\bf v})\subseteq\{k\}
implies
{\bf x}
is zero at the
k
-th entry. Therefore,
{\bf x}
vanishes outside
\alpha
, which leads to
{\bf x}={\bf 0}
because
M[\alpha,:]
has full row-rank. In summary,
{\bf x}={\bf 0}
is the only vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
that vanishes outside
\alpha^{\prime}
, and satisfies
{\bf x}^{\top}M={\bf 0}
, so
M^{\prime}
has full row-rank.

Next we will show that
(1)

\implies

(3)

\implies

(2)
. Suppose
\alpha\subsetneq[m]
is a set such that
M[\alpha\cup\{k\},:]
has full row-rank for all
k\in[m]\setminus\alpha
. Let
W
be the matrix obtained from
M
by permuting all rows in
\alpha
to the top. Recall that column operations do not change the linear dependency of rows. Since
M[\alpha,:]
has full row-rank, the first
|\alpha|
rows of
W
form a linearly independent set, and its column reduced echelon form has the form

\begin{pmatrix}I_{|\alpha|}&O\\
?&B\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, since for
k\in[m]\setminus\alpha
, the rows of
W
corresponding to
\alpha\cup\{k\}
are also independent, and the row of
B
corresponding to
k
is a nonzero vector. This establishes (3). With this property, one may find a nowhere zero vector in the column space of
B
, which means there is a vector in the column space of
W
whose support is
[m]\setminus\alpha
. Therefore, there is a nonzero vector
{\bf v}\in\mathrm{Col}(M)
with
\operatorname{supp}({\bf v})=[m]\setminus\alpha
. Again, by the column reduced echelon form of
W
, the rows of
W
in
\alpha
are independent, so are the rows of
M
in
\alpha
.
∎

In our application of Lemma
2.5
we will take
M
to be the verification matrix
\Psi

this will allow us to prove Lemma

Statement

3
Direct sum of matrices with the SSP

As an application of Lemma
2.2
, we consider matrices of the form
A\oplus B
with
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs on
m
and
n
vertices, respectively,
\beta
a set of edges between
G
and
H
, and
Y
an
m\times n
matrix. We say that

Y
vanishes on
\beta
, if under the labeling of the rows of
Y
by
V(G)
and the columns of
Y
by
V(H)
we have
Y_{u,v}=0
for all
\{u,v\}\in\beta
. We denote this by
Y|_{\beta}=0
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs on
m
and
n
vertices, respectively,
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
,
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
, and
\beta
a set of edges between
G
and
H
.

1.

Assume that
A
and
B
have the SSP, and that
Y=O
is the only matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
Y|_{\beta}=0
that satisfies
AY-YB=O
. Then
A\oplus B\in\mathcal{S}(G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H)
has the SSP with respect to
(G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H)+\beta
.

2.

Assume that
A
and
B
have the SAP, and that
Y=O
is the only matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with
Y|_{\beta}=0
, that satisfies
AY=YB=O
. Then
A\oplus B\in\mathcal{S}(G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H)
has the SAP with respect to
(G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H)+\beta
.

Proof.

Let

X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{A}&Y\\
Y^{\top}&X_{B}\end{pmatrix}

be a symmetric matrix such that
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H+\beta})
.
To prove the first item we assume that
[A\oplus B,X]=O
. From the SSP condition on
A
and
B
this reduces to
AY-YB=O
, and the conclusion follows.
Similarly, to prove the second item, we assume
(A\oplus B)X=O
. The SAP assumption for
A
and
B
reduces this equality to
AY=YB=O
, which implies
Y=O
and the second statement is proved.
∎

The following standard linear algebra result will help us to understand the set of solutions
Y
to the equation
AY-YB=O
. The proof is added for completeness.
For any two matrices
A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
and
B\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
we denote by
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
the set of solutions
Y\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})
of the equation
AY-YB=O
. The following proposition describes
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A
and
B
be symmetric matrices of order
m
and
n
, respectively. Suppose
A
and
B
have
k
distinct common eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
with
\mathrm{mult}_{A}(\lambda_{i})=a_{i}
and
\mathrm{mult}_{B}(\lambda_{i})=b_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Then

\mathcal{R}(A,B)=\operatorname{span}\{{\bf u}{\bf v}^{\top}:A{\bf u}=\lambda_{i}{\bf u},\ B{\bf v}=\lambda_{i}{\bf v}\text{ for some }i\in[k]\},

and has the dimension
\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{i}b_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
Q_{A}
and
Q_{B}
be orthogonal matrices that diagonalize
A
and
B
:
Q_{A}^{\top}AQ_{A}=D_{A}
and
Q_{B}^{\top}BQ_{B}=D_{B}
. Then the equation
AY-YB=O
is equivalent to
D_{A}Y^{\prime}-Y^{\prime}D_{B}=O
, where
Y^{\prime}=Q_{A}^{\top}YQ_{B}
. We deduce that the
(i,j)
-entry of
Y^{\prime}
has to be zero if the
(i,i)
-entry
(D_{A})_{i,i}
of
D_{A}
is different from the
(j,j)
-entry
(D_{B})_{j,j}
of
D_{B}
. Let
{\bf e}_{i}
and
{\bf f}_{i}
denote the
i
-th columns of
I_{m}
and
I_{n}
, respectively. With this notation we have

\mathcal{R}(D_{A},D_{B})=\operatorname{span}\{{\bf e}_{i}{\bf f}_{j}^{\top}:(D_{A})_{i,i}=(D_{B})_{j,j}\}.

Since
\mathcal{R}(A,B)=Q_{A}\mathcal{R}(D_{A},D_{B})Q_{B}^{\top}
, the statement follows.
∎

If both
A
and
B
have the SSP and they do not have any common eigenvalues, then
A\oplus B
has the SSP, see Theorem
1.1
. However, if
A
and
B
share one distinct eigenvalue, this no longer holds, and we aim to understand liberation sets for
A\oplus B
in this case. To this end we will impose some conditions on the eigenspaces of
A
and
B
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a
d
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
W
be an
n\times d
matrix whose columns form a basis of
\mathcal{W}
. Then
\mathcal{W}
is said to be
generic
if and only if every
d\times d
submatrix of
W
is invertible.

The generic subspace is well-defined above, as it does not depend on the choice of
W
. Indeed, if
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are
n\times d
matrices and columns of
W_{1}
and columns of
W_{2}
form bases of
\mathcal{W}
, then
W_{2}=W_{1}Q
for some invertible
Q\in\operatorname{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, a
d\times d
submatrix
B
in
W_{1}
is invertible if and only if the corresponding submatrix
BQ
in
W_{2}
is invertible. As an example, when
\mathcal{W}
is a
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then it is generic if and only if it is spanned by a nowhere zero vector.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
have the SSP. Suppose
\operatorname{spec}(A)\cap\operatorname{spec}(B)=\{\lambda\}
with
\mathrm{mult}_{A}(\lambda)=k
and
\mathrm{mult}_{B}(\lambda)=\ell
, where
\ker(A-\lambda I)
and
\ker(B-\lambda I)
are generic.
Then for any
V_{G}\subseteq V(G)
and
V_{H}\subseteq V(H)
with

•

|V_{G}|=k
and
|V_{H}|=\ell+1
or

•

|V_{G}|=k+1
and
|V_{H}|=\ell
,

the set

\beta=\{\{u,v\}:u\in V_{G}\text{ and }v\in V_{H}\}

is an SSP liberation set of
A\oplus B
. In particular, there exists a matrix
C\in S(G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H+\beta)
with the SSP such that
\operatorname{spec}(C)=\operatorname{spec}(A)\cup\operatorname{spec}(B)
.

Proof.

Let
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
be
|V(G)|\times k
and
|V(H)|\times\ell
matrices whose columns form bases of
\ker(A-\lambda I)
and
\ker(B-\lambda I)
, respectively. Let
\beta\subset V(G)\times V(H)
be as in the statement of the theorem, and
\beta^{\prime}\subset\beta
with
|\beta^{\prime}|=|\beta|-1
. Observe that by the assumption on
V_{G}
and
V_{H}
,
\beta^{\prime}
contains a
k\times\ell
grid, say
\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
, regardless the choice of
\beta^{\prime}
.

By Proposition
3.3
,

\mathcal{R}(A,B)=\{U_{A}SU_{B}^{\top}\colon S\in\operatorname{Mat}_{k,\ell}(\mathbb{R})\}.

Suppose
Y\in\mathcal{R}(A,B)
satisfies
Y|_{\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}}=0
. Then
Y=U_{A}S_{Y}U_{B}
for some
S_{Y}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{k,\ell}(\mathbb{R})
, and

U_{A}[\mathcal{I},:]S_{Y}U_{B}[\mathcal{J},:]^{\top}=O.

Since both
\ker(A-\lambda I)
and
\ker(B-\lambda I)
are assumed to be generic,
U_{A}[\mathcal{I},:]
and
U_{B}[\mathcal{J},:]
are invertible, so
S_{Y}=O
. This implies
Y=O
and proves that
A\oplus B
has the SSP with respect to
G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H+\beta^{\prime}
by Proposition
3.2
. This also proves that
\beta
is an SSP liberation set of
A\oplus B
, since our choice of
\beta^{\prime}
was arbitrary. The conclusion follows by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

When applying Theorem

, the assumption on genericity of eigenspaces can be hard to prove. However, this condition is straightforward to check for specific matrices of small dimension.

Example 3.6

Let

4
Zero forcing

In the following, we focus on disconnected graphs and build a technique that depends on the (classical) zero forcing game to identify SSP liberation sets of corresponding matrices. The analogous statements for the SAP is included at the end of the section.

We first recall the zero forcing game introduced in
[
2
]
. Let
G
be a graph. At each stage of the
zero forcing game
all vertices are assigned a color: blue or white. At the start of the game the initial set
F\subseteq V(G)
of blue vertices is chosen. The game is played by repeated application of the following
color change rule
. If
v
is the only white neighbor of a blue vertex
u
, then
v
turns blue in the next step. This action is called
a force
and is denoted by
u\rightarrow v
. If, starting with an initial set
F
of blue vertices, repeated application of the color change rule successfully turns all the vertices blue, then
F
is called a
zero forcing set
for
G
.

The
zero forcing number
of a graph
G
is the minimum size of a zero forcing set, denoted by
Z(G)
. For example, a leaf is a zero forcing set for
P_{n}
and
Z(P_{n})=1
, while in
C_{n}
, any two adjacent vertices form a zero forcing set and
Z(C_{n})=2
. It is known that
Z(G)
is an upper bound for the multiplicity of any eigenvalue of any matrix in
\mathcal{S}(G)

[
2
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G
be a graph. A set
F\subseteq V(G)
is a
zero forcing cover
of
G
if
F^{\prime}
is a zero forcing set of
G
for any
F^{\prime}\subset F
with
|F^{\prime}|=|F|-1
.

If
F
is a zero forcing cover of
G
, then clearly any superset of
F
is also a zero forcing cover of
G
. In general, the union of two or more disjoint zero forcing sets is a zero forcing cover. However, the following example shows that a zero forcing cover does not need to be of this form.

Example 4.2
.

It is not difficult to check that the colored sets of vertices of
\mathsf{G_{30}}
and
\mathsf{G_{36}}
in Figure
7
are examples of zero forcing covers. More generally, the set of all leaves in a generalized star
G
is a zero forcing cover of
G
.

\mathsf{G_{30}}
and
\mathsf{G_{36}}
, colored as blue.

As we will see below, a zero forcing cover of the Cartesian product of graphs
G
and

allows us to to find an SSP liberation set of some matrices in
\mathcal{S}(G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H)
. Recall that the
Cartesian product
of two graphs
G
and
H
is the graph
G\mathbin{\square}H
on the vertex set

V(G\mathbin{\square}H)=\{(u,v):u\in V(G),v\in V(H)\}

such that two vertices
(u_{1},v_{1})
and
(u_{2},v_{2})
are adjacent if either

•

u_{1}=u_{2}
and
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\in E(H)
, or

•

v_{1}=v_{2}
and
\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\in E(G)
.

It is known
[
2
]
that
Z(P_{s}\mathbin{\square}P_{t})=\min\{s,t\}
and
Z(C_{s}\mathbin{\square}P_{t})=\min\{s,2t\}
. For

, it is known
[
8
]

Z(C_{s}\mathbin{\square}C_{t})=\begin{cases}2s-1&\text{if }s=t\text{ and }s\text{ is odd},\\
2s&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs, and
\beta^{\prime}
a zero forcing set of
G\mathbin{\square}H
. Then
A\oplus B
has the SSP with respect to
G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H+\beta^{\prime}
for any
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
and
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
both with the SSP.

Proof.

Let us denote
\Gamma:=G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H+\beta^{\prime}
and let
A\in\mathcal{S}(G)
,
B\in\mathcal{S}(H)
have the SSP. Moreover, suppose
X\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{\Gamma})
satisfies
[A\oplus B,X]=O
. We may write

X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{A}&Y\\
Y^{\top}&X_{B}\end{pmatrix}

conformal with the partition of
A\oplus B
such that
X_{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{G})
,
X_{B}\in\mathcal{S}^{\rm cl}_{0}(\overline{H})
, and
Y|_{\beta^{\prime}}=0
. Because both
A
and
B
have the SSP, the condition
[A\oplus B,X]=O
is equivalent to
AY-YB=O.

Next we look at the entries of
AY-YB
, and use the notation
A=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
,
B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
, and
Y=\begin{pmatrix}y_{i,j}\end{pmatrix}
.

Let
(i_{0},j_{0}),(i_{1},j_{1})\in V(G\mathbin{\square}H)
and let
F\subset V(G\mathbin{\square}H)
be a set of blue vertices that allows the force
(i_{0},j_{0})\rightarrow(i_{1},j_{1})
on
G\mathbin{\square}H
(by one application of the color change rule).
First we prove that if
y_{i,j}=0
for all
(i,j)\in F
and
(AY-YB)_{i_{0},j_{0}}=0
, then
y_{i_{1},j_{1}}=0
.
The equation
(AY-YB)_{i_{0},j_{0}}=0
can be written as:

\sum_{k\in N_{G}[i_{0}]}a_{i_{0},k}y_{k,j_{0}}-\sum_{k\in N_{H}[j_{0}]}b_{k,j_{0}}y_{i_{0},k}=0.

From
(i_{0},j_{0})\rightarrow(i_{1},j_{1})
, we know that
(i_{0},j_{0})
and all its neighbors except
(i_{1},j_{1})
in
G\mathbin{\square}H
are in
F
. This means that all variables
y_{i,j}
appearing in the equation above are assumed to be zero except for
y_{i_{1},j_{1}}
. Moreover, since
(i_{1},j_{1})
is a neighbor of
(i_{0},j_{0})
, either
i_{1}=i_{0}
or
j_{1}=j_{0}
. If
i_{1}=i_{0}
, then the equation reduces to
-b_{j_{1},j_{0}}y_{i_{0},j_{1}}=0
. If
j_{1}=j_{0}
, then it reduces to
a_{i_{0},i_{1}}y_{i_{1},j_{0}}=0
. In either case we conclude
y_{i_{1},j_{1}}=0
.

Assuming that
Y|_{\beta^{\prime}}=0
and
AY-YB=O
, we can now conclude
Y=0
by repeated application of the claim above.
Therefore, by Proposition
3.2
,
A\oplus B
has the SSP with respect to
G\mathbin{\dot{\cup}}H+\beta^{\prime}
if
\beta^{\prime}
is a zero forcing set of
G\mathbin{\square}H
, as claimed.
∎

.

Let

If

4.3

\beta

with the SSP.
∎

6
Conclusion

The introduction of the strong spectral properties has made a powerful impact on the IEP-
G
and related problems. The notion of the SSP liberation set, introduced in this paper, further advances the approach to the problem through an investigation of what perturbations of the pattern of a given matrix guarantee the preservation of spectra or rank. While the paper offers a selection of examples where the SSP liberation set is applied, it does not aim to provide an exhaustive list of possible research directions that could benefit from it. A study of the direct sums of matrices and disjoint unions of graphs is a natural first step, especially since using the standard spectral properties in this context is fully understood. Even in this special case, the paper provides a foundation for future research supported with only initial examples that can be developed further. In particular, the paper introduces and motivates a purely combinatorial problem of determining the zero forcing covers of Cartesian products of graphs.
