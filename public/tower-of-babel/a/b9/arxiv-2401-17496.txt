Tensor invariants for classical groups revisited
William Q. Erickson, Markus Hunziker

Abstract
. We reconsider an old problem, namely the dimension of the G G -invariant subspace in V ⊗ p ⊗ V ∗ ⊗ q V^{\otimes p}\otimes V^{*\otimes q} , where G G is one of the classical groups GL ⁡ ( V ) \GL(V) , SL ⁡ ( V ) \SL(V) , O ⁡ ( V ) \operatorname{O}(V) , SO ⁡ ( V ) \SO(V) , or Sp ⁡ ( V ) \Sp(V) . Spanning sets for the invariant subspace have long been well known, but linear bases are more delicate. The main contribution of this paper is a combinatorial realization of linear bases via standard Young tableaux and arc diagrams, in a uniform manner for all five classical groups. As a secondary contribution, we survey the many equivalent ways — some old, some new — to enumerate the elements in these bases.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to write down an exhaustive list of combinatorial answers to the following classical problem: if we are given a finite-dimensional vector space
V
, then for each of the classical groups
G\subseteq\GL(V)
, what is the dimension of the invariant subspace
(V^{\otimes p}\otimes V^{*\otimes q})^{G}
?
It is straightforward to specify a spanning set in each case; this is the content of Weyl’s first fundamental theorems for tensor invariants
[
44
]
.
Due to the relations, however, which are described in the second fundamental theorems, it is less straightforward to determine a linear basis.
Surprisingly, we are unable to find a reference anywhere in the invariant theory literature which solves this combinatorial problem in a uniform manner for all five classical groups.
As a main result of the present paper (Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
), we give combinatorial realizations of linear bases as sets of arc diagrams, uniformly across all classical groups
\GL(V)
,
\SL(V)
,
\operatorname{O}(V)
,
\SO(V)
, and
\Sp(V)
.
As another main result (Theorems
6.1
and
6.4
), we catalog several equivalent ways to enumerate these linear bases.

Our paper takes the following approach.
Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field
K
of characteristic 0, and let
W\coloneqq V^{*p}\oplus V^{q}
.
We begin by addressing the (more general) problem of
polynomial invariants
, that is, elements of the invariant ring
K[W]^{G}
.
For each classical group
G
, there is a canonical linear basis for
K[W]^{G}
consisting of
standard monomials
, due to DeConcini–Procesi
[
10
]
, obtained via the straightening laws of Doubilet–Rota–Stein
[
12
]
.
The technique came to be known as
standard monomial theory
, introduced independently by Seshadri
[
36
]
.
A highly detailed treatment of the standard monomial bases for
K[W]^{G}
is also given by Lakshmibai–Raghavan
[
27
]
*Ch. 10–12.
We devote Section
2
to recalling these bases of standard monomials, and to realizing them as sets of semistandard tableaux which we denote by
\mathcal{S}^{G}
.

In Section
3
, we establish another linear basis for
K[W]^{G}
, which we denote by
\mathcal{B}^{G}
.
Unlike the basis
\mathcal{S}^{G}
, the basis
\mathcal{B}^{G}
consists of
ordinary monomials
, meaning true monomials in the contractions and determinants that generate
K[W]^{G}
.
(By contrast, the standard monomials in
\mathcal{S}^{G}
are not actually monomials in these generators.)
We obtain
\mathcal{B}^{G}
by applying certain variants of the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth (RSK) correspondence
[
23
]
to the basis
\mathcal{S}^{G}
: in particular, each RSK-type correspondence yields a multidegree-preserving bijection between the tableaux in
\mathcal{S}^{G}
and the degree matrices (in a set we call
\mathcal{M}^{G}
) of the ordinary monomials in
\mathcal{B}^{G}
:

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\text{tableaux in $\mathcal{S}^{G}$}&\xleftrightarrow{{\rm RSK}}&\text{$\mathbb{N}$-matrices in $\mathcal{M}^{G}$}&&\\
\updownarrow&&\updownarrow&&\\
\text{standard monomials}&&\text{ordinary monomials in $\mathcal{B}^{G}$}&\longleftrightarrow&\text{arc diagrams}\end{array}

This view of RSK as a transformation between linear bases seems to have been introduced by Sturmfels
[
41
]
, Herzog–Trung
[
20
]
, and Conca
[
9
]
in the context of determinantal rings; to the best of our knowledge, however, the basis
\mathcal{B}^{G}
has not arisen in the invariant theory literature.

From our viewpoint, the combinatorial advantage of
\mathcal{B}^{G}
is that its elements can naturally be viewed as
arc diagrams
, as indicated in (
1.1
).
This is due to the fact that the basis elements are ordinary monomials in certain contractions

(each of which can be viewed as an arc between vertices

The theorems in Section

In order to specialize from polynomial invariants to tensor invariants — the motivating problem in this paper — we simply restrict our attention to arc diagrams that are 1-regular (i.e., those in which each vertex has degree 1).
In this way, our theorems in Section

2.
Classical invariant theory and standard monomials

The classical groups

Throughout the paper, we let
\GL(V)
denote the
general linear group
of invertible linear operators on a finite-dimensional vector space
V
over a field
K
of characteristic 0.
If
V
is equipped with a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form
b
, then we write
\operatorname{O}(V)\subset\GL(V)
to denote the
orthogonal group
preserving
b
.
If the form is instead a nondegenerate skew-symmetric bilinear form
\omega
, then we write
\Sp(V)\subset\GL(V)
to denote the
symplectic group
preserving
\omega
.
When
G=\operatorname{O}(V)
or
\Sp(V)
, this bilinear form induces a canonical
G
-module isomorphism
V\cong V^{*}
, and for this reason we will not distinguish between
V
and its dual space
V^{*}
when considering these groups.
The
special linear group

\SL(V)\subset\GL(V)
and
special orthogonal group

\SO(V)\subset\operatorname{O}(V)
are the subgroups consisting of operators with determinant 1.
Collectively, the five families described above are known as the
classical groups
.
Throughout the paper, we use the parameter
n
to denote the dimension of
V
, except in the case
G=\Sp(V)
, where we put
\dim V=2n
.

Polynomial invariants and multidegree

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of nonnegative integers.
We write
V^{m}
or
V^{*m}
to denote the direct sum of
m
copies of
V
or its dual space
V^{*}
, respectively.
In the following exposition, when giving a unified treatment of the classical groups, we set

W\coloneqq\begin{cases}V^{*p}\oplus V^{q},&G=\GL(V)\text{ or }\SL(V),\\
V^{m},&G=\operatorname{O}(V)\text{ or }\SO(V)\text{ or }\Sp(V).\end{cases}

In the first case above, we write an element of
W
as
(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{p},v_{1},\ldots,v_{q})
, and in the second case we write an element of
W
as
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
.
The natural diagonal action of
G
on
W
extends to a linear action on the space
K[W]
of
K
-valued polynomial functions on
W
, via
(g\cdot f)(w)=f(g^{-1}\cdot w)
for all
g\in G
,
f\in K[W]
, and
w\in W
.
The object of interest in Sections
2
–
4
is the ring of invariants

K[W]^{G}\coloneqq\{f\in K[W]:f(g\cdot w)=f(w)\text{ for all $g\in G$ and $w\in W$}\}.

There is a natural multigradation on
K[W]^{G}
, which we will generally designate with a boldface
\mathbf{d}
.
For
G=\GL(V)
or
\SL(V)
, the action of the torus
(K^{\times})^{p}\times(K^{\times})^{q}
leads to an
\mathbb{N}^{p}\times\mathbb{N}^{q}
-gradation on
K[V^{*p}\oplus V^{q}]
; see
[
16
]
*p. 256.
For
G=\GL(V)
and
\SL(V)
, then, the symbol
\mathbf{d}
actually represents an ordered pair
\mathbf{d}=(\mathbf{d}_{p},\mathbf{d}_{q})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}\times\mathbb{N}^{q}
, and we write
K[V^{*p}\oplus V^{q}]_{(\mathbf{d}_{p},\mathbf{d}_{q})}
to denote the corresponding homogeneous multigraded component.
When
G=\operatorname{O}(V)
or
\SO(V)
or
\Sp(V)
, we write
K[V^{m}]_{\mathbf{d}}
where
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.
In general, we write
K[W]^{G}_{\mathbf{d}}\coloneqq K[W]^{G}\cap K[W]_{\mathbf{d}}
.

For

Standard monomials via tableaux

A
partition
is a weakly decreasing sequence of positive integers, which we denote by
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
.
We call
r
the
length
of
\lambda
, written as
\ell(\lambda)=r
.
We use the shorthand
(a^{r})\coloneqq(a,\ldots,a)
, and we can take a componentwise sum
\lambda+\mu
by setting
(\lambda+\mu)_{i}=\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}
, padding the shorter partition with zeros as necessary.
If
s=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
, then we say that
\lambda
is a partition of
s
, written as
\lambda\vdash s
.
By a
semistandard Young tableau
of shape
\lambda
, we mean a left-justified arrangement of boxes, where the
i
th row from the top contains
\lambda_{i}
many boxes, and where the boxes are filled with entries such that every row is weakly increasing and every column is strictly increasing.
Let
\SSYT(\lambda,m)
denote the set of all semistandard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
, with entries taken from the set
[m]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
The
weight
of a tableau
T\in\SSYT(\lambda,m)
is the vector
{\rm wt}(T)\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
whose
i
th component is the number of occurrences of the entry
i
in
T
.
In the case
G=\GL(V)
, we will study ordered pairs
(T,U)\in\SSYT(\lambda,p)\times\SSYT(\lambda,q)
, typically called
bitableaux
in the literature; the weight of a bitableau is the ordered pair of weights
{\rm wt}(T,U)=({\rm wt}(T),{\rm wt}(U))
.
When we refer to the
rows
and
columns
of a partition
\lambda
, we mean the rows and columns of a tableau with shape
\lambda
.
In particular,
\lambda
is said to have
even rows
(resp., columns) if every row (resp., column) of
\lambda
contains an even number of boxes.

There is a natural way to view a semistandard (bi)tableau as an element of
K[W]^{G}
, by viewing each tableau column as the set of row/column indices determining a minor (or Pfaffian) of the matrix formed by the generators
f_{ij}
, and then taking the product over all the columns in the (bi)tableau.
The elements of
K[W]^{G}
obtained in this way are called
standard monomials
, which explains our notation
\mathcal{S}^{G}
in Proposition
2.3
below.
In particular, if
T
is a semistandard tableau of shape
\lambda
, then let
T_{\ell}
denote the set of entries in the
\ell
th column of
T
.
We sometimes write a tableau as the concatenation of its columns from left to right:
T=T_{1}\cdots T_{\lambda_{1}}
.
If
I
and
J
are sets of positive integers such that
|I|=|J|\leq n
, then define the minor

(2.2)

{\rm m}(I,J)\coloneqq\det[f_{ij}]_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
j\in J\end{subarray}}.

Likewise, for
|I|
an even number at most
2n
, define the Pfaffian

(2.3)

{\rm pf}(I)\coloneqq{\rm pf}[f_{ij}]_{i,j\in I}.

Note that for
G=\operatorname{O}(V)
or
\Sp(V)
, the matrix
[f_{ij}]
is obtained by extending the definition of
f_{ij}
to all
1\leq i,j\leq m
, and is therefore symmetric or alternating, respectively.
This follows from the symmetry (resp., skew-symmetry) of the bilinear form
b
(resp.,
\omega
).

RSK correspondences and ordinary monomials

RSK correspondences

The Robinson–Schensted–Knuth (RSK) correspondence is well known for its remarkable properties and its ubiquity in algebraic combinatorics.
In Proposition
3.1
below, we recall three variations (corresponding to the three groups
\GL(V)
,
\operatorname{O}(V)
, and
\Sp(V)

The first, which we call
\RSK_{\rm A}

[
23
]
*§3; the second, which we call
\RSK_{\rm B}
, was also introduced by Knuth
[
23
]
*§5, and is described in
[
4
]
*p. 22,
[
20
]
*§1, and
[
9
]
*Thm. 1.8; the third, which we call
\RSK_{\rm C}
, is described by Knuth
[
23
]
*Thm. 4, as well as
[
4
]
*§2 and
[
20
]
*p. 25.

Referring the reader to the references above for the details in each variant (each of which consists of an “insertion” algorithm and its inverse “deletion” algorithm), we simply give the domain and codomain in Proposition
3.1
below.
Each RSK variant is a bijection between
\mathcal{S}^{G}
and a set
\mathcal{M}^{G}
consisting of nonnegative integer matrices.
We write
{\rm SM}_{m}(\mathbb{N})
to denote the set of
m\times m
symmetric matrices over
\mathbb{N}
.
Given a matrix

, we define its
support
to be
{\rm supp}(M)\coloneqq\{(i,j):m_{ij}\neq 0\}
.
Adopting the language of contingency tables, we define the
margins
of a matrix
M\in{\rm M}_{p,q}(\mathbb{N})
to be the ordered pair
((r_{1},\ldots,r_{p}),(c_{1},\ldots,c_{q}))
, where
r_{i}
denotes the sum of entries in row
i
of
M
, and
c_{j}
denotes the sum of entries in column
j
of
M
.
For
M\in{\rm SM}_{m}(\mathbb{N})
, the row and column sums are the same, so in this case the margins of
M
consist of the single vector
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})
.

In order to clearly describe the codomain
\mathcal{M}^{G}
of each RSK variant, we let
P
denote the set of ordered pairs
(i,j)
indexing the generators
f_{ij}
, as given in the top row of Table
1
.
We make
P
a poset by equipping it with the partial order
\preceq
given below in Proposition
3.1
.
Recall that a subset of
P
is said to be a

if its elements are pairwise comparable with respect to
\preceq
, and is said to be an

if no two of its elements are comparable.
The
height
(resp.,
width
) of a subset
S\subseteq P
is the cardinality of the largest chain (resp., antichain) contained in
S
.

Let

The corresponding monomial in

An

is a multigraph with self-loops on a vertex set
[m]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,m\}

As usual, the

of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it (where a self-loop contributes 2 to the degree).
We write the

m

d_{i}

i

A

-bipartite

\{1^{*},\ldots,p^{*},1,\ldots,q\}

\{i^{*},j\}
for some
i\in[p]
and

.
We depict such a diagram with a vertical line separating the
p
starred vertices from the
q
unstarred vertices.
Its degree sequence is thus written as an ordered pair
(\mathbf{d}_{p},\mathbf{d}_{q})\in\mathbb{N}^{p}\times\mathbb{N}^{q}

Two arcs are said to be a

A strict

is a set of
k
many arcs in which each pair is a strict nesting.
Vacuously, every arc is a
1

5.
Linear bases for tensor invariants via arc diagrams

Tensor (i.e., multilinear) invariants can be viewed as a special case of polynomial invariants.
As we explain below, by restricting our attention to arc diagrams which are
1-regular
(i.e., every vertex has degree 1), we obtain linear bases of tensor invariants as special cases of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{1}\coloneqq(1,\ldots,1)
.
There is a canonical isomorphism of
\GL(V)
-modules given by

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\otimes p}\otimes V^{*\otimes q}&\longrightarrow K[V^{*p}\oplus V^{q}]_{(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1})},\\
w_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes w_{p}\otimes\psi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\psi_{q}&\longmapsto\Big[(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{p},v_{1},\ldots,v_{q})\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{p}\varphi_{i}(w_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{q}\psi_{j}(v_{j})\Big]\end{split}

and extended by linearity.
By Theorem
4.1
(a)
, we see that
K[V^{*p}\oplus V^{q}]^{\GL(V)}_{(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1})}
is nonzero if and only if
p=q
, since this is the only way that a bipartite arc diagram can be 1-regular.
Therefore, from now on we will write
m
for the common parameter
p=q

Note that the set of 1-regular
(m,m)
-bipartite arc diagrams — that is, the set of
(m,m)
-bipartite

— is in natural bijection with the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{m}
: in particular, each element

is connected to the vertex

By Theorem
4.1
(a)
, this arc diagram represents the invariant polynomial
\prod_{i=1}^{m}f_{i,\sigma(i)}

(5.2)

\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in[n]^{m}}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}e_{c_{i}}\right)\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\varepsilon_{c_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}}\right),

where
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
and
\{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}\}
are dual bases for
V
and
V^{*}
, respectively.
There is an even more transparent (and coordinate-free) way to view the arc diagram as a basis element, if we view
V^{\otimes m}\otimes V^{*\otimes m}
as its own dual space, via the natural duality map
V^{*\otimes m}\cong(V^{\otimes m})^{*}

from this perspective, the perfect matching defined by

5.2
), instead represents the functional on

defined on simple tensors as follows (see
[
16
]
*§5.3.1 for further details):

(5.3)

v_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes v_{m}\otimes\varphi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\varphi_{m}\longmapsto\prod_{i=1}^{m}\varphi_{i}(v_{\sigma(i)}).

Likewise for

or

(5.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}V^{\otimes m}&\longrightarrow K[V^{m}]_{\mathbf{1}},\\

\prod_{i=1}^{m}\omega(v_{i},w_{i}),&G=\Sp(V)\end{cases}\right]\end{split}

and extended by linearity.
By parts

6.
Equivalent enumerations of basis elements

The 1-regular arc diagrams and hyperdiagrams we described in the previous section seem to give the most natural model for linear bases of tensor invariants, since arcs between pairs of vertices are viewed as contractions between pairs of vectors (or a vector and covector) in the obvious way.
If, however, one is merely interested in
enumerating
basis elements — that is, computing the dimensions of the
G
-invariant subspaces — then it is natural to seek other, perhaps more familiar combinatorial objects which are in bijection with these bases.
As it turns out, these dimensions can be counted in terms of several quite different types of objects.
Foremost among these are standard Young tableaux, which afford a uniform description of the dimension of the tensor invariants for all of the classical groups.
As far as possible, we also include in our theorems other combinatorial interpretations in terms of well-known objects.

Recall that a
standard Young tableau
of shape
\lambda\vdash m
is a semistandard tableau whose entries are
1,\ldots,m
, each occurring exactly once.
Equivalently, a semistandard tableau is standard if and only if it has weight
\mathbf{1}
.
Let
\SYT(\lambda)
denote the set of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
.
It is well known
[
14
]
*p. 53 that the number of these tableaux is given by the hook length formula
\#\SYT(\lambda)=m!/\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}{h_{\lambda}(i,j)}
, where
h_{\lambda}(i,j)
denotes the length of the hook determined by the box
(i,j)
in the Young diagram of
\lambda
.
