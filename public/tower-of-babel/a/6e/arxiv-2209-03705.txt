Losing Momentum in Continuous-time Stochastic Optimisation
Kexin Jin kexinj@math.princeton.edu, Jonas Latz jonas.latz@manchester.ac.uk, Chenguang Liu C.Liu-13@tudelft.nl, Alessandro Scagliotti scag@ma.tum.de

Abstract
The training of modern machine learning models often consists in solving high-dimensional non-convex optimisation problems that are subject to large-scale data. In this context, momentum-based stochastic optimisation algorithms have become particularly widespread. The stochasticity arises from data subsampling which reduces computational cost. Both, momentum and stochasticity help the algorithm to converge globally. In this work, we propose and analyse a continuous-time model for stochastic gradient descent with momentum. This model is a piecewise-deterministic Markov process that represents the optimiser by an underdamped dynamical system and the data subsampling through a stochastic switching. We investigate longtime limits, the subsampling-to-no-subsampling limit, and the momentum-to-no-momentum limit. We are particularly interested in the case of reducing the momentum over time. Under convexity assumptions, we show convergence of our dynamical system to the global minimiser when reducing momentum over time and letting the subsampling rate go to infinity. We then propose a stable, symplectic discretisation scheme to construct an algorithm from our continuous-time dynamical system. In experiments, we study our scheme in convex and non-convex test problems. Additionally, we train a convolutional neural network in an image classification problem. Our algorithm attains competitive results compared to stochastic gradient descent with momentum.

Introduction

Machine learning and artificial intelligence play a fundamental role in modern scientific research and modern life. In many instances, the underlying learning process consists of solving a high-dimensional non-convex optimisation problem with respect to large-scale data. These problems have been approached by applicants and researchers using a large range of different algorithms. Methods are based on, e.g. stochastic approximation, statistical mechanics, and ideas from biological evolution. Many of these methods deviate from simply solving the optimisation problem, but are actually also used for regularisation or approximate uncertainty quantification. Unfortunately, many successfully employed methods are theoretically hardly understood.

In this article, we investigate momentum-based stochastic optimisation methods in a continuous-time framework. We now introduce the optimisation problem, motivate stochastic and momentum-based optimisation, review past work, and summarise our contributions and main results.

1.1
Optimisation and continuous dynamics

We study optimisation problems of the form

\min_{\theta\in X}\bar{\Phi}(\theta),

(1)

on the space
X:=\mathbb{R}^{K}
, where

\bar{\Phi}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Phi_{i}(\theta).

We denote the number of terms by
N\in\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}

and assume that the functions
\Phi_{i}
, with
i\in I:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
, are continuously differentiable with Lipschitz gradients. We assume that (
1
) is well-defined and denote
\theta^{*}:\in\mathrm{argmin}\,\bar{\Phi}
. In this work, we study continuous-time dynamical systems that can be used to optimise functions of type
\bar{\Phi}
and to represent the dynamics of optimisation algorithms. The most basic of these dynamical systems is the
gradient flow

\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta_{t}}{\mathrm{dt}}=-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(\zeta_{t}),\qquad\zeta_{0}\in X,

(2)

a continuous-time version of the
gradient descent method
.
The ODE solution
(\zeta(t))_{t\geq 0}
converges to a stationary point of
\bar{\Phi}
under, e.g. certain convexity conditions. In practice, especially in modern machine learning, we often encounter optimisation problems that are non-convex, where the solution
(\zeta(t))_{t\geq 0}
may converge to a saddle point or a local minimiser. When discretising (
2
), the iterates actually only converge to (local) minimisers, see, e.g.

Lee et al. 2016
;
Panageas and Piliouras 2017
.
However, as observed in
Du et al. 2017
, the algorithm could take a lot of iterations to escape a saddle point.

The
stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
method going back to
Robbins and Monro 1951
has been a popular alternative to the gradient descent method.
In SGD, we randomly select one of the
\Phi_{i}

(i\in I)
and optimise with respect to that function before switching to another
\Phi_{j}

(j\in I\backslash\{i\})
. This process is repeated until a stationary state is reached. A continuous-time model for stochastic gradient descent has recently proposed by
Latz 2021
and extended by
Jin et al. 2023
and
Latz 2022
.
There, stochastic gradient descent is represented through the dynamical system

\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_{t}}{\mathrm{dt}}=-\nabla\Phi_{i(t)}(\theta_{t}),\qquad\theta_{0}\in X,

(3)

where the
index process

(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a suitable homogeneous continuous-time Markov process on
I
. The processes
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
,
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and any other stochastic processes and random variables throughout this work are defined on the underlying probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. The process
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
represents a randomised gradient flow whose potential is randomly replaced by another one when
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
jumps from one state to another. Both
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(i(t),\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are called
stochastic gradient process
. Since
\bar{\Phi}
often represents the averaging over data subsets, we refer to the process of replacing
\bar{\Phi}
by a
\Phi_{i}
as
subsampling
.

SGD has two main advantages:
Since we consider only one of the
\nabla\Phi_{i}
at a time, we significantly reduce the computational cost when discretising the dynamical system.
Moreover, the perturbation through the randomised sampling in stochastic gradient descent can help to overcome saddle points, see
Daneshmand et al. 2018
;
Jin et al. 2021
. However, as the method is purely gradient-based, we would expect that the escaping saddle points is still slow.
Saddle points and local minimisers can sometimes be escaped when using momentum-based optimisation methods. Here, we replace the gradient flow (
2
), by the
underdamped gradient flow
given through

m\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}-\alpha\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{t})=0,\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{0}}{\mathrm{d}t}\in X,\quad q_{0}\in X.

(4)

This dynamical system describes the motion of a ball with mass
m>0
constrained to slide on the graph
\bar{\Phi}
subject to a constant gravitational field and viscosity friction
\alpha>0
, see
Attouch et al. 2000
for details.
Intuitively, momentum is used to vault the optimiser out of saddle points and local minimisers.

1.2
Momentum

We now give a motivating example where the relevance of momentum in non-convex optimisation can be observed and then summarise relevant literature on momentum-based optimisation. The example is based on
a simple one-dimensional
non-smooth non-convex optimisation problem
inspired by the training of neural networks.
As we shall see, the introduction of momentum
can prevent the convergence of the gradient
flow to a stationary point that is not the global
minimiser. We later work in a framework that does not actually cover this introductory example, we present it only to give an intuition for the methodology.

Let us consider the function

\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\Phi(x):=(\mathrm{ReLU}(x)-1)^{2}+x^{2}=\begin{cases}x^{2}+1&\mbox{if }x\leq 0\\
2x^{2}-2x+1&\mbox{if }x>0\end{cases}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R})

(5)

where
\mathrm{ReLU}(\cdot):=\max\{0,\cdot\}
.

Figure 1:
Plot of the potential
\Phi
.

As shown in Figure
1
, the function
\Phi
attains its global
minimum at the point
x^{*}=\frac{1}{2}
.
However,
\tilde{x}=0

is the minimiser of

\Phi
restricted to the
non-positive half-line
(-\infty,0]
. Therefore,
any solution of the gradient flow equation (
2
)
with potential
\bar{\Phi}:=\Phi
and
initial value
\zeta_{0}<0
converges to the
saddle point
\tilde{x}
.
In order to avoid the convergence to

\tilde{x}
, we can replace the
gradient flow by the associated underdamped gradient flow (
4
) with mass
m>0
, viscosity friction
\alpha>0
, and potential
\bar{\Phi}
.
If the initial value
q_{0}

is negative,
then the equation of the motion reduces
to

m\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}+2q_{t}=-\alpha\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(6)

whenever the dynamical system stays
in the negative half-line.
At this point the natural question is
whether there exist combinations of

m
and
\alpha
such that,
assuming that

q_{0}<0
, the particle is not confined
in the negative half-line for every
time
t>0
.
Using elementary theory of linear
second-order differential equations, it turns
out that the particle manages to overcome the
“false” minimiser
\tilde{x}=0

if the following relation is satisfied:

\alpha^{2}-8m<0,

(7)

i.e. the friction
\alpha
is sufficiently small or the mass
m
sufficiently large. In summary, momentum can be used to overcome stationary points of the target function.

The use of momentum in optimisation dates back to the 1960s, when Polyak formulated the
heavy ball method
in convex optimisation, see
Polyak 1963
;
Polyak 1964
. In this context, the aim was to accelerate the convergence of the gradient descent method. Later, Nesterov proposed a momentum-based class of discrete accelerated methods for convex problems in his seminal paper
Nesterov 1983
.
Momentum-based optimisation methods have been investigated thoroughly from the perspective of continuous-time dynamical systems. In-depth studies about the mechanical systems underlying Polyak’s method can be found in
Attouch et al. 2000
– a work which has sparked considerable interest in the community. We recall, for instance,
Attouch and Alvarez 2000
which have studied constrained convex optimisation.
In
Su et al. 2014
, the authors have formulated a dissipative second order ODE that provides a continuous-time counterpart to the Nesterov method. Here, the viscosity friction parameter
\alpha
is time-dependent and vanishing as
t\to\infty
. This aspect was later further investigated in
Attouch et al. 2018
;
Shi et al. 2019
;
Shi et al. 2021
;
Scagliotti and Colli Franzone 2022
. In
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
, the authors combine vanishing viscosity with time-scaling of trajectories, leading to a dynamics similar to (
4
), but with an extra time-dependent coefficient
\beta(t)
appearing in front of the term
\nabla\bar{\Phi}
, i.e.

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\theta(t)-\alpha(t)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\theta(t)+\beta(t)\nabla\bar{\Phi}(\theta(t))=0.

(8)

The parameter
\beta
satisfies
\beta(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

Finally, we mention more recent advances on second-order dynamics with time scaling that include Hessian-driven damping
Attouch et al. 2020
and averaging
Attouch et al. 2022
.

Next, we see how momentum is used within stochastic optimisation practice.

1.3
Momentum-based stochastic optimisation and the Adam algorithm

The most basic momentum-based stochastic optimisation method combines a symplectic Euler discretisation of the underdamped gradient flow (
4
) with subsampling. This
stochastic gradient descent method with classical momentum
is given by

\begin{cases}v_{n}=\alpha v_{n-1}-\eta\nabla\Phi_{i_{n}}(\theta_{n-1}),\\
\theta_{n}=\theta_{n-1}+{v_{n}},\end{cases}

(9)

where
\alpha,\eta>0
are hyperparameters and
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots\sim\mathrm{Unif}(I)
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables. We refer to
\eta
as learning rate and refer to (
9
) just as
classical momentum
. The connection to the underdamped gradient flow is more obvious when considering the timestep size to be equal to
1
,
\eta
to be a scaling factor of the potential, and
\alpha
to be a parameter that incorporates the velocity at the previous timestep and the friction. Classical momentum has been studied by
Rumelhart et al. 1986
;
Sutskever et al. 2013
. In deep learning, more advanced momentum-based descent methods have been widely in use, including Adagrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
; Adadelta
(
Zeiler 2012
)
; RMSprop
(
Hinton 2012
)
; Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
, etc. Adam is one of the most commonly used optimisers when training neural networks. The updating rule is the following,

\begin{cases}u_{n}=\beta_{1}u_{n-1}+(1-\beta_{1})\nabla\Phi_{i_{n}}(\theta_{n-1}),\\
v_{n}=\beta_{2}v_{n-1}+(1-\beta_{2})\big[\nabla\Phi_{i_{n}}(\theta_{n-1})\big]^{2},\\
\theta_{n}=\theta_{n-1}-\alpha\frac{u_{n}/(1-\beta_{1}^{n})}{\sqrt{v_{n}/(1-\beta_{2}^{n})}+\beta_{3}},\end{cases}

(10)

where
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\alpha
are hyperparameters,
\beta_{3}
is a small constant, and
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots\sim\mathrm{Unif}(I)
(i.i.d.). Comparing to the classical momentum method, Adam uses not only an estimate of the first moment of the gradient but also an estimate of the second moment as the part of the adaptive learning rate.
The variable
u_{n}
is the biased first moment, similar to the one used in the classical momentum method. The variable
v_{n}
is the biased second moment estimated using the gradient squared. When updating
\theta_{n}
, Adam normalises
u_{n}
and
v_{n}
so that they become unbiased estimators. Moreover, it was shown
in
Défossez et al. 2022
that Adam converges in the sense of
Zinkevich 2003
with speed
O(\log(t)/\sqrt{t};t\rightarrow\infty)
.

While momentum-based stochastic optimisation methods are popular in machine learning practice, they are overall rather badly understood; see the discussion in
Liu et al. 2020
. In the present work, we analyse a continuous-time stochastic gradient process with momentum and propose an efficient discretisation technique for this dynamical system. Thus, we improve the understanding of momentum-based stochastic optimisation in a theoretical framework and machine learning practice. Our analysis complements the Langevin-based analyses by
Li et al. 2019
;
Shi 2022
, where the authors represent SGD in continuous time as a diffusion process. Before we summarise our contributions more precisely, we introduce our continuous-time stochastic gradient-momentum process.

1.4
The stochastic gradient-momentum process

Throughout this work, we discuss a continuous-time dynamical system that represents stochastic gradient descent with momentum – the stochastic gradient-momentum process. Due to the continuous-time nature of the system, it can equally represent classical momentum (
9
) and Adam (
10
) (although not the adaptive nature of the latter).
We obtain this system by combining the underdamped dynamical system (
4
) with the stochastic gradient process (
3
).
We now introduce the
index process
, the process that controls the subsampling.

Definition 1

(Index process)
.

Let
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous-time Markov process on
I
with transition rate matrix

\mathbf{A}_{N}=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{N}-N\gamma\mathbf{I}_{N}.

Here,
\gamma>0
denotes the jump rate,
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{N}
is an
N\times N
matrix whose entries are all equal to
\gamma
, and
\mathbf{I}_{N}
is the identity matrix. We assume that the initial distribution
\mathbb{P}(i(0)\in\cdot)
is the uniform distribution on
I
.
Moreover, when given a value
\nu>0
or an appropriate, strictly increasing function
\beta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, we define the rescaled index processes
i^{\nu}(t):=i(t/\nu)
and
i^{\beta}(t):=i(\beta^{-1}(t))
, for
t\geq 0
. We refer to each of
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
,
(i^{\nu}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, and
(i^{\beta}(t))_{t\geq 0}
as
index process
.

The function
\beta^{-1}
and the scalar
1/\nu
play the role of a time-dependent and constant learning rate, respectively; see
Jin et al. 2023
. Thus, we sometimes refer to them as learning rate.
Next, we make the following regularity assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

Let
\Phi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
, i.e. it is continuously differentiable, and
\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i}
be Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
L>0
, for
i\in I
.

Throughout this work, we study multiple versions of the stochastic gradient-momentum process and introduce them in Definitions
4
,
8
, and
17
.
We now introduce the prototype of the dynamical systems studied in this paper.
Note that we often choose the more compact form
\mathrm{d}z_{t}=f(z_{t})\mathrm{d}t
to represent
\frac{\mathrm{d}z_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=f(z_{t})
.
The general
stochastic gradient-momentum process
(SGMP) is given by
(p^{\dagger}_{t},q^{\dagger}_{t},i^{\beta}(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfying

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q_{t}^{\dagger}&=p_{t}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}t,\\
m(t)\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\dagger}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i^{\beta}(t)}(q_{t}^{\dagger})\mathrm{d}t-\alpha p_{t}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}t,\\
p^{\dagger}(t=0)&=p_{0},\\
q^{\dagger}(t=0)&=q_{0},\end{array}\right.

(11)

where
(p^{\dagger}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
describes the velocity of the particle,
(q^{\dagger}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
its position,
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
its mass (that may depend on time), and
\alpha>0
the viscocity friction. The function
\beta
controls the switching of the index process. In the following, we explain the importance behind the functions
m
and
\beta
.

Remark 2

(Mass
m
)
.

The introduction of momentum allows the particle to be vaulted out of stationary points. An effect that is particularly pronounced if
m
is large or
\alpha
is small. While this behaviour is convenient when escaping local minimisers and saddle points, it can also occur when approaching global minimisers. We give a very simple example in Figure
2
, where we see that the method shows oscillatory behaviour and very slow convergence when the mass
m
is large and constant. As a tuning of the mass
m
is difficult in practice, we suggest an alternative strategy: reducing the mass over time. Rationale: a large mass in the beginning leads to a fast escaping of local minimisers,
while a small mass later leads to fast convergence once the global minimiser is reached. This intuition is confirmed in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
We consider the minimisation of
\bar{\Phi}(\theta):=\theta^{2}/2
. Let
\alpha=1,m=1
,
p_{0}=1
,
q_{0}=0
. Then,
q_{t}=\exp(-t/2)\left(\sin(\sqrt{3}t/2)/\sqrt{3}+\cos(\sqrt{3}t/2)\right)(t\geq 0)
(solid line)
oscillates around the solution and converges ultimately to
\theta^{*}=0
. If we choose instead
m(t)=(1+t)^{-1}

(t\geq 0)
(dashed lines), we have
q_{t}=\exp(-t^{2}/2)
, which does not oscillate, but converges very quickly to
\theta^{*}
.

In
Attouch et al. 2000
, the authors have considered the singular perturbation of (
4
), corresponding to letting the mass
m\to 0
, and they established convergence of these dynamics to the gradient flow trajectories. In the present work, we also consider a vanishing mass and carefully bound the Euclidean distance between underdamped gradient flow and gradient flow. Moreover, we let
m
depend on the time, and investigate the case
m(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. This setting is conceptually similar to the dynamics studied in
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
that we give in (
8
). However,
\beta(t)\rightarrow\infty
as
t\rightarrow\infty
is not equivalent to the mass going to zero. We can rewrite the underdamped gradient flow to

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\theta(t)-\frac{\alpha}{m(t)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\theta(t)+\underbrace{\frac{1}{m(t)}}_{=\beta(t)}\nabla\Phi(\theta(t))=0,

(12)

where the factor multiplied by the velocity explodes as well, as
m(t)\rightarrow 0
. Thus, we cannot rely on the results obtained in
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
for the dynamics (
8
).
Moreover, we shall not actually focus our attention on the deterministic evolution, but we rather investigate a stochastic process related to the ODE (
12
).
However, an interesting similarity between our approach and
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
arises when discretising the respective dynamics. Indeed, the fact that
\beta(t)\to\infty
in (
8
) and
m(t)\to 0
in (
12
) make explicit schemes not suitable for deriving discrete approximations. In
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
this issue is addressed by considering a
proximal
discretisation, while in Section
4
we opt for a symplectic (semi-implicit) scheme.

Remark 3

(Learning rate and
\beta
)
.

The switching between the potentials leads to a similar effect as the momentum. As the flow approaches the minimisers of different target functions in each step, we are unlikely to converge to a single point. While this helps to escape local minimisers, it prohibits the convergence to a global minimiser. In several stochastic optimisation methods, we need to reduce the learning rate or step-size throughout the algorithm to reduce the time in-between switches of data sets. We can attain this reduction of learning rate by rescaling the time in the index process
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
through an appropriate function
\beta
. The rescaling will lead to the waiting times between two switches to decrease over time.

We now list and explain more assumptions that are required throughout this work. Indeed, some results of this work require the
(\Phi_{i})_{i\in I}
to be
(
\mu
-)strongly convex
, i.e.

\displaystyle(x-x^{\prime})\cdot(\nabla\Phi_{i}(x)-\nabla\Phi_{i}(x^{\prime}))\geq\mu\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|^{2}\qquad(x,x^{\prime}\in X,i\in I),

for some strong convexity parameter
\mu>0
. Others do not rely on strong convexity, but still need some restriction on the potential function, as given in the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

Let
\Phi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
admit a global minimiser
\theta^{i}_{*}
, for
i\in I
, and let there be constants
\lambda\in(0,1/4]
and
\bar{\alpha}>0
such that

(x-\theta^{i}_{*})\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i}(x)/2\geq\lambda(\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})+\bar{\alpha}^{2}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4),\qquad(x\in X,i\in I).

(13)

We use
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}
as a short hand to refer to (
13
) with parameters
\lambda
and
\bar{\alpha}
.
We note that Assumption
2
is also known as the quasi-strong convexity, see, e.g.
Aujol et al. 2022
;
Eberle et al. 2017
;
Necoara et al. 2019
.
Assumption
2
is implied by strong convexity. We prove this assertion in Lemma
30
in the appendix. In the appendix, we also give a counterexample showing that the two assumptions are not equivalent, see Example
29
.

In stochastic optimisation, it is often necessary to decrease the learning rate over time to obtain a method that converges to the minimiser. In practice, however, this is sometimes purposefully disregarded – convergence to, e.g. a probability distribution is preferred. In stochastic optimisation this leads to an
implicit regularisation
of the optimisation problem. Particularly in machine learning, this implicit regularisation can be necessary to get good generalisation results, see, for example,
Sekhari et al. 2021
;
Keskar et al. 2017
;
Neyshabur et al. 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Zou et al. 2021
. Thus, we also study this constant learning rate case in the present work. Similarly, whilst we have seen that a decreasing mass can be beneficial, we also study the case of a constant mass.
Indeed, when the mass
m
does not depend on
t
or the function
\beta
is linear, i.e.
\beta(u)=\nu u
, we refer to mass or learning rate as
homogeneous
, respectively. Since we are otherwise always interested in reducing them over time, we speak of
decreasing
mass or learning rate, respectively.
When decreasing mass and learning rate, an additional assumption – Assumption
4
– is needed. Since this assumption is rather technical, we delay its statement and discuss it instead along with the associated convergence result in Subsection
3.2
.

In order to establish well-posedness of the system, the mass
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
must not decrease too quickly. Here, we have the following assumption.

Assumption 3
.

There exists a constant
\lambda\in(0,1]
, such that
\left|m^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq\lambda m(t)
,
t>0
. Moreover, we assume that
m(0)=:m_{0}\leq 1
.

It is easy to verify that
m(t)=\frac{m_{0}}{\lambda^{-1}+t}
and
m(t)=m_{0}e^{-\lambda t}
satisfy Assumption
3
. Setting
m_{0}=\lambda=1
, we retrieve the example in Figure
2
. More generally, Assumption
3
implies
m(t)\geq m_{0}e^{-\lambda t}
. Hence, the mass decreases at most exponentially with a rate
\geq-1
.

1.5
Contributions and main results

We have introduced the stochastic gradient-momentum process above. In this work, we analyse this dynamical system, discuss its discretisation, and employ it in numerical experiments.
We are interested in both, convergence to global minimisers and implicit regularisation. Thus, we study three different stochastic gradient-momentum processes:
(1) homogeneous mass and homogeneous learning rate,
(2) decreasing mass and homogeneous learning rate, and
(3) decreasing mass and decreasing learning rate, respectively.
We now summarise our contributions:

•

We study the connection between SGMP and the gradient flow, the underdamped gradient flow, as well as the stochastic gradient process. These connections allow us to investigate the longtime behaviour of the stochastic gradient-momentum process.

We obtain those connections by considering momentum-to-no-momentum asymptotics (letting a homogeneous mass
m
approach
0
), and random-to-deterministic asymptotics (for a homogeneous
\beta(u)=\nu u
, letting
\nu
approach
0
).
Understanding the longtime behaviour eventually allows us to study the convergence of the underlying algorithm.
We summarise our three main results below and also give informal statements of the corresponding theorems – the complete versions are given later in the text.

•

In the fully homogeneous case, we show that the stochastic gradient-momentum process is close to the stochastic gradient process when the mass is small. This can be understood as the stochastic version of the deterministic result established in
Attouch et al. 2000
.

Theorem
6
(informal)

Let
(q_{t}^{m})_{t\geq 0}
be the stochastic gradient-momentum process (
15
) with constant mass
m
and
(\theta_{t}^{m})_{t\geq 0}
be the stochastic gradient process (
16
). Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\left\|q^{m}_{t}-\theta^{m}_{t}\right\|\Big]\to 0\ \ \ \ (m\downarrow 0).

•

If the mass decreases over time, the stochastic gradient-momentum process converges to the stochastic gradient process in the longtime limit.

Theorem

Let

•

If mass and learning rate decrease over time, the stochastic gradient-momentum process converges to the minimiser of the full target function in the longtime limit.

Surprisingly, the continuous relationship shown in Figure

Especially problematic is the instability of classical momentum when the mass is small. The step-size needs to be chosen as
O(m;m\downarrow 0)
. From this computational perspective, our contributions are the following:

•

We propose a discretisation strategy for the stochastic gradient-momentum process. The strategy is a semi-implicit method that is explicit in the position
(q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and implicit in the velocity
(p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. This discretisation technique allows us to choose the step-size independently of the mass
m
. Thus, the method is stable for small masses as well as masses converging to zero.
Similarly to the symplectic Euler method that is conventionally used in momentum-based optimisation, our scheme is symplectic. Thus, it respects (local) conservation laws in the underdamped dynamics.

•

We test our discretised algorithm in numerical experiments. We start with academic convex and non-convex optimisation problems in low and high dimensions. Then we study the training of a convolutional neural network (CNN), which we use to classify images from the CIFAR-10 dataset. The achieved train and test accuracy with our stable discretisation is comparable to stochastic gradient descent with classical momentum. Some of these experiments go beyond the convex setting that we study in our analysis.

Homogeneous-in-time

Definition 4
.

The

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q^{\nu,m}_{t}&=p^{\nu,m}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\

q^{\nu,m}_{0}&=q_{0}\in X,\end{array}\right.

(14)

where
\alpha,m,\nu>0
are
the viscosity friction
parameter, the mass, and the learning rate, respectively – all kept constant.
Finally,
\Phi_{j}
satisfies Assumption

j\in I

is given as in Definition

. In the following, we denote

(i)

and varying the learning rate

.

(ii)

, for some

, i.e. varying the mass, and choosing a constant or a mass-depending learning rate

.

The Lipschitz condition in Assumption

;

2.1
Momentum with and without subsampling

First, we study the interplay between subsampling and not subsampling in the momentum dynamics. Indeed, we show that the stochastic gradient-momentum process can approximate the underdamped gradient flow at any accuracy. More precisely, we are interested in the limiting behavior of hSGMP
(p^{\nu}_{t},q^{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as the learning rate approaches zero uniformly, i.e. we take
\nu\to 0
in (
14
). We prove that the hSGMP converges to the solution to the underdamped gradient flow, which is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q_{t}&=p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}p_{t}&=-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\alpha p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
p_{0},q_{0}&\in X,\end{array}\right.

(15)

where we implicitly set the mass
m:=1
. In this case, we show that hSGMP is a stochastic approximation to the underdamped gradient flow.

We now formulate the statement about the convergence of hSGMP to the underdamped gradient flow more particularly.
In principle, we show convergence of a random path to a deterministic path – both are contained in the space of continuous functions from
[0,\infty)
to
X^{2}
which we denote by
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),X^{2})
and equip with the metric

\rho\Big((\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0},(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}\Big):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\left(1\land\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\left\|\zeta_{s}-\xi_{s}\right\|\right)\mathrm{d}t,

where as usual
a\land b:=\min\{a,b\}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Probabilistically, we show convergence in the weak sense. We state and prove the convergence result below.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(q^{\nu}_{t},p^{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(q_{t},p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
14
) and (
15
). Then
(q^{\nu}_{t},p^{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges weakly to
(q_{t},p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty),X^{2})
as
\nu\to 0
, i.e. for any bounded continuous function

\mathbb{E}[F\big((p^{\nu}_{t},q^{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\big)]\to\mathbb{E}[F\big((p_{t},q_{t})_{t\geq 0}\big)]=F\big((p_{t},q_{t})_{t\geq 0}\big).

Proof.

We apply Theorem 4.3 in
Kushner 1990
, which studies tightness and weak convergence properties of solutions of a certain type of stochastic differential equations. The form of the differential equation is given in (2.2)
(
Kushner 1990
, Chapter 7)
, we also recall this result in Appendix
D
. This corresponds to equation (
15
) with
\xi_{t}=i(t)
,
\bar{G}(x,y)=(y,-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)-\alpha y)
,
\tilde{G}(x,y,i)=(0,-\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i}(x)+\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x))
, and
F=0
. In
Kushner 1990
, the differential operator
A
in our case is defined in (
14
), i.e.
Ah(x,y)=-\nabla h(x,y)\cdot(y,-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)-\alpha y)
for any

twice differentiable. If Assumptions (A4.2) to (A4.6) from
Kushner 1990
were satisfied, applying Theorem 4.3 in
(
Kushner 1990
, Chapter 7)
would imply that
(q^{\nu}_{t},p^{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight and the limit of any weakly convergent subsequence solves (
14
). Since
q^{\nu}_{0}=q_{0}
and

, we have
(q^{\nu}_{t},p^{\nu}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges weakly to
(q_{t},p_{t})_{t\geq 0}

Therefore, we just need to verify Assumptions (A4.2) to (A4.6) from

is càdlàg and bounded. Assumptions (A4.4) and (A4.6) trivially hold since

. The only non-trivial part is to verify Assumption (A4.5), that is as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t}^{t+\tau}\mathbb{E}[-\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i(s)}(x)+\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)|(i(s^{\prime}))_{s^{\prime}\leq t}]\mathrm{d}s\to 0,

almost surely.
By the Markov property of

and since

is stationary,

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t}^{t+\tau}\mathbb{E}[-\nabla_{x}

\displaystyle\Phi_{i(s)}(x)+\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)|\{i(s^{\prime})\}_{s^{\prime}\leq t}]\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{i(t)}[-\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i(s)}(x)+\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)]\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{i(t)}[\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i(s)}(x)-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)]\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big|\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i(s)}(x)-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)]\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big|\int_{0}^{\tau}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1-\exp(-Ns)}{N}\nabla_{x}\Phi_{j}(x)\Big)+\exp(-Ns)\Phi_{k}(x)-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Big|\int_{0}^{\tau}\exp(-Ns)\Big(\nabla_{x}\Phi_{k}(x)-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)\Big)\mathrm{d}s\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left|\nabla_{x}\Phi_{k}(x)-\nabla_{x}\bar{\Phi}(x)\right|\to 0.

as

.
∎

Hence, hSGMP

. Assumption

as

2.2
Subsampling with and without momentum

3
Heterogeneous-in-time

In the previous section, we have studied the effect of losing momentum and reducing the learning rate in a uniform fashion. Having a homogeneous mass and learning rate is unquestionably very popular in practice. However, while we could show convergence to the minimiser of the deterministic dynamics, this is not clear for the stochastic dynamics. To obtain convergence in SGMP, we need to reduce both, learning rate and momentum, over time. Hence, we need to discuss a heterogeneous version of the SGMP. Indeed, we now allow the mass
m
to be a non-constant function of time, especially to be reduced to zero over time, and the index process
(i^{\beta}(t))_{t\geq 0}
to have waiting times that get smaller as time progresses. Thus we study the case where the stochastic gradient-momentum process converges to the stochastic gradient process and the gradient flow, respectively, at runtime. We have already seen the advantage of reducing mass over time in the deterministic setting in Figure
2
.

We split this section into two parts. In Subsection
3.1
, we let only the momentum depend on and decrease over time. We introduce the dynamical system, show well-posedness, and discuss the longtime behaviour of the new dynamical system. We see that asymptotically, the dynamical system behaves like the stochastic gradient process. Then, in Subsection
3.2
, we decrease mass and learning rate over time. In this case, we discuss a setting, in which we see convergence of the stochastic dynamical system to the minimiser
\theta_{*}
of the target function
\bar{\Phi}
.

3.1
Losing momentum over time

We consider the stochastic gradient-momentum process with a time-dependent, decreasing mass.

Definition 8
.

The
decreasing-mass stochastic gradient-momentum process
(dmSGMP) is a solution of the following stochastic differential equation,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}\tilde{q}_{t}&=\tilde{p}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
m(t)\mathrm{d}\tilde{p}_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i(t)}(\tilde{q}_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\tilde{p}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
\tilde{p}_{0},\tilde{q}_{0}&\in X,\end{array}\right.

(19)

where
\Phi_{j}
satisfies Assumption
1
,
j=1,\cdots,N
. The stochastic process
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
is defined in Definition
1
. The mass
m(t)>0
is strictly decreasing and differentiable with
\lim_{t\to\infty}m(t)=0.

The formal limit of (
19
) is the stochastic gradient process without momentum, as given in (
3
). As in the previous section, we start our discussion considering the decreasing mass case with a fixed index, i.e. the following dynamical system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q^{i}_{t}&=p^{i}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
m(t)\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})\mathrm{d}t-p^{i}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
p^{i}_{0},q^{i}_{0}&\in X,\end{array}\right.

(20)

where the mass
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
is chosen as before. In addition, we denote
\mathcal{E}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}1/m(s)\mathrm{d}s.
The associated limiting equation is given by (
43
).
Next, we discuss the well-posedness of the system (
20
).

Well-posedness

We study the well-posedness of the deterministic dynamical system (
20
). Well-posedness of the stochastic dynamical system (
19
) then follows from, e.g. Proposition 1 in
Latz 2021
.

Proposition 9
.

For any fixed
i\in I
, let
\nabla\Phi_{i}
satisfy Assumption
1
with constant
L
and
m(t)
satisfy Assumption
3
. Then (
20
) admits a unique solution.

Proof.

We first rewrite equation (
20
) as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q^{i}_{t}&=p^{i}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})/m(t)\mathrm{d}t-p^{i}_{t}/m(t)\mathrm{d}t,\\
p^{i}_{0},q^{i}_{0}&\in X.\end{array}\right.

We define a map
\mathcal{T}_{0}:\mathcal{C}([0,t_{1}],\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}([0,t_{1}],\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{C}([0,t_{1}],\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}([0,t_{1}],\mathbb{R}),
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}(x,y)=\Big(q_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}\mathrm{d}s,\ p_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\Phi_{i}(x_{s})/m(s)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}y_{s}/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big)

where
0\leq t\leq t_{1}.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{T}_{0}
is a contraction for
t_{1}
small enough. Since
m(t)
is strictly decreasing and positive and using Assumption
3
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle(e^{\lambda t}m(t))^{\prime}\geq 0\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ m(t)\geq m_{0}e^{-\lambda t}.

We then conclude

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{T}_{0}(q,p)-\mathcal{T}_{0}(\hat{q},\hat{p})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq t_{1}\left\|p-\hat{p}\right\|_{\infty}+L\left\|q-\hat{q}\right\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{m(s)}\mathrm{d}s+\left\|p-\hat{p}\right\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{1}{m(s)}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq(\left\|q-\hat{q}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|p-\hat{p}\right\|_{\infty})\frac{L+1}{\lambda m_{0}}(e^{\lambda t_{1}}-1+t_{1}).

Set
t_{1}=\frac{\lambda m_{0}}{2(L+1)(2\lambda+1)}=:C(\lambda,m_{0},L)
, and since
e^{\lambda x}-1\leq 2\lambda x
when
\lambda x\leq 1,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{L+1}{\lambda m_{0}}(e^{\lambda t_{1}}-1+t_{1})\leq\frac{L+1}{\lambda m_{0}}(2\lambda+1)t_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Then
\mathcal{T}_{0}
is contracting with constant smaller than
1/2.
By the Banach Fixed Point Theorem, we conclude that (
20
) admits a unique solution on the interval
[0,t_{1}]
with initial value
(q_{0},p_{0}).
Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}:\mathcal{C}([t_{n},t_{n+1}],\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}([t_{n},t_{n+1}],\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{C}([t_{n},t_{n+1}],\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}([t_{n},t_{n+1}],\mathbb{R}),
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}(x,y)=\Big(q_{t_{n}}+\int_{t_{n}}^{t}y_{s}/m(s)\mathrm{d}s,\ p_{t_{n}}-\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\nabla\Phi_{i}(x_{s})/m(s)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t_{n}}^{t}y_{s}/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big).

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{T}_{n}(q,p)-\mathcal{T}_{n}(\hat{q},\hat{p})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(t_{n+1}-t_{n})\left\|p-\hat{p}\right\|_{\infty}+L\left\|q-\hat{q}\right\|_{\infty}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\frac{1}{m(s)}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|p-\hat{p}\right\|_{\infty}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\frac{1}{m(s)}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq(\left\|q-\hat{q}\right\|+\left\|p-\hat{p}\right\|_{\infty})\frac{L+1}{\lambda m_{0}}e^{\lambda t_{n}}(e^{\lambda h_{n}}-1+h_{n}),

where
h_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}.
Set
h_{n}=C(\lambda,m_{0},L)e^{-\lambda t_{n}}=\frac{\lambda m_{0}e^{-\lambda t_{n}}}{2(L+1)(2\lambda+1)}
, then

\displaystyle\frac{L+1}{\lambda m_{0}}e^{\lambda t_{n}}(e^{\lambda h_{n}}-1+h_{n})\leq\frac{L+1}{\lambda m_{0}}e^{\lambda t_{n}}(2\lambda+1)h_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

This implies
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is a contraction, and by the Banach Fixed Point Theorem, we conclude that (
20
) admits a unique solution on the interval
[t_{n},t_{n+1}]
with initial value
(\tilde{q}_{t_{n}},\tilde{p}_{t_{n}})
. Therefore, by Lemma
33
, we have
t_{n}\to\infty,
and, thus, (
20
) admits a unique solution globally.
∎

As the unique solution to the dynamical system (
20
) is a strong solution, the solution is continuously differentiable. Thus, the map
(p^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0})\mapsto(p^{i}_{t},p^{i}_{t})
is continuous for any
t\geq 0
. Hence, the differential equations (
19
) and (
20
) are well-posed.

Longtime behavior

Having understood the well-posedness of dmSGMP, we are now interested in its longtime behaviour. By reducing the mass over time, we aim to reduce the momentum and prove convergence to the stochastic gradient process (
3
).

To show this convergence result, we now collect a number of auxiliary results that mainly concern the deterministic system (
20
) that considers a single, fixed sample. Some of these results may remind the reader of similar results shown in Appendix
B
regarding the fixed sample, homogeneous momentum dynamics; we make these connections clear in the titles of the following results. The intermediate goal is to show a bound between the system (
20
) and its limiting equation (
43
). From there we then go back to the randomised setting and discuss the longtime behaviour and convergence of dmSGMP.
We first show that the deterministic system converges at exponential speed to its unique stationary point.

Lemma 10

(cf. Lemma
25
)
.

Let
(p^{i}_{t},q^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
20
). Let
\theta_{*}^{i}
be the critical point of
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in I
. Under the Assumptions
1
,
2
with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda_{i},2}
, and
3
with
\lambda=\min_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq 8e^{-\lambda t}V^{i}(0,q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0})\qquad(t\geq 0),

(21)

where
V^{i}(t,x,y)=m(t)(\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*}))+\frac{1}{4}\Big(\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}+m(t)y\right\|^{2}+\left\|m(t)y\right\|^{2}\Big).

Proof.

By Lemma
34
, we have

\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq 8V^{i}(t,q^{i}_{t},p^{i}_{t})\leq 8e^{-\lambda t}V^{i}(0,q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0}).

∎

In the next auxiliary result, we show Lipschitz continuity of the position
(q_{t}^{i})_{t\geq 0}
and boundedness of the velocity
(p_{t}^{i})_{t\geq 0}
.

Lemma 11

(cf. Lemma
26
)
.

Let
(p^{i}_{t},q^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
20
). Let
\theta_{*}^{i}
be the critical point of
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in I
. Under the Assumptions
1
,
2
with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda_{i},2}
, and
3
with
\lambda=\min_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}
, for any
0\leq s\leq t,
we have

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i}_{t}-q^{i}_{s}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C^{(1)}_{L}(t-s)\Big(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+(m_{0}+e^{-\mathcal{E}(s)})\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|\Big),

\displaystyle\left\|p^{i}_{t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L}(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+m_{0}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|)\qquad(t\geq 0),

where
C^{(1)}_{L}=1+8L^{2}+8L
.

Proof.

From (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}(e^{\mathcal{E}(t)}p^{i}_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}=-e^{\mathcal{E}(t)}\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})/m(t),

which implies

\displaystyle p^{i}_{t}=e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}p^{i}_{0}-e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{s})/m(s)\mathrm{d}s.

(22)

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|p^{i}_{t}\right\|\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}}{m(s)}\left\|\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{s})\right\|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}}{m(s)}\left\|\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{s})-\nabla\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})\right\|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+Le^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}}{m(s)}\left\|q^{i}_{s}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{Lemma4})}{\leq}

\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+8Le^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}(V^{i}(0,q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0}))^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}}{m(s)}e^{\frac{-\lambda s}{2}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+8L(V^{i}(0,q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0}))^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The last inequality holds since
\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}}{m(s)}e^{\frac{-\lambda s}{2}}\mathrm{d}s\leq e^{\mathcal{E}(t)}
, which can be seen by taking the derivative on both sides and comparing the initial values.
And since
V^{i}(0,q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0})\leq(1+L)(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}+m^{2}_{0}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}),
we have

\displaystyle\left\|p^{i}_{t}\right\|\leq e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+(1+8L^{2}+8L)(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+m_{0}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|).

We set
C^{(1)}_{L}=(1+8L^{2}+8L)
and have

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i}_{t}-q^{i}_{s}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{s}^{t}\left\|p^{i}_{m}\right\|\mathrm{d}m\leq C^{(1)}_{L}(t-s)(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+m_{0}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|)+\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{E}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C^{(1)}_{L}(t-s)\left(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+(m_{0}+e^{-\mathcal{E}(s)})\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|\right).

∎

In the third lemma, we give a bound on the system’s acceleration
({\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}/{\mathrm{d}t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Lemma 12

(cf. Lemma
27
)
.

Let
(p^{i}_{t},q^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
20
). Let
\theta_{*}^{i}
be the critical point of
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in I
. Under the Assumptions
1
,
2
with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda_{i},2}
, and
3
with
\lambda=\min_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\|\leq C^{(2)}_{L}\left(\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}+2\right)(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|)\qquad(t\geq 0),

(23)

where
C^{(2)}_{L}=8(C^{(1)}_{L})^{2}=8(1+8L^{2}+8L)^{2}
.

Proof.

From (
22
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{-e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}p^{i}_{0}}{m(t)}-\frac{\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})}{m(t)}+\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{s})/m(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})}{m(t)}=\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})/m(s)\mathrm{d}s+\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})}{m(t)}.

Then, we can rewrite
\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}
as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{-e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}(p^{i}_{0}+\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t}))}{m(t)}+\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}(\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{s})-\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t}))/m(s)\mathrm{d}s.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}+\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})-\nabla\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})\right\|+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}\left\|\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{s})-\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})\right\|/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+L)e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}\left\|q^{i}_{s}-q^{i}_{t}\right\|/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\leq}_{(a_{2})}\frac{4C^{(1)}_{L}e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\Big(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+C^{(1)}_{L}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+(m_{0}+e^{-\mathcal{E}(s)})\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{4C^{(1)}_{L}e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|\Big(1+C^{(1)}_{L}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{4C^{(1)}_{L}e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|\Big(1+C^{(1)}_{L}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}(m_{0}+e^{-\mathcal{E}(s)})/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 4C^{(1)}_{L}\Big(\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}+2C^{(1)}_{L}\Big)\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|+4C^{(1)}_{L}\Big(\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}+4C^{(1)}_{L}\Big)\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|,

where the last step follows since

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)e^{\mathcal{E}(s)}/m(s)\mathrm{d}s\leq 2m(t)e^{\mathcal{E}(t)}

and the inequality
(a_{2})
follows from Lemmas
10
and
11
and the fact that
V^{i}(0,q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0})\leq(1+L)(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}+m^{2}_{0}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 4C^{(1)}_{L}\Big(\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}+4C^{(1)}_{L}\Big)(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|)

\displaystyle\leq 8(C^{(1)}_{L})^{2}\Big(\frac{e^{-\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m(t)}+2\Big)(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|).

We set
C^{(2)}_{L}=8(C^{(1)}_{L})^{2}=8(1+8L^{2}+8L)^{2},
which completes the proof.
∎

We have now attained the aforementioned intermediate goal where we show a bound between the system (
20
) and its limiting equation (
43
). We need to additionally ask for some underlying convexity; strong convexity in this case: Indeed, we aim to show that
q_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}
is contractive. Assumption
2
would only lead to contractivity of
q_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{*}
.

Proposition 13

(cf. Proposition
28
)
.

Let
(p^{i}_{t},q^{i}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
20
). Let
\theta_{*}^{i}
be the critical point of
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in I
. We assume
\Phi_{i}
is strongly convex with constant
\mu
and let
\Phi_{i}
satisfy Assumption
1
and
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfy Assumption
3
with
\lambda=(\mu/4)\land(1/4)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|\leq e^{-\mu t/2}\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{0}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m^{\frac{1}{2}}_{0}\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|\Big)\qquad(t\geq 0),

where
C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}=2C^{(2)}_{L}\mu^{-1/2}(1+\mu^{-1/2}+\mu^{1/2})+\mu^{1/2},

\mu^{1/2}.

By Lemma
12
and Lemma

, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\left(q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}q^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}-\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i}_{t})-\nabla\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{t})\right)-m(t)\left(q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\mu\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}+\frac{m^{2}(t)}{2\mu}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{i}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{Lemma6})}{\leq}-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}+(C^{(2)}_{L})^{2}(\mu)^{-1}m^{2}(t)\Big(\frac{e^{-2\mathcal{E}(t)}}{m^{2}(t)}+4\Big)\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}+(2C^{(2)}_{L})^{2}(\mu)^{-1}\Big(e^{-2\mathcal{E}(t)}+m^{2}(t)\Big)\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big),

which implies

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu t}\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +(C^{(2)}_{L})^{2}(\mu)^{-1}e^{-\mu t}\int_{0}^{t}\Big(e^{-2\mathcal{E}(s)}+m^{2}(s)\Big)e^{\mu s}\mathrm{d}s\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big).

From Lemma
35
, we know that

e^{-\mu t}\int_{0}^{t}m^{2}(s)e^{\mu s}\mathrm{d}s\leq 2\mu^{-1}m^{2}(t)\leq 2\mu^{-1}m^{2}_{0}\leq 2\mu^{-1}m_{0},
and

e^{-\mu t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\mathcal{E}(s)}e^{\mu s}\mathrm{d}s\leq(1+\mu)m_{0}
, which implies

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq e^{-\mu t}\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}+(C^{(1)}_{L,\mu})^{2}m_{0}\Big(\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big).

∎

From Lemma
11
and Proposition
13
, we immediately have the following corollary.

.

Under the same condition as Proposition

. For
0<m_{0}\leq 1

We can now show the main statement of this section. The convergence of dmSGMP to the stochastic gradient process in the longtime limit. Importantly, we assume a coupling between the processes through the index process
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
that is identical in both dynamical systems. Throughout this section, we collected evidence for a contractive behaviour in between the deterministic processes (

) and (

). We now use these results to study the randomised version piece-by-piece. This, of course, is a very similar strategy to that used in Theorem
6
.

Theorem 15
.

Let
(\tilde{p}_{t},\tilde{q}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\theta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
19
) and (
3
), respectively. Let
\theta_{*}^{i}
be the critical point of
\Phi_{i}
for
i\in I
. We assume that
\Phi_{i}
is strongly convex with constant
\mu
and let
\Phi_{i}
satisfy Assumption
1
and
m(t)
satisfy Assumption
3
with
\lambda=(\mu/4)\land(1/4)
for
i\in I
. Let
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be the sequences of the jump times of the process
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
. If we assume that
m_{0}\leq
\frac{(C^{(2)}_{L,\mu})^{-2}\mu^{2}}{4(2\gamma N+\mu)^{2}}
, where
C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}=C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}+C^{(1)}_{L}
, then

\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|\Big]\to 0\ \ \ \ (n\rightarrow\infty).

Furthermore, if we additionally assume that
m
decays at least exponentially, i.e. there exist
C,c>0
such that
m(t)\leq Ce^{-ct},
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{q}_{t}-\theta_{t}\right\|\to 0\ \ \ \ (t\rightarrow\infty)

almost surely and in expectation.

Remark 16
.

After the statement of Assumption
3
, we have concluded that
m(t)\geq m_{0}e^{-\lambda t}
. Thus, the constant
c
mentioned above needs to be smaller than
\lambda
.

Proof.

For any
n\geq 0,
by Corollary
14
, and since the initial value of
m(t)
in the interval
[\tau_{n},\tau_{n+1})
is exactly
m(\tau_{n})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n+1}}-\theta_{\tau_{n+1}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/2}\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|

\displaystyle\ \ \ +C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{n})\Big(\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\theta_{\tau_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau_{n})}_{*}\right\|\Big)

and

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{n+1}}\right\|\leq\Big(e^{-\mathcal{E}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})}+C^{(1)}_{L}m(\tau_{n})\Big)\left\|\tilde{p}^{m}_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L}\Big(\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\theta_{\tau_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau_{n})}_{*}\right\|\Big).

Lemma
32
implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\theta_{\tau_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau_{n})}_{*}\right\|\leq\left\|\theta_{0}\right\|+K_{\Phi}=K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}.

We denote
a_{n}:=e^{-\mu(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/2}
and obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n+1}}-\theta_{\tau_{n+1}}\right\|\leq a_{n}\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{n})\Big(\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}\Big)

as well as

e^{-\mathcal{E}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})}\leq e^{-(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/m_{0}}\leq e^{-(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})(C^{(2)}_{L,\mu})^{2}}\leq e^{-\mu(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/2}:=a_{n}.

Hence, we also have

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{n+1}}\right\|\leq(a_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L}m(\tau_{n}))\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L}\Big(\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|+K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}\Big).

We denote

A_{n}:=\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{n}}-\theta_{\tau_{n}}\right\|,\ B_{n}:=\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{n}}\right\|

and then have the following iteration inequalities:

\displaystyle A_{n+1}\leq a_{n}A_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{n})\Big(A_{n}+B_{n}+K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}\Big),

\displaystyle B_{n+1}\leq(a_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L}m(\tau_{n}))B_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L}\Big(A_{n}+K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}\Big).

Moreover, denoting
D_{n}=A_{n}+m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n})B_{n}
,
C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}=C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}+C^{(1)}_{L}
, we have

\displaystyle D_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq(a_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{n}))A_{n}+m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n+1})(a_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L}m(\tau_{n}))B_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{n})B_{n}

(24)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n+1})C^{(1)}_{L}A_{n}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{n})K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}+C^{(1)}_{L}m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n+1})K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(a_{n}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n})\Big)D_{n}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n})K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(a_{n}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}m^{\frac{1}{2}}_{0}\Big)D_{n}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n})K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}

(25)

and since
\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}
are exponentially distributed with parameter
\gamma N
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{n}}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{n+1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[e^{-\mu(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/2}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}\sqrt{m_{0}}]\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}[m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n})],

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{n+1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[(e^{-\mu(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/2}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}\sqrt{m_{0}})^{2}]\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{n}]+(C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}})^{2}\mathbb{E}[m(\tau_{n})]

(27)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+C^{(3)}_{L,\mu}\sqrt{m_{0}}K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}\mathbb{E}[D_{n}],

where
C^{(3)}_{L,\mu}=2C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}(1+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}).

Since
m_{0}\leq\frac{(C^{(2)}_{L,\mu})^{-2}\mu^{2}}{4(2\gamma N+\mu)^{2}},
we have

\mathbb{E}[e^{-\mu(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/2}+C^{(2)}_{L,\mu}\sqrt{m_{0}}]\leq c_{1},

where
c_{1}=\frac{\mu+4\gamma N}{2(2\gamma N+\mu)}<1.
From the iteration inequality and Lemma
36
, we know that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]=0,
which finish the proof of the first part.
If we assume
m(t)\leq Ce^{-ct},
we then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[m^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tau_{n})]\leq C\mathbb{E}[e^{-\frac{c\tau_{n}}{2}}]=C\mathbb{E}\Big[\prod^{n}_{i=1}e^{-\frac{c(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1})}{2}}\Big]=C\prod^{n}_{i=1}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\frac{c(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1})}{2}}\Big]=C(c_{2})^{n}

where
c_{2}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\frac{c(\tau_{i}-\tau_{i-1})}{2}}\Big]=\frac{2\gamma N}{c+2\gamma N}
which is a constant does not depend on
i
and that is smaller than
1
. We then rewrite
(\ref{iteration Dn})
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{n+1}]\leq c_{1}\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]+CC^{(3)}_{L,\mu}\sqrt{m_{0}}K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}(c_{2})^{n},

which follows from the second part of Lemma
36
. We then have
\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]\leq\tilde{B}e^{-\tilde{b}n}.

This also implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{n+1}]\leq c_{3}\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{n}]+CC^{(3)}_{L,\mu}\sqrt{m_{0}}K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}(c_{4})^{n}.

Furthermore, by using Lemma
36
, we have
\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{n}]\leq\tilde{B}e^{-\tilde{b}n}
for some
\tilde{B},\tilde{b}>0.
The Markov inequality implies that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\left|D_{n}\right|\geq\delta)\leq\delta^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]\leq\delta^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]\leq\delta^{-1}\tilde{B}\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{-\tilde{b}n}<+\infty,

for any
\delta>0
.
This implies that
D_{n}\to 0
almost surely as
n\to\infty,
since
N_{t}\to\infty
almost surely as
t\to\infty.
We then have
D_{N_{t}}\to 0
almost surely as
t\to\infty.

By Corollary
14
, for any
t\geq 0,
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{q}_{t}-\theta_{t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu(t-\tau_{N_{t}})/2}\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{N_{t}}}-\theta_{\tau_{N_{t}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ +C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{N_{t}})\Big(\left\|\tilde{q}_{\tau_{N_{t}}}-\theta_{\tau_{N_{t}}}\right\|+\left\|\tilde{p}_{\tau_{N_{t}}}\right\|+\left\|\theta_{\tau_{N_{t}}}-\theta^{i(\tau_{N_{t}})}_{*}\right\|\Big)

\displaystyle\leq(1+C^{(3)}_{L,\mu})D_{N_{t}}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau_{N_{t}})K_{\Phi,\theta_{0}}.

This implies that
\left\|q_{t}-\theta_{t}\right\|\rightarrow 0
almost surely as
t\to\infty
since
D_{N_{t}}\to 0
and
m(\tau_{N_{t}})\to 0
almost surely.

Also, in order to prove
\mathbb{E}[\left\|\tilde{q}_{t}-\theta_{t}\right\|]\to 0,
it is sufficient to show
\mathbb{E}[D_{N_{t}}]+\mathbb{E}[m(\tau_{N_{t}})]\to 0
as
t\to+\infty.

Since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{N_{t}}]=\sum_{k=0}\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{k}1\!\!1_{N_{t}=k}]^{2}\leq\sum_{k=0}\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{k}]<+\infty,

which implies that
\{D_{N_{t}}\}_{t\geq 0}
is uniformly integrable. Then,
\mathbb{E}[D_{N_{t}}]\to 0
since
D_{N_{t}}\to 0
almost surely.
Note that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}m(\tau_{N_{t}})=0
. By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbb{E}[m(\tau_{N_{t}})]=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Losing, both, momentum and randomness over time

In the previous subsection, we have seen that the stochastic gradient process with decreasing momentum approaches the stochastic gradient process. Asymptotically, the stochastic gradient process converges to a stationary distribution, not necessarily to a single point.
As discussed before, to attain convergence to a single point, we usually need to decrease the learning rate over time, see
Latz 2021
. We now study the following model.

Definition 17
.

The
decreasing-mass, decreasing-learning-rate stochastic gradient-momentum process (ddSGMP)
is a solution of the following stochastic differential equation,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}\hat{q}_{t}&=\hat{p}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
m(t)\mathrm{d}\hat{p}_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i^{\beta}(t)}(\hat{q}_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\hat{p}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
\hat{p}_{0},\hat{q}_{0}&\in X,\end{array}\right.

(28)

where
\Phi_{j}
satisfies Assumption
1
,
j=1,\ldots,N
. The mass
m(t)>0
is strictly decreasing and differentiable with
\lim_{t\to\infty}m(t)=0.
The stochastic process
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
is defined in Definition
1
. For the re-scaled index process
(i^{\beta}(t))_{t\geq 0}=(i(\beta(t)))_{t\geq 0}
, we assume that
\beta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s)\mathrm{d}s,

t\geq 0
with
\eta:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
being a non-decreasing continuously differentiable function.

As opposed to the previous section, we do not consider the stochastic gradient process with constant learning rate as the limiting system, but actually the associated stochastic gradient process with
decreasing
learning rate, denoted by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}\xi_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i^{\beta}(t)}(\xi_{t})\mathrm{d}t,\\
\xi_{0}&\in X.\end{array}\right.

(29)

Next, we define the sequence
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
to be the jump times of process
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
\tau^{\beta}_{n}=\beta^{-1}(\tau_{n})
, to be the jump times of
(i^{\beta}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. We denote by

\displaystyle\Omega^{\alpha}_{n}:=\left\{\frac{\mu}{2\eta(\tau^{\beta}_{n+1})}\geq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \textit{and}\ m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})\leq\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\right\},

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
an event that is used to impose a growth condition on
\beta
and
m
. Regarding this event, we now denote the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

For
n\geq k
, let
W^{\alpha,n}_{k}=\bigcap^{n}_{i=k}\Omega^{\alpha}_{i}
. There exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}>0
such that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(W^{\alpha,\infty}_{k})=1.

The event
W^{\alpha,n}_{k}
is increasing in
k
and decreasing in
n.
Assumption
4
implies that the complement of
\Omega^{\alpha}_{n}
is eventually small. We describe a setting for which this assumption holds in Example
20
below, after stating and proving the main results of this section: the convergence of ddSGMP to the stochastic gradient process with decreasing learning rate and the convergence of ddSGMP to the minimiser of the target function. In neither of these cases, we obtain a convergence rate. We later study the speed of convergence through numerical experiments in Section
5
. But now we start with the first of the two aforementioned results.

Theorem 18
.

Let
(\hat{p}_{t},\hat{q}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solve (
28
) and (
29
), respectively.
For
i\in I
, we assume that
\Phi_{i}
is strongly convex with constant
\mu
and let
\Phi_{i}
satisfy Assumption
1
. Let
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfy Assumption
3
with
\lambda=(\mu/4)\land(1/4).
In addition, we assume that
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\beta(t))_{t\geq 0}
satisfy Assumption
4
. Then, we have

\left\|\hat{q}_{t}-\xi_{t}\right\|\to 0

almost surely, as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

For any
n\geq 0,
by Corollary
14
, we have the following iteration inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu(\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}-\tau^{\beta}_{n})/2}\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|

\displaystyle\ \ \ +C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})\Big(\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau^{\beta}_{n})}_{*}\right\|\Big)

and

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}\right\|\leq\Big(e^{-\mu(\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}-\tau^{\beta}_{n})/2}+C^{(1)}_{L}m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})\Big)\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\Big(\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau^{\beta}_{n})}_{*}\right\|\Big).

Since
\frac{\mathrm{d}\beta^{-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)=\frac{1}{\eta(\beta^{-1}(t))},
we have

\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}-\tau^{\beta}_{n}=\beta^{-1}(\tau_{n+1})-\beta^{-1}(\tau_{n})\geq(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})/\eta(\beta^{-1}(\tau_{n+1})).

Hence, under the event
W^{\alpha,n}_{k}
, for
n\geq k
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}\right\|

\displaystyle\ \leq e^{-\mu(\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}-\tau^{\beta}_{n})/2}\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})\Big(\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau^{\beta}_{n})}_{*}\right\|\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\ \leq\Big(e^{-\frac{\alpha_{1}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\Big)\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+K_{\Phi,\xi_{0}}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}

and

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|\leq\Big(e^{-\frac{\alpha_{1}(\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n})}{\sqrt{n}}}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\Big)\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\Big(\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|+\left\|\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\theta^{i(\tau^{\beta}_{n})}_{*}\right\|\Big).

Since
\tau_{n+1}-\tau_{n}
is independent of
\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|
and
W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}
, for
n\geq k
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n}_{k}}\Big]\leq\Big(\frac{\gamma N}{\gamma N+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}\Big]

\displaystyle+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}\Big]+K_{\Phi,\xi_{0}}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n+1}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n}_{k}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{\gamma N}{\gamma N+\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}}+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\Big)\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}\Big]+K_{\Phi,\xi_{0}}.

We denote

\displaystyle A^{\beta}_{n}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n}_{k}}\Big],\ B^{\beta}_{n}:=\mathbb{E}\Big[\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n}_{k}}\Big].

Let
D^{\beta}_{n}=A^{\beta}_{n}+\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}B^{\beta}_{n}.
We can find some constant
0<C_{\gamma,N,L,\mu}<1
such that for
n\geq k
and
k
large enough

\displaystyle D^{\beta}_{n+1}\leq\Big(1-\frac{C_{\gamma,N,L,\mu}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)D^{\beta}_{n}+C_{\Phi,\xi_{0},L}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}.

(30)

Let
G^{n}_{k}:=\prod^{n}_{i=k}\Big(1-\frac{C_{\gamma,N,L,\mu}}{\sqrt{i}}\Big)
with
G^{n}_{n}=1-\frac{C_{\gamma,N,L,\mu}}{\sqrt{n}}\geq\frac{1}{2}
. From (
30
), we have

\displaystyle D^{\beta}_{n+1}\leq G^{n}_{k}D^{\beta}_{k}+2C_{\Phi,\xi_{0},L}\sum^{n}_{i=k}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{i}}G^{n}_{i}.

From Lemma
37
, we know that there exist some constant
c>0
such that
G^{n}_{i}\leq e^{-c(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{i})}
. This implies

\displaystyle D^{\beta}_{n+1}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-c\sqrt{n}}D^{\beta}_{k}+2C_{\Phi,\xi_{0},L}\sum^{n}_{i=k}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{i}}e^{-c(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{i})}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-c\sqrt{n}}D^{\beta}_{k}+2C_{\Phi,\xi_{0},L}\sum^{n}_{i=k}e^{-c\land\alpha_{3}\sqrt{i}}e^{-c\land\alpha_{3}(\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{i})}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-c\sqrt{n}}D^{\beta}_{k}+2C_{\Phi,\xi_{0},L}ne^{-c\land\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}\mathbb{P}\Big(\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}\frac{D^{\beta}_{n}}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\sum^{\infty}_{n=k}ne^{-c\land\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}<+\infty.

Then, the Borel–Cantelli Lemma implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}=0,

almost surely.
Under the Assumption
4
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}1\!\!1_{(W^{\alpha,\infty}_{k})^{c}}=0,

almost surely.
Since

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}+\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{(W^{\alpha,n}_{k})^{c}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{W^{\alpha,n-1}_{k}}+\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|1\!\!1_{(W^{\alpha,\infty}_{k})^{c}},

for
k
large enough, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|=0,

almost surely.
Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|=0

almost surely.
Under the event
W^{\alpha,n}_{k}
,
m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})
is smaller than
\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{n}}\right\|=0,

almost surely.
Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau^{\beta}_{n}=+\infty
and

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{q}_{t}-\xi_{t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\mu(t-\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}})/2}\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}\right\|

\displaystyle\ \ +C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m(\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}})\Big(\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}\right\|+\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}\right\|+\left\|\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}-\theta^{i(\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}})}_{*}\right\|\Big),

where
\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}\leq t\leq\tau^{\beta}_{{N_{t}}+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{q}_{t}-\xi_{t}\right\|\leq(1+C^{(1)}_{L,\mu}m_{0})\left\|\hat{q}_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}-\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}\right\|+m(\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}})\Big(\left\|\hat{p}_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}\right\|+\left\|\xi_{\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}}}-\theta^{i(\tau^{\beta}_{N_{t}})}_{*}\right\|\Big).

This implies that
\left\|\hat{q}_{t}-\xi_{t}\right\|\to 0
, almost surely, as
t\to\infty
.
∎

Using this result and prior knowledge about the stochastic gradient process, we can now finally show convergence to the minimiser of
\bar{\Phi}
.

Corollary 19
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
18
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{q}_{t}-\theta_{*}\right\|\to 0

in probability, as
t\to\infty
,
where
\theta_{*}
is the unique minimiser of the function
\bar{\Phi}
.

Proof.

From
Latz 2021
, we know that
\xi_{t}
converges to
\theta_{*}
weakly, as
t\rightarrow\infty
. This is equivalent to
\left\|\xi_{t}-\theta_{*}\right\|\to 0
in probability since
\theta_{*}
is deterministic. Then the result follows by applying Theorem
18
.
∎

Hence, we have shown that when reducing – indeed, losing – momentum and decreasing the learning rate over time, we have convergence of the stochastic gradient-momentum process to the minimiser
\theta_{*}
.

We finish this section, by discussing the non-trivial Assumption
4
. Indeed, we give an example below for how
(\beta(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(m(t))_{t\geq 0}
can be chosen to satisfy the assumption.

Example 20
.

Let
\beta(t)=t^{2}
and
m(t)=m_{0}e^{-\lambda t}
, where
0<m_{0}<1
is a constant. In this case
\eta(t)=2t
and
\beta^{-1}(t)=\sqrt{t}.
Hence,

\displaystyle\Omega^{\alpha}_{n}=\Big\{\frac{\mu}{2\eta(\beta^{-1}(\tau_{n+1}))}\geq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \textit{and}\ m(\tau^{\beta}_{n})\leq\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\Big\{\frac{\mu}{4\sqrt{\tau_{n+1}}}\geq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\ \textit{and}\ m_{0}e^{-\lambda\sqrt{\tau_{n}}}\leq\alpha_{2}e^{-\alpha_{3}\sqrt{n}}\Big\}.

By a concentration inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\tau_{n}\leq\frac{n}{2\gamma N}\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(e^{-2\gamma N\tau_{n}}\geq e^{-n}\Big)\leq e^{n}\mathbb{E}[e^{-2\gamma N\tau_{n}}]=\frac{e^{n}}{3^{n}}

(31)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\tau_{n}\geq\frac{2n}{\gamma N}\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(e^{\gamma N\tau_{n}/2}\geq e^{n}\Big)\leq e^{-n}\mathbb{E}[e^{\gamma N\tau_{n}/2}]=\frac{2^{n}}{e^{n}}.

(32)

When
n
is sufficiently large,
\frac{n}{2\gamma N}\leq\tau_{n}\leq\frac{2n}{\gamma N}
already implies that the event
\Omega^{\alpha}_{n}
occurs for
\alpha_{1}=\frac{2}{\mu\sqrt{2\gamma N}},\alpha_{2}=m_{0},\alpha_{3}=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2\gamma N}}
. Hence, when
n
is sufficiently large,
(\Omega^{\alpha}_{n})^{c}\subset\{\tau_{n}\leq\frac{n}{2\gamma N}\}\cup\{\tau_{n}\geq\frac{2n}{\gamma N}\}.
Therefore, from
(\ref{probineqexample2})
and
(\ref{probineqexample22}),
we have

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{c}_{n})\leq\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\Big(\frac{e^{n}}{3^{n}}+\frac{2^{n}}{e^{n}}\Big)<+\infty.

The Borel-Cantelli Lemma then implies that
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\mathbb{P}(\bigcup^{\infty}_{i=k}(\Omega^{\alpha}_{i})^{c})=0
which is equivalent to Assumption
4
.

4
Discretisation of the continuous-time system

After the previous theoretical study of stochastic gradient-momentum processes, we now propose a numerical scheme to discretise those dynamical systems.
This is crucial to turn the continuous-time dynamics into practical algorithms, see the discussion in
Scagliotti and Colli Franzone 2022
;
Shi et al. 2019
;
Wang and Sirignano 2021
;
Wang and Sirignano 2022
;
Wang and Sirignano 2024
: the continuous-time analysis given in Sections
2
and
3
is only worthwhile, when the discrete algorithm behaves similarly to the continuous-time dynamics.
The most usual discretisation of SGMP with an explicit symplectic Euler method would yield an algorithm very similar to stochastic gradient descent with classical momentum.
However, this is hardly appropriate as explicit schemes are unstable when the mass is small or when it decreases over time.
In the following, we aim to obtain a stable, symplectic, and efficient discretisation strategy that retains the correct longtime behaviour for the deterministic parts of SGMP. We note that even in the decreasing mass case, we are committed to using a symplectic solver to retain local conversation laws in the dynamical system, see
McLachlan and Stern 2024
.

First, we discuss the discretisation of the underdamped gradient flow with constant or decreasing mass. For convenience, we briefly recall the ODE of interest:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q_{t}&=p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
m(t)\mathrm{d}p_{t}&=-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\alpha p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
p_{0},q_{0}&\in X.\end{array}\right.

(33)

Since our theoretical analysis investigates
the case
m(t)\to 0

as
t\to\infty
,
we need the discretisation
scheme to especially be stable for small values of
m(t)
.
In this framework, a fully explicit method, such as the forward or symplectic Euler method,
would not be suitable for the purpose. In particular,
since the evolution of
t\mapsto p_{t}
is
directly affected by
the value of the mass
m(t)
, it is natural to
consider an implicit discretisation for this variable:

m_{n}\frac{p_{n+1}-p_{n}}{h}=-(\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})+\alpha p_{n+1}),

for every
n\geq 0
,
yielding

p_{n+1}=\frac{1}{{m_{n}}/{h}+\alpha}\left(\frac{m_{n}}{h}p_{n}-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})\right),

(34)

where
h>0
denotes the discretisation step-size and
m_{n}:=m(nh)
.
For the
q
variable in (
33
)
we use the scheme

q_{n+1}=q_{n}+hp_{n+1}

(35)

for every
n\geq 0
. Finally, combining
(
34
) and (
35
), we
obtain the following update rule for the
discretised dynamical model:

\begin{cases}p_{n+1}=\frac{1}{{m_{n}}/{h}+\alpha}\left(\frac{m_{n}}{h}p_{n}-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})\right),\\
q_{n+1}=q_{n}+hp_{n+1},\end{cases}

(36)

for every
n\geq 0
. As we discretise
q
explicitly and
p
implicity, this discretisation scheme is a semi-implicit method. Moreover, the discretisation scheme employed here is symplectic
(
Hairer et al. 2003
, Theorem VI.3.3)
, so that the discretised dynamics satisfy (local) conservation laws. For more details, we refer the reader to the discussion in
(
Hairer et al. 2003
, Section VI.7)
.

Remark 21
.

As suggested in the previous sections and depicted in Figure
3
, the parameter

m
can be interpreted as an interpolation
variable between the gradient
flow and the underdamped gradient flow. We observe that this is still the case
for the discretised dynamical system
(
36
). Indeed, if we set

\alpha=1
and let
m\to 0
,
(
36
) turns into

\begin{cases}p_{n+1}=-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n}),\\
q_{n+1}=q_{n}+hp_{n+1},\end{cases}

which is exactly the gradient descent method.

We finish this section with a comparison of the semi-implicit discretisation of the underdamped gradient flow with other momentum-based optimisation methods. First, we note that we can rephrase the update rule (
36
) to obtain a recursive definition of
q_{n}
that does not involve the velocity variable
p_{n}
. Namely, from (
36
) we deduce that

q_{n+1}=q_{n}+\frac{m_{n}}{m_{n}/h+\alpha}p_{n}-\frac{h}{m_{n}/h+\alpha}\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n}).

Then, recalling that
hp_{n}=q_{n}-q_{n-1}
, we obtain that

q_{n+1}=q_{n}+\frac{m_{n}}{m_{n}+\alpha h}(q_{n}-q_{n-1})-\frac{h}{m_{n}/h+\alpha}\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n}).

(37)

This allows us to easily compare our scheme with other momentum-based optimisation schemes. In particular, we recall that Polyak’s Heavy Ball method has constant coefficients in front of the momentum term
q_{n}-q_{n-1}
and in front of
\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})
. Moreover, when writing Nesterov’s update rule in single-variable form, we obtain:

q_{n+1}=q_{n}+\xi_{n}(q_{n}-q_{n-1})-h\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})-\xi_{n}h(\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n-1})),

with
\xi_{n}\equiv\frac{1-\sqrt{\mu h}}{1+\sqrt{\mu h}}
when
\bar{\Phi}
is
\mu
-strongly convex or
\xi_{n}=\frac{n}{n+3}
when
\bar{\Phi}
is just convex.
Our scheme in (
37
) has a structure that is reminiscent of the one considered in
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
, where a time rescaling was introduced in the continuous-time dynamics, yielding a non-constant (and diverging) multiplicative factor in front of the gradient of the objective. However, it is important to note that in
Attouch et al. 2019a
;
Attouch et al. 2019b
the gradient is evaluated at
q_{n+1}
, while in (
37
) we have
\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})
.

4.1
Step-size choice

The first natural question about the discrete-time
optimisation method (
36
) concerns
the choice of the discretisation step-size
h>0
.
We derive a heuristic rule,
assuming for simplicity that

m_{n}=m
for every
n\geq 1
.
From (
36
),
we obtain

\displaystyle q_{n+1}

\displaystyle=q_{n}+\frac{h^{2}}{m+\alpha h}\left(\frac{m}{h}p_{n}-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=q_{n}-\frac{h^{2}}{m+\alpha h}\left(\frac{m}{h}\frac{h}{m+\alpha h}\left(\frac{m}{h}p_{n-1}-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n-1})\right)-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n})\right)

for every
n\geq 0
.
With a backward induction
argument, assuming that

p_{0}=0
, we deduce that

q_{n+1}=q_{n}-\frac{h^{2}}{m+\alpha h}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\frac{m}{m+\alpha h}\right)^{j}\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{n-j}).

The previous identity
suggests that the position
at the step
k+1
is obtained
through
k+1
evaluations of the
gradient at the previous points
of the discrete trajectory.
Moreover, each evaluation
is weighted by a power of the

forgetting coefficient

\frac{m}{m+\alpha h}<1
.
Therefore, it is reasonable
to ask that the sum of the
weights is of order

\frac{1}{L}
, where

L>0
is
the Lipschitz constant of

\nabla\bar{\Phi}
, see Assumption
1
. In particular,
we require

\frac{h^{2}}{m+\alpha h}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\frac{m}{m+\alpha h}\right)^{j}\simeq\frac{1}{L}.

Taking the limit
as
n\to\infty
in the previous sum, we obtain

\frac{1}{L}\simeq\frac{h^{2}}{m+\alpha h}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{m}{m+\alpha h}\right)^{j}=\frac{h}{\alpha},

or equivalently

h\simeq\frac{\alpha}{L}.

(38)

We first note that
the expression
at the right-hand side of
(
38
)
does not depend on
the parameter
m
.
Thus, according to the empirical
argument presented above,
there is no need to adjust the
magnitude of the step-size
according to
m
, also not if
m
changes over time.
This fact deviates from
the usual discretisation of
the
t\mapsto p_{t}
variable in
(
33
).
The second observation
is that, we not only see convergence of our discrete method to gradient descent as
m\downarrow 0
, see Remark
21
. We also recover
the correct step-size for that gradient descent scheme we converge to.

We end this section with a remark on a rescaling of the potential
\Phi
.

Remark 22
.

Let us assume that the potential
\Phi
is multiplied by
a positive constant
\rho>0
, i.e.
\Phi^{\prime}=\rho\bar{\Phi}
.
A natural question is how we should rescale the parameters
of the method (
36
) such that
the sequence of positions
(q_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}
coincides with
the sequence
(q_{n})_{n\geq 0}

corresponding to the original objective.
It turns out that it is sufficient to set

h^{\prime}=h/\rho
and
m^{\prime}_{n}=m_{n}/\rho
. Indeed, if we
we consider the sequences
(q_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}

and
(p_{n}^{\prime})_{n\geq 0}
obtained with

\begin{cases}p_{n+1}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\frac{m^{\prime}_{n}}{h^{\prime}}+\alpha}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}_{n}}{h^{\prime}}p_{n}^{\prime}-\nabla\Phi^{\prime}(q_{n}^{\prime})\right),&p_{0}^{\prime}=0\\
q_{n+1}^{\prime}=q_{n}^{\prime}+h^{\prime}p_{n+1}^{\prime},&q_{0}^{\prime}=q_{0},\end{cases}

then a direct computation yields

q_{n}^{\prime}=q_{n},\qquad p_{n}^{\prime}=\rho p_{n}

for every
k\geq 1
, where the sequences

(q_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are obtained
using (
36
) with the original
objective
\bar{\Phi}
and the parameters
h
and
m_{n}
.

4.2
Randomised version of the method

The stochastic dynamical systems discussed throughout this work consist of piecewise deterministic ODEs where the pieces are determined by random waiting times and a subsampling process. After having discussed the ODE discretisation in the previous subsections, we now incorporate the stochasticity into the discrete-time setting. In this regard, the random waiting time for the switching of the objective — despite being a cornerstone of the theoretical analysis — does not seem natural in view of practical implementations.
The use of random waiting times in practice within this framework has been discussed by
Jin et al. 2023
;
Latz 2021
. In the present work, we replace those random waiting times by deterministic waiting times that coincide with the time steps of the algorithm. This is exactly the well-established paradigm of the classical stochastic gradient descent method and of the classical momentum method as well. The waiting times may either be constant-in-time (homogeneous) or decreasing-in-time (heterogeneous).

Throughout this work, we have (implicitly) considered the case in which we choose one potential
\Phi_{i(t)}
at any time
t\geq 0
. Of course, we can also employ
batch subsampling
. There, we have

\begin{cases}p_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\frac{m_{n}}{h}+\alpha}\left(\frac{m_{n}}{h}p_{n}-\mathbf{v}_{n}\right),\\
q_{n+1}=q_{n}+hp_{n+1},\end{cases}

(39)

where

\mathbf{v}_{n}:=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\nabla\Phi_{i_{r}}(q_{n}),

(40)

and
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\}
is a subset
of
I
that is sampled uniformly without replacement, i.e. we
do not choose a single but rather a set of
\ell
potentials at once and optimise with respect to their sample mean. This setting is very useful in practice, as it reduces the subsampling error. This setting is also already fully contained in our theory. To see this, we can just define a new set of potentials
(\Phi^{\prime}_{K})_{K\in I^{\prime}}
, where
I^{\prime}:=\{K\subseteq I:\#K=\ell\}
and
\Phi_{K}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\in K}\Phi_{i}
for
K\in I^{\prime}
. Then, the mean of the
(\Phi^{\prime}_{K})_{K\in I^{\prime}}
is
\bar{\Phi}
.
What is not contained in our theory, but still helpful in practice, is
batch subsampling in epochs
, where at any time, we do not replace elements from which we sample in
I
before we have picked each index once. This setting is not trivially contained in our theory due to its non-Markovian nature.

Numerical Experiments

We now present numerical experiments in which we test the discretisation strategy proposed in Section
4
. We start with academic convex and non-convex examples. Then, we employ SGMP for the training of a convolutional neural network regarding the classification of the CIFAR-10 data set. We aim to show how the method compares with the classical momentum method and standard stochastic gradient descent.

5.1
One-dimensional non-smooth stationary point

The first numerical experiment involves the
one-dimensional example discussed in
Subsection
1.2
.
We recall that we want to minimise the function

\Phi(x):=(\mathrm{ReLU}(x)-1)^{2}+x^{2}=\begin{cases}x^{2}+1&\mbox{if }x\leq 0\\
2x^{2}-2x+1&\mbox{if }x>0\end{cases}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}),

which attains the global minimum at the point

x^{*}=\frac{1}{2}
. The point
\tilde{x}=0
is a non-smooth stationary
point, since
\Phi(x)\geq\Phi(\tilde{x})

for every

. In Subsection
1.2
, we are able to show that the underdamped gradient flow can overcome the local minimiser
\tilde{x}
if
\alpha^{2}-8m<0
.

We now test this property using our discrete-time method (
36
) with the step-size constant along the iterations. The discretisation scheme asks us to choose the learning rate
h
according to the Lipschitz constant of
\Phi^{\prime}
. Actually, the derivative
of
\Phi
is not continuous.
However, in this framework,
by “Lipschitz constant” of
\Phi^{\prime}

we mean

L_{0}=\max\{\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi^{\prime}|_{x\leq 0}),\mathrm{Lip}(\Phi^{\prime}|_{x\geq 0})\}
.
We test the scheme in this example using the precise Lipschitz constant and overestimated Lipschitz constants. The results are presented in Figure
4
.
We see that the condition
\alpha^{2}-8m<0
is also relevant for the discrete dynamical system. The inequality appears to be sharp whenever we overestimate the Lipschitz constant – unsurprisingly as a smaller step-size leads to a more accurate discretisation of the underdamped gradient flow. Using the correct Lipschitz constant lets
\alpha^{2}-8m<0
appear quite conservative. Indeed, a much larger range of
\alpha,m
allow for convergence to the global minimiser. A larger step-size makes the method more robust.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we have proposed and analysed the stochastic gradient-momentum process, a continuous-time dynamics representing momentum-based stochastic optimisation. We have especially analysed limiting behaviour when reducing learning rate and/or particle mass. In this context, learning rate and particle mass can either be reduced homogeneously or decrease over time. We have shown pathwise or longtime convergence to the underlying gradient flow or the stochastic gradient process, respectively. We have then proposed a stable discretisation strategy for the stochastic gradient-momentum process and tested the strategy in several numerical examples. In those, we especially saw that the stable discretisation of the stochastic gradient-momentum process can achieve a similar accuracy in a CNN training compared with (the possibly unstable) stochastic gradient descent with classical momentum algorithm.

Most of the theoretical results we have obtained throughout this work refer to the setting of convex optimisation. Convex optimisation is vital in, e.g. image reconstruction. The training of neural network usually requires non-convex optimisation and momentum-based methods are especially popular in non-convex settings. Hence, a natural future research direction are non-convex optimisation problems.
The stochastic gradient-momentum process does not represent the adaptivity in the Adam algorithm. To represent the adaptivity, we would need to study a two-sided dependence between
(i(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(p(t),q(t))_{t\geq 0}
and a non-linear weighting in front of the gradient. Both these additions to the stochastic gradient-momentum process are a very interesting and challenging direction for future research.

Appendix A
Longtime behavior of the underdamped gradient flow

We recall that the underdamped gradient flow (
15
) is defined as the following dynamical system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q_{t}&=p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}p_{t}&=-\nabla\bar{\Phi}(q_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\alpha p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
p_{0},q_{0}&\in X.\end{array}\right.

We now show that the position in the underdamped gradient flow converges to the global minimiser of the objective function
\bar{\Phi}
and the velocity converges to 0.
We shall assume that
\bar{\Phi}
satisfies Assumption
2
with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda,\alpha}
for some
\lambda
and
\alpha
defined in (
15
).
First, we note that since
\bar{\Phi}
achieves a global minimum at
\theta_{*}
,
\theta_{*}
is a critical point of
\bar{\Phi}
. Then, (
13
) implies that
\theta_{*}
is the unique critical point of
\bar{\Phi}
.
We use a technique similar to that of, e.g.
Aujol et al. 2022
;
Aujol et al. 2023
;
Eberle et al. 2017
;
Sebbouh et al. 2020
. Indeed, we introduce the Lyapunov function
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\displaystyle V(x,y):=

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}(x)-\bar{\Phi}(\theta_{*})+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\Big(\left\|x-\theta_{*}+\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}+\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}-\lambda\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big)

(41)

which we employ below. Using
V
, we can show Lyapunov exponential global stability for the underdamped gradient flow (
15
).

Proposition 23
.

Assume that
\bar{\Phi}
satisfies Assumption
1
and
2
with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda,\alpha}
. Let
(p_{t},q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the solution to (
15
) and
V
be the Lyapunov function defined in (
41
). Then we have the following inequality

\displaystyle V(q_{t},p_{t})\leq V(q_{0},p_{0})e^{-\alpha\lambda t}\qquad(t\geq 0).

Proof.

Notice that (
13
) implies that
\frac{\mathrm{d}V(q_{t},p_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq-\alpha\lambda V(q_{t},p_{t})
.
By Grönwall’s inequality, we obtain

V(q_{t},p_{t})\leq V(q_{0},p_{0})e^{-\alpha\lambda t}.

∎

In the previous proposition, we show that the Lyapunov function
V(q_{t},p_{t})
is bounded above by a number that exponentially decreases in
t>0
.
V(q_{t},p_{t})
is zero, if
q_{t}=\theta_{*}
and
p_{t}=0
, i.e. the particle is positioned at the unique minimiser of
\bar{\Phi}
and the particle velocity is 0: the dynamical system has reached a stationary state. However,
V
can have multiple zeros. Thus, we need some more work to show exponential convergence of the dynamical system.

Corollary 24
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
23
, we have

\left\|p_{t}\right\|^{2}+\left\|q_{t}-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha,\lambda}e^{-\alpha\lambda t}V(q_{0},p_{0})\qquad(t\geq 0).

Proof.

Since
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{4},
we have, by the
\varepsilon
-Young inequality:

\displaystyle V(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\Big((1-\lambda)\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}+2(x-\theta_{*})\cdot(\alpha^{-1}y)\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\Big((1-\lambda)\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}-2\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda\right)\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2},

which implies that

\displaystyle\left\|q_{t}-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{8}{\alpha^{2}(1-2\lambda)}V(q_{t},p_{t})\leq\frac{8}{\alpha^{2}(1-2\lambda)}V(q_{0},p_{0})e^{-\alpha\lambda t}.

Similarly, again by the
\varepsilon
-Young inequality, we have for
\left\|p_{t}\right\|
that

\displaystyle V(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\Big((1-\lambda)\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}+2(x-\theta_{*})\cdot(\alpha^{-1}y)\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\Big((1-\lambda)\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}-(1-\lambda)\left\|x-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{1-2\lambda}{1-\lambda}\left\|\alpha^{-1}y\right\|^{2}\right),

which implies

\displaystyle\left\|p_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{4(1-\lambda)}{1-2\lambda}e^{-\alpha\lambda t}V(q_{0},p_{0})\leq e^{-\alpha\lambda t}V(q_{0},p_{0}).

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\|q_{t}-\theta_{*}\right\|^{2}+\left\|p_{t}\right\|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha,\lambda}e^{-\alpha\lambda t}V(q_{0},p_{0}),

where
C_{\alpha,\lambda}=4+\frac{8}{\alpha^{2}(1-2\lambda)}.

∎

Appendix B
Decreasing momentum: the fixed-sample case

We now consider the SGMP dynamics subject to a fixed sample
i\in I
. More specifically, we define

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{d}q^{i,m}_{t}&=p^{i,m}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
m\mathrm{d}p^{i,m}_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i}(q^{i,m}_{t})\mathrm{d}t-p^{i,m}_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
p^{i,m}_{0}&=p^{i}_{0}\in X,\\
q^{i,m}_{0}&=q^{i}_{0}\in X,\end{array}\right.

(42)

with mass

When

, we have the following formal limiting equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{d}\theta^{i}_{t}&=-\nabla\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{t})\mathrm{d}t,\\
\theta^{i}_{0}&\in X.\end{array}\right.

(43)

This is the gradient flow with respect to the potential
\Phi_{i}
for some

. To prove this described limiting behaviour, we require three auxiliary results. In the first one we study, similarly to Corollary
24
, the longtime behaviour of the deterministic underdamped dynamical system (
42
). This time with emphasis on the influence of the mass
m
. Interestingly, we can see that the convergence rate is independent of the mass
m
, if it is sufficiently small. We note that these results are similar to previous results by
Attouch et al. 2000
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
(p^{i,m}_{t},q^{i,m}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the solution to (
42
).

\Phi_{i}
satisfy Assumption
1
and
2
with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda_{i},1}
and critical point
\theta^{i}_{*}
. We set

. Then for

, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\left\|q^{i,m}_{t}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq 16(L+2)e^{-\lambda t}(\left\|q^{i}_{0}-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}+m^{2}\left\|p^{i}_{0}\right\|^{2})\qquad(t\geq 0),

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of the
\nabla\Phi_{i}

Proof.

We recall Assumption
2
for
\Phi_{i}
with
\alpha=1

(x-\theta^{i}_{*})\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i}(x)/2\geq\lambda(\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})+\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4),

\displaystyle(x-\theta^{i}_{*})\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i}(x)/(2m)\geq\lambda(m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(x)-m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})+m^{-1}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4).

And since

we have

\displaystyle\lambda(m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(x)-m^{-1}\Phi_{i}

\displaystyle(\theta^{i}_{*})+m^{-1}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4)

\displaystyle=\lambda m(m^{-2}\Phi_{i}(x)-m^{-2}\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})+m^{-2}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda m(m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(x)-m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})+m^{-2}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4).

So Assumption
2
implies that

(x-\theta^{i}_{*})\cdot\nabla\Phi_{i}(x)/(2m)\geq\lambda m(m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(x)-m^{-1}\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})+m^{-2}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}/4)

which exactly means that
\Phi_{i}(x)/m
satisfies Assumption

with
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda m,m^{-1}}
. We define

\displaystyle V^{i,m}(x,y)=\frac{\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})}{m}+\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\Big(\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}+my\right\|^{2}+\left\|my\right\|^{2}-m\lambda\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big).

From Proposition
23
, we have

\displaystyle V^{i,m}(q^{i,m}_{t},q^{i,m}_{t})\leq V^{i,m}(q^{i}_{0},p^{i}_{0})e^{-\lambda t}.

Next, we are going to show that

\displaystyle cm^{-2}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq V^{i,m}(x,y)\leq(L+2)\Big(m^{-2}\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}+\left\|y\right\|^{2}\Big),

\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})\geq 0
and

we have

\displaystyle V^{i,m}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\Big(\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}+my\right\|^{2}+\left\|my\right\|^{2}-m\lambda\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}-m\lambda\Big)\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}\geq\frac{\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}}{16m^{2}}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(x)-\Phi_{i}(\theta^{i}_{*})=\int_{0}^{1}(x-\theta^{i}_{*})\Big[\nabla\Phi_{i}\Big((x-\theta^{i}_{*})s+\theta^{i}_{*})\Big)-\nabla\Phi_{i}(\theta_{*})\Big]ds\leq\frac{L\left\|x-\theta^{i}_{*}\right\|^{2}}{2}.
