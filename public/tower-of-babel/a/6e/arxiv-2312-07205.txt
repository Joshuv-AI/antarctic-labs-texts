Construction and application of an algebraic dual basis and the Fine-Scale Greens’ Function for computing projections and reconstructing unresolved scales
Suyash Shrestha, Joey Dekker, Marc Gerritsma, Steven Hulshoff, Ido Akkerman

Abstract
In this paper, we build on the work of [T. Hughes, G. Sangalli, VARIATIONAL MULTISCALE ANALYSIS: THE FINE-SCALE GREENS’ FUNCTION, PROJECTION, OPTIMIZATION, LOCALIZATION, AND STABILIZED METHODS, SIAM Journal of Numerical Analysis, 45(2), 2007] dealing with the explicit computation of the Fine-Scale Green’s function. The original approach chooses a set of functionals associated with a projector to compute the Fine-Scale Green’s function. The construction of these functionals, however, does not generalise to arbitrary projections, higher dimensions, or Spectral Element methods. We propose to generalise the construction of the required functionals by using dual functions. These dual functions can be directly derived from the chosen projector and are explicitly computable. We show how to find the dual functions for both the L 2 L^{2} and the H 0 1 H^{1}_{0} projections. We then go on to demonstrate that the Fine-Scale Green’s functions constructed with the dual basis functions consistently reproduce the unresolved scales removed by the projector. The methodology is tested using one-dimensional Poisson and advection-diffusion problems, as well as a two-dimensional Poisson problem. We present the computed components of the Fine-Scale Green’s function, and the Fine-Scale Green’s function itself. These results show that the method works for arbitrary projections, in arbitrary dimensions. Moreover, the methodology can be applied to any Finite/Spectral Element or Isogeometric framework.

2
Hughes and Sangalli approach

The theory of variational multiscale analysis that this work builds upon was presented by Hughes and Sangalli in
[
25
]
. This section serves as a brief review of the original work of
[
25
]
where we lay down the fundamental concepts for formulating the VMS approach. First, the continuous setup is reviewed, followed by the finite-dimensional setting.

2.1
Continuous Case

In
[
25
, §2]
, the following problem is considered. Let
V
be a Hilbert space with a norm
||\cdot||_{V}
and a scalar product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
. Let
V^{*}
be the dual of
V
, with
\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}_{V}
the duality pairing between
V^{*}
and
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}:V\to V^{*}
be a linear isomorphism. The problem can then be stated as: given
f\in V^{*}
, find
u\in V
such that

\mathcal{L}u=f.

(1)

The variational formulation of (
1
) reads as: find
u\in V
such that

\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle}_{V}=\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle f,v\rangle}_{V}\quad\forall v\in V.

(2)

The solution
u
is formally expressed as
u=\mathcal{G}f
, with the Green’s operator
\mathcal{G}:V^{*}\to V
, that is
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}
.

This formal problem may also be formulated in a variational multiscale setting. In order to do so, one introduces
\bar{V}
as a closed subspace of
V
, along with a linear projector
\mathcal{P}:V\rightarrow\bar{V}
with
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{P}
and
\text{Range}(\mathcal{P})=\bar{V}
.
\mathcal{P}
is assumed continuous in
V
. For the additional formal details, such as the required inf-sup conditions, see
[
25
]
. Then, defining
V{{}^{\prime}}=\text{Ker}(\mathcal{P})
, which is a closed subspace of
V
, combined with the continuity of
\mathcal{P}
gives

V=\bar{V}\oplus V{{}^{\prime}}.

(3)

This split implies that any
v\in V
can be uniquely written as
v=\bar{v}+v{{}^{\prime}}
with
\bar{v}\in\bar{V}
and
v{{}^{\prime}}\in V{{}^{\prime}}
.
In the VMS approach,
\bar{V}
represents the resolvable coarse scales, while
V{{}^{\prime}}
represents the unresolved fine scales. When using a VMS approach, one obtains
\bar{u}=\mathcal{P}u
as the solution for (
1
), i.e. the numerical error equals the interpolation error and not a multiple of the interpolation error which is usually the case for optimal Finite Element methods. Using (
3
), the variational formulation from (
2
) can be split into

\displaystyle\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}+\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}u{{}^{\prime}},\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle f,\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}\quad\forall\bar{v}\in\bar{V},

(4a)

\displaystyle\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\bar{u},v{{}^{\prime}}\rangle}_{V}+\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}u{{}^{\prime}},v{{}^{\prime}}\rangle}_{V}

\displaystyle=\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle f,v{{}^{\prime}}\rangle}_{V}\quad\forall v{{}^{\prime}}\in V{{}^{\prime}}.

(4b)

These equations are assumed to be well-posed for
\bar{u}\in\bar{V}
given
u{{}^{\prime}}
and
f
, and for
u{{}^{\prime}}\in V{{}^{\prime}}
given
\bar{u}
and
f
, respectively.

As for the non-split formulation in (
2
), a Green’s operator can also be associated with the split formulation. In particular, the Greens’ operator associated with the fine-scale equation in (
4b
) is the
Fine-Scale Greens’ operator

\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}:V^{*}\to V{{}^{\prime}}
. This gives
u{{}^{\prime}}
from the coarse-scale residual
\mathscr{R}\bar{u}=f-\mathcal{L}\bar{u}
,

u{{}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}(f-\mathcal{L}\bar{u})=\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}\mathscr{R}\bar{u}.

(5)

With
\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}
, one can eliminate
u{{}^{\prime}}
from (
4a
) and obtain the VMS formulation for
\bar{u}
as

\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}-\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}=\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle f,\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}-\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}f,\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}\quad\forall\bar{v}\in\bar{V}.

(6)

As a consequence of (
3
), (
6
) admits the unique solution
\bar{u}=\mathcal{P}u
.

The above formulation can be used to obtain
\bar{u}
, but requires obtaining the Fine-Scale Greens’ operator
\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}
. It turns out (see
[
25
, §2.3]
) that this operator can be expressed in terms of the full Greens’ operator, the projection, and the adjoint projection
\mathcal{P}^{T}:\bar{V}^{*}\to V^{*}
which is defined as

\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{P}^{T}\bar{\mu},v\rangle}_{V}=\prescript{}{\bar{V}^{*}}{\langle\bar{\mu},\mathcal{P}v\rangle}_{\bar{V}}\quad\forall v\in V,\bar{\mu}\in\bar{V}^{*},

(7)

where
\bar{V}^{*}
is the dual space of
\bar{V}
and
\prescript{}{\bar{V}^{*}}{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}_{\bar{V}}
the pairing between them.

The Fine-Scale Greens’ operator is then computed by considering a constrained version of (
5
) as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u^{\prime}+\mathcal{P}^{T}\bar{\lambda}=\mathscr{R}\bar{u}

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}u^{\prime}=0,

(9)

where the newly introduced
\bar{\lambda}\in\bar{V}^{*}
constrains
u{{}^{\prime}}
to live in the
V{{}^{\prime}}
space. Given the invertibility of
\mathcal{L}
, we may write

\displaystyle u^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}(\mathscr{R}\bar{u}-\mathcal{P}^{T}\bar{\lambda}),

(10)

which when substituted into (
9
) and rearranged for
\bar{\lambda}
gives

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}=\left(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{P}^{T}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{G}\mathscr{R}\bar{u}.

(11)

The fine-scale operator
\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}
can now be expressed in terms of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{P}
as

\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}=\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{GP}^{T}(\mathcal{PGP}^{T})^{-1}\mathcal{PG},

(12)

with the properties

\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}\mathcal{P}^{T}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{PG}{{}^{\prime}}=0.

(13)

See
[
25
, p.542]
for the proof. (
12
) can be used in (
6
) to obtain

\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}-\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mathcal{L}\mathcal{G}{{}^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle}_{V}=\prescript{}{\bar{V}^{*}}{\langle(\mathcal{PGP}^{T})^{-1}\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle}_{\bar{V}},

(14)

which, since
(\mathcal{PGP}^{T})^{-1}
is invertible, confirms that (
6
) is well-posed.

2.2
Finite-dimensional case

In the interest of practical implementations,
\bar{V}
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
V
and we seek a set of functionals
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\bar{V}^{*}
which act as the basis for the projection
\mathcal{P}
. For an
N
dimensional subspace
\bar{V}
we seek
N
functionals which satisfy

\displaystyle\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{i},v\rangle}_{V}=0\quad\forall v\in V^{\prime}\quad\forall i=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

(15)

which in turn implies that
\mathcal{P}v=0\>\>\forall v\in V^{\prime}
. These
\mu
’s form a basis for the range of
\mathcal{P}^{T}
and we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\mu_{i}=0\quad\forall i=0,1,\ldots N-1

(16)

\displaystyle\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{i},\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\nu\rangle}_{V}=0\quad\forall\nu\in V^{*}\>\>\forall i=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

(17)

following (
13
). Furthermore, the
\mu
’s may be represented as a vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in(\bar{V}^{*})^{N}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{0}\\
\mu_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{N-1}\end{array}\right]\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}=\left[\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1}\right],

through which
\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\in V^{N}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathcal{G}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}=\left[\mathcal{G}\mu_{0},\mathcal{G}\mu_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}\mu_{N-1}\right]

(22)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{0},\mathcal{G}\mu_{0}\rangle}_{V}&\ldots&\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{0},\mathcal{G}\mu_{N-1}\rangle}_{V}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{N-1},\mathcal{G}\mu_{0}\rangle}_{V}&\ldots&\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{N-1},\mathcal{G}\mu_{N-1}\rangle}_{V}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathcal{G}(\nu)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{0},\mathcal{G}\nu\rangle}_{V}\\
\vdots\\
\prescript{}{V^{*}}{\langle\mu_{N-1},\mathcal{G}\nu\rangle}_{V}\end{array}\right]\quad\forall\nu\in V^{*}.

The Fine-Scale Greens’ function from (
12
) may now be rewritten as

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\left[\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathcal{G}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{T}\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathcal{G}.

(29)

In theory, we are free to choose any set of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
which satisfy (
15
), (
16
), and (
17
). In
[
25
]
several definitions for
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are proposed for obtaining the Fine-Scale Greens’ function for the
H_{0}^{1}
and
L^{2}
projections of specific example problems. However, their generalisation for arbitrary projections or high-order spectral elements is not considered. This is precisely the knowledge gap that the present work attempts to fill. As such, we turn our attention to constructing a general definition for
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Naturally, the choice of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
depends on the projection as stated in (
15
). Ideally, we want a generalised approach for defining the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
corresponding to an arbitrary projector which then consequently yields a unique Fine-Scale Greens’ function associated with that projection. To achieve this, we propose the use of dual basis functions.

3
Primal and Dual bases

The dual basis functions considered here were initially created in the context of the Mimetic Spectral Element Method (MSEM)
[
26
]
. These are dual functions in the
L^{2}
-sense. In order to understand their link to projections and the use of dual basis functions as the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
’s, we first introduce the primal basis functions and their corresponding
L^{2}
duals. For a more detailed exposition and the extensions to multiple dimensions, see
[
26
]
.

3.1
Primal basis

A primal basis for nodal functions is constructed based on nodal degrees of freedom. Given such degrees of freedom, denoted by
\mathcal{N}^{0}_{j}
, the primal basis functions, denoted by
\psi^{(0)}_{i}
, should satisfy the following property

\mathcal{N}^{0}_{j}(\psi_{i}^{(0)})=\delta_{ij}.

(30)

We consider the mimetic spectral element method which uses the Gauss-Legendre-Lobatto (GLL) nodes for nodal degrees of freedom. To define the GLL-nodes, consider the interval
K=[-1,1]\subset\mathbb{R}
and let
\xi_{i}\in K,i=0,\dots,p
be the roots of the polynomial
\left(1-\xi^{2}\right)L^{\prime}_{p}(\xi)
, where
L_{p}(\xi)
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
p
and
L^{\prime}_{p}(\xi)
its derivative. The GLL-nodes are then used to define basis functions that satisfy (
30
) with
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{(0)}(f):=f(\xi_{j})

\psi^{(0)}_{i}(\xi):=\displaystyle\prod_{\shortstack{$\scriptstyle j=0$\\
$\scriptstyle j\neq i$}}^{p}\frac{\xi-\xi_{j}}{\xi_{i}-\xi_{j}},\quad\quad i=0,1,\ldots,p\;.

(31)

The basis functions
\psi_{i}^{(0)}(\xi)
,
i=0,\ldots,p
span the finite dimensional subspace
\bar{V}
.
An example of these basis functions for
p=3
is shown in
Figure

1(a)
.

Next to the basis functions for nodal functions, the MSEM also defines basis functions based on edge degrees of freedom. These are histopolant basis functions based on integral degrees of freedom, defined as

\mathcal{N}_{j}^{1}(g)=\int_{\xi_{j-1}}^{\xi_{j}}g(\xi)\,d\xi,\quad i=1,\dots,p,

(32)

where
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{1}
is a linear functional which extracts integral values from any function
g(\xi)

As with the nodal basis, in order to define an edge basis, one looks for basis functions, now denoted
\psi^{(1)}
, that satisfy

\mathcal{N}^{1}_{j}(\psi^{(1)}_{i})=\int_{\xi_{j-1}}^{\xi_{j}}\psi^{(1)}_{i}(\xi)\,d\xi=\delta_{ij}.

(33)

The edge basis functions on the GLL-grid can be expressed in terms of the nodal basis as
[
27
]

\psi^{(1)}_{i}(\xi):=-\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi^{(0)}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}{\xi}},\quad\quad i=1,2,\ldots,p.

(34)

An example of these basis functions for
p=3
is shown in
Figure

1(b)
. Note that the space spanned by these edge functions is the space of polynomials of degree
(p-1)
.

3.2
Dual basis

Based on the primal basis introduced in the previous section, one can derive an associated dual basis. Before doing so, we first review the definition of a dual space.

Definition 3.1

(
[
28
]
)
.

A
linear functional

\alpha
defined on a vector space
\mathcal{E}
is a real-valued linear function that is a mapping
\alpha:\mathcal{E}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}

\alpha(a\boldsymbol{u}+b\boldsymbol{v})=a\alpha(\boldsymbol{u})+b\alpha(\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\quad\forall\>\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{E}\>\text{ and }\>\forall\>a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

(35)

If
\mathcal{E}
is a finite-dimensional vector space with
n
dimensions which has
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
as its basis, any arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{E}
can be expanded as a linear combination of the basis vectors;
\boldsymbol{v}=\displaystyle\sum_{j}v^{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
. Subsequently, applying the functional
\alpha
to
\boldsymbol{v}
yields:

\alpha(\boldsymbol{v})=\alpha\left(\sum_{j}v^{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right)=\sum_{j}v^{j}\alpha(\boldsymbol{e}_{j}).

(36)

Note that the linear functional
\alpha
does
not
live in the vector space
\mathcal{E}
. Instead, the linear functional lives in a different space defined as follows.

Definition 3.2

(
[
28
]
)
.

The collection of all the linear functionals on the vector space
\mathcal{E}
form a separate vector space referred to as the
dual space
denoted by
\mathcal{E^{*}}
. If
\mathcal{E}
is n-dimensional, then so is
\mathcal{E^{*}}
, which enables one to define the
dual basis

\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2},\ldots,\sigma^{n}
. The elements in the dual space are referred to as
covectors
. Furthermore, the canonical dual basis satisfies the property:

\sigma^{i}(\boldsymbol{e}_{j})=\delta^{i}_{j},

(37)

which, along with the linearity of the
\sigma^{i}
, can be used to show that applying
\sigma^{i}
to a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{E}
simply extracts the
i^{th}
component of
\boldsymbol{v}

\sigma^{i}\left(\sum_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}v^{j}\right)=\sum_{j}\sigma^{i}(\boldsymbol{e}_{j})v^{j}=\sum_{j}\delta^{i}_{j}v^{j}=v^{i}.

(38)

The above definition can be used to construct a discrete dual space based on the primal spaces of
Section

3.1
. For the MSEM, the
L^{2}
inner product was used to create such a space
[
26
]
, which will be briefly explained in the next section.

3.3
L^{2}
dual basis

To construct the dual bases, we represent
p^{h}
with respect to a nodal basis as

p^{h}(\xi)=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\mathcal{N}_{i}^{0}(p^{h})\psi_{i}^{(0)}(\xi).

(39)

In this equation,
\mathcal{N}_{i}^{0}(p^{h})
are the nodal degrees of freedom, and
\psi^{(0)}_{i}(\xi)
are the associated basis functions (see (
31
)). For clarity, the following shorthand notation will be used
[
26
]
,

p^{h}(\xi)=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(\xi)\mathcal{N}^{0}(p^{h}),

(40)

where

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(\xi)=\left[\psi^{(0)}_{0}(\xi)\;\;\psi^{(0)}_{1}(\xi)\;\;\cdots\;\;\psi^{(0)}_{p-1}(\xi)\;\;\psi^{(0)}_{p}(\xi)\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{N}^{0}(p^{h})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{N}_{0}^{0}(p^{h})\\[6.0pt]
\mathcal{N}_{1}^{0}(p^{h})\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{N}_{p-1}^{0}(p^{h})\\[6.0pt]
\mathcal{N}_{p}^{0}(p^{h})\end{array}\right].

(41)

Now, let
p^{h}
and
q^{h}
be expanded as in (
40
). The
L^{2}
-inner product between the two is then given by

\left(p^{h},q^{h}\right)_{L^{2}(K)}=\int_{K}p^{h}(\xi)q^{h}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}K=\mathcal{N}^{0}(p^{h})^{T}\mathbb{M}^{(0)}\mathcal{N}^{0}(q^{h}),

(42)

where
\mathbb{M}^{(0)}
denotes the mass matrix built using the nodal basis functions as

\mathbb{M}^{(0)}=\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(\xi)^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}K.

(43)

This mass matrix, and its inverse, can be used to define the dual degrees of freedom and the associated dual basis. The dual edge degrees of freedom (dual to the primal nodes) are then defined as

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{1}(p^{h})=\mathbb{M}^{(0)}\mathcal{N}^{0}(p^{h}).

(44)

Note that these are the degrees of freedom for the dual line elements. The dual basis functions are then

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(1)}(\xi)=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}(\xi)\mathbb{M}^{(0)^{-1}}.

(45)

See
[
26
]
for the details and proofs. The plots of these dual edge polynomials are shown in
Figure

2(a)
.

Similarly, the basis dual to the primal edge polynomials can also be constructed. Defining
p^{h}
and
q^{h}
as

p^{h}(\xi)=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}(\xi)\mathcal{N}^{1}(p^{h}),\quad\quad q^{h}(\xi)=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}(\xi)\mathcal{N}^{1}(q^{h})

(46)

with

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}(\xi)=\left[\psi^{(1)}_{1}(\xi)\;\;\psi^{(1)}_{2}(\xi)\;\;\cdots\;\;\psi^{(1)}_{p-1}(\xi)\;\;\psi^{(1)}_{p}(\xi)\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{N}^{1}(p^{h})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{N}_{1}^{1}(p^{h})\\[6.0pt]
\mathcal{N}_{2}^{1}(p^{h})\\
\vdots\\
\mathcal{N}_{p-1}^{1}(p^{h})\\[6.0pt]
\mathcal{N}_{p}^{1}(p^{h})\end{array}\right].

(47)

The
L^{2}
-inner product is now given by

\left(p^{h},q^{h}\right)_{L^{2}(K)}=\int_{K}p^{h}(\xi)q^{h}(\xi)\,dK=\mathcal{N}^{1}(p^{h})^{T}\mathbb{M}^{1}\mathcal{N}^{1}(q^{h}),

(48)

where
\mathbb{M}^{(1)}
denotes the mass matrix built using the edge basis functions as

\mathbb{M}^{(1)}=\int_{K}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}(\xi)^{T}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}(\xi)\,dK.

(49)

Constructing the dual degrees of freedom and dual basis follows the exact same procedure as in the nodal case. Thus, the dual nodal degrees of freedom (dual to the primal edges) are defined as

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{0}(p^{h})=\mathbb{M}^{(1)}\mathcal{N}^{1}(p^{h}).

(50)

Note that these are the degrees of freedom for the dual nodal elements. The dual basis functions are then

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(0)}(\xi)=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}(\xi)\mathbb{M}^{(1)^{-1}}.

(51)

See again
[
26
]
for the details and proofs. The plots of these dual polynomials are shown in
Figure

2(b)
.

and

3.4
Projections

We define the projections as follows

Definition 3.3
.

Consider a real-valued infinite-dimensional function space
V
and a finite-dimensional function subspace
\bar{V}
. A
projection
is the mapping
\mathcal{P}:V\xrightarrow{}\bar{V}
from the infinite-dimensional space to the finite-dimensional one which minimises the difference between the infinite and finite-dimensional representations in a given norm.

3.4.1
L^{2}
projection

Effectively, the projection maps continuous quantities (functions) into the discrete finite-dimensional subspace such that the best possible discrete representation in the considered norm is obtained. We will show that dual basis functions encode the projector. To demonstrate this fact, consider the
L^{2}
projection
\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}}
of a function
\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
The minimisation problem thus reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}}\phi=\argmin_{\bar{\phi}\in\bar{V}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bar{\phi}-\phi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right\}\;,

(55)

which gives

\displaystyle\left(v^{h},\bar{\phi}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(v^{h},\phi\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall v^{h}\in\bar{V}.

(56)

Considering a 1D case and expanding
\bar{\phi}
in terms of primal edge polynomials and
v_{h}
in terms of the corresponding dual basis given by (
50
) and (
51
)

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}:=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}\mathcal{N}^{1}(\bar{\phi})

(57)

\displaystyle v^{h}:=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(0)}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{0}(v^{h}),

(58)

we obtain after substitution of these expansions into (
56
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{0}(v^{h})^{T}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(0)},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\mathcal{N}^{1}(\bar{\phi})=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{0}(v^{h})^{T}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(0)},\phi\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}^{0}(v^{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

(59)

Using the fact that the primal and the dual bases are bi-orthogonal, i.e.
\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(0)}_{i},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}_{j}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\delta_{i,j}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{1}(\bar{\phi})=\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{L^{2}},\phi\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(60)

where we introduced
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{L^{2}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(0)}
.
This indicates that the inner product between the
L^{2}
dual basis and any
\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
returns the expansion coefficients for the projection of
\phi
onto
\bar{V}
. The projected solution is then obtained using the primal edge functions, (

). The plots of these

\displaystyle\left(v^{h},\bar{\phi}\right)_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}=\left(v^{h},\phi\right)_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)},\quad\forall v^{h}\in\bar{V}.

(62)

For the 1D case, we substitute

\displaystyle-\Delta_{h}v^{h}:=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}\mathcal{N}^{0}(v^{h})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v^{h}=\left(-\Delta^{-1}_{h}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}\right)\mathcal{N}^{0}(v^{h}),

into (

) where

\displaystyle u(x)=0,\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

4.3
Computing the fine-scale terms of a projection for 1D advection-diffusion

In a manner similar to that considered for the 1D Poisson equation, we can apply this approach to construct the Fine-Scale Greens’ function for the 1D advection-diffusion equation. The governing equation for the 1D advection-diffusion problem reads as follows

\displaystyle c\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}-\nu\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}=f,\quad x\in\Omega=[0,1]

(91)

\displaystyle u(x)=0,\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

(92)

with the constants,
c
and
\nu
being the advection speed and diffusion coefficient, respectively.

In this simple 1D setting, the global Greens’ function for the advection-diffusion equation in
\Omega
can be found to be

\displaystyle g(x,s)=\begin{cases}\frac{1-e^{-2\alpha\left(1-\frac{x}{h}\right)}}{c\left(1-e^{-2\alpha}\right)}\left(1-e^{-2\alpha\frac{s}{h}}\right),\quad\>\>\quad&x\leq s\\
\frac{e^{2\alpha\frac{x}{h}}-1}{c\left(1-e^{-2\alpha}\right)}\left(e^{-2\alpha\frac{s}{h}}-e^{-2\alpha}\right),\quad&x>s\end{cases}

(93)

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{ch}{2\nu},

(94)

where
h
is the domain width and
\alpha
is the mesh Peclet number. In theory, we can take this Greens’ function and construct the Fine-Scale Greens’ function for any projector using the aforementioned approach. This poses two notable complications, however, one relating to the integration of the Greens’ function’s sharp gradients at high Peclet numbers and the second being the loss of generality in multi-dimensional settings where the global Greens’ function of the advection-diffusion problem is not readily available. We therefore adopt an alternate approach for the advection-diffusion problem which bypasses these shortcomings.

This entails rewriting the advection-diffusion equation as a Poisson problem with a modified right hand side term as follows

\displaystyle-\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}=\frac{1}{\nu}f-\frac{c}{\nu}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x},

(95)

for which the resolved component of the variational form reads

4.5
Iterative VMS approach

The need for the derivative of the exact solution in the above demonstration can be avoided using an iterative procedure. The goal of this iterative approach is to produce a VMS formulation for the advection-diffusion equation which yields the exact projection and the missing fine scales. Here, we focus on the
H_{0}^{1}
projection, motivated by the fact that the exact solution lives in the
H_{0}^{1}
space. We note, however, that this iterative concept is not limited to
H_{0}^{1}
and can be extended to other projections.

To construct an iterative approach that solves for
H_{0}^{1}
projection, we start with the resolved scale equation from (
96

v={\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{H_{0}^{1}}
as the test functions and apply an
L^{2}

\displaystyle-\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}},\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}},\frac{1}{\nu}f\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}},\frac{c}{\nu}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(101)

If we apply integration by parts to the left-hand side, we get

\displaystyle\cancel{-\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_0^1} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} \Bigg|_{\partial\Omega}}+\left(\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}}}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}},\frac{1}{\nu}f\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\cancel{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}}u\Bigg|_{\partial\Omega}}+\left(\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}}}{\partial x},\frac{c}{\nu}u\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(102)

where the boundary terms cancel due to
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}}
being zero at the boundaries. Furthermore, the remaining term on the left can be identified to be a duality pairing in
H_{0}^{1}
, which exactly yields the expansion coefficients of the projection (
\mathcal{N}^{0}(\bar{u})
) as noted in (
67

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{0}(\bar{u})=\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}},\frac{1}{\nu}f\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\dfrac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}^{H_{0}^{1}}}{\partial x},\frac{c}{\nu}\left(\bar{u}+u^{\prime}\right)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\rightarrow\quad\quad\bar{u}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(0)}\mathcal{N}^{0}(\bar{u})

\displaystyle u^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\mathscr{R}\bar{u}=\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}f-\frac{c}{\nu}\dfrac{\partial\bar{u}}{\partial x}-\frac{c}{\nu}\dfrac{\partial u^{\prime}}{\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial^{2}\bar{u}}{\partial x^{2}}\right),

(104)

6
Summary

In this paper, we have proposed a new approach for explicitly constructing the Fine-Scale Greens’ function by employing the concept of dual basis functions. We have shown that these dual basis functions encode the projector and are explicitly computable thus making them an appropriate choice for the
\boldsymbol{\mu}
’s presented in
[
25
]
. Moreover, the constructed Fine-Scale Greens’ function is clearly shown to successfully reconstruct all the missing fine scales truncated with the projection. Since the dual basis functions naturally extend to higher dimensions, we have a generalised approach for computing the Fine-Scale Greens’ function for any projector in arbitrary dimensions. Furthermore, even though we solely focus on the Fine-Scale Greens’ functions for the Poisson equation, we have demonstrated that it may be employed in VMS approaches for other problems such as the steady advection-diffusion problem albeit in a non-monolithic manner. We do remark that while everything we presented was implemented in the context of the MSEM, the concepts of the dual basis may be employed in any other framework to produce an appropriate Fine-Scale Greens’ function. Regarding future work, a natural continuation of this paper will be the development of numerical integration rules that accurately and efficiently integrate the Fine-Scale Greens’ function. Subsequently, the methodology could be expanded to address increasingly intricate non-linear problems, such as the Navier-Stokes equations, with consideration given to multi-dimensional advection-diffusion problems as intermediary stages.

Discrete Laplacian operator in 2D

In this section, we shall describe the construction of the discrete Laplacian operator used for defining the
H_{0}^{1}
projection in 2D from
Section

5
. We start with a Poisson problem defined as follows

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\nabla\phi=f,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega

(119)

\displaystyle\phi(\boldsymbol{x})=0,\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega.

(120)

We then rewrite the problem in a mixed formulation by defining a vector field
\underline{u}
as the gradient of
\phi

\displaystyle\underline{u}-\nabla\phi=0

(121)

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\underline{u}=f.

(122)

The Galerkin weak form of this mixed formulation is obtained by testing (
121
) with
\underline{v}\in H(div,\Omega)
and (
122
) with
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\left(\underline{v},\underline{u}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\nabla\cdot\underline{v},\phi\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi\underline{v}\cdot\underline{n}\>\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\quad\forall\underline{v}\in H(div,\Omega)

(123)

\displaystyle-\left(\varphi,\nabla\cdot\underline{u}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\left(\varphi,f\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(124)

where we have performed integration by parts on the gradient of
\phi
and the emerging boundary integral is used to weakly impose the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition by setting it to zero. Carrying out this integration by parts is what allows us to eventually perform the
H_{0}^{1}
projection in a discontinuous space.

An important remark about the choice of function spaces is that they satisfy the De Rham sequence, i.e. applying the divergence to an element of
H(div,\Omega)
maps the element to
L^{2}(\Omega)

H(div,\Omega)\xrightarrow{\nabla\cdot}L^{2}(\Omega).

This property must also be satisfied at the discrete level when selecting the finite-element subspaces, see
[
31
]
.

Transforming (
123
) and (
124
) into the discrete setting by expanding
\phi
in the space of dual nodal basis functions (

) and
\underline{u}
in (2D) edge basis functions (
\underline{u}=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{u}
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{M}^{(1)}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla^{T}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}=0

(125)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{\hat{f}},

(126)

where

(2D edge basis functions
\left(\psi^{(1)}_{i},\psi^{(1)}_{j}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\nabla_{h}\cdot
