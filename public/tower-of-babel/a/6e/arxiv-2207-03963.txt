The diagonal behaviour of the one-particle Coulombic density matrix
Peter Hearnshaw, Alexander V. Sobolev

Abstract
. We obtain bounds for all derivatives of the non-relativistic Coulombic one-particle density matrix γ ⁡ ( x , y ) \gamma(x,y) near the diagonal x = y x=y .



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 35B65; Secondary 35J10, 81V55

1.
Introduction

Consider on
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
the Schrödinger operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{H}_{0}+V,\quad\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\Delta=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta_{k},

(1.1)

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\ -Z\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{|x_{k}|}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|},

describing an atom with
N
particles
(e.g. electrons)
with coordinates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
,
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
k=1,2,\dots,N
,
and a nucleus with charge
Z>0
.
The notation
\Delta_{k}
is used for
the Laplacian w.r.t. the variable
x_{k}
.
The operator
\mathcal{H}
acts on the Hilbert space
\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
and by
standard methods one proves that it is self-adjoint on the domain

D(\mathcal{H})=\textup{{{W}}}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
,
see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem X.16]
. (Here and throughout the paper we use the standard notation

\textup{{{W}}}^{l,p}
for the Sobolev spaces, where
l
and
p
indicate the smoothness and summability
respectively).
Our methods allow consideration of the molecular
Schrödinger operator, but we restrict our attention to the atomic case for simplicity.
Let
\psi=\psi(\mathbf{x})
,
be an eigenfunction of the operator
\mathcal{H}
with an eigenvalue
E\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\psi\in D(\mathcal{H})
and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}-E)\psi=0.

For each
j=1,\dots,N
, we represent

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(x_{j},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}),\quad\textup{where}\ \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N}),

with obvious modifications if
j=1
or
j=N
.
The one-particle density matrix is defined as the function

(1.2)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\overline{\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}.

This function is one of the key objects in
the multi-particle quantum mechanics, see

[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
for details and futher references.
If one assumes that all
N
particles are spinless
fermions (resp. bosons), i.e. that the function
\psi
is
antisymmetric (resp. symmetric) under the permutations
x_{j}\leftrightarrow x_{k}
,
then the definition (
1.2
) simplifies:

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(x,y)=N\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\overline{\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})}d\hat{\mathbf{x}},\ \quad\textup{where}\ \hat{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}.

Our main result however does not require any symmetry assumptions.
We are interested in the smoothness properties of the function (
1.2
). It is clear that
for this purpose it suffices to study each term in (
1.2
) individually.
Moreover, using permutations of the variables it is sufficient to focus just on one term on the
right-hand side of (
1.2
):

(1.3)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\overline{\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}},\ \quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{N}).

Throughout the paper we refer to this function as the
one-particle density matrix
.
In
[
7
]
the
one-particle density

(1.4)

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\gamma(x,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}|\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})|^{2}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}},

was shown to be
a real-analytic function of
x\not=0
.
The real analyticity of the function
\gamma(x,y)
as a
function of two variables on the domain

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{(x,y):|x|\,|y|\not=0,\quad x\not=y\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},

was proved in
[
11
]
.
As was pointed out in
[
11
]
,
one cannot expect analyticity in
x
and
y
to hold on the diagonal
x=y
.
In fact, quantum chemistry calculations in

[
2
]
(see also
[
3
]
) show that in the neighbourhood of the diagonal

x=y\not=0
, the function
\gamma(x,y)
has the following behaviour:

(1.6)

\displaystyle\re\gamma(x,y)-\gamma(x,x)=C(x)|x-y|^{5}+o(|x-y|^{5}),\quad\textup{as}\quad y\to x,

with some non-zero function
C(x)
.
Motivated in part by the formula (
1.6
),
in the current paper we aim to obtain
explicit bounds for all partial derivatives of
\gamma(x,y)

near the diagonal
x=y
or the points
x=0
and
y=0
.
For the derivatives of
\gamma
we use the standard notation
\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{y}^{l}\gamma(x,y)
,
where
m,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,

\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
For two non-negative numbers (or functions)

X
and
Y
depending on some parameters,
we write
X\lesssim Y
(or
Y\gtrsim X
) if
X\leq CY
with
some positive constant
C
independent of those parameters.
The notation
B(x,R)
is used for the open ball of radius
R
centred at the point
x
.

For
b\geq 0
,
t>0
we define

(1.7)

\displaystyle h_{b}(t)=\begin{cases}1,\ \textup{if}\ b<5,\\
\log\big(t^{-1}+2),\ \textup{if}\ b=5,\\
1+t^{5-b},\ \textup{if}\ b>5.\end{cases}

The next theorem contains the main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\psi
be an eigenfunction, and let
R>0
be fixed.
Then for all
x\not=0,y\not=0
,
x\not=y
, and all
l,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
such that

|l|\geq 1,|m|\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{l}\partial_{y}^{m}\gamma(x,y)|\lesssim\big(1+|x|^{2-|l|-|m|}+|y|^{2-|l|-|m|}

\displaystyle+h_{|l|+|m|}(|x-y|)\big)

(1.8)

\displaystyle\ \times\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Furthermore, for all
|l|\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{l}\gamma(x,y)|+|\partial_{y}^{l}\gamma(x,y)|\lesssim\big(1+|x|^{1-|l|}+|y|^{1-|l|}

\displaystyle\ +h_{|l|}(|x-y|)\big)

(1.9)

\displaystyle\ \times\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The implicit constants in (
1.1
) and (
1.1
)
may depend on
l
,
m
and
R
, but are independent of
x
,
y
and the function
\psi
.

Remark 1.2
.

(1)

Theorem
1.1
naturally extends
the case of a molecule with several nuclei whose positions
are fixed. The modifications are straightforward.

(2)

Observe that
\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,R))}\leq\|\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})}^{2}
,
R>0
,
so that the right-hand sides of
(
1.1
) and (
1.1
) are finite.

(3)

The bound (
1.1
)
for
|l|=1
implies that
\gamma(x,y)
is a Lipschitz function on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

(4)

The bounds (
1.1
), (
1.1
) ensure that

\gamma\in\textup{{{W}}}^{5,p}_{\textup{\tiny loc}}(\mathcal{D})

with arbitrary
p<\infty
, see (
1.5
) for the definition of
\mathcal{D}
.
By Proposition
7.1
in the Appendix, this implies that

\gamma\in\textup{{{W}}}^{5,p}_{\textup{\tiny loc}}\big((\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})\big)
,
which means
that
\gamma\in\textup{{{C}}}^{4,\theta}_{\textup{\tiny loc}}\big((\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})\big)

for all
\theta<1
. This result just barely misses the
\textup{{{C}}}^{4,1}
-smoothness
of the factor
|x-y|^{5}
in the formula (
1.6
).
In this sense Theorem

1.1
is sharp, up to a
\log
-term.

(5)

The one-electron density
\rho(x)=\gamma(x,x)

is known to be real analytic for
x\not=0
, see
[
7
]
.
As shown in
[
8
]
, the function
\rho(x)
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{l}\rho(x)|\lesssim\big(1+|x|^{1-|l|}\big)\,\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,R))},\quad\textup{for all}\quad l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3},\quad x\not=0.

For
|l|\leq 4
this bound
follows from (
1.1
) and
(
1.1
).

(6)

There is an independent (indirect)
argument indicating that
\gamma(x,y)
should have
a
|x-y|^{5}
-singularity on the diagonal. More precisely,
we can show that if the function
\gamma(x,y)
has on the diagonal
a singularity of the type
|x-y|^{b}
with some
b>-3
, then necessarily
b=5
.
This argument is based on the analysis of spectral asymptotics
of the (non-negative) operator

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
with kernel
\tilde{\gamma}(x,y)
. It was shown in
[
17
]

that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
have asymptotics of order
k^{-8/3}
as
k\to\infty
.
On the other hand, according to the results
on spectral asymptotics for integral operators
with homogeneous kernels, see
[
1
]
(and also

[
17
]
for a summary),
the singularity
|x-y|^{b}
,
b\not=0,2,4,\dots
,
would produce the spectral asymptotics of order
k^{-(1+b/3)}
.
The exponent
1+b/3
coincides with
8/3
exactly for
b=5
, which proves the point.

Our proofs have two main ingredients.
At the heart of our method are regularity properties of the eigenfunction
\psi
.
By the standard elliptic argument, the function
\psi
is real analytic away from the singularities
of the potential (
1.1
), i.e. away from the particle coalescence points. According
to T. Kato’s seminal paper
[
12
]
, at the coalescence points the function

\psi
is Lipschitz. A detailed study of smoothness properties of
\psi

was conducted in the recent paper

[
8
]
, to which we also refer for further bibliography. In particular, this paper provides global
pointwise bounds for partial derivatives of
\psi
.
In the study of the one-particle density (
1.4
) conducted in
[
8
]
the key point was
to obtain bounds for certain directional derivatives of
\psi
.
Such derivatives are also critical for our analysis in the current paper, and we explain their importance
below.

Let
{\sf{R}}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
be the set of all particle labels.
A subset
{\sf P}\subset{\sf{R}}
is called
cluster
.
For each cluster
{\sf P}
we define the following

cluster (directional) derivative
:

(1.10)

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{{\sf P}}^{m}=

\displaystyle\ \biggl(\sum_{k\in{\sf P}}\partial_{x_{k}^{\prime}}\biggr)^{m^{\prime}}\bigg(\sum_{k\in{\sf P}}\partial_{x_{k}^{\prime\prime}}\bigg)^{m^{\prime\prime}}\bigg(\sum_{k\in{\sf P}}\partial_{x_{k}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\bigg)^{m^{\prime\prime\prime}},

Here
m=(m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime\prime})
with
m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and

x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime})
with
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that for all
m\not=0
,

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{\sf P}^{m}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}=0,\quad\textup{if}\quad j,k\in{\sf P}\quad\textup{or}\quad j,k\notin{\sf P}.

Therefore the potential (
1.1
) is infinitely smooth with respect to
{\sf D}_{\sf P}
as long as

x_{j}\not=0,j=1,2,\dots,N,
and
x_{j}\not=x_{k}
, where
j\in{\sf P},k\notin{\sf P}

or
j\notin{\sf P},k\in{\sf P}
.
As a consequence, the function
\psi
is also infinitely smooth with respect to
{\sf D}_{\sf P}

for the same values of the coordinates,
see
[
7
]
or
[
11
]
. In particular, the cluster derivatives of
\psi

do not have singularities at the coalescence points
x_{j}=x_{k}
, if
j,k\in{\sf P}

or
j,k\notin{\sf P}
.
One of the pivotal points in
[
8
]
was the pointwise
bound for the cluster derivative
{\sf D}_{\sf P}^{m}\psi(\mathbf{x})
with explicit dependence on the distance
of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
to the coalescence set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{{\sf P}}=\bigg\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}:\prod_{j\in{\sf P}}|x_{j}|\prod_{k\in{\sf P},l\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}}|x_{k}-x_{l}|=0\bigg\}.

For our purposes we need bounds of such type
for cluster derivatives
involving an arbitrary finite number
M
of clusters
{\sf P}_{1},{\sf P}_{2},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}
,
i.e. for
{\sf D}_{{\sf P}_{1}}^{m_{1}}{\sf D}_{{\sf P}_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots{\sf D}_{{\sf P}_{M}}^{m_{M}}\psi
,
see Corollary
4.6
.
This generalization is not immediate and requires substantial further work, which is done in Sect.

3
and
4
.

The next step of the proof is to use the bounds obtained for cluster
derivatives to estimate partial derivatives of the function (
1.3
). To
this end we use a partition of unity consisting of smooth functions
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})
of

3N+3
variables that we call
extended cut-off functions
, and study the integrals

(1.11)

\displaystyle\gamma(x,y;\Phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}}\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\overline{\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}.

Extended cut-offs were introduced in

[
11
]
to establish the
real analyticity of
\gamma(x,y)
outside the diagonal.

The support of each extended cut-off divides the particles
x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{N}

into three disjoint groups: the first two
groups consist of particles that are “close” to the particles
x
and
y
respectively, and the third one
contains the particles that are “far” from the first two groups.
This partition naturally gives rise to two clusters, denoted
{\sf P}
and
{\sf S}
: cluster
{\sf P}

labels the particles close
to
x
, and cluster
{\sf S}
– the particles close to
y
.
Thus, when differentiating the integral
(
1.11
) w.r.t.
x
and
y
, under the integral
these derivatives convert into the cluster derivatives
{\sf D}_{\sf P}
and
{\sf D}_{\sf S}
respectively.
The actual calculation is more involved, but we can
illustrate this conversion using the following simplified example.
Let us differentiate with respect to
x
the integral

\displaystyle F(x)=\int f(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})d\hat{\mathbf{x}},

where the integration is conducted over the space
\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
, and we assume for simplicity that
f\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
.
To this end under the integral we make the change of variables

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{w}}+\hat{\mathbf{z}}
, where

\hat{\mathbf{z}}=(z_{2},z_{3},\dots,z_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
is defined by

z_{j}=x,j\in{\sf P},
and
z_{j}=0,j\notin{\sf P}
, for some cluster
{\sf P}
.
Thus
F(x)
rewrites as

\displaystyle F(x)=\int f(x,\hat{\mathbf{w}}+\hat{\mathbf{z}})\,d\hat{\mathbf{w}}.

Consequently, for all
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{l}F(x)=\int\big({\sf D}_{\sf P}^{l}f\big)(x,\hat{\mathbf{w}}+\hat{\mathbf{z}})\,d\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\int{\sf D}_{\sf P}^{l}f(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}.

In order to estimate the derivatives of the integral
(
1.11
), we use the bounds for cluster derivatives of
\psi

obtained in the first stage of the proof. Integrating these bounds in

\hat{\mathbf{x}}
leads to (
1.1
) and (
1.1
) thereby completing the proof.
One should say that the application these bounds is not immediate,
but we defer the discussion of this technical step
until Section
6
, see Remark
6.1
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Sect.
2
we gather information about
cut-off functions and clusters associated with them. Most of the required facts are borrowed from

[
11
]
. Sect.
3

considers the general elliptic equation of the form (
3.1
) in a ball

B(\mathbf{x}_{0},\ell)=\{\mathbf{x}:|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|<\ell\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3N}
.
Here we study cluster derivatives of solutions of (
3.1
), and the main focus is on the
explicit dependence of these estimates on the radius
\ell
, see Theorem
3.2
.
In Sect.
4
these estimates are applied to the Schrödinger equation to derive the bounds
for the cluster derivatives of
\psi
, summarized in Corollary
4.6
.
Some estimates for integrals
emerging in the proof of Theorem
1.1
are gathered in Section
5
.
The proof itself
is completed in Sect.
6
.
The appendix (Section
7
)
contains an elementary extension property for the Sobolev spaces that was proved in
[
18
]
.

Notation.

We conclude the introduction with some general notational conventions.

Coordinates.

As mentioned earlier, we use the following standard notation for the coordinates:

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
, where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N
.
As a rule we represent
\mathbf{x}
in the form

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\hat{\mathbf{x}})
with

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
.

For
N\geq 3
it is also useful to introduce the notation
for
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
with
x_{j}
,
j\geq 2
, taken out. Let

(1.12)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=(x_{2},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N}),

so that
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{j},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{j},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
.

Clusters.

Let
{\sf{R}}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
.
A subset
{\sf P}\subset{\sf{R}}
is called

cluster
.
We denote
|{\sf P}|=\card{\sf P}
,

{\sf P}^{\rm c}={\sf{R}}\setminus{\sf P}
,
{\sf P}^{*}={\sf P}\setminus\{1\}
.
If
{\sf P}=\varnothing
, then
|{\sf P}|=0
and
{\sf P}^{\rm c}={\sf{R}}
.

For
M
clusters
{\sf P}_{1},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}
we write
\mathbf{P}=\{{\sf P}_{1},{\sf P}_{2},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}\}
,
and call
\mathbf{P}

cluster set
.
Clusters
{\sf P}_{1},{\sf P}_{2},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}
in a cluster set are not assumed to be all disjoint or distinct.

Derivatives.

Let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
If
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
m=(m^{\prime},m^{\prime\prime},m^{\prime\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
, then
the derivative
\partial_{x}^{m}
is defined in the standard way:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{m}=\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{m^{\prime}}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime}}^{m^{\prime\prime}}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime\prime}}^{m^{\prime\prime\prime}}.

This notation extends to
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with an arbitrary dimension
d\geq 1
in the obvious way.
Denote also

\displaystyle\partial^{\mathbf{m}}=\partial_{x_{1}}^{m_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots\partial_{x_{N}}^{m_{N}},\quad\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{N})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3N}.

Let
\mathbf{P}=\{{\sf P}_{1},{\sf P}_{2},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}\}
be a cluster set, and let

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{M})
,
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
k=1,2,\dots,M
. Then we denote

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{\mathbf{P}}^{\mathbf{m}}={\sf D}_{{\sf P}_{1}}^{m_{1}}{\sf D}_{{\sf P}_{2}}^{m_{2}}\cdots{\sf D}_{{\sf P}_{M}}^{m_{M}},

where each individual cluster derivative is defined as in (
1.10
).
It is easy to see that the cluster derivatives satisfy the Leibniz rule.
We use this fact without further comments throughout the paper.

Supports.

For any smooth function
f=f(\mathbf{x})
,
we define
\supp_{0}f=\{\mathbf{x}:f(\mathbf{x})\not=0\}
. It is clear that the closure
\overline{\supp_{0}f}

coincides with the support
\supp f
defined in the standard way.
With this definition we immediately get the useful
property that

\displaystyle\supp_{0}(fg)=\supp_{0}f\cap\supp_{0}g.

Bounds.

As explained earlier, for two non-negative numbers (or functions)

X
and
Y
depending on some parameters,
we write
X\lesssim Y
(or
Y\gtrsim X
) if
X\leq CY
with
some positive constant
C
independent of those parameters.
If
X\lesssim Y
and
Y\lesssim X
, then
X\asymp Y
.
To avoid confusion we often make explicit comments on the nature of
(implicit) constants in the bounds. In particular,
all constants (implicit or explicit) may depend on the eigenvalue
E
,
the number of particles
N
and the charge
Z
.

2.
Cut-off functions and clusters

2.1.
Admissible cut-off functions

Let

(2.1)

\displaystyle\xi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}):\ 0\leq\xi(t)\leq 1,\ \xi(t)=\begin{cases}1,\ {\rm if}\ |t|\leq 1,\\[5.69046pt]
0,\ {\rm if}\ |t|\geq 2.\end{cases}

Now for
\varepsilon>0
we define two radially-symmetric functions
\zeta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

\theta\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as follows:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\zeta(x)=\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x)=\xi\bigg(\frac{4N}{\varepsilon}|x|\bigg),\quad\theta(x)=\theta_{\varepsilon}(x)=1-\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

so that

\displaystyle\zeta(x)=0\quad\textup{for}\quad x\notin B\big(0,\varepsilon(2N)^{-1}\big),\qquad\qquad\theta(x)=0\quad\textup{for}\quad x\in B\big(0,\varepsilon(4N)^{-1}\big).

The dependence of the cut-offs on the parameter
\varepsilon
is important,
but it is not always reflected in the notation.

Our next step is to build out of the functions
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
and
\theta_{\varepsilon}

cut-off functions of
3N
variables.
Let
\{f_{jk}\},1\leq j,k\leq N
, be a set of functions such that
each of them is one of the functions
\zeta_{\varepsilon}

or
\theta_{\varepsilon}
, and

f_{jk}=f_{kj}
.
We call functions of the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi(\mathbf{x})=\prod\limits_{1\leq j<k\leq N}f_{jk}(x_{j}-x_{k}).

admissible cut-off functions

or simply
admissible cut-offs
.
Such cut-offs (or, more precisely, a slightly more general version thereof) were used
in
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and also in

We need only a subset of their properties established

[

]

[

]

We associate with the function
\phi

defined next.

Definition 2.1
.

For an admissible cut-off
\phi
, let
I(\phi)\subset\{(j,k)\in{\sf{R}}\times{\sf{R}}:j\not=k\}

be the index set such that

(j,k)\in I(\phi)
, iff
f_{jk}=\zeta
.
We say that two indices
j,k\in{\sf{R}}
,
are
\phi

to each other
if either
j=k
, or
(j,k)\in I(\phi)

there exists a sequence of pairwise

j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{s}

such that
(j,j_{1}),(j_{s},k)\in I(\phi)

(j_{p},j_{p+1})\in I(\phi)

The cluster
{\sf Q}(\phi)
associated with the cut-off
\phi

is defined as the set of all indices that are
\phi
-linked to index
1
.

It follows from the above definition that
{\sf Q}(\phi)
always contains index
1
. Note also that
the notion of being linked defines an equivalence relation on
{\sf{R}}
, and the cluster
{\sf Q}(\phi)
is
nothing but the equivalence class of index
1
.
On the support of the admissible cut-off
\phi
the variables

x_{j}
, indexed by
j\in{\sf Q}(\phi)
, are “close” to each other and are
“far” from the remaining variables. In order to quantify these facts below
we define a number of subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
and
\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}

For any cluster
{\sf P}
we
introduce the following sets depending on the parameter

\displaystyle X_{{\sf P}}(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}^{3N}\quad{\rm for}\ |{\sf P}|=0\ \textup{or}\ N,\\[5.69046pt]
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}:\ |x_{j}-x_{k}|>\varepsilon,\forall j\in{\sf P},k\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}\},\quad{\rm for}\ 0<|{\sf P}|<N,\end{cases}

The set

separates the points
x_{k}

and
x_{j}
labeled by the clusters
{\sf P}
and
{\sf P}^{\rm c}
respectively.
Note that
X_{{\sf P}}(\varepsilon)=X_{{\sf P}^{\rm c}}(\varepsilon)
.
Define also the sets separating

\displaystyle T_{\sf P}(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}^{3N},\quad{\rm for}\ |{\sf P}|=0,\\[5.69046pt]
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}:|x_{j}|>\varepsilon,\ \forall j\in{\sf P}\},\ \quad{\rm for}\ |{\sf P}|>0.\end{cases}

It is also convenient to introduce
corresponding sets in the space
\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{{\sf P}}(x,\varepsilon)=\{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}:(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\in X_{{\sf P}}(\varepsilon)\},\quad\textup{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

and

(2.5)

\displaystyle\widehat{T}_{{\sf P}}(\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}^{3N-3},\quad{\rm for}\ |{\sf P}^{*}|=0,\\[5.69046pt]
\{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}:|x_{j}|>\varepsilon,\ \forall j\in{\sf P}^{*}\},\ \quad{\rm for}\ |{\sf P}^{*}|>0.\end{cases}

Observe that
\widehat{T}_{{\sf P}}(\varepsilon)=\widehat{T}_{{\sf P}^{*}}(\varepsilon)

The support of the admissible cut-off
\phi
is easily described with the help of the sets introduced above.
The next proposition is adapted from

[

[

Proposition 2.2
.

For
{\sf P}={\sf Q}(\phi)
the inclusion

\displaystyle\supp_{0}\phi\subset X_{{\sf P}}\big(\varepsilon(4N)^{-1}\big)

holds.

Moreover, if
j\in{\sf Q}(\phi)

|x_{1}-x_{j}|<\varepsilon/2
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\supp_{0}\phi

If

\displaystyle\supp_{0}\phi(x_{1},\ \cdot\ )\subset\widehat{T}_{{\sf P}}(\varepsilon/2).

We do not use Proposition

present it in order to demonstrate the relevance of the associated cluster

.

2.2.
Extended cut-offs

In our analysis the central role
is played by another class of cut-off functions.
These cut-offs are
functions of
3N+3
variables and they are defined as follows: for each

x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
let

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\prod_{2\leq j\leq N}g_{j}(x-x_{j})\prod_{2\leq j\leq N}h_{j}(y-x_{j})\prod_{2\leq k<l\leq N}f_{kl}(x_{k}-x_{l}),

where each of the functions
g_{j},h_{j}
and
f_{jk}=f_{kj}
is one of the cut-offs

\theta
or
\zeta
defined in (

). We call such functions

extended
cut-offs.
Each extended cut-off uniquely defines two admissible cut-offs:

\displaystyle\phi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})=

\displaystyle\ \prod_{2\leq j\leq N}g_{j}(x-x_{j})\prod_{2\leq k<l\leq N}f_{kl}(x_{k}-x_{l}),

\displaystyle\mu(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})=

\displaystyle\ \prod_{2\leq j\leq N}h_{j}(y-x_{j})\prod_{2\leq k<l\leq N}f_{kl}(x_{k}-x_{l}),

see definition (
2.3
).
We say that the pair
\phi
,
\mu
and the extended
cut-off
\Phi
are associated to each other.
We denote by
{\sf P}={\sf Q}(\phi)
and
{\sf S}={\sf Q}(\mu)

the clusters associated with
\phi
and
\mu
respectively.

We remind that the functions
\phi,\mu
and
\Phi
all
depend on the parameter
\varepsilon
. Thus whenever necessary
we include
\varepsilon
in the notation and write, for example,
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)
.
Note however that the clusters
{\sf P}
and
{\sf S}
do not depend on
\varepsilon
.

Below we list some useful
properties
of the extended cut-offs
\Phi
and associated admissible
\phi
,
\mu
adapted from

[
11
, Lemmata 4.6, 4.8]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
{\sf P}^{*}\cap{\sf S}
is non-empty
and
|x-y|>\varepsilon
,
then
\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)=0

for all
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3}
.

In the estimates further on we assume, as a rule, that
|x-y|>\varepsilon
, so due
to Proposition
2.3
from now on we may suppose that

{\sf P}^{*}\subset{\sf S}^{\rm c}
(which is equivalent to
{\sf S}^{*}\subset{\sf P}^{\rm c}
).
Under this condition we obtain
the following information about the support of the extended cut-off
\Phi
:

Proposition 2.4
.

If
{\sf P}^{*}\subset{\sf S}^{\rm c}
, then for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have

\displaystyle\supp_{0}\Phi(x,y,\ \cdot\ ;\varepsilon)\subset\widehat{X}_{{\sf P}}\big(x,\varepsilon(4N)^{-1}\big)\cap

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{{\sf S}^{*}}\big(x,\varepsilon(4N)^{-1}\big)

(2.7)

\displaystyle\cap\widehat{X}_{{\sf S}}\big(y,\varepsilon(4N)^{-1}\big)\cap\widehat{X}_{{\sf P}^{*}}\big(y,\varepsilon(4N)^{-1}\big).

If, in addition

(2.8)

\displaystyle\supp_{0}\Phi(x,y,\ \cdot\ ;\varepsilon)\subset\widehat{T}_{{\sf P}^{*}}(\varepsilon/2)\cap\widehat{T}_{{\sf S}^{*}}(\varepsilon/2).

To complete this section we need to make a remark on the cluster derivatives of the
extended cut-offs. For any cluster
{\sf Q}\subset{\sf{R}}

we denote by
{\sf D}_{x,{\sf Q}}^{m}\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})

the cluster derivative of
\Phi
as a function of the variables

, i.e.

(2.9)

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{x,{\sf Q}}^{m}\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})={\sf D}_{{\sf Q}}^{m}\tilde{\Phi}_{y}(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}}),\quad\textup{where}\quad\tilde{\Phi}_{y}(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}}):=\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}}).

.
It immediately follows from the definition of

that
for any clusters
{\sf Q}_{1},{\sf Q}_{2}
and all

\displaystyle|{\sf D}_{x,{\sf Q}_{1}}^{m_{1}}{\sf D}_{y,{\sf Q}_{2}}^{m_{2}}\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)|\lesssim\begin{cases}1,\quad\textup{if}\quad|\mathbf{m}|=0,\\[5.69046pt]
\varepsilon^{-|\mathbf{m}|}\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}}),\quad\textup{if}\quad|\mathbf{m}|\geq 1,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\varepsilon}(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\xi\big(N\varepsilon^{-1}|x-x_{j}|\big)

(2.11)

\displaystyle\ +\sum_{2\leq j\leq N}\xi\big(N\varepsilon^{-1}|y-x_{j}|\big)+\sum_{2\leq j<k\leq N}\xi\big(N\varepsilon^{-1}|x_{j}-x_{k}|\big),

see (

) for the definition of the function

.

3.
Regularity estimates

3.1.
\textup{{{C}}}^{1}
-regularity for elliptic equations

In what follows we rely on the well-known
\textup{{{C}}}^{1}
-regularity bounds
for solutions of second order elliptic equations on bounded domains.
This type of regularity is discussed e.g. in
[
13
, Ch. 3]
and

[
9
, Ch. 8]
. To be precise, in
[
13
, Ch. 3]
and

[
9
, Ch. 8]
one can find bounds even
in the space
\textup{{{C}}}^{1,\theta}
with a suitable
\theta\in(0,1)
, but
we are not concerned with the Hölder properties.
Moreover, in this paper we do not need the most general form of the equation.
For our purposes it suffices to consider the equation

(3.1)

\displaystyle\big(-\Delta+\mathbf{a}(x)\cdot\nabla+b(x)\big)u=g,

on an open ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
all the coefficients are
\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B)
-functions.
The proposition below provides some convenient
\max
-bounds for the
weak solution and its first derivatives. Since the proof is quite short, we
provide it for the sake of completeness.
Note that the proof of
[
8
, Proposition A.2]

uses similar ideas.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
B_{R}=B(x_{0},R)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
R>0
.
Suppose that
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}(B_{R})
is a
weak solution of the equation (
3.1
),
where
\mathbf{a},b,g\in\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{R})
, and

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{R})}+\|b\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{R})}\leq M,

with some constant
M>0
.
Then for any
r\in(0,R)
the function
u
belongs
to
\textup{{{W}}}^{2,2}(B_{r})\cap\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{r}})
and

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{r}})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B_{R})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{R})},

with an implicit constant that depends only on the constant

M
, dimension
d
and the radii
r
and
R
.

Proof.

The inclusion
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{2,2}(B_{r})
is a direct consequence of the interior regularity.

In order to prove that the weak solution
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}(B_{R})
has
the
\textup{{{C}}}^{1}
-regularity in
B_{r}
we repeatedly apply the following
elementary fact.

Assume that
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}(B_{\rho})
with some
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\rho\leq R
. Then for any

\nu<\rho
the following is true:

(1)

If
p\leq d
, then
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q}(B_{\nu})
with
q=p(1+d^{-1})
and

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q}(B_{\nu})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{p}(B_{\rho})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{p}(B_{\rho})}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}(B_{\rho})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}.

(2)

If
p>d
, then
u\in\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{\nu}})
and

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{\nu}})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{p}(B_{\rho})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{p}(B_{\rho})}

(3.4)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}(B_{\rho})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}.

Indeed, under the assumption
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,p}(B_{\rho})
,
by the interior regularity we have
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{2,p}(B_{\nu})
and the standard bound
holds (see, e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 9.11]
):

(3.5)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{2,p}(B_{\nu})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{p}(B_{\rho})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{p}(B_{\rho})}.

If
p<d
, then we use the bounded embedding
\textup{{{W}}}^{2,p}\subset\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q}
, for all

q\in[p,p^{*}]
,
p^{*}=dp(d-p)^{-1}
. In particular, the value

q=p(1+d^{-1})
belongs to the interval
[p,p^{*}]
, which proves
(
1
).
If
p=d
, then
\textup{{{W}}}^{2,p}\subset\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q}
for all
q\in[p,\infty)
, and hence
for
q=p(1+d^{-1})
in particular. Hence (
1
) holds again.

If
p>d
, then we use the embedding
\textup{{{W}}}^{2,p}\subset\textup{{{C}}}^{1}
, so that
(
3.5
) leads to (
2
).

Let us proceed with the proof of (
3.2
).
If
d=1
, then the solution
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}(R)
satisfies (
2
) with

p=2
, which immediately implies (
3.2
).
Suppose that
d\geq 2
, and define the sequence

\displaystyle q_{n}=2(1+d^{-1})^{n},\ n=0,1,\dots.

Let
k\geq 1
be the index such that
q_{k-1}\leq d
and
q_{k}>d
.
Pick finitely many numbers
r_{n}>0
such that

\displaystyle r<r_{k}<r_{k-1}<\dots<r_{1}<r_{0}=R.

Since
u\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}(B_{R})
we can apply the bound (
1
) with
\nu=r_{1},\rho=R

and
p=q_{0}=2
,
q=q_{1}
. Repeating this step successively for
\rho=r_{n},\nu=r_{n+1}
and

p=q_{n}
,
q=q_{n+1}

for all
n=1,\dots,k
, we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q_{k}}(B_{r_{k}})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q_{k-1}}(B_{r_{k-1}})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r_{k-1}})}\lesssim\dots

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152541}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}}}}(B_{r_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152541}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}}}})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r_{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{207628}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152540}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle 1$}}}}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{\resizebox{152541}{196608}{\hbox{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle 1$}}}}}}}})}\lesssim\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B_{R})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{R})}.

As
q_{k}>d
, we can now use (
2
) which gives

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{r}})}\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{W}}}^{1,q_{k}}(B_{r_{k}})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r_{k}})}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B_{R})}+\|g\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{R})}.

This completes the proof of (
3.2
).
∎

3.2.
Cluster derivatives

The next result is tailored for later use with the multi-particle Schrödinger equation.
We assume that in the equation (
3.1
)
d=3N
and
the variable
\mathbf{x}
is given by
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N})
.

Now we obtain better regularity properties of the weak solution of (
3.1
)
assuming some additional smoothness of the coefficients with respect to cluster
derivatives.
Precisely, consider a weak solution
u
of the equation (
3.1
) in the ball

B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\ell)
with some
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
,
R>0
,
\ell\in(0,1]
.
Suppose that for a cluster set
\boldsymbol{\sf P}=\{{\sf P}_{1},{\sf P}_{2},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}\}

the coefficients
\mathbf{a}
and
b

satisfy the bounds

(3.6)

\displaystyle|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x})|+|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}b(\mathbf{x})|\lesssim\ell^{-|\mathbf{m}|},\quad\mathbf{x}\in B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\ell),

for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
, with
constants potentially depending on
\mathbf{m}
,
R
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, but not on
\ell
.
In the next theorem we obtain bounds for the cluster derivatives
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}u

with explicit dependence on the parameter
\ell\in(0,1]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume the conditions (
3.6
), and let
u
be a weak solution of the equation
(
3.1
) in
B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\ell)

with the right-hand side
g=0
.
Then for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
and all
r<R

the cluster derivatives
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}u
belong to

\textup{{{C}}}^{1}\big(\overline{B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r\ell)}\big)
.
Furthermore, if
|\mathbf{m}|+k\geq 1
, where
k=0,1
, then for all
\nu\in(r,R)
we have

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{k}{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r\ell))}\lesssim\ell^{1-|\mathbf{m}|-k}\,\big(\ell\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},\nu\ell))}+\|\nabla u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},\nu\ell))}\big).

If
|\mathbf{m}|\geq 2
, then also

\displaystyle\|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}

\displaystyle u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r\ell))}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \ell^{2-|\mathbf{m}|}\,\big(\max_{\mathbf{l}:|\mathbf{l}|=2}\|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{l}}u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},\nu\ell))}+\|\nabla u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},\nu\ell))}+\|u\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},\nu\ell))}\big).

The implicit constants in (
3.7
) and (
3.2
) may depend
on the constants
r,\nu,R
, order
\mathbf{m}
of the derivative, cluster set
\boldsymbol{\sf P}
, and the
constants in (
3.6
). In particular,
if the constants in (
3.6
) are independent of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, then
so are the constants in (
3.7
) and (
3.2
).

In the present paper we need the bound (
3.7
) only.
Nevertheless, the bound (
3.2
) may be of independent interest.

The idea of the proof follows a similar argument in
[
8
]
. Namely, we rescale the
problem by introducing the function

\displaystyle w(\mathbf{y})=u(\mathbf{x}_{0}+\ell\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{y}\in B(\mathbb{0},R),

and the coefficients

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x}_{0}+\ell\mathbf{y}),\quad\beta(\mathbf{y})=b(\mathbf{x}_{0}+\ell\mathbf{y}).

After the scaling the equation (
3.1
) takes the form

(3.9)

\displaystyle-\Delta w+2\ell\ \boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\nabla w+\ell^{2}\beta w=0.

Note that

(3.10)

\displaystyle|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\mathbf{y})|+|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\beta(\mathbf{y})|\lesssim 1,\quad\mathbf{y}\in B(\mathbb{0},R),

for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
.

We are interested in the bounds for the function

w_{\mathbf{m}}={\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}w
for
\mathbf{y}\in B(\mathbb{0},R)
.
For the sake of brevity throughout the proof we use the notation

B_{r}=B(\mathbb{0},r)
for
r>0
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\ell\leq 1
. Suppose that
w
is a weak solution of the equation (
3.9
).
Then for all
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
and all
r<R

the cluster derivatives
w_{\mathbf{m}}
belong to

\textup{{{C}}}^{1}\big(\overline{B_{r}})\big)
.
Furthermore, if
|\mathbf{m}|\geq 1
, then for all
\nu\in(r,R)
we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|w_{\mathbf{m}}\|_{\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{r}})}\lesssim\ell^{2}\,\|w\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\nu})}+\|\nabla w\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\nu})}.

If
|\mathbf{m}|\geq 2
, then also

(3.12)

\displaystyle\|w_{\mathbf{m}}\|_{\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B_{r}})}\lesssim\max_{\mathbf{l}:|\mathbf{l}|=2}\|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{l}}w\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\nu})}+\ell\|\nabla w\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\nu})}+\ell^{2}\|w\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{\nu})}.

Proof.

We begin the proof with a formal manipulation assuming
that all the cluster derivatives
w_{\mathbf{m}}
exist and are as smooth as necessary.
Applying the operator
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}
to the equation (
3.9
)
and using (
3.10
) we obtain the following equation for the function

w_{\mathbf{m}}
:

(3.13)

\displaystyle-\Delta w_{\mathbf{m}}+2\ell\ \boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\nabla w_{\mathbf{m}}+\ell^{2}\beta\,w_{\mathbf{m}}=g_{\mathbf{m}},

with

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{m}}=-2\ell\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{0}\leq\mathbf{q}\leq\mathbf{m}\\

4.
The Schrödinger equation

4.1.
Reduction of the Schrödinger equation

Here we apply the bounds obtained in the previous section to the Schrödinger equation

(4.1)

\displaystyle-\Delta\psi+(V-E)\psi=0,

where the potential
V
is defined in (
1.1
).
Throughout this section we do not impose the condition
\psi\in\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
,
but consider local solutions of the equation (
4.1
).
In order to reduce
(
4.1
) to the equation of the form (
3.1
) we use the
representation
\psi=e^{F}\phi
with a
function
F
such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle F,\nabla F\in\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}).

In the mathematical physics literature the function
e^{F}
is often called
the
Jastrow factor
, see e.g.
[
8
]
,
[
10
]
.
After the substitution the equation (
4.1
) rewrites as

(4.3)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi-2\nabla F\cdot\nabla\phi+(V-\Delta F-|\nabla F|^{2}-E)\phi=0.

More precisely, if
\psi\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{\tiny{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
is a weak
solution of (
4.1
), then we also have
\phi\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{\tiny{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})

and
\phi
is a weak solution of (
4.3
).
We make a standard choice of the function
F
which ensures that
the factor

\phi
has better smoothness properties than
\psi
:

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigg(-\frac{Z}{2}|x_{j}|+

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{4}\sum_{j<k\leq N}|x_{j}-x_{k}|\bigg)

(4.4)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\ \sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigg(\frac{Z}{2}\sqrt{|x_{j}|^{2}+1}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j<k\leq N}\sqrt{|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{2}+1}\bigg).

In fact, the conventional choice of
F
is given by the first sum
on the right-hand side, which has a bounded gradient,
whereas the second term is added to ensure that
F
itself is bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
.
Thus both conditions (
4.2
) are satisfied.
Due to the straightforward identities

\displaystyle\Delta|x_{j}-x_{k}|=\frac{4}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|},\quad\Delta|x_{j}|=\frac{2}{|x_{j}|},

we have

\displaystyle\Delta\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigg(-\frac{Z}{2}|x_{j}|+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j<k\leq N}|x_{j}-x_{k}|\bigg)\bigg]=V(\mathbf{x}).

Therefore

(4.5)

\displaystyle\nabla^{k}(-\Delta F+V)\in\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}),\ \quad\textup{for all}\quad k=0,1,\dots.

The following fact was proved in

[
8
, Proposition A.2]
. We provide a somewhat different (shorter)
proof that uses only Proposition

3.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\psi\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,2}_{\tiny{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
be a weak solution of (
4.1
).
Then
\psi\in\textup{{{W}}}^{1,\infty}_{\tiny{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
and for all numbers

r>0
and
R>0
such that
r<R
we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r))}+\|\nabla\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r))}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R))},

where the implicit constant does not depend on
\psi
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
,
but depends on
r
and
R
.

Proof.

The function
\phi=e^{-F}\psi
is a weak solution of the equation (
4.3
)
in
B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R)
with arbitrary
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
and
R>0
. As established above, the coefficients
in this equation are uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
. According to
Proposition
3.1
,
\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
and for any pair of radii

r,R
,
r<R
, we have the bound

(4.7)

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})}+\|\nabla\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})}\lesssim\|\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B_{R})},

where
B_{R}=B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R)
, and the implicit constant is independent of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.
Since
F
and
\nabla F
satisfy (
4.2
), we can write for
\psi
that

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})}\lesssim\|\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})},\quad\|\phi

\displaystyle\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B_{R})}\lesssim\|\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{2}(B_{R})}\quad\textup{and}

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})}\lesssim\|\nabla\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})}

\displaystyle+\|\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B_{r})}.

Therefore (
4.7
) implies (
4.6
), as required.
∎

4.2.
Cluster derivatives of
\psi
: application of Theorem
3.2

For a cluster
{\sf P}\subset\{1,2,\dots,N\}
introduce the set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{{\sf P}}=\bigg\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}:\prod_{j\in{\sf P}}|x_{j}|\prod_{k\in{\sf P},l\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}}|x_{k}-x_{l}|=0\bigg\},

and the distance

(4.8)

\displaystyle\dc_{{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})=\min\{|x_{j}|,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|x_{j}-x_{k}|:j\in{\sf P},\ k\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}\},\quad\lambda_{{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})=\min\{1,\dc_{{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})\}.

The paper
[
8
]
contains bounds for cluster derivatives
{\sf D}_{\sf P}^{m}\psi

(see e.g.
[
8
, Proposition 1.10]
) outside the set
\Sigma_{\sf P}
, depending explicitly
on the distance
\dc_{\sf P}
.
We need a generalization of this result to cluster sets

\boldsymbol{\sf P}=\{{\sf P}_{1},{\sf P}_{2},\dots,{\sf P}_{M}\}
. Define

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}=\cup_{j=1}^{M}\Sigma_{{\sf P}_{j}}

and

(4.9)

\displaystyle\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})=\min_{j}\dc_{{\sf P}_{j}}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})=\min\{1,\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})\}.

The presence of the factor
1/\sqrt{2}
in the definition (
4.8
) is convenient since
it ensures that the function
\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
=1
:

Lemma 4.2
.

For all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
we have

(4.10)

\displaystyle|\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})-\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{y})|\leq|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|.

The same inequality holds for the function
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the inequality just for one cluster
{\sf P}
.
Let
j\in{\sf P}
,
k\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}
, and estimate:

\displaystyle|x_{j}|\leq

\displaystyle\ |y_{j}|+|x_{j}-y_{j}|\leq|y_{j}|+|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|,

\displaystyle|x_{j}-x_{k}|\leq

\displaystyle\ |y_{j}-y_{k}|+|y_{j}-x_{j}|+|y_{k}-x_{k}|\leq|y_{j}-y_{k}|+\sqrt{2}\,|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|.

For the last inequality we used the elementary fact that
if
a^{2}+b^{2}\leq c^{2}
for some positive
a,b
and
c
, then
a+b\leq\sqrt{2}\,c
.
Taking the minimum (
4.8
), we get

\dc_{\sf P}(\mathbf{x})\leq\dc_{{\sf P}}(\mathbf{y})+|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|
, which gives (
4.10
).
The function
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
trivially satisfies the same inequality.
∎

Our objective is to find
\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}
-bounds for the
cluster derivatives
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\psi

outside the set
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
. Let us fix

\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\setminus\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
, and denote

\displaystyle\lambda:=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x}_{0}).

Observe that for any
R\in(0,1)
the inclusion holds:

B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3N}\setminus\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
.
Indeed, by Lemma
4.2
,

\displaystyle|\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})-\lambda|\leq|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|<R\lambda,\quad\mathbf{x}\in B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda),

which implies that

(4.11)

\displaystyle 0<(1-R)\lambda\leq\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})\leq(1+R)\lambda,\quad\textup{for all}\quad\mathbf{x}\in B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda),

and hence proves the claim.
In the next theorem we estimate the derivatives
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\psi
in the ball
B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\setminus\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}

and let
\lambda=\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
.
Then for any
r,R
such that
0<r<R<1
, and for all

\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3M}
,
the cluster derivatives
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\psi
belong to
\textup{{{C}}}^{1}\big(\overline{B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda)}\big)
.
Moreover, if
|\mathbf{m}|+k\geq 1
with
k=0,1
, then

(4.12)

\displaystyle\|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\nabla^{k}\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r\lambda))}\lesssim\lambda^{1-|\mathbf{m}|-k}\big(\|\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda))}+\|\nabla\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda))}\big),

with an implicit constant depending on
r,R
, but independent of

\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\setminus\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
.

For a single cluster
{\sf P}

the bound (
4.12
), even for arbitrary

\textup{{{L}}}^{p}
-norms with
p\in(1,\infty]

was found in
[
8
, Proposition 1.10]
.
The proof in
[
8
]
does not immediately generalize
to arbitrary cluster sets.
As in
[
8
]
we use the reduction to the equation (
4.3
).
In
[
8
]
the choice of the Jastrow factor depended on the cluster
{\sf P}
,
whereas we use the standard function (
4.1
) independent of the cluster set
\boldsymbol{\sf P}
.
To check that the coefficients in the equation (
4.3
) satisfy
the conditions of Theorem
3.2
, we start with the following elementary observation.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
g\in\textup{{{C}}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\})
be such that

\displaystyle|\partial^{m}g(x)|\lesssim|x|^{-|m|},\quad\textup{for all}\quad m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}.

Then for any cluster set
\boldsymbol{\sf P}
and any
j,k=1,2,\dots,N
, we have

\displaystyle|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}g(x_{j})|+|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}g(x_{j}-x_{k})|\lesssim\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})^{-|\mathbf{m}|},

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\setminus\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
.

Proof.

For simplicity we prove the lemma for one cluster, which is denoted
{\sf P}
.
Assume that
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
|m|\geq 1
.
It is clear that
D_{\sf P}^{m}g(x_{j}-x_{k})=0
if
j,k\in{\sf P}
or
j,k\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}
.
If
j\in{\sf P},k\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}
or
k\in{\sf P},j\in{\sf P}^{\rm c}
, then

\displaystyle|{\sf D}_{\sf P}^{m}g(x_{j}-x_{k})|=|\partial_{x}^{m}g(x)|_{x=x_{j}-x_{k}}\lesssim|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{-|m|}\lesssim\dc_{\sf P}(\mathbf{x})^{-|m|},

as claimed. In the same way we get the required estimate for
{\sf D}_{{\sf P}}^{m}g(x_{j})
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
F
be the function (
4.1
). Then for any cluster set
\boldsymbol{\sf P}
we have
for all
\mathbf{x}\notin\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
and all
\mathbf{m}
that

(4.13)

\displaystyle\big|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})\big|+\big|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}|\nabla F(\mathbf{x})|^{2}\big|\lesssim\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})^{-|\mathbf{m}|}.

If
|\mathbf{m}|\geq 1
, then

(4.14)

\displaystyle\big|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}e^{F(\mathbf{x})}\big|\lesssim\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})^{1-|\mathbf{m}|}.

Proof.

Since
F

is a sum of terms of the type
|x_{j}-x_{k}|
and
|x_{j}|
, the required bound for
\nabla F

immediately follows from the definition (
4.1
) and Lemma

4.4
. For
|\nabla F|^{2}
we use the Leibniz rule, which leads again to (
4.13
).

To prove the bound (
4.14
) observe that the derivative
{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}e^{F}
represents as a sum of finitely many terms of the form

\displaystyle({\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{k}_{1}}F\big)^{n_{1}}\,({\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{k}_{2}}F\big)^{n_{2}}\cdots({\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{k}_{s}}F\big)^{n_{s}}\,e^{F},

,

, and
|\mathbf{k}_{1}|\,n_{1}+|\mathbf{k}_{2}|\,n_{2}+\cdots+|\mathbf{k}_{s}|\,n_{s}=|\mathbf{m}|
.
Each such term estimates by

\displaystyle\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})^{n_{1}(1-|\mathbf{k}_{1}|)+n_{2}(1-|\mathbf{k}_{2}|)+\cdots n_{s}(1-|\mathbf{k}_{1}|)}=\dc_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})^{n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{s}-|\mathbf{m}|}\leq\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}(\mathbf{x})^{1-|\mathbf{m}|},

as required.

Proof of Theorem

In view of (

3.1
) where

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}=-2\nabla F,\quad b=V-\Delta F-|\nabla F|^{2}-E.

Let us fix a number

) and (

and

where

.
For the last inequality we have used (

Thus the condition (

with

{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\phi\in\textup{{{C}}}^{1}(\overline{B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r\lambda)})
for all

Now we need to replace
\phi
with the function
\psi=e^{F}\phi
. Let us prove (
4.12
)
with
k=0
,
|\mathbf{m}|\geq 1
. By the Leibniz rule,

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\big(e^{F}\phi\big)=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{0\leq\mathbf{q}\leq\mathbf{m}}{\mathbf{m}\choose\mathbf{q}}{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{q}}\big(e^{F}\big){\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{q}}\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq\mathbf{q}\leq\mathbf{m}\\

Using (

Consequently,

\displaystyle\|{\sf D}_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}^{\mathbf{m}}\psi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},r\lambda))}\lesssim\lambda^{1-|\mathbf{m}|}\,\big(\|\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda))}+\|\nabla\phi\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{\infty}(B(\mathbf{x}_{0},R\lambda))}\big).

\displaystyle\ +\int\limits_{\widehat{X}_{{\sf S}^{*}}(x;\varepsilon(4N)^{-1})\cap\widehat{T}_{{\sf S}^{*}}(\varepsilon/2)}\lambda_{{\sf S}^{*}}(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{-a}f_{\infty}(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}};R)f_{\infty}(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};R)d\hat{\mathbf{x}}.

By (
5.2
) with
t=x
, each of these integrals
is bounded by

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{-a}+h_{a+2}(\varepsilon)\big)\,\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which implies (
5.3
).
The integral
{\mathcal{J}}_{a}^{(2)}(x,y;\varepsilon,R)
is estimated in the same way.

The integral
\mathcal{J}_{a}^{(3)}(x,y;\varepsilon,R)
is estimated with the help of
(
5.4
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{a}^{(3)}(x,y;\varepsilon,R)\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \varepsilon^{3-a}\,\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ h_{a+2}(\varepsilon)\,\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining the obtained bounds we complete the proof of (

).
∎

Now we are in a position to prove Theorem

.

6.
Estimates for the density matrix: proof of Theorem
1.1

To cover both inequalities (

) and (
1.1
) in one proof we
study the following function:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\gamma_{r,p}(x,y):=\partial_{x}^{r}\partial_{y}^{p}\gamma(x,y)=\int\partial_{x}^{r}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\overline{\partial_{y}^{p}\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}},

where

,

.
By Corollary
4.6
the integrand in (

, see (

) for the definition of

. Therefore, the estimate (
5.2

\displaystyle|\gamma_{r,p}(x,y)|\lesssim\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,2R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

In order to estimate the derivatives of
\gamma_{r,p}(x,y)

we begin by studying “local” quantities. For a fixed
\varepsilon>0

let
\Phi=\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)

be an extended cut-off as defined in (

).
Define

\displaystyle\gamma_{r,p}(x,y;\Phi,\varepsilon)=\int\Phi(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)\partial_{x}^{r}\psi(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\overline{\partial_{y}^{p}\psi(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}})}\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}.

Remark 6.1
.

The motivation to study the function (
6.1
) instead of the original one-particle density matrix
(
1.3
) can be explained by the following intuitive argument.
As we saw earlier (see Corollary
4.6
) higher order cluster
derivatives of
\psi
become ever more singular
near the coalescence set
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
(i.e. for small
\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
)
as the order of derivative grows.
This however does not apply to
first order derivatives – together with the function
\psi
itself,
they remain bounded near
\Sigma_{\boldsymbol{\sf P}}
.
Thus, taking derivatives of first order under the integral (
1.3
) does not make
the integral “worse”. At the same time,
by virtue of the formula

(6.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{y}^{l}\gamma(x,y)=\partial_{x}^{m-r}\partial_{y}^{l-p}\gamma_{r,p}(x,y),

this reduces the order of the remaining
derivatives. In particular, when proving (
1.1
) further in this section,
where
\gamma
is differentiated with respect to
both variables
x
and
y
, we use (
6.4
) with
|p|=|r|=1
, thereby reducing the order of the derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
by one each.
On the other hand, in the proof of (
1.1
) where the differentiation is conducted with respect to one
of the variables
x
or
y
only, we cannot take full
advantage of this
order reduction. Rather, we derive (
1.1
)
from (
1.1
) using the fundamental theorem of calculus which introduces the “missing” derivative
artificially.

Let us estimate the derivatives of
\gamma_{r,p}
for arbitrary
|r|\leq 1
,
|p|\leq 1
.

6.2

6.3

Thus we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2}),\ \mathbf{n}=

Corollary 6.3
.

Suppose that for some
\varepsilon>0
we have
|x|>\varepsilon
,

|y|>\varepsilon
and
|x-y|>\varepsilon
.
Then for all
\alpha,\beta
and
|p|\leq 1,|r|\leq 1,

and all
R>0
the bound holds:

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{y}^{\beta}\gamma_{r,p}(x,

\displaystyle y)|

(6.9)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\big(1+|x|^{-|\alpha|-|\beta|}+|y|^{-|\alpha|-|\beta|}+h_{|\alpha|+|\beta|+2}(\varepsilon)\big)\,\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(x,R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\rho\|_{\textup{{{L}}}^{1}(B(y,R))}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

with an implicit constant that is independent of
x,y
and
\varepsilon
but may depend on
R
.

Proof.

In order to use Lemma
6.2

we build a partition of unity consisting of extended cut-offs.
Recall the notation
{\sf{R}}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
.
Let
\Xi=\{(j,k)\in{\sf{R}}\times{\sf{R}}:j<k\}
. For each subset
\Upsilon\subset\Xi

introduce the admissible cut-off (see (
2.3
) for the definition of admissible cut-offs)

\displaystyle\phi_{\Upsilon}(\mathbf{x};\varepsilon)=\prod_{(j,k)\in\Upsilon}\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})\prod_{(j,k)\in\Upsilon^{\rm c}}\theta_{\varepsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k}).

It is clear that

\displaystyle\sum_{\Upsilon\subset{\Xi}}\phi_{\Upsilon}(\mathbf{x};\varepsilon)=\prod_{(j,k)\in\Xi}\big(\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})+\theta_{\varepsilon}(x_{j}-x_{k})\big)=1.

Furthermore, for every cluster
{\sf S}\subset{\sf{R}}^{*}
define

\displaystyle\tau_{{\sf S}}(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)=\prod_{j\in{\sf S}}\zeta_{\varepsilon}(y-x_{j})\prod_{j\in({\sf S}^{\rm c})^{*}}\theta_{\varepsilon}(y-x_{j}).

It is clear that

\displaystyle\sum_{{\sf S}\subset{\sf{R}}^{*}}\tau_{{\sf S}}(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)=\prod_{j\in{\sf{R}}^{*}}\big(\zeta_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}-x_{j})+\theta_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}-x_{j})\big)=1.

Define

\displaystyle\Phi_{\Upsilon,{\sf S}}(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)=\phi_{\Upsilon}(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)\tau_{{\sf S}}(y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon),\quad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N-3},

so that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\Upsilon\subset\Xi,\ {\sf S}\subset{\sf{R}}^{*}}\Phi_{\Upsilon,{\sf S}}(x,y,\hat{\mathbf{x}};\varepsilon)=1.

Each function
\Phi_{\Upsilon,{\sf S}}
is an extended cut-off function, as
defined in Subsect.
2.2
.
Using the definition (
6.3
),
the function (
6.1
) can be represented as

\displaystyle\gamma_{r,p}(x,y)=\sum_{\Upsilon\subset\Xi,\ {\sf S}\subset{\sf{R}}^{*}}\gamma(x,y;\Phi_{\Upsilon,{\sf S}},\varepsilon).

Applying Lemma
6.2
to each summand we arrive at (

).
∎

Proof of the bound (

).

,

\displaystyle|y-y_{s}|=s\delta_{1},\,|z-y|=\delta_{1},\ |z-x|=\delta_{1}+d,\quad|z|\geq|y|-\delta_{1}\geq\frac{3}{4}|y|,

\displaystyle|(z-y)\cdot\nabla_{y}(

\displaystyle\ |z-y|\big(1+|y_{s}|^{1-|l|}+|x|^{1-|l|}+h_{|l|+1}(|x-y_{s}|)\big)A(x,y_{s};R/2,R/2)

\displaystyle\ \delta_{1}\big(1+|y|^{1-|l|}+|x|^{1-|l|}+h_{|l|+1}(d+s\delta_{1})\big)A(x,y;R/2,R).

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{l}\gamma(x,z)|\lesssim\big(1+|x|^{1-|l|}+|z|^{1-|l|}+h_{|l|+1}(|x-z|)\big)\,A\big(x,z;R/2,R/2\big).

Appendix

Here we provide an elementary fact concerning extensions of Sobolev spaces. The proof can be found
in the appendix to
[
18
]
.

Consider spaces of functions that depend either
on one variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
or on two variables
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{l}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Denote
K=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{l}:|t|<1\}
,
B=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|<1\}
and

B_{0}=B\setminus\{0\}
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let the dimension
l
be arbitrary, let

d\geq 2
,
m\geq 1
, and

p\in[d(d-1)^{-1},\infty]
. Then

\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}(B_{0})=\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}(B)
and
\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}\big(K\times B_{0}\big)=\textup{{{W}}}^{m,p}\big(K\times B\big)
.

We use this fact in Remark
1.2
to describe smoothness properties of
the one-particle density matrix
\gamma(x,y)
.
