Classical continued fractions for some multivariate polynomials generalizing the Genocchi and median Genocchi numbers
Bishal Deb and Alan D. Sokal, [5mm] Department of Mathematics, University College London, London WC1E 6BT, UK, [1mm] Department of Physics, New York University, New York, NY 10003, USA[1mm][5mm] bishal.deb.19@ucl.ac.uk , sokal@nyu.edu [1cm]

Abstract
A D-permutation is a permutation of [ 2 ​ n ] [2n] satisfying 2 ​ k − 1 ≤ σ ⁡ ( 2 ​ k − 1 ) 2k-1\leq\sigma(2k-1) and 2 ​ k ≥ σ ⁡ ( 2 ​ k ) 2k\geq\sigma(2k) for all k k ; they provide a combinatorial model for the Genocchi and median Genocchi numbers. We find Stieltjes-type and Thron-type continued fractions for some multivariate polynomials that enumerate D-permutations with respect to a very large (sometimes infinite) number of simultaneous statistics that measure cycle status, record status, crossings and nestings.

2.1
Classical continued fractions: S-fractions, J-fractions
and T-fractions

If
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of combinatorial numbers or polynomials
with
a_{0}=1
, it is often fruitful to seek to express its
ordinary generating function as a continued fraction.
The most commonly studied types of continued fractions
are Stieltjes-type (S-fractions),

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;,

(2.1)

and Jacobi-type (J-fractions),

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}t-\cfrac{\beta_{1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;.

(2.2)

A less commonly studied type of continued fraction is
the Thron-type (T-fraction):

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\delta_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;.

(2.3)

(Both sides of all these expressions are to be interpreted as
formal power series in the indeterminate
t
.)
This line of investigation goes back at least to
Euler
[
17
,
18
]
,
but it gained impetus following Flajolet’s
[
20
]

seminal discovery that any S-fraction (resp. J-fraction)
can be interpreted combinatorially as a generating function
for Dyck (resp. Motzkin) paths with suitable weights for each rise and fall
(resp. each rise, fall and level step).
More recently, several authors

[
24
,
37
,
29
,
45
,
13
]

have found a similar combinatorial interpretation
of the general T-fraction:
namely, as a generating function for Schröder paths with suitable
weights for each rise, fall and long level step.
These interpretations will be reviewed in
Section
5.1
below.

2.2
Contraction formulae

The formulae for even and odd contraction of an S-fraction
to an equivalent J-fraction are well known:
see e.g.
[
11
, Lemmas 1 and 2]

[
7
, Lemma 1]

for very simple algebraic proofs,
and see
[
50
, pp. V-31–V-32]

for enlightening combinatorial proofs
based on grouping pairs of steps in a Dyck path.
Here we will need an extension of these formulae
to suitable subclasses of T-fractions
[
45
]
:

Proposition 2.1

(Even contraction for T-fractions with

\delta_{2}=\delta_{4}=\delta_{6}=\ldots=0
)
.

We have

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\cfrac{1}{1-\delta_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\delta_{3}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{3}t}{1-\cdots}}}}\;\;=\;

\displaystyle\hskip-19.91692pt\cfrac{1}{1-(\alpha_{1}+\delta_{1})t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}t^{2}}{1-(\alpha_{2}+\alpha_{3}+\delta_{3})t-\cfrac{\alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}t^{2}}{1-(\alpha_{4}+\alpha_{5}+\delta_{5})t-\cfrac{\alpha_{5}\alpha_{6}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}}\;\;.\qquad

(2.4)

That is, the T-fraction on the left-hand side of
(
2.4
)
equals the J-fraction with coefficients

\gamma_{0}&=\alpha_{1}+\delta_{1}\\
\gamma_{n}=\alpha_{2n}+\alpha_{2n+1}+\delta_{2n+1}\quad\hbox{for $n\geq 1$}\\
\beta_{n}=\alpha_{2n-1}\alpha_{2n}

(2.5)

Here (
2.4
)/(
2.5
)
holds as an identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\delta}}_{\rm odd}][[t]]
,
where
{\bm{\delta}}_{\rm odd}=(\delta_{1},0,\delta_{3},0,\ldots)
.

Proposition 2.2

(Odd contraction for T-fractions with
\delta_{1}=\delta_{3}=\delta_{5}=\ldots=0
)
.

We have

\displaystyle\hskip-42.67912pt\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{3}t}{1-\delta_{4}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{4}t}{1-\cdots}}}}}\;\;=\;

\displaystyle 1\>+\>\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\delta_{2})t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}t^{2}}{1-(\alpha_{3}+\alpha_{4}+\delta_{4})t-\cfrac{\alpha_{4}\alpha_{5}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}\;\;.\qquad

(2.6)

That is, the T-fraction on the left-hand side of
(
2.6
)
equals
1
plus
\alpha_{1}t
times the J-fraction with coefficients

\gamma_{n}&=\alpha_{2n+1}+\alpha_{2n+2}+\delta_{2n+2}\\
\beta_{n}=\alpha_{2n}\alpha_{2n+1}

(2.7)

Here (
2.6
)/(
2.7
)
holds as an identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[{\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\delta}}_{\rm even}][[t]]
,
where
{\bm{\delta}}_{\rm even}=(0,\delta_{2},0,\delta_{4},\ldots)
.

Both the algebraic and the combinatorial proofs of the contraction formulae
for S-fractions can be easily generalized
[

]

to prove Propositions
2.1

and
2.2
.

One consequence of Proposition
2.1

is that if two T-fractions with only odd deltas —
say, one with coefficients
({\bm{\alpha}},{\bm{\delta}}_{\rm odd})

and the other with coefficients
({\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime},{\bm{\delta}}^{\prime}_{\rm odd})
—
give rise by contraction to the same J-fraction
({\bm{\beta}},{\bm{\gamma}})
,
then they must be equal.
In some cases this principle can be used to transform
a T-fraction into an S-fraction (that is,
{\bm{\delta}}^{\prime}_{\rm odd}={\bm{0}}
):
see, for instance, Corollary
4.3
below.

By combining Propositions
2.1

and
2.2
, we obtain:

Corollary 2.3

(Combining odd and even contraction)
.

If

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{3}t}{1-\delta_{4}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{4}t}{1-\cdots}}}}}\;,

(2.8)

then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n+1}t^{n}\;=\;\cfrac{a_{1}}{1-\delta^{\prime}_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha^{\prime}_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha^{\prime}_{2}t}{1-\delta^{\prime}_{3}t-\cfrac{\alpha^{\prime}_{3}t}{1-\cdots}}}}

(2.9)

whenever

\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\delta_{2}&=\alpha^{\prime}_{1}+\delta^{\prime}_{1}\\
\alpha_{2n+1}+\alpha_{2n+2}+\delta_{2n+2}=\alpha^{\prime}_{2n}+\alpha^{\prime}_{2n+1}+\delta^{\prime}_{2n+1}\quad\hbox{for $n\geq 1$}\\
\alpha_{2n}\alpha_{2n+1}=\alpha^{\prime}_{2n-1}\alpha^{\prime}_{2n}

(2.10)

Transformation formula

We now prove a useful transformation formula for T-fractions.
First, a lemma:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and let
f(t),g(t)\in R[[t]]
.
Then

1\>+\>{tf(t)\over 1-tf(t)-tg(t)}\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{tf(t)}{1-tg(t)}}

as an identity in
R[[t]]
.

Proof.
Trivial: both sides equal
\dfrac{1-tg(t)}{1-tf(t)-tg(t)}
.

\square

Proposition 2.5

(Augmentation/restriction of T-fraction)
.

1\>+\>\cfrac{\delta_{1}t}{1-\delta_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\ldots}}}\;\;=\;\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\delta_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\ldots}}}}\;\>.\quad

(2.12)

Proof.
Use the lemma with
f(t)=\delta_{1}
and

g(t)=\displaystyle\cfrac{\alpha_{1}}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\ldots}}\;\,
.

\square

Reading the identity (
2.12
) from left to right,
it says that if the ogf of a sequence
{\bm{a}}=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)

with
a_{0}=1

is given by a T-fraction with coefficients
{\bm{\alpha}}
and
{\bm{\delta}}
,
then the ogf of the “augmented” sequence

{\bm{a}}^{\prime}=(1,\delta_{1}a_{0},\delta_{1}a_{1},\delta_{1}a_{2},\ldots)

is given by a T-fraction with coefficients
{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}
and
{\bm{\delta}}^{\prime}
, where

\alpha^{\prime}_{1}&=\delta_{1}\\
\alpha^{\prime}_{n}=\alpha_{n-1}\quad\hbox{for $n\geq 2$}\\
\delta^{\prime}_{1}=\delta^{\prime}_{2}=0\\
\delta^{\prime}_{n}=\delta_{n-1}\quad\hbox{for $n\geq 3$}

(2.13)

In particular, if
\delta_{2}=\delta_{3}=\ldots=0
,
then the T-fraction on the right-hand side is an S-fraction.
Of course, this transformation gives something interesting

Alternatively, reading the identity (

)
from right to left,
it says that if the ogf of a sequence

{\bm{\alpha}}^{\prime}

{\bm{\delta}}^{\prime}

\delta^{\prime}_{1}=\delta^{\prime}_{2}=0

\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\neq 0

{\bm{a}}=(a_{1}/\alpha^{\prime}_{1},a_{2}/\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\ldots)

{\bm{\alpha}}
and
{\bm{\delta}}
, where

\alpha_{n}&=\alpha^{\prime}_{n+1}\\
\delta_{1}=\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\\
\delta_{n}=\delta^{\prime}_{n+1}\quad\hbox{for $n\geq 2$}

(2.14)

2.7
Permutation statistics: The record-and-cycle classification

We now define some permutation statistics that will play a central role
in what follows.

Given a permutation
\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{N}
, an index
i\in[N]
is called an

•

excedance
(exc) if
i<\sigma(i)
;

•

anti-excedance
(aexc) if
i>\sigma(i)
;

•

fixed point
(fix) if
i=\sigma(i)
.

Clearly every index
i
belongs to exactly one of these three types;
we call this the
excedance classification
.
We also say that
i
is a
weak excedance
if
i\leq\sigma(i)
,
and a
weak anti-excedance
if
i\geq\sigma(i)
.

A more refined classification is as follows:
an index
i\in[N]
is called a

•

cycle peak
(cpeak) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)<i>\sigma(i)
;

•

cycle valley
(cval) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)>i<\sigma(i)
;

•

cycle double rise
(cdrise) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)<i<\sigma(i)
;

•

cycle double fall
(cdfall) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)>i>\sigma(i)
;

•

fixed point
(fix) if
\sigma^{-1}(i)=i=\sigma(i)
.

Clearly every index
i
belongs to exactly one of these five types;
we refer to this classification as the
cycle classification
.
Obviously, excedance = cycle valley or cycle double rise,
and anti-excedance = cycle peak or cycle double fall.

On the other hand, an index
i\in[N]
is called a

•

record
(rec) (or
left-to-right maximum
)
if
\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)
for all
j<i

[note in particular that the indices 1 and
\sigma^{-1}(N)

are always records];

•

antirecord
(arec) (or
right-to-left minimum
)
if
\sigma(j)>\sigma(i)
for all
j>i

[note in particular that the indices
N
and
\sigma^{-1}(1)

are always antirecords];

•

exclusive record
(erec) if it is a record and not also
an antirecord;

•

exclusive antirecord
(earec) if it is an antirecord
and not also a record;

•

record-antirecord
(rar) (or
pivot
)
if it is both a record and an antirecord;

•

neither-record-antirecord
(nrar) if it is neither a record
nor an antirecord.

Every index
i
thus belongs to exactly one of the latter four types;
we refer to this classification as the
record classification
.

The record and cycle classifications of indices are related as follows:

(a)

Every record is a weak excedance,
and every exclusive record is an excedance.

(b)

Every antirecord is a weak anti-excedance,
and every exclusive antirecord is an anti-excedance.

(c)

Every record-antirecord is a fixed point.

Therefore, by applying the record and cycle classifications simultaneously,
we obtain 10 disjoint categories
[
46
]
:

•

ereccval: exclusive records that are also cycle valleys;

•

ereccdrise: exclusive records that are also cycle double rises;

•

eareccpeak: exclusive antirecords that are also cycle peaks;

•

eareccdfall: exclusive antirecords that are also cycle double falls;

•

rar: record-antirecords (these are always fixed points);

•

nrcpeak: neither-record-antirecords that are also cycle peaks;

•

nrcval: neither-record-antirecords that are also cycle valleys;

•

nrcdrise: neither-record-antirecords that are also cycle double rises;

•

nrcdfall: neither-record-antirecords that are also cycle double falls;

•

nrfix: neither-record-antirecords that are also fixed points.

Clearly every index
i
belongs to exactly one of these 10 types;
we call this the
record-and-cycle classification
.

Now suppose that
\sigma
is a D-permutation.
Then the cycle classification of a non-fixed-point index
i

is equivalent to recording the parities of
\sigma^{-1}(i)
and
i
:

\displaystyle\bullet\;\hbox{{cycle peak}\/: $\sigma^{-1}(i)<i>\sigma(i)$
$\implies$ $\sigma^{-1}(i)$ odd, $i$ even}

(2.47)

\displaystyle\bullet\;\hbox{{cycle valley}\/: $\sigma^{-1}(i)>i<\sigma(i)$
$\implies$ $\sigma^{-1}(i)$ even, $i$ odd}

\displaystyle\bullet\;\hbox{{cycle double rise}\/: $\sigma^{-1}(i)<i<\sigma(i)$
$\implies$ $\sigma^{-1}(i)$ odd, $i$ odd}

\displaystyle\bullet\;\hbox{{cycle double fall}\/: $\sigma^{-1}(i)>i>\sigma(i)$
$\implies$ $\sigma^{-1}(i)$ even, $i$ even}\hskip 48.36958pt

For fixed points, by contrast,
we will need to explicitly record the parity of
i
,
by distinguishing even and odd fixed points:

\displaystyle\bullet\;\hbox{{even fixed point}\/ (evenfix): $\sigma^{-1}(i)=i=\sigma(i)$ is even}

(2.48)

\displaystyle\bullet\;\hbox{{odd fixed point}\/ (oddfix): $\sigma^{-1}(i)=i=\sigma(i)$ is odd}\hskip 105.2751pt

We therefore refine the record-and-cycle classification
by distinguishing even and odd fixed points:

•

evenrar: even record-antirecords (these are always fixed points);

•

oddrar: odd record-antirecords (these are always fixed points);

•

evennrfix: even neither-record-antirecords that are also fixed points;

•

oddnrfix: odd neither-record-antirecords that are also fixed points.

This leads to the
parity-refined record-and-cycle classification
,
in which each index
i
belongs to exactly one of 12 types.
More precisely, each even index
i
belongs to exactly one of the 6 types

eareccpeak, nrcpeak, eareccdfall, nrcdfall, evenrar, evennrfix,

while each odd index
i
belongs to exactly one of the 6 types

ereccval, nrcval, ereccdrise, nrcdrise, oddrar, oddnrfix.

{\rm psnest}(j,\sigma)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\#\{i<j\colon\>\sigma(i)>j\}\;=\;\#\{i>j\colon\>\sigma(i)<j\}\;.

(2.51)

(Here the two expressions are equal because
\sigma
is a bijection
from

to itself.)
In

this quantity was called the

of the fixed point

and was denoted

.

For some purposes we will also require
a variant of (
2.50
)
in which the roles of second and third position are interchanged:

{\rm ucross}^{\prime}(k,\sigma)&=\#\{i<j<k<l\colon\>k=\sigma(i)\hbox{ and }l=\sigma(j)\}\\
{\rm unest}^{\prime}(k,\sigma)=\#\{i<j<k<l\colon\>k=\sigma(j)\hbox{ and }l=\sigma(i)\}\\
{\rm lcross}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)=\#\{i<j<k<l\colon\>i=\sigma(k)\hbox{ and }j=\sigma(l)\}\\
{\rm lnest}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)=\#\{i<j<k<l\colon\>i=\sigma(l)\hbox{ and }j=\sigma(k)\}

(2.52)

We remark that since nestings join the vertices
in second and third positions, we have

{\rm unest}^{\prime}(k,\sigma)&={\rm unest}(\sigma^{-1}(k),\sigma)\\
{\rm lnest}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)={\rm lnest}(\sigma^{-1}(j),\sigma)

(2.53)

Note that

and
{\rm unest}^{\prime}(k,\sigma)
can be nonzero

is an excedance
(that is, when

while
{\rm lcross}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)
and
{\rm lnest}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)
can be nonzero
only when
\sigma^{-1}(j)
is an anti-excedance
(that is,
j
is a cycle valley or a cycle double fall).
In a D-permutation, this means that

{\rm ucross}^{\prime}(k,\sigma)
and
{\rm unest}^{\prime}(k,\sigma)
can be nonzero
only when
\sigma^{-1}(k)
is odd and not a fixed point,
while
{\rm lcross}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)
and
{\rm lnest}^{\prime}(j,\sigma)
can be nonzero
only when
\sigma^{-1}(j)
is even and not a fixed point.
We call (

) the

variant index-refined crossing and nesting statistics
.

3
First T-fraction and its generalizations

In this section we state our first T-fraction for D-permutations,
in three increasingly more general versions.
The first and most basic version (Theorem
3.3
)
is a T-fraction in 12 variables that enumerates D-permutations
with respect to the parity-refined record-and-cycle classification;
it includes many previously known results as special cases.
The second version (Theorem

)
is a
(p,q)
-generalization of the first one:
it is a T-fraction in 22 variables that enumerates D-permutations
with respect to the parity-refined record-and-cycle classification
together with four pairs of
(p,q)
-variables
counting the refined categories of crossing and nesting
(Figure
3

as well as two variables corresponding to pseudo-nestings of fixed points.
Finally, our third version
— what we call the “first master T-fraction”
(Theorem
3.11
) —
is a T-fraction in six infinite families of indeterminates
that generalizes the preceding two
by employing the index-refined crossing and nesting statistics
defined in (
2.50

The plan of this section is as follows:
We begin (Section
3.1
) by establishing
some easy but important preliminary results concerning
record-antirecord fixed points.
Then, in Sections
3.2
–
3.4

we state the three versions of our first T-fraction

Finally, in Section
3.5

we state variant forms of the three T-fractions.
All these results will be proven in Section

An important role in our study of D-permutations

It is easy to see that, in any permutation,

More precisely:

Lemma 3.1

Consider a permutation

If

\{\sigma(i)+1,\ldots,N\}

so that
\sigma(i)\leq i

So if

is a record-antirecord,
then
i
must be a fixed point
and
\sigma
must map both
\{1,\ldots,i-1\}
and
\{i+1,\ldots,N\}

bijectively onto themselves.

The converse is obvious.

\square

Second T-fraction and its generalizations

It is natural to want to refine the foregoing polynomials

Unfortunately,

5
Preliminaries for the proofs

Our proofs are based on Flajolet’s
[
20
]

combinatorial interpretation of continued fractions
in terms of Dyck and Motzkin paths
and its generalization

[
24
,
37
,
29
,
45
,
13
]

to Schröder paths,
together with some bijections mapping combinatorial objects
(in particular, D-permutations)
to labeled Dyck, Motzkin or Schröder paths.
We begin by reviewing briefly these two ingredients.

5.1
Combinatorial interpretation of continued fractions

Recall that a
Motzkin path
of length
n\geq 0

is a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n})

in the right quadrant
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
,
starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{n}=(n,0)
,
whose steps
s_{j}=\omega_{j}-\omega_{j-1}

are
(1,1)
[“rise” or “up step”],
(1,-1)
[“fall” or “down step”]
or
(1,0)
[“level step”].
We write
h_{j}
for the
height
of the Motzkin path at abscissa
j
,
i.e.
\omega_{j}=(j,h_{j})
;
note in particular that
h_{0}=h_{n}=0
.
We write
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
for the set of Motzkin paths of length
n
,
and
{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{M}}_{n}
.
A Motzkin path is called a
Dyck path
if it has no level steps.
A Dyck path always has even length;
we write
{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}
for the set of Dyck paths of length
2n
,
and
{\mathcal{D}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}
.

Let
{\bf a}=(a_{i})_{i\geq 0}
,
{\bf b}=(b_{i})_{i\geq 1}

and
{\bf c}=(c_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be indeterminates;
we will work in the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}]]

of formal power series in these indeterminates.
To each Motzkin path
\omega
we assign a weight

W(\omega)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}]

that is the product of the weights for the individual steps,
where a rise starting at height
i
gets weight
a_{i}
,
a fall starting at height
i
gets weight
b_{i}
,
and a level step at height
i
gets weight
c_{i}
.
Flajolet
[
20
]
showed that
the generating function of Motzkin paths
can be expressed as a continued fraction:

Theorem 5.1

(Flajolet’s master theorem)
.

We have

\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{M}}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-c_{0}-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}}{1-c_{1}-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}}{1-c_{2}-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.1)

as an identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}]]
.

In particular, if
a_{i-1}b_{i}=\beta_{i}t^{2}
and
c_{i}=\gamma_{i}t

(note that the parameter
t
is conjugate to the length of the Motzkin path),
we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\gamma_{0}t-\cfrac{\beta_{1}t^{2}}{1-\gamma_{1}t-\cfrac{\beta_{2}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}\;\,,

(5.2)

so that the generating function of Motzkin paths with height-dependent weights
is given by the J-type continued fraction (
2.2
).
Similarly, if
a_{i-1}b_{i}=\alpha_{i}t
and
c_{i}=0

(note that
t
is now conjugate to the semi-length of the Dyck path), we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\,,

(5.3)

so that the generating function of Dyck paths with height-dependent weights
is given by the S-type continued fraction (
2.1
).

Let us now show how to handle Schröder paths within this framework.
A
Schröder path
of length
2n
(
n\geq 0
)
is a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{2n})

in the right quadrant
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}
,
starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{2n}=(2n,0)
,
whose steps are
(1,1)
[“rise” or “up step”],

(1,-1)
[“fall” or “down step”]
or
(2,0)
[“long level step”].
We write
s_{j}
for the step starting at abscissa
j-1
.
If the step
s_{j}
is a rise or a fall,
we set
s_{j}=\omega_{j}-\omega_{j-1}
as before.
If the step
s_{j}
is a long level step,
we set
s_{j}=\omega_{j+1}-\omega_{j-1}
and leave
\omega_{j}
undefined;
furthermore, in this case there is no step
s_{j+1}
.
We write
h_{j}
for the height of the Schröder path at abscissa
j

whenever this is defined, i.e.
\omega_{j}=(j,h_{j})
.
Please note that
\omega_{2n}=(2n,0)
and
h_{2n}=0

are always well-defined,
because there cannot be a long level step starting at abscissa
2n-1
.
Note also that a long level step at even (resp. odd) height
can occur only at an odd-numbered (resp. even-numbered) step.
We write
{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}
for the set of Schröder paths of length
2n
,
and
{\mathcal{S}}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}
.

There is an obvious bijection between Schröder paths and Motzkin paths:
namely, every long level step is mapped onto a level step.
If we apply Flajolet’s master theorem with

a_{i-1}b_{i}=\alpha_{i}t
and
c_{i}=\delta_{i+1}t

to the resulting Motzkin path
(note that
t
is now conjugate to the semi-length
of the underlying Schröder path),
we obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\sum_{\omega\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}}W(\omega)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\delta_{1}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{1}t}{1-\delta_{2}t-\cfrac{\alpha_{2}t}{1-\cdots}}}\;\,,

(5.4)

so that the generating function of Schröder paths
with height-dependent weights
is given by the T-type continued fraction (
2.3
).
More precisely, every rise gets a weight 1,
every fall starting at height
i
gets a weight
\alpha_{i}
,
and every long level step at height
i
gets a weight
\delta_{i+1}
.
This combinatorial interpretation of T-fractions in terms of Schröder paths
was found recently by several authors

[
24
,
37
,
29
,
45
]
.

5.2
Labeled Dyck, Motzkin and Schröder paths

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{A}}}=({\mathcal{A}}_{h})_{h\geq 0}
,
{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}=({\mathcal{B}}_{h})_{h\geq 1}

and
{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}=({\mathcal{C}}_{h})_{h\geq 0}
be sequences of finite sets.
An

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Motzkin path of length
\bm{n}

is a pair
(\omega,\xi)

where
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{n})

is a Motzkin path of length
n
,
and
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is a sequence satisfying

\xi_{i}\>\in\>\begin{cases}{\mathcal{A}}(h_{i-1})&\textrm{if step $i$ is a rise (i.e.\ $h_{i}=h_{i-1}+1$)}\\[2.84526pt]
{\mathcal{B}}(h_{i-1})&\textrm{if step $i$ is a fall (i.e.\ $h_{i}=h_{i-1}-1$)}\\[2.84526pt]
{\mathcal{C}}(h_{i-1})&\textrm{if step $i$ is a level step (i.e.\ $h_{i}=h_{i-1}$)}\end{cases}

(5.5)

where
h_{i-1}
(resp.
h_{i}
) is the height of the Motzkin path
before (resp. after) step
i
.
[For typographical clarity
we have here written
{\mathcal{A}}(h)
as a synonym for
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
, etc.]
We call
\xi_{i}
the
label
associated to step
i
.
We call the pair
(\omega,\xi)

an
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})
-labeled Dyck path

if
\omega
is a Dyck path (in this case
{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}
plays no role).
We denote by
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})

the set of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Motzkin paths of length
n
,
and by
{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})

the set of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})
-labeled Dyck paths of length
2n
.

We define a
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled
Schröder path

in an analogous way;
now the sets
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}
refer to long level steps.
We denote by
{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})

the set of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Schröder paths
of length
2n
.

Let us stress that the sets
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
,
{\mathcal{B}}_{h}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}
are allowed
to be empty.
Whenever this happens, the path
\omega
is forbidden to take a step
of the specified kind starting at the specified height.

Remark.
What we have called an

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Motzkin path
is (up to small changes in notation)
called a
path diagramme
by Flajolet
[
20
, p. 136]

and a
history
by Viennot
[
50
, p. II-9]
.
Often the label sets
{\mathcal{A}}_{h},{\mathcal{B}}_{h},{\mathcal{C}}_{h}
are intervals of integers,
e.g.
{\mathcal{A}}_{h}=\{1,\ldots,A_{h}\}
or
\{0,\ldots,A_{h}\}
;
in this case the triplet
({\bf A},{\bf B},{\bf C})

of sequences of maximum values is called a
possibility function
.
On the other hand, it is sometimes useful to employ labels
that are
pairs
of integers
(e.g.
[
46
, Section 6.2]

and Section
7
below).
It therefore seems preferable to state the general theory
without any specific assumption about the nature of the label sets.

\blacksquare

Following Flajolet
[
20
, Proposition 7A]
,
we can state a “master J-fraction” for

({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Motzkin paths.
Let
{\bf a}=(a_{h,\xi})_{h\geq 0,\;\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}(h)}
,

{\bf b}=(b_{h,\xi})_{h\geq 1,\;\xi\in{\mathcal{B}}(h)}

and
{\bf c}=(c_{h,\xi})_{h\geq 0,\;\xi\in{\mathcal{C}}(h)}

be indeterminates;
we give an
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Motzkin path
(\omega,\xi)

a weight
W(\omega,\xi)

that is the product of the weights for the individual steps,
where a rise starting at height
h
with label
\xi
gets weight
a_{h,\xi}
,
a fall starting at height
h
with label
\xi
gets weight
b_{h,\xi}
,
and a level step at height
h
with label
\xi
gets weight
c_{h,\xi}
.
Then:

Theorem 5.2

(Flajolet’s master theorem for labeled Motzkin paths)
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\!\!\sum_{(\omega,\xi)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})}\!\!\!W(\omega,\xi)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-c_{0}t-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}t^{2}}{1-c_{1}t-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}t^{2}}{1-c_{2}t-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}t^{2}}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.6)

as an identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}][[t]]
, where

a_{h}\;=\;\sum_{\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}(h)}a_{h,\xi}\;,\qquad b_{h}\;=\;\sum_{\xi\in{\mathcal{B}}(h)}b_{h,\xi}\;,\qquad c_{h}\;=\;\sum_{\xi\in{\mathcal{C}}(h)}c_{h,\xi}\;.

(5.7)

This is an immediate consequence of Theorem
5.1

together with the definitions.

By specializing to
{\bf c}={\bm{0}}
and replacing
t^{2}
by
t
,
we obtain the corresponding theorem
for
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})
-labeled Dyck paths:

Corollary 5.3

(Flajolet’s master theorem for labeled Dyck paths)
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\!\!\sum_{(\omega,\xi)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}})}\!\!\!W(\omega,\xi)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}t}{1-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}t}{1-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}t}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.8)

as an identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[{\bf a},{\bf b}][[t]]
, where

a_{h}
and
b_{h}
are defined by (
5.7
).

Similarly, for labeled Schröder paths we have:

Theorem 5.4

(Flajolet’s master theorem for labeled Schröder paths)
.

We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\!\!\sum_{(\omega,\xi)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})}\!\!\!W(\omega,\xi)\;=\;\cfrac{1}{1-c_{0}t-\cfrac{a_{0}b_{1}t}{1-c_{1}t-\cfrac{a_{1}b_{2}t}{1-c_{2}t-\cfrac{a_{2}b_{3}t}{1-\cdots}}}}

(5.9)

as an identity in
{\mathbb{Z}}[{\bf a},{\bf b},{\bf c}][[t]]
, where

a_{h},b_{h},c_{h}
are defined by (
5.7
),
with
c_{h,\xi}
now referring to long level steps.

First T-fraction: Proof of Theorems
3.3
,

3.9
,
3.11
,

3.12
and

In this section we prove the first master T-fraction
(Theorem
3.11
)
by a bijection from D-permutations to labeled Schröder paths.
Our construction combines ideas of
Randrianarivony
[
40
]

with a variant
[
46
, Section 6.1]

of Foata–Zeilberger
[
23
]
,
together with some new ingredients.
After proving Theorem
3.11
,
we deduce Theorems

and
3.9

by specialization.
Then, in Section

,
we prove the variant T-fractions
of Theorems

and

.

Let us define an
almost-Dyck path
of length
2n

to be a path
\omega=(\omega_{0},\ldots,\omega_{2n})

in the right half-plane
{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
,
starting at
\omega_{0}=(0,0)
and ending at
\omega_{2n}=(2n,0)
,
using the steps
(1,1)
and
(1,-1)
,
that stays always at height
\geq-1
.
Thus, an almost-Dyck path is like a Dyck path
except that

a down step from height 0 to height
-1
is allowed;
note, however, that it must be immediately followed by
an up step back to height 0.
Each non-Dyck part of the path is therefore of the form

(h_{2i-2},h_{2i-1},h_{2i})=(0,-1,0)
.
We write
{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{2n}
for the set of almost-Dyck paths of length
2n
.

Next let us define a
0-Schröder path

to be a Schröder path in which long level steps, if any,
occur only at height 0.
We write
{\mathcal{S}}^{0}_{2n}
for the set of 0-Schröder paths of length
2n
.
There is an obvious bijection
\psi\colon{\mathcal{D}}^{\sharp}_{2n}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{0}_{2n}

from almost-Dyck paths to 0-Schröder paths:
namely, we replace each down-up pair starting and ending at height 0
with a long level step at height 0.

In this section we will construct a bijection from
D-permutations of
[2n]
onto
labeled 0-Schröder paths of length
2n
, as follows:
We first define the path by constructing an almost-Dyck path
\omega

and then transforming it into a 0-Schröder path
{\widehat{\omega}}=\psi(\omega)
.
Then we define the labels
\xi_{i}
, which will lie in the sets

{\mathcal{A}}_{h}&=\{0,\ldots,\lceil h/2\rceil\}\qquad\quad\;\;\hbox{for $h\geq 0$}\\
{\mathcal{B}}_{h}=\{0,\ldots,\lceil(h-1)/2\rceil\}\quad\hbox{for $h\geq 1$}\\
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\{0\}\\
{\mathcal{C}}_{h}=\varnothing\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\hbox{for $h\geq 1$}

(6.1)

We also interpret our crossing, nesting and record statistics
in terms of the heights and labels.
Next we prove that the map
\sigma\mapsto({\widehat{\omega}},\xi)

really is a bijection from the set
{\mathfrak{D}}_{2n}
of D-permutations of
[2n]

onto the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{2n}({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})

of
({\bm{\mathcal{A}}},{\bm{\mathcal{B}}},{\bm{\mathcal{C}}})
-labeled Schröder paths of length
2n
.
Finally, we sum over the labels
\xi
to obtain the weight
W({\widehat{\omega}})

associated to a Schröder path
{\widehat{\omega}}
,
which upon applying (
5.4
)
will yield Theorem
3.11
.

•

If

, then

is a fall.
(In this case
\sigma^{-1}(i)

•

is a fixed point, then

and a fall if

is odd.

.]

8
Some final remarks

When we began this work, we envisioned it as analogous to,
though probably more complicated than, the study of the “linear family”
[cf. (
1.3
)]
that was undertaken in
[
46
]
:
our goal was to make a corresponding study of the “quadratic family”
[cf. (
1.4
)].
What has surprised us is that the final results,
as well as the associated methods of proof,
turned out to be, not merely
analogous

to those of
[
46
]
,
but in fact very closely
parallel

— much more closely parallel than we expected.

The key objects of
[
46
]
were permutations of
[n]
;
the key results were J-fractions;
and the proofs involved bijections from permutations of
[n]

to labeled Motzkin paths of length
n
,
using the Foata–Zeilberger bijection for the first J-fraction
(which did not involve the counting of cycles)
and the Biane bijection for the second J-fraction
(which involved the counting of cycles).
The two bijections have the same paths but different labels.

By contrast, the key objects of the present paper are D-permutations of
[2n]
;
the key results are 0-T-fractions
(that is, T-fractions that have
\delta_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 2
);
and the proofs involve bijections from D-permutations of
[2n]

to labeled 0-Schröder paths
(or equivalently, labeled almost-Dyck paths) of length
2n
.
Once again, the bijections for the first and second continued fractions
have the same paths but different labels.
Since these 0-Schröder paths are very different from
the Motzkin paths of
[
46
]
,
the definitions of the paths must also be very different, and indeed they are.
The surprise was that the definitions of the
labels

in our constructions turned out to be almost identical
to those employed in
[
46
]

(where “almost” means that fixed points are treated differently):
once again, we use the Foata–Zeilberger labels for the first T-fraction
(which does not involve the counting of cycles)
and the Biane labels for the second T-fraction
(which involves the counting of cycles).

The final results also turned out to be amazingly similar.
Compare, for instance, Theorem
3.9

with
[
46
, Theorem 2.7]
:
our coefficient
\alpha_{2k-1}
is identical
to the coefficient
\beta_{k}
in
[
46
, eq. (2.53c)]
;
our coefficient
\alpha_{2k}
includes the same ingredients
as the coefficient
\gamma_{k}
in
[
46
, eq. (2.53b)]
,
but combined as a product rather than a sum.
An analogous comparison holds between our first master T-fraction
(Theorem
3.11
)
and the first master J-fraction in the earlier work

[
46
, Theorem 2.9]
.
Furthermore, an analogous comparison
holds between our conjectured second T-fraction
(Conjecture
4.1
)
and the conjectured second J-fraction found in the earlier work

[
46
, Conjecture 2.3]
,
and between our proved second T-fraction
(Theorem
4.2
)
when specialized to
{\widehat{v}}_{2}={\widehat{y}}_{2}

and the proved second J-fraction found in the earlier work

[
46
, Theorem 2.4]
.
Finally, an almost analogous comparison holds between
our second master T-fraction (Theorem
4.10
)
and the second master J-fraction in the earlier work

[
46
, Theorem 2.14]
,
the only difference being that the treatment of
{{\sf d}}

looks a bit more natural in the present work.

All this suggests to us that D-permutations are,
among all the combinatorial models of the
Genocchi and median Genocchi numbers,
a particularly well-behaved one,
which is closely analogous to ordinary permutations.
It would be interesting to know whether similar continued fractions,
in a large (or infinite) number of independent indeterminates,
can be found for some of the other models
of the Genocchi and median Genocchi numbers.
Our approach in
[
46
]
and the present paper
has been to introduce a natural classification of indices
into mutually exclusive categories
— here the parity-refined record-and-cycle classification
(Section
2.7
) —
and to build a homogeneous multivariate generating polynomial
implementing this classification.
It would be interesting to find analogous natural classifications
for the other combinatorial models.
