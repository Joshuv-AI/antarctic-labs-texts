On the Construction of Averaged Deep Denoisers for Image Regularization
Pravin Nair, Kunal N. Chaudhury

Abstract
Plug-and-Play (PnP) and Regularization by Denoising (RED) are recent paradigms for image reconstruction that can leverage the power of modern denoisers for image regularization. In particular, these algorithms have been shown to deliver state-of-the-art reconstructions using CNN denoisers. Since the regularization is performed in an ad-hoc manner in PnP and RED, understanding their convergence has been an active research area. Recently, it was observed in many works that iterate convergence of PnP and RED can be guaranteed if the denoiser is averaged or nonexpansive. However, integrating nonexpansivity with gradient-based learning is a challenging task—checking nonexpansivity is known to be computationally intractable. Using numerical examples, we show that existing CNN denoisers violate the nonexpansive property and can cause the PnP iterations to diverge. In fact, algorithms for training nonexpansive denoisers either cannot guarantee nonexpansivity of the final denoiser or are computation intensive. In this work, we propose to construct averaged (contractive) image denoisers by unfolding ISTA and ADMM iterations applied to wavelet denoising and demonstrate that their regularization capacity for PnP and RED can be matched with CNN denoisers. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to propose a simple framework for training provably averaged (contractive) denoisers using unfolding networks.

Introduction

The problem of recovering a ground-truth image
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from incomplete or corrupted measurements
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
comes up in applications such as deblurring, superresolution, inpainting, demosaicking, despeckling, MRI and tomography
ribes2008linear
;
engl1996regularization
;
dong2011image
. The standard optimization framework for such problems involves a loss function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
derived from the measurement process, and a regularizer
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
that incorporates prior knowledge about the ground-truth. That is, the reconstruction is performed by solving the optimization problem

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\min}\ f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{x}).

(1)

The loss
f
is typically differentiable, while the regularizer
g
can be discontinuous
engl1996regularization
. For linear inverse problems such as deblurring, superresolution, inpainting and compressed sensing
dong2011image
;
jagatap2019algorithmic
, the loss is quadratic, i.e.,
f(\boldsymbol{x})={\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\bf{A}\boldsymbol{x}\rVert}^{2}
, where
\bf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the measurement matrix and
\boldsymbol{y}
is the measurement vector. On the other hand, for applications such as single photon imaging, Poisson denoising, and despeckling
chan2017plug
;
rond2016poisson
;
bioucas2010multiplicative
,
f
is non-quadratic.

For imaging applications, total-variation and other sparsity-promoting functionals
rudin1992nonlinear
;
candes2008enhancing
;
dong2011sparsity
are the most commonly used regularizers. For such regularizers,
g
is typically convex but non-differentiable
engl1996regularization
;
zhang2017image
. If the loss
f
is convex, (
1
) can be solved using iterative algorithms such as Iterative Shrinkage Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA), Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), Douglas Rachford Splitting (DRS), etc.
beck2009fast
;
bauschke2017convex
. Model-based optimization is interpretable, computationally tractable, and can be applied to a wide range of reconstruction problems. The flip side is that handcrafted regularizers are generally weaker than learning based models and the optimization process can be time consuming
zhang2017image
.

1.1
Plug-and-Play regularization

Classical algorithms such as ISTA and ADMM essentially perform repeated inversion of the measurement model following by a regularization (denoising) step.
More precisely, the regularization is performed using the proximal operator of
g
:

\mathrm{Prox}_{g}(\boldsymbol{x})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rVert^{2}+g(\boldsymbol{y})\right\},

(2)

where
\|.\|
is the Euclidean norm. The proximal operator can be seen as a denoiser—it takes a noisy image
\boldsymbol{x}
and returns an image which is close to
\boldsymbol{x}
and for which the penalty
g
is small
parikh2014proximal
. This operator is at the core of ISTA and ADMM.
In particular, the ISTA update
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
is given by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k+1}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}-\rho\nabla\!f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}),

(3)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1}=\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho g}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}).

where
\rho>0
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an arbitrary initialization. On the other hand, starting with
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the ADMM update
(\boldsymbol{v}_{k},\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\to(\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1})
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}=\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho g}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}_{k}),

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho f}(\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{k}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{k}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}),

where
\rho>0
is a penalty parameter.

The ISTA update is cheaper than the ADMM update when computing
\nabla\!f
is less expensive than computing
\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho f}
. In fact, the advantage with ADMM is that the loss
f
need not be differentiable unlike ISTA; however,
\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho f}
should be easily computable, either in closed-form or iteratively
parikh2014proximal
.

In Plug-and-Play (PnP) regularization
sreehari2016plug
, instead of having to handcraft the regularizer and apply it via its proximal operator, we directly “plug” a powerful denoiser such as BM3D
dabov2007image
or DnCNN
zhang2017beyond
in ISTA and ADMM. Thus, for example, in the PnP variant of ISTA, referred to as PnP-ISTA, the update is performed as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k+1}={\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}-\rho\nabla\!f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{k}).

(5)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k+1}=\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}),

where
\mathrm{D}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the plugged denoiser. Similarly in PnP-ADMM, the updates are performed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}=\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}_{k}),

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho f}(\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}+\boldsymbol{z}_{k}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{k}+(\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}).

Remarkably, PnP algorithms yield state-of-the-art results across a wide range of applications including superresolution, MRI, fusion, and tomography

sreehari2016plug
;
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
;
Sun2019_PnP_SGD
;
zhang2017learning
;
Tirer2019_iter_denoising
;
zhang2021plug
.
The best performing PnP algorithms, e.g.
zhang2017learning
;
Tirer2019_iter_denoising
;
zhang2021plug
, use trained deep denoisers like DnCNN
zhang2017beyond
and IRCNN
zhang2017learning
. This opened up the possibility of combining model-based and learning based methods
zhang2017learning
.

The idea of using denoisers for regularization has also been explored in another class of algorithms termed as Regularization by Denoising (RED). For example, in
romano2017little
, the authors proposed to work with the regularizer

g(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\!\big(\boldsymbol{x}-\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{x})\big),

(7)

and the corresponding reconstruction problem:

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\,f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\!\big(\boldsymbol{x}-\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{x})\big),

(8)

where
\mathrm{D}
is the denoiser as in PnP. This is solved iteratively using ADMM or gradient based methods, where the gradient of regularizer in (
7
) was approximated by
\boldsymbol{x}-\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{x})

romano2017little
. It was later shown that this gradient formulation is exact only when the denoiser
\mathrm{D}
has a symmetric Jacobian
reehorst2018regularization
.
RED and its variants have been shown to yield state-of-the-art performance for phase retrieval, tomography, superresolution, deblurring, and compressed sensing
metzler2018prdeep
;
wu2020simba
;
wu2019online
;
mataev2019deepred
;
sun2019block
;
hu2022monotonically
.

There is a different paradigm for solving inverse problems, termed as deep unfolding networks
gregor2010learning
;
sun2016deep
;
zhang2018ista
, where an iterative algorithm such as ISTA are “unrolled’ into a network which is then trained in an end-to-end fashion similar to deep learning methods.
Deep unfolding networks can produce impressive results but, unlike PnP or RED (where just the denoiser has to be trained), we have to train them end-to-end for different applications. The present work is motivated by deep unfolding networks—we propose to construct the nonexpansive denoiser by unfolding classical wavelet denoising, and then apply it within PnP and RED algorithms for image reconstruction.

1.2
Convergence of PnP algorithms

The success of PnP and RED algorithms has sparked interest in the theoretical understanding of these algorithms. For example, it has been shown that for a class of linear denoisers, the PnP is guaranteed to converge and the limit point is in fact the solution of a convex optimization problem
sreehari2016plug
;
nair2021fixed
;
gavaskar2021plug
. This is done using Moreau’s classical characterization of proximal operators, namely, that the class of linear denoisers in question can be expressed as the proximal operator of a (closed and proper) convex function. Unfortunately, this result does not hold for powerful nonlinear denoisers such as BM3D, DnCNN or IRCNN.
This is because the proximal operator of a convex function must be nonexpansive and such denoisers have been shown to violate the nonexpansive property
reehorst2018regularization
.

Though it is difficult to come up with a variational characterization for deep denoisers, it is nonetheless possible to establish iterate convergence for PnP algorithms. For example, convergence of PnP-ISTA has been established for generative denoisers in the context of compressed sensing and phase retrieval
jagatap2019algorithmic
;
liu2021recovery
;
raj2019gan
. For strongly-convex loss functions, convergence of PnP-ISTA and PnP-ADMM can be guaranteed if we use a CNN denoiser whose residual is nonexpansive
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
. Recently, motivated by RED, convergence was established for PnP-ISTA, PnP-ADMM, and PnP modeled on half-quadratic splitting and gradient descent
hurault2022gradient
;
cohen2021has
;
hurault2022proximal
.

On the other hand, convergence guarantees for RED algorithms require the denoiser to be nonexpansive
reehorst2018regularization
;
sun2019block
;
liu2021recovery
;
cohen2021regularization
.
In particular, the authors in
sun2019block
developed a block coordinate RED algorithm, which was shown to exhibit convergence for nonexpansive denoisers.
In
liu2021recovery
, the authors showed that a nonexpansive denoiser can effect convergence of a variant of RED algorithm for compressed sensing. In
cohen2021regularization
, RED is formulated as a convex optimization problem where a nonexpansive denoiser is used. As against these, a new variant of RED is proposed in
hu2022monotonically
, where the denoiser is not required to be nonexpansive for algorithmic convergence. We note that the connection between RED algorithms and PnP-ISTA has been explored in
liu2021recovery
;
cohen2021regularization
.

Our current understanding of convergence of PnP and RED can be summarized as follows:

•

convergence of PnP is guaranteed for averaged (cf. Definition
1
) denoisers
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
;
Sun2019_PnP_SGD
;
nair2021fixed
;
sun2021scalable
.

•

for nonexpansive denoisers, convergence is guaranteed for RED
reehorst2018regularization
;
sun2019block
;
cohen2021regularization
, PnP-ISTA applied to compressive sensing
liu2021recovery
, and variant of PnP-ISTA in
reehorst2018regularization
.

For the above reason, there has significant research on training averaged or more generally nonexpansive CNN denoisers. The core issue in this respect is that computing the Lipschitz constant of a CNN is intractable
virmaux2018lipschitz
. By Lipschitz constant, we mean the smallest
\beta
such that
\lVert\mathrm{T}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathrm{T}(\boldsymbol{v})\rVert\leqslant\beta\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert
for all inputs
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
to the network, represented as an operator
\mathrm{T}
. In particular, while the Lipschitz constant of a convolution layer is just the largest singular value of the matrix representation of the layer (which can be computed efficiently from the FFT of its filters
sedghi2018singular
) and the Lipschitz constant of common nonlinear activations (ReLU and sigmoid) is
1
, computing the Lipschitz constant for their cascaded composition is far from obvious. However, we can compute a (loose) upper bound for the Lipschitz constant, namely, the product of the largest singular value of the convolution layers
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
. Thus, for example, if we can force the singular value of each convolutional layer to be within
1
at each epoch of the training process, then the final network is guaranteed to be nonexpansive. Several methods have been proposed in the literature that are essentially based on this idea, e.g., see
sedghi2018singular
;
hertrich2021convolutional
;
terris2020building
;
pesquet2021learning
. In particular, we will later compare with the nonexpansive CNN denoiser in
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
, where the largest singular value of a convolutional layer is estimated using power iterations, which is used to force the Lipschitz constant of the layer to be within
1
after each gradient step.

1.3
Motivation

This work was motivated by the observation that existing CNN denoisers violate the nonexpansive property (see Fig.
1
). In fact, existing methods for training nonexpansive denoisers either do not guarantee the final network to be nonexpansive or are computationally expensive. For example, power iterations are used for approximate computation of the largest singular value in
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
;
pesquet2021learning
. Hence, the network is not guaranteed to be nonexpansive at each epoch. On the other hand, the methods in
sedghi2018singular
;
hertrich2021convolutional
;
terris2020building
for constraining the convolutional layers are computationally expensive— for example, the time taken for
sedghi2018singular
is about nine-fold that of training without constraints. In particular, the projection in
sedghi2018singular
requires the full singular value decomposition (SVD) of the convolutional layers after every gradient step, and this in turn requires the FFT of the filters followed by the SVD of a large matrix derived from the FFTs. Similarly, the projection step in
terris2020building
requires the FFT of the filters in every convolutional layer. The training mechanism in
hertrich2021convolutional
requires us to solve an optimization problem to impose orthogonality constraints on the convolutional layers.

We also highlight a technical issue which has been sidelined in existing works
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
;
sedghi2018singular
;
hertrich2021convolutional
;
terris2020building
;
pesquet2021learning
, where filters in CNN denoisers are trained on images of a fixed size but deployed on images of arbitrary size. Since the filters are zero-padded to match the image size, their Lispchitz constant depends on the image size.
For example, consider the
3\times 3
Sobel filter
[1,0,-1;2,0,-2;1,0,-1]
. The Lipschitz constant of this filter applied to images of size
25\times 25
is
7.9842
, whereas the Lipschitz constant of the same filter for
256\times 256
images is
8
.
Thus, a guarantee that the Lipschitz bound is independent of the image size is not available for existing CNN denoisers.

To substantiate our point, we present numerical evidence (see Fig.
1
) showing that BM3D
dabov2007image
, DnCNN
zhang2017beyond
, and the denoiser in
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
referred to as N-CNN, violate the nonexpansive property.
That is, we produce images
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
such that
\|\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathrm{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\|/\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|
is greater than
1
. The detailed procedure used to generate these counterexamples is described in Section
5.1
.
We note that the such counterexamples have also been reported in
reehorst2018regularization
.
Note that the denoiser N-CNN is trained for its residual to be nonexpansive. In Fig.

(c), both N-CNN and its residual are shown to violate the nonexpansive property.

We summarize our observations below:

•

The Lipschitz bound of a CNN (trained with filters of fixed size) depends on the size of the input image and is difficult to predict.

•

Existing CNN denoisers violate the nonexpansive property. Loss of nonexpansivity of the denoiser can result in divergence of the PnP iterates and poor PSNR values.

•

Existing algorithms to train nonexpansive CNN denoisers either lack the guarantee that the final denoiser is nonexpansive or are computationally expensive. In fact, coming up with efficient training algorithms to learn provably nonexpansive denoisers is a difficult task.

1.4
Contribution

Following the above observations, we moved away from CNNs and instead considered deep unfolding networks. In fact, we tried various methods to train a nonexpansive CNN denoiser and found that there is a trade-off between forcing nonexpansivity and the denoising performance—a large drop in denoising performance was observed if we force the denoiser to be nonexpansive. Indeed, it has been shown in
hurault2022proximal
;
hertrich2021convolutional
that imposing hard Lipschitz constraints on the CNN can adversely affect its denoising performance.

In the rest of the paper, we show that it is indeed possible to construct deep unfolding networks that comes with the following desirable properties:

•

Unlike
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
;
sedghi2018singular
, they are provably averaged (or contractive) by construction and hence automatically nonexpansive.

•

The training is not expensive compared to
sedghi2018singular
;
terris2020building
;
pesquet2021learning
. In particular, after every gradient step, we are required to perform cheap projections (of the hyperparameters) onto intervals.

•

When plugged into PnP and RED algorithms, the reconstruction is at par with state-of-the-art denoisers.

Our construction is motivated by the averaged (contractive) property of fixed-point operators arising in ISTA and ADMM, which are classically used to establish iterate convergence.
In fact, the building blocks of our denoiser are obtained by unfolding ISTA or ADMM applied to the wavelet denoising problem.
These blocks are provably averaged (contractive) and the proposed denoiser being a simple cascade (composition) of these building blocks is automatically averaged (contractive). While our denoiser is trained on small patches, we propose a formula for patch aggregation which comes with the guarantee that the end-to-end image denoiser is averaged (contractive). The denoising capacity of our denoiser is short of BM3D and DNCNN, however, importantly, their regularization capacity (within PnP and RED frameworks) is comparable with BM3D and DnCNN.

2
Contractive & Averaged Operators

In this section, we look at contractive and averaged operators and highlight some relevant properties of such operators. These are used to motivate the construction of the proposed denoiser. Importantly, we point out the connection between such operators and fixed-point convergence of PnP algorithms.

Definition 1
.

An operator
\mathrm{T}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
\beta
-Lipschitz for some
\beta>0
if

\forall\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\quad\left\lVert\mathrm{T}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathrm{T}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\rVert\leqslant\beta\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert.

In particular,
\mathrm{T}
is said to be nonexpansive if
\beta=1
, and
\mathrm{T}
is contractive if
\beta\in[0,1)
. An operator
\mathrm{T}
is said to be
\theta
-averaged, with relaxation parameter
\theta\in[0,1)
, if we can write
\mathrm{T}=(1-\theta)\mathrm{I}+\theta\mathrm{N}
, where
\mathrm{N}
is nonexpansive.

It is evident from the above definitions that an averaged operator is nonexpansive.
The converse is however not true; e.g., the operator
-\mathrm{I}
is nonexpansive but not averaged
bauschke2017convex
. On the other hand, every contractive operator is necessarily averaged, but not every averaged operator is contractive.
Thus, contractive operators form a strict subset of averaged operators, which itself is a strict subset of nonexpansive operators
bauschke2017convex
. The gradient and proximal operators
\mathrm{I}-\rho\nabla\!f
and
\mathrm{Prox}_{\alpha f}
associated with a convex function
f
are averaged. The precise results are collected below.

Lemma 1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
\nabla f
be
\beta
-Lipschitz. Then the operator
\mathrm{I}-\rho\nabla\!f
is
(\beta\rho/2)
-averaged for
0\leqslant\rho<2/\beta
. Moreover, if
f
is
\mu
-strongly convex, then
\mathrm{I}-\rho\nabla\!f
is contractive for
0<\rho<2/\mathrm{max}(\beta,\mu)
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
\alpha>0
. Then the operator
\mathrm{Prox}_{\alpha f}
is
1/2
-averaged.

A proof of Lemma
1
can be found in
(
nair2021fixed
, Lemma
7.3
)
and see

(
bauschke2017convex
, Proposition
2.28
, Proposition
4.4
)
for Lemma
2
.
We will later use Lemmas
1
and
2
to show that a single layer of our denoising network is either contractive or averaged.

Averaged operators are closed under composition. The exact relaxation parameter of the composition is described in Lemma
3
. Contractive operators are also closed under composition. We will use these observations to show that if the layers of the denoiser are averaged (contractive), then the end-to-end denoiser is averaged (contractive).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathrm{T}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{T}_{m}
be operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are
\theta
-averaged. Then their composition
\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{T}_{1}\circ\mathrm{T}_{2}\circ\cdots\circ\mathrm{T}_{m}
is
m\theta/\big(1+(m-1)\theta\big)
-averaged. If each
\mathrm{T}_{i}
is contractive, then their composition
\mathrm{T}
is contractive.

See
(
bauschke2017convex
, Proposition
4.46
)
for a proof. We next look at the average of such operators.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathrm{T}_{1},\mathrm{T}_{2},\ldots\mathrm{T}_{k}
be
\theta
-averaged (contractive) operators. Then
(1/k)(\mathrm{T}_{1}+\mathrm{T}_{2}\cdots+\mathrm{T}_{k})
is
\theta
-averaged (contractive).

A proof of this result can be found in
(
bauschke2017convex
, Proposition
4.42
)
. Later, we will use this to construct an averaged (contractive) image-level denoiser from an averaged (contractive) patch-based denoiser.

3
Fixed Point Convergence

Contractive and averaged operators can be used to establish fixed-point convergence of classical proximal (e.g. ISTA and ADMM) and PnP algorithms
Ryu2019_PnP_trained_conv
;
Sun2019_PnP_SGD
;

4
Averaged Deep Denoiser

Motivated by the convergence theory of averaged denoisers, we propose a mechanism to train denoisers that are contractive or averaged by construction and whose denoising capability is close to that of modern denoisers. As explained in Section
1.3
, training a nonexpansive CNN denoiser presents several challenges. This led us to consider a different solution to the problem based on unfolded networks. More specifically, we unfold ISTA and ADMM operators applied to classical wavelet denoising, which are used to construct trainable patch denoisers that are provably averaged (or contractive). This trained patch denoiser is then used to design an end-to-end image denoiser by using a patch aggregation formula. The novelty in this regard is that we prove that the image denoiser inherits the averaged (or contractive) property of the original patch denoiser.

4.1
Wavelet thresholding and unfolding

Given a noisy patch
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, consider the wavelet denoising problem

\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\min}\ \psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+g(\boldsymbol{x}),

(11)

where
\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=(1/2){\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert}^{2}
and
g
is the form

g(\boldsymbol{x})=\|\bf{W}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\bf{\Lambda}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}\lvert{(\bf{W}\boldsymbol{x})}_{j}\rvert,

where
\bf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is an orthogonal wavelet transform and
\bf{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p})
is a diagonal positive definite matrix. By definition, the closed-form solution of (
11
) is given by the proximal operator of
g
, namely,

\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho g}(\boldsymbol{x})=\bf{W}^{\top}\bf{S}_{\bf{\Lambda}}(\bf{W}\boldsymbol{x}),

(12)

where
\rho
is absorbed into
\Lambda
and
\bf{S}_{\bf{\Lambda}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the soft-thresholding operator:

\forall\,i=1,\ldots,p:\quad(\bf{S}_{\bf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{x}))_{i}=\mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\max(0,\lvert\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\rvert-\lambda_{i}).

Our present interest is using this closed-form solution in the iterative ISTA and ADMM algorithms for solving (
11
), which we wish to unfold.
For example, the ISTA updates are given by
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}=\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ISTA}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
, where
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ISTA}}
is given by (
9
) but with the difference that the loss term
f
in (
9
) is now
\psi
. In particular, substituting
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\psi(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=(1-\rho)\boldsymbol{x}+\rho\boldsymbol{y}
and (
12
) in
\mathrm{T}_{{\mathrm{ISTA}}}
, we obtain the ISTA block.

Definition 2
.

The ISTA block is the operator
\mathrm{U}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\mathrm{U}(\boldsymbol{x};\rho,\bf{\Lambda})=\bf{W}^{\top}\bf{S}_{\bf{\Lambda}}\big(\bf{W}\big((1-\rho)\boldsymbol{x}+\rho\boldsymbol{y}\big)\big).

(13)

The ISTA block can be interpreted as performing an inversion step using gradient descent on the loss
\psi
, followed by wavelet regularization via the proximal operator of
g
. The trainable parameters in (
13
) are
\rho
and
\bf{\Lambda}
, and the fixed parameters are the wavelet transform
\bf{W}
and input
\boldsymbol{y}
. Unlike the unfolding in LISTA
gregor2010learning
, we use a fixed transform
\bf{W}
. This is because we wish to retain the proximal structure in (
13
) and the orthogonality of
\bf{W}
plays a crucial role in this regard. It is difficult to train
\bf{W}
to satisfy orthogonality constraints.

Similar to ISTA block, we can unfold the ADMM iterations for solving (
11
). For this, we need to compute
\mathrm{R}_{\rho g}
and
\mathrm{R}_{\rho\psi}
in (
10
). Now, we can express
\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho g}
as (
12
), where
\rho
is absorbed in the tunable parameter
\bf{\Lambda}
. On the other hand,

\mathrm{Prox}_{\rho\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})={(1+\rho)}^{-1}\left(\rho\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}\right).

(14)

Substituting (
12
) and (
14
) in (
10
), we obtain the ADMM block.

Definition 3
.

The ADMM block is the operator
\mathrm{U}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\mathrm{U}(\boldsymbol{x};\rho,\bf{\Lambda})=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{1}{2}\Big(2\bf{W}^{\top}\bf{S}_{\bf{\Lambda}}\!\big(\bf{W}\mathrm{R}_{\rho\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)-\mathrm{R}_{\rho\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big),

(15)

where

\mathrm{R}_{\rho\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=2{(1+\rho)}^{-1}\left(\rho\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}\right)-\boldsymbol{x}.

The ADMM block can be interpreted as performing an inversion by using proximal operator of
\psi
, followed by regularization using proximal operator of
g
. As with the ISTA block, the trainable parameters in (
15
) are
\rho
and
\bf{\Lambda}
, while
\bf{W}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
are fixed.

The ISTA and ADMM blocks are depicted in Fig.

. We next show that they are averaged by construction; in fact, since
\psi
is strongly convex in
\boldsymbol{x}
, we can say more for the ISTA block.

The proposed patch denoiser
\mathrm{D}_{\text{patch}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

\mathrm{D}_{\text{patch}}=\mathrm{U}^{(L)}\circ\mathrm{U}^{(L-1)}\circ\cdots\circ\mathrm{U}^{(1)},

4.3
Patch aggregation

The denoiser
\mathrm{D}_{\text{patch}}
in the previous section can be used to denoise patches from the noisy image, which would then have to be aggregated to obtain the denoised image
zhang2017image
;
dabov2007image
. In particular, for patch aggregation, we extract overlapping patches using some stride, denoise the patches using denoisers in (15) and for
overlapped pixels between patches, average them. The challenge is to ensure that the final image-to-image denoiser is averaged or contractive. We next explain in detail how this can be done.

Let
\Omega=[0,q-1]^{2}
denote the image domain and
\bf{X}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be the input (noisy) image.
We assume periodic (or circular) boundary condition, i.e., for
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\boldsymbol{i}-\boldsymbol{\tau}
is defined as
\big((i_{1}-\tau_{1})\ \mathrm{mod}\ q,(i_{2}-\tau_{2})\ \mathrm{mod}\ q\big)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
q
is multiple of the patch size
k
, since we can always zero pad the image to satisfy the condition.

For
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Omega
, let
{\mathrm{P}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\bf{X})
be the vectorized representation of the patch starting at pixel
\boldsymbol{i}
. In particular,
{\mathrm{P}}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\bf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{k^{2}}
is obtained by vectorizing the samples
\{\bf{X}(\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{\tau}),\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in[0,k-1]^{2}\}
in some fixed order. Suppose we choose to denoise patches with a stride
s
, where
s
divides
k
(and hence
q
). For example,
s=1
corresponds to denoising every image patch. Let
q_{s}=q/s
and define
\bf{J}=\{0,s,2s,\ldots,(q_{s}-1)s\}\times\{0,s,2s,\ldots,(q_{s}-1)s\}\subseteq\Omega
. The points in
\bf{J}
are the starting coordinates of the extracted patches. We extract the patches
\{\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\bf{X}),\,\boldsymbol{j}\in\bf{J}\}
from
\bf{X}
with stride
s
, denoise them using (
16
), and finally average them to obtain the denoised image. More precisely, we define the image-to-image denoiser to be

{\mathrm{D}}(\bf{X})=\frac{1}{k_{s}^{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}\in\bf{J}}\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{\top}\big(\mathrm{D}_{\text{patch}}(\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\bf{X}))\big),

(17)

where
k_{s}=k/s
and
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{j}}^{\top}
is the adjoint of the linear operator
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
. The factor

is the number of patches that contain a given pixel; the assumption that

divides

is essential to ensure that this is the same for every pixel. Given a vector
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{z})
is an image with the same domain
\Omega
as the original image, where the vectorization of the patch at position
\boldsymbol{i}
is

and the remaining pixels are set to zero. A precise definition of

is provided in the Appendix.
We can show this as follows, if

is number of patches a pixel belong to, then

by double counting and hence

. In (

\lvert\bf{J}\rvert=q_{s}^{2}
is the total number of extracted patches.

Proposition 11

If

{\mathrm{D}}
is contractive, and if
\mathrm{D}_{\text{patch}}

In particular, we record the following important implication of the results so far.

Corollary 12

We train our denoiser

6
Conclusion

We trained averaged (contractive) patch denoisers by unfolding ISTA and ADMM applied to wavelet denoising, and showed how they can be extended for image denoising using patch aggregation while preserving their averaged (contractive) property. Moreover, we showed
that their regularization capacity for PnP and RED can be brought at par with BM3D and DnCNN by adding sufficiently many layers. Unlike existing CNN denoisers, we can guarantee convergence of PnP and RED using the proposed denoisers and this was verified numerically. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to exploit the properties of proximal operators in unfolded algorithms to come up with averaged and contractive denoisers. In future work, we wish to explore if we can go beyond classical wavelet denoising and come up with unfolded averaged (contractive) denoisers with superior denoising and regularization capacity.

Appendix: Proof of Proposition
11

We recall some notations and definitions from Section
4
. The input image is denoted by
\bf{X}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\Omega=[0,q-1]^{2}
is the image domain. We also consider the image in matrix form as
\bf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
. We use circular shifts, i.e., for
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,

\boldsymbol{i}-\boldsymbol{\tau}
is defined as
\left((i_{1}-\tau_{1})\ \mathrm{mod}\ q,(i_{2}-\tau_{2})\ \mathrm{mod}\ q\right)
.

For
\boldsymbol{i}\in\Omega
, the patch extraction operator
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}:\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a linear operator such that
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\bf{X})
is the vector representation of the samples
\{\bf{X}(\boldsymbol{i}+\boldsymbol{\tau}):\boldsymbol{\tau}\in[0,k-1]^{2}\}
, where
p=k^{2}
is the number of pixels in a patch. The adjoint of
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
appears in definition (
17
) and has the following mathematical characterization. Consider the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
:

\forall\,\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\quad\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\right\rangle=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{y},

and the Frobenius inner-product on
\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
:

\forall\,\bf{X},\bf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}:\hskip 11.49994pt\left\langle\bf{X},\bf{Y}\right\rangle=\mathrm{Trace}(\bf{X}^{\top}\!\bf{Y}).

We will use the same notation
\lVert\cdot\rVert
for the norms induced by these inner products; this should be clear from the context.
The adjoint of
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
is the unique linear operator
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{\top}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
such that

\forall\,\bf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q},\ \boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\hskip 11.49994pt\left\langle\boldsymbol{y},\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\bf{X})\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{y}),\bf{X}\right\rangle,

In particular,
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}^{\top}
maps a vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
to a
k\times k
patch (using the same ordering used to construct
\mathrm{P}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
) and places it in location
\boldsymbol{i}
and the remaining pixels are assigned zero value. We extract patches with stride
s
, where
s
divides
k
. Let
q_{s}=q/s
and
\bf{J}=\{0,s,2s,\ldots(q_{s}-1)s\}\times\{0,s,2s,\ldots(q_{s}-1)s\}
;
Note that that
|\bf{J}|=q_{s}^{2}
is the number of patches and every pixel belongs to
k_{s}^{2}
patches, where
k_{s}=k/s
.

The main idea behind Proposition
11
is to express the original overlapping patches in terms of multiple sets of non-overlapping patches; this allows us to use some form of orthogonality in the calculations. In particular, let
q_{k}=q/k
. Note that since we assume
q
to be a multiple of
k
,
q_{k}
is an integer. We can extract
q_{k}^{2}
non-overlapping patches from
\bf{X}
. More precisely, let

\displaystyle\bf{J}_{0}

\displaystyle=\big\{0,k,2k,\ldots(q_{k}-1)k\big\}

\displaystyle\times\big\{0,k,2k,\ldots(q_{k}-1)k\big\}.

By construction,
\bf{J}_{0}\subseteq\bf{J}
and the points in
\bf{J}_{0}
are the starting coordinates of non-overlapping patches.
It is not difficult to see that
\bf{J}
is the disjoint union of (circular) shifts of
\bf{J}_{0}
:

\bf{J}=\bigcup\limits_{m,n=0}^{k_{s}-1}\big(\bf{J}_{0}+s(m,n)\big).

(21)

In particular, using (
21
), we can write (
17
) as

\mathrm{D}=\frac{1}{k_{s}^{2}}\sum_{m,n=0}^{k_{s}-1}\mathrm{D}_{mn},

where

is defined as

(22)

We make the following easily verifiable claims.
