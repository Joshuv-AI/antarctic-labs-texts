A Coreset for Approximate Furthest-Neighbor Queries in a Simple Polygon
Mark de Berg, Leonidas Theocharous

Abstract
Let 𝒫 \mathcal{P} be a simple polygon with m m vertices and let P P be a set of n n points inside 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . We prove that there exists, for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , a set C ⊂ P C\subset P of size O ⁡ ( 1 / ε 2 ) O(1/\varepsilon^{2}) such that the following holds: for any query point q q inside the polygon 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , the geodesic distance from q q to its furthest neighbor in C C is at least 1 − ε 1-\varepsilon times the geodesic distance to its further neighbor in P P . Thus the set C C can be used for answering ε \varepsilon -approximate furthest-neighbor queries with a data structure whose storage requirement is independent of the size of P P . The coreset can be constructed in O ⁡ ( 1 ε ​ ( n ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + ( n + m ) ​ log ⁡ ( n + m ) ) ) O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+(n+m)\log(n+m)\right)\right) time.

Introduction

Background and problem statement.

In nearest-neighbor searching the goal is to preprocess a set
P
of
n
points in a metric space
such that, for a query point
q
, one can quickly retrieve its nearest neighbor in
P
.
Exact and approximate data structures for nearest-neighbor queries have been studied
in many settings, including general metric spaces, 2- and higher-dimensional
Euclidean spaces, and inside (simple) polygons.

Furthest-neighbor queries
, while not as popular as nearest-neighbor queries,
have been studied extensively as well. Here the goal is to report a point in
P
that

maximizes
its distance to the query point
q
. We will denote
an (arbitrarily chosen, if it is not unique) furthest neighbor of
q
in
P
by
\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)
.
Furthest-neighbor queries can be answered exactly by performing point location in

\mbox{\sc fpvd}(P)
, the furthest-point Voronoi diagram of
P
.
In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\mbox{\sc fpvd}(P)
has complexity
O(n)
and it can be computed
in
O(n\log n)
time
[
9
]
, and even in
O(n)
time
if the convex hull is given
[
4
]
.
Furthermore, point-location queries can be answered in
O(\log n)
time with a
linear-space data structure
[
9
]
,
giving an optimal solution for exact furthest-neighbor queries.
Furthest-site Voronoi diagrams in the plane have also been studied for non-point sites,
such as line segments
[
5
]
and polygons
[
8
]
,
and even in an abstract setting
[
13
]
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
the worst-case complexity of the furthest-point Voronoi diagram
is
O(n^{\left\lceil d/2\right\rceil})

[
19
]
.
(This also holds for polyhedral distance functions
[
6
]
.)
Hence, answering furthest-neighbor queries using
\mbox{\sc fpvd}(P)

leads to a data structure whose storage is exponential in the dimension
d
.
Bespamyatnikh
[
7
]
therefore studied

\varepsilon
-approximate furthest-neighbor queries
, where the goal is to
report a point in
P
whose distance to the query point
q
is at least
1-\varepsilon

times the distance from
q
to its actual furthest neighbor.
He presented a data structure with
O(dn)
storage that answers
\varepsilon
-approximate
furthest-neighbor queries in
O(1/\varepsilon^{d-1})
time.
Note that the storage is linear in
d
(and
n
), and that the query time is independent of
n
.
The query time is exponential in
d
, however, so in very high dimensions
this will not be efficient. Pagh
et al.

[
16
]
, building on earlier work
of Indyk
[
12
]
, presented a data structure for which both the storage and
the query time are linear in
d
: for a given
c>1
they
can build a data structure that uses
\tilde{O}(dn^{2/c^{2}})
storage and
can report a
c
-approximate
1

That is, a point
p\in P
whose
distance to the query point
q
is at least
1/c
times the distance from
q
to its actual furthest neighbor.
Note the slight inconsistency in notation when compared to the definition of
\varepsilon
-approximate nearest neighbor.

furthest neighbor with constant probability in time
\tilde{O}(dn^{2/c^{2}})
.
Finally, Goel, Indyk, and Varadarajan
[
10
]
observed that
a set
C\subset P
of at most
d+1
points defining the smallest enclosing ball of
P

has the property that, for any query point
q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the furthest neighbor of
q

in
C
is a
\sqrt{2}
-approximation to the actual furthest neighbor.

Our contribution.

In this paper we study furthest-neighbor queries on a set
P
of
n
points
inside a simple polygon
\mathcal{P}
with
m
vertices, under the geodesic distance.
The
geodesic distance
between two points
x,y\in\mathcal{P}

is the Euclidean length of the shortest path
\pi(x,y)
connecting them in
\mathcal{P}
.
(Shortest-path problems inside a simple polygon have received considerable attention,
and it is beyond the scope of our paper discuss all the work that has been done.
The interested reader may consult the survey by Mitchell
[
14
]

for an introduction into the area.)
It is known that
\mbox{\sc fpvd}_{\mathcal{P}}(P)
, the geodesic furthest-point Voronoi diagram of
P

inside
\mathcal{P}
, has complexity
O(n+m)
.
Wang
[
23
]
recently showed that
\mbox{\sc fpvd}_{\mathcal{P}}(P)
can be constructed in optimal
O(m+n\log n)
time,
improving an earlier result of Oh, Barba, and Ahn
[
15
]
.

Since the complexity of
\mbox{\sc fpvd}_{\mathcal{P}}(P)
is
O(n+m)

and point-location queries in the plane can be answered in logarithmic time
with a linear-size data structure, the
approach of answering furthest-neighbor queries using
\mbox{\sc fpvd}_{\mathcal{P}}(P)
is
optimal with respect to storage and query time. This only holds
for
exact
queries, however: in this paper we show that

\varepsilon
-approximate furthest-neighbor queries can be answered
using an amount of storage that is
independent of the size of
P
. More precisely,
we show that there exists a set
C\subset P
of
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
points
such that for any query point
q\in\mathcal{P}
, the geodesic furthest neighbor of
q

among the points in
C
is a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation of its actual furthest neighbor.
Note that the size of the coreset is not only independent of the size of
P
,
but also of the complexity of the polygon
\mathcal{P}
.
We call a set
C
with this property an
\varepsilon
-coreset for furthest-neighbor
queries
. Using such an
\varepsilon
-coreset we can answer
\varepsilon
-approximate
furthest neighbor queries with a data structure—a point-location structure on
the geodesic furthest-point Voronoi diagram of
C
—whose storage
is
O(m+1/\varepsilon^{2})
. Note that a similar result is not possible for

nearest
-neighbor queries, since for any point
p\in P

there is a query point
q
such that
p
is the only valid answer to an
\varepsilon
-approximate
nearest-neighbor query (even when
\varepsilon
is very large).

Related work.

To the best of our knowledge,
\varepsilon
-coresets for furthest-neighbor queries have
not been studied explicitly so far, although the observation by
Goel, Indyk, and Varadarajan
[
10
]
mentioned above
can be restated in terms of coresets: any set
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a
(1/\sqrt{2})
-coreset
of size
d+1
. It is easy to see that any set
P
in a space of
constant doubling dimension
d
has a coreset of size
O(1/\varepsilon^{d})
.
Indeed, if
r
denotes the radius of the smallest enclosing ball of
P
,
then the distance from any query point
q
to it furthest neighbor in
P

is at least
r
. Hence, covering
B
with
O(1/\varepsilon^{d})
balls of radius
\varepsilon\cdot r

and picking an arbitrary point from
P
in each non-empty ball, gives an
\varepsilon
-coreset
for furthest-neighbor queries.

In
\mathbb{R}^{d}
this can be improved using
\varepsilon
-kernels
[
1
]
.
An
\varepsilon
-kernel
of a point set
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subset
C\subset P

that approximates the width of
P
in any direction. More precisely, if
\mathrm{width}(\phi,P)

denotes the width of
P
in direction
\phi
, then we require that

\mathrm{width}(\phi,C)\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{width}(\phi,P)

for any direction
\phi
. It is easily checked that an
(\varepsilon/2)
-kernel
for
P
is, in fact, an
\varepsilon
-coreset for furthest-neighbor queries.
Since there always exists an
\varepsilon
-kernel of size
O((1/\varepsilon)^{(d-1)/2})

[
1
]
,
we immediately obtain an
\varepsilon
-coreset for furthest-neighbor queries in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
the same size.

Overview of our technique.

While obtaining a small coreset for furthest-neighbor queries is easy under the
Euclidean distance, it becomes much more challenging when
P
is
a point set in a simple polygon
\mathcal{P}
and we consider the geodesic distances.
It is known (and easy to see) that
\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)
is always a vertex of
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
,
the
relative convex hull
2
2

2

The relative convex hull
[
22
]
of a set
P
of
points inside a polygon
\mathcal{P}
is the intersection of all relatively convex sets (that is,
all sets
X
such that for any two points
x,y\in X
we have
\pi(x,y)\subset X
) containing
P
;
see Figure
1
(i). Obviously the relative convex hull not only
depends on
P
but also on the polygon
\mathcal{P}
, but to avoid cluttering the notation we
simply write
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
instead of
\mbox{\sc rch}_{\mathcal{P}}(P)
.
of
P
inside
\mathcal{P}
. Thus, one may be tempted
to cover the boundary of
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
by geodesic balls of radius
(\varepsilon/2)\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)
,
where
\mathrm{diam}(P)
denotes the diameter of
P
under the geodesic distance,
and pick a point from
P
in any non-empty ball. This will give an
\varepsilon
-coreset
since the geodesic distance from
q
to
\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)
is at least
\mathrm{diam}(P)/2
.
Unfortunately, the geodesic distance does not have bounded doubling
dimension, and the boundary of
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
can be arbitrarily much
longer than
\mathrm{diam}(P)
, the geodesic diameter of
P
inside
\mathcal{P}
.
To obtain a coreset size that depends only on
\varepsilon
, we must therefore use a completely different approach.

The idea behind our coreset construction is as follows.
Consider two points
p_{1},p_{2}\in P
that define
\mathrm{diam}(P)

and let
\Gamma:=\pi(p_{1},p_{2})
be the shortest path between them.
We will prove that the following property holds for any
query point
q
inside
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
: either there is a point
q^{*}\in\Gamma
with
\mbox{\sc fn}(q^{*},P)=\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)
,
or
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))
crosses
\Gamma
.

Query points of the former type are easy to handle:
we partition
\Gamma
into
O(1/\varepsilon)
pieces
\Gamma_{i}
of length
\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{diam}(P)
,
and we add one furthest neighbor for each piece
\Gamma_{i}
into our coreset.

Query points of the latter type are harder to handle. Let
\overline{q}

denote the point where
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))
crosses
\Gamma
. The difficulty
lies in the fact that
\mbox{\sc fn}(\overline{q},P)
need not be the same as
\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)

and, in fact, the geodesic distance from
q
to
\mbox{\sc fn}(\overline{q},P)
need not even
approximate the geodesic distance from
q
to
\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)
well enough. The key insight
is that we
can
get a good approximation if, when computing the furthest
neighbor of
\overline{q}
, we restrict our attention to points
p\in P
such that
the angle between the first link of
\pi(\overline{q},p)
and
the first link of
\pi(\overline{q},\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))
is at most
\varepsilon
. Thus we will
define
O(1/\varepsilon)
“cones of directions” and, for each
piece
\Gamma_{i}
we will add one “directed furthest neighbor” for each such cone.
The total size of the coreset created in this manner will be
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
.

The above does not immediately work for query points
q
that lie outside
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
,
because for such query points the path
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))
may not cross
\Gamma
.
To overcome this, we show that there exists a set
B
of
O(1)
segments, each of length
at most
2\|\Gamma\|
, such that the following property holds for any query point
q\notin\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
: either
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))
crosses
\Gamma
or
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))
crosses a segment in
B
. We can then split
B
in
O(1/\varepsilon)
pieces and then use the “cone-approach” for each piece.

2
A coreset of furthest-neighbor queries in a simple polygon

In this section we will prove our main theorem, which is as follows.

Theorem 1
.

For any
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-coreset
C\subset P
of size
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})

for the furthest-neighbor problem, that is, a set
C

such that for any query point
q\in\mathcal{P}
we have that
\|\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,C))\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\|\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P))\|
.
The coreset
C
can be constructed in
O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+(n+m)\log(n+m)\right)\right)
time.

For now on, we will use
\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
as a shorthand for
\mbox{\sc fn}(q,P)
, the actual nearest neighbor of
q
.
We will first prove the existence of an
\varepsilon
-coreset for query points that lie inside
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
,
then extend the result to query points outside
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
, and then present an efficient
algorithm to compute the coreset. Before we start we need to introduce some notation and terminology,

by

the geodesic triangle with vertices
p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}

p
denote the

\pi(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
and
\pi(p^{\prime},r^{\prime})

\pi(q,r)

\pi(r^{\prime},p^{\prime})
and

Note that
\triangle_{\pi}{pqr}

We will need the following property, illustrated in Figure

Observation 2
.

Let
\triangle_{\pi}{pqr}
be a pseudo-triangle. Then for all
u\in\triangle_{\pi}{pqr}
such that
u\notin\pi(q,r)
we have that the forward extension of
\mathrm{last}(\pi(p,u))
will intersect
\pi(q,r)
.

Now consider a path
\pi
. Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
be the ordered sequence edges of
\pi
, and let
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}

be their directions. We define the
span
of
\pi
as

\mathrm{span}(\pi):=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{S}^{1}[\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1}]
.
In other words,
\mathrm{span}(\pi)
contains the directions into which we move
while traversing
\pi
, where the “directions over which we turn” at each vertex are included. By definition,
Span(\pi)
is connected, since any two of its consecutive arcs on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
share an endpoint.
The following lemma provides important properties about the sets of directions
that occur in a pseudo-triangle. It is probably known, but since we could not find a reference
we provide a proof in the appendix.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\triangle_{\pi}{pqr}
be a pseudo-triangle. Then

(i)

the sets
\mathrm{span}(\pi(p,q)),\mathrm{span}(\pi(q,r)),\mathrm{span}(\pi(r,p))
are pairwise disjoint and their union contains no antipodal directions.

(ii)

for any
\phi_{1}\in\mathrm{span}(\pi(p,q))
and
\phi_{2}\in\mathrm{span}(\pi(q,r))
, we have
\mathrm{span}(\pi(p,r))\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}[\phi_{1},\phi_{2}]
.

Finally, we need the following observation about furthest neighbors. It states that
we extend the first edge of
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
backwards, and then move
q
along this extension,
then its furthest neighbor does not change.

Observation 4
.

For any
q^{*}\in\overleftarrow{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
we have
\mbox{\sc fn}(q^{*})=\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
.

Proof.

Intuitively, this is true because when we move
q
to
q^{*}
along
\overleftarrow{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q)))
, its distance to
\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
experiences the maximal possible increase (which is the full length of
\overline{q^{*}q}
). Formally, the observation follows if we can show that
\pi(q^{*},\mbox{\sc fn}(q))=\overline{q^{*}q}\cup\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
, since then
\|\pi(q^{*},p)\|\leqslant\|\overline{q^{*}q}\|+\|\pi(q,p)\|\leqslant\|\overline{q^{*}q}\|+\|\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))\|=\|\pi(q^{*},\mbox{\sc fn}(q)\|
for all
p\in P
. It is known that any path that lies in
\mathcal{P}
is shortest if and only if all its internal vertices bend around reflex vertices of
\mathcal{P}

[
3
, Lemma 1]
. Since the paths
\overline{q^{*}q}\cup\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
and
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
have the same set of internal vertices, this concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the lemma that forms the basis of our coreset construction.

Lemma 5
.

Let
q\in\mathcal{P}
be a query point. Then either
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
intersects
\Gamma^{*}
or there is a point
q^{*}\in\Gamma
with
\mbox{\sc fn}(q^{*})=\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
. Moreover, if
q\notin\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
then

\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
always intersects
\Gamma^{*}
.

Proof.

If
q\in\Gamma
, then clearly the lemma holds, so we can assume that

. We now will assume that

does not intersect
\Gamma^{*}
and show that then there is a point

with
\mbox{\sc fn}(q^{*})=\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
. For the rest of the proof, let
p=\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
and
assume without loss of generality that
q
lies in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
.

Since
\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
does not intersect
\Gamma^{*}
, we have
p\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
. Let
u
be the point where
\pi(p,p_{1})
and
\pi(p,p_{2})
split, let

be the point where
\pi(p_{1},p_{2})
and
\pi(p_{1},p)
split and

the point where
\pi(p_{2},p_{1})
and
\pi(p_{2},p)
split. Note that

and also that the angle formed between
\mathrm{first}(\pi(p,p_{1})),\mathrm{first}(\pi(p,p_{2}))
within the geodesic triangle
\triangle_{\pi}{pp_{1}p_{2}}
is convex. Now consider
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{ext}}\left(\pi(p_{1},p)\right)
and
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{ext}}\left(\pi(p_{2},p)\right)
and let
\ell_{1}=\overrightarrow{\mathrm{ext}}\left(\pi(p_{1},p)\right)\setminus\pi(p_{1},p)
and
\ell_{2}=\overrightarrow{\mathrm{ext}}(\pi(p_{2},p))\setminus\pi(p_{2},p)
. Also, let
p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}
denote the endpoints of
\Gamma^{*}
. We partition
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
into at most four disjoint non-empty regions, as illustrated in Figure

.

is empty, therefore it suffices to consider the case

. Then we have the following subcases:

•

Case I:
q\in R_{2}
.
Notice that

and

does not belong to

. Therefore by Lemma
2
, if
\pi(q,p)
is extended to the direction of

, it will intersect

at some point

. By Observation
4
,
this point

has the desired property.

•

Case II:

. Assume without loss of generality that

; the case where

is symmetrical. Now

has to intersect

. The reason is that

is bounded by parts of shortest paths that have

as an endpoint. Assume that
\pi(q,p)
crosses
\pi(p,p_{2})
at point

. We have that

\|\pi(q,p)\|>\|\pi(q,p_{1})\|

and we may assume that
\|\pi(q,p)\|\neq\|\pi(q,p_{1})\|

. Also

\|\pi(p_{1},p_{2})\|\geqslant\|\pi(p,p_{2})\|

\|\pi(p_{1},p_{2})\|=\mathrm{diam}(P)

Adding the above inequalities and using that

lies on the paths
\pi(q,p_{1})
and
\pi(p,p_{2})
we get

\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\pi(q,p)\|+\|\pi(p_{1},p_{2})\|&>&\|\pi(q,r)\|+\|\pi(r,p_{1})\|+\|\pi(p,r)\|+\|\pi(r,p_{2})\|\\

p\in R_{4}

.
∎

The coreset construction and its analysis

\Gamma_{i}

Proof of Theorem
1
when the query point lies inside
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
q\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
.
For brevity, we define
p:=\mbox{\sc fn}(q)

to be the furthest neighbor
q
. The following lemma will used repeatedly in our proof.

Lemma 6
.

Let
p^{*}\in C
be such that
\pi(q,p^{*})
intersects
\Gamma_{i}
,
and suppose
\|\pi(\gamma_{i},p^{*})\|\geqslant\|\pi(\gamma_{i},p)\|
.
Then
\|\pi(q,p^{*})\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\|\pi(q,p)\|
. Hence,
p^{*}
is a good approximation for
\mbox{\sc fn}(q)
.

Proof.

Let
z
be a point where
\pi(q,p^{*})
intersects
\Gamma_{i}
; see Figure
5
.
Then we have

\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\pi(q,p)\|&\leqslant&\|\pi(q,z)\|+\|\pi(z,\gamma_{i})\|+\|\pi(\gamma_{i},p)\|\hfill\mbox{(Triangle Inequality)}\\
&\leqslant&\|\pi(q,z)\|+\|\pi(z,\gamma_{i})\|+\|\pi(\gamma_{i},p^{*})\|\hfill\mbox{(by assumption)}\\
&\leqslant&2\|\pi(z,\gamma_{i})\|+\|\pi(q,z)\|+\|\pi(z,p^{*})\|\hfill\mbox{(Triangle Inequality)}\\
&\leqslant&2\|\Gamma_{i}\|+\|\pi(q,p^{*})\|\hfill\mbox{(since $\|\pi(z,\gamma_{i})\|\leqslant\|\Gamma_{i}\|$ and $z\in\pi(q,p^{*})$)}\\
&<&\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\|\Gamma\|+\|\pi(q,p^{*})\|\hfill\mbox{(since $\|\Gamma_{i}\|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{6}\cdot\|\Gamma\|$})\\
&\leqslant&\varepsilon\cdot\|\pi(q,p)\|+\|\pi(q,p^{*})\|\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{(since $\|\Gamma\|\leqslant 2\|\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))\|$ for any $q\in\mathcal{P}$)}\end{array}

and so indeed
\|\pi(q,p^{*})\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\|\pi(q,p)\|
.
∎

:

6
.

If
p\in\{p_{1},p_{2}\}

1
trivially holds since
\{p_{1},p_{2}\}\subset C
.
So now we assume that
p\notin\{p_{1},p_{2}\}

gives us that
either
\pi(q,p)
crosses
\Gamma^{*}
or the backwards extension of
\pi(q,p)

\Gamma

\pi(q,\mbox{\sc fn}(q))
actually intersects
\Gamma
,
since

and shortest paths between points inside
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
do not leave
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
.
We first consider the latter case, where the backwards extension of
\pi(q,p)
intersects
\Gamma
.

Lemma 7
.

If the backwards extension of
\pi(q,p)
intersects
\Gamma
, then there is a point
p^{*}\in C
such that
\|\pi(q,p^{*})\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\|\pi(q,p)\|
.

Proof.

Let
x
be the point where the backwards extension of
\pi(q,p)
hits
\Gamma
and let
\Gamma_{i}
be the piece containing
x
.
Let

r_{i}^{*}\in C
be the point reachable from
\Gamma_{i}
which is furthest from
\gamma_{i}
over all points
p\in P\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})

and that was put into the coreset
C
. Thus
the definition of
r_{i}
implies
\|\pi(\gamma_{i},r^{*}_{i})\|\geqslant\|\pi(\gamma_{i},p)\|
.
We now have

\begin{array}[]{lll}\|\pi(q,p)\|&=&\|\pi(x,p)\|-\|\pi(q,x)\|\hfill\mbox{(since $x$ is on the backwards extension)}\\
&\leqslant&\|\pi(x,\gamma_{i})\|+\|\pi(\gamma_{i},p)\|-\|\pi(q,x)\|\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{(Triangle Inequality)}\\
&\leqslant&\|\pi(x,\gamma_{i})\|+\|\pi(\gamma_{i},r^{*}_{i})\|-\|\pi(q,x)\|\hfill\mbox{(definition of~$r^{*}_{i}$)}\\
&\leqslant&2\|\pi(x,\gamma_{i})\|+\|\pi(x,r^{*}_{i})\|-\|\pi(x,q)\|\hfill\mbox{(Triangle Inequality)}\\
&\leqslant&2\|\Gamma_{i}\|+\|\pi(x,r^{*}_{i})\|-\|\pi(x,q)\|\hfill\mbox{(since $x\in\Gamma_{i}$ and $\gamma_{i}\in\Gamma_{i}$)}\\
&\leqslant&2\|\Gamma_{i}\|+\|\pi(q,r^{*}_{i})\|\hfill\mbox{(Triangle Inequality)}\\
&<&\varepsilon\|\pi(q,p)\|+\|\pi(q,r^{*}_{i})\|\hfill\mbox{(since $\|\Gamma_{i}\|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{6}\|\Gamma\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\pi(q,p)\|$)}\end{array}

and so
\|\pi(q,r^{*}_{i})\|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\|\pi(q,p)\|
. By taking

An efficient algorithm to construct the coreset

In this section we explain how to construct the coreset
C
defined in the
previous section. We will show that we can do that quite efficiently, namely in time

O\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(n\log(1/\varepsilon)+(n+m)\log(n+m)\right)\right)
.

Preprocessing.

Our approach crucially uses the diameter of
P
, so we start by computing
\Gamma
.
This can be done in
O((m+n)\log(m+n)
by first computing
\mbox{\sc rch}(P)
in
O(m+n\log(m+n))

time
[
22
]
and then computing its diameter in
O((m+n)\log(m+n))
time
[
21
]
.
We then preprocess the subdivision of
\mathcal{P}
induced by
\Gamma
for point
location
[
20
]
, so that we can compute for each point
in
P
whether it lies in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
or
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
, in
O(m+n\log m)
time in total.

We now show how to compute the set
C_{i}
of points we put into our coreset
C

for a given piece
\Gamma_{i}
. We start by preprocessing
\mathcal{P}
for single-source shortest-path queries
with
\gamma_{i}
as the fixed source. This takes
O(m)
time, after which we can compute the
distance from each point in
P
to
\gamma_{i}
in
O(\log m)
time
[
11
]
.
Thus computing all distances takes
O(n\log m)
time in total.
Recall that
C_{i}
contains the point furthest away from
\Gamma_{j-1}
from each of the following sets.

•

The sets
P\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
and
P\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
.

•

The sets
P\cap E_{1}
and
P\cap E_{2}
,
where
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are the two half-polygons into which
\mathcal{P}
is split by the intermediate edge

of
\Gamma_{i}
, if such an edge exists.

•

The sets
X_{i}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
and
X_{i}\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
, where
X_{i}\subset P
contains the
points that lie in a pocket of
\Gamma_{i}
.

•

The sets
F_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
and
L_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
, and the set
F_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
and
L_{i,j}\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Gamma^{*})
,
where
F_{i,j}
contains the points reachable from
\mathrm{first}(\Gamma_{i})
in the cone
\Phi_{j}
, and

L_{i,j}
contains the points reachable from
\mathrm{last}(\Gamma_{i})
in the cone
\Phi_{j}
.

The sets
P\cap E_{1}
,
P\cap E_{2}
,
X_{i}\cap\mathcal{P}_{1}(\Gamma^{*})
,

can each be determined
by performing a point-location query for each point

in a suitably defined subdivision
of complexity

. This can be done in
O((m+n)\log{m})
time in total
[
20
]
.
Since we precomputed all distances to
\gamma_{i}

from each of these sets. So far we spent

.

Efficiently determining the furthest neighbors in the sets

over the canonical cone directions, seen as sub-intervals

4
Concluding remarks

We proved that any set
P
of
n
points in a simple polygon
\mathcal{P}
of complexity
m

admits an
\varepsilon
-coreset of size
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
for furthest-neighbor queries.
Thus the size of the coreset is independent of
n
and
m
. This immediately
gives a data structure for approximate furthest-neighbor queries whose size is independent
of
n
. The size of the data structure will be linear in
m
,
but this is unavoidable: it is easy to see that for
n=2
we already
have an
\Omega(m)
lower bound on the storage of any data structure for
approximate furthest-neighbor queries.

Another application, which we did not mention so far, is the
1
-center
problem with
z
outliers in a simple polygon, for which we can now obtain an

\varepsilon
-coreset of size
O(z/\varepsilon^{2})
. To this end we use the
technique of Agarwal, Har-Peled, and Yu
[
2
]
:
compute an
\varepsilon
-coreset
C
for furthest-neighbor queries on
P
,
remove
C
from
P
, compute an
\varepsilon
-coreset for furthest-neighbor queries
on the remaining points and add these to the first coreset, and so on. If this is repeated
z+1
times, the result is an
\varepsilon
-coreset for the 1-center problem with
z
outliers.

There are several directions for further research. First of all, we do not expect our
bound to be tight: we believe that an
\varepsilon
-coreset of size
O(1/\varepsilon)
, or perhaps even
O(\sqrt{1/\varepsilon})
, should exist. One approach to reduce the size of the coreset is
to prove this may be to show that
in our method we can re-use many points, that is, that the
O(1/\varepsilon)
subsets
C_{i}

that we define for each of the
O(1/\varepsilon)
pieces
\Gamma_{i}
will have (or can be made to have)
many points in common.
Another challenging open problem is to extend our
result to polygons with holes. This seems to require a different approach, as
our reduction to points on the diameter fails in this setting.

\pi(p,r)
