A formula for the linking number in terms of isometry invariants of straight line segments
Matt Bright, Olga Anosova, Vitaliy Kurlin Computer Science and Materials Innovation Factory, University of Liverpool sgmbrigh@liverpool.ac.uk, oanosova@liv.ac.uk, vitaliy.kurlin@gmail.com

Abstract
In 1833 Gauss defined the linking number of two disjoint curves in 3-space. For open curves this double integral over the parameterised curves is real-valued and invariant modulo rigid motions or isometries that preserve distances between points, and has been recently used in the elucidation of molecular structures. In 1976 Banchoff geometrically interpreted the linking number between two line segments. An explicit analytic formula based on this interpretation was given in 2000 without proof in terms of six isometry invariants: the distance and angle between the segments and four coordinates specifying their relative positions. We give a detailed proof of this formula and describe its asymptotic behaviour that wasn’t previously studied.

The Gauss integral for the linking number of curves

This extended version of the conference paper
bright2020proof
includes all previously skipped proofs.
For any vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the
triple
product is
(\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w})=(\vec{u}\times\vec{v})\cdot\vec{w}
.

Definition 1

(Gauss integral for the linking number)
.

For piecewise-smooth curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the
linking number
can be defined as the Gauss integral
gauss1833integral

\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\dfrac{(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s),\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s))}{|\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)|^{3}}dtds,

[formula]

where
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s)
are the vector derivatives of the 1-variable functions
\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(s)
.

The formula in
1
gives an integer number for any closed disjoint curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
due to its interpretation as the
degree
of the
Gauss map

\Gamma(t,s)=\dfrac{\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)}{|\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)|}:S^{1}\times S^{1}\to S^{2}
, i.e.

\deg\Gamma=\dfrac{\mathrm{area}(\Gamma(S^{1}\times S^{1}))}{\mathrm{area}(S^{2})}
, where the area of the unit sphere is
\mathrm{area}(S^{2})=4\pi
.
This integer degree is the
linking number
of the 2-component link
\gamma_{1}\sqcup\gamma_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
formed by the two closed curves.
Invariance modulo continuous deformation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
follows easily for closed curves - indeed, the function under the Gauss integral in (
1
), and hence the integral itself, varies continuously under perturbations of the curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
. This should keep any integer value constant.

For open curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, the Gauss integral gives a real but not necessarily integral value, which remains invariant under rigid motions or orientation-preserving isometries (see
2
).
In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the Euclidean metric isometries consist of rotations, translations and reflections.
Isometry invariance of the real-valued linking number for open curves has found applications in the study of molecules
ahmad2020characterization
.

Any smooth curve can be well-approximated by a polygonal line, so the computation of the linking number reduces to a sum over pairs of straight line segments
L_{1},L_{2}
.
In 1976 Banchoff
banchoff1976self
has expressed the linking number
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
in terms of the endpoints of each segment, see details of this and other past work in
Section

3
.

In 2000 Klenin and Langowski
klenin2000computation
proposed a formula for the linking number
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
of two straight line segments in terms of six isometry invariants of
L_{1},L_{2}
, referring to a previous paper
Vologodskii1974
, which used the formula without any detailed proof.
The paper
klenin2000computation
also skipped all details of the invariant-based formula’s derivation.

The usefulness of the invariant-based formula can be seen by considering the analogy with the simpler concept of the scalar (dot) product of vectors.
The algebraic or
coordinate-based
formula expresses the scalar product of two vectors
\vec{u}=(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})
and
\vec{v}=(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2})
as
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}=x_{1}x_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}+z_{1}z_{2}
, which in turn depend on the co-ordinates of their endpoints. However, the scalar product for high-dimensional vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
can also expressed in terms of only 3 parameters
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}=|\vec{u}|\cdot|\vec{v}|\cos\angle(\vec{u},\vec{v})
.
The two lengths
|\vec{u}|,|\vec{v}|
and the angle
\angle(\vec{u},\vec{v})
are isometry invariants of the vectors
\vec{u},\vec{v}
.
This second geometric or
invariant-based
formula makes it clear that
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}
is an isometry invariant, while it is harder to show that
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}=x_{1}x_{2}+y_{1}y_{2}+z_{1}z_{2}
is invariant under rotations. It also provides other geometric insights that are hard to extract from the coordinate-based formula - for example,
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}
oscillates as a cosine wave when the lengths
|\vec{u}|,|\vec{v}|
are fixed, but the angle
\angle(\vec{u},\vec{v})
is varying.

In this paper, we provide a detailed proof of the invariant-based formula for the linking number in
8
and new corollaries in
Section

6
formally investigating the asymptotic behaviour of the linking number, which wasn’t previously studied.

Our own interest in the asymptotic behaviour is motivated by the
periodic linking number
by Panagiotou
panagiotou2015linking
as an invariant of crystalline networks
cui2019mining
that are infinitely periodic in three directions, by calculating the infinite sum of the linking number between one line segment and all translated copies of another such segment. In
panagiotou2015linking
there is a complex proof that this sum is convergent for a cubical lattice.
The convergence of the periodic linking numbers remains open for arbitrary lattices.

Outline of the invariant-based formula and consequences

Folklore Theorem
2
lists key properties of
\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
, which will be used later.

Theorem 2

(properties of the linking number)
.

The linking number defined by the Gauss integral in
1
for smooth curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
has the following properties:

(
2
a)
the linking number is symmetric:
\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{2},\gamma_{1})
;

(
2
b)

\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=0
for any curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
that belong to the same plane;

(
2
c)

\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is independent of orientation-preserving parameterisations of the open curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
with fixed endpoints;

(
2
d)

\mathrm{lk}(-\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=-\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
, where
-\gamma_{1}
has the reversed orientation of
\gamma_{1}
;

(
2
e)
the linking number
\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is invariant under any scaling
\vec{v}\to\lambda\vec{v}
for
\lambda>0
;

(
2
f)

\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is multiplied by
\det M
under any orthogonal map
\vec{v}\mapsto M\vec{v}
.

Proof.

(
2
a)
We note that the Euclidean distance is symmetric, and that since the triple product is anti-symmetric and
\gamma_{2}(s)-\gamma_{1}(t)=-(\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)
, the symmetry follows from

\displaystyle(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(t),\gamma_{2}(s)-\gamma_{2}(t))

\displaystyle=-(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s),\gamma_{2}(s)-\gamma_{1}(t))

\displaystyle=-(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s),-(\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)))

\displaystyle=(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s),\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)).

(
2
b) is obvious from the coplanarity of the normal vectors.

(
2
c) is simply a consequence of the path-independence of the integrals over
s,t
.

(
2
d) follows from
\dot{\gamma}_{1}(-t)=-\dot{\gamma}(t)
since the reverse orientation of
\gamma_{1}(t)
is
\gamma_{1}(-t)
.

(
2
e)
Any scaling
\vec{v}\mapsto\lambda\vec{v}
will result in a change of parameterisation
\gamma_{i}(t)\mapsto\gamma_{i}(\lambda t)=\lambda(\gamma_{I})(t)
.
Since
\dot{\lambda\gamma}_{i}(t)=\lambda\dot{\gamma}_{i}(t)
, the result follows below

\displaystyle\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1}(\lambda t),\gamma_{2}(\lambda s))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\dfrac{(\lambda\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\lambda\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s),\lambda(\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)))}{|\lambda(\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s))|^{3}}dtds

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\lambda^{3}(\dot{\gamma}_{1}(t),\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s),\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s))}{\lambda^{3}|\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}(s)|^{3}}dtds

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(s)),

(
2
f)
For an orthogonal transformation
M
, we have
M\vec{u}\times M\vec{v}=(\det M)M(\vec{u}\times\vec{v})
, while
M\vec{u}\cdot M\vec{v}=\vec{u}\cdot\vec{v}
.
Therefore
|M\vec{v}-M\vec{u}|=|\vec{v}-\vec{u}|
,
(M\vec{u},M\vec{v},M\vec{w})=\det M(\vec{u},\vec{v},\vec{w})

and
\mathrm{lk}(M\gamma_{1},M\gamma_{2})=(\det{M})(\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}))
, so the linking number is multiplied by
\det{M}
.

\blacksquare
∎

Corollary 3

(linking number for simple orthogonal segments)
.

For any simple orthogonal oriented line segments
L_{1},L_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with lengths
l_{1},l_{2}
and a distance
d
as defined above, the linking number is

\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\arctan\left(\dfrac{l_{1}l_{2}}{d\sqrt{l_{1}^{2}+l_{2}^{2}+d^{2}}}\right)
.

The above expression is a special case of general formula (
8

a_{1}=a_{2}=0
and
\alpha=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
.
If
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
, the linking number in
3
becomes
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\arctan\dfrac{l^{2}}{d\sqrt{2l^{2}+d^{2}}}
.
If
l_{1}=l_{2}=d
, then
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\arctan\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}=-\dfrac{1}{24}
.

3
implies that the linking number is in the range
(-\frac{1}{8},0)
for any simple orthogonal segments with

, which wasn’t obvious from
1
. If
L_{1},L_{2}
move away from each other, then

\lim\limits_{d\to+\infty}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\arctan 0=0

Alternatively, if segments with
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
become infinitely short, the limit is again zero:
\lim\limits_{l\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0
for any fixed
d
.
The limit
\lim\limits_{x\to+\infty}\arctan x=\dfrac{\pi}{2}
implies that if segments with
l_{1}=l_{2}=l
become infinitely long for a fixed distance
d
,
\lim\limits_{l\to+\infty}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\arctan\dfrac{l^{2}}{d\sqrt{2l^{2}+d^{2}}}=-\dfrac{1}{8}
.
If we push segments
L_{1},L_{2}
of fixed (possibly different) lengths
l_{1},l_{2}
towards each other, the same limit similarly emerges:

\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{8}
.

.

3
Past results about the Gauss integral for the linking number

The survey
ricca2011gauss
reviews the history of the Gauss integral, its use in Maxwell’s description of electromagnetic fields
maxwell1873treatise
, and its interpretation as the degree of a map from the torus to the sphere.
In classical knot theory
\mathrm{lk}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
is a topological invariant of a link consisting of closed curves
\gamma_{1}\sqcup\gamma_{2}
, whose equivalence relation is ambient isotopy. This relation is too flexible for open curves which can be isotopically unwound, and hence doesn’t preserve the Gauss integral for open curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
.

Computing the value of the Gauss integral directly from the parametric equation of two generic curves is only possible by approximation, but this problem is simplified when we consider simply straight lines.
The first form of the linking number between two straight line segments in terms of their geometry is described by Banchoff
banchoff1976self
. Banchoff considers the projection of segments on to a plane orthogonal to some vector
\xi\in S^{2}
. The Gauss integral is interpreted as the fraction of the unit sphere covered by those directions of
\xi
for which the projection will have a crossing.

This interpretation was the foundation of a closed form developed by Arai
arai2013rigorous
, using van Oosterom and Strackee’s closed formula for the solid angle subtended by a tetrahedron given by the origin of a sphere and three points on its surface.
An efficient implementation of the solid angle approach to the linking number is discussed in
bertolazzi2019efficient
.

An alternative calculation for this solid angle is given in
panagiotou2020knot
as a starting point for calculating further invariants of open entangled curves.
This form does not employ geometric invariants, but was used in
klenin2000computation
to claim a formula (without a proof) similar to
8
, which is proved in this paper with more corollaries in section
6
.

Six isometry invariants of skew line segments in 3-space

This section introduces six isometry invariants, which uniquely determine positions of any line segments
L_{1},L_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

It suffices to consider only

If

In a plane with fixed coordinates

-axis.

Lemma 6

=\dfrac{\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{R}}\cdot\dfrac{(r-p\cos\alpha)(1+r^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha)}{\sin^{2}\alpha(1+p^{2}+r^{2}-2pr\cos\alpha)+(p^{2}r^{2}\sin^{4}\alpha+2pr\sin^{2}\alpha\cos\alpha+\cos^{2}\alpha)}=

=\dfrac{\sin\alpha(r-p\cos\alpha)}{(1+p^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha)\sqrt{R}}=\dfrac{\sin\alpha(r-p\cos\alpha)}{(1+p^{2}\sin^{2}\alpha)\sqrt{1+p^{2}+q^{2}-2pq\cos\alpha}}.

Since we got the required expression under the integral
I(r)
,
11
is proved.

\blacksquare
∎

8
.

Consider the right hand side of the equation in
11
as the 3-variable function

F(p,r;\alpha)=\arctan\left(\dfrac{pr\sin^{2}\alpha+\cos\alpha}{\sqrt{1+p^{2}+r^{2}-2pr\cos\alpha}}\right)

The function in

is

I(r)=F(b_{1}/d,r;\alpha)-F(a_{1}/d,r;\alpha)
.
By
10

\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=

\dfrac{\Big(F(b_{1}/d,a_{2}/d;\alpha)-F(a_{1}/d,a_{2}/d;\alpha)\Big)-\Big(F(b_{1}/d,b_{2}/d;\alpha)-F(a_{1}/d,b_{2}/d;\alpha)\Big)}{4\pi}.

Rewrite a typical function from the numerator above as follows:

F\left(a/d,b/d;\alpha\right)=

\arctan\dfrac{(ab/d^{2})\sin^{2}\alpha+\cos\alpha}{\sqrt{1+(a/d)^{2}+(b/d)^{2}-2(ab/d^{2})\cos\alpha}}=\arctan\dfrac{ab\sin\alpha+d^{2}\cot\alpha}{d\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos\alpha+d^{2}}}.

If we denote the last expression as
\mathrm{AT}(a,b;d,\alpha)
, required formula (
8
) follows.

In
9
,
10
and above we have used that the signed distance
d
is positive.
By
7
the signed distance
d
and
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
simultaneously change their signs under a central symmetry, while all other invariants remain the same.
Since
\mathrm{AT}(a,b;-d,\alpha)=-\mathrm{AT}(a,b;d,\alpha)
due to the arcsin function being odd, formula (
8
) holds for
d<0
.
The formula remains valid even for
d=0
, when
L_{1},L_{2}
are in the same plane.
The expected value
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0
needs an explicit setting, see the discussion of the linking number discontinuity around

\blacksquare

Corollary 12

(bounds of the linking number)
.

For any line segments
L_{1},L_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the linking number
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
is between
\pm\dfrac{1}{2}
.

12
.

By
8

\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
is a sum of 4 arctan functions divided by
4\pi
.
Since each arctan is strictly between
\pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}
, the linking number is between
\pm\dfrac{1}{2}
.

\blacksquare
∎

Corollary 13

(sign of the linking number)
.

In the notation of
4
, we have

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0=\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\pi}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
.
Any non-parallel
L_{1},L_{2}
have
\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2}))=-\mathrm{sign}(d)
.
So
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0
if and only if
d=0
or
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=\pi
.

13
.

If
\alpha=0
or
\alpha=\pi
, then
\cot\alpha
is undefined, so
8
sets
\mathrm{AT}(a,b;d,\alpha)=\mathrm{sign}(d)\dfrac{\pi}{2}
.
Then
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=\mathrm{sign}(d)\dfrac{\pi}{2}(1+1-1-1)=0
.

8
also specifies that
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0
for
d=0
.
If
d\neq 0
and
\alpha\to 0
within
[0,\pi]
while all other parameters remain fixed, then
d^{2}\cot\alpha\to+\infty
.
Hence each of the 4 arctan functions in
8
approaches
\dfrac{\pi}{2}
, so
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})\to 0
.
The same conclusion similarly follows in the case
\alpha\to\pi
when
d^{2}\cot\alpha\to-\infty
.

If
L_{1},L_{2}
are not parallel, the angle
\alpha
between them belongs to
(0,\pi)
.
In
d>0
,
9
says that

\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{a_{1}/d}^{b_{1}/d}\;\int\limits_{a_{2}/d}^{b_{2}/d}\dfrac{\sin\alpha\;dp\;dq}{(1+p^{2}+q^{2}-2pq\cos\alpha)^{3/2}}
.
Since the function under the integral is strictly positive,
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})<0
.
By
7
both
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})
simultaneously change their signs under a central symmetry.
Hence the formula
\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2}))=-\mathrm{sign}(d)
holds for all

including
d=0
above.

\blacksquare
∎

Corollary 14

(lk for
d\to 0
)
.

If
d\to 0
and
L_{1},L_{2}
remain disjoint, formula (
8
) is continuous and
\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0
.
If
d\to 0
and
L_{1},L_{2}
intersect each other in the limit case
d=0
, then
\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=-\dfrac{\mathrm{sign}(d)}{2}
, where
d\to 0
keeps its sign.

14
.

Recall that
\lim\limits_{x\to\pm\infty}\arctan x=\pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}
.
By
13
assume that
\alpha\neq 0,\alpha\neq\pi
, so
\alpha\in(0,\pi)
.
Then
\sin\alpha>0
,
a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos\alpha>(a-b)^{2}\geq 0
and

\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{AT}(a,b;d,\alpha)=\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\arctan\left(\dfrac{ab\sin\alpha+d^{2}\cot\alpha}{d\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos\alpha+d^{2}}}\right)=

=\mathrm{sign}(a)\mathrm{sign}(b)\mathrm{sign}(d)\dfrac{\pi}{2},\text{so \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{theorem:lk_arctan} gives }

\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=\dfrac{\mathrm{sign}(d)}{8}(\mathrm{sign}(a_{1})-\mathrm{sign}(b_{1}))(\mathrm{sign}(b_{2})-\mathrm{sign}(a_{2})).

In the limit case
d=0
, the line segments
L_{1},L_{2}\subset\{z=0\}
remain disjoint in the same plane if and only if both endpoint coordinates
a_{i},b_{i}
have the same sign for at least one of
i=1,2
, which is equivalent to
\mathrm{sign}(a_{i})-\mathrm{sign}(b_{i})=0
, i.e.
\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=0
from the product above.
Hence formula (
8

Any segments that intersect in the plane

have endpoint coordinates

\lim\limits_{d\to 0}\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})=\dfrac{\mathrm{sign}(d)}{8}(-1-1)(1-(-1))=-\dfrac{\mathrm{sign}(d)}{2}
as required.

\blacksquare
∎

Corollary 15

(lk for
d\to\pm\infty
)
.

If the distance

, then
\mathrm{lk}(L_{1},L_{2})\to 0

Corollary 16

(lk for

.

If

The asymptotic linking number introduced by Arnold

, while

The invariant-based formula has allowed us to prove new asymptotic results of the linking number in Corollaries

17

Since the periodic linking number is a real-valued invariant modulo isometries, it can be used to continuously quantify similarities between periodic crystalline networks

One next possible step is to use formula (
8

The Milnor invariants generalise the linking number to invariants of links with more than two components.
An integral for the three component Milnor invariant

The interesting open problem is to extend the isometry-based approach to finer invariants of knots.

The Gauss integral in (

The underlying metabelian formula for

In conclusion, we have proved the analytic formula for the linking number based on 6 isometry invariants that uniquely determine a relative position of two line segments in
