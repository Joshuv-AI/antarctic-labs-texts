Sharp time decay estimates for the discrete Klein-Gordon equation
Jean-Claude Cuenin, Isroil A. Ikromov

Abstract
. We establish sharp time decay estimates for the the Klein-Gordon equation on the cubic lattice in dimensions d = 2 , 3 , 4 d=2,3,4 . The ℓ 1 → ℓ ∞ \ell^{1}\to\ell^{\infty} dispersive decay rate is | t | − 3 / 4 |t|^{-3/4} for d = 2 d=2 , | t | − 7 / 6 |t|^{-7/6} for d = 3 d=3 and | t | − 3 / 2 log | t | |t|^{-3/2}\log|t| for d = 4 d=4 . These decay rates are faster than conjectured by Kevrekidis and Stefanov (2005). The proof relies on oscillatory integral estimates and proceeds by a detailed analysis of the the singularities of the associated phase function. We also prove new Strichartz estimates and discuss applications to nonlinear PDEs and spectral theory.

1.
Introduction and main results

Dispersive estimates play a crucial role in the study of evolution equations. Proving such estimates often boils down to establishing decay estimates for the
\ell^{\infty}
norm of the solution at time
t
in terms of the
\ell^{1}
norm of its initial data. It is by now well-established that the
\ell^{1}\to\ell^{\infty}
decay estimates give rise to a whole family of mixed space-time norm estimates, called Strichartz estimates
[
29
,
12
,
18
]
. For the continuous Klein-Gordon equation such estimates have been established e.g. by Brenner
[
6
]
, Pecher
[
26
]
and Ginibre and Velo
[
10
,
11
]
; let us also mention the textbook exposition by Nakanishi–Schlag
[
24
]
. The dispersive estimates are halfway between those for the Schrödinger equation (for low frequencies) and the wave equation (for high frequencies), see
[
24
, 2.5]
.
In the discrete case the frequencies are bounded, and one might expect the same decay rate for the discrete Klein-Gordon equation (DKG) as for the discrete Schrödinger equation (DS). In fact, this was conjectured by Kevrekidis and Stefanov
[
19
]
, who proved the sharp
|t|^{-d/3}
decay rate for the DS in any dimension
d\geq 1
and for the DKG in one dimension. The obstruction to proving similar estimates for DKG in higher dimensions was that, contrary to the DS, the fundamental solution does not separate variables. Apparently unbeknownst to the authors, earlier, Schultz
[
27
]
had already made the striking observation that the decay rate for the discrete wave equation (to which the DKG reduces in the zero mass limit) in dimensions
d=2,3
is
|t|^{-3/4}
and
|t|^{-7/6}
, respectively, better than the conjectured estimates. The same decay rate for the
d=2
DKG was established by Borovyk and Goldberg
[
4
]
, who also proved that the fundamental solution decays exponentially outside the light cone and that the decay rate is independent of additional parameters (mass and wave speeds in the coordinate directions).

1.1.
Discrete Klein–Gordon equation (DKG)

Consider the Cauchy problem for the DKG:

\begin{cases}&u_{tt}(t,x)-\Delta_{x}u(t,x)+u(t,x)=F(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d},\\
&u(0,x)=f(x),\quad u_{t}(0,x)=g(x),\end{cases}

(1)

where
\Delta_{x}
is the discrete Laplacian,

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}u(t,x)=\sum_{|x-y|=1}u(y,t)-2du(t,x).

For the plane waves
e_{\xi}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}
we have
(1-\Delta_{x})e_{\xi}(x)=\omega(\xi)^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}}e_{\xi}(x)
, where
\omega:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the dispersion relation, given by

\displaystyle\omega(\xi):=\sqrt{1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}2(1-\cos(\xi_{j}))}.

(2)

We will identify the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi Z)^{d}
with the fundamental domain
[0,2\pi]^{d}
.
The solution to (
1
) is given (for sufficiently regular data
f,g,F
) by Duhamel’s formula,

\begin{split}u(t,x)&=\cos(t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}})f(x)+\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}})}{\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}}g(x)\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin((t-s)\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}})}{\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}}F(s,x){\rm d}s.\end{split}

(3)

Here,
\cos(t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}})e_{\xi}=\cos(t\omega(\xi))e_{\xi}
,
\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}})}{\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}}e_{\xi}=\frac{\sin(t\omega(\xi))}{\omega(\xi)}e_{\xi}
, and the action on Schwartz functions is defined by using the Fourier inversion formula.

1.2.
Decay estimates

The solution
u(t,x)
is thus a sum of oscillatory integrals of the form

\displaystyle I(t,x):=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(x\cdot\xi-t\omega(\xi))}a(\xi){\rm d}\xi,

(4)

where
a:[0,2\pi]^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is a smooth function; in fact,
a(\xi)=1
or
a(\xi)=\omega(\xi)^{-1}
. We will be interested in obtaining time decay estimates on
I(t,x)
, uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In other words, we want to find (the largest possible)
\sigma>0
such that

\displaystyle\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\sigma},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

(5)

for some constant
C
independent of
t
. The following theorem is our main result.

Theorem 1
.

For the oscillatory integrals (
4
) the following estimates hold for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
:

\displaystyle\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{3}{4}},

(6)

\displaystyle\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{7}{6}},

(7)

\displaystyle\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{4})}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log(2+|t|),

(8)

where
C
is a constant independent of
t
.

Remark 1
.

(i) The exponent in the estimates (
6
), (
7
), (
8
) is sharp. In fact, there are vectors
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and non-zero constants
c_{d}

(d=2,3,4)
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{\frac{3}{4}}|I(t,tv)|=c_{2}\quad\mbox{for}\quad d=2,

(9)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}t^{\frac{7}{6}}|I(t,tv)|=c_{3}\quad\mbox{for}\quad d=3,

(10)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{t^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\log t}|I(t,tv)|=c_{4}\quad\mbox{for}\quad d=4,

(11)

see
[
17
]
for the case
d=4
and
[
15
]
for similar observations.

(ii) We conjecture that for
d\geq 5
, the following estimate is true,

\displaystyle\|I(t,\cdot)\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq C_{a,d}(1+|t|)^{-\frac{2d+1}{6}}\log(2+|t|)^{d-4}.

(12)

We remark here that the original conjecture of Kevrekidis and Stefanov
[
19
]
can be proved by relatively simple stationary phase arguments (see Remark
5
). However, we expect that the better estimates (
12
) hold.

(iii) The estimates (
6
)–(
8
) continue to hold if
\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
is replaced by
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

(iv) It will be necessary to prove Theorem
1
in each case
d=2,3,4
separately (see Propositions
9
,
10
,
12
). This is in contrast to the Schrödinger case in
[
19
]
, where the same proof works in all dimensions.

If
x=vt
, where
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the velocity, then the oscillatory integral (
4
) takes the form

\displaystyle J(t,v):=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t\Phi(v,\xi)}a(\xi){\rm d}\xi,\quad\Phi(v,\xi)=v\cdot\xi-\omega(\xi).

(13)

In fact, the proof of Theorem
1
gives more precise information on the set of velocities for which the indicated decay occurs. These velocities are the images, under the map
\xi\mapsto\nabla\omega(\xi)
, of the critical points of the phase function
\Phi(\cdot,v)
. The time-decay of
J(t,v)
is governed by the degeneracy of the phase function at the critical points lying within the support of
a
. For a fixed value of the parameter
v=v_{0}
a critical point of
\Phi(\cdot)=\Phi(v_{0},\cdot)
is a point
\xi=\xi_{0}
where the gradient
\nabla\Phi(\xi)
vanishes; in the case of the phase function in (
13
) this happens if
\nabla\omega(\xi_{0})=v_{0}
. If there are no critical points, then
J(t,v_{0})
decays faster than any polynomial in
t
. Since the Klein-Gordon equation has finite speed of propagation,
|\nabla\omega(\xi)|\leq 1
, there are no critical points if
|v_{0}|>1
; this follows from the alternative formula

\displaystyle\omega(\xi)^{2}=1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sin^{2}(\xi_{j}).

The region
|v|\leq 1
in velocity space is called the light cone. Hence, the solution
u(t,x)
decays rapidly outside the light cone. In fact, since
\omega(\cdot)
is analytic, the decay is exponential; this can be established by the method of steepest descent, like in
[
27
,
4
]
, but we will not pursue the issue here. Inside the light cone, there are critical points. Generically (i.e. for most values of
v_{0}
) these critical points are non-degenerate, that is
\det\Hphi(\xi_{0})\neq 0
. We note in passing that the Hessian is invariantly defined (i.e. invariant under changes of coordinates) at a critical point. The stationary phase method (see e.g.
[
28
]
) yields a
|t|^{-d/2}
decay at such points. However, unlike in the continuous case, there are caustics, i.e. regions inside the light cone
|v|<1
where the solution decays slower, at a rate
|t|^{-\sigma}
(possibly with an additional logarithmic loss
\log^{k}|t|
), where
d/2-\sigma>0
is called the order of the caustic
[
9
]
or the singular index
[
2
]
. In the simplest case,

\displaystyle\rank\Hphi(\xi_{0})=d-1,\quad\nabla\det\Hphi(\xi_{0})\neq 0.

(14)

Then
\Sigma:=\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d}:\det\Hphi(\xi)=0\}
is a smooth
d-1
dimensional manifold, and there exists a (non-unique) kernel vector field, i.e. a smooth non-zero vector field
V
along
\Sigma
such that
\Hphi(\xi)(V(\xi))=0
for all
\xi\in\Sigma
. A phase function
\Phi
satisfying the condition (
14
) is said to have a
corank one
singularity. The simplest corank one singularity occurs when, for every
\xi\in\Sigma
,
\ker\Hphi(\xi)
intersects
T_{\xi}\Sigma
transversally, or equivalently,
(\nabla_{\xi}\det\Hphi)\cdot V\neq 0
on
\Sigma
. This singularity is called the (Whitney) “fold”. In the classification of Arnol’d
[
3
]
the fold is called an
A_{2}
singularity
[
1
]
, and the oscillatory integral (
13
) reduces to an Airy integral in one variable
[
23
,
14
]
(after integrating out the other
d-1
variables by stationary phase). The next more complicated singularity, called the “cusp” or
A_{3}
singularity, occurs at points in
\Sigma
where
(\nabla_{\xi}\det\Hphi)\cdot V
vanishes to first order. A systematization of these ideas gives rise to the Thom-Boardman classes. We refer the interested reader to
[
7
,
13
,
1
]
for an introduction to singularity theory. The situation becomes more complicated if we allow the parameter
v
to vary. Then we have to consider a
family
of functions as opposed to a single function. This introduces (topological) notions of “typicality” (or transversality) and “stability” in the space of functions depending on a given number of parameters. In our case, the number of parameters equals the dimension
d
of the underlying space
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Since we are considering
d\leq 4
here, the only stable singularities are Thom’s
seven elementary catastrophes

[
7
, 15.1]
; in the terminology of Arnol’d these are the
A_{2},A_{3},A_{4},A_{5},D_{4}^{-},D_{4}^{+},D_{5}
singularities. For the specific phase in (
13
), we will show in Section
2
that all these, except
D_{5}
, appear in
d\leq 4
dimensions. However, an additional (unstable) singularity appears in
d=4
. More precisely, in
d=2
, there are only
A_{k}
(
k\leq 3
) singularities. In
d=3
, the phase function has a more degenerate
D_{4}^{-}
type singularity. In
d=4
, the phase function has only critical points with finite multiplicity, and in the most degenerate case is similar to a hyperbolic singularity (
T_{4,4,4}
in the classification of Arnol’d
[
1
, 15.1]
).
The critical point of a hyperbolic singularity is (complex) isolated;
however, our uniform estimates hold true for more general phase functions having non-isolated critical points. From the knowledge of the type of singularity, we can determine the singular index. For the
A_{k}
,
D_{k}
singularities (and hence in
d=2,3
) this follows from the work of Duistermaat
[
9
]
.
For the unstable singularity in
d=4
we use a result of Karpushkin
[
16
]
.

For ease of reference, table
1
lists the normal forms and singularity indices for
A_{k}
,
D_{k}
and
T_{p,q,r}
type singularities (compare
[
1
, 15.1]
,
[
2
, 6.1.10]
). The normal form contains the “active variables” only. By this we mean the following: The Splitting Lemma
[
7
, 14.12]
says that, near a critical point, a smooth function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
can be expressed in local coordinates as

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},\cdot,x_{d})=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})+Q(x_{r+1},\ldots,x_{d}),

where
r
is the corank of
\phi
(or the number of active variables) at the critical point. Table
1
lists the normal forms of
f
, i.e. after a change of coordinates,
f
reduces to one of the tabulated normal forms in the cases encountered here (at least in
d\leq 3
; the
T_{4,4,4}
singularity is tabulated for comparison only).

\hline\cr D_{k},k\geq 4&x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{2}^{k-1}&\frac{k-2}{2k-2}\\
\hline\cr T_{4,4,4}&x_{1}^{4}+x_{2}^{4}+x_{3}^{4}+ax_{1}x_{2}x_{3},\,a\neq 0&\frac{1}{2}\\

Normal forms and singularity index for
A_{k}
,
D_{k}
and
T_{4,4,4}
type singularities.

1.3.
Strichartz estimates

As a consequence of Theorem
1
we obtain Strichartz estimates. The proof follows from the (by now) standard argument of Keel–Tao
[
18
]
. More precisely, we apply
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
to the operator
U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}}
. For fixed
t
,
U(t)
is a unitary operator on the Hilbert space
H=\ell^{2}_{x}
. Here and henceforth,
\ell_{x}^{r}=\ell^{r}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
denote the spatial Lebesgue spaces. The mixed space-time Lebesgue spaces
L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}
are endowed with the norms

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}}=\big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\big(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|F(t,x)|^{r}\big)^{\frac{q}{r}}\big)^{\frac{1}{q}}

for
1\leq q,r<\infty
, with obvious modifications for
q=\infty
or
r=\infty
. We recall that a pair of exponents
(q,r)
is called
\sigma
-admissible
[
18
, Definition 1.1]
if

\displaystyle q,r\geq 2,\quad(q,r,\sigma)\neq(2,\infty,1),\quad\frac{1}{q}+\frac{\sigma}{r}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}.

If equality holds in the last condition, then
(q,r)
is said to be
sharp

\sigma
-admissible.
Taking
\sigma
as the decay rate in (
5
)–(
8
), the combination of Duhamel’s formula (
3
) with Theorem
1
and
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
yields the following family of Strichartz estimates.

Theorem 2
.

Let
u
be a solution of the Cauchy problem (
1
). Let
q,r,\overline{q},\overline{r}\geq 2
,

\begin{split}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{q}\leq\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\quad&\mbox{if }d=2,\\
\frac{1}{q}\leq\frac{7}{6}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\quad&\mbox{if }d=3,\\
\frac{1}{q}<\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\quad&\mbox{if }d=4,\end{cases}\end{split}

(15)

and similarly for
\overline{q},\overline{r}
.
Then
u
satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}}\leq C_{q,r,\overline{q},\overline{r}}(\|f\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}+\|g\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}+\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\overline{q}^{\prime}}\ell_{x}^{\overline{r}^{\prime}}}).

(16)

Remark 2
.

(i)
We call
(q,r)
a Strichartz pair if it satisfies (
15
). For any such pair there exists
r_{0}\in[2,r]
such that
(q,r_{0})
satisfies (
15
) with equality (in
d=4
we subtract a fixed, arbitrarily small
\epsilon>0
from the right hand side). Thus there is a
\tau\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle(1/q,1/r_{0})=\tau(1/q_{0},0)+(1-\tau)(0,1/2),

where
q_{0}=8/3
if
d=2
,
q_{0}=12/7
if
d=3
and
q_{0}=4/3+\epsilon^{\prime}
if
d=4
(here
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
\epsilon
sufficiently small). Note that the Lebesgue spaces over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
form a filtration, i.e.
\ell^{p_{1}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\subset\ell^{p_{2}}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})
for
p_{1}\leq p_{2}
. This fact, together with the Gagliardo-Nirenberg and Young’s inequality (compare Remark 5 in
[
20
]
) yields

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}}\leq\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r_{0}}}\leq\|u\|^{\tau}_{L_{t}^{q_{0}}\ell_{x}^{\infty}}\|u\|^{1-\tau}_{L_{t}^{\infty}\ell_{x}^{2}}=:\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{0}}\ell_{x}^{\infty}\cap L_{t}^{\infty}\ell_{x}^{2}}.

Hence, all the Strichartz estimates in Theorem
2
can be subsumed in the inequality

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{0}}\ell_{x}^{\infty}\cap L_{t}^{\infty}\ell_{x}^{2}}\leq C_{\overline{q},\overline{r}}(\|f\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}+\|g\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}+\inf\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\overline{q}^{\prime}}\ell_{x}^{\overline{r}^{\prime}}}),

(17)

where the infimum is taken over all Strichartz pairs
(\overline{q},\overline{r})
.

(ii)
The Strichartz estimates are also commonly expressed in terms of mapping properties for the operator
U(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}}
,

\begin{split}\|U(t)f\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}}&\leq C\|f\|_{\ell^{2}_{x}},\\
\big\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}U(-t)F(s)\big\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}&\leq C\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\overline{q}^{\prime}}\ell_{x}^{\overline{r}^{\prime}}},\\
\big\|\int_{s<t}U(t-s)F(s)\big\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}}&\leq C\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\overline{q}^{\prime}}\ell_{x}^{\overline{r}^{\prime}}}.\end{split}

(18)

Note that
(\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x})^{-1/2}
is a bounded operator on
\ell_{x}^{r}
for every
r\in[1,\infty]
, see
[
19
, Lemma 1]
; hence (
16
) follows from (
18
) and (
3
).

(iii)
By
[
25
, Lemma 3.9]

the following sharp
\sigma
-admissible estimates are best possible,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(q,r)=(\frac{8}{3},\infty)\quad&\mbox{if }d=2,\\
(q,r)=(\frac{12}{7},\infty)\quad&\mbox{if }d=3,\\
(q,r)=(\frac{4}{3}+,\infty)\quad&\mbox{if }d=4,\end{cases}

in the sense that Strichartz estimates cannot hold for a pair
(\tilde{q},\infty)
with
\tilde{q}<q
.

1.4.
Discrete nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation

Strichartz estimates can be used in conjunction with a contraction mapping argument to prove global well-posedness for certain nonlinear equations with small initial data. Here we consider the discrete nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation

\displaystyle u_{tt}-\Delta_{x}u+u\pm|u|^{2s}u=0,\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(19)

where
(u(0),u_{t}(0))\in\ell_{x}^{2}\times\ell_{x}^{2}
and
s
satisfies

\begin{split}\begin{cases}s\geq\frac{4}{3}\quad&\mbox{if }d=2,\\
s\geq\frac{6}{7}\quad&\mbox{if }d=3,\\
s>\frac{2}{3}\quad&\mbox{if }d=4.\end{cases}\end{split}

(20)

Given the Strichartz estimates, the proof of the following theorem is standard (see e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 6]
), but we will give proofs of the PDE applications in Section
3
.

Theorem 3

(Global well-posedness for small data)
.

Assume that
s
satisfies (
20
). There exists
\epsilon>0
and a constant
C
so that, whenever
\|u(0)\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}+\|u_{t}(0)\|_{\ell_{x}^{2}}\leq\epsilon
, then (
19
) has a unique global solution. Moreover, the solution satisfies

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q}\ell_{x}^{r}}\leq C\epsilon

for any Strichartz pair
(q,r)
.

Interpolating between the bounds of Theorem
1
and energy conservation for the
linear
DKG (
1
) (without forcing term, i.e.
F=0
) we get the following decay estimates for the
\ell^{p}
-norm of the solution. For
2\leq p\leq\infty
,

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\ell^{p}_{x}}\leq C_{p}(1+|t|)^{-\sigma(p-2)/p}\|(u(0),u_{t}(0))\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}\times\ell^{p^{\prime}}},

(21)

where
\sigma=\sigma_{d}
is the decay rate for
p=\infty
, i.e.

\displaystyle\sigma_{d}:=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}\quad&\mbox{if }d=2,\\
\frac{7}{6}\quad&\mbox{if }d=3,\\
\frac{3}{2}-\quad&\mbox{if }d=4,\end{cases}

(22)

where
3/2-
means
3/2-\epsilon
for arbitrary fixed
\epsilon>0
. Before stating the next theorem we define

\begin{split}p_{2}:=\frac{1}{6}(13+\sqrt{97})\approx 3.8,\quad p_{3}:=\frac{1}{14}(27+\sqrt{337})\approx 3.2,\quad p_{4}:=3,\\
s_{2}:=\frac{1}{12}(1+\sqrt{97})\approx 0.9,\quad s_{3}:=\frac{1}{28}(\sqrt{337}-1)\approx 0.6,\quad s_{4}:=\frac{1}{2}.\end{split}

(23)

The next theorem is related to a conjecture by Weinstein
[
31
]
.

Theorem 4

(Decay of small solutions)
.

Let
d=2,3,4
and
s>s_{d}
. Assume that
u
satisfies the discrete nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation (
19
). There exists
\epsilon>0
such that, whenever
\|u(0)\|_{\ell^{p_{d}^{\prime}}}+\|u_{t}(0)\|_{\ell^{p_{d}^{\prime}}}\leq\epsilon
, then for every
p\in[2,p_{d}]
, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\ell^{p}_{x}}\leq C_{p}(1+|t|)^{-\sigma(p-2)/p}\|(u(0),u_{t}(0))\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}\times\ell^{p^{\prime}}},

(24)

where
\sigma=\sigma_{d}
.

Remark 3
.

Theorem
4
implies that no standing wave solutions
u(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\lambda t}\phi(x)
are possible under the stated smallness assumption. Weinstein
[
31
]
proved the existence of an excitation threshold for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation in the continuum and conjectured that, for
s\geq 2/d
, solutions with sufficiently small initial conditions satisfy
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)\|_{\ell^{p}_{x}}=0
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
. On the lattice, Kevrekidis and Stefanov
[
19

1.5.
Resolvent estimates and spectral consequences

Here we consider a stationary version of the discrete Klein–Gordon equation, namely

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta}\psi+V\psi=\lambda\psi,\quad\psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}).

(25)

We start with a resolvent estimate for the unperturbed operator
\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta}
. The idea of using Strichartz estimates to prove resolvent estimates is not new. It has appeared e.g. in
[
19
,
21
,
22
,
8
,
5
]
; the authors of
[
5
]
attribute the argument to T. Duyckaerts. The following results are analogues to
[
30
, Proposition 3.3]
and
[
19
, Theorem 4]
for the stationary Schrödinger equation. Again, in the Klein–Gordon case, better estimates are possible; in particular, our resolvent estimates hold in
d=3
, whereas the estimates in
[
30
,
19
]
require
d\geq 4
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
d=3,4
. There exists a constant
C
such that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma-1}}}\leq C\|(\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta}-\lambda)\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma+1}}},

where
\sigma=\sigma_{d}
is given by (
22

Corollary 6
.

Let
d=3,4
. There exists
\epsilon>0
such that, whenever
\|V\|_{\ell^{\sigma}}\leq\epsilon
, then the eigenvalue problem (
25
) has no nontrivial solution.

1.6.
Organization of the paper

Section

2.
Singularities of the phase function

In this section we consider the oscillatory integrals (
13
). To conform with standard notation we use (in this section only) the parameters
(\lambda,s)
instead of
(t,v)
and the integration variable
x
instead of
\xi
. That is, we consider

J(\lambda,s)=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{i\lambda\Phi(x,s)}dx,

(26)

where

\Phi(x,s):=\omega(x)-sx,\quad\omega(x):=\sqrt{1+2d-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cos(x_{j})}.

(27)

Here and in the following
sx
means
s\cdot x
.
Suppose
s=s^{0}
is a fixed vector and
\Phi(x,s^{0})
has a critical point
x^{0}
(perhaps non-unique, but we consider one of them).

Note that
\omega(x)\geq 1
for all
x\in[0,2\pi]
.
From now on we use the abbreviations
c_{j}:=\cos(x_{j}^{0}),s_{j}:=\sin(x_{j}^{0})
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
Consider the functions

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\Phi(x,s^{0})-\Phi(x^{0},s^{0}),

(28)

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(x)

\displaystyle:=\omega(x)+s^{0}x+\Phi(x^{0},s^{0}).

(29)

Obviously,
\phi_{1}(x^{0})=0
and
\nabla\phi_{1}(x^{0})=0
.
Moreover,
\phi_{2}(x^{0})\neq 0
; otherwise, we would have
0=\phi_{1}(x^{0})+\phi_{2}(x^{0})=2\omega(x^{0})\geq 2
. Instead of
\Phi
we will consider the new function

\displaystyle\phi(x):=\phi_{1}(x)\phi_{2}(x)

(30)

and investigate the type of singularities of the critical point
x^{0}
. By the properties of
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
just mentioned, the singularity type (for the so-called weighted homogeneous cases) of the functions
\Phi
and
\phi
at
x^{0}
is the same (and the critical value is zero for the latter). But, as we will see, singularities of
\phi
are easier to study since we can avoid radicals.
The critical point equation yields
s^{0}=\nabla\omega(x^{0})
. In order to avoid possible confusion between
s_{j}
and the components of
s^{0}
we will not use the latter notation any more.

We introduce the new variables by
\eta=x-x^{0}
and, by using (
28
)–(
30
), we have

\phi(\eta)=1+2d-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}\cos(x_{j}^{0}+\eta_{j})-\frac{(\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}\eta_{j}+1+2d-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j})^{2}}{1+2d-2\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{j}}.

(31)

For the Hessian matrix we have

\displaystyle(\Hphi(0))_{kj}:=\partial_{k}\partial_{j}\phi(0):=2\left(c_{j}\delta_{kj}-\frac{s_{k}s_{j}}{1+2d-2\sum_{l=1}^{d}c_{l}}\right),

(32)

where
\delta_{kj}
is the Kronecker “delta”, i.e.
\delta_{kj}=1
whenever
k=j
and otherwise
\delta_{kj}=0
.
Moreover, for the determinant of Hessian matrix the following relation

\displaystyle\det\Hphi(0)=2^{d}\prod_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{s_{j}^{2}}{c_{j}(1+2d-2\sum_{l=1}^{d}c_{l})}\right)

(33)

holds true, whenever
c_{j}\neq 0,j=1,\dots d
. Note that
\det\Hphi(0)
is a rational function of
\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
because
s_{j}^{2}=1-c_{j}^{2}
.
A standard limiting argument then yields

\displaystyle\det\Hphi(0)=2^{d}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}c_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{s_{j}^{2}\prod_{m\neq j}c_{m}}{(1+2d-2\sum_{l=1}^{d}c_{l})}\right)

(34)

for any
c_{j}
, not only for
c_{j}\neq 0
. The following observation about (
32
) will be useful later:
\Hphi(0)
is a rank one perturbation of a diagonal matrix.
By rank subadditivity we then have the following result.

Lemma 7
.

Let
D=2\diag(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d})
and
S_{kj}=2s_{k}s_{j}/\omega(x^{0})^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}}
. Then

\Hphi(0)=D-S
and

\displaystyle\rank(D)-1\leq\rank(\Hphi(0))\leq\rank(D)+1.

(35)

In the following, we will consider each of the cases
d=2,3,4
separately. In view of (
7
) we distinguish each case into
d+1
sub-cases according to how many of the
c_{j}
are zero.

We will need the following notion of homogeneity.

Definition 1
.

Let
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{d})
with
0<\kappa_{j}\leq 1
for all
j=1,\ldots,d
. We say that a function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is homogeneous of degree
r\geq 0
with respect to the weight
\kappa
if for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda^{\kappa_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\kappa_{d}}x_{d})=\lambda^{r}\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}).

For a given weight
\kappa
and degree
r
we will indicate terms of degree
>r
by
\ldots
. If
\kappa
and
r
are clear from the context, we will not comment this further. For example, in (
36
) the meaning of “
\ldots
” is the standard one (
\kappa_{j}=1
,
r=5
). We will usually normalize
\kappa
such that
r=1
(e.g.
\kappa_{j}=1/5
,
r=1
in the previous example).

If
\phi
is analytic (as will be the case here), then it has an expansion in
\kappa
-homogeneous polynomials of increasing degrees. The polynomial with the lowest degree will be called the “principal part”,
\phi_{\rm pr}
.

\displaystyle p_{2}(\eta)-\left(c_{1}-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3})}\right)\left(\eta_{1}-\frac{s_{1}(s_{2}\eta_{2}+s_{3}\eta_{3})}{c_{1}(7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}))-s_{1}^{2}}\right)^{2}

Case 4bi:

\displaystyle=\left(c_{1}-\frac{s_{1}^{2}}{7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3})}\right)y_{1}^{2}+\frac{c_{2}^{2}s_{3}^{2}y_{2}^{2}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}}

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=y_{1}+\frac{s_{1}y_{3}}{c_{1}(7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}))-s_{1}^{2}},

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=y_{1}+\frac{\frac{c_{2}c_{3}s_{1}}{c_{1}}y_{3}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}},

(88)

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{2}s_{3}^{2}y_{2}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}}+\frac{c_{3}s_{2}y_{3}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}},

(92)

\displaystyle=\frac{3s_{1}^{3}c_{2}^{2}c_{3}^{2}y_{1}y_{3}^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}(c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2})^{2}}+\frac{3y_{2}y_{3}^{2}c_{2}s_{2}s_{3}^{2}(c_{3}^{2}s_{2}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}s_{3}^{2})}{(c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2})^{3}}+\dots

>1

(1/2,1/2,1/4)

We claim that if the following condition (see (

\displaystyle s_{1}^{6}c_{2}^{5}c_{3}^{5}(7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}))+c_{1}^{5}s_{3}^{2}s_{2}^{2}(c_{3}^{2}-c_{2}^{2})^{2}\neq 0,

\displaystyle\phi(\eta(y))

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{c_{1}(c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2})}{c_{2}c_{3}(7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}))}\right)y_{1}^{2}+\frac{c_{2}^{2}s_{3}^{2}y_{2}^{2}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-\frac{s_{1}^{3}c_{2}^{2}c_{3}^{2}y_{1}y_{3}^{2}}{c_{1}^{2}(c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2})^{2}}-\frac{y_{2}y_{3}^{2}c_{2}s_{2}s_{3}^{2}(c_{3}^{2}-c_{2}^{2})}{(c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2})^{3}}}+\dots

\displaystyle c_{1}\eta_{1}^{4}+c_{2}\eta_{2}^{4}+c_{3}\eta_{3}^{4}

\displaystyle=c_{1}\left(y_{1}+\frac{\frac{c_{2}c_{3}s_{1}}{c_{1}}y_{3}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}}\right)^{4}+c_{2}\left(\frac{c_{2}s_{3}^{2}y_{2}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}}+\frac{c_{3}s_{2}y_{3}}{c_{3}s_{2}^{2}+c_{2}s_{3}^{2}}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle s_{1}^{6}c_{2}^{5}c_{3}^{5}(7-2(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}))+c_{1}^{5}s_{3}^{2}s_{2}^{2}(c_{3}^{2}-c_{2}^{2})^{2}=0.

A_{k}

(k\leq 3)
type singularities at

.

In summary, we have the following result for
d=3
.

.

If
d=3
, then the phase function can have
A_{k}

(k\leq 5)
and

type singularities. The corresponding oscillatory integral is estimated by

\lambda,s
.

Remark 5
.

(i)
Note that the singularity index for
A_{5}
and

\frac{1}{3}
.

(ii)
For

c_{j}\neq 0
for all

2.3.
Four dimensions

Let
d=4
. Again we first consider the most degenerate case.

Case 1:

c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}=c_{4}=0
. Then
s_{j}=\pm 1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\phi(\eta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{(s_{1}\eta_{1}+s_{2}\eta_{2}+s_{3}\eta_{3}+s_{4}\eta_{4})^{2}}{9}-\frac{2}{3!}((s_{1}\eta_{1})^{3}+(s_{2}\eta_{2})^{3}+(s_{3}\eta_{3})^{3}+(s_{4}\eta_{4})^{3})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{5!}((s_{1}\eta_{1})^{5}+(s_{2}\eta_{2})^{5}+(s_{3}\eta_{3})^{5}+(s_{4}\eta_{4})^{5})+\dots

where
\ldots
denotes higher order terms in the Taylor expansion that can be considered as a small perturbation of the principal part. We will show that this small perturbation can be removed by smooth change of variables, leading to a
T_{4,4,4}
type singularity.

Lemma 11
.

The function
\phi
has
T_{4,4,4}
type singularity at
\eta=0
.

Proof.

We use the change of variables
y_{4}=s_{1}\eta_{1}+s_{2}\eta_{2}+s_{3}\eta_{3}+s_{4}\eta_{4},y_{j}=s_{j}\eta_{j}
, for
j=1,2,3
. Then we have

\phi(\eta(y))=-\frac{y_{4}^{2}}{9}-\sigma_{1}(y)\sigma_{2}(y)+\sigma_{3}(y)+\dots,

(107)

where
\sigma_{j}
are elementary symmetric polynomials,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(y):=y_{1}+\dots+y_{n},

(108)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(y):=y_{1}y_{2}+y_{1}y_{3}+\dots+y_{n-1}y_{n},

(109)

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}(y)=y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}+\dots+y_{n-2}y_{n-1}y_{n},

(110)

and
n=d-1
is the number of “active variables” (so
n=3
in the present case).
Here we used the relation

y_{1}^{3}+\dots+y_{n}^{3}-\sigma_{1}^{3}=3\sigma_{3}-3\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}.

(111)

If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n=3}
, then it can be shown that
\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
is linearly equivalent (by linear change of variables)
to
z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}
.
Oscillatory integrals with such phase functions have been investigated by Karpushkin
[
16
]
.
They satisfy estimates of the form
\mathcal{O}(|\lambda|^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log|\lambda|)
as
|\lambda|\to\infty
.
It follows from the arguments of
[
15
]

that the same estimate holds when we add a small linear perturbation, i.e. the phase function is
z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}+O(|z|^{4})
. In fact, Karpushkin’s theorem holds for arbitrary small perturbations, but we only need this weaker result here.
We explain this now more precisely:
The function
\phi(\eta(y))
can be written as

\displaystyle\phi(\eta(y))=b_{1}(y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3},y_{4})(y_{4}-(y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3})^{2}\omega(y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}))^{2}+y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}+\dots,

where
b_{1}
and
\omega
are smooth functions of two and one variables respectively, with
b_{1}(0,0)\neq 0
and
\omega(0)\neq 0
. Hence, it is easy to see that the function

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\eta(y))=\frac{\phi(\eta(y))}{\phi_{1}(\eta(y))}

can be written as

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\eta(y))

\displaystyle=\tilde{b}_{1}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})(y_{4}-(y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3})^{2}\omega(y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3})+\omega_{1}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}))^{2}

\displaystyle+y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}+\dots,

where
\tilde{b}_{1},\omega_{1}(y)
are smooth functions satisfying
b_{1}(0,0,0,0)\neq 0
and
\omega_{1}
satisfies the condition that for any nonnegative integer triples
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
, with
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}\leq 3
, one has
\partial_{1}^{k_{1}}\partial_{2}^{k_{2}}\partial_{3}^{k_{3}}\omega_{1}(0,0,0)=0
; moreover, “
+\dots
” means
O(|y|^{4})
with
y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})
.
Then the total phase function can be written as

\Phi(y,s)=\phi_{2}(\eta(y))-sy
up to an irrelevant additive constant. By using the stationary phase method in the
y_{4}
variable, we obtain a perturbation of a function of three variables having the form
\phi_{1}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}):=y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}+\dots
. Thus, the total phase function
\Phi
of three variables and four parameters can be written as

\displaystyle\Phi(y,s)

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})-(s_{1}+g_{1}(s_{4}))y_{1}-(s_{2}+g_{2}(s_{4}))y_{2}-(s_{3}+g_{3}(s_{4}))y_{3}

\displaystyle+s_{4}((y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3})^{2}\omega(y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3})+s_{4}\omega_{2}(y,s_{4}))+s_{4}\omega_{3}(y,s_{4}),

where
g_{l}

(l=1,2,3)
are smooth functions, with
g_{k}(0)=0
, and
\omega_{j}(y,s_{4})

(j=2,3)
are smooth functions such that for any nonnegative integer triples
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
, with
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}\leq j
, we have the relation
\partial_{1}^{k_{1}}\partial_{2}^{k_{2}}\partial_{3}^{k_{3}}\omega_{j}(0,0,0,0)=0
.
Now we can apply the classical theorem of Karpushkin
[
16
]
to the function
\Phi
and obtain the required bound.
∎

is zero; without loss of generality,

and
c_{2}\neq 0,c_{3}\neq 0,c_{4}\neq 0
. Then by (
33
) we have

\det\Hphi(0)\neq 0
. Thus, the phase function has an
A_{1}
(non-degenerate) singularity.

Case 3:
Suppose that two of the
c_{j}
are zero; without loss of generality,
c_{1}=0=c_{2}
and
c_{3}\neq 0,c_{4}\neq 0
. Since
|s_{1}|=|s_{2}|=1
, the matrix
(S_{kj})_{kj=1}^{2}
(see Lemma
7
for the notation) has rank
1
. Hence the rank of the matrix
\Hphi(0)
equals
3
. Therefore, the corresponding integral decays as
\mathcal{O}(|\lambda|^{-3/2})
. This is sufficient for our result.

Case 4:
Suppose that three of the
c_{j}
are zero; without loss of generality,
c_{1}=c_{2}=c_{3}=0
and
c_{4}\neq 0
. Then we have

\phi(\eta)=c_{4}\eta_{4}^{2}-\frac{(\sum_{j=1}^{4}s_{j}\eta_{j})^{2}}{9-2c_{4}}-\frac{2}{3!}((s_{1}\eta_{1})^{3}+(s_{2}\eta_{2})^{3}+(s_{3}\eta_{3})^{3}+s_{4}\eta_{4}^{3})+\dots.

(112)

By a linear change of variables
y_{1}=s_{1}\eta_{1}
,
y_{2}=s_{2}\eta_{2}
,
y_{3}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}s_{j}\eta_{j}
,
y_{4}=\eta_{4}
,

\displaystyle\phi(\eta)

\displaystyle=c_{4}y_{4}^{2}-\frac{y_{3}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}9-2c_{4}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{2}{3!}(y_{1}^{3}+y_{2}^{3}-(y_{1}+y_{2})^{3})+\dots

\displaystyle=c_{4}y_{4}^{2}-\frac{y_{3}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}9-2c_{4}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{2}{3!}(y_{1}^{3}+y_{2}^{3}-(y_{1}+y_{2})^{3})

\displaystyle=c_{4}y_{4}^{2}-\frac{y_{3}^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}9-2c_{4}}}+y_{1}y_{2}(y_{1}+y_{2}).

where “
\dots

>1

(1/3,1/3,1/2,1/2)
, and
the principal part is given by the expression without “

”. In this case the phase function has a
D_{4}^{-}
type singularity. The corresponding oscillatory integral decays as

(see Table

Case 5:
Finally, we consider the case

7
the rank of the quadratic part is at least

type singularities. In this case we do not need to investigate the multiplicity of the corresponding phase function, because the corresponding oscillatory integral decays at least as
\mathcal{O}(|\lambda|^{-3/2})
, by stationary phase.

We record our findings for the

If

3.
Proofs of the PDE applications

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We are going to use a contraction mapping argument (Banach’s fix point theorem) in the metric space

\displaystyle X=\{u\in L_{t}^{\infty}\ell_{x}^{2}:\|u\|_{L_{t}^{q_{0}}\ell_{x}^{\infty}\cap L_{t}^{\infty}\ell_{x}^{2}}\leq 2C_{1,2}\|(f,g)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}\},

where
C_{1,2}
denotes the constant in the Strichartz estimate (
17
) with
(\overline{q},\overline{r})=(1,2)
and
q_{0}
as in Remark
2
. For convenience, we also introduced the notation
(f,g):=(u(0),u_{t}(0))
and

\displaystyle\|(f,g)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}:=\|f\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|g\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Consider the solution map

\displaystyle\Lambda u(t):=U_{0}(t)f+U_{1}(t)g\pm\int_{0}^{t}U_{1}(t-\tau)|u(\tau)|^{2s}u(\tau){\rm d}\tau,

where

\displaystyle U_{0}(t):=\cos(t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}),\quad U_{1}(t):=\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}})}{\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta_{x}}}.

A solution to the discrete nonlinear Klein–Gordon equation (
19
) is a fix point of
\Lambda
.
To apply the contraction mapping argument, we first check that
\Lambda X\subset X
. Indeed, applying the Strichartz estimate (
17
) with
(\overline{q},\overline{r})=(1,2)
, we have, for
u\in X
,

\displaystyle\|\Lambda u\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1,2}(\|(f,g)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}+\|u\|_{L_{t}^{2s+1}\ell_{x}^{2(2s+1)}}^{2s+1})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1,2}(\|(f,g)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}+\|u\|_{X}^{2s+1})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1,2}(\|(f,g)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}+(2C_{1,2}\epsilon)^{2s+1}).

In the second inequality we used that the pair
(2s+1,2(2s+1))
is a Strichartz pair and is thus controlled by the
X
-norm; in the last inequality we used the assumption on the initial data. For
\epsilon
sufficiently small, the last expression is bounded by
2C_{1,2}\|(f,g)\|_{\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}}
, and hence
\Lambda u\in X
. Similarly, using

\displaystyle\||u|^{2s}u-|v|^{2s}v\|_{L_{t}^{1}\ell_{x}^{2}}\leq C_{s}\|u-v\|_{L_{t}^{2s+1}\ell_{x}^{2(2s+1)}}(\|u\|^{2s}_{L_{t}^{2s+1}\ell_{x}^{2(2s+1)}}+\|v\|^{2s}_{L_{t}^{2s+1}\ell_{x}^{2(2s+1)}}),

one verifies that
\Lambda:X\to X
is a contraction.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Here we are use the contraction mapping argument in the metric space

\displaystyle X=\{u\in L_{t}^{\infty}\ell_{x}^{2}:\|u(t)\|_{\ell_{x}^{p}}\leq 2C_{p}\langle t\rangle^{-\sigma_{(}p-2)/p}\|(u(0),u_{t}(0))\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}\times\ell^{p^{\prime}}}\},

where
\langle t\rangle:=(1+|t|)
,
2\leq p\leq p_{d}
,
\sigma=\sigma_{d}
, and
C_{p}
denotes the constant in the
\ell^{p}
decay estimate for the linear equation (
21
). By the latter, we have, for
u\in X
,

\displaystyle\|\Lambda u\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\langle t\rangle^{-\sigma(p-2)/p}\|(u(0),u_{t}(0))\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}\times\ell^{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle+C_{p}\int_{0}^{t}\langle t-\tau\rangle^{-\sigma(p-2)/p}\|u(\tau)\|_{\ell_{x}^{(2s+1)p^{\prime}}}^{2s+1}{\rm d}\tau.

It suffices to show that the second term is bounded by the first.
The assumptions on
p,s
and (
23
) imply that

\displaystyle(2s+1)p^{\prime}\geq p,\quad\sigma(p-2)(2s+1)/p>1,

(114)

from which it follows that the second term is bounded by (using that
u\in X
)

\displaystyle C_{p}(2C_{p}\|(u(0),u_{t}(0))\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}\times\ell^{p^{\prime}}})^{2s+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle t-\tau\rangle^{-\sigma(p-2)/p}\langle\tau\rangle^{-\sigma(p-2)(2s+1)/p}{\rm d}\tau.

By the second inequality in (
114
), the integral is convergent and bounded by a constant times
\langle t\rangle^{-\sigma(p-2)/p}
. Since
\|(u(0),u_{t}(0))\|_{\ell^{p^{\prime}}\times\ell^{p^{\prime}}}^{2s}\leq\epsilon^{2s}
, we may choose
\epsilon
so small that
\Lambda u\in X
. The contractvity of
\Lambda
again follows in a similar manner. Banach’s fix point theorem then yields the existence of a unique solution, together with the a priori bound (
24
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
5
.

We first remark that, by standard arguments, the Strichartz estimates (
18
) can be localized in time to an interval
[0,T]
. We then apply the localized estimates with
(q,r)=(\overline{q},\overline{r})=(2,\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma-1})
to the function
u(t):=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t\lambda}\psi
, which satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u+H_{0}u=(H_{0}-\lambda)u,\quad u(0)=\psi,

where
H_{0}:=\sqrt{\mathbf{1}-\Delta}
.
Here we assume without loss of generality that
\lambda
is in the lower half plane; otherwise we consider
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}t\lambda}\psi
.
The result is that

\displaystyle c(\lambda,T)\|\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma-1}}}\leq C(\|\psi\|_{\ell^{2}}+c(\lambda,T)\|(H_{0}-z)\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma+1}}}),

where
c(z,T):=\|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}tz}\|_{L_{t}^{2}(0,T)}\geq T^{1/2}
by the assumption that
z
is in the lower half plane. Dividing by
c(z,T)
and letting
T\to\infty
yields the claimed bound.
∎

Proof of Corollary
6
.

Since
(H_{0}-\lambda)\psi=-V\psi
, we have, by Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma-1}}}\leq C\|V\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma+1}}}\leq C\|V\|_{\ell^{\sigma}}\|\psi\|_{\ell^{\frac{2\sigma}{\sigma-1}}}.

If
\epsilon
is so small that
C\epsilon<1
we get a contradiction, unless
\psi=0
.
∎
