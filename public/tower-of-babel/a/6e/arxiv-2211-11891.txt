A Bi-level Nonlinear Eigenvector Algorithm for Wasserstein Discriminant Analysis
Dong Min Roh, Zhaojun Bai, Ren-Cang Li

Abstract
Much like the classical Fisher linear discriminant analysis (LDA), the recently proposed Wasserstein discriminant analysis (WDA) is a linear dimensionality reduction method that seeks a projection matrix to maximize the dispersion of different data classes and minimize the dispersion of same data classes via a bi-level optimization. In contrast to LDA, WDA can account for both global and local interconnections between data classes by using the underlying principles of optimal transport. In this paper, a bi-level nonlinear eigenvector algorithm (WDA-nepv) is presented to fully exploit the structures of the bi-level optimization of WDA. The inner level of WDA-nepv for computing the optimal transport matrices is formulated as an eigenvector-dependent nonlinear eigenvalue problem (NEPv), and meanwhile, the outer level for trace ratio optimizations is formulated as another NEPv. Both NEPvs can be computed efficiently under the self-consistent field (SCF) framework. WDA-nepv is derivative-free and surrogate-model-free when compared with existing algorithms. Convergence analysis of the proposed WDA-nepv justifies the utilization of the SCF for solving the bi-level optimization of WDA. Numerical experiments with synthetic and real-life datasets demonstrate the classification accuracy and scalability of WDA-nepv.

Introduction

As widely used feature extraction approaches in machine learning, dimensionality reduction (DR) methods
[
53
,
7
,
20
,
12
]
learn projections such that the projected lower dimensional subspaces maintain the coherent structure of datasets and reduce computational costs of classification or clustering. The linear projection obtained from linear DR methods takes the form of a matrix such that the embedding to the lower dimensional subspace only involves matrix multiplications. Due to such ease in interpretation and implementation, linear DR methods are often the favored choice among numerous DR methods. For example, principal component analysis (PCA)
[
24
]
seeks to find a linear projection that preserves the dataset’s variation and is one of the most common and well-known DR methods. Other well-known DR methods include Fisher linear discriminant analysis (LDA)
[
24
]
to take into account the information of classes and compute a linear projection that best separates different classes, and Mahalanobis metric learning
[
35
]
to seek a distance metric that better models the relationship among dataset from a linear projection.

Wasserstein discriminant analysis (WDA)
[
19
]
is a supervised linear DR that is based on the use of regularized Wasserstein distances
[
13
]
as a distance metric.
Similar to Fisher linear discriminant analysis (LDA), WDA seeks a projection matrix to maximize the dispersion of projected points between different classes and minimize the dispersion of projected points within same classes. An important distinction between LDA and WDA is that while LDA only considers the global relations between data points
[
8
,
18
,
39
,
58
]
, WDA can dynamically take into account both global and local information through the choice of a regularization parameter.
A recent study has shown that WDA outperforms other linear DR methods in various learning tasks, such as sequence pattern analysis
[
50
,
51
]
, graph classification
[
62
]
, and multi-view classification
[
31
]
.

WDA is formulated as a bi-level nonlinear trace ratio optimization
[
19
]
. The inner optimization computes the optimal transport (OT) matrices of the regularized Wasserstein distance, an essential factor responsible for WDA’s ability to account for both global and local relations. The outer optimization involves a nonlinear trace ratio for the dispersion of data classes.

Related work.

The algorithmic aspects of WDA are the focus of this paper.
To the best of our knowledge, there are two algorithms specifically designed for WDA.
One approach is to use a projected gradient descent method
[
19
]
.
This approach requires the computation of derivatives of a highly nonlinear objective function at a cost that grows quadratically in the dimension size and the number of data points.
Another work considers a surrogate ratio trace model of WDA
[
37
]
. This approach lacks the quantification of the approximation error of the surrogate model. It is well-known that even in the case of the conventional trace ratio optimization with constant matrices, the surrogate ratio trace model could be a poor approximation
[
56
]
.

Contributions.

To overcome the theoretical and practical shortcomings of existing algorithms, we propose a new algorithm called WDA-nepv.
WDA-nepv is a derivative-free and surrogate-model-free algorithm that takes full advantage of the bi-level structure of WDA. Specifically, we formulate the computation of the OT matrices in the inner optimization and the outer trace ratio optimization as two eigenvector-dependent nonlinear eigenvalue problems (NEPvs).

We propose to solve both NEPvs by the self-consistent field (SCF) iteration.
Fast eigensolvers such as implicitly restarted Arnoldi method
[
49
]
enables efficient implementation of SCF.
In addition, we propose an efficient way to compute the cross-covariance matrices of WDA. Specifically, we convert multiple calls to level-2 BLAS (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms) to level-3 BLAS, which leads to a significant reduction in computing time.
We provide a convergence analysis for the SCF framework of WDA-nepv.
Numerically, we demonstrate linear convergence of WDA-nepv. Moreover, using real-life datasets, we demonstrate that the classification accuracy of WDA-nepv is comparable to or outperforms the other existing algorithms. We also present scalability of WDA-nepv algorithm, demonstrating its computational efficiency.

We emphasize that the primary purpose of this work is not to demonstrate the advantages of WDA compared with other DR methods. For an in-depth discussion of the relation of WDA with other discriminant analysis and the comparison of its classification accuracy with other DR methods, the reader is referred to
[
19
,
37
,
31
,
51
]
.

Transport polytope.

Given two probability vectors

For this reason, a matrix

and

Wasserstein distance.

Let

to

Given the matrix

When

Regularized Wasserstein distance.

Computing the Wasserstein distance, however, is subject to heavy costs that scale in super-cubic with respect to the size of the probability vector
[
42
]
. To reduce such heavy costs, Cuturi
[

]
proposed an entropic constraint on the transport matrix that not only lowers the computation cost of the problem but also smooths the search space. Namely, a convex subset of

U_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c}):=\bigg\{\boldsymbol{T}\mid\boldsymbol{T}\in U(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c})\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,h(\boldsymbol{T})\geq h(\boldsymbol{r})+h(\boldsymbol{c})-\alpha\bigg\},

(2.4)

where
\alpha\geq 0
and

and

h(\boldsymbol{r})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}\log r_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad h(\boldsymbol{T})=-\sum_{ij}T_{ij}\log T_{ij}.

(2.5)

Then, Cuturi
[
13
]
defined the following distance between

and
\boldsymbol{c}

W_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{T}\in U_{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c})}\langle\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{M}\rangle

\boldsymbol{T}^{\lambda}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{T}\in U(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{c})}\,\Big\{\langle\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{M}\rangle-\frac{1}{\lambda}h(\boldsymbol{T})\Big\}.

(2.7)

Applications.

The regularized Wasserstein distance serves as a powerful tool for computing distances between probability distributions and has found its popularity in machine learning. For example, in sequence pattern analysis, utilizing the regularized Wasserstein distance, a sequence metric that preserves the inherent temporal relationships of the instances in sequences was introduced in
[

3
Wasserstein Discriminant Analysis

Wasserstein discriminant analysis (WDA) seeks to find a projection matrix that maximizes the dispersion between different classes and minimizes the dispersion within same classes. Meanwhile, WDA can also control global and local inter-relations between data classes using a regularized Wasserstein distance. In this section, we present the formulation of WDA as a nonlinear trace ratio optimization. Then, we conclude the section with discussions on the advantages of WDA.

Data setup.

Consider the datasets

\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}\}_{i=1}^{n_{c}}
of data points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for different classes
c=1,2,\ldots,C
, where
n_{c}
denotes the number of data points in class
c
.
For each class
c
, a data matrix
\boldsymbol{X}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n_{c}}
is formed
by constructing its columns to be the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}

in class
c
:

\boldsymbol{X}^{c}=[\,\boldsymbol{x}^{c}_{1},\,\ldots,\,\boldsymbol{x}^{c}_{n_{c}}\,].

Without loss of generality, we assume that the data matrices
\boldsymbol{X}^{c}
are standardized, i.e., each feature is scaled such that it has mean
0
and standard deviation
1
. Otherwise, we can always preprocess
\boldsymbol{X}^{c}
as

\boldsymbol{X}^{c}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{X}^{c}-\frac{1}{n_{c}}(\boldsymbol{X}^{c}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c}})\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c}}^{T}

to have mean
0
then divide each feature by its standard deviation to set its standard deviation to be
1
.
Data standardization is a common practice in machine learning algorithms
to transform the features to a common scale while maintaining the structure of the data. This often leads to improved performance in algorithms
[
27
]
.

Definition of Wasserstein discriminant analysis.

WDA introduced in
[
19
]

presumes empirical measure as the underlying probability measure of the data matrices and uses the regularized Wasserstein distance as the distance metric. Specifically, the regularized Wasserstein distance between two projected data matrices by an orthonormal projection
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
(
p\ll d)
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}):=\langle\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}),\boldsymbol{M}_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}}\rangle,

(3.1)

where

\boldsymbol{M}_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}}
is the cost matrix defined as the Euclidean distances between projected points:

\boldsymbol{M}_{\boldsymbol{P^{T}X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P^{T}X}^{c^{\prime}}}:=\Big([\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}]_{ij}\Big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{c}\times n_{c^{\prime}}}

(3.2)

and
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
is the OT matrix, defined as the solution of the entropy-smoothed OT problem:

\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}):=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{T}\in U_{n_{c},n_{c^{\prime}}}}\,\Big\{\lambda\langle\boldsymbol{T},\boldsymbol{M}_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}}\rangle-h(\boldsymbol{T})\Big\},

(3.3)

where
U_{n_{c},n_{c^{\prime}}}
is the transport polytope between
\boldsymbol{P^{T}X}^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{P^{T}X}^{c^{\prime}}
defined as

U_{n_{c},n_{c^{\prime}}}:=\bigg\{\boldsymbol{T}\mid\boldsymbol{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n_{c}\times n_{c^{\prime}}},\,\boldsymbol{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c}},\,\boldsymbol{T}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c}}=\frac{1}{n_{c^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c^{\prime}}}\bigg\}.

(3.4)

The regularization parameter
\lambda\geq 0
plays a critical role in dynamically controlling the global and local relations between data points. We highlight the role of
\lambda
in the discussion of the advantages of WDA.

WDA adopts the formulation of Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to seek a projection matrix
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
by solving

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\frac{\sum_{c,c^{\prime}>c}W_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}})}{\sum_{c}W_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c})}.

(3.5)

The numerator and the denominator of (
3.5
) are the sums of the regularized Wasserstein distances (
3.1
) between the inter-classes and the intra-classes, respectively. As a maximization problem, WDA (
3.5
) seeks a projection that maximizes the numerator (the dispersion between different classes) and minimizes the denominator (the dispersion within same classes).

Nonlinear trace ratio formulation of WDA.

By (
3.2
) and the Frobenius inner product, the inter-class distance between data matrices
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}
is

\displaystyle W_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}),\boldsymbol{M}_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T},\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T}\boldsymbol{P})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}[\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T}]\boldsymbol{P})

\displaystyle=:\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P}),

(3.7)

where

\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}):=\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T}.

(3.8)

Similarly, the intra-class distance between
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c}
to itself is a trace operator

\displaystyle W_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c})

\displaystyle=:\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P}),

(3.9)

where

\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P}):=\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})^{T}.

(3.10)

Thus, by (
3.7
) and (
3.9
), WDA (
3.5
) can be reformulated as the nonlinear trace ratio optimization (NTRopt):

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\left\{f(\boldsymbol{P}):=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P})}\right\},

(3.11)

where
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})

are defined as the between and within cross-covariance matrices, respectively:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}>c}\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}>c}\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},

(3.12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{c}\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P})=\sum_{c}\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

(3.13)

We refer to the WDA problem (
3.11
) as
NTRopt-WDA
.

Advantages of WDA.

Among the number of advantages of WDA
[
19
]
, two outstanding ones are that WDA is a generalization of LDA and that WDA can dynamically consider both global and local relations of the data points.

•

When the regularization parameter
\lambda=0
, each relation between data points is treated equally and NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
) reduces to LDA. Specifically, by (
2.11
), the OT matrix is

\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}:=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{T}\in U_{n_{c},n_{c^{\prime}}}}h(\boldsymbol{T}).

The entropy maximizing matrix is simply

\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{n_{c}n_{c^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c},n_{c^{\prime}}},

and the cross-covariance matrices become

\boldsymbol{C}^{c,c^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{n_{c}n_{c^{\prime}}}\sum_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T}.

Consequently, NTRopt-WDA reduces to LDA:

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{b}\boldsymbol{P})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{S}_{w}\boldsymbol{P})}

where

\boldsymbol{S}_{b}:=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}>c}\frac{1}{n_{c}n_{c^{\prime}}}\sum_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{S}_{w}:=\sum_{c}\frac{1}{n_{c}^{2}}\sum_{ij}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})^{T}.

(3.14)

The between and within cross-covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{S}_{b}
and
\boldsymbol{S}_{w}
, respectively, indicate that all relations between data points carry equal weight. Therefore, LDA strictly enforces global relations among data points.

•

Unlike other linear DR methods that are strictly based on either global relations (such as LDA) or local relations (such as Large Margin Nearest Neighbor
[
58
]
) of the data points, WDA can dynamically consider both global and local relations by varying the regularization parameter
\lambda
in the regularized Wasserstein distances.

In the regularized Wasserstein distance (
3.6
), the transportation weight
{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
quantifies the importance of the distance
\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}
, i.e., the Euclidean distance between the projected data point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}
in class
c
and the projected data point
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}
in class
c^{\prime}
. By varying the strength of
\lambda
, the regularized Wasserstein distance is able to control the value of the transportation weights and thus the relations between data points. Small
\lambda
puts more emphasis on the global coherency and large
\lambda
puts more emphasis on the local structure of the class manifold.

Figure
1
illustrates the impact that
\lambda
has on the transportation weights. Shown in the figure is a synthetic dataset consisting of a class that displays a half-moon shape and another class that is a bi-modal Gaussian distribution with a mode on each side of the half-moon shape.
The transportation weights are represented as the edges between the data points such that their magnitudes are reflected by the visibility of the edges. The left column shows the inter-class relations and the right column shows the intra-class relations. Each row corresponds to a different
\lambda
value, with the first row corresponding to
\lambda=0.1
, the second row to
\lambda=0.5
, and the third row to
\lambda=1
.
For both inter-class and intra-class relations, we observe that as
\lambda
increases from
\lambda=0.1
to
\lambda=1
the structure of the class manifold shifts from globality to locality. For instance, when
\lambda=0.1
, if we focus on one of the red data points on the top mode we observe that it has strong inter-class relations with almost all the blue points and strong intra-class relations with almost all the red points. However, when
\lambda=1
, the same red data point has strong inter-class relations only with the blue points that are close-by and strong intra-class relations only with the red points that are in the top modal.

The impact of
\lambda
on the global and local relations between the projected data points can be explained by examining the formulation of the transportation weights and the sum constraint imposed on them.
Suppose that we have three data points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{c^{\prime}}
such that
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}
is closer to
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}
than it is to
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{c^{\prime}}
, i.e,

\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}<\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(3.15)

According to Theorem
2.1
, the transportation weights
T_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
and
T_{ik}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
between
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}
and between
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{c^{\prime}}
, respectively, are equal to

T_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})=u_{i}^{c,c^{\prime}}e^{-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}}v_{j}^{c,c^{\prime}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad T_{ik}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})=u_{i}^{c,c^{\prime}}e^{-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{c^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}}v_{k}^{c,c^{\prime}},

(3.16)

where
u_{i}^{c,c^{\prime}},v_{j}^{c,c^{\prime}},v_{k}^{c,c^{\prime}}
are found by an algorithm for matrix balancing problems such as the Sinkhorn-Knopp (SK) algorithm or the accelerated SK (Acc-SK) algorithm.
According to (
3.16
), the condition (
3.15
) suggests that
T_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})>T_{ik}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
, with the value of
\lambda
determining the magnitude difference between
T_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
and
T_{ik}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
.
Specifically, as
\lambda
increases, the disparity in size between
T_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
and
T_{ik}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
also increases. This indicates that the relationship between closely-distanced data points is emphasized more than the relationship between widely-distanced data points.
Furthermore, the sum constraint on the OT matrix
T^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})
, expressed as

\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c^{\prime}}}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c}}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P})^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c}}=\frac{1}{n_{c^{\prime}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{n_{c^{\prime}}},

(3.17)

implies that satisfying the constraint (
3.17
) requires an inverse relationship between transportation weights.
When one transportation weight becomes small, the other transportation weights must become large.
In other words, for large
\lambda
, the weaker data relations between distantly located data points contribute to the stronger data relations between closely located data points. This leads to a more pronounced locality in the relationships within the class manifold.

4
Existing Algorithms

To the best of our knowledge, there are two algorithms that are specifically designed for solving the NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
). In this section, we provide a summary of these algorithms and point out their shortcomings.

WDA-gd.

In
[
19
]
, NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
) is solved using the projected gradient descent method. The derivative of the OT matrix is computed as the derivative of the matrix
\boldsymbol{T}^{L}(\boldsymbol{P})=\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u}^{L})\boldsymbol{K}\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{v}^{L})
obtained from a preset
L^{\mbox{th}}
iteration of SK algorithm (see Section
5.1
for the discussion on SK algorithm). A recursion occurs in computing the derivative of
\boldsymbol{T}^{L}(\boldsymbol{P})
as
\boldsymbol{u}^{L}
depends on
\boldsymbol{v}^{L}
which in turn depends on
\boldsymbol{u}^{L-1}
. An automatic differentiation is used to compute the derivative of
\boldsymbol{T}^{L}(\boldsymbol{P})
in order to utilize the recursion scheme of SK algorithm. This derivative-based algorithm is referred to as WDA-gd.
However, WDA-gd is subject to some practical shortcomings.

•

A large number of SK iterations may be needed to obtain the OT matrices and their derivatives (see Example
2
).
If the iteration number
L
is not large enough, both the OT matrices and their derivatives may be inaccurate.

•

Computing the derivative of the objective function of NTRopt-WDA comes with a heavy cost that scales quadratically in the dimension size, the number of data points, and the number of data classes. Assuming that each class has
n
number of data points, at each iteration of WDA-gd, the costs of computing the derivatives of the OT matrices from automatic differentiation are
O(Lpn^{2}d^{2})
, where
L
is the number of SK iterations,
p
is the size of subspace dimension, and
d
is the size of the original dimension. The other computation costs in the derivative of the objective function are
O(pC^{2}n^{2}d)
where
C
is the number of classes. Therefore, the overall cost for computing the derivative at each iteration is
O(LpC^{2}n^{2}d^{2})
.

WDA-eig.

In
[
37
]
, the authors proposed to approximate NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
) with the following ratio trace surrogate model

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\mathrm{Tr}\bigg((\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P})^{-1}\bigg),

(4.1)

then further approximate this surrogate model using the NEPv:

\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P}\Lambda,

(4.2)

where
\boldsymbol{P}
is the eigenvector corresponding to the
p
largest eigenvalues of
(\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}),\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}))
.
The NEPv (
4.2
) was solved using the self-consistent field (SCF) iteration:

\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}\leftarrow\mbox{eigenvectors of the $p$ largest eigenvalues of $(\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P_{k}}),\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P_{k}}))$}.

(4.3)

The approach was named WDA-eig.
There are major issues with this approach.

•

The OT matrices needed in
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
are computed using SK algorithm. As a result, WDA-eig suffers from the same shortcomings as WDA-gd of needing a large number of SK iterations.

•

The surrogate model (
4.1
) may be a poor approximation to NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
) (See Figure
8
where the surrogate model (
4.1
) leads to a suboptimal solution).
In fact, when
p>1
and
(\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}),\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}))

are constant pair
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})
, it has been shown that the ratio trace optimization

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\mathrm{Tr}\bigg((\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P})(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P})^{-1}\bigg)

(4.4)

could be a poor approximation to the trace ratio optimization

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P})}.

(4.5)

and degrades the classification outcomes
[
56
]
.

•

There is no proper quantification for the quality of the surrogate model (
4.1
) to the NEPv (
4.2
). It is not known if the solution
\boldsymbol{P}
of the model (
4.1
) corresponds to the
p
largest eigenvectors of the NEPv (
4.2
). Therefore, the converged projection from the SCF iteration (
4.3
) may not be the solution to the surrogate model (
4.1
).

5
A Bi-Level Nonlinear Eigenvector Algorithm

In this section, we present a new algorithm, called WDA-nepv, to address the shortcomings of WDA-gd and WDA-eig.
Unlike WDA-gd, WDA-nepv eliminates the need for computing the derivatives of the objective function
f(\boldsymbol{P})
in (
3.11
).
Meanwhile, unlike WDA-eig, WDA-nepv directly tackles the objective function
f(\boldsymbol{P})
without using a surrogate function.
Therefore, WDA-nepv is
derivative-free
and
surrogate-model-free
.
WDA-nepv fully leverages the bi-level structure of NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
) by formulating both the inner and outer optimizations as NEPvs. This approach presents a unified framework for addressing NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
).

Algorithm outline.

WDA-nepv follows a bi-level or inner-outer iteration scheme in which at each iteration the projection dependence on the cross-covariance matrices is fixed and the projection is updated as the solution of the resulting trace ratio optimization (TRopt). There lie two NEPvs in WDA-nepv: one for computing the OT matrices of the cross-covariance matrices and the other for computing TRopt.

Here is an outline of WDA-nepv:

1.

Start with an initial projection
\boldsymbol{P}_{0}
with
\boldsymbol{P}^{T}_{0}\boldsymbol{P}_{0}=\boldsymbol{I}_{p}
.

2.

At the
k
th iteration:

(a)

Compute the OT matrices
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
by an NEPv (Sec.
5.1
).

(b)

Use the OT matrices
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
to form between and within cross-covariances
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
by level-3 BLAS (see Sec.
5.2
).

(c)

Compute
\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
:

\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\boldsymbol{P})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\boldsymbol{P})}

(5.1)

by another NEPv for TRopt (see Sec.
5.3
);

3.

Terminate when
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
are sufficiently close (see remarks of Algorithm
3
).

We solve both NEPvs using the self-consistent field (SCF) iteration. SCF iteration was originally implemented in solving Kohn-Sham equation arising in physics and quantum-chemistry
[
10
]
, and remains an active research topic and a popular method of choice for solving NEPvs
[
5
,
4
,
9
]
.

5.1
Computing OT Matrices by NEPv

In NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
), a key computation step involves computing the OT matrices defined in (
3.3
). The OT matrices take an integral part in quantifying the global and local relations of data points. Therefore, accurate and efficient computations of OT matrices are crucial. According to Theorem
2.1
, the computation of the OT matrices can be recast as a matrix balancing problem. In this section, we discuss Sinkhorn-Knopp (SK) algorithm, one of the most well-known algorithms for the matrix balancing problem, followed by its accelerated variant (named Acc-SK) that can converge in a smaller number of iterations. In contrast to WDA-gd and WDA-eig which use SK algorithm directly, we use Acc-SK to compute the OT matrices. We conclude the section with two examples to demonstrate the efficiency and accuracy of Acc-SK in comparison to SK.

Matrix balancing problem.

For the sake of simplicity, we denote the OT matrices
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and Euclidean distance matrix
\boldsymbol{M}_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c},\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{c^{\prime}}}
as
\boldsymbol{T}^{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}_{+}
and
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times m}
, respectively.
According to Theorem
2.1
,

\boldsymbol{T}^{\lambda}=\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{K}\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{v})

where
\boldsymbol{K}:=e^{-\lambda\boldsymbol{M}}
is an element-wise exponential, and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
satisfy the relation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{K}\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{1}_{m}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\\
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{1}_{n}=\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}.\end{cases}

(5.2)

Computing
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
from
\boldsymbol{K}
to satisfy the relation (
5.2
) is known as the matrix balancing problem.

There is a vast literature on the matrix balancing problem and one of its earliest works can be dated back as far as 1937
[
34
]
where the problem arose in the calculation of traffic flow. Its applications lie in many different areas such as balancing the matrix to improve the sensitivity of the eigenvalue problem
[
40
]
or balancing the matrix pencil to improve the sensitivity of the generalized eigenvalue problem
[
57
,
36
,
15
]
in order to compute more accurate eigenvalues, and in optimal transport
[
13
]
to compute the distance between probability vectors. See
[
28
]
for further discussions on the applications and the historical remarks of the matrix balancing problems.

The most notable work on the matrix balancing problem is Sinkhorn’s paper in 1964
[
46
]
that showed the matrix balancing problem is solvable for a positive square matrix. In 1967
[
47
]
, Sinkhorn further extended his result to prove the existence of a matrix with prescribed row and column sums from a positive rectangular matrix, and with Knopp
[
48
]
, derived conditions for the existence of a doubly stochastic matrix from a non-negative square matrix.

Sinkhorn-Knopp (SK) algorithm.

Sinkhorn-Knopp (SK) algorithm
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
, also known as RAS method or Bregman’s balancing method, is one of the most well-known algorithms for the matrix balancing problem. SK iteratively scales the matrix involving only matrix-vector multiplications, and converges linearly
[
30
,
21
,
32
]
. Applied to computing the OT matrix
\boldsymbol{T}^{\lambda}
, SK obtains the vectors
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfying (
5.2
) by an alternating updating scheme on the matrix
\boldsymbol{K}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}=\frac{1}{m}\boldsymbol{1}_{m}./(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{k})\\
\boldsymbol{u}_{k+1}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}./(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}).\end{cases}

(5.3)

Reformulation of SK as nonlinear mapping and NEPv.

While simple, the SK algorithm is subject to slow convergence
[
3
,
32
]
. There are variants of the SK algorithm for acceleration. They include a different updating scheme for scaling the matrix
[
41
]
, an inner-outer iteration algorithm based on Newton’s method
[
33
]
, a greedy Sinkhorn algorithm called Greenkhorn algorithm
[
1
,
2
]
, and the SCF iteration for solving the NEPv formulation
[
3
]
.

In our algorithm WDA-nepv, we consider accelerating the SK algorithm by solving the NEPv formulation. We note that the SK iteration (
5.3
) for obtaining the vector
\boldsymbol{v}

can be rewritten as the following iteration

\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}=R(\boldsymbol{v}_{k}),

(5.4)

where

R(\boldsymbol{v}):=\mathcal{D}^{-1}\bigg(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{-1}\bigg(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}\bigg)\frac{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}}{n}\bigg)\frac{\boldsymbol{1}_{m}}{m}

(5.5)

is a nonlinear mapping.

R
is a contraction mapping on the space of non-negative vectors
[
6
]
. Thus, the fixed point of
R
exists and is unique.

In the following, we show that the fixed point
\boldsymbol{v}
of the mapping
R
is an eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of
J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, the Jacobian of the mapping
R
. First, we have the following theorem due to
[
3
, Theorem 5]
.

3

Although only positive square matrices were subject to discussion in

Theorem 5.1
.

For a positive vector
\boldsymbol{v}
,
it holds

R(\boldsymbol{v})=J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v},

(5.6)

where
J_{R}
is the Jacobian of the
mapping
R

).

Proof.

The equation (
5.5
) can be written as the composition of mappings

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{n}{m}U(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v}))

(5.7)

where
U
and
S
are defined as

U(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{1}./\boldsymbol{v}\quad\mbox{and}\quad S(\boldsymbol{v})=U(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}).

(5.8)

Note that we define
U
as a component-wise reciprocal operator for vectors of any size. In particular, since
\boldsymbol{K}
is
n\times m
,
U
in the operator

operates on the vector of size
n
.

The Jacobian of

\displaystyle J_{U}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{D}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{v})=-\mathcal{D}^{2}(\boldsymbol{1}./\boldsymbol{v})=-\mathcal{D}^{2}(U(\boldsymbol{v})),

(5.9)

where the last equality holds by (
5.8

The Jacobian of
S

\displaystyle J_{S}(\boldsymbol{v})=-\mathcal{D}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{K}=-\mathcal{D}^{2}(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}./(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}=-\mathcal{D}^{2}(U(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}=-\mathcal{D}^{2}(S(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K},

(5.10)

where the last two equalities hold by (

By the chain rule, the Jacobian matrix of the mapping

is

\displaystyle J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=J_{\frac{n}{m}U\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S}(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{n}{m}J_{U}(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v}))\cdot\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\cdot J_{S}(\boldsymbol{v})

Utilizing the results (

), (
5.10

R
(

),
J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})
is further simplified as

\displaystyle J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{m}[-\mathcal{D}^{2}(U(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v})))][\boldsymbol{K}^{T}][-\mathcal{D}^{2}(S(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}]

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{2}(S(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}

(5.11)

Then,

\displaystyle J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{2}(S(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{2}(U(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{1}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}\mathcal{D}(U(\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{1}_{n}=\frac{m}{n}\mathcal{D}^{2}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{m}\mathcal{D}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{n}{m}\mathcal{D}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{1}_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{m}\mathcal{D}(U(\boldsymbol{K}^{T}S(\boldsymbol{v})))\boldsymbol{1}_{m}=\mathcal{D}(R(\boldsymbol{v}))\boldsymbol{1}_{m}=R(\boldsymbol{v})

∎

By Theorem
5.1
, the fixed point
\boldsymbol{v}
of the mapping

J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v},

(5.12)

i.e.,

corresponding to the eigenvalue

(5.13)

Accelerated Sinkhorn-Knopp (Acc-SK) algorithm.

The above discussion indicates that the SK algorithm (

To accelerate the SK iteration, we can apply the following SCF iteration for solving the NEPv (

\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}\leftarrow\mbox{eigenvector of $\mu_{\max}(J_{R}(\boldsymbol{v}_{k}))$.}

5.2
Computation of Cross-covariance Matrices
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})

Recall that the between and within cross-covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
defined in (
3.12
) and (
3.13
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}>c}\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})^{T},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c}\sum_{ij}{T}_{ij}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c})^{T}

are the weighted sums of the outer products of the data points, where the weights are the components of the OT matrices. The number of double sums in
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
are

n_{b}:=\sum_{c,c^{\prime}>c}^{C}n_{c}n_{c^{\prime}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad n_{w}:=\sum_{c}^{C}n_{c}^{2},

respectively. Overall, the number of double sums grow quadratically in the number of classes or data points.

A straightforward implementation for computing these cross-covariance matrices is by a double for-loop with
n_{b}
and
n_{w}
calls to level-2 BLAS in forming the outer products of
\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
, respectively.
However, memory access is the bottleneck on modern computing platforms.
Unlike level-2 BLAS, level-3 BLAS makes full reuse of data and avoids excessive movement of data to and from memory
[
14
]
.
Therefore, we propose to compute the cross-covariance matrices using level-3 BLAS to improve computational efficiency.
Specifically, we reformulate the sum of the outer products as a matrix-matrix multiplication:

\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}^{T}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})

(5.15)

where

\boldsymbol{\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n_{b}}
and
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n_{w}}
have columns

\sqrt{{T}_{ij}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c^{\prime}})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sqrt{{T}_{ij}^{c,c}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}^{c}),

respectively.
As level-3 BLAS, the matrix-matrix multiplications (
5.15
) are far more efficient than forming the sum of outer products. In particular, the improvement in efficiency becomes more prominent as the data dimension gets large. For an instance of the Synthetic dataset (see Section
6
for its description) with
d=2000
, the running time of the sum of outer products took 127 seconds while the level-3 BLAS formulation only took 0.63 seconds.

We note that efficient computations of the cross-covariance matrices using level-3 BLAS is a novelty of this work. In WDA-gd, cross-covariance matrices are not formed explicitly. Instead, each regularized Wasserstein distance is computed directly as the weighted sum (
3.6
) and the reciprocal of the objective function (
3.5
) is an input in Pymanopt
[
52
]
,
an optimizer on matrix manifold. In WDA-eig, the cross-covariance matrices are implemented directly from their double sum formulations using tensor operations.

5.3

Now let us consider the outer iteration for solving the TRopt (
5.1

For notation convenience, set

Note that by (
5.15

\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}

\boldsymbol{A}

Then, the TRopt (

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\left\{q(\boldsymbol{P}):=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{P})}\right\}.

38

\bigg(\boldsymbol{A}-q(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{B}\bigg)\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{P}\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{P}}

where

is an orthonormal eigenbasis corresponding to
the
p
largest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{A}-q(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{B}

[
61
]
.

The gateway algorithm for solving the NEPv (

\boldsymbol{P}_{j+1}\leftarrow\mbox{eigenvectors of the $p$ largest eigenvalues of }\boldsymbol{A}-q(\boldsymbol{P}_{j})\boldsymbol{B}.

(5.18)

The SCF iteration (
5.18

5.5
Convergence of WDA-nepv

In this section, we provide the convergence analysis of the proposed WDA-nepv.
We first note that given
\boldsymbol{P}_{k}
, the inner optimization (Algorithm
1
) to compute the OT matrices
\boldsymbol{T}^{c,c^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})
is globally convergent.
Furthermore, for applications of WDA, the desired accuracy is typically low, say at the range of
tol=10^{-3}
to
10^{-5}
.
Therefore, for simplicity, in our analysis we assume that the OT matrices are accurately computed so that they can be regarded as “
exact
”.
Similarly, the SCF iteration for TRopts (Algorithm
2
) in the outer optimization is also globally convergent so that
\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
is accurately computed to be regarded as “
exact
”. Therefore, we have

\underbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1})}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\right]}_{=:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}}\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{k},

(5.19)

where the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{k}
consist of the
p
largest eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}
.

Next, we make the following so-called monotonicity assumption whose rigorous verification remains open but is numerically demonstrated throughout all our numerical tests. Subsequently, we introduce the Ky Fan Trace Theorem
[
17
]
, which plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Assumption 1
.

For orthonormal
\boldsymbol{P},\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
and the function
f(\boldsymbol{P})
defined in (
3.11
), if

\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}})}\geq f(\boldsymbol{P})+\eta,

(5.20)

where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, then

f(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}})\geq f(\boldsymbol{P})+c\eta

(5.21)

for some constant
c>0
, independent of
\boldsymbol{P},\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}
.

Theorem 5.2

(Ky Fan Trace)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are ordered as
\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{A})\geq\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{A})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}(\boldsymbol{A})
. Then, for
1\leq p\leq d
, the sum of the
p
largest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{A}
and the sum of the
p
smallest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{A}
correspond to the following maximization and the minimization of the trace operator, respectively.

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{A}),

(5.22)

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{P})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=d-p+1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{A}).

(5.23)

A solution
\boldsymbol{P}
satisfies (
5.22
) if and only if it is an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix corresponding to the
p
largest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{P}
. Similarly, a solution
\boldsymbol{P}
satisfies (
5.23
) if and only if it is an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix corresponding to the
p
smallest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{P}
.

The convergence of WDA-nepv is stated in the following theorem. It provides a theoretical justification of the SCF framework for solving NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
).

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose Assumption (
1
) holds and that the sum of the
p
smallest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})
is uniformly bounded below for all orthonormal
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
. Similarly, suppose that the sum of the
p
largest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})
is uniformly bounded above for all orthonormal
\boldsymbol{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
.
Let the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
be generated by WDA-nepv (Algorithm
3
).
The following statements hold.

(a)

\{f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is monotonically increasing and convergent.

(b)

\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
has a convergent subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{I}}
, converging to
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
, and

\lim_{k\to\infty}f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})=f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*}).

(c)

\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}:=\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})=\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})-f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\,\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})

largest eigenvalues.

(d)

If

, then

Proof.

(a)

Recall that the projection
\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
is computed exactly to form an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix corresponding to the
p
largest eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}
. Therefore, by Theorem

\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
is a solution to the following optimization:

\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}\boldsymbol{P}),

(5.25)

and we have from (

0=\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1})=\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{k})\geq\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{k}\boldsymbol{P}_{k}),

which yields (

,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}=\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}
, and
\eta=0
. Hence, by assumption

According to (

\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})\boldsymbol{P})
is not smaller than the sum of the
p
smallest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})
. Since the sum of the

is assumed to be uniformly bounded from below for any orthonormal

is monotonically increasing and bounded and hence convergent.

(b)

Since
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a bounded sequence, it has a convergent subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{I}}
, converging to an orthonormal
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
. As

is continuous with respect to
\boldsymbol{P}
, by item (a), we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})=\lim_{\mathbb{I}\ni k\to\infty}f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})=f(\lim_{\mathbb{I}\ni k\to\infty}\boldsymbol{P}_{k})=f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*}).

(c)

Let

\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P})

Recall that we have (
5.19
).
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{I}}
, as a bounded sequence, has a convergent subsequence
\{\boldsymbol{P}_{k+1}\}_{k\in\mathbb{I}_{1}}
, converging to an orthonormal

, where

. Letting

\underbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})-\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}\,\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\right]}_{=:\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}{\Lambda}_{*},

where the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{*}
consist of the

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}
.
We claim that
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix of

associated with its

is.
Otherwise, according to (

\eta=\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})-\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*}^{T}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}\boldsymbol{P}_{*})>0,

yielding

\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}=f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})+\frac{\eta}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\boldsymbol{P}_{*})}\geq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})+\frac{\eta}{\omega}.

By continuity, there is an

such that

\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1})}\geq\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\eta}{\omega},\,\,

and

f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})\geq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})-\frac{1}{3}\frac{c\eta}{\omega}.

Therefore,

\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1})}\geq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})+\frac{\eta}{\omega}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\eta}{\omega}=f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta}{\omega}\geq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})+\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta}{\omega}.

By assumption
1
, we get

f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}+1})\geq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k_{0}})+c\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta}{\omega}\geq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})-\frac{1}{3}\frac{c\eta}{\omega}+c\frac{2}{3}\frac{\eta}{\omega}=f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})+\frac{1}{3}\frac{c\eta}{\omega}>f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*}),

contradicting all
f(\boldsymbol{P}_{k})\leq f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})
.

The contradiction indicates that
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}
associated with its
p
largest eigenvalues, and hence
\eta=0
, i.e.,

\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*})}=f(\boldsymbol{P}_{*}),

implying
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}=\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
. Therefore, in conclusion,
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
is an orthonormal eigenbasis matrix of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
associated with its
p
largest eigenvalues.

(d)

In proving item (c), we concluded that
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{H}}_{*}=\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
and so we have
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{*}
upon letting
\mathbb{I}_{1}\ni k\to\infty
in (
5.19
), where the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{*}
consist of the
p
largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
.
By item (c),
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}
are two orthonormal eigenbasis matrices of
\boldsymbol{H}_{*}
associated with its
p
largest eigenvalues. By the assumption that
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{p}(\boldsymbol{H}_{*})-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{p+1}(\boldsymbol{H}_{*})>0
, we conclude
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{*}=\boldsymbol{P}_{*}\boldsymbol{Q}
where
\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is an orthogonal matrix. Hence, we have

\boldsymbol{P}_{*}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{P}=I_{p}}\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{b}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\boldsymbol{P})}{\mathrm{Tr}(\boldsymbol{P}^{T}\boldsymbol{C}_{w}(\boldsymbol{P}_{*})\boldsymbol{P})}.

Non-optimal convergence.

Finding a global optimizer of a non-convex problem is NP-hard. Algorithms for non-convex problems are prone to converge towards a local optimizer, and consequently, lead to a suboptimal solution. A suboptimal solution is not desirable in optimization and for the task at hand. For instance, in a classification task, relying on a suboptimal projection often proves insufficient for identifying the subspace that effectively discriminates the projected data points.

6.3
WDA-nepv: Classification Accuracy

One of the many goals of DR methods is to derive a projection such that the projected data vectors maintain or amplify the coherent structure of the original dataset. For a supervised linear DR method, one hopes to obtain an optimal projection matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
such that the class structure of the original data vectors in the projected subspace is more pronounced. For this reason, the effectiveness of a supervised linear DR method is often measured by the accuracy of classification on the projected data vectors. Measuring the accuracy of NTRopt-WDA (
3.11
) is no exception. To evaluate the classification accuracy of an algorithm, we follow the following conventional steps:

1.

Randomly divide a given dataset into a training dataset and a testing dataset, with equal size of
50\%
each.

2.

Compute the optimal projection matrix
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
using the training dataset.

3.

Project the testing dataset onto the lower-dimensional subspace using the optimal projection matrix
\boldsymbol{P}_{*}
.

4.

Employ the K-Nearest-Neighbors classifier (KNN) on the projected testing dataset to compute the classification accuracy.

The classification accuracy is quantified in terms of prediction error. A smaller error indicates better performance.

7
Conclusion and Future Works

Conclusion.

We presented WDA-nepv, a new algorithm for Wasserstein discriminant analysis. WDA-nepv is a bi-level nonlinear eigenvector algorithm that fully utilizes the bi-level structure of WDA.
It employs an NEPv to compute the OT matrices at the inner optimization and another NEPv for the trace-ratio optimization at the outer iteration.
Both NEPvs can be solved efficiently by the SCF iteration. Unlike the existing algorithms WDA-gd and WDA-eig, WDA-nepv is derivative-free and surrogate-model-free. Specifically, in contrast to WDA-gd, WDA-nepv has no costs of computing the derivatives, which leads to lower running time. In contrast to WDA-eig, WDA-nepv solves the original problem directly, which results in higher accuracy. Additionally, we proposed an efficient level-3 BLAS implementation for computing cross-covariance matrices. We provided a convergence analysis of our algorithm, justifying the utilization of the SCF iteration for solving NEPvs. Numerical experiments demonstrate that WDA-nepv attains competitive classification accuracy and is scalable.

Future works.

A main advantage of WDA is the ability to control global and local relations of the data points by varying the regularization parameter
\lambda
. However, there is no set technique to determine an optimal
\lambda_{*}
. Our numerical experiments indicate that
\lambda
in the range from
0.01
