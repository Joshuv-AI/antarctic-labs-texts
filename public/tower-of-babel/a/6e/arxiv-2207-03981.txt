Perturbation of Systems with a First Integral: Motion on the Reeb Graph
Mark I. Freidlin

Abstract




2022

Abstract

We consider the long-time behavior of systems close to a system with a smooth first integral. Under certain assumptions, the limiting behavior, to some extent, turns out to be universal: it is determined by the first integral, the deterministic perturbation, and the initial point. Furthermore, it is the same for a broad class of noises. In particular, the long-time behavior of a deterministic system can, in a sense, be stochastic and stochastic systems can have a reduced stochasticity. The limiting distribution is calculated explicitly.

Introduction

Let
f(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
be a regular enough vector field and
H(x)
be a smooth first integral of the system

\dot{X}(t)=f(X(t)),\quad X(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(1)

This means that
H(X(t))\equiv H(x)
, what is equivalent to the identity
\nabla H(x)\cdot f(x)\equiv 0
. Consider a small perturbation of (
1
):

\dot{\tilde{X}}^{\varepsilon}(t)=f(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t))+\varepsilon b(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)),\quad\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(0)=x,

(2)

where
b(x)
is a bounded smooth vector field with
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.

An important class of dynamical system having a first integral is given by the Hamiltonian systems:

Let
d=2n
,
x=(p_{1},...,p_{n},q_{1},...,q_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,
f(x)=\overline{\nabla}H(x)=(-\nabla_{q}H(p,q),\nabla_{p}H(p,q))
, where
\nabla_{q}H
and
\nabla_{p}H
are the gradients of
H(p,q)
in
q
and
p
respectively. Then the system (
1
) has the form

\dot{X}(t)=\overline{\nabla}H(X(t)),\quad X(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}

(3)

and the perturbed system is

\dot{\tilde{X}}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\overline{\nabla}H(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t))+\varepsilon b(\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)),\quad\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(0)=x.

(4)

The Hamiltonian
H(x)
is a first integral of system (
3
). Of course,
\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)
will be uniformly close to
X(t)
on each finite time interval
[0,T]
as
\varepsilon
is close to zero. But
\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)
can deviate from
X(t)
on a distance of order 1 as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
on time intervals of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
. To describe the deviations, it is convenient to make a time change
X^{\varepsilon}(t)=\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t/\varepsilon)
. This yields

\dot{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\overline{\nabla}H(X^{\varepsilon}(t))+b(X^{\varepsilon}(t)),\quad X^{\varepsilon}(0)=x.

(5)

If
n=1
,
\lim H(x)=\infty
, and
H(x)
has just one minimum,
H(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})
describes the evolution of the slow component of
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. The classical averaging principle allows to calculate
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}H(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})
for any
t
. But, even in the
n=1
case, the standard averaging does not allow to describe the behavior of
H(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
for large enough
t
if
H(x)
has saddle points. The problem should be, in a sense, regularized. For the case of one degree of freedom, this was done in
bib2
;
bib9
. A noise of small intensity
\delta
was introduced in system (
5
). Then, the solution and its slow component became stochastic processes depending on
\varepsilon
and
\delta
. It was shown that there exists the double limit of the slow component as, first,
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and then
\delta\downarrow 0
. A similar approach is used here in the multidimensional case.

In reality, we always deal with systems perturbed by a small noise: the initial point can be perturbed by a small random variable and/or the noise can be added to the equation. Let a white-noise-type perturbation be added to the equation. Moreover, let the noise consist of two components: Component 1 is reversable and preserves the energy (the Hamiltonian
H(x))
, Component 2 destroys the energy integral
H(x)
.

More precisely, instead of dynamical system (
5
), we observe the diffusion process
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
governed by the operator
L^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}
,

\displaystyle L^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\overline{\nabla}H(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)+\frac{\varkappa}{2}\text{div}\left(a^{(1)}(x)\nabla u(x)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\delta\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{div}\left(a^{(2)}(x)\nabla u(x)\right)+\beta(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)\right]+b(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\overline{\nabla}H\cdot\nabla u+\varkappa L_{1}u\right](x)+\delta L_{2}u(x)+b(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)

(6)

Here
a^{(1)}(x)
and
a^{(2)}(x)
are
2n\times 2n
-matrices with smooth and bounded entries,
\beta(x)
is a smooth vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Parameters
\varkappa
and
\delta
characterize the intensities of random perturbations. We assume that

1.

H(x)
is smooth enough, has a finite number of critical points that are non-degenerate, and
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty

2.

a^{(1)}(x)\nabla H(x)\equiv 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},a^{(1)}(x)e\cdot e\geq\lambda^{(1)}\lvert e\rvert^{2}
for each
e
such that
e\cdot\nabla H(X)=0
with some
\lambda^{(1)}(x)>0

x
is not a critical point.

3.

a^{(2)}(x)
is positive definite, smooth, and bounded for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

Assumptions on the coefficients of the operator

are formulated in Section 3 in more detail.

One can check that, under the above assumptions with respect to the matrix
a^{(1)}(x)
, the process
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa}(t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
governed by the operator
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\varkappa L_{1}+\overline{\nabla}H(x)\cdot\nabla\right]
preserves
H(x)
:
H(X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa}(t))\equiv H(x)
for all

with probability 1 (see
bib7
).

We observe the process
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)
on a time interval
[0,T]
. It has a fast component that is roughly a motion on connected components
C_{k}(z)
of the level set
C(z)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:H(x)=z\}
, and a slow component that can be described by a motion on the graph
\Gamma
obtained after identification of points of each connected component of the Hamiltonian (see more details in
bib7
and in the Section 2).

The fast component on
C_{k}(z)
can be approximated for small
\delta>0
by the process
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa}_{*}(t)
,
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa}_{*}(0)=x\in C_{k}(z)
. The latter process has a unique invariant probability measure on
C_{k}(z)
. If
C_{k}(z)
does not contain critical points, this measure has the density

m_{z,k}(x):=\left(\lvert\nabla H(x)\rvert\oint_{C_{k}(z)}\frac{dl}{\lvert\nabla H(x)\rvert}\right)^{-1},x\in C_{k}(z),

and the process
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa}(t)
on
C_{k}(z)
is ergodic. This allows us to use the standard averaging inside the edges of
\Gamma
and describe the limiting behavior as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
of the slow component inside each edge

. Since
m_{z,k}(x)
is independent of
\varkappa>0
, the limiting slow component is also independent of
\varkappa
.

Let
Y:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\Gamma
be the projection of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
onto
\Gamma
:
Y(x)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is the point on
\Gamma
corresponding to the level set component containing
x
. Let us number the edges of
\Gamma
. Then the slow component
Y(X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t))=(z^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t),k^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t))
where
z^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)=H(X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t))
,
k^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)
is the number of the edge containing
Y(X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t))
.

It turns out that the exterior vertices (vertices corresponding to the extremums of
H(x)
) are not accessible in finite time for process
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)
, but the interior vertices (vertices corresponding to the saddle points) are accessible. This means that the standard averaging principle is not sufficient for description of the limiting slow motion. One should describe what the trajectory of the limiting slow motion is doing after hitting an interior vertex. The case of Hamiltonian systems with one degree of freedom
(n=1)
,
\varkappa=0
, and
b(x)=0
was considered in detail (see
bib9
and the references there). We calculated there the gluing conditions, at interior vertices that, together with standard averaging inside the edges, uniquely describe the limiting slow motion. It turns out that these gluing conditions are defined by only the coefficients
a^{(2)}(x)
; they are independent of the drift.

These results allowed to calculate, in the case of one degree of freedom, the double limit
\overline{y}(t)
of the slow component
Y(X^{\varepsilon,0,\delta}(t))
as, first
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and then
\delta\rightarrow 0

bib2
. It turns out that the limiting process
\overline{y}(t)
on
\Gamma
is deterministic inside the edges, but at certain interior vertices (three edges are attached to each interior vertex), we will have a residual stochasticity: the process
\overline{y}(t)
enters such a vertex along one of attached edges and leaves the vertex along the other two edges with a non-trivial probability distribution. So that the process that we observe for small
\varepsilon
and
\delta
will have certain stochasticity. Moreover, the distribution between these two exit edges is independent of the noise: it will be the same for various matrices
a^{(2)}(x)
(non-degenerated) and fields
\beta(x)
. If just the initial point is perturbed, say
X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(0)
is distributed uniformly in the
\delta
-neighborhood of
x
, the double limit of the slow component as first
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and then
\delta\rightarrow 0
may not exist. But it exists under reasonable additional assumptions and, if the double limit exists, it coincides with the double limit described above. This means that stochasticity of the long-time behavior of
\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in the case of one degree of freedom is an intrinsic property of the deterministic system
\tilde{X}^{\varepsilon}(t)
defined by (
4
). This stochasticity is actually caused by the instabilities in the Hamiltonian system.

The goal of this paper is to show that a similar effect can be observed in the case of multidimensional systems with a conservation law. We consider in detail the case of Hamiltonian systems. The general systems with conservation law are shortly considered in the last section.

In the case of one degree of freedom, under our assumptions, one ergodic invariant probability measure of the non-perturbed system is concentrated on each connected level set component. Therefore, even if
\varkappa=0
, the standard averaging principle allows us to calculate the limiting slow motion for
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
inside the edges of
\Gamma
. In the case of many degrees of freedom, as a rule, there are many invariant probability measures on the connected level sets components. Therefore, we have to consider
qualified
random perturbations – perturbations consisting of two parts: one of intensity
\varkappa
which reserves the first integral and another one of intensity
\delta
which destroy the first integral. We assume that the first part is non-degenerate on the level set components not containing critical points, and that
\delta\ll\varkappa
(see the description of qualified perturbations in Section 3).

One should note that the perturbations can have different origin, and consideration of qualified perturbations is natural. For instance, fluctuations of the exterior magnetic field are not changing the energy
H(x)
. Another example: perturbations of Landau-Lifshitz equation (see, for instance,
bib5
and references there).

The next question concerns the structure of the graph in multidimensional case. If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, each non-degenerate critical point of
H(x)
is either an extremum of
H(x)
, in this case just one edge attached to corresponding vertex of
\Gamma
, or a saddle point which corresponds to a vertex with 3 attached edges. If
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>2
, the situation is more sophisticated. It is described in Section 2.

Limiting behavior of the process
\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)=Y(X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\gamma}(t))
on the graph
\Gamma
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and
\varkappa,\delta>0
are fixed is considered in Section 3. The process
\mathcal{Y}^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)
converges weakly in the space of continuous function
\varphi:[0,T]\rightarrow\Gamma,0<T<\infty
to a diffusion process
Y^{\delta}(t)
on
\Gamma
. The limit
\mathcal{Y}(t)
of
\mathcal{Y}^{\delta}(t)
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
is considered in Section 4. We will see that the stochastic process
\mathcal{Y}(t)
on
\Gamma
will be deterministic inside each edge of
\Gamma
. But when trajectory of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
hits some of interior vertices it can go to other edges attached to this vertex with certain non-trivial distribution. This distribution will be the same for various qualified random perturbations. In this restricted sense the residual stochasticity is an intrinsic property of deterministic system (
4
) in the multidimensional case.

In the last section, some generalizations of problems studied in the previous sections are considered briefly. In particular, the metastable behavior of the perturbed system on time intervals growing together with
\delta^{-1}
and some non-Hamiltonian systems with a first integral, briefly considered there.

Small deterministic perturbations of stochastic systems are also considered briefly in Section 5. If the stochastic systems has a first integral
H(x)
, the long-time evolution of the perturbed system can be described by a stochastic process on the Reeb graph related to
H(x)
. This stochastic process is deterministic inside the edges and has a stochastic behavior at some interior vertices.

Finally, a general remark: as was explained in
bib4
, one of the main characteristics of the long-time evolution of a perturbed system is given by the limiting motion (in an appropriate time scale) of the projection of perturbed process on the simplex
\mathcal{M}
of invariant probability measures of the non-perturbed system. First integrals are, sometimes but not always, a convenient tool to describe this simplex. For instance, no smooth first integral can be introduced for systems with several asymptotically stable regimes
bib9
or for some area-preserving systems on torus
bib3
. In our case, the points of the graph parametrize the ergodic measures of the system – extreme points of
\mathcal{M}
. The Markov process
\mathcal{Y}(t)
induces a dynamical system on
\mathcal{M}
.

Structure of the Reeb Graph

Let
H(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},
be a twice continuously differentiable function and
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty

H(x)
has a finite number of critical points

Consider the level set

The following three propositions can be useful to understand the structure of the Reeb graph for a Morse function
H(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty

Proposition 1
.

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{H}
be the Reeb graph of a Morse function
H(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty,d>1
. Then
\Gamma
is a tree. Each vertex of
\Gamma
has order 1,2 or 3 and has one of the five types
\frac{0}{1},\frac{1}{0},\frac{1}{1},\frac{1}{2}
, and
\frac{2}{1}
.

Proof.

The first statement of the proposition is well known: it follows from the fact that the first Batty number
\beta_{1}(\Gamma)
of the set
\Gamma
is less than or equal to the first Batty number of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The second statement is proved in
bib7
. Note that the Reeb graph of a Morse function on a space with more sophisticated topology than
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for instance, on a torus, is not necessarily a tree.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{H}
be the Reeb graph of a Morse function
H(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty

(a)

the number of vertices

of the type
\frac{1}{2}
in
\Gamma
is equal to the number

of the vertices of type
\frac{1}{0}
in it, minus 1 (
\#(\frac{1}{2},\Gamma)
is equal to the number of local minima of

minus 1);

(b)

the number

of vertices of the types
\frac{2}{1}
in
\Gamma

.

(a) Let us prove our statement by induction in the number of edges. If the number of edges is equal to 1, the lower vertex of this edge is of type
\frac{1}{0}
, and there are no vertices of type
\frac{1}{2}
:
\#(\frac{1}{2},\Gamma)=0=\#(\frac{1}{0},\Gamma)-1
.

Now suppose that our statement is true for all graphs with less than

\Gamma
have

. Then

is a vertex of the type
\frac{1}{0}
. Let

be the edge whose lower end is

.

, or
\frac{2}{1}
.

. This case is already considered. In the case of

, obtaining a graph

our statement is true.

If

is of the type
\frac{1}{2}
, we also delete from

.

Finally, suppose

is of the type
\frac{2}{1}
. Let

, no maxima, and 3 saddle points. The results of Section 2 imply that one of the saddle points corresponds
to a
\frac{1}{2}
-type vertex (denote this vertex by
O
), another - to a vertex of
\frac{2}{1}
-type, and yet another - to a
\frac{1}{1}
-type vertex.

Let
b(x)=(b_{1}(x),b_{2}(x),b_{3}(x),b_{4}(x))
where
(b_{1}(x),b_{2}(x))=-Ap
, where
A=(A_{ij})
is a constant non-degenerate
2\times 2
-matrix with negative trace:
\text{tr}A=A_{11}+A_{22}=-\lambda<0
.

To describe the process
\mathcal{Y}(t)
in an explicit way, introduce new coordinates on
\Gamma:(z,i)-(v,i)
, where the second coordinate
i
is the same, and
v=v_{i}(z)
is the volume of domain
G_{i}(z)
bounded by
C_{i}(z)
. Since div
b(x)=-2\lambda
,

\hat{b}_{i}(z)=-2\lambda v(z)
. The evolution of
\mathcal{Y}(t)
inside an edge
I_{i}
is described by equations (
13
). In our case these equations have the form

v_{i}^{\prime}(z_{t})\dot{z}_{t}=-2\lambda v_{i}(z_{t})

(24)

Let
\tilde{v}_{i}(t)=v_{i}(z_{t})
. Then (
24
) implies that
\tilde{v}_{i}(s+t)=\tilde{v}_{i}(s)e^{-2\lambda t}
until
\mathcal{Y}(s)
and
\mathcal{Y}(t)
belong to
I_{i}
. When
\mathcal{Y}(t)
hits a vertex of
\frac{1}{1}
-type or
\frac{2}{1}
-type, it goes without any delay to the unique exit edge attached to this vertex. If
\mathcal{Y}(t)
hits vertex
O
, which has two exit edges: an edge
I_{i_{1}}
leading to the minimum
(0,0,q^{(1)})
of
H(x)
and an edge
I_{i_{2}}
leading to
(0,0,q^{(2)})
, then
\mathcal{Y}(t)
without delay goes to one of these edges with probabilities

p_{i_{1}}(0)=\frac{v_{1}}{v_{1}+v_{2}},p_{i_{2}}(0)=\frac{v_{2}}{v_{1}+v_{2}}.

Here
v_{1}
is the volume of one of two domains attached to
Y^{-1}(0)
and bounded by
C(H(0))=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4}:H(x)<H(0)\}
, namely the domain containing
Y^{-1}(I_{i_{1}})
,
v_{2}
is the volume of another domain attached to
Y^{-1}(0)
.

If trajectory
\mathcal{Y}(t),\mathcal{Y}(0)=y
, comes to
O
, then
\Phi(y)
consists of two points. In this case, for large

and
0<\varepsilon\ll\delta\ll 1,X^{\varepsilon,\varkappa,\delta}(t)
is distributed between small neighborhoods of points
(0,0,q^{(1)}),(0,0,q^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
with probabilities
p_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{v_{1}+v_{2}},i\in\{1,2\}
, If
\mathcal{Y}(t),\mathcal{Y}(0)=y
, does not go through

\Phi(y)
consists just of one exterior vertex, and

will be close to the corresponding minimum point of

.

5
Remarks and Generalizations

1.
Theorems
3
and
4
describe the double limit of
\mathcal{Y}(X^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{t})
on any finite, independent of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, time interval
[0,T]
. Similar results hold in the case
T=T(\delta)
growing as
\delta\rightarrow 0
but slow enough: if
\delta\ln T(\delta)\rightarrow 0
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
. If we are interested in longer time intervals, for instance, in deviations of order 1 as
\delta\rightarrow 0
from an asymptotically stable regime or in transitions between such stable regimes, one should take into account large deviations of the process
\mathcal{Y}^{\delta}(t)
from
\mathcal{Y}(t)
. Those deviations are governed by the action functional
\frac{1}{\delta}S_{0,T}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2\delta}\int_{0}^{T}\lvert(\overline{a^{(2)}}_{i}(\varphi_{s}))^{-1/2}(\dot{\varphi}_{s}-\overline{b}_{i}(\varphi_{s}))\rvert^{2}ds
(see
bib9
). Using this action functional, one can describe various asymptotics in the exit problem from a neighborhood of an asymptotically stable regime as well as the metastability effects for different time scales (compare with
bib9
).

2.
To be specific, we considered deterministic perturbations of Hamiltonian system by a deterministic vector field
b(x)
combined with a qualified noise. But, actually, our approach works in a more general situation, for instance, in the case of a divergence free dynamical system having a smooth first integral. Consider, for example, a system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\dot{X}(t)=\nabla F(X(t))\times\ell(X(t)),\quad X(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(25)

The function
F(x)
and the vector field
\ell(x)
assumed to be smooth enough. It is easy to see that
F(x)
is a first integral for system
(
25
). Let
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}F(x)=\infty
and assume also that curl
\ell(x)\equiv 0
. Then,

\text{div}\left(\nabla F(x)\times\ell(x)\right)=\ell(x)\cdot\text{curl}\nabla F(x)-\nabla F(x)\cdot\text{curl}\ell(x)=0,

so that the vector field
\nabla F(x)\times\ell(x)
is divergence free. Thus, the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is invariant for system
(
25
). In this case, the long-time evolution of the system obtained from (
23
) after addition to right-hand side a deterministic perturbation combined with a qualified noise can be described in the same way as in the case of Hamiltonian system. Using the results of
bib8

instead of
bib7
, perturbation of more general systems can be considered.

3.
Up to now, we considered perturbations of deterministic systems. Sometimes, perturbations of stochastic systems are of interest. Let
X_{t}
be the Markov process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
governed by the operator
L
,
Lu(x)=\frac{1}{2}\text{div}(a_{1}(x)\nabla u(x))
, where
a(x)
is a non-negative definite
d\times d
-matrix.

Assume a Morse function
H(x)
,
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}=\infty
, exists such that
a_{1}(x)\nabla H(x)=0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This condition implies that
H(x)
is a first integral for the process
X_{t}
(see, for instance,
bib7
). Let the process
X_{t}
be perturbed by a small vector field combined with even smaller noise. More precisely, let after an appropriate time change, the perturbed process
\dot{X}_{t}^{\varepsilon,\delta}
is governed by the operator
L^{\varepsilon,\delta}
:

\displaystyle L^{\varepsilon,\delta}u(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Lu+b(x)\cdot\nabla u+\frac{\delta}{2}\text{div}(a_{2}(x)\nabla u(x)),

where
b(x),x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is a smooth vector field and
a_{2}(x)
is positive definite
d\times d
-matrix.

Let
\Gamma
be the Reeb graph for
H(x)
, and
Y:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\Gamma
is the projection of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on
\Gamma
. Then one can expect that the process
\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon,\delta}=Y(X_{t}^{\varepsilon,\delta})
converge weakly as first
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and then
\delta\rightarrow 0
to a Markov process
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
on
\Gamma
. The characteristics of
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
can be calculated similarly to the case considered in Sections 3 and 4.

4.
The results of this paper can be reformulated in the terms of partial differential equations, and our approach can be useful in some PDE problems. Consider, for example, the following quasi-linear Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u^{\varepsilon,\delta}(t,x)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\text{div}(a_{1}(x)\nabla u^{\varepsilon,\delta})+b(x,u^{\varepsilon,\delta})\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon,\delta}+\frac{\delta}{2}\text{div}(a_{2}(x)\nabla u),

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon,\delta}(0,x)=g(x),

where
a_{1}(x)
a non-negative definite and
a_{2}(x)
is a positive definite matrices. Assume that a Morse function
H(x)
,
\lim_{\lvert x\rvert\rightarrow\infty}H(x)=\infty
, exists such that
a_{1}(x)\nabla H(x)\equiv 0
. Then, one can expect that when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
we get a Cauchy problem for a quasi-linear equation with the viscosity of order
\delta
and gluing conditions independent of
u
. Then, taking
\delta\rightarrow 0
we get a first order quasi-linear equation with nonlinear gluing conditions. The solutions of this equation usually have discontinuities – shock waves. One can expect that in this way, we can get some information about shock waves in the multi-dimensional equation (
24
).

5.
Systems with several first integrals can be considered. In this case, the limiting process lives on an open book space defined by the first integrals (see
bib9
, Ch. 8 and 9). Then the limiting process will be a deterministic motion inside the pages with gluing conditions on the binding of the open book.
