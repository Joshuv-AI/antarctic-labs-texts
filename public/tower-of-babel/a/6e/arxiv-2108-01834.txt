A Supersymmetric Approach to the Problem of Micro-bending Attenuation in Optical Waveguides Preprint: APS/123-QED
Stuart Ward 1,2, Rouzbeh Allahverdi 1, Arash Mafi 1,2

Abstract
Micro-bending is a well-known source of loss in optical waveguides. By treating the micro-bending as a stochastic process, the problem of loss mitigation can be modeled in terms of a Fokker-Planck equation. Given an initial refractive index profile, and taking micro-bending into account, we develop a formalism to derive a new refractive index profile which potentially results in less loss. Our formalism is based on applying the techniques of Supersymmetric Quantum Mechanics to a Fokker-Planck equation that is associated with a particular refractive index profile. We derive a non-linear differential equation, whose solutions determine whether an index profile can undergo a supersymmetric transformation that results in less loss. As an explicit example, we consider a monomial index profile. We show that there exists a range of values for the monomial exponent which results in the new index profile having less loss.

II.1
Supersymmetric Quantum Mechanics

Consider the following pair of Hamiltonians, written in terms of general creation and annihilation operators,

H_{-}\equiv A^{\dagger}A~~~,~~~H_{+}\equiv AA^{\dagger}.

(1)

The creation and annihilation operators are defined as

A=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\frac{d}{dx}+W(x)~~~,~~~A^{\dagger}=-\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\frac{d}{dx}+W(x),

(2)

where
W(x)
is a real valued function known as the Superpotential. Plugging Eq. (
2
) into Eq. (
1
) gives

H_{\pm}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V_{\pm}(x),

(3)

where the potentials
V_{+}(x)
and
V_{-}(x)
are known as the SUSY partner potentials. The partner potentials are given in terms of the superpotential as

V_{\pm}(x)=W(x)^{2}\pm\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\frac{dW(x)}{dx}.

(4)

Depending on the asymptotic behavior of the superpotential
W(x)
, a normalizable ground state of the Hamiltonian
H_{-}
, with energy eigenvalue equal to zero (
H_{-}\psi_{-}^{0}=0
), can be obtained by setting
A\psi_{-}^{0}=0.
This yields, up to a normalization constant,

\psi_{-}^{0}\sim\exp\big(-\frac{\sqrt{2m}}{\hbar}\int W(x)dx\big).

(5)

For
\psi_{-}^{0}
to be normalizable, the superpotential must take on a positive value at
+\infty
and a negative value at
-\infty
. When this is the case we say that SUSY is unbroken. Note that in the case of unbroken-SUSY, a superpotential can be derived from the normalized ground state wavefunction via

W=-\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\frac{\psi_{0}^{\prime}}{\psi_{0}}.

(6)

If
W(x)
does not allow for a normalizable ground state, the superpotential is said to exhibit broken SUSY. When this is the case, we will see that this results in the spectra being identical for
H_{-}
and
H_{+}
.

We can now relate the spectra of the Hamiltonians as follows. Let
H_{+}
act on
A\psi_{-}^{n}
, where
\psi_{-}^{n}
is the nth eigenstate of the Hamiltonian
H_{-}
. We find an isospectral relationship between the two Hamiltonians, namely

\displaystyle H_{+}(A\psi_{-}^{n})=AA^{\dagger}A\psi_{-}^{n}=A(H_{-}\psi_{-}^{n})=E_{-}^{n}A\psi_{-}^{n}\,.

Similarly, for
H_{-}
acting on
A^{\dagger}\psi_{+}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle H_{-}(A^{\dagger}\psi_{+}^{n})=A^{\dagger}AA^{\dagger}\psi_{+}^{n}=A(H_{+}\psi_{+}^{n})=E_{+}^{n}A^{\dagger}\psi_{+}^{n}\,.

This gives an exact isospectral relation between the two partner Hamiltonians in the case of broken SUSY. For the case of unbroken SUSY, we see that the ground state
\psi_{-}^{0}
cannot be mapped to a state of the
H_{+}
Hamiltonian. Thus there exists a one-to-one mapping between the excited eigenstates of
H_{-}
and
H_{+}
, but the
H_{-}
Hamiltonian now contains an extra state that does not get mapped to
H_{+}
. Since this extra state is the ground state, it has the lowest energy eigenvalue in the spectra.

There is a close analogy between the Schrödinger equation for a particle in a potential

The one-to-one analogy between the Schrödinger equation

-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\dfrac{\partial^{2}A(x)}{\partial x^{2}}+V(x)A(x)=EA(x),

and the Helmholtz equation Eq. (

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\hbar^{2}k_{0}^{2}}{2m}\Big(n_{0}^{2}-n^{2}(x)\Big)\,,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\hbar^{2}k_{0}^{2}}{2m}\Big(n_{0}^{2}-n^{2}_{\rm eff}\Big)\,,

(10)

where

Guided waves with

For simplicity in establishing the analogy in optics, it is convenient to set

is an arbitrary length scale

\displaystyle V_{\pm}(x)=k_{0}^{2}L^{2}_{c}\Big(n_{0}^{2}-n_{\pm}^{2}(x)\Big)=W^{2}(x)\pm L_{c}\frac{dW(x)}{dx}\,,

\displaystyle E_{\pm}=k_{0}^{2}L^{2}_{c}\Big(n_{0}^{2}-n^{2}_{\rm eff}\Big)\,.

In a typical waveguide structure,

\displaystyle n_{\pm}^{2}(x)=n_{0}^{2}+(k_{0}L_{c})^{-2}\Big(-W^{2}(x)\mp L_{c}\frac{dW(x)}{dx}\Big)\,,

\displaystyle\beta^{2}_{\pm}=\beta^{2}_{0}-E_{\pm}/L^{2}_{c}\,.

(14)

The above correspondence shows how the techniques of SUSY-QM can be imported to the study of guided wave optics.

II.3
Supersymmetry and Stochastic Processes

The relationship between SUSY-QM and classical stochastic dynamics has been well established

;
PolottoII
;
Bern
. For our purposes we review the connection between the Fokker-Planck equation and SUSY-QM.

Consider the set of Langevin equations of the following form

\frac{\partial x_{i}}{\partial t}=A_{i}(\textbf{x},t)+\sum_{k}B_{ik}(\textbf{x},t)\zeta_{k}(t),

(15)

with
\{x_{i}\}=\textbf{x}.
Taking the mean and auto-correlation to be defined as

\displaystyle\big<\zeta_{k}(t)\big>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

\displaystyle\big<\zeta_{i}(t_{1})\zeta_{j}(t_{2})\big>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2D\delta_{ij}\delta{(t_{1}-t_{2})}\,,

(16)

the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation
FP
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P(\textbf{x},t)}{\partial t}=\,

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\{[A_{i}(\textbf{x},t)+D\sum_{jk}B_{jk}(\textbf{x},t)\frac{\partial B_{ik}(\textbf{x},t)}{\partial x_{i}}]P\}\,

\displaystyle+D\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\{[\sum_{k}B_{ik}(\textbf{x},t)B_{jk}(\textbf{x},t)]P\}\,.

(17)

For the case of one spatial and one time dimension the FPE can be written as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}P(x,x_{0};t)=\dfrac{D}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}P(x,x_{0};t)-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}F(x)P(x,x_{0};t)\,.

(18)

This is a diffusion equation, where
D/2
is the diffusion constant and
F
is the drift coefficient. This equation can be transformed to a Schrödinger-like equation if we let

P(x,x_{0};t)=\exp\Big\{-\dfrac{1}{D}[U(x)-U(x_{0})]\Big\}~K(x,t),

(19)

where

U(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}~dz~F(z).

(20)

The transformed equation is given by

-D\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}K(x,t)=\Big(\dfrac{-D^{2}}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+W^{2}(x)+\dfrac{D}{\sqrt{2}}W^{\prime}(x)\Big)K(x,t),

(21)

where
F(x)=\sqrt{2}W(x)
. Equation (
21
) can be identified with the Schrödinger equation if
D=\hbar/\sqrt{m}
and we take the time to be imaginary. Clearly, the SUSY partner to this equation can be written by exchanging
W^{\prime}

with
-W^{\prime}
in Eq. (
21
).

The eigenstates of the SUSY partner Hamiltonians for Eq. (
21
) satisfy the following equation

H_{\pm}\phi^{\pm}_{n}(x)=\lambda^{\pm}_{n}\phi^{\pm}_{n}(x),

(22)

where

H_{\pm}=\dfrac{-D}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+W^{2}(x)\pm\dfrac{D}{\sqrt{2}}W^{\prime}(x).

(23)

Similar to the Schrödinger equation, where the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian evolve with a phase proportional to their energy,
the eigenstates of the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
21
) decay with a decay constant proportional to the eigenvalues
\lambda^{\pm}
.
As such, we can write a spectral representation for
P(x,x_{0};t)
as

\displaystyle P(x,x_{0};t)=\exp\Big\{-\dfrac{1}{D}[U_{\pm}(x)-U_{\pm}(x_{0})]\Big\}\,

\displaystyle\times\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\exp\Big\{-\dfrac{1}{D}t\lambda^{\pm}_{n}\Big\}\phi^{\pm}_{n}(x)\phi^{\pm}_{n}(x_{0})\,.

(24)

The presence of SUSY implies that all decay constants are positive. For broken SUSY, all
\lambda^{+}_{n}
are equal to their partners

\lambda^{-}_{n}
, while for unbroken SUSY the same is true except
\lambda^{-}_{0}
has no counterpart in
H_{+}
. So we see, given that the system exhibits unbroken SUSY, there exists a distribution associated with
\lambda^{-}_{0}
which does not decay over time. This will be the key feature we will utilize in deriving an alternative refractive index which mitigates micro-bending loss of the lowest mode (ground state) in the waveguide.

III
Optical waveguide with micro-bending

In this section we show how, in the presence of microbending, a refractive index profile can be related to a Fokker-Planck equation. From here the Fokker-Planck equation is brought into the same form as an imaginary time Schrödinger equation. This allows for a SUSY transformation to be performed, resulting in a new Fokker-Planck equation. We then show how to reverse the process and derive the refractive index profile from a corresponding Fokker-Planck equation.

III.1
Derivation of the Fokker-Planck Equation

y

The micro-bending effect can be modeled by taking the optical waveguide to be bent in the

Thus, in the presence of micro-bending, we can treat the bent waveguide as a straight waveguide with a modified index of refraction, given by

From Fermat’s principal of least time, the ray equation can by derived

In the approximation that the ray only moves appreciably in the

Also, it is assumed that we are working in the small bending approximation, thus resulting in

Taking Eq. (

\frac{d^{2}y}{dz^{2}}=F(y)-\kappa(z),

\displaystyle\big<\kappa(z)\big>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

\displaystyle\big<\kappa(z)\kappa(z^{\prime})\big>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}^{2}\delta(z-z^{\prime})\,.

(31)

The above second order stochastic differential equation can be separated into a pair of Langevin equations, given by

\displaystyle\frac{dy}{dz}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(z)\,,

\displaystyle\frac{d\theta}{dz}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(y)-\kappa(z)\,.

This FPE looks different from the Schrödinger equation, so it is not trivial to see how a SUSY transformation can be applied. However, it is possible to bring this equation to a canonical form, as we will show. First, the FPE depends on two variables

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I(E)}{\partial E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{E}_{0}\left(\dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{\partial_{y}U}\right)\dfrac{dU}{\sqrt{E-U}}\,,

\displaystyle\dfrac{2\pi\sqrt{2}}{\partial_{y}U}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial U}\int_{0}^{U}\frac{\partial I(E)}{\partial E}\frac{dE}{\sqrt{U-E}}\,.

and integrating both sides. This results in

y=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}\int_{0}^{U}I^{\prime}(E)\dfrac{dE}{\sqrt{U-E}}.

(53)

Equation (

U(y)
from our new action variable. Note that in the first section of the background material, at the beginning of the paper, the SUSY formalism assumes we begin with a potential which has a well behaved ground-state wavefunction in the unbroken-SUSY case, and from this we generate a partner potential with the same spectrum, with the exception of the ground state energy. In our current problem, such a transform would actually make an index profile with more loss. What we must now address is the question regarding whether our starting potential is a
V_{+}
potential derivable from a partner potential
V_{-}
, related through a superpotential with unbroken SUSY. We address this question in the latter part of the next section.

IV
Example of a monomial index profile

We now apply the above formalism to a specific example. We consider a common

(54)

V_{+}

IV.2
Lowering the Loss

Now that we have seen an example of using the above formalism to derive a new index profile, we move on to the more practical issue of finding a profile which has lower loss than the one given in Eq. (
54
). As noted in the beginning of this section, in order to obtain an index profile which exhibits less loss, we must assume that we are starting with a potential
V_{+}
which is derivable from a partner potential
V_{-}
through an unbroken SUSY superpotential. We now derive the condition that must be satisfied for this identification of the partner potentials to be true.

Assuming that
V(x)=V_{+}
means that there exists a superpotential
W
such that
V_{+}=W^{2}+W^{\prime}
, where we have set
\hbar/\sqrt{2m}=1
. This superpotential is generated from the ground state,
\psi_{0}
, of the partner potential
V_{-}
via
W=-\psi_{0}^{\prime}/\psi_{0}
. Plugging this expression into our formula for
V_{+}
gives

\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dx^{2}}+\psi_{0}V_{+}=\frac{2}{\psi_{0}}(\frac{d\psi_{0}}{dx})^{2}.

(79)

This tells us that for an unbroken SUSY partner potential
V_{-}
to exist, the above equation for
\psi_{0}
must have a normalizable solution. In the absence of such a normalizable
\psi_{0}
, we will not be able to write down a partner potential
V_{-}
. Lastly, we digress to point out that SUSY-QM can also be used to generate a family of potentials that are completely isospectral to a given potential
V(x)
, this results in a class of index profiles that present equal loss. In the SUSY-QM literature this method goes by the name of Isospectral Deformation
Gango
. We provide a brief example of this in Appendix
B
.

We now have the necessary tools needed to determine whether an index profile with less loss can be derived from the monomial profile. We see from Eq. (
79
) and Eq. (
61
) that for this to be the case there must exist a normalizable solution to the equation

\frac{d^{2}\psi_{0}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{\nu^{2}-1/4}{x^{2}}\psi_{0}=\frac{2}{\psi_{0}}(\frac{d\psi_{0}}{dx})^{2}.

(80)

The solution to this equation is of the form

\psi_{0}(x)=c_{2}\exp\Big[\frac{1}{2}\Big((\sqrt{4\nu^{2}}-1)\log{(x)}-2\log(x^{2\nu}+c_{1})\Big)\Big].

(81)

In order for
\psi_{0}(x)
to be normalizable, we see from the first argument in the exponential that
\nu
must satisfy
|\nu|>1/2.
Therefore, this tells us that given the index profile in Eq. (
54
), the possible range of
\alpha
which permits a less-lossy partner profile is given by
|\frac{2-\alpha}{2\alpha}|>1/2,
and thus forcing
\alpha<1.
We now can see that the choice of
\alpha=2
for the index profile in Eq. (
54
) cannot lead to an index profile with less loss, using the SUSY-QM formalism. For
\alpha<1
, the previous formalism can be applied to derive the corresponding refractive index profile.

V
Conclusion

We address the problem of mitigating micro-bending attenuation in a waveguide, by way of a supersymmetric quantum mechanical approach. Starting from an initial refractive index profile, and taking the micro-bending into account, it is shown how to derive an associated Fokker-Planck equation, which describes the probability of finding a light ray with a particular energy at some distance along the waveguide. The Fokker-Planck equation is then related to the Schrödinger equation, and the methods of SUSY-QM are applied, resulting in a new Fokker-Planck equation. The procedure for generating a new refractive index profile from the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation, is then developed. It is shown that it is always possible to derive a refractive index which results in more loss, but not always possible to derive a refractive index resulting in less loss. We show that the existence of a SUSY transformation, resulting in a partner index profile with less loss, is determined by the solutions of a non-linear differential equation. We apply our formalism to the monomial index profile, with exponent
\alpha
treated as a free parameter, and show that a refractive index profile with less loss may be derived only when
\alpha<1.

It should be noted that while this work focuses on a problem in classical stochastic optics, the analysis can be applied to other stochastic problems that are based on the use of the Fokker-Planck equation. Furthermore, the developed procedure in this work could be expanded to deal with an optical fiber, where the micro-bending would no longer be confined to one dimension. Interesting applications may be found by applying the above formalism to integrated optical waveguide structures.
