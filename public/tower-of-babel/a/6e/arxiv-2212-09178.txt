Support Vector Regression: Risk Quadrangle Framework
Anton Malandii, Stan Uryasev

Abstract
This paper investigates Support Vector Regression (SVR) within the framework of the Risk Quadrangle (RQ) theory. Every RQ includes four stochastic functionals – error, regret, risk , and deviation , bound together by a so-called statistic. The RQ framework unifies stochastic optimization, risk management, and statistical estimation. Within this framework, both ε \varepsilon -SVR and ν \nu -SVR are shown to reduce to the minimization of the Vapnik error and the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) norm, respectively. The Vapnik error and CVaR norm define quadrangles with a statistic equal to the average of two symmetric quantiles. Therefore, RQ theory implies that ε \varepsilon -SVR and ν \nu -SVR are asymptotically unbiased estimators of the average of two symmetric conditional quantiles. Moreover, the equivalence between ε \varepsilon -SVR and ν \nu -SVR is demonstrated in a general stochastic setting. Additionally, SVR is formulated as a deviation minimization problem. Another implication of the RQ theory is the formulation of ν \nu -SVR as a Distributionally Robust Regression (DRR) problem. Finally, an alternative dual formulation of SVR within the RQ framework is derived. Theoretical results are validated with a case study. Keywords: support vector regression, risk quadrangle, stochastic optimization, distributionally robust optimization, estimation, conditional value-at-risk, CVaR, value-at-risk, quantile, VaR, CVaR norm.

Introduction

Regression
approximates a
random variable
Y
by a function
\hat{f}
of an observed random vector
\mathbold{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{\top}
. The function
\hat{f}(\mathbold{X})
from a given class
{\cal F}

Z_{f}=Y-f(\mathbold{X})

{\cal L}^{1}
-regression,
{\cal L}^{2}
-regression), however, more generally, (cf.
[
Rockafellar et al. 2008
]
), axiomatically defined error measures can be used.
Usually, class
{\cal F}
consists of polynomials, splines, wavelets, or neural networks (see e.g.,
[
Watt et al. 2020
]
).

From the statistical perspective, the purpose of regression is to
estimate
a conditional

{\cal S}(Y|\mathbold{X})

Y

\mathbold{X}
by finding a function
\hat{f}
, which is called the
best estimator

\hat{f}_{{\cal L}^{1}}(\mathbold{X})\in\textrm{med}[Y|\mathbold{X}],

{\cal L}^{2}
-regression estimates the conditional mean
\hat{f}_{{\cal L}^{2}}(\mathbold{X})={\mathbb{E}}[Y|\mathbold{X}].

There is an extensive literature related to regression. For instance, Google search ‘‘linear regression’’ results in 323 million hits (on September 27, 2023). Here we refer only two general frameworks directly relevant to Support Vector Regression (SVR): 1) VC theory, popular in the machine learning community,
[
Vapnik 1995
]
and 2) RQ theory, well-known in risk management,
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
]
. The first framework considers error measures only of the expectation type, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}[\ell(Z_{f})]
, where
\ell
is a so-called loss function; hence the choice of error boils down to the choice of the loss function. The second framework considers axiomatically defined error measures
{\cal E}(Z_{f})
that are not necessarily of expectation type. Moreover, RQ links a selected error with other uncertainty measures: risk, deviation, and regret. A brief introduction to the RQ theory and its relationship formulae for quadrangle construction can be found in Appendix
A.1
.
Errors of expectation type play a crucial role in estimation theory. Indeed, the
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
, Regression Theorem]
states that in this case, the best estimator belongs to a conditional statistic from the corresponding quadrangle, i.e.,

\hat{f}_{\cal E}(\mathbold{X})\in{\cal S}(Y|\mathbold{X}).

Hence the choice of a loss function results in a particular statistic.

In the context of machine learning, regression is understood as a procedure for an optimal fitting of a given dataset
\left(\mathbold{x}_{i},y_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{l},\ \mathbold{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ y_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, which is called a
training sample
. The goal here is constructing a prediction model
\hat{f}(\mathbold{x})
, which gives a forecast
\hat{f}(\mathbold{x}_{l+1})
for the future outcome
y_{l+1}
. Assuming that an
error function
{\cal E}(Z_{f})
is minimized,
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
, Regression Thorem]
implies that

\hat{f}_{\cal E}(\mathbold{x}_{l+1})\in{\cal S}(Y|\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{x}_{l+1}),

i.e., the optimal prediction model is a conditional statistic.

This paper studies SVR using the RQ framework. SVR is a well-established machine learning method that has been extensively studied in the framework of VC theory. However, important questions are not addressed with VC theory. In particular, it is not clear which statistical quantity SVR estimates.

-SVR and
\nu

\varepsilon

2.1
The
\mathbold{\varepsilon}
-SVR

Consider a linear regression with a training sample

X^{l}=\left(\mathbold{x}_{i},y_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{l},

(2.1)

where
\mathbold{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a
feature

y_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is a

.
One needs to find a hyperplane
\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{x}+b,\;(\mathbold{w},b)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}

that optimally fits the given training data. This problem can be efficiently solved with
\varepsilon
-SVR introduced by
Vapnik 1995
. The
\varepsilon
-SVR is formulated as follows

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\quad\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}[|y_{i}-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{x}_{i}-b|-\varepsilon]_{+}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

(2.2)

where
\lambda>0
,
\varepsilon>0
,
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
denotes the
\ell^{2}
-norm , and

[a]_{+}=\max\{0,a\}
is a positive part of a number
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The
\varepsilon
-SVR searches for a hyperplane having at most
\varepsilon
deviation from targets
y_{i}
. The constant
\lambda>0
determines a trade-off between the flatness (magnitude
\|\mathbold{w}\|_{2}^{2}
of the weight vector
\mathbold{w}
) of the hyperplane and the amount up to which deviations larger than
\varepsilon
are tolerated.

In the probabilistic framework, we consider that
\mathbold{z}=\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w},b)

is a random variable taking with equal probabilities components of the vector

(y_{1}-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{x}_{1}-b,\ldots,y_{l}-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{x}_{l}-b)^{\top}

and define the expected loss as

{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w},b)|-\varepsilon]_{+}=\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}[|y_{i}-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{x}_{i}-b|-\varepsilon]_{+}\,.

Further, we reformulate
\varepsilon
-SVR (
2.2
) as follows

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\quad{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w},b)|-\varepsilon]_{+}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.

(2.3)

3
Risk Quadrangle Framework

This section formally introduces quantile (also called value-at-risk (VaR) in finance), CVaR, CVaR norm, Vapnik error, and related quadrangles.

3.1
CVaR and Optimization Formulas

We consider risk measures (stochastic functionals ranking random values) satisfying the following properties:
constant neutrality, convexity, aversity, closedness, monotonicity, and homogeneity
. A risk measure that possesses the first four properties is referred to as a
regular
risk measure.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space,
X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)
be a real-valued random variable, and the cumulative distribution function be denoted by
F_{X}(x)={\mathbb{P}}(X\leq x),\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition 3.1

(Regular Risk Measure,
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
]
)
.

A functional
{\cal R}:{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called a
regular measure of risk
if it satisfies the following axioms

(R1)

constant neutrality:

{\cal R}(C)=C,\quad\forall\;C=const\,;

(R2)

convexity:

{\cal R}\left(\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y\right)\leq\lambda{\cal R}(X)+(1-\lambda){\cal R}(Y),\quad\forall\;X,Y
and
\lambda\in[0,1]\,
;

(R3)

closedness:

\left\{X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)\,\big|\,{\cal R}(X)\leq c\right\}
is closed
\forall\;c<\infty\,
;

(R4)

aversity:

{\cal R}(X)>{\mathbb{E}}X,\quad\forall\;X\neq const\,.

Definition 3.2

(Quantile)
.

The
quantile
(value-at-risk or VaR) of a random variable X at confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1]
is a set defined as follows

q_{\alpha}(X)=[q_{\alpha}^{-}(X),q_{\alpha}^{+}(X)]\,,

(3.6)

where

q_{\alpha}^{-}(X)=\begin{cases}\sup\left\{x\,\big|\,F_{X}(x)<\alpha\right\},&\alpha\in(0,1]\\
{\rm ess\,inf}(X),&\alpha=0\par\end{cases}

(3.7)

q_{\alpha}^{+}(X)=\begin{cases}\inf\left\{x\,\big|\,F_{X}(x)>\alpha\right\},&\alpha\in[0,1)\\
{\rm ess\,sup}(X),&\alpha=1\par\end{cases}

(3.8)

If
q_{\alpha}^{-}(X)=q_{\alpha}^{+}(X)
then

q_{\alpha}(X)=q_{\alpha}^{-}(X)=q_{\alpha}^{+}(X)\,.

Remark 3.1

(Sum and scaling of quantiles)
.

Quantile, generally speaking, is an interval, therefore, the sum of two quantiles is defined as a Minkowski sum of convex sets, i.e., for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]

q_{\alpha_{1}}(X)+q_{\alpha_{2}}(X)=\big\{\,v+w\,\big|\,v\in q_{\alpha_{1}}(X),w\in q_{\alpha_{2}}(X)\,\big\}\,.

(3.9)

The scaling of a quantile by an arbitrary constant
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
is defined as follows

\lambda q_{\alpha}(X)=\big\{\,\lambda w\,\big|\,w\in q_{\alpha}(X)\,\big\}\,.

(3.10)

CVaR (also called, tail value-at-risk, average value-at-risk, expected shortfall) is a popular regular risk measure. It has favorable mathematical properties and can be efficiently optimized,
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2000
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
]
.

Definition 3.3

(CVaR)
.

The
Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)
of random variable X at confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1]
is defined as

\bar{q}_{\alpha}(X)=\dfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\displaystyle\int\limits_{\alpha}^{1}q_{\beta}(X)\,d\beta,\quad\alpha\in(0,1)\,.

(3.11)

For
\alpha=0:

\;\;\bar{q}_{0}(X)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\bar{q}_{\varepsilon}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[X]\,.

For
\alpha=1:

\;\;\bar{q}_{1}(X)={\rm ess\,sup}(X)\,.

The following Theorem
3.1
by
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
is used to build optimization algorithms for CVaR.

Theorem 3.1

(CVaR Optimization Formula)
.

For a random variable
X
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\bar{q}_{\alpha}(X)=\min_{C}\left\{C+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}{\mathbb{E}}[X-C]_{+}\right\}\,,

(3.12)

and the set of minimizers for (
3.12
) is
q_{\alpha}(X)
.

There is a deep relation between CVaR and the mean excess function
{\mathbb{E}}[X-x]_{+},\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}
(the mean excess function is also called regret or partial moment of order
1
).

Rockafellar and Royset 2014
proved the following theorem (see, also,
[
Guan et al. 2022
]
).

Theorem 3.2

(Dual CVaR Optimization Formula)
.

For a random variable
X
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathbb{E}[X-x]_{+}=\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\;(1-\alpha)(\bar{q}_{\alpha}(X)-x)\,,

(3.13)

and the set of maximizers for (
3.13
) is
[{\mathbb{P}}(X<x),{\mathbb{P}}(X\leq x)]\,.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that
(1-\alpha)\bar{q}_{\alpha}(X)
is a concave function of
\alpha
,
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
]
. Therefore, (
3.13
) is a concave maximization problem.

3.2
CVaR Norm and Related Quadrangles

Axiomatic analysis of general measures of error was introduced and developed by
Rockafellar et al. 2008
. CVaR norm, considered by
Pavlikov and Uryasev 2014
;
Bertsimas et al. 2011
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and extended by
Pichler 2013
;
Mafusalov and Uryasev 2016
to infinite-dimensional setting, is a particular case of a regular measure of error.

Definition 3.4

(Regular Error Measure,
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
]
)
.

A functional
{\cal E}:{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called a
regular measure of error
if it satisfies the following axioms:

(E1)

zero neutrality:

{\cal E}(0)=0
;

(E2)

convexity:

{\cal E}\left(\lambda X+(1-\lambda)Y\right)\leq\lambda{\cal E}(X)+(1-\lambda){\cal E}(Y),\quad\forall\;X,Y
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
;

(E3)

closedness:

\left\{X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)\,\big|\,{\cal E}(X)\leq c\right\}
is closed
\forall\;c<\infty
;

(E4)

nonzeroness:

{\cal E}(X)>0,\quad\forall\;X\neq 0
.

Definition 3.5

(Scaled CVaR Norm)
.

Let
X\in{\cal L}^{1}(\Omega)
be a real-valued random variable. Then
scaled CVaR norm
of
X
with parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
is defined by

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}^{S}=\bar{q}_{\alpha}\left(|X|\right)\,.

(3.14)

When referring to the CVaR norm, we assume its
scaled
version. However, following the
Pavlikov and Uryasev 2014
below we define an equivalent
non-scaled
CVaR norm.

Definition 3.6

(Non-scaled CVaR Norm)
.

Let
X\in{\cal L}^{1}(\Omega)
be a real-valued random variable. Then
non-scaled CVaR norm
of
X
with parameter
\alpha\in[0,1)
is defined by

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\bar{q}_{\alpha}\left(|X|\right).

(3.15)

Mafusalov and Uryasev 2016
proved the following Proposition
3.3
, defining the CVaR Norm Quadrangle.

Proposition 3.3

(CVaR Norm Quadrangle)
.

For
\alpha\in[0,1)
the error measure
{\cal E}_{\alpha}(X)=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}
generates the following regular quadrangle (see Definition
A.3
):

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(q_{(1-\alpha)/2}(X)+q_{(1+\alpha)/2}(X)\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big((1+\alpha)\bar{q}_{(1-\alpha)/2}(X)+(1-\alpha)\bar{q}_{(1+\alpha)/2}(X)\big)\,,

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big((1+\alpha)\bar{q}_{(1-\alpha)/2}(X-{\mathbb{E}}[X])+(1-\alpha)\bar{q}_{(1+\alpha)/2}(X-{\mathbb{E}}[X])\big)\,,

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}+{\mathbb{E}}[X]\,,

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}\,.

We call by Vapnik error, the error defined by
{\cal E}_{\varepsilon}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[|X|-\varepsilon]_{+}
. This name is inspired by Vapnik’s
\varepsilon
-insensitive loss function
\ell(\xi)=[|\xi|-\varepsilon]_{+}
. The following Proposition
3.4
presents a quadrangle based on the Vapnik error. It is closely related to the CVaR Norm Quadrangle considered in Proposition
3.3
.

Proposition 3.4

(Quantile Symmetric Average Quadrangle)
.

Let
X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega),

0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}({\rm ess\,sup}\,X-{\rm ess\,inf}\,X),
and
({\cal R}_{\alpha},{\cal D}_{\alpha},{\cal V}_{\alpha},{\cal E}_{\alpha})
be the CVaR Norm Quadrangle quartet with statistic
{\cal S}_{\alpha}
. Then the set

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(X)=\Big\{\,\alpha\,\Big|

\displaystyle\varepsilon\in\frac{1}{2}\bigl(q_{(1+\alpha)/2}(X)-q_{(1-\alpha)/2}(X)\bigr)\,\Big\}

(3.16)

is nonempty and the Vapnik error generates the following quadrangle:

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{\alpha\in{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(X)}{\cal S}_{\alpha}(X),

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle={\cal R}_{\alpha}(X)-(1-\alpha)\varepsilon,\quad\forall\;\alpha\in{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle={\cal D}_{\alpha}(X)-(1-\alpha)\varepsilon,\quad\forall\;\alpha\in{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[|X|-\varepsilon]_{+}+{\mathbb{E}}[X],

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{\varepsilon}(X)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[|X|-\varepsilon]_{+}=\textrm{Vapnik error.}

Remark 2

(Non-regularity of the Quantile Symmetric Average Quadrangle)
.

Note that in general, the Vapnik error
{\cal E}_{\varepsilon}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[|X|-\varepsilon]_{+}
is not regular for each
\varepsilon\geq 0
, since it fails to satisfy the nonzeroness axiom (i.e., there exists
X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega),\ X\not\equiv 0
such that
{\cal E}_{\varepsilon}(X)=0
). Indeed,
{\cal E}_{\varepsilon}(X)=0
for all
X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that
|X|\leq\varepsilon
almost surely.
On the other hand, CVaR Norm Quadrangle is regular and will play an important role in further analysis of SVR.

Remark 3

(Uniqueness of Statistic for Quantile Symmetric Average Quadrangle for Absolutely Continuous Random Variables)
.

For an absolutely continuous random variable
X
, the left and right quantiles coincide, i.e.,
q_{\alpha}^{+}(X)=q_{\alpha}^{-}(X)=q_{\alpha}(X)
. Therefore, for
0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}({\rm ess\,sup}\,X-{\rm ess\,inf}\,X)
, the set
{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(X)=\Big\{\,\alpha\in[0,1)\,\Big|\,\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}(q_{(1+\alpha)/2}(X)-q_{(1-\alpha)/2}(X))\,\Big\}
is a singleton, and consequently, the statistic of the Quantile Symmetric Average Quadrangle coincides with that of the CVaR Norm Quadrangle.

4
SVR as a Generalized Regression

This section considers SVR as a regularized regression corresponding to the quadrangles defined in Propositions
3.3
,
3.4
. Firstly, we establish an equivalence of two variants of SVR through the Dual CVaR Optimization Formula (Theorem
3.2
). Then we discuss the estimation properties of SVR. Further, we discuss SVR in the context of DRO and provide equivalent reformulations. Finally, we derive a dual formulation of the
\nu
-SVR and discuss its nonlinear extension using the well-known ‘‘kernel trick’’.

4.1
Equivalence of
\mathbold{\varepsilon}
-SVR and
\mathbold{\nu}
-SVR for Random Vectors

Below we formulate the
\varepsilon
-SVR and
\nu
-SVR for stochastic vectors.
We denote by
\mathbold{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{\top}
a vector of random variables (factors)
X_{i}\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega),\ i=1,\ldots,n
and by
Y\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)
a target random variable (regressant). This setting is more general, compared to the Section
2
because it does not assume that the random vectors have a fixed number of equally probable outcomes. Let us denote the linear regression residual by

Z(\mathbold{w},b)=Y-(\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{X}+b),\hskip 11.49994pt(\mathbold{w},b)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\,.

Definition 4.1

(
\varepsilon
-SVR for random vectors)
.

The
\varepsilon
-SVR (similar to (
2.3
))
is stated as

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z(\mathbold{w},b)|-\varepsilon\right]_{+}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.

(4.17)

Definition 4.2

(
\nu
-SVR for random vectors)
.

The
\nu
-SVR (similar to
(
2.5
)) with
\nu=1-\alpha
is stated as

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\quad\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Z(\mathbold{w},b)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.

(4.18)

Given the equivalency of problems (
2.3
) and (
2.5
), the natural question is whether a similar statement is valid for (
4.17
) and (
4.18
). The following Proposition
4.1
answers this question.

Proposition 4.1

(The
\varepsilon
-SVR &
\nu
-SVR Equivalence)
.

Let
\lambda>0
be a regularization constant. Then

(i)

if
(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is an optimal solution vector of (
4.18
) for some
\alpha\in[0,1)
, then

(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is also an optimal solution vector of (
4.17
) for each
\varepsilon\in q_{\alpha}(|Z(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|).

(ii)

if
(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is an optimal solution vector of (
4.17
) for some

\varepsilon\geq 0
, then

(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is also an optimal solution vector of (
4.18
)
for each

\alpha\in\big[{\mathbb{P}}(|Z(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|<\varepsilon),{\mathbb{P}}(|Z(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|\leq\varepsilon)\big)={\cal I}_{\varepsilon}.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.3

∎

Remark 4
.

Note that the regression residual
Z(\mathbold{w},b)=Y-(\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{X}+b)
can have a more general form, i.e.,
Z(\mathbold{w},b)=Y-f(\mathbold{w},\mathbold{X}),
where

is a class of functions that is wider than the class of affine functions. In other words, Proposition
4.1
holds in a more general nonlinear setting (cf. Problem
4.2
). However, in the case of
\ell^{2}
regularization, the class of affine functions is sufficient, since one may apply the ‘‘kernel trick’’.

4.2
The
\mathbold{\varepsilon}
-SVR and
\mathbold{\nu}
-SVR as Deviation Minimization Problems

This section studies
\varepsilon
-SVR and
\nu
-SVR as stochastic optimization problems through the concept of deviation measures.
Rockafellar et al. 2008
proved a theorem, which relates the generalized regression problem with the minimization of deviation measures.

Further, we consider the formulation of
\nu
-SVR and
\varepsilon
-SVR in the context of the generalized regression.

Problem 4.2

(Generalized Regression)
.

Given a random vector of factors
\mathbold{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})^{\top}
find a function from a given class
f\in\mathcal{F}
, solving the following optimization problem for approximating a regressant
Y

\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\quad{\cal E}(Z_{f}),\ \text{where}Z_{f}=Y-f(\mathbold{X})\,.

(4.19)

Below is the theorem from
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
]
about solving the regression problem by minimizing a deviation.

Theorem 4.3

(Error Shaping Decomposition of Regression)
.

Consider problem (
4.19
) for
\mathbold{X}
and
Y
in the
case of
{\cal E}
being a regular measure of error and
{\cal F}
being a class of
functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f\in{\cal F}\quad\hbox{$\Longrightarrow$}\quad f+C\in{\cal F}\text{for all}C\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

Let deviation
{\cal D}
and statistic
{\cal S}
correspond to
{\cal E}
.
Problem (
4.19
) is equivalent to:

\begin{split}\min_{f\in{\cal F}}&\quad{\cal D}(Z_{f})\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\quad 0\in{\cal S}(Z_{f})\,.\end{split}

(4.20)

Corollary 4.4

(Linear Regression)
.

Consider the generalized regression problem (
4.19
), where
{\cal F}
is a class of affine functions, i.e.,
Z_{f}=Y-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{X}-b.
Denote by
\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w})=Y-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{X}
. Then
(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is an optimal solution vector of (
4.19
) if and only if

\mathbold{w}^{*}
is an optimal solution to

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{w}}

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle D}(\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}))\,,

(4.21)

and
b^{*}\in{\cal S}(\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}^{*}))
.

Therefore, the Decomposition of Regression Theorem implies that the optimal intercept
b^{*}
is a known function (statistic) of
\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}^{*})
.

Remark 5

(The choice of
\varepsilon
in
\varepsilon
-SVR)
.

Denote by
(\mathbold{w}^{*}_{\varepsilon},b^{*}_{\varepsilon})
a solution vector of problem
(
4.17
).
Proposition
4.1
provides a constraint for parameter
\varepsilon,
i.e.,

0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}\,\big({\rm ess\,sup}\,Z(\mathbold{w}^{*}_{\varepsilon},b^{*}_{\varepsilon})-{\rm ess\,inf}\,Z(\mathbold{w}^{*}_{\varepsilon},b^{*}_{\varepsilon})\big)\,.

(4.22)

It can be proved that for sufficiently large
\varepsilon
the vector
(\mathbold{w}^{*}_{\varepsilon},b^{*}_{\varepsilon})=\mathbf{0}
is an optimal solution of the problem (
4.17
), where
\mathbf{0}
is the
(n+1)
-dimensional zero vector.
Therefore,
\varepsilon
should satisfy the inequality

0\leq\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}({\rm ess\,sup}\,Y-{\rm ess\,inf}\,Y)\,.

(4.23)

This bound for the parameter
\varepsilon
was earlier recommended by

Chang and Lin 2001
.

Corollary 4.5

(Decomposition of SVR)
.

Let
Y-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{X}=\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w})\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\lambda>0
be a regularization constant, and
({\cal S}_{\varepsilon},{\cal D}_{\varepsilon})
be the corresponding pairs of the statistic and deviation from CVaR Norm and Quantile Symmetric Average Quadrangles, respectively. Then

(
i
)

\forall\,\alpha\in[0,1),

(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is an optimal solution vector of problem (
4.18
) if and only if
\mathbold{w}^{*}
is a solution to

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{w}}

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle D}_{\alpha}(\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

(4.24)

and
b^{*}\in{\cal S}_{\alpha}(\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}^{*}))
.

(
ii
)

\forall\,\varepsilon\in\big[0,\frac{1}{2}({\rm ess\,sup}\,Y-{\rm ess\,inf}\,Y)\big),

(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is an optimal solution vector of problem (
4.17
) if and only if
\mathbold{w}^{*}
is an optimal solution to

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{w}}

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle D}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

(4.25)

and
b^{*}\in{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{Z}(\mathbold{w}^{*}))
.

Proof.

Items
(i), (ii)
follow from the Theorem
4.3
, since
\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert_{2}
obviously does not depend on
b
.
∎

4.3
Estimation

This subsection discusses generalized regression in the risk quadrangle framework. Regression Problem
4.2
approximates a random variable
Y
by a function
f(\mathbold{X})\in\mathcal{F}
. By the regression being ‘‘generalized’’, we mean that the approximation error (residual)
Z_{f}=Y-f(\mathbold{X})
is assessed by an error
{\cal E}
. The function
f
is called an
estimator
, and the class of functions
{\cal F}
is called a
class of estimators
. The function
\hat{f}\in{\cal F}
is called
the best estimator
in the class
{\cal F}
w.r.t. error
{\cal E}
if it solves the problem (
4.19
). The following Regression Theorem from
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
links the function
\hat{f}
and a statistic
{\cal S}
corresponding to an error
{\cal E}
.

Theorem 4.6

(Regression)
.

Consider regression problem (
4.19
) for a random vector
\mathbold{X}
and
Y
in the
case of
{\cal E}
being a regular error and
{\cal F}
being a class of
functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

f\in{\cal F}\quad\hbox{$\Longrightarrow$}\quad f+C\in{\cal F}\text{for all}C\in{\mathbb{R}}\,.

Let
{\cal D}
and
{\cal S}
correspond to
{\cal E}
. Moreover let
{\cal E}
be of
expectation type
and let
{\cal F}
include a function
f

satisfying

\begin{array}[]{lcr}f(\mathbold{x})\in{\cal S}\left(Y|\mathbold{x}\right)\text{almost surely for $\mathbold{x}\in G$,}\cr\!\text{where}Y|\mathbold{x}=Y_{\lower 1.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{x}$}}\text{(conditional distribution), }\end{array}

(4.26)

with
G
being the support of the distribution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
induced by

\mathbold{X}
.

Then
f
solves the regression problem and
estimates

this conditional statistic
in a sense that

f(\mathbold{X})\in{\cal S}\left(Y|\mathbold{X}\right)\text{almost surely.}

(4.27)

Further, we refer to two classical examples illustrating the above theorem.

Example 4.1

(Least Squares)
.

The Least Squares regression with the error,
{\cal E}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[X^{2}]
, is formulated as follows

\min_{f\in{\cal F}}\quad{\mathbb{E}}[Z_{f}^{2}].

(4.28)

By solving this problem, we obtain the best estimator
\hat{f}(\mathbold{X})={\mathbb{E}}\left[Y|\mathbold{X}\right]={\cal S}(Y|\mathbold{X})
= conditional statistic, which is a conditional mean, corresponding to the mean squared error
{\cal E}
in the Mean-based Quadrangle, cf.
[
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
]
.

Example 4.2

(Quantile Regression)
.

\min_{f\in{\cal F}}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}(Z_{f})_{+}+(Z_{f})_{-}\right].

(4.29)

The best estimator is
\hat{f}(\mathbold{X})\in q_{\alpha}\left(Y|\mathbold{X}\right)={\cal S}(Y|\mathbold{X})
= conditional statistic, which is conditional quantile, corresponding to the
{\cal E}
in the Quantile Quadrangle, cf.
[
Koenker 2005
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
]
.

Now, consider the Vapnik error, i.e.,
{\cal E}_{\varepsilon}(X)={\mathbb{E}}[|X|-\varepsilon]_{+},\ \varepsilon\geq 0
. According to Proposition
3.4
, the best estimator is

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\mathbold{X})\in\bigcup\limits_{\alpha\in{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(Z_{\hat{f}})}\Big\{\,\dfrac{1}{2}\big(q_{(1-\alpha)/2}\big(Y|\mathbold{X}\big)+q_{(1+\alpha)/2}\big(Y|\mathbold{X}\big)\big)\,\Big\}\,.

(4.30)

On the other hand, consider the CVaR norm, i.e.,
{\cal E}_{\alpha}(X)=\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}X\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}
. This error is not of expectation type. However, Proposition
4.1
implies (if conditions of regression theorem are satisfied) that for any
\alpha\in[0,1)
we can pick an equivalent expectation type Vapnik error with

\varepsilon\in q_{\alpha}(|Z_{\hat{f}}|)
such that (
4.30
) holds, where
\hat{f}
is a solution to the generalized regression problem with CVaR norm error. Therefore, we choose to work with
\nu
-SVR. This choice is preferable for two reasons. First, the error measure, in this case, is a norm, simplifying the derivation of the dual formulation of
\nu
-SVR. Second, the choice of
\alpha
is more intuitive and also plays an important role in
\nu
-SVR’s interpretation as DRR.

Regarding the estimation properties of SVR, adding the regularization penalty biases the estimator. In this case, the best estimator is a biased variant of (
4.30
). However, when the regularization parameter tends to zero, as the sample size increases, we obtain an asymptotically unbiased estimator (
4.30
), where
{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}(Z_{\hat{f}})
is possibly a singleton (cf. Remark
3
). For instance, in the popular SVR solver LIBSVM,
[
Chang and Lin 2011
]
, the regularization parameter
\lambda=\dfrac{1}{Cl}
and thus,
\lambda\to 0
as
l\to\infty
. The following property provides a formal statement on this subject.

Property 4.1

(SVR Estimation)
.

Given the dataset (
2.1
), consider the following generalized regression problem

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\quad\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}Z(\mathbold{w},b)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}_{\alpha}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

(4.31)

where
\lambda\to 0
as
l\to\infty
. Assume that the conditions of the Theorem
4.6
are satisfied,
{\cal F}
is a class of affine functions, and
(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})
is a unique optimal solution to (
4.31
) when
\lambda=0
.
Then the best estimator
\hat{f}_{l}(\mathbold{x})=\mathbold{w}^{*\top}_{l}\mathbold{x}+b^{*}_{l}
is an asymptotically unbiased estimator, i.e.,

\lim_{l\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[(\mathbold{w}^{*}_{l},b^{*}_{l})]=(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})

(4.32)

and

\hat{f}(\mathbold{x})=\mathbold{w}^{*\top}\mathbold{x}+b^{*}\in\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(q_{(1-\alpha^{*})/2}\big(Y|\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{x}\big)+q_{(1+\alpha^{*})/2}\big(Y|\mathbold{X}=\mathbold{x}\big)\Big)\,,

(4.33)

where
\alpha^{*}\in[0,1)
is such that

\frac{1}{2}\big(q_{(1+\alpha^{*})/2}(Z(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*}))-q_{(1-\alpha^{*})/2}(Z(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*}))\big)\ \ \bigcap\ \ q_{\alpha}(|Z(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|)\,\neq\,\varnothing.

According to
Schölkopf and Smola 2001
, SVR can be considered as a generalized mean estimator. The estimation of the mean is accomplished by employing a cross-validation procedure for
\alpha
, using the MSE as a performance metric, cf.
Schölkopf et al. 2000
. This approach leverages the flexibility of averaging two symmetric quantiles, which proves to be a versatile statistic. For a wide range of distributions, particularly those that are symmetric or heavy-tailed, there exists an optimal value of
\alpha^{*}
within the interval
[0,1]
such that

\dfrac{1}{2}\left(q_{(1-\alpha^{*})/2}\left(Z_{\hat{f}_{l}}\right)+q_{(1+\alpha^{*})/2}\left(Z_{\hat{f}_{l}}\right)\right)={\mathbb{E}}[Z_{\hat{f}_{l}}]\,,

(4.34)

where
\hat{f}_{l}
is an optimal estimator of problem (
4.31
). The following Lemma
4.7
states a more general result.

Lemma 4.7

(Estimating any statistic with SVR)
.

Let
{\cal S}:{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
(or in general a set-valued mapping
{\cal S}:{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega)\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{R}}
) be an arbitrary statistic (e.g., mean, median, quantile, etc.) and let
f\in{\cal F}
be a class of affine functions satisfying the conditions of the Theorem
4.6
with CVaR norm being the error function. Then for any
\alpha\in[0,1)

{\cal S}(Y|\mathbold{X})\ \ \bigcap\ \ \dfrac{1}{2}\left(q_{(1-\alpha)/2}\left(Y|\mathbold{X}\right)+q_{(1+\alpha)/2}\left(Y|\mathbold{X}\right)\right)\,\neq\,\varnothing

(4.35)

if and only if

0\in{\cal S}\left(Z_{\hat{f}}\right),

(4.36)

where
\hat{f}
is an optimal solution vector of (
4.2
) with CVaR Norm error.

Proof.

The proof is a direct implication of the
[
Rockafellar and Royset 2015
, Theorem 5.1]
.
∎

4.4
SVR as Distributionally Robust Regression

The formulation of
\nu
-SVR as the regularized CVaR norm minimization problem (
4.18
) admits an equivalent saddle point formulation leveraging the dual representation of CVaR,
[
Artzner et al. 1999
;
Rockafellar et al. 2006
]
. Specifically, for any random variable
X\in{\cal L}^{2}(\Omega,{\cal A},{\mathbb{P}})

\bar{q}_{\alpha}(X)=\max_{{\mathbb{Q}}\in{\cal Q}_{\alpha}}\quad{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}[X]\,,

(4.37)

where
{\cal Q}_{\alpha}=\left\{{\mathbb{Q}}\ll{\mathbb{P}}\,\big|\,0\leq\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}\leq 1/(1-\alpha)\right\},

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation w.r.t. a probability measure
{\mathbb{Q}},
and
\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}
is a Radon–Nikodym derivative.

Consider the
\nu
-SVR regression problem (
4.18
). Denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{(Y,\mathbold{X})}
the joint distribution of the regressant
Y
and factors
\mathbold{X}.
Then given (
4.37
), problem (
4.18
) can be equivalently rewritten as follows

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\max_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{(Y,\mathbold{X})}\in{\cal Q}_{\alpha}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{(Y,\mathbold{X})}}[|Z(\mathbold{w},b)|]+\lambda\|\mathbold{w}\|_{2}^{2}\,.

(4.38)

Formulation (
4.38
) is a regularized distributionally robust optimization problem with the uncertainty (ambiguity) set
{\cal Q}_{\alpha},

[
Shapiro 2017
]
. More concretely, (
4.38
) is a distributionally robust
{\cal L}^{1}
-regression. In particular, for
\alpha=0,
(
4.38
) is precisely the regularized
{\cal L}^{1}
-regression and when
\alpha\to 1,
it is the regularized
{\cal L}^{\infty}
-regression.

In practice, (
4.38
) is intractable since the joint distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{(Y,\mathbold{X})}
is frequently unknown, and instead the data (
2.1
) is given. Therefore, one usually approximates the
{\mathbb{P}}_{(Y,\mathbold{X})}
by empirical distribution (uniform distribution over the data) and solves

\min_{\mathbold{w},b}\max_{\mathbold{q}\in{\cal Q}_{\alpha}}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}|z_{i}(\mathbold{w},b)|+\lambda\|\mathbold{w}\|_{2}^{2}\,,

(4.39)

where
z_{i}(\mathbold{w},b)=y_{i}-\mathbold{w}^{\top}\mathbold{x}_{i}-b,\ i=1,\ldots,l
and

{\cal Q}_{\alpha}=\left\{\mathbold{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\ \middle|\ \sum_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}=1,\ 0\leq q_{i}\leq 1/l(1-\alpha)\right\}.

(4.40)

Formulation (
4.39
) was considered in
Bertsimas and Paskov 2020
, where the set
{\cal Q}_{\alpha}
was replaced with

{\cal Q}_{k}=\left\{\mathbold{q}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l}\ \middle|\ \sum_{i=1}^{l}q_{i}=k,\ 0\leq q_{i}\leq 1\right\},\quad 0\leq k\leq l,\ k\in\mathbb{N}\;.

(4.41)

It is obvious that (
4.40
) and (
4.41
) are equivalent for
k=\left\lfloor l(1-\alpha)\right\rfloor.

Let
(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*},\mathbold{q}^{*})
be an optimal solution vector of (
4.39
). Then the optimal
\mathbold{q}^{*}\in{\cal Q}_{\alpha}
in (
4.39
) is as follows (cf.
[
Royset and Wets 2021
, pp. 524–525]
)

q_{i}^{*}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})=\left\{\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{l(1-\alpha)}&\text{if}|z_{i}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|>q_{\alpha}(|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|)\\
\frac{P_{q}-\alpha}{m(1-\alpha)}&\text{if}|z_{i}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|=q_{\alpha}(|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|)\\
0&\text{ if}|z_{i}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|<q_{\alpha}(|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|)\end{matrix}\right.

(4.42)

with
P_{q}=\operatorname{prob}\{|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|\leq q_{\alpha}(|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|)\}
and
m
is a number of observations
i
such that
|z_{i}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|=q_{\alpha}(|\mathbold{z}(\mathbold{w}^{*},b^{*})|).
Evidently, (
4.42
) is sparse.

Due to the inherent sparsity of
\mathbold{q}^{*}
,
Bertsimas and Paskov 2020
proposed formulation (
4.39
) as a superior optimization-based alternative to the traditional random train-test split for selecting training data. They conducted several case studies that numerically verify this approach yields better out-of-sample performance, as measured by MSE.

Given Lemma
4.7
, this result is not surprising, as by appropriately adjusting
\alpha
, it is possible to approximate the conditional mean (the minimizer of MSE) with the conditional average of two symmetric quantiles, provided that such an
\alpha
exists.

Moreover, assuming that

y_{i}=\mathbold{w}^{*\top}\mathbold{x}_{i}+b^{*}+\epsilon_{i},\hskip 11.49994pti=1,2,\ldots

(4.43)

is the true regression model, where
\epsilon_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables, it is possible to select an optimal
\alpha
that minimizes the out-of-sample MSE, provided the distribution of
\epsilon_{i}
is known and such
\alpha
exists. Below, we provide a sequence of steps for the optimal
\alpha
selection.

1.

Compute
\mu={\mathbb{E}}[\epsilon_{i}];

2.

Find
\alpha
such that
q_{(1+\alpha)/2}(\epsilon_{i})+q_{(1-\alpha)/2}(\epsilon_{i})=2\mu
;

3.

Compute
x=\Big(q_{(1+\alpha)/2}(\epsilon_{i})-q_{(1-\alpha)/2}(\epsilon_{i})\Big)/2;

4.

Compute
\alpha^{*}=\operatorname{prob}\{|\epsilon_{i}|\leq x\};

5.

Set
\nu=1-\alpha^{*}
as a parameter of
\nu
-SVR or set
\varepsilon=x
as a parameter of
\varepsilon
-SVR.

6.

For
\epsilon_{i}
with symmetric distribution, choose any
\alpha\in[0,1).

6
Conclusion

This paper formulated SVR in the RQ framework, establishing connections between this machine-learning tool and classical statistics, risk management, and DRO. A key contribution is the derivation of the quadrangle corresponding to
\varepsilon
-SVR (Proposition
3.4
) revealing its risk, deviation, and statistic components.

We demonstrated that SVR is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of the average of two symmetric conditional quantiles. This result implies that by adjusting the parameter
\alpha=1-\nu
, the
\nu
-SVR can estimate various distributional statistics, including the mean, median, and expectiles. Moreover, the appropriate choice of performance metric during cross-validation, such as MSE for the mean or AMSE for expectiles – allows for precise estimation of these statistics (Lemma
4.7
) and understanding its limitations.

Additionally, we have reformulated SVR as a deviation minimization problem within the RQ theory (Corollary
4.5
). This reformulation has practical implications for dimension reduction in linear regression, where the intercept can be analytically computed.

Another result is the proof of equivalence between
\nu
-SVR and
\varepsilon
-SVR in a general stochastic setting (Proposition
4.1
). We provided analytical expressions for the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\nu
that establish this equivalence, unifying these two widely-used SVR formulations.

Furthermore, by applying duality theory of convex functionals within the RQ framework, we have reinterpreted
\nu
-SVR as a DRR problem, offering a novel perspective on SVR that has been underexplored in the literature.

Finally, we derived a new dual formulation of SVR that offers computational advantages by halving the number of variables compared to the standard dual formulation. This formulation is transparent, computationally efficient, and compatible with general-purpose optimization packages like CPLEX, Gurobi, CVX, and PSG, making it versatile for practical implementation.

Theoretical results are validated with a case study.

A.1
Theoretical Background

The fundamental risk quadrangle paradigm was developed in
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
. This framework established a connection between risk management, reliability, statistics, and stochastic optimization theories. In particular, the risk quadrangle theory provides a unified framework for generalized regression.

\begin{array}[]{lcr}\hskip 25.0pt\text{risk}{\cal R}\,\longleftrightarrow\,{\cal D}\text{deviation}&\cr\hskip 55.0pt\uparrow\hskip 9.0pt{\cal S}\hskip 9.0pt\uparrow&\cr\hskip 17.0pt\text{regret}{\cal V}\,\longleftrightarrow\;{\cal E}\text{error}&\cr\end{array}

Diagram 1:  The Fundamental Risk Quadrangle

The risk quadrangle methodology united risk functions for a random value
X
in groups (quadrangles) consisting of the following functions:

•

Risk
{\cal R}(X)
, which provides a numerical surrogate for the overall hazard in
X
.

•

Deviation
{\cal D}(X)
, which measures the ‘‘nonconstancy’’ in
X
as its uncertainty.

•

Error
{\cal E}(X)
, which measures the ‘‘nonzeroness’’ in
X
.

•

Regret
{\cal V}(X)
, which measures the ‘‘regret’’ in facing the mix of outcomes of
X
.

•

Statistic
{\cal S}(X)
associated with
X
through
{\cal E}
and
{\cal V}
.

The following diagram contains general relationships among elements of the quadrangle:

{\cal D}(X)=\min_{C}\!\big\{\,{\cal E}(X-C)\,\big\}={\cal R}(X-{\mathbb{E}}X)

{\cal R}(X)=\min_{C}\!\big\{\,C+{\cal V}(X-C)\,\big\}={\mathbb{E}}X+{\cal D}(X)

{\cal S}(X)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{C}\!\big\{\,{\cal E}(X-C)\,\big\}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{C}\!\big\{\,C+{\cal V}(X-C)\,\big\}

{\cal E}(X)={\cal V}(X)-{\mathbb{E}}X,\qquad{\cal V}(X)={\mathbb{E}}X+{\cal E}(X)

Diagram 2:  The Relationship Formulae

Here
{\mathbb{E}}X
denotes the mathematical expectation of
X
, and the statistic,
{\cal S}(X)
, can be a set if the minimum is achieved for multiple points.

The popular quantile quadrangle, cf.
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2013
is named after the quantile statistic. This quadrangle establishes relations between the CVaR optimization technique described in
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2000
;
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2002
and quantile regression, cf.
Koenker and Bassett 1978
,
Koenker 2005
. In particular, it was shown that CVaR minimization and quantile regression are similar procedures based on the quantile statistic in the regret and error representation of risk and deviation.

Definition A.1

(Regular Regret Measure)

(V2)

where

{\cal S}_{\varepsilon}(X)=\bigcup\limits_{\alpha\in{\cal A}_{\varepsilon}}\Big\{\,\dfrac{1}{2}\left(q_{(1-\alpha)/2}(X)+q_{(1+\alpha)/2}(X)\right)\,\Big\}

is the minimizer for (
A.64
).

Finally,
{\cal R}(X)={\cal D}(X)+{\mathbb{E}}X
and
{\cal V}(X)={\cal E}(X)+{\mathbb{E}}X
imply a complete quadrangle quartet.
∎

A.4
Proof of Proposition
4.8

Before directly going to the derivation of the dual formulation, let us introduce a couple of definitions and notations.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a normed space over
{\mathbb{R}}
with norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
(i.e.,
\left\lVert x\right\rVert\in{\mathbb{R}}
for
x\in\mathbb{X}
). Then, the dual space denoted by
\mathbb{X}^{*}
is defined as the set of all continuous linear functionals from
\mathbb{X}
into
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Definition A.4

(Dual norm)
.

For
f\in\mathbb{X}^{*}
, the
dual norm
, denoted by
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{*}
of
f
is defined by

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{*}

\displaystyle=\sup\big\{\,|f(x)|:x\in\mathbb{X},\ \left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq 1\,\big\}

(A.66)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\frac{|f(x)|}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert}:x\in\mathbb{X},\ x\neq 0\right\}.

Definition A.5

(Conjugate function,
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then a function on
\mathbb{X}^{*}
, defined by the following equality

f^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\operatorname{dom}f}\{\langle y,x\rangle-f(x)\},

(A.67)

is called a
conjugate
of
f
or the
Legendre–Young–Fenchel transform
.

Proof.

Define the Lagrangian

\displaystyle{\cal L}(\mathbold{w},b,\mathbold{z},\mathbold{\mu})

\displaystyle=C(1-\alpha)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathbold{z}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}^{S}_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}(\mathbold{y}-\hat{\textbf{X}}\mathbold{w}-\textbf{1}_{l}b-\mathbold{z})

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\mathbold{z}-C(1-\alpha)\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathbold{z}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}^{S}_{\alpha}\right)-\left((\hat{\textbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbold{\mu})^{\top}\mathbold{w}-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{w}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\right)+\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\mathbold{y}-\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{l}b,

where
\mathbold{\mu}=(\mu^{1},\ldots,\mu^{l})^{\top}
is a Lagrange multiplier vector.
Then

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbold{w},b,\mathbold{z}}

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle L}(\mathbold{w},b,\mathbold{z},\mathbold{\mu})

\displaystyle=\min_{b}\quad-C(1-\alpha)\left(\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathbold{\mu}/C(1-\alpha)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}^{S}_{\alpha}\right)^{*}-\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\textbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbold{\mu}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\right)^{*}+\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\mathbold{y}-\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{l}b,

which leads to the implicit constraint
\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\textbf{1}_{l}=0
. Note that in general, cf.
[
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
]
, for
\mathbold{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\left\lVert\mathbold{x}\right\rVert^{*}=\begin{cases}0\quad&\left\lVert\mathbold{x}\right\rVert_{*}\leq 1\\
\infty\quad&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right)^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mathbold{x}\right\rVert^{2}_{*}.

Hence

\displaystyle-C(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle\left(\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathbold{\mu}/C(1-\alpha)\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}^{S}_{\alpha}\right)^{*}=\begin{cases}0\quad&\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathbold{\mu}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}^{S}_{\alpha*}\leq C(1-\alpha)\\
-\infty\quad&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and

\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\textbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbold{\mu}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\right)^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\textbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbold{\mu}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\mathbold{\mu}^{\top}\hat{\textbf{X}}\hat{\textbf{X}}^{\top}\mathbold{\mu}.

Finally, noting that (cf.
[
Bertsimas et al. 2011
;
Mafusalov and Uryasev 2016
]
),

\mathopen{\hbox{${\langle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\langle}$}}\mathbold{\mu}\mathclose{\hbox{${\rangle}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${\rangle}$}}^{S}_{\alpha*}=\max\big\{\,\left\lVert\mathbold{\mu}\right\rVert_{1},l(1-\alpha)\left\lVert\mathbold{\mu}\right\rVert_{\infty}\,\big\}

and proceeding with the maximization of the Lagrangian w.r.t. dual variables
\mathbold{\mu}
completes the proof.
∎
