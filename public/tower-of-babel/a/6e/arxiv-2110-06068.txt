On multi-species diffusion with size exclusion
Katharina Hopf, Martin Burger

Abstract
. We revisit a classical continuum model for the diffusion of multiple species with size-exclusion constraint, which leads to a degenerate nonlinear cross-diffusion system. The purpose of this article is twofold: first, it aims at a systematic study of the question of existence of weak solutions and their long-time asymptotic behaviour. Second, it provides a weak-strong stability estimate for a wide range of coefficients, which had been missing so far. In order to achieve the results mentioned above, we exploit the formal gradient-flow structure of the model with respect to a logarithmic entropy, which leads to best estimates in the full-interaction case, where all cross-diffusion coefficients are non-zero. Those are crucial to obtain the minimal Sobolev regularity needed for a weak-strong stability result. For meaningful cases when some of the coefficients vanish, we provide a novel existence result based on approximation by the full-interaction case.

Approach

Exploiting the formal gradient-flow structure (

1.3.
New aspects of the present results

As discussed in the previous paragraph, so far, uniqueness results have only been available for small data settings or under rather restrictive assumptions on the model.
In the present work we remove such smallness restrictions on the model
and establish a weak-strong stability estimate for system (
1.1
) with general coefficients satisfying
(H1)
,
(H2enumi)
, see Theorem
1.3
.
Our method relies on an adaptation of the technique in
[
12
]

devised for proving weak-strong uniqueness in energy-reaction-(cross-)diffusion systems. This approach in turn was motivated by an adjusted relative entropy method due to Fischer
[
11
]
for reaction-diffusion systems.
A crucial ingredient allowing us to handle the new difficulties induced by the size-exclusion effect is a local coercivity-type estimate (cf. inequality (
3.21
)), which we deduce from the strict convexity of the entropy density.

We further establish existence of weak solutions and equilibration in the long-time limit under a fairly mild hypothesis on
K_{ij}
arguably covering all models relevant for applications.
Here, our idea is to take advantage of the existence result for full interactions
[
14
]
, which provides us with a family of approximate solutions. Upon an analysis of the compactness and convergence properties of these approximate solutions, we then
take a
‘vanishing-interaction limit’
, where the artificially introduced interaction parameters are being sent to zero, to eventually obtain weak solutions to the partially interacting system. Owing to poor compactness properties linked to the degeneracy of such systems, we are not able to establish a strong entropy dissipation inequality nor
monotonicity of the entropy in time.
However, we will show that a version of the strong entropy dissipation inequality holds true up to an error term (cf. inequality (
4.4
)) that is initially only known to tend to zero along a
some
sequence of times
t_{k}\to\infty
, along which stronger convergence properties hold.
Inequality (
4.4
), however, allows us to upgrade this result and prove the strong convergence to equilibrium along
any
sequence
t\to\infty
.

1.4.
Outline

In Section
2
, we use a change of variables to remove the degeneracy of equation (
1.1
) that is linked to the volume constraint.
We further establish basic algebraic identities and coercivity estimates involving the mobility matrix that will be needed in subsequent parts.
The stability results for fully interacting systems
are proved in Section
3
.
In Section
4
, the full-interaction hypothesis is dropped, and general, partially interacting systems relevant for applications are introduced. For these problems, we establish existence of weak solutions and convergence to equilibrium.

This paper has two appendices. Appendix
A
contains a technical lemma used in the proof of the weak-strong stability estimate.
For details concerning our notations we refer to Appendix
B
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The gradient system for
n
species

Consistent with the size constraint, the mobility
\mathbb{M}
underlying system (
1.1
) degenerates in the direction
\textbf{n}_{\mathcal{S}}:=(1,\dots,1)^{T}
normal to the hyperplane containing
\mathcal{S}
.
This degeneracy can be removed by means of an affine coordinate transformation mapping the
n
-dimensional domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}:=\left\{U^{\prime}=(U_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,U_{n}^{\prime})\in(0,\infty)^{n}:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}^{\prime}<1\right\},

diffeomorphically onto
\mathcal{S}
. Such a transformation can be realised by the map

(2.2)

\displaystyle\Psi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{S},\qquad U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{n})\mapsto(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i},U_{1},\dots,U_{n}).

By means of this change of variables, the gradient structure with respect to
\mathcal{S}

gives rise to a gradient flow for the vector of n densities
U(t,x)\in\mathcal{D}
, as will be detailed below.
Of course, we could have immediately written down a PDE system for
U
equivalent to (
1.1
) by replacing
u_{0}
by
1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
and rearranging terms.
However, we hope that the following few expository paragraphs
may help better understand some of the underlying structures.

Entropy density

For
U\in\mathcal{D}
we define the pulled-back entropy density

(2.3)

\displaystyle\hat{h}(U):=h(\Psi(U))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda(U_{i})+\lambda\big(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\big),

where as before
\lambda(s)=s\log s-s+1
.
Observe that
\hat{h}\in C(\mathcal{\overline{D}})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
is strictly convex.
Abbreviating
v:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
, we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle D_{i}\hat{h}(U)=\log(\uvec_i)-\log(v)=\log\big(\tfrac{\uvec_i}{v}\big)

and

(2.5)

\displaystyle D^{2}\hat{h}(U)=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac{1}{U_{1}},\dots,\tfrac{1}{U_{n}})+\tfrac{1}{v}E,

where
E_{ij}=1
for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Mobility

Since the Onsager operator
\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{M}(u)\nabla\,\cdot\,)
is acting on the dual variables, computing the appropriate mobility
\mathbb{\hat{M}}
on
\mathcal{D}
requires using the inverse
\Phi:=\Psi^{-1}
of
\Psi
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Phi:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{D},\quad u=(u_{0},\dots,u_{n})\mapsto U=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n}).

We may now define the
(n\times n)
mobility matrix

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U):=D\Phi_{|\Psi(U)}\mathbb{M}(\Psi(U))D\Phi^{T}_{|\Psi(U)},\quad U\in\mathcal{D},

which is again symmetric. (Since
\Phi
is affine, the argument in
D\Phi_{|\Psi(U)}
may be dropped.)

Gradient-flow equation

The above ingredients determine the gradient-flow equation (
1.4
) in the new variables as

(2.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\partial_{t}U-\nabla\cdot(\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)\nabla D\hat{h}(U))&=0&\text{ in }\quad(0,T^{*})\times\Omega,\\
\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)\nabla D\hat{h}(U)\cdot\nu&=0&\text{ on }\quad(0,T^{*})\times\partial\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

Introducing the
‘diffusion matrix’

(2.9)

\displaystyle\hat{A}(U):=\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)D^{2}\hat{h}(U),

equation (
2.8
) may further be written as the PDE system

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}\partial_{t}U-\nabla\cdot(\hat{A}(U)\nabla U)&=0&\text{ in }\quad(0,T^{*})\times\Omega,\\
(\hat{A}(U)\nabla U)\cdot\nu&=0&\text{ on }\quad(0,T^{*})\times\partial\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

A direct computation yields the following explicit form for the matrix
\hat{A}(U)

(2.11)

\hat{A}_{ij}(U)=\delta_{ij}\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}K_{il}u_{l}+K_{i0}(1-\sum_{l=1}^{n}u_{l})\right]-(K_{ij}-K_{i0})u_{i},\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Observe that system (
2.10
) can also directly be obtained from (
1.1
) by inserting
u_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
and rearranging terms.

Entropy dissipation

We emphasize that the information contained in (
2.8
) is the same as in the original system.
In particular, we assert that

(2.12)

\displaystyle D^{2}\hat{h}\mathbb{\hat{M}}D^{2}\hat{h}=D\Psi^{T}(D^{2}h\circ\Psi)(\mathbb{M}\circ\Psi)(D^{2}h\circ\Psi)D\Psi.

This follows from the identities
D^{2}\hat{h}=D\Psi^{T}(D^{2}h\circ\Psi)D\Psi
and

(2.13)

\displaystyle(D\Psi D\Phi)\mathbb{M}(u)(D\Psi D\Phi)^{T}=\mathbb{M}(u),

which can be verified explicitly by an elementary calculation using the structure of
\mathbb{M}
.
Hence, as a consequence of (
2.12
), the dissipated quantities of the flows in transformed and original coordinates are indeed the same:

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\hat{\mathscr{P}}(U):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\nabla U_{i}\cdot(D^{2}\hat{h}(U)\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)D^{2}\hat{h}(U))_{ij}\nabla U_{j}\\
\hskip 68.99895pt=\sum_{k,l=0}^{n}\nabla u_{k}\cdot(D^{2}h(u)\mathbb{M}(u)D^{2}h(u))_{kl}\nabla u_{l}=:\mathscr{P}(u).\end{multlined}

Parabolic structure

Assuming a mild non-degeneracy condition on the coefficients
K_{ij}
,
the problem formulated in terms of
n
species enjoys full parabolicity in a classical sense, as will be explained in the following.
For simplicity, we only consider the case of a fully interacting system obeying
(H2enumi)
, which guarantees that

\kappa:=\min_{i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\},i\not=j}K_{ij}
is strictly positive.
It has been shown in the proof of
[
2
, Lemma 2.3]
that

(2.16)

\displaystyle\zeta^{T}D^{2}\hat{h}(U)\hat{A}(U)\zeta\geq\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}^{-1}\zeta_{i}^{2}\qquad\text{ for }U\in\mathcal{D}.

See also Corollary
2.4
below, which actually implies this bound (although our reasoning is different and perhaps easier to grasp).
In terms of
\mathbb{\hat{M}}
, inequality (
2.16
) takes the form

(2.17)

\displaystyle\zeta^{T}D^{2}\hat{h}(U)\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)D^{2}\hat{h}(U)\zeta\geq\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}^{-1}\zeta_{i}^{2}\qquad\text{ for }U\in\mathcal{D}.

Since for each
U\in\mathcal{D}
the matrix
D^{2}\hat{h}(U)
is invertible, the last inequality
implies that
\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)>0
for all
U\in\mathcal{D}
, where we recall that
\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)
is symmetric. Hence, by continuity,
for every compact subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\subset\mathcal{D}

there
exists a positive constant

c_{*}(\mathcal{K})>0
such that

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)\geq c_{*}(\mathcal{K})\operatorname{diag}(1,\dots,1)\qquad\text{ for all }U\in\mathcal{K}.

We further note that, since for every
U\in\mathcal{D}
the diffusion matrix
\hat{A}(U)
is the product of the positive definite symmetric matrices
\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)
and
D^{2}\hat{h}(U)
, it has its spectrum
\sigma(\hat{A}(U))
contained in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
Therefore, thanks to Amann’s work
[
1
]
, which requires the real parts of the eigenvectors of
\hat{A}(U)
to be strictly positive, the Cauchy problem associated with system (
2.10
) has a unique local-in-time smooth solution for Lipschitz regular initial data
U^{\mathrm{in}}
satisfying
U^{\mathrm{in}}(\overline{\Omega})\subset\mathcal{D}
.

Remark
.

In fact, under hypothesis
(H2enumi)
, the inclusion
\sigma(\hat{A}(U))\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
sufficient for applying
[
1
]

even holds for all
U\in\mathcal{\overline{D}}

(and thus by continuity also in an open neighbourhood of
\mathcal{\overline{D}}
).
This is a consequence of
[
15
, Lemma 2.3]
.
Hence, local wellposedness is even valid under the more general condition
U^{\mathrm{in}}(\overline{\Omega})\subset\mathcal{\overline{D}}
.
Preservation of positivity and size constraint are, however, not fully trivial if
U^{\mathrm{in}}(\overline{\Omega})\cap\partial\mathcal{D}\not=\emptyset
and will have to be verified a posteriori.

Equivalence of the two PDE systems

The
n
-species diffusion system (
2.10
) precisely agrees with the last
n
components of the original system (
1.1
). This may be verified explicitly by using formula (
2.11
) and the relation
\sum_{i=0}^{n}u_{i}=1
.
Since the equation for the zeroth component
u_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
in (
1.1
) is obtained by summing up the equations for the components
i=1,\dots,n
, system (
2.10
) is equivalent to the original problem. For later reference, we formulate this observation for the concept of weak solutions in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

System (
1.10
) for
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
is equivalent to the weak form of (
2.10
), i.e. to

(2.19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}U_{i},\psi\rangle\,\differential t+\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\Omega}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{A}_{ij}(U)\nabla U_{j}\cdot\nabla\psi\,\differential x\differential t=0

for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
More precisely,
u=(u_{0},\dots,u_{n})
being a weak solution of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
is equivalent to
U=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
satisfying

u_{i}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
,
\partial_{t}u_{i}\in L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*})
for all
T<T^{*}
and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, fulfilling
U(t,x)\in\mathcal{\overline{D}}
for a.e.
(t,x)\in(0,T^{*})\times\Omega
, and obeying (
2.19
) for all
T<T^{*}
and all
\psi\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.

We should note that the dimension reduction leading to full parabolicity comes at a price: the entropy function in the new coordinates induces a strong coupling between the
n
unknowns, potentially introducing new difficulties in the uniqueness and stability analysis.

2.2.
Algebraic estimates

Here, we establish coercivity bounds for the quadratic form induced by the symmetric and positive semi-definite matrix

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(u):=D^{2}h(u)\mathbb{M}(u)D^{2}h(u),\quad u\in\mathcal{S},

on the tangent space
T_{u}\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{S}
.
Since the vector
\textbf{n}_{\mathcal{S}}:=(1,\dots,1)
points in the direction perpendicular to the hyperplane containing
\mathcal{S}
, we can identify

(2.21)

\displaystyle T_{u}\mathcal{S}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}:\xi\cdot\textbf{n}_{\mathcal{S}}=0\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K_{ij}
satisfy Hypotheses
(H1)
&
(H2)
.
Further recall that the mobility matrix
\mathbb{M}
is given by (
1.6
), and let
\mathbb{P}
be given by (
2.20
) (with
h
as in (
1.3
)).
For all
u\in\mathcal{S}
and all
\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{S}

(2.22)

\displaystyle\xi^{T}\mathbb{P}(u)\xi=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}\left|\frac{\xi_{i}}{u_{i}}-\frac{\xi_{j}}{u_{j}}\right|^{2}.

While identity (
2.22
) holds for general
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
, only tangent vectors
\xi
are relevant in our analysis.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Observe that
D^{2}h(u)_{ij}=\frac{1}{u_{i}}\delta_{ij}
, and hence

(2.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{ij}(u)=\frac{1}{u_{j}}\delta_{ij}\sum_{k=0}^{n}K_{ik}u_{k}-K_{ij}.

We then compute

(2.24)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{P}_{ij}(u)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k}K_{ik}\frac{u_{k}}{u_{i}}\xi_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}

(2.25)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}\big(\left|\tfrac{\xi_{i}}{u_{i}}\right|^{2}+\left|\tfrac{\xi_{j}}{u_{j}}\right|^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}2\tfrac{\xi_{i}}{u_{i}}\tfrac{\xi_{j}}{u_{j}},

where second step uses the symmetry of
K_{ij}
. The last line equals the right-hand side of (
2.22
) and the assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Matrix coercivity estimates)
.

Assume the hypotheses and use the notations of Lemma
2.2
.
Then for all
u\in\mathcal{S}
and
\xi\in T_{u}\mathcal{S}

(2.26)

\displaystyle\xi^{T}\mathbb{P}(u)\xi\geq\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n}\kappa^{(\alpha)}\bigg(\frac{1}{u_{\alpha}}|\xi_{\alpha}|^{2}+u_{\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{u_{j}}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\bigg),

where
\kappa^{(\alpha)}:=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\ell:\ell\not=\alpha}K_{\alpha\ell}\geq 0
for
\alpha\in\{0,\dots,n\}
.

In particular,

(2.27)

\displaystyle\xi^{T}\mathbb{P}(u)\xi\geq\min_{\alpha,\ell:\alpha\not=\ell}K_{\alpha\ell}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{u_{j}}|\xi_{j}|^{2}.

Proof.

Identity (
2.22
) allows us to estimate

(2.28)

\displaystyle\xi^{T}\mathbb{P}(u)\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n}\sum_{j:j\not=\alpha}K_{\alpha j}u_{\alpha}u_{j}\left|\frac{\xi_{\alpha}}{u_{\alpha}}-\frac{\xi_{j}}{u_{j}}\right|^{2}

(2.29)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n}\kappa^{(\alpha)}u_{\alpha}\sum_{j:j\not=\alpha}u_{j}\bigg(\frac{1}{u_{\alpha}^{2}}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{1}{u_{j}^{2}}\xi_{j}^{2}-2\frac{1}{u_{\alpha}u_{j}}\xi_{\alpha}\xi_{j}\bigg)

(2.30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n}\kappa^{(\alpha)}u_{\alpha}\bigg(\frac{1-u_{\alpha}}{u_{\alpha}^{2}}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}+\sum_{j:j\not=\alpha}\frac{1}{u_{j}}\xi_{j}^{2}+2\frac{1}{u_{\alpha}}\xi_{\alpha}^{2}\bigg)

(2.31)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n}\kappa^{(\alpha)}\bigg(\frac{1}{u_{\alpha}}|\xi_{\alpha}|^{2}+u_{\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{u_{j}}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\bigg),

where the penultimate equality uses the fact that
\xi\cdot\textbf{n}_{\mathcal{S}}=0
, that is,
\sum_{j=0}^{n}\xi_{j}=0
. This shows (
2.26
).

To infer inequality (
2.27
) it suffices to note that the right-hand side of (
2.26
) is bounded below by

(2.32)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\min_{\alpha,\ell:\alpha\not=\ell}K_{\alpha\ell}\cdot\sum_{\beta=0}^{n}\bigg(\frac{1}{u_{\beta}}|\xi_{\beta}|^{2}+u_{\beta}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{u_{j}}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\bigg)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\alpha,\ell:\alpha\not=\ell}K_{\alpha\ell}\cdot\bigg(2\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{u_{j}}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\bigg).

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
K_{ij}
satisfy Hypotheses
(H1)
,
(H2)
and define

(2.33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{\hat{P}}(U):=D^{2}\hat{h}(U)\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U)D^{2}\hat{h}(U),\qquad U\in\mathcal{D}.

Then, for all
U\in\mathcal{D}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(2.34)

\displaystyle\zeta^{T}\mathbb{\hat{P}}(U)\zeta\geq\kappa\bigg(|\sum_{l=1}^{n}\zeta_{l}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{U_{i}}|\zeta_{i}|^{2}\bigg),

where
\kappa:=\min_{j,k\in\{0,\dots,n\},j\not=k}K_{jk}
.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
2.3
by inserting the tangent vector
\xi:=D\Psi\zeta
and performing some elementary manipulations. Note that here we have also used the identity (
2.12
). ∎

2.3.
Existence of weak solutions under
hypothesis
(H2enumi)

Observe that under hypothesis
(H2enumi)
, i.e. for
\kappa:=\min_{i\not=j}K_{ij}>0
,

2.4
implies that
\zeta^{T}\mathbb{\hat{P}}(U)\zeta\gtrsim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{U_{i}}|\zeta_{i}|^{2}
.
This estimate, combined with the convexity properties of
\hat{h}
, allows us to apply
Theorem 2 of
[

]
to infer
global existence of weak solutions to system (

on the equivalence between the original and the transformed system:

Theorem 2.5

(See
[
14
]
& L.
2.1
)
.

Let
\min_{i\not=j}K_{ij}>0
. Let

satisfy

for a.e.

.
Then there exists a function

with

for a.e.

that satisfies system (

) in the weak sense and takes the initial datum

. Furthermore, the regularity

\displaystyle\sqrt{u_{i}}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty);H^{1}(\Omega))\qquad\textit{ for all }i\in\{0,\dots,n\}

holds true.

The main feature of the proof of Theorem

consists in a transformation to the Legendre conjugate variables

upon which the system can be regularised by a standard higher-order elliptic term while preserving the key entropy estimate.

assumes that

for a.e.

.
It is further necessary to point out that the regularity (

[

.

appears (formulated for the

]
.
But since it is stated in a slightly weaker form, we will not use it here.

3.
Stability results for full interactions

3.1.
Entropy dissipation balance

Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
both rely on the following entropy dissipation identity.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u=(u_{0},\dots,u_{n})
be a weak solution according to Definition
1.1
enjoying the regularity
\sqrt{u_{i}}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T^{*});H^{1}(\Omega))
for all
i
.
Then for all
T\in(0,T^{*})

(3.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h(u(T))\,\differential x=\int_{\Omega}h(u(0))\,\differential x-\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{P}(u)\,\differential x\differential t,

where
\mathscr{P}(u)=\sum_{i,j}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{ij}(u)\nabla u_{j}=\sum_{i,j}\nabla u_{i}\cdot(D^{2}h(u)\mathbb{M}(u)D^{2}h(u))_{ij}\nabla u_{j}
(cf. (
2.1
)).

As a consequence, for all
0\leq s<T<T^{*}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h(u(T))\,\differential x=\int_{\Omega}h(u(s))\,\differential x-\int_{s}^{T}\!\int_{\Omega}\mathscr{P}(u)\,\differential x\differential t.

Proof of Prop.
3.1
.

We begin by observing that
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))^{1+n}
. This is a consequence of the regularity hypotheses in Definition
1.1
combined with standard embeddings for Bochner spaces. Formally, identity (
3.1
) follows upon testing (
1.10
) with
D_{i}h(u)
, taking the sum over
i
and applying a chain rule.
However, the Boltzmann-type entropy density
h
is not smooth up to
u_{i}=0
, and hence, we need to regularise first. The procedure is very standard, but since it has not been carried out for this particular system, some details are required.

For
\varepsilon>0
let
h_{\varepsilon}(u):=h(u_{0}+\varepsilon,\dots,u_{n}+\varepsilon)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda(u_{i}+\varepsilon).

Then

(3.3)

\displaystyle D_{i}h_{\varepsilon}(u)=\log(u_i+\ve)\quad\in\,L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T^{*});H^{1}(\Omega))

and
D_{ik}h_{\varepsilon}(u)=\tfrac{1}{u_{i}+\varepsilon}\delta_{ik}
for all
i,k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
,
where
\delta_{ik}
denotes the Kronecker symbol.

Choosing
\psi=D_{i}h_{\varepsilon}(u)
in the weak equation (
1.10
) for
u_{i}
and taking the sum
\sum_{i=0}^{n}
gives

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}(u(T))\,\differential x-\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}(u(0))\,\differential x=-\int_{0}^{T}\!\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j,k}D_{ik}h_{\varepsilon}(u)A_{ij}(u)\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla u_{k}\,\differential x\differential t.\qquad

To pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, we wish to apply the dominated convergence theorem.
In the terms on the left-hand side this is immediate thanks to the bound
0\leq u_{i}\leq 1
.
Concerning the integral on the right-hand side, we need appropriate information about its integrand.
For this purpose, we compute for
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
, using the formula (
1.6
) for
\mathbb{M}
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}D_{ik}h_{\varepsilon}(u)A_{ij}(u)\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla u_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u_{i}+\varepsilon}\mathbb{M}_{ij}(u)\frac{1}{u_{j}}\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla u_{i}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u_{i}+\varepsilon}u_{i}(\delta_{ij}\sum_{l=0}^{n}K_{il}u_{l}-K_{ij}u_{j})\frac{1}{u_{j}}\nabla u_{j}\cdot\nabla u_{i}

(3.7)

4.
Existence and time asymptotics for partial interactions

In this section, we perform a global weak existence analysis for systems where not necessarily all species are interacting.
Except for the case of full interactions, which is covered by
[
14

Here, we take advantage of this result for full interactions, which easily gives rise to a family of approximate solutions to more general, partially interacting systems, and then perform a vanishing-interaction limit. Remark 4.3

(Generalised Sobolev gradients)
.

It is necessary to comment on the meaning of the gradients appearing in identity (

) (resp. (

)), since for

terms of the form

(resp.

) may not define a distribution in the classical sense. Let us first note that the regularity

and

‘
\nabla\sqrt{u_{i}}
’
and
‘
\nabla u_{i}
’
almost everywhere in the set

and uniquely in the weighted Lebesgue space

or

and

Simple examples show that under the general hypotheses of Theorem

, we cannot expect gradients to exist on a set larger than this. In fact, letting

would be the natural candidate for a solution. Yet, under the hypotheses of Theorem

does not need to have a pointwise meaning.
If one focuses on the question of giving a meaning, on a set (possibly) larger than

for

Let us note that the vector fields

Proof of Theorem

.

Below, the mass conservation property (

Similarly to (

), we infer from Lemma

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}(u)\chi_{\{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}u_{\alpha}>0\}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}|\nabla\sqrt{u_{\alpha}}|^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}\sum_{i}|\nabla\sqrt{u_{\alpha}u_{i}}|^{2}

(4.46)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i,l}K_{il}\big|\sqrt{u_{l}}\nabla\sqrt{u_{i}}-\sqrt{u_{i}}\nabla\sqrt{u_{l}}\big|^{2}\chi_{\{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}u_{\alpha}>0\}},

where we recall that the gradients in the last line are to be understood as explained in Remark

\displaystyle\sqrt{u_{\alpha}}(t_{k})\to\sqrt{\overline{u}_{\alpha}}\qquad\text{ in }H^{1}(\Omega).

We assert that this further gives

), pick

such that

(such

) combined with (

\displaystyle\chi_{\{u_{\hat{\alpha}}(t_{k})>0\}}\big(\sqrt{u_{\hat{\alpha}}}\nabla\sqrt{u_{i}}-\sqrt{u_{i}}\nabla\sqrt{u_{\hat{\alpha}}}\big)|_{t_{k}}\;\overset{k\to\infty}{\to}\;0\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega),

Appendix A
An auxiliary estimate

The estimate asserted in the lemma below has previously been employed in
[
12
, Theorem 2.8, Case
\mathcal{A}_{+}
]
in a similar context. For the purpose of being self-contained, we here also recall its elementary proof.

Lemma A.1

(Cf.
[
12
]
)
.

Let
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be convex, let
f\in C^{3}_{b}(G)
and let
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{N},\tilde{v}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega},G)
. Then, for a.e.
x\in\Omega
such that
v(x)\in G

(A.1)

\big|\nabla\big(f(v)-f(\tilde{v})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}f(\tilde{v})(v_{j}-\tilde{v}_{j})\big)\big|\\
\lesssim_{\|f\|_{C^{3}(G)}}|\nabla v-\nabla\tilde{v}||v-\tilde{v}|+\|\nabla\tilde{v}\|_{L^{\infty}}|v-\tilde{v}|^{2}\quad\text{at the point }x,

where we abbreviated
\nabla f(v):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}D_{k}f(v)\nabla v_{k}
.

Proof.

Using the definition of
\nabla f(v)
and rearranging terms, we may rewrite

(A.2)

\displaystyle\nabla\big(f(v)-f(\tilde{v})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}f(\tilde{v})(v_{j}-\tilde{v}_{j})\big)

(A.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(D_{k}f(v)\nabla v_{k}-D_{k}f(\tilde{v})\nabla\tilde{v}_{k}\big)-\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}f(\tilde{v})(\nabla v_{j}-\nabla\tilde{v}_{j})-\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}D_{lj}f(\tilde{v})(v_{j}-\tilde{v}_{j})\nabla\tilde{v}_{l}

(A.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(D_{k}f(v)-D_{k}f(\tilde{v}))(\nabla v_{k}-\nabla\tilde{v}_{k})

(A.5)

\displaystyle\hskip 86.25pt+\sum_{l=1}^{N}\big(D_{l}f(v)-D_{l}f(\tilde{v})-\sum_{j=1}^{N}D_{j}D_{l}f(\tilde{v})(v_{j}-\tilde{v}_{j})\big)\nabla\tilde{v}_{l}.

Notice that, in the last step, some indices have been relabelled.
The assertion now follows by means of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and an application of Taylor’s theorem to
D_{l}f\in C^{2}_{b}(G)
,
l\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
∎

Appendix B
Notations

For definiteness, we here provide some details concerning our notations, most of which are quite classical.

•

The convention
K_{ii}:=0
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
is adopted throughout the paper.

•

We will further adopt the convention that
K_{ij}\cdot Q\equiv 0
whenever
K_{ij}=0
, even if the quantity ‘
Q
’ is not well-defined mathematically. This will simplify notations as it avoids having to restrict sums involving
K_{ij}
to pairs
\{i,j\}
satisfying
K_{ij}>0
.

•

Summation: since we are dealing not only with
(n+1)
but also with
n
dimensional systems, we avoid the usual summation convention. Unspecified sums involving a Latin summation index (such as
i,j,k
) always indicate summing over all indices, that is, we let
\sum_{i}:=\sum_{i=0}^{n}
etc. Likewise

\sum_{i\not=j}:=\sum_{i\in\{0,\dots,n\}}\sum_{j\in\{0,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i\}}
and

\sum_{j:j\not=i}:=\sum_{j\in\{0,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i\}}
.

•

We further let

\min_{i\not=j}=\min_{i\in\{0,\dots,n\}}\min_{j\in\{0,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i\}}
and
\min_{i:i\not=j}=\min_{i\in\{0,\dots,n\}\setminus\{j\}}
.

•

The notation
A(u)\nabla u
for
u=u(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
and
A(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{(1+n)\times(1+n)}
will be used as a short hand for the
d\times(1+n)
matrix
(A(u)\nabla u)_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}(u)\nabla u_{j}
. Analogous short-hand notations will be adopted for the matrices
\mathbb{\hat{M}}(U),\hat{A}(U)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

•

For an interval
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
,
C(I;L^{2}_{\mathrm{weak}}(\Omega))
denotes the space of functions
v:I\to L^{2}(\Omega)
such that
t\mapsto v(t)
is weakly continuous in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

•

For non-negative quantities
A,B
, we write
A\lesssim B

to indicate that there exists a constant
C\in(0,\infty)
that only depends on fixed parameters
such that
A\leq CB
.
We define
A\gtrsim B
by
B\lesssim A
, and

A\sim B
by [
A\lesssim B
and
A\gtrsim B
].

•

For a smooth manifold
M
and
p\in M
, we denote by
T_{p}M
the tangent space of
M
at
p
.

•

For a sufficiently smooth function

and

. Analogous notations are used if

etc.
We further abbreviate

•

Unless specified otherwise, we abbreviate
\overline{u}_{i}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}u_{i}\,\differential x

.
