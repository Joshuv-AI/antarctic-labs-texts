Real exponential sums over primes and prime gaps
Luan Alberto Ferreira

Abstract
. We prove that given λ ∈ ℝ \lambda\in\mathbb{R} such that 0 < λ < 1 0<\lambda<1 , then π ⁡ ( x + x λ ) − π ⁡ ( x ) ∼ x λ log ⁡ ( x ) \pi(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi(x)\sim\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{\log(x)} . This solves a long-standing problem concerning the existence of primes in short intervals. In particular, we give a positive answer (for all sufficiently large number) to some old conjectures about prime numbers, such as Legendre’s conjecture about the existence of at least two primes between two consecutive squares.

The adaptation of Newman’s proof

The PNT, i.e., the affirmation

\pi(x)\sim\displaystyle\frac{x}{\log(x)},

is among the most beautiful and celebrated theorems in all of mathematics. In 1980, D. J. Newman found an ingenious proof of this statement, which can be the summarized in the following
10

Step 1:
Let
\theta(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\log(p)
the first Chebyshev function. Show that
\theta(x)\sim x
implies PNT.

In fact, these two statements are equivalents, but we only need one direction.

Step 2:
Show that if the integral
\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\theta(t)-t}{t^{2}}dt

\theta(x)\sim x
.

Step 3:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and suppose that for some
c>0

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)\sim\exp(cx^{1-\lambda}).

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{A}(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi_{A}(x)}{x^{\lambda}/\log(x)}\leq\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(1-\lambda)]-1}{c(1-\lambda)}.

Suppose by absurd that for some
\varepsilon>0
there are arbitrarily large

such that

\pi_{A}(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi_{A}(x)\geq\left(\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(1-\lambda)]-1}{c(1-\lambda)}+\varepsilon\right)\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{\log(x)}

and let

to simplify the notation.

For these
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and working where
w
is increasing,

\begin{split}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
x<a\leq x+x^{\lambda}\end{subarray}}w(a)&\geq\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\lceil u\rceil-1}w(x+j)\\
&\geq\displaystyle\int_{x-1}^{x+\lceil u\rceil-1}w(t)dt\\
&\geq\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+u-1}w(t)dt\\
&=\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+u}w(t)dt-\displaystyle\int_{x+u-1}^{x+u}w(t)dt\\
&\geq\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+u}w(t)dt-w(x+u).\end{split}

This implies

\begin{split}\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x+x^{\lambda}]}w(a)}{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)}&=1+\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\

&\geq 1+\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+u}w(t)dt}{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)}-\displaystyle\frac{w(x+u)}{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)}\\

&\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}1+\left(\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(1-\lambda)]-1}{c(1-\lambda)}+\varepsilon\right)c(1-\lambda)-0\\
&=\exp[c(1-\lambda)]+c\varepsilon(1-\lambda),\end{split}

by lemma
3.2
. Hence, by proposition
2.2

\exp[c(1-\lambda)]\geq\exp[c(1-\lambda)]+c\varepsilon(1-\lambda),

which implies, in turn, that
\varepsilon\leq 0
, absurd. ∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and suppose that for some
c>0
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)\sim\exp(cx^{1-\lambda}).

Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{A}(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi_{A}(x)}{x^{\lambda}/\log(x)}\geq\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp[c(\lambda-1)]}{c(1-\lambda)}.

Proof.

Suppose by absurd that for some
\varepsilon>0
there are arbitrarily large
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\pi_{A}(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi_{A}(x)\leq\left(\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp[c(\lambda-1)]}{c(1-\lambda)}-\varepsilon\right)\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{\log(x)}

and let

to simplify the notation.

For these
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and working where
w
is increasing,

\begin{split}\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\
x<a\leq x+x^{\lambda}\end{subarray}}w(a)&\leq\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor v\rfloor-1}w(x+x^{\lambda}-j)\\
&\leq\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-\lfloor v\rfloor+1}^{x+x^{\lambda}+1}w(t)dt\\
&\leq\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-v}^{x+x^{\lambda}+1}w(t)dt\\
&=\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-v}^{x+x^{\lambda}}w(t)dt+\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}}^{x+x^{\lambda}+1}w(t)dt\\
&\leq\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-v}^{x+x^{\lambda}}w(t)dt+w(x+x^{\lambda}+1).\end{split}

This implies

\begin{split}\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x+x^{\lambda}]}w(a)}{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)}&=1+\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\in A\\

&\leq 1+\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-v}^{x+x^{\lambda}}w(t)dt}{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)}+\displaystyle\frac{w(x+x^{\lambda}+1)}{\displaystyle\sum_{a\in A[x]}w(a)}\\

&\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}1+\left(\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp[c(\lambda-1)]}{c(1-\lambda)}-\varepsilon\right)c(1-\lambda)\exp[c(1-\lambda)]+0\\
&=\exp[c(1-\lambda)][1-c\varepsilon(1-\lambda)],\end{split}

by lemma
3.3
. Hence, by proposition
2.2

\exp[c(1-\lambda)]\leq\exp[c(1-\lambda)][1-c\varepsilon(1-\lambda)],

which implies, in turn, that
\varepsilon\leq 0
, absurd. ∎

j\in\{0,\ldots,J\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\in I_{j}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\leq\displaystyle\frac{Sc(1-\lambda)}{\lambda}\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{\log(x-jx^{\lambda})}{\log(x)}}_{\leq\ 1}\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x-jx^{\lambda})^{\lambda}}}_{\leq\ (1+j)^{\lambda}}\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x-jx^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}}_{\leq\ \exp[-c(1-\lambda)j]}\leq\displaystyle\frac{Sc(1-\lambda)}{\lambda}\displaystyle\frac{(1+j)^{\lambda}}{\exp[c(1-\lambda)j]},

by lemma
3.7
. Also note that
x-(J+1)x^{\lambda}<x_{0}+x^{\lambda}
, which implies that

\begin{split}\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x-(J+1)x^{\lambda}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}&\leq\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x_{0}+x^{\lambda}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\\
&\leq\displaystyle\frac{(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})w(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})},\end{split}

and since that

\displaystyle\frac{(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})w(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}=\displaystyle\frac{(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})c(1-\lambda)\log(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})\exp[c(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}{(x_{0}+x^{\lambda})^{\lambda}\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}0,

there exists
k>0
(independent of
x
) such that

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x-(J+1)x^{\lambda}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\leq k.

Putting all this information together,

\begin{split}\displaystyle\frac{W(x)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}&=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x-(J+1)x^{\lambda}}w(p)+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\displaystyle\sum_{p\in I_{j}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x-(J+1)x^{\lambda}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{p\in I_{j}}w(p)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\\
&\leq k+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{J}\displaystyle\frac{Sc(1-\lambda)}{\lambda}\displaystyle\frac{(1+j)^{\lambda}}{\exp[c(1-\lambda)j]}\\
&<k+\displaystyle\frac{Sc(1-\lambda)}{\lambda}\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(1+j)^{\lambda}}{\exp[c(1-\lambda)j]}<\infty,\end{split}

what concludes the proof. ∎

This may be a little surprising, but we don’t need to change anything!

Step 6

Definition 2.11
.

Define

\begin{matrix}\Psi:&\{s\in\mathbb{C};\ \Re(s)>0\}&\rightarrow&\mathbb{C}\\
&s&\mapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp\left[s\left(\displaystyle\frac{p^{1-\lambda}-1}{1-\lambda}\right)\right]}.\end{matrix}

Proposition 2.12
.

The Laplace transform of the function

\displaystyle\frac{W(g(x))}{\exp[cg(x)^{1-\lambda}]}-1

is

\mathscr{L}(s)=\displaystyle\frac{c(1-\lambda)}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\Psi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
q_{n}>0
such that
g(q_{n})=p_{n}
. Thus, for each
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
,

\begin{split}\mathscr{L}(s)&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\displaystyle\frac{W(g(x))}{\exp[cg(x)^{1-\lambda}]}-1\right)\exp(-sx)dx\\
&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{W(g(x))}{\exp[cg(x)^{1-\lambda}+sx]}dx-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}.\end{split}

To calculate the first integral, we will use the known trick of splitting it into intervals over which
W
is constant. Remembering that
W(x)=0
if
x<2
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{W(g(x))}{\exp[cg(x)^{1-\lambda}+sx]}dx=

\begin{split}{}&=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\int_{q_{n}}^{q_{n+1}}\displaystyle\frac{W(g(x))}{\exp[cg(x)^{1-\lambda}+sx]}dx\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}W(p_{n})\displaystyle\int_{q_{n}}^{q_{n+1}}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp[cg(x)^{1-\lambda}+sx]}dx\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}W(p_{n})\displaystyle\int_{q_{n}}^{q_{n+1}}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp[c+c(1-\lambda)x+sx]}dx\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{1}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}W(p_{n})\cdot\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp[c+c(1-\lambda)x+sx]}\bigg|_{q_{n+1}}^{q_{n}}\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\exp[s/(1-\lambda)]}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}W(p_{n})\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp[cp_{n}^{1-\lambda}+sp_{n}^{1-\lambda}/(1-\lambda)]}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{\exp[cp_{n+1}^{1-\lambda}+sp_{n+1}^{1-\lambda}/(1-\lambda)]}\right]\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\exp[s/(1-\lambda)]}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\left[\displaystyle\frac{W(p_{1})}{\exp[cp_{1}^{1-\lambda}+sp_{1}^{1-\lambda}/(1-\lambda)]}+\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{W(p_{n})-W(p_{n-1})}{\exp[cp_{n}^{1-\lambda}+sp_{n}^{1-\lambda}/(1-\lambda)]}\right]\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\exp[s/(1-\lambda)]}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{w(p)}{\exp[cp^{1-\lambda}+sp^{1-\lambda}/(1-\lambda)]}\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{c(1-\lambda)}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp\left[s\left(\displaystyle\frac{p^{1-\lambda}-1}{1-\lambda}\right)\right]}\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{c(1-\lambda)}{c(1-\lambda)+s}\Psi(s),\end{split}

what concludes the proof. ∎

Step 8

For
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
, define the functions

\Xi(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp[s(p^{1-\lambda}-1)]}\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad\tau(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp(sp^{1-\lambda})}.

Obviously these functions are analytic on
\Re(s)>0
.

Proposition 2.14
.

The function
\Psi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
if, and only if, the function
\Xi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
By assumption, there is an analytic function
s\mapsto F(s)
, defined on an open set containing
\Re(s)\geq 0
, such that

\Psi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=F(s),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. As
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
0<\lambda<1
, then

\Psi((1-\lambda)s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)s}=F((1-\lambda)s),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. Multiplying by
1-\lambda
, one gets

(1-\lambda)\Psi((1-\lambda)s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=(1-\lambda)F((1-\lambda)s),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
, that is

\Xi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=(1-\lambda)F((1-\lambda)s),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. This shows that
\Xi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
.

(\Leftarrow)
By assumption, there is an analytic function
s\mapsto G(s)
, defined on an open set containing
\Re(s)\geq 0
, such that

\Xi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=G(s),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. As
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
0<\lambda<1
, then

\Xi\left(\displaystyle\frac{s}{1-\lambda}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1-\lambda}{s}=G\left(\displaystyle\frac{s}{1-\lambda}\right),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. Dividing by
1-\lambda
, one gets

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\Xi\left(\displaystyle\frac{s}{1-\lambda}\right)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\lambda}G\left(\displaystyle\frac{s}{1-\lambda}\right),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
, that is,

\Psi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\lambda}G\left(\displaystyle\frac{s}{1-\lambda}\right),

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. This shows that
\Psi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
. ∎

Step 9

In turn, the same happens with the function
\tau(s)
.

Proposition 2.15
.

The function
\Xi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
if, and only if, the function
\tau(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
.

Proof.

Note that for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
holds

\begin{split}\Xi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}&=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp[s(p^{1-\lambda}-1)]}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp(sp^{1-\lambda})\exp(-s)}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\\
&=\exp(s)\tau(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\\
&=\exp(s)\left[\tau(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}-\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{s\exp(s)}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\right)\right].\end{split}

But since the function

Step 10

This is our last step, and the point where all our work culminates. First, for
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
, define the function

T(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{2-\lambda}\exp(sp^{1-\lambda})}.

Obviously this function is analytic on
\Re(s)>0
. Now, consider the following well-known theorem.

Theorem 2.16
.

The function

\Phi(s+1)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{s+1}}-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s},

initially defined on
\Re(s)>0
, extends to a meromorphic function on
\Re(s)>-\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}
that is analytic on
\Re(s)\geq 0
.

Proof.

See
[
18
]
. ∎

Let
\Omega(s)
be the extension referred in the theorem
2.16
. The main idea to finish the proof of formulas
1.1
and
1.2
is to calculate the Mellin transform of the function
T(s)
, rewrite the obtained expression in a convenient way, and then calculate the inverse Mellin transform. This will reveal to us the analytic continuation of the function
\tau(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
.

Proposition 2.17
.

The function
\tau(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
, initially defined for
\Re(s)>0
, extends analytically to
\Re(s)>-1
.

Proof.

As mentioned above, calculating the Mellin transform of the function
T(s)
for
\Re(s)>0
, we will obtain

\begin{split}\mathscr{M}(z)&=\mathscr{M}\left(\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{2-\lambda}\exp(sp^{1-\lambda})}\right)\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{2-\lambda}}\mathscr{M}[\exp(-sp^{1-\lambda})]\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)\log(p)}{p^{2-\lambda}}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z)}{p^{(1-\lambda)z}}\\
&=(1-\lambda)\Gamma(z)\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{1+(1-\lambda)(z+1)}}\\
&=(1-\lambda)\Gamma(z)\Phi(1+(1-\lambda)(z+1)).\end{split}

Note that this function is meromorphic on
\Re(z)>-1
with a simple pole at
z=0
, not because of the function
\Phi(1+(1-\lambda)(z+1))
, which is analytic on
\Re(z)>-1
, but because of the function
\Gamma(z)
with its simple pole at
z=0
. Using the information from theorem
2.16
, we can write

\begin{split}\mathscr{M}(z)&=(1-\lambda)\Gamma(z)\left[\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)(z+1)}+\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))\right]\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z)}{z+1}+(1-\lambda)\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1)).\end{split}

Now notice that this function has exponential decay along vetical lines, because
\Gamma(z)
does, and that the function
\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))
doesn’t change that. Therefore, we can get back the function
T(s)
by computing the inverse Mellin transform of
\mathscr{M}(z)
, and write

\begin{split}T(s)&=\mathscr{M}^{-1}(\mathscr{M}(z))\\
&=\mathscr{M}^{-1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z)}{z+1}+(1-\lambda)\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))\right)\\
&=\mathscr{M}^{-1}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(z)}{z+1}\right)+(1-\lambda)\mathscr{M}^{-1}[\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))]\\
&=\exp(-s)-s\cdot\Gamma(0,s)+(1-\lambda)\underbrace{\mathscr{M}^{-1}[\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))]}_{\text{analytic on }\Re(s)>-1},\end{split}

for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\Re(s)>0
. Differentiating twice, one gets

\begin{split}\tau(s)&=T^{\prime\prime}(s)\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\exp(-s)-s\cdot\Gamma(0,s)\right)+(1-\lambda)\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\mathscr{M}^{-1}[\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))]\right)\\
&=\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-s)}{s}+(1-\lambda)\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\mathscr{M}^{-1}[\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))]\right)}_{\text{analytic on }\Re(s)>-1}.\end{split}

Finally, subtracting
\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
on either side of this equation, we get

\tau(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-s)-1}{s}+(1-\lambda)\underbrace{\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\mathscr{M}^{-1}[\Gamma(z)\Omega((1-\lambda)(z+1))]\right)}_{\text{analytic on }\Re(s)>-1}.

But since the function
s\mapsto\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-s)-1}{s}
extends analytically to
\mathbb{C}
, we are done. ∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}(x+\varepsilon x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}-x^{1-\lambda}=\varepsilon(1-\lambda)
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}(x+\varepsilon x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}-x^{1-\lambda}=\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(1+\varepsilon x^{\lambda-1})^{1-\lambda}-1}{x^{\lambda-1}}\overset{\text{LH}}{=}\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon(1-\lambda)}{(1+\varepsilon x^{\lambda-1})^{\lambda}}=\varepsilon(1-\lambda)
. ∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
u=\displaystyle\frac{\alpha x^{\lambda}}{\log(x)}
. Then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+u}w(t)dt}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}=\alpha c(1-\lambda)
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+u}w(t)dt}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\overset{\text{LH}}{=}\displaystyle\frac{w(x+u)(1+u^{\prime})-w(x)}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})c(1-\lambda)x^{-\lambda}}

\qquad\ \ \ \ =\log(x)\left[\displaystyle\frac{\log(x+u)}{\log(x)}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+u)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+u)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}-1\right]+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\log(x+u)\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+u)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+u)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\alpha x^{\lambda-1}[\log(x)-1]}{\log(x)^{2}}

\qquad\ \ \ \ =\log(x)\left[\left(1+o\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{1-\lambda}}\right)\right)\left(1+o\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{1-\lambda}}\right)\right)\left(1+\displaystyle\frac{\alpha c(1-\lambda)}{\log(x)}+O\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log(x)^{2}}\right)\right)-1\right]+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\log(x+u)\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+u)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+u)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\alpha x^{\lambda-1}[\log(x)-1]}{\log(x)^{2}}

\qquad\ \ \ \ \xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\alpha c(1-\lambda).
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
v=\displaystyle\frac{\alpha x^{\lambda}}{\log(x)}
. Then
\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-v}^{x+x^{\lambda}}w(t)dt}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}=\alpha c(1-\lambda)\exp[c(1-\lambda)]

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\int_{x+x^{\lambda}-v}^{x+x^{\lambda}}w(t)dt}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\overset{\text{LH}}{=}\displaystyle\frac{w(x+x^{\lambda})(1+\lambda x^{\lambda-1})-w(x+x^{\lambda}-v)(1+\lambda x^{\lambda-1}-v^{\prime})}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})c(1-\lambda)x^{-\lambda}}

\qquad\qquad=\log(x)\left[\displaystyle\frac{\log(x+x^{\lambda})}{\log(x)}-\displaystyle\frac{\log(x+x^{\lambda}-v)}{\log(x)}\displaystyle\frac{(x+x^{\lambda})^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}\right]\times

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \ \ \times\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda})^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\log(x+x^{\lambda})\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda})^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{x^{1-\lambda}}+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\log(x+x^{\lambda}-v)\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{x^{1-\lambda}}+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\log(x+x^{\lambda}-v)\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\alpha x^{\lambda-1}[\lambda\log(x)-1]}{\log(x)^{2}}

\qquad\qquad=\log(x)\left[\left(1+o\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{1-\lambda}}\right)\right)-\left(1+o\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{1-\lambda}}\right)\right)\left(1+o\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{x^{1-\lambda}}\right)\right)\times\right.

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times\left.\left(1-\displaystyle\frac{\alpha c(1-\lambda)}{\log(x)}+O\left(\displaystyle\frac{1}{\log(x)^{2}}\right)\right)\right]\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda})^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\log(x+x^{\lambda})\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda})^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda})^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{x^{1-\lambda}}+

\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\log(x+x^{\lambda}-v)\displaystyle\frac{\exp[c(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{1-\lambda}]}{\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}\displaystyle\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(x+x^{\lambda}-v)^{\lambda}}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{x^{1-\lambda}}+

\qquad\qquad\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\alpha c(1-\lambda)\exp[c(1-\lambda)].

Lemma 3.4
.

Given
0<b<2
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp(-bx)}{bx}>\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}+1}{x+1},

for all
0<x<\delta
.

Proof.

By the development of the Taylor series of
\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp(-bx)}{bx}
and
\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}+1}{x+1}
around the origin, the inequality stated (for
x>0
) is equivalent to

\left(2-\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}}{3!}\right)x-\left(2-\displaystyle\frac{b^{3}}{4!}\right)x^{2}+\cdots<1-\displaystyle\frac{b}{2},

whence follows the result. ∎

Lemma 3.5

\varepsilon>0

by Bernoulli’s inequality. The last statement follows from lemma
3.1
. ∎

Final thoughts

A more attentive reader must have realized that the proof discovered by Newman is, in fact, an algorithm for solving a problem that can be put in a very general way: it starts with a lenght function

\ell:[2,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)

for which we want to know, at least asymptotically, how many prime numbers there are in the interval

(x,x+\ell(x)].

The first step of the algorithm is to find a weight function

w:\mathbb{P}\rightarrow(0,\infty)

with none or one (or, who knows, more than one if necessary) parameter
c
such that
w=w_{c}
and it will give a weight
w(p)
to each prime
p
. This function
w
will generate a cumulative sum function

W(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}w(p),

on which, according to
[
15
]
, we will try to show that it satisfies the expected approximation

W(x)\sim\displaystyle\int_{2}^{x}\displaystyle\frac{w(t)}{\log(t)}dt,

because showing this relation could imply

\pi(x+\ell(x))-\pi(x)\sim\displaystyle\frac{\ell(x)}{\log(x)}.

The rest of the algorithm should be easy to understand, but not necessarily to execute (in particular, on the part of the analytical extension), for those who got here. As a first test to see if this algorithm is indeed reasonably effective, the enthusiastic reader is invited to think about the following three problems.

Problem 4.1
.

Fix
C>0
. What happens in the case
\ell(x)=Cx^{\lambda}
?

Problem 4.2
.

Can the ideas presented here be used to prove what would be the equivalent of the PNT for arithmetic progressions in short intervals?

Problem 4.3
.

In the light of
[
7
]
, is it possible to reproduce what was done here in an abstract way for arithmetical semigroups and arithmetical formations?

A natural continuation of this work could be done by investigating the validity of formula
1.2
for functions
\ell(x)
that grow slower than
x^{\lambda}
for all
0<\lambda<1
, such as

\exp[(\log(x))^{\mu}],

for some
0<\mu<1
, or even

\log(x)^{\log(\log(x))}.

It is worth mentioning that, in the light of Maier’s Theorem (see
[
10
]
), this algorithm must have some limitation.

Problem 4.4
.

What happens in the critical case
\ell(x)=\log(x)^{2}
?

Now, let’s make three comments about result
2.18
. Firstly, an old problem from Erdős asks (see
[
4
]
) if the sequence

\left(\displaystyle\frac{p_{n+1}-p_{n}}{\log(p_{n})}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

of normalized differences between consecutive primes is dense in the interval
(0,\infty)
. For the author, the answer to this question may be beyond the scope of formulas
1.1
and
1.2
. In fact, consider the sequence

x_{n}=n\log(n),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

It is a nice exercise to show that this sequence satisfies formula
1.1
for all
0<\lambda<1
and for all
c>0
(which implies that it satisfies formula
1.2
too), even though

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{x_{n+1}-x_{n}}{\log(x_{n})}=1.

Secondly, consider the following old and well-known conjecture of Legendre:

Conjecture 4.5

(Legendre’s conjecture)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then there always exists at least two prime numbers between
n^{2}
and
(n+1)^{2}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Legendre’s conjecture is true for all
n
sufficiently large.

Proof.

Since theorem
2.18
is true for
\lambda=1/2
, then

\pi(x+\sqrt{x})-\pi(x)\sim\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{x}}{\log(x)}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi(n+\sqrt{n})-\pi(n)=\infty,

which proves the thesis. ∎

Is Legendre’s conjecture true for every
n\geq 1
?

Thirdly, a beautiful problem that I’ve seen for the first time in
[
14
]
is the following conjecture:

Conjecture 4.7

(Sierpiński’s conjecture)
.

Let
n
be an integer greater than
1
. If you write the numbers
1,2,\ldots,n^{2}
in a matrix in the following way

\begin{matrix}1&2&\cdots&n\\
n+1&n+2&\cdots&2n\\
2n+1&2n+2&\cdots&3n\\
\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots\\
(n-1)n+1&(n-1)n+2&\cdots&n^{2}\end{matrix}

then each row contains, at least, a prime number.

Proposition 4.8
.

Sierpiński’s conjecture is true for all
n
sufficiently large.

Obviously, the proof is the same as the previous one. Is Sierpiński’s conjecture true for every
n\geq 2
?

Andrica’s conjecture, Brocard’s conjecture (that there are at least four prime numbers between the squares of two consecutives primes), and Oppermann’s conjecture are also all consequences of theorem
2.18
, for all
sufficiently large
integer
n
. Are all these three conjectures true for every
n\geq 2
?

Finally, allow me to use the remaining space to briefly tell where the idea for solving this problem came from.

I believe it all started when I first saw Erdős’ proof of Bertrand-Chebyshev’s theorem
[
3
]
. His proof is a masterpiece of mathematics, and exemplifies very clearly the idea of using a weight function to testify the existence of prime numbers in a given interval of the real line (as long as it is large enough). That’s when I started looking for a suitable weight function that would effectively testify the existence of at least one prime number in the interval

I_{\lambda,x}=(x,x+x^{\lambda}].

Initially, I tried to reproduce Erdős’ idea for the binomial coefficient

\binom{\lfloor x+x^{\lambda}\rfloor}{\lfloor x\rfloor},

but then I realized that the weight function associated with this case, i.e., the product of the eventual prime numbers that lie in the interval
I_{\lambda,x}
, does not grow fast enough to guarantee the existence of at least one prime number in the interval
I_{\lambda,x}
.

So I turned my attention to Newman’s strategy to prove PNT and then it came to my mind the idea of trying a weight function with polynomial growth, i.e., studying a function of the type

W(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}p^{\omega},

for some
\omega>0
, and it was at that moment that I found out the article
[
16
]
. But even this function was not able to detect even a prime number in the interval
I_{\lambda,x}
; that’s a consequence of the following theorem.

Theorem 4.9

(Šalát-Znám)
.

If
\omega>0
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}p^{\omega}\sim\displaystyle\frac{x^{1+\omega}}{(1+\omega)\log(x)}.

Proof.

See
[
16
]
. ∎

For me, then, the natural step to follow was to try a weight function with exponential growth, i.e., to study the function

W(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\exp(p).

But where does this function depend on
\lambda
? Furthermore, does it not grow too fast? So I asked myself: which function grows faster than any polynomial, and slower than the exponential? A natural answer was, for me, the weight function

W(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\exp(p^{1-\lambda}).

With this function it is already possible to prove (following the same procedure as in Step 1) that

\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp(\lambda-1)}{1-\lambda}\leq\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{A}(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi_{A}(x)}{x^{\lambda}/\log(x)}\leq\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{A}(x+x^{\lambda})-\pi_{A}(x)}{x^{\lambda}/\log(x)}\leq\displaystyle\frac{\exp(1-\lambda)-1}{1-\lambda},

which indicates, in a certain way, the need to insert the parameter
c
in the function
w
. This brings us to the function

W(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\exp(cp^{1-\lambda}).

This function is already good enough to make all the steps described in section
2
work (including steps
8
and
9
of Newman’s proof), except for step
10
, in which its respective function
\Psi
starts to be written using other functions, which complicates things a lot. The solution to this technicality was then to try to find a function
w
whose associated cumulative function was asymptotic to
\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})
; that’s the case of

W(x)=\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq x}\displaystyle\frac{c(1-\lambda)\log(x)\exp(cx^{1-\lambda})}{x^{\lambda}}.

I was amazed at how well this function behaves in relation to the necessary adaptations proposed here (in particular, by step 3, whose change of variables is explicit), but then I got stuck looking for how to realize step 8. After many unsuccessful attempts, I thought about expanding
\Psi(s)
around
\lambda=1
, as follows: first, write

\Psi(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{\lambda}\exp\left[s\left(\displaystyle\frac{p^{1-\lambda}-1}{1-\lambda}\right)\right]}=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{1+s}}\cdot\displaystyle\frac{p^{s+1-\lambda}}{\exp\left[s\left(\displaystyle\frac{p^{1-\lambda}-1}{1-\lambda}\right)\right]}

Now, expanding the rightmost term around
\lambda=1
for each prime
p
, one gets

(4.1)

\Psi(s)=\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{p^{1+s}}\cdot\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(1-\lambda)^{j}\log(p)^{j}f_{j}(s\log(p))}{j!},

in which these
\{f_{j}(x)\}_{j\geq 0}
are a sequence of polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
defined recursively by

f_{0}(x)=1

and

f_{j+1}(x)=f_{j}(x)-x\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{j}\displaystyle\binom{j}{k}\displaystyle\frac{f_{j-k}(x)}{k+2},

for all
j\geq 0
, additionally having the marvelous property that the function

s\mapsto\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)^{j+1}f_{j}(s\log(p))}{p^{s+1}},

initially defined for
\Re(s)>0
, extends analytically to
\Re(s)\geq 0
, for all
j\geq 1
. This makes us very willing to change the order of the sums in equation
4.1
. Indeed, if we could do that, we would have

\begin{split}\Psi(s)&=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(\lambda-1)^{j}}{j!}\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)^{j+1}f_{j}(s\log(p))}{p^{1+s}}\\
&=\Phi(1+s)+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(\lambda-1)^{j}}{j!}\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)^{j+1}f_{j}(s\log(p))}{p^{1+s}},\end{split}

in such a way that by subtracting
\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}
from each side of this equation, we would have

\Psi(s)-\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}=\Omega(s)+\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\displaystyle\frac{(\lambda-1)^{j}}{j!}\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)^{j+1}f_{j}(s\log(p))}{p^{1+s}},

thereby proving theorem
2.18
. But I was unable to show that we could exchange the order of the sums.

The rest of the story is in the manuscript in hand. We close the presentation of this article by leaving the following question to the reader.

Problem 4.10
.

How does the function
\tau(s)
behave in a neighborhood of
\Re(s)=0
in the limit case
\lambda=0
, i.e., how does the function

s\mapsto\displaystyle\sum_{p}\displaystyle\frac{\log(p)}{\exp(sp)}

behave near
\Re(s)=0
? Does this have any meaning in relation to the distribution of prime numbers?
