Orbifold splice quotients and log covers of surface pairs Thanks: Neumann’s research supported under NSF grant no. DMS-1608600
Walter D. Neumann, Jonathan Wahl

Abstract
. A three-dimensional orbifold ( Σ , γ i , n i ) (\Sigma,\gamma_{i},n_{i}) , where Σ \Sigma is a rational homology sphere, has a universal abelian orbifold covering, whose covering group is the first orbifold homology. A singular pair ( X , C ) (X,C) , where X X is a normal surface singularity with ℚ \mathbb{Q} HS link and C C is a Weil divisor, gives rise on its boundary to an orbifold. One studies the preceding orbifold notions in the algebro-geometric setting, proving the existence of the universal abelian log cover of a pair. A key theorem computes the orbifold homology from an appropriate resolution of the pair. In analogy with the case where C C is empty and one considers the universal abelian cover, under certain conditions on a resolution graph one can construct pairs and their universal abelian log covers. Such pairs are called orbifold splice quotients.

(Orbifold End-Curve Theorem) A pair

However, in

If

Let
(X,C)

The paper is organized as follows: In Section 1 we recall the basics of orbifolds in dimension three, the universal orbifold covering and fundamental group, and orbifold homology. Section 2 introduces singular pairs and their log covers, with focus on cyclic covers. From now on, one assumes the link is a
\mathbb{Q}
HS. Section 3 gives an algebro-geometric construction of the universal abelian log cover of a pair. The orbifold homology is computed in Section 4 from a plumbing diagram; the crucial representation of the orbifold discriminant group occurs in Section 5. The definition and construction of orbifold splice quotients appears in Section 6, with an example presented in Section 7.

1.
Orbifolds

For the special purposes of this paper, an
orbifold
will be a compact
oriented
3
–manifold
\Sigma
, plus a disjoint set of embedded circles (or “knots”)
\gamma_{1},\cdots,\gamma_{r}\subset\Sigma
, to which are associated positive integers
n_{1},\cdots,n_{r}
(the
orbifold weights
). While this is not the standard definition of orbifold (e.g., [5]), it will be useful in considering branched covers with restricted ramification. See Example
1.2
below.

Definition 1.1
.

A (finite)
orbifold covering
consists of another orbifold

(\Sigma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}_{j},n^{\prime}_{j})
and a finite map
f:\Sigma^{\prime}\rightarrow\Sigma
, with the following properties:

(1)

the inverse image of a knot
\gamma_{i}
consists of various

\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
, and
n^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
dividing
n_{i}

(2)

f^{-1}(\cup\gamma_{i})=\cup\gamma^{\prime}_{j}
, and
f
is a covering map
off this set

(3)

locally around
\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
,
f
maps to a neighborhood of

\gamma_{i}
as an orbifold quotient of order
n_{i}/n^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
.

The last statement means that if the pairs
(\Sigma,\gamma_{i})
and

(\Sigma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{j}})
are given locally as
(\mathbb{R}^{3},z-\text{axis})
, then the map
f
is given by dividing out by the group of
rotations of order
n_{i}/n^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
around the
z
-axis. Since
f
is a
covering map of some degree
d
off the
\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
, it follows
that for each
i
,

\sum_{j}n_{i}/n^{\prime}_{i_{j}}=d.

More precisely, the orbifolds we are considering could be described as
“locally cyclic orientable
3
-orbifolds” since their local
structure is always
D^{3}/(\mathbb{Z}/(n))
for some
n
(
D^{3}
a three-disk). We
restrict to cyclic groups since all our
3
–manifolds are
boundaries of
4
-manifolds with complex structure.

Example 1.2
.

Let
M^{\prime}
be a compact oriented 3-manifold on which a finite group
G

acts smoothly and faithfully, preserving orientation, and with
the property that the isotropy group
G_{x}
at any point
x
is
cyclic. We make
M^{\prime}\rightarrow M\equiv M^{\prime}/G
an orbifold cover. The knots
\gamma_{i}\subset M
are the images of the components of the set of
points on
M^{\prime}
with non-trivial isotropy, and the weight
n_{i}
is the order of
this isotropy group. The
\gamma^{\prime}_{i_{j}}
are the inverse images of the
\gamma_{i}
, and the
n^{\prime}_{i_{j}}=1
.

From now on we will often use the notation
\Sigma
both for the orbifold
and its underlying space when what is meant is clear from context. Most of the definitions here and below are due to Thurston (e.g., Chapter 13 of his notes
[
14
]
).

An orbifold has an
orbifold fundamental group

\pi_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma)
, obtained by quotienting

\pi_{1}(\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i})
by the relations

\mu_{i}^{n_{i}}=1
, where
\mu_{i}
is represented by the boundary of a
small transverse disc to
\gamma_{i}
. This can also be described as

\pi_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma)=\pi_{1}(\Sigma^{\prime})
, where
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the result of
replacing a
D^{2}\times S^{1}
neighborhood of each
\gamma_{i}
by

K(\mathbb{Z}/(n_{i}),1)\times S^{1}
, glued so that
\mu_{i}
represents a
generator of
\pi_{1}(K(\mathbb{Z}/(n_{i}),1))
. (Here
K(\mathbb{Z}/(n_{i}),1)
is an
Eilenberg-Maclane complex—a CW-complex with fundamental group

\mathbb{Z}/(n_{i})
and contractible universal cover.)

The Galois correspondence between covering spaces and subgroups of the
fundamental group extends to orbifolds, using orbifold covers and
orbifold fundamental group (see e.g.,
[
6
]
). In particular, any
orbifold has a
universal abelian orbifold cover
, or

UAOC
, classified by the commutator subgroup of

\pi_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma)
. Its covering transformation group is the
abelianization
\pi_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma)^{ab}
of
\pi_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma)
;
this is also
H_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z}):=H_{1}(\Sigma^{\prime};\mathbb{Z})
, the degree
1

orbifold homology of
\Sigma
(see
[
6
]
,
[
12
]
, and
[
13
]
for more on orbifold
homology).

Not every orbifold is the quotient of a manifold by a finite group, as in Example
2.1
; an
example is
(S^{2}\times S^{1},\{p\}\times S^{1},n)
for
n>1
. However, if

\Sigma
is a
\mathbb{Q}
HS we prove:

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
(\Sigma,\gamma_{i},n_{i})
be an orbifold for which the
underlying space
\Sigma
is a
\mathbb{Q}
HS. Then the
UAOC

\widetilde{\Sigma}^{ab}
of

is a finite cover of
\Sigma
,
and its covering group
G=H_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})
sits in an exact

0\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/(n_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(n_{r})\rightarrow G\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow 0\,.

The Proposition follows from two Lemmas.

Lemma 1.4
.

If
\Sigma
is a
\mathbb{Q}
HS, there is an exact sequence

0\rightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i})\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma)\rightarrow 0,

where the first map is dotting with
(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r})
.

Proof.

The long-exact sequence of the pair
(\Sigma,\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i})
gives

0=H_{2}(\Sigma)\rightarrow H_{2}(\Sigma,\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i})\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i})\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma)\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma,\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i})=0.

The first term is
0
since
\Sigma
is
\mathbb{Q}
HS. Let

be the union of small closed tubular neighborhoods of the
\gamma_{i}
, each one a closed 2-disk bundle over an

and

denote their interiors. Then the inclusion

induces an isomorphism

H_{j}(\Sigma,\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}))\cong H_{j}(\Sigma,\Sigma-T^{o})
. By excision (removing

), one has

H_{j}(\Sigma,\Sigma-T^{o})\cong H_{j}(T,T-T^{o}))=H_{j}(T,\partial T)=\oplus_{i}H_{j}(T_{i},\partial T_{i}).

But
H_{2}(T_{i},\partial T_{i})=\ker H_{1}(\partial T_{i})\rightarrow H_{1}(T_{i})
is the free

-module generated by

, while
H_{1}(T_{i},\partial T_{i})=0
, whence the result.
∎

Lemma 1.5
.

H_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma)\cong H_{1}(\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}))/(n_{1}\mu_{1},\cdots,n_{r}\mu_{r}).

Proof.

If
G
is a group,
N
a normal subgroup, then abelianizing

is the same as abelianizing
G
and then modding out by the image of

in
G_{ab}
; either method gives

, where

denotes commutator subgroup. For, every element of the commutator subgroup is a product of commutators, so the map
G^{\prime}\rightarrow(G/N)^{\prime}
is surjective. Apply now to

and

. Then
G/N=\pi_{1}^{orb}(\Sigma),
and abelianizing gives

;
G_{ab}=H_{1}(\Sigma-\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}),
and the image of

is
(n_{1}\mu_{1},\cdots,n_{r}\mu_{r})
.
∎

Remark 1.6
.

The Proposition shows one could construct the UAOC by first forming
the UAC
\Sigma^{\prime}\rightarrow\Sigma
, taking the inverse images of the
\gamma_{i}

2.
Covers of singular pairs

The singularity version concerns
singular pairs

(X,C)
. Here,

(X,0)
is the germ of a complex normal surface singularity (thus
homeomorphic to the cone over its link
\Sigma
), and
C=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}C_{i}
is
a positive combination of reduced and irreducible Weil divisors. Intersecting with the boundary of a small ball neighborhood of the
singular point in a smooth ambient space gives an orbifold link in a
natural way.

The data of a singular pair
(X,C)
is more frequently encoded in the literature as a
log pair

(X,\Delta_{X})
, with the
boundary of
X
the effective
\mathbb{Q}
-divisor

\Delta_{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1/n_{i})C_{i}.

Log pairs are essential notions in algebraic geometry (e.g.
[
2
]
), and
1/n_{i}
could be replaced by any real number between
0
and
1
. However, for this paper the singular pair
(X,\sum n_{i}C_{i})
is a more convenient way to display branching data for branched covers.

We denote the
support of
C
by
|C|=\cup_{i=1}^{r}C_{i}
.
A
map of pairs

f:(X^{\prime},C^{\prime})\rightarrow(X,C)
is a finite map of normal germs
f:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
so that
f(|C^{\prime}|)=|C|.

Definition 2.1
.

A
log cover of pairs

f:(X^{\prime},C^{\prime})\rightarrow(X,C)

is a map of pairs with the
following additional properties:

(1)

f^{-1}(|C|)=|C^{\prime}|
, and
f
is an unramified covering
space off this set.

(2)

For
C=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}C_{i}
and
f^{*}C_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}m_{ij}C^{\prime}_{ij}
(as Weil divisors), one has
m_{ij}|n_{i}
, all
j
.

(3)

C^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}n_{i}/m_{ij}C^{\prime}_{ij}
.

In other words, for a log cover of pairs, the map
f:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
is ramified only over the
C_{i}
, and the ramification index
m_{ij}
on any curve above it divides
n_{i}
. (In particular, if
n_{i}=1
, no ramification is allowed above
C_{i}
.)

Remark 2.2
.

Note that if a map of pairs satisfies
(1)
, then the conditions
(2)
and
(3)
above are together equivalent to a relation on the boundary divisors, namely

f^{*}(\Delta_{X})=\Delta_{X^{\prime}}.

We note the easy

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
be a finite map of germs of normal surface singularities. If
f
is ramified off the singular points, then there are unique minimal divisors
C^{\prime}
on
X^{\prime}
and
C
on
X
so that
f:(X^{\prime},C^{\prime})\rightarrow(X,C)
is a log cover of pairs.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},\cdots,C_{r}
be the irreducible components of the branch curve of
f
on
X
. Write
f^{*}C_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}m_{ij}C^{\prime}_{ij}
as before. Define
n_{i}=lcm(m_{ij},j=1,\cdots,s_{i})
. Then set
C=\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}C_{i}
and
C^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{s_{i}}n_{i}/m_{ij}C^{\prime}_{ij}
. The result now follows.
∎

A log cover of pairs
(X^{\prime},C^{\prime})\rightarrow(X,C)
is said to be
abelian
(resp.,
cyclic
) if an abelian (resp., cyclic) group
G
acts on
X^{\prime}
with quotient
X
, permuting the curves lying above each

3.

4.

As always,
(\tilde{X},E)\rightarrow(X,0)
will denote a good resolution of a singularity with

and its dual

defined by

Proposition 4.1
.

is isomorphic to the
discriminant group

, with generators

\sum_{j=1}^{m}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}=0,\ \ i=1,2,\cdots,m.

This result follows from examining the
exact sequence

H_{2}(\Sigma)=0\rightarrow H_{2}(\tilde{X})\rightarrow H_{2}(\tilde{X},\Sigma)\rightarrow H_{1}(\Sigma)\rightarrow H_{1}(\tilde{X})=0.

Note
H_{2}(\tilde{X})=\oplus\mathbb{Z}[E_{i}]\cong\mathbb{E}

which is transversal to

in

The
\mathbb{Q}

is the inverse of the matrix
(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})
. From (
[
8
]

Proposition 4.2
.

The linking number satisfies

\ell(K_{i},K_{j})=-e_{i}\cdot e_{j}.

A surface pair

gives rise to an orbifold
(\Sigma,\gamma_{i},n_{i})
on the boundary, and we wlll prove one can compute the orbifold homology

from a

of the pair. That is, we consider a good resolution

intersects one exceptional curve

over a point of

More generally, assume one is given a good resolution with exceptional curves

For the orbifold

\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{i}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}=0,\ \ i=1,2,\cdots,m.

We use linking numbers to relate the classes of the

5.
Action of the orbifold discriminant group

With
(\tilde{X},E)
as before, suppose

are the ends (or

) of the graph
\Gamma
. Recall the diagonal representation of the discriminant group
D(\Gamma)
:

Proposition 5.1
.

(
[
7
]
, (5.2), (5.3)). There is a natural injection
D(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{t}
, given by
e\mapsto(e\cdot e_{1},\cdots,e\cdot e_{t})
. Exponentiating

via

Given a pair

, there is a log resolution so that each

intersects an end

of the graph (cf. (6.4) below). For this orbifold situation, to those ends

we are given knots

of type

plus a weight

; assign a weight
n_{j}=1
to any other end. We represent the extra data by a decorated graph

\Gamma
an arrow and the associated weight.

Definition 5.2
.

The orbifold discriminant group
D(\Gamma^{*})
is the group generated by
e_{1},\cdots,e_{m}
modulo the relations

\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{i}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}=0,\ \ i=1,2,\cdots,t.

\sum_{j=1}^{m}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}=0,\ \ i=t+1,\cdots,m.

According to Theorem 4.3,

is isomorphic to the orbifold homology group (all weights of interior

equal

).
Modding out the

’s by the further relations with all

(as in Proposition 4.1) gives the surjection

, with kernel the sum of cyclic groups of orders
n_{1}
through
n_{t}
. This is the same as the general result in Proposition 1.1.

D(\Gamma^{*})
has a natural diagonal representation compatible with that for

, using the
“power map”

given on points by

(a_{1},\cdots,a_{t})\mapsto(a_{1}^{n_{1}},\cdots,a_{t}^{n_{t}}).

Proposition 5.3
.

There is a natural injection
D(\Gamma^{*})\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{t}
and a commutative diagram

D(\Gamma^{*})\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{t}

\ \ \downarrow\Phi\ \\
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \downarrow N

D(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{t}

Proof.

We claim there is a map
D(\Gamma^{*})\rightarrow(\mathbb{Q}/Z)^{t}
given by

e\mapsto((e\cdot e_{1})/n_{1},(e\cdot e_{2})/n_{2},\cdots,(e\cdot e_{t})/n_{t}).

One needs to check that the relations defining

go to
0
in every entry of
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{t}
. But in the

th entry,

\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{i}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{m}n_{i}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}\cdot e_{k}/n_{k}=n_{i}/n_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(E_{i}\cdot E_{j})e_{j}\cdot e_{k}=n_{i}/n_{k}\delta_{ik},

which equals
0
or
1
, in either case
0
in
\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
. After exponentiation, one gets a map to

. This gives the commutative diagram asserted.

There remains to check injectivity in the top row, or equivalently injectivity of
\ker\Phi\rightarrow\ker N
. As these groups have the same order

, we show surjectivity. But by definition, the map above sends

to
(1/n_{1},0\cdots,0)
in

, hence to one of the obvious generators of
\ker N
.∎

Proof.

An interior vertex

∎

6.
orbifold splice quotients

We briefly outline the basics of splice quotient singularities; see Section
1
of
[
8
]
for a few more details, and
[
7
]
for the full story.

We retain the same notation as before for a good resolution of a singularity, including the graph
\Gamma
and the diagonal representation
D(\Gamma)\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{t}
.
\Gamma
has leaves (vertices of valence 1) and nodes (vertices of valence
\geq 3
). Associated to
\Gamma
is a
splice diagram

\Delta
, obtained by collapsing all vertices of valence two, and assigning a positive integer weight to every node and emanating edge by taking an appropriate determinant. From this data, for each node one can assign a weight to every leaf of
\Delta
; the
semigroup condition
on
\Delta
then requires that each weight on an emanating edge of that node is in the semigroup generated by the weights of the “outer” leaves. Choosing a coordinate
x_{i}
for each of the
t
leaves, one can then write a total of
t-2

splice diagram equations
in the
x_{i}
’s, by writing for each node a sum of monomials (one for each emanating edge) which have the same weight as the product of weights around the node (one uses the coefficients of the semigroup relations). The
congruence condition
allows one to insure that each equation so obtained transforms by a character under the action of the representation of
D(\Gamma)
on
\mathbb{C}^{t}
. One thus produces from
\Gamma
isolated complete intersection singularities in
\mathbb{C}^{t}
on which
D(\Gamma)
acts, freely off the origin. There are choices involved, and higher order terms can be added.

\Gamma
is called
quasi-minimal
if any string in the graph contains no
-1
vertex, or consists of a unique
-1
vertex. (A
string
is a connected subgraph containing no nodes.) The Main Theorem
7.2
of
[
7
]
starts with a graph
\Gamma
:

Theorem 6.1
.

[
7
]
Suppose
\Gamma
is quasi-minimal and satisfies the semigroup and congruence conditions. Then:

(1)

A set of splice diagram equations
\{f_{j}(x_{i})=0\}
for
\Gamma
defines an isolated complete intersection singularity
(X^{\prime},0)\subset(\mathbb{C}^{t},0)
.

(2)

The discriminant group
D(\Gamma)
acts freely on
X^{\prime}-\{0\}
.

(3)

The quotient
(X,0)\equiv(X^{\prime},0)/D(\Gamma)
has an isolated normal surface singularity, and a good resolution
(\tilde{X},E)\rightarrow(X,0)
whose associated dual graph is
\Gamma
.

(4)

f:(X^{\prime},0)\rightarrow(X,0)
is the universal abelian cover, in particular unramified off the origins.

(5)

Each curve
C_{i}^{\prime}=\{x_{i}=0\}\cap X^{\prime}
is mapped by
(X^{\prime},0)\rightarrow(X,0)
to an irreducible curve
C_{i}
, whose proper transform
\tilde{C_{i}}
on
\tilde{X}
is smooth and intersects the exceptional curve transversally, along an end
E_{i}
.

Such
(X,0)
are called
splice quotient singularities
.

In more detail, the curve
C_{i}^{\prime}
is reduced, with
|D(\Gamma)|/h
irreducible components, where
h
is the order of
e_{i}
in
D(\Gamma)
(follows from
[
7
]
, Section 3).
x_{i}^{h}
is
D(\Gamma)
invariant, hence a function on
(X,0)
; its zero-set is a Cartier divisor, which is
h
times an irreducible Weil divisor
C_{i}
.
x_{i}^{h}
is an
end-curve function
for
(X,0)
; on the resolution
(\tilde{X},E)
it vanishes only on
E
and an
end-curve
\tilde{C_{i}}
, which intersects
E_{i}
transversally at one point. Thus the above construction gives not only a splice-quotient singularity
(X,0)
, but also a collection of end-curve functions and their corresponding irreducible curves
C_{i}
. Note finally that
f^{*}(C_{i})
is the reduced sum of the irreducible components of

.

Whether a given
(X,0)
is a splice quotient singularity is given by the principal result of
[
8
]
, the
End-Curve Theorem
:

Theorem 6.2
.

[
8
]
Let
(X,0)
be a normal surface singularity with
\mathbb{Q}
HS link,
(\tilde{X},E)\rightarrow(X,0)
a good resolution. Suppose for every end
E_{i}
of the exceptional curve

there is a function on
(X,0)
whose zero set on
\tilde{X}
is an end-curve for

. Then
(X,0)
is a splice quotient singularity.

An immediate Corollary is the result of T. Okuma:

Theorem 6.3
.

[
10
]
Let
(\tilde{X},E)\rightarrow(X,0)
be a quasi-minimal resolution of a rational surface singularity. Then the graph
\Gamma
satisfies the semigroup and congruence conditions. Moreover, every end-curve
\tilde{C_{i}}
is cut out by an end-curve function on
X
.

Proof.

For any end-curve
\tilde{C_{i}}
on
\tilde{X}
, the image
C_{i}
on
X
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier divisor (since
X
is rational), so some multiple of it is the zero-set of a function on
X
.
∎

To analyze a pair
(X,C)
, one needs a well-adapted log resolution.

Proposition 6.4
.

A pair
(X,C=\sum n_{i}C_{i})
has a

minimal orbifold resolution
, a smallest log resolution
\pi:(\tilde{X_{C}},E)\rightarrow(X,0)
satisfying

(1)

each
C_{i}
has proper transform
\tilde{C_{i}}
which is smooth, intersecting
E
transversally

(2)

\tilde{C_{i}}
intersects a leaf
E_{i}
of
E

(3)

each leaf
E_{j}
of

intersects at most one
\tilde{C_{i}}

(4)

\tilde{X_{C}}
is quasi-minimal.

\tilde{X_{C}}
is unique except when a log resolution consists of a single curve plus two
\tilde{C}_{i}
intersecting it transversally at two points.

Proof.

Starting with the minimal good resolution of
X
, resolve in a minimal way the singularities of the reduced inverse image of each
C_{i}
until one has strong normal crossings (the minimal log-resolution). If the proper transform of some
C_{i}
does not intersect a leaf, blow-up that intersection point. If three or more proper transforms intersect the same leaf, blow-up each of the intersection points. If two intersect the same leaf, which has another connection with the graph, again blow-up those points. The only missing case is when two transforms intersect a graph consisting of a single curve; in that case, one could blow-up either of the intersection points. Otherwise, one has uniqueness of the resolution.

We claim the corresponding resolution is quasi-minimal. If a
-1
curve intersects some
\tilde{C_{i}}
, then it is an end-curve; if its neighboring curve had valency
2
, the
-1
curve could be blown down, contradicting the minimality of the blow-up process. If a
-1
curve intersects no

and it has a neighbor curve with valency

curve, violating the minimality process.
∎

We exclude the simple cases for which one has non-uniqueness of the resolution.

Thus, a pair gives rise to a quasi-minimal graph

which has arrows and weights at special edges. From this data, we can assign weight one to non-special edges, and encode all the weights in a power map

We give conditions on graphs

Let
(X,0)

(y,w)
, and
C_{2}
is a curve, equations to be determined, which on the minimal resolution is smooth and intersects tangentially the central curve, to order
2

X
to an orbifold resolution, one needs to blow up three times so that
\tilde{C_{2}}
intersects a unique leaf. The graph
\Gamma_{C}^{*}

The representation on

of
D(\Gamma)
is the four-group, acting only on the

coordinates, by multiplying two of the variables by
-1

2

4

x_{4}-x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}=0

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}=0

x_{5}-x_{4}^{2}-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2})=0.

x_{4}

x_{4}=y+z;\ x+y-z=0;\ x_{5}=x_{4}^{2}+w(x+y+z).

xyz=w^{2}

w^{2}=xy(x+y)

C_{2}
