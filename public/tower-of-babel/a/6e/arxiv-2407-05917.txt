Unveiling nonmagnetic phase and many-body entanglement in two-dimensional random quantum magnets Sr 2 CuTe 1-x W x O 6
Dian Wu, Fan Yang, Giuseppe Carleo

Abstract
We apply a random-plaquette J 1 J_{1} - J 2 J_{2} model on the square lattice to capture the physics of a series of spin- 1 / 2 1/2 Heisenberg antiferromagnet compounds Sr 2 CuTe 1-x W x O 6 . With the input of experimentally relevant coupling strengths, our exact diagonalization (ED) study probes the ground state properties beyond previous linear spin-wave approach. An intermediate range of x ∈ [ 0.08 , 0.55 ] x\in[0.08,0.55] is identified for a nonmagnetic phase without the long-range Néel or stripe order. The absence of both valence-bond-glass order and spin-glass non-ergodic dynamics renders its nature intriguing. Deep inside this phase around x = 0.3 x=0.3 , we observe signatures potentially linked to randomness-induced short-range spin-liquid-like (SLL) states, including close to zero spin-freezing parameter, vanishing spin-spin correlation beyond nearest neighbors, almost uniform static spin structure factor, as well as a broad tail in the dynamical spin structure factor. The nonmagnetic phase also features multipartite entanglement in the ground state witnessed by quantum Fisher information (QFI), which exhibits universal scaling behaviors at quantum critical points.

II
Properties of the model

Before proceeding to study physical observables, we first briefly introduce our reweighting method to compute the observables, which enables us to obtain the disorder-averaged ground-state expectation values with an arbitrary mixing ratio
x\in[0,1]
. For each system size
N=8,12,16,20,24,28,32
, we construct a lattice whose geometry allows both Néel and stripe orders under periodic boundary conditions (PBC). Then for each
k=0,1,\ldots,N
, we randomly generate
M_{k}
Hamiltonian instances, each with exactly
k

J_{2}
-dominated plaquettes. As
x
is the probability for a plaquette to be
J_{2}
-dominated in the disorder average, any physical observable
O
is averaged under a binomial distribution, denoted by
[\,\cdots]_{J}
:

\displaystyle\left[\!\expectationvalue*{O}\right]_{J}(N,x)=\sum_{k}B(k,N,x)\bar{O}_{k}(N),

(2)

\displaystyle\bar{O}_{k}(N)=\frac{1}{M_{k}}\sum_{i}\expectationvalue*{O}_{i},

(3)

where the binomial distribution takes the form
B(k,N,x)=\binom{N}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{N-k}
, and the binomial coefficient reads
\binom{N}{k}=N!/(k!\,(N-k)!)
. In
\bar{O}_{k}(N)
, the expectation
\expectationvalue*{O}_{i}
acts on the ground state of the random instance
i
. Without reweighting,
\bar{O}_{k}(N)
alone is an estimator of the uniform average over all random instances with a fixed
k
, under which circumstance
x
can only take discrete values
k/N
with a finite system size
N
. Our reweighting method makes it possible to vary
x
continuously and more precisely determine the phase boundaries, as shown in the right column of
Fig.

2
. More details on the ED computation, including choices of lattice geometries, reweighting, and estimation of uncertainties in the average and the extrapolation, can be found in
Appendix

A
.

We comment on the symmetries of the ground state. It inherits the SU
(2)
symmetry from Heisenberg exchanges, leading to zero magnetization
\expectationvalue*{S^z_j}=0
for each site
j
. The ground state also respects spin inversion symmetry and selects the even parity sector for all lattices we use, regardless of the value of
x
and the configuration of random plaquettes. Translational symmetry and
D_{4}
lattice symmetry, by contrast, are only present at
x=0
and
1
.

\displaystyle m_{\text{N}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{N(N+4)}\left[\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i,j\in\alpha}\!\expectationvalue*{\bm{S}_i \cdot\bm{S}_j}\right]_{J},

(4)

\displaystyle m_{\text{str}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{N(N+4)}\left[\sum_{\nu}\,\sum_{\alpha_{\nu}}\sum_{i,j\in\alpha_{\nu}}\!\!\expectationvalue*{\bm{S}_i \cdot\bm{S}_j}\right]_{J}.

In

III
Quantum Fisher information in 2D random quantum magnets

In this section, we introduce the general relation between the QFI and the spin structure factors, and demonstrate how to use the QFI density as a witness of multipartite entanglement in the random-plaquette
J_{1}
-
J_{2}
model.

III.1
Multipartite entanglement witness

Let us start from a generic equilibrium mixed state at the temperature
T
. Its density matrix has the form
\rho=\sum_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}\ket{\lambda}\bra{\lambda}
with the probability
p_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-E_{\lambda}/T}
and the partition function
Z=\sum_{\lambda}e^{-E_{\lambda}/T}
, where we set
\hbar=k_{B}=1
. Given a generator
O=\sum_{i=1}^{N}O_{i}
consisting of local operators
O_{i}
that are normalized with unit spectrum width, the QFI is defined by

f_{\text{Q}}(T)=2\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{(p_{\lambda}-p_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{2}}{p_{\lambda}+p_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\left|\!\matrixelement{\lambda}{O}{\lambda'}\right|^{2},

(16)

with
p_{\lambda}+p_{\lambda^{\prime}}>0
.
Physically, through the quantum Cramér–Rao bound
[
70
]
,
f_{\text{Q}}
quantifies the maximal precision of a parameter
\theta
one can reach after

M
independent measurements:
\Var\theta\geq 1/(Mf_{\text{Q}})
.
It is a lower bound for the variance of parameter
\theta
associated with the generator
O
. Under each measurement, the system undergoes a unitary transformation:
\rho^{\prime}=e^{-i\theta O}\rho e^{i\theta O}
. The QFI has been successfully measured in a single spin system, or a solid-state qubit
[
71
]
.

Going to multispin systems of higher complexity, an exact relation can be established
[
32
,
36
,
37
]
between the QFI density
f_{\text{Q}}=f_{\text{Q}}/N
and the dynamical spin structure factor
S_{\text{dyn}}
:

f_{\text{Q}}(\bm{k},T)=4\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\tanh(\frac{\omega}{2 T})\left(1-e^{-\frac{\omega}{T}}\right)S_{\text{dyn}}(\bm{k},\omega,T)\,\differential\omega,

(17)

with the generator chosen to be
O(\bm{k})=\sqrt{N}S^{z\dagger}_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i}^{z}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}
. Here,
S_{\text{dyn}}
is obtained from
Eq.

14
by applying
\expectationvalue*{S^{z \dagger}_{\bm{k}}(t) S^z_{\bm{k}}(0)}=\Tr[\rho\,S^{z\dagger}_{\bm{k}}(t)S^{z}_{\bm{k}}(0)]
, which acts on the thermal state when
T>0
and on the ground state at
T=0
.
Equation

17
expresses the entanglement in an explicit form of quantum fluctuations. A detailed derivation is provided in
Appendix

B
.

Notably, a quantum state is
(m+1)
-partite entangled when the QFI density satisfies the inequality
[
32
,
37
,
72
,
73
]
:
f_{\text{Q}}>m(h_{\text{max}}-h_{\text{min}})^{2}
, where
m
is an integer and
h_{\text{max}}
(
h_{\text{min}}
) is the largest (smallest) eigenvalue of the local generator
O_{i}
. In our case,
\expectationvalue*{O^\dagger_i O_i}=\expectationvalue*{(S_i^z)^2}=1/4
and
h_{\text{max}}=-h_{\text{min}}=1/2
. Therefore,
(m+1)
-partite entanglement is witnessed in a multispin system if

f_{\text{Q}}>m.

(18)

At the same time,
Eq.

18
sets the criterion according to which the quantum Cramér–Rao bound falls below the classical limits
[
74
]
.

Next, we focus on the features of the QFI in the ground state of the random-plaquette model. By taking the limit

in

, we can verify that

f_{\text{Q}}(\bm{k})=4\int_{0}^{\infty}S_{\text{dyn}}(\bm{k},\omega)\,\differential\omega=\frac{4}{3}S_{\text{sta}}(\bm{k}).

(19)

The extra factor

comes from one of the three

-symmetric components
\bm{S}_{i}\cdot\bm{S}_{j}=\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}S_{i}^{\alpha}S_{j}^{\alpha}

Equivalently, we can also evaluate the QFI of a pure ground state
\ket{\psi}

contributes twice to

in

It is noted that

V
Discussion

To summarize, in this work we perform extensive ED studies of the random-plaquette
J_{1}
-
J_{2}
model with experimentally relevant parameters for the material Sr
2
CuTe
1-x
W
x
O
6
. We resolve the phase boundaries of a nonmagnetic state
x\in[0.08,0.55]
by the finite-size scaling of various order parameters. The intermediate phase is absent of long-range Néel and stripe orders, valence-bond glass order, as well as glassy dynamics. Yet, it still exhibits short-range spin-spin correlations and a broad tail in
S_{\text{dyn}}
, which are characteristic of disordered SLL states. Deep inside the nonmagnetic phase around
x=0.3
, the randomness-induced frustration index reaches the maximum, bringing a series of non-trivial features including almost uniform
S_{\text{sta}}
(or equivalently,
f_{\text{Q}}
) over the BZ. The broad peaks in
S_{\text{dyn}}
are also stretched away from the two ordering wave vectors
\bm{k}=(\pi,\pi)
and
(\pi,0)
, marking the difference from magnon-like excitations.

Although the bond frustration from the small couplings
J^{\prime}_{1},J^{\prime}_{2},J^{\prime\prime}_{1}
is not strong enough to support an all-to-all disordered random-singlet state as
q_{\text{EA}}>0
, the frustration index in the model increases with structured randomness, thus favoring a disordered SLL state. Finally, from the analysis of the QFI, the nonmagnetic phase is multipatite entangled, and the vanishing universal scaling exponent of the QFI density with respect to
N
across phase transitions shows the robustness of our phase boundaries in the thermodynamic limit.

In future, one may generalize the calculation of the QFI to thermal equilibrium mixed states for
T>0
, using
Eq.

17
and the Hams–de Raedt method
[
77
]
. A perfect scaling collapse at low temperatures
[
32
]
is expected from vanishing critical exponent at
T=0
, and will extend the critical-
T
regime of the phase diagram in
Fig.

1
(b). It is also possible to study the QFI far from equilibrium in our model using small-size ED clusters, e.g.
N=10
spins
[
78
]
.

Going beyond ED, one may also apply variational Monte Carlo (VMC) to explore 2D random quantum magnets. The recently developed neural quantum states
[
79
,
80
]
, especially the transformer architectures
[
81
,
82

, can be expressive variational ansatzes to capture the rich complexity of the physical system. There have been successful attempts at applying neural quantum states to approximate the QSL state in the regular

-
J_{2}

[

[

Given the flexibility of neural networks, the access to larger system sizes is envisioned, which enables one to further resolve the dispersion of the dynamical spin structure factor and the spin excitation gaps, casting more light on the nature of the nonmagnetic state.

A.2

The ED is implemented using the software package

A.3
Averaged observables over random instances

We use stratified sampling to generate random instances of the Hamiltonian with various numbers of
J_{2}
-dominated plaquettes, then reweight them according to the binomial distribution when computing the averaged observables given the probability
x
. Therefore, we can reuse the observable values of the instances for all values of

For each
k=0,1,\ldots,N
, we generate
M_{k}=\min(M_{k\text{max}},\binom{N}{k})
instances randomly without replacement, each with exactly
k

J_{2}
-dominated plaquettes. Here
M_{k\text{max}}
is the maximal number of instances for each
k
limited by the computation budget, and the actual number
M_{k}
is also limited by the number of combinations
\binom{N}{k}
. We take
M_{k\text{max}}=100
when
N\leq 20
,
M_{k\text{max}}=30
when
N=24,28
, and
M_{k\text{max}}=10
when
N=32
.

When computing an averaged observable
[O]_{J}=[\!\expectationvalue*{O}]_{J}
, we first take a uniform average over instances with the same
k
, then average over the binomial distribution of
k
:

\displaystyle[O]_{J}(N,x)=\sum_{k}B(k,N,x)\bar{O}_{k}(N),

(S1)

\displaystyle B(k,N,x)=\binom{N}{k}x^{k}(1-x)^{N-k},

(S2)

\displaystyle\bar{O}_{k}(N)=\frac{1}{M_{k}}\sum_{i}O_{i},

(S3)

where
i
runs over all
M_{k}
instances whose number of
J_{2}
-dominated plaquettes equals
k
, and
O_{i}=\expectationvalue*{O}_{i}
is the observable value of this instance. As
O_{i}
is independent of
x
, we can reuse it for all values of
x
. For each
x
, we compute
[O]_{J}(N,x)
with finite values of

, then extrapolate to
[O]_{J}(N\to\infty,x)
, as in
Fig.

2

The standard error of this weighted average is given by

\displaystyle O_{\text{err}}^{2}(N,x)=\frac{1}{M_{\text{eff}}(N,x)}\sum_{k}B(k,N,x)O_{k\text{err}}^{2}(N),

(S4)

\displaystyle M_{\text{eff}}(N,x)=\frac{1}{\sum_{k}B(k,N,x)^{2}},

(S5)

\displaystyle O_{k\text{err}}^{2}(N)=\left(1-\frac{M_{k}}{\binom{N}{k}}\right)\frac{1}{M_{k}^{2}}\sum_{i}(O_{i}-\bar{O}_{k})^{2},

(S6)

where

A.4
Uncertainty of extrapolation

We use linear regression to extrapolate observables to
N\to\infty
, and propagate uncertainties of both the averages over random instances and the regression.

Consider a linear regression with
P
data points
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{P})^{\top}
,
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{P})^{\top}
. The standard errors in
\bm{y}
are
\bm{y}_{\text{err}}=(y_{\text{err}1},\ldots,y_{\text{err}P})^{\top}
, and there is no error in
\bm{x}
. We have

\bm{y}=\bm{X}\bm{\beta}+\bm{\epsilon},

(S7)

where
\bm{X}=(\bm{1},\bm{x})
is a
P\times 2
matrix including the constant term,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{0},\beta_{1})^{\top}
contains the intercept and the slope, and
\bm{\epsilon}
is the errors of regression. Note that
\bm{\epsilon}
is heteroscedastic and we cannot assume that all components of
\bm{\epsilon}
are from distributions with the same variance, because the deviation of
\bm{y}
from the linear relation is caused by the finite size effect, whose strength changes with the system size.

The parameters are estimated by least squares:

\bm{\beta}=\bar{\bm{X}}\bm{y},\quad\bar{\bm{X}}=(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top},

(S8)

where
\bar{\bm{X}}
is the pseudoinverse of
\bm{X}
. The variance matrix of parameters from the errors in
\bm{y}
is

\Var^{(1)}\bm{\beta}=\bar{\bm{X}}(\diag\bm{y}_{\text{err}})^{2}\bar{\bm{X}}^{\top},

(S9)

and another contribution from the errors of regression is

\displaystyle\Var^{(2)}\bm{\beta}=\frac{P}{P-2}\bar{\bm{X}}(\diag\bm{\epsilon})^{2}\bar{\bm{X}}^{\top},

(S10)

\displaystyle\bm{\epsilon}=\bm{y}-\bm{X}\bm{\beta}.

(S11)

The total variance
\Var\bm{\beta}=\Var^{(1)}\bm{\beta}+\Var^{(2)}\bm{\beta}
, and the standard error of the intercept
\beta_{0\text{err}}
is the square root of its first component. The error bars
\beta_{0\text{err}}
are shown in
Fig.

when
N\to\infty
.

Appendix B
QFI density from dynamical spin structure factor

Here, we briefly review the approach
[
32
,
36
,
37
]
of obtaining the QFI density
f_{\text{Q}}
through the dynamical spin structure in
Eq.

17
. It assumes thermal ensembles at the equilibrium and adapts the fluctuation-dissipation theorem for non-symmetrized
S_{\text{dyn}}(\bm{k},\omega,T)

[
88
]
.

Let us take a generator
O=\sqrt{N}S^{z\dagger}_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i}^{z}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}
. Considering the definition of QFI density
f_{\text{Q}}=f_{\text{Q}}/N
and the form of QFI in
Eq.

16
, we have

f_{\text{Q}}(\bm{k},T)=2\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{(p_{\lambda}-p_{\lambda^{\prime}})^{2}}{p_{\lambda}+p_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\left|\!\matrixelement{\lambda}{S^{z \dagger}_{\bm{k}}}{\lambda'}\right|^{2}.

(S12)

It is convenient to introduce the dynamical susceptibility

\chi(\bm{k},\omega,T)=i\int_{0}^{\infty}\Tr\left[\rho[S^{z\dagger}_{\bm{k}}(t),S^{z}_{\bm{k}}(0)]\right]e^{i\omega t}\differential t,

(S13)

which, after taking the integral, shares the form

\displaystyle\chi(\bm{k},\omega,T)=i\pi\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}

\displaystyle(p_{\lambda}-p_{\lambda^{\prime}})\left|\!\matrixelement{\lambda}{S^{z \dagger}_{\bm{k}}(0)}{\lambda'}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\delta(\omega+E_{\lambda}-E_{\lambda^{\prime}}).

(S14)

In reaching the form above, we have adopted the density matrix
\rho=\sum_{\lambda}p_{\lambda}\ket{\lambda}\bra{\lambda}
with
p_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-E_{\lambda}/T}
, and inserted the identity
\mathds{1}=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}}\ket{\lambda'}\bra{\lambda'}
.
Comparing
Eq.

S14
with
Eq.

S12
and taking into account

\frac{p_{\lambda}-p_{\lambda^{\prime}}}{p_{\lambda}+p_{\lambda^{\prime}}}=\tanh(\frac{E_{\lambda'} - E_\lambda}{2 T}),

(S15)

we arrive at

f_{\text{Q}}(\bm{k},T)=\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\tanh(\frac{\omega}{2 T})\chi^{\prime\prime}(\omega,\bm{k},T)\,\differential\omega,

(S16)

where
\chi^{\prime\prime}
denotes the imaginary (dissipative) part of the dynamical susceptibility.

On the other hand, one can also relate
\chi^{\prime\prime}
to the non-symmetrized dynamical spin structure in
Eq.

14
through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. Starting from the integral definition of
\chi
in
Eq.

S13
, it is straightforward to identify that

\chi^{\prime\prime}(\bm{k},\omega,T)=\frac{1}{2}\left(g_{1}(\bm{k},\omega,T)-g_{2}(\bm{k},\omega,T)\right),

(S17)

with

\displaystyle g_{1}(\bm{k},\omega,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\expectationvalue*{S^{z \dagger}_{\bm{k}}(t) S^z_{\bm{k}}(0)}e^{i\omega t}\,\differential t,

\displaystyle g_{2}(\bm{k},\omega,T)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\expectationvalue*{S^z_{\bm{k}}(0) S^{z \dagger}_{\bm{k}}(t)}e^{i\omega t}\,\differential t.

(S18)

The notation
\expectationvalue*{\cdots}=\Tr[\rho\cdots]
is adopted for a thermal state. Since
g_{1}=2\pi S_{\text{dyn}}
, if we can further associate
g_{2}
with
g_{1}
, our goal will be accomplished. It is again more convenient to compare these two quantities after integration. It turns out that

\displaystyle g_{2}(\bm{k},\omega,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{e^{-E_{\lambda^{\prime}}/T}}{Z}\left|\!\matrixelement{\lambda}{S^{z \dagger}_{\bm{k}}(0)}{\lambda'}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{{}=\sum_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}}\times 2\pi\delta(\omega+E_{\lambda}-E_{\lambda^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\omega}{T}}g_{1}(\bm{k},\omega,T).

(S19)

Thus we obtain the adapted fluctuation-dissipation theorem,

\chi^{\prime\prime}(\bm{k},\omega,T)=\pi\left(1-e^{-\frac{\omega}{T}}\right)S_{\text{dyn}}(\bm{k},\omega,T).

(S20)

Combining
Eqs.

S16
and
S20
, the entanglement witness
f_{\text{Q}}
is reconstructed from the quantum fluctuations contained in
S_{\text{dyn}}
, and we reach
Eq.

17
:

f_{\text{Q}}(\bm{k},T)=4\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\tanh(\frac{\omega}{2 T})\left(1-e^{-\frac{\omega}{T}}\right)S_{\text{dyn}}(\bm{k},\omega,T)\,\differential\omega.

(S21)
