A study for recovering the cut-elimination property in cyclic proof systems by restricting the arity of inductive predicates
Yukihiro Oda, Daisuke Kimura

Abstract
The framework of cyclic proof systems provides a reasonable proof system for logics with inductive definitions. It also offers an effective automated proof search procedure for such logics without finding induction hypotheses. Recent researches have shown that the cut-elimination property, one of the most fundamental properties in proof theory, of cyclic proof systems for several logics does not hold. These results suggest that a naive proof search, which avoids the Cut rule, is not enough. This paper shows that the cut-elimination property still fails in a simple cyclic proof system even if we restrict languages to unary inductive predicates and unary functions, aiming to clarify why the cut-elimination property fails in the cyclic proof systems. The result in this paper is a sharper one than that of the first authors’ previous result, which gave a counterexample using two ternary inductive predicates and a unary function symbol to show the failure of the cut-elimination property in the cyclic proof system of the first-order logic.

Introduction

Inductive definition is a way to define mathematical objects based on the induction principle.
Several notions, which are essential in both mathematics and computer science,
such as natural numbers, lists, and binary trees, are inductively defined.
Inductively defined predicates are called
inductive predicates
.
A typical example of inductive predicates is
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(x)
that means “
x
is a natural number”.
It is given by the following Martin Löf style schemata (called
productions
)
[
12
]
:

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(\mathtt{0})

and

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(x)

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(\mathtt{s}x)

,

where
\mathtt{0}
is a constant symbol, and
\mathtt{s}
is a unary function symbol.
The first production means that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(\mathtt{0})
holds without any assumptions, namely,
it says that “
\mathtt{0}
is a natural number”.
The second one means that
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(x)
implies
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(\mathtt{s}x)
, namely,
it says that “if
x
is a natural number, then
\mathtt{s}x
is also a natural number”.
It is also assumed that no other rule can be applied to obtain
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(t)
for any term
t
.
Hence, these productions say that the predicate
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}
is defined as the least one
that satisfies the following equivalence:

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(x)\Leftrightarrow(x=\mathtt{0}\lor\exists y.(x=\mathtt{s}y\land\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(y))).

This equivalence gives an alternative definition of the natural number predicate
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}

instead of giving its productions.

Proof systems for logics with inductive predicates have been studied in the literature
[
12
,
19
]
.
It is known that Gentzen’s sequent calculus LK can be extended with inductive predicates in a uniform way.
For example, the following inference rules for the natural number predicate
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}
are generated
from the productions of
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}
:

\Gamma\vdash\Delta,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(\mathtt{0})

,

\Gamma\vdash\Delta,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(t)

\Gamma\vdash\Delta,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(\mathtt{s}t)

,

\Gamma\vdash\Delta,F[\mathtt{0}]

\Gamma,F[x]\vdash\Delta,F[\mathtt{s}x]

\Gamma,F[t]\vdash\Delta

(IND)

\Gamma,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(t)\vdash\Delta

,

where
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are multisets of formulas,
x
is a fresh variable,
t
is a term,

F
is a formula with a fixed variable
z
, and
F[t]
is the result of substituting
t
for
z
in
F
.
The last inference rule (IND) corresponds to the induction principle on the natural numbers,
and the formula
F
is its induction hypothesis.
Although the last rule is a reasonable formalization of the induction principle,
it causes a difficult problem when we apply a naive proof search algorithm to this proof system
because we need to find (or guess) an appropriate induction hypothesis
F
in the upper sequents
from the lower sequent.

An alternative choice to formulate inductive predicates in sequent calculi is to adopt rules,
instead of the (IND) rules, that unfold inductive predicates on the left-hand side of a sequent,
according to the equivalence that defines the predicate.
Brotherston and Simpson
[
4
]
proposed the proof systems (called
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
and
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
)
for classical first-order logic, based on this idea.
In their systems, the unfolding rules (called the
casesplit
rule)
for the natural number predicate
\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}
is given as follows:

\Gamma,t=\mathtt{0}\vdash\Delta

\Gamma,t=\mathtt{s}y,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(y)\vdash\Delta

(\textsc{Case}\,{\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}})

\Gamma,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{N}(t)\vdash\Delta

,
where
y
is a fresh variable.

Although this idea gives a solution to avoid the problem of finding induction hypotheses in proof search,
it requires considering infinite proofs, which might have infinite paths.
The system
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
is a proof system that admits such infinite proofs which satisfy
a condition (called the
global trace condition
) that ensures the soundness of the system.
The cyclic proof system
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
is a reasonable restriction of
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}

that admits only proofs which are regular trees.
It is formulated as a finite derivation tree with open assumptions (called
buds
) and additional edges
that connects from each bud to an internal node (called
companion
of the bud).

The framework of cyclic proof systems gives a general way to formalize logics with inductive definitions
(or the least/greatest fixed point operators).
It has been proposed several cyclic proof systems other than classical first-order logic,
such as logic of bunched implications
[
3
]
, separation logic
[
5
]
, and
linear logic with the fixed point operators
[
1
,
9
]
.

The cut-elimination property of a proof system states that any provable sequent in the system is
also provable without the rule
(\textsc{Cut})
, which is given below:

\Gamma\vdash\Delta,F

F,\Gamma\vdash\Delta

(\textsc{Cut})

\Gamma\vdash\Delta

.

This property is one of the most fundamental properties of proof systems
because it helps us to investigate a given proof system.
For example, some important properties, such as the subformula property and consistency of the proof system,
are often obtained using the cut-elimination property.
It is also important for the proof search method
since it ensures that it is enough to search except for
(\textsc{Cut})
,
which requires to find an appropriate cut formula from possibly infinitary many candidates.

Some infinite proof systems are known to enjoy the cut-elimination property:
Brotherston and Simpson proved that the cut-elimination theorem of
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
by showing
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
is sound
and cut-free complete to a standard model for inductive predicates
[
4
]
.
Fortier and Santocanale
[
10
]
introduced a cyclic proof system for additive linear logic
with the least and greatest fixed point operators, and showed that the rule
(\textsc{Cut})
can be eliminated
if we admit to lose the regularity of proof-trees. Doumane
[
9
]
investigated
an infinite proof system
\mu\mathtt{MALL}^{\infty}
for the multiplicative and additive linear logic
with the least and greatest fixed point operators,
and showed its cut-elimination theorem.

In contrast, the situation for cyclic proof systems is totally different.
The open problem about the cut-elimination property of
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
by Brotherston
was negatively solved in the first authors’ recent work
[
13
]
.
The second author showed that the cut-elimination property does not hold in a cyclic proof system
of separation logic
[
11
]
.
Saotome showed the failure of the cut-elimination property for the cyclic proof system of the logic of bunched implications
even if we restrict inductive predicates to nullary predicates
[
15
]
.

There are automated theorem provers based on proof search algorithms
of cyclic proof systems
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Some of them adopt an additional mechanism that guesses cut formulas.
They record sequents which are found during an execution of proof search procedure, and then try to generate possible candidates of
cut formulas from the recorded sequents
[
7
,
8
,
16
,
17
]
.
This technique extends the ability of the provers to find cyclic proofs which may contain
(\textsc{Cut})
,
and also gives an efficient proof search procedure.
However, Saotome
[
14
]
suggested that there still exist sequents in
the symbolic heap separation logic that cannot be found by a normal proof search procedure
admitting
(\textsc{Cut})
whose cut formulas are presumable from the goal sequent.
Recently the framework of cyclic proof-search has been studied from the viewpoint of software verification
[
20
,
21
]
.

In our recent research, we have fixed our attention to the following two natural questions about the cut-elimination property in cyclic proofs.

•

Can we recover the cut-elimination property of cyclic proof systems (in particular
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
)
by restricting the definitions of inductive predicates?

•

Is there a reasonable restriction of
(\textsc{Cut})
whose cut formulas can be found in small search space,
and that does not lose provability of the original cyclic proof system.

This paper focuses on the first question.
We show that the cut-elimination property of
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
still fails
even if we restrict inductive predicates to two unary predicates (
\mathop{\mathrm{F\mathtt{s}T}}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{T\mathtt{e}F}}
) with two constant symbols (
\mathtt{s}
and
\mathtt{e}
) and
one unary function symbol (
\mathtt{n}
).
The proof technique of this paper is a modified and simplified one of the first author’s previous work
[
13
]
.
Our discussion starts from introducing a simple subsystem (called the base system
\mathcal{B}
) that only contains equality and inductive predicates
because other logical connectives and quantifiers are not necessary.
Then we define a cyclic proof system (called
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
) of
\mathcal{B}
, and apply the proof technique to
our counterexample
\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{T\mathtt{e}F}}}\left(\mathtt{s}\right)\vdash\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{F\mathtt{s}T}}}\left(\mathtt{e}\right)
.
This counterexample also works for
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
since we can easily check that
if
\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{T\mathtt{e}F}}}\left(\mathtt{s}\right)\vdash\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{F\mathtt{s}T}}}\left(\mathtt{e}\right)
is cut-free provable in
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
, then its proof is also a cut-free proof in
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
.

.
In

\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

where

3.2
Infinitary proof system
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

In this subsection,
we define an infinitary proof system
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
for
\mathcal{B}
.
The inference rules of
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
are the rules displayed in Figure
1

and the rules for inductive predicates given in the previous subsection.

We write
\left\langle n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\right\rangle

for the sequence of natural numbers
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
.
The length
|\sigma|
of a sequent
\sigma
is defined by the number of elements in
\sigma
.
Let
\mathbb{N}^{*}
be the set of finite sequences of natural numbers.
We write
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
for the concatenation of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
in
\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We abbreviate
\sigma\left\langle n\right\rangle
by
\sigma n

for
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
\mathbf{Rule}
and
\mathbf{Seq}
be the set of names of the inference rules and
the set of sequents of
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
, respectively.

Definition 6

(Derivation tree)
.

We define a
derivation tree
to be a partial function

\mathcal{D}\colon\mathbb{N}^{*}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{Seq}\times\left(\mathbf{Rule}\cup\left\{\text{({Bud})}\right\}\right)

satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

The domain
\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
of
\mathcal{D}
is prefixed-closed, namely,
for
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)

implies

\sigma_{1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
.

(2)

If
\sigma n\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
for
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
then
\sigma m\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
for any
m\leq n
.

(3)

For each
\sigma\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
,
we write
(\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right),\mathop{\mathrm{Rule}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right))
for
\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\left(\sigma\right)
.
Then the following hold.

(a)

If
\mathop{\mathrm{Rule}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)=\text{({Bud})}
, then
\sigma 0\notin\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
.

(b)

If
\mathop{\mathrm{Rule}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)\neq\text{({Bud})}
,

\sigma\,(n+1)\not\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
, and
\sigma 0,\ldots,\sigma n\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
, then
the following is a rule instance of the rule
\mathop{\mathrm{Rule}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)
:

\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma 0\right)

\cdots

\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma n\right)

\mathop{\mathrm{Rule}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)

\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)

An element in the domain of a derivation tree is called a
node
.
The empty sequence as a node is called the
root
.
The node
\sigma
is called a
bud
if
\mathop{\mathrm{Rule}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)
is
(\textsc{Bud})
.
We write
\mathop{\mathrm{bud}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)
for the set of buds in
\mathcal{D}
.
The node which is not a bud is called an
inner node
.
A derivation tree is called
infinite
if the domain of the derivation tree is infinite.

We sometimes identify a node
\sigma
with the sequent
\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)
.

Definition 7

(Path)
.

We define a
path
in a derivation tree
\mathcal{D}
to be a (possibly infinite) sequence

\left(\sigma_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}
of nodes in
\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)

such that
\sigma_{i+1}=\sigma_{i}n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}

and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}\cup\left\{\omega\right\}
, where

\mathbb{N}_{>0}
is the set of positive natural numbers and
\omega
is the least infinite ordinal.
A finite path
\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
is called

a path from
\sigma_{0}
to
\sigma_{n}
.
The
length of a finite path

\left(\sigma_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}

is defined as
\alpha
.
We define
the height of a node
as the length of the path from the root to the node.

We sometimes write
\left(\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}

for the path
\left(\sigma_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}
in a derivation tree
\mathcal{D}

if
\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma_{i}\right)=\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}
.

Definition 8

(Trace)
.

For a path
\left(\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}

in a derivation tree
\mathcal{D}
,
we define a
trace following

\left(\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}

to be a sequence of formulas
\left(\tau_{i}\right)_{0\leq i<\alpha}

such that the following hold:

(1)

\tau_{i}
is an inductive predicate in
\Gamma_{i}
.

(2)

If
\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}
is the conclusion of
(\textsc{Subst})
with
\theta
,
then
\tau_{i}
is
\tau_{i+1}\left[\theta\right]
.

(3)

If
\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}
is the conclusion of
(\textsc{$=\text{L}^{\prime}$})

with the principal formula
t=u
and

\tau_{i}
is
\varphi\left[x:=t,y:=u\right]
,
then
\tau_{i+1}
is
\varphi\left[x:=u,y:=t\right]
.

(4)

If
\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}
is the conclusion of
(\textsc{Case}\,{P_{i}})
,
then either

•

\tau_{i}
is the principal formula of the rule and
\tau_{i+1}

is a case-descendant of
\tau_{i}
, or

•

\tau_{i+1}
is the same as
\tau_{i}
.

In the former case,
\tau_{i}
is said to be a
progress point
of the trace.

(5)

If
\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}
is the conclusion of any other rules and
i+1<\alpha
,
then
\tau_{i+1}
is
\tau_{i}
.

Definition 9

(Global trace condition)
.

If a trace has infinitely many progress points,
we call the trace an
infinitely progressing trace
.
If there exists an infinitely progressing trace following a tail of the path

\left(\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}\right)_{i\geq k}
with some
k\geq 0

for every infinite path
\left(\Gamma_{i}\mathrel{\vdash}\Delta_{i}\right)_{i\geq 0}
in a derivation tree,
we say the derivation tree satisfies the
global trace condition
.

Definition 10

(
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof)
.

A (possibly infinite) derivation tree
\mathcal{D}
without buds is called a
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

pre-proof
.
The sequent
\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\left\langle\right\rangle\right)
at the root node
is called the conclusion of
\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 11

(
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof)
.

A
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof that satisfies the global trace condition is called a
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

proof
.

The global trace condition was originally introduced as a sufficient condition for the soundness of

\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
with respect to the standard models in Brotherston’s paper
[
2
,
4
]
.
It also ensures the soundness of
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
, since
a
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof can be transformed to a
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
proof by replacing
(\textsc{$=\text{L}^{\prime}$})
by
(\textsc{$=\text{L}$})
.
Brotherston also showed the cut-free completeness of
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
for the standard models.
The cut-free completeness of
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
follows from this result:
a cut-free
\mathtt{LKID}^{\omega}
proof of a sequent in
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
can contain only the rules

(\textsc{Axiom})
,
(\textsc{Weak})
,
(\textsc{Subst})
,
(\textsc{$=\text{L}$})
,
(\textsc{$=\text{R}$})
, and the rules for inductive predicates.
Hence the cut-free proof can be transformed to a cut-free
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof
by replacing
(\textsc{$=\text{L}$})
by
(\textsc{$=\text{L}^{\prime}$})
with
(\textsc{Weak})
.

3.3
Cyclic proof system
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

In this section, we introduce a cyclic proof system
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
.

Definition 12

(Companion)
.

For a finite derivation tree
\mathcal{D}
,
we define the
companion
for a bud
\sigma_{\textit{bud}}
as an inner node
\sigma
in
\mathcal{D}
with

\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{Seq}}\left(\mathcal{D},\sigma_{bud}\right)
.

Definition 13

(
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof)
.

We define a
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

pre-proof

to be a pair
\left(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C}\right)

such that
\mathcal{D}
is a finite derivation tree and

\mathcal{C}
is a function mapping each bud to its companion.
The sequent at the root node of
\mathcal{D}
is called the conclusion of the proof.

Definition 14

(Tree-unfolding)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof
\left(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C}\right)
.
A
tree-unfolding

\mathop{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
of
\mathcal{P}
is recursively defined by

\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}\left(\sigma\right)=\begin{cases}\mathop{\mathcal{D}}\left(\sigma\right),&\text{if $\sigma\in\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\setminus\mathop{\mathrm{bud}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)$,}\\
\mathop{\mathop{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)}\left(\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}\right),&\text{if $\sigma\notin\mathop{\mathrm{dom}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\setminus\mathop{\mathrm{bud}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)$ with $\sigma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}$, $\sigma_{1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{bud}}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)$ and $\sigma_{3}=\mathop{\mathcal{C}}\left(\sigma_{1}\right)$,}\end{cases}

Note that a tree-unfolding is a
\hbox{$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof.

Definition 15

(
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof)
.

A
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof
\mathcal{P}
of a sequent
S
is called a
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

proof
of
S

if its tree-unfolding
\mathop{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)
satisfies the global trace condition.
A
cut-free

\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof is a
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof that does not contain
(\textsc{Cut})
.
A sequent
S
is said to be
(cut-free) provable
in
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

if a (cut-free)
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
proof of
S
exists.

A
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof in which each companion is an ancestor of the corresponding bud is called
cycle-normal
.
The following proposition says that
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
satisfies the cycle-normalization property.

Proposition 16
.

For a
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
pre-proof
\mathcal{P}
, we have
a
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}
cycle-normal pre-proof
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
such that

\mathop{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{T}}\left(\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\right)
.

This property was already shown by Brotherston in a general setting that includes
\hbox{C$\mathcal{B}$}^{\omega}

[
2
]
.
Besides it, a shorter proof for
\mathtt{CLKID}^{\omega}
was given in
[
13
]
.
It can be applied straightforwardly to the current setting.
(We give the proof in the appendix for the reviewer’s convenience.)
