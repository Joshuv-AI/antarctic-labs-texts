Independence equivalence classes of cycles
Boon Leong Ng

Abstract
The independence equivalence class of a graph G G is the set of graphs that have the same independence polynomial as G G . Beaton, Brown and Cameron (2019) found the independence equivalence classes of even cycles, and raised the problem of finding the independence equivalence class of odd cycles. The problem is completely solved in this paper.

Definitions and Introduction

Let
G
be a simple graph with vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
. A set of vertices
U\subseteq V(G)
is said to be
independent
if no two vertices in
U
are adjacent. The
independence number

of

is the cardinality of the largest independent set of

independence polynomial

of

I(G,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha(G)}i_{k}(G)x^{k}
where

is the number of independent subsets of

of cardinality
k
. A survey of results on independence polynomials can be found in
[
16
]
.

Two graphs
G
and

are said to be

if
I(G,x)=I(H,x)
. The
independence equivalence class

\mathcal{I}(G)
of a graph
G
is the set of graphs which are independence equivalent to
G
. A graph
G
is said to be
independence unique
if
\mathcal{I}(G)=\{G\}
, that is, if
I(G,x)=I(H,x)
implies that
G\cong H
(
G
is isomorphic to

).

In the study of graph polynomials, the problem of finding nonisomorphic graphs that have equivalent polynomials is one that naturally arises. Factorisation of the Independence Polynomial of
C_{n}

In this section, we factorise
I(C_{n},x)
over
\mathbb{Z}[x]
to investigate properties of the factors.

The roots of the independence polynomials of cyclic graphs have been completely determined by Alikhani and Peng
[
3
]
.

Proposition 7
.

[
3
]

The roots of
I(C_{n},x)
are

c_{i}=-\frac{1}{2+2\cos\left(\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{n}\right)}

for
i=1,2,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
.

Now, the minimal polynomials of
\cos(2\pi k/n)
have been previously determined by Lehmer
[
15
]
and Watkins and Zeitlin
[
18
]
.

Proposition 8
.

[
15
]

If
\gcd(k,n)=1
and
n\geq 3
then the minimal polynomial of
2\cos(2\pi k/n)
has degree
\phi(n)/2
and leading coefficient
1
, where
\phi(n)
is Euler’s totient function.

Watkins and Zeitlin
[
18
]
give an explicit construction for this minimal polynomial in terms of Chebychev polynomials.

Proposition 9
.

[
18
]

The roots of the minimal polynomial of
2\cos(2\pi k/n)
are precisely those values of
2\cos(2\pi k/n)
for which
\gcd(k,n)=1
and
1\leq k<n/2
.

We are now in a position to find the minimal polynomials of
c_{i}
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer. For positive integers
i
where
\gcd(2i-1,n)=1
and
1\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor
, let
f(x)
be the minimal polynomial of
c_{i}
over
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. For positive integers
k
where
\gcd(k,n)=1
and
1\leq k<n/2
, let
g(x)
be the minimal polynomial of
2\cos((2\pi k)/(2n))
over
\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then
f(x)
and
g(x)
are of the same degree
d=\phi(n)/2
. Furthermore,
f(x)|I(C_{n},x)
.

Proof.

Let

x_{i}=2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi(2i-1)}{2n}\right)=-\frac{2c_{i}+1}{c_{i}}.

Since
n
is odd,
\gcd(2i-1,n)=1
iff
\gcd(2i-1,2n)=1
. Therefore, the set of values taken by

as

varies is precisely those of
2\cos((2\pi k)/(2n))
as
k
varies.

\displaystyle\left\{x_{i}:\gcd(2i-1,n)=1\textrm{ and }1\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{2\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{2n}\right):\gcd(k,n)=1\textrm{ and }1\leq k<\frac{n}{2}\right\}

From Proposition
8
,
g(x)
has degree
\phi(2n)/2=\phi(n)/2
and leading coefficient 1. From Proposition
9
, we now have

g(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(2i-1,n)=1,\\
1\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\end{subarray}}(x-x_{i})

Since we know that
g(x)
is an irreducible polynomial with integer coefficients and degree
d
, let

g(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{d}a_{t}x^{t}.

Now, we can translate
g(x)
along the

with integer coefficients and degree

, whose roots are
x_{i}+2
.

g(x-2)=\sum_{t=0}^{d}a_{t}(x-2)^{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{d}b_{t}x^{t}=h(x).

Since
x_{i}+2=-1/c_{i}
,

0=\sum_{t=0}^{d}a_{t}x_{i}^{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{d}b_{t}(x_{i}+2)^{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{d}b_{t}\left(-\frac{1}{c_{i}}\right)^{t}=\left(-\frac{1}{c_{i}}\right)^{d}\sum_{t=0}^{d}b_{t}(-c_{i})^{d-t}.

and therefore the

are roots of a polynomial

f(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{d}b_{t}(-x)^{d-t}.

Since

is irreducible,

is irreducible as well, and has degree

and integer coefficients.

The roots of

are precisely the values of

where
\gcd(2i-1,n)=1
and

. As all these values of

are also roots of

, we see that
f(x)|I(C_{n},x)
.
∎

We can therefore define a sequence of polynomials

3
Structure of a graph which is independence equivalent to
C_{n}

Definition 16
.

[
6
]

A polynomial
p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}p_{i}x^{i}
, where the
p_{i}
are all non-negative integers, is unicyclic if
p_{0}=1
and
p_{2}=\binom{p_{1}}{2}-p_{1}
.

Corollary 17
.

[
6
]

Let
G
be a connected graph. Then
G
has an independence polynomial that is unicyclic iff
G
is unicyclic.

Proposition 18
.

[
6
]

Suppose that
p(x)=q(x)r(x)
.

(i)

If
q(x)
and
r(x)
are unicyclic then
p(x)
is unicyclic.

(ii)

If
p(x)
and
q(x)
are unicyclic then
r(x)
is unicyclic.

Proposition 19
.

The polynomials
f_{n}(x)
for odd

are all unicyclic.

Proof.

Suppose that

has prime factorisation
p_{1}^{r_{1}}p_{2}^{r_{2}}\ldots p_{j}^{r_{j}}
. We use induction on
s_{n}=r_{1}+\ldots+r_{j}
. If

then

is prime, and therefore, by Corollary

which is unicyclic. Suppose it holds for all odd
n\geq 3
where
1\leq s_{n}<s
.

Let
n\geq 3
be odd where
s_{n}=s\geq 2
. Now

f_{n}(x)=\frac{I(C_{n},x)}{\displaystyle\prod_{m|n,m<n}f_{m}(x)}.

I(C_{n},x)
is unicyclic and, for all
m
such that
m|n
,
m<n
, we have
s_{m}<s
so

is unicyclic by our induction hypothesis. Therefore, by Proposition

(ii),
f_{n}(x)
is also unicyclic.

Proposition 20
.

If

, where

is odd, and

, then

are all unicyclic.

For each

,

is a factor of

. Therefore, it must be a product of some polynomials

. Since each of the

.

Therefore, if

, where

or

Definition 21

Let

.

[

If

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g_{i}=n

(ii)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i\cdot g_{i}=2n

(iii)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\binom{i}{2}g_{i}=n+n_{G}(C_{3})

Actually, Beaton, Brown and Cameron proved a stronger statement than Proposition

Proposition 23
.

If

, where

, then

n_{G}(C_{3})=\sum_{i=3}^{n-1}\binom{i-1}{2}g_{i}

Proof.

From Proposition

, if we add (i) and (iii) and subtract (ii), we get

\displaystyle n_{G}(C_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}g_{i}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}\binom{i}{2}g_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i\cdot g_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{0}+\sum_{i=3}^{n-1}\left(\binom{i}{2}-i+1\right)g_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{0}+\sum_{i=3}^{n-1}\binom{i-1}{2}g_{i}.

Now

is the number of isolated vertices. However, if

would be a factor of

, so

We now try to elucidate the structure of a graph

for odd

. If
G
is not
C_{n}
or

then, by Proposition

,

is not connected, and by Proposition

where the

are connected unicyclic graphs.

Since

does not have repeated roots, at most one of the connected components can be

. If any of the other connected components contain a triangle, then at least one of the vertices of the triangle must have degree 3 or larger.

Proposition 24
.

If
G\in\mathcal{I}(C_{n})
, where
n\geq 4
, then the maximum degree

of

is at most

Proof.

From Proposition

,

n_{G}(C_{3})=g_{3}+3g_{4}+6g_{5}+\ldots+\binom{n-2}{2}g_{n-1}.

Let

. Then

. Since every triangle, except at most one, has a vertex of degree 3 or larger, we see that

must be at least as large as the number of triangles less one, that is,
g_{3}+h\geq n_{G}(C_{3})-1
. Since we have
g_{3}+h\geq g_{3}+3h-1
and

is a nonnegative integer, the only possibility is that

, so

and the maximum degree of

is

. This also implies that

.

Proposition 25
.

If
G\in\mathcal{I}(C_{n})
, where
n\geq 4
, and
G
does not contain

as a connected component, then each connected component of

is either a cycle or a graph of the form

for some values of

.

Proof.

Let

be one of the connected components of
G
. From Proposition

, the maximum degree of

is at most

. Since

is unicyclic, if the maximum degree of

is

, then

must be a cycle.

If the maximum degree of

is

, then

must contain a triangle. Otherwise,

would need to have

as a connected component so that

. But this also implies that there must be exactly one vertex of degree 3 per triangle, so there is only one such vertex in

. Therefore,

must be isomorphic to

for some value of
m
.

Therefore, each connected component of
G
is either a cycle or a graph of the form

for some values of

.
∎

Proposition 26
.

If
G\in\mathcal{I}(C_{n})
where
n
is an odd positive number that is not a multiple of 3, then
G=C_{n}
or
G=D_{n}
.

Proof.

If
G
is not
C_{n}
or
D_{n}
then
G
is not connected, and by Proposition
20
,
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}G_{i}
where the
G_{i}
are connected unicyclic graphs. Since
n
is not a multiple of
3
, none of the
G_{i}
can be
C_{3}
, by Corollary
14
.

By Proposition
25
,
G
is a disjoint union of cyclic graphs and
D_{m}
’s. But each

is independence equivalent to
C_{m}
, hence
G
is independence equivalent to a disjoint union of cyclic graphs. In other words,
I(G,x)
is a product of independence polynomials of cyclic graphs:

Now I claim that

for all

. Suppose this was not the case, and that for some

. Then, from Proposition

is a root of both

and
I(C_{n_{j}},x)
, and hence a repeated root of

. However, also from Proposition

, none of the roots of

are repeated, which is a contradiction. Hence,

for all

.

Now, from Proposition

I(G,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\prod_{m|n_{i}}f_{m}(x).

For some

, note that

and

are included among the factors, but since

is not.

We also have

Therefore, since

and

are factors of

and

, so

is also a factor of

. This is a contradiction.

Hence,

must be a connected graph and so can only be

or

∎
