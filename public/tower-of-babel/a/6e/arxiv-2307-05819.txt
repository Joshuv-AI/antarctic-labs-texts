Linear and nonlinear transport equations with coordinate-wise increasing velocity fields
Pierre-Louis Lions, Benjamin Seeger

Abstract
We consider linear and nonlinear transport equations with irregular velocity fields, motivated by models coming from mean field games. The velocity fields are assumed to increase in each coordinate, and the divergence therefore fails to be absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure in general. For such velocity fields, the well-posedness of first- and second-order linear transport equations in Lebesgue spaces is established, as well as the existence and uniqueness of regular ODE and SDE Lagrangian flows. These results are then applied to the study of certain nonconservative, nonlinear systems of transport type, which are used to model mean field games in a finite state space. A notion of weak solution is identified for which a unique minimal and maximal solution exist, which do not coincide in general. A selection-by-noise result is established for a relevant example to demonstrate that different types of noise can select any of the admissible solutions in the vanishing noise limit.

Introduction

This paper has two main purposes. First, we develop a well-posedness theory for first- and second-order linear transport equations for a new class of irregular velocity fields, as well as the corresponding ODE and SDE regular Lagrangian flows. We then apply the results to the study of certain nonlinear transport systems, motivated in particular by applications to mean field games (MFG) with a finite state space.

For a fixed, finite time horizon
T>0
, we study both the terminal value problem (TVP) for the nonconservative equation

(1.1)

-\partial_{t}u-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla u=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad u(T,\cdot)=u_{0}

as well as the dual, initial value problem (IVP) for the conservative equation

\partial_{t}f+\operatorname{div}(b(t,x)f)=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad f(0,\cdot)=f_{0},

under the assumption that

\partial_{x_{j}}b^{i}(t,\cdot)\geq-C(t)\delta_{ij}\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$, $i,j=1,2,\ldots,d$, and some }C\in L^{1}_{+}([0,T]).

The divergence of such a vector field is bounded from below, which means that, formally, the flow

(1.4)

\mathbf{1}
denotes the vector
(1,1,\ldots,1)
in Euclidean space, the dimension being clear from context. Given two sets
A
and
B
,
A\triangle B:=(A\backslash B)\cup(B\backslash A)
.

2
Preliminary results

This section contains a collection of results regarding vector-valued notions of increasing/decreasing, as well as a vector-valued maximum principle.

2.1
Properties of increasing functions

We first introduce a partial order on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that is used throughout the paper.

Definition 2.1
.

For
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we will write

(2.1)

x\leq y\quad\text{if}\quad x_{i}\leq y_{i}\text{ for all }i=1,2,\ldots,d.

Given
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
a\leq b
, we denote by
[a,b]
the cube
\prod_{i=1}^{d}[a_{i},b_{i}]
. A function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
d,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, is said to be increasing if
\phi(x)\leq\phi(y)
whenever
x\leq y
. Equivalently,
\phi
is increasing if, for each
i=1,2,\ldots,m
,
\phi^{i}
is increasing in the
x_{j}
-coordinate for each
j=1,2,\ldots,d
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is increasing. Then
\phi\in BV_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and, for each
i=1,2\ldots,m
,

\liminf_{y\to x}\phi^{i}(y)=\liminf_{y\to x,\,y\gneq x}\phi^{i}(y)\quad\text{and}\quad\limsup_{y\to x}\phi^{i}(y)=\limsup_{y\to x,\,y\lneq x}\phi^{i}(y).

Proof.

For all
j=1,2,\ldots,d
and
i=1,2,\ldots,m
, the distribution
\partial_{j}\phi^{i}
is nonnegative, and is therefore a locally finite measure.

For any sequence
y_{n}\to x
, if
y_{n}\nleq x
, then
y_{n}
may be replaced with a value
y_{n}^{\prime}
such that
y_{n}^{\prime}\leq x
,
y_{n}^{\prime}\leq y_{n}
, and
\phi^{i}(y_{n}^{\prime})\leq\phi^{i}(x)
. A similar argument holds if
y_{n}\ngeq x
for all
n
, and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma
2.1
implies that each component of an increasing function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
has limits from both the “left” and “right.” We will call an increasing function
\phi
càdlàg if, for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
,

\phi^{i}(x)=\limsup_{y\to x}\phi^{i}(y)=\limsup_{y\to x,\,y\gneq x}\phi^{i}(y).

Remark 2.1
.

Given a nonnegative measure
\mu
, the repartition function

\phi(x):=\int_{(-\infty,x_{1})}\int_{(-\infty,x_{2})}\cdots\int_{(-\infty,x_{d})}d\mu

is an example of a càdlàg increasing function, but such functions do not cover the full range of increasing functions if
d\geq 2
; indeed, they are distinguished by the fact that mixed derivatives
\prod_{j=1}^{k}\partial_{x_{\ell_{j}}}\phi
for any distinct set
(\ell_{j})_{j=1}^{k}\subset\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
are still measures.

Consider a smooth surface
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that partitions
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
into two open sets, that is,
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}=U_{-}\cup\Gamma\cup U_{+}
,
\Gamma=\partial U_{+}=\partial U_{-}
,
U_{+}\cap U_{-}=\emptyset
. Let
n
be the normal vector to
\Gamma
that, at all points of
\Gamma
, points inward to
U_{+}
. If
n\geq 0
everywhere on
\Gamma
, in the sense of (
2.1
), then
\phi=\mathbf{1}_{U_{+}\cup\Gamma}
is a càdlàg increasing function that is not a repartition function.

2.2
ABV
functions

In one dimension, functions of bounded variation can be written as a difference of non-decreasing functions. With respect to the partial order (
2.1
), the generalization of this notion is a strict subspace of
BV
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
-\infty<a_{i}\leq b_{i}<\infty
for
i=1,2,\ldots d
and a function
\phi:Q:=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[a_{i},b_{i}]\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we say
\phi\in ABV(Q)
if

\left\|\phi\right\|_{ABV(Q)}:=\sup_{\gamma}\left\|\phi\circ\gamma\right\|_{BV([0,1])}<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all curves
\gamma:[0,1]^{d}\to Q
such that
\gamma_{i}:[0,1]\to[a_{i},b_{i}]
is increasing for all
i=1,2,\ldots,d
. We will say
\phi\in ABV=ABV({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
if
\left\|\phi\right\|_{ABV(Q)}<\infty
for all boxes
Q\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

For example,
C^{0,1}\subset ABV
. It is straightforward to see that
ABV=BV
when
d=1
.

Remark 2.2
.

Several generalizations of the notion of finite variation to multiple dimensions, besides the space
BV
, exist in the literature, and the one in Definition
2.2
is due to Arzelà
[
7
]
. It is a strictly smaller subspace than
BV
(as we show below). This notion of variation, along with several others, seems not to have had the same ubiquity in the theory of PDEs as the usual notion of
BV
, but is particularly relevant in this paper. More details about
ABV
functions, and many other notions of variation in multiple dimension, can be found in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
\phi\in ABV
if and only if
\phi=\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
for two increasing functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
a\leq b
. If
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\gamma:[0,1]\to[a,b]
are increasing, then
\phi\circ\gamma
is increasing, and thus
\left\|\phi\circ\gamma\right\|_{BV([0,1])}=\phi(b)-\phi(a)
. It follows that
\phi\in ABV
, and, by linearity, differences of increasing functions belong to
ABV
.

Now assume
\phi\in ABV([a,b])
, and, for
x\in[a,b]
, set

\phi_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\phi_{1}\right\|_{ABV([a,x])}+\phi(x)\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\phi_{1}\right\|_{ABV([a,x])}-\phi(x)\right).

Then
\phi=\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
, and
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are increasing.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\phi\in ABV
, then
\phi
is almost everywhere continuous and differentiable.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim about differentiability, and, by Lemma
2.2
, we may assume without loss of generality that
\phi
is increasing.

We argue using the characterization by Stepanoff
[
53
]
of a.e.-differentiability, that is, we prove that, for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{|\phi(y)-\phi(x)|}{|y-x|}<\infty,

or, equivalently,

\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{\phi(y)-\phi(x)}{|y-x|}<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\liminf_{y\to x}\frac{\phi(y)-\phi(x)}{|y-x|}>-\infty.

Denote by
Q_{r}:=[0,r]^{d}
, and note that, because
\phi
is increasing,

\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{\phi(y)-\phi(x)}{|y-x|}=\inf_{r>0}\sup_{y\in x+Q_{r}}\frac{\phi(y)-\phi(x)}{|y-x|}=\inf_{r>0}\frac{\phi(x+r\mathbf{1})-\phi(x)}{\sqrt{d}r}.

The function
[0,\infty)\ni r\to\phi(x+r\mathbf{1})
is increasing, and, thus, is differentiable almost everywhere in
[0,\infty)
. The finiteness of the above expression for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is then a consequence of Fubini’s theorem. The argument for the
\liminf
is identical.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

In view of Lemmas
2.1
and
2.3
, an increasing function is equal almost everywhere to a càdlàg function, and we assume for the rest of the paper that increasing functions are càdlàg.

2.3
One-sided regularizations

It will convenient at several times in the paper to specify regularizations of discontinuous functions that enjoy certain ordering properties. We specify two such regularization procedures here, each of which has merit in different situations.

We first discuss the
\inf
- and
\sup
-convolutions given, for some measurable function
\phi
, by

(2.2)

\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\{\phi(x-y)-\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\{\phi(x-y)+\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\right\}.

Then the following are either well-known properties or easy to check.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
\phi
is measurable and, for some
M>0
and all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
|\phi(x)|\leq M(1+|x|)
, and let
\phi_{\varepsilon}
and
\phi^{\varepsilon}
be defined by (
2.2
), which are finite as long as
\varepsilon<M^{-1}
. Then

(a)

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,M^{-1})
,
\phi_{\varepsilon}
and
\phi^{\varepsilon}
are Lipschitz with constant
\varepsilon^{-1}
, and
\phi_{\varepsilon}\leq\phi\leq\phi^{\varepsilon}
.

(b)

For all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)\searrow\phi^{*}(x)
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)\nearrow\phi_{*}(x)

(c)

If, moreover,
\phi
is increasing, then so are
\phi_{\varepsilon}
and
\phi^{\varepsilon}
.

Remark 2.4
.

If
\phi
is increasing, then the
\sup
or
\inf
can be restricted to “one side” of
x
. More precisely, given
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define

\hat{y}=-(|y_{1}|,|y_{2}|,\ldots,|y_{d}|),

Then
\hat{y}\leq y
and
|\hat{y}|=|y|
, and so, because
\phi
is increasing,

\phi(x-y)-\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\leq\phi(x-\hat{y})-\frac{|\hat{y}|}{\varepsilon}.

It follows that

\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\sup_{y\geq 0}\left\{\phi(x+y)-\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\right\},

and similarly

\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\inf_{y\leq 0}\left\{\phi(x+y)+\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\right\}.

The second example involves convolving with mollifying functions that are weighted to one side. Let
\rho
be a smooth, positive function with support contained in
[-1,1]^{d}
and
\int\rho=1
, and define

(2.3)

\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\rho\left(\frac{z-\varepsilon\mathbf{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\rho^{\varepsilon}(z)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\rho\left(\frac{z+\varepsilon\mathbf{1}}{\varepsilon}\right).

Lemma 2.5
.

Assume
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is locally bounded, and set
\phi^{\varepsilon}:=\rho^{\varepsilon}*\phi
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}*\phi
. Then, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}
converge to
\phi
in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
for any
p\in[1,\infty)
. Moreover, if
\phi
is increasing, then

(a)

\phi^{\varepsilon}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}
are increasing, and
\phi_{\varepsilon}\leq\phi\leq\phi^{\varepsilon}
.

(b)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}\searrow\phi^{*}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}\nearrow\phi_{*}
.

(c)

For all
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}(x-2\varepsilon\mathbf{1})=\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)
.

Proof.

The convergence in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is standard. For the rest of the proof, we assume
\phi
is increasing. We see immediately that
\phi^{\varepsilon}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}
are increasing. Observe that

\operatorname{supp}\rho_{\varepsilon}\subset(0,2\varepsilon)^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{supp}\rho^{\varepsilon}\subset(-2\varepsilon,0)^{d}.

As a consequence, because
\phi
is increasing,

\phi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi(x-y)\rho^{\varepsilon}(y)dy=\int_{(-2\varepsilon,0)^{d}}\phi(x-y)\rho^{\varepsilon}(y)dy\geq\phi^{*}(x),

and similarly
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq\phi_{*}(x)
, so that part (
a
) is established.

Now assume that
0<\varepsilon<\delta
. Using again that
\phi
is increasing, we find

\displaystyle\phi^{\delta}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta^{d}}\int_{(-2\delta,0)^{d}}\phi(x-y)\rho\left(\frac{y}{\delta}+\mathbf{1}\right)dy\geq\frac{1}{\delta^{d}}\int_{(-2\delta,0)^{d}}\phi\left(x-\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}y\right)\rho\left(\frac{y}{\delta}+\mathbf{1}\right)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\int_{(-2\varepsilon,0)^{d}}\phi(x-y)\rho\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}+\mathbf{1}\right)dy=\phi^{\varepsilon}(x),

and similarly
\phi_{\delta}\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}
. The convergence statements in part (
b
) then easily follow.

Finally, part (
c
) is seen upon computing

\phi^{\varepsilon}(x-2\varepsilon\mathbf{1})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi(y)\rho\left(\frac{x-y-\varepsilon\mathbf{1}}{\varepsilon}\right)dy=\phi_{\varepsilon}(x).

∎

Remark 2.5
.

An analogue of part (
c
) in Lemma
2.5
can also be seen for the
\sup
- and
\inf
-convolutions
\phi^{\varepsilon}
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}
in (
2.2
), namely, for all
R>0
, there exists
C_{R}>0
depending on the linear growth of
\phi
such that, for all
x\in B_{R}
,

\phi^{\varepsilon}(x-C_{R}\mathbf{1})\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}(x).

The advantage of the one-sided mollifications
\phi*\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
\phi*\rho_{\varepsilon}
is that the constant
C_{R}
can be replaced by a uniform constant that does not depend on the growth of
\phi
, which will be convenient when we consider
\phi
depending on an additional time parameter in an
L^{1}
way. On the other hand, the
\sup
- and
\inf
-convolutions are very flexible one-sided regularizations even when
\phi
is not itself increasing.

2.4
A maximum principle

The following multi-dimensional maximum principle is used at various points in the paper.

Lemma 2.6
.

For some
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
, assume that

(2.4)

\left\{\begin{split}&(a^{i})_{i=1}^{M}\subset C([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\mathbb{S}^{d}),\quad(b^{i})_{i=1}^{M}\subset C([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\quad\text{and}\\
&(c^{i})_{i=1}^{M},\;(d^{i})_{i=1}^{M},\;(\lambda^{i}_{j})_{i,j=1}^{M},\;(\mu_{k\ell}^{i})_{i,k,\ell=1}^{M},\\
&\text{are uniformly bounded}\end{split}\right.

and

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{split}&a^{i}(t,x)\geq 0,\quad d^{i}(t,x)\geq 0,\quad\lambda_{j}^{i}(t,x)\geq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{i}_{k\ell}(t,x)\leq 0\\
&\text{for all }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ and }i,j,k,\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}.\end{split}\right.

Let
(V^{i})_{i=1}^{M}\subset C^{1}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be bounded on
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and satisfy the system

(2.6)

\partial_{t}V^{i}-\operatorname{tr}[a^{i}D^{2}V^{i}]+b^{i}\cdot DV^{i}+c^{i}V^{i}+d^{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda^{i}_{j}V^{j}+\sum_{k,\ell\neq i}\mu^{i}_{k\ell}V^{k}V^{\ell}\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Suppose that, for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
i=1,2,\ldots,M
,
V^{i}(0,x)\leq 0
. Then, for all
(t,x)\in(0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
i=1,2,\ldots,M
,
V^{i}(t,x)\leq 0
.

Proof.

We prove the result first under the additional assumption that

\inf_{(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}d^{i}>0,\quad\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}V^{i}(0,x)<0,

and, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
,
x\mapsto V^{i}(t,x)
attains a global maximum on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In that case, define

t_{0}:=\inf\left\{t\in(0,T]:\max_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}}V^{i}(t,x)=0\right\},

so that
t_{0}>0
. Let
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
be such that the maximum is achieved. Note then that
DV^{i}(t_{0},x_{0})=0
,
D^{2}V^{i}(t_{0},x_{0})\leq 0
,
\partial_{t}V^{i}(t_{0},x_{0})\geq 0
, and, for all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
V^{j}(t_{0},x)\leq V^{i}(t_{0},x_{0})\leq 0
. We thus obtain

d^{i}(t_{0},x_{0})\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda^{i}_{j}V^{j}(t_{0},x_{0})+\sum_{k,\ell\neq i}\mu^{i}_{k\ell}V^{k}(t_{0},x_{0})V^{\ell}(t_{0},x_{0})\leq 0,

which is a contradiction in view of the assumption on
d^{i}
. In this case, we indeed conclude that
V^{i}(t,x)<0
for all
(t,x,i)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
.

We now turn to the general case. For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, define
\nu(x):=\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}}
, and note that
D\nu
and
D^{2}\nu
are globally bounded on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then standard arguments yield that, for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
,
\beta>0
, and
t\in[0,T]
, if
S_{\beta}
is set of maximum points of

x\mapsto V^{i}(t,x)-\beta\nu(x),

then
\lim_{\beta\to 0}\beta\sup_{x\in S_{\beta}}\nu(x)=0
. It follows that there exists a smooth bounded function
\nu_{\beta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\sup_{\beta>0}\left(\left\|D\nu_{\beta}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|D^{2}\nu_{\beta}\right\|_{\infty}\right)<\infty,\quad\lim_{\beta\to 0}\beta\left\|\nu_{\beta}\right\|_{\infty}=0,

and

\max_{(t,x,i)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}}\left(V^{i}(t,x)-\beta\nu(x)\right)=\max_{(t,x,i)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\{1,2,\ldots,M\}}\left(V^{i}(t,x)-\beta\nu_{\beta}(x)\right).

Fix
\delta>0
and a constant
C>0
to be determined. For
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
, set

\tilde{V}^{i}(t,x):=V^{i}(t,x)-\beta v_{\beta}(x)-\delta e^{Ct}.

Then, for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
,
\tilde{V}^{i}(0,\cdot)\leq-\delta
, and, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\tilde{V}^{i}(t,\cdot)
attains a global maximum over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Moreover, in
(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
(\tilde{V}^{i})_{i=1}^{M}
solves the system

\partial_{t}\tilde{V}^{i}-\operatorname{tr}[a^{i}D^{2}\tilde{V}^{i}]+b^{i}(t,x)\cdot D\tilde{V}^{i}(t,x)+c^{i}\tilde{V}^{i}+\tilde{d}^{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda^{i}_{j}\tilde{V}^{j}+\sum_{k,\ell\neq i}\mu^{i}_{k\ell}\tilde{V}^{k}\tilde{V}^{\ell},

where

\displaystyle\tilde{d}^{i}(t,x)

\displaystyle=d^{i}(t,x)+C\delta e^{Ct}-\beta\operatorname{tr}[a^{i}D^{2}\nu_{\beta}]+\beta b^{i}\cdot D\nu_{\beta}+\beta\nu_{\beta}(x)\left(c^{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda^{i}_{j}-\sum_{k,\ell\neq i}\mu^{i}_{k\ell}V^{k}\right)

\displaystyle+\delta e^{Ct}\left(c^{i}-\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda^{i}_{j}-\sum_{k,\ell\neq i}\mu^{i}_{k\ell}V^{k}\right).

From the boundedness of the coefficients and the
V^{i}
’s, and from the nonnegativity of
d^{i}
, it follows that there exists
\tilde{C}>0
depending only on the bounds of the coefficients and the
V^{i}
such that, as
\beta\to 0
,

\tilde{d}^{i}(t,x)\geq(C-\tilde{C})\delta e^{Ct}-o(1).

Taking
C>\tilde{C}
and letting
\beta
be sufficiently small, in relation to
\delta
, we then see that
\tilde{d}^{i}>0
. From the first step, we conclude that, if
\beta
is sufficiently small, then, for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
,

V^{i}(t,x)<\beta\nu_{\beta}(x)+\delta e^{Ct}.

Sending first
\beta\to 0
and then
\delta\to 0
yields the result.
∎

3
The regular Lagrangian flow

The object of this section is to study the forward-in-time flow

(3.1)

\partial_{t}\phi_{t,s}(x)=b(t,\phi_{t,s}(x))\quad\text{for }s\in[0,T],\quad t\in[s,T],\quad\phi_{s,s}(x)=x

for a vector field
b:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfying

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{split}&\text{for some }C_{0},C_{1}\in L^{1}_{+}([0,T]),\\
&|b(t,y)|\leq C_{0}(t)(1+|y|)\quad\text{for a.e. }(t,y)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\text{ and}\\
&x\mapsto b(t,x)+C_{1}(t)x\quad\text{is increasing for a.e. }t\in[0,T].\end{split}\right.

The second condition reads equivalently as
\partial_{x_{j}}b^{i}(t,\cdot)\geq-C_{1}(t)\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j=1,2,\ldots,d
. Moving forward, for convenience, we define the positive, increasing, absolutely continuous functions

(3.3)

\omega_{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C_{0}(s)ds,\quad\omega_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C_{1}(s)ds,\quad t\in[0,T].

Remark 3.1
.

Implicit in the assumption (
3.2
) is a choice of basis on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Indeed, the results of the paper continue to hold if
b
is replaced by
Ab(t,A^{T}x)
for a
d\times d
orthogonal matrix
A
.

The precise interpretation of the problem (
3.1
), wherein
b
is discontinuous, is made sense of as a differential inclusion. Namely, at every discontinuity
x
of
b(t,\cdot)
, we have
b_{*}(t,x)\lneq b^{*}(t,x)
, and so the natural formulation is

(3.4)

\partial_{t}\phi_{t,s}(x)\in[b_{*}(t,\cdot),b^{*}(t,\cdot)](\phi_{t,s}(x))\quad\text{for }s\in[0,T],\quad t\geq s,\quad\phi_{s,s}(x)=x.

Remark 3.2
.

In order to make notation less cumbersome, for a function
\phi:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we will always denote by
\phi_{*}
and
\phi^{*}
the lower and upper semicontinuous envelopes of
\phi
in the space variable only; that is, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\phi_{*}(t,x)=\phi(t,\cdot)_{*}(x)
and
\phi^{*}(t,x)=\phi(t,\cdot)^{*}(x)
.

If
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for
m>1
, then
\phi^{*}
and
\phi_{*}
are taken to be the coordinate-by-coordinate lower and upper-semicontinuous envelopes, so, for instance,
\phi_{*}=(\phi^{1}_{*},\phi^{2}_{*},\ldots,\phi^{d}_{*})
; equivalently,

\phi_{*}(x)=\sup_{r>0}\inf_{|x-y|\leq r}\phi(y)

with respect to the partial order (
2.1
) on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Remark 3.3
.

The differential inclusion (
3.4
), wherein
b(t,x)
is replaced with the smallest box
[\alpha,\beta]=\prod_{i=1}^{d}[\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]
containing all limit points of
b(t,y)
as
y\to x
, is a slightly weaker formulation than the standard Filippov regularization
[
30
]
, where boxes are replaced with general convex sets.

Before developing the general theory, it is useful to record the following a priori ODE bounds, which are an easy consequence of Grönwall’s lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume, for some
C_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}([0,T])
, that
b:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies

\esssup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{|b(t,x)|}{1+|x|}\leq C_{0}(t)\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
T>0
, such that

|X(t)-X(s)|\leq C(1+|x|)\int_{s}^{t}C_{0}(r)dr.

3.1
A comparison principle

The following comparison result for ODEs is at the heart of much of the analysis of this paper. It leads to the existence and uniqueness of the regular Lagrangian flow, as well as stable notions of solutions to the transport and continuity equations with velocity fields satisfying (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
B:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{split}&\left\{t\mapsto\left\|\nabla B(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\right\}\in L^{1}([0,T])\quad\text{for all }R>0,\text{ and}\\
&\partial_{x_{i}}B^{j}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }i\neq j.\end{split}\right.

Let
X,Y\in C^{0,1}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be such that, with respect to the partial order (
2.1
),
X(0)\leq Y(0)
and

(3.6)

\dot{X}(t)\leq B(t,X(t))\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{Y}(t)\geq B(t,Y(t))\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

Then
X(t)\leq Y(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

The continuity of
X
and
Y
implies that there exists
R>0
such that
|X(t,x)|\vee|Y(t,x)|\leq R
for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We may thus assume without loss of generality that
\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla B(t,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}dt<\infty
.

Define
\Delta=X-Y
and, for
i,j=1,2,\ldots,d
,

a_{ij}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x_{j}}B^{i}(t,\tau X(t)+(1-\tau)Y(t))d\tau.

Observe that
\int_{0}^{T}\left\|a_{ij}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}dt<\infty
for all
i,j
, and
a_{ij}\geq 0
whenever
i\neq j
. Then, for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
,

\Delta^{i}(0)\leq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{\Delta}^{i}(t)\leq a_{ii}(t)\Delta^{i}(t)+\sum_{i\neq j}a_{ij}(t)\Delta^{j}(t)\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

Fix
\delta>0
, set

\psi_{1}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\max_{i=1,2,\ldots,d}a_{ii}(s)ds\quad\text{and}\quad\psi_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\max_{i=1,2,\ldots,d}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}(s)ds,

and define

t_{0}:=\inf\left\{t\in(0,T]:\text{there exists }i\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}\text{ such that }e^{-\psi_{1}(t)}\Delta^{i}(t)-\delta e^{\psi_{2}(t)}>0\right\}.

We have
t_{0}>0
. Assume by contradiction that
t_{0}<T
, and let
i
be such that the maximum is attained. Then, for all
j
and
s\in[0,t_{0}]
,

(3.7)

e^{-\psi_{1}(s)}\Delta^{j}(s)\leq e^{-\psi_{2}(t_{0})}\Delta^{i}(t_{0})=\delta e^{\psi_{2}(t_{0})}.

We compute

\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\psi_{1}(t)}\Delta^{i}(t)\leq\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ji}(t)e^{-\psi_{1}(t)}\Delta^{j}(t),

and so

\displaystyle\delta e^{\psi_{2}(t_{0})}=e^{-\psi_{1}(t_{0})}\Delta^{i}(t_{0})\leq\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ji}(s)e^{-\psi_{1}(s)}\Delta^{j}(s)ds\leq\delta\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ji}(s)e^{\psi_{2}(s)}ds\leq\delta(e^{\psi_{2}(t_{0})}-1),

which is a contradiction. It follows that
\Delta^{i}(t)<\delta e^{\psi_{1}(t)+\psi_{2}(t)}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
i=1,2,\ldots,d
. Sending
\delta\to 0
yields the result.
∎

3.2
Maximal and minimal flows

The comparison principle from the previous subsection is now used to establish the existence of maximal and minimal (with respect to the order (
2.1
)) semicontinuous solutions of the differential inclusion (
3.4
) for vector fields satisfying
\partial_{x_{i}}b^{j}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
b:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies

\int_{0}^{T}\esssup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{|b(t,y)|}{1+|y|}dt<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{x_{i}}b^{j}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }i\neq j.

Then there exist solutions
\phi^{+}
and
\phi^{-}
of (
3.4
) that are absolutely continuous in time such that

(a)

For all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,
\phi^{+}_{t,s}
and
\phi^{-}_{t,s}
are increasing in the sense of (
2.1
),
\phi^{+}_{t,s}
is right-continuous, and
\phi^{-}_{t,s}
is left-continuous.

(b)

If
\phi
is any other solution of (
3.4
), then
\phi^{-}\leq\phi\leq\phi^{+}
.

(c)

If
0\leq r\leq s\leq t\leq T
, then
\phi^{+}_{s,t}\circ\phi^{+}_{r,s}=\phi^{+}_{r,t}
and
\phi^{-}_{s,t}\circ\phi^{-}_{r,s}=\phi^{-}_{r,t}

(d)

For all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,
(\phi^{+}_{t,s})_{*}\geq\phi^{-}_{t,s}
.

Remark 3.4
.

When
d=1
, the condition on the derivatives of
b
becomes vacuous, and we recover the existence of a unique minimal and maximal solution under the sole assumption that
b
is locally bounded.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
b^{\varepsilon}
and
b_{\varepsilon}
denote the coordinate-by-coordinate
\sup
- and
\inf
-convolutions as in (
2.2
). By Lemma
2.4
,
\nabla b^{\varepsilon}
and
\nabla b_{\varepsilon}
are bounded on
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and so, for all
(s,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exist unique solutions
\phi^{\pm,\varepsilon}
of

\partial_{t}\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)=b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)=b_{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\quad\text{in }[s,T],\quad\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{s,s}(x)=\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{s,s}(x)=x.

By Lemma
3.1
, for every
(s,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{\cdot,s}(x)
and
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{\cdot,s}
are bounded and continuous uniformly in
\varepsilon
.

The comparison result, Lemma
3.2
, immediately gives
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}\leq\phi^{+,\varepsilon}
, and, if
x\leq y
,
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)\leq\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(y)
and
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)\leq\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(y)
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<\delta
. Then Lemma
2.4
(
b
) implies that
b^{\varepsilon}\leq b^{\delta}
and
b_{\varepsilon}\geq b_{\delta}
. It follows that

\partial_{t}\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}\leq b^{\delta}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}),

and so Lemma
3.2
yields
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}\leq\phi^{+,\delta}
. A similar argument gives
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}\geq\phi^{-,\delta}
. It now follows that that there exist bounded and Lipschitz functions
\phi^{+}_{\cdot,s}(x)
and
\phi^{-}_{\cdot,s}(x)
on
[s,T]
, which are increasing in
x
, such that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)\searrow\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)\nearrow\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)\quad\text{uniformly for }t\in[s,T].

Fix
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. If
h\geq 0
, then, for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
,

\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)\leq\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x+h)\leq\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x+h).

Sending first
h\searrow 0
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
gives
\lim_{h\searrow 0}\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x+h)=\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)
, so that
\phi^{+}_{t,s}
is right-continuous. A similar argument shows that
\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)
is left-continuous, and this completes the proof of part (
a
).

We next prove part (
b
). If
\phi
is any other solution of (
3.4
), then, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

b_{\varepsilon}(t,\phi_{t,s}(x))\leq\partial_{t}\phi_{t,s}(x)\leq b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi_{t,s}(x))\quad\text{for }t\in[s,T]\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{s,s}(x)=x.

Appealing once more to Lemma
3.2
gives
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)\leq\phi_{t,s}(x)\leq\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)
, and sending
\varepsilon\to 0
gives the result.

We now prove the flow property stated in part (
c
). Suppose
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
0\leq r\leq s\leq t\leq T
. Then, for
\varepsilon>0
, because
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{s,t}
is increasing,

\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{s,t}\left(\phi^{+}_{r,s}(x)\right)\leq\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{s,t}\left(\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{r,s}(x)\right)=\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{r,t}(x).

Taking
\varepsilon\to 0
yields
\phi^{+}_{s,t}\left(\phi^{+}_{r,s}(x)\right)\leq\phi^{+}_{r,t}(x)
. For the opposite inequality, observe that
[s,T]\ni t\mapsto\phi^{+}_{r,t}(x)
solves (
3.4
) with value
\phi^{+}_{r,s}(x)
at time
t=s
. It follows from part (
b
) that
\phi^{+}_{r,t}(x)\leq\phi^{+}_{s,t}\left(\phi^{+}_{r,s}(x)\right)
. The argument for the flow property for
\phi^{-}
is analogous.

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Sending
y\nearrow x
in the inequality
\phi^{-}_{t,s}(y)\leq\phi^{+}_{t,s}(y)
, using the fact that
\phi^{-}
and
\phi^{+}
are increasing and
\phi^{-}
is left-continuous, yields
\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)\leq(\phi^{+}_{t,s})_{*}(x)
. This concludes the proof of part (
d
).
∎

3.3
Uniqueness and stability of the regular Lagrangian flow

We now use the full assumption (
3.2
), and in particular, the lower bounds on
\partial_{x_{i}}b^{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
that were not needed to prove the existence of the maximal and minimal solution of (
3.4
). In particular, we prove that
\phi^{+}
and
\phi^{-}
are equal almost everywhere, giving rise to a unique regular Lagrangian flow.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume that
b
satisfies (
3.2
). If
\phi^{+}
and
\phi^{-}
are the maximal and minimal flow from Proposition
3.1
, then, for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,
\left(\phi^{+}_{t,s}\right)_{*}=\phi^{-}_{t,s}
. Moreover, for all
(s,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a.e.
t\in[s,T]
,

\partial_{t}\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)=b^{*}(t,\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x))\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)=b_{*}(t,\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)).

Proof.

Let

\tilde{\phi}^{\pm}_{t,s}(x)=e^{\omega_{1}(t)}\phi^{\pm}_{t,s}\left(e^{-\omega_{1}(t)}x\right)

and

\tilde{b}(t,x)=e^{\omega_{1}(t)}b\left(t,e^{-\omega_{1}(t)}x\right)+C_{1}(t)x,

where
\omega_{1}
is as in (
3.3
). Then
\tilde{b}
satisfies (
3.2
) with
C_{1}=0
and with a possibly different
C_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}
, and
\tilde{\phi}^{\pm}
are the corresponding maximal and minimal flows. We may therefore assume without loss of generality that
b(t,\cdot)
is increasing for
t\in[0,T]
.

We first prove the statement involving the lower-semicontinuous envelope of
\phi^{+}_{t,s}
, and in view of Proposition
3.1
(
d
), we need only prove the opposite inequality
(\phi^{+}_{t,s})_{*}\leq\phi^{-}_{t,s}
. For
t\in[0,T]
, define

b^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)=b(t,\cdot)*\rho^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)=b(t,\cdot)*\rho_{\varepsilon},

where the one-sided mollifiers
\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}
are defined in (
2.3
), and let
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}
and
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}
be the corresponding flows. Arguing exactly as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, appealing to Lemma
2.5
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{\cdot,s}(x)\searrow\phi^{+}_{\cdot,s}(x)
and
\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{\cdot,s}(x)\nearrow\phi^{-}_{\cdot,s}(x)
uniformly on
[s,T]
, for any
s\in[0,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Fix
0<\varepsilon<\delta
and define
X^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t)=\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x-2\delta\mathbf{1})+2\delta\mathbf{1}
. Then, in view of Lemma
2.5
(
c
),
X^{\delta,\varepsilon}
satisfies

\dot{X}^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t)=b^{\varepsilon}(t,X^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t)-2\delta\mathbf{1})\leq b_{\varepsilon}(t,X^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t))\quad\text{in }[s,T]\quad\text{and}\quad X^{\delta,\varepsilon}(s)=x.

We may thus appeal to Lemma
3.2
, and find that, for all
t\in[s,T]
,

\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x-2\delta\mathbf{1})\leq\phi^{-,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)-2\delta\mathbf{1}.

Sending first
\varepsilon\to 0
and then
\delta\to 0
gives
(\phi^{+}_{t,s})_{*}\leq\phi^{-}_{t,s}
, as desired.

We now prove the final statement. For
0<\varepsilon<\delta
, we have the inequalities, for
t\in[s,T]
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

b^{*}(t,\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x))\leq b^{*}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\leq b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\leq b^{\delta}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)).

Since
b^{\delta}
is Lipschitz continuous in the space variable,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}b^{\delta}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))=b^{\delta}(t,\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)).

Therefore,

b^{*}(t,\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x))\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\leq b^{\delta}(t,\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)),

and so, sending
\delta\to 0
, it follows that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))=b^{*}(t,\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)).

For arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
,
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\phi^{\varepsilon,+}_{t,s}(x)=x+\int_{s}^{t}b^{\varepsilon}(r,\phi^{\varepsilon,+}_{r,s}(x))dr.

Sending
\varepsilon\to 0
and appealing to dominated convergence gives

\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)=x+\int_{s}^{t}b^{*}(r,\phi^{+}_{r,s}(x))dr,

and a similar argument gives

\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)=x+\int_{s}^{t}b_{*}(r,\phi^{-}_{r,s}(x))dr.

∎

Recalling that increasing functions are continuous almost-everywhere, it now follows from Proposition
3.2
that
\phi^{+}_{t,s}
and
\phi^{-}_{t,s}
are equal almost everywhere, given
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
. We thus finally arrive at the almost-everywhere unique solvability of the ODE (
3.1
), and the identification of the unique regular Lagrangian flow.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
s\in[0,T]
and for almost every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exists a unique absolutely continuous solution
[s,T]\ni t\mapsto\phi_{t,s}(x)
of the differential inclusion (
3.4
). Moreover,
\phi_{t,s}
satisfies the regular Lagrangian property: the map
C_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ni f\mapsto f\circ\phi_{t,s}
extends continuously to
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and there exists
C>0
depending only on
T
such that, for all
R>0
,
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
,
t\in[s,T]
,

(3.8)

\left\|f\circ\phi_{t,s}\right\|_{L^{1}(B_{R})}\leq e^{d(\omega_{1}(t)-\omega_{1}(s))}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}(B_{R+C(1+R)(\omega_{0}(t)-\omega_{0}(s))})}.

In particular,

\left\{[s,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ni(t,x)\mapsto b(t,\phi_{t,s}(x))\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},L^{1}([s,T]))\right\},

and for a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
[s,T]\ni t\mapsto\phi_{s,t}(x)
is the unique absolutely continuous solution of the integral equation

\phi_{t,s}(x)=x+\int_{s}^{t}b(r,\phi_{r,s}(x))dr.

Finally, if
0\leq r\leq s\leq t\leq T
, then the composition
\phi_{s,t}\circ\phi_{r,s}
is well-defined a.e. and is equal to
\phi_{r,t}
.

Remark 3.5
.

Taking
f=\mathbf{1}_{A}
in the estimate above, for some
A\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of finite measure, we find the regular Lagrange property

\left|\phi^{-1}_{t,s}(A)\right|\leq e^{d(\omega_{1}(t)-\omega_{1}(s))}|A|.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
s\in[0,T]
be fixed and, for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
t^{N}_{n}=s+\frac{n(T-s)}{N}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots,N
. Then, by Lemma
2.3
and Proposition
3.2
, there exist sets
B^{N}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of full measure such that
\phi^{+}_{t^{N}_{n},s}=\phi^{-}_{t^{N}_{n},s}
on
B^{N}_{n}
. Define now the full measure set

B=\bigcap_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\bigcap_{n=0}^{N}B^{N}_{n}.

Let
(t,x)\in[s,T]\times B
, and, for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
n(t)=0,1,2,\ldots,N
be such that
|t^{N}_{n}-t|
is minimized. Then, in view of the uniform continuity in time of
\phi^{+}
and
\phi^{-}
, for some
\omega_{N}>0
with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\omega_{N}=0
,

|\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)-\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)|\leq|\phi^{+}_{t^{N}_{n(t)},s}(x)-\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)|+|\phi^{-}_{t^{N}_{n(t)},s}(x)-\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)|\leq\omega_{N},

and, since
N
was arbitrary, we find that
\phi^{+}_{t,s}(x)=\phi^{-}_{t,s}(x)
. It follows that, up to a full measure set,
\phi^{+}_{\cdot,s}
and
\phi^{-}_{\cdot,s}
may be identified as absolutely continuous functions on
[s,T]
.

For
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap C_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, let
\varepsilon>0
and let
b^{\varepsilon}
and
\phi^{+,\varepsilon}
be as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Then

\partial_{t}\det(D_{x}\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))=\operatorname{div}b^{\varepsilon}(t,\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\det(D_{x}\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\geq-dC_{1}(t)\det(D_{x}\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)),

from which it follows that
\det(D_{x}\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))\geq\exp\left(-d\int_{s}^{t}C_{1}(r)dr\right)
. The change of variables formula then gives

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))dx\leq\exp\left(d\int_{s}^{t}C_{1}(r)dr\right)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)dx.

The set
(\phi^{+,\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x))_{x\in\operatorname{supp}f,\,\varepsilon>0}
is uniformly bounded in view of Lemma
3.1
, and so the bounded convergence theorem implies, upon taking
\varepsilon\to 0
, that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(\phi_{t,s}(x))dx\leq\exp\left(d\int_{s}^{t}C_{1}(r)dr\right)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)dx.

This implies that the map
f\mapsto f\circ\phi_{t,s}
extends continuously to
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The local statement follows from the finite speed of propagation implied by the a priori estimates in Lemma
3.1
.

It now follows easily that
b(\cdot,\phi_{\cdot,s})
belongs to
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and the uniqueness statement for absolutely continuous solutions of the integral equation follows from Proposition
3.2
and the fact that
\phi=\phi^{+}=\phi^{-}
a.e.

Finally, the composition
\phi_{t,s}\circ\phi_{r,s}
is justified because
\phi_{t,s}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, and its equality to
\phi_{r,t}
a.e. is a consequence of the a.e. uniqueness of the ODE and the flow properties in Proposition
3.1
(
c
).
∎

We now demonstrate that any regularizations of
b
lead to the flow
\phi
in the limit, not only the one-sided regularizations used above.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
(b^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is a family satisfying (
3.2
) uniformly in
\varepsilon
, and, for every
\varepsilon
,

\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla b^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}dt<\infty.

If
\phi^{\varepsilon}
is the unique flow corresponding to
b^{\varepsilon}
, then, for all
s\in[0,T]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}\to\phi
in
C([s,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
and in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},W^{1,1}([s,T]))
.

Proof.

The regular Lagrange property (
3.8
) in Theorem
3.1
implies that, for all
p\in[1,\infty)
and
R>0
,

\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\sup_{t\in[s,T]}\int_{B_{R}}|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{t,s}(x)|^{p}dx<\infty.

Moreover, for all
t\in[s,T]
,
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{t,s}
is increasing, and thus
BV
. This along with the a priori estimates in Lemma
3.1
imply that
(\phi^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,s})_{\varepsilon>0}
is precompact in
C([s,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, and therefore, for some subsequence

3.4
Some remarks on the backward flow

We discuss next the question of solvability of the backward flow: for fixed
s\in[0,T]
, this is the terminal value problem

(3.11)

\partial_{t}\phi_{t,s}(x)=b(t,\phi_{t,s}(x))\quad t\in[0,s],\quad\phi_{s,s}(x)=x.

Formally, the lower bound on

suggests that the backward flow should concentrate positive measure sets to null sets. As an example when

\phi_{t,s}(x)=x-(\operatorname{sgn}x)(s-t)\quad\text{for }x\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad t<s,

and so all trajectories eventually concentrate at

. This situation can be generalized to multiple dimensions when

satisfies the half-Lipschitz property

(3.12)

(b(t,x)-b(t,y))\cdot(x-y)\geq-C_{1}(t)|x-y|^{2},\quad C_{1}\in L^{1}_{+}([0,T]),

Example 3.1

For

Lemma 3.3

\left\{\begin{split}&\left\{t\mapsto\left\|\nabla B(t,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}\right\}\in L^{1}([0,T])\text{ and}\\

satisfies (

For

\gamma^{i}_{t}\leq B^{i}(t,X_{t})-B^{i}(t,Y_{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mu^{ij}_{t}(X^{j}_{t}-Y^{j}_{t})

Remark 4.1
.

The uniqueness of the semigroup is a consequence of the uniqueness of the flow established in the previous section. Note, however, that, solely under the assumption
\operatorname{div}b\geq-C_{1}
for some
C_{1}\in L^{1}_{+}([0,T])
, any weakly-limiting family of solution operators must lead to solutions in
C([0,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, in the strong topology.

More precisely, for
\varepsilon>0
, we have the easy a priori bound

\left\|S^{\varepsilon}(s,t)\overline{u}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}C_{1}(r)dr\right)\left\|\overline{u}\right\|_{L^{p}},\quad\overline{u}\in L^{p}.

It follows from a diagonal argument that there exists
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
and a family of continuous linear operators on
L^{p}
such that
S(r,s)S(s,t)=S(r,t)
for
r\leq s\leq t
, and, for all
t\in[0,T]
,

S^{\varepsilon_{n}}(\cdot,t)\overline{u}\rightharpoonup S(\cdot,t)\overline{u}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{\infty}([0,T],L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))\text{ for all }\overline{u}\in L^{p}.

In particular,
u:=S(\cdot,t)\overline{u}\in C([0,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{w}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, and so, for any
s\in[0,t]
,

\liminf_{h\to 0^{+}}\left\|u(s+h,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}}\geq\left\|u(s,\cdot)\right\|.

On the other hand,

\left\|u(s+h)\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\exp\left(\int_{s}^{s+h}C_{1}(r)dr\right)\left\|u(s)\right\|_{L^{p}},

so that

\limsup_{h\to 0^{+}}\left\|u(s+h,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq\left\|u(s,\cdot)\right\|.

We find then that
\left\|u(s+h,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}}\to\left\|u(s,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{p}}
, which, coupled with the weak convergence, means that
u(s+h,\cdot)\to u(s,\cdot)
strongly in
L^{p}
if
p>1
(and so for all
p
locally). A similar argument holds with
h
replaced by
-h
.

The following renormalization property for the solution operator
S(s,t)
,
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, is then immediate from the formula.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\beta:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is smooth and
|\beta(s)|\leq C(1+|s|^{r})
for some
r\geq 1
, then, for all
\overline{u}\in L^{r}_{\mathrm{loc}}
,
\beta(S(s,t)\overline{u})=S(s,t)(\beta\circ\overline{u})
.

4.1.2
Characterizing increasing/decreasing solutions

The solution of the transport equation in the previous subsection was characterized as the unique limit under arbitrary regularizations, as well as through the formula involving the regular Lagrangian flow. The remainder of the section is dedicated to understanding further ways to characterize the solution, and in particular on the level of the equation (
4.1
) itself. This will become useful in the study of nonlinear equations in the final section.

We first observe that, if
u_{T}
is increasing/decreasing with respect to the partial order (
2.1
), then so is
u(t,\cdot)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. While this is immediately clear from the formula
u(t,\cdot)=u_{T}\circ\phi_{T,t}
and the fact that
\phi_{T,t}
is increasing, it can also be seen directly from the equation. Indeed, if
u
is a smooth solution of (
4.1
) and
v_{i}=\partial_{x_{i}}u
,
i=1,2,\ldots,d
, then

-\partial_{t}v_{i}-b\cdot\nabla v_{i}-(\partial_{x_{i}}b^{i})v_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}(\partial_{x_{i}}b^{j})v_{j}.

Therefore, if
v_{i}\geq 0
(or
v_{i}\leq 0)
when
t=T
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,d
, then the same is true for
t<T
by the maximum principle Lemma
2.6
. The result for general
b
satisfying (
3.2
) follows from approximating
b
and using the limiting result in Theorem
4.1
. By linearity, we have thus established the following:

Proposition 4.2
.

For all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,
S(s,t):ABV\to ABV
.

In particular, since
ABV
is densely contained in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
S(s,t)
is continuous on
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, Proposition
4.2
implies that belonging to
ABV
is a suitable criterion for the propagation of compactness. Notice for instance that this provides another proof of the fact that the convergence statements in Theorem
4.1
are with respect to
strong
convergence in
C([0,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}})
for
p<\infty
.

We now demonstrate how the propagation of the increasing or decreasing property leads to a method for characterizing solutions of (
4.1
), independently of the solution formula. The idea is to regularize
u
in a one-sided manner, as in subsection
2.3
.

Formally, if
u
solves (
4.1
) and, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(recall that
\omega_{1}
is as in (
3.3
)),

\tilde{u}(t,x)=u\left(t,e^{\omega_{1}(T)-\omega_{1}(t)}x\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{b}(t,x)=e^{-(\omega_{1}(T)-\omega_{1}(t))}b\left(t,e^{\omega_{1}(T)-\omega_{1}(t)}x\right)+C_{1}(t)x,

then
\tilde{u}
solves (
4.1
) with
b
replaced by
\tilde{b}
, and
\tilde{b}
satisfies (
3.2
) with
C_{1}=0
and a possibly different
C_{0}
. We may therefore assume here, without loss of generality, that
b(t,\cdot)
is increasing for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
.

Recall the definition of the one-sided mollifiers
\rho_{\varepsilon}
and
\rho^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
2.3
).

Definition 4.1
.

A function
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called a super (sub)solution of (
4.1
) if, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
u(t,\cdot)
is decreasing, and, if
u_{\varepsilon}=u*\rho_{\varepsilon}
and
u^{\varepsilon}=u*\rho^{\varepsilon}
, then

(4.5)

-\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\geq 0\quad\left(\text{resp.}\quad-\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\leq 0\right)\quad\text{for a.e. }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

A solution is both a sub- and supersolution.

Remark 4.2
.

A well-posed notion of sub- and supersolutions can be defined where
u
is approximated using, instead of the one-sided mollifiers, the method of
\inf
- and
\sup
-convolution:

u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\inf_{y}\left\{u(x-y)+\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{y}\left\{u(x-y)-\frac{|y|}{\varepsilon}\right\}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume
b
satisfies (
3.2
) with
C_{1}=0
. Then, for all
R>0
, there exists a modulus of continuity
\omega_{R}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that, if
u,v:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are respectively a sub- and supersolution of (
4.1
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
, then, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
p\in[1,\infty)
,

(4.6)

\int_{B_{R}}\left(u(t,x)-v(t,x)\right)_{+}^{p}dx\leq\int_{B_{R+\omega_{R}(T-t)}}\left(u(T,x)-v(T,x)\right)_{+}^{p}dx.

In particular, for any decreasing
u_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exists a unique solution
u
of (
4.1
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
, which is given by
u(t,\cdot)=S(t,T)u_{T}
, and which is continuous a.e. in
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

Set
u^{\varepsilon}=u*\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
v_{\varepsilon}=v*\rho_{\varepsilon}
; then, combining the inequalities for
u^{\varepsilon}
and
v_{\varepsilon}
given by Definition
4.1
, we obtain

-\partial_{t}(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})\leq 0.

Let
\beta:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be smooth and increasing. Multiplying the above inequality by the positive term
\beta^{\prime}(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})
yields

-\partial_{t}\beta(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla\beta(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})\leq 0.

We may then take
\beta(r)=r_{+}^{p}
(arguing with an extra layer of regularizations if
p=1
) and find that, in the sense of distributions,

-\partial_{t}(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})_{+}^{p}-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon})_{+}^{p}\leq 0.

Let
\hat{\psi}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be smooth and decreasing, such that
\hat{\psi}=1
on
[0,1]
and
\hat{\psi}=0
on
[2,\infty)
, and set
\psi(t,x):=\hat{\psi}(|x|/R(t))
for some
R:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
to be determined. Using
\psi
as a test function, we discover

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-v_{\varepsilon}(t,x))_{+}^{p}\psi(t,x)dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle-\partial_{t}\psi(t,\cdot)-\operatorname{div}(b(t,\cdot)\psi(t,\cdot)),(u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)-v_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot))_{+}^{p}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-v_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\right)_{+}^{p}\left(-\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla\psi(t,x)\right)dx,

where in the last line we used the fact that
\operatorname{div}b\geq 0
and
\psi\geq 0
. Using the fact that
\hat{\psi}^{\prime}\leq 0
, with
\hat{\psi}^{\prime}\neq 0
only if
r\in[1,2]
, we find that

-\partial_{t}\psi-b\cdot\nabla\psi=-\hat{\psi}^{\prime}(x/R(t))\left(\frac{\dot{R}(t)}{R(t)^{2}}-\frac{b(t,x)\cdot x}{R(t)|x|^{2}}\right)\geq-R(t)^{-1}\hat{\psi}^{\prime}(x/R(t))\left(\dot{R}(t)-2C_{0}(t)(1+2R(t))\right).

For
t_{0}\in[0,T]
, this is made nonnegative on
[t_{0},T]
by choosing, for any fixed
R>0
,

R(t)=Re^{4[\omega_{0}(t)-\omega_{0}(t_{0})]}+\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{4[\omega_{0}(t)-\omega_{0}(t_{0})]}-1\right),

and so

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t_{0},x)-v_{\varepsilon}(t_{0},x))_{+}^{p}\hat{\psi}\left(\frac{|x|}{R(t_{0})}\right)dx\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(u^{\varepsilon}(T,x)-v_{\varepsilon}(T,x))_{+}^{p}\hat{\psi}\left(\frac{|x|}{R(T)}\right)dx.

We may then choose functions
\hat{\psi}
that approximate
\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}
from above, and then, by the monotone convergence theorem,

\int_{B_{R}}(u^{\varepsilon}(t_{0},x)-v_{\varepsilon}(t_{0},x))_{+}^{p}dx\leq\int_{B_{R(T)}}(u^{\varepsilon}(T,x)-v_{\varepsilon}(T,x))_{+}^{p}dx.

The proof of (
4.6
) is finished upon sending
\varepsilon\to 0
and setting

\omega_{R}(r):=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t\leq s+r\leq T}\left\{\left(R+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(e^{4[\omega_{0}(t)-\omega_{0}(s)]}-1\right)\right\}.

The comparison inequality (
4.6
) implies uniqueness for a solution with terminal condition
u_{T}
, and so it remains to show that
S(t,T)u_{T}
is a solution in the sense of Definition
4.1
.

Let
(b^{\delta})_{\delta>0}
be a family of smooth approximations of
b
satisfying (
3.2
) with
C_{1}=0
and uniform
C_{0}
, and let
u^{\delta}_{T}=u_{T}*\gamma_{\delta}
for a family of standard mollifiers
(\gamma_{\delta})_{\delta>0}
. Note then that, for
\delta>0
,
u^{\delta}_{T}
is decreasing, and, as
\delta\to 0
,
u^{\delta}_{T}\to u_{T}
in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
for all
p<\infty
. Let
u^{\delta}
be the corresponding solution of the terminal value problem (
4.1
). It follows that
u^{\delta}(t,\cdot)
is decreasing for all
t\in[0,T]
.

For any
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
,

-\partial_{t}(u^{\delta}*\rho^{\varepsilon})-b^{\delta}(t,x)\cdot\nabla(u^{\delta}*\rho^{\varepsilon})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(b^{\delta}(t,y)-b^{\delta}(t,x)\right)\cdot\nabla u^{\delta}(t,y)\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-y)dy\leq 0,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-y)\neq 0
only if
x\leq y
,
b^{\delta}(t,\cdot)
is increasing, and
\nabla u^{\delta}\leq 0
. Sending
\delta\to 0
, it follows from Theorem
4.1
that, in the sense of distributions, if
u=S(\cdot,T)u_{T}
and
u^{\varepsilon}=u*\rho^{\varepsilon}
,

-\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-b\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\leq 0.

It follows that
S(\cdot,T)u_{T}
is a subsolution. A similar argument shows that it is a supersolution, and therefore the unique solution in the sense of Definition
4.1
.

Now, for some
M>0
, define
\psi(t)=\exp(M\omega_{1}(t))
and

\tilde{u}^{\delta}(t,x):=u^{\delta}(t,x+\psi(t)\mathbf{1}).

For any fixed
R>0
, there exists
M>0
such that, in view of the linear growth of
b^{\delta}
given by (
3.2
) uniformly in
\delta
, and the fact that
u^{\delta}
is decreasing in the spatial variable, for any

,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\tilde{u}^{\delta}(t,x)=(-\nabla u^{\delta})\cdot\left(b^{\delta}(t,x+\psi(t)\mathbf{1})-\psi^{\prime}(t)\mathbf{1}\right)\leq C_{1}(t)(-\nabla u^{\delta})\cdot\left((1+|x|+\psi(t))-M\psi(t)\right)\leq 0.

Since
M
is independent of
\delta
, we may send
\delta\to 0
and conclude that
\tilde{u}(t,x):=u(t,x+\psi(t))
is increasing on
[0,T]\times[-R,R]^{d}
, and therefore continuous a.e. in that set by Lemma
2.3
. The transformation leading from
u
to
\tilde{u}
preserves null sets, and we conclude that

is continuous almost everywhere.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The idea behind Definition
4.1
is to establish a sign for the commutator between convolution and differentiation along irregular vector fields, as compared to the work of DiPerna and the first author
[
28
]
, where the commutator is shown to be small for Sobolev vector fields. We must take convolution kernels with a specific one-sided structure in order to analyze the commutators; a less crude example of this idea is seen in the work of Ambrosio
[
2
]
for general
BV
velocity fields.

A different notion of sub and supersolutions, which also selects
S(\cdot,T)u_{T}
as the unique solution of (
4.1
), can be obtained by instead regularizing
b
. Recall that we have assumed without loss of generality that
b
is increasing. Then a decreasing function
u
can be said to be a sub (super)solution if, for all
\varepsilon>0
, in the sense of distributions,

-\partial_{t}u-b_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla u\leq 0\quad\left(\text{resp. }-\partial_{t}u-b^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla u\geq 0\right),

where
b_{\varepsilon}\leq b\leq b^{\varepsilon}
are one-sided regularizations of
b
, for example the one-sided mollifiers or the
\inf
- and
\sup
-convolutions. The notion of sub and supersolution in Definition
4.1
turns out to be more amenable to the study of the nonlinear systems in Section
5
.

Remark 4.4
.

Throughout this section, we have studied the setting where
u_{T}
, and therefore
u(t,\cdot)
for
t<T
, are decreasing. The same analysis can be achieved for increasing solutions, in which case the inequalities in (
4.12
) are reversed. Note, in particular, that the solution flows
\phi_{t,s}(x)
are vector-valued solutions in this sense; i.e.

-\partial_{s}\phi_{t,s}(x)-b(s,x)\cdot\nabla\phi_{t,s}(x)=0,\quad\phi_{t,t}(x)=x.

We now observe that the full family of solution operators
S(s,t):L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}\to L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
,
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, can be constructed independently of the ODE flows
\phi_{s,t}
, which can then be recovered with the theory of renormalization. Indeed, Definition
4.1
, and its counterpart for increasing solutions, can be used to define
S(s,t)\bar{u}
for any
\bar{u}\in ABV
. The density of
ABV
in
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and the
L^{p}
-continuity of
S(s,t)
, then allow the solution operators to be continuously extended to
L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
. It is, however, not clear whether solutions of (
4.1
) can be characterized for arbitrary
u_{T}\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, other than by the formula (
4.3
) or as limits of solutions to regularized equations.

4.1.3
Lower order terms

We briefly explain how to extend the above results to equations with additional lower order terms, as in

(4.7)

-\partial_{t}u-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla u-c(t,x)u-d(t,x)=0\quad\text{in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad u(T,\cdot)=u_{T},

for functions

.

Assume

(a)

(b)

Let
(b^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
,
(c^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
, and

) and (

, such that, as

(b^{\varepsilon},c^{\varepsilon},d^{\varepsilon})\to(b,c,d)

, and let

be the corresponding solution of (

solves

Proof.

For

,

,

The convergence statements, and thus the formula in part (

are stable only under weak convergence in

.

To complement (

), we also consider the terminal value problem for the continuity equation:

\partial_{t}g+\operatorname{div}(b(t,x)g)=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad g(T,\cdot)=g_{T}.

The formula in this case should be

g(t,x)=g_{T}(\phi_{T,t}(x))\det(D_{x}\phi_{T,t}(x)).

In fact, both terms in the product have meaning:

is the solution of (

) with terminal value

, and

is well-defined almost-everywhere by Lemma

and the fact that

is increasing. Furthermore, by regularizing

is a measure bounded from below. However,

is not continuous in general, and so it is not possible to make sense of the product in (

above.

4.4
Second-order equations

We finish this section by briefly demonstrating how the first-order results can be extended to the second-order equations

-\partial_{t}u-b(t,x)\cdot\nabla u-\operatorname{tr}[a(t,x)\nabla^{2}u]=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad u(T,\cdot)=u_{T}

and

\partial_{t}f+\operatorname{div}(b(t,x)f)-\nabla^{2}\cdot(a(t,x)f)=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad f(0,\cdot)=f_{0},

Theorem 4.7
.

For all

,

(c)

(4.26)

where

Proof.

The argument follows almost exactly as in the first order case (Theorem

Remark 4.8

5
Nonlinear transport systems

We now turn to the study of the nonlinear transport systems discussed in the introduction, that is

(5.1)

\partial_{t}u+f(t,x,u)\cdot\nabla_{x}u+g(t,x,u)=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad u(T,\cdot)=u_{T},

where, for some integer
m\geq 1
,
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
f:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
g:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. We also consider the associated forward-backward system of characteristics posed for fixed
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
s\in[t,T]
by

(5.2)

\begin{cases}-\partial_{s}U_{s,t}(x)=g(s,X_{s,t}(x),U_{s,t}(x))&U_{T,t}(x)=u_{T}(X_{T,t}(x)),\\
\partial_{s}X_{s,t}(x)=f(s,X_{s,t}(x),U_{s,t}(x))&X_{t,t}(x)=x.\end{cases}

5.1
Weak solutions

We will introduce assumptions on
f
,
g
, and
u_{T}
, that, freezing
u
, make the equation (
5.1
) exactly of the form of those nonconservative linear equations studied in the previous section. This then leads to a natural notion of weak solution via a fixed point operator.

Assume

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{split}&(f,g):[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\quad f(t,x,\cdot),g(t,x,\cdot)\text{ are continuous for }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\\
&\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{(x,u)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left(\frac{|f(t,x,u)|}{1+|x|}+\frac{|g(t,x,u)|}{1+|u|}\right)dt<\infty,\\
&\text{and, for some }C_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}([0,T]),\text{ a.e. }t\in[0,T],\text{ and all }i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}\text{ and }k,\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\},\\
&\partial_{x_{i}}f^{j}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\geq-C_{0}(t)\delta_{ij},\quad\partial_{u_{k}}g^{\ell}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\geq-C_{0}(t)\delta_{k\ell},\\
&\partial_{x_{i}}g^{\ell}\leq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{u_{k}}f^{j}\leq 0.\end{split}\right.

Under these assumptions, any solution operator for (
5.1
) should preserve the decreasing property of solutions, which we show formally assuming the data are smooth.

Proposition 5.1
.

Assume
f
,
g
, and
u_{T}
are smooth with uniformly bounded derivatives,
f
and
g
satisfy (
5.3
), and
u_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is decreasing with respect to (
2.1
). If
u
is a smooth solution of the terminal value problem (
5.1
), then, for all
t\in[0,T]
,
u(t,\cdot)
is decreasing.

Proof.

Let
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
and
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
be fixed, and define
v_{ik}:=\partial_{k}u^{i}
. Taking the derivative in
x_{k}
of the
i
-component of (
5.1
) gives the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{ik}+f(t,x,u)\cdot\nabla v_{ik}+\left(\partial_{x_{k}}f^{k}(t,x,u)+\partial_{u_{i}}g^{i}(t,x,u)\right)v_{ik}+\partial_{x_{k}}g^{i}(t,x,u)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j\neq k}\partial_{x_{k}}f^{j}(t,x,u)v_{ij}-\sum_{\ell\neq i}\partial_{u_{\ell}}g^{i}(t,x,u)v_{\ell k}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\partial_{u_{\ell}}f^{j}(t,x,u)v_{\ell k}v_{ij}.

In view of (
5.3
), the system satisfied by
\left\{v_{ik}:i=1,2,\ldots,m,\;k=1,2,\ldots,d\right\}
, after reversing time, is of the form in (
2.6
), and so the result follows from Lemma
2.6
.
∎

We now make the connection with the linear transport equation theory of the previous sections. In particular, note that if
u(t,\cdot)
is decreasing for all
t\in[0,T]
, then
b(t,x):=f(t,x,u(t,x))
satisfies the assumptions of (
3.2
), while
c(t)=C_{0}(t)
and
d(t,x)=g(t,x,u(t,x))+C_{0}(t)u(t,x)
satisfy (
4.11
).

Definition 5.1
.

Assume
f
and
g
satisfy (
5.3
) and
u_{T}
is decreasing. Then a locally bounded function
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
that is decreasing in the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-variable and satisfies

(5.4)

\left\{(t,x)\mapsto\frac{u(t,x)}{1+|x|}\right\}\in L^{\infty}

is called a solution of (
5.1
) if
u
is a solution of the linear equation (
4.7
) with
b(t,x)=f(t,x,u(t,x))
,
c(t)=C_{0}(t)
, and
d(t,x)=g(t,x,u(t,x))+C_{0}(t)u(t,x)
.

Solving (
5.1
) in the sense of Definition
5.1
thus amounts to solving a fixed point problem. We similarly give a weak sense to the system (
5.2
) by using the properties of the flow from Section
3
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that
u_{T}\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}
and
u
is a solution of (
5.1
) in the sense of Definition
5.1
. Let
\phi
be the forward regular Lagrangian flow as in Section
3
corresponding to

\partial_{t}\phi_{t,s}(x)=f(t,\phi_{t,s}(x),u(t,\phi_{t,s}(x)))\quad t\in[s,T],\quad\phi_{s,s}(x)=x,

and define, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

X_{t,s}(x)=\phi_{t,s}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad U_{t,s}(x):=u(t,\phi_{t,s}(x)).

Then, for all
1\leq p<\infty
,
X_{\cdot,s},U_{\cdot,s}\in C([s,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}})\cap L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},W^{1,1}([0,T]))
, and, for a.e.
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, (
5.2
) is satisfied in the integral sense.

Proof.

The regularity properties of
X
are seen from Theorem
3.1
, as well as the fact that, for a.e.

,

X_{t,s}(x)=x+\int_{s}^{t}f(r,X_{r,s}(x),u(r,X_{r,s}(x))dr=x+\int_{s}^{t}f(r,X_{r,s}(x),U_{r,s}(x))dr.

Theorems
3.1
and
4.3
together imply that
U_{\cdot,s}\in C([s,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}})
. Also, in view of Theorem
4.3
(
c
),
u
satisfies, for any

,

U_{s,s}(x)=u(s,x)=u(t,X_{t,s}(x))+\int_{s}^{t}g(r,X_{r,s}(x),u(r,\phi_{r,s}(x)))dr=U_{t,s}(x)+\int_{s}^{t}g(r,X_{r,s}(x),U_{r,s}(x))dr.

It follows that, in the distributional sense,

\partial_{t}U_{t,s}(x)=g(t,X_{t,s}(x),U_{t,x}(x)).

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem

∎

5.2
Minimal and maximal solutions

In this section, we show that the assumptions (
5.3
) give an increasing structure to the equation (
5.1
), which allows for the identification of a unique minimal and maximal solution.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume
f
and
g
satisfy (
5.3
),
u_{T}
is decreasing, and
u_{t}(1+|\cdot|)^{-1}\in L^{\infty}
. Then there exist two decreasing solutions
u^{+}
,
u^{-}
of (
5.1
) in the sense of Definition
5.1
with the following properties:

(a)

For all
t\in[0,T]
,
u^{+}(t,\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous,
u^{-}(t,\cdot)
is lower semicontinuous, and both
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
are continuous a.e. in
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

(b)

If
u
is any other solution, then
u^{-}\leq u\leq u^{+}
.

Throughout this subsection, we may assume, without loss of generality, that the term
C_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}
in (
5.3
) is
0
. Indeed, the function

\tilde{u}(t,x)=\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)u\left(t,\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)x\right),

is decreasing in
x
, satisfies (
5.4
), and, formally, solves the equation (
5.1
) with
f
and
g
replaced by

\tilde{f}(t,x,u)=\exp\left(-\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)f\left(t,\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)x,\exp\left(-\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)u\right)+C_{0}(t)x

and

\tilde{g}(t,x,u)=\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)g\left(t,\exp\left(\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)x,\exp\left(-\int_{t}^{T}C_{0}(s)ds\right)u\right)+C_{0}(t)u.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

As described above, we assume without loss of generality that
C_{0}\equiv 0
. For some
\overline{C}>0
to be determined, set

(5.5)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}:=\Big\{u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:\;&u(T,\cdot)=u_{T},\;u(t,\cdot)\text{ is decreasing for all }t\in[0,T],\text{ and}\\
&|u(t,x)|\leq\overline{C}(1+|x|)\text{ for all }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\Big\}.\end{split}

We define a map
\mathcal{S}
on
\mathcal{L}
as follows: for
u\in\mathcal{L}
, let
v:=\mathcal{S}(u)
be the solution, as in Theorem
4.3
, of the linear transport equation

\partial_{t}v+f(t,x,u(t,x))\cdot\nabla v+g(t,x,u(t,x))=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\quad v(T,\cdot)=u_{T}.

Then, in view of the solution formula (
4.10
) and the bounds (
5.3
) on
f
and
g
, there exists a sufficient large
\overline{C}
, depending on
u_{T}
, such that
\mathcal{S}
maps
\mathcal{L}
into
\mathcal{L}
.

We now note that
\mathcal{L}
forms a
complete lattice
under the partial order

(5.6)

u\leq\tilde{u}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad u^{i}(t,x)\leq\tilde{u}^{i}(t,x)\quad\text{for all }(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,i=1,2,\ldots,m;

that is, every subset of
\mathcal{L}
has a greatest lower bound and least upper bound, which is a consequence of the uniformly bounded linear growth of solutions in
\mathcal{L}
.

Suppose now that
u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{L}
satisfy
u\leq\tilde{u}
under the order (
5.6
), and set

b(t,x):=f(t,x,u(t,x))\quad\text{and}\quad d(t,x):=g(t,x,u(t,x)).

Then (
5.3
) with
C_{0}\equiv 0
implies that
b(t,x)\geq f(t,x,\tilde{u}(t,x))
and
d(t,x)\leq g(t,x,\tilde{u}(t,x))
, and so, in particular,
v:=\mathcal{S}(u)
and
\tilde{v}:=\mathcal{S}(\tilde{u})
are respectively a sub and supersolution of the linear equation (
4.7
) with
c(t)\equiv 0
. It follows from Theorem
4.4
that
v\leq\tilde{v}
, and therefore
\mathcal{S}
is increasing on the complete lattice
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the partial order (
5.6
). The existence of a unique maximal and minimal solution are now a consequence of the Tarski lattice-theoretical fixed point theorem
[
54
]
.

The continuity a.e. in
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
now follows from Theorem
4.4
. Observe now that any version of the maximal solution
u^{+}
is a solution in the sense of Definition
5.1
. Because
u^{+}(t,\cdot)
is decreasing, it is continuous a.e., and its maximal version is upper semicontinuous, which is therefore also the unique maximal solution in the
everywhere
-pointwise sense. A similar argument shows that the minimal everywhere-pointwise solution
u^{-}
is lower-semicontinuous in the spatial variable, and we conclude.
∎

The fixed point theorem of
[
54
]
used above further characterizes
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
as

u^{+}=\sup\left\{u\in\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{S}(u)\geq u\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad u^{-}=\inf\left\{u\in\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{S}(u)\leq u\right\},

where the
\sup
and
\inf
are understood with respect to the partial order (
5.6
). We alternatively characterize the maximal and minimal solutions in terms of appropriately defined sub and supersolutions of the equation (
5.1
).

Recall that
\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}
are the one-sided mollifying functions from subsection
2.3
. We then define a notion of sub and supersolution.

Definition 5.2
.

Assume
f
and
g
satisfy (
5.3
) with
C_{0}\equiv 0
and
u_{T}
is decreasing. A function
u:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
that is decreasing in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called a subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (
5.1
) if
u(T,\cdot)\leq u_{T}
(resp.
u(T,\cdot)\geq u_{T}
) and
u^{\varepsilon}:=u*\rho^{\varepsilon}
(resp.
u_{\varepsilon}:=u*\rho_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-f(t,x,u(t,x))\cdot\nabla u^{\varepsilon}-g(t,x,u(t,x))\leq 0

\displaystyle\left(\text{resp. }-\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}-f(t,x,u(t,x))\cdot\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-g(t,x,u(t,x))\geq 0\right)\quad\text{a.e. in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

In other words, sub and supersolutions in the sense of (
5.2
) are sub and supersolutions of the corresponding linear equations identified in Definition
5.1
. It follows that a function which is both a sub and supersolution is in fact a solution.

Lemma 5.1
.

Under the conditions of Definition
5.2
, assume that
u
and
v
are two subsolutions (resp. supersolutions) of (
5.1
). Then
u\vee v
(resp.
u\wedge v
) is also a subsolution (resp. supersolution).

Proof.

We prove only the subsolution statement, as the other one is analogously proved. It is clear that
\max\{u(T,\cdot),v(T,\cdot)\}\leq u_{T}
and
u\vee v
is decreasing in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Set
u^{\varepsilon}=u*\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
v^{\varepsilon}=v*\rho^{\varepsilon}
. Let
\Psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be smooth, increasing, and satisfy
\Psi(u,v)\geq u\vee v
for all
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Note that, using (
5.3
) with
C_{0}\equiv 0
,

\displaystyle f(t,x,u(t,x))\wedge f(t,x,v(t,x))\geq f(t,x,u(t,x)\vee v(t,x))\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle g(t,x,u(t,x))\vee g(t,x,v(t,x))\leq g(t,x,u(t,x)\vee v(t,x)),

and so a simple application of the chain rule gives

\partial_{t}\Psi(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})+f(t,x,u\vee v)\cdot\nabla\Psi(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})+g(t,x,u\vee v)\geq 0.

In particular, for any smooth positive test function
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\Psi(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})(t,x)\partial_{t}\phi(t,x)-g(t,x,u\vee v)\phi(t,x)\right)dxdt+\langle\nabla(f(\cdot,\cdot,u\vee v)\phi),\Psi(u^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})\rangle\leq 0,

where the last term is understand as the pairing between locally finite measures and continuous functions, because
f(t,x,(u\vee v)(t,x))
is
BV
in
x
. We may then approximate
\max(\cdot,\cdot)
with such functions
\Psi
and determine that, in the distributional sense,

\partial_{t}(u^{\varepsilon}\vee v^{\varepsilon})+f(t,x,u\vee v)\cdot\nabla(u^{\varepsilon}\vee v^{\varepsilon})+g(t,x,u\vee v)\geq 0.

For
\delta>0
, we convolve both sides of the above inequality with the one-sided mollifier

5.4
A one-dimensional example

For the rest of the paper, we assume
d=m=1
and consider the example of (
5.1
) with
f(t,x,u)=-u
and
g(t,x,u)=0
:

(5.19)

-\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u=0\quad\text{in }(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}},\quad u(T,\cdot)=u_{T}.

Observe that (
5.19
) can be formally written in conservative form, where it becomes the Burgers equation with flux
\frac{u^{2}}{2}
. When
d>1
, it is not necessarily the case that (
5.1
) can be written as a conservation law, and the product of
f(t,x,u)
with
\nabla u
cannot be understood by integrating by parts.

If the decreasing function
u_{T}
is Lipschitz continuous, then the system of characteristics (
5.2
) can be solved in some maximal time interval
[T-\tau,T]
depending on the Lipschitz constant for
u_{T}
. This gives rise to a Lipschitz continuous solution of (
5.19
), which can easily be checked to be a viscosity solution in the sense of Definition
5.3
and is therefore unique. However, for
t<\tau
, the system of characteristics fails to be solvable on
[t,T]
, due to the formation of shocks. This is in contrast to the case where
u_{T}
is Lipschitz and
increasing
, in which case (
5.19
) has a Lipschitz continuous solution on
(-\infty,T]
. We therefore see that the situation where a continuous solution exists is not typical on an arbitrary time horizon, even if the function
u_{T}
is smooth.

We therefore study in this subsection a simple example of a decreasing and discontinuous terminal data, namely

(5.20)

u_{T}(x)=\mathbf{1}\{x\leq 0\}.

Viewed as a scalar conservation law with flux
\frac{u^{2}}{2}
, (
5.19
) is a Riemann problem whose solvability is resolved with the theory of entropy solutions. Indeed, the unique entropy solution is given by

(5.21)

u(t,x)=\mathbf{1}\left\{x\leq\frac{T-t}{2}\right\},

describing a shock wave moving with constant speed
\frac{1}{2}
(in reverse time), the constant
\frac{1}{2}
being uniquely determined from the Rankine-Hugoniot condition.

5.4.1
Nonuniqueness of discontinuous solutions

When viewed as a nonlinear transport equation, there is a strong failure of uniqueness for this problem.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
c:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfy
c(T)=0
and
-c^{\prime}\in[0,1]
a.e. Then

(5.22)

u_{c}(t,x):=\mathbf{1}\left\{x\leq c(t)\right\}

is a solution, in the sense of Definition
5.1
, of (
5.19
) with terminal data (
5.20
).

Proof.

Define
u_{c}^{\varepsilon}=u_{c}*\rho^{\varepsilon}
. Then

u_{c}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{-\infty}^{c(t)}\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-y)dy,

and so

\partial_{t}u_{c}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-c(t))c^{\prime}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{x}u_{c}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=-\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-c(t)).

Thus,

-\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{c}(t,x)+u_{c}(t,x)\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}_{c}(t,x)=\rho^{\varepsilon}(x-c(t))\left[-c^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{1}\{x\leq c(t)\}\right].

Recall that
\operatorname{supp}\rho^{\varepsilon}\in[-2\varepsilon,0]
, and so the above expression is nonzero only if
x\leq c(t)
. In that case, because
-c^{\prime}\leq 1
,

-c^{\prime}(t)-\mathbf{1}\{x\leq c(t)\}=-c^{\prime}(t)-1\leq 0,

and we conclude that
u^{\varepsilon}_{c}
is subsolution. The proof of the supersolution property is similar, and uses the fact that
-c^{\prime}\geq 0
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Note that
u_{c}
is continuous a.e. in
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and, therefore, by Lemma
5.3
, is also a viscosity solution in the sense of Definition
5.3

The system of characteristics (
5.2
) becomes, for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

(5.23)

\begin{cases}-\partial_{s}U_{s,t}(x)=0,&U_{T,t}(x)=\mathbf{1}\left\{X_{T,t}(x)\leq 0\right\},\\
\partial_{s}X_{s,t}(x)=-U_{s,t}(x),&X_{t,t}(x)=x.\end{cases}

Recall that the system, and, in particular, the equation for
X
, is viewed as a differential inclusion, where, for all
s\in[t,T]
,

U_{s,t}(x)=U_{T,t}(x)=\begin{cases}\{1\}&\text{if }X_{T,t}(x)<0,\\
\{0\}&\text{if }X_{T,t}(x)>0,\text{ and}\\
[0,1]&\text{if }X_{T,t}(x)=0.\end{cases}

By Proposition
5.2
, each solution
u_{c}
corresponds to a solution
(X^{c},U^{c})
of (
5.23
), which we can compute explicitly: namely, for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,

U^{c}_{s,t}(x)=u_{c}(t,x)=\mathbf{1}\left\{x\leq c(t)\right\}

and

(5.24)

X^{c}_{s,t}(x)=\begin{cases}x-s+t,&x\leq c(t),\\
x-c(t)\frac{s-t}{T-t},&x=c(t),\\
x,&x\geq c(t).\end{cases}

We note that, for any solution
(U,X)
, we must have
X_{s,t}(x)=x-s+t
for
x<0
and
X_{s,t}(x)=x
for
x\geq T-t
. However, for
x\in[0,T-t]
, there is ambiguity in the speed at which the
X_{s,t}(x)
travels: it can move with speed
-1
,
0
, or anything in between, where in the latter case the characteristic is constrained to end at
X(T,t,x)=0
. The precise value
x\in[0,1]
for which
X_{T,t}(x)=0
thus encodes the choice of the shock-wave speed
c(t)
in the definition of
u_{c}
.

5.4.2
Stochastic selection

An important feature in the theory of entropy solutions of scalar conservation laws is the stability under regularizations, and in particular under vanishing viscosity limits. In the above context, the entropy solution (
5.21
) of (
5.19
) arises as the strong limit in
C([0,T],L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, of the unique smooth solution
u^{\varepsilon}
of

(5.25)

-\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon},\quad u^{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot)=u^{\varepsilon}_{T},

where

(5.26)

u^{\varepsilon}_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ is smooth and }\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u^{\varepsilon}_{T}=\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}\text{ in }L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}.

By contrast, we show here that any solution
u_{c}
can arise as a limit from suitably regularized equations.

Theorem 5.4
.

Fix any
c\in W^{2,1}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
satisfying
c(T)=0
and
-c^{\prime}\in(0,1)
. Then there exists
\theta_{c}\in L^{1}([0,T])
such that, if
u^{\varepsilon}_{T}
is as in (
5.26
) and
u^{\varepsilon}
is the unique classical solution of

(5.27)

-\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\left(\partial_{x}^{2}u^{\varepsilon}+\theta_{c}(t)|\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right),\quad u^{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot)=u^{\varepsilon}_{T},

then, for all
1\leq p<\infty
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
u^{\varepsilon}\to u_{c}
strongly in
C([0,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}})
.

Proof.

For
\theta:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
to be determined, define, for
t\in[0,T]
,

f(t,v)=\begin{cases}\frac{\log(\theta(t)v+1)}{\theta(t)}&\text{if }\theta(t)\neq 0\text{ and }\theta(t)v>-1,\\
v&\text{if }\theta(t)=0\end{cases}

and note that

f(t,\cdot)^{-1}(u)=\frac{e^{\theta(t)u}-1}{\theta(t)}.

For
\varepsilon>0
, let
v^{\varepsilon}
be the classical solution of

(5.28)

-\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}+f(t,v^{\varepsilon})v^{\varepsilon}_{x}=\frac{\partial_{t}f(t,v^{\varepsilon})}{\partial_{v}f(t,v^{\varepsilon})}+\varepsilon\partial_{x}^{2}v^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{and}\quad v^{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot)=f(T,\cdot)^{-1}(u^{\varepsilon}_{T})).

By the maximum principle, it is easily checked that the values of
v^{\varepsilon}
fall within the domain of
f
and its derivatives, and the solution of (
5.27
) is given exactly by
u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=f(t,v^{\varepsilon}(t,x))
.

Standard stability results
[
37
]
yield that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
v^{\varepsilon}
converges strongly in
C([0,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}})
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
to the unique entropy solution
v
of

(5.29)

-\partial_{t}v+f(t,v)\partial_{x}v=\frac{\partial_{t}f(t,v)}{\partial_{v}f(t,v)}\quad\text{in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{and}\quad v(T,\cdot)=f^{-1}(T,\cdot)(1)\mathbf{1}_{x<0}+f^{-1}(T,\cdot)(0)\mathbf{1}_{x>0}.

We then set

F(t,v)=\frac{(\theta(t)v+1)\log(\theta(t)v+1)-\theta(t)v}{\theta(t)^{2}},

which satisfies
\partial_{v}F(t,v)=f(t,v)
, so that the equation (
5.30
) is equivalently written as

(5.30)

-\partial_{t}v+\partial_{x}F(t,v)=\frac{\partial_{t}f(t,v)}{\partial_{v}f(t,v)}\quad\text{in }[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{and}\quad v(T,\cdot)=f^{-1}(T,\cdot)(1)\mathbf{1}_{x<0}+f^{-1}(T,\cdot)(0)\mathbf{1}_{x>0}.

The unique entropy solution
v
of the conservative equation (
5.30
) is then given by

v(t,x)=\begin{cases}v^{-}(t),&x<c(t),\\
v^{+}(t),&x>c(t),\end{cases}

where, for
0\leq t\leq T
,
v_{t}^{+}
and
v_{t}^{-}
are defined by

-\partial_{t}v^{\pm}_{t}=\frac{\partial_{t}f(t,v_{t}^{\pm})}{\partial_{v}f(t,v_{t}^{\pm})}\quad t\in[0,T],\quad v^{-}_{T}=f^{-1}(T,\cdot)(0),\quad v^{+}_{T}=f^{-1}(T,\cdot)(1),

and
c(t)
is determined by the Rankine-Hugoniot condition

c(T)=0\quad\text{and}\quad-c^{\prime}(t)=\frac{F(t,v^{-}(t))-F(t,v^{+}(t))}{v^{+}(t)-v^{-}(t)}.

Observe that
t\mapsto f(t,v^{\pm}(t))
is constant, and therefore

-c^{\prime}(t)=\frac{F(t,f(t,\cdot)^{-1}(1))-F(t,f(t,\cdot)^{-1}(0))}{f(t,\cdot)^{-1}(1)-f(t,\cdot^{-1}(0)}C(\theta(t)),

where, for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

C(\theta)=\frac{\theta e^{\theta}-e^{\theta}-1}{\theta(e^{\theta}-1)}.

We observe that
C^{\prime}>0
,
\lim_{\theta\to-\infty}C(\theta)=0
, and
\lim_{\theta\to+\infty}C(\theta)=1
. Thus, letting
c:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
-c^{\prime}\in(0,1)
be as in the statement of the theorem, we finally choose
\theta_{c}(t):=C^{-1}(-c^{\prime}(t))
, so that
u^{\varepsilon}
converges strongly in
C([0,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}})
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, to
f^{-1}(t,\cdot)(v(t,x))=u_{c}(t,x)
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The restrictions that
c^{\prime\prime}\in L^{1}
and
c^{\prime}
lie strictly within
(-1,0)
are put in place to ensure that
\theta_{c}\in W^{1,1}
and
-\infty<\theta_{c}<\infty
, so that the equation for
v^{\varepsilon}
is well-posed. Achieving speeds
c
that are only Lipschitz, and where
c^{\prime}
is allowed to be either
0
or
1
, is possible by letting
\theta_{c}=\theta_{c}^{\varepsilon}
depend suitably on
\varepsilon
.

The selection principle in Theorem
5.4
can be recast in terms of the nonunique generalized characteristics problem (
5.23
). Namely, we fix a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\mathbb{P})
with a complete, right-continuous filtration
\mathbb{F}
and a Wiener process
W:[0,T]\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
progressively measurable with respect to
\mathbb{F}
, and, for
\varepsilon>0
and
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
, we consider the forward-backward SDE (FBSDE) on the interval
s\in[t,T]
:

(5.31)

\begin{cases}-d_{s}U^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)=Z^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)dW_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\theta_{c}(s)Z^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)^{2}ds,&U^{\varepsilon}_{T,t}(x)=u^{\varepsilon}_{T}(X^{\varepsilon}_{T,t}(x)),\\
d_{s}X^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)=-U^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)ds+\sqrt{2\varepsilon}dW_{s},&X^{\varepsilon}_{t,t}(x)=x.\end{cases}

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
c
and
\theta_{c}
be as in Theorem
5.4
. For every
\varepsilon>0
and
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
, there exists a unique strong solution
(X^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,t}(x),U^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,t}(x),Z^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,t}(x))
to the FBSDE (
5.31
). Moreover, with probability one, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
X^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,t}\to X^{c}_{\cdot,t}
in
C([t,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}))
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
, where
X^{c}
is as in (
5.24
).

Proof.

The existence of a unique solution is a consequence of the nondegeneracy of the noise; see
[
49
]
. Moreover, if
u^{\varepsilon}
solves (
5.27
), then

U^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)=u^{\varepsilon}(s,X^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x))\quad\text{and}\quad Z^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)=\sqrt{2\varepsilon}(\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon})(s,X^{\varepsilon}_{s,t}(x)).

It follows that
X^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,t}
is the solution of the SDE (
3.10
) with
b(t,x)=-u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
, and so, by Theorems
3.3
and
5.4
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
with probability one,
X^{\varepsilon}_{\cdot,t}
converges in
C([t,T],L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}))
to the regular Lagrangian flow corresponding to the drift
-u_{c}
. This is exactly
X^{c}
, and we conclude.
∎
