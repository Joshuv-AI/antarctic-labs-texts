Continuous Spiking Graph ODE Networks
Shiqi Fan, Zeqing Zhang, Nan Yin, Tong Li, Hongyi Nie, Die Hu, Wen Hua

Abstract
Spiking Graph Networks (SGNs) represent a biologically inspired paradigm that combines the energy-efficient properties of Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) with the relational modeling capabilities of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Despite their potential, existing SGNs are largely confined to static settings with real-valued or binary inputs, lacking the capacity to model continuous and dynamic graph evolution. Moreover, the discrete nature of spike-based representations introduces significant information loss, limiting predictive performance. In this work, we identify a critical gap in the literature: the absence of a unified, theoretically grounded framework for energy-efficient dynamic graph learning that leverages spiking dynamics. To this end, we propose Continuous Spiking Graph ODE Networks (CSGO), a novel framework that embeds SGNs within the continuous-time dynamics of Graph Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Graph ODEs). CSGO bridges the temporal spike-latency in SNNs with the continuous evolution of graph structures via ODEs, enabling both dynamic modeling and low power consumption. To address the intrinsic information loss in spiking representations, we further derive a second-order spiking mechanism and formulate the second-order CSGO, enhancing representational capacity. We provide theoretical analysis demonstrating the gradient stability of our framework, and validate its effectiveness through extensive experiments on benchmark datasets. CSGO achieves state-of-the-art performance while significantly reducing energy consumption, offering a principled and scalable solution for neuromorphic dynamic graph learning.

Introduction

Illustration of information loss and architecture inapplicability. Information loss arises from the sampling of real-value inputs, while the architecture inapplicability refers to the unsuitability of dynamic real-valued SGNs.

Spiking Graph Networks (SGNs)
[
30
,
26
]
combine the structural modelling capability of GNNs with the event-driven, low-power computation of Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), and have proved effective in object recognition, real-time data analysis, and static graph classification
[
13
,
30
,
26
]
.

However, existing SGNs are designed for
static
graphs and rely on Bernoulli sampling of real-valued inputs into binary spikes. Re-sampling at every step on dynamic inputs is expensive, undermining the SNN energy advantage, and the binarization itself discards fine-grained temporal and semantic information
[
27
]
.
As illustrated in Figure
1
, this leads to two obstacles: (i)
architectural inapplicability
to continuously evolving real-valued inputs, and (ii)
semantic information loss
from discrete spike encoding.

Graph Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Graph ODEs) offer a continuous-time tool for dynamic graph representation, while high-order neural mechanisms
[
14
]
can recover information lost in low-order encodings. Combining the two is, however, non-trivial: SGNs evolve over spike latency whereas Graph ODEs evolve over physical time; spike encoding loses semantic detail; and continuous-depth architectures are prone to vanishing or exploding gradients.

We propose Continuous Spiking Graph ODE Networks (
CSGO
) to address all three issues. We first formulate SGNs as a component of continuous-time ODEs and obtain a first-order variant
CSGO
-1st, then design a second-order spike encoding mechanism and embed it into a second-order Graph ODE, yielding
CSGO
-2nd. We further show that gradients remain bounded throughout training. Extensive experiments on node- and graph-classification benchmarks confirm that
CSGO
delivers state-of-the-art accuracy with substantially lower energy cost.

In summary, our contributions are:

•

We identify the limitations of existing SGNs on dynamic graph tasks and define a new setting requiring both continuous temporal modelling and low-power spike-based computation.

•

We propose CSGO, a unified SGN–Graph ODE framework, with theoretical guarantees that it avoids vanishing and exploding gradients.

•

Comprehensive experiments show that CSGO consistently outperforms state-of-the-art GNN, SNN, and Graph ODE baselines on both accuracy and energy.

[

,

typically require all nodes to be present at every timestamp
[

]
, which is impractical in real-world settings.
Graph ODEs avoid this by treating evolution as a continuous process and tolerate missing observations
[

; recent variants
[

]
couple GNN-encoded states with neural ODEs over both nodes and edges, while high-order extensions
[
14
]
better capture long-range temporal dependencies.
We embed energy-efficient SNNs into the Graph ODE framework, retaining the low-power advantage of spiking computation while leveraging the dynamic modelling capacity of ODEs.

Spiking Graph Networks.

Spiking Graph Networks (SGNs)
[
30
,
26
]
reduce the energy cost of graph learning by replacing real-valued activations with reactive spiking neurons, but are typically restricted to static graphs with real-valued or binary inputs.
Existing attempts to apply static SNNs to dynamic graphs
[
13
,
28
]
re-encode the spike representation at every step, which negates the energy benefit of SNNs.
Our framework instead unifies SGNs with Graph ODEs, capturing dynamic evolution while preserving low-power operation.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Problem Definition

We study the problem of energy-efficient dynamic graph classification. Given a temporal graph sequence
\mathcal{G}=\{G^{1},G^{2},\dots,G^{T}\}
, where each graph snapshot
G^{t}=(V^{t},E^{t})
contains a node feature matrix
\mathbf{X}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V^{t}|\times d}
, the goal is to predict a target label
\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
by learning a function
f_{\theta}
such that
\hat{y}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{G})
. To achieve this under neuromorphic energy constraints, we encode each real-valued feature
\mathbf{X}^{t}_{i,j}
into a binary spike train
\mathbf{S}^{t}_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
across N latency steps using stochastic Bernoulli sampling:

\mathbf{S}^{t}_{i,j,\tau}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(\mathbf{X}^{t}_{i,j}),\quad\tau=1,\dots,N
.
This yields a spiking input tensor
\mathbf{S}^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{|V^{t}|\times d\times N}
that reflects the temporal firing activity of each node. These spike trains are then used as input to a spiking graph encoder, which integrates structural message passing over
G^{t}
with temporal spike dynamics to compute node embeddings
\mathbf{H}^{t}
. A temporal evolution model—e.g., based on neural ODEs—then fuses the sequence
\{\mathbf{H}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{H}^{T}\}
into a unified representation
\mathbf{z}
, from which a classifier predicts the final output:
\hat{y}=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})
. This pipeline presents key challenges in accurately capturing graph-temporal dependencies while preserving energy efficiency and representation stability, which we address through our proposed continuous spiking graph ODE framework.

3.2
Continuous Graph Neural Networks

Let
G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with feature matrix
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
and adjacency
\mathbf{A}
.
The first ODE-based CGNN
[
24
]
extends the simple GNN
\mathbf{H}_{n+1}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{H}_{n}+\mathbf{H}_{0}
to continuous time, giving

\tfrac{d\mathbf{H}(t)}{dt}=\ln\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{H}(t)+\mathbf{E},

(1)

where
\mathbf{E}=\varepsilon(\mathbf{X})
is an encoder output.
GraphCON
[
21
]
introduces a second-order Graph ODE
\mathbf{X}^{\prime\prime}=\sigma(\mathbf{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X},t))-\gamma\mathbf{X}-\alpha\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
with a learnable coupling function
\mathbf{F}_{\theta}
, and solves it with the IMEX (implicit-explicit) symplectic-Euler scheme:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}^{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Y}^{n-1}+\Delta t[\sigma(\mathbf{F}_{\theta}(\mathbf{X}^{n-1},t^{n-1}))-\gamma\mathbf{X}^{n-1}-\alpha\mathbf{Y}^{n-1}],

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}^{n-1}+\Delta t\,\mathbf{Y}^{n},\quad n=1,\cdots,T,

with fixed time-step
\Delta t>0
and hidden features
\mathbf{Y}^{n},\mathbf{X}^{n}
at
t^{n}=n\Delta t
.

3.3
Spiking Neural Networks

First-order SNNs.

SNNs convert real-valued inputs into binary spikes over time; each neuron accumulates input spikes into a membrane potential and fires when the threshold
V_{th}
is exceeded:

u_{\tau+1,i}=\lambda(u_{\tau,i}-V_{th}s_{\tau,i})+\textstyle\sum_{j}w_{ij}s_{\tau,j}+b,\qquad s_{\tau+1,i}=\mathbb{H}(u_{\tau+1,i}-V_{th}),

(3)

where
\mathbb{H}(\cdot)
is the non-differentiable Heaviside function,

s_{\tau,i}\in\{0,1\}
is the binary spike, and
\lambda
is a leak constant.

Second-order SNNs.

First-order models assume an instantaneous current response, ignoring the gradual neurotransmitter dynamics observed in real synapses.
The synaptic-conductance-based LIF model
[
5
]
introduces a second-order recurrence with separate synaptic and membrane time constants:

I_{\tau+1}=\alpha I_{\tau}+WX_{\tau+1},\quad u_{\tau+1,i}=\beta u_{\tau,i}+I_{\tau+1,i}-V_{th}s_{\tau,i},\quad s_{\tau+1,i}=\mathbb{H}(u_{\tau+1,i}-V_{th}),

(4)

where

\alpha=\exp(-\Delta\tau/\tau_{syn})
and

\beta=\exp(-\Delta\tau/\tau_{mem})

control the synaptic-current and membrane-potential decay, respectively.

4
Methodology

In this part, we present the proposed CSGO for modeling the continuous spiking graph neural networks.
CSGO combines SNNs with CGNNs in a framework of PDE, which preserves the advantage of SNNs and CGNNs simultaneously. To mitigate the problem of information loss attributed to SNNs, we involve the derivation of second-order spike representation and differentiation for second-order SNNs, and then introduce the high-order PDE, referred to as CSGO-2nd. Finally, we present a theoretical proof to ensure that our proposed CSGO effectively mitigates the challenges associated with gradient exploding and vanishing. The framework of CSGO is shown in Figure
2
.

4.1
First-order CSGO

Specifically, SNNs propagate information within the time latency
\tau
, and the CGNNs evaluate the feature evolution along with time
t
. We propose the first-order CSGO to incorporate SNNs with CGNNs in the form of PDE with their respective timelines. Intuitively, information is interactively propagated in dual time dimensions by both the SNNs and the CGNNs, which is formulated as:

Proposition 1

Define the first-order SNNs as

\frac{du_{t}^{\tau}}{d\tau}=g(u_{t}^{\tau},\tau)
,
and first-order CGNNs as

\frac{du_{t}^{\tau}}{dt}=f(u_{t}^{\tau},t)
.
Let

A_{y}=\int_{0}^{N}g(u_{y}^{x},x)\,dx
.
Then the first-order CSGO can be formulated as

u_{T}^{N}=2\int_{0}^{T-1}f(A_{y})\,dy+\int_{T-1}^{T}f(A_{y})\,dy.

(5)

where
N
is the total latency of SNNs,
T
is the number of CGNN time steps,
and
u_{y}^{x}
denotes the neuron membrane potential at latency

x\in[0,N]
and time step
y\in[0,T]
.
Details of the derivation are provided in the Appendix.

Figure 2
:
Overview of the proposed CSGO. The proposed CSGO takes a graph with node features as input, which are initially encoded using the SNNs (first-order or second-order). Subsequently, a first-order or second-order continuous graph process is employed to evolve the representation over different time step. Finally, the representation is projected for downstream tasks.

To provide a comprehensive understanding of Proposition
1
, we approach it from two distinct perspectives. In Eq. (
5
), within the temporal dimension where
0\leq t\leq T
, the SNNs initially compute spike representations at each time step
t
. These representations are then employed in the evolution of the CGNNs process. Then, the CGNNs model the evolution of node embeddings along a time series
t
and subsequently integrates them across the latency dimension.

In our implementation of the first-order CSGO, we employ Eq. (
5
) by initially calculating spike representations at each time step, followed by modeling the evolution of node embeddings. As described in
[
16
]
, the first-order spike representation at time step
t
is denoted as:
\mathbf{H}(t)=\frac{\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\lambda^{N-\tau}s_{\tau}^{t}}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\lambda^{N-\tau}}
. By combining Eq. (
1
), we have:

\frac{d\mathbf{H}(t)}{dt}=ln\mathbf{AH}(t)+\frac{\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\lambda^{N-\tau}s_{\tau}^{0}}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{N}\lambda^{N-\tau}},

(6)

where
s_{\tau}^{t}
is the binary spike on latency
\tau
and time step
t
, and
\lambda=exp(-\frac{\Delta\tau}{\kappa})
with
\Delta\tau\ll\kappa
,
\kappa
is the time constant.

4.2
Second-order Spiking Neural Networks

The proposed first-order CSGO effectively addresses the challenge of combining SNNs with CGNNs to achieve energy-efficient modeling for continuous graph learning.
However, the first-order SNNs typically suffer from the information loss issue. Motivated by recent advancements in high-order structure modeling
[
14
]
, which addresses high-order correlations of nodes, we introduce the second-order CSGO to tackle the aforementioned issue. In this part, we begin by deriving the second-order spike representation and investigating the backpropagation of second-order SNNs.

Second-order SNNs Forward Propagation.

We set the forward propagation layer of SNNs to
L
. According to Eq.
4
, the spiking dynamics of layer
i
can be written as

u^{i}(n)=\beta^{i}u^{i}(n-1)+(1-\beta^{i})\frac{V_{th}^{i-1}}{\Delta\tau}(\alpha^{i}I^{i-1}(n-1)+\mathbf{W}^{i}s^{i-1}(n))-V^{i}_{th}s^{i}(n)
,
where
i=1,\cdots,L
,
n=1,\cdots,N
(latency steps),

\alpha^{i}=\exp(-\Delta\tau/\tau_{syn}^{i})
,
\beta^{i}=\exp(-\Delta\tau/\tau_{mem}^{i})
and
0<\Delta\tau\ll\{\tau_{syn}^{i},\tau_{mem}^{i}\}
.

Second-order Spike Representation.

Following the first-order spike representation framework
[
16
]
, we
define the weighted average input current as
\hat{I}(N)=\frac{1}{(\beta-\alpha)^{2}}\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta^{N-n}-\alpha^{N-n})I_{in}(n)}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta^{N-n}-\alpha^{N-n})}
and the scaled weighted firing rate as
\hat{a}(N)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\frac{V_{th}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\beta^{N-n}s(n)}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta^{N-n}-\alpha^{N-n})\Delta\tau}
.
Unrolling Eq. (
4
) and applying the limits
\Delta\tau\to 0
,
\hat{a}(N)\in[0,V_{th}/\Delta\tau]
together with the small-
V_{th}
assumption (full derivation in Appendix), we obtain the closed-form spike representation:

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\hat{a}(N)\approx\mathrm{clamp}\left(\tfrac{\tau_{syn}\tau_{mem}}{\tau_{mem}-\tau_{syn}}\hat{I}(N),0,\tfrac{V_{th}}{\Delta\tau}\right),

(7)

where
\mathrm{clamp}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(x,b))
.
This yields Proposition
2
, which extends the first-order result of
[
16
]
to second-order SNNs.

Proposition 2

For
i=1,\ldots,L
, define

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{0}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta_{i})^{N-n-2}s^{0}(n)}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left((\beta_{i})^{N-n}-(\alpha_{i})^{N-n}\right)\Delta\tau},

(8)

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{i}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{th}^{i}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta_{i})^{N-n-2}s^{i}(n)}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left((\beta_{i})^{N-n}-(\alpha_{i})^{N-n}\right)\Delta\tau}.

where

\alpha_{i}=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta\tau}{\tau_{syn}^{i}}\right),\qquad\beta_{i}=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta\tau}{\tau_{mem}^{i}}\right).

(9)

The differentiable mapping is

\mathbf{z}^{i}=\operatorname{clamp}\left(c_{i}\mathbf{W}^{i}\mathbf{z}^{i-1},0,\frac{V_{th}^{i}}{\Delta\tau}\right),\qquad c_{i}=\frac{\tau_{syn}^{i}\tau_{mem}^{i}}{\tau_{mem}^{i}-\tau_{syn}^{i}}.

(10)

If
\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{a}^{i}(N)=\mathbf{z}^{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,L-1
, then

\hat{a}^{i+1}(N)\approx\mathbf{z}^{i+1}
as
N\to\infty
.

Differentiation on Second-order Spike Representation.

We define the spike representation operator
r(s)=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\frac{V_{th}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\beta^{N-n}s(n)}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta^{N-n}-\alpha^{N-n})\Delta\tau}
and obtain the layer output
\mathbf{o}^{i}=r(s^{i})\approx\mathrm{clamp}(\mathbf{W}^{i}r(s^{i-1}),0,V_{th}^{i}/\Delta\tau)
. The gradient with respect to
\mathbf{W}^{i}
then follows from the chain rule:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{i}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{o}^{i+1}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{o}^{i+1}}{\partial\mathbf{o}^{i}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{o}^{i}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{i}},

(11)

which provides a tractable backpropagation rule for second-order SNNs.

4.3
Second-order CSGO

Having obtained the second-order spike representation for SNNs, we introduce the second-order CSGO. While obtaining an analytical solution for the second-order CSGO may not be feasible, we can derive a conclusion similar to Proposition
1
. The specifics are presented as follows.

Proposition 3

Define the second-order SNNs as

\frac{d^{2}u_{t}^{\tau}}{d\tau^{2}}+\delta\frac{du_{t}^{\tau}}{d\tau}=g(u_{t}^{\tau},\tau)
,
and second-order CGNNs as

\frac{d^{2}u_{t}^{\tau}}{dt^{2}}+\gamma\frac{du_{t}^{\tau}}{dt}=f(u_{t}^{\tau},t)
.
Then the second-order CSGO follows:

\displaystyle u_{t}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}h\left(\int_{0}^{N}e(u_{t}^{\tau})\,d\tau\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{N}e\left(\int_{0}^{T}h(u_{t}^{\tau})\,dt\right)d\tau,

where

e(u_{t}^{\tau})=\frac{\partial u_{t}^{\tau}}{\partial\tau}
and

h(u_{t}^{\tau})=\frac{\partial u_{t}^{\tau}}{\partial t}
satisfy

\frac{\partial e(u_{t}^{\tau})}{\partial\tau}+\delta e(u_{t}^{\tau})=g(u_{t}^{\tau}),\qquad\frac{\partial h(u_{t}^{\tau})}{\partial t}+\gamma h(u_{t}^{\tau})=f(u_{t}^{\tau}).

Here,
\delta
and
\gamma
are the hyperparameters of the second-order
SNNs and CGNNs.

The details are derived in Appendix.
Similarly to the first-order CSGO, we implement the second-order CSGO by calculating the spike representation on each time step and then model the node embedding with Eq. (
2
).
Furthermore, to optimize the second-order CSGO, we analyze the differentiation of the second-order CSGO.
Denote the loss function as
\mathcal{L}=\sum\limits_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\left|\mathbf{X}_{i}^{T}-\mathbf{\bar{X}}_{i}\right|^{2}
, and
\mathbf{\bar{X}}_{i}
is the label of node
i
. With the chain rule, we have:
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{l}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{o}^{N}_{T}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{o}^{N}_{T}}{\partial\mathbf{o}^{l}_{T}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{o}^{l}_{T}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{l}}
. From
[
21
]
, we have the conclusion that the traditional GNN model has the problem of gradient exponentially or vanishing, thus, we study the upper bound of the proposed CSGO.

Proposition 4

Let
\mathbf{X}^{n}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{n}
be the node features generated by
Eq. (
2
), and let
\Delta t\ll 1
.
The gradient of the second-order CGNN parameter
\mathbf{W}_{l}
is bounded
as in Eq. (
12
), while the gradient of the second-order SNN
parameter
\mathbf{W}^{k}
is bounded as in Eq. (
13
):

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{l}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta^{\prime}\hat{D}\Delta t(1+\Gamma T\Delta t)}{v}\max_{1\leq i\leq v}\left(|\mathbf{X}_{i}^{0}|+|\mathbf{Y}_{i}^{0}|\right)

(12)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta^{\prime}\hat{D}\Delta t(1+\Gamma T\Delta t)}{v}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq v}|\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{i}|+\beta\sqrt{T\Delta t}\right)^{2},

\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{k}}\right|\leq\frac{(1+T\Gamma\Delta t)(1+N\Theta\Delta\tau)V_{th}}{v\beta^{2}\Delta\tau}A.

(13)

where

\beta=\max_{x}|\sigma(x)|
,

\beta^{\prime}=\max_{x}|\sigma^{\prime}(x)|
, and

\hat{D}=\max_{i,j\in\mathcal{V}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{i}d_{j}}}
.
Moreover,

\Gamma:=6+4\beta^{\prime}\hat{D}\max_{1\leq n\leq T}\|\mathbf{W}^{n}\|_{1}

and

\Theta:=6+4\beta^{\prime}\hat{D}\max_{1\leq n\leq N}\|\mathbf{W}^{n}\|_{1}
.
Here,
d_{i}
is the degree of node
i
,

\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{i}
is the label of node
i
, and

A=\max_{1\leq i\leq v}|\mathbf{X}_{i}^{T}|+\max_{1\leq i\leq v}|\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{i}|.

Eq. (
12
) follows directly from
[
21
]
,
and the derivation of Eq. (
13
) is provided in the Appendix.

The upper bound in Proposition
4
demonstrates that the total gradient remains globally bounded, regardless of the number of CGNNs layers
T
and SNNs layers
N
, as long as
\Delta t\sim T^{-1}
and
\Delta\tau\sim N^{-1}
. This effectively addresses the issues of exploding and vanishing gradients.

6
Ablation Study

We further isolate the contribution of each high-order component by introducing two variants: CSGO-1st-2nd (first-order SNN + second-order Graph ODE) and CSGO-2nd-1st (second-order SNN + first-order Graph ODE), and report the full numbers in Appendix. Averaged across the six datasets, CSGO-2nd attains the best score (83.1), followed by CSGO-2nd-1st (80.5), CSGO-1st (80.1), and CSGO-1st-2nd (80.2). Two observations follow: (i) replacing the second-order SNN with a first-order one (CSGO-1st-2nd) is consistently the worst variant, confirming that mitigating spike-induced information loss is the dominant factor; (ii) CSGO-2nd-1st outperforms CSGO-1st and CSGO-1st-2nd, which suggests that high-order spike representation is more impactful than high-order Graph ODE for this task.

7
Conclusion

We presented
CSGO
, a unified framework that couples Spiking Neural Networks with Graph ODEs for energy-efficient continuous-time graph learning. By bridging spike-latency dynamics with continuous graph evolution,
CSGO
enables expressive yet low-power representation learning, and we further introduced a second-order spiking variant that mitigates the information loss inherent in first-order SNNs. Theoretical analysis shows that gradients remain bounded throughout training, and experiments on homophilic, heterophilic, and graph-classification benchmarks confirm that
CSGO
matches or surpasses state-of-the-art accuracy while substantially reducing energy consumption. Future work will extend the framework to asynchronous event-based and multi-agent dynamic graphs.

Proof of Proposition 1.

The coupled first-order SNN and Graph ODE updates satisfy

u_{t}^{\tau+1}=u_{t}^{\tau}+\int_{\tau}^{\tau+1}g(u_{t}^{x},x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad u_{t+1}^{\tau+1}=u_{t}^{\tau+1}+\int_{t}^{t+1}f(u_{y}^{\tau+1},y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Recursively expanding both dynamics and using the zero initial states gives

u_{T}^{N}=2\int_{0}^{T-1}f(A_{y})\,\mathrm{d}y+\int_{T-1}^{T}f(A_{y})\,\mathrm{d}y,

which proves Proposition 1.

Proof of Proposition 2.

Unrolling the second-order SNN dynamics over the latency steps and
rearranging the synaptic convolution gives

u(N)=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\beta^{N-n}-\alpha^{N-n})I_{in}(n)}{\beta-\alpha}-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\beta^{N-n-1}V_{th}s(n).

Using the spike-rate estimator with

\alpha=e^{-\Delta\tau/\tau_{syn}}
and

\beta=e^{-\Delta\tau/\tau_{mem}}
, we obtain

\hat{a}(N)\approx\operatorname{clamp}\left(\frac{\tau_{syn}\tau_{mem}}{\tau_{mem}-\tau_{syn}}\hat{I}(N),0,\frac{V_{th}}{\Delta\tau}\right).

Following
[
16
]
, the residual membrane term is neglected.
Setting
\hat{I}(N)=\mathbf{Wz}
yields Proposition 2, with

\hat{a}^{i+1}(N)\approx\mathbf{z}^{i+1}
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof of Proposition 3.

By the definitions of
e(u_{t}^{\tau})
and
h(u_{t}^{\tau})
,

\frac{\partial u_{t}^{\tau}}{\partial\tau}=e(u_{t}^{\tau}),\qquad\frac{\partial u_{t}^{\tau}}{\partial t}=h(u_{t}^{\tau}).

Integrating along both dimensions yields

u_{t}^{\tau}=\int_{0}^{T}h\left(\int_{0}^{N}e(u_{t}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{N}e\left(\int_{0}^{T}h(u_{t}^{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\mathrm{d}\tau,

which proves Proposition 3.

Proof of Proposition 4.

The Graph ODE gradient bound follows from
[
21
]
. For the
SNN parameters, the chain rule gives

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{k}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{Z}_{L}^{N}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{Z}_{L}^{N}}{\partial\mathbf{Z}_{l}^{N}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{Z}_{l}^{N}}{\partial\mathbf{Z}_{l}^{k}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{Z}_{l}^{k}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{k}}.

Using the corresponding Graph ODE and SNN bounds, we obtain

\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{W}^{k}}\right|\leq\frac{(1+T\Gamma\Delta t)(1+N\Theta\Delta\tau)V_{th}}{v\beta^{2}\Delta\tau}A,

which gives the second bound in Proposition 4.
