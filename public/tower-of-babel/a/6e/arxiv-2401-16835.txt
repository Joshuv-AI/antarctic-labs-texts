Efficient numerical approximations for a non-conservative Nonlinear Schrödinger equation appearing in wind-forced ocean waves
Agissilaos Athanassoulis, Theodoros Katsaounis, Irene Kyza

Abstract
. We consider a non-conservative nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NCNLS) with time-dependent coefficients, inspired by a water waves problem. This problem does not have mass or energy conservation, but instead mass and energy change in time under explicit balance laws. In this paper we extend to the particular NCNLS two numerical schemes which are known to conserve energy and mass in the discrete level for the cubic NLS. Both schemes are second oder accurate in time, and we prove that their extensions satisfy discrete versions of the mass and energy balance laws for the NCNLS. The first scheme is a relaxation scheme that is linearly implicit. The other scheme is a modified Delfour-Fortin-Payre scheme and it is fully implicit. Numerical results show that both schemes capture robustly the correct values of mass and energy, even in strongly non-conservative problems. We finally compare the two numerical schemes and discuss their performance. DEDICATION: This work is dedicated to the memory of our beloved professor, colleague and friend Vassilios Dougalis.

Introduction

Nonlinear Schrödinger equations (NLS) are used in a wide range of applications as approximate or first-principles models
[
34
]
, including water waves
[
31
,
32
]
and in particular the appearance of extreme water waves, often called rogue waves
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
33
,
38
]
. In that context, Monte Carlo simulation of stochastic sea states is often carried out
[
33
,
36
,
32
]
. More recently, it has been pointed out that richer models may be crucial in understanding rogue waves, including the presence of vorticity
[
35
]
, wavesystems crossing at an angle
[
36
,
37
]
, and extreme events during the growth phase of a sea state
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. This creates a need for accurate and efficient simulation for a range of nonstandard NLS-type equations.

In this work we consider a non-conservative nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NCNLS) which arises as an envelope equation of water waves growing under wind forcing. More specifically, we consider the following initial and boundary value problem, with either periodic or homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions: we seek a
wavefunction

u:\Omega\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{C}
such that

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{i}u_{t}+p(t)\Delta u+q(t)|u|^{2}u+\mathrm{i}r(t)u=0,&&\quad\mbox{$(x,t)\in{\Omega}\!\times\!(0,\ T]$,}&\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\qquad&&\quad\mbox{$x\in\Omega$,}&\\
&u=0\ \text{or}\ u\ \text{periodic},\qquad&&\quad\mbox{$(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,\ T]$,}&\end{aligned}\right.

where
T>0
and the coefficients
p(t),\ q(t),\ r(t)
are real valued and in general depend smoothly on time. The domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

d=1,2,3
is bounded, convex and polygonal in the Dirichlet case, and a d-dimensional parallelepiped in the periodic case. The initial condition is taken as
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and to satisfy the boundary conditions.

When considering the real-world problem of growth of ocean waves during storms, the energy input is substantial. For example, the significant wave height (rms wave height) can grow 20 times or more. So a key requirement for numerical schemes used on the NCNLS is that they handle the energy increase in a reliable way. To that end, we work with two schemes which are known to be conservative for the NLS, and extend each of them to the NCNLS in a way that produces exact discrete energy-balance and mass-balance laws in each case.

In particular, in Section
3
we consider a relaxation scheme, extending the relaxation scheme for the NLS appearing in
[
6
,
7
,
20
]
. A key feature is that it is linearly implicit in time, and finite elements are used for the discretization in space. With respect to the time discretization, the scheme is implicit in the Laplacian but explicit in the nonlinearity, requiring the solution of only a linear system in each time-step. In addition, it satisfies discrete analogues of the mass and energy balance laws, and it is second-order-accurate in time.

Moreover, in Section
4
, we consider an extension of the so called Delfour-Fortin-Payre(DFP) finite element scheme
[
12
,
1
]
to the NCNLS. This scheme also satisfies a discrete analogue of the energy and mass balance laws, and it is also second order accurate in time. It is however fully implicit and hence requires the solution of a nonlinear system in every time-step. In
[
1
]
, Newton’s method was considered and analyzed for the numerical solution of the nonlinear system in each time-step.

In Section
5
, we implement several numerical experiments using the two above mentioned schemes and we show numerically that both schemes capture very robustly the correct values of mass and energy, even in strongly non-conservative cases. We compare the behaviour of the two schemes and draw conclusions in Section
6
.

2.
Continuous Mass and Energy Balance

The standard NLS equation, i.e. taking
r(t)\equiv 0
and
p(t),q(t)
independent of time in (
1.1
), satisfies a series of
conservation laws
, with
mass
and
energy
being the most fundamental and of great physical importance in many applications. However, when the coefficients
p(t),q(t),r(t)
vary with time, (
1.1
) satisfies more general mass and energy
balance laws
instead of conservation.
We define the
mass

\mathcal{M}(t)
, the
kinetic

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)
and
potential

\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)
energies respectively

(2.1)

\mathcal{M}(t):=\|u(t)\|^{2},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t):=\|\nabla u\|^{2},\quad\mathcal{E}_{p}(t):=\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}.

Then one can show the following

Lemma 2.1

(Continuous Mass and Energy balance)
.

If
u
is a solution of (
1.1
) then for
0\leq t\leq T
we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle=-2r(t)\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle\text{Balance of Mass},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p(t)\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)+\frac{1}{4}q(t)\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)

\displaystyle=r(t)\Big(p(t)\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)-q(t)\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)\Big)

\displaystyle\text{Balance of Energy}.

Proof.

We derive first the balance of mass. We multiply the Schrödinger equation (
1.1
) by
\bar{u}
and integrate over
\Omega
yielding

\mathrm{i}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u_{t}\bar{u}\,\text{d}x-p(t)\|\nabla u\|^{2}+q(t)\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\mathrm{i}r(t)\|u\|^{2}=0,

Since
p,q,r
are real valued, taking imaginary parts, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\|u\|^{2}=-r(t)\|u\|^{2}

which yields immediately (
2.2
).

For the energy balance we multiply the Schrödinger equation (
1.1
) by
\bar{u}_{t}
and integrate over
\Omega
to get,

\mathrm{i}\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u_{t}\bar{u}_{t}\,\text{d}x+p(t)\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!\Delta u\bar{u}_{t}\,\text{d}x+q(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!|u|^{2}u\bar{u}_{t}\,\text{d}x+\mathrm{i}r(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u\bar{u}_{t}\,\text{d}x=0.

Integrating by parts the second term and taking real parts we obtain,

(2.4)

\frac{1}{2}p(t)\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\|\nabla u\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}q(t)\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}+\texttt{Re}\left(\mathrm{i}r(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u\bar{u}_{t}\,\text{d}x\right)=0.

For the third term in (
2.4
), using (
1.1
) we have

\displaystyle\texttt{Re}\left(\mathrm{i}r(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u\bar{u}_{t}\,\text{d}x\right)=\texttt{Re}\left(r(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u(-\overline{\mathrm{i}u}_{t})\!\,\text{d}x\right)=\texttt{Re}\left(r(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u\left(p(t)\Delta\bar{u}+q(t)|u|^{2}\bar{u}+\mathrm{i}r(t)\bar{u}\right)\!\!\,\text{d}x\right)=

\displaystyle\texttt{Re}\left(r(t)\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!u\left(p(t)\Delta\bar{u}+q(t)|u|^{2}\bar{u}\right)\!\!\,\text{d}x\right)=r(t)\left(-p(t)\|\nabla u\|^{2}+q(t)\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}\right),

and combined with (
2.4
) yields (
2.3
).
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Solving
equation (
2.2
) it can be seen that

(2.5)

\mathcal{M}(t)=\mathcal{M}(0)e^{\textstyle{-2\int_{0}^{t}r(s)\,\text{d}s}}.

In particular, depending on the sign of
r(t)
, mass can experience either exponential growth or decay.

Remark 2.2
.

In case
p,q,r
are independent of time, then (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) simplify

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}(0)e^{\textstyle{-2r_{0}t}}\quad 0\leq t\leq T,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{2}p_{0}\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)+\frac{1}{4}q_{0}\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=r_{0}\left(p_{0}\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)-q_{0}\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)\right),\quad 0\leq t\leq T.

If, furthermore,
r\equiv 0
then we recover the standard conservation of mass and energy for the NLS.

3.
A relaxation numerical scheme and discrete balance laws

We introduce now a numerical scheme for approximating solutions of (
1.1
). Our method is based on the relaxation approach introduced by Besse
[
6
]
for the classical NLS. A similar approach was also considered for the Schrödinger-Poisson system in
[
4
]
.
First we rewrite (
1.1
) as a coupled system of two equations by introducing an auxiliary variable
\phi
for the nonlinearity, and we consider the following enlarged system

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{t}-\mathrm{i}p(t)\Delta u-\mathrm{i}q(t)\phi u+r(t)u=0,&&\quad\mbox{$(x,t)\in{\Omega}\!\times\!(0,\ T]$,}&\\
&\phi=|u|^{2},&&\quad\mbox{$(x,t)\in{\Omega}\!\times\!(0,\ T]$,}&\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\qquad&&\quad\mbox{$x\in\Omega$,}&\\
&u=0\ \text{or}\ u\ \text{periodic},\qquad&&\quad\mbox{$(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,\ T]$,}&\end{aligned}\right.

which is equivalent to (
1.1
). The numerical scheme that we introduce next is based on this enlarged form of (
1.1
). To simplify the presentation we assume in the sequel homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, while periodic boundary condition can be easily incorporated with slight modifications. We present first the time discrete scheme and then we proceed to the fully-discrete scheme.

3.1.
Time discrete scheme

We introduce a sequence of nodes
0=:t_{0}<\dots<t_{n}<\dots<t_{N}:=T
of
[0,T]
and variable time steps
k_{n}=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
. Then the relaxation scheme for (
1.1
) based on (
3.1
) is defined as follows: we seek approximations
U^{n}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
to
u(t_{n})\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),n=1,\dots,N
such that

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{k_{n-1}}{k_{n}+k_{n-1}}\Phi^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{k_{n}}{k_{n}+k_{n-1}}\Phi^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=|U^{n}|^{2},\quad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\\
&\bar{\partial}U^{n}-\mathrm{i}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\Delta U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}-\mathrm{i}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\Phi^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}+r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}=0,\qquad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\end{aligned}\right.

where we have used the notation

(3.3)

t_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{t_{n+1}+t_{n}}{2},\ \bar{\partial}U^{n}:=\frac{U^{n+1}-U^{n}}{k_{n}}\ \text{ and }\ U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}:=\frac{U^{n+1}+U^{n}}{2},

with
f_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}=f(t_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}})
for
f=p,q,r
. At
n=0
, a straightforward choice to initialize the system would be
U^{0}=u_{0}
,
k_{-1}=k_{0}
,
\Phi^{-\frac{1}{2}}=|u_{0}|^{2}
,
[
6
,
7
]
. However this simple choice has been known to create computational issues for other equations
[
26
,
4
]
and the same seems to apply here. Thus, in Section
5.1
we introduce a modified initialization which addresses this issue.

3.2.
Fully discrete scheme

We introduce now a fully discrete scheme for (
1.1
) based on (
3.1
). The time discrete scheme (
3.2
) can be combined with various methods for spatial discretization, including finite differences
[
6
,
25
]
and spectral methods. Motivated by our previous works for Schrödinger-type models,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
4
]
we choose finite elements for space discretization.

We introduce now the fully-discrete relaxation scheme for (
1.1
) based on (
3.1
) which is given as follows: we seek approximations
U_{h}^{n}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}(\mathbb{C})
to
u(\cdot,t_{n})\in H^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
,
1\leq n\leq N
, such that

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{k_{n-1}}{k_{n}+k_{n-1}}\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}+\frac{k_{n}}{k_{n}+k_{n-1}}\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right),\quad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\\
&\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}-\mathrm{i}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\Delta_{h}U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}-\mathrm{i}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)+r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}=0,\qquad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\end{aligned}\right.

with
U_{h}^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{h}u_{0}
,
k_{-1}=k_{0}
and, for the straightforward initialisation,
\Phi_{h}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathcal{P}_{h}(|u_{0}|^{2}).
Recall that
\mathcal{P}_{h}
and
\Delta_{h}
denote the
L^{2}
-projection operator (
1.3
) and the discrete Laplacian operator (
1.4
), respectively. In the case of constant timestep
k_{n}=k
we get

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}=2\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)-\Phi_{h}^{{n-\frac{1}{2}}},\quad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\\
&\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}-\mathrm{i}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\Delta_{h}U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}-\mathrm{i}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)+r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}=0,\qquad 0\leq n\leq N-1,\end{aligned}\right.

3.3.
Discrete mass and energy balance laws

We define the
discrete mass

\mathcal{M}_{h}^{n}
as the discrete counterpart of
\mathcal{M}
in (
2.1
), namely
\mathcal{M}_{h}^{n}=\|U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}
. The discrete mass satisfies a discrete analogue of the continuous mass balance (
2.2
),

Lemma 3.1

(Local Discrete Mass balance)
.

The solution of the fully discrete relaxation scheme (
3.4
) satisfies

(3.6)

\mathcal{M}_{h}^{n+1}=\mathcal{M}_{h}^{n}-2k_{n}r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2},\quad\mbox{ where }\quad\mathcal{M}_{h}^{n}=\|U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}.

Proof.

We multiply (
3.4
)(b) by
\bar{U}_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}
and we integrate

\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!\bar{U}_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x+\mathrm{i}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2}-\mathrm{i}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}|U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}|^{2}\,\text{d}x+r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2}=0

taking real parts we get

\texttt{Re}\left(\!\int_{\Omega}\!\bar{U}_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)+r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2}=0

Also

\bar{U}_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}=\frac{1}{2k_{n}}\left(\bar{U}_{h}^{n+1}+\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\right)\left(U_{h}^{n+1}-U_{h}^{n}\right)=\frac{1}{2k_{n}}\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}-|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}-(\bar{U}_{h}^{n+1}U^{n}-\bar{U}_{h}^{n}U_{h}^{n+1})\right)

Since
\texttt{Re}\left(\bar{U}_{h}^{n+1}U_{h}^{n}-\bar{U}_{h}^{n}U_{h}^{n+1}\right)=0
, the last two relations yield (
3.6
).
∎

Remark 3.1
.

Relation (
3.6
) describes a local discrete mass balance in
[t_{n},t_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}]
. If
r(t)\equiv 0
then (
3.6
) reduces to the standard global discrete mass conservation for the NLS (
2.6
):
\mathcal{M}_{h}^{n}=\mathcal{M}_{h}^{0}
.

We show next a discrete energy balance for the fully discrete relaxation scheme. To do so, we need to define the
discrete kinetic
energy
\mathcal{E}_{\kappa,h}^{n}

(3.7)

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa,h}^{n}=\|\nabla U_{h}^{n}\|^{2},

and
discrete potential energy

\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}.
In particular,
\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}
is obtained through a non-trivial discretization of the potential energy, namely

(3.8)

\mathcal{E}_{p_{1},h}^{n}=\!\int_{\Omega}\!|\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\!\,\text{d}x,\quad\mathcal{E}_{p_{2},h}^{n}=\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\!\,\text{d}x,\quad\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}:=2\mathcal{E}_{p_{2},h}^{n}-\mathcal{E}_{p_{1},h}^{n}

The role of the
discrete potential energy

\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}
will be carried by
\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}=2\mathcal{E}_{p_{2},h}^{n}-\mathcal{E}_{p_{1},h}^{n}.

Lemma 3.2

(Local Discrete Energy balance)
.

The solution of the fully discrete relaxation scheme (
3.4
) satisfies

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\kappa,h}^{n}\right)+\frac{1}{4}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}\left(\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\left(p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2}-q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}|U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}|^{2}\!\,\text{d}x\right)

Proof.

We multiply (
3.4
)(b) by
\bar{\partial}\bar{U}^{n}
and we integrate over
\Omega:

\displaystyle\!\int_{\Omega}\!\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x-\mathrm{i}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Delta U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x-\mathrm{i}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x+r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x=0

\displaystyle\|\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}+\mathrm{i}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\nabla U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\nabla\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x-\mathrm{i}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x+r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x=0

Taking imaginary parts we obtain

(3.10)

\frac{p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\bar{\partial}\left(\|\nabla U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}\right)-\texttt{Im}\left(\mathrm{i}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)+\texttt{Im}\left(r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)=0

Using (
3.2
)(b), for the third term in (
3.10
), we have

(3.11)

\displaystyle\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle=\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathrm{i}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Delta\bar{U}_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\mathrm{i}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{U}_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{U}_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\,\text{d}x

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}+\mathrm{i}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\,\text{d}x-r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}\implies

\displaystyle\texttt{Im}\left(r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(-p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}+q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\,\text{d}x\right)

Similarly, for the second term in (
3.10
) we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\texttt{Im}\left(\mathrm{i}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)=\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2k_{n}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}-|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\,\text{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2k_{n}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}-\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}+\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}-\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\,\text{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\bar{\partial}\left(\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\,\text{d}x+\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2k_{n}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\left(\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\,\text{d}x\overset{\eqref{TDrelax}(a)}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\bar{\partial}\left(\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\,\text{d}x+\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{4k_{n}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left(\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\,\text{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\bar{\partial}\left(\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\,\text{d}x-\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{4}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\bar{\partial}\left(|\Phi_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\right)\,\text{d}x

Combining (
3.10
), (
3.11
), (
3.12
) (
3.7
) and (
3.8
), we obtain (
3.9
).
∎

Remark 3.2

(
Relationship between discrete and continuous balance laws
)
.

The discrete mass balance law, equation (
3.6
), is equivalent to

\bar{\partial}\mathcal{M}^{n}_{h}=-2r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|U^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{h}\|^{2}

which is a direct discretization of the continuous mass balance law, equation (
2.2
),

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{M}(t)=-2r(t)\mathcal{M}(t).

Moreover, the continuous energy balance law, equation (
2.3
), can be understood with

\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t):=\|\nabla u\|^{2},\quad\mathcal{E}_{p}(t):=\|u\|_{L^{4}}^{4}=\langle 2|u|^{2}-\phi,\phi\rangle

in the context of the augmented system (
3.1
).
Thus the discrete energy balance law, equation (
3.9
),

\frac{1}{2}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\kappa,h}^{n}\right)+\frac{1}{4}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\bar{\partial}\left(\mathcal{E}_{p,h}^{n}\right)=r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\left(p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|^{2}-q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\!\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\!\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}|U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}|^{2}\!\,\text{d}x\right)

is a direct discretization of the continuous energy balance law, equation (
2.3
),

\frac{1}{2}p(t)\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)+\frac{1}{4}q(t)\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)=r(t)\Big(p(t)\mathcal{E}_{\kappa}(t)-q(t)\mathcal{E}_{p}(t)\Big).

Unlike the conservative setting, discrete mass and energy balance laws do not automatically guarantee that the mass and energy are exactly correct at all times, only that their variation mimicks in a precise way that of the continuous problem. The same is true for the fully implicit scheme (
4.1
) which is examined in the next Section. Thus the ultimate validation of the discrete mass and energy laws is how close the mass and energy of the numerical solution are to the exact value, cf. Tables
5
-
7
in Section
5
.

4.
A fully implicit scheme and discrete balance laws

We also consider a fully discrete scheme based on the one proposed by Delfour-Fortin-Payre, in
[
12
]
for the classical cubic NLS. Its main ingredient is how the nonlinearity is treated, leading to exact conservation, at the discrete level, of mass and energy for classical cubic NLS with constant coefficients. However, the scheme is fully nonlinear and a linearization process is required to obtain the approximate solution. A variation of Newton’s method for this scheme was fully analyzed in
[
1
]
.

We present now the modified DFP scheme in the context of our model (
1.1
): we seek approximations
U_{h}^{n}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}(\mathbb{C})
to
u(\cdot,t_{n})\in H^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
,
1\leq n\leq N
, such that

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}-\mathrm{i}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{h}U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}-\mathrm{i}\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{i}r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=0,\\
&U_{h}^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{h}(u_{0}).\end{aligned}\right.

We proceed by examining the conservation properties of (
4.1
). Concerning the mass balance, scheme (
4.1
) satisfies the exact same balance relation (
3.6
) as the relaxation scheme (
3.4
), and the proof is straightforward, thus we omit its presentation. However, the corresponding energy balance that (
4.1
) satisfies differs from (
3.9
) and we present it next.

Lemma 4.1

(Local discrete energy balance)
.

The solution of the fully discrete scheme (
4.1
) satisfies

(4.2)

\frac{1}{2}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\|\nabla U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}=\\
=r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}-\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)|U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\!\,\text{d}x\right).

Proof.

We multiply (
4.1
) by
\bar{\partial}\bar{U}^{n}
and we integrate to get

\|\bar{\partial}U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}+\mathrm{i}p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\nabla U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\nabla\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\\
-\mathrm{i}\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x+r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x=0

Taking imaginary parts we obtain

\frac{p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\bar{\partial}\left(\|\nabla U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}\right)-\texttt{Im}\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)\\
+\texttt{Im}\left(r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)=0

For the second term we have

\texttt{Im}\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)=\\
=\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\frac{1}{2k_{n}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}-|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)\!\,\text{d}x

As before, see (
3.11
), for the third term we have

\texttt{Im}\left(r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\!\int_{\Omega}\!U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bar{\partial}\bar{U}_{h}^{n}\,\text{d}x\right)=r_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(-p_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}+\frac{q_{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)|U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\,\text{d}x\right)

and the result follows by combining the last three relations.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

In the case
r(t)\equiv 0
and
p,q
are constants then from (
4.2
) we recover the standard conservation of energy for the cubic NLS, namely

\frac{1}{2}p_{0}\|\nabla U_{h}^{n}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}q_{0}\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}=\frac{1}{2}p_{0}\|\nabla U_{h}^{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}q_{0}\|U_{h}^{0}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}.

In the context of the NCNLS,
the extended DFP scheme (
4.1
) offers a more straightforward discretization of the potential energy appearing in the left hand side of equation (
4.2
) compared to the relaxation scheme, namely
\|U^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{4}}^{4}
(cf. equations (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) for comparison). This, however, comes at the cost of scheme (
4.1
) being fully nonlinear, in contrast to the linearly implicit scheme (
3.4
). Observe moreover that a nontrivial discretization of the potential energy appears on the right hand side of (
4.2
) too, namely
\frac{1}{2}\!\int_{\Omega}\!\left(|U_{h}^{n+1}|^{2}+|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)|U_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}|^{2}\!\,\text{d}x.

5.
Numerical Experiments

We perform a series of numerical experiments to validate the numerical methods and study their behaviour. In the process, we discuss some details about the implementation of the schemes. The numerical results reported in this section are one dimensional (
d=1
) and the numerical scheme is implemented with in house
C
-codes using double precision arithmetic.

5.1.
Implementation details for the relaxation scheme

In this section we present some technical details concerning the efficient implementation of scheme (
3.5
) and its appropriate initialization. We rewrite the numerical scheme (
3.5
) as a Runge-Kutta method : we first update
\Phi^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}
, we then solve for the intermediate stage
U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}
and finally we update
U_{h}^{n+1}
for
0\leq n\leq N-1
,

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}=2\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(|U_{h}^{n}|^{2}\right)-\Phi_{h}^{{n-\frac{1}{2}}},\\
&\left(1+\frac{k}{2}r_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}-\mathrm{i}\frac{k}{2}p_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\Delta_{h}U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}-\mathrm{i}\frac{k}{2}q_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{P}_{h}\left(\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}U^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)=kU_{h}^{n},\\
&U_{h}^{n+1}=2U_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}-U_{h}^{n}.\end{aligned}\right.

A simple choice for initializing (
5.1
) is to take
U_{h}^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{h}u_{0}
and
\Phi_{h}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\mathcal{P}_{h}(|u_{0}|^{2})
. For these initial values, it is observed numerically that
U_{h}^{n}
is a second order approximation in time to
u(t_{n})
, but
\Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is only first order approximation to
|u(t_{n+\frac{1}{2}})|^{2}
. Consequently, this affects scheme’s behaviour towards mass and energy balance laws. The same phenomenon was observed also in
[
26
,
4
,
27
]
for similar schemes applied to different equations. In those same references an alternative approach to initialization was proposed to remedy the situation, which we describe below.

\displaystyle\text{Initialize:}\quad U_{h}^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{h}u_{0}

\displaystyle\text{Use the scheme \eqref{FDrelax2} with naive initialization and half timestep to compute:}\quad U_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\text{Set :}\quad\Phi_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathcal{P}_{h}(|U_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}|^{2})

\displaystyle\text{Use this $\Phi_{h}^{\frac{1}{2}}$ and the $2^{{nd}}$ equation in scheme \eqref{FDrelax2} to compute :}\quad U_{h}^{1}

\displaystyle\text{Continue with scheme \eqref{FDrelax2} for :}\quad n=1,\dots,N-1

This initialization process is observed numerically to lead to second order approximation in time for both
U_{h}^{n},\ \Phi_{h}^{n+\frac{1}{2}},
cf. Tables
1
,
2
and
7
. In the remainder of this Section, when we refer to scheme (
5.1
) we will always mean that the improved initialization discussed above is used.

5.2.
Implementation of the DFP scheme

The modified DFP scheme (
4.1
) is implicit, i.e. it requires the solution of a nonlinear equation for
U^{n+1}
at each timestep. This can be done with a Newton method; a detailed strategy is presented in
[
1
]
and convergence is proven under precise assumptions. To describe the main idea, let us denote by
U^{n+1}_{m}
the sequence of approximate solutions generated by Newton iteration for
U^{n+1}.
This sequence is initialized with
U^{n+1}_{(0)}=U^{n}.
Now in order to compute the first Newton iteration step,
U^{n+1}_{(1)},
one still needs to solve a nonlinear problem. To that end, a second “inner” iteration is required; a fixed point iteration is setup for that. It is found in
[
1
]
that one step of the Newton iteration with 4 steps of the inner iteration suffices.

5.3.
Manufacturing exact solutions for the NCNLS and validation

Let
w(x,t)
be a solution of the classical NLS equation, i.e. a solution of (
1.1
) with
p=p_{0},

q=\theta_{0},

r=0,

\mathrm{i}w_{t}+p_{0}\Delta w+\theta_{0}|w|^{2}w=0.

Then, given any smooth function
r(t),
it follows that the function

u(x,t):=w(x,t)\exp{\left(-\int_{0}^{t}r(s)\,\text{d}s\right)}

satisfies

\mathrm{i}u_{t}+p_{0}\Delta u+q(t)|u|^{2}u+\mathrm{i}r(t)u=0,\qquad q(t)=\theta_{0}\exp{\left(2\int_{0}^{t}r(s)\,\text{d}s\right)}.

Thus starting from a standard soliton solution of the classical NLS we can generate the solution

(5.2)

u(x,t)=\mathrm{i}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\left(2\omega x+(1-4\omega^{2})t\right)\right)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}r(s)\,\text{d}s\right)\text{sech}\left(x-4\omega t\right)

of (
1.1
) for any smooth function
r(t).
(This requires that the effective support of the initial soliton is well contained within
\Omega;
then periodic BCs can be used with no problem. Formula (
5.2
) applies until the effective support of the soliton reaches the boundary. For periodic BCs, a periodized soliton can still be used for long times.)

All computations reported in Tables
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
, were performed for
\Omega\times[0,T]=[a,b]\times[0,T]=[-30,30]\times[0,1]
with
\omega=0.3
and taking
r(t)=\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}t\right)
. For
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
we consider uniform partitions of
\Omega\times[0,T]
according to
h=\frac{b-a}{M},\ k=\frac{T}{N}
with
x_{i}=a+ih,\ i=0,1,\dots,M
,
t_{n}=nk,\ n=0,1,\dots,N
. For a given pair
(M,N)
we implement the method with corresponding mesh sizes; in order to investigate the EOC, sequences of runs with different mesh sizes
(h_{\mu},\ k_{\nu})
are used. The errors are computed in the

-norm and formally we expect that

\displaystyle E(u;h,k):=\max_{0\leq n\leq N}\|u(\cdot,t_{n})-U_{h}^{n}\|=O(h^{\ell+1}+k^{2}),

\displaystyle E(\phi;h,k):=\max_{0\leq n\leq N}\||u(\cdot,t_{{n+\frac{1}{2}}})|^{2}-\Phi_{h}^{{n+\frac{1}{2}}}\|=O(h^{\ell+1}+k^{2}),

5.4.
Discrete balance of mass and energy

Robust approximation of energy influx in the non-conservative problem (
1.1
) is a highly desirable feature for any numerical scheme used in that context. In this section we investigate numerically the behaviour of the numerical schemes (
5.1
) and (
4.1
) in that respect. In particular we are interesting in the long time behaviour of discrete mass and energy balance laws, and their convergence characteristics with respect to timestep
k
for various choices of the function
r(t)
. Given that an exact solution is known, cf. equation (
5.2
), we proceed by computing directly the error in mass and energy.
For a given function
r(t)

We investigate a number of different scenarios:

(1)

The conservative case,
r_{1}(t)=0.

(2)

Constant decay,
r_{2}(t)=1.

(3)

Constant growth,
r_{3}(t)=-1.

(4)

Growth followed by decay,
r_{4}=t-\frac{T}{2}.

(5)

Decay followed by growth,
r_{5}=\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}t\right).

(6)

A problem that initially is conservative, then exhibits a short phase of growth, and then becomes conservative again. This is the most realistic scenario for wind induced growth of water waves:
r_{6}(t)=\frac{-1}{C_{e}}\exp\left(-\mu^{2}(t-\frac{T}{2})^{2}\right)

C_{e}

\int_{0}^{T}r_{6}(t)\,\text{d}t=-1.

6.
Discussion

6.1.
Mass balance error comparison

Both schemes conserve the mass at the discrete level in the case of constant coefficients
p(t)=p_{0},\ q(t)=q_{0}
and
r(t)\equiv 0
which corresponds to the
r_{1}
-column of Tables
5
and
8
. In the non-conservatives cases,
r_{2},\ r_{3}
and
r_{4}
, the mass balance error exhibits similar behaviour for both schemes, however the corresponding error values in these cases are 1-2 orders of magnitude smaller for scheme (
4.1
) compared to the relaxation scheme (
5.1
). Furthermore, for
r_{5}
and
r_{6}
the two schemes have almost identical behaviour as it’s shown in the corresponding columns of Tables
5
and
8
respectively.

6.2.
Energy balance error comparison

Scheme (
4.1
), by design, conserves energy at the discrete level. This is depicted in the 1st column of Table
9
where the energy is conserved to double precision, while the relaxation scheme (
5.1
) only up to single precision as depicted in the corresponding column of Table
6
. This is the main difference between the schemes. For the other non-conservative choices
r_{2},\ r_{3}
and
r_{4}
the values in the corresponding columns of Tables
6
and
9
are comparable, with those for scheme (
4.1
) being slightly smaller. Lastly the values of energy balance error for cases
r_{5}
and
r_{6}
are very similar between the two methods.

6.3.
Computational comparison

Overall both schemes behave equally well when preserving the mass, while scheme (
4.1
) has a clear advantage over the relaxation scheme (
5.1
) when conservation of energy is concerned. However the computational cost of scheme (
4.1
) is significantly higher than of the relaxation one since an elaborate Newton-like scheme is used for the solution of the nonlinear system, see
[
1
]
for details. On the other hand the relaxation scheme introduces an auxiliary variable thus doubling the number of equations need it to be solved. Using the same discretization and physical parameters for both schemes, we observe that, on the average, to produce any of the columns of Tables
5
and
6
it requires about
106s
of computational time, while the corresponding time for scheme (
4.1
) is about
175s
, a
65\%
increase.

6.4.
Outlook

The relaxation scheme (
5.1
) is simpler to implement and analyze, by requiring the solution of only a complex linear system at every timestep. Crucially, this allows practical a posteriori error estimators to be constructed, see
[
19
]
for the conservative case. Thus, it is extremely promising for problems where adaptivity is required.
The implicit scheme (
4.1
), implemented with the Newton-type scheme introduced in
[
1
]
, is only moderately more computationally expensive. Moreover, building on the ideas behind it, even higher order conservative in time and computational efficient schemes can be proposed.
