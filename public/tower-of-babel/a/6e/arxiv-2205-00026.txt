Quantum advantage in charging cavity and spin batteries by repeated interactions
Raffaele Salvia, Martí Perarnau-Llobet, Géraldine Haack, Nicolas Brunner, Stefan Nimmrichter

Abstract
Recently, an unconditional advantage has been demonstrated for the process of charging of a quantum battery in a collisional model. Motivated by the question of whether such an advantage could be observed experimentally, we consider a model where the battery is modeled by a quantum harmonic oscillator or a large spin, charged via repeated interactions with a stream of non-equilibrium qubit units. For both setups, we show that a quantum protocol can significantly outperform the most general adaptive classical schemes, leading to 90% and 38% higher charging power for the cavity and large spin batteries respectively. Towards an experimental realization, we also characterise the robustness of this quantum advantage to imperfections (noise and decoherence) considering implementations with state-of-the-art micromasers and hybrid superconducting devices.

II
Framework

We first present some generic considerations for the sequential charging of the quantum battery by identical qubits, which serves as the basis for our treatment of oscillator and spin batteries.
Let us consider the battery Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{B}=E\sum_{n}n|n\rangle\langle n|=E\hat{N}
describing a uniform, bounded or unbounded, energy ladder in steps of
E>0
. Starting in the ground state of empty charge,
\rho_{B}(0)=|0\rangle\langle 0|
, the battery shall be charged by a sequence of resonant qubits with
\hat{H}_{Q}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
, as sketched in Fig.
1
.
Each qubit is prepared in a state

\displaystyle\rho_{Q}=

\displaystyle q|g\rangle\langle g|+(1-q)|e\rangle\langle e|

\displaystyle+c\sqrt{q(1-q)}\left(e^{-i\alpha}|g\rangle\langle e|+e^{i\alpha}|e\rangle\langle g|\right),

(1)

where
q\in[0,1]
is the ground-state population,
c\in[0,1]
is the amount of coherence in the state, and
\alpha\in[0,2\pi]
is a phase. Along this work, we make a distinction between quantum coherent protocols (where
c\neq 0
) and incoherent or classical protocols (
c=0
).

In the
k
th step of the battery charging process, a qubit unit and the battery interact for a time
t_{k}
via the resonant exchange Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\Omega\left(\hat{A}\otimes|e\rangle\langle g|+\hat{A}^{\dagger}\otimes|g\rangle\langle e|\right),

(2)

at a fixed Rabi frequency
\Omega
.
Here,
\hat{A}
represents a model-dependent ladder operator satisfying
\langle m|\hat{A}|n\rangle\propto\delta_{m,n-1}
. In our previous work Ref.
Seah et al. 2021
, we considered an ideal energy ladder with
\hat{A}=\sum_{k=1}\ket{k-1}\bra{k}
, whereas here we will focus on the following two physically relevant scenarios:

•

Quantum harmonic oscillator, with
\hat{A}
a bosonic annihilation operator,

\hat{A}=\hat{a}=\sum_{k=1}\sqrt{k}\ket{k-1}\bra{k}
.

•

Large spin with
\hat{A}=\hat{J}_{-}
, the exact definition being introduced in section
IV
.

Note that the total energy is preserved during the interaction process,
[\hat{H},\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{Q}]=0
, so that no additional work must be consumed in this process.

In the interaction picture with respect to
\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{H}_{Q}
, the state
\rho_{B}
of the battery evolves at the
k
th charging step as:

\displaystyle\rho_{B}(k+1)=\tr_{Q}\left\{e^{-it_{k}\hat{H}}\rho_{B}(k)\otimes\rho_{Q}e^{it_{k}\hat{H}}\right\},

(3)

Such interaction processes are applied for a total available time
\tau
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\tau=\sum_{k=1}^{K}t_{k},

(4)

where
K
is the total number of qubits. We regard the charging time
\tau
as the limiting resource, whereas
K
and
\{t_{1},...,t_{K}\}
can be freely chosen with the only constraint of Eq. (
4
). In what follows, it will be convenient to introduce the dimensionless interaction times (swap angles):

\displaystyle\theta_{k}=\Omega t_{k}.

(5)

Our goal is to maximise the final average energy of the quantum battery,
\bar{E}(K)=\tr\{\rho_{B}(K)\hat{H}_{B}\}
, under the time constraint
\tau
. In other words, we wish to maximise the charging
power

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{P}}=\frac{\bar{E}(K)}{\tau}.

(6)

We will consider this optimization for both classical (
c=0
) and quantum (
c\neq 0
) protocols. For convenience, we also introduce the transient charging power of the
k
-th step,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(k)=\frac{\bar{E}(k)-\bar{E}(k-1)}{t_{k}}.

(7)

Although (
6
) is the central figure of merit of this work, we note that not all energy in
\rho_{B}(K)
can be extracted as useful work. Some fraction of
\bar{E}(K)
will be
passive
energy in the form of heat, since the battery state has in general non-zero entropy. This motivates us to also compute the ergotropy
\mathcal{E}(K)
, which quantifies the amount of work that can be extracted from
\rho_{B}(K)
in a cyclic Hamiltonian process
Allahverdyan et al. 2004
;
Goold et al. 2016
. Given a quantum state
\rho
with Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
, the ergotropy
\mathcal{E}
is defined by
\mathcal{E}=\tr{\rho\hat{H}}-\tr{\rho^{\rm passive}\hat{H}}
, where
\rho^{\rm{passive}}=\sum_{n}r_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|
with
[\rho^{\rm passive},\hat{H}]=0
and
\{r_{n}\}
the eigenvalues of the initial state in descending order. Hence, the
useful
battery charge is always upper-bounded by the average energy,
\mathcal{E}_{B}\leq\bar{E}_{B}
, and equality only holds for pure battery states in our case.

Before moving forward, we also note that the evolution of the battery (
3
) can be effectively described by a master equation model. While this approach is not strictly required to derive the main results presented in this work, it is both physically insightful and technically useful (e.g., for assessing the quantum-coherent evolution of the battery). We present it in detail in Appendix
A
for the interested reader.

III.1
Bound on the incoherent charging power

We first focus on incoherent charging strategies where
c=0
in (
1
). Let us expand
\hat{H}_{B}=E\sum_{n}n|n\rangle\langle n|
and recall that initially the battery starts in the ground state,
\rho_{B}(0)=|0\rangle\langle 0|
. The state
\rho_{B}(k)
remains energy-diagonal under the evolution (
3
). The evolution of the populations
p(n,k)=\bra{n}\rho_{B}(k)\ket{n}
can be described by the rate equation

\displaystyle p(n,k+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{0}(n,k)p(n,k)+P_{-}(n+1,k)p(n+1,k)

(9)

\displaystyle+\,\,P_{+}(n-1,k)p(n-1,k).

where
P_{-}(n,k)=q\sin^{2}(\sqrt{n}\theta_{k})
,
P_{+}(n,k)=(1-q)\sin^{2}(\sqrt{n+1}\theta_{k})
, and
P_{0}(n,k)=1-P_{-}(n,k)-P_{+}(n,k)
.

Consider the mean energy gain of the battery at the
k
-th incoherent charge step,

\displaystyle\Delta\bar{E}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{E}(k)-\bar{E}(k-1)

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[P_{+}(n,k)-P_{-}(n,k)\right]p(n,k-1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle E\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sin^{2}(\sqrt{n+1}\theta_{k})p(n,k-1).

Naturally, this gain is optimal if we consider fully excited charge units (
q=0
), as stated in the last line. Note that we assume that the swap angle
\theta_{k}
can be adapted over the course of the sequence by changing the coupling time.

We now consider the transient charging power,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{P}(k)}{E\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\bar{E}(k)}{E\theta_{k}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}(\sqrt{n+1}\theta_{k})}{\theta_{k}}p(n,k-1).

(11)

It can be upper bounded by optimising the level-dependent excitation rates over the swap angle (see also
Andolina et al. 2018
),

\displaystyle\max_{\theta}\frac{\sin^{2}(\sqrt{n+1}\theta)}{\theta}=R_{0}\sqrt{n+1}\;,

(12)

with
R_{0}\approx 0.725
at
\theta\approx 0.742\,\pi/(2\sqrt{n+1})
.
Inserting this result into (
11
) and making use of Jensen’s inequality, we arrive at a non-tight bound,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{P}(k)}{E\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}p(n,k-1)

(13)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{0}\sqrt{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)p(n,k-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{E}(k-1)}{E}+1}.

Let us now define a continuous function
\bar{E}(\Omega\tau)
of time that interpolates linearly between the values of the mean energy at successive charge steps. Following the derivation of (
13
), this function must satisfy

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar{E}(\Omega\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\leq\Omega ER_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{E}(\Omega\tau)}{E}+1}\;,

(14)

which can be integrated by separation of variables. Given the initial condition
\bar{E}(0)=0
, we arrive at the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\Omega\tau)\leq\bar{E}(\Omega\tau)\leq ER_{0}\Omega\tau\left(1+\frac{R_{0}\Omega\tau}{4}\right).

(15)

It gives an upper bound for the gain in mean energy (and thus ergotropy) after the charging time
\tau
. Accordingly, it bounds the average charging power (
6
) as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{P}}\leq E\Omega R_{0}\left(1+\frac{R_{0}\Omega\tau}{4}\right).

(16)

This bound is shown in Figs.
2
,
3
and
5
, where it defines the grey region potentially accessible by classical charging protocols.
We discuss the saturability of this upper bound by classical protocols in the next section.

III.2
Classical strategies

A natural candidate for an optimal classical strategy is a full-swap protocol: one decreases the interaction time in each charge step
k
according to
\vartheta_{k}=\pi/2\sqrt{k}
, such that the battery remains in a pure charge state,
p(n,k)=\delta_{nk}
, with the mean energy and ergotropy climbing up in steps of
E
. The corresponding transient power at the
k
-th step is given by
\mathcal{P}(k)=\Omega\,\Delta\bar{E}(k)/\vartheta_{k}=2\sqrt{k}\Omega E/\pi
. Assuming further that there is no waiting time between subsequent charge steps, the cumulative charging time is

\Omega\tau=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\vartheta_{j}=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{\sqrt{j}}=\pi\sqrt{K}-\zeta_{1/2}+\mathcal{O}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\right\},

(17)

with the Riemann zeta value
\zeta_{1/2}\approx 1.46
. Hence the cumulatively averaged charging power becomes
\bar{\mathcal{P}}=\bar{E}(K)/\tau=KE/\tau\approx\mathcal{P}(K)/2
for
K\gg 1
.
Using (
17
), this can be expressed in terms of the total time as:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{P}}\approx\frac{E\Omega^{2}\tau}{\pi^{2}}.

(18)

For large
\tau
, it reaches
0.77
of the upper bound (
16
) (noting that both expressions grow linearly with
\tau
).

By numerically optimising over the interaction time, it is possible to find strategies that weakly outperform the full swap in terms of power. The example shown in Figs.
2
and
3
is the
greedy
incoherent protocol, i.e., the one that maximizes at each step the cumulative averaged power. We conclude that while the bound (
16
) is not tight, classical strategies can perform close to it. In what follows, we show that quantum strategies can outperform the classical bound.

One can beat the incoherent bound (
15
) in a non-adaptive protocol using pure coherent charge qubits and small swap angles

. Specifically, for transient charging of an empty battery up to the
N
-th level, a coherent advantage can be achieved in the short-time limit

where the charging process approximates a coherent driving term (see details in App.

and

Ideal performance is reached asymptotically in the short-time limit

(and

), in which the battery state remains a pure displaced vacuum state at all times,

\rho_{B}(K)\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\theta\to 0}\hat{D}\left(\frac{\Omega\tau}{2}\right)|0\rangle\langle 0|\hat{D}\left(-\frac{\Omega\tau}{2}\right),

(19)

with

the displacement operator. This follows from the small-

) of the coherent charging generator, which we perform in App.

The corresponding battery charge (dotted line) grows quadratically with time,
\mathcal{E}(\Omega\tau)=\bar{E}(\Omega\tau)=E\,(\Omega\tau)^{2}/4
, resulting in the average charging power

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{P}}=\frac{E\,\Omega^{2}\tau}{4}.

(20)

It outperforms the upper classical bound (
16
) by a factor
R_{0}^{-2}\approx 1.90
— corresponding to a
90\%
quantum advantage.
We stress that this is a theoretical limit in which each qubit unit transfers only infinitesimal amounts of energy (and thus wastes most of its useful energy) and the state of the battery remains pure. Nevertheless, in the next section we show that the quantum advantage prevails in state-of-the-art experimental platforms with decoherence and a finite number of qubit units.

III.4
Photon loss and suitable experimental platforms

Several experimental platforms can correspond to a quantum battery modeled as a quantum harmonic oscillator, for instance cold atoms and circuit QED setups. With these experimental platforms, it is important to assess the negative impact of photon loss on the coherent charging process, specifically, the critical loss rate at which the quantum advantage disappears. To capture the effect of photon loss, we consider that subsequent charging steps are separated by a finite waiting time
t_{0}
during which a fraction of the battery charge is lost. Employing the standard bosonic damping channel with rate
\kappa
, the battery state transforms in between subsequent charge steps according to the master equation

\partial_{t}\rho=\kappa\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\rho
,
where
\mathcal{D}[\hat{M}]\rho=\hat{M}\rho\hat{M}^{\dagger}-\{\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M},\rho\}/2
.
This results in the CPTP map
Ivan et al. 2011

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}\rho_{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\kappa t_{0}\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]}\rho_{B}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(e^{\kappa t_{0}}-1)^{n}}{n!}\hat{K}_{n}\rho_{B}\hat{K}_{n}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\kappa t_{0}}{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\right).

(21)

In the following, we employ the commonly used damping parameter
\gamma=1-e^{-\kappa t_{0}}
, which approximates
\gamma\approx\kappa t_{0}
when small.

Given this model of photon loss, we evaluate the cumulative charging power
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
in (
6
) taking for
\tau
the total time of the charging steps in which qubit and battery interact
(i.e. the preparation time of the qubit units is not accounted for in the charging power). The damping effect results in a correction of the incoherent charging limit (
15
), see App.
C
for a derivation. Figure
4
plots the numerically evaluated point in time at which the coherent advantage first appears (i.e. the charging power exceeds the incoherent bound) as a function of

for various

-values. It quickly diverges with growing

, and as

reaches a critical value

, above which the advantage is no longer there. For intermediate

-values, there is an optimal

-value for achieving the earliest coherent speedup in the protocol. We also observe that, as the best coherent charging protocols require small qubit-battery collision times

, they are also vulnerable to small losses

. For example, choosing

, a transient coherent advantage can be observed over a a finite window of charging times for

, as shown in Fig.

can be achieved in state-of-the-art experiments.
In the field of cold atoms, a setup similar to Ref.
Sayrin et al. 2011
could work. In this experiment, a high-quality microwave cavity would play the role of the battery, through which a sequence of individual atoms would be sent (the charging units). The state of individual atoms can be controlled via unitary operations, allowing for incoherent and coherent charging processes by tuning the parameter
c
as introduced in Eq. (
1
). Resonant interaction between the units and cavity could be implemented by choosing adequately the cavity mode. Moreover, projective energy measurements of the cavity and the charging atoms have been successfully implemented
Sayrin et al. 2011
and could be used to monitor the charging process. In the experimental setup of Ref.
Sayrin et al. 2011
, a cavity with an inverse photon loss rate
1/\kappa\approx 65\,
ms was made to collide with Rydberg atoms at intervals of
t_{0}=82\,\mu
s. This corresponds to a damping factor of
\gamma\approx 1.3\cdot 10^{-3}
between consecutive collisions, which is sufficiently low to support an observable quantum advantage in our model.

Superconducting platforms are also promising for demonstrating this quantum advantage. There, a microwave resonator would play the role of the battery, whereas a superconducting qubit repeatedly reinitialized in a coherent or incoherent state would play the role of the charging units. The resonant interaction between resonator and superconducting does correspond to flip-flop type interaction in the dispersive limit. As an example, we can cite a recent experiment Ref.
Linpeng et al. 2022
, where a cavity with coherence time 100
\mu
s is coupled to a superconducting qubit. The cavity coherence time does include the cavity dissipation rate
\kappa/2\pi\sim 0.5
MHz. Initialization and interaction times are estimated to be of the order of
10^{-1}\mu
s. This leads to a rate
\gamma
exceeding the value for which we predict a quantum advantage but technological advances in this field are promising. The coherence time of 100
\mu
s would allow for simulating
\sim 100
charging units.

IV
Large spin battery

We will now discuss coherent and incoherent charging of a large spin battery.
Following the previous section on suitable experimental platforms,
in this case we
bring the attention of the reader to recent hybrid experiments manipulating magnons, either within a cavity QED setup
Zhang et al. 2015
or within circuit QED circuits
Wolski et al. 2020
. Magnons are quanta of collective spin excitations considering ordered magnetic phases. In Ref.
Wolski et al. 2020
, they manipulate a YIG-crystal (yttrium-iron-garnet) magnetized by a uniform magnetic field. This leads to uniform magnetostatic mode (Kittel mode), which is coupled coherently with a superconducting transmon-type qubit. This constitutes the basic ingredients necessary for the charging protocol we propose, which we discuss in detail in what follows.

We assume a large spin battery, with half-integer or integer quantum number
j
and dimension
2j+1
. Its free Hamiltonian is given by the axial spin operator,
\hat{H}_{B}=E\hat{J}_{z}=E\sum_{m=-j}^{j}m|j,m\rangle
, with eigenvectors
|j,m\rangle
. As before, the battery starts in the ground state
|j,-m\rangle
and gets charged by a sequence of resonant qubits with
\hat{H}_{Q}=E|e\rangle\langle e|
, prepared in the state (
1
). In each collision, the qubit and the battery interact for a time
t_{k}
through the energy-preserving exchange interaction Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\Omega\left(\hat{J}_{-}\otimes|e\rangle\langle g|+\hat{J}_{+}\otimes|g\rangle\langle e|\right)

(22)

where the ladder angular momentum operators are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\pm}\ket{j,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(j,m)\ket{j,m\pm 1},

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(j,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm 1))}.

(23)

We follow along the lines of Sec.
III
and first derive a non-tight upper bound for adaptive incoherent charging protocols, we introduce the deterministic full-swap protocol, and finally we show the coherent advantage.

IV.1
Bound on the incoherent charging power

Starting from the ground state,
\rho_{B}(0)=|j,-m\rangle\langle j,-m|
, the state of the battery remains diagonal in the energy basis when charged by qubits without coherence. In full analogy with the rate equation (
9
) for the oscillator case, and calling
\theta_{k}=\Omega t_{k}
, we can write the rates for jumping one step up and down the spin ladder as

\displaystyle P_{-}(m,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q\sin^{2}\left[f_{-}(m,k)\theta_{k}\right]\;,

\displaystyle P_{+}(m,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-q)\sin^{2}\left[f_{+}(m,k)\theta_{k}\right]\;,

\displaystyle P_{0}(m,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-P_{-}(m,k)-P_{+}(m,k)\;.

(24)

Consequently, the mean energy gain of the battery in the
k
-th incoherent charge step reads as

\Delta\bar{E}(k)=E\sum_{m=-j}^{j}\left[P_{+}(m,k)-P_{-}(m,k)\right]p(m,k-1),

(25)

which is once again optimized by taking
q=0
such that
P_{-}(m,k)=0
.
Thus, in a completely analogous way as in (
13
), we can bound the transient charging rate of the spin battery by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{P}(k)}{E\Omega}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\bar{E}(k)}{E\theta_{k}}\leq R_{0}\sum_{m=-j}^{j}p(m,k-1)f_{+}(j,m)

(26)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{0}\sqrt{j(j+1)-\sum_{m=-j}^{j}p(m,k-1)m(m+1)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{0}\sqrt{j(j+1)-\tr\left\{(\hat{J}^{2}_{z}-\hat{J}_{z})\rho_{B}(k-1)\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{0}\sqrt{j(j+1)-\langle J_{z}(k-1)\rangle^{2}-\langle J_{z}(k-1)\rangle},

with
\langle J_{z}(k)\rangle=\tr\{\hat{J}_{z}\rho_{B}(k)\}=\bar{E}(k)/E
. Interpolating this expectation value linearly between successive steps, we arrive at the differential inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\leq R_{0}\Omega\sqrt{j(j+1)-\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle\left(\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle+1\right)}.

(27)

Given the initial condition
\langle J_{z}(0)\rangle=-j
, we can integrate (
27
) and obtain the inequality

\arctan\left[\frac{2\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle+1}{2f_{+}\left(j,\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle\right)}\right]-\arctan\frac{1-2j}{2}\leq R_{0}\Omega\tau.

(28)

The incoherent bound on the average charging power follows by setting both sides equal and solving for
\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle
, such that
\bar{\mathcal{P}}\leq E[\langle J_{z}(\Omega\tau)\rangle+j]/\tau
.

From (
28
), we can also estimate the time
\tau^{\ast}
it takes to fully charge a large-spin battery from
\langle J_{z}(0)\rangle=-j
to
\langle J_{z}(\tau^{\ast})\rangle=j
with an incoherent protocol,

\displaystyle\tau^{\ast}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{0}\Omega}\left[\arctan\frac{2j+1}{2}-\arctan\frac{1-2j}{2}\right]

(29)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}\frac{\arctan(\infty)-\arctan(-\infty)}{R_{0}\Omega}=\frac{\pi}{R_{0}\Omega}.

Hence for a full charge at
j\gg 1
, the minimal time no longer depends on the number of battery levels due to the
j
-scaling of the level-dependent coupling term (
IV
). Accordingly, the average power for a full incoherent charge of the battery does not exceed
\bar{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\leq 2Ej\Omega R_{0}/\pi
.

IV.2
Classical strategies

As in the oscillator case, we can consider a full-swap strategy in which we adapt the coupling times according to
\vartheta_{k}=\pi/2f_{+}(j,k)
, such that the battery is always in a pure charge state
|j,k-j\rangle
. The energy and ergotropy are equal and increase in steps of
E
. In order to completely charge the battery,
2j
such swaps must be performed, which requires the total time

\displaystyle\tau^{*}=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{k=-j}^{j-1}\frac{\pi}{2f_{+}(j,k)}\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}\frac{\pi^{2}}{2\Omega}.

(30)

At
j\gg 1
, the overall charging power
\bar{\mathcal{P}}=2Ej/\tau^{*}
is then
2/R_{0}\pi\simeq 0.88
times lower than the incoherent upper bound deduced from (
29
).

A slightly better performance is obtained by the
greedy
incoherent protocol (shown in Fig.
6
), which selects as the swap angle
\theta_{k}
in each step the local maximum of the cumulative average charging power.

IV.3
Coherent charging

The minimum full-charge time (
29
) and the associated power bound hold for all incoherent protocols, but they can be overcome with coherent qubits. As for the oscillator battery, the optimal performance is obtained with the choice
q=1/2
and
c=1
, regardless of the phase angle
\alpha
.
As an example, we compare coherent and incoherent charging protocols for a spin battery of dimension
d=2j+1=100
in Fig.

. We plot the average energy relative to the ground state,
\bar{E}(K)+Ej

in which the battery state remains pure and evolves according to

\rho_{B}(K)\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\theta\to 0}\hat{D}(\tau)|j,-j\rangle\langle j,-j|\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\tau).

(31)

Here, the unitary describes a coherent spin rotation by the angle
\Omega\tau
about the
x
-direction on the generalized Bloch sphere,

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\tau)=\exp\left[\frac{\Omega\tau}{2}\left(\hat{J}_{+}+\hat{J}_{-}\right)\right].

(32)

A complete charge of the battery corresponds to a
\pi
-rotation, which requires the time
\tau^{\star}=\pi/\Omega
and is therefore
R^{-1}_{0}\simeq 1.38
times faster than the incoherent bound (
29
), corresponding to a
38\%
quantum advantage.

V
Conclusions

Despite immense progress on our understanding of thermodynamics processes in the quantum regime
Goold et al. 2016
;
Vinjanampathy and Anders 2016
;
Mitchison 2019
;
Myers et al. 2022
, identifying quantum advantages in thermodynamics remains a challenging task. Quantum batteries offer a natural framework to explore this question, and previous efforts focused on quantum advantages of a collective nature based on entanglement
Campaioli et al. 2018
;
Bhattacharjee and Dutta 2021
;
Alicki and Fannes 2013
;
Hovhannisyan et al. 2013
;
Binder et al. 2015
;
Campaioli et al. 2017
;
Julià-Farré et al. 2020
;
Gyhm et al. 2022
. Building upon our previous work
Seah et al. 2021
, here we have shown that such quantum advantages can also appear at the level of a single battery: given a collisional model of charging, we have proven that quantum coherent charging processes can outperform arbitrary classical charging strategies. More precisely, we have considered a set of qubit units that interact sequentially with a quantum battery, given by either a quantum harmonic oscillator (thus realizing a micromaser) or a large-spin system. A quantum advantage arises in both set-ups, with
90% and 38% higher charging power through quantum resources for the micromaser and the spin batteries, respectively. We have characterised the robustness of the quantum advantage to decoherence and photon loss, and discussed its feasibility in both cavity QED
Sayrin et al. 2011
and hybrid superconducting
Linpeng et al. 2022
;
Wolski et al. 2020
set-ups. An experimental demonstration of these results is an exciting possibility that we hope can be achieved in the near future.

appeared, which is also concerned by charging an oscillator battery with a sequence of coherent qubits in a micromaser set-up. Complementary to our results, the authors focus on the regime of ultrastrong cavity-qubit coupling and include counter-rotating interaction terms to their model.
