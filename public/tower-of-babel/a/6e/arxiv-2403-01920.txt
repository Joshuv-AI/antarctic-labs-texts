Projected Newton method for large-scale Bayesian linear inverse problems Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Haibo Li

Abstract
Computing the regularized solution of Bayesian linear inverse problems as well as the corresponding regularization parameter is highly desirable in many applications. This paper proposes a novel iterative method, termed the Projected Newton method ( PNT ), that can simultaneously update the regularization parameter and solution step by step without requiring any expensive matrix inversions or decompositions. By reformulating the Tikhonov regularization as a constrained minimization problem and leveraging its Lagrangian function, a Newton-type method coupled with a Krylov subspace method is designed for the unconstrained Lagrangian function. The resulting PNT algorithm only needs solving a small-scale linear system to get a descent direction of a merit function at each iteration, thus significantly reducing computational overhead. Rigorous convergence results are proved, showing that PNT always converges to the unique regularized solution and the corresponding Lagrangian multiplier. Experimental results on both small and large-scale Bayesian inverse problems demonstrate its excellent convergence property, robustness and efficiency. Given that the most demanding computational tasks in PNT are primarily matrix-vector products, it is particularly well-suited for large-scale problems.

Introduction

Inverse problems arise in various scientific and engineering fields, where the aim is to recover unknown parameters or functions from noisy observed data. Applications include image reconstruction, computed tomography, medical imaging, geoscience, data assimilation and so on
[
28
,
25
,
6
,
30
,
49
]
. A linear inverse problem of the discrete form can be written as

\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(1.1)

where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the underlying quantity to reconstruct,
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the discretized forward model matrix,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the vector of observation with noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. We assume that the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is known, which follows a zero mean Gaussian distribution with positive definite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{M})
. A big challenge for reconstructing a good solution is the ill-posedness of inverse problems, which means that there may be multiple solutions that fit the observation equally well, or the solution is very sensitive with respect to observation perturbation.

To overcome the ill-posedness, regularization is a commonly used technique. From a Bayesian perspective
[
28
,
53
]
, this corresponds to adding a prior distribution of the desired solution to constrain the set of possible solutions to improve stability and uniqueness. By treating
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
as random variables, the observation vector
\boldsymbol{b}
has a conditional probability density function (pdf) of the form

p(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{x})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}\right).

To get a regularized solution, this paper considers a Gaussian prior about the desired solution with the form
\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mu^{-1}\boldsymbol{N})
, where
\boldsymbol{N}
is a positive definite covariance matrix. Then the Bayes’ formula leads to

p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{b},\lambda)\propto p(\boldsymbol{x}|\lambda)p(\boldsymbol{b}|\boldsymbol{x})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\right),

where
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{B}}:=(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x})^{1/2}
is the
\boldsymbol{B}
-norm of
\boldsymbol{x}
for a positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{B}
. Maximize the posterior pdf
p(\boldsymbol{x}|\boldsymbol{b},\lambda)
leads to the Tikhonov regularization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+\mu\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\},

(1.2)

where the regularization term
\mu\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}
enforces extra structure on the solution that comes from the prior distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}
.

The parameter
\mu
in the Gaussian prior
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mu^{-1}\boldsymbol{N})
is crucial for obtaining a good regularized solution, which controls the trade-off between the data-fit term and regularization term. There is tremendous effort in determining a proper value of
\mu
. For the standard 2-norm problem, i.e.
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{I}
and
\boldsymbol{N}=\boldsymbol{I}
, the classical parameter-selection methods include the L-curve criterion
[
23
]
, generalized cross-validation
[
22
]
, unbiased predictive risk estimation
[
47
]
and discrepancy principle
[
40
]
. There are also some iterative methods based on solving a nonlinear equation of
\mu
; see e.g.
[
37
,
2
,
46
,
20
]
. However, the aforementioned methods can not be directly applied to
eq.

1.2
. A common procedure needs to first transform
eq.

1.2
into standard 2-norm form

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}\},

(1.3)

where
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{L}_{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
are the Cholesky factorizations, and then apply the parameter-selection methods. This procedure needs the matrix inversions of
\boldsymbol{M}
and
\boldsymbol{N}
as well as the Cholesky factorizations of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
. For large-scale matrices, these two types of computations are almost impossible or extremely expensive.

For large-scale problems, there exist some iterative regularization methods that can avoid choosing
\mu
in advance. A class of commonly used iterative methods is based on Krylov subspace
[
35
]
, where the original linear system is projected onto lower-dimensional subspaces to become a series of small-scale problems
[
42
,
19
,
27
,
33
]
. For dealing with the general-form Tikhonov regularization term
\|\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{2}^{2}
, some recent Krylov iterative methods include
[
39
,
29
,
45
,
26
,
34
]
and so on. When the Cholesky factor
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
is not accessible, a key difficulty is dealing with the prior covariance
\boldsymbol{N}
, which means that the subspaces should be constructed elaborately such that the prior information of
\boldsymbol{x}
can be effectively incorporated into these subspaces
[
8
,
32
]
. Such methods have been proposed in
[
9
,
7
,
8
]
, where a statistically inspired priorconditioning technique is used to whiten the noise and the desired solution. However, these methods still require large-scale matrix inversions and Cholesky factorizations, which prohibits their applications to large-scale problems.

Recently, there are several Krylov methods for directly solving
eq.

1.1
without choosing
\mu
in advance and can avoid the matrix inversions and Cholesky factorizations
[
12
,
32
]
. These methods use the generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization (
gen-GKB
), which can iteratively reduce the original large-scale problem to small-scale ones and generate Krylov subspaces that effectively incorporate the prior information of
\boldsymbol{x}
encoded by
\boldsymbol{N}
. In
[
32
]
, the regularization effect of the proposed method comes from early stopping the iteration, where the iteration number plays the role of the regularization parameter, while in
[
12
]
, the authors proposed a hybrid regularization method that simultaneously computes the regularized parameter and solution step by step. Although these two methods are very efficient for large-scale problems, there may be some issues in certain situations. The method in
[
32
]
only computes a good regularized solution but not a good
\mu
. However, in some applications, we need an accurate estimate of
\mu
to get the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{x}
for sampling and uncertainty quantification
[
53
,
52
,
17
]
. For the hybrid method in
[
12
]
, the convergence property does not have a solid theoretical foundation, and it has been numerically found that the method sometimes does not converge to a good solution, which is a common potential flaw for hybrid methods
[
11
,
48
]
.

Many optimization methods have been proposed for inverse problems, particularly those stemming from image processing that leads to total variation regularization or
\ell_{p}
regularization. These methods include the Bregman iteration
[
43
,
56
,
21
]
, iterative shrinkage thresholding
[
15
,
3
]
, and many others
[
1
,
54
,
36
]
. However, these methods either need a good parameter
\mu
in advance or can not well deal with
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
. In
[
31
]
the author proposed a modification of the Newton method that can iteratively compute a good
\mu
and regularized solution simultaneously. However, this method needs to solve a large-scale linear system at each iteration, which is very costly for large-scale problems. This method was improved in
[
13
,
14
]
, where the Newton method is successfully combined with a Krylov subspace method to get a so-called projected Newton method. Compared with the original method, the projected Newton method only needs to solve a small-scale linear system at each iteration, thereby very efficient for large-scale Tikhonov regularization
eq.

1.3
. However, for solving
eq.

1.2
, this method needs to compute
\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2})=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}
to construct subspaces, which is also very costly. Besides, their methods lack rigorous proof of convergence.

In this paper, we develop a new efficient iterative method for
eq.

1.2
that simultaneously updates the regularization parameter and solution step by step, and it does not require any expensive matrix inversions or Cholesky factorizations. This method follows the Newton-type approach for noise constrained Tikhonov regularization proposed in
[
13
]
, where the
gen-GKB
process is integrated to compute a projected Newton direction by solving a small-scale linear system at each iteration, thereby it is also named the
projected Newton method
(
PNT
). The main contributions of this paper are listed as follows:

•

We reformulate the regularization of the original Bayesian linear inverse problem as a noise constrained minimization problem and prove the existence, uniqueness and positivity of its Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda
under a very reasonable assumption. The correspondence between the constrained minimization problem and Tikhonov regularization
eq.

1.2
is connected by
\mu=1/\lambda
.

•

We propose a
gen-GKB
based Newton-type method to compute the regularized solution by optimizing its Lagrangian function and obtaining the corresponding Lagrangian multiplier. A series of Krylov subspaces is generated by
gen-GKB
, avoiding the need for costly matrix inversions or Cholesky factorizations. Using the subspace projection technique, we only need to solve a small-scale linear system to compute the descent direction at each iteration.

•

A rigorous proof of convergence for the proposed method is provided. With a very practical initialization
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\lambda_{0})
, we prove that
PNT
always converges to the unique solution of the constrained minimization problem and the corresponding Lagrangian multiplier.

We use both small-scale and large-scale inverse problems to test the proposed method and compare it with other state-of-the-art methods. The experimental results demonstrate excellent convergence properties of
PNT
, and it is very robust and efficient for regularizing Bayesian linear inverse problems. Since the most computationally intensive operations in
PNT
primarily involve matrix-vector products, it is especially appropriate for large-scale problems.

eq.

1.1
and study its properties. In
Section

3
, we propose the
PNT
method. In
Section

4
, we prove the convergence of
PNT
. Numerical results are presented in
Section

and conclusions are provided in
Section

.

2
Noise constrained minimization for Bayesian inverse problems

In order to get a good estimate of
\mu
in
eq.

1.2
, the discrepancy principle (DP) criterion is commonly used, which depends on the variance of the noise. Based on DP, we can rewrite
eq.

1.2
as an equivalent form of noise constrained minimization problem.

2.1
Noise constrained minimization

If
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I})
is a white Gaussian noise, the DP criterion states that the
2
-norm discrepancy between the data and predicted output
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}({\mu})-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}
should be of the order of
\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|_{2}\approx\sqrt{m}\sigma
, where
\boldsymbol{x}(\mu)
is the solution to
eq.

1.2
; see
[
28
, §5.6]
. If
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a general Gaussian noise, notice that
eq.

1.1
leads to

\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

and
\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{I})
, thereby this transformation whitens the noise. Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}:=\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a white Gaussian noise with zero mean and covariance
\boldsymbol{I}
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{trace}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{trace}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\right)\right]=\mathrm{trace}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\top}\right]\right)=\mathrm{trace}\left(\boldsymbol{I}\right)=m.

Therefore, the DP for
eq.

1.1
can be written as

\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}({\mu})-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}({\mu})-\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{2}=\tau m,

(2.1)

where
\tau
is chosen to be marginally greater than
1
, such as
\tau=1.01
.

Using this expression of DP, we rewrite the regularization of
eq.

1.1
as the noise constrained minimization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\ \ \ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \ \ \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{\tau m}{2},

(2.2)

where its Lagrangian is

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\tau m\right)

(2.3)

with
\lambda\geq 0
the Lagrangian multiplier. To further investigate
eqs.

2.2
and
2.3
, we first state the following basic assumption, which is used throughout the paper.

Assumption 1
.

For all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\min\}
, it holds

\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}<\tau m<\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}.

(2.4)

The first inequality means that the naive solutions to (
1.1
) fit the observation very well, and it ensures the feasible set of
eq.

2.2
is nonempty. The second inequality comes from the condition
\|L_{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|_{2}<\|L_{\boldsymbol{M}}\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}
, meaning that the noise does not dominate the observation, which ensures the effectiveness of the regularization. Under this assumption, the following result describes the solution to
eq.

2.2
.

Theorem 1
.

The noise constrained minimization
eq.

2.2
has a unique solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
satisfying
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\tau m
. Furthermore, there is a unique
\lambda^{*}>0
, which is the Lagrangian multiplier corresponding to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
in
eq.

2.3
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{2}(\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\tau m)
, which is a convex function. In
eq.

2.2
we seek solutions to
\min\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}
in the feasible set
S:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0\}
, which is the
0
-lower level set of
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})
. Note that
S
is a compact and convex set and
\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}
is continuous and strictly convex. Thus, there is a unique solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
to
eq.

2.2
.
Suppose
\lambda^{*}
is a Lagrangian multiplier corresponding to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
. By the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) condition
[
41
, §12.3]
, the solution
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}+\lambda^{*}\nabla\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=\mathbf{0},\\
\lambda^{*}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=0,\\
\lambda^{*}\geq 0.\end{cases}

If
\lambda^{*}=0
, then
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, leading to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\mathbf{0}
. This means
\mathbf{0}\in S
, i.e.
\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\tau m
, a contradiction. Consequently, it must hold
\lambda^{*}>0
. From the relation
\lambda^{*}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=0
we have
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=0
, i.e.
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\tau m
.

For the uniqueness of
\lambda^{*}
, here we give two proofs. In the first proof, we note that
\nabla\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}-\boldsymbol{b})\neq\mathbf{0}
since
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\notin\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\min\}
by
Assumption

1
. Therefore, the linear independence constraint qualification (LICQ) holds at
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
, which leads to the uniqueness of
\lambda^{*}
; see
[
41
, §12.3]
.

In the second proof, we note that for any
\lambda\geq 0
, there is a unique
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
that solves the first equality of the KKT condition:

\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\nabla\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{0}\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ (\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A})x=\lambda\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b},

(2.5)

since
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}+\lambda\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}
is positive definite. Here we prove a stronger property:
there exist a unique
\lambda\geq 0
such that
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\tau m
. The existence of such a
\lambda
has been proved, since
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda^{*}}
. For the uniqueness, define two functions

K(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2},\ \ \ H(\lambda):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\tau m\right).

Note that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
is strictly convex for a fixed
\lambda>0
, which has the unique minimizer
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
. Thus, for any two positive
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
, we have

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{1}},\lambda_{1})<\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{2}},\lambda_{1})\Leftrightarrow K(\lambda_{1})+\lambda_{1}H(\lambda_{1})<K(\lambda_{2})+\lambda_{1}H(\lambda_{2}),

since
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{1}}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{2}}
; see the following
Lemma

2
. Similarly, we have

K(\lambda_{2})+\lambda_{2}H(\lambda_{2})<K(\lambda_{1})+\lambda_{2}H(\lambda_{1}).

Adding the above two inequalities leads to

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(H(\lambda_{1})-H(\lambda_{2}))<0
,
meaning that
H(\lambda)
is a strictly monotonic decreasing function. Therefore, there is a unique
\lambda
such that
H(\lambda)=0
.

We emphasize that
Assumption

1
is essential for ensuring the validity of
Theorem

1
and plays a key role in the regularization of
eq.

1.1
. If the left inequality of
Assumption

1
is violated, then either the feasible set of
eq.

2.2
is empty when
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}>\tau m
or it becomes the equivalent least squares problem

\min\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\ \ \ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \ \ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\min

when
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\tau m
.
The latter case means that no regularization is required, which makes the problem much easier to handle. The right inequality of
Assumption

1
ensures the positivity of the Lagrangian multiplier
\lambda^{*}
, which is a necessary condition. To see it, let us assume there exist a
\lambda^{*}>0
and follow the second proof for the uniqueness of
\lambda^{*}
. From the KKT condition it must hold that
\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda^{*}})=H(\lambda^{*})=0
. Now
H(0)=\frac{1}{2}(\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\tau m)\leq 0
and
H(+\infty)=\frac{1}{2}(\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{M^{-1}}^{2}-\tau m)<0
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}
. Since
H(\lambda)
is strictly monotonically decreasing, the only possible zero root of
H(\lambda)
is
\lambda=0
, which leads to a contradiction. In this case, it implies that the noise in
b
is too large, resulting in
\lambda^{*}=0
and a very poor regularized solution
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\mathbf{0}
.

Lemma 2
.

For each
\lambda\geq 0
, the regularization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\lambda\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2}\}

(2.6)

has the unique solution
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
. If
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{1}}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{2}}
.

Proof.

Note that the normal equation of
eq.

2.6
is equivalent to
eq.

2.5
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
is the unique solution to
eq.

2.6
. Using the Cholesky factors of
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
, and noticing that
\boldsymbol{L}_{N}
is invertible, we can write the generalized singular value decomposition (GSVD)
[
55
]
of
\{\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\}
as

\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}_{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1},\ \boldsymbol{L}_{N}=\boldsymbol{U}_{N}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{N}\boldsymbol{Z}^{-1}

with

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{A}=\vtop{\hbox{$\left(\vbox{\vbox{\halign{ \hfil$#$\hfil\kern 2\p@\kern\@leftbwd& \thinspace\hfil$#$\hfil&& \quad\hfil$#$\hfil\cr\boldsymbol{D}_{A}$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&&r\crcr\kern 2.0pt\cr$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&\boldsymbol{0}&m-r\cr r$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&n-r\crcr}}}\kern 0.0pt\right)\kern-6.66669pt\kern 0.0pt$}\kern 44.86485pt},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{N}=\vtop{\hbox{$\left(\vbox{\vbox{\halign{ \hfil$#$\hfil\kern 2\p@\kern\@leftbwd& \thinspace\hfil$#$\hfil&& \quad\hfil$#$\hfil\cr\boldsymbol{D}_{N}$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&&r\crcr\kern 2.0pt\cr$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&\boldsymbol{I}&n-r\cr r$\hfil\kern 2.0pt\kern 6.66669pt&n-r\crcr}}}\kern 0.0pt\right)\kern-6.66669pt\kern 0.0pt$}\kern 44.86485pt},

where
\boldsymbol{U}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are orthogonal,
\boldsymbol{Z}=(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{n})
in nonsingular,
r=\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{A})
, and
\boldsymbol{D}_{A}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r})
with
1>\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}>0
and
\boldsymbol{D}_{N}=\mathrm{diag}(\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{r})
with
0<\rho_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{r}<1
, such that
\sigma_{i}^{2}+\rho_{i}^{2}=1
. Then
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda\sigma_{i}}{\lambda\sigma_{i}^{2}+\rho_{i}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}_{M}\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{z}_{i}

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{A,i}
is the
i
-th column of
\boldsymbol{U}_{A}
. Since
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
are linear independent, if
\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{1}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda_{2}}
, then it must hold

\frac{\lambda_{1}\sigma_{i}}{\lambda_{1}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\rho_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\lambda_{2}\sigma_{i}}{\lambda_{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}+\rho_{i}^{2}}\ \ \Leftrightarrow\ \ (\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\sigma_{i}\rho_{i}^{2}=0,\ \ i=1,\dots,r.

Since
\sigma_{i}\rho_{i}>0
for
i=1,\dots,r
, we obtain
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}
.

Remark 1
.

From the proof of
Theorem

1
, we find that
\lambda
plays the role of
\mu^{-1}
in

1.2
, meaning that
\boldsymbol{x}({\mu})=\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda}
if
\lambda=\mu^{-1}
. In fact, there is a one-to-one correspondence between
eq.

1.2
and
eq.

2.2
. Note that
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\lambda^{*}}
. Comparing
eq.

2.6
with

1.2
, we can use
(\lambda^{*})^{-1}
as a good estimate of the optimal regularization parameter.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{+}=[0,\infty)
. Write the gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
as

F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\\
\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{\tau m}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.7)

Then
F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
has a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, which is the unique minimizer and corresponding Lagrangian multiplier of
eq.

2.2
.

2.2
Newton method

A modification of the Newton method was proposed in
[
31
]
to solve the nonlinear equation
F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
, which is referred to as the Lagrange method since it is based on the Lagrangian of
eq.

2.2
. In this method, the Jacobian matrix of
F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
is first computed as

J(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})\\
(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}&0\end{pmatrix}

(2.8)

at the current iterate
(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
, and then it computes the Newton direction
(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},\Delta\lambda)^{\top}
by solving inexactly the linear system

J(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}\\
\Delta\lambda\end{pmatrix}=-F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

(2.9)

using the MINRES solver
[
44
]
. We remark that this method is essentially a Newton-Krylov method
[
5
]
for optimizing the nonlinear and nonconvex Lagrangian function
eq.

2.3
. It was shown that the computed
(\Delta\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\lambda)
is a descent direction for the merit function

h_{w}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\|_{2}^{2}+w|\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)|^{2}\right)

with a
w>0
, which means that
(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}^{\top},\Delta\lambda)\nabla h_{w}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\leq 0
.
By a backtracking line search strategy to determine a step length
\gamma>0
, the iterate is updated as
(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\leftarrow(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)+\gamma(\Delta\boldsymbol{x},\Delta\lambda)
.

An advantage of this method is that it can compute a good regularized solution and its regularization parameter simultaneously. However, for large-scale problems, we need to compute
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
to form
F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
and
J(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
, which is almost impossible. Moreover, at each iteration, an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
linear system (
2.9
) needs to be solved, which is very computationally expensive even if we only compute a less accurate solution by an iterative algorithm.

In
[
13
]
, the authors proposed a projected Newton method, where at each iteration, the large-scale linear system (
2.9
) is projected to be a small-scale linear system that can be solved cheaply. However, this method can only deal with the standard
\ell_{2}-\ell_{2}
regularization, which means we can only apply this method to (
1.3
) by the substitution
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{L}_{N}\boldsymbol{x}
, requiring the expensive Cholesky factorization of
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
. A generalization of this method
[
14
]
can deal with a general-form regularization term. However, for (
2.2
), it needs to compute
\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}^{2})=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}x
to construct subspace for projecting (
2.9
), also very costly.

3
Projected Newton method based on generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization

To reduce expensive computations of the Newton method for large-scale problems, we design a new projected Newton method to solve
eq.

2.2
. This method uses the generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization (
gen-GKB
) to construct Krylov subspaces to compute projected Newton directions by only solving small-scale problems, and it does not need any expensive matrix inversions or decompositions. This method is composed by the following three main steps:

Step 1:

Construct Krylov subspaces.
We adopt
gen-GKB
to iteratively construct a series of low-dimensional Krylov subspaces; see
Algorithm

1
.

Step 2:

Compute the projected Newton direction.
At each iteration, we compute the projected Newton direction by solving a small-scale problem; see
eq.

3.12
.

Step 3:

Determine the step-length to update solution.
We use the Armijo backtracking line search to determine a step-length and update the solution; see
Routine

1
.

In the next subsection, we present detailed derivations of the whole algorithm. All the proofs can be found in
Section

3.2
.

3.1
Derivation of projected Newton method

This subsection presents detailed derivations for the above three steps.

Step 1: Construct Krylov subspaces by gen-GKB.

The
gen-GKB
process has been proposed for solving Bayesian linear inverse problems in
[
12
,
32
]
. The basic idea is to treat
\boldsymbol{A}
as the compact linear operator

\boldsymbol{A}:(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})\to(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}),\ \boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x},

where
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
are vectors under the canonical bases of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, respectively. Here the two inner products are defined as

\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}:=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
and

\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}:=\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}.

Therefore, we can define

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}:(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}),\ \boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y},

which is the adjoint operator of
\boldsymbol{A}
, by the relation
\langle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}
. Note that
(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Thus, the matrix-form expression of
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
is

\boldsymbol{A}^{*}=\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}.

Applying the standard Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization (GKB) to the compact operator
\boldsymbol{A}
with starting vector
\boldsymbol{b}
between the two Hilbert spaces
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}})
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}})
, we can obtain the
gen-GKB
process; see
[
10
]
for GKB for compact operators. The basic recursive relations of
gen-GKB
are as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{b},

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}-\beta_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i-1},

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\beta_{i+1}\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}-\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i},

(3.1c)

where
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are computed such that
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\|\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=1
, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}:=\boldsymbol{0}
. The whole iterative process is summarized in
Algorithm

1
. For more details of the derivation, please see
[
32
]
.

We remark that computing with
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
can not be avoided, but for the most commonly encountered cases that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a Gaussian noise with uncorrelated components,
\boldsymbol{M}
is diagonal and thereby
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
can be directly obtained. For applications that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a colored Gaussian noise such that
\boldsymbol{M}
is not diagonal, computing
\boldsymbol{M}
is the most expensive operation. In these cases, the proposed
PNT
method based on
gen-GKB
may not be the optimal choice.

,

The following result gives the basic property of
gen-GKB
; see
[
32
]
for the proof.

Proposition 4
.

The group of vectors
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is an
\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1},\boldsymbol{b})=\mathrm{span}\{(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1})^{i}\boldsymbol{b}\}_{i=0}^{k-1},

(3.2)

and
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
is an
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-orthonormal basis of the Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}_{k}(\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b})=\mathrm{span}\{(\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A})^{i}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}\}_{i=0}^{k-1}.

(3.3)

Define
\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{k+1})
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1})
. Then
Proposition

4
indicates that
\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{I}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{I}
. We remark that
gen-GKB
will eventually terminate in at most
\min\{m,n\}
steps, since the column rank of
\boldsymbol{U}_{k}
or
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
can not exceed
\min\{m,n\}
. If we define the
termination step
as

k_{t}:=\max\{k:\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}>0\},

(3.4)

then
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
will eventually expand to be
\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}
with
k_{t}\leq\min\{m,n\}
.

By
eq.

3.1a
–
eq.

3.1c
, we can write the
k
-step (
k\leq k_{t}
)
gen-GKB
in the matrix-form

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}e_{1}=\boldsymbol{b},

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k},

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}+\alpha_{k+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{k+1}e_{k+1}^{\top},

(3.5c)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{k+1}
are the first and
(k+1)
-th columns of the identity matrix of order
k+1
, respectively, and

\boldsymbol{B}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&&&\\
\beta_{2}&\alpha_{2}&&\\
&\beta_{3}&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\alpha_{k}\\
&&&\beta_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}.

(3.6)

Note that
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
has full column rank if
k\leq k_{t}
. At the
k_{t}
-th iteration, it is possible that either
\beta_{k_{t}+1}=0
occurs first or
\alpha_{k_{t}+1}=0
occurs first. For the former case, the relations
eq.

3.5
are replaced by

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k_{t}}e_{1}=\boldsymbol{b},

(3.7a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}=\boldsymbol{U}_{k_{t}}\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k_{t}},

(3.7b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k_{t}}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k_{t}}^{\top},

(3.7c)

where
\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k_{t}}
is the first
k\times k
part of
\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}
by discarding
\beta_{k_{t}+1}
.

Step 2: Compute the projected Newton direction.

At the
k
-th iteration, we update
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\in\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
and
\lambda_{k}
from the previous ones. For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
of the form
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
with
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, define the projected gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
as

F^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}&\\
&1\end{pmatrix}F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

(3.8)

and the projected Jacobian of
F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
as

J^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}&\\
&1\end{pmatrix}J(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}&\\
&1\end{pmatrix}.

(3.9)

Remark 2
.

Since
gen-GKB
must terminate at the
k_{t}
-th iteration and
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
eventually expands to be
\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}
, we need to discuss the two different cases that
k\leq k_{t}
and
k>k_{t}
. For notational simplicity, in the rest part of the paper, we use
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
by default unless stated otherwise to denote

\boldsymbol{V}_{k}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{V}_{k},\ \ k\leq k_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}},\ \ k>k_{t}\end{cases},\ \ \boldsymbol{B}_{k}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{B}_{k},\ \ k\leq k_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}},\ \ k>k_{t}\end{cases},\ \ \boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\ \ k\leq k_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\ \ k>k_{t}\end{cases},

where
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for
k\leq k_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{t}}
for
k>k_{t}
. Moreover, for the case
\beta_{k_{t}+1}=0
, the relations
eq.

3.5
are replaced by
eq.

3.7
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}
is replaced by
\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k_{t}}
. In the subsequent discussions, we employ the unified notations as presented in
eq.

3.5
, but the readers can readily differentiate between the two cases.

Notice that
\boldsymbol{y}
is uniquely determined by
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
since
\boldsymbol{V}_{k}
has full-column rank. Thus,
F^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)
and
J^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)
are well-defined. The next result shows how we can obtain
F^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)
and
J^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)
from
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
without any additional computations.

Lemma 5
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\}
with the form
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}
, the projected gradient of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
has the expression

F^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1})+\boldsymbol{y}\\
\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\tau m}{2}\end{pmatrix},

(3.10)

and the projected Jacobian of
F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
has the expression

J^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}+\boldsymbol{I}&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1})\\
(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(3.11)

Now we can compute the projected Newton direction for updating the solution. Starting from an initial solution
(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\lambda_{0})
, consider the following two cases.

Case 1: Update
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
from
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
for
k\leq k_{t}
.

Suppose at the
(k-1)
-th iteration, we have
\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k-1}\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}:=\mathbf{0}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}:=()
is an empty vector. Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}=(\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}^{\top},0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. If
J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
is nonsingular, we compute the Newton direction for the projected function
F^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)
at
(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
:

\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\\
\Delta\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}=-J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})^{-1}F^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1}).

(3.12a)

Then we update
(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k},\lambda_{k})
by

\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}+\gamma_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\ \ \ \lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k-1}+\gamma_{k}\Delta\lambda_{k}

(3.12b)

with a suitably chosen step-length
\gamma_{k}>0
, and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
.

Case 2: Update
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
from
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
for
k>k_{t}
.

At each iteration, we seek a solution of the form
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
with
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{t}}
. We compute the Newton direction

\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\\
\Delta\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}=-J^{(k_{t})}(\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})^{-1}F^{(k_{t})}(\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\lambda_{k-1}),

(3.12c)

and then compute

\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}+\gamma_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\ \ \ \lambda_{k}=\lambda_{k-1}+\gamma_{k}\Delta\lambda_{k}

(3.12d)

to get
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
.

For both the two cases, we call
(\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k},\Delta\lambda_{k})
the
projected Newton direction
, since it is the Newton direction of a projected problem. The corresponding update formula for
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
is

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}+\gamma_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\ \ \ \Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k},

which is easy to be verified.
For notational simplicity, in the subsequent part we always use the unified notation

\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}=\begin{cases}(\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}^{\top},\ 0)^{\top},\ \ k\leq k_{t}\\
\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ k>k_{t}\end{cases}

(3.13)

for
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}
. Following the notations stated in
2
and
3.13
, we can use
eq.

3.12a
and
eq.

3.12b
to describe the update procedure for both the two cases.

It is vital to make sure that the projected Jacobian matrix
J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
is always nonsingular. This desired property is given in the following result. The proof appears as a part of the proof of
Lemma

17
.

Proposition 6
.

If we choose
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}=0
, then at each iteration
J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
is nonsingular as long as
\lambda_{k-1}\geq 0
.

In order to investigate the convergence behavior of the method, define the following merit function:

h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}[\|\lambda\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{\tau m}{2})^{2}].

(3.14)

Notice from
Corollary

3
that
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
is the unique minimizer of
h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
and that
h(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})=0
. The following result shows that
(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k})^{\top}
is indeed a descent direction for
h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
. Then it holds

\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\\
\Delta\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}=-2h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\leq 0.

(3.15)

Theorem

7
is a desired property for a gradient descent type algorithm. At the
(k-1)
-th iteration, if
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})=0
, then we have
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})=(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
, meaning we have obtained the unique solution to
eq.

2.2
. Otherwise,
(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k})^{\top}
is a descent direction of
h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
at
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
, thereby we can continue updating the solution by a backtracking line search strategy.

Step 3: Determine step-length by backtracking line search.

For the case that
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\neq 0
, we need to determine a step-length
\gamma_{k}
such that
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
decreases strictly. To this end, we use the backtracking line search procedure to ensure that the
Armijo condition

[
41
, §3.1]
is satisfied:

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\leq h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})+c\gamma_{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k})\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1}),

(3.16)

where
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})+\gamma_{k}(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\Delta\lambda_{k})
, and
c\in(0,1)
is a fixed constant. At each iteration, we can quickly compute
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
based on the following result.

Lemma 8
.

Let

\bar{F}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{y},\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k}^{\top}(\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1})+y\\
\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\tau m}{2}\end{pmatrix},\ \ \ \bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&&&\\
\beta_{2}&\alpha_{2}&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\beta_{k+1}&\alpha_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{d})\|_{2}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\boldsymbol{d})+\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|_{2}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|_{2}=0,

since
\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\boldsymbol{d})=\mathbf{0}
. Now we have
\|\bar{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0
since
\boldsymbol{C}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}
is positive definite and all norms of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are equivalent. Therefore, we have
\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|_{2}\rightarrow 0
or the equivalent form
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Lemma 14
.

If the unique solution to
\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{t}}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}y-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}
is
\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}:=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}
is the unique solution to

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}\ \ \ \mathrm{s.t.}\ \ \ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\mathrm{min}.

(4.3)

Proof.

It is easy to verify that both
\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{t}}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}y-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}
and
eq.

4.3
have a unique solution. A vector
\boldsymbol{x}
is the unique solution to
eq.

4.3
if and only if

\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})=\mathbf{0},\ \ \ \boldsymbol{x}\perp_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{A}),

where
\perp_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}
means the orthogonality relation under the
\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}
-inner product.
Now we verify the above two conditions for
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}
. For the first condition, using the relations
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}=\boldsymbol{U}_{k_{t}+1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}
and
eq.

3.5c
, we get

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}-\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k_{t}+1}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}-\beta_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}+\alpha_{k_{t}+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{k_{t}+1}e_{k_{t}+1}^{\top})\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}-\beta_{1}e_{1})+\alpha_{k_{t}+1}\beta_{k_{t}+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{k_{t}+1}e_{k_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

since
\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}-\beta_{1}e_{1})=\mathbf{0}
and
\alpha_{k_{t}+1}\beta_{k_{t}+1}=0
. For the second condition, by
Proposition

4
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}\in\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{span}\{(\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A})^{i}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}\}_{i=0}^{k_{t}-1}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})=\boldsymbol{N}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{A})^{\perp}.

Write
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}=\boldsymbol{N}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathrm{min}}
with
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathrm{min}}\in\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{A})^{\perp}
. For any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{A})
, we have

\langle\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=\langle\boldsymbol{N}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathrm{min}},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}=\langle\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\mathrm{min}},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle_{2}=0.

Therefore, it holds that
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}\perp_{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{A})
.

Lemma 15
.

There exist a positive constant
C_{1}
such that for any
k\geq 1
,

\|\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b})\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}=\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})\|_{2}\geq C_{1}>0

(4.4)

Proof.

First, we get from
eq.

3.19
the first identity:

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \|\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b})\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\left((\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{N}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\right)\|_{2}^{2}.

Then, we prove

\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{\tau m}

(4.5)

by mathematical induction. For
k=1
, we have
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}>\sqrt{\tau m}
since
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
. Suppose
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}\geq\sqrt{\tau m}
for
k\geq 1
. We have

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}+\gamma_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k})-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+2\gamma_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-b)^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}+2\gamma_{k}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y},

since

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-b)^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}.

Writing
J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\\
\Delta\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}=-F^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
in the matrix form
and using
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{\tau m}
, we get from the second equality of the above equation that

(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\tau m\right)\leq 0.

Since
\gamma_{k}\leq 1
, we get

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}-\left(\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\tau m\right)

\displaystyle=\tau m+\gamma_{k}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}^{2}\geq\tau m.

Therefore, we prove
eq.

4.5
.

To obtain the lower bound in
eq.

4.4
, we investigate two cases:
k<k_{t}
and
k\geq k_{t}
.

Case 1:
k<k_{t}
.
For this case, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\right)\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k})\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k_{t}})\sqrt{\tau m}>0,

where
\sigma_{\min}(\cdot)
is the smallest singular value of a matrix, and
\underline{\boldsymbol{B}}_{k_{t}}
is the first
k_{t}\times k_{t}
part of
\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}
.

Case 2:
k\geq k_{t}
.
For this case, we can write
\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}
as
\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}
. Remember that
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}=\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}
if
k>k_{t}
. We first prove
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})\|_{2}\neq 0
. If it is not true, then
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}
. By
Lemma

14
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}
is the solution to
eq.

4.3
. Thus, it must hold that
\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}<\sqrt{\tau m}
by
Assumption

1
, which contradicts
eq.

4.5
. Now suppose the lower bound in
eq.

4.4
is not true. Then there exists a subsequence
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k_{j}-1}\}
with
k_{j}\geq k_{t}
such that

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k_{j}-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})\|_{2}=0.

By
Lemma

13
, we have

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k_{j}-1}=\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}:=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2},

leading to

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}-1}=\lim_{k_{j}\rightarrow\infty}\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k_{j}-1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}.

It follows from
Lemma

14
that
\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathrm{min}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}
, which is the solution to
eq.

4.3
. Therefore, it must hold

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}-1}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}=\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{min}}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}}<\sqrt{\tau m}

by
Assumption

1
, which contradicts
eq.

4.5
. Summarizing both the two cases, the desired result is proved.

Lemma 16
.

The points
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
generated by the
PNT
algorithm lie in a bounded set of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Proof.

First notice that
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\lambda_{0})\geq h(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\lambda_{1})\geq\cdots
. We only need to prove
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{k\geq k_{t}}
is bounded above. In this case, notice that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
and

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=\frac{1}{2}[\|\lambda_{k}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}^{2}+(\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\beta_{1}e_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\tau m}{2})^{2}].

If the points do not lie in a bounded set, there exists a subsequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\}
with
k_{j}\geq k_{t}
such that
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\rightarrow\infty
. If
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty
, then
\|\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{j}}\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty
, since
\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}=\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}
has full column rank. This leads to
\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{j}}\|_{2}\rightarrow\infty
since
\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}
has full column rank. It follows that the second term of
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})
tends to infinity and
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\rightarrow\infty
, a contradiction. Therefore, it must hold that
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}\|_{2}
is bounded above and
\lambda_{k_{j}}\rightarrow\infty
. By
Lemma

15
, we have
\|\lambda_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}-\boldsymbol{b})\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}\geq\lambda_{k_{j}}C_{1}
. Notice that
\{\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}\}
lie in a bounded set. It follows that
\|\lambda_{k_{j}}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}-\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{N}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}}\|_{\boldsymbol{N}}\rightarrow\infty
and
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\rightarrow\infty
, also a contradiction.

Lemma 17
.

There exist a positive constant

First, we prove that

J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{k-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}+\boldsymbol{I}&\boldsymbol{B}_{k}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})\\
(\boldsymbol{B}_{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k}&0\end{pmatrix}=:\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}

and notice that

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&\mathbf{0}\\
-\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&-\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&-\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\\

It follows that

\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}&\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&-\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&-(\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}_{k})^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{I}&\mathbf{0}\\
-\boldsymbol{d}_{k}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix},

(4.7)

Since
\boldsymbol{C}_{k}
is positive definite and
\|\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\|_{2}\geq C_{1}>0
.

To give an upper bound on
\|J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})^{-1}\|_{2}
, we only need to consider
k\geq k_{t}
, where
\boldsymbol{B}_{k}=\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}
in
J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
. Since
\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{C}_{k})=\sigma_{\min}(\lambda_{k-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}+\boldsymbol{I})\geq 1
, we have
\|\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 1
.
By
Lemma

16
, there exist a positive constant
C_{3}<+\infty
such that
\lambda_{k}\leq C_{3}
, thereby

\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{C}_{k})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\lambda_{k-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}})+\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{I})\leq C_{3}\sigma_{\max}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}})+1=:\bar{C}_{3}.

By
Lemma

15
we have
\|\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}-\beta_{1}e_{1})\|_{2}\geq C_{1}
.
On the other hand, by

16
we know that
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}\|_{2}=\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}\|_{2}

\|\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1}\|_{2}

\bar{C}_{1}

\|\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{C}_{k}^{-1}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{d}_{k}\|_{2}\leq\bar{C}_{1}\sigma_{\min}(\boldsymbol{C}_{k})^{-1}\leq\bar{C}_{1},

Lemma 18
.

There exists a positive constant
C_{4}
such that for any
k\geq 1
,

\gamma_{k}\geq C_{4}>0.

(4.8)

Proof.

By
Theorem

9
and
Theorem

7
, at each iteration the Armijo backtracking line search must terminate in finite steps with a
\gamma_{k}
satisfying

\gamma_{k}\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{4(1-c)\eta h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})}{\zeta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\|(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k})\|_{2}^{2}}\right\},

where
\zeta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla h
at
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
; see also
[
4
, pp. 122, Corollary 2.1]
. Now we prove
\zeta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
are bounded above. Notice that
\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)=J(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\widehat{N}F(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
. Thus, all the elements in the Jacobian of
\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
are polynomials of
(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
with degrees not bigger than 4. Since
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\}
lie in a bounded set, the norms of the Jacobians of
\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
at the points
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\}
are bounded above. Therefore, the Lipschitz constants
\zeta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})
are bounded above.

Let
\zeta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\leq\zeta_{0}
with
0<\zeta_{0}<+\infty
for any
k\geq 1
. Then by
Lemma

17
and
Lemma

10
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\\
\Delta\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}&\\
&1\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\\
\Delta\lambda_{k}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\|_{2}+1)\|J^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})^{-1}\|_{2}\|F^{(k)}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\|_{2}+1)(2h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1}))^{1/2}.

Then we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{4(1-c)\eta h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1},\lambda_{k-1})}{\zeta_{0}\|(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k})^{\top}\|_{2}^{2}}\right\}\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{2(1-c)\eta}{\zeta_{0}C_{2}^{2}(\|\boldsymbol{V}_{k_{t}}\|_{2}+1)^{2}}\right\}=:C_{4}.

The desired result is obtained.

By
Lemma

16
, the sequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is contained in a bounded set, thereby there exists a convergent subsequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. Suppose
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\rightarrow(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})
. It follows that
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\rightarrow h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})
since

is continuous. Note that
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})
is nonincreasing, thereby
h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})\leq h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})
for any
k_{j}
. Thus, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist a
k_{\star}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})<h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})+\varepsilon,\ k_{j}>k_{\star}.

Select one
k_{j}
that satisfies

. For any
k\geq k_{j}
, we have

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\leq h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})<h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})+\varepsilon,

which means that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda}).

The Armijo condition and
Theorem

7
lead to

h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1},\lambda_{k+1})-h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\leq c\gamma_{k}\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k}\right)\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\leq 0.

Taking the limit on both sides leads to

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}c\gamma_{k}\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k}\right)\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=0.

By
Lemma

18
we get
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k}\right)\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=0
. Noticing by
Theorem

7
that
-2h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=\left(\Delta\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{\top},\Delta\lambda_{k}\right)\nabla h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
, we get

h(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})=0.

This proves the desired result.

\hfill\Box

Now we can give the convergence result of
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
.

Corollary 19
.

The sequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
generated by the
PNT
algorithm eventually converges to
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
, i.e. the solution of
eq.

2.2
and the corresponding Lagrange multiplier.

Proof.

Using the same notations as the proof of
Theorem

12
, we obtain that
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}},\widehat{\lambda})=(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
, since
h(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
has the unique zero point
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
. Therefore, the subsequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{j}},\lambda_{k_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
defined in the proof of
Theorem

12
converges to
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
. Now we need to prove the whole sequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
. Assume that there is a subsequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{l_{j}},\lambda_{l_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
that does not converge to
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
. We can select a subsequence from
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{l_{j}},\lambda_{l_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
that converges to a point
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\lambda})\neq(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
. Since
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{l_{j}},\lambda_{l_{j}})
is nonincreasing with respect to
j
, using the same procedure as the proof of
Theorem

12
, we can obtain again that
h(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\lambda})=0
, leading to
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\lambda})=(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
, a contradiction. Therefore, any subsequence of
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
converges to
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
, thereby
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
converges to
(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\lambda^{*})
.

6
Conclusion

For large-scale Bayesian linear inverse problems, we have proposed the projected Newton (
PNT
) method as a novel iterative approach for simultaneously updating both the regularization parameter and solution without any computationally expensive matrix inversions or decompositions. By reformulating the Tikhonov regularization as a corresponding constrained minimization problem and leveraging its Lagrangian function, the regularized solution and the corresponding Lagrangian multiplier can be obtained from the unconstrained Lagrangian function using a Newton-type method. To reduce the computational overhead of the Newton method, the generalized Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization is applied to project the original large-scale problem to become small-scale ones, where the projected Newton direction is obtained by solving the small-scale linear system at each iteration. We have proved that the projected Newton direction is a descent direction of a merit function, and the points generated by
PNT
eventually converge to the unique minimizer of this merit function, which is just the regularized solution and the corresponding Lagrangian multiplier.

Experimental tests on both small and large-scale Bayesian inverse problems have demonstrated the excellent convergence property, robustness and efficiency of
PNT
. The most demanding computational tasks in
PNT
are primarily matrix-vector products, making it particularly well-suited for large-scale problems.

An important remaining question is the convergence rate of
PNT
, i.e., how fast the three quantities
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{DP}\|_{2}
,
|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{DP}|
and
h(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\lambda_{k})
converge to zero? The convergence rate may depend on several factors, including the ill-posedness of
eq.

1.1
, the smoothness of the true solution, the noise level, and the properties of
\{\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{N}\}
. We will conduct theoretical investigations into this issue in future work.
