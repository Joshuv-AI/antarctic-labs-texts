The chromatic number of random lifts of complete graphs
JD Nir, Xavier Pérez-Giménez

Abstract
An n n -lift of a graph G G is a graph from which there is an n n -to- 1 1 covering map onto G G . Amit, Linial, and Matoušek (2002) raised the question of whether the chromatic number of a random n n -lift of K 5 K_{5} is concentrated on a single value. We consider this problem for G = K d + 1 G=K_{d+1} , and show that for fixed d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 the chromatic number of a random lift of K d K_{d} is (asymptotically almost surely) either k k or k + 1 k+1 , where k k is the smallest integer satisfying d < 2 ​ k ​ log ⁡ k d<2k\log k . Moreover, we show that, for roughly half of the values of d d , the chromatic number is concentrated on k k . The argument for the upper-bound on the chromatic number uses the small subgraph conditioning method, and it can be extended to random n n -lifts of G G , for any fixed d d -regular graph G G .

Introduction

Given two loopless multigraphs (graphs, for short)
G
and
L
, a
covering map
is a surjective graph homomorphism
f:L\to G
which is also a local isomorphism: that is, for each vertex
v
of
L
, the set of edges incident with
v
is mapped bijectively to the set of edges incident with
f(v)
. This corresponds to the general topological notion of covering map restricted to the case of graphs, and can be described in a purely combinatorial way.
If such a map exists, we say that
L
is a
covering graph
or a
lift
of
G
, and call
G
the
base graph
.
For each vertex
v\in V(G)
, the set
f^{-1}(v)\subseteq V(L)
is called the
fiber
of
v
.
It is well-known and easy to show that if
G
is connected then all fibers must have the same cardinality.
We call a lift of a (not necessarily connected) graph
G
an
n
-lift if all the fibers have size
n
.
In view of all the above, given a fixed base graph
G
, we define a random lift
L_{n}(G)
to be an
n
-lift of
G
chosen uniformly at random.
This model of random lifts was introduced in a series of papers by Amit, Linial, Matoušek and Rozenman
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
19
]
.
Roughly speaking, one can generate a random instance of
L_{n}(G)
by first replacing each vertex in
G
by a fiber containing
n
vertices and then, for each edge of
G
, adding a random perfect matching between the two fibers corresponding to its endpoints, with all the perfect matchings being chosen independently.
Note that, in the case of multiple edges, two edges of
G
with the same endpoints contribute to two perfect matchings between their fibers.
This model can also be extended to the case in which the base graph
G
is allowed to have loops.
Here we distinguish two types of loops:
half-loops
and
whole-loops
. Both half- and whole-loops denote edges that are only incident with one single vertex, but each whole-loop contributes twice to the degree of its endpoint whereas each half-loop contributes only once.
To form a random lift
L_{n}(G)
of a base graph
G
with loops, for each half-loop with endpoint
v\in V(G)
, we add a random perfect matching to the fiber of
v
(which requires
n
to be even). Likewise, for each whole-loop with endpoint
v\in V(G)
, we add a random
2
-factor (i.e. a spanning 2-regular graph, possibly with whole-loops and double edges) to its fiber.
For a more detailed description of this and other related models of random lifts of a graph with loops and multiple edges we refer the reader to
[
12
]
.

If
G
is a
d
-regular graph, so is any lift of

. Therefore,

provides a model of random
d
-regular graphs on
n|V(G)|
vertices.
A simple and interesting case is when
G
is a bouquet
B_{d}
(i.e. one single vertex with
d
half-loops). Then the corresponding random lift is the union of
d
independent random perfect matchings on a common set of
n
vertices, which (conditional upon obtaining a simple graph) is known to be contiguous to the uniform model of random
d
-regular graphs on
n

[

]
, which we denote by

.
On the other hand, for any
d
-regular base graph

with more than one vertex,
L_{n}(G)
is not contiguous to the uniform model
G_{n|V(G)|,d}
. (This can be easily seen by bounding the expected number of covering maps from a uniform random

-regular graph to

.) The case when

is the complete

-regular graph

has been widely studied as well.

The chromatic number

Clearly, if

is a lift of

-colouring of

by assigning to all vertices of the fiber

the same colour as

. In general, this bound can be far from being tight since, for instance, one can easily build a bipartite lift of

for any given

One natural question is to determine the typical asymptotic behaviour of

In this direction, Amit, Linial and Matoušek
[

proved that, for any fixed simple graph

\chi(L_{n}(G))\leq(1+\epsilon_{\Delta})\Delta/\log\Delta
a.a.s.

Our contribution.

We analyze the chromatic number of a random
n
-lift of
G=K_{d+1}
, and show that it is a.a.s. in
\{k_{d},k_{d}+1\}
, where
k_{d}
is defined as above. Moreover, for roughly half of the values of
d
, the chromatic number of
L_{n}(K_{d+1})
is a.a.s.
k_{d}
.
In other words, we show that the results on the uniform model of
d
-regular graphs in
[
18
]
(that is,
G=B_{d}
) also hold for a random lift of
G=K_{d+1}
. Furthermore, our upper bounds on the chromatic number are also valid for a random lift of any fixed
d
-regular loopless graph
G
(possibly with multiple edges). Specifically, we prove the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
, and define

u_{k}=\frac{2\log k}{\log k-\log(k-1)}<(2k-1)\log k.

Suppose
d\geq u_{k}
. Then a.a.s. a random
n
-lift of
K_{d+1}
is not
k
-colourable.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
, and define

\ell_{k}=\frac{2(k-1)^{3}}{k(k-2)}\log(k-1)>2(k-1)\log(k-1).

Suppose
d<\ell_{k}
, and let
G
be any fixed
d
-regular loopless multigraph. Then a.a.s. a random
n
-lift of
G
is
k
-colourable.

We remark that the base graph
G
is allowed to have multiple edges in Theorem
1.2
. We exclude half- and whole-loops to make some of the technical calculations in the paper simpler and since we are mainly interested in the case
G=K_{d+1}
. It is conceivable that the result also holds for graphs with loops (of either type). In fact, such an extension would imply the main result in
[
18
]
for the uniform model
G_{n,d}
, by taking
G=B_{d}
.

Combining Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
for

immediately yields the following result.

Corollary 1.3
.

For each
k\geq 3
:

(i)

u_{k-1}<\ell_{k}<u_{k}
,

(ii)

if
d\in[u_{k-1},\ell_{k})
then a.a.s.
\chi(L_{n}(K_{d+1}))=k
, and

(iii)

if
d\in[\ell_{k},u_{k})
then a.a.s.
\chi(L_{n}(K_{d+1}))\in\{k,k+1\}
.

In particular, noting that
u_{k}<(2k-1)\log k
and
\ell_{k}>2(k-1)\log(k-1)
we can rewrite the above corollary in a slightly weaker but simpler form.

Corollary 1.4
.

For each
d\geq 3
, let
k_{d}=\min\{k:d<2k\log k\}
.

(i)

Then a.a.s.
\chi(L_{n}(K_{d+1}))\in\{k_{d},k_{d}+1\}
.

(ii)

If moreover
d>(2k_{d}-1)\log k_{d}
, then a.a.s.
\chi(L_{n}(K_{d+1}))=k_{d}+1
.

As observed in similar discussions in
[
2
]
and
[

, ‘roughly half’ of the integer values

satisfy the extra condition in part (ii) of the corollary.
It is conceivable that, by using similar methods as in
[
8
]
, one could extend the one-point concentration conclusion of part (ii) to hold for every sufficiently large
d
.

Outline of the argument.

The lower bound on the chromatic number follows from bounding the expected number of
k
-colourings
X
of a random lift
L_{n}(G)
for the case
G=K_{d+1}
. For the upper bound, we use the small subgraph conditioning method introduced by Robinson and Wormald (see
[
17
]
, Chapter 9, and
[
27
]
for a full exposition of the method) applied to the number
Y
of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of
L_{n}(G)
(that is,
k
-colourings in which all colours appear the same number of times in any fiber).
Our argument requires an accurate estimate of
\mathbf{E}X
,
\mathbf{E}Y
,
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
and some additional joint factorial moments of the short-cycle counts of
L_{n}(G)
.
In order to determine the exponential behaviour of each of these moments, we must solve a non-trivial optimization problem over some bounded polytope.

Somewhat surprisingly, the optimization problem that arises in the calculation of the first moment
\mathbf{E}X
has a “second moment flavour”, and is more involved than the corresponding one for
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
. Here is an intuitive explanation for this fact: in order to compute
\mathbf{E}X
we need to count triples
(f,g,L)
where
L
is a
d
-regular graph of order
(d+1)n
,
f:L\to K_{d+1}
is a covering map (which can also be seen as a proper
(d+1)
-colouring of
L
with the additional property that each vertex sees all the
d+1
colours in its closed neighbourhood) and
g
is a proper
k
-colouring of
L
.
So this can be seen as a lopsided version of a similar counting problem that arises when computing the second moment of the number of
k
-colourings in the uniform model
G_{n,d}
(see e.g.
[
1
,
18
]
).
To compute
\mathbf{E}X
, we solve an optimization problem over the set of
(d+1)\times k
stochastic matrices that generalizes a result of Achlioptas and Naor
[
2
]
for square stochastic matrices.
We believe that our generalization is of independent interest for future applications.
On the other hand, the corresponding optimization problem in the calculation of
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
can be reduced (after some work) to the original optimization over square stochastic matrices in
[
2
]
. (This relies on the fact that
Y
concerns only strongly balanced colourings.)
Finally, note that our analysis of
\mathbf{E}X
makes use of the fact that
G=K_{d+1}
(since our claims are false for general base graphs), whereas our calculations for
\mathbf{E}Y
and
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
generalize to any loopless
d
-regular graph
G
.

Next, we compute the polynomial factors in our estimates of
\mathbf{E}Y
and
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
. This can be done using (at least) two different methods, which we apply several times in the argument. One is the saddle-point method, which uses complex integration over an appropriate contour to obtain an asymptotic estimate of the coefficients of a generating function. The other one is a version of the Laplace summation method over lattices given in
[
16
]
. We reformulate the result in
[
16
]
in terms of counting maximal forests in an auxiliary graph that encodes the summation constraints. This reformulation unveils the role of the eigenvalues of this auxiliary graph, in view of the well-known matrix-tree theorem, and simplifies the calculations. We believe that our version of this tool can be useful in future applications.

Finally, the use of the small subgraph conditioning method requires us to investigate how the presence of short cycles in the random lift
L_{n}(G)
affects the number of colourings. (Here,
G
is any fixed
d
-regular graph.)
This requires the enumeration of non-backtracking closed walks in
G
, for which we use algebraic tools developed by Friedman
[
11
]
(see also
[
16
]
).

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\left.\prod_{i=1}^{|V|}\left(\frac{(k-1)^{4}}{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}+d-\alpha_{i}(k-1)^{2}}\right)\right)

Exponentiating,

\displaystyle\exp\left(\sum\limits_{j\geq 1}\lambda_{j}\delta_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{((k-1)^{2})^{|E|-|V|+|E|-|V|+2|V|}}{(\lambda\lambda^{\prime})^{|E|-|V|}\prod_{i=1}^{|V|}(\lambda\lambda^{\prime}+d-\alpha_{i}(k-1)^{2})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{((k-1)^{2})^{2|E|}}{(\lambda\lambda^{\prime})^{|E|-|V|}\prod_{i=1}^{|V|}(\lambda\lambda^{\prime}+d-\alpha_{i}(k-1)^{2})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}}

Now we turn to the left-hand side of the proposition. Using Propositions
2.2
and
2.3
,

\frac{\mathbf{E}Y^{2}}{(\mathbf{E}Y)^{2}}\sim\frac{C_{2}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)|V|}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2n}}{\left(C_{1}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)|V|/2}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{n}\right)^{2}}=\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}^{2}}.

and

\displaystyle\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{(k^{2}-k+1)|V|-\frac{1}{2}(k^{2}-1)|E|}(k-1)^{(2k^{2}-2k)|E|}}{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}|E|}(k-2)^{\frac{1}{2}(k^{2}-1)|E|}h(d,k)^{\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}}\left(k^{k|V|/2}\left(\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{k(k-2)}\right)^{(k-1)|E|/2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{(k-1)^{2}|V|-\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}|E|}(k-1)^{2(k-1)^{2}|E|}}{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}|E|}(k-2)^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}|E|}h(d,k)^{\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{(k-1)^{2}|V|}}{\left(\left(\frac{k^{2}}{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{|V|}\prod_{i=1}^{|V|}(\lambda\lambda^{\prime}+d-\alpha_{i}(k-1)^{2})\right)^{\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}}}\cdot\left(\frac{(k-1)^{4|E|}}{(\lambda\lambda^{\prime})^{|E|}}\right)^{(k-1)^{2}/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{((k-1)^{2})^{2|E|}}{(\lambda\lambda^{\prime})^{|E|-|V|}\prod_{i=1}^{|V|}(\lambda\lambda^{\prime}+d-\alpha_{i}(k-1)^{2})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}}

from which we conclude

\frac{\mathbf{E}Y^{2}}{(\mathbf{E}Y)^{2}}\sim\exp\left(\sum_{j\geq 1}\lambda_{j}\delta_{j}^{2}\right)

as required.
∎

3
Useful tools

In this section we prove two results which, in addition to being necessary for our arguments, may prove to be of independent interest.

First, in
[
2
]
, Achlioptas and Naor investigate the chromatic number of a random graph of average bounded degree,
G(n,d/n)
. In that article they prove a theorem regarding the optimization of square stochastic matrices which has
also been used in the context of uniform random regular graphs
(see
[
1
,
18
]
). We extend their result to non-square stochastic matrices.

Then, in
[
16
]
, Greenhill, Janson and Ruciński write about the number of perfect matchings in random lifts of graphs. To facilitate their calculations, they prove a theorem for estimating a summation over multiple dimensions using Laplace’s method. We reformulate their result in terms of counting maximal forests in an auxiliary graph that encodes the summation constraints. This reformulation unveils the role of the eigenvalues of this auxiliary graph, in view of the well-known matrix-tree theorem, and simplifies the calculations.

3.1
Optimization over stochastic matrices

In this section we will introduce some inequalities regarding stochastic
matrices. A matrix
M
is called
row-stochastic
if all of its entries are
nonnegative and the sum of the entries in each row equals
1
(i.e. each row of

M
defines a probability distribution). Similarly,
M
is

column-stochastic
if
M^{T}
is row-stochastic, and
M
is

doubly-stochastic
if it is both row- and column-stochastic.
Stochastic matrices arise naturally when estimating the second moment of the
number of colourings of a random graph or in other related problems. For
instance, suppose that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are two equitable
k
-vertex-colourings of the
same graph (i.e. colourings in which all the colour classes are of the same size).
Then we can describe how much
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
correlate in terms of a
doubly-stochastic matrix
M=(m_{i,j})_{i,j\in[k]}
, where each entry
m_{i,j}

denotes the (appropriately rescaled) proportion of vertices that receive colour

i
in
C_{1}
and colour
j
in
C_{2}
.
In second moment calculations, one typically needs to determine which pairs of
colourings
C_{1},C_{2}
give the main contribution, which can be formulated as an
optimization problem over the set of doubly-stochastic
k\times k
matrices.

The first inequality in this section, concerning square row-stochastic matrices,
was proved by Achlioptas and Naor
[
2
]
. They used this tool to obtain an
accurate second moment estimate of the number of colourings of an
Erdős-Rényi random graph of constant average degree. Their inequality has
also been used in the context of uniform random regular graphs
(see
[
1
,
18
]
).
Before stating the result, we need some notation.
Given a
q\times k
matrix
M=(m_{i,j})_{i\in[q],j\in[k]}
, let

\rho(M)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{i,j}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(M)=-\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{k}m_{i,j}\log m_{i,j}.

If in addition
M
is row-stochastic, then a standard convexity argument shows that

\frac{q}{k}\leq\rho(M)\leq q,

(7)

where the minimum
\rho(M)=q/k
is uniquely attained when all
m_{i,j}=1/k
, and
the maximum
\rho(M)=q
is achieved precisely at those row-stochastic matrices
with all entries in
\{0,1\}
.
For each integer
q\geq 3
, define

c_{q}=\frac{(q-1)^{3}}{q(q-2)}\log(q-1).

(8)

Theorem 3.1

(Achlioptas and Naor
[
2
]
)
.

Let
M=(m_{i,j})
be a
q\times q
row-stochastic matrix, where
q\geq 3
.
Then, for any
c<c_{q}
,

\frac{1}{q}h(M)+c\log\left(q^{2}-2q+\rho(M)\right)\leq\log q+c\log\left((q-1)^{2}\right),

Furthermore, we have equality if and only if
m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{q}
for all
i,j\in[q]
.

Remark
.

The second claim in the proposition is not explicitly stated in
[
2
]
.
However, it follows from their proof that equality holds if and only if

\rho(M)=1
, which in view of (
7
) corresponds to the case when
all
m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{q}
.

We will use Theorem
3.1
in our second moment
calculations in Section
5
for the number
Y
of strongly
equitable
k
-colourings of a random lift of an arbitrary
d
-regular graph. Our
estimate of
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
will involve solving an optimization problem
which we will manage to reduce to the inequality in
Theorem
3.1
.
Furthermore, and somewhat surprisingly, while Achlioptas and Naor’s inequality
typically arises in second moment arguments, we will require a more general
version of the inequality to handle a first moment calculation. Because
we must concern ourselves with both a random lift and a random coloring of that
lift, this first moment calculation takes on more of a second moment flavor.
When we bound the expected number
X
of (not necessarily equitable)

k
-colorings of a random lift of
K_{d+1}
in Section
4
, we will
use a generalization of Theorem
3.1
that applies to
rectangular
q\times k
row-stochastic matrices.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
M=(m_{i,j})
be a
q\times k
row-stochastic matrix, where
q\geq 3
and
k\leq q
. Then, for any
c<\frac{k-1}{q-1}c_{q}
,

\frac{1}{q}h(M)+c\log\left(kq-k-q+\frac{k}{q}\,\rho(M)\right)\leq\log k+c\log\left((q-1)(k-1)\right)

or

c\log\left(1+\frac{\frac{k}{q}\rho(M)-1}{(q-1)(k-1)}\right)\leq\log k-\frac{1}{q}h(M)

with equality if and only if
m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{k}
.

Proof.

Our first step is to extend
M
to a
q\times q
row-stochastic matrix
\hat{M}
by adding
q-k
extra columns and scaling each column appropriately. More precisely, let
\hat{M}=(\hat{m}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[q]}
with

\hat{m}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}m_{i,j}\cdot\frac{k}{q}&1\leq j\leq k\\
\frac{1}{q}&k+1\leq j\leq q.\end{cases}

Clearly,
\hat{M}
is a
q\times q
row-stochastic matrix. Hence, we can apply Theorem
3.1
to
\hat{M}
with
\hat{c}:=\frac{q-1}{k-1}c<c_{q}
, and obtain

\hat{c}\log\left(1+\frac{\rho(\hat{M})-1}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)\leq\log q-\frac{1}{q}h(\hat{M}),

(9)

which holds with equality if and only if
\hat{m}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{q}
(which is in turn equivalent to
m_{i,j}=\frac{1}{k}
).
Our next task is to derive relations between
\rho(\hat{M})
and
\rho(M)
and also between
h(\hat{M})
and
h(M)
, which will allow us to express the inequality in (
9
) in terms of
\rho(M)
and
h(M)
.
We have

\displaystyle\rho(\hat{M})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}\hat{m}_{i,j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{2}}{q^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}m_{i,j}^{2}+q\,(q-k)\,\frac{1}{q^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{q}\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}\right)+\frac{q-k}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{q}\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right)+1

(10)

Given a probability distribution
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})
, let
H(x)=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}\log x_{j}
denote the entropy of
x
.
For each
i\in[q]
, let
m_{i}=(m_{i,1},\ldots,m_{i,k})
and
\hat{m}_{i}=(\hat{m}_{i,1},\ldots,\hat{m}_{i,q})
. These are the
i
-th rows of matrices
M
and
\hat{M}
, which we regard as probability distributions. Also, let
u=(\frac{1}{q-k},\ldots,\frac{1}{q-k})
be the uniform distribution on a set of
q-k
elements.

Note that
\hat{m}_{i}
is a mixture of
m_{i}=(m_{i,1},\ldots,m_{i,k})
and
u=(\frac{1}{q-k},\ldots,\frac{1}{q-k})
, in the sense that we can sample from
\hat{m}_{i}
by sampling from
m_{i}
with probability
\frac{k}{q}
and from
u
with probability
\frac{q-k}{q}
.
Hence, by the chain rule,

\displaystyle H(\hat{m}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{q}H(m_{i})+\frac{q-k}{q}H(u)+H(\tfrac{k}{q},\tfrac{q-k}{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{q}H(m_{i})+\frac{q-k}{q}\log(q-k)+\frac{k}{q}(\log q-\log k)+\frac{q-k}{q}(\log q-\log(q-k))

\displaystyle=\log q-\frac{k}{q}\log k+\frac{k}{q}H(m_{i}).

Noting that
h(M)=\sum_{i=1}^{q}H(m_{i})
and
h(\hat{M})=\sum_{i=1}^{q}H(\hat{m}_{i})
, we conclude

\displaystyle\log k-\frac{1}{q}h(M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\left(\frac{k}{q}\log k-\frac{k}{q}H(m_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\left(\log q-H(\hat{m}_{i})\right)=\frac{q}{k}\left(\log q-\frac{1}{q}h(\hat{M})\right).

(11)

In view of (
10
) and (
11
), we can use the inequality
in (
9
) to write

\displaystyle\log k-\frac{1}{q}h(M)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{k}\left(\log q-\frac{1}{q}h(\hat{M})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q}{k}\hat{c}\log\left(1+\frac{\rho(\hat{M})-1}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{k}\hat{c}\log\left(1+\frac{\frac{k}{q}\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right)}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)

Note that
\frac{k}{q}\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right)>-\frac{k}{q}>-(q-1)^{2}
.
Since
f(x)=\log\left(1+\frac{x}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)
is a concave function for
x>-(q-1)^{2}
and
f(0)=0
, Jensen’s inequality gives

\log\left(1+\frac{\frac{k}{q}\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right)}{(q-1)^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{k}{q}\log\left(1+\frac{\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right)}{(q-1)^{2}}\right),

which combined with the inequality above yields

\log k-\frac{1}{q}h(M)\geq\hat{c}\log\left(1+\frac{\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right)}{(q-1)^{2}}\right).

(12)

In order to further bound the right-hand side from below, we introduce a new function

g(y)=y\log\left(1+\frac{a}{y}\right),\qquad\text{where}\quad a=\frac{1}{q-1}\left(\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1\right).

From (
7
), we know
0\leq a\leq 1
. We claim that
g(y)
is nondecreasing for all
y>0
.
Indeed, its derivative satisfies

g^{\prime}(y)=-\log\left(1-\frac{a}{y+a}\right)-\frac{a}{y+a}\geq 0,

where we’ve used
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x>-1
. Hence, from (
12
),

\log k-\frac{1}{q}h(M)\geq\frac{\hat{c}}{q-1}\,g(q-1)\geq\frac{\hat{c}}{q-1}\,g(k-1)=c\log\left(1+\frac{\frac{\rho(M)k}{q}-1}{(q-1)(k-1)}\right),

which is equivalent to

\frac{1}{q}h(M)+c\log\left(kq-k-q+\frac{k}{q}\,\rho(M)\right)\leq\log k+c\log\left((q-1)(k-1)\right),

as desired. Finally, in view of our remark below (
9
), equality holds
if and only if all
m_{i,j}=1/k
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

3.2
Laplace summation over lattices

Here we will prove Proposition
3.4
, which we will use in Sections
4.2
and
5.4
to precisely estimate expectations expressed as sums, as a consequence of a similar result of Greenhill, Janson and Ruciński
[
16
]
.

We include here the definitions required to state their theorem, but refer the reader to
[
16
]
for additional background.
A
lattice

\Lambda
is an additive subgroup of
N
such that every
bounded region in
N
contains a finite number of elements of
\Lambda
.
It is well-known that every lattice
\Lambda
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{r}
, for
some integer
r
(
0\leq r\leq N
) which we call the
rank
of
\Lambda
.
Moreover, every lattice
\Lambda
of rank
r\geq 1
admits a
basis

u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\in\Lambda
such that every point in
\Lambda
can be uniquely
represented as a linear combination
m_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+m_{r}u_{r}
with integer
coefficients
m_{i}
.

Given a lattice
\Lambda
with a basis
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
, we
define the
determinant
of the lattice as

\det(\Lambda)=\sqrt{\det U^{T}U},

where
U
is an
N\times r
matrix with columns
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
. This quantity does not depend on our choice of the basis. If
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an
r
-dimensional vector space spanning
\Lambda
(and thus with basis
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}
), for any symmetric
N\times N
matrix
H
we define

\det(H|_{\mathbb{V}})=\frac{\det U^{T}HU}{\det U^{T}U}.

(13)

This value also does not depend on our choice of basis.

Theorem 3.3

(Greenhill, Janson and Ruciński
[
16
]
)
.

Suppose the following:

(i)

\Lambda\subset\real^{N}
is a lattice with rank
1\leq r\leq N
.

(ii)

\mathbb{V}\subseteq\real^{N}
is the
r
-dimensional subspace spanned by
\Lambda
.

(iii)

\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{V}+\text{\boldmath$w$}
is an affine subspace parallel to
\mathbb{V}
, for some
\text{\boldmath$w$}\in\real^{N}
.

(iv)

K\subset\real^{N}
is a compact convex set with non-empty interior
K^{\circ}
.

(v)

\phi:K\to\real
is a continuous function and the restriction of
\phi
to
K\cap\mathbb{W}
has a unique maximum at some point
\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0}\in K^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{W}
.

(vi)

\phi
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0}
and
H:=D^{2}\phi(\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0})
is its Hessian at
\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0}
.

(vii)

\psi:K_{1}\to\real
is a continuous function on some neighbourhood
K_{1}\subseteq K
of
\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0}
with
\psi(\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0})>0
.

(viii)

For each positive integer
n
there is a vector
\ell_{n}\in\real^{N}
with
\ell_{n}/n\in\mathbb{W}
,

(ix)

For each positive integer
n
there is a positive real number
b_{n}
and a function
a_{n}:(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK\to\real
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

a_{n}(\ell)=O(b_{n}e^{n\phi(\ell/n)+o(n)}),\quad\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK

and

a_{n}(\ell)=b_{n}(\psi(\ell/n)+o(1))e^{n\phi(\ell/n)},\quad\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK_{1},

uniformly for
\ell
in the indicated sets.

Then provided
\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})\neq 0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK}a_{n}(\ell)\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{r/2}\psi(\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0})}{\det(\Lambda)\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}b_{n}n^{r/2}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0})}.

In order to state our proposition, we need some additional definitions. Let

\Gamma=(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
be a non-empty multigraph (possibly with multiple
edges, but no loops). Fix an arbitrary orientation of the edges in
E_{\Gamma}
.
The
signed incidence matrix
of
\Gamma
(with respect to that
orientation) is a
|V_{\Gamma}|\times|E_{\Gamma}|
matrix
\tilde{D}=(\tilde{D}_{v,e})_{v\in V_{\Gamma},e\in E_{\Gamma}}
, where
\tilde{D}_{v,e}=1
if
v
is the
tail of
e
,
\tilde{D}_{v,e}=-1
if
v
is the head of
e
and
\tilde{D}_{v,e}=0
otherwise. Similarly, the
unsigned incidence matrix
of
\Gamma

is a matrix
D=(D_{v,e})_{v\in V_{\Gamma},e\in E_{\Gamma}}
, where
D_{v,e}=|\tilde{D}_{v,e}|
, and does not depend on the orientation of the edges. Finally, recall
the definition of
\det(H|_{\mathbb{V}})
from (
13
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose the following:

(i)

\Gamma=(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
is a non-empty bipartite multigraph with at least one cycle.

(ii)

D
is the unsigned incidence matrix of
\Gamma
.

(iii)

\tau(\Gamma)
is the number of maximal forests in
\Gamma
.

(iv)

\mathbb{V}=\Ker(D)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
is a vector space of dimension
r
.

(v)

\text{\boldmath$y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{|V_{\Gamma}|}
such that

D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}

(14)

is a consistent linear system.

(vi)

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
is a compact convex set with non-empty interior
K^{\circ}
.

(vii)

\phi:K\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and the maximum of
\phi
in
K
subject to (
14
) is attained at a unique maximizer
\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}\in K^{\circ}
.

(viii)

\phi
is twice continuously differentiable in a neighbourhood of
\hat{x}
and
H
is its Hessian matrix at
\hat{x}
.

(ix)

\psi:K_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function on some neighbourhood
K_{1}\subseteq K
of
\hat{x}
with
\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})>0
.

(x)

For each positive integer
n
,

\mathbb{X}_{n}=\left\{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in K\cap\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}:D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}\right\}

is non-empty, and there is a positive real number
c_{n}
and a function
T_{n}:\mathbb{X}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=O(c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(n)}),\quad\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}

and

T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=c_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1))e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})},\quad\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}\cap K_{1},

uniformly for
x
in the indicated sets.

Then provided
\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})\neq 0
, as
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})\sim\frac{\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}{\tau(\Gamma)^{1/2}\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}(2\pi n)^{r/2}c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}.

Furthermore, in the case that
\Gamma
is not bipartite, the proposition remains valid if we replace
D
by the signed incidence matrix
\tilde{D}
of
\Gamma
(with respect to a fixed orientation of
E_{\Gamma}
).

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

We start by proving the more general result where
\Gamma
is not bipartite in which we use the signed incidence matrix
\tilde{D}
.

Let
\Lambda=\mathbb{V}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
so that
\Lambda
is the set of
all integer solutions of
\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$0$}
. Clearly,
\Lambda
is a
lattice that spans
\mathbb{V}
. As it spans
\mathbb{V}
, we have
\dim\mathbb{V}=\rank\Lambda=r
. As
\Gamma
is nonempty,
D
is not the zero matrix and so

r\geq 1
.

Set
\mathbb{W}=\{\text{\boldmath$x$}\,:\,\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}\}
. As we are guaranteed
\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}
is consistent, there is some
\text{\boldmath$w$}\in\real^{N}
such that
\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$w$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}
. For any
\text{\boldmath$v$}\in\mathbb{V}
we have

\tilde{D}(\text{\boldmath$v$}+\text{\boldmath$w$})=\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$v$}+\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$w$}=0+\text{\boldmath$y$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}

so
\mathbb{V}+\text{\boldmath$w$}\subseteq\mathbb{W}
. Furthermore, for any
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{W}
we have

\tilde{D}(\text{\boldmath$x$}-\text{\boldmath$w$})=\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}-\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$w$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}-\text{\boldmath$y$}=0

so
\text{\boldmath$x$}-\text{\boldmath$w$}\in\mathbb{V}
and
\text{\boldmath$x$}=(\text{\boldmath$x$}-\text{\boldmath$w$})+\text{\boldmath$w$}\in\mathbb{V}+\text{\boldmath$w$}
. We conclude
\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{V}+\text{\boldmath$w$}
is an affine subspace parallel to
\mathbb{V}
.

Note that the conditions on
K,\phi
and
\psi
in Proposition
3.4
exactly match those of Theorem
3.3
with
\hat{x}
replaced with
\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0}
.

For each positive integer
n
we have that
\mathbb{X}_{n}
is nonempty, so choose
\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
and set
\ell_{n}=n\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}
. Then
\ell_{n}/n=\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}
satisfies
\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}=\text{\boldmath$y$}
by the definition of
\mathbb{X}_{n}
so
\ell_{n}/n\in\mathbb{W}
.

Let
b_{n}=c_{n}
and define
a_{n}:(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK\to\real
by
a_{n}(\ell)=T(\ell/n)
. To see
a_{n}
is well-defined, let
\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK
and take
\text{\boldmath$x$}=\frac{1}{n}\ell
. Then certainly
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in K
. As
\ell_{n}=n\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}
, we have
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\frac{1}{n}\Lambda+\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}
, and as
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
and
\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}\in\mathbb{X}\subseteq\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
we get
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
as well. Finally, setting
\text{\boldmath$x$}=\frac{1}{n}\lambda+\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we have

\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\tilde{D}(\tfrac{1}{n}\lambda+\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n})=\tfrac{1}{n}\tilde{D}\lambda+\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}_{n}=0+\text{\boldmath$y$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}

from which we conclude
\frac{1}{n}\ell=\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
and
a_{n}
is
well-defined. Furthermore,

a_{n}(\ell)=T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=O(c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(n)})=O(b_{N}e^{n\phi(\ell/n)+o(n)}),\quad\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK

and

a_{n}(\ell)=T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=c_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1))e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})}=b_{n}(\psi(\ell/n)+o(1))e^{n\phi(\ell/n)},\quad\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK_{1}

uniformly for
\ell
as required.

Thus as Proposition
3.4
requires
\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})\neq 0
, we apply Theorem
3.3
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in(\Lambda+\ell_{n})\cap nK}a_{n}(\ell)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{r/2}\psi(\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0})}{\det(\Lambda)\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}b_{n}n^{r/2}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$z$}_{0})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}{\det(\Lambda)\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}(2\pi n)^{r/2}c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}

All that remains to complete the proof is to show
\det(\Lambda)=\tau(\Gamma)^{1/2}
. Lemma 14.7.3 in
[
14
]
gives this result in the case where
\Gamma
is connected. To extend the result to disconnected
\Gamma
we can simply apply the result to each component.

Lastly, we consider the case where
\Gamma
is bipartite in which we claim we may use the unsigned incidence matrix
D
rather than
\tilde{D}
. Select one of the parts, say
L\subseteq V_{\Gamma}
, and orient
E_{\Gamma}
from
L
toward the other part
R=V_{\Gamma}\setminus L
. Then the coefficients in each of the first
|L|
rows of
\tilde{D}
are positive while the last
|R|
rows contain all negative entries. Thus one can obtain
D
from
\tilde{D}
by multiplying the last
R
rows by
-1
. These are elementary row operations and do not change the kernel, so we conclude
\Ker D=\Ker\tilde{D}
and our definition of
\mathbb{V}
is unchanged. Now suppose
y
is the vector we’re given such that
D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}
is a consistent linear system. Define
\tilde{y}
by

\tilde{y}_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}&1\leq i\leq|L|\\
-y_{i}&|L|+1\leq i\leq|L|+|R|\end{cases}

Then
\tilde{D}\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$\tilde{y}$}
if and only if
D\text{\boldmath$x$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}
so we proceed with the rest of the proof above replacing
y
with
\hat{y}
.

∎

We prove an additional result that demonstrates all but
o(1)
of the weight of the sums of such functions occurs very close to the maximum value.

Proposition 3.5
.

Suppose the same set of conditions hold as in Proposition
3.4
. In addition, suppose there is
N>0
such that
\psi(x)\leq n^{N}
for each
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in K_{1}
. For any
\gamma>0
, define
\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)\subseteq\mathbb{X}_{n}
as

\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)=\left\{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}:||\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}||_{\infty}<\gamma\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}.

Then

\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})\sim\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$}).

Proof.

For any
\gamma>0
,
\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)
maximizes
\phi
in
K^{\circ}
and thus it suffices to show

\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}\setminus\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=o\left(e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}\right).

As
\hat{x}
is a local maximum,
\nabla\phi=0
at
\hat{x}
. By Taylor’s Theorem, as the Hessian matrix of
\phi
is nonvanishing (because
\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})\neq 0
), there is
\varepsilon>0
such that if
\delta:=||\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}||_{\infty}<\varepsilon
then
\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})=\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})-\Theta(\delta^{2})
. We partition
\mathbb{X}_{n}\setminus\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)=A\cup B
where
A
is the annulus consisting of
x
satisfying

\gamma\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}<||\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}-\text{\boldmath$x$}||_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

For any
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in A
, we have

\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})=\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})-\Theta(\delta^{2})=\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})-\Omega\left(\gamma^{2}\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\right)

and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in A}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in A}c_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1))\exp\left(n\left(\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})-\Omega\left(\gamma^{2}\frac{\log^{2}n}{n}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in A}c_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1))n^{-\gamma^{2}\log n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}

As there are at most
|E_{\gamma}|
many terms in the sum and
n^{|E_{\Gamma}|+N}=o\left(n^{\gamma^{2}\log n}\right)

we see

\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in A}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=o\left(e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}\right).

Now consider
\text{\boldmath$x$}\in B
. These
x
satisfy
||\text{\boldmath$x$}-\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}||_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon
. As
\phi
is a continuous function it attains a maximum value on the compact set
K\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})
. But
\phi
has a unique maximum in
K^{\circ}
at
\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$}\notin B
. Therefore there is some
\alpha>0
such that

\max_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in B}\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$})<\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})-\alpha.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in B}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})

\displaystyle<\sum_{\text{\boldmath$x$}\in B}c_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$x$})+o(1))\exp(n(\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})-\alpha))

\displaystyle=O\left(n^{|E_{\gamma}|+N}\right)e^{-n\alpha}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}

\displaystyle=o\left(e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{x}$})}\right).

∎

4
First moment ingredients

In this section we give two first moment arguments, one for
X
, the number of
k
-colourings of a random lift of
K_{d+1}
, and another for
Y
, the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a random lift of an arbitrary
d
-regular graph.

4.1
Colouring Optimization and Proof of Proposition
2.1

Let
X
be a random variable denoting the number of proper
k
-colourings of a
random lift
L
of
K_{d+1}
. The main goal of this section is to prove
Proposition
2.1
by providing an upper bound on
\mathbf{E}X
. Additionally, some
of the ideas developed here will be utilized again in
Section
4.2
when we study equitable
k
-colourings of a
random lift of a general
d
-regular graph. Throughout this section, we use
V

and
E
to denote the vertex and edge sets of
K_{d+1}
, respectively, so in
particular
|V|=d+1
and
|E|=\binom{d+1}{2}
. For convenience, we fix an
arbitrary orientation of the edges in
E
, so that for each pair of different
vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in V
exactly one of
vv^{\prime}
and
v^{\prime}v
belongs to
E
.

To calculate
\mathbf{E}X
, we will count the number of pairs
(L,C)
such that
L
is a lift of
K_{d+1}
and
C
is a proper
k
-colouring of
L
, and then divide by the
number of lifts of
K_{d+1}
.
First observe that we can build any lift
L
of
K_{d+1}
by selecting a perfect
matching between the fibers
\Pi^{-1}(v)
and
\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
, for each edge

vv^{\prime}\in E
. As a result, there are exactly
n!^{|E|}
possible lifts.
Let us fix a pair
(L,C)
, where
L
is a lift of
K_{d+1}
and
C
is a proper

k
-colouring of
L
. For each
v\in V
, we consider a (row) vector

a_{v}=(a_{v,i})_{i\in[k]}
, where
a_{v,i}
denotes the proportion of vertices
in fiber
\Pi^{-1}(v)
that receive colour
i
. By construction, the entries of
each
a_{v}
are in
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
and define a probability distribution on the
vertices in the fiber, which is to say

a_{v,i}\geq 0\quad\forall v\in V,i\in[k]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{v,i}=1\quad\forall v\in V.

(15)

We write
\text{\boldmath$a$}=(a_{v})_{v\in V}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k|V|}
, which we also
regard as a
|V|\times k
stochastic matrix where each row
a_{v}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}
is a probability distribution. Similarly, for each
e=vv^{\prime}\in E
, we define
b_{e}=(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}})_{i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}}
, where each

b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
denotes the proportion of edges in
\Pi^{-1}(e)
that connect a
vertex of colour
i
in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
to a vertex of colour
i^{\prime}
in

\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
. We write
\text{\boldmath$b$}=(b_{e})_{e\in E}
. The entries of each
b_{e}

must be in
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
and satisfy

\displaystyle b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\forall e\in E,i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a_{v,i}\quad\forall e=vv^{\prime}\in E,i\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}\quad\forall e=vv^{\prime}\in E,i^{\prime}\in[k].

(16)

For each choice of parameters
\text{\boldmath$a$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k|V|}
and
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k(k-1)|E|}
satisfying (
15
) and (
16
), we will enumerate all pairs
(L,C)
which agree on those parameters and then sum over all possible choices of
a
and
b
.

Given
a
and
b
, we generate a pair
(L,C)
in three steps. First, we assign colours to the vertices of
L
so that, for each
v\in V
and
i\in[k]
, exactly
a_{v,i}n
vertices in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
receive colour
i
. There are

\prod_{v\in V}\frac{n!}{\prod_{i\in[k]}(a_{v,i}n)!}=\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n}{a_{v}n}

ways to do this. (Recall the definitions for factorials and binomials of vectors in (
1
).)

Next, for every
e=vv^{\prime}\in E
and distinct colours
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
, we need to decide which sets of
b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n
vertices in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
and
\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
will be matched. We can do that in

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{i\in[k]}\frac{(a_{v,i}n)!}{\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)!}\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{i^{\prime}\in[k]}\frac{(a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n)!}{\prod_{i\neq i^{\prime}}(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)!}\bigg)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{(a_{v}n)!}{(b_{e}n)!}\frac{(a_{v^{\prime}}n)!}{(b_{e}n)!}

many ways.
Finally, we need to choose a perfect matching between these sets, which can be done in

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\in[k]\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)!=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\end{subarray}}(b_{e}n)!

different ways. Putting everything together, we get

\mathbf{E}X=\frac{1}{n!^{|E|}}\sum_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}}\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n}{a_{v}n}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{(a_{v}n)!(a_{v^{\prime}}n)!}{(b_{e}n)!},

(17)

where the sum is over all
\text{\boldmath$a$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k|V|}
and
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k(k-1)|E|}
satisfying (
15
) and (
16
).
Recall that one can write Stirling’s formula as
x!=\xi(x)(x/e)^{x}
, where
\xi
is a function satisfying
\xi(x)\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}
as
x\to\infty
and
\xi(x)\geq 1
for all
x\geq 0
.
Then, using (
15
) and (
16
), and after some tedious but simple calculations,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}X

\displaystyle=\big(\xi(n)(n/e)^{n}\big)^{-|E|}\sum_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}}\prod_{v\in V}\frac{\xi(n)(n/e)^{n}}{\prod_{i}\xi(a_{v,i}n)(a_{v,i}n/e)^{a_{v,i}n}}\quad\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\big(\prod_{i}\xi(a_{v,i}n)(a_{v,i}n/e)^{a_{v,i}n}\big)\big(\prod_{i^{\prime}}\xi(a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n)(a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n/e)^{a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n}\big)}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\xi(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n/e)^{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}}\prod_{v\in V}\frac{\xi(n)}{\prod_{i}\xi(a_{v,i}n){a_{v,i}}^{a_{v,i}n}}\quad\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\big(\prod_{i}\xi(a_{v,i}n)\big)\big(\prod_{i^{\prime}}\xi(a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n)\big)\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}})^{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n}\right)}{\xi(n)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\xi(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n){b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}^{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}}p(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})},

(18)

where

p(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)=\prod_{v\in V}\frac{\xi(n)}{\prod_{i}\xi(a_{v,i}n)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\big(\prod_{i}\xi(a_{v,i}n)\big)\big(\prod_{i^{\prime}}\xi(a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n)\big)}{\xi(n)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\xi(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)}

(19)

and

f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})=-\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{i}a_{v,i}\log a_{v,i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}\right).

(20)

In order to bound the exponential behaviour of
\mathbf{E}X
, we will maximize
f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
and show that the main contribution to the sum in (
18
) comes from the term where all the parameters are equal.
Let
\hat{a}=(\hat{a}_{i})_{i\in[k]}
with all
\hat{a}_{i}=1/k
, and
\hat{b}=(\hat{b}_{i,i^{\prime}})_{i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}}
with all
\hat{b}_{i,i^{\prime}}=1/k(k-1)
.
Also, define
\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}=(\hat{a})_{v\in V}
and
\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}=(\hat{b})_{e\in E}
.
Note that
\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
are only a valid set of parameters provided that
k(k-1)\mid n
, since otherwise their entries are not in
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
. However, since we are only interested in an upper bound on
\mathbf{E}X
we may proceed with these values by bounding
\mathbf{E}X
in a larger space that contains
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
d
and
k
be integers such that
d\geq 2
and
\frac{d^{2}-1}{d\log d}<2(k-1)
.
Let
f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
be defined as in (
20
). Then the maximum of
f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
for
\text{\boldmath$a$}\in\real^{k|V|}
and
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\real^{k(k-1)|E|}
subject to (
15
) and (
16
)
is uniquely attained at the point in which
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
and
\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
, and equals

f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})=\log\left(\frac{(k-1)^{d}}{k^{d-2}}\right)^{(d+1)/2}.

Before proving Proposition
4.1
, we need the following technical result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
d,k\geq 1
be integers. For any
\text{\boldmath$a$}=(a_{v})_{v\in V}
with
a_{v}=(a_{v,i})_{i\in[k]}\in\real^{k}
satisfying (
15
),

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\log\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)\leq\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{d+1}{dk}+\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\sum_{v\in V}\left\langle a_{v},a_{v}\right\rangle\right).

Proof.

Let
c=\sum_{v\in V}a_{v}
and
j=(1,\ldots,1)\in\real^{k}
. From (
15
), we have that
\left\langle j,a_{v}\right\rangle=1
and thus
\left\langle j,c\right\rangle=d+1
.
In particular,

1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\geq 1-\left\langle a_{v},j\right\rangle=0,

(21)

so the left-hand side of the inequality in the lemma is well-defined (but possibly
-\infty
).
Moreover, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\left\langle c,c\right\rangle=\frac{1}{k}\left\langle j,j\right\rangle\left\langle c,c\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{k}\left\langle j,c\right\rangle^{2}=\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{k}.

Hence, by writting

\rho=\sum_{v\in V}\left\langle a_{v},a_{v}\right\rangle,

we obtain

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\in V\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\sum_{v,v^{\prime}\in V}\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle-\sum_{v\in V}\left\langle a_{v},a_{v}\right\rangle=\left\langle c,c\right\rangle-\rho\geq\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{k}-\rho.

(22)

Before we proceed to prove our main inequality, recall that
E
is the edge set of
K_{d+1}
(with a fixed orientation). Thus we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\log\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\in V\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\log\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\in V\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)

\displaystyle=\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\in V\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)\right)^{1/d(d+1)}.

We apply the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means to all
1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle
above (which are non-negative, as observed in (
21
)), and conclude that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\log\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)\leq\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v,v^{\prime}\in V\\
v\neq v^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)\right).

Combining this and (
22
) yields

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\log\big(1-\left\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\right\rangle\big)\leq\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\Big(d(d+1)-\frac{(d+1)^{2}}{k}+\rho\Big)\right),

which completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Assume throughout the proof that
\frac{d^{2}-1}{d\log d}<2(k-1)
.
Fix any
\text{\boldmath$a$}\in\real^{k|V|}
satisfying (
15
), and let

g(\text{\boldmath$a$})=h(\text{\boldmath$a$})+\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\frac{d(d+1)-(d+1)^{2}/k+\rho(\text{\boldmath$a$})}{d(d+1)}\right),

where

h(\text{\boldmath$a$})=-\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{v,i}\log a_{v,i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho(\text{\boldmath$a$})=\sum_{v\in V}\left\langle a_{v},a_{v}\right\rangle.

We will maximize
f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
for such fixed
a
and with
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\real^{k(k-1)|E|}
subject only to

b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\in[k]\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}=1.

(23)

In view of this relaxation, we can regard each
b_{e}=(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}})_{i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}}
as an arbitrary probability distribution, and maximize each term

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}\right)
in (
20
) separately.
For each
e=vv^{\prime}\in E
, we define another probability distribution given by

b^{*}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}=\frac{a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}}{z_{e}},\qquad\text{for}\quad i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime},

where

z_{e}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}

is the normalizing factor, and we write
b^{*}_{e}=(b^{*}_{e,i,i^{\prime}})_{i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}}
.
Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}\right)=\log z_{e}-D_{KL}(b_{e}\|b^{*}_{e}),

where
D_{KL}(b_{e}\|b^{*}_{e})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}{b^{*}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}\right)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from
b_{e}
to
b^{*}_{e}
.
By Gibb’s inequality,
D_{KL}(b_{e}\|b^{*}_{e})\geq 0
with equality iff and only if
b_{e}=b^{*}_{e}
. As a result,

\max_{\text{\boldmath$b$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons1}}}f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})=h(\text{\boldmath$a$})+\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e},

(24)

with one unique maximizer at
\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$b^{*}$}:=(b^{*}_{e})_{e\in E}
. Note that if
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
then
\text{\boldmath$b^{*}$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
.
We proceed to bound the right-hand side of (
24
). In view of (
15
), we can write

\log z_{e}=\log\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{v,i}\left(1-a_{v^{\prime},i}\right)=\log\left(1-\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{v,i}a_{v^{\prime},i}\right)=\log(1-\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\rangle),

and then, by Lemma
4.2
,

\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\log(1-\langle a_{v},a_{v^{\prime}}\rangle)\leq\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{d+1}{dk}+\frac{1}{d(d+1)}\rho(\text{\boldmath$a$})\right),

so

h(\text{\boldmath$a$})+\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e}\leq g(\text{\boldmath$a$}).

Combining this with (
24
) yields

\max_{\text{\boldmath$b$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons1}}}f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})\leq g(\text{\boldmath$a$}).

(25)

We will bound
g(\text{\boldmath$a$})
by applying Proposition
3.2
to
\text{\boldmath$a$}=(a_{v,i})_{v\in V,i\in[k]}
, which is a row-stochastic
(d+1)\times k
matrix.
Note that our assumption
\frac{d^{2}-1}{d\log d}<2(k-1)
implies

\frac{d}{2}<\frac{k-1}{d}\frac{d^{3}\log d}{d^{2}-1}=\frac{k-1}{d}c_{d+1},

where
c_{d+1}
is defined as in (
8
).
Therefore, Proposition
3.2
(with
q=d+1
,
c=d/2
and
A=\text{\boldmath$a$}
) yields

\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{d(k-1)+\frac{k}{d+1}\rho(\text{\boldmath$a$})-1}{d(k-1)}\right)\leq\log k-\frac{1}{d+1}h(\text{\boldmath$a$}),

with equality iff
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
. After some elementary manipulations, we can rewrite this as

h(\text{\boldmath$a$})+\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\frac{d(k-1)+\frac{k}{d+1}\rho(\text{\boldmath$a$})-1}{dk}\right)\leq(d+1)\log k+\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\frac{d(k-1)}{dk}\right),

which immediately implies

h(\text{\boldmath$a$})+\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(1-\frac{d+1}{dk}+\frac{\rho(\text{\boldmath$a$})}{d(d+1)}\right)\leq(d+1)\log k+\binom{d+1}{2}\log\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right).

Therefore, noting that
\rho(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$})=(d+1)/k
,

g(\text{\boldmath$a$})\leq g(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$})=\log\left(\frac{(k-1)^{d}}{k^{d-2}}\right)^{(d+1)/2}=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}),

with equality iff
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
.
Combining this with (
25
), we obtain the desired bound

\max_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:acons1} and~\eqref{eq:abcons1}}}f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})\leq\max_{\text{\boldmath$a$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:acons1}}}\max_{\text{\boldmath$b$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons1}}}f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})\leq\max_{\text{\boldmath$a$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:acons1}}}g(\text{\boldmath$a$})=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}).

Note that for
\text{\boldmath$a$}\neq\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
, we have
g(\text{\boldmath$a$})<g(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$})
and thus
f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})<f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})
. Furthermore, if
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
but
\text{\boldmath$b$}\neq\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
then, recalling that the maximum in (
24
) is uniquely attained at
\text{\boldmath$b^{*}$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
, we conclude that
f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})<g(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$})=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})
. This finishes the proof.
∎

We now proceed to prove the main result in this section.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Recall from (
18
) that

\mathbf{E}X=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}}p(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})},

where we sum over all
\text{\boldmath$a$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k|V|}
and
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k(k-1)|E|}
satisfying (
15
) and (
16
). In particular, a crude upper bound on the number of terms is given by

O\left(n^{k|V|+k(k-1)|E|}\right),

since each entry in
a
or
b
can take at most
n+1
values.
Moreover, from (
19
) and since
1\leq\xi(x)=O(\sqrt{x})
as

, we can bound the polynomial factor

(26)

where the hidden constant in the big

notation does not depend on

. Hence, by Proposition

,

\mathbf{E}X\leq e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})}\sum_{\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$}}p(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)=O\left(n^{(d+1)(k+1)+\binom{d+1}{2}k(k+1)}\right)\left(\frac{(k-1)^{d}}{k^{d-2}}\right)^{(d+1)n/2},

which completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

4.2
Strongly equitable colourings. (Proof of Proposition
2.2
.)

In this section we prove Proposition
2.2
regarding the expected number of
strongly equitable colourings of a random lift. Here we allow
G
to be any fixed

d
-regular graph, not necessarily
G=K_{d+1}
, and always assume that
n
is
divisible by
k
. Let
Y
be the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a
random
n
-lift of
G
.

Note that the only place in Section
4.1
where we used the fact that
G=K_{d+1}
was in the proof of Lemma
4.2
. Therefore, equation (
18
) is still valid for a general
d
-regular graph
G
. In particular, restricting the sum to the terms in which
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
gives the expected number of strongly equitable colourings, that is

\mathbf{E}Y=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$b$}}p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})},

(27)

with

\displaystyle p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\xi(n)}{\xi(n/k)^{k}}\right)^{|V|}\left(\frac{\xi(n/k)^{2k}}{\xi(n)}\right)^{|E|}\prod_{e\in E}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\xi(b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)}

(28)

\displaystyle f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})

\displaystyle=|V|\log k-|E|\log k^{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\log b_{e,i,i^{\prime}},

(29)

and where the sum is over all
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{k(k-1)|E|}
satisfying

\displaystyle b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\forall e\in E,i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1/k\quad\forall e\in E,i\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=1/k\quad\forall e\in E,i^{\prime}\in[k].

(30)

Moreover, the discussion in the proof of Proposition
4.1
leading to (
24
) still holds for general

, and gives for
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}

\max_{\text{\boldmath$b$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons1}}}f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})=h(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$})+\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e}=|V|\log k+|E|\log\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}),

with one unique maximizer at
\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
. (Note that we did not use Lemma
4.2
to bound
\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e}
, since we are only concerned about the case
\text{\boldmath$a$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$}
.) Since
\hat{b}
trivially satisfies (
30
), we obtain the following analogue of Proposition
4.1
for general
d
-regular
G
but restricted to strongly equitable colourings.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
be defined as in (
20
). Then the maximum of
f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
subject to (
30
)
is uniquely attained at the point in which
\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
, and equals

f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})=\log\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right).

We will estimate the sum in (
27
) by using our version of Laplace’s method, Proposition
3.4
.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

We begin by defining a bipartite graph
\Gamma=\Gamma(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
so that we may express the equality constraints in (
30
) in terms of its unsigned incidence matrix
D
and use Proposition
3.4
.
The idea is to associate each equation in (
30
) to a vertex of
\Gamma
and every variable to an edge in a way that preserves the incidence relations. To do this, we assign label
w_{e,1,i}
to equation
\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}=1/k
and label
w_{e,2,i^{\prime}}
to equation
\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}=1/k
.
The vertex set of
\Gamma
is
V_{\Gamma}=V_{\Gamma,1}\cup V_{\Gamma,2}
, where

V_{\Gamma,1}=\{w_{e,1,i}:e\in E,i\in[k]\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{\Gamma,2}=\{w_{e,2,i^{\prime}}:e\in E,i^{\prime}\in[k]\}

are the two sides of a bipartition.
The edge set is

E_{\Gamma}=\{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}:e\in E,i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}\},

where each edge
b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
has endpoints
w_{e,1,i}
and
w_{e,2,i^{\prime}}
(i.e. the labels of the two equations in which variable
b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
appears).
Then the equality constraints in (
30
) are equivalent to

D\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$y$},

(31)

where
D
is the unsigned incidence matrix of
\Gamma
and
y
is the vector in
\real{}^{|V_{\Gamma}|}
whose entries are all
1/k
.
The equations in (
31
) are consistent, since they admit the solution

\displaystyle b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall e\in E,i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i^{\prime}\notin\{i,i+1\}

\displaystyle b_{e,i,i+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\quad\forall e\in E,i\in[k],

(32)

where we use arithmetic modulo
k
for indices
i,i^{\prime}
.
We observe a few easy facts about
\Gamma
. First,

|V_{\Gamma}|=2k|E|\qquad\text{and}\qquad|E_{\Gamma}|=k(k-1)|E|

Also,
\Gamma
has exactly
|E|
connected components. More precisely, for each
e\in E
, the set of all vertices of the form
w_{e,1,i}
or
w_{e,2,i^{\prime}}
induces a connected component of
\Gamma
. Each of these components is isomorphic to
F:=K_{k,k}-M
, i.e. the complete bipartite graph
K_{k,k}
minus one perfect matching
M
. In particular,
\Gamma
has at least one cycle (since
k\geq 3
). Since
\Gamma
is bipartite, it is well-known (see e.g. Theorem 8.2.1 in
[
14
]
) that
D
has rank
|V_{\Gamma}|-|E|
, and therefore
\mathbb{V}=\Ker(D)
has dimension

r=|E_{\Gamma}|-|V_{\Gamma}|+|E|=(k^{2}-3k+1)|E|.

Now we calculate
\tau(\Gamma)
. Since each maximal forest in
\Gamma
is bijectively determined by selecting a spanning tree in each component, we conclude that the number of maximal forests in
\Gamma
is

\tau(\Gamma)=\tau(F)^{|E|}.

Naturally, next we count the number of spanning trees of
F
.

Let
I_{k}
and
J_{k}
denote the
k\times k
identity matrix and the
k\times k
matrix whose entries are all
1
s, respectively.
With an appropriate ordering of the vertices, the adjacency matrix of
F
is

A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}\otimes(J_{k}-I_{k}),

and has eigenvalues

\{k-1,(1)_{k-1},(-1)_{k-1},1-k\},

where the subindices indicate multiplicities. Therefore, the Laplacian matrix

Q=(k-1)I_{2k}-A

of
F
has eigenvalues

\{0,(k-2)_{k-1},(k)_{k-1},2k-2\}.

By Kirchhoff’s Matrix Tree Theorem
[
6
]
, the number of spanning trees of
F
is

\tau(F)=\frac{1}{2k}(k-2)^{k-1}k^{k-1}(2k-2)=(k-2)^{k-1}k^{k-2}(k-1)

and thus

\tau(\Gamma)=\left((k-1)k^{k-2}(k-2)^{k-1}\right)^{|E|}.

(33)

Let

K=\{\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\real^{|E_{\Gamma}|}:0\leq b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\leq 1/k\}

and

K_{1}=\left\{\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\real^{|E_{\Gamma}|}:\tfrac{0.9}{k(k-1)}\leq b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\leq\tfrac{1.1}{k(k-1)}\right\}.

Clearly,
K
is a compact convex set with non-empty interior
K^{\circ}
, and any choice of
b
that satisfies (
30
) lies inside
K
. Let

\phi(\text{\boldmath$b$})=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})=|V|\log k-|E|\log k^{2}-\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\log b_{e,i,i^{\prime}},

which is continuous on
K
, and

\psi(\text{\boldmath$b$})=\prod_{e\in E}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}},

which is continuous and positive on
K_{1}
.
By Proposition
4.3
, the maximum of
\phi(\text{\boldmath$b$})
in
K
subject to (
31
) is uniquely attained at
\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}\in K_{1}\subset K^{\circ}
. Moreover,
\phi(\text{\boldmath$b$})
is twice continuously differentiable in the interior
K^{\circ}
, and its partial derivatives are

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}}=-(\log b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}+1)

and

\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}\partial b_{e^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}}}=\begin{cases}-1/b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}&\text{if $(e,i,i^{\prime})=(e^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence, the Hessian matrix of
\phi
at
\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
is

H=-k(k-1)I_{|E_{\Gamma}|}.

Then, for any
|E_{\Gamma}|\times r
matrix
U
whose columns are a basis of
\mathbb{V}
,

\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})=\frac{\det(-U^{T}HU)}{\det U^{T}U}=\frac{\det(k(k-1)U^{T}U)}{\det U^{T}U}=\big(k(k-1)\big)^{r}\neq 0.

Let

\mathbb{X}_{n}=\left\{\text{\boldmath$b$}\in K\cap\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}:D\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$y$}\right\}.

The solution described in (
32
) belongs to
K
and, since
k\mid n
, also to
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}^{|E_{\Gamma}|}
, so
\mathbb{X}_{n}
is not empty. For each
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
, let

T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$v$})=p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})}

and

c_{n}=k^{k|V|/2-k|E|}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)|V|/2-r/2}.

First, in view of (
26
), for
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}
,

p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)/c_{n}=O(n^{|V|+2k|E|}/c_{n})=e^{o(n)}

and combined with Proposition
4.3
this gives

T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=c_{n}(p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)/c_{n})e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$})}=O(c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})+o(n)}).

Now note that we have chosen
c_{n}
such that due to (
28
) and the fact that
\xi(x)\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}
as
x\to\infty
, and after a few computations, we have that for
\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}\cap K_{1}
,

p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)=c_{n}(\psi(\text{\boldmath$b$})+o(1)).

Finally, as we’ve met all of the conditions of Proposition
3.4
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Y

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$b$}\in\mathbb{X}_{n}}T_{n}(\text{\boldmath$b$})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\psi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})}{\tau(\Gamma)^{1/2}\det(-H|_{\mathbb{V}})^{1/2}}(2\pi n)^{r/2}c_{n}e^{n\phi(\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(k(k-1)\right)^{k(k-1)|E|/2}k^{k|V|/2-k|E|}}{\left((k-1)k^{k-2}(k-2)^{k-1}\right)^{|E|/2}\big(k(k-1)\big)^{r/2}}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)|V|/2}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=k^{k|V|/2}\left(\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{k(k-2)}\right)^{(k-1)|E|/2}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)|V|/2}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{n}.

This completes the proof of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

As in the previous section, let

-lift of

. In this section we continue to

5.1
Counting Argument

In order to calculate

, we count pairs of balanced colourings. To each

we assign a

where

\Pi^{-1}(v)
that receive colour
(i,j)
(that is, colour
i
in the first
colouring and colour
j
in the second colouring).
Each matrix
A_{v}
must satisfy

\displaystyle a_{v,i,j}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall v\in V,i,j\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{j}a_{v,i,j}

\displaystyle=1/k,\quad\forall v\in V,i\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{i}a_{v,i,j}

\displaystyle=1/k,\quad\forall v\in V,j\in[k].

(34)

In particular,
kA_{v}
is a doubly-stochastic matrix.

For each
e=(v,v^{\prime})\in E
, let
b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
denote the proportion of edges in
\Pi^{-1}(e)
which join a vertex of
\Pi^{-1}(v)
with colour
(i,j)
to a vertex of
\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
with colour
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
.
Here
i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[k]
, but we require
i
to be distinct from
i^{\prime}
and
j
to be distinct from
j^{\prime}
to assure the colourings are proper.
Hence, to each
e=(v,v^{\prime})\in E
, we assign a four-dimensional array
B_{e}=(b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{K}
, where

K=\{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[k]^{4}:i\neq i^{\prime},j\neq j^{\prime}\}.

Furthermore, each
B_{e}
must satisfy:

\displaystyle b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq 0,\quad\forall e=(v,v^{\prime})\in E,(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i,j^{\prime}\neq j}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a_{v,i,j},\quad\forall e=(v,v^{\prime})\in E,i,j\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime},j\neq j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}},\quad\forall e=(v,v^{\prime})\in E,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[k].

(35)

We will write
\text{\boldmath$A$}=(A_{v})_{v\in V}
and
\text{\boldmath$B$}=(B_{e})_{e\in E}
for short. Note that in addition to (
34
) and (
35
), each entry of
A_{v}
and
B_{e}
must be in
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
.

In the following calculation, we obtain
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
by summing, for every valid choice of
A
and
B
,
the number of triples (lift, colouring 1, colouring 2) compatible with such
A
and
B
divided by the total number of lifts.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Y^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!^{|E|}}\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}}\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n}{nA_{v}}\prod_{e\in E}\frac{(nA_{v})!(nA_{v^{\prime}})!}{(nB_{e})!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!^{|E|}}\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$}}\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n}{nA_{v}}\prod_{e\in E}(nA_{v})!(nA_{v^{\prime}})!\sum_{\text{\boldmath$B$}}\prod_{e\in E}\frac{1}{(nB_{e})!}

(36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}}\mathrm{poly}(n)\prod_{v\in V}\frac{1}{\prod_{i,j}{a_{v,i,j}}^{na_{v,i,j}}}\prod_{e\in E}\prod_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}\left(\frac{a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}\right)^{nb_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}}\mathrm{poly}(n)\exp\big(nf(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\big)

(37)

where
\mathrm{poly}(n)
is some function that is polynomial in
n
and

f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})=-\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{i,j}a_{v,i,j}\log a_{v,i,j}+\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}\right)

(38)

is the function we optimize in the next section.

5.2
Optimization

We will show that the exponential part in
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
is maximized by the term in which each
a_{v,i,j}=1/k^{2}
and each
b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=1/k^{2}(k-1)^{2}
.
We introduce some notation.
Let
\hat{A}=(\hat{a}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[k]}
with all
\hat{a}_{i,j}=1/k^{2}
, and
\hat{B}=(\hat{b}_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}
with all
\hat{b}_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=1/k^{2}(k-1)^{2}
.
We also write
\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}=(\hat{A},\ldots,\hat{A})
and
\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}=(\hat{B},\ldots,\hat{B})
. As in Section
4.1
, we note
\hat{A}
and

\hat{B}
are only valid assignments to
A
and
B
,
respectively, if
k^{2}(k-1)^{2}
divides
n
, but as we merely seek an upper bound
for
f
we may do so on a larger space that does include
\hat{A}
and

\hat{B}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
d<\ell_{k}
. Let
f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})
be defined as in (
38
). Then the maximum of
f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})
subject to (
34
) and (
35
)
is uniquely attained at the point in which
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}
and
\text{\boldmath$B$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}
, and equals

f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$})=\log\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2n}.

Proof.

This is easier than for
\mathbf{E}X
.

Fix any
A
satisfying (
34
), and let

g(\text{\boldmath$A$})=\sum_{v\in V}\left(h(A_{v})+\frac{d}{2}\log(1-2/k+\rho(A_{v}))\right),

where

h(A_{v})=-\sum_{i,j\in[k]}a_{v,i,j}\log a_{v,i,j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho(A_{v})=\sum_{i,j\in[k]}{a_{v,i,j}}^{2}.

We will maximize
f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})
for such fixed
A
and with
B
subject only to

b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=1.

(39)

In view of this relaxation, we can regard each
B_{e}=(b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}
as an arbitrary probability distribution, and maximize each term

\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}\right)
in (
38
) separately.
For each
e\in E
, we define another probability distribution given by

b^{*}_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\frac{a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{z_{e}},\qquad\text{for}\quad(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K,

where

z_{e}=\sum_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

is the normalizing factor, and we write
B^{*}_{e}=(b^{*}_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}
.
Then

\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}\right)=\log z_{e}-D_{KL}(B_{e}\|B^{*}_{e}),

where
D_{KL}(B_{e}\|B^{*}_{e})=\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{b^{*}_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}\right)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence from
B_{e}
to
B^{*}_{e}
.
By Gibb’s inequality,
D_{KL}(B_{e}\|B^{*}_{e})\geq 0
with equality if and only if
B_{e}=B^{*}_{e}
. As a result,

\max_{\text{\boldmath$B$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons}}}f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})=\sum_{v\in V}h(A_{v})+\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e},

(40)

with one unique maximizer at
\text{\boldmath$B$}=\text{\boldmath$B^{*}$}:=(B^{*}_{e})_{e\in E}
. Note that if
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}
then
\text{\boldmath$B^{*}$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}
.
We proceed to bound
\log z_{e}
. Using inclusion-exclusion, the fact that
A
satisfies (
34
), and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\log z_{e}

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{i,j\in[k]}a_{v,i,j}\left(\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[k]}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\sum_{j^{\prime}\in[k]}a_{v^{\prime},i,j^{\prime}}-\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[k]}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j}+a_{v^{\prime},i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\sum_{i,j\in[k]}a_{v,i,j}\left(1-2/k+a_{v^{\prime},i,j}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-2/k+\sum_{i,j\in[k]}a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i,j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\sqrt{1-2/k+\sum_{i,j\in[k]}a_{v,i,j}^{2}}\sqrt{1-2/k+\sum_{i,j\in[k]}a_{v^{\prime},i,j}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1-2/k+\rho(A_{v}))+\frac{1}{2}\log(1-2/k+\rho(A_{v^{\prime}})).

Since each
v\in V
has degree
d
,

\sum_{e\in E}\log z_{e}\leq\frac{d}{2}\log(1-2/k+\rho(A_{v})),

and combining this with (
40
) yields

\max_{\text{\boldmath$B$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons}}}f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\leq g(\text{\boldmath$A$}).

(41)

We will bound
g(\text{\boldmath$A$})
by applying Theorem
3.1
to matrix
kA_{v}
for each
v\in V
, which is a doubly-stochastic matrix.
Note that
d<\ell_{k}
implies
d/2<c_{k}
, where
c_{k}
is defined in (
8
).
Therefore, Theorem
3.1
(with
q=k
,
c=d/2
and
A=kA_{v}
) yields

\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{(k-1)^{2}+\rho(kA_{v})-1}{(k-1)^{2}}\right)\leq\log k-\frac{1}{k}h(kA_{v}),

with equality if and only if
A_{v}=\hat{A}
. Noting that
\rho(kA_{v})=k^{2}\rho(A_{v})
and
\frac{1}{k}h(kA_{v})=h(A_{v})-\log k
, we obtain

h(A_{v})+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{(k-1)^{2}+k^{2}\rho(A_{v})-1}{k^{2}}\right)\leq 2\log k+\frac{d}{2}\log\left(\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{k^{2}}\right).

Thus, after summing over
v\in V
and simplifying, we get

g(\text{\boldmath$A$})\leq g(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$})=\log\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2n}=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}),

with equality if and only if
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}
. Combining this with (
41
), we obtain the desired bound

\displaystyle\max_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:acons2} and~\eqref{eq:abcons2}}}f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\text{\boldmath$A$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:acons2}}}\max_{\text{\boldmath$B$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:brelaxedcons}}}f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\text{\boldmath$A$}\text{ s.t.~\eqref{eq:acons2}}}g(\text{\boldmath$A$})=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}).

Note that for
\text{\boldmath$A$}\neq\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}
, we have
g(\text{\boldmath$A$})<g(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$})
and thus
f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})<f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$})
. Furthermore, if
\text{\boldmath$A$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}
but
\text{\boldmath$B$}\neq\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}
then, recalling that the maximum in (
40
) is uniquely attained at
\text{\boldmath$B^{*}$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$}
, we conclude that
f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$},\text{\boldmath$B$})<g(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$})=f(\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$},\text{\boldmath$\hat{B}$})
. This finishes the proof.
∎

5.3
Inner Sum

In order to accurately approximate
\mathbf{E}Y^{2}
, we now return to (
36
) and find asymptotics for the inner sum over the
{\bf B}_{e}
’s. In particular, we prove the following proposition:

Proposition 5.2
.

Define
S
to be the set of
A
satisfying (
34
) and
||\text{\boldmath$\hat{A}$}-\text{\boldmath$A$}||_{\infty}<\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}
and fix
\text{\boldmath$A$}\in S
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\text{\boldmath$A$})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$B$}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n)!}

\displaystyle\sim(\nicefrac{{n}}{{e}})^{-n|E|}\gamma(n,k)^{\frac{1}{2}|E|}\exp\left(\frac{-nk^{2}(k-1)^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{vv^{\prime}\in E}\left(\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{i,j}\left(a_{v,i,j}+a_{v^{\prime},i,j}-\frac{2}{k^{2}}\right)^{2}\quad+\right.\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\quad\left.\left.\left.\frac{1}{2\lambda^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j}(a_{v,i,j}-a_{v^{\prime},i,j})^{2}+\frac{2}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}\log\frac{1}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)

where

\gamma(n,k):=\frac{k^{(3k^{2}+1)}(k-1)^{4k(k-1)}}{(2\pi n)^{(2k^{2}-1)}((k-1)^{2}+1)^{(2k^{2}-1)}(k-2)^{(k^{2}-1)}}

(42)

and

\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
are as defined in (
2
).

Proof.

We prove the proposition using the saddle point method. We start by expressing the
sum as the coefficient of a generating function.

Consider the generating function on variables
\{x_{e,i,j}\}\cup\{x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\}
for each
e\in E
and
(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K
:

\prod_{e\in E}\prod_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{(x_{e,i,j}x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})^{t}}{t!}

We want to extract the coefficient when
t=b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n
. Recalling (
35
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{\boldmath$B$}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n)!}

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{e=(v,v^{\prime})}\left(\prod_{i,j\in[k]}{x_{e,i,j}}^{a_{v,i,j}n}\prod_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[k]}{x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}^{a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n}\right)\right]\quad\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\prod_{e\in E}\prod_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\frac{(x_{e,i,j}x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})^{t}}{(\underbrace{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n}_{t})!}

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{e=(v,v^{\prime})}\left(\prod_{i,j\in[k]}{x_{e,i,j}}^{a_{v,i,j}n}\prod_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[k]}{x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}^{a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n}\right)\right]\quad\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\exp\left(\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}x_{e,i,j}x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)

We extract this coefficient using the residue theorem:

\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{|E|2k^{2}}}\int\frac{\exp(\sum\limits_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}z_{e,i,j}z^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})}{\prod_{e}\left(\prod_{i,j}{z_{e,i,j}}^{a_{v,i,j}n+1}\prod_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}{z^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}^{a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}n+1}\right)}d\vec{\bf z}

where we’ve set
x_{e,i,j}=z_{e,i,j}
and
x^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=z^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
to
emphasize that they are complex variables.

In using the saddle point method, we will use a circular path of radius
\rho
.
We use the same radius in every dimension. The method permits us to make this
choice, and we do so because it works.

We can now set
z_{e,i,j}=\rho e^{i\theta_{e,i,j}}
and
z^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\rho e^{i\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}
. The change of variables gives
dz_{e,i,j}=i\rho e^{i\theta_{e,i,j}}d\theta_{e,i,j}
(and similarly for the primed variables) so we cancel all

|E|2k^{2}
copies of
i
in the denominator as well as one copy of each
z_{e,i,j}
and
z^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
from the denominator and get

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{|E|2k^{2}}}\int\frac{\exp(\rho^{2}\sum\limits_{e}\sum\limits_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}e^{i(\theta_{e,i,j}+\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})})}{\rho^{|E|2n}\exp\left(in\sum\limits_{e}(\sum\limits_{i,j}\theta_{e,i,j}a_{v,i,j}+\sum\limits_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\right)}d\vec{\bm{\theta}}

where
\vec{\bm{\theta}}
is a vector of all of the
\theta
s and
\theta^{\prime}
s.

Now we set all of the
\theta
s to zero to find the value on the real line and
choose
\rho
to optimize (the log of) this value:

\left(\rho^{2}|E|k^{2}(k-1)^{2}-|E|2n\log\rho\right)^{\prime}=0

which is accomplished, after some elementary calculus, by

\rho=\sqrt{\frac{n}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}}.

Plugging in
\rho
, we can rewrite the equation above as:

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{|E|2k^{2}}}\left(\frac{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}{n}\right)^{|E|n}\int e^{h(\vec{\bm{\theta}})}\ d\vec{\bm{\theta}}

so that we may analyze
h({\bm{\theta}})
:

\displaystyle h({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}\sum_{e}\sum_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}e^{i(\theta_{e,i,j}+\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})}\quad-

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\quad in\sum_{e}\left(\sum_{i,j}\theta_{e,i,j}a_{v,i,j}+\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)

Consider

\displaystyle|e^{h({\bm{\theta}})}|

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\Re\left(\frac{n}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}\sum_{e}\sum_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}e^{i(\theta_{e,i,j}+\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})}\right.\right.\quad-

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\left.\left.\quad in\sum_{e}\left(\sum_{i,j}\theta_{e,i,j}a_{v,i,j}+\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{n}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}\sum_{e}\sum_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}\Re(e^{i(\theta_{e,i,j}+\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})})\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{n}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}\sum_{e}\sum_{(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}\cos(\theta_{e,i,j}+\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\right)

where we use
\Re(z)
to denote the real part of
z
.

Thus in order to maximize
|e^{h({\bm{\theta}})}|
, we should make
\theta_{e,i,j}+\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
close to zero for every pair
\theta_{e,i,j},\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
. If some pair has sum far from zero, we should be able to
show
|e^{h({\bm{\theta}})}|
is negligible. We now formalize this notion.

For each edge
e
, define

\theta_{e}=\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\left(\sum_{i,j}\theta_{e,i,j}-\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)

to be a weighted average of the
\theta
s. Then define
\delta_{e,i,j}
and
\delta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
by

\theta_{e,i,j}=\theta_{e}+\delta_{e,i,j}\qquad\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=-\theta_{e}-\delta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}.

Clearly, any choice of the
\theta_{e,i,j}
s and
\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
determines

\theta_{e}
s and the
\delta
s. Furthermore, as

\displaystyle\theta_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\left(\sum_{i,j}\theta_{e,i,j}-\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\left(\sum_{i,j}(\theta+\delta_{e,i,j})-\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}(-\theta-\delta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle=\theta_{e}+\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\left(\sum_{i,j}\delta_{e,i,j}+\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\delta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)

we see that every choice of the
\theta_{e}
s and
\delta
s satisfying the linear
constraints

\left(\sum_{i,j}\delta_{e,i,j}+\sum_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\delta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\right)=0

determines a valid choice of
\theta_{e,i,j}
s and
\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
s.

Let
R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{|E|(2k^{2}+1)}
be the subspace of choices
of
\theta_{e}
’s and
\delta
’s that correspond to valid choices of

\theta_{e,i,j}
’s and
\theta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
’s. Partition
R
into
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
,
where each point in
R_{1}
satisfies

|\delta_{e,i,j}|,|\delta^{\prime}_{e,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}|\leq\varepsilon=\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}

for all
e,i,j,i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}
(and the
\theta_{e}
s take any value in
[0,2\pi]
)
and
R_{2}
is the complementary region.

We claim that

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2n}

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{(k^{2}-k+1)|V|-\frac{1}{2}(k^{2}-1)|E|}(k-1)^{(2k^{2}-2k)|E|}}{\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}(k-1)^{2}|E|}(k-2)^{\frac{1}{2}(k^{2}-1)|E|}h(d,k)^{\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{2}}}(2\pi n)^{-(k-1)|V|}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2n}

This completes the proof of Proposition
2.3
.

6
Joint Moments

Recall that
Y
counts the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a random
n
-lift of
G
. In this section we allow
G
to be any
d
-regular graph. Recall that for fixed
j\geq 3
, we denote the number of
j
-cycles in a random lift by
Z_{j}
. In this section, we estimate the expected value of the joint moment
YZ_{j}
by proving the following proposition:

Proposition 6.1
.

If
Y
is the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a random
n
-lift
L
of
G
and, for
j\geq 3
,
Z_{j}
counts the number of
j
-cycles in
L
, then

\frac{\mathbf{E}(YZ_{j})}{\mathbf{E}Y}\sim\lambda_{j}(1+\delta_{j})

where
\lambda_{j}
and
\delta_{j}
are as defined in (
6
).

In order to prove Proposition
6.1
we require two lemmas. The first is a lemma of Friedman, originally proved in
[
11
]
, as presented in
[
16
]
:

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that
G
is
d
-regular with
d\geq 3
and let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{|V|}
be the eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of
G
. For
i=1,\ldots,|V|
, let
{\beta_{i}}^{+}
and
{\beta_{i}}^{-}
denote the roots of the quadratic
x^{2}-\alpha_{i}x+d-1=0
. That is,

{\beta_{i}}^{+}=\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha_{i}+\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}\alpha_{i}^{2}-(d-1)},\quad\beta_{i}^{-}=\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha_{i}-\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}\alpha_{i}^{2}-(d-1)}.

Then the number of non-backtracking closed
j
-walks in
G
is given by

\displaystyle c_{j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}|V|(d-2)(1+(-1)^{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{|V|}((\beta_{i}^{+})^{j}+(\beta_{i}^{-})^{j})

\displaystyle=(|E|-|V|)(1+(-1)^{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{|V|}((\beta_{i}^{+})^{j}+(\beta_{i}^{-})^{j})

where we note that in a
d
-regular graph we have

2|E|=\sum_{v\in V}d(v)=d|V|

and thus

\frac{1}{2}|V|(d-2)=\frac{1}{2}(d|V|-2|V|)=\frac{1}{2}(2|E|-2|V|)=|E|-|V|.

The second lemma considers properly colouring cycles.

Lemma 6.3
.

The number of ways to properly
k
-colour a rooted, directed cycle of length
j
is

(k-1)^{j}+(k-1)(-1)^{j}.

Proof.

Assign a unique color to each vertex in
K_{k}
. Each proper
k
-coloring of the

j
-cycle corresponds to a closed walk of length
j
in
K_{k}
: starting with the
color of the root, travel in the direction of the cycle to the vertex
corresponding to the next color. The total number of closed
j
-walks in
K_{k}

is
\tr(A^{j})
, where
A=J_{k}-I_{k}
is the adjacency matrix of
K_{k}
. As
A

has eigenvalues
(k-1)
with multiplicity one and
-1
with multiplicity
k-1
,
the number of directed, rooted
j
-walks is
(k-1)^{j}+(k-1)(-1)^{j}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
6.1
.

Proof of Propositon
6.1
.

We estimate
\mathbf{E}(YZ_{j})
by counting ordered triples (lift, cycle, colouring), where the lift contains the
j
-cycle and is properly coloured by the colouring, and then dividing by the total number of lifts.

First, it will be convenient to consider rooted, ordered cycles. Each cycle of length
j
contains
2j
rooted, oriented cycles, so we simply correct our answer by a factor of
2j
.

For any rooted, oriented cycle
C=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{j})
, we define the
cycle type
of
C
to be the sequence
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{j})
such that
c_{i}\in\Pi^{-1}(v_{i})
; that is, the cycle type is the sequence of fibers containing the vertices of the cycle. Note that every cycle type corresponds to a closed, non-backtracking walk in
G
. Thus our first step in counting ordered triples is to choose a closed, non-backtracking walk
w
to determine the cycle type of our cycle.

Next we fix a colouring,
Q
, of the cycle, after which we choose the vertices in the lift to realize the cycle of the appropriate cycle type and colour them according to
Q
. If the cycle type revisits a fiber, there may be fewer than
n
options for each vertex. However, as the cycle has fixed length
j
and
n
goes to infinity, there are
(1-o(1))n
choices for each vertex, for a total contribution of
(1-o(1))n^{j}
.

Having specified
w,Q
and the vertices of the cycle, we now follow a process very similar to that of Section
4.1
resulting in (
17
). For each
e=vv^{\prime}\in E
we define
b^{\ast}_{e}=(b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}})_{i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}}
where
b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
denotes the proportion of edges in
\Pi^{-1}(e)
that connect a vertex of colour
i
in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
to a vertex of colour
i^{\prime}
in
\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
, excluding edges already prescribed by
w
and
Q
. We set
\text{\boldmath$b^{\ast}$}=(b^{\ast}_{e})_{e\in E}
. The entries of each
b^{\ast}_{e}
must be in
\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
and satisfy

\displaystyle b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad\forall e\in E,i,i^{\prime}\in[k],i\neq i^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a(v,i,w,Q)\quad\forall e=vv^{\prime}\in E,i\in[k]

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq i^{\prime}}b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a(v^{\prime},i,w,Q)\quad\forall e=vv^{\prime}\in E,i^{\prime}\in[k].

(50)

where
a(v,i,w,Q)
is the proportion of vertices in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
that still need to receive colour
i
, after accounting for the vertices already coloured by
Q
according to
w
, so that each colour is assigned to
\frac{1}{k}
of the vertices in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
(as
Y
is a strongly equitable colouring). Specifically, if
\ell_{i}
vertices in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
have already been assigned colour
i
by
w
and
Q
, then

a(v,i,w,Q)=\frac{1}{k}-\frac{\ell_{i}}{n}.

For each vertex
v
in
G
, we must colour the uncoloured vertices in the fiber
\Pi^{-1}(v)
. Define
\epsilon(v,w)
to be the number number of times
v
is encountered in
w
, and let

a^{\ast}_{v}=(a^{\ast}_{v,i})_{i\in[k]}=(a(v,i,w,Q))_{i\in[k]}.

Then the number of ways to colour the vertices is

\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n-\epsilon(v,w)}{a^{\ast}_{v}n}.

We then decide, for every
e=vv^{\prime}\in E
and distinct colours
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
, which sets of
b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n
in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
and
\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
will be matched. For each
e=vv^{\prime}
, define
a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v}=(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v,i})_{i\in[k]}
to be the sequence of vertices in
\Pi^{-1}(v)
that have been assigned colour
i
and have not already been matched to a vertex in
\Pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
by
w
. Then the number of ways to choose sets of vertices to be matched is

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\bigg(\prod_{i\in[k]}\frac{(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v,i}n)!}{\prod_{i^{\prime}\neq i}(b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)!}\bigg)\bigg(\prod_{i^{\prime}\in[k]}\frac{(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v^{\prime},i^{\prime}}n)!}{\prod_{i\neq i^{\prime}}(b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)!}\bigg)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v}n)!}{(b^{\ast}_{e}n)!}\frac{(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v^{\prime}}n)!}{(b^{\ast}_{e}n)!}

Finally, we need to choose a perfect matching between these sets, which can be done in

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,i^{\prime}\in[k]\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}n)!=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\end{subarray}}(b^{\ast}_{e}n)!

different ways. Putting everything together, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(YZ_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2j}\sum_{w}\sum_{Q}(1+o(1))n^{j}\frac{1}{n!^{|E|}}\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n-\epsilon(v,w)}{a^{\ast}_{v}n}\quad\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\quad\sum_{\text{\boldmath$b^{\ast}$}\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:jointcons}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v}n)!(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v^{\prime}}n)!}{(b^{\ast}_{e}n)!}

(51)

We now seek estimates for the equation above. First, note that for any walk
w
,

\sum_{v\in V}\epsilon(v,w)=j

as the walk is of length
j
. Similarly, across all
v\in V
, there are exactly
j
vertices already coloured with some colour. As
Y
is a strongly equitable colouring, we have

\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n-\epsilon(v,w)}{a^{\ast}_{v}n}\sim\frac{\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{j}}{n^{j}}\cdot\frac{(n!)^{|V|}}{(\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)!)^{|V|}}.

(52)

Furthermore, for any choice of
b^{\ast}
, the product over all edges of the
a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v}
encounters all
j
edges of
w
. For
t=1,\ldots,j
, set
b_{t}=b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
where
e
is the edge that connects vertices
t
and
t+1
of
w
and
Q
colours those vertices with colours
i
and
i^{\prime}
, respectively, where the
b_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
are as defined in (
30
) (as opposed to the
b^{\ast}_{e,i,i^{\prime}}
defined in (
50
)). Then

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v}n)!(a^{\ast\ast}_{e,v^{\prime}}n)!}{(b^{\ast}_{e}n)!}\sim\prod_{t=1}^{j}\frac{b_{t}n}{(\frac{n}{k})^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E\\
e=vv^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{((\frac{n}{k})!)^{2}}{(b_{e}n)!}.

(53)

Putting these facts together allows us to rewrite (
51
)
in terms of
p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)
and
f(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})
as defined in
(
28
) and (
29
), respectively:

\mathbf{E}(YZ_{j})\sim\frac{1}{2j}\sum_{w}\sum_{Q}k^{j}\sum_{\text{\boldmath$b$}\text{ s.t. }\eqref{eq:bcons1equi}}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{j}b_{t}\right)p(\text{\boldmath$\hat{a}$},\text{\boldmath$b$},n)e^{nf(\text{\boldmath$a$},\text{\boldmath$b$})}.

(54)

Note that we may bound

\prod_{t=1}^{j}b_{t}n\leq 1

and that, furthermore, when
\text{\boldmath$b$}=\text{\boldmath$\hat{b}$}
, none of the
b_{t}
vanish.
Therefore, Proposition
4.3
holds. Though we omit the details, we may thus use Proposition
3.4
almost identically as in Section
4.2
to get

\mathbf{E}(YZ_{j})\sim\frac{1}{2j}\sum_{w}\sum_{Q}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{-j}\left(\frac{n}{k(k-1)}\right)^{j}\mathbf{E}(Y).

Finally, as the terms no longer depend on
w
or
Q
, we apply Lemmas
6.2
and
6.3
to get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(YZ_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2j}\cdot c_{j}\cdot((k-1)^{j}+(k-1)(-1)^{j})\cdot\frac{1}{(k-1)^{j}}\mathbf{E}(Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{j}}{2j}\left(1+\frac{(-1)^{j}}{(k-1)^{j-1}}\right)\mathbf{E}(Y)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}(1+\delta_{j})\mathbf{E}(Y)

Dividing by
\mathbf{E}(Y)
completes the proof.
∎

In order to apply the small subgraph conditioning method, we need estimations for higher moments. The argument for Proposition
2.4
is just an extension of the proof of Proposition
6.1
. We count ordered tuples (lift, cycle, …, cycle, colouring) so that the lift is properly coloured by the colouring and contains cycles of the specified length. We claim the contribution from cases where the cycles intersect turn out to be negligible, adapting an argument of
[
18
]
. Suppose that the cycles form a subgraph
H
with
\nu
vertices and
\mu
edges. If the cycles are disjoint, then
\nu=\mu
. If they overlap, then as the minimum degree in
H
is at least two and some vertex has degree at least three, we have
\nu<\mu
. We then follow the same argument as in the proof above with the following changes. When choosing vertices for the cycle, we have
(1+o(1))n^{\nu}
choices. In (
52
), the coefficient is
(\frac{n}{k})^{\nu}n^{-\nu}
. Then in (
53
), the product ranges from 1 to
\mu
. Thus unlike in the proposition, where by (
54
) all of the
n
’s have cancelled, we get a
\Theta(n^{\nu-\mu})
term. In the disjoint case the
n
’s do cancel, but as there are finitely many isomorphism types of
H
, the contribution from terms with overlapping cycles are on the order
\frac{1}{n}
times the rest. Finally, the disjoint terms decompose into a product of factors corresponding to the individual cycles, giving the desired result.

7
The case where
n
is not divisible by
k

Thus far all arguments have assumed that
n
is divisible by
k
so that strongly equitable colourings exist. We now consider the case where
n=qk+r
for some integer
r\in[1,k-1]
. We start by expanding the definition of equitable colourings.

Let
n=qk+r
for
r\in[0,k-1]
. A
k
-colouring of an
n
-lift
L
of a graph
G
is strongly equitable if for every vertex
v\in G
, the fiber
f^{-1}(v)
contains
q+1
vertices of colour
1,\ldots,r
and
q
vertices of colour
r+1,\ldots,k
. Note that our previous definition of strongly equitable is exactly the case when
r=0
.

Recall from Section
5.1
that if
Y
counts the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a random lift
L
of a
d
-regular graph
G
then

\mathbf{E}Y^{2}=\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}}p(n,\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\exp\big(nf(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\big)

where
p
is some function bounded by a polynomial in
n
and

f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})=-\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{i,j}a_{v,i,j}\log a_{v,i,j}+\sum_{e\in E}\sum_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\log\left(\frac{a_{v,i,j}a_{v^{\prime},i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}{b_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}}\right)

The next proposition describes the effect of adding a small constant
r
vertices to each fiber.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose
n=qk+r
for some integer
r\in[1,k-1]
. Let
Y_{n}
be the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a random
n
-lift of a given
d
-regular graph
G
. Let
n^{\prime}=n-r
and let
Y_{n^{\prime}}
count the number of strongly equitable
k
-colourings of a random
n^{\prime}
-lift of
G
. Then

\mathbf{E}Y_{n}^{2}\sim\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}\mathbf{E}Y_{n^{\prime}}^{2}.

Proof.

First assume
r>1
. Recall the definition of
\mathbb{Y}_{n}(\gamma)
given in Proposition
3.5
. Given
\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n}(1)
, we define:

\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast}=\left(\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}(na_{v,i,j}-\chi_{i,j})\right)_{v\in V,i,j\in[k]}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}=\left(\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}(nb_{e,i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}-\chi_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})\right)_{e\in E,(i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in K}

where

\chi_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&i=j\leq r\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

and

\chi_{i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}1&i,j,i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\leq r,i^{\prime}=i+1(\text{mod }r),j^{\prime}=j+1(\text{mod }r)\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}.

Then
\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}
define the overlap matrices of a pair strongly equitable
k
-colourings of
n^{\prime}
-lifts of
G
. (Essentially, we remove one vertex of each of the first
r
colours from each fiber from each colouring and removes edges of the appropriate colour pairs to ensure the
\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}
’s still satisfy (
35
). This is possible because
\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n}(1)
and therefore
\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast}
and
\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}

Recall from (
36
) that the expected number of lifts respecting
\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}
is

\frac{1}{n!^{|E|}}\prod_{v\in V}\binom{n}{nA_{v}}\prod_{e\in E}\frac{(nA_{v})!(nA_{v^{\prime}})!}{(nB_{e})!}.

Call this quantity
\ell(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})
. Then as
\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n}(1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\ell(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})}{\ell(\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast})}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n^{r}}{n_{(r)}}\right)^{|E|-|V|}\prod_{v\in V}\prod_{i\in[r]}\frac{1}{a_{v,i,i}}\prod_{e\in E}\prod_{i\in[r]}\frac{a_{v,i,i}a_{v^{\prime},i,i}}{b_{e,i,i,i+1,i+1}}

\displaystyle<\left(\frac{n^{r}}{n_{(r)}}\right)^{|E|-|V|}\left(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)^{r|V|}\left(\frac{(\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}})^{2}}{\frac{1}{k^{2}(k-1)^{2}}-\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}}\right)^{r|E|}

\displaystyle\to\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}

By Proposition
3.5
, noting
p(n,\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})
is bounded by a polynomial, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}Y_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n}(1)}p(n,\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\exp\big(nf(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\big)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{\{\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}\}}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}p(n^{\prime},\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast})\exp\big(n^{\prime}f(\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast})\big)

Now we claim that there is
\gamma>0

\mathbb{Y}_{n^{\prime}}(\gamma)\subset\{\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}\}
and thus by Proposition

\displaystyle\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}\mathbf{E}Y_{n^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n^{\prime}}(\gamma)}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}p(n^{\prime},\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\exp(n^{\prime}f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}p(n^{\prime},\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$})\exp(n^{\prime}f(\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}))

\sum_{\{\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}\}}\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}p(n^{\prime},\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast})\exp\big(n^{\prime}f(\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast},\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast})\big)\sim\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2r}\mathbf{E}Y_{n^{\prime}}^{2}.

We will show that for all
\text{\boldmath$C$},\text{\boldmath$D$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n^{\prime}}(\gamma)

\text{\boldmath$A$},\text{\boldmath$B$}\in\mathbb{Y}_{n^{\prime}}(1)

\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=\text{\boldmath$C$}
and
\text{\boldmath$B$}^{\ast}(\text{\boldmath$B$})=\text{\boldmath$D$}
. Pick

\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\gamma\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n^{\prime}}}\leq c_{v,i,j}\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n^{\prime}}}.

(55)

Then if
\text{\boldmath$A$}^{\ast}(\text{\boldmath$A$})=\text{\boldmath$C$}

\frac{1}{n}(n^{\prime}a_{v,i,j}+\chi_{i,j})=c_{v,i,j}

(56)

for some

\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\leq a_{v,i,j}\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

(57)

Solving (

) and plugging it into (

) shows that we

\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}(nc_{v,i,j}+\chi_{i,j})\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

Combining with (

) we see that we must choose a

\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{n^{\prime}}{n}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\gamma\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n^{\prime}}}\right)

\frac{1}{n}\left(n^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\log n^{\prime}}{\sqrt{n^{\prime}}}\right)+1\right)\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}+\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}.

Using loose bounds and the fact that

Now if

, we repeat the above process except we set
n^{\prime}=n-2
and continue as though
r=2
. Then
n^{\prime}=n-2=qk+1-2=(q-1)k+(k-1)
we see

\mathbf{E}Y_{n}^{2}\sim\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{4}\mathbf{E}Y_{n^{\prime}}^{2}\sim\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2(k+1)}\mathbf{E}Y_{n-(k+1)}^{2}.

Then as
k\mid n-(k+1)
and
k\mid n-1
,
Proposition
2.3
gives

\mathbf{E}Y_{n-(k+1)}^{2}=\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{-2k}\mathbf{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}

and so

\mathbf{E}Y_{n}^{2}\sim\left(k^{|V|}\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^{|E|}\right)^{2}\mathbf{E}Y_{n-1}^{2}.

as required.
∎

Using the same process of removing vertices and edges of specified colour, one can prove analogues of Proposition

, and we omit them. Then the same proof of Theorem
1.2
presented in Section
2
holds for

k
