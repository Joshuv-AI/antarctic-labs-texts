Regularisation of Lie algebroids and applications
Álvaro del Pino and Aldo Witte

Abstract
. We describe a procedure, called regularisation, that allows us to study geometric structures on Lie algebroids via foliated geometric structures on a manifold of higher dimension. This procedure applies to various classes of Lie algebroids; namely, those whose singularities are of b k b^{k} , complex-log, or elliptic type, possibly with self-crossings. One of our main applications is a proof of the Weinstein conjecture for overtwisted b k b^{k} -contact structures. This was proven in [ MO21 ] using a certain technical hypothesis. Our approach avoids this assumption by reducing the proof to the foliated setting. As a by-product, we also prove the Weinstein conjecture for other Lie algebroids. Along the way we also introduce tangent distributions, i.e. subbundles of Lie algebroids, as interesting objects of study and present a number of constructions for them.



1.
Introduction

Lie algebroids are the infinitesimal counterparts to Lie groupoids. They appeared first
[
Pra67
]
in the context of foliation theory and in the study of symmetry. Later on, they entered the world of geometric analysis in order to model various flavours of singularities. A classic example in this direction is the
b
-tangent bundle
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
of Melrose
[
Mel93
]
. It consists of those vector fields that are tangent to a given embedded hypersurface
Z
. We can think of
Z
as a hypersurface at infinity, which is why
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
plays a role in the study of the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer index theorem.

Some of the study of Lie algebroids has focused on particularly well-behaved families thereof. This is certainly the case for foliations (which are the Lie algebroids in which the anchor is a monomorphism) and
b
-tangent bundles (in which the anchor is an isomorphism almost everywhere, except along the hypersurface
Z
, where it vanishes transversely). Indeed, it is convenient to focus on Lie algebroids whose anchor is controlled in some way. In this article, we focus on Lie algebroids whose singularities are of
b^{k}

[
Sco13
]
, elliptic
[
CG18
]
, or self-crossing
[
GLPR17
,
CKW20
]
type.

Our main goal in this article is to relate the aforementioned Lie algebroids to the foliated setting. The idea behind this is that foliations, being non-singular, are somehow easier to handle.

1.1
.

Let
M
be a manifold endowed with a
b^{k}
-tangent bundle
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
with underlying coorientable hypersurface
Z
. Then, there exists a codimension-
1
foliation
{\mathcal{F}}
in
M\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, such that the projection to
M
induces a Lie algebroid submersion
{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
.

The pair
(M\times\mathbb{S}^{1},{\mathcal{F}})
is called the
regularisation
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
. In Section
4
we state and prove various results with this flavour. They apply to each of the families of Lie algebroids we mentioned above.

1.1.
Lifting geometric structures

The construction presented in Theorem
1.1
was discovered already, in the
b
-setting, by Osorno-Torres
[
Tor15
]
. Using the language of
b
-symplectic structures, he proved:

1.2
.

Let
(M,Z)
be a manifold together with an embedded hypersurface, and let
\omega\in\Omega^{2}({\mathcal{A}}_{Z})
be a
b
-symplectic form. Then,
M\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
admits a codimension-one symplectic foliation.

A similar statement, in the context of generalised complex geometry, later appeared in
[
CG18
]
.

Compared to these statements already available in the literature, Theorem
1.1
operates in the setting of Lie algebroids and does not require any additional geometry (i.e. symplectic or generalised complex). Nonetheless, once the regularisation is in place, we can indeed apply it to the study of geometric structures on Lie algebroids. Do note that there is an extensive body of work, within Poisson Geometry, studying symplectic structures on Lie algebroids
[
NT99
,
GMP14
,
Lan21
]
. The contact case has also attracted considerable attention
[
MO20
,
MO21
,
CO22
]
recently.

We have the following lifting statement:

1.3
.

Let
M
be a manifold endowed with a
b^{k}
-tangent bundle
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
with underlying coorientable hypersurface
Z
. Let
(M\times\mathbb{S}^{1},{\mathcal{F}})
be its regularisation. Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a
b^{k}
-contact or symplectic structure. Then, there is a foliated contact or symplectic structure
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
, lifting
{\mathcal{G}}
.

In fact, there is nothing special about the contact case. Given any tangent distribution (i.e. vector subbundle) on a Lie algebroid, it can be similarly lifted to the regularisation (Proposition
4.20
). Furthermore, lifting commutes with taking the curvature of the distribution, so all the relevant properties about it are preserved upon lifting.

1.2.
Singular Weinstein conjecture

We now focus on Lie algebroid contact structures. The Weinstein conjecture states that every Reeb vector field associated to a contact form on a compact manifold admits a periodic orbit. Hofer
[
Hof93
]
proved the conjecture for contact three-manifolds
(M^{3},\alpha)
under the additional assumption that
\alpha
is overtwisted or
\pi_{2}(M)\neq\emptyset
. Later, Taubes
[
Tau07
]
proved the conjecture for general three-dimensional manifolds by localizing the Seiberg-Witten equations along orbits of the Reeb vector field. This circle of ideas still applies in the Lie algebroid setting. This was pioneered by Miranda and Oms
[
MO20
,
MO21
]
, who proved the Weinstein conjecture (under a technical assumption called
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-invariance
) in the framework of
b^{k}
-geometry.

The regularisation allows us to study singular contact structures via foliated contact structures. Using the foliated Weinstein conjecture
[
dPP17
]
, we deduce:

Theorem
6.7
.

Let
(M^{2n+1},Z,\alpha)
be a
b^{k}
-contact manifold, and assume that there exists an overtwisted disk in
M\setminus Z
. Then either:

•

There is a periodic Reeb orbit in
M\setminus Z
.

•

There is a non-constant Reeb orbit in
Z
.

Do note that, in the
b^{k}
-setting, there may be Reeb orbits in
Z
that are constant. Upon regularising, these correspond to Reeb orbits in
Z\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
tangent to the second factor. The theorem states that the orbits we obtain are not of this type.

Assuming
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-invariance,
[
MO21
]
found Reeb orbits in
M^{3}\setminus Z
. This is a corollary of Theorem
6.7
:

Theorem
6.9
.

Let
(M^{2n+1},Z,\alpha)
be a closed overtwisted
b^{k}
-contact manifold. Assume that there is a neighbourhood of
Z
in which
\alpha
is
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-invariant. Then, there are contractible Reeb orbits in
M\setminus Z
.

We will also prove similar Weinstein-style statements for other types of singular contact structures.

1.3.
Organisation of the paper

Some of the elementary theory of Lie algebroids is recalled in Section
2
. Along the way we introduce tangent distributions, which we use to discuss Ehresmann connections on Lie algebroids. We then describe the classes of Lie algebroids that we will work with (Section
3
). The regularisation procedure for Lie algebroids is discussed in Section
4
. We present several interpretations of it, including its relation to the canonical representation. All throughout we work with the classes of Lie algebroids introduced in Section
3
. The section closes with Subsection
4.5
, where we explain the correspondence between distributions in a Lie algebroid and in its regularisation.

We then focus on applications to Lie algebroid distributions. We first provide an overview of the contact setting (Section
5
), which will then allow us to study the Weinstein conjecture in Section
6
. In particular, we will prove Theorems
6.7
and
6.9
, as well as similar results for other singular contact structures.

Section
7
is a brief introduction to bracket-generating distributions in Lie algebroids. We present various constructions and examples.

The article also includes an Appendix
A
where we explain how the regularisation procedure fits within the Poisson and Jacobi frameworks.

2.
Lie algebroids

In this section we recall some of the basic theory of Lie algebroids. This will allow us to introduce distributions (i.e. subbundles) in the Lie algebroid setting (Subsection
2.2
). As a particular example, we discuss Lie algebroid connections in the sense of Ehresmann (Subsection
2.3
).

2.1.
Lie algebroids

In this article we are interested in geometric structures with mild singularities. There are many in-equivalent geometric setups that allow for singularities, but the one that we concern ourselves with reads as follows:

2.1
.

A
Lie algebroid
on a manifold
M
is a triple consisting of:

•

a vector bundle
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
,

•

a bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
on its space of sections
\Gamma({\mathcal{A}})
,

•

and a vector bundle morphism
\rho:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow TM
, called the
anchor
,

satisfying:

[v_{1},fv_{2}]=f[v_{1},v_{2}]+\mathcal{L}_{\rho(v_{1})}(f)v_{2},

for all
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in\Gamma({\mathcal{A}})
.

2.0
.

It is customary to just write
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
for the Lie algebroid and leave the rest of the data implicit.

2.1.1.
Morphisms

Given Lie algebroids
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
and
{\mathcal{B}}\to N
, a
Lie algebroid morphism
is a bundle morphism

\Phi:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{B}}

lifting a map
\phi:M\to N
and commuting with anchors and brackets.

2.2
.

The tangent bundle
TM
, together with the usual Lie bracket of vector fields, and the identity as anchor, is a Lie algebroid. The unique Lie algebroid morphism
\Phi:TM\to TN
between tangent bundles lifting a map
\phi:M\to N
is the differential
d\phi
.

Similarly, the tangent space of a foliation
{\mathcal{F}}\subset TM
defines a Lie algebroid with the inclusion as anchor. A morphism of Lie algebroids between two foliated manifolds is necessarily the leafwise differential of a foliated map.

2.1.2.
Differential forms

Many geometric constructions associated to the tangent bundle can easily be extended to general Lie algebroids. For instance, we can define
{\mathcal{A}}
-differential forms using duality:

\Omega^{\bullet}({\mathcal{A}}):=\Gamma(\wedge^{\bullet}{\mathcal{A}}^{*})

and the corresponding differential

d_{{\mathcal{A}}}:\Omega^{\bullet}({\mathcal{A}})\rightarrow\Omega^{\bullet+1}({\mathcal{A}}).

using the Koszul formula. Some of the geometric structures to be considered later on will be written in terms of such forms.

2.2.
Distributions

The main objects of interest in this paper are distributions on Lie algebroids:

2.3
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold and
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
a Lie algebroid. An
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution
of rank
k
is a
k
-dimensional subbundle
\xi\subset\mathcal{A}
.

We henceforth write
\Gamma(\xi)\subset\Gamma({\mathcal{A}})
for the sheaf of sections of
\xi
.

2.2.1.
Lie flag

In order to measure the non-involutivity of
\xi
we associate to it a sequence of
C^{\infty}(M)
-modules, called the
Lie flag
:

\Gamma(\xi)^{(1)}:=\Gamma(\xi)\subset\Gamma(\xi)^{(2)}\subset\cdots\subset\Gamma(\xi)^{(i)}\subset\cdots

\Gamma(\xi)^{(i+1)}:=\langle[v,w]\mid v\in\Gamma(\xi)^{(1)},w\in\Gamma(\xi)^{(i)}\rangle_{C^{\infty}},

where the bracket is the bracket on sections of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

We say that
\xi
is
bracket-generating
if
\Gamma(\xi)^{(i_{0})}=\Gamma({\mathcal{A}})
for some
i_{0}
. The smallest such
i_{0}
is called the
step
of
\xi
. At the opposite end of the spectrum, we say that
\xi
is
involutive
if
\Gamma(\xi)^{i}=\Gamma(\xi)
for every
i
. This is equivalent to the fact that the bracket preserves
\xi
, meaning that
\xi
is a Lie subalgebroid.

2.4
.

If
\rho
is identically zero,
{\mathcal{A}}
is a bundle of Lie algebras. Then, a bracket-generating
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution
\xi
is a subbundle of
{\mathcal{A}}
that, fibrewise, is a generating set of the corresponding algebra. Similarly,
\xi
is involutive if it restricts fibrewise to a Lie subalgebra.

2.2.2.
Curvature

For simplicity, we will henceforth assume that
\xi
is
regular
, i.e. that there are
{\mathcal{A}}
-distributions
\xi_{i}
such that
\Gamma(\xi_{i})=\Gamma(\xi)^{(i)}
. Much like in the classic study of distributions, using the Leibniz rule for the bracket we deduce that:

2.5
.

For
\xi
regular and each pair of positive integers
i,j
, the Lie bracket defines a well-defined bundle morphism, called the
curvature
:

\Omega_{i,j}(\xi):\xi_{i}\times\xi_{j}\longrightarrow\xi_{i+j}/\xi_{i+j-1}

which factors through:

\xi_{i}/\xi_{i-1}\times\xi_{j}/\xi_{j-1}\longrightarrow\xi_{i+j}/\xi_{i+j-1}.

2.3.
Lie algebroid connections

Much like in the classic case, Lie algebroid Ehresmann connections are concrete examples of Lie algebroid distributions. Connections will play an important role in the constructions of Section
4
. Namely, they will allow us to “desingularise” certain Lie algebroids with mild singularities to Lie algebroids of foliation type.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
and
{\mathcal{B}}\rightarrow N
be Lie algebroids. Suppose that
\Phi:{\mathcal{B}}\to{\mathcal{A}}
is a Lie algebroid morphism that is a fibrewise epimorphism and lifts a surjective submersion
\phi:N\to M
. We can then write
{\operatorname{Vert}}(\phi):=\ker(d\phi)
and similarly
{\operatorname{Vert}}(\Phi):=\ker(\Phi)
. Due to the commutativity of the square
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{B}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TN}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TM,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d\phi}

it follows that
{\operatorname{Vert}}(\Phi)
is mapped to
{\operatorname{Vert}}(\phi)
by the anchor of
{\mathcal{B}}
.

2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
,
{\mathcal{B}}\to N
, and
\Phi
as above. An
Ehresmann connection
for
\Phi
is a subbundle
H\subset{\mathcal{B}}
transverse to
{\operatorname{Vert}}(\Phi)
and of complementary rank. An Ehresmann connection
H
is
flat
if its curvatures (as in Proposition
2.5
) vanish.

This means that the subsheaf of sections
\Gamma(H)\subset\Gamma({\mathcal{B}})
is involutive,
H\to N
is a Lie algebroid, and
\Phi|_{H}:H\to{\mathcal{A}}
is Lie algebroid morphism that, by construction, is a fibrewise isomorphism.

2.3.1.
Pullback Lie algebroids

Let us discuss a concrete case of Definition
2.6
. Our starting data is a Lie algebroid
\rho:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow TM
and a map
\phi:N\rightarrow M
which is transverse to
\rho
. Transversality allows us to consider the pullback bundle

\phi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}:=\{(v,w)\in{\mathcal{A}}\times TN\,\mid\,\rho(v)=d\phi(w)\},

which fits into the commutative square
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TN}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TM.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{{\mathcal{A}}}\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{!}\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d\phi}

Requiring that
d_{{\mathcal{A}}}\phi
is a Lie algebroid morphism uniquely defines a Lie algebroid structure on
\phi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}\to N
with anchor
\phi^{!}\rho:\phi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}\to TN
. By construction,
d_{{\mathcal{A}}}\phi
is a fibrewise epimorphism and fits into our previous discussion.

2.3.2.
The linear case

In the setting of vector bundles one can particularise this discussion to linear connections:

2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid, and
\pi:E\rightarrow M
be a vector bundle. A (linear)
{\mathcal{A}}
-
connection
on
E
is a map

\displaystyle\nabla:\Gamma({\mathcal{A}})\times\Gamma(E)

\displaystyle\quad\longrightarrow\quad\Gamma(E)

\displaystyle(v,w)

\displaystyle\quad\mapsto\quad\nabla_{v}(w),

which is
C^{\infty}(M)
-linear in the first entry, and satisfies

\nabla_{v}(fw)=f\nabla_{v}(w)+\mathcal{L}_{\rho(v)}(w),

for all
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

As in the classic case, a linear connection (as in Definition
2.7
) yields an Ehresmann connection (as in Definition
2.6
). This is easiest described using an associated horizontal lift operator

\operatorname{hor}^{\nabla}:\Gamma(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\Gamma(\pi^{!}\mathcal{A}),

for which the Ehresmann connection is the image. Write
\operatorname{hor}^{\nabla}(\alpha)=(\alpha,X)\in\Gamma({\mathcal{A}})\times\Gamma(TE)
. As
X
is characterised by its action on linear functions
s\in\Gamma(E^{*})
, one defines
X(s)=\nabla^{*}_{\rho(\alpha)}(s)
.

Furthermore, linear
{\mathcal{A}}
-connections can be described by their connection matrices. Namely, given a cover
\{U_{\alpha}\,\mid\,\alpha\in I\}
of
M
by open balls on which
E
trivialises, the connection is given by the associated matrix-valued one-forms

A_{\alpha}\in\Omega^{1}(\left.{{\mathcal{A}}}\right|_{U_{\alpha}};\text{End}(\mathbb{R}^{r})),

where
r
is the rank of
E
. In these local trivialisations the horizontal lift is given by

\operatorname{hor}^{\nabla}(X)=X-a(A_{\alpha}(X)),

where
a:\text{End}(E)\rightarrow\mathfrak{X}^{1}(E)
is the map given by
a(B)(s)=B^{*}(s)
.

2.3.3.
Representations

The (linear) curvature of the
{\mathcal{A}}
-connection is an element of
\Omega^{2}({\mathcal{A}};\text{End}(E))
, whose vanishing is equivalent to the vanishing of the curvature in the Ehresmann sense. In this case, the
{\mathcal{A}}
-connection is flat and we say that it is a
{\mathcal{A}}
-
representation
.

The main Lie algebroid representation we will use is the following:

2.8
[
ELW96
]
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid. The
canonical representation
of
{\mathcal{A}}
on

Q:=\det(A^{*})\otimes\det(TM)

is defined by

\nabla_{v}(\alpha\otimes\omega)={\mathcal{L}}_{v}(\alpha)\otimes\omega+\alpha\otimes{\mathcal{L}}_{\rho(v)}(\omega).\hfill

3.
Some relevant families of Lie algebroids

In this section we discuss some concrete families of Lie algebroids, putting particular emphasis on Lie algebroids of divisor type. The material in this section is standard and we refer the reader to
[
Kla18
]
for a more detailed introduction.

3.1.
b
-Geometry

All the Lie algebroids to be considered in this paper are either of foliation type or have an anchor that is an isomorphism everywhere except along a submanifold (or a union of submanifolds with controlled intersections).

The simplest type of singularity one may encounter occurs along an embedded hypersurface. Fix a manifold
M
and a hypersurface
Z
; we will say that the pair
(M,Z)
is a
b-pair
. It can be checked that the sheaf of vector fields tangent to
Z
is locally free. Consequently, the Serre-Swann theorem ensures the existence of a vector bundle
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}\rightarrow M
whose sections are precisely the vector fields tangent to
Z
.

Taking local coordinates
(z,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
in which
Z=\{z=0\}
, we have that vector fields tangent to
Z
are given as the span

\displaystyle\langle z\partial_{z},\partial_{x_{2}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\rangle,

and the vector fields shown are thus a frame for
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
.

3.1
.

Let
(M,Z)
be a b-pair. The vector fields tangent to
Z
are the sections of a Lie algebroid,
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
, called the
b-tangent bundle
.

These objects show up naturally in various areas of mathematics. In the context of PDE’s, their study is called
b
-geometry
[
Mel93
]
. Here “
b
” stands for “boundary”, and indeed
Z
is thought of as the ideal boundary of
M\setminus Z
at infinity. In the context of algebraic geometry, the study of these objects is called log(arithmic) geometry, which deals with divisors in algebraic varieties.

3.2.
b^{k}
-Geometry

Let
M
be a manifold,
\iota:Z\hookrightarrow M
an embedded hypersurface, and
k>1
. One may wish to consider
k
-order tangencies of vector fields with the hypersurface
Z
. As explained in
[
Sco13
]
, this notion is not well-defined without the fixing of auxiliary data.

Consider the sheaf of
k
-jets at
Z
, defined as
J_{Z}^{k}=\iota^{-1}(C^{\infty}_{M}/I_{Z}^{k+1})
. Given a function
f
, defined on a neighbourhood of
Z
, we denote by
j^{k}_{Z}f\in J_{Z}^{k}
its jet along
Z
.

3.2
[
Sco13
]
.

Fix a jet
j\in J_{Z}^{k-1}
with vanishing zero-jet and non-vanishing first-jet. I.e. it is representable by a function cutting
Z
transversely as its zero locus.

The
b^{k}
-tangent bundle
associated to
M
,
Z
and
j
is the Lie algebroid
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}\rightarrow M
whose sections are

\mathfrak{X}(I_{Z}^{k},j):=\{X\in\mathfrak{X}^{1}(M):\mathcal{L}_{X}(f)\in I_{Z}^{k}\text{ for all }f\in j\}.\hfill

Henceforth, whenever we talk about
b^{k}
-tangent bundles, we will consider jets
j
that satisfy the (non-)vanishing hypotheses of the definition. Such a jet
j
defines a coorientation of
Z
. Indeed, we can represent
j
locally by a function
f
cutting
Z
transversely. This implies that
Z
is locally two-sided and the side in which
f
is positive may be regarded as the positive one. However, do note that this coorientation is not canonically given by the algebroid:

3.3
.

Let
j_{0}
and
j_{1}
be jets in
J_{Z}^{k-1}
. Then, the associated Lie algebroids agree if and only if
j_{0}=\lambda j_{1}
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
.

There are Lie algebroids that are locally modelled on Definition
3.2
but that cannot be described globally in terms of a jet
j
, due to the presence of non-trivial holonomy. We refer the reader to the companion paper
[
dPA
]
.

In order to provide a unified treatment, we will still speak of
b^{k}
-Geometry with
k=1
. In that case, the data of
j
is vacuous.

3.3.
Ideals and divisors

A useful tool in
b
-geometry (as well as in some of the other singular geometries to be introduced in the upcoming subsections) is the notion of a divisor.

3.4
[
VdLD16
]
.

Let
I\subset C^{\infty}(M;\mathbb{R})
be a locally principal ideal, locally generated by functions with nowhere dense zero-set. Then there exists a line bundle
W\rightarrow M
and a section
s\in\Gamma(W)
such that

I_{s}:=s(\Gamma(W^{*}))\subset C^{\infty}(M)

coincides with
I
. This line bundle

is unique up to isomorphism, and the section
s
is unique up to scaling.

Proof.

Let
\{U_{\alpha}:\alpha\in I\}
be an open cover of
M
. For each
\alpha\in I
let
s_{\alpha}
be a local generator of
I
on
U_{\alpha}
. On overlaps
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}
there are functions
g^{\alpha}_{\beta}\in C^{\infty}(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta})
such that
s_{\alpha}=g^{\alpha}_{\beta}s_{\beta}
. These functions
g^{\alpha}_{\beta}
are then the transition functions of the claimed vector bundle
W
. The section
s
has
s_{\alpha}
as its local expression on
U_{\alpha}
.
∎

The pair
(W,s)
, as introduced in the Lemma

, is said to be the
b-divisor
associated to
(M,Z)
. We now collect a short list of useful remarks/constructions:

3.0
.

Due to the transverse vanishing of

, it follows that the differential of
s
along the normal bundle of
Z

d^{\nu}s:\nu(Z)\rightarrow\left.{W}\right|_{Z}

is an isomorphism.

3.0
.

Let
(M,Z)
be a b-pair. A defining function exists in a neighbourhood of
Z
if and only if
Z
is co-orientable. A global defining function for
Z
if and only if the associated line bundle

is trivial.

3.0
.

As shown in
[

]
, the b-divisor can be recovered from the Lie algebroid

using the canonical representation (Definition

). Namely:
W\simeq\det({\mathcal{A}}^{*}_{Z})\otimes\det(TM)
, and under this isomorphism

will be sent to
\det(\rho_{{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}})
. This shows that associated to any b-pair
(M,Z)
there is a completely canonical b-divisor:

(\det({\mathcal{A}}^{*}_{Z})\otimes\det(TM),\det(\rho_{{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}})).\hfill

3.5
.

Let
(M,Z)
be a b-pair, and let
\alpha\in\Omega^{k}({\mathcal{A}}_{Z})
. Its
residue
is the form

\Res(\alpha)\in\Omega^{k-1}(Z)

4.
Regularisation

We now present the main idea of this article. Suppose that
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
is one of the Lie algebroids introduced in Section
3
. We can then produce a Lie algebroid
{\mathcal{F}}\to W
of foliation type that submerses onto
{\mathcal{A}}
. We think of this as a desingularisation process. This will allow us (Subsection
4.5
) to lift geometric structures on
{\mathcal{A}}
to geometric structures on
{\mathcal{F}}
; the latter are easier to work with. Concrete applications to singular contact structures are given in Section
6
.

4.1.
The trivial regularisation in
b^{k}
-Geometry

We begin with the regularisation of
b^{k}
-tangent bundles. We are interested in the following concept:

4.1
.

Let

be a manifold,
Z\subset M
a hypersurface, and
j\in J_{Z}^{k-1}
a jet, and let
{\mathcal{A}}^{k}_{Z}
denote the associated
b^{k}
-tangent bundle. Let

be either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We consider the manifold

endowed with the
I

An
I
-invariant codimension-

in

if the following properties are satisfied:

(1)

There is a Lie algebroid submersion
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{F}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(M\times I)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TM.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d\pi}

(2)

The connected components of
Z\times I
are leaves.

(3)

All other leaves are transverse to the
I

M\setminus Z
.

The components of

are called the

of
{\mathcal{F}}
.

Do note that
\Psi
is necessarily a fibrewise isomorphism between

and
{\mathcal{A}}^{k}_{Z}
, for dimension reasons. It follows that a regularisation is an Ehresmann connection that additionally satisfies certain triviality properties in terms of its holonomy.

We distinguish between the cases

and

by referring to the former as the

and to the latter as the

. The following is immediate:

4.2
.

{\mathcal{A}}^{k}_{Z}\rightarrow M
admits a trivial regularisation if and only if it admits a compact regularisation.

4.1.1.
Regularisations from defining functions

Our goal now is to prove that regularisations exist. The key feature to be exploited is the local triviality provided by the jet
j\in J_{Z}^{k-1}
. Do note that, even if
k=1
, we work (for now) under the assumption that
Z
is coorientable. In the next lemma we also work under assumptions of global triviality:

4.3
.

Let
M
be a manifold. Let
Z\subset M
be a hypersurface. Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a global defining function for
Z
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{k}_{Z}
be the
b^{k}
-tangent bundle given by
j=j^{k-1}_{Z}f
. We write
s
for the
\mathbb{R}
-coordinate in
M\times\mathbb{R}
.

The foliation
{\mathcal{F}}_{f}:=\ker(\theta_{f})
given by
\theta_{f}:=df+f^{k}ds
is a trivial regularisation.

Proof.

The
\mathbb{R}
-invariance of
{\mathcal{F}}_{f}
is immediate from the definition of
\theta_{f}
. Involutivity follows from the computation
\theta_{f}\wedge d\theta_{f}=0
. For the first property, consider a point
p\in Z\times\mathbb{R}
. Then we have that
({\mathcal{F}}_{f})_{p}=T_{p}(Z\times\mathbb{R})
, showing that the connected components of
Z\times\mathbb{R}
are leaves.

For the second property we observe that, in the complement of
Z\times\mathbb{R}
, the foliation is defined by the one-form
d((k-1)^{-1}f^{-k+1})-ds
and hence it leaves are graphical.

We now address the last property. Away from
Z
,
\Psi
is necessarily given by
d\pi
. By density it follows that there is at most one extension of
\Psi
to
Z\times\mathbb{R}
. We prove that such an extension exists by working locally. On a tubular neighbourhood
U
of a point
p\in Z
we can find local coordinates
(z,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
in which
f=z
. It follows that
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
is spanned locally by

\langle z^{k}\partial_{z},\partial_{x_{2}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\rangle.

Similarly, we have that

{\mathcal{F}}_{f}=\langle z^{k}\partial_{z}-\partial_{s},\partial_{x_{2}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\rangle

in
U\times\mathbb{R}
. We then set
\Psi
to be given by
\Psi(z^{k}\partial_{z}-\partial_{s}):=z^{k}\partial_{z}
and by
d\pi
when applied to the rest of the frame.
∎

4.1.2.
Now without a global function

More generally, we consider the setting in which

is coorientable but not necessarily given by a global function:

.

Let

be a manifold. Let

be a coorientable hypersurface. Fix a jet

with associated

-tangent bundle

Proof.

On a tubular neighbourhood

for

; we require

(note that, for

be a function which is 1 near

and 0 near the boundary of

and only depends on

. Let

denote the

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}:=\begin{cases}\ker(\chi(z)dz+z^{k}ds)\quad\text{on }{\mathcal{U}}\\
\ker(ds)\quad\text{ elsewhere}.\end{cases}

Involutivity and

∎

4.1.3.
Coorientations

We remark:

4.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{k}_{Z}\rightarrow M
be the
b^{k}
-tangent bundle given by the hypersurface
Z\subset M
and the jet
j\in J_{Z}^{k-1}
.

•

If
k
is even, a regularisation
{\mathcal{F}}
defines a coorientation
\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
of
Z
.

•

If
k
is odd, a regularisation
{\mathcal{F}}
provides an orientation of the vertical bundle of

.

Proof.

We work on each component
Z_{0}\subset Z
separately. Consider a point
p\in Z_{0}
and a neighbourhood
U\ni p
. On
U
, we can consider a defining function
z
for
Z_{0}
, compatible with
j
. Due to coorientability,
Z_{0}
is two-sided and
z
marks which of the two sides is the positive one. This yields a coorientation
\tau_{j}
; this is not yet the coorientation claimed in the statement.

Now,
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
is locally spanned by

\langle z^{k}\partial_{z},\partial_{x_{2}},\ldots,\partial_{x_{n}}\rangle.

Given the Lie algebroid submersion
\Psi:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}^{k}
, we can consider the vector field
X=\Psi^{-1}(z^{k}\partial_{z})
. Since it is non-vanishing, it must have non-zero vertical component.

If
k
is even, and the vertical component of

is negative, we set
\tau_{\mathcal{F}}
around

to be given by
\tau_{j}
. Otherwise we set
\tau_{\mathcal{F}}=-\tau_{j}
. Equivalently,
Z_{0}
is two-sided, and we set the positive side to be the one along which the leaves of
{\mathcal{F}}
spiral upwards. We claim that this definition does not depend on choices. The first choice is the local defining function

and compatible with

itself. Recall that the only jets defining the same Lie algebroid as

are those of the form

. Observe that the function

is compatible with

. If we carry out the above construction for

. If
\lambda
is positive, the vertical components of

and

have the same sign and

holds. If
\lambda
is negative, the vertical components of

and

have opposite signs and we also have

.

If

is odd, we orient the vertical using

along

is attracting as we move positively along the vertical. To prove that this is well-defined we reason as above; when proving independence of

has the same sign as

∎

If

4.1.4.
Lack of uniqueness

We will say that two trivial regularisations are
equivalent
if there is an
\mathbb{R}
-equivariant diffeomorphism, fibered over
M
, taking one to the other. They are
isotopic
if the diffeomorphism may be assumed to be isotopic to the identity (through
\mathbb{R}
-equivariant diffeomorphisms fibered over
M
). It follows from Corollary
4.6
that:

4.7
.

Let
M
be a manifold. Let
Z
be a coorientable hypersurface. Fix a jet
j\in J_{Z}^{k-1}
and denote the corresponding
b^{k}
-tangent bundle by
{\mathcal{A}}
. Then,
{\mathcal{A}}
admits two non-isotopic regularisations.

Proof.

Use Corollary
4.6
to construct a regularisation whose induced (co)orientations are opposite from the standard ones. Then the claim follows due to the invariance of (co)orientations under isotopy.
∎

Similarly:

4.8
.

Let
M
be a manifold. Let
Z
be a coorientable hypersurface. Fix a jet
j\in J_{Z}^{k-1}
and denote the corresponding
b^{k}
-tangent bundle by
{\mathcal{A}}
. Suppose
k
is odd and
Z
has more than one connected component. Then,
{\mathcal{A}}
admits two non-equivalent regularisations.

Proof.

Using Corollary
4.6
we construct a regularisation whose induced vertical orientation in each component of

is positive. We can also construct a regularisation whose induced vertical orientation is positive in all components but one. These two regularisations cannot be equivalent.
∎

For
k
even, one can reason similarly and prove:

4.9
.

Let
M
be a manifold. Let
Z
be a coorientable hypersurface. Fix a jet

and denote the corresponding

-tangent bundle by
{\mathcal{A}}
. Suppose

(Co)orientations are the only invariant that we have used to distinguish regularisations. Our expectation is that there should be a non-trivial moduli, as suggested by the freedom we have in choosing the cut-off function in the proof of Lemma

4.2.1.
Uniqueness

We now explain how the intrinsic regularisation is actually intrinsic. In the statement of Proposition
4.10
we are fixing some auxiliary data; namely, the section

. Given some other section

is a non-vanishing function. Multiplication by

is then an
\mathbb{R}^{*}
-equivariant isomorphism of

that takes

to

.

We then say that two foliations in
W^{*}\setminus\mathbb{R}
are

if there is an
\mathbb{R}^{*}
-equivariant diffeomorphism taking one to the other. The previous discussion implies that:

4.11

Let

be sections whose transverse vanishing locus is
Z
. Then, the associated regularisations are equivalent.

Let us particularise to the case in which
W
is the trivial bundle
M\times\mathbb{R}
. We see

as its own dual using the standard scalar product in
\mathbb{R}
. We can then consider the positive cone
M\times\mathbb{R}^{>0}
and take exponential coordinates

\Psi:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow M\times\mathbb{R}^{>0}

fibrewise. The
\mathbb{R}
-action in the source by translations corresponds then to the
\mathbb{R}^{>0}
-action in the target by multiplication. Then:

4.12

Let
(M,Z)
be a b-pair with

given as the zero set of a global defining function

. Then, the exponential map
\Psi
maps the trivial regularisation to the intrinsic regularisation (both associated to

Proof.

For notational clarity, let us write
W^{*}=M\times\mathbb{R}
. We see

as a section of
W
, which we have identified with
W^{*}
. We use coordinates
(x,t)
in
W^{*}
. Then, the intrinsic regularisation in

, is given by the level sets of

.

We then consider
\Psi:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow W^{*}
given by
(x,s)\mapsto(x,e^{s})
. It follows that the pullback of the intrinsic regularisation is given by the level sets of

. Equivalently, it is the foliation given as the kernel of the

∎

In particular, the regularisations produced by Lemma

are unique up to

-equivariant equivalence.

Lie algebroid contact structures

Before we get into general distributions on Lie algebroids, let us discuss the concrete case of contact structures. There is an already existing body of literature dealing with them in the algebroid setting, paticularly within the frameworks of Foliation Theory

5.1.
The definition

5.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
be a Lie algebroid of rank
2r+1
. A corank-
1

is said to be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact structure
if its first curvature

\Omega_{1,1}(\xi):\xi\times\xi\longrightarrow{\mathcal{A}}/\xi

is a non-degenerate
2
-form.

Non-degeneracy implies, in particular, that
\xi
is bracket-generating of step
2

As per usual, the contact condition can be rephrased in terms of forms:

5.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
be a Lie algebroid of rank
2r+1
. An
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact form

\alpha\in\Omega^{1}({\mathcal{A}})
is a Lie algebroid 1-form such that

\alpha\wedge(d\alpha)^{r}\in\Omega^{2r+1}({\mathcal{A}})

is a Lie algebroid volume form.

Given an
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact form
\alpha
, we have that
\xi=\ker(\alpha)\subset{\mathcal{A}}
defines an
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact structure. This follows from the formula

d\alpha=-\alpha\circ\Omega_{1,1}(\xi).

Moreover, any
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact structure is locally given as the kernel of an
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact form, but this need not be the case globally.

5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{H}_{r}
be the Heisenberg Lie algebra, which has generators
p_{1},\ldots,p_{r},q_{1},\ldots,q_{r},z
satisfying
[p_{i},q_{j}]=\delta_{ij}z
and all other brackets zero. Then
\xi=\text{span}(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r},q_{1},\ldots,q_{r})
is a contact structure.

5.2.
Recap: Algebroid symplectic forms

Contact structures can be understood as homogeneous versions of symplectic structures, via the symplectisation functor (see Subsection
5.3
below). See
[
TYV20
]
for a more general incarnation of this phenomenon. We now recall some of the basic theory of symplectic forms in Lie algebroids.

5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
be a Lie algebroid of rank
2r
. An
{\mathcal{A}}
-symplectic form

\omega\in\Omega^{2}({\mathcal{A}})
is a closed Lie algebroid 2-form for which
\omega^{r}\in\Omega^{2r}({\mathcal{A}})
is a Lie algebroid volume form.

5.2.1.
The Liouville form

Cotangent bundles are the prototypical examples of symplectic manifolds. It turns out that, similarly, the dual of any Lie algebroid admits a tautological
1
-form whose differential is symplectic. Let us go through the construction; see also
[
dLMM04
,
Smi21
]

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid and write

for its dual. We can then consider the pullback algebroid
\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
. Do note that, if
{\mathcal{A}}
is one of the algebroids presented in Section

is of the same type.

5.5
.

The

is defined as

\lambda_{\operatorname{can}}(\alpha)(v)=\alpha(d_{{\mathcal{A}}}\pi(v)),

and

Let

the associated fibrewise coordinates on

Using this local expression is immediate to check that:

[
dLMM04
]
.

The two-form

5.3.
The symplectisation

We now discuss the symplectisation functor. It has appeared already in the literature in the foliated
[
dPP17
]
and
b
-settings
[
MO20
]
. We will need the following notation: if
E_{i}\rightarrow M_{i}
are vector bundles, and
\pi_{i}:M_{1}\times M_{2}\rightarrow M_{i}
denote the projections, then
E_{1}\boxtimes E_{2}
is the vector bundle
\pi_{1}^{*}E_{1}\oplus\pi_{2}^{*}E_{2}
.

We define:

5.9
.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}({\mathcal{A}})
be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact form on
M
. Consider the Lie algebroid
{\mathcal{A}}\boxtimes T\mathbb{R}\rightarrow M\times\mathbb{R}
and write

for the coordinate in the
\mathbb{R}

The pair
({\mathcal{A}}\boxtimes T\mathbb{R}\rightarrow M\times\mathbb{R},d(e^{t}\alpha))
is the

of
({\mathcal{A}}\to M,\alpha)
.

It can be checked that
d(e^{t}\alpha)
is a
{\mathcal{A}}\boxtimes T\mathbb{R}
-symplectic form.

We can also observe that the symplectisation is a functor. It takes Lie algebroid contact structures of a given subclass (e.g.
b^{k}
, complex-log, elliptic, self-crossing geometries) to symplectic structures of the same subclass. Indeed, any morphism
\psi:({\mathcal{A}}\to M)\rightarrow({\mathcal{B}}\to N)
(meaning a bracket-preserving bundle map between algebroids, commuting with the anchor, and lifting an equi-dimensional immersion) pulling back the contact structure
\eta=\ker(\beta)\subset{\mathcal{B}}
to
\xi=\ker(\alpha)\subset{\mathcal{A}}
has a conformal factor
f:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
\psi^{*}\beta=e^{f}\alpha
. The associated symplectomorphism between the symplectisations is then
(p,t)\mapsto(\psi(p),e^{f(p)}+t)
.

5.4.
The space of contact elements

Given a Lie algebroid
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
, we now explain how to produce a Lie algebroid contact structure in
\mathbb{S}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
, the sphere bundle of its dual. This generalises the usual construction of the space of contact elements in the sphere cotangent bundle.

Let
\mathbb{R}^{*}
act on

by scaling and consider the quotient
\mathbb{S}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}:=({\mathcal{A}}^{*}\setminus M)/\mathbb{R}^{*}
. Because

acts via
\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
-algebroid morphisms,
\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
descends to an algebroid on

. Alternatively, the same Lie algebroid may be obtained as the pullback
\pi_{\mathbb{S}}^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
, where
\pi_{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\rightarrow M
denotes the projection. Since
\lambda_{{\operatorname{can}}}\in\Omega^{1}(\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}})
is
\mathbb{R}^{*}
-homogeneous, its kernel is
\mathbb{R}^{*}
-invariant and thus descends to a corank-
1

{\mathcal{D}}_{\operatorname{can}}\subset\pi_{\mathbb{S}}^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
.

5.10
.

The canonical
\pi_{\mathbb{S}}^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution

is contact. Its symplectisation is isomorphic to
(\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}|_{{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\setminus M},\omega_{\operatorname{can}})
.

The pair
(\pi_{\mathbb{S}}^{!}{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}}_{\operatorname{can}})
is called the

of
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
.

5.7.
The elliptic setting

In this section we will comment on some ways to construct examples of elliptic contact structures and their relation with b-contact structures.

5.0
Generalized contact structures.

Elliptic symplectic structures arise from the study of special generalized complex structures. They are in one-to-one correspondence with a special class of generalized complex structures called stable.

An important question in generalized geometry is what the right notion of a “generalized contact structure” is. As of yet there are many competing notions (see for instance
[
VW15
]
and the citations therein), however all of these have a shortcoming: Boundaries of generalized complex structures don’t seem to provide examples of generalized contact structures.

Elliptic contact structures do appear as the contact boundaries of elliptic symplectic manifolds, see Lemma
5.12
. Given that elliptic symplectic structures correspond to stable generalized complex structures, it would be very interesting to investigate whether there is a notion of generalized contact structures which have elliptic contact structures as examples.

In contrast to the classic version of this result, the orbit

Using the foliated Weinstein conjecture we can re-obtain a Weinstein conjecture for overtwisted

-contact forms:

Let

-contact manifold. Assume that there exists an overtwisted disk in

•

There is a contractible Reeb orbit of
\alpha
in

•

Proof.

If

Let

6.1.4.
“True” orbits in the divisor

In the above theorems we showed that certain orbits in

and applying Lemma

, that is why we consider the following definition:

6.10
.

Let
(M,Z,\alpha)
be a
b^{k}
-contact manifold, and let
\gamma
in
Z
be a closed Reeb orbit. We say that
\gamma
is a
true

\gamma
lie in a closed orbit in
(Z\times\mathbb{R},\alpha_{\operatorname{reg},t})

6.0
.

Let
(M,Z,\alpha)
be an
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-invariant
b^{k}
-contact manifold. Then
\alpha=uz^{-k}dz+\beta
on a tubular neighbourhood, and
\alpha_{\operatorname{reg},Z}=uds+\beta
. If we let If we let
R=gz^{k}\partial_{z}+Y
denote the Reeb vector field of
\alpha

g\in C^{\infty}(Z)
and

In case

on a Reeb orbit
\gamma

The following example is discussed in
[
MO21
, Example 6.8]
, we comment on it because it neatly visualises the construction of orbits:

6.11
.

Consider the
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-invariant b-contact manifold
(T^{3},\alpha=\sin\theta_{2}\frac{d\theta_{1}}{\sin\theta_{1}}+\cos\theta_{2}d\theta_{3})
with Reeb vector field
R_{\alpha}=\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1}\partial_{\theta_{1}}+\cos\theta_{2}\partial_{\theta_{3}}

to

\cos\theta_{2}\partial_{\theta_{3}}

define orbits, which are non-constant if
\theta_{2}\neq\pi/2,3\pi/2

Note that

is contactomorphic to
(Z\times\mathbb{R}^{*},\alpha_{\operatorname{reg}}=\sin\theta_{2}ds+\cos\theta_{2}d\theta_{3})

Therefore, we cannot apply Theorem

•

Vertical : When

Slanted, and wrapping around: When

6.2.
The Weinstein conjecture for elliptic contact forms

We would also like to use the regularisation procedure to produce Reeb orbits in an elliptic contact manifold
(M,\alpha)
. Even though we can apply the foliated Weinstein conjecture to obtain orbits in the regularisation, unfortunately we can not prove that they yield orbits on
M
. For b-contact manifolds we were able to use contractibility of the orbits to exclude the case that they wrap around vertical in
Z\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This reasoning does not work in the elliptic setting.

However, we can prove the Weinstein conjecture for elliptic contact manifolds as long as some invariance is present. Following the notion of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
-invariance for
b^{k}
-contact forms, we can define the equivalent notion for elliptic contact:

6.12
.

An elliptic contact form
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\mathcal{A}_{\left|D\right|})
is said to be
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-
invariant
if there exists a tubular neighbourhood of
D
in which it has the form

\alpha=u_{1}d\log r+u_{2}d\theta+\beta,\quad,u_{1},u_{2}\in C^{\infty}(D),\beta\in\Omega^{1}(D).\hfill

6.0
.

Using an identical reasoning as in Proposition
5.26
one can show that any co-orientable elliptic contact structure can be represented by a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-invariant elliptic contact form. Natural examples of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-invariant contact forms are obtained through Proposition
5.28
.

6.13
.

Let
(M^{2n+1},I_{\left|D\right|},\alpha)
be a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-invariant elliptic contact manifold. Then there exists a closed Reeb orbit in
M\backslash D
.

Proof.

We consider the real oriented blow-up along
Z
, and the b-contact structure it inherits via Lemma
5.29
:
(\tilde{M},\partial\tilde{M},p^{*}{\alpha})
. Note that
p^{*}\alpha
is an
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-invariant contact structure and thus we apply Theorem
6.7
to obtain the existence of a Reeb orbit in
\tilde{M}\backslash\partial\tilde{M}
. Strictly speaking Theorem
6.1
(and consequently Theorem
6.7
) is only proven for manifolds without boundary. However, note that the compact regularisation on
\tilde{M}\times S^{1}
is well-behaved: All leaves have no boundary, and the leaf
(\partial\tilde{M})\times S^{1}
is precisely the boundary of
\tilde{M}\times S^{1}
. Therefore, it is clear that we may still apply Theorem
6.1
and Theorem
6.7
to obtain a closed Reeb orbit in
\tilde{M}\backslash\partial\tilde{M}

.
∎

6.3.
The Weinstein conjecture in self-crossing

-geometry

We can also straightforwardly adapt the reasoning to b-contact forms with immersed hypersurface:

Let

7.
Other Lie algebroid distributions

We now present various constructions of Lie algebroid distributions outside of the contact setting. Namely, we introduce the annihilator (Subsection
7.2
) and sphere annihilator constructions (Subsection
7.3
), which generalise the symplectisation and the space of contact elements, respectively.

7.1.
Even-contact structures and Engel structures

Before we discuss constructions, let us introduce a couple of relevant families of distributions. For algebroids of even rank, one can define the following analogue of contact structures:

7.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\to M
be a Lie algebroid of rank
2r
. A corank-
1
distribution
\xi\subset{\mathcal{A}}
is said to be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-even-contact structure
if its first curvature

\Omega_{1,1}(\xi):\xi\times\xi\longrightarrow{\mathcal{A}}/\xi

has
1
-dimensional kernel.

In analogy with the classic case, another interesting class of structures is:

7.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid of rank
4
. An
{\mathcal{A}}
-
Engel structure
, is a regular distribution
\xi\subset{\mathcal{A}}
of rank
2
with
[\xi,\xi]\subset{\mathcal{A}}
even-contact.

7.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{H}_{r}\oplus\mathbb{R}
, the sum of the Heisenberg Lie algebra and a trivial factor. Then
\xi
from Example
5.3
defines an even-contact structure.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the 4-dimensional Lie algebra with basis
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\}
with
[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{3}
and
[e_{2},e_{3}]=e_{4}
and all other brackets zero. Then
\xi=\text{span}(e_{1},e_{2})
is Engel.

7.2.
The annihilator of a distribution

In the classical setting, the annihilator of a distribution can be equipped with the restriction of the Liouville form. It turns out that the non-involutivity of the distribution is then encoded in the geometry of this restricted form. We now explain how this adapts to general Lie algebroids.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid and write
\pi:{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\to M
for its dual. Given a distribution
\xi\subset{\mathcal{A}}
, we write

\xi^{\bot}:=\{\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\,\mid\,\alpha|_{\xi}=0\}

for its
annihilator
. This is a submanifold of
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
(and in particular a vector subbundle). For simplicity, let us write
p:\xi^{\bot}\to M
for the restriction of
\pi
to
\xi^{\bot}
.

The pullback algebroid
p^{!}{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\xi^{\bot}
sits naturally inside
\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}|_{\xi^{\bot}}
and, as such, we can restrict
\lambda_{\operatorname{can}}
to it. We then define:

7.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid. The
Bott form
of the distribution
\xi\subset A
is:

\omega^{\xi}_{\operatorname{can}}:=\omega_{{\operatorname{can}}}|_{p^{!}{\mathcal{A}}}.

Similarly, we denote
\lambda^{\xi}_{\operatorname{can}}:=\lambda_{{\operatorname{can}}}|_{p^{!}{\mathcal{A}}}
.

We call it the Bott form because it defines the Bott connection of
\xi
if
\xi
is involutive; see Lemma
7.6
below.

In local terms, if
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
is a coframe of
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
such that
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k})
spans
\xi^{\bot}
, we have that:

\lambda^{\xi}_{{\operatorname{can}}}=t_{1}\alpha_{1}+\cdots+t_{k}\alpha_{k},

where
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
are the associated fibrewise coordinates in
{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
(so, in particular,
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
are fibrewise coordinates in
\xi^{\bot}
). Similarly, the Bott form reads:

\omega_{{\operatorname{can}}}^{\xi}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}dt_{i}\wedge\alpha_{i}+t_{i}d\alpha_{i},

which fails to be symplectic along the zero section unless
\xi=0
.

The more interesting question is whether
\omega^{\xi}_{{\operatorname{can}}}
is symplectic in
\xi^{\perp}\setminus M
. As in the classical case:

7.5
.

Let
\xi\subset{\mathcal{A}}
be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution of corank
1
. Then,
\xi
is contact if and only if its Bott form is symplectic away from the zero section.

Furthermore, if
\xi=\ker(\alpha)
, with
\alpha\in\Gamma({\mathcal{A}}^{*})
, then
(p^{!}{\mathcal{A}}|_{\xi^{\perp}\setminus M},\omega_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi})
has two components and both are isomorphic to the symplectisation of
({\mathcal{A}}\to M,\alpha)
.

At the opposite end of the spectrum:

7.6
.

Let
\xi\subset{\mathcal{A}}
be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution of rank
k
. Then,
\xi
is involutive if and only if its Bott form is of constant rank
2k
.

If this is the case, the kernel
\ker(\omega_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi})\subset p^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
defines a flat connection in
\xi^{\perp}
, which we call the
Bott connection
.

7.3.
The sphere annihilator of a distribution

Consider now the annihilator sphere bundle
p_{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}\xi^{\perp}\to M
. Since
\mathbb{R}^{*}
acts by
p^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
-algebroid morphisms we obtain a Lie algebroid
p^{!}_{\mathbb{S}}{\mathcal{A}}
on
\mathbb{S}\xi^{\perp}
. Due to homogeneity of
\lambda^{\xi}_{\operatorname{can}}
, we similarly obtain a corank-
1
distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi}\subset p^{!}_{\mathbb{S}}{\mathcal{A}}
.

The geometric interpretation of
{\mathcal{D}}_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi}
is that it is the universal object encoding hyperplane
{\mathcal{A}}
-distributions containing
\xi
.

In analogy with Lemma
7.6
, it can be checked that
\xi
is involutive if and only if
{\mathcal{D}}_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi}
is involutive. In the opposite direction, a case that is of interest (at least in the classical setting) is the following:

7.7
.

An
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution
\xi
is said to be
fat
if
{\mathcal{D}}_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi}
is contact.

Equivalently, combining Lemmas
5.10
and
7.5
, yields that
\xi
is fat if and only if its Bott form is symplectic.

7.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the
6
-dimensional Lie algebra defined by the brackets
[e_{1},e_{2}]=[e_{3},e_{4}]=e_{5}
and
[e_{1},e_{3}]=-[e_{2},e_{4}]=e_{6}
and consider the distribution
\xi=\langle e_{1},e_{2},e_{3},e_{4}\rangle
. This algebra may be endowed with a compatible grading, in which
\xi
is the degree-
1
piece and
\langle e_{5},e_{6}\rangle
is the degree-
2
piece. With this grading,
\mathfrak{g}
is said to be the
elliptic
algebra of rank 4 and dimension 6.

We then consider the annihilator
\xi^{\bot}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and the pullback Lie algebroid
\xi^{\bot}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
over it. If
\{e_{i}^{*}\}
denotes the basis dual to
\{e_{i}\}
,
\xi^{\bot}
is spanned by
e_{5}^{*}
and
e_{6}^{*}
; we write
(a,b)
for the corresponding coordinates. Then, the Bott form reads:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=ae_{5}^{*}+be_{6}^{*}

\displaystyle\omega_{\operatorname{can}}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=(da\wedge e_{5}^{*}+db\wedge e_{6}^{*})+ade_{5}^{*}+bde_{6}^{*}

\displaystyle=(da\wedge e_{5}^{*}+db\wedge e_{6}^{*})+a(e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{2}^{*}+e_{3}^{*}\wedge e_{4}^{*})+b(e_{1}^{*}\wedge e_{3}^{*}-e_{2}^{*}\wedge e_{4}^{*}),

which is readily seen to be symplectic away from
0\in\xi^{\bot}
, showing that
\xi
is fat.

7.4.
Prolongation of distributions

Dually to the previous subsection, we now define the universal object encoding all the lines contained in an
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution.

7.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid and
\xi
be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-distribution. Its
(Cartan) prolongation
is the pair

(\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}\xi,\mathbb{D}_{\operatorname{can}}),

where
\pi:\mathbb{P}\xi\to M
is the fibrewise projectivisation of
\xi
and

\mathbb{D}_{\operatorname{can}}(L):=\{v\in\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}\,\mid\,v\in L\},

where
L
is an element of
\mathbb{P}\xi
(i.e. a line in
\xi
).

The following Lemma provides many examples of algebroid Engel structures:

7.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid of rank
3
. Let
\xi\subset{\mathcal{A}}
be an
{\mathcal{A}}
-contact structure with projectivisation
\pi:\mathbb{P}\xi\to M
. Then, the prolongation
\mathbb{D}_{\operatorname{can}}
of
\xi\to\mathbb{P}\xi
is a
\pi^{!}{\mathcal{A}}
-Engel structure.

The projectivisation procedure can then be iterated (applying it to
\mathbb{D}_{\operatorname{can}})
to produce further examples of Lie algebroid bracket-generating distributions of rank
2
.

Appendix A
Regularisation from a Poisson and Jacobi perspective

In this appendix we will describe how the regularisation procedure interacts with Poisson and Jacobi geometry. In this paper we mostly focused on regularising
b^{k}
-contact manifolds, but of course many more geometric structures can be regularised. For
{\mathcal{A}}_{Z}
-symplectic structures we have:

A.1
.

Let
(M,Z,\omega)
be a b-symplectic manifold. Then,
(M\times S^{1},{\mathcal{F}}_{\operatorname{reg},c},(d\pi)^{*}\omega)
defines a codimension-one symplectic foliation.

Proof.

Because
\pi
is a Lie algebroid morphism, it is immediate that
(d\pi)^{*}\omega
defines a closed foliated form, so we are left to show that it is leafwise non-degenerate. Because
d\pi
is a surjective submersion between vector bundles of the same rank it is a fibre-wise isomorphism. From this it immediately follows that
(d\pi)^{*}\omega
is non-degenerate.
∎

Interestingly enough this result has already appeared in the literature under several different guises. We will elaborate on this in the following pages.

A.3.
Jacobi structures

Just as b-symplectic structures can be viewed as mildly degenerate Poisson structures, b-contact structures can be viewed as mildly degenerate Jacobi structures. In this appendix we will recall the relevant notions of Jacobi structures and show that the regularisation of a b-contact structure can be described in a Jacobi framework as well. This framework is a direct analogue of the Poisson case, but to the best of our knowledge it has not been discussed in the literature before. Nevertheless, there are some interesting differences between the Poisson and Jacobi case.

A.10
.

A
Jacobi structure
on a manifold is a line bundle
L
endowed with a Lie bracket on its sections
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
with the property that
D_{e}:=\{e,\cdot\}:\Gamma(L)\rightarrow\Gamma(L)
is a first order differential operator for all
e\in\Gamma(L)
.

A.11
.

A
Jacobi pair

(\Lambda,R)\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(M)\times\mathfrak{X}^{1}(M)
is a pair satisfying the following relations:

[\Lambda,\Lambda]=\Lambda\wedge R,\quad[\Lambda,R]=0.\hfill

A.12
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between Jacobi structures
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
on the trivial line bundle, and Jacobi pairs
(\Lambda,R)
given by:

\{f,g\}=\Lambda(df,dg)+fR(g)-gR(f),\quad\text{for all }f,g\in\Gamma(\underline{\mathbb{R}})=C^{\infty}(M).

A.3.1.

One way of dealing with Jacobi structures, is to consider an associated Poisson structure. We will first recall this construction for Jacobi pairs:

Appendix B
Recap: Convex surface theory

Convex surfaces play an essential role in the study of contact manifolds, and their theory was developed by Giroux in dimension 3 in
[
Gir91
]
, and in
[
HH19
]
in higher dimensions. Also in the study of

-contact structures they turn out to play an important role.

B.1
.

Let
(M,\xi=\ker\alpha)
be a contact manifold. A
contact vector field

X\in\mathfrak{X}^{1}(M)
is a vector field for which
(\mathcal{L}_{X}\alpha)|_{\xi}=0
. For a function

the unique contact vector field satisfying
\alpha(X_{f})=f
is the
Hamiltonian vector field
associated to

.

B.2
.

A hypersurface
\Sigma\subset(M,\xi)
is

if there exists a contact vector field transverse to
\Sigma
.

Note that when
\Sigma\subset(M,\xi)
is convex it is in particular co-orientable. For any co-orientable hypersurface
\Sigma\subset(M,\xi)
we may write
\alpha=u_{t}dt+\beta_{t}
with
u_{t}\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\beta_{t}\in\Omega^{1}(M)
. Convexity is equivalent with the fact that
u_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
are pull-backs from
Z
.

Convex hypersurfaces enter the study of b-contact forms via the following result:

[

]

Let
(M,\xi)
be a contact manifold and let

be a convex hypersurface in

. Then

admits a

-contact structure for all

that has

as critical set. Also
M
admits a

-contact structure for all
k
that has two diffeomorphic connected components

and
Z_{2}
as critical
set, one of which can be taken to coincide with

.

B.4
.

Let
\Sigma\subset(M,\xi=\ker\alpha)
be a convex hypersurface with transverse contact vector field

. The

is defined as
\Gamma(\Sigma)=\{x\in\Sigma:\alpha_{x}(X_{x})=0\}
and its complement

.

.

These definitions are consistent: The dividing set of a b-contact structure obtained using Theorem

In dimension three

is a disjoint collection of curves, which determines the contact structure (up to isotopy) completely around

\Gamma(\Sigma)

In higher dimensions the situation is more complicated, and
\Gamma
is a codimension two submanifold of
M
. Moreover, the dividing set will inherit the structure of an ideal Liouville domain:

B.6
[
Gir17
]
.

An
ideal Liouville domain
is a manifold with boundary
F
, endowed with an exact symplectic form
d\lambda
on
{\rm Int}F
such that there exists a defining function
u
for
\partial F
such that
u\lambda
extends to, and induces a contact form on,
\partial F
.

B.7
[
HH19
]
.

Let
\Sigma\subset(M,\xi)
be a convex hypersurface. Then

•

\Gamma(\Sigma)
is a codimension-two contact submanifold and

•

R_{\pm}
inherits the structure of an ideal Liouville domain, for which the induced contact structure on the boundary coincides with
\left.{\xi}\right|_{\Gamma}
.

We include the proof for completeness, and future reference:

Proof.

Convexity ensures that there exists a neighbourhood of
\Sigma
such that
\alpha=udt+\beta
, with
u\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
and
\beta\in\Omega^{1}(\Sigma)
. The contact condition states that

\displaystyle\alpha\wedge(d\alpha)^{n}

\displaystyle=udt\wedge(d\beta)^{n}+\beta\wedge(d\beta)^{n}+n\beta\wedge du\wedge dt\wedge(d\beta)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=udt\wedge(d\beta)^{n}+n\beta\wedge du\wedge dt\wedge(d\beta)^{n-1},

\displaystyle=dt\wedge(u(d\beta)^{n}+n\beta\wedge du\wedge(d\beta)^{n-1})

where the last term dropped out because of dimension reasons. Now define
\lambda=\beta/u
, then
d\lambda=u^{-1}d\beta+u^{-2}\beta\wedge du
, and consequently

\displaystyle(d\lambda)^{n}

\displaystyle=u^{-n}(d\beta)^{n}+nu^{-n-1}(d\beta)^{n-1}\beta\wedge du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u^{n+1}}(u(d\beta)^{n}+n(d\beta)^{n-1}\wedge\beta\wedge du),

and is therefore symplectic. We see that
R_{\pm}
has the structure of ideal Liouville domain, with the contact structure on the boundary induced by
\beta
.
∎

The fact that the contact manifold
\Gamma(\Sigma)
can be realized as the boundary of a Liouville manifold immediately implies the following:

B.8
.

Let
\Sigma\subset(M,\xi)
be a convex hypersurface. Then
(\Gamma(\Sigma),\left.{\xi}\right|_{\Gamma(\Sigma)})
is a tight contact manifold.

Proof.

Again, consider the decomposition
\alpha=udt+\beta
as before, and define
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=u^{-1}(\varepsilon)
. Note, that for
\varepsilon
small
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is diffeomorphic to
\Gamma
. Moreover,
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is (one component of) the boundary of
u^{-1}([\varepsilon,\infty))
and therefore fillable. But as the contact structure on
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is simply
\beta/\varepsilon
, we conclude that it is contactomorphic to
\Gamma
, which is then fillable.
∎
