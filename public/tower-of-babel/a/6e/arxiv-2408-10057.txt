Distributions with locally free tangent sheaf
Jorge Vitório Pereira, João Pedro dos Santos

Abstract
. In [ CP08 ] one finds a study of the locus Dec \mathrm{Dec} where the tangent sheaf of a family of foliations in ℙ ℂ n \mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n} is decomposable , i.e. a sum of line bundles. A prime conclusion is an “openness” result: once the singular locus has sufficiently large codimension, Dec \mathrm{Dec} turns out to be open. In the present paper, we study the locus LF \mathrm{LF} of points of a family of distributions where the tangent sheaf is locally free . Through general Commutative Algebra, we show that LF \mathrm{LF} is open provided that singularities have codimension at least three. When dealing with foliations rather than distributions, the condition on the lower bound of the singular set can be weakened by the introduction of “Kupka” points. We apply the available “openness” results to families in ℙ ℂ n \mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}} and in ℬ \mathcal{B} , the variety of Borel subgroups of a simple group. By establishing a theorem putting in bijection irreducible components of the space of two-dimensional subalgebras of a given semi-simple Lie algebra and its nilpotent orbits, we conclude that the space of foliations of rank two on ℙ ℂ n \mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n} and ℬ \mathcal{B} , may have quite many irreducible components as n n and dim ℬ \dim\mathcal{B} grow. We also set in place several algebro-geometric foundations for the theory of families of distributions in two appendices.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we wish to throw light on the distributions and foliations admitting a locally free tangent sheaf. More precisely, we wish to study how this property may vary in a family with the goal of showing that whenever singularities have “large codimension”, this property is “open”.

Now,
if we understand foliations as a generalisation of vector fields or 1-forms, then the idea of studying which topological properties are preserved by small deformations has a distinguished history and goes under the name of “structural stability”
[
AP37
,
Pe59
,
Ku64
,
dM77
]
. On the other hand, it is then only natural to look at
algebraic
output properties, as was
done in

[
CP08
]
in the case of forms on
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
. There, the authors use the precise description of
q
-forms on
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
in terms of homogeneous polynomial forms in order to show that if
\omega\in H^{0}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\Omega^{q}(m))
defines a singular foliation such that
\codim\sing\geq 3
and the tangent sheaf
T_{\omega}
associated to it is decomposable (i.e. a sum of invertible sheaves), then any
q
-form (inducing a foliation) sufficiently close to
\omega
also produces a decomposable tangent sheaf.
See Theorem
3.4
below for a restatement.
The present work seeks to expand the scope of this result in the following directions:

(i)

consider locally free sheaves which are not necessarily sums of invertible ones;

(ii)

consider varieties other than
\mathbb{P}^{n}
(and Lie algebras other than
\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}
);

(iii)

consider base fields other than
\mathbb{C}
;

(iv)

consider the “Kupka” condition on singularities for higher codimensions and more general ambient spaces.

In addition, we

(v)

wish
to find cases where the assumption on the codimension of singularities is naturally met and, once this is done,

(vi)

use the results to find irreducible components of the spaces of integrable forms (or of foliations).

In the ensuing sections, we describe more precisely the themes and results which are treated in this work.

1.1.
“Openess” results

The results obtained under this rubric are the raison d’être of this paper. They generalise
[
CP08
, Theorem 2]
(reproduced as Theorem
3.4
below) and are explained in Section
4
.

The concept of a holomorphic (algebraic) singular foliation
\mathcal{F}
on a given (algebraic) complex manifold
X
is well-established and it has become in the past decades crucial — or at least standard — to engage it by means of sheaf theory. So, the several qualifications that a sheaf can have become important indicators of the singular foliation itself. In this part of the Introduction, we wish to explain our results showing that the property of local freeness of tangent sheaves is “stable”, or “open”.

Even a superficial contact with the theory of foliations shows that the usual operations which one makes with a sheaf do not immediately correspond to the operations on the foliations side (see Example
4.9
for a sample of this). This adds complexity to the study of
families of foliations
and the application of known results of Commutative
Algebra to this setting requires special efforts. Indeed,
even in a very comfortable context, pulling back foliations is not synonym with pulling back sheaves. For example, let
f:X\to S
be a smooth morphism of smooth complex algebraic schemes and let
\mathcal{F}
be a singular foliation on
X
whose tangent sheaf
T_{\mathcal{F}}

is a subsheaf of
T_{f}=T_{X/S}
, the relative tangent sheaf.
Then,
given a closed point
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
, the restricted foliation
\mathcal{F}_{s}
induced on the fibre
X_{s}

does not

have, in general, the restriction
T_{\mathcal{F}}|_{X_{s}}
as tangent sheaf.
On the other hand, the link between
T_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}
and
T_{\mathcal{F}}|_{X_{s}}
can be given through Commutative Algebra, which is used to prove the following result. For unexplained notation, the reader is directed to Appendix
A
; here we content ourselves with pointing out that
\mathrm{Sing}
stands for the singular set of a relative distribution
\mathcal{V}
, i.e. the set of points where
T_{f}/T_{\mathcal{V}}
fails to be free.

Theorem A
.

Let
f:X\to S
be a proper and smooth morphism of Noetherian schemes. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a relative distribution on
X
, i.e. a strongly saturated coherent submodule of the relative tangent sheaf
T_{f}
.
For each
s\in S
, let
X_{s}
denote the (schematic) fibre of
X
above
s
and denote by
\mathcal{V}_{s}
the pull-back of
\mathcal{V}
to
X_{s}
.
Assume that for a certain
o\in S
, we have

(1)

that
\codim(\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})\cap X_{o},X_{o})\geq 3

and that

(2)

the tangent sheaf
T_{\mathcal{V}_{o}}
is locally free.

Then for all
s
in a certain neighbourhood of
o
, the tangent sheaf
T_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}
is also locally free.

In giving a proof of Theorem
A
, we employed a somewhat weaker hypothesis than the “smallness” of the singular set (explained above), which is the “smallness” of the set of
tangent singularities

\mathrm{TSing}
. (See Theorem
4.3
.)
By definition, tangent singularities are points where the tangent sheaf fails to be free.
Now, as explained in Lemma
B.3
, for any distribution
\mathcal{V}
, we have an inclusion
\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})\subset\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})

and one of the best tools to obtain points of
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})\setminus\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})
is, when
\mathcal{V}
is involutive, by means of “Kupka”
singularities
\mathrm{Kup}(\mathcal{V})
(cf. Section
B.4
). Indeed, as is shown in Proposition
B.31
at least when
S
is locally factorial, the inclusion
\mathrm{NKup}(\mathcal{V})\supset\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})
holds true. (Here
\mathrm{NKup}(\mathcal{V})
is the set of non-Kupka” singularities
\sing(\mathcal{V})\setminus\mathrm{Kup}(\mathcal{V})
.)
Hence, we can state a variant of Theorem
A
which reads.

Theorem B
.

Let
f:X\to S
be a proper and smooth morphism of Noetherian schemes. Let
q\geq 1
be an integer,
L
a line bundle on
X
and
\omega\in\Gamma(X,\Omega^{q}_{f}\otimes L)
an LDS form (cf. Section
B.1
). Denote the associated distribution by
\mathcal{V}
and suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is in fact involutive.

For each
s\in S
, let
X_{s}
denote the fibre of
X
above
s
and denote by
\mathcal{V}_{s}
the pull-back of
\mathcal{V}
to
X_{s}
.
Assume that for a certain
o\in S
,

(1)

we have
\codim(\sing(\mathcal{V})\cap X_{o},X_{o})\geq 2
,

(2)

if
\mathrm{NKup}(\mathcal{V})
stands for the “non-Kupka locus” of
\sing(\mathcal{V})
, then

\mathrm{codim}(\mathrm{NKup}(\mathcal{V})\cap X_{o}\,,\,X_{o})\geq 3,

and

(3)

the sheaf
T_{\mathcal{V}_{o}}
is locally free.

Then for all
s
in a certain neighbourhood of
o
, the tangent sheaf
T_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}
is also locally free.

1.2.
The space of Lie subalgebras

The parts under this rubric are in Section
2
; their relevance to this work lies in the fact that one of its outputs (Theorem
C
) shall enable us to give lower bounds to the number of irreducible components of the space of foliations (see Section
1.3
). But we highlight that the theme “spaces of subalgebras” is intrinsically an interesting one and may be pursued for its own sake. So much so that Section
2

can be read independently and only requires basic knowledge of Algebraic Geometry and Lie algebras.

An important class of foliations is given by (certain) actions of a Lie algebra. More precisely, given a field
\Bbbk
and a smooth
\Bbbk
-scheme
X
, any subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
of the Lie algebra
H^{0}(X,T_{X/\Bbbk})
gives rise to an
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-linear map
\gamma:\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}\to T_{X/\Bbbk}
compatible with Lie brackets. Granted reasonable hypothesis, this gives rise to a foliation
\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{h})
with tangent sheaf
\mathfrak{h}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}
, so that “openness results” (cf. in Section
1.1
) may lead to information about the “size” of foliations by actions inside a given family. This idea shall be explored in detail (see Section
1.3
).

Let
\Bbbk
be a field and let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite dimensional Lie algebra over
\Bbbk
. For a given integer
1\leq r<\dim\mathfrak{g}
, it is not difficult to construct a closed subscheme
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
of the Grassmann variety
\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})
of
r
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathfrak{g}
, whose
\Bbbk
-valued points correspond to
r
-dimensional subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
. This beautiful space was introduced by Richardson
[
R67
]
and has unfortunately drawn little attention from mathematicians. In Section
2
, we show :

Theorem C
.

Let
\Bbbk
be algebraically closed and of characteristic zero, and let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semi-simple Lie algebra over
\Bbbk
other than
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.
The irreducible components of

\mathbf{SLie}(2,\mathfrak{g})
are in
bijection
with
the set of conjugacy classes of nilpotent elements of
\mathfrak{g}
.

The veracity of this result can be grasped by the two observations: (1) the “abelian”
\Bbbk
-points of
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
are in close relation with the “variety of commuting elements”, which is irreducible by
[
R79
]
, and (2) given a non-abelian 2-dimensional subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
, the derived algebra
[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]
is a line of
nilpotent elements
(cf. Section
2.3
), and is hence contained in a
single
nilpotent orbit
[
BLie
, VIII.11.2-3]
.

1.3.
Irreducible components of the spaces of foliations

Let
m\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
be integers and consider the quasi-projective complex algebraic set

\mathrm{Fol}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},m)=\left\{\omega\in\mathbb{P}\left(H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},\Omega^{1}(m+2))\right)\,:\,\begin{array}[]{l}\mathrm{d}\omega\wedge\omega=0\\
\dim\mathrm{Sing}(\omega)\leq n-2\end{array}\right\},

the
space of foliations of codimension one and degree
m
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, or the space of
irreducible and integrable algebraic Pfaff equations

[
Jo79
, Ch. 2]
. Given its encompassing geometric content,
\mathrm{Fol}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},m)
has always attracted attention of geometers and the determination of the number of its irreducible components is an affordable and beautiful problem.
For the sake of discussion, let us write

\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},m):=\#\,\text{irreducible components of $\mathrm{Fol}^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{n},m)$}.

(As traditional in many areas of Geometry, in our notations, upper-indices refer to “codimension” while lower ones to “dimension”.)

If
m\leq 2
, it is known that

\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3},m)=\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{4},m)=\cdots.

More precisely,
\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},0)=1
(this is a simple exercise), while
\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},1)=2
provided that
n\geq 3

[
Jo79
, Ch. 1, Proposition 3.5.1]
. A central result of the theory
[
CLN96
]
affirms that

\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},2)=6\quad\text{whenever $n\geq 3$.}

On the other hand, an upper bound for
\mathrm{ic}^{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},m)
was obtained in
[
Bal94
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Needless to say, this circle of ideas is also relevant in higher codimension.
Let
m\geq 0
and
q\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
be given, and for each
\omega\in H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},\Omega^{q}(m+q+1))
, define

\mathrm{Ker}_{\omega}=\mathrm{Ker}\left(\lx@xy@svg{\hbox{\raise 2.5pt\hbox{\kern 6.61632pt\hbox{\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\hbox{\vtop{\halign{\entry@#!@&&\entry@@#!@\cr&&&\crcr}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern-6.61632pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{T\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern 6.63214pt\raise 5.84723pt\hbox{{}\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise-2.01389pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\mathrm{id}\otimes\omega}$}}}\kern 3.0pt}}}}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern 30.61632pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}{\hbox{\kern 30.61632pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{T\otimes\Omega^{q}(m+q+1)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern 118.54602pt\raise 5.84723pt\hbox{{}\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise-2.01389pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\text{contract}\otimes\mathrm{id}}$}}}\kern 3.0pt}}}}}}\ignorespaces{\hbox{\kern 172.5348pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\lx@xy@tip{1}\lx@xy@tip{-1}}}}}}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}{\hbox{\lx@xy@droprule}}{\hbox{\kern 142.5348pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{}$}}}}}}}{\hbox{\kern 172.5348pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\hbox{\kern 3.0pt\raise-2.5pt\hbox{$\textstyle{\Omega^{q-1}(m+q+1)}$}}}}}}}\ignorespaces}}}}\ignorespaces\right).

(Here
T
stands for the tangent sheaf of
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
.)
Then, inside
\mathbb{P}H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},\Omega^{q}(m+q+1))
, there exists a locally closed algebraic subset

\mathrm{Fol}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{n},m)=\begin{array}[]{c}\text{``Space of singular foliations }\\
\text{on $\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}$ of codimension $q$}\\
\text{ and degree $m$'',}\end{array}

such that

\omega\longmapsto\mathrm{Ker}_{\omega}

establishes a bijection between
\mathrm{Fol}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{n},m)
and the saturated, involutive
\mathcal{O}
-submodules of
T
of rank
n-q
, i.e. singular foliations of codimension
q
. Now, writing
r=n-q
and putting
\mathrm{Fol}_{r}=\mathrm{Fol}^{q}
, let

\begin{split}\mathrm{ic}_{r}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},m)&=\mathrm{ic}^{q}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},m)\\
&=\#\,\text{irreducible components of $\mathrm{Fol}_{r}(\mathbb{P}^{n},m)$}.\end{split}

Then, provided that
r<n
, it is known that

\mathrm{ic}_{r}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},0)=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{ic}_{r}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},1)=2.

The first equality can be read between the lines in
[
CD05
]
, while the second is
[
LPT13
, Corollary 6.3]
. In sharp contrast, we deduce the following from Theorem
C
and
[
CP08
, Corollary 6.1]
.

Corollary D
.

Let
\mathrm{ic}_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},2)
be the number of irreducible components of the space
\mathrm{Fol}_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n},2)
of foliations on
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
of degree
2
and dimension
2
. Then, for
n\geq 4
, we have

\mathrm{ic}_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},2)\geq\#\left\{\text{partitions of $n-4$}\right\}.

In particular, by “Hardy-Ramanujan” (an explicit lower bound is in
[
Mar03
, Corollary 3.1]
), we have

\mathrm{ic}_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}},2)>\frac{e^{2\sqrt{n-4}}}{14}.

In order to apply
[
CP08
, Corollary 6.1]
and Theorem
C
to prove Corollary
D
, we are required to construct foliations on
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
of
rank two

whose singular set has codimension
at least three
. This we obtain by direct computations with Lie algebras of vector fields in
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}}
, see Corollary
3.2
. Now, since Theorem
A
generalizes
[
CP08
, Corollary 6.1]
, the search for Lie algebras of vector fields with “small” singular sets becomes important.

Question E
.

Let
\Bbbk
be a field,
X
a smooth and projective variety,
\mathfrak{g}
the Lie algebra of global vector fields on
X
, and
\gamma:\mathcal{O}_{X}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\to T_{X}
the natural morphism of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules.
Are there subalgebras
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
such that the “singular set”

\left\{p\in X\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}(\mathcal{O}_{X}\otimes\mathfrak{h})(p)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma}}{{\to}}T_{X}(p)\\
\text{fails to be injective}\end{array}\right\}

has large codimension?

In order to have positive answers en masse, we are led to consider varieties of Borel subgroups (or Borel varieties), see Section
5
. The advantage of looking at these comes from the possibility of drawing from the vast literature established by group theorists on the theme of “regular elements” and “Springer fibres”. Indeed, early in the theory of algebraic groups, the importance of having information on the “number”
of Borel subgroups containing a given element was noticed
[
Sp69
]
. This produced an impressive body of work
[
Mc02
]
, allowing us to have deep results at hand. It then becomes possible to give satisfactory answers to Question
E
and then to
apply our “openess results” (Theorem
A
) in order to conclude prove:

Theorem F
.

Let
\Bbbk
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. Let
G
be a linear algebraic group of adjoint type with simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\Phi
be the reduced root system associated to the choice of a Borel subgroup of
G
and
X
the corresponding Borel variety. Assume that
\Phi
is not of type
\mathrm{A}_{1},\mathrm{A}_{2},\mathrm{A}_{3},\mathrm{B}_{2},\mathrm{C}_{3}
or
\mathrm{G}_{2}
.

(1)

Given
\mathfrak{h}
in
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})(\Bbbk)
, the natural morphism of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
–modules

\gamma:\mathcal{O}_{X}\otimes\mathfrak{h}\longrightarrow T_{X}

is injective on an open subscheme whose complement has codimension at least three. (See Section
5.6
.)

(2)

There exists a morphism of reduced schemes

\psi:\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fol}_{2}(X,\det T_{X})

with the following properties.

(a)

For
\mathfrak{h}\in\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})(\Bbbk)
, the point
\psi(\mathfrak{h})
is the
(\dim X-2)
-form associated to the foliation deduced from

\mathcal{O}_{X}\otimes\mathfrak{h}\to T_{X}
. (See Section
5.6
.)

(b)

The morphism
\psi
is open. (See Theorem
6.3
)

(3)

The number of irreducible components of
\mathrm{Fol}_{2}(X,\det T_{X})
is at least the number of nilpotent conjugacy classes in
\mathfrak{g}
.

1.4.
Generalities

In the last four decades there has been an interest in studying “spaces of distributions” or “spaces of foliations” using three different methods. The first tends to identify distributions (or foliations) with differential forms and finds its modern introduction in the influential text
[
Jo79
, 4.1]
.
Once we deal with distributions of codimension higher than 1, this approach depends on the correspondence between distributions and forms
[
dM77
,
dM00
]
. Another point of view, introduced by
[
P87
]
and adopted in
[
Q15
,
CJM22
,
Ve24
]
, uses Grothendieck’s Quot-scheme in order to study distributions by means of their sheaves. Finally, another method is that of
[
Ba87
]
, which changes the usage of the Quot-scheme by the Chow scheme.
These approaches are not identical and comparing them more carefully is to be done elsewhere. Here, we shall work with “distributions spaces”
as “spaces of twisted forms”, staying thus in the line of
[
Jo79
,
CLN96
,
dM00
,
CP08
]
.
This decision has a cost, which we now explain.

The reader
entering the bulk of our arguments will probably realize that a certain amount of non-trivial Commutative Algebra is necessary to attain our goals. Indeed, already a working definition of a family of distributions requires some care, since flatness “on the base” (which amounts to the approach through the Quot-scheme
[
Q15
]
) cannot be taken unreservedly (see for example
[
Q15
, Section 2]
).
This is the reason for which we end the paper with generalities in Commutative Algebra and in the theory of distributions in the algebraic case: Appendices
A
and
B
. In them, we profit to introduce a number of techniques from Commutative Algebra in the context of distributions, hoping that they may be useful not only as a guide to the non-initiated, but also as a set of first steps into a possible more harmonious theory for “families” of distributions.
That this is a worthy enterprise may be justified by the fact that very little is known about the Commutative Algebra of the “spaces of foliations”; having enough elbow-room to deal with these spaces in a uniform manner should be useful elsewhere.

Notations and conventions

(1)

All rings are commutative and unital and
\Bbbk
always stands for a field.

(2)

All Lie algebras are of finite dimension.

(3)

An open subset
U
of a Noetherian scheme is called
big
if
\mathrm{codim}(X\setminus U,X)\geq 2
.

(4)

For a vector space
V
, we let
\mathbb{P}(V)
be the projective space of
lines
in
V
.

(5)

Given a ring
A
and an
A
-module
M
, we let
M^{\vee}
be
\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(M,A)
. A similar notation is employed for sheaves of modules.

(6)

Given a point
x

, we let
\boldsymbol{k}(x)
stand for its residue field, i.e. the residue field of the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{x}
.

(7)

Given a morphism of schemes

, the fibre of

above a point
x\in X
, often denoted by

, is the scheme
Y\times_{X}\mathrm{Spec}\,\boldsymbol{k}(x)
. This is
homeomorphic
to the subspace
f^{-1}(x)
of
Y

[
EGA
, I, 3.6.1]
.

(8)

By a vector bundle on a scheme, we mean a locally free sheaf of finite and fixed rank.

(9)

For a smooth morphism of schemes
f:X\to S
, we shall write
T_{f}
for the relative tangent sheaf. (One usually writes
T_{X/S}
, which we avoid for the sake of simplicity.)

(10)

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a vector bundle and
\mathcal{E}
be an
\mathcal{O}
-submodule. We say that
\mathcal{E}
is a
subbundle
of
\mathcal{F}
if
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{E}
are vector bundles.

(11)

Given a noetherian scheme
X
and a coherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
on it, its singular scheme
\sing(\mathcal{M})
is the closed set of points
x\in X
where
\mathcal{M}_{x}
fails to be a free
\mathcal{O}_{x}
-module.

(12)

Given a noetherian scheme
X
and
\gamma:E\to F
a morphism of vector bundles, we define

as being the set of points

where the map induced on the
\boldsymbol{k}(x)
-spaces
\gamma(x):E(x)\to F(x)
fails to be
injective
. This is a closed set. If
s
is a section of

, then
\mathrm{Sing}(s)
is the singular set of the obvious morphism
s:\mathcal{O}_{X}\to E
.

2.
The scheme of Lie subalgebras

In this section, we go over the theory of the scheme of Lie subalgebras of a given Lie algebra. In order to give fluidity to the exposition, the schemes in sight shall all be algebraic (i.e. of finite type) over a given algebraically closed field
\Bbbk

[
EGA
,
\mathrm{I}
, 6.4.1]
. In addition,
\Bbbk
shall soon be taken to be of characteristic zero. Points on schemes are always closed, i.e.
\Bbbk
-points.

2.1.
The scheme
\mathbf{SLie}
parametrising subalgebras
[
R67
]

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra over
\Bbbk
and, given
1\leq r<\dim\mathfrak{g}
, let
\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})
stand for the Grassmann variety of
r
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathfrak{g}
. By construction, we have an exact sequence of locally free
\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})}
-modules

(2.1)

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{U}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q}\longrightarrow 0,

where
\mathcal{U}
is the universal subbundle. Now, if
\beta:\wedge^{2}\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{Q}
stands for the composition

\wedge^{2}\mathcal{U}\longrightarrow\wedge^{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[\cdot,\cdot]}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{O}_{\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathcal{Q},

we define
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
as the closed subscheme of
\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})
cut out by the vanishing of
\beta
. Hence, in particular, a point
V\in\mathrm{Gr}(r,\mathfrak{g})
lies in
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
exclusively when
V
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We call
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
the
scheme of
r
-dimensional Lie subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}

[
R67
, Sections 3 and 4]
.
Since we shall be mostly concerned with topological properties of
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
, it is convenient to denote by
\SLie(r,\mathfrak{g})
the
reduced subscheme of
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
associated to
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
.

The tangent space of
\Gr(r,\mathfrak{g})
at a point representing a subspace
V
(recall that points are always closed at this part of the text) is naturally identified with

\Hom(V,\mathfrak{g}/V).

When
V=\mathfrak{h}
is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
then the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}
on itself induces
a
\mathfrak{h}
-module structure on
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
. If
(\mathrm{Hom}_{\Bbbk}(\wedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}),d)

denotes the Chevalley-Eillenberg complex of the
\mathfrak{h}
-module
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}

[
We94
, Corollary 7.7.3, p.240]
then, by definition,

\Hom(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})=C^{1}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
. As shown by Richardson in
[
R67
, Proposition 6.1]
, the Zariski tangent space of
\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
at
\mathfrak{h}
equals

Z^{1}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})=\{\delta:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}\,:\,\delta([xy])=x\delta(y)-y\delta(x)\},

the subsapce of all derivations
[
We94
, 7.4.3]
or crossed homomorphisms.

Now, the Zariski tangent space of the orbit of
\mathfrak{h}\in\mathbf{SLie}(r,\mathfrak{g})
under the action of
G
(induced by
\mathrm{Ad}:G\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})
) equals, according to
[
R67
, Proposition 7.1]
, the space of coboundaries or inner derivations

B^{1}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})=\{dm:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}\,:\,m\in\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}\},

where
dm(x)=xm=[x,m]\mod\mathfrak{h}
cf.
[
We94
, 7.4.4]
.
See also
[
CSS14
]
for a recent presentation of the results quoted above.

2.2.
Commuting varieties

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra and let
r\geq 2
be an integer. The variety of
commuting
r
-tuples of
\mathfrak{g}
is, by definition,

\Commuting_{r}(\mathfrak{g})=\left\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})\in\mathfrak{g}^{r}\,;\,[x_{i},x_{j}]=0,\text{for all $1\leq i,j\leq r$}\right\}\,.

Clearly,
\Commuting_{r}(\mathfrak{g})
is a closed subscheme of the affine space
\mathfrak{g}^{r}
and questions about its nature immediately come to the fore.
Motzkin and Taussky
[
MT55
, Theorem 5]
were the first to show, when
\Bbbk=\mathbb{C}
and by using the Euclidean topology, that
\Commuting_{2}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
is irreducible
.
Gerstenhaber, apparently unaware of the work by Motzkin and Taussky, extended this result to
arbitrary algebraically closed fields
, see
[
Ge61
, Ch. II, Theorem 1]
. In
[
Ge61
, p. 342]
, Gerstenhaber explains that the irreducibility of
\Commuting_{2}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
was conjectured by Goto, who also observed that
\Commuting_{r}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
has more than one irreducible component when
4\leq r<n
. More recently, Guralnick
[
Gu92
]
proved that
\Commuting_{r}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})

cannot be irreducible

when
n\geq 4
and
r\geq 4
or when
n=3
and
r\geq 32
. The above list of works is a small sample of those discussing irreducibility of

\Commuting_{r}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
and, to the best of our knowledge, the complete list of values
(r,n)
for which
\Commuting_{r}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
is irreducible is still unknown. Although the study of commuting varieties has a classical flavour, it is an active area of research still today with
a number of open questions.
For Lie algebras other than
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, Richardson’s result below (cf.
[
R79
, Corollary 2.5]
) is fundamental and is a key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
C
.

Theorem 2.1

(Richardson)
.

If
\mathrm{char}(\Bbbk)=0
and
\mathfrak{g}
is reductive, then

\Commuting_{2}(\mathfrak{g})
is irreducible.

In the following Lemma, we shall require the notion of semi-simple and nilpotent elements in a semi-simple Lie algebra
[
Hu78
, 5.4 and 6.4]
. Hence, from now on, we

suppose that
\mathrm{char}(\Bbbk)=0
.

For brevity, we let
\mathfrak{g}_{\rm nil}
stand for the subset of nilpotent elements and
\mathfrak{g}_{\rm ss}
for the set of semi-simple ones.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semi-simple Lie algebra over the algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero. If
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
is a subalgebra such that
\mathfrak{g}_{\rm{nil}}\cap\mathfrak{h}=\{0\}
, then
\mathfrak{h}
is abelian.

Proof.

We first prove that
\mathfrak{h}
is solvable by assuming otherwise and arriving at a contradiction.
This being so,
\mathfrak{h}
contains a semi-simple subalgebra
\mathfrak{r}\not=0
(a Levi subalgebra
[
We94
, Theorem 7.8.13]
). If
\mathfrak{b}\subset\mathfrak{r}
is a Borel subalgebra (cf.
[
Hu78
, 16.3]
or
[
BLie
, VIII.3.3]
), then
[\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{b}]\not=0
only contains nilpotent elements of
\mathfrak{r}
. Since
\mathfrak{r}_{\rm nil}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\rm nil}
, we conclude that
[\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{b}]=0
, which forces
\mathfrak{r}
to have no roots. This contradiction shows that
\mathfrak{r}
cannot exist and
\mathfrak{h}
is solvable. Now, let
\mathfrak{b}

be a Borel subalgebra
of
\mathfrak{g}
containing
\mathfrak{h}
; since
[\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{b}]
only contains nilpotent elements we conclude that
[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=0
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\not=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
be a semi-simple Lie algebra over the algebraically closed field of characteristic zero
\Bbbk
. Let
\Sigma
be the set of all points
\mathfrak{h}\in\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
for which
\mathfrak{h}
is abelian. Then
\Sigma
is closed and is an irreducible component of
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

First, as we exclude
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, the set

\Sigma
is non-empty (a maximal toral subagebra will have always dimension at least two).
Adopting the notations employed in eq. (
2.1
),
\Sigma
is the set where the composition

\wedge^{2}\mathcal{U}\longrightarrow\wedge^{2}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(r,\mathfrak{g})}\otimes\mathfrak{g}\right)\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{\Gr(r,\mathfrak{g})}\otimes\mathfrak{g}

vanishes and is hence closed.
Theorem
2.1
implies that
\Sigma
is also irreducible. It remains to show that
\Sigma
is an irreducible component
of
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
, which is done by proving that
\Sigma
contains an open and non-empty subset of
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
. For that, note that
A=\{V\in\mathrm{Gr}(2,\mathfrak{g})\,:\,V\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\rm nil}=0\}
is open because of Bertini’s theorem and the fact that the dimension of the closed algebraic subset
\mathfrak{g}_{\rm nil}
is
\dim\mathfrak{g}-\mathrm{rank}\,\mathfrak{g}

[
Mc02
, Theorem 4.1]
and this is
\leq\dim\mathfrak{g}-2
. Then, employing
Lemma
2.2
, we see that
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})\cap A
is contained in
\Sigma
and is non-empty.
∎

2.3.
Two dimensional non-abelian subalgebras and nilpotent orbits

We keep on assuming that
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed and of characteristic zero and
\mathfrak{g}
is a semi-simple Lie algebra over
\Bbbk
. We now analyse the structure of the non-abelian elements of
\SLie_{2}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}\in\SLie_{2}(\mathfrak{g})
be non-abelian. As is well-known
[
Hu78
, 1.4]
, such an algebra is spanned by
\{e,h\}
satisfying
[h,e]=2e
.
Moreover, as
\mathfrak{g}
is assumed semi-simple, then
\mathfrak{h}
must contain a non-zero nilpotent element
n
of
\mathfrak{g}
(Lemma
2.2
).
Using the nilpotency of
\mathrm{ad}_{n}:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}
, it is not difficult to see that
n\in\Bbbk e
and hence

(2.2)

\Bbbk n=[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=\Bbbk e.

In particular,
e\in\mathfrak{g}_{\rm nil}
and in fact
\mathfrak{g}_{\rm nil}\cap\mathfrak{h}=[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]
.

Let us fix a connected linear algebraic group
G
of adjoint type, i.e. a linear connected linear algebraic group
G
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and such that
\mathrm{Ad}:G\to\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})
is a closed immersion. We observe that
\Bbbk n\setminus\{0\}=[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]\setminus\{0\}
is contained in single
G
-orbit, which we call

\mathrm{Orb}(\mathfrak{h}).

(This is a consequence of
[
CM93
, proof of 4.3.1]
, or of the Jacobson-Morozov Theorem
[
BLie
, VIII.11.2]
plus
[
BLie
, VIII.11.3, Proposition 6]
.)

For the sake of uniformity of notation, for any given
abelian

\mathfrak{a}\in\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
, we let

\mathrm{Orb}(\mathfrak{a})=\text{The orbit \lx@text@lbrace 0\lx@text@rbrace}.

Theorem 2.4

(Theorem
C
of the Introduction)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\not=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
be a semi-simple Lie algebra over
\Bbbk
. The irreducible components of
\mathbf{SLie}(2,\mathfrak{g})
are in bijection with
the set of conjugacy classes of nilpotent elements of
\mathfrak{g}
under the action of
G
.
More precisely, the bijection is obtained from the map

\mathrm{Orb}:\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})\longrightarrow\{\text{Nilpotent orbits}\}

constructed above.

Proof.

Let us abbreviate
\SLie(2,\mathfrak{g})
to
L
and let
L^{\rm na}=\{\mathfrak{h}\in L\,:\,[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]\not=0\}
be the (quasi-projective algebraic) subset of non-abelian subalgebras. We already know that
L\setminus L^{\rm na}
is an irreducible component (Corollary
2.3
) and we are left with showing that
\mathrm{Orb}
establishes a bijection between the irreducible components of
L^{\rm na}
and the non-trivial nilpotent orbits.

Let
N
stand for the set of nilpotent orbits in
\mathfrak{g}\setminus\{0\}
and for each
n\in N
, we pick an element
e_{n}\in n
; note that
e_{n}\not=0
. A fundamental result in the theory says that
N
is
finite
(cf.
[
CM93
, Corollary 3.2.15]
or
[
Mc02
, Theorem 3.11]
).
Define

A_{n}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\,:\,[x,e_{n}]=2e_{n}\};

this is a linear subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
and by the Jacobson-Morozov Theorem (cf.
[
BLie
, VIII.11.2]
or
[
Mc02
, Theorem 3.4]
) it is also non-empty. (Note that
A_{n}
is an affine space over the vector space
Z_{\mathfrak{g}}(e_{n})
.)
Let now

\varphi_{n}:G\times A_{n}\longrightarrow L^{\rm na}

be the map
(g,x)\mapsto\Bbbk g(x)+\Bbbk g(e_{n})
. Note that
\mathrm{Orb}(\varphi_{n}(g,x))=n
.
Then

L^{\rm na}=\bigsqcup_{n\in N}\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{n}).

Indeed, if
\mathfrak{h}\in L^{\rm na}
, then
[\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{h}]=\Bbbk g(e_{n})
(see the discussion around eq. (
2.2
)) for some
g\in G
and
n\in N
. Now there exists
x\in\mathfrak{h}
such that
[x,g(e_{n})]=g(e_{n})
; then
y:=g^{-1}(x)\in A_{n}
and
\mathfrak{h}=\Bbbk g(y)+\Bbbk g(e_{n})\in\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{n})
. Since each
G\times A_{n}
is irreducible, the same is true about the constructible set

B_{n}:=\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{n}).

It takes little effort to see that,

B_{n}=\{\mathfrak{h}\in L^{\rm na}\,:\,\mathrm{Orb}(\mathfrak{h})=n\}.

Let
\Sigma\subset L^{\rm na}
be an irreducible component. From
\Sigma=\cup_{n}\Sigma\cap\overline{B}_{n}
, it follows that

\Sigma=\overline{B}_{\gamma(\Sigma)}
for a certain
\gamma(\Sigma)\in N
.

Consequently, constructibility assures that
B_{\gamma(\Sigma)}
contains an open and dense subset of
\Sigma

[
EGA
,
0_{\rm III}
. 9.2.3]
. In addition, if
\nu\in N\setminus\{\gamma(\Sigma)\}
, then
B_{\nu}
cannot contain an open dense subset of
\Sigma
: otherwise
\Sigma\cap B_{\nu}\cap B_{\gamma(\Sigma)}\not=\varnothing
. In conclusion, there exists
a unique
\gamma(\Sigma)\in N
such that
B_{\gamma(\Sigma)}
contains an open and dense subset of
\Sigma
, and we arrive at a map

\gamma:\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{irreducible }\\
\text{components of $L^{\rm na}$}\end{array}\right\}\longrightarrow N.

We note that
B_{\gamma(\Sigma)}\subset\Sigma
and that

\Sigma
is the
only irreducible component of
L^{\rm na}
containing
it
. This shows as that the map
\gamma
is injective.

To prove surjectivity, it suffices to show that each
B_{n}
contains an open and non-empty subset.
This is the reason for the next result.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
e
be any non-zero nilpotent element of
\mathfrak{g}
in the orbit
n
. Let
(e,h,f)\in\mathfrak{g}
be an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple, i.e.

[h,e]=2e,\quad[h,f]=-2f,\quad[e,f]=h\,.

(The existence is assured by the Jacobson-Morozov theorem
[
BLie
, VIII.11.2]
.) Let
\mathfrak{h}=\Bbbk h+\Bbbk e
. Then there exits a smooth
\Bbbk
-scheme
U
and a smooth morphism
\psi:U\to\mathbf{SLie}(2,\mathfrak{g})
whose image is contained in
B_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{s}=\Bbbk e+\Bbbk f+\Bbbk h
. (Clearly
\mathfrak{s}\simeq\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.)
We will describe the infinitesimal deformations of
\mathfrak{h}
inside
\mathfrak{g}
and show that they are all realized by actual deformations. From Richardson’s description of the tangent spaces in
\mathbf{SLie}(2,\mathfrak{g})
(see Section
2.1
), we are required to study
Z^{1}(\mathfrak{h},\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
.

Let us give
\mathfrak{g}
and
M:=\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
the obvious structures of
\mathfrak{s}
-module and of
\mathfrak{h}
-module. We write
x_{M}
for the endomorphism of
M
obtained from the action of
x\in\mathfrak{h}
on it.
From the standard representation theory of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

[
Hu78
, 7.2]
, we know that

h
acts in a diagonal way on
\mathfrak{g}
having only integer eigenvalues. Since
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
is invariant under
\mathrm{ad}_{h}
, it follows that
h_{M}
acts diagonally on
M
with only integer eigenvalues. Hence, for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
, resp.
M_{i}
, stand for the eigenspace associated to the eigenvalue
i
.

We now define an injective linear map
\delta:M^{\mathfrak{h}}\to Z^{1}(\mathfrak{h},M)
by

\delta_{x}(e)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{x}(h)=x.

We claim that
\delta
induces an isomorphism

M^{\mathfrak{h}}\simeq H^{1}(\mathfrak{h},M).

Since injectivity is straightforward, we move on to verify surjectivity. Let
\varphi\in Z^{1}(\mathfrak{h},M)
be an arbitrary cocyle. If
\varphi(h)=y_{0}+\sum_{i\neq 0}y_{i}
with
y_{i}\in M_{i}
then, after replacing

\varphi
by
\varphi-d(\sum_{i\neq 0}i^{-1}y_{i})
, we can assume that
\varphi(h)\in M_{0}
.
Since

, we have

\displaystyle 2\varphi(e)

\displaystyle=\varphi([h,e])

\displaystyle=h_{M}\left(\varphi(e)\right)-\underbrace{e_{M}\left(\varphi(h)\right)}_{\in M_{2}},

and we deduce that
\varphi(e)\in M_{2}
; indeed, writing
\varphi(e)=\sum x_{i}
with
x_{i}\in M_{i}
, then
\sum(i-2)x_{i}\in M_{2}
, which proves that
x_{i}=0
unless
i=2
. Under this light, equation
2\varphi(e)=h_{M}(\varphi(e))-e_{M}(\varphi(h))
shows that
\varphi(h)
in addition belongs to
\mathrm{Ker}\,e_{M}
, so that
\varphi(h)\in M^{\mathfrak{h}}
.
Since the composition

\mathfrak{g}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ad}_{f}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathfrak{g}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{ad}_{e}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathfrak{g}_{2}

is multiplication by 2, the map
e_{M}:M_{0}\to M_{2}
is surjective. Hence, if

satisfies

, then

and

. It is then clear that

\delta:M^{\mathfrak{h}}\to Z^{1}(\mathfrak{h},M)
induces an isomorphism onto

.

Let
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to M
be the projection. Since

is surjective (with kernel

), there exists a subspace

. This implies that for each

, there exists

and

.

Now, if

, it is not difficult to see that

so that

is a subalgebra. Let

be the morphism
(g,v)\mapsto\Bbbk g(e)+\Bbbk g(h+v)
; if

, then
\mathrm{Orb}(\psi(g,v))
is
Ge
, the orbit of

. Let

be the restriction to

and that

. Also, the differential
\mathrm{D}_{(1_{G},0)}\psi:T_{1}G\times T_{0}V\to T_{\mathfrak{h}}\mathbf{SLie}(2,\mathfrak{g})
is surjective. Since

is also smooth over
\Bbbk
at

, and

. (These last claims are follow from an exercise which we were unable to find explicitly stated in the literature; we move it to Lemma

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let

be a morphism between algebraic
\Bbbk
-schemes and let

be a
\Bbbk
-rational point of

with image

. Assume that

is smooth and that

and

.

Indeed, let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
and

be the complete local rings at

and

respectively, and denote by

the associated map. By hypothesis, the induced

and let

. ∎

∎

We now want place ourselves in the following

Proposition 4.2

Let

4.2.
“Openness” of the locus of local freeness: first case

Let
R
be a discrete valuation ring with uniformizer
t
and field of fractions
K
. We write
R_{n}
for the quotient
R/(t^{n+1})
and, given an
R
-scheme

W_{n}
, respectively
W_{K}
, denote the scheme
W\otimes R_{n}
, respectively
W\otimes_{R}K
.
We assume that
S
is
\Spec R
so that we have a smooth morphism

f:X\longrightarrow\mathrm{Spec}\,R.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
F
be a reflexive coherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module.
Let

Z=\{x\in X\,:\,\text{$F_{x}$ is not free}\}.

Assume that:

(i)

the
\mathcal{O}_{X_{0}}
-module

\left({F}|_{X_{0}}\right)^{\vee\vee}
is locally free and

(ii)

the following inequalities

\mathrm{codim}(Z\cap X_{0},X_{0})\geq 3\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{codim}(Z\cap X_{K},X_{K})\geq 3

hold true.

Then
{F}
is locally free on an open neighbourhood of the special fibre
X_{0}
.

Proof.

There is no loss of generality in assuming that
X=\mathrm{Spec}\,A
with
A
a domain. The proof is structured with a sequence of Lemmas.

The following is a simple consequence of the codimension formula
[
EGA
,
\mathrm{IV}_{2}
, 5.1.3]
; we omit the details.

Lemma 4.4
.

\mathrm{codim}(Z,X)\geq 3
. ∎

Using
[
Ha80
, Proposition 1.1]
, we may assume that
F
fits into an exact sequence
0\to F\to E\to Q\to 0
, where
E
is locally free and
Q
is torsion-free.
We abbreviate
{F}|_{X_{n}}
to
F_{n}
and let
U
be the the open subset
X\setminus Z
. One of the main actors in what follows is the module of “formal functions”
\varprojlim_{n}H^{0}(U_{n},F_{n})
of
F
on
U
.

Lemma 4.5
.

The projection map

\varprojlim_{n}H^{0}(U_{n},F_{n})\longrightarrow H^{0}(U_{0},F_{0})

is surjective.

Proof.

We shall abuse notation and let
F_{n}
stand for the sheaf of abelian groups on
X_{0}
obtained from the fact that
X_{n}
and
X_{0}
underlie the same topological space. From the fact that
F
is free of
t

0\longrightarrow F_{0}\longrightarrow F_{n+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{pr}}}{{\longrightarrow}}F_{n}\longrightarrow 0.

Hence, in order for
H^{0}(U_{n+1},F_{n+1})\to H^{0}(U_{n},F_{n})
to be surjective it is sufficient that
H^{1}(U_{0},F_{0})=0
. Now, since
F
is locally free on
U
, we know that

H^{1}(U_{0},F_{0})\simeq H^{1}(U_{0},F_{0}^{\vee\vee})
. (During this proof, we shall write
F_{0}^{\vee\vee}
instead of
(F_{0})^{\vee\vee}
.) From the long cohomology exact sequence with supports of
F_{0}^{\vee\vee}
and the fact that
X_{0}
is affine, we obtain an isomorphism
H^{1}(U_{0},F^{\vee\vee}_{0})\simeq H^{2}_{Z_{0}}(X_{0},F^{\vee\vee}_{0})
. We can therefore rely on the fact that
F_{0}^{\vee\vee}
is locally free. Indeed, for every
z\in Z_{0}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathrm{depth}\left(F^{\vee\vee}_{0}\right)_{z}&=\mathrm{depth}\,\mathcal{O}_{X_{0},z}\\
&=\dim\mathcal{O}_{X_{0},z}\\
&\geq 3\end{split}

and hence
H^{2}_{Z_{0}}(X_{0},F_{0}^{\vee\vee})=0
, by Lemma 3.1 and Proposition 3.3 of
[
SGA2
, Exp. III]
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

The restriction map

\rho:H^{0}(X_{0},F_{0})\longrightarrow H^{0}(U_{0},F_{0})

is surjective.

Proof.

Let
A^{\prime}
stand for the
t
-adic completion of

and let

. Denoting by

the canonical
flat
morphism, we introduce the following notations:

,
U^{\prime}=X^{\prime}\setminus Z^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime}=h^{*}F
. Let us note that
\mathrm{codim}(Z^{\prime},X^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{codim}(Z,X)\geq 3

[
EGA
,
\mathrm{IV}_{2}
, 2.3.4-5]
.

Step 1.
We wish to show that the natural map

(4.1)

H^{0}(U^{\prime},F^{\prime})\longrightarrow\varprojlim_{n}H^{0}(U_{n},F_{n})

is bijective. For that we will employ Proposition 1.4 of
[
SGA2
, Exp. IX]
.

Let

be such that

. If

, then
\mathrm{codim}(\overline{\{x^{\prime}\}}\cap Z^{\prime},X^{\prime})\leq 2
, which is excluded. Hence,
\dim\mathcal{O}_{X^{\prime},x^{\prime}}=\mathrm{codim}(\overline{\{x^{\prime}\}},X^{\prime})\geq 2
for such

.
Since

is reflexive so is

and hence
\mathrm{depth}\,F^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}\geq 2

[
BH93
, Proposition 1.4.1]
.
In conclusion, by the aforementioned result of
[
SGA2
]
, we are right to affirm that the map in (

) is bijective.

Step 2.

Since
\mathrm{codim}(Z^{\prime},X^{\prime})\geq\mathrm{codim}(Z,X)\geq 3

is reflexive, we know that
\mathrm{depth}\,F^{\prime}_{z^{\prime}}\geq 2
for each

[

, Proposition 1.4.1]
. Then, the map

is an isomorphism because

[
SGA2
, Exp. III]
and Corollary 4 in
[
SGA2
, Exp. II]

\textstyle{H^{0}(X^{\prime},F^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{\varprojlim_{n}H^{0}(X_{n},F_{n})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{H^{0}(X_{0},F_{0})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\textcircled{\tiny 1}}
\textstyle{H^{0}(U^{\prime},F^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

we assure that the map

∎

Lemma 4.7
.

The
\mathcal{O}_{X_{0}}
-module

is reflexive (and hence
F_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}F_{0}^{\vee\vee}
).

Proof.

We wish to apply
[
Ha80
, Proposition 1.3]
(or
[
BH93
, Proposition 1.4.1]
).
Let

be such that

: it is to be shown that
\mathrm{depth}\,F_{0,x}\geq 2
. If
x\in U_{0}
, the we know that

is free, so that
\mathrm{depth}\,F_{0,x}=\mathrm{depth}\,\mathcal{O}_{X_{0},x}\geq 2
. Hence, all difficulty lies in the case where

. We then rely on the cohomological characterization of depth.

Because of Lemma
4.6
and the exact sequence of local cohomology

0\longrightarrow H^{0}(X_{0},F_{0})\longrightarrow H^{0}(U_{0},F_{0})\longrightarrow H^{1}_{Z_{0}}(X_{0},F_{0})\longrightarrow\underbrace{H^{1}(X_{0},F_{0})}_{0}

is torsion-free, we conclude from Proposition 3.3 of
[

, Exp. III]
in conjunction with Corollary 4 of

.
∎

We then finish the proof of the theorem: by Lemma
4.7
, we know that

is a flat
\mathcal{O}_{X_{0}}
-module. Since

is

-flat, the fibre-by-fibre flatness criterion

,
\mathrm{IV}_{3}
, 11.3.10]

is

-flat on each point of

and hence on an open neighbourhood of

. Now, over a local Noetherian ring, each flat module of finite type is actually free
[

, Theorem 7.10, p.51]
.
∎

We now state a variant of Theorem
4.3
in terms more akin to the theory of distributions. The definitions needed to grasp what follows are made in Appendix

.

Let

be a distribution on

, where

is the spectrum of the discrete valuation ring

. Assume that

The pull-back distribution

The inequality

\mathrm{codim}(\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})\cap X_{0},X_{0})\geq 2

The inequalities

It is enough to prove that

is locally free and apply Theorem

.
Indeed,
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is reflexive
[
Ha80

and hypothesis (ii) in the statement of Theorem

Consider the exact sequence of
\mathcal{O}_{X_{0}}
-modules

Letting

{\bf H2}

-(2).
Now, the definition of

The final statement concerning the case where

then

is a closed subset contained in

.
∎

We now end with a simple example showing the necessity of

Example 4.9
.

The notations in the beginning of this section are in force. Let

According to

5.1.
Notations and fundamental results

We set up some notations and terminology which are
in force in Section
5

\Bbbk
is to be algebraically closed and of characteristic zero. We shall work only with reduced algebraic
\Bbbk
-schemes; points are always assumed to be closed.

Let

. (So

[

, 13.5]

The subset of nilpotent, respectively semi-simple, elements in

shall be denoted by

, respectively

.

Given

stand for the centralizer of

in

for the adjoint action.
We let also

stand for the centralizer of

in
\mathfrak{g}

We fix a Borel subgroup

with maximal torus

and that of

Let

be the

of

, 23.3]

Among subvarieties of

, the

have been the centre of much attention. These are defined, once chosen

Let us now state a deep result in the theory on which much of our arguments hinge: Steinberg’s “dimension formula.”

Theorem 5.1

(
[

]
,
[
Mc02
, §7.4]
)
.

Assume that
G
is semi-simple and let

be its rank. Then, for any

, the following formula holds:

\dim Z_{G}(x)=r+2\dim\mathcal{B}_{x}.

∎

As a consequence, we have

\begin{split}2\,\codim(\mathcal{B}_{x},\mathcal{B})&=2\dim U-2\dim\mathcal{B}_{x}\\
&=2\dim U+r-\dim Z_{G}(x)\\
&=\mathrm{codim}\,Z_{G}(x).\end{split}.

(Note in particular that
\codim Z_{G}(x)
is always even.) Since we are in characteristic zero, we know that
\mathrm{Lie}\,Z_{G}(x)=Z_{\mathfrak{g}}(x)

[
Bo91
, Lemma 7.4]
and we shall work with

in place of

. In a nutshell, the convenient formula hods:

(5.1)

\codim(\mathcal{B}_{x},\mathcal{B})=\frac{1}{2}\codim Z_{\mathfrak{g}}(x).

The following shall be useful to us: its simple proof is omitted.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

have Jordan-Chevalley decomposition

∎

[
BH93
, 1.4]
).

ii)

The torsion-free, respectively torsionless, quotient of
M
is the image of
M
in
K\otimes M
, respectively in
M^{\vee\vee}
. These shall respectively be denoted by
M_{\rm{tof}}
and
M_{\rm{tol}}
.

The above definition has a slight imprecision since we did not establish that
M_{\rm{tof}}
, respectively
M_{\rm{tol}}
, is torsion-free, respectively torsion-less. That this is the case for
M_{\rm{tof}}
is simple to verify, while for
M_{\mathrm{tol}}
this is a consequence of:

Proposition A.2

(cf.
[
BV80
, Proposition 16.31]
)
.

A finite module
M
is torsion-less if and only if it is a submodule of a free module of finite rank. ∎

It comes as no surprise that
M\to M_{\mathrm{tof}}
, resp.
M\to M_{\rm{tol}}
, is “universal” with respect to morphisms to torsion-free, resp. torsion-less, modules.
Here is another fundamental property:

Lemma A.3
.

Let
M
be a finite
A
-module. Then the epimorphism
M\to M_{\rm{tol}}
factors through the epimorphism
M\to M_{\rm{tof}}
. In particular, each torsion-less module is also torsion-free.

With the previous results in sight, the following definition is justified.

Definition A.4
.

Let
M
and
F
be
A
-modules and
M\to F
an injection. Then the
strong saturation
of
M
in
F
is

M^{\rm ssat}=\mathrm{Ker}\,F\to(F/M)\to(F/M)_{\rm{tol}}.

The
saturation
is

M^{\rm sat}=\mathrm{Ker}\,F\to(F/M)\to(F/M)_{\rm{tof}}.

Here is a simple working property around the previous definition.

Lemma A.5
.

Let
M
be a submodule of
F
. Then

M^{\rm{ssat}}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in F^{\vee}\\
\lambda|_{M}=0\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Ker}\,\lambda\quad\text{and}\quad M^{\rm{sat}}=\left\{x\in F\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$ax\in M$ for some regular}\\
\text{ element $a\in A$}\end{array}\right\}.\qed

A fundamental result of Vasconcelos allows us to determine when the notions of torsion-free and torsion-less agree thus providing a converse to Lemma
A.3
. This result employs two fundamental properties of Commutative Algebra: Gorenstein rings (cf.
[
Mat89
, §18]
, specially Theorem 18.1) and Serre’s conditions
\mathrm{S}_{n}

[
Mat89
, §23, p.183]
.
The reader is asked to bear in mind the following summary scheme:

\begin{split}\mathrm{S}_{0}&\text{ is always satisfied,}\\
\mathrm{S}_{1}&\Leftrightarrow\text{there are no embedded primes,}\end{split}

\text{$A$ is reduced }\quad\Leftrightarrow\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$A$ is $\mathrm{S}_{1}$ and for}\\
\text{ each minimal $\mathfrak{p}$}\\
\text{the ring $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is a field.}\end{array}

If
(A,\mathfrak{m},k)
is a zero dimensional Noetherian local ring, then

\text{$A$ is Gorenstein}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathrm{Hom}_{A}(k,A)\simeq k.

It is expedient to make the following definition.

Definition A.6

(
[
Ha94
]
)
.

We say that a Noetherian scheme
X
satisfies property
\mathrm{G}_{n}
if for all
x\in X
of codimension
\leq n
, i.e.
\dim\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\leq n
, the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
is Gorenstein. A Noetherian ring satisfies
\mathrm{G}_{n}
if its spectrum satisfies
\mathrm{G}_{n}
.

We can then clearly state Vasconcelos’ result.

Theorem A.7

(
[
Va68
, Theorem A.1]
)
.

The following conditions are equivalent.

i)

Every finite torsion-free
A
-module is torsion-less.

ii)

The ring
A
enjoys
\mathrm{G}_{0}
and
\rm S_{1}
.

In particular, if
A
is reduced, then each torsion-free module is automatically torsion-less and vice-versa.

Remark A.8
.

A fundamental fact which lies at the heart of the previous result is that
all
finite torsionless modules are reflexive over a local Gorenstein ring of dimension at most one
[
Ba63
, Theorem 6.2]
.

We record another simple property
around torsion-less modules. (It is a consequence of the “commutation of flat base-change and duals”
[
Mat89
, Theorem 7.11]
.)

Lemma A.9
.

Let
A\to B
be a flat morphism of noetherian rings and
M
a finite
A
-module. If
M
is torsion-less, then
B\otimes_{A}M
is likewise. ∎

Let us then end this section with some illustrations.

Example A.10

(On the rank)
.

In opposition to the case of integral domains, torsion-less modules do not necessarily have a “reasonable rank”.
Let
P=\mathbb{C}[x,y]
and let
f
be an irreducible polynomial. Define
A=P/(f^{2})
and
I=fA
. Then, for each
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}\,A
, the
A_{\mathfrak{p}}
-module
I_{\mathfrak{p}}
is annihilated by the non-zero element
f/1\in A_{\mathfrak{p}}
. Hence,
I
has “rank zero” although being torsion-less.

Example A.11

(Failure of Lemma
A.3
)
.

Let
A=\mathbb{Q}[x,y]/(x^{2},xy,y^{2})=\mathbb{Q}1\oplus\mathbb{Q}x\oplus\mathbb{Q}y
; this is a local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}=\mathbb{Q}x\oplus\mathbb{Q}y
. Let
M=A/(x)
.
It is not difficult to see that
M
is torsion-free but not torsion-less. Note here that
A

is not Gorenstein
.

A.2.
Orthogonals and strong saturation

We fix a Noetherian ring
A
.
Let
F
be a projective
A
-module of finite rank and
\langle-,-\rangle:F\times F^{\vee}\to A
the canonical pairing. For
M\subset F
, we write
M^{\perp}=\{\theta\in F^{\vee}\,:\,\langle m,\theta\rangle=0,\,\,\forall\,\,m\in M\}\simeq(F/M)^{\vee}
.

Proposition A.12
.

Let
M\subset F
be a submodule as above.

(1)

The natural inclusions
M\subset M^{\mathrm{sat}}\subset M^{\mathrm{ssat}}
induce equalities

(M^{\mathrm{ssat}})^{\perp}=(M^{\mathrm{sat}})^{\perp}=M^{\perp}
.

(2)

The submodule
M^{\perp}
of
F^{\vee}
is strongly saturated.

(3)

The natural monomorphism
u:M\to M^{\perp\perp}
defined by
u_{m}(\varphi):=\langle m,\varphi\rangle
is an isomorphism if and only if
M
is strongly saturated in
F
.

(4)

We have
M^{\perp\perp}=M^{\mathrm{ssat}}
.

Proof.

Item (1)
. Clearly
M^{\perp}\supset(M^{\mathrm{sat}})^{\perp}\supset(M^{\mathrm{ssat}})^{\perp}
. Let now
\theta:F\to A
belong to
M^{\perp}
. By definition,
\theta|_{M}=0
and hence
M^{\rm ssat}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
(Lemma
A.5
), so that
\theta\in(M^{\rm ssat})^{\perp}
.

Item (2)
. By Lemma
A.5
we have

\begin{split}(M^{\perp})^{\rm ssat}&=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in F\\
\langle x,M^{\perp}\rangle=0\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Ker}\langle x,-\rangle.\end{split}

Now
x\in F
is annihilated by each
\lambda\in M^{\perp}
if and only if
x\in M^{\mathrm{ssat}}
by the same Lemma. Hence,
(M^{\perp})^{\mathrm{ssat}}=\bigcap_{x\in M^{\mathrm{ssat}}}\mathrm{Ker}\,\langle x,-\rangle
. But by definition the former intersection is just
(M^{\mathrm{ssat}})^{\perp}
, which equals
M^{\perp}
by (1).

Item (3)
. Consider an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow M\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\longrightarrow}}F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}Q\longrightarrow 0.

Then, we have a commutative diagram with exact rows

\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i}
\scriptstyle{u}
\textstyle{F\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{p}
\textstyle{Q\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{v}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{0\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{M^{\perp\perp}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{F\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(p^{\vee})^{\vee}}
\textstyle{Q^{\vee\vee}}

and from the Snake Lemma, we have

\mathrm{Ker}(u)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Coker}\,u\simeq\mathrm{Ker}\,v.

Hence,
M\to M^{\perp\perp}
is an isomorphism if and only if
\mathrm{Ker}\,v=0
, which is equivalent to
Q
being torsionless.

Item (4)
. Follows from (1) and (3).
∎

A.3.
Torsion in the case of sheaves

Let
X
be a Noetherian scheme with sheaf of meromorphic functions
\mathcal{K}_{X}

[
Kl79
]
.
In analogy with Definition
A.1
-(i), EGA puts forth the following.

Definition A.13
.

An
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}
is
strictly torsion-free

[
EGA
,
\mathrm{IV}_{4}
, 20.1.5]
if the natural map
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{K}_{X}\otimes\mathcal{M}
is injective.

As
X
is Noetherian, we know that
\mathcal{K}_{X,x}
is simply the total ring of fractions of
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}

[
Li02
, Lemma 7.1.12]
and hence
\mathcal{M}
is strictly torsion-free if and only if each
\mathcal{M}_{x}
is torsion-free.
Now, the
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{K}_{X}
is
not necessarily quasi-coherent

[
Kl79
, p.205]
, which renders the above definition unwieldy in general: indeed, in order to construct the “strictly torsion-free quotient of
\mathcal{M}
” we would need to look at the image of
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{X}
. (We note here that if
X
satisfies Serre’s
\mathrm{S}_{1}
property
[
Mat89
, §23, p.183]
, then
\mathcal{K}_{X}

is
quasi-coherent
[
Ha94
, Proposition 2.1(d)]
.)
Therefore, the notion of torsion-less is better suited to this level of generality than that of torsion-free.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a coherent sheaf and
\mathcal{F}
a locally free sheaf of finite rank throughout.

Definition A.14
.

We say that
\mathcal{M}
is
torsion-less
if the natural map
\chi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}^{\vee\vee}
is injective. The
torsion-less quotient
of
\mathcal{M}
, denoted
\mathcal{M}_{\rm{tol}}
, is
\mathrm{Im}(\chi)
.

Needless to say, in the above definition,
\mathrm{M}_{\rm{tol}}
is torsion-less, since it is a submodule of the torsion-less
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}^{\vee\vee}
.

We suppose from now on that
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{F}
.

Definition A.15
.

We define the
strong saturation
of
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathcal{F}
as being the kernel of the composition
\mathcal{F}\to(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{M})\to(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{M})_{\mathrm{tol}}
. Notation:
\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
.

Note that each torsion-less coherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module is automatically strictly torsion-free. Also, observe that, by construction, the strong saturation of
\mathcal{M}
in
\mathcal{F}
is always coherent.

Let us now state Proposition
A.12
in terms of sheaves. Write
\langle-,-\rangle:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}^{\vee}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}
for the canonical pairing and define
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
by
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=\mathrm{Ker}\,\mathcal{F}^{\vee}\to\mathcal{M}^{\vee}
. Clearly, if
U
is an affine open subset of
X
, then
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}(U)=\mathcal{M}(U)^{\perp}
, in the notation of Section
A.2
.
Proposition
A.12
now easily implies the following result.

Proposition A.16
.

(1)

The submodule
\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
of
\mathcal{F}^{\vee}
is strongly saturated.

(2)

The natural monomorphism
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}^{\perp\perp}

is an isomorphism if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is strongly saturated in
\mathcal{F}
.

(3)

We have
\mathcal{M}^{\perp\perp}=\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{ssat}}
.

Let us end this section with simple results which allow us to recognise what kind of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module the saturation shall be. This is particularly useful when
X
is a quasi-normal scheme (see Remark
A.18
) and when
\mathcal{M}
is already strongly saturated on some open subset.

Proposition A.17
.

Let
U\subset X
be a non-empty open subset and write
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
for the canonical extension of
\mathcal{M}|_{U}
inside
\mathcal{F}

[
EGA
, I, 9.4.1-2]
, that is,
\Gamma(V,\overline{\mathcal{M}})=\{s\in\Gamma(V,\mathcal{F})\,:\,s|_{V\cap U}\in\mathcal{M}(V\cap U)\}
.

(1)

Assume that
\mathrm{Ass}(X)\subset U
(which means that
U
is schematically dense) and that
\mathcal{M}|_{U}
is strongly saturated in
\mathcal{F}|_{U}
. Then
\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}=\overline{\mathcal{M}}
.

(2)

Suppose that
X
is quasi-normal, i.e. it satisfies
\mathrm{G}_{1}
and
\mathrm{S}_{2}
. Also, suppose that
U
is big in
X
and that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{M}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}
is strongly saturated in
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{F}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}
. Then the
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}=\mathcal{M}^{\rm{sat}}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{M}^{\vee\vee}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{M}
is reflexive, then
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}=\mathcal{M}^{\rm{sat}}
.

Proof.

Item (1)

Let
j:U\to X
be the inclusion. By definition,
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
is the kernel of the natural map

\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow j_{*}\left({\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{F}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}\right)\longrightarrow j_{*}\left[{\left.\kern-1.2pt(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{M})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}\right],

and hence

\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}\subset\overline{\mathcal{M}}
because
\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
is taken to zero under the above map. Also,

\mathcal{F}/\overline{\mathcal{M}}
is strictly torsion-free (cf. Definition
A.13
).
Indeed,
if
V
is any open subset of
X
and
a\in\mathcal{O}(V)
annihilates
{\left.\kern-1.2pt(\mathcal{F}/\overline{\mathcal{M}})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{V}}
, then
a|_{U\cap V}
annihilates
{\left.\kern-1.2pt(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{M})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{V\cap U}}
which implies that
a=0
since
{\left.\kern-1.2pt(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{M})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}
is strictly torsion-free — each torsion-less is strictly torsion-free — and

V\cap U\supset\mathrm{Ass}(V)
. (Here we are using that
\mathcal{O}_{X}
injects into
j_{*}\mathcal{O}_{U}
.)
Because
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
is torsion-less, it is strictly torsion-free. Consequently,
\overline{\mathcal{M}}/\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
is also strictly torsion-free and hence
[
EGA
,
\mathrm{IV}_{4}
, 20.1.6]

\mathrm{Ass}\left(\overline{\mathcal{M}}/\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}\right)\subset\mathrm{Ass}(X)\subset U.

Since
\overline{\mathcal{M}}/\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
vanishes on all its associated points, it follows that
\overline{\mathcal{M}}/\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
vanishes all-over
[
EGA
,
\mathrm{IV}_{2}
, 3.1.1.]
.

Item (2)
.
We know that
\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
is reflexive
[
Ha94
, Proposition 1.7]
. We then obtain a factorization
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}^{\vee\vee}\to\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
. From the assumption, we get isomorphisms

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{M}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{M}^{\vee\vee}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}.

We then conclude via
[
Ha94
, Theorem 1.12]
that
\mathcal{M}^{\vee\vee}\simeq\mathcal{M}^{\rm{ssat}}
.
∎

Remark A.18
.

Schemes satisfying
\mathrm{G}_{1}
and
\mathrm{S}_{2}
are called quasi-normal schemes in
[
Va68
, Definition 1.2]
.
Warning
: There exists in the literature the notion of “semi-normal”, which seems unrelated.

Appendix B
Generalities on the theory of distributions

Let
f:X\to S
be a smooth morphism of Noetherian schemes. The relative tangent sheaf, which is a vector bundle, is denoted by
T_{f}
in what follows.

Definition B.1
.

A distribution
\mathcal{V}
on
X/S
is a coherent
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-submodule
T_{\mathcal{V}}
of
T_{f}
such that the quotient
T_{f}/T_{\mathcal{V}}
is torsion-less. The sheaf
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is called the
tangent sheaf
of the distribution. The quotient
Q_{\mathcal{V}}=T_{f}/T_{\mathcal{V}}
is called the
quotient sheaf
.

The reader must have noticed that we could have easily abandoned from the notation the symbol
T_{\mathcal{V}}
: we avoided this because when studying restrictions, we want to avoid a notational conflict between the restriction of the distribution and the restriction of the tangent sheaf.

The next definition recalls some standard terminologies and introduces a new convenient one.

Definition B.2

(Singularities of distributions)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a distribution on
X/S
.

(a)

A point
x\in X
is called a
singularity
of the distribution
\mathcal{V}
if
Q_{\mathcal{V},x}
fails to be a free
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
-module. The set of all singularities is denoted by
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
. Similarly, a point which is
not
a singularity is called a
regular point
. The set of all regular points shall be denoted by
\mathrm{Reg}(\mathcal{V})
.

(b)

A point
x\in X
is called a
tangent singularity
, or is said to be
tangent-singular
, if
T_{\mathcal{V},x}
is
not
a free
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
-module. The set of tangent singularities is denoted by
\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})
.

Using standard Commutative Algebra (see for example
[
Mat89
, Theorem 22.5, p.176]
), it is a simple matter to prove the following result.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a distribution defined by the submodule
\alpha:T_{\mathcal{V}}\to T_{f}
. Then

(i)

We have the equality

\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V}):=\left\{x\in X\,:\,\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$\alpha(x):T_{\mathcal{V}}(x)\to T_{f}(x)$}\\
\text{is not injective}\end{array}\right\}.

(ii)

Each tangent-singularity is also a singularity. In symbols:

\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})\subset\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V}).

Lemma
B.3
shows that the term “tangent singularity” is unambiguous.
Notice that, in general, the inclusion envisaged in Lemma
B.3
-(ii) is strict. For instance, if
T_{\mathcal{V}}=\mathcal{O}_{X}\cdot v
for some vector field

v
, then
\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})=\varnothing
while
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
coincides with the zero set of
v
, i.e. the set where
v_{x}\in\mathfrak{m}_{x}T_{X,x}
.

The definition together with Proposition
A.2
show that, locally, a distribution is constructed as follows. Let
U\subset X
be an open subset where
Q_{\mathcal{V}}
can be injected into a free module
\mathcal{O}_{U}^{m}
, cf. Proposition
A.2
. Then, letting
T_{f}|_{U}\to Q_{\mathcal{V}}|_{U}\to\mathcal{O}_{U}^{m}
the composition be defined by relative 1-forms
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}):T_{f}|_{U}\to\mathcal{O}_{U}^{m}
, we conclude that
T_{\mathcal{V}}|_{U}=\cap_{i}\mathrm{Ker}\,(\omega_{i})
.

B.1.
Distributions and twisted exterior forms

Let
f:X\to S
be a smooth morphism of Noetherian schemes. Imposing certain regularity conditions on
S
, there exists a
fundamental and very concise way of expressing a distribution employing a twisted differential form
; the case of codimension one being rather well-known
[
Jo79
]
, while in higher codimensions being due to A. de Medeiros, cf.
[
dM77
, Section 1]
and
[
dM00
, Section 1]
.
The translation of the concept to the associated form serves then as a means to study the “moduli” of distributions.

B.1.1.
From forms to distributions

Definition B.4
.

Let
q\geq 1
be an integer and
L
be an invertible sheaf on
X
. A global section
\omega
of
\Omega_{f}^{q}\otimes L
is called a twisted
q
-form with values on
L
.

We being by explaining how to associate a distribution to certain twisted forms. Let
q
be a positive integer,
L
an invertible sheaf on
X
and
\omega
a twisted
q
-form with values on
L
. The
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-linear map
\wedge^{q}T_{f}\to L
obtained from
\omega
will be denoted likewise.
Let now
\mathrm{Ker}_{\omega}
be the
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-submodule of
T_{f}
defined
by decreeing that on an affine open subset
U
we have:

(B.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ker}_{\omega}(U)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{v\in T_{f}(U)\,:\,\boldsymbol{i}_{v}(\omega)=0\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap_{v_{1},\ldots,v_{q-1}\in T_{f}(U)}\{v\in T_{f}(U)\,:\,\omega(v_{1},\ldots,v_{q-1},v)=0\}.

B.3.
Families of distributions

Let
f:X\to S
be a smooth morphism of Noetherian schemes and, as usual, let
X_{s}
stand for the schematic fibre above
s\in S
. We want to single out certain distributions on
X/S
to be called “families”.

Definition B.24

(Families)
.

A family of singular distributions on
X/S
, is a distribution
\mathcal{V}
on
X/S

such that
for any
s\in S
, the open subset
\mathrm{Reg}(\mathcal{V})\cap X_{s}
is big in
X_{s}
, i.e.

\codim(\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})\cap X_{s}\,,\,X_{s})\geq 2.

Remark B.25
.

One possible way of defining the notion of a family of foliations is to impose that the sheaf
Q_{\mathcal{V}}
be flat over
S

[
CJM22
, p. 2713]
. On the other hand, as
[
Q15
, Section 2]
explains, this imposition turns out to exclude natural cases. Indeed, consider the morphism of affine schemes defined by the projection in the following diagram:

\textstyle{\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{C}[s,t,w,x,y,z]\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{C}[s,t]}
\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{S\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Then, if
\mathcal{V}
is the distribution on
X/S
given as the kernel of

x\,\mathrm{d}x+y\,\mathrm{d}y+sz\,\mathrm{d}z+tw\,\mathrm{d}w,

Quallbrunn shows that
T_{\mathcal{V}}
is not flat over
S
, which ensures that
Q_{\mathcal{V}}
is likewise not flat.

Lemma B.26
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a family of distributions on
X/S
and denote, for each
s\in S
, the pull-back of the distribution
\mathcal{V}
to
X_{s}
by
\mathcal{V}_{s}
. (See Section
B.2
.) The following are true.

(1)

We have
\mathrm{Reg}(\mathcal{V})\supset\mathrm{Ass}(X)
.

(2)

The function
s\mapsto\mathrm{rank}\,Q_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}
is locally constant.

(3)

For each
s\in S
we have

\det(T_{\mathcal{V}_{s}})=\det\left({\left.\kern-1.2ptT_{\mathcal{V}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X_{s}}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\det\left(Q_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}\right)=\det\left({\left.\kern-1.2ptQ_{\mathcal{V}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{X_{s}}}\right).

Proof.

(1) We know that the associated points of
X
are the generic points of the fibres
X_{s}

[
EGA
,
\mathrm{IV}_{2}
, 3.3.1]
, where
s\in\mathrm{Ass}(S)
. No such point can belong to
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
.

(2) We abbreviate
\mathrm{Reg}(\mathcal{V})
to
U
. We know that
{\left.\kern-1.2ptQ_{\mathcal{V}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U}}
is locally free. Write
\sqcup_{\lambda}U(\lambda)
for its decomposition into connected components and let
r_{\lambda}=\mathrm{rank}\,Q_{\mathcal{V}}|_{U(\lambda)}
. Let us denote
U_{s}
, respectively
U(\lambda)_{s}
, for the open subscheme of
X_{s}
defined by
U
, respectively
U(\lambda)
. Now

U_{s}=\sqcup_{\lambda}U{(\lambda)}_{s}
and since
U_{s}
is irreducible, there exists a unique
\lambda_{s}
such that
U(\lambda_{s})\supset U_{s}
. As
f
is an open map,
f(U(\lambda_{s}))
is open and for each
s^{\prime}\in f(U(\lambda_{s}))
we have
U_{s^{\prime}}\subset U(\lambda_{s})
. This means that
Q_{\mathcal{V}}
has rank
r_{\lambda_{s}}
on
f(U(\lambda_{s}))
.

(3) Then
{\left.\kern-1.2ptT_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U_{s}}}\simeq{\left.\kern-1.2ptT_{\mathcal{V}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U_{s}}}
and
{\left.\kern-1.2ptQ_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U_{s}}}\simeq{\left.\kern-1.2ptQ_{\mathcal{V}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{U_{s}}}
. Since
\codim(X_{s}\setminus U_{s},X_{s})\geq 2
, the result follows.
∎

Another useful property of families is:

Lemma B.27
.

Let
\tilde{S}\to S
be a morphism of Noetherian schemes and let
\mathcal{V}
be a family of distributions on
X/S
. Let
\tilde{X}=X\times_{S}\tilde{S}
and let
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
by the pull-back of
\mathcal{V}
to
\tilde{X}
. Then
\tilde{\mathcal{V}}
is a family of distributions on
\tilde{X}
.

Proof.

Letting
\psi:\tilde{X}\to X
stand for the natural projection, we know that
\mathrm{Sing}(\tilde{\mathcal{V}})\subset\psi^{-1}(\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V}))
(Proposition
B.22
-(2)). Hence,
\mathrm{Sing}(\tilde{\mathcal{V}})\cap\tilde{X}_{s}\subset\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})\cap\tilde{X}_{s}
.
∎

Remark B.28
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a family of distributions with regular set
R
. Then, for
s\in S
, we have
Q_{\mathcal{V}_{s}}|_{R_{s}}=Q_{\mathcal{V}}|_{R_{s}}
. Now, we know that
Q_{\mathcal{V}}|_{R}
is locally free, or rank

say, and hence
Q_{\mathcal{V}}|_{R_{s}}
is locally free of rank

B.4.
Kupka singularities

A very important type of singularity for a foliation defined by a differential form was unveiled by I. Kupka and A. de Medeiros and now bears Kupka’s name. In order not to change too much the context of this paper, let us remain in the complex analytic world, although
definitions can be made in the differentiable context.

Let
M
be an
n
-dimensional complex manifold and
\omega
a holomorphic
q
-form on
M
. A point
x\in M
where
\omega(x)=0
is called a Kupka singularity when
\mathrm{d}\omega(x)\not=0
.
The relevance of this definition comes from the following result, proved by I. Kupka in the case
q=1

[
Ku64
]
and A. Medeiros in general
[
dM00
, Proposition 1.3.1]
.

Theorem B.29
.

Suppose that
x\in M
is a Kupka singularity of the
integrable

q
-form
\omega
.

(1)

There exists a local system of coordinates
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
on an open neighbourhod
U
of
x
such that
\omega|_{U}
admits a “reduction to
q+1
variables”, viz.

\omega|_{U}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,q+1\}\\
\#I=q\end{subarray}}a_{I}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{q+1})\,dz_{I}.

(2)

There exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
x
, a local chart
(\varphi,\psi):U\to V\times W
, where
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{q+1}
and
W\subset\mathbb{C}^{n-q-1}
are open sets, and a holomorphic
q
-form
\zeta\in\Omega^{q}(V)
such that
\omega|_{U}=\varphi^{*}\zeta
.

(3)

The tangent sheaf of the foliation
\mathcal{Z}(\omega)
is free at
x
.

We note that conclusion (
3
)
in Theorem
B.29
is certainly weaker than the others, as the latter give a clear structure result for forms. Our goal now is to recuperate, in a completely algebraic setting, condition (
3
) of Theorem
B.29
.

Let
f:X\to S
be a smooth morphism of Noetherian schemes; assume that
X
is integral and locally factorial, i.e. we are in the setting of Section
B.1
.
Let
q
be a positive integer and
L

an invertible sheaf on
X
. We give ourselves a twisted
q
-form
\omega
with coefficients in
L
and denote by
\mathcal{V}
the associated distribution, i.e.
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{Z}(\omega)
(cf. Section
B.1.1
).
Let now
x\in\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
and write
L_{{}_{x}}=\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\ell
. Then

\omega=\xi\otimes\ell
with
\xi\in\Omega^{q}_{f,x}
, and since
x\in\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
, we have
\xi(x)=0
. It is a simple matter to prove that, if
\ell^{\prime}\in L_{\mathcal{V},_{x}}
is another generator, so that
\omega_{x}=\xi^{\prime}\otimes\ell
with
\xi^{\prime}\in\Omega_{f,x}^{q}
, then

\mathrm{d}\xi(x)=0\quad\text{ if and only if}\quad\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}(x)=0.

(Here the differential is the relative differential.) Consequently, the following definition makes perfect sense:

Definition B.30
.

A point
x\in\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
is a Kupka singularity or a Kupka point, if, in the above notation,
\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\not=0
. The set of Kupka points in
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
shall be denoted by
\mathrm{Kup}(\mathcal{V})
. (Note that
\mathrm{Kup}(\mathcal{V})
is open in
\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{V})
.) For expediency, we shall also let
{\rm NKup}(\mathcal{V})
denote the set
\sing(\mathcal{V})\setminus\mathrm{Kup}(\mathcal{V})
; this is the set of non-Kupka points.

Proposition B.31
.

Assume that
X
is locally factorial and

\omega
is
integrable
. Then, for each
x\in\mathrm{Kup}(\mathcal{V})
, the
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
-module
T_{\mathcal{V},x}
is free. Said differently,
\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})\subset{\rm NKup}(\mathcal{V})
.

Proof.

Let
\eta
be the generic point of
X
.
Let
U
be an open neighbourhood of
x
where
L|_{U}=\mathcal{O}_{U}\cdot\ell
and write
\omega=\xi\otimes\ell
. Let now
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{q}
be rational 1-forms such that
\xi_{\eta}=\pi_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\pi_{q}
. Then
\mathrm{d}\xi_{\eta}=\sum_{j}\pm\pi_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm{d}\pi_{j}\wedge\cdots\wedge\pi_{q}
. Now
\mathrm{d}\pi_{j}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{ij}\wedge\pi_{i}
with
\alpha_{ij}\in\Omega^{1}_{f,\eta}
due to integrability of
\omega
. Consequently,
\mathrm{d}\xi_{\eta}=\sum_{j}\pm\alpha_{jj}\wedge\pi_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\pi_{q}
is decomposable. Now, we consider the distribution on
U
defined by
\mathrm{d}\xi
. From
\mathrm{d}\xi(x)\not=0
, we know that
x\in\mathrm{Reg}(\mathcal{Z}(\mathrm{d}\xi))
. The Proposition is then a consequence of Proposition
B.32
below.

∎

Proposition B.32
.

We assume that
X
is a locally factorial integral scheme. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a distribution of codimension
q
. Assume that
\mathcal{V}
contains a distribution
\mathcal{W}
of codimension
q+1
. Then,
\mathrm{TSing}(\mathcal{V})\subset\mathrm{Sing}(\mathcal{W})
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathrm{Reg}(\mathcal{W})
, so that
T_{\mathcal{W},x}
and
T_{f,x}/T_{\mathcal{W},x}
are free
\mathcal{O}_{X,x}
-modules of finite rank. From the exact sequence
0\to T_{\mathcal{V},x}/T_{\mathcal{W},x}\to T_{f,x}/T_{\mathcal{W},x}\to T_{f,x}/T_{\mathcal{V},x}\to 0
we conclude that
T_{\mathcal{V},x}/T_{\mathcal{W},x}
is reflexive
[
Ha80
, Proposition 1.1]
. The hypothesis on the rank implies that
T_{\mathcal{V},x}/T_{\mathcal{W},x}
is free
[
Ha80
, Proposition 1.9]
. Hence
0\to T_{\mathcal{W},x}\to T_{\mathcal{V},x}\to T_{\mathcal{V},x}/T_{\mathcal{W},x}\to 0
splits on the right and
T_{\mathcal{V},x}
is free.
∎
