The massless single off-shell scalar box integral — branch cut structure and all-order epsilon expansion arXiv: 2211.14110
Juliane Haug, and Fabian Wunder

Abstract
We investigate the single off-shell scalar box integral with massless internal lines in dimensional regularization. A special emphasis is given to higher orders in the dimensional regularization parameter epsilon, its branch cut structure, and kinematic limits. Common representations of the box integral introduce superficial branch cuts, which we eliminate to all orders in the epsilon expansion. In the literature so far a satisfactory solution for this issue only exists up to finite order in the epsilon expansion. However, for certain calculations at NNLO in perturbation theory, higher orders in epsilon of this integral are required. In this paper, we present results to all orders in epsilon in terms of single-valued polylogarithms and explicitly determine the real and imaginary part of the box integral in all kinematic regions.

Introduction

With the upcoming electron-ion collider (EIC)
(
Accardi:2012qut
)
, it is essential to evaluate the hard-scattering coefficients for semi-inclusive deep-inelastic scattering (SIDIS) at next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO) in QCD
(
Anderle:2016kwa
;
Abele:2021nyo
;
Borsa:2022vvp
;
Abele:2022wuy
)
. One type of contributions arising at NNLO are the one-loop corrections to the next-to-leading-order processes of real gluon emission and photon-gluon fusion. One-loop integrals such as these arise in virtually all higher-order perturbative calculations and were thoroughly studied in the past 40 years
(
tHooft1979
;
vanNeerven1984
;
vanOldenborgh1990
;
Beenakker1990
;
Denner1991
;
vanOldenborgh1991
;
Hahn1999
;
Fleischer2003
;
Patel2015
;
Carrazza2016
;
FabiValery
)
. Given that tensorial loop integrals can be systematically reduced to scalar integrals using the Passarino-Veltman technique
(
PassarinoVeltman
)
, it is sufficient to evaluate scalar integrals. The case of massless propagators is of particular interest for perturbative QCD calculations, since light quark masses can be neglected in high-energy scattering processes and gluons are massless. Loop integrals with vanishing internal masses generally have soft and collinear divergences, which must be regularized. The most commonly used method is dimensional regularization, which simultaneously serves to regularize ultraviolet and infrared divergences
(
Bollini:1972ui
;
tHooft:1973mfk
;
Gastmans:1973uv
;
Leibbrandt:1975dj
)
. We will work in
d=4-2\varepsilon
dimensions.

Both real and imaginary part of a loop integral do in general yield contributions to the absolute value squared of a matrix element, hence we must know both. The imaginary part of a loop integral arises in certain kinematic regions and will be governed by the causal
+\mathrm{i}0
from the propagators. For massless two- and three-propagator scalar loop integrals in general dimensions
d
, it is easy to keep track of this infinitesimal imaginary part.
1
1

1

By dimensional analysis, massless one-propagator tadpole integrals must vanish since they depend on no mass scale.
Following the calculation given in ref.
(
FabiValery
)
, one finds that the external momentum invariants are essentially replaced by
(p_{i}\pm p_{j})^{2}\rightarrow(p_{i}\pm p_{j})^{2}+\mathrm{i}0
in the final results. This simple rule yields no unambiguously well-defined imaginary part for the four-propagator box integral in general dimension
d
, as we will discuss in section
2
below.

The scalar box integral with one off-shell external particle (i.e. with non-vanishing momentum squared) is especially important since it is needed for higher order (NNLO) perturbative calculations of processes such as SIDIS, Drell-Yan, or single inclusive annihilation (SIA), which all feature a single off-shell gauge boson. In ref.
(
FabriciusSchmitt
)
, this integral and its imaginary part were first given up to finite order
\varepsilon^{0}
in dimensional regularization. The result for general dimensions
d
written in terms of three Gauss hypergeometric functions was obtained in refs.
(
Matsuura1989
;
BernDixonKosower1
)
. However, the simple prescription
(p_{i}\pm p_{j})^{2}\rightarrow(p_{i}\pm p_{j})^{2}+\mathrm{i}0
given in ref.
(
BernDixonKosower1
)
for analytic continuation of the result to all kinematic regions only applies to the expansion up to finite order. Ref.
(
Matsuura1989
, eq. (D.4))
, which was used in the calculation of DIS structure functions at NNLO
(
Zijlstra1992
)
, does not touch on the question of analytic continuation. In
(
FabiValery
)
, the box integral was expanded to all orders in
\varepsilon
, however the branch cut structure was also not discussed. The causal
+\mathrm{i}0
from the propagators was systematically kept in ref.
(
DuplancicNizic
)
, but the result was only given up to finite order in dimensional regularization. To regularize divergences occurring in kinematic limits, however, we need the full
\varepsilon
-dependence of any terms divergent in these kinematic limits.

In this paper, we independently recalculate the massless single off-shell scalar box integral for general dimensions
d
. We explicitly keep the causal
+\mathrm{i}0
from the propagators, such that our results are valid for arbitrary (real) values of the kinematic variables. This allows us to explicitly determine real and imaginary part to all orders of the
\varepsilon
-expansion, which goes beyond the finite
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})
results of refs.
(
BernDixonKosower1
;
DuplancicNizic
)
and the
Mathematica
implementation in
Package
-
X

(
Patel2015
)
. In section
2

As a limiting case our result also includes the internal-massless box integral with on-shell massless external particles, which is needed for direct photon production and Compton-like scattering processes. In section
3
, we perform the
\varepsilon
-expansion to all orders and the explicit determination of real and imaginary parts. The special cases of vanishing Mandelstam variables, as well as several identities for the Gauss hypergeometric function, and the definition of
single-valued polylogarithms
, are collected in the appendices. Additionally, we provide an example illustrating the need of higher orders in
\varepsilon

.

Calculating the massless single off-shell scalar box integral

The massless scalar box diagram with one off-shell external particle carrying momentum

The massless single off-shell scalar box integral is defined through the loop integral

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{0}\,\equiv\,\frac{\mu^{4-d}}{\mathrm{i}\pi^{d/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}l\,\frac{1}{\left[l^{2}+\mathrm{i}0\right]\left[(l+p_{2})^{2}+\mathrm{i}0\right]\left[(l+p_{2}+p_{3})^{2}+\mathrm{i}0\right]\left[(l-p_{1})^{2}+\mathrm{i}0\right]}\,,

(1)

where the external momenta are labelled as indicated by the Feynman diagram depicted in figure
1
. It is
p_{1}^{2}=p_{2}^{2}=p_{3}^{2}=0
and
q^{2}=(p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3})^{2}\neq 0
. Note that we take all external momenta to be incoming. We have also explicitly kept the causal
+\mathrm{i}0
in the propagators, which is necessary to determine the physical side of the branch cuts that appear in the final result.
2
2

2

Here,
\mathrm{i}0
, with
0
taken to be positive, is an infinitesimal imaginary part indicating on which side of the branch cut a multivalued function should be evaluated.

The prefactor is chosen as in ref.
(
Ellis
)
.

To evaluate this loop integral, we first combine the propagators into one generalized propagator of higher power using the well-known Feynman parametrization
(
Peskin1995
, eq. (A.39))

\frac{1}{abcd}\,=\,6\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{3}\,\mathrm{d}x_{4}\,\frac{\delta(1-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}-x_{4})}{\left[ax_{1}\,+\,bx_{2}\,+\,cx_{3}\,+\,dx_{4}\right]^{4}}\,.

(2)

Next, we complete the square with respect to
l
and then shift the loop momentum in the usual manner, which leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{0}\,

\displaystyle=\,\mu^{4-d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{3}\,\mathrm{d}x_{4}\,\delta(1-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}-x_{4})

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}\,\times\,\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\pi^{d/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}l^{\prime}\frac{6}{\left[l^{\prime 2}+s_{1}x_{1}(x_{2}+x_{3})+s_{2}x_{3}(x_{1}+x_{4})+s_{3}x_{1}x_{3}+\mathrm{i}0\right]^{4}}\,,

(3)

where we introduced the Mandelstam variables

\displaystyle s_{1}\,

\displaystyle=\,(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}\,,

(4)

\displaystyle s_{2}\,

\displaystyle=\,(p_{2}+p_{3})^{2}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle s_{3}\,

\displaystyle=\,(p_{1}+p_{3})^{2}\,.

(6)

In the following, we treat all Mandelstam variables as real, but otherwise arbitrary.

Evaluating the loop integral on the second line of eq. (
3
) with the help of
(
Peskin1995
, eq. (A.44))

\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\pi^{d/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}l\,\frac{1}{\left[l^{2}-\Delta+\mathrm{i}0\right]^{\alpha}}\,=\,(-1)^{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha-d/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}(\Delta-\mathrm{i}0)^{d/2-\alpha}

(7)

results in

\displaystyle D_{0}\,

\displaystyle=\,\mu^{2\varepsilon}\,\Gamma(2+\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}x_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x_{3}\,\mathrm{d}x_{4}\,\delta(1-x_{1}-x_{2}-x_{3}-x_{4})}{\left[-s_{1}x_{1}(x_{2}+x_{3})-s_{2}x_{3}(x_{1}+x_{4})-s_{3}x_{1}x_{3}-\mathrm{i}0\right]^{2+\varepsilon}}\,.

(8)

Note that this expression is symmetric under exchanging
s_{1}\leftrightarrow s_{2}
. To reflect this, we write the scalar box integral as
D_{0}(s_{1},s_{2},q^{2})
, where
q^{2}=s_{1}+s_{2}+s_{3}
is the squared momentum of the off-shell external particle. To decouple the Feynman parameter integrals, which are currently coupled via the delta function, we use the substitution

\displaystyle x_{1}\,

\displaystyle=\,\eta_{1}\xi_{1}\,,\quad x_{4}\,=\,\eta_{1}(1-\xi_{1})\,,

(9)

\displaystyle x_{3}\,

\displaystyle=\,\eta_{2}\xi_{2}\,,\quad x_{2}\,=\,\eta_{2}(1-\xi_{2})\,.

(10)

The Jacobian of this substitution, which was inspired by ref.
(
Smirnov_Feynman_Integrals
, page 44)
, is
\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
. Through the substitution, the
\eta
- and
\xi
-integrals factorize,

\displaystyle D_{0}\!\left(s_{1},s_{2},q^{2}\right)\,

\displaystyle=\,\mu^{2\varepsilon}\,\Gamma(2+\varepsilon)\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\eta_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\eta_{2}\;\eta_{1}^{-\varepsilon-1}\,\eta_{2}^{-\varepsilon-1}\,\delta(1-\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}\,\times\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\,\left[-s_{1}\xi_{1}-s_{2}\xi_{2}-s_{3}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}-\mathrm{i}0\right]^{-\varepsilon-2}

\displaystyle=\,\mu^{2\varepsilon}\,\frac{\Gamma(2+\varepsilon)\Gamma^{2}(-\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(-2\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\,\left[-s_{1}\xi_{1}-s_{2}\xi_{2}-s_{3}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}-\mathrm{i}0\right]^{-\varepsilon-2}\,.

(11)

Here, the delta function was used to evaluate one of the
\eta
-integrals. The remaining
\eta
-integral
thus became a representation of the Beta function, which was evaluated in terms of Gamma functions,

B(a,b)\,\equiv\,\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,=\,\frac{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\,.

(12)

For the resulting integral to safely converge, we must take
\varepsilon<0
.
3
3

3

In the special cases of one or two (or three) vanishing Mandelstam variables
\varepsilon<-1

(\varepsilon<-2)
is required.

We will first evaluate the integral in this area of convergence and subsequently analytically continue the end result to larger
\varepsilon
.

So far, our calculation did not differ much from the way the scalar box integral was calculated before (see e. g. ref.
(
DuplancicNizic
)
) and was mainly presented for pedagogical reasons and introduction of notation. We will now deviate from the usual path by factoring the term
\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{s_{3}}
out of the integrand. This is of course not sensible for
{\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{s_{3}}=0,\pm\infty}
, that is
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}=0
. The special cases of one or several vanishing Mandelstam variables will be considered in appendix
A
. Using the identity (compare
(
DuplancicNizic
, eq. (19))
)

\displaystyle(a-\mathrm{i}0)^{\alpha}\,=\,(b-\mathrm{i}0)^{\alpha}\left(\frac{a}{b}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(b\right)\right)^{\alpha},\quad\text{where }a\in\mathbb{R}\,,\;b\in\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!\{0\}\,,\;\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(13)

which is not completely trivial since
\alpha
is not an integer, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{0}\!\left(s_{1},s_{2},q^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\mu^{2\varepsilon}\,\frac{\Gamma(2+\varepsilon)\Gamma^{2}(-\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(-2\varepsilon)}\left(\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{s_{3}}-\mathrm{i}0\right)^{\!-\varepsilon-2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{=}\times\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{2}\,\left[-\frac{s_{3}}{s_{2}}\xi_{1}-\frac{s_{3}}{s_{1}}\xi_{2}-\frac{s_{3}^{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\frac{s_{3}}{s_{1}s_{2}}\right)\right]^{-\varepsilon-2}\,.

(14)

The
\mathrm{i}0
prescription determines on which side of the branch cut the respective factors should be evaluated if their argument is negative. The remaining integral on the second line only depends on the two dimensionless variables

x_{1}\,\equiv\,-\frac{s_{3}}{s_{1}}\,,\quad x_{2}\,\equiv\,-\frac{s_{3}}{s_{2}}\,,

(15)

which is why factoring the term
\frac{s_{1}s_{2}}{s_{3}}
out of the integrand is advantageous. By substituting
\xi_{1}\rightarrow\zeta_{2}=x_{2}\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}\rightarrow\zeta_{1}=x_{1}\xi_{2}
,
all dependence on the dimensionless
x_{i}
is moved into the integration boundaries,

\displaystyle D_{0}\!\left(s_{1},s_{2},q^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\Gamma(2+\varepsilon)\Gamma^{2}(-\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(-2\varepsilon)}\,\frac{1}{s_{1}s_{2}}\left(\frac{s_{3}\mu^{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}}+\mathrm{i}0\right)^{\!\varepsilon}I_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad I_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})\,

\displaystyle\equiv\,\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\int_{0}^{x_{2}}\mathrm{d}\zeta_{2}\,\left[1-(1-\zeta_{1})(1-\zeta_{2})-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-2}\,.

(17)

Note that we inverted the prefactor using

(a\pm\mathrm{i}0)^{\alpha}\,=\,\left(\frac{1}{a}\mp\mathrm{i}0\right)^{\!-\alpha},\quad\text{where }a\in\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!\{0\}\,.

(18)

Additionally, we introduced the abbreviation

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\,\equiv\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\frac{s_{3}}{s_{1}s_{2}}\right).

(19)

And furthermore, we have written the integrand in a form suggesting the substitution
\zeta_{i}\rightarrow 1-\zeta_{i}
. Subsequently substituting
\zeta_{2}\rightarrow\zeta_{12}=1-\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}
allows us to evaluate the resulting
\zeta_{12}
integral,

\displaystyle I_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})\,=\,\int_{1}^{1-x_{1}}\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\int_{1}^{1-x_{2}}\mathrm{d}\zeta_{2}\left[1-\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-2}

\displaystyle=\,-\int_{1}^{1-x_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}}{\zeta_{1}}\int_{1-\zeta_{1}}^{1-\zeta_{1}(1-x_{2})}\mathrm{d}\zeta_{12}\left[\zeta_{12}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-2}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\int_{1}^{1-x_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}}{\zeta_{1}}\left\{\left[1-\zeta_{1}(1-x_{2})-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-\left[1-\zeta_{1}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}\right\}.

(20)

While this integrand is finite at
\zeta_{1}=0
, we must regularize the divergence at
\zeta_{1}=0
if we split the integral into two separate integrals. For this, we subtract and add
1
and subsequently substitute
\zeta_{1}\rightarrow\zeta=1-\zeta_{1}(1-x_{2})
in the first integral and
\zeta_{1}\rightarrow\zeta=1-\zeta_{1}
in the second integral,

\displaystyle I_{12}(x_{1},x_{2})\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\left\{\int_{1}^{1-x_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}}{\zeta_{1}}\left(\left[1-\zeta_{1}(1-x_{2})-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-\,1\right)\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{=\,\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\{}\left.-\int_{1}^{1-x_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}}{\zeta_{1}}\left(\left[1-\zeta_{1}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-\,1\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\left\{-\int_{x_{2}}^{1-(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(\left[\zeta-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-\,1\right)\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{=\,\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\{}\left.+\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(\left[\zeta-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-1\right)\right\}.

(21)

Splitting the first of these integrals in two, we find that the scalar box integral is given by

\displaystyle D_{0}\!\left(s_{1},s_{2},q^{2}\right)=\,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\frac{\Gamma(1+\varepsilon)\,\Gamma^{2}(1-\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}\,\frac{2}{s_{1}s_{2}}\left(\frac{s_{3}\mu^{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}}+\mathrm{i}0\right)^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\times\left\{\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(\left[\zeta-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-1\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hphantom{\times\lx@text@lbrace}\;\;+\int_{0}^{x_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(\left[\zeta-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-1\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\times\lx@text@lbrace}\;\;\left.-\int_{0}^{1-(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(\left[\zeta-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-1\right)\right\}.

(22)

Note that we have rewritten the Gamma functions in the prefactor such that they become unity in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, using
{\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)}
.

Since the result for the internal-massless single off-shell scalar box integral is commonly given in terms of Gauss hypergeometric functions, we will identify them here, even though this will introduce spurious additional branch cuts. The three integrals appearing in eq. (
22
) are of the form

I(\chi)\,\equiv\,\int_{0}^{\chi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left(\left[\zeta-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon-1}-1\right).

(23)

This is trivially zero if
\chi=0
. All three integrals have vanishing
\chi
for
s_{3}=0
. As mentioned above, this special case of vanishing Mandelstam variables will be considered separately in appendix
A
. The upper boundary of the third integral,

1-(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})\,=\,-\frac{s_{3}q^{2}}{s_{1}s_{2}}\,,

(24)

also vanishes if
q^{2}=0
. We will account for this by setting the third integral to zero in case
q^{2}=0
. In case
\chi\neq 0
, we can substitute
\zeta\rightarrow\chi^{-1}\zeta
,

\displaystyle I(\chi)\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\zeta}{1-\chi\zeta}\left(\left[\chi-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon}\zeta^{-\varepsilon-1}-\chi\right).

(25)

As
\zeta>0
, we could factor it out of the square brackets here. To split this integral into two separate integrals, we must regularize the divergence that occurs for
\zeta\,=\,1/\chi
if
\chi>1
. We regulate it by introducing an infinitesimal imaginary part
\chi\rightarrow\chi+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}
in the denominator, with
\tilde{0}
taken to be positive.
4
4

4

Here, we write
+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}
as an abbreviation for
\mathrm{i}\tilde{\eta}
and taking the limit
\tilde{\eta}\rightarrow+0
. This slightly unusual notation is chosen in analogy to the causal
+\mathrm{i}0
originating from the propagators, where
+\mathrm{i}0
is a common short hand notation for
\mathrm{i}\eta
and taking the limit
\eta\rightarrow 0
. The tilde marks the new regulator as an analogous but independent limiting process. The distinction between
\mathrm{i}0
and
\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}
allows us to explicitly check that the end result does not depend on the regulator introduced to split
I(\chi)
into two integrals.

Note that the end result will not depend on the regulator
\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}
, since the original integral
I(\chi)
was not divergent for
\zeta\,=\,1/\chi
.
Proceeding as described, we obtain

\displaystyle I(\chi)\,

\displaystyle=\left[\chi-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\zeta^{-\varepsilon-1}\left(1-(\chi+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0})\zeta\right)^{-1}-\,\chi\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}\zeta\,\frac{1}{1-(\chi+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0})\zeta}

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\chi-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;\chi+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}\right)+\,\ln(1-\chi-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0})\,.

(26)

Here, the first integral became a representation of the Gauss hypergeometric function discussed in appendix
B
(see eq. (
77
)), while the second integral could be elementarily evaluated in terms of a logarithm. Both hypergeometric function and logarithm are evaluated on the branch cut for
\chi>1
. The regulator
\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}
tells us which side of these branch cuts to choose. Also note that the prefactor of the hypergeometric function vanishes in case
\chi=0
, since we assumed
\varepsilon<0
for

to converge.

Applying eq. (
26
) to the term in curly braces in eq. (

) and using a different regulator
\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{i}
for each of the three integrals gives

\displaystyle-\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[x_{1}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;x_{1}+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle-\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[x_{2}-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;x_{2}+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[1-(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right]^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;1-(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})+\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{3}\right)

\displaystyle+\,\ln(1-x_{1}-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{1})\,+\,\ln(1-x_{2}-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{2})\,-\,\ln\!\left((1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{3}\right).

(27)

Writing the logarithms on the last line of this expression in terms of their real and imaginary parts, we see that only the imaginary parts yield a contribution,

\displaystyle\ln(1-x_{1}-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{1})\,+\,\ln(1-x_{2}-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{2})\,-\,\ln\!\left((1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})-\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\mathrm{i}\pi\left[-\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\tilde{0}_{1}\right)\Theta(x_{1}-1)\,-\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\tilde{0}_{2}\right)\,\Theta(x_{2}-1)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\hphantom{=\,\mathrm{i}\pi}\,+\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\tilde{0}_{3}\right)\left\{\Theta(x_{1}-1)\,\Theta(1-x_{2})\,+\,\Theta(1-x_{1})\,\Theta(x_{2}-1)\right\}\right].

(28)

Here,
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. Let us now choose the sign of the regulators
\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}_{i}
such that the contribution of the logarithms completely cancels. The kinematic regions in the

-plane in which the respective regulators yield a contribution are depicted in figure

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\tilde{0}_{1}\right)\,

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\tilde{0}_{3}\right)\quad\text{if }x_{1}>1\text{ and }x_{2}<1\text{ (yellow-green region)}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\tilde{0}_{1}\right)\,

3.1
Epsilon expansion of
\mathcal{F}_{\pm}(\varepsilon;x)

To perform the
\varepsilon
-expansion of

, which was defined in eq. (
38

\left(x-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right)^{-\varepsilon}
develops an imaginary part for

, while the hypergeometric function does so for

. Hence, there are 3 regions of interest:

1.

x\in(-\infty,0)
, where we need to expand
\left(x-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right)^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon,x\right)

2.

x^{-\varepsilon}\,{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon,x\right)
,

3.

, where we need to expand

3.1.1
Expansion for
x\in(-\infty,0)

For
x<0
,
\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!
reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,

\displaystyle=\left(x-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\right)^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon,x\right)

\displaystyle=\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}}|x|^{-\varepsilon}\left(1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}}\left[|x|^{-\varepsilon}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{k}}{k!}\ln^{k}|x|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\right],

(40)

where the
\varepsilon
-expansion of the hypergeometric function given in eq. (
78
) was used in the first step. Next, we substitute
n\rightarrow N=n+k
and then interchange the
N
- and
k
-sum,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,

\displaystyle=\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}}\left[|x|^{-\varepsilon}-\sum_{N=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}\ln^{k}|x|}{k!}\,\mathrm{Li}_{N-k}\!\left(x\right)\right].

(41)

For
N\geq 2
, we now introduce the
single-valued polylogarithms

\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\,
discussed in appendix
C
by adding and subtracting
\frac{(-1)^{N-1}}{N!}\ln^{N-1}|x|\ln|1-x|
in the sum. This yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,

\displaystyle=\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}}\left[\vphantom{\frac{(-1)^{N-1}}{N!}}|x|^{-\varepsilon}\,-\,\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Li}_{1}\!\left(x\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\left.\,-\,\sum_{N=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{N}\left(\mathcal{L}_{N}\!\left(x\right)\,-\,\frac{(-1)^{N-1}}{N!}\ln^{N-1}|x|\ln|1-x|\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}}\left[1\,+\,\ln\frac{x}{x-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\right].

(42)

Note that since the single-valued polylogarithms are bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, any divergences in the kinematic variable
x
are explicitly contained in the logarithms.

3.1.2
Expansion for
x\in(0,1)

In the area where
0<x<1
, neither
(x-\mathrm{i}0\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123})^{-\varepsilon}
nor
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;x\pm\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}\right)
develop an imaginary part. Following the same steps as before, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,

\displaystyle=\,x^{-\varepsilon}\,{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon,x\right)

\displaystyle=\,|x|^{-\varepsilon}\left(1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\,|x|^{-\varepsilon}\,-\,\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Li}_{1}\!\left(x\right)\,-\,\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\left(\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\,-\,\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|\ln|1-x|\right)

\displaystyle=1+\ln\frac{x}{1-x}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right).

(43)

Again, any divergences in the kinematic variable are contained in the logarithms since the single-valued polylogarithms are bounded on
\mathbb{R}
.

3.1.3
Expansion for
x\in(1,\infty)

In the area where
x>1
, the hypergeometric function
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon,x\pm\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}\right)
develops an imaginary part. To make it explicit, we employ the inversion formula given in eq. (
86
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,

\displaystyle=\,x^{-\varepsilon}{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon,x\pm\mathrm{i}\tilde{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\,|x|^{-\varepsilon}\left[-{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon;\frac{1}{x}\right)\,+\,1\,+\,(-x\mp\mathrm{i}\tilde{0})^{\varepsilon}\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{\sin(\pi\varepsilon)}\right].

(44)

By replacing
\varepsilon\rightarrow-\varepsilon
, we can use the
\varepsilon
-expansion from eq. (
78
) for the resulting hypergeometric function. Additionally writing the last term in terms of its real and imaginary part, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(x;\varepsilon\right)\,

\displaystyle=\,|x|^{-\varepsilon}\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-\varepsilon)^{n}\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\,+\,\pi\varepsilon\cot(\pi\varepsilon)\,\mp\,\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,.

(45)

The
\varepsilon
-expansion of the cotangent term is given by
(
Abramovitz_Stegun
, eqs. (4.3.70) & (23.1.18))

\displaystyle\pi\varepsilon\cot(\pi\varepsilon)\,=\,1\,-\,2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{2n}\,\varepsilon^{2n}\,=\,1-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\,[1+(-1)^{n}]\,\zeta_{n}\varepsilon^{n}\,,

(46)

where
\zeta_{n}=\zeta(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-n}
are the integer values of the Riemann zeta function.
Additionally expanding
|x|^{-\varepsilon}
and collecting orders of
\varepsilon
yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,=\,

\displaystyle 1\,-\,\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Li}_{1}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\,\mp\,\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon

\displaystyle-\,\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\left[(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\,\ln^{k}\left|\frac{1}{x}\right|\,\mathrm{Li}_{n-k}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)+[1+(-1)^{n}]\zeta_{n}\right].

(47)

Upon adding and subtracting the term
\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}\left|\frac{1}{x}\right|\ln\left|1-\frac{1}{x}\right|
, we can identify the single-valued polylogarithms
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)
, as defined in eq. (
94
). Inverting them with the help of eq. (
(i)
), we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,=\,1\,-\,\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\,\mp\,\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,-\,\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\left[\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)-\,\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}\left|x\right|\ln\left|\frac{x-1}{x}\right|\right].

(48)

Organizing this expression in terms of logarithms and single-valued polylogarithms yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,=\,1\,+\,\ln\frac{x}{x-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|\,-\,\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\,\mp\,\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,.

(49)

3.1.4
Representation of the expansion of
\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!
on the entire real axis

Combining the results found in eqs. (
42
), (
43
), and (
49
), we can give a representation for the
\varepsilon
-expansion of
\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\pm}\!\left(\varepsilon;x\right)\!\,=\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{sgn}_{123}\,\Theta(-x)}\mathfrak{F}(\varepsilon;x)\,\mp\,\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon\,\Theta(x-1)\,,

(50)

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(\varepsilon;x)\,\equiv\,1\,+\,\ln\!\left|\frac{x}{x-1}\right|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|\,-\,\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\,.

(51)

This compact expression is well suited to be used for the
\varepsilon
-expansion of the complete box integral. The imaginary part is made explicit and all functions of the real variable

An important feature of the expansion of

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\mathfrak{F}(\varepsilon;x)\,

\displaystyle=\,1\,-\,2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{2n}\zeta_{2n}\,,

(52)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}\mathfrak{F}(\varepsilon;x)\,

\displaystyle=1\,-\,2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{2n}(2^{1-2n}-1)\zeta_{2n}\,.

(53)

By collecting the

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}(\varepsilon;x)=|x|^{-\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\ln|1-x|-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\left[\frac{(-1)^{n}\ln|1-x|\ln^{n-1}|x|}{n!}+\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\right].

(54)

From this we can read off that

behaves like

near

and it diverges logarithmically like

near
x=1
. In the complete box integral the
\ln|1-x|
divergences always cancels.

4
Conclusion

We have revisited the massless scalar box integral with one external off-shell particle, keeping the causal
+\mathrm{i}0
throughout the calculation.
The main new result is extending the explicit determination of real and imaginary parts beyond finite order in the dimensional regularization parameter epsilon in all kinematic regions.
The epsilon expansion is expressed to all orders in terms of newly introduced single-valued polylogarithms. This makes our result free of the spurious branch cuts common to representations using ordinary polylogarithms. The single-valued polylogarithms are bounded, such that any divergent behavior in kinematic limits is contained in logarithmic terms.
Since we have determined the box integral in all kinematic regions, our result is suitable for all kinds of calculations in higher orders of perturbation theory, where imaginary parts or higher orders in epsilon become important.

The calculation method put forward in this work can be generalized to the scalar box integral with two non-adjacent end points off the light cone. A corresponding paper is in preparation
(
Haug2023
)
.

Appendix B
Gauss hypergeometric function
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)

For
|z|<1
the Gauss hypergeometric function is defined by the series
(
Abramovitz_Stegun
, eq. (15.1.1))

\displaystyle{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(a,b,c;z\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}\,\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\,,

(76)

where
(a)_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(a+n)}{\Gamma(a)}
is the Pochhammer symbol.
It is analytically continued by the integral representation
(
Abramovitz_Stegun
, eq. (15.3.1))

\displaystyle{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(a,b,c;z\right)\,\equiv\,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(c)}{\Gamma(b)\,\Gamma(c-b)}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\,t^{b-1}\,(1-t)^{c-b-1}\,(1-zt)^{-a}\,,

(77)

\displaystyle\text{where}\;\mathrm{Re}(c)>\mathrm{Re}(b)>0\,,\;z\in\mathbb{C}\!\setminus\!\mathbb{R}_{\geq 1}\,.

Note that the integral may be ill-defined for
z>1
, since in this case the term
{(1-zt)}
changes sign within the range of integration. This change of sign can, in general, give rise to a discontinuity or branch cut. In the following, we discuss selected properties (including the branch cut structure) of the specific hypergeometric function
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)
which appears in the internal-massless single off-shell scalar box integral.

B.1
Epsilon expansion of
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)

The
\varepsilon
-expansion of this hypergeometric function was derived in ref.
(
FabiValery
, eq. (B.28))
,

\displaystyle{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)\,=\,1\,-\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(z\right).

(78)

Since the polylogarithms
\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(z\right)
have a branch cut along the positive real axis greater than 1
(
Lewin_Polylogs_Associated_Functions
)
, the hypergeometric function also has a branch cut there. The branch cut structure will be discussed in section
B.3
.

B.2
Inversion relation for
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)

An equation relating the hypergeometric function
{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)
to another hypergeometric function with argument
\frac{1}{z}
is useful because it allows us to transform the argument away from the branch cut. To find this inversion relation, we write the hypergeometric function in terms of its
\varepsilon
-expansion and invert the appearing polylogarithms using the inversion relation for the polylogarithm given in ref.
(
Lewin_Polylogs_Associated_Functions
, eq. (7.20))
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(z\right)\,+\,(-1)^{n}\,\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\,=\,-\frac{\ln^{n}(-z)}{n!}\,+\,2\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{\ln^{n-2k}(-z)}{(n-2k)!}\,\mathrm{Li}_{2k}\!\left(-1\right)\,,

(79)

which holds for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
.

Plugging the second term on the left into eq. (

\displaystyle\,1\,+\,(-z)^{\varepsilon}-\,2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{\ln^{n-2k}(-z)}{(n-2k)!}\mathrm{Li}_{2k}\!\left(-1\right)\,.

\displaystyle{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,-\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon;z\right)+\,{{}_{2}}\mathrm{F}_{1}\!\left(1,\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon;\frac{1}{z}\right)\,

Appendix C
Single-valued polylogarithms

C.1
Single-valued polylogarithms in the literature

There are several definitions of so called
single-valued polylogarithms
in the literature. Their common feature is that they do not have branch cuts opposed to regular polylogarithms. Depending on the intended use, different versions prove themselves to be most suitable
(
Zhao2016
)
.

Bloch and Wigner devised an important version of a single-valued dilogarithm
(
Bloch2011
)

\displaystyle D_{2}(z)\,=\,\mathrm{Im}\left[\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(x\right)\right]+\mathrm{arg}(1-z)\ln|z|\,,

(89)

defined on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
. This was generalized to polylogarithms by Ramakrishnan
(
Ramakrishnan1986
)
. With a slight modification for odd
n
due to Wojtkowiak
(
Wojtkowiak1989
)
and the notation
\mathfrak{R}_{n}=\mathrm{Re}
/
\mathrm{Im}
if
n
is odd / even, his definition reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{n}\!\left(z\right)=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}\ln^{k}|z|}{k!}\mathrm{Li}_{n-k}\!\left(z\right)-\frac{(-1)^{n}\ln^{n}|z|}{2n!}\right].

(90)

Similarly Zagier proposed
(
Zagier1991
, eq. (33))

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{n}\!\left(z\right)=\mathfrak{R}_{n}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{2^{k}\mathrm{B}_{k}}{k!}\ln^{k}|z|\,\mathrm{Li}_{n-k}\!\left(z\right)-\frac{2^{n-1}\mathrm{B}_{n}}{n!}\ln^{n}|z|\right].

(91)

Both are single-valued real-analytic functions from
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Zagier’s version satisfies the functional equations of the polylogarithm in a clean fashion without product terms. However, both Ramakrishnan’s and Zagier’s functions are identically zero on
\mathbb{R}
for even
n
. Hence, they are not suitable for our purpose.

Also in an attempt to get rid of product terms in the functional equations, Lewin defined his version of polylogarithms by
(
Lewin1991
, eq. (3.19))

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\,\ln^{k}|x|\,\mathrm{Li}_{n-k}\!\left(x\right)+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|\ln(1-x)

(92)

for
x<1
, as a generalization of Rogers’ dilogarithm
\mathrm{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)

(
Rogers1907
, eq. (1))
.
They are related to Kummer’s functions
(
Kummer1840
, p. 330)

{\Lambda_{n}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{d}t\,\frac{\ln^{n-1}|t|}{1+t}}

by
(
Lewin1991
, eq. (3.18))

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n-1)!}\,\Lambda_{n}(-x)+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|\ln(1-x)\,.

(93)

Kummer’s functions are real-valued for real arguments, which makes them single-valued polylogarithms predating Bloch and Wigner’s construction by well over a century.
We observe from eq. (
93
) that
\mathrm{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
can be continued to a single-valued function for
x>1
by replacing
\ln(1-x)\rightarrow\ln|1-x|
in definition (
92
).

C.2
Definition and basic properties of continuous single-valued polylogarithms

We define for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}
and real
x
a version of single-valued polylogarithms by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\,\equiv\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\,\ln^{k}|x|\,\mathrm{Li}_{n-k}\!\left(x\right)+\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n!}\ln^{n-1}|x|\ln|1-x|\,.

(94)

For
x>1
the polylogarithms are taken on their principal branch, which is inherited from the principal branch of the logarithm, i.e.
{\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)\equiv\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x-\mathrm{i}0\right)}
. As a result we have a function of a real variable
x
with the following properties:

(a)

\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
is single-valued, i.e. there is no branch cut and no imaginary part, in contrast to
\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
.

(b)

\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
is continuous for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(c)

\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
, in contrast to
\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
.

(d)

\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
satisfies
clean
versions of the functional equations of
\mathrm{Li}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
, i.e. without product terms, as for Lewin’s and Zagier’s functions.

These properties make the function
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
well-suited for various applications, where one is interested in having a well-behaved version of polylogarithms with real arguments. Plots of the first few single-valued polylogarithms are shown in figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Plots of the single-valued polylogarithms defined in eq. (
94
), on the left for even
n
, on the right for odd
n
.

The qualitative behaviour of the functions is different for even and odd
n
. Here,
\zeta_{n}=\zeta(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-n}
are the integer values of the Riemann zeta function.

For even
n
:

\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
is monotonously increasing with
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-\infty\right)=2(2^{1-n}-1)\zeta_{n}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\infty\right)=2\zeta_{n}
. Special values are
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-1\right)=(2^{1-n}-1)\zeta_{n}
,
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(0\right)=0
,
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(1\right)=\zeta_{n}
. These functions are discontinuous at infinity, meaning
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\infty\right)\neq\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-\infty\right)
. Therefore functional equations mapping infinity to
0
or
1
will be discontinuous at those points.

For odd
n
:
It is
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-\infty\right)=0
,
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
is decreasing in
(-\infty,-1)
to a minimum value of
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-1\right)=(2^{1-n}-1)\zeta_{n}
, increases in
(-1,1)
, with
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(0\right)=0
, to a maximum of
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(1\right)=\zeta_{n}
and monotonously falls in
(1,\infty)
to
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\infty\right)=0
. Since
{\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\infty\right)=\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-\infty\right)}
these functions are continuous at infinity. Therefore functional equations mapping infinity to
0
or
1
are continuous.

For both odd and even
n
,
\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)
is real analytic on
\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0,1\}
. At
x=0
its derivative diverges logarithmically, at
x=1
it is
(n-2)
-times differentiable, the
(n-1)
th derivative diverges logarithmically at this point.

C.3
Functional equations of continuous single-valued polylogarithms

The functional equations satisfied by single-valued polylogarithms are inherited from the corresponding polylogarithms
(
Lewin_Polylogs_Associated_Functions
)
. Single-valued polylogarithms obey these equations in a
cleaner
fashion, where all product terms vanish.
There are equations satisfied for all
n
and additional identities for small values of
n
. We explicitly give the identities for di- and trilogarithm, identities for higher order polylogarithms can be found in
(
Lewin_Polylogs_Associated_Functions
;
Lewin1991
)
.

C.3.1
Equations of all single-valued polylogarithms

For all
n
there is an inversion relation and a duplication formula.

(i)

Inversion relation
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)+(-1)^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&\text{ for odd }n\,,\\
2\zeta_{n}&\text{ for even }n\text{ and }x>0\,,\\
2(2^{1-n}-1)\zeta_{n}&\text{ for even }n\text{ and }x<0\,.\end{array}\right.

Alternatively we can write this in the suggestive form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)+(-1)^{n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(x\right)\infty\right).

(98)

(ii)

Duplication formula
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(-x\right)-2^{1-n}\mathcal{L}_{n}\!\left(x^{2}\right)=0\,.

(99)

C.3.2
Equations of the single-valued dilogarithm

For the single-valued dilogarithm, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)=\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(x\right)+\ln|x|\ln(1-x)-\frac{1}{2}\ln|x|\ln|1-x|\,,

(100)

there is the richest structure. We have identities connecting the arguments
x
,
1-x
,
\frac{x}{x-1}
,
\frac{1}{x}
,
\frac{1}{1-x}
and
1-\frac{1}{x}
. Additionally there is the famous 2-variable 5-term relation discovered independently by Spence
(
Spence1809
, p.9)
and Abel
(
Abel1881
, XIV.(9))
.
All discontinuities in these identities are induced by the mapping of the discontinuity at infinity.

(i)

Euler identity
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(1-x\right)=\zeta_{2}\,.

(101)

(ii)

Landen identity
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(\frac{x}{x-1}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}0&\text{for }x<1\,,\\
3\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x>1\,.\end{array}\right.

(iii)

Inversion identity
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x<0\,,\\
2\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x>0\,.\end{array}\right.

(iv)

Inverted Euler identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)-\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x<1\,,\\
2\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x>1\,.\end{array}\right.

(v)

Inverted Landen identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)-\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(1-\frac{1}{x}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-2\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x<0\,,\\
\zeta_{2}&\text{for }x>0\,.\end{array}\right.

(vi)

Abel identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(y\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1-xy}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(1-xy\right)+\mathcal{L}_{2}\!\left(\frac{1-y}{1-xy}\right)=\pm 3\zeta_{2}\,,

(110)

where the sign on the right is negative if
1-xy<0
and
x,y<0
, else positive.

C.3.3
Equations of the single-valued trilogarithm

Besides the inversion relation, the single-valued trilogarithm, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(x\right)=\mathrm{Li}_{3}\!\left(x\right)-\ln|x|\,\mathrm{Li}_{2}\!\left(x\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|x|\ln(1-x)+\frac{1}{6}\ln^{2}|x|\ln|1-x|\,,

(111)

satisfies a single-variable identity connecting three terms and a three-variable 22 term relation discovered by Goncharov
(
Goncharov1995
)
. From the latter there follow a plethora of two variable identities as special cases.
Since the single-valued trilogarithm is continuous at infinity, these identities are continuous.

(i)

Inversion relation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(x\right)-\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)=0\,.

(112)

(ii)

Landen identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(1-x\right)+\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{x}{x-1}\right)=\zeta_{3}\,.

(113)

(iii)

Goncharov identity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(xyz\right)+

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\mathrm{Cyc}(x,y,z)}\left[\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(x\right)-\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(xy\right)+\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{x(1-y)}{x-1}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{y(1-x)}{y-1}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{1-x}{1-xyz}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{xy(1-z)}{1-xyz}\right)-\mathcal{L}_{3}\!\left(\frac{x(1-y)(1-z)}{(x-1)(1-xyz)}\right)\right]=3\zeta_{3}\,,

(114)

where cyclic summation for a function
f(x,y,z)
is defined as

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\mathrm{Cyc}(x,y,z)}f(x,y,z)=f(x,y,z)+f(y,z,x)+f(z,x,y)\,.

(115)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{su}\left|\frac{t\mu^{2}}{su}\right|^{\varepsilon}\left[\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{t}{s}\right)+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}\,\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{t}{u}\right)-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}\,\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{tQ^{2}}{su}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\sim(1-y)^{\varepsilon}\left[\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\varepsilon;(1-z)(1-y)\right)+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}\,\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{1-y}{y}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hphantom{\sim(1-y)^{-1-\varepsilon}}\,\,\left.-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}\,\mathfrak{F}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{(1-y)z}{y}\right)\right].

(122)

In the limit
y\rightarrow 1
the arguments of all three
\mathfrak{F}
functions become zero.
From eq. (
54
) we infer that for
\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}(\varepsilon;x)=\mathfrak{F}(\varepsilon;x)-|x|^{\varepsilon}
the limit
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}\frac{\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}(\varepsilon;x)}{x}
is finite order by order in
\varepsilon
.
Therefore, we can write

\displaystyle D(s,u,Q^{2})\sim(1-z)^{-\varepsilon}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}y^{\varepsilon}(1-z^{-\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\quad+(1-y)^{\varepsilon}\left[\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\!\left(\varepsilon;(1-z)(1-y)\right)+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{1-y}{y}\right)-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi\varepsilon}\,\tilde{\mathfrak{F}}\!\left(\varepsilon;-\frac{(1-y)z}{y}\right)\right].

(123)

Note that the term in square brackets stays finite in the limit
y\rightarrow 1
even when divided by
(1-y)
and hence

\displaystyle D(s,u,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=A_{su}(y)+(1-y)^{1+\varepsilon}B_{su}(y)\,,

(124)

with both
A_{su}(y)
and
B_{su}(y)
finite in the limit
y\rightarrow 1
order by order in
\varepsilon
.

To convert the singular behavior for
y\rightarrow 1
into poles in
\varepsilon
, which is required to combine real and virtual corrections ensuring cancellation of infrared poles, we use the expansion (see e.g. eq. (110) in
(
Altarelli1979
)
or eq. (69) in
(
Campbell2017
)
)

\displaystyle(1-y)^{-1-k\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{k\varepsilon}\,\delta(1-y)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-k\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{\ln^{n}(1-y)}{1-y}\right]_{+}\,,

(125)

where the plus distribution is defined by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}y\,f(y)\,[g(y)]_{+}\,\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}y\,[f(y)-f(1)]\,g(y)\,.

(126)

Using eqs. (
119
), (
121
), and (
124
), we find for the four types of contributions

(i)

(1-y)^{-\varepsilon}D_{0}(s,t,Q^{2})=\left(-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\,\delta(1-y)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-2\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{\ln^{n}(1-y)}{1-y}\right]_{+}\right)A_{st}(y)\,f_{1}(y)
,

(ii)

(1-y)^{-\varepsilon}D_{0}(s,u,Q^{2})=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\ln^{n}(1-y)\right)(A_{su}(y)+(1-y)B_{su}(y))\,f_{2}(y)
,

(iii)

(1-y)^{-1-\varepsilon}D_{0}(s,t,Q^{2})=\left(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\delta(1-y)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{\ln^{n}(1-y)}{1-y}\right]_{+}\right)A_{su}(y)\,f_{3}(y)

(1-y)^{-1-\varepsilon}D_{0}(s,t,Q^{2})=
\frac{1}{1}
+B_{su}(y)\,f_{3}(y)
,

(iv)

(1-y)^{-\varepsilon}D_{0}(t,u,Q^{2})=\left(-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\,\delta(1-y)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-2\varepsilon)^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{\ln^{n}(1-y)}{1-y}\right]_{+}\right)A_{tu}(y)\,f_{4}(y)
.

We see that the order
\varepsilon^{1}
of the box integral contributes to the order
\varepsilon^{0}
of the full result, since the
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
-poles generated by eq. (
125
) multiply the order
\varepsilon^{1}
of the functions
A_{st}(y)
,
A_{su}(y)
, and
A_{tu}(y)
. Hence, it is required to perform the
\varepsilon
-expansion of the box integral to one order higher than needed if no regularization would be required.
In the case of multiple regularizations, e.g. for
y,z\rightarrow 1
as in eq. (111) of
(
Altarelli1979
)
, multiple poles multiply the box integral, requiring knowledge of the box integral to even higher orders in
\varepsilon
.
Furthermore it was crucial to sum up any terms of the box integral singular in the kinematic limit to all orders, i.e. summing up terms of the form
\varepsilon^{n}\ln^{n}(1-y)
to
(1-y)^{-\varepsilon}
. This changed
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
to
\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}
in (i) and (iv).
