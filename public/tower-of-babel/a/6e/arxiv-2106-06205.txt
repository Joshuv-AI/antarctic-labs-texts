Time Warps, from Algebra to Algorithms
Sam van Gool, Adrien Guatto, George Metcalfe, Simon Santschi

Abstract
Graded modalities have been proposed in recent work on programming languages as a general framework for refining type systems with intensional properties. In particular, continuous endomaps of the discrete time scale, or time warps , can be used to quantify the growth of information in the course of program execution. Time warps form a complete residuated lattice, with the residuals playing an important role in potential programming applications. In this paper, we study the algebraic structure of time warps, and prove that their equational theory is decidable, a necessary condition for their use in real-world compilers. We also describe how our universal-algebraic proof technique lends itself to a constraint-based implementation, establishing a new link between universal algebra and verification technology.

Program types are almost as old as programs themselves.

For example, dependent types are expressive enough to serve as

Another line of research into type systems aims to classify not only

Such type systems describe the

Recently,

A graded modality

\Box_{f}A

The meaning of
\Box_{f}A
depends on the system at hand, but can generally

that includes the behavior prescribed by

In many cases, gradings come equipped with an ordered algebraic structure
that is relevant for programming applications.
Most commonly, they form a monoid whose binary operation corresponds to a notion
of composition such that
\Box_{gf}A

\Box_{f}\Box_{g}A

It is also often the case that gradings can be ordered by some sort

That is, we have a

As a consequence, the structure of this ordering is reflected by the

\Box_{g}B

The additional flexibility and descriptive power gained by adopting graded
modalities in a programming language comes at a price, however.
The language implementation must now be able to manipulate gradings in various
ways; in particular, it should be able to decide the ordering between gradings in order to
distinguish between well-typed and ill-typed programs.
In this paper, we address this issue for a specific class of gradings known as
time warps
: sup-preserving functions on
\omega^{+}=\omega\cup\{\omega\}
, or, equivalently, monotonic functions

]

Informally, time warps describe the growth of data along program execution.
In this setting, any type

The type

with a least element

The monoidal structure of time warps plays the expected role in programming applications. In particular,

From a programming perspective, residuals play a role similar to that of weakest

The type

The algebraic structure

Theorem 1
.

The equational theory of the time warp algebra

A

I. Pre-processing into a disjunction of basic terms.

In

II. Finitary characterization through diagrams.

The crucial step in our algorithm is the finitary characterization of ‘potential counterexamples’ for an equation of the form
{\textit{i}d}\leq t_{1}\vee\cdots\vee t_{n}
, where each
t_{i}
is a basic term. Our main tool for providing these finitary characterizations is the notion of a

The name ‘diagram’ recalls a similar concept used to prove the decidability of the equational theory of lattice-ordered groups in

Let us give an example to illustrate the basic idea. To falsify the equation
{\textit{i}d}\leq xy{x}^{\mathsf{\ell}}\vee{y}^{\mathsf{\ell}}

, we build a finite

\Gamma_{1}\supseteq\{\kappa,{x}^{\mathsf{\ell}}[\kappa],y[{x}^{\mathsf{\ell}}[\kappa]],x[y[{x}^{\mathsf{\ell}}[\kappa]]]\}

\Gamma_{1}

at relevant values. Similarly, we obtain a finite saturated sample set

\delta\colon\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}\to\omega^{+}
satisfying
\delta(x[y[{x}^{\mathsf{\ell}}[\kappa]]])<\delta(\kappa)
and
\delta({y}^{\mathsf{\ell}}[\kappa]]])<\delta(\kappa)

should determine partial sup-preserving functions
\hat{f}_{x}
and
\hat{f}_{y}
on

Clearly, not every function

III. Encoding as a satisfiability query.

In the last step of the algorithm, described in

2
A normal form for time warps

The main aim of this section is to provide a normal form for time warp terms. Our first step is to provide a more precise description of the left and right residuals of time warps. Note that to prove that two time warps are equal, it suffices to show that they coincide on every non-zero natural number, since for any time warp
f
, it is always the case that
f(0)=0
and
f(\omega)=\bigvee\{f(n)\mid n\in\omega\}
.

Lemma 2
.

For any time warps
f,g
and
p\in\omega^{+}
,

(a)

(f\backslash g)(p)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }p=0;\\
\bigvee\{q\in\omega^{+}\mid f(q)\leq g(p)\}&\text{if }p\in\omega{\setminus}\{0\};\\
\bigvee\{q\in\omega^{+}\mid(\exists m\in\omega)(f(q)\leq g(m))\}&\text{if }p=\omega\\
\end{cases}

(b)

(g/\penalty f)(p)=\bigwedge g[\{q\in\omega^{+}\mid p\leq f(q)\}]
.

Proof.

(a) Let
h
denote the function defined by cases on the right of the equation. Clearly,
h
is monotonic and satisfies
h(0)=0
and
h(\omega)=\bigvee\{h(n)\mid n\in\omega\}
, so
h
is a time warp. Moreover, since
f
preserves arbitrary joins,
fh\leq g
, and hence
h\leq f\backslash g
. For the converse, just observe that for any
n\in\omega{\setminus}\{0\}
, since
f((f\backslash g)(n))\leq g(n)
, also
(f\backslash g)(n)\leq h(n)
. So
h=f\backslash g
.

(b) Let
h
be the function defined by
h(p)\coloneqq\bigwedge g[\{q\in\omega^{+}\mid p\leq f(q)\}]
. Clearly,
h
is monotonic and satisfies
h(0)=0
and
h(\omega)=\bigvee\{h(n)\mid n\in\omega\}
, so
h
is a time warp. Moreover,
hf\leq g
, and hence
h\leq g/\penalty f
. For the converse, let
n\in\omega{\setminus}\{0\}
. If
q\in\omega^{+}
satisfies
n\leq f(q)
, then
(g/\penalty f)(n)\leq(g/\penalty f)(f(q))\leq g(q)
, and hence
(g/\penalty f)(n)\leq\bigwedge g[\{q\in\omega^{+}\mid n\leq f(q)\}]=h(n)
. So
h=g/\penalty f
.
∎

4

Let

be basic terms,
\kappa
a time variable, and
\Delta
the saturation of the sample set

\mathbf{W}
is decidable, concluding the proof of
1

Note that in the logic encoding, we will no longer allow
\omega
as a value for the variables. The theoretical reason why this is possible is that the ordinal
\omega^{+}
admits a first-order (even quantifier-free) interpretation in
\omega

Our construction of a first-order formula

\Delta

We define a formula
\psi

\Delta

\omega^{+}

and

and

are “
x=\omega
” and “
x=0
”, respectively.

2.

We obtain
\phi
by eliminating the symbols
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{I}
from
\psi
and re-interpreting
\preceq
and
\mathcal{S}
using an encoding of
\omega^{+}
in the structure

Let
\tau

\preceq
and
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{I}

\omega^{+}
as a

\omega^{+}

, and
\mathcal{O}^{\omega^{+}}\coloneqq\{\omega\}
. Note that a
\Delta
-prediagram is a valuation of the variables in
\Delta
in this structure.

We define
\psi
by translating the defining properties of being a

-diagram into quantifier-free formulas of first-order logic in the signature
\tau
with variables from
\Delta
. In the following definition, the symbols

and

\Delta

Definition 32
.

Let

Lemma 0.A.4
.

If
f_{1}
strongly extends
\lfloor{t_{1}}\rfloor_{\delta}
and
f_{2}
strongly extends
\lfloor{t_{2}}\rfloor_{\delta}
, then
f_{1}f_{2}
strongly extends
\lfloor{t_{1}t_{2}}\rfloor_{\delta}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
f_{1}
strongly extends
\lfloor{t_{1}}\rfloor_{\delta}
and
f_{2}
strongly extends
\lfloor{t_{2}}\rfloor_{\delta}
. Then for all
{t_{1}t_{2}}[{\alpha}]\in\Delta
,

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{2}(\delta(\alpha))

\displaystyle=f_{1}(f_{2}(\delta(\alpha)))

(by definition)

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\delta({t_{2}}[{\alpha}]))

(since
f_{2}
extends
\lfloor{t_{2}}\rfloor_{\delta}
)

\displaystyle=\delta({t_{1}}[{{t_{2}}[{\alpha}]}])

(since
f_{1}
extends
\lfloor{t_{1}}\rfloor_{\delta}
)

\displaystyle=\delta({t_{1}t_{2}}[{\alpha}])

\displaystyle\mbox{(by \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{c:prod})}.

So
f_{1}f_{2}
extends
\lfloor{t_{1}t_{2}}\rfloor_{\delta}

\lfloor{t_{1}t_{2}}\rfloor_{\delta}
is non-empty, since otherwise there is nothing to prove. Suppose that
\delta(\mathsf{last}(t_{1}t_{2}))=\omega
. Then
\delta(\mathsf{last}(t_{1}))=\delta(\mathsf{last}(t_{2}))=\omega

10

and

\mathop{\mathrm{last}}(f_{1}f_{2})=\omega

.
∎
