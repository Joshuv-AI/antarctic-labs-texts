On a cylindrical scanning modality in three-dimensional Compton scatter tomography \ddmmyyyydate August 9, 2026 \currenttime
James W. Webber † \dagger

Abstract
. We present injectivity and microlocal analyses of a new generalized Radon transform, ℛ \mathcal{R} , which has applications to a novel scanner design in three-dimensional Compton Scattering Tomography (CST), which we also introduce here. Using Fourier decomposition and Volterra equation theory, we prove that ℛ \mathcal{R} is injective and show that the image solution is unique. Using microlocal analysis, we prove that ℛ \mathcal{R} satisfies the Bolker condition (sometimes called the “Bolker assumption” [ 1 ] ), and we investigate the edge detection capabilities of ℛ \mathcal{R} . This has important implications regarding the stability of inversion and the amplification of measurement noise. In addition, we present simulated 3-D image reconstructions from ℛ ​ f \mathcal{R}f data, where f f is a 3-D density, with varying levels of added Gaussian noise. This paper provides the theoretical groundwork for 3-D CST using the proposed scanner design.

3.1.
Injectivity and inversion method

In this subsection, we prove injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
, and provide an inversion method using the theory of Volterra equations
[
31
]
and Neumann series. We now have our first main theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{L}
are injective on domain
L^{2}_{c}(C_{\epsilon})
, for any fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Taking the Fourier transform in
z_{0}
on both sides of (
3.2
) yields

(3.4)

\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}(p,R,\theta_{0},\eta)=2p\int_{0}^{\sqrt{p^{2}-R^{2}}}\cos(\eta z)\frac{t}{\sqrt{p^{2}-z^{2}}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\hat{f}\left(r,\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{t}{r}\sin\phi\right)+\theta_{0},\eta\right)\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}z,

where
\eta
is dual to
z
. Taking the Fourier components in
\theta_{0}
on both sides of (
3.4
) yields

(3.5)

\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}_{n}(p,R,\eta)=4\int_{0}^{\sqrt{p^{2}-R^{2}}}\cos(\eta z)\frac{tp}{\sqrt{p^{2}-z^{2}}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(n\theta)\hat{f}_{n}\left(r,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}z,

for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, where

\hat{f}_{n}\left(r,\eta\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\hat{f}(r,\theta_{0},\eta)e^{-in\theta_{0}}\mathrm{d}\theta_{0},

and

\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}_{n}(p,R,\eta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}(p,R,\theta_{0},\eta)e^{-in\theta_{0}}\mathrm{d}\theta_{0}.

Substituting
t=\sqrt{p^{2}-z^{2}}-R
in the
z
integral of (
3.5
)yields

(3.6)

\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}_{n}(p,R,\eta)=4\int_{0}^{h}\frac{t(t+R)}{\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}}\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(n\theta)\hat{f}_{n}\left(r,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}\phi\mathrm{d}t,

where
r=\sqrt{t^{2}+1-2t\cos\phi}
, and
\sin\theta=\frac{t}{r}\sin\phi
.

Let us now substitute
r=\sqrt{t^{2}+1-2t\cos\phi}
in the
\phi
integral of (
3.6
). We have

\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\phi}=\frac{t}{r}\sin\phi=\sin\theta,\ \ \ \cos\theta=\frac{r^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2r}.

Then

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}_{n}(p,R,\eta)=\\
&4\int_{0}^{h}\frac{t(t+R)}{\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}}\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\right)\int_{1-t}^{1-\epsilon}\frac{T_{|n|}\left(\frac{r^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2r}\right)}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{r^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2r}\right)^{2}}}\hat{f}_{n}\left(r,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}t\\
&=4\int_{1-h}^{1-\epsilon}\int_{1-r}^{h}\frac{t(t+R)\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\right)T_{|n|}\left(\frac{r^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2r}\right)}{\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{r^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2r}\right)^{2}}}\hat{f}_{n}\left(r,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}r,\end{split}

where
T_{|n|}
is a Chebyshev polynomial degree
|n|
. Substituting
r=1-u
yields

(3.8)

\begin{split}&\widehat{\mathcal{R}f}_{n}(p,R,\eta)=4\int_{\epsilon}^{h}K_{n}(\eta;p,R,u)\tilde{\hat{f}}_{n}\left(u,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}u,\end{split}

a Volterra equation of the first kind, where
\tilde{\hat{f}}_{n}\left(u,\eta\right)=\hat{f}_{n}\left(1-u,\eta\right)
, and

(3.9)

\begin{split}&K_{n}(\eta;p,R,u)=\\
&2(1-u)\int_{u}^{h}\frac{t(t+R)\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\right)T_{|n|}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right)}{\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\sqrt{t^{2}-u^{2}}\sqrt{2+t-u}\sqrt{2-t-u}}\mathrm{d}t\\
&=2(1-u)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t(t+R)\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\right)T_{|n|}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right)}{\sqrt{v}\sqrt{1-v}\sqrt{p+(t+R)}\sqrt{t+u}\sqrt{2+t-u}\sqrt{2-t-u}}\mid_{t=u+v(h-u)}\mathrm{d}v,\end{split}

after substituting
t=u+v(h-u)
in the last step. We have

(3.10)

\begin{split}K_{n}(\eta;p,R,h)&=\frac{\sqrt{ph(1-h)}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{v}\sqrt{1-v}}\mathrm{d}v\\
&=\frac{\pi\sqrt{ph(1-h)}}{2},\end{split}

which is non-zero for
h\in[\epsilon,1-\kappa]
, where
\kappa\in(0,1-\epsilon)
.

Now that we have a general expression for the Fourier decomposition of
\mathcal{R}f
, we prove injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{L}
. Let us define

p=p(h)=\frac{\alpha^{2}+h^{2}}{2h},\ \ \ \ \ \ R=R(h)=p(h)-h,\ \ \ \text{and},\ \ \ t=t(h,u)=u+v(h-u).

Then,

(3.11)

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{R}_{L}f}_{n}(h,\eta)&=4\int_{\epsilon}^{h}K_{n}(\eta;p(h),R(h),u)\tilde{\hat{f}}_{n}\left(u,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}u,\\
&=\int_{\epsilon}^{h}\widetilde{K_{n}}(\eta;h,u)\tilde{\hat{f}}_{n}\left(u,\eta\right)\mathrm{d}u.\end{split}

We now aim to prove that
\widetilde{K_{n}}(\eta,\cdot,\cdot)
, and its first order derivative with respect to
h
, is bounded on
T=\{(h,u):\epsilon\leq h\leq 1-\kappa,\epsilon\leq u\leq h\}
, for any fixed
n
and
\eta
. To do this, we show that all the terms dependent on
h
under the integral sign on the third line of (
3.9
) are bounded and have bounded first order derivative with respect to
h
within the limits of integration. First, for
h\in[\epsilon,1-\kappa]
, we have the inequalities

(3.12)

|p|\leq\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2\epsilon},\ \ \ \ \ \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}h}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}},

and

(3.13)

|R|\leq|p|\leq\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{2\epsilon},\ \ \ \ \ \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}h}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2h^{2}}-1\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}.

Further,

(3.14)

|t|\leq 1,\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}h}\right|=\left|v\right|\leq 1.

We have,
\left|\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-(t+R)^{2}}\right)\right|\leq 1
, and, letting
g_{1}=g_{1}(h,u)=t(h,u)+R(h)
,

(3.15)

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}h}\cos\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-g_{1}^{2}}\right)=\\
&-\frac{\eta\left[pp^{\prime}-g_{1}g^{\prime}_{1}\right]}{\sqrt{p^{2}-g_{1}^{2}}}\sin\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-g_{1}^{2}}\right)\\
&=-{\eta}^{2}\left[pp^{\prime}-g_{1}g^{\prime}_{1}\right]\sinc\left(\eta\sqrt{p^{2}-g_{1}^{2}}\right),\end{split}

which is bounded by (
3.12
)-(
3.14
). In (
3.15
),
p^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}_{1}
are the derivatives of
p
and
g_{1}
, respectively, with respect to
h
.

We have
\left|T_{|n|}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right)\right|\leq 1
, and, for
n\neq 0
,

(3.16)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}h}T_{|n|}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right)&=-\frac{v\cdot t}{1-u}T^{\prime}_{|n|}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right)\\
&=-\frac{|n|v\cdot t}{1-u}U_{|n|-1}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right),\end{split}

where
U_{n}
is a Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind. It follows that

(3.17)

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}h}T_{|n|}\left(\frac{(1-u)^{2}+1-t^{2}}{2(1-u)}\right)\right|\leq\frac{|n|^{2}}{\kappa},

noting
|U_{|n|}|\leq|n|+1
, and
\frac{1}{1-u}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}
. The
n=0
case is trivial since
T_{0}=1
.

Now, letting
g_{2}=g_{2}(h,u)=p(h)+t(h,u)+R(h)
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{p+(t+R)}\sqrt{t+u}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{2}}\sqrt{t+u}}\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon},

and

(3.18)

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}h}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{g_{2}}\sqrt{t+u}}\right)\right|&=\left|\frac{(t+u)g_{2}^{\prime}+v\cdot g_{2}}{2\left(g_{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(t+u\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right|\\
&\leq\frac{2|g_{2}^{\prime}|+|g_{2}|}{2(2\epsilon)^{3}},\end{split}

which is bounded, by (
3.12
)-(
3.14
). In (
3.18
),
g^{\prime}_{2}
denotes the derivative of
g_{2}
with respect to
h

Finally, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+t-u}\sqrt{2-t-u}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\kappa}},

and

(3.19)

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}h}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+t-u}\sqrt{2-t-u}}\right|&=\left|\frac{v\cdot t}{(2+t-u)^{\frac{3}{2}}(2-t-u)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right|\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}.\end{split}

It follows that the kernel,
\widetilde{K_{n}}(\eta;h,u)
, is bounded and has bounded first order derivative with respect to
h
on
T
. Further,
K_{n}(\eta;h,h)\neq 0
, for
h\in[\epsilon,1-\kappa]
, by (
3.10
). Thus, using classical theory on Volterra equations
[
31
]
, we can recover
\tilde{\hat{f}}_{n}\left(u,\eta\right)
uniquely for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
u\in[\epsilon,1]
, after letting
\kappa\to 0
. This proves injectivity of
\mathcal{R}_{L}
on domain
L^{2}_{c}(C_{\epsilon})
. Injectivity of
\mathcal{R}
follows, as
\mathcal{R}f=0\implies\mathcal{R}_{L}f=0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

4.1.
Analysis of
\mathcal{R}_{L}

In this case, the lemon surfaces have the defining function

\Psi({\mathbf{x}};s,\theta_{0},z_{0})=\frac{|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}|^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{\prime}|}-s,\ \ \ s\leq 0

where

, and
{\mathbf{x}}_{T}=(x-\cos\theta_{0},y-\sin\theta_{0},z-z_{0})^{T}=({\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{\prime},z-z_{0})^{T}
,
{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=(x,y)^{T}
,
{\mathbf{x}}=(x,y,z)^{T}
.
\Psi
is a defining function in the sense that
\mathcal{L}=\{\Psi=0\}
is a lemon surface, when
s\leq 0
. When
s>0

\{\Psi=0\}
is an apple surface (see
[
26

), and when
s=0

\{\Psi=0\}
is a sphere, radius

with center
(\cos\theta_{0},\sin\theta_{0},z_{0})
.

For
f\in L_{c}^{2}(C_{\epsilon})
, where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we have the alternate expression for
\mathcal{R}_{L}

(4.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{R}_{L}f(s,\theta_{0},z_{0})&=\int_{C_{\epsilon}}\left|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Psi\right|\delta\left(\Psi({\mathbf{x}};s,\theta_{0},z_{0})\right)f({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{C_{\epsilon}}\left|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Psi\right|e^{i\sigma\Psi({\mathbf{x}};s,\theta_{0},z_{0})}f({\mathbf{x}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{d}\sigma,\end{split}

where
\Phi=\sigma\Psi
is the phase function, and
a=\left|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Psi\right|
is the amplitude. The weight
\left|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Psi\right|
is included so that
\mathcal{R}_{L}f
defines the integrals of
f
over lemons with respect to the surface measure on the lemon surface, in line with the theory of
[
19
]
. Note, we now vary
s
instead of
h
as before in the injectivity proofs. We do this to make the calculations in this section easier. We now have our second main theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

\mathcal{R}_{L}
is an elliptic FIO order
-1
which satisfies the Bolker condition.

Proof.

We first prove
\mathcal{R}_{L}
is an FIO. Clearly,
\Phi=\sigma\Psi
is homogeneous order one, since
\Psi
does not depend on
\sigma
. Also,
\mathrm{d}_{s}\Phi=-\sigma\neq 0
, hence
\mathrm{d}\Phi,\mathrm{d}_{{\mathbf{y}}}\Phi\neq\mathbf{0}
. The lemon surfaces are smooth manifolds away from their points of self-intersection (i.e.,
(\cos\theta_{0},\sin\theta_{0},z_{0}\pm\alpha)
), which we do not consider as they are outside the support of
f
. Thus,
\Phi
is clean.

Let
g=|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{\prime}|
. Then, we have

(4.2)

\begin{split}\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Phi&=\sigma\left(\frac{2{\mathbf{x}}_{T}}{g}-\left(\frac{|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}|^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{g^{3}}\right){\mathbf{v}}\right)\\
&=\frac{\sigma}{g}\left(\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)(x-\cos\theta_{0}),\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)(y-\sin\theta_{0}),2(z-z_{0})\right),\end{split}

where where
{\mathbf{v}}=(x-\cos\theta_{0},y-\sin\theta_{0},0)
, and

. Thus,

(4.3)

\left|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Phi\right|=\sigma\sqrt{\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)^{2}+\frac{4(z-z_{0})^{2}}{g^{2}}}.

Now,

which is zero if and only if
(x-\cos\theta_{0})^{2}+(y-\sin\theta_{0})^{2}-(z-z_{0})^{2}+\alpha^{2}=0
. The two-sheeted hyperboloid

intersects the lemon only at its points of self-intersection (i.e.,
(\cos\theta_{0},\sin\theta_{0},z_{0}\pm\alpha)
), which we do not consider as they are outside the support of
f
. It follows that
\mathrm{d}_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Phi\neq\mathbf{0}
, and
\Phi
is non-degenerate. Further,

, and hence
a
is an elliptic symbol.
a
is order zero since it is smooth and does not depend on
\sigma

2.5

\mathcal{R}_{L}

5.0pt\hfil\hbox{\vrule height=0.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}$\scriptstyle{\mathrm{d}_{\theta_{0}}\Phi}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle\cdot$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle\mathbf{r}_{3}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt\crcr}}}}\right)$}}.

The
\mathbf{r}_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq 3
, are defined as

(4.7)

\begin{split}\mathbf{r}_{1}=\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}(s)&=\frac{2{\mathbf{x}}_{T}}{g}-\left(\frac{|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}|^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{g^{3}}\right){\mathbf{v}}\\
&=\frac{1}{g}\left(\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)(x-\cos\theta_{0}),\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)(y-\sin\theta_{0}),2(z-z_{0})\right),\end{split}

and

(4.8)

\begin{split}\mathbf{r}_{2}=\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\mathrm{d}_{z_{0}}\Phi\right)&=\frac{2\sigma}{g}\left(-\frac{(z-z_{0})(x-\cos\theta_{0})}{g^{2}},-\frac{(z-z_{0})(y-\sin\theta_{0})}{g^{2}},1\right),\end{split}

and

(4.9)

\begin{split}\mathbf{r}_{3}=\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\mathrm{d}_{\theta_{0}}\Phi\right)&=\frac{\sigma}{g}\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)(\sin\theta_{0},-\cos\theta_{0},0)+\frac{\sigma\left(\Theta_{0}^{\perp}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{T}^{\prime}\right)}{g^{3}}\left[-{\mathbf{v}}+\frac{{\mathbf{u}}}{g^{2}}\right],\end{split}

where
{\mathbf{v}}=(x-\cos\theta_{0},y-\sin\theta_{0},0)
, and

{\mathbf{u}}=\left(3\left[(z-z_{0})^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right](x-\cos\theta_{0}),3\left[(z-z_{0})^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right](y-\sin\theta_{0}),-2g^{2}(z-z_{0})\right),

noting

\frac{1}{g}\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)=\frac{1}{g}-\frac{(z-z_{0})^{2}-\alpha^{2}}{g^{3}}

in the calculation of
\mathbf{r}_{3}
.

D\Pi_{L}
is invertible if and only if the matrix
M=\left(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{T},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{T},\mathbf{r}_{3}^{T}\right)
is invertible. We have

\mathbf{r}_{1}\times\mathbf{r}_{2}=\frac{2\sigma\left(|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\right)}{g^{4}}\left(-(y-\sin\theta_{0}),(x-\cos\theta_{0}),0\right).

Thus

\text{det}(M)=\mathbf{r}_{3}\cdot(\mathbf{r}_{1}\times\mathbf{r}_{2})=-\left(2-\frac{s}{g}\right)\cdot\frac{2\sigma^{2}\left(|{\mathbf{x}}_{T}|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\right)}{g^{5}}\left((\Theta_{0}^{T},0)\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\right),

which is zero if and only if
2-\frac{s}{g}=0
or
(\Theta_{0}^{T},0)\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{T}=0
.
2-\frac{s}{g}
was shown earlier to be never zero for points on the lemon, except the singular points, which we do not consider. The plane
\{(\Theta_{0}^{T},0)\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{T}=0\}
does not intersect
C_{\epsilon}
, by convexity of the
C_{0}
boundary. Hence
(\Theta_{0}^{T},0)\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{T}\neq 0
for
{\mathbf{x}}\in C_{\epsilon}
. Thus,
\Pi_{L}
is an immersion.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The above theorem shows that any reconstruction artifacts due to the Bolker condition are reflections of the true singularities in planes tangent to
C_{0}
. Since
f
is supported on
C_{0}
, this means, due to the convexity of the
C_{0}
boundary, any artifacts due to Bolker must lie in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash C_{0}
, and thus do not interfere with the region of interest. If
f
were supported outside of
C_{0}
, e.g., if
\text{supp}(f)\subset\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash C_{0}
, where
\Omega
is an open subset, then the Bolker condition would fail. This is true since, for any point
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash C_{0}
, we can find a plane tangent to
C_{0}
which intersects
{\mathbf{x}}
, and thus the immersion part of Bolker would fail for
f
supported outside of
C_{0}
.

In this section, we use the following defining function for the lemon surfaces

Proof.

, and,

\left|\nabla_{{\mathbf{x}}}\Phi\right|=2\sigma\sqrt{\frac{(g+R)^{2}}{g^{4}}+(z-z_{0})^{2}}>0.

Thus,

is injective, given the convexity of the boundary of

5.1.
Reconstruction methods and data simulation

In this subsection, we explain how we simulate data and detail the image reconstruction methods used.
Let
A
denote the discretized
R_{L}
operator, let
{\mathbf{x}}
denote a vectorized image, and let
\mathbf{b}
be the measured data. Then, we simulate
\mathbf{b}
using

(5.1)

\mathbf{b}=A{\mathbf{x}}+\gamma\times\frac{\|A{\mathbf{x}}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{l}}\mathbf{\eta},

where
l
is the length of
A{\mathbf{x}}
,
\mathbf{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a vector, size
l\times 1
, of draws from a standard Gaussian, and
\gamma
is a parameter which controls the noise level. Then,
\|A{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b}\|/\|A{\mathbf{x}}\|\approx\gamma
, for large
l
. The image resolution used throughout this section is
41\times 41\times 41
, and we simulate
l=26691
lemon integrals to reconstruct
{\mathbf{x}}
. Specifically,
\mathcal{R}_{L}f(h,\theta_{0},z_{0})
is sampled for
(h,\theta_{0},z_{0})\in[0,2]\times[0,2\pi]\times[-3,3]
on a meshgrid with
l
pixels.
A
has size
26691\times 41^{3}
, and is underdetermined.
A
is also sparse, and we store
A
as a sparse matrix.

To recover
{\mathbf{x}}
from
\mathbf{b}
, we use the Landweber method and a Conjugate Gradient Least Squares (CGLS) and TV hybrid algorithm which are detailed below:

(1)

Landweber - to implement the Landweber method
[
15
]
, we apply the code provided in
[
9
]
, with the default settings.

(2)

CGLS-TV hybrid - we use a combination of the Non-Negative CGLS (NNCGLS) code of
[
5
]
and the 3-D TV denoising code of
[
20
]
; specifically we use the Matlab function “prox_tv3d” provided in
[
20
]
to implement TV smoothing. We run
m_{1}
iterations of NNCGLS, followed by
m_{2}
iterations of TV smoothing. We then repeat the whole process
m
times to obtain a reconstruction.

The Landweber method offers modest regularization, and is included to validate our microlocal theory and highlight the artifacts we expect to see if the solution is not sufficiently regularized. The CGLS-TV hybrid method is included to show the performance of more powerful regularizers, such as TV and non-negativity. The assumption of non-negativity is appropriate, as the reconstruction target in CST is an electron density, which is non-negative. is the ground truth, and

5.3.
Phantom reconstructions

In this subsection, we present simulated reconstructions of image phantoms using iterative methods, as detailed in subsection
5.1
. See figure
7
, where we show two test phantoms. The first test phantom, shown on the left-hand of figure
7
, is in the form of a spin top and is rotationally symmetric about the
z
axis. The spin top has density 1, and the background has density 0. The second test phantom, shown on the right-hand of figure
7
, is composed of layered bricks, with densities alternating between 1 and 2. The background of the layered brick phantom has density 0, as with the spin top phantom.

In figures
8
and
9
, we show reconstructions of the spin top and layered brick phantoms using the Landweber and CGLS-TV methods, when the noise level is set to
\gamma=0.001
. In figures
8
and
9
, we show 3-D renderings and
(x,z)
plane cross sections of the image reconstructions. In table
1
, we tabulate the least squares error values,
\epsilon_{r}
, for varying levels of added noise
\gamma
.

The Landweber reconstructions on the left-hand of figures
8
and
9
are blurry and corrupted by noise. This is also reflected in the least squares error values in table
1
. For example, the least squares error offered by Landweber on the spin top phantom is
43\%
. We see a significant blurring effect near the boundaries of the phantoms, which is particularly emphasized near edges in directions parallel to the
z
axis. The layered brick phantom has jump discontinuities in the
x
,
y
, and
z
directions. In the bottom left-hand subfigure of figure
9
, the oscillating layers in the
z
direction are heavily blurred together, and the edges are not well resolved. The edges in the
x
direction, while still slightly blurred, are better resolved than those in the
z
direction. In section
4.3
, we showed that there are parts of the image edge map which are not visible in
\mathcal{R}_{L}f
data, and that the missing edges are concentrated near directions parallel to the
z
axis.
This explains why we see a strong blurring effect in the reconstruction near edges with direction parallel to, or a small angle from the
z
direction.

The CGLS-TV reconstructions are shown in the right-hand of figures
8
and
9
. CGLS-TV offers better image quality, when compared to the Landweber method, and TV suppresses the noise and blurring due to limited angles. The least squares error scores are also significantly improved. For example, the least squares error offered by CGLS-TV on the spin top phantom is
4\%
, which is
39\%
lower than the
43\%
error offered by the Landweber method.

6.
Conclusions and further work

In this paper, we introduced a novel scanning modality in 3-D CST, and new generalized Radon transforms,
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{L}
, which mathematically model the Compton scatter signal. We showed that
\mathcal{R}_{L}
and
\mathcal{R}
, on domain
L^{2}_{c}(C_{\epsilon})
, for some fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, are injective and FIO which satisfy the Bolker condition. In section
4.3
, we investigated the edge detection capabilities of
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}_{L}
, and showed that there are elements of the edge map of
f
which are invisible in
\mathcal{R}f
and
\mathcal{R}_{L}f
data, and thus the problem is one of limited-angle tomography.
In section
5
, we presented simulated image reconstructions using
\mathcal{R}_{L}f
data. To validate our microlocal theory, we showed reconstructions of a delta function. The predicted and observed artifacts superimposed exactly, and were located outside the region of interest,
C_{\epsilon}
, as predicted by our theory. In subsection
5.3
, we presented reconstructions of image phantoms in an idealized setting, using iterative methods. The data was simulated using the exact model
\mathcal{R}_{L}f
. We then added varying levels of Gaussian noise to simulate noise. In further work, we aim to consider more accurate data generation methods, such as Monte Carlo simulation.

The proposed cylindrical scanner has the ability measure conventional X-ray CT data (straight line integrals), in addition to Compton scatter data. In further work, we aim to combine X-ray CT and Compton scatter data to address noise and inversion instabilities due to limited angles, e.g., as discussed in section
4.3
.
