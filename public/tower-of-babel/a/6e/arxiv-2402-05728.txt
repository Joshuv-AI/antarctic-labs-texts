Abstract
. The entertainment industry relies on 3D visual content to create immersive experiences, but traditional methods for creating textured 3D models can be time-consuming and subjective. Generative networks such as StyleGAN have advanced image synthesis, but generating 3D objects with high-fidelity textures is still not well explored, and existing methods have limitations. We propose the Semantic-guided Conditional Texture Generator (CTGAN), producing high-quality textures for 3D shapes that are consistent with the viewing angle while respecting shape semantics. CTGAN utilizes the disentangled nature of StyleGAN to finely manipulate the input latent codes, enabling explicit control over both the style and structure of the generated textures. A coarse-to-fine encoder architecture is introduced to enhance control over the structure of the resulting textures via input segmentation. Experimental results show that CTGAN outperforms existing methods on multiple quality metrics and achieves state-of-the-art performance on texture generation in both conditional and unconditional settings. We will release our code and data after publication.

1.
Introduction

3D visual content has become increasingly essential in entertainment, especially in movies, games, and virtual reality applications. These fields rely on 3D models to create immersive and engaging user experiences.
The most common representation of 3D content includes both geometry and texture information.
Traditional methods for creating textured 3D models often require multiple steps and can be time-consuming for professionals.
Manually creating textures involves a skilled artist hand-painting textures on the 3D model or capturing photographs of real-world objects to apply them as textures. These traditional methods can be subjective and labor-intensive and may not always result in the desired level of realism.

Advancements in generative learning techniques and the availability of large-scale 3D shape datasets have significantly enhanced the modeling of 3D geometry, resulting in highly realistic geometric representations of 3D objects. However, generating 3D objects with high-fidelity textures remains an area that has not been extensively explored.
Meanwhile, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), especially the style-based GAN architecture known as StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
;
Karras et al. 2020b
;
Karras et al. 2020a
)
, have significantly advanced image synthesis, achieving high visual quality and generating images with remarkable realism. Previous research
(
Yang et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2020
;
Collins et al. 2020
)
has also shown that StyleGAN has a disentangled latent code that enables control and editing of the generated results.

Recent research
(
Yu et al. 2021
;
Siddiqui et al. 2022
)
has aimed to learn the texture generator from real-world images by utilizing the generative capabilities of StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
;
Karras et al. 2020b
)
. These methods have produced superior visual quality when compared to learning implicit fields to model textures in 3D space
(
Oechsle et al. 2019
)
, as demonstrated in Figure
2
. However, existing methods have several limitations. Firstly, these models were designed to generate textures from a latent code randomly sampled from the latent space, lacking direct control to generate textures with style conditioned on a given reference image. Secondly, using a separated StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
)
generator for generating textures at different views may result in inconsistent style after texture mapping to the 3D shapes, as illustrated in Figure
2
. Lastly, the generated texture may not align with the underlying shape semantics due to either weak supervision on the shape silhouette
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
or insufficient mesh resolution
(
Siddiqui et al. 2022
)
.

To overcome the aforementioned limitations, we propose a new approach named the
Semantic-guided Conditional Texture Generator
(CTGAN), which can produce high-quality textures for 3D shapes consistent with the viewing angle. As shown in Figure
1
, our approach enables controllable texture generation while respecting the shape semantics.
Our method is based on the LTG architecture
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
, which employs a canonical-view projection-based texture atlas for texture parameterization and uses StyleGAN to generate textures for each canonical view. However, instead of using a separate StyleGAN generator for each texture map, we utilize the StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
which inherits the disentangled nature of StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
)
to finely manipulate the input latent codes, enabling explicit control over the style and structure of the generated textures and achieving more stable training
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
than StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
)
on small dataset.
We separate the disentangled latent codes of StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
into two parts: the
structure
codes and the
style
codes, which control the structure and appearance of the resulting textures, respectively. We then train two encoders, the structure encoder, and style encoder, to map a semantics segmentation map and a reference style image into their respective latent codes. We employ a coarse-to-fine encoder architecture for the structure encoder to represent the input semantic map better.
We evaluate our approach on the ShapeNet car dataset
(
Chang et al. 2015
)
and the FFHQ human face dataset
(
Karras et al. 2019b
)
, and compare it with state-of-the-art texture generation methods. The experimental results demonstrate that our approach achieves a significant performance boost in both conditional (FID score drops from 139.63 to 39.41) and unconditional (FID score drops from 87.06 to 66.73) settings.
In summary, we make the following contributions:

Generative Model on 3D Domain.

Generating 3D models with realistic appearance and texture has become popular in recent years. Early approaches relied on latent representations from the geometry and texture of the model
(
Chan et al. 2021
;
Oechsle et al. 2019
)
or 3D disentangled representations
(
Nguyen-Phuoc et al. 2019
;
Niemeyer and Geiger 2021
)
.
Style-based GANs
(
Karras et al. 2019a
;
Karras et al. 2020b
)
have achieved high-fidelity results by using disentangled latent codes to represent implicit attributes. Some works have introduced coordinate systems
(
Karras et al. 2021
)
or extracted segmentation maps from implicit fields
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Shi et al. 2022
)
to control 3D information, while others have learned 3D models from synthesized results
(
Gu et al. 2022
)
. Another approach generates high-quality 2D images from the 3D model using disentangled latent codes
(
Chan et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2021
;
Or-El et al. 2022
)
.
However, these works focus on generating 2D results rather than 3D textured models. In contrast, our method aims to produce 3D shapes with a realistic texture that are conditioned on the given style images.

Latent Space Manipulation.

To enable precise control over the output of GAN
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, various methods for style latent code editing
(
Chiu et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2020
;
Shen and Zhou 2021
)
have been proposed. These approaches encode a given style image into a latent space and then edit the latent code in a meaningful manner.
While existing methods are effective, using the segmentation maps to manipulate the latent code is still a less explored challenge.
To this end, we propose an architecture that combines both of them.
In detail, we use two encoders, structure encoder and style encoder, to encode both of the segmentation maps and style images into latent space and control the latent code with the segmentation maps and style images.

Texture Generation for 3D Shapes.

In recent years, the generation of textures for 3D shapes has received considerable attention from researchers, as evidenced by several recent works
(
Oechsle et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2021
;
Siddiqui et al. 2022
;
Gao et al. 2022
)
.
Texture Fields
(
Oechsle et al. 2019
)
utilizes implicit fields to model textures in 3D space, while LTG
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
uses a canonical-view projection-based texture atlas representation and employs a separate StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
)
to generate a texture map for each view. It also addresses texture inconsistency across views using multiple discriminators. Texturify
(
Siddiqui et al. 2022
)
generates textures directly on the surfaces of 3D shapes using convolutional operators that are conditioned on both 3D models and style latent codes. However, these methods have several limitations, including producing blurry results
(
Oechsle et al. 2019
)
, generating inconsistent textures across views
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
, and producing textured models of mediocre quality due to insufficient structure guidance
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
or low mesh resolution
(
Siddiqui et al. 2022
)
during the inference process.

Some of the texture generation works focus on synthesizing pure texture without input mesh
(
Frühstück et al. 2019
;
Rodriguez-Pardo and Garces 2022
)
, however, these methods cannot be used in our scenario since they do not provide UV maps.

Recently, the diffusion model
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
has become popular. There are some works that combine the power of a text encoder
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
with the diffusion model to synthesize textures given an input mesh
(
Metzer et al. 2023
;
Richardson et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
)
.
However, text-prompt guided methods are not ideal for generating textures that require fine-grained control over semantics and structure.

In contrast, our method leverages the disentanglement nature of StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
to explicitly control the structure and style of the generated textures through separated latent codes, enabling the conditional setting where our model learns the mapping from input structure and style maps using different encoders. As a result, we can generate high-quality textured 3D shapes that surpass those generated by prior methods.

\mathbb{U}

).
As for texture generator block, we adopt StyleGAN2

based on the segmentation maps
\mathbb{S}

In order to produce view-consistent results, we separate the style code

4.
Method

Figure
3
depicts the system overview of CTGAN, which comprises three main components: texture parameterization, texture generator, and style code encoders. In the following subsections, we will provide a detailed explanation of each of these components.

4.1.
Texture Parameterization

To achieve our objective of generating textures for a particular 3D model, it is necessary to define the texture parameterization process for mapping 2D textures onto the 3D domain. We employ the canonical-view projection-based texture atlas, similar to LTG
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
, to parameterize the input 3D model with six UV maps.
The process involves projecting the input 3D model onto
N
predefined viewpoints, assigning the projected coordinate as a vertex’s texture coordinate, and creating the corresponding UV maps
\mathbb{U}=\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Specifically, for the car shapes depicted in Figure
3
, we utilize six viewpoints, including the front, back, left, right, top, and bottom viewpoint.
We further generate the semantic segmentation maps
\mathbb{S}=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for the projected views by manually defining the semantic parts or employing other off-the-shelf methods
(
zllrunning 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
on the 2D projection of the input 3D model. These semantic segmentation maps serve as structural guidance for the subsequent texture generator.

4.2.
Texture Generator

In this study, we employ the StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
architecture as our texture generator to produce high-fidelity textures. The texture generator
G
accepts 12 1D vectors as input style codes, with each code corresponding to a block of the model. The generator outputs 2D texture maps
\mathbb{T}
in the projected views. To obtain the final textured 3D model, we map the 2D texture maps
\mathbb{T}
onto the 3D shape via the UV maps
\mathbb{U}
.
As mentioned earlier, StyleGAN
(
Karras et al. 2019a
)
incorporates a disentangled latent code
\mathrm{w}\in\mathcal{W}
, which can be extended to a latent space
\mathcal{W}+
to provide better control and editing capabilities for the results. Various GAN inversion works
(
Abdal et al. 2019
;
Jahanian et al. 2020
;
Tov et al. 2021
;
Xu et al. 2021
)
have demonstrated that
\mathcal{W}+
is capable of reconstructing better results compared to
\mathrm{w}
. Thus, we encode all style codes in
\mathcal{W}+
.
To analyze which layers of style codes control structural attributes, such as pose, shape, semantic parts, etc., and what other layers control the style attributes, we adopt the StyleSpace
(
Wu et al. [n. d.]
)
to separate the style codes
\mathrm{w}^{full}
into two parts: the first
n
layers control structural attributes, denoted as
\mathrm{w}^{struct}
, while the remaining layers control style attributes, denoted as
\mathrm{w}^{sty}
. This explicit separation in the latent code space enables our approach to support conditional texture generation, generate textures with better style consistency, and respect the given shape semantic guidance.

4.3.
Style Code Encoders

To explicitly control the structural and style attributes of the generated textures, we utilize two encoders: the
structure encoder

E_{struct}
and the
style encoder

E_{sty}
. These encoders respectively map the input semantic segmentation map and style image into structural latent codes
\mathrm{w}^{struct}
and style latent codes
\mathrm{w}^{sty}
. We adopt the pSp
(
Richardson et al. 2021
)
encoder as our style encoder
E_{sty}
and take the
n+1^{th}
to
12^{th}
layers of the output of the
style encoder

E_{sty}
as the style latent codes
\mathrm{w}^{sty}
.
As the semantic segmentation map contains structural information of different levels of detail, we devise a coarse-to-fine architecture, as depicted in Figure
4
(a), to better preserve the semantic information, as suggested by previous works
(
Zhu et al. 2020
;
Park et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2022
)
.
Specifically, the structure encoder
E_{struct}
resizes the input segmentation maps
s_{i}
to the same size as the feature map in each layer of the texture generator
G
. The resized segmentation maps are then fed into the convolution layers, and the output feature maps are added to the results of the previous layer, before the feature maps are transformed into 1D vectors through the Map2Style
(
Richardson et al. 2021
)
block. The structure latent codes
\mathrm{w}^{struct}
are formed by all 1D vectors encoded by the structure encoder
E_{struct}
.

4.4.
Loss Function and Training Process

As illustrated in Figure
4
(b), we adopt a 3-stage training process to train our network.
First, we train the texture generator
G
using a collection of real-world images following the same training procedure and the loss function of the StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
.
Second, we train the style encoder
E_{sty}
using a collection of real-world images as the training data. Since the goal of style encoder
E_{sty}
is to find style codes that correspond to the style image, we thus calculate the reconstruction loss between the input style image
I
and the generated texture
t_{i}
.
Finally, we train the structure encoder
E_{struct}
using the segmentation maps of the same collection of real-world images as the training data. Since the input segmentation map
s_{i}
is derived from the input style image
I
, we adopt the similar reconstruction loss between the input style image
I
and the generated image
t_{i}
to train the structure encoder
E_{struct}
while the texture generator
G
and the style encoder
E_{sty}
are both frozen.

Following the approach proposed in
(
Richardson et al. 2021
)
, we used a weighted combination of several objectives as the reconstruction loss function to train the two encoders. The first objective is the pixel-wise
\ell_{2}
loss, which is defined as:

(1)

\ell_{2}(I,t_{i})=||I-t_{i}||_{2}

where
I
is the original image and
t_{i}
is the synthesized image.

To learn perceptual similarities, we also utilized the LPIPS
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
loss, which measures the distance between the feature representations of the two images, and is defined as:

(2)

\ell_{LPIPS}(I,t_{i})=||F(I)-F(t_{i})||_{2}

where
{F}
(·) denotes the perceptual feature extractor.

We also incorporated the MOCO-based similarity loss
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, which was shown to improve the quality of reconstructed images. This loss is defined as:

(3)

\ell_{MOCO}(I,t_{i})=1-dot((M(I),M(t_{i}))

where
{M}
(·) denotes the pretrained MOCO
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
network, and
{dot}
(·, ·) is a dot product operation.

In summary, the total loss function is defined as:

(4)

\ell(I,t_{i})=\lambda_{1}\ell_{2}(I,t_{i})+\lambda_{2}\ell_{LPIPS}(I,t_{i})+\lambda_{3}\ell_{MOCO}(I,t_{i})

where
\ell(I,t_{i})
is the total loss of the style encoder and the structure encoder, and
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
, and
\lambda_{3}
are the hyperparameters for weighting the loss functions.

5.1.
Experimental Setting

Implementation Details.

We implement our network using PyTorch and Python3.7 and conduct experiments on a single NVIDIA V100 with 32GB VRAM and training for 7 days.
We train both our style
E_{sty}
and structure
E_{struct}
encoders using the Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
optimizer with a batch size of 8 and a learning rate of 0.0001 for 200k iterations.
The resolution of all training images are resized to
128\times 128
.
We empirically set
\lambda_{1}=1.0
,
\lambda_{2}=0.8
,
\lambda_{3}=0.5
in Equation
4
,
n=6
,
N=6
for the ShapeNet car dataset
(
Chang et al. 2015
)
and
n=7
,
N=1
the FFHQ human face dataset
(
Karras et al. 2019b
)
in Section
4.3
and Section
4.1
.

Baselines.

To assess the effectiveness of CTGAN, we compare our method with two baselines. The first baseline is Texture Fields
(
Oechsle et al. 2019
)
, which generates textures of 3D shapes and represents them with an implicit field. For the condition setting, we retrain Texture Fields using both real-world and synthetic data to perform a fair comparison with our method. In contrast, our method is trained solely on real-world datasets to produce photo-realistic results. For the unconditional setting, we fine-tuned the Texture Fields pretrained weights using real-world images.

The second baseline we consider is LTG
(
Yu et al. 2021
)
, which learns texture generation in UV space with multiple "constant starting vectors" and discriminators. For the conditional setting, we employ pSp
(
Richardson et al. 2021
)
as the style encoder and use the encoded style code as input for LTG. For the unconditional setting, we retrain LTG on our training dataset using the official configuration. Both the conditional and unconditional settings are tested on the same data split to ensure fairness in the comparison.

Although we attempted to compare our method with Texturify
(
Siddiqui et al. 2022
)
, which generates realistic textures directly on the surface of 3D shapes by taking the 3D shape and style latent code as input, we encountered some challenges and the code of Texturify
(
Siddiqui et al. 2022
)
was not available for our comparison experiment.

Datasets.

We evaluate our method under two datasets: CompCars dataset
(
Yang et al. 2015
)
(16,000/2000/2000 for train/validation/test split) and FFHQ
(
Karras et al. 2019b
)
dataset (65,747/64/3000 for train/validation/test split). For more details on two datasets, please refer to our supplemental material.

5.3.
Unconditional Setting

With regard to the unconditional setting, we evaluate both the quantitative and qualitative performance of our model and compare it with other baselines as well.
To demonstrate that our network can generate texture without a style image, we follow the same experimental settings as the conditional setting (Section
5.2
) but replace the style latent code

Conclusions

We propose a novel conditional texture generator conditioned on the structural input and style image and adopting the StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
as the backbone.
Our generated textures are high quality and can be well-aligned with the style images.
We propose two encoders to encode the structural input and style image separately, generating the texture with view consistency.
Our method outperforms existing methods both quantitatively and visually.

Limitations.

View-based texture projection simplifies our architecture to work with StyleGAN2-ADA
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
, but it struggles with complex geometry or self-occlusion problems (left of Figure
8
) or seams at edges of different views (right of Figure
8
).
Nevertheless, our model produces realistic and view consistent results.
