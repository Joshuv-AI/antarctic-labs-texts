Verifying the inverse Laplace transform of total expected stock-outs when demand is Poisson
Ettore Settanni

Abstract
This note aims to verify a Laplace transform pair, previously published without proof, concerning the expected stock-out that may occur in a production-inventory systems when demand is Poisson, and the time horizon is finite. Stock-out, or “backlog”, is understood as the non-negative difference between cumulative demand, a stochastic variable, and production over a finite time interval. The hoped for outcome is greater clarity on how the Laplace transform pair of interest might be arrived at, and a possible improvement in terms of accuracy on the original result.



1
Introduction

In the context of production-inventory systems, a “backlog” or “stock-out” is understood to be the non-negative difference between cumulative demand and production over a time interval. When demand is probabilistic, quantifying stock-out is equivalent to estimating the number of excess demand in a given time interval via a loss function. For the “textbook” case in which demand is normally distributed see
Nahmias 2005
.

Consider the case in which production is just a given quantity
P=1,2,\dots
, and demand is a Poisson random variable with known parameter
\lambda
. In this case, previous work suggests the following relationship between “cumulative” expected backlog and its Laplace transform:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{3}}\right\}&=\frac{\lambda^{P+1}}{(P-1)!}\iiint{e^{-\lambda t}t^{P-1}(dt)^{3}}\\
&=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt+\frac{P(P+1)}{2\lambda}-\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{2\lambda}\biggl[P(P+1)\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}==}\sum_{j=0}^{P-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j}}{j!}-2P\sum_{j=0}^{P-2}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j+1}}{j!}+\sum_{j=0}^{P-3}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j+2}}{j!}\biggr]\end{split}

(1.1)

The identity in Eq.
1.1
combines,
without altering
them, Eq. 2.13 in
Grubbström & Molinder 1996
and Eqs. 7.4-7.5 in
Grubbström 1996
. Both papers propose that the expected backlog be“cumulated” so that it may contribute to an aggregate inventory cost function which, when divided by the length of a chosen time horizon, yields an average cost per time unit.

The issue of interest for this note is that neither
Grubbström & Molinder 1996
nor
Grubbström 1996
provide details on how they arrive at the final result in Eq.
1.1
in the case of a
finite
time horizon. Instead, both papers cite a common source to which they refer the reader for proof. Yet such source remains, to the best of my knowledge, unpublished.

In the remainder of this note I address the lack of accessible details about how Eq.
1.1
may be arrived at by attempting direct proof. I provide a sequence of explicit statements (lemmas) that can be demonstrated directly, and discuss possible improvements in terms of accuracy on the original work. Throughout this note I assume familiarity with key results about Laplace transforms. For the less familiar reader,
Farlow 2012
provides an accessible yet exhaustive introduction to the topic. The specialist reader interested in an overview of applications of the Laplace transform in the context of managing production-inventory systems might benefit from
Grubbström 2007
.

2
Managerial relevance

The challenges of estimating expected shortfall has received renewed interest after the disruptive events linked to a recent pandemic. For example
Sodhi & Tang 2021
rely on newsvendor-type shortfall metrics to model trade-offs between strategic stockpiling and alternative policies at the national level. Unlike the normally-distributed “textbook” case
(
Nahmias 2005
, e.g.)
, they assume that the number of people affected by virus-related disasters follow a negative-exponential probability density function.

Shortfalls are of great practical relevance in the context of clinical trial supply chains. It is not uncommon to overcompensate stock-out risk with high inventory levels to achieve service levels such that clinical trials are carried out effectively. Research on trading-off inventory overage and the risk of a clinical study experiencing shortage, often relies on the Poisson process to model key aspects of clinical trial supply, such as patient recruitment processes
(
Anisimov 2009
)
and demand of investigational medicinal product in a distribution network
(
Fleischhacker et al. 2015
, e.g.)
. Yet research rarely grounds these assumptions into extensive analysis of empirical data
(
Settanni et al. 2018
)
.

3.1
Laplace transform of backlog

By definition, a backlog
B(t)=j
occurs if, in a given time interval
t
, cumulative demand exceeds cumulative production by
j
units. In the case of interest here production
P
is a given, and demand
\bar{d}(t)
is stochastic and approximately Poisson-distributed with known parameter
\lambda t
. Then, a backlog
B(t)=j
can be re-phrased as the probability of exactly
n=P+j
demand events:

\displaystyle\left[B(t)=j\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{Pr}\left[\bar{d}(t)=n\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(\lambda t)^{n}}{n!}e^{-\lambda t},\ n=0,1,2,\dots

(3.1)

My next step is to relate the Laplace transform of a backlog to its equivalent formulation in terms of stochastic demand.

Lemma 1
.

Let
B(t)=j
be a non-negative backlog of magnitude
j\in\mathbb{N}
occurring in a time interval
t
. Let the demand over the same time interval be
\bar{d}(t)\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda t)
, and production
P\in\mathbb{N}
be a given. Then,

\mathcal{L}\left\{B(t)=j\right\}=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{{j+P}}\frac{1}{\lambda+s}

Proof.

Taking the Laplace transform of both terms in Eq.
3.1
yields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{B(t)=j\right\}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{(\lambda t)^{n}}{n!}e^{-\lambda t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}\frac{(\lambda t)^{P+j}e^{-\lambda t}}{(n)!}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda t)^{n}}{n!}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-t(s+\lambda)}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{(\lambda t)^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{n!}{(\lambda+s)^{n}}\frac{1}{(\lambda+s)}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)^{n+1}\frac{1}{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{s+\lambda}

For
n=j+P
one obtains the identity in the Lemma
∎

Remark 1
.

In the above I exploit insights from the standard Laplace pair
\mathcal{L}\left\{t^{n}\right\}=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}

(see e.g.
Farlow 2012
, p.252)
. By definition of Laplace transform,
\mathcal{L}\left\{t^{n}\right\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-st}dt
. Integrating by parts yields
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-st}dt=\frac{n}{s}\mathcal{L}\left\{t^{n-1}\right\}
. By recursion one obtains a key result deployed in Lemma
1

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n}e^{-st}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{s}\frac{n-1}{s}\frac{n-2}{s}\dots\frac{2}{s}\frac{1}{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{n!}{s^{n}}\frac{1}{s}

a result which is linked with the concept of
Gamma
function
(
Muth 1977
, p.94)
.

3.2
Role of the negative exponential density function

Although without detail about its derivation, the Laplace transform pair for a known stockout level in Lemma
1
features in
Grubbström & Molinder 1996
and in
Grubbström 1996
. In these papers the pair is often expressed in terms of another Laplace transform pair, which involves the probability density function of interarrival times.

The Poisson and negative exponential distributions are related: stating that the probability of
n
arrivals in any time interval of length
t
is
\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda t)
is equivalent to saying that the density function for the time interval
t
between two consecutive arrivals is exponentially distributed with arrival rate per unit time
\lambda

(for a proof see e.g.,
Wagner 1975
, pp.860-861)
.

To rephrase Lemma
1
the next step is to work out the Laplace transform pair for the negative exponential density function. This well-known pair is often presented as self-evident – see e.g.,
Muth 1977
,
Grubbström 2007

Grubbström 1996
, and
Grubbström & Molinder 1996
:

Lemma 2
.

Let
f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}
and
\mathcal{L}\left\{f(t)\right\}=\tilde{f}(s)
. Then,

\tilde{f}(s)=\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}

Proof.

Taking the Laplace transform of both sides of
f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}
one obtains:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{f(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t(s+\lambda)}dt

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{-e^{-t(s+\lambda)}}{s+\lambda}\Big|_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(s)

which completes the proof
∎

Remark 2
.

Based on the above one deduces, after some algebra, that:

1-\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\right\}=\frac{s}{s+\lambda}

Corollary 1
.

The Laplace transform of backlog obtained in Lemma
1
can be re-written combining Lemma
2
and Remark
2
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{\mathrm{Pr}\left[B(t)=j\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\right\}^{n}\frac{1}{s}\left(1-\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}(s)^{n}\frac{1}{s}\left[1-\tilde{f}(s)\right]

One will notice that in Lemma
1
and, equivalently, in Corollary
1
the Laplace transform of the negative exponential density function is raised to the
n
–th power.
Muth 1977
shows that this exponentiation is equivalent to transforming the expected value of the sum of
n
random variables with the same exponential distribution.

Proposition 1

(
Muth 1977
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be continuous, independent random variables having the same probability density function
f_{X}(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}
. Let a new random variable
Y
be the sum
Y=X_{1}+X_{2}+\dots+X_{n}
. It follows that its Laplace transform is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{f_{Y}(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=E\left[e^{-sX_{1}}\right]E\left[e^{-sX_{2}}\right]\dots E\left[e^{-sX_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)^{n}

The above proposition is illustrated in Appendix
A
for the case
n=2
.

3.3
Laplace transform of
expected
backlog

In this section I move on to the
expected
backlog. The notion of expected value of a random variable
(
Wackerly et al. 2014
)
is key for demonstrating the following Laplace transform pair:

Lemma 3
.

Let
E[B(t)]
be the expected backlog occurring in a time interval
t
. As before, let the demand over the same time interval be
\bar{d}(t)\sim\mathrm{Pois}(\lambda t)
, and let production
P\in\mathbb{N}
be a given. Then,

\mathcal{L}\left\{E\left[B(t)\right]\right\}=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{2}}

Proof.

Following the hint in
Grubbström & Molinder 1996
and in
Grubbström 1996
, let
j=0,1,2,\dots
be a random variable whose probability density has the Laplace transform given in Lemma
1
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{E\left[B(t)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\left[\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P+j}\frac{1}{\lambda+s}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\ \mathcal{L}\left\{\mathrm{Pr}\left[\bar{d}(t)=j\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\mathcal{L}\left\{E\left[\bar{d}(t)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda t\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-st}}{s}dt

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{2}}

which completes the proof
∎

Remark 3
.

In Lemma
3
I adapt a standard result for the expected value of a Poisson-distributed random variable
(e.g.
Wackerly et al. 2014
, pp.134-135)
to the specific case of the total arrival of demand events
\bar{d}(t)
:

\begin{split}E[\bar{d(t)}]&=E\left[\frac{(\lambda t)^{n}e^{-\lambda t}}{n!}\right]\\
&=\lambda t\end{split}

which is analogous to
Grubbström 1996
and
Grubbström 1998
.

Corollary 2
.

The result in Lemma
3
can be rewritten using notation introduced in Lemma
2
and Remark
2
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{E\left[B(t)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\cfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P+1}\left(s\cfrac{s}{\lambda+s}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{f}(s)^{P+1}}{s(1-\tilde{f}(s))}

The notation introduced in Corollary
2
is frequently adopted in the literature when referring to the Laplace transform pair in Lemma
3

(
Grubbström & Molinder 1996
,
Grubbström 1996
,
Grubbström 1998
,
Grubbström 1999
, see e.g.,)
.

The next step is to show the equivalence between the Laplace transform pair in Lemma.
3
and the inner double integral on the right-hand side of in Eq.
1.1
:

Lemma 4
.

Let the Laplace transform of expected backlog be as defined in Lemma
3
. The corresponding time-domain expression for
E\left[B(t)\right]
is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\lambda t-P+e^{-\lambda t}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}P\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}-\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}i\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}\right]

Proof.

Recall that
(
Muth 1977
, p.198)
:

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)^{n}\right\}=\lambda^{n}\frac{t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-\lambda t}

(3.2)

and that
(
Wagner 1975
, p.859)
:

\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{n}\frac{x^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-\lambda x}dx=1-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{\lambda^{j}e^{-\lambda t}}{j!}t^{j}

(3.3)

Also recall the standard Laplace transform pair:
\mathcal{L}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}f(u)du\right\}=\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}\left\{f(t)\right\}

(see e.g.
Muth 1977
, p.65)
.
Then, substituting Eq.
3.2
and
3.3
where appropriate in Lemma
3
yields:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{1}{s^{2}}\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda^{P}\frac{t^{P-1}}{(P-1)!}e^{-\lambda t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{P}\frac{u^{P-1}}{(P-1)!}e^{-\lambda u}du\right]du\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\left[1-\sum_{j=0}^{P-1}\frac{\lambda^{j}e^{-\lambda u}}{j!}u^{j}\right]du\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\biggl\{\lambda t-\lambda\biggl[\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda^{0}e^{-\lambda u}}{0!}u^{0}du+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda u}}{1!}udu+

\displaystyle\dots+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\lambda^{P-1}e^{-\lambda u}}{(P-1)!}u^{P-1}du\biggr]\biggr\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda t-P+e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j}}{j!}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda t-P+e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}(n-i)\frac{(\lambda t)^{j}}{j!}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{\lambda t-P+e^{-\lambda t}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}P\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}-\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}i\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}\right]\right\}

Taking the inverse transform of both sides produces the sought identity
∎

Remark 4
.

The proof of Lemma
4
features a double sum with linked indices, which was simplified based on the identity
\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i}f(j)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(n-i)f(i)
. For more details on this identify see Appendix
B
.

3.4
Laplace transform of
cumulative
expected backlog

Taken together, Lemmas
3
and
4
provide important intermediate results towards understanding how
both sides
of Eq.
1.1
might be arrived at. The Laplace transform of
cumulative expected
backlog, which corresponds to the left-hand side of Eq.
1.1

3
invoking a standard result about the transform of an integral
(e.g.
Farlow 2012

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}\left\{E[\bar{B}(t)]\right\}&=\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}\left\{E[B(t)]\right\}\\
&=\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{2}}\end{split}

(3.4)

Lemma 5
.

Let the image function of the cumulative expected backlog in the complex frequency domain be as in Eq.
3.4
. The original (primitive) function in the time domain is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+s}\right)^{P}\frac{\lambda}{s^{3}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt-\frac{P(P+1)}{2\lambda}-\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{2\lambda}\biggl[P(P+1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}-2P\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i+1}}{i!}+\sum_{i=0}^{P-3}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i+2}}{i!}-4P\frac{(\lambda t)^{P-1}}{(P-2)!}\biggr]

Proof.

Invoking the standard Laplace transform pair
\mathcal{L}\left\{\int_{0}^{t}f(u)du\right\}=\frac{1}{s}\mathcal{L}\left\{f(t)\right\}
, I relate Eq.
3.4
and Lemma.
4
as follows:

\displaystyle E[\bar{B}(t)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda x-P+e^{-\lambda x}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}P\frac{(\lambda x)^{i}}{i!}-\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}i\frac{(\lambda x)^{i}}{i!}\right]dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt+\frac{P}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\left[-\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}e^{-\lambda t}+\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{i}\frac{x^{i-1}}{(i-1)!}e^{-\lambda x}dx\right]

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}=}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\biggl[-i\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}e^{-\lambda t}+i\int_{0}^{t}\lambda^{i}\frac{x^{i-1}}{(i-1)!}e^{-\lambda x}dx\biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt-\frac{P}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}e^{-\lambda t}+\frac{P}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\biggl[1-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j}}{j!}e^{-\lambda t}\biggr]

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}=}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}i\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}e^{-\lambda t}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}i\biggl[1-\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j}}{j!}e^{-\lambda t}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt-\frac{P(P+1)}{2\lambda}-\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}\biggl[P\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}-\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i+1}}{i!}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}=}+P\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}(P-1+i)\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}\biggr]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-1}i\left[\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^{j}}{j!}e^{-\lambda t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt-\frac{P(P+1)}{2\lambda}-\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}\biggl[P^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}-(P+1)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}=}\sum_{i=0}^{P-3}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i+1}}{i!}+\frac{(\lambda t)^{P-1}}{(P-1)!}\biggr]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}\biggl[\frac{P(P-1)}{2}-\frac{i(i+1)}{2}\biggr]\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}e^{-\lambda t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda t^{2}}{2}-Pt-\frac{P(P+1)}{2\lambda}-\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{2\lambda}\biggl[P(P+1)\sum_{i=0}^{P-2}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i}}{i!}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}=}-2P\sum_{i=0}^{P-3}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i+1}}{i!}+\sum_{i=0}^{P-4}\frac{(\lambda t)^{i+2}}{i!}+2\frac{(\lambda t)^{P-1}}{(P-1)!}\biggr]

Closing remarks

The proposed verification process yields a result which is very close to, but not the same as the alternatives summarised in Tab.

. Specifically, Lemma

, differ from Eq.

, for one additional term which seems to be absent in the original references

It is worth noting, however, the satisfying verification of intermediate results concerning the Laplace transform of

in Section

formulates, without proving it, a theorem akin to Lemma

Grubbström 1998

Whilst it is not the ambition of this note to claim a definitive answer, it is hoped that the process described brings greater clarity on how the Laplace transform pair of interest might be arrived at. Appendix A
Transform of sums of random variables

This section is meant to illustrate Proposition
1
. Following
Muth 1977
, I show that (1) the Laplace transform of a random variable
X
can be expressed in terms of expected value of a function of such variable; and (2) the density function of the sum of two independent random variables corresponds to the
convolution
of the density functions of these random variables.
In particular, the expected value of a function
g
of a random variable
X
is, by definition:

E[g(X)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)f_{X}(t)dt

(A.1)

For
g(X)=e^{-sX}
we can write

E[e^{-sX}]=\mathcal{L}\left\{f_{X}(t)\right\}

(A.2)

If, for example,
X
and
Y
are two independent random variables with probability density function
f_{X}(t)
and
f_{Y}(t)
, respectively, then their sum
Z=X+Y
has probability density function
f_{X+Y}(t)
, the Laplace transform of the density function of
Y
is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{f_{Z}(t)\right\}=E\left[e^{-s(X+Y)}\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[e^{-sX}\right]E\left[e^{-sY}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left\{f_{X}(t)\right\}\mathcal{L}\left\{f_{Y}(t)\right\}=\tilde{f}_{X}\tilde{f}_{Y}

(A.3)

It follows that

\displaystyle f_{X+Y}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\tilde{f}_{X}(s)\tilde{f}_{Y}(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=f_{X}(t)\ast f_{Y}(t)

(A.4)

If
X
and
Y
follow the negative exponential density function i.e.
f_{X}(t)=f_{Y}(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}
, the Laplace transform of their sum
Z=X+Y
is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left\{f_{Z}(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=E\left[e^{-sX}\right]E\left[e^{-sY}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)^{2}

The inverse Laplace transform of a 2-fold convoluted negative exponential density function is

\displaystyle f_{Z}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\ast\lambda e^{-\lambda t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda e^{-\lambda(t-x)}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}1dx

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}te^{-\lambda t}

which is an instance of the more general result
(
Muth 1977
, p.198)
:

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\right)^{n}\right\}=\lambda^{n}\frac{t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}e^{-\lambda t}

(A.5)

While the illustrative example involves just two variables, it shows the equivalence between raising the transform of an exponential density function to the
n
–th power and taking the Laplace transform of the expected value of the sum of
n
random variables that are exponentially distributed with the same parameter
\lambda
. A more general version of this result features in Lemma
1
, the Laplace transform of backlogs.

Appendix B
Double summations

Throughout the replication process described in Section

, I’ve encountered double summations where the inner index is linked to the outer one, and needed simplifying. I was unable to find ready-to-use results about double sums, which forced me to deduce the following equivalences from “tabulating” the double summations for small values of
n
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i}f(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(n-i)f(i)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}f(j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(n-1-i)f(i)

(B.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i\left[\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}f(j)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\left[\frac{n(n-1)}{2}-\frac{j(j+1)}{2}\right]f(j)

(B.3)
