Structure theorems for braided Hopf algebras
Craig Westerland

Abstract
We develop versions of the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt and Cartier-Milnor-Moore theorems in the setting of braided Hopf algebras. To do so, we introduce new analogues of a Lie algebra in the setting of a braided monoidal category, using the notion of a braided operad.



The purpose of this paper is to establish analogues of the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt and Cartier-Milnor-Moore theorems
[
Car62
,
MM65
]
in the context of
braided
Hopf algebras, that is: Hopf algebra objects in a linear braided monoidal category. Recall that Cartier-Milnor-Moore identify primitively generated Hopf algebras with universal enveloping algebras of Lie algebras, and Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt give a filtration on these algebras whose associated graded is a symmetric algebra. Results of this sort in the braided setting have been established by Kharchenko and Ardizzoni in
[
Kha07
,
Ard11a
,
Ard12a
,
Ard12b
]
. Our work, while based on a different foundation and having a somewhat different formulation, owes a lot to these papers.

Central to the Cartier-Milnor-Moore theorem is the fact that the primitives
P(A)
of a Hopf algebra
A
in the category of vector spaces form a Lie algebra, or more accurately, a sub-Lie algebra of
A
. One can see this easily: if
a,b\in P(A)
, then

\displaystyle\Delta(ab-ba)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a\otimes 1+1\otimes a)(b\otimes 1+1\otimes b)-(b\otimes 1+1\otimes b)(a\otimes 1+1\otimes a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ab-ba)\otimes 1+(a\otimes b+b\otimes a)-(b\otimes a+a\otimes b)+1\otimes(ab-ba)

The cross terms cancel, showing that
[a,b]=ab-ba
is primitive.

Notice that to achieve this cancellation, we have used the fact that the multiplication in
A\otimes A
gives
(1\otimes b)(a\otimes 1)=a\otimes b
, owing to the fact that the braiding on
A\otimes A
is simply the permutation of the two factors. A similar argument works in a general linear symmetric monoidal category, but not in a braided category where the braiding is not an involution.

Consequently, the same argument that
P(A)
is a Lie algebra fails in the braided context. A great deal of the technical work in this paper is spent developing the notion of a
braided primitive algebra

. This structure is encoded by the data of an action of a

Operads govern algebraic objects which are equipped with
n

symmetric
operads, parameterizing operations in a symmetric monoidal category. The more general concept of a braided operad was introduced by Fiedorowicz in
[
Fie

; these are the correct gadgets to encode algebraic structures on objects in braided monoidal categories.

Our understanding of the operad
\BrPrim
is far from complete. For many familiar symmetric operads, it is possible to give a presentation of the operad. For instance, the Lie operad is generated by the antisymmetric Lie bracket, subject to the Jacobi identity. We can offer little of the sort for
\BrPrim
at this point; see section
5.3
for partial results. However,
\BrPrim
is characterized by the fact that the free braided primitive algebra on a braided vector space
V
is the space of primitives in the tensor algebra
T(V)
:

\BrPrim[V]\cong P(T(V)).

This is a braided analogue of the familiar fact that in (characteristic zero, linear) symmetric monoidal categories, the free Lie algebra on
V
is isomorphic to the primitives in
T(V)
.

The primitives
P(A)
sit inside a larger space
P_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(A)\subseteq A
whose definition (in section
7.1
) requires some care, but can loosely be described as “all of the elements of
A
that become primitive after iteratively quotienting by decomposable primitives in
A

9.2

\mathfrak{W}_{*}

\BrPrim

For a large class of braided operads
\mathcal{C}
(including
\BrPrim
and
\mathfrak{W}_{*}
), we introduce a universal enveloping algebra functor
U_{\mathcal{C}}
in Definition
3.13
. Our analogue of the Cartier-Milnor-Moore theorem is:

Theorem 1
.

If
A
is a primitively generated, finitely braided Hopf algebra over a field
k
of characteristic zero, there is an algebra isomorphism

\mu:U_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(P_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(A))\to A.

Here, “finitely braided” means that the action of the braid group on tensor powers of
A
factors through a finite quotient. Theorem
1
can be promoted to an isomorphism of Hopf algebras if one incorporates the diagonal on
P_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(A)
. In that setting, this extends to an equivalence between the categories of primitively generated, finitely braided Hopf algebras and Woronowicz algebras over
\mathfrak{W}_{*}
equipped with a diagonal.

Classically, the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem equips the enveloping algebra
U(L)
of a Lie algebra
L
with a Hopf filtration by powers of
L=P(U(L))
, and gives an isomorphism
U(L)^{\gr}\cong\Sym(L)
between the associated graded of the filtration with the symmetric algebra on
L
. We establish an analogue in the braided setting.

If
L
is a Woronowicz algebra equipped with a diagonal, there is a filtration on
U_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(L)
by powers of the primitives
P(L)=P(U_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(L))
. Unlike the classical setting, it is possible that more primitives are produced in the associated graded Hopf algebra
U_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(L)^{\gr}
. We may, however, iterate this procedure, producing a graded Hopf algebra
U_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(L)^{(\infty)}
. Our analogue of the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt theorem is:

Theorem 2
.

Let
L
be a Woronowicz algebra equipped with a diagonal which makes it primitively generated. There is an isomorphism of graded, braided Hopf algebras

U_{\mathfrak{W}_{*}}(L)^{(\infty)}\to\mathfrak{B}(P(L^{(\infty)})).

Here
L^{(\infty)}
is the Woronowicz algebra produced in a similar fashion by iterating the associated graded under powers of the primitives. Further,
P(L^{(\infty)})
is its space of primitives, and
\mathfrak{B}(P(L^{(\infty)}))
denotes the associated
Nichols algebra
– this is the braided Hopf algebra analogue of a symmetric algebra.

We actually prove versions of Theorems
1
and
2
for a large class of operads
\mathcal{C}
; see Theorem
7.10
and Proposition
8.14
for precise statements. The Woronowicz ideal
\mathfrak{W}
is the smallest operad
\mathcal{C}
that we have identified for which these results hold, but still very large. It is very plausible that there is a class of simpler (or at least smaller) operads
\mathcal{C}
for which these structure theorems hold; see Question
9.4
for specifics on how this might be the case.

Theorem
1
is closely related to the main result of
[
Ard11a
]
. The essential difference between our construction and Ardizzoni’s is that our enveloping algebra is defined so that it has a universal property (see Proposition
3.15
). As a result, it is constructed in a single step rather than as a limit (although the proof of Lemma
9.1
makes it clear these constructions are equivalent), and has good functorial properties. A result similar to Theorem
2
is proved by Ardizzoni in Theorem 5.2 of
[
Ard12b
]
in the special case that the enveloping algebra is cosymmetric (extending
[
Ard12a
]
in combinatorial rank one). For a more detailed comparison of these results, see section
10
.

Background and technical lemmas

1.1
Braid groups and the Artin representation

Recall that the
n\textsuperscript{th}
braid group

B_{n}
is presented by

B_{n}:=\langle\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n-1}\;|\;\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{i+1}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},\;\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i},\mbox{if $|i-j|>1$}\rangle.

Pictorially,
\sigma_{i}
is the braid which permutes the
i\textsuperscript{th}
and
i+1\textsuperscript{st}
endpoints, where the strand beginning at the latter passes over the one beginning at the former. Additionally, we interpret
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
to be the trivial group.

There is a surjective homomorphism
B_{n}\to S_{n}
to the symmetric group. The symmetric group has the same presentation, with additionally
\sigma_{i}^{2}=1
; here
\sigma_{i}
corresponds to the transposition
(i,i+1)
. This map carries a braid to the permutation of its endpoints. The
Matsumoto section

S_{n}\to B_{n},\;\;\;\tau\mapsto\widetilde{\tau}

of this projection is a one-sided inverse; it is merely a map of sets and not a group homomorphism. If we write
\tau\in S_{n}
as a minimal length word in the transpositions
(i,i+1)
,
\widetilde{\tau}\in B_{n}
is gotten by replacing each of these with the braid
\sigma_{i}
.

The braid groups have a geometric interpretation:
B_{n}
is isomorphic to the mapping class group of diffeomorphisms of the
n
-punctured unit disk
D^{2}_{n}
which fix the boundary circle pointwise. This gives rise to an action of
B_{n}
on
\pi_{1}(D^{2}_{n},1)
.

We may (non-canonically) identify
\pi_{1}(D^{2}_{n},1)
with the free group
F_{n}=\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\rangle
by picking as generators loops
x_{1},\dots x_{n}
based at 1 which encircle only one puncture. This action is faithful, and Artin
[
Art47
]
proved that
B_{n}
may be identified with the subgroup of
\Aut(F_{n})
consisting of automorphisms of
F_{n}
which carry the generators
x_{i}
to conjugates of each other, and which preserves the product
x_{1}\dots x_{n}
. Explicitly, the action of
\sigma_{i}
fixes
x_{j}
for
j\neq i,i+1
, and

\sigma_{i}(x_{i})=x_{i+1}\;\mbox{ and }\;\sigma_{i}(x_{i+1})=x_{i+1}^{-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}=:x_{i}^{x_{i+1}}.

Consider the
n
-fold product
F_{n}^{\times n}
; this may be identified with the set
\End(F_{n})
of self-maps of
F_{n}
:

F_{n}^{\times n}=\Hom(F_{n},F_{n})=\End(F_{n}).

Let
B_{n}\leq\Aut(F_{n})
act on
\End(F_{n})
by pre-composition. Identifying this set with
F_{n}^{\times n}
, the action on tuples
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in F_{n}^{\times n}
is given by the Hurwitz representation:

\sigma_{i}(g_{i})=g_{i+1}\;\mbox{ and }\;\sigma_{i}(g_{i+1})=g_{i}^{g_{i+1}}.

Define

\mathcal{O}_{n}:=B_{n}\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\subseteq F_{n}^{\times n}

to be the orbit of our chosen (ordered) set of generators. That is,
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is the set of
n
-tuples
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
such that there exists an element
\gamma\in B_{n}
with
\gamma(x_{i})=g_{i}
for all
i
.

Proposition 1.1
.

\mathcal{O}_{n}
is a free, transitive
B_{n}
-set.

Proof.

The action on
\mathcal{O}_{n}
is transitive by construction. Notice that any
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
must also be an ordered set of generators of
F_{n}
. If
\delta\in B_{n}
fixes
(g_{1},\dots g_{n})
, it also acts trivially on the subgroup generated by
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{n}\}
, which is all of
F_{n}
. Thus
\delta
is the unit in
\Aut(F_{n})
, and hence in
B_{n}
, so the action is free.

∎

1.2
Braided vector spaces and braided monoidal categories

Let
k
be a field. A
braided vector space

V
over
k
is a vector space equipped with an automorphism
3
3

3

Some authors do not insist that
\sigma
be invertible; we will always require this to be the case.

\sigma:V\otimes V\to V\otimes V

which satisfies the braid equation on
V^{\otimes 3}
:

(\sigma\otimes\mbox{id})\circ(\mbox{id}\otimes\sigma)\circ(\sigma\otimes\mbox{id})=(\mbox{id}\otimes\sigma)\circ(\sigma\otimes\mbox{id})\circ(\mbox{id}\otimes\sigma).

Braided vector spaces form a category, where the morphisms are linear maps
f:V\to W
which satisfy
(f\otimes f)\circ\sigma_{V}=\sigma_{W}\circ(f\otimes f)
.

Example 1.2
.

A common example of a braided vector space comes from taking
G
to be a group, and
V:=k[G]
to be its group ring. We define the braiding
\sigma
on generators of
V\otimes V\cong k[G\times G]
by

\sigma(g\otimes h)=h\otimes g^{h}

where
g^{h}=h^{-1}gh
. Notice that if
c\subseteq G
is a conjugation-closed subset, then this action restricts to make
kc
into a braided vector space. Such
V
are called braided vector spaces of
rack type
. More generally, we may twist racks by 2-dimensional rack cocycles to obtain variants on this construction (see
[
AG03
]
in particular for details).

A general method for producing braided vector spaces is as follows. Let
(\mathbf{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1})
be a braided monoidal category equipped with a strongly monoidal forgetful functor

U:(\mathbf{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1})\to(\Vect_{k},\otimes,k)

to the category of
k
-vector spaces equipped with the usual tensor product (note that we do
not
insist that the braiding be preserved by
U
). Then for any object
X\in\mathbf{C}
,
U(X)
supports the structure of a braided vector space; here,
\sigma:U(X)\otimes U(X)\to U(X)\otimes U(X)
is given by
U
applied to the braiding in
\mathbf{C}
.

Henceforth, we will suppress the use of
U
in notating braided vector spaces gotten this way.

Definition 1.3
.

If
\Gamma
is a Hopf algebra over
k
, the category of (right-right)
Yetter-Drinfeld modules
for
\Gamma
is written as
\mathcal{YD}^{\Gamma}_{\Gamma}
. This consists of right
\Gamma
-modules
M
which are simultaneously right
\Gamma
-comodules in such a fashion that

\delta(m\cdot\gamma)=m_{(0)}\cdot\gamma_{(2)}\otimes S(\gamma_{(1)})m_{(1)}\gamma_{(3)}

using Sweedler notation for the diagonal of
M
and
\Gamma
, whose antipode is
S
. The braiding
\sigma:M\otimes N\to N\otimes M
is given by

\sigma(m\otimes n)=n_{(0)}\otimes(m\cdot n_{(1)}).

Example 1.4
.

Let
G
be a group; we will write
\mathcal{YD}_{G}^{G}
for the category of Yetter-Drinfeld modules for the group ring
k[G]
. This is our main example of a braided (but not necessarily symmetric) monoidal category. Unwinding the definition in this case, we see that objects in this category are right
k[G]
-modules
X
which decompose into a direct sum
X=\oplus_{g}X_{g}
over
g\in G
in such a fashion that
X_{g}\cdot h=X_{g^{h}}
.

If we take homogenous elements
a\in X_{g}
and
b\in X_{h}
, the braiding is given by

\sigma(a\otimes b)=b\otimes(a\cdot h).

The braided vector spaces of Example
1.2
are Yetter-Drinfeld modules for
G
; they are summands of the tautological such module,
k[G]
.

We will write
\fin({\mathcal{YD}}^{\Gamma}_{\Gamma})
for the full subcategory of Yetter-Drinfeld modules whose underlying
k
-vector space is finite dimensional. This is an example of a braided fusion category:

Definition 1.5
.

A braided monoidal, abelian category
(\mathbf{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1})
is

•

k
-linear
if
\mathbf{C}
is enriched over the category of
k
-vector spaces, for a field
4
4

4

Often taken to be algebraically closed, and of characteristic zero.

k
, and

•

semisimple
if every object is a direct sum of simple objects.

\mathbf{C}
is a
braided fusion category
if these conditions hold, and if, further, it is
rigid
(every object has a left and right dual), has finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects, finite dimensional morphism spaces, and
\End_{\mathbf{C}}(\mathbf{1})=k
.

See
[
ENO05
,
EGNO15
,
Mei22
]
for more background on fusion categories. With the exception of this and the next section, we will not need this notion beyond the examples it provides.

Example 1.6
.

If
k
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and
\Gamma
is finite dimensional and semisimple, then
\fin(\mathcal{YD}_{\Gamma}^{\Gamma})
is a braided fusion category. Recall that
\fin(\mathcal{YD}_{\Gamma}^{\Gamma})
may be identified with the category of finite dimensional modules for the Drinfeld double
D(\Gamma)
. This Hopf algebra is finite-dimensional and semisimple if
\Gamma
is
[
Rad93

, so it has finitely many simple modules, all of which are finite dimensional.

The vast majority of our results can be phrased in terms of
k
-linear, abelian, braided monoidal categories
\mathbf{C}
; occasionally we will require that
\mathbf{C}
is semisimple.

1.3
Finiteness conditions

If
V
is an object in a braided category
\mathbf{C}
, then we may define an action of
B_{n}
on
V^{\otimes n}
via

\sigma_{i}\mapsto\mbox{id}^{\otimes i-1}\otimes\sigma\otimes\mbox{id}^{\otimes n-i-1}

This leads a definition explored in
[
NR11
]
and related papers:

Definition 1.7
.

An object
V
in
\mathbf{C}
is
finitely braided
if, for each
n
, the action of
B_{n}
on
V^{\otimes n}
factors through a finite quotient. The category
\mathbf{C}
has
property
F
if all of its objects are finitely braided.

Naidu and Rowell conjecture that a braided fusion category
\mathbf{C}
has property
F
precisely when the Frobenius-Perron dimension of
\mathbf{C}
is an integer. In
[
GN21
]
, Green and Nikshych show that if
\mathbf{C}
is weakly group-theoretical, then it has property
F
. In particular, this holds for categories of Yetter-Drinfeld modules for forms of finite groups; we include a brief proof of this fact here:

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finite dimensional Hopf algebra with the property that the basechange to the algebraic closure

\Gamma\otimes_{k}\overline{k}\cong\overline{k}[G]

is a (finite) group ring. Then
\fin(\mathcal{YD}_{\Gamma}^{\Gamma})
has property
F.

We recall from the theorem of Cartier-Gabriel (e.g., Theorem 3.8.2 of
[
Car07
]
) that if
\car(k)=0
, the assumption that
\Gamma
is a form of a finite group ring is equivalent to it being cocommutative.

Proof.

Let
X\in\fin(\mathcal{YD}_{\Gamma}^{\Gamma})
, and consider the
\overline{k}[G]
-module
\overline{X}:=X\otimes_{k}\overline{k}
. Since
X
is finite dimensional,
\overline{X}
admits a finite, homogenous (with respect to the
G
-grading),
G
-invariant generating set. For instance, we may pick an arbitrary homogenous basis for
\overline{X}
, and take its orbit under
G
.

Let
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\}
be such a generating set. Then elements of the form
x_{i_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes x_{i_{n}}
form a generating set for
\overline{X}^{\otimes n}
. Further, the action of
B_{n}
preserves this set of generators by inspection of the formula for the braiding in
\mathcal{YD}_{G}^{G}
. Thus its action on
\overline{X}^{\otimes n}
factors through a subgroup of
S_{d^{n}}
, the symmetric group on
d^{n}
elements.

We conclude that the action of
B_{n}
on

\overline{X}^{\otimes_{\overline{k}}n}\cong X^{\otimes_{k}n}\otimes_{k}\overline{k}

factors through a finite quotient. But since this action is induced from the action on
X^{\otimes_{k}n}
, it must be that that action factors through the same finite quotient.

∎

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
\mathbf{C}
be a
k
-linear, abelian, braided monoidal category, and let
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{C}
be a finitely braided, semisimple subcategory with finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects. Then any object of
\mathbf{C}
which may be written as a filtered colimit of objects in
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
is finitely braided.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{C}
is abelian, colimits can be written as coequalizers of maps between coproducts (direct sums). So it suffices to show that an arbitrary direct sum of objects in
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
is finitely braided. Let
X
be such a direct sum. Since
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
is semisimple, we may in fact write
X
as a direct sum of simple objects. There are finitely many such objects; call them
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{m}
. Then we may write
X^{\otimes n}
as a direct sum of objects of the form

Y_{I}:=Y_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes Y_{i_{n}}

for
I\in\{1,\dots,m\}^{\times n}
. Note that there are only finitely many possibilities for
I
.

Since objects in
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
are finitely braided, the action of the pure braid group
PB_{n}
on
Y_{I}
trivializes on a finite index, normal subgroup
K_{I}\leq PB_{n}
. Define
K_{n}
to be a finite index, characteristic subgroup of
PB_{n}
which is contained in all of the
K_{I}
(this is possible since
PB_{n}
is finitely generated). Then
K_{n}
is normal in
B_{n}
, and the action of
B_{n}
on
X^{\otimes n}
factors through the finite group
B_{n}/K_{n}
.

∎

Example 1.10
.

If
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
is as in the previous Proposition, then the ind-completion
\Ind(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})
has property
F
. Recall that
\Ind(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})
is the universal example to which Proposition

all colimits of filtered diagrams in

.

Proposition 1.11
.

If
V\in\mathbf{C}
is finitely braided, so is any finite tensor power of
V
.

Proof.

For any
n
and
m
, the action of
B_{n}
on
(V^{\otimes m})^{\otimes n}
is through the latter’s identification with
V^{\otimes mn}
and the cabling homomorphism
B_{n}\hookrightarrow B_{mn}
. Since the action of

1.4
Finitely braided vector spaces detect the braid group

Proposition 1.12
.

The map

k[B_{n}]\to\prod_{V}\End(V^{\otimes n})

is injective, where
V
ranges over all isomorphism classes of finitely braided vector spaces. In fact, this result holds upon restriction to braided vector spaces
V=k[Q]
of rack type associated to a finite group
Q
.

Proof.

We first show that
k[B_{n}]
injects into
\End(V^{\otimes n})
, when
V
is an infinitely braided vector space. Specifically, let
V=k[F_{n}]
be the braided vector space of rack type associated to the free group on
n
elements. Then there is an isomorphism of vector spaces

V^{\otimes n}\cong k[F_{n}^{\times n}].

Notice that this is a permutation representation:
B_{n}
acts on
F_{n}^{\times n}
by

\sigma_{i}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})=(g_{1},\dots,g_{i-1},g_{i+1},g_{i}^{g_{i+1}},g_{i+2},\dots,g_{n}).

This is the same action of
B_{n}
on
F_{n}^{\times n}=\End(F_{n})
as described in section
1.1
by pre-composition with mapping classes. Therefore
V^{\otimes n}
contains the vector space
k[\mathcal{O}_{n}]
as a sub-representation. Since this is a permutation representation and the action on its basis is free,
k[B_{n}]
injects into
\End(V^{\otimes n})
.

Now,
V=k[F_{n}]
is evidently not a finitely braided vector space. However, for any finite quotient
Q
of
F_{n}
,
V_{Q}:=k[Q]
is finitely braided. As before, we may regard
F_{n}^{\times n}
as
\End(F_{n})
. Similarly,
V_{Q}^{\otimes n}=k[Q^{\times n}]
, and
Q^{\times n}=\Hom(F_{n},Q)
.

Consider an arbitrary nonzero element

\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\gamma_{i}\in k[B_{n}].

The previous argument ensures that there is some endomorphism
f:F_{n}\to F_{n}
for which
\alpha(f)=\sum a_{i}[f\circ\gamma_{i}]\in k[\End(F_{n})]
is nonzero. Define a subset

T=\{f(\gamma_{i}(x_{j}))\;|\;i=1,\dots,m,\,j=1,\dots,n\}\subseteq F_{n}.

Since
F_{n}
is residually finite, there is some finite quotient
Q
of
F_{n}
into which
T
injects. Let
q\circ f\in\Hom(F_{n},Q)
be the composite of
f
with the quotient map
q:F_{n}\to Q
; then by construction

\alpha(q\circ f)=\sum a_{i}[q\circ f\circ\gamma_{i}]\in k[\Hom(F_{n},Q)]

is nonzero.

Therefore the image of
\alpha
in
\End(k[Q]^{\otimes n})
is nonzero, and so
k[B_{n}]

2.1
Definitions

We recall some of the basic notions in the subject; strictly better references are
[

Following Majid in
[

]

\mathbf{C}

and diagonal

\mu_{A\otimes A}:(A\otimes A)\otimes(A\otimes A)\to A\otimes A

is the composite
(\mu_{A}\otimes\mu_{A})\circ(\mbox{id}_{A}\otimes\sigma\otimes\mbox{id}_{A})
.

A braided bialgebra

is a
braided Hopf algebra
if, additionally, it admits an

, a map

with the property that

\mu\circ(S\otimes\mbox{id}_{A})\circ\Delta=\eta\circ\epsilon=\mu\circ(\mbox{id}_{A}\otimes S)\circ\Delta.

(2.1)

Equivalently,
S
serves as an inverse to the identity in the convolution product on
\End(A)
. We will refer to Hopf algebras in symmetric monoidal categories as
symmetric Hopf algebras
unless the meaning is clear.

Remark 2.1
.

In
[
Tak00
]
, Takeuchi gives an alternate definition of braided bialgebras and Hopf algebras as braided vector spaces with suitably compatible products, coproducts, and so on. If
(\mathbf{C},\otimes,\mathbf{1})
is a braided category and
U:\mathbf{C}\to\Vect_{k}
is a strongly monoidal functor, and if
A\in\mathbf{C}
is a braided Hopf algebra in the sense of Majid, then
U(A)
is a braided Hopf algebra in the sense of Takeuchi. However, it is not the case that every braided category
\mathbf{C}
admits such a fiber functor

[
Ost03
]
). As such neither Majid’s nor Takeuchi’s braided Hopf algebras give a more general notion than the other.

In this paper, we use the braided categorical definition almost exclusively, as it seems to us that the structural results that we prove are more fundamentally statements about the Hopf algebras, and not their underlying braided vector space. That said, all of the constructions that we perform make perfect sense for braided Hopf algebras in Takeuchi’s sense. Indeed, working with objects which have an underlying vector space makes these ideas more transparent. We will endeavor to present definitions in terms of elements as well as in categorical terms when that is clarifying.

Finally, some of our results (Theorem
5.5
, Proposition
5.22
) about braided operads become biconditional when formulated in terms of braided vector spaces, thanks to the fact that finitely braided vector spaces detect the braid group. The converse statements don’t hold in the categorical setting, as
\mathbf{C}
may simply not be a rich enough category for the analog of Proposition
1.12
to hold.

Recall that the set of
grouplike

G(C):=\{g\in C\;|\;\Delta(g)=g\otimes g,\,\epsilon(g)=1\}.

If

One may formulate this definition for a coalgebra

.

The

.
We say that

Remark 2.2
.

For connected, braided bialgebras, (

An augmented algebra is an algebra

assemble into an operad in
\pro\!-\mathbf{D}
; by construction this operad will be profinitely braided. The structure maps

\circ_{i}:\mathcal{C}_{j,P}(n)\otimes\mathcal{C}_{k,Q}(m)\to\mathcal{C}_{\ell,R}(n+m-1)

(4.8)

are defined by descending the structure maps (
4.7
) on
\mathcal{C}
through the coinvariants. Note that the maps in (
4.7
) are equivariant with respect to the cabling homomorphism on the braid groups
\circ_{i}:B_{n}^{i}\times B_{m}\to B_{n+m-1}
(recall that in
B_{n}^{i}
, the
i\textsuperscript{th}
strand is pure). Thus the maps in (
4.8
) are well defined if

\ker_{P}\times\ker_{Q}\subseteq[\circ_{i}^{-1}(\ker_{R})].

(4.9)

Since we are working with inverse systems, we may simply restrict to the cofinal subsystem of
P
and
Q
with this property. However, for (
4.8
) to actually define a morphism in the category
\pro\!-\mathbf{D}
, we must verify that this subsystem is nonempty (once we do, it is clear that it is cofinal).

This is the case: since
\ker_{R}
is finite index in
B_{n+m-1}
, the subgroup
T:=[\circ_{i}^{-1}(\ker_{R})]\subseteq B_{n}^{i}\times B_{m}
is finite index.
B_{n}^{i}
is finite index in
B_{n}
, so in fact
T
is finite index in
B_{n}\times B_{m}
. Let

T_{1}:=T\cap[B_{n}\times\{1\}]\leq B_{n}\;\mbox{ and }\;T_{2}:=T\cap[\{1\}\times B_{m}]\leq B_{m}.

These are finite index subgroups. The braid groups are finitely generated, so there exists finite index subgroups
U_{i}\leq T_{i}
which are normal in the ambient braid group. If we let
P=B_{n}/U_{1}
and
Q=B_{m}/U_{2}
, these quotients have the desired property (
4.9
).

We conclude that (
4.8
) defines morphisms
\circ_{i}:\widehat{\mathcal{C}}(n)\otimes\widehat{\mathcal{C}}(m)\to\widehat{\mathcal{C}}(n+m-1)
in
\pro\!-\mathbf{D}
. These equip
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
with the structure of an operad: all of the axioms of Definition
3.1
are satisfied because they hold for
\mathcal{C}
. It is clear that the assignment
\mathcal{C}\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
is functorial.

The unit
\mathcal{C}\to\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
of the claimed adjunction is given by the collection of quotient maps

\mathcal{C}_{j}(n)\to\mathcal{C}_{j}(n)_{\ker_{Q}}

for each
n
and for each
j
in the inverse system defining
\mathcal{C}(n)
. It is easy to see that this is a map of operads.

With that in hand, verifying the existence of the adjunction between profinite completion and the forgetful functor is straightforward. It relies on the fact that every map of braided operads
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is in particular a collection of
B_{n}
-equivariant maps. If the
B_{n}
-action on
\mathcal{D}_{k}(n)
factors through a finite quotient
Q
, and if
f(n):\mathcal{C}_{j}(n)\to\mathcal{D}_{k}(n)
, then
f(n)
factors through
\mathcal{C}_{j}(n)_{\ker_{Q}}
; thus
f
factors uniquely through
\widehat{\mathcal{C}}
.

∎

Definition 4.5
.

The
profinite braided associative operad

is the profinite completion of
\BrAss
. Its
n\textsuperscript{th}
term is the inverse system
\widehat{\BrAss}(n)=\{k[Q],\;Q\in\Quot_{n}\}
of group rings of finite quotients

of
B_{n}
.

Definition 4.6
.

A braided sequence
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathcal{C}(n)\}
in
\pro-\mathbf{D}
will be called

or

if each

is, in the sense of Definition

Note that if

4.2
Algebras for the profinite completion of an operad

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a profinitely braided operad in

\mathbf{D}

in
\pro-\mathbf{C}

While the flexibility of the full generality of algebras of pro-objects can be helpful, we will often focus on pro-constant algebras. This forms a full subcategory

Now consider an operad
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathbf{D}

in

-algebra

\textstyle{\mathcal{O}(n)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

for sufficiently large quotients

-invariant; this need not be the case for an arbitrary

-algebra

:

Definition 4.7
.

For a unital operad
\mathcal{O}

-algebra

of

In this case, that the universal property of the enveloping algebra reads

\Hom_{\Alg_{\widehat{\mathcal{O}}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})}(\widehat{A},B)\cong\Hom_{\Alg_{\mathcal{O}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})}(A,B)

If

Consider the

Notation 4.9

Free algebras on finitely braided objects are also recognizable:

For finitely braided

Lemma 4.12

Let
\mathcal{O}

\mathbf{D}

\mathcal{C}\leq\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
be an essentially strict braided subsequence of its profinite completion. Then if

Proof.

Recall that
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}(n)

\{\mathcal{O}(n)_{\ker_{Q}},\;Q\in\Quot_{n}\},

\ker_{Q}=\ker(B_{n}\twoheadrightarrow Q)

(4.10)

Since
\mathcal{C}(n)
is essentially strict, this inclusion is an equality for sufficiently large

Now
\mathcal{C}_{n}[V]
is the pro-object
\{\mathcal{C}(n)_{\ker_{Q}}\otimes_{B_{n}}V^{\otimes n}\}
, again indexed over

. Once

is large enough that

\mathcal{C}(n)_{\ker_{P}}\otimes_{P}V^{\otimes n}=(\mathcal{C}(n)_{\ker_{P}})_{K^{P}_{Q}}\otimes_{Q}V^{\otimes n}=\mathcal{C}(n)_{\ker_{Q}}\otimes_{Q}V^{\otimes n},

\mathcal{C}_{n}[V]\cong\mathcal{C}(n)_{\ker_{Q}}\otimes_{Q}V^{\otimes n}

This in turn implies that

\mathcal{C}[V]=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{n}[V]

is pro-constant. Recall that direct sums of pro-objects are indexed on the products of the indexing categories of their terms, so
\mathcal{C}[V]
is indexed on
\prod_{n}\Quot_{n}
. For each

, we have established that there is some

such that the part of the system

is constant. Thus

is pro-constant, being constant above

∎

Proof of Proposition

We apply the previous Lemma with

4.4
Monads

If
\mathcal{O}
is a braided operad in
\mathbf{D}
, and
\mathbf{C}
is a braided monoidal category enriched and tensored over
\mathbf{D}
, we consider the corresponding Artin functor
\mathcal{O}[-]:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
. The
\mathcal{O}
-algebra structure maps
\gamma
assemble in (
3.5
) to a natural transformation

\Gamma:\mathcal{O}[-]\circ\mathcal{O}[-]\to\mathcal{O}[-].

The associativity axiom in the definition of an operad implies that
\Gamma
is also associative.

If
\mathcal{O}
is a unital operad, then
\mathcal{O}[-]
becomes a
monad
or
triple
: a monoid object in the category of endofunctors of
\mathbf{C}
, where the monoidal product is composition, and the unit is the identity. Furthermore,
\mathcal{O}
-algebras
A
are equivalent to
algebras
for this monad: they are equipped with a suitably associative and unital operation

\mu_{A}:\mathcal{O}[A]\to A.

In the case at hand, the components of a
\mathcal{O}[-]
-algebra structure map
\mathcal{O}[A]\to A
are precisely the maps defining a
\mathcal{O}
-algebra structure on
A
. See, e.g.,
[
May72
]
for a discussion of these facts in the symmetric monoidal setting; this adapts to the braided context, mutatis mutandis.

Not every monad on
\mathbf{C}
arises from the Artin functor associated to a braided operad
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathbf{D}
. Furthermore, it is quite conceivable that there could be multiple operads in
\mathbf{D}
whose associated monads are isomorphic, if
\mathbf{C}
is in some sense too small to fully “test” operads in
\mathbf{D}
. Proposition
1.12
partly addresses this: if
\mathbf{C}
has a suitably rich family of finitely braided vector spaces amongst its objects, then the
B_{n}
action on
\mathcal{O}(n)
should be determined by
\mathcal{O}[-]
(see Lemma
5.4
for an example of this).

If we restrict our attention to the full subcategory of finitely braided objects in
\mathbf{C}
, Proposition
4.11
implies that the natural map
\mathcal{O}\to\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
is an isomorphism of monads. As such, we should only ever be able to recover information about
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
(and not
\mathcal{O}
) from the restriction of
\mathcal{O}[-]
to this subcategory.

5
Operads for primitives in braided Hopf algebras

With the exception of subsection
5.4
, in this section the category
\mathbf{D}
will be
\Vect_{k}
, so
\pro-\mathbf{D}
(the category from which we draw our operads) will be inverse systems of vector spaces over a field

of characteristic zero. The category
\mathbf{C}
will be abelian, enriched and tensored over
\Vect_{k}
; it is in particular a
k
-linear, abelian, braided monoidal category. Most of the constructions in this section will naturally produce algebras in

. Using results like Proposition

5.1
The monad of primitives

Consider the endofunctor

PT:\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}\;\mbox{ given by }\;V\mapsto P(T(V))

defined by the primitives in the tensor algebra on
V
, equipped with the Hopf structure as given in section

.

Proposition 5.1
.

PT
is a monad, and if
A
is a braided Hopf algebra,
P(A)
is an algebra for
PT
.

Proof.

If
A
is a braided Hopf algebra, the universal property of the tensor algebra gives a unique algebra map
M_{A}:T(P(A))\to A
which is induced by the inclusion
P(A)\hookrightarrow A
. Since
P(A)
is primitive, this is a Hopf map. So if we restrict this map to the primitives in the tensor algebra, its image lies in
P(A)
, giving a map

\mu_{A}:=P(M_{A}):P(T(P(A)))\to P(A).

Taking
A=T(V)
defines the monadic composition
\mu:PT\circ PT\to PT
. For general
A
, this gives the algebra structure map.

The unit
\eta:\mbox{id}\to PT
is given by the natural inclusion
V\hookrightarrow PT(V)
; it is straightforward to verify that
\mu\circ(PT\eta)=\mbox{id}_{PT}=\mu\circ(\eta PT)
. Associativity of
\mu
will follow from commutativity of the following diagram of Hopf algebras, after restricting to primitives:

\textstyle{T(P(T(P(A))))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{T(P(M_{A}))}
\scriptstyle{M_{T(P(A))}}
\textstyle{T(P(A))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{M_{A}}
\textstyle{T(P(A))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{M_{A}}
\textstyle{A.}

This, in turn, can be verified by restriction to
P(T(P(A)))\leq T(P(T(P(A))))
, where it is the obvious assertion that
\mu_{A}=\mbox{id}_{P(T(P(A)))}\circ\mu_{A}
.

∎

5.4
The topology of
\BrPrim

In this section, we give a topological description of the braided primitive operad in terms of covering spaces of the little disk operad. From a topologist’s point of view, this is undoubtedly the “correct” definition of
\BrPrim
, in that it encodes more homotopy theoretic information, and so naturally fits into more general homotopical contexts (e.g., differential graded settings).

Let
E_{2}
be the little disks operad; for background, we refer the reader to the classic text
[
May72

as well as the profusion of more recent writing on operads. We recall the definition of

Write

for the closed unit disk. A

This is topologized as a subspace of
(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{C})^{\times n}

\mathbb{C}

Because a TD-map is uniquely determined by its image, we think of

(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\circ_{i}(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})=(f_{1},\dots,f_{i-1},f_{i}\circ(\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}),f_{i+1},\dots,f_{n}).

\displaystyle\ker(d_{n+1})

\displaystyle\bigcap_{p+q=n}\ker(\mathbf{S}_{p,q}:k[Q]\otimes\epsilon\to k[Q]\otimes\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma\BrPrim(n)_{Q}.

This shows that the underlying inverse systems of
k[B_{n}]
-modules are isomorphic; we must show that the composition in these operads is the same. On the topological side, one verifies that the composition map

\circ_{i}:\widetilde{E}_{2}(n)_{\ker_{P}}\times\widetilde{E}_{2}(m)_{\ker_{Q}}=(P\times Q)_{h(B_{n}^{i}\times B_{m})}\to R_{hB_{n+m-1}}=\widetilde{E}_{2}(n+m-1)_{\ker_{R}}

is induced by cabling
P\times Q\to R
(for
P
and
Q
large enough for this to be defined), along with the induced map of homotopy quotients, coming from the cabling homomorphism
B_{n}^{i}\times B_{m}\to B_{n+m-1}
. Consequently in the above description of the top homology of these spaces,
\circ_{i}
is induced by cabling in the module
k[P]\otimes k[Q]\to k[R]
. This is precisely operadic composition in
\widehat{\BrAss}
; since
\BrPrim
is a suboperad and the map corestricts, the result follows.

∎

Remark 5.20
.

If we let
\widetilde{E}_{1}(n)\subseteq\widetilde{E}_{2}(n)
be the preimage of
E_{1}(n)
, then
\widetilde{E}_{1}
forms a braided suboperad of
\widetilde{E}_{2}
. The components of
\widetilde{E}_{1}(n)
are contractible, and in bijection with
B_{n}
; one easily establishes an isomorphism of braided operads

H_{0}(\widetilde{E}_{1})\cong\BrAss.

This allows us to regard
\widetilde{E}_{1}
as a braided analogue of the
A_{\infty}
operad. Writing
\widehat{\widetilde{E}}_{1}=:E_{1}^{\fin}
for the profinite completion, we similarly have

H_{0}(E_{1}^{\fin})\cong\widehat{\BrAss}.

The natural map
E_{1}^{\fin}\to E_{2}^{\fin}
induces in
H_{0}
the projection
\widehat{\BrAss}\to\BrCom
.

5.5
The Woronowicz ideal and Nichols module

In in section 3 of
[
Wor89
]
, Woronowicz gives a description of the ideal of relations in
\mathfrak{B}(V)
. Following that work and its elucidation in section 3.2 of
[
Baz06
]
, we make the following definition:

Definition 5.21
.

Define the
Woronowicz ideal

\mathfrak{W}
as

\mathfrak{W}(n)=\ker\left(\mathbf{S}_{n}:\widehat{\BrAss}(n)\to\widehat{\BrAss}(n)\right).

The
Nichols module
is defined to be the quotient
\widehat{\BrAss}
-module

\Nic:=\widehat{\BrAss}/\mathfrak{W}.

These terms are justified by the following result:

Proposition 5.22
.

\mathfrak{W}
is a strict left ideal in
\widehat{\BrAss}
. For any finitely braided
V\in\mathbf{C}
, there is an isomorphism of algebras

\Nic[V]\cong\mathfrak{B}(V).

There is no intrinsic Hopf algebra structure on
\Nic[V]
, since it is defined in purely algebraic (and not coalgebraic) terms. That said, one can transport the Hopf structure on
\mathfrak{B}(V)
across this isomorphism.

Proof.

Again, part
1
of Proposition
4.15
yields strictness. Recall equation (
2.3
), which identifies
\mathfrak{B}(V)=T(V)/I
, where
I
is the two-sided ideal which in degree
n
is

I_{n}=\ker(\mathbf{S}_{n}:V^{\otimes n}\to V^{\otimes n}).

Part
2
of Proposition
4.15
allows us to rewrite this as

I_{n}=\ker\left(\mathbf{S}_{n}:\widehat{\BrAss}(n)\to\widehat{\BrAss}(n)\right)\otimes_{B_{n}}V^{\otimes n}=\mathfrak{W}(n)\otimes_{B_{n}}V^{\otimes n}

so
I=\mathfrak{W}[V]
. This gives the isomorphism
\mathfrak{B}(V)\cong\Nic[V]
, using Remark
3.9
.

To prove the first claim, we work with braided vector spaces
V
instead of objects in
\mathbf{C}
. Write
\mathcal{I}
for the left ideal generated by
\mathfrak{W}
. By Proposition
4.14
,
\mathcal{I}[V]\leq T(V)
is the two sided ideal generated by
\mathfrak{W}[V]=I
. But this already a two-sided ideal, so
\mathcal{I}[V]=I
.

Assume that
\mathcal{I}\neq\mathfrak{W}
, so there is some
n
and some element
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}(n)\setminus\mathfrak{W}(n)
. Then
\mathbf{S}_{n}(\alpha)\neq 0
, so by Proposition
1.12
there is some
V
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in V
with the property that
[\mathbf{S}_{n}(\alpha)](v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\neq 0
. Then
\alpha(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\notin I_{n}=\mathcal{I}[V]_{n}
, which is a contradiction.

∎

It is a consequence of the fact that
\mathbf{S}:T(V)\to T^{\co}(V)
is a map of coalgebras that that for any
p,q\in[1,n-1]
with
p+q=n
,

\mathbf{S}_{n}=(\mathbf{S}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{q})\circ\mathbf{S}_{p,q}

(5.11)

(see also
[
Sch96
]
). Taking kernels, we see that

\mathfrak{W}(n)\supseteq\BrPrim(n)

(5.12)

This is an equality if
n=2
since
\mathbf{S}_{2}=\mathbf{S}_{1,1}
, but the inclusion is proper otherwise. If these braided sequences were equal – even if the left ideal generated by
\BrPrim
equalled
\mathfrak{W}
– then Proposition
5.22
would imply that for any
V
,
\mathfrak{B}(V)
would be obtained as the quotient of
T(V)
by the ideal generated by decomposable primitives. This is equivalent to
V
being of
combinatorial rank
1 (see section
9
), but not all braided vector spaces are of this sort.

Proposition 5.23
.

Both
\mathfrak{W}(n)
and
Q_{\widehat{\BrAss}_{>1}}(\mathfrak{W})(n)
are nonzero, pro-cyclic
k[B_{n}]
-modules.

Proof.

As in Theorem
5.14
, we need to show
Q_{\widehat{\BrAss}_{>1}}(\mathfrak{W})(n)\neq 0
and that
\mathfrak{W}(n)
is pro-cyclic. The proof of the second fact is identical to that of Theorem
5.14
. The proof of the first fact follows the same path: we must find a braided vector space
V
with
\mathfrak{W}[V]_{m}
vanishing for
1<m<n
and nonzero for
m=n
.

The same braided vector space as in that proof works for this argument. Indeed, the nonzero class in
P(T(V))_{n}
gives a nonzero class in
\mathfrak{W}[V]_{n}
by (
5.12
). Further, Proposition
5.22
shows that
\mathfrak{W}[V]=\ker(\mathbf{S}:T(V)\to\mathfrak{B}(V))
. For the
V
in question, it is easy to see that
\ker(\mathbf{S}_{m})=0
for
m<n
.

∎

Definition 5.24
.

Let
h_{n}\in\mathfrak{W}(n)
be the pro-cyclic generators obtained in the same fashion as the Garsia idempotents
g_{n}
.

It is tempting to imagine that
h_{n}=g_{n}
. However, this is not the case unless
n=2
, again by (
5.12
). Rather, for each
Q\in\Quot_{n}
, there are elements
c_{n,Q}\in k[Q]
with

g_{n,Q}=c_{n,Q}h_{n,Q}.

The elements
c_{n,Q}
are not necessarily invertible, but if
V
is a braided vector space of combinatorial rank 1, then they act invertibly on
V^{\otimes n}
ensuring that the ideal in
T(V)
generated by
P(V)_{>1}
is
I
.

Proposition 5.25
.

\mathfrak{W}
is not a right ideal in
\widehat{\BrAss}
.

Proof.

The norm bracket
b
is an element of
\BrPrim(2)=\mathfrak{W}(2)
. It suffices to show that
b\circ_{1}\mu\notin\mathfrak{W}(3)
. For this, we may find a finitely braided vector space
V
and elements
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\in V
with

\mathbf{S}_{3}(b(v_{1}\otimes v_{2},v_{3}))\neq 0\in V^{\otimes 3}.

A simple example where this holds is gotten by taking
V
to be the
S_{3}
Yetter-Drinfeld module

V=k\{x,y,z\}\leq k[S_{3}]

where
x=(12)
,
y=(23)
and
z=(31)
are the transpositions. We omit the tensor symbol in the following computation; it is performed in
V^{\otimes 3}
. It is easy to see that

b(xx,y)=\frac{1}{4}(xxy-yzz+zzy-yxx),

from which one computes

\mathbf{S}_{3}(b(xx,y))=\frac{1}{2}(xyz-zxz+zyx-xzx)\neq 0.

∎

We conclude that
\Nic=\widehat{\BrAss}/\mathfrak{W}
is not an operad. Since
\Nic[V]=\mathfrak{B}(V)
, we observe that the operation
V\mapsto\mathfrak{B}(V)
is not a monad on the category of finitely braided vector spaces. This stands in contrast to the corresponding situation for symmetric vector spaces; the symmetric algebra functor
\Sym
is indeed a monad.

6
Hopf structures on enveloping algebras

In this section, we introduce universal enveloping algebras for suboperads of
\widehat{\BrAss}
and find conditions under which they support the structure of a Hopf algebra. Classically, for Lie-algebras
L
, the coalgebraic structure on
U(L)
is entirely mandated by the requirement that
L
be primitive. More data is required in the braided setting; this section is devoted to identifying that data.

Throughout,
\mathbf{C}
is a
k
-linear, abelian, braided monoidal category. Most of our constructions occur in the larger category
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
of pro-objects in
\mathbf{C}
, but at various points we examine conditions under which they restrict to the pro-constant objects.

6.1
Defining and presenting enveloping algebras

The rest of this paper will be concerned with this special case of Definition
3.13
:

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\leq\widehat{\BrAss}
be a essentially strict suboperad, and let
L
be a
\mathcal{C}
-algebra in
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
. The
universal enveloping algebra
of
L
is

U_{\mathcal{C}}(L):=U_{\mathcal{C}}^{\widehat{\BrAss}}(L).

Example 6.2
.

For an associative algebra
A
, profinite completion is a universal enveloping algebra:
\widehat{A}=U_{\BrAss}(A)
.

Remark 6.3
.

Notice that if
\mathcal{C}(1)=0
, the forgetful map

\Alg_{\mathcal{C}_{*}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})\longrightarrow\Alg_{\mathcal{C}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})

(see Definition
3.3
) is an equality; the only operation missing on the right is the identity. From this it is easy to see that since
\widehat{\BrAss}
is unital, if
L
is a
\mathcal{C}
-algebra, then when regarded as a
\mathcal{C}_{*}
-algebra, there is a natural isomorphism

U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)\cong U_{\mathcal{C}_{*}}(L).

Recall that Proposition
3.16
gives a presentation of
U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)
, given a presentation of
L
: if
L=\mathcal{C}[V]/R
, then

U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)\cong\widehat{\BrAss}[V]/(R),

where
(R)
is the
\widehat{\BrAss}
-ideal generated by
R
.

Definition 6.4
.

A
\mathcal{C}
-algebra
L
is
faithful
if it admits a presentation
L=\mathcal{C}[V]/R
where
R=\mathcal{C}[V]\cap(R)
.

It follows that the natural map
L\to U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)
is injective if and only if the
\mathcal{C}
-subalgebra
R\leq\mathcal{C}[V]
of relations satisfies
R=\mathcal{C}[V]\cap(R)
, so faithful
\mathcal{C}
-algebras are precisely those that embed into their enveloping algebras. In fact, if
L
is a
\mathcal{C}
-subalgebra of
any

\widehat{\BrAss}
-algebra
A
, then
L
is faithful, seeing that the universal property of
U_{\mathcal{C}}
provides a factorization of
L\hookrightarrow A
through
U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)
.

Define

\overline{L}:={\rm im\,}(L\to U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)).

Noting that
U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)
is a
\mathcal{C}
-algebra by restriction along
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\widehat{\BrAss}
, we observe that
\overline{L}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-algebra, being the image of one under a map of
\mathcal{C}
-algebras. This makes the following straightforward:

Proposition 6.5
.

The operation
L\mapsto\overline{L}
defines an endofunctor of
\Alg_{\mathcal{C}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})
. There is a natural transformation
\eta_{L}:L\to\overline{L}
which induces a natural isomorphism
U_{\mathcal{C}}(\eta_{L}):U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)\cong U_{\mathcal{C}}(\overline{L})
. The image of the functor
L\mapsto\overline{L}
is the subcategory of faithful
\mathcal{C}
-algebras, and its restriction to this subcategory is naturally equivalent to the identity.

With this result in hand, we will focus our attention on faithful
\mathcal{C}
-algebras, replacing any non-faithful
L
with
\overline{L}
.

6.2
Profinitely braided bialgebras

If
A
and
B
are algebras in
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
, then
A\otimes B
supports the structure of an algebra. Indeed, the assumption that
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
is braided is essential to this fact. If
A
and
B
are
\widehat{\BrAss}
-algebras, they are in particular complete associative algebras, and one may form their tensor product
A\otimes B
. This need not support a
\widehat{\BrAss}
-algebra structure; that is, there is no guarantee that
A\otimes B
is complete. This is, however, easily rectified:

Definition 6.6
.

Let
A,B\in\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})
. Their
\widehat{\BrAss}
-algebra tensor product
is the profinite completion

A\hattimes B:=\widehat{A\otimes B}

of their tensor product regarded as algebras in
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
. If
I\leq A
and
J\leq B
are ideals, then we also define
I\hattimes J
to be the ideal in
A\hattimes B
generated by
I\otimes J
.

We will need the following Lemma, whose proof amounts to a comparison of the universal properties of completion and quotients:

Lemma 6.7
.

If
A
and
B
are
\widehat{\BrAss}
-algebras and
I\leq A
is an ideal, then
(A/I)\hattimes B\cong(A\hattimes B)/(I\hattimes B)
.

It follows from this that for maps
f:A\to A^{\prime}
,
g:B\to B^{\prime}
,
\ker(f\hattimes g)=\ker(f)\hattimes B+A\hattimes\ker(g)
, as for the usual tensor product.

Proposition 6.8
.

The category
\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})
becomes monoidal under
\hattimes
, with unit
\mathbf{1}
, and profinite completion defines a strictly monoidal functor

(\Alg_{\BrAss}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C}),\otimes,\mathbf{1})\to(\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C}),\hattimes,\mathbf{1}).

The notion of a coalgebra makes sense in a general monoidal category, so we make the following definition:

Definition 6.9
.

A
profinitely braided bialgebra
is a coassociative, counital coalgebra object in the monoidal category
(\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C}),\hattimes,\mathbf{1})
.

The primitives
P(A)
and indecomposables
Q(A)
of a profinitely braided bialgebra are defined in exactly the same way as for usual bialgebras.

We have a diagram of functors between several categories of algebras:

\textstyle{\Alg_{\BrAss}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\cdot}}
\textstyle{\Alg_{\BrAss}(\mathbf{C})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\cdot}}
\textstyle{\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\pro\!-\mathbf{C})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{U}
\textstyle{\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\mathbf{C})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{U}

(6.13)

Here the functors labelled by
\widehat{\cdot}
are given by profinite completion, left adjoint to the forgetful functors
U
. The unlabelled functors are inclusion of constant objects. Both squares commute.

The upper three categories are monoidal with respect to
\otimes
or
\hattimes
, although
\hattimes
does not obviously restrict to a monoidal product on
\Alg_{\widehat{\BrAss}}(\mathbf{C})
. Profinite completion and inclusion of constant objects are strictly monoidal functors (when this makes sense). In contrast, the forgetful functor
U
is only lax monoidal, being a right adjoint.

Coalgebras in the two upper left categories in (
6.13
) are bialgebras in the usual sense in
\mathbf{C}
or
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
. Strictly monoidal functors preserve coalgebras. Lax monoidal functors do not in general, but will for those coalgebras for which the lax monoidal transformation is assumed to be an isomorphism:

Proposition 6.10
.

If
A\in\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
is a bialgebra in the usual sense, the profinite completion
\widehat{A}
is a profinitely braided bialgebra. Conversely, if
B
is a profinitely braided bialgebra, and if
U(B)\otimes U(B)
is complete, then
U(B)
defines a bialgebra in
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
. If, further,
U(B)
is pro-constant, it is a bialgebra in
\mathbf{C}
.

Ultimately, we are interested in studying bialgebras in
\mathbf{C}
. However, our methods are based upon the operad
\widehat{\BrAss}
and its suboperads, and so naturally produce profinitely braided bialgebras. We will employ the converse statement in the above Proposition to give conditions under which these are in fact bialgebras in
\mathbf{C}
.

6.3
Primitives in profinitely braided bialgebras

Let us examine the free
\widehat{\BrAss}
-algebra
\widehat{T}(V)=\widehat{\BrAss}[V]
. This is naturally graded, so yields a bigrading on the tensor product
\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)
. Since
T(V)
is a Hopf algebra, the previous result implies that
\widehat{T}(V)
is a profinitely braided bialgebra.

Proposition 6.11
.

In bidegree
(p,q)
with
p+q=n
, there is an isomorphism

\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{(p,q)}\cong\widehat{\BrAss}_{n}[V]

of objects in
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
. Further, the
(p,q)
-component of the diagonal on
\widehat{T}(V)_{n}=\widehat{\BrAss}_{n}[V]
is induced by multiplication by
\mathbf{S}_{p,q}
on
\widehat{\BrAss}(n)
.

Proof.

Note that
\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)
is the profinite completion of
A=T(V)\otimes T(V)
. We can present
A
as
A=T(V\oplus W)/R
, where
W
is a second copy of
V
, and
R
is the ideal generated by the image of the map

[-,-]_{\sigma}:W\oplus V\to T(V\oplus W)\;\mbox{ given by }\;[w,v]_{\sigma}=w\otimes v-\sigma(w\otimes v).

Thus
\widehat{A}=\widehat{\BrAss}[V\oplus W]/\widehat{R}
, where
\widehat{R}
is the ideal generated by
R
. Since
R
is homogenous, this decomposes into a sum over
n
of terms of the form
\widehat{\BrAss}[V\oplus W]_{n}/\widehat{R}_{n}
, where
\widehat{R}_{n}
is the image of the structure map

\lambda:[\widehat{\BrAss}(n-1)\otimes_{B_{n-1}^{n-1}}(V\oplus W)^{\otimes n-2}\otimes[W,V]_{\sigma}]\to[\widehat{\BrAss}(n)\otimes_{B_{n}}(V\oplus W)^{\otimes n}].

The codomain is the inverse system given by
(V\oplus W)^{\otimes n}_{\ker_{Q}}
, as
Q
ranges over
\Quot_{n}
. In taking the quotient by the image of
\lambda
, one may reorder terms in the
n
-fold tensor power so that all of the
V
terms are on the left. Thus
\widehat{A}_{n}
is the inverse system

\bigoplus_{p+q=n}(V^{\otimes p}\otimes W^{\otimes q})_{\ker_{Q}\cap B_{p,q}}

where
B_{p,q}
is the subgroup of
B_{n}
lying over
S_{p}\times S_{q}\leq S_{n}
. Since the collection
\{\ker_{Q}\cap B_{p,q}\}
is cofinal in
\Quot_{n}
and
W=V
, the computation of
\widehat{A}_{(p,q)}
follows. Further, the diagonal on
\widehat{T}(V)
is induced by the one on
T(V)
, and so is also given by
\mathbf{S}_{p,q}
.

∎

Along with part
2
of Proposition
4.15
, this allows us to extend Proposition
5.8
and Corollary
5.9
beyond the finitely braided setting:

Corollary 6.12
.

There is an isomorphism of monads on
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}

\BrPrim[-]\cong P\circ\widehat{T}.

Further, if
A
is a profinitely braided bialgebra, then
P(A)
is an algebra for
\BrPrim
.

Definition 6.13
.

A profinitely braided bialgebra
A
is a
profinitely braided Nichols algebra
if the map
P(A)\to Q(A)
is an isomorphism.

There is also an extension of Proposition
5.22
in this setting:

Proposition 6.14
.

For any
V\in\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
, the Woronowicz ideal
\mathfrak{W}[V]\leq\widehat{T}(V)
is a Hopf ideal, and the quotient
\Nic(V):=\widehat{T}(V)/\mathfrak{W}[V]
is a profinitely braided Nichols algebra.

Proof.

By part
2
of Proposition
4.15
,

\mathfrak{W}[V]_{n}=\ker(\mathbf{S}_{n}:\widehat{T}(V)_{n}\to\widehat{T}(V)_{n}).

Recall that, as elements of
\widehat{\BrAss}(n)
,
\mathbf{S}_{n}=(\mathbf{S}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{q})\circ\mathbf{S}_{p,q}
; thus the same equation holds as maps
\widehat{T}(V)_{n}\to\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{(p,q)}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{p,q}(\mathfrak{W}[V]_{n})

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\ker(\mathbf{S}_{p}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{q}:\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{(p,q)}\to\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{(p,q)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{W}[V]_{p}\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{q}+\widehat{T}(V)_{p}\hattimes\mathfrak{W}[V]_{q}.

Thus
\mathfrak{W}[V]
is a Hopf ideal.

Since
Q(\widehat{T}(V))=V
and
\mathfrak{W}[V]\cap V=0
, it follows that
Q(\Nic(V))=V
. Further,
\Nic(V)
is graded; since
\Nic(V)_{\leq 1}=\mathbf{1}\oplus V
, it is clear that
P(\Nic(V))_{\leq 1}=V
. We will show
P(\Nic(V))_{n}=0
for
n\geq 2
. Consider the commuting diagram

\textstyle{\widehat{T}(V)_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathbf{S}_{p,q}}
\textstyle{\widehat{T}(V)\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{(p,q)}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\Nic(V)_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Delta_{p,q}}
\textstyle{\Nic(V)\hattimes\Nic(V)_{(p,q)}}

If
x\in P(\Nic(V))_{n}
, then it has a representative
X\in\widehat{T}(V)_{n}
with the property that for all
p+q=n
, with
p,q>0
,

\mathbf{S}_{p,q}(X)\in\mathfrak{W}[V]_{p}\hattimes\widehat{T}(V)_{q}+\widehat{T}(V)_{p}\hattimes\mathfrak{W}[V]_{q}=\ker(\mathbf{S}_{p}\hattimes\mathbf{S}_{q}).

Thus
X\in\ker((\mathbf{S}_{p}\hattimes\mathbf{S}_{q})\circ\mathbf{S}_{p,q})=\ker(\mathbf{S}_{n})=\mathfrak{W}[V]_{n}
, so
x=0
.

∎

6.4
Monoidal categories of
\mathcal{C}
-algebras

Fix a profinitely braided, essentially strict suboperad
\mathcal{C}
of
\widehat{\BrAss}
.

Definition 6.15
.

For
\mathcal{C}
-algebras
L
and
M
in
\pro\!-\mathbf{C}
, define the
\mathcal{C}
-algebra tensor product

L\otimes_{\mathcal{C}}M
as the
\mathcal{C}
-subalgebra of
U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)\hattimes U_{\mathcal{C}}(M)
generated by
L\hattimes 1+1\hattimes M
:

L\otimes_{\mathcal{C}}M:=\mathcal{C}(L\hattimes 1+1\hattimes M)\subseteq U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)\hattimes U_{\mathcal{C}}(M).

Here, the algebra
U_{\mathcal{C}}(L)\hattimes U_{\mathcal{C}}(M)
becomes a
\mathcal{C}
-algebra by restriction along
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\widehat{\BrAss}

In

In
[
Ard12a
]
, Ardizzoni proves a restricted version of the Cartier-Milnor-Moore theorem (his Theorem 5.7): for a certain class of braided Hopf algebras
A

\mathcal{S}
), there is an isomorphism
A\cong U(P(A),c,b)

\mathcal{S}

P(A)

\BrPrim
-algebra, so one may recover this result from our Proposition

In
[
Ard12b
]
, Ardizzoni establishes an analogue of the Poincaré-Birkhoff-Witt Theorem for a class of enveloping algebras
U^{[\infty]}
as described above. He shows that if
U^{[\infty]}
is
cosymmetric
, there is a natural bialgebra isomorphism
(U^{[\infty]})^{\gr}\cong\mathfrak{B}(V)

V=P(U^{[\infty]})

2
. While our result holds for all enveloping algebras, his isomorphism occurs only after taking the associated graded of a single filtration.
