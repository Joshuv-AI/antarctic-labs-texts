Abstract
. We give a presentation of refined (dual) canonical Grothendieck polynomials and their skew versions using free-fermions. Using this, we derive a number of identities, including the skew Cauchy identities, branching rules, expansion formulas, and integral formulas.

05E05, 82B23, 14M15, 05A19

1.
Introduction

The (symmetric) Grothendieck functions
G_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\beta)
, where
\lambda
is a partition inside a
k\times(n-k)
rectangle, are symmetric functions used to study the K-theory of the Grassmannian, the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, arising from the work of Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
LS82
,
LS83
]
with the
\beta
parameter introduced by Fomin and Kirillov
[
FK94
]
.
(Strictly speaking, they live in a completion of the ring of symmetric functions, but this distinction is insignificant for our purposes.)
Many formulas are known for Grothendieck functions, such as a ratio of alternates
[
LS83
, Eq. (1.8)]
(see also
[
Len00
, Eq. (2.3)]
) and a sum over combinatorial objects
[
Buc02
]
.
An important property is that
G_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\beta)
is equal to
s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
, the classical Schur function, plus higher degree terms, which was first given explicitly by Lenart
[
Len00
]
.
As such, they form a basis for (a completion of)
\Lambda
and we can form the dual basis
\{g_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\beta)\}_{\lambda}
under the (continuously extended) Hall inner product

\left\langle s_{\lambda},s_{\mu}\right\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}
.
We can also apply the involution
\omega
, which acts by
\omega s_{\lambda}=s_{\lambda^{\prime}}
with
\lambda^{\prime}
the conjugate shape, to each basis, which produces new bases that are called the weak versions.
These other bases were first studied by Lam and Pylyavskyy
[
LP08
]
, where they were given combinatorial descriptions.

Galashin, Grinberg, and Liu
[
GGL16
]
refined the parameter
\beta
into a sequence of parameters
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots)
that record additional combinatorial information for the dual Grothendieck functions.
The refined dual Grothendieck functions
g_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\beta})
have been used to describe properties of a last-passage percolation model in probability theory
[
MS20
]
, which is a refinement of
[
Yel20
]
.
The refined version of Grothendieck functions
G_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\beta})
were introduced in
[
CP21
]
with applications to Brill–Noether varieties from algebraic geometry.
In a separate direction, Yeliussizov introduced the canonical Grothendieck functions
G_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\alpha,\beta)
in
[
Yel17
]
being inspired by canonical bases for Hecke algebras (more commonly known as Kazhdan–Lusztig bases) using
\omega
as the defining involution.
These specialize to the usual Grothendiecks at
\alpha=0
and the weak Grothendiecks at
\beta=0
, and similarly for the dual versions.
These generalizations were combined into the refined canonical Grothendieck functions
G_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
and their dual version
g_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
by Hwang
et al.

[
HJK+21
]
(they go even further and define flagged versions).
They proved Jacobi–Trudi formulas that specialize to other known formulas
[
AY22
,
Kim21
,
Kim22
,
MS20
,
Yel17
]
and the refined version of Yeliussizov’s symmetry

(1.1)

\omega G_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=G_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\qquad\qquad\omega g_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=g_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}).

For simplicity, from this point onward, we will drop the adjective “refined” in the nomenclature.

Our main result (Theorem
4.1
, Corollary
4.3
) in this paper is a description of the canonial Grothendieck functions and their duals using the boson-fermion correspondence (see,
e.g.
,
[
AZ13
,
DJKM83
,
Kac90
,
KRR13a
]
).
In more detail, a certain infinite wedge space known as (fermionic) Fock space
\mathcal{F}
has an action of the infinite dimensional Heisenberg algebra (a bosonic action) that we use to construct half-vertex operators
e^{H(\mathbf{x})}
in terms of a Hamiltonian using the powersum symmetric functions.
However, Fock space
\mathcal{F}
is also a Clifford algebra representation (which is where the fermionic name comes from), so we can express our half-vertex operators as an action of the Clifford algebra.
This correspondence allows us to write determinant formulas for the pairing of certain vectors and half-vertex operators by Wick’s theorem.
We define a new basis
\lvert\lambda\rangle^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}
of a certain subspace
\mathcal{F}^{0}
of Fock space and its dual basis
{}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}\langle\lambda\rvert
(under the natural pairing) in Theorem
3.10
by generalizing the first author’s previous work
[
Iwa20
,
Iwa22
,
Iwa23
]
.
By the natural Clifford algebra involution, we also have another orthonormal basis
\lvert\lambda\rangle_{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}
and its dual
{}_{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}\langle\lambda\rvert
.
Then taking the Jacobi–Trudi formulas of
[
HJK+21
]
as our
definition
of the skew (dual) canonical Grothendieck functions, we show the matrix elements

G_{\lambda/\!\!/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})={}^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}\langle\mu\rvert e^{H(\mathbf{x})}\lvert\lambda\rangle^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]},

where
G_{\lambda/\!\!/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the refined version of the corresponding functions of
[
Buc02
,
Yel17
]
, and

g_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})={}_{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}\langle\mu\rvert e^{H(\mathbf{x})}\lvert\lambda\rangle_{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}

(Theorem
4.1
, Corollary
4.3
).
We also introduce modified vectors
{}^{[[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]]}\langle\mu\rvert
so that

G_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})={}^{[[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]]}\langle\mu\rvert e^{H(\mathbf{x})}\lvert\lambda\rangle^{[\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}]}.

Using our free-fermion description, we obtain a number of additional results.
We give a new simple proof the symmetries (
1.1
) in Theorem
4.4
and the Schur expansion formulas for
g_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
(Theorem
4.5
) from
[
HJK+21
, Thm. 8.7, 8.8]
.
We also have expansion formulas for
G_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, which are similar to but slightly different from those in
[
HJK+21
, Thm. 8.2, 8.3]
, and
G_{\lambda/\!\!/\mu}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
A number of other results are generalized, such as branching rules
[
Yel17
, Prop. 8.7,8.8]
(Proposition
4.6
), the skew Cauchy identities
[
Yel19
, Thm. 5.1,Cor. 6.3]

(Theorem
4.7
),
the skew Pieri-type identities
[
Yel19
, Thm. 7.10]
(Theorem
4.8
),
the formula in
[
HIMN17
, Rem. 2.8]
(Proposition
4.10
), and integral formulas in
[
MS20
, Prop. 4.28]
(Theorems
4.19
and
4.20
).
We show determinant formulas for the expansions of
G_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
into
G_{\mu}(\mathbf{x};0,\boldsymbol{\beta})
and similarly for the dual and give combinatorial descriptions of the coefficients, which allows us to answer
[
Yel17
, Prob. 12.2]
in the negative (Section
4.7
).
Our last result is a free-fermionic presentation for a special case of the flagged canonical Grothendieck functions in Proposition
5.3
with a flagged version of Proposition
4.10
, which is a canonical version of
[
Mat19
, Thm. 4]
.

Let us briefly digress to discuss vertex models as there is a well-known relationship with free-fermions; see,
e.g.
,
[
Har21
]
and references therein.
There is a vertex model known for Grothendieck functions
[
MS13
,
MS14
,
WZJ19
,
ZJ09
]
.
However, this lattice model is not at the free-fermion point (a condition on the weights), so we cannot go between the two descriptions.
This extends to the recent work of Gunna and Zinn-Justin
[
GZJ23
]
, where they gave a vertex model for canonical Grothendieck functions, and we cannot establish a direct relationship with our results.

This is also the first in a series of papers where we study the relationship between Grothendieck functions and stochastic processes.
In our next paper
[
IMSb
]
, we will use our free-fermion presentation to study the four variants of the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP) studied by Dieker and Warren in
[
DW08
]
.
Indeed, we can already see that appropriate specializations of the Jacobi–Trudi formulas are precisely, up to a simple overall factor, the transition kernels in
[
DW08
]
, which was first noticed in
[
MS20
]
.
In particular, we will extend the noncommutative operators given in
[
Iwa20
,
Iwa22
]
to the refined settings and show these encode the dynamics of the particle motions.
In
[
IMSa
]
, we will show that we can recover the combinatorial description of canonical Grothendieck functions and their duals by using branching rules to reduce the computation to a single variable, which requires more technical analysis.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we give some background on supersymmetric functions and the boson-fermion correspondence.
In Section
3
, we describe new vectors in fermionic Fock space and prove a number of properties.
In Section
4
, we prove our main results and identities.
In Section
5
, we give a free-fermionic presentation of a special case of the flagged canonical Grothendieck functions.

2.
Background

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dotsc,\lambda_{\ell})
be a
partition
, a weakly decreasing finite sequence of positive integers.
We denote the set of all partitions by
\mathcal{P}
.
We draw the Young diagrams of our partitions using English convention.
We will often extend partitions with additional entries at the end being
0
, and let
\ell(\lambda)
denote the largest index
\ell
such that
\lambda_{\ell}>0
.
Let
\lambda^{\prime}
denote the conjugate partition.
We often write our partitions as words.
A
hook
is a partition
\lambda
of the form
a1^{m}=(a,1,\dotsc,1)
with
1
appears
m
times, where the
arm
is
a-1
and the
leg
is
m
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
denote a countably infinite sequence of indeterminates.
We will often set all but finitely many of the indeterminates
\mathbf{x}
to
0
, which we denote as
\mathbf{x}_{n}:=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n},0,0,\ldots)
.
We make similar definitions for another sequence of indeterminates
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)
.
We also require infinite sequences of parameters
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots)
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots)
, which we often treat as indeterminates.

2.1.
Supersymmetric functions

We set some additional standard notation from symmetric function theory.
Let

e_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{m}}x_{i_{1}}\dotsm x_{i_{m}},\qquad h_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i_{1}\leq\cdots\leq i_{m}}x_{i_{1}}\dotsm x_{i_{m}},\qquad p_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}^{m},

denote the elementary, homogeneous, and power sum, respectively.
For
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
, we set

e_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=e_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots e_{\lambda_{\ell}}
,

h_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=h_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots h_{\lambda_{\ell}}
, and

p_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=p_{\lambda_{1}}\cdots p_{\lambda_{\ell}}
.
Let
\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}
be the algebra of symmetric functions over
\mathbb{Q}
. It is known that

\Lambda_{\mathbb{Q}}=\mathbb{Q}[h_{1}(\mathbf{x}),h_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\dots]=\mathbb{Q}[e_{1}(\mathbf{x}),e_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\dots]=\mathbb{Q}[p_{1}(\mathbf{x}),p_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\dots].

We can define the polynomials
E_{\lambda}(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
,
H_{\lambda}(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
,
S_{\lambda}(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}
by the equations

E_{\mu}(p_{1}(\mathbf{x}),p_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots)=e_{\mu}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad H_{\mu}(p_{1}(\mathbf{x}),p_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots)=h_{\mu}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad S_{\mu}(p_{1}(\mathbf{x}),p_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots)=s_{\mu}(\mathbf{x}).

Now we recall some particular
supersymmetric functions
; we refer the reader to
[
Mac15
, Ch. I]
for more details.
We define the supersymmetric elementary, homogeneous, power sum, and Schur functions as

\displaystyle e_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-k}e_{k}(\mathbf{x})h_{m-k}(\mathbf{y}),\qquad h_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-k}h_{k}(\mathbf{x})e_{m-k}(\mathbf{y}),

\displaystyle p_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=p_{m}(\mathbf{x})-p_{m}(\mathbf{y}),\qquad s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mu}(-1)^{\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert-\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}s_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y}).

When
\mathbf{y}=0
, that is we have set all of the
\mathbf{y}
indeterminates to
0
, we have
f(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=f(\mathbf{x})
for any supersymmetric function
f
.

From
[
Mac15
, Sec. I.5.Ex. 23]
, we have
s_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=(-1)^{\left\lvert\lambda/\mu\right\rvert}s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y}/\mathbf{x})=s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}({-\mathbf{y}}/{-\mathbf{x}})
, and we can consider the supersymmetric Schur functions as the sum over bitableaux of shape
\lambda
, which are fillings of
\lambda
by elements in the totally ordered set

\{1<2<3<\cdots<1^{\prime}<2^{\prime}<3^{\prime}<\cdots\}

such that rows and columns weakly increase and no primed (resp. unprimed) letter is repeated in the same row (resp. column).
Therefore, we have

s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=\sum_{T}x_{1}^{T_{1}}x_{2}^{T_{2}}\cdots(-y_{1})^{T_{1^{\prime}}}(-y_{2})^{T_{2^{\prime}}}\cdots,

and have a natural definition of the supersymmetric skew Schur function
s_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})
analogous to the skew Schur function
s_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})
.

The supersymmetric functions can also be described in terms of plethystic substitution.
While we will not give a detailed account, we will briefly review the relevant descriptions for understanding the results in
[
HJK+21
]
and refer the reader to
[
LR11
]
and
[
Mac15
, Ch. I]
for a more detailed description.
Let
X=x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots
and
Y=y_{1}+y_{2}+\cdots
.
For a symmetric function
f
, we define
f[X]=f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
, and if
Z=z_{1}+z_{2}+\cdots+z_{n}
, then we have
f[Z]=f(z_{1},z_{2},\dotsc,z_{n},0,0,\ldots)
.
We also can define

\displaystyle h_{m}[X-Y]=h_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y}),\qquad\qquad e_{m}[X-Y]=e_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y}),\qquad\qquad p_{m}[X-Y]=p_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y}).

As a consequence, we have that
h_{m}[-Y]=(-1)^{m}e_{m}(\mathbf{y})
and
e_{m}[-Y]=(-1)^{m}h_{m}(\mathbf{y})
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle h_{m}\bigl((\mathbf{x}\sqcup\mathbf{x}^{\prime})/(\mathbf{y}\sqcup\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\bigr)

\displaystyle=h_{m}[X+X^{\prime}-Y-Y^{\prime}]=\sum_{a+b=m}h_{a}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})h_{b}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}/\mathbf{y}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle e_{m}\bigl((\mathbf{x}\sqcup\mathbf{x}^{\prime})/(\mathbf{y}\sqcup\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\bigr)

\displaystyle=e_{m}[X+X^{\prime}-Y-Y^{\prime}]=\sum_{a+b=m}e_{a}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})e_{b}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}/\mathbf{y}^{\prime}),

which are well-known identities (see,
e.g.
,
[
HJK+21
, Prop. 2.1]
).
Next, we recall the notation given in
[
HJK+21
, Def. 2.4]
:

h_{m}[X\ominus Y]:=\sum_{a-b=m}h_{a}[X]h_{b}[Y],\qquad\qquad e_{m}[X\ominus Y]:=\sum_{a-b=m}e_{a}[X]e_{b}[Y].

We note that these can have infinite nonzero terms and be nonzero even when
m
is negative.

In order to avoid confusion with the plethystic negative and negating the variables, we will not use plethystic notation, and instead write
h_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\!\!/\mathbf{y}):=h_{m}[X\ominus Y]
and
e_{m}(\mathbf{x}/\!\!/\mathbf{y}):=e_{m}[X\ominus Y]
.

2.2.
Free-fermions and Wick’s theorem

We describe free-fermions and Wick’s theorem here.

Let
\mathbf{k}
be a field of characteristic
0
.
The (
\mathbf{k}
-)algebra of
free-fermions

\mathcal{A}
is the associative unital
\mathbf{k}
-algebra generated by
\{\psi_{n},\psi_{n}^{*}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
subject to the anti-commuting relations

\psi_{m}\psi_{n}+\psi_{n}\psi_{m}=\psi_{m}^{*}\psi_{n}^{*}+\psi_{n}^{*}\psi_{m}^{*}=0,\qquad\qquad\psi_{m}\psi_{n}^{*}+\psi_{n}^{*}\psi_{m}=\delta_{m,n}.

This is the Clifford algebra that often appears in mathematical physics for the vector space with a basis indexed by
\mathbb{Z}\sqcup\mathbb{Z}
.
There exists an anti-algebra involution on
\mathcal{A}
defined by
\psi_{n}\leftrightarrow\psi_{n}^{\ast}
satisfying
(xy)^{\ast}=y^{\ast}x^{\ast}
for any
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
.

We define
(fermionic) Fock space

\mathcal{F}
as the subspace of
\bigwedge^{\infty}V
, where
V=\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbf{k}v_{i}
, with the basis

\{v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots\mid i_{1}>i_{2}>\cdots,\;i_{k}=-k+m\text{ for sufficiently large $k$ and some }m\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

We make
\mathcal{F}
into a left
\mathcal{A}
-representation generated by the
vacuum vector

\lvert 0\rangle=v_{-1}\wedge v_{-2}\wedge v_{-3}\wedge\cdots

with the action of
\mathcal{A}
given by

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots)

\displaystyle=v_{n}\wedge v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots,

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{*}(v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k-1}v_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{i_{k-1}}\wedge v_{i_{k+1}}\wedge\cdots&\text{if there exists $k$ such that $i_{k}=n$},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and extended linearly.
We can see that
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the relations

\psi_{n}\lvert 0\rangle=\psi_{m}^{*}\lvert 0\rangle=0,\qquad\qquad n<0,\quad m\geq 0,

and the basis can be described by the vectors

\psi_{n_{1}}\psi_{n_{2}}\cdots\psi_{n_{r}}\psi_{m_{1}}^{*}\psi_{m_{2}}^{*}\cdots\psi_{m_{s}}^{*}\lvert 0\rangle,\qquad(r,s\geq 0,n_{1}>\cdots n_{r}\geq 0>m_{s}>\cdots>m_{1}).

We refer the reader to
[
KRR13b
, Sec. 5]
and
[
MJD00
, Sec. 4]
for more information.

In the language of Clifford algebras, Fock space is a spinor representation of
\mathcal{A}
.
The basis elements of Fock space corresponds to a single (semi-infinite) wedge product of basis vectors
v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge\cdots
.
We can consider such basis elements as being a state in a system of particles on a line with particles at positions
i_{1}
,
i_{2}
, and so on.
Thus, the vacuum vector corresponds to the state where all of the particles are to the left of the position
-1/2
, which is called the step initial condition in the asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP) or the Dirac sea in the mathematical physics literature.

We can similarly define the dual Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{*}
as the right
\mathcal{A}
-representation generated by the (dual) vacuum vector

\langle 0\rvert=\cdots\wedge v_{2}\wedge v_{1}\wedge v_{0}

with the action of
\mathcal{A}
given by

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(\cdots\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge v_{i_{1}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k-1}\cdots\wedge v_{i_{k+1}}\wedge v_{i_{k-1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{i_{1}}\!&\text{if there exists $k$ such that $i_{k}=n-1$},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\psi_{n}^{*}(\cdots\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge v_{i_{1}})

\displaystyle=\cdots\wedge v_{i_{2}}\wedge v_{i_{1}}\wedge v_{n-1}.

We can see that the roles of
\psi_{j}
and
\psi_{j}^{*}
have been reversed compared to
\mathcal{F}
(albeit with the indices trivially shifted by one for their action on the wedge space).
Therefore, dual Fock space
\mathcal{F}^{*}
satisfies the relations

\langle 0\rvert\psi_{m}=\langle 0\rvert\psi_{n}^{*}=0,\qquad\qquad n<0,\quad m\geq 0,

has a basis given by

\langle 0\rvert\psi_{m_{s}}\cdots\psi_{m_{2}}\psi_{m_{1}}\psi_{n_{r}}^{*}\cdots\psi_{n_{2}}^{*}\psi_{n_{1}}^{*},\qquad(r,s\geq 0,n_{1}>\cdots n_{r}\geq 0>m_{s}>\cdots>m_{1}),

and has an isomorphism
\ast\colon\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{*}
given by
X\lvert 0\rangle\mapsto\langle 0\rvert X^{*}
.
This isomorphism encodes the particle-hole duality of Fock space, as the role the indices
i_{j}
give the positions of the locations without a particle.
We will also use the
shifted vacuum vectors

\lvert m\rangle=\begin{cases}\psi_{m-1}\dotsm\psi_{0}\lvert 0\rangle&\text{if }m\geq 0,\\
\psi_{m}^{*}\dotsm\psi_{-1}^{*}\lvert 0\rangle&\text{if }m<0,\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\langle m\rvert=\begin{cases}\langle 0\rvert\psi_{0}^{*}\dotsm\psi_{m-1}^{*}&\text{if }m\geq 0,\\
\langle 0\rvert\psi_{-1}\dotsm\psi_{m}&\text{if }m<0.\end{cases}

The
vacuum expectation value
is the unique
\mathbf{k}
-bilinear map

\mathcal{F}^{*}\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{F}\to\mathbf{k},\qquad\langle w\rvert\otimes_{\mathbf{k}}\lvert v\rangle\mapsto\langle w|v\rangle\quad\text{ satisfying }\quad\langle 0|0\rangle=1,\qquad(\langle w\rvert X)\lvert v\rangle=\langle w\rvert(X\lvert v\rangle),

for all
X\in\mathcal{A}
.
Note the relation
\langle w\rvert X\lvert v\rangle=\langle v^{\ast}\rvert X^{*}\lvert w^{\ast}\rangle
, where
\lvert v^{\ast}\rangle=(\langle v\rvert)^{\ast}
and
\langle w^{\ast}\rvert=(\lvert w\rangle)^{\ast}
.
We use the abbreviation
\langle X\rangle=\langle 0\rvert X\lvert 0\rangle
.
To compute vacuum expectation values, we use
Wick’s theorem
.

Theorem 2.1

(Wick’s theorem
[
MJD00
]
)
.

For any finite subsets

\{m_{1},\dotsc,m_{r}\},\{n_{1},\dotsc,n_{r}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\langle\psi_{m_{r}}\dotsm\psi_{m_{1}}\psi_{n_{1}}^{*}\dotsm\psi_{n_{r}}^{*}\rangle=\det\bigl[\langle\psi_{m_{i}}\psi_{n_{j}}^{*}\rangle\bigr]_{i,j=1}^{r}.

Next, we define the
current operators
as

a_{k}:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}:\mathrel{\psi_{i}\psi_{i+k}^{*}}:,

where
:\mathrel{\bullet}:
denotes the normal ordering of free-fermions, which only is important for when
k=0
(see,
e.g.

[
AZ13
, Sec. 2]
and
[
MJD00
, Sec. 5.2]
):

:\mathrel{\psi_{i}\psi_{i+k}^{*}}:\;:=\begin{cases}\psi_{i}\psi_{i+k}^{*}&\text{if }i\leq 0,\\
-\psi_{i+k}^{*}\psi_{i}&\text{if }i>0.\end{cases}

We have
a_{k}^{*}=a_{-k}
.
The current operators define the infinite dimensional Heisenberg algebra, which means they satisfy the relations

(2.1)

2.3.
Dual and transpose Hamiltonian operators

In the sequel, we will use other variations of the Hamiltonian operator.
We first define the
(formal) dual Hamiltonian operator

H^{*}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y}):=\bigl(H(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})\bigr)^{*}=\sum_{n>0}\frac{p_{n}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}{n}a_{-n}.

Dual to the usual Hamiltonian operator, by applying
\ast
to (
2.2
) we have

(2.3a)

\displaystyle e^{-H^{*}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\psi^{*}_{k}e^{H^{*}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}h_{i}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})\psi^{*}_{k-i},

(2.3b)

\displaystyle e^{H^{*}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\psi_{k}e^{-H^{*}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}h_{i}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})\psi_{k+i},

where the expansion is finite if and only if only finitely many
\mathbf{x}
are nonzero and
\mathbf{y}=\emptyset
.

To encode the action of
\omega
, we use the
transposed Hamiltonian operator
, which is denoted by

J(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y}):=\omega H(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=\sum_{k>0}\frac{\omega p_{k}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}{k}a_{k}=\sum_{k>0}(-1)^{k-1}\frac{p_{k}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}{k}a_{k}=-H(-\mathbf{x}/{-\mathbf{y}}).

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mu\rvert e^{J(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mu\rvert\omega e^{H(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda\rangle=\langle\mu\rvert e^{-H(-\mathbf{x}/-\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda\rangle=\omega s_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=s_{\lambda/\mu}(-\mathbf{y}/{-\mathbf{x}}).

We can realize this more fundamentally using free-fermions by realizing the
01
-sequence of
\lambda^{\prime}
is equal to that of
\lambda
in reverse and interchanging
0\leftrightarrow 1
, and so

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\lvert\lambda^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\left\lvert\lambda\right\rvert}\psi^{*}_{-\lambda_{1}}\psi^{*}_{1-\lambda_{2}}\dotsm\psi^{*}_{\ell-1-\lambda_{\ell}}\lvert\ell\rangle,

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\langle\mu^{\prime}\rvert

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\left\lvert\mu\right\rvert}\langle\ell\rvert\psi_{\ell-1-\mu_{\ell}}\psi_{\ell-2-\mu_{\ell-1}}\dotsm\psi_{-\mu_{1}},

(
cf.

[
AZ13
, Prop. 2.1]
).
Theorem
2.2
states
s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=\langle\mu^{\prime}\rvert e^{H(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda^{\prime}\rangle
, but by using these alternate descriptions and Wick’s theorem, we can obtain the dual Jacobi–Trudi formula for
(-1)^{\left\lvert\lambda/\mu\right\rvert}s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})
.
This is also the same determinant formula from
\langle\mu\rvert e^{J(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda\rangle
by Wick’s theorem, which yields the nontrivial relation

\langle\mu\rvert e^{J(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda\rangle=\langle\mu^{\prime}\rvert e^{H(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})}\lvert\lambda^{\prime}\rangle.

Hence, this identity is equivalent to
s_{\lambda/\mu}(\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y})=(-1)^{\left\lvert\lambda/\mu\right\rvert}s_{\lambda^{\prime}/\mu^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y}/\mathbf{x})
.

We also require the following expansions for a single variable
\gamma
:

(2.5)

e^{H(\gamma)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}H_{i}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)\gamma^{i},\qquad\qquad e^{J(\gamma)}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}E_{i}(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots)\gamma^{i}.

As a consequence, we have
[e^{H(\gamma)},a_{k}]=[e^{J(\gamma)},a_{k}]=0
for all

and
[e^{H(\gamma)},a_{-k}]=\gamma^{k}e^{H(\gamma)}

In order to make the indexing match the usual covariant and contravariant indexing, for a matrix

M^{*}=[(M^{*})_{\mu}^{\lambda}:=(M_{\lambda}^{\mu})^{*}]_{\mu,\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}

This agrees with thinking of
\ast
as the transpose conjugate and the
\ast
duality operator.

We give the rectification lemma, a key computational tool in showing how to transform vectors for a general sequence

Lemma 3.6

(Rectification lemma)

If
k\leq j

\displaystyle\psi_{k}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k}^{j}\gamma^{j-i+1}\psi_{i}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{k-1}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{j}\gamma^{j-i}\psi_{i}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{k},

Proof.

For the first equality, using (

\displaystyle\psi_{k}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\psi_{k}\gamma^{i}\psi_{j-i}e^{H(\gamma)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=0}^{j-k-1}\gamma^{i}\psi_{j-i}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{k}+\sum_{i=0}^{j-k-1}\gamma^{i+1}\psi_{j-i}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{k-1}+\gamma^{j-k+1}\psi_{k}e^{H(\gamma)}\psi_{k-1},
