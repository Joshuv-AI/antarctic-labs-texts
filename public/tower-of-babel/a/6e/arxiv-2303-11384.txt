Classical Density Functional Theory Reveals Structural Information of H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} Fluids Adsorbed in MOF-5
Elvis do A. Soares, Amaro G. Barreto Jr, Frederico W. Tavares

Abstract
This study employs classical Density Functional Theory (cDFT) to investigate the adsorption isotherms and structural information of H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} fluids inside MOF-5. The results indicate that the adsorption of both fluids is highly dependent on the fluid temperature and the shape of the MOF-5 structure. Specifically, the CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} molecules exhibit stronger interactions with the MOF-5 framework, resulting in a greater adsorbed quantity compared to H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} . Additionally, the cDFT calculations reveal that the adsorption process is influenced by the fluid-fluid spatial correlations between the fluid molecules and the external potential produced by the MOF-5 solid atoms. These findings are supported by comparison with experimental data of adsorbed amount and the structure factor of the adsorbed fluid inside the MOF-5. We demonstrate the importance of choosing the appropriate grid size in calculating the adsorption isotherm and the fluid structure factors within the MOF-5. Overall, this work provides valuable insights into the adsorption mechanism of H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} in MOF-5, emphasizing the importance of considering the structural properties of the adsorbed fluids in MOFs for predicting and designing their gas storage capacity at different thermodynamic conditions.



I
Introduction

The emergent global energy demand has resulted in a pressing need for the efficient storage of fuel gases such as hydrogen (
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) and methane (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
). In response to this challenge, one of the most promising solutions is the use of nanoporous materials for gas adsorption.
Broom et al. 2016
;
Anstine et al. 2022
These materials are characterized by their pore sizes that fall within the nanoscale range. To achieve effective gas storage, a detailed understanding of the adsorption processes of these gases on nanoporous materials is essential.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are a class of porous materials that emerged as promising alternatives for gas storage
Li et al. 2018
;
Ding et al. 2019
and separation applications
Qian et al. 2020
;
Lin et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2021
due to their high surface areas
Qiu et al. 2014
;
Bavykina et al. 2020
, tailorable topologies, tunable functional groups, and chemical, mechanical and thermal stabilities
Howarth et al. 2016
;
Yuan et al. 2018
. MOFs consist of metal nodes and organic linkers combined to form porous structures with a wide range of geometries, sizes, and functionalities, making them versatile materials for other applications, such as catalysis, sensing, and drug delivery.

Some experimental studies have reported on the ability of MOFs to store
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Suh et al. 2012
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
Kwon and Jiang 2022
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

Zhou et al. 2007
;
Kaye et al. 2007
, making them attractive materials for gas storage applications. For efficient gas storage, it is essential to model the adsorption processes of these gases on MOFs. This involves understanding the interaction between the gas molecules and the material surface, and the factors that affect adsorption, such as temperature and pressure. By modeling these processes, we can design MOFs with optimized properties for gas storage.

The traditional technique to study gas adsorption in MOFs or other materials is the Monte Carlo (MC) method
Keskin and Sholl 2009
;
Gallo and Glossman-Mitnik 2009
;
Getman et al. 2012
;
Prakash et al. 2013
. This method models the adsorption process at the molecular level, taking into account the complex interaction between the gas molecules and the MOF structure. As an example, the adsorption amount of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

Yang and Zhong 2006
;
Liu et al. 2009
;
Fu et al. 2015a
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

Düren et al. 2004
;
Pillai et al. 2015
;
Fu et al. 2015b
;
Altintas and Keskin 2016
in MOF-5 are well calculated from GCMC.

Another potential method to explore adsorption on nanoporous materials is the classical Density Functional Theory (cDFT) of fluids. In general, the formalism proposed by the cDFT allows evaluating the density distribution of the fluid inside the porous material, from which the absolute adsorbed amount can be easily obtained. Recently, some works have presented the ability of cDFT to calculate the adsorbed amount in MOFs materials. Liu
et al.

Liu et al. 2009
utilized the weighted density approximation (WDA) for both repulsive and attractive contributions, and they calculated the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
adsorption in MOF-5 and ZIF-8. They concluded that the WDA approach for the attractive contribution is more accurate than the Mean-Field Approximation (MFA). Fu
et al.

Fu et al. 2015b
;
Fu et al. 2015a
used cDFT calculations to investigate
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
storage capacity in several MOFs. They used the fundamental measure theory (FMT) to account for the repulsion effects, and they compared four versions of approximations to describe the attractive contribution: an MFA; a quadratic functional Taylor’s expansion method; a full WDA; and an MFA corrected by WDA, the called novel weighted density functional (WDFT) proposed by Yu
Yu 2009
. Their results demonstrate that WDFT is the most accurate formulation for MOF adsorption calculations, even at high pressures and low temperatures. Thereafter, FMT combined with WDFT has been utilized for high-throughput screening of MOFs for
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
storage
Liu et al. 2015
,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
/
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
separation
Guo et al. 2016
, and noble gas separation
Guo et al. 2018
. Trejos
et al.

Trejos et al. 2014
;
Trejos et al. 2018
have applied the SAFT-VRQ to model selectivities and adsorption isotherms of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
/
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
mixtures onto MOFs using an approximation of cDFT and
semiclassical quantum correction. Very recently, Sang
et al.

Sang et al. 2021
and Kessler
et al.

Kessler et al. 2021
;
Kessler et al. 2022
have combined the cDFT with PC-SAFT to calculate the adsorption of more complex fluids and mixtures in MOFs and COFs.

In this work, we use the cDFT of fluids to accurately model the intricate fluid-solid interactions and fluid-fluid correlations within MOF-5. Specifically, we investigate the behavior of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
fluids within the MOF-5 nanoporous material. According to the cDFT, the grand thermodynamic potential and Helmholtz free energy are functionals of the local density distribution,
\rho({\bf\it r})
. The interactions between the fluid particles can be taken into account by the use of the excess free energy functional. To achieve accurate predictions, we incorporate the FMT plus the WDFT as the excess free energy functional. The WDFT involves a corrected mean-field theory with a more accurate equation of state for the Lennard-Jones (LJ) fluid from Johnson, Zollweg, and Gubbins (JZG)
Johnson et al. 1993
, which improves the description of fluid-fluid spatial correlations and bulk thermodynamic properties. We investigate the grid size dependence of the cDFT calculation on the MOF-5 3D geometry. Our results show that the grid size slightly influences the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
adsorption isotherm calculation in MOF-5. We also discuss the ability of the cDFT to calculate the structure of the fluid inside the MOF-5 at different thermodynamic conditions. The grid size strongly influences the calculated structural information of the fluid inside the nanoporous material. Furthermore, we propose that the structure factor must be obtained experimentally or by GCMC simulation to be compared with our cDFT calculation in the future. Such information would be necessary to formulate new excess free energy functionals.

presents a brief background regarding the implementation of the cDFT and its application for describing gas adsorption in MOFs. The discussion of the results from the proposed strategy is performed in Section
III
and compared with the experimental data of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

IV
presents the conclusions of this work.

II
Theory and Methods

II.1
Classical Density Functional Theory

According to the cDFT of fluids
Henderson 1992
;
Wu 2008
;
Evans 2009
;
Hansen and McDonald 2013
, the grand thermodynamic potential,
\Omega[\rho({\bf\it r})]
, and the Helmholtz free-energy,
F[\rho({\bf\it r})]
, are functionals of the local density distribution
\rho({\bf\it r})
. The grand potential functional
\Omega[\rho({\bf\it r})]
is related to the free energy functional
F[\rho({\bf\it r})]
by a thermodynamic relation given as

\displaystyle\Omega[\rho({\bf\it r})]=F[\rho({\bf\it r})]+\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}}[\phi_{\text{ext}}({\bf\it r})-\mu]\rho({\bf\it r}),

(1)

where
\mu
is the equilibrium chemical potential and
\phi_{\text{ext}}({\bf\it r})
is an external potential acting on the fluid. The Helmholtz free energy functional is determined by the sum of two terms, in the form

\displaystyle F[\rho({\bf\it r})]=F_{\text{id}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]+F_{\text{exc}}[\rho({\bf\it r})],

(2)

where the first term is the ideal gas contribution and the second term is the excess free energy parcel (excess of ideal gas). The ideal-gas contribution
F^{\text{id}}
is given by the exact expression

\displaystyle F^{\text{id}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]=k_{B}T\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}}\rho({\bf\it r})[\ln(\rho(\vb*{r})\Lambda^3)-1],

(3)

where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the absolute temperature, and
\Lambda
is the well-known thermal de Broglie wavelength. The grand potential
\Omega[\rho({\bf\it r})]
has a minimum value when
\rho({\bf\it r})
is the equilibrium density distribution,
i.e.
, the minimum value of
\Omega[\rho({\bf\it r})]
is the equilibrium grand potential of the system. Then, the equilibrium density profile should be calculated extremizing the grand canonical potential, such that

\displaystyle\left.\functionalderivative{\Omega[\rho(\vb*{r})]}{\rho(\vb*{r})}\right|_{\mu,V,T}=\

\displaystyle k_{B}T\ln(\rho(\vb*{r})\Lambda^3)+\functionalderivative{F_\text{exc}[\rho(\vb*{r})]}{\rho(\vb*{r})}

\displaystyle+\phi_{\text{ext}}({\bf\it r})-\mu=0.

(4)

Using the definition of the chemical potential in the form
\mu=k_{B}T\ln(\rho_b\Lambda^3)+\mu_{\text{exc}}
, where
\rho_{b}
is the uniform fluid bulk density, we can write the simplified form of Eq. (
4
) as

\displaystyle\rho({\bf\it r})=\rho_{b}\exp[-\beta\phi_{\text{ext}}({\bf\it r})+\Delta c^{(1)}({\bf\it r})],

(5)

where
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
is the inverse of the thermal energy, and the term
\Delta c^{(1)}({\bf\it r})
is related to the first-order direct correlation function
c^{(1)}({\bf\it r})
through the relation
\Delta c^{(1)}({\bf\it r})=-\beta\functionalderivative*{F_\text{exc}[\rho(\vb*{r})]}{\rho(\vb*{r})}+\beta\mu^{\text{exc}}
, which acts as a correction for the external potential
\phi_{\text{ext}}({\bf\it r})
due to the fluid-fluid correlation.

The excess free energy functional
F_{\text{exc}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]
contains all the information about the interaction between particles given by the pair potential
u({\bf\it r}-{\bf\it r}^{\prime})
. In our problem, the molecule-molecule interactions of the fluid can be well described by the Lennard-Jones potential in the form

\displaystyle u_{\text{lj}}(r)=4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right].

(6)

To describe the
F_{\text{exc}}
generated by both the repulsive short-range and the attractive long-range interactions of the LJ potential, we use the frequent separation of this excess free energy into repulsive and attractive contributions
Henderson 1992
;
Peng and Yu 2008
;
Lutsko 2008
;
Evans 2009
,
F_{\text{rep}}
and
F_{\text{att}}
, respectively, such that

\displaystyle F_{\text{exc}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]=F_{\text{rep}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]+F_{\text{att}}[\rho({\bf\it r})].

(7)

The repulsive term
F_{\text{rep}}
in Eq. (
7
) can be modeled by the free energy functional of a reference hard-sphere fluid
Kierlik and Rosinberg 1991
defined by a short-range hard-sphere potential given by

\displaystyle u_{\text{hs}}(r)=\begin{cases}\infty,\quad&r<d,\\
0,&r>d,\end{cases}

(8)

where
d
is an effective diameter of the hard-core repulsion. Barker and Henderson’s
Barker and Henderson 1967a
;
Barker and Henderson 1967b
temperature-dependent diameter, defined by
d=\int_{0}^{\sigma}[1-e^{-\beta u_{\text{lj}}(r)}]\differential{r}
, can approximate this effective diameter. This integral can be approximated by the formula
Cotterman et al. 1986

\displaystyle\frac{d}{\sigma}=\frac{1+0.2977T^{*}}{1+0.33163T^{*}+1.0477\times 10^{-3}T^{*2}},

(9)

where
T^{*}=k_{B}T/\epsilon
is the reduced temperature. The modified fundamental measure theory (FMT)
Rosenfeld 1989
accurately describes the hard-sphere fluid structures and can represent the hard-sphere free energy functional,
F_{\text{hs}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]
. In this work, we have applied the antisymmetrized version of the White-Bear functional
Rosenfeld et al. 1997
;
Yu and Wu 2002
;
Roth et al. 2002
for the hard-sphere Helmholtz free energy contribution as

\displaystyle F_{\text{rep}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]=F_{\text{hs}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]=k_{B}T\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}}\Phi_{\text{FMT}}(\{n^{(\alpha)}({\bf\it r})\}),

(10)

where
\Phi_{\text{FMT}}
is the local reduced free energy density of a mixture of hard-spheres, which is a function of the set of weighted densities,
n^{(\alpha)}({\bf\it r})
.

The attractive term
F_{\text{att}}
in Eq. (
7
) represents the excess Helmholtz free energy contribution due to the particle-particle attractive interaction defined by the potential

\displaystyle u_{\text{att}}(r)=\begin{cases}0,\quad&r<\sigma,\\
-\epsilon_{1}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{1}(r/\sigma-1)}}{r/\sigma}-\epsilon_{2}\frac{e^{-\lambda_{2}(r/\sigma-1)}}{r/\sigma},&r>\sigma,\end{cases}

(11)

with
\epsilon_{1}=-\epsilon_{2}=1.8577\epsilon
,
\lambda_{1}=2.5449
, and
\lambda_{2}=15.4641
. The attractive parcel of the LJ potential was mapped onto a Two-Yukawa potential, as proposed previously
Kalyuzhnyi and Cummings 1996
;
Tang et al. 1997
;
Tang and Lu 2001
to facilitate the Fourier Transform on the numerical calculations as discussed in Supporting Information. The free energy functional
F_{\text{att}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]
can be described by the novel weighted density functional theory (WDFT)
Yu 2009
as the sum of a mean-field term and a correlation contribution, respectively, in the form

\displaystyle F_{\text{att}}[\rho({\bf\it r})]=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}}\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}'}\rho({\bf\it r})u_{\text{att}}(|{\bf\it r}-{\bf\it r}^{\prime}|)\rho({\bf\it r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+k_{B}T\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}}\Phi_{\text{corr}}(\bar{\rho}({\bf\it r})),

(12)

where the weighted density field here is given by
\bar{\rho}({\bf\it r})=\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}'}\rho({\bf\it r}^{\prime})\omega_{\text{wdft}}({\bf\it r}-{\bf\it r}^{\prime})
with
\omega_{\text{wdft}}({\bf\it r})=\Theta(d-|{\bf\it r}|)/(4\pi d^{3}/3)
, and
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside function. The correlation free energy density is defined as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{corr}}(\rho)=\beta\frac{F_{\text{JZG}}(\rho)}{V}-\beta\frac{F_{\text{hs}}(\rho)}{V}-\beta\rho^{2}a_{\text{mft}},

(13)

giving the appropriated free energy for the bulk LJ fluid. The MFT parameter can be identified as the integral
a_{\text{mft}}=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}u_{\text{att}}(r)r^{2}\differential{r}=-(16/9)\pi\epsilon\sigma^{3}
.

Finally, the absolute adsorption quantity can be calculated by the definition as

\displaystyle N_{\text{abs}}=\int_{V_{\text{uc}}}\rho({\bf\it r})\differential{\vb*{r}},

(14)

where the local density distribution
\rho({\bf\it r})
is given by Eq. (
5
). The excess adsorption quantity,
N_{\text{exc}}
, is related to
N_{\text{abs}}
by

\displaystyle N_{\text{exc}}=N_{\text{abs}}-\rho_{b}V_{\text{pore}},

(15)

where
\rho_{b}
is the fluid bulk density, and
V_{\text{pore}}
is the pore volume obtained from a helium (He) pycnometry
Myers and Monson 2002
. This pore volume also can be calculated by the relation

\displaystyle V_{\text{pore}}=\int_{V_{\text{uc}}}\exp[-\beta\phi_{\text{ext}}^{(\text{He})}({\bf\it r})]\differential{\vb*{r}},

(16)

where here
\phi_{\text{ext}}^{(\text{He})}({\bf\it r})
is the fluid-solid interaction of a single He atom (
\sigma_{\text{He}}=2.58~$\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolAngstrom}$
,
\epsilon_{\text{He}}/k_{B}=10.22~$\mathrm{K}$
)
Hirschfelder et al. 1964
. We can note that the exponential in Eq. (
16
) is higher than unity at the regions where
\phi_{\text{ext}}^{(\text{He})}<0
, and the exponential goes to zero at the solid atoms’ positions. As Eq. (
16
) is temperature-dependent, the pore volume is calculated at the standard temperature of 298 K.

The cDFT also possesses a notable capability of predicting the structural characteristics of the fluid confined within the porous material by utilizing the local density field,
\rho({\bf\it r})
. In our specific case, the local density field is a 3D field, which necessitates the utilization of isosurfaces to visualize our cDFT results. The structure factor
S(q)
is related to the power spectral density by

\displaystyle S(q)\propto|\tilde{\rho}_{{\bf\it k}}|^{2},

(17)

where
q=(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+k_{z}^{2})^{1/2}
is the magnitude of the wavenumber. Here,
\tilde{\rho}_{{\bf\it k}}
is the Fourier transform of the density profiles is computed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{{\bf\it k}}=\mathcal{F}\{\rho({\bf\it r})\}=\int_{V}\differential{\vb*{r}}\rho({\bf\it r})e^{-i{\bf\it k}\cdot{\bf\it r}}.

(18)

II.4
Numerical Methods

To speed up the numerical calculations, we used of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to calculate all the density convolutions. The analytical Fourier transform of the density profiles is computed from Eq. (
18
), where
\mathcal{F}
represents the Fourier transform operator and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
represents the inverse Fourier transform operator given by

\displaystyle\rho({\bf\it r})\equiv\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\tilde{\rho}_{{\bf\it k}}\}=\frac{1}{N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}}\sum_{{\bf\it k}}\tilde{\rho}_{{\bf\it k}}e^{i{\bf\it k}\cdot{\bf\it r}},

(21)

where
k_{x}=\{0,2\pi/L_{x},4\pi/L_{x},\ldots,(N_{x}-1)2\pi/L_{x}\}
,
L_{x}
is the length of the box in the
x
-direction, and
N_{x}=L_{x}/\Delta x
is the number of gridpoints in the
x
-direction. And the same approach is used in other directions. We implemented the Fourier transform of the weight distribution
\tilde{\omega}_{\alpha}({\bf\it k})
analytically in the reciprocal space. The weighted densities are then calculated

\displaystyle n^{(\alpha)}({\bf\it r})

\displaystyle=\int\differential{\vb*{r}'}\rho({\bf\it r}^{\prime})\omega^{(\alpha)}({\bf\it r}-{\bf\it r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\mathcal{F}\{\rho({\bf\it r})\}\mathcal{F}\{\omega^{(\alpha)}({\bf\it r})\}\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\tilde{\rho}_{{\bf\it k}}\tilde{\omega}^{(\alpha)}_{{\bf\it k}}\}.

(22)

All these convolutions, i.e., Eqs. (
22
) were solved using the FFT functions from the
Scipy
package.
Virtanen et al. 2020
Our group implements, the FMT and WDFT functionals in
Python
code.
Soares 2023
The Gibbs phenomenon was reduced by multiplicating the analytical Fourier transform
\tilde{\omega}_{\alpha}({\bf\it k})
by the Lanczos
\sigma
-factor,
\sigma(k)=\sin(k/k_\text{max})/(k/k_{\text{max}})
, where
k_{\text{max}}
is the maximum wavenumber from the FFT procedure.

The equilibrium condition for the cDFT, Eq. (
4
), is solved using a fast inertial relaxation engine (FIRE)
Bitzek et al. 2006
;
Guénolé et al. 2020
also implemented in
Python
by our group.
Soares 2020
;
Sermoud et al. 2021
The FIRE parameteres used in this work are
\alpha=0.2
and
dt=0.01
. The algorithm convergence criterion is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{x}N_{y}N_{z}}}\norm{\frac{\beta\fdv*{\Omega}{\rho(\vb*{r})}}{(atol + rtol|\rho(\vb*{r})|)} }_{F}\leq 1,

(23)

with
atol=10^{-6}
and
rtol=10^{-4}
, where
\norm{A}_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. The initial density was considered uniform and equal to
\rho_{b}
. In the highly repulsive region, where
\phi_{\text{ext}}({\bf\it r})/k_{B}>1.6\times 10^{4}
K, the initial density
\rho({\bf\it r})
was assumed to be zero. For the adsorption isotherm calculations, the density profile obtained at low pressures is used as the initial profile of the next step with higher pressure. We have used a pressure step of 0.1 bar from 0.1 bar to 2.0 bar, a step of 1 bar from 2 bar to 10 bar, and a step of 10 bar from 20 bar to 500 bar.

IV
Conclusions

The present study highlights the importance of temperature and pressure in the gas adsorption of MOF-5 for storage applications. For MOF-5, our results indicate that
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
adsorption is more favorable than
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
due to the higher LJ interaction parameter of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
. At 300 K,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
adsorption saturates at high pressures near the Zn atoms and tends to occupy the region near the organic linkers, while
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
concentration always increases near the Zn atoms. The results suggest that the interaction parameters, volume exclusion effects, and the size of the gas molecules influence the adsorption behavior.

By implementing of cDFT, the local density distribution of adsorbed fluids within MOF-5 can be precisely computed, enabling insights into the adsorption mechanisms of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
molecules in this material. Notably, the adsorption mechanism is not directly dependent on the pore description of MOF-5 as a spherical pore or its pore size. Our calculated density isosurfaces suggested that the adsorption behavior is strongly linked to the spatial distribution of the constituent atoms within the framework.

The fluid’s structure factor can be derived from the local density distribution of adsorbed fluids within MOF-5. The structure factor curves can provide valuable information about the spatial distribution of fluid molecules within MOF-5. The locations and concentrations of the adsorbed molecules can be determined by comparing the peaks in the structure factor curves with the geometrical information of the MOF-5 framework.

The results presented here are significant as they provide insight into
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
adsorption behavior in MOF-5 under different thermodynamic conditions for storage applications. The latest GCMC and cDFT works reported results at temperature values of 298 K for both fluids and 77 K for hydrogen. There is a lack of simulated data at intermediate temperatures. However, our cDFT calculations are predictive at intermediate temperatures without any additional adjustment of solid-fluid interaction parameters. Moreover, the combination of calculated fluid structure factor and small-angle scattering experiments (
e.g.
SAXS and SANS)
Lee et al. 2013
;
Ting et al. 2015
;
Welborn and Detsi 2020
can provide a comprehensive understanding of the adsorption behavior of fluid molecules inside the MOF-5, which is essential for developing efficient gas storage and separation technologies.
