A fully discrete evolving surface finite element method for the Cahn-Hilliard equation with a regular potential
Charles M. Elliott Thomas Sales

Abstract
. We study two fully discrete evolving surface finite element schemes for the Cahn-Hilliard equation on an evolving surface, given a smooth potential with polynomial growth. In particular we establish optimal order error bounds for a (fully implicit) backward Euler time-discretisation, and an implicit-explicit time-discretisation, with isoparametric surface finite elements discretising space.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we present optimal order error bounds for fully discrete evolving surface finite element (ESFEM) schemes for the Cahn-Hilliard equation posed on a closed, orientable evolving surface,
(\Gamma(t))_{t\in[0,T]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The evolution of the surface is assumed to be sufficiently smooth, and known a priori.
In this setting, the Cahn-Hilliard equation, as formulated in
[
13
]
, is given by

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\partial^{\bullet}u+u(\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)=\Delta_{\Gamma}w,\\
w=-\varepsilon\Delta_{\Gamma}u+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F^{\prime}(u),\end{gathered}

subject to the initial condition
u(0)=u_{0}
for suitable initial data. For simplicity we do not consider arbitrary parametrisations of
\Gamma(t)
, and focus on the setting where
\Gamma(t)
is parametrised using the advective tangential velocity associated to the model.
If one chooses an arbitrary parametrisation then the first equation of (
1
) changes as in
[
13
]
.

In this paper we focus on a smooth potential
F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
F(r)=F_{1}(r)-\frac{\theta}{2}r^{2},
and

(1)

F(r)\geq\beta
,

(2)

F_{1}\geq 0
is convex,

(3)

\exists q\in[1,\infty)
such that
|F_{1}^{\prime}(r)|\leq\alpha|r|^{q}+\alpha
,

where
\alpha\geq 0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
q\geq 1
.
A notable example is the quartic potential,
F(r)=\frac{(1-r^{2})^{2}}{4}
, as studied in
[
27
]
.
The system has an associated energy functional, known as the Ginzburg-Landau functional, given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle E^{\mathrm{CH}}[u;t]:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\frac{\varepsilon|\nabla_{\Gamma}u|^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(u),

where the constant
\varepsilon>0
is proportional to the width of a diffuse interface separating phase domains in which the solution
u
takes values close to the minima of
F
.
For a stationary domain it is known that the Cahn-Hilliard equation is the
H^{-1}
gradient flow of the Ginzburg-Landau (see for instance
[
6
]
).
This is not the case for an evolving surface, as remarked in
[
27
]
, but this functional remains useful in the the analysis nonetheless.

The Cahn-Hilliard equation originates from the work of Cahn and Hilliard in 1958,
[
16
]
, in modelling phase separation in a binary alloy.
From a modelling perspective, the function
u
is understood as the concentration of the mixture. In the case of a double well potential the two minima are associated with two phases.
The Cahn-Hilliard equation naturally finds applications in metallurgy, for example in the studying phenomenon of spinodal decomposition and phase separation
[
15
,
42
]
.
Outside of this application, modified versions of the Cahn-Hilliard equation have found use in the modelling tumour growth
[
34
,
35
]
, and in the study of the dynamics of lipid biomembranes
[
8
,
47
]
.
This study has typically been done on a stationary, Euclidean domain — see for example
[
25
,
43
]
.
The study of Cahn-Hilliard equations on evolving surfaces is motivated by applications such as
[
24
,
48
]
.
We refer to
[
13
,
14
,
29
]
for recent results on the analysis.

The study of spatially discrete numerical schemes for solving the Cahn-Hilliard equation on evolving surfaces was initiated in

[
27
]
and extended in
[
10
]
. Error bounds for fully discrete ESFEM schemes for second order parabolic equations have been shown in
[
21
,
23
,
41
,
33
,
38
,
39
]
. We also note that the Cahn-Hilliard equation on a stationary surface was studied in
[
20
]
, in which the authors show error bounds for a fully discrete scheme, and in
[
40
]
the authors look at numerical results for a fully discrete scheme on an evolving surface.

To the authors’ knowledge, this paper is the first to analyse fully discrete ESFEM schemes for a nonlinear fourth order problem.
The outline of this paper is as follows.
In Section
2
we introduce some prerequisite results regarding evolving surfaces and ESFEM.
In Section
3
we introduce our fully implicit scheme, and show well-posedness (and numerical stability).
Next, in Section
4
we prove error bounds — where we consider two cases based on the growth of
F_{1}
.
We then discuss in Section
5
how these results adapt for an implicit-explicit time discretisation such as the convex-concave splitting introduced in
[
11
,
30
]
.
Finally in Section
6
we end with some numerical results, which experimentally confirm the error bounds for linear finite elements.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Some geometric analysis

Throughout, we will be considering, for a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, closed, connected, oriented
C^{k+1}
surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.Given such a surface,
\Gamma
, we denote its unit oriented normal vector at a point
x\in\Gamma
by
\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)
.
As noted in
[
18
]
,
\Gamma
partitions
\mathbb{R}^{3}
into two regions, an interior region which we denote
\Omega
, and an exterior region
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\bar{\Omega}
.
The orientation of the normal is set by pointing
\boldsymbol{\nu}
outwards from
\Omega
and the oriented distance function for
\Gamma
is defined by

d(x):=\begin{cases}\inf_{y\in\Gamma}|x-y|,&x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\bar{\Omega}\\
-\inf_{y\in\Gamma}|x-y|,&x\in\bar{\Omega}.\end{cases}

Moreover it is known, see
[
32
,
36
]
, that, for a
C^{k+1}
surface,
d:\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{k+1}
function, where
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)
is some open, tubular neighbourhood of
\Gamma
.
Given a point
x\in\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)
, we may uniquely express
x
in Fermi coordinates as

(2.1)

\displaystyle x=p(x)+d(x)\boldsymbol{\nu}(p(x)),

for a unique
p(x)\in\Gamma
where

p:\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\Gamma
is the normal projection operator.
This will be used later in the triangulation of the surface as a way of relating functions on a triangulated surface to the exact surface.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be such that we have a differentiable extension of
f
, say
f^{e}
, defined on an open neighbourhood of
\Gamma
.
We define the tangential gradient of
f
at
x\in\Gamma
to be

\nabla_{\Gamma}f(x)=\nabla{f}^{e}(x)-\left(\nabla f^{e}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}(x).

We may express this componentwise as

\nabla_{\Gamma}f=\left(\underline{D}_{1}f,...,\underline{D}_{n+1}f\right).

It can be shown that this expression is independent of the choice of extension,
{f}^{e}
.

We then define the Laplace-Beltrami operator of
f
at
x\in\Gamma
to be

\Delta_{\Gamma}f=\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}f=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\underline{D}_{i}\underline{D}_{i}f.

2.1.1.
Sobolev spaces

As in
[
18
,
36
]
, we define Sobolev spaces on
\Gamma
as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the Sobolev space
H^{1,p}(\Gamma)
is defined by

H^{1,p}(\Gamma):=\{f\in L^{p}(\Gamma)\ |\ \underline{D}_{i}f\in L^{p}(\Gamma),\ i=1,...,n+1\},

and higher order spaces (
m\in\mathbb{N}
) are defined recursively by

H^{m,p}(\Gamma):=\{f\in H^{m-1,p}(\Gamma)\ |\ \underline{D}_{i}f\in H^{m-1,p}(\Gamma),\ i=1,...,n+1\},

where
H^{0,p}(\Gamma):=L^{p}(\Gamma)
.
These Sobolev spaces are known to be Banach spaces when equipped with norm,

\|f\|_{H^{m,p}(\Gamma)}:=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{m}\|\underline{D}^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma)}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\max_{|\alpha|=1,...,m}\|\underline{D}^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)},&p=\infty,\end{cases}

where we consider all weak derivatives of order
|\alpha|
.
We use shorthand notation,
H^{m}(\Gamma):=H^{m,2}(\Gamma)
, for the case
p=2
.
The spaces
H^{m}(\Gamma)
are in fact Hilbert spaces, where one can obtain the appropriate inner product by polarisation.

We refer the reader to
[
5
,
37
]
for further details on Sobolev spaces on manifolds, such as proofs of Sobolev embeddings.

Next we introduce some notation which will be used throughout.
For a
\mathcal{H}^{2}-
measurable set,
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we denote the
\mathcal{H}^{2}
measure of
X
by

|X|:=\mathcal{H}^{2}(X),

and for
f\in L^{1}(X)
we denote the mean value of
f
on
X
by

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{X}f:=\frac{1}{|X|}\int_{X}f.

2.2.
Evolving surfaces

We now generalise to surfaces which evolve in time.

Definition 2.3

(
C^{k+1}
evolving surface)
.

Let
\Gamma_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a closed, connected, orientable
C^{k+1}
surface and let
\Phi:\Gamma_{0}\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth map such that

(1)

For all
t\in[0,T]
,

\Phi(\cdot,t):\Gamma_{0}\rightarrow\Phi(\Gamma_{0},t)=:\Gamma(t)

is a
C^{k+1}
diffeomorphism.

(2)

\Phi(\cdot,0)=\mathrm{id}_{\Gamma_{0}}
.

Then we call the family
(\Gamma(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
a
C^{k+1}
evolving surface.

It follows that
\Gamma(t)
is closed, connected and orientable for all
t
.
We define the space-time surface to be the set

\mathcal{G}_{T}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Gamma(t)\times\{t\},

and given
(x,t)\in\mathcal{G}_{T}
we denote the unit normal by
\boldsymbol{\nu}(x,t)
.
We will assume throughout that there is a velocity field
V\in C^{1}([0,T];C^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3}))
such that for
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\Gamma_{0}
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(x,t)

\displaystyle=V(\Phi(x,t),t),

\displaystyle\Phi(x,0)

\displaystyle=x.

By compactness of
\mathcal{G}_{T}
, and assumed smoothness of
V
, there is a constant
C_{V}
independent of
t
such that

\|V(t)\|_{C^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}\leq C_{V},

for all
t\in[0,T]
.

2.2.1.
Time-dependent Lebesgue/Bochner spaces

Next we introduce a way of relating functions on the evolving surface back to the initial surface, which will be necessary for defining the evolving function spaces.
Let
t\in[0,T]
,
\eta\in H^{m,p}(\Gamma_{0})
and
\zeta\in H^{m,p}(\Gamma(t))
for some
m=0,...,k+1
, and
p\in[1,\infty]
.
We define the pushforward of
\eta
by

\Phi_{t}\eta=\eta(\Phi(\cdot,t))\in H^{m,p}(\Gamma(t)),

and the pullback of
\zeta
by

\Phi_{-t}\zeta=\zeta(\Phi(\cdot,t)^{-1})\in H^{m,p}(\Gamma_{0}).

It can be shown that the pairs
(H^{m,p}(\Gamma(t)),\Phi_{t})
are compatible in the sense of
[
3
,
4
]
for
m=0,...,k+1
.
Compatibility of these spaces allows one to obtain Sobolev inequalities independent of
t
.
With these definitions, we can define time-dependent Bochner spaces.

Definition 2.4
.

In the following we let
X(t)
denote a time-dependent Banach space, for instance
H^{m,p}(\Gamma(t))
.
The space
L^{2}_{X}
consists of (equivalence classes of) functions

\displaystyle u:[0,T]\rightarrow\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}X(t)\times\{t\},

\displaystyle t\mapsto(\bar{u}(t),t),

such that
\Phi_{-(\cdot)}\bar{u}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(0,T;X(0))
.
We identify
u
with
\bar{u}
.
This space is equipped with norm,

\|u\|_{L^{2}_{X}}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|_{X(t)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and if we have further that
X(t)
are a family of Hilbert spaces then this norm is induced by the inner product

(u,v)_{L^{2}_{X}}=\int_{0}^{T}(u(t),v(t))_{X(t)},

for
u,v\in L^{2}_{X}
.
As justified in
[
4
]
, we make the identification
(L^{2}_{X})^{*}\cong L^{2}_{X^{*}}
, and for
X=H^{1}
we write
L^{2}_{H^{-1}}:=(L^{2}_{H^{1}})^{*}
.

One can similarly define
L^{p}_{X}
for
p\in[1,\infty]
, which is equipped with a norm

\|u\|_{L^{p}_{X}}:=\begin{cases}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|^{p}_{X(t)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\underset{t\in[0,T]}{\esssup}\|u(t)\|_{X(t)},&p=\infty.\end{cases}

We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for further details.

Definition 2.5

(Strong material derivative)
.

Let
u:\mathcal{G}_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}_{C^{1}}
function.
We define the strong material time derivative by

\partial^{\bullet}u=\Phi_{t}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{-t}u\right).

Details on the material derivative are found in
[
4
,
3
]
.
As in the stationary setting, this can be generalised to define a weak material derivative.

Definition 2.6

(Weak material derivative)
.

Let
u\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}
.
A function
v\in L^{2}_{H^{-1}}
is said to be the weak material time derivative of
u
if for all
\eta\in\mathcal{D}_{H^{1}}(0,T)
we have

\int_{0}^{T}\langle v(t),\eta(t)\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Gamma(t))\times H^{1}(\Gamma(t))}=-\int_{0}^{T}\left(u(t),\partial^{\bullet}\eta(t)\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\\
-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma(t)}u(t)\eta(t)\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V(t),

where

\mathcal{D}_{H^{1}}(0,T):=\left\{u\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}\mid\Phi_{-t}u(t)\in C^{\infty}_{c}(0,T;H^{1}(\Gamma_{0}))\right\}.

We abuse notation and write
v=\partial^{\bullet}u
.

Clearly if
u\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}
has a strong material time derivative it has a weak material time derivative, and the two coincide.

We introduce the following bilinear forms to be used throughout,

\displaystyle m_{*}(t;\hat{\eta},\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\langle\hat{\eta},\zeta\rangle_{H^{-1}(\Gamma(t))\times H^{1}(\Gamma(t))},

\displaystyle m(t;\eta,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\eta\zeta,

\displaystyle g(t;\eta,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\eta\zeta\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V(t),

\displaystyle a(t;\eta,\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\zeta,

where
\eta,\zeta\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
,
\hat{\eta}\in H^{-1}(\Gamma(t))
.
The argument in
t
will often be omitted, as above.
For weakly differentiable functions we have the following transport theorem.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
22
]
, Lemma 5.2)
.

Let
\eta,\zeta\in H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\cap L^{2}_{L^{2}}.

Then
t\mapsto m(\eta(t),\zeta(t))
is absolutely continuous and such that

\frac{d}{dt}m(\eta,\zeta)=m_{*}(\partial^{\bullet}\eta,\zeta)+m_{*}(\eta,\partial^{\bullet}\zeta)+g(\eta,\zeta).

Moreover, if
\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial^{\bullet}\eta,\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial^{\bullet}\zeta\in L^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
then
t\mapsto a_{S}(\eta(t),\zeta(t))
is absolutely continuous and such that

\frac{d}{dt}a(\eta,\zeta)=a(\partial^{\bullet}\eta,\zeta)+a(\eta,\partial^{\bullet}\zeta)+b(\eta,\zeta).

Here

b(\eta,\zeta)=\int_{\Gamma(t)}\mathbf{B}(V)\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\zeta,

where

\mathbf{B}(V)=\left((\nabla_{\Gamma}\cdot V)\mathrm{id}-(\nabla_{\Gamma}V+(\nabla_{\Gamma}V)^{T})\right).

Using this notation the weak formulation of (
1
) is as follows.
Find
u\in H^{1}_{H^{-1}}\cap L^{\infty}_{H^{1}},w\in L^{2}_{H^{1}}
such that

(2.2)

\displaystyle m_{*}(u,\eta)+g(u,\eta)+a(w,\eta)=0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle m(w,\eta)=\varepsilon a(u,\eta)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m(F^{\prime}(u),\eta),

for all
\eta\in H^{1}(\Gamma(t))
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
and such that
u(0)=u_{0}
for some initial data
u_{0}
.
We refer the reader to
[
13
]
for the corresponding analysis.

2.3.
Triangulated surfaces

2.3.1.
Construction

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a closed, oriented
C^{k+1}
surface.
We introduce a discretised version of this surface, denoted
\Gamma_{h}
, which we call an triangulated (or interpolated) surface.

Definition 2.8
.

We let
(x_{i})_{i=1,...,N_{h}}\subset\Gamma
be a collection of nodes used to define a set of triangles
1
1

1

Here we mean “triangles” that are in fact curved for when we consider
k>1
- see
[
28
]
.
For
k=1
these are indeed triangles in the usual sense.

\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
The triangulated surface,
\Gamma_{h}
, is defined by an admissible subdivision of triangles,
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, such that

\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}K=\Gamma_{h}.

If
K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
are distinct, then we have
K_{1}^{\circ}\cap K_{2}^{\circ}=\emptyset
, and if
\bar{K}_{1}\cap\bar{K}_{2}\neq\emptyset
then this intersection is either a node of the triangulation, or an edge connecting two adjacent nodes.

For
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
we define following quantities

\displaystyle h_{K}:=\mathrm{diam}(\tilde{K}),

\displaystyle\rho_{K}:=\sup\{\mathrm{diam}(B)\ |\ B\text{ is a }2\text{-dimensional ball contained in }\tilde{K}\},

where
\tilde{K}
is the affine part of
K
, see
[
28
]
.
We assume that the subdivision
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is quasi-uniform, that is, there exists
\rho>0
such that for all
h\in(0,h_{0})

\min\{\rho_{K}\ |\ K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\}\geq\rho h.

Throughout this paper we work with polynomial Lagrange finite elements — that is our degrees of freedom are given by the point evaluations at some set points.
We will denote the set of shape functions as

S_{h}:=\left\{\phi_{h}\in C(\Gamma_{h})\mid\phi_{h}|_{K}\circ F_{K}^{-1}\text{ is a polynomial of degree }k,\ K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

where
F_{K}
is some appropriate reference map to a reference element, we refer to
[
28
]
for details.
The effect of the reference mapping here is so that the functions
\phi_{h}|_{K}\circ F_{K}^{-1}
are polynomials over the “flat triangles”.
Here the polynomial degree
k
is fixed as the same degree of our geometric approximation,
\Gamma_{h}
, of
\Gamma
.
This choice of matching the degree of the shape functions to the degree of the geometric approximations is known as isoparametric finite elements.
Here the normal
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{h}
is defined piecewise on each element of
\Gamma_{h}
.
As such this gives rise to a discrete tangential gradient,
\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}
, defined element-wise on
\Gamma_{h}
.

We note that (locally) one can view
\Gamma
as the graph of a
C^{k+1}
function,
g:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Hence the construction of
\Gamma_{h}
can be understood locally as being the graph of a polynomial interpolant of
g
.
We omit further details on construction of higher order polynomial approximations of
\Gamma
but refer the reader to
[
9
,
19
,
28
]
.

2.3.2.
Lifts and interpolation

Next we relate functions on
\Gamma_{h}
and
\Gamma
by defining lifts.
We will assume that our triangulated surface
\Gamma_{h}
is such that
\Gamma_{h}\subset\mathcal{N}
for
\mathcal{N}(\Gamma)
a sufficiently small tubular neighbourhood of
\Gamma
.
This is possible in practise by considering a sufficiently fine triangulation.
This allows us to define lifts of functions.

Definition 2.9
.

For a function
\eta_{h}:\Gamma_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we implicitly define the lift operation on
\eta_{h}
by

\eta_{h}^{\ell}(p(x)):=\eta_{h}(x),

where
p
is the normal projection operator (
2.1
).
Similarly, for
\eta:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we define the inverse lift by

\eta^{-\ell}(x)=\eta(p(x)).

In
[
28
]
the following result concerning lifts of functions is proven.

Lemma 2.10
.

There exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}
, independent of
h
, such that for
\eta_{h}\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{h})

(2.4)

\displaystyle C_{1}\|\eta_{h}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq\|\eta_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h})}\leq C_{2}\|\eta_{h}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)},

(2.5)

\displaystyle C_{1}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta_{h}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\leq\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\eta_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h})}\leq C_{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta_{h}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

This shows that there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}
independent of
h
such that

C_{1}|\Gamma|\leq|\Gamma_{h}|\leq C_{2}|\Gamma|.

Another useful consequence of the stability of the lift is that it allows one to obtain a Poincaré inequality on
\Gamma_{h}
independent of
h
.

We note that we consider Sobolev functions on
\Gamma_{h}
in the sense of “broken Sobolev spaces”.
That is to say, we understand the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1,p}(\Gamma_{h})}
to be

\|\eta_{h}\|_{H^{1,p}(\Gamma_{h})}=\begin{cases}\left(\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}}\|\eta_{h}\|_{H^{1,p}(K)}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},&p\in[1,\infty),\\
\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\|\eta_{h}\|_{H^{1,\infty}(K)},&p=\infty,\end{cases}

where
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1,p}(K)}
is understood in the usual sense.
We refer the reader to
[
12
,
28
]
for further details.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that our triangulation is exact if the lifted triangles
K^{\ell}:=\{x^{\ell}\mid x\in\Gamma_{h}\}
form a conforming subdivision of
\Gamma
.

2.3.3.
Evolving triangulated surfaces

We consider an evolving surface,
(\Gamma(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
, and construct an evolving triangulated surface as follows.
Firstly, we construct an admissible triangulation,
\mathcal{T}_{h}(0)
, of
\Gamma_{0}
, with nodes
(x_{i,0})_{i=1,...,N_{h}}
, as in the beginning of this section.
We denote this triangulated surface as
\Gamma_{h}(0)
.
The nodes of
\Gamma_{h}(0)
then evolve in time according to the ODE,

\frac{d}{dt}x_{i}(t)=V(x_{i}(t),t),\qquad x_{i}(0)=x_{i,0},

where
V
is the velocity field associated with the evolution of
\Gamma(t)
.
By assumptions on the smoothness of
V
and the Picard-Lindelöff theorem we find that there exists a unique nodal evolution
x_{i}(t)
, given
x_{i,0}
.
This induces a triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)
, where
K(0)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(0)
gives rise to a triangle
K(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)
where the nodes have evolved by the above ODE.
The triangulated surface
\Gamma_{h}(t)
is then defined as

\Gamma_{h}(t):=\bigcup_{K(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)}K(t),

which will be admissible by construction of
\Gamma_{h}(0)
.
Here the
h
parameter is now defined to be

h:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\max_{K(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)}h_{K(t)}.

Definition 2.12
.

•

The evolving triangulated surface,
\Gamma_{h}(t)
, is said to be uniformly quasi-uniform if there exists
\rho>0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
, and
h\in(0,h_{0})
we have

\min\{\rho_{K(t)}\ |\ K(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)\}\geq\rho h.

•

We say the triangulation is an exact evolving triangulation if for all
t\in[0,T]

\bigcup_{K(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)}K^{\ell}(t)=\Gamma(t).

In our analysis we consider an evolving triangulated surface,
\Gamma_{h}(t)
which is uniformly quasi-uniform, and exact all
t\in[0,T]
.
We also assume that for each
t\in[0,T]
that
\Gamma_{h}(t)\subset\mathcal{N}(\Gamma(t))
so that we may define lift at all time
t\in[0,T]
.
We denote the discrete spacetime surface as

\mathcal{G}_{h,T}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,T]}\Gamma_{h}(t)\times\{t\}.

We note that as the domain is evolving, the set of basis functions also evolves in time.
As such, we denote the set of basis functions at time
t
to be

S_{h}(t)=\left\{\phi_{h}\in C(\Gamma_{h}(t))\mid\phi_{h}|_{K(t)}\circ F_{K}(t)^{-1}\text{ is a polynomial of degree }k,K(t)\in\mathcal{T}_{h}(t)\right\},

where the reference map
F_{K}
now has some time dependence.
This definition allows one to characterise the velocity of the surface
\Gamma_{h}(t)
, as an arbitrary point
x(t)\in\Gamma_{h}(t)
will evolve according to the discrete velocity,
V_{h}
, given by

\frac{d}{dt}x(t)=V_{h}(x(t),t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{h}}\dot{x}_{i}(t)\phi_{i}(x(t),t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{h}}V(x_{i}(t),t)\phi_{i}(x(t),t),

where
\phi_{i}(t)
is the ‘
i
’th nodal basis function of
\Gamma_{h}(t)
.
From this we observe that
V_{h}
is the Lagrange interpolant of
V
.

The evolution of the nodes by
V
induces map
\Phi^{h}:\Gamma_{h}(0)\rightarrow\Gamma_{h}(t)
, from which we may define a discrete (strong) material time derivative, given by

\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\eta_{h}:=\Phi^{h}_{t}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\Phi^{h}_{-t}\eta_{h}\right),

for a sufficiently smooth
\eta_{h}
.
Here
\Phi^{h}_{t},\Phi^{h}_{-t}
are the pushforward/pullback maps respectively, defined similarly to the continuous surface.
One can similarly define a weak discrete material derivative in the standard way.

An important consequence of this is the transport property of basis functions, that is

\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\phi_{i}=0

for all of the nodal basis functions of
\Gamma_{h}(t)
.
This follows due to the fact that
\phi_{i}(t)=\Phi^{h}_{t}\phi_{i}(0)
for each basis function.
This is an important property, which is exploited in implementing evolving surface finite element schemes, as it eliminates any velocity terms in the formulation.

We now state a discrete analogue of the transport theorem, Proposition
2.7
.
Here we denote the (time-dependent) bilinear forms by

\displaystyle m_{h}(t;\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma_{h}(t)}\eta_{h}\zeta_{h},

\displaystyle a_{h}(t;\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma_{h}(t)}\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\eta_{h}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\zeta_{h},

\displaystyle g_{h}(t;\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma_{h}(t)}\eta_{h}\zeta_{h}\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\cdot V_{h},

where we typically omit the
t
argument, and it will be clear from context at what time the bilinear forms are understood.
We then have a discrete transport theorem for these bilinear forms.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
\eta_{h},\zeta_{h}\in S_{h}^{T}
, then we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}m_{h}(\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})=m_{h}(\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})+m_{h}(\eta_{h},\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\zeta_{h})+g_{h}(\eta_{h},\zeta_{h}),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}a_{h}(\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})=a_{h}(\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})+a_{h}(\eta_{h},\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\zeta_{h})+b_{h}(\eta_{h},\zeta_{h}).

Here

b_{h}(\eta_{h},\zeta_{h})=\int_{\Gamma_{h}(t)}\mathbf{B}_{h}(V_{h})\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\eta_{h}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\zeta_{h},

where

\mathbf{B}_{h}(V_{h})=\left((\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\cdot V_{h})\mathrm{id}-(\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}V_{h}+(\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}V_{h})^{T})\right).

We will not take note of the domain when discussing evolving Bochner spaces.
For example, we will write
L^{2}_{H^{1}}
, to be understood as either
L^{2}_{H^{1}}
on
(\Gamma(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
or
(\Gamma_{h}(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
, as it will be clear which we mean from context.

3.
A fully implicit scheme

3.1.
Scheme and main results

We begin by introducing some notation to be used throughout.

(1)

We define
\Gamma_{h}^{n}:=\Gamma_{h}(t_{n})
for the time discrete surfaces, and
S_{h}^{n}:=S_{h}(t_{n})
for the corresponding space of shape functions.

(2)

For functions
\phi_{h}^{n-1}\in S_{h}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
we define functions
\overline{\phi_{h}^{n-1}}\in S_{h}^{n}
, and
\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}\in S_{h}^{n-1}
to be the function in
S_{h}^{n}
(respectively
S_{h}^{n-1}
) with the same nodal value as
\phi_{h}^{n-1}
(respectively
\phi_{h}^{n}
).

(3)

We define the fully discrete material time derivative for a sequence of functions
(\phi_{h}^{n})_{n}
, where
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
by

\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\phi_{h}^{n}:=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\phi_{h}^{n}-\overline{\phi_{h}^{n-1}}\right)\in S_{h}^{n}.

It is worth noting that in
[
21
]
the authors define the discrete time derivative so that it belongs to
S_{h}^{n-1}
, which in our notation is
\underline{\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\phi_{h}^{n}}
.

We consider the following numerical scheme:
Find given some initial data
U_{h}^{0}=U_{h,0}\in S_{h}^{0}
, find
U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
solving

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(t_{n};U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(t_{n-1};U_{h}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n-1})\right)+a_{h}(t_{n};W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})\\
=m_{h}(t_{n-1};U_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\phi_{h}^{n}}),\end{aligned}

(3.2)

\displaystyle m_{h}(t_{n};W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(t_{n};U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(t_{n};F^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n}),

for all
\phi_{h}^{n-1}\in S_{h}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.
As usual we will often omit the time argument, as it will be clear from the arguments in the bilinear form.
Here the initial data
U_{h,0}\in S_{h}^{0}
is an approximation of some sufficiently regular function,
u_{0}
on
\Gamma(0)
.
More specifically, we assume that there is some
u_{0}\in H^{k+1}(\Gamma(0))
such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\|u_{0}-U_{h,0}^{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(0))}+h\|\nabla_{\Gamma}(u_{0}-U_{h,0}^{\ell})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(0))}\leq Ch^{k+1}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(0))}.

Examples of such initial data are the Lagrange interpolant and the Ritz projection of
u_{0}
.

This backward Euler time discretisation is analogous to the second order linear problem studied in
[
21
]
, and indeed we have written (
3.1
) in this way to be more in line with how their numerical scheme is posed.
However, (
3.1
) is in fact independent of choice of
\phi_{h}^{n-1}
, and noting that
\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}=\phi_{h}^{n-1}-\tau\underline{\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\phi_{h}^{n}}
, we may rewrite (
3.1
) as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(t_{n};U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(t_{n-1};U_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\right)+a_{h}(t_{n};W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0.

This form is more natural to the problem and will be used throughout.

3.2.
Well-posedness

We begin this subsection by showing uniqueness, given some restriction on the timestep size.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\tau<\frac{4\varepsilon^{3}}{\theta^{2}}

Then if the system (

Proof.

Assume there are two solution pairs
(U_{h,i}^{n},W_{h,i}^{n})
(
i=1,2

3.1
),(
3.2
).
We define the following differences

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=U_{h,1}^{n}-U_{h,2}^{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}=W_{h,1}^{n}-W_{h,2}^{n},

which solve

(3.5)

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\tau a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n}),

for all

Testing (

\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}
and (
3.6

and subtracting the resulting equations yields

\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}).

Convexity of

implies

is monotonic and hence

m_{h}(F^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\geq-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

m_{h}(t_{n};\overline{z_{h}^{n-1}}-z_{h,+}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n})=m_{h}(t_{n};\overline{z_{h}^{n-1}},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(t_{n-1};z_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}),

and hence taking
\phi_{h}^{n}=\overline{z_{h}^{n-1}}-z_{h,+}^{n-1}
and using (
3.9
) one obtains the result. ∎∎

Similarly we require an appropriate notion of a discrete inverse Laplacian.
Given
z_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
such that
m_{h}(z_{h}^{n},1)=0
, we define the discrete inverse Laplacian to be the unique solution,
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h}^{n}
, of

a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=m_{h}(z_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n}),

such that
m_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h}^{n},1)=0
.
From Poincaré’s inequality it is clear to see that this is well defined, and moreover that

\left\|z_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}:=\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}\leq C\|z_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

for a constant
C
independent of
n,h
.

By using the projection (
3.13
) and the discrete inverse Laplacian we find that (
3.1
) becomes

\frac{1}{\tau}a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n}))+a_{h}(W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0.

This implies that
\frac{1}{\tau}\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1})=\lambda^{n}-W_{h}^{n}
for some constant
\lambda^{n}
depending on
n
.
By using (
3.2
) it is clear that

\lambda^{n}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}U_{h}^{n}.

Hence we find that (
3.1
), (
3.2
) may be written as a single equation

(3.16)

\varepsilon a_{h}\left(U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n}\right)+\frac{\theta}{2\varepsilon}m_{h}\left(F_{1}^{\prime}\left(U_{h}^{n}\right),\phi_{h}^{n}-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}\phi_{h}^{n}\right)-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}\left(U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n}-\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}\phi_{h}^{n}\right)\\
+\frac{1}{\tau}m_{h}\left(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1}),\phi_{h}^{n}\right)=0.

Motivated by this observation, we define a functional,
J_{h}^{n}:D^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for

D^{n}:=\left\{z_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}\mid m_{h}(z_{h}^{n},1)=m_{h}(U_{h}^{n-1},1)\right\}

given by

J_{h}^{n}(z_{h}^{n}):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}\left(F(z_{h}^{n}),1\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}z_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h})}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\|z_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}.

D^{n}
is clearly a non-empty, affine subspace of
S_{h}
, and thus finite dimensional.
Our existence result now follows by using similar arguments to Theorem 2.1 of
[
17
]
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
U_{h,0}\in S_{h}^{0}
.
Then there exists a solution pair
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
solving (
3.1
),(
3.2
).

Proof.

We firstly note that for
z_{h}^{n}\in D^{n}
,

J_{h}^{n}(z_{h}^{n})\geq\beta|\Gamma_{h}^{n}|,

where
\beta
is the lower bound on
F
.
Hence
\inf_{z_{h}^{n}\in D^{n}}J_{h}^{n}(z_{h}^{n})>-\infty
, and we can consider a minimising sequence
(z_{h,m}^{n})_{m}\subset D^{n}
.
It is straightforward to see such a sequence is bounded in
H^{1}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})
.
Since
D^{n}
is finite dimensional there exists a subsequence of
(z_{h,m}^{n})_{m}
(for which we still keep the subscript
m
) converging to a minimiser,
U^{n}\in D^{n}
, of
J_{h}^{n}
.
By continuity of
J_{h}^{n}
we have that

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}J_{h}^{n}(z_{h,m}^{n})=J_{h}^{n}(U^{n}).

Moreover, by considering the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to this minimisation problem, we see that
U^{n}
solves, for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
,

\varepsilon a_{h}(U^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(U^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\tau}m_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1}),\phi_{h}^{n})\\
-\lambda^{n}m_{h}(1,\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

where

\lambda^{n}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}F_{1}^{\prime}(U^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}U^{n}

is a Lagrange multiplier associated to the constraint defining
D^{n}
.
This is precisely the decoupled equation (
3.16
), and hence setting

U_{h}^{n}:=U^{n},\quad W_{h}^{n}:=\lambda^{n}-\frac{1}{\tau}\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1})

we obtain a solution pair. ∎∎

3.3.
Stability

We now discuss difficulties in showing the scheme (
3.1
), (
3.2
) is numerically stable.
As is standard in the analysis of the Cahn-Hilliard equation this is expressed in terms of the (discrete) Ginzburg-Landau functional

E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}[U_{h};t]=\int_{\Gamma_{h}(t)}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(U_{h}).

We will omit the
t
argument, as it will be clear from context.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{0})
,
U_{h,0}\in S_{h}^{0}
be such that (
3.3
) holds,
and
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})\in S_{h}^{n}\times S_{h}^{n}
be the unique pair solving (
3.1
), (
3.2
).
Then for
1\leq N\leq\lfloor\frac{T}{\tau}\rfloor
,

(3.17)

\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{N}]+\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq CT\\
+C\tau\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h,0}]+C\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}]+\left(\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}]\right)^{q}\right)

where
C
denotes a constant independent of
h,\tau
but may depend on
\varepsilon,\theta,V,T,u_{0}
.

Proof.

Throughout we define
\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}=E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}+C
, where
C
is sufficiently large so that

\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[z_{h}^{n}]\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}z_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\geq 0,

for all
z_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.
This is possible by the assumptions on
F
, and moreover we can choose
C
independent of
h,\tau
by the assumptions on
V
.
We write the scheme (
3.1
), (
3.2
) in matrix form as

(3.18)

\displaystyle M^{n}\frac{(U^{n}-U^{n-1})}{\tau}+\frac{(M^{n}-M^{n-1})}{\tau}U^{n-1}+A^{n}W^{n}=0,

(3.19)

\displaystyle M^{n}W^{n}=\varepsilon A^{n}U^{n}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{F}_{1}^{n}(U^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}M^{n}U^{n},

where
U^{n},W^{n}
denote the vector of nodal values of
U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n}
respectively, and

\displaystyle(M^{n})_{ij}

\displaystyle=m_{h}(t_{n};\phi_{i}^{n},\phi_{j}^{n}),

\displaystyle(A^{n})_{ij}

\displaystyle=a_{h}(t_{n};\phi_{i}^{n},\phi_{j}^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}^{n}(U^{n})_{j}

\displaystyle=m_{h}(t_{n};F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\phi_{j}^{n}),

for
\phi_{i}^{n}
the ‘
i
’th nodal basis function in
S_{h}^{n}
.
We note that the only nonlinear term here is due to the convex part of the potential,
F_{1}
, as we have assumed that the concave part of the potential is given by a quadratic.

We dot (
3.18
) with
W^{n}
and (
3.19
) with
\frac{1}{\tau}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})
.
This yields

(3.20)

U^{n}\cdot A^{n}\frac{(U^{n}-U^{n-1})}{\tau}+\mathcal{F}_{1}^{n}(U^{n})\cdot\frac{(U^{n}-U^{n-1})}{\tau\varepsilon}+W^{n}\cdot A^{n}W^{n}\\
=\frac{\theta}{\tau\varepsilon}U^{n}\cdot M^{n}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})-W^{n}\cdot\frac{(M^{n}-M^{n-1})}{\tau}U^{n-1}.

It is then straightforward to show that

(3.21)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} U^{n}\cdot A^{n}\frac{(U^{n}-U^{n-1})}{\tau}&=\frac{1}{2\tau}\left[U^{n}\cdot A^{n}U^{n}-U^{n-1}\cdot A^{n-1}U^{n-1}\right]\\
&+\frac{1}{2\tau}\left(U^{n}-U^{n-1}\right)\cdot A^{n}\left(U^{n}-U^{n-1}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{2\tau}\left[U^{n-1}\cdot A^{n-1}U^{n-1}-U^{n-1}\cdot A^{n}U^{n-1}\right]\end{aligned}

This can be understood as a fully discrete form of the transport theorem, see
[
21
]
Lemma 3.5.
By using the convexity of
F_{1}
, we see that

\mathcal{F}_{1}^{n}(U^{n})\cdot(U^{n}-U^{n-1})=m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),U_{h}^{n}-\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}})\geq m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n})-F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}),1).

We then rewrite this final term as

m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n})-F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}),1)=m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n}),1)-m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n-1}),1)\\
+\left[m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n-1}),1)-m_{h}(F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}),1)\right],

where the term in square brackets may be written as

\displaystyle m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n-1}),1)-m_{h}(F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}),1)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\frac{d}{dt}m_{h}(F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}(t)),1)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}g_{h}(F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}(t)),1).

Hence by using the smoothness of
V_{h}
and arguing along the lines of (
3.7
) one obtains that

(3.22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau\varepsilon}\left|m_{h}(F_{1}(U_{h}^{n-1}),1)-m_{h}(F_{1}(\overline{U_{h}^{n-1}}),1)\right|\leq C\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n-1}],

for some constant
C
independent of
\tau,h
.

Next we notice that one can write

(3.23)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{\theta}{\tau\varepsilon}U^{n}\cdot M^{n}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})&=\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}\left(U^{n}\cdot M^{n}U^{n}-U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n-1}U^{n-1}\right)\\
&+\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\cdot M^{n}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\\
&+\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}\left[U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n-1}U^{n-1}-U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n}U^{n-1}\right],\end{aligned}

where we note that the first term on the right hand side corresponds to the quadratic part of the potential.
One dots (
3.18
) with
\frac{\theta}{2\varepsilon}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})
to see that

\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\cdot M^{n}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})=(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\cdot\frac{\theta(M^{n}-M^{n-1})}{2\varepsilon\tau}U^{n-1}\\
+\frac{\theta}{2\varepsilon}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\cdot A^{n}W^{n},

and by using (
3.9
), (
3.11
), (
3.12
) and a Young’s inequality this yields

(3.24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\cdot M^{n}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\right|&\leq\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}\left(\|U_{h}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}^{2}+\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\right)\\
&+\frac{C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}^{2}+\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2},\end{split}

where
C
is independent of
\varepsilon,\theta
.

Similarly, by using (
3.9
), (
3.11
) we find that

(3.25)

\displaystyle\left|W^{n}\cdot\frac{(M^{n}-M^{n-1})}{\tau}U^{n-1}\right|\leq C\|W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}\|U_{h}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}.

Now by testing (
3.2
) with
W_{h}^{n}
and using Young’s inequality we find that

(3.26)

\|W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+C\|F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
+C\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2},

where the constants
C
may depend on
\varepsilon,\theta
.
Next we recall the condition on the potential,

|F_{1}^{\prime}(r)|\leq\alpha|r|^{q}+\alpha,

for constants
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, hence

\|F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C|\Gamma_{h}^{n}|+C\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2q}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2q}\leq C|\Gamma_{h}^{n}|+C\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2q},

where we have used the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})\hookrightarrow L^{2q}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})
(and lifts if necessary so that the constants are independent of
h
).
By using lifts and the assumptions on
V
we can then bound
|\Gamma_{h}^{n}|\leq C
independent of
h,\tau
, and we can use Poincaré’s inequality to see that

\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}\leq C\left(\left|\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{0}}U_{h,0}\right|+\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}\right).

Using lifts and (
3.3
) it is straightforward to show that
|\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{0}}U_{h,0}|\leq C
for a constant
C
depending on
u_{0}
but independent of
h
.
Similarly we note that by construction,

\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}].

It then follows that

(3.27)

\displaystyle\|W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C+\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+C\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+C\left(\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}]\right)^{q},

where we have again used Poincaré’s inequality.
Finally, combining (
3.20
), (
3.21
), (
3.22
), (
3.23
), (
3.24
), (
3.27
), using (
3.9
), (
3.10
) and Poincaré’s inequality where necessary we find that, after multiplying by

(3.28)

\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}]-\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n-1}]+\frac{\tau}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C\tau\\
+C\tau\left(\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n-1}]+\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}]\right)+C\tau\left(\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{n}]\right)^{q},

where the constants
C
may depend on
\varepsilon,\theta
.
Now summing (
3.28
) over

we conclude that (
3.17
) holds. ∎∎

This proof mirrors that of
[
13
]
Proposition 4.5, and they are in a position where they can use a generalised Grönwall inequality to obtain a bound.
In our case we cannot do something analogous, as both sides of the inequality (

) depend on the final time

, whereas the usual discrete analogues of the Grönwall inequality (for example the Bihari-type inequalities in
[
46
]
) only go up to time
N-1
on the righthand side.
In fact the inequality we would require likely does not hold, as the above inequality remains true for arbitrarily large
\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h}^{N}]
.

If
q=1
then we can take
\tau
sufficiently small so that we can apply the usual discrete Grönwall inequality to (
3.17
) to obtain a bound in terms of
\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h,0}]
and

.
Moreover, it is clear that
\widetilde{E_{h}^{\mathrm{CH}}}[U_{h,0}]
can be bounded independent of
h
by (

).
Stability is not surprising in this case as we are considering a nonlinearity with linear growth, which is then similar to the linear case.
As such we firstly consider the error analysis for a quadratic potential,
q=1
, and return to the general setting,
q>1
, later.
Lastly, we note that upon inspection of this proof it is clear that this issue arises solely because of the evolution of the domain, and for a stationary surface one can neglect these considerations, as
M^{n}=M^{n-1}
.

Regardless of this stability issue for general
q
, we end this section with a result on the dependence on initial data.
For this we introduce a new time projection, similar to (
3.13
).

Definition 3.10
.

Given
z_{h}^{n-1}\in S_{h}^{n-1}
we define
z_{h,++}^{n-1}\in S_{h}^{n}
to be the unique solution of

(3.29)

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{h}(t_{n};z_{h,++}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n})&=a_{h}(t_{n-1};z_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}),\\
\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}z_{h,++}^{n-1}&=\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n-1}}z_{h}^{n-1},\end{split}

for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.

One can show that

(3.30)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}z_{h,++}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}z_{h}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})},

(3.31)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}(z_{h,++}^{n-1}-\overline{z_{h}^{n-1}})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}z_{h}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})},

by similar means as the proofs of (
3.14
), (
3.15
).
We do not do these calculations here.
The importance of this projection comes from the observation that

\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h,+}^{n-1}=(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}z_{h}^{n-1})_{++}.

To see this we compute

\displaystyle a_{h}(t_{n};(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}z_{h}^{n-1})_{++},\phi_{h}^{n})

\displaystyle=a_{h}(t_{n-1};\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}z_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})=m_{h}(t_{n-1};z_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})

\displaystyle=m_{h}(t_{n};z_{h,+}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n})=a_{h}(t_{n};\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h,+}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n}),

and use the fact that

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}z_{h}^{n-1})_{++}=0=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}z_{h,+}^{n-1}.

With this observation, we now show continuous dependence on the initial data.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
U_{h,0}^{1},U_{h,0}^{2}\in S_{h}^{0}
be such that
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}(0)}U_{h,0}^{1}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}(0)}U_{h,0}^{2}
.
Denoting the solution of (
3.1
),(
3.2
) with initial data
U_{h,0}^{i}
by
(U_{h}^{n,i},W_{h}^{n,i})
we have that for
1\leq N\leq N_{T}
,

(3.32)

\left\|U_{h}^{N,1}-U_{h}^{N,2}\right\|_{S_{h}^{N}}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{4}\tau}{\varepsilon^{3}-\tau\theta^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}(U_{h}^{N,1}-U_{h}^{N,2})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
\leq e^{Ct_{N}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\varepsilon^{3}-\tau\theta^{2}}\right)\left\|U_{h,0}^{1}-U_{h,0}^{2}\right\|_{S_{h}^{0}}^{2},

provided that
\tau<\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\theta^{2}}
.

Proof.

Firstly we define some shorthand notation

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=U_{h,1}^{n}-U_{h,2}^{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}=W_{h,1}^{n}-W_{h,2}^{n},

and note that from (
3.1
), (
3.2
)

(3.33)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\right)+a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

(3.34)

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n}),

holds for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.

We test (
3.33
) with
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}
for

m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})+\tau a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=0,

and we note that

a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=m_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\\
-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}),

and so one finds that

(3.35)

m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})+\varepsilon\tau a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})+\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\\
=\frac{\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}).

From the convexity of
F_{1}(\cdot)
one has that
m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\geq 0
.
The difficulty now is in rewriting the term on the previous surface.
For this we use (
3.13
) so that

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})

\displaystyle=m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})

(3.36)

\displaystyle=a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}),\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}),

where we notice that

(3.37)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1})=\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}.

Likewise it is a straightforward calculation to verify that

a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}),\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}+\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}\right),

and so (
3.35
) becomes

(3.38)

\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}+\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}+2\varepsilon\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq\frac{2\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
+\left(\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}\right).

We now show a bound for this final term.
To begin we calculate

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1})

\displaystyle=m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1},(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1})_{++})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1})

\displaystyle=m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1})_{++}}-\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1})

\displaystyle=a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1})_{++}}-\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}),

from which (
3.31
) yields that

(3.39)

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}\right|\leq C\tau\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}.

Lastly we use the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}
and Young’s inequality to see that

(3.40)

\displaystyle\frac{2\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}=\frac{2\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\leq\varepsilon\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{\tau\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}.

Now using (
3.39
), (
3.40
) in (
3.38
) and summing over
n
one obtains

\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{N}\right\|_{S_{h}^{N}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{0}\right\|_{S_{h}^{0}}^{2}+\varepsilon\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}\\
\leq\frac{\tau\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{N}\right\|_{S_{h}^{N}}^{2}+C\tau\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}.

The result then follows from our assumption that
\tau<\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\theta^{2}}
and a discrete Grönwall inequality. ∎∎

4.1.
Quadratic potential

By the assumptions on

we find

We denote the Lipschitz constant by

Lemma 4.1

(
Consistency errors).

For
E_{1},...,E_{4}
as defined above we have that

\displaystyle|E_{1}(\phi_{h}^{n})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ch^{k+1}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\left(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}

\displaystyle+C\sqrt{\tau}\left(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|(\partial^{\bullet})^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}

\displaystyle+Ch^{k+1}\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t^{n}))}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

\displaystyle|E_{2}(\phi_{h}^{n})|\leq Ch^{k+1}\|w^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

\displaystyle|E_{3}(\phi_{h}^{n})|\leq\frac{CL_{F}h^{k+1}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\|u^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\right)\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

\displaystyle|E_{4}(\phi_{h}^{n})|\leq\frac{C\theta h^{k+1}}{\varepsilon}\|u^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

and

denotes some constant independent of

Proof.

We firstly bound
E_{2},E_{3},E_{4}
as they are the simplest.
The bound for

is straightforward, and we write

|E_{2}(\phi_{h}^{n})|\leq|m_{h}(\Pi_{h}w^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m(\pi_{h}w^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})|+|m(\pi_{h}w^{n}-w^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})|\\
\leq Ch^{k+1}\|w^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

where we have used (
2.8
), (
2.18
) and the stability of the lift.
The bound for
E_{4}
follows identically, and the bound on
E_{3}
is similar, where we also use the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{1}^{\prime}
.
We see that

\displaystyle|E_{3}(\phi_{h}^{n})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left|m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})-m(F_{1}^{\prime}(\pi_{h}u^{n}),(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})\right|+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left|m(F_{1}^{\prime}(u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(\pi_{h}u^{n}),(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{CL_{F}h^{k+1}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\|u^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\right)\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

where we have used the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{1}^{\prime}
, (
2.8
), (
2.18
), and the stability of the lift.

Lastly we bound
E_{1}
.
We firstly notice that by comparing Proposition
2.7
and Proposition
2.15
that

(4.5)

\displaystyle m(\partial^{\bullet}u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})+g(u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})=m(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})+g_{\ell}(u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})+m(u^{n},(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}-\partial^{\bullet})(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell}).

Using this, we write

\displaystyle E_{1}(\phi_{h}^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\frac{d}{dt}m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u,\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))-m(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})-g_{\ell}(u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})\,dt+m(u^{n},(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}-\partial^{\bullet})(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

2.13

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{1}&=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\left[m_{h}(\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\Pi_{h}u,\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))-m(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}\pi_{h}u,(\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))^{\ell})\right]+\left[m(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}\pi_{h}u-\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}u,(\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))^{\ell})\right]\,dt\\

Combining these bounds in (

We also bound

I_{2}=m(u^{n},(\partial^{\bullet}_{\ell}-\partial^{\bullet})(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})\leq Ch^{k+1}\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\|\phi_{h}^{n}\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

.
With these bounds established it remains to show the error bound.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given initial data
u_{0}\in H^{k+1}(\Gamma(0))
,
U_{h,0}=\Pi_{h}u_{0}
, and assuming (
4.1
), (
4.2
) hold and the timestep size is such that

\tau\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}+C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4},

then we have the following:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varepsilon\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|(u^{n})^{-\ell}-U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\varepsilon h^{2}\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}((u^{n})^{-\ell}-U_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
\leq C_{h}h^{2({k+1})}+C_{\tau}\tau^{2},\end{aligned}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tau\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|(w^{n})^{-\ell}-W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\tau h^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}((w^{n})^{-\ell}-W_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C_{h}h^{2({k+1})}+C_{\tau}\tau^{2},\end{aligned}

where

\displaystyle C_{\tau}=C\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|(\partial^{\bullet})^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt,

\displaystyle C_{h}=C\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt+C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|w\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+\frac{CL_{F}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Proof.

We firstly test (
4.3
) with
\varepsilon\sigma_{u}^{n}
and (
4.4
) with
\sigma_{w}^{n}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})-m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\sigma_{u}^{n}})\right)+\varepsilon a_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})=\varepsilon E_{1}(\sigma_{u}^{n}),

and

\varepsilon a_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{w}^{n})=m_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\sigma_{w}^{n})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{w}^{n})+\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{w}^{n})\\
-E_{2}(\sigma_{w}^{n})-E_{3}(\sigma_{w}^{n})-E_{4}(\sigma_{w}^{n}).

Then, similar to as we have done before (see (
3.21
)), we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})-m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\sigma_{u}^{n}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau}\left(m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})-m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\sigma_{u}^{n-1})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\tau}{2}m_{h}(\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\sigma_{u}^{n},\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\sigma_{u}^{n})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\tau}\left[m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\sigma_{u}^{n-1})-m_{h}(\overline{\sigma_{u}^{n-1}},\overline{\sigma_{u}^{n-1}})\right].

Hence combining all of the above we find that

(4.9)

\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}\left(m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})-m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\sigma_{u}^{n-1})\right)+\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{2}m_{h}(\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\sigma_{u}^{n},\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\sigma_{u}^{n})+m_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\sigma_{w}^{n})\\
=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{w}^{n})+\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{w}^{n})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}\left[m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\sigma_{u}^{n-1})-m_{h}(\overline{\sigma_{u}^{n-1}},\overline{\sigma_{u}^{n-1}})\right]\\
+\varepsilon E_{1}(\sigma_{u}^{n})+E_{2}(\sigma_{w}^{n})+E_{3}(\sigma_{w}^{n})+E_{4}(\sigma_{w}^{n}).

From the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{1}^{\prime}
we find that

\left|\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{w}^{n})\right|\leq\frac{L_{F}}{\varepsilon}\|\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}\|\sigma_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})},

and from using (
3.9
), (
3.11
) we find

\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}\left|m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\sigma_{u}^{n-1})-m_{h}(\overline{\sigma_{u}^{n-1}},\overline{\sigma_{u}^{n-1}})\right|\leq C\varepsilon\|\sigma_{u}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}^{2}.

Now by using these bounds, the bounds in Lemma
4.1
, and Young’s inequality in (
4.9
) we obtain

(4.10)

\frac{\varepsilon}{2\tau}\left(\|\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}-\|\sigma_{u}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}^{2}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon\tau}{2}\|\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\|\sigma_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon\|\sigma_{u}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}^{2}\\
+\frac{1}{4}\|\sigma_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}+C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\|\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{5}(\hat{E}_{i}^{n})^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2},

where

\hat{E}_{1}^{n}=\frac{Ch^{k+1}}{\sqrt{\tau}}\left(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
+C\sqrt{\tau}\left(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|(\partial^{\bullet})^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{2}^{n}=Ch^{k+1}\|w^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))},

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{3}^{n}=\frac{CL_{F}h^{k+1}}{\varepsilon}\left(1+\|u^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}\right),

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{4}^{n}=\frac{C\theta h^{k+1}}{\varepsilon}\|u^{n}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t_{n}))},

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{5}^{n}=Ch^{k+1}\|u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t_{n}))}.

We want to bound the
\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}
term in (
4.10
), and to do this we test (
4.4
) with
\sigma_{u}^{n}
for

\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}=m_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{u}^{n})+\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\|\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
-\sum_{i=2}^{4}E_{i}(\sigma_{u}^{n})

Monotonicity of
F_{1}^{\prime}
, Young’s inequality, and the bounds in Lemma
4.1
then yield

(4.11)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\|\sigma_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}\|\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{4}(\hat{E}_{i}^{n})^{2}

Using (
4.11
) in (
4.10
), taking
\tau
sufficiently small, in particular

\tau\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}+C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4},

summing over
n=1,...,N_{T}
we find

\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\|\sigma_{u}^{N_{T}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{N_{T}})}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon\tau^{2}}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\partial^{\bullet}_{\tau}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\sigma_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq C\varepsilon\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\sigma_{u}^{n-1}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n-1})}^{2}\\
+C_{\tau}\tau^{2}+C_{h}h^{2({k+1})},

where
C_{h},C_{\tau}
are as given above.
We can then apply a discrete Grönwall inequality (noting
\sigma_{u}^{0}\equiv 0
) for the
L^{2}
error bounds for
\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{w}^{n}
.
The
L^{2}
bounds in (
4.7
), (
4.8
) follow as by (
2.18
) we also have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|(u^{n})^{-\ell}-\Pi_{h}u^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}&=\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|\rho_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
&\leq Ch^{2({k+1})}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t)}^{2},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tau\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|(w^{n})^{-\ell}-\Pi_{h}w^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}&=\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\rho_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
&\leq Ch^{2({k+1})}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|w\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t)}^{2}.\end{aligned}

The
H^{1}
error bound follows by applying an inverse inequality (see for example
[
12
]
), and the
H^{1}
bound in (
2.18
). ∎∎

Remark 4.3
.

From (
3.32
) it is clear that we obtain optimal order bounds in the
H^{1}
seminorm provided

\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{0}}U_{h,0}=\int_{\Gamma(0)}u_{0},\qquad\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}(\Pi_{h}u_{0}-U_{h,0})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{0})}\leq Ch^{k},

but it is not clear that one obtains optimal order in the
L^{2}
norm.

4.2.
Non-quadratic potentials

We now return to the general setting of a nonlinearity with polynomial growth,
q>1
.
As before, we assume that the solution pair
(u,w)
solving (
2.2
), (
2.3
) is such that (
4.1
), (
4.2
) hold.
In particular, we see that
u\in H^{1}_{H^{2}}
and by embeddings of Sobolev and evolving Sobolev-Bochner spaces (see
[
3
]
) we see
u\in C^{0}_{C^{0}}
.
Thus we find there exists some constant
u_{\text{max}}
such that

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\max_{x\in\Gamma(t)}|u(x,t)|\leq u_{\text{max}},

and moreover,
u_{\text{max}}
is determined by the choice of initial data.
Motivated by this observation, we can modify
F_{1}
to be quadratic past some given value, which will allow us to show stability and perform error analysis.
We define
F_{M}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by

F_{M}(r):=\begin{cases}F_{1}(M)+F^{\prime}_{1}(M)(r-M)+\frac{F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(M)}{2}(r-M)^{2},&r>M,\\
F_{1}(r),&|r|\leq M,\\
F_{1}(-M)+F^{\prime}_{1}(-M)(r+M)+\frac{F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(-M)}{2}(r+M)^{2},&r<-M,\end{cases}

where
M>u_{\text{max}}
is a constant to be determined.
We note that
F_{M}^{\prime}
is
C^{1}
and Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(M)
.
By construction we see that
F_{M}
satisfies our assumptions on
F_{1}
(it is convex for example), and we can apply the results for a quadratic potential.
In particular, Lemma
3.9
(and a discrete Grönwall inequality) shows we have a numerically stable scheme, and Theorem
4.2
provides an error bound for this modified potential.

What remains to be seen is that we can choose
M
, independent of
h,\tau
, so that the
|U_{h}^{n}|\leq M
for all
n=1,...,N_{T}
.
This then shows, by considering the results on the quadratic potential, that we have a numerically stable scheme with optimal order error bounds for a general nonlinearity with polynomial growth
(q>1)
.
This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume (
4.1
), (
4.2
) hold,
h
is sufficiently small, and
\tau
is chosen so that
\tau\leq Ch^{1+\gamma}
for some positive constants
C,\gamma
.
Then there exists some
M
independent of
\tau,h
such that

\max_{t\in[0,T]}\max_{x\in\Gamma(t)}|u(x,t)|\leq M,\qquad\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\max_{x\in\Gamma_{h}(t)}|U_{h}^{n}(x,t)|\leq M,

where
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
solves (
3.1
), (
3.2
) for the modified potential
F_{M}
.

Proof.

We firstly write

\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}\leq\|\Pi_{h}u^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}+\|U_{h}^{n}-\Pi_{h}u^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}.

Hence using (
2.17
), and the bound from (
4.7
) we see

\displaystyle\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+C_{h}h^{k}+\frac{C_{\tau}\tau}{h}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+(C_{h}+C_{\tau})h^{\gamma},

where we have used an inverse inequality, and
C_{h},C_{\tau}
are as in Theorem
4.2
.
Note that in using these error bounds we have a constraint on
\tau
given by

\tau\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}+\frac{F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(M)^{2}+C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

The result clearly holds if

C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+(C_{h}+C_{\tau})h\leq M,

where we recall that
C_{h}
depends on
F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(M)
.
Hence we can fix some
M>u_{\text{max}}
and take
h
sufficiently small so that the above holds. ∎∎

This result says that although we have considered a numerical scheme with a modified potential, for sufficiently small
\tau,h
(determined by the solution pair
(u,w)
solving (
2.2
), (
2.3
)) the numerical solution
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
“doesn’t see” the modification and we can still analyse it as we did for the quadratic potential.
In particular, we have the following result.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
F_{1}
satisfy the assumptions at the beginning of this chapter, with polynomial growth of
F_{1}^{\prime}
of order
q\geq 1
.
Then, given (
4.1
), (
4.2
) hold, for sufficiently small
\tau,h
the scheme (
3.1
), (
3.2
) is numerically stable and has optimal order error bounds when we consider
U_{h,0}=\Pi_{h}u_{0}
.
Moreover from Proposition
3.11
we obtain an optimal order
{H^{1}}
error bound if we consider initial data such that

\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{0}}U_{h,0}=\int_{\Gamma(0)}u_{0},\qquad\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}(\Pi_{h}u_{0}-U_{h,0})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{0})}\leq Ch^{k},

for some constant
C
.

Remark 4.6
.

We note that this corollary requires both the smallness condition
\tau=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\theta^{2}})
and the CFL condition
\tau=\mathcal{O}(h^{1+\gamma})
to hold.

5.
An implicit-explicit scheme

5.1.
Well-posedness

In this section we outline how results in the previous two chapters change for an implicit-explicit scheme.
Here we consider the scheme such that one finds
U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
such that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(t_{n};U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(t_{n-1};U_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\right)+a_{h}(t_{n};W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} m_{h}(t_{n};W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(t_{n};U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(t_{n};F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})\\
-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(t_{n-1};U_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}),\end{aligned}

for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
, given some initial data
U_{h}^{0}=U_{h,0}
.
It is worth comparing this discretisation to the backwards differentiation formulae time discretisations proposed in
[
10
]
in which the authors consider an extrapolated value in the nonlinear terms.
As before, the initial data
U_{h,0}\in S_{h}^{0}
approximates some sufficiently regular function,
u_{0}
, on
\Gamma(0)
.
The benefit of this scheme is that it no longer enforces a timestep restriction for the uniqueness of the scheme.
We show this in the following lemma, which is analogous to Lemma
3.1

Lemma 5.1
.

Given some
U_{h,0}\in S_{h}^{0}
, then if the system (
5.1
), (
5.2
) admits a solution pair
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
, it is unique.

Proof.

Assume there are two solution pairs
(U_{h,i}^{n},W_{h,i}^{n})
(
i=1,2
) of (
5.1
),(
5.2
).
We define the following differences

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=U_{h,1}^{n}-U_{h,2}^{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}=W_{h,1}^{n}-W_{h,2}^{n},

which solve

(5.3)

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\tau a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

(5.4)

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n}),

for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.
Testing (
5.3
) with
\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}
and (
5.4
) with
\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}
and subtracting the resulting equations yields

\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=0.

As noted throughout,
F_{1}^{\prime}
is monotonic and hence

m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\geq 0.

Thus we have

\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq 0,

and so
\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}=0
.
Observing that
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=0
we may use Poincaré’s inequality to conclude
\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=0
, and
\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}=0
follows. ∎∎

The existence of a solution to (
5.1
), (
5.2
) follows similar arguments to Lemma
3.8
.
We define a functional,
\widetilde{J_{h}^{n}}:D^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for

D^{n}:=\left\{z_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}\mid m_{h}(z_{h}^{n},1)=m_{h}(U_{h}^{n-1},1)\right\}

given by

\widetilde{J_{h}^{n}}(z_{h}^{n}):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}\left(F_{1}(z_{h}^{n}),1\right)-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(U_{h,+}^{n-1},z_{h}^{n})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}z_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h})}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau}\left\|z_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}.

It then follows that if
U_{h}^{n}={\arg\min}_{z_{h}^{n}\in D^{n}}\widetilde{J_{h}^{n}}(z_{h}^{n})
then
U_{h}^{n}
solves

\varepsilon a_{h}(U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{\theta}{2\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(U_{h,+}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\tau}m_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1}),\phi_{h}^{n})\\
-\lambda^{n}m_{h}(1,\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
, where

\lambda^{n}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}^{n}}U_{h}^{n}.

Then setting
W_{h}^{n}=\lambda^{n}-\frac{1}{\tau}\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}(U_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1})
, one observes that
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
solves

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}m_{h}(U_{h}^{n}-U_{h,+}^{n-1},\phi_{h}^{n})+a_{h}(W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

\displaystyle m_{h}(W_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(U_{h}^{n},\phi_{h})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(U_{h,+}^{n-1},{\phi_{h}^{n}}),

for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.
Clearly this is the same as (
5.1
), (
5.2
), and the existence of such a
U_{h}^{n}
follows as before.

The proof of (numerical) stability follows by similar arguments to Lemma
3.9
, provided that
\tau<\frac{C\varepsilon}{\theta}
where
C
is the constant appearing in (
3.9
).
We notice that instead of (
3.20
), one obtains

U^{n}\cdot A^{n}\frac{(U^{n}-U^{n-1})}{\tau}+\mathcal{F}_{1}^{n}(U^{n})\cdot\frac{(U^{n}-U^{n-1})}{\tau\varepsilon}+W^{n}\cdot A^{n}W^{n}\\
=\frac{\theta}{\tau\varepsilon}U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n-1}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})-W^{n}\cdot\frac{(M^{n}-M^{n-1})}{\tau}U^{n-1},

where the only difference is the term
\frac{\theta}{\tau\varepsilon}U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n-1}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})
.
One then writes this as

\frac{\theta}{\tau\varepsilon}U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n-1}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})=\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}\left(U^{n}\cdot M^{n}U^{n}-U^{n-1}\cdot M^{n-1}U^{n-1}\right)\\
-\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})\cdot M^{n-1}(U^{n}-U^{n-1})+\frac{\theta}{2\tau\varepsilon}\left[U^{n}\cdot M^{n-1}U^{n}-U^{n}\cdot M^{n}U^{n}\right],

where the first term corresponds to the non-coercive part of the potential, and the second term is non-positive and may be ignored.
The final term is problematic however, as we must control this using (
3.9
), which introduces a term
\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}\|U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}
, which must be controlled by using the discrete Grönwall inequality.
Following the proof of Lemma
3.9
one finds that this happens by taking
\tau
small enough so that this term can be “absorbed into the left hand side”.
In particular this requires us to take a timestep size such that
\tau=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta})
.
While numerical stability introduces a timestep requirement for our implicit-explicit scheme, we observe that this requirement is significantly less restrictive than that of the fully implicit scheme, which requires
\tau=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{\theta^{2}})
.
This reduced timestep requirement comes from the fact that this discretisation yields requires one to solve a system involving only monotone operators, as we have considered the concave term at the previous timestep.
We omit further details on this proof.

One may expect that modifying Proposition
3.11
will introduce some timestep restriction.
We consider this in the follows result, we show in the following result that there is continuous dependence on the initial data with no such timestep restriction.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
U_{h,0}^{1},U_{h,0}^{2}\in S_{h}^{0}
be such that
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}(0)}U_{h,0}^{1}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Gamma_{h}(0)}U_{h,0}^{2}
.
Denoting the solution of (
5.1
),(
5.2
) with initial data
U_{h,0}^{i}
by
(U_{h}^{n,i},W_{h}^{n,i})
we have that for
1\leq N\leq N_{T}
,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\left\|U_{h}^{N,1}-U_{h}^{N,2}\right\|_{S_{h}^{N}}^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}(U_{h}^{N,1}-U_{h}^{N,2})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\leq e^{Ct_{N}}\left\|U_{h,0}^{1}-U_{h,0}^{2}\right\|_{S_{h}^{0}}^{2}.

Proof.

As before, we define

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}=U_{h,1}^{n}-U_{h,2}^{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{h}^{n}=W_{h,1}^{n}-W_{h,2}^{n},

and note that from (
5.1
), (
5.2
)

(5.6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\right)+a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=0,

(5.7)

\displaystyle m_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}),

holds for all
\phi_{h}^{n}\in S_{h}^{n}
.

We test (
5.6
) with
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}
for

m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})+\tau a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=0,

and observe

a_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=m_{h}(\widehat{W}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\\
-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}}),

and one obtains

(5.8)

m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})+\varepsilon\tau a_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})+\frac{\tau}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\\
=\frac{\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}}).

Convexity of
F_{1}(\cdot)
implies
m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,1}^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h,2}^{n}),\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})\geq 0
as usual, and so we neglect this term.
As in the proof of Proposition
3.11
we observe from (
3.36
), (
3.37
) that

m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}+\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}\right),

and so (
5.8
) becomes

(5.9)

\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}+\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}+2\varepsilon\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
\leq\frac{2\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})+\left(\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}\right).

As shown in the proof of Proposition
3.11
we have that this final term is bounded by using

(5.10)

\displaystyle\left|\left\|\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}\right|\leq C\tau\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}.

All that remains is to bound
m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})
, for which we use (
3.8
), the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}
and Young’s inequality to see that

(5.11)

\displaystyle\frac{2\tau\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1},\underline{\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}})\leq\varepsilon\tau\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{C\tau\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}

Now using (
5.9
), (
5.10
) in (
5.8
) and summing over
n
one obtains

\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{N}\right\|_{S_{h}^{N}}^{2}-\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{0}\right\|_{S_{h}^{0}}^{2}+\varepsilon\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}-\widehat{U}_{h,+}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}\\
\leq C\tau\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\|\widehat{U}_{h}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2},

where
C
depends on
\varepsilon,\theta
.
The result follows from a discrete Grönwall inequality. ∎∎

5.2.
Error analysis

We use the same notation as in Section
4

\displaystyle(u^{n})^{-\ell}-U_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(u^{n})^{-\ell}-\Pi_{h}u^{n}}_{=:\rho_{u}^{n}}+\underbrace{\Pi_{h}u^{n}-U_{h}^{n}}_{=:\sigma_{u}^{n}},

\displaystyle(w^{n})^{-\ell}-W_{h}^{n}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(w^{n})^{-\ell}-\Pi_{h}w^{n}}_{=:\rho_{w}^{n}}+\underbrace{\Pi_{h}w^{n}-U_{h}^{n}}_{=:\sigma_{w}^{n}},

where
u^{n}=u(t_{n}),w^{n}=w(t_{n}),
and we understand
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
as the unique solution pair of (
5.1
), (
5.2
).
Note that (
4.1
) implies that
\Pi_{h}u^{n},\Pi_{h}w^{n}
are defined for all
n
.
Recall that

\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|\rho_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\rho_{w}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
\leq Ch^{2(k+1)}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}+\|w\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\right),

and so our focus is in bounding
\sigma_{u}^{n}
and
\sigma_{w}^{n}
as before.

As in Section
4
one finds that

(5.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\right)+a_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})=E_{1}(\phi_{h}^{n}),

where

\displaystyle E_{1}(\phi_{h}^{n}):=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\right)-m(\partial^{\bullet}u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})-g(u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell}),

which is the same as (
4.3
).
The analogue of (
4.4
) is now given by

(5.13)

m_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-\varepsilon a_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})+\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})\\
=E_{2}(\phi_{h}^{n})+E_{3}(\phi_{h}^{n})+E_{4}(\phi_{h}^{n})+E_{5}(\phi_{h}^{n})

where

\displaystyle E_{2}(\phi_{h}^{n}):=m_{h}(\Pi_{h}w^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m(w^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell}),

\displaystyle E_{3}(\phi_{h}^{n}):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[m(F_{1}^{\prime}(u^{n}),(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})-m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n}),\phi_{h}^{n})\right],

\displaystyle E_{4}(\phi_{h}^{n}):=\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\left[m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})-m(u^{n},(\phi_{h}^{n})^{\ell})\right],

\displaystyle E_{5}(\phi_{h}^{n}):=\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}\left[m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u^{n-1},\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}})-m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u^{n},\phi_{h}^{n})\right].

Noting the consistency errors are defined only in terms of the true solution pair
(u,w)
, one finds that Lemma
4.1
still holds and so we have bounded
|E_{i}(\phi_{h}^{n})|
for
i=1,...,4
but it remains to bound
|E_{5}(\phi_{h}^{n})|
.
This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
E_{5}
defined as above, one has

(5.14)

\displaystyle|E_{5}(\phi_{h}^{n})|\leq C\sqrt{\tau}\left(\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\phi_{h}^{n}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle E_{5}(\phi_{h}^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\frac{d}{dt}m_{h}(\Pi_{h}u(t),\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}m_{h}(\partial^{\bullet}_{h}\Pi_{h}u(t),\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))+g_{h}(\Pi_{h}u,\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t))\,dt.

We use (
2.15
), (
2.20
), and the bounds on
V_{h}
to see that

\displaystyle|E_{5}(\phi_{h}^{n})|\leq C\int_{t_{n-1}}^{t_{n}}\left(\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}+\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{H^{2}(\Gamma(t))}\right)\|\underline{\phi_{h}^{n}}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}(t))}\,dt.

The result then follows from (
3.7
), and an application of Hölder’s inequality. ∎∎

With this we can adapt Theorem
4.2
as follows.

Theorem 5.4
.

Given initial data
u_{0}\in H^{k+1}(\Gamma(0))
,
U_{h,0}=\Pi_{h}u_{0}
, and assuming (
4.1
), (
4.2
) hold then for sufficiently small
\tau,h
such that

\tau\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}+C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4},

we have the following:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varepsilon\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|(u^{n})^{-\ell}-U_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\varepsilon h^{2}\max_{n=1,...,N_{T}}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}((u^{n})^{-\ell}-U_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
\leq C_{h}h^{2({k+1})}+C_{\tau}\tau^{2},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tau\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|(w^{n})^{-\ell}-W_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\tau h^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{T}}\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}((w^{n})^{-\ell}-W_{h}^{n})\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}\\
\leq C_{h}h^{2({k+1})}+C_{\tau}\tau^{2},\end{aligned}

where
(U_{h}^{n},W_{h}^{n})
is the solution pair from the implicit-explicit scheme (
5.1
), (
5.2
).

\displaystyle C_{\tau}=C\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\|(\partial^{\bullet})^{2}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt,

\displaystyle C_{h}=C\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial^{\bullet}u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}\,dt+C\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\|w\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}+\frac{\theta^{2}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Gamma(t))}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+\frac{CL_{F}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Proof.

This proof is largely the same as that of Theorem
4.2
, with obvious modifications in line with the preceding lemma, and so we omit the calculations.
We note that here we assume
F_{1}^{\prime}
is Lipschitz continuous, and the extension to a non-Lipschitz
F_{1}^{\prime}
holds before.
As in seen in Theorem
4.2
, we take
\tau
sufficiently small so that

\tau\left(\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{C\theta}{\varepsilon}+\frac{L_{F}^{2}+C\theta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4},

and so we still require the same timestep restriction to obtain an error bound. ∎∎

While this proof gives an optimal order of convergence, it has the same timestep restriction as in Theorem
4.2
, which almost defeats the point of considering the implicit-explicit scheme.
One can possibly avoid this by proving a weaker error bound, similar to the arguments of
[
7
]
, which we outline here.

Firstly, testing (
5.12
) with
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n}
for

\frac{1}{\tau}\left(a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n})-a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\sigma_{u}^{n-1})\right)+a_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n})\\
+\frac{1}{\tau}a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n}}-\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\sigma_{u}^{n-1})=E_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n}),

where we have used the definition of
\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}
.
Likewise from (
5.13
) we find that

a_{h}(\sigma_{w}^{n},\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n})=\varepsilon a_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n},\sigma_{u}^{n})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{u}^{n})-\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\sigma_{u}^{n}})\\
+\sum_{i=2}^{5}E_{i}(\sigma_{u}^{n}).

Combining these one finds that

\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\left\|\sigma_{u}^{n}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n}}^{2}-\left\|\sigma_{u}^{n-1}\right\|_{S_{h}^{n-1}}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon\|\nabla_{\Gamma_{h}}\sigma_{u}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{h}^{n})}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{u}^{n})\\
=\frac{\theta}{\varepsilon}m_{h}(\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\sigma_{u}^{n}})+\frac{1}{\tau}a_{h}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\sigma_{u}^{n-1},\underline{\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n}}-\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n-1}}\sigma_{u}^{n-1})+E_{1}(\mathcal{G}_{S_{h}^{n}}\sigma_{u}^{n})-\sum_{i=2}^{5}E_{i}(\sigma_{u}^{n}).

The simplification now comes from using the monotonicity of
F_{1}^{\prime}
so that
m_{h}(F_{1}^{\prime}(\Pi_{h}u^{n})-F_{1}^{\prime}(U_{h}^{n}),\sigma_{u}^{n})\geq 0
, and one should be able to remove the
L_{F}
term on the timestep size restriction.
Similarly the non-coercive term now involves both
\sigma_{u}^{n}
and
\sigma_{u}^{n-1}
, which means we can obtain a less strict timestep size restriction.
We do not continue this argument, which is similar to the proof of Proposition
5.2
, but we note that the end result should be an optimal order
L^{2}_{H^{1}}
error bound for
U_{h}^{n}
.

6.3.
Sharp interface limit on an evolving sphere

In this subsection we consider the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
on the surface given by the zero level set of the function

\phi(x,y,z,t)=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}-0.9-0.1\cos(20\pi t),

over a time interval
t\in[0,0.3]
.
On a stationary Euclidean domain it is known that the sharp interface limit of the Cahn-Hilliard equation yields a free boundary problem for the zero level set of the solution,
u
, known as the Mullins-Sekerka problem
[
1
,
44
,
45
]
.
While this problem has been studied extensively on a stationary Euclidean domain, there are few results available for an evolving surface.
We perform some rudimentary numerical experiments to investigate the formation of a sharp interface as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
for initial data given by

u_{0}(x,y,z)=0.5x-0.1,

on a mesh with
h\approx 3.827328\cdot 10^{-2}
, and a timestep size
\tau=2\cdot 10^{-5}
.
We vary the interface width
\varepsilon
but keep these other parameters fixed.
In the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
we observe the formation (see Figure
4
) and propagation (Figure
5
) of a sharp interface.
Moreover computing the difference of the solutions for
\varepsilon=0.2,0.1
and the solution corresponding to
\varepsilon=0.05
we observe that halving
\varepsilon
results in roughly half the error in the
L^{2}
norm
3

3

The error in
u
for
\varepsilon=0.2

\approx 1.11

These numerical experiments indicate convergence to a sharp interface limit, which one would expect is given by some evolving surface analogue of the Mullins-Sekerka problem — however the results here are quite basic and should be explored further in future works devoted to the sharp interface limit.
