Asymptotics for the cubic 1D NLS with a slowly decaying potential Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. 35Q55
Gavin Stewart

Abstract
. We consider the asymptotics of the one-dimensional cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation with an external potential V V that does not admit bound states. Assuming that ⟨ x ⟩ 2 + ​ V ​ ( x ) ∈ L 1 \langle x\rangle^{2+}V(x)\in L^{1} and that u u is orthogonal to any zero-energy resonances, we show that solutions exhibit modified scattering behavior. Our decay assumptions are weaker than those appearing in previous work and are likely nearly optimal for exceptional potentials, since the Møller wave operators are not known to be bounded on L p L^{p} spaces unless ⟨ x ⟩ 2 ​ V ∈ L 1 \langle x\rangle^{2}V\in L^{1} . Our method combines two techniques: First, we prove a new class of linear estimates, which allow us to relate vector fields associated with the equation with potential to vector fields associated with the flat equation, up to localized error terms. By using the improved local decay coming from the zero-energy assumption, we can treat these errors perturbatively, allowing us to treat the weighted estimates using the same vector field arguments as in the flat case. Second, we derive the asymptotic profile using the wave packet testing method introduced by Ifrim and Tataru. Again, by taking advantage of improved local decay near the origin, we are able to treat the potential as a lower-order perturbation, allowing us to use tools developed to study the flat problem.

We will consider the long-time dynamics of small solutions to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

(1)

Our main goal in this paper is to prove modified scattering asymptotics for (

1

H=-\Delta+V
cannot have bound states, so we will always make the spectral assumption

(2)

H=-\Delta+V\text{ has no eigenvalues}

We also assume that
V
satisfies the decay assumption:

(3)

V\in L^{1}_{\gamma}\text{ with }\gamma>2

where
L^{1}_{\gamma}=\{V\in L^{1}:\langle x\rangle^{\gamma}V\in L^{1}\}
. This is an improvement on the assumption
\gamma>5/2
in previous works
[
8

, and may be essentially optimal for exceptional potentials in the scale of weighted
L^{1}
spaces, since
L^{p}

\gamma<2

[

u

(4)

\tilde{u}(0,t)\equiv 0

which is equivalent to assuming that
u
is orthogonal to any zero energy resonance. Note that (
4

V
is generic (see

), and will be satisfied if
V
is an even potential with an even zero energy resonance and the initial data for (
1

A central difficulty in proving asymptotics for equations with potentials is to understand the space-time resonance structure of the nonlinearity. In the flat case (
V=0
), the eigenfunctions of
H_{0}=-\Delta
are the complex exponentials
e^{ikx}
, which have the product property

e^{ik_{1}x}e^{-ik_{2}x}e^{ik_{3}x}=e^{i(k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3})x}

In particular, this means that the interaction of three waves with frequencies
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
and
k_{3}
through the cubic nonlinearity will produce an output wave with frequency
k=k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}
. Once a potential is introduced, however, this exact correspondence between input and output frequencies is lost, which leads to major difficulties in even defining resonances. It is only in the past decade that techniques for addressing this difficulty have been proposed. We will briefly review these methods in
Section

1.1
. A common thread through all methods is the following idea:
The nonlinear interactions in (
1
) resemble those of the flat problem (up to manageable errors)
.

We believe that our method embodies this philosophy with minimal technical complexity. In fact, after proving the appropriate linear estimates, we are able to reduce all nonlinear estimates for (
1
) to nonlinear estimates in the flat case, up to errors which are localized in space and can thus be controlled using improved local decay given by
Lemma

11
. Thus, we can completely sidestep the difficulties associated with multilinear estimates in distorted frequency space at the minor cost of proving certain linear estimates. We believe that this has the potential to simplify many arguments in the field.

1.1.
Previous work

An early result on long-time asymptotics for (
1
) with nonlinearities
|u|^{p}u
,
p>3

V\neq 0

[
12

. The assumption
p>3
is essential here, as the Klainerman-Sobolev estimate is known to be insufficient for
p=3

In dimension
3
, Léger was able to prove global existence and scattering for quadratic nonlinear Schrödinger equations with small potentials
[
38
]
. His technique is based on combining ideas from space-time resonances with a Born series expansion for the potential. This has the advantage of generalizing to equations with small electromagnetic potentials
[
37
]
. However, in order for the Born series to converge, the potential must be small, which is not always true in applications.

The first technique to take advantage of the resonant structure in the equation with arbitrarily large potential was introduced by Germain, Hani, and Walsh in
[
21
]
for three-dimensional NLS with nonlinearity
\overline{u}^{2}
and later extended to the one-dimensional cubic equation with generic potential by Germain, Pusateri, and Rousset
[
23

. Since this technique has become the most prominent in the literature, we will briefly outline the key ideas in the one-dimensional case. The main idea is to look at the
nonlinear spectral measure

1

\mu(k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\int\overline{\psi(x,k)}\psi(x,k_{1})\overline{\psi(x,k_{2})}\psi(x,k_{3})\;dx

where
\psi(x,k)
is a solution to

H\psi=k^{2}\psi

with appropriate boundary conditions: see
Section

2.2
for a precise definition. For the flat problem,
\psi(x,k)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{ikx}
, and we have

\mu_{\text{flat}}(k,k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta(k-k_{1}+k_{2}-k_{3})

In general,
\mu
is singular near
k=k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}
, and more regular elsewhere. Thus, we can decompose the nonlinear spectral measure into a singular part
\mu_{S}
and a regular part
\mu_{R}
.

1.2.
Main result

We will prove the following modified scattering result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
V
satisfy
eqs.

2
and
3
, and suppose that
u(x,t)
satisfies (
4
). Then, there exists an
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that if

u(x,1)=e^{iH}u_{*}(x)

with

(5)

\lVert\langle x\rangle u_{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u_{*}\rVert_{H^{1}}=\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}

then equation (
1
) has a global solution decaying at the linear rate:

(6)

\lVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{-1/2}

More precisely, we have the modified scattering asymptotics

(7)

u(x,t)=t^{-1/2}\exp\left(-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}\mp i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\tilde{u}(-\frac{x}{2t},s)|^{2}\;ds\right)u_{\infty}(x/2t)+o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}_{x}\cap L^{2}_{x}}(1)

where
o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}_{x}\cap L^{2}_{x}}(1)
denotes a function
R(x,t)
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{1/2}\lVert R\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x}}+\lVert R\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}=0

Before sketching the argument used to prove
Theorem

1
, let us make a few remarks on the result and possible generalizations:

Remark 1

(Decay rate of the error)
.

We can be more explicit about the decay rate of the error term in (
7
): Examining the proof, we find that

t^{1/2}|R(x,t)|\lesssim\epsilon t^{-1/4+\delta}+\epsilon^{3}t^{1-\gamma/2}

and

\lVert R(x,t)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon t^{-1/2+\delta}+\epsilon^{3}t^{3/4-\gamma/2}

Here, the terms which are cubic in
\epsilon
come from approximating the asymptotic profile
\alpha(v,t)
(see (
66
)) by
\tilde{u}(-v/2,t)
in the phase of the modified scattering term. It is interesting to note that the time decay rate for these phase error terms becomes dominant at exactly the point where the results
[
8
,
9
]
derived using the stationary phase method cease to apply.

Remark 2

(Generic potentials)
.

Our argument does not distinguish between generic and exceptional potentials. Since generic potentials have better low-energy behavior, it is likely that the decay conditions can be lowered further in the generic case. We plan to investigate this in future work.

Remark 3

(Assumptions on the nonlinearity)
.

Although we will focus on the constant coefficient cubic nonlinearity
|u|^{2}u
for simplicity, our methods apply equally well to nonlinearities of the form

a(x)|u|^{2}u+(W*|u|^{2})u

where
a(x)-1
and its derivative decays sufficiently rapidly as
|x|\to\infty
and
W\in L^{1}_{2}
.

1.3.
Sketch of the argument

At a very high level, our argument has two steps:

(1)

Assuming that the decay assumption (
6
) holds, show that
\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon\langle t\rangle^{\delta}
for some small
\delta>0
, where
J_{V}=e^{itH}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{*}(i\partial_{k})\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}e^{-itH}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is the distorted Fourier transform (see
Section

2.2.2
).

(2)

Assuming that
\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}
grows slowly in time, derive an asymptotic equivalent for
u
that is compatible with (
6
) and (
7
).

By a standard bootstrap argument, these two ingredients are enough to prove
Theorem

1
. We now explain how we obtain the slow growth of the weighted norm and the asymptotic equivalent for
u
.

1.3.1.
Slow growth of the weighted norm

To obtain slow growth of the weighted norm, we must control the integrals

\lVert J_{V}u(t)-e^{i(t-1)H}J_{V}u(1)\rVert_{L^{2}}=\left\lVert\int_{1}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}J_{V}(|u|^{2}u(s))\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

Let us briefly review how this is done for the flat NLS

(8)

i\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=\pm|u|^{2}u

since our goal is ultimately to emulate this approach as closely as possible. The weight for the flat Schrödinger equation is given by

J_{0}=e^{-it\Delta}\mathcal{F}^{*}(i\partial_{\xi})\mathcal{F}e^{it\Delta}=x-2it\partial_{x}

and an explicit calculation shows that

(9)

J_{0}(|u|^{2}u)=2|u|^{2}J_{0}u-u^{2}\overline{J_{0}u}

see
[
32
,
29
]
. Thus, if we make the bootstrap assumption that
u
decays like
\epsilon t^{-1/2}
pointwise, we find that

\begin{split}\lVert J_{0}u(x,t)-e^{-i(t-1)\Delta}J_{0}u(x,1)\rVert_{L^{2}}=&\left\lVert\int_{1}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)\Delta}2|u|^{2}J_{0}u(s)-u^{2}\overline{J_{0}u}(s)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\lesssim&\int_{1}^{t}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{s}\lVert J_{0}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\;ds\end{split}

which shows that
\lVert J_{0}u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}
grows no faster than
t^{C\epsilon^{2}}
.

As explained earlier, the main difficulty for (
1
) is to isolate the resonance structure in the presence of the potential. To resolve this difficulty, we prove that
J_{V}
can be written as

J_{V}=T_{V,0}J_{0}+E^{1}_{V,0}(t)+E^{2}_{V,0}(t)+E^{3}_{V,0}(t)

where
E^{j}_{V,0}(t)
are error terms and
T_{V,0}
is bounded on
L^{p}
for
1<p\leq 2
. Roughly speaking,
E^{1}_{V,0}(t)
has no growth in time and so can be controlled by using the
t^{-1/2}
pointwise decay of
u
;
\lVert E^{2}_{V,0}(t)w\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim t\lVert\langle x\rangle^{3/2-2\gamma+}w\rVert_{L^{2}}
, which allows us to use the improved local decay bounds

\lVert\langle x\rangle^{-1}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{-3/4}\lVert J_{v}u\rVert_{L^{2}}

to counterbalance the extra growth in time; and
\lVert E^{3}_{V,0}\rVert_{L^{1+}_{x}}\lesssim\lVert t\langle x\rangle^{-\gamma}w\rVert_{L^{1+}}
, which can be controlled by combining local decay with Strichartz estimate

\left\lVert\int_{1}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}F(s,x)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert F(s,x)\rVert_{L^{4/3+}_{s}(1,t;L^{1+}_{x})}

Thus, we find that

\lVert J_{V}u(t)-e^{i(t-1)H}J_{V}u(1)\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq\left\lVert\int_{1}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}T_{V,0}J_{0}(|u|^{2}u(s))\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\{\text{better terms}\}

Moreover, we can take advantage of the resonance structure (
9
) of the flat cubic NLS
to write

\lVert J_{V}u(t)-e^{i(t-1)H}J_{V}u(1)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\int_{1}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}T_{V,0}\left(2|u|^{2}J_{0}u-u^{2}\overline{J_{0}u}\right)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\{\text{better}\}

To close the estimates, we prove that
J_{0}
can be expressed in terms of
J_{V}
, up to errors:

J_{0}=T_{0,V}J_{V}+E^{1}_{0,V}(t)+E^{2}_{0,V}(t)+E^{3}_{0,V}(t)

The errors
E^{j}_{0,V}
have similar behavior to the
E^{j}_{V,0}
’s (
E^{1}_{0,V}
and
E^{3}_{0,V}
are in fact dual to
E^{1}_{V,0}
and
E^{3}_{V,0}
, respectively), so the terms
|u|^{2}E^{j}_{0,V}u
can again be controlled using a combination of
t^{-1/2}
pointwise decay, improved local decay, and Strichartz estimates. Thus, the leading-order behavior of
J_{V}u
for (
1
) is the same as that for
J_{0}u
in the flat NLS:

(10)

\lVert J_{V}u(t)-e^{i(t-1)H}J_{V}u(1)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\left\lVert\int_{1}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}T_{V,0}\left(2|u|^{2}T_{0,V}J_{V}u-u^{2}\overline{T_{0,V}J_{V}u}\right)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\{\text{better}\}

which is enough to prove that
\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}
can grow only slowly in time.

1.3.2.
Asymptotic equivalent for
u

With the slow growth of
\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}
in hand, we must now derive an asymptotic equivalent for
u
. To do this, we will use the method of testing with wave packets introduced by Ifrim and Tataru in
[
29
]
. This method has been used to find modified scattering asymptotics in a number of settings, including water waves
[
30
,
4
,
31
]
, the mKdV equation
[
26
]
, the SQG front equation
[
3
,
2
]
, and the derivative NLS equation
[
6
]
, among others. However, to the best of the author’s knowledge, it has not previously been used for problems involving potentials.

Intuitively, the idea of testing with wave packets is that the components of the solution of the Schrödinger equation localized to energy
\approx|k|^{2}
should travel with velocity
\approx-2k
. Dispersion then leads to a broadening of the wave packet over time, so it has width
\sqrt{t}
at time
t
. Since this is a semiclassical approximation, it can only be expected to hold in the classically allowed region where
|k|^{2}+V(x)>0
. If we make the approximation that
x\approx-2kt
and that
V(x)
decays like
|x|^{-2}
(which is true, at least in an averaged sense, for
V\in L^{1}_{2}
), then this amounts to restricting to
|k|\gtrsim t^{-1/2}
, or equivalently
|x|\gtrsim t^{1/2}
, so we cannot expect wave packets to give us meaningful information close to the origin. Fortunately, for
|x|\lesssim t^{1/2}
, the hypothesis (
4
) actually gives us the improved decay estimate

|u(x,t)|\lesssim t^{-3/4}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}},\qquad|x|\lesssim t^{1/2}

(see
Lemma

12
), which is compatible with (
6
) and (
7
). Thus, it is enough to obtain asymptotics in the classically allowed region
|x|\gtrsim t^{1/2}
, where we can expect the wave packets to accurately approximate the solution.

For the flat Schrödinger equation, the wave packets are given by

(11)

\Psi_{v}(x,t)=e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\chi\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)

In particular, for
k=-v/2
,
\Psi_{v}
behaves approximately like a solution to the free Schrödinger equation.
One might wonder if we need to modify this definition to accommodate the potential. In fact, since we will always work in the region
|x|\gtrsim t^{1/2}
, we can treat the potential perturbatively, allowing us to work with the flat wave packets (
11
). This is related to the fact that the dynamics of
e^{itH}
approach the free dynamics
e^{-it\Delta}
in an appropriate sense as
t\to\infty
. Using the wave packets, we define

(12)

\alpha(v,t)=\int u(x,t)\overline{\Psi_{v}}(x,t)\;dx

From
[
29
]
, we have that
u(x,t)\approx t^{-1/2}e^{i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\alpha(x/t,t)
provided that
J_{0}u
grows slowly in
L^{2}_{x}
, see
[
29
, Lemma 2.2]
. Since
J_{V}
and
J_{0}
are comparable up to localized errors and we are working in the region
|x|\gtrsim t^{1/2}
, we can effectively replace
J_{0}
with
J_{V}
(up to lower order errors), which shows that
u(x,t)
is also well approximated by
t^{-1/2}e^{i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\alpha(x/t,t)
in our problem. Thus, the problem of finding asymptotics for
u
reduces to proving asymptotics for
\alpha
. By differentiating (
12
) and using (
1
), we find that for
|v|\gtrsim t^{-1/2}

\partial_{t}\alpha(v,t)=\mp\frac{i}{t}|\alpha(v,t)|\alpha(v,t)+\{\text{better terms}\}

which can be integrated to show that
|\alpha(v,t)|=O(\epsilon)
and that the long-time asymptotics of
\alpha
involve a logarithmic phase rotation:

\alpha(v,t)\approx\exp\left(\int_{1}^{t}|\alpha(v,s)|^{2}\frac{ds}{s}\right)A(v,t)

for some
A
. Recalling the relationship between
\alpha
and
u
immediately gives us the decay (
6
), and the asymptotics (
7
) follow similarly once we note that
|\alpha(v,t)|\approx|\tilde{u}(-v/2,t)|
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and preliminaries

We write
A\lesssim B
to mean that
A\leq CB
for some implicit constant
C
. If the implicit constant depends on some parameter
P
, we will write
A\lesssim_{P}B
. We will also use the Japanese bracket notation

\langle x\rangle:=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}

We denote the (flat) Fourier transform by

\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-ix\xi}f(x)\;dx

As is well-known,
\mathcal{F}
is unitary on
L^{2}
, with inverse

\mathcal{F}^{-1}g(x)=\mathcal{F}^{*}g(x)=\check{g}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{ix\xi}g(\xi)\;dx

For a function
m:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the Fourier multiplier
m(D)
by

m(D)f:=\left[\mathcal{F}^{*}m(\xi)\mathcal{F}f\right](x)

By Plancherel’s theorem,
m(D)
defines a bounded operator on
L^{2}
whenever
m\in L^{\infty}
. More generally, we have that

m(D)f(x)=\sqrt{2\pi}\check{m}*f(x)

so if
\check{m}\in L^{1}
, then
m(D):L^{p}\to L^{p}
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Since we will make frequent use of Hardy’s inequality, we record it here for reference:

Lemma 2

(Hardy’s inequality)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function with
f(x_{0})=0
. Then,

\left\lVert\frac{f(x)}{x-x_{0}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\partial_{x}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

2.2.
Spectral theory for
-\Delta+V

2.2.1.
Jost functions and the scattering matrix

Here, we recall the basic spectral theory for
-\Delta+V
, and give bounds which will be used throughout the work. For
V\in L^{1}_{1}
, the equation

(13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(-\Delta+V)f_{\pm}(x,k)=k^{2}f_{\pm}(x,k)\\
\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f_{\pm}(x,k)=e^{\pm ixk}\end{array}\right.

has a solution
f_{\pm}(x,k)
, known as the Jost function. It is often useful to remove the effect of the oscillations and work with the functions

(14)

m_{\pm}(x,k)=e^{\mp ixk}f_{\pm}(x,k)

Then,
m_{\pm}
solves the Volterra integral equation

(15)

m_{+}(x,k)=1\pm\int_{I_{\pm}(x)}D_{k}(\pm(y-x))V(y)m_{\pm}(y,k)\;dy

where
I_{+}(x)=(x,\infty)
,
I_{-}(x)=(-\infty,x)
and

(16)

D_{k}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}e^{2ikz}\;dz=\frac{e^{2ikx}-1}{2ik}

Using the Jost functions, we can define generic and exceptional potentials as follows:

Definition 1
.

A potential
V
is called generic if
\int V(x)m_{\pm}(x,0)\;dx\neq 0
, and exceptional otherwise.

A potential is generic iff
m_{\pm}(x,0)=f_{\pm}(x,0)
is unbounded, which implies that
-\Delta+V
has no zero energy resonances.

The functions
m_{\pm}-1
and their derivatives decay at a rate determined by the spatial decay of the potential. Defining

\mathcal{W}_{\pm}^{a}(x)=\int_{I_{\pm}(x)}\langle y\rangle^{a}|V(y)|;dy

we have that

Lemma 3
.

Let
V\in L^{1}_{\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 1
. For
0\leq\theta\leq 1
and
0\leq n\leq\gamma-1
, we have that

(17)

\begin{split}\left|\partial_{k}^{n}\left(m_{\pm}(x,k)-1\right)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{|k|^{1-\theta}\langle k\rangle^{\theta}}\begin{cases}\mathcal{W}_{\pm}^{n+\theta}(x)&\pm x\geq-1\\
\langle x\rangle^{n+\theta}&\pm x\leq 1\end{cases}\end{split}

Moreover, for the
x
derivative, we have that

(18)

|\partial_{x}\partial_{k}^{n}m_{\pm}(x,k)|\lesssim\begin{cases}\mathcal{W}_{\pm}^{n}(x)&\pm x\geq-1\\
\langle x\rangle^{n}&\pm x\leq 1\end{cases}

Proof.

The proofs for (
18
) and (
17
) with
\theta=1
are standard and can be found in
[
14
,
57
,
15
]
. A form of (
17
) for
\theta\in[0,1)
appears in
[
57
, Lemma 2.1]
with weaker requirements on the potential but without an explicit decay rate in
x
. Since this improvement does not appear to be as well known, we give a proof assuming the estimate for
\theta=1
. We begin with the case
n=0
. Using the Volterra equation (
15
) and focusing on the bound for
m_{+}
for simplicity, we see that

m_{+}(x,k)-1=\int_{x}^{\infty}D_{k}(y-x)V(y)m_{+}(y,k)\;dy

Now, by (
16
), we see that
|D_{k}(y-x)|\lesssim|k|^{1-\theta}|y-x|^{\theta}
, while (
17
) with
\theta=1
implies that
m_{+}(y,k)
is bounded uniformly in
y
and
k
for
y\geq x\geq-1
. Thus, for
|k|\leq 1
it follows that

|m_{+}(x,k)-1|\lesssim\frac{1}{k^{\theta}}\int_{x}^{\infty}|y|^{\theta}|V(y)|\;dy\lesssim\frac{\mathcal{W}_{+}^{\theta}(x)}{|k|^{\theta}\langle k\rangle^{1-\theta}}

while for
|k|>1
, we use
|D_{k}(y-x)|\lesssim|k|^{-1}
to find that

|m_{+}(x,k)-1|\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\int_{x}^{\infty}|V(y)|\;dy\lesssim\frac{\mathcal{W}_{+}^{0}(x)}{|k|}\lesssim\frac{\mathcal{W}_{+}^{\theta}(x)}{|k|^{\theta}\langle k\rangle^{1-\theta}}

For
n\geq 1
, we distribute the derivatives to find that

\partial_{k}^{n}(m_{+}(x,k)-1)=\sum_{n=0}^{s}\int_{x}^{\infty}\partial_{k}^{n}D_{k}(y-x)V(y)\partial_{k}^{s-n}m_{+}(y,k)\;dy

and use the bound
|\partial_{k}^{s-n}m_{+}(y,k)|\lesssim_{V}1
from the
\theta=1
case together with the estimate

\partial_{k}^{a}|D_{k}(z)|\lesssim\min\left(\frac{\langle z\rangle^{a}}{|k|},\langle z\rangle^{a+1}\right)

to obtain the desired bound.
∎

In addition to the bounds (
18
), it will be useful later to have the identity

(19)

\begin{split}\partial_{x}m_{+}(x,k)=&\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{2ik(x-y)}V(y)m_{+}(y,k)\;dy\\
=&\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{2ik(x-y)}V(y)(m_{+}(y,k)-1)\;dy\\
&+\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{2ik(x-y)}V(y)\;dy\end{split}

which arises naturally in the proof of (
18
). The form (
19
) will prove useful for us later, since each of the individual terms have better properties than suggested by the bound (
18
):

(1)

The first term contains both
V(y)
and
m_{+}(y,k)-1
, and thus can be shown to decay like
\frac{\langle x\rangle^{2\gamma-\theta}}{|k|^{1-\theta}\langle k\rangle^{\theta}}
using (
17
).

(2)

The second term only depends on
k
through the complex exponential, which allows us to re-express
x
integrals in terms of the Fourier transform.

We note that a similar identity holds for
m_{-}
:

(20)

\begin{split}\partial_{x}m_{-}(x,k)=&\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{2ik(y-x)}V(y)(m_{-}(y,k)-1)\;dy\\
&+\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{2ik(y-x)}V(y)\;dy\end{split}

Returning to the eigenvalue problem (
13
), we see that for
k
nonzero
\{f_{\pm}(x,k),f_{\pm}(x,-k)\}
forms a basis for the solutions of (
13
). In particular, this implies that we can express
f_{\pm}(x,k)
as a linear combination of
f_{\mp}(x,k)
and
f_{\mp}(x,-k)
. This allows us to define the reflection and transmission coefficients, which for
k\neq 0
satisfy the equations

(21)

f_{+}(x,k)=\frac{R_{-}(k)}{T(k)}f_{-}(x,k)+\frac{1}{T(k)}f_{-}(x,-k)

(22)

f_{-}(x,k)=\frac{R_{+}(k)}{T(k)}f_{+}(x,k)+\frac{1}{T(k)}f_{+}(x,-k)

Intuitively, these equations can be understood as follows: Multiplying through by
T(k)
and rearranging, we see that

f_{\pm}(x,k)=T(-k)f_{\mp}(x,k)+R_{\pm}(-k)f_{\pm}(x,-k)

If we interpret the left-hand side as representing a wave
e^{\pm ikx}
coming toward the potential from
\pm\infty
(the incident wave), then the potential reflects a wave
R_{\pm}(-k)e^{\mp ixk}
back toward
\pm\infty
(the reflected wave) and allows a wave
T(-k)e^{\pm ikx}
to travel to
\mp\infty
(the transmitted wave).

Remark 4
.

Strictly speaking, the interpretation above is only correct when
\mp k>0
, since otherwise the dispersive relation for the equation suggests that the waves will be moving in the opposite directions (that is, the ‘incident’ wave will be moving outward, while the ‘transmitted’ and ‘reflected’ waves will be moving inward). In particular, it will not be valid under our convention for the distorted Fourier transform.

Recall that the Wronskian for (
13
) is

(23)

W(f,g)=\partial_{x}f(x)g(x)-f(x)\partial_{x}g(x)

and that it is independent of
x
for
f
and
g
solving (
13
). By taking Wronskians of various combinations of
f_{\pm}(x,\pm k)
, we obtain the explicit formulas

(24)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{T(k)}=&\frac{1}{2ik}W(f_{+}(x,k),f_{-}(x,k))=1-\frac{1}{2ik}\int V(x)m_{\pm}(x,k)\;dx\\
\frac{R_{\pm}(k)}{T(k)}=&\frac{1}{2ik}W(f_{-}(x,\pm k),f_{+}(x,\mp k))=\frac{1}{2ik}\int e^{\mp 2ikx}V(x)m_{\mp}(x,k)\;dx\end{split}

together with the relations

(25)

\begin{split}T(-k)=\overline{T(k)},&\qquad R_{\pm}(-k)=\overline{R_{\pm}(k)}\\
|R_{\pm}(k)|^{2}+|T(k)|^{2}=1,&\qquad T(k)\overline{R_{-}(k)}+\overline{T(K)}R_{+}(k)=0\end{split}

In particular, by the third relation,
|T(k)|,|R_{\pm}(k)|\leq 1
. Note that by
Definition

1
,
T(0)=0
and
R_{\pm}(0)=-1
for generic potentials.

A result of
[
18
]
implies that
T(D)
and
R_{\pm}(D)
are bounded on Lebesgue spaces:

Lemma 4
.

For
V\in L^{1}_{1}
, the operators
T(D)
and
R_{\pm}(D)
are bounded on
L^{p}
for any
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

We will also need the following derivative bounds:

Lemma 5
.

Let
V\in L^{1}_{2}
. Then,

(26)

|T^{\prime}(k)|,|R_{\pm}^{\prime}(k)|\lesssim\langle k\rangle^{-1}

Proof.

First, we recall that by
[
19
, Theorem 2.2]

T^{\prime}
and
R_{\pm}^{\prime}
belong to the Weiner algebra of functions having an integrable Fourier transform. It follows that
T^{\prime},R_{\pm}^{\prime}
are bounded, which gives (
26
) for
|k|\leq 1
. To handle the case
k>1
, we work with the representation formulas (
24
). Differentiating the integral representation for
T
, we find that

T^{\prime}(k)=[T(k)]^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(x)\partial_{k}\left(\frac{m_{+}(x,k)}{2ik}\right)\;dx

Now, by
Lemma

3
, we have that
|m_{+}(x,k)|,|\partial_{k}m_{+}(x,k)|\lesssim\frac{\langle x\rangle^{2}}{\langle k\rangle}
, so

|T^{\prime}(k)|\lesssim\frac{1}{k^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle x\rangle^{2}|V(x)|\;dx

which is better than required. Similarly,

R^{\prime}_{\pm}(k)=\partial_{k}\left(T(k)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\mp 2ikx}V(x)m_{\mp}(x,k)\;dx\right)

and a similar argument shows that

|R^{\prime}_{\pm}(k)|\lesssim\frac{1}{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\langle x\rangle^{2}|V(x)|\;dx

∎

2.2.2.
The distorted Fourier transform

With the Jost solutions in hand, we are now in a position to define the distorted Fourier transform. Let

(27)

\psi(x,k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\begin{cases}T(k)f_{+}(x,k)&k\geq 0\\
T(-k)f_{-}(x,-k)&k<0\end{cases}

Then, the distorted Fourier transform of a function
f
is given by

(28)

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}f(k)=\tilde{f}(k):=\int\overline{\psi(x,k)}f(x)\;dx

Note that for
V=0
,
\psi(x,k)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{ixk}
and we recover the flat Fourier transform. Many of the well-known properties of the flat Fourier transform carry over (with minor adjustments) to the distorted setting. The following lemma summarizes the properties that will be most useful for us:

Lemma 6
.

(i)

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}
is unitary from
L^{2}_{x}
to
L^{2}_{k}
, with inverse given by

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}g=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{*}g=\int\psi(x,k)g(k)\;dk

(ii)

If
V
is generic, then
\tilde{f}(0)=0
for
f\in L^{1}
.

(iii)

If
V
is exceptional and
\psi_{+}(x,0)
is even (resp. odd), then
\tilde{f}(0)=0
for
f
odd (resp. even). In particular,
\tilde{f}(0)=0
if
V
is even and
f
is odd.

(iv)

For
f\in L^{1}
,
\tilde{f}(k)
is continuous for all
k\neq 0
. In addition, if
\tilde{f}(0)=0
, then
\tilde{f}(k)
is continuous at
k=0
.

(v)

\lVert\partial_{k}\tilde{f}(k)\rVert_{L^{2}_{k}}\lesssim\lVert\langle x\rangle f\rVert_{L^{2}}

Proofs for each of these facts can be found in
[
23
,
1
,
17
,
58
]
. It will often be useful in our proof to write the distorted Fourier transform in terms of incident, reflected, and transmitted waves (see the discussion after
Equations

21
and
22
). To this end, we define the
partial distorted Fourier transforms
as

(29)

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,T}\phi(k)=&\mathds{1}_{k>0}\frac{\overline{T(k)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\chi_{+}(x)\overline{f_{+}(x,k)}\phi(x)\;dx\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{-,T}\phi(k)=&\mathds{1}_{k<0}\frac{\overline{T(-k)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\chi_{-}(x)\overline{f_{-}(x,-k)}\phi(x)\;dx\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,I}\phi(k)=&\mathds{1}_{k>0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\chi_{-}(x)\overline{f_{-}(x,-k)}\phi(x)\;dx\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{-,I}\phi(k)=&\mathds{1}_{k<0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\chi_{+}(x)\overline{f_{+}(x,k)}\phi(x)\;dx\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,R}\phi(k)=&\mathds{1}_{k>0}\frac{\overline{R_{-}(k)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\chi_{-}(x)\overline{f_{-}(x,k)}\phi(x)\;dx\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{-,R}\phi(k)=&\mathds{1}_{k<0}\frac{\overline{R_{+}(-k)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\chi_{+}(x)\overline{f_{+}(x,-k)}\phi(x)\;dx\end{split}

where
\chi_{+}(x)+\chi_{-}(x)=1
and
\chi_{\pm}(x)
is a smooth function supported in
\pm x>-1
. We will sometimes write
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{T}=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,T}+\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{-,T}
, and similarly for
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{R},\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{I}
. Although the partial DFTs are not unitary, they are bounded on
L^{2}
:

Lemma 7
.

For
\gamma>1
, the partial DFT operators defined in (
29
) are bounded on
L^{2}
. Moreover, if
\gamma>2
, then for
1<p<2
and
A\in\{T,R,I\}
,
\mathcal{F}^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pm,A}:L^{p}\to L^{p}
.

Proof.

We will give the argument for
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,T}
, since the arguments for the other operators are similar. Note that we can write

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,T}\phi(k)=\mathds{1}_{k>0}\overline{T(k)}\hat{\phi}(k)+\mathds{1}_{k>0}\overline{T(k)}\int\mathcal{K}_{+,T}(x,k)\phi(x)\;dx

where

\mathcal{K}_{+,T}(x,k)=\mathds{1}_{k>0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\chi_{+}(x)(\overline{m_{+}(x,k)}-1)e^{-ixk}

Since
T(k)
is bounded, we have that

\lVert\mathds{1}_{k>0}\overline{T(k)}\hat{\phi}\rVert_{L^{2}_{k}}\lesssim\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}

For the second term, we note that by
Lemma

3

(30)

|\mathcal{K}_{+,T}(x,k)|\lesssim\frac{\langle x\rangle^{\theta-\gamma}}{|k|^{1-\theta}\langle k\rangle^{\theta}}

which is
L^{2}_{x,k}
for
1/2<\theta<\gamma-1/2
. Thus, by Schur’s lemma,

\lVert\int\mathcal{K}_{+,T}(x,k)\phi(x)\;dx\rVert_{L^{2}_{k}}\lesssim\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}

To prove the second part of the theorem, we write

\mathcal{F}^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{+,T}\phi=\mathds{1}_{D>0}T(D)\phi+\mathds{1}_{D>0}T(D)\mathcal{F}^{*}\int\overline{(m_{+}(y,k)-1)}e^{-iyk}\chi_{+}(y)\phi(y)\;dy

Since
\mathds{1}_{D>0}
and
T(D)
are bounded on
L^{p}
, it is enough to show that the operator

\mathcal{F}^{*}\int\overline{(m_{+}(y,k)-1)}e^{-iyk}\chi_{+}(y)\phi(y)\;dy:=\mathcal{F}^{*}A\phi

is bounded on
L^{p}
. To do this, we will show that
A
and
\partial_{k}A
are bounded from
L^{1}_{x}
to
L^{2}_{k}
. This shows that
A:L^{1}_{x}\to\langle k\rangle^{-1}L^{2}_{k}\subset L^{1}_{k}
, and by interpolating with the bound
A:L^{2}_{x}\to L^{2}_{k}
proved earlier, we get the result. The
L^{1}_{x}\to L^{2}_{k}
bound for
A
follows immediately from (
30
), so we focus on the bound for
\partial_{k}A
. We have that

\begin{split}\partial_{k}A\phi=&-i\int y\overline{(m_{+}(y,k)-1)}e^{-iyk}\chi_{+}(y)\phi(y)\;dy\\
&+\int\overline{\partial_{k}m_{+}(y,k)}e^{-iyk}\chi_{+}(y)\phi(y)\;dy\\
=&B_{1}\phi+B_{2}\phi\end{split}

The first term is simply
yK_{+,T}(y,k)
, and with
\gamma>2
the bound (
30
) with
\theta=1
is sufficient to show that
\lVert yK_{+,T}(y,k)\rVert_{L^{2}_{k}L^{\infty}_{y}}<\infty
, so
\lVert B_{1}\rVert_{L^{1}_{x}\to L^{2}_{k}}<\infty
. Turning to
B_{2}
, we note that

|\partial_{k}m_{+}(y,k)\chi_{+}(y)|\lesssim\frac{\langle y\rangle^{2-\gamma}}{\langle k\rangle}\in L^{2}_{k}L^{\infty}_{y}

which gives the desired bound for
B_{2}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Note that
Lemma

7
also implies that the adjoint partial DFTs
\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\pm,A}^{*}
are bounded on
L^{2}
.

2.2.3.
Wave operators

The Møller wave operator
\Omega
is defined by

(31)

\Omega=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{*}\mathcal{F}

Since the flat and distorted Fourier transforms are unitary, so is the wave operator. In particular, its inverse is given by

(32)

\Omega^{-1}=\Omega^{*}=\mathcal{F}^{*}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}

For our purposes, it will only be necessary to note that the wave operator and its adjoint are bounded on
L^{p}
,
1<p<\infty
:

Lemma 8
.

For
1<p<\infty
and for
\gamma\geq 2
,
\Omega,\Omega^{*}:L^{p}\to L^{p}
.

For proof, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
. In general, the endpoints
p=1
and
p=\infty
are excluded, since the wave operator contains a Hilbert transform unless the potential is very exceptional
[
56
]
.

2.3.
Dispersive estimates

We now record several dispersive estimates that will be of use to us. The first is a decay estimate:

Lemma 9
.

Suppose
V\in L^{1}_{1}
. For
\phi\in L^{1}
, we have the estimate

(33)

\lVert e^{itH}\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{-1/2}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{1}}

Moreover, if instead
\tilde{\phi}\in L^{\infty}
and
\partial_{k}\tilde{\phi}\in L^{2}
, then

(34)

\lVert e^{itH}\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{-1/2}\lVert\tilde{\phi}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+t^{-3/4}\lVert\partial_{k}\tilde{\phi}\rVert_{L^{2}}

Proof.

Equation (
33
) follows from
[
18
]
(see also
[
25
]
), while (
34
) is proved in
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

Since
e^{itH}
is unitary, the abstract Strichartz estimate of Keel-Tao
[
33
]
immediately gives us the following lemma:

Lemma 10
.

Suppose
V\in L^{1}_{1}
. Then,

(35)

\lVert e^{itH}\phi\rVert_{S}\lesssim\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}

and

(36)

\left\lVert\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)H}F(s)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{\tilde{q}}_{t}L^{\tilde{r}}_{x}}\lesssim\lVert F\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}

where
(q,r)
and
(\tilde{q},\tilde{r})
are any exponents satisfying the admissibility condition

(37)

\frac{1}{r}+\frac{2}{q}=\frac{1}{2},\qquad q,r\geq 2

If
V\in L^{1}_{2}
and
\tilde{\phi}(0)=0
, then we get improved decay of
e^{itH}\phi
near
0
.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose
V\in L^{1}_{2}
. Then, if
\tilde{\phi}(0)=0
,

(38)

\lVert\langle x\rangle^{-1}e^{itH}\phi\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim t^{-3/4}\left(\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert\partial_{k}\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)

This result is proved in
[
8
]
. It also follows from
Lemma

9
and the following result, which gives local decay in the region
|x|\lesssim t^{1/2}
:

Lemma 12
.

Suppose
V\in L^{1}_{2}
, and let
A>0
. If
\tilde{\phi}(0)=0
and
|x|\leq At^{1/2}
, then

(39)

|e^{itH}\phi(x)|\lesssim_{A}t^{-3/4}\lVert\partial_{k}\tilde{\phi}\rVert_{L^{2}}

To prove
Lemma

12
, we will make use of the following abstract nonstationary phase result (cf.
[
23
, Lemma 3.3]
):

Lemma 13
.

Suppose
a(x,k)
satisfies

(40)

|a(x,k)|+|k||\partial_{k}a(x,k)|\lesssim 1

uniformly in
x
and
k
, and suppose that
f\in H^{1}_{k}
is a function vanishing at
k=0
. Define

(41)

\mathrm{I}(t,X,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}a(x,k)f(k)\;dk

Then, if
\supp(f)\cap(X-ct^{-1/2},X+ct^{-1/2})=\emptyset
,

(42)

|\mathrm{I}(t,X,x)|\lesssim_{c}t^{-3/4}\lVert\partial_{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

with implicit constant independent of
t,X
and
x
.

Proof.

We will first prove the case where
X\geq 0
. Based on the support condition, we can write

\begin{split}\mathrm{I}(t,X,x)=&\int_{X+ct^{-1/2}}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}a(x,k)f(k)\;dk+\int_{0}^{X-ct^{-1/2}}e^{-it(k-X)^{2}}a(x,k)f(k)\;dk\\
=:&\mathrm{I}_{1}+\mathrm{I}_{2}\end{split}

with the convention that
\mathrm{I}_{2}=0
if
X<ct^{-1/2}
. Integration by parts yields that

\begin{split}\mathrm{I}_{1}=&\frac{i}{2t}\int_{X+ct^{-1/2}}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}\partial_{k}\left(\frac{1}{k-X}a(x,k)f(k)\right)\;dk\\
=&-\frac{i}{2t}\int_{X+ct^{-1/2}}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}\frac{1}{(k-X)^{2}}a(x,k)f(k)\;dk\\
&+\frac{i}{2t}\int_{X+ct^{-1/2}}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}\frac{1}{k-X}\partial_{k}a(x,k)f(k)\;dk\\
&+\frac{i}{2t}\int_{X+ct^{-1/2}}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}\frac{1}{k-X}a(x,k)\partial_{k}f(k)\;dk\\
=:&\mathrm{I}_{1,1}+\mathrm{I}_{1,2}+\mathrm{I}_{1,3}\end{split}

For
\mathrm{I}_{1,3}
, Cauchy-Schwarz yields the bound

|\mathrm{I}_{1,3}|\lesssim\frac{1}{t}\left(\int_{X+ct^{-1/2}}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{(k-X)^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\lVert\partial_{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim t^{-3/4}\lVert\partial_{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

A similar argument holds for
\mathrm{I}_{1,1}
, since by Hardy’s inequality

\left\lVert\frac{f(k)}{X-k}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert\partial_{k}f\rVert_{L^{2}}

(recall that
f(X)=0
). For
\mathrm{I}_{1,2}
, we use the bound (
40
) to find that

|\mathrm{I}_{1,2}|\lesssim t^{-1}\int\frac{1}{|k-X|}\frac{|f(k)|}{|k|}\;dk

which can again be bounded using Hardy’s inequality. A similar argument holds for
\mathrm{I}_{2}
once we note that
f(X-ct^{-1/2})=f(0)=0
, so again there are no boundary terms.

For
X<0
, we have

I(t,X,x)=\int_{\min(X+ct^{-1/2},0)}^{\infty}e^{it(k-X)^{2}}a(x,k)f(k)\;dk

which can be handled using the same argument.
∎

We now use
Lemma

13
to prove
Lemma

12
.

Proof of
Lemma

12
.

To begin, we decompose

f=\chi\left(\frac{t^{1/2}\sqrt{H}}{2A}\right)f+(1-\chi\left(\frac{t^{1/2}\sqrt{H}}{2A}\right)f)=:f_{\text{lo}}+f_{\text{hi}}

where
\chi(x)
is a bump function supported on
|x|\leq 2

4.1.

The main difficulty in proving weighted estimates for (

as operators

5.
Leading order asymptotics

Now, we show how to obtain the leading-order asymptotics for
u(x,t)
as
t\to\infty
. To do this, we define the (free) wave packet with velocity
v
as

(64)

\Psi_{v}(x,t)=e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\chi\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)

where
\chi
is an even function supported on
(-10,10)
with
\int\chi(z)\;dz=1
. A straightforward calculation shows that
\Psi_{v}
is an approximate solution to the free Schrödinger equation:

(65)

(i\partial_{t}-\Delta)\Psi_{v}=\frac{e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}}{2t}\partial_{x}\left(t^{1/2}\chi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)+i(x-vt)\chi\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)

With the wave-packet in hand, we now define the asymptotic profile

(66)

\alpha(v,t)=\int u(x,t)\overline{\Psi_{v}(x,t)}\;dx

5.1.
Reduction to wave packet dynamics

The asymptotic profile
\alpha
is closely related to the pointwise behavior of
u
in physical and distorted Fourier space (cf.
[
29
, Lemma 2.2]
):

Lemma 18
.

We have that

(67)

\lVert\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}}\lesssim t^{1/2}\lVert u(x,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x}},\quad\lVert\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{v}}\lesssim\lVert u(x,t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}

Moreover, defining
R_{t}=\{v:v>100t^{-1/2}\}
, we have the physical space bounds

(68)

\begin{split}\lVert u(vt,t)-t^{-1/2}e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}(R_{t})}\lesssim&t^{-3/4}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\\
\lVert u(vt,t)-t^{-1/2}e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{v}(R_{t})}\lesssim&t^{-1}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\end{split}

In addition,
\alpha
and is a good approximation to
\tilde{u}
in the sense that

(69)

\begin{split}\lVert e^{-it\frac{v^{2}}{4}}\tilde{u}(-\frac{v}{2},t)-\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}(R_{t})}\lesssim t^{-1/4}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+t^{1-\gamma/2}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\lVert e^{-it\frac{v^{2}}{4}}\tilde{u}(-\frac{v}{2},t)-\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{v}(R_{t})}\lesssim t^{-1/2}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+t^{3/4-\gamma/2}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\end{split}

Proof.

Let us define
q=e^{i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}u
. Then

(70)

\begin{split}t^{-1/2}\alpha(v,t)=&t^{-1/2}\int q(x,t)\chi\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\;dx\\
=&\int q(tz,t)t^{1/2}\chi(t^{1/2}(z-v))\;dz\\
=&[q(t\cdot,t)*t^{1/2}\chi(t^{1/2}\cdot)](v)\end{split}

The first two inequalities in (
67

68

L^{\infty}
and
L^{2}
bounds for

. Using the fact that
\chi
has integral

(71)

q(vt,t)-t^{-1/2}\alpha(v,t)=\int(q(vt,t)-q((v-z)t,t))t^{1/2}\chi(zt^{1/2})\;dz

Let us express the difference as an integral:

(72)

q(vt,t)-q((v-z)t,t)=z\int_{0}^{1}t\partial_{x}q((v-hz)t,t)\;dh

Now, noting that

(73)

-2it\partial_{x}q(x,t)=e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}J_{0}u(x,t)

and using

\begin{split}t\partial_{x}q=&\frac{ie^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}}{2}J_{0}u\\

\end{split}

For
v\in R^{+}_{t}
,

, we have

, so

\begin{split}\left|q(vt,t)-q((v-z)t,t)\right|\lesssim&\frac{|z|^{1/2}}{t^{1/2}}\left(\lVert T_{0,V}J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert E^{1}_{0,V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+|vt|^{\frac{3}{2}+a-2\gamma}\lVert\langle x\rangle^{2\gamma-a-\frac{3}{2}}E^{2}_{0,V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right.\\

We now prove the estimates (

where

\begin{split}\int\chi_{1}(\xi)\;d\xi=&\int e^{-i\xi^{2}}\mathcal{F}\left\{e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4}}\chi(x)\right\}(\xi)\;d\xi\\

Of course, to prove (

Thus, using the Plancherel theorem for the distorted Fourier transform, we find that

\displaystyle\alpha(v,t)=

\displaystyle\int\tilde{u}(k,t)\overline{\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\Psi_{v}(k,t))}\;dk

(78)

\displaystyle\begin{split}=&t^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{k>0}T(k)\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}\left(t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{v}{2}+k\right)\right)\;dk+t^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{k<0}R(k)\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}\left(t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{v}{2}-k\right)\right)\;dk\\
&+t^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{k<0}\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}\left(t^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{v}{2}+k\right)\right)\;dk+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{u}(k,t)O(\langle k\rangle^{-1}t^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle vt\rangle^{1-\gamma})\;dk\end{split}

Focusing on the integral for the transmitted wave, we have that

(79)

\begin{split}\left|t^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{k>0}T(k)\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}(t^{\frac{1}{2}}(v/2+k))\;dk\right|\lesssim&\left\lVert\frac{\tilde{f}(k,t)}{k}\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{k}}\lVert t^{\frac{1}{2}}(v/2+k)\chi_{1}(t^{\frac{1}{2}}(v/2+k))\rVert_{L^{2}_{k}}\\
\lesssim_{N}&t^{-1/4}|t^{\frac{1}{2}}v|^{-N}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\end{split}

where on the last line we have used the fact that
\chi_{1}
is in the Schwartz class and therefore decays rapidly. A similar estimate holds for the reflected wave, and applying Cauchy-Schwarz shows that the last term in (
78
) is
O(t^{3/2-\gamma}|v|^{1-\gamma})\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}
. Hence, the main contribution to
\alpha
comes from the incident wave:

\alpha(v,t)=t^{1/2}\int_{k<0}\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}(\frac{v}{2}+k))\;dk+O\left(t^{-1/4}\lVert J_{v}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+t^{3/2-\gamma}|v|^{1-\gamma}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)

Since the bound (
79
) did not depend on any special properties of
T(k)
, we have that

\left|t^{1/2}\int_{k<0}\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}(\frac{v}{2}+k))\;dk\right|\lesssim_{N}t^{-1/4}|t^{\frac{1}{2}}v|^{-N}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}

Thus, noting that
t^{1/2}\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}\cdot)
has integral
1
, we can argue as in the proof of (
76
) to find that

\begin{split}t^{1/2}\int_{k<0}\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}(\frac{v}{2}+k))\;dk-\tilde{f}\left(-\frac{v}{2},t\right)=&t^{1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}\left(\kappa-\frac{v}{2},t\right)\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}\kappa)\;d\kappa-\tilde{f}\left(-\frac{v}{2},t\right)\\
&+O\left(t^{-1/4}|t^{\frac{1}{2}}v|^{-N}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\\
=&t^{1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\tilde{f}\left(\kappa-\frac{v}{2},t\right)-\tilde{f}\left(-\frac{v}{2},t\right)\right)\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}\kappa)\;d\kappa\\
&+O\left(t^{-1/4}|t^{\frac{1}{2}}v|^{-N}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\\
=&\int\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{k}\tilde{f}\left(h\kappa-\frac{v}{2},t\right)t^{1/2}\kappa\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}\kappa)\;dhd\kappa\\
&+O\left(t^{-1/4}|t^{\frac{1}{2}}v|^{-N}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\\
=&O\left(t^{-1/4}(1+|t^{\frac{1}{2}}v|^{-N})\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\\
\end{split}

so

|\tilde{f}(-\frac{v}{2},t)-\alpha(v,t)|\lesssim t^{-1/4}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+t^{3/2-\gamma}|v|^{1-\gamma}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}

which gives the
L^{\infty}
part of (
69
). For the
L^{2}
estimate, we note that

\begin{split}\left\lVert\tilde{f}(-v/2,t)-t^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}(k,t)\chi_{1}(t^{\frac{1}{2}}(v/2+k))\;dk\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{v}(R^{+}_{t})}\leq&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lVert\partial_{v}\tilde{f}\rVert_{L^{2}_{v}}|t^{1/2}\kappa\chi_{1}(t^{1/2}\kappa)|\;d\kappa\\
\lesssim&t^{-1/2}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\end{split}

Combining this with the estimates for the other terms, we find that

\lVert\tilde{f}(-\frac{v}{2},t)-\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{2}(R_{t})}\lesssim t^{-1/2}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+t^{3/4-\gamma/2}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}

which completes the proof of (
69
).
∎

5.2.
Asymptotics for
\alpha

Now, we show that the evolution of
\alpha(v,t)
is essentially given by a Hamiltonian ODE for
|v|\gtrsim t^{-1/2}
, which can be integrated to give the dynamics.

Lemma 19
.

For
|v|>100t^{-1/2}
, we have that

\partial_{t}\alpha(v,t)=\mp\frac{i}{t}|\alpha(v,t)|^{2}\alpha(v,t)+R(v,t)

where

(80)

\begin{split}\lVert R(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\lesssim&t^{-5/4}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\\
\lVert R(v,t)\rVert_{L^{2}(R_{t})}\lesssim&t^{-3/2}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\end{split}

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha(v,t)=

\displaystyle\int\partial_{t}u(x,t)\overline{\Psi_{v}(x,t)}+u(x,t)\overline{\partial_{t}\Psi_{v}(x,t)}\;dx

(81a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mp i\int|u|^{2}u(x,t)\overline{\Psi_{v}(x,t)}\;dx

(81b)

\displaystyle+\int u(x,t)\overline{(\partial_{t}+i\Delta)\Psi_{v}(x,t)}\;dx

(81c)

\displaystyle+\int iVu(x,t)\overline{\Psi_{v}(x,t)}\;dx

We first consider the terms
eqs.

81b
and
81c
, which will be subsumed into the error term
R
, and then show how to extract the leading-order asymptotics from (
81a
). By the assumption that
|v|>100t^{-1/2}
together with the improved local decay estimate
Lemma

11
, we see that

|\eqref{eqn:dt-alpha-pot-err}|\lesssim\lVert\langle x\rangle^{-1}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\int|\langle x\rangle V(x)|\chi(t^{-1/2}(x-vt))\;dx\lesssim t^{-3/4}|vt|^{1-\gamma}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}

which is consistent with the decay we require for
R
. Turning to (
81b
) and using (
65
), we see that

\begin{split}\left|\eqref{eqn:dt-alpha-lin-err}\right|=&\left|\frac{1}{2t}\int\partial_{x}\left(e^{it\frac{x^{2}}{4}}u(x,t)\right)\left(t^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)-i(x-vt)\chi\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)\;dx\right|\\
=&\left|\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\int e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}J_{0}u(x,t)\left(t^{\frac{1}{2}}\chi^{\prime}\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)-i(x-vt)\chi\left(\frac{x-vt}{\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)\;dx\right|\\
\end{split}

Using
Theorem

16
and arguing as in (
76
), we see that

\begin{split}\left|\eqref{eqn:dt-alpha-lin-err}\right|\lesssim&t^{-5/4}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\end{split}

Moreover, noting that this expression is a convolution, we can apply Young’s inequality to find that

\left\lVert\eqref{eqn:dt-alpha-lin-err}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(R_{t})}\lesssim t^{-3/2}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)

Now, let us consider the nonlinear term (
81a
). We have

(82)

\begin{split}\eqref{eqn:dt-alpha-nonlin}=&\mp\frac{i}{t}\alpha(v,t)|\alpha(v,t)|^{2}\\
&\mp i\alpha(v,t)(|u(vt,t)|^{2}-\frac{1}{t}|\alpha(v,t)|^{2})\\
&\mp i\int(|u(x,t)|^{2}-|u(vt,t)|^{2})u(x,t)\overline{\Psi_{v}(x,t)}\;dx\\
=&\mp\frac{i}{t}\alpha(v,t)|\alpha(v,t)|^{2}+R_{1}+R_{2}\end{split}

so it is enough to show that the remainder terms obey the estimates (
80
). For
R_{1}
, a simple application of (
67
) and (
68
) shows that

\begin{split}\lVert R_{1}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\lesssim&\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\left(t^{-1/2}\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\right)\lVert|u(vt,t)|-t^{-1/2}|\alpha(v,t)|\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\\
\lesssim&M^{2}\epsilon^{2}t^{-5/4}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\lVert R_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(R_{t})}\lesssim&\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\left(t^{-1/2}\lVert\alpha\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\right)\lVert|u(vt,t)|-t^{-1/2}|\alpha(v,t)|\rVert_{L^{2}(R_{t})}\\
\lesssim&M^{2}\epsilon^{2}t^{-3/2}\left(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}\right)\end{split}

Which is consistent with (
80
) since
M\ll\epsilon^{-2/3}
. Turning to
R_{2}
, by using the fact that
|u|=|q|
, we see that

|R_{2}(v,t)|\lesssim M^{2}\epsilon^{2}t^{-1/2}\int|\chi(t^{1/2}z)||q((v-z)t,t)-q(vt,t)|t^{1/2}\;dz

so, applying the same arguments as in the proof of (
68
), we see that
R_{2}
satisfies (
80
). ∎

Now, we use
Lemma

19
to obtain precise asymptotics for
\alpha
.

Proposition 20
.

For
t\geq 1
, we have the estimate

(83)

\sup_{t\geq 1}\lVert\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(R_{t})}\lesssim\epsilon

Moreover, assuming that the bootstrap bounds (
47
) hold globally, the leading order asymptotics of
\alpha
for
(v,t)\in\{(v,t):t\geq 1,v\in\overline{R_{t}}\}
are given by

(84)

\alpha(v,t)=\exp\left(\mp i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\alpha(v,s)|^{2}\;ds\right)A(v)+\mathfrak{R}(v,t)

where

(85)

\lVert\mathfrak{R}(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}(R_{t})}\lesssim\epsilon t^{-1/4+\delta}

(86)

\lVert\mathfrak{R}(v,t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{v}(R_{t})}\lesssim\epsilon t^{-1/2+\delta}

Proof.

We begin by proving (
83
). Define

\beta(v,t)=\exp\left(\pm i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\alpha(v,s)|^{2}\;ds\right)\alpha(v,t)

It follows from
Lemma

19
that
\partial_{t}\beta(v,t)=R(v,t)
, where
R
obeys (
80
). Moreover, since
|\alpha|=|\beta|
, it suffices to prove that
\beta
is bounded for
v
in
R_{t}
. Define
t_{0}(v)=\max(1,100v^{-2})
, and observe that for
|v|>10
,
v\in R_{t_{0}(v)}
, while for
|v|\leq 10
,
v\in\partial R_{t_{0}(v)}
. We first show that
\beta(v,t_{0}(v))
is bounded pointwise in
v
, and then use
Lemma

19
to extend this bound to
t>t_{0}(v)
. If
|v|\geq 10
, then
t_{0}(v)=1
and

|\beta(v,t_{0}(v)|=|\alpha(v,1)|\lesssim\lVert u(x,1)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim\epsilon

where the first inequality is (
67
) and the second comes from using
Lemma

9
together with (
5
). If
|v|<10
, then the wave packet
\Psi_{v}(x,t_{0}(v))
defined in (
64
) is supported in
|x|<1000t_{0}^{1/2}(v)
with
\lVert\Psi_{v}(x,t_{0}(v))\rVert_{L^{1}}=\sqrt{t_{0}(v)}
. Thus, using
Lemma

12
, we see that

(87)

\begin{split}|\beta(v,t_{0}(v))|=&|\alpha(v,t_{0}(v))|\\
\lesssim&\sqrt{t_{0}(v)}\lVert u(x,t_{0}(v))\rVert_{L^{\infty}(|x|<1000t_{0}^{1/2}(v))}\\
\lesssim&\epsilon[t_{0}(v)]^{-1/4+\delta}\\
\ll&\epsilon\end{split}

It follows that (
83
) holds for
t=t_{0}(v)
. For
t>t_{0}(v)
, we note that

(88)

\begin{split}|\beta(v,t)-\beta(v,t_{0}(v))|\leq&\int_{t_{0}(v)}^{t}|\partial_{s}\beta(v,s)|\;ds\\
\lesssim&\int_{t_{0}(v)}^{t}O(t^{-5/4}(\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}))\;ds\\
\lesssim&\epsilon\end{split}

which completes the proof of (
83
).

Now, let us assume (
47
) and prove (
84
). By modifying (
88
), we see that
\beta(v,t)
is Cauchy as
t\to\infty
, so we can define

(89)

A(v)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta(v,t)

To prove (
85
) and (
86
), we note that

|\mathfrak{R}(v,t)|=|\beta(v,t)-A(v)|\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}|\partial_{s}\beta(v,s)|\;ds=\int_{t}^{\infty}|R(v,s)|\;ds

so the result follows from (
80
).
∎

6.
Proof of
Theorem

1

We are now in a position to prove
Theorem

1
:

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

Let us begin by assuming (
47
). By
Proposition

15
, we have that

\sup_{t\in[1,T]}t^{-\delta}\lVert J_{V}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim C\epsilon

On the other hand, by using
Lemma

18
and (
83
), we have that

\begin{split}\lVert u(x,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(|x|\geq 100t^{1/2})}=&\lVert u(vt,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}(R_{t})}\\
\leq&t^{-1/2}\lVert\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert u(vt,t)-t^{-1/2}e^{-i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}\alpha(v,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{v}(R_{t})}\\
\lesssim&\epsilon t^{-1/2}+\epsilon t^{-3/4+\delta}\end{split}

so (
48
) follows. A standard continuity argument shows that (
48
) holds for all time, which together with
Lemma

12
is sufficient to prove (
6
). To prove the asymptotics (
7
), we note that in the region
|x|>100t^{1/2}
,

u(x,t)=t^{-1/2}\exp\left(i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}\right)\alpha(x/t,t)+o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}\cap L^{2}}(1)

Replacing
\alpha
by the asymptotic equivalent derived in
Proposition

20
and expanding
\alpha
in terms of
\tilde{u}
using
Lemma

18
, we find that

\begin{split}u(x,t)=&t^{-1/2}\exp\left(i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}\mp i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\alpha(v,s)|^{2}\;ds\right)A(x/t,t)+o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}\cap L^{2}}(1)\\
=&t^{-1/2}\exp\left(i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}\mp i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\tilde{u}(-x/(2t),s)|^{2}\;ds\right)A(x/t,t)+o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}\cap L^{2}}(1)\end{split}

Taking
u_{\infty}=A
, this gives (
7
) for
|x|>100t^{1/2}
. To obtain the result for
|x|\leq 100t^{1/2}
, we first notice that for
|v|<100t^{-1/2}
,
t<t_{0}(v)
, so by (
87
) and (
85
)

(90)

\begin{split}|A(v)|=&|\alpha(v,t_{0}(v))|+|\mathfrak{R}(v,t_{0}(v))|\\
\lesssim&\epsilon(t_{0}(v)^{-1/4+\delta}+t_{0}(v)^{1-\gamma/2})\\
\lesssim&\epsilon t^{-1/4+\delta}\end{split}

where we have used the definition (
64
) on the second line and
Lemma

12
on the second to last line. In particular, for
|x|<100t^{1/2}
the leading order term has the same size as the error term:

t^{-1/2}\exp\left(i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}\mp i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\tilde{u}(-x/(2t),s)|^{2}\;ds\right)A(x/t,t)=o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}_{x}\cap L^{2}_{x}}(1)

Since in this region
u(x,t)=o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}_{x}\cap L^{2}_{x}}(1)
by

, we trivially have that

u(x,t)-t^{-1/2}\exp\left(i\frac{x^{2}}{4t}\mp i\int_{1}^{t}\frac{i}{s}|\tilde{u}(-x/(2t),s)|^{2}\;ds\right)A(x/t,t)=o_{t^{1/2}L^{\infty}_{x}\cap L^{2}_{x}}(1)

which completes the proof.
∎
