Right large deviation principle for the top eigenvalue of the sum or product of invariant random matrices
Pierre Mergny, Marc Potters

Abstract
In this note we study the right large deviation of the top eigenvalue (or singular value) of the sum or product of two random matrices 𝐀 \mathbf{A} and 𝐁 \mathbf{B} as their dimensions goes to infinity. The matrices 𝐀 \mathbf{A} and 𝐁 \mathbf{B} are each assumed to be taken from an invariant (or bi-invariant) ensemble with a confining potential with a possible wall beyond which no eigenvalues/singular values are allowed. The introduction of this wall puts different models in a very generic framework. In particular, the case where the wall is exactly at the right edge of the limiting spectral density is equivalent, from the point of view of large deviations, to considering a fixed diagonal matrices, as studied previously in Ref. [ 1 ] . We show that that the tilting method introduced in Ref. [ 1 ] can be extended to our general setting and is equivalent to the study of a spherical spin glass model specific to the operation - sum of symmetric matrices / product of symmetric matrices / sum of rectangular matrices - we are considering.

Therefore, a natural question in RMT and related fields is the following: given a random symmetric (respectively rectangular) matrix

\mathbf{A}

21
,
22
,
23
,
24

,

,

or looking at small-rank deformation of an invariant ensemble, see Refs.
[

,
32

In this paper, we obtain

Second, we extend the tilting method introduced in Ref.

We argue that when one is considering respectively the product of two symmetric matrices or the sum of two rectangular matrices, one should replace the additive spherical integral with respectively the multiplicative spherical integral and the rectangular spherical integral. 2
Reminder on right large deviation for one random matrix: The pulled Coulomb gas approach

Before considering the addition or multiplication of two random matrices, let’s first briefly recall the simpler case of
one
random matrix in an invariant ensemble. We first start with
symmetric
random matrices.

2.1
Classical Rotationally Invariant Ensemble

For an analytic confining potential
V(.)
, we say that a matrix is drawn from a (classical rotationally) invariant ensemble if the probability to observe the matrix
\mathbf{A}\equiv\mathbf{A}_{N}
in a region
R
in the space of
(N\times N)
symmetric matrices is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left[\mathbf{A}\in R\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,V}}\int_{R}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{N}{2}{\rm Tr}\,V(\mathbf{A})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}\,,

(1)

where
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}
is the Lebesgue measure over the space of
(N\times N)
symmetric matrices and
Z_{N,V}
is a constant ensuring that this probability is normalized to one. When considering positive semi-definite matrices, we will implicitly assume the potential to be defined on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For any orthogonal matrix
\mathbf{O}
we have
V(\mathbf{O}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{O}^{\mathsf{T}})=\mathbf{O}V(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}
which together with the cyclical property of the trace gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left[\mathbf{O}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}\in R\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{V}\left[\mathbf{A}\in R\right]

\displaystyle\quad\left(\forall\mathbf{O}\in\mathsf{O}(N)\right)\,,

(2)

where
\mathsf{O}(N)
is the group of
(N\times N)
orthogonal matrix; hence the name (rotationally) invariant ensemble.

Example (GOE matrices):
If one considers the elements
A_{ij}
of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
to be independent (up to the symmetry) Gaussian random variables with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}/N
for the off-diagonal elements and
2\sigma^{2}/N
for the diagonal elements, then this ensemble corresponds to the famous
Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble
(GOE) for which the potential is equal to:

\displaystyle V(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\,.

(3)

Example (Wishart matrices):
Consider a
(N\times M)
rectangular matrix
\mathbf{X}
with
M\geq N
, where the elements of
\mathbf{X}
are Gaussian independent random variables with mean zero and variance one, from which we construct the
(N\times N)
square matrix
\mathbf{A}:=\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}
. The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is a (Gaussian White)
Wishart
matrix. It is rotationally invariant with potential:

\displaystyle V_{N}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{N}\lambda+\left(1-\frac{M}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\right)\log\lambda\to V(x)=\frac{\lambda}{q}+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\log\lambda\,,

(4)

where the asymptotic behavior in Eq. (
4
) corresponds to the double scaling limit
N\to\infty
and
M\to\infty
with
\frac{N}{M}\to q\in(0,1)
. The invariant ensemble with the potential
V(.)
in Eq. (
4
) is sometimes known as the
Laguerre Orthogonal Ensemble
.

From there, it is a standard result of RMT that
\mathbf{A}
admits the following spectral decomposition
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{V}\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}),\dots,\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A}))\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where the matrix of eigenvectors
\mathbf{V}
is taken uniformly over
\mathsf{O}(N)
. To ease notation when it is needed, we write
\lambda_{i}\equiv\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A})
for the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. The joint density of the eigenvalues is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(\lambda_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij|i\neq j}\log|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|\right]\,.

(5)

The term
\sum_{ij|i\neq j}\log|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|
is the Jacobian of the change of variable
\mathbf{A}\to(\mathbf{V},\{\lambda_{i}\})
and is exactly the pairwise repulsive interaction of the
2d
-Coulomb gas. In the large
N
limit, the empirical (random) spectral density converges to a non-random smooth density
\mu_{A}(.)
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\lambda-\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}))\underset{N\to\infty}{\to}\mu_{A}(\lambda)\,.

(6)

where
\mu_{A}(.)
is the solution of the Tricomi problem:

\displaystyle\mathrm{P.V.}\int\frac{\mu_{A}(\lambda^{\prime})}{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{V^{\prime}(\lambda)}{2}\,,

(7)

where
\mathrm{P.V.}
stands for Principal Value.

Example (GOE matrices and the semi-circle distribution):
For GOE matrices with a quadratic potential given by Eq. (
3
), the solution of this Tricomi equation is given by the famous
semi-circle distribution
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{sc}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{4\sigma^{2}-\lambda^{2}}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{[-2\sigma,2\sigma]}\,.

(8)

where
\mathbb{I}_{[a,b]}
is the indicator function, it is equal to
1
if
\lambda\in[a,b]
and zero otherwise.

Example (Wishart matrices and the Marčenko-Pastur distribution):
For Wishart matrices with potential given by Eq. (
4
), one obtains the
Marčenko-Pastur distribution
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{MP}_{q}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{(\mathrm{a}_{+}-\lambda)(\lambda-\mathrm{a}_{-})}}{2\pi q\lambda}\mathbb{I}_{[\mathrm{a}_{-},\mathrm{a}_{+}]}\,.

(9)

where the edges are given by
\mathrm{a}_{\pm}=(1\pm\sqrt{q})^{2}
.

2.2
Invariant Ensemble with a wall

Importantly, Eq. (
5
) for the joint density of eigenvalues still makes sense if we introduce a
wall
at a position
w_{A}\geq\mathrm{a}_{+}
beyond which the potential is infinite. We say that a matrix is taken from an invariant ensemble with a wall at
w_{A}
, which we denote by
\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{P}_{V,w_{A}}
, if
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{O}\mathrm{Diag}\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\right)\mathbf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}
with
\mathbf{O}
uniform over
\mathsf{O}(N)
and the
\{\lambda_{i}\}
follow the joint law of Eq. (
5
) with
V(.)
a confining potential such that
V(x>w_{A})=\infty
. By construction, we still have the property (
2
) for random matrices taken from this ensemble. It is important to notice that the introduction of this wall does not change the limiting equilibrium density
\mu_{A}(.)
since
w_{A}\geq\mathrm{a}_{+}
and the solution of the Tricomi problem of Eq. (
7
) only depends on the values of the potential between the two edges
\mathrm{a}_{\pm}
. The introduction of these invariant ensembles with a wall might seem odd at first, but as we will see later on, this construction allows the study of the sum of two matrices where one (or both

\mathbf{O}
uniform over
\mathsf{O}(N)
, such that
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{O}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{a}_{1}&0&\dots&0\\
0&\ddots&&\vdots\\

0&\dots&0&\tilde{a}_{N}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(10)

The
\{\tilde{a}_{i}\}
are ’frozen’ sequences of numbers such that as we increase the size of the matrix, their empirical distribution converges to the same
\mu_{A}(.)
:

\displaystyle\mu_{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\lambda-\tilde{a}_{i})\underset{N\to\infty}{\to}\mu_{A}(\lambda)\,.

(11)

and importantly the minimum and the maximum of the
\{\tilde{a}_{i}\}
converge to the edges
\mathrm{a}_{-}
and
\mathrm{a}_{+}
of
\mu_{A}(.)
(that is, in the large
N
limit, no outlier survives). To be more concrete, a typical example for the choice of the
\{\tilde{a}_{i}\}
is to take them as the quantiles of the distribution
\mu_{A}(.)
, sometimes called the ”classical positions” of the particles. For a given
N
and for each
i
ranging from
1
to
N
, this means that
\tilde{a}_{i}
is solution of the integral equation:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{a}_{-}}^{\tilde{a}_{i}}\mu_{A}(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{N+1}\,,

(12)

and by construction their empirical distribution converge to
\mu_{A}(.)
. Another natural choice is to draw independently each
\tilde{a}_{i}
from the distribution
\mu_{A}(.)
. Now in general, diagonal matrices of the form of Eq. (
10
) are very different from random matrices taken from an invariant ensemble since their eigenvalues are fixed and don’t have a true repulsion as in Eq. (
5
). However, when one consider the problem of large deviation of the top eigenvalue, we will see that they behave as an invariant ensemble with a wall exactly at the edge (
w_{A}=\mathrm{a}_{+}
). This will be made more precise later on, but one can already see that if there is a wall exactly at the edge, then at finite
N
the top eigenvalue cannot fluctuate outside the support of the bulk density
\mu_{A}(.)
and hence it is in a sense fixed. This construction is superfluous when considering just one matrix (in this case the question of large deviation for the top eigenvalue of a deterministic matrix as in Eq. (
10
) is trivial), but turns out to be very convenient when considering the sum or product of two matrices.

One may note that we recover the case of classical invariant ensemble of Sec.
2.1
by sending
w_{A}\to\infty
.

2.3
Coulomb gas approach

Coming back to random symmetric matrices from an invariant ensemble, if we order the eigenvalues in decreasing order,
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{N}(\mathbf{A})
then we have:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle\underset{N\to\infty}{\to}\mathrm{a}_{+}\,.

(13)

This result is valid in the formal
N\to\infty
and a natural question is to estimate the probability at large but finite
N
,
\mathbb{P}\left[\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\simeq x\right]
, of having the top eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
at a position
x
different from its typical value
\mathrm{a}_{+}
. If the density
\mu_{A}(.)
is ’non-critical’, by which we mean that it behaves as a square-root near the edge
\mathrm{a}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{A}(x)

\displaystyle\underset{x\nearrow\mathrm{a}_{+}}{\sim}\frac{\gamma_{0}^{3/2}}{\pi}\sqrt{\mathrm{a}_{+}-x}\quad\,,

(14)

then the small deviations of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})
around the limiting value
\mathrm{a}_{+}
are given by the Tracy-Widom law for fluctuations of order
O(N^{-\frac{2}{3}})
, see Refs.
[
41
,
42
]
. For a value of
x
far from the edge
\mathrm{a}_{+}
, one is outside the scope of the Tracy-Widom regime describing typical fluctuations and one needs to estimate a very rare event dictated by a
large deviation principle
. This can be summarized (see Ref.
[
25
]
) by the following set of equations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\simeq x\right]

\displaystyle\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathrm{exp}\left[-N^{2}\Psi_{-}(x)+o(N^{2})\right]&\mbox{for }x<\mathrm{a}_{+}\mbox{ and }|x-\mathrm{a}_{+}|\sim O(1)\,,\\
\\
\gamma_{0}N^{2/3}(\mathcal{F}^{(1)})^{\prime}\left(\gamma_{0}N^{2/3}(x-\mathrm{a}_{+})\right)&\mbox{for }|x-\mathrm{a}_{+}|\sim O(N^{-\frac{2}{3}})\,,\\
\\
\mathrm{exp}\left[-N\Psi(x)+o(N)\right]&\mbox{for }x>\mathrm{a}_{+}\mbox{ and }|x-\mathrm{a}_{+}|\sim O(1)\,.\end{array}\right.

The function
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
in Eq. (
2.3
) is the
\beta=1
Tracy-Widom function. The scaling (or speed of convergence) of the large deviation principle is different if
x
is either above or below the edge
\mathrm{a}_{+}
, and can be naturally interpreted thanks to the
2d
-Coulomb gas picture.

2.3.1
The pulled Coulomb gas (
x>\mathrm{a}_{+}
)

Let’s consider the case
x>\mathrm{a}_{+}
which is the main subject of this note. Integrating the joint density in Eq. (
5
), one has that computing the (logarithm of the) probability
\mathbb{P}[\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\simeq x]
is equivalent to computing the difference of energy between the configuration of a
2d
-Coulomb gas where the top particle is
pulled
at the position
x
and the configuration of the unperturbed
2d
-Coulomb gas. For the perturbed gas, we are just moving one particle away from the bulk, and thus we expect that this perturbation does not change the equilibrium density
\mu_{A}
of the
N-1
other particles inside the bulk. This induces the scaling in Eq. (
2.3
) for
x>\mathrm{a}_{+}
, and the right rate function is given by:

\displaystyle\Psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[V(x)-V(\mathrm{a}_{+})-2\int\log(x-\lambda)\mu_{A}(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda+2\int\log(\mathrm{a}_{+}-\lambda)\mu_{A}(y)\mathrm{d}\lambda\right]\,,

(20)

which can be written in integral form as:

\displaystyle\Psi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{a}_{+}}^{x}\left(\frac{V^{\prime}(t)}{2}-g_{A}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t\,,

(21)

where
g_{A}(.)
is the
Stieltjes transform
of
\mu_{A}
:

\displaystyle g_{A}(z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathrm{a}_{-}}^{\mathrm{a}_{+}}\frac{\mu_{A}(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,.

(22)

It will be convenient to introduce the
second branch of the Stieltjes transform
, (see App.
A.1
), which, in the case of invariant ensemble, is given for
x>\mathrm{a}_{+}
by:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{A}(x)

\displaystyle:=V^{\prime}(x)-g_{A}(x)\,,

(23)

so that from Eq. (
21
) we can interpret the rate function as (half) the area between the two branches of the Stieltjes transform up to the position
x
:

\displaystyle\Psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathrm{a}_{+}}^{x}\left(\bar{g}_{A}(t)-g_{A}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t\,.

(24)

Note that for
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})=\infty
, the rate function is finite.

Remark (Recovering the potential):
From the eigenvalue density
\mu_{A}(\lambda)
and the rate function
\Psi(x)
, one can recover the potential
V(x)
(up to an arbitrary constant) over the whole range of possible eigenvalues. Indeed, from the density one can compute the Stieltjes transform using Eq. (
22
) and rewrite Eqs. (
7
) and (
21
) as
2
2

2

The potential for
x<\mathrm{a}_{-}
can be recovered similarly using the rate function for
-A
.

\displaystyle\frac{V^{\prime}(x)}{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathfrak{Re}\,g_{A}(x-\mathrm{i}0^{+})&\mbox{for }\mathrm{a}_{-}\leq x\leq\mathrm{a}_{+}\\
\\
\Psi^{\prime}(x)+g_{A}(x)&\mbox{for }x\geq\mathrm{a}_{+}\,.\\
\end{array}\right.

For random matrices that are not necessarily drawn from an invariant ensemble, we can use this formula to define an effective potential, i.e. the potential of the invariant ensemble that has the same density and rate functions. In particular, for a fixed diagonal matrix with eigenvalue density
\mu_{A}(x)
the rate function is infinite, and the effective potential is infinite beyond
w=\mathrm{a}_{+}
exactly as described in Sec.
2.2
.

2.4.1
Singular value decomposition

Let
\mathbf{A}\equiv\mathbf{A}_{N,M}
be a
(N\times M)
real matrix. Without loss of generality, we consider
M\geq N
. We will be interested in the double scaling limit where
N\to\infty
and
M\to\infty
but the ratio stays finite:
3
3

3

In the rest of this section and when we are considering rectangular matrices, all limits are assumed to be taken under this setting.

\displaystyle\frac{N}{M}

\displaystyle\to q\in(0,1)\,.

(40)

The singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{A}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\,,

(41)

where
\mathbf{U}_{1}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}_{1}
) is a
(N\times N)
(resp.
(M\times M)
) orthogonal matrix,
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a diagonal rectangular matrix:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}s_{1}(\mathbf{A})&0&0\\
0&\ddots&0&\mathbf{0}_{M-N}\\
0&0&s_{N}(\mathbf{A})\end{pmatrix}\,,

(42)

and the
s_{i}(\mathbf{A})
are the
singular values
of
\mathbf{A}
. They are related to the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}
by the relation:

\displaystyle s_{i}(\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}})}\,.

(43)

2.4.2
Bi-invariant ensemble

We say that a rectangular matrix is taken from a bi-invariant ensemble if its law can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left[\mathbf{A}\in R\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,V}}\int_{R}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{N}{2}{\rm Tr}\,V(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{A}\,,

(44)

for an analytic potential
V(.)
, since it satisfies the property:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left[\mathbf{U}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}\in R\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{V}\left[\mathbf{A}\in R\right]\,,

(45)

for any
\mathbf{U}\in\mathsf{O}(N)
and any
\mathbf{V}\in\mathsf{O}(M)
. The change of variable
\mathbf{A}\to\left(\mathbf{U},\{s_{i}\equiv s_{i}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\},\mathbf{V}\right)
introduces a Jacobian
[
43
]
which is equal to:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Jac}\left[\mathbf{A}\to\left(\mathbf{U},\{s_{i}\},\mathbf{V}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j|i\neq j}\log|s_{i}^{2}-s_{j}^{2}|}\prod_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}^{M-N}\,.

(46)

In this case, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
admits the SVD of Eq. (
41
) where
\mathbf{U}_{1}
(resp.
\mathbf{V}_{1}
) is taken uniformly over
\mathsf{O}(N)
(resp. over
\mathsf{O}(M)
) and the singular values
s_{i}
admit the following joint density:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(V(s_{i}^{2})+\left(1-\frac{M}{N}\right)\log(s_{i}^{2})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j|i\neq j}\log|s_{i}^{2}-s_{j}^{2}|\right]\,.

(47)

If we introduce the modified potential

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{q}(x)

\displaystyle:=V(x)+\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\log x\,,

(48)

with
q=N/M
, this can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}(s_{1},\dots,s_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{N}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{V}_{q}(s_{i}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j|i\neq j}\log|s_{i}^{2}-s_{j}^{2}|\right]\,.

(49)

Note that if we do the change of variable
\{s_{i}(\mathbf{A})\}\to\{\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\}
given by Eq. (
45
) in the joint law of Eq. (
49
), we have that the matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}
is taken from an invariant ensemble with the modified potential
\tilde{V}_{q}(.)
(plus a vanishing term coming from the change of variable).
4
4

4

Taking into account the Jacobian amounts to do the change
\tilde{V}_{q}(x)\to\tilde{V}_{q}(x)+\frac{1}{N}\log x
. In particular, this vanishing term prevents the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\mathsf{T}}
to be negative for
q=1
where the coefficient in front of the logarithm in Eq. (
48
) is null in this case. For
q=1
, one has to consider
\tilde{V}_{1}(.)
as being infinite for negative values.
As a consequence, in the large double scaling limit of Eq. (
40
), the empirical singular value distribution converges to a smooth limit:

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathbf{A}}(s)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(s-s_{i}(\mathbf{A}))\to\rho_{A}(s)\,,

where the limiting singular value distribution (LSVD)
\rho_{A}
is given as:

\displaystyle\rho_{A}(s)

\displaystyle=2s\mu_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(s^{2})\,,

(50)

where
\mu_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}
is solution of the Tricomi problem of Eq. (
7
) with the potential
V(.)
replaced by
\tilde{V}_{q}(.)
of Eq. (
48
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{P.V.}\int\frac{\mu_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(\lambda^{\prime})}{\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\lambda^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{q}(\lambda)}{2}\,.

(51)

The edges
\mathrm{a}_{\pm}
of the LSVD
\rho_{A}
are the square root of the edges of the distribution
\mu_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}
. Equivalently, the LSVD
\rho_{A}
is the solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{P.V.}\int\frac{\rho_{A}(s^{\prime})}{s^{2}-{s^{\prime}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}_{q}(s^{2})}{2}\,,

(52)

which can be directly seen from the joint law of Eq (
49
).

Example (Gaussian rectangular random matrices and Ginibre matrices):
Let’s consider a

matrix

with Gaussian i.i.d entries with mean zero and variance

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{4\sigma^{2}-s^{2}}}{\pi\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{[0,2\sigma]}\,.

3
The tilting method

3.1
Notation

In the previous section, we have reviewed the results concerning the right large deviation of the top eigenvalue (or singular value) of
one
random matrix. The goal of this section is to describe the general framework to tackle the case of the large deviation of the largest eigenvalue (resp. singular value) of a matrix
\mathbf{C}
given as the (free) sum or product of two symmetric (resp. rectangular) random matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
. In the large
N
limit, the limiting density
\mu_{C}
(resp.
\rho_{C}
) of eigenvalues (resp. singular values) of the matrix
\mathbf{C}
is given by
free probability theory
and depends precisely on the operation (sum of symmetric matrices, product of symmetric matrices or sum of rectangular matrices
6
6

6

As we will see, the case of the product of two rectangular matrices can be deduced from the symmetric case.
) one is considering. This will be discussed in each dedicated section, see Secs.
4
, Sec.
5
and Sec.
6
respectively, but we argue that the strategy to get the right large deviation is the same. To put everything in the same framework we denote by

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}\left(\mathbf{C}\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{C})&\mbox{if }\mathbf{C}\mbox{ is the sum/product of symmetric matrices}\,,\\
\\
s_{i}(\mathbf{C})&\mbox{if }\mathbf{C}\mbox{ is the sum of rectangular matrices}\,.\end{array}\right.

In any case, if we denote by
\mathrm{c}_{+}
the edge of the spectrum of
\mu_{C}
(resp.
\rho_{C}
), we have:

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}\left(\mathbf{C}\right)

\displaystyle\underset{N\to\infty}{\to}\mathrm{c}_{+}\,,

(73)

and similar to the one-random matrix model, the natural question is to estimate the probability
\mathbb{P}\left[\zeta_{1}\left(\mathbf{C}\right)\simeq x\right]
at large but finite
N
. Since one recovers the original setting by taking the limit
\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{0}
(the null matrix) in the additive case or
\mathbf{B}\to\mathbf{I}
(the identity matrix) in the multiplicative case, one should expect again to have the same different scaling as before for
x>\mathrm{c}_{+}
. As before, to tackle both the symmetric and rectangular cases at the same time, let’s denote by:

\displaystyle\Pi_{C}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Psi_{C}(x)&\mbox{if }\mathbf{C}\mbox{ is the sum/product of symmetric matrices}\,,\\
\\
\Phi_{C}(x)&\mbox{if }\mathbf{C}\mbox{ is the sum of rectangular matrices}\,,\end{array}\right.

such that
\Pi_{C}
is the rate function we want to compute:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{C})\simeq x\right]

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{exp}\left[-N\Pi_{C}(x)+o(N)\right]\quad\quad\text{for}\,x>\mathrm{c}_{+}\,,

(77)

from the knowledge of the laws of the matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
.

3.2
Idea of the tilting method and general expression for the rate function

The starting point for the derivation of the large deviation of
one
random matrix is the joint law density of the eigenvalues/singular values
\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{C})
, from which we can use the Coulomb gas approach. For the sum or the product of matrices, one does not have a simple expression for the joint density
\mathcal{P}_{N}\left(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N}\right)
of the eigenvalues/singular values, except in some specific cases, see Ref.
[
44
]
. Instead of directly looking at the matrix
\mathbf{C}
, the idea introduced in Ref.
[
1
]
in the context of RMT, is to look at a
weighted
realization of this matrix. If we denote by
\mathcal{P}(.)
the probability density of the random matrix
\mathbf{C}
in the space of symmetric/rectangular matrix, let’s consider
another
random matrix
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
whose probability density is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(\theta)}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}}(\theta)\right]}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})\,.

(78)

where
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)\equiv\mathcal{Z}(\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}),\dots,\zeta_{N}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}),\theta)
is a function whose precise expression will be given later on. For now, let us note that this function depends on
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
only through its eigenvalues/singular values in addition to the free parameter
\theta
. The expectation in the denominator of the RHS of Eq. (
78
) is an average over
\mathbf{C}\sim\mathcal{P}(.)
(and hence an average over both
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
):
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}}(\theta)\right]:=\int\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}}(\theta)\,\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{C})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{C}
such that
\mathcal{P}^{(\theta)}(.)
is well normalized. Because
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)
only depends on the
\zeta_{i}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})
we can relate the joint density
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{(\theta)}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N})
of the eigenvalues/singular values of
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
to the (unknown) joint density
\mathcal{P}_{N}\left(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N}\right)
of eigenvalues/singular values of
\mathbf{C}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}^{(\theta)}(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}}(\theta)\right]}\mathcal{P}_{N}\left(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N}\right)\,.

(79)

by integrating Eq. (
79
) we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob.}\left[\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})\simeq x\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)\right]}\int\delta(\zeta_{1}-x)\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)\mathcal{P}_{N}\left(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{N}\right)\mathrm{d}\zeta_{1}\dots\mathrm{d}\zeta_{N}\,.

(80)

Now if we
choose

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}^{\prime}}(\theta)
such that for large
N
this function explicitly depend on the position of the largest eigenvalue/singular value
\zeta_{1}\equiv\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})
but is self-averaging with respect to the other
\zeta_{2},\dots,\zeta_{N}
and thus independent of them, then the integral over these variables is nothing else than the probability of Eq. (
77
) we want to estimate. At large
N
the density of eigenvalues (or singular values) of
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
is the same as
\mathbf{C}
, but the position of top eigenvalue/singular value might different. In other words, we have:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob.}\left[\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})\simeq x\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{N\left[J_{C}(x,\theta)-F_{C}(\theta)-\Pi_{C}(x)\right]+o(N)}\,.

(81)

where we have introduced the
quenched free energy
as the large
N
limit

\displaystyle J_{C}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle:\approx\frac{1}{N}\log\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}|\{\zeta_{1}=x\}}(\theta)\,,

(82)

where
\{\zeta_{1}=x\}
indicates that the limit is taken with the constraint that the top eigenvalue (or singular value) is fixed at
x
; and similarly the
annealed free energy
as:

\displaystyle F_{C}(\theta)

\displaystyle:\approx\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}}(\theta)\,.

(83)

Given a value of the free parameter
\theta
, what is the typical value
x^{*}(\theta)
of the largest eigenvalue (or singular value) of the matrix
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
as
N
goes to infinity? By Jensen inequality, the function in the bracket of the RHS of Eq. (
80

x^{*}(\theta)
corresponds to the case where this function is exactly zero, namely
x^{*}(\theta)
is given as the solution of:

\displaystyle\Pi_{C}(x^{*}(\theta))-J_{C}(x^{*}(\theta),\theta)-F_{C}(\theta)

\displaystyle=0\,.

(84)

Now the idea of the tilting method is to look at Eq. (
84
) the other way: fix any
x>\mathrm{c}_{+}
, can we vary the free parameter
\theta
until it reaches a value
\theta^{*}\equiv\theta^{*}(x)
such that now the event
\{\zeta_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})\simeq x\}
becomes typical? If so, we can relate the unknown rate function
\Pi_{C}
to the annealed and quenched free energies:

\displaystyle\Pi_{C}(x)

\displaystyle=J_{C}(x,\theta^{*}(x))-F_{C}(\theta^{*}(x))\,.

(85)

Since the LHS of (
80
) is a probability measure, it is always bounded by one, so this optimal
\theta^{*}
- if it exists - is given by:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\underset{\theta>0}{\mathrm{argsup}}\{I_{x}(\theta):=J_{C}(x,\theta)-F_{C}(\theta)\}\,.

(86)

In particular, if the supremum is a maximum, one has that
\theta^{*}
is solution of:

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{x}(\theta^{*}):=\partial_{\theta}J_{C}(x,\theta)|_{\theta^{*}}-F^{\prime}_{C}(\theta^{*})

\displaystyle=0\,.

(87)

Next since we have Eq. (
73
), by construction
\Pi_{C}(\mathrm{c}_{+})=0
. One can therefore take the derivative of Eq. (
85
) with respect to
x
to get the following integral representation for the rate function:

\displaystyle\Pi_{C}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{c}_{+}}^{x}\partial_{t}J_{C}(t,\theta^{*}(t))+\theta^{*\prime}(t)(\partial_{\theta}J_{C}(t,\theta)|_{\theta^{*}}-F^{\prime}(\theta^{*}))\mathrm{d}t\,,

(88)

but by Eq. (
87
) the second term is null, so that we have the following simple formula:

\displaystyle\Pi_{C}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{c}_{+}}^{x}\partial_{t}J_{C}(t,\theta^{*}(t))\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\mbox{with $\theta^{*}$ solution of Eq.\ \eqref{eq:Theta_opt.2} }\,.

(89)

Remark (Spherical function as partition function of spherical spin glass model):
We have used the notations of statistical physics to denote the quantities of Eq. (

Remark (the case of diagonal matrices and wall at the edge):

4.2
The additive spherical integral and the SSK model

Based on what we have discussed in Sec.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{O}(N)}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}_{1}+\mathbf{O}\mathbf{M}_{2}\mathbf{O}^{\mathsf{T}}}(\theta)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{O}

\displaystyle\,.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{SSK}}(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(x-g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(\theta)\right)

\displaystyle(\mbox{for }\theta>g_{A}(x))\,.

\theta=g_{A}(\mathrm{a}+)
and then it is increasing until it reaches the (possible infinite) value,
\theta=\mathrm{r}_{A}=\bar{g}_{A}(w_{A}\to\infty)
where it goes to infinity. Conversely, the function
I_{x}^{\prime}(\theta)
of Eq. (
129
), seen as function of
\theta
for

\theta=g_{A}(x)
and then is increasing until it reaches the point
\theta=g_{A}(\mathrm{a}+)
and then decreasing again, and goes to

as
\theta\to\mathrm{r}_{A}
. Thus, as one varies
\theta

, this function is positive and then negative and only crosses the real axis once. As a consequence, the supremum in Eq. (

is given at the unique point where the function
I_{x}^{\prime}(\theta)
of Eq. (
129
) crosses the real axis in the region
\theta>g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
. In other words, finding
\theta^{*}
amounts to solve the equation:

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(\theta^{*}(x))

\displaystyle\mbox{for }\theta^{*}(x)>g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\,,

(130)

which is nothing else than the definition of the second branch of the Stieltjes transform, that is we have:

\displaystyle\theta^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{A}(x)\,.

(131)

If we now use the integral representation Eq. (
89
) of the rate function, with the expression of Eq. (
4.3
) for the partial derivative of the quenched free energy, together with Eq. (

5.2
The multiplicative spherical integral and the LSSK model

For the multiplicative case, we argue that a good candidate for the tilt is the

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=\langle\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N}{2}\theta\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{LSSK}}(\bm{\sigma})}\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{LSSK}}(\bm{\sigma})

\displaystyle:=\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{M})\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)\,.

Since
\mathbf{M}
is the symmetric product of definite positive matrices, its eigenvalues are positive, so this Hamiltonian is well defined. In this paper, we are interested in the case

[

39
]
and are given in the following section. Due to its similarity with the original SSK model, one should expect to have a similar behavior, with a paramagnetic phase at high temperature and a spin glass phase at low temperature.

\mathbf{C}
conditioned to have its largest singular value at the position

\theta

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}J_{C}(x,\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(q)}_{C}(\theta)&\mbox{for }\theta\leq d_{C}(x)\,,\\
\\
\frac{U(\theta x)}{\theta}&\mbox{for }\theta\geq d_{C}(x)\,.\end{array}\right.

Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to

\displaystyle\partial_{x}J_{C}(x,\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{for }\theta\leq d_{C}(x)\,,\\
\\
\frac{\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}+4q\theta^{2}x^{2}}-\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}+4qd_{A}(x)^{2}x^{2}}}{2qx}&\mbox{for }\theta\geq t_{C}(x)\,.\end{array}\right.

The derivative of the annealed free energy
F_{C}
is the sum of the derivative of the annealed free energy
F_{A}
and
F_{B}
which are given by:

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}F_{A}(w_{A},\theta)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{A}^{(q)}(\theta)&\mbox{for }\theta\leq\bar{d}_{A}(w_{A})\,,\\
\\
\frac{U(\theta w_{A})}{\theta}&\mbox{for }\theta\geq\bar{d}_{A}(w_{A})\,,\end{array}\right.

and similarly for
F_{B}
. If the wall is at the edge
w_{A}=\mathrm{a}_{+}
, since

, we have Eq. (

1.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we have derived the right large deviation function for:

1.

the top eigenvalue of the sum of two
arbitrary
symmetric matrices;

2.

the top eigenvalue of the product of two
arbitrary
symmetric matrices;

3.

the top singular of the sum of two
arbitrary
rectangular matrices;

where by arbitrary we mean that we can take the matrices to be either taken from a rotationally invariant ensemble (resp. a bi-invariant for the case of rectangular random matrices) or to be a randomly rotated fixed diagonal matrix (resp. a fixed diagonal rectangular matrices). The results rely on a direct link with spherical spin models and are summarized in Sec.
4.6
for the case of the sum of symmetric matrices, in Sec.
5.5
for the case of the product of symmetric matrices and in Sec.
6.5
for the case of the sum of rectangular matrices. In each case, we find that the rate function has up to three different regimes, and we give an interpretation of the behavior in each regime. Let us mention right away that this construction directly extends to complex matrices: for unitary invariant random matrices (resp. complex rectangular matrices), it is known that the derivatives of the quenched and annealed free energies are twice the ones of the real case and so does the rate functions. A natural question is to extend the above construction to tackle the ’left’ large deviation, for which the speed of convergence of the large deviation is
N^{2}
. We leave this problem for future research.

Appendix A
Properties of transforms of RMT and Free Probability

A.1
The Stieltjes transform and its inverse

For a random matrix
\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{P}_{V}
taken from an invariant ensemble with empirical spectral distribution
\mu_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta(\lambda-\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{A))}\to\mu_{A}
, we recall that its Stieltjes transform is given by:

\displaystyle g_{\mathbf{A}}(z)

\displaystyle:={\rm Tr}\,(z-\mathbf{A})^{-1}=\int\frac{\mu_{\mathbf{A}}(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\to g_{A}(z):=\int_{\mathrm{a}_{-}}^{\mathrm{a}_{+}}\frac{\mu_{A}(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,.

(301)

Conversely, one obtains the density
\mu_{A}(.)
from the knowledge of the Stieltjes transform thanks to the so-called Sokhotski-Plemelj inversion formula:

\displaystyle\mu_{A}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathfrak{Im}\,g_{A}(\lambda-\mathrm{i}0^{+})\,.

(302)

It is clear from the expression (
301
) of the Stieltjes transform that for real
z>\mathrm{a}_{+}
,
g_{A}(.)
is a continuous decreasing function, with asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle g_{A}(z)

\displaystyle\underset{z\to\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{z}\,.

(303)

Since this function is decreasing its admits an inverse
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}
defined on
\left(0,g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\right)
which is also decreasing. Now, it turns out that one can generally extend analytically this function for values outside this interval, which we also denote by
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}
. If one can do so, necessarily the inverse function cannot continue to be decreasing, otherwise we can invert it again to obtain a real function, which would be in contradiction with the inversion formula (
302
). Note that this in turn gives us a way to find the top edge of the distribution:

•

if
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})<\infty
, then
\mathrm{a}_{+}=g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y^{*})
with
y^{*}
such that
(g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle})^{\prime}(y^{*})=0
;

•

otherwise,
\mathrm{a}_{+}=\lim_{x\to\infty}g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(x)
.

Now, in the case of invariant ensemble, the analytical continuation of the inverse of the Stieltjes admits a natural interpretation. Without loss of generality, let’s consider the case
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})<\infty
, since one can immediately see that there is no such analytical continuation of the inverse in the case
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})=\infty
. For
V(.)
an analytic potential on the real line, convex after the top edge
\mathrm{a}_{+}
, and
z
a complex variable outside the support of the distribution
\mu_{A}
, the corresponding Stieltjes transform
g_{A}\equiv g_{A}(z)
is known to satisfy the following algebraic equation:

\displaystyle g_{A}^{2}-V^{\prime}(z)g_{A}+P(z)

\displaystyle=0\,,

(304)

where the function
P
is defined as:

\displaystyle P(z)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{V^{\prime}(z)-V^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})}{z-\lambda_{i}}=\int_{\mathrm{a}_{-}}^{\mathrm{a}_{+}}\frac{V^{\prime}(z)-V^{\prime}(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\mu_{A}(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda\,.

(305)

Eq. (
304
) is of second order in
g_{A}
and therefore admits two solutions. Using the asymptotic behavior of Eq. (
303
) for the Stieltjes transform, one gets that the correct solution is given by the so-called Brézin-Itzykson-Parisi-Zuber (BIPZ) formula
[
63
]
:

\displaystyle g_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{{V^{\prime}}^{2}(z)-4P(z)}}{2}\,.

(306)

Interestingly, one can naturally look at the other non-physical solution of Eq. (
304
) given by:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{V^{\prime}(z)}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{{V^{\prime}}^{2}(z)-4P(z)}}{2}\,,

(307)

which is known as the
second branch of the Stieltjes transform
. For
z\geq\mathrm{a}_{+}
, this function starts at
\bar{g}_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})=g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
and is then continuously increasing with asymptotic behavior given by:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{A}(z)\underset{z\to\infty}{\sim}V^{\prime}(z)\,.

(308)

Note that this gives Eq. (
23
) in the main text. If now look at the algebraic equation (
304
) the other way by fixing the value of
g_{A}=y
for some
y
in
(0,g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}))
, the corresponding
z(y)\equiv z
is by definition the inverse
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)
. If now the parameter
y
is higher than
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
(but lower than
\mathrm{r}_{A}:=\lim_{z\to\infty}V^{\prime}(z)
), Eq. (
304
) is the implicit equation for the analytical continuation of
g^{\langle-1\rangle}
. Since this regime corresponds to the second branch of Stieltjes, we have a natural interpretation for the analytical continuation of
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}
beyond the point
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
: it is the inverse function of the second branch of the Stieltjes transform. Now, for a general smooth density
\mu_{A}
(not necessarily arising from the eigenvalue distribution of an invariant ensemble), we define the second branch of its Stieltjes as the inverse of the
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}
beyond
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
. We have represented the function
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}
associated to semi-circle distribution for
\sigma=1
in Fig.
8
.

Figure 8:
Inverse of the Stieltjes transform of the semi-circle distribution with
\sigma=1
. For
y\leq g_{\mathrm{sc}}(2\sigma)=1
, this function (in cyan) in the inverse of the Stieltjes transform while for

, this function (in blue) is the inverse of the second branch of the Stieltjes.

Similarly to the Stieltjes transform, one has the same behavior for the T-transform of Eq. (
178
) and D-transform of Eq. (

). In particular, the analytical continuation of the inverse of the T-transform
t_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(.)
(resp. D-transform
d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(.)
) beyond the point
t_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
(resp.
d_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
) corresponds respectively to invert the second branch of the T-transform
\bar{t}_{A}(.)
satisfying:

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=z\bar{g}_{A}(z)-1\,,

(309)

and to invert the second branch of the D-transform satisfying:

\displaystyle\bar{d}_{A}(z)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{qz^{2}\left(\bar{g}_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(z^{2})\right)^{2}+(1-q)\bar{g}_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(z^{2})}\,.

(310)

A.2
monotonic behavior of the linearizing transforms

In this section, we show that the R-transform, S-transform and C-transform are monotonously increasing.

A.2.1
for the R-transform

For any
y\in\left(0,\mathrm{r}_{A}\right)
, we recall that the R-transform is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{A}(y)

\displaystyle:=g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)-\frac{1}{y}\,,

(311)

where
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)
is the inverse of
g_{A}
on
(0,g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}))
and is the inverse of
\bar{g}_{A}
on
(g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}),\mathrm{r}_{A})

A.1

•

For

•

For
y\in(0,g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}))
, let’s take a look at the derivative of the R-transform:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{A}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g_{A}^{\prime}\left(g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)}+\frac{1}{y^{2}}\,,

(312)

but for any
z>\mathrm{a}_{+}
, we have:

\displaystyle-g_{A}^{\prime}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{1}{z-\lambda}\right)^{2}\mu_{A}(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda\geq\left(\int\frac{\mu_{A}(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)^{2}=g_{A}^{2}(z)\,.

(313)

If now one applies this inequality for
z=g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)
with
y\in(0,g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}))
, the RHS of Eq. (
313
) is simply given by
y^{2}
since
g_{A}(g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y))=y
and so:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{A}^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle\geq 0\,.

(314)

such that the R-transform is indeed monotonously increasing. If
\mu_{A}
is not a Dirac mass, then the above inequalities are strict, such that one can indeed take the inverse of the R-transform.

A.2.2
for the S-transform

For any
y\in\left(0,\mathrm{s}_{A}\right)
, we recall that the (modified) S-transform is given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{A}(y)

\displaystyle:=t_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\frac{y}{y+1}\,.

(315)

•

For
y\in\left(t_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}),\mathrm{s}_{A}\right)
, The (modified) S-transform is a continuous increasing function as the product of positive and increasing functions.

•

For
y\in\left(0,t_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\right)
, the derivative of the S-transform is given by

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{A}\right)^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(y+1)^{2}t_{A}^{\prime}\left(t_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)}\left(t_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\,t_{A}^{\prime}\left(t_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)+y(y+1)\right)\,.

(316)

Since the derivative of the T-transform is given by

\displaystyle t_{A}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathrm{a}_{-}}^{\mathrm{a}_{+}}\frac{\lambda}{\left(z-\lambda\right)^{2}}\mu_{A}(\lambda)\mathrm{d}\lambda\leq 0\,,

(317)

the term in front of the parenthesis is always non-positive. Next expressing the Stieltjes transform in terms of the T-transform in Eq. (
313
), thanks to the definition of the T-transform of Eq. (
178
), we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle-zt_{A}^{\prime}(z)\geq t_{A}(z)\left(t_{A}(z)+1\right)\,,

(318)

which for
z=t_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)
gives from Eq. (
316
) that the derivative of the S-transform is always positive:

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{A}\right)^{\prime}(y)\geq 0\,.

(319)

As a consequence, the S-transform is always monotonously increasing. For a non-degenerate (that is different from a Dirac mass) distribution
\mu_{A}
, one can again check that the inequalities are strict such that the S-transform is strictly continuously increasing and hence admits a well-defined inverse.

A.2.3
for the rectangular C-transform

We recall that the rectangular C-transform is given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(q)}_{A}(y)

\displaystyle:=\frac{U\left(y\,d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)}{y}=:W(y,d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y))\,,

(320)

with

\displaystyle W(y,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\frac{U(y\,z^{\prime})}{y}\,.

(321)

and the function
U
is given by Eq. (
225
).

•

For
y\in\left(d_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}),\mathrm{c}_{A}:=\lim_{w_{A}\to\infty}\bar{d}_{A}(w_{A})\right)
and
z^{\prime}>\mathrm{a}_{+}
, one can check that the function
W(y,z^{\prime})
is increasing with each variable while the other one is fixed (that is,
z^{\prime}\mapsto W_{y}(z^{\prime}):=W(y,z^{\prime})
and
y\mapsto W_{z^{\prime}}(y):=W(y,z^{\prime})
are continuously increasing). Furthermore, for
y\in\left(d_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+}),\mathrm{c}_{A}\right)
,
d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)
is also (continuously) increasing, thus the rectangular C-transform is continuously increasing as the composition of continuously increasing functions.

•

For
y\in\left(0,d_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\right)
, the derivative of the rectangular C-transform is given by:

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\mathcal{C}}^{(q)}_{A}\right)^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+q)}{2qy^{2}}+\frac{\left[\frac{4\,q\,y\,d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)}{d_{A}^{\prime}\left(d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)}-(1-q)^{2}-4q\left(d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)^{2}\right]}{2qy^{2}\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}+4q\left(d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)\right)^{2}}}\,.

(322)

Next for
z>\mathrm{a}_{+}>0
, we can rewrite Eq. (
313
) as:

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\left(g_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(z^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2zg_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(z^{2})\,.

(323)

From the definition of Eq. (
227
) of the D-transform, we can express
g_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}
in terms of the D-transform:

\displaystyle g_{AA^{\mathsf{T}}}(z^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1+q+\sqrt{(1-q)^{2}+4qz^{2}d_{A}(z)}}{2qz^{2}}

\displaystyle\,.

(324)

Injecting this into the inequality of Eq. (
323
) and then setting
z=d_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(y)
leads hopefully after several simplifications to the positivity of the term in bracket of Eq. (
322
). Since all the other terms are positive, the rectangular C-transform is a (continuously) increasing function.

A.3
Inequalities at the edge of the free convolutions

The goal of this section is to prove the set of inequalities given by the Eqs. (
102
) (
181
) (
241
). The proof of this statement in the additive case is also given in Ref.
[
1
]
. The idea is based on
subordination relations
:

•

For the case of the sum of symmetric matrices of Sec.
4
, let’s assume without any loss of generality
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\leq g_{B}(\mathrm{b}_{+})
. Using the definition of Eq. (
100
) of the R-transform, we can write the linearizing property of Eq. (
99
) as an implicit equation for
g_{C}
. For any
z\geq\mathrm{c}_{+}
, we have:

\displaystyle g_{C}(z)

\displaystyle=g_{A}\left(z-R_{B}\left(g_{C}(z)\right)\right)\,.

(325)

Since
z\geq\mathrm{c}_{+}
,
g_{C}(z)\in\mathbb{R}
and so for the RHS of Eq. (
325
) to be real, we must have
z-R_{B}\left(g_{C}(z)\right)\geq\mathrm{a}_{+}
. By decreasing property of
g_{A}
, this implies:

\displaystyle g_{C}(z)

\displaystyle\leq g_{A}\left(\mathrm{a}_{+}\right)\,,

(326)

and if we now set
z=\mathrm{c}_{+}+\epsilon
and take
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, this give the desired property of Eq. (

).

•

Similarly, in the case of the product of positive semi-definite matrix of Sec.
5
, we assume without loss of generality
t_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})<t_{B}(\mathrm{b}_{+})
and the subordination relation reads for
z\geq\mathrm{c}_{+}
:

\displaystyle t_{C}(z)

\displaystyle=t_{A}\left(\frac{z}{\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{B}\left(t_{C}(z)\right)}\right)\leq t_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\,,

(327)

where the inequality in Eq. (
327
) is due to the same reasoning as in the additive case and taking again the limit
z=\mathrm{c}_{+}+\epsilon
with
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
gives Eq. (
181
).

•

for the cases of the sum of rectangular matrices, we have the following subordination relation for
z\geq\mathrm{c}_{+}
:

\displaystyle d_{C}(z)

\displaystyle=d_{A}\left(\frac{U^{\langle-1\rangle}\left(U(d_{C}(z)z)-\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{B}^{(q)}(d_{C}(z))d_{C}(z)\right)}{d_{C}(z)}\right)\,,

(328)

which gives Eq. (

Appendix B
Asymptotic of the quenched and annealed free energy

The goal of this section is to describe the main steps to get the asymptotic behavior of the quenched and free energies of each model. For the annealed free energy, we primarily insist on the additive symmetric case (SSK model) as the way to get the annealed free energy for the LSSK model and BSSK model is similar.

B.1
For the SSK model (additive spherical integral)

In this section, we focus on the SSK model of Sec.
4
.

B.1.1
Complex integral representation and quenched free energy

The starting point of the computation of the free energies is a complex integral representation of the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)
of Eq. (
109
). If we denote again by
\bm{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
, the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{M}
, by removing the constraint over the sphere by introducing a Lagrange multiplier
z
and using a Gaussian integration, we can write the partition function as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathscr{C}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N}{2}G_{N}(\bm{\lambda},z,\theta)}\mathrm{d}z\,,

(329)

where
\mathscr{C}
is a vertical line in the complex plane that goes to the right of all the eigenvalues and

\displaystyle G_{N}(\bm{\lambda},z,\theta)

\displaystyle:=z\theta-1-\log\theta-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\log(z-\lambda_{i})+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

(330)

The integral representation is true for any symmetric matrix. If we now set
\mathbf{M}
to be equal to the matrix
\mathbf{C}
conditioned to have its top eigenvalue at the position
x
,
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{C}|\{\lambda_{1}=x\}
, and take the large
N
limit, we have the following saddle-point approximation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{C}|\{\lambda_{1}=x\}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{K}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N}{2}G_{N}(x,\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N},z^{*},\theta)}\,,

(331)

with
K
a constant and
z^{*}
the solution of the saddle point equation given by setting the partial derivative with respect to
z
in the expression of Eq. (
330
) to be equal to zero. A careful analysis shows that this saddle point
z^{*}
may exhibit a possible saturation at
x
, depending on the value of the parameter
\theta
, as shown in Ref.
[
36
]
and this gives the behavior of the partial derivatives of Eq. (
4.3
) and Eq. (
4.3
).

B.1.2
Computation of the annealed free energy

We now turn to the limiting behavior of the annealed free energy of the SSK model given by Eq. (
4.3
) for a random matrix
\mathbf{A}\sim\mathbb{P}_{V,w_{A}}
, based on ideas developed in Ref.
[
35
]
. Setting
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{A}
in Eq. (
329
) and taking the average over the law of Eq. (
5
) for the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
, we have the following integral representation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathscr{C}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N}{2}H_{N}(\bm{\lambda},z,\theta)}\mathbb{I}_{\{\lambda_{i}\leq w_{A}\}}\mathrm{d}\bm{a}\mathrm{d}z\,,

(332)

with:

\displaystyle H_{N}(\bm{\lambda},z,\theta)

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(\lambda_{i})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,j|j\neq i}\log|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(z-\lambda_{i})+z\theta-1-\log\theta+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

(333)

This can be understood as the Hamiltonian of a system of
N+1
particles
15
15

15

The variable
z
superficially looks like another eigenvalue repelled by all the other ones and with its own linear potential. But closer inspection reveals that the force between
z
and the
\lambda_{i}
is actually attractive. What is even stranger is that the equilibrium position of
z
is a local minimum of the probability. The reason for this is that in the integral of Eq. (
332
),
z
is integrated on a vertical line in the complex plane, so the second derivative in that direction should be positive for the integral to converge.
. In the large
N
limit, we argue that the integral is dominated by the most probable configuration given as the solution of the set of saddle-point equations:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{\lambda_{i}^{*}}H_{N}(\bm{\lambda}^{*},z^{*},\theta)=0&\mbox{for }i=1,\dots,N\,,\\
\\
\partial_{z^{*}}H_{N}(\bm{\lambda}^{*},z^{*},\theta)=0&\,.\end{array}\right.

that is:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}V^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}^{*})=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{j=1|j\neq i}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{*}-\lambda_{j}^{*}}+\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{z^{*}-\lambda_{i}^{*}}&\mbox{for }i=1,\dots,N\,,\\
\\
\theta=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{z^{*}-\lambda_{i}^{*}}&\,.\end{array}\right.

These two equations have to be understood with the additional constraints:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{1}^{*}\leq z^{*}&\,,\\
\\
\lambda_{N}^{*}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{1}^{*}\leq w_{A}&\,.\end{array}\right.

We need to distinguish three different cases:

1.

For
\theta\leq g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
, the bottom line of Eq. (
B.1.2
) can be satisfied with the
\lambda_{i}^{*}
’s in their classical positions. By direct inversion we find:

\displaystyle z^{*}(\theta)

\displaystyle=g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(\theta)\,,

(343)

and by self-averaging property, this gives the same result as in the quenched case.

2.

For
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})\leq\theta\leq\bar{g}_{A}(w_{A})
, for the RHS of the bottom line of Eq. (
B.1.2
) to be equal to
\theta
, one has to have the distance between
z^{*}
and
\lambda_{1}^{*}
be of order
O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
so Eq. (
B.1.2
) (bottom) has to be understood as:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\simeq g_{A}(z^{*})+\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{z^{*}-\lambda_{1}^{*}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

(344)

Next, since
V(.)
is analytic, we can approximate the potential and interaction term in Eq. (
B.1.2
) for
i=1
by:

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(\lambda_{1}^{*})

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}(z^{*})+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,,

(345)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{*}-\lambda_{j}^{*}}

\displaystyle\simeq g_{A}(z^{*})+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

(346)

injecting this in the top line of Eq. (
B.1.2
) for
i=1
one gets:

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(z^{*})

\displaystyle=2g_{A}(z^{*})+\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{z^{*}-\lambda_{1}^{*}}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\,.

(347)

Now making the difference of Eq. (
344
) and Eq. (
347
) to eliminate the term
\frac{1}{z^{*}-\lambda_{1}^{*}}
and neglecting term of order
O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
one obtain a simple self consistent equation for the unknown
z^{*}
:

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(z^{*})-g_{A}(z^{*})

\displaystyle=\theta\,,

(348)

which using Eq. (
23
) reads:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{A}(z^{*})

\displaystyle=\theta\,.

(349)

where
\bar{g}_{A}(.)
is the second branch of the Stieltjes transform as described in App.
A.1
. Following the properties of the second branch of the Stieltjes transform, inverting Eq. (
349
) yields:

\displaystyle z^{*}(\theta)

\displaystyle=g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(\theta)\,.

(350)

where
g_{A}^{\langle-1\rangle}(.)
is here the analytical continuation beyond the point
g_{A}(\mathrm{a}_{+})
of the inverse of the Stieltjes. This explains the result in the main text.

3.

for
\theta\geq\bar{g}_{A}(w_{A})
, the position of the top eigenvalue becomes fixed at the wall and since the distance between
z^{*}
and this top of eigenvalue is infinitely small in the large
N
limit, we have again a saturation, but now at the position
w_{A}
instead of
x
in the quenched case:

\displaystyle z^{*}(\theta)

\displaystyle=w_{A}\,.

(351)

Once we have the expression for the saddle points
z^{*}
, we get the annealed free energy for the SSK model by taking the logarithm and this gives the expression of Eq. (
4.3
) in the main text.

Remark (GOE matrices and Gaussian integration):
For
\mathbf{A}
a GOE matrix with variance
\sigma^{2}
, the annealed free energy can be directly obtained by Gaussian integration. By rotationally invariance, the average of the partition function is simply the moment generating function of one of the diagonal element, say
A_{11}
. Since this element is a Gaussian random variable with variance
2\sigma^{2}/N
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N\theta}{2}A_{11}-\frac{N}{4\sigma^{2}}A_{11}^{2}}}{\sqrt{4\pi\sigma^{2}/N}}\mathrm{d}A_{11}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N}{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}\theta^{2}}{2}}\,.

(352)

Using the expression of Eq. (
104
) for the R-transform, this gives indeed Eq. (
4.3
) for the annealed free energy.

Remark (Wishart matrices and Gaussian integration):
For
\mathbf{A}
a Wishart matrix of shape parameter
q=N/M
, the annealed free energy can be also directly obtained by Gaussian integration. Indeed, we have
\mathbf{A}\overset{\text{in law}}{=}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{m}
, where the
\{\bm{x}_{m}\}_{m=1,\dots,M}
are
M
independent
N
-dimensional standard Gaussian vectors. Since
\mathbf{A}
is rotationally invariant, we can remove the integral over the sphere and fix
\bm{\sigma}=\bm{e}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
without loss of generality. This gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N\theta}{2M}\bm{e}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}_{m}\right)\bm{e}_{1}}\prod_{i=1}^{M}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|\bm{x}_{m}|^{2}}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{-\frac{M}{2}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{m}=\left(\int\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{I}-q\theta\bm{e}_{1}\bm{e}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bm{x}}}{(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)^{M}\,.

(353)

Now for
\theta\geq 1/q
, the integral inside the bracket is diverging and hence we get
F_{A}^{\prime}(\theta)=\infty
in this case. Otherwise, we can do the Gaussian integration, and we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2q}\log(1-q\theta)

\displaystyle\left(\mbox{for }\theta<\frac{1}{q}\right)\,.

(354)

Using the expression of Eq. (
105
) for the R-transform of a Wishart matrix, this gives indeed Eq. (
4.3
) for the annealed free energy. A similar computation can be one for generalized Wishart matrices.

B.2
For the LSSK model (multiplicative spherical integral)

The behavior in the multiplicative case for which the partition function
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)
of the LSSK given by Eq. (
183
) is very similar to the additive case, so we only outline the main steps to compute the free energies. One can show see Ref.
[

]
that this spherical integral admit the following complex integral representation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

(355)

with

\displaystyle G_{N}(\bm{\lambda},z,\theta)

(356)

with
K
a constant and
\mathscr{C}
an integral in the complex plane crossing the real axis at a point
\gamma

) and Eq. (
5.3
) for the quenched free energy.

B.1.2
for the additive case by looking at the saddle points of Eq. (

) averaged over the law of the

’s. One has to consider the three different cases

B.3
For the BSSK model (rectangular spherical integral)

In this section, we consider the asymptotic behavior of the rectangular spherical integral
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)
of Sec.
6
defined by Eq. (
242
). In particular, we derive in detail the computation of the quenched free energy.

B.3.1
complex integral representation

Removing the constraints on the spheres by introducing Lagrange multipliers
z_{1},z_{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{N}{2}}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{M}{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2}}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N+M}}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\,\mathrm{e}^{\frac{z_{1}}{2}+\frac{z_{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}\bm{y}}\,,

(357)

where the matrix
\mathbf{Q}\equiv\mathbf{Q}(z_{1},z_{2},\theta,\mathbf{s})
is given by:

\mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\mathbf{I}_{N}&-\sqrt{NM}\theta{\rm Diag}(\bm{s})&\mathbf{0}_{M-N,N}\\
-\sqrt{NM}\theta{\rm Diag}(\bm{s})&z_{2}\mathbf{I}_{N}&\mathbf{0}_{M-N,N}\\
\mathbf{0}_{M-N,N}&\mathbf{0}_{M-N,N}&z_{2}\mathbf{I}_{M-N}\end{pmatrix}\,.

with
\bm{s}\equiv(s_{1}(\mathbf{M}),\dots,s_{N}(\mathbf{M}))
the vector of singular values.
By Gaussian integration, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2}}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{z_{1}/2+z_{2}/2}(\mathrm{det}\mathbf{M})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,,

(358)

so we need to compute the determinant of this matrix
\mathbf{Q}
. Expanding twice along the right-bottom block, we have:

\displaystyle\det\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\det(z_{2}\mathbf{I}_{M-N})\det\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}\mathbf{I}_{N}&-\sqrt{NM}\theta{\rm Diag}(\bm{s})&\\
-\sqrt{NM}\theta{\rm Diag}(\bm{s})&z_{2}\mathbf{I}_{N}&\\
\end{pmatrix}\,,

(359)

\displaystyle\det\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=z_{2}^{M-N}\det(z_{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})\det\left(z_{1}\mathbf{I}_{N}-\left(\sqrt{NM}\theta\right)^{2}\frac{1}{z_{2}}{\rm Diag}(\bm{s}^{2})\right)\,,

(360)

\displaystyle\det\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=z_{2}^{M-N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(z_{1}z_{2}-NM\theta^{2}s_{i}^{2}\right)\,.

(361)

which gives:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2}}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\,z_{2}^{\frac{N-M}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{z_{1}}{2}+\frac{z_{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(z_{1}z_{2}-NM\theta^{2}s_{i}^{2}\right)}\,.

(362)

Let’s do the change of variable
(z_{1}\to N\theta z_{1},\mathrm{d}z_{1}\to N\theta\mathrm{d}z_{1})
,
(z_{2}\to M\theta z_{2},\mathrm{d}z_{2}\to M\theta\mathrm{d}z_{2})
we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{M}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{N}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{M}{2}\right)}{\left(N\theta\right)^{\frac{N}{2}-1}\left(M\theta\right)^{\frac{M}{2}-1}}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\mathrm{i}}\right)^{2}\int_{\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2}}\mathrm{d}z_{1}\mathrm{d}z_{2}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{N\theta}{2}z_{1}+\frac{M\theta}{2}z_{2}+\frac{N-M}{2}\log(z_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(z_{1}z_{2}-s_{i}^{2}\right)}\,.

(363)

If we now consider the large
N
limit with the double scaling limit of Eq. (
40

C.1.2
Large deviation beyond the threshold and the Sherman-Morrison formula

To get the full behavior in the presence of an outlier at

and two vectors

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{u}_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}^{-1}-\frac{\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}}{1+\mathbf{v}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{0}}\,.

(427)

This formula allows one to relate the resolvent of a rank one perturbation of a matrix to the resolvent of the matrix plus an additional term. Namely, setting
\mathbf{M}=z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{B}
,
\mathbf{u}_{0}=-\gamma\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{v}
in Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)+\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)\mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)\,.

(428)

where
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)
is the resolvent of
\mathbf{B}
, defined by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(z)

\displaystyle:=(z\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{B})^{-1}\,,

(429)

and
\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{C}}(z)
is defined similarly. The resolvent contains all the information of the matrix
\mathbf{C}
and in particular the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{C}
corresponds to a value for which the resolvent is ill-defined. Now for the matrix
\mathbf{C}
to have just one outlier out of the bulk, say at a position

\displaystyle 1-\gamma\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(x)\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\delta(1-\gamma\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(x)\mathbf{v}=0)\right]\,.

(431)

z_{2}^{*}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{z_{2}^{*}}{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{w_{B}+\gamma-x}\,.

(442)

We have now all the tools to express the rate function in this regime. We have:

\displaystyle\Psi_{C}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\lambda^{*}}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}H(z_{1}^{*},z_{2}^{*},s)\mathrm{d}s=-\int_{\lambda^{*}}^{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}H(z_{1}^{*},z_{2}^{*},s)\mathrm{d}s\,,

(443)

since the partial derivatives with respect to
z_{1}^{*}
and
z_{2}^{*}
are exactly zero at the saddle points. The derivative of the function
H
with respect to
s
is given thanks to Eq. (
435
) by:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}H(z_{1}^{*},z_{2}^{*},s)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{z_{2}^{*}}{\gamma z_{1}^{*}+z_{2}^{*}(x-b_{i})}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{x-b_{i}}\,.

(444)

The first term in the RHS can be simplified since if we multiply the topline of Eq. (
C.1.2
) by
\frac{z_{2}^{*}}{\gamma}
we find that this term is exactly
\frac{z_{2}^{*}}{\gamma}
. Taking the large
N
limit, one obtain the final expression for the rate function. The result is summarized in the next section.

C.1.3
Expression for the rate function

•

if
\gamma\leq\frac{1}{g_{B}(\mathrm{b}_{+})}
, then for
x
not in
[\mathrm{b}_{+},w_{B}+\gamma]
the rate function is infinite and is otherwise given by:

\displaystyle\Psi_{C}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathrm{b}_{+}}^{x}\bar{g}_{B}(t)-g_{B}(t)\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{for }\mathrm{b}_{+}\leq x\leq x_{c_{1}}\,,\\
\\
K_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{c_{1}}}^{x}\left(\mathcal{R}_{B}^{\langle-1\rangle}(t-\gamma)-g_{B}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{for }x_{c_{1}}\leq x\leq x_{c_{2}}\,,\\
\\
K_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{w_{B}+\gamma-x}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{c_{2}}}^{x}g_{B}(t)\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{for }x_{c_{2}}\leq x\leq w_{B}+\gamma\,.\end{array}\right.

•

if
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{g_{B}(\mathrm{b}_{+})}
then for
x
not in
[\mathrm{b}_{+},w_{B}+\gamma]
the rate function is infinite and is otherwise given by:

\displaystyle\Psi_{C}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\lambda^{*}}^{x}\left(\mathcal{R}_{B}^{\langle-1\rangle}(t-\gamma)-g_{B}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{for }\mathrm{b}_{+}\leq x\leq x_{c_{2}}\,,\\
\\
K_{2}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{w_{B}+\gamma-s}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{x_{c_{2}}}^{x}g_{B}(t)\mathrm{d}t&\mbox{for }x_{c_{2}}\leq x\leq w_{B}+\gamma\,,\end{array}\right.

with

\displaystyle K_{2}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\lambda^{*}}^{x_{c_{2}}}\left(\bar{g}_{B}(t)-g_{B}(t)\right)\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\bar{g}_{B}(w_{B})}\right)\,.

(453)

Remark (Behavior near the edge):
Bellow the threshold when there is no outlier, if the density is non-degenerate, one recovers the Tracy-Widom ’3/2’ scaling for the rate function since the expression matches the ones of the classical case of Eq. (
24
). However above the threshold, because both the Stieltjes transform and the R-transform (and hence its shifted inverse) are analytic around
\lambda^{*}
, the rate function as a square behavior close to the outlier. This is expected because the fluctuations of the outliers are known to be Gaussian and of variance
N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
.

Similar to the additive case, we consider in this subsection the ’multiplicative’ rank-one deformation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}}\mathbf{B}\sqrt{\mathbf{I}+\gamma\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}}\,,

\displaystyle 1-\gamma\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\sqrt{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{B}}(x)\sqrt{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{v}

Appendix D
Rank-one plus rank-one

In this section, we consider the toy model of the sum of two rank-one matrix where one is randomly rotated:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle:=w_{A}\mathbf{e}\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf{T}}+w_{B}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\,,

(478)

with
\mathbf{e}
the unit vector in the first canonical direction and
\mathbf{v}
a unit vector taken uniformly on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, we choose
w_{A}\geq w_{B}
without loss of generality.
The largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{C}
is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{A}+w_{B}+\sqrt{(w_{A}-w_{B})^{2}+4w_{A}w_{B}|\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}|^{2}}}{2}\,,

(479)

Note that since
0\leq|\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}|^{2}\leq 1
, we have
w_{A}\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C})\leq w_{A}+w_{B}
as expected. In the limit
N\to\infty
, the vector
\bm{v}
is almost-surely orthogonal to the vector
\mathbf{e}
so that the top eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathbf{C}
is given by
w_{A}
. Note that this can be checked by taking the limit
|\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}|\to 0^{+}
in Eq. (
479
). Now at large but finite
N
, we can ask what is the probability of finding
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C})
at a position
x
higher than
w_{A}
. To do so, let’s remark, that since
\mathbf{v}
is uniform on the sphere, the square of each of its component - and hence the squared overlap - is known to follow a
Beta distribution
of parameters (
1/2
,
N/2
). Its probability density given by:

\displaystyle p\left(|\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}|^{2}=\phi\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi^{-1/2}(1-\phi)^{N/2-1}}{B(1/2,N/2)}\,,

(480)

where
B(1/2,N/2)
is the Euler Beta function. From this we can write the exact probability density
\mathcal{P}_{N}
of the law of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{N}(\lambda_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda_{1}-w_{A}-w_{B}}{B(1/2,N/2)w_{A}w_{B}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}(w_{A}+w_{B}-\lambda_{1})}{w_{A}w_{B}}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}(w_{A}+w_{B}-\lambda_{1})}{w_{A}w_{B}}\right)^{N/2-1}\,.

(481)

In the large
N
limit we obtain the following rate function for the large deviations of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C})

\displaystyle\Psi_{C}(x)

\displaystyle=-\log\left(\frac{w_{A}+w_{B}-x}{w_{B}}\right)-\log\left(\frac{x}{w_{A}}\right)\,.

(482)

This rate function is represented in Fig.
11
. This is the same rate function that we had in the third regime of Eq. (
4.6
) with
\mathrm{c}_{+}=x_{c_{2}}=w_{A}
and
g_{C}(z)=1/z
.
