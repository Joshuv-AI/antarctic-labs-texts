How catastrophic can catastrophic forgetting be in linear regression?
Itay Evron, Edward Moroshko, Rachel Ward, Nati Srebro, Daniel Soudry

Abstract
To better understand catastrophic forgetting, we study fitting an overparameterized linear model to a sequence of tasks with different input distributions. We analyze how much the model forgets the true labels of earlier tasks after training on subsequent tasks, obtaining exact expressions and bounds. We establish connections between continual learning in the linear setting and two other research areas – alternating projections and the Kaczmarz method. In specific settings, we highlight differences between forgetting and convergence to the offline solution as studied in those areas. In particular, when T T tasks in d d dimensions are presented cyclically for k k iterations, we prove an upper bound of T 2 ​ min ⁡ { 1 / k , d / k } T^{2}\min\{1/\sqrt{k},d/k\} on the forgetting. This stands in contrast to the convergence to the offline solution, which can be arbitrarily slow according to existing alternating projection results. We further show that the T 2 T^{2} factor can be lifted when tasks are presented in a random ordering.

Introduction

Continual learning or lifelong learning is a machine learning setting where data from different tasks are presented sequentially to the learner.
The goal is to adapt the model to new tasks while preserving its performance on previously-learned tasks
(
Schlimmer and Fisher 1986
;
Thrun and Mitchell 1995
;
Parisi et al. 2019
)
.
A key challenge in continual learning is the
catastrophic forgetting
phenomenon

(
McCloskey and Cohen 1989
;
Ratcliff 1990
;
Goodfellow et al. 2013
;
Ramasesh et al. 2020
)
, wherein adaptation of models to fit to new tasks often (unsurprisingly) leads to degradation in performance on previous tasks.

Despite recent advances in theoretical understanding of continual learning
(
Doan et al. 2021
;
Bennani et al. 2020
;
Knoblauch et al. 2020
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Asanuma et al. 2021
)
,
catastrophic forgetting is not
fully
understood even in simple models.
Consider sequentially learning from a stream of tasks.
One should ask:
what are the best and worst-case sequences of tasks?
How does the similarity between tasks effect catastrophic forgetting?
What can be said analytically about the benefits to revisiting (
i.e.,
replaying) tasks?
When does forgetting truly becomes “catastrophic”, so that it is impossible to learn
all
tasks sequentially?

In this work, we aim to theoretically characterize the
worst-case
catastrophic forgetting
in overparameterized linear regression models.
1
1

1

We believe it is necessary to do so before moving to more complex models.
Moreover, any linear regression result can be applied to complex models (e.g., deep networks) in the neural kernel regime (NTK), as in
Doan et al. 2021
;
Bennani et al. 2020
.

To this end, we sequentially fit a linear model to tasks observed in some ordering.
We analyze the forgetting convergence of linear regressors obtained by GD/SGD which is trained
to convergence
on data from the current task.

We explain how in this linear setting,
continual learning repeatedly
projects
previous solutions onto the solution spaces of newer tasks.
Interestingly, we show that, under a realizability assumption, these projections contract the distance to the minimum norm offline solution that solves
all
tasks.
We analyze this contraction and the resulting convergence.

A setting similar to ours has been extensively studied in the
alternating projections
literature.
There, a vector is iteratively projected onto closed subspaces (or convex sets in general),
in order to find a solution in their intersection.
For instance,
Kayalar and Weinert 1988
studied Halperin’s cyclic setting
Halperin 1962

and analyzed the convergence of

\big\|{({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}}\big\|^{2}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}
, and
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}
are orthogonal projections onto subspaces
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
, and
H_{1}\!\cap\!H_{2}
(respectively).
In contrast, our goal is to analyze the projection
residuals
(
i.e.,
the forgetting).
That is, we mainly focus on

\big\|{({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}}\big\|^{2}
,
rather than on the convergence to

H_{1}\!\cap\!H_{2}

(
i.e.,
an offline solution).
Due to this difference,
we are able to derive
uniform
data-independent upper bounds on forgetting, even when convergence to the offline solution is arbitrarily slow and “traditional” bounds become trivial.

Moreover, our fitting procedure
can also be seen as a Kaczmarz method
(
Kaczmarz 1937
)
,
where one solves a linear equation system
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\!=\!\boldsymbol{b}

by iteratively solving subsets (
i.e.,
tasks) of it.
Here also, typical bounds involve data-dependent properties like the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{A}
,
which are trivial in the worst case.

Our Contributions.

We thoroughly analyze catastrophic forgetting in linear regression
optimized by the plain memoryless (S)GD algorithm (
i.e.,
without actively trying to mitigate forgetting), and:

•

Identify cases where there is no forgetting.

•

Show that without any restrictions, one can construct task sequences where forgetting is maximal and essentially
catastrophic
.

•

Connect catastrophic forgetting
to a large body of research on alternating projections and the Kaczmarz method.
Then, we use this perspective to investigate
the
worst-case
forgetting when
T
tasks in
d
dimensions are seen
repeatedly
for
k
iterations,
under two different orderings:

–

Cyclic task orderings.

For
T\!\geq\!3
, we uniformly upper bound the forgetting by
\tfrac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{k}}
and
\tfrac{T^{2}d}{k}
, and lower bound it by
\tfrac{T^{2}}{k}
.
To the best of our knowledge,
our analysis uncovers novel bounds for residuals in the cyclic block Kaczmarz setting
(
Elfving 1980
)
as well.

–

Random task orderings.

We upper bound the expected forgetting by
\tfrac{d}{k}
,
independently of
T
.

•

Analyze the effect of similarity, or angles, between two consecutive tasks on the forgetting.
We find that after seeing these tasks
once
—
intermediate angles are most prone to forgetting,
but after
repeating
the tasks —
small angles (
i.e.,
nearly-aligned tasks) cause the highest forgetting.

Problem setting

Motivating example.

Before we formalize our problem, we give a motivating example to keep in mind. Suppose we are interested to learn a predictor for pedestrian detection in an autonomous car.
This detector is required to operate well in
T
geographically distant environments
(
e.g.,
different countries), or “tasks”.
To train the detector, we need to drive the car around, but can do this only in one environment at a time. We do this until the detector has good performance in that environment.
Then we ship the car to another environment, drive it there to train the detector, and so on (potentially revisiting past environments). Notably, while the overall problem remains constant (pedestrian detection), each time we need to solve it for a potentially different landscape (
e.g.,
city, forest, desert), which can radically change the input distribution.
The question we aim to answer is:
when would the detector be able to work well in all the environments it visited during training, even though it only observed them sequentially?

General setting.

We consider fitting a linear model

f(\boldsymbol{x})\!=\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}
,
parameterized by
\boldsymbol{w}\!\,\in\!\,\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
on a sequence of tasks
originating from an arbitrary set of
T
regression problems

\big\{\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)\big\}_{m=1}^{T}
.
During
k
iterations,
tasks are seen
according to a
task ordering

\tau\!\!:\mathbb{N}^{+}\!\!\to\!\left[T\right]
.
That is,
the learner is presented with a sequence

\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(1)},\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(1)}\big),\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(2)},\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(2)}\big),\dots,\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(k)},\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(k)}\big)
.
Starting from
{\boldsymbol{w}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}_{d}}
,
the model is fitted sequentially, yielding a sequence of iterates
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
.
At each iteration
t
we obtain
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
by fitting the
current
task
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)},\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}\big)
,
without
access to other tasks.

Tasks.

Each
task

m=1,2,\dots,T
corresponds to a regression problem

\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)
,

i.e.,
to a data matrix
\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}\times d}
with
n_{m}
samples in
d
dimensions
and a vector

\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n_{m}}

of the corresponding labels.
We focus on the overparametrized regime,
where every task
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)
is rank deficient,

i.e.,
r_{m}\!\,\triangleq\!\,\operatorname{rank}{({\boldsymbol{X}_{m}})}\!\,<\,\!d
, thus admitting infinitely-many linear models that perfectly fit the data.

Notation.

Denote by
\boldsymbol{A}^{+}
the Moore–Penrose inverse of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
Denote by
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}
the orthogonal projection onto the
null space
of a matrix
\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
,

i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
.
Denote the
\ell^{2}
norm of a vector by
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|
,
and the
spectral
norm of a matrix
by
\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|
.
Denote the
d
-dimensional closed Euclidean ball as

\mathcal{B}^{d}\triangleq\!\big\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\,\big|\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|\leq 1\big\}
.
To avoid ambiguity, we explicitly exclude zero from the set of natural numbers and use

\mathbb{N}^{+}\!\!\triangleq\!\mathbb{N}\setminus\!\left\{0\right\}
.
Finally, denote the natural numbers from
1
to
T
by

\left[{T}\right]\!\triangleq\!\left\{1,\ldots,T\right\}
.

2.1
Task collections

A
task collection
is an (unordered) set of
T
tasks,

i.e.,
S=\big\{\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)\big\}_{m=1}^{T}
.
Throughout the paper,
we focus on task collections from the set:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{T}\triangleq\Big\{\big\{\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)\big\}_{m=1}^{T}~\Big|~\underbrace{\forall m\!:~\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}}\|\leq 1}_{\text{Assumption \ref{assume:bounded_data}}},~\underbrace{\exists\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{B}^{d}\!:~\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\!=\!\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{w},~\forall m\in\left[{T}\right]}_{\text{Assumption \ref{assume:realizability}}}\Big\}.\end{split}

That is, we make the two following
assumptions on the task collections.

Assumption 1

(Bounded data)
.

Singular values of all data matrices are bounded (w.l.o.g., by
1
).

Assumption 2

(Realizability)
.

Tasks are jointly realizable by a linear predictor
with a bounded norm (w.l.o.g., bounded by
1
).

Assumption
1
is merely a technical assumption on the data scale,
simplifying our presentation.
Assumption
2
on joint realizability of tasks
is essential to our derivations, especially for Eq. (
5
).
We note that this is a reasonable assumption in a highly overparameterized models like wide neural networks in the NTK regime, or in noiseless settings with an underlying linear model.

2.2
Forgetting and catastrophic forgetting

We fit the linear model sequentially on tasks
\tau(1),\ldots,\tau(k)
.
Starting from the solution to its preceding task,
each task is fitted
without
access to previous tasks.
The goal in continual learning is to not
forget
what we learned on previous tasks.
For example, in the motivating example given in the beginning of the section, we want the pedestrian detector to still be able to work well in previous environments after we train it in a new environment.

Formally,
like in a recent work

(
Doan et al. 2021
)
,
we measure the forgetting of
\boldsymbol{w}
on the task presented at iteration
t
by the squared loss

\mathcal{L}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{w}\right)\!\triangleq\!\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}}\big\|^{2}
,
and define the forgetting as follows.

Definition 3

(Forgetting)
.

Let
S\!=\!\big\{\!\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)\!\big\}_{m=1}^{T}\!\in\!\mathcal{S}_{T}

be a task collection fitted according to an ordering

\tau\!\!:\mathbb{N}^{+}\!\!\to\!\left[T\right]
.
The forgetting at iteration
k

is the average
loss
of already seen tasks,
i.e.,

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)=\frac{1}{k}{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\mathcal{L}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\right)=\frac{1}{k}{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\Big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}}\Big\|^{2}~.

In words, we say that the model “forgets” the labels of a task seen at iteration
t
after fitting
k
tasks, if
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
does not perfectly predict

\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}
from
\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}
,

i.e.,
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k})>0
.
We note in passing that

forgetting
should not be confused with
regret
since they can exhibit different behaviors
(see Section
7
).

In the following, we capture the worst possible convergence behavior under a given ordering.

Definition 4

(Worst-case forgetting)
.

The worst-case forgetting of a task ordering
\tau
after
k
iterations is the
maximal forgetting at iteration
k
on
any
task collection in
\mathcal{S}_{T}
, i.e.,
\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}}F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)
.

For specific task orderings, we are able to bound this worst-case forgetting by non-trivial uniform (data-independent) bounds that converge to 0 with the number of iterations
k
.
When the worst-case forgetting does not converge to 0, we say that forgetting is
catastrophic
.
More formally,

Definition 5

(Catastrophic forgetting of a task ordering)
.

Given an ordering
\tau
over
T
tasks,
the forgetting is
not
catastrophic if

\lim_{k\to\infty}({\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}}{F_{\tau,S}}(k)})=0
.

In this paper we focus on the quantities
F_{\tau,S}(k)
and
\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}}\!F_{\tau,S}(k)
,
aiming to answer the following questions:
under what conditions on
\tau
and
S
there is no forgetting,

i.e.,
F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)=0
?
What can we say about

\sup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}}\!F_{\tau,S}(k)

for general task orderings?
Can forgetting be catastrophic when fitting a finite number of tasks in a cyclic or random ordering?

2.3
Fitting procedure

Our ultimate goal is to minimize the forgetting (Definition
3
).
To this end, in this paper we analyze the following fitting procedure of tasks, corresponding to the simplest continual learning setting.
At each iteration
t=1,..,k
,
we start from the previous iterate
\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}

and run (stochastic) gradient descent
to convergence

so as to minimize the squared loss
on the
current
task,

i.e.,
{\mathcal{L}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{w}\right)=\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}}\big\|^{2}}
,
obtaining the new iterate
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
.

Since we work in the overparameterized regime, where
r_{t}<d

for all
t
, each
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
perfectly fits its corresponding task
\tau(t)
,
i.e.,
\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}=\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}
.
In addition, for each task, (S)GD at convergence returns the unique solution which implicitly minimizes the
\ell^{2}
distance to the initialization
(
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Gunasekar et al. 2018
)
, hence we can express
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
as the unique solution to the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}}&\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}\right\|~,~~~~~\text{s.t.}~~\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}~.\end{split}

(1)

Our iterative update rule.

The solution to the above optimization problem
is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{t}=\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}^{+}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1})~.

(2)

When
\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}
contains one sample only (
i.e.,
its rank is 1),
our update rule is equivalent to those of the Kaczmarz method
(
Kaczmarz 1937
)
and the NLMS algorithm
(
Slock 1993
;
Haykin 2002
)
.
Moreover, it can be seen as an SGD update
on the forgetting from Definition
3
,

i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}=\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}\!+\!\tfrac{1}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau(t)}}\|^{2}}\big({y_{\tau(t)}\!-\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau(t)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}}\big)\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau(t)}}
.
Given tasks with
many
samples (rank greater than
1
), our rule is equivalent to that of the block Kaczmarz method
Elfving 1980
.
We discuss these connections in Section
7
.

Minimum norm offline solution.

Under the realizability assumption
2

there might be infinitely-many
offline solutions
that perfectly fit
all
tasks.
To facilitate our discussion,
we focus on the minimum
\ell^{2}
-norm offline solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
(often referred to as the
offline solution
for brevity),

i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}~\triangleq~{\argmin}_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}}\right\|^{2},~~\text{s.t.}~~\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{y}_{m},~\forall m\in\left[{T}\right]~.\end{split}

(3)

Task solution spaces.

Finally, from Eq. (
1
) we see that at the end of the
t
-th iteration, the iterate
\boldsymbol{w}_{t}
must lie in the
solution space
of task
\tau(t)
, which is an affine subspace defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\tau(t)}\triangleq\big\{\boldsymbol{w}~\big|\,\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}\big\}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}+\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)})~.

(4)

3
Forgetting dynamics

To analyze forgetting,
we emphasize the projective nature of learning.
We rewrite the update rule from
Eq. (
2
)
by employing the realizability Assumption
2

and plugging in
\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\!=\!\boldsymbol{y}_{m}
into
that equation.
Then, we subtract
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
from both sides,
and reveal an equivalent affine update rule,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\big(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\big)\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}~\triangleq~{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}\big(\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\big)~,

(5)

where we remind that

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\triangleq\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
is the projection operator on the solution space
\mathcal{W}_{m}
.

Geometric interpretation.

Using properties of pseudo-inverses and operator norms we get that

\forall m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]\!:\,\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}=\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\leq\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}}\big\|^{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}=\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}}\big\|^{2}\big\|{\big({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}}\big)\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}
.
Then, we recall that
\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}}\big\|\!\leq\!1

(Assumption
1
),
and reveal that the forgetting can be seen as the mean of the squared
residuals
from projecting
\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)

onto previously-seen solution spaces. That is,

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\,{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)}\big\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{k}\,{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\big({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}}\big)\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)}\big\|^{2}~.

(6)

{(\boldsymbol{w}_{k-2}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})}

{(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})}
,

Overall, the solution is continually getting closer to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
.

Learning as contracting.

Recursively,
the affine update rule from Eq. (

)
provides a closed-form expression for the

(recall
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}&={\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}\big(\,\cancel{\boldsymbol{w}_{0}}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}~\big)~.\end{split}

(7)

Since orthogonal projections are non-expansive operators, it also follows that

\displaystyle\forall{t\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]\!}:~\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|\leq\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|\leq\dots\leq\|{\,\cancel{\boldsymbol{w}_{0}}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|=\|{\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|~,

hinting at a possible convergence towards the offline solution,
as depicted in Figure

.

Combining Eq. (

),
we express the average forgetting (Definition

)
of an ordering

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\big({\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}}\big){{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}~.

(8)

Worst-case formulation.

So far,

{\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{u}}\|^{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\boldsymbol{u}}\|^{2}}

is the only inequality we used
(at Eq. (

), relying on Assumption

).
Importantly, this inequality saturates when all non-zero singular values of
\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\forall m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]
are

.
Consequentially,
the

can be simply expressed in terms of

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}\end{subarray}}\!F_{\tau,S}(k)\,=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k}\,{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\big({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}}\big){\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}{\cdots}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}}\big\|^{2}~,

(9)

where we also used Assumption
2

that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq 1
.

we mainly analyze Eq. (

) and (

) from different perspectives.
Multiplying from the left by
\left\{\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}\right\}_{t=1}^{k}

is what distinguishes our quantity of interest — the forgetting — from quantities studied in the area of alternating projections.

Principal angles between two tasks.

Finally, we briefly present
principal angles
, which affect forgetting dynamics, as discussed throughout this paper.
Much of the research on alternating projections has focused on establishing notions of angles between subspaces
(
Deutsch 1995
;
Oppenheim 2018
)
.

Principal angles
,
also known as canonical angles,
are a popular choice for angles between two linear subspaces

(
Björck and Golub 1973
;
Bargetz et al. 2020
)
,
having many applications in numerical analysis
(e.g., in the generalized eigenvalue problem
Ge et al. 2016
).
These angles geometrically describe a pair of subspaces,
by recursively taking the smallest angle between any two vectors in these subspaces that are orthogonal to previously chosen vectors.
We elaborate on the definition and the role of these angles in App
A.3
.
There, we visualize these angles and explain that the non-zero principal angles between the row spaces of two tasks,

i.e.,
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}),\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top})
,
are identical to those between the corresponding
solution spaces
\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2}
.

The rest of our paper.

We study forgetting
under different task orderings.
In Section
4
we consider arbitrary orderings
and show when there is provably
no
forgetting,
and when forgetting is arbitrarily high,

i.e.,
catastrophic.
We analyze cyclic and random orderings in Sections
5
and
6
.
For both these orderings,
we derive convergence guarantees
and prove
forgetting
cannot
be catastrophic.

4
Arbitrary task orderings

Identity ordering.

In this section we consider arbitrary sequences of tasks,
i.e.,
we do not impose any specific ordering.
To this end, we take
k\!=\!T
and an
identity ordering

\tau
s.t.

{\tau\!\left(t\right)\!=\!t,\,\forall t\!\in\!\mathbb{N}^{+}}
.
To simplify notation, in this section only,
we suppress
\tau

and use
\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(m)}\!=\!\boldsymbol{X}_{m}

interchangeably.

4.1
No forgetting cases

Consider learning two tasks sequentially:

\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\big)

and then
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\big)
.
Right after learning the second task,
we have

\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\!=\!\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y}_{2}}\|^{2}\!=\!0
.
Thus, the forgetting from Eq. (
8
) becomes

{F_{\tau,S}(2)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}
.
We now derive sufficient and necessary conditions for no forgetting.

Theorem 6

(No forgetting in two-task collections)
.

Let

S\!=\!\left\{\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\big),\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\big)\right\}\!\in\!\mathcal{S}_{T=2}

be a task collection with
2
tasks,
fitted under an identity ordering
\tau
,
i.e.,
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\big)
and then
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\big)
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

For
any
labeling
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}

(or equivalently, any minimum norm solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
),
after fitting the second task, the model does not “forget” the first one.
That is,

F_{\tau,S}(2)=0
.

2.

It holds that

\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}\times d}
.

3.

Each principal angle between the tasks, i.e.,
\range({\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}})
and

\range({\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}})
, is either
0
or
\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
.

The proof is given in App.
B.1
.
The exact definition of angles between tasks is given in App.
A.3
.

For instance, the above conditions hold when

\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})\subseteq\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top})

(or vice-versa),

i.e.,
there is no forgetting when tasks have maximum overlap in the row span of inputs.
The third condition aligns with the empirical observation in
Ramasesh et al. 2020
, wherein catastrophic forgetting in overparameterized neural networks is small when the tasks are either very similar or very distinct. On the other hand, this seemingly contradicts the conclusions in
Doan et al. 2021
that similar tasks are
potentially
bad for forgetting.
However, their conclusions are based on a loose upper bound on the forgetting.
In Section
5.1
,
we carefully analyze the forgetting dynamics of two tasks seen repeatedly in cycles
and show that increasingly-similar tasks
can
be worse for forgetting,
but only after multiple cycles.
We elaborate on these connections in App
B.1.3
.

4.2
Maximal forgetting cases: can
F_{\tau,S}(k)\rightarrow 1
?

Now, we present an adversarial task collection that yields arbitrarily high forgetting:
at the end of learning, the learner almost completely “forgets” previously-seen tasks.
We use this opportunity to build further intuition on two factors causing high forgetting.

Our construction is intuitively based on
the geometric interpretation from Eq. (
6
) and Figure
1
,
that the forgetting is the
mean
of the squared residuals from projecting
(\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})
onto the solution spaces of previously-seen tasks,

i.e.,
F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\!\leq\!\tfrac{1}{k}{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\big({\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}}\big)\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)}\big\|^{2}
.

Figure 2:

Illustrating the adversarial construction.

For the discussed residuals to be large,
our construction ensures that:

1.

The iterates are kept afar from the
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
.

Since

{F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\leq\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}\leq\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\,-\,\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}},\forall t\!\leq\!k
,
it is important to maintain a large
\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|

in all iterations.
We achieve this by using similar
consecutive
tasks.

2.

Most
solution spaces are orthogonal to the last one.

For the averaged residuals to be large,
the last

\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)

should be orthogonal to as many previous solution spaces as possible.
For this, we “huddle” most of the tasks near the first one, almost orthogonally to the last.

Theorem 7

(Forgetting can be arbitrarily bad)
.

When using the identity ordering
(i.e.,
\tau\!\left(t\right)\!=t
),
thus seeing each task once,
the worst-case forgetting after
k
iterations is arbitrarily bad,
i.e.,

\displaystyle 1-\!\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T=k}\end{subarray}}\!\!F_{\tau,S}(k)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{k}}}\right)~.

The exact construction details and the proof are given in App
B.2
.

By now, we understand that under arbitrary task orderings,
there exist task sequences where
the learner almost
completely
forgets previously-learned expertise
and forgetting is indeed
catastrophic
.
In the sections to follow, we show that cyclic and random orderings
do not
suffer from this flaw.

5
Cyclic task orderings

We again consider collections of
T
tasks
\left\{\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{m},\boldsymbol{y}_{m}\big)\right\}_{m=1}^{T}
,
but now we study the forgetting when tasks are presented in a cyclic ordering
\tau
,

i.e.,
\tau\left(t\right)=\big(\left(t-1\right)\mathrm{mod}~T~\big)+1,~\forall t\!\in\!\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
For example, suppose we want to train a pedestrian detector continuously during different times of the day (morning, noon, evening, and night), so that the task order forms a fixed cycle.
Such cyclic settings also arise in search engines, e-commerce, and social networks,
where tasks (
i.e.,
distributions)
are largely influenced by events that recur
either weekly (
e.g.,
weekdays vs. weekends),
monthly (
e.g.,
paydays),
annually (
e.g.,
holidays),
and so on.

Under cyclic orderings,
the forgetting from Eq. (
8
)
after
n
cycles, becomes

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}(k=nT)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\big({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\,\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}\big)^{n}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\big\|{(\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m})\big({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\,\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}\big)^{n}}\big\|^{2}.

(10)

5.1
Warm up: Exact forgetting analysis with
T=2
tasks

Exploiting the connection we made to the field of alternating projections,
we first analyze the convergence to the minimum norm offline solution
in terms of the
Friedrichs angle

(
Friedrichs 1937
)

between the tasks,

i.e.,
their minimal
non-zero
principal angle

\theta_{F}\!\triangleq\!\min_{i:\theta_{i}\neq 0}\theta_{i}>0

(as explained in App
A.3
).

Theorem 8

(Convergence to the minimum norm offline solution)
.

For any task collection of two distinct tasks
fitted in a cyclic ordering
\tau
,
the distance from the offline solution
after
k\!=\!2n
iterations (
n
cycles) is

tightly
upper bounded by

\big\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\leq\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{F}\right)^{k-1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}
,
where
\theta_{F}

is the Friedrichs angle
between the given tasks, as defined above.

Proof.

Plugging in
the cyclic ordering definition into
the recursive form of Eq. (
7
),
we obtain

{\big\|{{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}}\big\|=\big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|=\big\|{({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|}
.

A known alternating projection result by

Kayalar and Weinert 1988
(Theorem 2 therein) states that

{\big\|{({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}}\big\|^{2}=\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{F}\right)^{k-1}}
,
where in our context,

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}
projects onto
the null spaces’ intersection,
i.e.,
\kernel({\boldsymbol{X}_{1}})\cap\kernel({\boldsymbol{X}_{2}})
.
Since the
minimum norm
solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
must lie in

\range\!\left({\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}}\right)\cup\range\!\left({\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}}\right)
,
then by properties of orthogonal complements we have

{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{0}}
.
Then,
we see that

\|{{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}}\|^{2}=\big\|{\big({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}\big)^{n}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\!\!=\!\big\|{\big({({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}}\big)\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}
,
and conclude:

{\|{{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}}\|^{2}\!=\!\big\|{\big({({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}}\big)\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\!\leq\!\big\|{{({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})^{n}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1\cap\,2}}}\big\|^{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}=\!\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{F}\right)^{k-1}\!\big\|{\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}}\,.

Clearly, a carefully chosen
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}

(induced by
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
)
can saturate the inequality, making it tight.
∎

Note that the rate of convergence to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
can be arbitrarily slow when
the Friedrichs angle
{\theta_{F}\to 0}
.
Importantly, this means that there can be no data-independent convergence guarantees to
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
.
One might think that this implies that the forgetting
(
i.e.,
the residuals)
is also only trivially bounded, however a careful analysis shows that this is not the case.

In contrast to the above Theorem
8
, we now show that the forgetting

is
non-trivially bounded.

Lemma 9

(Angles’ effect on forgetting
T=2
)
.

For any task collection
S\in\mathcal{S}_{T=2}
of two tasks,
the forgetting after
k=2n
iterations (i.e.,
n
cycles) is tightly upper bounded by

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\leq~&\frac{1}{2}\max_{i}\left\{\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)^{k-1}\left(1-\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)\right\}~,\end{split}

where

\{\theta_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\left(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]

are the non-zero principal angles between the two tasks in
S
.
Moreover, the above inequality saturates when all non-zero singular values of the first task
(i.e., of
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
)
are
1
s.

In contrast to
\cos^{2}\theta_{F}
that bounds the distance to the offline solution (see Theorem
8
) and can be arbitrarily close to
1
, the quantities
\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)^{k-1}\left(1-\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)
in Lemma
9

are upper bounded
uniformly
for any
\theta_{i}
, which allows deriving a data-independent expression for the worst-case forgetting in the next theorem.

Theorem 10

(Worst-case forgetting when
T=2
)
.

For a cyclic ordering
\tau
of two tasks,
the worst-case forgetting after
k=2n
iterations
(i.e.,
n
cycles),
is

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T=2}\end{subarray}}\!\!F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)=\frac{1}{2e\left(k-1\right)}-\frac{1}{4e\left(k-1\right)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{3}}\right)~.

The proofs for both
Lemma
9
and
Theorem
10

are given in App
C.1
.

5.2
Main Result: Worst-case forgetting with
T\geq 3
tasks

For the general cyclic case,
we provide two upper bounds –
a dimension-dependent bound, and more importantly, a dimension-independent one.
Both follow a power-law
w.r.t.
the iteration number
k
.

Theorem 11

(Worst-case forgetting when
T\geq 3
)
.

For any number of tasks
T\geq 3
under a cyclic ordering
\tau
, the worst-case forgetting
after
k=nT\geq T^{2}
iterations (i.e.,
n\geq T
cycles), is

\displaystyle\frac{T^{2}}{24ek}~~\leq\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T\geq 3}:\\
\forall m:\,\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\leq r_{\max}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!F_{\tau,S}(k)~~\leq~~{\min\left\{\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{k}},~~\frac{T^{2}\left({d-r_{\max}}\right)}{2k}\right\}}~.

Moreover,
if the cyclic operator
\big({{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}\big)
from Eq. (
10
) is symmetric (e.g., in a
back-and-forth
setting where tasks
m
and
(T\!-\!m\!+\!1)
are identical
\forall m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]
),
then the worst-case forgetting is
sharply

{\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T\geq 3}\end{subarray}}F_{\tau,S}(k)=\Theta\left(\nicefrac{{T^{2}}}{{k}}\right)}
.

Proof sketch for the upper bound.

We briefly portray our proof for the above result (given fully in App
C.2
).
For brevity, denote the cyclic operator as

\boldsymbol{M}\triangleq{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\dots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}
.
Our proof revolves around the
maximal decrease at the
n
th cycle,

i.e.,
\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\triangleq\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}-\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}
.
We start by showing that the worst-case forgetting on the
first task
(see Eq. (
10
)) is upper bounded by the maximal decrease.
That is,

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{u}\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{d}\!:~\big\|{(\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1})\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(*)}}}{{=}}\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\!-\big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(**)}}}{{\leq}}\!\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\!-\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\!\!=\!\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{u}),

where (*) stems from the idempotence of
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}
,
and (**) is true since projections are non-expansive operators, meaning

\big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\geq\big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}\triangleq\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}}\big\|^{2}
.

More generally, we show that

\|{(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m})\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}}\|^{2}\!\leq\!m\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})
,
yielding the overall bound:

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{T}{\sum}_{m=1}^{T}\big\|{(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m})\boldsymbol{M}^{n}}\big\|^{2}\!\leq\!\tfrac{T-1}{2}\cdot\!\!\!\!\max_{\boldsymbol{u}:\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}=1}\!\!\!\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})~.

Then, we prove that

\max_{\boldsymbol{u}:\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}=1}\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq 2\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\!-\!\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\|\leq 2\sqrt{\nicefrac{{T}}{{n}}}
,
using telescoping sums on elements of

\{\|{\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{t}\!-\!\boldsymbol{M}^{t+1}\right)\!\boldsymbol{v}}\|^{2}\}_{t=1}^{n}
.
Finally, we prove

\max_{\boldsymbol{u}:\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}=1}\Delta_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq\tfrac{d-r_{\max}}{n}

by using telescoping sums on the traces of matrices

\left\{({\boldsymbol{M}^{t}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{t}\!-({\boldsymbol{M}^{t+1}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{M}^{t+1}\right\}_{t=1}^{n}.~\blacksquare

6
Random task orderings

So far, we saw in Section
4
that arbitrary task orderings provide no convergence guarantees and might forget
catastrophically
.
In Section
5
we saw that cyclic orderings
do not
suffer from catastrophic forgetting, since their forgetting converges to zero like a power law.
We now analyze
random
task ordering,
and show that they also have uniform (data-independent) convergence guarantees.

We consider a random task ordering
\tau
that matches a uniform probability to any task at any iteration,
i.e.,
\forall m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right],\forall t\!\in\!\mathbb{N}^{+}\!\!:~\Pr\left[~\tau\!\left(t\right)=m~\right]\!=\!\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}}
.
Below, we adjust the forgetting definitions in
3
and
4

to the random setting by defining the expected forgetting.

Definition 12

(Expected forgetting of a task collection)
.

After
k
iterations, the
expected forgetting
on a specific task collection
S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}

is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\left[F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\right]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\Big[\tfrac{1}{k}{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}}\right\|^{2}\Big]~.

Our main result in this section is a uniform bound on the expected forgetting under the uniform random task ordering.
The proof is given in App
D
.

Theorem 13

(Worst-case expected forgetting)
.

Under the uniform i.i.d. task ordering
\tau
,
the worst-case expected forgetting after
k
iterations is

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}:\\
\tfrac{1}{T}{\sum}_{m=1}^{T}\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})=r_{\operatorname{avg}}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\bar{F}_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\leq\frac{9\left(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}\right)}{k}~.

Remark 14

(Last iterate SGD results)
.

In the special case

Many practical methods have been proposed over the last decade to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning.
Clearly we cannot cover all methods here, but refer the reader to recent surveys
(
e.g.,
(
Parisi et al. 2019
;
Qu et al. 2021
)
)
and to App
F
.
In a nutshell, algorithmic approaches to continual learning can be roughly divided into three:

Memory-based replay approaches
(
e.g.,
(
Robins 1995
;
Shin et al. 2017
)
);
Regularization approaches
(
e.g.,
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Zeno et al. 2021
;
Li and Hoiem 2017
;
Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017
;
Benzing 2022
)
);
and
Parameter isolation approaches

(
e.g.,
(
Aljundi et al. 2017
;
Yoon et al. 2018
;
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
)
).

Theoretical results for catastrophic forgetting.

We briefly discuss few related theoretical works.

Doan et al. 2021
analyzed forgetting in linear regression (or, more generally, in the NTK regime).
Their derivations largely depend on a matrix
that captures principal angles between tasks,
very much like we do, but they focus on the smallest angle,
i.e.,
the Dixmier angle
(
Dixmier 1949
)
.
They conclude that increased task similarity leads to more forgetting.
In contrast, our
T\!=\!2
analysis reveals the precise role of task similarity at different training stages (see Figure
3
).
Their work
(
Doan et al. 2021
)
and others
(
Bennani et al. 2020
;
Farajtabar et al. 2020
)
also analyzed the OGD algorithm, deriving generalization guarantees and improved variants.

Lee et al. 2021
considered a teacher-student two-layer setup with
2
tasks, each having its own last layer.
They analyzed a different notion of task similarity
(
i.e.,
teacher similarity) and studied how it affects forgetting in student models.
They found that intermediate (teacher-)similarity leads to the greatest forgetting.
In our linear setup, we find that when two consecutive tasks are shown
once
(
i.e.,
in a single cycle),
intermediate principal angles cause higher forgetting
(see Figure
3
).

Asanuma et al. 2021
considered a linear regression setting, as we do, yet only for
2
tasks having a different teacher.
They assumed specific axis-aligned input distributions,
and studied forgetting in the infinite-dimension limit, taking into account both the input-space and weight-space similarities.
Under a “shared” teacher, their results imply zero forgetting due to their assumptions on inputs.

Alternating projections (AP).

Throughout our paper, we lay out connections between continual learning and the AP literature (see survey by
(
Ginat 2018
)
).
Our cyclic setting (Section
5
) is a special case of cyclic AP (
Von Neumann 1949
;
Halperin 1962
).
Studies from this field often bound the convergence to the subspace intersection either asymptotically or using notions of generalized angles between subspaces
(
e.g.,
Kayalar and Weinert 1988
;
Deutsch and Hundal 1997
;
Oppenheim 2018
).
Most results in this area are uninformative at the worst case.

Kaczmarz method.

Our fitting procedure (described in Section
2.3
and
3
)
shares a great resemblance with
Kaczmarz methods
for solving a system
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\!=\!\boldsymbol{b}
.
The “basic” method
(
Kaczmarz 1937
)

corresponds to tasks of rank
r\!=\!1
,
while the block variant
(
Elfving 1980
)
corresponds to tasks having
r\!\geq\!2
.
Traditionally, these methods used a deterministic cyclic ordering, like we do in Section
5
.
There is also a randomized variant
(
Strohmer and Vershynin 2009
;
Needell 2010
;
Xiang and Zhang 2017
)
,
related
to our random task ordering in Section
6
, which often achieves faster convergence both empirically and theoretically
Sun and Ye 2019
.
Studies of these methods often analyze convergence to a feasible set using the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{A}
(
e.g.,
its condition number; see

Needell et al. 2014
;
Oswald and Zhou 2015
;
Haddock and Ma 2021
).

Our convergence analysis is inherently different.

Most convergence results from the AP and Kaczmarz research areas
concentrate on bounding the convergence to the subspace intersection.
These results
are
valuable for the continual setting,

e.g.,
they help us bound
\|{\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\|
in Theorem
8
, which clearly upper bounds the forgetting.
However, most convergence results in these areas depend on the task specifics, precluding any informative
worst-case
analysis.
In contrast, we analyze the convergence of the
forgetting
(
i.e.,
projection residuals),
allowing us to derive
uniform
worst-case guarantees.
In Section
5.1
, we demonstrate these differences by comparing the two quantities.
Finally, we believe our bounds are novel and can
provide a new perspective in these areas,
especially in the cyclic block Kaczmarz setting
Elfving 1980
;
Needell and Tropp 2014
,

i.e.,
that
worst-case
last-iterate analyses become feasible when examining the residual convergence instead of the distance from
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
.

Normalized Least-Mean-Square (NLMS).

The NLMS algorithm is a popular choice for adaptive filtering.
In its basic form,
it fits one random sample at each iteration using update rules identical to those of our stochastic setting and the randomized Kaczmarz method.
There are known convergence guarantees for the single-sample algorithm

Slock 1993

and its multiple-samples counterpart, the APA
Sankaran and Beex 2000
,
but these are proven only under limiting assumptions on the input signals.

Other optimization methods.

It is interesting to note that when fitting
T
tasks in a cyclic or random ordering, our update rule in Eq. (
2
)
is equivalent to dual block coordinate descent on the dual of Eq. (
3
),

i.e.,
{\argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\big({\,\tfrac{1}{2}\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1:T}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\big\|^{2}-\,\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,}\big)
,
where
\boldsymbol{X}_{1:T}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and

\boldsymbol{y}_{1:T}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N}
(for
N\!=\!\sum_{m=1}^{T}n_{m}
) are the concatenation of the data matrices and label vectors from all tasks.
The primal variable
\boldsymbol{w}
and the dual one
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
are related through
\boldsymbol{w}\!=\!\boldsymbol{X}_{1:T}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Shalev-Shwartz and
Zhang 2013
analyzed the stochastic dual coordinate ascent method for minimizing a
regularized
loss,
and proved convergence rates for the primal suboptimality. However, when the regularization parameter vanishes, as in our case, their bounds tend to infinity.
Others
Sun and Ye 2019
analyzed the convergence of coordinate descent on quadratic functions like the dual above, and derived data-dependent bounds.

The stochastic proximal point algorithm (SPPA)
Ryu and Boyd 2016
;
Bertsekas 2011
;
Patrascu and Necoara 2017
follows an update rule (for a step size
\eta
)
of

{\boldsymbol{w}_{t}\!=\!\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}}\!\Big({\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}\!-\!\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)})^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2\eta}\left\|\boldsymbol{w}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}\right\|^{2}}\Big)}
,
equivalent when
\eta\!\to\!\infty

to our rule in Eq. (
1
).
As far as we know, no uniform convergence-rates were previously proved for
SPPA.

Minimizing forgetting over
T
tasks can also be seen as a finite sum minimization problem
Woodworth and Srebro 2016
;
Negiar et al. 2020
.
Many algorithms have been proposed for this problem, some of which achieve better convergence rates than ours using
additional
memory
(
e.g.,
for storing gradients
Johnson and Zhang 2013
;
Allen-Zhu 2018
).
We derive forgetting convergence rates for the fitting procedure in Eq. (
1
)
that is equivalent to running (S)GD to convergence for each presented task, which is a natural choice for continual learning settings.

Forgetting vs. regret.

Our Definition
3
of the forgetting should not be confused with the notion of regret,
mainly studied in the context of online learning
(
e.g.,
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2012
)
).
Specifically, using our notations, the average regret after
k
iteration is
\frac{1}{k}{\sum}_{t=1}^{k}\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}_{t-1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}}\|^{2}
.
Comparing the two quantities, we note that forgetting quantifies degradation on
previous
tasks,
while regret captures the ability to predict
future
tasks.
To illustrate the differences, consider a finite sequence of orthogonal tasks.
In this case, the forgetting is
0
(as discussed in Section
4.1
), but the regret is large. Conversely, given a task sequence like in Figure
2
, when
k\to\infty
the regret vanishes while forgetting goes to
1
(see Theorem
7
).
Nevertheless, in the cyclic and random settings,
both quantities will go to
0
when
k\to\infty
since we converge to an offline solution.
However, their rates of convergence may differ.

8
Conclusion

Catastrophic forgetting is not yet fully understood theoretically.
Therefore, one must first study it in the simplest model exhibiting this phenomenon — linear regression.
In this setting, we provide sharp uniform worst-case bounds.
Most of our analysis does not depend on task specifics,
but only on the number of tasks
T
,
the number of iterations
k
, and the task ordering.
On the one hand, we prove that for an arbitrary ordering, forgetting
can
be catastrophic.
On the other hand, for cyclic orderings,
we prove forgetting
cannot
be catastrophic
and that even in the worst-case it vanishes at most as

\frac{T^{2}}{\sqrt{k}}
or
\frac{T^{2}d}{k}
.
Lastly, we prove worst-case bounds for random orderings,
independent of
T
.

Our bounds complement existing Kaczmarz and alternating projection bounds, which focus on convergence to a feasible set.
Unlike ours, their bounds strictly depend on task specifics
(
e.g.,
principal angles or condition numbers)
and
become trivial in worst-case analysis.

There are many intriguing directions for future research.
These include extending our results to the non-realizable case,
analyzing forgetting in classification tasks,
and deriving optimal presentation task orderings.
Lastly, one can try to extend our results to more complex models
(
e.g.,
deep models)
and other training algorithms.
We hope a thorough theoretical understanding of catastrophic forgetting, will facilitate the development of new practical methods to alleviate it.

A.2
Useful general properties

Following are several
useful inequalities and properties that will facilitate our proofs. We only work with
real
matrices, so we often use the transpose and the Hermitian transpose interchangeably.

Lemma 16
.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\!\in\!\mathbb{C}^{d}
, it holds that
\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\!+\!\dots\!+\!\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\right\|^{2}\leq T\!\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\|^{2}+\dots+\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\right\|^{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle 0\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right\|^{2}=\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}-2\text{Re}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{*}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right)+\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right\|^{2}\iff 2\text{Re}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{*}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right)\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right\|^{2}\,.

Now, we see use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and show

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\dots+\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{i>j}2\text{Re}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{*}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{i>j}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{each appears $T$ times}\right]

\displaystyle=T\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\|^{2}=T\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\|^{2}+\dots+\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\right\|^{2}\right)~.

∎

Property 1

(Spectral norm properties)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and

\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
.
Then, the spectral norm holds the following properties:

1.

Definition.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|=\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|_{2}\triangleq\max_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\left\{0\right\}}\frac{\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{2}}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\\
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{2}=\sigma_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)
;

2.

Invariance to transposition.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|=\|{\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}}\|
;

3.

Triangle inequality.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{B}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|+\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}}\right\|
;

4.

Squared norm of matrix sum.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}_{1}+\dots+\boldsymbol{A}_{T}}\right\|^{2}\leq T\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}_{i}}\right\|^{2}
;

5.

Multiplicative norm inequality.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|\left\|{\boldsymbol{B}}\right\|
;

6.

Invariance to rotations.

Let
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}\times m}

and
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times n}
be matrices with orthonormal
columns
(
n^{\prime}\geq n,m^{\prime}\geq m
).
Then,

\left\|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|=\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|=\|{\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\top}}\|=\|{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\top}}\|
.

See Chapter 5.2 in
Meyer 2000
for the proofs and for more such properties.
The squared norm inequality follows immediately from
Lemma
16
and the definition of the spectral norm.

Property 2

(Orthogonal projection properties)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{P}}
be a
real
orthogonal-projection linear-operator that projects onto a linear subspace
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an arbitrary vector.
Then,
{\boldsymbol{P}}
holds the following properties:

1.

Geometric definition.

\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in H}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|
;

2.

Symmetry.

{\boldsymbol{P}}={\boldsymbol{P}}^{\top}
;

3.

Idempotence.

{\boldsymbol{P}}^{2}={\boldsymbol{P}}
;

4.

\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}
is also a projection operator, projecting onto the subspace orthogonal to
H
.

Consequentially,
\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}=\boldsymbol{0}
;

5.

Using the above,
we get

\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}=\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}
;

6.

Contraction.

\left\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|
, holding in equality if and only if
{\boldsymbol{P}}\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{v}
;

7.

Singular values.

All the singular values of
{\boldsymbol{P}}
are in
\left\{0,1\right\}
, implying that
\left\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}}\right\|\!=\!1\!\iff\!{\boldsymbol{P}}\!\neq\!\boldsymbol{0}
.

See
Zarantonello 1971
for the proofs and for more properties.

The next corollary stems directly from the properties above
(see Chapter 5.13 in
Meyer 2000
).

Corollary 17

(Projection onto solution spaces)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
be the matrix of task
m
.
Then, the projection onto its solution space,
or equivalently onto its null space,
is given by:

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\!=\!\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!\boldsymbol{X}_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\!=\!\boldsymbol{V}_{m}\left(\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{m}^{+}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{m}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{m}^{\top}\!=\!\boldsymbol{V}_{m}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}^{\perp^{\top}}_{m}
.

A.3
Principal angles between two tasks

We continue our discussion from Section
3
and present principal angles more thoroughly for completeness.
We mostly follow the definitions in
(
Björck and Golub 1973
;
Bargetz et al. 2020
;
Meyer 2000
)
.

Definition 18

(Principal angles between two tasks)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d},\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times d}

be two data matrices, corresponding to two given tasks.
Let
r_{\min}=\min(\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}),\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}))

be their minimal rank.
The principal angles
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r_{\min}}\in\left[0,\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}\right]

between the two tasks,
i.e., the angles between the row spaces of
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
,
are recursively defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\!\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\!=

\displaystyle\argmax\left|{\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}}\right|~~

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt\emph{s.t.}~\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\in\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}),~\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}),~\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}}\right\|=\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}}\right\|=1,~~

\displaystyle\hskip 43.0pt\forall j\!\in\!\left[{i-1}\right]\!:~\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\!\perp\!\boldsymbol{u}_{j},~\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\!\perp\!\boldsymbol{v}_{j}

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=

\displaystyle\arccos\left|{\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}}\right|~.

Notice that according to our definition,
the principal angles hold

\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}\geq\theta_{r_{\min}}\geq\dots\geq\theta_{1}\geq 0
.
Important for our analysis is the fact that
unlike the principal
vectors

\left\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\}_{i}
,
the principal
angles
between two subspaces are uniquely defined

(
Björck and Golub 1973
)
.
Two fundamental principal angles in the field of alternating projections are the minimal principal angle,
i.e.,
the Dixmier angle
(
Dixmier 1949
)
;
and the minimal non-zero principal angle,

i.e.,
the Friedrichs angle
(
Friedrichs 1937
)
.

Figure 5:

Principal angles between subspaces.

Since the two hyperplanes share an intersecting direction,
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
are chosen inside this intersection,
meaning that

{\theta_{1}\!=\!\arccos\left|{\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}}\right|\!=\!\arccos(1)\!=\!0}
.
In this case,
\theta_{1}
is the Dixmier angle.
From the remaining directions that are orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
, the recursive definition chooses two unit vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
, forming a non-zero angle
{\theta_{2}\!=\!\arccos\left|{\boldsymbol{u}^{*}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}}\right|}
.
In this case,
\theta_{2}
is the Friedrichs angle.

Claim 19

(Principal angles between ”equivalent” subspaces)
.

Let
\big({\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}\big),\big({\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}}\big)
be two tasks.
Then, the sets of
non-zero
principal angles between the following pairs of subspaces, are all the same:

1.

The data row-spaces, i.e.,
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})
and
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top})
;

2.

The null spaces, i.e.,
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{2})
;

3.

The affine solution spaces, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{1}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}+\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}=\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}+\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
.

Proof.

The equivalence between (1) and (2) is proven by
Theorem 2.7 of
Knyazev and Argentati 2006

(stating that the
non-zero
principal angles between
two subspaces are essentially the same as those between
their orthogonal complements).
Moreover, since the solution spaces
\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2}
are merely affine subspaces of the null spaces themselves, they induce the exact same principal angles.

It
is
possible however that these pairs of subspaces
do
have a different amount of zero principal angles between them, but this generally does not interfere with our analyses in the appendices.
∎

We now use principal angles to prove a lemma that will facilitate our proofs for Sections
4
and
5
.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
be two data matrices of two tasks
and let the corresponding orthogonal projections onto their null spaces
be

{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{1},~{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{2}}
.
Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
we have that

\displaystyle\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)^{n}}\right\|^{2}=\max_{i}\left\{\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)^{2n-1}(1-\cos^{2}\theta_{i})\right\}~,

where

\{\theta_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\left(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]

are the non-zero principal angles between the two tasks in
S
,
i.e., between
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})
and
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top})
or equivalently
between
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{2})

or between the affine solution spaces
\mathcal{W}_{1}
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}
.

Proof.

We use the SVD notation defined in Appendix
A.1

and denote by
\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp},\,\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}
the matrices whose orthonormal columns span
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}),\,\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{2})
respectively.
Thus, we can express the projections as

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}=\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}
.

Now, we show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)^{n}}\right\|^{2}&=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\right)^{n}}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{n-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}}\right\|^{2}\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\begin{subarray}{c}\text{spectral norm}\\
\text{properties}\end{subarray}\end{subarray}\right]&=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{n-1}}\right\|^{2}~.\end{split}

We now notice that the idempotence of
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}

and

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}=\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}

implies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}-\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}-\left({\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}}\right)^{2}~.

Since for the spectral norm of any real matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
it holds that

\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|^{2}=\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|
,
we get that

\displaystyle\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{n-1}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{n-1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)^{2}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{n-1}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{2n-1}-\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\right)^{2n}}\right\|~.

Denote the spectral decomposition of the
Gram matrix

\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}

as

\boldsymbol{Q}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}
,
with its (non-negative) eigenvalues ordered in a non-ascending order on the diagonal of
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
being some orthonormal matrix.
The upper bound thus becomes

\displaystyle\left\|{\big({\boldsymbol{Q}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}}\big)^{2n-1}-\big({\boldsymbol{Q}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}}\big)^{2n}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{2n-1}-{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{2n}}\right\|~.

\displaystyle=\max_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2n-1}(1-\lambda_{i})=\max_{i}\left\{\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)^{2n-1}(1-\cos^{2}\theta_{i})\right\}~,

where

\{\theta_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\left[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]

are all the principal angles (zeros included)
between
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{2})
.
The last equality stems from a known analysis result
(Theorem 2.1 in
Risteski and Trencevski 2001
)
relating the principal angles
between
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{2})

to the eigenvalues of the Gram matrix we defined,

i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp}\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}=\boldsymbol{Q}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}}
.
More formally, the result states
that
\forall i
,
\lambda_{i}=\cos^{2}\theta_{i}
.
See also Chapter 5.15 in
Meyer 2000
for more detailed explanations on this relation.

We conclude this proof by using
Claim
19
showing that the
non-zero
principal angles between

\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{1})
and
\kernel(\boldsymbol{X}_{2})

are essentially the same as those between
their orthogonal complements,

i.e.,
\range(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}_{1})
and
\range(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}_{2})
.
∎

We first state an auxiliary lemma for deriving lower bounds on the forgetting of tasks of rank

Lemma 21

(Lower bound on forgetting in the

.

Let
S\in\mathcal{S}_{T}
be a task collection with
T
data matrices of rank

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}_{T}

be the normalized vectors spanning the
T
rank-

solution spaces
\mathcal{W}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{W}_{T}

Let
\theta_{\tau\left({i,j}\right)}\in\left[0,\pi/2\right]

be the angle between
\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(j)}
,
\forall i,j\in\left[{k}\right]
,
meaning that

\cos^{2}\theta_{\tau\left({i,j}\right)}\!=\!\big({\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(i)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(j)}}\big)^{2}
.
Finally, let
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
be the minimum norm offline solution of
S

Then, the forgetting on
S

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}(k)\geq\min_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\big\{\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\big\}\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(1)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{k}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\cos^{2}\theta_{\tau\left({i,i+1}\right)}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(1-\cos^{2}\theta_{\tau\left({j,k}\right)}\right)~,

where
\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})
is the smallest squared non-zero singular value of
\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
.

Proof for Lemma
21
.

We start from Eq. (
8
)
and perform similar derivations to the ones that lead us to Eq. (
9
).
We have,

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)&=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{pseudo-inverse}\end{subarray}\right]&=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\big({\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}}\big){{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\\

\text{norm}\\
\text{properties}\end{subarray}\right]&\geq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)})\big\|{{\big({\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}}\big)}{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(k)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(1)}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big\|^{2}\vskip-2.84526pt\\

where we used the rank-1 SVD of the data matrices,

i.e.,
\boldsymbol{X}_{m}=\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{m}^{\top}

Now, we denote

\forall i,j:\left|{\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(i)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(j)}}\right|=\theta_{i,j}

and get

\displaystyle=\!\min_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\!\!\big\{\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\big\}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\bigg\|{\big({\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(t)}^{\top}}\big)\left({\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\cos\theta_{\tau\left({i,i+1}\right)}\!}\right)\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}^{\tau(k)}\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}^{\tau(1)^{\top}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\bigg\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{idempotence}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\!\min_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\!\!\big\{\sigma_{\min}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{m})\big\}\frac{({\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau(1)}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}})^{2}}{k}\!\left({\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\cos^{2}\theta_{\tau\left({i,i+1}\right)}\!}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\!\!\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}^{\tau(k)^{\top}}\!\!\!\left(\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}^{\tau(j)}\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}^{\tau(j)^{\top}}\right)\!\boldsymbol{v}_{\perp}^{\tau(k)}~

B.1
No forgetting cases (Section
4.1
)

Consider learning two tasks sequentially:

\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\big)

and then
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\big)
.
Right after learning the second task,
we have

\mathcal{L}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\!=\!\|{\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\!-\!\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{w}_{2}}\|^{2}\!=\!0
.
Thus, the forgetting from Eq. (
8
) becomes

{F_{\tau,S}(2)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}
.
We now derive sufficient and necessary conditions for no forgetting.

Recall Theorem
6
.
Let

S\!=\!\left\{\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\big),\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\big)\right\}\!\in\!\mathcal{S}_{T=2}

be a task collection with
2
tasks,
fitted under an identity ordering
\tau
,

i.e.,
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\big)
and then
\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\big)
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

For
any
labeling
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}

(or equivalently, any minimum norm solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
),
after fitting the second task, the model does not “forget” the first one.
That is,

F_{\tau,S}(2)=0
.

2.

It holds that

\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}_{n_{1}\times d}
.

3.

Each principal angle between the tasks,
i.e.,
\range({\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}})
and

\range({\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}})
, is either
0
or
\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}
.

B.1.1
Example: Sufficient conditions for no forgetting

Before we prove the theorem above,
we exemplify some of its implications by showing clear and simple sufficient conditions for holding the conditions of the theorem.

(a)

\mathrm{range}\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}\big)\subseteq\mathrm{range}\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}\big)
;
or

\mathrm{range}\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}\big)\supseteq\mathrm{range}\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}\big)
;
or

(b)

\mathrm{range}\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}\big)\subseteq\kernel\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\big)
;
or

\mathrm{range}\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}\big)\supseteq\kernel\big(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\big)
.

These conditions can help understand that maximal task (=sample) similarity or dissimilarity can help prevent forgetting in the linear setting.

B.1.2
Proving the theorem

Proof.

Like we explain in Section
4.1
,
the forgetting after learning the second task is equal to

{F_{\tau,S}(2)=\mathcal{L}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}
.
We notice that
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}

and

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
,
meaning that the labels
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
do not have any effect on the matrix

\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}
.
However, these labels
do
effect the minimum norm solution
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
.

Here, we briefly discuss the relation between
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
,
so as to facilitate our proof below.

Relating the minimum norm solution and the labelings.

Recall the constraints between the offline solution and the labels,
i.e.,
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
.
Also recall that under Assumption
2
, we have that
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
.
Note that a minimum norm solution
must
lie in the row span of
both
data matrices,

i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\in\range({\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top}})\cup\range({\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top}})}
,
since any contributions from the nullspaces will not affect its predictions
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
but
will
increase its norm.

Moreover,
notice that the
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
can yield
any
minimum norm solution that is inside
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})
, since we could just
choose an arbitrary vector
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\in\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})

and set
\boldsymbol{y}_{2}=\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}
(we do not have restrictions on the labelings).

We are now ready to complete our proof.

Condition (1)
\iff
Condition (2).

Clearly, since
{F_{\tau,S}(2)\!=\!\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}
,
we have that

\underbrace{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}}_{\text{(2)}}~\Longrightarrow~\underbrace{\forall\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}:~F_{\tau,S}(2)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}=0}_{\text{(1)}}~.

Since
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}
is a symmetric operator projecting onto the row span of
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
, we have that

\range({\boldsymbol{P}}_{1})\!=\!\range({\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}^{\top})\!=\!\range(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}_{1})
.
It is readily seen that

\left({\forall\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\!\in\range(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}_{1}):\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|\!=\!0}\right)~\iff~\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}~.

We explicitly denote the minimum norm solution that two labelings
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
induce as

\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2})
.
As explained,
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}
can yield
any
minimum solution inside
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})
.
Assume
\forall\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\!:\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{y}1,\boldsymbol{y}2)}\right\|\!=\!0
.
Then, it follows that

\forall\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\!\in\range(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}_{1}):\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|\!=\!0
.
In this case we proved that it follows that
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Overall we showed:

\underbrace{\left({\forall\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\!\in\range(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}_{1}):\,\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\right\|\!=\!0}\right)}_{\text{(1)}}~\Longrightarrow~\underbrace{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{0}}_{\text{(2)}}~,

thus completing the proof for (1)
\iff
(2).

Condition (2)
\iff
Condition (3).

First, we notice that

\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\boldsymbol{0}\iff\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\boldsymbol{0}

since simple norm properties give us:

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\right\|}_{>0}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|\propto\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|~,

and

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\right\|}_{>0}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\propto\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\boldsymbol{V}_{1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|.

Then, we use Lemma
20

and get that

\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\max_{i}\left\{\left(\cos^{2}\theta_{i}\right)(1-\cos^{2}\theta_{i})\right\}=\frac{1}{4}\max_{i}\left\{\sin^{2}(2\theta_{i})\right\}~,

where

\{\theta_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\left(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]

are the non-zero principal angles between the two tasks,

i.e.,
between
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{\top})
and
\range(\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{\top})
.
Finally, it is now clear that

\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\boldsymbol{0}\iff\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{2}\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|=\frac{1}{4}\max_{i}\left\{\sin^{2}(2\theta_{i})\right\}=\boldsymbol{0}\iff\forall i:\theta_{i}\in\left\{0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}~.

∎

B.1.3
Comparison to
Doan et al. 2021

Doan et al. 2021
also studied forgetting in a linear setting where a series of tasks are learned sequentially by SGD on squared loss with ridge penalty.
In the special case where only
T=2
tasks are given
and no regularization is used (
i.e.,
\lambda=0
),
their expression for forgetting in Theorem 1
(
Doan et al. 2021
)
is equivalent to our derivation in Eq. (
8
) up to scaling by
\frac{1}{2}
.
However, their subsequent upper bound stated in Corollary 1 is a looser characterization of forgetting, which can be paraphrased in terms of our notation and framework as follows
(for convenience, we attach their notations beneath the last equation):

\begin{split}F_{\tau,S}(2)&=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{+}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}){\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\!-\!w^{\star})}\right\|^{2}\\
&\overset{(*)}{=}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}}){\boldsymbol{P}}^{(1)}_{\perp}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\!-\!\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})\|^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\|^{2}\big\|{\underbrace{({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}})({\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}})}_{\Theta^{1\to 2}}}\big\|^{2}\big\|{\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})}_{=\boldsymbol{M}_{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{2}}}\big\|^{2},\end{split}

(11)

where (*) follows from
plugging in

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}={\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}-\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{I}
and
(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}){\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}=0.

Based on the above upper bound, the authors informally argue that higher similarity of the principal components between the source task
and target task leads to higher risk of forgetting. In contrast,
the sufficient condition (a) in our Appendix
B.1.1

shows that there is no forgetting when tasks have maximum overlap in the row spans of their inputs.

Concretely, consider the following two tasks that hold
the sufficient conditions of Theorem
6

presented in Appendix
B.1.1
:

\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(1)}=[1,0,0,0],~\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1]\right),~~~\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{(2)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&0&0&0\\
0&1&0&0\end{array}\right],~\boldsymbol{y}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\end{array}\right]\right)~.

Note that
\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,1,0,0]^{\top}
is a unit norm linear predictor that realizes both tasks. In this case, there is clearly no forgetting as the first task is also part of second task,
i.e.,
F_{\tau,S}(2)=0
.
However, we can verify that
\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\|\|{\boldsymbol{P}}^{(1)}{\boldsymbol{P}}^{(2)}\|=1
and
\|{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star})\|=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}
, thus the upper bound in Eq. (
11
) evaluates to
F_{\tau,S}(2)\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
. This demonstrates the weakness of the upper bound in Corollary
1
of
Doan et al. 2021
.

This gap can also be seen from a principal angle perspective.
Their so-called overlap matrix,

i.e.,
\Theta^{1\to 2}
,
is a diagonal matrix holding the singular values
of
\boldsymbol{V}_{2}^{\perp^{\top}}\boldsymbol{V}_{1}^{\perp}
.
As they explain in Corollary 1 and we explain in
the proof of Lemma
20
,
these singular values are actually connected to the principal angles between
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
.
Thus, when they use the spectral norm
\left\|{\Theta^{1\to 2}}\right\|
which equals
1
, they are actually using
only
the largest singular values,
i.e.,
the
smallest
principal angle which is called the Dimixer angle
(
Dixmier 1949
)
.
In the above example, this angle, which is the only principal angle, is zero (thus holding our conditions from Theorem
6
).
In contrast, our analysis in Lemma
9
uses
all

principal angles, revealing more delicate effects of task similarity on forgetting dynamics.

B.2

Recall Theorem
7
.
When using the identity ordering
(
i.e.,
\tau\!\left(t\right)\!=t
),
thus seeing each task once,
the worst-case forgetting after

i.e.,

\displaystyle 1-\!\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T=k}\end{subarray}}\!\!F_{\tau,S}(k)\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{k}}}\right)~.

Recall Figure

Sequence of tasks where

.
Each black arrow represents a solution space of a rank

\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
There are

k_{1}
tasks between
[0,\theta]

and

Proof sketch.

We show that for any
\epsilon>0

S

such that under the identity ordering
\tau

We construct a task sequence as follows
(illustrated in the figure in 2 dimensions):

1.

For some small angle
\theta\sim\sqrt{\epsilon}

;

2.

We add another

\left(\theta,\pi/2\right]
.

With this construction we show that on the one hand there is almost no contraction, but on the other hand there is a large forgetting especially on the first

We note in passing
a related phenomenon in quantum physics,
known as
the
quantum Zeno effect

(
Hacohen-Gourgy et al. 2018
)
,
where the state of a quantum system, described by a vector, can be manipulated by applying infinitesimally-spaced measurements, which act on it as orthogonal projections.

The full proof of Theorem

is given below.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

We show that for any
\epsilon\in\left(0,1\right)

there exists a task collection
S
of
k=\mathcal{O}\left({1}/{\epsilon^{2}}\right)

tasks such that under the identity ordering,

.
From Lemma
21
we know
that for any choice of a task collection
S
of rank

,

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\!\geq\!\frac{1}{k}\!\left(\prod_{m=1}^{k-1}\cos^{2}\theta_{\tau\left({m,m+1}\right)}\right)\!\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\!\left(1\!-\!\cos^{2}\theta_{\tau\left({m,k}\right)}\right)\!=\!\frac{1}{k}\!\left(\prod_{m=1}^{k-1}\cos^{2}\theta_{m,m+1}\right)\!\!\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}\sin^{2}\theta_{m,k}.

(12)

We construct a sequence with
T=k=\nicefrac{{72}}{{\epsilon^{2}}}+1
tasks as following: let
\theta=\theta\left(\epsilon\right)\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

and assume we have
k_{1}=k_{1}\left(\epsilon\right)
tasks uniformly
in
\left[0,\theta\right]
and
k_{2}=k_{2}\left(\epsilon\right)

tasks uniformly in
\left(\theta,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
. Note that we require

k_{1}+k_{2}=k
.

Then from Eq. (
12
) we have

\displaystyle F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\cos^{2(k_{1}-1)}\left(\frac{\theta}{k_{1}-1}\right)\cos^{2k_{2}}\left(\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta}{k_{2}}\right)\Bigg({\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}-1}\sin^{2}\!\!\underbrace{\theta_{i,k}}_{\geq\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}-\theta}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=k_{1}}^{k-1}\sin^{2}\theta_{i,k}}_{\geq 0}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\cos^{2k_{1}-2}\left({\frac{\theta}{k_{1}-1}}\right)\cos^{2k_{2}}\left(\frac{\pi-2\theta}{2k_{2}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k_{1}-1}\sin^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\cos^{2k_{1}-2}\left({\frac{\theta}{k_{1}-1}}\right)\cos^{2k_{2}}\left(\frac{\pi-2\theta}{2k_{2}}\right)\left(\left({k_{1}-1}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\cos^{2k_{1}-2}\left(\frac{\theta}{k_{1}-1}\right)}_{\triangleq F_{1}}~\cdot~\underbrace{\cos^{2k_{2}}\left(\frac{\pi-2\theta}{2k_{2}}\right)}_{\triangleq F_{2}}~\cdot~\underbrace{\frac{k_{1}-1}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\cos^{2}\theta}_{\triangleq F_{3}}~.

We show that for any
\epsilon\in\left(0,1\right)

we can choose
k_{1},k_{2},\theta
such that

F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\geq F_{1}\left(k_{1},\theta\right)\cdot F_{2}\left(k_{2},\theta\right)\cdot F_{3}\left(k_{1},k_{2},\theta\right)>1-\epsilon~.

Specifically, let

k_{1}=\frac{72-12\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}}+1,~~~~~~~k_{2}=\frac{12}{\epsilon},~~~~~~~\theta=\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{6}}
.

Then, we
use the inequality
\cos x\geq 1-x^{2}/2
,
and get

\displaystyle F_{1}\!\left(k_{1},\theta\right)

\displaystyle\!=\!\cos^{2(k_{1}-1)}\left(\frac{\theta}{k_{1}-1}\right)\geq\cos^{2(k_{1}-1)}\left(\frac{1}{k_{1}-1}\right)\!\geq\!\left(1-\frac{1}{2(k_{1}-1)^{2}}\right)^{2k_{1}-2}

\displaystyle\!\geq\!1\!-\!\frac{1}{k_{1}-1}=1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{72-12\epsilon}\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}

\displaystyle F_{2}\!\left(k_{2},\theta\right)

\displaystyle\!=\!\cos^{2k_{2}}\left(\frac{\pi-2\theta}{2k_{2}}\right)\!\geq\!\cos^{2k_{2}}\left(\frac{2}{k_{2}}\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{2}{k_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2k_{2}}\!\geq\!1-\frac{4}{k_{2}}=1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}

\displaystyle F_{3}\!\left(k_{1},k_{2},\theta\right)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{k_{1}-1}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\!\cos^{2}\theta\geq\frac{\frac{72-12\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}}}{\frac{72-12\epsilon}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{12}{\epsilon}+1}\left(1\!-\!\frac{\epsilon}{6}\right)\!=\!\frac{72-12\epsilon}{72+\epsilon^{2}}\left(1\!-\!\frac{\epsilon}{6}\right)\geq 1\!-\!\frac{\epsilon}{3}~.

Therefore, we get that

F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\geq F_{1}\left(k_{1},\theta\right)\cdot F_{2}\left(k_{2},\theta\right)\cdot F_{3}\left(k_{1},k_{2},\theta\right)\geq\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{3}\right)^{3}\geq 1-\epsilon
.

Finally note that
k=k_{1}+k_{2}=\nicefrac{{72}}{{\epsilon^{2}}}+1
, which
concludes the proof.
∎

Recall Theorem
10
.
For a cyclic ordering
\tau
of

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{T=2}\end{subarray}}\!\!F_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)=\frac{1}{2e\left(k-1\right)}-\frac{1}{4e\left(k-1\right)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{3}}\right)~.

C.2.1
Proving the upper bound

We prove the upper bound using the two following lemmas. The proofs of the lemmas are given on the following pages.

Generally, the proofs revolve around the quantity
\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}

that we bound both with and without using the dimension of the tasks.

Lemma 22

(Dimension-independent upper bounds)
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

be
T

Then:

(

For any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
m\in\left[{T-1}\right]

\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq m\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)~;

(
Lemma
lem:dimension_independentb)

For any
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
m\in\left[{T-1}\right]
, it holds that

\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}\!-\!\boldsymbol{M}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq T\!\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)~\!;

(

For any vector
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
holding

\left\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|\leq 1
,
after
n\geq 1
cycles, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right\|~;

(

The forgetting on task

\displaystyle\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\right)\boldsymbol{M}^{n}}\right\|^{2}\leq 2m\left\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right\|~;

(
Lemma

For any number of cycles

\left\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n}}\right\|\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{T}}{{n}}}

Moreover, when

is

\left\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n}}\right\|\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{e(n-1)}}

Lemma 23

(Rank-dependent upper bound)
.

For any vector
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}~\leq~\frac{\operatorname{rank}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)}{n}~\leq~\frac{d}{n}~.

A symmetric cyclic operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}&=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\ell+1}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\ell}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\\

\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{idempotence}\end{subarray}\right]&=T\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\left(\left\|{\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\ell}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{\ell+1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\right)\right)\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]&=T\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\right)~.\end{split}

∎

Remark.

We note in passing that after completing our proof above,
we found that a similar proof was already presented in
Netyanun and Solmon 2006
while discussing Kakutani’s lemma.
We still brought our proof here for the sake of completeness.
Moreover, they showed that for a non-expansive
self-adjoint positive semi-definite
operator
\boldsymbol{M}
, the factor
T
can be alleviated from the inequality.
That is,

\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\leq\cancel{T}\left(\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|^{2}\right)
.
But clearly this does not suit our general cyclic operators which are not necessarily self-adjoint.
Indeed, our proof for
(Lemma lem:dimension_independente)
yields a similar conclusion for
symmetric
cyclic operators.

Proof for
(Lemma lem:dimension_independentc)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
First, we notice that since
\boldsymbol{M}

is a non-expansive operator,
we have that

\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}
.
In turn, this means

\displaystyle 0\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\Big({\underbrace{\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\right)^{*}\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}_{\succeq{\boldsymbol{0}}~\text{because it is clearly symmetric as well}}}\Big)\boldsymbol{u}~.

Then, we use the fact that for any positive semi-definite matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}
,
we have that

\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u}\leq\lambda_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)=\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|
,
and get

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\right)^{*}\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right\|

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{B}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}=\left({\boldsymbol{A}^{*}-\boldsymbol{B}^{*}}\right)\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{B}^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{B}}\right)\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right)+\left({\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}-\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\right)^{*}}\right)\boldsymbol{M}^{n}}\right\|

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{triangle inequality}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right)}\right\|+\left\|{\left({\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}-\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\right)^{*}}\right)\boldsymbol{M}^{n}}\right\|

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}~\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}^{*}}\right\|=\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\right\|~\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\right)^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right)}\right\|

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{contraction}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}}\right\|~.

∎

Proof for Lemma
23
.

We follow a proof by

fedja https://mathoverflow.net/users/1131/fedja
.

Define the series of matrices
\left\{\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right\}
,
where
\boldsymbol{B}_{n}=\boldsymbol{M}^{n^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}
.
Notice that it holds that
\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\succeq 0
(since it is symmetric and
\forall\boldsymbol{v}:\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\boldsymbol{v}=\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
).

Furthermore,
Von Neumann’s trace inequality
and the fact that
\boldsymbol{M}
is a contraction operator, imply that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n+1}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\big({\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\boldsymbol{M}}\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big({\underbrace{\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}}_{\succeq 0}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{B}_{n}}_{\succeq 0}}\big)\leq\big\|{\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{M}^{\top}}\big\|_{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)~,

meaning that the sequence

\left\{\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)\right\}_{n}

is monotonically non-increasing.
Hence,
we get that

\displaystyle\begin{split}n\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)&\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell}-\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell+1^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell+1}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell}\right)-\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell+1^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{\ell+1}\right)\right)\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]&=\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{{}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}\right)-\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\right)}_{\geq 0}\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{{}^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{contraction}}}{{\leq}}\operatorname{rank}{\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)}\\
\Longrightarrow\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)&\leq\frac{\operatorname{rank}{\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)}}{n}~.\end{split}

We are now ready to conclude this lemma,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{\boldsymbol{v}:\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\left(\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)&=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}:\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{n^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{n}-\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1^{\top}}\boldsymbol{M}^{n+1}\right)\boldsymbol{v}\\
&=\max_{\boldsymbol{v}:\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{2}=1}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{B}_{n}}_{\succeq 0}\boldsymbol{v}=\underbrace{\lambda_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)}_{\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{B}_{n}\right)\leq\frac{\operatorname{rank}{\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)}}{n}~.\end{split}

∎

Remarks.

For the sake of completeness, we briefly discuss our result above.
First, this result is useful for our derivations since our cyclic operator
\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{T}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)
is essentially a contraction operator.
In fact, being a product of projections,
our cyclic operator has great expressiveness.
It was shown by
Oikhberg 1999
that
any
contraction operator can be decomposed into a product of sufficiently,
though sometimes infinitely, many projections.
This explains our interest in analyzing “general” contraction operators.

Finally, we should mention that the bound derived here is sharp (up to a constant).
For instance, for the Toeplitz operator
\boldsymbol{M}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}&1\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&1\\
1-\epsilon\end{smallmatrix}\right]
,
a proper choice of
\epsilon
can yield a rate of

\nicefrac{{d}}{{en}}
for the quantity we bound in the lemma above.
However, such an operator cannot be expressed as a product of a
finite
number of projection operators.
One can approximate this operator
arbitrarily-well
by replacing the
1
s with
\beta\in\left({0,1}\right)
.
However, we were not able to use this approximation to improve our lower bound
in Theorem
11
, since

\beta<1
implies a geometric contraction of

all
elements at every cycle and requires a very large number of constructing projections (
i.e.,
tasks).

.
That is, we bound the expected forgetting by

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{\tau,S}\left(k\right)\!\triangleq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\!\bigg[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\!\Big\|{\boldsymbol{X}_{\tau(t)}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}-\boldsymbol{y}_{\tau(t)}}\Big\|^{2}\bigg]\!\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\bigg[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\!\Big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}\right){{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(1\right)}}}\Big\|^{2}\bigg]~.

(18)

D.1
Detour: Rephrasing and bounding a more natural expression

D.1.1
Rephrasing

Before we will bound the upper bound above,
we start by bounding a slightly different
quantity which is easier to work with and can be seen as a straightforward MSE loss over
T
tasks (as studied in NLMS papers,
e.g.,
Slock 1993
;
Sankaran and Beex 2000
).
Instead of averaging the forgetting (
i.e.,
residuals) over
previously-seen
tasks,
we average over
all
tasks in the collection
S
.
That is, we start by bounding.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\bigg[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau,{\boldsymbol{P}}}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}~,

(19)

where
\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}}
means averaging over all
T
projection matrices w.r.t. a uniform distribution.

To further ease reading,
we slightly abuse notation
such that instead of explicitly sampling a task ordering
\tau
,
we simply uniformly sample
k+1
orthogonal projection operators,
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}
from
some i.i.d. uniform distribution
(
e.g.,
uniformly from a finite set of
T
such operators).
Finally, we obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}~.

(20)

Next, we will bound Eq. (
20
) which is equivalent to Eq. (
19
).
Then, we will use Eq. (
19
)
to bound the right hand side of
Eq. (
18
)
and conclude an upper bound for the expected forgetting, as required.

D.1.2
Bounding Eq. (
20
)

We begin by using a known inequality between the spectral norm and the Frobenius norm, that is,

{\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\right\|_{2}^{2}\!=\!\lambda_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\right)\leq\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\right)\!=\left\|\boldsymbol{A}\right\|_{F}^{2}}
,
and present an easier, but perhaps looser, surrogate quantity
f(k)
which we will bound:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]\triangleq f\!\left(k\right)~.

(21)

Key to our following derivations, is the fact that all the projection matrices including
{\boldsymbol{P}}
are identically distributed, allowing us to freely change variables. This property, together with the linearity of the expectation and trace operators, facilitates our derivations.

We now notice that the bound we got is
non-increasing
in
k
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle f\!\left(k-1\right)-f\!\left(k\right)=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k-1},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k-1}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k-1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]-

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{identically}\\
\text{distributed}\end{subarray}\right]=

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}\right]-

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\left[\text{linearity}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}}\Bigg[\operatorname{tr}\bigg[\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}}_{\triangleq\boldsymbol{M}}\bigg]-

\displaystyle\hskip 55.48277pt\operatorname{tr}\bigg[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{2}}_{\triangleq\boldsymbol{M}}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\bigg]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\bigg[\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\left[\boldsymbol{M}\right]-\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]}_{\geq 0}\bigg]\geq 0~,

where in the last inequality we (again) used Von Neumann’s trace inequality
to show that

\operatorname{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left({\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\big({\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}_{\succeq 0}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{M}}_{\succeq 0}}\big)\leq\big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}\big\|_{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\boldsymbol{M}\right)~.

Since the bounds are non-increasing,
we bound
f\!\left(k\right)
at iteration k, using

f\!\left(1\right),\dots,f\!\left(k\right)
:

\displaystyle f\left(k\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{t\in\left[k\right]}f\left(t\right)\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}f\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}{}_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]-\!\!\!\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}{}_{t}{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{identically}\\
\text{distributed}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]-\!\!\!\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t+1}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}{}_{t+1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{telescoping}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]-\underbrace{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}}\operatorname{tr}\left[{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}{}_{k+1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right]}_{\geq 0,\text{ because ${\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}{}_{k+1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\succeq\boldsymbol{0}$}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}}\operatorname{rank}\left({\boldsymbol{P}}\right)=\frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}(d-\operatorname{rank}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\right))\triangleq\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k}~,

and conclude that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\bigg[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{m}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{eq:expected-average}\&\ref{eq:random-projections}}}{{\equiv}}\!\!\!\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{eq:trace_bound}}}{{\leq}}f\!\left(k\right)\leq\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k}~.

D.2
Back to the expected forgetting

Recall
that our initial goal in Eq. (
18
) was to bound

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\Big[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\!\big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau(t)}\right){{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(1\right)}}}\big\|^{2}\Big]
.
Instead, we started by bounding the slightly different
quantities from Eq. (
19
)
and
(
20
),

i.e.,
we showed that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau,{\boldsymbol{P}}}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\tfrac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k}
.
We now use the bound we proved to bound the original quantity of interest.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}+\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{I}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left[{\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:norms}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\left[\text{i.i.d.}\right]

\displaystyle=2\bigg(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\boldsymbol{P}}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}}_{\leq\nicefrac{{(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}}{{\left(k-1\right)}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\left[\text{norms}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\!\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\boldsymbol{P}}\!\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k-1}\right)~.

Recall that both the spectral norms above are upper bounded by 1. We upper bound the norm consisting of less projections by 1 and keep the other one.

Assume
t>\nicefrac{{\left(k+1\right)}}{{2}}
, and thus
t-1>k-t
.
We get,
\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\boldsymbol{P}}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t-1\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{t-1}
.
Similarly, when
t\leq\nicefrac{{\left(k+1\right)}}{{2}}
and
t-1\leq k-t
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\boldsymbol{P}}\!\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t+1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k-t}
.

Overall, we get a final bound of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\right)\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(k\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(t\right)}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{\tau\left(1\right)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{k}\bigg(\left(k-1\right)\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k-1}+\sum_{t=1}^{\left\lfloor\left(k+1\right)/2\right\rfloor}\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k-t}+\sum_{t=\left\lfloor\left(k+1\right)/2\right\rfloor+1}^{k-1}\frac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{t-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}\bigg(1+\!\!\!\sum_{t=k-\left\lfloor\left(k+1\right)/2\right\rfloor}^{k-1}\!\!\!\nicefrac{{1}}{{t}}+\!\!\sum_{t=\left\lfloor\left(k+1\right)/2\right\rfloor}^{k-2}\!\!\!\nicefrac{{1}}{{t}}\bigg)\leq\frac{2(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}\bigg(1+2\!\sum_{t=\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}^{k-1}\!\!\nicefrac{{1}}{{t}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\left[k\geq 2\right]}}{{\leq}}\frac{2(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}\bigg(1+2+2\intop_{\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}^{k-1}\frac{1}{t}dt\bigg)\leq\frac{2(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}\left(3+2\left(\ln\left(k-1\right)-\ln\left(\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}\left(3+2\ln\left(\frac{k-1}{\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor}\right)\right)\leq\frac{2(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}\underbrace{\left(3+2\ln\left(2\right)\right)}_{\leq 4.5}\leq\frac{9(d-r_{\operatorname{avg}})}{k}~.

∎

Appendix E
Extension to the average iterate (Remark
15
)

Here we briefly demonstrate how our results from Sections
5
and
6
can be readily improved by considering the average iterate

\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\triangleq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}

instead of the last iterate
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
.
The average iterate is known to be easier to analyze, and yields generally stronger results when analyzing the SGD algorithm
Varre et al. 2021
and the Kaczmard method
Morshed et al. 2020
. Of course, working with the average iterate requires maintaining a running mean parameter vector, which is generally more memory consuming.

E.1
Proof sketch for the cyclic setting

As a proof of concept,
we prove better bounds for an “easier” iterate that averages only iterates at the end of cycles,

i.e.,
for
k\!=\!nT
we define

\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}\!=\!\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\!\boldsymbol{w}_{Tn^{\prime}}
.
For the first task, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\Big({\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{w}_{Tn^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big)\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\left(\cancel{\boldsymbol{w}_{0}}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left\|\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:square_ineq}}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}+1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Big({\underbrace{\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}_{\leq 1}-\underbrace{\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n+1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}_{\geq 0}}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{n}~.

Similarly, for a general task
m\in\left[{T}\right]
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{\ref{lem:bound_on_projection_to_m_task}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left(\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n^{\prime}+1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{m}{n}\left(\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\left(\boldsymbol{P}_{T}\cdots\boldsymbol{P}_{1}\right)^{n+1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{m}{n}~.

Overall, we get the following bound on the forgetting in the cyclic setting:

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\tau,S}(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{m}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}\right)\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T-1}\frac{m}{n}=\frac{T-1}{2n}\leq\frac{T^{2}}{2k}~.\end{split}

E.2
Proof sketch for the random setting

For this case, we will demonstrate how one can easily bound
a similar expected forgetting to the one in
Eq. (
20
),
by
\tfrac{1}{k}
instead of
\tfrac{d-r_{\operatorname{avg}}}{k}
.

Plugging in the average iterate we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)\big({\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)\big({\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{t}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{k}\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:square_ineq}}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right)\big({\boldsymbol{w}_{t}-\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Lemma~\ref{total_contraction}}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{P}}\right){\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:projections}}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k},{\boldsymbol{P}}}\left[\Big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}-\Big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{i.i.d.}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}}\left[\Big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}-\Big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{t+1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k}}\left[\Big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}-\Big\|{{\boldsymbol{P}}_{k+1}\cdots{\boldsymbol{P}}_{1}\boldsymbol{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{k}~.

Appendix F
Additional related works

F.1
Continual learning

Practical methods for continual learning.

Like we explained in Section
7
, algorithmic approaches for preventing catastrophic forgetting roughly partition into three categories:

1.

Memory-based replay approaches
actively repeat examples from previous tasks to avoid forgetting.
Some store examples from observed tasks
(
e.g.,
Robins 1995
),
while others replay
generated
synthetic data from previous tasks to the main model
(
e.g.,
Shin et al. 2017
).

2.

Regularization approaches
limit the plasticity of the learned model in order to enhance its stability.
One can perform regularization in the
parameter space
,
to protect parameters that are important to previous tasks
(
e.g.,
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Nguyen et al. 2018
;
Zeno et al. 2021
),
or in the
function space
,
to protect the
outputs
of previous tasks
(
e.g.,
Li and Hoiem 2017
;
Lopez-Paz and Ranzato 2017
;
Farajtabar et al. 2020
).
Few recent works (
Benzing 2022
,

Lubana et al. 2021
) focus on obtaining a better understanding of popular quadratic regularization techniques like EWC
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
)
,
MAS
(
Aljundi et al. 2018
)
,
and SI
(
Zenke et al. 2017
)
.

3.

Parameter isolation approaches
allocate different subsets of parameters to different tasks.
This can be done by expanding the model upon seeing a new task (
e.g.,
Yoon et al. 2018
),
or by compressing the existing architecture after each task, to free parameters for future tasks (
e.g.,
Mallya and Lazebnik 2018
;
Schwarz et al. 2021
).

Understanding customary training techniques

Some recent papers also test the effect of common deep learning training techniques that change the optimization dynamics and/or have an implicit regularizing implications.
For instance,
Mirzadeh et al. 2020

study how different training regimes affect the loss landscape geometry, thus influencing the overall degree of forgetting in continual learning settings.

Goodfellow et al. 2013
were the first to suggest that training with the
dropout

Delange et al. 2021
also show that dropout is fruitful in many continual learning methods (some of which are mentioned above).
They point out that it mainly improves the initial performance on learned tasks (by mitigating overfitting), but leads to an increased amount of forgetting when learning later tasks.

Optimization hyperparameters are known to change the geometry of the loss landscape and affect generalization (see for instance
Jastrzebski et al. 2018
).

Mirzadeh et al. 2020
point out that
most empirical papers in this field use small batch sizes and SGD with learning rate decay.
They also discuss how a large learning rate increases the plasticity of deep models, thus having an ill-effect on forgetting.

Finally,
Mirzadeh et al. 2022
pointed out that architectural decisions also have implications on catastrophic forgetting, and specifically observe that wide neural networks tend to forget less catastrophically.

F.2
Wider scope

Finally, we note that
similar questions to those we ask here and that are asked generally in the continual learning paradigm,
are often asked on other fields, such as multi-task learning, meta learning, transfer learning,
curriculum learning
Weinshall and Amir 2020
,
and online learning.
Despite the similarities, there are differences though.
For example, in multi-task learning, the data from all tasks are simultaneously available, in transfer learning the goal to adapt models for a new target tasks and preserving performance on old source task is not a priority, and in online learning, typically, training data for previously seen tasks are assumed to be available in sequentially adapting to data from new tasks.
However, a more detailed review of these related areas is beyond the scope of this work.
