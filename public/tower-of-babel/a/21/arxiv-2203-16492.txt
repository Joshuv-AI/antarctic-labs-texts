On the impact of dimensionally-consistent and physics-based inner products for POD-Galerkin and least-squares model reduction of compressible flows
Eric J. Parish, Francesco Rizzi

Abstract
Model reduction of the compressible Euler equations based on proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and Galerkin orthogonality or least-squares residual minimization requires the selection of inner product spaces in which to perform projections and measure norms. The most popular choice is the vector-valued L 2 ​ ( Ω ) L^{2}(\Omega) inner product space. This choice, however, yields dimensionally-inconsistent reduced-order model (ROM) formulations which often lack robustness. In this work, we try to address this weakness by studying a set of dimensionally-consistent inner products with application to the compressible Euler equations. First, we demonstrate that non-dimensional inner products have a positive impact on both POD and Galerkin/least-squares ROMs. Second, we further demonstrate that physics-based inner products based on entropy principles result in drastically more accurate and robust ROM formulations than those based on non-dimensional L 2 ​ ( Ω ) L^{2}(\Omega) inner products.As test cases, we consider the following well-known problems: the one-dimensional Sod shock tube, the two-dimensional Kelvin-Helmholtz instability and two-dimensional homogeneous isotropic turbulence.

This work aims to provide a simple but comprehensive overview of these aforementioned issues by studying a set of inner products for performing POD and building Galerkin and least-squares ROMs within the context of the compressible Euler equations. In our study we focus on the dimensional consistency of existing formulations and highlight the advantages of physics-based inner products. Using dimensional arguments, we additionally extend existing ideas in entropy-based ROMs by proposing a new inner product in which to perform POD for entropy variables as well as a new “entropy-least-squares" ROM based on this entropy inner product. In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows:

1.

A concise framework for building various dimensionally-consistent ROMs through the language of inner products.

2.

A new inner product space in which to perform POD based on entropy principles and dimensional arguments.

3.

4.

Numerical experiments via the open-source software

Lastly, we emphasize that we do not consider hyper-reduction in this work. Our focus is not to create the most effective ROM, but rather to directly highlight the impact of the inner product/norm on the ROM results.

2
The compressible Euler equations

In this work we consider model reduction of the compressible Euler equations in one and two-dimensions. In what follows we provide the formulation for the two-dimensional setting.

The compressible Euler equations on the two-dimensional spatial domain
\Omega
are given for
t\in[0,T]
as

\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U})=\boldsymbol{0},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{U}:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
comprise the density,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
momentum, and total energy. The inviscid fluxes are given by
\boldsymbol{F}=\left[\boldsymbol{F}_{1},\boldsymbol{F}_{2}\right]
where
\boldsymbol{F}_{1}(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
is the inviscid flux in the
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
direction and
\boldsymbol{F}_{2}(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
is the inviscid flux in the
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
direction. The state and inviscid fluxes are given by

\boldsymbol{U}=\begin{Bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{2}\\
\rho E\end{Bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{F}_{1}(\boldsymbol{U})=\begin{Bmatrix}\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{1}^{2}+p\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}\\
u_{1}(E+p)\end{Bmatrix},\qquad\boldsymbol{F}_{2}(\boldsymbol{U})=\begin{Bmatrix}\rho u_{2}\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+p\\
u_{2}(E+p)\end{Bmatrix},

where we have assumed a calorically perfect gas such that the thermodynamic pressure is given by

p=\left(\gamma-1\right)\left(\rho E-\frac{1}{2}\rho\left(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}\right)\right),

with
\gamma=1.4
being the heat-capacity ratio. We assume the existence of initial and boundary conditions such that Eq. (
1
) is well-posed.

The Euler equations are typically formulated in terms of conservative variables, as in Eq. (
1
). An alternative path relevant to this work is to express the Euler equations in terms of
entropy variables
. Specifically, leveraging the formulation developed by Hughes
[
24
]
, we introduce a set of entropy variables as

\boldsymbol{V}=\begin{Bmatrix}\frac{-s}{\gamma-1}+\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}-\frac{\rho E}{p}\\
\frac{\rho u_{1}}{p}\\
\frac{\rho u_{2}}{p}\\
-\frac{\rho}{p}\end{Bmatrix}

with
s=\ln(p)-\gamma\log(\rho)
. The Euler equations can be written in terms of the entropy variables as

\boldsymbol{A}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{F}_{1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{A}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{F}_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{A}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{A}\equiv\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\in\mathbb{S}^{4}
is the Jacobian of the variable transformation and
\mathbb{S}^{N}
is the space of
N\times N
symmetric matrices. The analytic form of the Jacobian
\boldsymbol{A}
is provided in
A
. Critically,
\boldsymbol{A}
,
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{F}_{1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{A}
, and
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{F}_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{A}
are all symmetric matrices, and thus Eq. (
2
) is “symmetrized". We emphasize that, while the Euler equations expressed in terms of entropy variables (
2
) is analytically equivalent to the Euler equations expressed in terms of conserved variables (
1
), the symmetric nature of the entropy formulation leads to numerical formulations that enjoy, e.g., enhanced stability properties
[
21
,
24
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

In this work we will consider several ROM formulations that employ entropy variables. In our numerical experiments, these ROMs are constructed from a flow solver that spatially discretizes the conservative from (
1
) rather than the entropy form (
2
). We consider this case as most flow solvers do not discretize in entropy variables. Consequences of this choice are a loss of symmetry and entropy stability at the discrete level.
3
3

3

With special care these properties can be retained, but we do not consider this case here.

3
Model reduction via POD

Projection-based reduced-order models generate approximate solutions to the system of interest by first restricting the state to live within a low-dimensional trial space. Various approaches exist for constructing this trial space (e.g., the reduced-basis method, space–time methods) and here we focus on the classic spatial-only POD-based approach
[
22
,
4
]
.
POD, which is closely related to principle component analysis and the Karhunen Loeve expansion, is a data-compression method that finds a
K
-dimensional orthonormal basis that minimizes the projection error of a dataset in an inner product space
\mathcal{I}
equipped with the inner product
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{I}}
. For a scalar-valued system with a state variable
u(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, POD results in the state being approximated as
4
4

4

We note that some bodies of work consider an affine subspace that, e.g., satisfies initial conditions or boundary conditions
[
20
]
; the ideas presented here generalize to this setting

u(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\hat{u}_{i}(t),

where
\phi_{i}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\ldots,K
are the POD basis vectors and
\hat{u}_{i}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\ldots,K
are generalized coordinates.

The situation is more complicated for vector-valued systems with a state variable
\mathbf{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t):\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
where
M>1
is the number of state variables (i.e.,
M=4
for the Euler equations in two dimensions). Using the taxonomy of
[
42
]
, two approaches exist for applying POD to vector-valued systems like the Euler equations:

1.

Scalar-valued POD: A separate POD basis is constructed for each state variable, i.e.,
M
separate POD problems are solved, one for each for variable.
The advantage of this approach is that each state variable is treated separately and as a result the process is dimensionally consistent. The disadvantages of this approach are that it cannot capture correlations between the different state variables and results in larger ROMs.

2.

Vector-valued POD: A global basis is constructed for all the state variables. In this approach one POD problem is solved and the result is used to construct a basis for all the variables. Advantages of this approach are that it can capture correlations between the different state variables and it leads to smaller ROMs. The primary disadvantage of the approach is that it results in dimensionally-inconsistent formulations if special care is not taken when defining the inner product.

In this work we exclusively consider vector-valued POD. The reasons for this are several, including (1) that vector-valued POD appears to be the more common approach, (2) past works have
not
demonstrated that scalar-valued POD outperforms vector-valued POD
[
42
,
40
,
41
]
, and (3) scalar-valued POD results in ROMs that are of a significantly higher dimension than vector-valued POD.

We now outline vector-valued POD within the context of the compressible Euler equations in two-dimensions. Specifically, given a dataset of solution “snapshots"
\boldsymbol{S}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times n_{S}}
, where
n_{S}
is the number of samples, vector-valued POD solves the minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\underset{\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}|\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\right)_{\mathcal{I}}=\mathbf{I}\}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\|\boldsymbol{S}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{S}_{i}\right)_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{\mathcal{I}},

(3)

where
\|\cdot\|\equiv\sqrt{\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{I}}}
and, to simplify the notation, we use
\boldsymbol{S}_{i}
to denote the
i
th column of the snapshot matrix, i.e., the
i
th sample. Assuming that the POD process is employed on snapshots of the conservative variables, the conservative variables are then approximated as

\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\approx\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}(t),

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}
is the ROM basis and
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
are the generalized coordinates. We note that a similar expression exists if POD was performed on a different variable set, e.g., entropy variables.

The accuracy of the POD basis and the resulting ROM clearly depends on the choice of the inner product space
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{I}}
. This is particularly true for vector-valued POD, where the minimization problem (
3
) combines variables with different units. The remainder of this section outlines various inner products within the context of the compressible Euler equations to study this issue.

3.1
Vector-valued
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product in conserved variables

We first study the classic vector-valued
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product with conserved variables.
For
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
we define the vector-valued
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product as

\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{4}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

This inner product is
not
dimensionally consistent for the compressible Euler equations with solution vectors defined in terms of the conserved variables as

\left(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{U}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\equiv\int_{\Omega}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Omega}\rho u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\rho u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Omega}\rho u_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\rho u_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Omega}\rho E(\boldsymbol{x})\rho E(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x},

which does not make sense on dimensional grounds.
Despite this inconsistency, this inner product has been widely employed for model reduction of compressible flows (particularly in the Galerkin/least-squares projection process; see, for example, the residual-minimization formulation in the seminal work
[
13
]
). The vector-valued
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product results in a trial basis defined as the solution to the minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}=\underset{\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}|\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\mathbf{I}\}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\big|\big|\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big|\big|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times n_{S}}
is the sample matrix of the conserved state variables.

3.2
Non-dimensional vector-valued
L^{2}
inner product in conserved variables

The next inner product we consider is similar to the
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product, but this time includes a scaling function that non-dimensionalizes the variables. Specifically, we define a non-dimensional
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product for
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
as

\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}\mathbf{D}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}

(5)

where
\mathbf{D}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
is a diagonal matrix given by

\mathbf{D}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\rho_{\infty}^{2}}&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{(\rho u_{1,\infty})^{2}}&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{(\rho u_{2,\infty})^{2}}&0\\
0&0&0&\frac{1}{(\rho E_{\infty})^{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

In the above
\rho_{\infty},\rho u_{1,\infty},\rho u_{2,\infty}
, and
\rho E_{\infty}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are the reference states for the density, momentum, and energy, and may or may not depend on the spatial location. This inner product is
dimensionally consistent
for the compressible Euler equations with solution vectors defined in terms of the conserved variables as

\left(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{U}\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}\equiv\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\rho_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})^{2}}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(\rho u_{1,\infty})^{2}}\rho u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\rho u_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}+\\
\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(\rho u_{2,\infty}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}}\rho u_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\rho u_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{(\rho E_{\infty})^{2}}\rho E(\boldsymbol{x})\rho E(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

(6)

This non-dimensional
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product results in a trial basis defined as the solution to the minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}=\underset{\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}|\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}=\mathbf{I}\}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}\|_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times n_{S}}
is the sample matrix of the conserved state variables.

We emphasize that scaled vector-valued POD processes like the one outlined above have been pursued in the literature. Ref.
[
23
]
, for instance, performs POD on the snapshots where each state variable is scaled by its
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm on the training set. Ref.
[
5
]
pursues a similar approach, but instead scales by the maximum value of each state variable.

Remark 3.1
.

Selection of the reference state.

The non-dimensional inner product requires specification of the reference state. Numerous choices exist, like those described above. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no approach has been demonstrated to be “superior".

3.3
Energy inner product in transformed variables

An interesting possibility for creating dimensionally-consistent ROMs is based off of the “energy" inner products proposed in
[
42
]
. Unfortunately, the energy inner products proposed in Ref.
[
42
]
are only applicable to the isentropic Euler equations, while the energy inner products proposed in Ref.
[
19
]
are not formal inner products, mathematically speaking (positivity is not guaranteed). As a result, we don’t consider these energy inner products.

3.4
Entropy inner product in entropy variables

The next inner product we consider is an “entropy-based" inner product that acts on solution vectors defined in terms of entropy variables. We start by noting that the product of the entropy variables and the time derivative of the conserved variables results in
[
24
]
5
5

5

We use the
\sim
notation as numerous definitions of the entropy exist, and different definitions may have constants of proportionality.

\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{U}\sim\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho s.

We observe that this product is
dimensionally consistent
and results in a physically meaningful quantity.
Via the chain rule we can write the above as

\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{V}.

On dimensional grounds we propose an inner product space for use with entropy variables that is based of an inner product of the type
\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\boldsymbol{V}
. We note the following important points:

1.

The matrix
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}
is symmetric positive definite so can be used to define an inner product.

2.

To create an inner product space that is of use for POD, the weighting matrix
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}
needs to be constant (e.g., defined for some background state).

Thus, to create our entropy inner product space, we start by defining the matrix

\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\left(\boldsymbol{V}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)

where
\boldsymbol{V}_{\infty}
is a reference state. Leveraging the fact that
\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{4}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix, we can define the inner product

\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

(8)

This inner product is naturally
dimensionally consistent
for the compressible Euler equations with solution vectors defined in terms of the entropy variables, and as a result does not require any additional scaling when deployed on dimensional problems.
This entropy-based inner product results in a trial basis for the entropy variables defined as the solution to the minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}}=\underset{\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}|\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\right)_{\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}}=\mathbf{I}\}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i}\right)_{\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}}\|_{L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}}(\Omega)}

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{V}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times n_{S}}
is the sample matrix of the entropy variables.

3.5
Entropy inner product in conserved variables

The last inner product we consider is an entropy-based inner product that acts on solution vectors defined in terms of the conserved variables. Similar to the entropy inner product space presented in the previous section, we derive this inner product based on dimensional arguments. We start by recalling the dimensional consistency of
\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\boldsymbol{V}
, and use this dimensional consistency to create an analogous inner product for use with the conserved variables. On dimensional grounds this results in the inner product
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{U}
, which has the same units as
\boldsymbol{V}^{T}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\boldsymbol{V}
. Similarly to our entropy inner product, we make the following comments:

1.

The matrix
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}
is positive definite and thus can be used to define an inner product and norm.

2.

To create an inner product space that is of use for POD, the weighting matrix
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}
needs to be constant.

Thus, to create this entropy inner product space we start by defining the matrix

\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)=\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x})

where
\boldsymbol{U}_{\infty}
is a reference state. Leveraging the fact that
\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{S}^{4}
is a symmetric positive definite matrix, we can define the inner product

\left(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\right)_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})^{T}\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

(10)

This inner product is
dimensionally consistent
for the compressible Euler equations with solution vectors defined in terms of the conserved variables, and as a result does not require any additional scaling when deployed on dimensional problems. This entropy-based inner product results in a trial basis for the conserved variables defined as the solution to the minimization problem

\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}=\underset{\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times K}|\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\right)_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}=\mathbf{I}\}}{\text{arg min}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{S}}\|\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{*},\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}\right)_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}\|_{L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)}

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{S}^{\boldsymbol{V}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4\times n_{S}}
is the sample matrix of the entropy variables.

Remark 3.2
.

A comment on orthogonality for Galerkin ROMs.

POD results in bases that are orthonormal in the inner product in which they were constructed. This orthonormality is typically leveraged in a Galerkin projection process to simplify the construction of the ROM. If the POD bases are constructed in an inner product which differs from the inner product used in Galerkin projection, then the typical simplifications stemming from orthonormality are lost. We emphasize here that this is not a significant issue as (1) ROM performance relies on the subspace described by the basis, not orthonormality (e.g., consider two ROMs: one with an orthonormal basis and one with a non-orthonormal basis. So long as the bases have the same range, Galerkin projection will result ROMs that yield the same solution), and (2) the basis can always be re-orthogonalized in the desired inner product via, e.g., a QR decomposition.

4
Reduced-order models

Thus far we have outlined the construction of a reduced basis for the state variables through POD in different inner products. In addition to construction of this reduced-basis, a ROM requires a projection process applied to the governing equations. This section outlines several such approaches. First, Section
4.1
outlines the construction of the Galerkin ROM through various inner products. Next, Section
4.2
outlines several least-squares ROMs that are obtained from residual minimization in norms induced by different inner product spaces.

4.1
Galerkin-based ROMs

Galerkin ROMs rely on a residual orthogonalization process where the residual of the full-order model is restricted to be orthogonal to the trial space in some inner product. Similar to POD, the performance of the Galerkin ROM depends on the definition of the inner product. We now outline the construction of several Galerkin-type ROMs via the inner products defined above.

4.1.1
Galerkin ROM

The Galerkin ROM is arguably the most popular ROM formulation and, in general, results in accurate and stable solutions for symmetric systems; in this case Galerkin projection can be interpreted to be optimal in a system-specific norm. In the present context the Galerkin ROM with solution vectors defined in terms of the conservative variables is given by

\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}})\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\boldsymbol{0}

(12)

for
i=1,\ldots,K
. We will refer to this ROM formulation as the Galerkin
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROM. As the compressible Euler equations are not symmetric with respect to the conservative variables, we expect the Galerkin ROM to, in general, perform poorly. Further, this Galerkin ROM is
not
dimensionally consistent for the same reasons outlined above. We further emphasize that, even if the POD bases are defined via a dimensionally-consistent inner product, this ROM formulation is still dimensionally inconsistent. We expect the Galerkin ROM to be the least accurate method we explore.

4.1.2
Non-dimensional Galerkin reduced-order model

To remedy the dimensional inconsistency of the Galerkin ROM outlined above, we can instead define a non-dimensional Galerkin ROM with solution vectors defined in terms of the conservative variables as

\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}+\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}})\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}=\boldsymbol{0}

(13)

for
i=1,\ldots,K
. We will refer to this ROM formulation as the Galerkin
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
ROM. Unlike Eq. (
12
), Eq. (
13
)
is
dimensionally consistent. Despite being dimensionally consistent, however, we still expect this ROM to perform poorly as the Euler equations are not symmetric with respect to the conserved variables.

4.1.3
Galerkin reduced-order model in entropy variables

We can develop a ROM addressing both of the deficiencies of the above Galerkin ROMs, namely dimensional consistency and a lack of symmetry, by creating a Galerkin ROM with solution vectors defined in terms of the entropy variables as

\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i},\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}}_{i},\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\left(\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}}\hat{\boldsymbol{V}}\right)\right)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\boldsymbol{0}

(14)

for
i=1,\ldots,K
. We will refer to this ROM as the Galerkin
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROM. This ROM is both dimensionally consistent
and
symmetric with respect to the entropy variables. As a result we expect this ROM to be more accurate and more stable than both the ROMs outlined above.

4.2
Least-squares reduced-order models

Least-squares ROMs comprise a popular alternative to Galerkin-based ROMs. Unlike Galerkin ROMs, which rely on residual orthogonalization, least-squares ROMs rely on residual minimization, i.e., they compute solutions that lie within a low-dimensional trial subspace that minimize the residual of the system of interest in some norm. A variety of least-squares ROM formulations exist, and here we formulate our least-squares ROMs within the context of the windowed least-squares (WLS) approach
[
35
]
, which addresses addresses several deficiencies of the more standard least-squares Petrov-Galerkin (LSPG) approach.

To describe WLS ROMs, we first define the residual of the compressible Euler equations expressed in terms of the conserved variables as

\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}:\boldsymbol{U}\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{U}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{U}).

(15)

Analogously, we define a residual for the compressible Euler equations in terms of entropy variables as

\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{V}}:\boldsymbol{V}\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{V})+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{F}\left(\boldsymbol{U}\left(\boldsymbol{V}\right)\right).

(16)

WLS ROMs operate by partitioning the time domain into a set of
N_{w}
non-overlapping time windows
[t_{s}^{n},t_{f}^{n}]\subset[0,T]
of length
\Delta T^{n}=t_{f}^{n}-t_{s}^{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,N_{w}
such that
t_{s}^{1}=0
and
t_{f}^{N_{w}}=T
and
t_{s}^{n+1}=t_{f}^{n}
,
n=1,\ldots,N_{w}-1
; see Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Depiction of the decomposition of the time domain into windows for WLS.

WLS then sequentially seeks approximate solutions that lie within the trial subspace that minimize the residual over each window in some norm defined by the inner product space
\mathcal{I}
, i.e., WLS solves the sequence of optimization problems for
n=1,\ldots,N_{w}

\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right),\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right)\right)_{\mathcal{I}}dt

(17)

subject to the initial condition constraints
\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{s}^{n})=\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{f}^{n-1})
for
n=2,\ldots,N_{w}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t_{s}^{1})=\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
is, e.g., projection of the initial conditions onto the trial subspace. The analogous ROM formulation for a solution vector defined in terms of entropy variables is given by

\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{V}}\left({\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}\right),\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{V}}\left({\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}\right)\right)_{\mathcal{I}}dt

(18)

subject to analogous initial condition constraints.

Various approaches exist to solve the sequence of WLS minimization problems, including
optimize-then-discretize
approaches that involve solving the Euler–Lagrange equations associated with the residual minimization principle, and
discretize-then-optimize
approaches that involve discretizing the objective function in space and time and solving the resulting nonlinear least-squares problem with a discrete least-squares solver (e.g., Gauss--Newton).
6
6

6

In this case WLS is essentially LSPG, but with the residual defined over multiple time steps.
We consider the latter here due to its simplicity and ease of implementation. In particular, we will consider a discretize-then-optimize scheme where the temporal derivative in Eq. (
17
) (or Eq. (
18
)) is discretized with the Crank-Nicolson method and the spatial derivatives are discretized with the finite volume method.

Similar to POD, the choice of inner product dictates the accuracy of WLS ROMs. The remainder of this section will outline several possible inner products for WLS ROMs within the context of the Euler equations to investigate this issue.

4.2.1
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product

The most straight-forward inner product to employ in the optimization problems (
17
) and (
18
) is the standard vector-valued
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product. In this case our ROM formulation with a basis for the conserved variables becomes

\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right),\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt.

(19)

A similar definition exists if we discretize in entropy variables.
We will refer to WLS ROMs that employ the above inner product and discretize the conserved variables as WLS
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROMs.

We emphasize that, like the classic
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product employed in POD, this choice of inner product is
dimensionally inconsistent
for the residual minimization problems (
17
) and (
18
) as it results in a norm that, e.g., adds the residual of the energy equation to the residual of the momentum equations. Regardless, this inner product as been widely employed in least-squares model reduction (see again Ref.
[
13
]
). Due to their symmetric nature and tie to an underlying residual minimization problem, least-squares ROMs based on the
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product have, in general, been observed to be more robust than their Galerkin counterparts.

4.2.2
Non-dimensional
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
inner product

A more consistent approach is to employ a non-dimensional
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
inner product like that defined in Eq. (
5
). With this inner product, and discretizing in conserved variables, our ROM formulation becomes

\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right),\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right)\right)_{L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)}dt.

(20)

A similar definition again exists if we discretize in entropy variables. We will refer to WLS ROMs that employ the above inner product and discretize the conserved variables as WLS
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
ROMs.

This choice of inner product is dimensionally consistent for the residual minimization problems (
17
) and (
18
). We emphasize that non-dimensional inner products have been considered in the literature, see, e.g., Refs.
[
23
,
5
]
, and typically have resulted in more robust ROMs than those obtained with direct
L^{2}(\Omega)
residual minimization. Despite being dimensionally consistent, this inner product is arguably not a “physics-based" norm as it simply adds scaled variables of different units.

4.2.3
Entropy inner product

Lastly, we consider WLS ROMs based on entropy inner products. Noting that the residual of the Euler equations has units of
\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{U}
, we employ the inner product
L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)
described in Section
3.5
. With this inner product, and discretizing in conserved variables, our ROM formulation becomes

\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right),\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right)\right)_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}dt.

(21)

We will refer to WLS ROMs that employ the
L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)
inner product and discretize the conserved variables as WLS
{}_{\text{conservative}}
-
L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)
ROMs.
A similar definition exists if we discretize in entropy variables, in which case we obtain the ROM formulation

\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{V}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{V}}\left({\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}\right),\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{V}}\left({\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}\right)\right)_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}dt.

(22)

We will refer to ROMs based on the above formulation as WLS
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)
ROMs. This choice of inner product is dimensionally consistent for the residual minimization problems (
21
) and (
22
), and as a result does not require any additional scaling if deployed on a dimensional problem.

Remark 4.1
.

We chose to construct our “entropy norm" based on a fixed background state
\boldsymbol{U}_{\infty}
. We note that an alternative option would be to left multiply our residual by the Cholesky decomposition of
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}
evaluated about the current state and minimize the residual in the standard
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm, i.e., we could solve

\tilde{\boldsymbol{U}}(t)=\underset{{\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}(t)\in\text{Range}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\boldsymbol{U}})}{\text{arg min}}\int_{t_{s}^{n}}^{t_{f}^{n}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*})}\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right),\sqrt{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*})}\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{U}}\left({\boldsymbol{U}}^{*}\right)\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}dt

where we use
\sqrt{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}}
to denote the Cholesky decomposition of
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}
such that
\sqrt{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}}^{T}\sqrt{\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{V}}{\partial\boldsymbol{U}}
. In this case our ROM formulation could be interpreted as a “preconditioned" ROM
[
34
]
. We choose a fixed background state as (1) it directly relates to an inner product space (e.g., that could be used in POD) and (2) it simplifies the numerical implementation, but note that employing the “preconditioned" version may have advantages.

L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product, and in a standard

inner product. Only the “entropy" ROMs employ “physics-based" inner products. Tables

5
Current results for numerical analyses of ROMs for the compressible Euler equations

Before proceeding, we briefly summarize available results (to the best of the authors’ knowledge) for numerical analyses of the above ROMs applied to the compressible Euler equations. We note that analyses for the Galerkin ROMs deployed on simplified versions of the Euler equations (e.g., the isothermal Euler equations, the linearized Euler equations) exist in the literature
[
27
,
42
]
, but here we restrict our discussion to the fully nonlinear Euler equations.

1.

Galerkin
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

There are presently no theoretical results guaranteeing stability of the Galerkin ROM with a basis for the conserved variables applied to the compressible Euler equations (numerical results have demonstrated that the Galerkin ROM is often unstable). There are additionally no practical, sharp
a priori
or
a posteriori
error bounds that exist. We note that analyses for the Galerkin ROM have been undertaken for a generic dynamical system in Ref.
[
11
]
; these analyses rely on the availability of difficult-to-compute Lipshitz constants and sharpness was not addressed.

2.

Galerkin
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
.

The non-dimensional Galerkin ROM, to the best of the authors knowledge, has not been considered in the literature. As a result there are no available analyses; we fully expect the non-dimensional Galerkin ROM to suffer from the same shortcomings of the standard Galerkin ROM.

3.

Galerkin
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

The Galerkin ROM with a basis for the entropy variables can leverage (in part) past analyses by e.g., Hughes, Tadmor, and Harten
[
24
,
45
,
46
]
, that was performed in the context of finite elements. The Galerkin
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROM has been analyzed within the ROM context in Ref.
[
26
]
, where Tezaur and Barone demonstrate that the Galerkin ROM discretized in entropy variables is “entropy stable", meaning that it can be shown to satisfy the second law of thermodynamics (the Clausius-Duhem inequality). In order to be entropy-stable in practice the Galerkin
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROM must employ an entropy stable discretization of the governing equations (e.g., via entropy conserving fluxes
[
25
]
). We remark that, even if the Galerkin
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROM is entropy stable, it is not totally clear if this guarantees numerical stability. Further, no error bounds have been derived for the Galerkin
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
ROM.

4.

WLS
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Analyses for WLS ROMs have been carried out for a generic dynamical system in Ref.
[
35
]
, but similar to the Galerkin ROM these analyses rely on difficult-to-compute Lipshitz constants and may not be sharp. These analyses indicated that minimizing the residual over larger windows resulted in stable and more accurate ROMs, but in general it is not clear if stability can be guaranteed. A similar ROM formulation that preceded WLS is the least-squares Petrov Galerkin (LSPG) ROM, and analyses of LSPG applied to a generic dynamical system were again presented in Ref.
[
11
]
. Stability for LSPG is not guaranteed, and the error bounds suffer from the same issues of sharpness and difficult-to-compute Lipshitz constants.

5.

WLS
{}_{\text{conserved}}
-
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
, WLS
{}_{\text{conservative}}
-
L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)
, and WLS
{}_{\text{entropy}}
-
L^{2}_{\boldsymbol{B}_{\infty}}(\Omega)
.

The WLS ROM in these norms has not been considered in the literature, and as a result there are no available analyses.

In summary, none of the ROMs described in this work are equipped with guarantees of stability when they are applied to the compressible Euler equations, nor are they equipped with
a priori
or
a posteriori
error bounds. We believe this should be an area for future work.

6
Description of numerical experiments

We now present numerical experiments studying the impact of the choice of inner product on the POD bases and the resulting ROM formulation. We consider three numerical experiments: the Sod shock tube in one dimension, the Kelvin Helmholtz instability in two dimensions, and homogeneous isotropic turbulence in two dimensions. For each problem we create a “non-dimensional" and “dimensional" configuration. These configurations are designed to operate at the same Mach number and non-dimensional time step, and are defined over the same non-dimensional time horizon. Methods that are dimensionally consistent should produce identical results on the two configurations, while methods that are not dimensionally consistent should produce different results on the two configurations. All ROMs considered are reproductive, and our aim is only to study the impact of the inner product.

All three problems are simulated in Python using
pressio-demoapps
(PDA).
PDA is an open-source project developed at Sandia National Laboratory that is aimed at providing benchmark ROM cases, and is part of the
Pressio
ecosystem.
Features of PDA include a cell-centered finite volume-based solver equipped with several flux schemes, exact Jacobians, sample mesh capabilities, and benchmark cases in one, two, and three dimensions. We emphasize that PDA’s Euler solver discretizes the governing equations in their conserved form with conserved variables. For ROMs employing entropy variables, we simply employ the required change of variables before evaluating the spatial fluxes in PDA.
We now describe the specific cases studied in this work.

6.1
Sod shock tube

The first problem considered is the Sod shock tube in one dimension, which is a typical benchmark problem for the compressible Euler equations.
The problem is governed by the compressible Euler equations on the 1D domain
\Omega\in[-0.5,0.5]
.
For the spatial discretization, we use a finite volume scheme with 500 uniformly sized cells and Weno5 flux reconstruction.
For the temporal discretization, we employ a low-storage fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) scheme. The initial conditions are

\left[\frac{1}{8}\rho_{\infty},\;0,\frac{\gamma}{10}p_{\infty}\right]&x\geq 0\end{cases}.

6.6
Stability

In our numerical experiments we will use the terms “stable" and “unstable". Numerous definitions of stability exist in the literature. In our work, we will refer to a ROM as “unstable" if it results in a solution containing a NaN, and thus the solver cannot continue. Similarly, we will refer to a ROM as “stable" if it results in a real-valued solution.

10
Conclusions

This work investigated the impact of the choice of inner product on the performance of POD Galerkin and POD least-squares reduced-order models within the context of the compressible Euler equations. We demonstrated that employing the classic
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product is inappropriate for POD, Galerkin, and least-squares ROMs discretized with conserved variables. This inner product resulted in ROMs that were dimensionally inconsistent and resulted in different solutions when applied to non-dimensional and dimensional versions of the same problem. Further, these formulations were often unstable. We showed that this issued can be alleviated by employing a non-dimensional
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
inner product, in which case a dimensionally-consistent POD process and Galerkin/least-squares ROM can be formulated. Our numerical experiments demonstrated that employing this non-dimensional inner product led to ROMs that were consistently more accurate and more stable. However, even when employing this non-dimensional inner product, the Galerkin ROM was still often unstable and least-squares ROMs, while stable, were not particularly accurate. We demonstrated that the use of a physics-based “entropy" inner product addressed this issue and vastly improved the ROM performance. We demonstrated that employing an entropy inner product in the POD process resulted in a dimensionally-consistent POD formulation that provided bases with a similar approximation power to a non-dimensional
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
inner product. Next, we demonstrated that the Galerkin ROM built off of entropy variables led to significant improvements over the standard Galerkin ROM. Lastly, we demonstrated that least-squares ROMs which minimize the residual in an entropy-based norm were significantly more accurate than least-squares ROMs that minimize the residual in a non-dimensional
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)
norm. Overall, the most accurate ROM formulations were the least-squares ROMs formulated in the entropy inner product. These methods provided solutions that were qualitatively accurate and associated with the lowest errors across every exemplar.

The results of this work demonstrated the promise of ROMs based on physics-based inner products, and in particular demonstrated the efficacy of entropy inner products for the compressible Euler equations. While we believe the presented results are promising, we emphasize that important aspects of a practical ROM formulation, such as online efficiency through hyper-reduction and certification via error analysis, were outside the scope this study. As a result, future work will investigate theoretical stability properties of entropy ROMs, integrating them with hyper-reduction and/or other techniques to enhance their online efficiency, and application of physics-based inner products to other domain areas.
