Abstract
Stablecoins have historically depegged due from par to large sales, possibly of speculative nature, or poor reserve asset quality. Using a global game which addresses both concerns, we show that the selling pressure on stablecoin holders increases in the presence of a large sale. While precise public knowledge reduces (increases) the probability of a run when fundamentals are strong (weak), interestingly, more precise private signals increase (reduce) the probability of a run when fundamentals are strong (weak), potentially explaining the stability of opaque stablecoins. The total run probability can be decomposed into components representing risks from large sales and poor collateral. By analyzing how these risk components vary with respect to information uncertainty and fundamentals, we can split the fundamental space into regions based on the type of risk a stablecoin issuer is more prone to. We suggest testable implications and connect our model’s implications to real-world applications, including depegging events and the no-questions-asked property of money.

Introduction

Stablecoins are blockchain-based cryptographic tokens whose price is supposed to be at par with respect to a fiat currency, most commonly the US dollar. To achieve this peg, stablecoin issuers claim to back each token with at least one dollar worth of reserve assets. The decentralization and lack of regulation around stablecoins means that issuers can choose from a large range of assets to hold as reserves and control the transparency or public perception of their reserves to a great extent. The range of assets has included US Treasuries, bank deposits, money market instruments, cryptocurrencies, and abstract “assets” like pricing algorithms. Some issuers publicly disclose their reserves, hiring custodians to monitor and maintain those reserves. Other issuers do not disclose verifiable information regarding their reserves, intentionally or not, leading to uncertainty among investors.

The choice of reserve assets and degree of uncertainty about them is related to types of risk that a stablecoin issuer faces. We provide a few examples. In March 2023, Circle, who issues USD Coin (USDC), disclosed that $3.3 billion of its reserves were held at Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) admist its collapse. This led to an immediate depeg, with USDC’s peg being restored only when the US government announced a bailout of SVB. In this case, a transparent stablecoin whose reserves are known fell vulnerable to credit events occurring to the issuer’s reserve assets.

To capture both types of risk, we extend a streamlined model of
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
to include an large agent that sells at random. To motivate the seller’s randomness, we note that large sales can occur for a variety of reasons, including speculation and the liquidity needs of a large agent, yet small agents will perceive a degree of induced selling pressure regardless. This can also be viewed as repurposing the model of
Corsetti et al. 2002
for stablecoins and relaxing it by making the large agent non-strategic. This relaxation allows us to establish uniqueness results and to derive novel comparative statics results with respect to the uncertainty in information of a stablecoin’s reserve asset quality.

We first show that a unique equilibrium in switching strategies exists both when a large sale occurs and does not occur; in this equilibrium, the selling pressure and conditional run probability increase in the presence of a large sale. We then decompose the run probability into two components: the conditional run probability given a large sale occurring and not occurring. The former component can be interpreted as the risk from by a large sale, and the latter as the risk from poor collateral. These conditional run probabilities decrease (increase) as public knowledge is more precise when fundamentals are sufficiently strong (weak). Intriguingly, as private signals become more precise, conditional run probabilities increase (decrease) for sufficiently strong (weak) fundamentals. The analysis with respect to private signals yields a novel result which suggests another potential source of a stablecoin’s stability, i.e. a higher degree of heterogeneity in how investors idiosyncratically assess a stablecoin’s reserves. Tokens that have large information uncertainty in this sense may thus benefit from it.

Isolating the different types of risk facing a stablecoin also allows us to partition the fundamental space into regions where the conditional run probabilities have differing behaviors with respect to both types of information uncertainty. This classification could be of possible use in assessing what types of risk a stablecoin is prone to given their quality of fundamentals and level of transparency about reserves. The implications generated by our model can be used to explain the events described earlier, and to provide more general insights about the stability mechanisms and regulatory challenges of stablecoins. We finish by discussing the model’s answer to the question of what qualities make a stablecoin to be perceived as money, using the no-questions-asked framework of
Holmström 2015
.

Institutional Details.

After the advent of the first cryptocurrencies in the mid-2010s, stablecoins were created to meet the demand for an on-chain asset with relative price stability. Since then, stablecoins have grown in market capitalization, peaking in early 2022 and hovering around the $150 billion mark. The uses of stablecoins are numerous, including cross-border funds transfers, stores of values for lending protocols, collateral for trading cryptocurrencies, and a medium of exchange in automated market makers.

The market microstructure of stablecoins can be split into the
primary market
and
secondary market
. In the primary market, the issuer interacts with arbitrageurs and reserve asset markets. The issuer holds a collection of reserve assets that “back up” the amount of stablecoins in circulation, and exchanges a minted token to arbitrageurs for a par value of the fiat currency that the stablecoin is pegged to. Arbitrageurs can redeem tokens at par, assuming that the issuer is solvent (i.e.​ can liquidate enough reserves to fulfill all redemption requests at a given time). Notably, the issuer has sole discretion over who can be an arbitrageur, which usually involves a vetting process. In the secondary market, the arbitrageurs trade with buyers and sellers at market prices on exchanges.

Related Literature.

Our paper contributes to the literature on stablecoin runs. On the game-theoretic side are the works of

Ma, Zeng, and Zhang 2023

Bertsch 2023
explores how the perceived quality of reserves affects the adoption of stablecoins in the presence of outside options.
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
study the effect that public information has on run probability, and is closely related to our work. Ours differs from their in a few key aspects: they do not include large sellers in their model, and we analyze uncertainty in both public and private information.

Compared to the aforementioned works, this paper breaks new ground by introducing a large seller in the context of stablecoins. Our contributions include analyzing the effect of a large seller on selling pressure, considering the dispersion of private signals as a factor influencing stability, and classifying fundamentals with regards to the individual decomposed risk components. For uncertainty in public information, we provide a slightly different result than
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
due to considering the ex-ante run probability (whose distribution also depends on public information uncertainty).

On the empirical side, see
Liu, Makarov, and Schoar 2023
which studies stablecoin flows during the Terra–Luna crash,
Lyons and Viswanath-Natraj March 2023
which finds a correlation between peg efficiency and arbitrage volume, and
Liao and Caramichael 2022
which examines the effect of stablecoin activity on the real economy. Catalini et al. offers a survey of the economics of stablecoins.

This paper is also related to the literature on global games pioneered by by
Carlsson and van Damme 1993
, and first applied to currency attacks by
Morris and Shin 1998
and Corsetti et al. (2002). Despite similarity in the reduced-form games between our model and currency attack models, the microfoundations differ. Agents in currency attack games all seek to profit from a large sale, whereas we consider the unique struture of the stablecoin market described above and assume that investors find value in holding stablecoins given issuer solvency.
Morris and Shin 1998
explore the behavior of speculators in the presence of a government that can choose to defend the peg. Similar to this paper is the work of
Corsetti et al. 2002
, studying the behavior of a large speculator and small speculators in simultaneous- and sequential-move global games. As previously mentioned, our model contrasts itself by relaxing the speculator’s behavior to be non-strategic in the sequential-move setting. This allows us to derive comparative statics and risk decomposition results, whereas they provide numerical results and characterization of equilibria in asymptotic regimes of the information structure (for the sequential-move setting).

Proposition
3.1
.

Collateral risk decreases and large sale risk increases as the large seller’s size grows. Specifically,
dR_{0}/d\delta<0
and
dR_{1}/d\delta>0
.

The above proposition states that collateral risk is decreasing and large sale risk is increasing in the size of the large seller. As the large seller becomes more influential relative to the other investors, when no large sale occurs, a smaller mass of investors make runs less likely. Conversely, when there is a large sale, the additional influence of the large seller drives up the run probability. How this affects the total ex-ante run probability depends on the value of
p
and the relative magnitude of the rates of change.

We now consider the effect of information uncertainty on the risk components and overall run probability. There are two parameters controlling the uncertainty of information in our model:
\sigma
, the scale of the common prior, and
\sigma_{x}
, the scale of dispersion in private signals. Shocks that are known to or perceived by all investors affect
\sigma
, while factors related to the heterogeneity of investors’ beliefs affect
\sigma_{x}
.

An example of a shock to the common prior’s variance could be an issuer’s disclosure of reserve assets. Aspects influencing the magnitude of the shock include frequency, verifiability, and content of the disclosure. Elements affecting the dispersion of investors’ beliefs include information asymmetry and the nature of collateral reserves. For example, as an opaque stablecoin issuer covertly recollateralizes, information about the transfers could be leaked to select market participants, widening the information gap between more and less informed investors. Having stablecoins collateralized across different assets or asset classes can also drive up the scale of signal dispersion, as variation in investors’ beliefs is amplified by the variety of collateral reserves.

To facilitate analysis, we use precision parameters
\tau=\sigma^{-2}
and
\tau_{x}=\sigma_{x}^{-2}
. Proposition 3.2 characterizes the comparative statics of risk components with respect to the precision of commonly known information, and Proposition 3.3 does the same but with respect to the precision of private signals.

Proposition
3.2
.

If the quality of fundamentals is sufficiently high (low), then collateral risk and large sale risk decrease (increase) as public knowledge is more precise. Specifically, for
a\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle\mu>\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{F^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\tau_{x}\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}\implies\frac{dR_{a}}{d\tau}<0,

\displaystyle\mu<\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})\implies\frac{dR_{a}}{d\tau}>0.

When varying the dispersion of the common prior, Proposition 3.2 reveals that for each risk component, there exists upper and lower thresholds at which the risk component increases in
\tau
for values of
\theta
below the lower threshold and decreases in
\tau
for values of
\theta
above the upper threshold. In other words, when quality of fundamentals is poor, more perceived uncertainty about the value of fundamentals from the investor’s point of view helps reduce the risk of a run. This is because low uncertainty would concentrate the signals of investors around a poor fundamental quality, meaning that an individual investor assesses that other investors have similar pessimistic beliefs about the state of reserves as themselves. Conversely, when the quality of fundamentals is good, more uncertainty increases the risk of a run. The economic reasoning for this is similar: low uncertainty concentrates the signals of investors around a good fundamental quality, so an investor gauges that other investors have similar optimistic beliefs.

Our result is similar to Proposition 2 of
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
. They find that a single threshold delineates where increased uncertainty heightens or reduces run risk, while we find two thresholds with a region of ambiguity in the middle. This is because, based on their proof, the notion of run probability that

dR_{a}/d\tau
that creates a small interval where the sign of

is ambiguous without further assumptions.

Carlsson and van Damme 1993

When the variance of private signals is low, the global game approaches the common knowledge game where there are multiple equilibria, one payoff dominant and one risk dominant. As the variance of private signals increases, the investors become more uncertain about each other’s signals and thus actions, heightening the degree of risk dominance. Intuitively, the “safe” option is to hold when fundamentals are good, and sell when fundamentals are poor. Thus, Propositions 3.2 and 3.3 contrast as the common prior’s variance drives investors’ uncertainty about the stablecoin reserves whereas the private signal’s variance drives investors’ uncertainty about each other’s actions.

4
Discussion

4.1
Case Studies

We begin the discussion by relating our model’s results and implications with the case studies described in the introduction.

•

Circle and the Collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.
Since USDC is a fiat-backed stablecoin whose issuer, Circle, makes frequent public disclosures about reserves which are generally trusted, USDC would correspond to a stablecoin with a low prior variance. Our model predicts good financial stability when fundamentals are good and poor financial stability when fundamentals are poor, relative to coins of a similar reserve structure but different levels of transparency. During SVB’s collapse, when Circle disclosed that SVB held a portion of their reserves, USDC depegged shortly after. Upon the announcement by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) that SVB would be bailed out, USDC repegged not long after (see Figure 5). In this case, as a consequency of transparency, collateral risk is a major determinant of financial stability, as opposed to large sale risk.

•

Tether’s Disclosures and Depegging Events.
For Tether, disclosures of reserves are less frequent and less verifiable due to operating outside of a regulatory framework, resulting in a higher prior variance. Compared to more transparent stablecoins, there is more exposure to run risk when fundamentals are good, but less exposure to large sale risk when fundamentals are lower but not too low (see the intermediate interval in Figure 2). Additionally, the asymmetric information generated by Tether’s transfers and recollateralizations corresponds to a large dispersion in private signals, which may additionally reduce run risk when fundamentals are good. These reasons could explain why despite the opaqueness and occasional depegging driven by large sales, Tether repegs in a short amount of time, avoiding more substantial depegging incidents or a full collapse.

•

Terra–Luna Collapse.
Endogenously-backed stablecoins like UST tend to have high prior variances due to their self-referential nature. For example,
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
find empirical evidence that Luna, the cryptoasset backing TerraUSD, had a non-constant conditional volatility, leading to unconditional fat tails. Additionally, our model suggests that single-collateralized stablecoins, including UST, should generate less heterogeneous beliefs and thus more precise private signals, heightening large sale risk when fundamentals are good. As the collapse of UST and Luna started with a large sale, possibly of speculative nature, it could be that a combination of relatively large prior variance and precise private signals contributed to the full collapse.

Our discussion adds on to that of
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
. They discuss the effect of public information and fat-tailed distributions on real-world depegging events, which we complement by (i) attributing the causes of events to either poor collateral or large sales and (ii) considering the affect of the variation in private signals on the events. In line to the above analysis, we also offer a rough characterization of the common prior variance and private signal variance based on the type of stablecoin.

Note that for real-world stablecoin designs, a large common prior variance is correlated with a large private signal variance. In the case of opaque fiat-backed stablecoins, the variance of the common prior arises from the issuer’s opaque disclosure policy, while the variance of the private signal arises from the information asymmetry generated from said policy. Also worthy of note is how the basket of collateral reserves affects parameters for crypto-collateralized stablecoins. Baskets consisting of anti-correlated cryptoassets have less systemic risk, and thus a less common prior variance, while the opposite is true for single-collateralized tokens or baskets with correlated assets. As investors may have different beliefs about each asset’s returns, having a basket of diverse assets may also widen the heterogeneity among investors, thus raising the private signal variance.

4.2
Possible Benefits of Opaqueness

The model results, case studies, and table above suggest that in certain contexts, there may be a benefit to opaqueness, whether that comes in the form of an increase in
\sigma
(common prior) or
\sigma_{x}
(private signal).

With regards to the dispersion of the common prior, Proposition 3.2 and Figure 2 suggest that there is an interval, in which fundamentals are neither too good or poor, where large sale risk decreases and collateral risk increases in variance. Based on the large sale probability and difference between the critical thresholds (
\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1})
and
\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0})
), the overall run probability may decrease in variance as well. For instance, when there is a moderate collateral shock to drive
\mu
in that interval, higher variance may shield the issuer from large sales, especially those of speculative nature. Nondisclosure or avoidance of traditional financial institutions for deposits may also shield stablecoins from shocks to those institutions. For example, Tether remained relatively stable during the SVB crisis by not having any exposure to SVB. This, coupled with a less transparent disclosure policy, suggests that a risk-dominant equilibrium where investors gravitate towards holding arises.

With respect to the dispersion of the private signal, Proposition 3.3 and Figure 3 suggest that a larger dispersion is beneficial for financial stability when fundamentals are good. To this end, a larger user base of the stablecoin or a more diverse basket of collateral reserves may achieve a more heterogeneous user base.
Ahmed, Aldasoro, and Duley 2024
run an event study when Dai, a leading crypto-collateralized stablecoin, switched from a single-collateral to a multi-collateral structure. They find that Dai’s peg stability improved, and explain this as a reduction in the volatility of the common prior as a result of risk diversification. However, risk is not necessarily diversified when the collateral assets are correlated, as may be the case for cryptocurrency. Our extended analysis posits that more contrasting investor beliefs could be a factor towards Dai’s improved peg stability.

The main policy implication for regulators is that while enforcing regulations on disclosures may improve trust in the DeFi ecosystem, disclosures would make shocks to the TradFi ecosystem spill over more easily into the world of DeFi, especially given the prominence of fiat-backed stablecoins whose issuers hold bank deposits. To build a resilient overall financial ecosystem, regulators must carefully consider and balance these two incentives. For stablecoin isusers, the main design implication is that the selling pressure of large sales and the diversification of collateral reserves should be considered when choosing what reserves to hold and how to go about disclosing them.

4.3
Testable Implications

The discussion so far lends itself to two empirically testable implications.

•

The Effect of Large Sales on Peg Stability.
Proposition 2.1 suggests that a large sale increases selling pressure on investors. Empirically, we should thus observe an adverse effect on peg stability immediately following a large sale for a short amount of time. As large sales usually follow collateral shocks, it would be interesting to conduct the analysis in times where fundamentals are good.

•

The Effect of Collateral Reserves on Peg Stability for Crypto-Collateralized Stablecoins.
While the number of fiat-backed stablecoin issuers who disclose reserves is likely not large enough for a thorough cross-sectional analysis, crypto-collateralized stablecoins have their reserves locked in on-chain smart contracts. We can then regress peg stability on weighted reserve volatility and a metric measuring reserve diversification. Our model predicts that peg stability should decrease in reserve volatility and increase in reserve diversification.

4.4
Distance to No-Questions-Asked

We conclude by discussing the differences between stablecoins and money using the no-questions-asked framework of
Holmström 2015
, describing money as a information insensitive and deep-in-the-money debt contract, i.e. something which can always be redeemed at par. While stablecoin prices behave more similarly to pegged currencies than bonds, there is a link between our model and the notions of information insensitivity and deep-in-the-moneyness. Reserve assets that are “deep-in-the-money” could correspond to a prior distribution that makes it unlikely for
\theta
to be realized below the critical thresholds (e.g. high
\mu
, relatively low
\sigma
).

Holmström 2015
measures information sensitivity using a metric called
information acquisition sensitivity (IAS)
, roughly described as the benefit that acquiring information has on making a decision (in our case, to sell or to hold). A low common prior dispersion implies a low IAS since as signals become more concentrated around the true value, the relative benefit of information is reduced. A high private signal dispersion also implies a low IAS due to the risk-dominance of the equilibrium strategy: as other investors are more likely to choose the “safe” option, knowing more information about the true value of reserves is less likely to change the equilibrium outcome. The attributes of parameters in our model that are desirable for financial stability are thus linked to desirable properties of money as described in
Holmström 2015
.

5
Conclusion

Reserve assets backing a stablecoin and the risks facing a stablecoin issuer can vary. With a model capturing two common types of risk, one from large sales and the other from collateral shocks, we find that large sales exert selling pressure on investors and show how uncertainty in information about stablecoin reserves influences run risk. Our model has implications consistent with real-world depegging events, and suggests that more uncertainty may improve an issuer’s financial stability in certain contexts. A careful understanding of these risks and the role that policies have on them is crucial when addressing the future trajectory and regulatory challenges surrounding stablecoins.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\delta}F_{\mu_{p}(\bar{x}),\sigma_{p}}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(\bar{x}))=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}F_{\mu_{p}(\bar{x}),\sigma_{p}}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(\bar{x}))\cdot\frac{d\theta_{1}}{d\delta}=f_{\mu_{p}(\bar{x}),\sigma_{p}}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(\bar{x}))\cdot\frac{d\theta_{1}}{d\delta}>0

By the Implicit Function Theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{\star}_{1}}{d\delta}=-\frac{\frac{d}{d\delta}F_{\mu_{p}(\bar{x}),\sigma_{p}}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(\bar{x}))}{\frac{d}{d\bar{x}}F_{\mu_{p}(\bar{x}),\sigma_{p}}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(\bar{x}))}

By Assumption 1 and the proof of Proposition 2.1, the denominator is negative. Since the numerator is positive, it follows that
dx^{\star}_{1}/d\delta>0
. By the Chain Rule,

\displaystyle\frac{dR_{a}}{d\delta}=\frac{d}{d\delta}F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{1}}{d\bar{x}}\cdot\frac{dx^{\star}_{1}}{d\delta}=f_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{1}}{d\bar{x}}\cdot\frac{dx^{\star}_{1}}{d\delta}>0.

A.3
Proof of Proposition 3.2

Let
a\in\{0,1\}
. The posterior parameters in terms of precisions are

\displaystyle\mu_{p}(\bar{x})=\frac{\tau\mu+\tau_{x}\bar{x}}{\tau+\tau_{x}},

\displaystyle\tau_{p}(\bar{x})=\frac{1}{\tau+\tau_{x}}.

The condition that
x^{\star}_{a}
solves, in terms of precisions, is then

\displaystyle F_{\mu_{p},\tau_{p}^{-1/2}}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x}))-\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}=\Phi\left(\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}\left(\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x})-\frac{\tau\mu+\tau_{x}\bar{x}}{\tau+\tau_{x}}\right)\right)-\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}=0.

Then for a switching threshold
\bar{x}
to be an equilibrium, it must satisfy

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}\left(\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x})-\frac{\tau\mu+\tau_{x}\bar{x}}{\tau+\tau_{x}}\right)\right)=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}\cdot\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x})-\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}\cdot\mu-\frac{\tau_{x}}{\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}\cdot\bar{x}=\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\right)

\displaystyle-\bar{x}+\frac{\tau+\tau_{x}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x})-\frac{\tau}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\mu-\frac{\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\right)=0

By the Implicit Function Theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{\star}_{a}}{d\tau}=-\frac{\frac{\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})}{\tau_{x}}-\frac{\mu}{\tau_{x}}-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\tau_{x}\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}}{-1+\frac{\tau+\tau_{x}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{a}}{d\bar{x}}}.

Using the formula for
d\bar{\theta}_{a}/d\bar{x}
from the proof of Proposition 2.1, it follows that Assumption 1 is a sufficient condition for the denominator to be negative. Thus
dx^{\star}_{a}/d\tau>0
if and only if

\displaystyle\mu<\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\tau_{x}\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}.

Note that
\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))<0
since
\eta<1
. By the Chain Rule,

\displaystyle\frac{dR_{a}}{d\tau}=\frac{d}{d\tau}F_{\mu,\tau^{-1/2}}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}F_{\mu,\tau^{-1/2}}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a}))+\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}F_{\mu,\tau^{-1/2}}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a}))\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{a}}{d\bar{x}}\cdot\frac{dx^{\star}_{a}}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\mu}{2\sqrt{\tau}}\cdot\phi(\sqrt{\tau}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\mu))+\sqrt{\tau}\cdot\phi(\sqrt{\tau}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\mu))\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{a}}{d\bar{x}}\cdot\frac{dx^{\star}_{a}}{d\tau}

Since
\phi(\cdot)>0
, the first term is positive if and only if
\mu<\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})
. Similarly, since
d\bar{\theta}_{a}/d\bar{x}>0
, the second term is positive if and only if
dx^{\star}_{a}/d\tau>0
. Thus, a sufficient condition for
dR_{a}/d\tau>0
is
\mu<\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})
, and a sufficient condition for
dR_{a}/d\tau<0
is
\mu<\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\tau_{x}\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}
. For values in between
\theta_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})
and
\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\tau_{x}\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}
, the sign of
dx^{\star}_{a}/d\tau
is not readily determinable.

A.4
Proof of Proposition 3.3

Recall that for a switching threshold
\bar{x}
to be an equilibrium, it must satisfy

\displaystyle-\bar{x}+\frac{\tau+\tau_{x}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x})-\frac{\tau}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\mu-\frac{\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\right)=0

\displaystyle-\tau_{x}\bar{x}+(\tau+\tau_{x})\cdot\bar{\theta}_{a}(\bar{x})-\tau\mu-\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\right)=0.

By the Implicit Function Theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{dx^{\star}_{a}}{d\tau_{x}}=-\frac{-x^{\star}_{a}+\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}}{\tau_{x}\left(-1+\frac{\tau+\tau_{x}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{a}}{d\bar{x}}\right)}

Using the formula for
d\bar{\theta}_{a}/d\bar{x}
from the proof of Proposition 2.1, it follows that Assumption 1 is a sufficient condition for the denominator to be negative. Thus
dx^{\star}_{a}/d\tau>0
if and only if

\displaystyle-x^{\star}_{a}+\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))}{2\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}>0.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{a}=\frac{\tau+\tau_{x}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{\tau}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\mu-\frac{\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\right).

Substituting this into the above expression, we have
dx^{\star}_{a}/d\tau>0
if and only if

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{\tau+\tau_{x}}{\tau_{x}}+1\right)\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})+\frac{\tau}{\tau_{x}}\cdot\mu+\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}{\tau_{x}}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}\right)\cdot\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\right)>0

\displaystyle\mu>\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{2\tau+\tau_{x}}{2\tau\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}\cdot\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta)).

Note that
\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))<0
since
\eta<1
. By the Chain Rule,

\displaystyle\frac{dR_{a}}{d\tau_{x}}=\frac{d}{d\tau_{x}}F_{\mu,\tau^{-1/2}}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a}))

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}F_{\mu,\tau^{-1/2}}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a}))\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{a}}{d\bar{x}}\cdot\frac{dx^{\star}_{a}}{d\tau_{x}}=\sqrt{\tau}\cdot\phi(\sqrt{\tau}(\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\mu))\cdot\frac{d\bar{\theta}_{a}}{d\bar{x}}\cdot\frac{dx^{\star}_{a}}{d\tau_{x}}

so
dR_{a}/d\tau_{x}>0
if and only if
\mu>\bar{\theta}_{a}(x^{\star}_{a})-\frac{2\tau+\tau_{x}}{2\tau\sqrt{\tau+\tau_{x}}}\cdot\Phi^{-1}(\eta/(1+\eta))
.

A.5
Proof of Proposition 3.4

We lastly examine the comparative statics with respect to reserve fundamentals. We have

\displaystyle S_{L}=\frac{p\cdot F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))}{(1-p)\cdot F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0}))+p\cdot F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))}=\frac{p\cdot\bar{F}_{\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}),\sigma}(\mu)}{(1-p)\cdot F_{\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0})}(\mu)+p\cdot\bar{F}_{\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}),\sigma}(\mu)}

by the symmetry of the normal distribution, where
\bar{F}
is the survival function. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{L}}{\partial\mu}=\frac{p(1-p)(f_{\bar{\theta}_{0},\sigma}(\mu)\cdot\bar{F}_{(\bar{\theta}_{1},\sigma}(\mu))-\bar{F}_{\bar{\theta}_{0}(\theta_{0}^{\star}),\sigma}(\mu)\cdot f_{\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}),\sigma}(\mu))}{((1-p)\cdot F_{\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0})}(\mu)+p\cdot\bar{F}_{\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}),\sigma}(\mu))^{2}}

Then
\partial S_{L}/\partial\theta>0
if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0}),\sigma}(\mu)}{\bar{F}_{\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0}),\sigma}(\mu)}>\frac{f_{\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}),\sigma}(\mu)}{\bar{F}_{\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}),\sigma}(\mu)}

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0}))}{F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{0}(x^{\star}_{0}))}>\frac{f_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))}{F_{\mu,\sigma}(\bar{\theta}_{1}(x^{\star}_{1}))}

where the last equivalence is due to the symmetry of the normal distribution. This condition holds since the normal distribution has a decreasing reverse hazard rate and
\bar{\theta}_{0}(x_{0}^{\star})<\bar{\theta}_{0}(x_{1}^{\star})<\bar{\theta}_{1}(x_{1}^{\star})
.
