An Eigenfunction Approach to Conversion of the Laplace Transform of Point Masses on the Real Line to the Fourier Domain
Michael Mckenna, Hrushikesh N. Mhaskar, Richard G. Spencer

Abstract
Motivated by applications in magnetic resonance relaxometry, we consider the following problem: Given samples of a function t ↦ ∑ k = 1 K A k ​ exp ⁡ ( − t ​ λ k ) t\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\exp(-t\lambda_{k}) , where K ≥ 2 K\geq 2 is an integer, A k ∈ ℝ A_{k}\in\mathbb{R} , λ k > 0 \lambda_{k}>0 for k = 1 , ⋯ , K k=1,\cdots,K , determine K K , A k A_{k} ’s and λ k \lambda_{k} ’s. Unlike the case in which the λ k \lambda_{k} ’s are purely imaginary, this problem is notoriously ill-posed. Our goal is to show that this problem can be transformed into an equivalent one in which the λ k \lambda_{k} ’s are replaced by i ​ λ k i\lambda_{k} . We show that this may be accomplished by approximation in terms of Hermite functions, and using the fact that these functions are eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform. We present a preliminary numerical exploration of parameter extraction from this formalism, including the effect of noise. We do not claim to have eliminated the inherent ill-posedness of the original problem, as reflected in the numerical results.

Introduction

Multiexponential decay signals, consisting of the superposition of two or more exponentials of the form:

F_{0}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\exp(-t\lambda_{k})+\epsilon_{0}(t),\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,

(1.1)

where
T>0
,
K
is a positive integer,
A_{k},\lambda_{k}>0
for
k=1,\cdots,K
, and
\epsilon_{0}(t)
’s are i.i.d. random variables with mean
0
, arise in innumerable settings in the natural sciences and mathematics
[
38
]
, including optics
[
18
]
, astrophysics
[
61
]
, food science
[
41
]
, geophysics
[
64
]
, and biology
[
17
,
51
]
.
The main problem is to determine the parameters
A_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
. The literature is less focused on determination of the number of components
K
, although that may also be of substantial interest and can be addressed by a number of statistical methods. The value of
K
is often assumed.

Our main motivating example is magnetic resonance relaxometry, in which the proton nuclei of water are first excited with radio frequency pulses and then exhibit an exponentially decaying electromagnetic signal. These applications most often have
K=2
, although applications with
K\geq 3
appear occasionally.
In general, biological tissue will contain multiple states of water defined by, among other factors, molecular mobility, often modeled as the co-existence of discrete compartments
[
32
]
. In principle, these compartments can then be separately mapped based on the fact that they exhibit different MR transverse decay time constants, or
T_{2}
values, as follows. A particularly important example is myelin mapping in the brain. Myelin forms an insulating sheath surrounding neuronal axons in the brain and spinal cord and is critical for the transmission of electrical signals
[
39
]
. Water within the myelin sheath itself exhibits limited mobility and therefore, according to MR theory, exhibits a more rapid electromagnetic signal decay as compared to the more mobile water. The more rapidly decying signal component is designated as the myelin water fraction (MWF). Thus, the MR signal is composed of sub-signals from both the MWF and the more mobile water, with these two contributions separately quantified by making use of their different relaxation time constants. MWF mapping in the human brain based on this principle was pioneered by Mackay,
[
45
]
, who focused on the related model of a discretized continuous range of tissue decay time constants. MWF mapping is applied widely in the investigation of cognitive decline
[
16
,
31
]
, Alzheimer’s disease
[
24
,
40
]
, neurodegenerative diseases such as multiple sclerosis
[
43
]
, and in studies of normative aging that are characterized by or exhibit myelin loss
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
16
,
27
,
43
,
55
,
69
]
. We provide preliminary corresponding results below, in the section entitled ”Numerical Results”.

In addition to widespread application, these techniques are still undergoing important refinement, due to the complexity of separating the signal generated by the MWF from the signal generated by the more mobile water. The difficulty in this and related multiexponential analyses may be understood in the following way. It is well-known that the solution of the Fredholm equation of the first kind (FEFK)

F(t)=\int_{a}^{b}K(t,\tau)d\mu(\tau)

(1.2)

is an ill-posed inverse problem, with the determination of the unknown measure
\mu
being highly sensitive to noise. When the kernel function is the exponential,
K(t,\tau)=exp(-t\tau)
, parameter extraction is a special case of the notoriously ill-posed inverse Laplace transform (ILT)
[
19
]
. If the support of the unknown distribution function is the sum of point masses, we recover the multiexponential decay problem (
1.1
), which inherits its ill-posedness from the FEFK.
In the discretized case, these difficulties are highlighted by the condition number of the matrix defining the discretization of the FEFK, which can become enormous with fine discretization, even for only two monoexponential components
[
8
,
65
]
. This effect of increasingly finer discretization may be understood by the fact that it brings the discretized problem closer to the ill-posed native integral equation of the FEFK.

This behavior is in marked contrast to that of the Fourier transform; as a unitary transform, with appropriate normalization, the FT enjoys the optimal condition number of unity; that is,
\|\mathfrak{F}^{*}\|\|\mathfrak{F}\|=1
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the
L^{2}
operator norm
[
65
]
.
Given this, the transformation of the ILT to an equivalent Fourier problem is an appealing approach, as was first (to our knowledge) described by Gardner. This allowed for the visualization of decay constants as peaks on a frequency spectrum
[
30
]
. This work was later built upon by Provencher through use of a of tapered window function to reduce sidelobes in the frequency domain defining the rates
\lambda_{k}

[
59
]
.

Here we present a further development in this area, with the goal of transforming the problem, stated most naturally in terms of parameter estimation from the inverse Laplace transform of point masses, to their inverse Fourier transform. To do this, we exploit the properties of the orthonormalized Hermite functions as eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform. Our approach is in some sense reminiscent of previous work on the expansion of multiexponential signals by orthogonal polynomials
[
37
]
, including an earlier approach for inversion of the Laplace transform through the use of the Jacobi and Laguerre polynomials.
[
53
]
. Furthermore, unlike many previous mathematical treatments of multi-component multiexponential decay signals, we incorporate experimental noise into our analysis.

Section
5

F_{0}
.
We describe the process of least square approximation and related quantities in Section
6
.
Based on these considerations, Section

7
are proved in Section
9
. The procedure for obtaining
\lambda_{k}
’s from the Fourier transform is based on our earlier work, and is summarized in the Appendix.
A numerical implementation is given in Section
8
.

Effectively, the Fourier transform converts

(without noise) to the so-called Stieltjes transform of a measure that associates the mass
A_{k}
with
i\lambda_{k}
.
It is well known that the denominator of the Padé approximation of this transform is the Prony polynomial, which is an orthogonal polynomial on the unit circle of degree
K
.
Our previous paper
[
29
]
is similar in spirit to this direction, and works without the assumption that the
A_{k}
’s are positive, but restricted to purely imaginary exponents.
Finally, we note that the paper
[
21
]
deals with the case when the exponents are complex numbers with small real parts and distinct imaginary parts.

Given theoretical bounds on the ability to define these parameters as a function of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), measurement intervals and total duration, and number of underlying exponentials

,
6
,
5

, development of further methodology represents attempts to extract the maximum information from a given signal, or to reformulate the problem to circumvent these limitations
[

,

We emphasize that in this work, we are analyzing the expression (

values; the underlying physics is similar for the two approaches, although the mathematical analysis differs between them.

3
Notation

The orthonormalized Hermite functions are defined (cf.
[
66
, Formula (5.5.3)]
) by

\psi_{k}(x)=(\pi^{1/2}2^{k}k!)^{-1/2}(-1)^{k}\exp(-x^{2}/2)\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{k}\exp(-x^{2}),\qquad k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

(3.1)

For
n>0
, we denote

\Pi_{n}=\mathsf{span}\{\psi_{k}:k<n\}.

Each element of
\Pi_{n}
is a function of the form
x\mapsto P(x)\exp(-x^{2}/2)
for some polynomial
P
of degree
<n
. Such a function will be called a
weighted polynomial of degree
<n
.
We note that the notation does not require
n
to be an integer; we interpret
\Pi_{n}=\Pi_{\lfloor n\rfloor}
if
n
is not an integer. This simplifies some notation later.

The Fourier transform of a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined formally by

\mathfrak{F}(f)(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\exp(-i\omega t)dt.

(3.2)

It is known (cf.
[
47
, Theorem 4.3.2]
; the notation is different) that

\mathfrak{F}(\psi_{k})(\omega)=(-i)^{k}\psi_{k}(\omega),\qquad k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\ \omega\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(3.3)

Next, we introduce some concepts and notation related to various measures which we will use in this paper.

Rather than writing sums of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}g(y_{j})
, it is convenient to use the Stieltjes’ integral notation.
Let
\delta_{x}
be the Dirac delta at
x
.
As a functional (or measure), this is expressed as
\int gd\delta_{x}=g(x)
.
We define a (possibly complex) measure
\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
(described as a measure that associates the mass
w_{j}
with each
y_{j}
) and write
\int g(y)d\nu(y)
or even shorter,
\int gd\nu
, instead of
\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}g(y_{j})
.
The
support
of
\nu
(denoted by
\mathsf{supp}(\nu)
) is
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, and
\nu
is said to be
supported on
any set that contains the support.
The quantity
\sum_{j=1}^{m}|w_{j}|
is the
total variation
of
\nu
, denoted by
\|\nu\|_{TV}
.
There is a measure associated with
\nu
called the total variation measure, denoted by
|\nu|
. For example, for the above measure
\nu
,

\int gd|\nu|=\sum_{j=1}^{m}|w_{j}|g(y_{j}).

The Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}(\nu)
and the Fourier transform
\mathfrak{F}(\nu)
for
\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\delta_{y_{j}}
are defined by

\mathcal{L}(\nu)(t)=\int\exp(-tu)d\nu(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\exp(-ty_{j}),\quad\mathfrak{F}(\nu)(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\exp(-i\omega y_{j}).

(3.4)

If
\nu
is any possibly signed measure, and
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is
\nu
-measurable, we will write formally

\|f\|_{\nu;p,A}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left\{\int_{A}|f|^{p}d|\nu|\right\}^{1/p},&\mbox{ if $1\leq p<\infty$,}\\[4.30554pt]
\sup_{t\in\mathsf{supp}(\nu)\cap A}|f(t)|,&\mbox{ if $p=\infty$.}\end{cases}

(3.5)

The space
L^{p}(\nu)
comprises measurable functions
f
for which
\|f\|_{\nu;p}<\infty
.
If
A={\mathbb{R}}
, we will omit the mention of
A
from the notation.
When
\nu
is the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
, we will omit its mention and write
\|f\|_{p}
in place of
\|f\|_{\nu;p}
,
L^{p}
instead of
L^{p}(\nu)
, etc.
As usual, we identify functions which are equal
|\nu|
-almost everywhere.

For
f\in L^{p}
, we define the degree of approximation of
f
by

E_{p,n}(f)=\min_{P\in\Pi_{n}}\|f-P\|_{p}.

(3.6)

Constant convention:

In the sequel,
c,c_{1},\cdots
will denote generic positive constants, whose values may be different at different occurrences, even within a single formula.
The notation
A\lesssim B
means
A\leq cB
,
A\gtrsim B
means
B\lesssim A
,
A\sim B
means
A\lesssim B\lesssim A
.
For example, in a formula of the form

A_{n}\lesssim n^{c}\exp(-c_{1}n)\lesssim\exp(-cn),

there are implied constants in
\lesssim
, and the constants
c,c_{1}
in the middle term are different from the constant
c
in the last term.

Finally, we recall a fundamental result from
[
47
, Theorem 6.2.4, Theorem 6.1.6]
.

Proposition 3.1

For any
\delta>0
,
n>0
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and
P\in\Pi_{n}
,

\|P\|_{p,{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-\sqrt{2n}(1+\delta),\sqrt{2n}(1+\delta)]}\leq\exp(-cn)\|P\|_{p,[-\sqrt{2n}(1+\delta),\sqrt{2n}(1+\delta)]}.

(3.7)

In particular,

\|P\|_{p}\sim\|P\|_{[-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{n}]}.

(3.8)

4
Fundamental Idea

To explain our main idea, we first define
a noiseless signal by (cf. (
1.1
))

F(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\exp(-t\lambda_{k}),\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.1)

With the measure

\mu^{*}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}},

(4.2)

where
\delta_{\lambda}
denotes the Dirac delta supported at
\lambda
, we observe that
F
is the Laplace transform of
\mu^{*}
.
The fundamental idea is to convert this Laplace transform of
\mu^{*}
into the Fourier transform of another measure
\tilde{\mu}
supported on
\{\lambda_{k}\}
as in (
4.5
) below.
Towards this end, we introduce another function

f(t)=F(t)\exp(-t^{2}/2)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\exp(\lambda_{k}^{2}/2)\exp(-(t+\lambda_{k})^{2}/2).

(4.3)

and observe the fact that
\mathfrak{F}(\exp(-\cdot^{2}/2))(\omega)=\exp(-\omega^{2}/2)
.
We obtain:

\mathfrak{F}(f)(-\omega)=\exp(-\omega^{2}/2)\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\exp(\lambda_{k}^{2}/2)\exp(-i\omega\lambda_{k}).

(4.4)

With

\tilde{\mu}(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\exp(\lambda_{k}^{2}/2)\delta_{\lambda_{k}},

(4.5)

we observe

\mathfrak{F}(\tilde{\mu})(\omega)=\exp(\omega^{2}/2)\mathfrak{F}(f)(-\omega).

(4.6)

We will describe in the appendix a method to estimate the
\lambda_{k}
’s from samples of
\mathfrak{F}(\tilde{\mu})(\omega)
using our theory of localized trigonometric polynomial kernels
[
21
,
29
,
49
,
50
]
.

In order to compute
\mathfrak{F}(\tilde{\mu})(\omega)
, our basic idea is to approximate
f
in terms of the Hermite functions, and use (
3.3
).

There are three technical barriers here: one is that the signal
F_{0}
as defined in (
1.1
) is defined only on a finite interval.
The second is that we know only finitely many values of
F_{0}
, not a formula for
F_{0}
.
Finally, there is noise.

We will address the first barrier in this section, the second in Section
5
, and study the effect of noise in Section
7
.

To remedy the barrier of knowing the signal only a finite interval, we will shift the signal to the left, so that the resulting interval on which the observations lie can contain all the quadrature points which are needed to evaluate the integrals from the values of the functions.
To carry out this program, we introduce a tunable parameter
L>0
, write
R=T-L
, and define

\displaystyle F_{\mbox{shifted}}(t)

\displaystyle=F_{0}(t+L)=\begin{cases}\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\exp(-L\lambda_{k})\exp(-t\lambda_{k})+\epsilon(t),&\mbox{ if $-L\leq t\leq R$,}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F(t)+\epsilon(t),&\mbox{ if $-L\leq t\leq R$,}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
F
is defined as in (
4.1
) with

a_{k}=A_{k}\exp(-L\lambda_{k}),\qquad\epsilon(t)=\epsilon_{0}(t+L).

(4.8)

We observe that with
f
defined in (
4.3
) and

E(t)=\begin{cases}\epsilon_{0}(t+L)\exp(-t^{2}/2),&\mbox{if $t\in[-L,R]$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4.9)

we have

F_{\mbox{shifted}}(t)\exp(-t^{2}/2)=\begin{cases}f(t)+E(t),&\mbox{ if $-L\leq t\leq R$,}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4.10)

To summarize, starting from the signal (
1.1
), we define
a_{k}
’s by (
4.8
),
F
by (
4.1
),
f
by (
4.3
), and modify the noise
\epsilon_{0}
in (
1.1
) as per (
4.9
).
In the rest of the paper, we will treat
f
as the signal, and
E
as the noise.

5
Discretization formulas

Another issue that must be addressed is the fact that in the context of signal analysis, we know the values of
f
only at finitely many points on
[-L,R]
; these are the points at which the signal is sampled. Therefore, the expansion of
f
must be calculated in terms of Hermite functions using only these values.
This motivates our use of the Gauss quadrature formula to compute the coefficients

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\psi_{k}(t)dt
.
However, it is a delicate matter to compute the quadrature nodes and weights
[
67
,
68
]
.
Moreover, this requires the knowledge of
f
at exactly the Gauss quadrature nodes, which is not the case in practice. The existence of
quadrature formulas based on arbitrary nodes are known to exist
[
48
]
, but
it will be seen that for our purposes we do not need quadrature formulas exact for integrating products of Hermite functions of some order.
Rather, it is sufficient to approximate
f
using
least squares approximation, provided the points at which
f
is evaluated are sufficiently dense in
[-L,R]
.
This is encapsulated in the following definition.

Definition 5.1

A sequence
\{\nu_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
of possibly signed measures is called a
strongly Marcinkiewicz Zygmund sequence
if
each of the following two conditions is satisfied.

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|P(t)|^{2}d|\nu_{m}|(t)\sim\int_{\mathbb{R}}|P(t)|^{2}dt,\qquad P\in\Pi_{m}.

(5.1)

\|\nu_{m}\|_{TV}\sim m^{1/2}.

(5.2)

By an abuse of notation, we may say that
\nu_{m}
is a strongly Marcinkiewicz Zygmund measure of order
m
, and write
\nu_{m}\in SMZ(m)
.

Example 5.1

Obviously, the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies (
5.1
) for every
m\geq 1
, but not the condition (
5.2
).
However, in view of Proposition
3.1
, the Lebesgue measure restricted to
[-2\sqrt{m},2\sqrt{m}]
belongs to
SMZ(m)
.

\blacksquare

Example 5.2

Let
x_{m,m}<\cdots<x_{1,m}
be the zeros of
\psi_{m}
, and
\lambda_{k,m}
be the corresponding weights in the Gauss quadrature formula:

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k,m}\exp(x_{k,m}^{2}/2)P(x_{k,m})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P(t)\exp(-t^{2}/2)dt,\qquad P\in\Pi_{2m}.

(5.3)

Writing
w_{k,m}=\lambda_{k,m}\exp(x_{k,m}^{2})
, it follows that

\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k,m}|P(x_{k,m})|^{2}=\int|P(t)|^{2}dt,\qquad P\in\Pi_{m}.

Let
\nu_{m}
be the measure that associates the mass
w_{k,m}
with each
x_{k,m}
.
Clearly,
\nu_{m}
satisfies (
5.1
).
In view of
[
47
, Theorem 8.2.7]
(used with
p=2
,
b=0
),
\nu_{m}
satisfies (
5.2
) as well, so that
\nu_{m}\in SMZ(m)
.

\blacksquare

Example 5.3

There exist
c,c_{1}
with the following property
[
2
]
: Let
\{x_{k,m}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be any real numbers,
x_{m,m}<\cdots<x_{1,m}
, such that

[x_{m,m},x_{1,m}]\subseteq[-\sqrt{2m}(1+cm^{-2/3}),\sqrt{2m}(1+cm^{-2/3})],

(5.4)

and

|x_{k,m}-x_{k+1,m}|\leq c_{1}m^{-1/2}.

(5.5)

Let
\nu_{m}
be the measure that associates the mass
x_{k,m}-x_{k+1,m}
with
x_{k,m}
.
Then for
1\leq p<\infty
,

\left|\|P\|_{\nu_{m};p}^{p}-\|P\|_{p}^{p}\right|\leq(1/4)\|P\|_{p}^{p},\qquad P\in\Pi_{m}.

(5.6)

Clearly,
\nu_{m}\in SMZ(m)
.

\blacksquare

6
Least squares approximation

Given integers
m\geq n\geq 1
, a measure
\nu\in SMZ(m)
, and
f\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}})
, we can define the least square approximation to
f
from
\Pi_{n}
by

S_{n}(\nu;f)=\mathop{\hbox{{arg min}}}_{P\in\Pi_{n}}\|f-P\|_{\nu;2}^{2}=\mathop{\hbox{{arg min}}}_{P\in\Pi_{n}}\int|f(x)-P(x)|^{2}d|\nu|(x).

(6.1)

In the sequel, we will assume that
\nu
is a positive measure, so that
\nu=|\nu|
.

Remark 6.1

We note that
S_{n}(\nu;f)
is defined entirely in terms of the restriction of
f
to
\mathsf{supp}(\nu)
. Thus, with the notation as in Section
3
, and
f
as defined in (
4.3
), if
\mathsf{supp}(\nu)\subset[-L,R]
, then
S_{n}(\nu;f)=S_{n}(\nu;f|_{[-L,R]})
.

\blacksquare

We will need an explicit expression for
S_{n}(\nu;f)
in the form
\int f(y)K_{n}(\nu;x,y)d\nu(y)
for a reproducing kernel
K_{n}(\nu;x,y)
(cf. (
6.8
)).

To obtain an expression for this kernel,
we define the Gram matrix
\mathbf{G}
by

\mathbf{G}_{\ell,k}=\int\psi_{\ell}(x)\psi_{k}(x)d\nu(x),\qquad\ell,k=0,\cdots,m-1.

(6.2)

A quadratic form for
\mathbf{G}
is

\sum_{\ell,k}d_{\ell}\overline{d_{k}}\mathbf{G}_{\ell,k}=\int\left|\sum_{j}d_{j}\psi_{j}\right|^{2}d\nu.

(6.3)

Since

\int\left|\sum_{j}d_{j}\psi_{j}(x)\right|^{2}dx=\sum_{j}|d_{j}|^{2},

the estimate (
5.1
) and the Raleigh-Riesz principle shows that
\mathbf{G}
is positive definite, and all of its singular values are bounded from above and below by constants independent of
m
.
In particular,
\mathbf{G}
is invertible and positive definite, with all of the singular values of
\mathbf{G}^{-1}
also bounded from above and below by constants independent of
m
.
Thus, there exists a lower triangular matrix
\mathbf{L}
so that the Cholesky decomposition

\mathbf{G}^{-1}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{*}

(6.4)

holds.
Moreover, for all
\{d_{\ell}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
,

\sum_{\ell,k}d_{\ell}\overline{d_{k}}\mathbf{G}_{\ell,k}\sim\sum_{\ell}|d_{\ell}|^{2}.

(6.5)

Let

\tilde{p}_{j}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\mathbf{L}_{\ell,j}\psi_{\ell},\qquad j=0,\cdots,m-1.

(6.6)

Using the definition of the Gram matrix and the Cholesky decomposition in (
6.4
), it is easy to verify that

\int\tilde{p}_{j}(x)\tilde{p}_{k}(x)d\nu(x)=\delta_{j,k},\qquad j,k=0,\cdots,m-1.

(6.7)

Next, we define the reproducing kernel

K_{n}(\nu;x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\tilde{p}_{j}(x)\tilde{p}_{j}(y)=\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{G}^{-1}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\psi_{\ell}(x)\psi_{\ell^{\prime}}(y),\qquad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(6.8)

The Fourier transform of
K_{n}(\nu;\cdot,y)
is given by

\widehat{K}_{n}(\nu;x,y)=\mathfrak{F}(K_{n}(\nu;\cdot,y))(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathfrak{F}(\tilde{p}_{j})(x)\tilde{p}_{j}(y)

(6.9)

It is not difficult to verify that for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

S_{n}(\nu;f)(x)=\int f(y)K_{n}(\nu;x,y)d\nu(y),\qquad\mathfrak{F}(S_{n}(\nu;f))(\omega)=\int f(y)\widehat{K}_{n}(\nu;\omega,y)d\nu(y).

(6.10)

Computationally, the procedure to find
S_{n}(\nu;f)
is as follows.
We define

\hat{f}(\nu;\ell)=\int f(y)\psi_{\ell}(y)d\nu(y),\qquad\ell=0,\cdots,n-1,

(6.11)

and solve the linear system of equations

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{G}_{\ell,k}d_{k}(f)=\hat{f}(\nu;\ell),\qquad\ell=0,\cdots,n-1.

(6.12)

for
d_{k}(f)
’s.
Then
S_{n}(\nu;f)
is given by

S_{n}(\nu;f)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d_{k}(f)\psi_{k}
.

Remark 6.2

In the case when
\nu
is the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
,
S_{n}(\nu;f)
is the partial sum of the Hermite expansion.
The corresponding reproducing kernel is given by

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi_{k}(x)\psi_{k}(y),\qquad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(6.13)

\blacksquare

|\nu|([-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{n}])\lesssim\sqrt{nm}\sqrt{1/m}\lesssim\sqrt{n}.

Hence, using Proposition
3.1
, with
K_{n}(\nu;x,y)
in place of
P
and
p=1
, followed by Schwarz inequality and (
9.8
),we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|K_{n}(\nu;x,y)|d|\nu|(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{-2\sqrt{n}}^{2\sqrt{n}}|K_{n}(\nu;x,y)|d|\nu|(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\{\int_{-2\sqrt{n}}^{2\sqrt{n}}|K_{n}(\nu;x,y)|^{2}d|\nu|(y)\right\}^{1/2}\left\{|\nu|([-2\sqrt{n},2\sqrt{n}])\right\}^{1/2}\lesssim n^{1/2}.

The estimates (
9.11
) (and hence, (
9.12
)) follow similary.
The estimate (
9.13
) follows similarly as well, using (
9.9
)) and (
6.7
).

\blacksquare

We note that we are working with a least squares approximation, but require estimates in the uniform norm, while the Fourier transform of a function is bounded uniformly by the
L^{1}
norm of the function.
To facilitate the transitions among these norms, we recall the following lemma
[
47
, Theorem 4.2.4]
relating the various norms for weighted polynomials.

Lemma 9.4

For any integer
n\geq 1
and
P\in\Pi_{n}
, we have

\|P\|_{1}\lesssim n^{1/2}\|P\|_{\infty}\lesssim n\|P\|_{1}.

(9.15)

Next, we obtain some constructions which approximate a function
f
in different norms at the same time; i.e., constructions which do not depend upon the norm in which the degree of approximation is measured.
It is not difficult to construct a near-best approximation to
f
from
\Pi_{n}
.
Let
n^{\prime}=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
For
f\in L^{1}+L^{\infty}
, we write

\hat{f}(k)=\int f(y)\psi_{k}(y)dy,

s_{\ell}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}\hat{f}(k)\psi_{k},

and

V_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n-n^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell=n^{\prime}}^{n-1}s_{\ell}(f).

(9.16)

Part (a) of the following lemma is proved in
[
47
, Lemma 4.1.5]
. In the present manuscript,
\exp(-x^{2}/2)
,
f
, and
V_{n}(f)
take the place of
w
,
wf
and
wv_{n}(f)
in that reference. Part (b) follows easily from (
9.12
) and the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem.

Lemma 9.5

If
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
f\in L^{p}
, then

(a)

E_{p,n}\leq\|f-V_{n}(f)\|_{p}\lesssim E_{p,n/2}(f).

(9.17)

and

(b)

E_{p,n}\leq\|f-s_{n}(f)\|_{p}\lesssim n^{1/2}E_{p,n}(f).

(9.18)

With this preparation, we are now ready to prove Theorem
7.1
.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

In view of (
6.10
) and Lemma
9.3
, we see that for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

|S_{n}(\nu;f)(x)|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)K_{n}(\nu;x,y)d\nu(y)\right|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|K_{n}(\nu;x,y)|d|\nu|(y)\lesssim n^{1/2}\|f\|_{\infty};

i.e.,

\|S_{n}(\nu;f)\|_{\infty}\lesssim n^{1/2}\|f\|_{\infty}.

Since
S_{n}(\nu;f)\in\Pi_{n}
,
Lemma
9.4
leads to

\|S_{n}(\nu;f)\|_{1}\lesssim n^{1/2}\|S_{n}(\nu;f)\|_{\infty}\lesssim n\|f\|_{\infty}.

(9.19)

Next, we observe that for any
P\in\Pi_{n}
,

S_{n}(\nu;P)(x)=\int P(y)K_{n}(\nu;x,y)d\nu(y)=P(x).

(9.20)

Hence, using (
9.19
), we have for
any

P\in\Pi_{n}
,

\|f-S_{n}(\nu;f)\|_{1}=\|f-P-S_{n}(\nu;f-P)\|_{1}\leq\|f-P\|_{1}+\|S_{n}(\nu;f-P)\|_{1}\lesssim\|f-P\|_{1}+n\|f-P\|_{\infty}.

(9.21)

We use this estimate with
V_{n}(f)
in place of
P
and use Lemma
9.5
(a) to arrive at (
7.2
).
If we use
s_{n}(f)
in place of
P
in (
9.21
) instead, then Lemma
9.5
(b) leads to (
7.3
).

\blacksquare

Next, we turn to the proof of Theorem
7.2
, which again requires some further preparation.

The generating function for Hermite polynomials is given by (cf.
[
66
, Formula (5.5.7)]
))

\exp(2xw-w^{2})=\pi^{1/4}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k/2}}{\sqrt{k!}}h_{k}(x)w^{k}.

(9.22)

If
\lambda>0
, then using
w=-\lambda/2
in (
9.22
), we obtain

\exp(-x\lambda)=\pi^{1/4}\exp(\lambda^{2}/4)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k/2}\sqrt{k!}}h_{k}(x)\lambda^{k},

so that

g_{\lambda}(x)=\exp(-x\lambda-x^{2}/2)=\pi^{1/4}\exp(\lambda^{2}/4)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2^{k/2}\sqrt{k!}}\psi_{k}(x)\lambda^{k}.

(9.23)

Next, we recall that

\|\psi_{k}\|_{1}\lesssim k^{1/4},\qquad\|\psi_{k}\|_{\infty}\lesssim k^{-1/4}.

(9.24)

We observe further using the Schwarz inequality that for any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\sqrt{2}x)^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}2^{-k/2}\leq\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\sqrt{2}x)^{2k}}{k!}\right\}^{1/2}\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\right\}^{1/2}=\exp(x^{2}).

(9.25)

Finally, we note that

\frac{(n+k-1)!}{(n-1)!}=k!\left(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n+k-1}{n-1}\right)\geq k!.

Using (
9.24
), (
9.25
), and the above estimate, we deduce that

\displaystyle\exp(\lambda^{2}/4)E_{1,n}(g_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{k^{1/4}}{2^{k/2}\sqrt{k!}}\lambda^{k}\leq\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{1/4}\sqrt{(k-1)!}}(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{k}\leq n^{-1/4}\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(k-1)!}}(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{k}

(9.26)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{n}}{n^{1/4}\sqrt{(n-1)!}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{k}\lesssim\frac{(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{n}}{n^{1/4}\sqrt{(n-1)!}}\exp(\lambda^{2}/2).

We estimate
E_{\infty,n}(g_{\lambda})
similarly and conclude that

E_{1,n}(g_{\lambda})\lesssim\frac{n^{1/4}}{\sqrt{n!}}(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{n}\exp(\lambda^{2}/4),\qquad E_{\infty,n}(g_{\lambda})\lesssim\frac{n^{-1/4}}{\sqrt{n!}}(\lambda/\sqrt{2})^{n}\exp(\lambda^{2}/4).

(9.27)

With this preparation, Theorem
7.2
is now easy to prove.

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

We apply Theorem
7.1
with
f
defined in (
4.3
), taking
\nu
to be supported on
[-L,R]
(i.e.,
\mathsf{supp}(\nu)\subseteq[-L,R]
).
We note that in view of Remark
6.1
, we may assume that
f
is defined on the entire real axis rather than just on
[-L,R]
.
We note that

f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}g_{\lambda_{k}}(t).

Hence, Theorem

is a simple consequence of Theorem

and the estimates (

\blacksquare

The next objective of this section is to estimate
\int E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)d\nu(y)
.
We will use the following lemma, Höffding’s inequality (
[
9
, Theorem 2.8]
), several times. Note that this inequality quantifies the degree to which the sum of the indicated random variables can exceed a given value; it is an example of a concentration inequality.

Lemma 9.6

(
Höffding’s inequality
) Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{M}
be i.i.d. random variables with mean
0
, and each
X_{k}\in[a_{k},b_{k}]
. Then for any
t>0
,

\mathsf{Prob}\left\{\left|\sum_{k=1}^{M}X_{k}\right|\geq t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}(b_{k}-a_{k})^{2}}\right).

(9.28)

We also need a covering lemma (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
).

Lemma 9.7

There exists a set
{\mathcal{C}}
with at most
\sim n
elements such that for any
P\in\Pi_{n}
,

\|P\|_{\infty}\sim\max_{y\in{\mathcal{C}}}|P(y)|.

(9.29)

With these preliminaries, we can now prove Theorem
7.3
.

Proof of Theorem
7.3
.

We observe first that in view of (
5.2
) and (
3.8
),

\left\|\int_{|y|\geq C\sqrt{m}}E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(\circ,y)d\nu(y)\right\|_{\infty}\lesssim\left\|\int_{|y|\geq C\sqrt{m}}|E(y)||\hat{K}_{n}(\circ,y)|d\nu(y)\right\|_{\infty}\lesssim Sm^{1/2}\exp(-cn).

(9.30)

Now, let
w\in{\mathbb{R}}
be fixed for the time being (until (
9.36
) is proved).
With
C_{1}
as in Lemma
9.2
, we divide the interval
[-C\sqrt{m},C\sqrt{m}]
into subintervals
I_{k}
, each of length at most
2C_{1}/\sqrt{m}
, so that
|\nu|(I_{k})\lesssim m^{-1/2}
.
We denote the characteristic (indicator) function of
I_{k}
by
\chi_{k}
. We then consider the random variables

X_{k}=\int_{I_{k}}E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)d\nu(y)=\int E(y)\chi_{k}(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)d\nu(y),

(9.31)

so that

\int_{|y|\leq Cm^{1/2}}E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)d\nu(y)=\sum_{k}X_{k}.

(9.32)

Since the expected value of each
E(y)
is
0
, that of each
X_{k}
is
0
as well.
Further, for each
k
,

|X_{k}|=\left|\int E(y)\chi_{k}(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)d\nu(y)\right|\leq S\int|\chi_{k}(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)|d\nu(y).

(9.33)

Using the Schwarz inequality and Lemma
9.2
, we see that

\left(\int|\chi_{k}(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)|d\nu(y)\right)^{2}\leq\nu(I_{k})\left\{\int_{I_{k}}|\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)|^{2}d\nu(y)\right\}\lesssim m^{-1/2}\left\{\int_{I_{k}}|\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)|^{2}d\nu(y)\right\};

(9.34)

i.e., with

r_{k}=Sm^{-1/4}\left\{\int_{I_{k}}|\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)|^{2}d\nu(y)\right\}^{1/2},

|X_{k}|\lesssim r_{k}\qquad\mbox{for all }k.

Using (
9.9
), we conclude that

\sum_{k}r_{k}^{2}\lesssim S^{2}(n/m)^{1/2}.

(9.35)

So, Höffding’s inequality implies that

\mathsf{Prob}\left\{\left|\int_{|y|\leq Cm^{1/2}}E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(w,y)d\nu(y)\right|\geq t\right\}=\mathsf{Prob}\left\{\left|\sum_{k}X_{k}\right|\geq t\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{S^{2}(n/m)^{1/2}}\right).

(9.36)

We note that this estimate holds for each
w\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
\sum_{k}X_{k}\in\Pi_{n}
.
In view of the covering lemma, Lemma
9.7
, this leads to

\mathsf{Prob}\left\{\left\|\int_{|y|\leq Cm^{1/2}}E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(\circ,y)d\nu(y)\right\|_{\infty}\geq t\right\}\lesssim n\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{S^{2}(n/m)^{1/2}}\right).

(9.37)

Choosing

t=cS(n/m)^{1/4}\sqrt{\log(n/\delta)},

we deduce that with probability

\left\|\int_{|y|\leq Cm^{1/2}}E(y)\hat{K}_{n}(\circ,y)d\nu(y)\right\|_{\infty}\lesssim S(n/m)^{1/4}\sqrt{\log(n/\delta)}.

Together with (
9.30

7.7
) with a judicious choice of

\blacksquare

10
Conclusions

The analysis of multiexponential decaying signals is a longstanding problem in mathematics and physics. We have introduced a novel method for parameter estimation in this setting based on the fact that the Hermite polynomials are eigenfunctions of the Fourier transform. This enables us to avoid direct use of the notoriously ill-posed inverse Laplace transform for multiexponential analysis, although as indicated by our numerical results, the transformation to the Fourier domain retains the native ill-posedness of the original problem. Our numerical results, presented for the special case of biexponential decay, demonstrate the success of the method for a wide range of decay constants, including in regimes where the more traditional method of NLLS fails. Again, the sensitivity of the method to noise serves as a sobering reminder of its genesis in the inverse Laplace transform.

Appendix A
Fourier-based estimation of the
\lambda
’s

In this section, we summarize the approach to obtaining the values of
\lambda_{k}
from the Fourier transform
\mathfrak{F}(\tilde{\mu})(\omega)
as in (
4.6
).

We consider a measure of the form

\mu^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}.

When we sample
\mathfrak{F}(\mu^{\prime})(\omega)
at
\omega=\delta\ell
,
|\ell|<N
for some
\delta
and write
\omega_{k}=\lambda_{k}\delta
, we obtain the Fourier coefficients of a periodic measure
\mu
:

\hat{\mu}(j)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}b_{k}\exp(-ij\omega_{k}),\qquad j=-N+1,\cdots,N-1.

(A.1)

Of course, it is assumed that the points
\omega_{k}\in[0,2\pi]
.
In this section
\|\cdot\|
will denote
|(\cdot)\mbox{ mod }2\pi|
.
The material in this section is based on
[
29
,
49
,
50
]
.

Clearly, the periodic measure
\mu
can be recovered formally by its Fourier series, and one is tempted to use the partial sum to take into account that only finitely many Fourier coefficients are known.
However, a judiciously filtered Fourier sum avoids sidelobes and yields greater accuracy.
Accordingly, we introduce such a sum (see (
A.3
)) using a kernel.
Let
h:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
be an infinitely differentiable, even function, such that
h(t)=1/2
for
|t|\leq 1/2
and
h(t)=0
for
|t|\geq 1
. We introduce the kernel

\Phi_{N}(t)=\sum_{\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}}h\left(\frac{|\ell|}{N}\right)\exp(i\ell t)=\sum_{|\ell|<N}h\left(\frac{|\ell|}{N}\right)\exp(i\ell t),\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ N>0.

(A.2)

It is not difficult to see that

\sigma_{N}(\mu)(x)=\sum_{|\ell|<N}h\left(\frac{|\ell|}{N}\right)\hat{\mu}(\ell)\exp(i\ell x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\Phi_{N}(x-\omega_{k}).

(A.3)

Now, the kernel
\Phi_{N}
has the localization property that

|\Phi_{N}(t)|\lesssim\frac{\Phi_{N}(0)}{\max(1,(N\|t\|)^{S})},\qquad N\geq 1,\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(A.4)

If
\eta=\min_{j\not=k}\|(\omega_{j}-\omega_{k}\|
, then it is clear that if
N\eta\geq 4
and
\|x-\omega_{k}\|\geq\eta/4
for all
k
, then

|\sigma_{N}(\mu)(x)|\lesssim\frac{\Phi_{N}(0)}{(N\eta)^{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|a_{k}|.

(A.5)

On the other hand, for any
x
which does not satisfy the above condition, there is a unique
j
such that
\|x-\omega_{j}\|<\eta/4
.
We have

|\sigma_{N}(\omega_{j})|\geq\Phi_{N}(0)|a_{j}|-c\frac{\Phi_{N}(0)}{(N\eta)^{S}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|a_{k}|

for a suitable constant
c>0
.
Using the Bernstein inequality, a concentration inequality related to Hoeffding’s inequality that incorporates the variance of the summed random variables, for the trigonometric polynomials, it follows that for sufficiently large values of
N
, the prominent peaks of
|\sigma_{N}(x)|
appear within
c_{1}/N
of the values of the
\omega_{j}
’s.
We refer the reader to the above cited literature for further details.
