Abstract
. We introduce a new Swarm-Based Gradient Descent (SBGD) method for non-convex optimization. The swarm consists of agents, each is identified with a position, 𝐱 \mathbf{x} , and mass, m m . The key to their dynamics is communication: masses are being transferred from agents at high ground to low(-est) ground. At the same time, agents change positions with step size, h = h ⁡ ( 𝐱 , m ) h=h(\mathbf{x},m) , adjusted to their relative mass: heavier agents proceed with small time-steps in the direction of local gradient, while lighter agents take larger time-steps based on a backtracking protocol. Accordingly, the crowd of agents is dynamically divided between ‘heavier’ leaders, expected to approach local minima, and ‘lighter’ explorers. With their large-step protocol, explorers are expected to encounter improved position for the swarm; if they do, then they assume the role of ‘heavy’ swarm leaders and so on. Convergence analysis and numerical simulations in one-, two-, and 20-dimensional benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of SBGD as a global optimizer.

1.
Introduction

The classical Gradient Descent (GD) methods for optimization,

\mathop{\argmin}_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(\mathbf{x})
, explore the ambient space by marching along the directions dictated by local gradients,
\nabla F(\mathbf{x})
.
Once the marching direction is determined, the remaining key aspect is a choice of step size.

More often than not, however, GD protocols get trapped in basins of attraction of local minima, and therefore are not suitable for global optimization of non-convex functions.

In this work we introduce a swarm-based gradient descent approach for global optimization.

Communication between agents of the swarm plays a key role in dictating their step size.

Here, the usual ambient space of positions is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]
: each agent is characterized by its time-dependent position,
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t^{n})\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and its relative weight,
\widetilde{m}_{i}(t^{n})\in[0,1]
.

An interplay between positions and weights proceeds by communicating a dynamic mass transition from high to low, thus our protocol favors agents positioned on ‘lower grounds’.

Looking ahead, the time-stepping protocol is then adjusted according to the distinction between ‘heavy’ agents taking small time steps, and ‘light’ agents taking large(-r) time steps.

While heavy agents take smaller time-steps, expecting their convergence toward a local minimum, light agents proceed with larger time-steps, so that they explore larger regions, away from local basins of attraction; they are expected to improve the global position of the swarm.
In the sequel, those light explorers are expected to encounter a ‘better’ minimizing ground.

Then, these light explorers are gradually converted into heavier, global leaders of the swarm.

Here, the dynamic distinction between heavy leaders and light explorers enables a simultaneous approach towards local minimizers, while keep searching for even better
global minimizers
.

Let us recall other well-known multi-agent optimization algorithms based on ‘wisdom of the crowd’ — particle swarm optimization
[
15
,
9
]
,
2-agent simulated annealing
[
7
]
, ant colony optimization
[
31
]
, genetic algorithms
[

,

]
and consensus-based optimization
[
21
,

]
.

Our Swarm-Based Gradient descent (SBGD) method is shown to be a most effective optimizer, in particular, when the unknown global minimizer is away from the initial swarm.

Visiting
larger portions of the ambient space, using explorers based on the communication in swarm dynamics, proved an essential feature for such optimization of remote
minimizers
.
Equally important role is played by the leading agents of the swarm: using the backtracking we prove the sequence of leaders must converge to a minimizer with a quantified rate.

Description
of the SBGD method, given in Section
2
, highlights the decisive role of communication; indeed, the SBGD can be viewed as alignment dynamics towards minimal heading.

A precise
time-stepping protocol based on backtracking line search, is outlined in
Section

.
Detouring the general paradigm of our swarm-based optimization, we note in Section
4
that our recipe for dynamically adjusting the weights can be extended to more general
protocols
.
In particular, our implementation of SBGD enforces elimination of ‘worst’ agent at each iteration. This ‘survival of the fittest’ approach can be relaxed, increasing the exploring capabilities at the expense of additional computational time. In Section
5
we present convergence and error analysis of SBGD. The time-stepping protocol of backtracking implies that the time sequence of SBGD minimizers has a limit set of one (or more) equi-height minima, and depending on the ‘flatness’ of
F

N

N

N

2.
The Swarm-Based Gradient Descent (SBGD) algorithm

The SBGD dynamics consists of three main ingredients.

☞

Agents
. Each agent is identified by its position,
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and its mass,
m_{i}(t)\in(0,1]
. The total mass is kept constant in time,
\sum_{i}m_{i}(t)=1
.

☞

Protocol for time step
. The position of each agent is dynamically adjusted by taking a time step
h_{i}
in the gradient direction,
\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}(t))

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)=-h_{i}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)).

The time step,
h_{i}
, depends on the position of the agent at,
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)
,
and
on its relative mass,
\widetilde{m}_{i}(t)
,

\widetilde{m}_{i}(t):=\frac{m_{i}(t)}{m_{+}(t)},\quad m_{+}(t)=\max_{i}m_{i}(t).

The precise dynamic protocol for choosing the step size, based on backtracking, is outlined below. A key aspect is choosing
h_{i}
as a decreasing function of the relative mass,
\widetilde{m}_{i}
: ‘heavier’ agents move slower, while ‘lighter’ agents take larger time steps.
An alternative point of view is to interpret the
\widetilde{m}_{i}
’s as the probabilities of agents to identify global minimum: those with mass
m_{i}(t)\ll m_{+}(t)
take large time steps to explore the region of interest, since their probability of identifying the global minimum at their current position,
\mathbf{x}_{i}(t)
, is low.

☞

Communication
. Let
\displaystyle F_{\textnormal{max}}(t)=\max_{j}F(\mathbf{x}_{j}(t))
and
\displaystyle F_{\textnormal{min}}(t)=\min_{j}F(\mathbf{x}_{j}(t))
denote the maximal and respectively, minimal heights of the swarm at time
t
. The mass of each agent,
m_{i}(t)
, is dynamically adjusted according to its
relative height
,
\displaystyle\eta_{i}(t)
,

\qquad\ \ \left\{\begin{split}\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}m_{i}(t)&=-\phi_{p}(\eta_{i}(t))m_{i}(t),\ i\neq i(t)\\
m_{i}(t)&=1-\sum\limits_{\ j\neq i(t)}\!\!m_{j}(t),\ \ \,i=i(t):=\argmin_{i}F(x_{i}(t)),\end{split}\right.\quad\eta_{i}(t):=\frac{F(\mathbf{x}_{i}(t))-F_{\textnormal{min}}(t)}{F_{\textnormal{max}}(t)-F_{\textnormal{min}}(t)}.

Thus, each agent ‘sheds’ a fraction of its mass,
\phi_{p}(\eta_{i}(t))\in(0,1]
, which is transferred to the current
global minimizer
at
\mathbf{x}_{i(t)}
(here we allow to adjust the mass transition,
\phi_{p}(\eta)=\eta^{p}
, using a user choice of a fine-tuning parameter
p>0
, with the default choice
p=1
).
As the global minimizer becomes ‘heavier’, it will be ‘cautious’, taking smaller time steps while enabling the other, ‘lighter’ agents, to take larger time steps. As the lighter agents explore the ambient space with larger time steps, it will increase their likelihood to encounter a new neighborhood of a global minimum, which in turn may place one of them as the new heaviest global minimizer and so on. Observe that the larger
p
is, the more tamed the mass transition of
\phi_{p}(\eta_{i}(t))
.

The discrete time
marching of SBGD is realized by agents positioned at
\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}(t^{n+1})
with masses
m^{n+1}_{i}=m_{i}(t^{n+1})
at discrete time steps
t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t
. We use the simple forward time discretization with time step
\Delta t=1
, acting on all non-empty agents,
m_{i}^{n}>0
,

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left.\begin{array}[]{lll}\ m_{i}^{n+1}&=m^{n}_{i}-\phi_{p}(\eta^{n}_{i})m^{n}_{i},&i\neq{i^{-}_{n}}\\
\\
\ m_{i^{-}_{n}}^{n+1}&=\displaystyle m_{i^{-}_{n}}^{n}+\sum\limits_{i\neq{i^{-}_{n}}}\phi_{p}(\eta^{n}_{i})m_{i}^{n},&\end{array}\right\}\quad{i^{-}_{n}}:=\mathop{\argmin}_{i}F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})\\
\\
\quad\displaystyle m^{n+1}_{+}:=\max_{i}m^{n+1}_{i}\\
\quad\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}-h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big)\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}),\quad\displaystyle\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}=\frac{m^{n+1}_{i}}{m^{n+1}_{+}}\par\end{array}\right\}\ \ m_{i}^{n}>0.

Initially, the agents are placed at random positions,
\{\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i}\}
with equi-distributed masses
\{m^{0}_{i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\}
. At each iteration, masses of agents are exchanged according to their relative heights, and the positions of agents are updated in the direction of the local gradient, with time step,

h^{n}_{i}=h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big)
, depending on these relative mass. Note that the agent with the worst configuration, positioned at

\mathbf{x}_{+}=\argmax F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})
, is eliminated from the computation; consequently, the size of the swarm decreases, one agent at a time, until it remains with the one heaviest agent. Our choice for the time-stepping protocol,
h\big(\mathbf{x},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m})\big)
, is the
backtracking line search
outlined in §
3.2
, which is weighted by the relative masses,
\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})
(again, here we allow fine-tuning the dependence on the relative mass,
\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m})=\widetilde{m}^{q}
, based on a user choice of
q>0
, with the default choice
q=1
).
The backtracking enforces a descent property for the SBGD iterations
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}
,
and the parameter,
\lambda\in(0,1)
, dictates how much the descent property holds in the sense that (
3.4
)
below
is fulfilled. The communication is designed so that the total mass of the swarm gradually concentrates with the agents most likely to become the global minimizers, that is, the agents which will most likely to reach the global minimum of the region explored so far by the swarm. Such ‘heavy’ agents are assigned with relatively small step sizes, as they are suspected to be close to ’good’ minimizers, hence their subsequent explorations should be sufficiently cautious. On the other hand, the ‘lighter’ agents should not be trapped in basins of attraction of local minimizers, so they proceed with larger step sizes, allowing them to explore a larger regions, during which they may encounter ‘better’ minimizers; then they may be gradually converted from ‘light explorers’ into ‘heavy leaders’ and so on.

We note the flexibility of the SBGD communication protocol, depending on fractional mass transition,
\phi_{p}(\eta^{n}_{i})
, and the mass-dependent step size,
h(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}))
. Their detailed construction is outlined in §
3
.
The algorithm (
2.1
) then forms a family of swarm-based methods, denoted SBGD
pq
whenever we want to emphasize its dependence on the parameters
(p,q)
; the ‘vanilla’ version corresponding to
(p,q)=(1,1)
is denoted simply by SBGD.

2.1.
Why communication is important

Consider the particular scenario in which all agents are assigned with the same constant mass, i.e.,
m^{n}_{i}\equiv\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}
and
\eta_{i}^{n}\equiv 0
, so that
\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}_{i}^{n})\equiv 1
yields

(2.2)

\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}-h(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda)\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}),\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,N.

In this case, there is no mass transition and the dynamics is reduced to a crowd of
non-communicating
agents. In particular, each agent makes its own decision to proceed with variable step size based on the backtracking protocol outlined in section
3.2
below.
We refer to this as the backtracking GD, or GD(BT) method. There is also the vanilla version of Gradient Descent, denoted GD(h), which proceeds with a fixed step size
h(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda)\equiv h
. In either case, we have
N
agents, exploring the region of interest independently of each other.
Of course, if there are
N
such agents exploring the region of interest, the corresponding GD(
h
) and GD(BT) method are expected to be “
N
times better” than their the single-agent versions. However, compared with the swarm of
N
communicating agents, we find that the SBGD dynamics has a superior
global
behavior. Specifically, the advantage of communication in SBGD dynamics becomes apparent in exploring larger regions for potential global minimum. This will be borne out in the numerical results presented in sections
6
,
7
and
8
. Here, we demonstrate the benefit of communication with a simple example of an objective function shown in Figure
2.1
,

(2.3)

F(x)=e^{\sin(2x^{2})}+\frac{1}{10}\big(x-\frac{\pi}{2}\big)^{2}.

The function admits multiple local minima, with a unique global minimum (
x^{*}\approx 1.5355

pq

2.1

h

h

h_{0}

2.2.
Alignment towards minimal heading

The dynamic adjustment of masses in SBGD leads to a gradual distinction between ‘leaders’ and ‘explorers’, according to their relative masses. This adjustment of masses (or probabilities),
\widetilde{m}_{i}^{n}
, is dictated by the communication among agents. The SBGD method (
2.1
) can be also interpreted as a particular case of
alignment dynamics
, e.g.,
[
26
]
, in which agents steer towards the
minimal
heading, instead of steering towards the average heading
[
23
]
. In the context of alignment for opinion dynamics, for example, the parameter
\widetilde{m}_{i}^{n}
can be viewed as fraction of the population supporting ‘opinion’
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}
.

This is reminiscent of the Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) method,
first proposed in
[
21
]
and further modified and analyzed in
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
11
]
; see recent survey
[
28
]
. The CBO method lets a swarm of agents evolve their positions,
\{\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}\}
, by a stochastic motion in search of a global minimizer,

(2.4)

\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}=-\lambda(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}-{\mathbf{m}}^{N}_{t}){\textnormal{d}}t+\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}-{\mathbf{m}}^{N}_{t}){\textnormal{d}}{\bf W}^{j}_{t},\quad\lambda,\sigma>0

Agents are driven by two types of motions: the drift towards an exponentially weighted average,
{\mathbf{m}}^{N}_{t}:=\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}(F_{e})^{j}_{t}\big)^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}(F_{e})^{j}_{t},\ (F_{e})^{j}_{t}:={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\textnormal{exp}}}(-\alpha F(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}))
, and the stochastic diffusion,
{\textnormal{d}}{\bf W}^{j}_{t}
(implemented by independent Brownian motion different components,
\sum^{d}_{k=1}(\mathbf{x}^{j}_{t}-{\mathbf{m}}^{N}_{t})_{k}{\textnormal{d}}W^{j,k}_{t}\vec{e}_{k}
). By the Laplace principle,
[
2
]
, the exponentially weighted average,
{\mathbf{m}}^{N}_{t}
, concentrates most of its weight with agents of minimal height (or smallest loss). Thus, the drift in (
2.4
)
aligns
towards those agents with minimal heading, while the stochastic diffusion is responsible for enhancing the other agents to explore a larger portion of the domain. This should be compared with the deterministic SBGD method, where explorers are driven by communication with lightweight agents.

Unlike gradient-based methods, the CBO has the advantage of avoiding computation of gradients, which are replaced here by a drift towards the
\alpha
-weighted average. In actual computation, however, in particular in high-dimensional problems, the CBO method is sensitive to the application of the
\alpha
-weighted Laplace principle, requiring
\alpha\gg 1
, which is likely to significantly damage the quality of the solution. It is also sensitive to the choice of parameters
\lambda
and
\sigma
,
[
5
]
.

3.
Implementation of the SBGD
pq
algorithm

The success of the SBGD
pq
method relies heavily on two main procedures: (i) a properly defined communication protocol which dictates mass transition factors,

\{\phi_{p}(\eta^{n}_{i})\}
; and (ii) an effective strategy for taking step size,
h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big)
, which is adjusted to the position and the relative mass of a given agent.
In this section, we discuss the details of these procedures, which are summarized in corresponding pseudo-codes.

3.1.
Communications and mass transition

Let
\displaystyle{i^{-}_{n}}=\argmin_{i}F(\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i})
denote the global minimizer at iteration number
n
or time level
t^{n}
. All other agents will shed part (or all) of their mass,
\phi_{p}(\eta^{n}_{i})m^{n}_{i},\ i\neq{i^{-}_{n}}
, which will be transitioned to the mass of the global minimizer
\displaystyle m^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}}\mapsto m^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}}+\sum_{i\neq{i^{-}_{n}}}\phi_{p}(\eta^{n}_{i})m^{n}_{i}
. The fraction of mass loss,
\eta^{n}_{i}
is determined by the
relative height

(3.1)

\eta^{n}_{i}:=\frac{F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-F^{n}_{\textnormal{min}}}{F^{n}_{\textnormal{max}}-F^{n}_{\textnormal{min}}+\epsilon},

where
\displaystyle F^{n}_{\textnormal{min}}=\min_{j}F(\mathbf{x}^{n}_{j})
and
\displaystyle F^{n}_{\textnormal{max}}=\max_{j}F(\mathbf{x}^{n}_{j})
are the current global extremes
1
1

1

To prevent vanishing denominator in the extreme case
F_{\textnormal{max}}=F_{\textnormal{min}}
, we introduce a small
\epsilon
-correction, say
\epsilon=10^{-10}
; the effect on the numerical performance is minimal.
. In this fashion, the ‘higher’ the agent is, the more mass it will lose, and indeed, the highest agent in each iteration will be eliminated
2
2

2

To be precise, the worst agent is eliminated whenever its relative height
\eta^{n}_{i}=1
. This can be realized only when
\epsilon=0
. When
\epsilon>0
, elimination of worst agents takes place whenever
1-\eta^{n}_{i}={\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon)\ll 1
.
.
The function
\phi
is user-dependent; for example
\phi_{p}(\eta)=\eta^{p},\ p>0
enables adjusting the amount of mass transition, with mass transition tamed as
p\uparrow\infty
.
This usage of a relative height keeps a minimal amount of global communication necessary to calibrate each agent relative to the current extremes of the crowd, while being invariant under the transition and dilation of the target function. Therefore, the computation of the relative height is more stable.

3.2.
Backtracking – a protocol for time stepping

Consider the vanilla Gradient Descent (GD) iteration

\mathbf{x}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}^{n}-h\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n}).

The new position,
\mathbf{x}^{n+1}=\mathbf{x}^{n+1}(h)
, is viewed as a function of the step size,
h
. A proper strategy for choosing the step size is now the key for the success of GD iterations. We recall the classical
backtracking line search
,
[
20
, §3]
, which is a computational realization of the well-known Wolfe conditions
[
30
,
1
]
for inexact line searches. The idea is to secure an acceptable step length,
h
, that enforces a sufficient amount of height reduction (or loss) in the target function

(3.2)

F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}(h))\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}^{n})-\lambda h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n})|^{2},\quad\lambda\in(0,1).

Of course, since
F((\mathbf{x}^{n+1}(h))=F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n})|^{2}+\textnormal{\lx@text@lbrace higher order terms\lx@text@rbrace}
, then
(
3.2
) holds for any fixed
0<\lambda<1
, provided the time step is small enough,
h\ll 1
. The purpose is to secure (
3.2
) for large enough
h
, so that we maximize the size of descent,
\lambda h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n})|^{2}
.
To this end, one employs a dynamic adjustment, starting with a relatively large
h
(for which one expects

\displaystyle F\big(\mathbf{x}^{n}-h\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n})\big)>F(\mathbf{x}^{n})-\lambda h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n})|^{2}
)
and then
repeatedly

until (

) is observed. At this stage, we have a final step size,

, which secure (
3.2
)

F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}(h))\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}^{n})-\lambda h(\mathbf{x}^{n},\lambda)|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n})|^{2}.

Thus, the protocol for step size hinges on the choice of the parameter
\lambda
which dictates the amount of descent property (
3.2
). One expects that as the iterations approach the (potentially) global minimum their descent property is ‘tamed’ with a larger
\lambda
, yet they should be able to avoid getting trapped in local basins of attraction by allowing smaller
\lambda\ll 1
. This is exactly where we take advantage of our swarm-based approach: as a compromise between these two conflicting requirements, we offer to use the relative mass of different agents,
\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}
, as an indicator which distinguishes between the ‘heavier’ agents who are potentially close to the global minimizer, and the ‘lighter’ agents which are allowed to take large(r) time steps.
We therefore adjust the descent parameter
\lambda
to each SBGD
pq
agent positioned at
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}
, according to its relative mass
\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}

(3.3)

h^{n}_{i}=h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big)\qquad\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}:=\frac{m^{n+1}_{i}}{m{}^{n+1}_{+}},\quad m{}^{n+1}_{+}:=\max_{i}m^{n+1}_{i},\ \ 0<\lambda<1.

The parameter
q>0
tunes the role of the relative mass
\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}
: as
q
increases then
\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})
decreases, and backtracking allows intermediate agents to take larger time steps.
The pseudo-code for computing the SBGD
pq

.
The results reported in §

\cdot

\cdot

Unless otherwise specified, all simulations reported in this paper employ the same thresholds

4.
A general outlook

We are aware that there are many possible extensions that can be worked out in connection with the SBGD algorithm, leading to a large class of swarm-based optimizers (SBO) with better communication protocols.
We mention three of them.

\cdot

General gradient descent directions

Our SBGD approach can be used with a more general set of gradient descent directions,
{\mathbf{p}}^{n}_{i}

\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}+h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big){\mathbf{p}}^{n}_{i},\qquad\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{n}_{i},\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})\rangle<0.

The convergence results in §
5
, with appropriate adjustments, remain valid. Moreover, the variety in choice of directions, other than local gradients, may offer a better ‘covering’ of the ambient space

\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
This swarm-based descent approach with
randomly
chosen descent directions satisfying
\langle{\mathbf{p}}^{n}_{i},\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})\rangle<0
was pursued in our recent work
[
27
]
.

\cdot

Swarm-based optimization — a general paradigm

The general paradigm for our swarm-based optimization is realized by embedding the
d
-dimensional ambient space in
(\mathbf{x},\widetilde{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times[0,1]
; here
\widetilde{m}
is an additional parameter space of masses/weights (or probabilities, or ‘fractional population’, …) which serves as a communication platform for the crowd of agents positioned at
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In this context, one can combine such communication-based swarm iterations with any single agent time-marching protocol. As examples we refer to the recent
adaptive GD method
[
17
]
and the references therein. In the present work we use the time-marching protocol of gradient-descent, based on backtracking search. Other time marching protocols can be used.

\cdot

Survival of the fittest

The communication in SBGD is designed so that in each iteration, the ‘worst’ agent, positioned at
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{{i^{+}}}:=\argmax_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})
, is eliminated, as it loses all of its mass (
\eta_{i^{+}}^{n}=1
). This policy of ‘survival of the fittest’ implies that the number of
N
initial active agents decreases in each iteration until the SBGD remains with only a single, ‘heaviest’ agent, which proceeds by the GD(BT) protocol. In particular, this policy implies that for small swarms, say
N\sim 10
, the performance of SBGD iterations is expected to be similar or only slightly better than GD(BT) iterations, as borne out in the numerical simulations reported in sections
7
and
8
. Alternatively, one can design a less restrictive evolutionary policy that will allow ‘worst’ agents below a certain threshold to survive. This will evolve a larger set of explorers for longer times, with a greater chance of exploring new and better minima unseen before. Our numerical experiments show that a balanced policy for the ‘fittest’ can indeed have a substantial effect on the final result, at the expense of
increased computational time.
The main target of these different policies is to computational efficiency; we emphasize that the convergence analysis carried out on §
5
is independent of the ‘evolutionary policy’.

5.
Convergence and error analysis

The study of convergence and error estimates for the SBGD method requires to quantify the behavior of
F
. Here we emphasize that the required smoothness properties of
F
are only sought in the region explored by the SBGD iterations. We assume that there exists a
bounded
region,
\Omega\ni\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}
for all agents. Since the SBGD allows light agents to explore the ambient space with large step size (starting with
h_{0}
), we do not have apriori bound on
\Omega
; in particular, the footprint of the SBGD crowd,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\cup_{n}}}\textnormal{conv}_{i}\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}\}
, may expand well beyond its initial convex hull

\textnormal{conv}_{i}\{\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i}\}
.
We let
\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}F(\mathbf{x})
denote the global minimum value in that region. This is the minimum that we would wish to converge to
.

We consider the class of loss functions,
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, with Lipschitz bound

(5.1)

|\nabla F(\mathbf{x})-\nabla F(\mathbf{y})|\leqslant L|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|,\quad\forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega.

We begin by recalling the lower bound on the step size, secured by the
backtracking line search in algorithm
1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider the SBGD
pq
iterations (
2.1
), with step size
h^{n}_{i}=h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big)
determined by the backtracking line search in algorithm,
1
, with shrinkage factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
and initial step size,
h_{0}
, large enough so that

(5.2)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{h_{0}>\frac{2}{L}}}.

Then we have the descent bound

(5.3)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i})\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-\frac{2\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}\big)\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2},\qquad\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}-h^{n}_{i}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}).}}

We make two comments before turning to the proof of the lemma.

First we note that if one employs a step size
h
small enough then there holds

(5.4)

F\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}-h\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})\big)\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-\frac{2\gamma}{L}\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2}.

However, in this case the ‘amount of descent’,
\displaystyle\frac{2\gamma}{L}\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2}
, is comparably small with
h\ll 1
. In contrast, (
5.3
) secures the amount of descent which depends on the relative mass, but otherwise is independent of the smallness of
h
. To this end, we need to secure a step size
h^{n}_{i}

large enough
; this is the goal of the backtracking algorithm
1
.

Second — it is customary to find stability results which state that “if
h
is small enough than such and such”; typically in this case, e.g., (
5.4
), “smallness” is measured by
1/L
and the difficulty is that in general we have no access to how large
L
is. The point of Lemma
5.1
is that the descant (
5.3
) holds
without
access to
L
(to be precise, (
5.2
) requires
1/L
; however, here
h_{0}
needs to be
large
relative to
1/L
)
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

We claim that the descent bound holds

(5.5a)

F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i})\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})h^{n}_{i}|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2},\qquad\

with step size satisfying the lower bound

(5.5b)

h^{n}_{i}\geqslant\frac{2\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big).

Clearly, (
5.3
) follows from (
5.5
).

Now, it may happen that (
5.5a
) holds for our initial choice of a step size
h_{0}
which already tuned to satisfy (
5.5b
), in which case we are done. But in general,
h_{0}
need not secure any descent at all and we appeal to the backtracking iterations.
By the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla F
,

\begin{split}F\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}-h\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})\big)&\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}^{n})-h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2}+\frac{L}{2}h^{2}|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2}\\
&=F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-\Big(1-\frac{L}{2}h\Big)h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2},\end{split}

and hence, if
h
is small enough

h\leqslant\frac{2}{L}\big(1-\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})\big)\ \ \leadsto\ \ F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}-h\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}))\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-\lambda\psi_{q}(\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i})h|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2}.

The backtracking line search iterations tell us that the inequality on the right holds for
h=h^{n}_{i}
, that is (
5.5a
) holds; but it does not hold for
\displaystyle\frac{h^{n}_{i}}{\gamma}
(the stopping criterion fails with
h^{n}_{i}/\gamma
). In particular, therefore,
\displaystyle\frac{h^{n}_{i}}{\gamma}
must satisfy the reverse inequality on the left,
that is, (
5.5b
) holds.
\square

5.1.
Convergence to a band of local minima

Our next proposition provides a rather precise quantitative description for the convergence of the SBGD method. The convergence is determined by the time series of SBGD minimizers,
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}
,

\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}},\qquad i^{-}_{n}:=\argmin_{i}F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n}),

and the time series of
parents
of the heaviest agents,
\{\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}\}
,

\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{+}_{n+1}},\qquad i^{+}_{n+1}:=\argmax_{i}m^{n+1}_{i}.

Note that
\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}
is the “parent” of
\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i^{+}_{n+1}}
which is, by definition, the heaviest agent at
t=t^{n+1}
.
The interplay between minimizers and communication of masses leads to a gradual shift of mass, from higher ground to the minimizers. Eventually, when the SBGD minimizers gain enough mass to assume the role of heaviest agents, the two sequences coincide.
Convergence is independent of the lighter agents.

We introduce the scaling
M=\max_{j}F(\mathbf{x}_{j}^{0})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})
; since
F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})
are decreasing, we conclude that the SBGD iterations remain within that range, namely

(5.6)

\forall n,i:\quad\max_{j}F(\mathbf{x}^{n}_{j})-F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})\leqslant M,\qquad M:=\max F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})

To simplify matters we restrict our attention to the vanilla version of SBGD,
(p,q)=(1,1)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Fix
\lambda\in(0,1)
and consider the SBGD iterations (
2.1
) with step size
h^{n}_{i}=h\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n},\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}\big)
determined by backtracking line search of algorithm
1
in (
3.3
).

Let
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
and
\{\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
denote the time sequence of SBGD minimizers and, respectively, (parent of) heaviest agents, at
t^{n}
.
Then, there exists a constant,
C=C(\gamma,L,M,\lambda)
given in (
5.15
) below, such that

(5.7)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}\times\min\big\{1,|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})|^{2}\big\}<CM}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
,
the descent property (
5.3
) of different agents is dictated by their relative mass

(5.8)

F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i})\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})-\frac{2\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}\big)\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{n})|^{2},\qquad\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i}=\frac{m^{n+1}_{i}}{m^{n+1}_{i^{+}_{n+1}}}.

In particular, application with
i=i^{+}_{n+1}
implies the descent of the heaviest agent positioned at
\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{+}_{n+1}}

(5.9)

F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i^{+}_{n+1}})\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})-\frac{2\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\big)\lambda|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}.

We also need to quantify the descent of the minimizer
positioned at
\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}}
; this requires a lower bound on its relative mass
\displaystyle m^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}}
on the right of (
5.8
).
To this end, we consider two sub-cases, depending on the size of
\displaystyle F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})-F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})>0

Case (i). Assume

(5.10)

Appealing to the descent property (
5.9
) for the heaviest agent

i=i^{+}_{n+1}
, then

(5.11)

\begin{split}F(\mathbf{X}^{n+1}_{-})&\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i^{+}_{n+1}})\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})-\frac{2\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\big)\lambda|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}\\
&\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})-\frac{\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\big)\lambda|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}.\end{split}

The inequality on the left follows since
\mathbf{X}^{n+1}_{-}
is the global minimizer at
t^{n+1}
; the middle inequality is the descent property for the heaviest agent, (
5.9
),
and the last inequality follows from the assumed bound (
5.10

Case (ii). We remain with the case

(5.12)

F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})-F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})\geqslant\frac{\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\big)\lambda|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}.

In this case, the (parent of the) heaviest agent positioned at
\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{+}_{n+1}}
, must different from the minimizer positioned at
\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}}
, or else
F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})=F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})
which is already covered by case (i), (
5.10
). Therefore, the heaviest agent had to shed a fraction of its mass,

\eta_{+}^{n}m^{n}_{i^{+}_{n+1}}
, which will be transferred to that minimzier:

\left\{\begin{split}m^{n+1}_{+}&=m^{n}_{+}-\eta_{+}^{n}m^{n}_{+},\qquad\qquad m^{n}_{+}:=m^{n}_{i^{+}_{n+1}},\ \ m^{n+1}_{+}:=m^{n+1}_{i^{+}_{n+1}},\ \ \eta_{+}^{n}=\frac{F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})-F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})}{F^{{}^{n}}_{\text{max}}-F^{{}^{n}}_{\text{min}}},\\

It follows that the
relative
mass of that minimizer is at least as large as

m^{n+1}_{-}>\eta{}^{n}_{+}m{}^{n}_{+}=\frac{\eta{}^{n}_{+}}{1-\eta{}^{n}_{+}}m^{n+1}_{+}\ \ \leadsto\ \ \widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}}=\frac{m^{n+1}_{-}}{m^{n+1}_{+}}>\frac{\eta{}^{n}_{+}}{1-\eta{}^{n}_{+}}.

Recall that the transition factors,
\eta^{n}_{i}
, are determined by the relative heights, (
3.1
). Using the assumed bound of case (ii), (
5.12
), we conclude

\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}}>\eta_{+}^{n}=\frac{F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})-F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})}{F^{{}^{n}}_{\text{max}}-F^{{}^{n}}_{\text{min}}}\geqslant\frac{\gamma}{ML}\big(1-\lambda\big)\lambda|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}.

As before, the descent property (
5.8
) for
\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}=\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}}
together with the lower bound we secured for
\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}}
in this case, imply

(5.13)

\begin{split}F(\mathbf{X}^{n+1}_{-})\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}})&\leqslant F(\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}})-\frac{2\gamma}{L}\big(1-\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}}\big)\lambda\widetilde{m}^{n+1}_{i^{-}_{n}}|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{n}_{i^{-}_{n}})|^{2}\\
&\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{ML^{2}}(1-\lambda)^{2}\lambda^{2}|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}\times|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})|^{2}.\end{split}

Combining (
5.11
) and (
5.13
), we find

(5.14)

\begin{split}F(\mathbf{X}^{n+1}_{-})&\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})-\frac{1}{C}\min\big\{|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2},|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}\times|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})|^{2}\big\}\\
&\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})-\frac{1}{C}|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+})|^{2}\times\min\big\{1,|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})|^{2}\big\},\end{split}

with

(5.15)

C=\max\Big\{\frac{L}{\gamma(1-\lambda)\lambda},\frac{ML^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}(1-\lambda)^{2}\lambda^{2}}\Big\},

and a telescoping sum of (
5.15
) implies the desired bound
on the right of
(
5.7
)
with
F(\mathbf{X}_{-}^{0})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\leqslant M
.
\square

Remark 5.3
.

We observe that the summability bound (
5.7
) is driven by a worst case scenario alluded in case (ii) above. In this case, there is a potentially large difference of heights between the minimizing agent and heaviest agent; consequently, the descent property of the relatively lighter minimizer could be small and we had to rely on the descent property of the heaviest agent in (
5.11
), which led to (
5.13
).

The descent rates of different agents can be arbitrarily slow, due to their time-dependent mass. The summability bound (
5.7
) depends solely on the time sequence of SBGD minimzers,
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}
, and (the parents of the) heaviest agents,
\{\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}\}
, but it is independent of the lightweight agents.
Eventually, for large enough
n
, the minimizers and heaviest agents of SBGD
pq
coincide into one time sequence,
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}\}
.
The key point is that time sub-sequences,
\{\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}\}
, satisfy a Palais-Smale condition
[
24
, §II.2]
: by monotonicity,
F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}})\leqslant\max_{i}F(\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i})
while
\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Consider the loss function
F\in C^{2}(\Omega)
such that the Lip bound (
5.1
) holds and let
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
denote the time sequence of SBGD minimizers, (
2.1
),(
3.3
). Then
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
consists of one or more sub-sequences,
\{\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-},\ \alpha=1,2,\ldots,\}
, that converge to a band of local minima with equal heights,

\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n_{\alpha}\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha}\ \ \textnormal{such that}\ \nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})=0,\ \textnormal{and}\ F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})=F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\beta})

In particular, if
F
admits only distinct local minima in
\Omega
(i.e., different local minima have different heights), then the whole sequence
\mathbf{X}^{n}
converges to a minimum.

Proof.
Since we assume the sequence
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}
is bounded in
\Omega
, it has a converging sub-sequences. Take
any
such converging sub-sequence
\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-}\rightarrow\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha}\in\Omega
. By (
5.7
),
\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})\rightarrow 0
for all sub-sequences, and hence
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha}
are local minimizers,
\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})=0
. Moreover, since
F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})
is a decreasing,
all
F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})
must have the same ‘height’. The collection of equi-height minimizers
\{\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha}\ \big|\ F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})=F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\beta})\}
is the limit-set of
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}
.
\square

Remark 5.5
.

We reiterate our earlier comment that the convergence analysis does not take into account the finitely many agents in the ‘survival of the fittest’ policy. Putting efficiency aside, theorem
5.4
applies to the general case where all agents from initial crowd survive the SBGD iterations.

5.2.
Flatness and convergence rate

Theorem
5.4
indicates the convergence of SBGD without imposing any convexity condition on the loss function
F
, and therefore it comes without any rate. To quantify convergence
rate
, we need access to the fact that
F
should be ‘curved up’, at least within a sufficiently small neighborhood of a local minimum
\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha}
. In the simplest case,
F
may be assumed to be locally convex. However, one must take into account that
F
may be more flat than just quadratic convexity. Indeed, these relatively flat local minima are the main hurdle in non-convex optimization. A precise classification for the level of ‘flatness’ is offered by the Lojasiewicz condition.
Accortoing
to
Lojasiewicz inequality,
[
18
,
19
]
, if

F
is analytic in
\Omega
then for every critical point of F,
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\Omega
, there exists a neighborhood
{\mathcal{N}}_{*}\ni\mathbf{x}^{*}
surrounding
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, an exponent
\beta\in(1,2]
and a constant
\mu>0
such that

(5.16)

\mu|F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})|\leqslant|\nabla F(\mathbf{x})|^{\beta},\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{*}.

The exponent
\beta
is tied to the
flatness
of
\nabla F
at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
: if
\nabla F(\mathbf{x})
vanishes of order
m
at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}
, then
\displaystyle\beta=\frac{m+1}{m}
. In the particular case of local convexity,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is a simple minimum and (
5.16
) is reduced to the
Polyak
-Lojasiewicz condition
[
22
]
corresponding to
\beta=2

(5.17)

\mu\big(F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big)\leqslant|\nabla F(\mathbf{x})|^{2},\qquad\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}).

A smaller value of
\beta<2
indicates a more flat configuration of
F
in a region
{\mathcal{N}}_{*}\ni\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

In the theorem below, we restrict attention to SBGD
1,1
and we assume that
n
is large enough which allows us to treat only the canonical scenario where minimizers and heaviest agents coincide,
\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}=\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Consider the loss function
F\in C^{2}({\mathcal{C}})
such that the Lip bound (
5.1
) holds, with minimal flatness
\beta
. Let
\{\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
denote the time sequence of SBGD minimizers, (
2.1
),(
3.3
). Then, there exists a constant,
C=C(\gamma,\lambda,\mu)
, such that

(5.18)

F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\leqslant\Big(1-\frac{2\mu\gamma\lambda(1-\lambda)}{L}\Big)^{n}\big(\min_{i}F(\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i})-F(\mathbf{x}^{*})\big),&\beta=2\\
\\
\displaystyle\lesssim C\Big(\frac{1}{n_{\alpha}}\Big)^{\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}},&\beta\in(1,2)\end{array}\right.

Observe that as ‘flatness’, increases,
\beta
is decreasing and the exponential decay in (
5.18
)
1
is replaced by a polynomial decay which may slow down all the way to first-order decay,
\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{\alpha}}}
.

Proof.
We limit ourselves to the canonical scenario in which the heaviest agent at
\mathbf{X}{}^{n}_{+}
coincides with the minimizer at
\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-}
. The descent property for the heaviest agent (
5.11
) then reads

F(\mathbf{X}^{n+1}_{-})\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})-\lambda h_{-}|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n}_{-})|^{2},\qquad h_{-}:=\frac{2\gamma}{L}(1-\lambda).

We focus on the converging sub-sequence
\{\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-}\}
,

F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}+1}_{-})\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-\lambda h_{-}|\nabla F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})|^{2}.

Let us first discuss the quadratic case,
\beta=2
, of Polyak-Lojasiewicz condition (
5.17
), which yields

F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}+1}_{-})\leqslant F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-\mu\lambda h_{-}\big(F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\big),\qquad\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha}.

Rearranging we find

(5.19)

F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}+1}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\leqslant\left(1-\mu\lambda h_{-}\right)\big(F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\big),

which yields exponential rate,
[
22
,
14
]

F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\leqslant\left(1-\mu\lambda h_{-}\right)^{n}\big(F(\mathbf{x}^{0})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\big).

The case of general Lojasiewicz bound (
5.16
) with
\beta<2
yields
that the error,
E_{n_{\alpha}}:=F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})
, satisfies

E_{n_{\alpha}+1}\leqslant E_{n_{\alpha}}-\lambda h_{-}(\mu E_{n_{\alpha}})^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\beta}}},\qquad\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-}\in{\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha}.

The solution of this Riccati inequality (e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem 3.1]
for the limiting case
\beta=1
), yields

F(\mathbf{X}^{n_{\alpha}}_{-})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})\lesssim_{\mu}\left\{|\min_{i}F(\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i})-F(\mathbf{X}^{*}_{\alpha})|^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta^{\prime}}}}+\lambda h_{-}\mu^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{\beta}}}n_{\alpha}\right\}^{-\beta^{\prime}},\quad\beta^{\prime}=\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}>1.

and (
5.18
) follows with
\displaystyle C=\Big(\frac{L}{2\gamma\lambda(1-\lambda)}\Big)^{\frac{\beta}{2-\beta}}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\mu}})^{\frac{2}{2-\beta}}
.
\square

7.1.
Optimization of parameters

Let us first comment on the general issue of optimization of parameter space.
As noted in
[
29
]
, once the parameterization of an adaptive GD method is fixed, it may not yield as good as or better results as simpler GD methods, yet adaptivity should be ‘judged’ after being optimized in space parameter,
[
8
]
. In this context, one can argue that optimizing a single agent method in parameter space is equivalent to a selective choice among many simulations of non-communicating multi-agent dynamics, whereas the swarm-based approach provides a
dynamic
, ‘on the fly’ selection of optimized parameters, which is precisely the type of comparisons we make below.

On the other hand, our SBGD method depends on several parameters: the initial step
h_{0}
, the descent parameter
\lambda
and shrinkage parameter
\gamma
tied to the backtracking, the tolerance parameters (
3.5
) and the
(p,q)
parameters. In the multi-dimensional computations reported below, we do
not
optimize these SBGD parameters.
Thus, unless otherwise stated, we examine the performance of the SBGD method in Algorithm
2

with initial step size
h_{0}=1
, a descent parameter
\lambda=0.2
, a shrinkage parameter
\gamma=0.9
and the threshold parameters (
3.5
). We begin here with the ‘vanilla’ version of SBGD,
(p,q)=(1,1)
, although later we shall find out that the choice
(p,q)=(2,1)
seems universally better.
We run
m
number of independent simulations, initiated with uniformly distributed positions,
\{\mathbf{x}^{0}_{i}\}
and measure the success of the SBGD according to the proportion of its successful end results.

We illustrate the performance of the SBGD algorithm in multiple dimensions on three benchmark test cases,
[
13
]
. First, the Ackley function

(7.1)

F_{\textnormal{Ackley}}(\mathbf{x})=-20\exp\Big\{-\frac{0.2}{\sqrt{d}}\Big\{\sum^{d}_{i=1}(\mathbf{x}_{B})_{i}^{2}\Big\}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Big\}-\exp\Big\{\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{i=1}\cos(2\pi(\mathbf{x}_{B})_{i})\Big\}+20+e+C.

Second, the Rastrigin function

(7.2)

F_{\textnormal{Rstgin}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{d}\sum^{d}_{i=1}\Big\{(\mathbf{x}_{B})_{i}^{2}-10\cos(2\pi(\mathbf{x}_{B})_{i})+10\Big\}+C.

Here
d
is the dimension of the ambient space,
\mathbf{x}_{B}:=(x_{1}-B,\ldots,x_{d}-B)
is the shifted variable in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
B,C\in\mathbb{R}
are the shift parameters. Both functions attain the global minimum
C
at the unique global minimizer
\mathbf{x}^{*}=B
.

Third, we consider the drop-wave function

(7.3)

F_{\textnormal{Drop}}(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1+\cos(12|\mathbf{x}|)}{0.5|\mathbf{x}|^{2}+2},

with SBGD parameters
\lambda=0.3,\ \gamma=0.9,\ h_{0}=1
.
The agents are initialized with uniform distribution of positions in the hypercube
[-3,3]^{d}
.

To evaluate the quality of the solution, we make use of the
success rate
among
m
independent simulations. We consider a simulation to be successful if
\mathbf{x}_{SOL}
is within the

-dimensional cube
[\mathbf{x}^{*}-0.25,\mathbf{x}^{*}+0.25]^{d}

[
5
, §4.2]
. This condition ensures that the approximate solution lies in the basin of attraction of the global minimizer. In fact, in a successful experiment the solution will lie in a much smaller neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.
