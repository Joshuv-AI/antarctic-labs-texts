Existence and Uniqueness of Exact WKB Solutions for Second-Order Singularly Perturbed Linear ODEs
Nikita Nikolaev

Abstract
We prove an existence and uniqueness theorem for exact WKB solutions of general singularly perturbed linear second-order ODEs in the complex domain. These include the one-dimensional time-independent complex Schrödinger equation. Notably, our results are valid both in the case of generic WKB trajectories as well as closed WKB trajectories. We also explain in what sense exact and formal WKB solutions form a basis. As a corollary of the proof, we establish the Borel summability of formal WKB solutions for a large class of problems, and derive an explicit formula for the Borel transform.



Keywords:

exact WKB analysis, exact WKB method, linear ODEs, Schrödinger equation, singular perturbation theory, exact perturbation theory, Borel resummation, Borel-Laplace theory, asymptotic analysis, exponential asymptotics, Gevrey asymptotics, resurgence

§ 1.
Introduction

Consider a singularly perturbed
2^{\text{nd}}
-order linear ordinary differential equation

\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi+p\hbar\partial_{x}\psi+q\psi=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(1)

where
x
is a complex variable,
\hbar
is a small complex perturbation parameter, and the coefficients
p,q
are holomorphic functions of
(x,\hbar)
in some domain in
\mathbb{C}^{2}_{x\hbar}
.
The question we study is a quintessential problem in singular perturbation theory.
Namely, in this paper we search for solutions of (
1
) that are holomorphic in both variables
x
and
\hbar
and admit well-defined asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
in a specified sector.

1.1. Results.

The main result of this paper (
Theorem 5.2
) establishes precise general conditions for the existence and uniqueness of
exact WKB solutions
.
These are holomorphic solutions that are canonically specified (in a precise sense via Borel resummation) by their exponential asymptotic expansions as
\hbar\to 0
in a halfplane.
They are constructed by means of the Borel-Laplace method for the associated singularly perturbed Riccati equation which we investigated in
[
Nik20
]
.

Our approach yields a general result (
Theorem 5.7
) about the Borel summability of
formal WKB solutions
.
These are exponential formal
\hbar
-power series solutions that assume the role of asymptotic expansions as
\hbar\to 0
of exact WKB solutions within appropriate domains in
\mathbb{C}_{x}
.
We also prove an existence and uniqueness result (
§​​ 3.2
) for formal WKB solutions that in particular clarifies precisely in what sense they form a basis of formal solutions.

The construction of exact WKB solutions involves the geometry of certain real curves in
\mathbb{C}_{x}
(the
WKB trajectories
) traced out using a type of Liouville transformation.
Two special classes of this geometry (
closed
and
generic WKB trajectories
) are especially important because they appear in wide a variety of applications.
Notably, our existence and uniqueness and the Borel summability results remain valid in both of these situations.
Thus, we construct an exact WKB basis both along a closed WKB trajectory (
§​​ 5.4
) as well as a generic WKB trajectory (
§​​ 5.4
).

The explicit nature of our approach yields refined information about the Borel transform of WKB solutions.
This includes an explicit recursive formula (
Part ​​ 5.10
) which we hope will facilitate the analysis of the singularity structure in the Borel plane and perhaps lead to a fuller understanding of the resurgent properties of WKB solutions in a large class of problems (see
Part ​​ 5.10
).

§ 2.2.
Examples

2.3 Example

(Classical differential equations)
.

The simplest interesting situation is when the coefficients
p,q
are polynomial functions of
x
only.
In this case,
X=\mathbb{C}_{x}
and
S
is ordinarily taken to be the right halfplane
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\operatorname{Re}(\hbar)>0}}\right\}
where one demands asymptotic control on solutions as
\hbar\to 0
.
The most famous example of this situation is the
\hbar
-Airy equation:

\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-x\psi=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(5)

Another example is the
\hbar
-Weber equation (sometimes also known as the quantum harmonic oscillator):

\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-\big(x^{2}-a\big)\psi=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(6)

where
a
is any complex number.

More general examples are provided by famous classical differential equations (with
\partial_{x}
replaced by
\hbar\partial_{x}
) for which the coefficients
p,q
are rational functions of
x
only.
This includes the Gauss hypergeometric equation, as well as Bessel, Heun, Hermite, and many others.
In these cases,
X
is the complement of finitely many points in
\mathbb{C}_{x}
.

2.3 Example

(The Mathieu equation)
.

All equations in the previous example extend to second-order equations on the Riemann sphere with a pole at infinity (beware, however, that this extension is not unique).
A famous example where this is not the case is the Mathieu equation, sometimes written as

\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-2\big(\cos(x)-\mathnormal{E}\,\big)\psi=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(7)

where
\mathnormal{E}
is a complex number.
This equation has an essential singularity at infinity, yet the methods in this paper are still directly applicable to this equation.

2.3 Example

(Mildly deformed coefficients)
.

More generally, the coefficients
p,q
can be polynomials in
\hbar
with coefficients which are rational or more general meromorphic functions of
x
.
Such examples appear, in Hermitian matrix models of the Gaussian potential
[
BE17
, §6.7, equation (6.86)]
and more generally in the study of quantum curves, where the following deformation of the
\hbar
-Weber equation (
6
) is encountered:

\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-\big(x^{2}-4+2\hbar\big)\psi=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

2.3 Example

(A nontrivially deformed
\hbar
-Airy equation)
.

Our methods are applicable to classical differential equations with much more sophisticated
\hbar
-dependence.
For example, let
X=\mathbb{C}^{\ast}_{x}
,
S=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\operatorname{Re}(\hbar)>0}}\right\}
, and consider the following nontrivial deformation of the
\hbar
-Airy equation (
5
):

\hbar^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}\psi-\big(x+\mathnormal{E}(x,\hbar)\big)\psi=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(8)

where

\mathnormal{E}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-\xi/\hbar}}{x+\xi}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(9)

The function
\mathnormal{E}(x,\hbar)
is not holomorphic at
\hbar=0
, but one can verify that it admits locally uniform Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along the closed arc
\overline{A}=[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},+\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
.

2.3 Example

(
The Schrödinger equation
)
.

The most famous special class of equations (
2
) is the complex one-dimensional time-independent Schrödinger equation

\Big(\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}-\mathnormal{Q}(x,\hbar)\Big)\varphi(x,\hbar)=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(10)

In fact, any second-order equation (
2
) can be put into the Schrödinger form (
10
) by means of the following transformation of the unknown variable:

\varphi(x,\hbar)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2\hbar}\int\nolimits^{x}_{x_{0}}p(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\psi(x,\hbar)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(11)

where

is a suitably chosen basepoint.
In terms of the coefficients of (
2
), the resulting potential is
\mathnormal{Q}=\tfrac{1}{4}p^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\partial_{x}p-q
.

However, in this paper we prefer not to use the transformation (

2

We have two main reasons for this preference.
Firstly, the transformation (

Secondly, and perhaps most importantly from the geometric point of view, the Schrödinger form (
10
) is not a coordinate-independent expression unless this differential equation is posed not on functions but on

These details will be explained in

]
.

§ 2.3.
Formal and Exact Solutions

Our goal is to construct holomorphic solutions of (
2
) with prescribed asymptotic behaviour as
\hbar\to 0
.
Because of the way our linear equation is perturbed (i.e., differentiation
\partial_{x}
is multiplied by a single power of
\hbar
), it turns out that the correct notion of asymptotics is
exponential asymptotics
.
This notion is briefly recalled in
Appendix B
.
Indeed, one can easily verify that nonzero holomorphic solutions generically cannot admit a usual power series asymptotic expansion at
\hbar=0
.
The following definition gives the precise class of solutions we seek in this paper.

2.3 Definition

( )
.

A
weakly-exact solution
of (
2
) on an open subset
U\subset X
is a holomorphic solution
\psi
defined on
U\times S^{\prime}\subset X\times S
for some sectorial subdomain
S^{\prime}\subset S
with nonempty opening
A^{\prime}\subset A
, which admits locally uniform exponential asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along
A^{\prime}
.
If
A^{\prime}=A
, then we call
\psi
a
strongly-exact solution
on
(U;A)
, or simply
exact solution
.

2.4.

Existence of weakly-exact solutions is a classical fact in the theory of differential equations (see, e.g.,
[
Was76
, Theorem 26.2]
).
However, weakly-exact solutions constructed by usual methods are inherently non-unique and in general there is no control on the size of the opening
A^{\prime}
(see, e.g., the remark in
[
Was76
, p.144]
, immediately following Theorem 26.1).
Our attention in this paper is instead focused on the strongly-exact solutions, which from this point of view form a more restricted class of solutions.

A priori
, these may not exist even if weakly-exact solutions are abundant.
The problem of finding strongly-exact solutions is a nontrivial sharpening of the more classical problem of finding weakly-exact solutions.

In this paper, we will in fact construct a special kind of exact solutions, called
exact WKB solutions
.
Their distinguishing property is that they are canonically associated (in a precise sense) with their exponential asymptotics.

2.5. The space of exact solutions.

We denote by
\mathbb{S}(U\times S)
the space of all holomorphic solutions of (
2
) defined on the domain
U\times S
(without any asymptotic restrictions).
Our differential equation is linear of order two, so
\mathbb{S}(U\times S)
is a rank-two module over the ring
{\mathcal{O}}(S)
of holomorphic functions on
S
.
More generally, we can consider the space
\mathbb{S}(U;A)
of
semisectorial germs
(see
Part ​​ A.6
) of holomorphic solutions defined on the pair
(U;A)
.
Again,
\mathbb{S}(U;A)
is a rank-two module for the ring
{\mathcal{O}}(A)
of
sectorial germs
(without asymptotic restrictions).

Let
\mathbb{ES}(U;A)\subset\mathbb{S}(U;A)
be the subset of
exact
solutions on
(U;A)
in the sense of
§​​ 2.3
.
This is a module over the ring
{\mathcal{A}}^{\exp}(A)
of holomorphic sectorial germs that admit exponential asymptotics along
A
.
We also let
\mathbb{GES}(U;A)\subset\mathbb{ES}(U;A)
be the subset of exact solutions on
(U;A)
which admit locally uniform exponential Gevrey asymptotics along
A
.
Similarly, it is a module over the ring
{\mathcal{G}}^{\exp}(A)
of holomorphic sectorial germs that admit exponential asymptotics along

.
These are the spaces in which we search for (and find!) solutions of (
2
).

( )
.

An

2.7. The space of formal solutions.

We denote the subset of

Evidently, the asymptotic expansion defines a map

Appendix B

In this section, we analyse the differential equation (
2
) in a purely formal setting where we ignore all analytic questions with respect to
\hbar
.
As a notational mnemonic used throughout the paper, objects decorated with a hat are formal.

3.1. Formal setup.

Thus, we consider a general formal second-order differential equation of the following form:

\hbar^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}\widehat{\psi}+\widehat{p}\>\hbar\partial_{x}\widehat{\psi}+\widehat{q}\;\widehat{\psi}=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(15)

where the coefficients
\widehat{p},\widehat{q}\in{\mathcal{O}}(X)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
are formal power series in
\hbar
with holomorphic coefficients on
X
:

\widehat{p}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(x)\hbar^{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{q}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q_{k}(x)\hbar^{k}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(16)

We search for formal solutions in the sense of
Part ​​ 2.6
.

§ 3.1.
The Semiclassical Limit

3.2.

A standard technique in solving linear ODEs is to introduce the characteristic equation.
We consider only the
leading-order characteristic equation
, which in for the second-order equation at hand is the following quadratic equation with holomorphic coefficients
p_{0},q_{0}\in{\mathcal{O}}(X)
for a holomorphic function
\lambda=\lambda(x)
:

\lambda^{2}-p_{0}\lambda+q_{0}=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(17)

We refer to its discriminant as the
leading-order characteristic discriminant
:

\mathnormal{D}_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=p_{0}^{2}-4q_{0}~\in{\mathcal{O}}(X)

(18)

We always assume that
\mathnormal{D}_{0}
is not identically zero.
The zeros of
\mathnormal{D}_{0}
are called
turning points
, and all other points in
X
are called
regular points
.
If
x_{0}\in X
is a regular point, then (
17
) has two distinct holomorphic solutions.
We call them the
leading-order characteristic roots
.
Upon fixing a local square-root branch
\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}
near
x_{0}
, we will always label them as follows:

\lambda_{\pm}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{p_{0}\pm\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}{2}\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(19)

3.2 Example

(The Schrödinger equation)
.

For the Schrödinger equation (
10
), the leading-order characteristic discriminant is
\mathnormal{D}_{0}=4\mathnormal{Q}_{0}
, and the leading-order characteristic roots are
\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}}
.

3.2 Remark

( )
.

In analysis, equation (
17
) is often called the
semiclassical limit
of the second-order differential operator in (
2
).
It can be obtained as the vanishing constraint of the following limit:

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\hbar\to 0\\
\hbar\in S\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e^{+\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits^{x}\lambda(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}\Big(\hbar^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}+p(x,\hbar)\hbar\partial_{x}+q(x,\hbar)\Big)e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits^{x}\lambda(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}}\right]=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

§ 3.2.
Existence and Uniqueness

Existence of formal solutions of (
15
) away from turning points is well-known.
For Schrödinger equations (i.e., with
\widehat{p}=0
), they are often called
(formal) WKB solutions
.
However, uniqueness statements are not usually made completely explicit.
It is also sometimes said that ‘formal WKB solutions are linearly independent and form a basis’, but the space they generate is again not usually made explicit.
The purpose of the following proposition is to make these statements precise and explicit.

3.2 Proposition

(
Existence and Uniqueness of Formal WKB Solutions
)
.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be a regular point.
If
U\subset X
is any simply connected neighbourhood of
x_{0}
free of turning points, then (
15
) has precisely two nonzero exponential power series solutions
\widehat{\psi}_{+},\widehat{\psi}_{-}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\exp}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
on
U
normalised at
x_{0}
by
\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x_{0},\hbar)=1
.
They form a basis for the space
\widehat{\mathbb{S}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
of all formal solutions.
Moreover, once a square-root branch
\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}
near
x_{0}
has been chosen, they can be labelled and expressed as follows:

\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{s}_{\pm}(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(20)

where
\widehat{s}_{+},\widehat{s}_{-}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
are the two unique formal solutions with leading-order terms
\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}
of the formal singularly perturbed Riccati equation

\hbar\partial_{x}\widehat{s}=\widehat{s}^{\,2}-\widehat{p}\,\widehat{s}+\widehat{q}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(21)

In fact,
\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}
are the unique formal solutions on
U
satisfying the following initial conditions:

\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x_{0},\hbar)=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\hbar\partial_{x}\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\big\rvert_{(x,\hbar)=(x_{0},0)}$}}=\lambda_{\pm}(x_{0})\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(22)

The proof of this theorem is essentially a computation, presented in
§​​ C.1
.

3.2 Definition

( )
.

The two formal solutions
\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}
from
§​​ 3.2
are called the
formal WKB solutions
normalised at the regular point
x_{0}\in U
.
The basis
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\smash{\widehat{\psi}_{+},\widehat{\psi}_{-}}}}\right\}
is called the
formal WKB basis
normalised at
x_{0}
.
We will also refer to the two formal solutions
\widehat{s}_{\pm}
as the
formal characteristic roots
.

3.3.

Thus,
§​​ 3.2
explains that the formal WKB solutions are uniquely specified either by the normalisation condition and the requirement that they be exponential power series (i.e., having only one exponential prefactor) or equivalently by the two initial conditions (
22
).
Note that the second initial condition is nothing but a way to select the correct exponential prefactor.

3.4. WKB recursion.

A practical advantage of formal WKB solutions is that the formal characteristic roots can be computed very explicitly by solving a recursive tower of linear algebraic equations.
The following lemma is a direct consequence of the proof of
§​​ 3.2
.

3.4 Lemma

( )
.

The coefficients
s^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}_{\pm}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)
of the formal characteristic roots

\widehat{s}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}(x)\hbar^{k}

(23)

are given by the following recursive formula:

s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}=\lambda_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{2}(p_{0}\pm\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}})
, and for
k\geqslant 1
,

s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k-1)$}}-\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}^{k_{1},k_{2}\neq k}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k_{1})$}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k_{2})$}}+\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}^{k_{2}\neq k}p_{k_{1}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k_{2})$}}-q_{k}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(24)

Explicitly, these formulas for low values of
k
are:

\displaystyle s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\Big(\partial_{x}\lambda_{\pm}+p_{1}\lambda_{\pm}-q_{1}\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(25)

\displaystyle s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\Big(\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}-(s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}})^{2}+p_{1}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}+p_{2}\lambda_{\pm}-q_{2}\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(26)

\displaystyle s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\Big(\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}-2s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}+p_{1}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}+p_{2}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}+p_{3}\lambda_{\pm}-q_{3}\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(27)

\displaystyle s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(4)$}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\Big(\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}-2s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}-(s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}})^{2}+p_{1}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}+p_{2}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}+p_{3}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}+p_{4}\lambda_{\pm}-q_{4}\Big){\color[rgb]{0.6,0.6,0.6}.}

(28)

3.4 Example

(unperturbed coefficients)
.

In the simplest but very common situation where the coefficients
p,q
are independent of
\hbar
, identities (
24
) simplify as follows:

s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}-\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}^{k_{1},k_{2}\neq k}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k_{1})$}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(k_{2})$}}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(29)

For low values of
k
, these are:

\begin{gathered}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}=\pm\frac{\partial_{x}\lambda_{\pm}}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}},\quad s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}=\pm\frac{\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}-\big(s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}\big)^{2}}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}},\quad s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}=\pm\frac{\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}-2s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}\\
s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(4)$}}=\frac{\pm 1}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\Big(\partial_{x}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}-2s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}-(s_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}})^{2}\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}\end{gathered}

(30)

3.4 Example

(Schrödinger equation)
.

For the Schrödinger equation (
10
), the leading-order characteristic roots are
\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}}
.
Formula (
24
) for the coefficients reduces to the following:

s^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\partial_{x}s^{\text{\tiny$(k-1)$}}_{\pm}-\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=k}^{k_{1},k_{2}\neq k}s^{\text{\tiny$(k_{1})$}}_{\pm}s^{\text{\tiny$(k_{2})$}}_{\pm}+\mathnormal{Q}_{k}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(31)

If, furthermore, the potential
\mathnormal{Q}
is independent of
\hbar
(i.e.,
\mathnormal{Q}=\mathnormal{Q}_{0}
), then every
\mathnormal{Q}_{k}
in the recursive formula (
31
) is
0
.
In this case, for low values of
k
:

s^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime}}{\mathnormal{Q}},\quad s^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}_{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{8}\frac{\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime\prime}}{\mathnormal{Q}^{3/2}}\mp\frac{5}{32}\frac{(\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime})^{2}}{\mathnormal{Q}^{5/2}},\quad s^{\text{\tiny$(3)$}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{16}\frac{\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\mathnormal{Q}^{2}}-\frac{9}{16}\frac{\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime}\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime\prime}}{\mathnormal{Q}^{3}}+\frac{35}{64}\frac{(\mathnormal{Q}^{\prime})^{3}}{\mathnormal{Q}^{4}}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

where
′
denotes
\partial_{x}
.
So, for instance, for the
\hbar
-Airy equation (
5
),
\mathnormal{Q}=x
, so these formulas reduce to:
\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{x}
,
s^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{4}x^{-1}
,
s^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}_{\pm}=\mp\frac{5}{32}x^{-5/2}
,
s^{\text{\tiny$(2)$}}_{\pm}=\frac{35}{64}x^{-4}
.

3.5. WKB exponents.

To express (
20
) more explicitly as an exponential power series, we separate out the leading-order part of the formal solutions to the Riccati equation.
Let us define

\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s^{\text{\tiny$(k+1)$}}_{\pm}(x)\hbar^{k}\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\widehat{s}_{\pm}=\lambda_{\pm}+\hbar\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(32)

Then the formal WKB solutions
\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Phi_{\pm}(x)/\hbar}\;\widehat{\Psi}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)

(33)

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\pm}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(34)

where
\Phi_{\pm}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)
and
\widehat{\Psi}_{\pm}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
are defined by the following formulas:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm}(x)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\pm}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\pm}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(35)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\Psi}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Psi_{\pm}^{\text{\tiny$(n)$}}(x)\hbar^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(36)

The functions
\Phi_{\pm}
are sometimes called the
WKB exponents
.
The integral of
\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}
in (
34
) is interpreted as termwise integration.
In principle, the basepoint of integration
x_{0}
may be taken on the boundary of
X
as long as these integrals make sense.

§ 3.3.
Remarks on Formal WKB Solutions

3.5 Remark

(
Behaviour at a turning point
)
.

Let
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}\in X
be a turning point of order
m\geqslant 1
, which means it is an
m
-th order zero of the leading-order characteristic discriminant
\mathnormal{D}_{0}
.
By examining the recursive formula (
24
), one can conclude that the coefficients
s^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}_{\pm}
of each formal characteristic root
\widehat{s}_{\pm}
have the following behaviour near
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
.

When
m
is odd, the leading-order characteristic roots
\lambda_{\pm}
have a square-root branch singularity at
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
, but they are bounded as
x\to x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
in sectors.
Every subleading-order coefficient
s^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}_{\pm}
has at worst a square-root branch singularity at
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
but in general it is unbounded as
x\to x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
.
On the other hand, when
m
is even, the leading-order characteristic roots
\lambda_{\pm}
are holomorphic at
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
.
Every subleading-order coefficient
s^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}_{\pm}
is single-valued near
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
but in general it has a pole there.

For either parity of
m
, in generic situation, the
k
-th order coefficient
s^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}_{\pm}
(with
k\geqslant 1
) is bounded below by
x^{-km/2}
, where
x
is a coordinate centred at
x_{{\mathrm{tp}}}
.
Therefore, the singular behaviour of the coefficients of
\widehat{s}_{\pm}
gets progressively worse in higher and higher orders of
\hbar
.
The upshot of this analysis is that in general it is not possible to expect the formal characteristic roots
\widehat{s}_{\pm}
(and therefore the corresponding formal WKB solutions
\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}
) to be the uniform asymptotic expansions of exact solutions near a turning point.
This phenomenon lies at the heart of the breakdown of singular perturbation theory in the vicinity of a turning point.

3.5 Remark

(
Alternative expression for formal WKB solutions
)
.

In the literature, a different but closely related expression and normalisation for the formal WKB solutions is commonly used (see, e.g.,
[
KT05
, equation (2.11)]
or
[
IN14
, equation (2.24)]
).
Consider the following
odd
and
even
parts of the formal characteristic solutions:

\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\widehat{s}_{+}-\widehat{s}_{-}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{s}_{\text{ev}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\widehat{s}_{-}+\widehat{s}_{+}\big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(37)

They satisfy
\widehat{s}_{\pm}=\pm\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}+\widehat{s}_{\text{ev}}
.
It follows from the Riccati equation that the even part
\widehat{s}_{\text{ev}}
can be expressed in terms of the odd part
\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}
as follows:

\widehat{s}_{\text{ev}}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\partial_{x}\log\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{p}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(38)

Substituting these expressions into (
20
) yields

\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}(x,\hbar)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\Big(\pm\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}(t,\hbar)-\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\log\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}(t,\hbar)-\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{p}(t,\hbar)\Big)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(39)

To integrate out the term involving the logarithmic derivative of
\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}
, we must first make sense of choosing a square root of the formal power series
\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}
.
To this end, we write
\widehat{s}_{\pm}=\lambda_{\pm}+\hbar\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}
and put
\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\text{od}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{+}-\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{-}\big)
.
Then using the identity
\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}
, we get

\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}+\hbar\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\text{od}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}\Big(1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\text{od}}\hbar\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(40)

Upon fixing a square-root branch
\mathnormal{D}_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}
of
\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}
, we let

\pm\sqrt{\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}(x,\hbar)}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathnormal{D}_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\sqrt{1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\text{od}}\hbar}=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathnormal{D}_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\text{od}}\hbar}}\right)^{k}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(41)

Notice that this is an invertible formal power series in
\hbar
with holomorphic coefficients.
We therefore obtain the following four exponential power series:

\widehat{\psi}_{\pm,\epsilon}(x,\hbar)=\frac{1}{\epsilon\sqrt{\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}(x,\hbar)}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\Big(\pm\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}(t,\hbar)-\tfrac{1}{2}\widehat{p}(t,\hbar)\Big)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(42)

where
\epsilon\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{+,-}}\right\}
.
These are the expressions that in the literature are often referred to as (formal)
WKB solutions
, although the choice of
\epsilon
is usually made implicitly.
Of course, it is always possible to choose the branch
\mathnormal{D}_{0}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}
such that
\widehat{\psi}_{\pm,\epsilon}=\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}
.

3.5 Remark

(
The first-order WKB approximation
)
.

Expression (
42
) is the more traditional form used to derive the WKB approximation for Schrödinger equations.
Indeed, if
p=0
and
q=-\mathnormal{Q}
, then
\mathnormal{D}_{0}=4\mathnormal{Q}_{0}
and
\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}}
, so the leading-order term of
\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}
is simply
\sqrt{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}}
.
Truncating
\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}
at the leading order yields the famous analytic expressions

\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}}}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pm\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\sqrt{\mathnormal{Q}_{0}(t)}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

3.5 Remark

(
Normalisation at a turning point
)
.

Expression (
42
) is often used to normalise WKB solutions at a turning point.
However, the existence of
exact
WKB solutions with such normalisation depends on some more global properties of the differential equation, which is not guaranteed in general.
In practical terms, even if an
exact
WKB solution normalised at a
regular
point exists, the corresponding exact WKB solution normalised at some nearby turning point may not exist due to the fact that the change of normalisation constant may not exist or does not have appropriate asymptotic behaviour as
\hbar\to 0
.
For this reason, in this paper we focus our attention exclusively on WKB solution normalised at regular points.
A more detailed explanation of this phenomenon will appear in
[
Nik21b
]
.

3.5 Remark

(
Formal characteristic discriminant
)
.

Since the formal characteristic roots
\widehat{s}_{\pm}
are uniquely determined, we can introduce the
formal characteristic discriminant
of the differential equation (
15
) by the usual formula for discriminants:

\widehat{\mathnormal{D}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\big(\widehat{s}_{+}-\widehat{s}_{-}\big)\big(\widehat{s}_{-}-\widehat{s}_{+}\big)=\big(\widehat{s}_{+}-\widehat{s}_{-}\big)^{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(43)

Its leading-order part is the leading-order characteristic discriminant
\mathnormal{D}_{0}
.
If we furthermore define
\sqrt{\widehat{\mathnormal{D}}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widehat{s}_{+}-\widehat{s}_{-}
, so that its leading-order part is just
\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}=\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}
, then we obtain the following relation:

\widehat{s}_{\text{od}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\widehat{\mathnormal{D}}}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(44)

Then (
42
) can be written as follows:

3.6. Convergence of the formal Borel transform.

Our final remark about formal WKB solutions in this section is a lemma that says that if
\widehat{p},\widehat{q}
are in a certain Gevrey regularity class, then the formal WKB solutions are in the corresponding exponential Gevrey regularity class.
Gevrey series are briefly reviewed in
§​​ A.3

Let
x_{0}\in X
be a regular point and
U\subset X
a simply connected neighbourhood of
x_{0}

Let

Consider their formal Borel transform (see

\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}\,\big](x,\xi)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\pm}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\Psi}_{\pm}\,\big](x,\xi)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(46)

It is convenient to also consider the following
exponentiated formal Borel transform
of

\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}^{\exp}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\pm}\,\big](x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\pm}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}\,\big](t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Denote the formal Borel transform of

\widehat{\sigma}_{\pm}(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}\,\big](x,\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{k+2}^{\text{\tiny$(i)$}}(x)\frac{\xi^{k}}{k!}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

3.6 Proposition

( )
.

Suppose the coefficients

Recall the leading-order characteristic discriminant

§ 5.2.
Borel Summability of WKB Solutions

In this subsection, we translate
Theorem 5.2
and its method of proof into the language of Borel-Laplace theory, the basics of which are briefly recalled in
Appendix C
.
Namely, it follows directly from our construction that the exact WKB solutions are the Borel resummation of the corresponding formal WKB solutions.
In what follows, we make this statement precise and explicit.

5.6.

Recall that we write the formal WKB solution
\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Phi_{\alpha}(x)/\hbar}\;\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}(x,\hbar)

(66)

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\alpha}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\alpha}(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(67)

The main result in this subsection is the following theorem.

5.7 Theorem

( )
.

Assume all the hypotheses of
Theorem 5.2
.
The exact WKB solution
\psi_{\alpha}
on
U\times S^{\prime}_{\theta}
is the locally uniform Borel resummation in the direction
\theta
of the formal WKB solution
\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}
on
U
: for all
x\in U
and all sufficiently small
\hbar\in S^{\prime}_{\theta}
,

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big](x,\hbar)

(68)

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\alpha}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}\big[\,\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\alpha}\,\big](t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

To be more precise, for every compactly contained subset
V\subset U
, there is a Borel disc
S^{\prime\prime}_{\theta}\subset S^{\prime}_{\theta}
of possibly smaller diameter
\delta^{\prime\prime}\in(0,\delta^{\prime}]
such that identity (
68
) is valid uniformly for all
(x,\hbar)\in V\times S^{\prime\prime}_{\theta}
.

Thus, exact and formal WKB solutions can be thought of as being canonically specified by their asymptotic expansions, and hence in some sense ‘identified’.
This is the reason that the vast majority of literature in WKB analysis speaks simply of “WKB solutions” without specifying whether the exact or the formal object is in question.
However, it is important to stress that this ‘identification’ is not global and highly depends on the location in
\mathbb{C}_{x}
.

Theorem 5.7
packs a lot of information, which we now unpack by breaking it down into a sequence of four lemmas (

,
5.9
,
5.9
, and
5.9

5.8. The formal Borel transform.

Recall that the formal WKB solution
\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}
is an exponential power series on
U
; in symbols,
\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\exp}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.
Instead of considering directly the Borel transform
\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]
of
\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}
, it is more convenient to consider the following
exponentiated formal Borel transform
of
\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}
, which we define as

\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}^{\exp}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big](x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\alpha}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\alpha}\,\big](t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(69)

Note that
\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]\neq\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}^{\exp}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]
because the Borel transform converts multiplication into convolution.
Denote the formal Borel transform of
\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\alpha}
by

\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\alpha}\,\big](x,\xi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{k+2}^{\text{\tiny$(i)$}}(x)\frac{\xi^{k}}{k!}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(70)

5.8 Lemma

(
Convergence of the formal Borel transform
)
.

The formal Borel transform
\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}
is a locally uniformly convergent power series in
\xi
.
Consequently, the exponentiated formal Borel transform
\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}^{\exp}\big[\,\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]
and hence the ordinary formal Borel transform
\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]
are locally uniformly convergent power series in
\xi
.
In symbols,
\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha},\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}^{\exp}\big[\,\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}\,\big],\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
.

5.9. The analytic Borel transform.

Similarly, it is more convenient to consider the
exponentiated analytic Borel
transform of the exact WKB solution
\psi_{\alpha}
in the direction
\theta
, defined as

\mathfrak{B}^{\exp}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big](x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\alpha}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\big[\,\mathnormal{S}_{\alpha}\,\big](t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Note again that
\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]\neq\mathfrak{B}^{\exp}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]

However, if
\mathfrak{B}^{\exp}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]

then clearly so is

Denote the analytic Borel transform of
\mathnormal{S}_{\alpha}
in the direction
\theta

\sigma_{\alpha}(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,\mathnormal{S}_{\alpha}\,](x,\xi)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\nolimits_{\theta}\mathnormal{S}_{\alpha}(x,\hbar)e^{\xi/\hbar}\frac{\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\hbar}}{\hbar^{2}}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(72)

(
Convergence of the analytic Borel transform).

For any compactly contained subset
V\Subset U
, there exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that the analytic Borel transform
\sigma_{\alpha}
is uniformly convergent for all
(x,\xi)\in V\times\Xi_{\theta}
where
\Xi_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi~\big|~\mathop{{\mathrm{dist}}}\nolimits\big(\xi,e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\big)<\varepsilon}}\right\}

Consequently, the exponentiated analytic Borel transform
\mathfrak{B}^{\exp}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]
and hence the analytic Borel transform
\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]
are uniformly convergent for all
(x,\xi)\in V\times\Xi_{\theta}

In particular,
\mathfrak{B}^{\exp}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]
and
\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]
are convergent for all
\xi\in e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

.

5.9 Lemma

(

The analytic Borel transform
\sigma_{\alpha}
defines the analytic continuation of the formal Borel transform
\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}
along the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}_{\xi}

Consequently, the exponentiated analytic Borel transform
\mathfrak{B}^{\exp}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]
and hence the analytic Borel transform
\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]
define the analytic continuations along the ray

of
\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}^{\exp}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}\big[\,\widehat{\psi}_{\alpha}\,\big]

In particular, there are no singularities in the Borel plane
\mathbb{C}_{\xi}
along the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Let us define the
exponentiated Laplace transform

\mathfrak{L}^{\exp}_{\theta}
the same way by applying the ordinary Laplace transform
\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}
to the exponent.

5.9 Lemma

(
Borel-Laplace identity for WKB solutions).

For any compactly contained subset
V\subset U
, there is a Borel disc
S^{\prime\prime}_{\theta}\subset S_{\theta}
of possibly smaller diameter

\theta

\mathnormal{S}_{\alpha}(x,\hbar)=s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}(x)+\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\sigma_{\alpha}\,](x,\hbar)=s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}(x)+\int\nolimits_{e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{-\xi/\hbar}\sigma_{\alpha}(x,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

is uniformly convergent for all

and satisfies the following identity:

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Phi_{\alpha}(x)/\hbar}\,e^{-s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}(x)}\,\mathfrak{L}^{\exp}_{\theta}\Big[\,\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}^{\exp}\big[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,\big]\,\Big]

\displaystyle=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\lambda_{\alpha}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\mathnormal{S}_{\alpha}(t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

In particular,
\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}\big[\,\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,\psi_{\alpha}\,]\,\big]
is uniformly convergent on

and equals
\psi_{\alpha}

We also define functions

A.1. Sectorial domains.

More precisely, we consider the

See

(b): straight sector with opening

A

In this case, the open arc

is called the

of

, and its length

is called the

of
S
.
A

S_{0}\subset S

in

is contained in

.
This means in particular that the opening

of

is compactly contained in

; i.e.,
\overline{A}_{0}\subset A
.

The simplest example of a sectorial domain

is of course a

of radius
\delta>0
and opening

, which is the set of points
\hbar\in\mathbb{C}_{\hbar}
satisfying

.
The most typical example of a sectorial domain encountered in this paper is a

of

S=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar\in\mathbb{C}_{\hbar}~\big|~\operatorname{Re}(1/\hbar)>1/\delta}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Its opening is

.
Notice that any straight sector with opening

It is also not difficult to see that any sectorial domain with opening

More generally, we will consider Borel discs bisected by some direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}

S_{\theta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar\in\mathbb{C}_{\hbar}~\big|~\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}/\hbar)>1/\delta}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(89)

§ A.1.
Poincaré Asymptotics in One Dimension

First, for the benefit of the reader and to fix some notation, let us briefly recall some basic notions from asymptotic analysis in one complex variable.
We denote by
\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
the ring of formal power series in
\hbar
, and by
\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar}}\right\}
the ring of convergent power series in
\hbar

Fix an arc of directions

We denote by

the ring of holomorphic functions on a sectorial domain

with opening
A

A.2. Sectorial germs.

For the purpose of asymptotic behaviour as
\hbar\to 0

is irrelevant.
So it is better to consider

of holomorphic functions defined on sectorial domains with opening

A.2 Definition

( )

A
sectorial germ
on
A

(f,S)
where

is a sectorial domain with opening

and

is a holomorphic function on
S

Two such pairs
(f,S)
and
(f^{\prime},S^{\prime})
are considered equivalent if the intersection

contains a sectorial domain

are equal.

Sectorial germs on

{\mathcal{O}}(A)

For any sectorial domain

{\mathcal{O}}(S)\to{\mathcal{O}}(A)
that sends a holomorphic function

Terminology:
For the benefit of the reader who is uncomfortable with the language of germs, we stress that every sectorial germ on
A
can be represented by an actual holomorphic function

In fact, we normally denote the equivalence class of any pair

A.3. Poincaré asymptotics.

Recall that a holomorphic function

is said to admit (

\hbar\to 0
along

\widehat{f}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket

\mathnormal{R}_{n}(\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f(\hbar)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f_{k}\hbar^{k}

(90)

is bounded by
\hbar^{n}
for all sufficiently small
\hbar\in S

That is, for every
{n\geqslant 0}
, and every compactly contained subarc
A_{0}\Subset A

S_{0}\subset S
with opening
A_{0}
and a real constant
\mathnormal{C}_{n,0}>0
such that

\big|\mathnormal{R}_{n}(\hbar)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{C}_{n,0}|\hbar|^{n}

(91)

for all
\hbar\in S_{0}

The constants
\mathnormal{C}_{n,0}
may depend on
n
and the opening
A_{0}
.
If this is the case, we write

f(\hbar)\sim\widehat{f}(\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $A$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(92)

Sectorial germs with this property form a subring

, and the asymptotic expansion map defines a ring homomorphism
\text{\ae}:{\mathcal{A}}(A)\to\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket

A.4. Asymptotics along a closed arc.

Furthermore, we will write

f(\hbar)\sim\widehat{f}(\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $\overline{A}$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}
}

(93)

if the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{n,0}

Obviously, if a function

admits asymptotics along

Sectorial germs with this property form a subring

§ A.2.
Uniform Poincaré Asymptotics

Now, suppose we also have a domain

A.5. Power series with holomorphic coefficients.

We denote by

\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(x)\hbar^{k}\quad\in\quad{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

§ A.3.
Gevrey Asymptotics

For the purposes of the main construction in this paper, the notion of Poincaré asymptotics is too weak.
A powerful and systematic way to refine Poincaré asymptotics is known as
Gevrey asymptotics
.
The basic principle behind it is to strengthen the asymptotic requirements by specifying the dependence on
n
of the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{n,0}
in (
91
) and
\mathnormal{C}_{n,x,0}
in (
95
).
In this paper, we use only the simplest Gevrey regularity class (more properly known as
1-Gevrey asymptotics
) which requires the asymptotic bounds to grow essentially like
n!
.
See, for example,
[
LR16
, §1.2]
for a more general introduction to Gevrey asymptotics.
As before, for the benefit of the reader we first recall Gevrey asymptotics in one complex dimension.

A.9. Gevrey asymptotics in one dimension.

A holomorphic function
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(S)
defined on a sectorial domain
S
with opening
A
is said to admit
Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along
A
if the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{n,0}
in (
91
) depend on
n
like
\mathnormal{C}_{0}\mathnormal{M}_{0}^{n}n!
.
More explicitly, there is a formal power series
\widehat{f}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
such that for every compactly contained subarc
A_{0}\Subset A
, there is a sectorial domain
S_{0}\subset S
with opening
A_{0}\Subset A
and real constants
\mathnormal{C}_{0},\mathnormal{M}_{0}>0
which for all
n\geqslant 0
give the bounds

\big|\mathnormal{R}_{n}(\hbar)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{C}_{0}\mathnormal{M}_{0}^{n}n!|\hbar|^{n}

(100)

for all
\hbar\in S_{0}
.
We will use the symbol “
\simeq
” to distinguish Gevrey asymptotics from Poincaré asymptotics.
Thus, if
f
admits Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along
A
, we will write

f(\hbar)\simeq\widehat{f}(\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $A$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(101)

We denote the subring of sectorial germs satisfying this property by
{\mathcal{G}}(A)\subset{\mathcal{A}}(A)
.
If in addition to (
100
), the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{0},\mathnormal{M}_{0}
can be chosen uniformly for all
A_{0}\Subset A
, then we will write

f(\hbar)\simeq\widehat{f}(\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $\overline{A}$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(102)

We denote the subring of sectorial germs satisfying this property by
{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A})\subset{\mathcal{G}}(A)
.
Explicitly,
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(\overline{A})
if and only if there is a sectorial domain
S_{0}
with opening
A
and real constants
\mathnormal{C},\mathnormal{M}>0
which give the following bounds for all
n\geqslant 0
and
\hbar\in S_{0}
:

\big|\mathnormal{R}_{n}(\hbar)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{C}\mathnormal{M}^{n}n!|\hbar|^{n}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(103)

A.10. Gevrey series in one dimension.

It is easy to see that if
f\in{\mathcal{G}}(A)
then the coefficients of its asymptotic expansion also grow essentially like
n!
.
By definition, a formal power series
\widehat{f}(\hbar)=\sum f_{n}\hbar^{n}\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is a
Gevrey power series
if there are constants
\mathnormal{C},\mathnormal{M}>0
such that for all
n\geqslant 0
,

|f_{n}|\leqslant\mathnormal{C}\mathnormal{M}^{n}n!\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(104)

Gevrey series form a subring
\mathbb{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\subset\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
, and the asymptotic expansion map restricts to a ring homomorphism
\text{\ae}:{\mathcal{G}}(A)\to\mathbb{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.
The ring
{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A})
for an arc with opening
|A|=\pi
plays a central role in Gevrey asymptotics because it is possible to identify and describe a subclass
\overline{\mathbb{G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\subset\mathbb{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
such that the asymptotic expansion map restricts to a bijection
\text{\ae}:{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A})\overset{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.35623pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,}{\longrightarrow}\overline{\mathbb{G}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.
This identification is done using a theorem of Nevanlinna and requires techniques from the Borel-Laplace theory, see
Appendix C
.

A.11. Examples in one dimension.

Any function which is holomorphic at
\hbar=0
automatically admits Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along any arc: its asymptotic expansion is nothing but its convergent Taylor series at
\hbar=0
whose coefficients necessarily grow at most exponentially.
Also, if a function admits Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along a strictly larger arc
A^{\prime}
which contains the closed arc
\overline{A}
, then it automatically admits Gevrey asymptotics along
\overline{A}
.

The function
e^{-1/\hbar}
admits Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
in the right halfplane where it is asymptotic to
0
.
So if
A=(-\pi/2,+\pi/2)
, then
e^{-1/\hbar}\in{\mathcal{G}}(A)
, but
e^{-1/\hbar}\not\in{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A})
as discussed before.
On the other hand, the function
e^{-1/\hbar^{\alpha}}
for any real number
0<\alpha<1
does not admit Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along any arc.

A.12. Gevrey series with holomorphic coefficients.

Now, suppose again that in addition we have a domain
U\subset\mathbb{C}_{x}
.
A formal power series
\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
on

is called a
Gevrey series
if, for every

\mathnormal{C}_{x},\mathnormal{M}_{x}>0
such that, for all
n\geqslant 0
,

\big|f_{n}(x)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{C}_{x}\mathnormal{M}_{x}^{n}n!\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(105)

Furthermore,
\widehat{f}
is a
uniformly Gevrey series
on
U
if the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{x},\mathnormal{M}_{x}
can be chosen to be independent of

.

\widehat{f}
is a
locally uniformly Gevrey series
on
U
if every
x_{0}\in U
has a neighbourhood

where
\widehat{f}
is uniformly Gevrey.
Such power series form subrings
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
and
{\mathcal{G}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
of
{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket

The ring
{\mathcal{G}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket

\partial_{x}
.

A.13. Uniform Gevrey asymptotics.

Again, consider the product space

where

be a domain of the form described in

.
A holomorphic function
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbb{U})
is said to admit (pointwise)
Gevrey asymptotics on
U
as
\hbar\to 0
along
A
if for every
x\in U
and every compactly contained subarc
A_{0}\Subset A
, there is a sectorial domain
S_{0}
with opening
A_{0}
and constants
\mathnormal{C}_{x,0},\mathnormal{M}_{x,0}>0
such that for all
n\geqslant 0
and all
\hbar\in S_{0}
,

\big|\mathnormal{R}_{n}(x,\hbar)\big|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|f(x,\hbar)-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f_{k}(x)\hbar^{k}}}\right|\leqslant\mathnormal{C}_{x,0}\mathnormal{M}^{n}_{x,0}n!|\hbar|^{n}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(106)

We say that
f
admits
uniform Gevrey asymptotics
on
U
as
\hbar\to 0
along
A
if the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{x,0},\mathnormal{M}_{x,0}
can be chosen to be independent of
x\in U
(i.e., so that
\mathnormal{C}_{x,0}=\mathnormal{C}_{0},\mathnormal{M}_{x,0}=\mathnormal{M}_{0}
).
We also say
f
admits
locally uniform Gevrey asymptotics
on
U
if every point
x_{0}\in U
has a neighbourhood
U_{0}\subset U
on which
f
has uniform Gevrey asymptotics.
In these cases, we write, respectively,

\displaystyle f(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle\simeq\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $A$, unif. $\forall x\in U$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8};}\hss}}
}

(107)

\displaystyle f(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle\simeq\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $A$, loc.unif. $\forall x\in U$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(108)

Such functions form subrings
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;A)\subset{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;A)
and
{\mathcal{G}}(U;A)\subset{\mathcal{A}}(U;A)
respectively.
The asymptotic expansion map
æ
restricts to ring homomorphisms
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;A)\to{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
and
{\mathcal{G}}(U;A)\to{\mathcal{G}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.
As in the case of uniform Poincaré asymptotics, an application of the Cauchy integral formula shows that the ring
{\mathcal{G}}(U;A)
(but not the ring
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;A)
) is preserved by differentiation: it has the property
\partial_{x}\big({\mathcal{G}}(U;A)\big)\subset{\mathcal{G}}(U;A)
.

A.14. Uniform Gevrey asymptotics along a closed arc.

If in addition to (
107
) or (
108
), the constants
\mathnormal{C}_{x,0},\mathnormal{M}_{x,0}
can be chosen uniformly for all
A_{0}\Subset A
(i.e., so that
\mathnormal{C}_{x,0}=\mathnormal{C}_{x}
and
\mathnormal{M}_{x,0}=\mathnormal{M}_{x}
), we will write, respectively,

\displaystyle f(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle\simeq\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $\overline{A}$, unif. $\forall x\in U$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8};}\hss}}
}

(109)

\displaystyle f(x,\hbar)

\displaystyle\simeq\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $\overline{A}$, loc.unif. $\forall x\in U$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(110)

Such functions form subrings
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;\overline{A})\subset{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;\overline{A})
and
{\mathcal{G}}(U;\overline{A})\subset{\mathcal{G}}(U;A)
respectively.
Again, we have
\partial_{x}\big({\mathcal{G}}(U;\overline{A})\big)\subset{\mathcal{G}}(U;\overline{A})
.

A.15. Example.

The following example is related to what is sometimes called the
Euler series

[
LR16
, Example 1.1.4]
.
Consider the following formal series on
U=\mathbb{C}_{x}^{\ast}
:

\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-x)^{-k}(k-1)!\hbar^{k}=\frac{\hbar}{x}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{k!}{(-x)^{k}}\hbar^{k}~\in~{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(111)

Incidentally,
\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}
is a formal solution of the differential equation
\hbar^{2}\partial_{\hbar}\mathnormal{E}+x\mathnormal{E}=\hbar
, but this fact is not important for the discussion in this example.

It is easy to see that the power series
\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}
has zero radius of convergence for any fixed nonzero
x
, but it is a Gevrey series for which the bounds (
105
) can be satisfied by taking
\mathnormal{C}_{x}=1
and
\mathnormal{M}_{x}=|x|^{-k}
.
This demonstrates that
\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}
is a locally uniform Gevrey series on
\mathbb{C}_{x}^{\ast}
.
One can show that
\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}
is not a uniform Gevrey series.
Thus,
\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}\in{\mathcal{G}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
but
\not\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.

Consider the function

\mathnormal{E}(x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-\xi/\hbar}}{x+\xi}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

It is well-defined and holomorphic for all
x
in the cut plane
U^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbb{C}_{x}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{-}
and all
\hbar
with
{\operatorname{Re}(\hbar)>0}
.
It is not holomorphic at
\hbar=0
for any
x\in U
, but it is bounded as
\hbar\to 0
in the right halfplane.
In fact,
\mathnormal{E}
admits the power series
\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}
as its locally uniform Gevrey asymptotics:

\mathnormal{E}(x,\hbar)\simeq\widehat{\mathnormal{E}}(x,\hbar)\qquad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $[-\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}},+\nicefrac{{\pi}}{{2}}]$, loc.unif. $\forall x\in U^{\prime}$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(112)

In symbols,
\mathnormal{E}\in{\mathcal{G}}(U^{\prime};A)
.
To see this, we can write:

\frac{1}{x+\xi}=\frac{1}{x}\frac{1}{1+\xi/x}=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{1}{(-x)^{k}}\xi^{k}+\frac{1}{(-x)^{n-1}}\frac{\xi^{n-1}}{x+\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Therefore, we obtain the relation

\mathnormal{E}(x,\hbar)=\frac{\hbar}{x}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{k!}{(-x)^{k}}\hbar^{k}+\frac{1}{(-x)^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\xi^{n-1}e^{-\xi/\hbar}}{x+\xi}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

So to demonstrate (
112
), one can find a locally uniform bound on the integral which is valid uniformly for all directions in the halfplane arc
(-\pi/2,+\pi/2)
.

Appendix B
Basics of Exponential Asymptotics

In this appendix section, we define the appropriate notion of parametric asymptotics necessary for the analysis of linear ODEs of the form (
2
).
As ever, for the reader’s convenience, we begin with a description in one complex variable.

§ B.1.
Exponential Asymptotics in One Dimension

B.0 Definition

( )
.

An
exponential power series
in
\hbar
is a formal expression of the form

\widehat{\psi}=e^{\Phi/\hbar}\,\widehat{\Psi}

where
\Phi=\Phi(\hbar^{-1})\in\mathbb{C}[\hbar^{-1}]
and
\widehat{\Psi}=\widehat{\Psi}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.
Moreover,
\widehat{\psi}
is an
exponential Gevrey series
if
\widehat{\Psi}\in\mathbb{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.
The polynomial
\Phi
is called the
exponent
of
\widehat{\psi}
.

Notice that the exponential prefactor
e^{\Phi/\hbar}
is just a holomorphic function of
\hbar\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
that has an essential singularity at
\hbar=0
.
The usual formal power series
\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
are the exponential power series with
\Phi=0
.

B.1. Exponential transseries.

The set of all exponential power series in
\hbar
with a fixed exponent
\Phi
has the structure of a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space, but unless
\Phi
is the zero polynomial it does not inherit the usual ring structure because it is not closed under products.
At the same time, the set of all exponential power series in
\hbar
is closed under multiplication but it loses the structure of a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space because sums of exponential power series with distinct exponents cannot be written as exponential power series.
We therefore want to consider finite linear combinations of exponential power series to yield a well-behaved algebraic structure.
So we introduce the following definition.

B.1 Definition

( )
.

We define the ring of
exponential transseries
in
\hbar
as the set of all formal finite combinations of exponential power series:

\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.~\sum_{k}^{\textup{\tiny{finite}}}e^{\Phi_{k}/\hbar}\,\widehat{\Psi}_{k}~~}}\right|~\Phi_{k}\in\mathbb{C}[\hbar^{-1}],\,\widehat{\Psi}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Similarly, an
exponential Gevrey transseries
in
\hbar
is one such that each formal power series
\widehat{\Psi}_{k}
is a Gevrey series.
The ring of exponential Gevrey transseries will be denoted by
\mathbb{G}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.

B.1 Remark

( )
.

Our ring
\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is a much simpler version of analogous formal objects first systematically considered in asymptotic analysis by Ilyashenko in
[
Ily91
]
(see
Dulac’s exponential series
), and at the same time independently by Écalle in
[
É92
]
(whence the terminology “transseries” is borrowed).
(See also
[
DDP93
]
.)
Since then, transseries have grown into a vast subject which we do not need here.
Quite approachable introductions may be found in
[
Cos05
,
Edg10
,
Dor19
]
.

B.1 Definition

( )
.

A holomorphic function

(113)

We denote by

Appendix C
Basics of the Borel-Laplace Theory

In this appendix section, we recall some basic definitions from the theory of Borel-Laplace transforms.

C.1.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{C}_{x}
be a domain.
Fix a direction
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, let
A_{\theta}
be the halfplane arc bisected by
\theta
, and let
S_{\theta}
be the Borel disc bisected by
\theta
of some diameter
\delta>0
:

A_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\theta-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\theta+\tfrac{\pi}{2})\quad\text{and}\quad S_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\operatorname{Re}\big(\smash{e^{i\theta}}/\hbar\big)>1/\delta}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(119)

Introduce another complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{\xi}
, sometimes called the
Borel plane
.
In the same vein, the complex plane
\mathbb{C}_{\hbar}
is sometimes called the
Laplace plane
.
Let
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}_{\xi}
be the nonnegative real ray in the direction
\theta
.
By a
tubular neighbourhood
of
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
of some
thickness

\varepsilon>0
we mean a domain of the form

\Xi_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{\xi}~\big|~\mathop{{\mathrm{dist}}}\nolimits(\xi,e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+})<\varepsilon}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(120)

§ C.1.
The Laplace Transform

C.2.

Let us first recall some well-known properties of the Laplace transform.
Suppose
\Xi_{\theta}\subset\mathbb{C}_{\xi}
is a tubular neighbourhood of
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and
\varphi=\varphi(x,\xi)
is a holomorphic function on
U\times\Xi_{\theta}
.
Its
Laplace transform
in the direction
\theta
is defined by the formula:

\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\varphi\,](x,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int\nolimits_{e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\varphi(x,\xi)e^{-\xi/\hbar}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(121)

The function
\varphi
is called
uniformly
or
locally uniformly Laplace transformable
in the direction
\theta
if this integral is respectively uniformly or locally uniformly convergent.
Clearly,
\varphi
is uniformly Laplace transformable in the direction
\theta
if
\varphi
has uniform
at-most-exponential growth
as
|\xi|\to+\infty
along the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
Explicitly, this means there are constants
\mathnormal{A},\mathnormal{L}>0
such that for all
(x,\xi)\in U\times\Xi_{\theta}
,

\big|\varphi(x,\xi)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{A}e^{\mathnormal{L}|\xi|}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(122)

C.3. Properties of the Laplace transform.

Recall that the Laplace transform converts (a) the convolution product of functions into the pointwise multiplication of their Laplace transforms, and (b) differentiation by
\xi
into multiplication by
\hbar^{-1}
.
Thus, if
\varphi,\sigma
are two uniformly Laplace transformable holomorphic functions on
U\times\Xi_{\theta}
, then:

\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\varphi\ast\sigma\,](x,\hbar)=\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\varphi\,](x,\hbar)\cdot\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\sigma\,](x,\hbar)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\partial_{\xi}\varphi\,](x,\hbar)=\hbar^{-1}\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\varphi\,](x,\hbar)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Let us also note that the convolution product is taken with respect to the variable
\hbar
and recall that it is defined by the following formula:

\varphi\ast\sigma(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int\nolimits_{0}^{\xi}\varphi(x,\xi-y)\sigma(x,y)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}y}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(123)

where the path of integration is a straight line segment from
0
to
\xi
.
Finally, if
\varphi
is a uniformly Laplace transformable holomorphic function on
U\times\Xi_{\theta}
, then for any
x_{0}\in U
, and all
(x,\xi)\in U\times\Xi_{\theta}
,

\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\varphi(t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}\,}}\right]=\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}\big[\,\varphi\,\big](t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(124)

where the path of integration is assumed to lie entirely in
U
.

§ C.2.
The Borel Transform

C.4.

Let
f=f(x,\hbar)
be a holomorphic function on a product domain
U\times S_{\theta}
, or more generally a holomorphic function on the pair
(U,A_{\theta})
as described in
Part ​​ A.6
.
In the latter situation, given
x\in U
, take a sufficiently small Borel disc
S_{\theta}
such that
U_{0}\times S_{\theta}
is contained in
\mathbb{U}
for some neighbourhood
U_{0}
of
x
.

The
analytic Borel transform
(a.k.a., the
inverse Laplace transform
) of
f
in the direction
\theta
is defined by the following formula:

\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,f\,](x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\nolimits_{\theta}f(x,\hbar)e^{\xi/\hbar}\frac{\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\hbar}}{\hbar^{2}}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(125)

Here, the notation “
\oint_{\theta}
” means that the integration is done along the boundary
\wp_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\theta}/\hbar)=1/\delta^{\prime}}}\right\}
of a Borel disc
S^{\prime}_{\theta}\subsetneq S_{\theta}
of strictly smaller diameter
\delta^{\prime}<\delta
, traversed anticlockwise (i.e., emanating from the singular point
\hbar=0
in the direction
\theta-\pi/2
and reentering in the direction
\theta+\pi/2
).
Observe that the integral kernel
e^{\xi/\hbar}\hbar^{-2}
has an essential singularity at
\hbar=0
for every nonzero
\xi
.
We therefore interpret this improper integral as the Cauchy principal value, which means it is defined as the limit of an integral over a sequence of path segments on the boundary
\partial S^{\prime}_{\theta}
approaching the singular point
\hbar=0
in both directions at the same rate.
Explicitly, parameterise the boundary path
\wp_{\theta}
by
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
as
\hbar(t)=e^{i\theta}(\delta^{\prime}+it)^{-1}
.
Then for every
\mathnormal{T}>0
, we take the path segment
\wp_{\theta}(\mathnormal{T})
for
t\in[-\mathnormal{T},+\mathnormal{T}]
and define

\oint\nolimits_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lim_{\mathnormal{T}\to+\infty}\int\nolimits_{\wp_{\theta}(\mathnormal{T})}

(126)

Finally, since the integrand
f(x,\hbar)e^{\xi/\hbar}\hbar^{-2}
is holomorphic in the interior of the Borel disc
S_{\theta}
, it is not difficult to see that the integral in (
125
) is independent of
\delta^{\prime}
, provided that it exists.
If the integral (
125
) converges for all
\xi\in e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and uniformly (resp. locally uniformly) for all
x\in U
, then we say
f
is
uniformly
(resp.
locally uniformly
)
Borel transformable
in the direction
\theta
.
The following lemma gives a criterion for Borel transformability.

C.4 Lemma

( )
.

If
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(U;A_{\theta})
admits uniform (resp. locally uniform) Gevrey asymptotics as
\hbar\to 0
along the closed arc
\overline{A}_{\theta}
(in symbols,
f\in{\mathcal{G}}(U;\overline{A}_{\theta})
), then it is uniformly (resp. locally uniformly) Borel transformable in the direction
\theta
.

A proof of this proposition (which is a simple complex analytic argument using the Gevrey bounds (
103
)) can be found in
[
Nik20
, Lemma B.5 and Theorem B.11]
.
We stress the importance in this lemma of having Gevrey asymptotics along the
closed
arc
\overline{A}_{\theta}
and not just
A_{\theta}
.
For example, recall from
Part ​​ A.11
that the function
f(\hbar)=e^{-1/\hbar}
is in
{\mathcal{G}}(A)
, where
A=(-\pi/2,+\pi/2)
, but not in
{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A})
, and we can see that its Borel transform in the direction
\theta=0
is not well-defined when
\xi=1
.

C.5.

If
f
is holomorphic at
\hbar=0
(i.e., if
f\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar}}\right\}
), then it is necessarily Borel transformable in every direction, and all these Borel transforms agree and define a holomorphic function
\mathfrak{B}[\,f\,](x,\xi)
of
(x,\xi)\in U\times\mathbb{C}_{\xi}
.
In fact, in this case the integration contour in (
125
) can be deformed to a circle around the origin, so that the Borel transform of
f
for any
\theta
is nothing but the residue integral:

\mathfrak{B}[\,f\,](x,\xi)=\underset{\hbar=0}{\operatorname{Res}}\>\frac{f(x,\hbar)e^{\xi/\hbar}}{\hbar^{2}}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(127)

C.6.

Using the residue calculus expression (
127
), it is easy to deduce the following helpful formulas:

\mathfrak{B}[\,1\,]=0\quad\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\quad\mathfrak{B}[\,\hbar^{k+1}\,]=\frac{\xi^{k}}{k!}\qquad\text{for all $k\geqslant 0$.}

(128)

They can be used to extend the Borel transform to formal power series in
\hbar
by defining the
formal Borel transform
:
for any
\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
,

\mbox{}\hskip-10.0pt\widehat{\varphi}(x,\xi)=\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}[\,\widehat{f}\,](x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varphi_{n}(x)\xi^{n}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\xi}}\right\rrbracket\quad\text{where}\quad\varphi_{k}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\tfrac{1}{k!}f_{k+1}(x){\color[rgb]{0.6,0.6,0.6}.}

(129)

Thus, the formal Borel transform essentially ‘divides’ the coefficients of the power series by
n!
.
The following lemma follows immediately from this formula and the Gevrey power series bounds (
105
).

C.6 Lemma

( )
.

If
\widehat{f}
is a uniformly or locally-uniformly Gevrey series on
U
(in symbols,
\widehat{f}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
or
\widehat{f}\in{\mathcal{G}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
, respectively), then its formal Borel transform
\widehat{\varphi}
is respectively a uniformly or locally uniformly convergent series in
\xi
.
In symbols,
\widehat{\varphi}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
or
\widehat{\varphi}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
, respectively.

C.7. Properties of the Borel transform.

Finally, we mention a few important properties of the Borel transform.
Given two uniformly Borel
\theta
-transformable holomorphic functions
f,g
on
(U,A_{\theta})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,fg\,]=\big(\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,f\,]\ast\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,g\,]\big){\color[rgb]{0.6,0.6,0.6};}

(130)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,\hbar^{-1}f\,]=\partial_{\xi}\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,f\,]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,\partial_{x}f\,]=\partial_{x}\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,f\,]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8};}\hss}}

(131)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}f(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}\,}}\right](x,\xi)=\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}\big[\,f\,\big](t,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(132)

for any

§ C.3.
Borel Resummation

One of the most fundamental theorems in Gevrey asymptotics is a theorem of Nevanlinna
[
Nev18
, pp.44-45]
which was rediscovered and clarified decades later by Sokal
[
Sok80
]
; see also
[
Mal95
, p.182]
, and
[
LR16
, Theorem 5.3.9]
.
It identifies a subclass of Gevrey formal power series with a class of sectorial germs admitting Gevrey asymptotics in a halfplane.
This identification is often called
Borel resummation
and it should be thought of in complete analogy with the familiar identification of convergent power series with germs of holomorphic functions.
The exceptions are that the identification for Gevrey series depends on a direction
\theta
and the operation of converting a formal power serious into a holomorphic function is much more involved.
For clarity, we also provide definitions and statements in the single-variable case.

C.7 Definition

( )
.

A Gevrey power series
\widehat{f}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is a
Borel summable series
in the direction
\theta
if its formal Borel transform
\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
admits an analytic continuation
\varphi(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\mathrm{AnCont}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\varphi}\,](\xi)
to a tubular neighbourhood
\Xi_{\theta}
of the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with at-most-exponential growth as
|\xi|\to+\infty
in
\Xi_{\theta}
.
The subring of Borel summable series in the direction
\theta
will be denoted by
\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\subset\mathbb{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.

More generally in the parametric situation, a uniformly Gevrey power series
\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is called a
uniformly Borel summable series
in the direction
\theta
if its (necessarily uniformly convergent) formal Borel transform
\widehat{\varphi}(x,\xi)=\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
admits an analytic continuation
\varphi(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\mathrm{AnCont}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\varphi}\,](x,\xi)
to a domain
U\times\Xi_{\theta}
for some tubular neighbourhood
\Xi_{\theta}
of the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with uniformly at-most-exponential growth as
|\xi|\to+\infty
in
\Xi_{\theta}
.

Locally uniformly Borel summable series
on
U
are defined the same way by allowing the thickness of the tubular neighbourhood
\Xi_{\theta}
to have a locally constant dependence on
x
.
We denote the subalgebras of uniformly and locally uniformly Borel summable series in the direction
\theta
by
\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta,{\mathrm{u}}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\subset{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
and
\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\subset{\mathcal{G}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
, respectively.

C.8 Theorem

(
Nevanlinna’s Theorem
)
.

In both the one-dimensional and the parametric cases, for any direction
\theta
, the asymptotic expansion map
\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits
along the halfplane arc
A_{\theta}=(\theta-\pi/2,\theta+\pi/2)
bisected by
\theta
restricts to an algebra isomorphism

\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits:{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A}_{\theta})\overset{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.35623pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,}{\longrightarrow}\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\quad\text{or}\quad\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits:{\mathcal{G}}(U;\overline{A}_{\theta})\overset{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.35623pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,}{\longrightarrow}\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(133)

For a detailed proof, see
[
Nik20
, Appendix §B.4]
.
Note that
\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits
restricts further to an isomorphism
{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;\overline{A}_{\theta})\overset{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.35623pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,}{\longrightarrow}\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta,{\mathrm{u}}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
.

C.8 Definition

( )
.

The inverse algebra isomorphism

{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits^{-1}:\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\overset{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.35623pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,}{\longrightarrow}{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A}_{\theta})\quad\text{or}\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits^{-1}:\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket\overset{\,\smash{\raisebox{-1.35623pt}{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}\,}{\longrightarrow}{\mathcal{G}}(U;\overline{A}_{\theta})

(134)

is called the
Borel resummation
in the direction
\theta
.
In particular,
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}
restricts to be the identity map on the subalgebra of convergent power series: i.e., if
\widehat{f}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar}}\right\}
or
\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar}}\right\}
, then
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}(\widehat{f})=\widehat{f}
.

C.9. Properties of Borel resummation.

As an algebra isomorphism, Borel resummation respects sum, products, and scalar multiplication: if
\widehat{f},\widehat{g}\in\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
(or
\widehat{f},\widehat{g}\in\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
) are any two (respectively locally uniformly) Borel summable series in the direction
\theta
, and
c\in\mathbb{C}
(or respectively
c=c(x)\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)
), then

{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}+c\widehat{g}\,]={\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]+c{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{g}\,]\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\cdot\widehat{g}\,]={\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]\cdot{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{g}\,]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(135)

It is also compatible with composition with convergent power series.
In particular, the identity that we use most often in this paper is

{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\exp\big(\,\widehat{f}\,\big)\,]=\exp\Big({\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(136)

We also note two more properties with respect to calculus in the
x
-variable.
If
\widehat{f}(x,\hbar)\in\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is a locally uniformly Borel summable series in the direction
\theta
, then

\partial_{x}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]={\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\partial_{x}\widehat{f}\,]\quad\text{and}\quad\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]={\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{f}\,}}\right]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(137)

for any
x_{0}\in U
where the path of integration is assumed to lie entirely in
U
.

From the proof of Nevanlinna’s Theorem
C.8
we can extract the following more explicit statement that yields a formula for the Borel resummation which is actually often taken as the definition of Borel resummation.

C.9 Lemma

(
Borel-Laplace identity
)
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{G}}(\overline{A}_{\theta})
and
\widehat{f}\in\overline{\mathbb{G}}_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
be such that
\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits(f)=\widehat{f}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]=f
.
Let
\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}[\,\widehat{f}\,](\xi)\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
be the (necessarily convergent) formal Borel transform of
\widehat{f}
, and let
\varphi(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\mathrm{AnCont}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\varphi}\,](\xi)
be its analytic continuation to a tubular neighbourhood
\Xi_{\theta}
of the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with at-most-exponential growth as
|\xi|\to+\infty
in
\Xi_{\theta}
.
Then we have the following two identities:

f(\hbar)=f_{0}+\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\varphi\,](\hbar)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(\xi)=\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,f\,](\xi)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(138)

More generally in the parametric situation, let
f\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U;\overline{A}_{\theta})
and
\widehat{f}\in\overline{{\mathcal{G}}}_{\theta,{\mathrm{u}}}(U;\overline{A}_{\theta})
be such that
\mathop{{\mathrm{\text{\ae}}}}\nolimits(f)=\widehat{f}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]=f
.
Let
\widehat{\varphi}(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}[\,\widehat{f}\,](\xi)\in{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathrm{u}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi}}\right\}
be the (necessarily uniformly convergent) formal Borel transform of
\widehat{f}
, and let
\varphi(x,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\mathrm{AnCont}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\varphi}\,](x,\xi)
be its analytic continuation to the domain
U\times\Xi_{\theta}
, where
\Xi_{\theta}
in a tubular neighbourhood of the ray
e^{i\theta}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, with uniformly at-most-exponential growth as
|\xi|\to+\infty
in
\Xi_{\theta}
.
Then we have the following two identities:

f(x,\hbar)=f_{0}(x)+\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}[\,\varphi\,](x,\hbar)\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi(x,\xi)=\mathfrak{B}_{\theta}[\,f\,](x,\xi)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(139)

In other words, in both situations we have the following formula for the Borel resummation:

f={\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]=f_{0}+\mathfrak{L}_{\theta}\Big[\,\mathop{{\mathrm{AnCont}}}\nolimits_{\theta}\big[\,\widehat{\mathfrak{B}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{f}\,]\,\big]\,\Big]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(140)

C.10. Borel resummation of exponential series.

Finally, we extend Borel resummation to exponential power series by simply factorising out the exponential prefactor.
Thus, an exponential series
\widehat{\psi}=e^{\Phi/\hbar}\widehat{\Psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is a
Borel summable exponential series
in the direction
\theta
if
\widehat{\Psi}
is Borel summable in the direction
\theta
, and we put

{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\psi}\,]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=e^{\Phi/\hbar}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\Psi}\,]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

More generally in the parametric case, an exponential power series
\widehat{\psi}=e^{\Phi/\hbar}\widehat{\Psi}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\exp}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
is a
Borel summable exponential series
in the direction

\widehat{\Psi}
is Borel summable in the direction

, and we again put
{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\psi}\,]\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=e^{\Phi/\hbar}{\mathcal{S}}_{\theta}[\,\widehat{\Psi}\,]

§ C.1.
§​​ 3.2
: Existence and Uniqueness of Formal WKB Solutions

We prove existence, uniqueness, and the property of forming the basis.
All other claims are established in the course of the proof.

1. Existence.

To prove existence, we search for exponential power series solutions in the form of the formal WKB ansatz:

\widehat{\psi}(x,\hbar)=\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int\nolimits_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{s}(t,\hbar)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}\right)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

where
\widehat{s}
is the unknown power series in
{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket

Note that
\widehat{\psi}(x_{0},\hbar)=1
for any
\widehat{s}
.
Substituting this expression back into the differential equation (
15
), we find that
\widehat{s}
must satisfy the formal Riccati equation (
21
).
Expanding (
21
) term by term in
\hbar
and using the fact that
U
contains no turning points, we obtain the two exponential power series solutions (
20
).

Let
\widehat{\psi}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=e^{-\Phi/\hbar}\widehat{\Psi}

\Phi\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)[\hbar^{-1}]

\hbar^{-1}

First, the normalisation condition forces
\Phi(x_{0},\hbar^{-1})=0

\widehat{\Psi}(x_{0},\hbar)=1

\Phi_{-i}(x_{0})=0

\Psi_{k}(x_{0})=0

\Psi_{0}(x_{0})=1

Next, plugging
\widehat{\psi}

\Psi_{0}(x_{0})=1
implies
\Psi_{0}=\exp\int_{x_{0}}^{x}s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}
.

In particular,
\Psi_{0}
is nonvanishing on
U
, so we can write
\widehat{\Psi}=\exp\widehat{\mathnormal{R}}
for some power series
\widehat{\mathnormal{R}}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
which satisfies
\mathnormal{R}_{k}(x_{0})=0
for all
k\geqslant 1
.
Then the same calculation shows that
\partial_{x}\mathnormal{R}_{k}=s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}
for each
k\geqslant 1
, and hence
\mathnormal{R}_{k}(x)=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(k)$}}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}
.
Therefore,
\widehat{\mathnormal{R}}=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\alpha}(t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}
, demonstrating uniqueness of formal WKB solutions amongst all possible exponential power series solutions.

3. Basis.

To see that the formal WKB solutions
\widehat{\psi}_{+},\widehat{\psi}_{-}
form a basis of generators for the
\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
-module
\widehat{\mathbb{ES}}(U)
of all formal solutions, we note first that they are linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
because their exponents
\Phi_{+},\Phi_{-}
are distinct nonconstant functions on
U
.
Now, suppose

\widehat{\psi}=\sum_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny{finite}}}\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}e^{-\Phi_{\alpha}/\hbar}\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}\in\widehat{\mathbb{S}}(U)

is a formal solution of (
15
) with all
\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}=\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
nonzero, and all exponents
\Phi_{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{O}}(U)[\hbar^{-1}]
mutually linearly independent over
\mathbb{C}[\hbar^{-1}]
.
We need to show that there are
\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{+},\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{-}\in\mathbb{C}^{\exp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
such that
\widehat{\psi}=\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{+}\widehat{\psi}_{+}+\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{-}\widehat{\psi}_{-}
.

We can immediately assume that the leading-order
\Psi_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}
of every power series
\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}
is nonzero by absorbing any excess powers of
\hbar
into the coefficient
\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}
.
Then, upon substituting
\widehat{\psi}
into (
15
), the same calculation that led to (
143
) yields

\sum_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny{finite}}}\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigg[\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}+\Big(-2(\partial_{x}\Phi_{\alpha})+\widehat{p}\Big)\hbar\partial_{x}\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}+\Big((\partial_{x}\Phi_{\alpha})^{2}-\hbar(\partial_{x}^{2}\Phi_{\alpha})-\widehat{p}(\partial_{x}\Phi_{\alpha})+\widehat{q}\Big)\widehat{\Psi}_{\alpha}\Bigg]e^{-\Phi_{\alpha}/\hbar}=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

This is a sum of exponential power series with linearly independent exponents, so for it to be zero, each Laurent series in large square brackets must be zero.
Let
\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(-m)$}}_{\alpha}
be the highest in
\hbar^{-1}
degree part of
\Phi_{\alpha}
.
As before, if
m\geqslant 1
, the vanishing of the expression in the bracket leads to the equation
(\partial_{x}\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(-m)$}}_{\alpha})^{2}=0
forcing
\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(-m)$}}_{\alpha}
to be a constant.
As a result, each factor
e^{-\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(-m)$}}_{\alpha}\hbar^{-m-1}}
can be absorbed into the coefficient
\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}
.
Continuing in this fashion to remove such exponential factors one by one from
e^{-\Phi_{\alpha}/\hbar}
, we are left with only the zeroth-order term
\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}_{\alpha}(x)
, which must satisfy the equation

\big(\partial_{x}\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}_{\alpha}\big)^{2}-p_{0}\big(\partial_{x}\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}_{\alpha}\big)+q_{0}=0\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

This is nothing but the leading-order equation (
17
) for
\lambda=\partial_{x}\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}_{\alpha}
.
Therefore,
\partial_{x}\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}_{\alpha}
can only either be the leading-order solution
\lambda_{+}
or
\lambda_{-}
, and so
\Phi^{\text{\tiny$(0)$}}_{\alpha}
can only differ from either
\Phi_{+}
or
\Phi_{-}
by a constant which can once again be absorbed into
\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{\alpha}
.
Thus, we conclude that each polynomial
\Phi_{\alpha}
is either
\Phi_{+}
or
\Phi_{-}
, and therefore the formal solution
\widehat{\psi}
is actually of the form
\widehat{\psi}=\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{+}e^{-\Phi_{+}/\hbar}\widehat{\Psi}_{+}+\widehat{\mathnormal{C}}_{-}e^{-\Phi_{-}/\hbar}\widehat{\Psi}_{-}
for some power series
\widehat{\Psi}_{\pm}
, which by the exact same calculation as above must be
\exp\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\widehat{\mathnormal{S}}_{\pm}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}
.
∎

§ C.2.
Existence and Uniqueness of Exact Characteristic Solutions

The strategy to construction an exact solution of the Riccati equation (
61
) is to restrict the problem to a horizontal halfstrip domain containing
x_{0}
and transform the equation into standard form which is easier to solve using the Borel-Laplace method.
First, we describe this standard form of the Riccati equation and explain how to solve it.

C.1. Singularly perturbed Riccati equations in standard form.

Fix
\varepsilon,\delta>0
.
Let
\Omega_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}_{z}
and
S_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}_{\hbar}
be respectively a tubular neighbourhood of radius
\varepsilon
of the positive real axis
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}_{z}
and a Borel disc of diameter
\delta

\Omega_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z~\big|~\mathop{{\mathrm{dist}}}\nolimits(z,{\mathbb{R}}_{+})<\varepsilon}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar~\big|~\operatorname{Re}(\hbar^{-1})>1/\delta}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Consider the following singularly perturbed Riccati equation on
\Omega_{+}\times S_{+}

\hbar\partial_{z}f-f=\hbar\big(\mathnormal{A}_{0}+\mathnormal{A}_{1}f+f^{2}\big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(144)

where
\mathnormal{A}_{1},\mathnormal{A}_{0}
are holomorphic functions of
(z,\hbar)\in\Omega_{+}\times S_{+}
which admit uniform Gevrey asymptotics:

\mathnormal{A}_{i}(z,\hbar)\simeq\widehat{\mathnormal{A}}_{i}(z,\hbar)\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $[-\pi/2,+\pi/2]$, unif. $\forall z\in\Omega_{+}$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

C.1 Lemma

(
Main Technical Lemma

[
Nik20
, Lemma 5.6]
)
.

The Riccati equation (

) has a unique formal solution
\widehat{f}\in{\mathcal{O}}(\Omega_{+})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket
and its leading-order term is
0
; i.e.,
\widehat{f}\in\hbar{\mathcal{O}}(\Omega_{+})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket\hbar}}\right\rrbracket

Moreover, for every
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, there is some
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta]
such that the Riccati equation (
144
) has a unique holomorphic solution
f
on

\Omega^{\prime}_{+}\times S^{\prime}_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{z~\big|~\mathop{{\mathrm{dist}}}\nolimits(z,{\mathbb{R}}_{+})<\varepsilon^{\prime}}}\right\}\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\hbar~\big|~\operatorname{Re}(\hbar^{-1})>1/\delta^{\prime}}}\right\}\subset\Omega_{+}\times S_{+}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(145)

which admits
\widehat{f}
as a uniform Gevrey asymptotic expansion in the right halfplane:

f(z,\hbar)\simeq\widehat{f}(z,\hbar)\quad\text{as $\hbar\to 0$ along $[-\pi/2,+\pi/2]$, unif. $\forall z\in\Omega^{\prime}_{+}$\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}
}

(146)

A proof is presented in
[
Nik20
]
, but since it does most of the heavy-lifting in the present paper, we include here a sketch for completeness.

Existence and uniqueness of the formal solution

is easy to deduce by expanding the Riccati equation (
144
) in powers of
\hbar
(see
[
Nik20
, Theorem 3.8]
for details).
We now prove the existence and uniqueness of
f
.

1. The analytic Borel transform.

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
be fixed.
The Riccati equation (
144
) is solved using the Borel-Laplace method.
The first step is to apply the analytic Borel transform:

\alpha_{i}(z,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathfrak{B}_{+}[\>\mathnormal{A}_{i}\>](z,\xi)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(147)

Here,
\mathfrak{B}_{+}
is the Borel transform (
125
) for
\theta=0
.

Since each
\mathnormal{A}_{i}
admits uniform Gevrey asymptotics on
\Omega_{+}
as
\hbar\to 0
along the closed arc
[-\pi/2,+\pi/2]
, it follows from Nevanlinna’s
Theorem C.8
that there is some tubular neighbourhood
\Xi_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\xi~\big|~\mathop{{\mathrm{dist}}}\nolimits(\xi,{\mathbb{R}}_{+})<\rho}}\right\}
of radius
\rho>0
(which we take to be so small that
\varepsilon^{\prime}+\rho<\varepsilon
) such that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
define holomorphic functions on
\Omega_{+}\times\Xi_{+}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}_{z\xi}
with uniformly at most exponential growth as
|\xi|\to+\infty
.
They furthermore satisfy the following relations: for all
(z,\xi)\in\Omega_{+}\times\Xi_{+}
,

\mathnormal{A}_{0}(z,\hbar)=a_{0}(z)+\mathfrak{L}_{+}[\>\alpha_{0}\>]\quad\text{and}\quad\mathnormal{A}_{1}(z,\hbar)=a_{1}(z)+\mathfrak{L}_{+}[\>\alpha_{1}\>]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(148)

Here,
\mathfrak{L}_{+}
is the Laplace transform (
121
) for
\theta=0
.
Thus, dividing (
144
) through by
\hbar
and applying the analytic Borel transform
\mathfrak{B}_{+}
, we obtain the following PDE with convolution product:

\partial_{z}\varphi-\partial_{\xi}\varphi=\alpha_{0}+a_{1}\varphi+\alpha_{1}\ast\varphi+\varphi\ast\varphi\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

(149)

where the unknown variables
f
and
\varphi
are related by

\varphi=\mathfrak{B}_{+}[\>f\>]\quad\text{and}\quad f=\mathfrak{L}_{+}[\>\varphi\>]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(150)

2. Solving the PDE.

To solve the PDE (
149
), we consider another holomorphic change of variables sending
(z,\xi)\mapsto(z+\xi,\xi)=\mathrel{\mathop{:}}(w,t)
which transforms the differential operator
\partial_{z}-\partial_{\xi}
into
-\partial_{t}
.
Then (
149

\varphi(z,\xi)=a_{0}(z)-\int\nolimits_{0}^{\xi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.{\Big(\alpha_{0}+a_{1}\varphi+\alpha_{1}\ast\varphi+\varphi\ast\varphi\Big)}}}\right|_{(z+\xi-u,u)}\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}u}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(151)

Introduce the following shorthand notation: for any function
\alpha=\alpha(z,\xi)
, let

\mathnormal{I}_{+}[\>\alpha\>](z,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\int\nolimits_{0}^{\xi}\alpha(z+\xi-u,u)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}u}=-\int\nolimits_{0}^{\xi}\alpha(z+t,\xi-t)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}t}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(152)

In this notation, the integral equation (
151
) becomes

\varphi=a_{0}+\mathnormal{I}_{+}\big[\>\alpha_{0}+a_{1}\varphi+\alpha_{1}\ast\varphi+\varphi\ast\varphi\>\big]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(153)

3. Method of successive approximations.

To solve (
153
), we use the method of successive approximations.
Consider a sequence of holomorphic functions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\varphi_{n}}}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
defined recursively by
\varphi_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=a_{0}
,
\varphi_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathnormal{I}_{+}\big[\alpha_{0}+a_{1}\varphi_{0}\big]
, and for
n\geqslant 2
by

\varphi_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathnormal{I}_{+}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a_{1}\varphi_{n-1}+\alpha_{1}\ast\varphi_{n-2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\geqslant 0\\
i+j=n-2\end{subarray}}\varphi_{i}\ast\varphi_{j}}}\right]\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(154)

Notice that each
\varphi_{n}
defines a holomorphic function on the domain

W\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(z,\xi)\in\Omega_{+}\times\Xi_{+}~\big|~z+\xi\in\Omega_{+}}}\right\}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Notice furthermore that
\Omega^{\prime}_{+}\times\Xi_{+}\subset W
.
The most technical part of the proof is to show that the infinite series

\varphi(z,\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\varphi_{n}(z,\xi)

(155)

is uniformly convergent for all
(z,\xi)\in W
to a holomorphic solution of the integral equation (
153
) on
W
with uniformly at-most-exponential growth at infinity in
\xi
.

Assuming that
\varphi
is uniformly convergent on
W
, it is a straightforward computation to show that
\varphi
indeed satisfies (
153
).
The bulk of the proof is therefore to demonstrate uniform convergence of (
155
).

4. Uniform convergence.

Let
\mathnormal{M},\mathnormal{L}>0
be constants such that
|a_{i}|\leqslant\mathnormal{M}
and
|\alpha_{i}|\leqslant\mathnormal{M}e^{\mathnormal{L}|\xi|}
for all
{(z,\xi)\in W}
.
These constants exist because
W\subset\Omega_{+}\times\Xi_{+}
.
We need to show that there are constants
\mathnormal{A},\mathnormal{B}>0
such that, for all
n\geqslant 0
,

\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\big(\forall(z,\xi)\in W\big)$\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad}\big|\varphi_{n}(z,\xi)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{A}\mathnormal{B}^{n}\frac{|\xi|^{n}}{n!}e^{\mathnormal{L}|\xi|}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(156)

To show this, we first recursively construct a sequence of positive real numbers
(\mathnormal{M}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that, for all
n\geqslant 0
,

\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\big(\forall(z,\xi)\in W\big)$\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad}\big|\varphi_{n}(z,\xi)\big|\leqslant\mathnormal{M}_{n}\frac{|\xi|^{n}}{n!}e^{\mathnormal{L}|\xi|}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(157)

We then show that there are constants
\mathnormal{A},\mathnormal{B}>0
such that
\mathnormal{M}_{n}\leqslant\mathnormal{A}\mathnormal{B}^{n}
for all
n
.

Since
\varphi_{0}=a_{0}
, the constant
\mathnormal{M}_{0}
can be taken to be
1
.
The constants
\mathnormal{M}_{n}
can then be constructed by induction with the help some elementary integral estimates.
In the end, our constants
\mathnormal{M}_{n}
are given by the following recursive formula:

\mathnormal{M}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathnormal{M}\bigg(\mathnormal{M}_{n-1}+\mathnormal{M}_{n-2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\geqslant 0\\
i+j=n-2\end{subarray}}\mathnormal{M}_{i}\mathnormal{M}_{j}\bigg)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(158)

To obtain constants
\mathnormal{A},\mathnormal{B}
for the bounds (
156
), we consider the following power series in an abstract variable

:

\widehat{p}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathnormal{M}_{n}t^{n}\in\mathbb{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\llbracket t}}\right\rrbracket\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

It is enough to prove that
\widehat{p}(t)
is convergent.
The key is to observe that it satisfies the algebraic equation
\widehat{p}=\Big(1+\widehat{p}t+\widehat{p}t^{2}+\widehat{p}^{2}t^{2}\Big)
.
Then we consider the holomorphic function
\mathnormal{F}=\mathnormal{F}(p,t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-p+\Big(1+pt+pt^{2}+p^{2}t^{2}\Big)
of two variables.
It satisfies
\mathnormal{F}(1,0)=0
and has a nonvanishing derivative with respect to
p
at
(p,t)=(1,0)
, so we can use the Holomorphic Implicit Function Theorem to conclude that there is a function
p(t)
, holomorphic at
t=0
, whose Taylor series is
\widehat{p}(t)
.

Finally, uniform convergence of
\varphi
follows from the following calculation, which simultaneously shows that
\varphi
has at most exponential growth at infinity in
\xi
:

\big|\varphi(z,\xi)\big|\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\varphi_{n}|\leqslant\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathnormal{A}\mathnormal{B}^{n}\frac{|\xi|^{n}}{n!}e^{\mathnormal{L}|\xi|}\leqslant\mathnormal{A}e^{(\mathnormal{B}+\mathnormal{L})|\xi|}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(159)

5. The Laplace transform.

Applying the Laplace transform to
\varphi
yields

f(z,\hbar)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathfrak{L}\big[\varphi\big](z,\hbar)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\xi/\hbar}\varphi(z,\xi)\mathinner{{\mathrm{d}}\xi}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(160)

Thus, whenever
\delta^{\prime}<(\mathnormal{B}+\mathnormal{L})^{-1}
and
\leqslant\delta

Moreover, Nevanlinna’s Theorem implies that

admits uniform Gevrey asymptotics on
\Omega^{\prime}_{+}
as

along
[-\pi/2,+\pi/2]

6. Uniqueness.

Uniqueness of

is another such solution, then

3. Transformation to the Riccati equation in standard form.

Now we transform the Riccati equation (
61
) into its standard form on
\Omega_{+}\times S_{+}
satisfying the hypotheses of
Part ​​ C.1
.
We break this transformation down into a sequence of three steps.
It is convenient to define functions
p_{\ast}
and
q_{\ast}
by the following relations:

p=p_{0}+p_{1}\hbar+p_{\ast}\hbar^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q=q_{0}+q_{1}\hbar+q_{\ast}\hbar^{2}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(164)

Thus, for example, the leading-order in
\hbar
of
p_{\ast},q_{\ast}
is respectively
p_{2},q_{2}

First, we change the unknown variable
s
to
s_{\ast}
given by the relation

s=\lambda_{\alpha}+s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}\hbar+s_{\ast}\hbar^{2}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(165)

In other words, we want to derive an equation for the subleading-orders of the solution
s
by removing the leading-order and the next-to-leading-order characteristic roots
\lambda_{\alpha},s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}

Substituting these expressions into the Riccati equation (
61
), using the identity
\epsilon_{\alpha}\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}=2\lambda_{\alpha}-p_{0}
where
\epsilon_{\pm}=\pm 1

\hbar

17
) and (
25
), we get:

\hbar^{2}\partial_{x}s_{\ast}-\epsilon_{\alpha}\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}\hbar s_{\ast}\\
=\hbar\Big(\hbar^{2}s_{\ast}^{2}+(\hbar p_{\ast}+p_{1}-2s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}})\hbar s_{\ast}+\big(q_{\ast}-p_{\ast}(\lambda_{\alpha}+\hbar s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}})+(s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}})^{2}-\partial_{x}s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}-p_{1}s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}\big)\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Next, we transform
s_{\ast}
to

\hbar s_{\ast}=\epsilon_{\alpha}\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}\,\mathnormal{T}\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

Substituting and dividing through by

\epsilon_{\alpha}\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}\partial_{x}\mathnormal{T}-\mathnormal{T}=\hbar\Big(b_{0}+\mathnormal{B}_{0}+(b_{1}+\mathnormal{B}_{1})\mathnormal{T}+\mathnormal{T}^{2}\Big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

\displaystyle\mathnormal{B}_{0}

(168)

\displaystyle b_{0}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{q_{2}-p_{2}\lambda_{\alpha}+(s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}})^{2}-\partial_{x}s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}-p_{1}s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}}{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{p_{1}-2s_{\alpha}^{\text{\tiny$(1)$}}-\partial_{x}\log\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}{\epsilon_{\alpha}\sqrt{\mathnormal{D}_{0}}}.

\mathnormal{T}(x,\hbar)=f\big(z(x),e^{-i\theta}\hbar\big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8}.}\hss}}

(169)

Then

\hbar\partial_{z}f-f=\hbar\big(\mathnormal{A}_{0}+\mathnormal{A}_{1}f+f^{2}\big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

5. Linear independence.

To prove linear independence, we compute the Wronskian of
\psi_{+},\psi_{-}
at the basepoint
x_{0}
using formula (
60

\mathnormal{W}\big(\psi_{-},\psi_{+}\big)=\det\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{-}&\psi_{+}\\
\partial_{x}\psi_{-}&\partial_{x}\psi_{+}\end{bmatrix}=\det\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
\frac{1}{\hbar}s_{-}&\frac{1}{\hbar}s_{+}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\big(s_{+}-s_{-}\big)\text{\hbox to0.0pt{\, {\color[rgb]{0.8,0.8,0.8},}\hss}}

This competes the proofs of
