Elliptic Harnack Inequality for ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d}
Siva Athreya, Nitya Gadhiwala, Ritvik R. Radhakrishnan

Abstract
We prove the scale invariant Elliptic Harnack Inequality (EHI) for non-negative harmonic functions on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} . The purpose of this note is to provide a simplified self-contained probabilistic proof of EHI in ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} that is accessible at the undergraduate level. We use the Local Central Limit Theorem for simple symmetric random walks on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} to establish Gaussian bounds for the n n -step probability function. The uniform Green inequality and the classical Balayage formula then imply the EHI.

Introduction

The scale invariant Elliptic Harnack Inequality (EHI) and its
applications to the theory of elliptic and parabolic partial
differential equations are well known. The work on regularity of
solutions dates back to 1950’s in the works of de Giorgi
[
DG57
]
,
Nash
[
Nas58
]
and Moser
[
Mos61
]
. Moser first proved EHI for
solutions to partial differential equations in divergence form in

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Bombieri-Giusti
[
BG72
]
showed that EHI holds for
minimal surfaces and EHI was proven for metric spaces by Sturm

[
Stu98
]
. We refer the interested reader to the survey article by
Kassmann
[
Kas07
]
, and the references therein for a comprehensive
review of the Harnack inequality (for elliptic and parabolic
operators) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The EHI was established for weighted graphs by Delmotte

[
Del99
]
. Barlow
[
Bar17
]
has a comprehensive treatment of random
walks on locally finite graphs and in
[
Bar17
, Theorem 7.19]

presents a proof of EHI for weighted graphs that have controlled
weights and are roughly isometric to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In this short note our
aim is to provide a simplified self-contained probabilistic proof of
EHI for harmonic functions on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
using properties of the simple symmetric random walk. Lawler in
[
Law13
, Theorem 1.7.2]
also provides a proof of EHI for
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In this short note our aim is to provide a simplified self-contained probabilistic proof of
EHI for harmonic functions on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
following the framework laid out in
[
Bar17
]
using properties of the simple symmetric random walk.

Let
d\geq 1
and
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be the integer lattice. We will think of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as a graph with vertex set

\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\{(x_{i})_{i=1}^{d}:x_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}

endowed with the (graph) distance
d(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{i}|
between any two points
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We may view edge set
E=\{\{x,y\}:x,y\in V,\,d(x,y)=1\}.
For any
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we shall say
x\sim y
if
\{x,y\}\in E
. For
r>0
, we define the ball of radius
r
around a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as

B(x_{0},r)=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:d(x,x_{0})\leq r\}.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We define the boundary of
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
by

\partial A=\{y\in A^{c}:\exists\,x\in A\mbox{ with }x\sim y\}

and set
\bar{A}=A\cup\partial A
. For
f:\bar{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we define the Laplacian
\Delta
of
f
as

\Delta f(x)=\frac{1}{2d}\sum_{x\sim y}(f(x)-f(y)),\qquad\mbox{for all }x\in A.

A function
f:\bar{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is said to be harmonic in

A
if
\Delta f(x)=0
for all
x\in A
. The scale invariant EHI is then stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(
EHI).

Let
d\geq 1
. There exists
C
such that for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
R\geq 1
,
h:\overline{B(x_{0},R)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
h
harmonic in
B(x_{0},R)
we have

\max_{y\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)}h(y)\leq C\min_{z\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)}h(z)

(1.1)

We work with simple symmetric random walks in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, whose
transition probability matrix
P
is connected with the Laplacian

\Delta
. The proof has three key steps – the Gaussian bounds for
the
n
-step probability function of the simple symmetric random walk
(Proposition
2.1
), the uniform Green inequality for the Green
function asssociated with the random walk (Proposition
2.2
),
and the Balayage formula for harmonic functions (Proposition

2.3
).

The proof of Theorem
1.1
that we present here proceeds along
the framework given in the proof of
[
Bar17
, Theorem 7.19]
(which
applies for those graphs which have controlled weights and are
roughly isometric to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
). Our proof uses methods specific
to the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and has appropriate simplifications
whenever possible. To establish Proposition
2.1
for
weighted graphs one first shows the Poincaré inequality along with
a growth condition for volume of the balls in the graph. Then the
equivalence results shown in
[
Del99
]
will imply the result
(See also
[
Bar17
, Theorem 6.28]
). For
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we use the Local Central
Limit Theorem for simple random walks to obtain near diagonal
bounds on the
n
-step transition probability function (See
Proposition
3.2
). Then the Gaussian lower bound is
obtained via a classical chaining of balls argument. The Gaussian
upper bound follows from exponential bounds for the distribution
of exit times of the simple random walk from a ball. For this, we
use the specific structure of the walk in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, by reducing the
question for the
d
dimensional random walk to a one dimensional
walk which follow from Chernoff bounds (See Lemma

3.3
). We also note that Gaussian bounds for the simple
symmetric random walk in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are part of folklore and are well
known but it is hard to point to explicit references in the
literature where they can be found.

As mentioned before,
[
Law13
, Theorem 1.7.2]
also contains a proof for the EHI in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. There the uniform Green inequality is obtained using explicit bounds on the hitting distribution of the walk for three and higher dimensions
[
Law13
, Proposition 1.5.10]
and on the Green function for two dimensions in
[
Law13
, Proposition 1.6.7]
. The Local Central Limit Theorem is used to derive precise asymptotics on the Green function and the propositions are proved via an application of the optional stopping theorem for martingales. For proving the uniform Green inequality we use Proposition

2.1
to establish Gaussian bounds for
n
-step transition
probabilities of walk killed on exiting the ball. Plugging these
estimates and exit time bounds obtained above into the definition
of the Green function one obtains the result. The Balayage
formula is shown using the probabilistic representation for the
solution to the Dirichlet problem and standard potential theory
techniques. The proofs of uniform Green inequality and the Balayage formula translate to graphs that have controlled weights and are roughly isometric to
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(See
[
Bar17
, Theorem 4.26, Theorem 7.15]
).

The Balayage formula is analogous to the classical Poisson integral
formula for Harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Green function
playing the role of the Poisson Kernel. The uniform Green inequality
then enables the scale invariant Harnack inequality. The uniform Green
inequality are implied by the Gaussian bounds.

We remark that the constant
C
in Theorem
1.1
does not depend on
R
but only on dimension
d
and the
\frac{1}{2}
-scaling used to obtain the smaller ball. There are many applications of the EHI. For instance, using EHI one can show that the probability of exiting the boundary of
B(x_{0},R)
via a particular set is comparable for different starting points inside
B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}
). The EHI implies the oscillation inequality for harmonic functions, in the sense that if for any set
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d},

\mbox{Osc}(h,A)=\sup_{x\in A}h(x)-\inf_{x\in A}h(x)

then there is a
0<\delta<1
such that

\mbox{Osc}\left(h,B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)\right)\leq(1-\delta)\mbox{Osc}\left(h,B(x_{0},R)\right).

The oscillation inequality will then imply Hölder continuity for
h
and more importantly the Strong Liouville property, which asserts that if
h\geq 0
and is harmonic on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
then
h
is constant (See
[
Bar17
, Theorem 1.46]
).

Proof of Theorem
1.1

When
d=1
, it is easy to see that any harmonic function
h:\mathbb{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is of the form
h(k)=\alpha k+\beta
for some real numbers
\alpha,\beta.
Then (
1.1
) follows immediately. We will now present a proof when
d\geq 2.

Let
X
be the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. That is,
X
is a discrete time Markov chain
(\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0},\{\mathbb{P}^{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}})
with transition matrix
P=[p_{xy}]
given by

p_{xy}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2d}&\mbox{ if }x\sim y,\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

for any
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
on a measure space
(\Omega,{\cal F})
. Under the probability
\mathbb{P}^{x}
we have
\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{0}=x)=1
. Note that
\Delta=(P-I)
. For any
n\geq 1
, we will denote the
n
-step probability function of
X
by

p_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y),

(2.1)

with
p_{1}(x,y)=p_{xy}
. The first step in the proof is to establish the following Gaussian upper and lower bounds for the
n
-step probabilities of the random walk.

Proposition 2.1

(
Gaussian Bounds).

Let
X_{n}
be the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
n
-step probability function
p_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
given by (
2.1
). Then

(a)

there exists
L_{1}>0
and
L_{2}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\geq\max\{1,d(x,y)\}

p_{n}(x,y)+p_{n+1}(x,y)\geq\frac{L_{1}}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-L_{2}\frac{d^{2}(x,y)}{n}\right),

(2.2)

(b)

there exists
U_{1}>0
and
U_{2}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\geq 0

p_{n}(x,y)\leq\frac{U_{1}}{(\max\{n,1\})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-U_{2}\frac{d^{2}(x,y)}{\max\{n,1\}}\right).

(2.3)

The second step in the proof is to use the Gaussian bounds to
establish the Uniform Green Inequality. Now, for any subset
D\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
define the entrance time to
D
and exit time from
D

T_{D}=\inf\{k\geq 0:X_{k}\in D\}\text{ and }\tau_{D}=\inf\{k\geq 0:X_{k}\in D^{c}\}.

We define the killed
n
-step probability function of
X
by

p^{D}_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y,n<\tau_{D}).

(2.4)

The Green function corresponding to
D
,
g_{D}:D\times D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, is given by

g_{D}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}^{D}(x,y).

(2.5)

Thus the Green function represents the expected time spent (or number of visits) by the walk at vertex
y
before exiting the domain
D
when it starts at
x
. We now state the Uniform Green Inequality.

Proposition 2.2

(
Uniform Green Inequality).

There exists
G_{1},

G_{2}>0
such that if
R\geq 2,B=B(x_{0},R),x,y\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2})
,
r=\max\{1,d(x,y)\}
, then

(a)

for
d\geq 3

\frac{G_{1}}{r^{d-2}}\leq g_{B}(x,y)\leq\frac{G_{2}}{r^{d-2}},

(2.6)

(b)

and for
d=2

G_{1}\log\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)\leq g_{B}(x,y)\leq G_{2}\log\left(\frac{R}{r}\right).

(2.7)

The uniform Green inequality is a key tool in the proof of EHI due to
the fact that Green function plays a role similar to that of the
Poisson Kernel for harmonic functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This established by
the Balayage formula for harmonic functions. To state the result, we
need one additional notation. For any
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the
interior boundary of
A
by

\partial_{i}A=\{y\in A:\exists\,x\in A^{c}\mbox{ with }x\sim y\}.

Figure 1:
(Balayage Formula) For harmonic function
h:B\rightarrow R
and a
A\subset B
one can find a
f:\partial_{i}A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
h
is a weighted sum of
f
in
A
with the weights given by the Green function.

Proposition 2.3

(
Balayage formula).

Let
R\geq 1
,
x_{0}\in V
,
B=B(x_{0},R),A\subsetneq B
. Assume
h:\overline{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
h
being harmonic in
B.
Then there exist
f:\partial_{i}A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

h(x)=\sum_{y\in\partial_{i}A}g_{B}(x,y)f(y)\,\quad\forall\,x\in A

(2.8)

Assuming the above Propositions we are now ready to prove the main
result. The proof is essentially an application of the Balayage
formula and the uniform Green inequality, followed by a standard
chaining of balls argument in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We will first consider the case when
R\leq 32
. For
x,y\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2})
such that
x\sim y
we have

h(x)=\sum_{z\sim x}\frac{1}{2d}h(z)\geq\frac{1}{2d}h(y).

(2.9)

Now for arbitary
x_{1},x_{2}\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2})
there exists
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots z_{k}
such that
x_{1}\sim z_{1}\sim z_{2}\ldots z_{k}\sim y
with
k\leq 32.
Using (
2.9
), we have that

h(x_{1})\geq\frac{1}{(2d)^{k}}h(x_{2})\geq\frac{1}{(2d)^{32}}h(x_{2}).

As
x_{1},x_{2}
were arbitrary,

\max_{y\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)}h(y)\leq(2d)^{32}\min_{z\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)}h(z).

(2.10)

Thus proving (
1.1
) whenever
R\leq 32
. We shall now assume
R>32
. Set
B=B(x_{0},R),A=B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right).
Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{4}\right)
and
y\in\partial_{i}A
. Firstly,

\frac{R}{4}\leq d(x_{i},y)\leq\frac{3R}{4}\qquad\mbox{for $i=1,2$}.

Applying Proposition
2.2
, by (
2.7
) or (
2.6
), we have that

g_{B}(x_{1},y)\leq\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}g_{B}(x_{2},y)\qquad\mbox{ for all }y\in\partial_{i}(A).

(2.11)

Using (
2.8
) from Proposition
2.3
, we have that there exists
f
supported on
\partial_{i}A
such that

h(x)=\sum_{y\in\partial_{i}A}g_{B}(x,y)f(y)\,\quad\forall\,x\in A.

Applying (
2.11
) in the above we have that

h(x_{1})=\sum_{y\in\partial_{i}A}g_{B}(x_{1},y)f(y)\leq\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}\sum_{y\in\partial_{i}A}g_{B}(x_{2},y)f(y)=\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}h(x_{2}).

(2.12)

whenever
x_{1},x_{2}\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{4}).
To complete the proof we use a standard chaining of balls argument. As
R>32
, it is easy to see that there exists
N\geq 1
such that for all
u,v\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2})
there exists
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{N}\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2})
such that

Figure depicts that standard chain of balls argument used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

\displaystyle u\in B\left(z_{1},\frac{R}{8}\right),\qquad v\in B\left(z_{N},\frac{R}{8}\right),\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad B\left(z_{j},\frac{R}{8}\right)\cap B\left(z_{{j+1}},\frac{R}{8}\right)\neq\emptyset\mbox{ for $1\leq j\leq N-1.$}

Let us choose corresponding arbitrary points
x_{j}\in B(z_{j},\frac{R}{8})\cap B(z_{{j+1}},\frac{R}{8})
. Note that
B(z_{j},\frac{R}{2})\subset B(x_{0},R)
for all
1\leq j\leq N-1
. Thus
h
satisfies the hypothesis when
R
is replaced by
\frac{R}{2}
and
x_{0}
is replaced by
z_{j}
. Applying (
2.12
) with
R
replaced by
\frac{R}{2}
and
x_{0}
successively replaced by
z_{j}
we have that

h(u)\leq\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}h(x_{1})\leq\left(\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}\right)^{2}h(x_{2})\leq\left(\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}\right)^{3}h(x_{3})\leq\ldots\left(\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}\right)^{N}h(v).

As
u,v
were arbitary we have that,

\max_{y\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)}h(y)\leq\left(\frac{3^{d-2}G_{1}}{G_{2}}\right)^{N}\min_{z\in B\left(x_{0},\frac{R}{2}\right)}h(z).

(2.13)

We have thus established (
1.1
) whenever
R>32
. This completes the proof.

3
Gaussian Bounds

In this section we prove Proposition
2.1
which provides Gaussian
upper and lower bounds for the
n
-step probability function of the simple
random walk. First we shall use the well known Local Central
Limit Theorem to obtain bounds for the
n
-step probability function near the diagonal. Using this and a chaining of balls argument we will obtain the Gaussian lower bound. For the Gaussian upper bound, we use the near diagonal bounds obtained along with an application of Chernoff bound to a one-dimensional projection of the random walk.

3.1
Near diagonal bounds

The following is the version of the local central limit theorem we use. We shall say that
n
and
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
have the same parity if
n+|z_{1}|+\cdots+|z_{d}|
is even.

Theorem 3.1

(
Local Central Limit Theorem).

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
denote a simple symmetric random walk in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. There exists positive constants
A,B,C
such that for all positive integers
n
and points
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we have

\left|p_{n}(x,y)-\frac{A}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{Bd(x,y)^{2}}{n}\right)\right|\leq\frac{C}{n^{\frac{d}{2}+1}},

(3.1)

whenever
n
and
y-x
have the same parity.

Proof.

We refer the reader to Theorem 1.2.1 in
[
Law13
]
for the proof (or for more detailed explanation of the same in
[
Gad20
]
). We note that the statement in
[
Law13
]
has
d(x,y)
to be standard Euclidean metric namely
||\cdot||_{2}
. The above statement where
d(x,y)
is the graph distance easily follows because we have
c_{1}||x-y||_{2}\leq d(x,y)\leq c_{2}||x-y||_{2}
for some constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
.
∎

Proposition 3.2

(
Near diagonal bounds).

There exists
N_{1}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\geq 0

p_{n}(x,y)\leq\frac{N_{1}}{(\max\{n,1\})^{\frac{d}{2}}},

(3.2)

and
there exists
N_{2}>0
and
L\in(0,1)
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},n\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\max\{1,d^{2}(x,y)\}

p_{n}(x,y)+p_{n+1}(x,y)\geq\frac{N_{2}}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}.

(3.3)

Proof.

We first establish equation (
3.2
) assuming Theorem
3.1
. Choose
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We want to bound
p_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y)
from above. Using the Local Central Limit Theorem we get

\displaystyle p_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{n^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}+\frac{A}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{Bd(x,y)^{2}}{n}\right)\leq\frac{C+A}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}.

Thus the proof of (
3.2
) is complete.

Next we show (
3.3
). Choose
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}),y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We want find a lower bound for
p_{n}(x,y)+p_{n+1}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y)+\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n+1}=y)
. Since the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
has period
2
, exactly one of
p_{n}(x,y)
and
p_{n+1}(x,y)
will be nonzero. The first is nonzero if
n+|y_{1}-x_{1}|+\cdots+|y_{d}-x_{d}|
is even and the second if it is odd, in which case,
n+1+|y_{1}-x_{1}|+\cdots+|y_{d}-x_{d}|
is even. Let
m
be either
n
or
n+1
such that
p_{m}(x,y)
is nonzero, so that
m
and
y-x
have the same parity.

It suffices to show that there exists
c>0
and
L\in(0,1)
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
n\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\max\{1,d^{2}(x,y)\}
, we have

p_{m}(x,y)\geq\frac{c}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}.

Using the Local Central Limit Theorem we have

\displaystyle p_{m}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{A}{m^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{Bd(x,y)^{2}}{m}\right)-\frac{C}{m^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\left(c_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}d(x,y)^{2}}{n}\right)-\frac{c_{3}}{n}\right).

(3.4)

Now, let
n\geq\frac{1}{L^{2}}\max\{1,d^{2}(x,y)\}
, for some
L\in(0,1)
that will be determined later. So we have
L^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n}\max\{1,d^{2}(x,y)\}
. Replacing this in (
3.4
) we get

p_{m}(x,y)\geq\frac{1}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\left(c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}L^{2})-c_{3}L^{2}\right)

Now choose
0<\delta<1
such that for
0<L<\delta
we have

c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}L^{2})>\frac{c_{1}}{2}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad c_{3}L^{2}<\frac{c_{1}}{4}.

So for
0<L<\delta
we get

p_{m}(x,y)\geq\frac{c_{1}}{2n^{\frac{d}{2}}}.

The proof of (
3.3
) is complete.
∎

3.2
Lower Bound

To establish the Gaussian lower bound we consider three cases, divided according to how
R=d(x,y)
compares with

3.3
Upper Bound

Recall that,
B=B(x_{0},R)
and
\tau_{B}=\inf\{k\geq 0:X_{k}\in B^{c}\}
.
We note that
\tau_{B}
is finite with probability one because

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{B}>n)\leq(1-p)^{\lfloor\frac{R}{n}\rfloor}

(3.12)

with (say)
p=(\frac{1}{2d})^{3R}
. We will need the following lemma for the proof of the upper bound.

Lemma 3.3
.

\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{B}<n)\leq 2d\exp(-\frac{R^{2}}{4dn}).

Proof.

We first reduce the question for the
d
dimensional random walk to a one dimensional walk as follows.
Without loss of generality we may assume
x_{0}=0
and consider
B=B(0,R)
. Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
X_{0}=0
. Now, let
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the lazy random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
, starting from
0
with transition matrix given by
q

q(u,v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2d}&\mbox{ if }u=v\pm 1,\\
&\\
\frac{d-1}{d}&\mbox{ if }u=v,\\
&\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Let
X_{n}(k)
denote the
k
th coordinate of
X_{n}
for
1\leq k\leq d
. Note that for each
k
,
\{X_{n}(k)\}_{n\geq 0}
have the same distribution as
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. Also, if
d(X_{n},0)>R
then there is a coordinate
1\leq k\leq d
for which
X_{n}(k)\geq\frac{R}{d}
, consequently using a union bound

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{B}\leq n)\leq d\mathbb{P}(\tau_{\frac{R}{d}}\leq n)

(3.13)

where
\tau_{S}=\inf\{k\geq 0:Y_{k}\not\in[-S,S]\}
for any
S>0
.

We will understand
\tau_{S}
for any
S=\frac{R}{d}
and for that we define two auxiliary stopping times:

\tau_{S}^{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{S}&\mbox{ if }Y_{\tau_{S}}>0,\\
\infty&\mbox{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\tau_{S}^{-}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau_{S}&\mbox{ if }Y_{\tau_{S}}<0,\\
\infty&\mbox{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Further it is easy to see that

\{\tau_{S}\leq n\}=\{\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n\}\dot{\cup}\{\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n\}

implying

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}\leq n)=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n)+\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{-}\leq n).

We will estimate
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n)
and the other term will follow similarly.

For
S\leq k\leq n,
let
A_{k}=\{\tau^{+}_{S}=k\}.
Note that
\mathbb{P}(A_{k})>0
and

\{\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n\}=\cup_{k=S}^{n}A_{k}.

Note that,

\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}|A_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{k}+Y_{n}-Y_{k}|A_{k}]\geq S+\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}-Y_{k}]=S

Let
\lambda>0
and define

T_{n}=\exp(\lambda Y_{n})\qquad\mbox{ for }S\leq n.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]=\sum_{k=S}^{n}{\mathbb{E}[T_{n}|A_{k}]\mathbb{P}(A_{k})}\geq\sum_{k=S}^{n}\exp(\lambda\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}|A_{k}])\mathbb{P}(A_{k})=\exp(\lambda S)\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n)

This implies,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]}{\exp(\lambda S)}.

(3.14)

Now it is standard that

\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]=\left(\frac{d-1}{d}+\frac{\exp(\lambda)}{2d}+\frac{\exp(-\lambda)}{2d}\right)^{n}.

For
0<\lambda<1,
we obtain

\frac{d-1}{d}+\frac{\exp(\lambda)}{2d}+\frac{\exp(-\lambda)}{2d}=\frac{d-1}{d}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2k}}{(2k)!}=1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{d}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2(k-1)}}{(2k)!}\right)\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{d},

as
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2(k-1)}}{(2k)!}<\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k)!}<1.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]\leq\left(1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{d}\right)^{n}\leq\exp\left(n\frac{\lambda^{2}}{d}\right).

Substituting this estimate in
(
3.14
)
and choosing
\lambda=\frac{dS}{2n}
(note that
\lambda<1
) gives

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{+}\leq n)\leq\exp\left(n\frac{\lambda^{2}}{d}-\lambda S\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{dS^{2}}{4n}\right).

Using symmetry of the distribution of the walk, we also have

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}^{-}\leq n)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{dS^{2}}{4n}\right).

Therefore, we get

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{S}\leq n)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{dS^{2}}{4n}\right).

As
S=\frac{R}{d}
in (
3.13
) we get that,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{B}\leq n)\leq d\times\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{\frac{R}{d}}\leq n\right)\leq 2d\exp\left(-\frac{R^{2}}{4dn}\right),

as required.

∎

4

In this section we shall prove Proposition
2.2
. To prove the

n

Recall that
B=B(x_{0},R)
and for any

the
n

p^{B}_{n}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y,n<\tau_{B}).

Clearly,

So the Gaussian upper
bounds follows from Proposition

. The Gaussian lower bound is not that immediate and is presented below.

Lemma 4.1

(Killed -Gaussian Lower Bound)
.

Let
d\geq 1
.
\exists A>0,C>0
such that for all
R\geq 2
,
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
B=B(x_{0},R)

\max\{1,d(x,y)\}\leq n\leq R^{2}

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
x,y\in B(x_{0},\frac{R}{2})
. We shall first prove that
\exists\,0<\delta<\gamma<1
such that if

d(x,y)\leq\delta R
and
d(x,y)\leq n\leq\gamma R^{2}
then (
4.1
) holds.

Then we shall use a chaining argument similar to proof of (
2.2
) for the general case. First we have

\displaystyle p_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y,\tau_{B}>n)+\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y,\tau_{B}\leq n)

\displaystyle=p_{n}^{B}(x,y)+\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y,\tau_{B}\leq n).

As,

\mathbb{P}^{x}(X_{n}=y,\tau_{B}\leq n)=\mathbb{E}^{x}(I_{(\tau_{B}\leq n)}\mathbb{P}^{X_{\tau_{B}}}(X_{n-\tau_{B}}=y))\leq\max_{z\in\partial B}\max_{1\leq m\leq n}\mathbb{P}^{z}(X_{m}=y)

we have

p_{n}^{B}(x,y)\geq p_{n}(x,y)-\max_{z\in\partial B}\max_{1\leq m\leq n}p_{m}(z,y).

(4.2)

We also have a similar lower bound for
p^{B}_{n+1}(x,y)
. Now, for
z\in\partial B
and

\displaystyle p^{B}_{n}(x,y)+p^{B}_{n+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c_{1}^{m}\frac{\exp(-c_{2}\frac{mr^{2}}{s})}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}}\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{s}}\right)^{d(m-1)}.

Using (
4.7
) in the above we have

\displaystyle p^{B}_{n}(x,y)+p^{B}_{n+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-c_{2}\frac{u^{2}}{n})}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}c_{1}^{m}m^{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\frac{u}{2\sqrt{mn}}\right)^{d(m-1)}.

Using the fact that
u\geq\delta R
and
n\leq R^{2}
we have .

\displaystyle p^{B}_{n}(x,y)+p^{B}_{n+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c_{1}\frac{\exp(-c_{2}\frac{u^{2}}{n})}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}c_{3}^{m}m^{d-\frac{dm}{2}}.

As
m=\lfloor\frac{2}{\delta}\rfloor
we have

\displaystyle p^{B}_{n}(x,y)+p^{B}_{n+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c_{4}\frac{\exp(-c_{2}\frac{u^{2}}{n})}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}

(4.8)

So the proof is complete.

∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Recall that
r=\max\{1,d(x,y)\}.
We begin with the lower bounds. From (
2.5
) we have that

g_{B}(x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{p_{n}^{B}(x,y)}\geq\sum_{n=r^{2}}^{R^{2}}{p_{n}^{B}(x,y)}.

Using Lemma
4.1
, we get that there exists
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

g_{B}(x,y)\geq c_{1}\sum_{n=r^{2}}^{R^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\geq c_{2}\int_{r^{2}}^{R^{2}}{s^{-\frac{d}{2}}}ds.

(4.9)

In the last inequality we have used the fact that sum is a upper bound for the Reimann integral. In the case
d=2
, evaluating the integral in (
4.9
) we obtain

g_{B}(x,y)\geq 2\log(R/r).

In the case
d>2
, evaluating the integral in (
4.9
) we obtain

\displaystyle g_{B}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{r^{2}}^{R^{2}}{s^{-\frac{d}{2}}ds}=\frac{2}{d-2}\left(\frac{1}{r^{d-2}}-\frac{1}{R^{d-2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{d-2}\left(\frac{1}{r^{d-2}}-\frac{1}{(2r)^{d-2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{d-2}\right)\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{d-2}}\right)\frac{1}{r^{d-2}},

where we have used the assumption
r\leq\frac{R}{2}
in the second last inequality. Thus we have completed the proof of the lower bound.

Next, we prove the upper bound for the case
d>2
. As
p_{n}^{B}(x,y)\leq p_{n}(x,y)
, we will use using the Gaussian upper bound (
2.3
) in Proposition
2.1
(b) to obtain

\displaystyle g_{B}(x,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p^{B}_{n}(x,x)\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}(x,x)\leq 1+c_{1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}\leq c_{2},

as required in the upper bound and for
x\neq y
, we get

\displaystyle g(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{p_{n}(x,y)}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\frac{c_{1}}{n^{\frac{d}{2}}}}\exp{\left(-c_{2}\frac{r^{2}}{n}\right)}\leq c_{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{c_{1}}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{r^{2}}{s}\right)ds}.

Changing variables in the final integral using
t=\frac{s}{r^{2}}
we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{c_{1}}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{r^{2}}{s}\right)ds}=\frac{c_{1}}{r^{d-2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}}{t}\right)}{s^{\frac{d}{2}}}}ds=\frac{c_{4}}{r^{d-2}},

for some
c_{4}>0
.

Finally, we prove the upper bound for
d=2
. Splitting
g_{n}(x,y)
suitably and using
p_{n}^{B}(x,y)\leq p_{n}(x,y)
we have that there exists
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}>0
such that

\displaystyle g_{B}(x,y)

\displaystyle=p_{0}^{B}(x,y)+\sum_{n=1}^{r^{2}}{p_{n}^{B}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=r^{2}+1}^{R^{2}}{p_{n}^{B}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=R^{2}}^{\infty}{p_{n}^{B}(x,y)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{n=1}^{r^{2}}{p_{n}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=r^{2}}^{R^{2}}{p_{n}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=R^{2}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{B}>n)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{n=1}^{r^{2}}{p_{n}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=r^{2}}^{R^{2}}{p_{n}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=R^{2}}^{\infty}{c_{2}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{R}{n}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{n=1}^{r^{2}}{p_{n}(x,y)}+\sum_{n=r^{2}+1}^{R^{2}}{\frac{c_{3}}{n}}+c_{4},

where we have used (
3.12
) in third inequality and (
3.2
) in the final one.
For the third term we have the estimate

\sum_{n=r^{2}+1}^{R^{2}}{\frac{c_{1}}{n}}\leq c_{3}\log(\frac{R}{r})

and for the second term using (
2.3
) and using the bound with Riemann integral we have that there exists,
c_{5},c_{6}>0
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{r^{2}}{p_{n}(x,y)}\leq c_{5}\int_{0}^{r^{2}}{t^{-1}\exp\left(-B\frac{r^{2}}{t}\right)}dt=c_{6}\int_{0}^{1}{s^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{B}{s}\right)}ds=c_{7}.

So we get that there exists
c_{7}>0
such that

g_{B}(x,y)\leq c_{7}\log\left(\frac{R}{r}\right),

whenever
R\geq 2r
. This completes the proof for the upper bound in the case
d=2
.
∎

5
Balayage Formula

In this section we shall prove Proposition
2.3
. We begin with another classical tool, a probabilistic representation for solutions of the Dirichlet problem in a domain.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
R\geq 1,x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
D\subset B(x_{0},R),
and
\phi:\partial B(x_{0},R)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then
h:\overline{B(x_{0},R)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

h(x)=\mathbb{E}^{x}[\phi(X_{\tau_{D}})]

(5.1)

is a unique solution to

\displaystyle\Delta h=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on }D,

\displaystyle h=\phi

\displaystyle\mbox{on }\partial D.

(5.2)

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

By (
3.12
), we know that
\tau_{B(x_{0},R)}<\infty
with probability 1 and hence
\tau_{D}<\infty
with probability 1. Then using the Markov property and the one-step probability we have

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{x}[\phi(X_{\tau_{D}})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{E}^{x}[\phi(X_{\tau_{D}});X_{1}=y]

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)\mathbb{E}^{y}[\phi(X_{\tau_{D}})]

\displaystyle=Ph(x)

This along with the fact that
\mathbb{P}^{y}[\tau_{D}=0]=1
for all
y\in\partial D
implies that
h
solves (
5.2
).

We will now show uniqueness. Suppose there are two solutions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
to (
5.2
). Then
h=h_{1}-h_{2}
solves (
5.2
) with
\phi\equiv 0
. Extend
h
to be
0
on all of
\overline{B(x_{0},R)}^{c}.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)(h(x)-h(y))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)(h(x)-h(y))h(x)-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)(h(x)-h(y))h(y)

Since the terms in the summand are non-zero only for finitely many terms, we can interchange the sums and we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)(h(x)-h(y))^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)(h(x)-h(y))h(x)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}h(x)\left(\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{1}(x,y)(h(y)-h(x))\right)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}h(x)(Ph(x)-h(x))

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}h(x)\Delta h(x)

\displaystyle=0.

Thus
h\equiv 0
and
h_{1}=h_{2}
.
∎

Remark 2
.

An alternative argument for uniqueness proof is the following. Let
h
be as in the proof. The maximum principle for harmonic functions implies that the maximum and the minimum of
h
is attained on the boundary, and hence

must be zero.

We are now ready to prove Proposition
2.3

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Define
h_{A}:\bar{B}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

h_{A}(x)=\mathbb{E}^{x}[h(X_{T_{A}});T_{A}<\tau_{B}].

From the definition of
\tau_{B}
and

it is immediate that

h_{A}(x)=\mathbb{E}^{x}[1_{A}(X_{\tau_{B\setminus A}})h(X_{\tau_{B\setminus A}})]=\mathbb{E}^{x}[\phi(X_{\tau_{B\setminus A}})],

(5.3)

where

(5.4)

For
