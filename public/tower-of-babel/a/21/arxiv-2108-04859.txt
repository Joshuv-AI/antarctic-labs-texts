Deformed Explicitly Correlated Gaussians
Matthew Beutel, Alexander Ahrens, Chenhang Huang, Yasuyuki Suzuki, Kálmán Varga

Abstract
Deformed correlated Gaussian basis functions are introduced and their matrix elements are calculated. These basis functions can be used to solve problems with nonspherical potentials. One example of such potential is the dipole self-interaction term in the Pauli-Fierz Hamiltonian. Examples are presented showing the accuracy and necessity of deformed Gaussian basis functions to accurately solve light-matter coupled systems in cavity QED.

The goal of this paper is to introduce Deformed Explicitly Correlated Gaussians (DECGs).
In the DECGs, the Gaussian parameters are different in the

z

to be placed in an arbitrary position and use the position as a
variational parameter. The DECGs can be used to solve problems where the
potential is non-spherically symmetric.

We consider a system of
N

\vec{r}_{1},...\vec{r}_{N}
, where
\vec{r}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
, and charges
q_{1},...,q_{N}

We define

{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
\vdots\\
x_{N}\end{array}\right)

(1)

and
y
and
z
similarly. We also define

\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}\\

r_{2}\\
\vdots\\
r_{N}\\
r_{N+1}\\
\vdots\\
r_{2N}\\
r_{2N+1}\\
\vdots\\
r_{3N}\end{array}\right).

(2)

So in the following
\vec{a}
will be used for 3-dimensional vectors, and
a
will be used for a set of single-particle coordinates in a given direction as defined by
Eq. (
1
), and
\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}
is a three dimensional vector formed by a set of single-particle coordinates.

A simple form of DECG functions are defined as

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}A_{xx}^{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}A_{yy}^{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}}A_{zz}^{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}\right\}

(3)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}A_{xy}^{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}-\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}A_{xz}^{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}-\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}A_{yz}^{k}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}\right\},

where
A_{\alpha\beta}
are
N\times N
symmetric matrices. The scalar (inner)
product
(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}\cdot\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}})
for
N
-dimensional
vectors
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}=(a_{1},a_{2},...,a_{N})
and
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}=(b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{N})
is to be understood as

(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}\cdot\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}})=\sum_{m=1}^{N}a_{m}b_{m}
. Assuming
A_{xx}=A_{yy}=A_{zz}=A

and
A_{xy}=A_{xz}=A_{yz}=0
, one gets back the original definition of ECGs.

Now we can define the block matrix
A
as

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}A_{xx}&A_{xy}&A_{xz}\\
A_{xy}&A_{yy}&A_{yz}\\
A_{xz}&A_{yz}&A_{zz}\end{array}\right),

(4)

and the DECG function can be written as

\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A^{k}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}\right\},

(5)

where the tilde is dropped for simplicity. The superscript
k
stands for the
k
-th
basis function and

\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A^{k}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3N}r_{i}A^{k}_{ij}r_{j}.

(6)

We multiply the simple DECG by

\exp\left\{\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{3N}s_{i}r_{i}\right\},

(7)

to form a basis that can describe nonzero angular momentum states and
systems of multiple centers (molecules):

\Psi_{k}=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A^{k}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{k}\right\}.

(8)

As an example, assume that we write the trial function in the following form

\displaystyle{\rm exp}\left\{-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\alpha_{ij}^{xx}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\alpha_{ij}^{yy}(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i<j}^{N}\alpha_{ij}^{zz}(z_{i}-z_{j})^{2}\right\}

(9)

\displaystyle\times{\rm exp}\left\{-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}^{xy}(x_{i}-y_{j})^{2}-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}^{xz}(x_{i}-z_{j})^{2}-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\alpha_{ij}^{yz}(y_{i}-z_{j})^{2}-{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{i}({\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{c}_{i}})^{2}\right\}.

In this case, we have a correlation between the particle coordinates and
a single particle function centered at
\vec{c}_{i}
. The relation between the coefficients in Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
9
) is shown in Appendix

II.1
Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian of the system is

H=H_{e}+H_{ph}=H_{e}+H_{p}+H_{ep}+H_{d}.

(10)

H_{e}
is the electronic Hamiltonian, and

H_{p}
is the photon Hamiltonian. The electron-photon coupling is
denoted as
H_{ep}
, and the dipole self-interaction is
H_{d}
.
In this case the electron-photon interaction is described by using the PF nonrelativistic QED Hamiltonian.
The PF Hamiltonian can be derived

Ruggenthaler et al. 2018
;
Rokaj et al. 2018
;
Mandal et al. 2020b
;
Mandal et al. 2020a
;
Tokatly 2018
by
applying the Power-Zienau-Woolley gauge transformation
Power et al. 1959
,
with a unitary phase transformation on the minimal coupling (
p\cdot A
) Hamiltonian in the Coulomb gauge,

H_{ph}={\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{p}}\left[p_{\alpha}^{2}+\omega_{\alpha}^{2}\left(q_{\alpha}-\frac{{\vec{\lambda}_{\alpha}}}{{\omega_{\alpha}}}\cdot\vec{D}\right)^{2}\right],

(11)

where
\vec{D}
is the dipole operator. The photon fields are described by quantized oscillators.

q_{\alpha}={\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\alpha}}}}(\hat{a}^{+}_{\alpha}+\hat{a}_{\alpha})
is the displacement field and

p_{\alpha}
is the conjugate momentum.
This Hamiltonian describes
N_{p}
photon modes with frequency

\omega_{\alpha}
and coupling
\vec{\lambda}_{\alpha}
.
The coupling term is usually written as
Ruggenthaler et al. 2014

\vec{\lambda}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,S_{\alpha}(\vec{r})\vec{e}_{\alpha},

(12)

where
S_{\alpha}(\vec{r})
is the mode function at position
\vec{r}

and
\vec{e}_{\alpha}

is the transversal polarization vector of the photon modes.

The electronic Hamiltonian is the usual Coulomb Hamiltonian and the three components of the electron-photon
interaction are as follows: The photonic part is

H_{p}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{p}}\left(\frac{1}{2}p_{\alpha}^{2}+\frac{\omega_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}q_{\alpha}^{2}\right)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{p}}\omega_{\alpha}\left(\hat{a}_{\alpha}^{+}\hat{a}_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(13)

and the interaction term is

H_{ep}=-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{p}}\omega_{\alpha}q_{\alpha}\vec{\lambda}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{D}=-\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{p}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\alpha}}{2}}(\hat{a}_{\alpha}+\hat{a}_{\alpha}^{+})\vec{\lambda}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{D}.

(14)

Only photon states
|n_{\alpha}\rangle
,
|n_{\alpha}\pm 1\rangle
are connected by
\hat{a}_{\alpha}

and
\hat{a}_{\alpha}^{+}
. The matrix elements
of the dipole operator
\vec{D}
are only nonzero between spatial basis functions
with angular momentum
l
and
l\pm 1
in 3D or
m
and
m\pm 1
in 2D.

The dipole self-interaction is defined as

H_{d}={\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{p}}\left(\vec{\lambda_{\alpha}}\cdot\vec{D}\right)^{2},

(15)

and the importance of this term for the existence of a ground state is discussed in Ref.
Rokaj et al. 2018
.

In the following, we will assume that there is only one important
photon mode with frequency
\omega
and coupling
\vec{\lambda}
. Thus the suffix
\alpha
is omitted in what follows. The
formalism can be easily extended for many photon modes but here we
concentrate on calculating the matrix elements and it is sufficient to
use a single-mode.

For one photon mode Eqs. (
13
) (
14
) and (
15
) can be simplified and the Hamiltonian becomes

H=T+V+U+\omega\left(\hat{a}^{+}\hat{a}+{1\over 2}\right)+\omega\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D}q+{\frac{1}{2}}(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2},

(16)

where
T
is the kinetic operator

T=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{i}^{2}}\right).

(17)

V
is the Coulomb interaction

V=\sum_{i<j}V_{c}(\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}),\ \ \ \ \ \ V_{c}(\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j})={\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{|\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j}|}}.

(18)

U
is an external potential

U=\sum_{i=1}^{N}U(\vec{r}_{i}),

(19)

and the dipole moment
\vec{D}
of the system is defined as

\vec{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}q_{i}\vec{r}_{i}.

(20)

The operators act in real space, except
q
which acts on the photon space

\displaystyle q|n\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega}}\left(a+a^{+}\right)|n\rangle

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega}}\left(\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle+\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle\right).

Most of the matrix elements calculated previously for ECGs remain
the same except the one that uses the DECG block matrix.

II.2
Overlap matrix

The overlap matrix is given by

\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle=\int\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}d{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}},

(22)

where
A
and
\vec{s}
are defined as

A=A^{i}+A^{j}\ \ \ \ \ \vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}=\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,i}+\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,j},

(23)

and can be calculated using Eq. (
69
) in Appendix
B
:

\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle={\frac{(2\pi)^{3N/2}}{({\rm det}A)^{1/2}}}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}A^{-1}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}.

(24)

II.3
Kinetic energy

We will write the kinetic energy operator in the following form

T={\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}\Lambda{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}},

(25)

where the momentum operator is given by

p_{i}=-i\hbar{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}}\ \ \ \ \ (i=1,...,3N).

(26)

For a system of particles with masses
m_{1},...,m_{N}
,
\Lambda
is
a block diagonal matrix

\Lambda=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Lambda^{x}&0&0\\
0&\Lambda^{y}&0\\
0&0&\Lambda^{z}\end{array}\right),

(27)

where the matrix elements of the block diagonal matrix are given by

\Lambda^{\alpha}_{ij}={\frac{1}{2m_{i}}}\delta_{ij},

(28)

for systems where the external potential fixes the center of the system
(e.g. electrons in a harmonic oscillator potential, or electrons in an atom
where the mass of the nucleus is taken to be infinity). Otherwise, we have to remove the center of mass motion of the system using

\Lambda^{\alpha}_{ij}={\frac{1}{2m_{i}}}\delta_{ij}-{\frac{1}{2M}},

(29)

where
M=m_{1}+m_{2}+...m_{N}
. In principle
\Lambda^{x}
,
\Lambda^{y}
and
\Lambda^{z}

can be different if the masses of particles depend on the directions.

Taking the derivative on the right-hand side

{\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{i}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A^{j}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}=\left(-(A^{j}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}})_{i}+s_{i}\right)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A^{j}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}.

(30)

Using analogous results on the left side, the overlap with the kinetic energy operator can be given by

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{i}|T|\Psi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}(A^{i}\Lambda A^{j})\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,i}\Lambda\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,j}-\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,i}\Lambda A^{j}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}-A^{i}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}\Lambda\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,j}\right)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}d\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\rm Tr}(A^{i}\Lambda A^{k}A^{-1})-\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}\Lambda\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}\right)\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle,

where we used Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
) and define
\vec{y}
as

\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}=A^{j}A^{-1}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,i}-A^{i}A^{-1}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}^{\,j}.

(32)

II.4
Potential energy

Both
V_{c}
and
U
can be rewritten using a
\delta
function,

V_{c}(\vec{r}_{i}-\vec{r}_{j})=\int\delta(\tilde{w}^{ij}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})V_{c}(\vec{r})d\vec{r},

(33)

where
\tilde{w}^{ij}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}
is a short-hand notation for

\sum_{k=1}^{N}w^{ij}_{k}{\vec{r}}_{k}
and in this case
w^{ij}_{k}=\delta_{ik}-\delta_{jk}
. The corresponding formula for
U
is

U(\vec{r}_{i})=\int\delta(\tilde{w}^{i}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})U(\vec{r})d\vec{r},

(34)

with
w^{i}_{k}=\delta_{ik}
. This form allows us to calculate the matrix elements
for
\delta(\tilde{w}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})
for a general case without using the
particular form of the potential, and to calculate the matrix element of the
potential by integration over
{\vec{r}}
. The
\delta

(we drop the superscript
ij
and
i
of

for simplicity)

\delta(\tilde{w}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})={\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}}\int{\rm e}^{i\vec{k}(\tilde{w}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})}d{\vec{k}}.

(35)

We want to calculate the matrix elements

(36)

This can be done by defining
\vec{t}
as

\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}ik_{1}\tilde{w}\\
ik_{2}\tilde{w}\\
ik_{3}\tilde{w}\end{array}\right)+\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}.

(37)

Using Eq. (
69
), we can express the matrix element as

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{i}|\delta(\tilde{w}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})|\Psi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}}\left({\frac{(2\pi)^{3N}}{{\rm det}A}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int{\rm e}^{-i\vec{k}{\vec{r}}}{\rm exp}\Big({\frac{1}{2}}{{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}}}A^{-1}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$t$}}\Big)d{\vec{k}}

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}}\left({\frac{(2\pi)^{3N}}{{\rm det}A}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int{\rm e}^{-i\vec{k}{\vec{r}}}{\rm exp}\Big(-{\frac{1}{2}}{{\vec{k}}}B\vec{k}+{\frac{1}{2}}{{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}}A^{-1}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}+i{{\vec{k}}}\vec{b}\Big)d{\vec{k}},

where

B_{12}&B_{22}&B_{23}\\

where

The last integral can again be calculated using Eq. (

\langle\Psi_{i}|\delta(\tilde{w}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-{\vec{r}})|\Psi_{j}\rangle={\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}({\rm det}B)^{1/2}}}{\rm exp}\Big(-{\frac{1}{2}}(\vec{r}-{\vec{b}})B^{-1}(\vec{r}-\vec{b})\Big)\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle.\ \ \ \

(42)

Integrating over
\vec{r}
should give back the overlap, and using Eq. (

Dipole self-interaction

The dipole self-interaction can also be readily available using Eq.
(
71
):

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\Psi_{i}|{\frac{1}{2}}(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}|\Psi_{j}\rangle&=\int{\frac{1}{2}}(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}A\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}+{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}}{\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right\}d\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}\\
&={\frac{1}{2}}\left(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}}A^{-1}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}}+\left(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}}A^{-1}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}}\right)^{2}\right)\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle.\end{aligned}

(46)

II.7
Eliminating the dipole self-interaction

One motivation of DECG is that the dipole self-interaction
term of the Hamiltonian can be eliminated using a special choice of
DECG exponentials producing a much simpler Hamiltonian.

The dipole self-interaction term is a special quadratic form and this
quadratic form can be represented with DECG exponent. One can try to
find a suitable
\alpha
to eliminate the dipole self-interaction using
the kinetic energy operator:

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3N}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{i}^{2}}\right)\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right).

(47)

To solve this we need to evaluate the second derivative of the exponential with respect to
\vec{r}_{i}
. The first derivative with respect to
x_{i}
is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right)=2\alpha\lambda_{1}{q}_{i}(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right),

(48)

and the second derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}}\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\alpha\lambda_{1}^{2}{q}_{i}^{2}\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 4\alpha^{2}\lambda_{1}^{2}{q}_{i}^{2}(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right),

with similar expressions for
y_{i}
and
z_{i}
.
By choosing
\alpha
as

\alpha=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{q}_{i}^{2}}\,\lambda},

(50)

where
\lambda
is the magnitude of
\vec{\lambda}
,
we can express the kinetic energy operator acting on the exponential as

-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3N}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{i}^{2}}\right)\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right)=-\left({\frac{1}{4\alpha}}+{\frac{1}{2}}(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right)\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right).

(51)

This means that by multiplying the basis with the factor

\exp\left(\alpha(\vec{\lambda}\cdot\vec{D})^{2}\right),

(52)

the dipole self-interaction can be removed and the numerical solution is
much simpler. In this way, the nonspherical dipole self-interaction
is eliminated. In other words, it is built in the basis functions.
Note that the above exponential form can be recast into a DECG,
but not into an ECG. The generalization of Eq.
51
to multiphoton mode can be found in Appendix
E
.

IV
Summary

We have introduced a new variant of ECG basis functions that are suitable
for problems with nonspherical potentials. All necessary matrix elements
are calculated and numerically tested. The treatment of the Coulomb interaction is more complicated than in the conventional ECG case due to the
nonspherical integrals that appear in the interaction part. Two ways are proposed to solve this problem. One can either expand the Coulomb potential
in Gaussians and the integration becomes analytical, or use numerical integration.

We have shown that using the DECG basis the coupled light-matter equations can be efficiently solved even when the coupling and thus the dipole self-interaction term is large. This opens up the way to calculate light-matter coupled few-body systems with high accuracy in cavity QED systems.

The approach might be useful in other problems with nonspherical potentials e.g. calculation of atoms and molecules in magnetic fields.

Appendix C
Matrix elements of potentials

Analytical integration over
\vec{r}
in Eq. (
42
) for certain potentials is possible.
These are listed in this Appendix.

C.1
Gaussian potential

The Gaussian potential operator is given by

V(r)={\rm e}^{-\mu{\vec{r}}^{\,2}}.

(72)

The matrix element of the Gaussian
potential is obtained with the use of Eq. (
42
) by

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{i}|V|\Psi_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{({\rm det}B)^{1/2}}}\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle{\frac{1}{({\rm det}(B^{-1}+2\mu I))^{1/2}}}{\rm exp}\Big(-{\frac{1}{2}}{\vec{b}}B^{-1}\vec{b}+{\frac{1}{2}}(B^{-1}{\vec{b}})(B^{-1}+2\mu I)^{-1}(B^{-1}\vec{b})\Big)

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\frac{{\rm det}B^{-1}}{{\rm det}(B^{-1}+2\mu I)}}\right)^{1/2}{\rm exp}\Big(-\mu(B^{-1}{\vec{b}})(B^{-1}+2\mu I)^{-1}\vec{b}\Big)\langle\Psi_{i}|\Psi_{j}\rangle,

where substituting
\mu=0
gives back the overlap matrix as expected.

C.2
Harmonic oscillator

The harmonic oscillator operator is given by

V(r)={\vec{r}}^{\,2},

(74)

and its matrix element is given by

\langle\Psi_{i}|V|\Psi_{j}\rangle={\rm Tr}(B)+\vec{b}^{\,2}.

(75)

C.3
Coulomb Potential

Using the definition

\frac{1}{r}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,e^{-r^{2}\rho^{2}}d\rho,

(76)

we can evaluate the matrix element of the Coulomb potential

\displaystyle\langle\Psi|\frac{1}{|\vec{r_{i}}-\vec{r_{j}}|}|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,\langle\Psi|e^{-\rho^{2}(w^{ij}\vec{\bm{r}})^{2}}|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle d\rho

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}({\rm det}B)^{1/2}}\langle\Psi|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\rho\,\int\,\exp\Big(-\rho^{2}{\vec{{r}}}^{\,2}-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})B^{-1}(\vec{{r}}-\vec{b})\Big)d\vec{r}.

The integration over
\vec{r}
can be done by diagonalizing
3\times 3
matrix

B^{-1}
:

\rho^{2}{\vec{r}}^{\,2}+\frac{1}{2}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})B^{-1}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Big[(\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i}+\rho^{2})t_{i}^{2}-\mu_{i}t_{i}\Big]+\frac{1}{2}\vec{b}B^{-1}\vec{b},

(78)

where
\gamma_{i}
is the eigenvalue of
B^{-1}
and
t_{i}
is the corresponding
eigenvector and
\mu_{i}
is easily determined. Then the integral in Eq. (
77
) is

\displaystyle\int\,\exp\Big(-\rho^{2}{\vec{r}}^{\,2}-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})B^{-1}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})\Big)d\vec{r}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{b}}B^{-1}\vec{{b}}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\big(-(\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i}+\rho^{2})t_{i}^{2}+\mu_{i}t_{i}\big)dt_{i}=2^{3}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{{b}}B^{-1}\vec{{b}}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,e^{-(\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i}+\rho^{2})t_{i}^{2}}\cosh(\mu_{i}t_{i})dt_{i}.

(79)

We can perform the integration over
t_{i}
and
\rho
using

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\,e^{-a^{2}x^{2}}\cosh(bx)dx=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2a}\,e^{\frac{b^{2}}{4a^{2}}},\ \ \ a>0,

(80)

which when used to evaluate Eq. (
79
) leads to

\int\,\exp\Big(-\rho^{2}{\vec{r}}^{\,2}-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})B^{-1}(\vec{r}-\vec{{b}})\Big)d\vec{r}=\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\vec{b}B^{-1}\vec{b}}\prod_{i=1}^{3}(\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{\frac{\mu_{i}^{2}}{4(\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{i})}}.

(81)

By changing
\rho
to
t
by (see Ref.
Suzuki et al. 2008
)

\rho=\sqrt{a}\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}},

(82)

\rho
integration in Eq. (
77
) reduces to a general form

\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{3}(\rho^{2}+a_{i})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{\frac{b_{i}}{\rho^{2}+a_{i}}}d\rho=\sqrt{a}\int_{0}^{1}\prod_{i=1}^{3}\big[a_{i}+(a-a_{i})t^{2}\big]^{-\frac{1}{2}}e^{\frac{b_{i}(1-t^{2})}{a_{i}+(a-a_{i})t^{2}}}dt.

(83)

It is clear that the integral reduces to the error function when
a_{i}
is independent of
i
and
a
is set to that
common value of
a_{i}
. Even though
a_{i}
differs from each other, by choosing
a
equal to, say, the maximum
of
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
, the above integrand is a smooth function of
t
in
[0,1]
and therefore the integral can be
accurately evaluated numerically.

Appendix D
Two-particle probability

We want to calculate the probability of finding a particle in position

\vec{r}
and a second one at
\vec{r}^{\prime}
defined as

P(\vec{r},\vec{r}^{\prime})=\sum_{i<j}\langle\Psi_{i}|\delta(\tilde{w}^{i}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-\vec{r})\delta(\tilde{w}^{j}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}-\vec{r^{\prime}})|\Psi_{j}\rangle,

(84)

where
\tilde{w}^{j}
is defined for
U
in the main part. See Eq. (
34
). Using again the Fourier representation of
the
\delta

(85)

we want to evaluate the integral

(86)

We define a 3N-dimensional vector

\displaystyle(k_{1}^{j}\tilde{w}^{j},k_{2}^{j}\tilde{w}^{j},k_{3}^{j}\tilde{w}^{j})\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}\equiv\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}_{j}\vec{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}},
