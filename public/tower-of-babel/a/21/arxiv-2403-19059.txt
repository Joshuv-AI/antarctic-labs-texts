Classical simulation of non-Gaussian bosonic circuits
Beatriz Dias, Robert König

Abstract
We propose efficient classical algorithms which (strongly) simulate the action of bosonic linear optics circuits applied to superpositions of Gaussian states. Our approach relies on an augmented covariance matrix formalism to keep track of relative phases between individual terms in a linear combination. This yields an exact simulation algorithm whose runtime is polynomial in the number of modes and the size of the circuit, and quadratic in the number of terms in the superposition. We also present a faster approximate randomized algorithm whose runtime is linear in this number. Our main building blocks are a formula for the triple overlap of three Gaussian states and a fast algorithm for estimating the norm of a superposition of Gaussian states up to a multiplicative error. Our construction borrows from earlier work on simulating quantum circuits in finite-dimensional settings, including, in particular, fermionic linear optics with non-Gaussian initial states and Clifford computations with non-stabilizer initial states. It provides algorithmic access to a practically relevant family of non-Gaussian bosonic circuits.



1
Introduction

If one is to believe in Feynman’s vision
[
1
]
, understanding quantum dynamics may one day be a prime field of application of a working universal quantum computer. Without access to such a powerful computational tool, however, one has to resort to efficient, i.e., polynomial time, classical methods that can be used to
study the behavior of certain quantum many-body systems evolving e.g., under unitaries and measurement.
A difficulty here is to identify classes of quantum computations which allow for efficient classical simulation algorithms. Well-known examples include matchgate circuits
[
2
,
3
]
, Gaussian dynamics of either fermions
[
4
,
5
]
or bosons, and Clifford circuits (stabilizer computations)
[
6
]
. For each of these classes of quantum dynamics, polynomial-time simulation methods have been constructed.

For bosonic systems consisting of say
n
modes, low-order moments of the mode operators of a state (that is, expectation values of constant-degree polynomials of the quadratures) can easily be kept track of under Gaussian dynamics (in particular, Gaussian unitaries and measurement): This requires only a polynomial-time computation, and implies that (bosonic) linear optics, i.e., computations with such operations starting from an initial Gaussian state, can be simulated efficiently. This is the case for both strong simulation (where the goal is to compute the output probability for a given measurement outcome) as well as weak simulation (where the goal is to sample from the output distribution defined by measurement), assuming the classical algorithm has access to independent unit-variance centered Gaussian random variables.

While the framework of quantum linear optics is amenable to efficient classical simulation algorithms, it is not sufficient to describe a number of information-processing applications of interest. Consider for example quantum error correction. To quote from the abstract of a pioneering paper
[
7
]
,
“[…] Gaussian operations are of no use for protecting Gaussian states against Gaussian errors […]”
(see
[
8
]
for another no-go result).
It is thus necessary to augment quantum optics by non-Gaussian resource states.
For instance, displacement errors can be protected against by encoding a qubit into an (approximate) Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) code
[
9
]
.
Other proposals for protecting against displacement errors include the family of oscillator-to-oscillator codes
[
10
]
, where encoding proceeds by applying a Gaussian unitary to a bosonic mode, with a collection of auxiliary systems prepared in GKP-states. To protect against photon loss (another ubiquitous example of Gaussian errors), coherent
cat codes
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
have been developed.

What do GKP-, oscillator-to-oscillator- and coherent cat-codes have in common? Most importantly, they are experimentally feasible, and corresponding schemes have been realized in the lab. What makes this possible is the simple structure of the associated circuits: While the initial states are non-Gaussian, subsequent operations (e.g., for applying logical gates) typically only involve Gaussian unitaries. Second, analyzing their properties presents a difficult challenge for theory. In particular, unlike for Gaussian initial state, it is unclear in general how to construct classical simulation algorithms which work for non-Gaussian initial states. It is clear that the hardness, i.e., complexity of classical simulation is a function of these states.
Our goal is to quantify this, i.e., we ask: What is the computational effort required to (strongly) simulate a Gaussian quantum circuit on a non-Gaussian input state?

In an effort to go beyond this framework of linear optics, several alternative methods for simulating bosonic dynamics have been developed. For example, it has been shown that if
a circuit of Gaussian unitaries is applied to an initial state with a positive Wigner function, then it is possible to efficiently sample from the output distribution of a subsequent measurement
[
15
,
16
]
. Since Gaussian states have positive Wigner function, this constitutes a strict generalization of the covariance matrix formalism to a larger class of states. One way of putting this result is to say that Wigner function negativity is a precondition for a quantum advantage
[
17
]
.

In more recent work
[
18
]
, a different set of non-Gaussian initial states for subsequent Gaussian operations have been shown to also lead to a class of computations that can be simulated efficiently in the strong sense. The authors of Ref.
[
18
]
give an algorithm which works for initial pure states whose coefficients in the Fock basis have bounded support. Their simulation algorithms have polynomial runtime in the size of this support, as well as exponential runtime in the mean photon number of the initial state.

For GKP- and cat-codes (and more generally when dealing with infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces), perhaps the most widely used approach to numerical simulation (see e.g.,
[
19
]
) is to project onto a finite-dimensional Hilbert space (and subsequently do, e.g., state vector simulation). Concretely, e.g., in the work
[
19
]
, this
is done by replacing GKP- and cat-states
by finite Fock state superpositions
\sum_{n=0}^{N_{\max}}c_{n}|n\rangle
(with
N_{\max}
as large as manageable). While this is sufficient for the analysis of single-mode bosonic codes, it is desirable to find better approaches that may be more scalable.
In this context, the consideration of number (Fock) states also does not appear to be ideal as these have non-trivial dynamics under Gaussian operations.
In fact, GKP- and cat-states have an important commonality that should be exploited: These are all states that are superpositions of (well-understood) Gaussian states. Motivated by this, we refine our goal and ask: can the set of initial states to a Gaussian quantum circuit be extended in a different direction beyond Gaussian states, namely superpositions of Gaussian states, while maintaining efficient (strong) simulability?

3.

Measurement of

In summary, this quantum algorithm produces a sample

, from a distribution with density

\displaystyle p(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{k}}\left|\left(\langle\alpha|\otimes I^{\otimes n-k}\right),U^{(s)}\cdots U^{(1)}\Psi\rangle\right|^{2}\ ,

(2)

where we write

\mathcal{S}=\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}

with finite support in the Fock-basis and bounded photon number (with
\mathcal{S}
defined correspondingly) – as well as the direction taken here, i.e., computations starting from superpositions of Gaussian states.

2
Our contribution

Here we are interested in the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}(\chi):=\left\{\Psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes n}\ \Bigg|\ \|\Psi\|=1,\Psi=\sum_{j=1}^{\chi}c_{j}\psi_{j}\ ,\psi_{j}\in\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}\right\}

(3)

which are linear combinations of
\chi
pure Gaussian states. Our first main result is the following:

Theorem 2.1

(Exact strong simulation)
.

There is a classical algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateExactly}
which, given

(i)

a classical description of a state
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}
,

(ii)

classical descriptions of
s
Gaussian unitaries
\{U^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{s}
,

(iii)

a vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
,

computes the value
p(\alpha)
exactly in time
O(s\chi^{2}n^{3})
.

Here the runtime of the algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateExactly}
is defined as the number of elementary arithmetic operations over the reals, and we assume that we have arbitrary-precision arithmetic available. This precision is needed since we are not making any assumptions about the initial state
\Psi
(except for the fact that it is a linear combination of Gaussian states with
\chi
terms): The algorithm

\mathsf{SimulateExactly}
involves real numbers that are essentially the inverse of the energy (mean photon number) of
\Psi
. In particular, if no energy bound is imposed, these scalars can become arbitrarily small.

Imposing a mean number constraint on
\Psi
is natural from a physical perspective, and also circumvents the need for infinite-precision arithmetic. Another more important aspect is that it allows us to improve the runtime to a linear instead of quadratic scaling in
\chi
. We give a corresponding algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateApproximately}
. In contrast to
\mathsf{SimulateExactly}
, it is a probabilistic algorithm and provides only an approximation to the value
p(\alpha)
of interest.

To state its properties, it is convenient to
introduce the set of states

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}_{N}(\chi)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\Psi\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}(\chi)\ \big|\ \langle\Psi,\sum_{j=1}^{\chi}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}\Psi\rangle\leq N\right\}

(4)

which have mean photon number bounded by a constant
N
and are linear combinations of
\chi
pure Gaussian states. For
a sequence
\{U^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{s}
of Gaussian unitaries, we also need a measure of the total amount of squeezing introduced, namely

\displaystyle z_{\mathsf{tot}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{s}z^{(t)}\ .

(5)

Here we set
z^{(t)}=1
if the unitary
U^{(t)}
at time step
t
is not a single-mode squeezing operator. We note that this quantity provides the upper bound
Ne^{2z_{\mathsf{tot}}}
on the mean photon number of the final state
|\Psi_{\textrm{final}}\rangle:=U^{(s)}\cdots U^{(1)}|\Psi\rangle
, assuming that this state is centered, see
Section

5.2
. We then have the following:

Theorem 2.2

(Approximate strong simulation)
.

Let
p_{f}\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Then there is a classical probabilistic algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateApproximately}
which, given

(i)

a classical description of a state
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}_{N}(\chi)
,

(ii)

classical descriptions of
s
Gaussian unitaries
\{U^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{s}
,

(iii)

a typical vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
of measurement results,

computes the value
p(\alpha)
to within a multiplicative error
\varepsilon

with probability at least
1-p_{f}
. It has runtime bounded by
O(s\chi n^{3}Ne^{2z_{\mathsf{tot}}}/(\varepsilon^{3}p_{f}))
.

In the algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateApproximately}
, the necessary arithmetic precision is set by
\varepsilon
and the inverse of the mean photon number of the final state
|\Psi_{\textrm{final}}\rangle
.
We refer to
Section

5.2

for a thorough discussion of the notion of typicality for a measurement result
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
(see
Lemma

5.5
). It amounts to the statement that
\alpha
belongs to a set that has high probability mass under
p_{\Psi}
, namely a ball of large radius compared to the energy of
|\Psi_{\textrm{final}}\rangle
, and the normalized post-measurement state for the measurement outcome
\alpha
has a moderate mean photon number (again compared to the state
|\Psi_{\textrm{final}}\rangle
).

The computational complexity of our algorithms is tied to the (bosonic) Gaussian rank of the initial state
\Psi
, i.e., the minimal integer
\chi\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}(\chi)
. This definition is analogous to the stabilizer rank
[
20
,
20
]
, as well as to the fermionic Gaussian rank
[
21
,
22
]
. Assuming that the initial state is provided as a superposition of Gaussian states with the minimum number
\chi
of terms, the algorithms
\mathsf{SimulateExactly}
and
\mathsf{SimulateApproximately}
scale quadratically and linearly in
\chi
, respectively. Starting from this observation, one may introduce the notion of the bosonic Gaussian extent of
\Psi
, similar to the way the stabilizer
[
23
]
and fermionic Gaussian extent
[
21
,
22
,
24
]
are defined. This quantity is typically much smaller than the Gaussian rank and is a proxy for an approximate notion of Gaussian rank. The runtime of an appropriately modified version of the algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateApproximately}
scales linearly
in this quantity.

Classical strong simulation is indeed stronger than weak simulation in the sense that an algorithm for strong simulation gives an algorithm for weak simulation, provided that there is an efficient algorithm to sample from the output probability distribution associated to measurement
[
25
]
. This statement carries over to continuous variable computations by discretizing the output probability density, provided there is an efficient binning of the space of outcomes and the discretized probabilities can be computed efficiently from the output probability density and sampled from efficiently
[
18
]
.
Then, if provided, an efficient binning of the outcome space as well as efficient algorithms to compute and sample discretized probabilities from the output probability density augment our classical simulation methods in a way that allows weak simulation.

Our work mirrors earlier findings in the context of stabilizer
[
20
,
23
,
26
]
, matchgate and fermionic linear optics computations
[
21
,
24
]
. It makes an experimentally important new class of non-Gaussian circuits accessible to numerical simulation.

2.1
Technical building blocks

Our work introduces new methods for describing and working with superpositions of Gaussian states that may be of independent interest.
One of our main technical tools is a formula for
the expression

\displaystyle\tr\left(D(\alpha)\rho(\cov_{1},d_{1})\rho(\cov_{2},d_{2})\rho(\cov_{3},d_{3})\right)

(6)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
specifies a displacement operator
D(\alpha)
, and where
\rho(\Gamma,d)
denotes a Gaussian state with covariance matrix
\Gamma
and displacement vector
d
. Using this formula, which is a generalization of an expression previously derived in
[
27
]
for the case of centered Gaussian states and
\alpha
set to zero, one can compute expressions of the form

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{3},D(\alpha)\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2},\psi_{3}\rangle

(7)

for three Gaussian states
\psi_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
, in terms of their covariance matrices and displacement vectors, see
Lemma

4.4
.
Our construction then relies on using a description of Gaussian states (including their global phase) that can be combined with this formula to evaluate inner products (including phases). We have previously used such an approach in the context of non-Gaussian operations using fermions
[
21
]
.

To describe a single pure Gaussian state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}
including its phase, we augment the standard description of the corresponding density operator
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
(by its first and second moments, i.e., the covariance matrix and displacement vector) by a single scalar
r\in\mathbb{C}
: This is the overlap
r(\psi)=\langle\alpha(\psi),\psi\rangle
with a coherent state
|\alpha(\psi)\rangle

that has the same displacement as
\psi
. The latter coherent state is the one that maximizes the squared overlap with
|\psi\rangle
, that is,

\displaystyle\alpha(\psi)

\displaystyle=\arg\max\left\{|\langle\beta,\psi\rangle|^{2}\ |\ \beta\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\ .

(8)

The triple
\Delta(\psi):=(\Gamma(\psi),\alpha(\psi),r(\psi))
then is what we use as a description of a pure Gaussian state
\psi\in\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}
.

To represent a superposition
\Psi=\sum_{j=1}^{\chi}c_{j}\psi_{j}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}(\chi)
of Gaussian states, we then simply use the collection
\{c_{j},\Delta(\psi_{j})\}_{j=1}^{\chi}

of corresponding coefficients and descriptions of the individual terms in the superposition.

To construct our classical simulation algorithms, we then argue how these descriptions can be updated under Gaussian unitaries, and how measurement outcome probabilities can be computed from these descriptions. A key building block here (which ultimately leads to the
O(\chi)
-runtime of the algorithm
\mathsf{SimulateApproximately}
) is a probabilistic subroutine that estimates the norm of any vector
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}}_{N}(\chi)
in linear time in
\chi
.

3
Background

In this section, we give some background on Gaussian states and operations.
We consider the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{n}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes n}
associated with a system of
n
bosonic modes.

We introduce bosonic creation- and annihilation- operators,
\{a^{\dagger}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
and
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, which satisfy the canonical commutation relations

\displaystyle[a_{j},a_{k}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{j,k}I\qquad,\qquad[a_{j},a_{k}]=[a_{j}^{\dagger},a_{k}^{\dagger}]=0\qquad\text{for}\qquad j,k\in[n]\ .

(9)

A number state is defined as

\displaystyle|k\rangle=(a_{1}^{\dagger})^{k_{1}}\cdots(a_{n}^{\dagger})^{k_{n}}|0\rangle\qquad\text{for}\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\ ,

(10)

where
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state defined (up to a global phase) as the state annihilated by any annihilation operator, that is

\displaystyle a_{j}|0\rangle=0\qquad\text{for all}\qquad j\in[n]\ .

(11)

The set of number states
\{|k\rangle\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}}
is an orthonormal basis of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

Instead of using creation and annihilation operators, one can work with field operators
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n},P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}
which are defined as

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} Q_{j}&=(a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}\\
P_{j}&=-i(a_{j}-a_{j}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}\end{aligned}\end{gathered}\qquad\text{for}\qquad j\in[n]

and which satisfy the canonical commutation relations

\displaystyle\left[R_{j},R_{k}\right]=i\Omega_{k,l}I\ .

(13)

Here we defined the vector of field operators

\displaystyle R=(Q_{1},P_{1},\ldots,Q_{n},P_{n})

(14)

where the matrix

\displaystyle\Omega=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\

(15)

defines the symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

3.1
Gaussian states

The characteristic function
\chi_{\rho}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of a density operator
\rho
is defined as

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}(\xi)=\tr\left(\rho D(\hat{d}^{-1}(\xi))\right)\qquad\text{for}\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\ ,

(16)

with the displacement operator

\displaystyle D(\alpha)=e^{i\hat{d}(\alpha)^{T}\Omega R}\qquad\text{for}\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\ ,

(17)

where we defined the function

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \hat{d}\ :\quad\mathbb{C}^{n}\quad&\rightarrow\quad\mathbb{R}^{2n}\\
\alpha\quad&\mapsto\quad\hat{d}(\alpha)=\sqrt{2}\left(\re(\alpha_{1}),\im(\alpha_{1}),\ldots,\re(\alpha_{n}),\im(\alpha_{n})\right)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}\ .

The characteristic function
\chi_{\rho}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of a state
\rho

\rho
via the Weyl transform

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}d^{2n}\xi\,\chi_{\rho}(\xi)D(-\hat{d}^{-1}(\xi))\ .

(19)

The displacement vector

of a state
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
is the vector whose entries are the first moments of the field operators, i.e.,

\displaystyle d_{j}=\tr(\rho R_{j})\qquad\text{for}\qquad j\in[2n]\ .

(20)

The covariance matrix
\cov\in\mathsf{Mat}_{2n\times 2n}(\mathbb{R})
of a state
\rho\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
is the matrix whose entries

\displaystyle\cov_{j,k}=\tr(\rho\{R_{j}-d_{j}I,R_{k}-d_{k}I\})\qquad\text{for}\qquad j,k\in[2n]

(21)

are the second moments of the field operators.
By definition, the covariance matrix is symmetric. It satisfies

\displaystyle\Gamma+i\Omega\geq 0

(22)

which follows from
\rho\geq 0
.
We say a symmetric matrix
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Mat}_{2n\times 2n}(\mathbb{R})
is a valid covariance matrix if it satisfies
Eq.

22
.

A Gaussian state
\rho\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
is a state whose characteristic function
\chi_{\rho}
has the form

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}(\xi)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\xi^{T}\Omega^{T}\cov\Omega\xi-d^{T}i\Omega\xi\right)\qquad\text{ with }\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\ .

(23)

The pair
(\Gamma,d)
are the covariance matrix and displacement of
\rho
.
Since the characteristic function
\chi_{\rho}
of a state
\rho

\rho

We will denote by
\rho(\cov,d)

According to Williamson theorem

28

\displaystyle\Gamma=S(D\oplus D)S^{T}\ ,

denotes the group of

satisfies

. Additionally, any symplectic matrix

3.2
Gaussian unitary operations

An operation is Gaussian if and only if it maps Gaussian states to Gaussian states.
Gaussian unitary operators

\displaystyle=S\cov S^{T}\ ,

\displaystyle d^{\prime}

\displaystyle=Sd+s\ ,

is related to

by

and a vector

3.3
Coherent states

Coherent states play an important role in our algorithms and are a useful tool in many calculations. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the coherent state
|\alpha\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{n}
is the pure Gaussian state obtained by applying the displacement operation
D(-\alpha)
to the vacuum state, i.e.,

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle=D(-\alpha)|0\rangle\ .

(38)

The coherent state
|\alpha\rangle
has covariance matrix and displacement vector

\displaystyle\cov

\displaystyle=I\ ,

(39)

\displaystyle\hat{d}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}(\re(\alpha_{1}),\im(\alpha_{1}),\dots,\re(\alpha_{n}),\im(\alpha_{n}))\ .

(40)

Coherent states are eigenvalues of the annihilation operators
a_{j},j\in[n]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle a_{j}|\alpha\rangle=\alpha_{j}|\alpha\rangle\ ,

(41)

and form an overcomplete basis, satisfying the completeness relation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}d^{2n}\alpha\,|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|=I_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\ .

(42)

We note that displacement operators, phase shifts and beamsplitters take coherent states to coherent states.
More precisely, for
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle D(\beta)|\alpha\rangle=|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle\qquad\text{with}\qquad\alpha^{\prime}=\exp(i\im(\alpha^{T}\overline{\beta}))(\alpha-\beta)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\qquad\text{for all}\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\ .

(43)

For
\phi\in\mathbb{R}
and
j\in[n]
, we have

\displaystyle F_{j}(\phi)|\alpha\rangle=|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{j-1},e^{-i\phi}\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\quad\text{for all}\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

(44)

and, for
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
j,k\in[n]
,

\displaystyle B_{j,k}(\omega)|\alpha\rangle=|\alpha^{\prime}\rangle

(45)

with
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
the vector with entries

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}_{j}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{j}\cos\omega-i\alpha_{k}\sin\omega\ ,

(46)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=-i\alpha_{j}\sin\omega+\alpha_{k}\cos\omega\ ,\qquad\qquad\text{for all}\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\ .

(47)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\ell}\qquad\text{for}\qquad\ell\notin\{j,k\}\ ,

(48)

3.4

We consider a
n
-mode Gaussian state

on a bipartite system where
\mathcal{H}_{A}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{k}
includes
k

\mathcal{H}_{B}\simeq H_{n-k}
includes the remaining
n-k
modes.
The state
\rho_{AB}=\rho(V,s)
has covariance matrix

\displaystyle\cov=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{A}&\Gamma_{AB}\\
\Gamma_{AB}^{T}&\Gamma_{B}\end{pmatrix}\ ,

(49)

where
\Gamma_{A}\in\mathsf{Mat}_{2k\times 2k}(\mathbb{R})
is the covariance matrix of the reduced density operator

on subsystem
A
and
\Gamma_{B}\in\mathsf{Mat}_{2(n-k)\times 2(n-k)}(\mathbb{R})
is the covariance matrix of the reduced density operator
\rho_{B}
on subsystem
B
,
and displacement vector

\displaystyle s=(s_{A},s_{B})\ ,

(50)

where
s_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2k}
and
s_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{2(n-k)}
are the displacements of
\rho_{A}
and

A heterodyne measurement of subsystem
A
with outcome
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
corresponds to projecting the state
\rho_{AB}
onto

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}=|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\otimes I^{n-k}\ .

(51)

The probability of obtaining outcome
\alpha
is given by

\displaystyle p(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{k}}\|\Pi_{\alpha}\rho_{AB}\|^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{k}\sqrt{\det((\Gamma_{A}+I)/2)}}\exp\left(-(d(\alpha)-s_{A})^{T}(\Gamma_{A}+I)^{-1}(d(\alpha)-s_{A})\right)\ ,

(53)

and the post-measurement state is

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{AB}=\frac{1}{p(\alpha)}\Pi_{\alpha}\rho_{AB}\Pi_{\alpha}\ .

(54)

The post-measurement state

is a Gaussian state with covariance matrix (see calculation e.g. in
[
31
, Appendix B]

\displaystyle\cov^{\prime}=I\oplus\left(\Gamma_{B}-\Gamma_{AB}^{T}(\Gamma_{A}+I)^{-1}\Gamma_{AB}\right)

(55)

and displacement vector

\displaystyle d^{\prime}=(\hat{d(\alpha)},s_{B}+\Gamma_{AB}^{T}(\Gamma_{A}+\Gamma_{M})^{-1}(\hat{d}(\alpha)-s_{A}))\ .

(56)

3.5
Inner product formulas for Gaussian states

The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of density operators
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{n})
is connected with the
L^{2}
-inner product of their characteristic functions
\chi_{\rho},\chi_{\rho^{\prime}}
by Parseval’s relation

\displaystyle\tr\left(\rho^{\dagger}\rho^{\prime}\right)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}d^{2n}\xi\,\overline{\chi_{\rho}(\xi)}\chi_{\rho^{\prime}}(\xi)\ .

(57)

This allows us to compute the trace of two Gaussian states
\rho(\cov_{1},d_{1})
and
\rho(\cov_{2},d_{2})
, with
\cov_{1},\cov_{2}\in\mathsf{Mat}_{2n\times 2n}(\mathbb{R})
two covariance matrices and
d_{1},d_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. This amounts to evaluating a Gaussian integral, giving
[
27
,
32
]

\displaystyle\tr\left(\rho(\cov_{1},d_{1})\rho(\cov_{2},d_{2})\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\left((\cov_{1}+\cov_{2})/2\right)}}\exp\left(-(d_{2}-d_{1})^{T}(\cov_{1}+\cov_{2})^{-1}(d_{2}-d_{1})\right)\ .

(58)

For pure states
\rho(\cov_{1},d_{1})=|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|
and
\rho(\cov_{2},d_{2})=|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|
this formula gives the inner product squared
|\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle|^{2}
. Notice that describing Gaussian states by their covariance matrix and displacement vector, e.g., using the characteristic function given in
Eq.

23
, does not allow computing the inner product
\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\rangle
(i.e., the inner product itself, not its absolute value). This is because the covariance matrix and displacement vector are functions of the density operator
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
(recall the definitions in
Eqs.

20
and
21
), not of the state vector
|\psi\rangle
.

On the other hand, single-mode coherent states
|\alpha\rangle,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
can be written explicitly in the number state basis as

\displaystyle|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-|\alpha|^{2}/2\right)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}|j\rangle\ .

(59)

It follows that the overlap between a coherent state
|\alpha\rangle,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and the vacuum
|0\rangle
is

\displaystyle\langle 0,\alpha\rangle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}\right)\ .

(60)

More generally, the overlap between two coherent states
|\alpha_{1}\rangle
and
|\alpha_{2}\rangle
for
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is

\displaystyle\langle\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{1}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{2}|^{2}+\overline{\alpha_{1}}^{T}\alpha_{2}\right)\ .

(61)

4.1
Extended description of a Gaussian state

Here we give a description of a Gaussian state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}
which includes information about its phase.
While this phase is irrelevant when considering a single state, it will be important when considering superpositions, as relative phases are physically observable.
Our proposal is to fix the phase of the state
|\psi\rangle
relative to a reference state with which
|\psi\rangle
has non-zero overlap. We use coherent states
|\alpha\rangle,\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
as reference states. The overlap
|\langle\alpha,\psi\rangle|^{2}
is maximal when choosing as the reference state the coherent state
|\alpha\rangle
with the same displacement vector as the state
|\psi\rangle
.

Definition 4.1

(Extended description of a Gaussian state)
.

Let
|\psi\rangle\in\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}

(i)

and

|\psi\rangle
.

(ii)

, i.e.,

is the overlap of the state
|\psi\rangle
with the coherent state

that has the same displacement.

the set of all descriptions of

-mode pure Gaussian states. A description in

uniquely fixes a Gaussian state and each Gaussian state

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \psi:\quad\mathsf{Desc}_{n}\quad&\rightarrow\quad\mathrm{Gauss}_{n}\\
\Delta\quad&\mapsto\quad\psi(\Delta)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}\ .

5
Superpositions of Gaussian states and measurements

Here we establish various routines that can be used to deal with a superposition

We describe how to compute or estimate the probability density functions when applying a homodyne measurement to a superposition of Gaussian states specified by this data. We also explain how to update descriptions of such superpositions after a Gaussian unitary or measurement is applied. This relies on the routines outlined in
Section

4
for tracking relative phases in linear optics, as well as (in the case of measurements) on the norm estimation algorithms laid out in

For a more detailed explanation of this procedure, we refer readers to

where analogous

In
Section

, we sketch how to (exactly) compute the norm
\|\Psi\|
of

from the data (

). The corresponding algorithm has a runtime scaling of

5.1
Naïve norm estimation algorithm

It is straightforward to show the following:

Lemma 5.1
.

There is an algorithm
\mathsf{exactnorm}

which, on input

\left\{c_{j},\Delta(\psi_{j})\right\}_{j\in[\chi]}
, computes the norm
\|\Psi\|
of

in time

Proof.

Because

\displaystyle\|\Psi\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{\chi}\overline{c_{k}}c_{j}\langle\psi_{k},\psi_{j}\rangle\ ,

(143)

the norm

∎

5.2

Here we give an algorithm called

(144)

Central to the construction of this algorithm is the following probabilistic process, which is specified by a vector

,

and

:

(i)

Consider i.i.d. real-random variables

for

is obtained as follows: We pick

uniformly at random (according to the Lebesgue measure) and set

\displaystyle X_{j}

\displaystyle=R^{2}|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{2}\ .

(145)

(ii)

Set

\displaystyle\overline{X}:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}X_{j}\ .

(146)

Then the following holds:

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose

and

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime},H\Psi^{\prime}\rangle\leq E\qquad\textrm{ where }\qquad\Psi^{\prime}=\Psi/\|\Psi\|\ .

(147)

Let

and

Set

\displaystyle R:=\sqrt{\frac{E}{\varepsilon}}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad L:=\left\lceil\frac{E}{4\pi p_{f}\varepsilon^{3}}\right\rceil\ .

(148)

Then

\displaystyle\Pr\left[(1-\varepsilon)\|\Psi\|^{2}\leq\overline{X}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\Psi\|^{2}\right]\geq 1-p_{f}\ .

(149)

Proof.

We prove the claim for single-mode states. The generalization to
n
-mode states is straightforward.

We write the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=2(a^{\dagger}a+I)=2a^{\dagger}a

(150)

\displaystyle\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}2|\alpha|^{2}|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{2}d\alpha\ ,

(151)

see
Eq.

277
in
Appendix

Define

\displaystyle p(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{2}}{\|\Psi\|^{2}}\qquad\textrm{ for }\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\ .

(152)

Then

is a probability density function (the one associated with the outcome distribution when applying the heterodyne measurement to the normalized state
\Psi/\|\Psi\|

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle}{2\|\Psi\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha\sim p}\left[|\alpha|^{2}\right]\ .

In particular, Markov’s inequality implies that

\displaystyle\Pr_{\alpha\sim p}\left[|\alpha|^{2}\geq R^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle}{2R^{2}\|\Psi\|^{2}}\ .

(154)

Consider the random variable

defined by

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=R^{2}|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{2}\ .

(155)

The random variable

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\int_{B_{R}(0)}R^{2}|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{2}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{B_{R}(0)}|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{2}d\alpha

\displaystyle\|\Psi\|^{2}\left(1-\frac{\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle}{2R^{2}\|\Psi\|^{2}}\right)

(159)

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi R^{2}}\int_{B_{R}(0)}R^{4}|\langle\alpha,\Psi\rangle|^{4}d\alpha

\displaystyle=\pi R^{2}\|\Psi\|^{4}\int_{B_{R}(0)}p(\alpha)^{2}d\alpha

where we used

\displaystyle\var(X)

(164)

Let

be i.i.d. realizations of

\displaystyle\overline{X}:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}X_{j}\ .

(165)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\overline{X}\right]

(166)

\displaystyle=\var(X)/L\leq\pi R^{2}\|\Psi\|^{4}/L\ ,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\var(\overline{X})}{\kappa^{2}\|\Psi\|^{4}}=\frac{\pi R^{2}}{\kappa^{2}L}\ .

Since

\displaystyle\left|\overline{X}-\|\Psi\|^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\overline{X}-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}]\right|+\left|\|\Psi\|^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\overline{X}-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}]\right|+\|\Psi\|^{2}\cdot\frac{\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle}{2R^{2}\|\Psi\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|\overline{X}-\|\Psi\|^{2}|\geq\left(\varepsilon/2+\frac{\langle\Psi,H\Psi\rangle}{2R^{2}\|\Psi\|^{2}}\right)\|\Psi\|^{2}\right]

where we substituted

Thus Eq. (

Because of its central relevance for the complexity of our algorithm, let us briefly comment on how the energy (i.e., mean photon number) of the states occuring during the computation can be bounded. The corresponding bound depends on the mean photon number

of the initial superposition, as well as the unitaries applied. Indeed, one can use that these unitaries correspond to energy-limited channels.

Recall we use the Hamiltonian given in

A channel

is said to be energy-limited if it maps energy bounded states to energy bounded states
[

Gaussian channels including finite displacements, phase shifters, beamsplitters and finite squeezing are energy-limited. Concretely, we have

\displaystyle\tr(HD(\alpha)^{\dagger}\rho D(\alpha))

\displaystyle=\tr(H\rho)+d(\alpha)^{T}d(\alpha)+2s^{T}d(\alpha)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all}\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\ ,

where

is the displacement of the state
\rho
, and

\displaystyle\tr(HP_{j}(\phi)^{\dagger}\rho P_{j}(\phi))

\displaystyle=\tr(H\rho)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all}\qquad\phi\in\mathbb{R}\ ,

\displaystyle\tr(HB_{j,k}(\phi)^{\dagger}\rho B_{j,k}(\phi))

\displaystyle=\tr(H\rho)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all}\qquad\omega\in\mathbb{R}\ ,

\displaystyle\tr(HS_{j}(z)^{\dagger}\rho S_{j}(z))

\displaystyle\leq e^{2z}\tr(H\rho)

\displaystyle\qquad\text{for all}\qquad z>1\ ,

for all

.

Performing a heterodyne measurement and averaging over the measurement result amounts to the channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\rho|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha\ .

(185)

This channel is also energy-limited because we have

\displaystyle\tr(H\mathcal{E}(\rho))

\displaystyle=\tr(H\rho)+2\ .

(186)

We refer to
Appendix

for a proof of Eq. (

We note that somewhat more natural-looking expressions can be obtained for non-centered states by considering the variance

\displaystyle\var_{\rho}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\var_{\rho}(R_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\tr(R_{j}^{2}\rho)-\tr(R_{j}\rho)^{2}\right)\ .

\displaystyle=\var_{\rho^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\var_{\rho^{\prime}}\leq e^{2z}\var_{\rho}\ .

Here we describe an algorithm

which, given

of Gaussian states, computes output probabilities associated with measuring some (respectively all) modes using a heterodyne measurement.

In more detail, suppose we would like to estimate the probability density function when measuring
k\leq n
modes. Without loss of generality, let us consider the case where we measure the first
k
modes. The corresponding output distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to the Haar measure on
\mathbb{C}^{k}
, with corresponding probability density function given by

\displaystyle p_{\Psi}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi^{k}}\|\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi\|^{2}\qquad\textrm{ where }\qquad\Pi_{\alpha}=|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\otimes I^{\otimes n-k}\

(190)

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{k}

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, we have that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\chi}c_{j}\Pi_{\alpha}\psi_{j}

(191)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\chi}c^{\prime}_{j}(\alpha)\psi^{\prime}_{j}(\alpha)\qquad\textrm{ where }\qquad\begin{matrix}c^{\prime}_{j}(\alpha)&=&\kern-20.00003ptc_{j}\|\Pi_{\alpha}\psi_{j}\|\\
\psi^{\prime}_{j}(\alpha)&=&\Pi_{\alpha}\psi_{j}/\|\Pi_{\alpha}\psi_{j}\|\end{matrix}

(192)

is a superposition of
\chi
(or fewer) Gaussian states

. Furthermore, descriptions of these states and the value of the coefficients
\{c_{j}(\alpha)\}_{j=1}^{\chi}

can be computed using the algorithm
\mathsf{postmeasure}
.
Since the quantity
p_{\Psi}(\alpha)
in
Eq.

190
essentially is the squared norm of this superposition, this can then be estimated using a norm estimation routine.
We immediately obtain the following:

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
E>0
.
There are algorithms
\mathsf{measureprobexact}
and
\mathsf{measureprobapproximate}_{E}

that, given as an input
a tuple
\left\{c_{j},\Delta(\psi_{j})\right\}_{j\in[\chi]}
specifying a state

and

(i)

The algorithm
\mathsf{measureprobexact}
computes
p_{\Psi}(\alpha)

in time
O(\chi^{2}n^{3})
.

(ii)

Assume that the normalized post-measurement state

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi/\|\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi\|

(193)

satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime}_{\alpha},H\Psi^{\prime}_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq E\ ,

(194)

the output of

is a value

that satisfies

, except with probability

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\rho|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha
,
see Eq. (
186
).
Here and below we use the following consequence of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha

\displaystyle=I\ .

(276)

We have

\displaystyle aa^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha|^{2}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha\ .

(277)

Proof.

Let
\varphi,\psi
be arbitrary states. Then

\displaystyle\left\langle\varphi,aa^{\dagger}\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle a^{\dagger}\varphi,a^{\dagger}\psi\right\rangle

(278)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}d\alpha\left\langle a^{\dagger}\varphi,\alpha\right\rangle\left\langle\alpha,a^{\dagger}\psi\right\rangle

(279)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}d\alpha\left\langle\varphi,a\alpha\right\rangle\left\langle a\alpha,\psi\right\rangle

(280)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}d\alpha\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\langle\varphi,\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha,\psi\rangle\ .

(281)

∎

Consider the channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\rho|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha\ .

(282)

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\dagger}(a^{\dagger}a)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(a^{\dagger}a)

(283)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|a^{\dagger}a|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha

(284)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\alpha|^{2}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|d\alpha

(285)

\displaystyle=aa^{\dagger}\

(286)

by Eq. (
277
) and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(aa^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(a^{\dagger}a+I)

(287)

\displaystyle=aa^{\dagger}+I\ .

(288)

Thus, for
H=2(a^{\dagger}a+I)=2aa^{\dagger}
we have obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(H)

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{E}(aa^{\dagger})

(289)

\displaystyle=2(aa^{\dagger}+I)

(290)

\displaystyle=H+2I\ .

(291)

This gives the claim (
186
).
Thus
\mathcal{E}
is energy-limited.

Appendix D
On the variance of post-measurement states

We need a bound on the variance

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi/\|\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi\|}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\mathsf{Var}_{\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi/\|\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi\|}(Q_{j})+\mathsf{Var}_{\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi/\|\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi\|}(P_{j})\right)

(292)

of the post-measurement state
\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi/\|\Pi_{\alpha}\Psi\|
.
Here we argue that information on the first two moments of
\Psi
is insufficient to bound this.

Let
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
p\in[0,1]
and
z\geq 1
. We consider the family of states

\displaystyle|\Psi_{p,r,z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-p}|0\rangle|0\rangle+i\sqrt{p}|r\rangle S(z)|0\rangle\

(293)

where
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state and
|r\rangle
is the coherent state.
Then we have the following:

Lemma D.1
.

Let
z>1
be arbitrary. For
p\in(0,1)
, define
\Psi(p)=\Psi_{p,1/p,z}
.
Then

(i)

The covariance matrix
\Gamma(\Psi(p))
and displacement
d(\Psi(p))
of
\Psi(p)
satisfy

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \Gamma(\Psi(p))&\ \rightarrow\ I_{4\times 4}\\
d(\Psi(p))&\ \rightarrow\ (0,0,0,0)\end{aligned}\end{gathered}\qquad\textrm{ for }\qquad p\rightarrow 0\ .

(ii)

The normalized post-measurement state
\Psi^{\prime}(p):=\Pi_{1/p}\Psi(p)/\|\Pi_{1/p}\Psi(p)\|
for measurement outcome
1/p\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} \mathsf{Var}_{\Psi^{\prime}(p)}&\ \rightarrow\ 1+\cosh(2z)\\
\langle\Psi^{\prime}(p),H\Psi^{\prime}(p)\rangle&\ \rightarrow\ \infty\end{aligned}\end{gathered}\qquad\textrm{ for }\qquad p\rightarrow 0\ .

Since
z>1
was arbitrary, this shows that the variance and energy (mean photon number) of the post-measurement state can be arbitrarily large even though the first two moments are bounded.

Proof.

Consider the state
\Psi_{p,r,z}
defined in (
293
). For brevity, let us write

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-p}|0\rangle|\Psi_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{p}|r\rangle|\Psi_{1}\rangle\ .

(296)

Here
\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{1}

are defined by

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=|0\rangle

(297)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=iS(z)|0\rangle\ .

(298)

That is, they are normalized Gaussian states with relative phases such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Re}\langle\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=0\ .

(299)

Using
Eq.

299
and the fact that
\langle 0,r\rangle=e^{-r^{2}/2}\in\mathbb{R}
, it is straightforward to check that
\Psi
is normalized for any
p\in[0,1]
,
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\geq 1
.

For
R=(Q_{1},P_{1},Q_{2},P_{2})
and
j\in[4]
we have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi,R_{j}\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-p)\left(\langle 0|\langle\Psi_{0}|\right)R_{j}\left(|0\rangle|\Psi_{0}\rangle\right)+p\left(\langle 1|\langle\Psi_{1}|\right)R_{j}\left(|1\rangle|\Psi_{1}\rangle\right)

(300)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sqrt{p(1-p)}\mathsf{Re}\left(\left(\langle 0|\langle\Psi_{0}|\right)R_{j}\left(|1\rangle|\Psi_{1}\rangle\right)\right)

(301)

\displaystyle\rightarrow(\langle 0|\langle\Psi_{0}|)R_{j}(|0\rangle|\Psi_{0}\rangle)\qquad\textrm{ for }\qquad p\rightarrow 0\ .

(302)

We similarly have, for any
j,k\in[4]
, that

\displaystyle\langle\Psi,\left(R_{j}R_{k}+R_{k}R_{j}\right)\Psi\rangle\rightarrow(\langle 0|\langle\Psi_{0}|)(R_{j}R_{k}+R_{k}R_{j})(|0\rangle|\Psi_{0}\rangle)\qquad\textrm{ for }\qquad r\rightarrow 0\ .

(303)

Using the definition
|\Psi_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle

the claim (
i
) follows by considering the covariance matrix and displacement of the state
|0\rangle|0\rangle
.

We have

\displaystyle\Pi_{r}|\Psi\rangle

\displaystyle=|r\rangle(\sqrt{1-p}e^{-r^{2}/2}|\Psi_{0}\rangle+\sqrt{p}|\Psi_{1}\rangle)\ .

(304)

Again using (
299
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{r}|\Psi\rangle\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-p)e^{-r^{2}}+p\ .

(305)

This means that the normalized post-measurement state is

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}:=\Pi_{r}\Psi/\|\Pi_{r}\Psi\|

\displaystyle=|r\rangle\left(\alpha(p,r)|0\rangle+i\beta(p,r)S(z)|0\rangle\right)

(306)

where

\displaystyle\alpha(p,r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{1-p}e^{-r^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-r^{2}}+p}}\ ,

(307)

\displaystyle\beta(p,r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{(1-p)e^{-r^{2}}+p}}\ .

(308)

Using that both
\alpha(p,r)
and
\beta(p,r)
are real, we have as before

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime},R_{j}\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha(p,r)^{2}\left(\langle r|\langle 0|\right)R_{j}\left(|r\rangle|0\rangle\right)+\beta(p,r)^{2}\left(\langle r|\langle S(z)0|\right)R_{j}\left(|r\rangle|S(z)0\rangle\right)\ ,

(309)

and similarly for the second moments. Thus the covariance matrix displacement of
\Psi^{\prime}
are convex combinations of
|r\rangle|0\rangle
and
|r\rangle S(z)|0\rangle
, respectively, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\Psi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha(p,r)^{2}I_{4\times 4}+\beta(p,r)^{2}I_{2\times 2}\oplus\mathsf{diag}(e^{-2z},e^{2z})\ ,

(310)

\displaystyle d(\Psi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha(p,r)^{2}(\sqrt{2}r,0,0,0)+\beta(p,r)^{2}(\sqrt{2}r,0,0,0)\ ,

(311)

where we used that
\alpha(p,r)^{2}+\beta(p,r)^{2}=1
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}_{\Psi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\tr(\Gamma(\Psi^{\prime}))/2

(312)

\displaystyle=2\alpha(p,r)^{2}+\beta(p,r)^{2}(1+\cosh(2z))\ .

(313)

We also have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime},H\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\alpha(p,r)^{2}+\beta(p,r)^{2}(1+\cosh(2z))+2r^{2}+2

(314)

because

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{\prime},H\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\langle\Psi^{\prime},(Q_{j}^{2}+P_{j}^{2}+I)\Psi^{\prime}\rangle

(315)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left(\langle\Psi^{\prime},((Q_{j}-d_{Q_{j}}I)^{2}+(P_{j}-d_{P_{j}}I)^{2}+I),\Psi^{\prime}\rangle+d_{Q_{j}}^{2}+d_{P_{j}}^{2}\right)

(316)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Var}_{\Psi}^{\prime}+2\|\Psi^{\prime}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{2}(d_{Q_{j}}^{2}+d_{P_{j}}^{2})\ .

(317)

It is straightforward to check that
\lim_{p\rightarrow 0}\beta(p,1/p)=1
, hence the claim follows.
