Equitable Core Imputations via a New Adaptation of The Primal-Dual Framework
Vijay V. Vazirani

Abstract
The classic paper of Shapley and Shubik [ SS71 ] characterized the core of the assignment game. We observe that a sub-coalition consisting of one player (or a set of players from the same side of the bipartition) can make zero profit, and therefore its profit under a core imputation can be an arbitrary amount. Hence an arbitrary core imputation makes no fairness guarantee at the level of individual agents . Can this deficiency be addressed by picking a “good” core imputation? To arrive at an appropriate solution concept, we give specific criteria for picking a special core imputation, and we undertake a detailed comparison of four solution concepts. Leximin and leximax core imputations come out as clear winners; we define these to be equitable core imputations . These imputations achieve “fairness” in different ways: whereas leximin tries to make poor agents more rich, leximax tries to make rich agents less rich. We give combinatorial strongly polynomial algorithms for computing these imputations via a novel adaptation of the classical primal-dual paradigm. The “engine” driving them involves insights into core imputations obtained via complementarity. It will not be surprising if our work leads to new uses of this powerful technique. Furthermore, we expect more work on computing the leximin and leximax core imputations of other natural games, in addition to the recent follow-up work [ GGSV24 ] .

1.1
How was the Primal-Dual Framework Adapted to our Problem?

The primal-dual method has three distinguishing features:

1.

It provides a malleable, high level framework whose details can be custom-designed to the specific problem at hand.

2.

It is able to find a globally acceptable (optimal or near-optimal) solution by making only local improvements.

3.

It yields efficient, combinatorial algorithms.

This method had major successes, first in
combinatorial optimization

[
LP86
,
Sch86
]
and then in
approximation algorithms

[
Vaz01
]
. These settings involved finding an
integral
optimal (or near-optimal) solution to the primal LP, in tandem with an optimal (or near-optimal) solution to the dual LP. Complementary slackness conditions — or an appropriate relaxation of these conditions — helped transform the global task into a sequence of local tasks. Following these successes, researchers successfully adapted it to solve certain non-linear convex programs as well
[
DPSV08
,
JV08
,
Vaz12
,
DGM16
]
. Indeed, today it is appropriate to call this method the
primal-dual paradigm
.

A common thread in all these works is that the algorithm starts off with an easily-computed, suboptimal dual solution which is modified iteratively. An iteration ends when modifications are blocked; at this point a suitable integral change is made to the current primal solution to enable further modifications. When all (relaxed) complementary slackness conditions are satisfied, the algorithm terminates.

Despite its malleability, there seemed little reason to pick this method for our problem. By Lemma
3
, the leximin core imputation is unique. The closest comparison will be with the maximum weight matching problem in a bipartite graph – both problems seek an optimal, rather than near-optimal, solution. As is well known, a maximum weight matching appears at a vertex of the bipartite matching polytope, i.e., the polytope defined by the constraints of the primal LP (
1
). Therefore, the solution lies at one of finitely — though possibly exponentially — many points and a lot is known about their combinatorial properties to help design the algorithm. In contrast, at present we do not know where in the polytope of optimal dual solutions does the leximin (and leximax) core imputation lie, see Section
9
.

Computing optimal solutions to our primal and dual LPs, namely a maximum weight matching and a core imputation, is straightforward. Indeed, this is the first step in our current algorithm. Then starts the main problem, of transforming the arbitrary core imputation into a leximin or leximax imputation. However, the starting primal and dual solutions already satisfy all complementary slackness conditions, so how will the global problem be broken into local steps?

Our answer emerged after reducing the primal-dual method to its bare bone essentials: The method involves two processes which alternately make
local improvements
to two entities. Additionally, a
suitable mechanism
is devised which nudges these local improvements towards a
global solution
.

Our primal-dual algorithm for leximin core imputation works as follows: The dual update steps leximin-improve the current dual solution by altering the profits of certain vertices; however, the optimality of the dual is never tampered with. This process gets blocked when an unmatched edge is just about to go under-tight, which would lead to an infeasible dual. At this point, the primal-update step takes necessary actions so dual improvement can resume. Importantly, the set of matched edges is kept unchanged throughout. Hence, complementary slackness conditions, which held at the start of the algorithm, in fact hold throughout. Clearly, a different mechanism needs to be devised that transforms local improvements into a global solution.

The answer to this question is another novel feature of our approach: in our case, the mechanism is a
clock
that helps
synchronize
all events. Indeed, the progress made by the algorithm is a direct function of time as follows: Let the given instance be
G=(U,V,E)
with weight function
w:E\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{+}
. For each time
t
, we can identify a vertex set
W_{t}\subseteq(U\cup V)
such that in the induced subgraph
H_{t}
of
G
, the profit shares form the unique leximin core imputation for
H_{t}
. The proof of this fact crucially depends on the
correct resolution of some subtle algorithmic choices
, see Sections
7.1
and
7.3.1
. The set
W_{t}
grows with time and we prove that in polynomial time
W_{t}=(U\cup V)
, hence terminating in the required imputation.

We note that some previous primal-dual algorithms had also used the notion of time but it in a different and much less prominent manner. In
[
JV01
]
duals grow with time and this aspect is used for enforcing relaxed complementary slackness constraints; however, it is the latter which transform the global problem to a local one.
[
JV08
]
also grow duals with time. Again, it is not used for transforming the global problem to a local one, but for ensuring that the cost sharing method derived satisfies the property of cross-monotonicity.

The broad outline given above is far from sufficient for designing the entire algorithm. The latter requires a precise understanding of the way core imputations allocate the total profit to agents and teams; in particular, we need to answer the following questions:

1.

Do core imputations spread the profit more-or-less evenly or do they restrict profit to certain well-chosen agents? If the latter, what characterizes these “chosen” agents?

2.

An edge
(i,j)
in the underlying graph is called a
team
. By definition, under any core imputation, the sum of profits of
i
and
j
is at least the weight of this edge. For which teams is the sum strictly larger?

3.

How do core imputations behave in the presence of degeneracy? An assignment game is said to be
non-degenerate
if it admits a unique maximum weight matching.

Our answers, using complementary slackness conditions and strict complementarity, are provided in Section
4
.

The answer to the third question is useful for pedagogical purposes, in case the reader wishes to understand the algorithm for the case of a non-degenerate game. In this case, the underlying structure is simpler: there are no viable teams or agents. This follows from Theorems

Proof.

Definition 15
.

At any point in the algorithm, for any connected component
C
of

will denote the

\mbox{\rm left}(C)
(
\mbox{\rm right}(C)
) if there is a vertex in
\mbox{\rm left}(C)
(
\mbox{\rm right}(C)
) whose profit is
\min(C)
.

The nature of the leximin order dictates that in order to leximin-improve the current core imputation, we
must first try to increase the profit share of essential vertices having minimum profit
, see also Remark
1
. Let
C
be a fundamental component which is not a unique imputation component; we call the process

C
. Suppose

has min in
\mbox{\rm left}(C)
, see Definition
15
. Since the essential and viable edges of

is to rotate
C
clockwise, see Definition
16
. If

has min in

anti-clockwise.

Definition 16
.

Let
C
be a connected component in the tight subgraph. By

clockwise
we mean continuously increasing the profit of
\mbox{\rm left}(C)
and decreasing the profit of
\mbox{\rm right}(C)
at unit rate, and by

we mean continuously decreasing the profit of
\mbox{\rm left}(C)
and increasing the profit of
\mbox{\rm right}(C)
at unit rate.

How long can we carry out this repair? The answer is: until either

has min on both sides or a subpar edge

incident at

(
\mbox{\rm right}(C)
) edge

(

6.4
Three Key Ideas

1).
Section
6.3
, described how to leximin-improve a fundamental component. However, our task is to iteratively improve the entire core imputation. Is there an orderly way of accomplishing this? Towards this end, a central role is played by variable
\Omega
which helps
synchronize
all events, as mentioned in Section
1.1
.

\Omega
is initialized to the minimum profit share of an essential vertex under the starting core imputation,
(u,v)
. Thereafter,
\Omega
is
raised at unit rate
until the algorithm terminates. Therefore, in effect
\Omega
defines a
notion of time
and we will call it
the clock
. In a real implementation,
\Omega
is not raised continuously; instead it is increased in spurts, since at each time, it is easy to compute the next value of
\Omega
at which a significant event will take place. However, the description of the algorithm becomes much simpler if we assume that
\Omega
is raised continuously, as is done below. A particularly succinct (and attractive) description is:

\Omega
keeps increasing and the entities of
G
simply respond via rules given in Algorithm
1
.

\Omega
provides a way of synchronizing the simultaneous repair of several components. Which components? A superficial answer is: the components in the set
Active
; a more in-depth answer is provided in Section
7
using Definition
18
.

2).
Can tight subpar edges be added to
T
when they go exactly tight? If not, why not and which tight subpar edges need to be added to
T
and when?

Our algorithm adds a tight subpar edge to the tight subgraph only if it is
legitimate
, see Definition
17
. This rule is critical for ensuring correctness of the algorithm, as shown in Example
4
.

3).
Assume that a valid component,
D
, has min on both sides. If so, both clockwise and anti-clockwise rotations of
D
will lead to a worse profit allocation for its minimum profit essential vertices. Should we declare
D
fully repaired?

It turns out that only a sub-component of
D
should be declared fully repaired and identifying the right one is critical for ensuring correctness of the algorithm, as shown in Example
5
. Furthermore, analogous issues arise when other events happen in the algorithm, see Section
7.3.1
.

Theorem 8
.

Algorithm
1
runs in strongly polynomial time; in particular, in time
O(m^{2+o(1)})
.

Proof.

In Section
6.1
, we showed that the time required to classify vertices and edges requires a total time of
O(m^{2+o(1)})
. Below we will show that the rest of the steps of Algorithm
1
require time
O(mn\log n)
, using the data structure of Fibonacci Heap
[

]
, which executes operations of insert and find-min in

time, amortized. Since the first term dominates the second, the theorem follows.

We will partition a run of Algorithm
1
into

. The first epoch starts with the start of Algorithm
1
and an epoch ends when a component gets moved to
(\mbox{{Frozen}}\cap\mbox{{Fully-Repaired}})

There will be at most

entries in the Fibonacci Heap, one corresponding to each edge and each fundamental component. The key of each entry specifies the time at which it will participate in an event. For each fundamental component
C\in\mbox{{Bin}}
, there is an entry whose key is
\min(C)
, since
C
will move into

when
\Omega=\min(C)
. Once a fundamental component
C
moves into

, in Step 2(a) or 2(c)(iii), all subpar edges incident at

are inserted in the Fibonacci Heap with the appropriate key.

The minimum key in the Fibonacci Heap indicates the next event that will happen. Hence there is no need to decrease
\Omega
continuously; it can be dropped in discrete steps. When Step 2(d) is executed, all the edges participating in the cleanup are deleted from the Fibonacci Heap. These
O(m)
operations, which take
O(m\log n)
time, dominate the rest of the operations.
∎

Is there a simpler combinatorial procedure, than Algorithm
1
, for finding the leximin core imputation for the assignment game? Before answering this question, we consider the following problem: Find the set of essential vertices which are assigned the smallest profit in the leximin core imputation.

An LP-based algorithm will require ideas from
[
FRT85
]
— for identifying the set of
always-active constraints
. How about an efficient combinatorial algorithm? The only method we are aware of is to run Algorithm
1
for the first
epoch
(see Theorem
8
for a definition) and pick the set of essential vertices having the smallest profits in
V_{t}
. This leads us to believe that the answer to the question stated above is “No”.

Interestingly enough, the two principles underlying primal-dual algorithms also underlie algorithms for finding Nash equilibria for a two-player zero-sum game, namely:

1.

Finding globally optimal solutions by making only local improvements and

2.

Two processes, a minimizer and a maximizer, alternately making improvements to dual entities by responding to each other’s progress.

For the Nash equilibrium problem, the two regret minimization algorithms take local improvement steps in the direction of the gradient, and the average of the strategies played converges to the set of Nash equilibria. This is a folklore fact that traces back to at least the works by Hannan
[
Han57
]
and Foster and Vohra
[
FV97
]
; see also the recent paper
[
APFS22
]
.
