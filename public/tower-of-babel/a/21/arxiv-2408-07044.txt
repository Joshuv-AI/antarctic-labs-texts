Short curves of end-periodic mapping tori
Brandis Whitfield

Abstract

1.
Introduction

The interplay between laminations, mapping classes of surfaces and hyperbolic
3
-manifolds is central to the study of surfaces and the geometry of their mapping tori.
The geometry of a closed fibered hyperbolic
3
-manifold may be further understood by analyzing the combinatorial data of its monodromy.
While the connections between such objects are well-studied in the finite-type setting, our work examines fibered hyperbolic
3
-manifolds constructed via end-periodic homeomorphisms of infinite-type surfaces.

Let
S
be a boundaryless infinite-type surface with finitely many ends, all non-planar. We say a homeomorphism
f
of
S
is
end-periodic
if each of its ends is either
attracting
or
repelling
under
f
, and that it is
atoroidal
if it admits no periodic curve up to isotopy. Field–Kim–Leininger–Loving
[
10
, Section 6]
provide plentiful examples of such maps as the composition of pairwise commuting handle shifts with sufficiently large powers of a pseudo-Anosov map on a compact subsurface of
S
. In recent work, Field–Kent–Leininger–Loving
[
9
, Section 2]
introduce a description of the complexity of an end-periodic map by the pair
(\chi(f),\xi(f))
known as the
capacity
of f. Intuitively,
\xi(f)
measures the amount of shifting in and out of the ends and
\chi(f)
is the (absolute value of the) Euler characteristic of a compact subsurface known as a
minimal core
of the map
f
.
We refer the reader to
Section

2.5.1
for precise definitions of these terms.

The
mapping torus
of
f
is the boundaryless fibered
3
-manifold with monodromy
f
defined by the quotient

M_{f}:=S\times[0,1]/(x,1)\sim(f(x),0).

Interestingly, the end-periodic nature of the homeomorphism allows its mapping torus to admit a compactification
\overline{M_{f}}
as a
3
-manifold with boundary (see e.g.
Section

2.6
). The structure of the homeomorphism’s action on curves determine topological properties of the manifold. In particular, if
f
is atoroidal, then
M_{f}
is atoroidal and admits a hyperbolic structure by Thurston’s Hyperbolization Theorem for manifolds with boundary (see
[
10
, Proposition 3.4]
as stated for irreducible end-periodic maps). In general, when an irreducible manifold with incompressible boundary
M
satisfies
\chi(\partial M)<0
, if its interior admits a hyperbolic structure, then it admits a plethora of hyperbolic structures parameterized by the deformation space
\text{AH}(M)
(see
[
13
, Theorem 8.4]
).

In unpublished work carried out during the 1990s, Handel and Miller developed a lamination theory for end-periodic maps introducing the
positive
and
negative Handel–Miller laminations

\Lambda^{+}
and
\Lambda^{-}
of
S
. There are many parallels between the Handel–Miller laminations of an end-periodic homeomorphism and the unstable and stable measured laminations
\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}
of a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism of a closed surface (see
[
16
, Section 8]
,
[
5
, Section 4]
). From the perspective of a compact subsurface
Y\subset S
, the leaves of the positive (resp. negative) Handel–Miller lamination project to
\pi_{Y}(\Lambda^{\pm})
, a finite (possibly empty), disjoint collection of arcs in the arc and curve complex
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
of
Y
. From there we may consider the quantity
d_{Y}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-})
which measures the distance between the respective subsurface projections
\pi_{Y}(\Lambda^{+})
and
\pi_{Y}(\Lambda^{-})
in
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
.

Given a compact essential subsurface
Y\subset S\subset M_{f}
and a hyperbolic structure
s\in\AH(M_{f})
, we consider
\ell_{s}(\partial Y)
, the total length of the geodesic representative of the multicurve
\partial Y
in
(M_{f},s)
.

Theorem A
.

For any
D,\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K=K(D,\varepsilon)
such that for any atoroidal end-periodic homeomorphism
f:S\to S
with
\chi(f),\xi(f)\leq D
, there exists
s\in\AH(M_{f})
satisfying the following.
For any compact essential subsurface
Y\subset S\subset M_{f}
, we have:

d_{Y}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-})\geq K\,\implies\,\ell_{s}(\partial Y)\leq\varepsilon.

We note that the proofs of our main theorems begin with an arbitrary atoroidal end-periodic homeomorphism
f
and produce a hyperbolic metric
s\in\AH(M_{f})
satisfying the implication of
Theorem

A
. In particular, although the constant
K
depends on
\varepsilon
and
D
, the metric
s
can actually be chosen independently of
D
and
\varepsilon
. We hope such a statement, with constants controlled only by the capacity of the homeomorphism, holds for any hyperbolic metric in
\AH(M_{f})
.

When
\overline{M_{f}}
is additionally known to be acylindrical, equivalently when
f
is
strongly irreducible
(see
Theorem

2.16
), there exists a unique metric
s^{*}\in\AH(M_{f})
whose convex core has totally geodesic boundary
[
19
, Section 4]
. For a strongly irreducible homeomorphism
f
, let
s^{*}
denote this unique structure. We wish to strengthen our main theorem so that
s^{*}
realizes the bounds of
Theorem

A
in the following way:

Conjecture 1.1
.

For any
D,\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K=K(D,\varepsilon)
such that for any strongly irreducible end-periodic homeomorphism
f:S\to S
with
\chi(f),\xi(f)\leq D
and any compact essential subsurface
Y\subset S\subset M_{f}
, we have:

d_{Y}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-})\geq K\,\implies\,\ell_{s^{*}}(\partial Y)\leq\varepsilon.

Such a result would gain control over the totally geodesic surfaces which arise as boundaries of compact irreducible acylindrical hyperbolic manifolds with boundary. The next theorem confirms the conjecture when
\partial M
is restricted to consist solely of genus-
2
surfaces.

Theorem B
.

For any
D,\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K=K(D,\varepsilon)
satisfying the following: suppose that
f:S\to S
is a strongly irreducible end-periodic homeomorphism such that
\partial\overline{M_{f}}
consists solely of genus-
2
surfaces and
\chi(f),\xi(f)\leq D
; then for any compact essential subsurface
Y\subset S

\subset M_{f}
, we have:

d_{Y}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-})\geq K\,\implies\,\ell_{s^{*}}(\partial Y)\leq\varepsilon.

Results in
Section

8
confirm that this phenomenon is not restricted to the genus-
2
setting as one may pass to covers
M_{f^{n}}\to M_{f}
.

1.1.
Totally geodesic boundary structures

Let
\Sigma
be a closed surface and let
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
denote its moduli space, the space of unmarked hyperbolic structures on
\Sigma
. Let
\SI(\Sigma)
denote the collection
of strongly irreducible homeomorphisms
f
with
\Sigma\subset\partial\overline{M_{f}}
, considered over all boundaryless infinite-type surfaces with finitely many ends, all non-planar.

When
f
is strongly irreducible, we let
(\overline{M_{f}},s^{*}_{f})
denote its acylindrical compactified mapping torus adorned with its unique totally geodesic boundary structure. Let
\dS(\Sigma)\subset\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
denote the collection of unmarked totally geodesic boundary metrics recovered over all
f\in\SI(\Sigma)
, i.e.:

\dS(\Sigma):=\{(\Sigma,s^{*}_{f})\,|\,f\in\SI(\Sigma)\}.

Question 1.2
.

Suppose
\SI(\Sigma)
is non-empty. Is
\dS(\Sigma)
dense in
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
?

Question

1.2
is inspired by work of Fujii–Soma
[
11
]
who show that
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)
, the space of totally geodesic boundary structures represented by acylindrical manifolds with boundary homeomorphic to
\Sigma
, is dense in
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
. We also take inspiration from work of Kent
[
14
]
who conjectures that such a statement is true for the collection of knot complements with totally geodesic boundary.

Question 1.3

(Kent)
.

Suppose
\SI(\Sigma)
is non-empty. Are the systoles of
\dS(\Sigma)
arbitrarily small?

Example

8.7
gives a positive answer to
Question

1.3
. Our results mirror her main result in
[
14
]
which produces arbitrarily short curves of totally geodesic boundaries of knot complements. Namely, we show for every
\varepsilon>0
,
g\geq 2
,
\dS(\Sigma_{g})
escapes each
\varepsilon
-thick part of
\mathcal{M}(\Sigma)
. We note that this construction can be extended so that
\partial\overline{M_{f}}
is the union of an even number of connected genus-
g
surfaces
\Sigma^{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\Sigma^{2n}
.

Finally, our work in the world of infinite-type surfaces culminates in a result about closed totally geodesic surfaces in closed fibered hyperbolic
3
-manifolds.

Theorem C
.

For each
g\geq 2
,
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a closed fibered hyperbolic manifold
N=N(g,\varepsilon)
satisfying the following.
N
admits a totally geodesic embedding of a genus-
g
hyperbolic surface
\Sigma_{g}
whose systole length is at most
\varepsilon
.
Further,
\Sigma_{g}
can be made (almost) transverse to a circular pseudo-Anosov flow on
N
.

A positive answer to
Question

1.2
would show that up to arbitrarily small deformations, every hyperbolic surface can be realized simultaneously within a closed hyperbolic
3
-manifold as an embedded totally geodesic surface and depth-zero leaf of a depth-one foliation. We wonder if tools such as the doubling construction of Landry–Minsky–Taylor
[
16
]
can be carefully adapted to yield an isometric embedding of any
(\overline{M_{f}},s^{*})
into a fibered hyperbolic manifold so that the boundary is (almost) transverse to the circular pseudo-Anosov flow. This would allow us to conclude that any hyperbolic surface, up to arbitrarily small deformation, appears as a totally geodesic surface of some fibered hyperbolic manifold.

1.2.
Motivation from the finite-type setting

Theorems A and B are directly inspired by Theorem B of Minsky
[
23
]
which formed one implication of his
Bounded Geometry Theorem
, a part of the program developed to resolve the
Ending Lamination Conjecture
(now the
Ending Lamination Theorem

[
4
]
). The Bounded Geometry Theorem establishes a combinatorial condition using the manifold’s ending laminations to detect when a Kleinian surface group has bounded injectivity radius, a setting in which the Ending Lamination Conjecture was first resolved
[
22
]
.

As reflected in the statement of
Theorem

A
, Minsky’s theorem relates the subsurface projection distance
d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})
between the unstable and stable laminations of a pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism to the length of the geodesic representative of
\partial Y
in the hyperbolic mapping torus of the pseudo-Anosov homeomorphism. In this setting
\lambda^{+}
and
\lambda^{-}
are precisely the ending laminations of the cover corresponding to the given fiber of the hyperbolic manifold. We rephrase the theorem in this special setting
1
1

1

This follows from Theorem B of
[
23
]
by considering
(F\times\mathbb{R},\widetilde{s})
, the infinite-cyclic cover of
(N_{\phi},s)
corresponding to the fiber, with its hyperbolic metric
\widetilde{s}
given by the pullback of
s
under the covering map. In this setting, the ending laminations of
(F\times\mathbb{R},\widetilde{s})
are precisely the unstable and stable laminations
\lambda^{+}
,
\lambda^{-}
. Minsky observes that for the Bers constant
L_{0}
, the collection of curves
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\widetilde{s},L_{0})\subset\mathcal{N}_{1}(\pi_{Y}(\lambda^{+})\cup\pi_{Y}(\lambda^{-}))
. In other words,
d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\asymp\diam_{\mathcal{C}(Y)}(\mathcal{C}_{0}(\widetilde{s},L_{0}))
, up to a negligible constant.
:

Theorem 1.4

(Minsky
[
23
]
)
.

Given
F
, a closed connected genus-
g
surface, and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K=K(g,\varepsilon)>0
so that if
N
is a hyperbolic manifold fibered by
F
with pseudo-Anosov monodromy
\phi
and corresponding unstable and stable laminations
\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-}
, then:

d_{Y}(\lambda^{+},\lambda^{-})\geq K\,\implies\,\ell_{N}(\partial Y)\leq\varepsilon,

where

is any essential subsurface of

.

We use this theorem both as motivation and a tool to extend its result to the setting of infinite-type surfaces and their atoroidal end-periodic homeomorphisms. The constant

in

depends only on
\varepsilon
and the topological complexity of

; i.e. for a fixed

and fixed genus, the constant is independent of the choice of pseudo-Anosov monodromy, and hence the hyperbolic metric on

. Rather than depending on any notion of “complexity” of the surface

, the constant

and the aforementioned capacity of

.

2.
Background, terminology and notation

2.1.
Surfaces and subsurfaces

In this subsection, let
X
be a hyperbolic surface with compact (possibly empty) boundary and finitely many ends, all non-planar. We discuss the underlying topology of the surface. All surfaces in this work are orientable. When
X
is of finite type we let
g(X)
denote its number of genus and
b(X)
its number of boundary components. We let
\chi(X)=2-2g(X)-b(X)
be its Euler characteristic and
\xi(X)=3g(X)+b(X)-3
its complexity. When the surface is disconnected its Euler characteristic and complexity are simply the sums of the Euler characteristics and complexities of each connected component. All finite-type surfaces within this work satisfy
\chi(X)\leq 0
so that for any subsurface
Y\subset X
,
|\chi(Y)|\leq|\chi(X)|
.

We consider isotopy classes of essential, simple, closed loops and proper isotopy classes of essential, properly embedded, simple paths on
X
. Recall that a path or loop is
simple
if it is an embedding of the circle or closed interval, and
essential
if it is non-peripheral and non-nullhomotopic in the surface. We use the terms
arcs
and
curves
to refer to the proper isotopy classes of essential paths and loops, and reserve the terms
paths
and
loops
for a particular (smooth) embedding of the closed interval or circle. We refer to a disjoint union of loops as a
multiloop
and a disjoint union of distinct curves as a
multicurve
.

Let
\mathcal{AC}(X)
denote the
arc and curve graph
of
X
, the simplicial graph whose vertex set
\mathcal{AC}^{(0)}(X)
consists of arcs and curves of
X
with edges between vertices whose representatives realize the minimum intersection number on the surface (outside of sporadic cases, this corresponds to disjointness).
The graph
\mathcal{AC}(X)
is a metric space endowed with the path metric
d_{\mathcal{AC}(X)}
in which all edges have unit length. The
curve graph

\mathcal{C}(X)
is the induced subgraph of
\mathcal{AC}(X)
whose vertex set is the collection of curves of
X
. When
X
is of finite type both
\mathcal{AC}(X)
and
\mathcal{C}(X)
have infinite diameter and are quasi-isometric to one another
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]

[
15
, Theorem 1.3]
. We refer the reader to
[
20
]
and
[
26
]
for more information about this graph and its (coarse) geometry.
Given a compact annulus
Y
, the definition of
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
requires more care. We define
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
as the graph whose vertices are essential arcs from one end of
Y
to the other, modulo isotopies
relative to the endpoints
in
\partial Y
, again with edges between vertices which admit disjoint representatives.

We say a subset
Y\subset X
is an
essential subsurface
if it is compact, connected, not homeomorphic to a pair of pants and its boundary is a disjoint collection of essential loops. We let
X\bbslash Y
denote the metric closure of
X-Y
, typically embedded as the complement of a sufficiently small tubular neighborhood
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\partial Y)
. Mapping classes of
Y
are represented by elements of
\Homeo^{+}(Y,\partial Y)
, the set of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms which restrict to the identity on
\partial Y
. We naturally embed
\Map(Y)
, the mapping class group of
Y
, into
\Map(X)
by extending a representative of a mapping class of
Y
to be the identity on
X-Y
. When
Y
is compact we refer to a mapping class
g\in\Map(Y)
as
fully supported
if
g
acts loxodromically on
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
. When
Y
is an annulus each non-trivial element (i.e. some power of a Dehn twist) of
\Map(Y)
is fully supported. For non-annular surfaces, fully supported mapping classes are
pseudo-Anosov
. In fact, Masur–Minsky prove the following:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
20
, Lemma 3.1]
, c.f.
[
18
, Theorem 2.10]
)
.

Let
Y
be a compact connected surface with
\chi(Y)\leq 0
. Then there exists a constant
c=c(Y)
, depending only on the topological type of
Y
, so that for any fully supported
g\in\Map(Y)
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{AC}^{(0)}(Y)
we have:

c|n|\leq d_{\mathcal{AC}(Y)}(\alpha,g^{n}(\alpha)).

Let
\tau_{Y}(g)
denote the
stable translation length
of
g
on
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
, i.e.:

\tau_{Y}(g):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{d_{\mathcal{AC}(Y)}(\alpha,g^{n}(\alpha))}{n}.

Recall that the definition is independent of the choice of
\alpha
and that
\tau_{Y}(g)\leq\inf d_{Y}(\alpha,g(\alpha))
, where the infimum is taken over all
\alpha\in\mathcal{AC}^{(0)}(Y)
.
It follows from the theorem above that when
g\in\Map(Y)
is fully supported,
\tau_{Y}(g)\geq c
.

2.2.
Subsurface projections

Following
[
21
]
, given an essential
Y\subset X
we define the
subsurface projection map

\pi_{Y}:\mathcal{AC}(X)\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{AC}(Y))

from
\mathcal{AC}(X)
to
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{AC}(Y))
, the power set of
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
, by the following.
Consider the cover
p:{\widetilde{X}_{Y}}\to X
corresponding to the subgroup
\pi_{1}(Y)\leq\pi_{1}(X)
and its compactification
X_{Y}=\overline{\mathbb{H}^{2}}/\pi_{1}(Y)
homeomorphic to
Y
. Given any representative
\alpha\in\mathcal{AC}^{(0)}(X)
, its lift
p^{-1}(\alpha)\subset{Y}
is a disjoint collection of paths and loops, and we define
\pi_{Y}(\alpha)
to be the collection of components of
p^{-1}(\alpha)
which represent (essential) arcs and curves of
X_{Y}
. Whenever the projection is non-empty,
\diam_{\mathcal{AC}(Y)}(\pi_{Y}(\alpha))\leq 1
. This provides a coarsely well-defined map from
\mathcal{AC}(X)\to\mathcal{AC}(Y)\cup\{\emptyset\}
. Note that we may realize
\alpha
and
\partial Y
in minimal position by considering their geodesic representatives
\alpha^{*}
and
\partial Y^{*}
(see
Figure

1
). When
Y
is a non-annular subsurface of
X
this produces a subsurface
Y^{*}
with totally geodesic boundary
\partial Y^{*}
. In this setting,
\pi_{Y}(\alpha)
is the set of vertices in
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
comprising of each component of
Y^{*}\cap\alpha^{*}
which represents an essential curve or arc in
Y^{*}
.

Figure 1.
The lift of a path in
X
to an annular cover
p:\widetilde{X}_{Y}\to X
. While the preimage of the path consists of many arcs, only one represents a vertex of
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
.

We will say that a subset
A\subset\mathcal{AC}(X)
and
Y
are
disjoint
if
\pi_{Y}(A)=\emptyset
, and that they
meet
otherwise. If
A
is disjoint from
Y
we will typically choose representative loops and paths which realize it as such. We note that for any
A\subset\mathcal{AC}(X)
and isotopic surfaces
Y
,
Y^{\prime}
, we have that
Y
meets
A
if and only if
Y^{\prime}
meets
A
.

Given subsets
A,B\subset\mathcal{AC}(X)
, we define their
subsurface projection distance in
Y
as:

d_{Y}(A,B):=\text{diam}_{\mathcal{AC}(Y)}(\pi_{Y}(A)\cup\pi_{Y}(B)).

Naturally, if
A
is disjoint from
Y
, but
B
meets
Y
, then
d_{Y}(A,B)=\diam(\pi_{Y}(B))
. For convenience, if both
A
and
B
are disjoint from
Y
, we set
d_{Y}(A,B)=0
. We list a few observations about subsurface projection distances which will be helpful for calculations.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
A=A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be subsets of
\mathcal{AC}(X)
with
\pi_{Y}(A_{i})\neq\emptyset
.

(i)

\displaystyle d_{Y}(A_{1},A_{n})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}d_{Y}(A_{i},A_{i+1}).

(ii)

For any
h\in\Map(X)
,
d_{Y}(A_{1},A_{2})=d_{h(Y)}(h(A_{1}),h(A_{2}))
. So, if
h
is supported on
Y
, then
d_{Y}(A_{1},A_{2})=d_{Y}(h(A_{1}),h(A_{2}))
.

(iii)

If
h
is supported on
Y
then for any subset
A\subset\mathcal{AC}(Y)
with
\pi_{Y}(h(A))\neq\emptyset
,

\tau_{Y}(h)\leq d_{Y}(A,h(A)).

(iv)

For subsets
A^{\prime}_{i}\subset A_{i}
,

d_{Y}(A^{\prime}_{1},A^{\prime}_{2})\leq d_{Y}(A_{1},A_{2}).

(v)

If there exists a subsurface
W
for which
\pi_{W}(A)
,
\pi_{W}(\partial Y)\neq\emptyset
and
d_{W}(A,\partial Y)\geq 2
, then
\pi_{Y}(A)\neq\emptyset
.

Note that
\pi_{Y}(\alpha)\neq\emptyset
does not imply
\pi_{Y}(h(\alpha))\neq\emptyset
for all
h\in\Map(X)
. In light of this, we apply (v) to ensure the subsurface projections are non-empty during relevant calculations.

2.3.
Foliations and laminations of surfaces

A
geodesic lamination

\Lambda
of
X
is a closed subset of
X
which is the union of complete simple geodesics known as
leaves
of the geodesic lamination. We are mostly concerned with the underlying topology of
\Lambda
rather than any local structure, so we will simply refer to a closed subset of
X
as a lamination if it is topologically equivalent to a geodesic lamination.

Given
\mathcal{G}
, a disjoint union of complete geodesics on
X
, we let
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
denote its closure as a subset in
X
; its closure
\overline{\mathcal{G}}
is (the support of) a geodesic lamination of
X
(see
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
).

For an essential subsurface
Y
and geodesic lamination
\Lambda
of
X
, we may again lift the lamination to
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{Y}\subset\widetilde{X}_{Y}
and define
\pi_{Y}(\Lambda)
to be the (possibly empty) collection of vertices of
\mathcal{AC}(Y)
which represent (essential) arcs and curves of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{Y}
. We similarly say that a lamination
\Lambda
and subsurface
Y

meet
if
\pi_{Y}(\Lambda)\neq\emptyset
and that they are disjoint otherwise. Notice this convention essentially ignores leaves of the lamination which spiral around some closed geodesic, as any such arc is non-proper. This will not be a problem for the Handel–Miller laminations (see
Lemma

2.7
).

2.3.1.
From foliations to laminations

A singular foliation
\mathcal{F}
of a surface is a decomposition of
X
into
1
-manifolds known as
leaves
and a finite collection
\mathcal{P}
of
singularities
so that the collection
\mathcal{F}-{\mathcal{P}}
forms a foliation of
X-\mathcal{P}
. A leaf which is disjoint from the singular collection
\mathcal{P}
is a
regular
leaf, while one which meets
\mathcal{P}
is called
singular
. For a singular foliation, a
leaf line
of
\mathcal{F}
is either a regular leaf of
\mathcal{F}
or a bi-infinite line formed by a union of at least two singular leaves so that any pair of leaves which meet at a singularity are adjacent. A closed subset
\Lambda
of
\mathcal{F}
formed by the union of leaf lines of
\mathcal{F}
is known as a
(singular) sublamination
of
\mathcal{F}
. We use the same terminology of leaf, leaf line, and singular leaf to refer to the leaves of the sublamination.

A Reebless singular foliation
\mathcal{F}
on a hyperbolic surface
X
has a corresponding lamination
\Lambda({\mathcal{F}})\subset X
obtained by the following process of “straightening” leaf lines of
\mathcal{F}

[
17
, Theorem 1]
. The lifts of each leaf line of
\mathcal{F}
form a collection of disjoint bi-infinite quasi-geodesics of

which defines a subset

of
\partial^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{2})

\mathbb{H}^{2}
. For each point

{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
which descends to a lamination

of

For an essential subsurface

. Similarly, if
\Lambda

to be the union of

We will later refer to singular

-dimensional foliations of

-manifolds, but only those obtained as the suspension of a singular foliation of a surface. For such singular foliations, the

2.4.
End-periodic homeomorphisms

From this section onward, let
S
be a boundaryless infinite-type surface with finitely many ends. We denote by
\Ends(S)
the finite set of ends of
S
. We refer the reader to the survey paper of Aramayona–Vlamis
[
1
]
for further exposition of surfaces of this type.
A homeomorphism
f
of
S
is
end-periodic
if for each end
\epsilon\in\text{Ends}(S)
there exists
p\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
U_{\epsilon}
, a connected neighborhood of
\epsilon
with compact essential frontier, so that the end
\epsilon
is either
attracting
or
repelling
under
f
in the following sense:

•

(
\epsilon
is an
attracting
end):
f^{p}(U_{\epsilon})\subset U_{\epsilon}
is a proper inclusion and the collection
\{f^{pn}(U_{\epsilon})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
forms a neighborhood basis of
\epsilon
.

•

(
\epsilon
is a
repelling
end):
f^{-p}(U_{\epsilon})\subset U_{\epsilon}
is a proper inclusion and the collection
\{f^{pn}(U_{\epsilon})\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
forms a neighborhood basis of
\epsilon
.

Following terminology of Field–Kim–Leininger–Loving
[
10
]
, we refer to a neighborhood
U_{\epsilon}
which realizes
\epsilon
as an attracting or repelling end as a
nesting neighborhood
of
\epsilon
. The action of
f
partitions
\Ends(S)
into the disjoint union
\Ends^{+}(S)\,\sqcup\,\Ends^{-}(S)
, where
\Ends^{+}(S)
denotes the set of attracting ends and
\Ends^{-}(S)
denotes the set of repelling ends.
\Ends^{\pm}(S)
further decomposes into a collection of
end orbits

\mathcal{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{O}_{k}
. The period of an end
\epsilon
is the cardinality of its end orbit; or equivalently, it is the minimal positive
p
for which
f^{p}(\epsilon)=\epsilon
. We will use
+
(respectively
-
) to denote an attracting (resp. repelling) end, one of its neighborhoods, or a curve in such a neighborhood. We typically order the ends in an attracting orbit so that
\epsilon_{i}=f^{i}(\epsilon_{0})
and in a repelling orbit so that
\epsilon_{i}=f^{-i}(\epsilon_{0})
.

As in
[
8
]
, we organize nesting neighborhoods of
\Ends^{\pm}(S)
and define a positive (resp. negative)
ladder
of
f
as a neighborhood of
\Ends^{\pm}(S)
of the form

U_{+}=\bigcup_{\epsilon\in\Ends^{+}(S)}U_{\epsilon}\quad\text{ and }\quad U_{-}=\bigcup_{\epsilon\in\Ends^{-}(S)}U_{\epsilon},

where
\{U_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in\Ends^{\pm}(S)}
are pairwise disjoint and each
U_{\epsilon}
is a nesting neighborhood of
\epsilon
. We say a positive (resp. negative) ladder
U_{\pm}
is
tight
if
f^{\pm}(U_{\pm})\subsetneq U_{\pm}
is a proper inclusion. Observe that each end-periodic map admits a tight ladder. To see this, let
U
be an attracting nesting neighborhood of an end of period
p
. Choose a collection
j_{1},\ldots,j_{p-1}
of distinct separating loops of
U\bbslash f^{p}(U)
so that each separates
\partial U
from
\partial f^{p}(U)
and that
j_{i+1}
is contained in the non-compact component of
U_{p}\bbslash j_{i}
. One can verify the union
U_{0}=U\cup f^{-1}(U_{1})\cup f^{-2}(U_{2})\ldots\cup f^{-(p-1)}(U_{p-1})
properly contains its image. Here,
U_{i}
is the component of
U-j_{i}
which is a nesting neighborhood of
\epsilon
under
f
.

We use ladders to define the
positive escaping set
and
negative escaping set
of
f
respectively:

\mathcal{U}_{+}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{n}(U_{+})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathcal{U}_{-}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{n}(U_{-}).

We also refer to the
escaping set of an end

\epsilon
of period
p
by:

\mathcal{U}_{\epsilon}=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{pn}(U_{\epsilon}),

where
U_{\epsilon}
is a nesting neighborhood of
\epsilon
. From the definition of end-periodicity one can verify that these definitions are independent of the choice of
U_{\pm}
(or
U_{\epsilon}
). As the name implies, the escaping sets form the collection of points whose positive (resp. negative) iterates under
f
eventually land in an attracting (resp. repelling) nesting neighborhood, hence “escape” the end.

We say that a loop is
positively
(resp.
negatively
)
escaping
under
f
if it is contained in the positive (resp. negative) escaping set of
f
. A curve is escaping under
f
if some loop in its homotopy class is. The positive and negative ladders themselves are surfaces of infinite type, thus there are uncountably many distinct curves which escape into the positive or negative ends under iteration of the map
f
.

Lemma 2.3

(Field–Kim–Leininger–Loving
[
10
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

Let
f
be an end-periodic homeomorphism. Then
f
acts freely, properly discontinuously and cocompactly on
\mathcal{U}_{\pm}
.
The quotient of the action
\mathcal{U}_{\pm}/\langle f\rangle
defines the following surfaces

\Sigma_{+}:=\mathcal{U}_{+}/\langle f\rangle\quad\text{ and }\qquad\Sigma_{-}:=\mathcal{U}_{-}/\langle f\rangle.

Further, the surfaces satisfy
\chi(\Sigma_{+})=\chi(\Sigma_{-})
.

Observe that the components of
\Sigma_{+}\cup\Sigma_{-}
are in bijection with the end orbits of
f
.

2.4.1.
Types of end-periodic homeomorphisms

A loop or line
\alpha
is
periodic
under
f
if there exists
m\neq 0
s.t.
f^{m}(\alpha)
is properly isotopic to
\alpha
. We say a loop or line
\alpha
is
reducing
if
\alpha\subset\mathcal{U}_{+}\,\cap\,\mathcal{U}_{-}
, in other words, if
\alpha
is both positively and negatively escaping. We say a curve or arc is periodic or reducible if one of its representatives is.

An end-periodic homeomorphism is

if it admits no periodic curves. As the name implies, an atoroidal end-periodic homeomorphism yields an atoroidal mapping torus
M_{f}
(see
Theorem

2.16
). Field–Kim–Leininger–Loving introduce
strongly irreducible
end-periodic homeomorphisms, which are atoroidal end-perioidic homeomorphisms which admit no periodic arc nor reducing curve. We direct the reader to
Section

8
for more examples of end-periodic homeomorphisms, including those arising from a construction of Field–Kim–Leininger–Loving.

2.4.2.
Juncture orbits

We say that an essential multiloop in
\mathcal{U}_{+}
(resp.
\mathcal{U}_{-}
) is a positive (resp. negative)
juncture
of
f
if it is the boundary of a tight
positive (resp. negative) ladder. This is not an isotopy-invariant property; a multiloop need not bound a tight ladder of a given homeomorphism just because another loop in its homotopy class does. In light of this, we emphasize that we are often concerned with the genuine representative loop rather than its isotopy class.

Fix a positive jucture
j_{+}
and negative juncture
j_{-}
of
f
. We define the
positive juncture orbit

J_{+}
of
j_{+}
and
negative juncture orbit

J_{-}
of

as the multiloops

J_{+}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{k}({j_{+}})\quad\text{ and }\quad J_{-}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{k}({j_{-}}).

To verify that these unions are indeed multiloops, consider a tight ladder
U_{+}
bounded by
j_{+}
. It follows that
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},f^{k}(U_{+})\subset U_{+}
is a proper inclusion, which implies that each component of
f^{i}(j_{+})
is either disjoint from or coincides with some component of
f^{k}(j_{+})
for all
i,k

\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Consider the partial juncture orbits

\displaystyle J_{+}^{\leq k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\leq k}f^{i}(j_{+})

\displaystyle J_{+}^{\geq k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\geq k}f^{i}(j_{+})

\displaystyle J_{-}^{\geq k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\geq k}f^{i}(j_{-})

\displaystyle J_{-}^{\leq k}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i\leq k}f^{i}(j_{-}).

The partial juncture orbits

and

are both closed subsets of

While the juncture orbits are somewhat uninteresting in the ends of

2.5.
Handel–Miller laminations

Fix a hyperbolic metric
X
on

Let

be the union of geodesic representatives for each curve in

\Lambda^{+}=\overline{\mathcal{J}_{-}}-{\mathcal{J}_{-}}

When non-empty, it is helpful to consider the Hausdorff distance between the junctures and the respective laminations. Let

denote the geodesic representative of the multiloop

and let

\mathcal{J}_{+}^{K}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda^{-})
and
\Lambda^{-}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{J}_{+}^{K})
(likewise,
\mathcal{J}_{-}^{K}\subset N_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda^{+})
and
\Lambda^{+}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{J}_{-}^{K})

Observation 2.5

For any positive juncture orbit, its geodesic multicurve
\mathcal{J}_{+}
is disjoint from the negative Handel–Miller lamination

. Likewise, for any negative juncture orbit, its geodesic multicurve

is disjoint from the positive Handel–Miller lamination

.

Furthermore, for any

The Handel–Miller laminations detect escaping curves in the following sense:

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose
f
is atoroidal and let
\gamma
be a curve of
S
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\gamma
is positively (resp. negatively) escaping under
f
.

(ii)

\gamma
meets only finitely many components of any positive juncture orbit
J_{+}
(resp. negative juncture orbit
J_{-}
).

(iii)

\gamma
is disjoint from the negative Handel–Miller lamination
\Lambda^{-}
(resp. positive Handel–Miller lamination
\Lambda^{+}
).

Proof.

We prove the statement for a representative of a positively escaping curve as the proof for a negatively escaping curve is analogous. Consider a representative
\gamma\subset\mathcal{U}_{+}
and let
J_{+}=\bigcup f^{k}(j_{+})
be any positive juncture orbit of
f
. We are pleased if
\gamma
is disjoint from
J_{+}
and otherwise we can isotope the representative and juncture orbits so that
\gamma
meets
J_{+}
transversely and has finite intersection with each component of
J_{+}
. If a sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
J_{+}\cap\gamma
converges then it must eventually be contained in a single component of
J_{+}
. This implies that
J_{+}\cap\gamma
must be finite, as otherwise would produce an infinite intersection between
\gamma
and some component of
J_{+}
. It follows that there are only finitely many
i
for which
f^{i}(j_{+})
meet
\gamma
. So,
\gamma
is disjoint from
J_{+}^{\leq k}
for sufficiently negative
k
and therefore also from the limiting lamination
\Lambda^{-}
. This shows
(i)\implies(ii)\implies(iii)
.

We now show that
(iii)\implies(i)
, assuming
\Lambda^{-}
is non-empty (otherwise, the statement is vacuously true and all curves are positively escaping). If
\gamma
is disjoint from
\Lambda^{-}
, then there exists
\varepsilon>0
for which a representative of
\gamma
is disjoint from
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}(\Lambda^{-})
. Thus, for some
k_{0}
we have that
J_{+}^{\leq k_{0}}
is disjoint from
\gamma
.
Each
f^{i}(j_{+})
defines a positive ladder
U_{i}
and we argue that
\gamma
must be contained in one of them. Suppose otherwise and let
C=S-(U_{+}\sqcup U_{-})
be a core containing
\gamma
so that
\partial_{+}C\subset J_{+}
. For all

,
f^{k}(\gamma)
is disjoint from both
U_{-}
(by tightness and the nesting property of
f
) and
U_{+}
(by our assumption). So for all such
k
,
f^{k}(\gamma)
is contained in the core.
Since the space of geodesic laminations on
C
is compact, some subsequence of
\{(f^{k}(\gamma))^{*}\}_{k\geq 0}
converges to an
f
-invariant (up to isotopy) geodesic lamination
\lambda_{\gamma}
.
Let
Y_{\gamma}\subset C
be the minimal surface with geodesic boundary containing
\lambda_{\gamma}
. Then
Y_{\gamma}
must also be
f
-invariant as its supporting lamination is. This implies the finite boundary curves of
Y_{\gamma}
are permuted by
f
, contradicting that

is atoroidal.
Thus,
\gamma
must be contained in some positive ladder.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any essential subsurface

,

(1)

\pi_{Y}(\Lambda^{\pm})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{AC}(Y))
is either empty or a diameter-

(2)

If

(3)

The following are equivalent:

(ii)

\Lambda^{+}
and
\Lambda^{-}
meet
\partial Y

(iii)

.

Proof.

The first conclusion follows immediately as the leaves of a lamination are pairwise disjoint.
Recall that
\pi_{Y}(\Lambda^{\pm})
consists of the essential components of the lift
\widetilde{\Lambda}^{\pm}_{Y}
of
\Lambda^{\pm}
under the covering map

. Consider the convex core
\widetilde{C}_{Y}
of

Now suppose

2.6.1.

We say a positive or negative juncture

We let

2.6.2.

Corollary 2.14
.

Suppose that

∎

of

Here we identify

∎

\partial^{2}\left({\Lambda^{\pm}_{\flat}}\right)=\partial^{2}\left({\Lambda^{\pm}}\right).

3.1

6.2.
Good homeomorphisms

To find a homeomorphism

for which

is atoroidal, it suffices to verify that

does not identify the boundaries of any two essential annuli of

. For each component

denote the collection of curves which comprise of the boundaries of essential annuli of

which meet

is empty if

is acylindrical.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

be a component-wise homeomorphism of
\partial M
for which

in
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)
for each component
\Sigma\subset\partial M
. Then

is atoroidal.

Proof.

Suppose

is an essential torus of

. Since
M
is atoroidal, the torus must meet
\partial M
. By a strengthening of a theorem of Roussarie–Thurston (see
[

, Theorem 2.9]
), we may isotope

so that the torus is transverse to
\mathcal{F}\subset N
. With a further isotopy we can remove any disks in

and assume

\partial M
in an essential

-manifold

. Namely

, the torus cut along the multicurve, is the disjoint union of compact essential annuli.

If

is strongly irreducible its mapping torus is acylindrical, thus, no such annulus exists and

is atoroidal. Otherwise, let

be a component of
\Gamma
and consider the annuli

and

whose boundaries meet at

we see curves

, and
\alpha^{\downarrow}\subset M^{\downarrow}
, both bounding the respective annuli, which were identified by

. This contradicts the stated assumption.
∎

The JSJ decomposition for manifolds with boundary provides

, a (possibly empty) disjoint collection of essential annuli, so that any essential annulus of

is homotopic into a component of

[

or

.

We say

setwise and acts by

(see Farb–Margalit

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose that

is a hyperelliptic involution homeomorphism about an oriented loop

Then for each

We can always find sufficiently small

and annular neighborhoods

of

Before proving Theorems

and

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
h:\partial M\to\partial M
be a homeomorphism such that

is hyperbolic. Then there exists a hyperbolic metric
s\in\AH(M_{f})
and an isometric immersion
\iota:(M_{f},s)\to N
such that for each simple closed curve
\alpha\subset M
,
\ell_{s}(\alpha)=\ell_{N}(\iota(\alpha))
.

Proof.

Consider the inclusion
i:M\hookrightarrow N
, and let
\pi:N_{\pi_{1}(M)}\to N
be the cover of
N
corresponding to
i_{*}(\pi_{1}(M))\leq\pi_{1}(N)\leq\Isom^{+}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
. Naturally,
N_{\pi_{1}(M)}
is homeomorphic to
M_{f}
. Let
s
be the metric corresponding to the pullback of the metric on
N
under
\pi:M_{f}\to N
. This gives an isometric immersion
\pi:(M_{f},s)\to N
. For each curve
\alpha\subset N
and each component
\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}\subset\widetilde{\alpha}=\pi^{-1}(\alpha)
, the restriction
\pi|_{\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}}:\widetilde{\alpha}_{0}\to\alpha
is degree-one. Thus,
\ell_{s}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{0})=\ell_{N}(\alpha)
. ∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose that
f
is strongly irreducible and let

be its convex-hyperbolic compactified mapping torus with totally geodesic boundary. Let

be the

-double of

Then

isometrically embeds and there is an isometric immersion

so that for each simple closed curve

,
\ell_{s^{*}}(\alpha)=\ell_{N}(\iota(\alpha))
.

Proof.

The first claim is immediate by our assumptions. As above, we consider the cover

corresponding to

, where

is the pullback metric from the hyperbolic structure on

induced by the gluing. For concreteness, we check that

. Since

is a convex totally geodesic submanifold of

, its lift

is a

-invariant convex subset of

whose boundary consists of totally geodesic embeddings of

. The convex core of

is

, the above conditions are satisfied with ease by extending the core
C
and taking sufficiently large powers of any pseudo-Anosov
g
as needed.

Consider a family of atoroidal end-periodic homeomorphisms of the form
f_{n}=\rho g^{n}
, constructed as above for
n\neq 0
where
g\in\Homeo(C,\partial C)
is a representative of a pseudo-Anosov mapping class. It follows that each
f_{n}
has the same end complexity, and since
C
remains a core of each, its core characteristic is bounded above by
\chi(C)
. Let
\Lambda^{+}_{n}
,
\Lambda^{-}_{n}
denote the Handel–Miller laminations of
f_{n}
. The following proposition shows that
d_{C}(\Lambda^{+}_{n},\Lambda^{-}_{n})\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
\rho
be an end-periodic homeomorphism with juncture orbits
P_{+}
,
P_{-}
. Let
Y=\bigsqcup Y_{i}
be the union of finitely many connected essential subsurfaces. Let
g\in\Homeo(Y,\partial Y)
be so that
g|_{Y_{i}}
represents a fully supported mapping class on each connected component
Y_{i}\subset Y
. Consider the end-periodic homeomorphism
f=\rho g
with its Handel–Miller laminations
\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-}
.
Suppose that:

(1)

f
is not a translation; and

(2)

each
Y_{i}
meets
\Lambda^{+}
,
\Lambda^{-}
,
P^{+}
and
P^{-}
.

Then,

\tau_{Y_{i}}(g)-d_{Y_{i}}(P_{+},P_{-})-2\leq d_{Y_{i}}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-}).

Recall that
\tau_{Y_{i}}(g)
is the stable translation length of
g
in
\mathcal{AC}(Y_{i})
, and that any fully supported mapping class has positive translation length with
\tau(g^{n})=|n\tau(g)|
. In the special case when
\rho
is a translation, one may take
P_{+}=P_{-}
so that
d_{Y_{i}}(P_{+},P_{-})=0
.

Proof.

We work in one component
Y_{i}
at a time. Let
\alpha,\eta
be a pair of positive and negative junctures in
P_{+}
,
P_{-}
respectively so that
\rho^{-1}(\alpha)
and
\rho(\eta)
meet
Y_{i}
, but for
k\geq 0
,
\rho^{k}(\alpha)
and
\rho^{-k}(\eta)
are disjoint from
Y_{i}
(this exists by the compactness of
Y
, see the related
Lemma

5.3
). It follows that
\alpha
and
\eta
define juncture orbits
J_{+}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{k}(\alpha)
and
J_{-}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f^{k}(\eta)
of
f
. Recall from
Lemma

5.2
that
d_{Y_{i}}(J_{+},J_{-})-2\leq d_{Y_{i}}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-})
.

\displaystyle\tau_{Y_{i}}(g)=\tau_{Y_{i}}(g^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y_{i}}(g^{-1}p^{-1}(\alpha),p^{-1}(\alpha))

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y_{i}}(g^{-1}p^{-1}(\alpha),\rho(\eta))+d_{Y_{i}}(\rho(\eta),\rho^{-1}(\alpha)).

We observe from the properties above that
\pi_{Y_{i}}(\rho(\eta))=\pi_{Y_{i}}(\rho g(\eta))=\pi_{Y_{i}}(f(\eta))
.
So, continuing the calculation from the previous line, we have:

\displaystyle=d_{Y_{i}}(f^{-1}(\alpha),f(\eta))+d_{Y_{i}}(\rho(\eta),\rho^{-1}\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y_{i}}(J_{+},J_{-})+d_{Y_{i}}(P_{+},P_{-})

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y_{i}}(\Lambda^{+},\Lambda^{-})+2+d_{Y_{i}}(P_{+},P_{-}).

This concludes the proof.
∎

8.4.
Short non-peripheral curves

The Handel–Miller laminations and juncture orbits give an obstruction to a curve being peripheral in the manifold. Recall that a loop
\gamma\subset S
is positively (resp. negatively) escaping with respect to
f
if and only if it is contained in
\mathcal{U_{+}}
(resp.

. The following remark follows from

2.6

Remark 8.4
.

Let
\alpha
be a curve in

. If there is a subsurface

, then
\alpha
is not negatively escaping. Similarly,
d_{W}(\Lambda^{-},\alpha)\geq 2
implies

is not positively escaping.

Example 8.5

(Short non-peripheral curves)
.

Let
L
be the ladder surface and suppose
Y
is the disjoint union of essential subsurfaces
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
f=f_{\varepsilon}
, a strongly irreducible end-periodic homeomorphism, and associated mapping torus
M_{f}
so that:

(1)

no component of
\partial Y
is peripheral in
\overline{M_{f}}
; and

(2)

the corresponding structure on
(M_{f},s^{*})
whose convex core has totally geodesic boundary satisfies

\ell_{s^{*}}(\partial Y)\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

We will first construct an auxiliary end-periodic map
f_{0}
for which
\Gamma=\partial Y
is non-escaping.
Let
\rho
be the translation with fundamental domains
C_{i}
depicted in
Figure

7
. We direct the reader to
Remark

2.2
to remind them of a few facts which we will use for subsurface projection calculations throughout this argument and the rest of the section.

Let
C^{\prime}
be a union of consecutive

which contains

as an essential subsurface. We extend

to

so that
\pi_{C}(\rho(\Gamma))
,
\pi_{C}(\rho^{-1}(\Gamma))\neq\emptyset
and the multicurves
\rho(\partial_{-}C)
,
\rho^{-1}(\partial_{+}C)
, and

are mutually disjoint and contained in
C
.
By considering sufficiently large powers of some pseudo-Anosov mapping class on
C
, we choose
h\in\Homeo(C,\partial C)
such that:

(i)

d_{C}(h\rho(\partial_{-}C),\rho(\partial_{-}C))\geq 9
.

Note that this implies:

(ii)

d_{C}(h\rho(\partial_{-}C),\rho^{-1}(\Gamma))\geq 7
and

(iii)

d_{C}(h^{-1}(\Gamma),\Gamma)\geq 7
.

From the conditions above and
Proposition

8.2
, it follows that
f_{0}=\rho h
is strongly irreducible. Observe that
C
must meet both
\Lambda_{0}^{+}
and
\Lambda_{0}^{-}
, the Handel–Miller laminations of
f_{0}
, by
Corollary

2.8
; otherwise some component of
\partial C
is reducing. With a direct application of
Remark

8.4
, we see that a component of
\Gamma
is neither negatively nor positively escaping under the strongly irreducible map
f_{0}
, since its Handel–Miller laminations satisfy:

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{\rho C}(\Lambda_{0}^{+},\Gamma)&\geq d_{\rho C}(f_{0}^{2}(\partial_{-}C),\Gamma)-1\\
&=d_{\rho C}(\rho h\rho(\partial_{-}C),\Gamma)-1\\
&\geq 6,\\
\end{split}

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{C}(\Lambda_{0}^{-},\Gamma)&\geq d_{C}(f_{0}^{-1}(\partial_{+}C),\Gamma)-1\\
&\geq d_{C}(\Gamma,h^{-1}(\Gamma))-d_{C}(h^{-1}(\Gamma),h^{-1}\rho^{-1}(\partial_{+}C))-1\\
&\geq 5.\end{split}

For each component
Y_{i}
of
Y
, let
g_{i}\in\Homeo(Y_{i},\partial Y_{i})
be a representative of a fully supported mapping class. We let
g=\prod g_{i}
be the composition over all components. Consider
f_{n}=\rho hg^{n}=f_{0}g^{n}
with its associated Handel–Miller laminations
\Lambda^{+}_{n}
,
\Lambda^{-}_{n}
. Recall that each
f_{n}
has bounded capacity and that
g
preserves
\partial_{\pm}C
. For sufficiently large n, it follows that
f_{n}=\rho hg^{n}
satisfies the conditions of
Proposition

8.2
, hence is a strongly irreducible homeomorphism. Our goal is to apply
Proposition

8.3
to
f_{0}
and
g^{n}
to show that
d_{Y_{i}}(\Lambda^{+}_{n},\Lambda^{-}_{n})
is arbitrarily large for each
Y_{i}\subset Y
.

Let
\eta=\partial_{-}C
and
\alpha=\partial_{+}C
. Note that
\alpha
,
\eta
are positive and negative junctures of
f_{n}
for all
n
. The equations
eq.

1
and
eq.

2
with the conditions above on
h
ensure that every component of
\Gamma
meets both
f_{0}^{-1}(\alpha)
and
f_{0}^{2}(\eta)
(recall that
\Gamma\subset\rho(C)
). Thus the
f_{0}
-juncture orbits for
\alpha
and
\eta
,

P^{+}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{0}^{k}(\alpha),\quad P^{-}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{0}^{k}(\eta),

both meet
Y_{i}
for all
i
(see
Remark

2.2
). In the following calculations we use the same logic to confirm that
\Lambda_{n}^{+}
and
\Lambda_{n}^{-}
meet
Y_{i}
, this verifies that
\Gamma
is neither positively nor negatively escaping under any
f_{n}
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{C}(\Lambda_{n}^{-},\Gamma)&\geq d_{C}(f^{-1}_{n}(\alpha),\Gamma)-1\geq d_{C}(g^{-n}h^{-1}\rho^{-1}(\alpha),\Gamma)-1\\
&\geq d_{C}(f_{0}^{-1}(\alpha),\Gamma)-1\geq 5.\end{split}

For this calculation, let
W=\rho h(C)
. Recall that
g
and
h
are supported on
Y
and
C
respectively, that
\rho(\eta)
and
\rho^{-1}(\alpha)
are both disjoint from
\Gamma
and that

.

\displaystyle d_{W}(\Lambda_{n}^{+},\Gamma)

\displaystyle\geq d_{W}(f_{n}^{2}(\eta),\Gamma)-1

\displaystyle=d_{W}(\rho hg^{n}\rho hg^{n}(\eta),\Gamma)-1=d_{W}(\rho hg^{n}\rho(\eta),\Gamma)-1

\displaystyle=d_{C}(g^{n}\rho(\eta),h^{-1}\rho^{-1}(\Gamma))-1

\displaystyle\geq-1-d_{C}(g^{n}\rho(\eta),g^{n}(\Gamma))+d_{C}(g^{n}(\Gamma),h^{-1}(\Gamma))-d_{C}(h^{-1}(\Gamma),h^{-1}\rho^{-1}(\Gamma))

So,

is an end-periodic homeomorphism whose juncture orbits

,
P^{-}
meet

for each component

. For each

both meet

. By

\tau_{Y_{i}}(g^{n})-d_{Y_{i}}(P^{+},P^{-})-2\leq d_{Y_{i}}(\Lambda^{+}_{n},\Lambda^{-}_{n}).

Now, since

be the constant from

8.5.

Our results in this section mirror Kent’s main result in
[

]
which produces arbitrarily short curves of totally geodesic boundaries of knot complements. Let
\Sigma^{+}=\Sigma^{+}_{g}
and

each be a closed surface of genus

Suppose that

Setup

We use

, the core of

.

Remark 8.6

(Some bookkeeping)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,

be the corresponding constant from

There is a power

Example 8.7

(Thin totally geodesic structures)

For every

,
