Continual Learning in Linear Classification on Separable Data
Itay Evron, Edward Moroshko, Gon Buzaglo, Maroun Khriesh, Badea Marjieh, Nathan Srebro, Daniel Soudry

Abstract
We analyze continual learning on a sequence of separable linear classification tasks with binary labels. We show theoretically that learning with weak regularization reduces to solving a sequential max-margin problem, corresponding to a special case of the Projection Onto Convex Sets (POCS) framework. We then develop upper bounds on the forgetting and other quantities of interest under various settings with recurring tasks, including cyclic and random orderings of tasks. We discuss several practical implications to popular training practices like regularization scheduling and weighting. We point out several theoretical differences between our continual classification setting and a recently studied continual regression setting.

1
Introduction

Continual learning deals with learning settings where distributions, or tasks, change over time,
breaking traditional i.i.d. assumptions.
While models trained sequentially are expected to accumulate knowledge and improve over time, practically they suffer from

catastrophic forgetting

(
McCloskey & Cohen 1989
;
Goodfellow et al. 2013
)
,

i.e.,
their performance on previously seen tasks deteriorates over time.

Much research in continual learning has focused on heuristic approaches to remedying forgetting.
Recent approaches achieve impressive empirical performance, but often require storing examples from previous tasks
(
e.g.,
Robins 1995
;
Rolnick et al. 2019
), iteratively expanding the learned models
(
e.g.,
Yoon et al. 2018
),
or lessening the plasticity of these models and harming their performance on new tasks
(
e.g.,
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
).

We theoretically study the continual learning of a linear classification model on separable data with binary classes.
Even though this is a fundamental setup to consider, there are still very few analytic results on it,
since most of the continual learning theory thus far has focused on regression settings
(
e.g.,
Bennani et al. 2020
;
Doan et al. 2021
;
Asanuma et al. 2021
;
Lee et al. 2021
;
Evron et al. 2022
;
Goldfarb & Hand 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
).
Even in the broader deep learning scope, theoreticians often start from very simple models and use them to gain insight into phenomena arising in more practical models
(
e.g.,
Belkin et al. 2018
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
).

Our paper reveals a surprising algorithmic bias of linear classifiers trained continually to minimize the exponential loss on separable data with weak regularization.
Specifically, we prove that the weights converge in the same direction as the iterates of a Sequential Max-Margin scheme.
This creates a bridge between the popular regularization methods
for continual learning

(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
;
Zenke et al. 2017
)

and the well-studied POCS framework – Projections Onto Convex Sets (also known as the convex feasibility problem or successive projections).

Our results complement those of a recent paper

(
Evron et al. 2022
)

that analyzed the worst-case performance of continual linear regression.
That paper showed that continually learning linear regression tasks with vanilla SGD,
implicitly performs sequential projections onto closed
subspaces
,
and connected that regime to the area of Alternating Projections
(
Von Neumann 1949
;
Halperin 1962
)
.
In our paper, we draw comparisons between our continual
classification
setting and their continual
regression
setting
(summarized in App.
A
).
We point out inherent differences between these two settings, emphasizing the need for a proper and thorough analytical understanding dedicated to continual
classification
settings.

Our Contributions

Our analysis reveals the following:

•

Explicit regularization methods for continual learning of linear classification models are linked to the framework of Projections Onto Convex Sets (POCS).

•

Each learned task
brings the learner
closer
to an “offline” feasible solution solving

all
tasks.
However, there exist task sequences for which the learner stays arbitrarily far from offline feasibility, even after infinitely many tasks.

•

When tasks recur cyclically or randomly, the learner converges to an offline solution with linear rates.

•

If we converge to an offline solution, it may not be the minimum-norm solution (in contrast to continual regression), but it still needs to be
2
-optimal (minimal).

•

Scheduling the regularization strength endangers convergence to an offline solution and optimality guarantees.

•

Using popular regularization weighting schemes based on Fisher-information matrices,
does not necessarily prevent forgetting
(in contrast to continual regression).

•

Early stopping (without regularization) does
not
yield the same solutions as weak regularization (unlike in stationary settings with a single task).

Setting

We consider
T\geq 2
binary classification tasks.
Each task
m=1,\dots,T

is defined by
a dataset
S_{m}

D

i.e.,
each tuple is

(\mathbf{x},y)\!\in\!{\,\mathbb{R}^{D}\!\times\!\left\{-1,+1\right\}\,}
,
for a finite

Notation.

Throughout the paper, we denote the (isotropic) Euclidean norm of vectors by
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|
,
and the weighted norm by

\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|_{\mathbf{B}}\triangleq\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}
,
for some
\mathbf{B}\succ\mathbf{0}_{D\times D}
.
We denote the set of natural numbers starting from
1
by
{\mathbb{N}^{+}\triangleq\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}}

and the natural numbers from
1
to
n
by
\left[{n}\right]
.
We define the distance of a vector

from a closed set

{{d}(\mathbf{w},\mathcal{C})\triangleq\min_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{v}}\right\|}
.
Finally, we denote the maximal norm of any data point by
{R\triangleq\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\max_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}}\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|}
.

Our main assumption in this paper is that the tasks are jointly-separable,

i.e.,
they can be perfectly fitted simultaneously, as in “offline” non-continual settings.
This can be formally stated as follows.

Assumption 2.1

(Separability)
.

Each task
m\in\left[{T}\right]
is separable,

i.e.,
it has a
non-empty
feasible set
defined as

\mathcal{W}_{m}\,\!\triangleq\!\,\left\{\mathbf{w}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D}\mid y\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\geq 1,~\forall(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}\right\}\,.

Moreover, the
T
tasks are
jointly
-separable —
there exists a non-empty offline
feasible set:

\mathcal{W}^{\star}\,\!\triangleq\!\,\mathcal{W}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{W}_{T}\neq\emptyset\,.

A similar assumption was made in the continual regression setting
(
Evron et al. 2022
)
.
It is a reasonable assumption in overparameterized regimes,
where feasible offline solutions often
do
exist.
Practically, this is commonly the case in modern deep networks.
Theoretically, with sufficient overparameterization
(
Du et al. 2019
)
or high enough margin
(
Ji & Telgarsky 2020
)
,
it is often easy to converge to a zero-loss solution.

To facilitate our results and discussions,
we specifically define the minimum-norm offline solution.
This solution is traditionally linked to good generalization.

Definition 2.2

(Minimum-norm offline solution)
.

We denote the offline solution with the minimal norm by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}^{\star}\triangleq{\argmin}_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{w}}\right\|\,.\end{aligned}

Figures
1(a)
and
1(b)
illustrate our definitions.
Notice how both the feasible set
\mathcal{W}_{m}
of each task and the offline feasible set
\mathcal{W}^{\star}
are closed, convex, and affine polyhedral cones.

Sequential Max-Margin Projections

Now, we turn to exploit the connection we have established between the weakly-regularized continual
Scheme
1

and the Sequential Max-Margin Scheme
2
.
Using tools from existing literature on Projections Onto Convex Sets (POCS), we gain valuable insights into
the dynamics of training
models continually.
Specifically, we derive optimality guarantees and convergence bounds in several interesting settings.

4.1
Quantities of Interest

We have three quantities of interest.
While the first two are widely used in the POCS literature,
the latter is specific to our continual classification setting.

Definition 4.1

(Quantities of interest)
.

Let
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be the
t
th
iterate,
obtained while continually learning
T
jointly-separable tasks.
The following quantities are of interest:

1.

Distance to the offline feasible set:

{d}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)

2.

Maximum dist. to
any
feasible set:

\displaystyle\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\!{d}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)

3.

Forgetting:

We define the forgetting of a previously-seen task
{m\in\left[{t}\right]}
as the maximal squared hinge loss on
any
sample
(\mathbf{x},y)
of the task.
More formally,

F_{m}(\mathbf{w}_{t})\triangleq{\max}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}}\!\left(\max\left\{0,\,1-y\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\right\}\right)^{2}\,.

Throughout our paper,
we
analyze both the maximal and the average forgetting,

i.e.,
\max_{m\in\left[{t}\right]}F_{m}(\mathbf{w}_{t})

and

\tfrac{1}{t}\sum_{m=1}^{t}F_{m}(\mathbf{w}_{t})
.

Explaining our forgetting.

Previous works on continual linear
regression
defined forgetting using the MSE on previously-seen tasks,

i.e.,
{\forall m<t\!:~F_{m}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=\tfrac{1}{\left|{S_{m}}\right|}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}}\left({\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-y}\right)^{2}}

(
e.g.,
Doan et al. 2021
;
Evron et al. 2022
).
Then, lower forgetting implies better
training
loss on previous tasks.

In continual linear
classification
,
hinge losses capture similar properties.
First, per our definitions,
immediately after learning the
m
th
task,
the forgetting on it is
0
,
since
\forall\mathbf{v}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D}\!:{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})\!\in\!\mathcal{W}_{m}

and

\forall\mathbf{u}\!\in\!\mathcal{W}_{m}\!:F_{m}(\mathbf{u})=0
.
Moreover,
a lower forgetting implies
better training margins and generalization performance on previous tasks.
Since the squared hinge loss is a surrogate for the 0-1 loss,
F_{m}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right)\!<\!1

implies no prediction errors on the
m
th
task.

Remark 4.2

(Forgetting vs. Regret)
.

Forgetting
is different from the

used to analyze online learning algorithms
(
Crammer et al. 2006
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2012
;
Hoi et al. 2021
)
.
Forgetting quantifies the degradation on previous tasks in hindsight,
while regret cumulatively captures the ability to predict future datapoints (or tasks).

A favorable property of the quantities we defined, is that they bound each other.

Lemma 4.3

(Connecting quantities)
.

Recall our definition of

{R\triangleq\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\max_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}}\!\!\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|}
.
The quantities of Def.

\forall\mathbf{w}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D},\,m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]

\displaystyle F_{m}(\mathbf{w})\leq{d}^{2}(\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}_{m})\!\!\max_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}}\!\!\!\!\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\leq{d}^{2}(\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}^{\star})R^{2}.

Moreover, for the Sequential Max-Margin iterates

(\mathbf{w}_{t})
of Scheme
2

4.3
Convergence to the Minimum-Norm Solution

When solving feasibility problems for classification
(
e.g.,
in hard-margin SVM), the minimum-norm solution is also the max-margin solution.
In turn, max-margin solutions are theoretically linked to better generalization performance.

In realizable continual linear regression
(or generally, in alternating projections onto closed
subspaces
),
it is known that if iterates converge to an offline (or globally feasible) solution,
then that solution must
be the closest to
\mathbf{w}_{0}
,

i.e.,
have a minimum norm

(
Evron et al. 2022
;
Halperin 1962
)
.

In contrast, in separable continual linear classification settings like ours
(or generally, in projections onto closed convex sets),
there is no such guarantee
and we can converge to a suboptimal offline solution,
as depicted in Figure
1(c)
.

Nevertheless, the following optimality guarantee
does
hold.

Theorem 4.7

(Optimality guarantee)
.

Any iterate
\mathbf{w}_{t}
obtained by Scheme
2

holds

\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right\|\leq 2\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|
,
where
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is the minimum-norm offline solution
(Def.
2.2
).

If additionally,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
is “offline”-feasible,
i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}
,
then

\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right\|\leq 2\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\,.

The proof is given in Appendix
C.2
.
Further comparisons to continual regression are drawn in Appendix
A
.

4.4
Recurring Tasks

Naturally, in many practical continual problems,
certain concepts and experiences recur at different tasks
(
e.g.,

Several recent papers have observed empirically that task repetitions mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning

(

In this section, we analytically study the influence of repetitions.
To accomplish this, we leverage the connection that we have established between continual learning and successive projection algorithms for convex feasibility problems.
Importantly, we

The recurring tasks setting should not be confused with standard batch training

The following is a key lemma in our paper.
Much of the research on POCS has focused on defining and applying linear regularity conditions, under which iterates
(\mathbf{w}_{t})

(like

4.4.1
Cyclic Ordering

Mathematically,

This setting has been at the center of focus in many theoretical papers (

Practically, in continual learning, cyclic orderings are indeed less flexible.

Many papers have investigated various aspects of catastrophic forgetting, including when it occurs

A sound understanding of forgetting can potentially advance the continual learning field significantly.

Throughout our paper, we discussed many connections to other works from many fields.
Interestingly, our Sequential Max-Margin (SMM) scheme can be seen as a

To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to highlight the connection between regularization methods for continual learning and Adaptive SVM.

For the special case where each task has one datapoint, the SMM scheme corresponds to the online Passive-Aggressive algorithm
(
Crammer et al. 2006
)

If additionally, we consider only two tasks learned in a cyclic ordering, the regret after training task

(see Remark

Consequently, our forgetting bound in Prop.
4.13
matches the regret bound in their Theorem 2.

7.1

Remark B.1

(Simplification)

B.1
Auxiliary Lemmas

We first present two auxiliary results that we need for our main proof.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mu
-strongly convex objective function
(holding
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{w})\succeq\mu\mathbf{I}

for some
\mu>0
).
Then, for any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the (Euclidean) distance between
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
(the minimizer of the objective
f
), can be upper bounded by:

\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\left\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\right\|\,.

Proof.

This lemma is a known convex optimization result
(
e.g.,
see Lemma 10 in
Sidford 2020
).
We prove it here for the sake of completeness.

We make use of the following property of strongly convex functions.

Property (9.8) from
Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
\mu
-strongly convex objective function
(holding
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{w})\succeq\mu\mathbf{I}

for some
\mu>0
).
Then, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we have

f(\mathbf{y})\geq f(\mathbf{x})+\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\left({\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}}\right\|^{2}\,.

Using the above property, we prove our lemma, which is a slightly stronger result than Property (9.11) in
Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004
.
Our proof follows the one by
Sidford 2020
,
and is brought here for completeness.

First, we set
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{w}^{\star},\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{w}
(from our lemma).
Since
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
is a (global) minimizer, its gradient is zero,

i.e.,
\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\!=\!\mathbf{0}_{D}
.
We thus get that

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle\geq f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})+\underbrace{\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})^{\top}}_{=\mathbf{0}_{D}}\left({\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\,.

Using the optimality of
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
, we get that

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:~f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})=\min_{\mathbf{y}}f(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\mathbf{y}}\left({f(\mathbf{w})+\nabla f(\mathbf{w})^{\top}\left({\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{w}}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{w}}\right\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{w})+\min_{\mathbf{y}}\left({\nabla f(\mathbf{w})^{\top}\left({\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{w}}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{w}}\right\|^{2}}\right)\,.

Then, by plugging in the minimizer of the right term, we get

\displaystyle\forall\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:~f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})

\displaystyle\geq f(\mathbf{w})+\nabla f(\mathbf{w})^{\top}\Big({\mathbf{w}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{w})-\mathbf{w}}\Big)+\frac{\mu}{2}\Big\|{\mathbf{w}-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{w})-\mathbf{w}}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{w})-\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{w})^{\top}\nabla f(\mathbf{w})+\frac{\mu}{2}\Big\|{\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\Big\|^{2}=f(\mathbf{w})-\frac{1}{\mu}\big\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\big\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\big\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{w})-\frac{1}{2\mu}\big\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\big\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq f(\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\,.

Overall, we showed that

\forall\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\big\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\big\|^{2}\geq f(\mathbf{w})-f(\mathbf{w}^{\star})\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}~~~\Longrightarrow~~~\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}\big\|{\nabla f(\mathbf{w})}\big\|^{2}\geq\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\,,

as required.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

and let
\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{k}\succ\mathbf{0}_{D\times D}

Consider sequentially solving

(S_{t}=(\mathbf{X}_{t},y_{t}=\mathbf{1}))_{t\in\left[{k}\right]}

(recall the simplification in Remark

)
using the following iterative update rule:

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}_{D}

\displaystyle\forall t\in\left[{k}\right]:~~~\mathbf{w}_{t}

\displaystyle={\argmin}_{\mathbf{w}}\!\left\|{\mathbf{w}-c_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t-1}}\right\|^{2}_{{\mathbf{B}_{t}}}\enskip~\textrm{s.t.}\,\enskip\,\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\geq 1,\,\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}_{t}~.

(4)

Then,
for almost all datasets sampled from

when

the unique dual solution

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-c_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t-1})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S_{t}}\mathbf{x}\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\text{AND}\quad\Big({\forall\mathbf{x}\in S_{t}:~\left({\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})>0\wedge\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1}\right)~\text{OR}~\left({\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})=0\wedge\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}>1}\right)}\Big)\,.

(That is, there is no support vector for which
\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})=0

Proof for Lemma

.

The proof follows the techniques of the proof of Lemma 12 in Appendix B of
Soudry et al. 2018
.

Here, we focus only on task sequences where
\mathbf{w}_{t}={\mathbf{P}}_{t}({c_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t-1}})\neq c_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t-1},~\forall t\in\left[{k}\right]

(we can always reduce to such cases by simply removing from the sequence any task

Thus,
\mathbf{w}_{t}
must lie on the boundary of the
t

Consequently, the support set

{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}_{t}\mid\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1\right\}}
is nonempty.

For almost all datasets (all except measure zero),
no more than
D

e.g.,
the
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1

Therefore, for any task
t\in\left[{k}\right]

D

i.e.,
|\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}|\leq D
.
Also, for almost all datasets, any set of at most
D

\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
) is linearly independent.

Let
\bm{\alpha}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|{S_{t}}\right|}_{\geq 0}

We denote the matrix whose columns are the support vectors by

and the dual solution, restricted to its components corresponding to support vectors, by
\bm{\alpha}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}
.
Due to the complementary slackness,

i.e.,
\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}>1

\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})=0
.
We now recall that
\mathbf{B}_{t}
is invertible, and rewrite the stationarity condition as,

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{t}(\mathbf{w}_{t}-c_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S_{t}}\mathbf{x}\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}\mathbf{x}\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})+\!\!\!\!\cancel{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in S_{t}:\,\mathbf{w}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}>1}\!\!\!\mathbf{x}\underbrace{\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})}_{=0}}=\mathbf{X}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}\bm{\alpha}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{t}-c_{t}\mathbf{w}_{t-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{B}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}\bm{\alpha}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}\,.

(5)

Multiplying from the left by the full row rank matrix

\bm{\alpha}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbf{X}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t}}_{=\mathbf{1}}-c_{t}\mathbf{X}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{t-1}

In Remark

More related tools are the ones that were used in

Here, however, we analyze the convergence of the (unique) minimizer of the

While there are some technical similarities, there are also differences and challenges of a different nature.

Recall Theorem
3.1
.
Let

2.

When

(and possibly

):

B.3
Limitations of our Analysis

We proved that Lemma
B.3

and Corollary
B.4
,
which are pivotal for our proofs of Theorems
3.1
,
5.1
, and
5.4
,
hold for almost all datasets and weighting schemes.
We took the approach of
Soudry et al. 2018
who analyzed a simpler non-continual single-task case
and derived similar results from the perspective of the roots of some polynomials.
Below, we discuss the limitations of this analytical approach in our case.

1.

Task recurrence.

In our proof of Lemma
B.3
, we employed a construction where tasks do not recur. When tasks recur, the constructed polynomials have a higher-order dependence on the elements of
\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{T}
(
T
is the number of possible tasks, in contrast to the number of iterations
k
).
Then, the analysis becomes more subtle
due to some additional constraints on the number of support vectors at each iteration
(N_{t})

(
e.g.,
when the same task is seen at iterations
t
and
\left({t+1}\right)
, it must hold that
N_{t}=N_{t+1}
).
Finding a construction for a general sequence of support sizes
(N_{t})
is thus more challenging because not any sequence is attainable.
Without such a general construction, it remains possible that task recurrence leads to
a collapse into the measure-zero scenarios
where the lemma does not hold.

On the other hand, we
are
able to prove that the lemma holds for some cases where tasks recur.
For instance, we can design constructions that allow task recurrence, as long as
the support sizes are fixed and hold
{N_{t}=N\leq D/2,~\forall t\in\left[{k}\right]}
.
Specifically, we can construct a task sequence of
k
iterations over
T\leq k
tasks (using a task ordering
\tau
as in Def.
4.9
).
We construct the datasets so as to form a “cyclic” sequence with two types of tasks.
That is, the columns of
\mathbf{X}_{\tau(t)}=\mathbf{X}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau(t)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
are

\mathbf{x}^{\tau(t)}_{n}=\begin{cases}+\cos\left({\theta}\right)\mathbf{e}_{n}+\sin\left({\theta}\right)\mathbf{e}_{D-n}&\tau(t)=\tau(k)\\
-\cos\left({\theta}\right)\mathbf{e}_{n}+\sin\left({\theta}\right)\mathbf{e}_{D-n}&\tau(t)\neq\tau(k)\end{cases}
.
Under this construction, which is easy to analyze
(as in Appendix
E.1
),
one can show that at the
k
th
iteration, if
\mathbf{w}_{k}\neq c_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k-1}
then

\bm{\alpha}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}}=\gamma\mathbf{1}_{N}\succ\mathbf{0}
, for some
\gamma>0
.
This suffices for showing that the polynomials are nonzero (when
{N_{t}=N\leq D/2,~\forall t\in\left[{k}\right]}
, for an arbitrary length
k
, with any form of recurrence of the task seen at iteration
k
),
and conclude that recurrence does not necessarily collapse to measure zero events where the lemma does not hold.

2.

Weighting schemes.

We initially proved our lemma for the isotropic weighting scheme where
\mathbf{B}_{t}=\mathbf{I}
(notably, this corresponds exactly to the case in our main result in Theorem
3.1
).
Subsequently, we used these isotropic weighting schemes to establish that our lemma applies to almost all weighting schemes as well.
However, common weighting schemes, such as Fisher-information-based schemes, rely on the data observed in previously encountered tasks.
Again, this makes the construction of a general task sequence where
\bm{\alpha}_{\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{t}}\succ\mathbf{0}
more complicated.
Thus, it is possible that such weighting schemes will collapse into the measure zero cases where the lemma does not hold.

We hypothesize that the reservations we expressed above are merely limitations of the analytical tools we utilized.
Various simulations we conducted demonstrated an agreement between the weakly-regularized iterates and the Sequential Max-Margin iterates. Closing these gaps in our analysis will likely require an alternative analytical approach.

B.3.1
Example of a Measure Zero Case

Finally, we briefly demonstrate a measure zero case where Lemma
B.3

and Corollary
B.4
do not hold.

Let the first task be
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\left[\mathbf{e}_{1}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times 1}
. Then,
\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{1}
.

The second task is
\mathbf{X}_{2}=\left[\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times 2}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{2}

\displaystyle=\arg\min\left\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{1}\right\|\,\text{s.t. }\left(\mathbf{e}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{w}\geq 1\right)\wedge\left(\mathbf{e}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{w}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2}\,.

Both
\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}
are support vectors of the 2
nd
task, but from the stationarity condition
(plug in
\mathbf{B}_{2}=\mathbf{I}
,
c_{2}=1
into Eq. (
B.1
)), it holds that,

\alpha_{2}(\mathbf{e}_{1})\,\mathbf{e}_{1}+\alpha_{2}(\mathbf{e}_{2})\,\mathbf{e}_{2}=\mathbf{w}_{2}-\mathbf{w}_{1}=\mathbf{e}_{2}\,,

thus requiring that
\alpha_{2}(\mathbf{e}_{1})=0

Crucially, this prevents the existence of a finite
\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
that holds
\exp\left({-\tilde{\mathbf{w}}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{1}}\right)=\alpha_{2}(\mathbf{e}_{1})
.

Recall Lemma

D.1
General Properties

We start by stating a few general properties of projections and norms that will help us throughout the appendices.

Property 1

(Projection properties)
.

Let
{\mathbf{P}}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a projection operator onto a nonempty closed convex set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Then,
{\mathbf{P}}
holds the following properties:

1.

Geometric definition.

{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{C}}\left\|{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}}\right\|

for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
;

2.

Idempotence.

{\mathbf{P}}^{2}\triangleq{\mathbf{P}}\circ{\mathbf{P}}={\mathbf{P}}
;

3.

Contraction (Non-expansiveness).

For all
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
it holds that

\left\|{{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})-{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{u})}\right\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}\right\|
.
Consequently, when
\mathbf{0}_{D}\in\mathcal{C}
, it holds that

\left\|{{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|

(see Fact 1.9 in
Deutsch & Hundal 2006b
) ;

4.

The operator
\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}
.

Let
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}
be the identity operator
and define the operator
(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}})(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})
.
Then, for all
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
it holds that

\left\|{(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}})(\mathbf{v})-(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}})(\mathbf{u})}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})-{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{u})}\right\|^{2}
.
Consequently, when
\mathbf{0}_{D}\in\mathcal{C}
,
it holds that

\left\|{\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|^{2}

for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}

(see Propositions 4.2 and 4.8 in
Bauschke et al. 2011

and Fact 1.7 in
Deutsch & Hundal 2006b
).

As a result of the non-expansiveness property above, we get the following monotonicity result
(stronger than
4.5
).

Corollary D.1
.

Let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}
be an arbitrary offline solution and
{\mathbf{P}}_{t}
be the projection onto the feasible set of the
{t}
th
task.
The following monotonicity holds for the iterates
(\mathbf{w}_{t})
of Scheme
2
:

\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-\!\underbrace{\mathbf{w}}_{\in\mathcal{W}_{t}}}\|=\|{{\mathbf{P}}_{t}\left({\mathbf{w}_{t-1}}\right)-{\mathbf{P}}_{t}\left({\mathbf{w}}\right)}\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{t-1}-\mathbf{w}}\right\|,~~~\forall t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\,.

Specifically, this holds for the minimum-norm solution
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
:

\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{t-1}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|,~~~\forall t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\,.

Next, we state a known property of squared Euclidean norms, stemming from their convexity and from Jensen’s inequality.

Claim D.2
.

For any
m
vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{D}
, it holds that
\left\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\!+\!\dots\!+\!\mathbf{v}_{m}\right\|^{2}\leq m\!\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{1}\right\|^{2}+\cdots+\left\|\mathbf{v}_{m}\right\|^{2}\right)
.

Lemma D.3
.

The residual from task
m
, defined by

\left\|\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}=\left\|(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m})(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}
,
is a convex function in
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, than for every
\alpha\in[0,1]
it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m})(\alpha\mathbf{u}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\alpha\mathbf{u}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\alpha\mathbf{u}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{projection properties}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\triangleq\!\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{m}}\!\left\|{\alpha\mathbf{u}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{w}}\right\|^{2}\leq\Big\|\alpha\mathbf{u}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\mathbf{v}-\underbrace{(\alpha\overbrace{{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{u})}^{\in\mathcal{W}_{m}}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\overbrace{{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})}^{\in\mathcal{W}_{m}})}_{\in\mathcal{W}_{m}\text{, due to its convexity}}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\alpha(\mathbf{u}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{u}))+(1\!-\!\alpha)(\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{v}))\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{squared norm is convex}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\left\|\mathbf{u}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{u})\right\|^{2}+(1\!-\!\alpha)\left\|\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}\,.

∎

D.2
Linear Regularity of Classification Tasks

We start by proving an important property of our setting –
that the feasible set has a nonempty interior.

Lemma D.4
.

Recall our definition of

{R\triangleq\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}\max_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}}\!\!\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|}
.
Let
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
be the minimum-norm solution defined in Def.
2.2
.
Then, the vector
2\mathbf{w}^{\star}
has a
feasible
ball of radius
\frac{1}{R}
around it.
More formally,

\mathcal{B}\left({2\mathbf{w}^{\star},\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\right)\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\,\mid\,\left\|{\mathbf{w}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}\right\}\subset\mathcal{W}^{\star}\,.

Proof.

By the definition of feasibility,

\forall m\in\left[{T}\right],\,\forall(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}\!:\,y{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\geq 1
.
Let
\mathbf{w}
be an arbitrary vector in
\mathcal{B}\left({2\mathbf{w}^{\star},\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\right)
.
Clearly,
\mathbf{w}
can be instead denoted as
\mathbf{w}=2\mathbf{w}^{\star}+\mathbf{v}
for some
\mathbf{v}
such that
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}
.
We then conclude that
\mathbf{w}
is feasible:

\forall m\in\left[{T}\right],\,\forall(\mathbf{x},y)\in S_{m}\!:\,\,y\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=2\underbrace{y{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}_{\geq 1}+y\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\geq 2-\left|{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\right|\geq 2-\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|}_{\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{x}}\right\|}_{\leq R}\geq 1~~\Longrightarrow~~\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}\,.

∎

We are now ready to prove the linear regularity, using Lemma
D.4
and techniques from

Gubin et al. 1967

(in their proof of Lemma 5)
and
Nedić 2010

(in their proof of Proposition 8).
To be more exact, we prove
bounded
linear regularity (as defined in
Bauschke & Borwein 1993
;
Deutsch & Hundal 2008
),
which suffices for our needs in this paper.

Recall Lemma
4.8
.
At the
t
th
iteration, the distance to the offline feasible set is tied to the distance to the farthest feasible set of any
specific
task.
Specifically, it holds that
\forall t\!\in\!\mathbb{N}^{+}
,

\displaystyle{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)\leq 4\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}R^{2}\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}{d}^{2}\big({\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\big)\,.

Proof.

We start by showing (bounded) regularity for an arbitrary
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
(not necessarily an iterate).
We define
\varepsilon\triangleq\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}d\left({\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)

and consider a convex combination between
\mathbf{w}
and
2\mathbf{w}^{\star}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\mathbf{w}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}2\mathbf{w}^{\star}\,=\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\mathbf{w}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}2\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})+\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\mathbf{z}_{m}+\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})\,,

where we denoted

\mathbf{z}_{m}\triangleq 2\mathbf{w}^{\star}+\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathbf{w}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})}\right)
, for any
m\in\left[{T}\right]
.

We notice that
\mathbf{z}_{m}\in\mathcal{B}\left({2\mathbf{w}^{\star},\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\ref{lem:feasible_interior}}}}{{\subset}}\mathcal{W}^{\star}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{m}
,
since
\left\|{\mathbf{z}_{m}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|=\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}{\varepsilon}\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{w}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})}\right\|}_{={d}(\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}_{m})\leq\varepsilon}\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{y}
is a convex combination of
\mathbf{z}_{m},{\mathbf{P}}_{m}\left({\mathbf{w}}\right)\in\mathcal{W}_{m}
and is therefore also contained in
\mathcal{W}_{m}
.
Since this is true
\forall m\in\left[{T}\right]
, we get that
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}
.
Then, a bounded linear regularity property follows, since

\displaystyle{d}(\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}}\right\|=\overbrace{\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{R}}}}^{\leq R\varepsilon}\left\|{\mathbf{w}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|R\varepsilon=\left\|{\mathbf{w}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|R\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}{d}\big({\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\big)\,.

When
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}_{t}
is an iterate of the sequential Scheme
2
, we can use the monotonicity from Corollary
D.1

(notice that
2\mathbf{w}^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}
)
to show that

\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|=\left\|{2\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|=2\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|
,
and finally, conclude that

\displaystyle{d}(\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathcal{W}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|R\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}{d}\big({\mathbf{w}_{t},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\big)\,.

∎

D.3
Proofs for Cyclic Orderings
(Section
4.4.1
)

Recall Lemma
4.11
.
Under a cyclic ordering
(and the separability assumption
2.1
),
the iterates converge to a 2-optimal

{\mathbf{w}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}}
.
That is,

\lim_{k\to\infty}{d}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star})=0,~~~\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{\infty}}\right\|\leq 2\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\,.

Proof.

First, notice that we work in a finite-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and that from Assumption
2.1
we have that
the offline feasibility set,

i.e.,
the convex sets’ intersection

\mathcal{W}^{\star}=\mathcal{W}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{W}_{T}
, is nonempty.
Under these conditions, the (weak and strong) convergence of the cyclic iterates
(\mathbf{w}_{t})
to a vector
\mathbf{w}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star}

can be deduced from the rates we derive (independently of the lemma here) in
Proposition
4.13
.
The second part of our corollary stems directly from
our Theorem
4.7
.

Similar optimality guarantees for cyclic settings have also been proved in previous papers (
e.g.,
Theorem 1 in
Gubin et al. 1967

and Theorem 2.7 in
Deutsch & Hundal 2006a
).
∎

Recall Theorem
4.12
.
For
{T\geq 2}

jointly-separable tasks
learned cyclically,
after

{k\!=\!nT}
iterations (
n
cycles),
our quantities of interest
(Def.
4.1
)
converge linearly as

\displaystyle\underbrace{\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}F_{m}(\mathbf{w}_{k})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Maximum forgetting}\end{subarray}}\leq\underbrace{\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Maximum distance}\\
\text{to any feasible set}\end{subarray}}R^{2}\leq\underbrace{{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Dist.~to}\\
\text{offline feasible set}\end{subarray}}R^{2}

\displaystyle\leq g(k)\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\!R^{2}\,,

where

g(k)\triangleq\begin{cases}\enskip\,\exp\!\left({-\tfrac{k}{4\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right)&T=2\\
4\exp\!\left({-\tfrac{k}{16T^{2}\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right)&T\geq 3\end{cases}

and

\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\!R^{2}\!\geq\!1
is the problem complexity (Rem.
4.4
).

Proof.

Here, we exploit the regularity of our setting,
in which the convex sets are defined by the intersections of halfspaces.

1.

Cyclic orderings of
T=2
tasks.

Given the regularity of our problems
(Lemma
4.8
),
the required upper bound on
{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)

is given from existing results on projection algorithms with two closed convex sets (specifically from Theorem 3.12 and Corollary 3.14 in
Bauschke & Borwein 1993

and Theorem 2.10 in
Bauschke 2001
).
However, the indexing in some of these previous works may be confusing. Therefore, for the sake of completeness, we prove it here as well.

Assume w.l.o.g. that
2\mid k
. Define the projection operator
{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}
onto the intersection

\mathcal{W}^{\star}=\mathcal{W}_{1}\cap\mathcal{W}_{2}
.
It follows that,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}R^{2}}{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)

\displaystyle\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:regularity}}}}{{\leq}}\!\!\!\max_{m\in\{1,2\}}{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{cyclic}}}{{=}}\!\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{k})}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{k})-{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k})+{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k})}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{k})-{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k})+{\mathbf{P}}_{1}({\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k}))}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\left({\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1}}\right)(\mathbf{w}_{k})-\left({\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1}}\right)({\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k}))}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:projections}}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k})}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{{\mathbf{P}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{k})-{\mathbf{P}}_{1}({\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k}))}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k})}\right\|^{2}}_{={d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)}-\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{P}}_{1\cap 2}(\mathbf{w}_{k})}\right\|^{2}}_{\geq\,{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k+1},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)}\leq{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)-{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k+1},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)

\displaystyle{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k+1},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left({1-\frac{1}{4\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right){d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)\leq\dots\leq\left({1-\frac{1}{4\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right)^{k+1}\underbrace{{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)}_{\triangleq\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}}\,.

Finally, we conclude the
T=2
case by using
the ordering property of our quantities of interest
(Lemma
4.3
),
and using the algebraic identity stating that

\forall z\!\in\!\left({0,1}\right),k\!>\!0:~\left({1-z}\right)^{k}\leq\exp\left({-kz}\right)
.

Remark D.5
.

A similar upper bound on

{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)
,
with a slightly worse rate,
could be deduced from the stochastic upper bound in
Theorem
4.15
,
by using the fact that for
T=2
tasks, random orderings lead to exactly the same projections as cyclic ones, but slower
(the idempotence of the projections implies that applying the same projection many consecutive times in a random ordering has the same effect as applying it once like in a cyclic ordering).

2.

Cyclic orderings of
T\geq 3
tasks.

Given the regularity of our problems
(Lemma
4.8
),
the required upper bound is an almost immediate
corollary from
Theorem 3.15 in
Deutsch & Hundal 2008
),
stating that in our case we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{\infty}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left({1-\frac{1}{4T\cdot 4\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right)^{k/T}\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}_{\infty}}\right\|^{2}=\left({1-\frac{1}{16T\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right)^{k/T}\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{\infty}}\right\|^{2}\,.

We use
Lemma
4.11

(
\mathbf{w}_{\infty}\in\mathcal{W}^{\star},\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{\infty}}\right\|\leq 2\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|
)
and
the aforementioned algebraic identity,
and get

\displaystyle{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{\infty}}\right\|^{2}\leq 4\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\exp\left({-\frac{k}{16T^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}R^{2}}}\right)\,.

∎

D.3.1
Detour: Universal bounds for general cyclic Projections Onto Convex Sets settings (POCS)

First, we establish a lemma equivalent to Lemma 22 in
Evron et al. 2022
for our POCS setting.
They used
linear
projections solely while we analyze more general projection operators.
Thus, while we
closely
follow their statements and proofs, we are required to perform several adjustments to capture the wider family of convex operators.

Lemma D.6

(Cyclic-case auxiliary bounds)
.

Let
{\mathbf{Q}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}

be
T
projection operators
onto the nonempty closed convex sets
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{T}
(respectively)
such that
\mathbf{0}_{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{C}_{T}
.
Let
\mathbf{M}={\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}

be the cyclic operator formed by these projections.
Moreover, let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be an arbitrary vector.
Then:

(
Lemma
lem:dimension_independenta)

For any
m\in\left[{T-1}\right]
, it holds that

{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{v},\mathcal{C}_{m}}\right)=\left\|\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}\leq m\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}\right)~;

(
Lemma
lem:dimension_independentb)

It holds that

\left\|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}\leq T\!\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}\right)~\!;

(
Lemma
lem:dimension_independentc)

After
n\geq 1
cycles it holds that

\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}-\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|{\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|
;

(
Lemma
lem:dimension_independentd)

For any
m\in\left[{T-1}\right]
,
after
n\geq 1
cycles it holds that

\displaystyle{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v}),\mathcal{C}_{m}}\right)=\left\|{\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v}))}\right\|^{2}\leq 2m\left\|{\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|~;

(
Lemma
lem:dimension_independente)

For any number of cycles
n\geq 1
, it holds that

\left\|{\mathbf{M}^{n-1}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|\leq\sqrt{\nicefrac{{T}}{{n}}}\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|
.

We will prove this lemma after stating a few additional results including our universal bounds.

Remark D.7

(Translates of convex sets)
.

For simplicity, many of our results (
e.g.,
Lemma
D.6
above)
are derived using
nonempty closed convex sets having
\mathbf{0}_{D}
in their intersection.
We wish to apply these results to our feasible sets

\mathcal{W}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{W}_{T}
from Assumption
2.1
,
but their nonempty intersection
\mathcal{W}^{\star}

does not contain
\mathbf{0}_{D}
.
As a remedy, it is common to translate the entire space by some feasible solution (
e.g.,
see
Deutsch & Hundal 2006a
).

We take this approach and translate our space by
-\mathbf{w}^{\star}
(where
\mathbf{w}^{\star}\in\mathcal{W}
is the minimum-norm offline solution defined in Def.
2.2
).
In turn, the resulting feasible sets

C_{m}\triangleq\mathcal{W}_{m}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{\star},~\forall m\in\left[{T}\right]
, now have an equivalent intersection
\mathcal{W}^{\star}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{\star}
that
does
contain
\mathbf{0}_{D}
.
Importantly, this simple translation does not change the properties of the space we work in or the ones of any projection that we apply.
In particular,
we can apply either “type” of these projections interchangeably, in the sense that:

{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})\triangleq{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathcal{W}_{m}}(\mathbf{w})={\mathbf{P}}_{\mathcal{C}_{m}}(\mathbf{w}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{\star})+\mathbf{w}^{\star}\triangleq{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})+\mathbf{w}^{\star}\,.

This also holds recursively,

e.g.,

\mathbf{w}_{2}={\mathbf{P}}_{2}({\mathbf{P}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{0}))={\mathbf{P}}_{2}({\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})+\mathbf{w}^{\star})={\mathbf{Q}}_{2}({\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}))+\mathbf{w}^{\star}
.
Moreover, our translation preserves all distances,
such that for instance

\forall\mathbf{w}\!:~\left\|{\mathbf{w}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}(\mathbf{w})}\right\|={d}\!\left({\mathbf{w},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)={d}\!\left({\mathbf{w}\!-\!\mathbf{w}^{\star},\mathcal{C}_{m}}\right)=\left\|{\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})}\right\|\,.

We will also need the following lemma to prove the universal result for the cyclic
T=2
case.

Lemma D.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}

be two closed convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with a nonempty intersection,
and let
\left({\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right)
be a sequence of iterates induced by cyclic projections onto
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}

starting from an arbitrary
\mathbf{w}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Then, the projection “residuals” are monotonically decreasing.
That is, For any iteration
t\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}\right\|\leq\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t-1}\right\|\,.

Proof.

Assume w.l.o.g. that
2\mid t
.
By the definition of projections,

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right\|=\left\|{{\mathbf{P}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right\|=\min_{\mathbf{w}\in{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}\|\mathbf{w}-\!\underbrace{\mathbf{w}_{t}}_{\in{\mathcal{W}_{2}}}\!\|\leq\|\!\underbrace{\mathbf{w}_{t-1}}_{\in{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}\!-\mathbf{w}_{t}\|\,.

∎

Now, we recall and prove our main result for this section (using the above lemmas).
Afterward, we will prove Lemma
D.6
.
Our result here generalizes important parts of Theorems 10 and 11 in
Evron et al. 2022
and extends these results from projections onto closed subspaces to projections onto general convex sets.

Recall Proposition
4.13
.
Let
\mathcal{W}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{W}_{T}
be closed convex sets with nonempty intersection
\mathcal{W}^{\star}
.
Let

\mathbf{w}_{k}=({\mathbf{P}}_{T}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{P}}_{1})^{n}(\mathbf{w}_{0})

be the iterate after
k\!=\!nT
iterations (
n
cycles)
of cyclic projections onto these convex sets.
Then, the maximal distance to any (specific) convex set,
is upper bounded
universally
as,

For
T=2
:

\displaystyle\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{k+1}\,{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)\,,

For
T\geq 3
:

\displaystyle\max_{m\in\left[{T}\right]}{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)\leq\frac{2T^{2}}{\sqrt{k}}\,{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)\,.

Proof.

We divide our proof into two parts.

1.

Cyclic two tasks (
T=2
)

The proposition deals with cases where
k=nT=2n
(thus
2\mid k
).
We get

\displaystyle\max\Big\{{d}\left(\mathbf{w}_{k},{\mathcal{W}_{1}}\right),{d}\big(\underbrace{\mathbf{w}_{k}}_{\in{\mathcal{W}_{2}}},{\mathcal{W}_{2}}\big)\Big\}={d}\left(\mathbf{w}_{k},{\mathcal{W}_{1}}\right)=\min_{\mathbf{w}\in{\mathcal{W}_{1}}}\left\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\right\|\triangleq\left\|\mathbf{w}_{k+1}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\right\|\,.

We thus focus on
\left\|\mathbf{w}_{k+1}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\right\|
and bound it using all of the above, using ideas from
Evron et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{w}_{k+1}-\mathbf{w}_{k}\right\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:two_tasks_monotonicity}}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}+\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=~~~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\|{\left({\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1}}\right)\left({\mathbf{w}_{t}}\right)-\left({\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1}}\right)\left({\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:projections}}\end{subarray}\right]~~\leq~~~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\left({\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}-\|{{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1}\mathbf{w}_{t}-{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1}\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle~~=~~~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\left({\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}-\|{\mathbf{w}_{t+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]~~=~~~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\left\|\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}-{\left\|\mathbf{w}_{k+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{k+1}\|\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\|^{2}\triangleq\frac{1}{k+1}{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)\,.

2.

Cyclic
T\geq 3
tasks

Using Lemma
D.6
and the translations from Remark
D.7
, it follows that

\displaystyle\begin{split}{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\ref{rmk:translations}}}}{{=}}{d}^{2}\!\left({\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}^{\star},\mathcal{C}_{m}}\right)&=\left\|{\left({\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right)-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})}\right\|^{2}\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Remark~\ref{rmk:translations}}\end{subarray}\right]&=\left\|{\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}))}\right\|^{2}\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{\ref{lem:m_task_forgetting}}\end{subarray}\right]&\leq 2m\left\|{\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})-\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star})}\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{\ref{lem:T_over_n_norm}}\end{subarray}\right]&\leq 2m\sqrt{\frac{T}{n+1}}\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\\
&\leq 2T\sqrt{\frac{T}{n}}\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}=\frac{2T^{2}}{\sqrt{k}}\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}\triangleq\frac{2T^{2}}{\sqrt{k}}{d}^{2\!}\left({\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathcal{W}^{\star}}\right)~.\end{split}

∎

Proof for Lemma
D.6

Proof for
(Lemma lem:dimension_independenta)
.

For
m=1
,
using the fact that
\mathbf{0}_{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\dots\cap\mathcal{C}_{T}
, Prop.

.

1.

\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{v}

2.

Subtracting both of the equations above, we get

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\right)-\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{m}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\right)+\mathbf{M}_{m}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{m}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{\ell}-\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{m}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}+\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{m}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{\ell}-\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}+\cancel{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{m-1}}+\!\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{\ell}-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\hskip 0.8pt\!\circ\hskip 0.9pt\!\mathbf{M}_{\ell}~,

where the third term is canceled since

\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}={\mathbf{Q}}_{m}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}^{2}=\mathbf{0}
due to the idempotence of projection operators.

3.

Finally, we use the above and Claim
D.2
to show that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left\|{\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}=\left\|{(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m})(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\\
&=\left\|{(\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1})(\mathbf{v})+{\sum}_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left(\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\circ\mathbf{M}_{\ell}-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\circ\mathbf{M}_{\ell}\right)(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\\
&\leq m\left(\left\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)(\mathbf{v})-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(\mathbf{v})\right)}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})\right)-\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\left(({\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\circ\mathbf{M}_{\ell})(\mathbf{v})\right)}\right\|^{2}\right)~.\end{split}

And since
according to Prop.
1

we have

\forall\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\!:\|{{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)(\mathbf{v})\!-\!\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)(\mathbf{u})}}\|^{2}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}}\right\|^{2}
,
we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|{\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}&\leq m\left(\left\|{\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left\|{\mathbf{M}_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})-{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})\right)}\right\|^{2}\right)\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:projections}}\end{subarray}\right]&\leq m\bigg({\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|^{2}-\|{\underbrace{{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(\mathbf{v})}_{=\mathbf{M}_{1}(\mathbf{v})}}\|^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\Big({\left\|{\mathbf{M}_{\ell}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}-\|{\underbrace{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\left(\mathbf{M}_{\ell}\right(\mathbf{v}))}_{=\mathbf{M}_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{v})}}\|^{2}}\Big)}\bigg)\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]&=m\left(\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\mathbf{M}_{m}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\right)=m\left(\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\right)(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\right)\\
\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{contraction}\end{subarray}\right]&\leq m\left(\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|^{2}-\left\|{\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\right)(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\right)~.\end{split}

∎

Proof for
(Lemma lem:dimension_independentb)
.

Above we defined the identity operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|{\mathbf{v}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\cdots\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}&=\left\|{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}=\left\|{\left({\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\right)+{\sum}_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell+1}\right)\circ\mathbf{M}_{\ell}}\right)(\mathbf{v})}\right\|^{2}\\

∎

Proof for

.

\displaystyle=\|{\left(\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-\left(\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})+\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})-\left(\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})\right)^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})}_{=0}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})\right\|}_{\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|}\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})\right\|+\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})\right\|\underbrace{\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})\right\|}_{\leq\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\mathbf{M}^{n}(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{M}^{n+1}(\mathbf{v})\right\|\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|~.

First, we use

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{n=1}^{\left\lfloor k/T\right\rfloor}\Big\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)^{n}\Big({\underbrace{{\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\left(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)}_{\triangleq\mathbf{u}_{t}}}\Big)}\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{n=1}^{\left\lfloor k/T\right\rfloor}\left\|\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{Q}}_{m}\right)\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)^{n}\left(\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\begin{subarray}{c}\ref{lem:bound_on_projection_to_m_task}\end{subarray}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{n=1}^{\left\lfloor k/T\right\rfloor}\left\|\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)^{n}\left(\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}-\left\|\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)^{n+1}\left(\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{T}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}-\left\|\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)^{\left\lfloor k/T\right\rfloor+1}\left(\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{t+1}\right)\left(\mathbf{u}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}=\frac{T}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\left({\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\!\circ\!{\mathbf{Q}}_{T}\circ\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\cdotp\!\hskip 0.6pt\circ{\mathbf{Q}}_{1}\right)\left(\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{contraction}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}=\frac{T^{2}}{k}\left\|\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}\,.

Overall, we bounded the distance in the cyclic setting:

{d}^{2}\left({{\overline{\mathbf{w}}}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m}}\right)\leq\frac{T}{n}\left\|\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right\|^{2}=\frac{T^{2}}{k}\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|^{2},~\,\forall m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]
.
Then, given Lemma
4.3
,
we conclude that

F_{m}({\overline{\mathbf{w}}}_{k})\leq{d}^{2}({\overline{\mathbf{w}}}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m})R^{2}\leq\frac{T^{2}}{k}\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}R^{2},~\,\forall m\!\in\!\left[{T}\right]
.

D.5.2
Random ordering

Our random ordering result is related to Proposition 6 and Equations (12) and (13) in
Nedić 2010
which analyzed the expected average and maximum distance to any feasible set.
Even more strongly related is Remark 15 in
Evron et al. 2022
which analyzed the
expected
average
forgetting in continual linear regression.
Importantly, their proof in Appendix E.2 does not exploit the linearity of the projection operators and works for general projections as well.
For completeness only, we closely follow their proof and present our own proof below.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{2}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\Big[\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}F_{m}({\overline{\mathbf{w}}}_{k})}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Average forgetting}\end{subarray}}\Big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:euclidean_to_hinge}}}}{{\leq}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\Big[\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}{d}^{2}({\overline{\mathbf{w}}}_{k},\mathcal{W}_{m})\Big]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}\Big[\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\left\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}_{m}\right){\overline{\mathbf{w}}}_{k}}\right\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{i.i.d.~uniform}\end{subarray}\right]=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{k},{\mathbf{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}\right)\big({\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{k}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{k},{\mathbf{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}\right)\big({\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbf{w}_{t}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:residual_is_convex}, Jensen's inequality}\end{subarray}\right]\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{t},{\mathbf{P}}}\Big\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}\right)\big({\mathbf{w}_{t}}\big)}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{t},{\mathbf{P}}}\bigg[\Big\|{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}\right)\big({\mathbf{w}_{t}}\big)-\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{I}-{\mathbf{P}}\right)\big({\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\big)}_{=\mathbf{0}}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Prop.~\ref{prop:projections}}\end{subarray}\right]\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{t},{\mathbf{P}}}\bigg[\Big\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}-\Big\|{{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\underbrace{{\mathbf{P}}(\mathbf{w}^{\star})}_{=\mathbf{w}^{\star}}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{i.i.d.}\end{subarray}\right]=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1}}\bigg[\Big\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}-\Big\|{\underbrace{{\mathbf{P}}_{t+1}(\mathbf{w}_{t})}_{=\mathbf{w}_{t+1}}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\left[\begin{subarray}{c}\text{telescoping}\end{subarray}\right]=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{P}}_{k}}\bigg[\underbrace{\Big\|{\mathbf{w}_{1}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{2}^{2},\\
\text{by
Corollary~\ref{cor:monotonicity}
}\end{subarray}}-\underbrace{\Big\|{\mathbf{w}_{k+1}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\Big\|_{2}^{2}}_{\geq 0}\bigg]\leq\frac{1}{k}\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}~.

∎

Remark E.1

(Applicability of our analysis for finite
\lambda
)
.

When the singular values of the weight matrices of all tasks are between

{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0<\mu\leq\mu_{t}\leq M_{t}\leq M<\infty}}}
,
the residual from our analysis becomes of the order

{\big\|{\mathbf{r}_{t}^{(\lambda)}}\big\|\triangleq\big\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(\lambda)}-\ln({\tfrac{1}{{{{\color[rgb]{0.9414,0.0664,0.0664}\lambda_{t}}}}}})\mathbf{w}_{t}}\big\|=\mathcal{O}\left({\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\big({{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{M}/{\mu}}}}}\big)^{t-t^{\prime}+1}\ln\ln\left({\tfrac{1}{{{{\color[rgb]{0.9414,0.0664,0.0664}\lambda_{t^{\prime}}}}}}}\right)}\right)}
.
When this bound on the condition number,
i.e.,
{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{M}/{\mu}}}}
, is strictly larger than
1
,
we get an exponential growth of the residuals.
Therefore,
within a few tasks under a
finite
regularization strength
\lambda
, the bound on the residuals might become even larger than the scale of the scaled SMM solutions

i.e.,
larger than

\ln({\tfrac{1}{{{{\color[rgb]{0.9414,0.0664,0.0664}\lambda_{t}}}}}})
.
In turn, this will invalidate our analysis.
Of course, in the limit of
\lambda\to 0
, our analysis still applies.
As we explained in Remark
4.6
,
we take
\lambda\to 0

after
fixing the number of iterations
k
(or
t
).

The question of determining the specific value of
\lambda
that practically ensures that

\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(\lambda)}}{\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}^{(\lambda)}}\|}\approx\frac{\mathbf{w}_{t}}{\|{\mathbf{w}_{t}}\|}
, as well as understanding the true impact of
{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{M}/{\mu}}}}>1
on the residuals,
remains an intriguing and open research question.

E.1
Proofs for Regularization Strength Scheduling (Section
5.1
)

Recall Proposition
5.2
.
There exists a construction of two jointly-separable tasks
in which the iterates of the cyclic ordering do not converge to
\mathcal{W}^{\star}
, for any
p>1
.
Specifically, for any
{p>1,\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|>1}
,
it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{{d}(\mathbf{w}_{k},\mathcal{W}^{\star})}{\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|}=\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}(p-1)}{2+\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}(p-1)}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}}\,.

Proof.

First, we explain our construction in the following figure.

Figure 10
:

We consider a 2-dimensional setting of 2 tasks, one positively-labeled normalized sample per task,

i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}_{1},+1),\,(\mathbf{x}_{2},+1)
.
Both
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}
are on the unit sphere (hence
R=1
)
and are symmetric w.r.t. the vertical axis.

In this setting, when
p>1
, it can be readily seen that at each iteration we perform an orthogonal projection onto a closed affine subspace. Therefore, if we converge to
\mathcal{W}^{\star}
, we must converge to the minimum-norm solution
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
specifically (see
Halperin 1962
for instance).
We thus wish to study
\lim_{k\to\infty}{\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|}
.

To ease our notations, we again translate our space by
-\mathbf{w}^{\star}
such that instead of using the “affine” halfspaces
\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2}
,
we use their “homogeneous” counterparts

\mathcal{C}_{m}\triangleq\mathcal{W}_{m}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}

(see Remark
D.7
).
Under this “change of coordinates”, instead of analyzing
\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|
, we can simply analyze
\left\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}}\right\|
.

Since the setting is 2-dimensional and each task has a single sample, we can parameterize
the orthogonal projections as

{{\mathbf{P}}_{1}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\!=\mathbf{I}\!-\!\mathbf{x}_{1}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}}
and
{{\mathbf{P}}_{2}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\!=\mathbf{I}\!-\!\mathbf{x}_{2}\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\top}}

(where
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}\right\|\!=\!\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|\!=\!1
).
We denote the angle between the two subspaces that we project onto as

{\theta\triangleq\arccos\left({\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}\right)=\pi\!-\!\arccos{\left(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)}}
.

Notice that (before the translation) the min-norm solution is
\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}^{\star}\!=\!\frac{1}{\sin\left({{\theta}/{2}}\right)}\!\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}}
.

In the Scheduled Sequential Max-Margin Scheme
3
,
each iterate is multiplied by
\beta\triangleq\frac{1}{p}

before being projected onto the next subspace,
equivalently to

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{k}=\mathbf{w}_{2n}={\mathbf{P}}_{2}\Big({(1-\beta)\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\big({(1-\beta)\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta\mathbf{w}_{2\left({n-1}\right)}}\big)}\Big)

To understand this recursive expression,
let
n=1
.
We get (the projections are linear)

\mathbf{w}_{2}=(1-\beta){\mathbf{P}}_{2}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta{\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{0}
.
Then, for
n=2
, we get

\mathbf{w}_{4}=(1-\beta){\mathbf{P}}_{2}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta(1-\beta){\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta^{2}(1-\beta){\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}{\mathbf{P}}_{2}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta^{3}\left({\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\right)^{2}\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

Recursively, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{k}=\mathbf{w}_{2n}=(1\!-\!\beta)\!\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\beta^{2k}\left({\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\right)^{k}{\mathbf{P}}_{2}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\beta^{2k-1}\left({\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\right)^{k}\mathbf{w}_{0}\right)\!+\beta^{2n-1}\left({\mathbf{P}}_{2}{\mathbf{P}}_{1}\right)^{2}\mathbf{w}_{0}
.

Plugging in the parameterization of the projections,
we get

\displaystyle{\mathbf{w}_{k}}={\mathbf{w}_{2n}}=(1-\beta)\left(\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\beta\cos{\theta})^{2k}\right]\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(\beta\cos{\theta})^{2k-1}\right]\mathbf{v}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}\right)+(\beta\cos{\theta})^{2n-1}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}\,.

Since
\beta=\tfrac{1}{p}<1
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{w}_{2n}}=(1-\beta)\left({\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}}{1-(\beta\cos\theta)^{2}}+\frac{\beta\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}\cos{\theta}}{1-(\beta\cos\theta)^{2}}}\right)\mathbf{v}=(1-\beta)\left({\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}+\beta\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}\cos{\theta}}{1-(\beta\cos\theta)^{2}}}\right)\mathbf{v}\,.

From the definition of
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}
it holds that
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}=\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{0}=\cos{\frac{\theta}{2}}\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}}\|

and also that
\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}}\|=\frac{1}{\sin\left({{\theta}/{2}}\right)}

(after the translation).
Putting it all together, we get

{\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\!{\mathbf{w}_{k}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{w}_{2n}}=(1\!-\!\beta)\cos{\tfrac{\theta}{2}}\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}}\|\left({\frac{1+\beta\cos{\theta}}{1-(\beta\cos\theta)^{2}}}\right)\mathbf{v}=\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}}\|\frac{(1\!-\!\beta)\cos{\tfrac{\theta}{2}}}{1-\beta\cos\theta}\mathbf{v}}
.

Recalling that
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}\right\|\!=\!1
and returning to the original coordinate system (by reversing the aforementioned translation), we get:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\|{\mathbf{w}_{k}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|}{\|{\mathbf{w}_{0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\|}=\frac{\left({1-\beta}\right)\cos{\frac{\theta}{2}}}{1-\beta\cos{\theta}}=\frac{1-\beta}{1-\beta\big({1-\tfrac{2}{\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}}\big)}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}}=\frac{p-1}{p-1+\tfrac{2}{\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|^{2}}}\,.

∎
