Equivalence after extension and Schur coupling for Fredholm operators on Banach spaces Thanks: This work is based on the research supported in part by the National Research Foundation of South Africa (Grant Numbers 90670 and 118513).
Sanne ter Horst, Niels Jakob Laustsen

Abstract
. Schur coupling (SC) and equivalence after extension (EAE) are important relations for bounded operators on Banach spaces. It has been known for 30 years that the former implies the latter, but only recently Ter Horst, Messerschmidt, Ran and Roelands disproved the converse by constructing a pair of Fredholm operators which are EAE, but not SC. Motivated by this result, we investigate when EAE and SC coincide for Fredholm operators. Fredholm operators which are EAE have the same Fredholm index. Surprisingly, we find that for each integer k k and every pair of Banach spaces ( 𝒳 , 𝒴 ) ({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}) , either no pair of Fredholm operators of index k k acting on 𝒳 {\mathcal{X}} and 𝒴 {\mathcal{Y}} , respectively, is SC, or every pair of this kind which is EAE is also SC. Consequently, the question whether EAE and SC coincide for Fredholm operators of index k k depends only on the geometry of the underlying Banach spaces 𝒳 {\mathcal{X}} and 𝒴 {\mathcal{Y}} , not on the properties of the operators themselves. We quantify this finding by introducing two numerical indices which capture the coincidence of EAE and SC, and provide a number of examples illustrating the possible values of these indices. Notably, this includes an example showing that the above-mentioned result of Ter Horst et al , which is based on a pair of essentially incomparable Banach spaces, does not extend to projectively incomparable Banach spaces.

Introduction

Equivalence after extension (EAE), matricial coupling (MC) and Schur coupling (SC) are three relations for bounded operators on Banach spaces that originate in the study of Wiener–Hopf integral operators
[
5
]
and have found numerous applications since. A key feature in many of these applications is that the three relations coincide. This observation led Bart and Tsekanovskiĭ
[
9
]
to ask whether this is always true. They already knew that EAE and MC are equivalent
[
5
,
7
]
and that SC implies EAE
[
8
,
9
]
, so their precise question was whether EAE implies SC.

Despite numerous results confirming this implication in special cases
[
8
,
9
,
6
,
31
,
51
,
27
,
30
]
, recently Ter Horst, Messerschmidt, Ran and Roelands
[
29
]
showed that EAE does not in general imply SC. Their counterexample uses bounded operators defined on a pair of Banach spaces which is essentially incomparable, in which case EAE (and hence SC) can occur only for Fredholm operators, while SC additionally requires that the operators have index zero. By contrast, it is known that EAE and SC coincide for Fredholm operators acting on isomorphic Banach spaces
[
28
, Proposition 6.1(iv)]
.

These results motivated the present work, in which we study EAE and SC for Fredholm operators without imposing any restrictions on the underlying Banach spaces. Further justification for focussing on Fredholm operators comes from the prominent role this class plays in many applications of the theory, as the following studies from the last decade evidence:
diffraction problems
[
15
,
48
]
; Wiener–Hopf factorization
[
46
,
26
]
and
invertibility of Wiener–Hopf plus Hankel operators
[
16
]
;
truncated Toeplitz operators
[
14
,
35
]
;
Riemann–Hilbert problems
[
13
]
;
Helmholtz and Sylvester equations
[
45
,
47
]
and
[
17
]
, respectively;
completeness theorems for integral and differential operators
[
33
]
;
and problems concerning electrical networks
[
10
]
.

Before we state our main results, let us introduce some notation and terminology, most of which is standard.
We follow the convention that
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces, either real or complex, with
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
denoting the scalar field. The term “operator” means a bounded linear map between Banach spaces, and
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
denotes the Banach space of all operators from
{\mathcal{X}}
to
{\mathcal{Y}}
. As usual,
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
is abbreviated
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
. This convention applies whenever we consider sets of operators: Once a subset
\Sigma({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
of
\mathscr{B}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
has been defined, we write
\Sigma({\mathcal{X}})
instead of
\Sigma({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
.

The identity operator on a Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
is denoted by
I_{{\mathcal{X}}}
, while the kernel and the range of an operator
T
are denoted by
\ker T
and
\operatorname{ran}T
, respectively. Two Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are
isomorphic,
written
{\mathcal{X}}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}
, if
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
contains a bijection, called an
isomorphism
. The Banach Isomorphism Theorem ensures that the inverse of an isomorphism is automatically bounded.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}})
. We say that:

(i)

U
and
V
are
equivalent after extension
, abbreviated EAE, if there exist Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}
and isomorphisms
E\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{0},{\mathcal{X}}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{0})
and
F\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{0},{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{0})
such that

(1.1)

\begin{bmatrix}U&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}\end{bmatrix}=E\begin{bmatrix}V&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{0}}\end{bmatrix}F.

(ii)

U
and
V
are
Schur coupled
, abbreviated SC,
if there exist isomorphisms
A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
D\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}})
and operators
B\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
C\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that

(1.2)

U=A-BD^{-1}C\quad\mbox{and}\quad V=D-CA^{-1}B.

As noted above, whenever
U
and
V
are SC, they are also EAE. Motivated by many applications in which the converse implication holds, Bart and Tsekanovskiĭ asked the following question in
[
9
]
:

Question 1.2
.

Under which conditions on the Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
and/or on the operators
U
and
V
is it true that whenever the operators
U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}})
are EAE, they are also SC?

We shall address this question in the case where
U
and
V
are Fredholm operators. Before doing so, let us recall some basic facts about this class of operators. An operator
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is called a
Fredholm operator
if the quantities

\alpha(T)=\dim\ker T\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta(T)=\dim{\mathcal{Y}}/\operatorname{ran}T

are both finite. The latter condition implies that
\operatorname{ran}T
is closed. As usual, we write
\Phi({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for the subset of
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
consisting of Fredholm operators. The
index
of a Fredholm operator
T
is defined by

i(T)=\alpha(T)-\beta(T)\in\mathbb{Z},

and for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
denotes the set of
T\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
i(T)=k
.

In the 1990s, Bart and Tsekanovskiĭ gave the following characterization of equivalence after extension for Fredholm operators; see
[
8
, Theorem 4]
, and also
[
6
, Theorem 6, page 211]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}})
for some Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
.

(i)

Suppose that
U
and
V
are EAE. Then
U
is a Fredholm operator if and only if
V
is a Fredholm operator.

(ii)

Suppose that
U
and
V
are Fredholm operators.
Then
U
and
V
are EAE if and only if

\alpha(U)=\alpha(V)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta(U)=\beta(V).

In particular, Fredholm operators which are EAE have the same index.

As a consequence, the following sets, defined for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and every pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, provide the natural setting in which to study
1.2
for Fredholm operators:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\{(U,V)\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}):U\ \text{and}\ V\ \text{are EAE}\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\{(U,V)\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}):U\ \text{and}\ V\ \text{are SC}\}.

In view of
1.3
(ii)
, the former set can alternatively be written as

(1.4)

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\{(U,V)\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}):\alpha(U)=\alpha(V)\}.

These sets are useful in our investigation because they allow us to express the statement that EAE and SC are equivalent for every pair of Fredholm operators of index
k
on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively, in the concise form

\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, where we note that the inclusion
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is always true because SC implies EAE.

Using this notation, we can state easily two important results that motivated our work. First,
the answer to
1.2
is always affirmative for Fredholm operators of index
0
(see
[
8
, Theorem 3]
and
[
6
, Theorem 5]
). In the above notation, this simply means that

(1.5)

\operatorname{SC}_{0}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{0}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

for every pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Second, we can state the seminal result of Ter Horst, Messerschmidt, Ran and Roelands
[
29
]
showing
that there are pairs of Fredholm operators which are EAE, but not SC. This requires the following piece of terminology.

Definition 1.4
.

A pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is
essentially incomparable
if
I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}})
for every
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Theorem 1.5
.

(i)

Let
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
be a pair of essentially incomparable Banach spaces.
Then
U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}})
are SC if and only if
(U,V)\in\operatorname{EAE}_{0}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(ii)

There exists a pair of essentially incomparable Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\quad\text{for every}\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

Hence EAE and SC are not equivalent for Fredholm operators of non-zero index on such Banach spaces.

An example is given by
{\mathcal{X}}=\ell_{p}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}=\ell_{q}
for
1\leqslant p<q<\infty
.

The significance of analyzing whether SC and EAE are equivalent for each value
k
of the Fredholm index separately will become clear from the next result, which is the first main outcome of our work. To state it concisely, we introduce a numerical index
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
as follows:
Set
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset\}
and then define

(1.6)

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\begin{cases}0\ &\text{if}\ \mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})\cap\mathbb{N}=\emptyset,\\
\min\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})\cap\mathbb{N}\ &\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, and let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces.

(i)

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
k
is a multiple of
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(ii)

Suppose that
k
is a multiple of
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Then
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})={\operatorname{EAE}}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
if and only if
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
.

(iii)

Suppose that
k
is not a multiple of
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Then
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})=\emptyset
or
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})=\emptyset
, and consequently
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\emptyset
.

The most remarkable part of
1.6
is the implication
\Leftarrow
in
(ii)
which, when written out, states that as soon as
one
pair of operators
(U,V)\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
is SC, then EAE and SC are equivalent for
all
pairs
(U,V)\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
. In other words, equivalence of EAE and SC for Fredholm operators depends only on the geometry of the underlying Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
and on the Fredholm index
k
, not on the Fredholm operators themselves.

In view of
1.6
(ii)
, it would be of great interest to establish a counterpart of
1.6
(i)
for SC.
In analogy with (
1.6
), we introduce the set

(1.7)

\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\}

and the associated index

(1.8)

\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\begin{cases}0\ &\text{if}\ \mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\cap\mathbb{N}=\emptyset,\\
\min\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\cap\mathbb{N}\ &\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Combining
1.6
(i)
with the inclusion
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, we see that
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
. By
1.6
(ii)
–
(iii)
, our main question — whether EAE and SC are equivalent for every pair of Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively — boils down to whether

\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
.
We address this question in the following proposition.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces. Then
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
if and only if
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

In general,
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=n\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and the following chain of inclusions holds:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\}

\displaystyle\subseteq\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\}=\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}}).

Remark 1.8
.

The first part of Proposition
1.7
implies that the second inclusion in (
1.9
) is an equality if and only if
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, in which case the first inclusion is also an equality. In fact, we do not know any instances where the first inclusion in (
1.9
) is proper, and we conjecture that it may always be an equality; see
Section

5
for a more detailed discussion of this question.

We conclude this overview of our main findings with some results that illustrate the values which the numerical indices
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
can take when various incomparability conditions are imposed on the Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
. This work is motivated by, and closely related to,
the seminal result of Ter Horst, Messerschmidt, Ran and Roelands that we stated in
1.5
.
We begin with a result whose first part is simply a restatement of
1.5
(i)
, while its second part contains
1.5
(ii)
as a special case, corresponding to
k_{0}=1
.

Theorem 1.9
.

(i)

Let
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
be a pair of essentially incomparable Banach spaces. Then
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
.

(ii)

For every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exists a pair of essentially incomparable Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}
.

1.9
(i)
immediately raises the question whether we can weaken the hypothesis that the pair
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is essentially incomparable without losing the conclusion that
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
. The most obvious, very modest weakening would be to assume that
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are projectively incomparable in the following sense.

Definition 1.10
.

A pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is
projectively incomparable
if no infinite-dimensional, complemented subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}
is isomorphic to a complemented subspace of
{\mathcal{Y}}
.

However, it turns out that this hypothesis is too weak to imply that
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
, as our next result will show. It also contains some information about the possible values of the indices
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Theorem 1.11
.

(i)

For every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a pair of projectively incomparable Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}
.

(ii)

For every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a pair of projectively incomparable Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=1
and
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}
.

1.11
is highly surprising because the difference between essential and projective incomparability is very subtle, as evidenced by the fact that it took nearly 30 years to find an example which distinguishes them. Indeed, Tarafdar
[
49
,
50
]
asked in 1972 whether projective incomparability implies essential incomparability, having noted that the converse is true, but it was not until 2000 that Aiena and González answered this question by giving a counterexample (see
[
2
, Proposition 3.7]
). It relied on a sophisticated Banach space constructed by Gowers and Maurey
[
25
]
. To the best of our knowledge, no simpler examples have subsequently been found.
We shall discuss the relationship between essential and projective incomparability in more detail in
Section

2
.

In view of
1.11
, let us consider what may happen when
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are
not
projectively incomparable. Then they contain isomorphic, infinite-dimensional complemented subspaces; that is,
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
admit decompositions of the form

(1.10)

{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}\quad\text{with}\quad{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2},

where
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
are infinite-dimensional. The next proposition answers the question when is
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
in the case where
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
are pairwise essentially incomparable? Its statement involves the greatest common divisor (gcd) of two quantities that could potentially both be
0
, in which case the gcd is not defined. We fix this issue by adopting the convention that
\gcd(0,0)=0
.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces that decompose as in (
1.10
), and suppose that each of the pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
is essentially incomparable. Then

(1.11)

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\gcd(\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}),\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})).

Suppose additionally that the pair
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
is essentially incomparable. Then

(1.12)

\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}).

In particular, EAE and SC coincide for all pairs of Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, i.e.,
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, if and only if
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
divides
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
.

Note that we do not demand that the isomorphic subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
are infinite-dimensional in
1.12
. Therefore part
(i)
of
1.9
appears as a special case of
1.12
corresponding to
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}={\mathcal{Y}}_{2}=\{0\}
, while the case where
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are isomorphic is obtained by taking
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}={\mathcal{Y}}_{1}=\{0\}
.

In analogy with
1.11
, it turns out that the second part of
1.12
is no longer true if we replace the hypothesis that the pair
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
is essentially incomparable with the weaker hypothesis that it is projectively incomparable.

Theorem 1.13
.

(i)

For every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}
such that:

(1)

The pair
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
is projectively incomparable.

(2)

The pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Z}})
and
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Z}})
are essentially incomparable.

(3)

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathcal{Z}})=0
.

(4)

The Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}
satisfy

\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}.

(ii)

For every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}
satisfying
(i)(1)
–
(i)(3)
above, and such that the Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}
satisfy

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=1
and
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}
.

Remark 1.14
.

Let us compare and contrast
1.13
with
1.12
. To align notation, note that
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}={\mathcal{Y}}_{2}={\mathcal{Z}}
.
1.13
(i)(2)
implies that (
1.11
) holds true. However, (
1.12
) fails for the pair
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
in both parts
(i)
and
(ii)
of
1.13
because they satisfy
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}\neq 0=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{Z}},{\mathcal{Z}})
. This difference is due to the fact that (
1.12
) requires that the pair
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
is essentially incomparable, but we only know that it is projectively incomparable in
1.13
.

Conclusion

Prior to this paper, at the level of general Banach spaces, the only conclusive results regarding the question whether EAE and SC coincide for Fredholm operators were that they do if the underlying spaces are isomorphic, and that there exist examples where they do not if the underlying spaces are essentially incomparable. We have shown that the result for essentially incomparable spaces does not carry over to projectively incomparable spaces (see Theorem
1.11
), despite the fact that the difference between these two incomparability notions is very subtle.

In the case where the Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
admit decompositions of the form (
1.10
) in which the subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\,(\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
are pairwise essentially incomparable, the question whether EAE and SC coincide for Fredholm operators is completely resolved in
1.12
; the answer can be expressed in terms of the values of the Fredholm index of operators on the subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
.
1.13
shows that, once again, this result does not carry over to projectively incomparable spaces; see
1.14
for details.

The question that remains is whether one can always find a decomposition of the form (
1.10
) in which the subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
are pairwise essentially incomparable. Unfortunately, this is not possible, even if we replace “essentially incomparable” with “projectively incomparable”, as we shall see in
2.8
and
2.10
.

The above results rely on the remarkable observation in
1.6
that, for Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, either no pair of operators
(U,V)\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
is SC, or a pair of this kind which is SC exists, in which case the set of all such pairs that are SC is the same as the set of all such pairs that are EAE. This means that the question whether EAE and SC coincide for Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
reduces to determining the sets of indices for which pairs of Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
with these particular indices that are EAE or SC, respectively, exist.

Our analysis of these sets led us to define the numerical indices
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
which satisfy that
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
if and only if EAE and SC coincide for all Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
. We have computed their values in various cases; see Theorems
1.9
,
1.11
and
1.13
.

2.
Incomparability notions for Banach spaces and their connection to operator theory

The notions of essential and projective incomparability of a pair of Banach spaces will play a key role in the final section of this paper, where Theorems
1.9
,
1.11
and
1.13
are proved. However, there are certain related notions and results that we shall require beforehand. For that reason, we survey the relevant material at this point.

The formal definitions of essential and projective incomparability were already given in Definitions
1.4
and
1.10
, respectively. We refer to
[
1
, Section 7.5]
for a much more comprehensive treatment of them than we can give here. Indeed, we shall consider only the aspects that we require later, namely their relationship and certain connections to operator theory. This will involve a third incomparability notion, which is stronger, older and arguably more “natural” than the other two. It is defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

A pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is
totally incomparable
if no closed, infinite-dimensional subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}
embeds isomorphically into
{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Totally incomparable Banach spaces are clearly projectively incomparable, and it is well known and not hard to see that the converse fails; for instance,
\ell_{2}
embeds into
L_{1}[0,1]
, but not complementably, so
L_{1}[0,1]
and
\ell_{2}
are projectively incomparable without being totally incomparable.

However, more is true, namely that essential incomparability lies between these two properties, in the sense that total incomparability implies essential incomparability, which in turn implies projective incomparability. The easiest way to explain this goes via the following two operator-theoretic notions, which will also be useful elsewhere in this work.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces. An operator
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is:

(i)

strictly singular
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
, every infinite-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
contains a unit vector
w
such that
\lVert Tw\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon
. In other words, the restriction of
T
to
{\mathcal{W}}
is not an isomorphism onto its range.

(ii)

inessential
if
I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}})
for every operator
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
.

We write
{\mathscr{S}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for the collections of strictly singular and inessential operators, respectively, from
{\mathcal{X}}
to
{\mathcal{Y}}
. They generalize the ideal
{\mathscr{K}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
of compact operators in several ways. The following remark lists the main properties that we require.

Remark 2.3
.

(i)

Every compact operator is strictly singular, and every strictly singular operator is inessential (see for instance
[
1
, Theorems 7.36 and 7.44]
or
[
43
, §26.7.3]
).

(ii)

The assignments
{\mathscr{S}}
and
{\mathscr{E}}
are closed operator ideals in the sense of Pietsch (see for instance
[
1
, page 388 and Theorem 7.5]
or
[
43
, Theorem 1.9.4, Proposition 4.2.7 and Section 4.3]
).

(iii)

For every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the class of Fredholm operators of index
k
is stable under inessential perturbations in the sense that
U+T\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
whenever
U\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
(see for instance
[
1
, Theorem 7.23]
).

Comparing Definitions
1.4
and
2.2
(ii)
, we see that a pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is essentially incomparable if and only if every operator from
{\mathcal{X}}
to
{\mathcal{Y}}
is inessential. Both definitions display an obvious lack of symmetry, which raises the question what happens if we replace the condition that
I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}})
with
I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS\in\Phi({\mathcal{Y}})
in either of them?
It turns out that it makes no difference because the following well-known elementary lemma implies that
I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}})
if and only if
I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS\in\Phi({\mathcal{Y}})
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for some Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
. Then
\ker(I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST)\cong\ker(I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS)
. Moreover, if
\operatorname{ran}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST)
and
\operatorname{ran}(I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS)
are closed, then
{\mathcal{X}}/\operatorname{ran}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST)\cong{\mathcal{Y}}/\operatorname{ran}(I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS)
.

Proof.

The earliest mention of the first part of this result that we know of is
[
42
, Satz 1]
, while both parts can be found in
[
41
, Sätze 2.4-A–2.5-A]
. Alternatively, the result follows from
[
6
, page 211, properties 1 and 6]
, see also
[
7
, Proposition 1]
, because the operators
I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST
and
I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS
are Schur coupled via
A=I_{\mathcal{X}}
,
B=S
,
C=T
and
D=I_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
∎

Using
2.2
and
2.3
(i)
, we can explain the relationship between total, essential and projective incomparability of a pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
as follows.

Remark 2.5
.

(i)

If
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are totally incomparable, then clearly every operator between
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
is strictly singular and therefore inessential, so
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are essentially incomparable.

(ii)

If
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are essentially incomparable, then they are also projectively incomparable. Indeed, suppose contrapositively that
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
contain isomorphic, complemented infinite-dimensional subspaces. Then it is easy to construct operators
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST
is a projection with infinite-dimensional kernel and therefore not a Fredholm operator. Hence
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are not essentially incomparable. We refer to
[
1
, Theorem 7.69]
and the paragraph following Definition 7.102 for further details.

The fact that
{\mathscr{S}}
and
{\mathscr{E}}
are operator ideals has the following important consequence, which we shall use repeatedly without further reference.
Suppose that we express an operator
T\colon{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
between two direct sums of Banach spaces as an operator-valued matrix in the usual way, that is,

T=\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad T_{ij}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{j},{\mathcal{Y}}_{i})\quad\text{for}\quad i,j\in\{1,2\}.

Then
T
is strictly singular (respectively, inessential) if and only if
T_{11}
,
T_{12}
,
T_{21}
and
T_{22}
are strictly singular (respectively, inessential).

We conclude this section by answering a natural question about
a pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
which is
not
projectively incomparable. This material will not play any role in the remainder of the paper; we have included it simply because the question is very natural in our context.
Negating the definition of projective incomparability, we see that
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
admit decompositions of the form (
1.10
),
where the isomorphic subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
are infinite-dimensional.
If the subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
fail to be projectively incomparable, then they
contain isomorphic, complemented, infinite-dimensional subspaces. One may wonder whether
all
such subspaces can somehow be “transferred” to
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
, respectively, leading to the following question:

Question 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces which are not projectively incomparable. Is it always possible to find decompositions of the form (
1.10
), where the subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
are projectively incomparable?

The answer to this question is “no”. We shall present two short examples showing this.
In the first, we consider Banach spaces which are
c_{0}
-direct sums of certain sequences of finite-dimensional Banach spaces. The formal definition is as follows. The
c_{0}
-direct sum
of a sequence
({\mathcal{X}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Banach spaces is

\Bigl(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\Bigr)_{c_{0}}=\bigl\{(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:x_{n}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\ (n\in\mathbb{N})\ \text{and}\ \lVert x_{n}\rVert\to 0\ \text{as}\ n\to\infty\bigr\},

endowed with the pointwise vector-space operations and with the norm given by
\lVert(x_{n})\rVert=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lVert x_{n}\rVert
.

Bourgain, Casazza, Lindenstrauss and Tzafriri
[
12
, §8]
classified the complemented subspaces of
\bigl(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
in certain cases, including the following result.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\ell_{1}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
or
{\mathcal{Z}}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
, and let
{\mathcal{W}}
be a complemented, infinite-dimensional subspace of
{\mathcal{Z}}
. Then either
{\mathcal{W}}\cong c_{0}
or
{\mathcal{W}}\cong{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{X}}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\ell_{1}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}=\bigl(\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\ell_{2}^{n}\bigr)_{c_{0}}
are decomposed as in (
1.10
), with
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
infinite-dimensional. Then
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}
. In particular
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
both contain a complemented subspace isomorphic to
c_{0}
, so they are not projectively incomparable.

Proof.

This follows immediately from
2.7
because
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are not isomorphic to each other or to
c_{0}
, so we must have
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}\cong c_{0}
.
∎

Our second example is similar, but uses only reflexive Banach spaces.
It relies on the following well-known, important properties of
L_{p}[0,1]
for
1<p<\infty
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
.

(i)

The Banach space
L_{p}[0,1]
is primary; that is, if it is decomposed into a direct sum of two closed subspaces, then (at least) one of them is isomorphic to
L_{p}[0,1]
.

(ii)

Let
q\in(1,\infty)
. Then
L_{p}[0,1]
contains a complemented subspace which is isomorphic to
L_{q}[0,1]
if and only if

q=p
or
q=2
.

Proof.

(i)
. This is shown in
[
4
]
.

(ii)
. This follows easily from
[
3
, Theorem 6.4.21]
.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}=L_{p}[0,1]
and
{\mathcal{Y}}=L_{q}[0,1]
for distinct
p,q\in(1,2)\cup(2,\infty)
, and suppose that
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are decomposed as in (
1.10
), with
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
infinite-dimensional. Then
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}
. In particular
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
both contain a complemented subspace isomorphic to
L_{2}[0,1]
, so they are not projectively incomparable.

Proof.

Since
L_{p}[0,1]
is primary, either
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{X}}
or
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{X}}
. However, the latter is impossible by
2.9
(ii)

because
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
, which is a complemented subspace of
L_{q}[0,1]
, where
q\notin\{2,p\}
. Therefore
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{X}}
. The proof that
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}
is similar.

Another application of
2.9
(ii)
shows that
L_{p}[0,1]
and
L_{q}[0,1]
both contain a complemented subspace which is isomorphic to
L_{2}[0,1]
. ∎

3.
Non-emptiness of the set
\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

The main purpose of this section is to prove the following characterization of the integers
k
for which the set
\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
defined in (
1.3
) is non-empty. This is a natural starting point for our investigation because when
\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is empty, it is obviously equal to its subset
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The following three conditions are equivalent for every pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and every
k\in\mathbb{Z}\colon

(i)

\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset
and
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
.

(ii)

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
.

(iii)

k\in\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
.

The proof of
3.1
is relatively simple, using only (
1.4
) and two lemmas that follow from basic Fredholm theory. Both of these lemmas are almost certainly known to specialists; we include their (short) proofs for completeness.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for some infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
. Then, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cap[i(T),\infty)
, there exists a finite-rank operator
R\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
\alpha(T+R)=m
.

Proof.

We consider three cases:

Case 1:
If
\alpha(T)=m
, then we can simply take
R=0
.

Case 2:
If
\alpha(T)>m
, set
n=\alpha(T)-m\in\mathbb{N}
, and note that
\beta(T)=\alpha(T)-i(T)\geqslant n
, so we can find an
n
-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{Z}}
of
{\mathcal{Y}}
such that
{\mathcal{Z}}\cap\operatorname{ran}T=\{0\}
. Take an operator
A\in{\mathscr{B}}(\ker T,{\mathcal{Y}})
with range
{\mathcal{Z}}
and a bounded linear projection
P
of
{\mathcal{X}}
onto
\ker T
, and define
R=AP\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Then
\ker(T+R)=\ker A
, which has dimension
\alpha(T)-\dim\operatorname{ran}A=m
.

Case 3:
If
\alpha(T)<m
, choose an
m
-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
such that
\ker T\subseteq{\mathcal{W}}
, and let
P\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})
be a projection onto
{\mathcal{W}}
. Then
\ker T(I_{\mathcal{X}}-P)={\mathcal{W}}
, so
R=-TP
has the required property.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The condition that
m\geqslant i(T)
is necessary in
3.2
because

\alpha(T+R)\geqslant i(T+R)=i(T)

for every finite-rank operator
R\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For every Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
, the set

\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset\}

is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The result is clear if
{\mathcal{X}}
is finite-dimensional because
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})=\{0\}
in this case. In general the set
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
contains
0
because
I_{\mathcal{X}}\in\Phi_{0}({\mathcal{X}})
. Moreover, it
is closed under addition and under multiplication by positive integers because
the Index Theorem implies that
ST\in\Phi_{k+m}({\mathcal{X}})
and
T^{n}\in\Phi_{mn}({\mathcal{X}})
for
S\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in\Phi_{m}({\mathcal{X}})
whenever
k,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

It remains to show that
-k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
whenever
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
. Suppose that
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
k\geqslant 0
,
3.2
implies that
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
contains a surjection
T
. Since
\ker T
is finite-dimensional, it follows that
T
has a right inverse, which must be a Fredholm operator of index
-k
. A similar argument works for
k\leqslant 0
, except that we find that
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
contains an injection which has a left inverse.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces. Then
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

3.4
implies that
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})
is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}
, which is a principal ideal domain, so
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})=n\mathbb{Z}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. It is clear from the definition (
1.6
)
of
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
that
n=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Elaborating on these ideas, we obtain an alternative formula for
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, which will be useful later. Indeed, for every Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
, the ideal
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
has a unique non-negative generator, which we shall denote by
\gamma({\mathcal{X}})
; in other words,
\gamma({\mathcal{X}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is the unique number such that
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})=\gamma({\mathcal{X}})\mathbb{Z}
.

It follows from elementary number theory that, for every pair of Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, the ideal
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})=\gamma({\mathcal{X}})\mathbb{Z}\cap\gamma({\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
is generated by the lowest common multiple (lcm) of
\gamma({\mathcal{X}})
and
\gamma({\mathcal{Y}})
, provided that we set
\operatorname{lcm}(n,0)=\operatorname{lcm}(0,n)=0
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Combining this result with
3.5
, we see that

(3.1)

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{lcm}(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}),\gamma({\mathcal{Y}})).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Conditions
(i)
and
(iii)
are equivalent because

\bigl(\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset\ \text{and}\ \Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\bigr)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})\ \Longleftrightarrow\ k\in\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z},

where the final bi-implication follows from
3.5
.

We complete the proof by showing that conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
are also equivalent.
Here, the implication
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i)
is obvious because
\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
by definition.
Conversely, suppose that
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
and
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
are both non-empty. If
{\mathcal{X}}
or
{\mathcal{Y}}
is finite-dimensional, then necessarily
k=0
, in which case
\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is non-empty because it contains
(I_{\mathcal{X}},I_{\mathcal{Y}})
. Otherwise we may apply
3.2
to find operators
U\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
such that
\alpha(U)=\alpha(V)
. This implies that

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is non-empty because it contains
(U,V)
by (
1.4
).
∎

Remark 3.7
.

The index
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

satisfies:
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
if and only if
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})=\emptyset
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
or
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})=\emptyset
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Indeed, the implication
\Leftarrow
is immediate from the definition of
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Conversely, suppose that
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset
and
\Phi_{m}({\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
for some
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the Index Theorem implies that
\Phi_{n}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset
and
\Phi_{n}({\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
for every common multiple
n\in\mathbb{N}
of
k
and
m
, so
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\geqslant 1
. (Alternatively, this follows from (
3.1
) because
\operatorname{lcm}(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}),\gamma({\mathcal{Y}}))=0
if and only if
\gamma({\mathcal{X}})=0
or
\gamma({\mathcal{Y}})=0
.)

3.1
naturally raises the question: What are the possible values of the index
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
? Obviously,
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
if
{\mathcal{X}}
or
{\mathcal{Y}}
is finite-dimensional. Theorems
1.9
(ii)
,
1.11
(i)
and
1.13
(i)

show that all non-negative integers can be realized as
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
satisfying various additional conditions. At this stage, let us use the index
\gamma({\mathcal{X}})
from
3.6
to verify that all non-negative integers can be realized as
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}})
. Although this example may appear simpler than the three above-mentioned theorems, ultimately they all rely on the same family of “exotic” Banach spaces constructed by Gowers and Maurey in
[
25
]
.

Example 3.8
.

We claim that, for every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exists an infinite-dimensional Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}_{k_{0}}
such that
\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{k_{0}})=k_{0}
. Consequently

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{k_{0}},{\mathcal{X}}_{k_{0}})=k_{0}
by (
3.1
), and
3.1
shows that

{\operatorname{EAE}}_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{k_{0}},{\mathcal{X}}_{k_{0}})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
k\in k_{0}\mathbb{Z}
.

To verify this claim for
k_{0}=0
, we require an infinite-dimensional Banach space on which all Fredholm operators have index
0
. Gowers and Maurey constructed such a Banach space in
[
24
]
. We shall encounter another space with this property in
6.8
.

For
k_{0}=1
, any Banach space which is isomorphic to its hyperplanes satisfies the claim. Virtually every infinite-dimensional Banach space known prior to 1990 has this property.

Finally, to see that the claim is true for every
k_{0}\geqslant 2
, we use a family of Banach spaces which Gowers and Maurey constructed in
[
25
, §(4.3)]
.
These Banach spaces will play a key role in
Section

6
, where
6.1
summarizes their main properties; we refer to
(iv)
for the particular result required at this point.

We conclude this section with two easy observations. The first is that the index
\gamma({\mathcal{X}})
, and therefore the associated quantity
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, is an isomorphic invariant in the following precise sense.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
be isomorphic Banach spaces. Then
\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1})=\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
.

Consequently, if
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
is another pair of isomorphic Banach spaces, then

\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}).

Proof.

Let
R\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
be an isomorphism. For each
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})
, we can take
T\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})
. The Index Theorem implies that
RTR^{-1}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
, so
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
. This proves that
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
. The opposite inclusion follows by interchanging
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
. Hence the ideals
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})
and
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
are equal, so they must have the same non-negative generator; that is,
\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1})=\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
.

The final clause is an immediate consequence of (
3.1
).
∎

Our second easy observation will be the key ingredient in the proof of the first part of
1.12
, as we shall show immediately after it.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
be a Banach space.

(i)

\gamma({\mathcal{X}})
divides
\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2}))
.

(ii)

Suppose that
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
are essentially incomparable. Then

(3.2)

\gamma({\mathcal{X}})=\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2})).

Proof.

(i)
. Suppose that
k_{j}\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{j})
for
j\in\{1,2\}
, and take
T_{j}\in\Phi_{k_{j}}({\mathcal{X}}_{j})
. Then we have

\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}&0\\
0&T_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k_{1}+k_{2}}({\mathcal{X}}),

so
k_{1}+k_{2}\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
. This shows that

(3.3)

\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})+\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}).

Recall that we have defined
\gcd(0,0)=0
. This ensures that the formula
m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z}=\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}
holds true for all values of
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Using it, we can rewrite the identity (
3.3
) as
\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2}))\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\gamma({\mathcal{X}})\mathbb{Z}
, which proves
(i)
.

(ii)
.
Suppose that the subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
are essentially incomparable. Then, for each
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
, we can take

T=\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}),

where
T_{12}
and
T_{21}
are inessential. In view of
2.3
(iii)
, this implies that

\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\ni T-\begin{bmatrix}0&T_{12}\\
T_{21}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&0\\
0&T_{22}\end{bmatrix},

which in turn means that
T_{11}\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}}_{1})
and
T_{22}\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
with

k=i(T_{11})+i(T_{22})\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})+\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{2}).

Hence
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})+\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})=\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
, and (
3.2
) follows.
∎

Proof of Equation (
1.11
).

Since the pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
are essentially incomparable,
3.10
(ii)
implies that

\gamma({\mathcal{X}})=\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2}))
and
\gamma({\mathcal{Y}})=\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{Y}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{Y}}_{2}))
, where we observe that
\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2})=\gamma({\mathcal{Y}}_{2})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
because
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
.
Therefore, applying (
3.1
) twice and using the standard identity

\operatorname{lcm}(\gcd(a,c),\gcd(b,c))=\gcd(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b),c)\qquad(a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}),

we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{lcm}(\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2})),\gcd(\gamma({\mathcal{Y}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2})))

\displaystyle=\gcd(\operatorname{lcm}(\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{1}),\gamma({\mathcal{Y}}_{1})),\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{2}))=\gcd(\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}),\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})),

as required.
∎

4.
The key technical theorem and the deduction of
1.6
from it

The main aim of this section is to prove the following theorem, which will be the key tool in the remainder of our investigation, and is arguably the most important new insight in the paper, despite the somewhat technical nature of conditions
(i)
–
(ii)
. Notably,

Theorem 4.1
.

The following four conditions are equivalent for every pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and every
k\in\mathbb{Z}\colon

(i)

There exist operators
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that

(ii)

There exist operators
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
.

(iii)

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and this set is non-empty.

(iv)

\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
.

The proof of

involves two lemmas. The first of these reformulates SC in a way that is much closer to condition

Lemma 4.2

Let

and

and
{\mathcal{Y}}

UM=I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST\qquad\text{and}\qquad VN=I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS.

Proof.

This is a straightforward verification. On the one hand, if the operators

and

), then

,

), and on the other, if

,

,

and
T

,

and

∎

Lemma 4.3

\alpha(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{1}T_{1}-R)=m.

Take a finite-dimensional subspace

of

such that
\operatorname{ran}R\subseteq{\mathcal{W}}
and

for some

, and let

denote the operator

regarded as a map into
{\mathcal{W}}

2.4
shows that

{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
are finite-dimensional, the operators
S_{2}=S-S_{1}
and
T_{2}=T-T_{1}
have finite rank, and

\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})\ni I_{\mathcal{Y}}-TS=I_{\mathcal{Y}}-T_{1}S_{1}-(T_{1}S_{2}+T_{2}S_{1}+T_{2}S_{2}),

where
T_{1}S_{2}+T_{2}S_{1}+T_{2}S_{2}
is a finite-rank operator. Hence

\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})\ni I_{\mathcal{Y}}-T_{1}S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\operatorname{ran}V}-T_{11}S_{11}&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}}\end{bmatrix},

which in turn implies that
I_{\operatorname{ran}V}-T_{11}S_{11}\in\Phi_{k}(\operatorname{ran}V)
. Therefore the operators
B_{1}=T_{11}
and
B_{2}=S_{11}
satisfy (
4.6
).

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
. Suppose that
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
, and take
(U,V)\in\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Then, by the result from
[
28
]
stated above, we can find operators
B_{1}\in{\mathscr{B}}(\operatorname{ran}U,\operatorname{ran}V)
and
B_{2}\in{\mathscr{B}}(\operatorname{ran}V,\operatorname{ran}U)
which satisfy (
4.6
). As before, take finite-dimensional subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, respectively, such that (
4.7
) is satisfied, and define

S=\begin{bmatrix}B_{2}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\colon{\mathcal{Y}}=\operatorname{ran}V\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\to\operatorname{ran}U\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{1}={\mathcal{X}}

and

T=\begin{bmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\colon{\mathcal{X}}=\operatorname{ran}U\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\to\operatorname{ran}V\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}={\mathcal{Y}}.

Then

I_{{\mathcal{Y}}}-TS=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\operatorname{ran}V}-B_{1}B_{2}&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}),

which shows that
(ii)
is satisfied.
∎

5.
Non-emptiness of
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and the proofs of Propositions
1.7
and
1.12

The aim of this section is to investigate the set of integers
k
for which there
exist
Schur-coupled operators
U\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
; that is,
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset

We follow a similar strategy to the one successfully employed in
Section

3
, beginning with a partial analogue of
3.4
for the set
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\}
defined in (

As we shall see, the situation for SC is considerably more complicated than for EAE, primarily due to the difficulty of analyzing the technical conditions
(i)
–
(ii)
in
4.1
.
In particular,
we have been unable to obtain an exact counterpart of

for SC because we do not know if the set

is always closed under addition.

Lemma 5.1
.

The set
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
has the following properties for every pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\colon

(i)

0\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(ii)

\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(iii)

km\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for every
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(iv)

Suppose that

\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\in\{0,\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\}
.
Then
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The first two properties are easy to verify. Indeed,
(i)
follows from the fact that
(I_{\mathcal{X}},I_{\mathcal{Y}})\in\operatorname{SC}_{0}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, while
(ii)
follows from
(i)
if

\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
, and otherwise from the definition (
1.8
) of
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

However, the proof of
(iii)
requires more work.
Take
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. By
(i)
, we may suppose that
k\neq 0
and
m\neq 0
. Since
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
,
4.1
implies that we can find operators
S_{1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that

I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{1}T_{1}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
.
We claim that there exist operators
S_{m}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{m}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that

(5.1)

I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{m}T_{m}\in\Phi_{km}({\mathcal{X}}).

Once we have established this claim, the conclusion will follow from another application of
4.1
.

We prove the claim by considering three different cases:
m\geqslant 2
,
m=-1
and
m\leqslant-2
. (Note that the case
m=1
is already covered by the choice of
S_{1}
and
T_{1}
.)

Case 1.
For
m\geqslant 2
, we can apply the Binomial Theorem because
I_{\mathcal{X}}
and
S_{1}T_{1}
commute. It shows that

(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{1}T_{1})^{m}=I_{\mathcal{X}}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(-S_{1}T_{1})^{j}=I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{1}T_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(-S_{1}T_{1})^{j-1},

so the Index Theorem implies that the operators
S_{m}=S_{1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{m}=T_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\binom{m}{j}(-S_{1}T_{1})^{j-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
satisfy (
5.1
).

Case 2.
For
m=-1
, we consider the cases
k>0
and
k<0
separately. For
k>0
,
4.3
implies that we can find operators
U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
S_{1}T_{1}-UV
is a finite-rank operator and
I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\alpha(I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV)=k
. Then
\beta(I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV)=0
, so
I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV
is a surjective Fredholm operator, and therefore it has a right inverse
R\in\Phi_{-k}({\mathcal{X}})
. Consequently

I_{\mathcal{X}}=(I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV)R=R-UVR,

which implies that
S_{-1}=-U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{-1}=VR\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
satisfy (
5.1
) because
I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{-1}T_{-1}=R\in\Phi_{-k}({\mathcal{X}})
.

The argument for
k<0
is very similar. In this case, we can apply
4.3
to find
U\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
S_{1}T_{1}-UV
is a finite-rank operator and
I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\alpha(I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV)=0
. Then, being an injective Fredholm operator,
I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV
has a left inverse
L\in\Phi_{-k}({\mathcal{X}})
, which implies that the operators
S_{-1}=-LU\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{-1}=V\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
satisfy

I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{-1}T_{-1}=L(I_{\mathcal{X}}-UV)+(LU)V=L\in\Phi_{-k}({\mathcal{X}}).

Case 3.
Finally, for
m\leqslant-2
, we apply the argument from Case 1 to the
-m^{\text{th}}
power of the operator
I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{-1}T_{-1}
, where
S_{-1}
and
T_{-1}
are the operators found in Case 2, to conclude that the operators
S_{m}=S_{-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{m}=T_{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{-m}\binom{-m}{j}(-S_{-1}T_{-1})^{j-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
satisfy (
5.1
).

(iv)
. First, if
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
, then the definition (
1.8
) of
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
implies that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\cap\mathbb{N}=\emptyset
, and therefore
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\{0\}=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
by
(i)
and
(iii)
.

Second, suppose that
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and call this number
k_{0}
. Then, by
(ii)
,
(iii)
and
3.5
, we have

k_{0}\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}\mathbb{Z},

which shows that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=k_{0}\mathbb{Z}
, as required.
∎

Proof of Proposition

.

We begin by verifying the chain of inclusions (

\displaystyle\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\}

(ii)

.

Next, to prove the first claim of Proposition

\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

Remark 5.2
.

To illustrate the applicability of our work thus far, let us explain how it leads to an explicit algorithm for deciding whether EAE and SC are equivalent for all pairs of Fredholm operators on a given pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(i)

Find, if possible, the least
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\neq\emptyset
and
\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
. This is
k_{0}=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(ii)

If no such
k\in\mathbb{N}
exists, then
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and EAE and SC are equivalent for all pairs of Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
by
1.7
. More precisely, we have

\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\ \begin{cases}\neq\emptyset\quad&\text{for}\ k=0\\
=\emptyset\quad&\text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.\end{cases}

(iii)

Otherwise choose any pair of Fredholm operators
(U,V)\in\Phi_{k_{0}}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k_{0}}({\mathcal{Y}})
with
\alpha(U)=\alpha(V)
(and hence
\beta(U)=\beta(V)
),
and decide whether
U
and
V
are SC.

(1)

If
U
and
V
are SC, then
1.7
implies that EAE and SC are equivalent for all pairs of Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, and

\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\ \begin{cases}\neq\emptyset\quad&\text{for}\ k\in k_{0}\mathbb{Z}\\
=\emptyset\quad&\text{for}\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus k_{0}\mathbb{Z}.\end{cases}

(2)

Otherwise EAE and SC are evidently not equivalent for all pairs of Fredholm operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
, as
(U,V)
is a concrete example of a pair which is EAE, but not SC.

Our next lemma is the counterpart of
3.9
for SC, showing that the set
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and hence the associated index
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, is an isomorphic invariant.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
,
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
be Banach spaces satisfying
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
. Then
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})=\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
, and
consequently
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
.

Proof.

Take isomorphisms
R\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
. For each
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
, we can find
U\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})
and
V\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
which are SC, so that there exist isomorphisms
A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1})
and
D\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
and operators
B\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{1})
and
C\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
such that (
1.2
) is satisfied. The Index Theorem implies that
RUR^{-1}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
SVS^{-1}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
, and it is easy to check that they are SC, using the operators
RAR^{-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
,
SDS^{-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
,
RBS^{-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{2},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
SCR^{-1}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
to verify (
1.2
).
This implies that
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
, so
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
. The opposite inclusion follows by interchanging
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
, and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
.

The final statement is immediate from the definition (
1.8
) of
\operatorname{sc}
.
∎

As another consequence of
5.1
, we obtain the following variant of
3.1
for SC.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces. Then the set
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition if and only if

\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The implication
\Leftarrow
is obvious because the set
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
is closed under addition. Conversely, suppose that
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition. Then, in view of
5.1
(i)
and
(iii)
, it is an ideal of
\mathbb{Z}
, so
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=m\mathbb{Z}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Combining this identity with the definition (
1.8
) of
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, we conclude that
m=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.
∎

5.4
is not entirely satisfactory because we have been unable to answer the following question.

Question 5.5
.

Is the set
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
closed under addition for every pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
?

We know that the answer to this question is “yes” in certain cases because
5.1
(iv)
shows that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition if
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=0
or
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. We can also obtain a positive answer to it by imposing suitable conditions on the Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
.
To state this result precisely, we require the following additional notation and terminology.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces.

(i)

Set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})=\{ST:S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}}),\,T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\}
.

(ii)

We say that a subset
\Sigma
of
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is
essentially closed under addition
if, for every pair of operators
U,V\in\Sigma
, there exists an inessential operator
R\in{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
U+V-R\in\Sigma
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces, and suppose
that at least one of the sets
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})
and
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\mathcal{Y}})
is essentially closed under addition.
Then
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})
is essentially closed under addition, and take
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Then by
4.1
, we can find operators
S_{j}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{j}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{j}T_{j}\in\Phi_{k_{j}}({\mathcal{X}})
for
j=1,2
. The Index Theorem shows that

(5.2)

\Phi_{k_{1}+k_{2}}({\mathcal{X}})\ni(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{1}T_{1})(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{2}T_{2})=I_{\mathcal{X}}-[S_{1}T_{1}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{2}T_{2})+S_{2}T_{2}].

Both of the operators
S_{1}T_{1}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{2}T_{2})
and
S_{2}T_{2}
belong to
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})
, so by
the hypothesis, we can find operators
S_{3}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
,
T_{3}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
and
R\in{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{X}})
such that
S_{1}T_{1}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{2}T_{2})+S_{2}T_{2}=S_{3}T_{3}+R
. Combining (
5.2
) with
2.3
(iii)
, we deduce that

\Phi_{k_{1}+k_{2}}({\mathcal{X}})\ni I_{\mathcal{X}}-[S_{1}T_{1}(I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{2}T_{2})+S_{2}T_{2}]+R=I_{\mathcal{X}}-S_{3}T_{3},

and therefore
\operatorname{SC}_{k_{1}+k_{2}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
by another application of
4.1
. This shows that
k_{1}+k_{2}\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, as required.

The case where
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\mathcal{Y}})
is essentially closed under addition is similar, just using condition
(ii)
in
4.1
instead of condition
(i)
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

The set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})
is closed under addition (without the need for any inessential perturbations) if the Banach space
{\mathcal{Y}}
contains a complemented subspace isomorphic to
{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}
. This result is “folklore”; it can for instance be found in
[
36
, the paragraph following Definition 3.6]
. Most “classical” Banach spaces
{\mathcal{Y}}
satisfy the even stronger condition that
{\mathcal{Y}}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}
. The two conditions are not equivalent because Gowers and Maurey
[
25
, §(4.4)]
have constructed a Banach space
{\mathcal{Y}}
which is isomorphic to its cube
{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}
, but not to its square
{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}
. Hence
{\mathcal{Y}}
contains a complemented subspace isomorphic to
{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}
without being isomorphic to it.

There are infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
{\mathcal{Y}}
which do not contain any complemented subspaces isomorphic to
{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}
. James’ quasi-reflexive Banach space, which will feature prominently in the next example, was the first such example.

Example 5.9
.

The purpose of this example is to show that the converse of
5.7
fails; that is, we shall construct Banach spaces
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
such that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition, but neither
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})
nor
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\mathcal{Y}})
are essentially closed under addition.
This construction relies heavily on the quasi-reflexive James spaces
{\mathcal{J}}_{p}
for
1<p<\infty
. These Banach spaces originate in James’ paper
[
32
]
, where only the case
p=2
was considered. Subsequently, Edelstein and Mityagin
[
18
]
observed that James’ methods and results carry over to arbitrary
p\in(1,\infty)
.
We require the following specific facts about this family of Banach spaces:

(i)

{\mathcal{J}}_{p}
is isomorphic to its hyperplanes for every
p\in(1,\infty)
, so
\gamma({\mathcal{J}}_{p})=1
.

(ii)

{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{J}}_{q},{\mathcal{J}}_{p})={\mathscr{K}}({\mathcal{J}}_{q},{\mathcal{J}}_{p})
for
1<p<q<\infty
by
[
40
, Theorem 4.5]
, and therefore
{\mathcal{J}}_{p}
and
{\mathcal{J}}_{q}
are essentially incomparable whenever
p,q\in(1,\infty)
are distinct.

(iii)

{\mathscr{K}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})={\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})\subseteq{\mathscr{W}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})
for every
p\in(1,\infty)
by
[
34
, Proposition 4.9]
, where
{\mathscr{W}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})
denotes the ideal of weakly compact operators on
{\mathcal{J}}_{p}
.

Berkson and Porta
[
11
, page 18]
for
p=2
and Edelstein and Mityagin
[
18
]
for general
p\in(1,\infty)
observed that
{\mathscr{W}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})
has codimension
1
in
{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})
, so we have a unital algebra homomorphism
\varphi\colon{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})\to\mathbb{K}
with
\ker\varphi={\mathscr{W}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})
. We shall in fact require the amplification of this homomorphism to the
2\times 2
matrices, that is, the unital algebra homomorphism
\varphi_{2}\colon M_{2}({\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p}))\to M_{2}(\mathbb{K})
given by

\varphi_{2}\left(\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
R_{21}&R_{22}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi(R_{11})&\varphi(R_{12})\\
\varphi(R_{21})&\varphi(R_{22})\end{bmatrix}.

We are now ready to begin our construction: Choose distinct numbers
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, and set
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{J}}_{p}\oplus{\mathcal{J}}_{p}\oplus{\mathcal{J}}_{q}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathcal{J}}_{p}\oplus{\mathcal{J}}_{q}\oplus{\mathcal{J}}_{q}
.

First, we observe that
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition. This follows immediately from the fact that
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
(i)
implies that we can take
R\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{J}}_{p})
. Then the operators

S=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}-R&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad T=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

satisfy

I_{{\mathcal{X}}}-ST=\begin{bmatrix}R&0&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\\
0&0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{q}}\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}),

so
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
by
4.1
, and therefore
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, as desired.

Second, we shall show the set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{Y}}({\mathcal{X}})

is not essentially closed under addition. Assume the contrary, and consider the operators

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}})

and

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}).

They both belong to
{\mathscr{G}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}({\mathcal{X}})
as the indicated factorizations show.
Therefore, by the hypothesis, we can find operators
S\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
U+V-ST\in{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{X}})
. By
(ii)
, we can write
S=S_{1}+S_{2}
and
T=T_{1}+T_{2}
, where

S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&0&0\\
S_{21}&0&0\\
0&S_{32}&S_{33}\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}&0\\
0&0&T_{23}\\
0&0&T_{33}\end{bmatrix},

and
S_{2}\in{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})
and
T_{2}\in{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. Since
{\mathscr{E}}
is an operator ideal, we deduce that

{\mathscr{E}}({\mathcal{J}}_{p}\oplus{\mathcal{J}}_{p})\ni\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\end{bmatrix}(U+V-S_{1}T_{1})\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\\
=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}T_{11}&S_{11}T_{12}\\
S_{21}T_{11}&S_{21}T_{12}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence, applying the algebra homomorphism
\varphi_{2}
from
(iii)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=\varphi_{2}\left(\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{J}}_{p}}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{2}\left(\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}T_{11}&S_{11}T_{12}\\
S_{21}T_{11}&S_{21}T_{12}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi(S_{11})\varphi(T_{11})&\varphi(S_{11})\varphi(T_{12})\\
\varphi(S_{21})\varphi(T_{11})&\varphi(S_{21})\varphi(T_{12})\end{bmatrix}.

However, this is impossible because the diagonal entries imply that
\varphi(S_{11})
,
\varphi(T_{11})
,
\varphi(S_{21})
and
\varphi(T_{12})
are all non-zero, but then the off-diagonal entries
\varphi(S_{11})\varphi(T_{12})
and
\varphi(S_{21})\varphi(T_{11})
are also non-zero. This contradiction proves that
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\mathcal{Y}})
cannot be essentially closed under addition.

Finally, a similar argument with
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
interchanged shows that the set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathcal{X}}({\mathcal{Y}})
is not essentially closed under addition.

Remark 5.10
.

The purpose of this remark is to summarize our knowledge about the values of
k\in\mathbb{Z}
for which the equation
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
holds true for a given pair of Banach spaces
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, and explain how this problem is related to
5.5
. Recall from (
1.9
) and
1.6
(iii)
that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset\quad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}),

\displaystyle\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\emptyset\quad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}.

We now split in two cases, beginning with the case where the answer to
5.5
is affirmative, so that the set
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under addition. Then
5.4
shows that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}
and

\emptyset=\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\quad\text{for}\quad k\in\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}),

where we have applied
3.1
to conclude that
\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
.
Together with (
5.3
), this covers all possible values of
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. It follows in particular that EAE and SC coincides for all pairs of Fredholm operators
(U,V)\in\Phi({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi({\mathcal{Y}})
if and only if
\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, as we have already seen in
1.7
.

Otherwise, when the answer to
5.5
is negative, so that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
fails to be closed under addition,
1.7
implies that
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=n\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
for some
n\geqslant 2
, and

\emptyset=\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

for every
k\in\{\pm m\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}}):1\leqslant m<n\}
because
k=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is the smallest positive number for which
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset
, and
\mathbb{I}_{\text{SC}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is closed under sign changes. However, since
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
fails to be closed under addition, there must be some number
k=m\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, where
m\in\mathbb{N}\cap(n,\infty)\setminus n\mathbb{N}
, for which

\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{EAE}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\neq\emptyset.

We conclude this section with a couple of results about direct sums which will be useful in the proof of
1.12
, as well as in the next section.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces, and suppose that
{\mathcal{Y}}
is isomorphic to a complemented subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Then

(5.4)

\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}}),

and consequently
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

Proof.

In view of Lemmas
3.9
and
5.3
, we may suppose that
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}
for some Banach space
{\mathcal{Z}}
.

The inclusion
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})
is clear because
\operatorname{SC}_{k}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\times\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
.

Conversely, for
k\in\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})
, we can take
R\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})
. Then the operators

S=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}}-R\\
0\end{bmatrix}\colon\ {\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}={\mathcal{X}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad T=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}}&0\end{bmatrix}\colon\ {\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{Y}}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}

satisfy

(5.5)

I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST=\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}}&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{Z}}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}}-R&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{Z}}\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}),

so
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
by
4.1
. This shows that
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})
, while
the inclusion
\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})
is an immediate consequence of (
5.5
). (Alternatively, the latter inclusion follows easily from
3.10
(i)
.)

Finally, we have
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{eae}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
because (
5.4
) shows that

\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{X}})\cap\mathbb{I}_{\Phi}({\mathcal{Y}})
.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
be Banach spaces. Then:

(i)

\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})\subseteq\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(ii)

Suppose that each of the pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
,
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
and
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
is essentially incomparable. Then
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
.

Proof.

(i)
. For each
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
, we can take Schur-coupled operators
U\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
V\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
. Choose
isomorphisms
A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
D\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
and operators
B\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}_{2},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
C\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
such that (
1.2
) is satisfied. Then it is easy to see that the operators

\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{1}}&0\\
0&U\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}}&0\\
0&V\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{Y}})

are Schur-coupled via

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{1}}&0\\
0&U\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{1}}&0\\
0&A\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}}&0\\
0&D^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&C\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}}&0\\
0&V\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}}&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&C\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{1}}&0\\
0&A^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix},

so we conclude that
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
.

(ii)
. Suppose that
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
. By
4.1
, we can find operators

S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\
S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{Y}},{\mathcal{X}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad T=\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

such that
I_{{\mathcal{X}}}-ST\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})
. The hypothesis implies that
S_{11}
,
T_{11}
,
S_{12}
,
T_{12}
,
S_{21}
and
T_{21}
are inessential.
Since
{\mathscr{E}}
is an operator ideal, it follows that the operator

ST-\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&S_{22}T_{22}\end{bmatrix}

is inessential, and therefore, using
2.3
(iii)
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\mathcal{X}}-ST\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})\

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \begin{bmatrix}I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{1}}&0\\
0&I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{2}}-S_{22}T_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{2}}-S_{22}T_{22}\in\Phi_{k}({\mathcal{X}}_{2}),

so
k\in\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
by another application of
4.1
.
∎

We can now complete the proof of
1.12
.

Proof of Proposition
1.12
.

We have already proved Equation (
1.11
) on page
3
.

To prove (
1.12
), suppose that each of the pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
,
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
and
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
is essentially incomparable. Then the hypothesis of

5.12
(ii)
is satisfied because
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\cong{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
, so
\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\mathbb{I}_{{\text{SC}}}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
, and therefore
\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})=\operatorname{sc}({\mathcal{X}}_{2},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
. Moreover,
5.11
implies that

, which completes the proof of (

).

Combining (
1.11
) and (

), we see that

if and only if

6.
The Gowers–Maurey–Aiena–González–Ferenczi cycle of ideas and the proofs of Theorems
1.9
,
1.11
and
1.13

The Banach space that Aiena and González used in
[
2
]
to show that projective incomparability does not imply essential incomparability is the so-called “shift space” constructed by Gowers and Maurey in
[
25
, §(4.2)]
.
Refining the approach of Aiena and González, Ferenczi
[
20
, Section 4]
has more recently used this space to prove that there is no largest proper operator ideal, thereby solving a famous open problem going back to Pietsch’s monograph
[
43
]
.

The proofs of Theorems
1.11
and
1.13
are inspired by this body of work, notably the proof of
6.3
(ii)
. However, the shift space itself will not suffice for our purposes; we need to work with a larger family of “higher-order shift spaces” which Gowers and Maurey also constructed in
[
25
]
. We shall now give a brief introduction to this family.

Following the terminology introduced in
[
25
, page 549]
, for two infinite subsets
A=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots\}
and
B=\{b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots\}
of
\mathbb{N}
, we define the associated
spread

S_{A,B}
to be the linear map on
c_{00}
determined by

S_{A,B}e_{j}=\begin{cases}e_{b_{k}}\ &\text{if}\ j=a_{k}\ \text{for some}\ k\in\mathbb{N},\\
0\ &\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the standard unit vector (Hamel) basis for
c_{00}
.
Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and set
\mathscr{M}_{k_{0}}=\{[k_{0}m+1,\infty)\cap\mathbb{N}:m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
.
Then

\mathscr{S}_{k_{0}}=\{S_{A,B}:A,B\in\mathscr{M}_{k_{0}}\}

is a “proper set of spreads” as defined in
[
25
, page 549]
, but since we do not need the precise definition of this term in the sequel, we omit the details. The important point is that, by
[
25
, Theorem 5]
,
\mathscr{S}_{k_{0}}
induces a Banach space, which we shall call the
k_{0}
-fold Gowers–Maurey shift space
and denote by
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
. (It is denoted
X(\mathscr{S}_{k_{0}})
in
[
25
]
.)

As already mentioned, Gowers and Maurey defined and investigated this family of Banach spaces in
[
25
]
. More precisely, they studied the space
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(0)
in
[
25
, §(4.1)]
,
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(1)
in
[
25
, §(4.2)]
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(2)
in
[
25
, §(4.3)]
, before outlining the general case of
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
for
k_{0}\geqslant 3
in the final paragraph of
[
25
, §(4.3)]
. The following theorem summarizes the results from
[
25
]
that we require, together with the necessary notation and terminology.

Theorem 6.1

(Gowers and Maurey)
.

Let
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(i)

The Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
has a normalized Schauder basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
which admits an isometric
k_{0}
-fold right shift operator
R_{k_{0}}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0}))
given by
R_{k_{0}}e_{n}=e_{n+k_{0}}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with left inverse
L_{k_{0}}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0}))
given by
L_{k_{0}}e_{n}=0
for
n\leqslant k_{0}
and
L_{k_{0}}e_{n}=e_{n-k_{0}}
for
n>k_{0}
.

(ii)

The Banach space

{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
satisfies a lower
f
-estimate for the function
f(t)=\log_{2}(t+1);
that is,

\log_{2}(n+1)\,\biggl\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\biggr\rVert\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lVert x_{k}\rVert

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and vectors
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
which are consecutive in the sense that there are integers
0\leqslant m_{0}<m_{1}<\cdots<m_{n}
such that
x_{k}\in\operatorname{span}\{e_{j}:m_{k-1}<j\leqslant m_{k}\}
for each
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
.

(iii)

The Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
is indecomposable; that is, every complemented subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
is either finite-dimensional or finite-codimensional.

(iv)

The index
\gamma
introduced in Remark
3.6
is given by
\gamma({\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0}))=k_{0}
, and

{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
is not isomorphic to any of its subspaces of infinite codimension.
Therefore a closed subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
if and only if

\dim{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})/{\mathcal{W}}\in k_{0}\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(v)

The Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
contains no unconditional basic sequences.

Proof.

Parts
(i)
and
(ii)
follow from
[
25
, Theorem 5]
and the definitions and conventions that it relies on.

For
k_{0}=0
, parts
(iii)
–
(v)
are all derived in
[
25
, §(4.1)]
. (Note in this context that
L_{0}=R_{0}=I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(0)}
.) Hence it remains to consider
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)
. This result is contained in the proof of
[
25
, Theorem 13]
for
k_{0}=1
, with
[
25
, Remarks, page 559]
explaining how to generalize that proof to arbitrary
k_{0}\geqslant 2
.

(iv)
. This result is a restatement of
[
25
, Theorem 16]
for
k_{0}=1
. For
k_{0}\geqslant 2
, it follows from
[
25
, Theorem 19]
and
[
25
, Remarks, page 559]
.

(v)
. This result is proved in the final paragraph on
[
25
, page 567]
.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{Y}}
be a Banach space with an unconditional basis. Then, for every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
are totally incomparable.

Proof.

As explained in the comment after
[
39
, Problem 1.d.5]
, every closed, infinite-dimensional subspace of
{\mathcal{Y}}
contains an unconditional basic sequence. Hence

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

.

(i)

Suppose that

Remark 6.4
.

6.3
(i)
is also true for

(even though it may be debatable whether this space has an unconditional basis). This observation will be important in the proofs of Theorems

(i)

and
1.13
(i)

Proof of Lemma
6.3
(i)
.

To unify notation, set
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}={\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
. The proof is by contradiction, so assume that
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
contains an infinite-dimensional, complemented subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
which is isomorphic to a complemented subspace
{\mathcal{Z}}
of
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Y}}_{2}
.
6.2
shows that each of the pairs
({\mathcal{X}}_{1},{\mathcal{X}}_{2})
and
({\mathcal{Y}}_{1},{\mathcal{Y}}_{2})
is totally incomparable, so a theorem of Edelstein and Wojtaszczyk (see

, Theorem 3.5]
, or

, Theorem 2.c.13]
for an exposition) implies that
{\mathcal{W}}\cong{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}\cong{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}
, where
{\mathcal{W}}_{j}
and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{j}
are complemented subspaces of
{\mathcal{X}}_{j}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{j}
, respectively, for
j\in\{1,2\}
.
Take an isomorphism

U=\begin{bmatrix}U_{11}&U_{12}\\
U_{21}&U_{22}\end{bmatrix}\colon{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}_{2}\to{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{Z}}_{2}.

The hypothesis that
{\mathcal{X}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
are essentially incomparable implies that the operator
U_{11}
is inessential because essential incomparability clearly passes to complemented subspaces. Moreover,
U_{12}
and
U_{21}
are strictly singular and therefore inessential by
6.2
and
2.5
(i)
. Consequently

\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&U_{22}\end{bmatrix}=U-\begin{bmatrix}U_{11}&U_{12}\\
U_{21}&0\end{bmatrix}

is an inessential perturbation of the isomorphism
U
and hence a Fredholm operator. This implies that
U_{22}
is a Fredholm operator and that
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
is finite-dimensional, so
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
must be infinite-dimensional. Since it is complemented in
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
,
6.1
(iii)
shows that
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
has finite codimension in
{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
.

Choose a closed subspace
{\mathcal{W}}_{3}
of finite codimension in
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
such that

{\mathcal{W}}_{3}\cap\ker U_{22}=\{0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dim{\mathcal{X}}_{2}/{\mathcal{W}}_{3}\in k_{0}\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Then
{\mathcal{W}}_{3}\cong{\mathcal{X}}_{2}
by
6.1
(iv)
, and the restriction of

to

is an isomorphic embedding into

shows that no such embedding exists because

has infinite codimension in

∎

In order to prove the second part of
6.3
, we require two lemmas. The statement of the first of these involves the following standard piece of terminology. An operator
T\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
(where
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
can be any Banach spaces) is
bounded below
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lVert Tx\rVert\geqslant\varepsilon\lVert x\rVert
for every
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
. This is equivalent to saying that
T
is injective and has closed range, or in other words that
T
is an isomorphic embedding.

Lemma 6.5
.

For every
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}}\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0}))
is injective, but not bounded below. Consequently its range is not closed in
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
.

Proof.

The proof is a simple variant of an argument given by Ferenczi in
the text preceding
[
20
, Proposition 16]
. First, to show that

I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}}
is injective, suppose that

x=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}e_{j}\in\ker(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}})
. Then we
have

0=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j+k_{0}}e_{j},

so
a_{j}=a_{j+k_{0}}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
. By induction, we deduce that
a_{j}=a_{j+mk_{0}}
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Keeping
j
fixed and letting
m\to\infty
, we have
a_{j+mk_{0}}\to 0
, so
a_{j}=0
. Since this is true for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we conclude that
x=0
.

Second, to verify that
I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}}
is not bounded below, we consider the vector
w_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})
for
n>k_{0}
.
6.1
(ii)
implies that

\log_{2}(n+1)\,\lVert w_{n}\rVert\geqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lVert e_{j}\rVert=n,

while

\lVert(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}})w_{n}\rVert=\biggl\|\sum_{j=n-k_{0}+1}^{n}e_{j}\biggr\|\leqslant k_{0},

so

\frac{\lVert(I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}})w_{n}\rVert}{\lVert w_{n}\rVert}\leqslant\frac{k_{0}\log_{2}(n+1)}{n}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Consequently
I_{{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{GM}}}(k_{0})}-L_{k_{0}}
is not bounded below.
∎

The other lemma that we require originates in the work of Lebow and Schechter
[
37
, Theorem 5.4]
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
be Banach spaces, and suppose that
A\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is an operator whose range is not closed. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a nuclear operator
B\in{\mathscr{B}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
such that
\lVert B\rVert<\varepsilon
and the closure of the range of the operator
A-B
has infinite codimension in
{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Proof.

One can be prove this lemma by following the steps of the proof of
[
37
, Theorem 5.4]
, starting in line 4 with the hypothesis that the range of the operator
A
is not closed. The only modification required is that to ensure that the nuclear operator
B=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(A^{\prime}y_{k}^{\prime})\otimes y_{k}
has norm less than
\varepsilon
, we must replace the third inequality in
[
37
, Equation (5.4)]
with the estimate
\lVert A^{\prime}y_{k}^{\prime}\rVert<\varepsilon/2^{k}a_{k}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
6.3
(ii)
.

Combining Lemmas
6.5
and
6.6
, we can find a nuclear operator

To show that

let

, and let

Remark 6.7
.

6.6
does not follow from the statement of
[

itself. In fact, Lebow and Schechter could have concluded their proof of

The proofs of the two parts of

are somewhat more complicated variants of the proofs of the corresponding parts of
1.11
given above. They involve one additional ingredient, namely Gowers’ solution to Banach’s hyperplane problem, which was the first infinite-dimensional Banach space shown not to be isomorphic to its hyperplanes (see
[
23
]
, as well as
[
25
, §(5.1)]
for further results). The following result summarizes the properties of this space that we require.

Theorem 6.8

(Gowers)
.

There exists an infinite-dimensional, reflexive Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
with an unconditional basis such that
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
fails to be isomorphic to any proper subspace of itself.

Proof.

The only part of this statement that Gowers did not prove explicitly in
[
23
]
is that
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
is reflexive. We believe that this fact is known to specialists, but as we have been unable to locate a proof of it in the literature, we outline one here. Since
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
is not isomorphic to its hyperplanes, it cannot contain any complemented subspace which is isomorphic to its hyperplanes, so in particular no complemented subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
or
\ell_{1}
. Hence, no subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
is isomorphic to
c_{0}
by Sobczyk’s Theorem (see,
e.g.,

[
39
, Theorem 2.f.5]
) or to
\ell_{1}
by a much more recent theorem of Finol and Wójtowicz
[
21
]
. (This result was previously stated without proof in
[
38
]
.) Therefore, a classical result of James (see
[
32
]
, or
[
39
, Theorem 1.c.12(a)]
for an exposition) shows that
{\mathcal{X}}_{{\text{G}}}
is reflexive.
∎
