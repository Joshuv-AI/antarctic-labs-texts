Continued Fractions and Orthogonal Polynomials in Several Variables Thanks: The second author was partially supported by Simons Foundation Grant #849676
Tomas Sauer, Yuan Xu

Abstract
. We extend the close interplay between continued fractions, orthogonal polynomials, and Gaussian quadrature rules to several variables in a special but natural setting which we characterize in terms of moment sequences. The crucial condition for the characterization is the commutativity of the multiplication operators on finite polynomial subspaces modulo an ideal. Moreover, starting from the orthogonal polynomials or the three-term recurrence, our method constructs a sequence of moment sequences that provides an approximation of the maximal order for recovering the moment sequence that defines the orthogonality.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
41A21, 42C05, 65D15, 65D32

1.
Introduction

It is well-known that continued fractions are closely related to orthogonal polynomials
and both, in turn, are related to the Gaussian quadrature rules. The interrelation
between the three is elegant and revealing. We are interested in a possible extension
of such relations in several variables. A moment of reflection,
however, shows several obvious
obstacles to extending from one variable to the multi-variable setting. As a start,
it is not even clear how to define a continued fraction expansion
utilizing multivariate orthogonal polynomials.

In the literature, there exist already several generalizations of
multivariate continued fractions, especially in terms of partitions
and lattices; see, for example,
[
9
,
20
]
.
Our approach will be fundamentally different and focus on rational
approximations of formal power
series based on polynomials defined by a three-term recurrence formula.
This is in the spirit of Gauss’ original approach when developing Gaussian quadrature
formulas from
[
5
]
. Therefore, it is natural that our
extension will be based on the three-term recurrences in
d

variables, which then consist of
d
relations, one for each
coordinate multiplication. They are known for multivariate orthogonal
polynomials. The
matrix

coefficients of the three-term relations will be embedded into an
appropriate definition
of continued fractions, which, however, can only give a complete
extension of the univariate case when there is an appropriate
extension of the Gaussian quadrature rules to several variables. The latter relies on
the existence of the maximal number of
common
zeros of orthogonal
polynomials of the same degree, which may or may not exist. Thus, the ideal
generated by all such orthogonal polynomials of the same total degree must play
a fundamental role, which translates to special structures of the matrix coefficients
and to the moment sequences that define the orthogonality. This approach is
fundamentally different from the ones found in
[
9
,
20
]
and therefore
yields yet another generalization of continued fractions to several variables, a
generalization that is natural in our context.

Our main result shows that there is a natural and complete extension
to several variables whose existence can be characterized in
terms of the moment sequence.

In one direction, we start with
a moment functional
{\mathcal{L}}
, defined via a moment sequence
\mu
in

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and assume that the moment
functional
{\mathcal{L}}
is
definite
so that orthogonal polynomials defined
via
{\mathcal{L}}
exist. These polynomials then
satisfy three-term relations which are characteristic properties of
orthogonality by Favard’s theorem. The
three-term relations define the Jacobi matrices, which are
representations of multiplication operators by
coordinates. Our main assumption is that the truncated Jacobi matrices
commute, which leads to the existence
of a maximal number of common zeros of orthogonal polynomials and then
to Gaussian cubature rules. If
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive
definite, this is how the Gaussian cubature rules are characterized in

[
4
]
. In the above narrative. however, we do not require anymore
that
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive definite.
This is in accordance with the well-known and classical theory of univariate
continued fractions and the fast convergence of the associated
convergents, see
[
16
]
, since the existence of a so-called

associated continued fraction
is equivalent to
the definiteness. Moreover, the positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
can even
be expressed in terms of the signs of the recurrence coefficients in
the univariate case. In several variables, it is well-known that the
issue of positivity
is significantly more complex, which is essentially due to the fact that
positive polynomials are no longer sums of squares (cf.
[
19
]
).
In the framework that we present here, many results have to be
reconsidered and modified. For example,
without positivity, the common zeros may not be real or simple anymore. As a result,
it is necessary to recast many of the results on the structure of OPs in several
variables for a functional
{\mathcal{L}}
that is only definite but not
necessarily positive definite, which we
shall carry out in some detail and provide proof whenever
necessary. In return, this framework enables us to give a definition
of continued fractions that can be regarded as an
extension from one to several variables.

In the other direction, we start with a polynomial sequence that satisfies three-term
relations and assume that their truncated multiplication operators commute.
By Favard’s theorem, these polynomials
are orthogonal with respect to a linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}
which can be regarded as a
moment functional defined by
\mu_{\alpha}={\mathcal{L}}(x^{\alpha})
. Using continued
fractions, we
can then define a sequence of moment sequences
\mu^{n}
and show that

\mu^{n}\to\mu
with an optimal rate in the sense that a maximal
number of terms in
\mu^{n}
coincides with those in
\mu
;
in other words, we can efficiently recover the moment sequence that
determines the orthogonality.
The main requirement for this setup lies in the commutativity of the
truncated Jacobi matrices, which can in turn be reduced to a
restriction on the moments.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we recall the
classical results of one variable,
which serves as a roadmap for our extension to several variables. In
the third section,
we provide a detailed account of orthogonal polynomials in several variables and
Gaussian cubature rules, where the results are presented and proved when necessary,
without assuming the positive definiteness. In the fourth section, the
multivariate

continued fraction expansion is defined and used to recover the moment sequence and
we provide a characterization of the moments for which such an extension is possible.

2.
Preliminary

We recall results in one variable in this section. To that end, let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a
linear moment functional, defined at least on the polynomials, and
let
\mu_{n}={\mathcal{L}}(x^{n})
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, denote the moments of

{\mathcal{L}}
. In particular, if
w
is a (not necessarily nonnegative) weight
function on
{\mathbb{R}}
, then

{\mathcal{L}}(f)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x

is such a moment functional. We assume that all
\mu_{n}
are finite and
call
{\mathcal{L}}
, or
w
,

definite
if the Hankel matrices

M_{n}:=\left[\mu_{k+j}:\begin{matrix}0\leq k\leq n\\
0\leq j\leq n\end{matrix}\right]

are nonsingular for all
n=0,1,\ldots
, and
positive definite
if, in addition, the matrices
are positive definite. If
w\neq 0
is nonnegative, then the Hankel
matrices are positive definite
for all
n
, so that
{\mathcal{L}}(f)
is positive definite. If
w
changes
sign on its domain, however,
the determinant of
M_{n}
could be zero for some
n
, in which case
orthogonal polynomials could
degenerate in their degrees; see, for example,
[
11
,
21
]
. Our
definition of definiteness avoids these degenerate cases.

A real polynomial
p_{n}
of degree
n
is called orthogonal with
respect to
{\mathcal{L}}
if

{\mathcal{L}}(p_{n}q)=0
for all
q
of degree at most
n-1
. It is known that
if
{\mathcal{L}}
is definite, then
the orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
exist and they satisfy a
three-term relation

(2.1)

p_{n+1}(x)=\left(a_{n}x+b_{n}\right)p_{n}(x)+c_{n}\,p_{n-1}(x),\qquad a_{n}c_{n}\neq 0,\qquad n\geq 0.

Moreover, this relation characterizes the orthogonality according to Favard’s theorem. For
{\mathcal{L}}
definite,
the polynomial
p_{n}
has
n
zeros counting multiplicity. If
{\mathcal{L}}
is
positive definite, the zeros are all real and simple. If
{\mathcal{L}}
is
definite but not positive definite, the zeros can be complex and
appear in conjugate pairs; that is, if
z
is a zero of
p_{n}
then so
is
\bar{z}
.

When
w
is positive definite, the zeros of
p_{n}
,
\{x_{k}:1\leq k\leq n\}
, are nodes of
the Gaussian quadrature rule,

(2.2)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{k}f(x_{k}),\qquad\deg f\leq 2n-1,

where
{\lambda}_{k}>0
. This remains true if
{\mathcal{L}}
is definite but not positive definite, and
p_{n}
and
p_{n+1}

do not have common zeros, for which
{\lambda}_{k}\neq 0
for
1\leq k\leq n
. The quadrature rule
(
2.2
) has been studied extensively. For signed weight function, it holds for all even

n
if
w
is a weight function on
[-1,1]
and
\det\left[\mu_{2(k+j)-1}\right]_{k,j=1}^{n}\neq 0
, for
example; see
[
6
, Section 3.1]
. Another interesting case is the oscillatory weight functions. Let

p_{n}(w)
denote the orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
with respect to
w
. The oscillatory weight
function
\widehat{w}
is defined by

\widehat{w}(t)=p_{m}(w;t)w(t),\qquad t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In this case, the moment
\mu_{k}(\widehat{w})=0
if
k<m
and
\mu_{m}(\widehat{w})>0
, so that the
m+1
Hankel
matrix satisfies
\det M_{m+1}>0
. In particular, the polynomial
p_{n}(\widehat{w})
,
n=m+1
, is well defined. Moreover,
it has all real and simple zeros in some cases; for example, when
w(t)=(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}
and
0<{\lambda}\leq 2
(see,
for example,
[
6
,
13
]
).

We are mostly interested in the case when
w
is definite and
p_{n}(w)
has distinct real simple zeros, which
holds whenever
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive definite. Below is an example when
{\mathcal{L}}
is definite but not positive definite.

Example 2.1
.

For
{\lambda}>-\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu>0
, we define the weight function

w_{{\lambda},\mu}(t)=t^{2\mu+1}(1-t)(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad-1<t<1.

Then
w_{{\lambda},\mu}
changes sign on
(-1,1)
. The orthogonal polynomial
p_{n}(w_{{\lambda},\mu})
has
n
simple,
real zeros in
[-1,1]
and they are nodes of the Gaussian quadrature of degree
2n-1
.

Indeed, let
C_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}
be the generalized Gegenbauer polynomials that are orthogonal with respect to
the weight function
|t|^{2\mu}(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}
on
[-1,1]
. These polynomials can be given explicitly in
terms of the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
(cf.
[
4
, Section 1.5.2]
). In particular,
up to a multiple constant,

\displaystyle C_{2n}^{(\lambda,\mu)}(t)=b_{n}P_{n}^{(\lambda-\frac{1}{2},\mu-\frac{1}{2})}(2t^{2}-1).

Then it can be easily verified (cf.
[
1
]
) that

p_{2n}(w_{{\lambda},\mu};t)=C_{2n}^{({\lambda},\mu+\frac{1}{2})}(t)\quad\hbox{and}\quad p_{2n+1}(w_{{\lambda},\mu};t)=(1+t)C_{2n}^{({\lambda}+1,\mu+\frac{1}{2})}(t)

for
n=0,1,2,\ldots
. Moreover, it is easy to see that, if
\mu
is an integer, then

\int_{-1}^{1}\left|p_{2n}(w_{{\lambda},\mu};t)\right|^{2}w_{{\lambda},\mu}(t)\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{-1}^{1}\left|C_{2n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}\right||t|^{2\mu+2}(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t<0.

The properties of the zeros of
p_{n}(w_{{\lambda},\mu})
follow from those of the zeros of the Jacobi polynomials. In particular,
it follows that
w_{{\lambda},\mu}
admits Gaussian quadrature rules of degree
2n-1
for all
n
.

The recurrence relation relates orthogonal polynomials closely to continued
fractions, a fact used by Gauss to obtain the quadrature rules in

[
5
]
. Indeed, defining the (infinite) continued fraction
as

r(x)=\frac{b_{1}|}{|a_{1}(x)}+\frac{b_{2}|}{|a_{2}(x)}+\frac{b_{3}|}{|a_{3}(x)}\cdots=\frac{b_{1}}{a_{1}(x)+\dfrac{b_{2}}{a_{2}(x)+\dfrac{b_{3}}{\ddots}}}.

Its
convergents
of order
n
, defined by

r_{n}(x)=\frac{b_{1}|}{|a_{1}(x)}+\frac{b_{2}|}{|a_{2}(x)}+\dots+\frac{b_{n}|}{|a_{n}(x)}=\frac{p_{n}(x)}{q_{n}(x)},

are rational functions, where the numerator
p_{n}
and denominator
q_{n}
are
polynomials and they satisfy a recurrence relation

\displaystyle p_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{n}(x)p_{n-1}(x)+b_{n}p_{n-2},\qquad p_{-1}=1,\,p_{0}=0,

\displaystyle q_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=a_{n}(x)p_{n-1}(x)+b_{n}p_{n-2},\qquad q_{-1}=0,\,q_{0}=1.

If the polynomials
a_{n}
are
affine polynomials
, i.e.,
a_{n}(x)=\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n}
, then the above recurrence is the classical

three-term recurrence relation
for orthogonal polynomials. In
fact, the polynomials

q_{n}

are
orthogonal polynomials provided that
\alpha_{n}\,c_{n}<0
.

A classical result in continued fractions says that a formal Laurent
series

\mu(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n-1}z^{-n}

has an
associated
continued fraction expansion, i.e.,
\mu(z)-r_{n}(z)=O\left(z^{2n}\right)
, if and only if

\det\left[\mu_{j+k}:\begin{array}[]{c}j=0,\dots,n\\
k=0,\dots,n\end{array}\right]\neq 0,\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

If
\mu
is the moment sequence associated with a definite linear
functional, then the denominator polynomials
q_{n}
are the respective
orthogonal polynomials and the maximal exactness of Gaussian quadrature
is eventually a consequence of the fact that the Laurent series for
the quadrature moments is
p_{n}/q_{n}
. This is the central idea in
Gauss’ original paper
[
5
]
.

Continued fraction expansions are closely related to
Prony’s problem

which consists of recovering a multi-exponential function

f(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}\,\zeta_{k}^{x},\qquad\zeta_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\},

from integer samples
f(n)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In fact, interpreting these
samples of
f
as the
moment sequence
\mu_{n}=f(n)
, the central connection is that the infinite

Hankel operator

\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{j+k}:j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}
has finite rank in this case and, by Kronecker’s
Theorem, the associated Laurent series
\mu(z)
is a rational
function, hence has a
finite
continued fraction
expansion. Moreover, the denominator of the rational function is the
so-called
Prony polynomial
whose zeros are exactly

\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{m}
.
The monograph
[
16
]
provides more details on the connection
between continued fractions and Prony’s problem, and how the continued
fraction expansion and the construction of the associated recurrence
coefficients can be used to solve Prony’s problem even numerically.

3.
Orthogonal polynomials and cubature rules

We now consider OPs in several variables with respect to a linear
functional
{\mathcal{L}}
or, equivalently, a moment sequence
\mu
.
As mentioned in the introduction, we shall need results on the structures of these
polynomials for
{\mathcal{L}}
being definite, but not necessarily positive definite. This
requires some modifications of the existing theory that has been
established for the positive definite case;
most of these modifications are straightforward but by no means all of
them. For example, the proof for the existence of a maximal number of
common zeros requires additional algebraic tools. For the record and
the reader’s convenience, we
will point out the modifications and provide proof whenever necessary.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a linear functional. For
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, let

\mu_{\alpha}={\mathcal{L}}(x^{\alpha})
denote the
{\alpha}
th moment of
{\mathcal{L}}
. We assume for
simplicity that
\mu_{0}={\mathcal{L}}(1)\neq 0
and that all moments
\mu_{\alpha}
are real-valued.
Let
\Pi_{n}^{d}
denote the space of polynomials of total degree at most
n
in
d

variables and let
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{d}
denote the space of homogeneous
polynomials of degree
n
. Then

r_{n}:=\dim\Pi_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d}{d}\quad\hbox{and}\quad r_{n}^{0}:=\dim{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{d-1}.

For
P\in\Pi_{n}^{d}
, we write
\widehat{P}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}}
for the
coefficient vector so that
P(x)=\widehat{P}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}=\sum_{\alpha}\widehat{P}_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}:=\left[x^{\alpha}:|\alpha|\leq n\right]
. We arrange

\widehat{P}
in a graded fashion as

\widehat{P}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{P}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{P}_{n}\end{array}\right],\qquad\widehat{P}_{j}:=\left[\widehat{P}_{\alpha}:|\alpha|=j\right],\quad j=0,\dots,n,

and decompose
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}
accordingly. The moment sequence
\mu
defines a
linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}
on
\Pi
by linearity as

{\mathcal{L}}(P)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq n}p_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha},\qquad P(x)=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq n}p_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\in\Pi_{n}^{d}.

A polynomial
P\in\Pi_{n}^{d}
is called orthogonal with respect to the linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}
if

{\mathcal{L}}(PQ)=0,\qquad\forall Q\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}.

In the usual setting of OPs in several variables, we assume that
{\mathcal{L}}
is (square) positive definite, which requires

{\mathcal{L}}(p^{2})>0
for all non-zero polynomials. This ensures the existence of an orthonormal basis of polynomials.
In this section, we state the structure of OPs in the general setting, which are known in the positive definite case,
but require some modification if
{\mathcal{L}}
is definite but not necessarily positive definite.

3.1.
Structure of orthogonal polynomials

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, let

M_{n}=\left[\mu_{{\alpha}+{\beta}}:\begin{matrix}|{\alpha}|\leq n\\
|{\beta}|\leq n\end{matrix}\right]

be the moment matrix of size
r_{n}\times r_{n}
. Clearly,

{\mathcal{L}}(PQ)=\widehat{P}^{\mathsf{T}}M_{n}\widehat{Q},\qquad P,Q\in\Pi_{n}^{d}.

We assume that the matrices
M_{n}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, are
non-singular, which ensures the existence of orthogonal polynomials. Indeed, the usual construction of OPs
from the moments (cf.
[
4
, Section 3.3.2]
) applies, which gives a monic OP of the form

P_{\alpha}(x)=x^{\alpha}+q_{\alpha},\qquad q_{\alpha}\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d},

for each
{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
be the space of OPs of degree
n
. Then
\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}=r_{n}^{0}
.
Let
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
be the column vector

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)=\left[P_{\alpha}:|{\alpha}|=n\right]=\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n},\qquad\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}:=\left[\hat{P}_{\alpha}:|{\alpha}|=n\right]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}\times r_{n}^{0}}.

Then
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
consists of a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
. Partitioning
M_{n}

as

M_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{n-1}&M_{n-1,n,}\\
M_{n-1,n}^{\mathsf{T}}&M_{n,n}\end{array}\right],

with

M_{n-1,n}=\left[\mu_{\alpha+\beta}:\begin{array}[]{c}|\alpha|\leq{n-1}\\
|\beta|=n\end{array}\right]\quad\hbox{and}\quad M_{n,n}=\left[\mu_{\alpha+\beta}:\begin{array}[]{c}|\alpha|=n\\
|\beta|=n\end{array}\right],

it can easily be verified that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-M_{n-1}^{-1}M_{n-1,n}\\
I_{r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}}\end{array}\right],

and that the matrix

{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}M_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}=M_{n,n}-M_{n-1,n}^{\mathsf{T}}M_{n-1}^{-{\mathsf{T}}}M_{n-1,n}

is the symmetric and non-singular Schur complement of
M_{n,n}
in

M_{n}
. This matrix satisfies a decomposition

{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}})=Q^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda Q,\qquad\Lambda=|\Lambda|^{\frac{1}{2}}S_{n}|\Lambda|^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
Q
is an orthogonal matrix and
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}\{{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,{\lambda}_{r_{n}^{0}})
is a non-singular
diagonal matrix, which is further decomposed by using

|\Lambda|^{\frac{1}{2}}=\mathrm{diag}\{|{\lambda}_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2}},\ldots,|{\lambda}_{r_{n}^{d}}|^{\frac{1}{2}})
and a
signature matrix

S_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}}
, the latter being a diagonal matrix with the
diagonal elements being either
1
or
-1
. We now define

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=|\Lambda|^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
consists of a basis of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
, so that

{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{P}}_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{m}^{\mathsf{T}})=\delta_{n,m}S_{n},\qquad n\neq m,

which we call a
sign-orthonormal
basis. If
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive
definite, then
S_{n}
is the identity matrix,
and the sign-orthonormal basis becomes an orthonormal basis.

3.2.
Recurrences

Orthogonal polynomials are described by a three-term recurrence
relation as follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
be a sign-orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots

and
{\mathbb{P}}_{-1}:=0
. Then there exist unique matrices
A_{n,i}:r_{n}^{0}\times r^{0}_{n+1}
and
B_{n,i}:r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}
, such that

(3.1)

x_{i}S_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)=A_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)+B_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)+A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}(x),\qquad 1\leq i\leq d,

where the matrix
B_{n}
is symmetric and

(3.2)

\mathrm{rank}\,A_{n,i}=r_{n}^{0}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\mathrm{rank}\,(A_{n,1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,A_{n,d}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}=r_{n+1}^{0},

Indeed, since the
vectors
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
,
n\geq 0
, are a basis of
\Pi^{d}
, there must exist
matrices
A_{n,i},B_{n,i},C_{n,i}
of proper size such that

x_{i}S_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)=A_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)+B_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)+C_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}(x),

and using the
orthogonality, it is easy to see that

(3.3)

A_{n,i}S_{n+1}=S_{n}{\mathcal{L}}(x_{i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}^{\mathsf{T}}),\qquad B_{n,i}S_{n}=S_{n}{\mathcal{L}}(x_{i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}),

which shows that
B_{n}
is symmetric since the signature matrix
S_{n}

satisfies
S_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}=S_{n}=S_{n}^{-1}
; moreover, using
(
3.3
) with
n
replaced by
n-1

in the second equation, we obtain

C_{n,i}S_{n-1}=S_{n}{\mathcal{L}}(x_{i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}})=S_{n}(S_{n}A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n-1})=A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n-1},

which implies that
C_{n,i}=A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

The rank conditions can be established as in the positive definite case: for example, since
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is
sign-orthonormal, its leading coefficient matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}
, defined by

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{0}+\ldots,\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{0}:=\left[x^{\alpha}:|{\alpha}|=n\right],

is a non-singular matrix of size
r_{n}^{d}
. Comparing the leading coefficients of (
3.1
) gives

S_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}L_{n,i}=A_{n,i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n+1},

where
L_{n,i}
is the shift matrix of dimension
r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n+1}^{0}
, defined by
L_{n,i}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n+1}_{0}=x_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{0}
; this matrix
has full rank, thus
A_{n,i}
has rank
r_{n}^{0}
.

The coefficient matrices of the three-term relation for
sign-orthonormal polynomials satisfy a set of commutativity
relations: for
1\leq i,j\leq d
and
k\geq 0
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{n,i}S_{n+1}A_{n+1,j}&=A_{n,j}S_{n+1}A_{n+1,i},\\
A_{n,i}S_{n+1}B_{n+1,j}+B_{n,i}S_{n}A_{n,j}&=B_{n,j}S_{n}A_{n,i}+A_{n,j}S_{n}B_{n+1,i},\\
A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n-1}A_{n-1,j}+B_{n,i}S_{n}B_{n,j}+&A_{n,i}S_{n+1}A_{n,j}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
=A_{n-1,j}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n-1}A_{n-1,i}&+B_{n,j}S_{n}B_{n,i}+A_{n,j}S_{n+1}A_{n,i}^{\mathsf{T}},\end{split}

where
A_{-1,i}:=0
. This follows from computing
{\mathcal{L}}(x_{i}x_{j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n\pm 1}^{\mathsf{T}})

via the three-term relation in two different ways. For example,

\displaystyle S_{n}{\mathcal{L}}(x_{i}x_{j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}^{\mathsf{T}})S_{n+1}^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{L}}(x_{i}S_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x_{j}S_{n+1}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1})^{\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=A_{n,i}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}^{\mathsf{T}})A_{n+1,j})=A_{n,i}S_{n+1}A_{n+1,j},

and the left-hand side is unchanged if the order of
i
and
j
is
exchanged, which shows the first identity; the others are proved in
exactly the same way.

In fact, for
any
set of basis functions
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\in\left(\Pi_{n}^{d}\right)^{r_{n}^{0}}
and the multiplication rule

(3.5)

x_{i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n}(x)=A_{n,i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}(x)+B_{n,i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n}(x)+C_{n,i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n-1},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},\,1\leq i\leq d,

the consistency conditions
x_{i}x_{j}{\mathbb{F}}_{n-1}=x_{j}x_{i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n-1}

implies the identities

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{n-1,i}A_{n,j}&=A_{n-1,j}A_{n,i},\\
A_{n-1,i}B_{n,j}+B_{n-1,i}A_{n-1,j}&=A_{n-1,j}B_{n,i}+B_{n-1,j}A_{n-1,i},\\
A_{n-1,i}C_{n,j}+B_{n-1,i}B_{n-1,j}+&C_{n-1,i}A_{n-2,j}\\
=A_{n-1,j}C_{n,i}&+B_{n-1,j}B_{n-1,i}+C_{n-1,j}A_{n-2,i}\\
B_{n-1,i}C_{n-1,j}+C_{n-1,i}B_{n-2,j},&=B_{n-1,j}C_{n-1,i}+C_{n-1,j}B_{n-2,i},\\
C_{n-1,i}C_{n-2,j}&=C_{n-1,j}C_{n-2,i},\end{split}

which reduces to (
3.4
) for
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}={\mathbb{P}}_{n}
. Note that the
conditions (
3.6
) and (
3.4
), respectively,
become trivial for
d=1
and thus
are a purely multivariate phenomenon, i.e., are relevant for
d\geq 2

only.

We can use the matrices occurring in the three-term recurrence
(
3.1
) to define a family of tridiagonal infinite
matrices
{\mathsf{J}}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
, as follows:

(3.7)

{\mathsf{J}}_{i}:=\left[\begin{matrix}B_{0,i}&A_{0,i}&&\bigcirc\cr A_{0,i}^{\mathsf{T}}&B_{1,i}&A_{1,i}&&\cr&A_{1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}&B_{2,i}&\ddots\cr\bigcirc&&\ddots&\ddots\end{matrix}\right],\qquad 1\leq i\leq d.

The relations (
3.4
) are then equivalent to the formal
commutativity of
{\mathsf{J}}_{i}
, that is,
{\mathsf{J}}_{i}{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{J}}_{j}={\mathsf{J}}_{j}{\mathsf{S}}{\mathsf{J}}_{i}
,

1\leq i,j\leq d
, where
{\mathsf{S}}=\mathrm{diag}\{S_{0},S_{1},S_{2},\ldots\}
is the infinite block diagonal matrix formed by the
sign matrices of the functional
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Since
A_{n,i}:r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n+1}^{0}
has full rank, there exist
matrices
D_{n,i}:r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n+1}^{0}
,
i=1,\dots,d
, such that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{n,i}^{\mathsf{T}}A_{n,i}=I.

The matrices
D_{n,i}
are not unique and we can take, for example,

(D_{n,1},\ldots D_{n,d})
as the generalized
inverses of
(A_{n,1},\ldots,A_{n,d})
. The three-term relations
(
3.1
) lead to a recursive definition of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
, given by

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{n,i}^{\mathsf{T}}x_{i}S_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)-E_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)-F_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}(x),

where the matrices
E_{n}
and
F_{n}
are given by

E_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{n,i}^{\mathsf{T}}B_{n,i}\quad\hbox{and}\quad F_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{n,i}^{\mathsf{T}}A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}},

respectively.
The relation (
3.8
) can be used for a recursive definition of the sequence of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}

if
A_{n,i}
,
B_{n,i}
are given. In one variable, the sequence of polynomials so defined automatically
satisfies the three-term relation (
3.1
). In several
variables, however, we have to require the matrices

A_{n,i}
and
B_{n,i}
to satisfy some necessary conditions. Indeed,
a slightly modified version of the proof given in

[
4
, Theorem 3.5.1]
for the positive definite setting shows that
if
A_{n,i}
and
B_{n,i}
satisfy the
commuting conditions (
3.4
) and the rank condition (
3.2
), then a sequence
of polynomials
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
defined recursively by (
3.8
) satisfies the
three-term relations (
3.1
).

The three-term relations characterize the orthogonality by Favard’s
theorem. We call the linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}

quasi-determinate

if there is a basis
{\mathcal{B}}
of
\Pi^{d}
such that, for any
P,Q\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

{\mathcal{L}}(PQ)=0\quad\hbox{if $P\neq Q$ and ${\mathcal{L}}(P^{2})\neq 0$}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}=\{P_{\alpha}\in\Pi_{n}^{d}:|{\alpha}|=n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
be an arbitrary polynomial sequence.
Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

There exists a linear functional
{\mathcal{L}}
which defines a
quasi-definite linear functional on
\Pi^{d}
and which makes

\{P_{\alpha}^{n}:{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\}
an orthogonal basis of
polynomials.

(ii)

For
n\geq 0
,
1\leq i\leq d
, there exist matrices
A_{n,i}

and
B_{n,i}
such that

(a)

the polynomials
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
satisfy the three-term relation
(
3.1
);

(b)

the matrices in the relation satisfy the rank conditions
(
3.2
).

For the OPs in the positive definite case, the above theorem holds
with
S_{n}=I
,
n\geq 0
. The proof of the theorem
follows with obvious modification of that in the positive definite
case; see
[
4
, Theorem 3.3.7]
.

As a consequence of the three-term relation, we can derive a Christoffel-Darboux formula. Let

(3.9)

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{S}}_{k}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(y)},\qquad x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}.

Then
K_{n}
is the reproducing kernel of the space
\Pi_{n}^{d}
since, for
0\leq j\leq n
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

{\mathcal{L}}(K_{n}(x,\cdot){\mathbb{P}}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}S_{k}{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbb{P}}_{k}{\mathbb{P}}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})={\mathbb{P}}_{j}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Theorem 3.3
.

For
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, the following Christoffel-Darboux formula holds

(3.10)

K_{n}(x,y)=\frac{\left[A_{n,j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(y)}-{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\left[A_{n,j}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(y)}\right]}{x_{j}-\overline{y}_{j}},\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d},

for
1\leq j\leq d
. In particular, taking the limit shows that

(3.11)

K_{n}(x,x)=\left[A_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\partial_{j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)-{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\left[A_{n,i}\partial_{j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)\right].

Proof.

By the three-term relation (
3.1
),

\displaystyle x_{j}S_{k}{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(y)}-\overline{y}_{j}S_{k}{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(y)}

\displaystyle\quad=\left([A_{k,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{k+1}(x)]^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(y)}-[A_{k,i}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k+1}(y)}]^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left([A_{k-1,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(x)]^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k-1}(y)}-[A_{k-1,i}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{k}(y)}]^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbb{P}}_{k-1}(x)\right),

and summing this identity over
k
gives (
3.10
). Since

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
has real coefficients, we see that

\overline{{\mathbb{P}}}_{n}(x)={\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\bar{x})
. Hence, we can write

\displaystyle\left[A_{n,j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(y)}-{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}\left[A_{n,j}\overline{{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(y)}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[A_{n,j}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)\right]^{\mathsf{T}}({\mathbb{P}}_{n}(\bar{y})-{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x))+{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}A_{n,j}({\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(x)-{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}(\bar{y})).

Dividing by
x_{j}-\overline{y}_{j}
and taking the limit
\overline{y}_{j}\mapsto x
proves (
3.11
).
∎

3.3.
Jacobi matrices and common zeros

We now recall the
truncated Jacobi matrices

{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
, defined
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
by

(3.12)

{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}:=\left[\begin{matrix}B_{0,i}&A_{0,i}&&&\bigcirc\cr A_{0,i}^{\mathsf{T}}&B_{1,i}&A_{1,i}&&\cr&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\cr&&A_{n-3,i}^{\mathsf{T}}&B_{n-2,i}&A_{n-2,i}\cr\bigcirc&&&A_{n-2,i}^{\mathsf{T}}&B_{n-1,i}\end{matrix}\right]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n-1}\times r_{n-1}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq d.

An
element
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is called a generalized
joint eigenvalue
of
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,1},\ldots,{\mathsf{J}}_{n,d}
,
if there is
\xi\neq 0
,
\xi\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
, such that

{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}\xi=\lambda_{i}{\mathsf{S}}_{n}\xi,\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,\qquad{\mathsf{S}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}S_{0}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&S_{n}\end{array}\right];

the vector
\xi
is called a joint eigenvector of the generalized eigenvalue
{\lambda}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

A point
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
is a zero of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
if and only if it is a
generalized joint eigenvalue of
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,1},\ldots,{\mathsf{J}}_{n,d}
, the associated
joint eigenvector of
z
is
({\mathbb{P}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}(z),\ldots,{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}(z))^{\mathsf{T}}
. Furthermore, if
z
is a
complex-valued generalized joint eigenvalue, then
\bar{z}
is also a
generalized joint eigenvalue.

Proof.

The proof that a common zero is the same as a joint eigenvalue
follows almost verbatim the proof for the positive definite case. In
particular,
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
can have at most
r_{n-1}=\dim\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
zeros
since
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
is of size
r_{n-1}\times r_{n-1}
. Now, since
the moments are real, so are the matrices
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
. If
z
is a
joint eigenvalue with joint eigenvector
\xi
, then
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}\xi=z{\mathsf{S}}_{n}\xi
; taking the conjugate of this identity shows that
\bar{z}
is also a joint eigenvalue with
\bar{\xi}
as its joint
eigenvector.
∎

In the positive definite case,
{\mathsf{S}}_{n}
is the identity matrix and the
generalized eigenvalues are just the ordinary
eigenvalues, so that the common zeros of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
are exactly the
eigenvalues of
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
. Since
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
are symmetric, this
implies that all zeros are real when
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive definite. We
call
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
a simple zero of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
if the Jacobian
D{\mathbb{P}}_{n}

of the polynomials in
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}^{0}}
is nonsingular.

Proposition 3.5
.

If
x
is a common zero of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
and
K_{n}(x,x)\neq 0
, then
x

must be simple and
x
cannot be a zero
of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}
.

This is a consequence of the identity (
3.11
). In the positive definite setting,
{\mathsf{S}}_{n}
is identity,
so that
K_{n}(x,x)\geq K_{0}(x,x)=1
. Hence, all zeros of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
must be simple by (
3.11
) and,
furthermore,
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}
do not have common zeros. In the non-positive definite case,

K_{n}(x,x)
may not be positive because the sign changes in
{\mathsf{S}}_{n}
and it can be zero. However, since

{\mathsf{S}}_{n}
is a signature matrix, it follows that

K_{n}(x,x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{|{\alpha}|=k}{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}|P_{\alpha}(x)|^{2},\qquad{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}=\pm 1,

so that
K_{n}(x,x)
is real–valued even if
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
.

By Theorem
3.4
, the polynomials in
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
can have at most
r_{n-1}=\dim\Pi_{n-1}^{d}

common zeros. The following theorem characterizes when the number of common zeros is maximal. To
prove it, we need some algebraic preparations, which we shall tailor to our needs.

The
ideal
generated by
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is defined as

\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle:={\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\Pi^{d}\right)^{r_{n}^{0}}=\left\{\sum_{|\alpha|=n}P_{\alpha}Q_{\alpha}:Q_{\alpha}\in\Pi^{d}\right\},\qquad{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\left[P_{\alpha}:|\alpha|=n\right],

and
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is called an
H-basis
provided that

(3.13)

\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle\cap\Pi_{m}^{d}={\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\left(\Pi_{m-n}^{d}\right)^{r_{n}^{0}},\qquad m\geq n,

which means that any polynomial of degree
m
in the ideal

\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle
has a representation with respect
to
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
in which the degree of no term in the sum exceeds

m
. Since
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}

consists of
r_{n}^{0}
linearly independent polynomials of degree
n

whose leading forms span
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}^{d}
, it follows that
\Pi^{d}/\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle=\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
if and only if
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is
an H-basis. For more information on H-bases cf.
[
8
,
17
]
.
Moreover,
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
defines a
reduced multiplication
by the
factors
Q\mapsto(x_{i}Q)_{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
on
\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
by setting

(x_{i}Q)(x)=x_{i}Q(x)-{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}q,

where the factor
q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}^{0}}
is chosen such that
\deg\left(x_{i}Q(x)-{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}q\right)\leq n-1
. In other words, if

\deg x_{i}Q(x)=n
, then
q
is chosen such that the homogeneous
leading form of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}(x)^{\mathsf{T}}
coincides with the leading form of

x_{i}Q(x)
, otherwise we have
q=0
. Of course,
(x_{i}\cdot)_{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}
is a linear map from
\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
to
\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
and its
matrix representation with respect to the basis

{\mathbb{P}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}
is
{\mathsf{M}}_{n,i}:={\mathsf{S}}_{n}{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
,

i=1,\dots,d
. More precisely, if
Q=\widehat{Q}_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{P}}_{0}+\cdots+\widehat{Q}_{n-1}^{T}{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}
, then

(x_{i}Q)_{{\mathbb{P}}_{n}}=\left[\widehat{Q}_{0}^{T},\dots,\widehat{Q}_{n-1}^{T}\right]{\mathsf{S}}_{n}{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}\end{array}\right].

This is a
a direct consequence of multiplying both sides of (
3.1
) by

S_{n}
and the definition in (
3.12
). The matrices

{\mathsf{M}}_{n,i}
are called the
multiplication tables
for the
coordinate multiplication modulo
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
. They can be used to
characterize whether
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is an H-basis; the following result is a special
case of characterization for border bases, given for example in
[
12
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is an H-basis if and only if the multiplication tables

{\mathsf{M}}_{n,i}
commute.

Indeed, if
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is an H-basis, then
M_{n,i}
describes the action
of multiplication by
x_{i}
on
\Pi^{d}/\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle=\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
and since multiplication is commutative,
so are the matrices that represent it. If, on the other hand, the
matrices commute, then for any
|\alpha|\leq n-1
the matrix

{\mathsf{M}}^{\alpha}={\mathsf{M}}_{n,1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots{\mathsf{M}}_{n,d}^{\alpha_{d}}
is
well defined and its first row contains the coefficients of the
monomial
x^{\alpha}
with respect
to
{\mathbb{P}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1}
. Hence, there are
r_{n-1}
linearly
independent reduced polynomials, hence
\dim\Pi^{d}/\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle\geq r_{n-1}
and therefore
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is an H-basis and, in
particular, has
r_{n-1}
common zeros counting multiplicities.

Theorem 3.7
.

The polynomials in
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
have
r_{n-1}
common zeros if and only if

(3.14)

\displaystyle A_{n-1,i}S_{n}A_{n-1,j}^{\mathsf{T}}=A_{n-1,j}S_{n}A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq d.

Proof.

According to Lemma
3.6
, to have the maximal number of
common zeros is equivalent to the commuting of the
{\mathsf{S}}_{n}{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
which
is in turn equivalent to

{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}{\mathsf{S}}_{n}J_{n,j}={\mathsf{J}}_{n,j}{\mathsf{S}}_{n}J_{n,i}
,
1\leq i\neq j\leq d
. Using the commutativity of the Jacobi matrices, it is not
difficult to see that we only need to consider the commutativity of

Q_{i}S_{n}Q_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
Q_{i}
and
Q_{j}
denote the last row of

{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}
and
{\mathsf{J}}_{n,j}
, respectively; that is,

\displaystyle A

{}_{n-2,i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n-2}A_{n-2,j}+B_{n-1,i}S_{n-1}B_{n-1,j}=A_{n-2,j}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n-2}A_{n-2,i}+B_{n-1,j}S_{n-1}B_{n-1,i},

which is equivalent to (
3.14
) by the third identity of
(
3.4
) with
n
replaced by
n-1
.
∎

The same argument can also be applied to an arbitrary recurrence
relation of the form (
3.5
) with the multiplication
table

(3.15)

{\mathsf{J}}_{n,i}^{\prime}:=\left[\begin{matrix}B_{0,i}&A_{0,i}&&&\bigcirc\cr C_{1,i}&B_{1,i}&A_{1,i}&&\cr&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\cr&&C_{n-2,i}&B_{n-2,i}&A_{n-2,i}\cr\bigcirc&&&C_{n-1,i}&B_{n-1,i}\end{matrix}\right],\qquad 1\leq i\leq d,

which leads to the following variant of Theorem
3.7
, that
in particular includes the monic orthogonal polynomials
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

The polynomials
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
from (
3.5
) have
r_{n-1}

common zeros if and only if

(3.16)

A_{n-2,i}\,C_{n-1,j}=A_{n-2,j}\,C_{n-1,i},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq d.

The conditions (
3.14
) and (
3.16
) are highly
non-trivial. For example, the condition does not hold if
{\mathcal{L}}
is centrally
symmetric, which means that
{\mathcal{L}}
satisfies

{\mathcal{L}}(x^{\alpha})=0,\qquad|\alpha|\in 2{\mathbb{N}}_{0}+1;

if
{\mathcal{L}}f=\int_{\Omega}f(x)W(x)\mathrm{d}x
, then
{\mathcal{L}}
is centrally symmetric if
W(x)=W(-x)
whenever
x\in\Omega

implies
-x\in\Omega
. It is known that if
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive definite and centrally symmetric, then
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
has no
common zeros for
n
even and only one common zero (the origin) if
n
is odd
[
4
, Section 3.7]
.

When
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
has maximal common zeros, we can consider interpolation
and cubature rules as in one variable.

Proposition 3.9
.

Assume (
3.14
) that holds. Let
Z_{n}=\{(\xi_{k,1:}\ldots,\xi_{k,d}),1\leq k\leq r_{n-1}\}
be
the set of common zeros of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
. Assume that
\xi\in Z_{n}
are not zeros of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}
. Then the
unique polynomial,
L_{n}f
, in
\Pi_{n}^{d}
that satisfies

L_{n}f(\zeta)=f(\zeta),\qquad\zeta\in Z_{n},

for any generic function
f
is given by the formula

(3.17)

L_{n}f(x)=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}f(\zeta)\frac{K_{n}(x,\zeta)}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}.

Proof.

By our assumption on the zeros,
K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)\neq 0
for
\zeta\in Z_{n}
. By Theorem
3.4
, if

\zeta
is in
Z_{n}
, then so is
\bar{\zeta}
. If
\zeta,\eta\in Z_{n}
and
\zeta\neq\eta
, then there must be a
j
such
that
\zeta_{j}\neq\overline{\eta_{j}}
. Hence, by (
3.10
),
K_{n}(\zeta,\eta)=0
. Consequently, it follows that

L_{n}f
in (
3.17
) satisfies the interpolation conditions.
∎

Applying
{\mathcal{L}}
to
L_{n}f
leads to a cubature rule on
Z_{n}
, which is
a Gaussian cubature.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}(f)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x
for a weight
w
defined on

\Omega
and assume that
Z_{n}

is defined as in Proposition
3.9
. Then

(3.18)

\int_{\Omega}f(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}f(\zeta),\qquad f\in\Pi_{2n-1}^{d}.

Moreover, the cubature rule is real-valued for all polynomials.

Proof.

That the cubature rule is of degree
2n-1
follows as in the positive definite case. If
\zeta\in Z_{n}
is a complex
number, then
\bar{\zeta}\in Z_{n}
. Since
f
is a real-valued polynomial,
\overline{f}(\zeta)=f(\bar{\zeta})
. Moreover,

K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)=K_{n}(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\zeta})
is real, so that

\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}f(\zeta)+\frac{1}{K_{n}\left(\bar{\zeta},\bar{\zeta}\right)}\overline{f(\zeta)}=\frac{2}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}\Re f(\zeta)

is real-valued for each complex-valued
\zeta\in Z_{n}
.
∎

Since the Gaussian cubature rule requires
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
to have
r_{n-1}
simple common zeros, they exist rarely and
do not exist, for example, if
{\mathcal{L}}
is centrally symmetric. Nevertheless, they do exist in some cases. In the
next subsection, we recall a class of weight functions for which they exist.

3.4.
Example of Gaussian cubature rules

We start with a weight function
w
on
{\mathbb{R}}
, which is definite and may change
signs in its support set
[a,b]
. Let
p_{n}(w;x)
be the orthogonal polynomial with respect to
w
that satisfies

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}p_{n}(w;t)p_{m}(w;t)w(t)\mathrm{d}t={\varepsilon}_{n}\delta_{n,m},\qquad n\neq m,\quad{\varepsilon}=\pm 1.

We assume that
p_{n}(w)
has
n
simple, real zeros in
[a,b]
so that
w
admits the Gaussian quadrature
rule (
2.2
) of degree
2n-1
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)w(t)\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{k,n}f(t_{k,n}),\qquad\deg f\leq 2n-1.

We define two families of symmetric polynomials, indexed by
partitions in

\Lambda^{d}=\{{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d+1}:{\lambda}_{1}\geq{\lambda}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq{\lambda}_{d}\}.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{d}
be the symmetric group of
d
elements.
For
{\alpha}\in\Lambda^{d}
, define

Q_{\alpha}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x):=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}p_{\alpha_{1}}(x_{\beta_{1}})\ldots p_{\alpha_{d}}(x_{\beta_{d}}).

Then
\{Q_{\alpha}^{-\frac{1}{2}}:{\alpha}\in\Lambda^{d}\}
is a family of symmetric polynomials and satisfies

(3.19)

\frac{1}{d!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}Q_{\alpha}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x)\,Q_{\beta}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x)\,\prod_{i=1}^{d}w(x_{i})\mathrm{d}x=m_{1}!\ldots m_{d^{\prime}}!\,{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}_{1}}\ldots{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}_{d}}\delta_{{\alpha},{\beta}},

where
d^{\prime}
is the number of distinct elements in
{\alpha}
and
m_{i}
is the number of occurrences of the
i
-th
distinct element in
\alpha
. Moreover, define

J_{\alpha}(x):=\det\,[p_{\alpha_{i}+d-i}(x_{j})]_{i,j=1}^{d}\quad\hbox{and}\quad J(x)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{i}-x_{j}).

Then the family of polynomials
\{Q_{\alpha}^{\frac{1}{2}}:{\alpha}\in\Lambda^{d}\}
, defined by

Q_{\alpha}^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)=\frac{J_{\alpha}(x)}{J(x)},\qquad{\alpha}\in\Lambda^{d},

consists of symmetric orthogonal polynomials that satisfy

(3.20)

\frac{1}{d!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}Q_{\alpha}^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)\,Q_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)\,\left[J(x)\right]^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}w(x_{i})\mathrm{d}x={\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}_{1}}\ldots{\varepsilon}_{{\alpha}_{d}}\delta_{{\alpha},{\beta}};

see, for example,
[
4
, Sect. 5.4]
. These symmetric polynomials can be mapped to a family of ordinary
orthogonal polynomials. Let

\mathcal{R}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid\,x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{d},\,x_{j}\in\mathrm{supp}(w)\}.

The mapping
x\in\mathcal{R}\mapsto u\in\Omega
, defined by

(3.21)

u_{k}=e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots x_{d}):=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq d}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,d,

sends
\mathcal{R}
onto a domain
\Omega
and the Jacobian of the map is
J(x)
. Under the mapping
(
3.21
), the polynomials
Q_{\alpha}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}(x)
become
polynomials in the variable(s)
u
, and these polynomials are
orthogonal with respect to the weight function
W_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}
on

\Omega
, where

W_{-\frac{1}{2}}(u)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}w(x_{i})[\Delta(u)]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad W_{\frac{1}{2}}(u)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}w(x_{i})[\Delta(u)]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
Q_{\alpha}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}
be the symmetric orthogonal polynomials in (
3.19
) and
(
3.20
). For
{\alpha}\in\Lambda^{d}
and
n={\alpha}_{1}\geq\ldots\geq{\alpha}_{d}
, define

P_{\alpha}^{n,-\frac{1}{2}}(u)=Q_{\alpha}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(x)\quad\hbox{and}\quad P_{\alpha}^{n,\frac{1}{2}}(u)=Q_{\alpha}^{\frac{1}{2}}(x),

where
u=u(x)
as in (
3.21
). Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}=\{P_{\alpha}^{n,\pm\frac{1}{2}}:n={\alpha}_{1}\geq\ldots\geq{\alpha}_{d}\geq 0\}
is an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{\pm\frac{1}{2}})
.

The polynomials in
\{P_{\alpha}^{n,\pm\frac{1}{2}}:|{\alpha}|=n\}
have
\dim\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
common zeros and
they are nodes of the Gaussian cubature rules. More precisely, we have the following
[
3
]
:

Theorem 3.12
.

The weight function
W_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}
admits Gaussian cubature rules of degree
2n-1
for
n=1,2,\ldots
.
More precisely,

(3.22)

\int_{\Omega}f(u)W_{-\frac{1}{2}}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\sum_{{\gamma}_{1}=1}^{n}\sum_{{\gamma}_{2}=1}^{{\gamma}_{1}}\cdots\sum_{{\gamma}_{d}=1}^{{\gamma}_{d-1}}\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}f(u_{{\gamma},n}),

where
u_{{\gamma},n}
is the image of
x_{{\gamma},n}=(t_{{\gamma}_{1},n},t_{{\gamma}_{2},n},\ldots,t_{{\gamma}_{d},n})
under the mapping
(
3.21
), and

(3.23)

\int_{\Omega}f(u)W_{\frac{1}{2}}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\sum_{{\gamma}_{1}=1}^{n+d-1}\sum_{{\gamma}_{2}=1}^{{\gamma}_{1}-1}\cdots\sum_{{\gamma}_{d}=1}^{{\gamma}_{d-1}-1}\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(\frac{1}{2})}f(u_{{\gamma},n+d-1}),

where
u_{{\gamma},n+d-1}
is the image of
x_{{\gamma},n+d-1}=(t_{{\gamma}_{1},n+d-1},t_{{\gamma}_{2},n+d-1},\ldots,t_{{\gamma}_{d},n+d-1})

under the mapping (

) and, furthermore,

(3.24)

\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}=\frac{{\lambda}_{\hat{\gamma}_{1},n}^{m_{1}}\dots{\lambda}_{\hat{\gamma}_{d^{\prime}},n}^{m_{d^{\prime}}}}{m_{1}!\cdots m_{d^{\prime}}!}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(\frac{1}{2})}=J(t_{{\gamma},n})^{2}{\lambda}_{{\gamma}_{1},n}\dots{\lambda}_{{\gamma}_{d},n},

where
\hat{\gamma}_{1}\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{d^{\prime}}
denote the distinct elements in
{\gamma}
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{d^{\prime}}
denote

The proof of this theorem is purely algebraic and holds also for the case that
w
may not be positive.
For another example of the Gaussian cubature formula, see
[
10
]
, where the domain is the image of
the simplex under the mapping of elementary symmetric functions. For
d=2
, the domain is bounded by
a hypocycloid.

4.
Continued fraction expansions of moment sequences

To recover an analogy for the continued fractions in several variables, we let

\mu=\left(\mu_{\alpha}:\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right)
be a moment
sequence such that
\det M_{n}\neq 0
,
n\geq 0
, and consider the
associated formal Laurent series

\mu(z)=\sum_{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\mu_{\alpha}z^{-\alpha},\qquad z\in z\in({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{d}.

If there is a rational function
r_{n}(z)
of degrees
\leq n
for
n\geq 0
, such that

\mu(z)-r_{n}(z)=O\left(z^{-2n}\right),\qquad\text{i.e..}\qquad\mu(z)-r_{n}(z)=\sum_{|\alpha|\geq 2n}\lambda_{\alpha}z^{-\alpha},

then
r_{n}(z)
can be regarded as an analogy for the continued fraction in several variables.

Our goals in this section are twofold. The first one is to construct a
sequence of moment
sequences
\mu^{n}
,
n\geq 0
, iteratively from a polynomial sequence that satisfies the
three-term relations and show that
\mu^{n}\to\mu
, where
\mu
is the
moment sequence
that makes the polynomials orthogonal. The second goal is to define
and construct multivariate continued fractions based on
\mu^{n}
, and
to characterize the moment sequence for which these continued
fractions exist.

4.1.
Moments from three-term recurrence

As shown in the previous section, if
{\mathcal{L}}
is a
definite
moment
functional and
\mu
is its moments, then the orthogonal polynomials
satisfy the three-term relations (
3.5
), whose matrix
conditions in turn satisfy (
3.6
),
(
3.16
), and (
3.2
).

We start from the three-term recurrence relation satisfied by some polynomial sequence. Let

{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\in(\Pi_{n}^{d})^{r_{n}^{0}}
,
n\geq 0
, be a sequence of polynomials that satisfies

(4.1)

x_{i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n}(x)=A_{n,i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}(x)+B_{n,i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n}(x)+C_{n,i}{\mathbb{F}}_{n-1}(x),\qquad i=1,\dots,d,

and whose coefficient matrices satisfy (
3.6
), (
3.16
)
and the rank condition (
3.2
).
By Theorem
3.2
, there exists a quasi-determinate linear
functional
{\mathcal{L}}
such that the
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
form an orthogonal basis with respect to
{\mathcal{L}}
, i.e.,

(4.2)

{\mathcal{L}}(Q{\mathbb{F}}_{n})=0,\quad Q\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d},\qquad\det\left[{\mathcal{L}}\left({\mathbb{F}}_{n}{\mathbb{F}}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\right]\neq 0,

holds for any
n\geq 0
. We normalize
{\mathcal{L}}
such that
{\mathcal{L}}(1)=1
and
denote by
\mu_{\alpha}={\mathcal{L}}\left((\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, the moments of this functional. In this setting, the moment sequence

\mu
is well-defined but not explicitly given.

We construct a sequence
\mu^{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, of moment sequences
recursively in
n
and based entirely on the recurrence
(
4.1
). This will be done in such a way that

\mu^{n}
converges to
\mu
. In other words, we provide a constructive method
for the moment sequence
\mu
from
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
defined by
(
4.1
).

By Corollary
3.8
, all
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
,

n\geq 0
, are H-bases and
\Pi^{d}/\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\right\rangle=\Pi_{n-1}^{d}
is the
associated interpolation space modulo
\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\right\rangle
. This implies
that
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
has
r_{n-1}
common zeros, counting multiplicities. Recall that the multiplicity
of common zeros of an ideal is not a simple number anymore, but a structural quantity,
namely a finite dimensional subspace of
\Pi
which is closed under
differentiation or
D
-invariant
for short. More precisely,

{\mathcal{Q}}
is
D
-invariant if
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
implies that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
as well; in particular, this implies that
1\in{\mathcal{Q}}
.
Therefore, that
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
has
r_{n-1}
common zeros is equivalent to that there exist a
set
Z_{n}
of finitely many points in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
and
D
-invariant subspaces
{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}
,

\zeta\in Z_{n}
, describing the multiplicity, such that
P\in\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\right\rangle

if and only if

(4.3)

\left(Q(D)P\right)(\zeta)=0,\qquad Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta},\quad\zeta\in Z_{n},

and
\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\dim{\mathcal{Q}}_{\zeta}=r_{n-1}

(cf.
[
7
]
).
Let
Q_{n,\zeta}
be a graded basis of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}
that thus contains the
constant polynomial. Then the interpolation problem with respect to

\left(Q(D)P\right)(\zeta)
,
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}
,
\zeta\in Z_{n}
,
is
poised
, i.e., uniquely solvable, in
\Pi_{n-1}
. Equivalently,
the
Vandermonde matrix

(4.4)

V_{n}:=\left[\left(Q(D)(\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)(\zeta):\begin{array}[]{c}Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta},\,\zeta\in Z_{n}\\
|\alpha|\leq n-1\end{array}\right]

is nonsingular. Note that the case of simple zeros is described in
this way as

\dim{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}=1
and
{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}={\mathbb{C}}
, and the above
Vandermonde matrix is the classical one for the
Lagrange
interpolation
problem, in general (
4.4
)
corresponds to a
Hermite interpolation problem
, however.

To reconstruct the moment sequence, the
\Theta
-operator
is
useful. It is defined as

\theta_{j}:=(\cdot)_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,d,\qquad\Theta^{\alpha}:=\theta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\theta_{d}^{\alpha_{d}},

and
Q(\Theta)
,
Q\in\Pi^{d}
, is defined accordingly. It has been shown, for
example in
[
18
]
, that
V_{n}
is nonsingular
if and only if the
\Theta
-Vandermonde matrix

V_{\Theta,n}:=\left[\left(Q(\Theta)(\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)(\zeta):\begin{array}[]{c}Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta},\,\zeta\in Z_{n}\\
|\alpha|\leq n-1\end{array}\right]

is nonsingular. Moreover, by construction,

\left[\left(Q(\Theta)(\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)(\zeta):\begin{array}[]{c}Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta},\,\zeta\in Z_{n}\\
|\alpha|\leq n\end{array}\right]\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}=0

since
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
is an H-basis for the ideal defined by the dual conditions
in (
4.3
).

We now use the
Prony’s method
to define a sequence of moment
sequences
\mu^{n}
,
n\geq 0
, by iteration, which is defined by using the recurrence
(
4.1
).
To that end, we note that
{\mathbb{F}}_{0}=1
and set, accordingly
\mu^{0}_{\alpha}=1
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
. Next, suppose that we have already computed a moment
sequence
\mu^{n-1}
. To define
\mu^{n}
, we first find an exponential
polynomial
g_{n}
of the form

(4.5)

g_{n}(x):=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}G_{n,\zeta}(x)\,\zeta^{x},\qquad G_{n,\zeta}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta},

such that

(4.6)

g_{n}(\alpha)=\mu^{n-1}_{\alpha},\qquad|\alpha|\leq n-1.

Expanding
G_{n,\zeta}
with respect to the basis
Q_{n,\zeta}
of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}
, we
note that

g_{n}(\alpha)=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\Big(\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}}c_{Q}Q(\alpha)\Big)\,\zeta^{\alpha}=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}}c_{Q}\left(Q(\Theta)(\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)(\zeta),

hence the coefficients
c_{Q}
, when (
4.6
) is satisfied, are obtained by solving
the linear system

\left[\mu^{n-1}_{\alpha}:|\alpha|\leq n-1\right]=V_{\Theta,n}^{T}\left[c_{Q}:Q\in Q_{n,\zeta},\zeta\in Z_{n}\right].

Since
V_{\Theta,n}
is square and nonsingular, the coefficients
c_{Q}
, therefore
g_{n}
, are
uniquely defined. Thus, once
\mu^{n-1}
is present, the exponential
polynomial
g_{n}
that satisfies (
4.6
) is uniquely
determined by
\mu^{n-1}
.

We are now ready to define the moment sequence
\mu^{n}
, which is simply

\mu^{n}_{\alpha}=g_{n}(\alpha),\qquad\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}.

The polynomials
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
generate the so-called
Prony ideal
for
the sequence
\mu^{n}
and the associated Hankel matrices

M^{n}_{k}:=\left[\mu^{n}_{\alpha+\beta}:\begin{array}[]{c}|\alpha|\leq k\\
|\beta|\leq k\\
\end{array}\right]

can be factored into

M^{n}_{k}=V_{\Theta,n,k}^{T}T_{n}V_{\Theta,n,k},\qquad V_{\Theta,n,k}:=\left[\left(Q(\Theta)(\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)(\zeta):\begin{array}[]{c}Q\in Q_{n,\zeta},\,\zeta\in Z_{n}\\
|\alpha|\leq k\end{array}\right],

where
T_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n-1}\times r_{n-1}}
is a nonsingular block
diagonal matrix with blocks of the size
\dim{\mathcal{Q}}_{\zeta}
,
\zeta\in Z_{n}
, see
[
18
]
.
In particular,
\det M^{n}_{n-1}\neq 0
, i.e.,
\mu^{n}
is
definite up to degree
n-1
and
M^{n}_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}=0
. In the same way, the infinite Hankel matrix
M^{n}
factorizes into

M^{n}=V_{\Theta,n,\infty}^{T}T_{n}V_{\Theta,n,\infty},\qquad V_{\Theta,n,\infty}:=\left[\left(Q(\Theta)(\cdot)^{\alpha}\right)(\zeta):\begin{array}[]{c}Q\in Q_{n,\zeta},\,\zeta\in Z_{n}\\
|\alpha|\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}\end{array}\right],

hence the associated Hankel operator has rank
r_{n-1}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
n\geq 1
, the moments
\mu^{n}
and
\mu
from above satisfy

(4.7)

\mu_{\alpha}=\mu^{n}_{\alpha},\qquad|\alpha|\leq 2n-1.

Proof.

For
n\geq 1
we have that

\mu^{n}_{\alpha}=\mu^{n-1}_{\alpha}=\mu_{\alpha}

for
|\alpha|\leq n-1
; for
n=1
this is the fact that
\mu^{0}_{0}=\mu_{0}=1
and for
n\geq 2
it immediately follows by induction.
We now set
\omega_{\alpha}:=\mu_{\alpha}-\mu^{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
, hence

\omega_{\alpha}=0
,
|\alpha|\leq n-1
. We partition the
coefficients of
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{F}_{n,n-1}\\
\widehat{F}_{n,n}\end{array}\right],\qquad\widehat{F}_{n,n-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n-1}},\,F_{n,n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}},

with
\det{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n,n}\neq 0
and note that due to the orthogonality
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
we have
M_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}=0
, hence

0=\left(M_{n}-M_{n}^{n}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}=W_{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}W_{n-1}^{0}&W_{n}^{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
W_{n-1}^{n}&W_{n}^{n}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n,n-1}\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n,n}\end{array}\right],

where

W_{n-1}^{k}=\left[\omega^{n}_{\alpha+\beta}:\begin{array}[]{c}|\alpha|=k\\
|\beta|\leq n-1\\
\end{array}\right],\quad W_{n}^{k}=\left[\omega^{n}_{\alpha+\beta}:\begin{array}[]{c}|\alpha|=k\\
|\beta|=n\\
\end{array}\right],

which yields that

W_{n}^{k}=-W_{n-1}^{k}\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n,n-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{n,n}^{-1}.

Then
W_{n-1}^{0}=0
implies that
W_{n}^{1}=0
and since
W^{k+1}_{n-1}=\left[W^{k}_{n-1}\,W_{n}^{k}\right]
, a simple induction on
k
yields
that

0=W_{n-1}^{n}=\left[\omega^{n}_{\alpha+\beta}:\begin{array}[]{c}|\alpha|=n\\
|\beta|\leq n-1\\
\end{array}\right].

Since any
\alpha
with
|\alpha|\leq 2n-1
can be written as

\beta+\gamma
,
|\beta|\leq n
,
|\gamma|\leq n-1
, the claim follows.
∎

At this point, it is worthwhile to remark that the above construction
inductively computes the initial segments
\mu^{n}
of
\mu
without
knowing
\mu
at any point of this process. This holds in accordance
with the nature of
continued fractions.

In the case that the Gaussian cubature rules exist, the moment sequence
\mu^{n}
that satisfies
(
4.7
) has a simple formulation. Indeed, the existence of the Gaussian cubature
rule means that all zeros in
Z_{n}
are simple, so that
\dim{\mathcal{Q}}_{n,\zeta}=1
and, consequently,

G_{n,\zeta}
are scalars. Moreover, they are exactly equal to the coefficients in the Gaussian cubature
rules, which are
K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)^{-1}
, with
K_{n}
being the reproducing kernel defined in
(
3.9
). Indeed, let
g_{n}(x)=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}\zeta^{x}
.
Then, the Gaussian cubature rule (
3.18
) implies immediately that (
4.6
) holds for
every positive integer
n
. We state the result as a corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}(f)=\int_{\Omega}f(x)w(x)\mathrm{d}x
for a weight function defined on
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for
which the Gaussian cubature rule (
3.18
) holds. Then the moment
\mu^{n}
in Theorem

4.1
is precisely given by

\mu_{\alpha}^{n}=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}\zeta^{\alpha},\qquad{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}.

We need the Theorem
4.1
in its full generality for the characterization
of rational approximation for the Laurent series in the last subsection.

4.2.
Rational approximation to Laurent series

Continued fractions of univariate polynomials have rational functions
as convergents. The same holds for our three-term expansions
here, provided the recurrence matrices satisfy (
3.4
) and
(
3.14
) or (
3.6
) and (
3.16
),
respectively. To that end, we use the exponential polynomial
g_{n}

(
4.5
) from which
\mu^{n}
is generated as
\mu^{n}_{\alpha}=g_{n}(\alpha)
, and consider the formal Laurent series

(4.8)

\mu^{n}(z):=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\mu_{\alpha}^{n}\,z^{-\alpha},\qquad z\in({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{d}.

It has already been shown by Power
[
15
]
that the Laurent series
associated with any finite rank Hankel operator induces a rational
Laurent series. In addition, one can even derive an explicit
representation of this rational function from (
4.5
). Recall
that
G_{n,\zeta}
are coefficients of
g_{n}(x)
and
\Theta
is the theta
operator.

Proposition 4.3
.

For the sequences
\mu^{n}
given as
\mu^{n}_{\alpha}=g_{n}(\alpha)
, we have

(4.9)

\mu^{n}(z)=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\left(G_{n,\zeta}(-\Theta)\frac{(\cdot)^{\epsilon}}{\left((\cdot)-\zeta\right)^{\epsilon}}\right)(z),\qquad\epsilon:=(1,\dots,1).

Proof.

By (
4.5
), we have that

\displaystyle\mu^{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}G_{n,\zeta}(\alpha)z^{-\alpha}\,\zeta^{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\left(G_{n,\zeta}(-\Theta)\left(\zeta\cdot(\cdot)^{-\epsilon}\right)^{\alpha}\right)(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\left(G_{n,\zeta}(-\Theta)\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\left(\zeta\cdot(\cdot)^{-\epsilon}\right)^{\alpha}\right)(z)=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\left(G_{n,\zeta}(-\Theta)\frac{1}{1-\zeta\cdot(\cdot)^{-\epsilon}}\right)(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\left(G_{n,\zeta}(-\Theta)\frac{(\cdot)^{\epsilon}}{\left((\cdot)-\zeta\right)^{\epsilon}}\right)(z),

which is (
4.9
).
∎

In the case of simple zeros, or the existence of Gaussian cubature rules, we deduce from Corollary
4.2

the following:

Corollary 4.4
.

Under the assumption of Corollary
4.2
, the rational function
\mu_{n}(z)
in (
4.9
)
is given by

(4.10)

\mu^{n}(z)=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}\frac{z^{\epsilon}}{(z-\zeta)^{\epsilon}}=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta,\zeta)}\frac{z_{1}\cdots z_{d}}{(z_{1}-\zeta_{1})\cdots(z_{d}-\zeta_{d})}.

This shows that the rational functions coming from moment sequences
or finite rank Hankel operators have some special properties: they
have finitely many poles of order
d
and their denominator
polynomials can be factored into simple linear factors. And obviously
the denominators are related to the ideal
\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\right\rangle
by the fact that the denominator is the product of the
common zeros of this ideal.

Let us consider the Laurent series and its rational approximants for
the examples in Subsection
3.4

which admit the Gaussian cubature rules. Instead of dealing with integrals over the domain
\Omega
there,
we work with integrals over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
using the mapping (
3.21
).

Let
w
be a weight function defined on
[-1,1]
and assume that it
admits a Gaussian quadrature formula. Let

{\mathcal{L}}_{-\frac{1}{2}}(f)=\frac{1}{d!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(e_{1}(x),\ldots,e_{d}(x))\prod_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x

and

{\mathcal{L}}_{\frac{1}{2}}(f)=\frac{1}{d!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(e_{1}(x),\ldots,e_{d}(x))\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where
e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
is the
k
-th elementary symmetric function defined in (
3.21
).
For the moment sequence
\{{\mathcal{L}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}(x^{\alpha})\}
, the Laurent series (
4.8
) can be
easily seen to satisfy

\mu_{-\frac{1}{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{d!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{z_{1}\cdots z_{d}}{(z_{1}-e_{1}(x))\cdots(z_{d}-e_{d}(x))}\prod_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x

and

\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}(z)=\frac{1}{d!}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{z_{1}\cdots z_{d}}{(z_{1}-e_{1}(x))\cdots(z_{d}-e_{d}(x))}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}x.

By (
3.22
) and (
3.23
), the corresponding rational functions that approximate

\mu_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}(z)
, as in (
4.10
), are given by

(4.11)

\mu_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{n}(z)=\sum_{{\gamma}_{1}=1}^{n}\sum_{{\gamma}_{2}=1}^{{\gamma}_{1}}\cdots\sum_{{\gamma}_{d}=1}^{{\gamma}_{d-1}}\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(-\frac{1}{2})}\frac{z_{1}\cdots z_{d}}{(z_{1}-u_{{\gamma}_{1},n})\cdots(z_{d}-u_{{\gamma}_{d},n})}

and

(4.12)

\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}^{n}(z)=\sum_{{\gamma}_{1}=1}^{n+d-1}\sum_{{\gamma}_{2}=1}^{{\gamma}_{1}-1}\cdots\sum_{{\gamma}_{d}=1}^{{\gamma}_{d-1}-1}\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(\frac{1}{2})}\frac{z_{1}\cdots z_{d}}{(z_{1}-u_{{\gamma}_{1},n+d-1})\cdots(z_{d}-u_{{\gamma}_{d},n+d-1})},

where
u_{{\gamma},n}
and
u_{{\gamma},n+d-1}
are nodes of the corresponding Gaussian cubature rules and

\Lambda_{{\gamma},n}^{(\pm\frac{1}{2})}
are given by (
3.24
). Evidently,
\mu_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{n}(z)

are rational functions and, by Theorem
4.1
, they satisfy

\mu_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}(z)-\mu_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{n}(z)=O(|z|^{2n}).

4.3.
Continued fraction expansions of moment sequences

We can now assemble the theory of the preceding sections to give a
characterization of moment sequences that admit an associated
multivariate continued fraction expansion.

To that end, we start with a sequence
\mu=\left(\mu_{\alpha}:\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right)
such that
\det M_{n}\neq 0
,
n\geq 0
. Define

\mu(z)=\sum_{{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}}\mu_{\alpha}z^{-\alpha},\qquad z\in z\in({\mathbb{C}}\setminus 0)^{d}.

As shown in Proposition
3.1
, there exists a
sign-orthonormal basis
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
,
n\geq 0
, of
\Pi^{d}
that satisfies
the recurrence relation (
3.1
) and the rank condition
(
3.2
). Moreover, the commutativity conditions
(
3.4
) hold for the recurrence matrices. If, in addition, the
commuting conditions (
3.14
) are satisfied for
n\geq 0
,
then the polynomials
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
are H-bases and there exist sequences

\mu^{n}=\left(\mu^{n}_{\alpha}:\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\right)
such that

\mu^{n}_{\alpha}=\mu_{\alpha}
,
|\alpha|\leq 2n-1
, and therefore

\mu(z)-\mu^{n}(z)=O\left(z^{\alpha}:|\alpha|=2n\right),

where
\mu^{n}(z)
is a rational function by (
4.9
). This
is the perfect analogy for the continued fraction expansion of a given
Laurent series.

The above procedure can be performed for
any
vector polynomial
sequence
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}\in\Pi_{n}^{r_{n}^{0}}
given by a three-term recurrence

{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}=A_{n}(x){\mathbb{F}}_{n}+B_{n}{\mathbb{F}}_{n}+C_{n}{\mathbb{F}}_{n-1},\qquad A_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{n,i}\,x_{i}

with matrix coefficients as in the recurrence relations of orthogonal polynomials.
Moreover, we let
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
be a lower set, i.e.,
\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}

implies
\beta
in
{\mathcal{A}}
whenever
\beta\leq\alpha
componentwise that has
the property that
\{x^{\alpha}:\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}\}
spans the quotient space

\Pi^{d}/\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n-1}\right\rangle
. With
Z_{n}
denoting the set of
common zeros of
{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}
, we then define
g_{n}
of the form (
4.5
)
by requiring that

(4.13)

g_{n}(\alpha)=\mu^{n-1}_{\alpha},\qquad\alpha\in{\mathcal{A}}_{n}.

This interpolation problem is uniquely solvable (cf.
[
18
]
)
and again yields
\mu^{n}
by sampling
g_{n}
:
\mu^{n}_{\alpha}=g_{n}(\alpha)
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{d}
. Finally, obtain by this procedure
a sequence of rational functions
\mu^{n}(z)
whose explicit expression is given
in Proposition
4.3
. Now we can give a formal
definition of continued fractions that coincides with the one from

[
14
,
16
]
for
d=1
.

Definition 4.5
.

For matrices
A_{n,i}:r_{n+1}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}
,
i=1,\dots,d
,
B_{n}:r_{n+1}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}
,
C_{n}:r_{n+1}^{0}\times r_{n-1}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the
continued fraction

(4.14)

\frac{\left.C_{1}\right|}{\left.A_{1}(z)+B_{1}\right|}+\cdots+\frac{\left.C_{n}\right|}{\left.A_{n}(z)+B_{n}\right|}:=\sum_{\zeta\in Z_{n}}\left(G_{n,\zeta}(-\Theta)\frac{(\cdot)^{\epsilon}}{\left((\cdot)-\zeta\right)^{\epsilon}}\right)(z),

where
Z_{n}
are the common zeros of
{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}
and the

G_{n,\zeta}
are determined iteratively by (
4.13
). The
ideal
\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}\right\rangle
is called the

denominator ideal
of the continued fraction.

The left-hand side of (
4.14
) is a symbolic notation for
the continued fraction in the spirit of the classical notation of one variable.
In words, with each matrix sequence, the continued fraction expansion
associates a sequence of rational functions, which is precisely the continued
fraction expansion of rational functions, exactly like the case for
d=1
.
Note, however, that the situation is significantly more intricate in several
variables since
{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}
may
not
be an H-basis of the
denominator ideal, and even
Z_{n}=\emptyset
is possible. Hence, in
contrast to the univariate case, we cannot expect these continued
fractions to exist for all moment functionals. Finally, we find it worthwhile
to mention that
\left\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{n+1}\right\rangle
is the so-called

Prony ideal
for the associated exponential polynomial
g_{n}
.

We are now ready to give a fundamental definition.

Definition 4.6
.

A Laurent series
\mu(z)
has an
associated continued fraction

with coefficients
A_{n,i},B_{n},C_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
if

\mu(z)-\left(\frac{\left.C_{1}\right|}{\left.A_{1}(z)+B_{1}\right|}+\cdots+\frac{\left.C_{n}\right|}{\left.A_{n}(z)+B_{n}\right|}\right)=O\left(z^{\alpha}:|\alpha|=2n\right),\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

In our construction of signed orthogonal polynomials above, the
Laurent ideals, hence
Z_{n}
and

{\mathcal{Q}}_{\zeta}
,
\zeta\in Z_{n}
, depend only on the
ideal

\left\langle{\mathbb{P}}_{n}\right\rangle
, hence we can also start with a sequence of orthogonal
polynomials defined by the implicit recursion (
3.1
), and
then refer to the explicit formula (
3.8
), use the notation

G_{n}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}G_{n,i}z_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}D_{n,i}^{\mathsf{T}}S_{n}z_{i},

and consider the continued fractions

(4.15)

\frac{-F_{1}|}{|G_{1}(z)-E_{1}}+\frac{-F_{2}|}{|G_{2}(z)-E_{2}}+\cdots\frac{-F_{n}|}{|G_{n}(z)-E_{2}},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

For the continued fraction expansion defined in Definition
4.6
,
we can now summarize our findings in the following way.

Theorem 4.7
.

A Laurent series
\mu(z)=\sum\mu_{\alpha}z^{-\alpha}
has an
associated continued fraction expansion if and only if

(i)

the Hankel matrices
M_{n}
are definite, i.e.,
\det M_{n}\neq 0
,
n\geq 0
, and

(ii)

the coefficient matrices of the associated sign-orthonormal
polynomials satisfy (
3.4
), (
3.2
) and
(
3.14
).

As an example, the rational functions
\mu_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}^{n}
given in
(
4.11
) and (
4.12
) are the associated
continued fraction expansions of the Laurent series
\mu_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}(z)
.

Once more we want to emphasize that the critical condition is
(
3.14
). Whenever
\det M_{n}\neq 0
, then the rank condition
(
3.2
) and the commuting conditions (
3.4
)
follow automatically, they are needed, however, to ensure that a three
term recurrence yields a well-defined multiplication.

To further understand the meaning of (
3.14
), we consider
the
monic
orthogonal basis
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
associated to the
definite sequence
\mu
, where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}*\\
I_{r_{n}^{0}\times r_{n}^{0}}\end{array}\right]
. Here, the matrices
A_{n,i}
,
B_{n,i}
and
C_{n,i}
of the
recurrence relation (
3.5
) can be given explicitly
from the moment matrix, cf.
[
2
]
, in particular

A_{n,i}=L_{n,i}=:\sum_{|\alpha|=n}e_{\alpha}e_{\alpha+\epsilon_{i}}^{T},\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{n,i}=H_{n}L_{n-1,i}^{T}H_{n-1}^{-1},

where

H_{n}=M_{n,n}-M_{n,n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}M_{n-1}^{-1}M_{n,n-1},\qquad M_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{n-1}&M_{n,n-1}\\
M_{n,n-1}&M_{n,n}\end{array}\right],

denotes the Schur complement of
M_{n-1}
in
M_{n}
, which is
nonsingular since
M_{n}
and
M_{n-1}
are nonsingular. For these
matrices, (
3.16
) (with
n
replaced by
n+1
for
convenience) becomes

L_{n-1,i}H_{n}L_{n-1,j}^{T}H_{n-1}^{-1}=L_{n-1,j}H_{n}L_{n-1,i}^{T}H_{n-1}^{-1},

or, equivalently,

L_{n-1,i}H_{n}L_{n-1,j}^{T}=L_{n-1,j}H_{n}L_{n-1,i}^{T}.

By
[
22
, Lemma 4.2]
, this identity implies that
H_{n}
is a Hankel matrix. that is,

(H_{n})_{\alpha,\beta}=h_{\alpha+\beta}
.
Since
M_{n,n}
is a Hankel matrix, we obtain the following characterization:

Theorem 4.8
.

A sequence
\mu
has an associated continued fraction
expansion if and only if it is definite and the matrix

M_{n,n-1}^{\mathsf{T}}M_{n-1}^{-1}M_{n,n-1}

is a Hankel matrix for all
n\geq 1
.
