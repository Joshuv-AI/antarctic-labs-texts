A comparison between the deflection angles of massive and massless particles in the Shchwarzschild space-time and their consequences on black hole shadows
S. Mendoza, M. Santibañez

Abstract
We present comparisons of the deflection angles of massless and massive particles in the Schwarzschild space-time. For the case of photons in a general static space-time, we construct a spatial 3D equation of motion for their path that leads to an implicit formula for the deflection angle. We then compare our results with well known results of the literature in the Schwarzschild space-time. For the case of massive particles we calculate the deflection angle only in the Schwarzschild space-time. The end result is that as the velocity of any massive particle diminishes, the deflection angle increases. To show the relevance of these comparisons, we constructed different black hole shadows for massive particles in order to be compared with a shadow made by of photons.



I
Introduction

Since
Dyson, Eddington, and Davidson [1]
obtained evidence for the deflection of light generated by our Sun, telescopes had caught pictures that show how light beams are affected by gravitational fields generated by mass distributions, as we can see in some images of Hubble and James Webb telescope.

The first expression which described the deflection angle of light beams passing close to a massive point source
M
was found by Soldner (1802) (see e.g.
Mignonat [2]
for
an excellent review on this). This Newtonian formula for the deflection angle of a test particle is given by:

\Delta\phi_{\text{Soldner}}=\dfrac{2GM}{v^{2}b},

(1)

where
v
is the velocity of the test particle,
G
is Newton’s gravitational constant and
b
is the impact parameter shown in Figure
1
. Since the mass of the test particle does not appear on equation (
1
), the original idea of this Soldner deflection angle, was that it would predict also the deflection of massless photon test particles passing close to the gravitational field of the massive object
M
, by simply assuming that the velocity
v
is the velocity of light
c
.

The diagram shows the trajectory of a light beam in blue. This path starts from an infinite distance to the left with respect to the mass
M
, at an impact parameter
b
. The trajectory is deflected an angle
\Delta\phi
by the gravitational field produced by the mass
M
.

Using general relativistic arguments,
Einstein [3]
calculated the correct
expression for the small deflection angle of light beams passing close to a weak
gravitational field produced by a point mass source
M
:

\Delta\phi_{\text{Einstein}}=\dfrac{4GM}{c^{2}b},

(2)

which is twice the deflection angle calculated by Soldner.

Over the years, the interaction of light rays with gravitational fields have become
a very active research subject with plenty of astrophysical applications. Among these
are the first shadows generated by a black hole
[
4
]
and the observations by the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)
[
5
]
, and the extensive studies of gravitational
lenses
[
6
,
7
, e.g.]
.

The study of the deflection of massive relativistic particles passing close to a weak gravitational field has been studied using different variational techniques
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Neutrinos are relativistic massive particles
[
11
]
, difficult to detect
[
12
]
but they follow geodesics on a curved space-time. In fact, since neutrinos could be emitted by an accretion disc of
a compact object
[
13
,
14
]
, one could in principle draw the shadow of
neutrino particles emitted from a black hole.

The article is organised as follows. In Section
II
, we obtain a new set of 3D equations for the path of a light beam valid for any stationary space-time and we test the results in Section
III

by calculating the deflection angle for a Schwarzschild space-time.
In Section
IV
we performed a similar analysis for ultrarelativistic massive particles, finding deflection angles on different ways. Finally, we evolved the light motion equations obtained and the geodesic equation of a massive particle using the free GNU Public Licensed software aztekas-shadows ©2024 Magallanes, Mendoza & Santibañez that will be publicly available in the near future, to generate Schwarzschild black hole shadows of massless and massive particles.

II
3D motion equations of a light beam

On any four-dimensional pseudo-Riemannian manifold adapted to general relativity, the
four-interval
\mathrm{d}s
as a function of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
can be decomposed
as follows
[
15
, see e.g.]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=g_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu},

(3)

\displaystyle=g_{00}(\mathrm{d}x^{0})^{2}+2g_{0i}\mathrm{d}x^{0}\mathrm{d}x^{i}+g_{ik}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathrm{d}x^{k},

\displaystyle=\left(\sqrt{g_{00}}~\mathrm{d}x^{0}+\dfrac{g_{0i}}{\sqrt{g_{00}}}~\mathrm{d}x^{i}\right)^{2}-\mathrm{d}l^{2},

where
x^{\mu}=(x^{0},\ x^{1},\ x^{2},\ x^{3})
represent four coordinates,
\mathrm{d}l^{2}:=\gamma_{ik}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathrm{d}x^{k}
is the spatial 3D-arch length and:

\gamma_{ik}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left(-g_{ik}+\dfrac{g_{0i}g_{0k}}{g_{00}}\right),

(4)

represents the spatial 3D-metric. In the previous equations and in what follows Greek indices take space-time values
(
0,1,2,3
) and Latin ones take spatial values (
1,2,3
). We use Einstein’s summation convention
over repeated indices and we use a (
+,-,-,-
) signature for the metric
[
16
, see e.g.]
.

Light trajectories occur in null
\mathrm{d}s=0
geodesics and so, using
equation (
3
), it turns out that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}x^{0}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{g_{0i}}{g_{00}}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{g_{00}}}\mathrm{d}l,

(5)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{g_{00}}\left[-g_{0i}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x^{i}}{\mathrm{d}l}\pm\sqrt{g_{00}}\right]\mathrm{d}l.

The common definition of the refraction index is given by
[
17
]
:

n\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\dfrac{c}{\mathrm{d}l/\mathrm{d}t}=\dfrac{1}{g_{00}}\left[-g_{0i}e^{i}+\sqrt{g_{00}}\right],

(6)

with the positive sign for a time evolution to the future and so,
we can rewrite equation (
5
) as:

\mathrm{d}x^{0}=n\mathrm{d}l,

(7)

where
e^{i}:=\mathrm{d}x^{i}/\mathrm{d}l
is a tangent vector to the space 3D-path of the
light beam. Note that since

e_{i}=\gamma_{ik}e^{k},\qquad\text{and so}\qquad e^{i}=\gamma^{ik}e_{k},

(8)

then:

e_{i}e^{i}=\gamma_{ik}e^{k}e^{i}=\gamma_{ik}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x^{k}}{\mathrm{d}l}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}x^{i}}{\mathrm{d}l}=\dfrac{\gamma_{ik}\mathrm{d}x^{k}\mathrm{d}x^{i}}{\mathrm{d}l^{2}}=1,

(9)

i.e.,
e^{i}
is a unit vector.

For the development of this article it will be very important Fermat’s principle,
which can be expressed simply as
‘The minimum possible time it takes for light to travel a path between two points in space’
, and can be interpreted as an extremum relativistic principle in time as follows
[
6
]
:

Consider an event
A
where the radiation is emitted and a time-like world-line
\mathit{l}

for which
\mathrm{d}s^{2}>0
. A ray of light is a soft null curve
\Gamma
that propagates from
A
through

\mathit{l}
if and only if the arrival time
t
on
\mathit{l}
is stationary under
first order variations of
\Gamma
within the set of soft null curves from
A
through
\mathit{l}
, in other words

\delta\int{\mathrm{d}t}=\delta t=0,

(10)

which, according to equation (
7
) yields:

\delta\int n~\mathrm{d}l=0.

(11)

In here and in what follows we will only deal with
stationary
space-times in the sense
defined by
Landau and Lifshitz [15]

As described by
Landau and Lifshitz [15]
, a
constant gravitational field is one for which there exists a system of reference where the metric tensor components do not depend on the time coordinate. Furthermore, a constant gravitational
field for which
g_{0i}\neq 0
for some
i
is called stationary. If
g_{0i}=0
, for all
i
then the gravitational field is called static.
. For such space-times
equation (
11
) with a general refraction index takes the form:

\delta x^{0}=\int\left[\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x^{k}}~\delta x^{k}~\mathrm{d}l+n~\delta\left(\mathrm{d}l\right)\right]=0.

(12)

Since the null variations of
\mathrm{d}l
are given by:

\displaystyle\delta\left(\mathrm{d}l\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\sqrt{\gamma_{ik}\mathrm{d}x^{i}\mathrm{d}x^{k}},

(13)

\displaystyle=\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}e^{i}e^{j}\right]\delta x^{k}\mathrm{d}l+e_{k}\mathrm{d}\left(\delta x^{k}\right),

equation (
12
) can be integrated by parts to yield:

\displaystyle\delta x^{0}=\int

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x^{k}}\delta x^{k}\mathrm{d}l+\left[\dfrac{n}{2}\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}e^{i}e^{j}\right]\delta x^{k}\mathrm{d}l

(14)

\displaystyle-\left[\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x^{i}}e^{i}e_{k}+n\dfrac{\mathrm{d}e_{k}}{\mathrm{d}l}\right]\mathrm{d}l\delta x^{k},

\displaystyle=\int

\displaystyle\left[\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x^{k}}+\dfrac{n}{2}\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}e^{i}e^{j}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x^{i}}e^{i}e_{k}-n\dfrac{\mathrm{d}e_{k}}{\mathrm{d}l}\right]\mathrm{d}l~\delta x^{k}=0.

Since the previous integral is null for any variation
\delta x^{k}
, it then follows that:

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}e_{k}}{\mathrm{d}l}=\dfrac{\partial\ln n}{\partial x^{k}}+\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}e^{i}e^{j}-\dfrac{\partial\ln n}{\partial x^{i}}e^{i}e_{k},

(15)

or by using equation (
8
):

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}e^{q}}{\mathrm{d}l}=

\displaystyle\left[\gamma^{kq}\dfrac{\partial\ln n}{\partial x^{k}}-\dfrac{\partial\ln n}{\partial x^{i}}e^{i}e^{q}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle+\gamma^{kq}\left[\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}e^{i}e^{j}-\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{kp}}{\partial x^{m}}e^{m}e^{p}\right].

Direct substitution of equation (
6
) into the previous equation yields:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}e^{q}}{\mathrm{d}l}=\dfrac{\gamma^{kq}}{n}

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{1}{g_{00}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{g_{00}}}\dfrac{\partial g_{00}}{\partial x^{k}}-e^{i}\dfrac{\partial g_{0i}}{\partial x^{k}}\right]\right.

(17)

\displaystyle\left.-\dfrac{1}{g_{00}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial g_{00}}{\partial x^{k}}\left(\sqrt{g_{00}}-g_{0i}e^{i}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle-\dfrac{e^{i}e^{q}}{n}

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{1}{g_{00}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{g_{00}}}\dfrac{\partial g_{00}}{\partial x^{i}}-e^{m}\dfrac{\partial g_{0m}}{\partial x^{i}}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\dfrac{1}{g_{00}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial g_{00}}{\partial x^{i}}\left(\sqrt{g_{00}}-g_{0m}e^{m}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle+\gamma^{kq}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{ij}}{\partial x^{k}}e^{i}e^{j}-\dfrac{\partial\gamma_{kp}}{\partial x^{m}}e^{m}e^{p}\right].

This expression represents the rate of change of the vector
e^{i}
between two points
along the path of a null geodesic, as a function only of the components of the 3-metric, its
derivatives and the vector
e^{i}
. In other words, it represents the 3D equation of motion of a light beam.

III
Deflection angle of a light beam in Schwarzschild space-time

Let us consider a spherically symmetric coordinate system. In Schwarzschild-like coordinates (
ct,r,\theta,\varphi
), the interval is given by
[
18
]
:

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=g_{00}c^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+g_{rr}\mathrm{d}r^{2}-r^{2}\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(18)

where
\mathrm{d}\Omega
is the angular displacement and the components
g_{00}
and
g_{rr}
are functions that depend only on the
r
coordinate.

In this coordinate system, equations (
17
) are written as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}r}{\mathrm{d}l^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{g_{rr}}\left[\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\ln n}{\mathrm{d}r}+\dfrac{1+g_{rr}\left(e^{r}\right)^{2}}{r}+\dfrac{\left(e^{r}\right)^{2}}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}g_{rr}}{\mathrm{d}r}\right]

(19)

\displaystyle-\left(e^{r}\right)^{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\ln n}{\mathrm{d}r},

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\theta}{\mathrm{d}l^{2}}=

\displaystyle\left(e^{\varphi}\right)^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta-\dfrac{2e^{r}e^{\theta}}{r}-e^{r}e^{\theta}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\ln n}{\mathrm{d}r},

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}l^{2}}=

\displaystyle-2e^{\theta}e^{\varphi}\cot\theta-\dfrac{2e^{r}e^{\varphi}}{r}-e^{r}e^{\varphi}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\ln n}{\mathrm{d}r}.

with
n=1/\sqrt{g_{00}}
, i.e.,
n=n(r)
. These are a system of coupled second order differential
equations.

For the Schwarzschild space-time the metric tensor takes the following form
[
18
, e.g.]
:

g_{00}=1-\frac{r_{\mathrm{S}}}{r}\hskip 28.45274ptg_{rr}=\left(1-\frac{r_{\mathrm{S}}}{r}\right)^{-1},

(20)

where
r_{\mathrm{S}}=2GM/c^{2}
is the Schwarzschild radius. With this change of variable and knowing the initial values of the position
x^{i}
and the tangent vector
e^{i}
for a given massless particle, we used a 4th order Runge-Kutta method to
integrate numerically equations (
19
) for any
r>r_{\mathrm{S}}
with a given
impact parameter
b
. The results are presented in Figure
2
, with light beams
emitted so far to the left of the diagram (
r_{\text{initial}}=10^{4}r_{\mathrm{S}}
), where the space-time is essentially flat
[
19
]
.

The Figure shows different light beam trajectories in blue deflected by a Schwarzschild black hole (represented as a black circle). All trajectories start horizontally far to the left of the figure at position

If we analyze impact parameters greater than

\displaystyle\alpha=\cos^{-1}\dfrac{e^{k}_{\text{(initial)}}e_{k\text{(final) }}}{|e^{k}_{\text{(initial)}}|\,|e^{k}_{\text{(final) }}|}.

(21)

The diagram shows the vectors

In the weak field limit of approximation, this deflection angle converges to the Einstein deflection angle (

Equation (

\beta=2\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}\dfrac{[-g_{00}(r)g_{11}(r)]^{1/2}}{r\left[\left(\dfrac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}g_{00}(r_{0})-g_{00}(r)\right]^{1/2}}\mathrm{d}r-\pi.

A numerical comparison between

IV
Deflection angle for massive particles

We can write the equations for the three constants of motion in Schwarzschild spacetime
as
[
18
, cf.]
:

\displaystyle k=

\displaystyle\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{S}}}{r}\right)\dfrac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}\tau},

(23)

\displaystyle c^{2}=

\displaystyle c^{2}\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{S}}}{r}\right)\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\left(1-\dfrac{r_{\mathrm{S}}}{r}\right)^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right)^{2}-r^{2}\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle h=

\displaystyle r^{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}\tau},

where
k
is proportional to the energy and
h
is proportional to the
angular momentum
[
23
]
.

If we define
u=1/r
the system of equations (
23
) can be
rewritten as:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}u}{\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2}}+u=\dfrac{GM}{h^{2}}+\dfrac{3GM}{c^{2}}u^{2}

(24)

Figure
5
shows the symmetric trajectory of
a massive test particle as it moves about the coordinate origin where a point mas
M
is located. For sufficiently large impact parameters
b
, the deflection angle is small with a value
\Delta\varphi=|2\varphi(r=\infty)|
.

Figure 5:
The Figure shows the trajectory of a massive test particle as it moves around a central mass
M
with a sufficiently large impact parameter
b
, that produces a small deflection angle on its path.

The homogeneous solution of equation (
24
) is
u_{0}(\varphi)=\sin\varphi/b
,
and so the first order correction in
\Delta u
is:

\Delta u=\dfrac{3GM}{2c^{2}b^{2}}\left(1+\dfrac{1}{3}\cos(2\varphi)\right)+\dfrac{GM}{h^{2}}.

(25)

In the limit
r\rightarrow\infty
and small angle approximation
\sin\varphi\approx\varphi

which implies
u\rightarrow 0
and
\cos 2\varphi\approx 1
results in an analytic expression
for small deflection angles given by:

\Delta\varphi=\dfrac{4GM}{c^{2}b}+\dfrac{2GM}{c^{2}b}\left(\dfrac{1-\beta_{\infty}^{2}}{\beta_{\infty}^{2}}\right),

(26)

where
\beta_{\infty}:=v_{\infty}/c
and
v_{\infty}
is the velocity of the
massive particle at infinity where it was emited. This result is coherent with the previous one obtained by
Accioly and Ragusa [8]
,
Pang and Jia [9]
,
Li et al. [10]
to first order in
1/b
.

If we take the ultrarelativistic limit
\beta_{\infty}\rightarrow 1
, equation (
26
) becomes Einstein’s deflection angle presented in relation (
2
). For the non-relativistic limit, where
\beta_{\infty}\ll 1
,
the deflection angle converges to the Soldner angle shown in (
1
).

In general terms, the deflection can be obtained using the system of equations (
23
) by integrating the following expression:

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}u}=-\dfrac{bV_{\infty}}{\sqrt{V_{\infty}^{2}+ur_{\mathrm{S}}+b^{2}V_{\infty}^{2}u^{2}(ur_{\mathrm{S}}-1)}},

(27)

where
V_{\infty}=\beta_{\infty}/\sqrt{1-\beta_{\infty}}
.

), whereas for the case of light beams it is the
result of integrating numerically equation (

V
Discussion

In this article we have calculated a new set of coupled 3D spatial differential
equations
17
to evolve the trajectories of massless particles in any static
space-time. We used these equations to find the deflection angle
\alpha
produced by a light beam on a
Schwarzschild space-time in equation (
21
) and compared it with the very well known
result of the deflection angle
\beta
by
Keeton and Petters [21]
shown in equation (
22
).
With this motivation, we calculated the weak deflection angle for massive particles in a Schwarzschild space-time
and showed that for a given impact parameter the deflection angle grows as the particle’s velocity decreases. This
fact motivates the idea as to how a black hole shadow will look if an accretion disc surrounding a central
black hole emits massive particles (e.g. neutrinos). Figure
7
shows
different black hole shadows of a Schwarzschild black hole surrounded by a
thin accretion disc. The images were produced with the use of the free GNU licensed software aztekas-shadows
©2024 Magallanes, Mendoza & Santibañez that will be publicly available in the near future.
The software uses a 4th order Runge-Kuta integration method to solve
the system of equations (
19
) for the case of massless particles and the standard
geodesic equation for the case of massive ones. As expected, the observed image thickens with a decreasing
particle velocity due to greater deflection angles as compared with light.
