Capacity-Insensitive Algorithms for Online Facility Assignment Problems on a Line Thanks: This work was partially supported by joint project of Kyoto University and Toyota Motor Corporation, titled “Advanced Mathematical Science for Mobility Society.”
Tsubasa Harada, Toshiya Itoh, Shuichi Miyazaki

Abstract




Abstract:

In the online facility assignment problem
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
,
there exist
k
servers with a capacity
\ell\geq 1
on a metric space
and a request arrives one-by-one. The task of an online algorithm is to
irrevocably match a current request with one of the servers with vacancies
before the next request arrives.
As special cases for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
,
we consider
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)

on a line
, which is denoted by

{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
and
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
, where the latter
is the case of
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
with equidistant servers. In this paper,
we perform the competitive analysis for the above problems.
As a natural generalization of the greedy algorithm
grdy
,
we introduce a class of algorithms called
MPFS (Most Preferred Free Servers) and show
that any MPFS algorithm has the capacity-insensitive property,
i.e., for any
\ell\geq 1
,
alg
is
c
-competitive
for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)
iff

alg
is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
.
By applying the capacity-insensitive
property of the greedy algorithm
grdy
, we
derive the matching upper and lower bounds
4k-5

on the competitive ratio of
grdy
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
.
To investigate the capability of MPFS algorithms,
we show that the competitive ratio of
any MPFS algorithm
alg
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)

is at least
2k-1
. Then we propose a new
MPFS algorithm
idas
(Interior Division for Adjacent Servers)
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
and show that the competitive ratio of

idas
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
is at most
2k-1
, i.e.,

idas
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
is best possible
in all the MPFS algorithms.

Key Words:
Online algorithm,
Competitive analysis, Online metric matching,
Online facility assignment problem, Greedy algorithm.

1
Introduction

Online optimization (profit maximization or cost minimization) problems are
real-time computation, in which a sequence of requests is an input,
each request is given to an online algorithm one-by-one, and an online algorithm
must decide how to deal with the current request before the next request arrives.
Once the decision is fixed for the current request, the online algorithm is not
allowed to change it later. In general, the efficiency of online algorithms is measured
by competitive analysis which is initiated by Sleator and Tarjan
[
33
]
.
Informally, we say that an online algorithm
alg
is
c
-competitive

(or the
competitive ratio
of
alg
is at most
c
) if the cost of output
by
alg
is at most
c
times worse than the optimal cost (the formal definition
will be given in Section
2.3
).

The
online metric matching problem
is initiated independently by
Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs
[
17
]
and
Khuller et al.
[
21
]
as an online variant of the minimum
cost bipartite matching problem, and is formulated as follows:

k
servers are located on a given metric space and
k
requests (on the metric
space) are given one-by-one in an online manner. The task of an online algorithm
is to match each request immediately with one of the
k
servers.
The cost of matching a request with a server is determined by the distance
between them. The goal of the problem is to minimize the sum of the costs
of matching
k
requests to
k
distinct servers.
For this problem,
Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs
[
17
]
and
Khuller et al.
[
21
]
presented a deterministic online algorithm, which is
called
Permutation

[
17
]
,
and showed that it is
(2k-1)
-competitive and best possible.

Later, Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs
[
19
]
restricted the underlying metric
space to be a line and introduced a problem referred to as the
online matching problem on a line. For this restricted problem,
Kalyanasundaram and Pruhs
[
19
]
conjectured that (i) there exists a
9-competitive algorithm and
(ii) the
Work Function
algorithm
[
23
]
has
a constant competitive ratio, but
(i) and (ii) were disproved in

[
12
]
and
[
22
]
, respectively.
There have been
extensive studies on
this problem
[
3
,
2
,
13
,
28
,
30
,
31
]

and the best upper bound on the competitive ratio
[
28
,
31
]

is
O(\log k)
, which is achieved by the

robust-matching
algorithm
[
30
]
.
While the best lower bound on the competitive ratio
[
12
]

has been 9.001 for a long time, Peserico and Scquizzato
[
29
]

drastically improved it to
\Omega(\sqrt{\log k})
.

As a variant of the online metric matching problem,
Ahmed et al.
[
1
]
formulated the
online facility assignment

problem as follows: There exist
k
servers located
equidistantly on a line and
each request appears (one-by-one)
on the line. Each server has a
capacity
, which corresponds to
the possible number of requests that can be matched to the server.
Ahmed et al.
[
1
]
showed (with rough proofs) that the greedy
algorithm
grdy

[
18
]
is
4k
-competitive
and the
Optimal-fill
algorithm is
k
-competitive for any
k>2
.
Itoh et al.
[
16
]
also analyzed the competitive
ratio for small
k\geq 2
, and showed that (i) for
k=2
, the competitive ratio
of any algorithm is at least 3 and
grdy
is 3-competitive, i.e.,

grdy
is best possible for
k=2
, and
(ii) for
k=3
,
4
, and
5
, the competitive ratio of any algorithm is at least

1+\sqrt{6}>3.449
,
\frac{4+\sqrt{73}}{3}>4.181
, and

\frac{13}{3}>4.333
, respectively. Further results on this problem were
extensively obtained by Satake
[
32
]
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Online Facility Assignment Problem

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, where
X
is a (possibly infinite) set of points and

d:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is a distance function.
We use
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
to denote the set of
k
servers and use

\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}
to denote
a request sequence. For each
1\leq j\leq k
, a server

s_{j}
is characterized by the position
p(s_{j})\in X
and

s_{j}
has capacity

c_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
s_{j}
can be matched with at most
c_{j}
requests.
We assume that
n\leq c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
,
a request
r_{i}
is also characterized by the position
p(r_{i})\in X
.

The set
S
is given to an online algorithm in advance, while requests are given
one-by-one from
r_{1}
to
r_{n}
. At any time of the execution of an algorithm,
a server is called
free
if the number of requests matched with it is less
than its capacity, and
full
otherwise. When a request
r_{i}
is revealed,
an online algorithm must match
r_{i}
with one of free servers. If

r_{i}
is matched with
s_{j}
, the pair
(r_{i},s_{j})

is added to the current matching and the cost

\mbox{{\sc cost}}(r_{i},s_{j})=d(p(r_{i}),p(s_{j}))
is incurred for this pair.
The cost of the matching is the sum of the costs of all the pairs contained in it.
The goal of online algorithms is to minimize the cost of the final matching.
We refer to such a problem as the
online facility assignment
problem
with
k
servers and denote it by
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
.
For the case that
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{k}=\ell\geq 1
, it is immediate
that
n\leq k\ell
and
we simply use
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
to denote the online facility assignment
problem with
k
servers (of uniform capacity
\ell
).

2.2
Online Facility Assignment Problem on a Line

By setting
X=\mathbb{R}
, we can regard
the online facility assignment problem with
k
servers
as the online facility assignment problem
on a line
with
k
servers, and
we denote such a problem by

{\rm OFAL}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
for general capacities and

{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
for uniform capacities.
In this case, it is immediate that

p(s_{j})\in\mathbb{R}
for each
1\leq j\leq k

and
p(r_{i})\in\mathbb{R}
for each
1\leq i\leq n
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
p(s_{1})<\cdots<p(s_{k})
and
let

d_{j}=p(s_{j+1})-p(s_{j})

(2.1)

for each
1\leq j\leq k-1
.
For the case
that
d_{1}=\cdots=d_{k-1}=d
with some constant
d>0
, we use

{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
and
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)

to denote
{\rm OFAL}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
and

{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
with equidistant
k
servers, respectively.
For the subsequent discussion, we assume without loss of generality
that
d=1
for both

{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
and
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
.

In the rest of the paper, we will abuse the notations

r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
s_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)

instead of
p(r_{i})\in\mathbb{R}
and
p(s_{j})\in\mathbb{R}
, respectively,
when those are clear from the context.

2.3
Notations and Terminologies

For a request sequence
\sigma
, let
|\sigma|
be
the length of
\sigma
,
i.e.,
|\sigma|=n
for
\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}
.
For a request sequence
\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}
and
a request sequence
\tau=\tilde{r}_{1}\cdots\tilde{r}_{m}
,
we use
\sigma\circ\tau
to denote the concatenation of

\sigma
and
\tau
, i.e.,
\sigma\circ\tau=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}\tilde{r}_{1}\cdots\tilde{r}_{m}
.

For
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
, let
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
be the
set of
k
servers.
For an (online/offline) algorithm
da
for
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})

and a request sequence

\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}
,
we use
s_{\rm da}(r_{i};\sigma|S)
to denote
the server with which
da
matches
r_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq n
when

da
processes
\sigma
.
Let
\mbox{{\sc da}}(r_{i};\sigma|S)

be the cost incurred by
da
to match
r_{i}
with

s_{\rm da}(r_{i};\sigma|S)
, i.e.,

\mbox{{\sc da}}(r_{i};\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc cost}}(r_{i},s_{\rm da}(r_{i};\sigma|S))
.
For a subsequence
\tau=r_{i_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{m}}
of

\sigma
, we use
\mbox{{\sc da}}(\tau;\sigma|S)
to denote
the total cost incurred by
da
to match each
r_{i_{h}}

with the server
s_{\rm da}(r_{i_{h}};\sigma|S)
, i.e.,

\mbox{{\sc da}}(\tau;\sigma|S)=\sum_{h=1}^{m}\mbox{{\sc da}}(r_{i_{h}};\sigma|S).

When
\tau=\sigma
, we simply write
\mbox{{\sc da}}(\sigma|S)
instead of

\mbox{{\sc da}}(\sigma;\sigma|S)
.
On defining
s_{\rm da}(r_{i};\sigma|S)
,
\mbox{{\sc da}}(r_{i};\sigma|S)
,

\mbox{{\sc da}}(\tau;\sigma|S)
, and

\mbox{{\sc da}}(\sigma|S)
,
it is crucial to indicate the set
S
of servers explicitly, whose role will become
clear in Theorem
5.3
(especially
in Claims
5.1
and
5.2
).
We use
opt
to denote the optimal
offline
algorithm, i.e.,

opt
knows the entire sequence
\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}

in advance and
minimizes
the total cost to match each request
r_{i}
with the server
s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)
.
Let
alg
be an online algorithm for
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
and

\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}
be a request sequence.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
,
we define the
type
of a request
r_{i}
w.r.t.
alg
by

{\rm type}_{\rm alg}(r_{i})=\langle{s_{\rm alg}(r_{i};\sigma|S),s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)}\rangle.

To evaluate the performance of an online algorithm
alg
,
we use the (strict) competitive ratio.
We say that
alg
is
c
-competitive if

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
for
any request sequence
\sigma
.
The competitive ratio
{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}})
of
alg
is defined
to be the infimum of
c\geq 1
such that
alg
is
c
-competitive, i.e.,

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}})=\inf\{c\geq 1:\mbox{{\sc alg} is $c$-competitive}\}
.

2.4
Technical Lemmas

As mentioned in Section
2.1
, the online facility
assignment problem

{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
is defined by the set

S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\}

of
k
servers, where the server
s_{j}
has the capacity
c_{j}

for each
1\leq j\leq k
, and for any request sequence

\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}

to
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
, the condition that

n\leq c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}
must be met.

In this subsection, we show that for the design of online algorithms for

{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
,
it is sufficient to deal with the case that
n=c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}

(in Lemma
2.1
) and
it is sufficient to deal with the case that

c_{1}=\cdots c_{k}=\ell
(in Lemma
2.2
).

Lemma 2.1
.

For
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
, let

L=c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}
.
For any
c\geq 1
,
\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)

for any request sequence
\sigma
such that
|\sigma|=L
iff

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)
for
any request sequence
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
|\sigma^{\prime}|\leq L
.

Proof:
If

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)
for
any request sequence
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
|\sigma^{\prime}|\leq L
, then
it is obvious that

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
for
any request sequence
\sigma
such that
|\sigma|=L
.

We show that if
\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
for
any request sequence
\sigma
such that
|\sigma|=L
, then

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)
for
any request sequence
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
|\sigma^{\prime}|<L
.
For a request sequence
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
|\sigma^{\prime}|<L
,
define
a request sequence
\sigma
as follows:
Append
L-|\sigma^{\prime}|
requests at the end of
\sigma^{\prime}
to make
free servers of
opt

full
with zero cost.
Note that
|\sigma|=L
, and
we have that
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
and

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)\leq\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)
. Thus it follows that
for any request sequence
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
|\sigma^{\prime}|<L
,

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S)\leq\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)=c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma^{\prime}|S),

where the 2nd inequality follows from the assumption that

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
for any request sequence

\sigma
such that
|\sigma|=L
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
\ell\geq 1
, any
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
such that

1\leq c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\leq\ell
, and any
c\geq 1
,
there exists a
c
-competitive algorithm for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)

iff there exists a
c
-competitive algorithm for

{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
.

Proof:

If there exists a
c
-competitive algorithm
alg
for

{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
, then
by setting
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{k}=\ell
, it is obvious that

alg
is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
.

We show that if
alg
is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
, then
there exists a
c
-competitive algorithm
\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime}
for

{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
.
For each
1\leq j\leq k
, let
m_{j}=\ell-c_{j}\geq 0

and
\sigma^{\prime}_{j}
be
a sequence of

. Let

\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma^{\prime}_{k}
.
Define an online

\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime}
for
{\rm OFA}(k,\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k})
as follows:
From Lemma
2.1
, it suffices to consider

\sigma
such that

|\sigma|=c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}
, and

\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime}
simulates

. Note that

,

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\rho|S)\leq c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\rho|S)

3
Capacity-Insensitive Algorithms

In this section, we introduce a novel notion of “capacity-insensitive algorithms.”
We first define a class of MPFS (Most Preferred Free Servers) algorithms.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
alg
be an online algorithm for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
. We say that

alg
is an
MPFS (Most Preferred Free Servers)
algorithm if
for any request sequence
\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{n}
such that
n=k\ell
,
it behaves as follows: For each
1\leq i\leq n
,

1.

the priority of servers for
r_{i}
is determined by only the position of
r_{i}
;

2.

alg
matches
r_{i}
with a server with the highest priority
among free servers.

Let
{\cal MPFS}
be the class of MPFS algorithms.
In the subsequent discussion, we show that for any
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}

and any
\ell\geq 1

We begin by introducing several ingredients to analyze the properties of

Definition 3.2

For a bipartite graph

Theorem 3.1

From Theorem

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

and
\sigma

be a request sequence such that

. Then

of

Proof:

Fix a request sequence
\sigma
with
|\sigma|=k\ell
arbitrarily and define
a bipartite graph

and

We show the lemma by induction on

For

\ell

For the bipartite graph

for each

there exists a request

such that

Let

by deleting
\sigma_{\ell}

from

and from the induction hypothesis, it follows that

of
\sigma^{\prime}

. Thus

is a

and this completes the proof of the lemma.

Informally, an algorithm

of

\sigma_{i}

on

for

.

Let

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S),

(3.2)

where

The following lemma plays a crucial role to discuss the properties
of algorithms in
{\cal MPFS}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
alg
be a
separable
online algorithm for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
.
For any
\ell\geq 1
, if
alg
is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)
, then

alg
is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
.

Proof:
Fix a request sequence
\sigma
such that

|\sigma|=k\ell
arbitrarily. Then from the assumption that

alg
is separable for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
, there exists a
coprime partition
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
of
\sigma
w.r.t.
alg

that satisfies (
3.1
) and (
3.2
),
where
\sigma_{i}=r_{1}^{i}\cdots r_{k}^{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
. Then

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma;\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mbox{{\sc alg}}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma|S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mbox{{\sc alg}}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma_{i}|S);

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma;\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma|S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i}|S).

Since

is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)
, we have that

\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma_{i}|S)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i}|S)=c\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i}|S)=c\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S),

and this implies that

is

-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)

for any

.

The following theorem is one of the main results that captures the
crucial property of algorithms in
{\cal MPFS}

and plays an important role in the subsequent discussions.

Theorem 3.2
.

If
alg
for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
is in
{\cal MPFS}
, then
alg
is separable.

Proof:
Fix an arbitrary
algorithm
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}

for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
and a request sequence
\sigma
such
that
|\sigma|=k\ell
.
From Lemma
3.1
, it follows that there exists a coprime partition

\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
of
\sigma
w.r.t.
alg
, where

\sigma_{i}=r_{1}^{i}\cdots r_{k}^{i}
is a subsequence of
\sigma
for
each
1\leq i\leq\ell
. To complete the proof of the theorem,
it suffices to show the following two facts:

(1)

there exists an optimal offline algorithm
opt
such that

s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma|S)=s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S)

for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
and each
1\leq j\leq k
;

(2)

s_{\rm alg}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma|S)=s_{\rm alg}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S)

for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
and each
1\leq j\leq k
.

For the fact (1), it is obvious that

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i}|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i};\sigma_{i}|S)\leq\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i};\sigma|S)
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
.
Assume that there exists an
1\leq h\leq\ell

such that
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h}|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S)<\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)
. Define
a subsequence
\sigma-\sigma_{h}
of
\sigma
by deleting
\sigma_{h}

from
\sigma
. Then

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)+\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma-\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)>\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S)+\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma-\sigma_{h};\sigma|S),

and this contradicts the optimality of
opt
. Thus we have that

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i}|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{i};\sigma|S)

for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
,
which is achieved
in such a way that
opt
for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
matches

r_{j}^{i}
with
s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S)
, i.e.,

s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma|S)=s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma_{i}|S)

for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
and each
1\leq j\leq k
.

We turn to show the fact (2). For simplicity, let

s_{\rm alg}(r_{j}^{i};\sigma|S)=s_{j}^{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
and

1\leq j\leq k
. From the definition of coprime partition, it follows that

\{s_{1}^{i},\ldots,s_{k}^{i}\}=S
for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
.
After
alg
matches
r_{j}^{i}
with
s_{j}^{i}
,

alg
matches
r_{j+1}^{i},\ldots,r_{k}^{i}
with

s_{j+1}^{i},\ldots,s_{k}^{i}
, respectively,
and this implies that
s_{j}^{i},\ldots,s_{k}^{i}
are
free

just before
alg
matches
r_{j}^{i}
with
s_{j}^{i}
.
Since
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
, we have that

s_{j}^{i}
has the highest priority for
r_{j}^{i}
among

s_{j}^{i},\ldots,s_{k}^{i}
. As mentioned in Remark
2.1
,
we regard
alg
as an algorithm for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)

for the request sequence
\sigma_{i}
. When processing
r_{j}^{i}
,
it is immediate that
s_{1}^{i},\ldots,s_{j-1}^{i}
are
full
and

s_{j}^{i},\ldots,s_{k}^{i}
are
free
. Thus from the fact that

s_{j}^{i}
has the highest priority for
r_{j}^{i}
among
free servers

s_{j}^{i},\ldots,s_{k}^{i}
, it follows that
alg
for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)

matches
r_{j}^{i}
with
s_{j}^{i}
.

Then
we have the following immediate
corollary to Theorem
3.2
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
.
For any

and any
\ell\geq 1
,

is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)
iff

alg
is
c
-competitive for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
.

Proof:
It is obvious that

alg
for
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
is
c
-competitive for any
\ell\geq 1
,
then
alg
for
{\rm OFA}(k,1)
is
c
-competitive. The converse follows from
Lemma
3.2
and Theorem
3.2
.

In this section, we introduce a notion of

algorithms,
which will play a crucial role to analyze upper bounds on the
competitive ratio of algorithms in
{\cal MPFS}
. Before discussing
the faithful algorithms, we introduce
tours
for a set of fixed points
on a line and we also observe the related notions and properties.

4.1
Tours and Their Properties

Let
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
be a set of
distinct
n
points (on a line), i.e.,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
We say that

T:v_{1}\to v_{2}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
is a
tour
on
V
and
define the
length
of
T
by

\ell(T)=|v_{n}-v_{1}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|v_{i}-v_{i+1}|.

For each
2\leq i\leq n
,
we identify
v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
with

v_{i}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{i}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
T:v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
be a tour on

V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. We say that a pair
(v_{i},v_{j})
is
conflicting
in
T

if
v_{i}\leq v_{j+1}<v_{i+1}\leq v_{j}
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
T:v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
be a tour on

V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
.
We say that
v_{i}
is a
relay point
in
T
if

v_{i-1}<v_{i}<v_{i+1}
or
v_{i-1}>v_{i}>v_{i+1}
,
where
v_{0}=v_{n}
for
i=1

and
v_{n+1}=v_{1}
for
i=n
,
and say that
v_{i}

is a
turning point
in
T
otherwise.

For a tour
T
on
V
, we use
{\rm cf}(T)
to denote the set of all
conflicting pairs in
T
and use

{\rm tp}(T)
to denote the set of all turning points in
T
.

Remark 4.1
.

|{\rm tp}(T)|
is even
for any tour
T
on
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
.
\square

Definition 4.3
.

Let
T:v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
be a tour on

V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
.
We say that
\tilde{T}:t_{1}\to\cdots\to t_{2m}\to t_{1}

is a

of

consists of
all the turning points in

by skipping
all the relay points in
T
.

For the contracted tour
\tilde{T}
of

, it is immediate that

|{\rm tp}(T)|=|{\rm tp}(\tilde{T})|
. Note that conflicting pairs in
\tilde{T}
can be defined in a way
similar to the conflicting pairs in
T
.
Let
{\rm cf}(\tilde{T})
be the set of all

\tilde{T}
.

Remark 4.2
.

For a tour
T:v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
on
V
,
let

.
Then for a conflicting pair

is

\square

For a tour
T:v_{1}\to\cdots\to v_{n}\to v_{1}
,
let

of

. For each
1\leq p\leq 2m

let

,

Lemma 4.1
.

For a tour

Proof:

For a conflicting pair

to
t_{i+1}

t_{j}

Definition 4.4
.

For the contracted tour

If
t_{2i}\leq t_{2i+2}
, then

t_{2i-1}\to t_{2i}\to t_{2i+1}\to t_{2i+2}

Lemma 4.3

Let

Proof:
Let
\tilde{T}:t_{1}\to\cdots\to t_{2m}\to t_{1}

be the contracted tour of

Since

We show this by induction on

For the case that

conflicting pair in
\tilde{T}
.

Assume that the lemma holds for

for any contracted tour
\tilde{T}
with

there exist at least
m

conflicting pairs in
\tilde{T}
. Let

be the

From Lemma

, there exists a detour

\tau_{i}\to\tau_{i+1}\to\tau_{i+2}\to\tau_{i+3}

For the contracted tour

Note that

Then by the induction hypothesis,

Definition 4.5

r_{i}

r_{i}>q_{i}\geq s_{\rm alg}(r_{i};\sigma|S)

Let

Then we have the following lemma on

for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)

Lemma 4.4
.

opt
is faithful for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
.

Proof:

We consider

for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
.
For a request sequence
\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{k\ell}
,
let
r_{i}
be a request such that
|r_{i}-s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)|>0

For the request

be a request such that

r_{i}>r_{i}^{\prime}\geq s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)

r_{i}<r_{i}^{\prime}\leq s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)

Define a request

\tau=q_{1}\cdots q_{k\ell}

by replacing

with

in
\sigma

for each

r_{h}^{\prime}&h=i\end{array}\right.

Note that the request sequence
\tau
is closer to
S
than
\sigma
w.r.t.

.
Let

\displaystyle s_{\rm opt}(\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s_{\rm opt}(r_{1};\sigma|S),\ldots,s_{\rm opt}(r_{k\ell};\sigma|S));

\displaystyle s_{\rm opt}(\tau|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s_{\rm opt}(q_{1};\tau|S),\ldots,s_{\rm opt}(q_{k\ell};\tau|S)),

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{k\ell}|q_{h}-s_{\rm opt}(r_{h};\sigma|S)|+|q_{i}-r_{i}|

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{k\ell}|q_{h}-s_{\rm opt}(q_{h};\sigma|S)|+|q_{i}-r_{i}|

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{k\ell}|r_{h}-s_{\rm opt}(q_{h};\sigma|S)|\geq\sum_{h=1}^{k\ell}|r_{h}-s_{\rm opt}(r_{h};\sigma|S)|.

This implies that

Then it follows that

and

By iterating this process, we can conclude that

To analyze the competitive ratio for faithful algorithms, the following notion is

Definition 4.7
.

Let

be an online algorithm for

for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

Then for any request sequence

, there exists an opposite

\mbox{{\sc rate}}(\sigma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)}{\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)}&\mbox{\rm if }\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)>0;\\
\infty&\mbox{\rm if }\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)=0,\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)>0;\\
1&\mbox{\rm if }\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)=0.\end{array}\right.

Proof:
If
\sigma
is opposite w.r.t.
alg
, then
it suffices to set
\tau=\sigma
. Then we assume that

\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{k\ell}
is
not

opposite w.r.t.

(1)

(2)

r_{i}>\max\{s_{\rm alg}(r_{i};\sigma|S),s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)\}

For the case (1), let
s\in S
be the server closest to

r_{i}
among

Let

be a request that is located on

For the case (2),

Single Tour for
GRDY

We first consider the case that a request sequence
\sigma

has a single tour w.r.t.
grdy

(and in Section
5.2.2
,
we also consider the case that
\sigma
has
multiple tours w.r.t.
grdy
).

Lemma 5.2
.

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)|
for any opposite
request sequence
\sigma
.

Proof:
Fix an opposite request sequence
\sigma

w.r.t.
grdy
arbitrarily and we
partition
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)
into

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)
and
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(\sigma)

as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(r_{i},r_{j}):(r_{i},r_{j})\in{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)\wedge i<j\};

\displaystyle{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(r_{i},r_{j}):(r_{i},r_{j})\in{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)\wedge i>j\}.

For each request
a
in

\sigma
, let
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a;\sigma)
be
the set of consuming pairs (in
\sigma
)
of the form
(a,\ast)\in{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)
, i.e.,

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a;\sigma)=\{(a,r):(a,r)\in{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)\}
, and
enumerate nonempty
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a;\sigma)
’s by

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{1};\sigma),\ldots,{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{\mu};\sigma)
.
Note that

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{1};\sigma),\ldots,{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{\mu};\sigma)
is a
partition of
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)
.
For each request
b
in

\sigma
, let
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b;\sigma)
be
the set of consuming pairs (in
\sigma
) of the form

(\ast,b)\in{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(\sigma)
, i.e.,

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b;\sigma)=\{(r,b):(r,b)\in{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(\sigma)\}
, and
in a way similar to the definition of
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a;\sigma)
’s,
we use

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{1};\sigma),\ldots,{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{\nu};\sigma)
to denote a partition of

{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(\sigma)
. It is immediate that

|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)|=|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)|+|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(\sigma)|=\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{i};\sigma)|+\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{j};\sigma)|.

Note that
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\mu}\}\cap\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{\nu}\}=\emptyset
.
If
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(a_{i};\sigma|S)\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{i};\sigma)|
for each
1\leq i\leq\mu
and

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(b_{j};\sigma|S)\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{j};\sigma)|
for each
1\leq j\leq\nu
, then
it follows that

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(a_{i};\sigma|S)+\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(b_{j};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{i};\sigma)|+\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{j};\sigma)|=\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)|.

Thus it suffices to show that (1)

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(a_{i};\sigma|S)\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{i};\sigma)|
for each
1\leq i\leq\mu
and
(2)
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(b_{j};\sigma|S)\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{j};\sigma)|
for each
1\leq j\leq\nu
.

For the case (1), fix
1\leq i\leq\mu
arbitrarily and
consider
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(a_{i};\sigma|S)
.
We assume that
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{i};\sigma)=\{(a_{i},r_{i_{1}}),\ldots,(a_{i},r_{i_{u}})\}
, where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{u}
are ordered in such a way that

s_{\rm opt}(a_{i};\sigma|S)\leq s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)<\cdots<s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)<s_{\rm grdy}(a_{i};\sigma|S).

Since
a_{i}
is the earliest request among
a_{i},r_{i_{1}},\ldots,r_{i_{u}}

by the definition of
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(\sigma)
,
we have that

s_{\rm grdy}(a_{i};\sigma|S),s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S),\ldots,s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)
are
free just before
grdy
matches
a_{i}
with

s_{\rm grdy}(a_{i};\sigma|S)
. This implies that

a_{i}\geq\frac{s_{\rm grdy}(a_{i};\sigma|S)+s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)}{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc opt}}(a_{i};\sigma|S)=|a_{i}-s_{\rm opt}(a_{i};\sigma|S)|=a_{i}-s_{\rm opt}(a_{i};\sigma|S)\geq a_{i}-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{i}-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)+s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{s_{\rm grdy}(a_{i};\sigma|S)+s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)}{2}-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)+s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s_{\rm grdy}(a_{i};\sigma|S)-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)}{2}+s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{1}};\sigma|S)-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{d_{min}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{u-1}\{s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{j}};\sigma|S)-s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{j+1}};\sigma|S)\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{d_{min}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{u-1}d_{min}\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot u=\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{+}(a_{i};\sigma)|,

where the 1st inequality is due to the assumption that

s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i_{u}};\sigma|S)\geq s_{\rm opt}(a_{i};\sigma|S)
.

For the case (2),
fix
1\leq j\leq\nu
arbitrarily and
consider
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(b_{j};\sigma|S)
.
We assume that
{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{j};\sigma)=\{(r_{j_{1}},b_{j}),\ldots,(r_{j_{v}},b_{j})\}
, where
j_{1},\ldots,j_{v}
are ordered in such a way that

s_{\rm grdy}(b_{i};\sigma|S)<s_{\rm grdy}(r_{j_{1}};\sigma|S)<\cdots<s_{\rm grdy}(r_{j_{v}};\sigma|S)\leq s_{\rm opt}(b_{j};\sigma|S).

In a way similar to the case (1),
we can show that

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(b_{j};\sigma|S)\geq\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}^{-}(b_{j};\sigma)|

for each
1\leq j\leq\nu
, and this complete the proof the
lemma.

By applying Lemma
4.6
to
grdy

for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
, we can show the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

For a request sequence
\sigma
, if

\sigma
has a single tour w.r.t.
grdy
on
S
,
then
\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma|S)\leq(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
.

Proof:
For
k=1
, it is immediate that

\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)\leq(4U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
with

U(S)=1
.
For any
k\geq 2
, let

T_{\sigma}:s_{h_{1}}\to\cdots\to s_{h_{k}}\to s_{h_{1}}
be a
single tour on
S
and

H(T_{\sigma})=|{\rm tp}(T_{\sigma})|\cdot(s_{k}-s_{1})
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cs}_{\rm grdy}(\sigma)|=\frac{d_{min}}{2}\cdot|{\rm cf}(T_{\sigma})|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{d_{min}}{4}\cdot|{\rm tp}(T_{\sigma})|=\frac{|{\rm tp}(T_{\sigma})|\cdot(s_{k}-s_{1})}{4\cdot U(S)}=\frac{H(T_{\sigma})}{4\cdot U(S)},

where the 1st inequality follows from Lemma
5.2
,
the 1st equality follows from Remark
5.1
, and
the 2nd inequality follows from Lemma
4.3
.
It is easy to see that

\ell(T_{\sigma})\leq H(T_{\sigma})
. Thus from Lemma

4.6
, we have that

\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma|S)\leq(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
.

5.2.2
Multiple Tours for
GRDY

In general, all the opposite request sequences
do not necessarily have a single tour w.r.t.
grdy
.
For the case that an opposite request sequence
\sigma
has multiple tours

\{T_{\sigma}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{t}
w.r.t.
grdy
, we regard each
T_{\sigma}^{i}

as a single tour for a subsequence
\sigma_{i}
of
\sigma

and derive an upper bound of the competitive ratio by combining each
of them for the request sequence
\sigma
.

Theorem 5.3
.

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc grdy}})\leq 4\cdot U(S)-1

for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
.

Proof:
In Theorem
5.2
,
we already showed that
\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma|S)\leq(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
for any request sequence

\sigma=r_{1}\cdots r_{k}
with
a single tour
T_{\sigma}
on
S
, i.e., a bijection
\pi_{\sigma}:s_{\rm opt}(r_{i};\sigma|S)\mapsto s_{\rm grdy}(r_{i};\sigma|S)
is cyclic on
S
.
In the remainder of the proof, we show that

\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma|S)\leq(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)
for any request sequence
\sigma

with multiple tours

T_{\sigma}^{1},\ldots,T_{\sigma}^{t}
on
S
, i.e., the bijection

\pi_{\sigma}
is not cyclic on
S
.
Assume that
\pi_{\sigma}=\pi_{\sigma}^{1}\circ\cdots\circ\pi_{\sigma}^{t}

for some
t\geq 2
, where

\pi_{\sigma}^{h}
is a cyclic permutation on
S_{h}
and
can be regarded as a directed cycle on
S_{h}

for each
1\leq h\leq t
.
Note that
S_{1},\ldots,S_{t}
is a partition of
S
.
For each
1\leq h\leq t
,
we define a subsequence

\sigma_{h}=r_{1}^{h}\cdots r_{k_{h}}^{h}
of a request sequence

\sigma
such that

s_{\rm grdy}(r_{j}^{h};\sigma_{h}|S)\in S_{h}
and

s_{\rm opt}(r_{j}^{h};\sigma_{h}|S)\in S_{h}

for each
1\leq j\leq k_{h}
.
Then we have that
|\sigma_{h}|=|S_{h}|

for each
1\leq h\leq t
. The following claims hold.

Claim 5.1
.

For each
1\leq h\leq t
,

\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})
.

Claim 5.2
.

For each
1\leq h\leq t
,

\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})
.

The proofs of Claims
5.1
and
5.2

are given in Sections
A.1
and
A.2
,
respectively.
Recall that
\pi_{\sigma}^{h}
is a cyclic permutation on
S_{h}

for each
1\leq h\leq t
. Then
from Theorem

5.2
, we have that
\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})\leq(4\cdot U(S_{h})-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})

for each
1\leq h\leq t
. Thus

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma|S)=\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma;\sigma|S)=\sum_{h=1}^{t}\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{t}\mbox{{\sc grdy}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})\leq\sum_{h=1}^{t}(4\cdot U(S_{h})-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\sum_{h=1}^{t}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma_{h}|S_{h})=(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\sum_{h=1}^{t}\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma_{h};\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma;\sigma|S)=(4\cdot U(S)-1)\cdot\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S),

where the 3rd equality is due to Claim
5.1
,
the 1st inequality is due to Theorem
5.2
,
and the 4th equality is due to Claim
5.2
,
and this completes the proof of the theorem.

5.3
Competitive Ratio for Greedy Algorithm
for
{\bf OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)

As an immediate consequence, we have the following corollary to Theorems

5.1
and
5.3
.

Corollary 5.2
.

For
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
such that

k\geq 2
,
{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc grdy}})=4k-5
.

Proof:
Since the distance between adjacent
servers
s_{j}
and
s_{j+1}
is the same, i.e.,
d_{j}=s_{j+1}-s_{j}=d

for each
1\leq j\leq k-1
, it is immediate that

U(S)=\frac{s_{k}-s_{1}}{d_{min}}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\{s_{j+1}-s_{j}\}}{\min_{1\leq j\leq k-1}\{s_{j+1}-s_{j}\}}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}d}{\min_{1\leq j\leq k-1}d}=k-1.

From Theorem
5.3
, it follows that

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc grdy}})\leq 4(k-1)-1=4k-5
. Since

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc grdy}})\geq 4k-5
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
by Theorem

5.1
, we have that
{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc grdy}})=4k-5
.

6
A Lower Bound on the Competitive Ratio of MPFS

In this section, we derive a lower bound on the competitive ratio
of algorithms in
{\cal MPFS}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
. Then

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}})\geq 2L(S)+1
,
where
L(S)=0
for
k=1
, and for any
k\geq 2
,

L(S)=\frac{s_{k}-s_{1}}{d_{max}}=\frac{s_{k}-s_{1}}{\max_{1\leq j\leq k-1}d_{j}}.

Note that
d_{j}=s_{j+1}-s_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq k-1
as defined in
(
2.1
).

Before presenting the proof of Theorem
6.1
, we introduce
several notions, e.g., surrounding servers
[
22
,
3
]
,
surrounding-oriented algorithms
[
22
,
3
]
,
specification of algorithms (in Definition
6.3
), and
feature points (in Definition
6.4
)
and we also provide several technical lemmas related to those notions.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a request
r
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
,
the
surrounding servers
for
r
are
s^{L}
and
s^{R}
, where

s^{L}
is
the closest free server to the left of
r
(if any) and
s^{R}
is
the closest free server
to the right of
r
(if any). If
r=s
for some
s\in S
and
s
is free,
then the surrounding
server of
r
is only the server
s
.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
alg
be an online algorithm for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
. We say that

alg
is
surrounding-oriented
for a request sequence

\sigma
if it matches every request
r
of
\sigma
with one of the
surrounding servers of
r
.
We say that
alg
is

surrounding-oriented
if it is
surrounding-oriented for every request sequence
\sigma
.

For surrounding-oriented algorithms, the following useful lemma

[
3
,
16
]
is known.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
alg
be an online algorithm for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
. Then there exists
a surrounding-oriented algorithm
\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime}
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
such that

\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime}(\sigma)\leq\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma)
for any
\sigma
.

According to Lemma
6.1
, it suffices to consider only

\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
that is
surrounding-oriented. To complete the proof of Theorem

6.1
, the following notions are necessary.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
.
For any pair of
1\leq i<j\leq k
, we say that
alg
follows
the specification
\mbox{{\sc spec}}(i,j)
if
alg
matches a request
r
with
the server
s_{i}
when
s_{i}
is free,
s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{j}
are full, and
r
occurs on
s_{j}
.

Definition 6.4
.

For any
k\geq 3
, let
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
.
We say that
P_{\rm alg}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}\subseteq\{2,\ldots,k-1\}
is a set of

feature points
of
alg
if it satisfies the following conditions:
Let
p_{0}=1
and for each
0\leq i\leq m-1
,

p_{i+1}=\max\{j\in\{p_{i}+1,\ldots,k-1\}:\mbox{{\sc alg} follows $\mbox{{\sc spec}}(p_{i},j)$}\}.

For
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
,
if
p_{1}
cannot be defined,
then
P_{\rm alg}=\emptyset
.

By classifying algorithms in
{\cal MPFS}

due to Definition
6.4
,
we show Theorem
6.1
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
:

For simplicity, assume that
s_{1}=0
and then
L(S)=\frac{s_{k}}{d_{max}}
. Let

a_{j}=\frac{s_{j+1}\cdot s_{k}}{s_{k}+d_{j}}

for each
1\leq j\leq k-1
, and it is immediate that

s_{j}<a_{j}<s_{j+1}
.

Let
k=1
and
\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime}\in{\cal MPFS}
for
{\rm OFAL}(1,\ell)
.
Since
{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime})=1
and
L(S)=0
for
k=1
, we have that

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime})=1=2L(S)+1
. Let
k=2
and

\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime\prime}\in{\cal MPFS}
for
{\rm OFAL}(2,\ell)
.
Since
{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime\prime})\geq 3

[
15
, Theorem 3.7]
and

L(S)=1
for
k=2
, we have that

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}}^{\prime\prime})\geq 3=2L(S)+1
. Thus
it suffices to consider the case that
k\geq 3
.

For
k\geq 3
, fix
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)

arbitrarily and let
P_{\rm alg}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}\subseteq\{2,\ldots,k-1\}

be the set of feature points of
alg
.
To derive a lower bound on the competitive ratio of

\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}
, we construct a request sequence

\sigma
as follows:

1.

For each
1\leq j\leq k
, generate
\ell-1
requests on
s_{j}
,
which leads to the state that the remaining capacity of

s_{j}
is 1 for each
1\leq j\leq k
.

2.

For each
2\leq j\leq p_{m}
such that
j\not\in P_{\rm alg}
,
generate a request on
s_{j}
, which leads to
the state that
s_{j}
is full for each

2\leq j\leq p_{m}
such that
j\not\in P_{\rm alg}
, and
the remaining capacity of
s_{h}
is 1 for
h=1
,
h\in P_{\rm alg}
, or

p_{m}<h\leq k
.

3.

Generate a request
r^{(1)}
on
a_{p_{m}}
and
let
s^{(1)}
be the server with which
alg
matches
r^{(1)}
.
Note that
s_{p_{m}}<a_{p_{m}}<s_{p_{m}+1}
.

4.

Generate a request
r^{(i+1)}
on
s^{(i)}
for each
i\geq 1
,
and continue the process
until a request is generated on
s_{1}
or
s_{k}
.

Since
alg
is surrounding-oriented, it suffices to
consider the following two cases:
(Case 1)
alg
matches
r^{(1)}
with
s_{p_{m}+1}
;
(Case 2)
alg
matches
r^{(1)}
with
s_{p_{m}}
.

(Case 1)
Since

alg
matches
r^{(1)}
with
s_{p_{m}+1}
, we have
two surrounding servers
s_{p_{m}}
and
s_{p_{m}+2}
for

r^{(2)}
appearing on
s_{p_{m}+1}
. If
alg
matches
r^{(2)}
with

s_{p_{m}}
, then this implies that
alg
follows
\mbox{{\sc spec}}(p_{m},p_{m}+1)
, but
p_{m}
is the last element of

P_{\rm alg}
. Thus

alg
must match
r^{(2)}
with
s_{p_{m}+2}
.
From this observation,
it is obvious that
alg
matches
r^{(i+1)}
with
s_{p_{m}+i+1}

for each
1\leq i\leq k-p_{m}-1
and matches
r^{(k-p_{m}+1)}
with

s_{p_{m}}
, where
r^{(k-p_{m}+1)}
is the last request of
the request sequence
\sigma
. Then we have that

\displaystyle\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-p_{m}-1}(s_{p_{m}+i+1}-s_{p_{m}+i})+(s_{p_{m}+1}-a_{p_{m}})+(s_{k}-s_{p_{m}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\cdot(s_{k}-s_{p_{m}})-(a_{p_{m}}-s_{p_{m}}),

and it is immediate that
\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)\leq a_{p_{m}}-s_{p_{m}}
.
Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{{\sc alg}}(\sigma|S)}{\mbox{{\sc opt}}(\sigma|S)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{2\cdot(s_{k}-s_{p_{m}})-(a_{p_{m}}-s_{p_{m}})}{a_{p_{m}}-s_{p_{m}}}=2\cdot\frac{s_{k}-s_{p_{m}}}{a_{p_{m}}-s_{p_{m}}}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\cdot\frac{s_{k}-s_{p_{m}}}{\frac{s_{p_{m}+1}\cdot s_{k}}{s_{k}+d_{p_{m}}}-s_{p_{m}}}-1=2\cdot\frac{(s_{k}-s_{p_{m}})\cdot(s_{k}+d_{p_{m}})}{d_{p_{m}}\cdot(s_{k}-s_{p_{m}})}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\cdot\frac{s_{k}}{d_{p_{m}}}+1\geq 2\cdot\frac{s_{k}}{d_{max}}+1=2\cdot L(S)+1.

(Case 2)
Recalling that

alg
matches
r^{(1)}
with
s_{p_{m}}
, we have
two surrounding servers
s_{p_{m-1}}
and
s_{p_{m}+1}
for
r^{(2)}
appearing on
s_{p_{m}}
. Since
alg
follows
\mbox{{\sc spec}}(p_{m-1},p_{m})
,
alg
must match
r^{(2)}
with
s_{p_{m-1}}
.
From this observation,
it is obvious that
alg
matches
r^{(i+1)}
with
s_{p_{m-i}}

for each

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{p_{m}}-s_{p_{m}})+(s_{p_{m}+1}-s_{1})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(s_{p_{m-i+1}}-s_{p_{m-i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(s_{p_{m}+1}-s_{1})+(a_{p_{m}}-s_{1})=s_{p_{m}+1}+a_{p_{m}},

\displaystyle\frac{s_{p_{m}+1}+a_{p_{m}}}{s_{p_{m}+1}-a_{p_{m}}}=\frac{s_{p_{m}+1}+\frac{s_{p_{m}+1}\cdot s_{k}}{s_{k}+d_{p_{m}}}}{s_{p_{m}+1}-\frac{s_{p_{m}+1}\cdot s_{k}}{s_{k}+d_{p_{m}}}}=\frac{2s_{p_{m}+1}\cdot s_{k}+d_{p_{m}}\cdot s_{p_{m}+1}}{d_{p_{m}}\cdot s_{p_{m}+1}}

for

for

Proof:

Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we dealt with the online facility assignment problem

{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
,
where
k\geq 1
is the number of servers and
\ell\geq 1

is a capacity for each server. As special cases of
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)
,
we also dealt with
{\rm OFA}(k,\ell)

on a line
, which is denoted by

{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
and
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
, where the latter
is the case of

{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
with equidistant servers.

In Section
3
, we introduced the class of MPFS (Most
Preferred Free Servers) algorithms and showed that any MPFS algorithm
has the capacity-insensitive property (in Corollary
3.1
).
In Section
4
, we formulated the
faithful
property
crucial for the competitive analysis in the paper.
In Section
5
, we analyzed the competitive ratio of
grdy

for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
and showed that
{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc grdy}})=4k-5

(in Corollary
5.2
).
In Section
6
,
we showed that for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
,

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc alg}})\geq 2k-1
for any
\mbox{{\sc alg}}\in{\cal MPFS}

(in Corollary
6.1
).
In Section
7
,
we proposed a new MPFS algorithm
idas
(Interior Division for Adjacent Servers)
for
{\rm OFAL}(k,\ell)
and showed that for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
,

{\cal R}(\mbox{{\sc idas}})\leq 2k-1
(in Corollary
7.1
), i.e.,

idas
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
is best possible
in all of the MPFS algorithms.

Notice that for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
,
any algorithm in
{\cal MPFS}
has the
capacity-insensitive property and the competitive ratio of

\mbox{{\sc idas}}\in{\cal MPFS}
matches
the lower bound of any algorithm in
{\cal MPFS}
. This implies that
for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
,
there does not exist an algorithm in
{\cal MPFS}
with the competitive ratio
better than that of
idas
. Thus for
{\rm OFAL}_{eq}(k,\ell)
,
one of the most interesting problems is to design
capacity-insensitive algorithms not in
{\cal MPFS}
with the better competitive
ratio than that of
idas
.
