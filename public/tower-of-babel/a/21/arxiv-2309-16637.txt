The p p -Adic Valuation Trees for Quadratic Polynomials for Odd Primes
Will Boultinghouse, Emily Hammett, Stephen Hu, Olena Kozhushkina, Rachel Snyder, Justin Trulen

Abstract
We examine the behavior of the sequences of p p -adic valuations of quadratic polynomials with integer coefficients for an odd prime p p through tree representations. Under this representation, a finite tree corresponds to a periodic sequence, and an infinite tree corresponds to an unbounded sequence. We use the polynomial coefficients to determine whether the p p -adic valuation trees are finite or infinite, the number of infinite branches, the number of levels, the valuations at terminating nodes, and their relationship to the corresponding sequences.



Keywords:
valuations, polynomials,
p
-adic integers, valuation trees, Hensel’s lemma.

Introduction

Let
p
be an odd prime. The
p
-adic valuation of a natural number
n
is defined as the highest power of
p
that divides
n
. If we write
n=p^{k}d
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}=\left\{0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
and
d
is an integer not divisible by
p
, then the
p
-adic valuation is defined as
\nu_{p}(n):=k
. A natural question to investigate is to classify the behavior of the sequence
(\nu_{p}(x_{n}))
, given some prime
p
and an integer sequence
(x_{n})
. For instance, Legendre’s formula
[
7
]
gives a closed form for the sequence
(\nu_{p}(n!))
:

\nu_{p}(n!)=\frac{n-s_{p}(n)}{p-1},

where
s_{p}(n)
denotes the sum of the base-
p
digits of
n
.
Medina et al.
[
8
]
considered sequences of
p
-adic valuations generated by polynomials; Bell
[
3
]
investigated sequences generated by polynomials with rational coefficients to study
p
-regular sequences; Byrnes et al.
[
4
]
considered sequences of
p
-adic valuations for polynomials of the form
f(n)=an^{2}+c
; Boultinghouse et al.
[
5
]
examined the sequences of 2-adic valuations generated by quadratic polynomials with integer coefficients. In this paper, we study the
p
-adic valuation of sequences generated by quadratic polynomials of the form
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
, where
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a\neq 0
, for an
odd
prime
p
.
If
p\mid\gcd(a,b,c)
, then define
k=\min\{\nu_{p}(a),\nu_{p}(b),\nu_{p}(c)\}
. It suffices to consider the corresponding function
f(n)p^{-k}
since the valuations for
f(n)
are those for
f(n)p^{-k}
increased by the value of
k
. Therefore, unless otherwise specified, we assume
p\nmid\gcd(a,b,c)
throughout.

We construct tree diagrams for each sequence to visualize these sequences. The top node of the tree represents the valuation of
f(n)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\nu_{p}(f(n))
does not depend on
n
, we stop the construction and label the node with its
p
-adic valuation: the sequence
(\nu_{p}(f(n)))
is constant. Otherwise, the top node splits into
p
branches, each branch corresponding to a subsequence
pq,pq+1,pq+2,\ldots,pq+p-1
for some
q\in\mathbb{N}
. We then repeat the evaluation process for each node to construct the tree, splitting when necessary. See Figure

for example.

We call any node with no variation

As mentioned above, we represent the terms of the subsequence as nodes. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n=p^{m}q+r
is a non-terminating node, then the successive branches are
p^{m}(pq)+r,p^{m}(pq+1)+r,\ldots,p^{m}(pq+p-1)+r
, and they are arranged from left to right in the order presented.

Define the
m^{th}
level of a tree
as the nodes that can be written in the form
n=p^{m}q+r
, where
r\in\left\{0,1,2,\ldots,p^{m}-1\right\}
. We call a tree
finite
if there exists a level that contains only terminating nodes. Otherwise, we refer to such a tree as
infinite
. In the case of a finite tree, we define the
number of levels
as the smallest
m
such that the valuation of
f(p^{m}q+r)
is constant for all
r
with respect to
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

Finite trees can also be viewed as
p
-
automatic sequences
(see Definition 1.1 in
[
1
]
). The set of subsequences
(\nu_{p}\circ f(p^{m}q+r))
, where
m\geq 0
and
0\leq r<p^{m}
, is called the
p
-
kernel
of the sequence
(\nu_{p}\circ f(n))
. We refer the reader to Allouche and Shallit’s book
[
2
]
and Bell’s paper
[
3
]
for more information.

In infinite trees, an
infinite branch
refers to a path that travels through non-terminating nodes and branches. An infinite tree contains at least one infinite branch. See Figures
2
and
3
in the Appendix for examples of infinite trees.

An infinite branch in the tree provides some key insight into the zeros of the polynomial
f
: any infinite branch in the tree associated to the polynomial
f
corresponds to a root of
f(x)=0
in the
p
-adic ring of integers
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. This result can be attributed to Byrnes et al.
[
4
]
.
We should point out that the case of a tree being finite corresponds to the sequence of
p
-adic valuations being both bounded and periodic. If a tree contains at least one infinite branch, then the sequence of
p
-adic valuations is unbounded. Lastly, we discuss the shape of the trees – this pertains to the tree structure: which branches terminate, at what level they terminate, at what level the branches split, and the valuations at the terminating nodes.

The following theorem provides the main results of the paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
such that
p\nmid\gcd(a,b,c)
.

1.

If
a\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
b\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then the tree has one node with valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))=0
.

2.

If
a\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
b\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then the tree has one infinite branch.

3.

If
a\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then we consider the discriminant
D=b^{2}-4ac
.

(a)

If
D=0
, then the tree has one infinite branch.

(b)

If
D=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
where
\nu_{p}(D)
is even and
\Delta
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
, then the tree has two infinite branches.

(c)

If
D=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
where
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd or
\Delta
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
, then the tree is finite with
\ell=\lceil\nu_{p}(D)/2\rceil
levels.

The result above will be proven by extending the ideas from Boultinghouse et al.
[
5
]
by using Hensel’s lemma and the theory of quadratic residues.

The following theorem describes the exact structure of all finite trees from part 3(c) of Theorem
1.1
regarding the valuations at each terminating node.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
such that
\nu_{p}(a)=0
. Let
D=b^{2}-4ac\neq 0
denote the discriminant. Suppose the discriminant can be expressed as
D=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
so that
p\nmid\Delta
, and the valuation
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd or
\Delta
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
. Set
\ell=\lceil\nu_{p}(D)/2\rceil
. Then there exists a natural number
S_{\ell}
with the property

\nu_{p}\left(\frac{b}{2a}-S_{\ell}\right)\geq\ell,

such that

\nu_{p}(f(n))=\begin{cases}2(m-1),&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv(p^{m-1}r-S_{\ell})\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{m}\mbox{\rm)}\ \text{for}\ m=1,2,\ldots,\ell;\\
\nu_{p}(D),&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv-S_{\ell}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{\ell}\mbox{\rm)},\\
\end{cases}

where
r\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,p-1\right\}
.

The theorem above will be proven using the translation arguments for the structures of finite trees developed in Boultinghouse et al.
[
5
]
.

Note that this theorem can be stated in the context of the tree associated with the function
f(n)
. Consider the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
. Let
r\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. At a given level
m
, where
1\leq m<\ell
, the tree has
p-1
terminating nodes at the nodes
n\equiv(p^{m-1}r-S_{\ell})\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{m}\mbox{\rm)}
with valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))=2(m-1)
. At the
\ell^{th}
level, the nodes are terminating. Additionally, each node of the form
n\equiv(p^{\ell-1}r-S_{\ell})\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{\ell}\mbox{\rm)}
has a valuation of
\nu_{p}(f(n))=2(\ell-1)
and the node of the form
n\equiv-S_{\ell}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{m}\mbox{\rm)}
has a valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))=\nu_{p}(D)
. See Figure
4
in the Appendix for such an example.

2
The
p
-adic numbers and other preliminaries

The
p
-adic numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
are all elements expressible as
x=\sum_{i=m}^{\infty}a_{i}p^{i}
where
m\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
,
a_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
, and
a_{m}\neq 0
.

With the
p
-adic expansion of
x
we have the equivalent definition of the
p
-adic valuation as

\nu_{p}(x)=\nu_{p}\left(\sum_{i=m}^{\infty}a_{i}p^{i}\right)=m.

By definition we let
\nu_{p}(0)=\infty
. Additionally, for
x,y\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
we have the identity

\nu_{p}(xy)=\nu_{p}(x)+\nu_{p}(y).

A space of interest will be the ring of
p
-adic integers, denoted as
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
:

\mathbb{Z}_{p}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}:x=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}p^{i}\right\}.

The next result is a well-known lemma that could be used to determine the existence of zeros in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for polynomials with integer coefficients. We cite the lemma without proof here, as stated in Section 6.4 in the work by Robert
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Hensel’s lemma)
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[x]
and suppose there is some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
that satisfies

f(x_{0})\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{n}\mbox{\rm)}.

If
\phi=\nu_{p}(f^{\prime}(x_{0}))<\frac{n}{2}
, then there exists a unique zero
\xi
of
f(x)
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that

\xi\equiv x_{0}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{n-\phi}\mbox{\rm)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu_{p}(f^{\prime}(\xi))=\nu_{p}(f^{\prime}(x_{0}))=\phi.

The conditions on
\xi
imply that only one zero can exist with those particular conditions. However, observe that there can exist more than one zero in general.

Lemma 2.2

(Theorem 2.6 Byrnes et al.
[
4
]
)
.

Any infinite branch in the tree associated to the polynomial
f(n)
corresponds to a root of
f(x)=0
in the
p
-adic field
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

Lemma 2.3

(Theorem 2.1 Medina et al.
[
8
]
)
.

Let
p
be a prime number and
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. Then
(\nu_{p}(f(n)))
is either periodic or unbounded. Moreover,
(\nu_{p}(f(n)))
is periodic if and only if
f(n)
has no zeros in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. In the periodic case, the minimal period length is a power of
p
.

From the proof of Theorem 4.1 by Medina et al.
[
8
]
, we have the following result regarding the existence of square roots in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
p
be an odd prime, and let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. For nonzero
a
, let
d
be the unique integer which satisfies
a=p^{\nu_{p}(a)}\cdot d
. Then there exists a square root of
a
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
if and only if
\nu_{p}(a)
is even and
d
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
.

Determining whether a tree associated with the quadratic function is finite or infinite is tied to the zeros of that quadratic function being in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The zeros of the quadratic depend on the square root of the discriminant being in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Hence, there is a need to work with quadratic residues. Next, we present the definition and some basic facts.

Definition 2.5
.

A number
r\in\mathbb{Z}
is a
quadratic residue modulo

p
if there exists
x\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
x^{2}\equiv r\pmod{p}
.

Next, we prove some simple ideas involving quadratic residues that will be useful later in the paper.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. If there exists
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
x^{2}=a
, then
\nu_{p}(a)=2k
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and suppose there exists
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
x^{2}=a
. Then
\nu_{p}(x^{2})=2\nu_{p}(x)=\nu_{p}(a)
. Since
p
-adic valuations must be integers, then
\nu_{p}(a)=2k
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. If there exists
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
x^{2}=a
, then
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Suppose there exists
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
such that
x^{2}=a
. From Lemma
2.6
, we have
2\nu_{p}(x)=\nu_{p}(a)
. Since
\nu_{p}(a)\geq 0
, it follows that
\nu_{p}(x)\geq 0
and thus
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
∎

In conjunction with quadratic residues, we will later make use of the Legendre symbol. We define the
Legendre symbol
, denoted by
\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)
, as a function of an integer
a
and an odd prime
p
such that

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\ \text{if}\ a\,\text{is a quadratic residue modulo}\ p\ \text{and}\ a\not\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)};\\
-1,&\ \text{if}\ a\,\text{is not a quadratic residue modulo}\ p;\\
0,&\ \text{if}\ a\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}.\end{cases}

Note the Legendre symbol can be calculated by

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\equiv a^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}\ \text{and}\ \left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}.

In the following lemma, we state several other properties of the Legendre symbol.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
a
,
b
be any integers. Then:

1.

If
a\equiv b\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
, then
\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{b}{p}\right)
.

2.

The Legendre symbol has the multiplicative property. That is,
\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\left(\frac{b}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{ab}{p}\right)
.

To determine the valuations, we frequently use properties of primes and polynomials. To simplify calculations, we mention a property of considering polynomials modulo a power of a prime
p
, extending the property introduced by Byrnes et al.
[
4
]
when the power of
p
is
1
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
f(n)
be a polynomial with integer coefficients, and let
p
be a prime. Then for
q,r\in\mathbb{Z}
with
i\geq 1
, we have
f(p^{i}q+r)\equiv f(r)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{i}\mbox{\rm)}
.

Proof.

Let
f(n)=a_{m}n^{m}+\cdots+a_{1}n+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
. Then applying the binomial theorem gives

\begin{split}f(p^{i}q+r)&=a_{m}(p^{i}q+r)^{m}+a_{m-1}(p^{i}q+r)^{m-1}+\cdots+a_{0}\\
&=a_{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}(p^{i}q)^{k}r^{m-k}+a_{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{k}(p^{i}q)^{k}r^{m-1-k}+\cdots+a_{0}\\
&\equiv a_{m}r^{m}+a_{m-1}r^{m-1}\cdots+a_{0}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{i}\mbox{\rm)}\\
&\equiv f(r)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{i}\mbox{\rm)}.\end{split}

In other words, when considering a polynomial
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
modulo
p^{i}
, the input can be reduced modulo
p^{i}
before proceeding with the evaluation of the polynomial.
∎

3
The case for
a\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}

In this section, we will prove parts (1) and (2) from Theorem
1.1
. We need the following lemma to prove part (1) of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
. Then
\nu_{p}(f(n))=0
if and only if
f(r)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
holds for all
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
. Then

\nu_{p}(f(n))=0
if and only if

\nu_{p}(f(pq+r))=0
for all
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
. From Proposition
2.9
, we see that
f(pq+r)\equiv f(r)\pmod{p}
. Therefore,
\nu_{p}(f(n))=0
if and only if
f(r)\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
holds for all
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
. If
a\equiv 0\pmod{p}
,
b\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, and
c\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then the corresponding tree is a single node with valuation zero.

Proof.

Let
p
be an odd prime. Let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
such that
a\equiv 0\pmod{p}
,
b\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, and
c\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. This implies that
a=p\alpha
and
b=p\beta
for some integers
\alpha
and
\beta
. Therefore for all
r\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
, we have that
f(r)=p\alpha r^{2}+p\beta r+c
. Since
c\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, then
f(r)=p\alpha r^{2}+p\beta r+c\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. Therefore by Lemma
3.1
, the tree of
f(n)
is a dot tree with a valuation of 0.
∎

The trees described in the above proposition are the simplest types of trees one can have, and we will refer to them as
dot trees
. The dot trees in Proposition
3.2
have only the valuation of zero. However, dot trees may have valuations other than zero. This case occurs when the quadratic coefficients are all divisible by
p
to some power. We can state this formally as the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c
. Then the corresponding tree has a single valuation of
k\geq 0
if and only if there exists a
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p^{k}
divides
a
,
b
, and
c
, but
p^{k+1}
divides
a
and
b
but not
c
.

Next, we prove part (2) of Theorem
1.1
. Again, we first need a lemma. As noted above, the tree associated with a quadratic is infinite if a zero of the quadratic is in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The nature of these zeros is tied to the discriminant of the quadratic. Furthermore, we see that the
p
-adic valuation of the discriminant plays a role in determining when the tree associated to a quadratic is finite or infinite. The next lemma is used to simplify a calculation.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
D=b^{2}-4ac
where
a\equiv a_{0}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
,
b\equiv b_{0}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
, and
c\equiv c_{0}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
with
a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
. Then
\nu_{p}(D)=0
if and only if
\nu_{p}(b_{0}^{2}-4a_{0}c_{0})=0
.

Proof.

Suppose
\nu_{p}(D)=0
. Notice that from a straightforward calculation,
D
is of the form
D=pt+b_{0}^{2}-4a_{0}c_{0}
, where
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
D
is not divisible by
p
if and only if
b_{0}^{2}-4a_{0}c_{0}
is not divisible by
p
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
. If
a\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
and
b\not\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
, then the corresponding tree is infinite with exactly one infinite branch.

Proof.

We first show that the corresponding tree has at least one infinite branch, and then we show that it has at most one infinite branch to reach the desired result of exactly one infinite branch.

To determine that there is at least one infinite branch, we apply Hensel’s lemma (Lemma
2.1
). Let
f(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
. Taking the derivative gives
f^{\prime}(x)=2ax+b
. We want to find some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
so that
f(x_{0})\equiv 0\pmod{p^{n}}
and
\nu_{p}(f^{\prime}(x_{0}))<\frac{n}{2}
.

Let
x_{0}=-cb^{p-2}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Recalling
a\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and applying Fermat’s Little Theorem, we have

f(x_{0})=a(-cb^{p-2})^{2}+b(-cb^{p-2})+c\equiv-cb^{p-1}+c\equiv 0\pmod{p},

so that
n\geq 1
. Then

f^{\prime}(x_{0})=2a(-cb^{p-2})+b\equiv b\pmod{p},

which implies
\phi=\nu_{p}(f^{\prime}(x_{0}))=0
. Since
\phi<\frac{n}{2}
, Hensel’s lemma guarantees the existence of a zero. Therefore the tree has at least one infinite branch.

We apply Lemma
3.4
to determine that there is exactly one infinite branch and exactly one zero of
f(n)
is in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and is not in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. That is, we determine that exactly one zero has a negative
p
-adic valuation. Consider the zeros
n_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{D}}{2a}
, where
D=b^{2}-4ac=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
. By hypothesis we have
a=p\alpha
,
b=p\beta+b_{0}
, and
c=p\gamma+c_{0}
with
b_{0}\in\{1,2,\ldots,p-1\}
, and
c_{0}\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
. Then it follows from Lemma
3.4
that
\nu_{p}(D)=0
, since
\nu_{p}(b_{0}^{2})=0
. Furthermore,

D=p(p(\beta^{2}-4\alpha\gamma)-2(2\alpha c_{0}-\beta b_{0}))+b_{0}^{2}\equiv b_{0}^{2}\pmod{p}.

It follows that
D=\Delta\equiv b_{0}^{2}\pmod{p}
is a quadratic residue. Since the valuation of the discriminant is even and
\Delta
is a quadratic residue, we have
\sqrt{D}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
by Proposition
2.4
. Furthermore, neither
b
nor
\sqrt{D}
is congruent to zero modulo
p
. Since
\sqrt{D}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
, it follows that
2\sqrt{D}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and therefore
\sqrt{D}\not\equiv-\sqrt{D}\pmod{p}
.

Furthermore, at least one
-b-\sqrt{D}
or
-b+\sqrt{D}
cannot be congruent to zero modulo
p
. If both were congruent to zero mod
p
, we could combine the two statements of
-b-\sqrt{D}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
and
-b+\sqrt{D}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
to get
\sqrt{D}\equiv-\sqrt{D}\pmod{p}
, which is a contradiction. Without loss of generality, suppose
-b-\sqrt{D}\not\equiv 0\pmod{p}
. This implies
\nu_{p}\left(-b-\sqrt{D}\right)=0
. Recalling
\nu_{p}(a)\geq 1
, we have

\nu_{p}(n_{1})=\nu_{p}\left(\frac{-b-\sqrt{D}}{2a}\right)=\nu_{p}\left(-b-\sqrt{D}\right)-\nu_{p}(2a)=0-\nu_{p}(a)<0,

and thus
n_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. It follows that at least one zero is not in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, and thus the tree has at most one infinite branch.
∎

With a little more work, we can have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
.

If
a\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
and
b\not\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
, then on the
m^{th}
level, there is only one non-terminal node with a valuation greater than or equal to
m
. The other
p-1
nodes are terminal with valuation equal to
m-1
.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.5
there must exist at least one non-terminating node at
n=pq+r_{0}
, where
r_{0}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
, such that
p\mid f(pq+r_{0})
. Furthermore, a calculation yields

a(pq+r_{0})^{2}+b(pq+r_{0})+c=p(apq^{2}+bq+2aqr_{0}+r_{0}^{2}a/p)+br_{0}+c,

thus
p\mid(br_{0}+c)
. That is,

br_{0}+c=pk_{1}\ \text{for}\ k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Thus, the valuation at this node is greater than or equal to
1
.

Additionally, suppose there exists a second non-terminating node at
n=pq+r_{0}^{\prime}
, where
r_{0}^{\prime}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}\setminus\left\{r_{0}\right\}
, such that
p\mid f(pq+r_{0}^{\prime})
. A similar calculation shows that
p\mid(br_{0}^{\prime}+c)
as well. That is,

br_{0}^{\prime}+c=pk_{2}\ \text{for}\ k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Taking the difference of
br_{0}+c=pk_{1}
and
br_{0}^{\prime}+c=pk_{2}
, we get

b(r_{0}-r_{0}^{\prime})=p(k_{1}-k_{2}).

Since
r_{0},r_{0}^{\prime}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
, it follows that
p\nmid(k_{1}-k_{2})
. But then
p\mid b
, which is a contradiction. Thus there exists exactly one non-terminating node. Furthermore, since

a(pq+r_{0}^{\prime})^{2}+b(pq+r_{0}^{\prime})+c=p(apq^{2}+bq+2aqr_{0}^{\prime}+r_{0}^{\prime 2}a/p)+br_{0}^{\prime}+c,

for all
r_{0}^{\prime}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}\setminus\left\{r_{0}\right\}
, the valuations at all terminating nodes must be zero.

Suppose by induction for all levels
i
up to
m-1
with
m\geq 2
the terminating nodes have a valuation equal to
i-2
and there is only one non-terminating node, with a valuation greater than or equal to
i-1
.

Now suppose the only non-terminating node is at
n=p^{m-1}q+r_{0}
, and so
p^{m-1}\mid f(p^{m-1}q+r_{0})
. This node splits into the nodes of the form
n=p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0}
, where
r\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Then

\displaystyle f(p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0})=a(p^{m}q+r_{0})^{2}

\displaystyle+b(p^{m}q+r_{0})+c

\displaystyle+2ap^{m-1}rr_{0}+2ap^{2m-1}qr+ap^{2(m-1)}r^{2}+bp^{m-1}r.

Without loss of generality, let the non-terminating node be at
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
, that is
r=0
. Since
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
is the non-terminating node, and we know the valuations are bigger than or equal to
m-1
, the above implies

\displaystyle f(p^{m}q+r_{0})

\displaystyle=a(p^{m}q+r_{0})^{2}+b(p^{m}q+r_{0})+c

\displaystyle=p^{m-1}(ap^{m+1}q^{2}+2apqr_{0}+bpq)+ar_{0}^{2}+br_{0}+c

\displaystyle=p^{m-1}k_{3}\ \text{for}\ k_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}\ \text{depending on}\ q.

Additionally, since
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
is the non-terminating node and
p^{m-1}\mid f(p^{m-1}q+r_{0})
, this implies
p^{m-1}\mid(ar_{0}^{2}+br_{0}+c)
. But if
p^{m}\nmid(ar_{0}^{2}+br_{0}+c)
, then the node
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
would have constant valuation equal to
m-1
, which contradicts the fact that this node is non-terminating. Thus we must have
p^{m}\mid f(p^{m-1}q+r_{0})
, which implies that
p\mid k_{3}
.

This fact, and the equation above, then imply

\displaystyle f(p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0})

\displaystyle=p^{m-1}k_{3}+2ap^{m-1}rr_{0}+2ap^{2m-1}qr+ap^{2(m-1)}r^{2}+bp^{m-1}r

\displaystyle=p^{m-1}(k_{3}+2arr_{0}+2ap^{m}qr+ap^{m-1}r^{2}+br),

for all other
r\in\left\{1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Since
b
and
r
are not divisible by
p
, then
br
is not divisible by
p
. Thus all other nodes are terminal with valuation
m-1
on the
m^{th}
level. This then completes the proof.
∎

4
The case for
a\not\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}

In this section, we first prove the case (3) from Theorem
1.1
and then we prove Theorem
1.2
. The following proposition comes in three parts. The first two parts prove cases (3a) and (3b) of Theorem
1.1
. The third part proves case (3c). Then, with the help of Corollary
4.4
, we prove Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
such that
\nu_{p}(a)=0
. Let
D=b^{2}-4ac
denote the discriminant and express
D=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
so that
p\nmid\Delta
.

1.

If
D=0
, then the tree has exactly one infinite branch corresponding to the zero
n_{1}=-\frac{b}{2a}
.

2.

For
D\neq 0
, if
\nu_{p}(D)
is even and
\Delta
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
, then the tree has exactly two infinite branches corresponding to the zeros
n_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{D}}{2a}
.

3.

For
D\neq 0
, if
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd or
\Delta
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
, then the tree is finite with
\ell=\lceil\nu_{p}(D)/2\rceil
levels. Additionally, there exists a number
S_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the tree associated to
f(n-S_{\ell})
has the following properties:

(a)

The number of levels is equal to
\ell=\lceil\nu_{p}(D)/2\rceil
.

(b)

Other than the last level, the node of the form
n=p^{j}q
where
1\leq j\leq\ell-1
is the only non-terminating node.

(c)

The valuation of the terminating nodes is equal to
2(m-1)
where
m
is the level. The only exception is the node
n=p^{\ell}q
on the last level.

(d)

The node
n=p^{\ell}q
on the last level has valuation
\nu_{p}(D)
. This is the maximum valuation of the tree.

Proof.

Part (1) is trivial with
D=0
.

For part (2), consider
D=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
with
\nu_{p}(D)
even and
\Delta
a quadratic residue modulo
p
. By Proposition
2.4
,
\sqrt{D}
is a
p
-adic integer. Since
\nu_{p}(2a)=0
, then
n_{1,2}=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{D}}{2a}
are
p
-adic integers as well.

For part (3), define
S
to equal in its
p
-adic expansion to

S=\frac{b}{2a}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}s_{i}p^{i},

where
s_{i}\in\left\{0,1,2,\ldots,p-1\right\}
.

Define
S_{\ell}
as

S_{\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}s_{i}p^{i},

which is the truncation of
S
. Note that by definitions of
S
and
S_{\ell}
from above we have

\displaystyle\nu_{p}\left(\frac{b}{2a}-S_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}(b-2aS_{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\ell.

Then

b-2aS_{\ell}=p^{\ell}k_{1},

where
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. A simple calculation gets us

\displaystyle f(n-S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=a(n-S_{\ell})^{2}+b(n-S_{\ell})+c

\displaystyle=an^{2}+(b-2aS_{\ell})n+aS_{\ell}^{2}-bS_{\ell}+c

\displaystyle=an^{2}+p^{\ell}k_{1}n+aS_{\ell}^{2}-bS_{\ell}+c

\displaystyle=an^{2}+p^{\ell}k_{1}n+f(-S_{\ell}).

The discriminant of
f(n)
is equal to the discriminant of
f(n-\tau)
, where
\tau\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus we have

p^{2\ell}k_{1}^{2}-4af(-S_{\ell})=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta.

Since
\ell=\lceil\nu_{p}(D)/2\rceil
, then
2\ell\geq\nu_{p}(D)
(that is, either
2\ell=\nu_{p}(D)
or
2\ell=\nu_{p}(D)+1
). It follows that
p^{\nu_{p}(D)}
divides
f(-S_{\ell})
since
4a
and
\Delta
are not divisible by
p
. Then we have

f(-S_{\ell})=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2},

where
k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
is not divisible by
p
. Now we have

f(n-S_{\ell})=an^{2}+p^{\ell}k_{1}n+p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2}.

At the
m^{th}
level the nodes are of the form
n=p^{m}j+p^{m-1}r
, where
1\leq m\leq\ell
and
r\in\left\{0,1,2,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Then

\displaystyle f(n-S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=f(p^{m}j+p^{m-1}r-S_{\ell})

\displaystyle=a(p^{m}j+p^{m-1}r)^{2}+p^{\ell}k_{1}(p^{m}j+p^{m-1}r)+p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2}

\displaystyle=ap^{2m}j^{2}+2ap^{2m-1}rj+ap^{2m-2}r^{2}+p^{\ell+m}k_{1}j+p^{\ell+m-1}k_{1}r+p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2},

when
r\neq 0
and

f(n-S_{\ell})=ap^{2m}j^{2}+p^{\ell+m}k_{1}j+p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2},

when
r=0
.

At a given level
m<\ell
, if
r\neq 0
, then notice that
2m-2
is the smallest exponent on
p
. We get

f(n-S_{\ell})=p^{2m-2}(ap^{2}j^{2}+2aprj+ar^{2}+p^{\ell-m+2}k_{1}j+p^{\ell-m+1}k_{1}r+p^{\nu_{p}(D)-2m+2}k_{2}).

But notice that
ar^{2}
is not divisible by
p
. Thus we have

\nu_{p}(f(n-S_{\ell}))=2m-2.

If
r=0
, then
2m
is the smallest exponent on
p
. We get

f(n-S_{\ell})=p^{2m}(aj^{2}+p^{\ell-m}k_{1}j+p^{\nu_{p}(D)-2m}k_{2}).

Since
j
is a natural number, then

\nu_{p}(f(n-S_{\ell}))\geq 2m.

In other words, at every level
m<\ell
, there is only one non-terminal node which is of the form
n=p^{m}j
, and all other nodes are terminating with
p
-adic valuation equal to
2m-2
.

At the
\ell
th level, our equations will now take the form

f(n-S_{\ell})=ap^{2\ell}j^{2}+2ap^{2\ell-1}rj+ap^{2\ell-2}r^{2}+p^{2\ell}k_{1}j+p^{2\ell-1}k_{1}r+p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2},

when
r\neq 0
, and

f(n-S_{\ell})=ap^{2\ell}j^{2}+p^{2\ell}k_{1}j+p^{\nu_{p}(D)}k_{2},

when
r=0
.

When
r\neq 0
, then
2\ell-2
is the smallest exponent on
p
. We get

f(n-S_{\ell})=p^{2\ell-2}(ap^{2}j^{2}+2aprj+ar^{2}+p^{2}k_{1}j+pk_{1}r+p^{\nu_{p}(D)-2\ell+2}k_{2}).

Since
ar^{2}
is not divisible by
p
, we have

\nu_{p}(f(n-S_{\ell}))=2\ell-2.

When
r=0
, consider two cases: either
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd or
\nu_{p}(D)
is even. If
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd, then
2\ell>\nu_{p}(D)
. Thus, we have

f(n-S_{\ell})=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}(apj^{2}+pk_{1}j+k_{2}).

Recall that
k_{2}
is not divisible by
p
, and so we have

\nu_{p}(f(n-S_{\ell}))=\nu_{p}(D).

If
\nu_{p}(D)
is even, then
2\ell=\nu_{p}(D)
. Then we have

f(n-S_{\ell})=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}(aj^{2}+k_{1}j+k_{2}).

Recall that
\nu_{p}(a)=\nu_{p}(k_{2})=0
and
\nu_{p}(k_{1})\geq 0
. Then we have

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(aj^{2}+k_{1}j+k_{2})

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}(4a^{2}j^{2}+4ak_{1}j+4ak_{2})

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}((2aj+k_{1})^{2}-k_{1}^{2}+4ak_{2})

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}((2aj+k_{1})^{2}-\Delta).

Assume there exists an
r\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
such that when
j=pk+r
, then

\nu_{p}((2a(pk+r)+k_{1})^{2}-\Delta)\geq 1,

which implies

(2a(pk+r)+k_{1})^{2}-\Delta=p\kappa,

where
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then it follows that

(2a(pk+r)+k_{1})^{2}-\Delta\equiv(2ar+k_{1})^{2}-\Delta\pmod{p}\equiv 0\pmod{p}.

Thus

(2ar+k_{1})^{2}\equiv\Delta\pmod{p}.

This implies that
\Delta
is a quadratic residue, which contradicts the assumption that
\Delta
is not a quadratic residue in the case of
\nu_{p}(D)
even. Therefore,

\nu_{p}(aj^{2}+k_{1}j+k_{2})=0

for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

With the above assumption, if we have an infinite tree, then we must have that
\nu_{p}(D)
is even and
\Delta
is a quadratic residue modulo
p
. To see this, consider the case when either
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd or
\Delta
is not a quadratic residue modulo
p
. Then
\sqrt{D}
does not exist in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, hence neither of
n_{1,2}
are
p
-adic integers. Therefore, the corresponding tree is finite.

Lemma 4.3

(Translation)
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
. Suppose
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, and set
g(n)=f(n-s)
. Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
0\leq r<p^{m}
, it follows that

\nu_{p}(f(p^{m}q+r))=\nu_{p}(g(p^{m}q+(r+s)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{m}\mbox{\rm)})).

Proof.

If the node
n=p^{k}q+d
terminates, the valuation
\nu_{p}(f(n))
at the node of the form
n=p^{k}q+r
is moved to the node of the form
n=p^{k}q+(r+s)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{k}\mbox{\rm)}
under translating an equation from
f(n)
to
f(n-s)=g(n)
. Similarly, if the node
n=p^{k}q+d
continues under
f(n)
, the node of the form
n=p^{k}q+(r+s)\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{k}\mbox{\rm)}
continues under translating an equation from
f(n)
to
f(n-s)=g(n)
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
f(n)=an^{2}+bn+c\in\mathbb{Z}[n]
such that
\nu_{p}(a)=0
. Let
D=b^{2}-4ac\neq 0
denote the discriminant. Suppose the discriminant can be expressed as
D=p^{\nu_{p}(D)}\Delta
so that
p\nmid\Delta
, and the valuation
\nu_{p}(D)
is odd or
\Delta
is a quadratic non-residue modulo
p
. Set
\ell=\lceil\nu_{p}(D)/2\rceil
. Define a natural number
S_{\ell}
as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
. Then

\nu_{p}(f(n))=\begin{cases}2(m-1),&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv(p^{m-1}r-S_{\ell})\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{m}\mbox{\rm)}\ \text{for}\ m=1,2,\ldots,\ell;\\
\nu_{p}(D),&\ \text{if}\ n\equiv-S_{\ell}\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p^{\ell}\mbox{\rm)}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

This follows immediately from applying Lemma
4.3
to Proposition
4.1
.
∎

Additionally, with a little more work we can get the following corollary from Proposition
4.1
part 1.

Corollary 4.5
.

If
a\not\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod}\ p\mbox{\rm)}
and
D=b^{2}-4ac=0
, then on the
m^{th}
level, the terminal nodes have valuations equal to
2(m-1)
and the non-terminal nodes have valuation greater than or equal to
2m
.

Proof.

First, note that

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(an^{2}+bn+c)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}(4a^{2}n^{2}+4abn+4ac)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}(4a^{2}n^{2}+4abn+b^{2}-b^{2}+4ac)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}((2an+b)^{2}-b^{2}+4ac)

\displaystyle=\nu_{p}((2an+b)^{2})

\displaystyle=2\nu_{p}((2an+b)).

By Proposition
4.1
part 1, there must exist at least one non-terminal node at
n=pq+r_{0}
, where
r_{0}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}
, such that
p\mid(2a(pq+r_{0})+b)
. Thus the valuation at this node is greater than or equal to two by the last line of the above equality. Now suppose there exists a second non-terminating node
r_{0}^{\prime}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
. Thus
p\mid(2a(pq+r_{0}^{\prime})+b)
. From these two statements, we have

2a(pq+r_{0})+b=pk_{1}\ \text{and}\ 2a(pq+r_{0}^{\prime})+b=pk_{2},

for some
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
which depend on
q
. Taking the difference, we have

2a(r_{0}-r_{0}^{\prime})=p(k_{1}-k_{2}).

Since
p\nmid(r_{0}-r_{0}^{\prime})
, we have
p\mid a
, which is a contradiction to the hypothesis. Thus all other nodes must be terminating with a valuation equal to zero.

Suppose by induction for all levels
i
up to
m-1
with
m\geq 2
the terminating nodes have a valuation equal to
2(i-2)
. Furthermore, suppose there is only one non-terminating node. This node has valuation greater than or equal to
2(i-1)
.

Suppose the only non-terminating node is at
n=p^{m-1}q+r_{0}
, and so
p^{m-1}\mid(2a(p^{m-1}q+r_{0})+b)
. This node then splits into nodes of the form
n=p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0}
, where
r\in\left\{0,1,2,\ldots,p-1\right\}
. Without loss of generality, suppose the non-terminating node is at
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
, that is,
r=0
. Then a calculation yields

2a(p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0})+b=2ap^{m}q+2ap^{m-1}r+2ar_{0}+b.

Since
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
is the non-terminating node, and we know the valuations of
2a(p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0})+b
are bigger than or equal to
m-1
, the above implies

2a(p^{m}q+r_{0})+b=p^{m-1}k_{3},

for
k_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}
depending on
q
. Suppose that
p^{m}\nmid(2a(p^{m}q+r_{0})+b)
. Then this non-terminating node
n=p^{m}q+r_{0}
would have valuation
\nu_{p}(2a(p^{m}q+r_{0})+b)=2(m-1)
, which contradicts the fact it is non-terminating. Therefore,
p^{m}\mid(2a(p^{m}q+r_{0})+b)
and this node must have a valuation greater than or equal to
2m
. But furthermore, we must have
p\mid k_{3}
. This and the above equation imply that

\displaystyle 2a(p^{m}q+p^{m-1}r+r_{0})+b

\displaystyle=2ap^{m}q+2ap^{m-1}r+2ar_{0}+b

\displaystyle=2ap^{m-1}r+p^{m-1}k_{3}

\displaystyle=p^{m-1}(2ar+k_{3}).

Since
p\mid k_{3}
and
p\nmid ar
, all other nodes on the
m^{th}
level must be terminating with valuation
2(m-1)
. This completes the proof.
∎

5
