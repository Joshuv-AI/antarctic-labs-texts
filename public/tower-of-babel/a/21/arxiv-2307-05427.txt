Effective Whitney Stratification of Real Algebraic Varieties
Martin Helmer, Anton Leykin, Vidit Nanda

Abstract
. We describe new algorithms to compute Whitney stratifications of real algebraic varieties. Using either conormal or polar techniques, these algorithms stratify a complexification of a given real variety. We then show that the resulting stratification can be described by real polynomials. We also extend these methods to stratification problems involving the so-called full semialgebraic sets as well as real algebraic maps.



1.
Introduction

The algorithmic tools available for studying algebraic varieties differ substantially depending on whether one works over the real numbers or the complex numbers. For real varieties, the
cylindrical algebraic decomposition
algorithm
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
15
,
53
]
serves as a fundamental tool, while complex varieties are often studied through
Gröbner basis
techniques
[
11
,
16
,
41
]
.
While these techniques are distinct both in approach and in the type of output produced, they share a worst-case complexity bound: both are doubly exponential in the number of variables. Nevertheless, a clear contrast exists between the complex and real cases in terms of the practical difficulty of solving fundamental problems; consider, for instance, the task of obtaining a closed form description of the connected components of a variety. This task is typically accomplished much more effectively over
\mathbb{C}
than over
\mathbb{R}
by computing a primary decomposition of the defining ideal via Gröbner basis calculation. Such disparities compel us to solve algorithmic tasks in real algebraic geometry by adapting computational methods which are available for complex varieties.

Real varieties arise more naturally in applications than their complex counterparts. Unfortunately, the geometry of a real variety is not readily revealed by its defining polynomials: for instance, every real variety is the vanishing locus of a single polynomial, whereas the minimal number of defining polynomials forms an important (and non-trivial) invariant for complex varieties. As such, one often seeks methods to subdivide a given variety into simpler regions which fit together coherently. And indeed, cylindrical algebraic decomposition provides precisely such a subdivision into contractible cells. One of our goals in this work is to outline what we view as a key step in an alternative framework for studying broad classes of applied and computational problems in real algebraic geometry.

Whitney Stratifications

There are many advantages to obtaining a cell decomposition of a variety — it becomes possible, for instance, to directly compute the Euler characteristic, and (with a bit more work) the Betti numbers. On the other hand, cells, being homeomorphic images of open balls, are rather rigid objects. Cylindrical algebraic decomposition tends to be impractical on larger (or more complicated) varieties precisely because one often has to perform many subdivisions in order to obtain cells; similarly, the number of these cells can also be doubly exponential relative to the number of variables
[
7
]
. A more general and flexible paradigm is furnished by the stratifications introduced by Whitney

3.3.
Polar Varieties and Equi-multiplicity

Our new algorithm to compute a Whitney stratification will make use of the criterion of Teissier from Theorem
3.3
. This criterion in turn is phrased in terms of the constancy of the Hilbert-Samauel multiplicity of points relative to a list of varieties; the property of having constant multiplicity relative to a variety for all points in a Zariski open set is often called
equi-multiplicity

[
31
]
. The following lemma gives a simple sufficient condition for equi-multiplicity; in spirit it follows many related results which have been summarized in Theorems 2.2.2 and Theorem 2.2.5 of the Appendix of
[
31
]
. The main difference is that here we use a multiplicity formula from
[
22
]
, which lends itself to a particularly elementary proof.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider varieties
Y\subset X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, with
X
a pure dimensional and
Y
nonempty, irreducible, and different from
P_{i}(X,L_{\bullet})
for all
i
. Let
\mu=e_{Y}X
be the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
Y
in
X
. For all points
z\in Y_{\rm reg}-\text{\rm P}_{r}(X,L_{\bullet})
, we have
e_{z}X=\mu
where
r=\dim(X)-\dim(Y)
.

Proof.

First note that multiplicity is unchanged under projective closure, hence we may replace the setup in the statement of the Lemma with
Y\subset X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. By
[
22
, Sec 2.1.4]
we may further suppose that

where all the
f_{i}
have the same degree. Let
\lambda_{ij}
be an

matrix of generic complex numbers, and for each

define the polynomial

. Setting

, Theorem 5.3 of
[
22
]
gives

(8)

\displaystyle\deg(Y)\mu=\deg(X)\deg(W)-\deg((X\cap W)-Y).

Fix a point

, and let

be a linear space of codimension

which contains

) changes when all varieties in sight are intersected with

. For generic choices of

, it is known (by
[
18

for instance) that

holds for

. We remark that a sufficient condition for

to be generic in the sense required here is for

to be transverse to

Now, by

, for generic

we also have

e_{z}X=e_{Y\cap A_{z}}(X\cap A_{z}).

Therefore,

(9)

\displaystyle e_{z}X-\mu=\frac{\deg((X\cap W)-Y)-\deg((X\cap W\cap A_{z})-(Y\cap A_{z}))}{\deg Y}.

Next, we seek to establish that the numerator on the right side vanishes unless

\text{\rm P}_{r}(X;L_{\bullet})
. To this end, let us recall that the numbers
{\left\{{\lambda_{ij}}\right\}}
which define

are generic. Therefore we may safely assume that

, and that
\dim(X\cap W)=\dim Y
, with the latter equality holding due to transversality. Therefore – at least when

is generic – both

and

are finite sets of cardinality

and

respectively. Returning to (

L_{\dim Y}
in the flag

, we conclude that

.
∎

We now employ the preceding result to construct a new Whitney stratification algorithm. It will be convenient to represent an

Remark 3.7

Note that since

Let

4.1.
Real Varieties

Our immediate goal here is to show that certain Whitney stratifications of

induce Whitney stratifications of

. We let
\iota:\mathbb{R}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the embedding of real points in complex Euclidean space.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a real algebraic variety.

(1)

The embedding
\iota
identifies
X
with the real points of
X(\mathbb{C})
.

(2)

Assume that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}X
equals
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X(\mathbb{C})
. If
\iota(p)
is a smooth point of
X(\mathbb{C})
for some

, then

is a smooth point of

.

Proof.

The first assertion is a tautology. Turning to the second assertion, set

. Since the roots of real polynomials occur in complex conjugate pairs, the variety
X(\mathbb{C})
is invariant under complex conjugation and
\iota(X)
equals the fixed point set of this conjugation. Noting that
\iota(p)
is a smooth point by assumption, the tangent space
T_{\iota(p)}X(\mathbb{C})
exists and has complex dimension
d
; this tangent space also inherits invariance under complex conjugation. Thus,
T_{\iota(p)}X(\mathbb{C})
is the complexification of a real
d
-dimensional vector space
V
whose elements consist of all real tangent vectors at
\iota(p)
; this
V
is evidently isomorphic to
T_{p}X
, as desired.
∎

Every finite descending chain

of radical ideals in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

produces an ascending flag

X_{0}\subset X_{1}\subset\cdots\subset X_{m}=X.

The next result shows that successive differences of

inherit a smooth manifold structure from the successive differences of

.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
W\subset Z
be a pair of real algebraic varieties in

i
-dimensional real manifold.

Proof.

There are two cases to consider — either the image
\iota(Z)
lies entirely within the singular locus

, or there exists some

lies entirely within

and hence that

; but since we have assumed

, we must have

, whence

is empty. On the other hand, let

be a point in

for which
\iota(p)
is a smooth point of

. It now follows from

has dimension

∎

It follows from the above result that if

is a Whitney stratification of

are either empty or smooth manifolds of the expected dimension. We show below these successive differences also satisfy Condition (B).

Corollary 4.4

X\subset\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
Y\subset\mathbb{K}^{m}
be algebraic varieties defined by ideals
I_{X}
and
I_{Y}
over a field
\mathbb{K}\in{\left\{{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}}\right\}}
. Let
\mathbb{K}[X]
denote the coordinate ring
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]/I_{X}
and similarly for
Y
; any morphism of varieties
f:X\to Y
canonically induces a contravariant ring homomorphism
f^{*}:\mathbb{K}[Y]\to\mathbb{K}[X]
.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a morphism of algebraic varieties over
\mathbb{K}\in{\left\{{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}}\right\}}
.

(1)

we say that
f
is
dominant
if
f^{*}
is a monomorphism (or equivalently, if the image
f(X)
is dense in
Y
).

(2)

we say that
f
is
finite
if it is dominant, and moreover, if
f^{*}
gives
\mathbb{K}[X]
the structure of an integral extension of
\mathbb{K}[Y]
.

It is a classical result, see e.g.
[
43
, Chapter II, Corollary 6.7]
,
that if
f:X\to Y
is finite in the above sense, then it is also a proper map for any field (see Remark
2.4
for the significance of this result in our context). Over
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, a dominant morphism is finite if and only if it is proper.

Throughout this section,
f:X\to Y
will denote a morphism between real algebraic varieties of the same dimension
d
; we will further require that the map
f_{\mathbb{C}}:X(\mathbb{C})\to Y(\mathbb{C})
is dominant and that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}X(\mathbb{C})=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}Y(\mathbb{C})=d
.

Definition 4.7
.

The
Jelonek set
of
f
is the subset
\mathbf{Jel}(f)\subset Y(\mathbb{C})
consisting of all points
y
for which there exists a sequence
{\left\{{x_{k}}\right\}}\subset X(\mathbb{C})
satisfying both

\lim_{k\to\infty}|x_{k}|=\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{\mathbb{C}}(x_{k})=y.

It is shown in
[
33
]
that
\mathbf{Jel}(f)
is either empty or an algebraic hypersurface of
Y(\mathbb{C})
; a Gröbner basis algorithm for computing the Jelonek set is given in
[
50
]
. It follows from this algorithm that if the Jelonek set is non-empty, then it is defined by a polynomial with real coefficients. Finally, it is shown in
[
33
]
that
\mathbf{Jel}(f)
is precisely the locus of points at which
f
fails to be finite. Thus, if we define

V(\mathbb{C}):=\mathbf{Jel}(f)\quad\text{and}\quad W(\mathbb{C}):=\overline{f^{-1}(V)},

then the restriction of
f
forms a proper map
(X(\mathbb{C})-W(\mathbb{C}))\to(Y(\mathbb{C})-V(\mathbb{C}))
— see
[
34
, Proposition 6.1]
for details. Note that the polynomials defining
V(\mathbb{C})
and
W(\mathbb{C})
are real; take
V
to be the real zero set of the polynomials defining
V(\mathbb{C})
, and similarly let
W
be the real zero set of the polynomials defining
W(\mathbb{C})
. It follows immediately that the restriction of
f
to the difference
(X-W)
constitutes a proper map to the difference
(Y-V)
.

Definition 4.8
.

The
Jelonek flag
of
f:X\to Y
is a pair
(W_{\bullet},V_{\bullet})

of flags, both of length
d=\dim X=\dim Y
:

\displaystyle\emptyset=W_{-1}\subset W_{0}\subset\cdots\subset W_{d}=X

\displaystyle\emptyset=V_{-1}\subset V_{0}\subset\cdots\subset V_{d}=Y,

defined via reverse-induction on
i\in{\left\{{d-1,d-2,\ldots,1,0}\right\}}
as follows. Starting with
V_{d-1}
as the real points of
\mathbf{Jel}(f)
, we

(1)

let
W_{i}
be
\overline{f^{-1}(V_{i})}
, and

(2)

let
V_{i-1}
be the real points in
\mathbf{Jel}(f|_{W_{i}}:W_{i}\to V_{i})
.

By construction of the Jelonek flag, at each
i
we have the following alternative:

(1)

if
V_{i}(\mathbb{C})
is nonempty
, then
\dim V_{i}(\mathbb{C})=W_{i}(\mathbb{C})=i
and the restriction of
f
forms a proper map
{(V_{i}-V_{i-1})\to(W_{i}-W_{i-1})}
; otherwise,

(2)

if
V_{i}(\mathbb{C})
is empty
, then
f|_{W_{i}}:W_{i}\to V_{i}
is not dominant; on the other hand,
f|_{W_{i-1}}
is dominant, but with
\dim W_{i-1}(\mathbb{C})>\dim V_{i-1}(\mathbb{C})
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(W_{\bullet},V_{\bullet})
be the Jelonek flag of
f:X\to Y
. Assume that
X_{\bullet}
is a Whitney stratification of
X
subordinate
W_{\bullet}
and that
Y_{\bullet}
is a Whitney stratification of
Y
subordinate to
V_{\bullet}
such that
f
is stratified with respect to
X_{\bullet}
and
Y_{\bullet}
(in the sense of Definition
2.3
). Whenever
\dim V_{i}=\dim W_{i}
holds, we have that for every
Y_{\bullet}
-stratum
R\subset Y
with
R\subset V_{i}-V_{i-1}
, the map
f|_{f^{-1}(R)}:f^{-1}(R)\to R
is a locally trivial fiber bundle.

Proof.

Since the stratification
Y_{\bullet}
of
Y
is subordinate to
V_{\bullet}
, for each stratum
R\subset Y
we have that
R\subset V_{i}-V_{i-1}
for some
i
, and also since
X_{\bullet}
is subordinate to
W_{\bullet}
we also have
f^{-1}(R)\subset W_{i}-W_{i-1}
. If
\dim(V_{i})=\dim(W_{i})
holds, then the map
f:(W_{i}-W_{i-1})\to(V_{i}-V_{i-1})
is proper. An appeal to the semialgebraic version of Thom’s first isotopy lemma
[
10
, Theorem 1]
achieves the desired result.
∎

5.
Full Semialgebraic Stratifications

A (basic, closed)
semialgebraic set
is any subset
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
which can be expressed as an intersection of the form
B:=X\cap C
where
X
is a real algebraic subvariety of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, while the set
C
, called an
inequality locus
, is given as follows:

(10)

\displaystyle C:={\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid g_{i}(x)\geq 0\text{ for }0\leq i\leq k}\right\}}.

Here the
g_{i}
’s are a finite collection of polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. By convention, when the number of inequalities
k
equals zero, we have
C=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, every algebraic variety is automatically a semialgebraic set in the above sense. The sets
X
and
C
are not uniquely determined for a given
B
in general — we may, for instance, safely remove any polynomial generator
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
from the defining ideal of
X
while adding
f\geq 0
and
-f\geq 0
to the inequality locus. It is therefore customary to omit
X
entirely and simply define
B
as the set of points which satisfy a collection of polynomial inequalities. We find it convenient to write
B=X\cap C
here because this allows us to isolate a relevant sub-class of semialgebraic sets.

Definition 5.1
.

A semialgebraic set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
full
if it admits an inequality locus
C
of the form (
10
), with the additional requirement that its subset

C^{\circ}:={\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid g_{i}(x)>0\text{ for }0\leq i\leq k}\right\}}

is an
n
-dimensional smooth manifold whose closure equals
C
.

The following example highlights an important class of full semialgebraic sets which arise in the Morse theoretic study of complex analytic varieties — see
[
20
, II.2]
.

Example 5.2
.

Whitney showed in
[
52
]
that every complex analytic variety
W\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
admits a Whitney stratification
W_{\bullet}
where each intermediate
W_{i}
is a complex analytic subvariety. Associated to each
i
-stratum
M\subset W_{i}-W_{i-1}
is a stratified space
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{L}_{M}
, which is called the
complex link
of
M
and defined as follows. Consider a point
p\in M
and let
A\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a generic
(n-i)
-dimensional affine subspace which intersects
W
transversely at a point
p\in M
. Let
B_{\epsilon}(p)
denote the disk containing all points within a sufficiently small radius
\epsilon>0
around
p
with respect to the standard euclidean distance in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Finally, let
\pi:A\to\mathbb{C}
be a generic linear form sending
p
to zero. For some positive
\delta\ll\epsilon
, one defines

\mathscr{L}_{M}:=W\cap B_{\epsilon}(p)\cap{\left\{{\pi=\delta}\right\}}.

The stratified homeomorphism type of
\mathscr{L}_{M}
depends only on the stratum
M
, and not on the auxiliary choices of
p,A,\epsilon,\pi
and
\delta
. Complex links provide
normal Morse data
for critical points of stratified Morse functions
f:W\to\mathbb{R}
. Although such a
\mathscr{L}_{M}
will not be a complex (or even real) variety in general, it is always a full semialgebraic subset of the affine space
{\left\{{\pi=\delta}\right\}}
. In particular, its inequality locus is the disk
B_{\epsilon}(p)\cap{\left\{{\pi=\delta}\right\}}
.

Given such an inequality locus
C
of a full semialgebraic set, we call
C^{\circ}
its interior and define its boundary as the difference

\partial C:=C-C^{\circ}.

This boundary is a semialgebraic subset of the real algebraic variety

(11)

Y_{C}:=\mathbf{V}\left(\prod_{1}^{k}g_{i}\right).

We adopt the usual convention that the product over the empty set equals
1
, which forces
\partial C=Y_{C}=\varnothing
when
k=0
.

Example 5.3
.

Below is a toy example of a full semialgebraic set
B=X\cap C
inside
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Here
X
is the union of red parabolas
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
while
C
is the region bounded by the black lines
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
and the black parabola
P_{3}
:

The interior
C^{\circ}
has been shaded gray, so
B
consists of that part of
P_{1}\cup P_{2}
which lies within the closure of the gray region. The variety
Y_{C}
is the union of curves
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
and
P_{3}
. The boundary
\partial C
consists of the four highlighted points which lie on this union of curves.

Our next result establishes that every full semialgebraic set
B=X\cap C
inherits a Whitney stratification from Whitney stratifications of the real algebraic varieties
X
and
X\cap Y_{C}
. (We recall for the reader’s convenience that flag-subordinate stratifications have been described in Section
2.2
).

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
B=X\cap C
be a full semialgebraic set, with
Y_{C}
as in (
11
), and let
X_{\bullet}
be a Whitney stratification of
X
. If
Y_{\bullet}
is a Whitney stratification of
X\cap Y_{C}
which is subordinate to the flag
X_{\bullet}\cap Y_{C}
, then setting

B_{i}:=(X_{i}\cup Y_{i})\cap C

produces a Whitney stratification of
B
.

Proof.

Since
B
is full, we know that the interior
C^{\circ}
of its inequality locus is a smooth open
n
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This means any open neighborhood of
X
, which is contained in
C^{\circ}
, inside
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is identical to the same neighborhood of
X
inside
C^{\circ}
and therefore, the intersections
X_{i}\cap C^{\circ}
form a Whitney stratification
X^{\prime}_{\bullet}
of
X\cap C^{\circ}
. Alternatively the fact that
X_{i}\cap C^{\circ}
forms a Whitney stratification
X^{\prime}_{\bullet}
of
X\cap C^{\circ}
follows since
C^{\circ}
is full dimensional, meaning the intersection
X\cap C^{\circ}
is transverse and hence the conclusion follows because the Whitney stratification of two spaces which intersect transversely is given by their intersection,
[
9
]
, see also
[
20
, Chapter 1, §1.2, (5)]
.

Let
Y^{\prime}_{\bullet}
be the subset of
Y_{\bullet}
-strata which intersect
\partial C
. Since
C
is the disjoint union of
C^{\circ}
and
\partial C
, it follows that the union of
X^{\prime}_{\bullet}
-strata and
Y^{\prime}_{\bullet}
-strata partitions
B
. It remains to check that Condition (B) holds for those strata pairs
(M,N)
of this union for which
N
intersects the closure of
M
. There are now three cases to consider, of which the two easy ones are handled as follows:

(1)

if both
M
and
N
are strata of
X^{\prime}_{\bullet}
, then Condition (B) holds because both are full-dimensional open subsets of
X_{\bullet}
-strata by construction, and
X_{\bullet}
is assumed to be a Whitney stratification.

(2)

if
M
is a
Y^{\prime}_{\bullet}
-stratum, then the fact that
N
intersects the closure of
M
forces
N
to be contained in
X\cap\partial C
, since both
X
and
\partial C
are closed subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus,
N
must also be a
Y^{\prime}_{\bullet}
-stratum in which case Condition (B) holds because
Y^{\prime}_{\bullet}
is Whitney.

Turning now to the third case, assume that
M
is an
X^{\prime}_{\bullet}
-stratum and
N
is a
Y^{\prime}_{\bullet}
-stratum. By construction,
M
must be (a connected component of) the intersection
M_{*}\cap C^{\circ}
for some
X_{\bullet}
-stratum
M_{*}
. Since
C^{\circ}
is
n
-dimensional, the tangent spaces
T_{x}M
and
T_{x}M_{*}
coincide for every
x
in
M
. Fix a point
p\in N
, and let
N_{*}
be the unique
X_{\bullet}
-stratum containing
p
in its interior. Since
Y_{\bullet}
is chosen subordinate to
X_{\bullet}\cap Y_{C}
, the
Y_{\bullet}
-strata are refinements of
X_{\bullet}
-strata, so
N
must be obtained by removing some (possibly empty) set from
N^{*}\cap\partial C
. It follows that
N
is a subset of
N^{*}
in a small ball around
p
. Finally,
(M,N)
must satisfy Condition (B) at
p
because
(M_{*},N_{*})
satisfy Condition (B) at
p
.
∎

The stratifications obtained in Theorem
5.4
provide a complete description of the flag
B_{i}
. Using the techniques of
[
23
,
36
,
47
]
, one can perform various fundamental algorithmic tasks involving such strata. These include testing whether the
i
-stratum
B_{i}-B_{i-1}
is empty for each
i
, and sampling points from the non-empty strata.

Remark 5.5
.

Using Theorem
5.4
in practice on a full semialgebraic set
B=X\cap C
requires constructing a stratification of the real variety
X\cap Y_{C}
; this is somewhat unsatisfactory from an efficiency perspective, since
X
and
Y_{C}
may intersect far away from
B
— returning to Example
5.3
, note that both
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
will intersect
L_{2}
at points far from
B
. We are not aware of any existing technique which can easily overcome such suboptimality.

6.
Complexity and Performance Analysis

There is a thorough complexity analysis of
WhitStrat
in
[
26
, Sec 7.2]
; the complexity analysis of
WTBStrat
is similar. Here we derive new complexity bounds and examine the practical performance of the new
WhitneyPolar
algorithm presented in Section
3
.

6.1.
Complexity Analysis of WhitneyPolar

Here we show that the complexity of
WhitneyPolar
from Section
3
is singly exponential in the number of variables. The main algorithmic task is the computation of polar varieties
[
4
,
3
,
47
]
, which reduces immediately to ideal elimination. Therefore, we will express the complexity of our algorithms in terms of the complexity of elimination.

Definition 6.1
.

Given positive integers
m,n,r
and
\delta
, let
\mathcal{E}(n,m,r,\delta)
denote the complexity of computing the elimination ideal
I\cap\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, where
I=\langle f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{r}\rangle
is a polynomial ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}]
with
\delta:=\max_{i}{\left\{{\deg f_{i}}\right\}}
.

A variety of classical sources (such as
[
49
, Theorem 1]
) reveal that

\mathcal{E}(n,m,r,\delta)\cong r^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(m^{2}n)}.

Most such bounds are based on resultant computation, but in practice it is often more effective to compute elimination ideals using Gröbner basis techniques, see
[
11
, Chapter 3, Theorem 2]
. It is shown in
[
5
, Theorem 2]
that after a general linear change of coordinates, a Gröbner basis of
I
may be computed in a number of operations bounded by
\mathcal{O}(9r\delta^{3(n+m)})
when
r\leq n+m
. Using either resultants or Gröbner bases, we may crudely bound
\mathcal{E}(n,m,r,\delta)\cong r^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n^{3}+m^{3})}
.

Remark 6.2
.

In the statements of the results below, we take our varieties in an
(n-1)
dimensional ambient space. When computing polar varieties, we perform elimination after adding one extra variable, hence obtaining a total of
n
variables in the setup of Remark
3.4
(1), or of
2(n-1)+1=2n-1
variables in the setup of Remark
3.4
(3). To avoid a proliferation of 1’s, we will frequently bound
n-1
by
n
below. Further, to be agnostic with regards to (1) vs (3) in Remark
3.4
, we use a bound of
2n
in some places.

The following result establishes that the cost of checking where Condition (B) fails via Lemma
3.5
and Theorem
3.3
is singly exponential in
n
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose that
X
is a variety (in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
) whose defining ideal
I
is generated by
r
polynomials of degree at most
\delta
. Let
Y
be a subvariety of
X_{\rm Sing}
with degree bounded by
\delta^{n^{2}}
. Then the number of arithmetic operations required to find all points in
Y
where Whitney’s Condition (B) fails with respect to
X
(via Lemma
3.5
combined and Theorem
3.3
), is bounded in
(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{5})}
.

Proof.

Although we use irreducible decomposition in our presentation of
WhitneyPolar
for simplicity of presentation, in fact one only requires equidimensional decomposition in Lines 3, 7 and 10. The equidimensional decomposition of an ideal generated by
s
polynomials in
k
variables of degree at most
D
may be computed in
\mathcal{O}(sD^{k^{2}})
, see
[
37
, Theorem 1]
or
[
14
]
. Hence, for
I
, we may bound this complexity in
\mathcal{E}(n,m,r,\delta)
.

From (
7
) and Remark
3.4
, and recalling we can always bound the degree of a variety by that of any complete intersection containing it, Bézout’s theorem guarantees that the degree of any polar variety of
X
is no larger than
\delta^{n^{2}}
. From Remark
3.4
, we see that the number of equations defining the polar variety prior to elimination is
2r+1
. Since the number of operations required to compute the elimination is

(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}(\delta)^{n^{2}\mathcal{O}(2n^{3})}\cong(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}(\delta)^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{5})},

it follows that the resulting number of generators is also bounded in this order.
∎

With the result for a single pair in hand we may now state and prove our complexity result for the entire
WhitneyPolar
algorithm.

Theorem 6.4
.

Suppose
X=\mathbf{V}_{\mathbb{C}}(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})
is an algebraic variety (in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
) where
\deg(f_{i})\leq\delta
and with
\mu=\dim(X_{\rm sing})
.
The number of arithmetic operations required by
WhitneyPolar
is of the order
(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}{\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n)}}^{2\mu+3}
.

Proof.

The degree of
X_{\rm sing}
is bounded by
\delta^{n^{2}}
, whence the complexity of the first step of
WhitneyPolar
algorithm is bounded in
(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}(\delta)^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{5})}
by Lemma
6.3
. The output stratification can have at most
\mu-1=\dim(X_{\rm sing})-1
many levels below the singular locus; at each level, the next level is computed by calculating polar varieties on the output of previous steps. Hence, having obtained a polar variety, on the subsequent level we will perform elimination on an ideal with at most
(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{5})}
generators of degree at most
(\delta^{n^{2}})^{n^{2}}=\delta^{n^{4}}
. This gives a cost of

(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{5})}(\delta^{n^{4}})^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{3})}\cong(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n^{7})}.

Performing equidimensional decomposition on such a polar variety would be bounded in
(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(2n^{5})}(\delta^{n^{4}})^{\mathcal{O}(n^{2})}

, and hence bounded in
(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}(\delta)^{\mathcal{O}(n^{7})}
as well.
Starting from the singular locus and iterating this procedure at most
\mu-1
times gives the total cost of

(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n^{2(\mu-1)+5})}=(2r+1)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n^{2\mu+3})},

as desired.
∎

In the setting of the Theorem, suppose
r^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n})
(which is most often the case). The
WhitStrat
algorithm applied to
X
has complexity bounded by
{\delta^{\mathcal{O}({n2^{n}})}}^{\mu}
, see
[
26
, Theorem 7.3]
. On the other hand,
WhitneyPolar
has complexity roughly
{\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n)}}^{2\mu+3}
, so the base of the
\mu
exponent is singly rather than doubly exponential in the number of variables
n
. This difference is primarily due to the fact that in
WhitneyPolar
we only need to iterate over irreducible (or equidimensional) components, both of which have number bounded in
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n})
. On the other hand,
WhitStrat
requires iterating over primary components of an ideal; these can only be bounded in
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{n2^{n}})
, see Subsection
2.1
.

Remark 6.5
.

The complexity estimates derived above form a (very slight) improvement over the
\delta^{\mathcal{O}(n)^{6\mu}}
bounds of Rannou et al from
[
42
,
45
]
. The key difference is that the complexity estimates of Rannou rely on asymptotically fast critical points based quantifier elimination methods of Grigoriev-Vorobjov
[
21
]
and Renegar
[
46
]
, whereas our bounds use only standard elimination theory over algebraically closed fields. As noted in
[
26
, §7.3]
, the asymptotically fast critical points based quantifier elimination methods of Grigoriev-Vorobjov
[
21
]
and Renegar
[
46
]
have never been implemented, despite having been extensively studied for the last 30 years, and further there is little hope they ever will be implemented. In practice, all implementations of the quantifier elimination, for example in Maple or Mathematica, rely on cylindrical algebraic decomposition which, while very powerful, is also well known as very computationally expensive both in theory and in practice.

Remark 6.6
.

All computations in Table
1
are run over a finite field of prime characteristic, 32749, rather than, e.g. ,over the rationals. The reason for this is that the practical performance of the
WhitneyPolar
algorithm is dependent on the availability of an implementation of a Gröbner basis algorithm such as F4
[
16
]
which works well for dense systems in practice. Currently, in Macaulay2, the F4 algorithm can only be reliability used over a finite field. Additionally, implementations in, e.g., Julia, where a reliable F4 Gröbner basis algorithm over
\mathbb{Q}
is already available, would be expected to match the relative treads in the table.
All algorithms above also execute correctly over
\mathbb{Q}
; however, the need of the
WhitneyPolar
algorithm to fall back on a Buchberger type Gröbner basis in Macaulay2 makes the comparison less informative.

These exhibit varying levels of complexity in terms of degree of generators, number of variables, density of polynomials, etc. The other varieties, besides the Whitney cusp and umbrella, have Whitney stratifications given by iterated singular loci; these have been included as control examples. Of these, the smooth example is obtained by intersecting 2 random quadrics in 10 variables. While the Barth Sextic is naturally written in 3 variables, we require an additional variable to represent the golden ratio
\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2}
algebraically. Thus, our version of the sextic is represented by an ideal of degree 12 in 4 variables.
