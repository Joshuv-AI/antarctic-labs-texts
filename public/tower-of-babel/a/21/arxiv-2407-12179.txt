Abstract
. Data-driven control of discrete-time and continuous-time systems is of tremendous research interest. In this paper, we explore data-driven optimal control of continuous-time linear systems using input-output data. Based on a density result, we rigorously derive error bounds for finite-order polynomial approximations of elements of the system behavior. To this end, we leverage a link between latent variables and flat outputs of controllable systems. Combined with a continuous-time counterpart of the fundamental lemma by Willems et al., we characterize the suboptimality resulting from polynomial approximations in data-driven linear-quadratic optimal control problems. Finally, we draw upon a numerical example to illustrate our results.



Keywords.
continuous time, data-driven control, differential flatness, identifiable, persistency of excitation, polynomial approximation

Introduction

Data-driven control, i.e., the design of controller and feedback laws directly from measured data, is a topic of ongoing research interest, see
[
28
,
27

and the range of articles in these special issues. At the core of many developments for linear discrete-time systems is a pivotal result, known as Willems et al.’s

[

[
9

and the references therein. The interest in this result has been catalyzed by recent works such as
[

8
,
18

[

4

In
[

15
]
and
[
23
]
continuous-time extensions of the fundamental lemma have been proposed. While the former works require to solve a scalar ODE to compute future trajectories, in the latter paper an approach to compute a generating representation based on polynomial series expansions of input-output trajectories is proposed.
However, the latter result has not yet been used to design data-driven controllers.
In the present paper, we address this gap:
We extend the results from
[
23
]
by deriving errors bounds for polynomial series expansions on elements of the behavior.
We also prove that the set of polynomial system trajectories is dense in the set of all system trajectories.
We use the approximation bounds to derive bounds on the optimality gap resulting from using finite-order polynomials to solve linear–quadratic regulator (LQR) problem formulated in terms of the behavior.
Finally, we establish a continuous-time fundamental lemma involving the Gramians of trajectories and, based on this, present a data-driven approximation for the LQR problem.

Linear differential systems

2.2.
Polynomial lift

Let

Given

(17)

\hat{f}_{i}:=\langle f,\pi_{i}\rangle\lVert\pi_{i}\rVert^{-2}

.

Any function

(18)

\hat{f}_{i}:=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{\langle f,e_{k}\pi_{i}\rangle}{\lVert\pi_{i}\rVert^{2}}e_{k},

where
\{e_{0},\dots e_{d-1}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\hat{f}=(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We define

(19)

\Pi:L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad f\mapsto\hat{f},

which is an isometric isomorphism.

The differential operator

can be represented as an operator

(20)

(\widehat{f^{(1)}})_{i}=(\mathcal{D}\hat{f})_{i}:=(2i+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=i+1\\
i+j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{j},\quad i\in\mathbb{N},

or equivalently written by means of an infinite matrix

(21)

\begin{bmatrix}(\widehat{f^{(1)}})_{0}\\
(\widehat{f^{(1)}})_{1}\\
(\widehat{f^{(1)}})_{2}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I&0&I&0&I&\dots\\
&0&3I&0&3I&0&\dots\\
&&0&5I&0&5I&\dots\\
&&&0&7I&0&\dots\\
&&&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{f}_{0}\\
\hat{f}_{1}\\
\hat{f}_{2}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly to the differential operator
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}
one can define powers and polynomials of
\mathcal{D}
.

Employing the kernel representation (

Lemma 6

(Behavioral lift)
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{I}},\mathbb{R}^{m+p})
. Then
w\in\mathcal{B}
if and only if

(22)

R(\mathcal{D})\Pi w=0.

Example 7

(No finite expansion)
.

We show for the linear time-invariant system described by (
7
) with
A=C=I
and
B=D=0
that its trajectories with nontrivial output have no series expansion involving only finitely many polynomials
\pi_{i}
. Let
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}
and assume that
y
has a finite expansion, i.e. there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\hat{y}_{i}=0
for
i\geq N
. It is no restriction to assume
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{I}},\mathbb{R}^{q})
. Note that the kernel representation (
2
) of
\mathcal{B}
is given via
R(s)=\begin{bmatrix}0&(s-1)I\end{bmatrix}
. With
Lemma

6
and the definition of
\mathcal{D}
in (
20
) we see that

(23)

\hat{y}_{i}=(\mathcal{D}\hat{y})_{i}=(2i+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=i+1\\
i+j\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\hat{y}_{j},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}.

The finiteness of the expansion yields that for
i=N-1
all summands on the right hand side in (
23
) vanish and, hence,
\hat{y}_{N-1}=0
. It is not difficult to see that this successively implies
\hat{y}_{i}=0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore
y
is trivial. ∎

In
Example

7
we illustrated the case when the dynamics of a particular linear time-invariant system (
7
) cannot be described by a finite representation in terms of Legendre polynomials. This is a generic situation: the Legendre series representation of exponential functions
e^{\lambda t}
,
\lambda\neq 0
(generically present in the solution of (

[
16
]
). Such considerations lead naturally to working with truncated Legendre expansions of solutions of (

).

2.3.
Truncated expansion

In the light of
Example

7
, we study approximation bounds when considering truncated series of Legendre polynomials. To this end we introduce the orthogonal projection
P_{N}:L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

(24)

P_{N}f:=\sum_{i<N}\hat{f}_{i}\pi_{i},

Note that
\operatorname{im}P_{N}
coincides with the
N
-dimensional space of
\mathbb{R}^{d}[s]
-polynomials of degree up to
N-1
, which is spanned by
\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{N-1}
.
Since
(\pi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthogonal basis,
P_{N}f
converges to
f
as
N\rightarrow\infty
with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm. The speed of convergence is related to the smoothness of
f
, as we discuss in the following.

Recall that
\pi_{i}
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the
i
th eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}:=i(i+1)
of the Sturm–Liouville operator

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}f&=\ell f:=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(p\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f\right),\quad p(t)=(1-t^{2}),\\
D(\mathcal{L})&:=\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\,\middle|\,\begin{aligned} f,pf^{(1)}\in\mathcal{AC}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}),\\
\ell\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C}),\\
pf^{(1)}(-1)=pf^{(1)}(1)=0\end{aligned}\right\},\end{split}

see
[
13
, Theorem 3.6]
. Here,
\mathcal{AC}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
denotes the space of locally absolutely continuous functions from
\mathcal{I}
to
\mathbb{C}
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{s}
for
s\in(0,\infty)
denote the
s
th power the self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}
, defined via functional calculus, see e.g. Section 5.3 in
[
26
]
.

Lemma 8
.

If
f\in D(\mathcal{L}^{s})
for some
s>0
, then

(25)

\left\lVert(I-P_{N})f\right\rVert=\lVert\mathcal{L}^{s}f\rVert\mathcal{O}(N^{-2s})\qquad(N\rightarrow\infty).

Moreover,
H^{k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k}{2}})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\pi}_{i}=\pi_{i}/\lVert\pi_{i}\rVert
, i.e.
(\tilde{\pi}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
form an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
. For
f\in D(\mathcal{L}^{s})
we have

\displaystyle\lVert(I-P_{N})f\rVert^{2}=\Bigg\lVert f-\sum_{i<N}\langle f,\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rangle\tilde{\pi}_{i}\Bigg\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lVert\sum_{i\geq N}\langle f,\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rangle\tilde{\pi}_{i}\Bigg\rVert^{2}=\Bigg\lVert\sum_{i\geq N}\lambda_{i}^{-s}\langle f,\mathcal{L}^{s}\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rangle\tilde{\pi}_{i}\Bigg\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lVert\sum_{i\geq N}\lambda_{i}^{-s}\langle\mathcal{L}^{s}f,\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rangle\tilde{\pi}_{i}\Bigg\rVert^{2}.

Since
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an increasing sequence, we find

\displaystyle\lVert(I-P_{N})f\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{N}^{-2s}\Bigg\lVert\sum_{i\geq N}\langle\mathcal{L}^{s}f,\tilde{\pi}_{i}\rangle\tilde{\pi}_{i}\Bigg\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big(N(N+1)\big)^{-2s}\lVert\mathcal{L}^{s}\rVert^{2},

which shows the first claim.

We show the inclusion
H^{k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k}{2}})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For
k=0
there is nothing to prove. For
k=1
we find that
D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{1}{2}})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\cap\mathcal{AC}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\,|\,\sqrt{p}f^{(1)}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\}
by
[
2
, Theorem 6.8.5 (i)]
. With the uniform boundedness of
p
on
[-1,1]
this shows
H^{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{1}{2}})
.

We continue with the case of even
k\geq 2
. Let
f\in H^{k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
. Then it is clear that
f
,
pf^{(1)}\in\mathcal{AC}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
and
\ell f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
. Moreover,
f^{(1)}
is bounded on
[-1,1]
as
f^{(1)}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
. Consequently,
f\in D(\mathcal{L})
and
\mathcal{L}f\in H^{k-2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
. Repeating this argument yields
f\in D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k}{2}})
, showing
H^{k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k}{2}})
for even
k
.

Finally, we consider
f\in H^{k+1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})
for even
k\geq 2
. From the previous observations we know that
f\in D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k}{2}})
and
\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k}{2}}f\in H^{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{1}{2}})
. Therefore,
f\in D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k+1}{2}})
, which shows
H^{k+1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{C})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\frac{k+1}{2}})
.
∎

Corollary 9
.

If
f\in H^{k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then

(26)

\lVert(I-P_{N})f\rVert=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k})\qquad(N\rightarrow\infty).

2.4.
Polynomial trajectories

Next we show that the space of polynomial trajectories
\mathcal{B}\cap\bigcup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\operatorname{im}P_{N}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}\cap H^{s}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
, and, particularly, in
\mathcal{B}
.

Proposition 10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{B}
is controllable and let
w\in\mathcal{B}\cap H^{\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})+s+k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m+p})
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. For all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
N\geq\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})+s+1
, there is
w^{N}\in\mathcal{B}\cap\operatorname{im}P_{N}
such that

\Lambda_{s}(w^{N}-w)(-1)=0

satisfying

(27)

\lVert w-w^{N}\rVert_{H^{s-1}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k})\qquad(N\rightarrow\infty).\vskip 2.84526pt

The integers
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
in
Proposition

10
determine the convergence order and the highest derivative up to which the asymptotic behavior is valid, cf. (
27
). These can be considered as user specifiable, provided
w
is sufficiently smooth.

Proof of
Proposition

10
.

Controllability of
\mathcal{B}
implies the existence of an image representation (
13
). Here we consider a particular image representation given by a flat output, see
Lemma

5
. Let
w\in\mathcal{B}\cap H^{\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})+s+k}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m+p})
. Then there is
\ell\in H^{\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})+s+k+1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m})
such that

(28)

w=M\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)\ell

holds. We construct a polynomial which approximates
\ell
and its derivatives up to order
\gamma:=\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})+s+1
, while matching the initial values. Let
v_{\gamma}^{N}:=P_{N-\gamma}\ell^{(\gamma)}
. Then by
Corollary

9
as
N\rightarrow\infty
one has

(29)

\lVert v_{\gamma}^{N}-\ell^{(\gamma)}\rVert=\lVert(I-P_{N-\gamma})\ell^{(\gamma)}\rVert=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k}).

Define

(30)

v_{i}^{N}(t):=\ell^{(i)}(-1)+\int_{-1}^{t}v_{i+1}^{N}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad i=0,\dots,\gamma-1.

By construction
(v_{0}^{N})^{(i)}=v_{i}^{N}
,
(v_{0}^{N})^{(i)}(-1)=v_{i}^{N}(-1)=\ell^{(i)}(-1)
, i.e.

(31)

\Lambda_{\gamma}(v_{0}^{N}-\ell)(-1)=0.

Moreover, one sees with the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\begin{split}\lVert\ell^{(i)}-(v_{0}^{N})^{(i)}\rVert^{2}&=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left\lvert\int_{-1}^{\tau}\ell^{(i+1)}(t)-(v_{0}^{N})^{(i+1)}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\leq 4\lVert\ell^{(i+1)}-(v_{0}^{N})^{(i+1)}\rVert^{2}\end{split}

and, thus,

(32)

\lVert\ell-v_{0}^{N}\rVert_{H^{\gamma}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k}).

By construction
v_{0}^{N}\in\operatorname{im}P_{N}
.
Recall that the polynomial matrix
M
in (
28
) satisfies
\deg(M)\leq\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})+1
. Let
w^{N}=M(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})v_{0}^{N}
, which is an element of
\mathcal{B}\cap\operatorname{im}P_{N}
. Now, (
31
) and (
32
) yield

\Lambda_{s}(w^{N}-w)(-1)=\Lambda_{s}\bigl(M(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})(v_{0}^{N}-\ell)\bigr)(-1)=0

and

\lVert w^{N}-w\rVert_{H^{s-1}}=\lVert M(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})(v_{0}^{N}-\ell)\rVert_{H^{s-1}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k}).\qed

3.
The LQR problem and its approximation

Our aim is to solve the quadratic optimal control problem

(33a)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{w\in\mathcal{B}\cap H^{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{q})}\ J(w)\quad\text{s.t.\ }

(33b)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(w)(-1)=\xi^{0},

with the cost function

(34)

J(w):=\lVert y\rVert^{2}+\lVert u^{(\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B}))}\rVert^{2},\quad w=\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}.

The initial condition (
33b
) uniquely determines the latent state, provided the latter is observable from the inputs and the outputs.
Including the higher-order derivative term of the input into the objective function (
34
) ensures feasibility of the LQR problem.

Lemma 11
.

Problem (
33
) has a unique solution
w^{\star}
, and
w^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{I}},\mathbb{R}^{q})
. Moreover, every feasible trajectory
w
satisfies

(35)

J(w-w^{\star})\leq 2\bigl(J(w)-J(w^{\star})\bigr).

Proof.

We fix a minimal input-state-output representation (
7
), that is
(A,C)
is observable. Consider any
w=\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}\cap H^{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}
. Then there is
x\in H^{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying (
7
). With

(36)

\mathcal{T}_{k}:=\begin{cases}D&\text{if }k=0,\\
\begin{bmatrix}D\\
\mathcal{O}_{k-1}B&\mathcal{T}_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}&\text{if }k\geq 1,\end{cases}

with
\mathcal{O}_{k}
being the Kalman observability matrix defined in (
38
)
one has

(37)

\Lambda_{k+1}(y)=\mathcal{O}_{k}x+\mathcal{T}_{k}\Lambda_{k+1}(u)

and by employing observability

(38)

x=\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1}\bigl(\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(y)-\mathcal{T}_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1}\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(u)\bigr).

Inserting (
38
) into (
37
) for
k=\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})
by rearranging terms one obtains a linear auxiliary system

(39)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\xi=\tilde{A}\xi+\tilde{B}\nu

with state
\xi=\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(w)
, input
\nu=u^{(\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B}))}
. Note that (
33
) is equivalent to the LQR problem

\operatorname*{minimize}_{\xi,\nu}\,\int_{\mathcal{I}}\xi(t)^{\top}\mathcal{Q}\xi(t)+\nu(t)^{\top}\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\mathcal{Q}=\operatorname{diag}(0,I_{p},0,\dots,0)
, subject to the dynamics (
39
) and the initial condition
\xi(-1)=\xi^{0}
. By standard LQR theory the latter problem has a solution
(\xi^{\star},\nu^{\star})
, which is infinitely differentiable as
\nu^{\star}
is a state feedback involving a solution of a Riccati differential equation. In particular,
w^{\star}:=\operatorname{diag}(I_{m},I_{p},0,\dots,0)\xi^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{I}},\mathbb{R}^{q})
solves (
33
).

Let
w
be any trajectory. It is not difficult to see that
J
satisfies the parallelogram identity

J\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}(w^{\star}+w)\bigr)+J\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}(w^{\star}-w)\bigr)=\tfrac{1}{2}J(w^{\star})+\tfrac{1}{2}J(w).

Convexity of the feasibility region together with
J(w^{\star})\leq J(\tfrac{1}{2}(w^{\star}+w))
yield

J\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}(w^{\star}-w)\bigr)\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(J(w)-J(w^{\star})\bigr).

This shows (
35
).
∎

Instead of solving the OCP (
33
) directly, given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we solve the problem restricted to polynomial trajectories, i.e.,

(40a)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{w\in\mathcal{B}\cap\operatorname{im}P_{N}}\ J(w)\quad\text{s.t.\ }

(40b)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(w)(-1)=\xi^{0}.

Observe that the restriction
w\in\mathcal{B}\cap\operatorname{im}P_{N}
enforces polynomial trajectories of degree at most
N-1
. In the following we show that solving (
40
) leads to an approximately optimal control and, as
N\rightarrow\infty
, the optimality gap decays at an polynomial rate of arbitrary order.

Theorem 12

(Convergence of optima)
.

For given approximation order
N
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
w^{\star}
and
w^{N}
be the solutions to the OCPs (
33
) and (
40
), respectively. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
one has

(41)

0\leq J(w^{N})-J(w^{\star})=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k})

and

(42)

\lVert w^{\star}-w^{N}\rVert=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k})

as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Recall that the solution
w^{\star}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{I}},\mathbb{R}^{m+p})
, see
Lemma

11
. Thus, by
Proposition

10
for arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is
v^{N}\in\mathcal{B}\cap\operatorname{im}P_{N}
with
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(w^{\star}-v^{N})(-1)=0
and

(43)

\lVert w^{\star}-v^{N}\rVert_{H^{\mathfrak{l}(B)}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{-k}).

As
w^{\star}
and
w^{N}
are solutions of (
33
) and (
40
), respectively, one has

J(w^{\star})\leq J(w^{N})\leq J(v^{N}),\quad N\in\mathbb{N},

which shows the left-hand-side inequality in (
41
). Further, by the reverse triangle inequality

\left\lvert J(v^{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}-J(w^{\star})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rvert\leq J(v^{N}-w^{\star})^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\lVert v^{N}-w^{\star}\rVert_{H^{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}},

which together with (
43
) implies

\displaystyle J(v^{N})-J(w^{\star})={}

\displaystyle 2J(w^{\star})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigl(J(v^{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}-J(w^{\star})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)

\displaystyle{}+\bigl(J(v^{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}-J(w^{\star})^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(N^{-k}).

Next we show (
42
). Let
\operatorname{col}(u^{\star},y^{\star})=w^{\star}
and
\operatorname{col}(u^{N},y^{N})=w^{N}
. By
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(w^{\star}-w^{N})(-1)=0
. We find

\displaystyle\lVert(u^{\star})^{(j)}-(u^{N})^{(j)}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\left\lvert\int_{-1}^{\tau}(u^{\star})^{(j+1)}(t)-(u^{N})^{(+1)}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq 4\lVert(u^{\star})^{(j+1)}-(u^{N})^{(j+1)}\rVert^{2}

for all
j=0,\dots,\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1
. Thus,

\lVert w^{\star}-w^{N}\rVert^{2}\leq 4^{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}J(w^{\star}-w^{N}).

This together with (
35
) in
Lemma

11
and (
41
) yields (
43
).
∎

4.
A “fundamental lemma”

The main result of this section is a parametrization of the trajectories of a controllable linear differential system in terms of a constant matrix obtained from “sufficiently-informative” data.
To this end, we first define some new concepts and notation and state some preliminary results.

4.1.
Persistency of excitation

Given
L\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
and
f\in H^{L-1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the Gramian

(47)

\Gamma_{L}(f):=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\Lambda_{L}(f)\Lambda_{L}(f)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Definition 15
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. A function
f:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called
persistently exciting of order
L
, if
f\in H^{L-1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the Gramian
\Gamma_{L}(f)
in (
47
) is positive definite.

This definition is reminiscent of the notion of
excitation
in
[
17
, Definition 2]
. In the following result we relate it to the concept of persistency of excitation used in
[
22
]
, specifically property (3) in the lemma below.

Lemma 16
.

For
f\in H^{L-1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
L\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

f
is persistently exciting of order
L
;

(2)

\ker(\Gamma_{k}(L))=\{0\}
;

(3)

If
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{Ld}
is such that
\eta^{\top}\Lambda_{L}(f)=0
a.e., then
\eta=0
;

(4)

The functions
f,f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(L-1)}
are linearly independent in
L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

We show only the equivalence of (2) and (3), as the equivalence of (1) and (2) as well as (3) and (4) are straightforward. Observe that
\eta\in\ker(\Gamma_{L}(f))
if and only if

0=\eta^{\top}\Gamma_{L}(f)\eta=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\lVert\Lambda_{L}(f)(t)^{\top}\eta\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\lVert\Lambda_{L}(f)^{\top}\eta\rVert^{2},

which shows the equivalence of (2) and (3).
∎

Example 17
.

Let
f:\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a monic polynomial of degree
L-1
with
L\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Then
f^{(k)}
for
k=0,\dots,L-1
is a polynomial of degree
L-1-k
. Therefore, it is clear that
f,f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(L-1)}
are linearly independent functions in
L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R})
, and, thus,
f
is persistently exciting of order
L
by
Lemma

16
.

Remark 18

(Discrete-time excitation analogue)
.

The above concept of persistency of excitation in continuous time aligns seamlessly with its discrete-time counterpart (see
[
35
]
). In discrete time, the time-shift operator serves as the analogue to differentiation, whereas summation corresponds to integration.
With this in mind, given a discrete-time signal

f:\{0,\dots,N-1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define

\Lambda_{L}(f)(t):=\begin{bmatrix}f^{\top}(t)&\dots&f^{\top}(t+L-1)\end{bmatrix}{}^{\top}

and consider the Hankel matrix

H_{L}(f):=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{L}(f)(0)&\dots&\Lambda_{L}(f)(N-L)\end{bmatrix}.

Now, the corresponding Gramian

\displaystyle\Gamma_{L}(f)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=0}^{N-L}\Lambda_{L}(u)(j)\Lambda_{L}(u)(j)^{\top}=H_{L}(u)H_{L}(u)^{\top}

is positive definite if and only if
H_{L}(u)
has full row rank, i.e.
f
is persistently exciting of order
L
, cf.
[
35
]
.

4.2.
Fundamental lemma

In the following, we also need Gramians constructed from input-state trajectories of a system (
7
).
Given
u\in H^{L-1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
x\in H^{K-1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
L,K\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, we extend the notation of stacked derivatives (
1
) to

(48)

\Lambda_{L,K}(u,x):=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{L}(u)\\
\Lambda_{K}(x)\end{bmatrix}

We define the Gramian
\Gamma_{L,K}(x,u)

by

(49)

\Gamma_{L,K}(u,x):=\int_{\mathcal{I}}\Lambda_{L,K}(u,x)\Lambda_{L,K}(u,x)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}t\;.

We now state some results instrumental to establishing
a continuous-time fundamental lemma.
To this end, we consider an input-state-output representation (
7
). Then, for fixed
L\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, we define for
i\in\mathbb{N}

(50)

\mathcal{C}_{i}(A,B):=\begin{cases}I_{n+Lm}&\text{if }i=0\\
\begin{bmatrix}A^{i}&A^{i-1}B&\dots&B&0\\
0&0&\dots&0&I_{Lm}\end{bmatrix}&\text{if }i\geq 1\end{cases}\;.

Lemma 19
.

Consider an input-state-output representation (
7
) of
\mathcal{B}
and let
\operatorname{col}(u,x)
be an input-state trajectory with
u\in H^{L+n}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m})
. For
i=0,\ldots,n-1
, the following equalities hold:

(51)

\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}(x^{(i)})\\
\Lambda_{L}(u^{(i)})\end{bmatrix}=\mathcal{C}_{i}(A,B)\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}(x)\\
\Lambda_{L+i}(u)\end{bmatrix}\;.

Proof.

The case
i=0
is trivial. To prove (
51
) in the case
i\geq 1
, use the equation

x^{(i)}=A^{i}x+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}A^{i-1-j}Bu^{(j)}.\qed

The next result is analogous to
[
22
, Proposition 1]
; since the proof needs to be adapted to the language and notation of this paper, we provide it in full detail.

Proposition 20
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is controllable and consider a minimal input-state-output representation (
7
) of
\mathcal{B}
such that
(A,B)
is controllable. Let
\operatorname{col}(x,u)
be a input-state trajectory. Assume that
u
is persistently exciting of order at least
n+L
, with
L\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. Then

(1)

If
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{Lm+n}
satisfies

(52)

\xi^{\top}\Lambda_{L,1}(u,x)=0

almost everywhere on
\mathcal{I}
, then
\xi=0
;

(2)

\Gamma_{L,1}(u,x)
is positive definite.

Proof.

The second statement follows in a straightforward way from the first one, cf. proof of
Lemma

16
. We show (1).
Let
\xi^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\eta&\zeta\end{bmatrix}
,
\eta=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{0}&\dots&\eta_{L-1}\end{bmatrix}
, with
\eta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times m}
,
j=0,\dots,L-1
, and
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
. Differentiating (
52
)
i
times,
i=0,\ldots,n
, we conclude that

\begin{bmatrix}\zeta&\eta\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}(x^{(i)})\\
\Lambda_{L}(u^{(i)})\end{bmatrix}=0\;,

almost everywhere on
\mathcal{I}
for
i=0,\ldots,n
.

Using equation (
51
) established in the proof of
Lemma

19
, we conclude that for
i=0,\ldots,n
it that

(53)

0=\begin{bmatrix}\zeta A^{i}&\ldots&\zeta B&\eta_{0}&\ldots&\eta_{L-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}(x)\\
\Lambda_{L+i}(u)\end{bmatrix}\;,

holds almost everywhere on
\mathcal{I}
. Now define

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta&\eta_{0}&\ldots&\eta_{L-1}&0_{nm}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta A&\zeta B&\eta_{0}&\ldots&\eta_{L-1}&0_{(n-1)m}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle w_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta A^{n}\ldots&\zeta B&\eta_{0}&\ldots&\eta_{L-1}\end{bmatrix}\;.

From (
53
) we have that the following equations hold true almost everywhere on
\mathcal{I}
:

(54)

w_{i}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}(x)\\
\Lambda_{L+n}(u)\end{bmatrix}=0\;,\;i=0,\ldots,n\;.

Since
u
is persistently exciting of order at least
L+n
, using statement 3 of
Lemma

16
we conclude that the vector-valued function
\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}(x)^{\top}&\Lambda_{L+n}(u)^{\top}\end{bmatrix}{}^{\top}
has at most
n
“almost everywhere annihilators” on
\mathcal{I}
: it follows that the
n+1
vectors
w_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,n
are linearly dependent.

Since the last components of the
w_{i}
’s are zero,
i=0,\ldots,n
, we conclude that
\eta_{L-1}=0_{1\times m}
, then
\eta_{L-2}=0
, and so on until
\eta_{0}=0
. Consequently

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta&0_{1\times(n+L)m}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta A&\zeta B&0_{1\times(n+L-1)m}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta A^{2}&\zeta AB&\zeta B&0_{(n+L-2)m}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle w_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\zeta A^{n}&\zeta A^{n-1}B&\ldots&\zeta B&0_{1\times Lm}\end{bmatrix}\;.

Denote by
\alpha_{i}
,
i=0\ldots,n
the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial of
A
, and using
\sum_{i=0}^{n}A^{i}\alpha_{i}=0
conclude that
\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}\alpha_{i}
equals

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\zeta A^{i}\alpha_{i}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\zeta A^{i-1}B&\ldots&\zeta B&0_{1\times Lm}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0_{1\times n}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta\alpha_{i}A^{i-1}B&\ldots&\zeta B&0_{1\times Lm}\end{bmatrix}\;.

By construction, almost everywhere on
\mathcal{I}
it holds that

\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\zeta A^{i-1}B&\ldots&\alpha_{n}\zeta B\end{bmatrix}\Lambda_{n}(u)=0\;;

since
u
is persistently exciting of order at least
L+n
, we conclude that

\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\zeta A^{i-1}B&\sum_{i=2}^{n}\alpha_{i}\zeta A^{i-2}B&\ldots&\alpha_{n}\zeta B\end{bmatrix}=0\;.

It follows from the last
m
equations that
\alpha_{n}\zeta B=0
; since the highest coefficient
\alpha_{n}
of the characteristic polynomial of
A
equals 1, we conclude that
\zeta B=0
. The previous
m
-dimensional block-entry of the vector is
\alpha_{n-1}\zeta B+\alpha_{n}\zeta AB=0+\alpha_{n}\zeta AB=0
. We conclude that
\zeta AB=0
. The same argument can be used to prove
\zeta A^{i}B=0
,
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. Since the pair
(A,B)
is controllable we conclude that
\zeta=0
and consequently that Statement (1) is true.
∎

We now have all the necessary ingredients to formulate a continuous-time ”fundamental lemma“.

Theorem 21

(Continuous-time ”fundamental lemma“)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{B}
is controllable. Let
\operatorname{col}(\overline{u},\overline{y})\in\mathcal{B}
be such that
\overline{u}
is persistently exciting of order
L+\mathfrak{n}(B)
, with
L\geq\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})+1
.
For
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in H^{L-1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{q})
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})+1\leq K\leq L
, the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}
;

(2)

There exists
g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{Lm+Kp})
such that

(55)

\Lambda_{L,K}(u,y)=\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})g.

Moreover,
\operatorname{rank}\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})=Lm+\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B})
.

Proof.

Fix a minimal input-state-representation (
7
) of
\mathcal{B}
and let

(56)

\mathcal{S}_{L,K}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{mL}&0\\
\mathcal{T}_{K-1}&\mathcal{O}_{K-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is the Kalman observability matrix, see (
11
), and
\mathcal{T}_{K}
is defined as in (
36
). Then given
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}
with corresponding state
x
satisfies

(57)

\Lambda_{L,K}(u,y)=\mathcal{S}_{L,K}\Lambda_{L,1}(u,x).

Let
\overline{x}
be the state corresponding to
\operatorname{col}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
. In a first step we show

(58)

\operatorname{im}\mathcal{S}_{L,K}=\operatorname{im}\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y}).

Note that in order to show (
58
) it suffices to prove

(59)

\ker\mathcal{S}_{L,K}^{\top}=\ker\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y}).

The former equality then follows by taking the orthogonal complements of the null spaces and employing the symmetry of
\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
. With (
57
) and (
49
) one has

(60)

\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})=\mathcal{S}_{L,K}\Gamma_{L,1}(\overline{u},\overline{x})\mathcal{S}_{L,K}^{\top}.

By
Proposition

20
the matrix
\Gamma_{L,1}(\overline{u},\overline{x})
is positive definite. This shows (
59
), cf. Observation 7.1.8 in
[
10
]
. In particular,

\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{S}_{L,K}=Lm+\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{O}_{K-1}

\displaystyle=Lm+\mathfrak{n}(\mathcal{B}).

We show the implication (1) to (2). Let
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}\cap H^{L}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m+p})
with state
x
. Then (
57
) holds. Therefore, with (
58
) the function
\Lambda_{L,K}(u,y)
maps pointwise a.e. into
\operatorname{im}\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
and, thus,
g:=\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})^{\dagger}\Lambda_{L,K}(u,y)
satisfies (
55
).

We show the converse implication. Assume that (
55
) holds. We consider a input-output representation (
6
) of
\mathcal{B}
with polynomial matrices
Q
and
P
. It is no restriction to assume that the degree of
Q
and
P
is bounded by
\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})
, i.e.
Q(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}Q_{k}s^{k}
and
P(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}P_{k}s^{k}
. Define

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{0}&\dots&Q_{\mathfrak{l}(B)}&0_{p\times m(L-\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1)}\end{bmatrix}\;,

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}P_{0}&\dots&P_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}&0_{p\times p(K-\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1)}\end{bmatrix}\;.

Since
\operatorname{col}(\overline{u},\overline{y})\in\mathcal{B}
, it holds that

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}&\widetilde{P}\end{bmatrix}\Lambda_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{x})=0

and, consequently,

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}&\widetilde{P}\end{bmatrix}\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{x})=0\;.

Therefore,

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}&\widetilde{P}\end{bmatrix}\Lambda_{L,K}(u,y)=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{Q}&\widetilde{P}\end{bmatrix}\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{x})g=0,

that is (
6
) holds and
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{B}
.
∎

Remark 22
.

Instead of using the data matrix
\Gamma_{L,K}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
, any other matrix with the same image is suitable in the description of trajectories (
55
). One advantageous approach, especially from a numerical perspective, is to utilize the
reduced singular value decomposition
of
\Gamma_{L}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
, i.e.

\Gamma_{L}(\overline{u},\overline{y})=U_{1}\Sigma_{1}V_{1}^{\top},

with
\Sigma_{1}
nonsingular of dimension equal to the rank of
\Gamma_{L}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
. Observe that the columns of
U_{1}
form an orthogonal basis for
\operatorname{im}\Gamma_{L}(\overline{u},\overline{y})
.

In the absence of a feedthrough term (i.e.,
D=0
in the input-state-output representation), the constraint
K\leq L
in Theorem
21
can be relaxed to
K\leq L+1
. If, in addition, the state is directly observable (i.e.
C=I_{n}
), we get the following statement.

Corollary 23
.

Suppose

(61)

\mathcal{B}=\Big\{\operatorname{col}(u,x)\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m+n})\,\Big|\,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}}x=Ax+Bu\Big\}

is controllable. Let
\operatorname{col}(\overline{u},\overline{x})\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\overline{u}
is persistently exciting of order
2+n
. Consider the partition

\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{u}\\
\Gamma_{x}\\
\Gamma_{x^{(1)}}\end{bmatrix}=\Gamma_{1,2}(\overline{u},\overline{x})

with
\Gamma_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m+2n)}
,
\Gamma_{x},\Gamma_{x^{(1)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m+2n)}
. Then, for
u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m})
and
x\in H^{1}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{n})
the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{col}(u,x)\in\mathcal{B}
;

(2)

There exists
g\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I},\mathbb{R}^{m+2n})
such that

(62)

u=\Gamma_{u}g,\quad x=\Gamma_{x}g,\quad\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\Gamma_{x}g\right)=\Gamma_{x^{(1)}}g.

Moreover,
\operatorname{rank}\Gamma_{1,2}(\overline{u},\overline{x})=m+n
.

Corollary
23
allows for an complete description of
\mathcal{B}
based only on sufficiently informative data, without knowledge of the system matrices
A
and
B
. Note that, however, the verification of condition (
55
) involves solving a system of linear equations. The solution of a system of linear differential equations (with time-varying coefficients) arises also in the version of the fundamental lemma in
[
14
]
, see Theorem 2 therein.

4.3.
System identification

The data matrix, as applied in the fundamental lemma, enables the reconstruction of behavioral representations. Suppose that the assumptions of
Corollary

23
hold. The representation (
62
) is equivalent to

(63)

\widetilde{R}(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})\operatorname{col}(u,x)=\widetilde{M}(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t})g,

where
\widetilde{R}
and
\widetilde{M}
are polynomial matrices given by

(64)

\widetilde{R}(s)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
I_{m}&0\\
0&I_{n}\end{bmatrix},\ \widetilde{M}(s)=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{x^{(1)}}-s\Gamma_{x}\\
\Gamma_{u}\\
\Gamma_{x}\end{bmatrix}.

Observe, that
g
serves as a latent variable in the representation (
63
).

We are going to eliminate the latent variable
g
, cf. Theorem 6.2.6. in
[
21
]
Let

(65)

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{B}&\widetilde{A}\end{bmatrix}=\Gamma_{x^{(1)}}\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{u}\\
\Gamma_{x}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger},\quad\widetilde{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},\ \widetilde{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\;.

Note that

\operatorname{rank}\Gamma_{1,2}(\overline{u},\overline{x})=\operatorname{rank}\Gamma_{1,1}(\overline{u},\overline{x})=m+n,

cf.
Proposition

20
and
Corollary

23
, and
\Gamma_{1,1}(\overline{u},\overline{x})
is a submatrix of
\Gamma_{1,2}(\overline{u},\overline{x})
. Therefore, the rows of
\Gamma_{x^{(1)}}
are linearly dependent on those of
\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{u}^{\top}&\Gamma_{x}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}{}^{\top}
.
As a consequence, multiplication with the unimodular matrix
U
,

(66)

U(s)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&-\widetilde{B}&(sI_{n}-\widetilde{A})\\
0&I_{m}&0\\
0&0&I_{n}\end{bmatrix},

yields

(67)

U(s)\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{R}(s)&\widetilde{M}(s)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\widetilde{B}&(sI_{n}-\widetilde{A})&0\\
I_{m}&0&\Gamma_{u}\\
0&I_{n}&\Gamma_{x}\end{bmatrix}.

Finally, using the first
n
rows in
U(s)\widetilde{R}(s)
, a kernel representation (
2
) of
\mathcal{B}
is obtained,

(68)

R(s)=\begin{bmatrix}-\widetilde{B}&(sI_{n}-\widetilde{A})\end{bmatrix}

It is not difficult to see that
\widetilde{A}
and
\widetilde{B}
(together with
C=I_{n}
,
D=0
) are suitable matrices for the input-state-output model of (
7
).

Expansion-based formulation

Employing the polynomial lift, see

2.2
, we obtain the following two corollaries of
Theorem

21
and

, respectively.

Corollary 24
.

Let the assumption of

hold. Consider the partition

\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{u}\\
\Gamma_{u^{(1)}}\\

\Gamma_{u^{(L-1)}}\\

.

Let the assumption of

hold. Let
For

5.
Data-driven optimal control

Finally, utilizing the approximation result of
Section

3
in conjunction with the fundamental lemma, we propose a data-driven approach for optimal control of input-output systems (see also
[
6
]
for a recent application of orthogonal bases of functions in iteratively solving finite-length continuous-time tracking problems).

5.1.
Data-driven formulatation

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

21
and
Corollary

24
(with
K=L=\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})+1
) hold. We consider the optimization problem

(71a)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{minimize}_{\hat{g}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{R}^{Lq})}\ \sum_{i<N}\bigl(\lVert\Gamma_{y^{(0)}}\hat{g}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\Gamma_{u^{(\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B}))}}\hat{g}_{i}\rVert^{2}_{2}\bigr)\lVert\pi_{i}\rVert^{2}

subject to

(71b)

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{i}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad i\geq N,

(71c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Gamma_{u^{(k-1)}}\hat{g})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{u^{(k)}}\hat{g},

(71d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Gamma_{u^{(k-1)}}\hat{g})

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{u^{(k)}}\hat{g},\qquad k=1,\dots,\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B}),

(71e)

\displaystyle\xi^{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<N}(-1)^{i}\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{u^{(0)}}\\
\Gamma_{y^{(0)}}\\
\vdots\\
\Gamma_{u^{(\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1)}}\\
\Gamma_{y^{(\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})-1)}}\end{bmatrix}\hat{g}_{i}.

Note, that the relationship between optimization problems (
71
) and (
40
) is established by

(72)

\begin{split}\Pi u=\hat{u}=\Gamma_{u^{(0)}}\hat{g},&\qquad\Pi y=\hat{y}=\Gamma_{y^{(0)}}\hat{g},\\
u^{(k)}=\sum_{i<N}(\Gamma_{u^{(k)}}\hat{g}_{i})\pi_{i},&\qquad y^{(k)}=\sum_{i<N}(\Gamma_{y^{(k)}}\hat{g}_{i})\pi_{i}.\end{split}

Constraints (
71c
) and (
71d
) ensure that
w=\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\mathcal{\mathcal{B}}
, while constraint (
71b
) guarantees
w\in\operatorname{im}P_{N}
. The initial condition
\Lambda_{\mathfrak{l}(\mathcal{B})}(w)(-1)=\xi^{0}
is reflected by (
71e
), where
\pi_{i}(-1)=(-1)^{i}
is used.

The following proposition summarizes the relationship between the polynomially restricted LQR problem (
40
) and its data-driven formulation (
71
).

Proposition 26
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

21
and
Corollary

24
hold. Then the polynomnially restricted LQR problem (
40
) and the data-driven LQR problem (
71
) are equivalent in the sense that
w=\operatorname{col}(u,y)
solves (
40
) if and only if
\hat{g}
is a solution to (
71
) such that (
72
) holds. In particular, their optimal values coincide.

Proof.

Via the relationship (
72
) the target function in (
40
) can be equivalently rewritten into that in (
71a
). Further,
Corollary

24
directly yields the equivalence of LQR problem (
40
) and a modified data-driven formulation of (
71
), where in the latter problem the constraint (
71b
) is replaced by the seemingly more restrictive constraint

(73)

\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{u^{0}}\\
\Gamma_{y^{0}}\end{bmatrix}\hat{g}_{i}=0,\qquad i\geq N.

Note that the modified condition (
73
) in combination with (
72
) is equivalent to
\operatorname{col}(u,y)\in\operatorname{im}P_{N}
. Replacing (
71b
) with (
73
), however, does not affect the feasibility or optimality of a trajectory
\operatorname{col}(u,y)
with (
72
). Indeed, this follows form the fact that (
73
) together with (
71c
), (
71d
