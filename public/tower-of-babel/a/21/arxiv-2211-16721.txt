Where Am I Now? Dynamically Finding Optimal Sensor States to Minimize Localization Uncertainty for a Perception-Denied Rover
Troi Williams, Po-Lun Chen, Sparsh Bhogavilli, Vaibhav Sanjay, Pratap Tokekar

Abstract
We present DyFOS, an active perception method that dynamically finds optimal states to minimize localization uncertainty while avoiding obstacles and occlusions. We consider the scenario where a perception-denied rover relies on position and uncertainty measurements from a viewer robot to localize itself along an obstacle-filled path. The position uncertainty from the viewer’s sensor is a function of the states of the sensor itself, the rover, and the surrounding environment. To find an optimal sensor state that minimizes the rover’s localization uncertainty, DyFOS uses a localization uncertainty prediction pipeline in an optimization search. Given numerous samples of the states mentioned above, the pipeline predicts the rover’s localization uncertainty with the help of a trained, complex state-dependent sensor measurement model (a probabilistic neural network). Our pipeline also predicts occlusion and obstacle collision to remove undesirable viewer states and reduce unnecessary computations. We evaluate the proposed method numerically and in simulation. Our results show that DyFOS is faster than brute force yet performs on par. DyFOS also yielded lower localization uncertainties than faster random and heuristic-based searches.

III-A

Preliminaries

Consider a world with a planar surface, a rover, a sensor mounted on a viewer (another robot), and a collection of static obstacles (Figure
3
). The rover is an autonomous ground vehicle (AGV) that wants to visit a sequence of waypoints along a path. It also has an environment model
\boldsymbol{M}
that contains information about the environment (for example, an occupancy grid and the location and direction of light sources). At time
i
, we use
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}(i)\in\text{SE}(2)}
to denote a posterior estimate of the rover’s state and the covariance
{\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}(i)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}}
to denote the posterior state uncertainty.

Due to damage or failure of the rover’s exteroceptive sensors, the rover cannot localize or avoid obstacles; it can only compute basic odometry (for example, via wheel encoders). Therefore, it relies on a viewer to help estimate its state belief. The viewer is an autonomous (aerial or ground) vehicle that can localize itself accurately. It has a copy of
\boldsymbol{M}
, but does not have access to the rover’s waypoints. We use
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{v}(i)\in\text{SE(3)}}
to denote the estimated 6D pose of the viewer. The viewer uses a downward, front-facing camera to measure the rover’s belief. The camera’s estimated pose is denoted as
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s}(i)\in\text{SE(3)}}
. Without loss of generality, we assume the camera is fixed to the viewer, and
{{}^{v}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\in\text{SE(3)}}
describes the sensor frame relative to the viewer frame.

We define the sensor measurement (observation) model as

\boldsymbol{z}(i)=h\left(\mathcal{I}(i),\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s}(i),\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)\right).

(1)

Here,
h\left(\cdot\right)
is a vision-based algorithm that detects the rover in a camera image
\mathcal{I}(i)
and computes the rover’s state
\boldsymbol{z}(i)\in\text{SE(2)}
in the map frame (using the sensor’s pose
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s}(i)
). Due to sensor and environmental-related phenomena,
\boldsymbol{z}(i)
contains measurement noise
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
6
,
31
]
. We assume
each
state-dependent noise
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(i)\sim\mathcal{N}({0,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)})
is a zero-mean Gaussian random variable with covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)
. Furthermore, we assume
each
measurement noise covariance is state-dependent (that is, a function of the states of the viewer’s sensor, rover, and environment):

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)=g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s}(i),\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}(i),\boldsymbol{M}),

(2)

where
g(\cdot)
is a function that outputs the state-dependent measurement noise covariance at time
i
.

At time
i
, the rover moves to the next waypoint, where it plans to rendezvous with the viewer to localize. We refer to such a waypoint as a
rendezvous
(or predicted) state
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}(i+1)
.
Between times
i
and
i+1
, the viewer computes an optimal sensor state and moves to such a state to observe the rover.

III-B

Problem Statement

Our goal is two-fold. First, we find an optimal sensor state
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{s}(i+1)
(in the
future
) that minimizes the rover’s predicted posterior localization uncertainty
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}(i+1)
as the rover navigates along a path. Second, we seek sensor and viewer states that avoid collisions and occlusions due to obstacles. To accomplish these goals, we propose an algorithm called DyFOS, which dynamically finds an optimal data acquisition state that will 1) avoid obstacles, 2) avoid occlusions, and 3) minimize the rover’s predicted posterior localization uncertainty (after a measurement update)
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}(i+1)
. We search for an optimal state
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{s}(i+1)
using the following objective:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{s}(i+1)&=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}(i+1)~\in~\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}(i+1)}J\big(\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}(i+1)\big)\\
\text{s.t.}&~c_{l}\!\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{s}(i+1),\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}(i+1),\boldsymbol{M}\right)=0,\forall l\in[1,L].\end{split}

(3)

Here,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}(i+1)
is the rover’s rendezvous state.
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}(i+1)
is a candidate sensor state that is sampled from
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}
, the set of all sensor states.
{\mathcal{C}=\{c_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}}
are a set of constraints that predict obstacle collision or occlusion. Finally,
\boldsymbol{M}
is our environment model. Inspired by
[
32
,
33
]
, we choose
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}(i+1)}
as a waypoint along the rover’s path. We compute
{\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}(i+1)}
by propagating the rover’s current localization uncertainty
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}(i)
from time
i
to
i+1
using the Kalman Filter equations. Finally, we assume these states can be predicted or estimated (offline and online), and, given these states, we can use an SDSMM to predict or estimate
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
dynamically.

IV
Dynamically Finding Optimal States to Minimize Localization Uncertainty

The DyFOS algorithm consists of 1) an optimization method that encapsulates 2) a localization uncertainty prediction pipeline (LUPP) (Figure
1
). The pipeline contains four main steps. First, we sample a candidate sensor state
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
from the sensor state space
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}
(Section
IV-A
). Next, the pipeline uses a set of constraints (Section
IV-B
) to determine if the current (rover, environment, and candidate sensor) state tuple is a feasible solution (for example, testing for obstacle collisions and occlusions). If the tuple is not feasible, the pipeline short-circuits and samples another candidate sensor state. However, if the state tuple is feasible, we use it to construct a feature vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and feed the vector to an SDSMM, which predicts the expected measurement noise covariance
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
(Section
IV-C
). Finally, the pipeline predicts the posterior localization uncertainty
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}
and then uses
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}
to compute an evaluation metric (Section
IV-D
).

The optimizer executes the pipeline many times to find
one
optimal sensor state
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{s}
that produces the lowest metric value. Discussions on selecting an optimizer and finding its appropriate parameters are beyond the scope of this paper. However, Section
V
mentions the optimizer we employed.

IV-A

The Sensor State Space

The pipeline begins with sampling a candidate sensor state
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
from the state space
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}
. We could define
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}
as the set of all 3D (camera) sensor poses within a map. However, this definition leads to unnecessary evaluations because the rover (at the rendezvous pose
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}
) may be out of view of, too far from, or too close to the sensor for many candidate poses. Therefore, to reduce the number of (unnecessary) evaluations, we design a state space where we
predict
that the rover will be within the sensor’s detection range and field of view. Assuming the sensor’s velocities and accelerations are near zero, we define our state space as:

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}=&~\{~\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\in\text{SE}(3)~|~\rho_{\text{min}}\leq\rho\leq\rho_{\text{max}},\\
&|\psi|\leq 0.5\cdot f_{\text{horz}},~|\phi|\leq 0.5\cdot f_{\text{vert}}~\}.\end{split}

(4)

The scalars
\rho_{\text{min}}
and
\rho_{\text{max}}
denote the camera’s detection range, while the scalars
f_{\text{horz}}
and
f_{\text{vert}}
represent the camera’s horizontal and vertical fields of view. Let
{}^{s}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}=[{}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{r},{}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{r}]^{\top}
denote the rover’s 3D pose (position
{}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}
and orientation
{}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{r}
) relative to the sensor. The scalar
\rho=||{}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}||_{2}^{2}
is the distance between the rover and the sensor. Finally, the scalars
{\psi=\arctan({}^{s}\hat{y}_{r},{}^{s}\hat{x}_{r})}
and
{\phi=\arctan({}^{s}\hat{z}_{r},{}^{s}\hat{x}_{r})}
(where
{{}^{s}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}=[{}^{s}\hat{x}_{r},{}^{s}\hat{y}_{r},{}^{s}\hat{z}_{r}]^{\top}}
) are the horizontal and vertical angles of the rover relative to the sensor, respectively.

Given the limits of
\rho
,
\phi
, and
\psi
in (
4
), we can independently sample values for each variable to create our state space
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}
, the set of 3D camera poses where we predict the rover is detectable and within the camera’s field of view.

IV-B

Sensor and Viewer State Constraints

During the online search, we use constraints to
predict
if a candidate state is feasible beyond the line-of-sight of the sensor (Figure
3
). Let
{\mathcal{C}=\{c_{{}_{l}}\}_{l=1}^{L}}
contain a set of constraints that determines if a sampled state
{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{s}}
is a feasible solution. Collectively, the set of constraints
\mathcal{C}
defines a feasible region that varies with time and space, and this feasible region contains the sensor state we seek for optimal data acquisition. In addition, the constraints prevent the pipeline from running the SDSMM and computing the posterior localization uncertainty if
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
is not feasible, reducing unnecessary computation.

We use two constraints and a 3D occupancy grid (such as an Octomap
[
34
]
) to determine if
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
is feasible. The first constraint predicts collisions between the viewer (robot) and obstacles by emitting fixed-length rays horizontally and vertically from
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{v}
. Here, we assume
{}^{v}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
, the transform from the sensor to the vehicle frame, is known. The second constraint predicts if obstacles will occlude the rover. To predict occlusions, we emit rays from
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
to points along the perimeter of the rover at
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}
, where the perimeter is a generic bounding shape. For either constraint,
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
is
not
feasible if
any
ray intersects with a voxel within the occupancy grid.

Overview

An SDSMM is a model that outputs a state-dependent measurement distribution
[
6
,
31
]
(Figure
4
). We can use such distributions to derive statistics such as the measurement bias and noise. Examples of an SDSMM include linear models, multi-layer perceptions, or Gaussian Processes. The input to an SDSMM is a set of features
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
that correlate with the measurement bias and noise
1
1

1

Williams and Sun
[
6
,
31
]
refer to such features as a combined state. However, we avoid using the term to alleviate confusion with robot states.
, and, in our case, the output is the predicted or estimated covariance of the measurement noise
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
of the rover’s position. For our experiments, we implement an SDSMM as a fixed linear model. However, we describe how to learn an SDSMM in the context of neural networks below (such as in
[
6
,
31
]
).

IV-C
2
Training an SDSMM

We train an SDSMM using a dataset
{\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{e}(k),\boldsymbol{\lambda}(k))\}}_{k=1}^{K}
that contains
K
measurement errors and their corresponding features. The measurement error is defined as
{\boldsymbol{e}(k)=(\boldsymbol{z}(k)-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}(k))}
, where
\boldsymbol{z}(k)
is a measurement of the rover’s pose using a pose estimation algorithm and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{r}(k)
is the ground truth pose of the rover. Generally speaking, the feature vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}(k)
for a given sensor and estimation problem varies. For instance, a feature vector for a camera used in a rover localization problem may contain the ambient lighting of the area and the distance and relative velocity between the camera and the rover. We note that these features must be calculable using the ground truth, estimated, or predicted of the states of the sensor, rover, and surrounding environment. For example, we use the ground truth and estimated states to compute the features during training. However, during runtime, we use the estimated or predicted states to compute the features. Section
V
describe the SDSMM and features used in our experiments.

Given the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we use the negative log-likelihood loss
\mathcal{L}
to train our model. Since our measurement noise is normally-distributed, we simplify the loss to

\mathcal{L}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\ln{(|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(k)|)}+\boldsymbol{e}(k)^{\top}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(k)]^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}(k)\right),

(5)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(k)
is the output covariance from the SDSMM.

IV-C
3
Dual Usage

In this paper, the SDSMM has two purposes. First, while evaluating candidate sensor poses (via the LUPP), the SDSMM
predicts
the measurement noise covariance using predicted features derived from a candidate sensor pose, rover’s rendezvous pose, and environment (OctoMap). This capability allows us to predict the measurement noise beyond the sensor’s line of sight. In the second case (when the sensor is at the optimal pose), the SDSMM
estimates
the measurement noise covariance using features estimated from the current sensor, rover, and environment states. Then we transmit the estimated noise covariance to the rover (as shown in Figure
2
).

IV-D

Posterior Localization Uncertainty Prediction

The final step in our pipeline predicts a posterior localization uncertainty (
after
a future measurement update)
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}
and its associated metric value
\mathcal{E}_{\textbf{P}}
. Following Gürcüoğlu
et al.

[
30
]
, we use the following function

\mathcal{E}_{\textbf{P}}=J\big(\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}\big)=\ln\left(\det\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}\right)\right).

(6)

Since we use a Kalman Filter to track the rover’s position and uncertainty, we predict
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}
using the Kalman Filter Riccati equations (like in
[
30
,
9
]
):

\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}=\left\{\textbf{I}-\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}\textbf{H}^{\top}\textbf{S}_{t}^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}\right)\textbf{H}\right\}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r},

(7)

where
\textbf{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is an identity matrix,
{\textbf{S}_{t}=\textbf{H}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}\textbf{H}^{\top}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{r}}
is the innovation covariance,
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}
is the rover’s
a priori
localization uncertainty (propagated from time
i
to
i+1
), and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{r}
is the predicted measurement uncertainty (using the SDSMM and a set of
predicted
features). Finally, we highlight that
\bar{\boldsymbol{P}}_{r}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\textbf{P}}
also vary with each candidate sensor state because
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
is a function of the sensor state.

Once the pipeline computes
\mathcal{E}_{\textbf{P}}
for a candidate state
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
, the optimizer checks if it should continue searching for an optimal state or terminate. If the pipeline terminates, it either performed the maximum number of iterations or did not improve over some period. In either case, we set the optimal sensor state
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{s}
as the state with the lowest cost. Then the viewer moves the sensor to
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{s}
.

V
Evaluations

We performed numerical and simulated evaluations to compare our proposed method. In each evaluation, we compared DyFOS with the random and center view baseline algorithms, where each algorithm computed a pose for the viewer’s sensor. The random algorithm arbitrarily placed the viewer and is akin to using a traditional, state-
independent
measurement noise model with a constant variance. Center view placed the viewer such that the rover is in the center of the image. This algorithm implicitly assumes measurement noise varies and is lowest when the rover is in the center of an image (this heuristic is similar to
[
11
]
). Finally, DyFOS used the LUPP (Section
IV
) with a differential evaluation global optimizer
[
35
]
in SciPy
[
36
]
. In our numerical evaluation, we also used a brute force algorithm with the LUPP to find the “best possible” sensor pose. All algorithms employed the constraints in Section
IV-B
. We also used belief propagation to compute the rendezvous belief.

In DyFOS and brute force, the LUPP used an SDSMM to predict and estimate the measurement noise covariance. We implemented our SDSMM using a linear model for both evaluations. The 2D position noise model was defined as
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y})}
, where
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
have the same value:
{\sigma_{\ast}=\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}+(c\cdot\sigma_{3})^{2}}
. Here,
\sigma_{1}^{2}=0.03^{2}
was the minimum measurement noise. The term
\sigma_{2}^{2}\in[0.03^{2},0.3^{2}]
represented the noise due to the location of the rover in the camera image, where
0.03^{2}
was used if the rover was in the center and
0.3^{2}
was used if the rover was closer to the edge. Finally,
(c\cdot\sigma_{3})^{2}
was the measurement noise due to the size of the rover in the image (which correlates with distance) and a simulated reflection scalar
c
due to the sun, where
\sigma_{3}\in[0.03,0.5]
. We used the law of reflection to compute
c\in[0,2]
, where
0
means no specular reflection and
2
means the sun’s reflection partially obscured the rover.

When executing the pipeline, the SDSMM predicted the measurement noise covariance given predictions of the reflected sunlight
c
and the marker’s predicted size and location within a camera image. We predicted these features (the intensity and the marker’s size and location in an image) using the predicted states (that is, the rover’s rendezvous belief, the viewer’s candidate pose, and the position of the sun). We estimated the measurement noise covariance using the estimated corresponding states whenever the viewer observed the rover (in the simulated evaluation).

We discuss the simulated results below.

Rover Pose Error.
Our results showed that DyFOS also had the lowest absolute pose error (APE) (see Table
I
). We believe DyFOS achieved the lowest APE because it actively searches for viewpoints that will reduce the rover’s localization uncertainty with the help of the SDSMM. This results in behaviors such as looking away from the sun to remove reflections. Random view was the second-best performer, mainly due to its random behavior. For example, sometimes random view may choose a good viewpoint with low noise, and other times it may choose poorly. Finally, the center view algorithm performed the worst. The poor performance may be due to the camera’s angle and not considering other sources of measurement noise (for example, sunlight reflections). At the camera’s current angle, the viewer must be at least
0.8
m away from the rover so that it appears in the middle of the image, which results in higher amounts of noise. Furthermore, since center view does not consider other sources of noise, this algorithm (like random view) may select viewpoints with high amounts of noise, even though it is satisfying its heuristic.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed DyFOS, a novel active perception method that
Dy
namically
F
inds
O
ptimal
S
tates to minimize localization uncertainty while avoiding obstacles and occlusions. DyFOS relies on user-defined constraints, a learned state-dependent sensor measurement model, and an objective function to evaluate the quality of each candidate state with respect to the localization task. DyFOS exploits an SDSMM to minimize the rover’s predicted posterior localization uncertainty in a hazard-filled world. Our experiments showed that DyFOS performs on par with brute force methods but is significantly faster. The average runtime was under two seconds using one CPU core, which may be reasonable for some real-time applications if we optimized the algorithm. DyFOS also achieves lower predicted localization uncertainties than random and heuristic search algorithms.
