Instrumental Variable Estimation of Distributional Causal Effects
Lucas Kook and Niklas Pfister

Abstract
Estimating the causal effect of a treatment on the entire response distribution is an important yet challenging task. For instance, one might be interested in how a pension plan affects not only the average savings among all individuals but also how it affects the entire savings distribution. While sufficiently large randomized studies can be used to estimate such distributional causal effects, they are often either not feasible in practice or involve non-compliance. A well-established class of methods for estimating average causal effects from either observational studies with unmeasured confounding or randomized studies with non-compliance are instrumental variable (IV) methods. In this work, we develop an IV-based approach for identifying and estimating distributional causal effects. We introduce a distributional IV model with corresponding assumptions, which leads to a novel identification result for the interventional cumulative distribution function (CDF) under a binary treatment. We then use this identification to construct a nonparametric estimator, called DIVE, for estimating the interventional CDFs under both treatments. We empirically assess the performance of DIVE in a simulation experiment and illustrate the usefulness of distributional causal effects on two real-data applications.

Introduction

Unlike average causal effects, which only capture how a given treatment affects
the response on average, distributional causal effects capture the effect on the
entire response distribution, including the mean, variance and higher moments of

Observational data, despite the presence of hidden confounding, may, under
appropriate assumptions, be used to draw causal conclusions

Pearl, 2009
;
Wooldridge, 2015

. Often, the estimand is
the average causal effect, i.e., the difference in the average response between
treated and untreated experimental units. One way to estimate average causal
effects from observational data is via instrumental variables (IVs), which
induce heterogeneity in the treatment assignment that is independent of both
the hidden confounders and only affects the response via the treatment

;
Angrist et al., 1996
;

.
IV approaches for estimating average causal effects have been proposed for

Most existing IV-based methods make use of conditional moment restrictions;
however, recent work

;

Saengkyongam et al., 2022

,
has proposed to use a stronger

Using the identification strategy (Theorem

Definition 3

(

6

The CvM and HSIC terms entering the distributional IV loss enforce uniformity of

Definition 4

6
Discussion and outlook

We propose a method for identifying and estimating distributional causal effects
using IV based on two properties of the iPIT residuals: Under the assumptions
stated in Theorem
1
, only the interventional CDFs given the random
observed treatment
D
produces iPIT residuals that are both uniformly
distributed and independent of the instrument. As shown both in simulations and
in real-data applications, the resulting estimator, DIVE, can be estimated
consistently and provides valuable information beyond the average causal effect.
Further, DIVE circumvents the issue of quantile crossing
(
Bassett and Koenker, 1982
)
by
employing basis expansions instead of estimation over a grid of quantiles.

IVQR is the most similar method that applies in our setting (binary treatment
and absolutely continuous response). In contrast to our proposed IV-SCM, it is
based on the potential outcome model and uses a different identification
strategy. However, as shown in Proposition
2
the two models
are related: For any IVQR model in which the structural quantile function is
identified, there exists a corresponding IV-SCM with the same observational and
interventional distributions (w.r.t. interventions on the treatment). Thus,
under appropriate relevance conditions, whenever IVQR is applicable, DIVE is
too. While available implementations of IVQR are limited to linear quantile
regression, they provide inference on the estimated quantile treatment effect
(for a fixed quantile) based on asymptotic normality established in

Chernozhukov et al. (2007)
.

In this work, we did not consider adjusting for additional observed covariates.
A possible extension of DIVE would allow for this by estimating the conditional
interventional CDFs under treatment and control of the response given some
features, for which normalizing flows
(
Papamakarios et al., 2021
)
or other
nonparametric distributional regression methods could be used.
When considering conditional IV, the choice between a distributional or
quantile-based estimand becomes important. This is because, in general, an
average quantile function and the corresponding inverse average CDF need not
agree. Both theoretical and computational advantages and disadvantages have been
discussed in
Leorato and Peracchi (2015)
. Furthermore, it is an open problem whether
our identification strategy can be extended to continuous-valued treatments and
whether the control function approach proposed in

Imbens and Newey (2009)
or the recently proposed copula-based approach

(
Chernozhukov et al., 2024
)
can be adapted to our setting. We further
discuss potential extensions of DIVE for censored and discrete responses in
Appendix
A
. Whether asymptotic confidence intervals,
analogously to IVQR, for the distributional causal effect at pre-specified
values of the response can be obtained is also left for future work.

equivalent to: for all

\displaystyle p(0,0)l_{1}(w)+\mathds{1}(\gamma(0,0)=w)+p(1,0)l_{2}(w)+\mathds{1}(\gamma(1,0)=w)

(98)

\displaystyle\qquad=p(0,1)l_{1}(w)+\mathds{1}(\gamma(0,0)=w)+p(1,1)l_{2}(w)+\mathds{1}(\gamma(1,0)=w).

(99)

Simplifying this expression we get that
W\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp Z
is equivalent to for all

w\in\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle(p(0,0)-p(0,1))l_{1}(w)+(p(1,0)-p(1,1))l_{2}(w)=0.

Now, the functions
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
both take values in
\{-1,0,1\}
, hence since
by assumption
p(0,0)-p(0,1)\neq p(1,0)-p(1,1)
and for all
z,h\in\{0,1\}
,

p(z,h)\neq 1
it holds that

\{(p(0,0)-p(0,1))l_{1}(w)\mid w\in\mathcal{W}\}\cap\{-(p(1,0)-p(1,1))l_{2}(w)\mid w\in\mathcal{W}\}\subseteq\{0\}.

Hence, the only way for (
98
) to be satisfied is if for all

w\in\mathcal{W}
it holds that
l_{1}(w)=l_{2}(w)=0
. This is however equivalent to

\gamma(0,1)=\gamma(0,0)
and
\gamma(1,0)=\gamma(1,1)
, which is equivalent to

k(0)=k(1)=0
. Therefore, we have shown that

Z\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp W\iff k\equiv 0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Definition 5

(Population CvM criterion)
.

The population Cramér–von Mises criterion w.r.t. the standard uniform

\operatorname{CvM}(X)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\mathbb{P}(X\leq u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Definition 6

(Empirical CvM criterion)
.

The empirical Cramér–von Mises criterion w.r.t. the standard uniform

(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
is
defined as

\widehat{\operatorname{CvM}}((X_{i})_{i=1}^{n})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\leq u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{12n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{i-0.5}{n}-X_{(i)}\right)^{2},

where, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
X_{(i)}
denotes the
i
th order statistic
and the equality is shown in Lemma
5
.

Definition 7

(Population HSIC)
.

Let
(Y,X)
be random variables taking values in
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
and
k:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

(Y^{\prime},X^{\prime})

(Y^{\prime\prime},X^{\prime\prime})

(Y,X)

\displaystyle\operatorname{HSIC}(Y,X;k,l)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})l(X,X^{\prime})]+\mathbb{E}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})]\mathbb{E}[l(X,X^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}[k(Y,Y^{\prime})l(X,X^{\prime\prime})].

(100)

Definition 8

(Empirical HSIC)
.

Let
(Y_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be independent and identically distributed random
variables taking values in
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
and
k:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\operatorname{HSIC}}((Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n},(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n};k,l)

(101)

\displaystyle\quad\coloneqq\ \frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}^{n}k(Y_{i},Y_{j})l(X_{i},X_{j})+\frac{1}{n^{4}}\sum_{i,j,q,r}^{n}k(Y_{i},Y_{j})l(X_{q},X_{r})-\frac{2}{n^{3}}\sum_{i,j,q}^{n}k(Y_{i},Y_{j})l(X_{i},X_{q}).

(102)

Lemma 1
.

Let
Y
be an absolutely continuous random variable and
F:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
be a CDF. Then, it holds that

F(Y)\sim\UD(0,1)\iff Y\sim F.

Proof.

First, assume
Y\sim F
. For all
t\in(0,1)
define the generalized inverse CDF
by
F^{-1}(t)\coloneqq\inf\{y\in\mathbb{R}\mid F(y)\geq t\}
. Then, it holds for
all
t\in(0,1)
that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:cdf_{p}arta}\mathbb{P}(F(Y)\leq t)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq F^{-1}(t))=F(F^{-1}(t)).

(103)

Next, since
Y
is absolutely continuous, we know that
F
is continuous. For
all
t\in(0,1)
, by the intermediate value theorem there must exist

y^{*}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
F(y^{*}_{t})=t
. Hence we get by the definition of
the generalized inverse that
F^{-1}(t)\leq y^{*}_{t}
, which further implies that

F(F^{-1}(t))\leq F(y^{*}_{t})=t
. Since it also holds that
F(F^{-1}(t))\geq t
, we
get that
F(F^{-1}(t))=t
. Together with (
103
) this implies that

F(Y)\sim\UD(0,1)
.

Second, assume that
F(Y)\sim\UD(0,1)
. Then, it holds for all
y\in\mathbb{R}

that

\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y)=\mathbb{P}(F(Y)\leq F(y))=F(y)

and hence
Y\sim F
.
∎

Definition 9

(Conditional independence,
Dawid
,

1979
)
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be random vectors. We say
X
is conditionally independent of
Y

given
Z
and write
Y\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp X\mid Z
if and only if, for all bounded
measurable
\phi
,

\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)\mid X,Z]=\mathbb{E}[\phi(Y)\mid Z].

Lemma 2

(Properties of conditional independence)
.

Let
(A,B,C)\sim P
. Then, the following statements hold

[
Dawid, 1980
]
:

Decomposition:

A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(B,C)\implies(A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp B)\land(A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp C).

Weak union:

A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(B,C)\implies(A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp B\mid C)\land(A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp C\mid B).

Contraction:

(A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp B\mid C)\land(A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp C)\implies A\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(B,C).

Lemma 3
.

Under Setting
1
, it holds that
(N_{Y},H)\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(D,Z)\mid H
.

Proof.

By Setting
1
, we have

(N_{Z},N_{D})\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp N_{Y}\mid N_{H}.

By
Dawid [1979, Lemma 4.1]
, it holds

(N_{Z},N_{D})\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp N_{Y}\mid N_{H}\iff(N_{Z},N_{D},N_{H})\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(N_{Y},N_{H})\mid N_{H}.

Now, since
f
from Setting
1
is measurable,
H\coloneqq N_{H}
,
Z\coloneqq N_{Z}
and
D\coloneqq f(N_{Z},H,N_{D})
, we have

\displaystyle(N_{Z},N_{D},N_{H})\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(N_{Y},N_{H})\mid N_{H}

\displaystyle\implies(N_{Z},f(N_{Z},N_{D},N_{H}))\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(N_{Y},N_{H})\mid N_{H}

(104)

\displaystyle\implies(Z,D)\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp(N_{Y},H)\mid H,

(105)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4

(Balance equation,
Rosenbaum and Rubin
,

1983
)
.

In Setting
1
, it holds that

D\perp\nolinebreak\hskip-6.0pt\perp H\mid\pi(H),

where
\pi(H)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[D\mid H]
.

Proof.

By

\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid H,\pi(H))=\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid H)=\pi(H),

and

\mathbb{P}(D=1\mid\pi(H))=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}(D=1)\mid H,\pi(H)]\mid\pi(H)]=\mathbb{E}[\pi(H)\mid\pi(H)]=\pi(H),

we arrive at the desired conclusion.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent and identically standard uniformly
distributed random variables and let
X_{(1)},\dots,X_{(n)}
denote their
order statistic. Then, it holds that

\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\leq u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{12n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{i-0.5}{n}-X_{(i)}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

The result follows from the following computation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\leq u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{X_{(k)}}^{X_{(k+1)}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\leq u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\int_{X_{(k)}}^{X_{(k+1)}}\left(\tfrac{k}{n}-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[-\tfrac{1}{3}(\tfrac{k}{n}-X_{(k+1)})^{3}+\tfrac{1}{3}(\tfrac{k}{n}-X_{(k)})^{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{3}\left[(\tfrac{k}{n}-X_{(k)})^{3}-(\tfrac{k-1}{n}-X_{(k)})^{3}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{3}\left[(\tfrac{k}{n})^{3}-(\tfrac{k-1}{n})^{3}\right]+\left[\tfrac{1}{n}X_{(k)}^{2}-\tfrac{2k-1}{n}X_{(k)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\tfrac{1}{12n^{3}}+\tfrac{1}{n}\left(\tfrac{2k-1}{2n}-X_{(k)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{12n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\tfrac{k-0.5}{n}-X_{(k)}\right)^{2}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
5
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent and identically copies of
X
taking values
in
\mathbb{R}
. Then, it holds that

\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\leq u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\;\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\;\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{P}(U\leq u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\quad\text{as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

Proof.

For all
u\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\widehat{F}_{n}(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\leq u)
and
F(u)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(X\leq u)
.
We then have,

\displaystyle\quad\,\,\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

(106)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u)+F(u)-u\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

(107)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u))^{2}+2(\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u))(F(u)-u)+(F(u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

(108)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u+2\int_{0}^{1}(\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u))(F(u)-u)\,\mathrm{d}u

(109)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\,\,+\ \int_{0}^{1}(F(u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

(110)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{u\in(0,1)}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u)\right\rvert\right)^{2}+\sup_{u\in(0,1)}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u)\right\rvert\left(\int_{0}^{1}(F(u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{1/2}

(111)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\,\,+\ \int_{0}^{1}(F(u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u.

(112)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{u\in(0,1)}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u)\right\rvert+\int_{0}^{1}(F(u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u,

(113)

where the last inequality uses that for all
u\in(0,1)
,
F(u)\in[0,1]
.
By the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem, it holds that,

\sup_{u\in(0,1)}\left\lvert\widehat{F}_{n}(u)-F(u)\right\rvert\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}0,

which concludes the proof of Lemma
6
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
X
be an absolutely continuous real-valued random variable. Then, it holds
that

\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{P}(X\leq u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=0\iff X\sim\UD(0,1).

Proof.

The backward direction is immediate by
\mathbb{P}(X\leq u)=u
. For the
forward direction,

\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbb{P}(X\leq u)-u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u=0\implies\mathbb{P}(X\leq u)-u=0\mbox{ a.e.}\implies X\sim\UD(0,1),

where the first implication follows because the
L_{2}
norm of a function is zero
if and only if the function zero almost everywhere and the second implication
follows by right-continuity of
\mathbb{P}(X\leq\boldsymbol{\cdot})
, absolute continuity of
X

and the uniqueness of the CDF.
∎
