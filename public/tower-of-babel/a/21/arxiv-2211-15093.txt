Basic Notations

In the sequel of this article, we will use the lower case letters (e.g.,
x
) to represent scalars, lower case bold letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}
) to denote column vectors, bold-face upper case letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{X}
) to denote matrices. Given a vector
\mathbf{x}
, its length is denoted as
\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|
. Given a matrix
\mathbf{X}
, we denote
\mathbf{X}(i,:)
and
\mathbf{X}(:,j)
as its
i_{th}
row and
j_{th}
column, respectively. The (
i_{th}
,
j_{th}
) entry of matrix
\mathbf{X}
can be denoted as either
\mathbf{X}(i,j)
or
\mathbf{X}_{i,j}
, which will be used interchangeably. We use
\mathbf{X}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\top}
to represent the transpose of matrix
\mathbf{X}
and vector
\mathbf{x}
. The cross product of vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
is represented as
\mathbf{x}\times\mathbf{y}
. A coordinate system is denoted as
\Sigma
, and the vector
\mathbf{x}
in coordinate system
\Sigma
can also be specified as
\mathbf{x}^{[\Sigma]}
. For a scalar
x
, vector
\mathbf{x}
and matrix
\mathbf{X}
, we can also represent their first-order derivatives as
\dot{x}
,
\dot{\mathbf{x}}
and
\dot{\mathbf{X}}
, and second-order derivatives as
\ddot{x}
,
\ddot{\mathbf{x}}
and
\ddot{\mathbf{X}}
.

Robot Motion

2.1
Homogeneous Transformation Matrix

In the previous tutorial article, most of the materials we have introduced are about the robot
rotation matrix
and its transformation impact on changing the
position
,
orientation
,
linear velocity
and
angular velocity
of the robot arm end point. We have also briefly introduced the
homogeneous transformation matrix
for readers but we didn’t discuss much about its property or usage in robot motion actually, which will be covered in this section.

2.1.1
Special Orthogonal Group and Special Euclidean Group

The set of
rotation matrix
we introduce in the previous article can also be clearly represented as the
special orthogonal group
defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

(
Special Orthogonal Group
): The special orthogonal group
SO(3)
, also known as the group of rotation matrices in
\mathbbm{R}^{3}
, denotes the set of all
3\times 3
matrices
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3}
that satisfy (1)
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}
and (2)
\det\mathbf{R}=1
.

In the above definition, the first constraint
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}
denotes the
rotation matrix

\mathbf{R}
is
orthogonal
and each column vector is a unit vector. Meanwhile, the second constraint
\det\mathbf{R}=1
indicates that the rotation is a right-handed frame.

Meanwhile, based on the rotation matrix, we can also represent the set of
homogeneous transformation matrix
as the
special Euclidean group
as follows.

Definition 2
.

(
Special Euclidean Group
): The special Euclidean group
SE(3)
, also known as the homogeneous transformation matrix in
\mathbbm{R}^{3}
, denotes the set of all
4\times 4
matrices
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbbm{R}^{4\times 4}
in the following form

\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{1,1}&\mathbf{R}_{1,2}&\mathbf{R}_{1,3}&\mathbf{p}_{1}\\
\mathbf{R}_{2,1}&\mathbf{R}_{2,2}&\mathbf{R}_{2,3}&\mathbf{p}_{2}\\
\mathbf{R}_{3,1}&\mathbf{R}_{3,2}&\mathbf{R}_{3,3}&\mathbf{p}_{3}\\
0&0&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where the matrix
\mathbf{R}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{1,1}&\mathbf{R}_{1,2}&\mathbf{R}_{1,3}\\
\mathbf{R}_{2,1}&\mathbf{R}_{2,2}&\mathbf{R}_{2,3}\\
\mathbf{R}_{3,1}&\mathbf{R}_{3,2}&\mathbf{R}_{3,3}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in SO(3)
is a rotation matrix and
\mathbf{p}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{1}\\
\mathbf{p}_{3}\\
\mathbf{p}_{3}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}
is a column vector denoting the robot arm position.

In the above definitions, the numerical number
3
in the notations
SO(3)
and
SE(3)
indicates that these matrices are defined in the
\mathbbm{R}^{3}
space, and it doesn’t denote the dimensions of the matrices. Like for a homogeneous transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}\in SE(3)
, it actually has a dimension of
4\times 4
.

2.1.2
Chain Rule on Homogeneous Transformation Matrix

The
homogeneous transformation matrix
defined across coordinate systems follows the chain rule.

Figure 1:
An Example of Rigid-Body Robot Structure (involving 1 base link and 3 movable links connected via joints
a
,
b
and
c
). The arm rotation angles are denoted as
\theta
,
\phi
and
\psi
, respectively.

Example 3
.

Here, we will take a 4-link robot arm shown in Figure
1
as an example to illustrate the forward kinematics process. Specifically, the robot arm involves 1 base link from the origin
o
of the world coordinate
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
to joint
a
, and 3 movable links connected via joints
a
,
b
and
c
. Attached to each joint, we define a local coordinate with origin attached to them, which are denoted as
\Sigma_{a}
,
\Sigma_{b}
and
\Sigma_{c}
, respectively. Within the local coordinate systems, we can represent the robot arm links as vectors
\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
,
\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}
, and
\mathbf{p}_{c\to e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}
, respectively.

According to the previous tutorial article
RobotBasics_2022
, based on the robot arm rotation angles, we can define the homogeneous transformation matrix between the coordinate systems as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp^{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

(2)

where the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\exp^{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}}
is obtained from the previous tutorial article
RobotBasics_2022
. Notation
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
projects the angular velocity vector
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
to the matrix representation. As introduced before, the angular velocity vector can also be represented as
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\mathbf{e}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
denotes the axis unit vector of the joint
a
and scalar
\dot{q}_{a}
denotes the angular velocity with the unit rad/s.

Similar to the homogeneous transition matrix
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
introduced above, we can also define the homogeneous transition matrices between other local coordinate systems, including

\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\text{, and }\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}&\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.\\

(3)

Via the chain rule, we can obtain the homogeneous transition matrix
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, which can be denoted as follows:

\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}.

(4)

2.1.3
Robot Arm Link Projection across Coordinate Systems

Via the
homogeneous transformation matrix
, we can project a robot arm from a local coordinate to another local/world coordinate system.

Example 4
.

Still based on the example illustrated in Figure
1
, with the end point’s current position vector within the local coordinate
\Sigma_{c}
, we can calculate its end point’s position within the world coordinate as

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{c\to e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

(5)

In the above example, vector
\mathbf{p}_{c\to e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}
denotes the third movable robot arm link pointing from the joint
c
to the end point
e
in the local coordinate system
\Sigma_{c}
. Via the
homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, we can project it to the world coordinate system and the result vector
\mathbf{p}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
pointing from the origin
o
of the world coordinate system
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
to the end point
e
in
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
.

2.2
Robot Twist Velocity

At the last section of the previous tutorial article
RobotBasics_2022
, we have introduced the transformation of the robot arm end point’s
linear velocity
and
angular velocity
during the robot arm rotation. The previous analytic representations of the
linear velocity
and
angular velocity
are derived separately from each other. In this part, we will combine the robot
linear velocity
and
angular velocity
together, which is also named as the
twist
of robots.

2.2.1
Twist Velocity in World Coordinate System

Definition 5
.

(
Twist
): Formally, given the angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and linear velocity
\mathbf{v}
of a point in robot body, we can combine
\boldsymbol{\omega}
and
\mathbf{v}
into one vector, which defines the twist velocity vector of the robot body:

\boldsymbol{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}.

(6)

In the previous article, we introduce the operators
\land:\boldsymbol{\omega}\to\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
(or
\land:\boldsymbol{\omega}\to\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}
) and
\lor:\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\to\boldsymbol{\omega}
(or
\lor:\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\to\boldsymbol{\omega}
) to create the relationships between the
angular velocity vector

\omega
and the
rotation matrix

\mathbf{R}
. Here, we can introduce similar operators to the
twist vector

\boldsymbol{\nu}
and the
homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}
.

Definition 6
.

(
Operator Definition
): In this paper, we introduce ab operator
\land
to project the twist velocity vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}
to its matrix representation as follows:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}&\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

The above matrix representation of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is closely correlated with the
homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}
actually, and their relationship will be analyzed as follows.

Example 7
.

To clearly explain the physical meanings of the terms to be derived below, we can take the homogeneous transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}
as an example.
Let’s first calculate the product of matrices
\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}
, which can be represented as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}

(8)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}&-(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(9)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}({\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}&\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}-\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}({\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Considering that
\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left({\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}=\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left({\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{\top}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\times\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, the above representation of matrix
\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}
can be represented with

\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}-{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\times\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

The physical meaning of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left({\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}
is very straight-forward and we have introduced the operator in the previous article already. As to the term
\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}-{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\times\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, it actually denotes the linear velocity of joint
a
within the world coordinate system
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
with the velocity caused by the rotation being excluded, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\times\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
. Such a linear velocity can be caused by the movement of the robot arm base, which introduce extra velocity for all points in the robot arm.

For simplicity, we can denote
\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}-{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\times\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, so we can simlify rewrite the homogeneous transformation matrix product as follows:

\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(12)

Based on the above analysis, we observe that with in the world coordinate system
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
, we can apply the
\land
operator to the
twist velocity

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, the result matrix is identical as the corresponding
homogeneous matrix
product
\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}
actually:

({\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\land}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}=\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}.

(13)

2.2.2
Twist in Local Coordinate System

In the above analysis, we multiply
\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}
on the right side of matrix
\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
. Some readers may also wonder if we multiple it to the left side will affect the result or not? In this part, we will answer this question.

Similar to the above analysis, we can first calculate the left multiplication of
\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}
to
\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

(14)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}&-(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(15)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(16)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(17)

In the above representation, the physical meaning of term
(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
should be trivial for the readers to figure out. As to
(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, as illustrated as follows, it actually denotes
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
.

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}

\displaystyle=\left((\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{a}^{\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}}\right)^{\land}

(18)

\displaystyle=\left((\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{a}^{\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}}\right)^{\land}

(19)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})

(20)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\left(\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\right)\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

(21)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left((\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{R}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}.

(23)

Therefore, we observe that the left multiplication of the homogeneous transformation matrix terms will be denoted as

\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}&\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\land}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(24)

In other words, the left matrix multiplication
\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
will be equivalent to the result we can get by applying the
\land
operator to the
twist velocity

{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
at joint
a
within the local coordinate system
\Sigma_{a}
.

2.3
Twist Velocity Transformation across Coordinate Systems

In the previous subsection, we introduce the
twist velocity
vector for readers, and also illustrate the
twist velocity
vector representations within different coordinate systems subject to the new
\land
operator. In this part, we will further discuss about the relationships between the
twist velocity vectors

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, as well as between
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, across coordinate systems.

2.3.1
Relationship Between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
And
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

Let’s first talk about the relationship between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
in different coordinate systems. Based on the detailed derivations in the previous two subsections, we know that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}.

(25)

Meanwhile, since

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1},

(26)

by multiplying
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
to both sides of the above equation, we can get

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}.

(27)

By replacing the representation of
\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
in the above equation into Equation
25
, we can illustrate the relationship between
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

(28)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}.

(29)

From it, we can also obtain that

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}.

(30)

In other word, via the
homogeneous transformation matrix
, we can project the
twist velocity
vectors subject to the
\land
operator to each other.

2.3.2
Relationship Between
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
And
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

Based on the above analysis, we can further derive the relationship between the
twist velocity
vectors
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
. By replace the terms
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
,
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
with their concrete matrix representation into Equation
30
, we can get

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(31)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}&\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}&-(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(32)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}&\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}&-(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(33)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}&-\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(34)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}&-\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\top}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(35)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{\land}&-\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{\land}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(36)

Based on some basic properties about cross-product and the
\land
operator on the rotation matrix, the term
-\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{\land}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{\land}\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=-\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)\times\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

(38)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\times\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{\land}\left(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(41)

Therefore, from the above Equation
31
, we can get that

\begin{cases}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&=\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&=\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.\end{cases}

(42)

We can also represent the above Equation
42
with linear algebra representation, which will illustrate the relationship between
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{0}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{adjoint representation }Ad({{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}})}\begin{bmatrix}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{v}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\end{bmatrix}

(43)

Definition 8
.

(
Adjoint Representation
): Given the homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(44)

We can represent its adjoint representation as follows:

Ad({{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{0}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(45)

The
adjoint representation
of the homogeneous transformation matrix will project the
twist velocity
vectors across different coordinate systems. For instance, from the above analysis, we can get that

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=Ad({{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(46)

With a similar process, we can also obtain that

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=Ad({{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}\to\Sigma_{a}]}})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}.

(47)

2.4
Screw

We have been discussing about the robot
body motion
and
twist velocity
vector above. Some questions may naturally arise in readers’ mind: “why do we need to study both ‘robot rotation’ and ‘robot motion’?”

To answer the question, we need to distinguish the differences between “robot rotation” and “robot motion”. As indicated by the name, “robot rotation” as introduced in
RobotBasics_2022
denotes the rotational movement of the robot body (or part of the body). Meanwhile, “robot motion” involves both the
rotational
movement, as well as the
translational
movement, so it will be much more complicated to model than the the
robot rotation
.

2.4.1
A Toy Example

Some readers probably may propose another question “Can we project the ‘robot motion’ as a type of ‘robot rotation’ movement, or combine them and model them as one movement?” In stead of directly answering the question, we can use a toy example below to illustrate our answer.

Figure 2:
A Example of Robot Motion. (The motion of coordinate
\Sigma_{a}
to
\Sigma_{b}
via both rotation and translation can be modeled as a rotation around point
q
with an angle of
\theta
degrees.)

.

As shown in Figure

, in the 3D space, we provide three coordinate systems: (1) the world coordinate system
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
with origin

\Sigma_{a}

after motion with origin at point

2.4.2
Screw Axis

To formally model the rigid robot motion as a rotational movement, in this part, we will introduce the concept of
screw axis
and will also introduce two operators to project
screw axis
with the
transformation matrix
.

If the readers still remember, when introducing the
angular velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, we mention that it can be represented as
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is a unit vector indicating the direction of the
angular velocity
vector and scalar
\dot{q}
denotes the rate of ration around the axis with the unit rad/s. It helps interpret the physical meaning of the
angular velocity
vector. Meanwhile, when it comes to the
twist velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, the readers probably may also wonder if it can also be interpreted in a similar way or not. In this part, we aim to answer this question and represent the
twist velocity
vector with the
screw
motion.

Example 10
.

Figure 3
:
An Example of Screw Rotational Movement. (Unit vector
\mathbf{e}
: the direction of the screw axis; Scalar
\dot{q}
: rotation rate of screw; Scalar
h
: pitch of screw and it equals to “linear velocity scalar along screw axis/angular velocity scalar”; Vector
\mathbf{p}
: it denotes a point on the screw axis.)

The twist velocity vector contains both angular velocity and linear velocity, which may remind us about the screw rotational movement. In Figure
3
, we show an example of a screw rotating around the axis with direction indicated by a unit vector
\mathbf{e}_{s}
. The screw rotating rate can be denoted by a scalar
\dot{q}
with unit rad/s, and the screw pitch is represented by another scalar
h
in this example, which denotes the forward movement distance as the screw rotates an angle of
360^{\circ}
degrees. One of the point on the axis is denoted by position vector
\mathbf{p}
. The collection of
\mathbf{s}=(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{e}_{s},h)
together defines the screw axis representation, and
\dot{q}
denotes the angular velocity scalar around the screw axis.

Screw Representation
: Based on the above screw representation, given a
twist velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, by choosing proper elements of the
screw axis
, we can also represent it with the
screw axis
together with the corresponding rotational
angular velocity
scalar
\dot{q}
as follows:

\boldsymbol{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{s}\\
-\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{s}\times\mathbf{p}+h\mathbf{e}_{s}\dot{q}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(52)

The physical meaning of
\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{s}
denotes the
angular velocity
of the
screw
in rotation, As to the
linear velocity
representation
-\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{s}\times\mathbf{p}+h\mathbf{e}_{s}\dot{q}
, it has two parts: (1) term
h\mathbf{e}_{s}\dot{q}
denotes the translational linear velocity that the screw moves forward as it rotates, and (2)
-\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{s}\times\mathbf{p}
denotes the linear velocity at the origin due to the rotational movement about the axis.

2.4.3
Screw Axis Representation Simplification

The above
screw axis
vector
\mathbf{s}
and
rotational velocity
scalar
\dot{q}
representations carry concrete physical meanings. Meanwhile, given a random
twist velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, calculating the configurations of the
screw axis
vector
\mathbf{s}
and
rotational velocity
scalar
\dot{q}
that can make the equation hold is not an easy task. In this part, we propose to simplify the above calculation process.

Simplified Version 1
: In application, for any
twist velocity
vector
\mathbf{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}
input,

•

if
\boldsymbol{\omega}\neq\mathbf{0}
, there also exist an equivalent representation of the
screw axis
vector
\mathbf{s}
and
rotational velocity
scalar
\dot{q}
that can make Equation
52
hold, where
\mathbf{e}_{s}=\boldsymbol{\omega}/\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|
,
\dot{q}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|
, and
h=\mathbf{e}_{s}^{\top}\mathbf{v}/\dot{q}
. Meanwhile, the position vector
\mathbf{p}
is chosen so that the term
-\dot{q}\mathbf{e}_{s}\times\mathbf{p}
provides the portion of
\mathbf{v}
orthogonal to the screw axis;

•

if
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{0}
, then the pitch
h
will be
+\infty
and
\mathbf{e}_{s}=\mathbf{v}/\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|
and
\dot{q}
is interpreted as the linear velocity
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|
along
\mathbf{e}_{s}
.

The readers may have also observe some potential problems with the above simplified representation of the
twist velocity
vector as the
screw axis
and
angular velocity
representation,
e.g.
, the pitch
h
can be
+\infty
, vector
\mathbf{p}
is not unique, and the calculation process is very cumbersome. Therefore, we will introduce to define the
screw axis
vector
\mathbf{s}
as a normalized version of any
twist velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
corresponding to the motion along the screw.

Simplified Version 2
: For any
twist velocity
vector
\mathbf{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}
input,

•

if
\boldsymbol{\omega}\neq\mathbf{0}
, we define the
screw axis

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{\nu}/\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|}\\
\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|}\\
\end{bmatrix}
, and the angular velocity scalar about the axis as
\dot{q}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|
.

•

if
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{0}
, we define the
screw axis

\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{\nu}/\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|}\\
\end{bmatrix}
, and the angular velocity scalar about the axis as
\dot{q}=\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|
.

Based on the above simplified version, we can provide the formal definition of
screw axis
as follows:

Definition 11
.

(
Screw Axis
): For a given coordinate system, a screw axis can be formally defined as

\mathbf{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6},

(53)

where either (1)
\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|=1
or (2)
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{0}
and
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=1
.

According to the above definition, the
screw axis
vector is actually a normalized version of the
twist velocity
vector, and we have the equation
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\dot{q}\mathbf{s}
hold. Also many of the properties, operators and transformation that can be applied to the
twist velocity
vector will also hold and applied to the
screw axis
vector,
e.g.
, the
\land
operator.

Given a
screw axis
representation
\mathbf{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}
, we can represent its matrix representation subject to the
\land
operator as follows:

\widehat{\mathbf{s}}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}&\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\text{ where }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega_{3}&\omega_{2}\\
\omega_{3}&0&-\omega_{1}\\
-\omega_{2}&\omega_{1}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(54)

With the
adjoint representation
of the
homogeneous transformation matrix
, we can also convert the
screw axis
vectors across coordinate systems,
e.g.
,

\widehat{\mathbf{s}}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})\widehat{\mathbf{s}}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\text{, and }\widehat{\mathbf{s}}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}\to\Sigma_{a}]})\widehat{\mathbf{s}}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]},

(55)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{s}}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
denote two
screw axis
vectors in the coordinate systems
\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}
and
\Sigma_{a}
, respectively. Terms
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
and
\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}\to\Sigma_{a}]}
represent the
homogeneous transformation matrices
between these two coordinate systems.

2.5
Motion Exponential and Logarithm

In the previous tutorial article
RobotBasics_2022
, we have introduced that the
rotation matrix

\mathbf{R}
can be represented as the exponential of its corresponding
angular velocity vector

\boldsymbol{\omega}
,
i.e.
,

\mathbf{R}(t)=\exp^{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}t}.

(56)

Since we have modeled the rigid-body motion of robots as the rotational movement in this section, the readers probably may also wonder if the
homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}
will also have similar exponential representations of the
screw axis
representation
\mathbf{s}
or not? This is what we plan to introduce in this part.

2.5.1
Motion Exponential Representation

We have spent lots of space introducing the rigid-body
robot motion
,
twist velocity
and the
screw axis
before already. Here, instead of showing the detailed derivation steps, we will directly define the
exponential operator
that will project the
screw axis
to the
homogeneous transformation matrix
.

Definition 12
.

(
Exponential Operator
): Given the screw axis
\mathbf{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}
and the rotation angle
q
, we define a matrix exponential operator to project the screw rotation to the corresponding homogeneous transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbbm{R}^{4\times 4}
as follows:

\exp:\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q\to\mathbf{T}.

(57)

Specifically, for the screw axis
\mathbf{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\mathbf{v}\\
\end{bmatrix}
, depending on its element values, we can also provide the closed-form representation of term
\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q}
illustrated as follows:

•

if
\left\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\right\|=1
, we can also provide the closed-form representation of the exponential term
\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q}
as follows:

\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp^{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}q}&\left(\mathbf{I}q+(1-\cos q)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}+(q-\sin q)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{2}\right)\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(58)

•

if the angular velocity vector
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{0}
and
\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|=1
, the above matrix representation can also be simplified as

\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{i}&\mathbf{v}q\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(59)

Based on the exponential operator, given a
screw axis
vector, we can calculate the corresponding
homogeneous transformation matrix
that can describe the identical robot motion across coordinate systems.

2.5.2
Matrix Logarithm Representation

Let’s also look at the reversed direction. Given a
homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}
, can we also find a
screw axis

\mathbf{s}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}
and a rotation angle scalar
q\in\mathbbm{R}
such that
\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q}=\mathbf{T}
? Besides the
exponential operator
, we will also introduce the a
logarithm operator
here to achieve such an objective.

Definition 13
.

(
Logarithm Operator
): Given a homogeneous transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{bmatrix}
, via the logarithm operator, we can project
\mathbf{T}
to a screw axis
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}
and a rotation angle scalar
q\in\mathbbm{R}
shown as follows:

\ln:\mathbf{T}\to\widehat{\mathbf{s}}q.

(60)

Specifically, depending on the values in matrix
\mathbf{T}
, we can also provide the closed-form representation of the corresponding screw axis
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}
and rotation angle scalar
q\in\mathbbm{R}
:

•

if
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}
, then we can set
\boldsymbol{\omega}=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{p}/\left\|\mathbf{p}\right\|
, and
q=\left\|\mathbf{p}\right\|
.

•

otherwise, we can use the logarithm operator defined before for rotation matrix to calculate the angular velocity vector and the angle scalar
q
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=\left(\ln\mathbf{R}\right)^{\lor}

(61)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=\text{atan2}\left(\left\|(\mathbf{R}_{32}-\mathbf{R}_{23},\mathbf{R}_{13}-\mathbf{R}_{31},\mathbf{R}_{21}-\mathbf{R}_{12})^{\top}\right\|,\mathbf{R}_{11}+\mathbf{R}_{22}+\mathbf{R}_{33}-1\right).

(62)

As to the linear velocity vector
\mathbf{v}
, we can represent it as

\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{p}\text{, where matrix }\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{I}q+(1-\cos q)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}+(q-\sin q)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{2}.

(63)

2.6
Wrench

At the end of this section, we also plan to provide a brief introduction about the
wrench
of robot motion, which illustrate the relationship between forces and torques. More detailed information about force and torque will be discussed in the last section about
robot dynamics
for readers.

2.6.1
Wrench Definition

From basic knowledge we learn from physics, given a force denoted by vector
\mathbf{f}_{e}
acting on the end point
e
of a robot body, whose position can be denoted as vector
\mathbf{p}_{e}
, we can represent the generated
torque
as

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}=\mathbf{p}_{e}\times\mathbf{f}_{e}.

(64)

Similar to
twist velocity
vector, the force and generated torque vectors can also be organized into a vector of
6
elements, which is formally defined as the
wrench vector
as follows:

\mathbf{w}_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}\\
\mathbf{f}_{e}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}.

(65)

For the multiple
wrenches
are acting on the same body, the total
wrench
is defined as the sum of these individual
wrench vectors
. Readers probably have also noticed that the above
wrench vector
is very similar to the
twist velocity
vector, and the
torque
vector can also be expressed as the cross-product of the
force
and the
position
vectors. Meanwhile, they also have minor differences,
e.g.
, (1)
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{f}
(vector
\mathbf{p}
is multiplied at the left side) but
\mathbf{v}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{p}
(vector
\mathbf{p}
at the right side); and (2) in the wrench vector
\mathbf{w}
, term
\boldsymbol{\tau}
obtained via
\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{f}
is at the top, but in the twist velocity vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, the linear velocity
\mathbf{v}
obtained by cross product term
\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{p}
is at the bottom instead. Due to these differences, we cannot simply apply the operators introduced before to the
wrench vector
here.

2.6.2
Wrench Transformation

Actually, via the power quantity that drives the motion, we can further illustrate the relationships between
wrench
and
twist
more clearly. The
wrench
vectors in different coordinate systems can also be converted into each other via the
homogeneous transformation matrix
. Given two coordinate systems
\Sigma_{a}
and
\Sigma_{b}
, we can represent two
wrench vectors
acting on the end point
e
defined in each of them as
\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}
, respectively. At the same time, we can also represent the
twist vectors
of the end point
e
in these two coordinate systems as vectors
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}
. We know that the power that drives the motion of the rigid-body robot can be represented as

P=\mathbf{f}_{e}^{\top}\times\mathbf{v}_{e}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}.

(66)

Also the power
P
is scalar that is independent of the coordinate systems. Viewed in such a perspective, we can get that

\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}}_{(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}+(\mathbf{f}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\times\mathbf{v}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=2P}=\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}}_{(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}+(\mathbf{f}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]})^{\top}\times\mathbf{v}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}=2P}.

(67)

According to the above Equation
46
and Equation
47
, we know that

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{b}]})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}

(68)

By replacing the above representation of
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}
into Equation
67
, we will have

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}

(69)

\displaystyle=\left(Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{b}]})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}

(70)

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{b}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}.

(71)

Since the above equation should hold for any
twist velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}
, it can lead to

\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{b}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}.

(72)

With the same method, we can also derive that

\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(73)

3
Forward Kinematics

In this section, we will talk about the
forward kinematics
for readers on robot motion. Based on the robot motion introduced in the previous article
RobotBasics_2022
, in this part, we will show readers how the robot body (involving multiple joints and links) will move to the desired position and state.

3.1
What is Forward Kinematics?

The rigid robot body can actually be viewed as mapping, which projects the input motor movement parameters (
e.g.
, desired angles
\mathbf{q}
, velocity
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
and acceleration
\ddot{\mathbf{q}}
) to the desired robot position and state (
e.g.
, position
\mathbf{p}
, orientation
\mathbf{R}
, velocity
\mathbf{v}
and force
\mathbf{f}
of certain robot body parts).

Definition 14
.

(
Forward Kinematics
): Formally, forward kinematics is a calculation to obtain the desired robot position, orientation and other properties of links in the body from a given input parameter.

If we represent the input parameters as a vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)
, subject the pre-defined rigid robot body, we can represent the desired robot outputs as

\mathbf{o}(t)=g\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\right).

(74)

Since the input parameter and the desired output are both changing with time, we specify that vectors
\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)
and
\mathbf{o}(t)
both have time as their variable. Meanwhile, the calculation process of the mapping
g\left(\cdot\right)
to achieve the output is called the forward kinematics process.

Robot
forward kinematics
serves as the basis for robot simulation and control. It helps display the current state of the robot, calculate the center of mass of the robot body and detect potential collisions of the robot with its body parts and the environment. In this section, we will introduce the
forward kinematics
of robots in terms of the
velocity
,
orientation
,
velocity

force
and
torque
for readers, respectively.

3.2
Pose in Forward Kinematics

We have been discussing about the
position
and
orientation
changes of robot parts in both the previous article
RobotBasics_2022
and in the previous Section
2
already. Here, we will just provide a very brief introduction about updating robot
pose
, covering both
position
and
orientation
, in forward kinematics with the
chain rule
and
exponential representation
, respectively.

The Robot Arm Example We Use Before.

3.2.1
Chain Rule

According to our previous discussions, readers should be able to understand how the
rotation matrix
projects
position
vectors across coordinate systems. In some sense, the
rotation matrix
also indicates the
orientation
(relative to the world coordinate system) of robot actually within local or world coordinate systems.

Definition 15
.

(
Pose
): The combination of both position and orientation defines the pose of the robot, which can be represented as the position vector and rotation matrix pair
\left(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{R}\right)
. Since the homogeneous transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}&\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{bmatrix}
covers both
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{R}
, we can also say the homogeneous transformation matrix
\mathbf{T}
represents the pose of robots.

As introduced in the previous section, for the robot arm shown in Figure
1
, the position and orientation of its end point can be calculated via the
chain rule
, which will obtain the
homogeneous transition matrix

\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

(75)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}

(76)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}&\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(77)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(78)

Since the rigid-body robot arm is normally straight, the orientation of the end point is actually the same as the orientation of the joint that controls the last arm link. From the above representation of the
homogeneous transformation matrix

\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, we can denote the orientation of the end point of the arm with the rotation matrix

\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}.

(79)

As introduced in the previous section, based on the end point’s current position vector within the local coordinate
\Sigma_{c}
, we can calculate its end point’s position within the world coordinate as

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{c\to e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\\
1\end{bmatrix}.

(80)

The vector
\mathbf{p}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
indicating the robot arm end point’s position can also be represented as the following summation formula:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}

(81)

\displaystyle+\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}\mathbf{p}_{c\to e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}.

(82)

3.2.2
Product of Exponentials

In the above derivation, we assume the rotation matrix for each joint is known and can be used for calculating both the orientation and position of the robot arm end point. Meanwhile, when the rotation matrices are unknown, we can still calculate the end point orientation and position via the exponential operator we introduce in the previous article
RobotBasics_2022
based on the rotation angles or the joints.

As introduced in
RobotBasics_2022
, the
homogeneous transformation matrix
of the robot arm joint can be represented with its
angular velocity vector
with the
exponential operator
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}}&\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

(83)

where vector
{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
and scalar
\dot{q}_{a}
denote the rotation axis and rotation velocity, respectively.

In a similar way, we can also represent the
rotation matrix
and
homogeneous transformation matrix
with origins at other joints with their corresponding
angular velocity vector
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}&\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\dot{q}_{b}}&\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

(84)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}&\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\dot{q}_{c}}&\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(85)

By replacing them into the rotation matrix and end point position vector calculation, we can obtain their representations as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\dot{q}_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\dot{q}_{c}}\right),

(86)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}+\left(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}}\mathbf{p}_{a\to b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)+\left(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\dot{q}_{b}}\mathbf{p}_{b\to c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\right)

(87)

\displaystyle+\left(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\dot{q}_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{e}}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{b}]}\dot{q}_{c}}\mathbf{p}_{c\to e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\right).

(88)

The above representations of rotation matrix and end point position vector is also named as the
product of exponentials
of the joint angular velocity vectors.

3.3
Velocity in Forward Kinematics

Let’s revisit the Equaton
74
provided at the beginning of this section, if we take the desired output vector as the position vector
\mathbf{p}(t)
and the input as the angles of the joints
\mathbf{q}(t)
, we can represent the desired output as follows:

\mathbf{p}(t)=g\left(\mathbf{q}(t)\right).

(89)

3.3.1
Jacobian Matrix

By taking the derivatives of both size of the above equation with regard to the time variable
t
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial g\left(\mathbf{q}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{q}(t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial g\left(\mathbf{q}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{q}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t)

(90)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{q}}(t),

(91)

where term
\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t)
denotes the
linear velocity
vector and the matrix
\mathbf{J}=\frac{\partial g\left(\mathbf{q}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{q}}
about the joint variables
\mathbf{q}
is called the Jacobian matrix.

Example 16
.

For instance, let’s assume the length of the robot arm links starting from the origin
o
, joints
a
,
b
and
c
are
l_{1}
,
l_{2}
,
l_{3}
and
l_{4}
, respectively. Based on the rotation angles of these links, we can provide the analytic representation of the end point position in the world coordinate system as

\displaystyle x_{e}

\displaystyle=0,

(92)

\displaystyle y_{e}

\displaystyle=l_{2}\sin\theta+l_{3}\sin(\theta+\phi)+l_{4}\sin(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(93)

\displaystyle z_{e}

\displaystyle=l_{1}+l_{2}\cos\theta+l_{3}\cos(\theta+\phi)+l_{4}\cos(\theta+\phi+\psi).

(94)

By taking derivatives of both sides of the above equations, we can obtain

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{e}

\displaystyle=0,

(95)

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{e}

\displaystyle=l_{2}\dot{\theta}\cos\theta+l_{3}(\dot{\theta}+\dot{\phi})\cos(\theta+\phi)+l_{4}(\dot{\theta}+\dot{\phi}+\dot{\psi})\cos(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(96)

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{e}

\displaystyle=-l_{2}\dot{\theta}\sin\theta-l_{3}(\dot{\theta}+\dot{\phi})\sin(\theta+\phi)-l_{4}(\dot{\theta}+\dot{\phi}+\dot{\psi})\sin(\theta+\phi+\psi).

(97)

The above equations can also be represented in the form of
\dot{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{q}}
as follows:

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot{x}_{e}\\
\dot{y}_{e}\\
\dot{z}_{e}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{p}}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
\mathbf{J}_{2,1}&\mathbf{J}_{2,2}&\mathbf{J}_{2,3}\\
\mathbf{J}_{3,1}&\mathbf{J}_{3,2}&\mathbf{J}_{3,3}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{J}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\theta}\\
\dot{\phi}\\
\dot{\psi}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{q}}},

(98)

where the matrix elements

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{2,1}

\displaystyle=l_{2}\cos\theta+l_{3}\cos(\theta+\phi)+l_{4}\cos(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(99)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{2,2}

\displaystyle=l_{3}\cos(\theta+\phi)+l_{4}\cos(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(100)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{2,3}

\displaystyle=l_{4}\cos(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(101)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{3,1}

\displaystyle=-l_{2}\sin\theta-l_{3}\sin(\theta+\phi)-l_{4}\sin(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(102)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{3,2}

\displaystyle=-l_{3}\sin(\theta+\phi)-l_{4}\sin(\theta+\phi+\psi),

(103)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{3,3}

\displaystyle=-l_{4}\sin(\theta+\phi+\psi).

(104)

Example 17
.

For instance, if the arm link length
l_{1}=l_{2}=l_{3}=l_{4}=1
, and the arm joint angles
\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}
,
\phi=\frac{\pi}{2}
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\

\left(-\sin\frac{\pi}{4}-\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}-\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)&\left(-\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}-\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)&\left(-\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)\\
\end{bmatrix}

(105)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&0\\
1-\sqrt{2}&1-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(106)

3.3.2
Velocity Kinematics in World Coordinate System

As introduced in Section
2.2.1
, the
linear velocity
and
angular velocity
of robots can be described with its
twist velocity
, which can be represented with the
homogeneous transformation matrix
effectively. In this part, we will discuss about the robot
velocity
within the world coordinate system, whereas the
velocity kinematics
within the local coordinate system will be introduced in the following subsection.

According to Equation
13
introduced in the previous Section
2.2.1
, the
twist velocity
vector of the robot end point can be represented as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}.

(107)

Meanwhile, as introduced in the previous Section
2.5
, the
homogeneous transformation matrix
can also be effectively represented as the
screw axis
and
rotation angle
with the
exponential operator
. Viewed in such a perspective, we can represent the matrix
{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}

(108)

\displaystyle=\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\mathbf{T}_{0},

(109)

where the constant matrix
\mathbf{T}_{0}
to represent the initial state of the robot when
q_{a}=q_{b}=q_{c}=0
.

With the above exponential representation of matrix
{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
, we can represent its derivative with respect to the time as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{{d}}{{d}t}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\right)\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\mathbf{T}_{0}+\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\left(\frac{{d}}{{d}t}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\right)\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\mathbf{T}_{0}

(110)

\displaystyle+\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\left(\frac{{d}}{{d}t}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\right)\mathbf{T}_{0}

(111)

\displaystyle=\Big(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}\dot{q}_{a}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}+\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}\dot{q}_{b}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}

(112)

\displaystyle+\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}\dot{q}_{c}\Big)\mathbf{T}_{0}.

(113)

On the other hand, we can also represent the inverse of matrix
\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}\right)^{-1}

(114)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{b}]}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}

(115)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{T}_{0}^{-1}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}.

(116)

Based on the above derivation, we can formally represent the
twist velocity
vector with the above exponential representation as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}

(117)

\displaystyle=\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}\dot{q}_{a}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}+\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}\dot{q}_{b}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}

(118)

\displaystyle+\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}\dot{q}_{c}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}.

(119)

From the previous Section
2.3.1
and Section
2.3.2
, we already know that for
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]})^{-1}
, we can get
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=Ad({{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
. Since the
screw axis
is also defined as a normalized
twist velocity
, with the
\lor
operator be applied to both sides of the above equation, we can simplify the equation as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{Ad(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}){\mathbf{s}}_{a}}_{\mathbf{j}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}}\dot{q}_{a}+\underbrace{Ad(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}){\mathbf{s}}_{b}}_{\mathbf{j}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}}\dot{q}_{b}+\underbrace{Ad(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}){\mathbf{s}}_{c}}_{\mathbf{j}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}}\dot{q}_{c}

(120)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{j}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{a}+\mathbf{j}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{b}+\mathbf{j}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{q}_{c}

(121)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[\mathbf{j}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]},\mathbf{j}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]},\mathbf{j}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right]}_{\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{q}_{a}\\
\dot{q}_{b}\\
\dot{q}_{c}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(122)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\dot{\mathbf{q}}.

(123)

Definition 18
.

(
Space Jacobian
): Formally, the matrix

\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}=\begin{bmatrix}Ad(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}})&Ad(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}){\mathbf{s}}_{b}&Ad(\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}q_{a}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}){\mathbf{s}}_{c}\end{bmatrix}

(124)

we introduce above is also named as the space Jacobian matrix in the world coordinate system. The column vectors in matrix
\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}
actually denote the screw axes of different joints of the robot body in the world coordinate, respectively.

3.3.3
Velocity Kinematics in Local Coordinate System

Meanwhile, according to Equation
24
introduced in the previous Section
2.2.2
, we can also obtain the
twist velocity
vector representation of the end point within a local coordinate system
\Sigma_{c}
, which can be denoted as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{e}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}\right)^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{T}}^{[\Sigma_{c}\to\Sigma_{\textsc{w}}]}

(125)

\displaystyle=\left(\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\right)\dot{q}_{a}+\left(\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}\exp^{\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\right)\dot{q}_{b}+\left(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}\right)\dot{q}_{c}

(126)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{Ad\left(\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{a}}_{\mathbf{j}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}}\dot{q}_{a}+\underbrace{Ad\left(\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\right)\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}}_{\mathbf{j}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{c}]}}\dot{q}_{b}+\underbrace{\left(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}\right)}_{\mathbf{j}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}}\dot{q}_{c}

(127)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[\mathbf{j}_{a}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]},\mathbf{j}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{b}\to\Sigma_{c}]},\mathbf{j}_{c}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\right]}_{\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{q}_{a}\\
\dot{q}_{b}\\
\dot{q}_{c}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(128)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\dot{\mathbf{q}}.

(129)

Definition 19
.

(
Body Jacobian
): Formally, the matrix

\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}=\begin{bmatrix}Ad\left(\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{b}q_{b}}\right)&Ad\left(\exp^{-\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}q_{c}}\right)&\left(\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_{c}\right)\end{bmatrix}

(130)

we introduce above is also named as the body Jacobian matrix in the local coordinate system
\Sigma_{c}
that the end point lies in. The column vectors in matrix
\mathbf{J}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}
actually denote the screw axes of different joints of the robot body in the local coordinate
\Sigma_{c}
, respectively.

3.4
Torque in Forward Kinematics

The
Jacobian matrix
also plays an important role in robot
static analysis
. Formally, at the end of the robot arm, we can represent the generated force vector as
\mathbf{f}_{e}
and the joint torque vector as
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}
.

3.4.1
Static Analysis

According to the physics, in the rotational system, the system power equals to the product of
torque vector

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}
and the
angular velocity vector

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}
of the end point,
i.e.
,

P=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}.

(131)

Meanwhile, the power of the end point can also be represented with its end point force vector
\mathbf{f}_{e}
and its
linear velocity vector

\mathbf{v}_{e}
,
i.e.
,

P=\mathbf{f}_{e}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{e}.

(132)

These above two equations will derive that

\mathbf{f}_{e}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{e}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}.

(133)

3.4.2
Force-Torque Relationship

Meanwhile, according to the Equation
90
we present before when defining the
Jacobian Matrix
before, we know that the
linear velocity vector

\mathbf{v}_{e}=\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}
, so we can have the following equation,

\mathbf{f}_{e}^{\top}\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}.

(134)

The above equation holds for any
angular velocity vector

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{e}
if

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}=\left(\mathbf{f}_{e}^{\top}\mathbf{J}\right)^{\top}=\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{e}.

(135)

The above equation calculates the required torque
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}
needed to generate the desired force
\mathbf{f}_{e}
at the end point. Meanwhile, when the
Jacobian Matrix
is not singular and its transpose is invertible, we can also obtain

\mathbf{f}_{e}=(\mathbf{J}^{\top})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}=\mathbf{J}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{e}.

(136)

Such an equation indicates that, under the static equilibrium state, the force that can be generated at the end point given the torques of the robot arm. This equation is very important, and will be used in many scenarios. For instance, to pick up a box of certain weight
w
, we need to decide the needed torques of each joint motors so the robot can hold the box. More discussions about the robot torque and generated forces will be provided in the following sections when talking about the robot dynamics.

4
Inverse Kinematics

In this section, we will introduce another important topic about robotic for readers, the
inverse kinematics
. Just from the name, we can know that it is a reversed process of
forward kinematics
introduced in the previous section. In this section, we will introduce two different methods to address the
inverse kinematics
problem for readers, and also provide detailed analysis of the Jacobian singularity.

4.1
What is Inverse Kinematics?

We can still take the Equation
74
we use at the beginning of the previous Section
3
on
forward kinematics
to explain “what is
inverse kinematics
” for readers.

•

Forward Kinematics
: Given the parameter inputs
\mathbf{q}(t)
, the process of calculating the desired output
\mathbf{o}(t)
of the robot body is called
forward kinematics
,
i.e.
,

\mathbf{o}(t)=g\left(\mathbf{q}(t)\right).

(137)

•

Inverse Kinematics
: Given the desired output
\mathbf{O}(t)
, the problem to find the parameter solution
\mathbf{q}(t)
that can satisfy

\arg_{\mathbf{q}(t)}g\left(\mathbf{q}(t)\right)=\mathbf{O}(t),\forall t\in\{1,2,\cdots\},

(138)

is named as the
inverse kinematics
problem.

Compared with
forward kinematics
that give clear “instructions” (denoted by parameters
\mathbf{q}(t)
) to robots that will perform accordingly to illustrate the outputs,
inverse kinematics
is more frequently used in real-world robot control,
e.g.
,
standing
,
walking
,
picking up items
. In this part, we will try to introduce how to find the parameter vector
\mathbf{q}(t)
that will generate the output satisfying the constraint
g\left(\mathbf{q}(t)\right)=\mathbf{O}(t)
.

4.2
Analytic Solution

Before we introduce the
numerical solution
to the
inverse kinematics
problem, we would like to use a toy example to illustrate how to solve the problem analytically.

Example 20
.

Figure 5
:
An Example of 2-Link Robot Ram.

As shown in Figure
5
, we illustrate a robot arm in the 3D coordinate system with two links of length
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
, respectively. For the first arm link, it forms an angle of
\theta
degrees with respect to the
y
axis. Meanwhile, the second arm link further forms an angle of
\phi
degrees with the first arm link. We aim to configure the parameter values of the angles
\theta
and
\phi
to make the end point reaches position denoted by coordinate pair
(x_{e},y_{e},0)
in the space.

Analytic Solution
: Based on the robot arm example shown in Figure
5
, we will show below about how to use the
analytic solution
to calculate the desired angles
\theta
and
\phi
.

First of all, for the triangle
\triangle oae
, according to the
law of cosine
, we know that

\displaystyle oe^{2}=oa^{2}+ae^{2}-2(oa)(ae)\cos\beta

(139)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}=l_{1}^{2}+l_{2}^{2}-2l_{1}l_{2}\cos\beta

(140)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\beta=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{l_{1}^{2}+l_{2}^{2}-y_{e}^{2}-z_{e}^{2}}{2l_{1}l_{2}}\right).

(141)

Following the same law, we can also obtain that

\displaystyle ae^{2}=oa^{2}+oe^{2}-2(oa)(oe)\cos\alpha

(142)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle l_{2}^{2}=l_{1}^{2}+y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}-2l_{1}\sqrt{y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}}\cos\alpha

(143)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\alpha=\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{l_{1}^{2}+y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}-l_{2}^{2}}{2l_{1}\sqrt{y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}}}\right).

(144)

Meanwhile, according to the coordinate of end point
e
,
i.e.
,
(0,y_{e},z_{e})
, we can get that

\displaystyle\tan 2(\alpha+\theta)=(z_{e},y_{e})

(145)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(\alpha+\theta)=atan2(z_{e},y_{e}).

(146)

Based on the above analysis, we can easily obtain the representations of angles
\theta
and
\alpha
as follows:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=(\alpha+\theta)-\alpha=atan2(z_{e},y_{e})-\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{l_{1}^{2}+y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}-l_{2}^{2}}{2l_{1}\sqrt{y_{e}^{2}+z_{e}^{2}}}\right),

(147)

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\pi-\beta=\pi-\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{l_{1}^{2}+l_{2}^{2}-y_{e}^{2}-z_{e}^{2}}{2l_{1}l_{2}}\right).

(148)

According to the above analysis, via the
analytic method
shown in the example, we can obtain the closed-form solution of the
inverse kinematics
problem. The readers may have also notice that the
analytic method
calculation process is already very cumbersome for the 2-link robot arm. When it comes to more complicated robot structures, like a 6-link robot arm, it will become much more challenging to solve the problem analytically.

4.3
Inverse Position and Orientation Kinematics

Compared with the
inverse kinematics
, we have noticed that the
forward kinematics
calculation process we introduce in the previous section is much simpler and easier. Readers may also wonder if we can use
forward kinematics
introduced before to help identify the solution to the
inverse kinematics
problem. This is also the problem we plan to study in this part, and we will introduce a
numerical solution
that doesn’t need to analyze the

4.3.1
Numerical Solution Method

Before we introduce the detailed information about the
numerical solution
, we would like to first provide the general framework of the
numerical solution
algorithm.

•

Step 1
: Define the position vector
\mathbf{p}_{start}
and rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}_{start}
of the robot’s start position and orientation.

•

Step 2
: Define the position vector
\mathbf{p}_{target}
and rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}_{target}
of the robot’s target position and orientation.

•

Step 3
: Initialize a variable vector
\mathbf{q}
that denotes the angles of the robot’s link angles.

•

Step 4
: Given the variable vector
\mathbf{q}
and the current robot position and orientation, calculate the robot position vector
\mathbf{p}
and orientation matrix
\mathbf{R}
outputs with
forward kinematics
.

•

Step 5
: Calculate the introduced error (or loss) term of the
forward kinematics
outputs
\mathbf{p}
,
\mathbf{R}
compared with the target outputs
\mathbf{p}_{target}
,
\mathbf{R}_{target}
,
i.e.
,
\delta\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{target}
and
\delta\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{target}
.

•

Step 6
: If
\delta\mathbf{p}
and
\delta\mathbf{R}
are sufficiently small (less than the pre-defined threshold), stop the calculation and return the current variable vector
\mathbf{q}
as the output.

•

Step 7
: Otherwise, calculate the term
\delta\mathbf{q}
with the introduced error terms
\delta\mathbf{p}
and
\delta\mathbf{R}
, and update the current variable vector
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}+\delta\mathbf{q}
and go to
Step 4
.

Figure 6:
Outline of the Numerical Solution in Updating Variable Vector via Forward Kinematics.

An outline of the above algorithm is also illustrated in Figure
6
. Such an iterative adjustment process continues until the stop criteria is met. Meanwhile, the above algorithm also create several new open questions:

1.

Error Function
: How to define “whether
\delta\mathbf{p}
and
\delta\mathbf{R}
are sufficiently small”?

2.

Adjustment Term
: How to calculate the “
\delta\mathbf{q}
” term to be used for updating
\mathbf{q}
?

To address the first problem, a loss function is introduced to quantify “whether
\delta\mathbf{p}
and
\delta\mathbf{R}
are sufficiently small” or not. Formally, given
\delta\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{target}
and
\delta\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{target}
, we can represent the introduced loss term to be

\ell(\delta\mathbf{p},\delta\mathbf{R})=\left\|\delta\mathbf{p}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\left(\ln\delta\mathbf{R}\right)^{\lor}\right\|^{2}

(149)

According to the previous tutorial article, the “
\ln
” and “
\lor
” operators will project the
rotation matrix
to the corresponding
angular velocity
vector,
i.e.
,
\delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}t)=(\ln\delta\mathbf{R})^{\lor}
. The term
\boldsymbol{\omega}
denotes the
angular velocity
vector of the interested point of the robot body. For the loss term
\ell(\delta\mathbf{p},\delta\mathbf{R})\leq\gamma
(where
\gamma=1e^{-6}
is a pre-defined threshold parameter), then the updating process will stop and we can also generally guarantee that the robot position vector and orientation matrix approaches to the targets.

As to the second problem, we will introduce its solution in the following subsection for readers with the
Newton-Raphson Method
.

4.3.2
Newton-Raphson Method

Let’s assume we change the parameter vector
\mathbf{q}
with a very minor term
\delta\mathbf{q}
, where
\delta\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}
. Its impact on the position vector
\mathbf{q}
and orientation matrix
\mathbf{R}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{p}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{q}+\delta\mathbf{q})-g_{p}(\mathbf{q}),

(150)

\displaystyle\delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}t)

\displaystyle=g_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\mathbf{q}+\delta\mathbf{q})-g_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}(\mathbf{q}).

(151)

In the above equation, we already replace the term
\delta\mathbf{R}
with the
\delta\boldsymbol{\omega}
just to simplify the representations below.

As introduced before in Equation
90
on
velocity kinematics
, if the term
\delta\mathbf{q}
is small enough and approaches to
\mathbf{0}
, we can actually combine the above two equations and rewrite them as follows:

\begin{bmatrix}\delta{\mathbf{p}}\\
\delta({\boldsymbol{\omega}}t)\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{J}\delta{\mathbf{q}},

(152)

where
\mathbf{J}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6\times n}
is the
Jacobian matrix
we introduce before and vectors
\mathbf{v}=\dot{\mathbf{p}}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
denote the
linear
and
angular velocity
, respectively. The notation
n
denotes the number of links in the robot, which is equal to the dimension of the variable vector
\mathbf{q}
. The combined vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\end{bmatrix}
is also the
twist velocity
vector we define before.

If matrix
\mathbf{J}
is invertible, we can represent the term
\delta\mathbf{q}
as follows:

\delta{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{J}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

(153)

The above equation answer the second problem on “
how to calculate the ‘
\delta\mathbf{q}
’ term to be used for updating
\mathbf{q}
”. In application, instead of directly applying the calculated term
\delta{\mathbf{q}}
to update the variable vector
\mathbf{q}
, we usually also includes a parameter
\lambda\in(0,1)
to control the variable updating speed,
i.e.
,

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}+\lambda\cdot\delta{\mathbf{q}}.

(154)

The introduced parameter
\lambda
can stabilize the updating process and avoid drastic changes to the variables, and this method is formally named as the
Newton-Raphson method
.

If the readers are familiar with
mathematical optimization
or
gradient descent
algorithms, the
Newton-Raphson method
has been widely applied in parameter updating to optimize the model performance. Meanwhile, in the above calculation process, we have a strong assumption that “the
Jacobian matrix

\mathbf{J}
is invertible”. At the end of this section, we will have a detailed analysis about the
Jacobian matrix
and discuss about how to update the variables when
\mathbf{J}
is
singular
.

Inverse Velocity and Torque Kinematics

In the previous Section

and Section
3.4
, we discuss about the robot

and

we provide in the previous section on defining the

Depending on the coordinate chosen to study the

4.5
Singular Jacobian Matrix

In this part, we will talk about an important problem about the Jacobian matrix. According to the previous discussion, the Jacobian matrix plays an important role in calculating (1)
twist velocity
vector and
force
vector of the end point in
forward kinematics
, (2) needed joint
angle
vector and
torque
vector to generate the
twist velocity
and
force
in
inverse kinematics
. We make lots of assumption on the Jacobian matrix before in the derivation,
e.g.
, it is full rank and invertible. In this part, we will discuss about some potential problems with Jacobian matrix,
i.e.
, the
singularity
problem.

4.5.1
Singularity and Gimbal Lock

We can first use the classic “gimbal lock” problem to describe “what is singularity”. The “gimbal lock” term becomes famous in the movie Apollo 13, which happens when the rotational axis of the middle term in the sequence becomes parallel to the rotational axis of the first or the third term.

Figure 7:
An Illustration of the IMU (Inertial Measure Unit) used in Apollo Lunar Module.

Example 21
.

As illustrated in Figure
7
, we show the IMU (inertial measurement unit) used in the Apollo Lunar Module and its schematic representation, which is a mechanical gyroscope used for spacecraft navigation. This IMU has three orthogonal gyroscopes that hold it at a constant orientation with respect to the universe. According to the orientations of these three axes and the right-hand rule, we can mark them as the
x
,
y
and
z
axis, respectively.

Figure 8
:
An Illustration of the IMU Gimbal Lock Problem.

As the spacecraft pose changes in the universe, the stable member of the IMU will remain relatively stable in terms of the orientation, but the outer, middle and inner gimbal will rotate around their axes with certain angles. As shown in Figure
8
, we can represent the rotation angles around these three axes as
\theta_{x}
,
\theta_{y}
and
\theta_{z}
, respectively. From these rotation angles, we will be able to infer the orientation of the spacecraft.

Meanwhile, when the middle gimbal rotates around the
y
axis with an angle
\theta_{y}=\pi/2
, we observe that the axis of the inner and outer gimbal (i.e., the
x
axis and
z
axis) will be aligned and share the same rotation axis. For such a case, the IMU will have only two degrees of freedom and one degree of freedom will be lost.

Such a degree of freedom lose in the above example can also be illustrated via the mathematical representations of the rotation matrix about the IMU. For the rotation around the
x
,
y
and
z
axis with angles of
\theta_{x}
,
\theta_{y}
and
\theta_{z}
degrees, we can represent the corresponding rotation matrix of the IMU to be

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{x})\mathbf{R}_{y}(\theta_{y})\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta_{z}).

(157)

Such rotation matrix follows the cyclic rotation rules,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{x}(\pi/2)\mathbf{R}_{y}(\theta)\mathbf{R}_{x}(\pi/2)^{\top}=\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta),

(158)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta)\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)^{\top}=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta),

(159)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{z}(\pi/2)\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta)\mathbf{R}_{z}(\pi/2)^{\top}=\mathbf{R}_{y}(\theta);

(160)

as well as the anti-cyclic rotation rules,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta)\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)=\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta),

(161)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{z}(\pi/2)^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{y}(\theta)\mathbf{R}_{z}(\pi/2)=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta),

(162)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{x}(\pi/2)^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta)\mathbf{R}_{x}(\pi/2)=\mathbf{R}_{y}(\theta).

(163)

We will not provide the proofs of the above rules, and readers can easily prove them with the concrete representations of rotation matrices
\mathbf{R}_{x}
,
\mathbf{R}_{y}
and
\mathbf{R}_{z}
to calculate their product result representations by yourselves.

According to the above rules as well as the
orthogonality
of rotation matrix, when the middle gimbal rotation angle
\theta_{y}=\pi/2
, we can rewrite the rotation matrix of the IMU as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{x})\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta_{z})

(164)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{x})\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta_{z})\left(\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)\right)

(165)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{x})\left(\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)\mathbf{R}_{z}(\theta_{z})\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)^{\top}\right)\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)

(166)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{x})\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{z})\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2)

(167)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}_{x}(\theta_{x}+\theta_{z})\mathbf{R}_{y}(\pi/2).

(168)

The above representation of the rotation matrix also indicates that the IMU will not be able to represent the rotation in the
z
axis anymore and render the IMU fail to work. Such a problem happens only at certain special circumstances,
e.g.
,
\theta_{y}=\pi/2
as show above.

4.5.2
Jacobian Singularity

A robot singularity is a physical blockage, not some kind of abstract mathematical problem.

Definition 22
.

(
Robot Singularity
): At a singularity, a robot loses one or more degrees of freedom.

In the previous subsection, we have illustrate the
singularity
of a simple “robot”,
i.e.
, the IMU with three degrees of freedom, which will lose one degree of freedom when the middle gimbal rotates with an angle of
\theta_{y}=\pi/2
degrees.

Besides the simple IMU,
singularity
widely exists in many robots, like a robot arm or a legged robot. Readers may also wonder what are the causes of the
robot singularity
. In this part, we will illustrate that
robot singularity
is closely related to the singularity of the corresponding
Jacobian matrix
.

Example 23
.

To illustrate the relationship of robot singularity with the Jacobian matrix, we provide an example of robot arm in Figure
9
, whose links are connected via the
6
joints, and the DH parameters are illustrated in the figure as well.

A Note
: We haven’t introduced the robot DH parameters for readers yet. For now, you just need to know robot DH parameters can describe the pose and structure of rigid-body robots. Once the DH parameters are specified, then we can build the mathematical model to recover the current pose and the full structure of the robot. More detailed information about DH parameter will be described in detail in the follow-up tutorial articles instead.

In the DH parameter representation, the local coordinates are attached to the links on the far-away side of the joints with the
z

x
axis, index finger:
y

z

:

6

According to the DH table, we can define its corresponding Jacobian matrix as follows:

\mathbf{J}=\begin{bmatrix}X&X&X&X&X&X\\
X&X&X&X&X&X\\
0&X&X&X&X&X\\
0&s_{1}&-c_{1}s_{2}&-c_{3}s_{1}-c_{123}&c_{1}s_{24}-c_{4}s_{13}+c_{1234}&X\\
0&-c_{1}&-s_{12}&c_{13}-c_{2}s_{13}&s_{124}+c_{14}s_{3}+c_{234}s_{1}&X\\
1&0&c_{2}&-s_{23}&c_{34}s_{2}-c_{2}s_{4}&c_{24}s_{5}-c_{5}s_{23}+c_{3}s_{245}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(169)

where the simplified notations of terms
c_{i}
and
s_{i}
denote
\cos q_{i}
and
\sin q_{i}

and

represent
\sin(q_{i}+q_{j}+q_{k}+\cdots)
and
\cos(q_{i}+q_{j}+q_{k}+\cdots)

Via symbolic computation, we can calculate the determinant of the Jacobian matrix as

\det(\mathbf{J})=-\cos(q_{5})\sin(q_{2})\Gamma(q_{4})\text{, where }\Gamma(q_{4})=(d_{3}^{2}d_{5})/2+d_{3}^{2}d_{5}\cos(2q_{4})/2.

(170)

In the derivation of inverse kinematics we introduce before, the inverse of the Jacobian matrix play a critical role. We assumed the Jacobian matrix is invertible, whose inverse will help calculate the desired joint angles, angular velocity and torque vector parameters. Meanwhile, the Jacobian matrix is invertible when Jacobian is not singular, and a matrix is singular iff its determinant is
0
. Therefore, we can derive the singularity condition to be

\det(\mathbf{J})=0\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}&\text{Condition 1}:\cos(q_{5})=0\\
&\text{Condition 2}:\sin(q_{2})=0\\
&\text{Condition 3}:\Gamma(q_{4})=0\\
\end{cases}\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}&q_{5}=\pm\pi/2\\
&q_{2}=0\\
&q_{4}=-\pi/2\\
\end{cases},

(171)

where assume the joint angles are all within the range
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
and some cases like
q_{2}=\pi
have been pruned from the results.

The internal singularity (also known as joint space singularity) are generally caused by an alignment of the robots axes in space. The conditions derived above can introduce three different internal singularity as follows:

–

4.6
Singularity-Robust Inverse of Jacobian

By now, readers should know that the Jacobian matrix and its inverse plays an important role in robot inverse kinematics. When the Jacobian matrix is singular, we will suffer from lots of problems in calculating its inverse kinematics parameters. Meanwhile, in applications, people has almost zero tolerance for wired performance of the robots due to the singularity problem. At the end of this section, we will introduce a
singularity robust inverse
of the Jacobian matrix, which allows the robots still be able to work with no worries about the singularity problem.

To illustrate the effectiveness, we also use an example in this part to compare the performance of the robot using conventional Jacobian inverse in Newton Raphson Method introduced in Section
4.3.2
and the new method to calculate the
singular robust inverse
of Jacobian matrix.

Figure 14:
An Illustration of Legged Robot Suffering from Singularity
10.5555/3100040
.

Example 25
.

As illustrated in Figure
14
, we aim to calculate the robot joint angularity velocity to move the robot right foot forward from a non-singular pose with the Newton-Raphson method. The right plot shows the angles of the hip joint, keen joint and ankle joint at the given target foot position. The solid line denote the solution obtained by the numerical method. For comparison, we also provide the analytic solution in the plot, which is denoted by the dotted line on the right-hand side when the robot reaches its singular pose.

We observe that the robot will reach the singularity, since the axes of the hip pitch, knee pitch and ankle pitch will collinear. On the right plot, we observe that the angles of the hip, knee and ankle angles vibrate and go off the chart.

Let’s take a look at the equation illustrating the relationship between the
twist velocity
vector of the end point and the robot joint
angular velocity
:

\boldsymbol{\nu}=\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{q}}.

(175)

We aim to find the desired vector
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
to make the above equation hold. When the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{J}
is not singular, we can easily solve the problem with
\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}
. However, when matrix
\mathbf{J}
is singular, we have to find another way to calculate the optimal
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
vector.

From the above description, some readers probably already get a sense that “can we solve the problem as an mathematical optimization problem?”. Finding the solution to equation
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{q}}
is actually equivalent to find the vector
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
that can make the error vector
\mathbf{e}
equal to zero, where

\mathbf{e}=\boldsymbol{\nu}-\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{q}}.

(176)

Making the error vector
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{0}
is not an easy task. Sometimes, we will not be able to find such a solution actually, which will render the robot failing to work. Then, can we slightly relax our expectation? Instead of find the error vector
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
to make
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{0}
, we propose to find the optimal vector
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
to make the error vector
\mathbf{e}
as close to the zero vector
\mathbf{0}
as possible,
i.e.
,

\min_{\dot{\mathbf{q}}}\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}}),

(177)

where we can introduce a loss function notation
\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{e}
to represent the optimization objective function. If the function
\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})
approaches
0
, the error vector
\mathbf{e}
will be close to the zero vector as well, and vice versa.

The optimal variable
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
can be obtained by making the derivative of
\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})
with respect to variable
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
equal to zero,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}=\mathbf{0}

(178)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \ -\mathbf{J}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\nu}+\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}

(179)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \ \dot{\mathbf{q}}=\left(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{J}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

(180)

Meanwhile, the matrix
\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}
is actually
positive semidefinite
, and it can still be singular. Since
\det(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J})=\det(\mathbf{J}^{\top})\det(\mathbf{J})
, if the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{J}
is singular,
i.e.
,
\det(\mathbf{J})=0
, we can easily get that
\det(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J})=0
,
i.e.
, matrix product
\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}
is singular as well. In other words, if
\mathbf{J}
is singular, the above representation doesn’t exist neither.

If the readers have any experiences with mathematical optimization, especially with variable regularizations, you probably can propose different definition of the loss function
\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})
definition. To address the above solution non-existence problem when
\mathbf{J}
is singular, we propose to modify the loss function slightly by incorporating a regularization term on variable
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
as follows:

\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{e}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\dot{\mathbf{q}}^{\top}\dot{\mathbf{q}}.

(181)

By solving the equation
\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}=\mathbf{0}
, we can obtain the representation of the solution to be

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}(\dot{\mathbf{q}})}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}=\mathbf{0}

(182)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \ -\mathbf{J}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\nu}+\left(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{0}

(183)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \ \dot{\mathbf{q}}=\left(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{J}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

(184)

We know
\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}
is
positive semidefinite
and if
\lambda\neq 0
, their summation
\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
will be
positive definite
and its inverse always exists, which ensures we can find a solution
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
no matter what the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{J}
is actually.

We can introduce a new notation for the term
\left(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{J}^{\top}
as follows

\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}=\left(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{J}^{\top},

(185)

and the solution can be represented with the new notation as

\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

(186)

We also name
\mathbf{J}^{\dagger}
as the
singularity-robust inverse
of the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{J}
.

.

Let’s come back to the example we show at the beginning of this part. As shown in Figure
15
, to move the robot right foot forward, with the new singularity-robust inverse of the Jacobian matrix
\mathbf{J}
, we can get rid of the singular pose of the robot perfectly in the movement. As shown in the right plot of the joint angles, we can still control the robot pretty without singularity problem at all.

5
Robot Dynamics

We have been introducing the robot joint rotation, robot motion, forward kinematics and inverse kinematics for readers already, which covers the transformation of robot
position
,
orientation
,
linear velocity
and
angular velocity
within as well as across coordinate systems. Meanwhile, we didn’t discuss much about the driven forces that lead to such changes yet, which will be the main topic to be studied in this section for readers,
i.e.
, the
robot dynamics
.

5.1
What is Robot Dynamics?

Formally,
robot dynamics
aims to study the relationship between the forces (or torques) that act on a robot and the accelerations that they produce. For the robot studied in this article, the forces covers both the internal forces,
e.g.
, the driving forces of joint motors, as well as external forces,
e.g.
, the gravity of robot links and body, as well as the items the robot end points trying to pickup. Meanwhile, the generated accelerations overtime will create velocity and further change the pose of robot parts and body.

There exist different formulation methods of
robot dynamics
. In this section, we will introduce two dynamics formulation methods for readers that are initially proposed in Physics but can also be used for formulating robot dynamics as well,
i.e.
,

•

Lagrangian Dynamics Formulation
: In the
Lagrangian dynamics
, motion is described by energies as scalars and it is based on the principle of least action. Generalized coordinates are used in
Lagrangian dynamics
instead of constraint forces, and conservation laws can be derived easily.
Lagrangian dynamics
is not idea for non-conservative forces,
e.g.
, friction, and widely used in all areas of physics.

•

Newton-Euler Dynamics Formulation
: In the
Newtonian dynamics
, motion is described by forces with vectors and it is based on the Newton’s laws of motion.
Newtonian dynamics
involves constraint forces, and doesn’t have a systematic methods for deriving conservation laws.
Lagrangian dynamics
handles non-conservative forces very well, and mainly applicable to classic physics.

There are two main problems that are studied in
robot dynamics
, which include

•

Forward Dynamics
: Given the known
forces
, calculate the
acceleration
(as well as
velocity
and
pose
) of the robot generated by the forces.

•

Inverse Dynamics
: Given the desired
acceleration
(as well as
velocity
and
pose
), figure out the needed
forces
to generate them.

Similar to the
forward kinematics
and
inverse kinematics
discussed before, the
forward dynamics
is normally used for simulation, while the
inverse dynamics
has very diverse usage in
robot control
.

In this section, we first will introduce
Lagrangian dynamics formulation
and
Newton-Euler dynamics formulation
for readers. At the end, we will use a concrete example of motor driven robot link to discuss about the
forward dynamics
and
inverse dynamics
for readers, and the following subsections are also organized according to these topics.

5.2
Lagrangian Dynamics Formulation

In this part, we will introduce the Lagrangian formulation of dynamics and discuss about how to use it to model the dynamics of robot arms.

5.2.1
Euler-Lagrange Equation

To use Lagrangian formulation of robot dynamics, the first step is to choose a set of independent coordinates
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
that defines the robot configuration. The chosen coordinates can not only be the Cartesian coordinate but also other configurations of the robot,
e.g.
, the angles of the joints, which are also named as the
generalized coordinates
. The set of all potential configurations
\mathbf{q}
will define the
configuration space

\mathbbm{C}
.

Definition 27
.

(
Lagrangian Dynamics
): Formally, the Lagrangian dynamics formulates a dynamics system as a pair
(\mathbbm{C},L)
, where
\mathbbm{C}
denotes the configuration space and
L
is the Lagrangian function defined as follows:

L(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})=K(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})-P(\mathbf{q})\text{, where }\mathbf{q}\in\mathbbm{C}.

(187)

In the above equation, terms
K(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})
and
P(\mathbf{q})
denote the kinetic energy and potential energy of the system, respectively. Notation
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
denotes the first-order derivative of term
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbbm{C}
.

After choosing the
generalized coordinates
, we can also further define the
generalized forces

\mathbf{f}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
subject to the constraint that
\mathbf{f}^{\top}\mathbf{q}
corresponds to power. Depending on the chosen
generalized coordinates
, the
generalized forces

\mathbf{f}
can not only represent regular forces, but can also represent other properties of the robot,
e.g.
, the torque of joints. For instance, if
generalized coordinates

\mathbf{q}
denotes the position in Cartesian coordinate, then
\mathbf{f}
will be regular forces, since the product of forces and moving distance will denote the power. Meanwhile, if
\mathbf{q}
denotes the rotation angles, then
\mathbf{f}
should represent the torques, since the product of torque and rotation angles denotes the power.

Based on the
Lagrangian function
defined above, any equations of motion can now be expressed in terms of the Lagrangian as follows:

\mathbf{f}=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{q}},

(188)

where
\mathbf{f}
denotes the
generalized forces
. Such an equation is also referred to as the
Euler-Lagrange equation
.

Instead of forces,
Lagrangian dynamics
uses energy in the system formulation, and the
Lagrangian function
defined above summarizes the dynamics of the entire system. Some readers may wonder “why the
Lagrangian function
is defined as the kinetic energy minus the potential energy?” According to Physics, defining
Lagrangian function
in this way will allow us to generate the correct equations of motion in agreement with classic physical laws,
e.g.
, the “
Newton’s laws of motion
”.

Example 28
.

To illustrate that the above Lagrangian function can be used to define the equations of motion, we also provide an example of a particle of mass
m
that is moving within a 1D space along a vertical line. We represent the position (i.e., the height) of the particle as a variable
q
, while its speed and acceleration can be denoted as
\dot{q}
and
\ddot{q}
, respectively. The gravitational force acting on the particle will generate an acceleration represented by
g
. According to what we learn from high-school Physics course, we can represent the motion of the particle as

f-mg=m\ddot{q},

(189)

where
f
is the external force acting on the particle to drive its motion. If readers still remember, the above equation is derived according to the classic Newton’s second law of motion.

Meanwhile, the Euler-Lagrange equation introduced above will also help derive the identical representation of the external force. For the particle, we can represent its kinetic energy and potential energy as follows:

K(q,\dot{q})=\frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^{2}\text{, }P(q)=mgq,

(190)

which will define the Lagrangian function

L(q,\dot{q})=K(q,\dot{q})-P(q)=\frac{1}{2}m\dot{q}^{2}-mgq.

(191)

According to Equation
211
, we can represent the equation of motion as

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{{q}}}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial{q}}

(192)

\displaystyle=m\ddot{q}+mg,

(193)

which is identical to Equation
189
derived from Newton’s second law of motion.

(197)

\displaystyle K_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}m_{2}(\dot{x}_{2}^{2}+\dot{y}_{2}^{2})

(198)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}m_{2}\left((L_{1}^{2}+2L_{1}L_{2}\cos(\theta_{2})+L_{2}^{2})\dot{\theta}_{1}^{2}+2(L_{2}^{2}+L_{1}L_{2}\cos(\theta_{2}))\dot{\theta}_{1}\dot{\theta}_{2}+L_{2}^{2}\dot{\theta}_{2}^{2}\right),

(199)

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=m_{1}gy_{1}=m_{1}gL_{1}\sin(\theta_{1}),

(200)

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=m_{1}gy_{1}=m_{2}g\left(L_{1}\sin(\theta_{1})+L_{2}\sin(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})\right).

(201)

By combining them together, we will be able to define the
Lagrangian functoin

L(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(K_{i}-P_{i}).

(202)

By choosing the joint angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\theta_{1}\\
\theta_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}
as the
generalized coordinates
, as discussed in the previous Section
5.2.1
, then the corresponding
generalized forces
derived in Equation
211
will actually be the
torques

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1}=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\theta}_{1}}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{1}}\text{, and }\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2}=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\theta}_{2}}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_{2}}.

(203)

These two terms above together will define the torque vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}
as follows

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(204)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}m_{1}L_{1}^{2}+m_{2}(L_{1}^{2}+2L_{1}L_{2}\cos{\theta_{2}}+L_{2}^{2})&m_{2}(L_{1}L_{2}\cos{\theta_{2}}+L_{2}^{2})\\
m_{2}(L_{1}L_{2}\cos(\theta_{2})+L_{2}^{2})&m_{2}L_{2}^{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{symmetric positive-definite mass matrix}},

(205)

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-m_{2}L_{1}L_{2}\sin(\theta_{2})(2\dot{\theta}_{1}\dot{\theta}_{2}+\dot{\theta}_{2}^{2})\\
m_{2}L_{1}L_{2}\dot{\theta}_{1}^{2}\sin(\theta_{2})\\
\end{bmatrix}

(206)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-m_{2}L_{1}L_{2}\sin(\theta_{2})2\dot{\theta}_{1}\dot{\theta}_{2}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Coriolis torque}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-m_{2}L_{1}L_{2}\sin(\theta_{2})\dot{\theta}_{2}^{2}\\
m_{2}L_{1}L_{2}\sin(\theta_{2})\dot{\theta}_{1}^{2}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{centripetal torque}}

(207)

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}(m_{1}+m_{2})L_{1}g\cos(\theta_{1})+m_{2}gL_{2}\cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})\\
m_{2}gL_{2}\cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{gravitational torque}}.

(208)

Formally, term
\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbbm{R}^{2\times 2}
is a symmetric positive-definite mass matrix about the two robot arm links; vector
\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\in\mathbbm{R}^{2}
contains both the
Coriolis torque
and the
centripetal torque
torque; and vector
\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbbm{R}^{2}
is the
gravitational torque
. All these matrix and vectors are defined based on the general coordinate vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and its first-order derivative
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

5.2.3
Lagrangian Dynamics of Multi-Link Robot Arm

In this part, we further provide a general Lagrangian formulation of robot arms with multiple links. Specifically, we can denote the robot arm link number to be
n
(
n\geq 2
). Similar to the previous subsection, we can denote the joint rotation angle vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
as the chosen
general coordinates
. Meanwhile, the
general forces
calculated with the
Euler Lagrange equation
will represent the
torques

\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
instead.

The
Lagrangian function
defined base don the chosen generalized coordinates can be represented as

L(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=K(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-P(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(209)

where

K(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\theta}_{i}\dot{\theta}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

(210)

Meanwhile, term
P(\boldsymbol{\theta})
denotes a vector of elements as represented similar to the
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
terms in Equation
197
. The matrix
\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
is the mass matrix with term
m_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as its
(i,j)_{th}
element, which will be discussed in detail in the following Section
5.3
instead.

According to the
Euler-Lagrange equation
, we can represent the corresponding
torque
vector as

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+g(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(211)

where
g(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the second-order derivative of the coordinate vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. As to
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n\times n}
, it is a 3-way tensor with the
(i,j,k)_{th}
element

\Gamma_{ijk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial m_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{k}}+\frac{\partial m_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{j}}-\frac{\partial m_{j,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right).

(212)

The term
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
used in the above equation will be a vector in the following form

\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\
\vdots\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(213)

where notation
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
is a
n\times n
matrix with
\Gamma_{ijk}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
as its
(j,k)_{th}
element.

5.3
Newton-Euler Dynamics Formulation

In this part, we will introduce another formulation method,
i.e.
, the
Newton-Euler method
, to model the robot dynamics. Different from the Lagrangian method, which models the dynamics from the perspective of system energy, the
Newton-Euler method
models the dynamics based on the concepts and transformation of motion.

5.3.1
Newton-Euler Formulation for Rigid Body

Figure 17:
Dynamics of Rigid Body.

In this part, we will take the single rigid body as an example to introduce the
Newton-Euler formulation
method for readers. The rigid body can be in various shapes depending on the dimension of space we are studying the problem.

Example 30
.

As shown in Figure
17
, a rigid body consists of a number of rigidly connected particles. Depending on the dimension of space we are studying the rigid body, it can be in different shapes, e.g., a line in 1D space as shown in the left plot of Figure
17
or a cashew shaped diagram in 2D space as shown in the right plot of Figure
17
.

To be general, we assume we are studying the problem within a 3D space. We can represent the set of particles in the rigid body as
\mathcal{S}=\{(m_{i},\mathbf{r}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
m_{i}
denotes the mass of the
i_{th}
particles and
\mathbf{r}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z_{i})
denotes the initial position of the particles within the local coordinate about the rigid body. Formally, the origin of the local coordinate system is the unique point such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{0}.

(214)

Such a point is also named as the center of mass about the rigid body.

Here, we assume the rigid body is moving with a body twist
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\end{bmatrix}
(the subscript
b
denotes the rigid body), then all the particles within the rigid body will also change their position with time. For instance, for the
i_{th}
particle, we can represent its position as a vector
\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)
. Within the local coordinate system, we can also calculate the
linear velocity
,
acceleration
,
external force
and
torque
about the
i_{th}
particle in the rigid body as

\displaystyle\textbf{Velocity}:\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{v}_{b}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\times\mathbf{p}_{i},

(215)

\displaystyle\textbf{Acceleration}:\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}

(216)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{v}_{b}+\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\times\mathbf{p}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\times\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{p}_{i}

(217)

\displaystyle=\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\times\mathbf{p}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\times\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}

(218)

\displaystyle=\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\times\mathbf{p}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\times(\mathbf{v}_{b}+\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\times\mathbf{p}_{i})

(219)

\displaystyle=\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i},

(220)

\displaystyle\textbf{Force}:\mathbf{f}_{i}

\displaystyle=m_{i}\ddot{\mathbf{p}}_{i}=m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right),

(221)

\displaystyle\textbf{Torque}:\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{r}_{i}\times\mathbf{f}_{i}=\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\mathbf{f}_{i}.

(222)

Furthermore, based on the force and torque representations of the
i_{th}
particle, we can further calculate the force acting on the whole rigid body as

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{f}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)

(223)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{i}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}\right)-\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{i}}\right)\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}}_{=\mathbf{0}}+\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\right)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}}_{=\mathbf{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}\right)

In the above derivation, we use many properties about the
\land
operator on vectors,
e.g.
, (1)
\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}=-\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{i}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}
, (2)
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}=(\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b})^{\top}
, and (3)
(\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b})^{\top}=-\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}
. Also we know that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
, by applying the
\land
operator to both sides of the equation, we can get that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}=\widehat{\mathbf{0}}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{0}}
is an all-zero matrix.

As to the torque of the whole rigid body, we can represent it as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{b}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}m_{i}\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)

(224)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}m_{i}\right)\left(\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}\right)}_{=\mathbf{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}m_{i}\left(\widehat{\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{b}\mathbf{r}_{i}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{2}\mathbf{r}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(-\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}-\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\left(-\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\right)\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}.

In the above derivation, we use a property about vector cross-product that
\mathbf{a}\times\left(\mathbf{b}\times(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\right)=\mathbf{b}\times\left(\mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\right)
to convert
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}
into
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}
. As to proof of equation
\mathbf{a}\times\left(\mathbf{b}\times(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\right)=\mathbf{b}\times\left(\mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\right)
, we will leave it as an exercise for the readers.

5.3.2
Rotational Inertia Matrix

In the previous subsection, we try to derive the representations of
velocity
,
acceleration
,
force
and
torque
of both particles in a rigid body, as well as the whole rigid body. For the torque of the rigid body, we can also simplify its representation derived in Equation
225
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{b}

\displaystyle=\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\right)\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}

(225)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{b}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\mathbf{I}_{b}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}.

In the above representation, we introduce a new matrix representation

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{b}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{2}

(226)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\begin{bmatrix}0&-z_{i}&y_{i}\\
z_{i}&0&-x_{i}\\
-y_{i}&x_{i}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&-z_{i}&y_{i}\\
z_{i}&0&-x_{i}\\
-y_{i}&x_{i}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(227)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}(y_{i}^{2}+z_{i}^{2})&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}x_{i}y_{i}&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}x_{i}z_{i}\\
-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}x_{i}y_{i}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}(x_{i}^{2}+z_{i}^{2})&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}y_{i}z_{i}\\
-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}x_{i}z_{i}&-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}y_{i}z_{i}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}(x_{i}^{2}+y_{i}^{2})\\
\end{bmatrix}

(228)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I_{xx}&I_{xy}&I_{xz}\\
I_{xy}&I_{yy}&I_{yz}\\
I_{zx}&I_{zy}&I_{zz}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 3},

(229)

which is also named as the rigid body’s
rotational inertia matrix
. For a given rigid-body, we know that matrix
\mathbf{I}_{b}
will be a constant and it is also symmetric and positive definite.

The
rotational inertia matrix
also provides a simple representation of the
rotational kinetic energy
of the rigid body. Formally, for all the particles in the rigid body, we can represent the
rotational kinetic energy
of the rigid body as

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{i}

(230)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}_{i})\dot{(}\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}_{i})

(231)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{i}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}_{i})

(232)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\mathbf{r}_{i}\times(-\mathbf{r}_{i}\times\boldsymbol{\omega})

(233)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{i}}(\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\omega})

(234)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\top}(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\widehat{\mathbf{r}_{i}}^{2})\boldsymbol{\omega}

(235)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}\boldsymbol{\omega}.

(236)

Via the
rotational kinetic energy
value, we can also transform the
rotational inertia matrix
across different coordinate systems. Given two coordinate systems
\Sigma_{a}
and
\Sigma_{c}
, we can denote the rigid body’s
angular velocity
vectors in this two vectors as
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}
, where
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}=\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
. Since the rigid body’s
rotational kinetic energy
in these two coordinate systems should be equal, we can have

\displaystyle K^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=K^{[\Sigma_{c}]}

(237)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{c}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}

(238)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})

(239)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\ \

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\left((\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}\right)\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(240)

Since the above equation holds for any angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
, so we can get

\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=(\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\mathbf{R}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}.

(241)

As introduced before, the subscript
b
of the
rotational inertia matrix
representations above denote it is defined for the rigid body.

In addition to transformation across coordinate systems, the
rotational inertia matrix
can also be transformed within the same coordinate system but at a different point. In the above derivation, matrix
\mathbf{I}_{b}
is defined for the center of the rigid body,
i.e.
, the center of the local coordinate. Given another point
\mathbf{q}=(q_{x},q_{y},q_{z})
within the coordinate, we can also define the
rotational inertia matrix
with point
\mathbf{q}
as the new origin to be

\mathbf{I}_{q}=\mathbf{I}_{b}+m\left(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{q}\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\right).

(242)

It is also referred to as the
parallel axis theorem
, we will leave its proof as an exercise for the readers.

5.3.3
Twist-Wrench Formulation

The representation of
force
and
torque
acting on the rigid body we derive in Equation
223
and Equation
225
can also be organized and represented together as follows:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{b}\\
\mathbf{f}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{w}_{b}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(243)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(244)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{=\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}=\mathbf{v}_{b}\times\mathbf{v}_{b}=\mathbf{0}}

(245)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\mathbf{0}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(246)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{G}_{b}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\dot{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{b}}-\underbrace{{\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}}}_{Ad(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b})}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{G}_{b}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}}.

(247)

In the above equation derivation, we use many properties,
e.g.
, (1)
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\mathbf{v}_{b}=\mathbf{v}_{b}\times\mathbf{v}_{b}=\mathbf{0}
and (2)
\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}\\
\mathbf{0}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}^{\top}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}^{\top}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}=-\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
. Several notations are also introduced for the terms used in the above equation, which can be further simplified as

\mathbf{w}_{b}=\mathbf{G}_{b}\dot{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{b}-Ad(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b},

(248)

where
\mathbf{G}_{b}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6\times 6}
is the
spatial inertia matrix
and
Ad(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b})={\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{b}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{\top}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6\times 6}
is the adjoint matrix representation of vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}
.

With the
\mathbf{G}_{b}
matrix, we can also represent the overall kinetic energy of the rigid body as

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{I}_{b}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}+\frac{1}{2}m\mathbf{v}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{b}

(249)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}&\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{b}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&m\mathbf{I}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{b}\\
\mathbf{v}_{b}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(250)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}.

(251)

Furthermore, the matrix
\mathbf{G}_{b}
define above can also be transformed across coordinate systems. Given two coordinate systems
\Sigma_{a}
and
\Sigma_{c}
, we can represent the
twist velocity
vectors of the rigid body in them as
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}
, respectively. According to the adjoint representation of the homogeneous transformation matrix introduced in Equation
43
in the previous Section
2.3.2
, we know that

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(252)

Meanwhile, since the kinetic energy of the rigid body is independent of the chosen coordinate system, we can have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}

(253)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}\left(Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\right)

(254)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]})^{\top}\left(Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}.

(255)

Since the above equation should hold for any
twist velocity
vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}\in\mathbbm{R}^{6}
, then we can get

\mathbf{G}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{a}]}=Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]})^{\top}\mathbf{G}_{b}^{[\Sigma_{c}]}Ad(\mathbf{T}^{[\Sigma_{a}\to\Sigma_{c}]}).

(256)

5.4
A Practical Case Study: Robot Joint Actuator Dynamics

An Example of Robot Arm Joint Components in the DC Motor.

Finally, at the end of this section, we will use a concrete robot arm joint motor to illustrate robot dynamics in practice. As shown in Figure
18
, we provide the key components of the robot arm joint DC motor, which include
motor driver
,
motor
,
encoder
,
motor inertia
,
motor friction
,
gearbox
,
output load inertia
and
output link
. In the following part, we will introduce these key components one by one for readers.

5.4.1
Actuator

We take the electrical DC motor as an example, whose input is the voltage
u
from the used power supply. Depending on the power supply and motor used in the robot arm joint, the voltage
u
value can be different,
e.g.
,
5V
,
24V
or
48V
. Nowadays, the DC motors we use are normally current controlled,
i.e.
, the current will control the motor pose, rotational speed and output torque. Via the
motor driver
shown in Figure
18
, we can calculate the current into the motor as

i_{m}=K_{a}(u),

(257)

where the mapping
K_{a}:V\to A
will calculate the output current corresponding to the input voltage.

Meanwhile, the output torque by the motor is proportional to the current, which can be represented as

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}=K_{m}(i_{m}),

(258)

where the mapping
K_{m}:A\to Nm
calculates the output torque for the input current.

5.4.2
Angular Velocity

The torque
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}
will drive the motor and accelerate the rotational velocity of the
motor inertia

\mathbf{I}_{m}
to generate the
angular velocity

\omega_{m}
and the motor current rotation angle
\theta
.

According to the previous Equation
225
, we have already illustrated the relationship between the motor output
torque
and its
angular velocity
, which is rewritten for readers below as well:

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}=\mathbf{I}_{m}\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{m}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{m}\mathbf{I}_{m}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{m}.

(259)

The
angular velocity

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}
and the current actuator position (
i.e.
, the rotated angle)
\theta
will be outputted to high-level controller via the encoder of the motor.

5.4.3
Friction

In the real-world, as the motor actuator rotates, it will be affected by frictions, which can be caused either by the
viscous friction
or by the
Coulomb friction
. The frictions will be related to the
angular velocity
of the motor, and will introduce an offset torque to oppose the motion.

Formally, we can represent the torque created by the
viscous friction
can be represented as

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{v}=B(\boldsymbol{\omega}).

(260)

Here, mapping
B:rad/s\to Nm
is also called the
viscous friction
mapping, which is a linear function of the input with coefficient
b
.

Meanwhile, the torque caused due to the
Coulomb friction
depends on the rotation direction and can be represented as

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}^{+}&\text{, if }\boldsymbol{\omega}>0\\
\mathbf{0}&\text{, if }\boldsymbol{\omega}=0\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}^{-}&\text{, if }\boldsymbol{\omega}<0\\
\end{cases},

(261)

where
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}^{-}
are two constants.

In sum, we can represent the sum of torque caused by frictions as

\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{v}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}.

(262)

By taking the frictions into consideration, we can represent the output torque by the
actuator
as

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{f}.

(263)

5.4.4
Gearbox

For the motors used in practice, there usually exist a
gearbox
attached to the motor as the
reducer
to lower down the rotational velocity. The
reducer
is a gear train between the motor and the machinery that is used to reduce the speed with which power is transmitted. It is normally mechanical gadget and its essential use is to duplicate the measure of torque produced by an information power source to expand the measure of usable work.

As shown in Figure
18
, we assume the
gearbox
reduction ratio to be
G:1
,
i.e.
, the motor shaft rotates
G
rounds, the output shaft will rotate
1
round. Due to the existence of the
gearbox
, the quantities measured about the motor at the internal motor side and at the output side will be different.

Let
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{l}
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{l}
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{l}
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c,l}
,
b_{l}
and
\mathbf{I}_{l}
denote the
angular velocity
,
angular acceleration
,
torque
,
torque due to the Coulomb friction
,
viscous friction torque coefficient
and
inertia matrix
measured at the output load side, respectively. With consideration about the reduction ratio, those quantities measures at the motor side will be very different, which can be denoted as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}_{l}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{l}/G\text{, }\ \ \ \ \dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\prime}_{l}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{l}/G,

(264)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}_{l}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{l}G\text{, }\ \ \ \ \boldsymbol{\tau}^{\prime}_{c,l}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c,l}G,

(265)

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{l}

\displaystyle=b_{l}G^{2}\text{, }\ \ \ \ \mathbf{I}^{\prime}_{l}=\mathbf{I}_{l}G^{2}.

(266)

5.4.5
Motor Torque Balance

Dynamics of Two-Link Robot Arm.

Meanwhile, as to the output link, we have discussed about the dynamics of 2-link robot arm in the previous Section
5.2.2
already. Furthermore, according to the previous Equation
204
we derive before, we can represent the torque at the end point of the 2-link robot arm as

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ddot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+{\mathbf{c}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\mathbf{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})},

(267)

Based on the above descriptions, we can summarize all the torques we derive above and get the torque balance on the motor shaft as follows:

K_{m}(K_{a}(u))-(B(\boldsymbol{\omega};b_{m})+B(\boldsymbol{\omega};b_{l}/G^{2}))-(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{c,l}/G)-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{d}/G=(\mathbf{I}_{m}+\mathbf{I}_{l}/G^{2})\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}.

(268)

The dynamics of the motor can be analyzed based on the above torque balance equation. According to the dynamics analysis result, we can further control the robot joint motor to perform the necessary movement according to our task requirements.
