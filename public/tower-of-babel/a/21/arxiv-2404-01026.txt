Modelling Multiplicative Linear Logic via Deep Inference
Tomer Galor, Andrea Schalk

Abstract
Multiplicative linear logic is a very well studied formal system, and most such studies are concerned with the one-sided sequent calculus. In this paper we look in detail at existing translations between a deep inference system and the standard sequent calculus one, provide a simplified translation, and provide a formal proof that a standard approach to modelling is indeed invariant to all these translations. En route we establish a necessary condition for provable sequents related to the number of pars and tensors in a formula that seems to be missing from the literature.



1
Introduction

Linear logic is a constructive logic created by Girard
[
Gir87
]

that has gained increased interest since its inception. It has shown
potential applications from theorem provers and optimising and
compiling concurrent programming languages as a result of the way in
which linear logic is able to encode state transitions
[
Gir87
,
Wad93
]
, to providing an embedding for classical logic and a
categorical interpretation for proof equivalences. Our main focus,
being a body of work that arose from a master’s dissertation

[
Gal18
]
, is aiming to shed a different light on the area by
using
deep inference
systems
[
Gug15
]
as an alternative
mode of description. One of our motivations was to explore to what
extent the different system affects the formulation of the properties
required of a model, and we use Girard’s
coherence
spaces

[
Gir87
,
Dav92
]
to make the comparison.

Deep inference systems for multiplicative linear logic share a close
relationship to the work done by Retoré on pomset logic
[
Ret21
]

which influenced the work on deep inference
[
Gug07
,
Ret21
]
.
Indeed, Retoré gave an account of coherence semantics applied to
the deep pomset system
[
Ret21
]
and showed equivalence to the
SBV system given by Guglielmi and Strasburger
[
GS01
]
.
Our work is quite different from this body of work however because our
primary focus is to explore how coherence and categorical semantics hold
invariance by transformation procedures between the sequent calculus and
deep inference systems.

The Equivalence of Proofs is a subcategory of Proof Theory that asks
the question of how one is able to determine if two derivations are
equivalent, or semantically “equal”. Multiplicative linear logic,
\textbf{MLL}^{-}
,
(unlike more powerful systems) has a well-established answer to this
question: At least for the sequent calculus formulation we have a
notion of
proof net

[
Str06
,
DR89
]
,
which identifies two derivations provided they only differ trivially
(at least in some sense) regarding the order in which the inference
rules have been applied.

A proof net boils down a derivation to the following ingredients:

•

The syntactic structure of the formula or sequent at issue; and

•

a bijection between positive and negative occurrences of
atoms which preserves names which captures which pairs were
created by instances of the only axiom of the system, known as
the
axiom links
.

Based on these ideas Strasburger
[
Str06
]
argues that
one may readily transfer this notion to deep inference systems, and
that since the translation process does not make any changes to the
pairs of atoms created together, the notion of proof net stays the
same. However, the translation process is not straightforward,
introduces a large number of cuts and increases the size of an
interpretation significantly, and for that reason we wanted to
develop a formal argument that a standard way of modelling
derivations in coherence spaces is indeed not affected by the
translation process. We additionally provide an alternative to
Strasburger’s translation which keeps the size of derivations
smaller by making more assiduous choices regarding when to employ the
cut rule.

In our studies of
\textbf{MLL}^{-}
in a deep inference formulation we noted
that there is what seems to be an unpublished necessary condition for
a formula to be provable, and while one may readily establish the
result for the sequent calculus version, it is rather more obvious in
the former. We do not expect the result to be a surprise to those
familiar with the proof theory of
\textbf{MLL}^{-}
.

There is a significant body of literature (for example

[
See89
,
BBHdP92
,
Hag00
,
Mel09
]
) concerned with what is
required for a category to provide a model of linear logic. We should
note here that our aim was not to use
dinatural
transformations

[
Blu93
,
BS96
,
Tan97
,
SS15
]

to model derivations, but instead to pick morphisms dependent on a
valuation for atoms. To obtain a case study it is sufficient to look at one
such model, for which we chose Girard’s coherence
space
[
Gir87
]
—the reader interested in generalisations is
invited to look at our description of the deep inference model which
readily suggests a generalisation to categories with suitable
infrastructure.

We find it more convenient to work with an alternative axiomatization
of coherence spaces, and we introduce the isomorphic category of

concordance space
below.

We were surprised to be unable to track down a published description
of how to model a derivation using coherence spaces;
Girard’s description
[
Gir87
]
merely defines the interpretation
of various connectives on objects. The only source we were able to
find is
[
Dav92
]
, which has some shortcomings discussed below.

Hughes observes
[
Hug04
]
that deep inference systems are
much closer to a categorical setting, and indeed one may think of
such systems as describing various free categories. Indeed, modelling
a sequent calculus system requires significant overhead to deal with
the infrastructure required, and in our case study we find it rather
easier to describe that model. Most studies of sequent calculus
systems of linear logic remove the cut rule, and since there is a
cut elimination result
[
Pfe94
]
this can be justified.
However, in order to translate from a deep inference system to a
sequent calculus one the cut rule has to be present. From a
categorical point of view the cut rule is ultimately concerned with
composition, but due to the overheads required to deal with the
infrastructure this is not very clear when looking at the categorical
interpretation of a sequent calculus derivation. In contrast the way
deep inference derivations are jointed together
provides
a
composition (up to some mild quotienting), and so it should come as
no surprise that it gives us a syntactic system that is much closer to
the categorical model.

We hope that this paper is helpful to newcomers to linear logic by
aiming to be largely self-contained while also encouraging others to
explore this space further. For this reason we have kept the use of
abstract category theory to a minimum, and have decided instead to
provide a fairly concrete description of our model. One may think of
this as a study into interpretations of proofs without requiring the
need to talk about proof nets. The literature on deep inference
systems for linear logic is scarce, and we hope that this paper
encourages others to further study these.

We aim to give a summary of our starting point in
Section

2

which includes a description of the systems under consideration,
before establishing some results regarding their properties in

Section

3
. We then turn to the question of modelling
derivations and prove that this model is preserved by translation
between the two systems.

2.1
Derivation systems under consideration

Linear logic
[
Gir87
]
is a very well studied formal system, and
much attention has been given to its multiplicative fragment without
units. The standard presentation of linear logic is as a single-sided
sequent calculus from which the cut rule has been eliminated. This
simplifies some considerations but it also obscures other matters. In
some sense the sequent calculus formulation is merely an example of
Gentzen style systems
[

one might describe here, and it
is chosen here because it is very familiar in the context of linear
logic. The following system is standard for Multiplicative Linear

This system enjoys a cut elimination property and so the system most
widely used in the literature is the above without the cut rule.
Since we wish to compare this inference system with another which
follows quite a different philosophy, we will make a few comments on
the choices made here which are usually taken for granted.

While there are introduction rules for the two binary connectives,

\mathbin{\otimes}
and
\upand
, there are no such rules for negation, and the
only way of obtaining a negated formula is the use of the axiom rule.
In order to treat negation as a proper connective in its own right a
two-sided system is needed, and then applying negation moves the
formula in question onto the other side of the turnstile.

As a consequence we view negation as a derived rule. We have positive
and negative occurrences for each atom, and we use the following

DeMorgan rules
to define negation recursively for more complex
formulae.

\overline{A\mathbin{\otimes}B}=\overline{A}\upand\overline{B}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{A\upand B}=\overline{A}\mathbin{\otimes}\overline{B}.

Note that in this system a formula is equal to its double negation.
This is the standard treatment for such systems. One could, in
principle, decide that one should allow negation to appear anywhere
within a well-formed formula. In that situation one needs to have a
notion of
DeMorgan equivalence
, which is generated by the
above DeMorgan rules. Then one either needs to allow the cut rule to
be applied to pairs of formulae which are negations of each other up
to DeMorgan equivalence, or one needs a ‘DeMorgan derivation rule’
which allows the replacement of a formula in the sequent by a
DeMorgan equivalent formula. In either of these systems a formula is
equivalent to its double negation.

Our aim is to contrast
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{SC}
with a very different style of
formal derivation setting, known as
deep inference

[
Gug15
]
, or
the calculus of structures
.
In the former, formulae are derived step-by-step, and
connectives have to be created in the order in which they appear in
the parse tree. In the latter, one may apply an instance of a rule

at arbitrary depth inside a formula
. Whereas, viewed from
bottom to top, derivations in a sequent calculus spread out in a
tree-like manner, deep inference derivations are linear. There is a
natural connection between deep inference systems and categorical
logic as observed by Hughes
[
Hug04
]
. Indeed, one may
think of deep inference as providing the syntax required for
describing a free category with some structure (corresponding to the
logical connectives), but describing the identifications required is
non-trivial once one has two connectives that interact with each
other. In this publication we concentrate on looking at more concrete
interpretations. Most deep inference systems studied in the
literature are concerned with classical logic, and not much has been
written about such systems that describe (fragments of) linear logic.
In
[
Str02
]
the author gives a system for full
linear logic, while in
[
Str06
]
we find a system
for
\textbf{MLL}^{-}
, and that is the system we use.

We assume that readers will be familiar with derivations in a sequent
calculus, but not necessarily with deep inference systems. A number
of issues are worth commenting on:

•

match well with the usual treatment of formulae in linear logic,
and indeed the system given in
[

does not adopt
this convention either.

Once again we adopt a notion of

which allows negation on atoms only. Again this is the approach
chosen in

2.2
Models of linear logic

Models of linear logic are well studied, and even in the original
publications, such as
[
Gir87
]
, two models were
proposed. One based on
phase spaces
where the interpretation
takes place inside the powerset of some set, with the usual partial
order, and one based on
coherence spaces
, sets equipped with a
binary ‘coherence relation’. The latter is more interesting as a
categorical model since it is non-degenerate as a category.

In the background research for the dissertation that the present work
is founded on we were surprised to discover that it
is quite difficult, however, to find an account of how exactly
derivations are interpreted in this latter model. We were unable to find
an account in a journal, and the only record that provides
significant detail that we were able to track down is the
hand-written
[
Dav92
]
. This account does cover full classical
logic, and spends significant time on stable and linear functions,
which is not relevant to our investigation. The interpretation of
derivations as cliques is given informally which is sufficient to
look at results of interest to that account, but not for the purposes
of comparing models of different derivation systems. For this reason
we give a detailed account of this model in

Section

5.3
.

Coherence spaces

We give a brief overview of coherence spaces as they appear
in
[
Gir87
]
, indicating how to model the connectives, but we
add here the generally accepted notion of morphism to obtain a
category.

A
coherence space
is given by a set together with a
symmetric reflexive binary relation, written as
{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}}
, the

coherence relation
.

The dual
\overline{(R,{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}})}
of a coherence space
(R,{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}})
is
given by the set
R
together with the reflexive closure of the
complement of
{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}}
, and the result is typically written as

{\overset{\lower 2.71246pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}}
. We use ‘incoherent’ (with respect to the original space)
when referring to this relation, but note that incoherent might mean
equal.

A
morphism of coherence spaces
from some
(R,{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}})
to
some
(S,{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}})
is given by a relation
F
from
R
to
S
such
that if preserves the coherence relation going forward, and going in
the opposite direction it preserves incoherence:

•

If
r
and
r^{\prime}
are coherent and
r\mathrel{F}s
as well as

r^{\prime}\mathrel{F}s^{\prime}
then
s
and
s^{\prime}
must be coherent; and

•

If
s
and
s^{\prime}
are incoherent, and if
r\mathrel{F}s
as well
as
r^{\prime}\mathrel{F}s^{\prime}
then
r
and
r^{\prime}
must be incoherent.

The various connectives of multiplicative linear logic have
ready-made interpretations, and we reuse the corresponding symbols.
The tensor product of two coherence spaces
(R,{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}})
and

(S,{\overset{\lower 2.41112pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\frown$}}{\lower 0.86108pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\smile$}}})
has the Cartesian product
R\times S
as its
underlying set and two pairs are coherent if they are coherent
component-wise. The par of two coherence spaces is given via the
DeMorgan rules from above: one forms the dual of the two spaces,
forms their tensor product, and takes the dual of the result. As a
consequence coherence spaces satisfy the DeMorgan rules from above as
equalities, and the double dual of a coherence space is the space
itself.

The category
\mathord{\mathsf{Coh}}
of coherence space is
*
-autonomous, with
unit
I
consisting of the set
\{*\}
with the only possible
coherence relation serving as a unit for tensor.

A
clique
in a coherence space is given by a subset of the
underlying set such that any two elements of this subset are
coherent. An
anticlique
is a clique for the dual space.
This corresponds to a clique in a graph and indeed one can construct a reflexive
graph with elements of a set as vertices and the coherence relation as a set of
edges.
In
Section

5.3
we describe how to use cliques to model
derivations in the sequent calculus system.
Note that we consider the empty set
both a clique and an anticlique.

In
Section

5.1
we provide an alternative description of coherence
spaces.

Identity of proofs

It is generally accepted that two derivations in
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{SC}
starting
with no assumptions should be considered equivalent if they derive
the same sequent, and if they create the ‘same’ pairs of atoms via the
axioms, where we have to take care to refer to instances of atoms in
the final sequent. This relation may be characterised syntactically
in the sense that one derivation may be transformed into any
equivalent one by applying a sequence of
commuting conversions

which allow certain instances of derivation rules to be moved past
each other.

We can see some of these ideas at work in the proofs below that show our
interpretations of sequent calculus and deep inference derivations are the same
under various transformations.

2.3
Notation

In what follows, most of the notation we use should be
self-explanatory or following standard conventions. For example, we
employ lower case letters for atoms, upper case for arbitrary
formulae and capital Greek letters for sequents. Note that we use a
line over a formula to indicate its negation, and that our logical
systems do not treat negation as a logical connective in its own
right as explained above.

We use the name of an object to also denote its identity morphism in
some category.

3
Properties of
\textbf{MLL}^{-}

We start our exploration of the two systems introduced above, by
first of all establishing a necessary property for derivable sequents
or formulae. This seems to have been overlooked in the literature,
although we do not expect it to be a surprise to anybody familiar with

\textbf{MLL}^{-}
. Admittedly, it is a property much easier to spot in the
deep inference system since the commas between sequents act as a
distraction.

3.1
A necessary condition for derivable formulae/sequents

When manipulating formulae in either system one frequently writes
expressions which have to be rewritten according to the DeMorgan
rules to become well-formed formulae. The result we wish to establish
is a necessary condition for derivable formulae or sequents. It works
even for expressions which are not themselves well-formed formulae
and so we establish it at this level of generality.

For the purposes of this section we consider all formulae that may be
created using
\mathbin{\otimes}
,
\upand
and negation, and we consider two
such formula equivalent if one can be rewritten into the other using
the DeMorgan laws.

A formula whose only negated parts are atoms (that is a well-formed
formula in our system) is considered the
normal form
of such
an expression.

Definition 1
.

We say that a connective in an
\textbf{MLL}^{-}
formula
occurs
positively
if the number of negation overlines above it is
even; else we say the connective
occurs negatively
.

We have reason to count the positive and negative occurrences of the
two connectives in a formula. Given a formula
A
we use

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A
for the number of positive occurrences of
\mathbin{\otimes}

in
A
, and we extend that notation in the expected way.

Proposition 3.1
.

If two formulae
A
and
B
are equivalent then we have the
following:

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}B+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}B

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}B+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}B.

We can see that applying the DeMorgan rule on two formulae
connected by a tensor means we decrease
\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}
by one and increase

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}
by one while the other two numbers stay the same. Similar
rules apply to using this rule on the negation of two formulae connected by a
par, or using the equivalent rules with pars and tensors swapped.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Given a formula
A
and its negation
\overline{A}
we have that

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\overline{A}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}\overline{A}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\overline{A}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}\overline{A},

and so

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\overline{A}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}\overline{A}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}A

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\overline{A}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}\overline{A}.

Forming the negation of
A
adds one line over every connective in

A
, so positive ones become negative and vice versa.
∎

Surprisingly, we were unable to find
Theorems

3.3
and
3.5
in the
literature.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
A
is a formula derivable in
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{DI}
then

(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}A)-(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}A)=1.

The proof proceeds by induction. Clearly the result holds for a
formula of the form
\overline{A}\upand A
. It is trivially preserved
by the symmetry, associativity and switch rules and also by
DeMorgan equivalence, see
Proposition

3.1
. That leaves the two
non-axiomatic rules
i
. Considering i
\downarrow
, assume that

S\{B\}
satisfies the criterion. Then in

S\{B\mathbin{\otimes}(\overline{A}\upand A)\}
the various counts of
occurrences of connectives in
A
balance out those in
\overline{A}
.
There are two new connectives,
\mathbin{\otimes}
and
\upand
, and they
both occur either positively or negatively, so when counting we add
one each to the number of positive
\mathbin{\otimes}
s and positive

\upand
s, or do the same for the number of negative
\mathbin{\otimes}
s and

\upand
s. This maintains the property. For i
\uparrow
similar
considerations apply, only here the counts are variously reduced.
∎

Note in particular that when we have a formula
A
in normal form
then

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}A=0,

so we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

If a formula
A
derivable in
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{DI}
is in normal form then
its number of occurrence of
\upand
is one greater than the number
of occurrences of
\mathbin{\otimes}
.

We extend our notion of number of negative or positive occurrences of
the connectives to sequents in the obvious way by writing, for
example,
\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\Gamma
for the number of positive
occurrences of
\mathbin{\otimes}
in
\Gamma
, that is, if

\Gamma\text{ is }A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}

then

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}A_{i}.

We also need to count the number of commas that occur in
\Gamma
,
using
\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}\Gamma
. Alternatively
we could use the rule
\upand
to
turn
\Gamma
into a formula by replacing all the commas
by
\upand
s.

This allows us to extend our result for the number of occurrences of
the connectives to the sequent calculus formulation of
\textbf{MLL}^{-}
.

Theorem 3.5
.

If
\Gamma
is a sequent derivable in
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{SC}
then

(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}\Gamma)-(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}\Gamma)=1.

We could use the translation process from
Proposition

4.2
but we
prefer instead to reason about the sequent calculus
system
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{SC}
. This is another proof by induction. Clearly
the statement holds for the only axiom. The property is trivially
maintained by the exchange and the
\upand
rules. Considering the

\mathbin{\otimes}
rules we can see that

\displaystyle{\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(\Gamma,A\mathbin{\otimes}B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}(\Gamma,A\mathbin{\otimes}B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}(\Gamma,A\mathbin{\otimes}B,\Delta)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}(B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}(B,\Delta)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}(\Gamma,A))+(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}(B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}(B,\Delta))

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}(\Gamma,A)+1)+(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}(B,\Delta)+1)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(B,\Delta)+1)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}(B,\Delta)+1

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}(\Gamma,A\mathbin{\otimes}B,\Delta)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}(\Gamma,A\mathbin{\otimes}B,\Delta)+1.

It remains to look at the cut rule. We use the following derived
counters:

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\upand}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{-}_{\mathbin{\otimes}}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits^{+}_{\upand}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{\mbox{\tiny{co}}}.

We need to show that

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\Gamma,\Delta)-\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\Gamma,\Delta)=1

knowing that

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\Gamma,A)-\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\Gamma,A)=1

and

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\overline{A},\Delta)-\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\overline{A},\Delta)=1,

as well as, by
Proposition

3.2
,

\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}A=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\overline{A}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}A=\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\overline{A}.

\displaystyle{\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\Gamma,\Delta)-\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\Gamma,\Delta)}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}\Delta+1)-(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\Delta)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}\overline{A}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}\Delta+1)-(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\Gamma+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}A+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\overline{A}+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}\Delta)

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\Gamma,A)-1+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\overline{A},\Delta)-1+1)-(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\Gamma,A)+\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\overline{A},\Gamma))

\displaystyle=(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\Gamma,A)-\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\Gamma,A))+(\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{p}(\overline{A},\Delta)-\operatorname*{n}\nolimits_{t}(\overline{A},\Delta))-1

\displaystyle=1+1-1

\displaystyle=1.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

If
\Gamma
is a sequent derivable in
\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{SC}
consisting
entirely of formulae in normal form then the number of
\upand
s
added to the number of commas is one greater than the number of

\mathbin{\otimes}
s occurring in
\Gamma
.

Arguably, we owe a justification for the claim that the two systems
introduced in
Section

2.1

by providing processes of translations of derivations going in both
directions. The reader might want to know from the start that the two
translations do not undo each other’s effect, no matter whether we
start in the deep inference or in the sequent calculus system.
Indeed, if we are not careful about how we define these translations
then the size of the derivation increases significantly each time we
apply one of them.

5
Modelling derivations

We describe how to model derivations as cliques in some coherence
space. We begin by looking at an alternative description of coherence
spaces before giving details for modelling both systems. We conclude
by showing that our interpretations are preserved by the translation
processes defined in the previous section.

5.1

In

If we have a morphism of coherence spaces it is easy to check that it
will map cliques of the source to cliques of the target, and
anticliques of the target to anticliques of the source, and so be a
morphism of the corresponding concordance spaces.

If, on the other hand, we have a morphism of concordance spaces then
it has to map two-element cliques of the source to cliques of the
target, which means it preserves the corresponding coherence
relation. It also has to map two-element anticliques of the target to
anticliques of the source, which means it preserves the incoherence
relation of the corresponding coherence spaces. Hence we have
the following result.

6
Conclusions

In this paper we consider two formal systems describing
multiplicative linear logic. We have provided a new translation from
the deep inference system to the sequent calculus one, and we have
illustrated that such translations cannot be expected to be mutually
inverse due to the use of the cut rule. Our translation reduces the
number of cuts introduced and it produces smaller derivations such that
the number of axiomatic and cut rules are equal.

We formulate a necessary property for sequents or formulae to be
derivable.

We finally describe for both systems how to interpret derivations as
cliques in coherence (or concordance) spaces, based on a valuation of
atoms. These interpretations are invariant under the translations.
We may think of this as being summarised informally by the following
diagram. The triangles we may form do commute, but as argued above,
the translation processes are not mutually inverse. We have shown that
this model of linear logic is a representation of proofs that maintains
equality under the bureaucracy of both sequent calculus and deep inference.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{SC}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textbf{MLL}^{-}_{DI}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathord{\mathsf{Con}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathop{T^{{SC}}_{{SC}\setminus\{\text{{cut}{}}\}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathop{T^{{SC}}_{{DI}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathop{T^{\prime{DI}}_{{SC}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathop{T^{{DI}}_{{SC}}}}

There are of course other models of linear logic, such as those
that use a more complete or full system of linear logic, or those that make use of alternative models
that are quite different to ours,
such as handsome proof nets (based on cographs) that uses disjoint unions instead of cross products
[
Ret03
]
.
Some questions that are not covered are if this model maintains equality of all
cliques of the same formula under a full system of linear logic, or if other models
do the same under transformation procedures. We leave these as future research questions.
