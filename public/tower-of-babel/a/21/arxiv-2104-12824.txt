3.

Here we prove Theorem

Moreover, we use the isometric isomorphism between

and

\displaystyle H^{\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})=\Bigl\{z(t)=\sum_{k}\hat{z}_{k}e_{k}(t)\text{ s.t. }\hat{z}\in{h^{\nu}}\Bigr\}

{H^{1+\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}\to{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}+\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}
for

and the following embedding:
{C^{0,\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}\to{H^{\tilde{\nu}}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}
for

, cf. Lemma

in the Appendix. The latter embedding means that

provided

and

.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume (
C0
), (
C1
), (
C2
) and in addition
\Phi^{\prime}_{k}(0)=O(k)
. For every
\hat{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
with

we have
\hat{\alpha}\in h^{\nu}
for every
\nu\in(0,1/4)
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
with

. Recall from (

(\hat{\alpha}*\hat{\alpha}*\hat{\alpha})_{k}=\hat{\eta}_{k}\hat{\alpha}_{k}\quad\mbox{ where }\quad\hat{\eta}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{2T\Phi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}{\gamma\omega^{4}k^{2}}\mbox{ for }k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}

so that

.
If we define the convolution of two

-periodic functions

on the torus

as

then the equation

for the

-periodic function

is equivalent to the equation (

) for the sequence

Step 1:

We show that

-function since

Step 2:

We fix

and show that if

with

of the Appendix,

and hence

with

\displaystyle\norm{\alpha*\eta}_{H^{1+q\nu_{n}}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}^{2}=\norm{\hat{\alpha}\hat{\eta}}_{h^{1+q\nu_{n}}}^{2}\leq\sum_{k}(1+k^{2})^{1+q\nu_{n}}\hat{\alpha}_{k}^{2}\frac{C^{2}}{k^{2}}\leq C^{2}\|\hat{\alpha}\|_{h^{q\nu_{n}}}<\infty.

Hence by (

Starting with

∎

With this preparation the proof of Theorem

Let

4.
Existence of Infinitely Many Breathers

In this section we extend Theorem
1.5
by the following multiplicity result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume (
C0
), (
C1
) and (
C2
). Then (
1
) has infinitely many nontrivial,
T
-periodic weak solution
w
in the sense of Definition
1.3
with
T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}
provided

(i)

\gamma<0
and there exists an integer

such that for infinitely many
j\in\mathbb{N}
the sequence
\Bigl(\Phi^{\prime}_{m\cdot l_{-}^{j}}(0)\Bigr)_{m\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}}

(ii)

\gamma>0
and there exists an integer

such that for infinitely many
j\in\mathbb{N}
the sequence
\Bigl(\Phi^{\prime}_{m\cdot l_{+}^{j}}(0)\Bigr)_{m\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}}
has at least one negative element.

Remark 4.2
.

In the above Theorem, conditions (
C1
) and (
C2
) can be weakened: instead of requiring them for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
it suffices to require them for
k\in l_{-}^{j}{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
,
k\in l_{+}^{j}{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
respectively. We prove this observation together with the one in Remark
1.6
at the end of this section.

We start with an investigation about the types of symmetries which are compatible with our equation. The Euler-Lagrange equation (
9
) for critical points
\hat{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
of
J
takes the form
(\hat{\alpha}*\hat{\alpha}*\hat{\alpha})_{k}=\hat{\eta}_{k}\hat{\alpha}_{k}
with
\hat{\eta}_{k}\coloneqq\frac{2T\Phi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}{\gamma\omega^{4}k^{2}}
for
k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}
. Next we describe subspaces of
{\mathcal{D}(J)}
which are invariant under triple convolution and pointwise multiplication with
(\hat{\eta}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}}
. It turns out that these subspaces are made of sequences
\hat{z}
where only the
r^{th}
entry modulus
2r
is occupied.

Definition 4.3
.

For
r\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}},p\in{\mathbb{N}_{even}}
with
r<p
let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}(J)}_{r,p}=\{\hat{z}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}:\forall\,k\in\mathbb{Z},k\neq r\penalty\ \mathrm{mod}\penalty\ p\colon\hat{z}_{k}=0\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

For
r\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}},p\in{\mathbb{N}_{even}}
with
r<p
and
p\not=2r
we have
{\mathcal{D}(J)}_{r,p}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{z}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}_{r,p}
. For all
k\not\in r+p\mathbb{Z}
we have
\hat{z}_{k}=0
by definition of
{\mathcal{D}(J)}_{r,p}
. Let therefore
k=r+pl_{1}
for some
l_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
-k=-r-pl_{1}\not\in r+p\mathbb{Z}
because otherwise
2r=-p(l_{1}+l_{2})=p|l_{1}+l_{2}|
for some

. Since by assumption

we get
|l_{1}+l_{2}|<2
. But clearly
|l_{1}+l_{2}|\not\in\{0,1\}
since
r\not=0
and
p\not=2r
by assumption. By this contradiction we have shown
-k\not\in r+p\mathbb{Z}
so that necessarily
0=\hat{z}_{-k}=-\hat{z}_{k}
. This shows
\hat{z}=0
.
∎

In the following we continue by only considering
\mathcal{D}_{r}\coloneqq{\mathcal{D}(J)}_{r,2r}
for
r\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
.

(i)

The elements
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
are exactly those elements of
{\mathcal{D}(J)}
which generate
\frac{T}{2r}
-antiperiodic functions
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}}\frac{\hat{z}_{k}}{k}\Phi_{k}(x)e_{k}(t)
.

(ii)

If
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
then
(\hat{z}*\hat{z}*\hat{z})_{k}=0
for all
k\not\in r+2r\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

(i) An element
\hat{z}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
generates a
\frac{T}{2r}
-antiperiodic function
z(x,t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}}\frac{\hat{z}_{k}}{k}\Phi_{k}(x)e_{k}(t)
if and only if
z(x,t+\frac{T}{2r})=-z(x,t)
. Comparing the Fourier coefficients we see that this is the case if for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}
we have
\hat{z}_{k}\bigl(\exp(\frac{\i\omega kT}{2r})+1\bigr)=0
, i.e., either
k\in r+2r\mathbb{Z}
or
\hat{z}_{k}=0
. This is exactly the condition that
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
.

(ii) Let
\hat{z}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
and assume that there is
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
0\not=(\hat{z}*\hat{z}*\hat{z})_{k}=\sum_{l,m}\hat{z}_{l}\hat{z}_{m-l}\hat{z}_{k-m}
. So there is
l_{0},m_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}}
such that
\hat{z}_{l_{0}},\hat{z}_{m_{0}-l_{0}},\hat{z}_{k-m_{0}}\not=0
which means by the definition of
\mathcal{D}_{r}
that
l_{0},m_{0}-l_{0},k-m_{0}\in r+2r\mathbb{Z}

k=l_{0}+m_{0}-l_{0}+k-m_{0}\in 3r+2r\mathbb{Z}=r+2r\mathbb{Z}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We give the proof in case (i); for case (ii) the proof only needs a trivial modification. Let
r=l^{j}
where

is an index such that the sequence
\Bigl(\Phi^{\prime}_{k\cdot l^{j}}(0)\Bigr)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}}
has a positive element (we have changed the notation from

5.
Approximation by Finitely Many Harmonics

Here we give some analytical results on finite dimensional approximation of the breathers obtained in Theorem
1.5
. The finite dimensional approximation is obtained by cutting-off the ansatz (
8
) and only considering harmonics of order
|k|\leq N
. Here a summand in the series (
8
) of the form
\Phi_{k}(|x|)e_{k}(t)
is a called a harmonic since it satisfies the linear wave equation in (
6
). We will prove that
J
restricted to spaces
{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}
of cut-off ansatz functions still attains its minimum and that the sequence of the corresponding minimizers converges up to a subsequence to a minimizer of
J
on
{\mathcal{D}(J)}
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
N\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
. Define

\displaystyle J^{(N)}\coloneqq J|_{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})},\qquad{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}\coloneqq\left\{\hat{z}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ \forall\,\absolutevalue{k}>N\colon\hat{z}_{k}=0\right\}

Lemma 5.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
the following holds:

(i)

For every
N\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
sufficiently large there exists
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\in{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}
such that
J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})=\inf J^{(N)}<0
and
\lim_{N\to\infty}J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})=\inf J
.

(ii)

There is
\hat{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
such that up to a subsequence (again denoted by
(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})_{N}
) we have

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\to\hat{\alpha}\qquad\text{ in }\penalty\ {\mathcal{D}(J)}

and
J(\hat{\alpha})=\inf J
.

Remark 5.3
.

The Euler-Lagrange-equation for
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}
reads:

\displaystyle 0=J^{\prime}\left(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\right)[\hat{y}]=\left(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}*\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}*\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}*\hat{y}\right)_{0}+\frac{2T}{\gamma\omega^{4}}\sum_{k}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}{k^{2}}\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}_{k}\hat{y}_{k}\qquad\forall\,\hat{y}\in{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}.

This amounts to satisfying (
2
) in Definition
1.3
for functions
\psi(x,t)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}_{odd}},|k|\leq N}\hat{\psi}_{k}(x)e_{k}(t)
with
\hat{\psi}_{k}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Clearly, in general
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}
is not a critical point of

.

Proof.

(i) We choose
N\in{\mathbb{N}_{odd}}
so large, such that we have the assumed sign of the the one element in
\left(\Phi_{k}^{\prime}(0)\right)_{\absolutevalue{k}\leq N}
. The restriction of
J
to the
\frac{N+1}{2}
-dimensional space
{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}
preserves coercivity. The continuity of
J^{(N)}
therefore guarantees the existence of a minimizer
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\in{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}
. As before we see that
J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})=\inf J^{(N)}<0
, so in particular
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\neq 0
. Next we observe that
{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}\subset{\mathcal{D}(J)}
, i.e.,
J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})\geq\inf J=J(\hat{\beta})
for a minimizer
\hat{\beta}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
of
J
. Let us define
\hat{\beta}^{(N)}_{k}=\hat{\beta}_{k}
for
\absolutevalue{k}\leq N
and
\hat{\beta}^{(N)}_{k}=0
. Since the Fourier-series
\beta(t)=\sum_{k}\hat{\beta}_{k}e_{k}(t)
converges in

, cf. Theorem 4.1.8 in
[

, we see that
\hat{\beta}^{(N)}\rightarrow\hat{\beta}
in
{\mathcal{D}(J)}
. By the minimality of
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\in{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}
and continuity of

we conclude

\displaystyle\inf_{{\mathcal{D}(J)}}J\leq J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})\leq J(\hat{\beta}^{(N)})\longrightarrow J(\hat{\beta})=\inf_{{\mathcal{D}(J)}}J.

Hence
\lim_{N\to\infty}J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})=\inf J
as claimed.

(ii) Since
{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N)})}\subset{\mathcal{D}(J^{(N+1)})}\subset{\mathcal{D}(J)}
we see that
J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})\geq J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N+1)})\geq\inf J
so that in particular the sequence
(J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}))_{N}
is bounded. By coercivity of
J
we conclude that
(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})_{N}
is bounded in
{\mathcal{D}(J)}
so that there is
\hat{\alpha}\in{\mathcal{D}(J)}
and a subsequence (again denoted by
(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})_{N}
) such that

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\rightharpoonup\hat{\alpha}\qquad\text{ in }\penalty\ {\mathcal{D}(J)}.

By part (i) and weak lower semi-continuity of

we obtain

\displaystyle\inf J=\lim_{N\to\infty}J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})\geq J(\hat{\alpha}),

i.e.,
\hat{\alpha}
is a minimizer of
J
. Recall that
J(\cdot)=\frac{1}{4}{\interleave\cdot\interleave}^{4}+J_{1}(\cdot)
where

is weakly continuous, cf. proof of Theorem
2.6
. Therefore, since
\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\rightharpoonup\hat{\alpha}
and
J(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})\to J(\hat{\alpha})
we see that
{\interleave\hat{\alpha}^{(N)}\interleave}\to{\interleave\hat{\alpha}\interleave}
as

. Since
{\mathcal{D}(J)}
is strictly uniformly convex, we obtain the norm-convergence of
(\hat{\alpha}^{(N)})_{N}
to
\hat{\alpha}
.
∎

6.2.
Details on Bloch Modes for periodic step potentials

Here we consider a second-order periodic ordinary differential operator

\displaystyle L\coloneqq-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V(x)

with
V\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
which we assume to be even and
2\pi
-periodic. Moreover, we assume that
0
does not belong to the spectrum of
L:H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We first describe what Bloch modes are and why they exists. Later we show that this is the situation which occurs in Theorem
1.5
and we verify conditions (
C1
) and (
C2
).

A function
\Phi\in{C^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
which is twice almost everywhere differentiable such that

(24)

L\Phi=0\quad\text{ a.e. in }\mathbb{R},\qquad\Phi(\cdot+2\pi)=\rho\Phi(\cdot).

with
\rho\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
is called the (exponentially decreasing for
x\to\infty
) Bloch mode of
L
and
\rho
is called the Floquet multiplier. The existence of
\Phi
is guaranteed by the assumption that
0\notin\sigma(L)
.
This is essentially Hill’s theorem, cf.
[
5
]
. Note that
\Psi(x)\coloneqq\Phi(-x)
is a second Bloch mode of
L
, which is exponentially increasing for
x\to\infty
. The functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
form a fundamental system of solutions for operator
L
on
\mathbb{R}
. Next we explain how
\Phi
is constructed, why it can be taken real-valued and why it does not vanish at
x=0
so that we can assume w.l.o.g
\Phi(0)=1
.

According to
[
5
]
, Theorem 1.1.1 there are linearly independent functions
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and Floquet-multipliers
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
L\Psi_{j}=0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
and
\Psi_{j}(x+2\pi)=\rho_{j}\Psi_{j}(x)
for
j=1,2
. We define
\phi_{j}
,
j=1,2
as the solutions to the initial value problems

\displaystyle\begin{cases}L\phi_{1}=0,\\
\phi_{1}(0)=1,\quad\phi_{1}^{\prime}(0)=0,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\qquad\begin{cases}L\phi_{2}=0,\\
\phi_{2}(0)=0,\quad\phi_{2}^{\prime}(0)=1\end{cases}

and consider the Wronskian

(25)

\displaystyle W(x)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}(x)&\phi_{2}(x)\\
\phi^{\prime}_{1}(x)&\phi^{\prime}_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix}

and the monodromy matrix

(26)

\displaystyle A\coloneqq W(2\pi)=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}(2\pi)&\phi_{2}(2\pi)\\
\phi^{\prime}_{1}(2\pi)&\phi^{\prime}_{2}(2\pi)\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\det A=1
is the Wronskian determinant of the fundamental system
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
and the Floquet multipliers
\rho_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tr(A)\pm\sqrt{\tr(A)^{2}-4}\right)
are the eigenvalues of
A
with corresponding eigenvectors
v_{1}=(v_{1,1},v_{1,2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
v_{2}=(v_{2,1},v_{2,2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Thus,
\Psi_{j}(x)=v_{j,1}\phi_{1}(x)+v_{j,2}\phi_{2}(x)
. By Hill’s theorem (see
[
5
]
) we know that

\displaystyle 0\in\sigma(L)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\absolutevalue{\tr(A)}\leq 2.

Due to the assumption that
0\not\in\sigma(L)
we see that
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are real with
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
and
\rho_{1}\rho_{2}=1
, i.e., one of the two Floquet multipliers has modulus smaller then one and other one has modulus bigger than one. W.l.o.g. we assume
0<|\rho_{2}|<1<|\rho_{1}|
. Furthermore, since
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are real and
A
has real entries we can choose
v_{1},v_{2}
to be real and so
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}
are both real valued. As a result we have found a real-valued Bloch mode
\Psi_{2}(x)
which is exponentially decreasing as
x\to\infty
due to
|\rho_{2}|<1
. Let us finally verify that
\Psi_{2}(0)\not=0
so that we may assume by rescaling that
\Psi_{2}(0)=1
. Assume for contradiction that
\Psi_{2}(0)=0
. Since the potential
V(x)
is even in
x
this implies that
\Psi_{2}
is odd and hence (due to the exponential decay at
+\infty
) in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. But this contradicts that
0\not\in\sigma(L)
.

Now we explain how the precise choice of the data

and

for the step-potential

and extend

6.3.
Embedding of Hölder-spaces into Sobolev-spaces

Lemma 6.1
.

For
0<\tilde{\nu}<\nu<1
there is the continuous embedding
{C^{0,\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}\to{H^{\tilde{\nu}}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}
.

Proof.

Let
z(t)=\sum_{k}\hat{z}_{k}e_{k}(t)
be a function in
{C^{0,\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}
. We need to show the finiteness of the spectral norm
\|z\|_{H^{\tilde{\nu}}}
. For this we use the equivalence of the spectral norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{\tilde{\nu}}}
with the Slobodeckij norm, cf. Lemma
6.2
. Therefore it suffices to check the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\frac{|z(t)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{|t-\tau|^{1+2\tilde{\nu}}}\differential{t}\differential{\tau}\leq\|z\|_{C^{\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}|t-\tau|^{-1+2(\nu-\tilde{\nu})}\differential{t}\differential{\tau}\leq C(\nu,\tilde{\nu})\|z\|_{C^{\nu}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}^{2}

where the double integral is finite due to
\nu>\tilde{\nu}
.
∎

For
0<s<1
recall the definition of the Slobodeckij-seminorm for a function
z:\mathbb{T}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle[z]_{s}\coloneqq\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\frac{|z(t)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{|t-\tau|^{1+2s}}\differential{t}\differential{\tau}\right)^{1/2}.

Lemma 6.2
.

For functions
z\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}_{T})
,
0<s<1
the spectral norm
\|z\|_{H^{s}}=(\sum_{k}(1+k^{2})^{s}|\hat{z}_{k}|^{2})^{1/2}
and the Solobodeckij norm
{\interleave z\interleave}_{H^{s}}\coloneqq(\|z\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{T})}^{2}+[z]_{s}^{2})^{1/2}
are equivalent.

Proof.

The Solobodeckij space and the spectrally defined fractional Sobolev space are both Hilbert spaces. Hence, by the open mapping theorem, if suffices to verify the estimate
{\interleave z\interleave}_{H^{s}}\leq C\|z\|_{H^{s}}
. By direct computation we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\frac{|z(t)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{|t-\tau|^{1+2s}}\differential{t}\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-\tau}^{T-\tau}\frac{|z(x+\tau)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{|x|^{1+2s}}\differential{x}\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T-\tau}\frac{|z(x+\tau)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{x^{1+2s}}\differential{x}+\int_{T-\tau}^{T}\frac{|z(x+\tau)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{(T-x)^{1+2s}}\differential{x}\right)\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{|z(x+\tau)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{g(x,\tau)^{1+2s}}\differential{x}\differential{\tau}

with

\displaystyle g(x,\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x&\mbox{ if }0\leq x\leq T-\tau,\\
T-x&\mbox{ if }T-\tau\leq x\leq T.\end{array}\right.

Since
g(x,\tau)\geq\dist(x,\partial\mathbb{T}_{T})
and due to Parseval’s identity we find

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\frac{|z(t)-z(\tau)|^{2}}{|t-\tau|^{1+2s}}\differential{t}\differential{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\frac{\|\widehat{z(\cdot+x)}-\hat{z}\|_{l^{2}}^{2}}{\dist(x,\partial\mathbb{T}_{T})^{1+2s}}\differential{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}_{T}}\sum_{k}\frac{|\exp(\i k\omega x)-1|^{2}|\hat{z}_{k}|^{2}}{\dist(x,\partial\mathbb{T}_{T})^{1+2s}}\differential{x}

\displaystyle=4\int_{0}^{T/2}\sum_{k}\frac{1-\cos(k\omega x)}{x^{1+2s}}|\hat{z}_{k}|^{2}\differential{x}

\displaystyle\leq 4\tilde{C}\sum_{k}k^{2s}|\hat{z}_{k}|^{2}

with
\tilde{C}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-\cos(\omega\xi)}{\xi^{1+2s}}\differential{\xi}
. This finishes the proof.
∎
