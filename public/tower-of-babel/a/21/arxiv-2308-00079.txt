Abstract
In this paper we derive a moment relaxation for large-scale nonsmooth optimization problems with graphical structure and spherical constraints. In contrast to classical moment relaxations for global polynomial optimization that suffer from the curse of dimensionality we exploit the partially separable structure of the optimization problem to reduce the dimensionality of the search space. Leveraging optimal transport and Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality we decouple the problem and derive a tractable dual subspace approximation of the infinite-dimensional problem using spherical harmonics. This allows us to tackle possibly nonpolynomial optimization problems with spherical constraints and geodesic coupling terms. We show that the duality gap vanishes in the limit by proving that a Lipschitz continuous dual multiplier on a unit sphere can be approximated as closely as desired in terms of a Lipschitz continuous polynomial. The formulation is applied to sphere-valued imaging problems with total variation regularization and graph-based simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM). In imaging tasks our approach achieves small duality gaps for a moderate degree. In graph-based SLAM our approach often finds solutions which after refinement with a local method are near the ground truth solution.

Polynomial optimization, Manifold optimization, Sum-of-squares, spherical harmonics, Markov random fields

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

In this paper we develop a convex relaxation approach for optimization problems with a graphical coupling structure whose variables are constrained to live in a manifold. Given a directed graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
the problem takes the form

\text{minimize}~\left\{F(x)\equiv\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}f_{u}(x_{u})+\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}f_{(u,v)}(x_{u},x_{v}):x\in\Omega^{\mathcal{V}}\right\},

(P)

where
(\Omega,d)
is a compact space with metric
d
,
f_{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous and the coupling terms
f_{(u,v)}:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous.
In the context of graphical models and exponential families this amounts to
maximum a posteriori
(MAP) inference in a continuous pairwise
Markov random field
(MRF)
[
1
]
.
Optimization problems of this form appear in a variety of tasks such as language processing
[
2
]
, image processing
[
3
]
, bioinformatics
[
4
]
or graph-based
simultaneous localization and mapping
(SLAM)
[
5
]
to name a few.
In image processing
Eq.

P
can be used to formulate denoising or inpainting tasks where
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
represents the pixel grid,
\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}f_{u}(x_{u})
is a data fidelity term and
\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}f_{(u,v)}(x_{u},x_{v})
is a prior such as the
total variation
(TV) that favors images
x
that are spatially smooth
[
6
]
.

However, in many situations
\Omega
is a manifold such as the space of rotation matrices or the unit shere and the coupling terms
f_{(u,v)}(x_{u},x_{v})=d(x_{u},x_{v})
amount to the nonsmooth nonconvex geodesic distance. As a result the optimization problem
Eq.

P
is nonconvex and nonsmooth and therefore local optimization techniques such as subgradient descent can get stuck in a poor local optimum.
Global optimization techniques, instead, suffer from the curse of dimensionality, since the optimization variable lies in a high-dimensional product space
\Omega^{\mathcal{V}}
(in particular
\mathcal{V}
is large, e.g., the number of pixels in the image).

As a tradeoff the problem can be reformulated in terms of a more tractable linear program, the
local marginal polytope relaxation

[
1
]
. Despite the fact that the local marginal polytope relaxation in general only yields a lower bound to the so-called global marginal polytope relaxation, which is tight but intractable, its solutions are often near globally optimal in practice.
When
\Omega
is finite and thus
Eq.

P
is discrete there exists an abundance of efficient methods that solve the local marginal polytope relaxation such as message passing and dual coordinate ascent
[
7
,
8
]
.
However, for continuous
\Omega
, as considered in this work, the local marginal polytope relaxation amounts to an infinite-dimensional linear program and is thus still intractable.
As a remedy we consider a hierarchy of implementable dual programs obtained by polynomial subspace discretization and show that the duality gap between the dual approximation and the infinite-dimensional local marginal polytope relaxation vanishes if the degrees goes to infinity.
Our approach is related to optimization of (sparse) polynomials
[
9
]
. However, in contrast to
[
9
]
our cost functions
f_{u}
and
f_{(u,v)}
are in general not polynomial. In particular we consider geodesic pairwise couplings
f_{(u,v)}=d
that are not even smooth.

1.2
Related work

Polynomial optimization

Semi-definite programming relaxations for polynomial programming have attracted a lot of attention
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
. The idea is to lift nonconvex polynomial optimization problems to equivalent problems into the measure space where the problem is infinite-dimensional, but crucially a linear optimization problem. The infinite-dimensional problem is then approximated by a finite dimensional one by bounding the degree of polynomials to be maximized over, and representing the measures as truncated moment sequences. These relaxations promise convex methods to solve nonconvex polynomial optimization problems, which are in general NP-hard. The backbone of these methods are easily computable nonnegativity certificates such as Putinar’s
[
14
]
or Krivine–Stengele Positivstellensätze
[
15
,
16
]
, or sum-of-squares certificates
[
17
]
. The most notable drawback of these very powerful, general approaches is that they suffer greatly from the curse of dimensionality. Approaches to reduce this problem have
been studied from the viewpoint of exploiting structured sparsity
[
18
,
19
]
.

Markov Random Fields

The class of weakly coupled problems considered in this work can be interpreted in terms of MAP inference for a continuous, higher order MRF
[
1
]
, an approach which provides a powerful model of many imaging problems. This class of problems is often studied via a lifting procedure known as the local marginal polytope relaxation, which reformulates the problem as a
linear program
(LP). For MRF problems with continuous state spaces, this LP-relaxation is infinite dimensional, which poses major challenges. The local marginal polytope relaxation is closely related to lifting for spatially continuous variational problems
[
20
]
. In
[
21
]
a piecewise linear dual subspace approximation for nonconvex variational problems is considered which can be viewed as a
sublabel-accurate
lifting in the primal space. In
[
22
]
a piecewise polynomial dual space approximation scheme is proposed as a relaxation of the infinite dimensional problem. The framework studied in
[
23
]
builds upon the analysis in
[
22
]
, even providing a tractable hierarchy for solving MAP-MRF problems using dual space approximation with piecewise polynomial multipliers, albeit restricted to the unit interval. In particular, a refinement for metric coupling terms is proposed exploiting Kantorowich–Rubinstein duality. The goal of the current paper is to continue in this direction by extending the work in
[
23
]
to more general metric spaces, with particular focus on the unit sphere.

Manifolds

In order to minimize geodesic coupling terms, we will rephrase the Wasserstein distance with geodesic cost as a maximization problem and approximate its value with a semi-definite program. In
[
24
]
the problem of computing the Wasserstein distance is also rewritten as a semi-definite program, particularly for the case of the circle
\mathcal{S}^{1}
, for which the optimal transport plan is known up to an angle
\alpha
. In
[
25
]
a finite element approach is proposed to solve continuous manifold-valued MRF-problems with more general coupling terms.

1.3
Summary and contributions

In
Section

2
, we revisit the infinite-dimensional local marginal polytope relaxation of
Eq.

P
which is based on marginalization and optimal transport.
As such it is an optimization problem formulated in the space of measures. Leveraging optimal transport duality we consider two equivalent dual formulations which eventually lead to different implementations. The first one is more general but can only be implemented for polynomial coupling terms. The second one leverages Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality to derive a tailored dual formulation for metric and in particular geodesic couplings. This allows us to provide an efficient implementation using a characterisation of Lipschitz continuity of polynomials on manifolds in terms of nonnegativity of polynomials.

In
Section

3
we consider hierarchies of dual programs obtained by polynomial subspace discretization and study its convergence properties. Under continuity of the coupling part we show that the duality gap between the general dual discretization and the infinite-dimensional primal relaxation vanishes if the degree
n
of the polynomial goes to infinity. If, in addition, the underlying graph is a tree we show that the duality gap between the general dual discretization and the original nonconvex problem
Eq.

P
vanishes in the limit. More specifically, for
\Omega
being a unit sphere we provide explicit convergence rates for the the duality gaps between the dual discretization and the infinite-dimensional relaxation: for the general dual discretization we show that the duality gap vanishes with rate
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
. For the dual formulation tailored to geodesic coupling terms we similarly obtain the rate
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

In
Section

4
we cast the semi-infinite polynomial dual discretization in terms of a finite semi-definite program using nonnegativity of polynomials and
sum-of-squares
(SOS). In particular we describe how the Lipschitz constraint on the multiplier obtained via optimal transport duality can be implemented in terms of linear matrix inequalities.

In
Section

5
we present extensive numerical tests considering manifold-valued image processing tasks and graph-based SLAM. In image processing we show that our approach achieves small duality gaps for a moderate degree of the polynomial dual variables. In graph-based SLAM we show that after rounding our approach yields solutions which are close to the ground truth solution while it reduces the memory footprint when compared to classical moment relaxations for sparse polynomial optimization.

2
Local marginal polytope relaxation and optimal transport duality

2.1
Local vs. global marginal polytope

It is a well-known fact that any nonconvex problem of the form
Eq.

P
can be cast as a convex (even linear) problem considering a reformulation over the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathcal{V}})
on the product space
\Omega^{\mathcal{V}}
:

\min_{x\in\Omega^{\mathcal{V}}}~F(x)=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathcal{V}})}~\langle\mu,F\rangle.

(2.1)

In the context of Markov random fields and linear programming this is equivalent to the so-called global marginal polytope relaxation.
However, for large
\mathcal{V}
the relaxation suffers from a curse of dimensionality even when
\dim\Omega
is moderate.

As a remedy, we consider the so-called local marginal polytope relaxation which lifts the optimization variable
x\in\Omega^{\mathcal{V}}
to a product space of probability measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)^{\mathcal{V}}
rather than the space of measures on the product space
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{\mathcal{V}})
as in
Eq.

2.1

\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)^{\mathcal{V}}}\left\{\mathcal{F}(\mu):=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\langle\mu_{u},f_{u}\rangle+\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}\ot_{f_{(u,v)}}(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})\right\}.

(R-P)

Here,
\ot
is the optimal transportation
[
26
]
with marginals
(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})
and cost
f_{(u,v)}
defined by

\ot_{f_{(u,v)}}(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})=\inf_{\mu_{(u,v)}\in\Pi(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})}\langle\mu_{(u,v)},f_{(u,v)}\rangle.

(2.2)

The constraint set
\Pi(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})
is the set of all Borel probability measures on
\Omega^{2}
with prescribed marginals
\mu_{u}
and
\mu_{v}
:

\Pi(\mu_{u},\mu_{v})=\left\{\mu_{(u,v)}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{2}):\pi_{u}\sharp\mu_{(u,v)}=\mu_{u},\;\pi_{v}\sharp\mu_{(u,v)}=\mu_{v}\right\},

(2.3)

where
\pi_{u}:\Omega\times\Omega\to\Omega
corresponds to the canonical projection onto the
u\textsuperscript{th}
component.

Since

restricting
\mu_{u}
(and therefore

) to be Dirac probability measures, the formulation

is equivalent to the original problem

P
. This shows that when optimizing over the larger set of all probability measures we obtain a lower bound to
Eq.

2.1

\Div_{u}(\varphi)=-\sum_{v:(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}\varphi_{(u,v)}+\sum_{v:(v,u)\in\mathcal{E}}\varphi_{(v,u)}.

(2.7)

We have the following result which is also known as Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality in the context of optimal transport.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
(\Omega,d)
be a compact metric space and assume that
f_{(u,v)}=d
and
f_{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is lsc. Then
\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope}=\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope_dual_metric}
and there exists a minimizer of
Eq.

R-P
and a maximizer of
Eq.

mR-D
.

As we will see
Eq.

mR-D
can be used to derive a tailored implementation for geodesic coupling terms leveraging a simple characterization of Lipschitz continuity on manifolds whereas
Eq.

R-D
is applicable for polynomial couplings only.

3
Polynomial dual approximation

3.1
Metric vs. general dual problem

In this section we consider polynomial subspace approximations of
Eq.

R-D
and
Eq.

mR-D
. Although both problems are equivalent in infinite dimensions they lead to different hierachies once we restrict the maximimization to the finite-dimensional subspace of polynomials. This leads us to examine the convergence properties of both hierachies in parallel restricting
\Omega
to a unit sphere.

Recall that we denote by
\mathbb{R}[x]
the ring of polynomials,
\deg p
denotes the maximum degree of
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
and for
n\geq 1
let
\mathbb{R}_{n}[x]\subset\mathbb{R}[x]
be the finite-dimensional subspace of the space of polynomials with some fixed degree
n
.

3.2
General dual problem

Firstly, we consider the general dual problem
Eq.

R-D
.
We restrict the dual variables
\varphi_{e},\psi_{e}
in
Eq.

R-D
to be polynomials with degree
n
on the embedding space
\mathbb{E}\supset\Omega
. Then the discretized dual problem amounts to

\sup_{\varphi,\psi\in(\mathbb{R}_{n}[x])^{\mathcal{E}}}~-\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\sigma_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}(A_{u}(\varphi,\psi)-f_{u})-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\iota_{\mathcal{K}_{e}}(\varphi_{e},\psi_{e}).

(R-D
n
)

Next we will show that the duality gap
\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope}-\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope_dual_discrete}
vanishes as
n\rightarrow\infty

Intuitively, the maximization process attempts to find the best polynomial approximation to the optimal dual variable whose existence was proved in
Theorem

2.2
. More precisely, the duality gap can be bounded in terms of the difference (in the sup-norm topology) between the optimal dual variable and its best polynomial approximation. Invoking the Stone–Weierstrass theorem we thus have the following qualitative result whose proof follows the proof of
[
22
, Theorem 2]

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\Omega,d)
be a compact metric space and let
f_{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be lsc and
f_{(u,v)}:\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Then we have that the duality gap vanishes, i.e.,
\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope}-\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope_dual_discrete}\searrow 0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
. If, in addition,
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
is a tree we have in particular
\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:mrf}-\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope_dual_discrete}\searrow 0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
.

For proving the claim we resort to an unconstrained version of
Eq.

R-Dn
obtained via the change of variable
\varphi_{e}=\gamma_{e}+\xi_{e}
for
\xi_{e}\in\mathbb{R}
for each edge

.
Then

-A_{u}(\varphi,\psi)=\sum_{v:(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}\gamma_{(u,v)}+\sum_{v:(v,u)\in\mathcal{E}}\psi_{(v,u)}+\sum_{v:(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}\xi_{(u,v)}=-A_{u}(\gamma,\psi)+\sum_{v:(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}\xi_{(u,v)}

and

becomes

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\psi\in\mathcal{C}(\Omega)^{\mathcal{E}}\\
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\min_{x\in\Omega}f_{u}(x)-A_{u}(\gamma,\psi)(x)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\xi_{e}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle f_{e}(x,y)-\gamma_{e}(x)-\psi_{e}(y)\geq\xi_{e}\quad\forall x,y\in\Omega.

Interpreting
\xi_{e}
in terms of a slack variable the problem can be written equivalently in terms of an unconstrained one

\sup_{\gamma,\psi\in\mathcal{C}(\Omega)^{\mathcal{E}}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\min_{x\in\Omega}f_{u}(x)-A_{u}(\gamma,\psi)(x)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\min_{x,y\in\Omega}f_{e}(x,y)-\gamma_{e}(x)-\psi_{e}(y),

(3.1)

and in particular
\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:local_marginal_polytope_dual}=\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:dual_relax_alt}
. Restricting
\gamma_{e},\psi_{e}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}[X]
to the subspace of polynomials with degree
n
we consider

\sup_{\gamma,\psi\in(\mathbb{R}_{n}[x])^{\mathcal{E}}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\min_{x\in\Omega}f_{u}(x)-A_{u}(\gamma,\psi)(x)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\min_{x,y\in\Omega}f_{e}(x,y)-\gamma_{e}(x)-\psi_{e}(y).

(3.2)

The rest of the proof is deferred to
Appendix

D
.

Remark 3.3

3.3

In this section we consider a hierarchy of polynomial discretizations for the metric dual formulation

\sup_{\varphi\in(\mathbb{R}_{n}[x])^{\mathcal{E}}}~-\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\sigma_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}(\Div_{u}(\varphi)-f_{u})-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\iota_{\lip(\Omega,d)}(\varphi_{e}),

(mR-D
n
)

All results in this section are specialized to the unit sphere

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(\Omega,g)
be the
m

be lsc and

We will prove this theorem in a style very similar to the proof of
Theorem

3.2
, again using the polynomial approximants defined in
[
31
]
. To generalize that proof to one for the metric problem formulation we require that for any degree
n
, there exist polynomial approximants
\varphi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}[x]
to
\varphi
that are Lipschitz continuous if
\varphi
is, with
\lip\varphi_{n}=\lip\varphi
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will prove this fact first.

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{S}^{m}
that is invariant to the left group action of
\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
, where
\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
is the special orthogonal group in dimension
m+1
which can be identified with the space of rotation matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
:

\mu(B)=\mu(r^{-1}B),\quad\forall B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{S}^{m}),~r\in\mathcal{SO}(m+1).

In
[
31
]
it is proved that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}:
there exists a
\kappa_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}[x]
such that
\kappa_{n}(\tau)
is nonnegative for
\tau\in[-1,+1]
, and for any
x\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
,

\int_{\mathcal{S}^{m}}\kappa_{n}(\langle x,y\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=1.

(3.3)

Then an approximant
\varphi_{n}
of
\varphi
can be computed as

\varphi_{n}(x)=[K_{n}\varphi](x):=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{m}}\varphi(y)\kappa_{n}(\langle x,y\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

(3.4)

It can be shown
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
that for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
C_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\|\varphi-K_{n}\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq C_{m}\tfrac{L}{n},

(3.5)

which is the approximation result also used in
Theorem

3.2
. Before proving that
K_{n}\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous with
\lip\varphi=L
, we require a lemma:

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
there exists
r\in\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
such that
x^{\prime}=rx
and
\displaystyle\min_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\langle y,ry\rangle=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
.

Proof.

We will identify any element of
y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
with points in
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
such that
\|y\|=1
, and
r\in\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
with a rotation matrix
R\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
. Assume
x
and
x^{\prime}
are not linearly dependent, and let
U_{S}=[x,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle^{2}}}x^{\prime}-\tfrac{\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle^{2}}}x]
, which has orthonormal columns that span
S=\ran([x,x^{\prime}])
. Furthermore, let
U_{S^{\perp}}
be any matrix such that its columns are an orthonormal basis for
S^{\perp}
. One can define

R=\begin{bmatrix}U_{S^{\perp}}&U_{S}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle&-\sqrt{1-\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle}\\
0&\sqrt{1-\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle}&\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{S^{\perp}}&U_{S}\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=:U\tilde{R}U^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

and readily check that
Rx=x^{\prime}
. Furthermore,
R
is unitarily similar to the rotation matrix
\tilde{R}
, hence
R
is itself a rotation matrix. Constructing
\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2}
, we have

\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{S^{\perp}}&U_{S}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0&0\\
0&\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle&0\\
0&0&\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{S^{\perp}}&U_{S}\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

which is an eigendecomposition of
\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2}
. Since for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
we have
\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle\leq 1
, the smallest eigenvalue of
\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2}
is
\lambda_{\text{min}}(\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2})=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
. Finally, we have the Rayleigh quotient

\min_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\langle y,Ry\rangle=\min_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\langle y,\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2}y\rangle=\lambda_{\text{min}}(\tfrac{R+R^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}}{2})=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle.

If
x
and
x^{\prime}
are linearly dependent, the argument holds via a limiting process by replacing
x^{\prime}
by
\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^{2}}x^{\prime}+\varepsilon r
where
r\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
can be chosen random, when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. In particular, when
x=x^{\prime}
, we get
R=I
and we have
\langle y,Ry\rangle=\|y\|^{2}=1=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
for all
y\in\mathcal{SO}(m)
. If
x=-x^{\prime}
, then
R
can be any rotation such that
Rx=x^{\prime}
(depending on the choice of
r
), but since
Ry\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
for any
y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
, we have by Cauchy-Schwartz
\langle y,Ry\rangle\geq-\|y\|\|Ry\|\geq-1=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle
with equality for
y=x
.
∎

We prove that this approximant is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{S}^{m}
:

Theorem 3.7
.

If
\varphi:\mathcal{S}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous with
\lip\varphi=L
, then
\varphi_{n}=K_{n}\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous with
\lip\varphi_{n}=L
.

Proof.

Denoting the action of
r\in\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
on a function
\varphi:~\mathcal{S}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
r\cdot\varphi(x):=\varphi(r^{-1}x)
,
[
32
, Proposition 3.1]
shows that
K_{n}
is an
\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
-invariant operator:

r\cdot K_{n}\varphi=K_{n}(r\cdot\varphi),\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{SO}(m+1).

For any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
, choose
r\in\mathcal{SO}(m+1)
as in
Lemma

3.6
, for which we have
rx=x^{\prime}
and that

\min_{z\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}z^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}rz=\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle.

(3.6)

Then

\varphi_{n}(x^{\prime})=[K_{n}\varphi](x^{\prime})=[K_{n}\varphi](r^{-1}x)=r\cdot[K_{n}\varphi](x)=[K_{n}(r\cdot\varphi)](x),

and
\varphi_{n}(x)=[K_{n}\varphi](x)
. By linearity of
K_{n}
, we then obtain

\varphi_{n}(x^{\prime})-\varphi_{n}(x)=[K_{n}(r\cdot\varphi-\varphi)](x).

Writing
K_{n}
out in full, we obtain

\displaystyle|\varphi_{n}(x^{\prime})-\varphi_{n}(x)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\mathcal{S}^{m}}\varphi(r^{-1}y)-\varphi(y)\kappa_{n}(\langle x,y\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{S}^{m}}|\varphi(r^{-1}y)-\varphi(y)|\kappa_{n}(\langle x,y\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq L\int_{\mathcal{S}^{m}}d_{g}(r^{-1}y,y)\kappa_{n}(\langle x,y\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq L\|d_{g}(r^{-1}\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{m}}\kappa_{n}(\langle x,y\rangle)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle=L\|d_{g}(r^{-1}\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\infty}.

(3.7)

The first inequality follows since
\kappa_{n}(\tau)\geq 0
, for any
\tau\in[-1,+1]
, the second inequality follows from the Lipschitz continuity of
\varphi
. The final equality follows from
Eq.

3.3
.

The result is proved if
\|d_{g}(r^{-1}\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\infty}=d_{g}(x,x^{\prime})
. For any
m
, we have that
d_{g}(x,x^{\prime})=\arccos(\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle)
for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
. Hence
d_{g}(r^{-1}y,y)=\arccos(y^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}r^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}y)
. Since
\arccos
is a monotonically decreasing function, we have

\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\big\{\arccos(y^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}r^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}y)\big\}=\arccos\Big(\min_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\big\{y^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}r^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}y\big\}\Big)=\arccos\Big(\min_{z\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\big\{z^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}rz\big\}\Big),

(3.8)

where the last equality follows from the change of variables
y=rz
. We may write
\min
and
\max
as we are optimizing a bounded function on a compact set. By
Eq.

3.6
together with
Eq.

3.8
this yields

\|d_{g}(r^{-1}\cdot,\cdot)\|_{\infty}=\max_{y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}\big\{\arccos(y^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}r^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}y)\big\}=\arccos\big(\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle\big)=d_{g}(x,x^{\prime}).

and using
Eq.

3.7
we get

|\varphi_{n}(x^{\prime})-\varphi_{n}(x)|\leq d_{g}(x,x^{\prime})\qed

\inf_{x\in(\mathbb{R}^{M})^{\mathcal{V}}}\Big\{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}f^{**}_{u}(x_{u})+\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}f^{**}_{(u,v)}(x_{u},x_{v})\Big\},

4
Semi-definite program using sum-of-squares certificates

In this section we finally construct an implementable algorithm to optimize manifold valued MRF problems. We will write our problem as a semi-definite optimization problem where the positive semi-definiteness constraints encode polynomial nonnegativity certificates. For the case of
Eq.

mR-Dn
, we rewrite the geodesic Lipschitz continuity condition as a box constraint with respect to the manifold gradient. We then derive a polynomial inequality that is equivalent to this gradient condition, hence implementable in the
semidefinite programming
(SDP) framework. Then we write down the final SDP relaxation hierarchy.

4.1
Evaluation of the support function

In both the general
Eq.

R-Dn
and metric relaxation
Eq.

mR-Dn
, a support function over the space of probability measures needs to be evaluated. This requires optimizing over the space of measures. We assume for the rest of this work that the unary terms are polynomial. For any polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
, we can evaluate the the negative support function

-\sigma_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}(p)=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}\langle p,\mu\rangle=\min_{x\in\Omega}p(x),

where the last equality follows from
Eq.

2.1
, by evaluating the dual problem

\displaystyle\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle p(x)\geq\zeta\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

This is a one-dimensional optimization problem, but with a semi-infinite constraint. Inspired by the literature on semidefinite programming relaxations for polynomial optimization, we will relax this to a finite-dimensional
positive semidefinite
(PSD) constraint in
Section

4.3
.

4.2
Implementation of the manifold gradient condition

In order to implement an algorithm to solve the metric relaxation
Eq.

mR-Dn
, we require a certificate that ensures Lipschitz continuity of a polynomial. A continuously differentiable function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the Riemannian metric with
\lip\varphi=L
, if and only if the norm of the manifold gradient of
\varphi
is bounded by
L

[
33
, Proposition 10.43]
; also see
[
34
]
:

\|\nabla_{\!\!g}\varphi_{n}\|\leq L,

(4.1)

where
\nabla_{\!\!g}
is used to denote the manifold gradient.

Let
m
and
M
be the dimensions of the Riemannian submanifold
\Omega
and its Euclidean embedding space
\mathbb{E}
, respectively. There exists an operator
\Pi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, which we assume to be elementwise polynomial, that projects a vector
v\in\mathbb{E}
at the point
x\in\Omega
to the tangent vector
\Pi(x)v\in\mathrm{T}\Omega(x)
, albeit expressed in the basis of the Euclidean embedding space. Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
, then the tangent vector
\Pi(x)\nabla\varphi(x)
equals the manifold gradient of
\varphi
at
x\in\Omega
. Since
\Pi(x)
is an orthogonal projection matrix, it is symmetric, idempotent and its eigenvalues are all either
0
or
1
. If there exists an elementwise polynomial
J(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times m}
, such that the spectral decomposition of
\Pi(x)
can be written

\Pi(x)=J(x)J^{*}(x)\quad\forall x\in\Omega,

then the columns of
J(x)
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{T}\Omega(x)
. Equivalently, for any
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

\langle\Pi(x)v,\Pi(x)w\rangle=\langle\Pi(x)v,w\rangle=\langle J^{*}(x)v,J^{*}(x)w\rangle,

(4.2)

where we have used the symmetry and idempotency of
\Pi(x)
. This shows that the metric tensor in the inner product between vectors
J^{*}(x)v
and
J^{*}(x)w
is the identity, hence
J(x)
maps vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{E}
into an
m
-dimensional vector space with an orthonormal basis. This means in particular that
J^{*}(x)v
represents
\Pi(x)v
in an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{T}\Omega(x)
.

Remark 4.1

(hairy ball theorem)
.

No such
J(x)
can exist in the case that
\Omega=\mathcal{S}^{2m}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
; otherwise, one could take any column
j(x)
of
J(x)
, which would be a polynomial (hence continuous) vector field everywhere tangent to
\mathcal{S}^{2m}
with
\|j(x)\|=1
. By the hairy ball theorem, no such nonvanishing continuous tangent vector field can exist.

In this case however, there may still exist a
J(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times p}
for some
p\geq m
, and
Eq.

4.2
still holds. Furthermore, if
v\in\mathrm{T}\Omega(x)
(expressed in the basis of
\mathbb{E}
), then
v=\Pi(x)v
and

\|v\|^{2}=\langle v,\Pi(x)v\rangle=\|J^{*}(x)v\|^{2}

regardless of whether
p=m
or not.

Assuming that an elementwise polynomial
J(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times p}
exists such that
\Pi(x)=J(x)J^{*}(x)
, then the gradient condition can be implemented via the dual norm. Since

\|\nabla_{\!\!g}\varphi(x)\|=\|J^{*}(x)\nabla\varphi(x)\|,

(which comes from
Eq.

4.2
when
v=w=\nabla\varphi
) we have via dualization that

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\langle\xi,J^{*}(x)\nabla\varphi(x)\rangle\leq L

is equivalent to
Eq.

4.1
. The gradient condition is met whenever

p(x,\xi)=L-\langle\xi,J^{*}(x)\nabla\varphi(x)\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall\xi\in\mathcal{S}^{p}.

(4.3)

This can be implemented as a polynomial inequality in both the variables
x
and
\xi
, and the coefficient vector of
p
is linearly dependent on that of
\varphi
. The main advantages of using the matrix
J^{*}(x)
rather than
\Pi(x)
in
Eq.

4.3
is that the degree of the polynomial
J^{*}(x)\nabla\varphi(x)
will be lower than that of
\Pi(x)\nabla\varphi(x)
, as well as requiring a
p
-dimensional dual variable
\xi
, rather than an
M
-dimensional one, noting that
p\leq M
. Both of these facts will result in smaller PSD constraints in our final implementation.

4.3
Semi-definite problem formulation

Using the ideas from
Section

4.1
and
Section

4.2
, the infinite dimensional problems
Eq.

R-Dn
and
Eq.

mR-Dn
can be reframed as finite-dimensional optimization problems with semi-infinite polynomial positivity constraints. It is already a classical idea to rephrase polynomial optimization problems, using various Positivstellensätze
[
14
,
16
,
15
]
, into large optimization hierarchies
[
11
,
13
]
. The idea is to replace polynomial positivity constraints (which are generally intractable to verify) by a positivity certificate that is easy to compute. Following these works, we use Putinar’s Postivstellensatz to relax the semi-infinite constraints as the intersection of a semidefinite cone and an affine space. The resulting problem is a finite-dimensional convex semidefinite programming problem. We do not discuss the details of these ideas here, but rather refer to the relevant literature, e.g. the references
[
11
,
13
]
. We simply add the following remarks:

Remark 4.2

(ball constraint)
.

Often the additional inequality constraint
q_{I+1}(x)=R^{2}-\|x\|^{2}\leq 0
for some
R>0
is introduced in the polynomial hierarchy in order to ensure tightness of the relaxation. The defining equation
\|x\|^{2}=1
of the unit sphere renders this additional PSD constraint trivially true for any
R\geq 1
, such that it does not need to be implemented.

Remark 4.3

(coordinate ring)
.

It is possible to reduce the size of the problem by working in the coordinate ring of the maximal ideal that defines the manifold
\Omega
as its variety. Especially for
m
-dimensional manifolds embedded in an
M
-dimensional space where
m\ll M
this approach leads to a dramatic reduction. Even for the case
\mathcal{S}^{M-1}
we consider in this work, however, this dimensionality reduction is significant for dual multipliers with higher degrees.

Remark 4.4

(semidefinite relaxation and moments)
.

The approach we have taken in this work follows the style of
[
13
]
. Dual to this approach is the
Lasserre moment hierarchy

[
11
]
, in which one optimizes over (pseudo)moment vectors. In our implementation of this relaxation we compute these (pseudo)moment vectors in order to extract a feasible primal point, cf.
Section

5.1
.

One can prove that the semi-definite program that results from applying the polynomial optimization hierarchy to solve the problems
Eq.

R-Dn
and
Eq.

mR-Dn
converges to
Eq.

R-P
as the degree
n
of the relaxation and the degrees of the SOS-multipliers are increased.

In
pose graph optimization
(PGO), an agent travels along an unknown path, which is modeled by a sequence of positions the agent has visited. These positions along with the direction the agent was pointing towards at that positions (encoded by a rotation from a reference direction) are named poses, and these form the vertices of the underlying graph for this problem. The agent keeps track of (noisy) measurements of its relative rotations
r
and displacements
x
between two subsequent poses. These measurements are naturally elements of the special Euclidean group:
(x,r)\in\mathcal{SE}(2)\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathcal{SO}(2)
. Two vertices are connected whenever they are subsequent positions in the path, but also whenever the agent has returned to a previous position, in which case the edge is called a loop closure. The goal of PGO is to estimate the true path of travel based only on noisy relative measurements and the loop closures. The cost function for PGO is
[
42
]

\min_{(y,q)\in(\mathcal{SE}(2))^{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{E}}\tfrac{\kappa_{e}}{2}\big\|y_{v}-y_{u}-q_{u}x_{(u,v)}\big\|^{2}_{2}+\tfrac{\lambda_{e}}{2}\big\|q_{v}-q_{u}r_{(u,v)}\big\|^{2}_{2}

(5.2)

where
(x,r)\in(\mathcal{SE}(2))^{\mathcal{E}}
contains the noisy relative measurements between positions, including those estimated for the loop closure edges. The solution
(y,q)\in(\mathcal{SE}(2))^{\mathcal{V}}
contains the absolute poses.

Semidefinite relaxations for the PGO problem have been studied widely, including from the viewpoint of sparse polynomial programming
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
. We solve the PGO problem using our relaxation, using a polynomial of degree
2
as dual variable, and either use a least squares optimization step to update the positions while keeping the angles fixed (LSQ rounding), or initialize a local Riemannian optimization method with our solution (Manopt
[
40
]
) rounding. The results are found in
Fig.

6
. While a local method is required to refine the solution for the PGO problem, we remark that for the rotation estimation problem (which is obtained by fixing the poses
y
and optimizing only over the rotations
q
in
Eq.

5.2
) our relaxation is empirically tight in low-noise regimes even at degree
2
. The appeal of our hierarchy for these problems is that leads to smaller optimization problems than existing semidefinite relaxations.

6
Conclusion

In this paper we have considered an efficient moment relaxation for optimization problems with graphical structure and spherical constraints. Leveraging optimal transport duality we derive a tractable dual subspace approximation of the infinite-dimensional problem which allows us to tackle possibly nonpolynomial optimization problems with manifold constraints and geodesic coupling terms. We showed that the duality gap vanishes in the limit by proving that a Lipschitz continuous dual multiplier on a unit sphere can be approximated as closely as desired in terms of a Lipschitz continuous polynomial. The formulation is applied to manifold-valued imaging problems with total variation regularization and graph-based SLAM. In imaging tasks our approach achieves small duality gaps for a moderate degree. In graph-based SLAM our approach often finds solutions which after refinement with a local method are near the ground truth solution.
This shows that in contrast to existing approaches our approach scales to large problems with millions of variables.

Appendix A
A counterexample to tightness for the local marginal polytope relaxation

In
[
23
]
the authors prove tightness of the local marginal polytope relaxation for distance-like coupling terms by leveraging results from
[
27
]
. We construct a simple example that shows that for any graph containing at least one loop, the local marginal polytope relaxation is not tight, even for spheres under coupling terms penalizing geodesic distance.

Example A.1

(Local marginal polytope relaxation is not tight)
.

Consider a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
K
edges, indexed by the integers
0\ldots K-1
. Assume that
\mathcal{E}
contains all edges
(k,k+1)
for
k=0\ldots K-1
. Here, for notational convenience, we have indexed the
0
-th vertex also by
K
so as to write the edge
(K-1,K)
rather than
(K-1,0)
. Furthermore, consider a set of
2K
equally spaced points distributed around the unit circle, indexed by integers mod
0\ldots 2K-1
. These points are ordered around the circle in increasing order such that
d_{g}(y_{k},y_{k+1})=\tfrac{\pi}{K}
. Then
d_{g}(y_{k},y_{k+K})=\pi
and
d_{g}(y_{k},y_{k+K+1})=\tfrac{(K-1)\pi}{K}
. Finally we state the optimization problem, where for every vertex
k
we optimize a variable
x_{k}\in\mathcal{S}^{1}
:

\min_{x\in(\mathcal{S}^{1})^{\mathcal{V}}}F_{K}(x)=\min_{x\in(\mathcal{S}^{1})^{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\iota_{\{y_{k},y_{K+k}\}}(x_{k})+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}d_{g}(x_{k},x_{k+1}).

(A.1)

Consider the pair
(x_{k},x_{k+1})
. The
k^{\text{th}}
data term enforces that
x_{k}=y_{k}
or
x_{k}=y_{k+K}
, and similarly for
x_{k+1}
. Notice that
d_{g}(x_{k},x_{k+1})=\tfrac{1}{K}\pi
if
(x_{k},x_{k+1})=(y_{k},y_{k+1})
or
(x_{k},x_{k+1})=(y_{k+K},y_{k+K+1})
, and
d_{g}(x_{k},x_{k+1})=\tfrac{K-1}{K}\pi
otherwise. We prove that
x^{*}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{K})
is an optimizer to this problem.

To see this, note that there must be at least one edge
(x_{k},x_{k+1})
which achieves the cost
d_{g}(x_{k},x_{k+1})=\tfrac{K-1}{K}\pi
. Conversely, assume there exists a solution for which this is not true, and let
x_{0}=y_{l}
(where
l=0
or
l=K
). Since
d_{g}(x_{0},x_{1})=\tfrac{1}{K}\pi
, we must have
x_{1}=y_{l+1}
. Repeating this argument for every edge, we obtain
x_{K}=y_{l+K}
, which is impossible since
x_{0}=x_{K}
, and
y_{l}\neq y_{l+K}
. Hence at least one edge achieves cost
d_{g}(x_{k},x_{k+1})=\tfrac{K-1}{K}\pi
, and every other edge achieves a cost
d_{g}(x_{k},x_{k+1})\geq\tfrac{1}{K}\pi
, which means that
F_{K}(x)\geq 2\tfrac{K-1}{K}\pi
. This cost is achieved by
x^{*}
(as well as
2K
equivalent solutions obtained by symmetry):
F(x^{*})=2\tfrac{K-1}{K}\pi
.

Now consider the local marginal polytope relaxation for this problem:

\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S}^{1})^{\mathcal{V}}}\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S}^{1})^{\mathcal{V}}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\langle\iota_{\{y_{k},y_{K+k}\}},\mu_{k}\rangle+\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\ot_{d_{g}}(\mu_{k},\mu_{k+1}).

(A.2)

We can consider the solution
\mu^{*}
with
\mu_{k}^{*}=(\tfrac{\delta_{y_{k}}+\delta_{y_{K+k}}}{2})
. This solution turns out to be optimal, but it suffices for this example that it is feasible and achieves lower cost than
\delta_{x^{*}}
. To see this, note that
\mu_{k}^{*}
is supported only on
\{y_{k},y_{K+k}\}
, hence
\langle\iota_{\{y_{k},y_{K+k}\}},\mu^{*}_{k}\rangle=0
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\ot_{d_{g}}(\mu^{*}_{k},\mu^{*}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\ot_{d_{g}}(\tfrac{\delta_{y_{k}}+\delta_{y_{K+k}}}{2},\tfrac{\delta_{y_{k+1}}+\delta_{y_{K+k+1}}}{2})

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\langle d_{g},\delta_{y_{k}}\times\delta_{y_{k+1}}+\delta_{y_{K+k}}\times\delta_{y_{K+k+1}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{d_{g}(y_{k},y_{k+1})+d_{g}(y_{K+k},y_{K+k+1})}{2}=\tfrac{1}{K}\pi.

Since this inequality holds for every edge, we have that the cost of the local marginal polytope relaxation is upper bounded by
K\cdot\tfrac{1}{k}\pi=\pi
(in fact
\mathcal{F}(\mu^{*})=\pi
) which is strictly less than the minimal cost
2\tfrac{K-1}{K}\pi
obtained for the unlifted problem. Therefore, the local marginal polytope relaxation is not tight.

It is interesting to note that it is not necessary to resort to extended real-valued functions for this result to hold. With some extra effort the same result can be shown for
f_{k}(x)=2\pi|x_{1}(y_{k})_{2}-x_{2}(y_{k})_{1}|
, rather than explicitly constraining
x_{k}
via the indicator functions
f_{k}(x)=\iota_{\{y_{k},y_{-k}\}}(x)
. The proof is obtained by showing that, for any fixed
x_{k-1}
and
x_{k+1}
, the optimal value for
x_{k}
is either
y_{k}
or
y_{k+K}
. The rest of the proof then follows the proof above. Finally, this example can be generalized to any arbitrary graph that contains at least one loop of size
K
, by simply setting
f_{u}=0
for all vertices
u
that are not part of the loop; the local marginal polytope relaxations is only tight for trees.
