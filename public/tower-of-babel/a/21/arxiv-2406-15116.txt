Dynamic Modelling of a Controlled Orthotropic Plate: Analytic and Data-Driven Approaches in the Frequency Domain
Alexander Zuyev 1,3 , Francesco Pellicano 2 , Antonio Zippo 2 , Giovanni Iarriccio

Abstract
This paper is devoted to the mathematical modelling of a vibrating orthotropic plate equipped with a laminated piezosensor, under the influence of a lumped force actuation. We employ the Kirchhoff plate theory to derive the corresponding partial differential equation, assuming free boundary conditions. Analytical solutions for this boundary value problem are explored in the form of series expansions, using products of Krylov functions. Utilizing Galerkin’s method, this mathematical model is transformed into an infinite-dimensional control system characterized by modal coordinates. The transfer function of such a system is explicitly evaluated in the single-input single-output case. The computation of coefficients for finite-dimensional approximate systems is formalized in an algorithm with an arbitrary number of degrees of freedom. Our numerical study confirms that the modeled input-output behavior shows acceptable agreement over the given frequency range.

Introduction

Flexible structures containing composite plates and shells are widely used in modern mechanical engineering.
Analyzing the vibrations in such structures requires accurate modeling that accounts for anisotropic material properties.
While finite element methods (FEM) offer good accuracy for the modal analysis of anisotropic plates, the numerical results typically do not provide sufficient insight into the influence of mechanical parameters and the effects of time-varying input actions.
These effects are essential for addressing control-theoretic problems, particularly in relation to the task of active vibration suppression within dynamic environments.
This paper aims at comparing results of analytic and data-driven modeling of a controlled composite plate, which is controlled by a point force actuation.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. A mathematical model of a controlled orthotropic Kirchhoff plate with free and simply supported edges is proposed in Section
2
.
The spectral problem of the associated fourth-order differential operator is studied in Section
3
,
and the transfer function is derived in Section
4
with the use of Galerkin’s method.
We justify the obtained analytical results with experimental data in Section
5
.
The input-output behavior of a finite-dimensional models is compared with the results from the data-driven identification of the transfer function, conducted for the experimental setup at the Department of Engineering “Enzo Ferrari” of the University of Modena and Reggio Emilia.

3
Separation of variables and spectral problem

In order to study spectral properties of the considered mathematical model, we first substitute
w(t,x)=q(t)W(x)
into (
2.1
), (
2.4
), (
2.5
) with
F=0
.
As a result, we obtain the ordinary differential equation

\ddot{q}(t)+\alpha\dot{q}(t)=-\lambda q(t)

(3.1)

together with the following boundary value problem for
W(x)
:

({\cal L}W)(x)=\lambda W(x),\quad W\in D({\cal L})\subset L^{2}(D).

(3.2)

Here,
L^{2}(D)
denotes the complex Hilbert space of square-integrable functions equipped with the standard inner product
\left<W^{i},W^{j}\right>_{L^{2}(D)}=\int_{D}W^{i}(x)\overline{W^{j}(x)}dx
for
W^{1},W^{2}\in L^{2}(D)
, and the linear differential operator
{\cal L}:D({\cal L})\to L^{2}(D)
is defined by the rule

{\cal L}W=d_{11}W_{x_{1}x_{1}x_{1}x_{1}}+2(d_{12}+2d_{66})W_{x_{1}x_{1}x_{2}x_{2}}+d_{22}W_{x_{2}x_{2}x_{2}x_{2}},

(3.3)

where

D({\cal L})=\left\{W\in H^{4}(D)\,:\,\begin{aligned} &W_{x_{1}x_{1}}+\nu_{2}W_{x_{2}x_{2}}=0\;\text{at}\;x_{1}\in\{0,\ell_{1}\},\\
&W_{x_{1}x_{1}x_{1}}+\left(\nu_{2}+\frac{4d_{66}}{d_{11}}\right)W_{x_{1}x_{2}x_{2}}=0\;\text{at}\;x_{1}\in\{0,\ell_{1}\},\\
&\nu_{1}W_{x_{1}x_{1}}+W_{x_{2}x_{2}}=0\;\text{at}\;x_{2}\in\{0,\ell_{2}\},\\
&\left(\nu_{1}+\frac{4d_{66}}{d_{22}}\right)W_{x_{1}x_{1}x_{2}}+W_{x_{2}x_{2}x_{2}}=0\;\text{at}\;x_{2}\in\{0,\ell_{2}\}\end{aligned}\right\},

and
H^{k}(D)
is the Sobolev space of functions
f\in L^{2}(D)
such that, for each
0\leq\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\leq k
, the weak derivative
\frac{\partial^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}f}{\partial^{\alpha_{1}}x_{1}\partial^{\alpha_{2}}x_{2}}
exists and belongs to
L^{2}(D)
.
We introduce the symmetric bilinear form
{\cal B}:H^{2}(D)\times H^{2}(D)\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle{\cal B}(W^{i},

\displaystyle W^{j})=\int_{D}\Bigl(d_{11}W^{i}_{x_{1}x_{1}}W^{j}_{x_{1}x_{1}}+d_{22}W^{i}_{x_{2}x_{2}}W^{j}_{x_{2}x_{2}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle+d_{12}(W^{i}_{x_{1}x_{1}}W^{j}_{x_{2}x_{2}}+W^{i}_{x_{2}x_{2}}W^{j}_{x_{1}x_{1}})+4d_{66}W^{i}_{x_{1}x_{2}}W^{j}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\Bigr)dx.

By exploiting the integration by parts in (
3.4
), it is easy to see that

{\cal B}(W^{i},W^{j})=\int_{D}({\cal L}W^{i})(x)W^{j}(x)dx,

(3.5)

provided that
W^{i}\in D({\cal L})
and
W^{j}\in H^{2}(D)
.
Some important properties of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions are summarized below.

Proposition 1
.

The solutions to boundary value problem (
3.2
) satisfy the following properties.

1)

All eigenvalues
\lambda
of boundary value problem (
3.2
) are real.

2)

Let
(\lambda_{i},W^{i})
and
(\lambda_{j},W^{j})
be solutions of problem (
3.2
).
If
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
, then
\left<W^{i},W^{j}\right>
= 0.

P
roof.
Let
W(x)\not\equiv 0
be a solution of boundary value problem (
3.2
) with some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, because of (
3.5
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\lambda\left<W,W\right>_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\left<{\cal L}W,W\right>_{L^{2}(D)}={\cal B}(W,\overline{W})={\cal B}(\overline{W},W)

\displaystyle=\overline{{\cal B}(W,\overline{W})}=\overline{\left<{\cal L}W,W\right>}_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda\left<W,W\right>}_{L^{2}(D)}=\overline{\lambda}\left<W,W\right>_{L^{2}(D)}.

Hence,
\lambda=\overline{\lambda}
, which proves assertion 1).

To prove assertion 2) for
W^{i},W^{j}\in D({\cal L})
such that
{\cal L}W^{i}=\lambda_{i}W^{i}
and
{\cal L}W^{j}=\lambda_{j}W^{j}
, we employ identity (
3.5
) and the equalities

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\left<W^{i},W^{j}\right>_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle={\cal B}(W^{i},\overline{W^{j}})=\overline{{\cal B}(W^{j},\overline{W^{i}})}

\displaystyle=\overline{\int_{D}({\cal L}W^{j})(x)\overline{W^{i}}(x)dx}=\overline{\lambda_{j}\left<W^{j},W^{i}\right>}_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda_{j}}\left<W^{i},W^{j}\right>_{L^{2}(D)}={\lambda_{j}}\left<W^{i},W^{j}\right>_{L^{2}(D)}.

Therefore,
(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\left<W^{i},W^{j}\right>_{L^{2}(D)}=0
, so that the eigenfunctions
W^{i}
and
W^{j}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}(D)
whenever
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
.

\square

The boundary value problem, as stated in (
3.2
), can be reformulated into its weak form by employing identity (
3.5

This leads to the following formulation: find a
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

and

{\cal B}(W,V)=\lambda\int_{D}W(x)V(x)dx,

(3.6)

for each
V\in H^{2}(D)

We will search for solutions of (

are normalized eigenfunctions of the Euler–Bernoulli beam model with free ends.
Namely, the functions
\phi_{n}(x)
are solutions of the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\beta_{n}^{4}\phi_{n}(x),\quad x\in(0,1),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(0)=\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime}(1)=0,

\displaystyle\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)=0.

The eigenvalues
\beta_{k}^{4}
are such that
\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=0
, and
\beta_{n}>0

\cos\beta_{n}\cdot\cosh\beta_{n}=1\quad\text{for}\;n=2,3,...\;.

The above transcendent equation implies that

\beta_{n}=\frac{\pi(2n-1)}{2}+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\quad\text{as}\;n\to\infty.

The normalized eigenfunctions of (
3.8
) are represented in the form

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,\;\phi_{1}(x)=\sqrt{3}(2x-1),

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{n}(x)}{\|\psi_{n}(x)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}},

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sin\beta_{n}x+\sinh\beta_{n}x-\frac{\cosh\beta_{n}-\cos\beta_{n}}{\sinh\beta_{n}+\sin\beta_{n}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\cos\beta_{n}x+\cosh\beta_{n}x\right)\;\;\text{for}\;n=2,3,...,

such that

\left<\phi_{n},\phi_{m}\right>_{L^{2}(0,1)}=0
for all

, where
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{L^{2}(0,1)}
stands for the standard inner product in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
.

To determine the coefficients

in (

), we apply Galerkin’s method.
This requires that equation (
3.6
) is satisfied for each function

of the form

\phi_{kj}(x)=\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\ell_{1}}\right)\,\phi_{j}\left(\frac{x_{2}}{\ell_{2}}\right).

In other words, we assume that

{\cal B}(W,\phi_{kj})=\lambda\int_{D}W(x)\phi_{kj}(x)dx\quad\text{for}\;\;k,j=0,1,2,\,...\,.

(3.10)

After substituting the expansion given in (

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d_{11}\beta_{k}^{4}}{\ell_{1}^{4}}+\frac{d_{22}\beta_{j}^{4}}{\ell_{2}^{4}}-\lambda\right)c_{kj}+\frac{d_{12}}{\ell_{1}^{2}\ell_{2}^{2}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}(\varkappa_{nk}\varkappa_{jm}+\varkappa_{kn}\varkappa_{mj})c_{nm}

(3.11)

\displaystyle+\frac{4d_{66}}{\ell_{1}^{2}\ell_{2}^{2}}\sum_{n,m=0}^{\infty}\theta_{nk}\theta_{mj}c_{nm}=0,

where

\varkappa_{nk}=\left<\phi_{n}^{\prime\prime},\phi_{k}\right>_{L^{2}(0,1)},\;\theta_{nk}=\left<\phi_{n}^{\prime},\phi_{k}^{\prime}\right>_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

(3.12)

Note that
\varkappa_{nj}=0

\displaystyle\frac{d_{11}}{\ell_{1}^{4}}B_{N}^{4}\tilde{C}

\displaystyle+\frac{4d_{66}}{\ell_{1}^{2}\ell_{2}^{2}}\theta_{N}{\tilde{C}}\theta_{N}=\lambda\tilde{C},

are square matrices of size

6
Conclusion

We have derived the mathematical model for an orthotropic vibrating plate, actuated by a point force and equipped with a piezoelectric sensor.
The boundary value problem, described in Section
2
, accommodates plates with both simply supported and free edges.
For a plate with free edges, the displacement representation (
3.7
), using Krylov functions, provides a constructive approach
to solving the associated spectral problem through the infinite matrix equation (
3.13
).
A finite-dimensional approximation of this system is effectively implemented in a vectorized form (equation (
3.15
)) and has been successfully tested using Matlab.
The validity of our analytical model and computational approach is further supported by the experimental results presented in Section
5
.
Future work will focus on developing data-driven methods for inferring reduced-order models of plates. This includes exploring the Loewner framework
[
12
,
13
]
, both in theoretical and experimental contexts with our setup.
