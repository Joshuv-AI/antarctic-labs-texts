A stable and causal model of magnetohydrodynamics
Filippo Camilloni

Abstract
We formulate the theory of first-order dissipative magnetohydrodynamics in an arbitrary hydrodynamic frame under the assumption of parity-invariance and discrete charge symmetry. We study the mode spectrum of Alfvén and magnetosonic waves as well as the spectrum of gapped excitations and derive constraints on the transport coefficients such that generic equilibrium states with constant magnetic fields are stable and causal under linearised perturbations. We solve these constraints for a specific equation of state and show that there exists a large family of hydrodynamic frames that renders the linear fluctuations stable and causal. This theory does not require introducing new dynamical degrees of freedom and therefore is a promising and simpler alternative to Müller-Israel-Stewart-type theories. Together with a detailed analysis of transport, entropy production and Kubo formulae, the theory presented here is well suited for studying dissipative effects in various contexts ranging from heavy-ion collisions to astrophysics.

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is the theory describing the long-wavelength dynamics of dynamical electromagnetic fields coupled to matter at finite temperature. The broad interest in MHD stems from the fact that it is the standard theory describing aspects of plasma physics

Hydrodynamics is an effective theory that can be constructed order-by-order in a gradient expansion, constrained by symmetry and the second law of thermodynamics. MHD is not an exception. In the past years, MHD has been formulated using these same principles under the usual assumption that the fluid/plasma is electrically neutral at hydrodynamic length scales and electric fields are Debye screened

The purpose of this paper is to provide a family of frames that renders first order dissipative MHD causal and stable. This is not a trivial task due to the sheer number of transport coefficients involved and the different excitations/modes present in the system. Parity-violating MHD, without the presence of a conserved particle/baryon number, is characterised by 19 transport coefficients in the Landau frame (once microscopic CPT symmetry is imposed)

However, while keeping track of 7 coefficients may appear simple, when studying MHD in a general frame in this setting, we are required to work with 28 transport coefficients. In addition, we need to require stability and causality for three types of mode excitations (Alfvén waves, fast and slow magnetosonic waves) due to inherent anisotropy. Despite the increased difficulty of MHD compared to earlier similar studies of relativistic hydrodynamics, we are able to provide a method by which the desired family of frames can be obtained for a given equation of state. We expect this result to significantly contribute to the understanding of heavy-ion collisions, accretion disc dynamics and plasma physics.

2.1
Equations of motion

MHD describes the coupling of Maxwell’s equations to thermal degrees of freedom of matter. We can describe this coupling in terms of the conservation of a stress tensor
T^{\mu\nu}
that includes both fluid and electromagnetic fields together with Maxwell equations in the presence of an external current
J^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}
such that

\nabla_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=F^{\mu\rho}J_{\rho}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ J^{\mu}+J^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \nabla_{[\mu}F_{\nu\lambda]}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(2.1)

where the electromagnetic current

is decomposed into a Maxwell part and a matter part such that
J^{\mu}=\nabla_{\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+J^{\mu}_{\text{matter}}
where
J^{\mu}_{\text{matter}}
is the current associated with matter fields and
F_{\mu\nu}
is the electromagnetic field strength which we can decompose as

F_{\mu\nu}=2u_{[\mu}E_{\nu]}-\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u^{\rho}B^{\sigma}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(2.2)

where

are the electric and magnetic fields respectively,

is the four velocity and

is the Levi-Civita tensor. Note that in (

[

The system of equations (

T\sim u^{\mu}\sim B^{\mu}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)

J^{\mu}_{\text{ext}}\sim\mathcal{O}(\partial)
which, generically, is equivalent to
\nu\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
and
q\sim E^{\mu}\sim\mathcal{O}(\partial)

Frame transformations

\varepsilon_{i},\chi_{i},k_{i},\varrho_{i},n_{i}
to zero or by a particular frame transformation that we specify in App.
B
.

2.4
Entropy inequalities and microscopic CPT

The coefficients appearing above are constrained by entropy production and microscopic CPT symmetry. In order to address the former, we consider the canonical entropy current

S^{\mu}=su^{\mu}-\frac{1}{T}T^{\mu\nu}_{(1)}u_{\nu}-\frac{\mu}{T}J^{\mu\nu}_{(1)}h_{\nu}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(2.18)

and demand
\nabla_{\mu}S^{\mu}\geq 0
for all physical configurations. By
physical configurations
we mean fluid configurations that satisfy the equations of motion (
2.4
). By doing so we obtain the same constraints as those found in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
but in terms of specific combinations of transport coefficients, in particular

\bm{\zeta}_{\perp},\bm{\zeta}_{||},\bm{r}_{\perp},\bm{\eta}_{\perp},\bm{\eta}_{||},\bm{r}_{||},\bm{r}_{\perp}\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{\zeta}_{\perp}\bm{\zeta}_{||}\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\bm{\zeta}_{\times}+\bm{\zeta}_{\times}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(2.19)

where we have defined

\begin{split}&\bm{\eta}_{\perp}=\eta_{\perp}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{r}_{||}=r_{||}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{\eta}_{||}=\tilde{\ell}_{1}-\mu\tilde{\ell}_{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{r}_{\perp}=\tilde{m}_{1}-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon+P}\tilde{m}_{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\bm{\zeta}_{\perp}=\tilde{f}_{1}-T\frac{\partial P}{\partial\epsilon}|_{\rho}\penalty\ \tilde{f}_{3}-\frac{1}{T}\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}|_{\epsilon}\penalty\ \tilde{f}_{4}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\bm{\zeta}_{\times}=\tilde{f}_{2}-T\frac{\left(P-\mu\rho\right)}{\partial\epsilon}|_{\rho}\penalty\ \tilde{f}_{3}-\frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{\left(P-\mu\rho\right)}{\partial\rho}|_{\epsilon}+\mu\right)\tilde{f}_{4}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\bm{\zeta}_{\times}^{{}^{\prime}}=\tilde{\tau}_{1}-T\frac{\partial P}{\partial\epsilon}|_{\rho}\tilde{\tau}_{3}-\frac{1}{T}\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}|_{\epsilon}\tilde{\tau}_{4}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{\zeta}_{||}=\tilde{\tau}_{2}-T\frac{\left(P-\mu\rho\right)}{\partial\epsilon}|_{\rho}\tilde{\tau}_{3}-\frac{1}{T}\left(\frac{\left(P-\mu\rho\right)}{\partial\rho}|_{\epsilon}+\mu\right)\tilde{\tau}_{4}\penalty\ \penalty\ .\end{split}

(2.20)

Each of these coefficients (in bold) is built out of the frame invariants (
2.17
). The entropy current imposes inequalities in 8 transport coefficients but does not constrain the remaining ones.

To understand the constraints imposed by microscopic CPT invariance we compute the Kubo formulae by perturbing the background sources
g_{\mu\nu}
and
b_{\mu\nu}
with plane wave profiles with non-zero frequency
\omega
and vanishing momentum. Defining

\delta\mathbb{T}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}G_{TT}^{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}\delta g_{\lambda\rho}+\frac{1}{2}G_{TJ}^{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}\delta b_{\lambda\rho}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \delta\mathbb{J}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}G_{JT}^{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}\delta g_{\lambda\rho}+\frac{1}{2}G_{JJ}^{\mu\nu,\lambda\rho}\delta b_{\lambda\rho}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(2.21)

where we have introduced the one point functions
\mathbb{T}^{\mu\nu}=\sqrt{-g}\langle T^{\mu\nu}\rangle
and
\mathbb{J}^{\mu\nu}=\sqrt{-g}\langle J^{\mu\nu}\rangle
(see
[
16
]
), we explicitly extract the non-zero Green’s functions

\begin{split}&\lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{ii,ii}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{\eta}_{\perp}+\bm{\zeta}_{\perp}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{ii,zz}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{\zeta}_{\times}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{zz,ii}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{\zeta}_{\times}^{{}^{\prime}}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{zz,zz}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{\zeta}_{||}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{ii,jj}-G_{TT}^{ii,ii}}{2(-i\,\omega)}\right)=-\bm{\eta}_{\perp}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{xy,xy}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{\eta}_{\perp}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{TT}^{iz,iz}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{\eta}_{||}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{JJ}^{xy,xy}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{r}_{\parallel}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lim_{\omega\to 0}\left(\frac{G_{JJ}^{iz,iz}}{-i\omega}\right)=\bm{r}_{\perp}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\end{split}

(2.22)

where we have introduced flat cartesian coordinates
(t,x,y,z)
and the index
i=x,y
. Given these Kubo formulae, Onsager’s relations require that

\bm{\zeta}_{\times}=\bm{\zeta}_{\times}^{{}^{\prime}}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(2.23)

As can be noticed, only the combinations of transport coefficients appearing in (
2.20
) enter the Kubo formulae. Therefore, only these coefficients are strictly hydrodynamic, while other coefficients, which will be required to make the formulation of MHD presented here stable and causal are non-hydrodynamic. As we will show in the next section, several of the coefficients will have to satisfy certain constrains due to causality and stability of the equilibrium state.

3

|
Constraints from stability and causality

In this section we find the necessary conditions on the various transport coefficients introduced in (
2.10
) in order for the system of equations (
2.4
) to yield a causal and stable evolution for linear perturbations of a generic equilibrium state. We begin by defining the equilibrium state, the different classes of perturbations that we consider and the criteria we employ for stability and causality. In the next section we present a detailed analysis of the constrains implied by these criteria on the several transport coefficients for a particular plasma.

3.1
Equilibrium state, perturbations, causality and stability

Equilibrium states of MHD have been extensively discussed in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. The most general equilibrium state is characterised by the vanishing of (
2.11
) together with the no-monopole constraint

\delta_{B}g_{\mu\nu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \delta_{B}b_{\mu\nu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \nabla_{\mu}\left(T\rho h^{\mu}\right)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.1)

The first condition above implies that such states are characterised by the existence of a Killing vector field
K^{\mu}
such that
u^{\mu}=K^{\mu}/|K|
where
|K|=|-g_{\mu\nu}K^{\mu}K^{\nu}|^{1/2}
. In turn the temperature is given by
T=T_{0}/|K|
where
T_{0}
is a constant. The second and third conditions in (
3.1
) are non-trivial and express the degeneracy of the equilibrium state very akin to the degeneracy encountered in the context of superfluids. In the present setting, this degeneracy expresses the fact that there are many possible magnetic field configurations in thermal equilibrium.
6
6

6

From a more formal point of view, this degeneracy is rooted in the possible equilibrium profiles for the magnetic scalar potential
\varphi
that we introduce in App.
A
.
We focus on flat backgrounds without external currents, in particular

g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ b_{\mu\nu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(3.2)

with coordinates
(t,x,y,z)
and we take the fluid to be at rest
u^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
as well as thermodynamic parameters to be constant (e.g.
T=T_{0}
,
\mu=\mu_{0}
). In addition, we take
h^{\mu}=(0,0,0,1)
. This is the most generic equilibrium in which
h^{\mu}
is aligned with a single spatial isometry of the background (in this case flat spacetime). It is possible to find other equilibrium configurations for which the direction of the magnetic field
h^{\mu}
is not aligned with a spatial isometry (e.g. rotating magnetised plasmas/stars
[
15
]
). Such configurations involve fluid velocities and magnetic fields that depend on the spacetime coordinates but which locally are described by the equilibrium configurations that we consider here. It would be interesting to consider such configurations but for simplicity we take
h^{\mu}=(0,0,0,1)
.
7
7

7

This is similar to the approach taken in
[
30
,
32
]
in which the analysis assumes equilibrium configurations with constant fluid velocities.
We also discuss equilibrium states at finite velocity
\bm{\beta}
, which can be obtained by performing a Lorentz boost (see App.
C
). In addition, the equilibrium states considered here satisfy the following inequalities

\begin{split}&(\epsilon+p)>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mu\rho>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ s>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ T>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}=\chi\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\frac{V^{2}}{\chi}=T\left(\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}+\mu\left(\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}=cT^{2}+2\mu T\lambda+\mu^{2}\chi\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\frac{U^{2}}{T}=\left(\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}-\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}\left(\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}=T(c\chi-\lambda^{2})\geq 0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\end{split}

(3.3)

where we have dropped the subscript
0
in all equilibrium thermodynamic quantities for simplicity and defined

c=\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lambda=\left(\frac{\partial s}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T}=\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.4)

The last set of equalities in (
3.4
) arises from the fact that both the entropy
s
and the "charge density"
\rho
in equilibrium are determined in terms of the pressure
p
. The last three inequalities in (
3.3
) can be derived from general thermodynamic considerations (see App.
A
). However, such inequalities can also be obtained by studying the constraints arising from stability and causality of the excitations of the system, as we will comment throughout.

Given the equilibrium state, we subsequently consider linear perturbations around it of the plane wave form. Specifically

u^{\mu}\rightarrow u^{\mu}+e^{ik\cdot x}\,\delta u^{\mu}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ h^{\mu}\rightarrow h^{\mu}+e^{ik\cdot x}\,\delta h^{\mu}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mu\rightarrow\mu+e^{ik\cdot x}\,\delta\mu\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ T\rightarrow T+e^{ik\cdot x}\,\delta T\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(3.5)

where we have parameterised the four wave vector as
k^{\mu}=(\omega,k\sin\theta,0,k\cos\theta)
for frequency
\omega
and wavenumber
k
and where
\theta
is an angle between the direction of the background magnetic field and the waves’ momentum. Introducing these perturbations in (
2.4
) leads to a characteristic equation of the form
F(\omega,k,\bm{\beta})=0
whose solutions
\omega(k,\bm{\beta})
are the excitations/modes of the system. These perturbations can be decomposed into two channels. The Alfvén channel is defined via the constraints on perturbations

\delta\mu=\delta T=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ u_{\mu}\delta h^{\mu}=0\penalty\ \Rightarrow h_{\mu}\delta u^{\mu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ k_{\mu}\delta u^{\mu}=k_{\mu}\delta h^{\mu}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.6)

In this case the characteristic equation
F(\omega,k,\bm{\beta})=0
yields 2 gapless modes and 2 gapped modes (i.e. modes for which
\omega(k\to 0)\neq 0
). In turn the magnetosonic channel is defined by non-vanishing
\delta T
and
\delta\mu
and by amplitudes
\delta u^{\mu}
and
\delta h^{\mu}
that lie in the subspace spanned by
\{u^{\mu},\,h^{\mu},\,k^{\mu}\}
(see e.g.
[
12
,
5
]
). In this channel, we find 4 gapless modes and 5 gapped modes at low wavenumber
k
.

Our goal in this work is to study the spectrum of excitations arising from these linear perturbations and make sure that the transport coefficients appearing in (
2.10
) are constrained in such a way as not to lead to instabilities of the equilibrium state or to violations of causality. To that aim, we adopt the same criteria as in
[
30
,
32
]
, in particular

\text{Im}\left(\omega(k)\right)<0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ 1>\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\frac{\text{Re}\left(\omega(k)\right)}{k}\right|>0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.7)

The first condition in (
3.7
) ensures stability of the perturbations while the second condition ensures causality (i.e. signals do not propagate faster than the speed of light).
8
8

8

See
[
37
]
for a discussion of causality criteria.
It should be noted that the second condition involves the limit
k\to\infty
and the first condition must be imposed for all
k
. In other words, we are aiming at having a well defined evolution even for very short wavelengths, that is, beyond the traditional regime of validity of hydrodynamics (i.e. the small
k
regime).
In practice, the constrains can be quite unwieldy so the strategy we adopt here is to first study the constraints in the small and large
k
regimes and then use those constraints to simplify the analysis at arbitrary
k
. We approach both channels separately.

3.2
Constraints in the Alfvén channel

We wish to find a fluid frame that is stable and causal for any boost parameter
\bm{\beta}
. However, in the present case explicit results for any
\bm{\beta}
are cumbersome. In addition, Ref.
[
31
]
has established a theorem stating that
9
9

9

See sec. VI of
[
31
]
for an explicit statement of the theorem.
, for isotropic fluids without magnetic fields, whenever the linearised equations of motion around an equilibrium state with
\bm{\beta}=0
are stable and causal, they remain so for
\bm{\beta}\neq 0
.
10
10

10

Notice that these conditions are not met for the Landau frame, as was shown for instance in the case of an uncharged fluid in ref.
[
30
]
. The Landau frame with
\bm{\beta}=0
is stable but not causal.
Even though we have not proven this in the context of MHD, we expect it to hold and we explicitly verify this to be the case for the specific plasma of Sec.
4
. Hence, we can limit our considerations to the rest frame, as this is sufficient to ensure stability and causality for
\bm{\beta}\neq 0
in both channels. We thus begin by studying the small
k
regime for
\bm{\beta}=0
. In this case, the characteristic equation leads to two gapless modes and two gapped modes of the form

\begin{split}&\omega=\pm\mathcal{V}_{A}k\cos\theta-\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{\bm{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}}{\epsilon+p}+\bm{\mathcal{T}_{r}}\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\omega=i\frac{\rho}{\mu\,n_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \omega=i\frac{(\epsilon+p)}{k_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\end{split}

(3.8)

where we have defined

\begin{split}&\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}=\frac{\mu\rho}{\epsilon+p}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}=\bm{\eta}_{||}\cos^{2}\theta+\bm{\eta}_{\perp}\sin^{2}\theta\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{\mathcal{T}}_{r}=\bm{r}_{\perp}\cos^{2}\theta+\bm{r}_{||}\sin^{2}\theta\penalty\ \penalty\ .\end{split}

(3.9)

In particular
\mathcal{V}_{A}
denotes the velocity of Alfvén waves. At ideal order, stability implies that
\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}>0
which is compatible with (
3.3
). The form of the gapped modes in (
3.8
) implies that in the Landau frame, in which
n_{2}=k_{2}=0
, the criteria for stability in (
3.7
) leads to instabilities of the equilibrium state. As such, the Landau frame, similarly to previous cases studied in
[
30
]
, is not the appropriate frame for a well defined initial value problem. From (
3.8
), (
3.3
) and the criteria (
3.7
) one concludes that the necessary conditions for the gapped modes to be stable are

k_{2}<0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\quad n_{2}<0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.10)

The form of the gapless modes in (
3.8
) agrees with that of
[
12
]
in the Landau frame, and their stability in the hydrodynamic regime is guaranteed by the fact that
\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{r}>0
and
\bm{\mathcal{T}}_{\eta}>0
due to the entropy constraints (
2.19
) and the conditions (
3.3
) on the equilibrium state.

Let us now study the fluid at rest but at arbitrary wave number
k
. To this aim we consider the quantity
\Delta=i\omega
, determined by the zero of the characteristic function

\begin{split}F(\Delta,\bm{k},\bm{\beta}=0)=&\left[k^{2}\mathcal{T}_{\eta}+\Delta(\epsilon+p-k_{2}\Delta)\right]\left[\mu k^{2}\mathcal{T}_{r}+\Delta(\rho-n_{2}\Delta\mu)\right]\\
&+k^{2}\mu\left[\rho-(k_{1}+\ell_{2})\Delta\right]\left[\rho-(m_{2}+n_{1})\Delta\right]\cos^{2}\theta\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\end{split}

(3.11)

where we have defined

\mathcal{T}_{\eta}=\ell_{1}\cos^{2}\theta+\eta_{\perp}\sin^{2}\theta\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{T}_{r}=m_{1}\cos^{2}\theta+r_{||}\sin^{2}\theta\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.12)

More specifically,
\Delta
is the solution of a quartic algebraic equation
\Delta^{4}+A^{(k)}_{3}\Delta^{3}+A^{(k)}_{2}\Delta^{2}+A^{(k)}_{1}\Delta+A^{(k)}_{0}=0
. We record the explicit expression for the coefficients

\begin{split}A^{(k)}_{3}&=-\left(\frac{\rho}{n_{2}\mu}+\frac{\mu\rho}{\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}k_{2}}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
A^{(k)}_{2}&=\frac{\rho}{n_{2}\mu}\frac{\mu\rho}{\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}k_{2}}-k^{2}\left[\frac{\mathcal{T}_{r}}{n_{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}{k_{2}}-\frac{(k_{1}+\ell_{2})(m_{2}+n_{1})}{k_{2}n_{2}}\cos^{2}\theta\right]\\
A^{(k)}_{1}&=k^{2}\left[\frac{\mathcal{T}_{r}}{n_{2}}\frac{\mu\rho}{\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}k_{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}{k_{2}}\frac{\rho}{n_{2}\mu}-\rho\frac{(k_{1}+\ell_{2}+m_{2}+n_{1})}{k_{2}n_{2}}\cos^{2}\theta\right]\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
A^{(k)}_{0}&=\frac{\rho}{n_{2}\mu}\frac{\mu\rho}{\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}k_{2}}k^{2}\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+k^{4}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{r}}{n_{2}}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}{k_{2}}\penalty\ \penalty\ .\end{split}

(3.13)

We have to study stability and causality for this polynomial for
k>0
. The stability conditions follow from the Routh-Hurwitz theorem, which for the polynomial at hand amounts to demand

A^{(k)}_{3}>0\penalty\ ,\penalty\ A^{(k)}_{0}>0\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \quad\left(A^{(k)}_{3}A^{(k)}_{2}-A^{(k)}_{1}\right)A^{(k)}_{1}-\left(A^{(k)}_{3}\right)^{2}A^{(k)}_{0}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.14)

Even though eq. (
3.10
) presents necessary conditions for the modes to be stable, stability must be imposed at arbitrary values of
k
, as well as for every angle
\theta
. From the conditions in (
3.14
) we see that the first is already ensured by (
3.10
) and (
3.3
) while the second implies that

\mathcal{T}_{r}\mathcal{T}_{\eta}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ \Rightarrow\penalty\ \penalty\ \ell_{1}m_{1}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \ell_{1}r_{||}+\eta_{\perp}m_{1}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(3.15)

where we have used the entropy constraints
\eta_{\perp}r_{||}>0
. It is unwieldy to deduce constraints on the last condition in (
3.14
) but a necessary condition is that
A^{(k)}_{1}>0
which can be satisfied by imposing
\mathcal{T}_{r}>0
and
\mathcal{T}_{\eta}>0
in turn implying
m_{1},\ell_{1}>0
.

The causality conditions applies at short-wavelengths
k\to\infty
according to eq. (
3.7
). In this limit the dispersion relations becomes linear
\omega=Wk+\mathcal{O}(k^{0})
and

W^{2}
is determined by the second order equation
W^{4}+A_{2}^{(\infty)}W^{2}+A_{0}^{(\infty)}=0
, with

A_{2}^{(\infty)}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{r}}{n_{2}}+\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}{k_{2}}-\frac{(k_{1}+\ell_{2})(m_{2}+n_{1})}{k_{2}n_{2}}\cos^{2}\theta\penalty\ ,\penalty\ A^{(\infty)}_{0}=\frac{\mathcal{T}_{r}}{n_{2}}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{\eta}}{k_{2}}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.16)

Given that
W
is the phase velocity, one must have
1>W^{2}\geq 0
, which follows after imposing

\left(A^{(\infty)}_{2}\right)^{2}-4A^{(\infty)}_{0}>0\penalty\ ,\penalty\ A^{(\infty)}_{2}<0\penalty\ ,\penalty\ A^{(\infty)}_{0}+A^{(\infty)}_{2}+1>0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.17)

The first of these conditions ensures that
W
is real, and thus
W^{2}>0
, whereas the other two conditions are meant to prevent superluminal propagation of Alfvén modes. It is possible to solve the conditions (
3.17
) by a suitable choice of frame. In particular choosing
k_{1}=-\ell_{2}
or
m_{2}=-n_{1}
eliminates the dependence on
\theta
from
A_{2}^{(\infty)}
. The inequalities (
3.17
) can then
be solved by requiring

\mathcal{T}_{r}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{T}_{\eta}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{T}_{r}k_{2}+n_{2}\mathcal{T}_{\eta}+\mathcal{T}_{r}\mathcal{T}_{\eta}+n_{2}k_{2}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(3.18)

which in turn lead to sufficient conditions

m_{1}\ell_{1}+k_{2}m_{1}+\ell_{1}n_{2}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \eta_{\perp}r_{||}+k_{2}r_{||}+n_{2}\eta_{\perp}>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \eta_{\perp}(m_{1}-r_{||})+\ell_{1}(r_{||}-m_{1})>0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.19)

We note that even though the conditions (
3.18
) were derived for the specific frame
k_{1}=-\ell_{2}
or
m_{2}=-n_{1}
, they are actually necessary conditions since causality at
\theta=\pi/2
enforces them.

To summarize, in the Alfvén channel the stability conditions at arbitrary
k
and
\theta
and for a generic equation of state are given by eqs. (
3.10
) and (
3.14
) while the causality conditions at arbitrary
\theta
and for a generic equation of state are given in eq. (
3.17
).

3.3
Constraints in the magnetosonic channel

As in the Alfvén channel, we study the system in the rest frame
\bm{\beta}=0
and at small
\bm{k}
. In this limit one finds four gapless modes, corresponding to the polarizations of the fast and slow magnetosonic waves

\omega=\pm v_{M}\,k-\frac{i}{2}\mathcal{\tau}k^{2}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(3.20)

where the phase velocity reads

v_{M}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2})\cos^{2}\theta+\mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right]\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left[(\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}-\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2})\cos^{2}\theta+\mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right]^{2}+4\mathcal{V}^{4}\cos^{2}\theta\sin^{2}\theta}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(3.21)

and where we have defined

\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2}=\frac{s\chi}{T(c\chi-\lambda^{2})}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}=\frac{s^{2}\chi+c\rho^{2}-2s\rho\lambda}{(\epsilon+p)(c\chi-\lambda^{2})}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{V}^{4}=\frac{s(\rho\lambda-s\chi)^{2}}{T(c\chi-\lambda^{2})^{2}(\epsilon+p)}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.22)

The expression (
3.21
) agrees with that obtained in
[
12
]
in the Landau frame. Reality of the phase velocity implies
\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2}>0
and
\mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}>0
which is consistent with the thermodynamic assumptions (
3.3
). In addition,
\mathcal{V}^{4}>0
is implied by (
3.3
). The signs appearing in (
3.21
) characterise the fast (
+
) and slow (
-
) magnetosonic modes. The diffusion rate
\tau
satisfies
\tau>0
given the entropy constraints (
2.19
). The expression for
\tau
is lengthy but it simplifies for specific choices of angles, e.g.

\begin{split}&\tau(v_{M}=0,\theta=\pi/2)=\frac{1}{sT}\left[\bm{\eta}_{\parallel}+\frac{(\epsilon+p)\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2}}{\chi\mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}}\bm{r}_{\perp}\right]\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\tau(v_{M}=\mathcal{V}_{s},\theta=\pi/2)=\frac{1}{sT}\left[(1-\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2})(\bm{\eta}_{\perp}+\bm{\zeta}_{\perp})+\frac{\mathcal{V}_{0}^{2}}{\chi\mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}}[(\epsilon+p)-\mathcal{V}_{s}^{2}(cT^{2}+2\mu T\lambda+\mu^{2}\chi)]\bm{r}_{\perp}\right]\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\tau(v_{M}=\mathcal{V}_{A},\theta=0)=\frac{(1-\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2})}{sT}\bm{\eta}_{\parallel}+\frac{\mu}{\rho}\bm{r}_{\perp}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
&\tau(v_{M}=\mathcal{V}_{0},\theta=0)=\frac{1}{sT}\bm{\zeta}_{\parallel}\penalty\ \penalty\ .\end{split}

(3.23)

Additionally, there are five different gapped modes, which read

\begin{split}\omega&=i\frac{\rho}{\mu n_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \omega=i\frac{\epsilon+p}{k_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\\
\omega&=i\frac{sT}{\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}\rho T}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \omega=i\,\frac{g\pm\sqrt{g^{2}+4T^{4}(\varepsilon_{4}\varrho_{3}-\varepsilon_{3}\varrho_{4})(c\chi-\lambda^{2})}}{2T(\varepsilon_{4}\varrho_{3}-\varepsilon_{3}\varrho_{4})}+\mathcal{O}(k^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\end{split}

(3.24)

where we have defined
g=T\lambda(T^{2}\varrho_{3}+\varepsilon_{4}-2\mu\varrho_{4})-T^{2}\chi(\varepsilon_{3}-\mu\varrho_{3})+\mu\chi(\varepsilon_{4}-\mu\varrho_{4})-cT^{2}\varrho_{4}
. Note that, as in the Alfvén channel, (
3.24
) predicts that the equilibrium state is unstable in the Landau frame. Stability of the gapped modes requires that the
\mathcal{O}(k^{0})
terms in (
3.24
) are strictly negative. This implies that we must have

n_{2}<0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ k_{2}<0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ (\chi_{1}+\chi_{2}\rho T)<0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ g>0\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ (\varepsilon_{4}\varrho_{3}-\varepsilon_{3}\varrho_{4})<0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.25)

A general investigation of stability and causality in this channel is more involved compared to the Alfvén channel. The spectral function is in general a
9
th-degree polynomial for
\Delta=i\omega
of the kind

P_{9}(\Delta)=\Delta^{9}+\sum_{n=0}^{8}B_{n}^{(k)}\Delta^{n}=0\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(3.26)

The expressions for the coefficients are exceedingly cumbersome, and in general they are combination of ratios among the transport parameters and thermodynamics functions, together with trigonometric functions of the angle
\theta
and organised in powers in the momentum
k
. The stability conditions arising from this polynomial are detailed in app.
D
. In general, as the polynomial does not factorise at arbitrary angle, an analytic analysis of the constraints is out of reach and a numerical analysis is necessary.

Causality must be imposed on this channel. Similarly to the Alfvén channel, in the small wavelength regime
k\to\infty
the dispersion relations becomes linear
\omega=Wk+\mathcal{O}(k^{0})
, and the polynomial
P_{9}(\Delta)
in eq. (
3.26
) reduces to a
4
th-degree polynomial in
W^{2}

P^{(\infty)}_{4}(W^{2})=W^{8}+B^{(\infty)}_{7}W^{6}+B^{(\infty)}_{5}W^{4}+B^{(\infty)}_{3}W^{2}+B^{(\infty)}_{1},

(3.27)

where the coefficient
B^{(\infty)}_{n}
is defined as the leading order coefficient in a large
k
expansion of the generic coefficient
B^{(k)}_{n}
in eq. (
3.26
). In order to derive causality conditions we ensure Schur stability, which is detailed in app.
D
. The conditions arising from this polynomial must be satisfied for every angle
\theta
.

To summarize, in the magnetosonic channel the stability and causality conditions at arbitrary
k
and
\theta
and for a generic equation of state are given by eqs. (
3.25
) together with the general expressions provided in app.
D
, in particular conditions (
D.2
), (
D.5
) and (
D.6
). We will now study the constraints in detail in order to find a stable and causal class of frames for a specific plasma.

Discussion

In this work we formulated MHD in a general frame at first order in gradients, under the assumptions of parity-invariance and discrete charge symmetry, and determined general constraints on transport coefficients that render the theory linearly stable and causal for generic equilibrium states with constant magnetic fields. As these constraints are unwieldy analytically due to the sheer number of transport coefficients involved, our strategy consisted in providing a method by which for a given plasma (with equation of state and transport properties given by Kubo formulae (
2.22
)) one can scan parameter space numerically for suitable frames. Once a choice of stable and causal frame is identified, the model is fixed and one can proceed with analysing it in different contexts (e.g. by choosing different types of initial data and study their evolution).

We applied the method outlined above to a specific equation of state arising from a holographic toy model
[
5
]
in sec.
4
and found that besides the 7 transport coefficients obtained via Kubo formulae, an additional 11 parameters are needed to ensure stable and causal linear perturbations at every angle
\theta
.

12

12

Our analysis of the equation of state of
[
5
]
was performed in the weak field limit in which signatures of anisotropy are small but significant. It would be interesting to also explore the strong field limit in which features of anisotropy are more salient.
We have not explored the region of parameter space in full generality and as such it is possible that causality and stability could be enforced with a reduced number of additional coefficients. Regardless, our work shows that applying the BDNK method
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
32
,
31
]
to MHD can be accomplished successfully.

\mathcal{O}(\partial)
. The constraints we obtained are nevertheless required but it is possible that when including the correction
\mathcal{O}(\partial)
these constraints become stricter.
The model presented here can be used for studying various processes of interest in astrophysics and heavy-ion collisions and presents an interesting alternative to second order MHD theories such as
[
38

.

Developing a systematic numerical method for scanning parameter space and identifying suitable frames would be useful, in particular when addressing more complicated scenarios.

Appendix A
Details on one-form hydrodynamics

In this appendix we give further details on one-form hydrodynamics following
[
16
]
. The comments and clarifications made here are not necessary for understanding the core of the paper. We begin with a discussion of the equilibrium partition function and then summarise the transformations of the relevant MHD fields under various discrete symmetries.

A.1
Equilibrium partition function

We consider one-form hydrodynamics coupled to a background metric
g_{\mu\nu}
and a two-form gauge field
b_{\mu\nu}
. These background fields transform under infinitesimal diffeomorphisms parametrised by
\chi^{\mu}
and one-form gauge transformations parametrised by
\Lambda^{\chi}_{\mu}
according to

\delta_{\mathfrak{X}}g_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{\chi}g_{\mu\nu}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \delta_{\mathfrak{X}}b_{\mu\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{\chi}b_{\mu\nu}+2\partial_{[\mu}\Lambda^{\chi}_{\nu]}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(A.1)

where
\mathfrak{X}=(\chi^{\mu},\Lambda^{\chi}_{\mu})
and where
\mathcal{L}_{\chi}
denotes the Lie derivative along
\chi^{\mu}
. In turn, the hydrodynamic fields are described by the set
B=(\beta^{\mu},\Lambda^{\beta}_{\mu})
. These fields can be expressed in terms of the more usual hydrodynamic fields, in particular fluid velocity
u^{\mu}
, temperature
T
and one-form chemical potential
\mu_{\mu}
according to

\beta^{\mu}=\frac{u^{\mu}}{T}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \Lambda^{\beta}_{\mu}+\beta^{\nu}b_{\nu\mu}=\frac{\mu_{\mu}}{T}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(A.2)

However, it is straightforward to see that
\mu_{\mu}
is not invariant under one-form gauge transformations. In order to define a gauge-invariant one-form chemical potential it is required to include another dynamical field, in particular a scalar "Goldstone"
\varphi
akin to a magnetic scalar potential that transforms according to

\delta_{\mathfrak{X}}\varphi=\mathcal{L}_{\chi}\varphi-\beta^{\mu}\Lambda_{\mu}^{\chi}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(A.3)

This is equivalent to state that the one-form symmetry is spontaneously broken along the fluid velocity
u^{\mu}

[
15
,
16
]
.
Given this transformation, we can define a gauge-invariant vector such that

\mu h_{\mu}=\mu_{\mu}-T\partial_{\mu}\varphi\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(A.4)

where
h^{\mu}
is normalised such that
h^{\mu}h_{\mu}=1
. We note that the one-form chemical potential
\mu_{\mu}
encodes both the chemical potential
\mu
and the vector
h^{\mu}
. It may be observed that
h^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-T^{2}\delta_{B}\varphi/\mu
and hence
u^{\mu}
and
h_{\mu}
are not orthogonal at this point. However, it may be shown, using the adiabaticity equation (see
[
16
]
) that
\delta_{B}\varphi=\mathcal{O}(\partial)
. One is free to use the redefinition freedom associated with
\mu_{\mu}
, namely
\mu_{\mu}\to\mu_{\mu}+\delta\mu_{\mu}
to set
\delta_{B}\varphi=0
at all orders, which is a type of Josephson condition. This means that we are free to take
h^{\mu}u_{\mu}=0
as an exact all order statement. This is the choice we made throughout this paper, which leads to the physical interpretation of the fluid field variables
\mu
and
h^{\mu}
being related to the strength and the direction of the magnetic field lines respectively. However we note that it is possible not to use this redefinition freedom and to consider an even larger family of MHD frames.

Given these considerations we now discuss aspects of equilibrium partition functions in one-form hydrodynamics. The on-shell equilibrium partition function, that is the equilibrium partition function in the grand canonical ensemble for which
\delta_{B}\varphi=0
and
\varphi
obeys the constraint
\nabla_{\mu}\left(T\rho h^{\mu}\right)=0
, can be written as

\mathcal{Z}[g_{\mu\nu},b_{\mu\nu}]=\text{exp}\int_{\Sigma}d\sigma_{\mu}\left(T^{\mu\nu}K_{\nu}+(\Lambda^{K}_{\nu}+K^{\lambda}b_{\lambda\nu})J^{\mu\nu}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(A.5)

where
K^{\mu}
is a Killing vector field and
\Lambda^{K}_{\mu}
is such that
\delta_{B}g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{B}b_{\mu\nu}=0
. In addition,
\Sigma
denotes an arbitrary Cauchy slice with volume element
d\sigma_{\mu}
. For the specific equilibrium states that we consider in Sec.
3.1
with
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}
and
b_{\mu\nu}=0
we have that
K^{\mu}=\delta^{\mu}_{t}/T_{0}
and
\Lambda_{\mu}^{K}=\mu_{0}/T_{0}\delta_{\mu}^{z}
where
T_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
denote the constant global temperature and chemical potential respectively. In the case of these global thermal states, and given that all first order corrections (
2.10
) vanish in equilibrium, the grand canonical partition function becomes

\mathcal{Z}[g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu},b_{\mu\nu}=0]=\text{exp}\int_{\Sigma}d\sigma_{\mu}u^{\mu}\frac{1}{T_{0}}\left(-\varepsilon_{0}+\mu_{0}\rho_{0}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(A.6)

which is the same as encountered in the context of zero-form hydrodynamics with energy
\varepsilon_{0}
, chemical potential
\mu_{0}
and “particle number"
\rho_{0}
. We note that
\mu_{0}
is the value of the z-component of the one-form chemical potential
\mu_{\mu}
for equilibrium configurations with
h^{\mu}=(0,0,0,1)

\Delta\varepsilon^{2}=\langle\varepsilon^{2}\rangle-\langle\varepsilon\rangle^{2}>0
,
\Delta\rho^{2}>0
and
\Delta(\varepsilon\rho)^{2}>0

In addition we note that the partition function (

below. This is due to the fact it it only depends on
T
and
\mu

\mathcal{Z}[g_{\mu\nu},b_{\mu\nu}]=\int\mathcal{D}\varphi\exp\left(-S^{\text{HS}}[g_{\mu\nu},b_{\mu\nu};\varphi]\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(A.7)

where

S^{\text{HS}}[g_{\mu\nu},b_{\mu\nu};\varphi]=\int_{\Sigma}d\sigma_{\mu}N^{\mu}_{\text{HS}}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

For one-form fluids describing MHD, the component of the free energy current along
u^{\mu}
takes the form

\begin{split}u_{\mu}N^{\mu}_{\text{HS}}=-\frac{1}{T}\big(&p-\frac{\alpha}{6}e^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u_{\mu}H_{\nu\rho\sigma}-\beta e^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u_{\mu}h_{\nu}\partial_{\rho}u_{\sigma}-\tilde{\beta}_{1}h^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}T\\

(A.9)

where

.

In particular,

&t_{i}=-\left(\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial T}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial\mu}\omega_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\right)\penalty\ \penalty\ .\end{split}

Appendix C
Equilibrium states with finite spatial velocity

In the bulk of the paper we also discuss states with finite constant velocity. Such states can be obtained by performing a Lorentz boost, with boost velocity
\bm{\beta}
, starting from equilibrium configurations with zero velocity, as introduced in Sec.
3.1
. Similarly, the dispersion relations found throughout Sec.
3
for configurations with zero velocity can be "boosted" to dispersion relations characterising states with finite velocity. To wit, the fluid velocity for a state with vanishing spatial velocity is related to the fluid velocity of the state with non-vanishing spatial velocity by a Lorentz transformation

u^{\mu}={\Lambda^{\mu}}_{\nu}u^{\prime\nu}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ with }\penalty\ \penalty\ u^{\prime\mu}=\gamma(1,\bm{\beta})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\bm{\beta}^{2}}}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(C.1)

where
{\Lambda^{\mu}}_{\nu}
is a matrix in SO(1,3),
\bm{\beta}^{2}
is the modulus of the boost velocity squared and
u^{\prime\mu}
is the fluid velocity for the state with non-vanishing spatial velocity. Analogously, we introduce the wave vector
k^{\prime\mu}
and direction of magnetic field lines
h^{\prime\mu}
in the state with non-vanishing spatial velocity such that

k^{\prime\mu}=(\omega^{\prime},\bm{k}^{\prime})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ h^{\prime\mu}=(h_{t}^{\prime},\bm{h}^{\prime})\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(C.2)

These quantities are related to the respective quantities in the frame with vanishing velocity
(\omega,\bm{k},h_{t},\bm{h})
according to the following relations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\omega=\gamma(\omega^{\prime}-\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime})\\
&\bm{k}=\bm{k}^{\prime}+\gamma\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime}\right)\bm{\beta}\end{cases}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&h_{t}=0=\gamma(h_{t}^{\prime}-\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{h}^{\prime})\\
&\bm{h}=\bm{h}^{\prime}+\gamma\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{h}^{\prime}-h_{t}^{\prime}\right)\bm{\beta}\end{cases}\penalty\ \penalty\ .

(C.3)

The original rest frame configuration satisfies
u^{\mu}h_{\mu}=0
. To ensure that this is the case in the new (boosted) frame it is sufficient to require that
\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{h^{\prime}}=0
. Eq. (
C.3
) then implies that
h_{t}^{\prime}=0
and
\bm{h^{\prime}}=\bm{h}
. As a consequence, for this class of frames one has that

\bm{k}\cdot\bm{h}\equiv k\cos\theta=\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{h}=\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{h}^{\prime}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(C.4)

where
k
is given as a function of

and

(C.5)

Therefore, in order to obtain dispersion relations for states with non-vanishing constant spatial velocity, it is sufficient to make the following substitutions

\displaystyle\omega\longrightarrow\gamma(\omega^{\prime}-\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

(C.6)

\displaystyle\bm{k}\longrightarrow\bm{k}^{\prime}+\gamma\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime}\right)\bm{\beta}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

\displaystyle k^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\longrightarrow\left[k^{\prime 2}+2\gamma\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime}\right)\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime}+\gamma^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\bm{\beta}\cdot\bm{k}^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime}\right)^{2}\beta^{2}\right]\cos^{2}\theta\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

\displaystyle\bm{h}\rightarrow\bm{h}^{\prime}\penalty\ \penalty\ ,

in all expressions obtained for states with
\bm{\beta}=0
