Sextic polynomials in two variables do not represent all non-negative integers
Stanley Yao Xiao

Abstract
. In this paper we prove that for all degree 6 6 polynomials with rational coefficients that F ⁡ ( ℤ 2 ) ≠ ℤ ≥ 0 F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\neq{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0} . The answers a question of B. Poonen and J. S. Lew in the degree 6 case. This work builds on previous work with S. Yamagishi but does not subsume it.



1.
Introduction

The study of representation of integers by bivariate polynomials go back to ancient times, at least to the time of Bhramagupta who proved in the 7th century that the product of two numbers representable as a sum of two squares is again a sum of two squares. Fermat had stated, without proof, that the odd primes which are sums of two squares are precisely those which are congruent to
1
modulo
4
. This assertion was proved for the first time by Euler nearly a century later.

Legendre, Lagrange, and Gauss investigated sums of more than two squares. Legendre proved that every number not of the form
4^{m}(8n+7)
can be written as a sum of three squares, and Lagrange famously proved that every natural number is the sum of four integer squares. Gauss proved that every natural number is the sum of three triangular numbers.

Motivated by these classical theorems, B. Poonen asked on the popular website MathOverflow
[
9
]
whether there exists a polynomial
F
in two variables satisfying
F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})={\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. It turns out that this question was asked much earlier by J. S. Lew
[
7
]
.

In
[
14
]
we addressed the Lew–Poonen question in the case of degree
4
polynomials in two variables, giving a negative answer. The goal of the present paper is to show that the answer for degree six polynomials is also negative. Clearly, to answer the Ponnen-Lew question it suffices to show that for all polynomials
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
and integers
D_{1},D_{2}
with
D_{1}>0
, one has
F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\neq D_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}+D_{2}
. To simplify the exposition, let us give the following definition:

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
F(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
is
arithmetically complete
if there exists a positive integer
D_{1}
and an integer
D_{2}
such that
F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})=D_{1}{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}+D_{2}
.

Our main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

There is no degree 6 polynomial defined over the integers in two variables which is arithmetically complete.

As in
[
14
]
, for a subset
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
we define

{\mathcal{R}}_{F}(\mathfrak{B})=\{n\in\mathfrak{B}:\exists(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\text{ s.t. }F(x,y)=n\}.

R. Stanley had asked about the asymptotic behaviour of the size of the set
{\mathcal{R}}_{F}([N,2N))
when
F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
. In particular he asked whether the upper bound

(1.1)

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}([N,2N))=O_{F}\left(\frac{N}{\sqrt{\log N}}\right)

always holds. In
[
14
]
we answered Stanley’s question in the affirmative for degree 4 polynomials. Our proof of Theorem
1.2
will also answer Stanley’s question in the affirmative for degree six polynomials.

Theorem 1.3
.

For all degree six polynomials
F
satisfying
F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
we have

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}([N,2N))=O_{F}\left(\frac{N}{\sqrt{\log N}}\right).

In the case of binary forms, the question of estimating
R_{F}(N)=\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}([-N,N])
has been the subject of intense study, going back to the important work of Mahler
[
8
]
and Erdös–Mahler
[
5
]
. In
[
11
]
, C. L. Stewart and I proved that whenever
F
has non-zero discriminant and
\deg F\geq 3
there exists a positive number
C(F)
such that

R_{F}(N)\sim C(F)N^{\frac{2}{d}}.

In the case of degree 2, the seminal work of Landau
[
6
]
and Bernays
[
1
]
proved that whenever
F
is an irreducible binary quadratic form that there exists a positive number
C(F)
such that

R_{F}(N)\sim C(F)\frac{N}{\sqrt{\log N}}.

We will now discuss the approach taken to prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

1.1.
Outline of proof

The plan of attack in the present work is very similar to
[
14
]
, but the exposition is different. We will again consider the decomposition of a generic sextic polynomial into homogeneous components, namely

F(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{6}F_{j}(x,y),

with each
F_{j}(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
and homogeneous of degree
j
. Further, in
[
14
]
we essentially showed that all polynomials in two variables fail to be arithmetically complete for one of two reasons. To make this precise, we will make two definitions:

Definition 1.4
.

We say that a polynomial
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
is
arithmetically positive
if there exists an integer
D
such that
F({\mathbb{Z}}^{2})\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq D}
.

Definition 1.5
.

We say that a polynomial
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
is
dearth
if we have the asymptotic relation

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}([-N,N])=o_{F}(N).

We note immediately that
F
is arithmetically complete only if it is arithmetically positive and it is not dearth.

In
[
14
]
, we showed that every quartic polynomial in two variables either fails to be arithmetically positive, meaning that
\inf_{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}F(x,y)=-\infty
, or
F
is dearth. Further, in
[
14
]
, it is shown that the most difficult case corresponds to those
F
(of degree
4
) taking the shape

F(x,y)=H(x,y)^{2r}G_{4}(x,y)+H(x,y)G_{3}(x,y)+F_{2}(x,y)+F_{1}(x,y),

the so-called
quadratically composed
polynomials. Here our situation is quite different. Indeed, the difficult case in the degree six situation is quite distinct from the one encountered in
[
14
]
and requires a different set of tools and ideas to handle. We emphasize that the ideas introduced in this paper do not subsume the ones in
[
14
]
. The main new ingredient, introduced to us by J. Rouse
[
13
]
, is Lemma
5.4
.

Our proof will be achieved through the following propositions:

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
be a degree six polynomial. Suppose that
F_{6}
is square-free as a polynomial over an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
F
is arithmetically complete.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
be a degree six polynomial. Suppose that
F_{6}
is divisible by the square of a binary form but not a cube over an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
F
is not arithmetically complete.

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
be a degree six polynomial. Suppose that
F_{6}
is divisible by the fourth power of a binary form but not a fifth power over an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
F
is not arithmetically complete.

Proposition 1.9
.

Let
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
be a degree six polynomial. Suppose that
F_{6}
a perfect 6th power over an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
F
is not arithmetically complete.

Plainly, Propositions
1.6
to
1.9
exhaust all possible degree six polynomials in two variables defined over the integers, and thus implies Theorem
1.2
.

Propositions
1.6
and
1.8
are essentially elementary, with Propositions
1.7
and
1.9
being more difficult to prove. The difficulty in the proofs of Propositions
1.7
and
1.9
is dealing with a type of polynomial similar to an example given by T. Tao in
[
9
]
. In particular, he stated that
1

f(x,y)=(x^{2}-y^{3}-y)^{4}-y+C
for some large constant
C
already looks very tough to analyse.” - T. Tao,

the polynomial

(1.2)

f(x,y)=(x^{2}-y^{3}-y)^{4}-y+C,C\in{\mathbb{Z}}

looks difficult to analyse. This is similar to the most difficult case that needs to be dealt with for the proof of Proposition

. Such polynomials take the form

(1.3)

F(x,y)=a(y^{2}-x^{3}-Ax-B)^{2}+g(x,y)

where
\deg g\leq 2
.

The main insight needed to tackle this difficult case differs depending on whether

or
A\neq 0
. In the former case, a Pell-type identity due to L. V. Danilov

addressing Hall’s conjecture is enough to show that such polynomials cannot be arithmetically complete. When

.

2.
Preliminary lemmas and proof of Proposition
1.6

We recall the following lemmata from
[
14
]
:

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose there exists a polynomial
\mathfrak{F}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[t]
of degree
d\geq 2
and
G\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x,y]
such that
F=\mathfrak{F}\circ G
. Then

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}([N,2N])\ll N^{\frac{1}{d}}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
F_{6}
is positive definite. Then

\#\mathcal{R}_{F}([N,2N])\ll N^{\frac{1}{3}}.

Proof.

Since
F_{6}
is positive definite, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

F_{6}(x,y)\geq c\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{6}

for all
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
It is clear that

F(x,y)=F_{6}(x,y)+O(\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{5}).

Let
N
be a sufficiently large positive integer.
Then we obtain

\displaystyle\#\mathcal{R}_{F}([N,2N])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:N\leq F(x,y)\leq 2N\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\#\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\max\{|x|,|y|\}\ll N^{\frac{1}{6}}\right\}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle N^{\frac{1}{3}}.

∎

Thus, Proposition
1.6
is proved in the case when
F_{6}
is positive definite. The indefinite case follows from another lemma in
[
14
]
:

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
F_{6}
is negative semi-definite or indefinite. Then

\inf_{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}F(x,y)=-\infty.

Proof.

Since
F_{6}
is negative definite or indefinite, there exists
(x_{0},y_{0})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that

F_{6}(x_{0},y_{0})<0.

It is clear that

F(Nx_{0},Ny_{0})=N^{6}F_{6}(x_{0},y_{0})+O(N^{5}),

and the result follows.
∎

The proof of Proposition
1.6
follows from the observation that a square-free sextic form
F_{6}
is either positive definite, negative definite, or indefinite.

3.
Proof of Proposition
1.7

Next we address the problem when
F_{6}
is divisible by the square of a binary form but not a
4
-th power, say
F_{6}(x,y)=f(x,y)^{2}G_{6}(x,y)
with
f
irreducible and
G_{6}
co-prime to
f
. Note that
\deg f\leq 6/2=3
. We may further suppose, by Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
, that
F_{6}
is positive semi-definite but not definite.

We will require the following adaptation of Lemma 3.1 from
[
14
]
:

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
F_{6}
is positive semi-definite and
\gcd(F_{6},F_{5})=1
.
Then

\inf_{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}F(x,y)=-\infty.

Proof.

We begin by noting that the hypotheses of the statement ensure that
F_{5}
is not the zero polynomial.
Let
(\cos(\xi),\sin(\xi))
be a real zero of
F_{6}
, which exists by the assumption that
F_{6}
is positive semi-definite but not definite. We then write

\mathfrak{L}(x,y)=\sin(\xi)x-\cos(\xi)y

and

F_{6}(x,y)=\mathfrak{L}(x,y)^{2r}\mathfrak{F}_{6}(x,y),

where
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathfrak{L}\nmid\mathfrak{F}_{6}
. In particular, since
\gcd(F_{6},F_{5})=1
, we have
\mathfrak{L}\nmid F_{5}
.
By Dirichlet’s theorem on Diophantine approximation,
there exist infinitely many
(u,v)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
satisfying

|\sin(\xi)u-\cos(\xi)v|\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}}.

For these
(u,v)
, we have

F_{6}(u,v)=\mathfrak{L}(u,v)^{2r}\mathfrak{F}_{6}(u,v)\ll(u^{2}+v^{2})^{-r}(u^{2}+v^{2})^{2-r}\ll(u^{2}+v^{2})^{2-2r}\ll 1,

|F_{5}(u,v)|\gg(u^{2}+v^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad|F_{4}(u,v)+\cdots+F_{1}(u,v)|\ll\left(u^{2}+v^{2}\right)^{2}.

Therefore, we obtain

F(u,v)=F_{5}(u,v)+O\left(u^{4}+v^{4}\right).

By replacing
(u,v)
with
(-u,-v)
if necessary, it follows that

F(u,v)<0\quad\textnormal{and}\quad|F(u,v)|\gg(u^{2}+v^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}},

provided
\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}
is sufficiently large, and the result follows.
∎

Next we shall need:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\delta>0
, and for a subset
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
put

{\mathcal{R}}_{F}^{(\delta)}(\mathfrak{B})=\left\{n\in\mathfrak{B}:\exists(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\text{ s.t. }F(x,y)=n\text{ and }F(x,y)\gg_{\delta}\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{1+\delta}\right\}.

Then there exists
\kappa=\kappa(\delta)>0
such that

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}^{(\delta)}([N,2N))=o_{\delta}\left(N^{1-\kappa(\delta)}\right).

Proof.

Suppose that
F(x,y)=n
with
N\leq n<2N
and
F(x,y)\gg_{\delta}\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{1+\delta}
. It then follows that

\max\{|x|,|y|\}\ll_{\delta}N^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}.

It thus follows that

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}^{(\delta)}([N,2N))\ll_{\delta}\#\left\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:\max\{|x|,|y|\}\ll_{\delta}N^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}\right\}=O\left(N^{\frac{2}{1+\delta}}\right).

If
\frac{2}{1+\delta}<1
, then we may take
\kappa=2/(1+\delta)
. Thus we may assume that
\delta\leq 1
, and we consider the set

{\mathcal{R}}_{F}^{(\delta,\ast)}([N,2N))=

\left\{n\in[N,2N):\exists(x,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\text{ s.t. }F(x,y)=n\text{ and }\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{1+\delta}\ll_{\delta}F(x,y)\ll\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{2}\right\}.

The upper bound condition and the fact that
\deg F=6
implies that
F_{6}(x,y)\ll\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{2}
. In particular, for a real linear factor
\ell(x,y)
of
F_{6}
we must have
|\ell(x,y)|=O(1)
. Thus,
y
is determined up to a constant factor given
x
. Then the condition

\max\{|x|,|y|\}^{1+\delta}\ll_{\delta}N

implies that there are
O_{\delta}\left(N^{1/(1+\delta)}\right)
choices for
x
. It follows that

\#{\mathcal{R}}_{F}^{(\delta,\ast)}([N,2N))=O_{\delta}\left(N^{\frac{1}{1+\delta}}\right)

and the claim follows.

∎

Let
\ell

f

\gcd(\ell,{\mathcal{F}}_{6})=1

.

Suppose

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

Q_{2}

Q(x,y)=Q_{2}(x+q_{1},y+q_{2})+q_{3}.

Next we treat the separate cases when
F_{6}

.

When

In this case

4.
Proof of Proposition
1.8

Next we address the problem when
F_{6}
is divisible by a perfect 4-th power, but not a fifth. Then the divisor must in fact be defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and up to a
\operatorname{GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
-change of variables, we may assume that the divisor is
x^{4}
. That is, we assume that

F_{6}(x,y)=x^{4}f_{6}(x,y),\deg f_{6}=2.

Suppose that
x^{2}\nmid F_{5}
. Now suppose that
|x|\asymp T^{\theta},|y|\asymp T
for

we find that
