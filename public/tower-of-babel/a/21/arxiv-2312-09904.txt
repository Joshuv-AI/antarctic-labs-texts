Gabriel’s Theorem for Infinite Quivers
Nathaniel Gallup, Stephen Sawin

Abstract
We prove a version of Gabriel’s theorem for (possibly infinite dimensional) representations of infinite quivers. More precisely, we show that the representation theory of quiver Ω \Omega is of unique type (each dimension vector has at most one associated indecomposable) and infinite Krull-Schmidt (every, possibly infinite dimensional, representation is a direct sum of indecomposables) if and only if Ω \Omega is eventually outward and of generalized ADE Dynkin type ( A n A_{n} , D n D_{n} , E 6 E_{6} , E 7 E_{7} , E 8 E_{8} , A ∞ A_{\infty} , A ∞ , ∞ A_{\infty,\infty} , or D ∞ D_{\infty} ). Furthermore we define an analog of the Euler-Tits form on the space of eventually constant infinite roots and show that a quiver is of generalized ADE Dynkin type if and only if this form is positive definite. In this case the indecomposables are all locally finite-dimensional and eventually constant and correspond bijectively to the positive roots (i.e. those of length 1 1 ).



1
Introduction

Gabriel’s famous theorem (
[
Gab72
]
) states that a quiver
\Omega
is of finite-type (has finitely many isomorphism classes of indecomposable representations) if and only if it is of ADE Dynkin type (the underlying graph of
\Omega
is one of the ADE Dykin diagrams
A_{n},D_{n},E_{6},E_{7},E_{8}
). In this case, the function which sends the isomorphism class of a representation
V
of
\Omega
to its dimension vector (the function which assigns to every vertex
i
the dimension of
V_{i}
) gives a bijection between the isomorphism classes of indecomposable representations and the dimension vectors whose length is
1
with respect to the Tits form
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}_{0}}n_{i}^{2}-\sum_{a\in\Omega^{\prime}_{1}}n_{s(a)}n_{t(a)}
. In particular this shows that the representation category of an ADE Dynkin quiver is of
unique type
as well, meaning each dimension vector has at most one indecomposable associated to it.

The category
\text{Reps}_{\text{f}}(\Omega)
of finite-dimensional representations of any finite quiver
\Omega
is clearly Krull-Schmidt (every object is uniquely a finite direct sum of indecomposables). However Ringel (
[
Rin16
]
) showed that infinite-dimensional representations of ADE Dynkin quivers can also uniquely be written as a (possibly infinite) direct sum of indecomposables (we then call the category
\text{Reps}(\Omega)
of possibly infinite-dimensional representations of
\Omega

infinite Krull-Schmidt
).

The situation for infinite quivers is complicated by the fact that it is no longer obvious that even locally finite-dimensional representations are infinite Krull-Schmidt. However, in
[
BLP11
]
, Bautista, Liu, and Paquette showed that this is the case for locally finite-dimensional representations of certain infinite quivers including an eventually outward
A_{\infty,\infty}
quiver and identified the indecomposables as being in bijection with the connected full subquivers (as is true in the
A_{n}
case as well).

In
[
GS22
]
, we simultaneously weakened the finiteness hypotheses of
[
Rin16
]
and
[
BLP11
]
, and showed that for an eventually outward
A_{\infty,\infty}
quiver
\Omega

\text{Reps}(\Omega)
is of unique type and infinite Krull-Schmidt. We furthermore showed that again the indecomposables in this case are FLEI (meaning locally finite-dimensional and isomorphic on all but finitely many arrows) and in bijection with the connected full subquivers. We also gave an example of a representation of an
A_{\infty,\infty}
quiver which is not eventually outward whose category of representations is not infinite Krull-Schmidt. In this paper, we prove the following analogous result for
D_{\infty}
.

Theorem A

The representation theory
\text{Reps}(\Omega)
of any eventually outward
D_{\infty}
quiver
\Omega
is of unique type and infinite Krull-Schmidt. Taking dimension vectors gives a bijection between the indecomposables of
\Omega
(which are all FLEI) and the positive roots of
\Omega
(by which we mean functions
n
from
\Omega_{0}
to
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
which are constant along a cofinite set of arrows and have the property that the directed limit
\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}
taken over certain finite subquivers
\Omega^{\prime}
is equal to
1
).

We combine this with our
A_{\infty,\infty}
result from
[
GS22
]
to prove the following version of Gabriel’s theorem for infinite quivers.

Theorem B
(Gabriel’s Theorem for Infinite Quivers)

the representation theory
\text{Reps}(\Omega)
of a connected quiver
\Omega
is of unique type infinite Krull-Schmidt if and only if
\Omega
is of generalized ADE Dynkin type and eventually outward. In this case all indecomposables of
\Omega
are FLEI and in bijection with the positive roots of
\Omega
.

A vertex

For

A

is

if it is union of finitely many journeys. It is called

if every journey contains at most finitely-many arrows that point towards the source of those journeys.

A representation

of
\Omega
is an assignment

of a vector space (over a fixed field which will be denoted by

) to each vertex

and an assignment of a map

. Vector spaces will not be assumed to be finite-dimensional unless specified. A morphism

. We say that

of

if every vector space

Recall that a category is

if every object can be written as a possibly infinite direct sum of indecomposable objects, and given two such decompositions there is a bijection between the two sets of summands which takes each indecomposable to an isomorphic indecomposable. Note that if the object is
isotypic
, that is to say all the indecomposables in the summand are isomorphic, then the bijection exists as long as the cardinalities are the same.

FLEI Representations

Definition 1

A representation

of a quiver
\Omega

l

e

if for each vertex

is finite-dimensional and there are only finitely many arrows

\Omega

is not an isomorphism. More generally, if
\Omega^{\prime}
is a full subquiver of

\Omega^{\prime}

is an isomorphism, we say that

is

\Omega^{\prime}
.

whose objects are FLEI, and
\operatorname{rep}_{\text{FLEI}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})

\Omega^{\prime}
. Write
\operatorname{indec}_{\text{FLEI}}(\Omega)
for the set of equivalence classes of indecomposable objects in

Definition 2

If
\Omega
is a quiver and

is a full subquiver, we say that

.

Notice that if

\Omega^{\prime}
(called the

\Omega^{\prime}
to

Proposition 3.1

Let
\Omega
be a connected, eventually outward, finitely branched tree quiver.

1.

Every subrepresentation and quotient representation of a FLEI representation is FLEI.

2.

Finite sums of FLEI representations are FLEI.

3.

The category of FLEI representations is Krull-Schmidt.

Proof:

1.

Let
V
be a FLEI representation of
\Omega
and let
0\to W\to V\to X\to 0
be a subrepresentation and a quotient. At each vertex
i
we have
0\to W(i)\to V(i)\to X(i)\to 0
so clearly
W
and
X
are locally finite-dimensional. If
V(a)
is an isomorphism then
W(a)
is injective, and
X(a)
is surjective, so
W(a)
and
X(a)
are injective/surjective for all but finitely many
a
. Since
\Omega
is a finitely branched tree it is a union of journeys
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}
. Each journey
p_{l}
contains finitely many arrows with
X(a)
not surjective, and by eventually outward only finitely many arrows that point towards the start of the journey, so each contains an infinite oriented path
p
in which each arrow is surjective for
X
. Since
X(s(p))
is finite-dimensional and the dimensions of the spaces assigned to vertices in
p
are nonincreasing, all but finitely many arrows must leave the dimension constant, and therefore for all but finitely many arrows
a
in
p
,
X(a)
is an isomorphism, and hence
W(a)
is an isomorphism. So for all but finitely many arrows
a
in
\Omega
,
W(a)
and
X(a)
are isomorphisms. Hence
W
and
X
are FLEI.

2.

Suppose
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}
are FLEI. Clearly their sum is locally finite-dimensional. On the other hand since there are only finitely many arrows that are nonisomorphic for each
V_{m}
, there are only finitely many for which any
V_{m}
are nonisomorphic, so the sum is FLEI.

3.

If
V
is FLEI, it is supported on some finite subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
. We know that locally finite-dimensional representations on a finite quiver are Krull-Schmidt, so
\mathscr{F}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(V)
can be written as a finite direct sum of indecomposables and hence by Lemma
3.1
so can
V\in\operatorname{rep}_{\text{FLEI}}(\Omega,\Omega^{\prime})
. By Lemma
3.2
this writes
V
as a finite direct sum of indecomposables in
\operatorname{rep}_{\text{FLEI}}(\Omega)
and thus in
\operatorname{rep}(\Omega).

\diamond

The hypotheses on

are all necessary, at least for the first part (we could relax connected to having finitely many components clearly). If

is not, nor is the quotient by it. Finally, if

has infinitely many vertices all connected by an arrow to a single sink (not finitely branching), the representation with all vector spaces having dimension

and all maps being isomorphisms is FLEI, but the quotient by the subrepresentation with only the sink having positive dimension is not (reversing the arrows gives an example where the subrepresentation is not FLEI).

Proposition 5.1

Proposition 5.2

Proof:

It suffices by Proposition

6
Poset Filtration of Subrepresentations

In this section the quiver is a tree (only necessary for Lemma
6.5
), but we assume that the FLEI indecomposables form a well-founded partial order under the ordering of Section
3
.

When we speak in the following of a (possibly infinite) sum of subrepresentations of a quiver representation, we mean the subrepresentation that assigns to each vertex all finite linear combinations of vectors in individual subrepresentations at that vertex. Recall a set
S
of subrepresentations of some
V
is independent if and only if for every finite subset
W\in S
,
W\cap\sum_{U\in S\setminus\{W\}}U=0
. Thus if
S
is independent
\sum_{U\in S}U
is isomorphic to the direct sum
\bigoplus_{U\in S}U
.

Remark 6.1

Independence is purely finite in the sense that if
S
is a dependent set of subrepresentations (i.e. not independent), then there exists a finite subset
\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\}\subset S
which is dependent, and indeed if we have written each
U_{k}=\sum_{j}W_{k,j}
as a sum of subrepresentations, we can choose for each
i
a
U_{i}^{\prime}
which is a finite sum of the
W_{i,j}
so that
\{U^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,U^{\prime}_{n}\}
is dependent. Also, since independence implies independence at each vertex, if
S
is dependent and is a disjoint union of
T
and
T^{\prime}
, then either
T
,
T^{\prime}
or
\{\sum_{U\in T}U,\sum_{U\in T^{\prime}}U\}
is dependent.

For the balance of this section assume all representations are subrepresentations of some fixed representation
V
of
\Omega
. So for instance below if
\beta
is an isomorphism class of indecomposable FLEI representations then
\sum_{X\in\alpha<\beta}X
is the sum of all suprepresentations of
V
that are in some isomorphism class
\alpha
of indecomposables with

.

Definition 7

Let
V
be a representation of
\Omega
. Define for each
\beta\in\operatorname{indec}_{\text{FLEI}}(\Omega)
,
F^{\beta}=\sum_{X\in\alpha\leq\beta}X
and

.

The following proposition is then immediate.

Proposition 6.1

The map
\beta\mapsto F^{\beta}
is order-preserving.

Lemma 6.1

If
U,V\in\alpha
are indecomposable FLEI representations of a quiver
\Omega
and there is a nonzero map

then

is an isomorphism.

Proof:

The image of

is a FLEI submodule of

by Proposition
3.1
which is nonzero by hypothesis, and is therefore a direct sum of nonzero indecomposable FLEI modules. Let
W\in\beta
be one of these modules. Then the composition of

with the projection onto

is nonzero, hence
\alpha\geq\beta
but the inclusion of

into

is also nonzero, hence
\beta\geq\alpha
. Therefore
\beta=\alpha
, so

and yet

. Since

is locally finite-dimensional,

so the direct sum has one module in it, and

is surjective. Again because

and

are locally finite-dimensional, this implies

\diamond

Lemma 6.2

Let

be a representation.

1.

If

are submodules with

then there exists an independent family of nonzero submodules

2.

Suppose that

(i.e. a set of subrepresentations of

\beta

1.

By hypothesis

is FLEI, hence by part (2) of Proposition

\sum_{j=1}^{J}U_{j}
is also FLEI and by part (3) is therefore a finite direct sum of indecomposable FLEI submodules, say

by definition. If

is an isomorphism by Lemma

. The final claim holds because for each

is the projection map which is nonzero because

is, hence one of the

2.

Assume that

is dependent. Then by Remark

, we can assume

is finite, and replace

with some

which is a finite sum of elements of

where

is a direct sum of elements of
\beta
and

is a direct sum of elements of

is

to

surjective. Thus by local dimension count

must be a direct sum of at most

subrepresentations isomorphic to

. If it is exactly

, then again by dimension count the map

to

is a bijection. In that case

is independent, so

is dependent. But the map from

\diamond

Proposition 6.2

For each
\beta\in\text{FLEI}(\Omega)
there is a subrepresentation

such that

is a direct sum of elements of
\beta

.

Proof:

Let

be the set of subrepresentations of

which are in
\beta
, and place a well-order on

. We prove by transfinite induction the claim that for each

there exists a subrepresentation

of

, either equal to

or to

, with the properties that the family

.

Suppose the claim is satisfied for all

and

part (2), so either

Suppose not. Since each

Notice that

Lemma 6.4

For each
\beta
we have

.

Proof:

Suppose this is not true. Then by well-foundedness there is some

which is minimal among the set of indecomposable FLEI subrepresentations which do not satisfy the desired condition. Then by Lemma

we have that

. But

and by minimality of

. Hence

\diamond

Lemma 6.5

If
V
is a representation then
V=\sum_{\beta}F_{\beta}
.

Proof:

If not, there is a vertex

of
\Omega
so that
\sum_{\beta}F_{\beta}(i)
is a proper subset of

, which is to say there is an

with

not in
\sum_{\beta}F_{\beta}(i)
. We build an indecomposable FLEI subrepresentation

that contains

recursively, so that each

has dimension

or

. First, let

be the span of

. Then if

is one-dimensional and there is an arrow

, then define

which is either one-dimensional or

\diamond

Theorem 1

If
V
is a representation of
\Omega
and the poset of indecomposable FLEI subrepresentations of
V
is well-founded, then
V=\bigoplus_{\alpha}G_{\alpha}
. In particular every such representation can be written uniquely up to isomorphism and reordering, as a direct sum of indecomposable FLEI modules, and therefore the category of representations of a quiver with this property is infinite Krull-Schmidt.

Proof:

By Lemmas
6.5
and
6.4
,
V=\sum_{\beta}G_{\beta}
and by Lemma
6.3

V=\bigoplus_{\beta}G_{\beta}
. Since each
G_{\beta}
is a direct sum of indecomposable FLEI, so is
V
.

Suppose now that
V=\bigoplus_{\beta}H_{\beta}
where each
H_{\beta}
is a direct sum of subrepresentions in
\beta
. Then by definition of
F_{\beta}
each
H_{\beta}\subset F_{\beta}
, and the projection of
F_{\beta}
onto
H_{\alpha}
for
\alpha>\beta
is zero by the definition of the ordering. So for each
\beta
,
F_{\beta}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\leq\beta}H_{\alpha}
and therefore
H_{\beta}\cong F_{\beta}/F_{<\beta}\cong G_{\beta}.
If
H_{\beta}
is isomorphic to
G_{\beta}
, by dimension count at vertices they must have the same cardinality of indecomposable summands, so the uniqueness property of infinite Krull-Schmidt follows.

\diamond

.

(resp. each component of

).

.

components and is not IC, then there exist vertices

in

to

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\Omega^{\prime}_{0}}n_{i}m_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in\Omega^{\prime}_{1}}\left(n_{s(a)}m_{t(a)}+n_{t(a)}m_{s(a)}\right)

(8)

\Omega^{\prime}
not in
\Omega^{n}
must have either its source or target not in
\Omega^{n}
because
\Omega^{n}
is a full subquiver by definition. Hence this also contributes only a zero term to the Tits form, so we obtain the desired equality.

\diamond

We will use Lemma

to show
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{n}}
. Define
\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)
to be the set of all finite subquivers
\Omega^{\prime}
whose intersection with
\Omega^{n}
is IC in
\Omega^{n}
, which is clearly cofinal in the directed set of finite subquivers and hence a directed set by Lemma
7.1
. Since
n
is in the root space, there exists a finite
n
-anticonstant subquiver. By Lemma
7.1
we can extend this to be IC, and again by Lemma
7.1
we can extend it to
\Omega^{*}
so that its intersection with
\Omega^{n}
is IC in
\Omega^{n}
(it will still be IC in
\Omega
because we did not add any new components). Define
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{n}(\Omega)
to be the subsets of
\mathcal{I}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)
respectively of subquivers containing
\Omega^{*}
. It is easy to see that the following equations hold (in fact they true for any net in any topological space).

\displaystyle\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

(9)

\displaystyle\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{n}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

Lemma 7.4

Given an element
n
of the root space of a quiver
\Omega
, the set
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is a stabilizing subset of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{n}(\Omega)
for the Tits form.

Proof:

First we need to prove that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{n}(\Omega)
, that is if
\Omega^{\prime}
is IC and contains
\Omega^{*}
then its intersection with
\Omega^{n}
is IC in
\Omega^{n}
. Second we need to prove that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is stabilizing, that is if
\Omega^{\prime}
has an IC intersection with
\Omega^{n}
and contains
\Omega^{*}
, it is contained in an
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
that is IC and has intersection with
\Omega^{n}
that is IC in
\Omega^{n}
and agrees with
\Omega^{\prime}
on the Tits form.

For the first suppose it is not the case that
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
is IC in
\Omega^{n}
. Then there exist
i,j\in\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
with a path connecting them in
\Omega^{n}
but no path connecting them in
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
. In particular since
\Omega^{\prime}
is IC there is a path connecting them in
\Omega^{\prime}
but not one in
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
. We can assume that
i
and
j
are minimal with this property in that there is a path
p
connecting them in
\Omega^{\prime}
with no vertices in the path in
\Omega^{n}
except the endpoints. That means that
n_{i}
and
n_{j}
are nonzero but
n
is equal to zero on every vertex in between on
p
. That means that the arrows joining
i
and
j
to the path are not constant (if
p
consisted of just one arrow connecting
i
and
j
then it would be in
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
since
\Omega^{n}
is full) and therefore are in
\Omega^{*}
. That means that
i
and
j
are in
\Omega^{*}\cap\Omega^{n}
and since this is IC in
\Omega^{n}
there is a path in
\Omega^{*}\cap\Omega^{n}
, which is a contradiction.

For the second, consider any two vertices
i,j
in
\Omega^{\prime}
that are connected in
\Omega
by a path that does not include vertices in
\Omega^{\prime}
other than the endpoints. Thus no arrows in the path are in
\Omega^{*}
and therefore
n
is constant along this path. If the constant value is nonzero then the entire path is in
\Omega^{n}
and so since
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
is IC as a subquiver of
\Omega^{n}
, there is a path connecting
i
and
j
in
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}
. Otherwise, all vertices in the path are not in
\Omega^{n}
an we add all those vertices to
\Omega^{\prime}
. We repeat this until no such vertices
i
and
j
remain, and this process terminates because each time we add a path, it decreases the number of components of
\Omega^{\prime}
which is finite. The result is
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
which is IC and contains
\Omega^{*}
and has the same intersection with
\Omega^{n}
as
\Omega^{\prime}
. Therefore
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and by Lemma
7.3

\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}
.

\diamond

Proposition 7.1

For all
\Omega
and
n
,
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{n}}
, that is to say if one exists the other does and in that case their values are equal.

Proof:

By Equation
9
we have

\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

By Lemmas
7.4
and
7.2
we obtain

\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\prime}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

and again by Equation
9
it follows that

\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}^{\prime}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

Thus
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}
.
Finally, consider the map from
\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)
to
\mathcal{I}(\Omega^{n})
, given by intersection. It is order preserving, onto, by Lemma
7.3
preserves the Tits form, and has the property that for any
\Omega^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\Omega^{n})
and
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)
with
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime\prime}
and
\Lambda^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}=\Omega^{\prime}
, there exists
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)
with
\Lambda^{\prime}\subseteq\Lambda^{\prime\prime}
and
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}=\Omega^{\prime\prime}
. Thus it follows that

\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}(\Omega)}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}=\lim_{\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\Omega^{n})}\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}

and the proposition is proven.

\diamond

Now that we can calculate the Tits form on
\Omega^{n}
, we can determine when it converges to a finite or infinite value.

Lemma 7.5

Let
\Omega
be a quiver and
\Omega^{\prime}\subsetneq\Omega^{\prime\prime}
finite IC subquivers. Then there exists a sequence of finite IC subquivers
\Omega^{\prime}=\Lambda^{0}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq\Lambda^{\ell}=\Omega^{\prime\prime}
such that
\Lambda^{p+1}
can be obtained from
\Lambda^{p}
by one of the following moves: (1) add a vertex not in
\Lambda^{p}
and an arrow connecting it to a vertex that is in
\Lambda^{p}
, (2) add an arrow not in
\Lambda^{p}
between two vertices which are in the same component of
\Lambda^{p}
, or (3) add a vertex in a component of
\Omega
which does not contain any vertices from
\Lambda^{p}
.

1.

If
\Omega^{\prime}
is a BC subquiver, then move (3) cannot occur.

2.

If
\Omega^{\prime}
contains all cycles of
\Omega
then move (2) cannot occur.

Proof:

We proceed by induction on the total number of edges and vertices in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
not in
\Omega^{\prime}
. If
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
has an arrow not in
\Omega^{\prime}
whose removal does not create a new component (leaves it as IC), then it comes via a move (2) from an IC subquiver that inductively can be built from moves 1-3. If
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
contains a vertex with no arrows, it is necessarily in a distinct component of
\Omega
and comes via a move (1) from an IC subquiver that inductively can be built from moves 1-3. Suppose that neither such a redundant arrow, nor an isolated vertex exists. Then
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
must contain an arrow not in
\Omega^{\prime}
, the removal of which separates
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
into two pieces, one of which contains no elements of
\Omega^{\prime}
(since
\Omega^{\prime}
is IC) and therefore must be a tree (since otherwise an arrow of the first type above would exist). This implies that an arrow whose removal minimizes the number of arrows in that second piece will be a leaf whose removal leaves an isolated vertex, and therefore it comes via a move 1 from an IC subquiver that inductively can be built from moves 1-3.

1.

If
\Omega^{\prime}
is a BC subquiver, then any IC subquiver containing it is also BC and therefore there are no components of
\Omega
which do not contain vertices from any
\Lambda^{p}
.

2.

If
\Omega^{\prime}
contains all cycles of
\Omega
, then no cycle of
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
contains any arrow not in
\Omega^{\prime}
, so it is not possible to add an arrow through move (2).

\diamond

Lemma 7.6

Suppose
n
is supported on
\Omega
(i.e.
\Omega=\Omega^{n}
) and anticonstant on some finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
which is contained in another finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
.

1.

If
\Omega^{\prime}
is a BC subquiver then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\leq\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}
with the inequality strict if
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
contains a cycle not in
\Omega^{\prime}
.

2.

If
\Omega^{\prime}
contains all cycles of
\Omega
then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\geq\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}
with the inequality strict if
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
has more components than
\Omega^{\prime}
.

Proof:

By Lemma
7.5
there exists a sequence of finite IC subquivers
\Omega^{\prime}=\Lambda^{0}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq\Lambda^{\ell}=\Omega^{\prime\prime}
such that
\Lambda^{p+1}
can be obtained from
\Lambda^{p}
by a move of type (1), (2), or (3). The fact that
n
is anticonstant on
\Omega^{\prime}
and is supported on
\Omega
implies the following. If the move is type (1) then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p+1}}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p}}
, if the move is type (2) then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p+1}}<\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p}}
and if the move is type (3) then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p+1}}>\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p}}
.

1.

In this case, move (3) cannot occur, and if there is a cycle in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
not in
\Omega^{\prime}
then move (2) must occur, so we obtain the desired inequalities.

2.

In this case, move (2) cannot occur, and if
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
has more components than
\Omega^{\prime}
then move (3) must occur, so again we obtain the desired inequalities.

\diamond

Definition 13

A quiver is said to have
finite zeroth homology
if the rank of the zeroth homology of the underlying graph is finite, i.e. it has only finitely many connected components and
finite first homology
if the rank of the first homology of the underlying graph is finite, i.e. it has only finitely many cycles.

Lemma 7.7

A quiver has finite zeroth and first homology if and only if it has a finite retraction.

Proof:

If
\Omega
has a finite retraction it obviously has only finitely many cycles and connected components.
If
\Omega
has finite zeroth and first homology, then there is some finite subquiver which contains all cycles and contains a point from every connected component of
\Omega
. By Lemma
7.1
this is contained in a finite IC subquiver which still contains a vertex from each connected component of
\Omega
and is therefore BC as well.

\diamond

Corollary 7.1

Suppose
n
is supported on
\Omega
and constant on all arrows not in some finite retraction
\Omega^{*}
of
\Omega
then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}
.

Proof:

A retraction is both BC and contains all cycles of
\Omega
, hence Lemma
7.6
gives the desired equality.

\diamond

Proposition 7.2

Let
\Omega
be a quiver and
n
an element of the root space of
\Omega
.

1.

If
\Omega^{n}
has finite zeroth and first homology, then there exists a finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{*}
of
\Omega
such that
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}
.

2.

If
\Omega^{n}
has finite first homology but infinite zeroth homology then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=+\infty
.

3.

If
\Omega^{n}
has finite zeroth homology but infinite first homology then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=-\infty
.

Proof:

First of all, by Proposition
7.1
we have
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{n}}
. If
n
is eventually constant on
\Omega
, it is eventually constant on
\Omega^{n}
, hence we may assume without loss of generality that
\Omega^{n}=\Omega
.

1.

If
\Omega
has finite zeroth and first homology, by Lemma
7.7
,
\Omega
has a finite retraction, call it
\Omega^{\prime}
. Furthermore, because
n
is eventually constant on
\Omega
, there exists a finite subquiver
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
of
\Omega
such that
n
is constant on all arrows that are not in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
. Let
\Omega^{*}
be a finite IC subquiver which contains
\Omega^{\prime}\cup\Omega^{\prime\prime}
. Then
n
is constant on all arrows not in
\Omega^{*}
and
\Omega^{*}
is IC and contains a retraction of
\Omega
, hence it is itself a retraction. By Corollary
7.1
,
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}
.

2.

If
\Omega
has finite first homology, it has a finite subquiver containing all cycles. Furthermore there is a finite subquiver such that
n
is constant on all arrows not contained in it. Hence by Lemma
7.1
there exists a finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{*}
containing both which therefore contains all cycles and has the property that
n
is constant on all arrows outside of it. Then every finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
containing
\Omega^{*}
has
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}\geq\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}
by Lemma
7.6
, and because
\Omega
has infinitely many components and
\Omega^{*}
only has finitely many, there is an infinite sequence of finite IC subquivers
\Omega^{*}=\Lambda^{1}\subseteq\Lambda^{2}\subseteq\ldots
where each
\Lambda^{p+1}
has more components than
\Lambda^{p}
and hence again by Lemma
7.6
,
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p}}<\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p+1}}
, so
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=+\infty
.

3.

If
\Omega
has finite zeroth homology there is a finite subquiver which contains at least one vertex from every connected component of
\Omega
. Furthermore there is a finite subquiver outside of which
n
is constant on arrows, hence by Lemma
7.1
there is a finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{*}
containing these, which is then BC as well and
n
is constant on arrows outside of it. Then every finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
containing
\Omega^{*}
has
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}
by Lemma
7.6
, and because
\Omega
has infinitely many cycles and
\Omega^{*}
only has finitely many, there is an infinite sequence of finite IC subquivers
\Omega^{*}=\Lambda^{1}\subseteq\Lambda^{2}\subseteq\ldots
where each
\Lambda^{p+1}
contains a cycle not in
\Lambda^{p}
and hence again by Lemma
7.6
,
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p}}>\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Lambda^{p+1}}
, so
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=-\infty
.

\diamond

Corollary 7.2

If
\Omega
has either finite zeroth or first homology then for any element
n
of the root space of
\Omega
, the limit
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}
converges to an element of
\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.

Proof:

Given an element
n
of the root space of
\Omega
, if
\Omega
has finite first homology then it is clear that
\Omega^{n}
does too. On the other hand we claim that if
\Omega
has finite zeroth homology so does
\Omega^{n}
. Indeed, if this is not the case then there must be a single component of
\Omega
which contains infinitely many components of
\Omega^{n}
. Therefore we may assume without loss of generality that
\Omega
has one component. Then there exists a finite subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
of
\Omega
on which
n
is anticonstant, hence given any vertex
i
of
\Omega^{n}
, there is a minimal path from
i
to a vertex
j
in
\Omega^{\prime}
whose arrows are not contained in
\Omega^{\prime}
. Hence
n
is constant along each of these arrows, so the path is in
\Omega^{n}
. Therefore
i
is in the same component of
\Omega^{n}
as
j
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
is finite, this implies there can only be finitely many components of
\Omega^{n}
as desired.

\diamond

Remark 7.4

If the zeroth and first homology of
\Omega
are finite, then it follows that the Euler form is well-defined on roots, though we do not need this result for what follows.

Definition 14

A quiver is called
positive definite
if, for each nonzero element
n
of the root space for which the Tits form converges,
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}>0
or
+\infty
.

Lemma 7.8

If
\Omega
is a quiver the Tits form on
\Omega
is positive definite if and only if the Tits form on every finite IC subquiver of
\Omega
is positive definite.

Proof:

Suppose that the Tits form is positive definite on every finite IC subquiver of
\Omega
and let
n
be a nonzero element of the root space of
\Omega
. Then for any finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
of
\Omega
we have
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}\geq 0
with the inequality strict if
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\Omega^{n}\neq\emptyset
. Since
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}
is the limit of non-negative integers, it must be non-negative itself if it exists. Suppose for contradiction that
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=0
. Then by definition there exists
\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\Omega)
such that
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}=0
for all
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(\Omega)
containing
\Omega^{\prime}
. But since
\Omega^{n}
is nonempty, by Lemma
7.1
there exists a finite IC subquiver
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
of
\Omega
containing
\Omega^{\prime}
and a single vertex from
\Omega^{n}
. Then
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}\neq 0
by hypothesis, contradiction.

Conversely, suppose that the Tits form on
\Omega
is positive definite. If
\Omega^{\prime}
is a finite IC subquiver which does not have a positive-definite Tits form, there exists an element
n\neq 0
of the root space of
\Omega^{\prime}
such that
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq 0
. Since
\Omega^{\prime}
is finite, it only intersects finitely many components of
\Omega
. Let
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
be the union of these components. We claim that
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
must have finite first homology. Indeed if not, then one component
\Omega^{\dagger}
of
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
(and thus of
\Omega
) has infinite first homology, and let
n
be the element of the root space of
\Omega
defined by
n_{i}=1
for all
i\in\Omega^{\dagger}_{0}
and
n_{i}=0
for all other
i
. Then
\Omega^{n}=\Omega^{\dagger}
, hence by Proposition
7.2
, we have
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=-\infty
, contradiction. Thus
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
has finite zeroth and first homology, so by Lemma
7.7
, there is a finite retraction
\Omega^{*}
of
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
which we can assume contains
\Omega^{\prime}
.

Extend
n
to
\Omega^{*}
by defining it to be zero outside of
\Omega^{\prime}
. Then we have
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}
by Lemma
7.3
. Because
\Omega^{*}
is a retraction, each vertex
i
of
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
is connected to
\Omega^{*}
by a unique minimal path ending at a vertex
j
. Extend
n
to
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
by letting
n_{i}=n_{j}
. Then because
\Omega^{*}
is finite,
n
is constant along all but finitely many arrows so it is an element of the root space of
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
, and furthermore we have
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}
by Lemma

. Finally extend
n
to all of
\Omega
by letting it be zero on all vertices not in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
. This is clearly still an element of the root space of
\Omega
, and since the support of
n
is contained in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
by Proposition
7.1
we have
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}
.

Hence all together we have a nonzero element
n
of the root space of
\Omega
with the property that
\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}}\leq 0
, contradiction.

\diamond

The Tits form is positive definite on the root space of

if and only if the underlying graph of every component of

is of the form

,

,

,

, or

It is positive definite if and only if every component is positive definite. If a component of

Definition 15

We say that quiver
\Omega
is of generalized

if

that assigns to each vertex the dimension of the associated vector space. We say that a root

if each

Proposition 7.4

If
\Omega

\Omega
is the dimension vector of an indecomposable FLEI representation unique up to isomorphism.

Proof:

Let

be a FLEI representation, let

be its dimension vector (necessarily a root with nonnegative integer entries) and let
\Omega^{*}
be a finite retraction such that all arrows outside it are mapped to isomorphisms, and consider the functor
\mathscr{F}.
By Lemma

and the fact that

\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle n,n\right\rangle_{\Omega^{*}}

\diamond

8
Gabriel’s Theorem for Infinite Quivers

Recall that the represention theory of a quiver is of
unique type
if indecomposables with the same dimension vector are isomorphic and is
infinite Krull-Schmidt
if every representation is uniquely a (possibly infinite) direct sum of indecomposables.

Theorem 2
(Gabriel’s Theorem for Infinite Quivers)

The category
\text{Reps}(\Omega)
of a connected quiver
\Omega
is of unique type and infinite Krull-Schmidt if and only if it is eventually outward and of generalized ADE Dynkin type. In this case all indecomposables of
\Omega
are FLEI and in one-to-one correspondence with positive roots.

Proof:

Suppose that
\Omega
is eventually outward and of generalized ADE Dynkin type. If
\Omega
has underlying graph
A_{n}
,
D_{n}
,
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
, or
E_{8}
then
\Omega
is Krull-Schmidt with a one-to-one correspondence between isomorphism classes of indecomposables and positive roots by Gabriel’s theorem (see
[
Gab72
]
) and it is completely reducible by
[
Rin16
]
. (Note that it is obvious that every finite-dimension representation of
\Omega
is a finite direct sum of indecomposables, but here Ringel proved that any (possibly infinite-dimensional) representation of such an
\Omega
is a (possibly infinite) direct sum of indecomposables.) Furthermore Gabriel’s theorem gives the one-to-one correspondence between the positive roots and the indecomposables. If
\Omega
has underlying graph
A_{\infty}
or
A_{\infty,\infty}
, then the results of
[
GS22
]
show that
\text{Reps}(\Omega)
is of unique type, is infinite Krull-Schmidt, and its indecomposables are in one-to-one correspondence with the connected subquivers, which are clearly in bijection with the positive roots in this case. If
\Omega
has underlying graph
D_{\infty}
, then the corresponding results are given by Theorem
1
and Proposition
7.4
.

Conversely, suppose that
\Omega
is not eventually outward. Then there is a journey in
\Omega
beginning at a vertex
i
which has infinitely many arrows pointing toward
i
. By assigning all vertices not on this journey the zero vector space, all vertices at the target of an an inward-pointing arrow along this journey according to the representation described in Section 8 of
[
GS22
]
, and all outward-pointing arrows along this journey to be isomorphisms, we obtain a representation of
\Omega
which is not completely reducible and hence the category is not Krull-Schmidt (see Corollary 8.2 of
[
GS22
]
).

Finally if
\Omega
is not of generalized ADE Dynkin type, then by Proposition
7.3
the Tits form is not positive definite, so by Lemma
7.8
there is a finite subquiver
\Omega^{\prime}
on which the Tits form is not positive definite, so by the proof of Gabriel’s theorem (see e.g.
[
Bri
]
), there are nonisomorphic indecomposable representations of
\Omega^{\prime}
with the same dimension vector, i.e.
\text{Reps}(\Omega^{\prime})
is not of unique type. Extending this dimension vector by zero outside of
\Omega^{\prime}
and extending the corresponding indecomposables by zero as well gives that
\text{Reps}(\Omega)
is not of unique type either.

\diamond

9
Future Work

Theorem
2
gives one possible generalization of Gabriel’s theorem to infinite quivers, but a second option restricts to the category of locally finite-dimensional representations. We conjecture that in this case the “eventually outward” condition is not necessary. Precisely, we say that a quiver is
locally finitely reducible
if every locally finite-dimensional representation of
\Omega
is a direct sum of indecomposables. Then we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 9.1
(Locally Finite-Dimensional Infinite Gabriel’s Thm.)

For a connected quiver
\Omega
the category
\text{Rep}_{\text{lf}}(\Omega)
is of unique type and is infinite Krull-Schmit if and only if it is of generalized ADE Dynkin type. In this case all indecomposables of
\Omega
are FLEI and in one-to-one correspondence with positive roots.
