On the uniqueness of the infinite cluster and the cluster density in the Poisson driven random connection model
Mikhail Chebunin and Günter Last

Abstract
We consider a random connection model (RCM) on a general space driven by a Poisson process whose intensity measure is scaled by a parameter t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . We say that the infinite clusters are deletion stable if the removal of a Poisson point cannot split a cluster in two or more infinite clusters. We prove that this stability together with a natural irreducibility assumption implies uniqueness of the infinite cluster. Conversely, if the infinite cluster is unique then this stability property holds. Several criteria for irreducibility will be established. We also study the analytic properties of expectations of functions of clusters as a function of t t . In particular we show that the position dependent cluster density is differentiable. A significant part of this paper is devoted to the important case of a stationary marked RCM (in Euclidean space), containing the Boolean model with general compact grains and the so-called weighted RCM as special cases. In this case we establish differentiability and a convexity property of the cluster density κ ⁡ ( t ) \kappa(t) . These properties are crucial for our proof of deletion stability of the infinite clusters but are also of interest in their own right. It then follows that an irreducible stationary marked RCM can have at most one infinite cluster. This extends and unifies several results in the literature.



Keywords:
Random connection model, Poisson process, percolation,
Margulis–Russo formula, cluster density, uniqueness of infinite cluster.

2
Formal definition of the RCM

It is convenient to model a RCM as a suitable point process.
Let
{\mathbf{N}}
denote the space of all simple locally finite counting measures on
{\mathbb{X}}
, equipped with the standard
\sigma
-field, see e.g.
[
25
]
.
A measure
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
is identified with its support
\{x\in{\mathbb{X}}:\nu(\{x\})=1\}
and describes the set of vertices of a (deterministic) graph.
If
\nu(\{x\})=1
we write
x\in\nu
.
Using the Dirac measure
\delta_{x}
at point
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
any
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
can be written as a finite or infinite sum

\nu=\delta_{x_{1}}+\delta_{x_{2}}+\cdots
, where the
x_{i}
are pairwise distinct
and do not accumulate in bounded sets.
The space of (undirected) graphs with vertices from
{\mathbb{X}}
(and no loops) is
described by the set
{\mathbf{G}}
of all counting measures
\mu
on
{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}}

with the following properties. First we assume that the measure
V(\mu):=\mu(\cdot\times{\mathbf{N}})

is locally finite and simple, that is, an element of
{\mathbf{N}}
.
Hence, if
x\in V(\mu)
(that is
\mu(\{x\}\times{\mathbf{N}})=1
), then
there is a unique
\psi_{x}\in{\mathbf{N}}
such that
(x,\psi_{x})\in\mu
. We assume that

x\notin\psi_{x}
. Finally, if
x\in V(\mu)
and
y\in\psi_{x}
then we assume that

(y,\psi_{y})\in\mu
and
x\in\psi_{y}
. Also
{\mathbf{G}}
is
equipped with the standard
\sigma
-field.
There is an edge between
x,y\in V(\mu)
if
y\in\psi_{x}
(and hence
x\in\psi_{y}
). If
\psi_{x}=0
, then
x
is

isolated
.

We write
|\mu|:=\mu({\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{N}})
for the cardinality of
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}

and similarly for
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
. Hence
|\mu|=|V(\mu)|
.
For
x,y\in V(\mu)
we write
x\sim y
(in
\mu
) if there is an edge between
x
and
y

and
x\leftrightarrow y
(in
\mu
) if there is a path in
\mu
leading from
x
to
y
.
For
A\subset{\mathbb{X}}
we write
x\sim A
(in
\mu
) if there exists
y\in A\cap V(\mu)

such that
x\sim y
.

Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{G}}
. Then
\mu
is a
subgraph
of
\mu^{\prime}
if

V(\mu)\leq V(\mu^{\prime})
(as measures) and if
(x,\psi)\in\mu
and

(x,\psi^{\prime})\in\mu^{\prime}
together imply that
\psi\leq\psi^{\prime}
. Note that this is not the same
as
\mu\leq\mu^{\prime}
.

Let
\chi
be a simple point process on
{\mathbb{X}}
, that is a random element of

{\mathbf{N}}
. The reader should think of
a Poisson process possibly augmented by additional (deterministic) points.
By
[
25
, Proposition 6.2]
there exist random elements
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
of

{\mathbb{X}}
such that

\displaystyle\chi=\sum^{|\chi|}_{n=1}\delta_{X_{n}},

(2.1)

where
X_{m}\neq X_{n}
whenever
m\neq n
and

m,n\leq|\chi|
.
Let
(Z_{m,n})_{m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a double sequence of random elements
uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
such that
Z_{m,n}=Z_{n,m}
for all

m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and such that the
Z_{m,n}
,
m<n
, are independent.
Then the RCM (based on
\chi
) is the point process

\displaystyle\xi:=\sum^{|\chi|}_{m=1}\delta_{(X_{m},\Psi_{m})},

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}:=\sum^{|\chi|}_{n=1}{\mathbf{1}}\{n\neq m,Z_{m,n}\leq\varphi(X_{m},X_{n})\}\delta_{X_{n}}.

In this notation we suppress the dependence on the
Z_{m,n}
.
While the definition of
\xi
depends on the ordering of the points of
\chi
,
its distribution does not.

We now introduce some notation used throughout the paper.
For
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{G}}
we often interpret
\mu+\mu^{\prime}
as the
measure in
{\mathbf{G}}
with the same support as
\mu+\mu^{\prime}
.
A similar convention applies to
\nu,\nu^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Let
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
. For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
we write
\mu(B):=\mu(B\times{\mathbf{N}})
.
More generally, given a measurable function
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
we write

\int f(x)\,\mu(dx):=\int f(x)\,\mu(dx\times{\mathbf{N}})
.
Similarly, given
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
, we write

x\in\mu
instead of
x\in V(\mu)
(=
\mu(\cdot\times{\mathbf{N}})
).
In the same spirit we write
g(\mu):=g(V(\mu))
, whenever

g
is a mapping on
{\mathbf{N}}
.
These (slightly abusing) conventions lighten the notation and should not cause any confusion.
For
B\in{\mathcal{X}}

we denote by
\mu[B]\in{\mathbf{G}}
the
restriction
of
\mu
to
B
, that is the graph
with vertex set
V(\mu)\cap B
which keeps only those edges
from
\mu
with both end points from
B
. In the same way we use the
notation
\mu[\nu]
for
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Similarly for a measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{X}}
(for instance for
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
)
we denote by
\nu_{B}:=\nu(B\cap\cdot)
the restriction of
\nu
to a set
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
.
Assume now that
v\in V(\mu)
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
let

C^{v}_{n}(\mu)\in{\mathbf{G}}
denote the graph restricted to the set of vertices
x\in V(\mu)
with

d_{\mu}(v,x)=n
, where
d_{\mu}
denotes distance within the graph
\mu
.
Note that
C^{v}_{0}(\mu)
is just the isolated vertex
v
. Slightly abusing our notation
we write
C^{v}_{0}(\mu)=\delta_{v}
. For
v\notin V(\mu)
we set
C^{v}(\mu):=0
,
interpreted as an empty graph (a graph with no vertices).
The
cluster

C^{v}(\mu)
of
v
in
\mu
is the graph
\mu
restricted to

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}V(C^{v}_{n}(\mu)),

while
C^{v}_{\leq{n}}(\mu)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, is the graph
\mu
restricted to

V(C^{v}_{0}(\mu))+\cdots+V(C^{v}_{n}(\mu))
.
For later purposes it will be convenient to define
C^{v}_{\leq{-1}}(\mu)=C^{v}_{-1}(\mu):=0
as the zero measure.
For
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
and
x\in{\mathbb{X}}

we denote by
\mu-\delta_{x}:=\mu[V(\mu)-\delta_{x}]

the graph resulting from
\mu
by removing the point
x
.
If
x\notin V(\mu)
then
\mu-\delta_{x}=\mu
.

3
Basic properties of the RCM

Let
\xi
be a RCM based on a Poisson process
\eta
on

{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
\lambda
.
Our first crucial tool is a version of the Mecke equation (see

[
25
, Chapter 4]
) for
\xi
. Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{X}}
we denote

\eta^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}:=\eta+\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}
(removing possible multiplicities)
and let
\xi^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}
denote a RCM based on
\eta^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}
.
It is useful to construct
\xi^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}
in a specific way as follows.
We connect
x_{1}
with the points in
\eta
using independent connection decisions which
are independent of
\xi
.
We then proceed inductively finally connecting
x_{n}
to

\eta+\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n-1}}
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and a measurable function

f\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,\infty]
the Mecke equation for
\xi
states
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi)\,\eta^{(n)}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=\mathbb{E}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}})\,\lambda^{n}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})),

(3.1)

where integration with respect to the
factorial measure

\eta^{(n)}

of
\eta
means summation over all
n
-tuples of pairwise distinct points
from
\eta
. A convenient way to prove this and related formulas is
to introduce a probability kernel
\Gamma
from
{\mathbf{N}}
to
{\mathbf{G}}
, satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}((\eta,\xi)\in\cdot)=\mathbb{E}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{(\eta,\mu)\in\cdot\}\,\Gamma(\eta,d\mu).

(3.2)

The kernel
\Gamma
is just a regular version of the conditional distribution of
\xi

given
\eta
and can be defined explicitly; see Section
2
.
A crucial property of this kernel is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Gamma(\eta^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}},\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\xi^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}\in\cdot),\quad\lambda^{n}\text{-a.e.\ $(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{n}$}.

(3.3)

It follows from
[
25
, Theorem 4.4]

that the left-hand side of (
3.1
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\mu)\,\Gamma(\eta^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}},d\mu)\,\lambda^{n}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

Therefore (
3.1
) follows from (
3.3
).

Given
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
we sometimes use (
3.1
) in the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi^{v})\,\eta^{(n)}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=\mathbb{E}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi^{v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}})\,\lambda^{n}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

(3.4)

This can be derived from (
3.1
) as follows. We can write
\xi^{v}=h(\xi,v,U)
, where
U
is a random element
of
[0,1]^{\infty}
with
independent and uniformly distributed components, independent of
\xi
;
see the proof of Lemma
6.2
for more detail.
It remains to note that
h(\xi^{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}},v,U)
has the
same distribution as
\xi^{v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}}
, provided that
v,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are pairwise distinct.

To state another useful version of
(
3.1
)
we recall the
notation
\mu-\delta_{x}=\mu[V(\mu)-\delta_{x}]
for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
and
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
.
Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in{\mathbb{X}}
we define

\mu-\delta_{x_{1}}-\cdots-\delta_{x_{n}}
inductively.
The kernel
\Gamma
has the property

\displaystyle\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu-\delta_{x_{1}}-\cdots-\delta_{x_{n}}\in\cdot\}\,\Gamma(\nu,d\mu)=\Gamma(\nu-\delta_{x_{1}}-\cdots-\delta_{x_{n}},\cdot),\quad\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}.

Therefore we obtain from
[
25
, Theorem 4.5]

for each measurable
f\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{n}\times{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,\infty]
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi-\delta_{x_{1}}-\cdots-\delta_{x_{n}})\,\eta^{(n)}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=\mathbb{E}\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\xi)\,\lambda^{n}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

(3.5)

Given
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
we also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int f(x,\xi^{v}-\delta_{x})\,\eta(dx)=\mathbb{E}\int f(x,\xi^{v})\,\lambda(dx).

(3.6)

This follows similarly as (
3.4
). Indeed, given
\eta
and
x\in\eta

the random graph
h(\xi,v,U)-\delta_{x}
has the same distribution as
h(\xi-\delta_{x},v,U)
,
provided that
v\neq x
.

We define
\bar{\varphi}:=1-\varphi
and for
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}(\nu,x):=\prod_{y\in\nu}\bar{\varphi}(x,y),\quad\varphi(\nu,x):=1-\bar{\varphi}(\nu,x),\ \quad\varphi_{\lambda}(\nu):=\int\varphi(\nu,x)\,\lambda(dx).

(3.7)

We recall our general convention
\varphi(\mu,x):=\varphi(V(\mu),x)

and
\varphi_{\lambda}(\mu):=\varphi_{\lambda}(V(\mu))
for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
.
Throughout we often abbreviate

C^{v}:=C^{v}(\xi^{v})
,
C^{v}_{n}:=C^{v}_{n}(\xi^{v})
and
C^{v}_{\leq n}:=C^{v}_{\leq n}(\xi^{v})
. Moreover we write

C^{v!}:=C^{v}-\delta_{v}
.

We shall need the following consequence of
(
3.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
h\colon{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,\infty)
be measurable.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int h(\xi^{v}-\delta_{x})\,C^{v!}(dx)=\mathbb{E}h(\xi^{v})\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v}).

(3.8)

Proof.

Let
I
denote the left-hand side of the asserted formula.
Then

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int h(\xi^{v}-\delta_{x}){\mathbf{1}}\{x\in C^{v}(\xi^{v})\}\,\eta(dx)=\int\mathbb{E}h(\xi^{v,x}-\delta_{x}){\mathbf{1}}\{x\in C^{v}(\xi^{v,x})\}\,\lambda(dx),

where we have used the Mecke equation (
3.4
) to obtain the second identity.
By definition we have that

\xi^{v,x}-\delta_{x}=\xi^{v}
for each
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
. Hence we obtain that

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{E}h(\xi^{v}){\mathbf{1}}\{x\in C^{v}(\xi^{v,x})\}\,\lambda(dx)=\int\mathbb{E}h(\xi^{v})\mathbb{P}(x\in C^{v}(\xi^{v,x})\mid\xi^{v})\,\lambda(dx).

By definition of
\xi^{v,x}
we have
\mathbb{P}(x\in C^{v}(\xi^{v,x})\mid\xi^{v})=\varphi(x,C^{v})
,
concluding the proof.
∎

Next we turn to the
Margulis–Russo formula
. Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
be two
measures on
{\mathbb{X}}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is locally finite and
\lambda_{2}
is finite.
Given
t\geq 0
we consider a RCM driven by a Poisson process
\eta
with
intensity measure
\lambda_{1}+t\lambda_{2}
. The associated
expectation operator is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{t}
. Let

f\colon{\mathbf{G}}\to[-\infty,\infty]
be a measurable function and
assume that
\mathbb{E}_{t_{0}}|f(\xi)|<\infty
for some
t_{0}>0
. From

[
25
, Exercise 3.8]
and (
3.2
) we then obtain
that
\mathbb{E}_{t}|f(\xi)|<\infty
for all
t\leq t_{0}
. We assert that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\xi)=\int\mathbb{E}_{t}[f(\xi^{x})-f(\xi)]\,\lambda_{2}(dx),\quad t\in[0,t_{0}).

(3.9)

Using the kernel
\Gamma
, this can be seen as follows. From

[
25
, Theorem 19.3]
we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\xi)=\int\mathbb{E}_{t}[\tilde{f}(\eta^{x})-\tilde{f}(\eta)]\,\lambda_{2}(dx),\quad t\in[0,t_{0}),

where
\tilde{f}(\nu):=\int f(\mu)\,\Gamma(\nu,d\mu)
,
\nu\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Note that
\tilde{f}(\xi)
is
\mathbb{P}_{t}
-a.s. well-defined.

Take
t\in[0,t_{0})
. Theorem 19.3 in
[
25
]
shows that

\int\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\tilde{f}(\eta^{x})-\tilde{f}(\eta)|]\,\lambda_{2}(dx)<\infty
.
Furthermore we have

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\tilde{f}(\eta)|]\,\,\lambda_{2}(dx)=\lambda_{2}({\mathbb{X}})\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\tilde{f}(\eta)|]\leq\lambda_{2}({\mathbb{X}})\mathbb{E}_{t}[|f(\xi)|]<\infty,

where we have used the triangle inequality and (
3.2
).
Therefore we also have
\int\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\tilde{f}(\eta^{x})|]\,\lambda_{2}(dx)<\infty
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\xi)=\int\big(\mathbb{E}_{t}\tilde{f}(\eta^{x})-\mathbb{E}_{t}\tilde{f}(\eta)\big)\,\lambda_{2}(dx)=\int\big(\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\xi^{x})-\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\xi)\big)\,\lambda_{2}(dx),

where we have used (
3.3
).
Since the above right-hand side is finite we have

|\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\xi^{x})|<\infty
and hence also
\mathbb{E}_{t}|f(\xi^{x})|<\infty

for
\lambda_{2}
-a.e.
x
. This implies (
3.9
).

4
The stationary marked RCM

In this section we introduce an important special case of the general RCM. The setting is that of
[
10
,
12
]
.
Special cases were studied in
[
11
,
17
,
18
]
.

Let
{\mathbb{M}}
be a complete separable metric space equipped with a
probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
. This is our
mark space
, while
\mathbb{Q}

is said to be the
mark distribution
. In this section we
consider the space
{\mathbb{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
equipped with the product of
the Borel
\sigma
-field
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the Borel

\sigma
-field on
{\mathbb{M}}
. We assume that
\lambda=t\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
,
where
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\lambda_{d}
denotes Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. If

(x,p)\in{\mathbb{X}}
then we call
x
location of
(x,p)
and
p
the mark of

x
. Instead of
{\mathbf{N}}
we consider the (smaller set)
{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
of all
counting measures
\chi
on
{\mathbb{X}}
such that
\chi(\cdot\times{\mathbb{M}})
is
locally finite (w.r.t. the Euclidean metric) and simple. The
symmetric connection function
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}\to[0,1]

is assumed to satisfy

\displaystyle\varphi((x,p),(y,q))=\varphi((0,p),(y-x,q)).

(4.1)

This allows us to write
\varphi(x,p,q):=\varphi((0,p),(x,q))
, where

0
denotes the origin in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We also assume that

\displaystyle d_{\varphi}:=\iint\varphi(x,p,q)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}(d(p,q))<\infty,

(4.2)

referring to Remark
4.2
for some comments.
Let
t>0
and let
\eta
be a Poisson process on
{\mathbb{X}}

with intensity measure
t\lambda
.
We can and will assume that
\eta
is a random element of
{\mathbf{N}}({\mathbb{X}})
.
We consider a RCM
\xi
based on
\eta
and connection function
\varphi
.

The RCM
\xi
is
stationary
in the sense
that
T_{x}\xi\overset{d}{=}\xi
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
for
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
, the measure
T_{x}\mu
is (as usual) defined by

\displaystyle T_{x}\mu:=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{(y-x,q,\nu)\in\cdot\}\,\mu(d(y,q,\nu)).

To see this, it is convenient to define
\xi
in a slightly different way,
without changing its distribution. As at (
2.1
) we can write

\displaystyle\eta=\sum^{\infty}_{m=1}\delta_{(X_{m},Q_{m})},

(4.3)

where
X_{1},X,\ldots
are pairwise distinct random elements
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots
are random elements of
{\mathbb{M}}
.
Let
Z^{\prime}_{m,n}
,
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, be independent random variables uniformly distributed
on
[0,1]
and set
Z^{\prime}_{m}:=(Z^{\prime}_{m,n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
By the marking theorem (see
[
25
, Theorem 5.6]
),

\displaystyle\eta^{*}:=\sum^{\infty}_{m=1}\delta_{(X_{m},Q_{m},Z^{\prime}_{m})}

(4.4)

is again a Poisson process.
We then connect
(X_{m},Q_{m})
with
(X_{n},Q_{n})
if
X_{m}
is lexicographically
smaller than
X_{n}
and
Z^{\prime}_{m,\tau}\leq\varphi(X_{n}-X_{m},Q_{m},Q_{n})
, where the
{\mathbb{N}}
-valued random variable

\tau
is determined by the fact that
X_{n}
is the
\tau
-th nearest neighbour
of
X_{m}
in the set
\{X_{k}:k\neq m\}
, where we can use the lexicographic order
to break ties. Then we have
\xi=F(\eta^{*})
for a well-defined
measurable mapping
F
. Since the nearest neighbour relation
is translation invariant it follows from (
4.1
) that
F
can be assumed to satisfy

T_{x}\xi=F(T_{x}\eta^{*})
for each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Since
T_{x}\eta^{*}\overset{d}{=}\eta^{*}
it follows
that
\xi
is stationary. The same argument combined with

[
25
, Exercise 10.1]
shows that
\xi
is
ergodic
, i.e. we have
\mathbb{P}(\xi\in A)\in\{0,1\}
for each translation invariant measurable

A\subset{\mathbf{G}}
. If
{\mathbb{M}}
contains only one element, we identify
{\mathbb{X}}
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
In this case
\xi
is said to be a
stationary
RCM.

The following consequence of the Mecke equation will be often used to treat cluster expectations.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B\}f(|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|)\,\eta(d(x,p))=t\lambda_{d}(B)\mathbb{E}_{t}\int f(|C^{(0,p)}|)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp).

(4.5)

Proof.

By the Mecke equation (
3.1
) the left-hand side of (
4.5
) equals

\displaystyle t\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B\}f(|C^{(x,p)}(\xi^{(x,p)})|)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)=t\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B\}f(|C^{(0,p)}(T_{x}\xi^{(x,p)})|)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),

where we have used that
|C^{(x,p)}(\mu)|=|C^{(0,p)}(T_{x}\mu)|
for all
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
.
It follows from stationarity of
\xi
and definition of
\xi^{(x,p)}
, that
T_{x}\xi^{(x,p)}\overset{d}{=}\xi^{(0,p)}
for
\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
-a.e.
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
. Therefore the result follows.
∎

Let
Q_{0}
be a random element of
{\mathbb{M}}
with distribution
\mathbb{Q}
which is independent
of
\eta^{*}
given by (
4.4
). In accordance with Palm theory
we refer to
C^{(0,Q_{0})}(\xi^{(0,Q_{0})})
as cluster of the
typical vertex
(of
\xi
).

Remark 4.2
.

Let
p\in{\mathbb{M}}
. Then the
degree
D_{p}
of
(0,p)
(the origin marked with
p
) in
\xi^{(0,p)}
has a Poisson distribution
with parameter
t\int\varphi(x,p,q)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)
.
Our integrability assumption (
4.2
) means
that
\int\mathbb{E}D_{p}\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)<\infty
. This means that
the expected degree of the
typical vertex is finite. Hence (
4.2
) excludes Pareto type degree distributions
but is still much weaker than the integrability assumption made in

[
10
]
.

The function

\displaystyle\kappa(t):=\int\mathbb{E}_{t}|C^{(0,p)}|^{-1}\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)=\mathbb{E}_{t}|C^{(0,Q_{0})}|^{-1},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

(4.6)

plays a crucial role in Section
11
.
To interpret it, we introduce a point process
\eta_{c}\leq\eta(\cdot\times{\mathbb{M}})

modeling finite clusters as follows.
Let
(x,p)\in\eta
. Then
x\in\eta_{c}
if
|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|<\infty

and
x
is the lexicographically smallest spatial coordinate of the points in

C^{(x,p)}(\xi)
. Since
\xi
is stationary, it is easy to see that
\eta_{c}

is a stationary point process. The following result shows that
t\kappa(t)
is the
intensity

of
\eta_{c}
, that is the density of finite clusters. With a slight
abuse of language we refer to
\kappa(t)
as
cluster density
. In the unmarked
case this function is also called
free energy
; see
[
1
,
6
,
13
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
we have that

t\kappa(t)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\eta_{c}([0,1]^{d})
.

Proof.

The result follows from
[
27
, Proposition 3.1]

upon taking there
\eta
as the projection of the point process
\{(x,p)\in\eta:|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|<\infty\}

onto
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\xi:=\eta_{c}
.
A direct proof can start with

\displaystyle t\kappa(t)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\}|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|^{-1}\,\eta(d(x,p)),

(4.7)

a consequence of (
4.5
). The right-hand side can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\}|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|^{-1}{\mathbf{1}}\{\tau(x,p)=y\}\,\eta(d(x,p))\,\eta_{c}(dy),

where
\tau(x,p)
is the lexicographic minimum of the spatial coordinates of

C^{(x,p)}(\xi)
. The key step is then the application of the refined Campbell theorem for

\eta_{c}
.
∎

The cluster density can also be obtained as an ergodic limit:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
B_{n}\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, be an increasing sequence of compact
convex sets whose inradius diverges to
\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|^{-1}\,\eta(d(x,p))=t\kappa(t),\quad\mathbb{P}_{t}\text{-a.s.}

Proof.

For each
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}

\displaystyle M_{\mu}:=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in\cdot\}|C^{(x,p)}(\mu)|^{-1}\,\mu(d(x,p))

is a locally finite measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
For
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
we have

C^{(x,p)}(T_{y}\mu)=T_{y}C^{(x+y,p)}(\mu)
. Therefore, we obtain for
B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\displaystyle M_{T_{y}\mu}(B)

\displaystyle:=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B\}|C^{(x+y,p)}(\mu)|^{-1}\,T_{y}\mu(d(x,p))

\displaystyle=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x-y\in B\}|C^{(x,p)}(\mu)|^{-1}\,\mu(d(x,p)).

This means that
M_{T_{y}\mu}=T_{y}M_{\mu}
. Therefore
M_{\xi}
is a stationary
and ergodic random measure. By (
4.7
) it has intensity
t\kappa(t)
.
Hence the result follows from
[
33
, Satz 3]
;
see also
[
22
, Theorem 30.10]
.
∎

We continue with a basic fact from percolation theory. Define

\displaystyle\theta(t):=\mathbb{P}_{t}\big(\big|C^{(0,Q_{0})}(\xi^{(0,Q_{0})})\big|=\infty\big)=\int\mathbb{P}_{t}\big(|C^{(0,p)}|=\infty\big)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),\quad t\geq 0,

(4.8)

as the probability that the cluster of a typical vertex has infinite size.
Let
C_{\infty}
denote the set of all
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
such that
\mu
has an infinite cluster.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
t>0
. Then
\theta(t)>0
iff
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=1
.

Proof.

Let
B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be a Borel set with
\lambda_{d}(B)\in(0,\infty)
.
By (
4.5
),

\displaystyle\theta(t)=(t\lambda_{d}(B))^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B\}{\mathbf{1}}\{|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|=\infty\}\,\eta(d(x,p)).

Hence, if
\theta(t)>0
, then the probability that there is some
(x,p)\in\eta
with

|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|=\infty
must be positive. Since
\xi
is ergodic and
C_{\infty}
is
translation invariant, we obtain
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=1
.
If, on the other hand,
\theta(t)=0
, then the probability that

|C^{(x,p)}(\xi)|=\infty
for some
(x,p)\in\eta
with
x\in B
is zero.
Letting
B\uparrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we obtain that
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=0
.
∎

The
critical intensity
(percolation threshold) is defined by

\displaystyle t_{c}:=\inf\{t>0:\theta(t)>0\}.

(4.9)

If
t<t_{c}
then
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=0
and if
t>t_{c}
then
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\xi\in C_{\infty})=1
.
Under a natural
irreducibility assumption
our Theorem
12.1
will show that
\xi
can have at most
one infinite cluster.

We finish this section with some examples.

Example 4.6
.

In the unmarked case the
connection function
\varphi
is just a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Under the minimal assumption
d_{\varphi}\in(0,\infty)

it was shown in
[
34
]
that
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
.

Example 4.7
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{M}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and

\varphi(x,p,q)={\mathbf{1}}\{\|x\|\leq p+q\}
, where
\|x\|
denotes the Euclidean norm of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
The RCM
\xi
is then said to be the
Gilbert graph
with
radius distribution
\mathbb{Q}
; see e.g.
[
25
, Chapter 16]
for more detail.
The integrability assumption (
4.2
) is then equivalent with

\int r^{d}\,\mathbb{Q}(dr)<\infty
, which is the minimal assumption for having
a reasonable model.
Under the assumption
\mathbb{Q}\{0\}<1
it was proved in
[
16
,
19
]
that
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
.

Example 4.8
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{M}}
equals the space
\mathcal{C}^{d}

of all non-empty compact subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, equipped with the Hausdorff metric
cf.
[
25
,
38
]
.
Let
V\colon\mathcal{C}^{d}\cup\{\emptyset\}\to[0,\infty]
be measurable and translation invariant
with
V(\emptyset)=0
. For instance,
V
could be the volume or, if
\mathbb{Q}

is concentrated on the convex bodies, a linear combination
of the
intrinsic volumes
; see
[
38
]
.
Assume that the connection function is given by

\displaystyle\varphi((x,K),(y,L))=1-e^{-V((K+x)\cap(L+y))},\quad(x,K),(y,L)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{d}.

Then (
4.1
) follows from translation invariance of
V
.
The case of the Gilbert graph arises if
\mathbb{Q}
is concentrated on balls centered
at the origin. A sufficient condition for (
4.2
) is

\displaystyle\int D(K)^{d}\,\mathbb{Q}(dK)<\infty,

where
D(K)
is the radius of the smallest ball centered at the origin
and containing
K
. This easily follows from

\displaystyle\varphi(x,K,L)\leq{\mathbf{1}}\{K\cap(L+x)\neq\emptyset\}\leq{\mathbf{1}}\{\|x\|\leq D(K)+D(L)\}.

The random closed set
\bigcup_{(x,K)\in\eta}K+x
is known as the
Boolean model

and a fundamental model of stochastic geometry (see
[
25
,
38
]
)
and continuum percolation (see
[
31
]
).
This model corresponds to the choice
V(K)=\infty\cdot{\mathbf{1}}\{K\neq\emptyset\}
.
In that case and under some additional assumptions on
\mathbb{Q}

it was proved in
[
19
]
that
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
.
The present much more general model
is taken from
[
5
]
and is partially motivated by
statistical physics.

Example 4.9
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{M}}=(0,1)

equipped with Lebesgue measure
\mathbb{Q}
. Assume that

\displaystyle\varphi((x,p),(y,q))=\rho(g(p,q)\|x-y\|^{d}),

for a decreasing function
\rho\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
and a
function
g\colon(0,1)\times(0,1)\to[0,\infty)
which is increasing in both arguments.
We assume that
m_{\rho}:=\int\rho(\|x\|^{d})\,dx
is positive and finite.
This model was studied in
[
18
]

under the name
weigt-dependent random connection model
.
A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle d_{\varphi}=m_{\rho}\iint g(p,q)^{-1}\,dp\,dq.

To ensure (
4.2
) we have to assume that
g^{-1}
is integrable. This is the case
in all examples studied in
[
18
]
, where it is also asserted that
t_{c}<\infty
.
Sufficient conditions for
t_{c}\in(0,\infty)
can also be found in
[
10
,
11
]
.

5
Irreducibility

In this section, we first consider a general RCM
\xi
based on a Poisson process
\eta
on
{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
t\lambda
.
We fix the intensity parameter
t>0
and therefore drop the lower index

t
in
\mathbb{P}_{t}
. To simplify the notation, we take
t=1
.
We say that
\xi
is
irreducible
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{$x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}$ in $\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}}$})>0,\quad\lambda^{2}\text{-a.e. $(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}$}.

(5.1)

Given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and random elements
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
of
{\mathbb{X}}
we let

\Xi[Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}]
be a RCM
based on the point process
\delta_{Y_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{Y_{k}}
.
Of course we can allow here some of the
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
to be deterministic.
Further we define for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
a measurable function

\varphi^{(n)}\colon{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\to[0,\infty)
recursively by

\varphi^{(1)}:=\varphi
and

\displaystyle\varphi^{(n+1)}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\int\varphi^{(n)}(x_{1},z)\varphi(z,x_{2})\,\lambda(dz),\quad x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{X}},\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

These functions are symmetric. It follows straight from the Mecke equation (
3.1
)
that

\displaystyle\varphi^{(n)}(x_{1},x_{2})=\mathbb{E}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{1}}\{\text{$y_{i-1}\sim y_{i}$ in $\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}}$}\}\,\eta^{(n-1)}(d(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})),

(5.2)

where
y_{0}:=x_{1}
and
y_{n}:=x_{2}
. This is the expected number of paths of length

n
from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
in
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}}
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The following six statements are equivalent:

(i)

\xi
is irreducible.

(ii)

There exist for
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
a set
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
with

\lambda(B)\in(0,\infty)
, an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and independent random
variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
with distribution
\lambda_{B}/\lambda(B)
such
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{$x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}$ in $\Xi[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]$})>0.

(5.3)

(iii)

There exists for
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such
that

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(\text{$x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}$ in $\Xi[x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]$})\lambda^{n}(d(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}))>0.

(5.4)

(iv)

For
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\varphi^{(n)}(x_{1},x_{2})>0.

(5.5)

(v)

For
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq k}\varphi^{(n)}(x_{1},x_{2})>0,\quad k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(5.6)

(vi)

There exist for
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
a set
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
with

\lambda(B)\in(0,\infty)
, an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and independent random
variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
with distribution
\lambda_{B}/\lambda(B)
such
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{$x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}$ in $\Xi^{\prime}[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]$})>0,

(5.7)

where
\Xi^{\prime}[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]
is the graph obtained from
\Xi[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]

by removing the edge between
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
(if there is one).

Proof.

Assume that (i) holds, and take
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{X}}
be such that
\mathbb{P}(\text{$x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}$ in $\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}}$})>0
.
Let
(B_{m})
be a sequence of measurable sets of finite
\lambda
-measure
increasing towards
{\mathbb{X}}
. By monotone convergence
there exists
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
x_{1},x_{2}\in B_{m}
and

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that

for

(i)

There exist

and

\|x\|\leq R

(ii)

be another bounded measurable function with

and let

. Then there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

.

Proof.

(i) The convolution of an integrable and a bounded function is bounded and uniformly continuous;
see

. It follows that

is bounded and uniformly continuous. Since
\int f^{\ast 2}(x)\,dx=\big(\int f(x)\,dx\big)^{2}>0

there exist a ball

with positive radius and
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that

on

. Since
f
is symmetric,

f^{\ast 2}
is symmetric as well. Hence
we can find a ball
B
with center
0
and positive radius and some
\varepsilon>0
such that

f^{\ast 4}\geq\varepsilon
on
B
. Finally we find
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

f^{\ast 4m}(x)\geq\varepsilon
whenever
\|x\|\leq R
.

(ii) By assumption
\int g(x){\mathbf{1}}\{g(x)\geq\varepsilon_{0}\}\,dx>0

for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Set
C:=\{g\geq\varepsilon_{0}\}
.
Then we have for each

that

\displaystyle(f^{\ast n}\ast g)(x)\geq\varepsilon_{0}\int f^{\ast n}(x-z){\mathbf{1}}\{z\in C\}\,dz.

Choose
R>0
so large that

\displaystyle\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\|x-z\|\leq R,z\in C\}\,dz>0.

By the first part of the lemma we can find
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

\varepsilon>0
such that
f^{\ast n}(y)\geq\varepsilon
whenever
\|y\|\leq R
.
It follows that

\displaystyle(f^{\ast n}\ast g)(x)

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon_{0}\int f^{\ast n}(x-z){\mathbf{1}}\{\|x-z\|\leq R,z\in C\}\,dz

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\|x-z\|\leq R,z\in C\}\,dz.

. In view of our goal and assumption (

) we can
assume that
\int d_{\varphi}(p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)>0
and
\int d_{\varphi}(p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)>0
.
From (
5.20
) we obtain for each
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
that

\displaystyle\varphi^{(n+2)}(x,p,q)

\displaystyle=\iint\varphi(z,p,r)\varphi^{(n)}(w,r,s)\varphi(x-z-w,s,q)\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}(d(r,s))\,d(z,w),

\displaystyle\geq\int g(z)\psi^{(n)}(w)h(x-z-w)\,d(z,w),

where
g(z):=\int\varphi(z,p,r)\,\mathbb{Q}(dr)
and
h(z):=\int\varphi(z,r,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dr)
.
Therefore we obtain from (
5.21
) that

\displaystyle\varphi^{(n+2)}(x,p,q)\geq(g\ast\psi^{\ast n}\ast h)(x)=(\psi^{\ast n}\ast g\ast h)(x).

By the choice of
p,q
we have
\int g(x)\,dx>0
and
\int h(x)\,dx>0
.
Hence we deduce from Lemma
5.4

that
(\psi^{\ast m}\ast g\ast h)(x)
is positive for some sufficiently large
m
.
Hence Proposition
5.1
(iv) yields the assertion.
∎

Corollary 5.14
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{M}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
is an interval
and that
\varphi(x,p,\cdot)
is non-decreasing for all
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
.
Then the RCM
\xi
is irreducible iff (
5.19
) holds.

Proof.

Since any probability measure on
{\mathbb{M}}
is associated
(see e.g.
[
28
]
), the result follows from Theorem
5.13
.
∎

6
Deletion stability and uniqueness

In this section, we consider a general RCM
\xi
based on a Poisson process
\eta
on
{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
\lambda
.
Given
(x,\mu)\in{\mathbb{X}}\times{\mathbf{G}}

we let

N^{\infty}(x,\mu)
denote the number of infinite clusters
in
C^{x}(\mu)-\delta_{x}
.
We say that the infinite clusters in
\xi
are
deletion stable
if

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N^{\infty}(x,\xi^{x})\geq 2)=0,\quad\lambda\text{-a.e.\ $x\in{\mathbb{X}}$}.

(6.1)

Using the Mecke equation it is not difficult to see that
the infinite clusters in
\xi
are deletion stable if
N_{ds}=0
a.s., where

\displaystyle N_{ds}:=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{N^{\infty}(x,\xi)\geq 2\}\,\eta(dx).

(6.2)

Theorem 6.1
.

Assume that
\xi
is irreducible and that the infinite clusters of
\xi
are deletion stable.
Then
\xi
has
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely at most one infinite cluster.

We prove Theorem
6.1
in stages.
Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
be random elements
of
{\mathbb{X}}
, which are a.s. pairwise distinct.
In accordance with Section
3

we define a random connection model
\xi^{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}}
based
on the point process
\eta+\delta_{Y_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{Y_{n}}

as follows. We connect
Y_{1}
with
the points in
\eta
using independent connection decisions which
are independent of
\xi
.
We then proceed inductively finally connecting
Y_{n}
to

\eta+\delta_{Y_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{Y_{n-1}}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose that

B\in{\mathcal{X}}
with
\lambda(B)\in(0,\infty)
and let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}

be independent random variables with distribution
\lambda_{B}/\lambda(B)
,
independent of
\xi
. Assume that the infinite clusters of
\xi
are deletion stable, then

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(N^{\infty}(Y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}})\geq 2)\,\lambda^{2}(d(x_{1},x_{2}))=0.

(6.3)

Proof.

It is useful to add a point
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
to a graph
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}

in the following explicit way.
There are measurable mappings
\pi_{n}\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such
that
\mu(\cdot\times{\mathbf{N}})=\sum_{n=1}^{|\mu|}\delta_{\pi_{n}(\mu)}
,
for each
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
.
Let
(\mu,x)\in{\mathbf{G}}\times{\mathbb{X}}
and
u=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in[0,1]^{\infty}
.
Define
\mu^{x}_{u}\in{\mathbf{G}}
as the graph with vertex measure
V(\mu)+\delta_{x}
,
edges from
\mu
and further edges between
\pi_{n}(\mu)
and
x
if

\varphi(\pi_{n}(\mu),x)\geq u_{n}
. Define
h(x,\mu,u):={\mathbf{1}}\{N^{\infty}(x,\mu^{x}_{u})\geq 2\}
.
Assume that
U
is a random element of
[0,1]^{\infty}
with
independent and uniformly distributed components, independent of
\xi
. Then

{\mathbf{1}}\{N^{\infty}(x,\xi^{x})\geq 2\}
has the same distribution as
h(x,\xi,U)

and deletion stability means that

\displaystyle\iiint h(x,\mu,u)\,\mathbb{P}(\xi\in d\mu)\,\lambda(dx)\,\mathbb{P}(U\in du)=0.

(6.4)

Given
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{X}}
we also have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{1}}\{N^{\infty}(Y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}})\geq 2\}\overset{d}{=}h(Y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n-1}},U_{n}),

where
U_{n}
is independent of the pair
(Y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n-1}})
and has the same distribution
as
U
. Therefore

\displaystyle\iint\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(N^{\infty}(Y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}})\geq 2)\,\lambda^{2}(d(x_{1},x_{2}))

\displaystyle=(\lambda(B))^{-1}\iiint\mathbb{E}h(y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n-1}},u)\,\lambda_{B}(dy_{n})\,\mathbb{P}(U\in du)\,\lambda^{2}(d(x_{1},x_{2}))

\displaystyle=(\lambda(B))^{-n}\iiint\mathbb{E}h(y_{n},\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1}},u)\,\mathbb{P}(U\in du)\lambda_{B}^{n}(d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}))\,\lambda^{2}(d(x_{1},x_{2})),

where we have used the definition of
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n-1}}
. From the Mecke equation we obtain that the above equals

\displaystyle(\lambda(B))^{-n}\mathbb{E}\iiint h(y_{n},\xi,u){\mathbf{1}}\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1}\in B\}\,\lambda_{B}(dy_{n})\,\mathbb{P}(U\in du)\,\eta^{(n+1)}(d(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1})).

By (
6.4
), the integral
\iint h(y,\xi,u)\,\lambda_{B}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}(U\in du)
does almost
surely vanish. This concludes the proof.
∎

For given
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{X}}
let
A(x_{1},x_{2})
be the event that the clusters
C^{x_{1}}(\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}})
and

C^{x_{2}}(\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}})
are infinite and not connected.
Further, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
let
B_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})
be the event that
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are connected
in
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}}
, where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are defined in Lemma
6.2
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
6.2

be in force. Then for a given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(A(x_{1},x_{2})\cap B_{n}(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\lambda^{2}(d(x_{1},x_{2}))=0.

(6.5)

Proof.

We can remove the points
Y_{n},\ldots,Y_{1}
from

\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}}
one by one. Each time we
can apply Lemma
6.2
. Hence removing
Y_{i}
(for
i\leq n
) cannot
split the cluster of
Y_{i}
in
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i}}

into more than one infinite cluster.
Take
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that
B_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})
holds and assume
for the sake of contradiction that
A(x_{1},x_{2})
holds. In particular

C^{x_{1}}(\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}})
and
C^{x_{2}}(\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}})
are vertex disjoint, so that
there must be an
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that
x_{1},x_{2}
are connected
in
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i}}
but not in
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i-1}}
.
Hence, the removal of
Y_{i}
would split the cluster of
Y_{i}
in
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{i}}

into two infinite clusters. This is a contradiction, showing that
almost surely
B_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})\subset A^{c}(x_{1},x_{2})
for
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

We need to show that almost surely two
points of
\eta
cannot belong to two different infinite clusters. By the Mecke equation
(
3.1
) for
n=2
the latter is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(A(x_{1},x_{2}))\,\lambda^{2}(d(x_{1},x_{2}))=0.

(6.6)

The following arguments apply to
\lambda^{2}
-a.e.
(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}
. By Proposition
5.1
(vi)
there exist a set
B\in{\mathcal{X}}
with
0<\lambda(B)<\infty
, an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}

with distribution
\lambda_{B}/\lambda(B)
such that
\mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2}))>0
, where

\displaystyle B^{\prime}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\{x_{1}\leftrightarrow x_{2}\text{ in }\Xi^{\prime}[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]\}.

We can couple the random graphs
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}}
and

\Xi^{\prime}[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]
in such a way that
\xi^{x_{1},x_{2}}
and
\Xi^{\prime}[x_{1},x_{2},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}]

are independent and every edge in the latter graph
is also present in the former. Then
B^{\prime}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})
implies
B_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})
and we obtain
from Lemma
6.3
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A(x_{1},x_{2})\cap B^{\prime}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2}))=0.

By the above coupling the events
A(x_{1},x_{2})
and
B^{\prime}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2})
are independent.
Hence
\mathbb{P}(A(x_{1},x_{2}))=0
, as required.
∎

In Theorem
6.4
below we will see
that deletion stability of infinite clusters is in fact necessary
for uniqueness of the infinite cluster.
In fact, uniqueness implies an even stronger property.
Following
[
32
]
we call a graph
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}

2
-indivisible
if
the removal of a finite number of vertices results in at most
one infinite cluster. This means that
the graph
\mu[V(\mu)-\nu]
has at most one infinite component
for each finite
\nu\leq V(\mu)
. If
\xi
is almost surely

2
-indivisible, then, in particular, there is at most one infinite cluster.

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume that
\xi

has almost surely at most one infinite cluster. Then

\xi
is almost surely
2
-indivisible. In particular
the infinite cluster of
\xi
is deletion stable.

Proof.

Let
A_{\infty}
denote set of all
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
such that
\mu
has at least two infinite clusters.
Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By the uniqueness assumption and the Mecke equation (
3.5
) we have

\displaystyle 0=\int\mathbb{P}(\xi\in A_{\infty})\,\lambda^{n}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))=\mathbb{E}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\xi-\delta_{x_{1}}-\cdots-\delta_{x_{n}}\in A_{\infty}\}\,\eta^{(n)}(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})).

Since
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is arbitrary, this proves the first result.

If
x\in\eta
satisfies
N^{\infty}(x,\xi)\geq 2
then
\xi-\delta_{x}\in A_{\infty}
.
Therefore by the Mecke equation (
3.1
) we obtain the second assertion.
∎

red

7
A spatial Markov property

We again consider a general RCM
\xi
based on a Poisson process
\eta
on
{\mathbb{X}}
with diffuse intensity measure
\lambda
.
Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
. In the next section we shall establish and exploit a useful explicit change of measure for the distribution of
C^{v}=C^{v}(\xi^{v})
.
This is possible since for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
the conditional distribution of
C^{v}_{n+1}
given
C^{v}_{\leq n}

can be described in terms of a RCM driven by Poisson process with a thinned intensity measure.
In this section we derive a general version of this
spatial Markov property
.

Let
\nu
be a locally finite and diffuse
measure on
{\mathbb{X}}
. Then we denote by
\Pi_{\nu}
the distribution
of a Poisson process with this intensity measure.
We define a kernel
K_{\nu}
from
{\mathbf{N}}\times{\mathbf{N}}
to
{\mathbb{X}}
,
by

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},dx):=\bar{\varphi}(\mu,x)\varphi(\mu^{\prime},x)\nu(dx),

(7.1)

where we recall the definitions (
3.7
).
Proposition
7.2
will provide an interpretation of this kernel.
Denoting by
0
the zero measure, we note that

\displaystyle K_{\nu}(0,\mu^{\prime},dx)=\varphi(\mu^{\prime},x)\nu(dx),\quad K_{\nu}(\mu,0,dx)=\bar{\varphi}(\mu,x)\nu(dx).

(7.2)

We write
K_{\nu}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}):=K_{\nu}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},\cdot)
.
Note that
K_{\lambda}(0,\mu,{\mathbb{X}})=\varphi_{\lambda}(\mu)
; see (
3.7
).

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
,
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
and
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
let
\Gamma^{v}_{n}(\lambda,\mu,\cdot)
denote the
distribution of a random graph
\xi_{n}
defined as follows.
Let
\xi_{n}^{\prime}
be a RCM based on
\eta_{n}+C^{v}_{n}(\mu)
,
where
\eta_{n}
is a Poisson process with intensity measure

K_{\lambda}(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}(\mu),0)
,
and where we recall that
C^{v}_{\leq{-1}}:=0
.
Remove in
\xi_{n}^{\prime}
all edges between
vertices from
C^{v}_{n}(\mu)
to obtain a random graph
\xi_{n}^{\prime\prime}
. Finally
set
\xi_{n}:=C^{v}_{\leq n}(\mu)\oplus\xi_{n}^{\prime\prime}
, with an obvious definition of the operation
\oplus
.
We set
C^{v}_{\leq 0}(\mu):=\delta_{v}
, which is the graph with vertex set
\{v\}
and
no edges.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{v}\in\cdot\mid C^{v}_{\leq n})=\Gamma^{v}_{n}(\lambda,C^{v}_{\leq n},\cdot),\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

(7.3)

Proof.

This follows from the proof of
[
20
, Lemma 3.3]
;
see also Proposition 2 in
[
29
]
.
Essentially
the assertion is equivalent to equation (3.6) in this proof. The arguments given there apply to a RCM on a general state space
{\mathbb{X}}
and not only to
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
∎

A quick consequence of Theorem
7.1
is that

\{(V(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}),V(C^{v}_{n}))\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a Markov process.

Proposition 7.2
.

The sequence
\{(V(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}),V(C^{v}_{n}))\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a Markov
process with transition kernel

\displaystyle(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\mapsto\int{\mathbf{1}}\{(\mu+\mu^{\prime},\psi)\in\cdot\}\,\Pi_{K_{\lambda}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}(d\psi).

We also note that

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},{\mathbb{X}})\leq\int\varphi(\mu^{\prime},x)\,\lambda(dx)\leq\iint\varphi(y,x)\,\mu^{\prime}(dy)\,\lambda(dx),

where we have used the Bernoulli inequality. Hence

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(\mu,\mu^{\prime},{\mathbb{X}})\leq\int D_{\varphi}(y)\,\mu^{\prime}(dy).

(7.4)

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then we have for
\lambda
-a.e.
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
that

\mathbb{P}(|C^{v}_{n}|<\infty)=1
.

Proof.

We can proceed by induction.
For
n=0
the assertion is trivial.
Assume that
\mathbb{P}(|C^{v}_{n}|<\infty)=1
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
From Proposition
7.2
we know that
the conditional distribution of
V(C^{v}_{n+1})
given

(V(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}),V(C_{n}^{v}))
is that of a Poisson process
with intensity measure
K_{\lambda}(V(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}),V(C_{n}^{v}))
.
By (
7.4
) we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|C^{v}_{n+1}|\mid(V(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}),V(C_{n}^{v}))]\leq\int D_{\varphi}(y)\,C_{n}^{v}(dy)

which is for
\lambda
-a.e.
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
a.s. finite by our general assumption (
1.1
)
and induction hypothesis.
∎

The following useful property of the kernel
K_{\lambda}
can easily be proved by induction.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in{\mathbf{N}}
.
Then

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(0,\mu_{0})+K_{\lambda}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})+\cdots+K_{\lambda}(\mu_{0}+\cdots+\mu_{n-1},\mu_{n})=K_{\lambda}(0,\mu_{0}+\cdots+\mu_{n}).

8
Perturbation formulas

In the next sections we vary the intensity measure
\lambda
and
consider
t\lambda
for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. We fix
v\in{\mathbb{X}}

and let
\mathbb{P}_{t}
be a probability
measure governing a RCM
\xi
based on
\eta
, where
\eta

is a Poisson process with intensity measure
t\lambda
. The associated expectation is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{t}
.
Recall the definition (
3.7
).

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\tilde{\xi}
be a RCM based on a Poisson process
\tilde{\eta}
with finite
intensity measure
\nu
. Let
f\colon{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,\infty)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}f(\tilde{\xi})=\mathbb{E}_{1}f(\tilde{\xi})t^{|\tilde{\eta}|}e^{(1-t)\nu({\mathbb{X}})}

Proof.

It is well-known that

\displaystyle\Pi_{t\nu}=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{\mu\in\cdot\}t^{|\mu|}e^{(1-t)\nu({\mathbb{X}})}\,\Pi_{\nu}(d\mu),\quad t\geq 0.

(8.1)

This follows, for instance from
[
25
, Exercise 3.7]

and an easy calculation. The assertion then follows by conditioning, using
the kernel
\Gamma
in (
3.2
).
∎

Proposition 8.2
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t_{0}>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(C^{v}_{\leq n}\in\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{t_{0}}{\mathbf{1}}\{C^{v}_{\leq n}\in\cdot\}(t/t_{0})^{\big|C^{v}_{\leq n}\big|-1}e^{(t_{0}-t)\varphi_{\lambda}\big(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}}\big)}.

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider the special case
t_{0}=1
.
The general case can be proved similarly or can be derived from the special case.
We omit the dependence on
v
in our notation by writing
C_{n}:=C^{v}_{n}
,
and
C_{\leq n}:=C^{v}_{\leq n}
. Given
\mu\in{\mathbf{G}}
we let
C_{n}^{+}(\mu)
denote the graph
\mu[V(C_{n-1}(\mu))+V(C_{n}(\mu))]

with the edges between vertices of
C_{n-1}(\mu)
removed.

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,\infty)
be measurable.
By Theorem
7.1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}f(C_{\leq n})=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int f(C_{\leq{n-1}}\oplus C_{n}^{+}(\mu))\,\Gamma_{n-1}(t\lambda,C_{\leq{n-1}},d\mu).

By (
7.4
) we have

\displaystyle K_{t\lambda}(C_{\leq{n-2}},C_{n-1},{\mathbb{X}})\leq t\int D_{\varphi}(y)\,C_{n-1}(dy)

which is almost surely finite by Corollary
7.3
.
By definition of
\Gamma_{n}
and the thinning properties of a Poisson
process, the distribution of
C^{+}_{n}(\cdot)
under

\Gamma_{n-1}(t\lambda,C_{\leq{n-1}},\cdot)
is that of a RCM
driven by a Poisson process with intensity measure

K_{t\lambda}(C_{\leq{n-2}},C_{n-1})

with additional independent connections to
V(C_{n-1})
; see also Proposition
7.2
.
Therefore we obtain from Lemma
8.1
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}f(C_{\leq n})=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int f(C_{\leq{n-1}}\oplus C_{n}^{+}(\mu))e^{(1-t)K_{\lambda}(C_{\leq{n-2}},C_{n-1},{\mathbb{X}})}t^{|\mu|}\,\Gamma_{n-1}(\lambda,C_{\leq{n-1}},d\mu).

Iterating this identity yields that the above equals

\displaystyle\idotsint

\displaystyle f(C_{1}^{+}(\mu_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n}^{+}(\mu_{n}))e^{(1-t)K_{\lambda}(\delta_{v}+\mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{n-2},\mu_{n-1},{\mathbb{X}})}\cdots e^{(1-t)K_{\lambda}(\delta_{v},\mu_{1},{\mathbb{X}})}t^{|\mu_{n}|}\cdots t^{|\mu_{1}|}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n-1}(\lambda,C_{1}^{+}(\mu_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n-1}^{+}(\mu_{n-1}),d\mu_{n})\cdots\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,\delta_{v},d\mu_{1}).

By Lemma
7.4
this equals

\displaystyle\idotsint

\displaystyle f(C_{1}^{+}(\mu_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n}^{+}(\mu_{n}))e^{(1-t)K_{\lambda}(0,\delta_{v}+\mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{n-1},{\mathbb{X}})}t^{|\mu_{1}|+\cdots+|\mu_{n}|}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{n-1}(\lambda,C_{1}^{+}(\mu_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{n-1}^{+}(\mu_{n-1}),d\mu_{n})\cdots\Gamma_{0}(\lambda,[\delta_{v}],d\mu_{1}).

By Theorem
7.1

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}f(C_{\leq n})=\mathbb{E}_{1}f(C_{\leq n})t^{|C_{\leq n}|-1}e^{(1-t)\varphi_{\lambda}(C_{\leq{n-1}})}

and hence the assertion.
∎

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
t_{0}>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(C^{v}\in\cdot,|C^{v}|<\infty)=\mathbb{E}_{t_{0}}{\mathbf{1}}\{C^{v}\in\cdot,|C^{v}|<\infty\}(t/t_{0})^{|C^{v}|-1}e^{(t_{0}-t)\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})}.

(8.2)

Proof.

Again it is sufficient to consider the special case
t_{0}=1
.
By Proposition
8.2
the distribution

\mathbb{P}_{t}(C^{v}_{n}\in\cdot)
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}_{1}(C^{v}_{n}\in\cdot)
with Radon–Nikodym derivative

M^{v}_{n}:=t^{|C^{v}_{\leq n}|-1}e^{(1-t)\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v}_{\leq{n-1}})}
.
In particular
\{M^{v}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}
is a (non-negative) martingale with respect to
\{\sigma(C^{v}_{\leq n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}

and converges therefore a.s. towards
M^{v}_{\infty}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}M^{v}_{n}
.
By
[
37
, Theorem VII.6.1]
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{t}(C^{v}\in\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{1}{\mathbf{1}}\{C^{v}\in\cdot\}M^{v}_{\infty}+\mathbb{E}_{t}{\mathbf{1}}\{C^{v}\in\cdot,M^{v}_{\infty}=\infty\}.

On the event
\{|C^{v}|<\infty\}
we clearly have

\displaystyle M^{v}_{\infty}=t^{|C^{v}|-1}e^{(1-t)\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})}

which is finite. This concludes the assertion.
∎

Let
f\colon{\mathbf{G}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a measurable mapping.
Define for
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

\displaystyle F_{n}(\xi^{v}):=f(C^{v}){\mathbf{1}}\{|C^{v}|=n\},\

\displaystyle\ f^{v}_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}F_{n}(\xi^{v}),

(8.3)

\displaystyle F_{\leq n}(\xi^{v}):=f(C^{v}){\mathbf{1}}\{|C^{v}|\leq n\},\

\displaystyle\ f^{v}_{\leq n}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}F_{\leq n}(\xi^{v}),

(8.4)

\displaystyle F(\xi^{v}):=f(C^{v}){\mathbf{1}}\{|C^{v}|<\infty\},\

\displaystyle\ f^{v}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}F(\xi^{v}).

(8.5)

We also write

|f|^{v}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}|F(\xi^{v})|
and define
|f|^{v}_{n}(t)
and

|f|^{v}_{\leq n}(t)
similarly. We are interested in the analytic properties of
the function
f^{v}(t)
under the assumption
|f|^{v}(t)<\infty
. A key
example is the
position dependent cluster density

\displaystyle\kappa^{v}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{t}|C^{v}|^{-1},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(8.6)

Our terminology is motivated by the stationary marked case
(see Lemma

) and also supported by the Mecke equation,
implying

\displaystyle\int t\kappa^{v}(t)\,\lambda_{B}(dv)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int|C^{v}(\xi)|^{-1}\,\eta_{B}(dv),\quad B\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Suppose that
|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0})<\infty
for some
t_{0}>0
and

\displaystyle f^{v}_{n}(t)=\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{n-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,\nu_{f,_{n},t_{0}}(du),

(8.7)

where the signed measure
\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}
is defined by

\displaystyle\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}(\cdot):=\mathbb{E}_{t_{0}}{\mathbf{1}}\{\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})\in\cdot\}e^{t_{0}\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})}F_{n}(\xi^{v}).

(8.8)

By Corollary

this is a locally finite signed measure on

.
It follows from Theorem

that the function

is monotone decreasing on
(0,\infty)

\displaystyle|f|^{v}_{n}(t)\leq\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{n-1}|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0}),\quad t\geq t_{0}.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t_{0}>0
. If

, then for

\displaystyle f_{n}^{v}(t)=\frac{t^{n}}{t_{0}^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}[0,u]e^{-tu}\,du.

Proof.

We obtain from (
8.7
)
that

\displaystyle f^{v}_{n}(t)=\frac{t^{n}}{t_{0}^{n-1}}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{u\leq s\}e^{-ts}\,ds\,\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}(du).

(8.9)

Since
\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}
is locally finite, we can apply Fubini’s theorem to obtain the assertion.
∎

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t_{0}>0
. If
|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0})<\infty
, then the function
f^{v}_{n}

is analytic on
(t_{0},\infty)
and for
t>t_{0}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f_{n}^{v}(t)=\frac{nt^{n-1}}{t_{0}^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}[0,u]e^{-tu}\,du-\frac{t^{n}}{t_{0}^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}u\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}[0,u]e^{-tu}\,du.

(8.10)

Proof.

Let

\Omega_{t_{0}}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re(z)>t_{0}\}
,
and extend
f^{v}_{n}
to
\Omega_{t_{0}}
by setting

\displaystyle f^{v}_{n}(z):=\frac{z^{n}}{t_{0}^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}[0,u]e^{-zu}\,du,\quad z\in\Omega_{t_{0}}.

By (

) we have

\displaystyle|\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}[0,u]|\leq e^{t_{0}u}|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0}).

Since
|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0})<\infty

this implies that
f^{v}_{n}
is a complex analytic function on
\Omega_{t_{0}}
. Since
(t_{0},\infty)\subset

\Omega_{t_{0}}\cap{\mathbb{R}}
,
the restriction of this function to
(t_{0},\infty)
is real analytic. The formula (

)
follows from Lemma
8.4
the product rule of calculus and the Leibniz rule for differentiating integrals.
The latter can be applied since for each
\varepsilon>0
and all

\displaystyle u|\nu_{f,n,t_{0}}[0,u]|e^{-tu}\leq ue^{(t_{0}-t)u}|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0})\leq ue^{-\varepsilon u}|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0})

uniformly for

(8.11)

Lemma 8.6
.

Let

By Theorem

,

\displaystyle f_{n}^{v}(t)=\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{t_{0}}\big[F_{n}(\xi^{v})e^{(t_{0}-t)\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})}\big].

Lemma 8.7
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t_{0}>0
. If
|f|^{v}_{\leq n}(t_{0})<\infty
, then
function
f^{v}_{\leq n}

and for

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f_{\leq n}^{v}(t)=t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[M^{v}_{t}F_{\leq n}(\xi^{v})\big].

(8.13)

The result follows from the definition of

Theorem 8.8
.

Let
0<t_{0}<t_{1}<\infty
. Assume for each
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]

that
|f|^{v}(t)<\infty
. Assume moreover that for each
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[t_{0}+\varepsilon,t_{1}]}\left|\sum_{k>n}\frac{d}{dt}f_{k}^{v}(t)\right|=0.

(8.14)

Then
f^{v}
is continuously differentiable on
(t_{0},t_{1}]

with derivative given by

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}t^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}_{\leq n}(t)=t^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}_{n}(t).

(8.15)

Proof.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Since
|f|^{v}_{\leq n}(t_{0})\leq|f|^{v}(t_{0})<\infty
, we can apply Lemma
8.7

to obtain that the function
f^{v}_{\leq n}
is analytic on
(t_{0},\infty)
, with derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f_{\leq n}^{v}(t)=t^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d}{dt}f_{n}^{v}(t)=t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[M^{v}_{t}F_{\leq n}(\xi^{v})\big],\quad t>t_{0}.

By dominated convergence

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{v}_{\leq n}(t)=f^{v}(t).

Furthermore we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}(t)-\frac{d}{dt}f_{\leq n}^{v}(t)\right|=t^{-1}\left|\sum_{k>n}\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}_{k}(t)\right|.

By assumption (
8.14
) this tends to zero uniformly
in
t\in[t_{0}+\varepsilon,t_{1}]
for each
\varepsilon>0
.
A standard result of analysis gives us that

f^{v}
is continuously differentiable on
(t_{0},t_{1}]
with derivative given by the right-hand side of
(
8.15
).
∎

Theorem 8.9
.

Let
0<t_{0}<t_{1}<\infty
. Assume for each
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]

that
|f|^{v}(t)<\infty
. Assume moreover that for each
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[t_{0}+\varepsilon,t_{1}]}\sum_{k>n}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big|M^{v}_{t}F_{k}(\xi^{v})\big|=0.

(8.16)

Then
f^{v}
is continuously differentiable on
(t_{0},t_{1}]

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}(t)=t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[M^{v}_{t}F(\xi^{v})\big].

(8.17)

Proof.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Since
|f|^{v}_{n}(t_{0})\leq|f|^{v}_{\leq n}(t_{0})\leq|f|^{v}(t_{0})<\infty
, then by Lemma
8.5

the function
f^{v}_{n}
is analytic on
(t_{0},\infty)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{d}{dt}f_{n}^{v}(t)\right|=t^{-1}\left|\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[M^{v}_{t}F_{n}(\xi^{v})\big]\right|\leq t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big|M^{v}_{t}F_{n}(\xi^{v})\big|,\quad t>t_{0}.

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{k>n}\frac{d}{dt}f_{k}^{v}(t)\right|\leq t^{-1}\sum_{k>n}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big|M^{v}_{t}F_{n}(\xi^{v})\big|.

By assumption (
8.16
) this tends to zero uniformly
in
t\in[t_{0}+\varepsilon,t_{1}]
for each
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore by Theorem
8.8
the function
f^{v}(t)
is continuously differentiable on
(t_{0},t_{1}]
with derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}(t)=t^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}f^{v}_{n}(t)=t^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[M^{v}_{t}F(\xi^{v})\big],

where the last equality we get from Fubini’s theorem, since by assumption (
8.16

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\big|M^{v}_{t}F(\xi^{v})\big|=\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\big|M^{v}_{t}F_{n}(\xi^{v})\big|<\infty.

In this section we prove in particular that the position dependent cluster density

Theorem 10.1
.

Suppose that
f\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a function satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)\sqrt{n\log n}=0.

(10.1)

Then

is for each

continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)
with derivative given by (
8.17
).

We prove the theorem via some lemmas, partially following
the proof of
[
6
, (LP) (3.6)]
. Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
.
For
t>0
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define

\displaystyle p^{v}_{n}(t):=\mathbb{P}_{t}(|C^{v}|=n).

Specializing definition (
8.8
) in the case
f\equiv 1
we set

\displaystyle\nu^{v}_{n}(\cdot):=\mathbb{E}_{1}{\mathbf{1}}\{\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})\in\cdot\}{\mathbf{1}}\{|C^{v}|=n\}e^{\varphi_{\lambda}(C^{v})}.

(10.2)

Then we obtain from (
8.7
) in the case

that

\displaystyle p^{v}_{n}(t)=t^{n-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,\nu^{v}_{n}(du).

(10.3)

Since
p^{v}_{1}(t)=e^{-tD_{\varphi}(v)}

we have

\displaystyle\nu^{v}_{1}=\delta_{D_{\varphi}(v)},\quad v\in{\mathbb{X}}.

(10.4)

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
u>0
. Then

\displaystyle\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]\leq\left(\frac{eu}{n-1}\right)^{n-1},

(10.5)

where the right-hand side has to be interpreted as
1
if
n=1
.

Proof.

In view of (
10.4
) we can assume that
n\geq 2
.
Since
p^{v}_{n}(t)\leq 1
for
t>0
, we have that

\displaystyle t^{-n+1}\geq\int_{0}^{u}e^{-tu}\nu^{v}_{n}(du)\geq e^{-tu}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u).

Optimizing over
t\in(0,\infty)
yields the assertion.
∎

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t>0
. Then

\displaystyle p_{n}^{v}(t)=t^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]e^{-tu}\,du.

Proof.

The assertion follows from Lemma
8.4
.
∎

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

t\mapsto p_{n}^{v}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}p_{n}^{v}(t)=nt^{n-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]e^{-tu}\,du-t^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}u\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]e^{-tu}\,du

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{d}{dt}p_{n}^{v}(t)\Big|\leq\frac{n}{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]\left|1-\frac{ut}{n}\right|t^{n}e^{-tu}\,du.

(10.7)

The next lemma provides a bound for the above right-hand side.

Lemma 10.5
.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{X}}
,
n\geq 2
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. Then we have
for all
t>0
that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]\left|1-\frac{ut}{n}\right|t^{n}e^{-tu}du\leq\delta p^{v}_{n}(t)+(1-\delta)^{n}e^{\delta n}+(1+\delta)^{n}e^{-\delta n}.

(10.8)

Proof.

By Lemma
10.3
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]\left|1-\frac{ut}{n}\right|t^{n}e^{-tu}du

\displaystyle\leq\delta p^{v}_{n}(t)+\int_{\left|1-\frac{tu}{n}\right|>\delta}\nu^{v}_{n}[0,u]t^{n}e^{-tu}\left|1-\frac{tu}{n}\right|du

\displaystyle\leq\delta p^{v}_{n}(t)+\left(\frac{e}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}\int_{\left|1-\frac{tu}{n}\right|>\delta}t^{n}u^{n-1}e^{-tu}\left|1-\frac{tu}{n}\right|\,du.

Changing variables yields that the above equals

\displaystyle\delta p^{v}_{n}(t)+\left(\frac{e}{n-1}\right)^{n-1}\int_{\left|1-\frac{u}{n}\right|>\delta}u^{n-1}e^{-u}\left|1-\frac{u}{n}\right|\,du.

Splitting the integral on the above right-hand side into two
pieces corresponding to
tu<n(1-\delta)
and

of
(x,p)
in
\xi^{(x,p)}
, that is the points in
\eta
which are
directly connected to
(x,p)
in
\xi^{(x,p)}
. For a Borel set
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we let
\Psi_{B}(x,p)

denote the restriction of
\Psi(x,p)
to
B\times{\mathbb{M}}
.
We further denote
d_{\varphi}(p):=\int d(p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dq)
so that

d_{\varphi}=\int d_{\varphi}(p)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)
. By Lemma
11.5
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{t}M_{n,\star}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{d}(B_{n})d_{\varphi}\frac{t^{2}}{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{d}(B_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)+t\lambda_{d}(B_{n})d_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{d}(B_{n})+\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}\big(td_{\varphi}(p)-\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)\big)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{d}(B_{n})+\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}\big(\mathbb{E}_{t}|\Psi(x,p)|-\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)\big)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{d}(B_{n})+\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}\left(\mathbb{E}_{t}|\Psi_{B^{c}_{n}}(x,p)|+\mathbb{E}_{t}\big(|\Psi_{B_{n}}(x,p)|-N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)\big)\right)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),

Clearly
\mathbb{E}_{t}|\Psi_{B^{c}_{n}}(x,p)|
is increasing in
t
.
We shall now argue that

\mathbb{E}_{t}\big[\Psi_{B_{n}}(x,p)-N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)\big]
is increasing in
t
.
Applying the Margulis-Russo formula (
3.9
)
similarly as in the proof of Lemma
11.5
, we see that
it is sufficient to check that
\Psi_{B_{n}}(x,p)-N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)

cannot strictly decrease when adding a point
(y,q)\in B_{n}\times{\mathbb{M}}
to
\eta
.
Assume first that
(y,q)
is not directly connected to

(x,p)
. Then
\Psi_{B_{n}}(x,p)
does not change while
N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)
can
only decrease (namely by connecting some of the clusters in

\xi_{n,\star}
which are connected to
(x,p)
in

\xi^{(x,p)}_{n,\star}
). Assume now that
(y,q)
is directly
connected to
(x,p)
, so that
\Psi_{B_{n}}(x,p)
increases by one. In
that case
N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)
can increase by at most
1
,
namely if some of the clusters in
\xi_{n,\star}
which are not
connected to
(x,p)
in
\xi^{(x,p)}_{n,\star}
get connected to the
new point
(y,q)
while none of the clusters in
\xi_{n,\star}
which
are connected to
(x,p)
in
\xi^{(x,p)}_{n,\star}
are connected by

(y,q)
. This proves the asserted monotonicity and hence the
convexity assertion.
∎

Now we are in the position to prove the first main result in this section.

Theorem 11.7
.

The function
t\mapsto t\kappa(t)+d_{\varphi}t^{2}/2
is continuously differentiable
on
(0,\infty)
, convex on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and right differentiable at zero.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from Theorem

10.7
while the second follows from Lemmas

11.3
and
11.6
and the (elementary) fact that
the limit of a sequence of convex functions is convex. The
function is right differentiable at zero since
\kappa
is a
monotone function.
∎

In the final step of the proof of Theorem
11.1
we need to identify
the limits of the derivatives in Lemma
11.5
.

Lemma 11.8
.

Let
t\in[0,t_{0}]
. Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}N^{0}_{n}(x,p)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)

\displaystyle\geq\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{0}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}N^{+}_{n}(x,p)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)

\displaystyle\leq\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{+}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp).

Proof.

Similarly as in the proof of Proposition
4.5
by stationarity, we have

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{\star}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)=(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}N^{\star}(x,p,\xi^{(x,p)})\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Hence our task is to show that
N^{\star}(x,p,\xi^{(x,p)})
is well approximated by
N^{\star}_{n}(x,p)
.
For a given Borel set
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
we denote by
N^{0}_{B}(x,p)

the number of clusters in
\xi
to which the Poisson neighbors of
(x,p)

in
B\times{\mathbb{M}}
belong. Note that a.s.

\displaystyle N^{0}_{B}(x,p)+N^{0}_{B^{c}}(x,p)\geq N^{0}(x,p,\xi^{(x,p)}).

It is, moreover, easy to see that
N^{0}_{n}(x,p)\geq N^{0}_{B_{n}}(x,p)
a.s.
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It follows that a.s.

\displaystyle N^{0}_{n}(x,p)\geq N^{0}(x,p,\xi^{(x,p)})-N^{0}_{B_{n}^{c}}(x,p).

(11.5)

Obviously
N^{0}_{B_{n}^{c}}(x,p)
is dominated by the number
of points from
\eta_{B_{n}^{c}}
which are directly connected to
(x,p)
in
\xi^{(x,p)}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{0}_{B_{n}^{c}}(x,p)\leq t\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{y\in B^{c}_{n}\}\varphi(y-x,p,q)\,dy\,\mathbb{Q}(dq).

It now follows from (
11.5
) and exactly as in the proof of Lemma
11.3
that
for each
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}\iint{\mathbf{1}}\{x\in B_{n}\}N^{0}_{n}(x,p)\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)

\displaystyle\geq\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{0}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)-\varepsilon t.

This implies the first asserted inequality. The second follows from

N^{+}_{n}(x,p)\leq N^{+}(x,p,\xi^{(x,p)})
a.s..
∎

Proof of Theorem
11.1
.

The convex function in Theorem
11.7
is differentiable and approximated
by the differentiable convex functions

(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{t}M_{n,\star}+d_{\varphi}t^{2}/2
; see Lemmas
11.3

and
11.5
.
A classical result from convex analysis (see
[
36
, Theorem 25.7]
) implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(\lambda_{d}(B_{n}))^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}_{t}M_{n,\star}=\frac{d}{dt}t\kappa(t).

Therefore we obtain from Lemma
11.5
that the limes inferior in Lemma
11.8

coincides with the limes superior. Hence Lemma
11.8
yields

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{0}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)\leq\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{+}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp),

or

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{E}_{t}(N^{0}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})-N^{+}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)}))\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)\leq 0.

Since

\displaystyle N^{0}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})-N^{+}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})={\mathbf{1}}\{N^{\infty}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\geq 1\}(N^{\infty}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})-1),

we obtain

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}_{t}(N^{\infty}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\geq 2)\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)=0.

(11.6)

Using stationarity as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
we see, that this is equivalent to the assertion.
∎

The preceding proof yields the following corollary.

Corollary 11.9
.

The cluster density is continuously differentiable function and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(t\kappa(t))=1-\int\mathbb{E}_{t}N^{0}(0,p,\xi^{(0,p)})\,\mathbb{Q}(dp).

Remark 11.10
.

Assume that
\varphi(x,p,q)=\tilde{\varphi}(\|x\|,p,q)
and
t=1
,
for a measurable function
\tilde{\varphi}\colon[0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{M}}\times{\mathbb{M}}\to[0,1]

which is decreasing and right-continuous in the first coordinate.
Using the notation at (
2.2
) and (
4.3
) we define

\displaystyle W_{m,n}:=\frac{\|X_{m}-X_{n}\|}{\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}(Z_{m,n},Q_{m},Q_{n})},\quad m,n\in{\mathbb{N}},

(11.7)

where
\tilde{\varphi}^{-1}(s,p,q):=\inf\{r\geq 0:\varphi(r,p,q)\leq s\}
,
(s,p,q)\in[0,1]\times{\mathbb{M}}\times{\mathbb{M}}
.
Given
r>0
we define a RCM
\xi_{r}
with vertex set
\eta
by connecting
X_{m}
with
X_{n}

if
W_{m,n}\leq r
. Note that
W_{m,n}\leq r
iff

\displaystyle Z_{m,n}\leq\tilde{\varphi}(r^{-1}\|X_{m}-X_{n}\|,Q_{m},Q_{n}).

Since
\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\delta_{(r^{-1}X_{n},Q_{n})}
is under
\mathbb{P}_{1}
a Poisson process
with intensity measure
r^{d}\lambda_{d}\otimes\mathbb{Q}
, we hence have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{1}(\xi_{r}\in\cdot)=\mathbb{P}_{r^{d}}(\xi\in\cdot),\quad r>0,

(11.8)

i.e. a joint coupling of the RCMs with different intensity parameters. In the unmarked case this
construction can be found in
[
4
, Example 1.3]
.

Remark 11.11
.

Consider the setting of Remark
11.10

and the complete graph with vertex set
\eta
. We can interpret the random variable
(
11.7
) as
weight
of the edge between
(X_{m},Q_{m})
and
(X_{n},Q_{n})
.
As in
[
3
]
we define the associated
minimal spanning forest

T
as the
forest (a graph without cycles) with vertex set
\eta
and an edge between

(X_{m},Q_{m})
and
(X_{n},Q_{n})
if there is no path between these points with weights strictly
less than
W_{m,n}
. In special cases it was observed in
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
6
]

that there is a close relationship between the RCM
\xi_{r}
and
T
. For instance it was proved in

[
3
]
that the trees (clusters) of
T
are all infinite and can only have one or two ends.
Two-ended trees
T
can only occur if
r
equals the percolation threshold in which case
T
contains
all points of the infinite clusters (should they exist). It would be interesting to explore the consequences of deletion stability of
\xi_{r}
for
T
.

12
The stationary marked RCM: irreducibility and uniqueness

In this section we consider a stationary marked RCM
\xi
as
introduced in Section
4
.
When combined with Theorem
11.1
, Theorem
6.1

immediately yields the following result.

Theorem 12.1
.

An irreducible stationary
marked random connection model can almost surely have
at most one infinite cluster.

Remark 12.2
.

Theorems
12.1
and
6.4
show that an irreducible stationary
marked RCM is
2
-indivisible. In particular this holds
at the critical intensity
t_{c}
.
This provides some evidence for
the absence of doubly-infinite paths at criticality.
In fact, it is a common belief that in Euclidean space there
is no infinite cluster in the critical phase.

We now present several examples, starting with the classical stationary RCM;
see Example
4.6
.

Example 12.3
.

By Theorem
12.1
and Proposition
5.3

the (unmarked) stationary RCM can have at most one infinite component.
This generalizes
[
31
, Theorem 6.3]
, where it is assumed
that
\varphi(x)=\tilde{\varphi}(\|x\|)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for a
decreasing function
\tilde{\varphi}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
.
The proof there is based on an extension of
the approach from
[
9
]
to the continuum and is very different from ours.

Next we treat the simple case, where the connection
factorizes; see also
[
10
, Section 1.2]
.

Example 12.4
.

Let
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a symmetric
function with
0<m_{\psi}:=\int\psi(x)\,dx<\infty
and let
K\colon{\mathbb{M}}^{2}\to[0,1]

be measurable and symmetric. Assume that

\varphi(x,p,q)=\psi(x)K(p,q)
,
(x,p,q)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}^{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\varphi^{(n)}(x,p,q)=\psi^{\ast n}(x)K^{n}(p,q),\quad(x,p,q)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}^{2},

(12.1)

where
K^{1}=K
and
K^{n}(p,q)=\int K^{n-1}(p,r)K(r,q)\,\mathbb{Q}(dr)
,
n\geq 2
.
By Proposition
5.1
(iv) and Lemma
5.4
(i)

\xi
is irreducible iff

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}K^{n}(p,q)>0,\quad\mathbb{Q}^{2}\text{-a.e.\ $(p,q)\in{\mathbb{M}}^{2}$}.

We continue with the examples from Section
4
.

Example 12.5
.

Let us consider Example
4.8
.
Assume that there exists
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(for instance
x_{0}=0
) such for
\mathbb{Q}
-a.e.
K\in\mathcal{C}^{d}
there exists

\varepsilon>0
such that
B(x_{0},\varepsilon)\subset K
,
where
B(x_{0},\varepsilon)
denotes the ball with center
x_{0}
and radius
\varepsilon
.
Assume also that the function
V
is increasing w.r.t. set inclusion and that

V(K)>0
if
K\neq\emptyset
. Under these assumptions the model is
irreducible. As point
p_{0}
in (
5.15
) we can take the ball
B(x_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
,
where
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is chosen such that
\mathbb{Q}(\{K:B(x_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\subset K\})>0
.
To check (
5.12
) we take
K\in\mathcal{C}^{d}
such
that
B(x_{0},\varepsilon)\subset K
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
. We have

\displaystyle d_{\varphi}(B(x_{0},\varepsilon_{0}),K)=\int\big(1-e^{-V(B(x_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\cap(K+x))}\big)\,dx\geq\int\big(1-e^{-V(B(x_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\cap(B(x_{0}+x,\varepsilon))}\big)\,dx.

By assumption on
V
this is positive, since

\displaystyle\int{\mathbf{1}}\{B(x_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\cap B(x_{0}+x,\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset\}\,dx=\int{\mathbf{1}}\{B(0,\varepsilon_{0})\cap B(x,\varepsilon)\neq\emptyset\}\,dx>0.

We can now apply Theorem
12.1
to conclude that
the infinite cluster is unique. For the spherical Boolean model this result
can be found as Theorem 3.6 in
[
31
]
. For general Boolean models
(i.e.
\varphi(x,K,L)={\mathbf{1}}\{K\cap(L+x)\neq\emptyset\}
) the result seems to be new.

Example 12.6
.

The weighted RCM from Example
4.9

is irreducible. Indeed we can take any
p_{0}\in(0,1)
and
A=[p_{0},1)
. Furthermore
we have for all
p,q\in(0,1)
that
d_{\varphi}(p,q)=m_{\rho}g(p,q)^{-1}
, which is positive.
By Theorem
12.1
the infinite cluster is unique.
This was asserted in
[
18
]
without providing details of a proof.
A more detailed proof in a special case (based on the approach in
[
9
]
)
was given in
[
21
]
.

Example 12.7
.

Consider a stationary marked RCM with
{\mathbb{M}}
as the space of
all locally finite simple counting measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
\mathbb{Q}
be a distribution of a simple stationary point process

\chi
satisfying
\mathbb{Q}\{0\}=0
.
For
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
p\in{\mathbb{M}}
let
d(x,p)
be the distance
between
x
and
p
.
Similarly as in Example
12.6
we consider a connection function of the form

\displaystyle\varphi(x,p,q)=\rho(d(-x,p)^{-\alpha}d(x,q)^{-\alpha}\|x\|^{d})

for a decreasing function
\rho\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,1]
such
that
m_{\rho}:=\int\rho(\|x\|^{d})\,dx
is positive and finite
and where
\alpha>0
is a fixed parameter.
By stationarity,

\displaystyle\iint\varphi(x,p,q)\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}(d(p,q))\,dx

\displaystyle=\iint\rho(d(0,p)^{-\alpha}d(0,q)^{-\alpha}\|x\|^{d})\,dx\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}(d(p,q))

\displaystyle=m_{\rho}\int{\mathbf{1}}\{d(0,p)<\infty,d(0,q)<\infty\}d(0,p)^{\alpha}d(0,q)^{\alpha}\,\mathbb{Q}^{2}(d(p,q))

\displaystyle=m_{\rho}\bigg(\int d(0,p)^{\alpha}\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)\bigg)^{2}.

To ensure (
4.2
) we assume that
\int d(0,p)^{\alpha}\,\mathbb{Q}(dp)<\infty
,
which is a rather weak assumption. Since
m_{\rho}>0
it is clear
that
d_{\varphi}(p)
is positive whenever
p\neq 0
.

The function
\varphi(x,p,\cdot)
is for all

(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{M}}
non-decreasing with respect to the natural
partial ordering on
{\mathbb{M}}
. Therefore, if
\mathbb{Q}
is associated,
then Theorem
5.13
implies that
\xi
is irreducible.
For instance we might take
\mathbb{Q}
as the distribution
of a Poisson process; see e.g.
[
26
]
. Hence Theorem
12.1
applies.

Remark 12.8
.

Here we provide an example where
Theorem
5.13
applies but not Theorem
5.9
.
Consider Example
12.7
in the special case
d=1
.
We assume that
\rho
is strictly decreasing.
Take some
p_{0}\in{\mathbb{M}}
. We assert that
\mathbb{P}(C)=0
, where

\displaystyle C:=\{\text{$\varphi(x,p,\chi)\geq\varphi(x,p,p_{0})$ for $\lambda_{1}\otimes\mathbb{Q}$-a.e.\ $(x,p)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{M}}$}\}.

Since
\rho
is strictly decreasing and
\mathbb{P}(\chi=0)=0

the equation
\mathbb{P}(C)=0
is equivalent with
\mathbb{P}(C^{\prime})=0
, where

\displaystyle C^{\prime}:=\{\text{$d(x,\chi)\geq d(x,p_{0})$ for $\lambda_{1}$-a.e.\ $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}\}.

Let us first assume that
p_{0}[a,\infty)=0
for some
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
we have for each
c>0
that

\displaystyle C^{\prime}\subset\{\text{$d(x,\chi)>c$ for $\lambda_{1}$-a.e.\ $x>a+c$}\}=\{\text{$\chi[x-c,x+c]=0$ for $\lambda_{1}$-a.e.\ $x>a+c$}\}.

This event is contained in
\{\chi(a,a+2c)=0\}
, whose probability does not depend
on
a
and tends to
0
as
c\to\infty
; see
[
22
, Lemma 30.9 (i)]
.
Similarly we can treat the case
p_{0}(-\infty,a]=0
for some
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Let us assume now that
p_{0}
has atoms
\cdots<x_{-1}<x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots

with
\lim_{n\to-\infty}x_{n}=-\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=\infty
.
Take
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and assume that
\chi(x_{n},x_{n+1}]\geq 1
.
Then
d(x,\chi)\geq d(x,p_{0})
cannot hold for
\lambda_{1}
-a.e.
x\in[x_{n},x_{n+1}]
.
Hence
C^{\prime}\subset\bigcap\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\{\chi(x_{n},x_{n+1}]=0\}=\{\chi({\mathbb{R}})=0\}
.
Therefore
\mathbb{P}(C^{\prime})=0
so that the assumptions of Theorem
5.9

are not satisfied.
