LLMSat: A Large Language Model-Based Goal-Oriented Agent for Autonomous Space Exploration
David Maranto

Abstract
As spacecraft journey further from Earth with more complex missions, systems of greater autonomy and onboard intelligence are called for. Reducing reliance on human-based mission control becomes increasingly critical if we are to increase our rate of solar-system-wide exploration. Recent work has explored AI-based goal-oriented systems to increase the level of autonomy in mission execution. These systems make use of symbolic reasoning managers to make inferences from the state of a spacecraft and a handcrafted knowledge base, enabling autonomous generation of tasks and re-planning. Such systems have proven to be successful in controlled cases, but they are difficult to implement as they require human-crafted ontological models to allow the spacecraft to understand the world. Reinforcement learning has been applied to train robotic agents to pursue a goal. A new architecture for autonomy is called for. This work explores the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) as the high-level control system of a spacecraft. Using a systems engineering approach, this work presents the design and development of an agentic spacecraft controller by leveraging an LLM as a reasoning engine, to evaluate the utility of such an architecture in achieving higher levels of spacecraft autonomy. A series of deep space mission scenarios simulated within the popular game engine Kerbal Space Program (KSP) are used as case studies to evaluate the implementation against the requirements. It is shown the reasoning and planning abilities of present-day LLMs do not scale well as the complexity of a mission increases, but this can be alleviated with adequate prompting frameworks and strategic selection of the agent’s level of authority over the host spacecraft. This research evaluates the potential of LLMs in augmenting autonomous decision-making systems for future robotic space applications.

Chapter 1
Introduction

As spacecraft journey further from Earth with more complex missions, systems of greater autonomy and onboard intelligence are called for. Reducing reliance on human-based mission control becomes increasingly critical if we are to increase our rate of solar-system-wide exploration. This work explores the potential of
Large Language Models (LLMs)
to achieve higher levels of autonomy in spacecraft.
LLM
s have demonstrated remarkable emergent capabilities in high-level reasoning and planning tasks, and as such have seen recent success applied as embodied agents in robotic systems
[
63
]
. Most space systems are engineered to fulfill their objectives with a minimal likelihood of failure and under limited margins, considering the great expenses associated with space exploration, unlike typical terrestrial systems. This raises an important question of how to safely achieve higher levels of autonomy in such systems and what the risk-benefit tradeoff looks like. As of writing, no work has explored the integration of an
LLM
into the command and control architecture of a space system. The goal of this work is to design and develop an
LLM
-based spacecraft control system that can execute goal-oriented mission operations onboard to assess the utility of
LLM
s in achieving higher levels of spacecraft autonomy. This work is focused on space systems, but the key insights are transferable to any such system where minimal likelihood of failure can be tolerated, such as safety-critical systems.

1.1
Motivation

Presently, there are 36 active solar system probes, with this number expected to grow by over 60% in the coming decade
[
68
]
. Figure
1.1
illustrates the accelerating growth of exploration across the solar system.

Figure 1.1:

[

]
as of January 10th, 2024.

Each of these missions requires a trained operations team of hundreds of individuals consisting of scientists, engineers, and support staff to conduct operations planning, monitor the spacecraft, and solve problems. NASA operation centres are typically staffed 24 hours a day, 7 days a week
[
4
]
. This results in significant operating costs. The cost to operate OSIRIS-REx over its 9-year primary mission lifespan was USD$
283
million
[
13

. This accounted for over 50% of its development costs (USD$
588.5
million). If greater levels of onboard autonomy can reduce the operating costs of active missions by just 10%, the direct savings from this reduction alone would be sufficient to fund entirely new missions, thereby accelerating our exploration of the solar system.

Achieving higher levels of autonomy in our space systems also unlocks mission profiles that would be incredibly challenging to conduct with present-day technology. For example, robotic exploration of extreme environments like the subsurface oceans of Europa and Enceladus where a probe would have little to no communication with Earth for the duration of its mission. Or robotic care-taking of habitats and spacecraft like the Lunar Gateway, which is expected to be uncrewed up to 90% of its operational lifetime
[
15
]
. Elevating the autonomy level of the next generation of space missions is key to advancing scientific, technological, and commercial outcomes
[
7
]
. This technology is an accelerator for the evolution of humanity into an interplanetary species.

•

What advantages do neuro-symbolic agents like

Chapter 2
Background

2.1
Autonomous Systems

NASA
defines autonomy as
[
45
]
,

…the ability of a system to achieve goals while operating independently of external control. Autonomy is not the same as artificial intelligence (AI), but may make use of AI methods. Autonomy is not the same as automation, but often relies on automation as a building block.

Where a system is defined by the
NASA
systems engineering handbook as
[
22
]
,

…the combination of elements that function together to produce the capability required to meet a need. The elements include all hardware, software, equipment, facilities, personnel, processes, and procedures needed for this purpose.

An autonomous system therefore requires self-directedness to achieve its goals, and self-sufficiency to operate independently
[
16
]
. The purpose of autonomy technology is not necessarily about making a system intelligent (although this is one way to build autonomy), but rather to enable a system to achieve its objectives while operating independently. Autonomy is multifaceted and can be defined in terms of many capabilities.
NASA
provides a common set of definitions and functional decomposition of autonomous systems in their taxonomy (see figure
2.1
).

Figure 2.1:
NASA’s Autonomy Taxonomy. Adapted from
[
3
]
.

The focus of this work is on building a novel autonomous agent for reasoning and acting, although, as we shall see, a system that can reason and act implies it has situational and self-awareness, and may be able to collaborate through goal and task negotiation. These dimensions of autonomy are not necessarily mutually independent. Scaling to handle more complex problems with greater numbers of constraints within uncertain and dynamic environments through reasoning and acting is an unsolved problem
[
16
]
. Autonomy is identified by
NASA
a key enabler for space exploration in the following scenarios
[
16
]
:

•

When the cadence of decision-making exceeds communication constraints (delays, bandwidth, and communication windows)

•

When time-critical decisions (control, health, life-support, etc.) must be made on-board the system, vehicle, etc.

•

When decisions can be better made using rich on-board data compared to limited downlinked data (e.g., adaptive science)

•

When local decisions improve robustness and reduce the complexity of the system architecture

•

When autonomous decision-making can reduce system costs or improve performance

•

When variability in training, proficiency, etc. associated with manual control is unacceptable

Missions that travel to the furthest reaches of the solar system where communication is lagged upwards of several hours, or missions that require tunnelling underneath the surface of celestial bodies and so are cut off from communication with Earth over long periods are prime candidates for automation.

2.1.1
Paradigms for Robotic Autonomy

In the field of robotics, the concepts of connectionism, behaviourism, and computationalism represent three distinct approaches to designing artificial intelligence and autonomous systems. Cognitive scientists argue these three approaches may be overlapping aspects of cognition
[
59
]
, but they are helpful definitions to understand the field of robotics nonetheless.

Connectionism

Connectionism posits that intelligent behaviour emerges from the interactions of a large number of simple processing units, akin to neurons in the brain
[
43
]
. This approach is characterized by the use of artificial neural networks, where knowledge is represented through the strength of connections between units. Unlike traditional computational models that rely on explicit programming, connectionist systems learn and adapt through experience, altering the connections’ strength based on input and feedback. One of the key examples of connectionism in robotics is the Braitenburg vehicle, a concept conceived in a thought experiment by the cyberneticist Valentino Braitenberg which demonstrates how complex behaviours can arise from simple sensor-motor connections without explicit programming. These vehicles showcase the potential of connectionist systems to develop autonomous, agentic behaviour in robots through simple, interconnected networks (see figure
2.2
).

Figure 2.2:
The Braitenburg vehicle. Reproduced from
[
67
]
.

Behaviourism

Behaviourism asserts that all observable behaviours of a robot are direct responses to environmental stimuli, influenced by the robot’s history of interactions with its environment. This paradigm, anchored in the physical grounding hypothesis, stresses the significance of real-world interactions and experiences in moulding behaviour, as opposed to depending on pre-programmed instructions or internal models. This perspective is exemplified by Rodney Brooks’ Subsumption Architecture (see figure
2.3
), which structures robot behaviour in layers, where each layer responds to specific stimuli. More complex behaviours can override simpler ones. According to behaviourism, a robot’s actions result directly from sensory inputs and past experiences, eliminating the necessity for intricate internal cognitive processes or representations
[
51
]
. Consequently, this approach fosters the development of robots whose behaviours emerge from the combination of simpler, reflex-like responses to particular stimuli, rather than arising from predetermined notions or internal contemplations. This alignment with the Subsumption Architecture underscores the practical application of behaviourism in creating adaptive, responsive robotic systems.

Figure 2.3:
Brooke’s subsumption architecture. Higher behaviours can override or “subsume” lower-level behaviours. Reproduced from
[
5
]
.

Computationalism

Computationalism is grounded in the physical symbol system hypothesis, which asserts that intelligent behaviour arises from the manipulation of symbolic representations of information. This paradigm, pioneered by Newell and Simon in 1976
[
37
]
, posits that cognitive processes can be understood as computations performed on symbols, analogous to operations in a computer. Sensory inputs are transformed into symbolic representations, which are then processed, reasoned about, and used to guide decision-making and actions. Computationalism implies that the mind, or in the case of robots, artificial intelligence, functions as a complex computational system where intelligence is a product of symbolic processing. This approach has been influential in the development of systems that perform complex tasks like problem-solving, planning, and reasoning, by using algorithms to manipulate symbolic representations of knowledge. Unlike connectionism or behaviourism, computationalism emphasizes the importance of abstract reasoning and high-level cognitive functions, suggesting that intelligence and cognitive abilities are the result of sophisticated symbol manipulation processes rather than emergent properties of simple units or direct responses to environmental stimuli.

Figure 2.4:
Newell’s system levels. Adapted from
[
43
]
.

Summary

Across all three paradigms, the idea that intelligence and autonomy are hierarchical is a recurring theme. This is immediately clear in the case of the subsumption architecture (figure
2.3
) and Newell’s system levels (figure
2.4
), but it is also evident in the connectionist paradigm, where each layer of a neural network is known to process information at progressively higher levels of abstraction, with the lowest level inputs to the network being signals, and the highest being recognition of geometric shapes and objects in the case of image recognition networks, or reasoning and language in the case of
LLMs
. This aligns with the theory that intelligence is emergent. In recognition of this, it is clear that to design systems of greater autonomy, and therefore intelligence, they must possess architectures that allow them to operate at higher levels of abstraction.
LLMs
and their progeny may be one such architecture.

2.2
Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs)
are neural network models trained for next-word prediction on large corpora of human natural language data. In a rigorous sense, language models merely function as conditional probability models, using the input to predict the next output
[
69
]
, but in doing so, they learn an ontological model of the world.

2.2.1
Architecture

At the heart of an
LLM
is a transformer-based neural architecture. Transformers were introduced in 2017 by the landmark paper “Attention is all you need”
[
58
]
as a solution to reduce the computational cost of training the then state-of-the-art recurrent neural network architectures in
Natural Language Processing (NLP)
tasks. Transformers forgo recurrence altogether and instead rely entirely on an attention mechanism to learn global dependencies between input and output
[
58
]
. Attention is a mechanism that allows the transformer model to focus on specific parts of the input data more when making predictions or generating responses
[
58
]
. This allows them to be trained much faster on hardware designed to process large swaths of data in parallel, such as graphics cards
[
40
]
. Transformers possess an encoder-decoder structure (see figure
2.5
).

Figure 2.5:
The transformer architecture. Reproduced from
[
58
]
.

They operate as follows:

1.

Input Encoding
: Initially, the input (such as a sentence in English) is broken down into smaller parts, often words or subword units (tokens)
[
66
]
. Each of these parts is represented by a unique numerical vector. These vectors are usually of high dimensionality.

2.

Dimensionality Reduction
: As the input passes through the layers of the model, these high-dimensional vectors are transformed into a lower-dimensional space. This is done through a series of mathematical operations (like matrix multiplications, non-linear activations, etc.) performed by the model’s neural network layers. The goal here is to capture the essential features and relationships of the input while reducing the complexity and size of the data.

3.

Feature Extraction
: In the lower-dimensional space, the model attempts to capture the contextual relationships between the different parts of the input. This is where the model learns to understand high-level, abstract semantics of language, like meaning, sentiment, grammar, etc.

4.

Output Generation
: Finally, based on the encoded input, the model generates an output using the decoder. This output could be a continuation of text, an answer to a question, or any other form of response relevant to the task at hand.

At each step the model is auto-regressive, consuming the previously generated symbols as additional input when generating the next.

2.2.2
Training

LLMs
possess upwards of billions of trainable parameters, with some
LLMs
now reaching trillions of parameters
[
47
]
.
LLMs
exhibit emergent behaviours and capabilities that scale with the number of parameters, the amount of data they are fed, and the amount of computation used to train them
[
6
]
. Foundation models (
LLMs
trained for general tasks) are typically trained for next-word prediction on internet-scale datasets, although this is changing with the advent of
Large Multimodal Models (LMMs)
models like GPT-4V(ision)
[
39
]
that allow transformers to move beyond just language and into multi-modal signal understanding like images and sound. This has raised the need for pursuing training paradigms that move beyond next-word prediction
[
6
]
.

2.2.3
Context Window

One limitation of the
LLM
architecture is they are constrained by the number of inputs that can fit within their context window. OpenAI’s GPT-4 (
gpt-4-1106-preview
)
[
6
]
has a context window length of
128\,000
tokens. This may make acting as the controller for a long-duration space mission challenging because of the amount of information expected to consume the context window at any given time. This is where strategies such as
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
that allow the
LLM
to interact with an external knowledge base come into play.

2.2.4
Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering is the act of passing an intentionally designed natural language prompt to an
LLM
to steer its behaviour towards a certain task. Prompt engineering is a method of control flow
[
54
]
. As a result of the
LLM
’s generalization ability, it is possible to steer its output through prompting without the need to re-train the neural network for each new task. This can be done through few-shot prompting, where examples are provided in the prompt that demonstrates to the agent how to respond to a given input, or through
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
, which injects information from an external source such as a database or the internet into the prompt to provide additional context to the agent.

2.2.5
Fine-Tuning

An alternative learning strategy to few-shot learning is fine-tuning, which involves retraining a subset of the
LLMs
parameters or appending a small set of new weights to the model that are trained on task-specific data, such as in
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

[
20
]
. However, this technique requires more computation than in-prompt learning, as it requires the execution of gradient descent over the model weights during training.

2.2.6
LLMs as Embodied Agents

LLMs
have seen recent success applied as embodied agents in robotic control tasks within open-ended, uncertain environments. An
embodied agent
refers to a system that has a physical presence in the world. A system is said to be
agentic
if it is imbued with the ability to take actions which consistently contribute towards achieving goals over an extended period, without their behaviour having been specified in advance
[
49
]
. This presence can be through a robot or another form of physical embodiment. This work focuses primarily on agentic systems with
Large Language Models (LLMs)
at their core, as these have driven recent progress. This is in contrast to earlier generations of agentic AI systems, which do not explicitly reason through language. This work argues that language empowers agents with an explicit framework for modelling and reasoning about the world, in contrast to the implicit world models built by agents trained using classical reinforcement learning like AlphaGo
[
1
]
or Tesla Autopilot
[
38
]
. OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT and the GPT family of models
[
8
]
, refers to systems exhibiting high degrees of agenticness as “agentic AI systems,” to emphasize that agenticness is a property rather than a category or classification
[
49
]
.

Embodied
LLM
agents are at a
NASA

Technology Readiness Level (TRL)
of 6 (system model or prototype demonstration in a relevant environment
[
34
]
) in the field of terrestrial robotics, but the concept of applying
LLMs
in space systems is at
TRL
2 (technology concept formulated)
[
48
]
, with this work helping to raise the
TRL
to 3 (analytical and experimental proof-of-concept). Scholemer et al. investigated the benefits of using digital assistants throughout the satellite life cycle
[
48
]
. They conducted the integration of the Astri chatbot into command-and-control software, analysis tools, and design data, but did not investigate the application of
LLMs
onboard spacecraft themselves.

Multiple strategies have emerged to apply
LLMs
to the problem of automated planning and control in agents. Language translation and tool use are described in this section as strategies for agentic planning.

Language Translation

Language translation is a technique where an
LLM
is leveraged to transform natural language instructions into structured planning languages such as
Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL)

[
52
]
or behaviour trees
[
29
]
, to be fed into automated planning and execution engines downstream. This technique can bridge the gap between scientific objectives expressed in natural language and machine-understandable formats. Silver et al. found that
LLMs
alone can solve some nontrivial PDDL problems, but fail to solve many others
[
52
]
. However, even in problems that
LLMs
cannot solve, they showed that their outputs can be useful in guiding or refining a heuristic search planner. There remains a large gap between state-of-the-art and
LLM
-based planners, but their results suggest that further research is well-motivated.

Tool Use

Rather than generating structured plans as in language translation, tool use is a technique whereby an
LLM
is empowered with the ability to call upon predefined subroutines or functions that operate on the world. AutoGPT and Tool LLaMa
[
44
]
are notable examples of agents empowered with tools that allow them to browse the web, ping
APIs
, and interface with
CLIs
. In a similar vein as tool use, the idea of empowering LLMs to directly control their actions using code has also been explored
[
72
,
28
,
62
]
. Wang et al. developed VOYAGER, a GPT-4 embodied lifelong learning agent in Minecraft that continuously explores the world and acquires skills through the writing of its own executable code, without human intervention. Some limitations of this approach that have been observed by the research community are that LLMs often hallucinate non-existent tools, overuse a single tool, and face scaling challenges with multiple tools
[
41
]
. Overcoming these issues is key to enabling
LLMs
to effectively leverage the tooling provided to them.

Summary

Future agentic spacecraft, and robotic systems in general, may need to make use of combinations of the above strategies to execute their mission objectives. An agentic spacecraft may call on pre-programmed tools to generate and simulate a maneuver plan to lower its orbit, enter a code interpreter to modify the operating code of a payload subsystem to recalibrate it for a new target, and interact with a planning engine to schedule a sequence of imaging passes over a planetary body.

2.3
Existing Autonomous Space Systems

Various autonomous space systems have been developed. A subset are used as reference designs to reveal the current state of the art and identify areas for improvement.

2.3.1
Criteria

To objectively compare existing systems, it’s necessary first to establish a validation framework with criteria that encapsulate the systems’ requirements. Brann devised such a framework in 1996 for assessing the GENIE system
[
4
]
, later adapted by Anderson 2010
[
2
]
. Although GENIE was an autonomous ground-based satellite controller
[
4
]
, the criteria extend well to space-based autonomous systems, and autonomous systems in general. The criteria are:

•

Inspectability
: Can the system’s current actions and decision-making be understood?

•

Predictability:
Is it possible to foresee the system’s future actions?

•

Repairability
: Can the system be easily repaired?

•

Extensibility
: Can the system be easily improved?

•

Intelligence
: Is the system capable of learning from its mistakes?

In addition to Brann’s five criteria, the overall autonomy level and type of autonomy technology are compared between the reference designs. The first five criteria are measured according to the score definitions defined by Anderson 2010
[
2
]
.

Inspectability

The varying levels of inspectability scores measure the extent to which the inner workings of the autonomous system are revealed to mission control
[
2
]
. Higher levels of inspectability are preferred.

1.

Not Implemented
: No display is provided to the user

2.

Poor
: Only the system’s inputs and outputs are displayed

3.

Satisfactory
: High-level operations tasks are shown but small details such as individual uplinks are not displayed

4.

Good
: Decisions made by the system are displayed with reasonable justification

Predictability

The predictability scores assess the extent to which a human operator can anticipate the actions of the system
[
2
]
. Higher levels of predictability are preferred.

1.

Not Implemented
: Actions appear to be randomly generated

2.

Poor
: The system generally follows a predictable path with some deviations during nominal operations

3.

Satisfactory
: Decisions are predictable except for decisions made in response to error conditions

4.

Good
: Decisions are predictable including decisions made in error conditions

Repairability

Repairability scores measure the ease of repairing and rebooting an autonomous operations system following the occurrence of an unrecoverable error
[
2
]
. Higher levels of repairability are preferred.

1.

Not Implemented
: When a fatal error occurs, the system must be completely restarted from the beginning of the task sequence

2.

Poor
: When a fatal error occurs, the system can be restarted at some point during the last executing task with some lost progress

3.

Satisfactory
: When a fatal error occurs, the system can be restarted with no lost progress but the repair requires programmer involvement

4.

Good
: When a fatal error occurs, the system can be restarted with no lost progress and the repair can be made by a mission operator

Extensibility

The extensibility score evaluates the simplicity of implementing modifications in the autonomous operations system
[
2
]
. Higher levels of extensibility are preferred.

1.

Not Implemented
: A compiled binary is distributed which allows for no modifications

2.

Poor
: Modifications require editing and a recompilation of the source code

3.

Satisfactory
: Modifications can be made via a configuration file but require programmer involvement

4.

Good
: Modifications can be made via a configuration file and can be completed by a mission operator

Intelligence

Intelligence scores measure the extent to which an autonomous operations system can adapt its behaviour and retain these changes
[
2
]
. Higher levels of intelligence are preferred.

1.

Not Implemented
: No attempt to modify behaviour based on prior executions. A human is required to make all behavioural modifications

2.

Poor
: A flexible programming model is used that changes its behaviour based on system inputs. No behaviour modifications are saved to the system for future executions

3.

Satisfactory
: Behavior modifications are dynamically generated and stored in the system across executions. Modified behaviours are generated and stored offline using log information from prior executions

4.

Good
: Behavior modifications are dynamically generated and stored in the system across executions. Modified behaviours are generated and stored online such that behaviours learned during execution can be used later in that same execution

Autonomy Level

Autonomy levels are defined here according to an expanded version of the autonomy levels for
ESA
missions (see table
2.1
).

Table 2.1:
Space system autonomy levels. Adapted from the autonomy levels for
ESA
missions
[
57
]
.

Autonomy Level

Description

0

Ground-Controlled Real-Time Operations
: Requires direct and continuous control from a ground station. The system has minimal onboard autonomy

1

Pre-Programmed Onboard Execution
: Capable of executing mission operations that have been predefined and scheduled by ground control. Ability to store and execute time-based commands using an onboard scheduler

2

Onboard Adaptive Operations
: Capable of adaptive mission operations autonomously. Equipped to handle event-based operations, making autonomous decisions based on specific events or conditions encountered during the mission

3A

Basic Goal-Oriented Operations
: Capable of executing simple, predefined goals with limited parameters. This might include basic navigational adjustments or simple data collection tasks

3B

Advanced Goal-Oriented Operations
: Capable of handling more complex goals specified at higher levels of abstraction that involve a series of operations, such as adjusting its trajectory based on sensor data or conducting a dynamic sequence of scientific experiments in response to the results of previous experiments

4

: Capable of completely overhauling the mission plan based on evolving objectives, unexpected opportunities, or emergency scenarios, with minimal ground intervention

Level 3 autonomy of the
ESA
definition is further split into levels 3A and 3B, to help better differentiate advanced from basic goal-oriented autonomy. Note that the ability to understand goals in natural language is not assigned to any particular level. It is argued that the ability to interpret goals in natural language, as is the case with
LLMs
, does not inherently increase the autonomy level of a system, but rather natural language understanding is one particular implementation for understanding mission directives
[
12
]
. A system could be level 3+ autonomous without needing to process information in natural language, as is the case for specialized reinforcement learning agents or autonomous vehicles. This work argues natural language provides a good human-machine interface through which operator trust can be established.

Autonomy Type

Autonomy type refers to the kind of underlying computing paradigm or algorithm used to achieve autonomy. This may be a heuristic rule-based system, or it may be an AI system based on neural networks.

2.3.2
Reference Designs

ASPEN

Activity Scheduling and Planning Environment (ASPEN)
is a modular and reconfigurable framework designed for automated planning and scheduling, particularly in spacecraft control and operations
[
18
]
. It was initially developed in the late 1990s by NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory. It integrates planning and scheduling processes under a unified framework, enabling simultaneous plan generation and scheduling by a single system. The architecture of
ASPEN
includes an expressive constraint modelling language, a constraint management system, a temporal reasoning system, and a graphical interface.
ASPEN
is used in various applications like spacecraft commanding and maintenance scheduling. It supports the rapid development of automated scheduling systems for NASA applications, addressing complex spacecraft operability constraints and resource management. Notably,
ASPEN
has been instrumental in several space missions, such as the Modified Antarctic Mapping Mission, Orbital Express, Earth Observing One, and the European Space Agency’s Rosetta Orbiter
[
18
]
. However, developing real-world systems with
ASPEN
can be time-consuming due to the need for detailed domain modelling and complex data structures
[
18
]
. Despite this,
ASPEN
represents a significant advancement in the field, offering a comprehensive solution for managing intricate planning and scheduling tasks in aerospace operations.

Inspectability

Good.
ASPEN
’s graphical user interface allows for the visualization of plans and schedules, overlaying activities on timelines, and direct manipulation of these activities
[
18
]
. This interface supports a high level of transparency in the system’s operations and decisions, enabling mission operators to understand the system’s current actions and reasons behind them.

Predictability

Satisfactory.
ASPEN
utilizes planning and scheduling algorithms that are based on both constructive and repair-based approaches, ensuring that decisions generally follow a predictable path
[
18
]
. However, in situations involving error conditions or unexpected events, the predictability may be less certain as the system may require iterative repair and re-planning.

Repairability

Satisfactory. The system supports rapid revision of plans in response to unexpected events such as equipment failures or resource unavailabilities, minimizing schedule disruption
[
18
]
. While it does involve some level of automated recovery, the need for re-planning and the potential involvement of programmers or operators in response to errors suggests a level of repairability that is more than basic but requires expert intervention.

Extensiblity

Satisfactory.
ASPEN
is designed to be modular and reconfigurable, with provisions for creating domain-specific models using its modelling language and extending the application framework code (Section 2.8). While this allows for significant customization and extension, it does indicate that programmer involvement is necessary for modifications, thus meeting the criteria for a satisfactory rating.

Intelligence

Satisfactory. The system incorporates planning and scheduling algorithms that adapt to changing conditions and requirements. While it shows an ability to dynamically adjust plans and schedules, it may not fully retain these adaptations across executions.

Autonomy Level

2.
ASPEN
is capable of adaptive mission operations autonomously, handling event-based operations and making autonomous decisions based on specific events or conditions encountered. This aligns with Level 2 autonomy, as it goes beyond pre-programmed executions and adapts to changing conditions autonomously.

CASPER

The
Continuous Activity Scheduling, Planning, Execution, and Replanning (CASPER)
system, integrates planning and execution processes in real-time, continuously updating and adjusting plans based on new information and changing circumstances
[
24
]
.
CASPER
is an evolution on top of
ASPEN
developed from 1999 onwards. The core of
CASPER
’s architecture is its iterative repair planning technique, which efficiently addresses plan flaws without needing to develop an entirely new plan. Instead of viewing planning as a one-time process where a planner is given goals and an initial state as was common at the time, the planning system constantly holds a set of current goals, an existing plan, a present state, and a predicted future state. At any moment, an update to the goals or present state can alter the plan’s current status, triggering the planning process. Such updates could stem from unforeseen events or simply the passage of time, à la receding horizon control
[
35
]
.
CASPER
’s capabilities include managing finite state requirements, handling depletable and renewable resources, task decomposition, and managing complex functional relationships and metric time constraints between activities. These features enable it to effectively plan and execute complex missions with various operational constraints.
CASPER
has been tested and demonstrated in various space missions, including the Three Corner Sat (3CS) and Autonomous Sciencecraft Constellation (ASC)
[
24
]
. However,
CASPER
relies on predefined methods for conflict resolution, which may not cover all possible scenarios encountered in space missions. Moreover, the effectiveness of
CASPER
’s planning and execution depends on the accuracy and comprehensiveness of the input data and models it uses, which could be a limiting factor in highly unpredictable or poorly understood environments.

CASPER

Predictability

Satisfactory. While

This means that while its responses to anomalies might not always be predictable, its overall behaviour aligns with its programmed objectives and constraints.

Good.
CASPER

has a modular and adaptable design

MiRAGE, developed by the AIKO company, is an advanced onboard automation software aiming to enhance satellite autonomy

MiRAGE’s unique feature is its integration of an Expert System, which processes various data types to generate high-level mission goals and tasks. The system follows the knowledge-enabled programming paradigm, separating knowledge from code, and using a modular, reusable knowledge base. This approach allows the algorithm to be application-agnostic, with the reasoning manager utilizing a rule set based on propositional logic. The Expert System employs an inference engine to derive information from a comprehensive knowledge base, mimicking a human expert’s problem-solving capabilities. MiRAGE has been validated in Software-in-the-Loop simulations and, as of the paper’s publication, had reached Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 6, with plans to advance to TRL 8

Chapter 3
LLMSat

This chapter presents the design and implementation of LLMSat, a goal-oriented
LLM
-based spacecraft agent for autonomous mission operation. The chapter begins by presenting a set of driving requirements, followed by the architecture. The exact implementation used for testing is then described.

3.1
Requirements

A set of requirements that future agentic spacecraft should satisfy to enable their high autonomy operation is presented. The requirements are framed for space systems, but these may also apply more generally to any agentic robotic system, and they are independent of the underlying technology.

1.

The spacecraft shall be capable of executing natural language-based goal-oriented mission operations onboard as verified through simulation to enable autonomous operation and rapid mission adaptation

(a)

The spacecraft shall be capable of translating a mission-level objective into tasks to be performed by the spacecraft

(b)

The spacecraft shall be capable of conducting goal-oriented mission re-planning in the event of off-nominal mission scenarios or system faults

(c)

The spacecraft shall be able to justify its decisions to mission control

(d)

The spacecraft shall learn from its experiences to improve its performance

(e)

The spacecraft should learn from feedback from human operators to improve its performance

2.

The spacecraft shall be designed with safeguards in place to reduce the likelihood of self-termination to avoid a premature end to the mission

(a)

The spacecraft shall be restricted from executing autonomous operations above a certain operational risk threshold unless permission is granted by mission control to avoid executing actions that carry a risk of harm to itself or its subsystems, harm to human life, damage to existing infrastructure, or that may violate space and planetary protection policy

(b)

The spacecraft shall allow for overriding of its behaviour and lower-level systems by mission control to enable fault recovery or diagnosing via human intervention or another agent

3.

The spacecraft shall inform mission control when a provided goal cannot be met and ask for guidance to avoid the pursuit of an unachievable goal

4.

The spacecraft should be able to reprogram the functional code of its subsystems to enable fault recovery from software defects or repurposing of instrumentation

The LLMSat implementation is evaluated against these requirements in section
5
. Although requirement
4
is beyond the scope of the architecture implemented in this work, it is presented here as a thought experiment to raise an important question about mission assurance. Suppose an agent is free to modify the code of the spacecraft’s subsystems. How does one guarantee the modified code still passes the same set of verification tests conducted on the ground, and won’t cripple the instrument permanently? This capability introduces significant risk with current day
LLM
agents, but the answer may lie in having onboard functional testing of all spacecraft systems such that an agent’s code must pass at least these safety checks before it is deployed, then scaling the permissions afforded to autonomous agents as they become smarter and more trusted by the engineering community in embodied applications. To make this possible, spacecraft agents will need an understanding of themselves and their systems that is equal to or exceeds that of mission control and the engineers that built it.

3.2
Design Drivers

The following key design drivers are identified.

Computational Cost of Inference

Each inference on the
LLM
is computationally costly in terms of time. The number of times the
LLM
is invoked should be minimized to avoid significant response time delays, while ensuring the required performance is met.

Context Window Limit

Due to
LLM
context window limits, it may not be possible to fit all the information the agent may need to make an optimal decision as input. Must be strategic about what information is passed to the agent, and in what form. Additionally, the cost of each call to OpenAI’s ChatGPT
API
scales with the number of input tokens. Minimizing the number of input tokens necessary to achieve the required performance is key to minimizing the monetary cost of the implementation.

3.3
Architecture

An architecture for an agentic spacecraft is derived based on the requirements model. Following the findings in section
2
that autonomy is hierarchical and consistent with reference designs like
CASPER
, this work proposes the addition of a
Large Language Model
as the next layer up in the control hierarchy (see figure
3.1
).

Figure 3.1:
Spacecraft-level architecture. The scope of this work is outlined.

Under this hierarchical architecture, the agent’s responsibilities are scoped to the mission operations level. It receives state information about the spacecraft from the conventional flight software, generates an operational plan, and returns commands to the flight software. Just as a human mission control operator does not individually command the low-level hardware on a spacecraft, the agent does not directly operate the low-level hardware. Instead, control is afforded to it through predefined functions implemented by the conventional flight software. This enables the spacecraft to ensure its safety through a “correctness-by-construction” approach, where each callable function has been validated on the ground and so has been proven to a reasonable degree of certainty to be safe to be called by the agent. The flight software then implements validation and safety checks at three levels:

1.

Function-level validation
: Input arguments for functions callable by the agent are checked to be within acceptable ranges before execution. Feedback is provided to the agent in the case of a failed validation check.

2.

Plan-level validation
: Sequences of function calls generated by the agent are validated before execution by simulating the spacecraft’s state forward in time.

3.

Spacecraft-level validation
: The immediate safety of the spacecraft is checked in real-time, and a reflexive response can be engaged if deemed necessary without the need for intervention by the agent, such as in the case the temperature of a component exceeds operating temperatures and must be shut down. This is typical for traditional flight software.

A drawback of this architecture is it imposes additional technical complexity on the flight software to ensure the safety of the spacecraft while under command by the agent. This requires the flight software to have a high-fidelity world model to validate commands against. Figure
3.2
presents the agent-level architecture.

Figure 3.2:
System block diagram of proposed agent architecture.

The agent is composed of the following systems.

Large Language Model

The
Large Language Model (LLM)
is the reasoning engine responsible for conducting onboard mission operations planning. To achieve this, the
LLM
’s natural language understanding and innate ontological world model is used to reason about mission objectives and how best to achieve them given the capabilities of the spacecraft. It interfaces with a
Command-Line Interface
and a Memory Bank.

Command-Line Interface (CLI)

The
Command-Line Interface
provides a natural language interface between the spacecraft services and the
LLM
. It is responsible for accepting commands, dispatching services, and relaying feedback to the
LLM
.

Memory bank

The Memory Bank serves as a solution to overcome the context window limit using
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
. It accepts semantic queries from the
LLM
and retrieves relevant information from the agent’s prior experiences to enable prompt-based learning
[
75
]
. This avoids the need for fine-tuning the model’s weights to achieve the requirement that the agent must learn from its experiences.

Spacecraft Services

The spacecraft services enable the
LLM
to interact with its host spacecraft and the external world in controlled ways. Commands are organized into relevant services. Every command has built-in validation to promote the safety of the spacecraft.

Future work may explore the utility of fine-tuning the model’s weights in situ, but such an architecture presents significant risk. Training in situ means that the characterization and validation of the model performed on the ground would no longer be representative of the model’s flight behaviour. Running inference on current
LLMs
is computationally expensive, let alone fine-tuning these models. Significant advances would need to be made in space computing hardware to enable in situ fine-tuning. Fine-tuning should be leveraged on the ground to train the agent on how to operate a particular spacecraft through simulation before flight.

Figure
3.3
illustrates the agent operations over time as a sequence diagram.

Figure 3.3:
Agent operational sequence diagram.

The sequence of operations is described in detail (from top to bottom):

1.

The Agent Manager requests a connection to the console.

2.

The Console responds with a dashboard prompt containing general spacecraft state information such as fuel and power levels, the current
UTC
, the current mission objective(s), and the available commands.

3.

The Agent Manager invokes the
LLM
with the dashboard prompt and a system message instructing it on how to operate using few-shot prompting.

4.

The
LLM
may choose to run a command from the console such as taking an instrument reading through the Payload Manager, or sleep until woken by an alarm event.

5.

The Console returns a command response if a command was run or an alarm event if an alarm is triggered. Alarms may be raised asynchronously by the flight software in the event of a fault, or the completion of a long-running task such as an orbital manoeuvre executed by the Autopilot Service, or they may be set to trigger at a future time by the agent itself through the Alarm Service.

6.

The Agent Manager disconnects from the console to hibernate until called on again.

This architecture affords the agent the ability to sleep while waiting for long-running processes to transpire. This is key for a long-duration space mission during which much downtime may be spent in transit or waiting for experiments to run, and over that time it may be unnecessary and costly to have the agent continuously running.

3.5.3
Agent Roles

In this work, the role of an embodied agent is defined as the scope of responsibilities afforded to it by its host system. It is helpful to define different types of roles an embodied agent can take to enable comparison. Within the context of an agentic spacecraft, the following roles are defined.

Supervisor

In this architecture, the agent acts as a supervisor that monitors the various high-level telemetry streams from the spacecraft systems, focusing on both what the spacecraft is doing and its environment. Primary control of the spacecraft remains within the purview of the conventional flight software. The agent only interferes if it anticipates a high-risk scenario is imminent or recognizes a critical fault that could not be resolved by the flight software, but the time criticality of the fault is such that waiting for mission control is not an option; it must be resolved immediately to prevent harm from befalling the spacecraft. At this point, it notifies the flight software to take action. The supervisor is a backseat driver. This architecture provides the lowest level of agency, and therefore potential risk, to a spacecraft. This architecture is akin to rule-based
[
19
,
14

[

60

FDIR
architectures, the difference here being that an
LLM

Scientist

In the scientist role, the flight software consults the agent to evaluate and interpret collected data. This can be useful in maximizing scientific return in scenarios when the spacecraft encounters short-lived phenomena, such as dust devils on Mars
[
56
]
or diurnal variations in Enceladus’ plume activity
[
21
]
. Having been trained on the latest human knowledge, the agent could be leveraged to judge what is and isn’t interesting scientific phenomena based on this knowledge. The agent may then be allowed to command the spacecraft to point its sensor suite at specific locations, or plan trajectories that move it closer towards interesting anomalies to be studied further. Such opportunistic science is possible with an agentic architecture. Existing autonomous space systems like
AEGIS
(which runs onboard the Curiosity Mars rover)
[
17
]
require predefined features to look for, and so must be known, at least partially, ahead of time. An
LLM
agent has no such restriction.

Pilot

The pilot architecture combines aspects of the previous architectures and allows the agent to take primary control of the spacecraft, much like a human would onboard a crewed spacecraft. The agent is responsible for generating plans based on the mission objectives, conducting orbit selection, monitoring resource consumption, commanding payloads for data acquisition, and responding to faults that cannot be resolved by the lower-level systems. The pilot may fine-tune plans generated by a conventional planning engine. This architecture provides the greatest level of autonomy to the agent, and therefore potentially the highest risk.

This work investigates the implementation of the Pilot architecture.

3.5.5
Memory

A memory system is necessary to overcome the context window size limitation of
LLMs
. The memory system needs to support two basic actions: reading and writing. The following memory architectures are evaluated holistically.

Entity

Entity memory stores facts about specific entities in a conversation. It extracts information on entities using the
LLM
and builds up its knowledge about that entity over time.

Knowledge Graph

A knowledge graph is a data structure that represents information in a network of interlinked entities. Nodes are related through semantic relationships. Knowledge graphs encode ontological models about the world.

Conversation Summary Buffer

Summarizes the conversation as it happens using the
LLM
and stores the current summary in memory. This memory can then be used to inject the summary of the conversation so far into a chain. This is useful for longer conversations that would otherwise exceed the context window limit.

Vector Store

Stores memories in a vector database and returns the most relevant memories for a given query. The vector database is populated using the
LLM
as an encoder that embeds the tokens of a conversation into a higher dimensional embedding space. The retrieved memories are selected by evaluating the proximity of the query to its nearest neighbours in the embedding space.

A recommendation is made to adopt two memory storage solutions: Conversation Summary Buffer for short-term memory, and Vector Store for long-term memory. While entity memory is a powerful memory architecture for embodied terrestrial agents that need to keep track of actors in their surroundings that may evolve over time, an embodied spacecraft agent operates in a more solitary environment. There are few external entities the spacecraft needs to interact with and keep track of over time, especially if the agent is operating as a standalone spacecraft and not in a collaborative constellation. To keep the complexity of the architecture low, the
LLM
should be leveraged as the sole ontological model as opposed to incorporating a second knowledge graph.

3.6
Implementation

The LLMSat prototype is implemented in Python. The prototype consists of two independent Python applications: the Console and the Agent Manager. The Console application is implemented using the
cmd2
python library
[
11
]
, which facilitates the creation of interactive command-line applications. The
cmd2
library allows for the modular composition of services like the
Autopilot Service
,
Alarm Manager
, and
Payload Manager
. This enables independent development and testing of each of these services. The LangChain framework
[
27
]
interfaces the Agent Manager application to OpenAI’s GPT API. The Console and Agent Manager communicate over sockets using
ZeroMQ

[
74
]
.

Figure
3.6
illustrates the prompt template structure fed to the agent on startup. The prompt template is composed of three primary sections: the system prompt, the console prompt, and the operation logs. The system prompt instructs the agent to behave as the controller of the spacecraft and provides it with examples of how to interact with the console. The console prompt is populated by the
Console App
at runtime to contain the current timestamp, mission brief, task plan, spacecraft status, and available commands from the spacecraft services. The design philosophy for the agent-to-console interface follows a
self-discovery
approach, where the agent is provided limited knowledge of how to operate the spacecraft console interface ahead of time, and instead is expected to learn how to use it on the fly. The console interface prompt provides the agent with an overview of all the top-level services on startup, but additional details regarding the exact command signatures are hidden away, accessible through the
help <topic>
command. Self-discovery has the notable advantage that it significantly reduces the initial token count that needs to be passed to the agent, at the cost of requiring the agent to spend additional calls learning how to use the interface through trial and error, especially at the start of a run. A future implementation could leverage self-discovery to build up the agent’s memory system and/or to build a fine-tuning dataset over many simulation runs to make the agent learn how to operate its spacecraft host correctly. This would eliminate the need for humans to assemble this fine-tuning dataset by hand.

Systems Tool Kit (STK)

4.2
Case Study: Enceladus Exploration Mission

Using a series of simulated Enceladus exploration scenarios as case studies, the LLMSat system is evaluated against the requirements model. Saturn’s icy moon has been the subject of great interest by the scientific community
[
30
,
31
,
36
]
. Due to tidal forces and geothermal activity, Enceladus possesses a liquid ocean underneath its kilometres-thick crust of ice. Missions have been proposed to study the composition of its southern ice plumes to search for signs of life. An Enceladus mission presents a challenge in autonomy, due to its distance from Earth, which results in a communication delay of an hour. A spacecraft studying Enceladus from orbit, its surface, or its subsurface ocean may benefit from the autonomy provided by the LLMSat architecture. Each mission scenario is run using OpenAI’s
gpt-4-1106-preview
model with a temperature of 0.7. The temperature setting in an
LLM
is a hyperparameter that controls the model’s level of randomness or creativity in generating responses. When set to a higher value, the
LLM
tends to produce more varied and imaginative outputs, but this can sometimes lead to responses that deviate from the relevant context. On the other hand, a lower temperature setting results in more consistent and predictable responses from the agent. Future work may explore the effect the
LLM
temperature has on its performance in embodied space mission applications. All scenarios begin on Jan, 3 2045 with the spacecraft initialized in a
145\text{\,}\mathrm{km}
,
0\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
inclination circular orbit around Enceladus. Each scenario is run five times with the same initial conditions. A success rate across runs is computed. The success rate is defined as the number of successful runs to failed runs, with the success condition being the agent achieving all the mission requirements defined in the mission briefing for the scenario.

The operations log generated by the agent is stored for each scenario run for qualitative analysis. The results are presented in the form of a requirement verification matrix in section
5
. In addition to evaluating LLMSat against the design requirements, this work characterizes the agent examining the common failure modes across scenarios and evaluating how the agent’s performance scales with the complexity of the mission profile. The following mission scenarios are conducted.

5.1.4
Summary

Figure
5.1
presents the agent’s success rate as a function of the average token count across scenarios. Average token counts are used as a proxy for mission complexity. This is sensible since the agent is expected to go through more thought-action-observation cycles for missions of greater complexity with longer time horizons, thus yielding longer operation logs with more tokens.

Figure 5.1:
Success rate vs average token count.

From the average token counts, the complexities of the mission scenarios are, in ascending order: scenario A, scenario C, and scenario B. Based on the graph, it is evident that the success rate is reduced as the token count is increased. The data shows the agent’s success rate scales poorly as the complexity of a mission increases. This could be due to two factors: The first is that as the agent’s context window is filled, it fails to remain coherent over that longer context window. This aligns with existing research that
LLMs
have difficulty remaining coherent over long prompt chains
[
32
]
. The coherence problem is an open problem. The second potential cause is that missions of larger complexity are simply of too high complexity for present-day
LLM
agents to plan and reason about as a consequence of their architecture and training data. The literature agrees that current
LLMs
struggle with planning complex tasks without additional frameworks in place, such as hierarchical task decomposition
[
53
]
. The answer is likely to be a combination of both factors. Although the agent was provided a
Task Manager
to help it plan over longer time horizons in this implementation, the agent failed to make effective use of it. An improved implementation may have explicitly prompted the agent to break the mission into smaller subgoals or tasks using the
Task Manager
first, then prompted the agent with a task taken from the top of the task queue. This is akin to the BabyAGI
[
65
]
and AutoGPT
[
71
]
systems, or the
Planning Domain Definition Language
planners of Tom Silver et. al.
[
52
]
.

5.3
Question & Answer

In this section, answers to the research questions laid out in section
1.3
are presented based on the findings of this work.

To what extent can an
LLM
enable a spacecraft to conduct goal-based mission operations?

Large Language Models
have shown evidence to be an early example of a future agentic space system. LLMSat demonstrated the ability to conduct goal-based mission operations, potentially reaching autonomy levels 3B and 4. This work showed that the coherence of an
LLM
scales poorly as the complexity and duration of a mission increases. Alternative frameworks that explicitly prompt the agent to decompose tasks into subtasks have demonstrated to be more effective at maintaining coherence over longer operating sequences. Future
Large Multimodal Models
with longer context windows, more parameters, and fine-tuned on real or simulated mission data may greatly extend the coherence of these agents.

What advantages do
LLMs
bring to space systems?

This work argues the primary advantages of an
LLM
over rule-based, fuzzy logic, or conventional neural network systems trained through reinforcement learning are 1) the opportunity to raise human operator trust by justifying agent behaviour through natural language, 2) leveraging the internet-scale world model encoded in an
LLMs
to enable it to respond to out-of-operational-envelope cases while exploring uncharacterized environments and conduct opportunistic science, and 3) the ability to rapidly adapt the system to a wide range of mission profiles with little to no tuning, as afforded by the
LLMs
generalization ability. The major tradeoffs are the computational cost associated with running
LLMs
on embedded hardware (this is an even greater engineering challenge to overcome for space hardware, which must often be radiation-hardened, leading to traditionally lower clock speeds and performance), and the risks associated with higher levels of autonomy—if not implemented responsibly.

How can the operation of an agentic spacecraft be verified before launch?

Systems of high autonomy are, by their nature, difficult to test and verify. This is because the environment for which they are built can be very high dimensional (hence the need for autonomy in the first place). Systems built for terrestrial applications like self-driving cars and mobile robots that co-operate in shared spaces with humans such as warehouses and factory floors are rigorously tested through high-fidelity simulations of the real world using digital twins, across a breadth of possible scenarios
[
42
]
. An agentic spacecraft should be tested in much the same way, as was demonstrated in this work, using high fidelity space environment simulations with adequate harnessing to allow for the injection of diverse hazards and failure modes that may be encountered over a mission, and assessing the agent on its ability to balance risk versus reward. The agent itself should also play role in the development of the system. Throughout the development and testing of LLMSat, the agent routinely performed actions that surfaced software bugs in the application code. Future applications may benefit from keeping the agent in the loop as a fuzzy tester over the course of development of the flight software.

5.4
Risk Versus Reward

Historically, the space industry has been risk-averse when it comes to automation due to cultural and political factors
[
25
]
. This cautious approach isn’t just limited to flight software teams; scientists themselves are also hesitant about advanced automation systems, unsure if these can effectively and accurately meet their specific objectives
[
25
]
. Ultimately, however, it is the achievement of these scientific objectives, not the mechanisms through which scientific objectives are met, that matter to the scientific community. We need a step function increase in trust to reap the benefits of higher scientific yields and lower operating costs afforded to us by higher degrees of onboard automation. Simply improving the AI and software, however, won’t suffice to significantly enhance trust between humans and AI-operated systems. A comprehensive systems approach is necessary—one that involves the operators of these highly autonomous systems interacting with the agentic systems long before launch to build a mental model of how they respond to (natural language) input, the engineers building the high-fidelity simulations that cover a sufficiently large breadth of possible scenarios on uncharacterized planetary bodies, and the scientific community who defines the scientific objectives the systems are instructed to pursue.

Chapter 6
Conclusion

This report evaluated the utility of
Large Language Model (LLM)
-based agents to raise the autonomy level of space systems. A set of requirements for future agentic spacecraft were developed. The design and system architecture of a prototype implementation were presented based on the requirements. The implementation was simulated across three mission scenarios within
Kerbal Space Program
to assess its performance. Its behaviour was qualitatively characterized and a success rate was quantified across trial runs. It is found that GPT-4’s reasoning and planning abilities do not scale well as the complexity of the mission profile increases. Alternative cognitive architectures that combine
ReAct
with task managers and schedulers that explicitly prompt the model to generate long-term plans in natural language or
Planning Domain Definition Language
may allow the model to operate over mission-length timescales. This implementation was centred primarily around the Pilot role architecture for orbital navigation. Future work should explore alternative role architectures like those described by the Supervisor and Scientist agent roles in section
3.5.3
, which have a reduced locus of control over spacecraft systems, resulting in lower mission risk, and so may be achievable with nearer-term
LMM
models. Highly autonomous agentic systems call for a paradigm shift in our approach to engineering, verifying, operating, and assessing risks for complex systems. Such systems have the capacity to unlock mission profiles for space and extra-planetary robotics that would have been previously unattainable, thereby accelerating humanity’s exploration of the solar system.
