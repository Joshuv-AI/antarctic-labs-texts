Pursuing Truth: Improving Retrievals on Mid-Infrared Exo-Earth Spectra with Physically Motivated Water Abundance Profiles and Cloud Models.
Björn S. Konrad, Sascha P. Quanz, Eleonora Alei, Robin Wordsworth

Abstract
Atmospheric retrievals are widely used to constrain exoplanet properties from observed spectra. We investigate how the common nonphysical retrieval assumptions of vertically constant molecule abundances and cloud-free atmospheres affect our characterization of an exo-Earth (an Earth-twin orbiting a Sun-like star). Specifically, we use a state-of-the-art retrieval framework to explore how assumptions for the H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} profile and clouds affect retrievals. In a first step, we validate different retrieval models on a low-noise simulated 1D mid-infrared (MIR) spectrum of Earth. Thereafter, we study how these assumptions affect the characterization of Earth with the Large Interferometer For Exoplanets (LIFE). We run retrievals on LIFE mock observations based on real disk-integrated MIR Earth spectra. The performance of different retrieval models is benchmarked against ground truths derived from remote sensing data. We show that assumptions for the H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} abundance and clouds directly affect our characterization. Overall, retrievals that use physically motivated models for the H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} profile and clouds perform better on the empirical Earth data. For observations of Earth with LIFE, they yield accurate estimates for the radius, pressure-temperature structure, and the abundances of CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} , H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} , and O 3 \text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} . Further, at R = 100 R=100 , a reliable and bias-free detection of the biosignature CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} becomes feasible. We conclude that the community must use a diverse range of models for temperate exoplanet atmospheres to build an understanding of how different retrieval assumptions can affect the interpretation of exoplanet spectra. This will enable the characterization of distant habitable worlds and the search for life with future space-based instruments.

Mettler et al. [66]

By running atmospheric retrievals on empirical MIR disk-integrated spectra from Earth observing satellites, the authors study how their characterization of Earth depends on the viewing angle and season. Mettler et al. [64]

[

80

60

II.1
Atmospheric retrieval routine

We run retrievals using a modified version of the retrieval routine initially introduced in
Konrad et al. [52]
and later improved and modified in
Alei et al. [3]
and
Konrad et al. [53]
. Here, we provide a brief summary of the original routine and focus on the additions to the routine made for this publication. For an in-depth description of the retrieval framework, we refer to the original publications.

The retrieval framework is based on the radiative transfer code
petitRADTRANS

[
68
,
69
,
3
]
, which is used to calculate the theoretical MIR thermal emission spectrum of a 1D plane-parallel parametric atmosphere model. The model atmosphere is defined via a set of forward model parameters (see Section
II.2
for a description of the forward models we used). To calculate the MIR emission spectrum corresponding to a set of model parameters,
petitRADTRANS
assumes the planetary surface to emit black-body radiation. It then models the interaction of each discrete atmospheric layer with the radiation by accounting for absorption, emission, and scattering. This yields the MIR flux at the top of the atmosphere.

The goal of a retrieval is to search the space spanned by the forward model parameters for combinations of parameter values that best reproduce an observed planet spectrum. The prior probability distributions (or “priors”) of the model parameters specify the parameter space to be probed by the retrieval. To efficiently explore this prior space, we use
py
-
MultiNest

[
13
]
, a python package based on the
MultiNest

[
23
]
implementation of Nested Sampling
[
85
]
. For this study, we ran all retrievals using 700 live points and a sampling efficiency of 0.3
1
1

1

As suggested for evidence evaluation by the
MultiNest
documentation:
https://github.com/farhanferoz/MultiNest
.
.

The output of a retrieval are posterior probability distributions (or “posteriors”) for the model parameters. The posteriors summarize how likely different combinations of parameter values are. In addition, our retrieval routine yields estimates of the Bayesian evidence
\mathcal{Z}
, which quantifies how well the forward model fits the input spectrum. Thus,
\mathcal{Z}
provides a means of comparing the performance of different forward models. If we run two retrievals on the same spectrum using different forward models
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, the retrievals will yield different log-evidences
\ln\left(\mathcal{Z_{A}}\right)
and
\ln\left(\mathcal{Z_{B}}\right)
. From the log-evidences, we calculate the Bayes’ factor
K
:

\log_{10}(K)=\frac{\ln\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)-\ln\left(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{B}}\right)}{\ln\left(10\right)}.

(1)

The value of
K
specifies which model is preferred. A positive
\log_{10}(K)
marks a preference for model
\mathcal{A}
, whereas negative values indicate preference for
\mathcal{B}
. The Jeffreys scale
[
42
, Table
1
]
provides a quantification for the strength of the model preference.

II.1.1
Model for water condensation

Retrievals commonly assume that the atmospheric trace-gas abundances are vertically constant. While this assumption helps reduce the computational complexity, recent studies have shown that it can bias the retrieved posteriors
[
81
, e.g.,]
. For Earth,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
has strong spectral features throughout the MIR
[
52
, e.g., Figure 1 in]
. The bulk of Earth’s MIR emission originates from the lowermost atmospheric layers (between 1 bar and 0.1 bar), where the atmospheric
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
content decreases strongly with altitude by more than 3 orders of magnitude
[
64
, see, e.g., Figure
1
;]
. The main process responsible for this decrease in
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
in Earth’s lower atmosphere is the convection of moist air from the surface to higher atmospheric layers. The rising moist air cools causing
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
to condense, form clouds, and rain out. Consequentially, the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance drops significantly with altitude in Earth’s atmosphere, and we expect signatures thereof to be imprinted in the MIR emission.

To model the condensation induced vertical variations in the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile in our retrieval forward model, we start by calculating the saturation vapor pressure of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
for each atmospheric layer. For this, we use the temperature
T
of the layer as well as one of the two experimentally determined Goff-Gratch equations
[
31
,
30
, Eqs.
2
and
3
;]
. If
T
is greater than the triple point temperature of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
(
T_{\mathrm{tp}}=273.16
K), we use the equation for the saturation vapor pressure over water (
p_{\mathrm{v,\,water}}
in hPa):

\begin{split}p_{\mathrm{v,\,water}}\left(T\right)&=l_{1}\cdot(1-T_{\mathrm{st}}/T)+l_{2}\cdot\log_{10}(T_{\mathrm{st}}/T)\\
&+l_{3}\cdot 10^{-7}\cdot\left(1-10^{l_{4}\cdot\left(1-T/T_{\mathrm{st}}\right)}\right)\\
&+l_{5}\cdot 10^{-3}\cdot\left(10^{l_{6}\cdot\left(1-T_{\mathrm{st}}/T\right)}-1\right)+\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{st}}).\end{split}

(2)

Here,
T_{\mathrm{st}}=373.15
K and
p_{\mathrm{st}}=1013.25
hPa are the steam-point temperature and pressure of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, and the factors
l_{i}
are constants:
l_{1}=7.90298
,
l_{2}=5.02808
,
l_{3}=1.3816
,
l_{4}=11.344
,
l_{5}=8.1328
, and
l_{6}=3.49149
. Conversely, if
T
lies below
T_{\mathrm{tp}}
, we use the Goff-Gratch equation for the saturation vapor pressure over ice (
p_{\mathrm{v,\,ice}}
in hPa):

\begin{split}p_{\mathrm{v,\,ice}}\left(T\right)&=s_{1}\cdot(1-T_{\mathrm{tp}}/T)-s_{2}\cdot\log_{10}(T_{\mathrm{tp}}/T)\\
&+s_{3}\cdot(1-T/T_{\mathrm{tp}})+\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{tp}}).\end{split}

(3)

Here,
p_{\mathrm{tp}}=6.1173
hPa is the triple point pressure of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, and the factors
s_{i}
are constants:
s_{1}=9.09718
,
s_{2}=3.56654
, and
s_{3}=0.876793
. The vapor pressure of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
(
p_{\mathrm{v}}
) as a function of
T
can be summarized as:

p_{\mathrm{v}}\left(T\right)=\begin{cases}p_{\mathrm{v,\,water}}\left(T\right),&\text{if }T\geq T_{\mathrm{tp}},\\
p_{\mathrm{v,\,ice}}\left(T\right),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4)

Next, we assume a vertically constant volume mixing ratio of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
(
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}
). Starting from the surface layer, we follow the atmospheric
P
-
T
structure to the higher atmospheric layers with pressures
p
and temperatures
T
. For each layer, we compare the partial pressure of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
(
p_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}{}=\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}\cdot p
) to
p_{\mathrm{v}}(T)
. If
p_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}
lies below
p_{\mathrm{v}}(T)
, we proceed to the next layer. In contrast, if
p_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}
in a layer exceeds
p_{\mathrm{v}}(T)
, we reduce
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}
in that layer and all lower-pressure layers such that
p_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}
in the current layer equals
p_{\mathrm{v}}(T)
. This yields a
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}{}(p)
profile that is either constant or decreasing with altitude (depending on the
P
-
T
structure and
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}
in the surface layer).

. In our retrievals, we allow for a dry upper atmosphere by adding a drying parameter,
\delta_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}{}\in\left[0,1\right]
, to the forward model. Using the parameter
\delta_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}
, we calculate the dried
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile,
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O},\,dry}}(p)
, as follows:

\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O},\,dry}}(p)=\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}(p)\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}^{\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}{}_{\mathrm{,\,TOA}}/\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}(p)}.

Here,
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}{}_{\mathrm{,\,TOA}}

\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}(p)

5

\delta_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}
and for atmospheric layers where
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}(p)
is comparable to
\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}{}_{\mathrm{,\,TOA}}
. A value of
\delta_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}{}=1

\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O},\,dry}}(p)=\nu_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}(p)
, which means that there is no drying in the upper atmosphere.

II.1.2
Model for partial cloud coverage

The condensation of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
in Earth’s atmosphere is linked to the formation of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
clouds. Thus, if
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
condenses in our model atmosphere, we expect clouds to form. To account for this effect, we add the option to include a simple model for partial cloud coverage in the retrieval forward model.

To account for clouds in our retrievals, we position gray clouds at an atmospheric pressure of
p_{\mathrm{C}}
. We calculate
p_{\mathrm{C}}
by taking the median pressure of all atmosphere layers where
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
condensation occurs that exhibit a relative humidity greater than 10% (i.e.,
p_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}}(p)/p_{\mathrm{v}}(T)>0.1
). This assumption for
p_{\mathrm{C}}
positions the clouds within the atmospheric layers where
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
condenses. If no
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
condensation occurs, we assume the atmosphere to be cloud-free.

To model an Earth-like partial cloud cover, we use our retrieval forward model to calculate the spectra for a cloud-free (
F_{\mathrm{CF}}
) and cloudy (
F_{\mathrm{C}}
) model atmosphere. We then mix the two spectra as follows to obtain the total flux
F_{\mathrm{tot}}
as a function of the wavelength
\lambda
:

F_{\mathrm{tot}}(\lambda)=f_{\mathrm{C}}\cdot F_{\mathrm{C}}(\lambda)+\left(1-f_{\mathrm{C}}\right)\cdot F_{\mathrm{CF}}(\lambda).

(6)

Here, the forward model parameter
f_{\mathrm{C}}{}\in\left[0,1\right]
describes the cloud-fraction. A value of
f_{\mathrm{C}}{}=0
corresponds to the cloud-free case, whereas
f_{\mathrm{C}}{}=1
yields a fully cloudy atmosphere.

II.3
Input spectra for the retrievals

In the context of this study, we consider two different types of input spectra. All considered spectra are characterized by their spectral resolving power (
R
) and signal-to-noise ratio (
S/N
). We define the
R
of a spectrum as
\lambda/\Delta\lambda
, where
\Delta\lambda
is the width of a given wavelength bin and
\lambda
the wavelength at the bin’s center. We introduce the simulated 1D Earth spectrum used to test the different forward models in Section
II.3.1
. Next, in Section
II.3.2
, we provide information on the empirical, disk-integrated Earth spectra and the corresponding ground truths studied in the main analysis of this work. Last, we provide details on the assumptions made for the wavelength dependent
S/N
of all spectra in Section
II.3.3
.

II.3.3
Considered noise models

In the present study, we use two different approaches to model the wavelength-dependent
S/N
of the considered MIR spectra. First, to test the different forward models on the simulated Earth spectrum, we assume a low-noise scenario. Second, for the retrievals on the disk-integrated Earth spectra, we use a more realistic LIFE-like
S/N
model. Because the
S/N
is not constant over the spectrum for both cases, we define the
S/N
of the spectrum as the
S/N
in the
11.2~\text{\textmu m}
wavelength bin (see Figure
4
). We choose the
11.2~\text{\textmu m}
bin since it does not coincide with strong absorption features and thus probes Earth’s continuum emission. This reference wavelength was also used by previous LIFE-related retrieval studies
[
52
,
3
,
53
,
64
,
4
]
.

Figure 4:
Noise models for the
R=100
,
S/N=20
spectra. The blue-dashed line shows the photon noise assumed for the simulated Earth spectrum. The LIFE
sim
noise assumed for the disk-integrated Earth spectra is shown as solid-red lines. Both noise models reach an
S/N
of 20 at
11.2~\text{\textmu m}
(indicated by the black-dotted lines).

For the low-noise scenario, we only assume photon noise of the source to be present. Thus, if
N
photons are detected in a given wavelength bin, we assume the noise to be
\sqrt{N}
. To calculate the
S/N
, we scale the flux such that the desired
S/N
is reached at
11.2~\text{\textmu m}
. The wavelength-dependent photon noise is indicated by the blue-dashed line in Figure
4
.

To obtain the more realistic LIFE-like noise estimates, we use the LIFE
sim
model
[
19
]
. In addition to accounting for photon noise of the planet emission, LIFE
sim
models contributions from major astrophysical noise sources (stellar leakage and local- as well as exozodiacal dust emission). Thus, LIFE
sim
makes the implicit assumption that observations with a LIFE-like instrument will not be dominated by instrumental noise terms (Dannert et al., in prep.; Huber et al., in prep.). To calculate the LIFE
sim
noise, we assume the observed planet to orbit a G2V star at
1
AU at a separation of
10
pc from us. The exozodiacal dust emission is set to three times the local zodiacal dust emission, which is the median exozodi emission found for Sun-like stars by the HOSTS survey
[
22
]
. The wavelength-dependence of the LIFE
sim
noise we assume for the disk-integrated Earth spectra is indicated by the solid-red lines in Figure
4
.

For all spectra, we treat the wavelength-dependent
S/N
as uncertainty on the flux. This implies that the spectral points are set to the true flux values and are not randomized according to the
S/N
. While randomized spectra provide more accurate simulated observations, retrievals on such noise realizations yield biased parameter posteriors. Ideally, our retrieval study would consider a large number (
\gtrsim 10
) of different noise realizations of one spectrum. However, such a study is computationally unfeasible due to the large number of required atmospheric retrievals. Alternatively, as motivated in
Konrad et al. [52]
, retrievals on unrandomized spectra can be used to obtain estimates for the average expected retrieval performance for randomized spectra.

II.4
Prior distributions of model parameters

All prior distributions assumed to run the retrievals are listed in Table
3
. We choose the priors for the
P
-
T
parameters
a_{i}
and the surface pressure
P_{0}
such that the resulting
P
-
T
structures cover a wide range of profiles (from cold and thin Mars-like to hot and massive Venus-like atmospheres). For the atmospheric gases
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, we assume broad uniform priors in log-space that extend from
1
to
10^{-10}
in mass fraction. The lower limit of
10^{-10}
lies significantly below the estimated minimal detectable abundances presented in
Konrad et al. [52]
(
\approx 10^{-7}
in mass fraction).

Based on the retrieved abundance constraints form
Mettler et al. [64]
, we exclude
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
dominated atmospheres by setting the upper limit of
10^{-1}
for the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
prior. For planets on Earth-like orbits around G-stars, high
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundances can occur for high partial pressures of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(
p_{{}\mathrm{CO}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}\approx 0.1-1
bar) or other greenhouse gases due to the increased surface temperature
[
94
, e.g.,]
. Yet, such atmospheres lie close to the critical runaway greenhouse limit and are out of scope for this study. For the
\delta_{\mathrm{H_{2}O}}
parameter, we choose a log-uniform prior between
1
to
10^{-5}
. This allows for atmospheric
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
to deplete by up to five orders of magnitude in the upper atmosphere. Further, the uniform
f_{\mathrm{C}}
prior between 0 and 1 covers the full range from cloud-free to fully cloudy atmospheres.

We assumed Gaussian priors for the planet radius
R_{\text{pl}}
and mass
M_{\text{pl}}
. The
R_{\text{pl}}
prior is motivated by
Dannert et al. [19]
, who find that already the detection of a planet with LIFE yields a first

estimate. For terrestrial planets in the HZ, they predict a radius estimate

for the true radius
R_{\mathrm{true}}
of
R_{\mathrm{est}}/R_{\mathrm{true}}=0.97\pm 0.18
. We derive the prior for
\log_{10}(M_{\text{pl}}{})
from the
R_{\text{pl}}
prior using the statistical mass-radius relation
Forecaster

[
18
]
. These
R_{\text{pl}}
and
M_{\text{pl}}
priors are in accordance with previous LIFE-related retrieval studies
[
52
,
53
,
3
,
64
]
.

and Table

, we summarize the retrieved posteriors for the forward model parameters. We provide the posteriors for the surface conditions, the planet radius
R_{\text{pl}}
, the atmospheric trace gases, the drying parameter

f_{\mathrm{C}}

a_{i}

-
T

\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

and

Mettler et al. [64]
.

is significantly higer (lower limit on
f_{\mathrm{C}}{}
:
\approx 0.5
).

IV
Discussion

Here, we outline implications of our work for the characterization of terrestrial HZ exoplanets (Section
IV.1
) and for the LIFE mission requirements (Section
IV.2
). Thereafter, in Section
IV.3
, we point out the limitations of the present study.

IV.1
Implications for Characterizing Rocky HZ Exoplanets

We find that the biased estimates for the
P
-
T
structure and the atmospheric composition obtained in the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
retrievals are predominantly attributable to two simplifying assumptions commonly made by retrieval models: vertically constant abundance profiles and a cloud-free atmosphere. Our results for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
retrieval models demonstrate that including physically motivated models for Earth’s atmospheric
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile and partial cloud coverage helps significantly improve the accuracy of the retrieved parameter estimates. However, despite the improved parameter estimates, neither the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
nor the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
model is preferred over the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
model by Bayesian model comparison for LIFE-like
R
and
S/N
scenarios. Yet, we argue that the usage of the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
models is justifiable, since both impose physics informed constraints on the model atmosphere. This allows to exclude unrealistic atmospheric states and thereby yields more reliable parameter estimates. Especially for HZ exoplanets, unbiased parameter constraints are a critical requirement to enable the accurate assessment of their habitability. Further, in the same context, biased estimates for the abundances of potential biosignature gases, such as
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, can be highly problematic.

From Figure
3
and
Mettler et al. [66]
, we know that Earth’s MIR emission spectrum depends significantly on both the observed view and the season. Since both the viewing geometry and the season will be unknown for future observations of HZ terrestrial exoplanets, it is crucial to understand if and how these factors affect our characterization. We find that, independent of the assumed retrieval model, the retrieved constraints for most of the model parameters exhibit no significant dependence on the view and season of the Earth spectrum. Only
T_{0}
,
T_{\mathrm{Op.}}
,
T_{\mathrm{eq}}
, and
A_{\mathrm{B}}
exhibit a perceptible dependence on the considered spectrum in the high
R
and
S/N
scenarios (see Figures
A1
and
A2
). These findings are in agreement with
Mettler et al. [64]
, who performed retrievals on the same set of disk-integrated Earth spectra considered here using the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
forward model.

IV.2
Implications for the LIFE Initiative

Several retrieval studies have evaluated how well rocky HZ exoplanets could be characterized from their MIR spectrum by a LIFE-like observatory. In
Konrad et al. [52]
, preliminary minimal requirements for LIFE are obtained by analyzing simulated Earth spectra. By requiring a detection of the potential biosignature
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
with LIFE, the authors find lower limits of
4-18.5~\text{\textmu m}
for the wavelength coverage,
50
for
R
, and
10
for
S/N
. In two follow-up studies
[
3
,
53
]
, these findings are reevaluated on simulated spectra of Earth at different temporal epochs from
Rugheimer & Kaltenegger [82]
and a cloudy Venus-like planet. Last,
Mettler et al. [64]
test the LIFE requirements on the same real disk- and time-averaged MIR Earth spectra we considered here.

While the analyzed spectra vary significantly in complexity, all previous studies assumed retrieval models with vertically constant abundance profiles. Further, only
Konrad et al. [53]
accounted for clouds in their retrieval models. All studies yield similar results for the strengths of the parameter constraints and the detectability of trace-gases. They find Earth-like levels of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
to be detectable. Further, the retrieved
16\%-84\%
posterior range does not exceed
\pm 0.5
dex for pressures,
\pm 20
K for temperatures,
\pm 0.1\,R_{\oplus}
for
R_{\text{pl}}
, and
\pm 1.0
dex for trace-gas abundances. These constraints from the previous LIFE studies are consistent with our results for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
retrieval model, which also assumes constant abundance profiles and neglects clouds.

However, similar to our findings for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
retrievals, both
Alei et al. [3]
and
Mettler et al. [64]
observe significant biases in the retrieved posteriors relative to the ground truths. Both studies analyzed spectra of cloudy atmospheres with non-constant trace-gas abundances.
Mettler et al. [64]
argue that underestimated pressures and overestimated trace-gas abundances are primarily evoked by the retrieval model assumption of a vertically constant
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile. In contrast, biased temperature,
R_{\text{pl}}
, and
A_{\mathrm{B}}
estimates are attributable primarily to neglecting clouds. Our findings for the simulated Earth spectrum (Section
III.1
) validate these claims. In the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
retrievals, biases on pressure and abundance estimates are reduced significantly by modeling Earth’s
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance profile. Yet, temperature,
R_{\text{pl}}
, and
A_{\mathrm{B}}
estimates are largely unaffected. These biases are only reduced in the cloudy
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
retrievals. Also our findings for the real disk-integrated Earth views (Section
III.2
) validate the claims from
Mettler et al. [64]
by exhibiting similar bias reductions. However, the improved estimates for individual parameters are no longer attributable to solely the variable
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile or the inclusion of clouds.

We observe two additional important differences between the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
retrieval results and the previous studies. First, we do not retrieve accurate estimates for
P_{0}
and
T_{0}
for most views independent of the
R
and
S/N
of the spectrum. The retrieved
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profiles indicate that the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance in the lower atmosphere is increased by
\geq 0.5
dex compared to the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
model. These elevated
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
levels lead to an optically thick lower atmosphere due to the strong MIR continuum opacity of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
. As a result, the high-pressure layers of the model atmosphere do not contribute to the exoplanet’s MIR spectrum and therefore only lower limits for
P_{0}
and
T_{0}
are attainable. The pressure
P_{\mathrm{Op.}}
of the lowest atmosphere layer that still contributes to the modelled MIR spectrum roughly corresponds to the ground truth
P_{0}
value. Thus, the model atmosphere becomes opaque to MIR radiation close to Earth’s surface. This agrees well with measurements of the MIR opacity of Earth’s atmosphere
[
36
, see, e.g.,]
. This finding can be generalized to exoplanets, where clouds and the MIR opacity sources set a fundamental limit on how deep an atmosphere can be probed.

Second, at the minimal
R
and
S/N
requirements from
Konrad et al. [52]
(
R=50
,
S/N=10
), Earth-like
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
mass fractions of
10^{-6}
are not reliably detected in the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
retrievals. Instead, most of these retrieval runs yield upper limits of
\log_{10}({}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{4}}})\leq 5.0\pm 0.5
dex for the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundance. This occurs since Earth’s main MIR
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
signature at
\sim 8~\text{\textmu m}
overlaps with a strong
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
band (
5-8~\text{\textmu m}
). Thus, changes to the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance model directly impact the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
retrieval. Importantly, the main driver for the initial
R
and
S/N
requirements was the capability to detect
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
in an Earth-like atmosphere. Our results indicate that a bias-free constraint on the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundance (mean of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
posterior within
0.5
dex of 1 bar ground truth; uncertainty
\leq\pm 1.0
dex) at
R=50
is only possible for
S/N{}\geq 20
. For the
R=100
scenarios, such
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
constraints remain possible for
S/N=10
.

Last, while the simulation-based LIFE studies analyzed the MIR emission between
4-18.5~\text{\textmu m}
, the empirical Earth spectra studied here and in
Mettler et al. [64]
cover a smaller range (
3.75-4.6~\text{\textmu m}
,
6.2-15.4~\text{\textmu m}
). Yet, the constraints we retrieved for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
model are comparable to the previous studies. This suggests that measurements of the MIR flux above
15.4~\text{\textmu m}
and between
4.6-6.2~\text{\textmu m}
are not essential to accurately characterize an exo-Earth with LIFE. However, additional studies are required to investigate whether this finding generalizes to arbitrary HZ terrestrial exoplanets.

IV.3
Limitations and Future Work

The present study demonstrates, that adding simple physical constraints to the retrieval forward model can help significantly reduce biases in the retrieved posteriors for an Earth-like spectrum. Yet, there are limitations inherent to our work, the effect of which must be investigated in future studies.

First, our model for the atmospheric
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile assumed that condensation occurs if the relative humidity reaches 100%. Thus, physical tropospheric states, such as the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
supersaturation of air
[
87
,
28
, e.g.,]
, are not possible. Yet, since we are only sensitive to order of magnitude changes in the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance for the
R
and
S/N
scenarios considered, we do not expect such factors to significantly affect our findings. Further, we assumed that only condensation affects the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile. However, in the stratosphere and above, the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance is strongly affected by additional processes such as the photochemical oxidation of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

[
43
,
25
, e.g.,]
. Yet, since the bulk of Earth’s MIR emission originates from the troposphere, we argue that the stratospheric
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile only affects our characterization negligibly.

Second, we assume constant abundance profiles for all trace-gases except for
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
. On Earth, especially the
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
but also the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
abundances exhibit significant altitude dependencies (see Figure
3
). These variations are predominantly evoked by photochemical processes in the stratosphere
[
17
,
43
]
. However, in contrast to
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, a simple physical constraint for the
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
profiles does not exist
[
55
, e.g.,]
. As a result, models for these profiles would be relatively unconstrained. In addition, the MIR spectral feature of
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
at
9.7~\text{\textmu m}
originates from the high pressure layers in Earth’s atmosphere (
p>0.3
bar). Consequentially, we are not sensitive to the large
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
variations in the upper atmosphere at the considered
R
and
S/N
. Also for
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, the main contribution to the MIR feature at
7.7~\text{\textmu m}
originates from the high pressure layers (
p>10^{-1}
bar). The vertical variations in the ground truths in these layers (
\pm 0.1
dex) are significantly smaller than the uncertainties on the
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
posteriors (
\geq\pm 0.5
dex). Given the limited spectral information available, the impact of the
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
variations on our results is likely negligible.

Third, instead of modelling the wavelength-dependent MIR opacity of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
clouds, we assume gray clouds. Further, we assume the clouds to form in a single atmosphere layer. In contrast, for Earth, the position of the cloud-top is variable
[
50
,
p_{\mathrm{C}}{}
ranges from
\sim 0.1-0.9
bar; e.g.,]
. However, the MIR optical properties of clouds do not vary strongly with wavelength or pressure
[
74
]
. Furthermore, findings presented in
Konrad et al. [53]
suggest that physical cloud properties (such as cloud composition and particle size) cannot be constrained for the LIFE
R
and
S/N
scenarios considered here. Thus, given the limited information content of the spectra studied, the assumption of gray clouds at a fixed height is justifiable.

Fourth, we parameterized the atmospheric
P
-
T
profile using a fourth order polynomial. This model has the benefit of being highly flexible. Since it does not impose any physical constraints, it can fit any
P
-
T
structure. However, limiting the atmospheric
P
-
T
structure to physically viable states by using a learning-based parametrization
[
27
, see, e.g.]
could help improve our characterization. This is especially promising for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
models, since they link the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance profile and the clouds to the atmospheric
P
-
T
structure. However, for terrestrial atmospheres, the reliability of learning-based
P
-
T
models is currently limited by the availability of sufficient training data.

Fifth, we focus on Earth’s MIR emission spectrum, which provides an excellent benchmark
[
80
, e.g.,]
. Our finding that neglecting vertical abundance variations and clouds affects the retrieved parameter estimates can be generalized to terrestrial exoplanets. Yet, rocky exoplanets will exhibit atmospheres different from Earth. Thus, detailed studies similar to what
Rowland et al. [81]
did for cloudy L-dwarfs are required for terrestrial exoplanets to search for additional model assumptions that could affect the interpretation of MIR thermal emission spectra.

Last, the comparison of different retrieval approaches has revealed that the obtained parameter posteriors can depend on framework particularities such as the radiative transfer implementation, the parameter sampling algorithm, or the used molecular line opacities
[
59
,
8
,
2
, e.g.,]
. Community efforts (e.g., the CUISINES Working Group
5
5

5

https://nexss.info/cuisines/
) that aim to benchmark, compare, and validate different retrieval frameworks on real and simulated spectral data are indispensable to ensure the correct characterization of HZ terrestrial exoplanet atmospheres in the future.

V
Summary and Conclusions

In this study, we investigated how common atmospheric retrieval assumptions can bias the characterization of terrestrial HZ exoplanets. Specifically, we tested how model assumptions for the vertical
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance profile and the atmospheric clouds impact our characterization of Earth as an exoplanet by running retrievals with different forward models. The baseline model (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
) assumed a cloud-free atmosphere with constant abundance profiles for all trace-gases. While still cloud-free, the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
model estimated the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance profile by accounting for
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
condensation. Last, the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
model allowed for both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
condensation and a partially cloudy atmosphere. We first validated these three models in test retrievals on a simulated 1D low-noise Earth spectrum (
R=100
,
S/N=20
). Thereafter, we ran retrievals on LIFE mock observations (
R=50,100
;
S/N=10,20
) of empirical disk-integrated MIR thermal emission spectra of Earth representative of different views and seasons. The performance of the three models was benchmarked against ground truth averages from remote sensing data.

Independent of the assumed forward model, our retrieval results highlight the unique strength of considering an exoplanet’s MIR thermal emission. Earth-like
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
concentrations are easily constrained to within
\pm 1.0
dex, and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
is reliably detected at
R=100
. Further, we significantly constrain the atmospheric
P
-
T
structure (
\pm 0.5
dex for pressures,
\pm 20
K for temperatures) and the planet radius (
\pm 0.1\,R_{\oplus}
). Yet, our results also demonstrate that simplifying assumptions made by retrieval forward models can bias the characterization. For the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{C,CF}}
model, pressures are underestimated by
\sim 0.5
dex, and the abundances of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{O}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
, and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
overestimated by
\geq 0.6
dex. Also, the obtained
R_{\text{pl}}
estimates are consistently
\geq 0.05~R_{\oplus}
too low. This translates to biased equilibrium temperature (
T_{\mathrm{eq}}
) and Bond albedo (
A_{\mathrm{B}}
) estimates (
T_{\mathrm{eq}}

\geq 5
K too high;
A_{\mathrm{B}}

\geq 0.05
too low). With the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
forward model, these offsets can already be reduced significantly for most parameters. The
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
model yields bias-free estimates for the shape of the
P
-
T
structure,
R_{\text{pl}}
, the trace-gas abundances,
T_{\mathrm{eq}}
, and
A_{\mathrm{B}}
. For the surface pressure
P_{0}
and temperature
T_{0}
, the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CF}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{V,CL}}
retrievals yield lower limits. For both models, the high-pressure atmosphere layers are opaque to MIR radiation due to their high
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundance. Consequentially, these layers do not contribute to the MIR thermal emission spectrum and thus
P_{0}
and
T_{0}
are not constrainable. These results demonstrate that physically motivated forward models can facilitate the accurate interpretation of spectra.

In addition, our work has important implications for the LIFE technical requirements.
Konrad et al. [52]
derived preliminary minimal wavelength coverage,
R
, and
S/N
requirements by running a grid of retrievals on simulated Earth spectra assuming constant abundance profiles and a cloud-free atmosphere. The authors derived lower requirement limits of
4-18.5~\text{\textmu m}
,
R=50
, and
S/N=10
by requiring a detection of the potential biosignature
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
. Yet, our results on empirical Earth spectra suggest, that a bias-free
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
detection is not possible for Earth with the current baseline
R
and
S/N
requirements. Instead, we find that at
S/N=10
a minimal
R
of 100 is required to ensure a confident and bias-free
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
detection. With a proper treatment of the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
profile and clouds, an
R=100
MIR LIFE observation would allow us to correctly identify an exo-Earth as habitable and quantify the abundance of the biosignature gas
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
. Further, our findings suggest that flux measurements above
15.4~\text{\textmu m}
and between
4.6-6.2~\text{\textmu m}
are not essential for an accurate characterization of an exo-Earth with LIFE. However, this must be confirmed for non Earth-like HZ terrestrial exoplanets.

First detailed spectral measurements with the JWST have set us off on a journey to characterize and understand the conditions on terrestrial exoplanets. These efforts will intensify with future generations of optimized space missions like LIFE capable of acquiring detailed spectral measurements of nearby temperate terrestrial exoplanets. Inverse modeling approaches such as atmospheric retrievals will be used to analyze these data. Yet, as we demonstrate, simplifying assumptions in retrievals can lead to a biased interpretation. Studies that extend beyond the Earth scenario are essential to build a profound understanding of how different model assumptions may impact the interpretation of observed exoplanet spectra. These efforts are indispensable steps forward in our pursuit of understanding the habitability of distant worlds and are crucial to identifying signs of life outside the Solar System.
