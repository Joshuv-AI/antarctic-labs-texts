Collective Counterfactual Explanations via Optimal Transport
Ahmad-Reza Ehyaei, Ali Shirali, Samira Samadi

Abstract
Counterfactual explanations provide individuals with cost-optimal actions that can alter their labels to desired classes. However, if substantial instances seek state modification, such individual-centric methods can lead to new competitions and unanticipated costs. Furthermore, these recommendations, disregarding the underlying data distribution, may suggest actions that users perceive as outliers. To address these issues, our work proposes a collective approach for formulating counterfactual explanations, with an emphasis on utilizing the current density of the individuals to inform the recommended actions. Our problem naturally casts as an optimal transport problem. Leveraging the extensive literature on optimal transport, we illustrate how this collective method improves upon the desiderata of classical counterfactual explanations. We support our proposal with numerical simulations, illustrating the effectiveness of the proposed approach and its relation to classic methods.

Introduction

For individuals adversely affected by algorithmic decisions, such as a rejection from a loan application, counterfactual explanations (
CE
)
Wachter et al. 2017
provide the least costly action (e.g., increasing savings or salary to a specific threshold) to attain a favorable outcome, such as securing loan approval.
To provide a mathematical formulation, consider a population characterized by a feature space
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the decision process modeled by a classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, where
h(x)
maps instances to a discrete set, such as
\mathcal{Y}=\{\pm 1\}
. In this context, an individual
x
dissatisfied with her label (
h(x)=-1
) is advised by
\textbf{CE}(x)
to take a minimum-cost action to achieve the desired label:

\textbf{CE}(x)=\underset{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}{\text{arg\,min}}\,\{c(x,x^{\prime}):\quad h(x^{\prime})=+1\},

(1)

here,
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the modification cost function, with
c(x,x^{\prime})
representing the cost of modifying
x
to
x^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
is the subset of feasible actions
Ustun et al. 2019
.
Let’s denote the decision boundary of
h
as
L
. In a finely-grained feature space with a continuous cost function, we expect that
\textbf{CE}(x)
will lie on
L
.

Figure 1:
CE
often transitions a data point from a negative decision space to a position near the decision boundary
L
. In classifiers with a margin, this can result in the point being located in a low-probability region, an outlier concerning the underlying distribution. Such mappings may also accumulate data points in certain areas, creating new competition.

As depicted in Fig.
1
, while the
\textbf{CE}(x)
achieves cost optimization, it fails to consider the underlying distribution of the feature space, denoted by
P
. As a result, the proposed explanations, concentrated in low-density areas proximal to the decision boundary, may represent an outlier concerning
P
.
This oversight is particularly significant in classifiers such as SVM, which emphasizes the margin between the decision boundary
L
and the nearest data point.

Neglecting the underlying distribution also questions the robustness of the solution to misspecified costs or feasibility constraints. In fact, social structure or unobserved costs may have already pushed away individuals from low-density areas. Recommending to such areas might no longer be beneficial to the users. To address the above issues, it is often recommended to include
data manifold closeness
among the
CE
’s desiderata
Karimi et al. 2022
;
Guidotti 2022
;
Verma et al. 2020
.

Another issue with the current approach to
CE
is its individual-centric formulation. Since
CE
chooses the actions for each individual independently, if a substantial portion of the population seeks to change their states, an accumulation of modified states may be observed near the boundary. This
externality
can lead to increased competition, thereby escalating the cost of modification. Such phenomenon exemplifies the
tragedy of the commons

Gross and De Dreu 2019
, similar to a navigation algorithm directing users to individually optimal routes, which could be avoided or minimized with a collective approach.

1.1
Our Contribution

Towards addressing the aforementioned issues, we propose
collective counterfactual explanations
(
CCE

This involves a collective approach to recommendation and cost minimization. We choose target regions using the underlying distribution, classifier level curves, and the cost function. To prevent
CE

Optimal transport
(

Further Related Works

Poyiadzi et al. 2020
;
Kanamori et al. 2020
encode the population probability into the cost function, effectively resulting in finding
CE
in denser areas near the boundary
L
. While these approaches are beneficial in mitigating outlier recommendations, they remain individual-centric and are prone to overcrowding. Additionally, these approaches lack a principled foundation, as the integration of density into the cost function appears somewhat arbitrary.

Recently,
Carrizosa et al. 2024
introduced a notion of collective
CE
, focusing on optimizing the modification cost for a group of instances rather than for individuals. This approach aims to harmonize the behavior of
CE
within a group, thereby mitigating the occurrence of cost-outlier
CE
. However, by overlooking the underlying density, this method might still result in
CE
that are outliers with respect to probability measures. Additionally, coupling
CE
s within a group could amplify the externalities.

2
Optimal Transport

This section provides an overview of
OT
and its extensions utilized in this study.
OT
initially introduced by
Monge 1781
, focused on cost-efficient transportation of soil for fortifications, but in general,
OT
aims to transfer probability measures from space
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. While
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
are generally Polish spaces, in the scope of this work, they are considered open or closed bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Monge Problem.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
be two probability measures, and let
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a ground-cost function representing the cost of transporting a unit mass from
x
to
y
.
The
Monge’s
OT
problem
is to find a map
T:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
that
push-forward
s
P
to
Q
(i.e.,
T_{*}P=Q
)
and minimizes the effort of transportation:

\underset{T:T_{*}P=Q}{\text{min}}\,\quad\underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},T(\mathbf{X}))].

(2)

An
optimal map
is defined as
T
that minimizes the given equation.
The function
T
satisfying the property
T_{*}P=Q
is called
push-forward map

(
Ambrosio et al. 2021
,
\lx@sectionsign
1.2)
.

Regardless of the cost function
c
, Monge’s problem may be ill-posed due to the nonexistence of a push-forward map and the lack of weak sequential closure issues w.r.t. the
weak topology

Ambrosio et al. 2013
.
After 150 years,
Kantorovich 1942
addressed these weaknesses by relaxing the problem.

Kantorovich Problem.

Instead of finding an optimal map, Kantorovich proposed to minimize the transportation cost for
coupling

\pi\in\Gamma(P,Q)
:

\underset{\pi\in\Gamma(P,Q)}{\text{min}}\,\quad\underset{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})].

(3)

The solution of the Kantorovich problem, when it exists, is called the
optimal plan

(
Ambrosio et al. 2021
,
\lx@sectionsign
2.1)
.
The set of push-forward maps, denoted by
\Gamma_{0}(P,Q)
and known as Monge couplings, are specific instances of couplings characterized by
\pi\sim(X,T(X))
.

Kantorovich–Rubinstein Duality.

The
OT
problem, as introduced by Kantorovich, is formulated as a convex problem on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
, and its dual is presented as a constrained concave maximization problem
(
Ambrosio et al. 2021
,
\lx@sectionsign
3.1)
.
The Kantorovich duality states that the minimum of the Kantorovich problem is equal to the maximum of the dual problem in terms of bounded and continuous
Kantorovich potentials

\varphi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\underset{(\varphi,\psi)\in\Phi_{c}}{\text{sup}}\,\quad\underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(\mathbf{X})]+\underset{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\sim Q}{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})],

(4)

over the constraint
\Phi_{c}=\{(\varphi,\psi):\varphi(x)+\psi(x^{\prime})\leq c(x,x^{\prime})\}
.
To understand the duality concept, consider a scenario where a logistics company aims to transport goods from a resource point
x
to a manufacturing location
x^{\prime}
. The company sets a loading fee
\varphi(x)
at
x
and an unloading fee
\psi(x^{\prime})
at
x^{\prime}
. To be competitive, their profit margin, represented by
\varphi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})
, should not exceed the cost
c(x,x^{\prime})
. To maximize their overall profits, the firm strategically modifies the pricing functions
\varphi
and
\psi
in Eq.
4
.
(For more examples, see
Galichon 2018
,
\lx@sectionsign
2).

OT
with Linear Constraints.

In practical applications, solutions to the
OT
problem often need to meet certain constraints. In
Zaev 2015
work, these conditions are incorporated by introducing linear constraints.
The
OT
problem with linear constraints seeks optimal couplings with additional constraints over the family
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{C}_{L}(P,Q)
of continuous and
L^{1}
-integrable function on product space
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that:

\displaystyle\underset{\pi\in\Gamma(P,Q)}{\text{min}}\,\quad\underset{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})]\ \text{s.t.}\ \underset{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}[w(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})]=0,\quad\forall w\in\mathcal{W}.

(5)

Invariant and martingale
OT
are examples of this constrained problem.

Unbalanced OT.

In standard OT, the total supply equals the total demand.
Unbalanced
OT
extends the classical
OT
problem to scenarios where the total mass of the source and target distributions are different or not necessarily preserved during transport.
Unbalanced optimal transport (
UOT
) incorporates additional terms to account for the creation and annihilation of mass, providing a more flexible framework for real-world applications
Séjourné et al. 2022
. Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}),\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
be two positive measures and
\pi\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
be positive measure on product space. The
UOT
problem can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\underset{\pi\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}^{\prime})}{\text{min}}\,\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}c(x,x^{\prime})\,d\pi(x,x^{\prime})+\lambda_{1}D_{\varphi_{1}}(\mu,\pi_{1})+\lambda_{2}D_{\varphi_{2}}(\nu,\pi_{2}),

where
D_{\varphi_{1}}
and
D_{\varphi_{2}}
are
\varphi-
divergence
terms for mass creation and annihilation,
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
their hyper-parameters, and
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
are
\pi
’s marginal distributions on spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
.

\delta
-confidence, reducing the risk of decision boundary manipulation. (a) Boundary manipulation using classical

through circles. (b) The manipulation strategy yields weaker results in identifying the boundary in

.

Dynamic OT.

Dynamic optimal transport (
DOT
) is an extension of the classical
OT
problem, incorporating a temporal dimension to consider the evolution of mass distributions over time
Benamou and Brenier 2000
. It’s a framework for studying how a distribution of mass or probability evolves optimally from one state to another over a continuous time interval.
Let
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\mu(t,\mathbf{x})
be path of probability measures such that
\mu_{0}=P
and
\mu_{1}=Q
and
v_{t}(x)=v(t,\mathbf{x})
be a velocity field, then
DOT
can be formulated as follows:

\underset{(\mu_{t},v_{t})}{\text{arg\,min}}\,\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}c(x,v_{t}(x,t),t)\,d\mu_{t}(x)\,dt,

subject to the continuity equation

\partial\mu_{t}/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}v_{t})=0
,
where,
c(x,x^{\prime},t)
represents the cost of transporting a unit mass from point
x
to point
x^{\prime}
at time
t
, and the integral calculates the total cost used in moving the mass along the path
\mu_{t}
. The continuity equation ensures the conservation of mass during the transport.

3
Collective Counterfactual Explanations

This section introduces
CCE
, which addresses the side effects of individualized CE by considering both the collective transition cost and population probability measures.

3.1
Counterfactual Explanations Via
OT

In practical applications, we can assume the feature space
\mathcal{X}
is a closed and bounded subset, with feature variables
\mathbf{X}
and a known or empirically estimated population probability measure
P
. The classifier
h
partitions
\mathcal{X}
into two subsets:
\mathcal{X}^{-}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:h(x)=-1\}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\textbf{+}}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:h(x)=+1\}
. For simplicity, we assume
h
is constructed by
h(x)=\text{sign}(f(x))
, where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a non-constant, Lipschitz continuous score function that avoids assigning a single label to all points. So, the decision boundary is obtained by the inverse image of
f
, i.e.,
L=f^{-1}(0)
.
In establishing
OT
, with no assumption about who wants to change its state, naturally
\mathcal{X}^{-}
can be considered as the source space. Traditional
CE
aims to map points in
\mathcal{X}^{-}
to a minimal cost point on
L
. However, treating
L
as the target space leads to a concentration of points in low-density areas, altering the cost function. To circumvent this issue, we expand the boundary
L
to ensure individuals moving there face no competition for limited resources. This approach leads to the following definition.

Definition 1

(
\delta
-confidence region
)
.

For classifier
h
with above assumptions, the
\delta
-confidence region consists of smallest super-level set with probability more than
\delta
, i.e.,

L^{\textbf{+}}_{\delta}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}^{\textbf{+}}:0<f(x)<r\}
,
where
r=\inf\{\lambda:P(0<f(\mathbf{X})<\lambda))>\delta\}
.
If
\delta<P(\mathcal{X}^{\textbf{+}})
, then
L^{\textbf{+}}_{\delta}
exists.

The choice of
\delta
is guided by several intuitions: moving away from low-density areas along
L
, ensuring societal stability and minimal impact on the cost function when individuals move to the target area, and considerations of robustness. By determining the source and target, we are now prepared to set up a
OT
framework.

Definition 2

(
Collective Counterfactual Explanations
)
.

By the assumptions of Def.
1
, let
P_{\textbf{-}}
and
P_{\delta}
be
truncated probability
measures of
P
over subsets of
\mathcal{X}^{-}
and
L^{\textbf{+}}_{\delta}
.
A collective counterfactual explanations (
CCE
) plan is a coupling
\pi\in\Gamma(P_{\textbf{-}},P_{\delta})
that minimizes the total cost of modification,

\pi=\underset{\pi\in\Gamma(P_{\textbf{-}},P_{\delta})}{\text{arg\,min}}\,\quad\underset{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})].

(6)

When there is a plan
CCE
,
CE
for a given instance
x
is produced by sampling the conditional distribution
\pi
given
x
, i.e.,
x^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|x)
.

Similarly to Monge’s problem, the
CCE
map is the measurable function
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
that pushes forward
P_{\textbf{-}}
to
P_{\delta}
and minimizes the total cost of modification:

T=\underset{T:T_{*}P_{\textbf{-}}=P_{\delta}}{\text{arg\,min}}\,\quad\underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P_{\textbf{-}}}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},T(\mathbf{X}))].

(7)

If
T
exists,
CE
are obtained via
T(x)
.

The following theorem investigates the conditions for the existence of a
CCE
plan and its relationship to the dual problem.

Theorem 1

(
Villani 2009
,
\lx@sectionsign
5)
.

If modification cost function
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is
lower semi-continuous
and bounded from below, then the
CCE
plan
\pi
exists. Moreover, the collective cost of the
CCE
plan is equal to the supremum of the dual problem,

\underset{(\varphi,\psi)\in\Phi_{c}}{\text{sup}}\,\underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P_{\textbf{-}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(\mathbf{X})]+\underset{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\sim P_{\delta}}{\mathbb{E}}[\psi(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})].

(8)

Thm.
1
’s assumption regarding the cost function is more general and encompasses commonly used costs in the
CE
literature. Moreover, the dual problem offers a competitive interpretation of
CCE
as a market equilibrium.

CCE
as Market Equilibrium.

Remember the loan example in the introduction: an individual in state
x
is rejected for a loan and must change to state
x^{\prime}=\textbf{CE}(x)
to be eligible.
Let’s assume there are intermediate financial institutions that purchase loans from banks at a price
\psi(x^{\prime})
where
\psi(x^{\prime})
denotes the monetary risk value of issuing a loan to an individual in state
x^{\prime}
and selling these loans to applicants in state
x
at a price
\varphi(x)
.
Applicants have the option to either purchase loans directly or alter their state to become eligible for a loan. The incentive to purchase rather than modify their state hinges on ensuring the profit
\varphi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})
is lower than the cost of change
c(x,x^{\prime})
. Otherwise, they would prefer altering their state over buying a loan.

In a competitive market, financial institutions aim to maximize collective profit, so reaching an equilibrium where profit is at its peak, expressed as:

\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in\Phi_{c}}\underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P_{\textbf{-}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\varphi(\mathbf{X})]+\underset{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\sim P_{\delta}}{\mathbb{E}}[-\psi(\mathbf{X}^{\prime})].

According to Thm.
1
, this equilibrium reflects the collective efficient effort required for state modification.
This fact is instrumental in evaluating the subjective cost function concept. It suggests that in market equilibrium, the cost can be inferred from the seller profit, which means
c(x,x^{\prime})=\varphi(x)-\psi(x^{\prime})
.

While a
CCE
plan exists under minimal assumptions,
CCE
maps are more suited for practical applications. The upcoming theorem delineates conditions under which an optimal plan can be approximated by an optimal map, even when a global optimal map solution is not available.

Proposition 1

(
Gangbo 1999
)
.

By the assumptions of Def.
2
, if
P_{\textbf{-}}
is
continuous measure
then optimal maps are dense in the space of couplings:

\Gamma(P_{\textbf{-}},P_{\delta})=\overline{\Gamma_{0}(P_{\textbf{-}},P_{\delta})},

where
\Gamma_{0}
is set of Monge couplings and
\overline{\Gamma_{0}}
is its set
closure
. If
c
is continuous and bounded then:

\underset{\pi\in\Gamma}{\text{min}}\,\ \underset{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})]=\underset{T\in\Gamma_{0}}{\text{min}}\,\ \underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P_{\textbf{-}}}{\mathbb{E}}[c(\mathbf{X},T(\mathbf{X}))].

An immediate consequence of Prop.
1
is that
CCE
plans and estimated
CCE
maps can converge arbitrarily closely in terms of total cost.
Finally, we outline the sufficient conditions for the existence of a
CCE
map.

Theorem 2

(
Villani 2009
,
\lx@sectionsign
10)
.

By Def.
2
assumptions, let
P
be continuous measure, and assume that
c
is twice continuously differentiable and for every compact set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is
c_{K}>0
such that for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}|c(x_{1},x^{\prime})\textendash c(x_{2},x^{\prime})|\leq c_{K}|x_{1}\textendash x_{2}|,

and also has the twist condition holds, i.e., for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
,

\nabla_{x}c(x,x^{\prime}_{1})=\nabla_{x}c(x,x^{\prime}_{2})\Rightarrow x^{\prime}_{1}=x^{\prime}_{2}.

Then by the assumptions of Def.
2
we have:

1.

Both
CCE
plan and map exist and the collective cost of them are equal.
Moreover, the plan is unique and is generated by map
T
;

2.

The solution
(\varphi,\psi)
of the dual problem is unique (up to an additive constant), and
\varphi
is almost-everywhere differentiable. Moreover
\psi(x^{\prime})=\underset{x\in\mathcal{X}}{\inf}(c(x,x^{\prime})\textendash\varphi(x))
;

3.

T
and
\varphi
are related by

T(x)=e_{x}(\nabla\varphi(x)),

where
e_{x}(\cdot):=\nabla_{x}c(x,\cdot)^{\textendash 1}
.

3.2
Collective Counterfactual Explanations Desiderata

In this section, we explore how
CCE
augments traditional
CE
by integrating a list of additional desiderata as mentioned in surveys
Verma et al. 2020
;
Karimi et al. 2022
.

Actionability.

Actionability refers to the feasibility of implementing proposed changes in counterfactual scenarios
Ustun et al. 2019
. Variables such as birthplace, immutable, or age, characterized by limited modifiability and a naturally increasing trajectory, should be regarded as constraints when designing
CE
.
One method to incorporate these constraints is to embed them into the cost function. For instance, the cost of changing gender can be set sufficiently high.
Alternatively, constraints can be formulated as functions and integrated to
CCE
by using extensions of OT with
linear constraints
. This approach is feasible because conditions, encoded as Boolean functions, which are the limits of bounded continuous functions, therefore can be employed as linear constraints.
The following proposition suggests that the existence of a
CCE
plan is assured, as long as there is at least one coupling that meets the specified linear constraints.

Proposition 2

(
Zaev 2015
)
.

With conditions of Prop.
1
, the
OT
problem with linear constraints has a solution if and only if the set
\Gamma_{\mathcal{W}}:=\{\pi\in\Gamma:\int wd\pi=0,w\in\overline{\mathcal{W}}\}

is not empty, where
\overline{\mathcal{W}}
, the closure of
\mathcal{W}
in the
C_{L}
topology.

Sparsity.

To achieve sparsity in
CE
—which focuses on minimizing feature alterations in recommendations state
x^{\prime}
—a sparsity objective function
g(x^{\prime}-x)
, such as
L_{0}/L_{1}
norms as suggested by
Miller 2019
, can be employed. This approach is also applicable to
CCE
by integrating the sparsity objective into the cost function, a concept further detailed in
Cuturi et al. 2023
.

Amortized Inference.

Traditional
CE
generation typically involves an optimization process for each instance. In contrast, amortized inference enhances
CE
generation by utilizing patterns learned from previous instances, eliminating the need for individual optimization for each new data point.
When a
CCE
map
T
exists, numerical methods can be used to approximate it. This approximation function is not limited to instances present in the training data; it can be extended to other cases as well.

CCE

Data Manifold Closeness.

To ensure credibility and avoid outlier positions, a
CE
should resemble the training dataset and preserve feature correlations. In
CCE
, recommendations are spread across the data manifold by targeting a significant part of the positive region, thereby avoiding minimum-cost outliers. Additionally, adjusting
UOT
hyperparameters can steer recommendations toward denser areas.

Security & Privacy.

The classical
CE

(a). Each
CE
suggests that instances within a radius
c(x,\textbf{CE}(x))
centered at
x
belong to the negative region, enabling swift manipulation of the decision boundary. Conversely, in
CCE
, the recommendations are influenced by the choice of
L^{\textbf{+}}_{\delta}
, the probability measure
P
, and the collective modification cost considered in the optimization process. Combining these diverse elements in the search for
CCE
complicates the boundary manipulation, as shown in Fig.
3
(b).

Robustness & Individual Fairness.

In real-world decision-making applications, robustness and individual fairness are crucial aspects of the algorithm. They ensure that individuals with similar features receive similar recommendations.
In recent work,
Otto et al. 2021
(Cor. 1.4) shows that under mild conditions on the probability density function
P
and for a general family of cost functions, the
CCE
map constitutes a diffeomorphism. Given that the space
\mathcal{X}
is compact, it implies that minor modifications in state result in only slight variations in the explanations. The classical
CE
does not consider these two properties. Regarding the boundary shape, it may result in jumps in
\textbf{CE}(x)
recommendations.

4.1
Path-Guided Counterfactual Explanations

Applying the standard
CE
method faces challenges due to the uncertain trajectory towards the final modification state, especially in prolonged processes like obtaining a loan. Path-guided
CE
overcomes this by demonstrating the progression towards the goal through temporal steps, similar to displacement interpolation in dynamic
OT
. This interpolation, a continuous-time version of
OT
, connects initial and final probability distributions. The subsequent theorem emphasizes that in a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
with geodesics, if a
CCE
map exists, displacement interpolation generates geodesics in the probability measure space, relying on the geodesics of the underlying space
\mathcal{X}
between
x
and the
CCE
map
T(x)

Khan and Zhang 2022
.

Theorem 3

(
Villani 2009
,
\lx@sectionsign
7)
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a Polish,
locally compact

length space
. Then, given any two measures
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, and a continuous curve
(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}\in

\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})_{p}
, the following properties are equivalent:

i.

\mu_{t}
is the push-forward by the flow of minimizing, constant-speed geodesics from
x
to
T(x)
, where
T
is the solution to the OT map.

ii.

(\mu_{t})_{0\leq t\leq 1}
is a geodesic curve in the space
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})_{p}
.

In the case of the squared-distance cost function, the displacement interpolation
\mu_{t}
for
t\in[0,1]
is defined by:

\mu_{t}=((1-t)\text{id}+tT)_{*}P,

where
id
is the identity map on
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
T_{t}(x):[0,1]\to\mathcal{X}
denote the
constant speed geodesic
connecting
x
to
T(x)
. Then, according to Thm.
3
, the optimal geodesic in the probability space is achieved by
\mu_{t}=(T_{t})_{*}P
. This means that the optimal flow maps
x
along the geodesics
T_{t}(x)
towards its target. Such a parametrization is instrumental in constructing path-guided
CE
based on discrete temporal steps that can be seen in Fig.
4
and Fig.
7
.

4.2
CE
for Ordered Classifiers

In traditional
CE
, it is commonly assumed that eligibility is determined by a single classifier. However, in real-world scenarios, like loan applications, eligibility may vary across different loan amounts. For example, a loan request for the amount
l
is rejected, but for a lower amount
l^{\prime}
, it will be accepted. As a result, distinct classifiers are employed at each loan value to make individualized decisions. It means for each loan amount
l
, there exists a classifier
h_{l}:\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}=\{\pm 1\}
. This family of classifiers follows a natural order, i.e.,
l_{1}<l_{2}\implies h_{l_{1}}(x)\geq h_{l_{2}}(x)
.

Figure 7:
Utilizing the back-and-forth method, the
CCE
map is estimated, and through constructing the displacement interpolation, the optimal flow that pushes forward the negative area into the
\delta
-confidence region is visualized.

In this scenario, the
CE
problem aims to identify the optimal state modification
x^{\prime}
for each
x
in
\mathcal{X}
and
l
in space of loan values
\mathcal{T}=[0,B]
:

\textbf{CE}_{l}(x):=\underset{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}{\text{inf}}\ c(x,x^{\prime})\ \text{s.t.}\ h_{l}(x^{\prime})=1.

To formulate the ordered classifier version of
CE
within the
OT
framework, in addition to the feature space probability
P
, it is essential to know
T
, the loan amounts probability distribution.
Let
S
represent the joint probability of features and loans over the space
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T})
.

For simplicity assume
h_{l}
is defined as the sign of a real-valued, continuously differentiable function
f:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at level
l
, i.e.,
h_{l}(x)=\text{sign}(f(x,l))
. To maintain the properties of an ordered classifier,
h
should be a decreasing function with respect to
l
. This is equivalent to assuming that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial l}<0
. Under this formulation, the decision boundary for the family of classifiers is determined as follows:

L=\{(x,t)\in(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{T}):f(x,t)=0\}.

Given that
\nabla f
is non-zero everywhere, the implicit function theorem
(
Lang 2012
,
\lx@sectionsign
5)
states that
L
is a
C^{1}

n
-dimensional manifold in an
(n+1)
-dimensional space
\mathcal{S}
.
It is presumed that the probability measure
S
is absolutely continuous, hence it possesses a probability density function
g
.
We select
L
as the target space. Given the presence of a continuous family of ordered classifiers, it is reasonable to assume that the margins are sufficiently enriched to map points to these classifiers. Furthermore, the concept of buffering the decision boundary of
L^{\textbf{+}}_{\delta}
by a margin
\delta
is applicable in this context.
To derive the target probability measure
S_{L}
on surface
L
, we apply the coarea formula
(
Evans 2018
,
\lx@sectionsign
3)
with the density function
g(s)
leading to:

S_{L}(A)=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{A}g(s)\frac{1}{|\nabla f(s)|}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(s)\quad\text{for }A\subset L,

here
\mathcal{H}^{n}
is the
(n)-dimensional Hausdorff measure
,
|\nabla f(s)|
is the norm of the gradient of
f
at
s
, and
Z
is a normalization factor ensuring that
S_{L}
is a probability measure on
L
, with
S_{L}(L)=1
. The term
\frac{1}{|\nabla f(s)|}
ensures proper scaling along the surface
L
.

To construct the source measure, let
S^{-}
be the restriction of the probability measure
S
to the space
\{(x,t)\in\mathcal{S}:f(x,t)<0\}
.
OT
can now be applied to the probability measures
S^{-}
and
S_{L}
with the cost function
c^{*}((x,t),(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))=c(x,x^{\prime})
. To ensure that in the explanations
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
,
t^{\prime}\geq t
, a constraint
1_{\{T^{\prime}>T\}}
is added to
OT
problem where
T,T^{\prime}
are the values of a loan in source and target densities.
Utilizing
OT
with linear constraints enables the identification of the most efficient plan and the creation of
CCE
for ordered classifiers.

4.3
Utility-based Counterfactuals Explanations

To better align
CE
with human decision-making contexts like loan applications, it is advantageous to restructure it using scenarios based on human utility functions. In classical
CE
, the utility function of an individual at state
x
who wishes to modify their state to
x^{\prime}
for loan approval can be expressed as follows:

u(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}(1+h(x^{\prime}))l-c(x,x^{\prime}),

(9)

here,
l
is the additional utility from a positive decision. It is a natural assumption that for all
x^{\prime}
satisfying
h(x^{\prime})=1
, the expression
l-c(x,x^{\prime})
is not negative. By this formulation it is easy to see that the optimal solution of Eq.
9
is found by minimizing the cost function,
\text{argmin}\ c(x,x^{\prime});\ h(x^{\prime})=1
, aligning with the counterfactual optimization problem as described in Eq.
1
.
The utility function in Eq.
9
represents a simplified real-world scenario by assuming that transitioning to state
x^{\prime}
does not alter individual utility and ignores the fact that certain actions, such as increasing saving, enhance utility independently of the loan’s approval. Therefore, we can assign utility to each state:
u:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then moving to a better creditworthy condition
x^{\prime}
, not only entails a cost
-c(x,x^{\prime})
but also yields an additional utility
\eta(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq u(x^{\prime})-u(x)
. Consequently, a more nuanced model can be expressed as follows:

u(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}(1+h(x^{\prime}))l-c(x,x^{\prime})+\eta(x,x^{\prime}).

(10)

In Eq.
10
, it is assumed that the individual requires a loan to address their financial challenges, i.e., for all
x^{\prime}
where
h(x^{\prime})=-1
, it holds that
-c(x,x^{\prime})+\eta(x,x^{\prime})<0
. Additionally, within a finite time frame (e.g., one year), the net benefit of changing states, given by
\eta(x,x^{\prime})-c(x,x^{\prime})
, is not always positive. For example, the cost of acquiring savings of 1 million dollars for an average worker goes to infinity, while the utility
\eta(x,x^{\prime})
remains finite. Hence, the optimal solution can be formulated as:

\underset{x^{\prime}:h(x^{\prime})=1}{\text{arg\,max}}\,l-c(x,x^{\prime})+\eta(x,x^{\prime}),

(11)

This advanced formulation accounts for both the costs and benefits associated with changing states, providing a more nuanced understanding of the decision-making process in financial contexts.
In Eq.
11
, reporting a single point as a
CE
is not obligatory. However, if a customer aims for a minimum utility of
u_{o}
, then any points of set
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{x}=\{x^{\prime}:l-c(x,x^{\prime})+\eta(x,x^{\prime})>u_{0}\}
can be selected as a target. If the
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\underset{x\in\mathcal{X}^{-}}{\bigcap}\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{x}
is non-zero measurable set (assume a case that the value of
u^{+}
is more than cost
c(x,x^{\prime})
alongside decision boundary
L
, so
L\subset\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
), then we can define
OT
problem for
\mathcal{X}^{-}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
spaces.

We extend this model with a temporal dimension in
\lx@sectionsign

A.3
. In particular, we consider a setting where utilities are not observable and demonstrate that taking the underlying density of individuals into account, as we do in our optimal transport formulation, gives the optimal action.

6
Discussion and Future works

CCE
is specifically designed to address the limitations of traditional
CE
. It takes into account the probability distribution of the population, effectively preventing the significant performative impacts that may arise from substantial shifts in individual behavior on the cost function. Our research demonstrates that
OT
theory, along with its computational tools, is highly effective in addressing various concerns in the design of counterfactuals.

To compute the
CE
solution, the estimation of
P
is not crucial.
The Sinkhorn methods facilitate working with sample distributions rather than entire distributions. Moreover, the assumption regarding
h
is not obligatory; we can employ black-box models for classifiers.
A further challenge involves implementing
CCE
with real-world datasets. As we can only report additional costs relative to classical
CE
, we confine our analysis to synthetic examples. These examples intuitively demonstrate the impact of
CCE
on recommendations.

Selecting the appropriate value for
\delta
requires a mathematical framework for modeling the externality’s impact on the cost function, which requires a separate research endeavor. In our exploration of the various desiderata of classical
CE
, we omitted a discussion on causality. Given the evolving theory of optimal transport within a causal framework, this subject will be the focus of our subsequent research.

Definition 3

(
Push-forward Measure
)
.

Let
P
,
Q
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is map, the measure
Q
is called the push-forward of
P
through
T
is denoted by
T_{*}P
if:

Q(B)=P(T^{-1}(B)),\quad\forall B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

Definition 4

(
Weak Topology
)
.

Weak topology on a space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is defined by convergence in distribution. A sequence of probability measures
(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
converges weakly to a probability measure
P
if for every bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\underset{\mathbf{X}_{n}\sim P_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}[f(\mathbf{X}_{n})]=\underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P}{\mathbb{E}}[f(\mathbf{X})].

Definition 5

(
Set of Couplings
)
.

The set
\Gamma(P,Q)
represents the couplings of probability distributions
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, comprising distributions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with margins
P
and
Q
. A measure
\pi
belongs to
\Gamma(P,Q)
if and only if

\pi(A\times\mathbb{R}^{d})=P(A)\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times B)=Q(B)\quad\forall A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

By extension, a random pair
(X,Y)\sim\pi
, where
\pi\in\Gamma(P,Q)
, will also be called a coupling of
P
and
Q
.

Definition 6

(
Linear Constraints
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{L}(P)
and
\mathcal{C}_{L}(Q)
be the space of continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and
L^{1}
-integrable respect to
P
and
Q
measures. Let
\mathcal{C}_{L}(P,Q)
be the family of continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
such that:

\mathcal{C}_{L}(P,Q)=\{h\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}):\exists f=f_{1}+f_{2}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ |h|\leq f\},

where
f_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}(P)
,
f_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{L}(Q)
.

Definition 7

(
c-cyclical monotonicity
(Ch. 4
Ambrosio et al. 2021
))
.

A probability measure
\pi
on
X\times Y
satisfies
c
-cyclical monotonicity if, for any finite set
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
in its support and any permutation
\sigma
, the inequality
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x_{i},y_{\sigma(i)})
holds.

Definition 8

(
c-transforms; c-superdifferential
(
Ambrosio et al. 2021
,
\lx@sectionsign
.4))
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
be a function and
c
a cost function. The
c_{+}
-transform of
\varphi
, denoted
\varphi^{c_{+}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, is defined by:

\varphi^{c_{+}}(x^{\prime}):=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\{c(x,x^{\prime})-\varphi(x)\}

The
c
-superdifferential of
\varphi
,
\partial^{c+}\varphi\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is given by:

\partial^{c+}\varphi:=\{(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}:\varphi(x)+\varphi^{c_{+}}(x^{\prime})=c(x,x^{\prime})\}

Definition 9

(
The Wasserstein Metric
)
.

In the metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
, OT naturally defines a metric known as the Wasserstein or Earth Mover’s distance. To define the Wasserstein metric, we consider the space of probability measures with finite
p
-th moment (Wasserstein space):

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})=\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\ \underset{\mathbf{X}\sim P}{\mathbb{E}}[d^{p}(\mathbf{X},x^{\prime})]<\infty,\forall x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\}

For two probability measures
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{X})
, the
p
-Wasserstein distance is defined as:

\displaystyle W_{p}(P,Q)=\bigg(\underset{\pi\in\Gamma(P,Q)}{\text{inf}}\,\quad\underset{(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})\sim\pi}{\mathbb{E}}[d^{p}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}^{\prime})]\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

This metric is positive-definite, finite, symmetric, and adheres to the triangle inequality
(
Villani 2009
,
\lx@sectionsign
6)
.

Definition 10

(
Truncated Probability Measure
)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a probability measure and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
P(A)>0
, the truncated probability measure
P_{A}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as:

P_{A}(B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}

for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 11

(
lower semi-continuous
)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is said to be lower semi-continuous (l.s.c) at a point
x_{0}\in rd
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
such that for all
x\in U
,
f(x)>f(x_{0})-\epsilon
. In economic terms, this concept implies that small perturbations in the input do not lead to a substantial decrease in the function value, signifying a form of stability or predictability in economic models, such as cost functions in production processes.

Definition 12

(
Closure
)
.

Given a set
A
in a topological space
X
, the
closure
of
A
, denoted by
\overline{A}
, is the smallest closed set in
X
that contains
A
. Equivalently, it includes all the points of
A
along with all its limit points (i.e., points that can be approached arbitrarily closely by points in
A
).

Definition 13

(
Continuous Measure
)
.

A measure
\mu
on a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{F})
is said to be
continuous
if for every
A\in\mathcal{F}
,
\mu(A)=0
whenever
A
is a set of a single point. In other words,
\mu(\{x\})=0
for every
x\in X
.

Definition 14

(
c-Conjugate
)
.

Given a cost function
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
are two metric spaces, the
c
-conjugate of a function
\varphi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function
\varphi^{c}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as follows:

\varphi^{c}(x^{\prime})=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{c(x,x^{\prime})-\varphi(x)\}

This transformation is crucial in optimal transport theory, as it relates the potential functions on the spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
concerning the cost function
c
. The
c
-conjugate is used to establish duality theorems and to find solutions to the Monge-Kantorovich problem.

Definition 15

(
\varphi
-divergence
)
.

The
\varphi
-divergence between two probability distributions
P
and
Q
over the same probability space, for a convex function
\varphi
, is defined as

D_{\varphi}(P||Q)=\int\varphi\left(\frac{dP}{dQ}\right)dQ

where
\frac{dP}{dQ}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
P
with respect to
Q
. The divergence measures the difference between the two distributions, with different choices of
\varphi
leading to different divergence measures. Common examples include the Kullback-Leibler divergence for
\varphi(x)=x\log x
, the Total Variation distance for
\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x-1|
, and the squared Hellinger distance for
\varphi(x)=(\sqrt{x}-1)^{2}
.

Definition 16

(
Length Space
)
.

A length space is a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
such that for every pair of points
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, the distance
d(x,x^{\prime})
between them is equal to the infimum of the lengths of continuous curves in
X
connecting
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Formally, this can be written as:

d(x,x^{\prime})=\inf\{L(\gamma)\mid\gamma\text{ is a curve connecting }x\text{ and }x^{\prime}\}

where
L(\gamma)
denotes the length of the curve
\gamma
, and a curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathcal{X}
is a continuous map from a closed interval
[a,b]
in the real line
\mathbb{R}
to the space
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 17

(
Locally Compact Space
)
.

A topological space
\mathcal{X}
is called locally compact if, for every point
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that the closure of
U
, denoted as
\overline{U}
, is compact in
\mathcal{X}
. In other words, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there is an open set
U
containing
x
such that the smallest closed set containing
U
is compact.

Definition 18

(
constant speed geodesic
)
.

A constant speed geodesic in a metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{X}
that satisfies the following properties:

1.

Geodesic Property:
For any
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,1]
, the distance
d(\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{2}))
is proportional to
|t_{2}-t_{1}|
. This means
\gamma
is the shortest path between its endpoints.

2.

Constant Speed:
There exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,1]
, we have
d(\gamma(t_{1}),\gamma(t_{2}))=c\cdot|t_{2}-t_{1}|
. This implies that the curve
\gamma
traverses distance at a uniform rate.

In simple terms, a constant speed geodesic is the shortest path in a metric space that is traveled at a uniform speed.

Definition 19

(
n-dimensional Hausdorff Measure
)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. The n-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{n}
of a subset
A\subseteq X
is defined as follows:

\mathcal{H}^{n}(A)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\operatorname{diam}(U_{i})\right)^{n}:\{U_{i}\}\text{ is a }\delta\text{-cover of }A\right\}

where a
\delta\text{-cover}
of
A
is a countable collection of sets
\{U_{i}\}
with
\operatorname{diam}(U_{i})<\delta
such that
A\subseteq\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
, and
\operatorname{diam}(U_{i})
is the diameter of the set
U_{i}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{H}^{n}
generalizes the notion of n-dimensional volume, with
\mathcal{H}^{1}
representing length,
\mathcal{H}^{2}
area, and
\mathcal{H}^{3}
volume.

(

\delta

L^{\textbf{+}}_{\delta}
consists of smallest buffer of

Theorem 4

(Fundamental theorem of optimal transport)
.

Assume that
c:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous and bounded from below and let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
be such that

c(x,x^{\prime})\leq a(x)+b(x^{\prime})
,
for some
a\in L^{1}(\mu),b\in L^{1}(\nu)
. Also, let
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
. Then the following three are equivalent:

•

the plan
\pi
is optimal,

•

the set
supp(\pi)
is c-cyclically monotone,

•

there exists a c-concave function
\varphi
such that
max\{\varphi,0\}\in L_{1}(\mu)
and
supp(\pi)\subset\partial^{c+}\varphi

•

Then the minimum of the Kantorovich problem is equal to the supremum of the dual problem
4
. Furthermore, the supremum of the dual problem is attained, and the maximizing couple
(\varphi,\psi)
is of the form
(\varphi,\varphi^{c+})
for some c-concave function
\varphi
.

A.3
Motivating Example

Assume individuals live in a finite set of states
\mathcal{X}
. A binary classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
divides these states into positive states
\mathcal{X}^{+}
and negative states
\mathcal{X}^{-}
. By paying a cost of
c(x,x^{\prime})
, one can move from state
x
to
x^{\prime}
. In the original formulation of CE, the cost function is assumed observable, and individuals are willing to pay any cost to get into
\mathcal{X}^{+}
from
\mathcal{X}^{-}
, so the question is about which positive state costs less. This formulation simply treats all states in
\mathcal{X}^{+}
as equally beneficial,
neglects the temporal dimension of improvement, and is unaware of the incentives underlying individual progress even in the absence of the decision made by
h
.

In a more realistic formulation, we assume state
x
provides a utility of
u(x)
to an individual at
x
at each time step. Unlike the cost function, these utilities may not be directly observable to us. The individuals live for an infinite horizon however they discount future additions to the utility by a factor of
\gamma<1
per time step (or effectively live for
1/(1-\gamma)
steps). We may assume individuals are not capable of incurring a cost more than
B
at every step. So, to move from
x
to
x^{\prime}
, since there is no intermediate state in our finite state model, they have to wait for roughly
c(x,x^{\prime})/B
time steps at
x
. Without this movement, the effective utility of an individual staying at
x
forever is
u(x)/(1-\gamma)
. The movement from
x
to
x^{\prime}
comes with an additional utility of

u(x^{\prime})\frac{\gamma^{c(x,x^{\prime})/B}}{1-\gamma}+u(x)\frac{1-\gamma^{c(x,x^{\prime})/B}}{1-\gamma}-\frac{u(x)}{1-\gamma}.

Define effective cost as
\tilde{c}(x,x^{\prime})=1-\gamma^{c(x,x^{\prime})/B}
. Then an individual maximizing her utility from movement should solve

\underset{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}{\text{arg\,max}}\,u_{0}(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq(1-\tilde{c}(x,x^{\prime}))(u(x^{\prime})-u(x)).

(12)

Now in the presence of a decision made by
h
(e.g., a loan), we assume all the positive states come with an additional utility of
l
. We assume individuals are not aware of
h
and therefore in the absence of CE the best action would come from maximizing
u_{0}
. This solution, although aligned with the general interest of the individual, might miss the opportunity that
h
has generated. Optimally, CE should solve

\underset{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{+}}{\text{arg\,max}}\,u_{l}(x,x^{\prime})\coloneqq(1-\tilde{c}(x,x^{\prime}))(u(x^{\prime})+l-u(x))=u_{0}(x,x^{\prime})+l(1-\tilde{c}(x,x^{\prime})).

(13)

However, the standard method of CE only partially solves this problem by maximizing
(1-\tilde{c}(x,x^{\prime}))
, or equivalently neglecting
u(x^{\prime})-u(x)
. We next discuss how the current actions of individuals may guide CE to solve Eq.
12
instead of this simplification.

CE to Partially Rational Individuals.

In fact, although utilities may not be directly observable, assuming a level of rationality for individuals, their actions partially reveal their perceived utilities. Formally, let
\pi_{\beta}(x^{\prime}|x)
be the distribution of the current actions taken by individuals at state
x
. Recall that in our simplified model we assumed the knowledge of
f
is not publicly available and individuals aim at maximizing
u_{0}
. We assume a
\lambda
-rational individual chooses the action from

\pi_{\beta}(x^{\prime}|x)=\exp(\lambda\,u_{0}(x,x^{\prime}))/z,

where
z
is a normalizing factor. A fully rational individual corresponds to
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Using this model, we can write
u_{0}
as
\log(\pi_{\beta})/\lambda
up to an additive constant. This allows us to write the optimization of Eq.
13
as

\underset{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{+}}{\text{arg\,min}}\,\tilde{c}(x,x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\lambda l}\log(\pi_{\beta}(x^{\prime}|x)).

(14)

Unfortunately,
\pi_{\beta}
may not be directly observable and requires longitudinal studies. Alternatively, under the stationary assumption, we can approximate
\pi_{\beta}
with a coupling that moves the mass from
\mathcal{X}^{-}
to
\mathcal{X}^{+}
. The Eq.
14
turns into an unbalanced Sinkhorn’s algorithm (
\lx@sectionsign

B.2
) with appropriately chosen entropy regularization.

B.1
Sinkhorn’s algorithm

The Sinkhorn algorithm (
Peyré et al. 2017
,
\lx@sectionsign
4
) approximates optimal transport plans between distributions by using entropy regularization, which speeds up computation and stabilizes solutions in large datasets. This iterative method adjusts scaling vectors to minimize regularized transport costs, beneficial for machine learning and data analysis in optimal transport contexts. The computational complexity per Sinkhorn iteration is
O(n^{2})
, with total complexity dependent on the number of iterations for convergence.

For two discrete distributions
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
, with
N
and
M
points respectively, and a cost matrix
\mathbf{C}
, the algorithm solves the entropic regularized optimal transport problem. This problem is formulated as minimizing:

\min_{\mathbf{T}\geq 0}\langle\mathbf{T},\mathbf{C}\rangle_{F}-\epsilon\cdot H(\mathbf{T})\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{T}\mathbf{1}_{M}=\mathbf{P},\quad\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}=\mathbf{Q}

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
denotes the Frobenius dot product,
\epsilon
is a small regularization parameter,
H(\mathbf{T})
is the entropy of the transport plan
\mathbf{T}
, and
\mathbf{1}_{M}
,
\mathbf{1}_{N}
are vectors of ones of appropriate sizes.

Algorithm 1

Sinkhorn Method for Optimal Plan

Input:
probability measures
P=(p_{i})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q=(q_{j})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, cost matrix
C=(c_{ij})_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, regularization parameter
\epsilon
and number of iterations
N
;

Output:
approximated optimal transport matrix
\pi
;

Initialize:

u_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
,
v_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{m}
;

Compute:

K=e^{-\epsilon C}

for

n=0

to

N-1

do

Update
u_{n+1}=\frac{P}{Kv_{n}}

Update
v_{n+1}=\frac{Q}{K^{\top}u_{n}}

end

for

Return:

\mathbf{T}=\text{diag}(u_{N})K\text{diag}(v_{N})
;

B.2
Unbalanced Sinkhorn’s algorithm

The unbalanced Sinkhorn algorithm (
Peyré et al. 2017
,
\lx@sectionsign
10
) is an extension of the classic Sinkhorn algorithm, adapted for solving optimal transport problems where the mass of the distributions does not necessarily match.
The Unbalanced Sinkhorn algorithm modifies this problem to allow for differences in mass between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
. The constraints are relaxed using so-called Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence terms, leading to the unbalanced optimal transport problem:

\min_{\mathbf{T}\geq 0}\langle\mathbf{T},\mathbf{C}\rangle_{F}-\epsilon\cdot H(\mathbf{T})+\lambda_{1}\cdot\text{KL}(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{1}_{M}\|\mathbf{P})+\lambda_{2}\cdot\text{KL}(\mathbf{T}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{N}\|\mathbf{Q})

Here,
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are regularization parameters for the marginal constraints, and the KL divergence terms
\text{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)
measure the discrepancy between the marginals of the transport plan
\mathbf{T}
and the given distributions

In empirical settings, the algorithm deals with discrete distributions often derived from data samples. This involves computing a transport plan between empirical distributions, which are represented as sums of Dirac masses. The empirical part of the algorithm refers to its application to empirical distributions, i.e., distributions represented by samples (data points), which is a common scenario in practical applications.

Unbalanced Sinkhorn Optimal Transport

Input:
probability measures
P=(p_{i})_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Q=(q_{j})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, cost matrix
C=(c_{ij})_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, regularization parameter
\epsilon
,
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
regularization parameters and number of iterations
N
;

Output:
approximated optimal transport matrix
\pi
;

Initialize:

u_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{n}
,
v_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{m}
;

Compute:

K=e^{-\epsilon C}

for

n=0

to

N-1

do

Update
u_{n+1}=\big(\frac{P}{Kv_{n}}\big)^{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\epsilon+\lambda_{1}}}

Update
v_{n+1}=\big(\frac{Q}{K^{\top}u_{n}})^{\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\epsilon+\lambda_{2}}}

end

for

Return:

\mathbf{T}=\text{diag}(u_{N})K\text{diag}(v_{N})
;

B.3
The back-and-forth method

The Back-and-Forth
Jacobs and Léger 2020
method offers a robust solution for computing optimal transport maps with strictly convex costs, including p-power costs, for probability densities
P
and
Q
on an
n
-point grid. This method, characterized by its computational efficiency, requires
O(n)
storage and
O(n\log(n))
computation per iteration. The iteration count needed to achieve
\epsilon
accuracy is proportional to
O(\max(\|P\|_{\infty},\|Q\|_{\infty})\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon}))
, showcasing the method’s effectiveness in both storage and computational resource optimization.

In the back-and-forth method
\Omega
, is consider as a convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and focuses on a specific cost function
c\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
c(x,y)=h(y-x)
. Here,
h\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly convex and even function. The dual Kantorovich problem is considered in the two following equivalent forms:

I(\psi)=\int\psi\,d\mu+\int\psi^{c}\,d\nu\quad\text{or}\quad J(\phi)=\int\varphi\,d\nu+\int\varphi^{c}\,d\mu,

where
\varphi^{c},\psi^{c}
are
c-
transformation of
\varphi,\psi
. The gradient of
J(\phi)
in the space of functions from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
can be written as (
Jacobs and Léger 2020
, Lemma 3
):

\nabla J(\phi)=(-\Delta)^{-1}\big(\nu-T_{\varphi\,*}P\big),

where the
\Delta
is Laplacian operator and

T_{\varphi}(x)=x-(\nabla h)^{-1}(\nabla\varphi^{c}(x))

is the explicit solution of c-transformation (
Jacobs and Léger 2020
, Lemma 1
).
We now introduce the gradient descent back-and-forth method.

Algorithm 3

Back-and-Forth
Method for Optimal Map

Input:
probability measures
P
and
Q
, cost function
c
and number of iterations
N
;

Output:
approximated Kantorovich potential functions;

Initialize:
set
\varphi_{0}=0,\psi_{0}=0

for

n=0

to

N-1

do

\varphi_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\varphi_{n}+\sigma\nabla J(\varphi_{n})
,

\psi_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(\varphi_{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{c}
,

\psi_{n+1}=\psi_{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\sigma\nabla I(\psi_{n+\frac{1}{2}})
,

\varphi_{n+1}=(\psi_{n+1})^{c}
.

end

for

Return:

\varphi_{N},\psi_{N}
;

Theorem
2
establishes that if
\varphi
is the solution to the dual Kantorovich problem, then
T_{\varphi}
constitutes the optimal map.
