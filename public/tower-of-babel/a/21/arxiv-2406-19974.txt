Generalizing self-normalized importance sampling with couplings
Nicola Branchini, and

Abstract
An essential problem in statistics and machine learning is the estimation of expectations involving probability density functions (PDFs) with intractable normalizing constants . The self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS) estimator, which normalizes the importance sampling (IS) weights, has become the standard approach due to its simplicity. However, the SNIS has been shown to exhibit high variance in challenging estimation problems, e.g, involving rare events or posterior predictive distributions in Bayesian statistics. Further, most of the state-of-the-art adaptive importance sampling (AIS) methods adapt the proposal as if the weights had not been normalized. In this paper, we propose a framework that considers the original task as estimation of a ratio of two integrals. In our new formulation, we obtain samples from a joint proposal distribution in an extended space, with two of its marginals playing the role of proposals used to estimate each integral. Importantly, the framework allows us to induce and control a dependency between both estimators. We propose a construction of the joint proposal that decomposes in two (multivariate) marginals and a coupling. This leads to a two-stage framework suitable to be integrated with existing or new AIS and/or variational inference (VI) algorithms. The marginals are adapted in the first stage, while the coupling can be chosen and adapted in the second stage. We show in several realistic examples the benefits of the proposed methodology, including an application to Bayesian prediction with misspecified models.



Keywords:
Importance sampling, Monte Carlo, Bayesian inference, coupling

Contributions.

•

We show that the asymptotic variance of the
SNIS
estimator admits an interpretable decomposition with three terms by exploiting a perspective that views the estimator as implicitly estimating a ratio of two integrals. The third term, which has received less attention, can be exploited for variance reduction according to the dependence between the numerator and denominator estimators.

•

We exploit the decomposition above to develop a generalization of the
SNIS
estimator that uses a joint distribution in an extended space to parameterize the dependence between the numerator and denominator estimators. The formulation puts under a unifying framework and considerably generalizes works using two proposals for ratio estimation using
IS
, which either (i) did not introduce dependence, (ii) were restricted to certain parametric families of marginals, or (iii) used restricted types of dependence.

•

We develop a construction of the joint distribution by decomposing it into two multivariate marginals, which play the role of interpreted proposals, and a coupling, defined in this paper as a certain generalization of the notion of copula
[
19
]
. The generality of our approach is supported by the theory of vector copulas
[
33
]
. We open up a new framework to design proposal distributions in IS, where adaptation can be carried out both for marginals and for the coupling.

•

We demonstrate the usefulness of our framework on synthetic examples where the asymptotic variance is closed form and in the estimation of the posterior predictive of Bayesian models when outliers or misspecification arise at test time.

2.1
Unnormalized and self-normalized importance sampling

We are interested in the general problem of approximating an expectation w.r.t. a
PDF

p({\boldsymbol{x}})
on
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}},d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathbb{N}
of a measurable, possibly unbounded test function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}d{\boldsymbol{x}}<\infty
. We denote the value of the expectation as
\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, that is

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{p}[f({\boldsymbol{x}})]=\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})p({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(1)

The integral can be approximated by the
MC
estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\text{MC}}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}),\qquad\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}p({\boldsymbol{x}}),

(2)

which is unbiased, consistent, asymptotically normal, and asymptotic confidence intervals can be constructed easily. We evaluate performance by the
MSE
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{p}[(\mu-\widehat{\mu}_{\text{MC}}^{N})^{2}]
, as it is standard in the
MC
literature
[
63
]
. Next, we review the unnormalized
IS
estimator, which, importantly, can only be used when the normalizing constant of
p
is known.

Unnormalized Importance Sampling.
Importance sampling generalizes the principle of
MC
integration, allowing us to sample from an arbitrary
PDF

q({\boldsymbol{x}})
, called proposal. The
unnormalized importance sampling (UIS)
estimator can be seen as a generalization of the
MC
estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{MC}}^{N}
, and is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\underbrace{\frac{p({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}}_{w^{(n)}}f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}),\qquad\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}q({\boldsymbol{x}}),

(3)

where
w^{(n)}
are known as importance weights (or ratios). The
MC
estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{MC}}^{N}
is a particular case with
q({\boldsymbol{x}})=p({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
The
MSE
of the
UIS
estimator takes an interpretable form, as

\displaystyle\text{MSE}_{q}(\mu,\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[(\mu-\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N})^{2}]=\mathbb{V}_{q}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N}]=\frac{\mu^{2}}{N}\chi^{2}\left(\mu^{-1}p({\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid q({\boldsymbol{x}})\right),

(4)

where
\chi^{2}(p_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})||p_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}))=\int p_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}/p_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}-1
denotes the Pearson chi-squared divergence
[
76
]
.
An immediate consequence of
Eq.

4
is that the
UIS
estimator improves over the
MC
estimator, i.e.,
\text{MSE}(\mu,\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N})<\text{MSE}(\mu,\widehat{\mu}_{\text{MC}}^{N})
exactly when
\chi^{2}\left(\mu^{-1}p({\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid q({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)<\chi^{2}\left(\mu^{-1}p({\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid p({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)
. For example, when
p({\boldsymbol{x}})
is a Bayesian posterior, this implies that sampling from the true posterior does not minimize MSE of posterior estimates. The relationship between the
MSE
of the estimator and a statistical divergence involving the proposal
q
is recognized in the
IS
literature as a useful guide for the adaptation of proposals
[
61
,
3
,
43
]
with clear connections to
variational inference (VI)
. A reformulation of
Eq.

4
as an optimization problem over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})
leads to a natural definition of optimal proposal.

Definition 1

(Optimal
UIS
proposal)
.

The optimal
UIS
proposal
PDF
is defined as the solution to the following optimization problem in the infinite-dimensional space of
PDFs
,

q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})}\operatorname{MSE}_{q}(\mu,\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N})=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})}\chi^{2}\left(\mu^{-1}p({\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid q({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)=\mu^{-1}p({\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}}).

While
\operatorname{MSE}_{q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}}(\mu,\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N})=0
, we can never use
q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}
, because it requires to use
\mu
, which is unknown by definition. The most popular
IS
algorithms choose a parametric family of
PDFs

\mathcal{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{q({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})
indexed by parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
for one or several proposals and adapt them iteratively with
AIS
. See
[
7
]
and
[
63
, Chapter 10]
for thorough overviews of
AIS
and
[
27
]
for a framework with multiple proposals.

Therefore, a more realistic notion of optimal proposal is as follows.

Definition 2

(Parametric optimal
UIS
proposal)
.

The parametric optimal
UIS
proposal
PDF
for a parametric class of proposals indexed by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{q({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}_{\theta\in\Theta}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})
, is defined as

q^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\bigstar}}_{\text{UIS}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\chi^{2}\left(q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid q({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)\iff\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\bigstar}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\chi^{2}\left(q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid q({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right).

Importantly, if
q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}\notin\mathcal{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, i.e., there is no value of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
for which
q({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta})=q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
, then
\operatorname{MSE}_{q^{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\bigstar}}_{\text{UIS}}}(\mu,\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N})>0\Rightarrow\chi^{2}(\mu^{-1}p({\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}})\mid\mid q({\boldsymbol{x}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\bigstar}))>0
. As we see in
Section

3
, this fact will be an important part of the motivation behind our generalization of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
.

Self-normalized importance sampling.
We are interested in problems where the normalizing constant of
p({\boldsymbol{x}})
is unknown so we cannot use the
UIS
estimator
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N}
. In these cases, the
SNIS
estimator is the standard approach. We now present
SNIS
as it is commonly derived and highlight the implicit assumptions behind its construction. The
SNIS
estimator can be obtained by replacing the
IS
weights in the
UIS
estimator from
Eq.

3
with their normalized counterparts,
\widebar{w}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{w^{(n)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{(i)}},n=1,\dots,N
,
leading to the common expression

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\widebar{w}^{(n)}f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}),\qquad\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}q({\boldsymbol{x}}).

(5)

While
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
is consistent just as
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{UIS}}^{N}
and asymptotically normal
[
38
]
, characterizing its
MSE
is more complicated, as we see next.

Implicit construction behind the
SNIS
estimator.
While the formulation of
Eq.

5
is widespread and clearly explains how to implement the estimator, it hides that
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
is implicitly estimating two integrals and combining their estimates in a ratio. Indeed, by defining the normalizing constant of
p
as
Z_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\int\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}
and so
p({\boldsymbol{x}})=\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}})/Z_{p}
, we see that
\mu
can be equivalently expressed as a ratio of two integrals

\displaystyle\mu=\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})p({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{\int f({\boldsymbol{x}})\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\int\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\frac{I}{Z_{p}},

(6)

where we have denoted the integral in the numerator as
I
. The observation leads to a more informative expression for
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
. By defining two
UIS
estimators constructed with a shared set of samples
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}q({\boldsymbol{x}})
, i.e.,
\widehat{I}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}
and
\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}
,
we can express the
SNIS
estimator as a ratio of the two UIS estimators as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{I}^{N}}{\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}}=\frac{\frac{1}{Z_{p}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}}{\frac{1}{Z_{p}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)})}}.

(7)

Notice that
\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}
is the denominator of
\widebar{w}^{(n)}
, except for the factor
1/N
which cancels in the normalization. While
Eq.

7
is mathematically equivalent to
Eq.

5
, it shows explicitely the ratio of the two
UIS
estimators,
\widehat{I}^{N}
and
\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}
, which share the same set of samples
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. As in any
IS
method, the key challenge lies in selecting a good proposal
q
. Here we take the
MSE
as the main metric of interest instead other approaches that consider the
effective sample size (ESS)
(see
[
31
]
for a discussion). With the
UIS
estimator, it is at least theoretically possible to obtain zero
MSE
as a consequence of
Eq.

4
if
q^{\bigstar}_{\text{UIS}}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Unfortunately, it is not possible to define a similar optimal proposal for the
SNIS
estimator to achieves zero
MSE
, as we see next.

2.2
Analysis of the
MSE
and optimal
SNIS
proposal

We summarize the difficulties in studying the
MSE
of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
, which translates into the impossibility of deriving an exact expression for the proposal
q
that minimizes the
MSE
and the need to resort to asymptotic variance. Indeed, since
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
is a ratio, the
MSE
involves terms such as
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{\widehat{I}^{N}}{\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}}\right]
.
In general,
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
is not unbiased since
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{\widehat{I}^{N}}{\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}}\right]\neq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\widehat{I}^{N}]}{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}]}=\mu
. The variance term is similarly problematic to analyse.

In this work, we follow the standard practice of previous works that studied or used
SNIS

[
46
,
63
,
16
,
24
,
78
]
who consider the asymptotic variance, defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{q}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\lim_{N\rightarrow+\infty}N\cdot\mathbb{V}_{q}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}],

(8)

as the proxy for the performance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
.
Hesterberg [46, Chapter 2, Sec. 9]
first showed the analytic expression for the proposal
PDF
that minimizes
\mathbb{V}_{q}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]
, as follows.

Definition 3

(Asymptotically optimal
SNIS
proposal)
.

The asymptotically optimal proposal
PDF
for
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
has an analytic solution and is defined as the minimizer (when it exists) of the asymptotic variance, as

\displaystyle q_{\text{SNIS}}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})}\mathbb{V}_{q}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]=\frac{p({\boldsymbol{x}})|f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\mu|}{\int p({\boldsymbol{x}})|f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\mu|\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}.

(9)

As we shall see in
Section

2.3
,
q_{\text{SNIS}}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})
does
not
lead to zero variance, in sharp contrast with the optimal
UIS
proposal of
Definition

1
.

2.3
Limitations of the
SNIS
estimator

We start our discussion of the fundamental limitations of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
with a motivating example.

Motivating example

(Out-of-sample predictive accuracy of Bayesian high-dimensional models with outliers/misspecification)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
be the parameter of interest of a statistical model (family of
PDFs
)
\{g(y;\boldsymbol{\theta})\}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}
with
y\in\mathbb{R}
, likelihood function
\mathcal{L}(y_{1:S};\boldsymbol{\theta})
and prior
PDF

\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, so that the posterior distribution has
PDF

\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})=Z_{\pi}(y_{1:S})^{-1}\widetilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})
where
\widetilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})=\mathcal{L}(y_{1:S};\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Suppose that the true parameter is
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bigstar}
, i.e.,
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}g(y;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bigstar})
.

In practice, to evaluate the predictive performance, test data is often generated from a different distribution. This can be due to outliers
[
66
]
and/or model misspecification, i.e.,
y_{S+1}\sim g(y;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{wrong}})
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{wrong}}\neq\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bigstar}
,
2
2

2

The following discussion can be easily generalized for multiple test observations
y_{S+1},y_{S+2},\dots
, and multivariate observations.
or
y_{S+1}\sim h(y;\boldsymbol{\gamma})
for a different model
\{h(y;\boldsymbol{\gamma})\}_{\gamma\in\Gamma}
where there is no
\boldsymbol{\gamma}:h(y;\boldsymbol{\gamma})=g(y;\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\bigstar})
. In both cases, for a fixed
y_{S+1}
one evaluates the out-of-sample performance by estimating the posterior predictive integral

\displaystyle\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})}[g(y_{S+1}|\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\int g(y_{S+1}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})d\boldsymbol{\theta}=\frac{\int g(y_{S+1}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\widetilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})d\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\int\widetilde{\pi}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})d\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

(10)

Such a quantity is of interest in many cases, such as in conformal Bayesian computation
[
34
]
. A possible option for estimation is to sample from
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})
(e.g., with
MCMC
),
3
3

3

The above problem was considered also in the related work of
target-aware Bayesian inference (TABI)

[
69
]

but is inefficient
since the variance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{MC}}^{N}

is
\propto~\chi^{2}(\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S+1})||\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S}))
(discussed in
Section

2.1
). This variance is large since outliers lead to large divergences
[
67
]
. We may instead turn to
SNIS
with a proposal
q(\boldsymbol{\theta})

[
83
]
,
whose asymptotic variance is
\propto~~\chi^{2}(\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S+1})||q)+\chi^{2}(\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})||q)-2\cdot\mathcal{C}(q)
(as we show in
Section

3
).
In this context, we want
to choose a
q
that is close to the posteriors
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S+1})
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1:S})
, and that also maximizes the third term,
\mathcal{C}(q)
. For realistic posterior distributions, finding such a
q
is hard. We will propose a general methodology that allows us to construct a proposal that can match the two target posteriors and, at the same time, control the third term, significantly generalizing several previous works (see
Section

3.5
and
Section

3.7
).

Asymptotic variance lower bound.
As noted in
[
65
,
63
]
, the asymptotic variance obtained by
q_{\text{SNIS}}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})
from
Eq.

9
is positive and its expression can be derived analytically. For any
q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\boldsymbol{x}}})
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{q}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]\geq\mathbb{V}_{q_{\text{SNIS}}^{\bigstar}}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]=\left(\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\mid f({\boldsymbol{x}})-\mu\mid\right]\right)^{2}>0

(11)

with equality being possible only if
q=q_{\text{SNIS}}^{\bigstar}
.
Rainforth et al. [69]
, who also proposes an estimator to improve over
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
(see
Section

3.5
), finds empirically that this lower bound leads to high variance. Further,
[
64
]
proved that the bound prevents a certain notion of exponential convergence in adaptive IS. Therefore, while with the
UIS
estimator, we can drive the
MSE
to zero with ever better choices of
q
, the performance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
has fundamental theoretical limitations. In practice, the situation is even more challenging. Indeed, even attempting to simulate with
MCMC
from
q_{\text{SNIS}}^{\bigstar}
would require further research, as the unnormalized target
PDF
involves
\mu
. We now introduce our framework which will allow us to better understand the celebrated
SNIS
estimator, generalize it to reduce variance, and guide the design of new
AIS
algorithms.

3
Generalized SNIS

We now present our framework, which allows us to understand when
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
has large asymptotic variance, and how to reduce it. First, we derive a decomposition of the asymptotic variance that intuitively explains the limitations of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
. In
Section

3.1
we introduce our new framework, where samples are obtained from a joint distribution in an extended space. We propose suitable definitions of optimal joint distributions and a two-stage optimization strategy that allows us to combine any existing
AIS
method to adapt the marginals of the joint. In
Section

3.2
we show how expressive joint distributions, possibly semi-parametric, with marginals of different parametric families can be used. Then, in
Section

3.3
and
Section

3.4
we propose concrete ways to parameterize and optimize the joint. Finally, we develop theoretical guarantees in
Section

3.6
and in
Section

3.5
we unify some previous methods as special cases of our framework, also connecting more broadly with related literature in
Section

3.7
.

To introduce our decomposition of the asymptotic variance, recall from
Section

2.1
that
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
is a ratio of two UIS estimators.

Proposition 1
.

We let the optimal UIS proposals for numerator and denominator of
\mu=I/Z_{p}
, respectively as
q_{1,\text{UIS}}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})=I^{-1}\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot f({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
q_{2,\text{UIS}}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})=p({\boldsymbol{x}})
. The relative asymptotic variance
\mathbb{V}_{q}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]
is given by

\displaystyle\mu^{-2}\cdot\mathbb{V}_{q}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}]=\chi^{2}(q_{1}^{\bigstar}||q)+\chi^{2}(q_{2}^{\bigstar}||q)-2\left(\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{q_{1}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})q_{2}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}})}{q({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}}\right]}^{\mathcal{C}(q)}-1\right),

(12)

where we omit from now on the
UIS
subscripts. We remark that
q_{1}^{\bigstar}
includes the test function
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Eq.

12
provides new intuition for how
q
should be chosen for the problem targeted by
SNIS
. The first two terms force
q
to be close to the two optimal UIS proposals
q_{1}^{\bigstar},q_{2}^{\bigstar}
, where the first one depends on the test function
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
. The third term
\mathcal{C}(q)
has a direct correspondence with
\operatorname{Cov}_{q}[\widehat{I}^{N},\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}]
(as we discuss in
Appendix

F
) and can affect the asymptotic variance significantly. For instance, overall zero asymptotic variance is theoretically possible when
\mathcal{C}(q)
cancels the other two terms (see
Section

3.6
). While a few previous works in ratio estimation do consider the use of two proposals
q_{1},q_{2}

[
42
,
52
,
41
,
66
]
to (implicitly) minimize the first two terms, they do not take
\mathcal{C}(q)
explicitly into consideration in the construction of the estimates. In
Section

3.5
, we review these works showing their estimators and
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
are special cases of our generic framework introduced next. We will show that our new estimator’s variance decomposes like
Eq.

12
and it will allow us to explicitly control a similar term to
\mathcal{C}(q)
to reduce variance.

3.1
Introducing an extended space joint proposal

We construct the new estimator by obtaining samples from a joint distribution in an extended space,
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{2d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}})
with marginals
\mathbb{Q}_{1}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}),\mathbb{Q}_{2}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})
, as

\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{\widehat{I}^{N}}{\widehat{Z}_{p}^{N}}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{(n)})\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{(n)})}{q_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{(n)})}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\widetilde{p}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}_{2})}{q_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}_{2})}},\qquad[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{(n)},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(n)}_{2}]^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}),~n=1,\dots,N.

(13)

The key element of our new methodology is the use of a generic joint distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
, and its construction (which we detail in
Section

3.2
. Marginals of
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
need to admit
PDFs

q_{1}
and
q_{2}
to compute the estimator. Yet, since the joint
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
does not appear in the expression of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}
, we do not require its

to exist. Exploiting this, we will formalize a semi-parametric model for the joint, which allows us to interpret
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{SNIS}}^{N}
more formally as a special case, as well as other recent methods using two proposals (see
Section

3.5
).

Now we turn to the key question of how to design
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
. We extend the asymptotic variance decomposition of
Eq.

12
to
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}
to obtain an objective function for the definition of an optimal joint in terms of asymptotic variance.

Proposition 2

(Asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}
)
.

The asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}
satisfies (proof in
Appendix

G
)

\mu^{-2}\cdot\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}]=\chi^{2}(q_{1}^{\bigstar}||q_{1})+\chi^{2}(q_{2}^{\bigstar}||q_{2})-2\Big(\overbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}}\left[\frac{q_{1}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})q_{2}^{\bigstar}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}{q_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})q_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}\right]}^{\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Q}_{1:2})}-1\Big).

(14)

The decomposition in

allows us the following strategy: we design a joint such that its marginal
q_{1}
minimizes the first term
\chi^{2}(q_{1}^{\bigstar}||q_{1})
, its marginal
q_{2}
minimizes the second term
\chi^{2}(q_{2}^{\bigstar}||q_{2})
, and the third term

is maximized.

Remark 2
.

Choosing typical families used in

3.2
Constructing the joint with couplings and Brenier maps

Our proposed construction of the joint
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
decomposes it into a coupling (defined next) and its marginals. In
Section

3.6
, we develop theoretical support for this construction.

Definition 6

(Coupling)
.

A coupling is a joint probability distribution
\mathbb{C}_{1:2}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{1},d\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
for random vectors
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\in[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}
with the constraint that the marginals of
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
are uniform, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\sim\mathbb{U}_{[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{1}),\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\sim\mathbb{U}_{[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
. We denote its corresponding
PDF
, when it exists, as
c_{1:2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
.

For
d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=1
, the above definition amounts to a copula
[
19
]
. Importantly, the notion of copula is
not
sufficient for our purposes, as it provably cannot model multivariate marginals
[
37
]
.

Sampling from a joint with fixed marginals.
We now discuss how to obtain samples from a joint with given multivariate marginals, a nontrivial task in general
[
48
]
. Assume that
(i)
we know how to sample from a coupling
\mathbb{C}_{1:2}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{1},d\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
and
(ii)
have access to measurable functions
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}:[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}
that transport the uniform distribution to the desired marginals, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}\sharp\mathbb{U}_{[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{i})=\mathbb{Q}_{i}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})
for
i=1,2
. Then, we can obtain a sample
[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}]^{\top}
with the following two steps.

•

Step 1 (Coupling sampling)
: Obtain a sample
[\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}]\sim\mathbb{C}_{1:2}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{1},d\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
.

•

Step 2 (Marginal transformation)
: Apply the maps
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}
to
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
to obtain
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{T}_{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})
, for
i=1,2
. Denote the joint distribution of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}
as
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})
.

The intuition is that we explore the space of
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
by selecting different couplings and fixing the marginals by use of
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}
. We now detail the two steps.
Step 1 (Coupling sampling).
As a possible way to implement step (1), we exploit the use of an easy-to-deal-with
auxiliary
joint distribution
\mathbb{G}_{1:2}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\prime},d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{\prime})
, e.g., a
MVN
or a Student-t distribution, by sampling from it
[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{\prime}]\sim\mathbb{G}_{1:2}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{\prime},d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{\prime})
and transforming the sample into one from a coupling by applying the inverse maps
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}^{-1}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}\rightarrow[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}
to transform the marginals
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}^{-1}\sharp\mathbb{G}_{i}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})=\mathbb{U}_{[0,1]^{d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{i})
for
i=1,2
as before. Concretely, for
d_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=1
,
\boldsymbol{T}_{i}
can be chosen as the inverse
CDFs
(quantile) of the marginals. In general, for

, we propose the use of

introduced in
[

[

3.3
From optimal joint to optimal coupling

We are now equipped to rewrite

\mathbb{C}_{1:2}
, taking advantage of the joint construction just developed. This allows us to make the optimization problem of

Rewriting the objective in

for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}}\left[\frac{q_{1}^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right)q_{2}^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right)}{q_{1}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right)q_{2}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{C}_{1:2}}\left[\omega(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})\right],

where the term inside the expectation is a kind of joint-space importance weight in the hypercube

, given by

\displaystyle\omega(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\omega_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})\cdot\omega_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),~\text{where}~\omega_{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{q_{i}^{\bigstar}(\boldsymbol{T}_{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}))}{q_{i}(\boldsymbol{T}_{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}))},i=1,2.

(21)

Notice that no assumptions about independence of the joint proposal
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
is made in the above derivation, and
Eq.

21
follows by change of variable. Provided a coupling as per
Definition

6
is used, we have now eliminated the challenging constraint of
Eq.

16
.
Using the new objective, we can now straightforwardly provide definitions for asymptotically optimal coupling (and parametric) mirroring

and

. As in our framework, the marginals are fixed, we can equivalently talk about asymptotically optimal joints or asymptotically optimal couplings.

3.4
Selection and adaptation of the coupling

We now propose concrete coupling choices that can be used in our framework. We will also propose a method for coupling adaptation to maximize
Eq.

16
, i.e., explicitly targeting an asymptotically optimal coupling
\mathbb{C}_{1:2}^{\bigstar}
.

To begin with, let us first summarize our overall two-stage framework for combining
AIS
or with coupling adaptation in the following:

•

Inputs to the methods are the marginals
q_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}),q_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})
, their corresponding maps
\boldsymbol{T}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{2}
, their inverses; a coupling
\mathbb{C}_{1:2}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{1},d\boldsymbol{u}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c})
, possibly admitting
PDF

c_{1:2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c})
.

•

Stage (1): marginals adaptation
. Use any
AIS
or
VI
algorithm to obtain marginals.

•

Stage (2): coupling selection/adaptation
. Select a coupling to maximize
Eq.

16
, possibly with an iterative process.

We start by describing coupling adaptation. A natural approach for stage (2) is first-order stochastic optimization, known for its scalability to large-scale problems
[
6
]
. Yet, we stress that in our generic framework, other optimization approaches (e.g., second-order, or sample average approximation (SAA)) could be used.
We estimate the gradient of
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c}}\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c})=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{C}_{1:2}(d\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c})}[\omega(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})].

(22)

We can estimate the gradient
Eq.

22
with either the pathwise or the score function estimator
[
58
]
, depending on the differentiability of
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
. Stochastic gradient ascent (SGA) starts from initial value
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{c}^{(0)}
and iteratively (over
t=1,\dots

3.6
Theoretical properties of the framework

Our framework allows us to understand the maximum reduction in asymptotic variance for estimating
\mu
, explicitly linking it to the quality of the joint
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}
and therefore of the coupling
\mathbb{C}_{1:2}
. While identifying which coupling lead to maximum variance reduction in general is intractable, we identify a few special cases and prove their optimality. We then show that our construction of a joint via couplings and marginals enjoys theoretical guarantees of expressiveness thanks to a recent generalisation of the celebrated Sklar’s theorem for copulas.

Maximum asymptotic variance reduction.
The maximum asymptotic variance reduction is achieved when using the optimal joint distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}^{\bigstar}(d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},d{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})
defined in
Definition

4
or, equivalently, the optimal coupling
\mathbb{C}_{1:2}^{\bigstar}
, leading to the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

The asymptotic variance of
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}
with two given marginals
q_{1},q_{2}
is lower bounded as

\displaystyle\mu^{-2}\cdot\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}]\geq\mu^{-2}\cdot\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}^{\bigstar}}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}]=\left(\sqrt{\chi^{2}(q_{2}^{\bigstar}||q_{2})}-\sqrt{\chi^{2}(q_{1}^{\bigstar}||q_{1})}\right)^{2}.

(25)

The above follows by applying the Cauchy-Swartz inequality to the three terms in
Eq.

19
(see
Appendix

G
for the proof). The joint, which appears in
\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1:2}}^{\infty}[\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}]
, has disappeared in the lower bound, which only depends on the marginals. An interesting implication of
Eq.

25
is that it is possible for
\widehat{\mu}_{\text{GenSNIS}}^{N}
to have low variance even if both marginals are far from their respective
q_{1}^{\bigstar},q_{2}^{\bigstar}
, as long as the divergences
\chi^{2}(q_{1}^{\bigstar}||q_{1})
and
\chi^{2}(q_{2}^{\bigstar}||q_{2})
obtain a similar value.

Proposition 4
.

Let the marginals be product

We now consider a Bayesian linear regression model where the asymptotic variance of

Denote with

\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{X},y_{1:N})=\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{N},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{N})

\mu=\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{X},y_{1:N})}[g(y_{N+1}|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N+1},\boldsymbol{X},y_{1:N})]

5
Conclusions

We have proposed a methodological framework to understand variance reduction for estimating expectations that involve intractable normalizing constants, exploiting the view of the problem as estimating a ratio of integrals. Within our framework, samples are obtained from a generic joint distribution in an extended space, while two of its (multivariate) marginals are used to construct a ratio of two estimators. Our methodology allows one to take the output of any two adaptive IS (or
VI
) algorithms and further reduce estimation variance by coupling them through a joint distribution. We have shown how the widely used
SNIS
estimator and several other ratio estimators can be viewed as special cases that make an implicit choice of this joint. The framework highlights the limitations of existing approaches, allows us to develop new methodology, for which we provide concrete examples here, and paves the way for further variance reduction techniques.
