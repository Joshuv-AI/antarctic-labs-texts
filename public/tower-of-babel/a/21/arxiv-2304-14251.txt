Variational Bayes Made Easy
Mohammad Emtiyaz Khan emtiyaz.khan@riken.jp

Abstract
Variational Bayes is a popular method for approximate inference but its derivation can be cumbersome. To simplify the process, we give a 3-step recipe to identify the posterior form by explicitly looking for linearity with respect to expectations of well-known distributions. We can then directly write the update by simply “reading-off” the terms in front of those expectations. The recipe makes the derivation easier, faster, shorter, and more general.

Introduction

Since its introduction in the early 90s
[
Hinton and Van Camp 1993
,
Saul et al. 1996
,
Jaakkola and Jordan 1996
]
, variational Bayes (VB) has become a prominent method for approximate inference but, despite significant progress in probabilistic programming, deriving VB updates remains cumbersome for many. The main source of difficulty is the need to derive closed-form expressions for the integrals. For example, consider observed data
\mathbf{y}
and latent vector
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{1},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{2},\ldots,\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{K})
for a model
p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})
,
and assume that we seek a mean-field posterior approximation:

p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})\approx q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})=\prod_{i}q_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})
.
Then, a stationary point
q^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})
of the evidence lower-bound (ELBO) satisfies the following
[
Bishop 2006
, Eq. 10.9]
,

\displaystyle\log q_{i}^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\backslash i}}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})\right]+\text{const.},

(1)

where the integral in the right hand side marginalizes out the rest of the variables (denoted by
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i}
) using the marginal
q^{*}_{\backslash i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i})
; see a proof in
Appendix

A
.

For conjugate-exponential (CE) family models, the integrals have closed-form expressions, but deriving them can still be cumbersome. It is not uncommon to find papers where these derivations take many pages of work.
With such long derivations, it is easy to lose the main intuition and elegance of the updates. Difficulty increases when non-conjugate factors are also present. For such cases, despite using automatic differentiation, we still need to make several difficult choices: Which optimizer to choose and are they compatible with the conjugate updating? How to set the learning rate so that overall updates converge? And, when using coordinate-wise updates, in what
sequence should we update the variables? Here, we present a recipe to derive the updates that simplify these difficulties.

Our recipe consists of 3-steps based on an alternate way to express
Eq.

1
. We assume an exponential-family form for

q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}_{i}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})\propto\exp\left(\langle\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}_{i}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}}_{i}),\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}_{i}\rangle\right)

with natural parameter
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}
, sufficient statistics
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}_{i}(\cdot)
, and a constant base-measure. We can then write
Eq.

1
in a form shown below that uses the pair
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i})
where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}_{i}}}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})]
is the expectation parameter:

\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}^{*}=\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}}\left.\mathbb{E}_{q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}}}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})\right]\right|_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}^{*}}.

(2)

Here, we marginalize with respect to
q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})
where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}=(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{1},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{2},\ldots,\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{K})
and denote by
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}^{*},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}^{*})
the natural and expectation parameter pair for the optimal
q_{i}^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})
.
A proof is given in
Appendix

A
by using results from
Khan and Lin 2017
, along with an extension for non-constant base-measures. The advantage of this expression is that we do not have to think about the distributional forms of
q_{i}
or any integrals. Rather, we can simply look up the definition of
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i})
; see
Table

1
for a list.

We give an example below. For notational ease, we will write
\mathbb{E}_{q_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}}}[\cdot]
as
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\cdot]
.

1
.

(A simple mixture model)
We consider two components
p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})
and
p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})
for an observation
\mathbf{y}
where mixture indicator
z\in\{0,1\}
is sampled from a Bernoulli prior,

\displaystyle p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}|z)=p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})^{z}p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})^{1-z},\text{ where }p(z)=\pi_{0}^{z}(1-\pi_{0})^{1-z}.

The posterior over
z
can be obtained by using Bayes’ rule,

p(z=1|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})=\frac{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}})}{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}})+(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}})}
, but let us suppose that we do not know the posterior and we want to recover it from
Eq.

2
.

Denoting the posterior by
q(z)
, we can figure out its form by expanding

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},z)]&=\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}|z)+\log p(z)]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[z\log p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})+(1-z)\log p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})+z\log\pi_{0}+(1-z)\log(1-\pi_{0})\right]\\
&=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}(z)}_{=\mu}\underbrace{\log\frac{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}{(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}}_{\text{Coeff. in front of $\mu$}}+\underbrace{\log[(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})]}_{\text{constant}}.\end{split}

(3)

The last line suggests to choose
q(z)
with expectation parameter
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{q}(z)
. From
Table

1
, we find Bernoulli distribution to have this expectation parameter, therefore we set
q(z)\propto\pi_{1}^{z}(1-\pi_{1}^{1-z})
where
\pi_{1}>0
is the probability of
z=1
under
q
.

Having figured out the form of
q
, we can now use
Eq.

2
to find its parameter. For this we first look up from
Table

1
the natural parameter:
\lambda=\log\frac{\pi_{1}}{1-\pi_{1}}
and set it according to
Eq.

2
.
It is clear from
Eq.

3
that
\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},z)\right]
is linear in
\mu
, therefore the gradient
\nabla_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},z)\right]
is simply the coefficient in front of
\mu
. This gives us

\lambda^{*}=\log\frac{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}{(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}\quad\implies\pi_{1}^{*}=\frac{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})+(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}.

(4)

where in the second equality we rewrite the update in terms of
\pi_{1}
.
This recovers the posterior
p(z=1|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})
obtained with the Bayes’ rule.

The procedure is an instance of our 3-step recipe shown below where we identify the posterior form by explicitly looking for linearity with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and use it to update
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
:

1.

Identify all
q_{i}
at once, by looking for linearity of
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})]
w.r.t. some
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
.

2.

Compute
\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})]
(hint: for CE, just read-off the coefficient in front of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
).

3.

Update
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}\leftarrow(1-\rho_{i})\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}+\rho_{i}\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})]
with a learning rate
\rho_{i}
(often set to 1).

For most cases, there is no need to derive any integrals: we just expand
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})]
as a multi-linear function of all
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
at once and look-up the rest from a table (similar to
Table

1
).
We can then directly write the update by simply “reading-off” the coefficient in front of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
, which is now a function of the
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{\backslash i}
and observation
\mathbf{y}
.

The steps differ fundamentally from the standard way of deriving VB where each
q_{i}
is identified
separately
. Such derivations can be very long and tedious because we need to investigate each node separately by looking for a known distributional form for each of them
[
Blei et al. 2017
, App. A]
[
Bishop 2006
, Sec. 10.2]
.
The distributional form is not used anyways because the updates are ultimately written and implemented using the parameters of the distributions.
Our recipe makes the direct use of the
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i})
pair, and leads to an easier, faster, and shorter derivation where all nodes are handled altogether.

The recipe is also more general and covers a wide-variety of cases, including conjugate, non-conjugate, and even deterministic factors.
This is due to Step 3 which uses the Bayesian learning rule (BLR)
[
Khan and Rue 2021
]
that contains many algorithms as special cases. Below, we give additional sub-steps for Step 3 to derive many variants:

(3a)

Derive Bayes-rule by using
\rho_{i}=1
; rewrite it in your parameter of choice (see
1
).

(3b)

Derive Coordinate Ascent VI (CAVI) and variational message passing (VMP) by setting
\rho_{i}=1
and doing a coordinate-wise update (see
2
and
3
).

(3c)

Derive Stochastic VI (SVI) by setting
\rho_{i}=1
for the local variables and updating the global variable after a local update on any node
i
(see
4
).

(3d)

Derive updates of deterministic nodes (and Laplace’s approximation) by invoking the delta method to approximate the expectation (see
5
).

(3e)

In presence of the non-conjugate terms, just use the gradient and simplify it using reparameterization trick whenever feasible
[
Khan and Rue 2021
]
(see
6
).

Another benefit is that, Steps 3a-3d do not only not require any integrals but we do not even need any derivatives either; due to linearity we just need the coefficients in front. With Step 3d,
even MAP estimates can be derived without any derivatives
.
Moreover, there is no need to use different optimizers for conjugate and non-conjugate parts, and the learning rate and update schedule need not be set carefully because the
update converges under fairly general conditions
[
Khan et al. 2016
]
. In contrast, coordinate-wise update may not always converge and require the solution to be unique for each coordinate
[
Paquet 2014
]
.

Examples

Let us see another example with a slightly more complex model where a mean-field approximation is required. We will see that, unlike standard derivation where each factor requires a separate expansion of the log-joint, we can identify both distributions simultaneously. Another advantage is that we do not have to start with a mean-field structure, rather we can figure this out by simply looking for linearity.

2
.

(A mixture-model with two levels)

We will add an additional latent variable:
\pi_{0}\rightarrow z_{i}\rightarrow\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}
where we have multiple
vector
observations
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}
, and two sets of latent variables:
z_{i}
and
\pi_{0}
. We assume the following model with a beta prior over
\pi_{0}
,

\begin{split}p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{Y}$}$}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})&=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})^{z_{i}}p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})^{1-z_{i}},\,\,p(z_{i}|\pi_{0})=\pi_{0}^{z_{i}}(1-\pi_{0})^{1-z_{i}},\,\,p(\pi_{0})\propto\pi_{0}^{\alpha_{0}-1}(1-\pi_{0})^{\beta_{0}-1}\end{split}

Our goal is to estimate
p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$},\pi_{0}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{Y}$}$})
but the model is not a conjugate one, therefore we have to use a mean-field approximation for tractability.

In the
1^{\text{st}}
step we can figure out
q
for both
z_{i}
and
\pi_{0}
at once by using linearity in the expected log-joint; there is no need to decide the factorization yet. We show this below where the second-line is obtained by using
Eq.

3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{Y}$}$}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})p(\pi_{0})]=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}|z_{i})+\log p(z_{i})\right)+\log p(\pi_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(z_{i}\log\frac{\pi_{0}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})}{(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})}+\log[(1-\pi_{0})p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})]\right)+\log\left(\pi_{0}^{(\alpha_{0}-1)}(1-\pi_{0})^{(\beta_{0}-1)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}(z_{i})}_{=\mu_{i}}\Bigg(\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\log\pi_{0}\\
\log(1-\pi_{0})\end{array}\right]}}_{=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{0}}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\log p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\\
\log p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\end{array}\right]\Bigg)^{\top}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}+1\\
-1\end{array}\right]+\sum_{i}\log p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\log\pi_{0}\\
\log(1-\pi_{0})\end{array}\right]}}_{=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{0}}}^{\top}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{0}-1\\
N+\beta_{0}-1\end{array}\right],

where in the last step we assumed that
q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$},\pi_{0})=\prod_{i}q(z_{i})q(\pi_{0})
to get linearity with respect to both
\mu_{1}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{0}
. Using
Table

1
, we have
q(z_{i})
as Bernoulli and
q(\pi_{0})
as Beta.

After this, the
2^{\text{nd}}
step is straightforward to simply read the coefficient in front of
\mu_{i}
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{0}
respectively, and use them in the
3^{\text{rd}}
step with
\rho_{i}=1
. This is shown below in both natural parameters
and posterior parameters of Bernoulli (denoted by
\pi_{i,1}
) and Beta distributions (denoted by
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
) obtained by using
Table

1
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\Bigg(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{0}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\log p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\\
\log p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\end{array}\right]\Bigg)^{\top}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\right]\implies\pi_{i,1}\leftarrow\frac{e^{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log\pi_{0}]}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}{e^{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log\pi_{0}]}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})+e^{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log(1-\pi_{0})]}p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})}

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{0}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}+1\\
-1\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{0}-1\\
N+\beta_{0}-1\end{array}\right]\implies\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\beta_{1}\end{array}\right]\leftarrow\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{0}+\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{q}(z_{i})\\
\beta_{0}+\sum_{i}(1-\mathbb{E}_{q}(z_{i}))\end{array}\right]

The recipe identifies the distributions for all nodes at once by looking for linearity; see the last line in
2
. In addition, it reuses the already-known integrals by looking-up the required expectations. For instance, in the example above, we need to find

\mathbb{E}_{q}(z_{i})
,
\mathbb{E}_{q}(\log\pi_{0})
, and
\mathbb{E}_{q}(\log(1-\pi_{0}))
, whose expressions can be found in Wikipedia pages for
Bernoulli
and
Beta
distributions.

We are now ready to discuss the most-commonly used example for VB, which is the Gaussian mixture model. Unlike the standard derivation covered in
[
Bishop 2006
]
, we can derive all the updates with just a single expansion of the expected log-joint. The example clearly demonstrates the usefulness of the recipe in simplifying the derivation.

3
.

(Gaussian mixture model)
The model uses a Gaussian likelihood for the two components and a Gaussian-Wishart prior on their means and covariances,

\displaystyle p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}^{-1}),\quad\quad

\displaystyle p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a}|0,(\gamma_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a})^{-1}\right)\mathcal{W}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{W}$}$}_{0},\nu_{0})

\displaystyle p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})

\displaystyle=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{b},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{b}^{-1}),\quad\quad

\displaystyle p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{b},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{b})=\mbox{${\cal N}$}\left(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{b}|0,(\gamma_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{b})^{-1}\right)\mathcal{W}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{b}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{W}$}$}_{0},\nu_{0})

for scalars
\gamma_{0}>0
and
\nu_{0}>D-1
, and positive-definite matrix
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{W}$}$}_{0}
. For this, we simply need to make two changes in
2
. First, we replace
\log p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})
and
\log p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})
by their expected values. We show one of them below in terms of the required expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})]=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}|]-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a})^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a})\right]+c

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}|]}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{p_{a},1}}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{Tr}\big(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}^{\top}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}]}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{p_{a},2}}\big)+\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i}^{\top}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a}]}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{p_{a},3}}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a}^{\top}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a}]}_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{p_{a},4}}+c.

The term is linear in terms of the expectation-parameter
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{p_{a},1:4}
which corresponds to the Gaussian-Wishart distribution (
Table

1
); see the Wikipedia pages for their expressions.
Second, we need to add the contribution of the prior, which is also linear in
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{p_{a},1:4}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a})]=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\nu_{0}-D)\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}|]-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{Tr}\big(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{W}$}$}_{0}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a}]\big)-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a}^{\top}(\gamma_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{S}$}$}_{a})\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{a}]+c

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\nu_{0}-D)\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{p_{a},1}-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mbox{Tr}\big(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{W}$}$}_{0}^{-1}\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{p_{a},3}\big)-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\gamma_{0}\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{p_{a},4}+c.

Then, we expand the expected log-joint by using model definition in the first line, then by plugging
2
to get the second line, and do rearrangement afterward to get linearity,

Suppose that, in
1

,

p(\pi_{0})\propto\frac{1}{\pi_{0}(1-\pi_{0})}\exp\left[-\frac{(\text{logit}(\pi_{0})-m)^{2}}{2}\right].

Still, the derivation proceeds in the same way by expanding
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{Y}$}$}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})p(\pi_{0})]=

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}(z_{i})}_{=\mu_{i}}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\log\pi_{0}\\
\log(1-\pi_{0})\end{array}\right]}^{\top}}_{=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{0}^{\top}}\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\\
p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\end{array}\right]}_{\text{Coeff. in front}}\Bigg)+N\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log(1-\pi_{0})]}_{\mu_{0}^{(2)}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\pi_{0})]}_{\textrm{non-conjugate term}}.

and combine the last two terms by collecting the linear terms together,

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\log\pi_{0}\\
\log(1-\pi_{0})\end{array}\right]}^{\top}}_{=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{0}^{\top}}\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-1\\
N-1\end{array}\right]}_{\text{Coeff. in front}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[-\frac{(\text{logit}(\pi_{0})-m)^{2}}{2}\right]}_{\textrm{non-conjugate term}}.

We will now just have to add the last term to the derivative of
\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{0}}
, and rewrite the update. The form of the update does not change much, rather the non-conjugate prior can simply be written as a “pseudo” conjugate Beta prior,

\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{0}\leftarrow(1-\rho_{0})\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{0}+\rho_{0}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\alpha}_{0}-1\\
N+\hat{\beta}_{0}-1\end{array}\right]+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}p_{a}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\\
p_{b}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\end{array}\right]\right).

where

\hat{\alpha}_{0}=\nabla_{\mu_{0}^{(1)}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\text{logit}(\pi_{0})-m)^{2}\right]
and

\hat{\beta}_{0}=\nabla_{\mu_{0}^{(2)}}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[-\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}(\text{logit}(\pi_{0})-m)^{2}\right]
are the parameters of the pseudo beta prior. The gradients automatically convert the non-conjugate prior form into a conjugate one, which is due to
Khan and Lin 2017
.

The elegance of the update above is not a coincidence, rather it is by design.
Khan and Lin 2017
linearize the whole VB objective with respect to all
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. This linearizes the non-conjugate terms while preserving the linearity of the conjugate terms; see
[
Khan and Lin 2017
, Lemma 1]
. This is a remarkable property of such linearizations. The BLR builds on this but it is the linearization which enables generalization to all sorts of algorithms. This insight is under-appreciated
so far, but we do hope that the examples shown in this paper motivates many readers to exploit such (Bayesian) linearization procedure.

Future Work

One drawback of the recipe is that it depends heavily on the
(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$})
parameterization. There are several works that extend beyond this, for example, to mixture distributions
[
Lin et al. 2019
]
, structured Gaussian distributions
[
Lin et al. 2021
]
, and also to transformation families
[
Kıral et al. 2023
]
. Such approaches have shown to be useful in deriving existing algorithms, as well as designing new ones
[
Khan and Rue 2021
]
. We believe that such extensions
exist for generic distributions and that it is possible to derive all sorts of updates in the same fashion as described in this paper.

q_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})
, that is, if there exists
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{i}(\cdot)
such that the log-conditional can be expressed in terms of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})
as follows,

\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$})=\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})\,,\,\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\rangle+\text{const.},

(58)

then
\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})]=\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}\,,\,\mathbb{E}_{q_{\backslash i}}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\right]\rangle+\text{const.}
, which is linear with respect to
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
.

This also justifies Step 2 because the gradient with respect to
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
becomes

\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})]=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\backslash i}}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\right]

(59)

which is simply the term in front of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
.

Equivalence:
Due to
Eq.

2
, we can show that, at a fixed point, the optimal natural parameter is equal to the coefficient in front of
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{i}
,

\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}^{*}=\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}}\left.\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})\right]\right|_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}^{*}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\backslash i}^{*}}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{\backslash i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\right],

(60)

Using this, we can show that
Eq.

2
leads to
Eq.

1
by using the definition of
q_{i}^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})
,

\begin{split}\log q_{i}^{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})&=\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i}),\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}^{*}\rangle+c,\\
&=\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i}),\mathbb{E}_{q_{\backslash i}^{*}}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{/i},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$}_{i})\right]\rangle+c\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\backslash i}^{*}}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})\right]+c^{\prime},\end{split}

(61)

where the second and third line follow by using
Eq.

60
and
Eq.

58
respectively (
c
and
c^{\prime}
are constants that do not depend on
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i}
). The derivation is reversible and we can also derive
Eq.

2
from
Eq.

1
, making the two statements equivalent whenever the posterior is a conjugate-exponential family.

Extension for non-constant base-measures:
If the exponential-family includes a base-measure
h_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})
as shown below,

q_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})\propto h_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})\exp\left(\langle\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{T}$}$}}_{i}(\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}}_{i}),\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}}_{i}\rangle\right),

then the condition in
Eq.

2
is modified to the following,

\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\lambda}$}$}_{i}^{*}=\nabla_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}}\left.\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{y}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})-\log h_{i}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}_{i})\right]\right|_{\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}=\text{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}}_{i}^{*}},

(62)

The proof is exactly the same so we omit it.
