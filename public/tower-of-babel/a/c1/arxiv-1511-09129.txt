Abstract
. Linear spectral transformations of orthogonal polynomials in the real line, and in particular Geronimus transformations, are extended to orthogonal polynomials depending on several real variables. Multivariate Christoffel–Geronimus–Uvarov formulæ for the perturbed orthogonal polynomials and their quasi-tau matrices are found for each perturbation of the original linear functional. These expressions are given in terms of quasi-determinants of bordered truncated block matrices and the 1D Christoffel–Geronimus–Uvarov formulæ in terms of quotient of determinants of combinations of the original orthogonal polynomials and their Cauchy transforms, are recovered. A new multispectral Toda hierarchy of nonlinear partial differential equations, for which the multivariate orthogonal polynomials are reductions, is proposed. This new integrable hierachy is associated with non-standard multivariate biorthogonality. Wave and Baker functions, linear equations, Lax and Zakharov–Shabat equations, KP type equations, appropriate reductions, Darboux/linear spectral transformations, and bilinear equations involving linear spectral transformations are presented. Finally, the paper includes an Appendix devoted to multivariate Uvarov transformations. Particular attention is paid to 0D-Uvarov perturbations and also to the 1D-Uvarov perturbations, which require of the theory of Fredholm integral equations.

1.2.
Results and layout of the paper

First, we complete this introduction with some background material from
[
13
]
. Then, in §
2
we discuss the Geronimus type transformation for multivariate orthogonal polynomials. We introduce the resolvents and find the connection formulæ. The multivariate extension of the Geronimus determinantal formula depends on the introduction of a semi-infinite matrix

However, no such connection exists in this more general scenario, and the multivariate Cauchy transform of the MVOPR does not provide the necessary aid for finding the multivariate formula for Geronimus transformations (aid which is provided by the semi-infinite matrix
R
). Then, we end the section by discussing the 1D reduction and recovering the Geronimus results
[
44
]
. A similar approach can be found in §
3
for the linear spectral for which we present a multivariate quasi-determinantal Christoffel–Geronimus–Uvarov formula
[
81
]
, and we give a brief discussion of the existence of poised sets.

In
[
13
]
we considered semi-infinite matrices having the adequate symmetries, that we call multi-Hankel, so that a multivariate moment functional or moment semi-infinite matrix appeared. In section
4
we are ready to abandon this more comfortable MVOPR situation and explore different scenarios by assuming that

could be arbitrary, as far it is Gaussian factorizable. We are dealing with perturbations of non-standard multivariate biorthogonality. We first give the general setting for this integrable hierarchy, that we have named multi-spectral Toda lattice hierarchy, finding the corresponding Lax and Zakharov–Shabat equations and the role played by the Baker and adjoint Baker functions. Some reductions, like the multi-Hankel that leads to dynamic MVOPR, and extensions of it are presented. We also consider the action of the discussed multivariate linear spectral transformations and find the Christoffel–Geronimus–Uvarov formula in this broader scenario. To end the paper, we find generalized bilinear equations that involve linear spectral transformations.

We have also included an appendix to discuss multivariate Uvarov transformations. For the 0D-Uvarov transformation, which can be considered an immediate extension of the results of Uvarov

1.3.
Preliminary material

Following
[
14
]
, a brief account of complex multivariate orthogonal polynomials in a
D
-dimensional real space (MVOPR) is given. Cholesky factorization of a semi-infinite moment matrix will be keystone to built such objects. Consider
D
independent real variables
\boldsymbol{x}=\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{D}\right)^{\top}\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
, and the corresponding ring of complex multivariate polynomials
\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]\equiv\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{D}]

Given a multi-index
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{D})^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}
of non-negative integers write
\boldsymbol{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{D}^{\alpha_{D}}
and say that the length of

is
|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\coloneq\sum_{a=1}^{D}\alpha_{a}
. This length induces a total ordering of monomials:
\boldsymbol{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}<\boldsymbol{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\prime}|
. For each non-negative integer
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
introduce the set

\displaystyle[k]\coloneq\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}:|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|=k\},

built up with those vectors in the lattice
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}
with a given length
k
.
The graded lexicographic order for
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\in[k]
is

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}>{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}\Leftrightarrow\exists p\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\text{ with }p<D\text{ such that }\alpha_{1,1}=\alpha_{2,1},\dots,\alpha_{1,p}=\alpha_{2,p}\text{ and }\alpha_{1,p+1}<\alpha_{2,p+1},

and if
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(k)}\in[k]
and
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}\in[l]
, with
k<l
then
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(k)}<{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(l)}
.
Given the set of integer vectors of length
k
use the lexicographic order and write

\displaystyle[k]=\big\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1}^{(k)},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2}^{(k)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(k)}_{|[k]|}\big\}\text{ with }{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{a}^{(k)}>{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{a+1}^{(k)}.

Here

is the cardinality of the set
[k]
, i.e., the number of elements in the set.
This is the dimension of the linear space of homogenous multivariate polynomials of total degree

Either counting weak compositions or multisets one obtains the multi-choose number,

The dimension of the linear space

\displaystyle N_{k}=1+|[2]|+\dots+|[k]|=\binom{D+k}{D}.

The dual space of the symmetric tensor powers is isomorphic to the set of symmetric multilinear functionals on

Each multi-index

2.
Geronimus transformations

In this section a Geronimus transformation for MVOPR is discussed, if we understand the Christoffel transformation as the perturbation by the multiplication by a polynomial, its right inverse, the Geronimus transformation, might be thought as the perturbation obtained by dividing by a polynomial. We also need a discrete part concentrated at the zeroes of the polynomial denominator, now an algebraic hypersuface.

2.1.
Geronimus transformations in the multivariate scenario

Given a polynomial
\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
we may consider its principal ideal

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\coloneq\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})P(\boldsymbol{x}):P(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]\big\}.

This ideal is closely related to the algebraic hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}^{D}
of its zero set

\displaystyle Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2}):=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{D}:P(\boldsymbol{x})=0\}.

The kernel of a linear functional
v\in\big(\mathbb{R}[\boldsymbol{x}]\big)^{\prime}
is defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(v)\coloneq\big\{P(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]:\langle v,P(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle=0\big\}.

We know that
\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
acts on
(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}])^{\prime}
by left multiplication, but for the transformations we are dealing with we also need the notion of division by polynomials.

Definition 2.1
.

Given fastly decreasing generalized function
u\in\mathcal{O}_{c}^{\prime}
and a polynomial
\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
, such that
Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\cap\operatorname{supp}(u)=\varnothing
,
the set of all the linear functionals
\check{u}\in\big(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]\big)^{\prime}
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}\check{u}

\displaystyle=u,

is called its Geronimus transformation.

Notice that there is not a unique linear functional
\check{u}\in(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}])^{\prime}
satisfying such a requirement. Indeed, suppose that a solution is found and denote it by
\frac{u}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}
, then all possible perturbations
\check{u}
verifying (
2.1
) will have the form

(2.2)

\displaystyle\check{u}=\frac{u}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}+v,

where the linear functional
v\in(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}])^{\prime}
is such that
(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(v)
; i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}v=0.

For example, given a positive Borel measure
\operatorname{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x})
and the associated linear functional

\displaystyle\langle u,P(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle=\int P(\boldsymbol{x})\operatorname{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x}),

we can choose
\frac{u}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}\in(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}])^{\prime}
as the following linear functional

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{u}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}},P(\boldsymbol{x})\Big\rangle=\int P(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\operatorname{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x})}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})},

which makes sense if
Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\cap\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{d}\mu)=\varnothing
.
Any multivariate polynomial has a unique, up to constants, factorization in terms of prime polynomials

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\mathcal{Q}_{2,i}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{d_{i}},

where
\mathcal{Q}_{2,i}
are prime polynomials for
i\in\{1,\dots D\}
and the multiplicities
\{d_{1},\dots,d_{N}\}
are positive integers such hat
m_{2}=\deg\mathcal{Q}_{2}=d_{1}\deg\mathcal{Q}_{2,1}+\dots+d_{2}\deg\mathcal{Q}_{2,N}
.
Let us consider for each prime factor
\mathcal{Q}_{2,i}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
a set of measures
\big\{\operatorname{d}\xi_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\big\}_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<d_{i}\end{subarray}}
with
\operatorname{supp}\big(\operatorname{d}\xi_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\big)\subseteq Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2,i})
. Then, a linear functional
v
of the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle\langle v,P(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\\
|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<d_{i}\end{subarray}}\int_{Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2,i})}\frac{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}P}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\boldsymbol{x})\operatorname{d}\xi_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},

is such that
(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(v)
.

In the
D=1
context, where up to constants

\mathcal{Q}_{2}(x)=(x-q_{1})^{d_{1}}\cdots(x-q_{N})^{d_{N}}
, with different roots
\{q_{,}\dots,q_{N}\}
, and multiplicitities
\{d_{1},\dots,d_{N}\}
such that

d_{1}+\dots+d_{N}=m_{2}
, the most general form of
v
is, in terms of the Dirac linear functional
\delta
and its derivatives, given by

(2.4)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{d_{i}-1}\zeta_{i}^{(j)}\delta^{(j)}(x-q_{i}),

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that for multiplicities greater than 1 we have linear functionals of higher order and therefore not linked to measures, which are linear functionals of order zero.

From hereon we assume that both linear functionals
u
and
\check{u}
give rise to well defined families of MVOPR, equivalently that all their moment matrix block minors are nonzero
\det G^{[k]}\neq 0
,
\det\check{G}^{[k]}\neq 0
,
\forall k\in\{1,2,\dots\}
.

Proposition 2.1

3.
Linear spectral transformations

Once we have discussed the multivariate Geronimus transformation we are ready to consider the more general linear spectral transform, that might be thought as the multiplication by a rational function, plus an extra contribution living in the zeroes of the polynomial in the denominator. Uvarov perturbations are treated in the Appendix.

Definition 3.1
.

For a given generalized function
u\in\mathcal{O}_{c}^{\prime}
, let us consider two coprime polynomials
\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
, i.e., with no common prime factors, degrees

\deg\mathcal{Q}_{1}=m_{1}
and
\deg\mathcal{Q}_{2}=m_{2},
and such that
Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\cap\operatorname{supp}(u)=\varnothing
. Then, the set of linear functionals
\hat{u}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}\hat{u}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{1}u.

is called a linear spectral transformation.

Again, there is not a unique
\hat{u}
satisfying this condition. In fact, assume we have found such linear functional that we denote as
\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}u
, then all possible perturbations
\hat{u}
verifying (
2.1
) will have the form

\displaystyle\hat{u}=\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}u+v

where, as for the Geronimus transformation, the linear functional
v\in(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}])^{\prime}
is such that
(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(v)
; i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}v=0.

Proposition 3.1
.

A linear spectral transformation
u\mapsto\hat{u}
can be obtained by performing first a Geronimus transformation and then and a Christoffel transformation:

\displaystyle u\mapsto\check{u}\mapsto\hat{u},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}\check{u}

\displaystyle=u,

\displaystyle\hat{u}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{1}\check{u}.

For example, for a given a positive Borel measure
\operatorname{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x})
with associated zero order linear functional

\displaystyle\langle u,P(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle=\int P(\boldsymbol{x})\operatorname{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x}),

we can choose
\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}u\in(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}])^{\prime}
as the following linear functional

\displaystyle\Big\langle\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{1}}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}u,P(\boldsymbol{x})\Big\rangle=\int P(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})}\operatorname{d}\mu(\boldsymbol{x}),

which makes sense if
Z(\mathcal{Q}_{2})\cap\operatorname{supp}(\operatorname{d}\mu)=\varnothing
.

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\hat{G}
is the moment matrix of the perturbed linear functional
\hat{u}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\hat{G}=\hat{G}\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top})=\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})G=G\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}).

Proof.

It is proven as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\big\langle\hat{u},\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big(\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\hat{u},\mathcal{Q}_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big(\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\mathcal{Q}_{2}\hat{u},\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big(\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\mathcal{Q}_{1}u,\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big(\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle

use (
3.1
)

\displaystyle=\big\langle u,\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big(\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\big\langle u,\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big(\chi(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle

∎

|\boldsymbol{\beta}\leq|\deg Q\end{subarray}}P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}G_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}}Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta}},

\displaystyle G_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\big\rangle.

Whenever the sum
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}\end{subarray}}P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}G_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}}Q_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
converges in some sense, the corresponding extension of this bilinear form to the linear space of power series
\mathbb{C}[\![\boldsymbol{x}]\!]
can be considered.

In general, the semi-infinite matrix

has no further structure and, consequently, we do not expect it to be symmetric or to be related to a linear functional, for example. We say that weare dealing with a non standard bilinear form. The bilinear form (
4.1
) induces another bilinear form which is a bilinear map from semi-infinite vectors of polynomials (or power series when possible) into the semi-infinite matrices.

Definition 4.2
.

Given to semi-infinite vectors of polynomials

\displaystyle\big\langle v(\boldsymbol{x}),\big(w(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\big\rangle_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle\coloneq\big\langle v_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{x}),w_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{x})\big\rangle,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}.

Appendix A
Uvarov perturbations

Uvarov considered in §2 of
[
75
]
the addition of a finite number of masses, Dirac deltas, to a given measure in the OPRL situation.
In this appendix we discuss some elements of the multivariate extension of this construction. There is an immediate extension when one considers masses, see
[
27
]
. A bit more involved case is to consider higher multipoles, i.e., derivatives of the Dirac distributions.
In
[
27
]
a Sobolev type modification was considered, see for example equation (2.16) in that paper, but this can not be modeled by a perturbation
\hat{u}=u+v
of a linear functional
u
(or measure in that case,
u=\operatorname{d}\mu
).
All this can be considered as a 0-dimensional additive perturbation. However, more interesting and less trivial extensions are to consider higher dimensional additive perturbations. For example, 1D-Uvarov perturbations, i.e., additive perturbations supported over curves. For the 0D-Uvarov perturbations, as was found in
[
75
]
, one needs to solve a linear system constructed in terms of the non-perturbed Christoffel–Darboux kernel evaluated at the 0D discrete support of the perturbation. We will se that for the 1D scenario the linear system of the 0D case is replaced by a Fredholm integral equation evaluated at the 1D support of the perturbation..

Our approach to the problem is based on a simple relation among perturbed and non perturbed MVOPR which involves the non perturbed Christoffel–Darboux kernel.
Let us consider a generalized function
u\in\big(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]\big)^{\prime}
such that is quasidefinite and consider an additive perturbation of it given by another generalized function
v\in\big(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle\hat{u}=u+v.

Proposition A.1
.

For an additive perturbation we have

(A.1)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})-\left\langle v,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{y})K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{[n]}

\displaystyle=H_{[n]}+\left\langle v,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big(P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\right\rangle.

Proof.

From (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) we deduce

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{u},\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big(P_{[m]}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)^{\top}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle m\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\},

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{u},\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big(P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\Big)^{\top}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{[n]},

and, consequently,

\displaystyle\left\langle u,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big(P_{[m]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle v,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big(P_{[m]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle m\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}.

Thus, in terms of the Christoffel–Darboux kernel, see Definition
1.4

\displaystyle\left\langle u,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right\rangle=-\left\langle v,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right\rangle.

Observe that

is a multivariate polynomial of degree

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})-P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\left\langle u,\big(\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{y})-P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{y})\big)K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle u,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{y})K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle v,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{y})K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\right\rangle.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{[n]}

\displaystyle=\left\langle u,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big(P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\right\rangle+\left\langle v,\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big(P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})\big)^{\top}\right\rangle

∎

A.1.
0D-Uvarov multipolar perturbations. Masses (or charges) and dipoles

Here we discuss the more general additive perturbation with finite discrete support. As we have a finite number of points for the support we say that is a 0 dimensional perturbation.
Let us proceed and consider a set of couples

\displaystyle v_{S}:=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\leq\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}}\frac{(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}!}\xi_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\delta^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}).

Here the sum over multi-indices extend to all those multi-indices below a given one. The Dirac delta distribution and its derivatives are given by

\displaystyle\langle\delta^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),P(\boldsymbol{x})\rangle

. From a physical point of view, the delta functions can be understood as point masses. For higher order derivatives, we have an electromagnetic interpretation, for zero order derivatives we have point charges, and first order derivatives could be understood as dipoles, and in general for

. Then, the charge density is expressed as
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum\limits_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}}\frac{(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}!}\xi_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\delta^{({\boldsymbol{\alpha}})}(\boldsymbol{x})

Equivalently, that is to say

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(S)\boldsymbol{C}

\displaystyle\in\Big(\{P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq n}\Big)^{\perp}=\big\{\tilde{u}\in\big(\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]\big)^{*}:\langle\tilde{u},P_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\rangle=0,\forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{D}:|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq n\big\}.

In the one hand, the orthogonality relations for the MVOPR leads us to conclude that
\Big(\{P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq n}\Big)^{\perp}=\mathbb{C}\big\{P_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{*}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<n}
, with the covectors defined by
\langle P^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},P_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rangle=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
; thus,
\dim\Big(\{P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq n}\Big)^{\perp}=N_{n-1}
.
In the other hand, we notice that
\mathbb{C}\big\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}u\big\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<n}\subset\Big(\{P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq n}\Big)^{\perp}
, but as

\dim\mathbb{C}\big\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}u\big\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<n}=N_{n-1}
we deduce

\Big(\{P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|\geq n}\Big)^{\perp}=\mathbb{C}\big\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}u\big\}_{|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}|<n}
.
Consequently, there exists a non-zero polynomial
Q(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{C}[\boldsymbol{x}]
,
\deg Q\leq n-1
, such that
\mathcal{J}(S)\boldsymbol{C}=Qu
.
Finally, let us notice that
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{q}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}}\in\operatorname{Ker}(\mathcal{J}(S)\boldsymbol{C})
, so that

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{q}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}}Q(\boldsymbol{x})u=0
,
and
u
can not be quasidefinite, in contradiction with the initial assumptions.

Now, as
I_{N_{s}}+\varXi\mathcal{K}_{n-1}(S)
is not singular we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\hat{P}_{[n]}}(S)=\mathcal{J}_{P_{[n]}}(S)\big(I_{N_{s}}+\varXi\mathcal{K}_{n-1}(S)\big)^{-1}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})=P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})-\mathcal{J}_{P_{[n]}}(S)\big(I_{N_{s}}+\varXi\mathcal{K}_{n-1}(S)\big)^{-1}\varXi\big(\mathcal{J}_{K_{n-1}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})}(S)\big)^{\top}

and the result follows.
From (
A.2
) we have

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{[n]}

\displaystyle=H_{[n]}+\mathcal{J}_{\hat{P}_{[n]}}(S)\varXi\big(\mathcal{J}_{P_{[n]}}(S)\big)^{\top}

\displaystyle=H_{[n]}+\mathcal{J}_{P_{[n]}}(S)\big(I_{N_{s}}+\varXi\mathcal{K}_{n-1}(S)\big)^{-1}\varXi\big(\mathcal{J}_{P_{[n]}}(S)\big)^{\top}.

∎

A.2.
1D-Uvarov perturbations and Fredholm integral equations

We have discussed 0-dimensional additive perturbations of
D
-dimensional generalized functions in full generality.
However, we reckon that this is a very limited analysis, as in this multivariate context much more general perturbations do exist, as is illustrated by (
2.3
).
We now discuss a very particular example, adding a 1D massive string. For this aim we assume that we have a parametrized curve, i.e. a smooth map from the interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{D}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}:I\to\mathbb{R}^{D},

as well as a weight function
w:I\to\mathbb{C}
. Then, the linear functional
v
is

\displaystyle\langle v,P\rangle=\int_{I}P(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))w(t)\operatorname{d}t.

Recalling (
A.1
) we can write

(A.3)

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})=P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})-\int_{I}\hat{P}_{[n]}\big(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)\big)K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s),\boldsymbol{x})w(s)\operatorname{d}s.

Now, let us remark one of the basic ideas in the proof of Theorem
A.1
. First, one uses (
A.1
) and then evaluates on the support of the distribution. In that case, we evaluated again at the points where the delta functions and its derivatives where supported. In this case, we should evaluate it again at the curve
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

Definition A.2
.

We introduce some notation

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)

\displaystyle:=\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)),

\displaystyle\pi_{[n]}(t)

\displaystyle:=P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)),

\displaystyle\kappa_{n-1}(t,s)

\displaystyle:=K_{n-1}\big(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t),\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)\big)w(s).

Then, (
A.3
) implies the following integral Fredholm equation

(A.4)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)=\pi_{[n]}(t)-\int_{I}\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(s)\kappa_{n-1}(s,t)\operatorname{d}s.

This integral equation, having as integral kernel
\kappa_{n-1}(t,s)
a separable one, can be solved explicitly. In fact,

Proposition A.2
.

The solution of the separable Fredholm equation (
A.4
) can be expressed as a last quasi-determinant as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)=\Theta_{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}H_{[0]}+\int_{I}\pi_{[0]}(s)\big(\pi_{[0]}(s)\big)^{\top}w(s)\operatorname{d}s&\dots&\int_{I}\pi_{[0]}(s)\big(\pi_{[n-1]}(s)\big)^{\top}w(s)\operatorname{d}s&\pi_{[0]}(t)\\

Proof.

The separability of the kernel means that the kernel
\kappa_{n-1}(t,s)
can be written

\displaystyle\kappa_{n-1}(s,t)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\big(\pi_{[m]}(s)\big)^{\top}\big(H_{[m]}\big)^{-1}\pi_{[m]}(t).

Now, with the notation

(A.5)

\displaystyle C_{[n],[m]}:=\int_{I}\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(s)\big(\pi_{[m]}(s)\big)^{\top}w(s)\operatorname{d}s

we can write the Fredholm equation

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)=\pi_{[n]}(t)-\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}C_{[n],[m]}\big(H_{[m]}\big)^{-1}\pi_{[m]}(t),

that can be introduced in (
A.5
) to get

\displaystyle C_{[n,[m]}

\displaystyle=A_{[n],[m]}-\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}C_{[n],[l]}\big(H_{[l]}\big)^{-1}A_{[l],[m]},

\displaystyle A_{[n],[m]}

\displaystyle:=\int_{I}\pi_{[n]}(s)\big(\pi_{[m]}(s)\big)^{\top}w(s)\operatorname{d}s.

This linear system for the
C
’s can be written

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}C_{[n],[0]},\dots,C_{[n],[n-1]}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{[n],[0]},\dots,_{[n],[n-1]}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}C_{[n],[0]},\dots,C_{[n],[n-1]}\ \end{bmatrix}\big(H^{[n]}\big)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}A_{[0],[0]}&\dots&A_{[0],[n-1]}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
A_{[n-1],[0]}&\dots&A_{[n-1],[n-1]}\end{bmatrix},

and the result follows.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Given the solution
\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)
to the Fredholm equation (
A.4
) we find the perturbed MVOPR and squared norms can be expressed

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=P_{[n]}(\boldsymbol{x})-\int_{I}\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)K_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t),\boldsymbol{x})w(t)\operatorname{d}t,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{[n]}

\displaystyle=H_{[n]}+\int_{I}\hat{\pi}_{[n]}(t)\big(\pi_{[n]}(t)\big)^{\top}w(t)\operatorname{d}t.
