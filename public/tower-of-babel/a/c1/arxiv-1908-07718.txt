On card guessing game with one time riffle shuffle and complete feedback
Pengda Liu

Abstract
. This paper studies the game of guessing riffle-shuffled cards with complete feedback. A deck of n n cards labelled 1 to n n is riffle-shuffled once and placed on a table. A player tries to guess the cards from top and is given complete feedback after each guess. The goal is to find the guessing strategy with maximum reward (expected number of correct guesses). We give the optimal strategy for this game and prove that the maximum expected reward is n / 2 + 2 / π ⋅ n + O ⁡ ( 1 ) n/2+\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1) , partially solving an open problem of Bayer and Diaconis [ 2 ] .

1.
Introduction

Consider the following game in which a deck of
n
cards is shuffled according to some method. A player is then asked to guess the shuffled deck from top down. Each round, the player only guesses the card at the top of the deck and receives certain feedback after the guess. Then the top card is removed and the player continues to guess the next card until the deck is empty. The
reward
of the game is the total number of correct guesses. The goal is to find the optimal guessing strategy so that the expected reward is maximized. The best strategy would then depend on the {shuffling method, feedback} combination. This problem is studied in
[
3
]
in the framework of sequential experiments and is shown to have great applications in statistical testings like taste testing and partially randomized clinical trials. Three common feedbacks that they consider are

.

zero feedback

In this scenario, no feedback of any kind is given.

.

correctness feedback

In this scenario, after each guess, the player is told whether the guess is correct or not.

.

complete feedback

In this scenario, after each guess, the correct card is revealed to the player.

They mostly focus on the uniform shuffling setting. In this paper, we consider the game of
dovetail shuffle
(also called
riffle shuffle
and we will use the two names interchangeably) with complete feedback. In
[
2
]
Bayer and Diaconis raised the open problem of deciding the optimal guessing strategy with complete feedback when a deck has been riffle shuffled an arbitrary number of times and calculating the expected reward. We make the first known progress towards this problem by completely solving the case where the cards are riffle shuffled once. This already requires new techniques and the asymptotic formula for the expected reward appears to be highly non-trivial. We present our main results next.

1.1.
Main Results

Consider the following algorithm for playing the game.

Algorithm 1.1
.

1.

(guessing consecutive stage) Guess 1 at the first step. After that, if the previously revealed card sequence
[1,2,...,m]
is still consecutive, then we guess the next to be card
m+1
.

2.

(separating pile stage) Suppose that when card
k
is shown, the revealed sequence
1,2...m,k
becomes non-consecutive,
separate the remaining cards into two sequences
A=\{m+1,...,k-1\}
and
B=\{k+1,...,n\}
.

3.

(guess interleave stage) Each time guess the first card in the longer sequence and cross the revealed card from its sequence.

Remark 1.2
.

When
m+1>k-1
, we let
A
to be empty and if
k+1>n
, we let
B
to be empty.

We show that the algorithm is optimal and give its expected return as the next theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Algorithm
1.1
is the optimal strategy for guessing a deck of
n
cards riffle shuffled once with complete feedback and has expected reward
\frac{1}{2}\cdot n+\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1)
.

This will be proved in section 3 and next we introduce some notations to be used in the paper.

1.2.
Notations

For an event or statement
A
, we use
\delta(A)
to denote the indicator function. For two functions
f(n)
and
g(n)
of non-negative integers,
f\sim g
means
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f/g=1

. For two probability distributions

is defined to be

TV(P_{1},P_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}|P_{1}(\omega)-P_{2}(\omega)|.

An equivalent definition is

TV(P_{1},P_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{f:\Omega\rightarrow[-1,1]}\{\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}P_{1}(\omega)f(\omega)-\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}P_{2}(\omega)f(\omega)\}.

We let
[n]
denote the sequence
1,2,...,n
and use
S_{n}
for the permutation group of
[n]
.
For a sequence
a=a_{1},...,a_{n}
that are distinct numbers in
[n]
, its

is a maximal increasing consecutive subsequence. Let
rs(a)
denote the number of rising sequences of
a
. For example, for
a=[1,4,2,5,3,6]
, its rising sequences are
[1,2,3]
and
[4,5,6]
, thus

. For any

, let

.

1.3.
Formulation of shuffling

Throughout this paper we assume that initially a deck of
n
cards are laid and labeled as
1,2,...,n
from top to bottom. A given shuffling method is an algorithm
f
that upon query outputs a shuffled sequence
(c_{1},...,c_{n})
representing that from top to bottom, the shuffled cards are
c_{1},...,c_{n}
. We formulate such algorithm
f
as
follows. First
f
is a random permutation and thus defines a probability distribution
Q_{f}
over the symmetry group
S_{n}
. Each time when we query the algorithm
f
, it samples a permutation
\pi
from the symmetry group
S_{n}
with respect to
Q_{f}
and applies
\pi([n])
to get the shuffled deck. When there is an need, we use
f_{n}
to denote that this algorithm is for
n
cards. With such formulation,
f
also induces an
n
by
n
transition matrix
P^{f}
where
P^{f}_{ij}
is the probability that card
i
ends up at position
j
after the shuffle. When the shuffling method
f
used in context is unambiguous, we just use
Q
and
P
.

1.4.
High level proof idea

If the player guesses with complete feedback, let
\mathcal{U}
be the set of all sequences of length no more than
n
, then the optimal strategy is a map
\lambda:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow[n]
such that

\lambda(x_{1}...x_{m-1})=\arg\max_{a}\Pr(x_{m}=a|x_{1}...x_{m-1})\hskip 14.22636pt\forall x_{1}....x_{m-1}\in\mathcal{U}.

Let
R^{*}(\pi,f)
be the reward with this optimal strategy when the sampled permutation is
\pi
, then we have the maximum expected reward satisfies

(1.1)

R^{*}(f)=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}R^{*}(\pi,f)Q_{f}(\pi).

Directly computing these conditional probabilities and summing over all
S_{n}
may be applicable to some cases like uniform shuffle. For other cases, it may be hard to compute all the conditional probabilities to arrive at an operational description of the optimal strategy and we sometimes we need to adopt the following recursive approach. The best guess for the first card is always
\max_{j}\{P^{f}_{j1}\}
. After the first card is revealed to be
i
, we can think of the remaining
n-1
cards as being shuffled according to a conditional shuffle
f^{(i)}
and

(1.2)

R^{*}(f)=\max_{j}\{P^{f}_{j1}\}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}P^{f}_{i1}R^{*}(f^{(i)}).

This is exactly the main idea behind the proof—the riffle shuffle can be approximated by another shuffling method with recursive structure.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
The mathematical model for riffle shuffle

Dovetail shuffle is a common shuffling method used in real life. The shuffler first divides the deck into two piles and then drops the cards from the two piles to make a new shuffled pile. We will use the following GSR model introduced by Gilbert and Shannon (see
Gilbert (1955)) and Reeds (1981) to mathematically describe the dovetail shuffling algorithm.

GSR(Gilbert-Shannon-Reeds) Model

(1)

The deck of
n
cards is cut at a position into two piles randomly according to the binomial distribution such that the probability of cutting it at position
k
(where
0\leq k\leq n
) is
\frac{\binom{n}{k}}{2^{n}}
. After this step, we end up with two piles of cards
A,B
of size
k
and
n-k
.

(2)

Start with the shuffled pile
C
being empty and
drop the cards from the bottom of
A
and
B

onto
C
one at a time in the following way. At each time, let
x
be the number of remaining cards in pile
A
and
y
be the number of remaining cards in pile
B
, drop a card from pile
A
with probability
\frac{x}{x+y}
and a card from pile
B
with probability
\frac{y}{x+y}
.

Diaconis
[
5
]
gives an analysis showing that the GSR model is a
good model for riffle shuffle in real life. Diaconis
[
4
]
briefly mentioned another way to interpret step (2) without proof, we prove it here for completeness as the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Step (2) is equivalent to interleaving the two piles uniform randomly.

Proof.

Suppose initially pile
A
has
a
cards and pile
B
has
b
cards.
For a sequence
x_{1}...x_{m}
, let
P_{1}(.)
be the probability that, with step (2), this sequence ends up being the first
m
cards in the shuffled pile from bottom to top and let
P_{2}
be defined similarly if we interleave the two piles uniformly. Define
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
both be 1 when
m=0
. We prove by induction on
m
that
P_{1}(x_{1}....x_{m})=P_{2}(x_{1}....x_{m})
.

The base case
m=0
holds by definition. Now suppose the result holds for all sequences of length
m
, consider any sequence
x_{1},...,x_{m+1}
that could be the layout of the first
m+1
cards of the shuffled deck, that is,
rs(x_{1},...,x_{m+1})\leq 2
. Let

a^{\prime}=|A\setminus\{x_{1},...,x_{m}\}|,b^{\prime}=|B\setminus\{x_{1},...,x_{m}\}|.

Let
P_{1}(x_{m+1}|x_{1},...,x_{m})
denote the probability that the
m+1^{th}
card being
x_{m+1}
conditioning on the first
m
cards being
x_{1},...,x_{m}
. Note that conditioning on step 1,
x_{m+1}
can assume only two values, either the first card from pile
A
or the first card from pile
B
. WLOG, suppose
x_{m+1}
is from pile
A
, then we have

P_{1}(x_{1}...x_{m+1})=P_{1}(x_{m+1}|x_{1},...,x_{m})P_{1}(x_{1},...,x_{m})=\frac{a^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}P_{1}(x_{1},...,x_{m}).

Now consider the case where we interleave the two piles uniformly, given that the first
m
cards are
x_{1},...,x_{m}
, there are
\binom{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}}
ways to interleave the remaining cards and
\binom{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}-1}{a^{\prime}-1}
ways to interleave if the next card is
x_{m+1}
, thus

\displaystyle P_{2}(x_{m+1}|x_{1},...,x_{m})=\frac{\binom{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}-1}{a^{\prime}-1}}{\binom{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}}}=\frac{a^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle P_{2}(x_{1},...,x_{m+1})=P_{2}(x_{m+1}|x_{1},...,x_{m})P_{2}(x_{1},...,x_{m})=\frac{a^{\prime}}{a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}}P_{2}(x_{1},...,x_{m}).

By induction, we have
P_{1}(x_{1},...,x_{m+1})=P_{2}(x_{1},...,x_{m+1})
thus step (2) is equivalent to interleave the two piles uniformly.
∎

Given a deck already cut, the probability of each of the configuration is given by the following corollary.

Corollary 2.2
.

If the deck is cut at position
k
, the resulting deck’s configuration is one of the possible interleavings each with probability
\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}
.

The GSR model gives an operational description of riffle shuffle, now we provide its distribution
Q
and transition matrix
P
. First from the operational description we note that every permutation
\pi
in the support of
Q
satisfies
rs(\pi)=1
or 2, that is, it is either the identity or has exactly 2 rising sequences. Bayer and Diaconis
[
2
]
proves a more general result about
a
-shuffle where the original deck is separated into
a
packs instead of 2. Here we use the version of
a=2
and a simpler proof is provided for completeness.

Theorem 2.3
.

For dovetail shuffle, the distribution
Q
on
S_{n}
satisfies

Q(\pi)=\begin{cases}\frac{n+1}{2^{n}}&\text{if }\pi=id\\
\frac{1}{2^{n}}&\text{if }rs(\pi)=2\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\Pr(\text{cut}_{k})
be the probability of cutting at position
k
and
\Pr(\pi|\text{cut}_{k})
be the probability of
\pi
conditioning on that it is cut at
k
. By Lemma
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
we have

\displaystyle Q(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pr(\text{cut}_{k})\Pr(\pi|\text{cut}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{k}}{2^{n}}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\delta(\text{there is an interleaving to generate }\pi([n])\text{ when cut at }k)

\displaystyle=\frac{M(\pi)}{2^{n}}

where
M(\pi)
is the number of cuts that can generate
\pi([n])
.

Every cut could generate the identity, so
M(id)=n+1
. For the other
\pi
such that
rs(\pi)=2
, exactly one cut could generate it (which is to cut it at the minimum of the ends of the two rising sequences), thus
M(\pi)=1
, so the theorem is proved.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

This theorem shows that the number of rising sequences is a sufficient statistics and this is true in general for
a
-shuffle as well. We also have that the entropy of
Q
is
n\log 2-\frac{n+1}{2^{n}}\log(n+1).

Corollary 2.5
.

Recall that in section 1.2 we let
R_{n}
denote the number of permutations with 2 rising sequences. From this theorem we have that
R_{n}=(1-Q(id))2^{n}=2^{n}-n-1
.

In
[
1
]
Ciucu has made great contributions to guessing riffle shuffled deck with zero feedback in which he proves the following theorem

Theorem 2.6
.

The optimal strategy for guessing a deck of
n

\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1)

In his proof, the transition matrix

Theorem 2.7
.

For dovetail shuffle, the transition matrix
P
satisfies

P_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\binom{j-1}{j-i}&\text{if }i<j\\
\frac{1}{2^{n}}(2^{j-1}+2^{n-j})&\text{if }i=j\\
\frac{1}{2^{n-j+1}}\binom{n-j}{i-j}&\text{if }i>j.\\
\end{cases}

Proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof consists of two parts. We first show that the algorithm is optimal in Proposition
3.1
and prove its expected reward in Proposition

.

Proposition 3.1
.

Algorithm
1.1
is the optimal strategy.

In showing the optimality, we first prove a lemma explaining the
guessing consecutive stage
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given a deck of
n
cards riffle shuffled once, let the true cards be
a_{1},....,a_{n}
from top down, then we have

\arg\max_{l}\Pr(a_{m}=l|a_{i}=i\text{ for }1\leq i<m)=m.

Proof.

Let the true underlying permutation be
\pi
and

I_{m}=\{\pi|\pi([m])=id([m])\text{ and }rs(\pi)=2\}

then by Corollary
2.5
we have

|I_{m}|=|\{\pi|rs(\pi[m+1,...,n])=2\}|=R_{n-m}=2^{n-m}-n+m-1.

By Theorem
2.3

\Pr(\pi([m])=id([m]))=\Pr(\pi=id)+\sum_{\pi\in I_{m}}\Pr(\pi)=\frac{n+1}{2^{n}}+|I_{m}|\frac{1}{2^{n}}=\frac{2^{n-m}+m}{2^{n}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\Pr(\pi(m)=m|\pi([m-1])=id([m-1]))

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr(\pi([m])=id([m]))}{\Pr(\pi([m-1])=id([m-1]))}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n-m}+m}{2^{n-m+1}+m-1}>\frac{1}{2}

which gives
\arg\max_{l}P(a_{m}=l|a_{i}=i\text{ for }1\leq i<m)=m
.
∎

With the above lemma, it’s easy to prove that our algorithm is optimal.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By Theorem
2.7
, we have
P_{11}=\frac{1+2^{n-1}}{2^{n}}>\frac{1}{2}
, thus at the first step, the best guess should be 1. By Lemma
3.2
and induction, if the previous revealed sequence is consecutive, the best guess should be the next card in the consecutive sequence. Finally, if the revealed sequence
[1,2,....,m,k]
became non-consecutive when
k
is flipped, by step 2 of GSR model and Lemma
2.1
, we know that the remaining deck is formed by interleaving
A=\{m+1,...,k-1\}
and
B=\{k+1,...,n\}
uniformly. Then we have

\Pr(\text{next card=}m+1)=\frac{\binom{|A|+|B|-1}{|A|-1}}{\binom{|A|+|B|}{|A|}}=\frac{|A|}{|A|+|B|}.

Thus in this stage the best guess is the beginning of the longer sequence.
∎

The next proposition gives the expected reward.

Proposition 3.3
.

Algorithm
1.1
has expected reward
n/2+\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1).

In proving this proposition, we first introduce the following sequence which was also appears in Ciucu
[
1
]
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let the sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
be such that
a_{i}=\frac{1}{2^{i}}\binom{i}{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}
.

[
1
]
proves a lemma about
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}
and we prove something similar with a more refined asymptotic bound.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
A_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}
, then
A_{n}=c\sqrt{n}+O(1)
where the constant
c=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\approx 1.60
.

Proof.

By Sterling’s formula of the form
n!=\sqrt{2\pi n}(\frac{n}{e})^{n}e^{\lambda_{n}}
where
\frac{1}{12n+1}<\lambda_{n}<\frac{1}{12n}
, we have

\displaystyle a_{2i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2i)!}{2^{2i}i!i!}=\frac{\sqrt{4\pi i}(\frac{2i}{e})^{2i}e^{\lambda_{2i}}}{2^{2i}\Big(\sqrt{2\pi i}(\frac{i}{e})^{i}e^{\lambda_{i}}\Big)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\lambda_{2i}}}{\sqrt{\pi i}e^{2\lambda_{i}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi i}}e^{\lambda_{2i}-2\lambda_{i}}.

By the bound on
\lambda_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{24i+1}-\frac{2}{12i}<\lambda_{2i}-2\lambda_{i}<\frac{1}{24i}-\frac{2}{12i+1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\lambda_{2i}-2\lambda_{i}=\Theta(\frac{1}{i})\Rightarrow e^{\lambda_{2i}-2\lambda_{i}}=1+O(\frac{1}{i})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow a_{2i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi i}}+O(\frac{1}{i^{\frac{3}{2}}}).

where the second last equality is by the expansion of
e^{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{i}}{i!}
.

We have
a_{2i}=a_{2i-1}
and since the series
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{1}{i^{\frac{3}{2}}}
converges,

A_{2n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_{i}+O(1)=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{2i}+O(1)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}+O(1).

To estimate the series
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}
, we have

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{n+1}-2=\int_{1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}dx\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}\leq 1+\int_{1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}dx=2\sqrt{n}-1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow A_{2n}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\sqrt{2n}+O(1).

Since
A_{2n}\leq A_{2n+1}\leq A_{2n+2}
, we have
A_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1)
.
∎

Let
f_{n}
denote the riffle shuffle algorithm for
n

R^{*}(f_{n})

1.2

g_{n}

Lemma 3.6
.

(a)

The optimal guessing algorithm for
g_{n}
is the same as
f_{n}
.

(b)

R^{*}(g_{n})=R^{*}(f_{n})+o(1)
.

Proof.

For (a), since Algorithm

f_{n}

For (b), given any permutation
\pi
on
[n]

\pi^{\prime}
to be a permutation on
[n+1]
such that
\pi^{\prime}(1)=1
and
\pi^{\prime}(i)=\pi(i-1)+1

\pi^{\prime}
acts on
[2,...,n+1]
the same as
\pi
acts on
[1,...,n]

First, we compute the distribution
Q_{g_{n}}

. By Theorem

\displaystyle Q_{g_{n}}(id_{[n]})

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}_{f_{n+1}}(id_{[n+1]}|\text{ the first card is 1})

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{f_{n+1}}(id_{[n+1]})}{P^{f_{n+1}}_{11}}=\frac{n+2}{2^{n}+1}.

For any
\pi
with two rising sequences,
\pi^{\prime}
also satisfies

Q_{g_{n}}(\pi)=\text{Pr}(\pi^{\prime}|\pi^{\prime}(1)=1)=\frac{Q_{f_{n+1}}(\pi^{\prime})}{P^{f_{n+1}}_{11}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}+1}.

Thus the total variation distance of their distributions satisfies

\displaystyle TV(Q_{f_{n}},Q_{g_{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}|Q_{f_{n}}(\pi)-Q_{g_{n}}(\pi)|=\frac{1}{2}|Q_{f_{n}}(id)-Q_{g_{n}}(id)|+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\pi\in I_{n}}|Q_{f_{n}}(\pi)-Q_{g_{n}}(\pi)|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|Q_{f_{n}}(id)-Q_{g_{n}}(id)|+\frac{1}{2}|I_{n}|\cdot|\frac{1}{2^{n}}-\frac{1}{2^{n}+1}|=\frac{2^{n}-n-1}{(2^{n}+1)2^{n}}.

Recall that in section 1.4 we defined
R^{*}(\pi,f_{n})
as the reward of the optimal strategy for

\pi

R^{*}(\cdot,f_{n})=R^{*}(\cdot,g_{n})
as functions on

R^{*}(\cdot,f_{n})\leq n
, by the equivalent definition of total variation distance we have

\displaystyle|R^{*}(f_{n})-R^{*}(g_{n})|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}R^{*}(\pi,f_{n})Q_{f_{n}}(\pi)-\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}R^{*}(\pi,f_{n})Q_{g_{n}}(\pi)|

\displaystyle\leq 2n\cdot TV(Q_{f_{n}},Q_{g_{n}})=\frac{2n(2^{n}-n-1)}{2^{n}(2^{n}+1)}\leq\frac{n}{2^{n-1}}=o(1).

Thus we have
R^{*}(g_{n})=R^{*}(f_{n})+o(1)
.
∎

),

(3.1)

R^{*}(f_{n})=P_{11}+P_{11}R^{*}(g_{n-1})+\sum_{k=2}^{n}P_{k1}f(k-1,n-k).

Let
G(n)=R^{*}(f_{n})
, by Theorem
2.7
and Lemma
3.6
we have

G(n)=\frac{1+2^{n-1}}{2^{n}}\big(1+G(n-1)+o(1)\big)+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}{2^{n}}f(k-1,n-k).

Define

S(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}f(k,n-k)\text{ and }F(n)=\frac{S(n)}{2^{n+1}}.

Since
G(n)\leq n
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}G_{n}/2^{n}=0
, we have

(3.2)

G(n)=\frac{1}{2}G(n-1)+F(n-1)+\frac{1}{2}+o(1)

with condition
G(1)=1
.

For
f(a,b)
, by considering where the next card comes from, we have

(3.3)

f(a,b)=\max\{\frac{a}{a+b},\frac{b}{a+b}\}+\frac{b}{a+b}f(a,b-1)+\frac{a}{a+b}f(a-1,b)

with conditions
f(a,b)=f(b,a)
and
f(a,0)=f(0,a)=a
.

Next, we attempt to solve the above recursive formulas, by Equation (
3.3
) we have

\displaystyle S(n)

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}(\max\{\frac{k}{n},1-\frac{k}{n}\}+\frac{k}{n}f(k-1,n-k)+\frac{n-k}{n}f(k,n-k-1))

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\max\{\frac{k}{n},1-\frac{k}{n}\}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k}{n}f(k-1,n-k)+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n-k}{n}f(k,n-k-1)

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\max\{\frac{k}{n},1-\frac{k}{n}\}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k-1}f(k-1,n-1-(k-1))

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}f(k,n-1-k)

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\max\{\frac{k}{n},1-\frac{k}{n}\}+2S(n-1)

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\binom{n}{k}\frac{n-k}{n}+\sum_{k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\frac{k}{n}+2S(n-1)

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\binom{n-1}{k}+\sum_{k=\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k-1}+2S(n-1)

\displaystyle=n+\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}\binom{n-1}{k}+\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}+2S(n-1)

\displaystyle=2^{n-1}+\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}+n-2+2S(n-1).

Thus we end up with the following recursive formula for
S(n)

S(n)=2^{n-1}+\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}+n-2+2S(n-1)

which gives

\displaystyle F(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\binom{n-1}{\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\rfloor}+n-2}{2^{n+1}}+F(n-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{n-2}{2^{n+1}}+\frac{a_{n-1}}{4}+F(n-1)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow F(n)=\frac{n}{4}+\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}a_{k}}{4}+O(1).

By Lemma
3.5
we have

(3.4)

F(n)=\frac{n}{4}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}+O(1).

Recall that from earlier we have

(3.5)

G(n)=\frac{1}{2}G(n-1)+F(n-1)+\frac{1}{2}+o(1).

Next, we prove that
G(n)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot n+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1)
.

By Equation (
3.4
), there is a constant
c_{0}
such that
F(n)=\frac{n}{4}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}+x_{n}
and
|x_{n}|\leq c_{0}
for all
n
. By Equation (
3.5
), there is a constant
c_{1}
such that
G(n)=\frac{1}{2}G(n-1)+F(n-1)+y_{n}
and
|y_{n}|\leq c_{1}
for all
n
.

Suppose
G(n)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot n+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n}+z_{n}
let
C=\max\{2c_{0}+2c_{1}+2,|z_{1}|\}
, we prove by induction that
|z_{n}|\leq C
.

By our choice of
C
, the base case of
n=1
is true. Now suppose the statement holds for
n
, consider
n+1
. Then we have

\displaystyle G(n+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}G(n)+F(n)+y_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}\cdot n+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n}+z_{n})+\frac{n}{4}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi}}+x_{n}+y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n}+\frac{z_{n}}{2}+x_{n}+y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\cdot\sqrt{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})+\frac{z_{n}}{2}+x_{n}+y_{n+1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow z_{n+1}=\frac{z_{n}}{2}+x_{n}+y_{n+1}-\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|z_{n+1}|\leq|\frac{z_{n}}{2}|+|x_{n}|+|y_{n+1}|+\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{n+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{2}+c_{0}+c_{1}+1\leq C.

Thus the statement holds for
n+1
, which means
|z_{n}|\leq C
for all
n
, thus we have the expected reward is

G(n)=n/2+\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\sqrt{n}+O(1).

∎

Remark 3.7
.

Currently our approximation technique is not refined enough to find a constant
c
to replace the
O(1)
term by
c+o(1)
. By doing numerical calculation for
1\leq n\leq 10000
, the error term is below 0.5.

4.
Open problems

The notation in this section is the same as in section 3.

1.

Calculating the variance of the number of correct guesses under optimal strategy. Since we already have a formula for the expected value
R^{*}(f_{n})
, it suffices to compute the following expected square sum

(4.1)

T^{*}(f_{n}):=\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}R^{*}(\pi,f_{n})^{2}Q_{f}(\pi).

It satisfies

T^{*}(f_{n})=P_{11}(1+R^{*}(g_{n-1}))^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}P_{k1}f(k-1,n-k)^{2}

=\frac{1+2^{n-1}}{2^{n}}(1+R^{*}(f_{n-1}))^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}{2^{n}}f(k-1,n-k)^{2}+o(1).

Thus it remains to estimate

F^{\prime}(n):=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}f(k-1,n-k)^{2}.

Currently we are not able to get a good estimate of
F^{\prime}
since an expansion using Equation (
3.3
) would involve cross terms and be very complicated to solve.

2.

Generalize to
k
riffle shuffles
f^{(k)}_{n}
. A natural next step is to prove an optimal guessing strategy and find its expected number of correct guesses for a deck of cards riffle shuffled for
k\geq 2
times. A conjectured optimal strategy is given in
[
2
]
. The main difficulty in extending the current method to the general setting is the following. With
k=1
, the two piles of cards before interleaving would be fully determined once the first non-consecutive card appears. For larger
k
, the natural generalization of this would be based on the sequential description of
a
-shuffle formulated in
[
2
]
on page 299. However, the original card piles before interleaving are much harder to be determined by the revealed cards if
k
is large.

3.

Generalize to yes or no feedback. For the case of uniform shuffle, it is solved in
[
3
]
. However, this question remains wide open for riffle shuffle, primarily because the conditional shuffle of receiving negative feedback is less understood.
