Abstract
We consider partitions of a point set into two parts, and the lengths of the minimum spanning trees of the original set and of the two parts. If w ⁡ ( P ) w(P) denotes the length of a minimum spanning tree of P P , we show that every set P P of n ≥ 12 n\geq 12 points admits a bipartition P = R ∪ B P=R\cup B for which the ratio w ⁡ ( R ) + w ⁡ ( B ) w ⁡ ( P ) \frac{w(R)+w(B)}{w(P)} is strictly larger than 1 1 ; and that 1 1 is the largest number with this property. Furthermore, we provide a very fast algorithm that computes such a bipartition in O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) time and one that computes the corresponding ratio in O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log{n}) time. In certain settings, a ratio larger than 1 1 can be expected and sometimes guaranteed. For example, if P P is a set of n n random points uniformly distributed in [ 0 , 1 ] 2 [0,1]^{2} ( n → ∞ n\to\infty ), then for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , the above ratio in a maximizing partition is at least 2 − ε \sqrt{2}-\varepsilon with probability tending to 1 1 . As another example, if P P is a set of n n points with spread at most α ​ n \alpha\sqrt{n} , for some constant α > 0 \alpha>0 , then the aforementioned ratio in a maximizing partition is 1 + Ω ⁡ ( α − 2 ) 1+\Omega(\alpha^{-2}) . All our results and techniques are extendable to higher dimensions.

Disjoint disks.

Let

Observation 6

Let

Proof.

Assume that

2.1
Key lemmas

The following lemmas can be formulated for general graphs; however, here we restrict ourselves
to the simpler geometric setting.

Lemma 9
.

Let
T
be a minimum spanning tree of a complete geometric graph
G=K_{n}(P)
, and
p,q\in P

be the centers of two disjoint disks in
\mathcal{D}(P)
.

Then
w(P\setminus\{p,q\})\geq w-r(p)-r(q)
, where
w=w(P)
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
w(P\setminus\{p,q\})<w-r(p)-r(q)
, and let
T^{\prime}
be
a MST of
P\setminus\{p,q\}
. Add
p,q
to
T^{\prime}
by connecting them via edges of length

r(p)
and
r(q)
, respectively. We obtain a spanning tree of
G
whose length is strictly less than

w(P\setminus\{p,q\})+r(p)+r(q)<w-r(p)-r(q)+r(p)+r(q)=w,

a contradiction.
∎

We can formulate this statement in a slightly more general form that will be used in the proof
of Theorem
5
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
T
be a minimum spanning tree of a complete geometric graph

,
where
w=w(P)
is the length of

Let

Then
w(P\setminus\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\})\geq w-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|p_{i}q_{i}|

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that

w(P\setminus\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\})<w-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|p_{i}q_{i}|
, and let
T^{\prime}
be
a MST of
P\setminus\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}

Add
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}
to

by connecting them via the

are

.
We obtain a spanning tree of

Each cell contains at most

.
Thus the total number of points in
P
is strictly less than
n
, a contradiction
that completes the proof.
∎

The following upper bound on
w
will be needed at the end of the proof.

Lemma 14
.

We have
w\leq 1.4\alpha n
, for
n
sufficiently large.

Proof.

Few
[
15
]
proved that the (Euclidean) length of a shortest cycle (tour)
through
n
points in the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
is at most
\sqrt{2n}+7/4
, and
the same upper bound holds for the minimum spanning tree.
A slightly better upper bound
1.392\sqrt{n}+7/4
, is due to Karloff
[
20
]
.
For large
n
, this is at most
1.4\sqrt{n}
. Scaling by
\alpha\sqrt{n}
immediately
yields that
w\leq 1.4\alpha n
.
∎

We continue with the proof of the theorem.
Decompose
\mathcal{R}
into
25
subsets of rich cells as in the decomposition of a
5\times 5
square
into unit squares. More precisely, consider the tiling of the plane by
5\times 5
axis-aligned tiles
of side-length
2\alpha
. Arbitrarily fix one of the tiles.
A subset corresponds to the rich cells in
Q
having the same position
in the fixed tile. Each subset satisfies the following properties
(recall the side-length of each cell
2\alpha
):

i

the distance between any two points in the same cell is at most
2\sqrt{2}\alpha
.

ii

the distance between any two points in different cells is at least
8\alpha
.

By averaging, one of the subsets,
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, contains
k\geq\lceil|\mathcal{R}|/25\rceil
rich cells. Note that

k\geq\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{200(2\alpha+1)^{2}}\,n,

(3)

by Lemma
13
.
Arbitrarily choose two points
p_{i},q_{i}
in each of the
k
rich cells.
Now consider the bipartition of
P
:

R=P\setminus\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\},\ \ B=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}.

By Lemma
10
and the first property of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
we have

w(R)\geq w-\sum_{i=1}^{k}|p_{i}q_{i}|\geq w-2\sqrt{2}\alpha k.

On the other hand, by the second property of
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
we have

w(B)\geq 8\alpha(k-1).

Consequently, by Lemma
14
and the fact that
n
(thus also
k
according to (
3
))
is sufficiently large, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma(P)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{w(R)+w(B)}{w}\geq\frac{w+2(4-\sqrt{2})\alpha k-8\alpha}{w}\geq\frac{w+5\alpha k}{w}

\displaystyle\geq 1+\frac{5\alpha k}{1.4\alpha n}=1+\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{56(2\alpha+1)^{2}}\geq 1+\frac{1}{11(2\alpha+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=1+\Omega(\alpha^{-2}),

as required. This completes the proof of Theorem
5
.
∎

It is likely that using a hexagonal tiling gives a slightly better constant in the bound,
however, the dependence of the bound on
\alpha
remains quadratic.

5
Concluding remarks

The techniques used to handle the planar case immediately extend to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for any fixed dimension
d
.
Specifically we need the
disjoint balls
property; we rely on estimates
due to Füredi and Loeb
[
16
]
, given in (
4
). Let
\kappa(d)
denote the maximum
number of balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that can form a configuration where each ball intersects every other ball
but does not contain the center of any other ball. As mentioned earlier, it is known that

8\leq\kappa(2)\leq 11
. The following bounds hold in
d
-space:

1.25^{d}\leq\kappa(d)\leq(1.887\ldots+o(1))^{d}.

(4)

The proof of Theorem
15
is analogous to that of Theorem
3
and
is therefore omitted.

Theorem 15
.

For every fixed
d
, there exists
n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
with the following property.
For any set
P
of
n\geq n_{d}
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\gamma(P)>1
.
A suitable bipartition with this ratio can be computed in
O(1)
time.

We have seen that the disjoint balls property implies that the maximum MST ratio is
strictly larger than
1
. However, the two properties are not equivalent.

Another related question is how the
\gamma
ratio changes when points are added to
a set. Suppose that
P_{0}
is a point set with
\gamma(P_{0})>1
, and an infinite sequence of
points,
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots
, are successively added to
P_{0}
. By Theorem
3
,

\gamma(P_{0}\cup\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\})>1
for every
n
such that
|P_{0}|+n\geq 12
.
Is it possible that
\gamma(P_{0}\cup\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\})
tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
?
We give a positive answer. Assuming that the first
n
points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}

are known, let
P_{n}=P_{0}\cup\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
,
w_{n}=w(P_{n})
, and
D_{n}={\rm diam}(P_{n})
,
where
a_{n},b_{n}
is a diameter pair in
P_{n}
. Assume that
\gamma(P_{n})\leq 1+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
,
we can select
p_{n+1}
so that
\gamma(P_{n+1})\leq 1+\varepsilon/2
, for every
n
.
Let
p_{n+1}
be a point on the line incident to
a_{n},b_{n}
at a sufficiently large distance

f(w_{n},\varepsilon)
from
b
, with
a_{n},b_{n},p_{n+1}
appearing in this order.
The verification that the above inequality holds, for a suitable function
f
,
is left to the reader. Observe that the above construction is analogous to the upper bound
construction in Proposition
2
; and works in any dimension.

We conclude with three open problems:

1.

We have seen that
\gamma(P)>1
can be arbitrarily close to
1
even for large
n
,
whereas it is separated from
1
by a positive gap for randomly uniformly distributed sets
and for
\alpha
-dense sets. In what other cases can one deduce
\gamma(P)>1+\lambda

for a positive constant
\lambda
?

2.

Is there an efficient algorithm for computing the maximum ratio,
\gamma(P)
?

3.

Is the
\sqrt{2}
lower bound for randomly uniformly distributed sets
in the unit square tight?
