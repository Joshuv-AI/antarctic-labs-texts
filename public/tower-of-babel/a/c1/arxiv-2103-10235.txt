An infinite interval version of the α \alpha -Kakutani equidistribution problem
M. Pollicott and B. Sewell

Abstract
In this article we extend results of Kakutani, Adler–Flatto, Smilansky and others on the classical α \alpha -Kakutani equidistribution result for sequences arising from finite partitions of the interval. In particular, we describe a generalization of the equidistribution result to infinite partitions. In addition, we give discrepancy estimates, extending results of Drmota–Infusino [ 8 ] .

Introduction

Uniform distribution of sequences of numbers
(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in the unit interval has been an important area of interest for over a century. For example, it was shown by Weyl
[
20
]
that, for any irrational
\alpha
, the sequence
x_{n}=\alpha n
(mod
1
) is uniformly distributed and Hardy and Littlewood showed that, for almost all
\lambda>1
, the sequence
x_{n}=\lambda^{n}
(mod
1
) is uniformly distributed
[
13
]
. In this note we consider another simple family of examples based on subdividing intervals.
Before introducing the original motivating example, we first fix
our terminology: a
partition

\mathcal{P}
is a set of closed, positive-length intervals, which have pairwise disjoint interiors and cover
[0,1]
up to a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

1.1
The original
\alpha
-Kakutani equidistribution result

In 1973

Our main result is the following generalization of Kakutani’s
equidistribution theorem. Let
\|I\|
denote the length of an interval
I
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a countable partition. Then, provided that

-\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{P}}\|I\|\log\|I\|<\infty,

the set
L_{n}
becomes uniformly distributed as
n\to\infty
.

A brief outline of this note: In section 2, we give a new dynamical viewpoint of the problem and in section 3, we apply renewal theory to prove Theorem
1
in two cases.
In section 4 we use a generating function to estimate the discrepancy in the rank one case.
In section 5 we use methods of analytic number theory to estimate the discrepancy in the higher rank case, with a generic Diophantine-type assumption.

Our interest in this problem, and the starting point for our analysis, began with the
very elegant work of
Smilansky
[
17
]
.

2
Partitions and similarities

Our approach to Theorem
1
is to express the elements of the partition
\mathcal{P}
in terms of the images of similarities. The refinements into finer partitions,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, can then be expressed in terms of words formed from the index set of
\mathcal{P}
.

That is, one can write
\mathcal{P}=\{T_{i}[0,1]\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, where each
T_{i}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is an orientation preserving similarity with contraction ratio
\alpha_{i}>0
. We see that
\mathcal{P}
being a partition is equivalent to the following:

•

T_{i}[0,1)\cap T_{j}[0,1)=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
; and

•

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}=1
.

Example 5
.

Given
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots
with
(t_{n})\to 1
, the partition
\{[t_{n-1},t_{n}]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equal to
\{T_{n}[0,1]\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

T_{n}(x)=(t_{n}-t_{n-1})x+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}t_{n}.

Example 6
.

Setting
t_{n}=1-\frac{1}{6}\,3^{-n}
for
n\geq 1
gives rise to
\mathcal{P}_{1}
in Figure
3
.

We now explain how this can be used to give an explicit description of the splitting process.

Definition

(
(T_{i})
-refinement)
.

Given a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{S_{k}[0,1]\}_{k}
, where the
\{S_{k}\}_{k}
are orientation preserving similarities, the
(T_{i})
-refinement of
\mathcal{P}
is the refinement obtained by taking all intervals of maximal length in
\mathcal{P}
and replacing them by subintervals in the following manner: if
S[0,1]\in\mathcal{P}
has maximal length in
\mathcal{P}
, then it is replaced by the elements of the set

\{S\circ\,T_{i}[0,1]\;|\;i\in\mathcal{I}\}.

Definition

(Interval substitution scheme)
.

The interval substitution scheme generated by
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is the sequence of partitions
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
defined as follows:

•

\mathcal{P}_{0}
is the trivial partition,
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\big\{[0,1]\big\}
; and

•

\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
is the
(T_{i})
-refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

This gives a convenient presentation of the partitions.

Example 7
.

The
\alpha
-Kakutani scheme is the interval substitution scheme generated by the pair
T_{1}:x\mapsto\alpha x
and
T_{2}:x\mapsto(1-\alpha)x+\alpha
.

Example 8
.

Similarly, the interval substitution scheme generated by the triple
T_{1}:x\mapsto x/2
,
T_{2}:x\mapsto(x+3)/6
and
T_{3}:x\mapsto(x+2)/3
gives the sequence of partitions depicted in Figure

.

We now associate to the sequence of partitions
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
a sequence of families of left endpoints of split intervals,
(L_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
.

Definition

(
L_{n}
)
.

Given an interval substitution scheme
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
generated by similarities
(T_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, we define the finite sets
L_{n}
(
n\geq 0
) to be

L_{n}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n}\ \bigcup_{I\in\mathcal{P}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{k+1}}\min(I).

Remark
.

One can consider a generalisation of the above process by dropping the assumption that the
(T_{i})_{i}
have to be affine. We will not consider this more general setting, but we note it is easy to give superficial examples where
E_{n}
(or
L_{n}
) is not uniformly distributed: take, for example,
T_{1}(x)=\sqrt{x}/2
,
T_{2}(x)=(x+1)/2
.

Considering the interval substitution scheme generated by
\{T_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, it follows inductively that every interval appearing in the process is obtained by applying a sequence of maps from
\{T_{i}\}_{i}
to
[0,1]
, and so each is naturally described by a finite word in
\mathcal{I}
. It is convenient to introduce the following notation.

Definition

(
W(\mathcal{I})
,
\ast
,
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}
,
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}
)
.

Given a countable set
\mathcal{I}
, the word set
W(\mathcal{I})
is the semigroup consisting of all words in
\mathcal{I}
: i.e.

W(\mathcal{I})=\{\emptyset\}\cup\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}^{n},

where
\emptyset
denotes the empty word (unique word of length zero), and the semigroup operation
\ast:W(\mathcal{I})\times W(\mathcal{I})\to W(\mathcal{I})
denotes concatenation of words, for which
\emptyset
acts as the identity:

(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\ast(m_{1},\ldots,m_{j})=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k},m_{1},\ldots,m_{j});\qquad{\boldsymbol{v}}\ast\emptyset=\emptyset\ast{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{v}}.

Furthermore, for ease of notation, we extend the definitions of
\alpha_{i}
and
T_{i}
to the whole of
W(\mathcal{I})
: For the word
{\boldsymbol{v}}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{I}^{k}
, define

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}:=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{i_{j}},\qquad T_{\boldsymbol{v}}:=T_{i_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ T_{i_{k}},

and also define

\alpha_{\emptyset}=1
and
T_{\emptyset}=\text{Id}_{[0,1]}
.

That is, given any interval
I
which appears in the process,
I=T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1]
for some word
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
*
*

*

Similarly, every word
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{I}
gives rise to an interval in some
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and will be split between
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
(i.e.,
I\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
) precisely when
n
satisfies

\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\max_{I\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\big\{\|I\|\big\},

and consequently its left endpoint
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)
will appear in
L_{n}
, if not already present in
L_{n-1}
.

Rather than using
n
in
\{L_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
to parametrise this process, we want to reparameterise this family to reflect the lengths of the maximal intervals, and rewrite it as
(X_{\lambda})
as follows.

Definition
.

For
\lambda>1
, let
X_{\lambda}=\emptyset
, and for
\lambda\in(0,1]
, let

X_{\lambda}:=L_{n(\lambda)},\quad\text{where}\quad n(\lambda):=\max\{n\geq 0\,:\,\forall I\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\ \|I\|\geq\lambda\}.

I.e., given
\lambda\leq 1
,
P_{n(\lambda)+1}
is the first partition in the process for which all intervals have lengths strictly smaller than
\lambda
. From the previous discussion, one obtains a convenient, dynamical formula for
X_{\lambda}
:

X_{\lambda}=\big\{T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)\,:\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I}),\,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\geq\lambda\big\}.

As
\lambda\to 0^{+}
,
n(\lambda)\to\infty
and so the uniform distribution of
L_{n}
as
n\to\infty
is equivalent to that of
X_{\lambda}
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
.
As an aside, this is equivalent to the convergence of the probablity measure
\mu_{\lambda}
,

\mu_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{|X_{\lambda}|}\sum_{x\in X_{\lambda}}\delta_{x},

to the Lebesgue measure (which we will henceforth denote as
\leb
), as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac delta measure at
x
, and
|\cdot|
denotes cardinality (of a finite set).

3
Proof of Theorem 1

This section is devoted to proving the main result, which we can conveniently rephrase in the following way.

Theorem 1
.

Provided that

-\sum_{I\in\mathcal{P}}\|I\|\log\|I\|<\infty
,
the measures
\mu_{\lambda}
converge to the Lebesgue measure
\leb
as Borel measures as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, i.e., for any interval
J\subset[0,1]
, we have
\mu_{\lambda}(J)\to\leb(J)=\|J\|
.

We first prove the convergence for a given interval of the form
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)
. For each of these elementary sets, their
\mu_{\lambda}
-measure is intimately related to the asymptotics of
|X_{\lambda}|
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
.
To proceed, we can relate
|X_{\lambda}|
to the set of words
|A_{\lambda}|
, where

A_{\lambda}:=\{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W(\mathcal{I})\,|\,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{w}}\geq\lambda\},

and exploit a natural renewal equation for the quantity
\lambda|A_{\lambda}|
.

Lemma 1
.

One of the following two cases hold. Either there is a (unique) symbol in
1\in\mathcal{I}
, say, such that
T_{1}(0)=0
; in which case, for all
\lambda>0
,

|A_{\lambda}|=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|X_{\lambda\alpha_{1}^{-k}}|\ \iff\ |X_{\lambda}|=|A_{\lambda}|-|A_{\lambda/\alpha_{1}}|;

(1)

or no element of
\{T_{\boldsymbol{w}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W(\mathcal{I})}
fixes 0, and
|X_{\lambda}|=|A_{\lambda}|
for all
\lambda>0
.

Proof of Lemma 1.

Let
{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\in A_{\lambda}
satisfy
T_{\boldsymbol{w}}(0)=T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)
. If
{\boldsymbol{w}}\neq{\boldsymbol{v}}
, the disjointness of
T_{i}[0,1)
and the injectivity of the
T_{i}
imply inductively that one of these words is obtained from the other by concatenation with a word fixing the identity; i.e., without loss of generality,
{\boldsymbol{w}}={\boldsymbol{v}}\ast{\boldsymbol{j}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
satisfies
T_{\boldsymbol{j}}(0)=0
. We now have the two cases:

If there is no symbol
i\in\mathcal{I}
for which
T_{i}(0)=0
, we must have
{\boldsymbol{j}}=\emptyset
; which implies the map
{\boldsymbol{v}}\mapsto T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)
is a bijection
A_{\lambda}\to X_{\lambda}
.

For the other case, let
1\in\mathcal{I}
be such that
T_{1}(0)=0
; it is unique by the disjointness of
T_{i}[0,1)
. Then
{\boldsymbol{j}}\neq\emptyset
only if
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\{1\}^{k}\subset\mathcal{I}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{j}}
is a tuple of 1’s. It follows that, for each
y\in X_{\lambda}
, there is a unique word
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}(y)\in A_{\lambda}
satisfying

•

T_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}(y)}(0)=y
; and

•

T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)=y\implies{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}(y)\ast{\boldsymbol{j}}
, for some
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\{\emptyset\}\cup\{1\}^{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In particular,
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)=y
implies
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}(y)}\alpha_{1}^{k}
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. In other words, there is exactly one element of
A_{\lambda}\setminus A_{\lambda/\alpha_{1}}
,
n_{0}(y)
, which gets mapped onto
y
. Therefore,
{\boldsymbol{v}}\mapsto T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(0)
gives a bijection
A_{\lambda}\setminus A_{\lambda/\alpha_{1}}\to X_{\lambda}
. The right hand side of (
1
) follows, completing the proof.
∎

Now, to continue the proof of the theorem, we combine the previous lemma with the following, which expresses
\mu_{\lambda}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))
as a ratio involving
|X_{\lambda}|
.

Lemma 2
.

For all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
,

|T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)\cap X_{\lambda\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}}|=|X_{\lambda}|.

(2)

In particular, for all
\lambda\in(0,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}]
,

\mu_{\lambda}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))=\frac{|X_{\lambda/\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}}|}{|X_{\lambda}|}.

(3)

Moreover, if
{\boldsymbol{v}}=\emptyset
, or
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{w}}\ast i
with
T_{i}(0)\neq 0
, these hold for all
\lambda>0
.

Proof of Lemma 2.

Fix
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
and consider (
2
). First let
\lambda\leq 1
. By induction, using the properties of the
T_{i}
, if
T_{\boldsymbol{w}}(0)\in T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)
, then, either
{\boldsymbol{w}}={\boldsymbol{v}}\ast{\boldsymbol{j}}
or
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{w}}\ast{\boldsymbol{j}}
, for some
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
. Moreover, if
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in A_{\lambda\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}}
, the second option gives a contradiction:
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{w}}>\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\geq\lambda\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\geq\alpha_{\boldsymbol{w}}
. Thus, we have

T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)\cap X_{\lambda\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}}=\{T_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\ast{\boldsymbol{j}}}(x)\,:\,{\boldsymbol{j}}\in A_{\lambda}\}.

By injectivity of
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}
, the right hand side bijectively corresponds to
X_{\lambda}
, giving (
2
). Applying (
2
), with
\lambda/\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}
in place of
\lambda
and dividing by
|X_{\lambda}|
yields (
3
).

As for the final remark, now let
\lambda>1
. This implies the second option holds,
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{w}}\ast{\boldsymbol{j}}
, since the first is satisfied only if
\lambda\leq 1
, by a similar contradiction argument. It follows that
T_{\boldsymbol{j}}(0)=0
and thus, as in the proof of Lemma 1,
{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\{1\}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. This contradicts the assumptions of the remark; thus both the left and the right hand side of
(
2
)
are empty when
\lambda>1
. The lemma follows.
∎

The significance of relating
|X_{\lambda}|
to
|A_{\lambda}|
will now become clear from the following renewal equation.

Lemma 3
.

The following holds for all
\lambda>0
.

|A_{\lambda}|=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}|A_{\lambda/\alpha_{i}}|+\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{$\chi$}\vphantom{\big(}}_{\{\lambda\leq 1\}},

(4)

where
\chi
is the indicator function.
Equivalently, the following renewal equation applies, for
Z(t):=e^{-t}|A_{e^{-t}}|
.

Z(t)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}Z\big(t-\log({\alpha_{i}}^{-1})\big)+e^{-t}\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{$\chi$}\vphantom{\big(}}_{\{t\geq 0\}},\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof of Lemma 3.

The second equation is a restatement of the first, (
4
). One can obtain (
4
) by partitioning the non-empty words in
A_{\lambda}
according to their first symbol. That is, one can write the following disjoint union.

A_{\lambda}\setminus\{\emptyset\}=\bigsqcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\underbrace{\{i\ast{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})\,|\,\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\leq\lambda/\alpha_{i}\}}_{\text{in bijection with }A_{\lambda/\alpha_{i}}}.

This gives rise to the sum in (
4
). Accounting for
\emptyset
,
\emptyset\in A_{\lambda}
if and only if
\lambda\leq 1
, thus providing the indicator term in (
4
), and completing the proof.
∎

To make use of this renewal equation, just as in
[
2
,
8
,
17
]
it is necessary to consider two cases which behave somewhat differently. These cases correspond to, for example, the
\alpha
-Kakutani schemes for
\alpha=1/3
and
\alpha=1/2
, as described in the introduction.

Definition

(Rank)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we will say the collection
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is
rank
n
if the smallest additive subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
containing the set
\{-\log(\alpha_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}
is not rank
n
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we will say
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is
infinite rank
. Also, whenever
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}
is not rank one, we say it is
higher rank
.

Example 9
.

The following examples illustrate the different ranks:

1.

\{1/2^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is rank one;

2.

\{1/2\}\cup\{1/3^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is rank two;

3.

\{1/2\}\cup\{1/3\}\cup\{1/7^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is rank three; and

4.

\{1/n^{s}\}_{n=2}^{\infty}
is infinite rank, where
s\approx 1.728
satisfies

3.1
Uniform distribution in the rank one case

In this subsection, we concentrate on the rank one case, also known as the
arithmetic

rationally-related
or
commensurable
case (see
[
2
,
8
,
17
]
). The characteristic feature of this case is that the contraction ratios are all powers of a common number,
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\subset\{x^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Thereby, fixing the minimal such
x>0
, equation (

) defines a discrete renewal equation on the lattice
-\log(x)\mathbb{Z}
, and one can apply the Erdős-Feller-Pollard renewal theorem (see
[
11
]
) to obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 4

(Erdős-Feller-Pollard renewal theorem)
.

Suppose that
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is rank one, that
x>0
is the minimal positive number for which
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\subset\{x^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and that
H:=-\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\log(\alpha_{i})<\infty
, then

Z\big(-n\log(x)\big)\to\frac{1}{H}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,\ n\in\mathbb{N};

where
Z(\cdot)
is defined in Lemma 3.

This lemma yields the following corollary, all but completing the proof of the theorem in this case.

Corollary
.

For
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and
H
as in the previous lemma, the following hold as
n\to\infty
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

•

|A_{x^{n}}|\sim x^{-n}/H
.

•

For all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
,
\mu_{x^{n}}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))\to\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\leb(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))
.

Proof of Corollary.

The first item is simply a restatement of the conclusion of Lemma 4. The second item follows with an application of Lemmas 1 and 2. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have one of the following, according to the two cases of Lemma 1: Either no
T_{i}
fixes
0
, and

\mu_{x^{n}}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))=\frac{|A_{x^{n}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-1}}|}{|A_{x^{n}}|}\sim\frac{\big(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-1}x^{n}\big)^{-1}/H}{x^{-n}/H}=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}};

or, for
T_{1}(0)=0
and
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}
the longest subword of
{\boldsymbol{v}}
not ending in a 1,

\displaystyle\mu_{x^{n}}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))

\displaystyle=\frac{|A_{{x^{n}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}^{-1}}|-|A_{{x^{n}}(\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\alpha_{1})^{-1}}|+\raisebox{-2.0pt}{\scalebox{1.3}{$\chi$}\vphantom{\big(}}_{\{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}<{x^{n}}\leq\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{0}}\}}}{|A_{{x^{n}}}|-|A_{{x^{n}}\alpha_{1}^{-1}}|}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1-\alpha_{1}}{1-\alpha_{1}}\frac{\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}x^{-n}/H}{x^{-n}/H}=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}

as

, as required.
∎

The last, easy, step in the proof of Theorem 1 concerns packing intervals of the form
T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)
into a given interval. This will be written as if in the continuous case. For the rank one case, for
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, one can read
\lambda=x^{n},

n\to\infty
; in fact, it is a trivial matter to prove they are equivalent.

Proof of Theorem 1.

Let
I\subset[0,1]
be an interval, and let
\alpha_{\max}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(\alpha_{i})
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

U_{n}:=\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}\,:\,T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)\subset I\}.

We claim the total length of intervals corresponding to
U_{n}
approximates the length of
I
, as follows:

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in U_{n}}\|T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)\|\geq\|I\|-2\alpha_{\max}^{n}.

(5)

To prove this, take
x\in I\setminus\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in U_{n}}T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)
. Then one of the following hold:

1.

x\in K_{n}:=[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}}T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)
. An inductive argument shows, for all
\lambda
and
n
,
\mu_{\lambda}(K_{n})=0=\text{Leb}(K_{n})
.

2.

x\in T_{\boldsymbol{w}}[0,1)
for some
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}\setminus U_{n}
. Since
T_{\boldsymbol{w}}[0,1)\not\subset I
, it is an interval meeting
\partial I
; thus there are at most two
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}
with this property.

In other words,
I\setminus\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in U_{n}}T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1)\setminus K_{n}
comprises at most two intervals, each with length at most
\alpha_{\max{}}^{n}
. (
5
) follows.

To apply this, let
U
be a union of a finitely number of intervals in
U_{n}
, and let
\|U\|
denote its total length (i.e.,
\leb(U)
). Then
\mu_{\lambda}(I)\geq\mu_{\lambda}(U)
and

\liminf_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\mu_{\lambda}(I)\geq\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\mu_{\lambda}(U)=\|U\|.

Taking the supremum over all such finite unions
U
gives

\liminf_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\mu_{\lambda}(I)\geq\|S\|\geq\|I\|-2\alpha_{\max}^{n}.

Repeating the above argument for the 0, 1 or 2 intervals comprising
[0,1]\setminus I
yields a converse inequality for
\|I\|
:

\limsup_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\mu_{\lambda}(I)\leq\|I\|+2\alpha_{\max}^{n},

and the proof is completed by taking
n\to\infty
.
∎

3.2
Uniform distribution in the higher rank case

In the remaining, generic case, the proof is very similar to the above. It continues with the following lemma, a convenient application of the Blackwell renewal theorem (see
[

]
).

Lemma 5

(Blackwell renewal theorem)
.

Suppose that
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is not rank one and that

Z(t)\to\frac{1}{H}\quad\text{as }t\to\infty,

where

is defined in Lemma 3.

This gives the following corollary. The proof is similar to that of the preceding, with the convergence now taking place as

Corollary
.

For
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
as in the previous lemma, the following hold as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
.

•

|A_{\lambda}|\sim 1\big/\lambda H
,

•

For all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
,
\mu_{\lambda}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))\to\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}=\leb(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))
.

The conclusion of Theorem 1 in this case is the same as given in the proceeding case, see p.
3.1
.

Remark
.

The same method of proof gives a more general result.
Suppose we have a set
\mathbb{X}
equipped with a probability measure
\nu
, and there is a collection
\{T_{i},\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
such that

a)

T_{i}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}
are injective

b)

for all

distinct,
T_{i}(\mathbb{X})\cap T_{j}(\mathbb{X})=\emptyset
;

c)

for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in W(\mathcal{I})
,
\nu(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\mathbb{X}))=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}
;

d)

\alpha_{i}>0
for all

and

; and

-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}\log(\alpha_{i})<\infty
.

Then, for any

, and for any

which can be written as

(with

w(\mathcal{I})
),
we have, as

\frac{|S\,\cap\,X_{\lambda}(x)|}{|X_{\lambda}(x)|}\to\nu(S),

where

It is also possible to generalise the method of proof to include substitution schemes starting from arbitrary partitions, and one recovers results analogous to those of

4
Discrepancy estimates

In
[
18
]
, the author both generalised the method used by Adler and Flatto in
[
1
]
to general finite partitions, and posed questions which inspired various other papers. Of particular interest is the behaviour of the
discrepancy
. This corresponds to estimating the speed of convergence (where the
I
are intervals) of

\sup_{I\subset[0,1]}\big|\,\mu_{\lambda}(I)-\|I\|\,\big|\to 0\hbox{ as }\lambda\to 0^{+}.

The Koksma inequality uses such estimates to give convergence rates for integrals of bounded-variation functions. Hence, fast-decaying discrepancies provide potential computationally-efficient numerical integration techniques. The theory of discrepancies (of equidistributing sets and sequences) has naturally received a lot of attention (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
for an overview). There are also interesting open problems, for example on optimal discrepancy in higher dimensions.

Regarding interval substitution schemes, in the finite-partition case, discrepancy estimates are provided by Drmota–Infusino in
[
8
]
, extending the application-focused work of Carbone in
[
5
]
. We in turn extend this to the context of infinite partitions. The results obtained here are different, depending on whether we are in the rank one case or the higher rank case.

4.1
Discrepancy estimates in the rank one case

In this final section, we extend the analysis of the rank one case to estimate the discrepancy between the measure
\mu_{\lambda}
and the Lebesgue measure. More precisely, we have the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that

1.

\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is rank one;

2.

x>0
is the smallest number for which
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\subset\{x^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
; and

3.

there is some
\varepsilon>0
for which
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}^{1-\varepsilon}<\infty
.

Then there is an
R^{\ast}\in(0,1)
, made explicit in Lemma 6 below, such that, for all
\rho\in(x/R^{\ast},1)
, there is a constant
C>0
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all intervals
I\subset[0,1]
,

\big|\,\mu_{x^{n}}(I)-\|I\|\,\big|\leq C\rho^{n}.

The proof of Theorem 2 begins with the following light lemma, defining
R^{\ast}
in terms of a generating function for
|A_{x^{n}}|
.

Lemma 6
.

Given
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
as in Theorem 2, the function formally defined by

g(z)=(z-x)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|A_{x^{n}}|z^{n}

has a holomorphic extension to the open disk of radius
R^{\ast}
about 0, where

R^{\ast}:=\min\left(\{x^{1-\varepsilon}\}\cup\big\{|z|\;:\>z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{x\},\ \sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{n_{j}}=1\big\}\right)>x

and where
n_{i}:=\log_{x}(\alpha_{i})
. Therefore, denoting by
b_{n}
the
n
th Taylor coefficient of
g
, given
R<R^{\ast}
, there exists
C>0
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
b_{n}\leq CR^{-n}
.

Proof of Lemma 6.

From (
4
), the renewal equation of Lemma 3, one has, for
|z|\leq x^{1-\varepsilon}
and
z\neq x
,

\displaystyle\frac{g(z)}{z-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|A_{x^{n}}|z^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}|A_{x^{n-n_{j}}}|+1\right)z^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}|A_{x^{n-n_{i}}}|z^{n}+\frac{1}{1-z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{n_{j}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|A_{x^{n-n_{j}}}|z^{n-n_{i}}+\frac{1}{1-z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{n_{j}}\frac{g(z)}{z-x}+\frac{1}{1-z},

which rearranges to

g(z)=\frac{z-x}{(z-1)(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{n_{j}}-1)}.

Therefore,
g
has a meromorphic expansion on the disk of convergence of

z\mapsto\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}z^{n_{j}};

(6)

which has radius at least
x^{1-\varepsilon}
, by assumption on the decay of
\{\alpha_{i}\}
.

An elementary argument (see
[
11
, pp.201–2]
) shows that
z=x
is not only a simple root of (
6
) with residue
1/H
(see the statement of Lemmas 4 or 5), but it is also the only root of (
6
) in the closed disk
\{|z|\leq x\}
.
Therefore,
g
is holomorphic on the open disk of radius
R^{\ast}>x
, where
R^{\ast}
is the absolute value of the next smallest root of (
6
), or equal
x^{1-\varepsilon}
if no other root exists.
∎

Example 10
.

In certain nice cases, one can say more. For the simplest infinite example,
\alpha_{n}=2^{-n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
),
g(z)\equiv 1/2
is constant.

The final stage of the proofs of Theorems 2 and 3 is similar to that of Theorem 1, but the method of splitting up a general interval needs a little more care. We will only prove the case that none of the

Proof of Theorem 2.

For simplicity, consider the interval

, for fixed

For

and, for

\displaystyle V_{n}

\displaystyle:=\big\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\ast i\in\mathcal{I}^{n}\;:\;i\in\mathcal{I},\ T_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\ast i}[0,1)\subset I\text{ but }T_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}[0,1)\not\subset I\big\}

is disjoint, contained in

To say more, it is necessary to give a partial description for

Definition

(Itinerary,

.

This definition has two cases. First suppose that

•

Otherwise, we say that

Returning to the proof: From a similar argument to that in Theorem 1, if

has finite itinerary, then

is empty for all

\displaystyle I_{k}

\displaystyle=\{(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k-1},i)\in\mathcal{I}^{k}\ |\ T_{i_{k}}(0)<T_{i}(0)\}

\displaystyle\subset\{\It_{k}(b)\}\times\mathcal{I}\qquad\text{and similarly,}

\displaystyle I_{n+1}

\displaystyle\subset\{\It(b)\}\times\mathcal{I}.

Otherwise, if

(it is as if

).

Now let

and

. It follows from the nullity of

\displaystyle\mu_{x^{n}}(I)-\|I\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V}\mu_{x^{n}}(T_{\boldsymbol{v}}[0,1))-\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V\\

4.2
Discrepancy estimates in the higher rank case

When the collection
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
is not rank one, we require not only a strict decay property on the
\{\alpha_{j}\}
—as in Theorem 2—, but also a kind of Diophantine condition. To proceed, we need the following definition.

Definition

(
R
-badly approximable)
.

For
R\in[2,\infty)
we say a number
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
is
R
-badly approximable if there exists a
d>0
such that

\forall\,(l,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ s.t.\ l\neq 0,\quad\left|\gamma-\frac{k}{l}\right|>\frac{d}{|l|^{R}}.

Remark
.

Larger values of
R
correspond to more easily approximable numbers:

•

For any
R=2
, the property is equivalent to
\gamma
having bounded continued fraction coefficients. (Such
\gamma
comprise a set of measure 0 containing all quadratic algebraic numbers.)

•

For
R>2
, the property holds Lebesgue almost-everywhere: by Jarnik’s theorem
[
12
, Thm. 10.3]
, the Hausdorff dimension of the complementary set is
2/R<1
.

•

Certain transcendental numbers (e.g.,
Liouville numbers
) do not satisfy the property for any
R
whatsoever.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is not rank one, that there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{1-\varepsilon}<\infty
, and that there is a pair
\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k}\in\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
such that
\log(\alpha_{j})/\log(\alpha_{k})
is
(2+r)
-badly approximable, for some
r\in[0,1/2)
. Then, for all
P\in(0,P^{*})
, there exists a constant
C
such that, for all intervals
I\subset[0,1]
,

\left|\,\mu_{\lambda}(I)-\|I\|\,\right|\leq C\big(-\log(\lambda)\big)^{-P};

where

P^{*}=\frac{1-2r}{8(1+r)}.

The proof of Theorem 3 requires us to consider the Mellin transform,

g(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-z-1}|A_{1/t}|\ \mathrm{d}t,

which has the following explicit form, courtesy of the renewal equation for
|A_{\lambda}|
. Let
\Re
,
\Im
denote the real and imaginary parts of a complex number, respectively.

Lemma 7
.

For
\Re(z)>1
, the Mellin transform

As is well-known, one can obtain asymptotic information about a function from the distribution of poles of its Mellin transform.

In particular, this depends on the zeros of the almost-periodic function

f(z)=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}{\alpha_{j}}^{\!z}-1.

There is a lot we can say straight away. From our assumptions on
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j}
, we have that
z=1
is a simple zero of
f
, and by the triangle inequality, there are no zeros of
f
for
\{\Re(z)>1\}
. Moreover, if there were another zero of

on the line
\{\Re(z)=1\}
, it would follow that
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j}
is rank one, a contradiction.

The role of the poles of the Mellin transform is illustrated in the proof of the following result.

Proposition 1
.

For any given collection of positive numbers
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
which sum to 1 such that
H=-\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\log(\alpha_{j})<\infty

\varepsilon>0
for which

(9)

as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
.

Fix a collection
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}}
as above and assume for contradiction that there is an
\varepsilon>0
for which (
9
) holds. Taking the Mellin transform of this equation yields that

\frac{1}{zf(z)}=\frac{1}{H(z-1)}+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-z-\varepsilon}\mathcal{O}(1)\ \mathrm{d}t.

Since this second integral converges absolutely for all

\Re(z)>1-\varepsilon
, we see that the left hand side has a meromorphic extension to the half plane
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\;:\;\Re(z)>1-\varepsilon\}

with only one pole at

. To obtain a contradiction, we provide a sequence of zeros

with

[

It can quickly be seen that

is almost-periodic, by
[
7
, Cor. to Thm. 3.12]
, and is bounded on

since, in this half-plane,

Also, since

, the definition of almost-periodicity provides a sequence of positive numbers
(y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
for which

as

In other words, the holomorphic functions

Furthermore, fixing any index

This thus provides us with a sequence of zeros of

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:\lambda/\alpha_{\It_{n-1}(b)}\leq\alpha_{i}<\lambda/\alpha_{\It_{n}(b)}\}\big|

\displaystyle=\phantom{\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}}\big|\{i\in\mathcal{I}:\lambda\leq\alpha_{i}\}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\alpha_{i}^{1-\varepsilon}\;\lambda^{\varepsilon-1}.

Thus, the second sum of (
14
) is bounded by a multiple of
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
. The finite itinerary case is similar, involving a finite sum in
n
.

It remains to bound the first sum of (
14
), using the asymptotics for
|A_{\lambda/\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}}|
provided by the previous lemma. One finds that there exists
C,C^{\prime}
such that, for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V
with
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\geq\lambda/\alpha_{\max}
,

\left|\frac{|X_{\lambda/\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}}|}{|X_{\lambda}|}-\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right|\leq C\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\left((-\log(\lambda/\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}))^{-P}\right)\leq C^{\prime}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{v}}\left((-\log(\lambda))^{-P}\right),

the last inequality following from the fact that
x\mapsto\log(\lambda)/\log(\lambda/x)
is uniformly bounded on
[\alpha_{\max},\infty)
. Summing over
{\boldsymbol{v}}
gives the required estimate.
∎
