Abstract
. The Black-Scholes-Merton model is a mathematical model for the dynamics of a financial market that includes derivative investment instruments, and its formula provides a theoretical price estimate of European-style options. The model’s fundamental idea is to eliminate risk by hedging the option by purchasing and selling the underlying asset in a specific way, that is, to replicate the payoff of the option with a portfolio (which continuously trades the underlying) whose value at each time can be verified. One of the most crucial, yet restrictive, assumptions for this task is that the market follows a geometric Brownian motion, which has been relaxed and generalized in various ways. The concept of robust finance revolves around developing models that account for uncertainties and variations in financial markets. Martingale Optimal Transport, which is an adaptation of the Optimal Transport theory to the robust financial framework, is one of the most prominent directions. In this paper, we consider market models with arbitrarily many underlying assets whose values are observed over arbitrarily many time periods, and demonstrates the existence of a portfolio sub- or super-hedging a general path-dependent derivative security in terms of trading European options and underlyings, as well as the portfolio replicating the derivative payoff when the market model yields the extremal price of the derivative given marginal distributions of the underlyings. In mathematical terms, this paper resolves the question of dual attainment for the multi-period vectorial martingale optimal transport problem.



Keywords: Robust finance, Hedging, Martingale, Optimal transport, Duality, Dual attainment, Multi-period, Infinite-dimensional linear programming

Introduction

The Black-Scholes-Merton model, often referred to as the Black-Scholes model, is a mathematical framework for calculating the theoretical pricing of options and other derivatives
[
6
]
. It was developed by economists Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in collaboration with mathematician Robert C. Merton in the early 1970s.

The Black-Scholes-Merton model revolutionized the way options are priced in financial markets by providing a mathematical formula to determine the fair value of options based on various factors, and their pricing model has become a fundamental tool in options trading and valuation. The Black-Scholes-Merton model combines concepts from mathematics, statistics, and economics to provide a rigorous framework for understanding option pricing and risk management. As a result, it influenced subsequent research in financial economics and inspired the development of more sophisticated models and techniques

The idea of replication, also known as the principle of no-arbitrage, is arguably the most fundamental concept underlying the Black-Scholes-Merton model. It states that the value of an option can be recreated by establishing a risk-neutral portfolio of the underlying asset in such a way that the portfolio replicates the option’s cash flows and payoffs. This idea has influenced the development of the efficient market hypothesis and has had a profound impact on the study of financial economics.

Despite its importance, it is worth noting that the Black-Scholes-Merton model has certain limitations, such as the assumption of the market following a geometric Brownian Motion and the assumption of continuous trading. These assumptions may not hold in real-world markets, and there have been subsequent models and refinements that address some of these limitations

The concept of robust finance revolves around the development of models and approaches that account for market uncertainties. It recognizes the limitations of traditional financial models, which presume exact and accurate information about the market and its underlying assets, and instead attempts to construct frameworks that can adapt to the inherent uncertainties and risks in real-world financial systems

One of the most renowned and prospering directions is the theory of Martingale Optimal Transport (MOT), which is an adaptation of the Optimal Transport (OT) theory to the robust financial framework. The OT theory, also known as the theory of Monge-Kantorovich transportation, is a mathematical framework that deals with the problem of efficiently moving mass from one distribution to another. OT theory seeks to find the optimal way to transport one distribution of mass to another while minimizing the cost or distance associated with the transportation. The theory originated in the 18th century with the works of mathematicians Gaspard Monge and Leonid Kantorovich, and has since been developed and extended by various researchers. The importance of OT theory lies in its wide range of applications across various disciplines, including mathematics, statistics, economics, physics and computer science

By combining OT theory with the concept of martingales, MOT extends the applicability of optimal transportation models to dynamic and stochastic settings. It provides a valuable tool for understanding and solving transportation problems under uncertainty, with applications in various fields including finance, risk management, stochastic control, and data analysis

One of the most prominent applications of MOT is in mathematical finance and option pricing. It provides a framework for modeling and valuing derivative securities in the presence of uncertainty and stochastic dynamics. MOT-based approaches can be used to analyze optimal hedging strategies and calculate prices and risk measures for options and other financial derivatives

The OT and MOT are infinite-dimensional linear programming problems, hence the problem has a dual programming problem. In MOT, the dual problem has an important interpretation in terms of determining the best sub- or super-hedging portfolio against a derivative security payout liability. As a result, one of the most significant concerns in MOT theory has been the topic of
dual attainment
, which refers to whether the dual problem attains an appropriate solution
[
4
,
5
,
12
,

.

While the dual attainment has been well established to be affirmative in OT, researchers discovered that the added term reflecting trading strategy appearing in the dual of MOT makes establishing the dual attainment for MOT theory far more subtle. Specifically, unlike the OT theory, the dual attainment in MOT is highly sensitive to the spatial dimension, which represents the number of assets in the financial market. Because many derivative instruments are traded in the market and their payoffs are dependent on the values of various underlying assets, it is critical to understand the dual problem and its solution in a multidimensional context, i.e., a market with multiple assets as well as derivatives depending on them. As a result, efforts have been made to comprehend the dual attainment in the higher-dimensional case
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
32
,
47
,
51
]
, but a thorough knowledge of this question appears to be far from its completion. In particular, to the best of the author’s knowledge, all research articles on the dual attainment of multidimensional MOT assume two future maturities. In OT, this means that there are two distributions for which we attempt to move mass efficiently from one to the other. While this is a natural and sufficiently general setting in OT, it represents a significant restriction in MOT because it indicates that the reward of the derivative security under consideration can only depend on the values of the underlyings at two future maturities. It is desirable, both theoretically and practically, to investigate the martingale transport theory of price path-dependent derivative instruments whose reward can depend on arbitrarily many assets and maturities.

The goal of this paper is to establish the dual attainment of the martingale optimal transport problem over an arbitrary number of time periods and assets. We show that there exists a portfolio sub- or super-hedging a path-dependent derivative security in terms of trading European options and underlyings, such that the portfolio replicates the derivative payoff when the market model yields the derivative’s extremal price given marginal distributions of the underlyings. We contend this is fundamental and relevant given that the price path of many underlying assets over time frequently affects financial instruments and their payouts.

2.
Martingale transport problem with multi assets and periods

Consider the asset price processes
(X_{t,i})_{t\geq 0}
in the market indexed by
i\in[d]:=\{1,2,...,d\}
representing the
i
th underlying asset. We will not assume in this paper that the joint probability law of the underlyings, often called as the market model, is known, because we cannot determine this joint law from market information. On the other hand, using a standard reasoning by Breeden and Litzenberger
[
8
]
, we will suppose that the market can witness the distribution of each price at each fixed maturity
t>0
, denoted by
{\rm Law}(X_{t,i})\in{\cal P}({\mathbb{R}})
, where
{\cal P}({\cal X})
denotes the set of all probability distributions over
{\cal X}
. We consider an arbitrary number of finite maturity times
0<T_{1}<T_{2}<...<T_{N}
, denote
X_{t,i}:=X_{T_{t},i}
and
X_{t}=(X_{t,1},...,X_{t,d})
, and assume that
(X_{t})_{t\in[N]}
is a
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-valued martingale, which is generally assumed in the financial literature through the concept of the risk-neutral probability. Now according to the above consideration, we do not assume that the market model
{\rm Law}(X_{t})_{t}\in{\cal P}({\mathbb{R}}^{Nd})
is known, but only the
Nd
-number of marginal distributions
\mu_{t,i}:={\rm Law}(X_{t,i})\in{\cal P}({\mathbb{R}})
are known and fixed. This leads us to consider the
Vectorial Martingale Optimal Transport
(VMOT) problem: Assume the marginals
(\mu_{t,i})_{t,i}
have finite first moment, write
\mu_{t}=(\mu_{t,1},...,\mu_{t,d})
,
\mu=(\mu_{1},...,\mu_{N})
, and
X=(X_{1},...,X_{N})
. We consider the space of
Vectorial Martingale Transports
from
\mu_{t}
to
\mu_{t+1}
,
t=1,...,N-1
, defined as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\rm VMT}(\mu):=\{\pi\in{\cal P}({\mathbb{R}}^{Nd})\ |\

\displaystyle\pi={\rm Law}(X),\ \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X_{t+1}|X_{t}]=X_{t},

\displaystyle{\rm Law}(X_{t,i})=\mu_{t,i}\,\text{ for all }t\in[N],\,i\in[d]\}.

Given a
cost function

c:{\mathbb{R}}^{Nd}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the VMOT problem as

(2.2)

\displaystyle\max/\,{\rm minimize}\ \ \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(X)]\ \text{ over }\ \pi\in{\rm VMT}(\mu).

In finance, the function
c
is naturally interpreted as a derivative security whose payoff
c(X)
is fully determined at the terminal maturity
T_{N}
by the price path
X=(X_{t})_{t}
of the
d
-number of underlyings. In this case,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(X)]
can be regarded as a fair price for the derivative security
c
under the market model
\pi
. Because
\pi
cannot be observed in the market, we must take into account all feasible models
{\rm VMT}(\mu)
which are consistent with the marginal information
\mu=(\mu_{t,i})_{t,i}
. With this knowledge, the maximum and minimum values in (
2.2
) can be interpreted as the upper and lower price bounds for the derivative security
c
, respectively.

The VMOT problem is distinguished from the (ordinary) optimal transport problems by the martingale constrant
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[X_{t+1}|X_{t}]=X_{t}
for all
t=1,...,N-1
, which necessitates that every pair of marginals
\mu_{t,i},\mu_{t+1,i}
must be in
convex order
:

\mu_{t,i}\preceq_{c}\mu_{t+1,i}\ \ \text{if}\ \ \mu_{t,i}(f)\leq\mu_{t+1,i}(f)\ \ \text{for every convex function }f\text{ on }{\mathbb{R}},

where
\mu(f):=\int f(x)\mu(dx)
, in which case
{\rm VMT}(\mu)\neq\emptyset
and vice versa, as shown by Strassen
[
56
]
. Thus we will assume
\mu_{t,i}\preceq_{c}\mu_{t+1,i}
for all
t\leq N-1
and
i\in[d]
.

The VMOT problem belongs to the class of
infinite-dimensional linear programming
, hence the problem has its
dual programming
problem. When the primal problem (
2.2
) is a minimization problem, its dual problem is given by

(2.3)

\sup_{(\varphi,h)\in\Psi}\mu(\varphi),

where
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{N})
,
\varphi_{t}=(\varphi_{t,1},...,\varphi_{t,d})
,
\varphi_{t,i}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
, and
\mu(\varphi):=\sum_{t,i}\mu_{t,i}(\varphi_{t,i})
. Meanwhile,
h=(h_{1},...,h_{N})
with the convention
h_{N}\equiv 0
,
h_{t}=(h_{t,1},...,h_{t,d})
where
h_{t,i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{td}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a function of
(X_{1},...,X_{t})
. Finally,
(\varphi,h)\in\Psi
means that
\varphi_{t,i}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t,i})
,
h_{t,i}
is bounded, and the following inequality holds:

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{t,i}(x_{t,i})+h_{t,i}(x_{1},...,x_{t})(x_{t+1,i}-x_{t,i})\leq c(x),

where
x=(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Nd}
,
x_{t}=(x_{t,1},...,x_{t,d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
represents a price path. Note that

(2.5)

\mu(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{t,i}(X_{t,i})+h_{t,i}(X_{1},...,X_{t})(X_{t+1,i}-X_{t,i})\bigg]

for any
\pi\in{\rm VMT}(\mu)
and
(\varphi,h)\in\Psi
, since
\pi
has marginals
\{\mu_{t,i}\}_{t,i}
and the martingale property of
\pi
implies
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[h_{t,i}(X_{1},...,X_{t})(X_{t+1,i}-X_{t,i})]=0
. Now if (
2.2
) is a maximization problem, its dual problem reads
\inf_{(\varphi,h)\in\Psi}\mu(\varphi)
, with the inequality (
2.4
) reversed (and
\varphi_{t,i}
taking on their values in
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
).

The dual problem also has a significant interpretation and implication in finance. Let us assume that a financial firm is obligated to pay
c(X)
at the terminal maturity
N
for a derivative instrument
c
. To mitigate risk, the firm may consider purchasing or selling European options
\varphi_{t,i}
available from the market, the payoff of which is based solely on the price
X_{t,i}
at the maturity
t
. Furthermore, the company may consider holding
h_{t,i}
shares of the
i
th asset between the
t
and
t+1
maturities, so that its return at time
t+1
is
h_{t,i}(X_{1},...,X_{t})\cdot(X_{t+1}-X_{t})
. It is worth noting that
h_{t,i}
is a function of all underlying prices up to time
t
. Then the left hand side of (
2.4
) represents the payout of the hedging portfolio
(\varphi,h)
, and the inequality (
2.4
) mandates that the position must subhedge the liability for all possible market realizations
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Nd}
. Having stated that, notice the dual of the maximization problem in (
2.2
) represents an optimal superhedging problem.

3.
Duality and our contribution

The celebrated
duality
result asserts, under a mild assumption on
c
and the marginals, that the primal and dual optimal values coincide (see e.g.
[

58

(3.1)

\displaystyle P(c):

\displaystyle=\inf_{\pi\in{\rm VMT}(\mu)}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(X)]

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\varphi,h)\in\Psi}\mu(\varphi)=:D(c).

In addition, the primal problem is known to be attained; there exist an optimizer (VMOT)
\pi
that yields
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(X)]=P(c)
. Observe the VMOTs describe extreme market movements in the sense of maximizing or minimizing the fair price of
c
.

Unlike the primal problem, whose attainment can be easily established by standard arguments under a mild condition on
c
,

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\mu_{t,i})_{t\in[N]}
be an irreducible sequence of marginals on
{\mathbb{R}}
for each
i\in[d]
. Let
c(x)
be a lower semi-continuous cost satisfying
|c(x)|\leq\sum_{t,i}v_{t,i}(x_{t,i})
for some continuous functions
v_{t,i}\in L^{1}(\mu_{t,i})
. Then there exists a dual optimizer, that is a pair of function sequences
(\varphi,h)=(\varphi_{t,i},h_{t,i})_{t\in[N],i\in[d]}
that satisfies (
2.4
) tightly in the following pathwise sense (but needs not be in
\Psi
):

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{t,i}(x_{t,i})+h_{t,i}(x_{1},...,x_{t})(x_{t+1,i}-x_{t,i})=c(x)\quad\pi-a.s.

for every
VMOT

\pi
which solves the minimization problem in (
2.2
).

The
(\varphi,h)
presented in the theorem is known as a
dual maximizer
since it is a solution concept to the problem (
2.3
), which is dual to the primal minimization problem. The equality (
3.2
) indicates that the portfolio composed of a dual maximizer replicates the derivative security
c
by yielding the same payout for all feasible price paths
x
given by any VMOT
\pi
minimizing the price
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(X)]
. And (
2.4
) shows the portfolio otherwise subhedges the derivative
c
for all price paths. Theorem
3.1
also yields the existence of a dual minimizer such that the portfolio built of a dual minimizer superhedges an upper semi-continuous
c
by reversing the inequality in (
2.4
), and it replicates
c
with respect to the primal maximizers.

We emphasize that a dual optimizer does not necessarily belong to
\Psi
. Studies showed the dual problem (
2.3
) is generally not attained within the class
\Psi
even when
(d,N)=(1,2)
(see
[
3
,
5
]
), unless

satisfies a specific regularity property
[
4
]
. Although
\Psi
can be seen as a natural domain for the dual problem, it is rather “narrow” as it lacks suitable compactness due to its infinite dimensionality. This implies that establishing dual attainment is substantially more involved than establishing duality (
3.1
), which can usually be derived via standard argument in functional analysis and variational calculus. In summary, a dual optimizer
(\varphi,h)
needs not be in
\Psi

is real-valued

is also real valued. Since the marginal distributions

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})+h_{t,i}(\bar{x}_{t})\Delta x_{t,i}\leq c(x)\leq 0,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mu(\varphi_{n}):=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int\varphi_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})\,d\mu_{t,i}(x_{t,i})\nearrow P(c)\ \ \text{as}\ \ n\to\infty,

where
\bar{x}_{t}=(x_{1},...,x_{t})
,
\Delta x_{t,i}=x_{t+1,i}-x_{t,i}
and
\Delta x_{t}=x_{t+1}-x_{t}
. Denote
\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varphi_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})
,
h_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t})=\big(h_{t,1,n}(\bar{x}_{t}),...,h_{t,d,n}(\bar{x}_{t})\big)
. Define

(4.3)

\displaystyle\chi_{t,n}(x_{t}):=\sup_{x_{1},...,x_{t-1}}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\big(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}(x_{s})+h_{s,n}(\bar{x}_{s})\cdot\Delta x_{s}\big)

with the convention
\chi_{1,n}=\chi_{N+1,n}\equiv 0
. Notice
\chi_{t,n}
is a convex function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, since it is a supremum of affine functions of
x_{t}
. We now show

(4.4)

\displaystyle\chi_{t,n}\leq\chi_{t+1,n}-\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\ \text{ for all }t\in[N]\text{ and }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

This inequality can be shown as follows:

\displaystyle\chi_{t+1,n}(x_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1},...,x_{t}}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\big(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}(x_{s})+h_{s,n}(\bar{x}_{s})\cdot\Delta x_{s}\big)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},...,x_{t-1}\\
x_{t}=x_{t+1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\big(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}(x_{s})+h_{s,n}(\bar{x}_{s})\cdot\Delta x_{s}\big)+\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\chi_{t,n}(x_{t+1})+\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t+1}).

We can now establish the following crucial bound

(4.5)

\displaystyle\int\chi_{t,n}\,d(\mu_{t}^{\otimes}-\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes})\leq C\ \text{ for all }t=2,...,N\text{ and }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

To see this, by repeated application of (
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle\mu_{t}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t,n})

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{t}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t+1,n})-\mu_{t}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{t+1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t+1,n})-\mu_{t}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{t+1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t+2,n})-\mu_{t+1}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t+1,n}^{\oplus})-\mu_{t}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\leq\dots\leq-\sum_{s=t}^{N}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}),

where the second inequality is due to
\mu_{t}^{\otimes}\preceq_{c}\mu_{t+1}^{\otimes}
and convexity of
\chi
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t,n})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t-1,n})+\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t-1,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t-2}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t-1,n})+\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t-1,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{t-2}^{\otimes}(\chi_{t-2,n})+\mu_{t-2}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t-2,n}^{\oplus})+\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{t-1,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\dots\geq\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}).

The two inequalities combine to give

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int\chi_{t,n}\,d(\mu_{t}^{\otimes}-\mu_{t-1}^{\otimes})\leq-\sum_{s=1}^{N}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})=-\mu(\varphi_{n})

which, in conjunction with (
4.2
), yields (
4.5
).

From (
4.5
), we can obtain local uniform boundedness of
\{\chi_{t,n}\}_{n}
via Proposition
4.1
. We need to meet the proposition’s second condition. For this, fix any
a\in I_{1}
, and let
L_{2,n}
be an affine functions satisfying
L_{2,n}\leq\chi_{2,n}
and
L_{2,n}(a)=\chi_{2,n}(a)
. By linearity,
L_{2,n}(x_{2})=\nabla L_{2,n}(x_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})+L_{2,n}(x_{1})
, which allows us to modify (
4.1
) by replacing
\varphi_{1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{1})
with
\varphi_{1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{1})-L_{2,n}(x_{1})
,
\varphi_{2,n}^{\oplus}(x_{2})
with
\varphi_{2,n}^{\oplus}(x_{2})+L_{2,n}(x_{2})
, and
h_{1,n}(x_{1})
with
h_{1,n}(x_{1})-\nabla L_{2,n}(x_{1})
(
\nabla L_{2,n}
is constant and does not depend on
x_{1}
). Notice this yields
\chi_{2,n}\geq 0
and
\chi_{2,n}(a)=0
. We can continue subtracting appropriate linear functions
L_{t,n}
,
t=2,...,N
, and obtain

(4.7)

\displaystyle\chi_{t,n}\geq 0\ \text{ and }\ \chi_{t,n}(a)=0\quad\text{for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\text{ and }t\in[N].

Note that the modifications have no effect on the value
\mu(\varphi_{n})
.

To quantitatively state the local bound of
\chi
, let
\{\epsilon_{k}\}_{k}
be a positive decreasing sequence tending to zero as
k\to\infty
, and write
I_{t,i}=]a_{t,i},b_{t,i}[
where
-\infty\leq a_{t,i}<b_{t,i}\leq+\infty
. Then we define the compact interval
J_{t,i,k}:=[c_{t,i,k},d_{t,i,k}]
for
t\in[N-1]
,
i\in[d]
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\text{If }a_{t,i}>-\infty,\text{ then define $c_{t,i,k}$ by:}

\displaystyle\ \mu_{t+1,i}(a_{t,i})=0\Rightarrow c_{t,i,k}:=a_{t,i}+\epsilon_{k};\ \ \mu_{t+1,i}(a_{t,i})>0\Rightarrow c_{t,i,k}:=a_{t,i};

\displaystyle\text{If }b_{t,i}<+\infty,\text{ then define $d_{t,i,k}$ by:}

\displaystyle\ \mu_{t+1,i}(b_{t,i})=0\Rightarrow d_{t,i,k}:=b_{t,i}-\epsilon_{k};\ \ \mu_{t+1,i}(b_{t,i})>0\Rightarrow d_{t,i,k}:=b_{t,i};

\displaystyle\text{If }a_{t,i}=-\infty,\text{ then }c_{t,i,k}:=-1/\epsilon_{k};

\displaystyle\text{If }b_{t,i}=+\infty,\text{ then }d_{t,i,k}:=+1/\epsilon_{k}.

Set
J_{0,i,k}:=J_{1,i,k}
. For example, if
\mu_{t+1,i}(a_{t,i})=0
and
\mu_{t+1,i}(b_{t,i})>0
, then
J_{t,i,k}=[a_{t,i}+\epsilon_{k},b_{t,i}]
. Let
\epsilon_{1}
be so small so that
\mu_{t,i}(J_{t,i,1})>0
,
\mu_{t+1,i}(J_{t,i,1})>0
for every
t,i
. Observe that
J_{t,i,k}\nearrow J_{t,i}
as
k\to\infty
. Let
J_{t,k}:=J_{t,1,k}\times J_{t,2,k}\times...\times J_{t,d,k}
.
Then by Proposition
4.1
, we deduce there exists
M_{k}\geq 0
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(4.8)

\displaystyle 0\leq\sup_{n}\chi_{t,n}\leq M_{k}\ \ \text{in}\ \,J_{t-1,k}.

Step 2.
Given an approximating dual maximizer
(\varphi_{n},h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
(where we recall that each of
\varphi_{n}=(\varphi_{t,i,n})_{t,i}
and
h_{n}=(h_{t,i,n})_{t,i}
is in
{\mathbb{R}}^{Nd}
), our goal is to show pointwise convergence of
\varphi_{t,i,n}
to some function
\varphi_{t,i}

\mu_{t,i}
-a.s. as
n\to\infty
, where
\varphi_{t,i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{-\infty\}
is
\mu_{t,i}
-a.s. finite.
But as noted in
[
47
]
, there is an obstacle for the convergence when
d\geq 2
, that is, in the duality relation (
4.1
) one can always replace
(\varphi_{t,i,n})_{t,i}
by
(\varphi_{t,i,n}+C_{t,i,n})_{t,i}
for any constants
C_{t,i,n}
satisfying

\sum_{t,i}C_{t,i,n}=0
. This implies that the convergence cannot hold for any approximating dual maximizer. In view of this, our goal is to show that there exists a suitable approximating dual maximizer that yields the almost sure convergence.

To this end, take any approximating dual maximizer
(\varphi_{n},h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfying (
4.1
), (
4.2
). By repeatedly applying (
4.4
), we deduce

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\geq-\sum_{s=1}^{N}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{N}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N,n})-\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N,n})-\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle=\,\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N,n})-\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N-1,n})+\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N-1,n})-\sum_{s=1}^{N-1}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle=\,\parallel\chi_{N,n}-\chi_{N-1,n}-\varphi_{N-1,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes})}+\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N-1,n})-\sum_{s=1}^{N-2}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\,\parallel\chi_{N,n}-\chi_{N-1,n}-\varphi_{N-1,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{N-1}^{\otimes})}+\mu_{N-2}^{\otimes}(\chi_{N-1,n})-\sum_{s=1}^{N-2}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})

\displaystyle\geq\dots\geq\sum_{s=2}^{N}\parallel\chi_{s,n}-\chi_{s-1,n}-\varphi_{s-1,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{s-1}^{\otimes})}

where the third and sixth inequality is due to the convexity of
\chi
with
\mu_{t}^{\otimes}\preceq_{c}\mu_{t+1}^{\otimes}
, and the fifth equality is by the nonnegativity
\chi_{t,n}-\chi_{t-1,n}-\varphi_{t-1,n}^{\oplus}\geq 0
from (
4.4
). On the other hand, the nonpositivity (
4.1
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}(x_{s})+h_{s,n}(\bar{x}_{s})\cdot\Delta x_{s}\big)\leq\chi_{N,n}(x_{N})+\varphi^{\oplus}_{N,n}(x_{N})\leq 0

where the first inequality follows by taking supremum over
x_{1},...,x_{N-1}
(recall (
4.3
) and
h_{N,n}\equiv 0
). Integrating with any
\pi\in{\rm VMT}(\mu)
yields
\parallel\chi_{N,n}(x_{N})+\varphi^{\oplus}_{N,n}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{N}^{\otimes})}\leq-\sum_{s=1}^{N}\mu_{s}^{\otimes}(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus})\leq C
. We thus conclude

(4.9)

\parallel\chi_{t+1,n}-\chi_{t,n}-\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{t}^{\otimes})}\,\leq C\ \text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}},t\in[N].

This uniform
L^{1}
bound, in conjunction with the local uniform bound (
4.8
) and Komlós compactness theorem, can imply the desired almost sure convergence that we now present. For this, we will extend the argument given in
[
47
]
for two-period case
N=2
into arbitrary
N\geq 2
. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\mu_{t,i,k}
be the restriction of
\mu_{t,i}
on
J_{t-1,i,k}
(where
J_{0,i,k}:=J_{1,i,k}
) then normalized to be a probability distribution. Let
\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k}=\otimes_{i}\mu_{t,i,k}
, so that
\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k}(J_{t,k})=1
where
J_{t,k}:=\otimes_{i}J_{t,i,k}
. Define

\displaystyle v_{t,i,k,n}:=\int\varphi_{t,i,n}\,d\mu_{t,i,k},\quad t\in[N],i\in[d],k\in{\mathbb{N}},n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we will show that there exists
C=C(k)
such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\parallel\varphi_{t,i,n}-v_{t,i,k,n}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{t,i,k})}\,\leq C.

To see this, observe that (
4.8
), (
4.9
) and the fact
J_{t-1,k}\subseteq J_{t,k}
imply

\displaystyle\parallel\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k})}

\displaystyle\leq\,\parallel\chi_{t+1,n}-\chi_{t,n}-\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k})}+\parallel\chi_{t,n}-\chi_{t+1,n}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k})}

\displaystyle\leq C+M_{k}=:C

where we used
\chi_{t+1,n}-\chi_{t,n}-\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\geq 0
to get the bound
\parallel\chi_{t+1,n}-\chi_{t,n}-\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k})}\,\leq C
from (
4.9
). From this, we obtain the bound

(4.11)

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{t,i,k,n}\bigg|\leq\,\parallel\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k})}\,\leq C\,\text{ for all }n,

where the first inequality is by Jensen’s inequality.
Next, because
\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\leq M_{k}
on
J_{t-1,k}
by (
4.4
) and (
4.8
), by taking supremum, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sup_{x_{t,i}\in J_{t,i,k}}\varphi_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})\leq M_{k}\,\text{ for all }n,

and note that clearly
v_{t,i,k,n}\leq\sup_{x_{t,i}\in J_{t,i,k}}\varphi_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})
, so in particular,

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{t,1}\in J_{t,1,k}}\varphi_{t,1,n}(x_{t,1})+\sum_{i=2}^{d}v_{t,i,k,n}\leq M_{k}.

Define
\hat{v}_{t,1,k,n}:=-\sum_{i=2}^{d}v_{t,i,k,n}
. Since
\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\leq M_{k}
on
J_{t-1,k}
, we have

\displaystyle C\geq\,\,\parallel M_{k}-\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu^{\otimes}_{t,k})}\,=M_{k}-\int(\varphi_{t,1,n}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}v_{t,i,k,n})d\mu_{t,1,k}.

This implies that
\sup_{n}\parallel\varphi_{t,1,n}-\hat{v}_{t,1,k,n}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{t,1,k})}
is bounded, and then by (
4.11
),
\sup_{n}\parallel\varphi_{t,1,n}-v_{t,1,k,n}\parallel_{L^{1}(\mu_{t,1,k})}
is bounded. This yields (
4.10
).

We can now apply the Komlós lemma, which states that every
L^{1}
-bounded sequence of real functions contains a subsequence such that the arithmetic means of all its subsequences converge pointwise almost everywhere.

Let
\tilde{v}_{t,1,k,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\hat{v}_{t,1,k,m}
, and
\tilde{v}_{t,i,k,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}v_{t,i,k,m}
for
i\geq 2
.
Now for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a repeated application of Komlós lemma yields that there exists a subsequence
\{\varphi_{t,i,k,n}\}_{n}
of
\{\varphi_{t,i,n}\}_{n}
, such that

(i)
\{\varphi_{t,i,k+1,n}\}_{n}
is a further subsequence of
\{\varphi_{t,i,k,n}\}_{n}
, and

(ii)
\tilde{\varphi}_{t,i,k,n}(x_{t,i})-\tilde{v}_{t,i,k,n}
converges
\mu_{t,i,k}
- a.s.   as
n\to\infty
,

where
\tilde{\varphi}_{t,i,k,n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\varphi_{t,i,k,m}
. Note that for each
k
, our choice of a subsequence index can be made identical for every
t
and
i
, since there are finitely many indices of
t,i
. Then we select the diagonal sequence

\Phi_{t,i,n}:=\varphi_{t,i,n,n}

and again define

\displaystyle w_{t,i,k,n}

\displaystyle=\int\Phi_{t,i,n}d\mu_{t,i,k},\quad 2\leq i\leq d,

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{t,1,k,n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=2}^{d}w_{t,i,k,n},

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\Phi_{t,i,m}(x_{t,i}),

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{t,1,k,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{t,1,k,m},

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{t,i,k,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m=1}^{n}w_{t,i,k,m},\quad 2\leq i\leq d.

We finally claim that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})-\tilde{w}_{t,i,1,n}\,\text{converges }\,\mu_{t,i}-a.s.\,\text{ for all }t\in[N],i\in[d].

Note that the dependence on
k
has now been removed. To prove the claim, since
\{\Phi_{t,i,n}\}_{n}
is a subsequence of
\{\varphi_{t,i,k,n}\}_{n}
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, Komlós lemma implies

(4.13)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})-\tilde{w}_{t,i,k,n}\ \text{converges }\,\mu_{t,i,k}-a.s.\,\text{ for all }t\text{ and }i.

In particular, both
\{\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})-\tilde{w}_{t,i,1,n}\}_{n}
and
\{\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})-\tilde{w}_{t,i,k,n}\}_{n}
converge
\mu_{t,i,1}
- a.s. as
n\to\infty
, hence their difference
\{\tilde{w}_{t,i,1,n}-\tilde{w}_{t,i,k,n}\}_{n}
also converges for any fixed
k
. With (
4.13
), this implies (
4.12
). Finally, the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\tilde{w}_{t,i,1,n}=0
allows us to replace the approximating dual maximizer
(\varphi_{n},h_{n})_{n}
by
(\psi_{n},\tilde{h}_{n})_{n}
, where
\psi_{t,i,n}:=\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}(x_{t,i})-\tilde{w}_{t,i,1,n}
and
\tilde{h}_{t,i,n}
is a suitable Cesàro mean of a subsequence of
(h_{t,i,n})_{n}
which is chosen consistently with the selection of
\tilde{\Phi}_{t,i,n}
.

Step 3.
We will prove the convergence of
\{\chi_{t,n}\}_{n}
defined in (
4.3
). Let
(\varphi_{n},h_{n})_{n}
be an approximating dual maximizer such that

(4.14)

\{\varphi_{t,i,n}\}_{n}\text{ converges to a function }\varphi_{t,i}\ \mu_{t,i}-a.s.\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Now it is unclear that those nice properties
\chi_{t,n}
enjoyed in Step 2, e.g., (
4.7
) or (
4.8
), keep holding, because
(\varphi_{n},h_{n})_{n}
that we now consider is a Cesàro mean of a subsequence of an approximating dual maximizer. But notice that this implies at least the upper bound in (
4.8
) continues to hold, that is,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\chi_{t,n}\leq M_{k}\ \ \text{on}\ \ J_{t-1,k}.

We will show that the normalization of
\chi_{t,n}
(
4.7
) can be restored. Fix any
a\in I_{1}
as in Step 2, and define
\varphi^{\oplus}_{t}:=\sum_{i}\varphi_{t,i}
. First, (
4.14
) implies that there exists
a_{t}\in I_{t}
for every

such that

(4.16)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(a_{t})=\varphi_{t}^{\oplus}(a_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}.

In view of (
4.4

\varphi^{\oplus}_{1,n}\leq\chi_{2,n}
, this implies

(4.17)

\inf_{n}\chi_{2,n}(a_{1})>-\infty.

On the other hand, since
I_{1}={\rm int}(J_{1})

J_{1,k}\nearrow J_{1}
, for large enough

\{a,a_{1}\}\subseteq{\rm int}(J_{1,k})
. Now (

\chi_{2,n}(a)

\nabla\chi_{2,n}(a)

\nabla\chi_{2,n}(a)\in\partial\chi_{2,n}(a)

\chi_{2,n}

\{\chi_{2,n}(a)\}_{n}

\{\nabla\chi_{2,n}(a)\}_{n}

, and replace
\varphi_{1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{1})

\varphi_{1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{1})-L_{2,n}(x_{1})

\varphi_{2,n}^{\oplus}(x_{2})

\varphi_{2,n}^{\oplus}(x_{2})+L_{2,n}(x_{2})
, and finally

. This yields
\chi_{2,n}(a)=\nabla\chi_{2,n}(a)=0

\chi_{3,n}\geq\chi_{2,n}+\varphi^{\oplus}_{2,n}

\varphi_{t,i,n}
and the bound (
4.15
) are still retained. Now (
4.5

\lim_{n\to\infty}\chi_{t,n}=\chi_{t}

J_{t-1}
.

Step 4.
We have obtained the almost sure limit functions
(\varphi_{t,i})_{t,i}
. We may define
\varphi_{t,i}:=-\infty
on a
\mu_{t,i}
-null set which includes
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus I_{t}
, so that they are defined everywhere on
{\mathbb{R}}
. We will now show there exist functions
h_{t}=(h_{t,i})_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{td}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for all
t\in[N]
with
h_{N}\equiv 0
, such that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{t}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)\leq c(x)\ \ \text{for all }x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Nd}.

For any function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
which is bounded below by an affine function, let
{\rm conv}[f]:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
denote the lower semi-continuous convex envelope of
f

l\leq f
(If there is no such

{\rm conv}[f]\equiv-\infty
.) We will inductively obtain
h_{N-1},h_{N-2},...,h_{1}
. Let us rewrite (
4.1
) as

(4.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\big(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)\leq c(x)-\varphi_{N,n}^{\oplus}(x_{N}).

Define
H_{N-1,n}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N})={\rm conv}[c(\bar{x}_{N-1},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{N,n}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{N})
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N-1}\big(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)\leq H_{N-1,n}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N})\leq c(x)-\varphi_{N,n}^{\oplus}(x_{N})

because the left hand side is affine in
x_{N}
. If we let
x_{N}=x_{N-1}
, we get

(4.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N-2}\big(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)\leq H_{N-1,n}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N-1})-\varphi_{N-1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{N-1}).

Notice (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) have the same structure. With the convention
H_{N,n}(x):=c(x)
,
this allows us to inductively deduce, backward in
t
,

(4.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}\big(\varphi_{s,n}^{\oplus}(x_{s})+h_{s,n}(\bar{x}_{s})\cdot\Delta x_{s}\big)\leq H_{t+1,n}(\bar{x}_{t+1},x_{t+1})-\varphi_{t+1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t+1})

for
t=1,...,N-1
, where

(4.22)

\displaystyle H_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1}):={\rm conv}[H_{t+1,n}(\bar{x}_{t},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{t+1,n}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{t+1})

with an abuse of notation
H_{t+1,n}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1}):=H_{t+1,n}(\bar{x}_{t+1},x_{t+1})
.

Now by dropping
n
, we analogously define

(4.23)

\displaystyle H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1}):={\rm conv}[H_{t+1}(\bar{x}_{t},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{t+1}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{t+1})

with
H_{N}(x):=c(x)
. Next, since the
\limsup
of convex functions is convex, in conjunction with the almost sure convergence, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}H_{N-1,n}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\rm conv}[c(\bar{x}_{N-1},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{N,n}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{N})

\displaystyle\leq{\rm conv}[\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(c(\bar{x}_{N-1},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{N,n}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)\big)](x_{N})

\displaystyle\leq{\rm conv}[c(\bar{x}_{N-1},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{N}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{N})

\displaystyle=H_{N-1}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N}).

This allows us to inductively deduce, for
t=1,...,N-1
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}H_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\rm conv}[H_{t+1,n}(\bar{x}_{t},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{t+1,n}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq{\rm conv}[\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(H_{t+1,n}(\bar{x}_{t},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{t+1,n}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)\big)](x_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq{\rm conv}[H_{t+1}(\bar{x}_{t},\,\cdot\,)-\varphi_{t+1}^{\oplus}(\,\cdot\,)](x_{t+1})

\displaystyle=H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1}).

We need to discuss continuity of the convex function
x_{t+1}\mapsto H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1})
. The following inequality from (
4.23
)

H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1})\leq H_{t+1}(\bar{x}_{t+1},x_{t+1})-\varphi_{t+1}^{\oplus}(x_{t+1})

becomes
H_{N-1}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N})\leq c(x)-\varphi_{N}^{\oplus}(x_{N})
when
t=N-1
. Then the
\mu_{N}^{\otimes}
-a.s. finiteness of
\varphi_{N}^{\oplus}
gives, by convexity,
H_{N-1}(\bar{x}_{N-1},x_{N})<\infty
if
x_{N}\in J_{N-1}
. Backward induction in
t
then gives
H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},x_{t+1})<\infty
if
x_{t+1}\in J_{t}
. This implies that for any
\bar{x}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{td}
, if there exists
y_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},y_{0})>-\infty
, then
y\mapsto H_{t}(\bar{x}_{t},y)
is real-valued thus continuous in
J_{t}
. Now (
4.21
), (
4.22
) gives

\displaystyle\varphi_{1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{1})+h_{1,n}(x_{1})\cdot\Delta x_{1}\leq H_{1,n}(x_{1},x_{2})\leq H_{2,n}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})-\varphi_{2,n}^{\oplus}(x_{2}).

Letting
x_{2}=x_{1}
gives
\varphi_{1,n}^{\oplus}(x_{1})\leq H_{1,n}(x_{1},x_{1})
. From this and the almost sure convergence, taking
\limsup
yields

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{\oplus}(x_{1})\leq H_{1}(x_{1},x_{1})\ \text{ and }\ H_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\leq H_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})-\varphi_{2}^{\oplus}(x_{2}).

Set
A_{t}:=\{x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,|\,\varphi_{t}^{\oplus}(x_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}\}
,
t\in[N]
, and note that
A_{t}\subseteq I_{t}
. Since
x_{2}\mapsto H_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})
is continuous in
J_{1}
for every
x_{1}\in A_{1}
, the subdifferential
\partial H_{1}(x_{1},\,\cdot\,)(x_{2})
is nonempty, convex and compact for every
x_{2}\in I_{1}={\rm int}(J_{1})
. This allows us to choose a measurable function
h_{1}:A_{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfying
h_{1}(x_{1})\in\partial H_{1}(x_{1},\,\cdot\,)(x_{1})
. Then for
x_{1}\in A_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{\oplus}(x_{1})+h_{1}(x_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})

\displaystyle\leq H_{1}(x_{1},x_{1})+h_{1}(x_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})

\displaystyle\leq H_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq H_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})-\varphi_{2}^{\oplus}(x_{2}).

In particular, for
x_{1}\in A_{1}
and
x_{2}\in A_{2}
, it holds
H_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})>-\infty
. Hence again we can choose
h_{2}:A_{1}\times A_{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that satisfies
h_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\in\partial H_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},\,\cdot\,)(x_{2})
. Then for every
x_{1}\in A_{1}
and
x_{2}\in A_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}^{\oplus}(x_{1})+\varphi_{2}^{\oplus}(x_{2})+h_{1}(x_{1})\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})+h_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\cdot(x_{3}-x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq H_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{2})+h_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\cdot(x_{3}-x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq H_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle\leq H_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{3})-\varphi_{3}^{\oplus}(x_{3}).

By induction in
t
, we obtain
h_{t}:A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{t}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(with
h_{N}\equiv 0
), satisfying (
4.18
) as desired. We may define
h_{t}=0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{td}\setminus A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{t}
, noting that the left hand side of (
4.18
) is
-\infty
if
x_{t}\notin A_{t}
for some
t
.

Step 5.

(4.24)

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{t}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)=c(x),\quad\pi^{*}-a.s..

In other words, every minimizer
\pi^{*}
is concentrated on the contact set

whenever

Recall
\varphi_{t,i,n}\to\varphi_{t,i}

\mu_{t,i}

\chi_{t,n}\to\chi_{t}
in

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}

\displaystyle\int\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)d\pi

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t,n}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)d\pi^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\int\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{t}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+h_{t}(\bar{x}_{t})\cdot\Delta x_{t}\big)d\pi^{*}

From this, as

\displaystyle\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}+\chi_{t,n}-\chi_{t+1,n})\,d\pi_{t}\leq\int(\varphi_{t}^{\oplus}+\chi_{t}-\chi_{t+1})\,d\pi_{t},

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}

\displaystyle\int\sum_{t=1}^{N}\big(\varphi_{t,n}^{\oplus}(x_{t})+\chi_{t,n}(x_{t})-\chi_{t+1,n}(x_{t})
