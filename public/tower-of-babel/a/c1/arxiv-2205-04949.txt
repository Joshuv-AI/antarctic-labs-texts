Two-dimensional diffusion orthogonal polynomials ordered by a weighted degree
S. Yu. Orevkov

Abstract
We study the following problem: describe the triplets ( Ω , g , μ ) (\Omega,g,\mu) , μ = ρ ​ d ​ x \mu=\rho\,dx , where g = ( g i ​ j ​ ( x ) ) g=(g^{ij}(x)) is the (co)metric associated with the symmetric second order differential operator 𝕃 ⁡ ( f ) = 1 ρ ​ ∑ i ​ j ∂ i ( g i ​ j ​ ρ ​ ∂ j f ) \mathbb{L}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{ij}\partial_{i}(g^{ij}\rho\partial_{j}f) defined on a domain Ω \Omega of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} and such that there exists an orthonormal basis of L 2 ​ ( μ ) L^{2}(\mu) made of polynomials which are eigenvectors of 𝕃 \mathbb{L} , where the polynomials are ranked according to some weighted degree. In a joint paper with D. Bakry and M. Zani this problem was solved in dimension 2 for the usual degree. In the present paper we solve it still in dimension 2, but for a weighted degree with arbitrary positive weights.

1. Introduction

In [3] the following problem is studied
(see also [1], [2], [5], [6]): describe all triples

(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)
where
\Omega
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{L}
is
an elliptic second order operator of the form

\mathbb{L}(f)=\sum_{i,j}g^{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}f+\sum_{i}b^{i}(x)\partial_{i}f

[formula]

with
g^{ij}
and
b^{i}
continuous
in
\Omega
and
\mu
a probability measure on
\Omega

which is absolutely continuous with respect to the
Lebesgue measure, and such that there exists a polynomial orthogonal basis of

L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
formed by eigenvectors of
\mathbb{L}
, which is also a basis
(in the algebraic sense) of
\mathbb{R}[x]
,
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
. It is clear that
in this case
\mathbb{L}
is symmetric on the space of polynomials, i.e.,

\int_{\Omega}f_{1}\mathbb{L}f_{2}\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}f_{2}\mathbb{L}f_{1}\,d\mu

[formula]

for any two polynomial functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
.

Suppose that
e_{1},e_{2},\dots
is such a basis, and let
V_{n}
be the subspace spanned
by
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
. Then we have an increasing sequence of
\mathbb{L}
-invariant
subspaces
V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset V_{3}\subset\dots
of
\mathbb{R}[x]

whose union is
\mathbb{R}[x]
, and
\mathbb{R}[x]
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
.

Vice versa, given an increasing sequence of finite-dimensional

\mathbb{L}
-invariant subspaces of
\mathbb{R}[x]
whose union is
\mathbb{R}[x]
, one can always choose
an orthogonal polynomial eigenbasis of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
provided
that
\mathbb{L}
is symmetric on polynomials and the space of polynomials
is dense in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
.

It does not seem that this problem can be solved in such generality,
without imposing a condition that the filtration
V_{1}\subset V_{2}\subset\dots

is somewhat natural. So, in [3] the above problem was considered with
an additional condition that for any
n
, the space of polynomials
of degree
\leq n
is invariant under
\mathbb{L}
and thus occurs among the
V_{i}
’s.
It was also assumed in [3] that,
when
\Omega
is not bounded, (2) holds for any pair of
compactly supported functions (for bounded domains the latter condition easily
follows from the symmetry of
\mathbb{L}
on the polynomials combined with the density
of
\mathbb{R}[x]
in
L(\Omega,\mu)
). Under these assumptions,
a complete list of solutions of the above problem is given in [3]
in dimensions
2
. Up to affine transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there is a
one-parameter family of bounded domains and also 18 rigid domains
(10 of them are bounded) for which there exists a solution.

Remark 1.1.

In dimension 1, the only solutions under the aforementioned assumptions are
the classical systems of orthogonal polynomials: Hermite, Laguerre, and Jacobi
polynomials. They are obtained by Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization
process for the measure densities, respectively,

Ce^{-x^{2}/2}
on
\mathbb{R}
,
C_{a}x^{a-1}e^{-x}
with
a>0
on
[0,\infty)
, and

C_{p,q}(1-x)^{p-1}(1+x)^{q-1}
with
p,q>0
on
[-1,1]
(here
C
,
C_{a}
, and

C_{p,q}
are normalizing constants).
The corresponding operators are
\partial^{2}-x\partial
,
x\partial^{2}+(a-x)\partial
,
and
J_{p,q}=(1-x^{2})\partial^{2}-\big((p-q)+(p+q)x\big)\partial
.

It turns out that the filtration by the usual degree is too restrictive in
dimension
d\geq 2
. Several
natural systems of orthogonal polynomial are not covered. However,
they can be obtained by this procedure if one considers a weighted degree
instead (see [1]). As usually, the weighted degree of a polynomial

P=\sum_{k}a_{k}x^{k}
,
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})
, with real positive weights

\mathbb{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
is defined as

\deg_{\mathbb{w}}(P)=\max_{a_{k}\neq 0}(w_{1}k_{1}+\dots+w_{d}k_{d}).

In this paper (in §6),
for any pair of positive weights
\mathbb{w}=(w_{1},w_{2})
, we give a complete list
of two-dimensional solutions satisfying the condition that for any
n
, the set of
polynomials
P
with
\deg_{\mathbb{w}}(P)\leq n
is invariant under
\mathbb{L}
.

Let us give precise definitions. We say that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

is a
natural domain
if it is a connected open set which coincides
with the interior of its closure.

Definition 1.2.
(cf. [1, 3])
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a natural domain,
\mathbb{L}
be an elliptic second
order differential operator of the form (1) with coefficients continuous
in
\Omega
, and
\mu(dx)=\rho(x)\,dx
be a probability measure on
\Omega

such that
\rho
is continuous in
\Omega
and the space of all polynomials is dense
in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
. Let
\mathbb{w}
be a
d
-tuple of positive real numbers.
We say that the triple
(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)

is a solution of the
Diffusion Orthogonal Polynomial Problem with weights
\mathbb{w}

(
\mathbb{w}
-DOP problem for short) if, for any
n
, the space

\{P\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\dots,x_{d}]\mid\deg_{\mathbb{w}}(P)\leq n\}
(considered as a subspace of

L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
) has an orthogonal basis formed by eigenvectors of
\mathbb{L}
.
If in addition the equality (2) holds for any two smooth functions compactly
supported in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we say that
(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)
is a solution of
the
Strong

\mathbb{w}
-DOP problem (
\mathbb{w}
-SDOP problem).

It is shown in
[3, Prop. 2.11] that if
(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)
is a
solution of the
\mathbb{w}
-SDOP problem, then
\mathbb{L}
is determined by the measure
density
\rho
and the cometric
g=(g^{ij})
(the
g^{ij}
are
the coefficients in (1)), namely,

\mathbb{L}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}\Big(g^{ij}\rho\,\partial_{j}f\Big).

[formula]

We shall then speak also
of the triple
(\Omega,g,\rho)
as a solution of the
\mathbb{w}
-SDOP problem.
Notice that (3) is the Laplace-Beltrami operator for the riemannian
metric
(g_{ij})=g^{-1}
in the case when
\rho=(\det g)^{-1/2}
.

As we mentioned above, any solution of the
\mathbb{w}
-DOP problem with a bounded domain

\Omega
is a solution of the
\mathbb{w}
-SDOP problem (see [3, Prop. 2.12]).
It is also shown in [3] that any
solution of SDOP-problem is a solution of a certain algebraic problem formulated
in terms of the metric
g^{ij}
only
(AlgDOP-problem; see §2 below). This fact is proven in [3] for the
usual degree but all the proofs extend without changes to any weighted degree as well.
Then, to find all two-dimensional solutions of the weighted SDOP problem,
we follow the same strategy as in [3]. Namely, we first find
solutions of the algebraic problem over
\mathbb{C}

using some basic properties of plane complex algebraic curves (see §§4–5),
and then we look for
\Omega
and
\rho
(see §6).

All the bounded domains
\Omega
admitting a solution,
appeared already in the literature except, maybe, one
infinite family: the case (B4) with
m\neq n
in Theorem 6.2.
However orthogonal polynomials are being studied so long time in so many aspects
and the literature is so vast that we cannot be sure that this family is really new.

2. Some general facts about solutions of weighted
DOP/SDOP problem in an arbitrary dimension

2.1. The AlgDOP Problem

Let
\mathbb{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})
and let

(\Omega,g,\rho)
be a solution of the

\mathbb{w}
-SDOP problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Let

whose

-weighted degree

Let

is a minimal polynomial vanishing on the boundary of

In particular, if
\Gamma
is not identically zero, then it is

, i.e., it does not have multiple factors.
By convention we set

in the case when

\Omega
is the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.2. Admissible changes of variables

A
\mathbb{w}
-admissible change of variables
is
a bijective polynomial mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

x\mapsto(y_{1}(x),\dots,y_{d}(x))
with
\deg_{\mathbb{w}}(y_{i})=w_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
.
If
d=2
, then
(1,w)
-admissible automorphisms of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for
w=1
are
affine linear transformations, and for
w>1

the form

(x,y)\mapsto(\alpha x+\beta,\,\gamma y+p(x)),\qquad\alpha\gamma\neq 0,\;\deg(p)\leq w.

[formula]

The following proposition is similar to [3, Prop. 2.5]
and we omit its proof.

(a).
Let

be a solution of
\mathbb{w}
-DOP (resp.
\mathbb{w}
-SDOP) problem

-admissible change of variables.

\mathbb{L}_{1}(f)=\mathbb{L}(f\circ\Phi)\circ\Phi^{-1}

\mu_{1}(E)=\mu(\Phi^{-1}(E))

-DOP (resp.

(b). Let

-AlgDOP Problem over

2.3. From AlgDOP to DOP/SDOP

As we told in §2.1, any solution
(\Omega,g,\mu)
of the
\mathbb{w}
-SDOP
problem gives a solution
(\Gamma,g)
of the
\mathbb{w}
-AlgDOP problem where

\Gamma
is a generator of the ideal
I(\partial\Omega)
.
In this subsection we discuss how to find all possible
(\Omega,g,\mu)

from a given
(\Gamma,g)
.

So, let
(\Gamma,g)
be a solution of the
\mathbb{w}
-AlgDOP problem over
\mathbb{R}
.
First of all we find all connected components
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\Gamma=0\}

such that
g
is positive definite on
\Omega
. Then it remains to find all
measure densities
\rho

such that the operator
\mathbb{L}
given by (3) is of the form (1)
with
b^{i}\in P_{\mathbb{w}}(w_{i})
for each
i=1,\dots,d
. By comparing (1) with (3)
we obtain

b^{i}=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}g^{ij}+\sum_{j}g^{ij}\partial_{j}h,\qquad h=\log\rho.

Hence

\partial_{j}h=\sum_{i}g_{ij}L^{i},\qquad L^{i}=b^{i}-\sum_{j}\partial_{j}g^{ij},

[formula]

where
(g_{ij})=g^{-1}
. We have

b^{i}\in P_{\mathbb{w}}(w_{i})\Leftrightarrow L^{i}\in P_{\mathbb{w}}(w_{i})
. The identities

\partial_{k}(\partial_{j}h)=\partial_{j}(\partial_{k}h)
combined with (6) yield

\partial_{k}\Big(\sum_{i}g_{ij}L^{i}\Big)=\partial_{j}\Big(\sum_{i}g_{ik}L^{i}\Big)

[formula]

which is a system of linear equations for the coefficients of all
L^{i}
.
These observations lead to the following algorithm of finding all solutions for
\rho
.
We start with polynomials
L^{i}
,
\deg_{\mathbb{w}}L^{i}=w_{i}
, whose coefficients we compute
by solving the system of linear equations coming from (7).
The solution may depend on several parameters.
Then we find
h
by integrating the
L^{i}
’s and set
\rho=\exp(h)
.
Finally we choose the values of the parameters such that
Q(x)\rho(x)
,
is integrable over
\Omega
for any polynomial
Q

(when
\Omega
is bounded, it is enough to demand that
\int_{\Omega}\rho\,dx<\infty
).

These observations allow us to prove the following fact.

(\Omega,g,\rho)
be a solution of the

\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that

of the set of indices
\{1,\dots,d\}=J_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup J_{m}

Proof

We consider only the case
d=2
.
It is not difficult to derive from it the general case. Let
h=\log\rho
.
It is enough to prove that either
g
is constant, or
\partial_{12}h=0
and then

h=h_{1}(x_{1})+h_{2}(x_{2})
whence
\rho=\rho_{1}(x_{1})\rho_{2}(x_{2})
.
Indeed, the inverse matrix of

\operatorname{diag}(g_{11},g_{22})
where

.
Then by (7) we have

\partial_{12}h
is a polynomial, hence

divides
\partial_{j}L^{i}
for

.
Thus, if

3.3. Local branches of the curve

Let

for any local branch of

if both

The following fact is well-known and immediately follows from the definitions.

c_{02}=-n^{2}
. In (B5) it
is constant if and only if
c_{02}=-\frac{1}{4}n^{2}
.
If
m\neq n
in (B4), the curvature cannot be constant
because there are no biangles of constant curvature with different angles.
For
m=n
in (B4) and for (B5), the curvature is

\frac{-\lambda n^{2}\big(2(n^{2}+\alpha)x^{k}+\alpha\big)}{\big((n^{2}+\alpha)x^{k}-\alpha\big)^{2}},\qquad(k,\lambda,\alpha)=\begin{cases}(2,2,c_{02}),&\text{(B4), $m=n$},\\
(1,\frac{1}{2},4c_{02}),&\text{(B5)},\end{cases}

which is evidently non-constant when
\alpha\neq-n^{2}
(cf. [3, §4.5]).
The similarity of the formulas for the curvature is not occasional:
up to adjusting the constants, (B5) is the image of (B4) through

(x,y)\mapsto(x^{2},y)
.

According to our classification, the only bounded domains which admit solutions
but are not covered by
Table 1 are those in Theorem 6.2(B4) for
m\neq n
.
The simplest one is the nodal cubic
y^{2}=x^{2}-x^{3}
which corresponds to
(m,n)=(1,2)
.
This solution is realized in [3, §4.8] as the image of
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{\mathbb{S}^{3c}}
,

c=1,2,4,8
.
The realization for
c=1
immediately extends to any
(m,n)
as follows
(it could be interesting to do the same for
c=2,4,8
).
We consider
\mathbb{S}^{3}
as the unit sphere in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with coordinates
(z_{1},z_{2})
.
Then the image of
\frac{1}{4}{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
through

(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto(X,Y)=\big(\,|z_{1}|^{2},\,\operatorname{Re}(z_{1}^{n}\bar{z}_{2}^{m})\,\big)

is an operator on the domain bounded by the curve

(1-X)^{m}X^{n}-Y^{2}=0
. Its image through the affine change

(X,Y)\mapsto(x,y)=(\,2X-1,\,2^{(m+n)/2}Y\,)

is the operator corresponding to (B4) with
p=q=\frac{1}{2}
and

c_{02}=-\frac{1}{4}(m+n)^{2}
.
