The reflection complexity of sequences over finite alphabets


Abstract
In combinatorics on words, the well-studied factor complexity function ρ 𝐱 \rho_{\mathbf{x}} of a sequence 𝐱 \mathbf{x} over a finite alphabet counts, for every nonnegative integer n n , the number of distinct length- n n factors of 𝐱 \mathbf{x} . In this paper, we introduce the reflection complexity function r 𝐱 r_{\mathbf{x}} to enumerate the factors occurring in a sequence 𝐱 \mathbf{x} , up to reversing the order of symbols in a word. We prove a number of results about the growth properties of r 𝐱 r_{\mathbf{x}} and its relationship with other complexity functions. We also prove a Morse–Hedlund-type result characterizing eventually periodic sequences in terms of their reflection complexity, and we deduce a characterization of Sturmian sequences. We investigate the reflection complexity of quasi-Sturmian, episturmian, ( s + 1 ) (s+1) -dimensional billiard, complementation-symmetric Rote, and rich sequences. Furthermore, we prove that if 𝐱 \mathbf{x} is k k -automatic, then r 𝐱 r_{\mathbf{x}} is computably k k -regular, and we use the software Walnut to evaluate the reflection complexity of some automatic sequences, such as the Thue–Morse sequence. We note that there are still many unanswered questions about this reflection measure.

Introduction

Given an infinite sequence
\mathbf{x}
over a finite alphabet,
it is natural to study the combinatorial properties of the
factors of
\mathbf{x}
. (The term
factor

refers to a contiguous block occurring in
\mathbf{x}
.) For example, writing
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, many authors have studied the
factor complexity function

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}
,
which maps
n\geq 0
to the number of distinct factors of

\mathbf{x}
of length
n
. Note that
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(0)=1
,
since every—finite or infinite—sequence has a unique
factor of length
0
; namely, the empty word.

Variations on this definition can be considered as a measure of how “complicated” a sequence is. For example, the
abelian complexity function
of
\mathbf{x}
counts the number of factors of
\mathbf{x}
of a given length, where two factors

u
and
v
are considered the same if they have the same length and one is a permutation of the other.
Similarly, the
cyclic complexity function

c_{\mathbf{x}}
, introduced in 2017

[
CassaigneFiciSciortinoZamboni2017
]
, is equal to
the number of length-
n
factors of
\mathbf{x}
, up to equivalence under rotations (cyclic permutations). By analogy, the abelian and cyclic complexity functions
lead us to introduce, in this paper, a
reflection complexity function
on sequences involving reversals.

Our reflection complexity function
r_{\mathbf{x}}
, defined below, does not seem to have been previously studied, but may be thought of as natural in terms of its relationships with automatic
sequences such as the Thue–Morse sequence. To begin with, we require the equivalence relation
\sim_{r}
defined below.

Definition 1
.

Let
m,n
be nonnegative integers.
Given a finite word
u=u(1)u(2)\cdots u(m)
, its
reversal
is the word
u^{R}=u(m)u(m-1)\cdots u(1)
, i.e.,
u^{R}(i)=u(m+1-i)
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
A
palindrome
is a word that is equal to its reversal.
Two finite words
u
and
v
are
reflectively equivalent
if
v=u
or
v=u^{R}
.
We denote this equivalence relation by
u\sim_{r}v
.

Example 2
.

Over the alphabet
\{\text{{\tt a}},\text{{\tt b}},\ldots,\text{{\tt z}}\}
, the English word
reward
is reflectively equivalent to
drawer
, while
deed
,
kayak
, and
level
are palindromes.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence.
The
reflection complexity function

r_{\mathbf{x}}\colon\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}
of
\mathbf{x}
maps every
n\geq 0
to the number of distinct length-
n
factors of
\mathbf{x}
, up to equivalence by
\sim_{r}
.

Example 4
.

Let

\mathbf{t}=011010011001011010010110011010011\cdots

(1)

denote the Thue–Morse sequence,
where
the
n
th term in (
1
)
for
n\geq 1

is defined as the number of
1
’s, modulo
2
,
in the base-2 expansion of
n-1
.
The initial terms of the integer sequence
(r_{\mathbf{t}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
are such that

(r_{\mathbf{t}}(n))_{n\geq 0}=1,2,3,4,6,6,10,10,13,12,16,16,20,20,22,\ldots.

(2)

We see that
r_{\mathbf{t}}(2)=3
, for example,
since there are
3
length-2 factors of
\mathbf{t}
, up to reflection complexity, i.e., the factors
00

and
11
and one member of the equivalence class
\{01,10\}
, with respect to
\sim_{r}
.

The integer sequence in (
2
) was not, prior to this paper, included in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
[
Sloane
]
, which
suggests that our notion of “reflection complexity” is new.
(Now it is present as sequence

A373700
.)
Also see the work of Krawchuk
and Rampersad in
[
KrawchukRampersad2018
]
,
which introduced the notion of

cyclic/reversal complexity
for sequences.
The evaluation of reflection complexity functions is closely related to the work of Rampersad and Shallit
[
RampersadShallit2005
]
,
who investigated sequences
\mathbf{x}
such that all sufficiently long factors

have the property
that
w^{R}
is not a factor of
\mathbf{x}
.
Also, the evaluation of reflection complexities for sequences
is related to the enumeration of palindromes contained in sequences; see, e.g., Fici and Zamboni
[
FiciZamboni2013
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation and definitions needed for the paper.
In Section
3
, we give general results about reflection complexity. In particular, we investigate its growth properties and relationships
with other complexity functions. In Section
4
, we give a graph-theoretic interpretation of reflection-equivalent classes and prove an inequality for reflection complexity. In Sections
5
,

6
, and

7
,
respectively, we

Sturmian sequences and generalizations, and

Next, in Section

We also study reflection complexity for famous automatic sequences such as the Thue–Morse sequence.
Finally, some further research directions and open questions
are considered in Section
.

2
Preliminaries

Generalities.

For a general reference on words, we cite
[
Lothaire1997
]
.
An
alphabet
is a finite set of elements called
letters
.
A
word
over an alphabet
A
is a finite sequence of letters from
A
.
The
length
of a word, denoted between vertical bars, is the number of its letters (counting multiplicities).
The
empty word
is the only
0
-length word, denoted by
\varepsilon
.
For all
n\geq 0
, we let
A^{n}
denote the
set of all length-
n
words over
A
.
We let
A^{*}
denote the set of words over
A
, including the empty word and equipped with the concatenation operation.
In order to distinguish finite words and infinite sequences, we write the latter in bold.
Except for complexity functions, we start indexing words and sequences at
1
, unless otherwise specified.
A
factor
of a word or a sequence is one of its (finite and contiguous) subblocks. A
prefix
(resp.,
s
uffix) is a starting (resp., ending) factor.
Given a word
w
, its
n
th term is written
w(n)
for
1\leq n\leq|w|
.
The factor starting at position
n
and ending at position
m
with
1\leq m\leq n\leq|w|
is written
w[m..n]
.
We let
\Fac_{w}
denote the set of all factors of
w
and, for each natural number
n
, we let
\Fac_{w}(n)
denote the set of all length-
n
factors of
w
.
A factor
u
of a word
w
over
A
is
right
(resp.,
left
)
special
if
ua
and
ub
(resp.,
au
and
bu
) are factors of
w
for some distinct letters
a,b\in A
.
A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is
reversal-closed
if, for every factor
w
of
\mathbf{x}
, the word
w^{R}
is also a factor of
\mathbf{x}
.
A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is
eventually periodic
if there exist finite words
u,v
, with
v
nonempty, such that
\mathbf{x}=uv^{\omega}
where
v^{\omega}=vvv\cdots
denotes the infinite concatenation of
v
.
A sequence that is not eventually periodic is said to be
aperiodic
.
A sequence is said to be
recurrent
if every factor occurs infinitely many times; it is
uniformly recurrent
if each factor occurs with bounded gaps, i.e., for all factors
w
, there is some length
m=m(w)
such that
w
occurs in every length-
m
block.

Morphisms.

Let
A
and
B
be finite alphabets.
A
morphism

f\colon A^{*}\to B^{*}
is a map satisfying
f(uv)=f(u)f(v)
for all
u,v\in A^{*}
.
In particular,
f(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
, and
f
is entirely determined by the images of the letters in
A
.
For an integer
k\geq 2
, a morphism is
k
-uniform
if it maps each letter to a length-
k
word.
A
1
-uniform morphism is called a
coding
.
A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is
pure morphic
if it is a fixed point of a morphism, i.e., there exist a morphism
f\colon A^{*}\to A^{*}
and a letter
a\in A
such that
\mathbf{x}=f^{\omega}(a)
, where
f^{\omega}(a)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(a)
.
A sequence
\mathbf{y}
is
morphic
if there exist a pure morphic sequence
\mathbf{x}
and a coding
g\colon A^{*}\to B^{*}
such that
\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{x})
.
We let
E\colon\{0,1\}^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}
be the
exchange morphism
defined by
E(0)=1
and
E(1)=0
.
We naturally extend
E
to sequences.

Numeration systems.

Let
U=(U(n))_{n\geq 0}
be an increasing sequence of integers with
U(0)=1
.
Any integer
n
can be decomposed in a greedy way as
n=\sum_{i=0}^{t}c(i)\,U(i)
with non-negative integer coefficients
c(i)
.
The word
c(t)\cdots c(0)\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
is said to be the
(greedy)
U
-representation
of
n
.
By convention, the greedy representation of
0
is the empty word
\varepsilon
, and the greedy representation of
n>0
starts with a non-zero digit.
For
c_{t}\cdots c_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we let
\val_{U}(c(t)\cdots c(0))
denote the integer
\sum_{i=0}^{t}c(i)\,U(i)
.
A sequence
U
satisfying all the above conditions defines a
positional numeration system
.

Automatic and regular sequences.

For the case of integer base numeration systems, a classical reference on automatic sequences is
[
AlloucheShallit2003
]
, while
[
RigoMaes2002
,
Shallit1988
]
treat the case of more exotic numeration systems.

Let
U=(U(n))_{n\geq 0}
be an positional numeration system.
A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is
U
-automatic
if there exists a deterministic finite automaton with output (DFAO)
\mathcal{A}
such that, for all
n\geq 0
, the
n
th term
\mathbf{x}(n)
of
\mathbf{x}
is given by the output
\mathcal{A}(\rep_{U}(n))
of
\mathcal{A}
.
In particular, if
U
is the sequence of consecutive powers of an integer
k\geq 2
, then
\mathbf{x}
is said to be
k
-automatic
.

It is known that a sequence is
k
-automatic if and only if it is the image, under a coding, of a fixed point
of a
k
-uniform morphism
[
AlloucheShallit2003
]
.

A generalization of automatic sequences to infinite alphabets is the following
[
AlloucheShallit2003
,
RigoMaes2002
,
Shallit1988
]
.
Let
U=(U(n))_{n\geq 0}
be a positional numeration system.
A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is
U
-regular
if there exist a column vector
\lambda
, a row vector
\gamma
and matrix-valued morphism
\mu
such that
\mathbf{x}(n)=\lambda\mu(\rep_{U}(n))\gamma
.
Such a system of matrices forms a
linear representation
of
\mathbf{x}
.
In particular, if
U
is the sequence of consecutive powers of an integer
k\geq 2
, then
\mathbf{x}
is said to be
k
-regular
.

Another definition of
k
-regular sequences is the following one
[
AlloucheShallit2003
]
.
Consider a sequence
\mathbf{x}
and an integer
k\geq 2
.
The
k
-kernel
of
\mathbf{x}
is the set of subsequences of the form
(\mathbf{x}(k^{e}n+r))_{n\geq 0}
where
r\in\{1,2,\ldots,k^{e}\}
.
A sequence is
k
-regular if the
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by its
k
-kernel is finitely generated.
A sequence is then
k
-automatic if and only if its
k
-kernel is finite
[
AlloucheShallit2003
]
.

Sturmian sequences.
A sequence

\mathbf{x}
is
Sturmian
if its factor complexity
function satisfies
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+1
(see,
e.g.,
[
AlloucheShallit2003
,
Lothaire2002
]
). Sturmian
sequences have minimal factor complexity among all
non-eventually periodic sequences, as proved by
Morse and Hedlund
[
MorseHedlund1938
]
.

Theorem 5

(
[
MorseHedlund1938
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence and let
\ell
be the number of distinct letters occurring in
\mathbf{x}
.
The following properties are equivalent.

(a)

The sequence
\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic.

(b)

We have
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)
for some
n\geq 0
.

(c)

We have
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)<n+\ell-1
for some
n\geq 1
.

(d)

The factor complexity
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
is bounded.

Remark 6
.

This theorem implies in particular that
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
is either bounded or it satisfies
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\geq n+\ell-1
for all
n
.
Thus the minimal factor complexity among all non-eventually periodic sequences is
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+1
for all
n
, i.e., the
complexity of Sturmian sequences. Actually there is another “growth gap” for
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
. Recall that a sequence
\mathbf{x}

is called
quasi-Sturmian
if there exists a constant
C
such that, for
n
large enough, one has
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+C

(see
[
Cassaigne1997
]
; also see
[
Chernyatev2008
]
).
It is known that if
\mathbf{x}
is neither eventually
periodic nor quasi-Sturmian, then

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)-n
tends to infinity: this result
is due to Coven
[
Coven1975
, Lemma 1.3]
;
also see Cassaigne’s proof cited
in
[
Allouche2000
, Proof of Theorem 3, p. 23]
. Thus

(a)

either
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
is bounded,
which happens if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is eventually
periodic;

(b)

or else
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+C
for
some constant
C
and all
n
large enough, which
means that
\mathbf{x}
is quasi-Sturmian;

(c)

or else
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)-n
tends to
infinity.

One more point (once explained to the first author by
Jean Berstel) is that if
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+1
for

n
large enough, then
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+1
for
all
n
. Namely, let
n_{0}
be the least integer
n
for
which
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=n+1
, and suppose that

n_{0}>1
. Hence
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0}-1)\neq n_{0}
. The
sequence
\mathbf{x}
cannot be eventually periodic, since
its factor complexity is not bounded. Thus, one has

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\geq n+1
for all
n
. Hence, in
particular,
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0}-1)\geq n_{0}
. Thus

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0}-1)>n_{0}
.
Since
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
is non-decreasing, we have that

n_{0}<\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0}-1)\leq\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0})=n_{0}+1
. This gives
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0}-1)=n_{0}+1=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n_{0})
,
which is impossible since
\mathbf{x}
is not
eventually periodic. In other words, in the second item
above, if
C=1
, then
\mathbf{x}
is Sturmian.

3
General results

Given a sequence
\mathbf{x}
, we can decompose its
factor complexity function
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
and its
reflection complexity function
r_{\mathbf{x}}
by using
the following functions: for all
n\geq 0
, we let

(a)

\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
denote the number of “unreflected”
length-
n
factors
w
of
\mathbf{x}
such that

w^{R}
is not a factor of
\mathbf{x}
;

(b)

\Refl_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
denote the number of “reflected”
length-
n
factors
w
of
\mathbf{x}
such that

w^{R}
is also a factor of
\mathbf{x}
; and

(c)

\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
denote the number of length-
n

palindrome factors
w
of
\mathbf{x}
(i.e., the

palindrome complexity
function of
\mathbf{x}

[
AlloucheBaakeCassaigneDamanik2003
]
).

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}=\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}+\Refl_{\mathbf{x}},\\
r_{\mathbf{x}}=\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}+\dfrac{1}{2}(\Refl_{\mathbf{x}}-\Pal_{\mathbf{x}})+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}.\end{array}

Example 7
.

Let
\mathbf{f}=01001010\cdots
denote the Fibonacci
sequence, which is the fixed point of
0\mapsto 01,1\mapsto 0
.
Its length-
5
factors are
u_{1}=01001
,
u_{2}=10010
,
u_{3}=00101
,
u_{4}=01010
,
u_{5}=10100
, and
u_{6}=00100
.
Observe that
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3},u_{5}
are reflected (second type), and
u_{4}
and
u_{6}
are palindromes (second and third types).
Furthermore
\mathbf{f}
has no unreflected factor, because
Sturmian words are reversal-closed; see

[
DroubayPirillo1999
, Theorem 4, p. 77]
.
We therefore obtain
r_{\mathbf{f}}(6)=0+\frac{1}{2}(6-2)+2=4
.

The relationships among the complexity functions
\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}
,
\Refl_{\mathbf{x}}
,
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
,
r_{\mathbf{x}}
,
and
\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}
motivates the study of the combinations of these
functions indicated in Equalities (
3
).
This is illustrated below.

Lemma 8
.

For a sequence
\mathbf{x}
and for all
n\geq 1
, we have

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)-r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\frac{1}{2}(\Refl_{\mathbf{x}}(n)-\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n))

and

2r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)-\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n).

Proof.

Immediate from Equalities (
3
).
∎

This lemma implies the following bounds on the ratio
r/\rho
.

Theorem 9
.

For a sequence
\mathbf{x}
and for all
n\geq 0
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n))\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n).

Furthermore, the equality cases are as follows:

(a)

We have
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)

if and only if every reflected length-
n
factor of

\mathbf{x}
is a palindrome.

(b)

We have
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n))

if and only if
\mathbf{x}
has no unreflected
length-
n
factors. In particular, if the sequence

\mathbf{x}
is reversal-closed, we have

r_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}})
.

(c)

We have
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
if and only if
\mathbf{x}
has no
palindrome of length
n
and each of its length-
n

factors is reflected.

Proof.

The inequalities and the equality cases are immediate
consequences of Lemma
8
.
∎

Remark 10
.

It is known that if a sequence
\mathbf{x}
is reversal-closed, then
\mathbf{x}
is recurrent: it suffices to adapt the proof
of
[
Dekking80-81
, Proposition 1, p. 176]
, as indicated in
[
BrlekLadouceur2003
]
.
Also note that if a sequence
\mathbf{x}
is uniformly
recurrent and contains infinitely many distinct palindromes,
then
\mathbf{x}
is reversal-closed
[
BalkovaPelantovaStarosta2010
, Theorem 3.2]
.

One can say more for uniformly recurrent sequences.
The following dichotomy holds.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a uniformly recurrent sequence.
Then either it is reversal-closed, or else it has no
long reflected factors (which implies that
\mathbf{x}

has no long palindromes). In other words,

(a)

either
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}=\Refl_{\mathbf{x}}
, which implies the equality

r_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}})
;

(b)

or else there exists

such that

for all

, which implies

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)

for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{x}
is reversal-closed, then
r_{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}})
from Theorem
9
(b) above.
Now suppose that
\mathbf{x}
has an unreflected factor

w

\mathbf{x}
is uniformly recurrent, every sufficiently long

\mathbf{x}
contains

as a
factor, which implies that this long factor itself
is unreflected.
This exactly says that

for

\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=0
for
n
large enough). This implies from Equalities (

) that, for

, and so
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
.
∎

Theorem 19
.

Let

be a reversal-closed sequence.
For all

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)+1.

Proof.

Using the first inequality in Theorem

, the statement in Theorem

and Theorem

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n))\leq\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)-\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+2)

Let

be a reversal-closed sequence.
Then we have

Proof.

It is enough to combine Theorems

On the other hand, we can use a result of

Theorem 21
.

Let

be a non-eventually periodic and reversal-closed sequence.
For all

, we have

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)<\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\frac{8}{n}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\left(n+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor\right).

Proof.

Given a non-eventually periodic sequence

, we have from
[

, Theorem 12]
the

\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n)<\frac{16}{n}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\left(n+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{4}\right\rfloor\right)

for all

The statement follows from this inequality and Theorem

Corollary 22
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a non-eventually periodic and reversal-closed sequence.
If its factor complexity satisfies

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)\sim\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)

or

\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(2n)}{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)}=o(n)
, then

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\sim\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)

when
n
tends to infinity.
In particular, this equivalence holds if
\mathbf{x}
is non-eventually periodic,
reversal-closed, and morphic.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a non-eventually periodic and reversal-closed sequence.
If
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)\sim\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
,
then, from Theorem
19
,
we obtain that

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\sim\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
when
n
tends to infinity.
Now, if
\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(2n)}{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)}=o(n)
, we obtain, from
Theorem
21
, and using the fact that
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
is non-decreasing, that

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)<\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\frac{8}{n}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(2n)=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+o(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)),

which is enough.

Now suppose that, in addition, the sequence
\mathbf{x}
is morphic. We know that either
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\Theta(n^{2})
or
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=O(n^{3/2})

(see
[
Devyatov2018
,
Deviatov2008
]
).
In the first case, then

\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(2n)}{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)}
is
bounded, and hence
o(n)
.
If
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=O(n^{3/2})
, since

{\mathbf{x}}
is not eventually periodic (hence

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\geq n+1
), there exists a constant

C>0
such that

\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(2n)}{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)}\leq C\frac{n^{3/2}}{n+1}=o(n).

This finishes the proof.
∎

The upper bound in
Theorem
21
raises questions about the growth properties of the function

r_{\mathbf{x}}
more generally, apart from the
case where the set of factors of
\mathbf{x}

satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem
21
. This leads
us to the growth property in
Theorem
23
below.

Theorem 23
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence. Then
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)
for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

The result is clear for
n=0
, so assume
n>0
in what follows.
Let
c
be a letter not in the alphabet of
\mathbf{x}
, and define

{\mathbf{y}}=c{\mathbf{x}}
. Then
r_{\mathbf{y}}(n)=r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+1

for all
n>0
, since
\mathbf{y}
has exactly one additional factor for each length
n\geq 1
; namely, the prefix of length
n
. Thus, it suffices to prove the claim for
\mathbf{y}

instead of
\mathbf{x}
.

With each length-
n
factor
w
of
\mathbf{y}
associate a set
S_{w}
of length-
(n+2)

factors of
\mathbf{y}
, as follows: if
w
is the length-
n
prefix of
\mathbf{y}
, then
S_{w}:=\{w^{\prime}\}
, where
w^{\prime}
is the prefix of length
n+2
of
\mathbf{y}
.
We call such a factor
exceptional
.
Otherwise, define
S_{w}:=\{z\in\Fac({\mathbf{y}})\,:\,z=awb\text{ for some letters $a,b$}\}
. Note that the sets
S_{w}
, over all length-
n
factors of
\mathbf{y}
, are pairwise disjoint, and cover all the length-
(n+2)
factors of
\mathbf{y}
.

For a factor
w
of
\mathbf{y}
, define
[w]_{1}=1
if
w
is a palindrome, and
0
otherwise.
Similarly,
[w]_{2}=1
if
w^{R}
is not a factor of
\mathbf{y}
and
0
otherwise.
Finally, define
[w]_{3}=1
if
w^{R}
is also a factor of
\mathbf{y}
but

w
is not a palindrome, and

0
otherwise. Notice that these three cases are disjoint and subsume all possibilities for factors of
\mathbf{y}
(also recall the decomposition at the beginning of the section).
We extend this notation to sets by defining

[S]_{i}=\sum_{w\in S}[w]_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Define
[w]=[w]_{1}+[w]_{2}+[w]_{3}/2
and similarly for

[S]
.
From Equalities (
3
), we know that

r_{\mathbf{y}}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|w|=n\\
w\in\Fac({\mathbf{y}})\end{subarray}}[w]

while

r_{\mathbf{y}}(n+2)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|w|=n\\
w\in\Fac({\mathbf{y}})\end{subarray}}[S_{w}].

Therefore, to show the desired inequality

r_{\mathbf{y}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{y}}(n+2)
, it suffices to show that

[w]\leq[S_{w}]
for all length-
n
factors
w
of
\mathbf{y}
.

Suppose
w
is exceptional. Recall that
w
starts with
c
,
which appears nowhere else in
\mathbf{y}
. Then
[w]_{1}=[w]_{3}=0
, but
[w]_{2}=1
.
And
S_{w}=\{wab\}
, so
[S_{w}]_{1}=[S_{w}]_{3}=0
, but
[S_{w}]_{2}=1
.
Therefore
[w]\leq[S_{w}]
.

Now suppose
w
is not exceptional. There are three cases to consider, noting that when
n=1
, we have that
[w]_{1}=1
and
[w]_{2}=0
.

Case 1: If
[w]_{1}=1
, then
w
is a palindrome.
Consider a factor
awb\in S_{w}
. If it is
a palindrome, then
[awb]_{1}=1
, so
[w]\leq[awb]
. If
awb

is not a palindrome, then
awb\not=(awb)^{R}=bw^{R}a=bwa
.
Thus
a\not=b
. If
bwa
is not a factor of
\mathbf{y}
,
then
[awb]_{2}=1
, so
[w]\leq[awb]
. If
bwa
is a factor
of
\mathbf{y}
, then
bwa\in S_{w}
and
[awb]_{3}+[bwa]_{3}=2
, so in all cases
[w]\leq[awb]
.
Thus
[w]\leq[S_{w}]
.

Case 2: If
[w]_{2}=1
, then
w^{R}
is not a factor of
\mathbf{y}
. Consider
a factor
awb\in S_{w}
. Then
(awb)^{R}=bw^{R}a
, so
(awb)^{R}

cannot be a factor of
y
either. Hence
[awb]_{2}=1
,
[w]\leq[awb]
, and
hence
[w]\leq[S_{w}]
.

Case 3: If
[w]_{3}=1
, then
w^{R}
is a factor of
\mathbf{y}
, but
w
is not a
palindrome. Consider a factor
awb\in S_{w}
. If
awb
is a palindrome,
then
awb=(awb)^{R}=bw^{R}a
, so
w^{R}
would be a palindrome, a contradiction.
So
awb
is not a palindrome and
[awb]_{1}=0
.
If
(awb)^{R}=bw^{R}a
is a factor of
\mathbf{y}
,
then
[awb]_{3}=1
, so
[w]\leq[awb]
.
If
(awb)^{R}
is not a factor of
\mathbf{y}
, then

[awb]_{2}=1
, so
[w]<[awb]
. Thus
[w]\leq[S_{w}]
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 24
.

Another formulation of Theorem
23

above is that the sequence

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)+r_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}

is non-decreasing.

With regard to the conjectures listed at the end of this section, we can prove a weaker form of Conjecture

28
for reversal-closed sequences, and a weaker form of Conjecture
27

for sequences without
long palindromes. Also we can prove that

morphic sequence.

Theorem 25
.

Let
n_{0}\geq 0
be an integer and let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence with no palindrome of
length
\geq n_{0}
. Then
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}

is eventually non-decreasing:

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)
for
n\geq n_{0}
.
Furthermore, if
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)=r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)

for some
n\geq n_{0}
, then the sequence

\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic.

Proof.

By combining the assumption and the second equality of Lemma
8
, we have that

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\dfrac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n))

(5)

for
n\geq n_{0}
. Since both

(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
and

(\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
are
non-decreasing, we see that
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq n_{0}}
is
non-decreasing, which gives that

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)
for
n\geq n_{0}
.
This shows the first part of the statement.
For the second part, if we have

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)=r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
for some

, then Equality (
5
) implies that
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)+\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
. Hence

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)+\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n+2)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)+\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}(n).

(6)

Hence
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
,
which implies that
\mathbf{x}
is eventually
periodic from Theorem
5
.
∎

4
Flye Sainte-Marie–Rauzy graphs for reflec-tion-equivalent classes

In this section, we prove the following inequality:

Theorem 30
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence. Then
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)+1
for all
n\geq 0
.

Note that we can have equality in Theorem
30
for infinitely many
values of
n
—see Example
16
above.

Initially, we discovered this growth property empirically.
We prove it here by means of a graph construction related to the work of Flye Sainte-Marie.
We remark that another recent application of this idea leads to a bound on the maximal number of powers in finite words
[
BrlekLi-2025
,
LiPachockiRadoszewski-2024
]
.

Flye Sainte-Marie graphs (also known as Rauzy graphs or De Bruijn graphs) for sequences
\mathbf{x}
can be defined in the following way: for an integer
n\geq 0
, the graph
\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
is a directed graph whose vertex set is
\Fac_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
and edge set is
\Fac_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)
.
An edge
e\in\Fac_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)
starts at the vertex
u
and ends at the vertex
v
if and only if
u
is a prefix of
e
and
v
is a suffix. To simplify the notation, for words
w_{1},w_{2}
of length
n
and a word
w_{3}
of length
n+1
, we write
w_{1}\xrightarrow{w_{3}}w_{2}
if
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are a prefix and a suffix of
w_{3}
, respectively.

Remark 31
.

Flye Sainte-Marie first introduced the previous graph construction in
[
Flye-Sainte-Marie
]
to represent the overlaps between all the binary words of the same length. De Bruijn rediscovered the same graph construction in
[
de-Bruijn46
]
and acknowledged Flye Sainte-Marie’s priority of
[
Flye-Sainte-Marie
]
later in
[
de-Bruijn75
]
. Rauzy used the same construction in
[
Rauzy
]
to study the overlaps of factors in a word.

A directed graph is
(weakly) connected
if there is an undirected path between any pair of its vertices.

The following proposition is “folklore”. To the authors’ knowledge, the proof of this proposition does not seem to properly appear
in the literature.

Proposition 32
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence.
For all
n\geq 0
, the graph
\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
is connected.

Proof.

Let
u
and
v
be two distinct length-
n
factors of
\mathbf{x}
and let us suppose that
u
occurs before
v
in
\mathbf{x}
. Then there are two integers
i<j
such that
\mathbf{x}[i..i+n-1]=u
and
\mathbf{x}[j..j+n-1]=v
. Then the path
\mathbf{x}[i..i+n],\mathbf{x}[i+1..i+n+1],\ldots,\mathbf{x}[j-1..j+n-1]
connects
u
and
v
.
∎

Remark 33
.

Proposition
32
has already been (indirectly) mentioned in
[
Rote1994
, p. 199, lines 4–6]
, which states that every graph
\Gamma_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
necessarily has a vertex
o
from which every other vertex can be reached by a direct path. Thus each pair of vertices
u
,
v
can be connected by an undirected path passing through
o
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence. For all
n\geq 0
, let

5
Eventually periodic sequences

We can characterize eventually periodic sequences
(i.e., sequences that are periodic from some index on)
in terms of their reflection complexity.

Theorem 39
.

A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic if
and only if both sequences
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n\geq 0}
and

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n\geq 0}
are eventually constant.

Proof.

From Theorem
23
both sequences

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n\geq 0}
and
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n\geq 0}

are non-decreasing. Also, from the inequalities

\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)
in Theorem
9
,
and the fact that the sequence
(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is
non-decreasing, we have that either the three integer
sequences
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n\geq 0}
,

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n\geq 0}
, and
(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}

are all bounded, or else none of them is.
Furthermore, we know that
(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is
bounded if and only if the sequence
\mathbf{x}
is
eventually periodic (Theorem
5

above). Hence, we have two cases depending on the periodicity of
\mathbf{x}
.

(a)

If
\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic, then

(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is bounded,
so
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n\geq 0}
and

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n\geq 0}
are eventually constant.

(b)

If
\mathbf{x}
is not eventually periodic, its
factor complexity is not bounded, thus both sequences

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n\geq 0}
and

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n\geq 0}
tend to infinity.

This ends the proof.
∎

Remark 40
.

If
\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic, the eventual
values of
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n\geq 0}
and

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n\geq 0}
can be either equal or
distinct, as seen from the examples of the sequences

(01)^{\omega}
and
(011)^{\omega}
.

6
Sturmian sequences and generalizations

In this section, we study Sturmian sequences as well as some generalizations.
First we state the following result, which
characterizes Sturmian sequences in terms of their
reflection complexity.

Theorem 41
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a non-eventually periodic sequence over a finite alphabet.

(a)

For all
n\geq 1
, we have
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\geq 1+\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
;

(b)

We have
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=1+\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor
if and only if
\mathbf{x}
is Sturmian.

Proof.

For each integer
n\geq 1
, let
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
be the permutation group on
n

elements. Let
\sigma_{n}
be the permutation defined by

\sigma_{n}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&n-1&n\\
n&n-1&\ldots&2&1\\
\end{pmatrix}

and
G_{n}
be the subgroup of
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
generated by
\sigma_{n}
, i.e., the group

\{\sigma_{n},{\rm id}_{n}\}
. The number of distinct orbits of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
under
G_{n}
is
equal to
n/2
if
n
is even, and to
(n+1)/2
if
n
is odd, which can be written

\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor
in both cases. Thus, applying
[
CharlierPuzyninaZamboni2017
, Theorem 1]

proves the first item of the theorem and the implication

\Longrightarrow
of the second item.

To prove the last assertion, suppose that
\mathbf{x}

is a Sturmian sequence. We know that every Sturmian
sequence is reversal-closed (see

[
DroubayPirillo1999
, Theorem 4, p. 77]
, where
reversals are called mirror images). Furthermore, it is
proved in
[
DroubayPirillo1999
, Theorem 5, p. 77]

that a sequence is Sturmian if and only if it has one palindrome of all even lengths and two palindromes of all odd lengths.
Now, from Theorem
9
(b) we have
that

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)+\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}(n))=\begin{cases}\frac{n+2}{2}=1+\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor,&\text{if $n$ even;}\\
\frac{n+3}{2}=1+\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor,&\text{if $n$ odd}.\end{cases}

This completes the proof.
∎

With regard to the above referenced work of
Charlier et al.
[
CharlierPuzyninaZamboni2017
]
,
also see the related and recent work by
Luchinin and Puzynina
[
LuchininPuzynina2023
]
.

The following is an analog of
the Morse–Hedlund theorem (which is recalled in Theorem

above).

Corollary 42

A sequence
\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic if and
only if there exists

such that

.
Furthermore both sequences
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n))_{n}

and
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(2n+1))_{n}
are then eventually
constant.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a sequence. Contraposing Property (a)
of Theorem
41
, we obtain that if

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor

for some

\mathbf{x}
must be eventually

Conversely, if
\mathbf{x}
is eventually periodic,
it has a bounded number of factors, hence there exists

∎

Recall that every quasi-Sturmian sequence
\mathbf{x}

can be written as
\mathbf{x}=yf(\mathbf{z})

Theorem 43
.

Let

\mathbf{z}
is a Sturmian sequence, and

(a)

(b)

or else

is not reversal-closed and

Since

.
Since

Definition 44

.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be an

Then, for all

r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=(\ell-1)\left\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be an
\ell
-strict episturmian sequence.
The case

is true.
Assume that

Then by
[

So-called complementation-symmetric Rote sequences, which were defined and studied

Definition 47
.

Let
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}

\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=2n

The sequence
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}

We consider the mapping

There is a natural extension of

\mathbf{x}=(x(n))_{n\geq 0}

is the sequence whose

.
Observe that
\Delta(\mathbf{x})
is the sequence of first differences of
\mathbf{x}
, taken modulo
2
.

(
[

]
)
.

A binary sequence
\mathbf{x}
is a complementation-symmetric Rote sequence if and only if
\Delta(\mathbf{x})
is Sturmian.

In fact, for each Sturmian sequence

and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}

The factors in

Proposition 49

(

Rich sequences have several equivalent definitions.
It is known that a word

contains at most
|w|+1
palindromic factors
[
DroubayJustinPirillo2001

A sequence is called

Theorem 52
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a reversal-closed sequence.
Then
\mathbf{x}
is rich if and only if
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)+r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n+1)+1
for all

.

Proof.

From
[
BucciDeLucaGlenZamboni2009
, Theorem 1.1]
, the sequence
\mathbf{x}
is rich if and only if the inequality in Theorem
17
(b) is an equality.
The result then follows from Theorem
9
(b).
∎

, Corollaries 2.27 and 2.29]
, it is possible to bound the reflection complexity of rich sequences as follows.

Proposition 54
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be a rich sequence over an alphabet of
q
letters and write
\delta=\frac{2}{3(\log 3-\log 2)}
.
Then
r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\frac{nq}{2}(2q^{2}n)^{\delta\log n}(1+nq^{3}(2q^{2}n)^{\delta\log n})
for all
n\geq 1
.

Other sequences have a reflection complexity satisfying the equality of Theorem
52
.
For instance, it is the case for complementation-symmetric sequences, sequences canonically associated with some specific Parry numbers, and sequences coding particular interval exchange transformations.
For more details, see
[
BalaviMasakovaPelantova2007
, Section 3]
.

8
Automatic sequences

In this section, we study the reflection complexity of automatic sequences.
First, in a positional numeration system
U
having an
adder
(i.e., addition is recognizable by an automaton reading
U
-representations), we show that if a sequence is
U
-automatic, then its reflection complexity is a
U
-regular sequence.
Furthermore we show how to effectively compute a linear representation for the sequence, making use of the free software
Walnut

[
Mousavi2016
,
Shallit2023Walnut
]
.
Next, we explore the reflection complexity of some famous automatic sequences, namely the Thue–Morse, the period-doubling, generalized paperfolding, generalized Golay–Shapiro, and the Baum–Sweet sequences.

8.1
Reflection complexity is computably regular

We now show that the reflection complexity of an automatic sequence is regular.

Theorem 55
.

Let
U=(U(n))_{n\geq 0}
be a positional numeration system such that there is an adder, and let
\mathbf{x}
be a
U
-automatic sequence.
Then
(r_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is a
U
-regular sequence.
Furthermore, a linear representation for

(r_{\mathbf{x}}(n))_{n\geq 0}
is computable from
the DFAO for
\mathbf{x}
.

Proof.

Here is a sketch of the proof before we give the details: We
create a first-order logical formula asserting that the factor

\mathbf{x}[i..i+n-1]
is the first occurrence of this factor, or its reversal. Then the number of such
i
is precisely the reflection complexity at
n
.
From this, we can create
a linear representation for the number of such
i
.

Now some more details. We define the following logical formulas:

\displaystyle\FactorEq(i,j,n)

\displaystyle:=\forall t\ (t<n)\implies\mathbf{x}[i+t]=\mathbf{x}[j+t]

\displaystyle\FactorRevEq(i,j,n)

\displaystyle:=\forall t\ (t<n)\implies\mathbf{x}[i+t]=\mathbf{x}[(j+n)-(t+1)]

(7)

\displaystyle\RefComp(i,n)

\displaystyle:=\forall j\ (j<i)\implies((\neg\FactorEq(i,j,n))

\displaystyle\ \wedge\ (\neg\FactorRevEq(i,j,n))).

Now we use the fundamental result on Büchi arithmetic to translate each of these formulas to their corresponding automata accepting the base-
U
representation of those pairs
(i,n)
making the formula true. Next, we use a basic result to convert
the automaton for
\RefComp
to the corresponding linear representation computing the reflection complexity.
∎

Once we have a linear representation for the reflection complexity, we can easily compute it for
a given
n
. Furthermore, we can compare it to a guessed formula, provided that this
formula can also be expressed as a linear representation (see
[
Shallit2023Walnut
]
). In the next section we carry this out in detail for a number of famous sequences.

We conclude the paper by considering some further research directions to pursue in relation to
reflection complexities of sequences and by raising some open problems.
\par We encourage further explorations of the evaluation
of $r_{\mathbf{x}}$ for sequences $\mathbf{x}$ for which
properties of $\Pal_{\mathbf{x}}$ and/or $\rho_{\mathbf{x}}$
are known, especially if {Walnut} cannot be used
directly in the investigation of $r_{\mathbf{x}}$.
For example, by letting the \emph{Chacon sequence}
$\mathbf{c}$ be the fixed point of the morphism
$0\mapsto 0010$ and $1\mapsto 1$, it is known that
$\Pal_{\mathbf{c}}(n)=0$ for all $n\geq 13$. Also, its
factor complexity satisfies $\rho_{\mathbf{c}}(n)=2n-1$
for $n\geq 2$~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Ferenczi1995}{}{}]}.
We have
$$(r_{\mathbf{c}}(n))_{n\geq 0}=1,2,2,4,4,6,7,10,11,14,16,20,23,25,27,29,31,33,\ldots.$$
The sequence $\mathbf{c}$ is not automatic in a given so-called
\emph{addable} numeration system (i.e., where there is an adder).
Therefore, we cannot use {Walnut}, in this case.
However, an inductive argument can be applied to prove that
$r_{\mathbf{c}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{c}}(n)$ for all $n\geq 13$.
\par We propose the following natural questions.
\par\begin{question}To what extent can the reflection complexity
be used to discriminate between different families of sequences, by analogy with our characterizations of Sturmian and eventually periodic sequences?
\end{question}
\par The complexity function $\Unr_{\mathbf{x}}$ defined above may be of interest in its own right, as is the case with the ``reflection-free''
complexity function enumerating factors such that the reversal of every sufficiently large factor is not a factor.
\par\begin{question}How can Theorem~\ref{thm:theoremrTM} be generalized
with the use of standard generalizations of the
Thue--Morse sequence?
\end{question}
\par For example, if we let
$$ \mathbf{t3} = (t3(n))_{n\geq 0} = 011212201220200112202001200\cdots$$
denote the generalized Thue--Morse sequence for which the $n$th term $t3(n)$ is equal to the number of $1$'s, modulo 3, in the base-$2$
expansion of $n$, it can be shown that
$r_{\mathbf{t3}}(n)=\rho_{\mathbf{t3}}(n)$
for all $n\geq 3$,
and it appears that a similar property
holds for the cases given by taking the number of $1$'s modulo $\ell>4$.
\par\begin{question}What is the reflection complexity of the Thue--Morse sequence
over polynomial extractions, with regard to the work of Moshe~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Moshe2007}{}{}]}?
\end{question}
\par\begin{question}How can the upper bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:upper-bound-abcd}
be improved? If $r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)$ is of the
form $\Omega(n)$, then how can this be improved?
\end{question}
\par\begin{question}How does the reflection complexity compare with other complexity functions, e.g., the complexity functions
listed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}?
\end{question}
\par This leads us to ask about the respective growths of the complexity functions listed in Section \ref{sec:intro}, in particular
for morphic sequences. In this direction, recall that the factor complexity of a pure morphic sequence is either $\Theta(1)$, $\Theta(n)$, $\Theta(n\log\log n)$, $\Theta(n\log n)$ or $\Theta(n^{2})$, see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Pansiot1984}{}{}]}
(more details can be found, e.g., in~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Cisternino2018}{}{}]}).
The factor complexity of a morphic sequence is either
$\Theta(n^{1+1/k})$ for some positive integer $k$, or else
it is $O(n\log n)$, see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Devyatov2018,Deviatov2008}{}{}]}).
As an illustration with a result that has not
been already cited above, a comparison between growths for
the factor complexity and the Lempel-Ziv complexity can be
found in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ConstantinescuIlie2007}{}{}]}. We end with an easy
result for the growth of the reflection complexity in the
case of pure morphic and morphic sequences.
\par\begin{proposition}\par\ { }
\par\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@[$*$]The reflection complexity of a pure morphic sequence
is either $\Theta(1)$, $\Theta(n)$,
$\Theta(n\log\log n)$, $\Theta(n\log n)$ or
$\Theta(n^{2})$.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@[$*$]The reflection complexity of a morphic sequence is either
$\Theta(n^{1+1/k})$ for some positive integer $k$, or else
it is $O(n\log n)$.
\end{itemize}\end{proposition}
\par\@proof Use the inequalities in Theorem~\ref{thm:r-and-rho}:
for a sequence $\mathbf{x}$ and for all $n\geq 0$, we have $\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq r_{\mathbf{x}}(n)\leq\rho_{\mathbf{x}}(n)$.
\qed\end@proof
