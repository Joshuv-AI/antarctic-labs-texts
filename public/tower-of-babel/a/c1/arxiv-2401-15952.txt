A Class-aware Optimal Transport Approach with Higher-Order Moment Matching for Unsupervised Domain Adaptation
Tuan Nguyen, Van Nguyen, Trung Le, He Zhao, Quan Hung Tran, Dinh Phung

Abstract
Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) aims to transfer knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain. In this paper, we introduce a novel approach called class-aware optimal transport (OT), which measures the OT distance between a distribution over the source class-conditional distributions and a mixture of source and target data distribution. Our class-aware OT leverages a cost function that determines the matching extent between a given data example and a source class-conditional distribution. By optimizing this cost function, we find the optimal matching between target examples and source class-conditional distributions, effectively addressing the data and label shifts that occur between the two domains. To handle the class-aware OT efficiently, we propose an amortization solution that employs deep neural networks to formulate the transportation probabilities and the cost function. Additionally, we propose minimizing class-aware Higher-order Moment Matching (HMM) to align the corresponding class regions on the source and target domains. The class-aware HMM component offers an economical computational approach for accurately evaluating the HMM distance between the two distributions. Extensive experiments on benchmark datasets demonstrate that our proposed method significantly outperforms existing state-of-the-art baselines.

Introduction

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) allows us to transfer knowledge
from a model trained on a source domain with labels to a target domain
without any labels. To cope more efficiently and effectively with
structural data, deep domain adaptation (DDA)

data shift issue and learn domain-invariant features, DDA aims to
bridge the distribution gap between the source and target domains
in a latent space using a feature extractor. Guided by this principle,
most existing works in DDA propose minimizing the divergence between
the source and target distributions in the latent space. Popular choices
of divergence include the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence
[

,

,

]
,

[

,

and the Wasserstein (WS) distance
[

,
28
]
.

Recently, optimal transport (OT)
[
45
,
54

,
a discipline in mathematics with a rich and rigorous theory, has been
widely applied in deep learning, particularly in domain adaptation

[

,
7

,

,

,
59
,
60
]
.
From a conceptual perspective, the OT-based objective function encourages
the target examples to move to the source examples by minimizing a
transportation cost. However, since the transportation cost generally
considers pairs of target and source examples without taking into
account the label information of the source examples, the movement
of the target examples to the source domain seems to be unaware of
the class regions in that domain, thus unable to resolve the label
shift issue. Although OT has been initially used to address this problem

[
8
,
10
]
, the performance of
the existing methods is still less satisfactory compared to state-of-the-art
approaches.

In this paper, we propose a novel class-aware optimal transport approach
that incorporates source label information when matching target and
source examples. Specifically, we consider a distribution of distributions
where each component distribution represents a class-conditional distribution
of the source domain. We then compute an OT distance between this
distribution and the mixture of target and source data distribution,
where the cost function determines the matching degree between an
example and a class-conditional distribution. By minimizing this OT
distance, we aim to find the optimal matching between target examples
and source class-conditional distributions, effectively incorporating
source label information. To handle the OT distance, we propose an
amortization solution that utilizes deep neural networks to amortize
transportation probabilities and the cost function. Additionally,

CL

In summary, our contributions in this paper are as follows:

•

We propose a novel class-aware optimal transport method that addresses
the challenges of data and label shift in Unsupervised Domain Adaptation
(UDA). Our approach considers source label information and seeks the
optimal matching between target and source examples to mitigate these
issues.

•

We introduce a Class-aware Higher-order Moment Matching (CaHoMM) distance
to accurately approximate the distributions of the source and target
domains in the latent space. This CaHoMM distance is evaluated using
an elegant and efficient formulation, improving the class-aware matching
between the two domains.

•

We conduct extensive experiments to compare our proposed method, CLOTH,
with state-of-the-art baselines in standard UDA, class-aware UDA,
and OT-based UDA on benchmark datasets such as Digits, Office-31,
Office-Home, and ImageCLEF-DA. Our proposed CLOTH achieves state-of-the-art
performance on these benchmark datasets, surpassing existing methods.

Overall, our contributions include the development of a class-aware
OT method, the introduction of class-aware Higher-Order Moment Matching,
and the empirical validation of our approach’s superior performance
on various benchmark datasets.

Some recent approaches, such as

Distributional OT Approach for Class-Aware UDA

3.1
Problem Formulation

We consider the vanilla setting of unsupervised domain adaptation

\mathbb{P}^{S}
and

\mathbb{P}^{T}
as the data distributions of the source and target
domains, respectively. Moreover, given a class
m

\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S}
as the
m
-th class-conditional distribution
of the source domain (i.e., the distribution with the density function

p^{S}\left(\mathbf{x}\mid y=m\right)
).

consists of atoms representing the source and target
examples

, while
\mathcal{P}^{S}
consists of
atoms representing the source class-conditional distribution

3.2
Our Proposed Class-aware OT

In the given scenario with a total of
N
samples, comprising

samples from the source domain and

samples from the target
domain, the source samples are denoted as

we define an empirical mixture distribution

\mathbb{P}=\frac{N_{S}}{N}\mathbb{P}^{S}+\frac{N_{T}}{N}\mathbb{P}^{T}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},

where
\delta_{\mathbf{x}}
represents the Dirac delta distribution
concentrated at the sample
\mathbf{x}
. Additionally, we introduce

\mathcal{P}^{S}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\pi_{m}\delta_{\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S}}
,
where the mixing proportion
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{M}
(i.e., the
M-1

dimensional simplex). In other words,
\mathcal{P}^{S}
is a discrete
distribution of distributions, taking
\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S}
with the
probability

.

We now examine an OT distance between two discrete distributions

\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle=\min_{A}\Bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{im}c\left(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{A}\Bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{im}c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right)

3.3
Our Amortization Solution

The optimization problem in (
3
) can be solved analytically
using the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
9
]
. However,
this approach becomes computationally infeasible due to its high time
complexity of
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
for each iteration. To address
this computational challenge, one has explored a mini-batch version
of the Sinkhorn algorithm. Nevertheless, this approach may introduce
batch bias and yield inaccurate solutions
[
38
]
.

In order to achieve an accurate distribution-matching solution and
enable batch training with a theoretical guarantee (cf. Theorem
1
),
we propose an amortization solution for minimizing the OT distance
in (
3
). Specifically, we employ a deep network called
the transportation network
\mathcal{T}\left(\cdot\right)
with
M

outputs, which allows us to amortize the transportation matrix
A
.

Given
\mathbf{x}_{i}\,
, for
1\leq i\leq N
, we use the transportation
network
\mathcal{T}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right))
to predict
the probability
\mathbf{a}_{i}=\left[a_{im}\right]_{m}
, where
a_{im}=\frac{1}{N}\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)
.
This probability distribution specifies the likelihood of matching
or transporting
G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)
to
\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}
.
The constraint
\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{im}=\frac{1}{N}
is naturally satisfied.
Consequently, the optimization problem in (
3
) is rewritten
as:

\min_{G,\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right).

(4)

The following theorem justifies solving the optimization problem in
(
4
) rather than directly solving the one in (
3
).
Specifically, we provide a theoretical proof demonstrating that when
the transportation network
\mathcal{T}
is sufficiently expressive,
the optimal solution
\mathcal{T}^{*}
obtained from (
4
)
can effectively approximate the optimal transportation matrix
A^{*}

derived from (
3
).

Theorem 1
.

(Proof is presented in Appendix
B
).
Assuming that the transportation network
\mathcal{T}
belongs to
a family of models with infinite capacity, which means it has the
ability to approximate any continuous function with arbitrary precision,
then the optimization problem in (
4
) is equivalent
to the optimization problem in (
3
).

The introduction of the network
\mathcal{T}
also motivates us to
transport not only target examples but also source examples to their
respective source class-conditional distributions. This approach strengthens
the training of the network
\mathcal{T}
on both the source and
target domains, allowing it to leverage its sufficient power and capacity
to effectively amortize the transportation matrix
A
.

We now present optimization problems related to transportation network

\mathcal{T}
. For a source sample
G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)=G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{S}\right)\,
,
where
1\leq i\leq N_{S}
, the prediction
\mathcal{T}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

should be the one-hot vector
\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{S}}

rewrite the optimization problem in (

\displaystyle\min_{G,\mathcal{T}}\Bigg\{\mathcal{L}^{t}=\sum_{i=N_{S}+1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}CE\left(\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{S}},\mathcal{T}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right)\Bigg\},

(5)

where

G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)=G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i-N_{S}}^{T}\right)\,
,
where

1.

Minimize the entropy of the prediction

to encourage the transportation network to make the clear decisions

2.

Maximize the entropy of the average of

for all target samples to encourage an equal movement to the class

To achieve these objectives, we minimize the following term:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{ent}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{i=N_{S}+1}^{N}\left[\mathbb{H}\left(\mathcal{T}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{H}\left(\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{i=N_{S}+1}^{N}\mathcal{T}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right),

(6)

where

denotes the entropy function.

3.4
Cost Function and Adversarial Training Framework

In what follows, we explain how to define the cost function
c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right)=-\log p_{m}^{S}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)
.
Drawing inspiration from the adversarial training framework
[
16
]
,
which has proven effective in unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA)

[
50
,
33
,
19
]
, we propose
the use of a multi-class discriminator
\mathcal{D}
. The purpose
of
\mathcal{D}
is twofold: i) to provide a reliable measure for
defining the cost
c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right)
,
and ii) to enhance the alignment between
G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)

and the class-conditional distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}
(i.e.,
the higher the alignment, the lower the cost) by encouraging target
samples to align with the entire source domain. To achieve these objectives,
we design
\mathcal{D}
with
M+1
outputs. For an example
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,

\mathcal{D}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\,(1\leq m\leq M)

represents the probability that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
belongs to the source domain
and has the label
m
, while
\mathcal{D}_{M+1}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

specifies the probability that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
belongs to the target domain.
This implies that
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{D}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)=1-\mathcal{D}_{M+1}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

specifies the probability that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
belongs to the source domain.

The discriminator
\mathcal{D}
is trained to distinguish the source
from target examples and predict the labels for source examples. The
loss to train the discriminator
\mathcal{D}
is as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{i=N_{S}+1}^{N}\log\mathcal{D}_{M+1}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,-\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\log\left(1-\mathcal{D}_{M+1}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,+\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}CE\left(1_{y_{i}^{S}},\mathcal{D}_{1:M}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right),

(7)

where
\mathcal{D}_{1:M}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)=\left[\mathcal{D}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right]_{m=1}^{M}.

The cost function in optimization problem (
5
)
captures the extent to which
G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)
matches, and
we rely on the output of the discriminator
\mathcal{D}
to define

c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right)=-\log\mathcal{D}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

reasonably. Therefore, the loss function in (
5
)
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{t}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=N_{S}+1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\log\mathcal{D}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\>\,+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}CE\left(\mathbf{1}_{y_{y}^{S}},\mathcal{T}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right).

(8)

With more specific guidance from the discriminator
\mathcal{D}
,
the feature extractor
G
is trained to push the target examples

G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),(N_{S}+1\leq i\leq N)
to the
entire
region
of source examples, and vice versa.

For source examples.
Our objective is to move the source
examples
G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),(1\leq i\leq N_{S})
to the entire
region of the target examples characterized by high values of
\mathcal{D}_{M+1}
.
Therefore, we propose minimizing the following term:

\mathcal{L}^{G,S}=-\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}\log\mathcal{D}_{M+1}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right).

(9)

For target examples.
Our goal is to move the target examples

G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),(N_{S}+1\leq i\leq N)
to the entire region
of the source examples characterized by low values of
\mathcal{D}_{M+1}
.
To achieve this, we minimize the following term:

\mathcal{L}^{G,T}=\frac{1}{N_{T}}\sum_{i=N_{S}+1}^{N}\log\mathcal{D}_{M+1}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right).

(10)

Furthermore, we utilize a source classifier
\mathcal{C}
that is
trained on the source domain:

\mathcal{L}^{C}=\frac{1}{N_{S}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{S}}CE\left(\mathbf{1}_{y_{i}^{S}},\mathcal{C}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)\right).

(11)

Networks
\mathcal{C},G
, and
\mathcal{T}
are trained by minimizing
the loss function:

\mathcal{L}^{C}+\mathcal{L}^{G,S}+\mathcal{L}^{G,T}+\alpha\mathcal{L}^{t}+\beta\mathcal{L}^{ent},

(12)

where
\alpha,\beta>0
are trade-off parameters. We alternatively
update
\mathcal{D}
by minimizing the loss function
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{D}}.

According to
[
11
]
, minimizing the Wasserstein
distance as in (
2
) encourages mode-covering behavior,
where the target examples tend to scatter over all modes of the source
data. On the other hand, minimizing the GAN loss in (
9
,

10
) or the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence encourages mode-seeking
behavior, where the target examples tend to cover specific modes of
the source data. Therefore, by minimizing
\mathcal{L}^{t}
(a surrogate
of the Wasserstein distance) and
\mathcal{L}^{G,S}+\mathcal{L}^{G,T}

(a surrogate of the JS divergence), we aim to encourage both mode-seeking
and mode-covering behaviors to achieve a more precise matching of
the source and target data.

3.5
Higher-Order Moment Matching

To further enhance the matching extent of a target sample to a proper
source class distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}
, we leverage the higher-order
moments (HM) method which has been successfully employed for cross-domain
matching in neural style transfer
[
15
,
30
]
,
knowledge distillation
[
61
]
, and domain adaptation

[
5
]
. By considering higher-order statistics (greater
than second-order), we can achieve fine-grained domain alignment,
enabling the approximation of complex distributions in the latent
space of both the source and target domains. This fine-grained alignment
facilitates a more accurate matching process. In the upcoming sections,
we introduce an efficient solution for measuring higher-order moment
matching between the distributions of the source and target domains,
leading to our proposed class-aware higher-order moment matching method.

3.6
Higher-Order Moment with Lower Complexity

Given a vector
\mathbf{z}=G\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, which represents
a data sample in an adapted layer. With
q\leq p
, we define a
q
-order
moment of
\mathbf{z}
is a
q
-dimensional tensor
A_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{q}}
,
where:

A_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\left[i_{1},...,i_{q}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{q}z_{i_{j}}

(13)

for any index
i_{j=1,2,...,q}\in\left\{1,2,...,p\right\}.
For
example, with
q=1
,
A_{1}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p},A_{1}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\left[i_{1}\right]=z_{i_{1}}

and
q=2
,
A_{2}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}},A_{2}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\left[i_{1},i_{2}\right]=z_{i_{1}}z_{i_{2}}
.

Given two distributions
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
, we consider
an HM distance between these two distributions as:

\displaystyle HM\left(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}}\left[A_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{V}}\left[A_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\right]\right\|_{F}^{2},

(14)

where
\left\|.\right\|_{F}
represents a Frobenius norm. Unfortunately, calculating
this HM distance is infeasible in practice due to the space complexity
reaching
\mathcal{O}\left(p^{q}\right)
, especially as the order

q
increases. To mitigate this complexity,
[
5
]

proposed a random sampling matching strategy to perform arbitrary-order
moment matching between the distributions of the source and target
domains in the output layer. Specifically, the authors randomly selected

n
values from
A_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)
and only matched these
n

values in the source and target domains. This approach reduces the
space complexity to
\mathcal{O}\left(n\right)
. However, there are
limitations in this method: (i) randomly sampling can lead to the
loss of important higher-order statistics that potentially characterize
the distribution; and (ii) the approach achieves competitive results
only when
n\geq 1000
. To address these concerns, we propose a simple
and economical solution for more accurate and efficient computation
of the HM distance to facilitate higher-order moment matching. We
define
\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)
as the vector obtained by flattening
the
q
-dimensional tensor
A_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)
, which means
that:

\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)=\left[\prod_{j=1}^{q}z_{i_{j}}\right]_{1\leq i_{1},...,i_{q}\leq p}.

(15)

The HM distance in Eq. (
24
) is rewritten as:

\displaystyle HM\left(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{U}}\left[\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{V}}\left[\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\right]\right\|_{2}^{2},

(16)

where
\left\|.\right\|_{2}
represents the L2 norm. We now devise an economical
way to compute
HM\left(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\right)
. We begin with
a well-known equality:

\left(a_{1}+...+a_{p}\right)^{q}=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},...,i_{q}\leq p}\prod_{j=1}^{q}a_{i_{j}}.

(17)

Given
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
, the dot product
<\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right),\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\right)>

can be further derived as

\sum_{1\leq i_{1},...,i_{q}\leq p}\prod_{j=1}^{q}\left(\mathbf{z}_{i_{j}}\mathbf{z}_{i_{j}}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{i}\right)^{q}=\left\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\right\rangle^{q},

(18)

where we apply Eq. (
27
) for
a_{i}=\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}_{i}
.
The HM distance
HM\left(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\right)
in Eq. (
26
)
can be further derived as

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{U}}\left[<\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}>^{q}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{V}}\left[<\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}>^{q}\right]

\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\>\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,-2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathbb{U},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{V}}\left[<\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}>^{q}\right].

(19)

It is important to note that the calculation of the HM distance in
Eq. (
24
) becomes feasible, with the space complexity
reduced from
\mathcal{O}\left(p^{q}\right)
to
\mathcal{O}\left(p\right)
.
In practice,
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
are empirical distributions,
allowing for a convenient approximation of
HM\left(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}\right)

in Eq. (
29
) based on mini-batches.

3.7
Class-aware Higher-Order Moment Matching

We leverage the transportation network
\mathcal{T}
to propose a
more elegant loss as:

\mathcal{L}^{HMM}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}}\left[\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}^{T}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\phi_{q}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)\right]\right\|_{2}^{2}

=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}

\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S}}\left[\phi_{q}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{T}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\phi_{q}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\right]\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(20)

The objective function in (
30
) aims to match a
class in the source domain and the corresponding class in the target
domain with the guidance from the transformation network. The transportation
network
\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
provides
a value close to
1
if the target example
\mathbf{x}
appears to belong
to class
m
. Expanding the above expression, we arrive at:

\mathcal{L}^{HMM}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\biggl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S}}\left[<G\left(\mathbf{x}\right),G\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)>^{q}\right]\\
+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}^{T}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)\right)<G\left(\mathbf{x}\right),G\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)>^{q}\right]\\
-2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{P}_{m}^{S},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}^{T}}\left[\mathcal{T}_{m}\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)\right)<G\left(\mathbf{x}\right),G\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)>^{q}\right]\biggr).

(21)

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we present CLOTH, a novel method for unsupervised domain
adaptation (UDA). Our approach utilizes class-aware optimal transport
(OT) to measure the distance between a distribution over source class-conditional
distributions and a mixture distribution of source and target data.
To efficiently handle class-aware OT, we propose an amortization solution
that uses deep neural networks to amortize the transportation probabilities
and the cost function. In particular, we employ a multi-class source
discriminator as a deep network to amortize the cost function. Additionally,
we integrate class-aware Higher-order Moment Matching (HMM) into our
CLOTH framework, which combines both OT distance and HMM distance.
Extensive experiments demonstrate the superiority of our proposed
CLOTH over state-of-the-art baselines on benchmark datasets.

Appendix A
Background

In what follows, we present the background of OT for two discrete
distributions. Consider two discrete distributions:
\mathbb{P}^{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\pi_{i}^{1}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}}

and
\mathbb{P}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\pi_{j}^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}}

where
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1}=\left[\pi_{i}^{1}\right]_{i=1}^{M}
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{2}=\left[\pi_{j}^{2}\right]_{j=1}^{N}

are probability masses,
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}\right\}_{i=1}^{M}

and
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
are the sets of atoms,
and
\delta_{\mathbf{x}}
is the Dirac delta distribution concentrated at

\mathbf{x}
. Let
c\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right)
be a cost function.
The OT distance between
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}
w.r.t.
the cost function
c
is defined as

\min_{A\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times N}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}c\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right),

(32)

where
A=\left[a_{ij}\right]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M\times N}
of non-negative
elements satisfying
\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}=\pi_{i}^{1},\forall i\in\left\{1,...,M\right\}

and
\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{ij}=\pi_{j}^{2},\forall j\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
.

In addition,
a_{ij}\in\left[0;1\right]
is interpreted as the probability
to match
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}
or to transport
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1}

to
\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}
, which suffers the cost
c\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right)
.
Therefore, the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}c\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{1},\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right)

can be viewed as the total cost to match
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}

or to transport
\mathbb{P}^{1}
to
\mathbb{P}^{2}
.

methods such as HoMM
[
5
]
, SPL
[
56
]
,
and SHOT
[
31
]
. By leveraging the extracted ResNet-50
features, we only need to design a simple network for the generator

G
. For the
Office-Home
and
ImageCLEF-DA
datasets,
the generator consists of a single dense layer with
256
hidden
neurons. For the
Office-31
dataset, it includes two hidden
dense layers with
1024

D.3
Performance Comparison: Sinkhorn Algorithm vs. Our Amortization Solution

One interesting study is the comparison between our amortization solution
and the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
9
]
in solving the
optimization problem in (3). We first rewrite the optimization problem
in (3) in the form of an entropic regularized version:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{c,\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\epsilon}\left(\mathbb{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{S}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{A}\biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{im}c\left(G\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\right),\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}\right)

\displaystyle-\epsilon H(A):

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M}a_{im}=\frac{1}{N_{T}},\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{im}=\pi_{m}\biggr\},

(37)

is the regularization rate. During the training, we solve this OP
using the Sinkhorn algorithm and achieve
A^{*}
at every mini-batch.
We now design two optimization problems to fairly compare two approaches:

•

To solve the optimization problem in (3) using the Sinkhorn algorithm,
the final objective function is defined as:

\min_{\mathcal{C},G,\mathcal{T}}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{C}+\mathcal{L}^{G,S}+\mathcal{L}^{G,T}+\alpha\mathcal{W}_{c,\boldsymbol{\pi}}^{\epsilon}\left(\mathbb{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{S}\right)\right\}

(38)

•

To solve the optimization problem in (3) using our amortization solution,
we formulate the final objective function, which resembles the one
in (12):

\min_{\mathcal{C},G,\mathcal{T}}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{C}+\mathcal{L}^{G,S}+\mathcal{L}^{G,T}+\alpha\mathcal{L}^{t}+\beta\mathcal{L}^{ent}\right\}

(39)

In both scenarios, we update alternatively
\mathcal{D}
by minimizing
the loss function
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{D}}
. Two experiments were
conducted on the
Office-31
dataset, and the results are reported
in Table
9
. The accuracy scores obtained
using the Sinkhorn algorithm (the
Sinkhorn
setting) are significantly
lower compared to our approach (the
Amortization
setting).
These results highlight the difficulty of the Sinkhorn algorithm in
accurately approximating the Wasserstein distance between the empirical
mixture distribution
\mathbb{Q}
of the source and target domains
in the latent space and the source class-conditional distributions

\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}
. This is attributed to the limited batch size,
which makes it challenging to precisely approximate the optimal transportation
matrix
A^{*}
. Consequently, pushing target samples to the desired

\mathbb{Q}_{m}^{S}
distributions becomes challenging, leading to
a decline in model performance. On the other hand, our approach (
Amortization
)
significantly outperforms the
Sinkhorn
setting by a large margin
(24.2%). This demonstrates the effectiveness of approximating
A^{*}

using the transportation network
\mathcal{T}
trained on mini-batches.
The transportation network
\mathcal{T}
produces accurate transportation
probabilities for each target sample, enabling them to be effectively
transported to the appropriate source class regions.
