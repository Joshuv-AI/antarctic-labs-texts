On geometry of coned-off spaces and Cannon-Thurston maps
Pranab Sardar, Ravi Tomar

Abstract
. A typical question addressed in this paper is the following. Suppose Z ⊂ Y ⊂ X Z\subset Y\subset X are hyperbolic spaces where Z Z is quasiconvex in both Y Y and X X . Let Y ^ \widehat{Y} and X ^ \widehat{X} denote the spaces obtained from Y Y and X X respectively by coning off Z Z as defined by Farb ( [ Far98 ] ). If the inclusion of the coned-off spaces Y ^ → X ^ \widehat{Y}\rightarrow\widehat{X} admits the Cannon-Thurston (CT) map then does the inclusion Y → X Y\rightarrow X also admit the Cannon-Thurston map? The main result of this paper answers this question affirmatively provided Y ^ → X ^ \widehat{Y}\rightarrow\widehat{X} satisfies Mitra’s criterion (see Lemma 2.29 ) for the existence of CT maps, although the answer in general is negative. The main application of our theorem is in the context of acylindrical complexes of hyperbolic groups. In [ Mar14 ] A. Martin proved a combination theorem for developable, acylindrical complexes of hyperbolic groups. Suppose ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 ) (\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}) is an acylindrical complex of hyperbolic groups with universal cover B B which satisfy the hypotheses of Martin’s theorem. Suppose 𝒴 1 ⊂ 𝒴 {\mathcal{Y}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{Y}} is a connected subcomplex such that the subcomplex of groups ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 1 ) (\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}) also satisfies the hypotheses of Martin’s theorem, it has universal cover B 1 B_{1} and the natural homomorphism π 1 ​ ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 1 ) → π 1 ​ ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 ) \pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}) is injective. It follows from the main theorem of this paper that the inclusion π 1 ​ ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 1 ) → π 1 ​ ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 ) \pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}) admits the CT map if the inclusion B 1 → B B_{1}\rightarrow B satisfies Mitra’s criterion. Also π 1 ​ ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 1 ) \pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}) is quasiconvex in π 1 ​ ( 𝒢 , 𝒴 ) \pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}}) if in addition B 1 B_{1} is qi embedded in B B .

We recall that the notion of Cannon-Thurston maps or CT maps was

It was introduced in Geometric Group Theory by Mahan Mitra (Mj) in

Given a map

to the Gromov boundaries
\partial f:\partial X\rightarrow\partial Y

Since in this context even

would require an explanation. One is refered to Section

In particular, if

The CT maps in Geometric Group Theory became popular mostly through the work of
Mahan Mitra (Mj). One is referred to
[

Theorem 1.

(Theorem

Suppose we have the hypotheses of Question

(1) If the inclusion

(2) Suppose the CT map

B_{i}

Two problems on complexes of hyperbolic groups

Motivated by the celebrated combination theorem of Bestvina and Feighn
[
BF92
]
for graphs of hyperbolic
groups, Misha Kapovich (
[
Kap08
, Problem 90]
) asked if one can prove a combination theorem for complexes of hyperbolic groups.
We recall the relevant definitions and results about complexes of groups in Section
4.2
.
However, one may pose an important special case of M. Kapovich’s problem as follows.

Problem 1.

Let
(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}})
be a developable complex of groups such that
the following hold:

(a)
{\mathcal{Y}}
is a finite connected simplicial complex,

(b) local groups are hyperbolic and local maps are quasiisometric embeddings,

(c) the universal cover of
(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}})
is a hyperbolic space.

Then find sufficient conditions under which
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}})
is a hyperbolic group.

Remark 1.2
.

We shall refer to a complex of groups
({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{Y}})
satisfying (a), (b), (c) of Problem 1 to be a

complex of hyperbolic groups
.

For graphs of hyperbolic groups Bestvina-Feighn’s hallway flaring condition proved to be necessary and sufficient,

[
BF92
]
,
[
Ger98
, Corollary 6.7]
.
Similar results follow from
[
MS12
]
when the local maps are quasiisometries, i.e. isomorphisms
onto finite index subgroups. In contrast to that in
[
Mar14
]
(along with
[
Mar15
, Corollary, p. 805]
)
A. Martin proved a combination theorem for acylindrical complexes of hyperbolic groups.
We recall the statement of Martin’s theorem in Section
4.2.2
.

However, these combination theorems motivate the following.

2.1.
Basic coarse geometric notions

In this subsection we recall some standard notions of coarse geometry and set up
some notation and convention.
Let
X
be a metric space. For all
x,y\in X
their distance in
X
is denoted by
d_{X}(x,y)
or
simply
d(x,y)
when
X
is understood.
For any
A\subset X
and
D\geq 0
we denote the closed
D
-neighborhood, i.e.

\{x\in X:d(x,a)\leq D\mbox{ for some }\,a\in A\}
by
N_{D}(A)
. For
A,B\subset X
we shall denote by
d_{X}(A,B)
the
quantity
\inf\{d_{X}(a,b):a\in A,b\in B\}
. The
Hausdorff distance
between
A,B
in
X
is defined to be

Hd(A,B):=\inf\{D\geq 0:A\subset N_{D}(B),B\subset N_{D}(A)\}
. If
A\subset X
we say that
A
is
rectifiably connected

in
X
if for all
x,y\in A
, there is a path
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow X
joining
x,y
which is of finite length such that

\alpha([0,1])\subset A
. For a rectifiably connected subset
A\subset X
, by induced length metric on
A
we mean
the length metric associated to the restriction of
d_{X}
on
A
. We shall assume that this is a geodesic
metric as defined next.

Suppose
x,y\in X
. A
geodesic (segment)
joining
x
to
y
is an
isometric embedding
\alpha:[a,b]\rightarrow X
where
[a,b]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
is an interval such that
\alpha(a)=x,\alpha(b)=y
.
Most of the time we are interested only in the image of this embedding rather than the embedding itself.
We shall denote by
[x,y]_{X}
or simply by
[x,y]
the image of a geodesic joining
x,y
.
If any two points of
X
can be joined by a geodesic segment
then
X
is said to be a
geodesic metric space
and
d
is called a geodesic metric on
X
.
In this paper, graphs are assumed to be connected
and it is assumed that each edge is assigned a unit length so that the graphs are naturally geodesic metric spaces
(see
[
BH99
, Section 1.9, I.1]
). Below are some other definitions important for us.

(1)

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are two metric spaces and
\rho:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is any map. A map
f:X\rightarrow Y
is said to be a

\rho
-
proper embedding
if
d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq M
implies
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\leq\rho(M)
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. A map
f:X\rightarrow Y

is called a proper embedding if it is a
\rho
-proper embedding for some map
\rho:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
.

In all instances in this paper the space
X
is a subspace of
Y
,
f
is the inclusion map and the metric on
X
is the
induced length metric
[
BH99
, Definition 3.3, I.3]
from
Y
.
Throughout the paper we have used
\iota

to denote inclusion maps.

(2)

If
L\geq 0
then an
L
-Lipschitz

map
f:X\rightarrow Y
between two metric spaces is one such that
d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq Ld_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
for all

x,x^{\prime}\in X
. A
1
-Lipschitz map will simply be called a Lipschitz map.

A very common instance of Lipschitz maps that will be encountered later is the following: If
Y
is a length space
and
X
is a subspace with the induced length metric from
Y
, then the inclusion map
\iota:X\rightarrow Y
is Lipschitz.

(3)

A map
f:X\rightarrow Y
is said to be
L
-
coarsely Lipschitz
for a constant

L\geq 0
if
d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq L+Ld_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. A map
f:X\rightarrow Y
is said to be
coarsely Lipschitz if it is
L
-coarsely Lipschitz for some
L\geq 0
.

(4)

Given
\lambda\geq 1,\epsilon\geq 0
, a map

f:X\rightarrow Y
is said to be a
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasiisometric embedding
if for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
we have,

\dfrac{1}{\lambda}d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})-\epsilon\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq\lambda d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})+\epsilon.

A
(\lambda,\lambda)
-qi embedding is simply called a
\lambda
-qi embedding.

The map
f
is said to be
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-
quasiisometry
if
f
is a
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasiisometric embedding

and moreover,
N_{D}(f(X))=Y
for some
D\geq 0
.

(5)

A
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-
quasigeodesic
in a metric space
X
is a
(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasiisometric embedding
from an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
in
X
.

A (quasi)geodesic
\alpha:I\rightarrow X
is called a
(quasi)geodesic ray
if
I=[0,\infty)
and it is called a
(quasi)geodesic

line if
I={\mathbb{R}}
.
A
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasigeodesic segment or ray or line will simply be called a
\lambda
-quasigeodesic segment or
ray or line resepectively.

Convention.
Occasionally we use phrases like
uniform qi embedding
,
uniform quasigeodesic
etc
if (1) we do not need an explicit value of the corresponding parameters and (2) it is clear that there is
such a value given the hypotheses of the lemma or proposition; e.g. see the second part of the following lemma.

The first part of the following lemma is very standard and the second part follows from the first immediately,
and hence we skip both their proofs.

Lemma 2.1

2.2.
Gromov hyperbolic spaces

In this subsection we briefly recall some definitions and results about (Gromov) hyperbolic metric spaces that will be
relevant for us. We refer the reader to Gromov’s original article
[
Gro85
]
as well as some of the standard references
like
[
GdlH90
]
,
[

]
for more details.

Definition 2.4
.

(1) By a geodesic polygon with
n

we mean a choice of

n
points in
X

x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}
and
n
geodesic segments
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]
,
1\leq i\leq n

where
x_{n+1}=x_{1}
.

(2) Given
\delta\geq 0
, a geodesic triangle

in a geodesic metric space is said to be
\delta
-
slim
if any side of
\triangle
is contained in the union of
\delta
-neighborhood of remaining two sides.

A geodesic metric space

is said to be

\delta\geq 0
such that every geodesic

is
\delta
-slim.

In this case the space is called
\delta
-hyperbolic.

The following lemma is immediate from the definition of hyperbolic spaces.

Lemma 2.5
.

A geodesic polygon with
n
sides in a
\delta
-hyperbolic space is
(n-2)
-slim, i.e.
any side of the polygon is contained in the
(n-2)\delta
-neighborhood of the union
of the remaining sides.

The conclusion of the following theorem is one of the most important properties of hyperbolic metric spaces.

Theorem 2.6
.

(
[
BH99
, Theorem 1.7, III.H]
(
Stability of quasigeodesics
)
) For
\delta\geq 0,\lambda\geq 1,\epsilon\geq 0
there exists a constant

D_{\ref{stability}}=D_{\ref{stability}}(\delta,\lambda,\epsilon)
with the following property:

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space. Then, the Hausdorff distance between a

(\lambda,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic and a geodesic joining the same pair of end points is
less than or equal to
D_{\ref{stability}}
.

It easily follows from this theorem that hyperbolicity is invariant under quasiisometry.

Definition 2.7
.

A finitely generated group
G
is said to be hyperbolic if the Cayley graph of
G
with respect to some (any) finite generating
set is hyperbolic.

It is a standard consequence of Milnor-S̆varc lemma
[
BH99
, Proposition 8.19, I.8]

that the Cayley graphs
of any group
G
with respect to any two finite generating sets are quasiisometric. That hyperbolicity of a group
is well-defined follows from this and the fact that hyperbolicity is a qi invariant property
for geodesic metric spaces.

Definition 2.8

(
Quasiconvex subspaces).

Let

be a (hyperbolic) metric space and let

.
Let

. Then

is said to be

-quasiconvex in

with end points in

is

is said to be quasiconvex if it is

-quasiconvex for some

. A

If

then we say that

is quasiconvex in

is a quasiconvex subset of a Cayley graph
of

The following lemma gives natural examples of quasiconvex subspaces in hyperbolic spaces.
The proof of the first part of the lemma is immediate from the definition of quasiconvexity,
and the second part follows from stability of quasigeodesics. Hence we omit the proofs.

Lemma 2.9
.

(1) Any geodesic in a
\delta
-hyperbolic space is
\delta
-quasiconvex.

(2) Any
(k,\epsilon)
-quasigeodesic in a
\delta
-hyperbolic space is

-quasiconvex.

The next lemma shows persistences of quasiconvexity under qi embeddings of hyperbolic spaces.
Thus it follows that quasiconvexity of any subset, for instance any subgroup, of a hyperbolic group
is well-defined, i.e. independent of the Cayley graphs.

Lemma 2.10
.

Given
\delta\geq 0,k\geq 1,K\geq 0
there is a constant

K_{\ref{qi map qc}}=K_{\ref{qi map qc}}(k,\delta,K)
such that the following holds:

Suppose

is a

-qi embedding of
\delta
-hyperbolic metric spaces.
If

is

-quasiconvex then

is

-quasiconvex.
In particular,

is uniformly quasiconvex in

Proof.

Let

be a geodesic in

On the other hand, since

Lemma 2.11

Given

The following lemma shows that finite union of quasiconvex sets is quasiconvex.
The proof is clear by induction and hence we skip it.

Lemma 2.12

Lemma 2.13
.

Given
\delta\geq 0,K\geq 0
there is a constant

2.3.
Gromov boundary

Now, we briefly recall some basic facts about Gromov boundary of hyperbolic spaces.
For more details see
[
GdlH90
]
,
[
BH99
]
.
Let
X
be a hyperbolic geodesic metric space. Two (quasi)geodesic rays
\alpha,\beta
are said to be

asymptotic
if the Hausdorff distance between
\alpha
and
\beta
is finite. This gives an equivalence
relation on the set
Geo(X)
of all geodesic rays (resp. on the set
QGeo(X)
of all quasigeodesics) in
X
.
The equivalence class of a (quasi)geodesic ray
\alpha
is denoted by
\alpha(\infty)
. We denote the set of
all equivalence classes of (quasi)geodesic rays by
\partial X
(resp.
\partial_{q}X
) and call them the
geodesic

(resp.
quasigeodesic
)
boundary
of

If

is a proper hyperbolic space then
\bar{X}:=X\cup\partial X
is a compact metrizable space for
a natural topology, in which
X
is an open subset of
\bar{X}
.
However, since the spaces that we consider later could also be non-proper we shall use the following definition.

We recall that for any metric space
Z
and points
z,z_{1},z_{2}\in Z
, the Gromov product of
z_{1},z_{2}
with respect to

z
is the number
\frac{1}{2}(d(z,z_{1})+d(z,z_{2})-d(z_{1},z_{2}))
and it is denoted by
(z_{1}.z_{2})_{z}
.

Definition 2.14
.

[
Alo91
, Definition 4.1]

Suppose
X
is a hyperbolic metric space. A sequence of points
\{x_{n}\}
in
X
is said to converge
to infinity if
\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}(x_{m}.x_{n})_{x}=\infty
for some (any)
x\in X
.

Let
S_{\infty}(X)
denote the set of all sequence of points in
X
which converge to infinity. On this set one defines
an equivalence relation by setting
\{x_{n}\}\sim\{y_{n}\}
if and only if
\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}(x_{m}.y_{n})_{x}=\infty
.
The following is a very basic lemma. See
[
Alo91
]
for a proof.

Lemma 2.15
.

(1) If
\{x_{n}\}\in S_{\infty}(X)
and
\{x_{n_{k}}\}
is a subsequence of
\{x_{n_{k}}\}
then
\{x_{n_{k}}\}\in S_{\infty}(X)

and
\{x_{n}\}\sim\{x_{n_{k}}\}
.

(2) If
\{x_{n}\},\{y_{n}\}\in S_{\infty}(X)
then
\{x_{n}\}\sim\{y_{n}\}
if and only if
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(x_{n}.y_{n})_{x}=\infty
.

Definition 2.16

(

The sequential boundary
\partial_{s}X
of a hyperbolic metric space

is defined to be
S_{\infty}(X)/\sim
.

If
\{x_{n}\}\in S_{\infty}(X)
then the equivalence class of

will be denoted by
[\{x_{n}\}]
.
If

then we say

converges to
\xi
.
Here are some of the basic facts about boundaries of hyperbolic spaces. For the parts (1) and (3) of the Lemma below see

[

, Chapter III.H]

(1)

Lemma 2.23
.

Suppose

2.4.
Cannon-Thurston maps

The following proposition is very standard.

Proposition 2.24
.

(
[
V0̈5
, Theorem 5.38]
,
[
Gro85
, Theorem 7.2.H]
,
[
BH99
, III.H, Theorem 3.9]
)

If
f:X\rightarrow Y
is a qi embedding where
X,Y
are hyperbolic spaces then the
map
\partial f:\partial X\rightarrow\partial Y
, as defined in Lemma
2.17
(3)
is continuous. Moreover, if
X,Y
are proper metric spaces then it is a closed
embedding.

If
f
is quasiisometry then
\partial f
is a homeomorphism.

Consequently it is natural to ask if non-qi embeddings could also induce continuous maps
between the Gromov boundaries of hyperbolic spaces. This provides a motivation
to the following definition.

Definition 2.25

(
Cannon-Thurston map).

Let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be a map between two hyperbolic metric spaces. We say that the
Cannon-Thurston (CT) map exists for
f
or
f
admits the CT map
if the following hold:

(1) Given any
\xi\in\partial X
and any sequence
\{x_{n}\}
in
X
with
\xi=[\{x_{n}\}]
,
the sequence
\{f(x_{n})\}\in S_{\infty}(Y)
and
[\{f(x_{n})\}]
depends only
\xi
and not on
on the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
. Thus we have a map
\partial f:\partial X\rightarrow\partial Y
.

(2) The map
\partial f
is continuous.

As mentioned in the introduction, the notion of CT maps between hyperbolic metric spaces were introduced
by Mahan Mitra (
[
Mit98b
]
). Our definition is an adaptation from his definition; however,
it is easy to check that these definitions are equivalent. One also notes that the CT map is unique if it exists.
However, the Lemma below shows that (2) in the definition of CT maps follows from (1).

Lemma 2.26
.

Suppose
f:X\rightarrow Y
is any map between hyperbolic metric spaces which satisfies the condition (1) of Definition
2.25
.
Then
\partial f:\partial X\rightarrow\partial Y
is continuous too, i.e.
\partial f
is the CT map.

Proof.

Fix
x_{0}\in X,y_{0}\in Y
. Suppose
\{\xi_{n}\}
is a sequence in
\partial_{s}X
and
\xi_{n}\rightarrow\xi\in\partial_{s}X
.
We want to show that
\partial f(\xi_{n})\rightarrow\partial f(\xi)
. Suppose this is not the case.
Let
\xi_{k}=[\{x^{k}_{n}\}]
and
\xi=[\{x_{n}\}]
. Then there is
R\geq 0
such that upto passing to a subsequence
of
\{\xi_{n}\}
we may assume that
\liminf_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}(f(x^{k}_{m}).f(x_{n}))_{y_{0}}\leq R
and

\liminf_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}(x^{k}_{m}.x_{n})_{x_{0}}\geq k
for all
k
. This implies that for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is
m_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}

such that
(x^{k}_{m_{k}}.x_{m_{k}})_{x_{0}}\geq k
but
(f(x^{k}_{m_{k}}).f(x_{m_{k}}))_{y_{0}}\leq R
.
Consequently, by Lemma
2.15

x^{k}_{m_{k}}\rightarrow\xi
but
f(x^{k}_{m_{k}})\not\rightarrow\partial f(\xi)
- a contradiction. ∎

Corollary 2.27
.

Suppose
X,Y
are hyperbolic metric space, and
f:X\rightarrow Y
and
g:\partial_{s}X\rightarrow\partial_{s}Y

are any maps which satisfy the following property:

For any
\xi\in\partial_{s}X
and any sequence
x_{n}\rightarrow\xi
where
x_{n}\in X
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is a
subsequence
\{x_{n_{k}}\}
of
\{x_{n}\}
such that
f(x_{n_{k}})\rightarrow g(\xi)
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Then
g
is the
CT map induced by
f
.

Proof.

Suppose
\xi\in\partial_{s}X
and
\{x_{n}\}
is a sequence in
X
converging to
\xi
.
Then any subsequence of
\{f(x_{n})\}
has a subsequence which converges to
g(\xi)
. Since
\bar{Y}

is a Hausdorff space it follows by Lemma
2.3
that
f(x_{n})\rightarrow g(\xi)
. Then we are done by Lemma

2.26
. ∎

Remark 2.28
.

When CT map does not exist:

Suppose
X,Y
are hyperbolic metric spaces and
f:X\rightarrow Y
is any map. We note that the CT map does not exist for
f
means
that there is a point
\xi\in\partial_{s}X
and a sequence
x_{n}\rightarrow\xi
in
X
such that
\{f(x_{n})\}
does not converge
to any point of
\partial_{s}Y
. This in turn implies that either (1) there are two subsequences of
\{f(x_{n})\}
converging
to two distinct points of
\partial_{s}Y
or (2) there is subsequence of
\{f(x_{n})\}
which has no subsequence
converging to a point of

is bounded, which,
of course, is impossible if

is a proper embedding.

The following lemma gives a sufficient condition for the existence of Cannon-Thurston maps.
Although it was proved by Mitra for proper hyperbolic metric spaces, it is true
for any hyperbolic metric space. In fact, it is immediate from Lemma

(0)
and Lemma

.

Lemma 2.29
.

(
Mitra’s criterion
,
[

, Lemma 2.1]
)

Suppose

are hyperbolic geodesic metric spaces and

is a proper embedding.
Then

admits the Cannon-Thurston map if the following holds:

Given
y_{0}\in Y
there exists a non-negative function
M(N)
, such that

as

and
such that for all geodesic segments
\lambda
lying outside

in

, any geodesic segment in
X
joining the end points of

lies outside

in

.

Remark 2.30
.

(1)

In Mitra’s criterion properness of

is redundant since it follows from the rest.

(2)

It is clear from the proof of
[

that if

4.1.
Application to (relatively) hyperbolic groups

Complexes of groups are natural generalizations of graphs of groups (see
[

Gerstein and Stallings in
[

Subsequently, a more general theory of complexes of groups was studied independently by Corson
[

]

and Haefliger

]

of groups needed for our purpose. For more details, one is referred to
[

]

[

]

For the rest of this subsection, we shall denote by
{\mathcal{Y}}
a finite connected simplicial complex. Let
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{Y}})

denote the directed graph whose vertex set is the set of simplices of
{\mathcal{Y}}
and given two simplices
\tau\subset\sigma

we have a directed edge
e
from
\sigma
to
\tau
. In this case we write
e=(\sigma,\tau)
,
o(e)=\sigma
and
t(e)=\tau
.
Two directed edges

are said to be

if
t(e)=o(e^{\prime})
. In that case the composition
is denoted by
e*e^{\prime}
.

Definition 4.3

(
Complex of groups).

A complex of groups
(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}})=(G_{\sigma},\psi_{a},g_{a,b})
over
Y
consists of the following data:

(1)

For each
\sigma\in V({\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}))
, there is a group
G_{\sigma}
- called the local group at
\sigma
.

(2)

For each edge
e\in E({\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}))
, there is an injective homomorphism
\psi_{e}:G_{i(e)}\rightarrow G_{t(e)}
.
These homomorphisms are referred to as the local maps.

(3)

For each pair of composable edges
e,e^{\prime}\in E({\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}))
, a twisting element
g_{e,e^{\prime}}\in G_{t(e)}
with the following properties:

(i)
Ad(g_{e,e^{\prime}})\psi_{e*e^{\prime}}=\psi_{e}\psi_{e^{\prime}}
where
Ad(g_{e,e^{\prime}})
denotes the conjugation by
g_{e,e^{\prime}}
,

(ii)(Cocycle condition)
\psi_{e}(g_{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}})g_{e,e^{\prime}*e^{\prime\prime}}=g_{e,e^{\prime}}g_{e*e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}}
for each triple

e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}
of composable edges of
E({\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{Y}}))
.

The cocycle condition is empty if dimension of
{\mathcal{Y}}
is
2
. If
{\mathcal{Y}}
is
1

{\mathcal{Y}}

is the same as the graph of groups over
{\mathcal{Y}}
. However, given a complex of groups one can define
a complex of space.

Definition 4.4

(

[

A complex of spaces

(1)

\sigma

(2)

The

(i_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(x),s)\\

4.2.2.
Acylindrical complexes of groups

In
[
Mar14
]
(along with
[

) A. Martin proved the following theorem for complexes of

Theorem
(
[
Mar14
, p. 34]
)
Let
(\mathcal{G},{\mathcal{Y}})
be a developable complex of groups such that
the following holds:

•

(M1)
{\mathcal{Y}}

•

(M2) all the face groups are hyperbolic and local maps are quasiisometric embeddings,

.

(2)

(3)

the natural map

are the universal covers
of

5.1.
Polygons of groups

Following is the main result of this subsection that we obtain as an application of Theorem

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{Y}
is a regular Euclidean polygon with at least
4
edges and let
({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{Y}})
be a simple complex of hyperbolic groups
such that the following are satisfied:

(1)

In any vertex group, intersection of two different edge groups is equal to the subgroup coming from the barycenter of
{\mathcal{Y}}
.

(2)

The universal cover
B
of

is a hyperbolic metric space.

(3)

The action of
G=\pi_{1}({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{Y}})
on
B
is acylindrical.

Suppose
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
is an edge of
{\mathcal{Y}}
and
({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{Y}}_{1})
is the restriction of
({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{Y}})
to
{\mathcal{Y}}_{1}
. Let

.
Then
G
is a hyperbolic group and
H
is a quasiconvex subgroup of

.

Proof.

We begin with the following simple observation.
By
[
BH99
, 12.29, p. 390, II.12]
,
({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{Y}})
is non-positively curved and
hence it is developable. Also

B
is a piecewise Euclidean polygon complex, and by
[
BH99
, Theorem 7.50, I.7]

is a complete geodesic metric space since all simplices are isometric to each other.
It follows that

is a CAT(0) space (see
[
BH99
, paragraph after Theorem 4.17, p. 562, III.C]
).
Thus all the hypotheses of Martin’s theorem are satisfied for

and hence

is a hyperbolic group.

Next part of the proof makes use of the following three lemmas. We shall need the following definition.
Let
d
denote the metric on
B
and let
d_{P}
denote the metric on any polygon
P
in
B
. We also assume that each edge in
B
is of length
1
.

Definition 5.2
.

(
[
BH99
, Chapter I.7, Definition 7.8]
)
Suppose
x\in B
. Define

\epsilon(x)=\inf\{\epsilon(x,P):\text{$P$ is a polygon in $B$ containing $x$}\}

where
\epsilon(x,P):=\{d_{P}(x,e):e\text{ is an edge of $P$ not containing $x$}\}
.

The lemma below can be proved in the same way as
[
BH99
, Lemma 7.9]
.
For the sake of completeness, we include a sketch of proof.

Lemma 5.3
.

Fix
x\in B
. If
y\in B
is such that
d(x,y)<\epsilon(x)
then any polygon
P

which contains
y
also contains
x
and
d(x,y)=d_{P}(x,y)
.

Sketch of proof:
To prove the lemma, it is sufficient to show that if

\Sigma=(x=x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{n}=y)
is an
m
-string (see
[
BH99
, I.7, Definition 7.4]
)
of length
l(\Sigma)<\epsilon(x)
, with
m\geq 2
, then
\Sigma^{\prime}=(x_{0},x_{2},...,x_{n})
is an
(m-1)
-string
with length
l(\Sigma^{\prime})\leq l(\Sigma)
. Now, by definition of
m
-string, there is a polygon
P_{1}
such
that
x_{1},x_{2}\in P_{1}
. Since
l(\Sigma)<\epsilon(x)
,
x_{0}\in P_{1}
. By triangle inequality,

d_{P_{1}}(x_{0},x_{2})\leq d_{P_{1}}(x_{0},x_{1})+d_{P_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2})
. Thus,
(x_{0},x_{2},...,x_{n})
is an

(m-1)
-string of length less that or equal to
l(\Sigma)
. ∎

The following lemma must be well known or obvious but we could not find a written proof anywhere in the literature.
Hence we include a proof here.

Lemma 5.4
.

The inclusion of any polygon
P
in
B
is an isometric embedding where
P
is given its Euclidean metric.

Proof.

Suppose
x,y\in P
are two interior points and
[x,y]_{P}
is the straightline joining
x,y
in
P
.
For all

the ball of radius
\epsilon(z)
in

is isometrically embedded in
B
by Lemma
5.3
.
Hence, a small neighborhood of
z
in
[x,y]_{P}
is isometrically embedded in
B
. This shows that
[x,y]_{P}
is a local
geodesic in

. Since
B
is a CAT(0) space, it follows that
[x,y]_{P}
is a geodesic in

. Thus the interior of

is isometrically embedded in

.
The lemma follows easily from this and the following standard fact (see
[

, Proposition 1.4(1), II.1]

Suppose

are two sequences in a CAT(0) space

such that

. Let

in

converges

.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

and

be two distinct polygons in

. Suppose

Let

are geodesics in

.
Let

. Let

be the open ball of radius

around

in
\alpha

are the endpoints
of

respectively. Note that

is a

and

.
If there is no string in

, connecting

and

\Sigma=(x_{0}=x,x_{1},...,x_{n}=y)
is any other

then

. By definition of an

there exists a sequence of polygons, say
(P_{0}^{\prime},P_{1}^{\prime},...,P_{n-1}^{\prime})
, such that

for

.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that

. Note
that if

and hence

. Thus we assume that

belongs to a side of

, containing

. Since the angle between

and

,

. By the same reasoning,

belongs to a side of

containing

. By continuing in

Definition 5.8

In
