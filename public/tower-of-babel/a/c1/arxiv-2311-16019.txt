Sketched and truncated polynomial Krylov subspace methods: matrix Sylvester equations
Davide Palitta, Marcel Schweitzer, Valeria Simoncini

Abstract
. Thanks to its great potential in reducing both computational cost and memory requirements, combining sketching and Krylov subspace techniques has attracted a lot of attention in the recent literature on projection methods for linear systems, matrix function approximations, and eigenvalue problems. Applying this appealing strategy in the context of linear matrix equations turns out to be far more involved than a straightforward generalization. These difficulties include analyzing well-posedness of the projected problem and deriving possible error estimates depending on the sketching properties. Further computational complications include the lack of a natural residual norm estimate and of an explicit basis for the generated subspace. In this paper we propose a new sketched-and-truncated polynomial Krylov subspace method for Sylvester equations that aims to address all these issues. The potential of our novel approach, in terms of both computational time and storage demand, is illustrated with numerical experiments. Comparisons with a state-of-the-art projection scheme based on rational Krylov subspaces are also included.

1.
Introduction

In the recent literature sketching has been successfully combined with Krylov subspace methods for the solution of linear systems of equations and eigenvalue problems
[
4
,
5
,
33
,
47
]
as well as the numerical approximation of the action of a matrix function on a vector
[
36
,
15
,
24
]
. In this paper we address another central problem in numerical linear algebra, namely the solution of large-scale Sylvester matrix equations of the form

(1.1)

\bm{A}\bm{X}+\bm{X}\bm{B}=C_{1}C_{2}^{\top},

where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}}
,
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}}
, and the right-hand side is supposed to be of low rank, i.e.,
C_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r}
,
C_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r}
with
r\ll\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
. The low rank of the right-hand side, along with suitable additional conditions on the coefficient matrices, like, e.g., a large enough relative distance between the spectra of
\bm{A}
and
-\bm{B}
, implies that the desired solution
\bm{X}
admits accurate low-rank approximations; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
21
,
39
]
.

For the sake of simplicity, in the following we focus on the case
n\equiv n_{1}=n_{2}
, namely the solution
\bm{X}
is an
n\times n
matrix, but everything we present here can be straightforwardly generalized to the case
n_{1}\neq n_{2}
.

The Sylvester equation (
1.1
) can be encountered in a variety of applications. For instance, several model order reduction techniques give rise to Sylvester equations that feature very large and sparse coefficients
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}

[
1
]
. Similarly, the discretization of certain elliptic and parabolic PDEs yields Sylvester equations of the form (
1.1
); see, e.g.,
[
37
,
35
]
.
Another source of applications are linearizations of nonlinear problems, such as the algebraic Riccati equation, where, in general, the solution of (
1.1
) is needed in iterative solvers
[
12
]
. We refer to the
surveys
[
44
,
11
]
for further applications and details.

Our goal is to design an efficient projection procedure for the solution of (
1.1
) based on the polynomial Krylov subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{d}(\bm{A},C_{1})
and
\mathcal{K}_{d}(\bm{B}^{\top},C_{2})
, where the Krylov subspace is defined as

(1.2)

\mathcal{K}_{d}(\bm{A},C_{1})=\text{Range}\{[C_{1},\bm{A}C_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}^{d-1}C_{1}]\},

and analogously for
\mathcal{K}_{d}(\bm{B}^{\top},C_{2})
.

Early contributions to matrix equation solvers suggested the use of (
1.2
) as approximation space as well; see the pioneering work in
[
41
]
for the Lyapunov equation, and later contributions in, e.g.,

[
29
]
for the Sylvester equation.
However, it is well-known that the rate of convergence of plain polynomial Krylov subspace methods for Sylvester equations is rather poor in general, and a significant number of iterations is often needed to converge, thus worsening the performance of the overall solution procedure. Attempts to lower the computational cost per iteration and the memory requirements in case of symmetric
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
can be found in
[
38
]
. With the same goals, a restarted procedure is presented in
[
31
]
. These contributions come with their limitations and restrictions, so that in practice, projection schemes based on the extended Krylov subspace
and the rational Krylov subspace are usually preferred, in spite of their more expensive basis construction; see
[
44
]
.

Projection-based methods are not the only procedures available in the literature for solving large-scale Sylvester equations. A non-complete list of different solvers sees low-rank Alternating Direction Implicit (ADI) methods
[
10
]
, sign function methods
[
8
]
, and hierarchical algorithms
[
22
]
.

We refer to
[
11
,
44
]
for more detailed accounts of different approaches available for small and large scale problems.

In this paper we significantly improve the performance of polynomial Krylov subspace methods for Sylvester equations by combining the projection scheme with randomized sketching
[
32
,
50
]
. In particular, we tackle two of the main computational issues due to the large number of iterations needed by polynomial Krylov subspace methods: (i) the high cost of the orthogonalization step and (ii) the large storage demand due to the full allocation of the basis of (
1.2
).

As has been shown for linear systems and matrix functions
[
15
,
24
,
36
,
33
]
,
one way to unlock the potential of sketching is to combine it with a truncated Krylov scheme, i.e., a Krylov subspace method where the constructed basis is only locally orthogonal.
The latter approach dramatically reduces the cost of the orthogonalization step. On the other hand,
working with a non-orthogonal basis
often leads to a convergence delay; such phenomenon is well-documented for linear systems
[
46
]
and matrix functions
[
18
]
. Sketching comes into play to overcome this drawback. In particular, we show that the use of sketching is able to restore the rate of convergence of the full Krylov subspace method without significantly increasing the overall cost of the truncated technique.
We would like to mention that, to the best of our knowledge,
plain (non-sketched) truncated polynomial Krylov subspace methods for the solution of matrix equations have also never been proposed in the literature. This can thus be seen as a further contribution of this paper.

Thanks to the truncated orthogonalization step, the whole basis is not required during the basis construction,
and it is thus not stored, with significant memory savings
. On the other hand, the full basis is needed to retrieve the final solution.
We avoid
allocating
the whole basis by employing a so-called two-pass strategy.
This is a common procedure
which in our case consists in first running the sketched and truncated method without storing the basis. Then, once the prescribed level of accuracy is met, the solution is constructed by computing its low-rank factors during a second, cheaper truncated Arnoldi step; see, e.g.,
[
30
]
.

The truncated and sketched Krylov machinery has not been used so far in the solution of matrix equations, and as we will illustrate, its formulation and implementation require special attention to obtain an efficient and reliable method.

2.2.
Oblivious subspace embeddings

The basis of sketched Krylov methods is the use of so-called
oblivious subspace embeddings

[
43
,
16
,
50
]
. These allow to embed a (low-dimensional) subspace
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into
\mathbb{R}^{s}
,
s\ll n
, such that norms (or inner products) are distorted in a controlled manner.

To be specific, for a given
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
, a matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
is called an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for
\mathcal{V}
(or for brevity, simply a
“sketching matrix”
) if it fulfills
,

(2.2)

(1-\varepsilon)\|v\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{S}v\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|v\|^{2},

for all vectors
v\in\mathcal{V}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean vector norm.
By the parallelogram inequality, this is equivalent to requiring

(2.3)

{}\langle u,v\rangle-\varepsilon\|u\|\|v\|\leq\langle\bm{S}u,\bm{S}v\rangle\leq\langle u,v\rangle+\varepsilon\|u\|\|v\|,

for all
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
.

In the context of sketched Krylov methods, so-called
oblivious
subspace embeddings are very important. These are embeddings which
can be constructed without explicit knowledge of the subspace
\mathcal{V}
that shall be embedded.
To be precise,
\bm{S}
is an oblivious
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for subspaces of dimension
d
, if—with high probability—condition (
2.2
) holds for
any
subspace
\mathcal{V}
with
\dim(\mathcal{V})=d
. More precisely, the inputs for constructing
\bm{S}
are the embedding quality
\varepsilon
, the sketching dimension
s
and an accepted failure probability
\delta
. Based on these inputs,
\bm{S}
is then constructed using probabilistic methods. We refer to
[
32
, Section 8]
and
[
50
]
for an extensive treatment of oblivious subspace embeddings. As a specific example, assume that
\bm{S}
is constructed as a subsampled trigonometric transform,

\bm{S}=\sqrt{\frac{s}{n}}\bm{D}\bm{N}\bm{E},

where
\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\pm 1
chosen randomly (with equal probability),
\bm{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
contains
s
randomly selected rows of the identity matrix, and
\bm{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a trigonometric transform (e.g., discrete Fourier transform, discrete cosine transform, Walsh–Hadamard transform). Then, one can show that
\bm{S}
is an oblivious
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding with failure probability
\delta
if the sketching dimension satisfies
s=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}(d+\log\frac{n}{\delta})\log\frac{d}{\delta})
. When implemented properly, such
\bm{S}
can be applied at a cost of
\mathcal{O}(n\log s)
flops.
2
2

2

When selecting
\bm{S}
as a matrix with Gaussian random entries, one actually obtains the stronger guarantee that
s=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}(d+\log\frac{1}{\delta}))
is sufficient, but these are less attractive computationally because they are more costly to apply.
Numerical evidence suggests that, despite certain academic examples for which it fails, selecting
s=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}\frac{d}{\delta})
works well in practice; see, e.g.,
[
48
]
and
[
32
, Section 9]
.

All computationally feasible classes of matrices from which one can draw
\bm{S}
have in common that the necessary sketching dimension
s
depends quadratically on the embedding quality
\varepsilon
. As a consequence
one needs to work with rather crude accuracies,
such as

\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{2}

or
\varepsilon=1/2
, i.e., relatively large distortions in (
2.2
).

Oblivious subspace embeddings are key in our context, as the spaces we want to embed will be certain Krylov subspaces (see
Section

2.3
and
Section

3
for details), that we do not know a priori,
at the start of the method.

We mention in passing that an alternative viewpoint on subspace embeddings is
that a subspace embedding
\bm{S}
induces a semidefinite inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{\bm{S}}:=\langle\bm{S}u,\bm{S}v\rangle,

which, when restricted to
\mathcal{V}
, is an actual (positive definite) inner product; see, e.g.,
[
6
, Section 3.1]
.

This viewpoint is particularly relevant for establishing a connection between our methodology and other randomized Krylov techniques that have been recently proposed in the literature; see the discussion in
Section

2.3
.

2.3.
The sketched Arnoldi relation

The combination of Krylov subspace methods with randomized oblivious subspace embeddings in order to increase computational efficiency in the orthogonalization step (and potentially reduce storage requirements and communication) is a topic that has recently gained a lot of traction and was investigated in the context of linear systems and eigenvalue computations
[
4
,
5
,
33
]
as well as in the approximation of matrix functions
[
13
,
15
,
24
,
36
]
. The approach taken in
[
24
,
33
,
36
]
advocates the use of partially orthogonalized Krylov bases together with a subspace embedding
\bm{S}
that allows to “approximately orthogonalize” the Krylov basis at a much lower cost.

In order to analyze the resulting methods and allow to compare them to their standard, non-sketched counterparts, a
sketched Arnoldi relation
is derived in
[
36
]
, which we recall in the following. Assume that a truncated (block) Arnoldi procedure is employed for constructing bases of
\mathcal{K}_{d}(\bm{A},C_{1})
and
\mathcal{K}_{d}(\bm{B}^{\top},C_{2})
, where orthogonalization
only
against the previous
k<d
blocks is performed,
rather than against all
d
previous blocks, followed by the orthogonalization of the columns of the new block; see also
[
42
, Algorithm 6.6]
for a discussion of the non-block case
. This results in the truncated Arnoldi relations

(2.4)

\bm{A}\bm{U}_{d}=\bm{U}_{d+1}\underline{\bm{H}}_{d},\qquad\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{V}_{d}=\bm{V}_{d+1}\underline{\bm{G}}_{d},

where
\underline{\bm{H}}_{d}
and
\underline{\bm{G}}_{d}

kr

\bm{U}_{d}

\bm{V}_{d}

\underline{\bm{H}}_{d}=[\bm{H}_{d};H^{\top}]

\underline{\bm{G}}_{d}=[\bm{G}_{d};G^{\top}]

r\times r

and
G^{\top}=\bm{g}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}

Let

\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{V}_{d+1}=\bm{Q}_{V,d+1}\bm{T}_{V,d+1},\qquad\bm{T}_{V,d+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{T}_{V,d}&T_{G}\\

\end{bmatrix},

then

\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{V}_{d}=\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{V}_{d}(\bm{G}_{d}+R_{G}E_{d}^{\top})+Q_{V,d+1}\bm{\theta}_{d+1}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top},

where

The reduced QR decompositions of
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{U}_{d+1}

Thanks to the

Rewriting the relations (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{A}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}=\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{H}E_{d}^{\top})+Q_{\bm{U},d+1}\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top},\\
\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{B}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}=\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{G}E_{d}^{\top})+Q_{V,d+1}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top},\end{split}

where

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}=\bm{T}_{\bm{U},d}\bm{H}_{d}\bm{T}_{\bm{U},d}^{-1},&\qquad\widehat{H}=T_{H}\bm{h}_{d+1,d}\bm{\tau}_{d}^{-1},\\

Finally, from (

) we can derive the relations

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{A}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}=\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{H}E_{d}^{\top},\qquad\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{B}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}=\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{G}E_{d}^{\top},\end{split}

which emphasize the role of the right-hand side matrices as projection and reduction
of the matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}^{\top}
onto the generated spaces, in the
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}

and
\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}
inner product, respectively.

We stress that the full
matrices
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d},\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}
are never explicitly constructed in practice, whereas the small
matrices
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d},\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}
are used to derive the sought after problem approximation.

We end this section by commenting on the connection between the methodology outlined above and other randomized Krylov methods that were recently proposed, in particular those based on the
randomized Gram–Schmidt (RGS) process

[
4
,
5
,
15
,
47
]
. In these methods, one uses a Gram-Schmidt procedure—with respect to the sketched inner product (
2.2
) instead of the usual Euclidean inner product—for constructing the Krylov basis. In exact arithmetic, this approach yields the same bases as (
2.7
) if the QR decompositions are computed without interchanging columns. In particular, the WS-Arnoldi relations (
2.8
) and (
2.10
) are equivalent to relations obtained in
[
47
, Section 3.2]
, but they are phrased differently and reveal different structural properties.

Thus, as was already noted in
[
47
]
, methods based on the RGS process and those based on truncated Arnoldi and sketching are mathematically equivalent. There are several notable differences, though: Performing the RGS process necessarily requires keeping the whole Krylov basis in memory, and the asymptotic cost of the orthogonalization still scales as
\mathcal{O}((rd)^{2}n)
. Indeed, while inner products are computed with vectors of length
s
, all other vector operations have the same cost as in standard Gram–Schmidt. In contrast, when using truncated Arnoldi with sketching, only the last
k
basis blocks need to be kept in memory, as the coefficients for the orthogonalization in the semidefinite inner product (
2.2
) are computed from the sketched bases
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{U}_{d+1},\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{V}_{d+1}
. As we will explain in
Section

3
, the transformations (
2.7
) need not be performed explicitly, so that using this approach allows to use a two-pass strategy for reducing memory consumption; see
Section

5
below. In summary, our approach reduces the asymptotic cost and memory consumption of the basis construction in comparison to RGS. On the other hand, methods based on RGS can in general be expected to be more stable, as the truncated block Arnoldi process and the retrospective orthogonalization via
\bm{T}_{\bm{U},d}^{-1}
and
\bm{T}_{V,d}^{-1}
can potentially be sources of instability. We refer to
[
15
]
, where it is illustrated (for the problem of computing
f(A)b
, the action of a matrix function on a vector) that RGS-based methods might be successful in certain examples where those based on truncated orthogonalization are not. The precise effects causing this difference in performance are not yet fully understood, though. In particular, they are not merely related to ill-conditioning of the bases from truncated Arnoldi: As examples in
[
24
,
36
]
as well as in
Section

6
below show, the methods often continue to work well even in presence of a numerically singular basis.

3.
Sketched and truncated Krylov methods for matrix equations

We now turn more specifically to the solution of the
Sylvester equation (
1.1
) by means of projection onto
the generated spaces; see, e.g.,
[
44
]
. We seek an approximation in the form
\bm{X}_{d}=\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}\bm{V}_{d}^{\top}
, with
\bm{U}_{d},\bm{V}_{d}
generated
above
and
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{dr\times dr}
computed by solving some
surrogate
problem.
Using the truncated Arnoldi relations in (
2.4
) and
\bm{X}_{d}=\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}\bm{V}_{d}^{\top}
,
the residual matrix
\bm{R}_{d}=\bm{A}\bm{X}_{d}+\bm{X}_{d}\bm{B}-C_{1}C_{2}^{\top}
can be written as

(3.1)

\bm{R}_{d}=[\bm{U}_{d},U_{d+1}]\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}+\bm{Y}_{d}\bm{G}_{d}^{\top}-E_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{\top}E_{1}^{\top}&\bm{Y}_{d}E_{d}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}\\
\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{d}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}[\bm{V}_{d},V_{d+1}]^{\top},

where
C_{1}=\bm{U}_{d}E_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
,
C_{2}=\bm{V}_{d}E_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{2}
,
and

{\bm{\beta}}_{1},{\bm{\beta}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
.

The matrix
\bm{Y}_{d}
can be obtained by, e.g., imposing some optimality condition on the residual matrix, such as orthogonality with respect to the approximation bases, i.e., the so-called Galerkin condition,
\bm{U}_{d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{d}\bm{V}_{d}=0
.

If
\bm{U}_{d+1}=[\bm{U}_{d},U_{d+1}]
and
\bm{V}_{d+1}=[\bm{V}_{d},V_{d+1}]
had orthonormal columns,
this condition would correspond to zeroing out the top left term in the inner block matrix in (
3.1
),
thus solving

\bm{H}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}+\bm{Y}_{d}\bm{G}_{d}^{\top}=E_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{\top}E_{1}^{\top}
for
\bm{Y}_{d}
.
Hence, it would also hold that

\|\bm{R}_{d}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{d}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{Y}_{d}E_{d+1}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}
;
see, e.g.,
[
44
]
.
In our setting, not all the columns of
\bm{U}_{d+1}
and

\bm{V}_{d+1}
are orthonormal. Nonetheless, by denoting
\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}=\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\bm{V}_{d}^{\top}
,
we can still compute
\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}
by solving the same equation, namely

\bm{H}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}+\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\bm{G}_{d}^{\top}=E_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{\top}E_{1}^{\top}.

Notice that, in contrast to what happens in the full Arnoldi method, this matrix equation is not what would be obtained by imposing a Galerkin condition on the residual matrix, due to the non-orthogonality of the bases
4
4

4

The condition
\bm{U}_{d}^{\top}\bm{R}_{d}\bm{V}_{d}=0
yields
the reduced equation

\bm{U}_{d}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}\bm{V}_{d}^{\top}\bm{V}_{d}+\bm{U}_{d}^{\top}\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}\bm{V}_{d}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{V}_{d}=\bm{U}_{d}^{\top}C_{1}C_{2}^{\top}\bm{V}_{d}
.
.
On the other hand, it allows us to derive an approximation without explicitly storing the two bases, and a computable estimate

for the residual norm. In particular, for the residual matrix
\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}
stemming from the truncated scheme, the following result holds true.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}=\bm{A}\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}+\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\bm{B}-C_{1}C_{2}^{\top}
,
\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}=\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}V_{d}^{\top}
, be the residual matrix stemming from the
d
th iteration of the truncated Krylov method. Then

(3.2)

\|\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{dr}(\|\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}E_{d}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}\|_{F}+\|\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\|_{F}).

Proof.

The expression in (
3.1
) is equivalent to writing
\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}
as

\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}=\bm{U}_{d}\bm{Y}^{\textsc{tr}}_{d}E_{d}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}V_{d+1}^{\top}+U_{d+1}\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\bm{V}_{d}^{\top}.

Therefore, by recalling that
U_{d+1}^{\top}U_{d+1}=V_{d+1}^{\top}V_{d+1}=\bm{I}_{r}
, it holds

\|\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{U}_{d}\|_{F}\|\bm{Y}^{\textsc{tr}}_{d}E_{d}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}\|_{F}+\|\bm{V}_{d}\|_{F}\|\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\|_{F}.

The result follows by noticing that
\|\bm{U}_{d}\|_{F}=\|\bm{V}_{d}\|_{F}=\sqrt{dr}
, since
\bm{U}_{d},\bm{V}_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}^{n\times dr}}
have unit norm columns.
∎

The truncated Krylov method we just outlined is reminiscent of truncated methods for linear systems;
see, e.g.
[
42
, Sections 6.4.2 and 6.5.6]
. To the best of our knowledge,
these truncated Arnoldi methods have never been used in the matrix equation setting.

We now turn our attention to how we can integrate sketching and Krylov schemes for matrix equations.
If sketching is employed, we look for an approximate solution
\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}^{\top}
.
Thanks to the WS-Arnoldi relations (
2.8
), we generalize the previous relation (
3.1
) for the residual to get

(3.3)

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d+1}\begin{bmatrix}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{H}E_{d}^{\top})\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}+\bm{Y}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{G}E_{D}^{\top})^{\top}-E_{1}\bm{\beta}_{1}\bm{\beta}_{2}^{\top}E_{1}^{\top}&\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}E_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}\\
\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d+1}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top},

where now
C_{1}=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}E_{1}\bm{\beta}_{1}
,
C_{2}=\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}E_{1}\bm{\beta}_{2}
.
It is thus again natural to compute
\bm{Y}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}
so as to annihilate the (1,1)-block in the inner
matrix, that is

(3.4)

(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{H}E_{d}^{\top})\bm{Y}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}+\bm{Y}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{G}E_{d}^{\top})^{\top}=E_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{1}{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{\top}E_{1}^{\top}.

This way of determining
\bm{Y}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}
stands on more solid grounds than in the truncated case. Indeed,
the columns of
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}
and
\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}
are orthogonal bases with
respect to the sketched inner products defined by
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}
and
\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}
, respectively.
Thus, solving (
3.4
) is equivalent to imposing the following
Galerkin
\bm{S}_{*}^{\top}\!\bm{S}_{*}
-orthogonality condition on the residual matrix, namely

\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}=0.

The equivalence follows from the expression (
3.1
)
and the WS-Arnoldi relations (
2.10
).

For the sketched-and-truncated method, the Frobenius norm of the sketched residual, namely
\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}
, can be
cheaply evaluated as

(3.5)

\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}=\|\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}E_{d}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}+\|\bm{h}_{d+1,d}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\|_{F}^{2},

where (
3.1
) was used together with the orthogonality of the bases
\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}
and
\widehat{\bm{V}}_{d}
with respect to the sketched inner products.
Indeed, this corresponds to using the
\bm{S}_{*}^{\top}\!\bm{S}_{*}
-norm, supported by
the fact that this will be close to the Frobenius norm
thanks to the
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding property.
More precisely,

with high probability,

(3.6)

(1+\widetilde{\varepsilon})^{-1}\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\|_{F}^{2}\leq(1-\widetilde{\varepsilon})^{-1}\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2},

3.1.
Distance from the Arnoldi approximation

It is relevant to study the discrepancy between

to the sketched reduced equation (

The full Galerkin method determines the Arnoldi approximation

\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}

with
\bm{\mathscr{U}}_{d}
,
\bm{\mathscr{V}}_{d}
the orthonormal bases,
\bm{\mathscr{G}}_{d},\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d}
the projected matrices from (

), and
C_{1}=\mathpzc{U}_{1}\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\textsc{full}}
,
C_{2}=\mathpzc{V}_{1}\bm{\beta}_{2}^{\textsc{full}}
; see, e.g.,
[
44
]

Thanks to the sketched Arnoldi relation in

, it is possible to easily

instead of the basis

First, note that there exist upper triangular matrices

and

Analogously to how (
2.8
) follows from (

) and (

\displaystyle\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{U}}(\bm{\widehat{H}}_{d}+\widehat{H}E_{d}^{\top})\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{U}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d}-\bm{\mathscr{U}}_{d}^{\top}\widehat{U}_{d+1}\bm{h}_{d+1,d}\bm{t}_{U,d}^{-1}E_{d}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{V}}(\bm{\widehat{G}}_{d}+\widehat{G}E_{d}^{\top})\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{V}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\mathscr{G}}_{d}-\bm{\mathscr{V}}_{d}^{\top}\widehat{V}_{d+1}\bm{g}_{d+1,d}\bm{t}_{V,d}^{-1}E_{d}^{\top},

where
\bm{t}_{U,d}=E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{U}}E_{d}
and
\bm{t}_{V,d}=E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{V}}E_{d}

\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{U}}
and

\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}=\bm{\mathscr{U}}_{d}\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}_{d}^{\top}

\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}=\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{U}}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\bm{\mathscr{T}}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}

Subtracting (

Hence,
for
\bm{\mathcal{L}}=\bm{\mathscr{G}}_{d}\otimes\bm{I}+\bm{I}\otimes\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\|_{F}=}\|\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\|_{F}\,\|{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}+\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}E_{d}{\widehat{R}}_{G}\phantom{)\!\!\!\!\!\!}^{\top}\|_{F}.

The above bound estimates the distance

solving the reduced equation, the residual matrix can be written in the (ideal) orthonormal bases as

\displaystyle\bm{R}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}={\bm{\mathscr{U}}}_{d+1}\begin{bmatrix}0&\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}E_{d}{\widehat{R}}_{G}^{\top}\\
{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{Y}}^{\textsc{sk}}_{d}&0\end{bmatrix}{\bm{\mathscr{V}}}_{d+1}^{\top},

so that both addends in the bound factor
\|{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}+\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}E_{d}{\widehat{R}}_{G}\phantom{)\!\!\!\!\!\!}^{\top}\|_{F}
go to zero as the residual norm goes to zero.

Hence, for a well-posed full orthogonalization procedure, we expect
that, in case of convergence,
the whitened-sketched scheme yields a solution not far from that of the full orthogonalization method.

One could also write an analogous bound for the error norms in terms of the bottom entries of
\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}
, for which we have a better understanding of the convergence behavior. The proof follows the derivation above, by first substituting
\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}
with
(\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}-\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}})+\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}
into (
3.8
) to get

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt(\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d}-{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top})(\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}})+(\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}})(\bm{\mathscr{G}}_{d}-{\widehat{R}}_{G}E_{d}^{\top})^{\top}

\displaystyle=-{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}E_{d}{\widehat{R}}_{G}\phantom{)\!\!\!\!\!\!}^{\top},

so that

\|\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{\mathcal{\widetilde{L}}}^{-1}\|_{F}\,\|{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top}\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}+\bm{\mathscr{Y}}_{d}^{\textsc{full}}E_{d}{\widehat{R}}_{G}\phantom{)\!\!\!\!\!\!}^{\top}\|_{F},

where
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}=(\bm{\mathscr{G}}_{d}-{\widehat{R}}_{G}E_{d}^{\top})\otimes\bm{I}+\bm{I}\otimes(\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d}-{\widehat{R}}_{H}E_{d}^{\top})
.
On the other hand, the spectral term
\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}^{-1}\|_{F}
of the resulting bound is not easy to control as it depends on low-rank modifications to
\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d}
and
\bm{\mathscr{G}}_{d}
.

Following the same reasonings as above, very similar results can be derived for the truncated approach.
In this case, we can also borrow some of the results from the theory
of truncated algorithms for (vector) linear systems.
Indeed, using the vector form, we have that
x_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}=\vecop(\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}})=(\bm{V}_{d}\otimes\bm{U}_{d})y_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}
with
y_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}=\vecop(\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{tr}})
. Under this form we can
derive a relation with the optimal Arnoldi solution
x_{d}^{\textsc{full}}
, that is the one that keeps both bases orthonormal, namely
\bm{\mathscr{V}}_{d}\otimes\bm{\mathscr{U}}_{d}
. To this end, let
\bm{\mathcal{M}}=\bm{I}\otimes\bm{A}+\bm{B}\otimes\bm{I}
and
\bm{\mathcal{W}}:=\bm{V}_{d}\otimes\bm{U}_{d}
be the Kronecker sum and product with quantities as in (
2.4
), and let
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}
be the oblique projection
\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}=\bm{\mathcal{M}}\bm{\mathcal{W}}(\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\top}\!\bm{\mathcal{M}}\bm{\mathcal{W}})^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{W}}^{\top}
, where we assume that the inner matrix is nonsingular, that is, the two spaces spanned by
\bm{U}_{d},\bm{V}_{d}
keep growing. The following equality was proved in
[
46
, Th. 3.1]
,

\frac{\|r_{d}^{\textsc{full}}-r_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}\|}{\|r_{d}^{\textsc{full}}\|}=\frac{\|\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{\mathcal{M}}}w\|}{\|(\bm{I}-\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{\mathcal{M}}})w\|},\qquad w=v_{d+1}\otimes u_{d+1},

where
r_{d}^{\textsc{full}},r_{d}^{\textsc{tr}}
are the vector residuals corresponding to the above approximations and for simplicity we have assumed a rank-one right-hand side matrix
C_{1}C_{2}^{\top}
.
This
equality
shows that as long as the next vectors of the bases,

u_{d+1},v_{d+1}
, provide significant new information with respect to the previously computed
vectors, then the two residuals will be close to each other.
In other words, orthogonality is not paramount, only the angle between the new vectors and the previous spaces is.

4.
Theoretical investigation

In this section we explore some of the theoretical aspects related to the presented sketching technique.

4.1.
On the field of values of the projected problem

It is well-known that for standard Krylov subspace methods for Sylvester equations, the well-posedness of the projected problems can be guaranteed by assuming that the fields of values of
\bm{A}
and
-\bm{B}
have empty intersection, namely
W(\bm{A})\cap W(-\bm{B})=\emptyset
; see, e.g.,
[
44
]
. In the sketching framework, stronger assumptions have to be considered to ensure that equation (
3.4
) has a unique solution for any
d
.

In general, the field of values of the matrix associated with the sketching procedure is not a significant tool to analyze well-posedness, as sketching may substantially change the field of values of
\bm{A}
. In the following we illustrate the quality of this change. To this end, we first describe a general result on “sketched” fields of values that will then be adapted to our setting.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\bm{S}
be an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for
\mathcal{V}
.
Then for any complex vector
v
such that
v,\bm{A}v\in\mathcal{V}
it holds that

|\Re(v^{*}\bm{S}^{\top}\!\bm{SA}v)-\Re(v^{*}\!\bm{A}v)|\leq\varepsilon\|v\|\|\bm{A}v\|,

with high probability,

where
\Re(\cdot)
denotes the real part of a complex number
, so that

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\Re\left(\frac{v^{*}\!\bm{A}v}{v^{*}v}\right)-\varepsilon\|v\|\|\bm{A}v\|\leq\Re(v^{*}\bm{S}^{\top}\!\bm{SA}v)\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\Re\left(\frac{v^{*}\!\bm{A}v}{v^{*}v}\right)+\varepsilon\|v\|\|\bm{A}v\|.

Proof.

Let
v=v_{1}+\imath v_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\Re(v^{*}\bm{S}^{\top}\!\bm{SA}v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\bm{S}v_{1},\bm{S}\bm{A}v_{1}\rangle+\langle\bm{S}v_{2},\bm{S}\bm{A}v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle(I_{2}\otimes\bm{S})\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix},(I_{2}\otimes\bm{S})\begin{bmatrix}Av_{1}\\
Av_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle.

By
Propositions

B.1
to
B.2

\varepsilon

so that by (

\Re(v^{*}\!\bm{A}v)-\varepsilon\|v\|\,\|\bm{A}v\|\leq\Re(v^{*}\bm{S}^{\top}\!\bm{SA}v)\leq\Re(v^{*}\!\bm{A}v)+\varepsilon\|v\|\,\|\bm{A}v\|.

The factor involving
1\pm\varepsilon
stems from (
2.2
).
∎

As a prerequisite for our analysis, we require the following technical lemma.
A similar result was proved in
[
36
, Section 7]
using the eigenvalue decomposition of the given matrix. To deal with matrix equations, here we generalize the result to the use of the Schur decomposition, which appears to be more appropriate in this context.
We stress that this result, together with the subsequent
Proposition

4.4
, are possible thanks to the low-rank modification form for the projected matrix derived in
Proposition

2.1
. Expressions for the projected matrix using the pseudoinverse, such as in
[
13
,
15
,
24
]
, would not allow one to obtain these simple derivations.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})^{\top}=\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{R}\mathcal{Q}^{*}
be the Schur decomposition of (the transpose of) the given matrix. For
\eta>0
, assume that there exists an index
\bar{i}\leq d

such that
\dist(\mathcal{R}_{ii},W(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}))

=\mathcal{O}(\eta^{-1})
,
i=1,\ldots\bar{i}
for an appropriate
spectral ordering in the Schur decomposition.
Then it holds that
\mathcal{Q}_{1,i}=\mathcal{O}((\eta/\xi)^{d})
, for
i=1,\ldots\bar{i}
, with

\xi=\max_{j\leq\bar{i}}|1/(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d})_{j+1,j}|
.

Proof.

Let us write
\mathcal{Q}=[q_{1},\ldots,q_{d}]
.
Consider the first column in the Schur decomposition, that is

(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}^{\top}+e_{d}\widehat{h}^{\top})q_{1}=q_{1}\mathcal{R}_{1,1}
. Let

\bm{N}:=\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}^{\top}
and recall that
\bm{N}
is lower Hessenberg.
For each row
k<d
of this eigenvalue equation we have

\bm{N}_{k,1:k+1}q_{1:k+1,1}=q_{k,1}\mathcal{R}_{1,1}.

Reordering terms,
\bm{N}_{k,k+1}q_{k+1,1}=(\bm{N}_{k,k}-\mathcal{R}_{1,1})q_{k,1}+\bm{N}_{k,1:k-1}q_{1:k-1,1}
,
from which we obtain

q_{k+1,1}=\frac{(\bm{N}_{k,k}-\mathcal{R}_{1,1})}{\bm{N}_{k,k+1}}q_{k,1}+\frac{\bm{N}_{k,1:k-1}}{\bm{N}_{k,k+1}}q_{1:k-1,1}.

Taking into account the unit norm of
q_{1}
it follows

|q_{k+1,1}|\leq\frac{|\bm{N}_{k,k}-\mathcal{R}_{1,1}|}{|\bm{N}_{k,k+1}|}|q_{k,1}|+\frac{\|\bm{N}_{k,1:k-1}\|}{|\bm{N}_{k,k+1}|}.

Hence, the entries of
q_{1}
satisfy the relation

|q_{k+1,1}|=\mathcal{O}(\xi\eta^{-1}|q_{k,1}|)
. Normalization of
q_{1}
yields the
sought after result.

For a subsequent Schur vector
q_{i}
with
1<i\leq\bar{i}
we have

\bm{N}_{k,1:k+1}q_{1:k+1,i}=q_{k,i}\mathcal{R}_{i,i}+q_{k,1:i-1}\mathcal{R}_{1:i-1,i},

from which

\bm{N}_{k,k+1}q_{k+1,i}=(\bm{N}_{k,k}-\mathcal{R}_{i,i})q_{k,i}+\bm{N}_{k,1:k-1}q_{1:k-1,i}+q_{k,1:i-1}\mathcal{R}_{1:i-1,i}

follows, so that

q_{k+1,i}=\frac{(\bm{N}_{k,k}-\mathcal{R}_{i,i})}{\bm{N}_{k,k+1}}q_{k,i}+\frac{\bm{N}_{k,1:k-1}}{\bm{N}_{k,k+1}}q_{1:k-1,i}+\frac{\mathcal{R}_{1:i-1,i}}{\bm{N}_{k,k+1}}q_{1:k-1,i-1}.

Taking once again absolute values we finally obtain

|q_{k+1,i}|\leq\frac{|\bm{N}_{k,k}-\mathcal{R}_{i,i}|}{|\bm{N}_{k,k+1}|}|q_{k,i}|+\frac{\|\bm{N}_{k,1:k-1}\|}{|\bm{N}_{k,k+1}|}+\frac{\|\mathcal{R}_{1:i-1,i}\|}{|\bm{N}_{k,k+1}|},

so that
|q_{k+1,i}|=\mathcal{O}(\xi\eta^{-1}|q_{k,i}|)
.
∎

If some of the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}

are significantly far from the field of values of
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}
,
the first entry of the corresponding Schur vectors will be far below
machine precision, orders of magnitude smaller, especially for
d\gg 10
.
Therefore, to simplify the derivation, in the following proposition we will assume
that these Schur vector entries are in fact zero.
Such sparsity property
allows us to derive the following result on the rank structure of the solution to the

Sylvester equation.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}^{\top}+e_{d}\widehat{h}^{\top}=\mathcal{Q}R\mathcal{Q}^{*}
and
\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}^{\top}+e_{d}\widehat{g}^{\top}=\mathcal{P}K\mathcal{P}^{*}
be Schur decompositions of
the given matrices, with
\mathcal{Q}=[q_{1},\ldots,q_{d}]
and
\mathcal{P}=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}]
.
Assume that the spectra of
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}^{\top}+e_{d}\widehat{h}^{\top}
and

-(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}^{\top}+e_{d}\widehat{g}^{\top})
do not intersect. Under the hypotheses of

Lemma

4.3
in the limit for
\eta=0
, define the partitions
\mathcal{Q}=[\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q}_{1}]
with
e_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{Q}_{0}=0

and
e_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{Q}_{1}\neq 0
, and
\mathcal{P}=[\mathcal{P}_{0},\mathcal{P}_{1}]
with
e_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{0}=0

and
e_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{1}\neq 0
.
Then the solution
\bm{Y}_{d}
to

(4.1)

(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\bm{Y}_{d}+\bm{Y}_{d}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{g}e_{d}^{\top})^{*}=e_{1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}e_{1}^{*}

where
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}>0
are arbitrary, is given by
\bm{Y}_{d}=\mathcal{Q}_{1}\mathcal{Z}\mathcal{P}_{1}^{*}
where
\mathcal{Z}
solves

(4.2)

\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}_{1}\mathcal{Z}+\mathcal{Z}\mathcal{P}_{1}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{g}e_{d}^{\top})^{*}\mathcal{P}_{1}=\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}e_{1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}e_{1}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{1}.

Proof.

Spectral separation ensures existence and uniqueness of
\bm{Y}_{d}
.
Left and right multiplication of (
4.1
) by
\mathcal{Q}^{*}
and
\mathcal{P}
, respectively,
gives

(4.3)

\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}^{*}\bm{Y}_{d}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{Q}^{*}\bm{Y}_{d}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{g}e_{d}^{\top})^{*}\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}^{*}e_{1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}^{*}e_{1}^{*}\mathcal{P}.

Using the hypotheses,
\mathcal{Q}^{*}e_{1}\beta_{1}=[0;q]
with
q=\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}e_{1}\beta_{1}
, and

\mathcal{P}^{*}e_{1}\beta_{2}=[0;p]
with
p=\mathcal{P}_{1}^{*}e_{1}\beta_{2}
.
Let us use the partitions

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle=:\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{11}^{*}&0\\
\mathcal{R}_{12}^{*}&\mathcal{R}_{22}^{*}\end{bmatrix},\,\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{G}}_{d}+\widehat{g}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=:\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{K}_{11}^{*}&0\\
\mathcal{K}_{12}^{*}&\mathcal{K}_{22}^{*}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{*}\bm{Y}_{d}\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=:\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{Z}_{11}&\mathcal{Z}_{12}\\
\mathcal{Z}_{21}&\mathcal{Z}_{22}\end{bmatrix},\,\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{*}e_{1}e_{1}^{*}\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=:\!\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&qp^{*}\end{bmatrix}.

Then equating all blocks in (
4.3
) we obtain

(4.4)

\mathcal{R}_{11}^{*}\mathcal{Z}_{11}+\mathcal{Z}_{11}\mathcal{K}_{11}=0,\quad\mathcal{R}_{12}^{*}\mathcal{Z}_{11}+\mathcal{R}_{22}^{*}\mathcal{Z}_{21}+\mathcal{Z}_{21}\mathcal{K}_{11}=0,

and

(4.5)

\mathcal{R}_{11}^{*}\mathcal{Z}_{12}+\mathcal{Z}_{11}\mathcal{K}_{12}+\mathcal{Z}_{12}\mathcal{K}_{22}=0,\quad\mathcal{R}_{12}^{*}\mathcal{Z}_{12}+\mathcal{R}_{22}^{*}\mathcal{Z}_{22}+\mathcal{Z}_{21}\mathcal{K}_{12}+\mathcal{Z}_{22}\mathcal{K}_{22}=qp^{*}.

From the left matrix equation in (
4.4
)
we obtain
\mathcal{Z}_{11}=0

and

∎

4.2.
Convergence analysis for the Lyapunov setting

For the sake of simplifying the derived bounds, in this section we
focus on the Lyapunov equation

(4.6)

\bm{A}\bm{X}+\bm{X}\bm{A}^{\top}=cc^{\top},

where, for convenience, we assume that the right-hand side has rank 1 and that
\|c\|=1
.

We next show that if
\bm{A}
and the reduced matrix
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}
are both negative definite, a convergence analysis is possible in a very similar way to the standard, non-sketched setting. We closely follow the techniques from
[
45
]
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\bm{A}
be negative definite and let
\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}
denote the sketched-and-whitened Arnoldi approximation for the solution of (
4.6
). Assume that
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}
is negative definite as well and denote
\alpha_{\max}:=\max\{\alpha(\bm{A}),\alpha(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\}
.

Further, let
\mathbb{E}\subset\mathbb{C}^{-}
be a compact, convex set containing the field of values of both
\bm{A}
and
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}
, let
\Phi_{k},k=0,1,\dots
with
\deg(\Phi_{k})=k
denote the Faber polynomials with respect to
\mathbb{E}
and let

(4.7)

\exp(\tau z)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)\Phi_{k}(z),

be the Faber series of the exponential. Then,

with high probability,

(4.8)

\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}\|\leq 2\eta_{\varepsilon}\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(\tau\alpha_{\max})|f_{k}(\tau)|d\tau,

with
\eta_{\varepsilon}=\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}\right)

and
\varepsilon
the parameter controlling the embedding quality; see (
2.2
) or (
2.3
)
.

Proof.

We have
\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}^{\top}
with

(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}+\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})^{\top}=e_{1}\beta^{2}e_{1}^{\top},

where
\beta=\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}c\|
. Therefore, using the standard integral representation for the solution of the Lyapunov equation,

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\bm{Y}_{d}^{\textsc{sk}}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\exp(\tau(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}))e_{1}\beta^{2}e_{1}^{\top}\exp(\tau(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{t}e_{d}^{\top})^{\top})\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}^{\top}d\tau,

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)(x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau))^{\top}d\tau,

with the short-hand notation
x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau):=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\exp(\tau(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}))e_{1}\beta
for the sketched Krylov approximation of
x(\tau):=\exp(\tau\bm{A})c
.

Subtracting (
4.9
) from the integral representation
\bm{X}=\int_{0}^{\infty}x(\tau)(x(\tau))^{\top}d\tau
gives

\displaystyle\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\|x(\tau)(x(\tau))^{\top}-x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)(x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau))^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}(\|x(\tau)\|+\|x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|)\|x(\tau)-x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|d\tau

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\|)\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(\tau\alpha_{\max})\|x(\tau)-x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|d\tau,

where for the last inequality we have used
\|x(\tau)\|\leq\exp(\tau\alpha_{\max})
and
\|x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|\leq\exp(\tau\alpha_{\max})\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\|
. To bound the norm inside the integral in (
4.10
), we use the Faber series (
4.7
) together with the polynomial exactness property

(4.11)

\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}p_{d-1}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})e_{1}=p_{d-1}(\bm{A})c,

for any polynomial of degree at most
d-1
, which gives

(4.12)

x(\tau)-x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)=\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)(\Phi_{k}(\bm{A})c-\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\Phi_{k}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})e_{1}\beta).

Taking norms in (
4.12
) yields

(4.13)

\|x(\tau)-x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|\leq\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}|f_{k}(\tau)|(\|\Phi_{k}(\bm{A})\|+\beta\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\|\|\Phi_{k}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\|).

By
[
9
, Théorème 1.1]
, we have
\|\Phi_{k}(\bm{A})\|\leq 2,\|\Phi_{k}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\|\leq 2
, by
[
5
, Corollary 2.2]
it holds
\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}
and further
\beta=\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}c\|\leq\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\|c\|=\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}
, because
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}
is an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for
\mathcal{K}_{d+1}(\bm{A},c).
Inserting these relations into (
4.13
) gives

\|x(\tau)-x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|\leq 2\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}}\right)\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}|f_{k}(\tau)|.

Further inserting this into (
4.10
) and using the bound
\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}
once again completes the proof.
∎

Note that for the usually assumed sketching quality
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{2}
, we have

2\eta_{\varepsilon}\approx 19.45.

The result of
Theorem

4.6
is very similar to
standard results for full Arnoldi in the (non-sketched) setting; see
[
45
]
. In that case, a bound like (
4.8
) holds, where the factor

2\eta_{\varepsilon}

is replaced by
8
, one can use
\exp(\tau\alpha(\bm{A}))
instead of
\exp(\tau\alpha_{\max})
, and
\mathbb{E}
only needs to contain
W(\bm{A})
, as it then also contains
W(\bm{\mathscr{H}}_{d})
. This shows that as long as
W(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})
is not much larger than
W(\bm{A})
, one can expect the sketched method to converge similarly to the
full Arnoldi method
.
Let us also note that it is well-known that bounds of the form (
4.8
) are often not tight in practice, also in the deterministic case. In particular, while the convergence slope might often be predicted quite well, the constants involved are often a large overestimate. These bounds are therefore not well-suited to be used as a stopping criterion for the method.

We would like to remark that the polynomial exactness property (
4.11
) used in the proof of
Theorem

4.6
is readily available thanks to the WS-Arnoldi relations (

). Indeed, in
[

it has been shown that the only modifications to the upper Hessenberg matrix stemming from the Arnoldi process (
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{d}
in our case) that preserve the polynomial exactness are indeed low-rank modifications acting only on the last (block) column. Once again, this result would not be available if we had to rely on expressions of the projected matrices involving pseudoinverses.

For specific shapes of the set
\mathbb{E}
, the bound in

can be made more explicit. The following corollary exemplifies this for the case that

is an ellipse.
It depends a lot on the specific

at hand whether assuming an enclosing ellipse is a reasonable assumption or not, and we merely show this as an example of the type of bound that can be obtained. For some other cases, e.g., a wedge-shaped set, similar bounds are possible.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

4.6
hold and further assume that
\mathbb{E}
is an ellipse in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
with center
(-c,0)
, foci

and semi-axes

and

, so that

. Define
\rho_{1}=\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}}{2}
and

. Then

Proof.

The proof works along the same lines as that of
[

, Proposition 4.1]
.

The results above may appear to be of rather limited use, since

W(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})
may significantly
differ from
W(\bm{A})
. However, the results that led us to introduce the
effective field of values of
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}
enable us to obtain more informative convergence bounds.
Indeed, under the hypotheses of
Proposition

4.4
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\mathcal{Q}_{1})\mathcal{Z}(\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\mathcal{Q}_{1})^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\mathcal{Q}_{1}\exp(\tau(\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}_{1})\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}e_{1}\beta^{2}e_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{Q}_{1}\exp(\tau(\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{t}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}_{1})^{*})\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}^{\top}d\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)(x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau))^{\top}d\tau,

where

x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\exp(\tau(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}))e_{1}\beta=\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\mathcal{Q}_{1}\exp(\tau(\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}_{1}))\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}e_{1}\beta,

and the last equality follows from noticing that under the proposition hypotheses,

\mathcal{Q}^{*}e_{1}=[0;\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}e_{1}]
.
In particular, in the proof of
Theorem

4.6
, we have that

\|x^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|\leq\exp(\tau\alpha_{\max}^{\prime})
, where

\alpha_{\max}^{\prime}
is the rightmost point of
W_{\textnormal{eff}}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})
.
Moreover,

\|x(\tau)-x_{d}^{\textsc{{sk}}}(\tau)\|\leq\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}|f_{k}(\tau)|(\|\Phi_{k}(\bm{A})\|+\beta\|\widehat{\bm{U}}_{d}\|\|\Phi_{k}(\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{*}(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})\mathcal{Q}_{1})\|).

It thus follows that we can obtain a result such as

based on the

field of values of
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top}
, which can be significantly closer to that of
\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}
than
W(\widehat{\bm{H}}_{d}+\widehat{h}e_{d}^{\top})
.

Corresponding convergence results can be obtained for the Sylvester equation, taking into account spectral properties of both left and right coefficient matrices, with only technical modifications. To avoid proliferation of similar results, we omit this derivation.

7.
Conclusions

By fully exploiting the promising combination of sketching and Krylov methods, in this paper we developed a sketched-and-truncated Krylov subspace method for matrix equations. In addition to making polynomial Krylov methods feasible, the approach turns out to be competitive also with respect to state-of-the-art schemes based on rational Krylov subspaces. In particular, the proposed method reduces the storage demand of the overall solution process, and concurrently provides a 50% CPU time speed-up over RKSM. Therefore, the sketched-and-truncated polynomial Krylov subspace method can be a valid candidate for solving large-scale matrix equations for which projection methods based on more sophisticated subspaces struggle due to, e.g., expensive linear systems solves.

The whitened-sketched recurrence produces matrices that can, in theory, endanger the applicability of the generated space as projection method; fortunately, the projected linear equations are usually solvable in practice, and very effective performance resulted in our numerical experiments. We have theoretically explained this perceivable contradiction. Indeed, we have proved that sketching takes place into what we have called the effective field of values of the involved matrices, for which the projected solution can be well-defined.

A convergence analysis for the sketched approximation has also been presented, which conveniently extends results obtained in the literature for the full Arnoldi method.

As a supplementary numerical contribution of this paper, we have derived and analyzed truncated Krylov methods, which appear to be new in the matrix equation context.

\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}
and
v\in\mathcal{K}_{d+1}(\bm{B}^{\top},C_{2})
. We now investigate what this implies for

\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{M}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}
in relation to
\|\bm{M}\|_{F}
, when
\bm{M}
is of the form
\bm{M}=\bm{\mathscr{U}}_{d+1}\bm{\mathscr{Z}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}_{d+1}^{\top}
(as it is the case for the Sylvester equation residual).

Our derivations closely follow the ideas in
[
14
]
, generalizing their results from random Gaussian matrices to general
\varepsilon
-subspace embeddings. We start with the following simple observation.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
be an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding of
\mathcal{V}
with
\dim(\mathcal{V})=d+1
and let the columns of
\bm{\mathscr{V}}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{V}
. Then
\bm{S}\bm{\mathscr{V}}
is an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Then
\bm{\mathscr{V}}z\in\mathcal{V}
, so that by (
2.2
), we have

(1-\varepsilon)\|\bm{\mathscr{V}}z\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{S}\bm{\mathscr{V}}z\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|\bm{\mathscr{V}}z\|^{2},

The result follows by noting that
\|\bm{\mathscr{V}}z\|=\|z\|
due to the orthonormality of the columns of
\bm{\mathscr{V}}
.
∎

We further need the following auxiliary result.

Proposition B.2

(Lemma 1 and Corollary 3 in
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\bm{S}_{1},\bm{S}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times(d+1)}
be
\varepsilon
-subspace embeddings of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Then
\bm{S}_{i}\otimes\bm{I}_{n}
and
\bm{I}_{n}\otimes\bm{S}_{i},i=1,2
are
\varepsilon
-subspace embeddings of
\mathbb{R}^{n(d+1)}
and
\bm{S}_{1}\otimes\bm{S}_{2}
is an
\varepsilon(2+\varepsilon)
-subspace embedding of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)^{2}}
.

With these results, we can now state a tensorized embedding property.

Theorem B.3
.

Let
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n_{1}}
be an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for the subspace
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n_{2}}
be an
\varepsilon
-subspace embedding for the subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
, with
\dim(\mathcal{U})=\dim(\mathcal{V})=d+1
. Further, let the columns of
\bm{\mathscr{U}},\bm{\mathscr{V}}
contain orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}
, respectively.

Then, for any
\bm{M}=\bm{\mathscr{U}}\bm{\mathscr{Z}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}^{\top}
, we have

(B.1)

(1-\widetilde{\varepsilon})\|\bm{M}\|_{F}\leq\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{M}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}\leq(1+\widetilde{\varepsilon})\|\bm{M}\|_{F}

with
\widetilde{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon(2+\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Using the vectorized form, for any
\bm{M}
, we have
\|\bm{M}\|_{F}=\|\vecop(\bm{M})\|
. By a direct calculation, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{M}\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}^{\top}\|_{F}=\|(\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}\otimes\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{\mathscr{U}})\vecop(\bm{\mathscr{Z}})\|.

By
Proposition

B.1
,
\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{\mathscr{U}}
and
\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}
are
\varepsilon
-subspace embeddings of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, so that by
Proposition

B.2
,
\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}\otimes\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{\mathscr{U}}
, is an
\widetilde{\varepsilon}
-subspace embedding of
\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)^{2}}
. Thus,

(B.2)

(1-\widetilde{\varepsilon})\|\vecop(\bm{\mathscr{Z}})\|\leq\|(\bm{S}_{\bm{V}}\bm{\mathscr{V}}\otimes\bm{S}_{\bm{U}}\bm{\mathscr{U}})\vecop(\bm{\mathscr{Z}})\|\leq(1+\widetilde{\varepsilon})\|\vecop(\bm{\mathscr{Z}})\|.

Furthermore,
\|\vecop(\bm{\mathscr{Z}})\|=\|\vecop(\bm{M})\|
, because
(\bm{\mathscr{V}}\otimes\bm{\mathscr{U}})\vecop(\bm{\mathscr{Z}})=\vecop(\bm{M})
and
\bm{\mathscr{V}}\otimes\bm{\mathscr{U}}
has orthonormal columns. Inserting this into (
B.2
) completes the proof.
∎

Remark B.4
.

The lower bound in (
B.1
) is only informative if
1-\widetilde{\varepsilon}>0
, or equivalently,
\varepsilon<\sqrt{2}-1\approx 0.4142
. This means that in order to obtain theoretical guarantees, one needs to impose a stricter subspace embedding condition than when solving linear systems or approximating the action of matrix functions via sketched Krylov methods (where any
\varepsilon<1
works in principle, and
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{2}
guarantees that, e.g., the sketched residual norm differs from the true residual norm by a factor of at most
6
).

However, our numerical experiments indicate that in practice, a cruder tolerance typically also suffices for obtaining a method that works well.
