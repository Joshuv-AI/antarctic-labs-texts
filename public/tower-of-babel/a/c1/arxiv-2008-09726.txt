Variational approach to time-dependent fluorescence of a driven qubit
Yiying Yan, Lipeng Chen, JunYan Luo, Yang Zhao

Abstract
We employ the Dirac-Frenkel variational principle and multiple Davydov ansatz to study time-dependent fluorescence spectra of a driven qubit in the weak- to strong qubit-reservoir coupling regimes, where both the Rabi frequency and spontaneous decay rate are comparable to the transition frequency of the qubit. Our method agrees well with the time-local master-equation approach in the weak-coupling regime, and offers a flexible way to compute the spectra from the bosonic dynamics instead of two-time correlation functions. While the perturbative master equation breaks down in the strong-coupling regime, our method actually becomes more accurate due to the use of bosonic coherent states under certain conditions. We show that the counter-rotating coupling between the qubit and the reservoir has considerable contributions to the photon number dynamics and the spectra under strong driving conditions even though the coupling is moderately weak. The time-dependent spectra are found to be generally asymmetric, a feature that is derived from photon number dynamics. In addition, it is shown that the spectral profiles can be dramatically different from the Mollow triplet due to strong dissipation and/or multiphoton processes associated with the strong driving. Our formalism provides a unique perspective to interpret time-dependent spectra.

IV.2
Spectral features

In this section, we focus on the features of the time-dependent fluorescence spectra and seek to understand them
in terms of the photon number dynamics.
Figures
5
-
7
show that regardless of the RWA employment, the time-dependent fluorescence spectrum is generally asymmetric as we go from the weak to strong coupling regime. In general, we find that it is nontrivial to obtain symmetric time-dependent spectrum with the present model when the driving is strong. This can be intuitively understood by considering the photon number dynamics. If the time-dependent spectrum is symmetric about a central frequency, the photon number dynamics should also have mirror symmetry about the central frequency. However, this situation cannot be trivially realized except for some limiting cases. It is clear that the coupling strength between the qubit and bosonic mode varies from mode to mode, as given by the spectral density
J(\omega)
. Consequently, one has no reason to expect that the photon number dynamics of one mode is exactly the same as that of another. In fact, the photon number dynamics varies from mode to mode as illustrated in Figs.
3
and
4
. This explanation for the asymmetry is different from the previous attempts based on the quantum optical master equation, which ascribes the asymmetry of the time-dependent spectrum to the “turn-on” effect of interaction between emitter and laser field
Renaud
;
Eberly
. In addition, we can state one limiting case where the symmetric time-dependent spectrum is observed. When
\Omega\ll\omega_{0}
and
\alpha\ll 1
, the equations of motion (
30
) and (
31
) can be well approximated by the quantum optical master equation. In such a situation, the symmetric transient fluorescence spectrum can be observed under certain conditions inferred from the previous works
Renaud
;
Eberly
.

Let us analyze how the spectrum varies with
\alpha
. Figures
5
-
7
show that at a given time, the intensity of spectrum is much greater in the strong-coupling regime than in the weak coupling regime, which results from the fact that the increase of coupling strength

leads to the enhancement of spontaneous decay rate. The last columns of
Figs.

-
7
show that the emission bands are connected and form an integrated whole in the strong coupling regime; in contrast, the emission bands are well separated in the weak coupling regime.
For instance, Fig.

\alpha

This means that in the weak-coupling regime, few photons can be scattered into the modes at the gaps among the emission bands. However, in the strong-coupling regime, these modes can be considerably populated with photons. The present results suggest that the spectral profiles can be significantly modified by the strong dissipation.

V
Conclusions

To summarize, we have studied time-dependent fluorescence spectrum in the moderately weak to strong coupling regime by using the Dirac-Frenkel variational principle and the multiple Davydov D
1
ansatz. The validity of this method is shown by the comparison of the reduced dynamics with that of the HEOM and by the calculation of the ansatz deviation. In contrast with the master-equation approach, this method allows us to bypass two-time correlation functions and directly evaluate the number of scattered photons. We have compared the variational results with those from the two versions of TLME approach: one is based on RWA, and the other is without RWA. In the moderately weak-coupling regime, the variational approach and the TLME approach are found to agree with each other. However, in the strong-coupling regime, results from the two methods diverge at long times. In the case of resonant strong driving, the discrepancy is caused by the inadequate accuracy of the multi-D
1
trial state. In the cases of vanishing, weak, and far-off-resonant driving, the discrepancy is attributed to the breakdown of the second-order perturbation used in the master equation approach.
By comparing the RWA-TLME results with those of TLME and multi-D
1
,
we have illustrated that the counter-rotating coupling between qubit and reservoir has considerable contributions to photon number dynamics and spectra when driving is comparable with the transition frequency of qubit.
Employing the three methods, we have shown that time-dependent fluorescence spectra are generally asymmetric. This can be understood from the viewpoint of photon number dynamics. In addition, we have shown that the strong dissipation and/or multiphoton processes can cause the spectral profile to be substantially different from the Mollow triplet when the driving strength is comparable with to or exceeds the transition frequency.

Our variational formalism equipped with the multiple Davydov D
1
ansatz provides a flexible way to compute the time-dependent spectrum, which captures not only the qubit dynamics but also the field dynamics. The present formalism is capable of treating relatively complicated models of interest in quantum optics. For instance, we can extend the ansatz to study bosonic dynamics when the multiple emitters interact with a common bath
hzk-zfl
. We can also study the bosonic dynamics when two bosonic baths interact with an emitter. An concrete example of the latter is semiconductor quantum dots whose electromagnetic and phonon environments should be taken into account. In addition, although only harmonic driving is considered here, the formalism is applicable to studying pulse or aperiodic driving fields.

By calculating the deviation, we are capable to track the accuracy of the variational results.

Appendix B
Equation of motion for the reduced effective density matrix

The effective density operator
\Lambda(t,t^{\prime})=U(t,t^{\prime})\sigma_{x}\rho(t^{\prime})U^{\dagger}(t,t^{\prime})

satisfies the Liouville equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda(t,t^{\prime})=-i[H(t),\Lambda(t,t^{\prime})]

(44)

with the initial condition
\Lambda(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\sigma_{x}\rho(t^{\prime}).

The equation of motion can be transformed into the interaction picture,
yielding

\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})=-i[H_{{\rm SR}}(t),\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})]\equiv{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}),

(45)

where

\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})=U_{{\rm{\rm S}}}^{\dagger}(t)\exp(iH_{{\rm R}}t)\Lambda(t,t^{\prime})U_{{\rm S}}(t)\exp(-iH_{{\rm R}}t),

(46)

\displaystyle H_{{\rm SR}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{{\rm S}}^{\dagger}(t)\exp(iH_{{\rm R}}t)H_{{\rm SR}}U_{{\rm S}}(t)\exp(-iH_{{\rm R}}t)

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{x}(t)}{2}\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}(b_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}t}+b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{k}t}),

are operators in the interaction picture.

Let the projection operator
{\cal P}
be defined as

{\cal P}\rho={\rm Tr}_{{\rm R}}(\rho)\otimes\rho_{{\rm R}},

(48)

where
\rho_{{\rm R}}
is a fixed state of the reservoir. Let
{\cal Q}

be the complementary projection operator such that

{\cal P}+{\cal Q}=I

(49)

with
I
the identity matrix. Accordingly, one finds that
{\cal P}^{2}={\cal P}
,

{\cal Q}^{2}={\cal Q}
, and
{\cal PQ}={\cal QP}=0
. With
{\cal P}

and
{\cal Q}
, Eq. (
45
) can be partitioned into two parts:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)({\cal P}+{\cal Q})\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}),

(50)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal Q}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)({\cal P}+{\cal Q})\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}).

(51)

{\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})
and
{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

are called the relevant part and irrelevant part, respectively. To
proceed, one solves the second equation and substitute its solution
into the first equation to derive a differential equation for the
relevant part. The second equation can be formally solved as

\displaystyle{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal G}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(52)

\displaystyle+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}ds{\cal G}(t,s){\cal Q}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(s,t^{\prime}),

where

{\cal G}(t,t^{\prime})={\cal T}_{\leftarrow}\exp\left[\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}{\cal Q}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s)ds\right]

(53)

with
{\cal T}_{\leftarrow}
being the time-ordering operator. The
operator
\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(s,t^{\prime})
at time
s
can be related
to
\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})
via

\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(s,t^{\prime})=G_{b}(t,s)\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}),

(54)

with
G_{b}(t,s)={\cal T}_{\rightarrow}\exp\left[-\int_{s}^{t}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(\tau)d\tau\right]

being the backward unitary evolution operator. Substituting Eq. (
54
)
into (
52
), one arrives at

\displaystyle{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal G}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}ds{\cal G}(t,s){\cal Q}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P}

(55)

\displaystyle\times G_{b}(t,s)({\cal P}+{\cal Q})\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}).

Using

\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}ds{\cal G}(t,s){\cal Q}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P}G_{b}(t,s),

(56)

the irrelevant part
{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})
can
be expressed as

\displaystyle{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1-\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{-1}\left[{\cal G}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right.

(57)

\displaystyle\left.+\Sigma(t,t^{\prime}){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})\right],

where we used fact that
1-\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})
can be inverted in a weak-coupling regime or at short times in strong coupling regimes
Breuer
. Substituting Eq. (
57
)
into (
50
), we get the equation of motion for the relevant
part of
\Lambda(t,t^{\prime}):

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})+{\cal I}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})

(58)

\displaystyle+{\cal K}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}),

where

{\cal I}(t,t^{\prime})={\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)\left[1-\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{-1}{\cal G}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q},

(59)

{\cal K}(t,t^{\prime})={\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)\left[1-\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{-1}\Sigma(t,t^{\prime}){\cal P}.

(60)

The inhomogeneous part
{\cal I}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})

can be simplified by using
{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\sigma_{x}(t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\rho^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime})

and

{\cal Q}\rho^{{\rm I}}(t)=\left[1-\Sigma(t,t_{0})\right]^{-1}\Sigma(t,t_{0}){\cal P}\rho^{{\rm I}}(t).

(61)

In deriving Eq. (
61
), we used the factorized initial
state
\rho^{{\rm I}}(t_{0})=\rho_{{\rm S}}^{{\rm I}}(t_{0})\otimes\rho_{{\rm R}}

and the fact that
{\cal Q}\rho^{{\rm I}}(t)
satisfies the same
equation as
{\cal Q}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})
. The equation
of motion becomes

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})+{\cal I}^{\prime}(t,t_{0}){\cal P}\rho^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime})

(62)

\displaystyle+{\cal K}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}),

where

\displaystyle{\cal I}^{\prime}(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)\left[1-\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{-1}{\cal G}(t,t^{\prime}){\cal Q}\sigma_{x}(t^{\prime})

(63)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-\Sigma(t^{\prime},t_{0})\right]^{-1}\Sigma(t^{\prime},t_{0}).

To proceed, we use the expansion
\left[1-\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{n}

and
\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Sigma_{n}(t,t^{\prime})

(
n
indicates the order in the coupling strength
\lambda_{k}
). Up
to the second order in the coupling strength, and using
{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t){\cal P}=0

(as we are interested in
\rho_{{\rm R}}=|\{0_{k}\}\rangle\langle\{0_{k}\}|
),
the kernels are given as

\displaystyle{\cal{\cal I}^{\prime}}(t,t_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}ds{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)\sigma_{x}(t^{\prime}){\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P},

(64)

{\cal K}(t,t^{\prime})=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}ds{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t){\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P}.

(65)

Finally, we obtain the second-order equation of motion

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t^{\prime}}ds{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t)\sigma_{x}(t^{\prime}){\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P}\rho^{{\rm I}}(t^{\prime})

(66)

\displaystyle+\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}ds{\cal P}{\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(t){\cal L}_{{\rm I}}(s){\cal P}\Lambda^{{\rm I}}(t,t^{\prime}).

From Eq. (
66
), one readily derives Eq. (
24
)
in the Schrödinger picture.

Similarly to
\Lambda_{{\rm S}}(t,t^{\prime}),
the reduced density
matrix
\rho_{{\rm S}}(t)
is also obtained via the projection method.
To arrive at Eq. (
25
) , we use the factorized initial state

\rho(0)=\rho_{{\rm S}}(0)\otimes|\{0_{k}\}\rangle\langle\{0_{k}\}|
.

Appendix C
Equations of motion in the Floquet picture

We numerically solve Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) with
the aid of Floquet theory
shirley
;
grifoni
, which states that the evolution operator
of the driven qubit takes the form:

U_{{\rm S}}(t)=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{2}|u_{\gamma}(t)\rangle\langle u_{\gamma}(0)|e^{-i\varepsilon_{\gamma}t},

(67)

where
|u_{\gamma}(t)\rangle=|u_{\gamma}(t+2\pi/\omega_{x})\rangle

is the Floquet state with the real quasienergy
\varepsilon_{\gamma}.

It is straightforward to show that
|u_{\gamma}(t)\rangle
and
\varepsilon_{\gamma}

satisfy the following equation:

[H_{{\rm S}}(t)-i\partial_{t}]|u_{\gamma}(t)\rangle=\varepsilon_{\gamma}|u_{\gamma}(t)\rangle.

(68)

By employing the Sambe space, this differential equation can be solved numerically to yield the Floquet states
and quasienergies
shirley
;
grifoni
. In terms
of the Floquet states, we define the following matrix elements and decay rate:

\Lambda_{\mu\nu}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle u_{\mu}(t)|\Lambda_{{\rm S}}(t,t^{\prime})|u_{\nu}(t)\rangle,

(69)

\rho_{\mu\nu}(t)=\langle u_{\mu}(t)|\rho_{{\rm S}}(t)|u_{\nu}(t)\rangle,

(70)

X_{\mu\nu}(t)=\langle u_{\mu}(t)|\sigma_{x}|u_{\nu}(t)\rangle,

(71)

X_{\mu\nu,n}=\frac{\omega_{x}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi/\omega_{x}}X_{\mu\nu}(t)e^{-in\omega_{x}t}dt,

(72)

\Gamma(\omega,t,t^{\prime})=\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}C(\tau)e^{-i\omega\tau}d\tau.

(73)

With these quantities, we can rewrite the equation of motion for the
effective density operator as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Lambda_{{\rm\mu\nu}}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\Delta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda_{{\rm\mu\nu}}(t,t^{\prime})-\sum_{\gamma,\delta}{\cal K}_{\mu\nu,\gamma\delta}(t-t^{\prime},0)\Lambda_{\gamma\delta}(t,t^{\prime})-{\cal I}_{\mu\nu}(t,t^{\prime}),

(74)

where

\Delta_{\mu\nu}=\varepsilon_{\mu}-\varepsilon_{\nu},

(75)

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{\mu\nu,\gamma\delta}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}e^{in\omega_{x}t}\left\{\sum_{\lambda}\delta_{\nu,\delta}X_{\mu\lambda}(t)X_{\lambda\gamma,n}\Gamma(\Delta_{\lambda\gamma,n},t,t^{\prime})-X_{\mu\gamma,n}X_{\delta\nu}(t)\Gamma(\Delta_{\mu\gamma,n},t,t^{\prime})\right.

(76)

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{\lambda}\delta_{\mu,\gamma}X_{\delta\lambda,n}X_{\lambda\nu}(t)\Gamma^{\ast}(-\Delta_{\delta\lambda,n},t,t^{\prime})-X_{\mu\gamma}(t)X_{\delta\nu,n}\Gamma^{\ast}(-\Delta_{\delta\nu,n},t,t^{\prime})\right\},

and
{\cal I}_{\mu\nu}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle u_{\mu}(t)|{\cal I}(t,t^{\prime})|u_{\nu}(t)\rangle

is the element of the following matrix:

{\cal I}(t,t^{\prime})=[X(t),\sigma_{x}(t,t^{\prime})F(t,t^{\prime})\rho_{{\rm S}}(t,t^{\prime})]+[\sigma_{x}(t,t^{\prime})\rho_{{\rm S}}(t,t^{\prime})F^{\dagger}(t,t^{\prime}),X(t)],

(77)

where

X(t)=\sum_{\mu,\nu}|u_{\mu}(t)\rangle\langle u_{\nu}(t)|X_{\mu\nu}(t),

(78)

\sigma_{x}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{\mu,\nu}|u_{\mu}(t)\rangle\langle u_{\nu}(t)|X_{\mu\nu}(t^{\prime})\exp(-i\Delta_{\mu\nu}t),

(79)

\rho_{{\rm S}}(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{\mu,\nu}|u_{\mu}(t)\rangle\langle u_{\nu}(t)|\rho_{\mu\nu}(t^{\prime})\exp(-i\Delta_{\mu\nu}t),

(80)

F(t,t^{\prime})=\sum_{\mu,\nu}|u_{\mu}(t)\rangle\langle u_{\nu}(t)|\sum_{n}e^{in\omega_{x}t}X_{\mu\nu,n}\Gamma(\Delta_{\mu\nu,n},t,t-t^{\prime}).

(81)

Similarly,
\rho_{\mu\nu}(t^{\prime})
is also calculated in the
Floquet picture.

Appendix D
Hierarchy equations of motion

Let us denote the eigenstates for
\sigma_{z}
as
\sigma
, then the reduced density matrix element for the two-level system is expressed in the path integral form with the factorized initial condition as
PI1
;
PI2
;
PI3

\displaystyle\rho(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\sigma\int\mathcal{D}\sigma^{\prime}\rho(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{\prime};t_{0})

(82)

\displaystyle\times e^{iS[\sigma;t]}F(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)e^{-iS[\sigma^{\prime};t]}.

Here,
S[\sigma;t]
is the action of the two-level system, and
F(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)
is the Feynman-Vernon influence functional, given by

F(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega{J(\omega)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}{d\tau^{\prime}}V^{\times}(\tau)\left[V^{\times}(\tau^{\prime})\coth(\frac{\beta\omega}{2})\cos(\omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))-iV^{\circ}(\tau^{\prime})\sin(\omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))\right]\right).

(83)

Here, we have introduced the abbreviations:

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{x}}{2},

(84)

\displaystyle V^{\times}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V[\tau]-V[\tau^{\prime}],

(85)

\displaystyle V^{\circ}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V[\tau]+V[\tau^{\prime}].

(86)

The correlation function can be written as

C(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega{J}(\omega)\left[\coth(\frac{\beta\omega}{2})\cos(\omega{t})-i\sin(\omega{t})\right],

(87)

where
\beta
is the inverse of the temperature. For the zero temperature case considered in this work, we have

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\alpha\left[\frac{\cos(\omega_{c}t)-1}{t^{2}}+\frac{\omega_{c}\sin(\omega_{c}t)}{t}\right]

(88)

\displaystyle-2i\alpha\left[\frac{\sin(\omega_{c}t)}{t^{2}}-\frac{\omega_{c}\cos(\omega_{c}t)}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle C_{R}(t)+iC_{I}(t).

where
C_{R}(t)
and
C_{I}(t)
are real and imaginary parts of the correlation function, respectively. We apply an incomplete set of oscillatory exponentially decaying functions (OEDFs) for an approximate decomposition
BCD
,

C_{X}^{\mathrm{fit}}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{X}}a_{X;2n-1}\cos(\omega_{X;n}t)e^{-\gamma_{X;n}t}+a_{X;2n}\sin(\omega_{X;n}t)e^{-\gamma_{X;n}t}.

(89)

The fitting parameters,
\left\{{a}_{X;n},\omega_{X;n},\gamma_{X;n}\right\}
, are allowed to be uncorrelated for the real (
X=R
) and imaginary (
X=I
) parts.

We can thus express the correlation function (
88
) as

C(t)\approx\sum_{n=1}^{\mathrm{2N_{R}}}a_{R;n}\varphi_{R;n}(t)+i\sum_{m=1}^{\mathrm{2N_{I}}}a_{I;m}\varphi_{I;m}(t).

(90)

The basis functions are OEDFs, given by

\left\{\varphi_{X;n}(t)\right\}=\left\{\cos(\omega_{X;n}{t})e^{-\gamma_{X;n}t},\sin(\omega_{X;n}{t})e^{-\gamma_{X;n}{t}},n=1,\cdots,N_{X}\right\},

(91)

with
X=R
and
I
. For the two separated basis functions,
\left\{\varphi_{R;n}(t)\right\}
and
\left\{\varphi_{I;m}(t)\right\}
, we have relations,
\partial_{t}\varphi_{R;n}(t)=\sum_{n^{\prime}}\eta_{R;n,n^{\prime}}\varphi_{R;n^{\prime}}(t)
and
\partial_{t}\varphi_{I;m}(t)=\sum_{m^{\prime}}\eta_{I;m,m^{\prime}}\varphi_{I;m^{\prime}}(t)
, where

\eta_{X,n,n^{\prime}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}-\gamma_{X;1}&-\omega_{X;1}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\omega_{X;1}&-\gamma_{X;1}&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&-\gamma_{X;2}&-\omega_{X;2}&\cdots&0&0\\
0&0&\omega_{X;2}&-\gamma_{X;2}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&-\gamma_{X;N_{X}}&-\omega_{X;N_{X}}\\
0&0&0&0&\cdots&\omega_{X;N_{X}}&-\omega_{X;N_{X}}\\
\end{array}\right).

(92)

The influence functional equation can be expressed as

\displaystyle F(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}\exp\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}V^{\times}(\tau)V^{\times}(\tau^{\prime})a_{R;n}\varphi_{R;n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right)

(93)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}\exp\left(-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}V^{\times}(\tau)V^{\circ}(\tau^{\prime})ia_{I;m}\varphi_{I;m}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right).

Taking the derivative of Eq. (
82
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\rho(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\mathcal{L}\rho(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)-V^{\times}(t)\int\mathcal{D}\sigma\int\mathcal{D}\sigma^{\prime}\rho(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{\prime};t_{0})

(94)

\displaystyle\times\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau{V}^{\times}(\tau)\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}a_{R;n}\varphi_{R;n}(t-\tau)+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau{V}^{\circ}(\tau)\sum_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}ia_{I;m}\varphi_{I;m}(t-\tau)\right]

\displaystyle\times{e}^{iS[\sigma,t]}F(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)e^{-iS[\sigma^{\prime};t]},

where
{\cal L}
is the Liouville superoperator describing the unitary evolution governed by
H_{\rm S}(t)
.
In order to derive the equation of motion, we introduce the auxiliary operator
\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}
by its matrix element as

\displaystyle\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\sigma\int\mathcal{D}\sigma^{\prime}\rho(\sigma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{\prime};t_{0})\prod_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau{V}^{\times}(\tau)\varphi_{R;n}(t-\tau)\right)^{j_{n}}

(95)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau{V}^{\circ}(\tau)\varphi_{I;m}(t-\tau)\right)^{k_{m}}e^{iS[\sigma,t]}F(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)e^{-iS[\sigma^{\prime};t]}

for non-negative integers
j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}
. It should be noted that
\rho_{0,\cdots,0}(t)=\rho(t)
denote the true reduced density matrix, while other auxiliary density matrices are introduced to take into account all orders of system-bath couplings. Differentiating
\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)
with respect to
t
, we obtain the following hierarchy of equations in operator form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\mathcal{L}\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

(96)

\displaystyle+V^{\times}(t)\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}j_{n}\varphi_{R;n}(0)\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{n}-1,\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle+V^{\circ}(t)\sum_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}k_{m}\varphi_{I;m}(0)\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{m}-1,\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{2N_{R}}j_{n}\eta_{R;n,n^{\prime}}\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{n}-1,\cdots,j_{n^{\prime}}+1,\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{2N_{I}}k_{m}\eta_{I;m,m^{\prime}}\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{m}-1,\cdots,k_{m^{\prime}}+1,\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle-V^{\times}(t)\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}a_{R;n}\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{n}+1,\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t)

\displaystyle-V^{\times}(t)\sum_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}ia_{I;m}\rho_{j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{m}+1,\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime};t).

The HEOM consists of an infinite number of equations, which must be truncated for practical simulations. For this purpose, the integers
j_{1},\cdots,j_{2N_{R}};k_{1},\cdots,k_{2N_{I}}
should satisfy
\sum_{n=1}^{2N_{R}}j_{n}+\sum_{m=1}^{2N_{I}}k_{m}\leq\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{trun}}
, where
\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{trun}}
is the depth of the hierarchy.
