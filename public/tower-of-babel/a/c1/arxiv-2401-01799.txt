Optimization and Identification of Lattice Quantizers
Erik Agrell, Daniel Pook-Kolb, Bruce Allen

Abstract
Lattices with minimal normalized second moments are designed using a new numerical optimization algorithm. Starting from a random lower-triangular generator matrix and applying stochastic gradient descent, all elements are updated towards the negative gradient, which makes it the most efficient algorithm proposed so far for this purpose. A graphical illustration of the theta series, called theta image, is introduced and shown to be a powerful tool for converting numerical lattice representations into their underlying exact forms. As a proof of concept, optimized lattices are designed in dimensions up to 16. In all dimensions, the algorithm converges to either the previously best known lattice or a better one. The dual of the 15-dimensional laminated lattice is conjectured to be optimal in its dimension and its exact normalized second moment is computed.



I
Introduction

Aclassical problem in geometry is how to construct a structure of infinitely many points in
n
-dimensional space so that the mean square distance between a random real vector and its closest member of the point structure is minimal, for a fixed density of points per unit volume. Apart from its fundamental theoretical value, the problem has important practical applications in, e.g., digital communications (both source
[
1
]
and channel coding
[
2
, p. 70]
,
[
3
, Ch. 9]
), pattern recognition
[
4
]
, cryptography
[
5
]
, hashing
[
6
]
, machine learning
[
7
]
, and data analysis
[
8
]
.

The construction of structures with small mean square distance is traditionally called the
quantizer problem
and the figure of merit is the (possibly normalized)
second moment.
The problem was first formulated in 1959 by Fejes Tóth, who also solved it for the two-dimensional case
[
9
]
. The optimal structure in two dimensions is, not surprisingly, the hexagonal lattice.
Gersho in 1979 suggested lattice structures in three and four dimensions
[
10
]
, which still today offer the smallest known normalized second moments (NSM) known in their respective dimensions. The proposed three-dimensional structure, which is the body-centered cubic lattice, was proved to be optimal among three-dimensional lattices by Barnes and Sloane
[
11
]
. Lattices with small NSM are called
lattice quantizers.

In 1982, Conway and Sloane calculated the NSMs of the most common lattice families and tabulated the best known lattice quantizers in dimensions up to
10

[
12
]
. They further conjectured that the best lattice quantizer in any dimension is the dual of the lattice that solves the more well-studied
packing problem.
Two years later, the same authors found improved lattices in dimensions
6
and
7
and numerically computed the NSMs of known lattices in dimensions
12
,
16
, and
24
, which all supported their duality conjecture
[
13
]
. The pioneering lattice research by Conway and Sloane is summarized in their now classic textbook
[
2
]
.

Improved lattice quantizers in dimensions
9
and
10
(as well as improved nonlattice structures in dimensions
7
and
9
) were found by Agrell and Eriksson in 1998 using a numerical optimization technique
[
14
]
. These lattices are not duals of the densest lattice packings known, and thus indicate that Conway and Sloane’s conjecture might not be true. Dutour Sikirić
et al.
found an improved lattice in
11
dimensions and evaluated its NSM exactly, thus providing further evidence against the conjecture
[
15
]
. Allen and Agrell described exactly the conjectured optimal
9
-dimensional lattice quantizer, which was previously only approximated numerically
[
16
]
. Recently, some improved lattices in dimensions
12
and above have been reported
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

While every lattice for which the exact NSM has been computed serves to constructively prove the achievability of that particular NSM value, there is no known “converse theorem” in the sense of proving that no better lattice exists. Lower bounds on the NSM are known or conjectured
[
21
]
, but they are not tight in any dimension above
2
.

In this paper, we take a fresh look at numerical optimization of lattice quantizers, which as far as we know has not seen any progress since 1998
[
14
]
. A more efficient algorithm for the purpose is proposed. Like the previous algorithm, the new one employs stochastic gradient descent (SGD), which is a standard technique in numerical optimization. The main difference lies in how the gradient is computed. In the previous algorithm, one degree of freedom is reserved to maintain a fixed number of lattice points per unit volume, while the other degrees of freedom are updated guided by the gradient of the
unnormalized
second moment. Thereby the NSM decreases, but unfortunately not in the direction of its gradient. This shortcoming is resolved in the new algorithm, which makes it more efficient.

We also address the question of identifying an exact form for a lattice that is only known numerically. To this end, we introduce a new graphical tool, called
theta image,
and use it to derive a linear system of equations whose solution gives the desired exact form in many cases. Even in cases where the method does not yield an exact form, most of the unknown degrees of freedom are identified as functions of a small number of unknown parameters.

As a proof of concept, lattices with conjectured minimal NSMs are constructed in dimensions up to
16
, and exact forms are identified for all of them. The results revisit previously best known lattice quantizers in most considered dimensions and reveals a new best lattice quantizer in
15
dimensions.

The paper is organized as follows. Sec.
II
compactly summarizes relevant lattice definitions and theory. The new algorithm is theoretically developed in Sec.
III
. Sec.
IV
provides pseudocode for the algorithm along with some implementational considerations. This section is mainly aimed at programmers and more or less stand-alone from the theoretical foundation in Sec.
III
. Other possible algorithms for numerical optimization of lattice quantizers are reviewed in Sec.
V
, where also the improvements over
[
14
]
are discussed. In Sec.
VI
, we discuss how the exact form of a lattice that is only known approximatively can be obtained using the theta series. Sec.
VII
, finally, demonstrates the potential of the new framework by constructing conjectured optimal lattice quantizers.

II
Lattices and Their Second Moments

Notation:

The norm (Euclidean length) of a vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is denoted by
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|
. The
n\times n
identity matrix is denoted by
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{n}
. The
i
th element of a vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is denoted by
[{\boldsymbol{x}}]_{i}
, while the element in row
i
, column
j

{\boldsymbol{X}}
is denoted by
[{\boldsymbol{X}}]_{i,j}
. All vectors are row vectors.

Given a set of
n
linearly independent

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, a

{\mathcal{L}}
is the set of all linear combinations of these vectors with integer coefficients. The basis vectors are collected as rows in an
n\times m

{\boldsymbol{B}}
, where the condition of linearly independent rows implies
m\geq n
. Expressed in terms of the generator matrix, the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
consists of the points
{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. By definition, the all-zero vector
{\boldsymbol{0}}
belongs to any lattice. The inner products of all basis vectors with each other are collected in the symmetric, positive definite
Gram matrix

{\boldsymbol{A}}={\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\mathrm{T}}
.

The basis vectors span an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space, which is a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
The set of points in this subspace that are closer to
{\boldsymbol{0}}
than to any other point in
{\mathcal{L}}
is the
Voronoi region

\Omega
of the lattice. The Voronoi region
\Omega
is a
fundamental cell
of
{\mathcal{L}}
, which means that infinitely many copies of
\Omega
, each translated by a vector in
{\mathcal{L}}
, fills the
n
-dimensional space in which the lattice resides without gaps or overlaps, except on the boundaries. Another fundamental cell of
{\mathcal{L}}
is the parallelotope
[0,1)^{n}{\boldsymbol{B}}
.

The standard figures-of-merit for lattices can all be expressed in terms of properties of
\Omega
. In this paper, we focus on the NSM
[
10
]
,
[
2
, pp. 34, 56–62]

\displaystyle G=\frac{1}{nV^{1+2/n}}\int_{\Omega}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

(1)

where
V=(\det{\boldsymbol{A}})^{1/2}
is the
n
-volume of
\Omega
. The normalization coefficient
1/V^{1+2/n}
ensures that
G
is dimensionless and hence invariant to lattice scaling, while the coefficient
1/n
ensures that lattices constructed as the product of identical lower-dimensional lattices have the same NSM as these constituent lattices
[
18
, Sec. IV]
. For example, the cubic lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
has
G=1/12
for any
n
.

Two lattices are
equivalent
in Euclidean space if their Voronoi regions are congruent, and two generator matrices are equivalent if they generate equivalent lattices. Equivalent lattices have the same NSM. The packing density, covering density, kissing number, and all other metrics invariant to scaling and orthogonal transformation are also the same. Mathematically, two generator matrices
{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}
of sizes
n\times m_{1}
and
n\times m_{2}
, respectively, where
m_{1}\leq m_{2}
, are equivalent if and only if
[
2
, p. 10]

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}=c\,{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{B}}{\boldsymbol{R}},

(2)

where
c
is a real scalar,
{\boldsymbol{U}}
is an
n\times n
integer matrix with determinant
\pm 1
, and
{\boldsymbol{R}}
is a real
m_{1}\times m_{2}
matrix with orthonormal rows, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{R}}{\boldsymbol{R}}^{\mathrm{T}}={\boldsymbol{I}}_{m_{1}}
.
Left-multiplication by
{\boldsymbol{U}}
corresponds to a basis change and right-multiplication by
{\boldsymbol{R}}
to an orthogonal transformation, i.e., rotation and/or reflection.
The corresponding relation between Gram matrices is

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime}=c^{2}{\boldsymbol{U}}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(3)

For every generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\prime}
of size
n\times m_{2}
, there exists a square generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
that satisfies (
2
) with
m_{1}=n
. The analysis of lattices in Euclidean space can therefore without loss of generality be confined to square generator matrices, which is exploited in this work.

Another important characteristic of a lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
is the
theta series

[
2
, pp. 44–47]

\displaystyle\theta(q)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{L}}}q^{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}}=1+\tau q^{4\rho^{2}}+\cdots

(4)

where
q
is a dummy variable. It characterizes how the lattice points
are distributed on spherical
shells
around the origin. The
first term arises from the lattice vector
{\boldsymbol{0}}
. The second term
contains information about both the
kissing number

\tau
,
i.e., the number of shortest nonzero lattice vectors, and the

packing radius

\rho
, i.e., half the minimal distance between
two lattice vectors. Equivalent lattices have the same theta series.

III
Stochastic Gradient Descent

The starting point for minimization by stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is to write the objective function as the expected value of a real function of (i) a set of random variables with a given distribution and (ii) a set of deterministic variables, whose values are to be optimized. The gradient of this real function with respect to the deterministic variables is calculated, or if the exact gradient is not known, the gradient of a Monte-Carlo estimate of the objective function serves as a surrogate. The distribution of the random variables does not have to be known analytically, as long as an algorithmic procedure is available to generate random samples. Independent samples are generated sequentially using this procedure, and for every sample, the deterministic variables are shifted a small step in the direction of the negative gradient. In this section, theory is developed for applying SGD to NSM minimization. This leads to our lattice construction algorithm, whose implementation is described in the next section.

III-A

Second Moment Estimation

An elegant method to generate random vectors uniformly in the Voronoi region
\Omega
of a given lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
was proposed in
[
13
]
for the purpose of NSM estimation. Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be a random vector drawn uniformly from the unit
n
-cube
[0,1)^{n}
and let, for a given generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
,

\displaystyle{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\|({\boldsymbol{z}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{B}}\|^{2}.

(5)

Now
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
is the lattice point closest to
{\boldsymbol{z}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
(which is normally not a lattice point). Since the Voronoi region
\Omega
and the parallelotope
[0,1)^{n}{\boldsymbol{B}}
are both fundamental cells of
{\mathcal{L}}
,
{\boldsymbol{e}}=({\boldsymbol{z}}-{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}){\boldsymbol{B}}
is uniformly distributed in
\Omega
. The generation of uniform random vectors in
\Omega
is discussed in detail in
[
3
, Sec. 4.2]
.

To calculate (
5
) requires solving the
closest point problem
for a given lattice. Algorithms for this purpose are available for classical, well-structured lattices
[
22
,
13
]
as well as arbitrary lattices
[
23
,
24
]
.

Using these definitions of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
,
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{e}}
, the NSM in (
1
) can be written as

\displaystyle G={\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}[g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})],

(6)

where

\displaystyle g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})=\frac{1}{n}V^{-2/n}\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}.

(7)

Here
V
is a function of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}
is a function of both
{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}
.
Note that even if (
5
) has multiple solutions,
g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})
is still well-defined,
because
\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}
has the same value for all solutions
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
. These multiple solutions
occur iff
{\boldsymbol{z}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
lies on the boundary of
\Omega
(or its images
under translation by the lattice).

If
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{T}
denote
T
independent realizations of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
, then an unbiased estimate of
G
follows immediately from (
6
) as

\displaystyle\hat{G}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}).

(8)

To quantify the estimation accuracy, we use an unbiased estimator of the variance of
\hat{G}

[
25
, Sec. IV]

\displaystyle{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}g^{2}({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t})-\hat{G}^{2}\right)\,,

(9)

which is much more accurate than the “jackknife” estimator recommended in earlier literature.

It is easily verified that (
8
) remains unchanged if the lattice, represented by
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, is rescaled. However, previous descriptions of the same NSM estimation method are valid only for lattices with
V=1
. This is because of an unfortunate error in the original publication
[
13
]
, where the right-hand sides of
[
13
, Eqs. (2), (4)]
are missing a factor corresponding to the volume of the Voronoi region (here denoted by
V
). This error appears to have propagated to
[
17
, Eqs. (73)–(74)]
and
[
25
, Eqs. (12)–(15)]
.

III-B

Iterative basis vector update

If the gradient of the NSM
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}G
were known analytically for arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, then this gradient could be used to minimize
G
. In a few special cases where we know
G
exactly, it is an infinitely differentiable (
C^{\infty}
) function of certain elements of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
in a certain domain, or
phase,
within which the overall topology of
\Omega
(by which we mean the number of faces in any dimension and their pattern of intersection) is fixed. Furthermore, it is many times differentiable (approximately
n
times) on the boundaries between such domains, called
phase transitions

[
16
,
20
]
. If these examples are indicative of how
G
behaves in general, then the function
G({\boldsymbol{B}})\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
C^{\infty}
except on these phase transitions. Thus,
generic values of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
have open neighborhoods in which
G
is smooth, so gradient descent could be effective. We are however not aware of any general expression for
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}G
.

In the absence of the exact
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}G
, the NSM estimate
\hat{G}
in (
8
) can be straightforwardly differentiated and is well suited for minimization by SGD.
Specifically, we generate a sequence of random vectors
{\boldsymbol{z}}
, and for each
{\boldsymbol{z}}
, we calculate
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}
. Then the generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is updated a small step in the direction that decreases
g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})
most, which is the negative gradient with respect to the elements of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
.

By a suitable choice of coordinate system, every lattice can be represented by a
square, lower-triangular
generator matrix with
positive diagonal elements.
Therefore, without loss of generality, our lattice construction method considers only such generator matrices. From now on and until the end of Sec.
IV
, the generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
has size
n\times n
and its elements satisfy
[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}=0
for
i<j
and
[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}>0
for
i=j
. Apart from reducing the number of degrees of freedom from
nm
to
n(n+1)/2
, these conditions greatly facilitate the calculation of the volume
V
as a function of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, which is now simply

\displaystyle V=\prod_{k=1}^{n}[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{k,k}.

(10)

Furthermore, the closest point algorithms we use to compute
{\boldsymbol{e}}
from
{\boldsymbol{z}}
need a triangular matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}

[
23
,
24
]
.

We are now ready to calculate the gradient of
g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})

in (
7
), which consists of the partial derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}=\frac{1}{n}V^{-2/n}\frac{\partial\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}+\frac{1}{n}\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}\frac{\partial V^{-2/n}}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}.

(11)

Note that for the multiple solution case described after (
7
),
the first partial derivative on the right-hand side of (
11
) is
undefined. For generic
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, when
{\boldsymbol{z}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
crosses the boundary of

\Omega
, the derivative steps abruptly from a well-defined value on
one side of the boundary to a different well-defined value on the
other side. For a given
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, the set of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
for which this occurs
is a set of measure zero. Thus, from here forward, we assume that

{\boldsymbol{z}}
denotes one of the
T
values
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1},\ldots{\boldsymbol{z}}_{T}
, which
do not lie in this set of measure zero.

We define
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{z}}-{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
, so that
{\boldsymbol{e}}={\boldsymbol{y}}{\boldsymbol{B}}
. For a given
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and infinitesimal changes of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
,
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
are unchanged.
Hence
\partial{\boldsymbol{y}}/\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}=-\partial{\hat{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}/\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}=0
for all
i
and
j
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{e}}}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}{\boldsymbol{e}}^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=2[{\boldsymbol{y}}]_{i}[{\boldsymbol{e}}]_{j},\quad\textrm{if $i\geq j$},

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{-2/n}}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{k,k}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\textrm{if $i>j$}.

(13)

For the diagonal elements,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V^{-2/n}}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,i}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{k,k}^{-2/n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{n}[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,i}^{-1-2/n}\prod_{k\neq i}[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{k,k}^{-2/n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2V^{-2/n}}{n[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,i}}.

(14)

Combining (
11
), (
12
), (
13
), and (
III-B
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,j}}=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{n}V^{-2/n}[{\boldsymbol{y}}]_{i}[{\boldsymbol{e}}]_{j},&i>j,\\
\frac{2}{n}V^{-2/n}\left([{\boldsymbol{y}}]_{i}[{\boldsymbol{e}}]_{i}-\frac{\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2}}{n[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,i}}\right),&i=j.\end{cases}

(15)

Now introducing explicit notation for the steps in the SGD algorithm, we denote the
T
random realizations of
{\boldsymbol{z}}
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}
for
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, with
t
being referred to as “time.” In each time instant
t
, the generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}={\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}
is updated as

\displaystyle[{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t+1}]_{i,j}=[{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}]_{i,j}-\epsilon\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{B}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t})}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}]_{i,j}}

(16)

for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
and
i\geq j
, where
\epsilon
is a small step size.
1
1

1

With time-dependent
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, other quantities such as
V
and
{\boldsymbol{e}}
also become time-dependent, although we will still write them without explicit subscripts
t
.

As usual when gradient descent is applied to a smooth function, the iterations converge for small enough step sizes. If a too large step is taken in (
16
), then
{\boldsymbol{B}}
may move to the other side of the “valley”, where
g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})
increases again. For very large steps, a diagonal element
[{\boldsymbol{B}}]_{i,i}
may even become negative, in which case the optimization fails. To avoid this,
\epsilon
should be chosen small enough. What is small enough depends greatly on the structure of the generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. Generally, an ill-conditioned
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is more sensitive to the size of
\epsilon
, whereas a
reduced

{\boldsymbol{B}}
is more stable. A reduced generator matrix is one whose rows (basis vectors) are relatively short and orthogonal to each other, according to some heuristic criterion
[
26
]
. We therefore apply
lattice reduction
to
{\boldsymbol{B}}
regularly throughout the iterations
[
14
]
. Reduction also brings the added benefit of speeding up the closest point search algorithm
[
23
]
, which dominates the complexity of the optimization procedure. On the other hand, reduction generally brings the generator matrix away from the desired lower-triangular form, but this can be alleviated by rotating the coordinate system.

The elements of the initial generator matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{0}
are chosen as independent zero-mean unit-variance Gaussian random variables, immediately followed by reduction and orthogonal transformation to bring it into the desired lower-triangular form with positive diagonal elements. Repeating the optimization procedure multiple times with different
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{0}
enables the algorithm to find local minima in different parts of the optimization space, which is essential when searching for a global minimum of the nonconvex function
G
. There are infinitely many global minima in any dimensions
n
, reflecting the fact that equivalent generator matrices (
2
) yield the same NSM
G
.

III-C

Step size

The convergence of the algorithm depends strongly on the choice of step size
\epsilon
in (
16
). The step size may be selected as a function of
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}
and
t
(but not
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t}
,
i
, or
j
). In our optimization algorithm, we define

\displaystyle\epsilon=(n/2)V^{2/n}\mu_{t},

(17)

where
\mu_{t}
is an annealing scheme to be discussed later.

The factor
V^{2/n}
in (
17
) makes the algorithm insensitive to the scale of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. To see this, consider how the update rule (
16
) affects a family of generator matrices
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}={\boldsymbol{B}}=c\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}
for any fixed generator matrix
\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}
and a variable scale factor
c>0
.
For a given
{\boldsymbol{z}}
,
{\boldsymbol{e}}
scales linearly with
c
,
V
scales with
c^{n}
, and
g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})
in (
7
) remains constant. Furthermore, the gradient (
15
) scales with
c^{-1}
and
\epsilon
in (
17
) scales with
c^{2}
. Therefore,
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t+1}
in (
16
) scales linearly with
c
if
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}
scales with
c
, as desired. The coefficient
n/2
is not important and only serves to simplify the notation in Sec.
IV
.

Substituting (
17
) into (
16
), the update rule becomes

\displaystyle[{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t+1}]_{i,j}=[{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}]_{i,j}-\mu_{t}\frac{nV^{2/n}}{2}\frac{\partial g({\boldsymbol{B}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{t})}{\partial[{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}]_{i,j}},

(18)

where the coefficient
(n/2)V^{2/n}
is conveniently absorbed by its inverse in (
15
).

The purpose of
\mu_{t}
is that the speed of movement in the variable space should decrease gradually with time, which is why it is called an annealing (or cooling) scheme. A large step size is beneficial initially, allowing
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}
to move fast to the vicinity of a minimum and escape possible plateaus and shallow local minima, whereas a small step size is better near the end of the optimization process, in order to fine-tune
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}
towards the exact minimum.

As usual in SGD optimization, there is no guarantee that the algorithm will converge to the global optimum, or even to a local minimum. It may with a nonzero probability terminate literally anywhere in the vast space of generator matrices
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. The annealing scheme and other optimization parameters should be chosen to maximize the probability of a successful outcome, i.e., a lattice very near a local optimum.

Lacking an analytic strategy to find the best annealing scheme for our purpose, we tested many options numerically. We focused on
n=8
, where, according to a 1998 conjecture
[
14
]
, there is a unique local optimum: the Gosset lattice
E_{8}
. We designed many thousands of
8
-dimensional lattices using the proposed SGD method and estimated how similar the obtained lattices were to
E_{8}
. However, comparing two lattices is not a trivial task, since any given lattice, including
E_{8}
, can be represented using infinitely many different generator matrices. Instead of comparing the generator matrices directly, we therefore numerically found the shortest nonzero lattice vectors in each of the obtained lattices. This was done using a modified version of the “kissing number” algorithm in
[
23
, Sec. VI-B]
. In the
E_{8}

Algorithm

V-B

Full-matrix stochastic gradient descent

In Algorithm
1

n\times n
generator matrix according to the negative gradient of the NSM with respect to all matrix elements. Even if the initial matrix would be lower-triangular, the NSM gradient would soon bring it away from that structure. An optimization algorithm based on this principle can be defined as follows.

Let

\displaystyle g({\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{z}})=\frac{1}{n}|\det{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{-2/n}\|{\boldsymbol{e}}\|^{2},

V-C

Whitening the quantization noise

In 1996, Zamir and Feder proved that the optimal lattice quantizer in every dimension has white quantization noise; i.e., a covariance matrix

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{U}}=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}

(31)

proportional to the identity
[
30
]
. Furthermore, if a lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
has a covariance matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}
that is not proportional to the identity, then
{\mathcal{L}}{\boldsymbol{U}}^{-1/2}
has a lower NSM than
{\mathcal{L}}
. The whiteness property was generalized to locally optimal lattices in
[
18
]
and applied to optimize a parametric lattice in
[
20
]
.
The second moment matrix can be empirically estimated as
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}={\boldsymbol{E}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\boldsymbol{E}}/p
, where
{\boldsymbol{E}}
is a
p\times n
vector whose rows are independent random vectors, uniformly distributed in
\Omega
, and
p\geq n
. The generation of uniform random vectors in
\Omega
is discussed in Sec.
III-A
.

A simple lattice optimization algorithm follows straightforwardly from the white noise property. The idea is to repeatedly estimate the covariance matrix and update the generator matrix. Every update consists of computing

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}^{\mathrm{T}}{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}}{p}\right)^{-1/2},

(32)

where
{\boldsymbol{E}}_{t}
is composed of
p
vectors in the Voronoi region of the lattice generated by
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t}
, possibly followed by reduction and renormalization. Much fewer iterations are needed than with any of the previously described algorithms, but every iteration is more complex, involving
p
closest point searches. Like in the previous algorithm, the generator matrix needs to be rotated into lower-triangular form in every iteration to enable closest point search.

Good results were obtained when
p
increases with
t
. Apart from this simple observation, we leave the meta-optimization of the algorithm parameters as an open question.

V-D

Simulated annealing

Simulated annealing
is a classical heuristic in numerical optimization. The idea is to accept suboptimal updates with some probability, so that the algorithm will sometimes move ‘‘uphill,’’ away from local minima.
5
5

5

The annealing scheme used to adjust the step size in Sec.
III-C
is not simulated annealing in this sense.

The probability is decreased with time as the optimization progresses and is sometimes referred to as “temperature.” Simulated annealing has been successfully applied in the design of several types of codes and quantizers
[
31
,
32
]
, but not lattices, as far as we know.

All algorithms mentioned in this paper can be modified to include some aspects of simulated annealing. We propose to introduce it in the closest lattice point search, which consumes a large share of the computational resources in all considered optimization algorithms. Such search methods
[
23
,
24
]
work by enumerating lattice points inside a sphere and reducing the size of the sphere every time a lattice point is found, until the sphere is empty. Simulated annealing can be implemented in this context by aborting the search with some probability every time a new lattice point is found. Thereby we achieve not only the desired erratic movement but also reduced computational complexity. If the probability, or temperature, is
100\,\%
, then the returned vector is the so-called
Babai point,
which is found very fast
[
33
,
23
]
, and if it is
0\,\%
, then the search method reverts to its normal, optimal behavior.

VI
Identification of Exact Lattices

When a numerical lattice optimization algorithm terminates, the result is a generator matrix with numerical entries. Such matrices can be used to numerically estimate various lattice parameters, such as the NSM, packing radius, and covering radius. They do not immediately offer any geometrical insights about for example symmetries or algebraic construction methods,
nor do they enable any efficient search strategies such as the closest point algorithms in
[
34
, Ch. 20]
, but they can pave the way for such progress if their underlying exact forms can be identified. Therefore, the algorithm’s outputs should if possible be refined. We do this in three steps: first, computing and visualizing the theta series; second, replacing the numerically defined lattice with a similar but exact lattice, whose theta series has a certain desired form; and third, checking the obtained exact lattice for consistency.

is the
quadratic form

\|{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{B}}\|^{2}={\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. If
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is not known exactly but only through an approximation
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
, then we can use the theta image of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
to identify a set of integer vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{M}
for which
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\approx\cdots\approx{\boldsymbol{u}}_{M}\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{M}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Thereafter we replace
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}
with an unknown, generic
{\boldsymbol{A}}
of the same dimension and solve the equations
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}=\cdots={\boldsymbol{u}}_{M}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{M}^{\mathrm{T}}
with the same integer vectors. Additional equations can be obtained from other vertical steps in the theta image. We do not impose a certain value for the norms, only that they should be equal to each other. The first diagonal element of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is set to
1
to fix the scale and
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is symmetric, giving a system of equations with
n(n+1)/2-1
unknowns.

The system of equations is linear and can be solved exactly using standard methods. One of three possible outcomes can occur: one solution, multiple solutions, or no solution. If a single solution occurs, then it is presumably a locally optimal lattice quantizer and the investigation can proceed to the next stage, which is to characterize its properties and compare it with known lattices. If multiple solutions occur, then the system of equations was underdetermined and it may help to extract equations from additional vertical steps in the theta image. If these additional equations do not reduce the number of solutions, it may be because the symmetry group of the underlying exact lattice is separable into disjoint groups involving separate lower-dimensional rotations. The solution may still identify lower-dimensional sublattices and thus reduce the number of unknowns from
n(n+1)/2-1
, even though at least one unknown remains. Such
parametric lattices
require additional numerical and analytical methods to be identified exactly
[
20
]
. The smallest dimension for which this occurs is
n=9
, where a single parameter cannot be identified by symmetry considerations
[
14
,
16
]
. The third and final outcome is that no solution is returned. Then the system was overdetermined and a plausible explanation is that what looks like a single vertical step in the theta image may theoretically consist of two steps at different but similar norms. We have not yet encountered this outcome in any practical trials.

VI-C

Validation and Identification

An important consistency check is now to compute the theta series of the obtained exact lattice, including more terms than those considered in the equation-solving framework described in Sec.
VI-B
. If those additional terms match vertical steps in the theta image of the numerically optimized lattice, then it is a strong indication that its exact form has been correctly determined. Furthermore, the theta series can be compared with other theta series found numerically, analytically, or in the literature, to help identify equivalences.

We return
to the
10
-dimensional lattice example of Sec.
VI-A
. When the optimization algorithm terminates, it has
180
points with squared norms between
1.952
and
2.043
and
512
points with squared norms between
2.460
and
2.544
. The lattice has no points with squared norms between
2.043
and
2.460
, and no nonzero points with squared norm less than
1.952
. The two point sets, which are visualized in the first two vertical steps in Fig.
1
, provide together
179+511=690
linear equations in
54
unknowns. The system of equations has a unique solution, apart from an overall scale factor, which gives a Gram matrix with rational elements.
The mean square error between the elements of the numerical and exact Gram matrices is
7.0\cdot 10^{-4}
, when both matrices are scaled to determinant
1
.
When instead the “Slow” parameters in Tab.
I
were used, a mean square error of
7.4\cdot 10^{-5}
was obtained.
These error values should be interpreted as mere indications, since they were not averaged over many trials.

The theta image of the resulting lattice, scaled to
\det{\boldsymbol{A}}=1
, is shown in Fig.
2
. Indeed all vertical steps, not only the first two, agree perfectly with steps in Fig.
1
.
The theta series corresponding to Fig.
2
is

\displaystyle 1+180q^{2}+512q^{5/2}+3380q^{4}+5120q^{9/2}+\cdots.

(34)

This result suggests that the obtained lattice might be
D^{+}_{10}
, which has exactly the same theta series
[
2
, p. 120]
and has been previously suggested as the best
10
-dimensional lattice quantizer
[
14
]
.

The NSM

We conjecture that all seven lattices are optimal in their respective dimensions.

VIII
Conclusions

Using a new numerical optimization algorithm and a new technique to derive exact lattices from numerically defined ones, conjectured optimized lattice quantizers are obtained in selected dimensions up to
16
. In dimensions
13
and
14
, new parametric lattices are discovered. The dual of the
15
-dimensional laminated lattice emerges as a new best lattice in dimension
15
. In dimensions
10
,
11
,
12
, and
16
, numerical evidence is obtained that the previously best known lattices might indeed be optimal.

It took
27
years until the benchmark algorithm was improved and the range of numerically designed lattice quantizers was extended from
10
dimensions up to
16
. If anyone in the next decades would embark on a journey towards dimensions beyond
16
, we believe that the algorithms reviewed in Sec.
V
may deserve to be carefully explored and refined.
