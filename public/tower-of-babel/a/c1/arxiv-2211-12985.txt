The first negative Fourier coefficient of an Eisenstein series newform
Sebastián Carrillo Santana

Abstract
. There have been a number of papers on statistical questions concerning the sign changes of Fourier coefficients of newforms. In one such paper, Linowitz and Thompson gave a conjecture describing when, on average, the first negative sign of the Fourier coefficients of an Eisenstein series newform occurs. In this paper, we correct their conjecture and prove the corrected version.



1.
Introduction

For a Dirichlet character
\chi
and a positive integer
N
, we will denote by
M_{k}(N,\chi)
the vector space of modular forms on
\Gamma_{0}(N)
of weight
k
, level
N
and character
\chi
. Let
E_{k}(N,\chi)
be the subspace of Eisenstein series and
S_{k}(N,\chi)
the subspace of cusp forms. For a prime
p
, we let
T_{p}
be the
p
th Hecke operator.

Let
H_{k}^{*}(N)
be the subspace of
S_{k}(\chi_{0},N)
of newforms with trivial character
\chi_{0}
. Given a newform
f\in H_{k}^{*}(N)
, let
\lambda_{f}(p)
be the eigenvalue of
f
with respect to the Hecke operator
T_{p}
. The restriction to the trivial character ensures that the sequence
\{\lambda_{f}(p)\}
is real. Many authors have studied the sequence of signs of the Hecke eigenvalues of
f
. For example, one could pose questions such as:

(1)

Are there infinitely many primes
p
such that
\lambda_{f}(p)>0
(or
\lambda_{f}(p)<0
)?

(2)

What is the first change of sign? More specifically, what is the smallest
n\geqslant 1
(or prime
p
) such that
\lambda_{f}(n)<0
(or
\lambda_{f}(p)<0
)? This is an analogue of the least quadratic non-residue problem.

(3)

Given an arbitrary sequence of signs
\varepsilon_{p}\in\{\pm 1\}
, what is the number of newforms
f
(in some family) such that
\sgn\lambda_{f}(p)=\varepsilon_{p}
for all
p\leqslant x
?

In the cusp form setting, questions (i) and (ii) are answered in
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
9
]
. In this paper, we focus on (iii). Kowalski, Lau, Soundararajan and Wu
[
6
]
obtained a lower bound for the proportion of newforms
f\in H_{k}^{*}(N)
whose sequence of eigenvalues
\lambda_{f}(p)
has signs coinciding with a prescribed sequence
\{\varepsilon_{p}\}
:

Theorem 1.1

(Kowalski, Lau, Soundararajan, Wu, 2010)
.

Let
N
be a squarefree number,
k\geqslant 2
an even integer, and
\{\varepsilon_{p}\}
a sequence of signs. Then, for any
0<\varepsilon<\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{2}$}}
, there exists some
c>0
such that

\frac{1}{|H_{k}^{*}(N)|}\#\{f\in H_{k}^{*}(N)\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;\sgn\lambda_{f}(p)=\varepsilon_{p}\mbox{ for }p\leqslant z,\;p\nmid N\}\geqslant\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\Big)^{\pi(z)}

for
z=c\sqrt{\log{kN}\log\log{kN}}
provided
kN
is large enough. Here
\pi(z)
is the number of primes less than or equal to
z
.

Now, let
\chi_{1}
,
\chi_{2}
be Dirichlet characters modulo
N_{1},N_{2}
and for an integer
k>2
, define the following variant of the sum of divisors function:

(1.1)

\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(n)=\sum_{d|n}\chi_{1}\big(\frac{n}{d}\big)\chi_{2}(d)d^{k-1}.

Now assume that
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are not simultaneously principal
\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 1)
. It is well known (see, for example,
[
3
]
) that if
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}(-1)=(-1)^{k}
, then the function

E_{k}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2},z)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\delta(\chi_{1})}{2}L(1-k,\chi_{2})+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(n)q^{n},

is an Eisenstein series of weight
k
, level
N_{1}N_{2}
and character
\chi_{1}\chi_{2}
. Here
q=\me^{2\pi iz}
and

\delta(\chi_{1})=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }\chi_{1}\mbox{ is principal}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In 1977, Weisinger
[
11
]
developed a newform theory for
E_{k}(N,\chi)
analogous to the one developed by Atkin and Lehner
[
1
]
for cusp forms. In this theory, we have:

•

The newforms of
E_{k}(N,\chi)
are functions of the form
E_{k}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2},z)
for which
N=N_{1}N_{2}
,
\chi=\chi_{1}\chi_{2}
, and
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
are primitive.

•

The eigenvalue of
E_{k}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2},z)
with respect to the Hecke operator
T_{p}
is
\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(p)
. In other words, the eigenvalues of this type of Eisenstein series coincide with its Fourier coefficients.

By exploiting the analytical properties of
\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(n)
, Linowitz and Thompson
[
8
]
answered the three questions mentioned at the beginning of this article for Eisenstein series newforms.

Note that by (
1.1
),
\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(n)\in\mathbb{R}
when
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
are real characters. Since we want
E_{k}(\chi_{1},\chi_{2},z)
to be an Eisenstein series, we exclude the case when
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are principal. We call these types of characters
quadratic
because for every fundamental discriminant
D
, i.e., for each discriminant arising from a quadratic number field, we can associate a real character defined by
\chi_{D}(m)=\big(\frac{D}{m}\big)
. Therefore, counting Eisenstein series newforms of level
N\leqslant x
is equivalent to counting fundamental discriminants
D_{1},D_{2}
with
|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x
. Let

\mathscr{D}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\{(D_{1},D_{2})\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\}.

Taking all of these facts into consideration, Linowitz and Thompson
[
8
]
showed:

Theorem 1.2

(Linowitz, Thompson, 2015)
.

Let
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
be a sequence of primes and
\{\varepsilon_{p_{1}},\ldots,\varepsilon_{p_{k}}\}\in\{-1,0,1\}
a sequence of signs. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathscr{D}|}\#\{(D_{1},D_{2})\in\mathscr{D}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;

\displaystyle{}\sgn\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(p_{i})=\varepsilon_{p_{i}},\;1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}

\displaystyle{}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon_{p_{i}}=0\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(p_{i}+1)^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon_{p_{i}}\neq 0\\
1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}\frac{p_{i}(p_{i}+2)}{2(p_{i}+1)^{2}}\cdot

Now, let
\eta(D_{1},D_{2})
represent the smallest prime
p
such that
\sgn(\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(p))=-1
. Linowitz and Thompson
[
8
]
then conjectured:

Conjecture 1.1
.

We have

\frac{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta(D_{1},D_{2})}{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}1}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\theta,

where

(1.2)

\theta\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}^{2}(p_{k}+2)}{2(p_{k}+1)^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{2+p_{j}(p_{j}+2)}{2(p_{j}+1)^{2}}\approx 3.9750223902\ldots

They gave a heuristic argument as evidence towards their conjecture, showing:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta(D_{1},D_{2})}{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}1}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}{}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\,\mbox{Prob}(\eta(D_{1},D_{2})=p_{k})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\,\mbox{Prob}(\varepsilon_{p_{k}}=-1)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\mbox{Prob}(\varepsilon_{p_{i}}=0\mbox{ or }1)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}^{2}(p_{k}+2)}{2(p_{k}+1)^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\bigg(\frac{1}{(p_{i}+1)^{2}}+\frac{p_{i}(p_{i}+2)}{2(p_{i}+1)^{2}}\bigg),

where the last equality follows from Theorem
1.2
. The problem with this argument is that Theorem
1.2
fixes a set of primes and then lets
x\to\infty
. In this argument we need to allow the primes to tend to infinity with
x
. The authors stated: “[W]e have a good understanding of the effect of the small primes, but one would need to argue that the primes after some cutoff point do not make much of an impact on the average. Presumably, this would require using the large sieve”.

The goal of the present article is to correct their conjecture by proving the following result:

Theorem 1.3
.

We have

\frac{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta(D_{1},D_{2})}{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}1}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}{}\Theta\cdot(1-\beta)+\alpha,

where

\Theta=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2(p_{k}+1)}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)},

\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2(p_{k}+1)^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)},

and

\beta=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{2(p_{k}+1)^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)}\cdot

Numerically,

\Theta\cdot(1-\beta)+\alpha\approx 4.63255603509332\ldots

The numerical computation was done using Sage. We used RIF for interval arithmetic and we truncated at
k=1000
.

2.
Main Tools

First we will need asymptotic estimates for some sets of fundamental discriminants. It is well known (see, for example,
[
2
]
) that

(2.1)

\sum_{|D|\leqslant x}1\sim\frac{x}{\zeta(2)},

where
D
runs over all fundamental discriminants with
|D|\leqslant x
. Here
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function. Now, let
n_{1}(m)
be the smallest integer
n\geqslant 1
relatively prime to
m
such that the congruence
x^{2}\equiv n\ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)
has no solutions. Even though Vinogradov’s conjecture remains open, it is possible to show that large values of
n_{1}(p)
are rare. More specifically, using the large sieve, Linnik
[
7
]
showed that for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\#\{p\leqslant x\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;n_{1}(p)>x^{\varepsilon}\}\ll_{\varepsilon}1.

Using similar ideas to the ones from Linnik’s paper, Erdős
[
4
]
obtained a result concerning the average of
n_{1}(p)
as
p
varies over prime numbers less than or equal to
x
:

(2.2)

\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{p\leqslant x}n_{1}(p)\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{2^{k}},

where
p_{k}
is the
k
th prime and
\pi(x)
is the prime counting function. In a similar fashion, Pollack
[
10
]
considered a variation of (
2.2
). We summarize his result in the following theorem:

Theorem 2.1

(Pollack, 2012)
.

For each fundamental discriminant
D
, let
\chi_{D}
be the associated Dirichlet character, i.e.,
\chi_{D}(m)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\big(\frac{D}{m}\big)
. For each character
\chi
, let
n_{\chi}
denote the least
n
for which
\chi(n)\notin\{0,1\}
. Finally, let
n(D)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=n_{\chi_{D}}
. Then

(1)

Uniformly in
k
such that the
k
th prime satisfies
p_{k}\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}
, we have

\#\{|D|\leqslant x\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;n(D)=p_{k}\}=\frac{p_{k}}{2(p_{k}+1)}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)}\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}+O(x^{\frac{2}{3}}).

(2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D|\leqslant x\\
n(D)>(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}n(D)=o(x).

Therefore, using (
2.1
), we have

(2.3)

\frac{\sum_{|D|\leqslant x}n(D)}{\sum_{|D|\leqslant x}1}\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\Theta,

where

\Theta\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2(p_{k}+1)}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)}\approx 4.9809473396\ldots

We will also need the following lemma from Linowitz and Thompson
[
8
]
:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon,p)
denote the proportion of fundamental discriminants
D
with
\big(\tfrac{D}{p}\big)=\varepsilon
. Then, we have

\mathbb{P}(\varepsilon,p)=\begin{cases}\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{p}{2p+2}$}},&\mbox{if }\varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}\\
\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{p+1}$}},&\mbox{if }\varepsilon=0.\end{cases}

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Let
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
be Dirichlet characters associated with the fundamental discriminants
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
. For a prime
p
,

\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(p)=\sum_{d|p}\chi_{1}\big(\frac{p}{d}\big)\chi_{2}(d)d^{k-1}=\chi_{1}(p)+\chi_{2}(p)p^{k-1},

so that

(3.1)

\sgn{\sigma_{\chi_{1},\chi_{2}}^{k-1}(p)}=\begin{cases}\chi_{1}(p),&\mbox{if }p|D_{2}\\
\chi_{2}(p),&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By (
2.1
),

\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}1=\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\sum_{D_{2}\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{1}|}}1\sim\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{1}{|D_{1}|}\cdot

Let
A(x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{|D|\leqslant x}1
and
f(x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{x}$}}
. Since
A(x)\sim\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{x}{\ze(2)}$}}
, then by partial summation

\displaystyle\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{1}{|D_{1}|}={}

\displaystyle A(x)f(x)-A(1)f(1)-\int_{1}^{x}A(t)f^{\prime}(t)\dif t

\displaystyle\sim{}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}-1+\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\dif t}{\zeta(2)t}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sim{}

\displaystyle\frac{\log{x}}{\zeta(2)}\cdot

Hence

(3.3)

\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}1\sim\frac{x\log{x}}{\zeta(2)^{2}}\cdot

Now let us estimate the numerator. For the sake of simplicity, let
\eta\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\eta(D_{1},D_{2})
. Then,

\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}\eta+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta\nmid D_{2}\end{subarray}}\eta.

If
\eta|D_{2}
, then by (
3.1
),
\eta
is the smallest prime
p
such that
\chi_{1}(p)\notin\{0,1\}
, and with the notation of Theorem
2.1
, this means that
\eta=n(D_{1})
. Similarly, if
\eta\nmid D_{2}
, then
\eta=n(D_{2})
. Therefore,

\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{1})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta\nmid D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{2}).

Now,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta\nmid D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{2})=\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}n(D_{2})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{2}),

so that

(3.4)

\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta=\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}n(D_{2})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{1})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{2}).

By (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}n(D_{2})=

\displaystyle{}\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\sum_{|D_{2}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{1}|}}n(D_{2})

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle{}\Theta\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{1}{|D_{1}|}

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle{}\Theta\frac{x\log{x}}{\zeta(2)^{2}},

where the final estimate follows from (
3.2
). Now, by Lemma
2.1
, the proportion of fundamental discriminants such that
p|D
is
\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{p+1}$}}\cdot
Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{1})={}

\displaystyle\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}n(D_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{2}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{1}|}\ \\
n(D_{1})|D_{2}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{n(D_{1})}{n(D_{1})+1}\sum_{|D_{2}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{1}|}}1

\displaystyle\sim{}

\displaystyle\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{n(D_{1})}{|D_{1}|(n(D_{1})+1)}\cdot

To find an asymptotic for the last sum we again use partial summation. Let

B(x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{n(D_{1})}{n(D_{1})+1}\cdot

Then, by (i) of Theorem
2.1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant x\\
n(D_{1})\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{n(D_{1})}{n(D_{1})+1}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p_{k}\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{p_{k}+1}\#\{|D_{1}|\leqslant x\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;n(D_{1})=p_{k}\}

\displaystyle\sim{}

\displaystyle\alpha\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}

where

\alpha=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}^{2}}{2(p_{k}+1)^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)}\cdot

Now, by (ii) of Theorem
2.1
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant x\\
n(D_{1})>(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{n(D_{1})}{n(D_{1})+1}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant x\\
n(D_{1})>(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}n(D_{1})=o(x).

Hence,

B(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant x\\
n(D_{1})\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{n(D_{1})}{n(D_{1})+1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant x\\
n(D_{1})>(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{n(D_{1})}{n(D_{1})+1}\sim\alpha\frac{x}{\zeta(2)}\cdot

Therefore,

\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant x}\frac{n(D_{1})}{|D_{1}|(n(D_{1})+1)}=B(x)f(x)-B(1)f(1)-\int_{1}^{x}B(t)f^{\prime}(t)\dif t\sim\alpha\frac{\log{x}}{\zeta(2)},

so that

(3.6)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{1})\sim\alpha\frac{x\log{x}}{\zeta(2)^{2}}\cdot

Finally,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{2})=\sum_{|D_{2}|\leqslant x}n(D_{2})\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}}\frac{1}{n(D_{1})+1}\cdot

To get an estimate for the inner sum, let

C(x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.03334pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}}\frac{1}{n(D_{1})+1}\cdot

Then, by (i) of Theorem
2.1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}\\
n(D_{1})\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n(D_{1})+1}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
p_{k}\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p_{k}+1}\#\{|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}\;\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\;n(D_{1})=p_{k}\}

\displaystyle\sim{}

\displaystyle\beta\frac{x}{\zeta(2)|D_{2}|}

where

\beta=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{2(p_{k}+1)^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{j}+2}{2(p_{j}+1)}\cdot

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}\\
n(D_{1})>(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n(D_{1})+1}\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\sum_{|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}}\frac{1}{(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}+1}

\displaystyle\sim{}

\displaystyle\frac{x}{|D_{2}|\zeta(2)((\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}+1)}

\displaystyle\leqslant{}

\displaystyle\frac{x}{(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}+1}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle o(x).

Hence,

C(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}\\
n(D_{1})\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n(D_{1})+1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}|\leqslant\frac{x}{|D_{2}|}\\
n(D_{1})>(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n(D_{1})+1}\sim\beta\frac{x}{\zeta(2)|D_{2}|}\cdot

From this we see that

(3.7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x\\
\eta|D_{2}\end{subarray}}n(D_{2})\sim\sum_{|D_{2}|\leqslant x}\beta\frac{n(D_{2})x}{\zeta(2)|D_{2}|}\sim\Theta\beta\frac{x\log{x}}{\zeta(2)^{2}},

where the last estimate follows from partial summation and applying Theorem
2.1
. Therefore, plugging (
3.5
), (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) into (
3.4
) shows that

\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta\sim(\Theta+\alpha-\Theta\beta)\frac{x\log{x}}{\zeta(2)^{2}}\cdot

This together with (
3.3
) completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

We can give the following explanation of why Linowitz and Thompson’s Conjecture
1.1
was slightly off from the correct number: the result from Theorem
1.2
is not uniform in
k
for the choice of the
p_{k}
(we fix a set of primes beforehand), while the result from Theorem
2.1
is uniform in
k
satisfying
p_{k}\leqslant(\log{x})^{\frac{1}{3}}
. In order to make Linowitz and Thompson’s heuristic argument rigorous we would first need to show that Theorem
1.2
holds uniformly in
k
such that
p_{k}\leqslant f(x)
for some function
f
with
f(x)\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}\infty
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}\eta(D_{1},D_{2})}{\sum_{|D_{1}D_{2}|\leqslant x}1}=\sum_{p_{k}\leqslant f(x)}{}

\displaystyle p_{k}\,\mbox{Prob}(\eta(D_{1},D_{2})=p_{k})

\displaystyle+\sum_{p_{k}>f(x)}p_{k}\,\mbox{Prob}(\eta(D_{1},D_{2})=p_{k})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}{}

\displaystyle\theta+\mu,

where
\theta
is the conjectured constant (
1.2
) and

\mu=\lim_{x\to\infty}\sum_{p_{k}>f(x)}p_{k}\,\mbox{Prob}(\eta(D_{1},D_{2})=p_{k}).

Linowitz and Thompson conjectured that
\mu=0
, but according to Theorem
1.3
,
\mu
does make a small contribution.
