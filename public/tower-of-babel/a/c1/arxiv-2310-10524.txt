Abstract
. In this paper, we present a novel second-order generalised rotational discrete gradient scheme for numerically approximating the orthonormal frame gradient flow of biaxial nematic liquid crystals. This scheme relies on reformulating the original gradient flow system into an equivalent generalised “rotational” form. A second-order discrete gradient approximation of the energy variation is then devised such that it satisfies an energy difference relation. The proposed numerical scheme has two remarkable properties: (i) it strictly obeys the orthonormal property of the tensor field and (ii) it satisfies the energy dissipation law at the discrete level, regardless of the time step sizes. We provide ample numerical results to validate the accuracy, efficiency, unconditional stability and S ​ O ​ ( 3 ) SO(3) -preserving property of this scheme. In addition, comparisons of the simulation results between the biaxial orthonormal frame gradient flow model and uniaxial Oseen-Frank gradient flow are made to demonstrate the ability of the former to characterize non-axisymmetric local anisotropy.

In this work, we focus on the numerical approximation of frame models.
Let us denote the orthonormal frame field as
\mathbf{p}=\left(\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\bm{n}_{3}\right)\in SO(3)
.
The full biaxial frame hydrodynamics is a coupling system between
\mathbf{p}
and the fluid velocity field
\bm{v}
.
Here, we consider a simplification of small velocity by assuming that
\bm{v}=0
.
In this case, the frame dynamics is written as

\displaystyle\chi_{1}\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{2}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{3}+\mathscr{L}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{Bi}=0,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{3}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}+\mathscr{L}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{Bi}=0,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\chi_{3}\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{1}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}+\mathscr{L}_{3}\mathcal{F}_{Bi}=0,

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=\left(\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\bm{n}_{3}\right)\in SO(3).

(1.1d)

In the above,

\mathscr{L}_{i},\,i=1,2,3
are the (variational) differential operators of infinitesimal rotations, defined as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathscr{L}_{1}:=\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{n}_{2}}}-\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{n}_{3}}},\\[4.0pt]
\mathscr{L}_{2}:=\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{n}_{3}}}-\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{n}_{1}}},\\[4.0pt]
\mathscr{L}_{3}:=\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{n}_{1}}}-\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta{\bm{n}_{2}}}.\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

The biaxial orientational elasticity,

\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]=\int f_{Bi}(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

(1.3)

takes the form

\displaystyle f_{Bi}(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big\{K_{1}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2}+K_{2}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\right)^{2}+K_{3}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{3}\right)^{2}

(1.4)

\displaystyle+K_{4}\left(\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2}+K_{5}\left(\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{2}\right)^{2}+K_{6}\left(\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{3}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+K_{7}\left(\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2}+K_{8}\left(\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{2}\right)^{2}+K_{9}\left(\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{3}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+K_{10}\left(\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2}+K_{11}\left(\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{2}\right)^{2}+K_{12}\left(\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{3}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\gamma_{1}\nabla\cdot\big[\left(\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{n}_{1}-\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\right)\bm{n}_{1}\big]+\gamma_{2}\nabla\cdot\big[\left(\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{n}_{2}-\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\right)\bm{n}_{2}\big]

\displaystyle+\gamma_{3}\nabla\cdot\big[\left(\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{n}_{3}-\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{3}\right)\bm{n}_{3}\big]\Big\}.

The three
\gamma_{i}
terms can be rewritten as surface integrals that vanish under suitable boundary conditions.
The coefficients in the elastic energy, as well as three diffusion coefficients
\chi_{i}
, can be calculated from molecular parameters
[
20
,
44
]
.
We will demonstrate that system (
1.1
) can be viewed as a constrained gradient flow for the biaxial elastic energy (
1.3
) later.

To the knowledge of the authors, there is no attempt to numerically solve the above system, maybe due to several challenges.
First of all, it is a highly nonlinear and coupled system involving three unknown variables
\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\bm{n}_{3}
and six point-wise constraints. The nonlinearity and coupling come from both the nonlinear terms in the energy variation of the biaxial elastic energy functional and the explicit orthonormality constraint. A possible way to eliminate the constraint is to reparameterize the local frame
\mathbf{p}(\bm{x},t)
in terms of Euler angles
\theta(\bm{x},t)
,
\phi(\bm{x},t)
and
\psi(\bm{x},t)
,

\mathbf{p}(\bm{x},t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\cos\phi\cos\psi-\sin\phi\sin\psi&-\cos\theta\cos\phi\sin\psi-\sin\phi\cos\psi&\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
\cos\theta\sin\phi\cos\psi+\cos\phi\sin\psi&-\cos\theta\sin\phi\sin\psi+\cos\phi\cos\psi&\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
-\sin\theta\cos\psi&\sin\theta\sin\psi&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

Actually, earlier work writes down the model as PDEs w.r.t. the three Euler angles
[
14
]
.
However, the free energy and its derivatives then turn into expressions extremely complicated.
Secondly, suggested by the derivation from molecular parameters
[
44
]
, the elastic coefficients in the free energy density (
1.4
) can become highly disparate with the change of molecular architecture,
leading to highly anisotropic derivative terms that are difficult to compute numerically.
This requires efficient and robust numerical methods which can deal with highly anisotropic derivatives, strong nonlinearity and coupling.
Last, and most importantly, this system obeys two fundamental physical properties: the point-wise orthonormality constraint of
\mathbf{p}(\bm{x},t)
and the energy dissipation law.
It is highly desirable for a numerical method to preserve these two properties simultaneously at the discrete level.
On one hand, the form of biaxial elastic energy density
f_{Bi}(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p})
relies upon the assumption that
\mathbf{p}\in SO(3)
, and a violation of this constraint may lead to unpredictable computational results that are not characterized by biaxial elastic energy.
On the other hand, a naive correction or projection of
\mathbf{p}
onto
SO(3)
may induce numerical instability and cause energy of the gradient flow system to increase unphysically.

The aim of this paper is to propose an efficient and robust numerical scheme for the orthonormal frame gradient flow system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) that can preserve the properties of orthonormality and energy dissipative law simultaneously at the discrete level. Among these two properties, preservation of the orthonormality constraint discretely plays a crucial role in producing physically meaningful simulation results.
If focusing on this sole target, one may be tempted to use Euler angles.
However, it is tricky to deal with the discontinuity across periods for these Euler angles and the energy functional becomes too cumbersome to design unconditionally energy-stable schemes based upon this formulation.
Another possible technique is to introduce Lagrange multipliers to reformulate the
SO(3)
-constrained gradient flow system into a saddle point problem
[
38
]
.
But systems of this kind are generally harder to solve numerically.
Meanwhile, since (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) is a dissipative system, it is favorable to solve it using energy-stable schemes.
Classical method for developing energy stable schemes include, but not limited to convex splitting
[
10
,
11
]
, linear stablization
[
7
,
32
]
, and auxiliary variable methods such as IEQ
[
46
,
48
]
and SAV
[
30
,
31
,
47
]
.
However, preserving energy dissipation and orthonormal constraint simultaneously using classical methods is a highly nontrivial task. This challenge necessitates the development of new numerical methods.

In this paper, we propose a second-order unconditionally energy-stable and
SO(3)
-preserving generalised rotational discrete gradient (gRdg) scheme for the orthonormal frame gradient flow system of biaxial nematic liquid crystal. Two key steps are essential for preserving these properties at the same time. One is to reformulate the gradient flow system with orthonormality constraint into an equivalent system, which can be viewed as a generalised “rotational” form. The other is to develop a discrete gradient approximation for the variational derivative of biaxial elastic energy functional such that it satisfies a certain energy difference relation.
These two ingredients, together with a time-centered Crank-Nicolson scheme, constitute the proposed method.
We present rigorous proof that the gRdg scheme strictly preserves the orthonormality constraint and satisfies a discrete energy dissipative law.
An inexact Newton-Krylov iterative method is employed to solve the nonlinear equation resulted from the gRdg method.
We also propose a time-adaptive strategy to the proposed scheme, which proves to be quite efficient for accelerating the nonlinear solution algorithm by ample numerical experiments.
We examine the evolution of the orthonormal frames with various elastic parameters, compare them with those obtained from Oseen-Frank gradient flows of uniaxial nematics, and report a few featured evolution patterns of biaxial nematic phases.
In particular, we investigate the dynamics with scale-different elastic coefficients obtained from molecular parameters.
In simulating these challenging numerical examples, the gRdg scheme demonstrates its robustness and accuracy, and intriguing dynamics is revealed that could draw further research interests.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we reformulate the original orthonormal frame gradient flow system with explicit orthonormality constraint into an equivalent rotational form. We then present the gRdg scheme for temporal discretization of the reformulated system, and develop a discrete gradient approximation for the variation derivative of biaxial elastic energy satisfying the energy difference relation.
We rigorously prove that the gRdg scheme is strictly
SO(3)
-preserving and unconditionally energy-stable.
Techniques for implementation, including the iterative solver and time-adaptive strategy are also discussed.
In Section
3
, we conduct ample numerical experiments of orthonormal frame gradient flow with various elastic coefficients to demonstrate the accuracy, efficiency, exact orthonormality preservation and energy stability of gRdg method.
Concluding remarks are given in Section
4
.

2.
The generalised rotational discrete gradient Method

2.1.
Notations

We briefly introduce some notations and conventions that will be used throughout the paper. We use
\bm{n}=(n_{i})
and
\mathbf{p}=(p_{ij})
to denote vectors and matrices respectively.
Define

SO(3):=\big\{\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\big|\Lambda^{\intercal}\Lambda=\mathbf{I},\;{\rm det}(\Lambda)=1\big\},\qquad so(3):=\big\{\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\big|\Lambda^{\intercal}+\Lambda=\mathbf{0}\big\},

(2.1)

are the set of noncommutative Lie group of orthonormal frames and the set of all skew symmetric tensors, respectively. The tangential space of
\mathbf{p}=(\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\bm{n}_{3})\in SO(3)
is given by

T_{\mathbf{p}}SO(3):=\left\{{\mathbf{\Theta}}_{\mathbf{p}}:=\mathbf{p}{\mathbf{\Theta}},\;\forall\mathbf{\Theta}\in so(3)\right\},

(2.2)

for which we choose a basis as

\mathbf{V}_{1}=\left(0,\bm{n}_{3},-\bm{n}_{2}\right),\quad\mathbf{V}_{2}=\left(-\bm{n}_{3},0,\bm{n}_{1}\right),\quad\mathbf{V}_{3}=\left(\bm{n}_{2},-\bm{n}_{1},0\right).

(2.3)

A brief derivation for the expression of
T_{\mathbf{p}}SO(3)
is provided in Appendix
A
. For further details on
SO(3)
group, we refer readers to
[
9
, p. 181-194]
.

2.2.

Proposition 2.1
.

The system (
1.1
) is equivalent to the following generalised rotational form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{p}}{\partial t}=\mathbf{p}\mathbf{A},

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=(\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\bm{n}_{3})\in SO(3),

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{\chi_{3}}\mathscr{L}_{3}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]&-\frac{1}{\chi_{2}}\mathscr{L}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]\\
-\frac{1}{\chi_{3}}\mathscr{L}_{3}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]&0&\frac{1}{\chi_{1}}\mathscr{L}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]\\
\frac{1}{\chi_{2}}\mathscr{L}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]&-\frac{1}{\chi_{1}}\mathscr{L}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.5)

Proof.

For

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{1}}{\partial t}=\big(\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{1}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\big)\bm{n}_{2}+\big(\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{1}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{3}\big)\bm{n}_{3},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{2}}{\partial t}=\big(\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{2}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\big)\bm{n}_{1}+\big(\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{2}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{3}\big)\bm{n}_{3},

(2.6b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{3}}{\partial t}=\big(\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{3}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{1}\big)\bm{n}_{1}+\big(\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{3}}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}_{2}\big)\bm{n}_{2},

(2.6c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\bm{n}_{j})}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{i}}{\partial t}\cdot{\bm{n}}_{j}+\frac{\partial\bm{n}_{j}}{\partial t}\cdot{\bm{n}}_{i}=0.

(2.6d)

By replacing

terms on the right hand side of equations (

)-(

1.1

On the other hand, by taking dot product respectively between
{\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{2}},{\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{3}},{\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{1}}
and
\bm{n}_{3},\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2}
in equation (
2.4

1.1

∎

Theorem 2.1

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\Big)\cdot\Big(\bm{\hat{\chi}}\odot(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\Big)\cdot\Big(\bm{\hat{\chi}}\odot(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle+\Big(\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\Big)^{\intercal}\cdot\Big(\bm{\hat{\chi}}\odot(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)^{\intercal}dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\Big)\cdot\Big(\bm{\hat{\chi}}\odot(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)

\displaystyle-\Big(\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\Big)^{\intercal}\cdot\Big(\bm{\hat{\chi}}\odot(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)\cdot\Big(\bm{\hat{\chi}}\odot(\mathcal{S}\big[\mathbf{p}^{\intercal}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big]\Big)dV.

Since
\hat{\chi}
is symmetric and every entry of it is negative, it is direct to verify that
\mathbf{A}\cdot\hat{\chi}\odot\mathbf{A}\leq 0
for arbitrary
\mathbf{A}
, thus right-hand side of equation (
2.16
) is guaranteed to be non-positive, which ends the proof.
∎

2.3.
An energy-stable and
SO(3)
-preserving scheme

With the help of the generalised rotational form (
2.4
), we shall propose a second-order unconditionally energy-stable and
SO(3)
-preserving scheme. Let
\tau
be the time step, and the superscript
n
is used to represent variables at
t=n\tau
.

The gRdg scheme takes the form

\frac{\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n}}{\tau}=\mathbf{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(2.17)

with
\mathbf{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}:={(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}+\mathbf{p}^{n})}/{2}
and

A^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{1}{\chi_{3}}(\mathscr{L}_{3}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}&-\frac{1}{\chi_{2}}(\mathscr{L}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\\
-\frac{1}{\chi_{3}}(\mathscr{L}_{3}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}&0&\frac{1}{\chi_{1}}(\mathscr{L}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\\
\frac{1}{\chi_{2}}(\mathscr{L}_{2}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}&-\frac{1}{\chi_{1}}(\mathscr{L}_{1}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.18)

Let us first show that the above scheme is
SO(3)
-preserving.
From equation (
2.17
), we have

\mathbf{p}^{n+1}\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)=\mathbf{p}^{n}\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big).

(2.19)

For
\mathbf{p}^{n}\in SO(3)
, since
\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is
3\times 3
and skew-symmetric, its eigenvalues are
\lambda,-\lambda,0
for
\lambda
being pure imaginary. Thus, it is direct to obtain that
\mathbf{I}\pm({\tau}/2)\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is invertible and
{\rm det}\big(\mathbf{I}\pm({\tau}/{2})\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)>0.

Taking right multiplication of
\big(\mathbf{I}-({\tau}/{2})\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)^{-1}
on equation (
2.19
), one obtains

\mathbf{p}^{n+1}=\mathbf{p}^{n}\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}.

(2.20)

Again, using the fact that
\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is skew-symmetric, one can take transpose of equation (
2.20
) to obtain

\displaystyle(\mathbf{p}^{n+1})^{\intercal}=

\displaystyle\Big[\mathbf{p}^{n}\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}\Big]^{\intercal}

(2.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)(\mathbf{p}^{n})^{\intercal}.

Combining equations (
2.20
) and (
2.21
), we arrive at

\displaystyle(\mathbf{p}^{n+1})^{\intercal}\mathbf{p}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)(\mathbf{p}^{n})^{\intercal}\mathbf{p}^{n}\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}\Big(\mathbf{I}+\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbf{I}-\frac{\tau}{2}\mathbf{A}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\Big)^{-1}=\mathbf{I},

where the fact that
\mathbf{p}^{n}\in SO(3)
and the identity
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A})=\big(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{A})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}\big)
have been used. Moreover, from the above equation, we have
\text{det}(\mathbf{p}^{n+1})=\pm 1
. Together with equation (
2.20
) and
\text{det}(\mathbf{p}^{n})=1
, we can deduce
\text{det}(\mathbf{p}^{n+1})=1
. Thus, we conclude
\mathbf{p}^{n+1}\in SO(3)
.

Now we can focus on the design of
\mathbf{A}^{n+1/2}
such that the proposed gRdg scheme satisfies the discrete energy dissipation law. The
(\mathscr{L}_{i}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}},\;i=1,2,3
terms in equation (
2.18
) are discretized by

(\mathscr{L}_{i}\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}])^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbf{V}_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\left.D_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\right|^{n+\frac{1}{2}},

(2.23)

with
\mathbf{V}_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=({\mathbf{V}_{i}^{n}+\mathbf{V}_{i}^{n+1}})/{2}
and
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is a second-order approximation of
{\delta\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]}/{\delta\mathbf{p}}
satisfying the energy difference relation

\int_{\Omega}D_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV=\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}^{n}].

(2.24)

One can take the inner product between equation (
2.17
) and
D_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
and utilizing the energy difference relation (
2.24
) to obtain the desired energy dissipation law by exactly the same procedure as the continuous case. Hence, it suffices to construct the discrete gradient satisfying the discrete difference relation (
2.24
).

2.4.
The biaxial discrete gradient

In what follows, we shall propose a second-order discrete gradient approximation of
{\delta\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}]}/{\delta\mathbf{p}}
, which satisfies the energy difference relation (
2.24
).
To fix the idea, we assume periodic boundary conditions, and will explain how to deal with non-periodic boundary conditions afterwards.
In this case, the three
\gamma
terms vanish after integrated, so that
\gamma_{i}
can be arbitrarily chosen.
To ensure that
\mathcal{F}_{Bi}[\mathbf{p}]
is bounded from below, the twelve elastic coefficients
K_{i}
are assumed positive.
We begin with reformulating the biaxial energy density function into an equivalent form to fully utilize its structure.

Proposition 2.2
.

The biaxial elasticity
f_{Bi}(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p})
can be reformulated as

f_{Bi}(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\left|\nabla\bm{n}_{i}\right|^{2}+W(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p}),

(2.25)

where

\displaystyle W(\mathbf{p},\nabla\mathbf{p})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}\right)^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\left(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}\right)^{2}\Big),

(2.26)

and the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\min\left\{K_{1},K_{4},K_{7},K_{10}\right\}>0,\gamma_{2}=\min\left\{K_{2},K_{5},K_{8},K_{11}\right\}>0

\displaystyle\gamma_{3}=\min\left\{K_{3},K_{6},K_{9},K_{12}\right\}>0

\displaystyle k_{1}=K_{1}-\gamma_{1},\quad k_{2}=K_{2}-\gamma_{2},\quad k_{3}=K_{3}-\gamma_{3}

\displaystyle k_{11}=K_{4}-\gamma_{1},\quad k_{22}=K_{5}-\gamma_{2},\quad k_{33}=K_{6}-\gamma_{3},

\displaystyle k_{31}=K_{7}-\gamma_{1},\quad k_{12}=K_{8}-\gamma_{2},\quad k_{23}=K_{9}-\gamma_{3},

\displaystyle k_{21}=K_{10}-\gamma_{1},\quad k_{32}=K_{11}-\gamma_{2},\quad k_{13}=K_{12}-\gamma_{3}.

\displaystyle k_{i}\geq 0,k_{ij}\geq 0\quad\quad(i,j=1,2,3).

(2.27)

Proof.

Under periodic boundary conditions, the last three term of (
1.4
) do not contribute to the gradient, so we can just let
\gamma_{i}
take the value in (
2.2
).
Since
\mathbf{p}=(\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\bm{n}_{3})^{\intercal}\in SO(3)
, one has

\displaystyle\left|\bm{n}_{1}\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{1}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2}+\left(\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{1}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\left|\bm{n}_{2}\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{2}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\bm{n}_{3}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{2}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\left|\bm{n}_{3}\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{3}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{n}_{1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{3}\right)^{2}+\left(\bm{n}_{2}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{3}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\bm{n}_{i}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}\right)^{2}+\left(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{i}\right)^{2}+\left|\bm{n}_{i}\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{i}\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\nabla\cdot\big[\left(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\right)\bm{n}_{i}-\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}\right)\bm{n}_{i}\big],\quad i=1,2,3.

(2.28)

Inserting the above identities into equation (
1.4
), we arrive at the reformulated form (
2.25
).
∎

With the help of the reformulated form (
2.25
) and under periodic boundary conditions, one has the following form of energy variation

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}]}{\delta\bm{n}_{i}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{i}\Delta\bm{n}_{i}-k_{i}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ji}\nabla\times\big[\left(\bm{n}_{j}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{i}\right)\bm{n}_{j}\big]

(2.29)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\left(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}\right)\left(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}\right),\quad i=1,2,3.

Now, we are ready to propose the second-order discrete gradient approximation for the energy variation in equation (
2.29
).

Proposition 2.3
.

Define

D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\left(D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|_{1}^{n+\frac{1}{2}},D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|_{2}^{n+\frac{1}{2}},D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|_{3}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right),

(2.30)

where
D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is given by

\displaystyle D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=

\displaystyle-\gamma_{i}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2})-k_{i}\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2})+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ji}\nabla\times\left(\beta_{ji}^{n+1/2}\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)

(2.31)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}\left(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right),

with
\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}
take the form

\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}=\big(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+1}+\bm{n}_{i}^{n}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n}\big)/2.

(2.32)

The approximation
D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
serves as a second-order discrete gradient approximation of the biaxial elastic energy variation
{\delta\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}]}/{\delta\mathbf{p}}
in equation (
2.29
) and it satisfies the energy difference relation

\int_{\Omega}D_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV=\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}^{n}].

Proof.

Taking no account of boundary terms, the biaxial elasticity can be divided into three parts:

\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{1}[\mathbf{p}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}[\mathbf{p}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{F}_{3}[\mathbf{p}],

(2.33)

and these three parts are

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}[\mathbf{p}]=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\bm{n}_{i}\right|^{2}dV,

(2.34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}[\mathbf{p}]=\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}\right)^{2}dV,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{3}[\mathbf{p}]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\int_{\Omega}\left(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}\right)^{2}dV.

For the first term, using integration by parts and the periodic boundary conditions, we can derive that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{1}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{1}[\mathbf{p}^{n}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int(|\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}|^{2}-|\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n}|^{2})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int(\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}+\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n})(\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int\nabla(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2})\nabla(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{i}^{n})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2})\cdot(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{i}^{n})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int 2\left(\gamma_{1}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{1}^{n+1/2}),\gamma_{2}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{2}^{n+1/2}),\gamma_{3}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{3}^{n+1/2})\right)\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV.

Define

D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=-2\left(\gamma_{1}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{1}^{n+1/2}),\gamma_{2}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{2}^{n+1/2}),\gamma_{3}\nabla\cdot(\nabla\bm{n}_{3}^{n+1/2})\right),

(2.35)

and one readily observes that

\int D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}_{1}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV=\mathcal{F}_{1}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{1}[\mathbf{p}^{n}].

(2.36)

For the second term, using integration by parts and the periodic boundary condition, it is direct to calculate that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{2}[\mathbf{p}^{n}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int(|\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}|^{2}-|\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n}|^{2})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}+\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n})(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int\nabla\cdot\big(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2}\big)\nabla\cdot\big(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{i}^{n}\big)dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2}\big)\cdot\big(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{i}^{n}\big)dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int-2\left[k_{1}\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{1}^{n+1/2}\big),k_{2}\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{2}^{n+1/2}\big),k_{3}\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{3}^{n+1/2}\big)\right]\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV.

Hence, we can define

D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=-2\left(k_{1}\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{1}^{n+1/2}\big),k_{2}\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{2}^{n+1/2}\big),k_{3}\nabla\big(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{3}^{n+1/2}\big)\right)

(2.37)

to obtain

\int D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}_{2}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV=\mathcal{F}_{2}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{2}[\mathbf{p}^{n}].

(2.38)

For the third term, we need to resort to the following identity

\displaystyle\big|\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+1}\big|^{2}-\big|\bm{n}_{i}^{n}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n}\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\big((\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{i}^{n})\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+1/2}+\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2}\cdot\nabla\times(\bm{n}_{j}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{j}^{n})\big)\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2},

where the definition of
\beta_{ij}
in equation (
2.32
) has been used.
Thus, we can follow a similar manner as the first two terms to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{3}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{3}[\mathbf{p}^{n}]=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\int\Big(\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+1/2}\Big)\cdot(\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{i}^{n})

(2.39)

\displaystyle+\big(\nabla\times(\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}\bm{n}_{i}^{n+1/2})\big)\cdot(\bm{n}_{j}^{n+1}-\bm{n}_{j}^{n})dV.

As a consequence, we can define the discrete gradient approximation for the last term as

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{F}_{3}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+1/2}=2

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{j1}\nabla\times\big(\beta_{j1}^{n+1/2}\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{1j}\beta_{1j}^{n+1/2}\big(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big),

(2.40)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{j2}\nabla\times\big(\beta_{j2}^{n+1/2}\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{2j}\beta_{2j}^{n+1/2}\big(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big),

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{j3}\nabla\times\big(\beta_{j3}^{n+1/2}\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{3j}\beta_{3j}^{n+1/2}\big(\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\big)\Big),

which satisfies the energy difference relation

\int D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}_{3}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV=\mathcal{F}_{3}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{3}[\mathbf{p}^{n}].

(2.41)

Combining the equations (
2.36
), (
2.38
) and (
2.41
) we can arrive at the discrete energy relation (
2.24
)

Moreover, by comparing the form of the discrete gradient approximation (
2.31
) and the continuous energy variation (
2.29
), one can directly see that the terms except
\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}
in (
2.31
) are second order central difference approximations of the corresponding terms in (
2.29
). Nevertheless, we can directly calculate that

\displaystyle 2\beta_{ij}^{n+1/2}=

\displaystyle\big(\bm{n}_{i}(t_{n+1/2})+\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{i}(t_{n+1/2})\Delta t+O({\Delta t}^{2})\big)\cdot\nabla\times\big(\bm{n}_{j}(t_{n+1/2})+\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{j}(t_{n+1/2})\Delta t+O({\Delta t}^{2})\big)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\big(\bm{n}_{i}(t_{n+1/2})-\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{i}(t_{n+1/2})\Delta t+O({\Delta t}^{2})\big)\cdot\nabla\times\big(\bm{n}_{j}(t_{n+1/2})-\partial_{t}\bm{n}_{j}(t_{n+1/2})\Delta t+O({\Delta t}^{2})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{n}_{i}(t_{n+1/2})\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}(t_{n+1/2})+O({\Delta t}^{2}).

Thus, we conclude that
D^{B}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}
is a second-order discrete gradient approximation of the energy variation
{\delta\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}]}/{\delta\mathbf{p}}
, which ends the proof.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Let us now explain how to deal with non-periodic boundary conditions. Let
\mathbf{p}
perturb along
\delta\mathbf{p}
.
Still, we use the form (
2.25
) as an example.
We can obtain the following variational form:

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}+s\delta\mathbf{p}]-\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}]}{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\bm{n}_{i})\cdot(\nabla\delta\bm{n}_{i})dV+\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i})(\nabla\cdot\delta\bm{n}_{i})dV

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\int_{\Omega}(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j})(\delta\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}+\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\times\delta\bm{n}_{j})dV

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\bm{n}_{i})\cdot(\delta\bm{n}_{i})dV-\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}))\cdot(\delta\bm{n}_{i})dV

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ij}\int_{\Omega}(\bm{n}_{i}\cdot\nabla\bm{n}_{j})\nabla\times\bm{n}_{j}\cdot\delta\bm{n}_{i}dV+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ji}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\times\left(\left(\bm{n}_{j}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{i}\right)\bm{n}_{j}\right)\cdot\delta\bm{n}_{i}\,dV

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\gamma_{i}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\nabla\bm{n}_{i})^{\intercal}\bm{\nu}\cdot(\delta\bm{n}_{i})\,dS+\sum_{i=1}^{3}k_{i}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i})\bm{\nu}\cdot\delta\bm{n}_{i}\,dS

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}k_{ji}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{n}_{j}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{i})\bm{n}_{j}\times\bm{\nu}\cdot\delta\bm{n}_{i}\,dS,

where
\bm{\nu}
is the outward unit normal vector.
We recognize that the volume integrals yield the same terms as in the case of periodic boundary conditions, while the surface integrals might give extra conditions.
When the Dirichlet boundary condition is adopted, we have
\delta\bm{n}_{i}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we can directly to observe that the surface integral vanishes. Since
\delta\bm{n}_{i}
is perpendicular to
\bm{n}_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
, the Neumann boundary condition shall be given by

\bm{n}_{i}\times\big[\gamma_{i}(\nabla\bm{n}_{i})^{\intercal}\bm{\nu}+k_{i}\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ji}(\bm{n}_{j}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{n}_{i})\bm{n}_{j}\times\bm{\nu}\big]=0,\quad i=1,2,3.

(2.42)

We now need to equip the discrete gradient with appropriate approximation of the boundary condition, which turns out to be

\bm{n}_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\times[\gamma_{i}(\nabla\bm{n}_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}})^{\intercal}\bm{\nu}+k_{i}\nabla\cdot\bm{n}_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ji}\beta_{ji}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\bm{n}_{j}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\times\bm{\nu}]=0,\quad i=1,2,3.

(2.43)

If additional boundary energy is present, we use the same way to get the suitable discretization.

Remark 2.2
.

Besides the proposed discrete gradient approximation, other discrete gradient approximations that satisfy the energy difference relation (
2.24
) can be adopted, e.g. the mean-value discrete gradient
[
8
,
16
]
:

D^{M}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}}{\delta\mathbf{p}}\big[(1-s)\mathbf{p}^{n+1}+s\mathbf{p}^{n}\big]ds,

(2.44)

and the Gonzalez discrete gradient
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle D^{G}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathbf{p})\Big|^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}}{\delta\mathbf{p}}[\mathbf{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}]

(2.45)

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}^{n+1}]-\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}[\mathbf{p}^{n}]-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}_{B_{i}}}{\delta\mathbf{p}}[\mathbf{p}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}]\cdot(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n})dV}{\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n}\big)dV}(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n}).

However, these two approximations have their drawbacks. The implementation of the mean-value discrete gradient relies on numerical integration, while it preserves the discrete energy dissipation law up to the numerical quadrature error. Thus, it results in a substantial increase in the computational cost in each iteration when we solve the nonlinear scheme. As for the Gonzalez discrete gradient, it is sensitive to roundoff errors and suffers from convergence issue when the gradient flow system tends to equilibrium (when the denominator
\int_{\Omega}\big(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n}\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf{p}^{n+1}-\mathbf{p}^{n}\big)dV
approaches
0
).

2.5.
Time adaptivity strategy

The proposed discrete scheme is nonlinear and one needs to adopt nonlinear solvers such as inexact Newton-Krylov (INK) method with proper line search technique to solve it efficiently (see more details in
[
17
]
). It is worthwhile to note that due to the sensitivity of convergence of nonlinear solver to initial guess, suitable choice of time step can overcome this issue and improve the accuracy and efficiency of the computation. Moreover, as is shown in numerical experiments in the next section, the evolution of liquid crystal driven by biaxial elastic energy may exhibit multiple stages. It may evolve dramatically in relatively short time and keep stable or evolve slowly for the rest of the time. Thus, it is necessary to adopt appropriate time-adaptivity strategy to make efficient and accurate simulations. Inspired by the method proposed in
[
27
]
, we adopt the following time-adaptive strategy

\tau_{n+1}={\rm max}\bigg(\tau_{\rm min},\frac{\tau_{\rm max}}{\sqrt{1+\alpha|(\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}^{n}]-\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{p}^{n-1}])/\tau_{n}|^{2}}}\bigg),

(2.46)

where
\tau_{n}
is the
n
-th time step,
\alpha
is a constant chosen to adjust the speed of change of the step size, and
\tau_{\rm max}
and
\tau_{\rm min}
are the upper and lower bounds of the time step sizes, respectively.

3.
Representative numerical experiments

In this section, we conduct several numerical experiments to demonstrate the accuracy and efficiency of the proposed gRdg method for orthonormal frame gradient flow system, as well as its ability to preserve the orthonormality constraint and energy dissipation law at the discrete level.
The periodic boundary conditions are assumed, discretized using the Fourier spectral method (
2.15
).
An inexact Newton-Krylov (INK) solver is adopted to solve the resultant nonlinear equation with the stopping tolerance
10^{-8}
.
We first present the spatial and temporal convergence rates using manufactured solutions. Then for a prescribed initial profile of the frame field, we examine the discrete orthonormality error and energy curve as functions of time.
Moreover, we compare the simulation results obtained by biaxial orthonormal frame and uniaxial Oseen-Frank gradient flows for special choice of elastic coefficients such that the biaxial elasticity reduces to the uniaxial Oseen-Frank energy.
Last, we investigate the dynamics of frame field with highly anisotropic elastic coefficients derived from molecular parameters.
In what follows, let us denote the elastic coefficients
\hat{\bm{K}}=\left(K_{1},K_{2},\cdots,K_{12}\right)^{\intercal}
for notational convenience. And without loss of generality, we take

4.
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have developed a novel gRdg scheme for orthonormal frame gradient flow system modeling the quasi-static evolution of biaxial liquid crystals. The proposed scheme is of second-order accuracy, unconditionally energy-stable and
SO(3)
-preserving. Ample numerical experiments demonstrate its efficiency and accuracy and ability to compute highly anisotropic biaxial nematics. Since the orthonormal frame gradient flow system characterizes the dissipation of the frame field in the biaxial hydrodynamic models, the proposed method is expected to be a cornerstone for efficient and robust schemes of these problems.
