One-dimensional half-harmonic maps into the circle and their degree
Ali Hyder, Luca Martinazzi

Abstract
Given a half-harmonic map u ∈ H ˙ 1 2 , 2 ​ ( ℝ , 𝕊 1 ) u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1}) minimizing the fractional Dirichlet energy under Dirichlet boundary conditions in ℝ ∖ I \mathbb{R}\setminus I , we show the existence of a second half-harmonic map, minimizing the fractional Dirichlet energy in a different homotopy class. This is based on the study of the degree of fractional Sobolev maps and a sharp estimate à la Brezis-Coron. We give examples showing that it is in general not possible to minimize in every homotopy class and show a contrast with the 2-dimensional case.

Introduction

Minimizing fractional Sobolev energies of maps between manifolds, as pioneered e.g. in
[
30
]
, leads naturally to the notion of (fractional)
s
-harmonic maps (
0<s<1
). The model case
s=\frac{1}{2}
(corresponding to the so called half-harmonic maps) was first studied by Da Lio and Rivière
[
9
,
10
]
as critical points of the
L^{2}
-norm of the
\tfrac{1}{4}
-Laplacian. In this paper we will focus on the special case of half-harmonic maps from
\mathbb{R}
into the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. More precisely, consider the space
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
of measurable maps
u
from
\mathbb{R}
into the complex plane such that

[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|u(x)-u(\xi)|^{2}}{(x-\xi)^{2}}dxd\xi<\infty,

and its non-convex subset

\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})=\left\{u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}):|u(x)|=1\text{ for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

endowed with the same seminorm.

For such maps, following
[
9
]
, we introduce the fractional energy

E(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u\right|^{2}dx,\quad u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1}).

(1)

This energy can be characterized in different equivalent ways, as we shall briefly recall. First of all, for
u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
, its Poisson harmonic extension is well defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)
as

\tilde{u}(x,y):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{yu(\xi)}{y^{2}+(x-\xi)^{2}}d\xi.

Notice that if
u
takes values into
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\tilde{u}
takes values into the closure of the unit disk

D^{2}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}+y^{2}<1\}.

Then we have (see the Appendix, (
68
) and (
69
) in particular)

\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}dxdy=\frac{1}{2\pi}[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},\quad u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}),

(2)

and we can equivalently use any of these formulas to define
E(u)
. While the characterizations of
E(u)
in terms of the extension
\tilde{u}
or in terms of a double integral are more convenient to do estimates, the original definition of
E(u)
as in (
1
) makes the analogy with harmonic maps more evident. Indeed, interpreting
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u
as a half-derivative of
u
,
E(u)
can be seen as the fractional analog for functions in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
of the classical Dirichlet integral

E_{2}(u):=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dxdy,\quad u\in H^{1,2}(\Omega,M),

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an embedded Riemannian manifold, and

H^{1,2}(\Omega,M):=\left\{u\in H^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}):u(x)\in M\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\right\}.

In this analogy,
an important property that
E
shares with
E_{2}
, and that will play an important role is the conformal invariance:

E(u)=E(u\circ\phi)\quad\text{for }u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1}),

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the restriction of a conformal map sending
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
into itself and its boundary to itself.

Concerning
E_{2}
, one of the model problems in the calculus of variations is the study of its minimizers or, more generally, its critical points under suitable constraints, leading to harmonic maps (harmonic functions, in the special case
M=\mathbb{R}
). In particular, the following uniqueness question was raised by Giaquinta and Hildebrandt
[
16

\Omega=D^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

g\in C^{\infty}(\partial D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{2})

be a minimizer of

H^{1,2}_{g}(D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{2}):=\{u\in H^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{2}):u|_{\partial D^{2}}=g\}.

Assume that

Degree for maps in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})

We first recall some facts about the degree of maps in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. To our knowledge, this was first introduced by L. Boutet de Monvel and O. Gabber back in 1984, see the discussion in
[
2
]
. For a classical introduction to the topic we refer to
[
7
]
.

Given a function
u\in C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, one typically defines its Brouwer degree via a lifting, i.e. a map
\varphi\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
such that

u(e^{i\theta})=e^{i\varphi(\theta)}.

(9)

Since
u(e^{i\theta})=u(e^{i(\theta+2\pi)})
, it follows that
\varphi(2\pi)-\varphi(0)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and one defines

\deg(u):=\frac{\varphi(2\pi)-\varphi(0)}{2\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(10)

On the other hand, due to the nature of our problems, it will be more natural to work with integro-differential definitions of the Brouwer degree.
We start recalling that for
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
its Brouwer degree can be computed via the de Rahm cohomology as

\deg(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}u^{*}(d\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}u\wedge\dot{u}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{u}\dot{u}\,d\theta.

(11)

Here we used that for vectors
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}
,
\bar{v}w=v\cdot w+i(v\wedge w)
, where
\bar{v}w=(v_{1}-iv_{2})(w_{1}+iw_{2})
is the usual product in
\mathbb{C}
, while
v\wedge w:=(v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1})
and
v\cdot w:=v_{1}w_{1}+v_{2}w_{2}
, and we used that
u\cdot\dot{u}=0
.

This definition is equivalent to the previous one, since for
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
lifting
u
in the sense of (
9
), we have

\frac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{u}\dot{u}\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\varphi^{\prime}(x)\,dx={\varphi(2\pi)-\varphi(0)}.

Further, writing
u
as Fourier series

u(e^{i\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}e^{ik\theta},\quad a_{k}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}u(e^{i\theta})e^{-ik\theta}d\theta,

with a simple computation we obtain (still assuming
u
to be smooth)

\frac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{u}\dot{u}\,d\theta=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k|a_{k}|^{2}.

(12)

\deg(u):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{u}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}ud\theta,\quad u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1}).

(16)

Going back to (
11
), for
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, which we write as
u=u_{1}+iu_{2}
, considering a smooth extension
U=U_{1}+iU_{2}
to the unit disk
\overline{D^{2}}
, and using Stokes’ theorem we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{u}\dot{u}d\theta&=\frac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{u}du=\frac{1}{i}\int_{D^{2}}d(\bar{U}dU)=\frac{1}{i}\int_{D^{2}}d[(U_{1}-iU_{2})(dU_{1}+idU_{2})]\\
&=2\int_{D^{2}}dU_{1}\wedge dU_{2}=2\int_{D^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial U_{1}}{\partial x}\frac{\partial U_{2}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial U_{1}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial U_{2}}{\partial x}\right)dxdy=2\int_{D^{2}}JUdxdy.\end{split}

Considering that
|JU|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla U|^{2}
, the right-hand side is well defined for any
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
choosing as
U
any
H^{1}
extension, for instance the harmonic extension
\tilde{u}\in H^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{C})
. Hence we can also define

\deg(u):=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{D}JUdxdy,\quad u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1}),\,U\in H^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}),\,U|_{\partial D^{2}}=u.

(17)

In a similar fashion, if
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
is constant outside a bounded interval, say
u\equiv a
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]
, we can write

\displaystyle\deg(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{*}(d\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\wedge u^{\prime}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}u^{\prime}dx.

(18)

Now, writing
w=u-a
, we have that
w
is compactly supported and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}u^{\prime}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{w}w^{\prime}dx
.
Then, we can write
w^{\prime}
as Fourier integral and compute

\displaystyle\deg(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{w}w^{\prime}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{w}(x)\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi x}\xi\hat{w}(\xi)d\xi\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\hat{w}(\xi)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\xi x}\bar{w}(x)dx\right)d\xi=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\hat{w}(\xi)\check{\bar{w}}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi|\hat{w}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi,

(19)

where in the last equality we have used that
\bar{\hat{w}}=\check{\bar{w}}
.
On the other hand

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w}]

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}wdx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{-i\xi}{|\xi|}\mathcal{F}((-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w})\right)(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}wdx

\displaystyle=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\mathcal{F}((-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w})\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left((-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}w\right)d\xi=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\sqrt{|\xi|}\hat{\bar{w}}(\xi)\sqrt{|\xi|}\check{w}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi\bar{\check{w}}(\xi)\check{w}(\xi)d\xi=-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi|\check{w}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi=i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi|\hat{w}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi,

(20)

where
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}
is defined in (
63
) and
\mathcal{H}
denotes the Hilbert transform:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}({v}):=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{-i\xi}{|\xi|}\hat{{v}}\right).

(21)

Putting (
19
) and (
20
) together yields

\deg(u)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}{\bar{w}}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}{w}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{u}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}udx,

(22)

which, using Lemma
A.2
, can be extended by density to every
u\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.
It follows from (
22
) and Hölder’s inequality

|\deg(u)|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}E(u).

(23)

Finally we remark that by composing with the conformal map
\Psi(z)=-i\frac{z+i}{z-i}
, sending
D^{2}
onto
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
(see (
40
)), one infers from (
17
) also

\deg(u)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}JUdxdy,\quad U\in H^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}),\,U|_{\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}}=u.

(24)

Proposition 2.1

Given
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})

3
Degree jumps and surgeries

3.1
Degree jumps under weak convergence

The first type of degree jump that we will consider is due to the fact that the degree is not continuous with respect to the weak convergence in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, or in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

We shall say that a sequence
(u_{n})\subset\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
weakly converges to
u
in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u_{n}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}vdx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}vdx,\quad\text{for }v\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}).

As already mentioned, weakly converging sequences might jump from a homotopy class to another. The next proposition, which is the analog
[
19
, Lemma 3]
, and (15) in
[
4
]
(see also
[
18
]
and
[
5
, Lemma 1]
), quantifies the minimal energy cost of such jumps.

Proposition 3.1

Let
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}_{k}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1}):=\{v\in\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1}):\deg(v)=k\}
, converge weakly in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
to
u\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
. Then

E(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E(u_{n})-2\pi|k-\ell|.

Equivalently, if
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{E}_{k}=\mathcal{E}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1}):=\{v\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1}):\deg(v)=k\}
converge weakly in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
to
u\in\mathcal{E}_{\ell}
, then

E(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E(u_{n})-2\pi|k-\ell|.

In the proof of Proposition
3.1
we will use the following:

Lemma 3.2

For
v,w\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
, we have
\mathcal{H}((-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}w)=(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}(\mathcal{H}(w))
and

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}((-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{v})(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}wd\theta=-\overline{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{(}-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}v(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}(\mathcal{H}(\bar{w}))d\theta}.

(25)

Proof.

The first identity follows easily from the definition of the Hilbert transform and
(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}
on
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
. From this, and from the identities

\overline{\mathcal{H}(f)}=\mathcal{H}(\bar{f}),\quad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}(\bar{f})gd\theta=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{f}\mathcal{H}(g)d\theta,\quad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}),

we immediately get (
25
). ∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1

We prove the proposition for
(u_{n})\subset\mathcal{E}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. We define

F_{\pm}(u)=E(u)\pm 2\pi\deg(u),

and prove that
F_{\pm}
is weakly lower semicontinuous in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.
It will be convenient to use the degree formula (
16
)
Set
w_{n}:=u_{n}-u\rightharpoonup 0
. Using (
16
), and Lemma
3.2
we get

\displaystyle F_{\pm}(u_{n})

\displaystyle=E(u)+E(w_{n})+o(1)\pm\frac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}(\bar{u}+\bar{w}_{n})](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}(u+w_{n})d\theta

\displaystyle=F_{\pm}(u)+E(w_{n})\pm\frac{1}{i}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w}_{n}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}w_{n}d\theta+o(1),

provided

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w}_{n}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}ud\theta\to 0\quad\text{and }\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{u}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}w_{n}d\theta\to 0.

(26)

Then, using that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\mathcal{H}[(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w}_{n}](-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}w_{n}d\theta\right|\leq\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}\bar{w}_{n}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=E(w_{n})

we obtain
F_{\pm}(u)\leq F_{\pm}(u_{n})+o(1)
as
n\to\infty
, and we conclude that
F_{\pm}
is weakly lower-semicontinuous. Then the lemma follows from

E(u)=F_{\pm}(u)\mp 2\pi\ell\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{\pm}(u_{n})\mp 2\pi\ell=\liminf_{n\to\infty}E(u_{n})\pm 2\pi(k-\ell).

It follows from the definition of weak convergence and Lemma
3.2
that (
26
) hold true, and we conclude the proof of the proposition.
\square

Remark.

The constant
2\pi
is sharp, as can be proven by considering a concentrating sequence
(u_{n})
of (restrictions of) Möbius functions with
\deg(u_{n})=1
,

,
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\equiv C
as

. More generally, using (restriction of) Blaschke products as in (

), one can construct a sequence
(u_{n})
with
\deg(u_{n})=k>0
,

(by Lemma
5.1
),
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
,
\deg(u)=\ell\in[0,k)
,
E(u)=2\pi\ell
.

One can give an analogous proof of Proposition
3.1
by working with
\tilde{u}_{k}\rightharpoonup\tilde{u}
in
H^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and using (
24
), together with
|J\tilde{u}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\tilde{u}|^{2}
.

Remark.

In attempting to formulate a minimization problem in
\dot{W}^{s,\frac{1}{s}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
or in

with

[

,

, one of the main obstacles would be finding the analog of the sharp constant

.

3.2
Surgeries

Next we will consider a degree change due to surgery, namely, given a closed arc
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
we will replace a function
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
in
A
with another map
v:A\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that the resulting map is still in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Up to a Möbius transformation, we can assume
A=\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}:=\mathbb{S}^{1}\cap\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y\geq 0\}
.

Set the gluing of
v
to
u
as

v\&u(e^{i\theta}):=\begin{cases}v(e^{i\theta})&\text{for }0\leq\theta\leq\pi\\
u(e^{i\theta})&\text{for }\pi<\theta<2\pi,\end{cases}

(27)

and the gluing of
v
to the reflection of
u
as

v\#u(e^{i\theta}):=\begin{cases}v(e^{i\theta})&\text{for }0\leq\theta\leq\pi\\
u(e^{-i\theta})&\text{for }\pi<\theta<2\pi.\end{cases}

(28)

If
u\in C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
,
v\in C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
u(\pm 1)=v(\pm 1)
, so that
v\&u,v\#u\in C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, it is fairly easy to see that

\deg(v\&u)=\deg(u)+\deg(v\#u).

In particular the degree change only depends on the values of
u
and its replacement in
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}
, as it is well known.

We want to prove the same formula for
u,v\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Two main difficulties arise. First of all we do not have an analog of
u(\pm 1)=v(\pm 1)
in terms of traces of functions in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}
. As Proposition
A.10
shows, and contrary to what happens for maps in
H^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{S}^{2})
with
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a map
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
might not have a trace on the boundary of an arc
A
of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and in fact, for some point
e^{i\theta_{0}}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
u(e^{i\theta})
can wind infinitely many times around
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as
\theta\to\theta_{0}^{+}
and infinitely many times, but with opposite orientation as
\theta\to\theta_{0}^{-}
. The heavy cancelation due to the opposite orientation of the windings allow the degree of
u
to still be finite, but this suggests extra care in cutting, gluing and counting the degree of functions in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

This might appear to be only a problem of trace (in particular of the unboundedness of functions in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
), but problems arise even if one considers functions in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}\cap C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, as Proposition
A.8
shows. In fact, one can take a map
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}\cap C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and replace it locally by a constant map obtaining a new map that, in spite of being continuous, does not belong to
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Both phenomena are essentially due to the nonlocal nature of the
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}
-seminorm, and in particular to cancelations in the double integral defining it. Because of them, it will be necessary to assume that
v\&u,v\#u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
:

Proposition 3.3

Consider
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
v:\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Set
v\&u
and
v\#u
as in (
27
) and (
28
) and assume that
v\&u,v\#u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Then

\deg(v\&u)=\deg(u)+\deg(v\#u).

(29)

Proof.

Consider
\gamma:[-1,1]\times\{0\}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}
given by
\gamma(x,0)=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(x-1)}
. Clearly
\gamma
is a diffeomorphism and

|\gamma^{\prime}|\equiv\frac{\pi}{2},\quad|(\gamma^{-1})^{\prime}|\equiv\frac{2}{\pi}.

(30)

On
[-1,1]\times\{0\}
define the function

u^{*}(x,0)=u(\gamma(x,0))=u(e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(x-1)}).

Now define the maps
u^{*}_{\pm}:\partial D^{2}_{\pm}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

\begin{cases}u^{*}_{\pm}:=u&\text{on }\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\pm}\\
u^{*}_{\pm}=u^{*}&\text{on }(-1,1)\times\{0\},\end{cases}

and
v^{*}:\partial D^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

\begin{cases}v^{*}=v&\text{on }\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}\\
v^{*}=u^{*}&\text{on }(-1,1)\times\{0\}.\end{cases}

Since
|(x,0)-(\xi,0)|\geq\frac{2}{\pi}|\gamma(x,0)-\gamma(\xi,0)|
for
x,\xi\in[-1,1]
, also using (
30
), we get

\begin{split}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{|u^{*}(x,0)-u^{*}(\xi,0)|^{2}}{|(x,0)-(\xi,0)|^{2}}dxd\xi&\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{|u(\gamma(x,0))-u(\gamma(\xi,0))|^{2}}{|\gamma(x,0)-\gamma(\xi,0)|^{2}}dxd\xi\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}}\frac{|u(e^{is})-u(e^{it})|^{2}}{|e^{is}-e^{it}|^{2}}dsdt.\end{split}

(31)

We now claim

\displaystyle|e^{is}-(x,0)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|}{2},\quad\text{for }e^{is}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-},\,x\in[-1,1],

(32)

\displaystyle|e^{is}-(x,0)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|}{4},\quad\text{for }e^{is}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+},\ x\in[-1,1].

(33)

To prove (
32
), by symmetry it suffices to consider the case
-\frac{\pi}{2}\leq s\leq 0
. We consider
2
cases.

Case 1:

0\leq\frac{\pi}{4}(1-x)\leq-s\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
We have

|e^{is}-(x,0)|\geq|\sin s|\geq\frac{2}{\pi}|s|\geq\frac{|s|}{2}

(34)

(the second inequality follows from showing that
f(t)=\frac{\sin t}{t}
is decreasing for
0<t\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
).
Moreover, estimating
|e^{it_{1}}-e^{it_{2}}|\leq|t_{1}-t_{2}|
, we get

|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|\leq\left|s+\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)\right|\leq|s|\leq 2|e^{is}-(x,0)|.

Case 2:

-s\leq\frac{\pi}{4}(1-x)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. We have

|e^{is}-(x,0)|\geq 1-x,

(35)

since the circle centred at
(x,0)
of radius
1-x
is contained
D^{2}
and
e^{is}\in\partial D^{2}
. Then

|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|\leq\left|s+\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)\right|\leq\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)\leq 2|e^{is}-(x,0)|.

Then (
32
) is proven. In order to prove (
33
), again by symmetry, it suffices to prove it for
0\leq s\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
and we consider three cases.

Case 1:

0\leq x\leq 1
,
\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)\leq s\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
.
As before we have (
34
).
Moreover
|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|\leq s+\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)
. Hence, by our assumption,

\frac{|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|}{4}\leq\frac{s}{4}+\frac{\pi}{8}(1-x)\leq\frac{s}{4}+\frac{s}{4}\leq|e^{is}-(x,0)|.

Case 2:

0\leq x\leq 1
,
0\leq s\leq\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. We have (
35
) as before. Then

|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|\leq s+\frac{\pi}{2}(1-x)\leq\pi(1-x)\leq 4(1-x)\leq 4|e^{is}-(x,0)|.

Case 3:

-1\leq x<0
,
0\leq s\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. Since

|e^{is}-(x,0)|\geq 1
, we have

|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|\leq 2\leq 2|e^{is}-(x,0)|.

Then also (
33
) is proven.

Now (
32
) together with (
30
) gives

\begin{split}\int_{[-1,1]\times\{0\}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}}\frac{|u^{*}_{-}(e^{is})-u^{*}_{-}(x,0)|^{2}}{|e^{is}-(x,0)|^{2}}dsdx&\leq{4}\int_{[-1,1]\times\{0\}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}}\frac{|u(e^{is})-u(\gamma(x,0))|^{2}}{|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|^{2}}dsdx\\
&=\frac{8}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}}\frac{|u(e^{is})-u(e^{it})|^{2}}{|e^{is}-e^{it}|^{2}}dsdt.\end{split}

With (
31
), it follows that

[u^{*}_{-}]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\partial D^{2}_{-})}\leq C[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-})}.

For (
33
) we infer

\begin{split}\int_{[-1,1]\times\{0\}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}}\frac{|u^{*}_{+}(e^{is})-u^{*}_{+}(x,0)|^{2}}{|e^{is}-(x,0)|^{2}}dsdx&\leq 16\int_{[-1,1]\times\{0\}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}}\frac{|u(e^{is})-u(\gamma(x,0))|^{2}}{|e^{is}-\gamma(x,0)|^{2}}dsdx\\

66
) to get

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}udx=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}(x)P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(\xi)}{(x-\xi)^{2}}d\xi dx

and exchanging the roles of
x
and
\xi
and summing to obtain

\displaystyle 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}udx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}(x)P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(x)-u(\xi)}{(x-\xi)^{2}}d\xi dx+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bar{u}(y)P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{u(\xi)-u(x)}{(x-\xi)^{2}}dxd\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|u(x)-u(\xi)|^{2}}{(x-\xi)^{2}}dxd\xi.

Then, using the first identity in (
68
), (
69
) follows.

We can do similar computations for functions defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We start by recalling that any map
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
can be written in terms of its Fourier series

u(e^{i\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}e^{ik\theta},

where

a_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}u(e^{i\theta})e^{-ik\theta}d\theta.

(70)

Defining the fractional Laplacian (in this case a half-derivative)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u(e^{i\theta}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sqrt{|k|}a_{k}e^{ik\theta},

(71)

we can set in analogy with (
64
)

\begin{split}H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})&=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}):(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{4}}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})\right\}\\
&=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C}):\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k||a_{k}|^{2}<\infty\right\},\end{split}

(72)

and

[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k||a_{k}|^{2}.

Considering in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
the harmonic extension
\tilde{u}
of
u
to
D^{2}

\tilde{u}(re^{i\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}r^{|k|}e^{ik\theta},

(73)

we can easily compute

\|\nabla\tilde{u}\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}=[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}.

(74)

On the other hand, we can also compute (see e.g.
[
8
, Proposition A.2]
)

(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u(e^{i\theta})=\frac{1}{\pi}P.V.\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{u(e^{i\theta})-u(e^{it})}{|e^{i\theta}-e^{it}|^{2}}dt,

(75)

and, similar to (
69
) obtain

[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\bar{u}(-\Delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}u\,d\theta=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(e^{i\theta})-u(e^{it})|^{2}}{|e^{i\theta}-e^{it}|^{2}}dtd\theta.

(76)

We also recall that the harmonic extension of
u
can be expressed using the Poisson kernel:

\tilde{u}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|z-e^{i\theta}|}u(e^{i\theta})d\theta,\quad z\in D^{2}.

(77)

Finally, we mention a characterisation of fractional Sobolev spaces in terms of traces of Sobolev maps on arbitrary bounded Lipschitz domains. For such a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}
with
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, we define

[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\Gamma)}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{|u(z)-u(\zeta)|^{2}}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}d\sigma(z)d\sigma(\zeta),

where
d\sigma
is the arc-length on
\Gamma
, and set

H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\Gamma):=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Gamma):[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\Gamma)}<\infty\right\}.

Then we have the following trace theorem, see e.g.
[
14
,
31
]
:

Proposition A.1

For any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
with
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, the trace maps
u\mapsto u|_{\Gamma}
is surjective from
H^{1,2}(\Omega)
to
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\Gamma)
.

A.2
Density results

Proposition A.2

The space of functions
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
which are constant outside a compact set is dense in
\dot{H}^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Equivalently, the space of functions
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
which are constant in a neighborhood (depending on
u
) of any fixed point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
are dense in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

In the proof of Proposition
A.2
we will borrow ideas from
[
7
]
. We first need two lemmas.

Lemma A.3

Let
(u_{k})\subset H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
be such that
u_{k}\to u
a.e. in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
for some
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. If
u_{k}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
and
|u_{k}|\geq\delta
a.e. in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
for some
\delta>0
independent of
k
, then
\frac{u_{k}}{|u_{k}|}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Proof.

Setting
\varphi(z)=\frac{z}{|z|}-z
we get that
\varphi
is smooth in
\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, and
|\nabla\varphi|\leq C(\delta)
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus B_{\delta}
. The lemma follows if we show that
\varphi(u_{k})\to\varphi(u)\equiv 0
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
.

We set

g_{k}(z,\zeta):=\frac{|\varphi(u_{k}(z))-\varphi(u_{k}(\zeta))|^{2}}{|z-\zeta|^{2}},\quad z,\zeta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Then

\displaystyle|g_{k}(z,\zeta)|

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{|u_{k}(z)-u_{k}(\zeta)|^{2}}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}\leq C\frac{|(u_{k}(z)-u(z))-(u_{k}(\zeta)-u(\zeta))|^{2}}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}+C\frac{|u(z)-u(\zeta)|^{2}}{|z-\zeta|^{2}}.

This shows that the family
(g_{k})
is uniformly integrable in
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. In particular, as
g_{k}\to 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, by Vitali’s convergence theorem we get that
g_{k}\to 0
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1})
, which gives
\varphi(u_{k})\to 0
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
.
∎

Lemma A.4

Given
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and
\varepsilon>0
we set

u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta}):=\fint_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}u(e^{it})\,dt.

Then
|u_{\av,\varepsilon}|=1+o_{\varepsilon}(1)
,
u_{\av,\varepsilon}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
,
u_{\av,\varepsilon}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
and
\frac{u_{\av,\varepsilon}}{|u_{\av,\varepsilon}|}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Proof.

By the Jensen’s inequality

\begin{split}(1-|u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta})|)^{2}&\leq\fint_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}|u(e^{it})-u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta})|^{2}dt\\
&=\fint_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}\left|\fint_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}u(e^{it})-u(e^{is})\,ds\right|^{2}dt\\
&\leq\fint_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}\fint_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}|u(e^{it})-u(e^{is})|^{2}\,dsdt\\
&\leq\int_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}\int_{\theta-\varepsilon}^{\theta+\varepsilon}\frac{|u(e^{it})-u(e^{is})|^{2}}{|e^{it}-e^{is}|^{2}}\,dsdt.\\
\end{split}

where in the last inequality we used that
|e^{it}-e^{is}|\leq 2\varepsilon
for
s,t\in[\theta-\varepsilon,\theta+\varepsilon]
.
By (
76
) and the absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral, the last integral goes to
0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
uniformly with respect to
\theta
, hence
|u_{\av,\varepsilon}|\to 1
uniformly as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

We now prove that
u_{\av,\varepsilon}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. To this end we write

\displaystyle u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta_{1}})-u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta_{2}})=\fint_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\left(u(e^{i(\theta_{1}+t)})-u(e^{i(\theta_{2}+t)})\right)dt.

Then for any
A_{1},A_{2}\subset(0,2\pi)
,

\displaystyle\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{|u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta_{1}})-u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta_{2}})|^{2}}{|e^{i\theta_{1}}-e^{i\theta_{2}}|^{2}}d\theta_{2}d\theta_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\int_{A_{1}}\int_{A_{2}}\frac{|u(e^{i(\theta_{1}+t)})-u(e^{(i(\theta_{2}+t)})|^{2}}{|e^{i(\theta_{1}+t)}-e^{i(\theta_{2}+t)}|^{2}}d\theta_{2}d\theta_{1}dt

\displaystyle=\fint_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}\int_{A_{1}+t}\int_{A_{2}+t}\frac{|u(e^{i\theta_{1}})-u(e^{(i\theta_{2}})|^{2}}{|e^{i\theta_{1}}-e^{i\theta_{2}}|^{2}}d\theta_{2}d\theta_{1}dt.

This shows that the family
\frac{|u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta_{1}})-u_{\av,\varepsilon}(e^{i\theta_{2}})|^{2}}{|e^{i\theta_{1}}-e^{i\theta_{2}}|^{2}}
is uniformly integrable in
(0,2\pi)\times(0,2\pi)
. Therefore, as
u_{\av,\varepsilon}\to u
a.e., by the Vitali’s convergence theorem we get that
u_{\av,\varepsilon}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{C})
.

Now the convergence
\frac{u_{\av,\varepsilon}}{|u_{\av,\varepsilon}|}\to u
in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
follows from Lemma
A.3
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
A.2
We first prove that the space of functions
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
which are constant in a neighborhood of a fixed point, which we can assume to be
i
, is dense in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Let
u\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. It follows from Lemma
A.4
that it suffices to consider
u\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Let
\tilde{u}\in H^{1,2}(D^{2})
be the harmonic extension of
u
in
D^{2}
. Writing the Poisson kernel as

K(z,e^{i\theta}):=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{2\pi|z-e^{i\theta}|^{2}}=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\pi|re^{it}-e^{i\theta}|^{2}},\quad z=re^{it}\in D^{2},

from (
77
), and using that
u\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, we have

\begin{split}\tilde{u}(re^{it})&=u(e^{it})+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}K(re^{it},e^{i\theta})\left(u(e^{i\theta})-u(e^{it})\right)d\theta\\
&=u(e^{it})+O\left(\int_{\partial D^{2}}K(re^{it},e^{i\theta})|e^{it}-e^{i\theta}|d\theta\right)\\
&=u(e^{it})+o(1)\quad\text{as }r\to 1.\end{split}

In particular,
|\tilde{u}|\to 1
in
D^{2}\setminus B_{1-\varepsilon}
uniformly as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and
|\nabla\tilde{u}|\in L^{\infty}(D^{2})
. Hence, one can find
U_{k}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{D}^{2})
such that
U_{k}\to U
in
H^{1,2}(D^{2})
,
|\nabla U_{k}|\leq C
, and
|U_{k}|\to 1
as
k\to\infty
uniformly on
\partial D^{2}
.

For
\varepsilon>0
let
\eta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{C})
be such that
\eta_{\varepsilon}\equiv 0
in
B_{\varepsilon}(i)
,
\eta_{\varepsilon}\equiv 1
in
B^{c}_{2\varepsilon}(i)
,

, and

U_{k,\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{D^{2}},\mathbb{C}),\quad U_{k,\varepsilon}:=U_{k}\eta_{\varepsilon}+U_{k}(i)(1-\eta_{\varepsilon}).

Clearly each
U_{k,\varepsilon}
is constant in

\nabla(U_{k,\varepsilon}-U)=(U_{k}(i)-U_{k})\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}+\nabla U_{k}(1-\eta_{\varepsilon}),

which shows that
U_{k,\varepsilon}\to U_{k}
in
H^{1,2}(D^{2})
as

. Moreover, as

, one easily obtains that
|U_{k,\varepsilon}|=1+o_{k,\varepsilon}(1)

. Thus, for some suitable choice of

in
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})

The second part of the proposition follows from the equivalence of the Dirichlet energies

for any

,
which is turn follows from (

), (

\Pi_{+}
can be extended to the conformal map
\Psi:\overline{D^{2}}\setminus\{i\}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}

\square

A.3
Two examples

Lemma A.7

Let
\varphi\in C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})
be such that
\varphi(x)=0
for
|x|\geq\frac{1}{2}
and for
x=0
, and

\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{|x|}}},\quad\text{for }0<|x|\leq\frac{1}{4}.

Then
\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})
,
but
\varphi\chi_{[0,\infty)}\not\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

To prove that
\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})
we consider an extension
\Phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{2}_{+}\setminus\{0\})
with

\Phi(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log\frac{1}{|(x,y)|}}},\quad\text{for }0<|(x,y)|\leq\frac{1}{4},\ y\geq 0.

Then
\nabla\Phi\in L^{2}(B_{\frac{1}{4}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, and
\Phi\in H^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
follows by smoothness and compact support. Then
\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})
by (
67
), and
\|\nabla\tilde{\varphi}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\nabla\Phi\|_{L^{2}}
, where
\tilde{\varphi}
is the Poisson harmonic extension of
\varphi
.

To show that
\varphi\chi_{[0,\infty)}\not\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})
we compute for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{4}

\begin{split}\int_{x=0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{y=-\varepsilon}^{0}\frac{(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))^{2}}{(x-y)^{2}}dydx&=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left(\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{-1}\int_{-\varepsilon}^{0}\frac{dy}{(y-x)^{2}}dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left(\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{x+\varepsilon}\right)dx=\infty,\end{split}

(78)

so that
[\varphi\chi_{[0,\infty)}]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}}=+\infty
.
∎

Proposition A.8

Let
\varphi\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{R})
be defined as in Lemma
A.7
. Then
u:=e^{i\varphi\circ\Pi_{-}}\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})\cap C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, while the function

w(e^{i\theta}):=\begin{cases}u(e^{i\theta})&\text{for }\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta\leq\frac{3\pi}{2}\\
1&\text{for}-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}\end{cases}

belongs to
C^{0}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
but not to
H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Proof.

To prove that

[u]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|u(e^{i\theta})-u(e^{it})|^{2}}{|e^{i\theta}-e^{it}|^{2}}dtd\theta<\infty,

which in turn, considering that
u

\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon}^{\frac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon}\int_{\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon}^{\frac{\pi}{2}+\varepsilon}\frac{|u(e^{i\theta})-u(e^{it})|^{2}}{|e^{i\theta}-e^{it}|^{2}}dtd\theta<+\infty,

for some
\varepsilon>0
, which easily follows from

\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(\varphi(x)-\varphi(\xi))^{2}}{(x-\xi)^{2}}dxd\xi<+\infty

(Lemma
A.7
) and the fact that
\Pi_{-}
is bi-Lipschitz from an arc of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
around

and an interval in
\mathbb{R}
around
0
.

Similarly, from (
78
) it follows that
[w]_{H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})}=+\infty
, hence

. The continuity of

and

follow easily by construction.
∎

\psi(x)=\log\log\frac{1}{|x|}\quad\text{for }0<|x|<\frac{1}{4},

With the same proof as for Proposition

we obtain:

Proposition A.10

Let

v:=e^{i\psi\circ\Pi_{-}}\in H^{\frac{1}{2},2}(\mathbb{S}^{1},\mathbb{S}^{1})
.
