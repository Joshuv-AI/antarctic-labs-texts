Claudio Giorgi, Federico Zullo

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to develop a general constitutive scheme within continuum thermodynamics to describe the behavior of heat flow in deformable media. Starting from a classical thermodynamic approach, the rate-type constitutive equations are defined in the material (Lagrangian) description where the standard time derivative satisfies the principle of objectivity. All constitutive functions are required to depend on a common set of independent variables and to be consistent with thermodynamics. The statement of the Second Law is formulated in a general nonlocal form, where the entropy production rate is prescribed by a non-negative constitutive function and the extra entropy flux obeys a no-flow boundary condition. The thermodynamic response is then developed based on Coleman-Noll procedure. In the local formulation, the free energy potential and the rate of entropy production function are assumed to depend on temperature, temperature gradient and heat-flux vector along with their time derivatives. This approach results in rate-type constitutive equations for the heat-flux vector that are intrinsically consistent with the Second Law and easily amenable to analysis. A huge class of linear and nonlinear models of the rate type are recovered (e.g., Cattaneo-Maxwell’s, Jeffreys-like, Green-Naghdi’s, Quintanilla’s and Burgers-like heat conductors). In the (weakly) nonlocal formulation of the second law, both the entropy production rate and an entropy extra-flux vector are assumed to depend on temperature, temperature gradient and heat-flux vector along with their spatial gradients and time derivatives. Within this (classical) thermodynamic framework the nonlocal Guyer-Krumhansl model and some nonlinear generalizations devised by Cimmelli and Sellitto are obtained.

1.
Introduction

In the last years, numerous heat conduction models beyond Fourier have been developed to account for relaxational and nonlocal effects, fast phenomena or wave propagation, such as being typical for biological systems, nanomaterials or nanosystems. Non-Fourier models mainly differ for their various thermodynamic backgrounds (thermodynamics of irreversible processes, extended irreversible thermodynamics, etc.). A challenging question is their possible compatibility with the Second Law. In the book of Straughan
[
1
]
many of them are presented and discussed in connection with wave propagation properties. A recent review by Kovács
[
2
]
discusses properties concerning their possible practical applications in light of experiments.
This article aims to discuss their deduction in the context of classical Continuum Thermodynamics and possible compatibility with the Second Law stated therein.

Fourier’s law gives a macroscopic description of the microscopic phenomena associated with heat diffusion and is an excellent approximation at length scales much greater than the mean free path and at time scales much greater than the thermal relaxation time. Nevertheless, one of the predicted results of the Fourier law is that temperature disturbances propagate at infinite speeds. This violates the law of special relativity, and also, since in metals the heat conduction is typically attributed to the migration of free electrons and in semiconductors it is explained by the migration of phonons, which are collective vibrations of the atoms in the crystal structure, it would be a reasonable requirement that the propagation speed proves to be finite. This view has spurred much academic interest in the last half century towards seeking a model compatible with a finite speed of propagation (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
and refs therein).

The first non-classical heat conduction model capable of predicting the propagation of heat waves is the Maxwell-Cattaneo-Vernotte law (see
[
4
,
8
,
9
]
).
The MCV theory is based on a

rate-type
constitutive equation for the heat flux that predicts heat-wave propagation.
Nevertheless, in the recent past some controversy raised about the non-objective character of
the constitutive equation which limits its application to rigid bodies at rest only.
Several efforts have been devoted to circumvent such a difficulty (cf.
[
10
,
11
,
12

and references therein).
Yet the MCV model suffer from some other drawbacks. In particular, the non-negativity of the absolute temperature at any time is not preserved (see, e.g.,
[
13

,
15
]
).

A new class of models for heat conduction in a rigid body has been developed
in the nineties by Green and Naghdi
[
16
]
. In the framework of their general theory, the
propagation of thermal waves at finite speed is allowed. Unlike the MCV model, their constitutive equations are completely immune from criticism of objectivity,
since do not contain the material derivative of any vector field.
Green and Naghdi proposed
three types of models, named
type I, type II
and
type
III
, respectively, the latter being the most general, which (formally) includes the others
as particular instances.
An empirical temperature scale is used, not necessarily the absolute one.

In the past decades, Green-Naghdi’s theories have attracted growing interest. In particular, Green-Naghdi type III (GN III) theory owes its success to the capability
of describing heat propagation by means of thermal waves in addition to diffusive propagation and for this reason it has been applied in a number of disparate physical circumstances, where propagation of heat is coupled with elastic deformations of solids, flow of viscoelastic fluids, etc. (see
[

On the other hand, some criticisms have been raised about the GN III heat conduction theory
[

20

21

Moreover, it has been observed that the GN III model fits well to propagation processes of finite duration (transient regime), but leads to unrealistic effects, at least when either asymptotic or stationary phenomena are involved
[
22

Recently, Quintanilla
[
23

proposed a new thermoelastic heat conduction model which emerged from the development of the GN III by adding a relaxation factor. This theory gives rise to a third-order differential equation for the temperature that looks like the linearized Moore-Gibson-Thompson (MGT) equation in high intensity ultrasound

Guyer and Krumhansl

studied the heat wave propagation in dielectric crystals at low temperature. They observed that in the regime of low temperature the heat flux
{\bf q}
is proportional to the momentum flux of the phonon gas. On the basis of kinetic theory they found a macroscopic equation governing its evolution.
When the relaxation time is negligible, the Guyer-Krumhansl (GK) equation reduces to a nonlocal perturbation of the Fourier law (see
[

The possibility of utilizing a continuum approach to derive the Guyer-Krumhansl equation results in a model with a wide range of validity since it allows to fit the parameters to the observed phenomenon.
Considering an undeformable medium, the GK equation has been derived within the framework of classical irreversible thermodynamics with internal variables

We are not aware whether the GK model and its consistency with the Second Law have been obtained in the context of classical continuum thermodynamics. Moreover, it is not entirely clear which boundary conditions should be assigned for the heat flux.

1.1.
Aims and plan of the paper

In this work we propose a new approach to heat conduction theories that is inherently thermodynamic, as it originates directly from the Clausius-Duhem inequality.
The
specific production of entropy

\sigma
enters as a non-negative constitutive function, so that the Second Law is automatically satisfied.
The idea that
\sigma
be given by a constitutive equation traces back to Green and Naghdi
[
16
]
.
They assumed from the outset that all constitutive functions depend on the common set of (physical) variables.
Conceptually our approach extends this idea.

The set of independent variables includes only the macroscopically observable fields and their temporal and spatial derivatives, without making any recourse to internal variables or ambiguous state variables (such as thermal displacement). In particular, no constitutive prescription on the energy influx
{\bf q}
is made, rather it is treated as an independent variable. The Coleman-Noll procedure
[
39
]
is applied to derive thermodynamic restrictions on the Helmholtz free energy
\psi
. However, unlike the Green-Naghdi theories, here
\sigma
is independent of the constitutive prescription of the free energy
\psi
and, what is more, it is proved to be non-negative along whatever process.

This strategy allows us to increase the generality of the models considered here, for instance new classes of rate-type constitutive equations for the heat flux.
Since time derivatives are involved, we adopt a material description in order to avoid the problem of their objectivity. Therefore, this approach is compatible with both rigid and deformable bodies.
We mention that a similar approach was developed in
[
40
,
41
]
for deformable ferroelectrics, in
[
42
]
for elastic-plastic materials and in
[
43
,
44
]
for viscoelastic and viscoplastic materials.

We consider both local and (weakly) nonlocal models of heat conduction.
Constitutive local theories have to satisfy the classical local formulation of the Second Law where
the specific entropy production
\sigma
is prescribed as a constitutive function in terms of the independent variables and is required to be non-negative in all processes.
Depending on the choice of the set of variables (especially the order of the time derivatives of
{\bf q}
involved) we consider different classes of rate-type models (basic rather than first, second or higher order). Among others, we obtain GNIII and Quintanilla models in their differential form, and their thermodynamic consistency is shown by exhibiting a (non-unique) explicit expressions of
\psi
and
\sigma
. Then, a more general heat flow model inspired by the Burgers fluid is proposed. Its thermodynamic consistency, in terms of inequalities involving the material parameters of the model, is proved by assuming a quadratic form of the free energy.

As for non-local theories, we refer to Green and Laws
[
45
]
who first investigated the restrictions imposed on constitutive equations by a global entropy inequality. According to
[
46
]
we reformulated the Green-Laws pioneering idea by arriving at an entropy equation in which the entropy production density
\rho\sigma
is the sum of two terms; an internal entropy production,
\rho\zeta
, which is required to be non-negative along whatever process, and the divergence of a vector field
{\bf k}
, called
extra entropy flux
, whose flow across the boundary of the body is zero. Both
\zeta
and
{\bf k}
are the object of a constitutive prescriptions in terms of the same independent variables as
\psi
. Among others, linear and nonlinear Guyer-Krumhansl-like heat conduction models are derived using this scheme and their thermodynamic consistency is shown by exhibiting an explicit expressions of
\psi
,
\zeta
and
{\bf k}
. Restrictions imposed on
{\bf q}
and its gradient by the no-flow boundary condition for
\kappa
are discussed. In our opinion these conditions provide an important suggestion for the choice of the most appropriate boundary conditions in applications.
Finally, a connection with the evolution equation of the heat flux in the framework of extended irreversible
thermodynamics (see
[
35
]
) is established.

2.
Balance laws and the thermodynamic principles

We consider a body occupying the time dependent region
\Omega_{t}\subset{\mathcal{E}}^{3}
.
The motion is described by function
\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t)
providing the position vector
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{t}
in terms of the position vector
{\bf X}
, in a reference configuration
{\mathcal{R}}
, and the time
t
, so that

{\bf x}=\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t),\qquad\Omega_{t}=\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\mathcal{R}},t).

The deformation is described by means of the deformation gradient

{\bf F}({\bf X},t)=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t),\qquad(\hbox{in suffix notation }F_{iK}=\partial_{X_{K}}\chi_{i}),

satisfying the constraint
J:=\det{\bf F}>0
.
Here,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,:=\partial_{\bf X}
denotes the gradient in the reference configuration
{\mathcal{R}}
, whereas the symbol
\nabla:=\partial_{\bf x}
denotes the gradient in the current configuration
\Omega
. For any regular vector field
{\bf w}({\bf x},t)
, they are related as follows

\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\hat{\bf w}=\nabla{\bf w}{\bf F},\qquad\nabla{\bf w}=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\hat{\bf w}{\bf F}^{-1},

where
\hat{\bf w}={\bf w}\big(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t),t\big)
. In addition, using the Nanson’s formula, we have

(1)

\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot\hat{\bf w}=J\nabla\cdot\left(J^{-1}{\bf F}{\bf w}\right),\qquad\nabla\cdot{\bf w}=J^{-1}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot(J{\bf F}^{-1}\hat{\bf w}).

Hereafter, a superposed dot denotes the standard derivative with respect to time. In particular,

\dot{f}({\bf X},t):=\partial_{t}f({\bf X},t),\qquad\dot{g}({\bf x},t):=\frac{\,d}{dt}g(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t),t)=\partial_{t}g({\bf x},t)+({\bf v}\cdot\nabla)g({\bf x},t),

where
f
and
g
are scalar-, vector- or tensor-valued differentiable functions of the reference and current position, respectively. The velocity field
{\bf v}({\bf x},t)
is such that
{\bf v}(\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t),t)=\partial_{t}\mbox{\boldmath$\chi$}({\bf X},t)
and the velocity gradient
{\bf L}:=\nabla{\bf v}
is related to
\dot{\bf F}
as follows

(2)

{\bf L}=\dot{\bf F}{\bf F}^{-1}.

Let
\varepsilon
be the internal energy density (per unit mass),
{\bf T}
the Cauchy stress,
{\bf q}
the heat flux vector,
\rho
the mass density,
r
the (external) heat supply and
{\bf b}
the mechanical body force per unit mass.
The local form of the linear momentum and internal energy balance equations can be written as

(3)

\rho\dot{{\bf v}}=\nabla\cdot{\bf T}+\rho{\bf b},

(4)

\rho\dot{\varepsilon}={\bf T}\cdot{\bf L}-\nabla\cdot{\bf q}+\rho r.

The total energy balance, also named First Law of continuum thermodynamics, follows from taking the dot product of (
3
) by
{\bf v}
and then adding the result to (
4
),

\rho\big[\tfrac{1}{2}|{\bf v}|^{2}+{\varepsilon}\big]^{\cdot}=-\nabla\cdot\big[{\bf q}-{\bf T}{\bf v}\big]+\rho[{\bf b}\cdot{\bf v}+r].

Balance of entropy.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
be any convecting subregion of
\Omega_{t}
.
All processes which are compatible with the balance equations (
3
)-(
4
) must satisfy the following integral equation,

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{{\mathcal{P}}_{t}}\rho\,\eta\,dv=-\int_{\partial{\mathcal{P}}_{t}}{{\bf h}}\cdot{\bf n}\,da+\int_{{\mathcal{P}}_{t}}\rho s\,dv,

where
\eta
is the entropy density and
s
the entropy supply (per unit mass),
{{\bf h}}
the entropy-flux vector,
{\bf n}
the outward normal to the boundary.

This general statement has been adopted by Müller
[
47
]
by allowing the entropy flux
{\bf h}
to be an unknown function.
The classical form of the Second Law, usually named after Clausius-Duhem, is obtained by letting

{{\bf h}}=\frac{{\bf q}}{\theta},\qquad s=\frac{r}{\theta}+\sigma,

where
\theta
denotes the (positive) absolute temperature and the quantity
\sigma
is referred to as
specific entropy production

[
39
,
48
]
.
Exploiting the arbitrariness of the convecting domain
{\mathcal{P}}_{t}
, we obtain the local form of the entropy balance,

(5)

\rho\dot{\eta}+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf q}}{\theta}\right)-\frac{\rho r}{\theta}=\rho\sigma.

Within continuum thermodynamics two versions of the Second Law can be established depending on the assumptions about
\sigma
.

Second Law of Thermodynamics (local form).

Along all compatible processes the specific entropy production
\sigma
is nonnegative,

(6)

\sigma({\bf x},t)\geq 0.

Owing to (
5
) and (
6
), this statement leads to the classical form of the Clausius-Duhem inequality

\rho\dot{\eta}+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf q}}{\theta}\right)-\frac{\rho r}{\theta}\geq 0.

A more general formulation was introduced and discussed in
[
45
]
; it can be summarized as follows.

Second Law of Thermodynamics (nonlocal weak form).

The specific entropy production
\sigma
is
assumed to satisfy the integral condition

(7)

\int_{\Omega_{t}}\rho\,\sigma\,dv\geq 0.

This statement is weaker than (
6
) because the inequality is assumed to be valid only over the entire domain
\Omega_{t}
and not point-wise.

Now, let
\zeta
be a nonnegative unknown field and
{\bf k}
be an unknown regular field, usually called
entropy extra flux
, such that

\int_{\Omega_{t}}\nabla\cdot{\bf k}\,dv=\int_{\partial\Omega_{t}}{\bf k}\cdot{\bf n}\,da=0,

which means that the net extra flux across the whole body boundary is zero. This condition is also relevant from a physical point of view since the flux of
{\bf k}
at the boundary is not an observable quantity.
Then, condition (
7
) can be satisfied by letting

(8)

\rho\sigma=\rho\zeta-\nabla\cdot{\bf k},\qquad\int_{\Omega_{t}}\rho\,\zeta({\bf x},t)\,dv\geq 0.

We stress that equation (
8
)
1
could be interpreted as a microscopic local balance at any point
{\bf x}\in\Omega_{t}
, where the entropy production density
\sigma
is due to a
local entropy supply

\zeta
and an internal entropy flux
{\bf k}
that is exchanged on a microscopic level with the surrounding points. The idea to introduce the extra entropy flux traces back to and is discussed in
[
46
]
.

Hereafter, the validity of the Second Law is ensured by the following stronger formulation.

Second Law of Thermodynamics (nonlocal strong form).

During all compatible processes the entropy extra flow at any point of the body boundary is zero and the local entropy supply at any point of the body interior is nonnegative, namely

(9)

{\bf k}\cdot{\bf n}\big|_{\partial\Omega_{t}}=0,\quad\zeta\geq 0\quad\hbox{in }\Omega_{t}.

Consequently, after replacing (
8
)
1
into (
5
) it follows

(10)

\rho\dot{\eta}+\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{{\bf q}}{\theta}+{\bf k}\right)-\frac{\rho r}{\theta}=\rho\zeta\geq 0,

that resembles the classical entropy inequality except that the flux vector is redefined by adding the extra contribution
{\bf k}
. This suggests that
{\bf k}
is a characteristic nonlocal contribute and it must disappear when only local processes are involved.

Henceforth, we assume that
{\bf k}
and
\zeta
are given as constitutive functions of the assigned set of variables, as well as the internal energy and the entropy. The idea that the entropy production
\sigma
be given by a constitutive equation traces back to Green and Naghdi

[
16
]
, however they do not require that
\sigma\geq 0
along whatever process. This idea was extended in
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
assuming from the outset that all constitutive functions depend on the common set of physical variables and imposing
\sigma\geq 0
. Conceptually our contribution follows the same scheme (see also
[
49
]
).

Upon substitution of
\nabla\cdot{\bf q}-\rho r
from the energy equation (
4
) into (
10
) and multiplication by
\theta
we obtain the basic thermodynamic relation

(11)

-\rho(\dot{\psi}+\eta\dot{\theta})+{\bf T}\cdot{\bf L}-\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}\cdot\nabla\theta+\theta\nabla\cdot{\bf k}=\rho\theta\zeta,

where
\psi=\varepsilon-\theta\eta
denotes the
Helmholtz free energy density
.
Within the isothermal setting, the term
\rho\theta\zeta
is usually referred to as
rate of (mechanical) dissipation

[
50
]
.
Due to (
9
), equation (
11
) becomes an inequality that must be satisfied along whatever process.

Finally, multiplying (
11
) by
J
and using the identities (
1
), (
2
) and
\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=J\rho
,
we obtain the the basic thermodynamic inequality in the material description

(12)

\begin{split}-{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\big(\dot{\psi}+\eta\dot{\theta}\big)+{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}-\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\theta\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\theta\zeta\geq 0,\end{split}

where
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,:={\bf F}^{T}\nabla
and

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}:=J{\bf F}^{-1}{\bf T}{\bf F}^{-T},\qquad{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=J{\bf F}^{-1}{\bf q},\qquad{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=J{\bf F}^{-1}{\bf k},\quad{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot{\bf n}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\big|_{\partial{\mathcal{R}}}=0.

3.
Local theories of heat conduction

In the first part of this paper we restrict our attention to
local
theories, that is we assume
{\bf k}=0
and then
\sigma=\zeta\geq 0
.
Consequently (
12
) reduces to

(13)

-{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\dot{\psi}+\eta\dot{\theta})+{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}-\frac{1}{\theta}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma\geq 0.

In recent papers
[
42
,
43
,
44
]
several local models of viscoelastic, viscoplastic and elastic-plastic materials (solids and fluids) are developed on the basis of (
13
) by specifying three elements;

-

the set
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
of admissible variables,

-

the free energy density function
\psi=\psi(\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
,

-

the entropy production function
\sigma=\sigma(\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
.

Those papers mainly scrutinized the mechanical characteristics of materials deriving quite general stress-strain constitutive equations from the Clausius-Duhem inequality.

Likewise, thermodynamically consistent constitutive equations of the rate type are devised here for heat-conduction in solids. The key idea is to exploit the formal similarity of the scalar products

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\dot{{\bf E}},\qquad-{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\ln\theta)

that represent the mechanical and thermal powers of internal forces, respectively. In
[
43
,
44
]
the exploitation of the thermodynamic inequality involving the mechanical power leads to some well-known viscoelastic models. By mimicking such a procedure, the exchange of
{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
with
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
, as well as that of
\dot{\bf E}
with
-\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\ln\theta)
, give rise to as many models of heat conduction.

3.1.
Basic local models

The simplest local models can be obtained by choosing a small set of independent variables, namely the temperature field and its gradient along with a strain measure and its rate. Since invariance requirements demand that the dependence on time and space derivatives occurs in an objective way, we let

\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf E},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\dot{\bf E})

be the basic set of invariant variables. Accordingly, Euclidean invariance of
\psi,\eta
and
\sigma
implies that their dependence be a function of
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
.
Here and below, we assume
\eta
is continuous while
\psi
is continuously differentiable.

Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf E}}\psi\cdot\ddot{{\bf E}}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma.

Due to the constitutive assumption on

Representation Lemma.

Given a vector (or a tensor)

{\mathsf{M}}

with

{\bf N}=\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{M}}$}\dot{{\bf E}}/|\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{M}}$}\dot{{\bf E}}|,\qquad{\bf Z}=\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{M}}$}^{-1}[{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\bf E}\psi],\qquad{\bf G}={\bf 0},

we obtain the nonlinear constitutive equation

{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\bf E}\psi+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\frac{\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{M}}$}^{2}\dot{{\bf E}}}{|\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{M}}$}\dot{{\bf E}}|^{2}}.

In particular, the linear Kelvin-Voigt relation follows from (
15
) with the special choice
\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{K}}$}=\mbox{\boldmath${\mathsf{M}}$}^{2}
.

Let
{\bf A}
be a any symmetric, non-singular, second-order tensor, possibly parametrized by the temperature
\theta
. Applying (
17
) with
{\bf N}={\bf A}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta/|{\bf A}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta|
,
{\bf Z}={\bf A}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
, and
{\bf G}={\bf 0}
, from (
14
)
2
it follows that

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\frac{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta^{2}\sigma^{q}}{|{\bf A}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta|^{2}}{\bf A}^{2}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.

In particular, assuming
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}={\bf A}^{2}
and
\sigma^{q}
as in (
16
), we recover Fourier’s law. Note however that in this case
\kappa
turns out to be strictly positive definite because
{\bf A}
is non-singular.

4.
Rate-type local models

To describe rate-type models we expand the basic set of Euclidean invariant variables by adding some quantities that are usually not assumed to be independent, but considered as constitutive functions; specifically,
{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
and
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
. Hence we let

\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf E},{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\dot{{\bf E}})

be the set of independent variables and assume that
\psi
,
\eta
,
\sigma
are scalar-valued functions of
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
.
In view of the introduction of rate-type models, we look for a scheme where
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, as well as
\dot{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
and
\dot{{\bf E}}
, are regarded as mutually dependent variables
2
2

2

A similar approach occurs in anholonomic system described by a set of independent parameters subject to differential constraints that make their rates mutually dependent.
.

Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
13
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\cdot\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{{\bf E}}}\psi\cdot\ddot{{\bf E}}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=&-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma.\end{split}

The linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
,
\ddot{{\bf E}}
,

\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
, imply that

(18)

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf E},{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi.

and the thermodynamic inequality reduces to

(19)

(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\cdot\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma\leq 0.

We can say that the arguments of
\psi
in (
18
) provide the set of
state variables
,

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\theta,{\bf E},{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}).

Now, let
\dot{{\bf E}}
and
\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
be independent of
\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
. Since
\psi
is also independent of
\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, then letting
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta={\bf 0}
we can write (
19
) in the form

(20)

(\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\bf E}\psi-{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}})\cdot\dot{{\bf E}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\cdot\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\leq 0,

where
\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
is the entropy production density
\sigma
when
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta={\bf 0}
.
Likewise, assuming that
\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
, and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
are independent of
\dot{{\bf E}},\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
, as usually happens, then

(21)

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma^{q}\leq 0,

where
\sigma^{q}
is the entropy production density when
\dot{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\dot{{\bf E}}={\bf 0}
.
Apparently,
\sigma=\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}+\sigma^{q}
.
The entropy productions
\sigma^{{\scriptscriptstyle E}{\scriptscriptstyle T}}
and
\sigma^{q}
, as well as
\sigma
, are nonnegative constitutive functions of
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
to be determined according to the constitutive model.

By exploiting inequality (
20
) and disregarding heat conduction, memory properties of viscoelasticity, elastoplasticity and viscoplasticity were modeled with suitable nonlinear rate-type stress-strain relations
[
42
,
43
]
.
However, since the aim of this paper is to establish nonlinear rate-type models of heat conduction, we limit our attention to (
21
) and disregard (
20
).
In detail, the dependence of constitutive functions on
{{\bf E}},{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
is neglected so that (
18
) reduces to
\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
.

Although inequality (
21
) is common to many approaches where both
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
are independent variables
[
51
,
52
]
our scheme has the advantage that the material time derivative is objective
[
53
]
and makes consistency with thermodynamics much easier than it happens when heat conduction involves histories
[
54
]
, summed histories
[
6
]
or internal variables
[
55
,
56
]
.

Since (
21
) holds for any pair of functions
\psi
and
\sigma^{q}\geq 0

of the variables
\Xi_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
, below we present some examples by choosing special expressions for these functions. Many of them match with well-known thermal conduction models.

4.1.
Green-Naghdi type II heat conductors

A (linear) type II heat conductor according to Green and Naghdi
[
16
]
is characterized by

(22)

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\alpha,

where
\alpha
is an internal variable, named thermal displacement, that represents, by definition, a time primitive of the temperature, namely
\dot{\alpha}=\theta
. Since this relation enters the heat equation for the temperature
\theta
only after a differentiation with respect to time, namely

\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

it is reasonable to consider this GN II constitutive equation instead of (
22
). Such a rate-type model can be obtained from inequality (
21
) by appealing to the Representation Lemma.

Assuming that
\sigma^{q}=0
eqn. (
21
) becomes

(23)

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.

This condition characterizes heat conductors without entropy intrinsic production, that means without any dissipation.
An explicit relation for
\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
then follows by applying (
17
) with

{\bf N}=\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi/|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|,\qquad{\bf Z}={{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}},\qquad{\bf G}={\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

where
{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}={\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
is an arbitrary second-order tensor-valued function. Using (
23
) and assuming
\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\neq{{\bf 0}}
we have

{\bf Z}\cdot{\bf N}=-\frac{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}

and then

(24)

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\left[\frac{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|^{2}}\right]\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi+({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.

Finally, letting

(25)

\begin{split}{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})&=\frac{1}{\rho_{{}_{R}}\theta|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}{\bf N}\otimes{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}-({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\\
&=-{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\frac{1}{\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|^{2}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\otimes({\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}/\theta+\rho_{{}_{R}}{{\bf J}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{T}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi),\end{split}

we can write (
24
) in the more compact form,

(26)

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.

Owing to the arbitrariness of
{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
there are infinitely many tensors
{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}
compatible with a given free energy
\psi
. Moreover,
{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}
need not be positive-definite or symmetrical and this is a notable difference from the conductivity tensor in Fourier-like models.

Thanks to the aforementioned correspondence between
{\bf T}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}},\dot{\bf E}
and
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\ln\theta)
, respectively, an heat conduction theory of this type matches hypoelasticity. Therefore, many achievements from that field can be borrowed here (see
[
42
]
).
As an example, let
\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
depend on
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
via
\xi=|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n}
,
n\geq 2
. Hence

\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=n\,\partial_{\xi}\psi\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\quad\partial_{\xi}\psi\neq 0,

and (
25
) becomes

\begin{split}{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}=-{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\frac{1}{\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\xi}\psi\,n|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\otimes\Big(\frac{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}{\theta}+n\,\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\xi}\psi\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}\,{{\bf J}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{T}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\Big).\end{split}

Finally, choosing

{{\bf J}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\frac{1}{n\,\theta\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\xi}\psi\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}}{\bf 1}

we obtain

{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}=-{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\frac{1}{n\,\theta\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\xi}\psi\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}}{\bf 1}

and then

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\frac{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}{n\,\theta\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\xi}\psi\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}}.

On the contrary, if the constitutive relation is given in advance, for instance

(27)

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\hat{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

\hat{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}
being a fixed second-order tensor, one can ask whether this model is thermodynamically consistent.
Upon substitution into (
23
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot\hat{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\end{split}

and the arbitrariness of
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
implies

(28)

{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}=-\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\theta\hat{\bf K}^{T}_{{}_{R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi.

If we replace (
28
) into (
25
) we obtain

\begin{split}{\bf K}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}&={\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\frac{1}{|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|^{2}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\otimes[(\hat{\bf K}^{T}_{{}_{R}}-{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{T})\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi].\end{split}

Then, by letting
{\bf J}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\hat{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}
, the identity
{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}=\hat{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}
follows. We infer that
(
27
) is consistent with the Second Law if and only if there exists a free energy
\psi
satisfying (
28
). This is the case provided that
\hat{\bf K}_{{}_{R}}
is a non-singular symmetric constant tensor and the free energy is given by

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2\theta}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\hat{\bf K}^{-1}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

Assuming, for instance,

\hat{\bf K}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=f(\theta,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|){\bf 1},

its thermodynamic consistency is ensured by letting

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{1}{\theta}g(\theta,\xi),\quad\xi=|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n},\ n\geq 2,

provided that

\partial_{\xi}g(\theta,\xi)=\frac{1}{f(\theta,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|)\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}}.

4.2.
Maxwell-Cattaneo-Vernotte-like models

Assume
\sigma^{q}>0
and
\partial_{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\neq{\bf 0}
. Equation (
21
) can then be written in the form

\frac{\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi}{|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\frac{1}{\rho_{{}_{R}}|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}\Big(\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma^{q}\Big).

Eexploiting this relation and applying (
17
) with
{\bf N}=\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi/|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|
,
{\bf Z}={{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

we obtain

(29)

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\Big(\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma^{q}\Big)\frac{{\bf N}}{\rho_{{}_{R}}|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}+({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf G},

where
{\bf G}
is an arbitrary vector-valued function dependent on
(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta)
.

We now show that a class of nonlinear isotropic models of the Maxwell-Cattaneo-Vernotte (MCV) type
[
1
]
,
can be derived from (
29
) by properly choosing
\psi
and
\sigma^{q}
(see, for instance,
[
43
]
).
Let
\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
depend on
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
via
\xi=|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n}
,
n\geq 2
. Hence

\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=n\,\partial_{\xi}\psi\,|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},

and inequality (
21
) becomes

\left(n\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\xi}\psi|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\frac{1}{\theta}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\right)\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma^{q}\leq 0.

Let

\sigma^{q}=\frac{|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta^{2}\kappa},

where
\kappa
is a positive-valued scalar function. In view of (
29
), if
\partial_{\xi}\psi\neq 0
it follows

n\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\xi}\psi|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2}\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\frac{1}{\theta}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\frac{1}{\kappa\theta}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

Consequently,

\tau\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\kappa\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\qquad\tau=\kappa n\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\partial_{\xi}\psi|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{n-2},

can be viewed as a MCV equation with
\tau

playing the role of relaxation time and
\kappa
representing the heat conductivity. If
n=2
then

\tau=2\kappa\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\partial_{\xi}\psi.

In this case,
\tau
reduces to a function of the temperature alone provided that
\kappa=\kappa(\theta)
and
\psi
is a linear function of
\xi=|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}
.

In addition, a more general class of anisotropic models of the MCV type can be derived from (
29
). To this end let

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\tau}{2\theta}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\qquad\sigma^{q}=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta^{2}}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

where
\kappa
must be a positive-definite second-order tensor in order to have
\sigma^{q}\geq 0
. In view of (
29
) it follows

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\frac{1}{\tau}(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}{\bf N}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot{\bf N}){{\bf N}}+({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf G}={\bf G}-\frac{1}{\tau}{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\tau{\bf G}),

where
{\bf N}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}/|\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{-1}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|
, and letting
\tau{\bf G}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
we obtain

(30)

\tau{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.

The sign of
\tau
is not prescribed by thermodynamic arguments. However, the common assumption
\tau>0
implies that
\psi
has a minimum at
{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}={\bf 0}
. In this case
\tau
is called
relaxation time
in that we recover the Fourier law when
\tau\to 0
.

5.
Higher-order rate-type local models

As we are interested in heat conduction only, hereafter we neglect all variables involving stress and strain.
Accordingly, to describe local effects of higher order in time we expand the previously considered set
(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta)
by adding first-order time derivatives of these variables.
Hence we let

\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,\dot{\theta},{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta})

be the set of independent and invariant variables. Moreover, let
\psi,\eta,\sigma
be dependent on
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
3
3

3

Hereafter we neglect the superscript
q
in
\sigma^{q}
.
. Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
13
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\theta}}\psi\cdot\ddot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma.\end{split}

The linearity and arbitrariness of
\ddot{\theta}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\ddot{\theta}
imply that
\psi
is independent of
\dot{\theta}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
,
Assuming, for simplicity, that
\sigma
is also independent of
\dot{\theta}
, the linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
imply

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi,

so that
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta)
and the entropy inequality reduces to

(31)

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma\leq 0.

In the light of the rate-type models considered below,
\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
must be regarded as dependent on
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
.
A similar procedure has been adopted in
[
43
]
to evaluate the thermodynamic consistency of some non-linear viscoelastic models of the rate-type, such as the Oldroyd-B and Burgers’ fluids.

5.1.
Heat conductors of the Jeffreys type

The constitutive equation of a heat conductor of the Jeffreys type is given by
4
4

4

{\rm Sym}^{+}
denotes the set of symmetric and positive-definite tensors of the second order.

(32)

\tau\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta},\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}.

where
\tau>0
is referred to as
relaxation time
. This model can also be written as a system

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+{\bf y}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\qquad\tau\dot{\bf y}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf y}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=(\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$})\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta.

In this way we can interpret the Jeffreys model as the constitutive law of the heat flux vector
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
in a mixture of two different conductors,
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}={\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}
, one of the Fourier type,
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(1)}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(1)}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(1)}=\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}
, the other of the MCV type,
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{(2)}={\bf y}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}^{(2)}=\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}
. A similar result has been obtained in
[
32
]
within the framework of classical irreversible thermodynamics.

In the limits as
\tau\to 0^{+}
and
\tau\to+\infty
we have, respectively,

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\simeq-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\qquad\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\simeq-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}.

Furthermore, when
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}={\bf 0}
the anisotropic MCV model (
30
) is recovered.

The rate equation (
32
) can be derived from (
31
) by paralleling the procedure devised in
[
43
, § 8.2]
for the Oldroyd-B fluid.
After neglecting the dependence of
\psi
on
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
, (
31
) becomes

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma.

This equality holds for any pair of functions
\psi
and
\sigma
, where the former depends on the variables
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
, whereas the latter depends on the whole set
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and is subject to the thermodynamic condition
\sigma\geq 0
.

The selection
{\bf Z}=\dot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
and
{\bf N}=\partial_{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi/|\partial_{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi|
into the representation formula (
17
) together with the exploitation of (

\dot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\Big[\frac{1}{\theta}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\theta}}\,\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma\Big]\frac{\partial_{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|\partial_{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi|^{2}}+({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf G},

where
{\bf G}
is an arbitrary second-order tensor-valued function of
\Xi_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
.

Let
{\rm Sym}^{*}
denotes the set of symmetric and non singular second-order tensors and let

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\tau}{2\theta}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta]\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$})^{-1}[{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta],

with
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}^{*}
. After denoting

{\bf Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$})^{-1}\big({\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\big),

we obtain

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=\frac{\tau}{\theta}{\bf Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\qquad\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\theta}}\,\psi=\frac{\tau}{\theta}\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}{\bf Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\qquad{\bf N}=\frac{{\bf Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}{|{\bf Q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|}.

Now we define the entropy production as

A straightforward but boring calculation yields

Taking into account that

\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\beta\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}
,
\beta\geq 0

Jeffrey’s temperature equation

To describe the evolution of temperature alone we restrict our attention to a rigid body. Hence, the energy balance equation reads

(35)

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}\dot{\theta}=-\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}r.

After combining (
32
) with (
35
) we get

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}(\tau\ddot{\theta}+\dot{\theta})=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta})+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}(r+\tau\dot{r}).

The temperature evolution of this type of models depends on the relative size of the material parameters.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume the isotropic case, i.e.
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}=\xi{\bf 1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}=\kappa{\bf 1}
where
\xi,\kappa
are constant, and
let
r=0
(no external heat source). So we get

(36)

\tau\ddot{\theta}+\dot{\theta}=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}\left(\xi\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}\theta+\tau\kappa\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}\dot{\theta}\right).

In
[
33
]
a similar equation, namely eqn.(45), is derived in the framework od classical irreversible thermodynamics with internal variables. Without reasonable justification, it is therein referred to as Guyer-Krumhansl type temperature equation.

Physically, we assume
\tau>0
to avoid a singularity at
t=\infty
. Equation (
36
) is linear and can be solved by the standard techniques, such as the separation of variables. By setting
\theta({\bf X},t)=T(t)Y({\bf X})
we get

\left(\tau\ddot{T}+\dot{T}\right)Y=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}\left(\xi T+\tau\kappa\dot{T}\right)\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}Y,

Then applying the separation of variables we obtain

(37)

-\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}Y=\Lambda Y,

(38)

\tau\ddot{T}+\dot{T}=-\tilde{\Lambda}(\xi T+\tau\kappa\dot{T}),\qquad\tilde{\Lambda}=\frac{\Lambda}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}.

Actually with
\Lambda
here we indicate a set of constants. For a given domain,
\Lambda
denotes some eigenvalue of the Laplacian operator as defined by (
37
) and the boundary conditions imposed to
Y({\bf X})
. Notice that for the most common boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, Robin, etc.) the eigenvalues are countable infinite, let say
\Lambda_{n}
,
n=\mathbb{N}
, non-negative and not bounded by any constant value. Usually, the eigenvalues are ordered to form an ascending sequence,
\Lambda_{n}<\Lambda_{n+1}
.

For any fixed value of
\Lambda_{n}
, the corresponding solution
T_{n}
of (
38
) is given by

T_{n}(t)=C_{1}e^{w_{+}t}+C_{2}e^{w_{-}t},\quad w_{\pm}=\frac{-(\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\kappa+1)\pm\sqrt{(\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\kappa+1)^{2}-4\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\xi\tau}}{2\tau}.

According to Proposition
5.1
, we let
\kappa\geq 0
,
\xi>0
. Consequently, both
w_{+}
and
w_{-}
have negative real part. Indeed, when
\kappa=0
it follows that
w_{\pm}=(-1\pm\sqrt{1-4\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\xi\tau})/{2\tau}
are complex with negative real part provided that
n
is sufficiently large.
For a given small value of
\kappa
there are, in general, a certain number of eigenvalues
\Lambda_{n}
that give rise to complex values of
w_{\pm}
: the number of these eigenvalues increases by decreasing
\kappa
. Depending on the existence of complex values of
w_{\pm}
, the solution
T_{n}
can be oscillatory but modulated by a an exponential decay in time. For a numerical characterization of these properties the reader can see e.g.
[
57
]
. As a last remark, let us notice that the conditions
\kappa\geq 0
and
\xi>0
naturally follow by seeking solutions of (
38
) not blowing up at infinity. Indeed, for any value of
\Lambda_{n}
, if
T_{n}(t)=e^{w_{n}t}
, then
w_{n}
solves the quadratic equation

\tau w_{n}^{2}+(1+\tau\kappa\tilde{\Lambda}_{n})w+\xi\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}=0.

Both solutions of the previous equation has negative real part if and only if all the coefficients have the same sign. Since we let
\tau>0
, we are forced to assume
\xi>0
and
1+\tau\kappa\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}>0
. The last inequality must hold for all values of
n
, namely

\kappa>-{1}/{\tau\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}},

so that taking the limit as
n\to\infty
we get
\kappa\geq 0
.

5.2.
Green-Naghdi type III heat conductors

A type III heat conductor according to Green and Naghdi

is characterized by

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\alpha.

Following the procedure of the previous subsection, to prove the thermodynamic consistency of this model we must provide explicit expressions for

We start by letting

Now, if we define the entropy production as

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\sigma=\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,

a straightforward calculation yields

\frac{1}{\theta}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\theta}}\,\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\sigma=\frac{1}{\theta}\,[\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}]\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}^{-1}\big({\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta\big).

After replacing this result within the representation formula (

) we obtain

\begin{split}\dot{{\bf q}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\big([\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}]\cdot{\bf N}\big){{\bf N}}+({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf G}.\end{split}

Then choosing the arbitrary vector
{\bf G}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta-\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}

The thermodynamic consistency of the GN III model in its differential form (

\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}\in{\rm Sym}^{*}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\kappa$}\in{\rm Sym}^{+}

GN III temperature equation

Joseph-Preziosi temperature equation

Let us briefly discuss the temperature equation corresponding to Burgers-like model. For simplicity we restrict the discussion to the rigid and isotropic case with all the parameters constant. Also, we assume there is no external heat supply. By combining the energy equation (
35
)
with (
57
) we get

\lambda\dddot{\theta}+\tau\ddot{\theta}+\dot{\theta}=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}\left(\mu\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,^{2}\theta+\tau\nu\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,^{2}\dot{\theta}\right).

This third order equation looks like a generalization of the MGT temperature equation. Really, an equation of this form (see
[
7
, eqn.(5.7)]
) had already been obtained by Joseph and Preziosi starting from the linearization of heat conduction with memory according to the Gurtin-Pipkin model
[
6
]
.

Letting
\theta({\bf x},t)=T(t)Y({\bf X})
, we obtain the temperature equation

\left(\lambda\dddot{T}+\tau\ddot{T}+\dot{T}\right)Y=\frac{1}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}\left(\mu T+\tau\nu\dot{T}\right)\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}Y,

and then the equation for
T(t)
reads

(65)

\lambda\dddot{T}+\tau\ddot{T}+\dot{T}=-\tilde{\Lambda}(\mu T+\tau\nu\dot{T}),\qquad\tilde{\Lambda}=\frac{\Lambda}{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}},

where
\tilde{\Lambda}
is the set of all eigenvalues
\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, that we remember must be non-negative and unbounded in order to comply with the given boundary conditions.
If
T(t)=e^{wt}
, then
w
solves the cubic equation

(66)

\lambda w^{3}+\tau w^{2}+(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)w+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\mu=0,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

To avoid blow up of solutions at large time, we seek only for decaying or oscillating solutions to equation (
65
). Accordingly, we look at the conditions giving all roots with negative real part. By applying the Routh-Hurwitz criterion, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we get

(67)

\lambda>0,\;\tau>0,\ 1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu>0,\ \tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\mu>0,\ \tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(\tau^{2}\nu-\lambda\mu)>0.

or

(68)

\lambda<0,\ \tau<0,\ 1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu<0,\ \tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\mu<0,\ \tau+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}(\tau^{2}\nu-\lambda\mu)<0.

Let us look at the compatibility of (
67
) or (
68
) with the conditions about the thermodynamical consistency stated in Proposition
5.4
.

First consider the compatibility of condition i),
\mu>0
,
\lambda<0
and
\tau\nu=0
, with (
68
). In this case the polynomial (
66
) takes the form

\lambda w^{3}+\tau w^{2}+w+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\mu.

and (
68
) is violated in that the coefficients of
w^{3}
and
w
have different sign. We conclude that condition i) is thermodynamically consistent but gives an unphysical model from the point of view of the behavior of the solution, since it diverges for large times.

Then assume condition ii),
\tau\neq 0
,
\mu=0
,
\nu>0
. In this case a root of (

\lambda w^{2}+\tau w+(1+\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}\tau\nu)=0.

The coefficients of the above polynomial must all have the same sign for both of its roots to have a negative real part. This is achieved by assuming either
\lambda>0
,
\tau>0
or

(69)

\lambda<0,\quad\tau<0,\quad 1-\tilde{\Lambda}_{n}|\tau|\nu<0.

The last inequality must be satisfied for all the eigenvalues

\Lambda_{n}
is an ascending sequence and
\nu>0

\Lambda_{1}>0,\qquad\nu>\frac{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}c_{v}}{\Lambda_{1}|\tau|}.

Such a relation is very peculiar since the actual value of

Finally, let us consider the case iii), i.e
\nu\tau\neq 0

\mu>0
and

which leads to an unbounded value of

Summarizing: the Burgers-like model are thermodinamically and dinamically consistent (i.e. the entropy production is nonnegative and all solutions of the temperature equation are bounded and eventually approach equilibrium values) if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

\mu\geq 0,\;\nu>0,\;\lambda>0,\;\tau>0,\;\nu\tau^{2}\geq\lambda\mu.

We excluded the case (

\tau

and

from which it follows

(70)

\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt{\bf q}}\limits^{{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=2.5pt\kern 2.5pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\rm\,{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=2.5pt\kern 2.5pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\rm}}+\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt{\bf q}}\limits^{{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=2.5pt\kern 2.5pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\rm}}+{\bf q}=-\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{s}\nabla\theta-\tau\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{s}(\nabla\theta)^{\scriptscriptstyle{\bigtriangleup}},

where

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{s}:=J^{-1}{\bf F}\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}{\bf F}^{T},\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{s}:=J^{-1}{\bf F}\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}{\bf F}^{T}

are the viscosity tensors in the spatial description. In particular, when

and

are isotropic, namely
\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}=\mu{\bf 1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}=\nu{\bf 1}
, then

\mbox{\boldmath$\mu$}_{s}=J^{-1}\mu{\bf B},\qquad\mbox{\boldmath$\nu$}_{s}=J^{-1}\nu{\bf B},\qquad{\bf B}:={\bf F}{\bf F}^{T}.

Neglecting that the viscosity tensors depend on
{\bf F}
, (

) represents a linear rate-type constitutive equation for the heat flux vector in the spatial description. Note that all time derivatives involved there are objective.

6.
Weakly nonlocal theories of heat conduction

Nonlocal effects in generalized equations for heat conduction are of particular interest in systems where the mean free path is comparable to (or bigger than) the characteristic volume size, i.e., where the Knudsen number is equal or greater than one. This may occur when considering nanosystems (the size of the system is comparable to the mean free path) or studying propagation of phonons at low temperatures or in rarefied systems (the mean free path is comparable to the size of the system).

We focus here on
non-local
theories by assuming
\sigma=\zeta+\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
subject to
\zeta\geq 0
and
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot{\bf n}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|_{\partial{\mathcal{R}}}=0
, in agreement with the nonlocal statement of the Second Law (
9
).
As in the previous sections, we neglect the dependence on
{{\bf E}}
and
{{\bf T}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}{\scriptscriptstyle R}}
. Hence (
12
) reduces to

(71)

-{\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\dot{\psi}+\eta\dot{\theta})-\frac{1}{\theta}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\theta\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\zeta\geq 0.

Weakly nonlocal models are developed here on the basis of four elements,

-

the set
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
of admissible variables;

-

the free energy density function
\psi=\psi(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R})
;

-

the internal entropy production function
\zeta=\zeta(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R})

-

the extra entropy flux function
{\bf k}={\bf k}(\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R})

6.1.
Basic nonlocal models

To describe weakly nonlocal effects in heat conduction, we add
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
to the set of variables.
Hence,

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}).

is the basic set of independent variables and
\psi,\eta
,
\sigma
and
{\bf k}
are assumed to
depend on
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
. In addition, we assume
\eta
is continuous while
\psi
is continuously differentiable.
Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
71
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\theta\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\theta\zeta\leq 0.\end{split}

The linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
,
\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
imply that

\psi=\psi(\theta),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi,

and (
71
) reduces to

(72)

\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta^{2}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta\leq 0.

For definiteness, we exhibit a nonlocal perturbation of the Fourier law which is consistent with the Second Law in the form (
72
). According to
[
31
]
we let the following macroscopic constitutive equation,

(73)

{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\kappa\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\lambda^{2}[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})],

where
\kappa(\theta)
is the usual bulk thermal conductivity and
\lambda^{2}(\theta)
is a (possibly small) positive function. To prove its thermodynamic consistency we let
\psi=\psi(\theta)
and

(74)

\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta=\frac{\ell^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+\ell^{2}\big[|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+2|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}\big],\quad{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\ell^{2}\big[{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}){\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\big].

where
\ell
is an undetermined constant parameter. After computing

\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\ell^{2}\Big[|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+2|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+[\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})]\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\Big]

and noticing that
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,=\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}
, from (
72
) and (
74
) it follows

(75)

\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta^{2}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=-\frac{\ell^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+{\ell^{2}}\big[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})\big]\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}.

So, after taking
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta
from equation (
73
) and substituting it into (
75
) we obtain

\Big[\frac{1}{\kappa\theta^{2}}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\Big]{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\Big[-{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\lambda^{2}[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})]\Big]=0.

This means that (
73
) is thermodynamically consistent provided that
\kappa
and
\lambda
are related by

\kappa(\theta)=\frac{\lambda^{2}(\theta)}{\ell^{2}\theta^{2}}.

Since
\ell
is an arbitrary constant parameter, it is then sufficient that
\kappa
is positive and proportional to
\lambda^{2}/\theta^{2}
.

6.2.
Rate-type nonlocal models

In this case the set of variables is allowed to contain the time derivative of the heat flux vector,

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}:=(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta,\dot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})

and constitutive functions for
\psi,{\bf k}
and
\zeta
depend on
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
.
Upon evaluation of
\dot{\psi}
and substitution in (
13
) we obtain

\begin{split}\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}(\partial_{\theta}\psi+\eta)\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\ddot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}&\\
+\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}}\psi\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\frac{1}{\theta}{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\nabla\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}&=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta.\end{split}

The linearity and arbitrariness of
\dot{\theta}
,
\ddot{{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
,
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\theta}
and
\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
imply that

\psi=\psi(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}),\qquad\eta=-\partial_{\theta}\psi,

so that the Clausius-Duham inequality reduces to

(76)

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}+\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\nabla\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta\leq 0.

6.2.1.
Guyer-Krumhansl conductors

Guyer and Krumhansl
[
30
]
studied the heat wave propagation in dielectric crystals at low temperature. They observed that in the regime of low temperature the heat flux
{\bf q}
is proportional to the momentum flux of the phonon gas and then found the following macroscopic equation governing its evolution

(77)

\tau\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\kappa\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta=\lambda^{2}[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})],

where
\tau
is the relaxation time for resistive phonon scattering,
\lambda^{2}
is the mean-free path of phonons and
\kappa
is the usual bulk thermal conductivity
[
60
]
. Both
\lambda
and
\kappa
possibly depend on
\theta
.
Hereafter we prove the consistency of this linear equation with the Second Law in the nonlocal form (
76
).

Assuming
\partial_{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\psi\neq{\bf 0}
equation (
76
) can be written in the form

\frac{\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi}{|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}\cdot{\dot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=-\frac{\theta}{\rho_{{}_{R}}|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}\Big(\frac{{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}{\theta^{2}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta+\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta+\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\Big),

and applying (
17
) with
{\bf N}=\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi/|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|
,
{\bf Z}={{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}

we obtain

(78)

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}=\Big({{\bf q}_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}}\cdot\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\frac{1}{\theta}-\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta-\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\Big)\frac{\theta{\bf N}}{\rho_{{}_{R}}|\partial_{{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi|}+({\bf 1}-{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}){\bf G},

where
{\bf G}
is an arbitrary vector-valued function dependent on
(\theta,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R},\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\theta)
. Now we let

(79)

\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi=\rho_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\psi_{0}(\theta)+\frac{\tau\theta}{2\varkappa(\theta)}|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2},\quad\rho_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\zeta=\frac{1}{\varkappa(\theta)}|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+\ell^{2}|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}+2\ell^{2}|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2},

{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=-\ell^{2}[{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}){\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}].

where
\ell
is a constant parameter and
\varkappa
a positive function.
Noting that

[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R})]\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}=\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot[{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}+2(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}){\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}]-|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2}-2|\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|^{2},

the representation formula (
78
) gives

{{\dot{\bf q}}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}={\bf G}+\frac{1}{\tau}{\bf N}\otimes{\bf N}\Big(\varkappa(\theta)\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\frac{1}{\theta}-{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\ell^{2}\varkappa(\theta)[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q})]-\tau{\bf G}\Big),

where
{\bf N}={\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}/|{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}|
. Finally, letting

\tau{\bf G}=\varkappa(\theta)\Big(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\frac{1}{\theta}-{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}+\ell^{2}[\nabla_{\scriptscriptstyle R}^{2}{\bf q}+2\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q})]\Big)

we obtain (
77
) provided that

(80)

\lambda^{2}(\theta)=\ell^{2}\varkappa(\theta),\qquad\kappa(\theta)=\varkappa(\theta)/\theta^{2}.

These conditions reveal that temperature-dependent material parameters of the GK model are functionally connected. Notably, this can also be considered a non-trivial consequence of the
Onsagerian relation
(see, e.g.,
[
61
, eqn.7]
).

When
\tau
is negligible then equation (
77
) reduces to (
73
).
If this is the case, the free energy
\psi
is independent of
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
. However, from the comparison of (
74
) and (
79
) we observe that
\zeta
and
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
have the same expression, whether
\tau=0
or
\tau>0
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Since
\ell
and
\varkappa(\theta)
are undetermined, (
77
) is thermodynamically consistent with the nonlocal statement (
76
) of the Second Law provided that
\kappa(\theta)
is proportional to

and the nonlinear boundary condition

{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot{\bf n}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\Big|_{\partial{\mathcal{R}}}={\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}{\partial n_{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\Big|_{\partial{\mathcal{R}}}+2(\nabla\!_{{\scriptscriptstyle R}}\,\cdot{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}){\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\cdot{\bf n}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}\Big|_{\partial{\mathcal{R}}}=0

In the seminal paper
[
30

steady one-dimensional solutions are derived in a cylinder assuming

on its boundary, a condition that certainly implies (

[

in connection with steady solutions in nanowires.
It is worth noting that (

This condition allows an axially symmetric profile of heat flux in a cylindrical pipe, a profile reminiscent of Hagen-Poiseuille flow for viscous Newtonian fluids.

7.
Conclusions

This paper is devoted to develop a general constitutive scheme within continuum thermodynamics to describe the behavior of heat flow in deformable media. Starting from a classical thermodynamic approach in the material (Lagrangian) description, a wide class of rate-type constitutive equations for the heat flux vector
{\bf q}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
are obtained. Their thermodynamic consistency with the Second Law in the local form (
13
) is established by exhibiting functional expressions of the specific free energy
\psi
and entropy production rate
\sigma
. Some nonlinear anisotropic models are generated by means of a suitable Representation Lemma. For instance, heat conduction models with zero dissipation (
26
), nonlinear Maxwell-Cattaneo-Vernotte-like equations (
29
) and anisotropic equations of the Jeffreys type (
32
). For each of these,
restrictions on the material parameters are derived to ensure non-negativity of entropy production.

Linear models involving the notion of “thermal displacement” (such as Green-Naghdi and Quintanilla constitutive equations) are reformulated here as equations of the rate-type, namely (
39
) and (
43
), and their free energy and entropy production are explicitly determined as quadratic functions. When Quintanilla’s model is considered, such quadratic functions are non trivial;
we also note that the necessary and sufficient condition on the material parameters for entropy production
\sigma
to be non-negative (see Proposition
5.3
) is equivalent to the estimate
iii)
of
[
23
]
which guarantees the stability of the thermoelastic problem.
A new linear model, inspired by the constitutive equation of a Burgers fluid, is proposed considering a mixture of two different substances both characterized by a Maxwell-Cattaneo type heat exchange mechanism. This results in the rate equation (
55
) involving the second time derivative of the heat flux; its thermodynamic consistency is proved in Proposition
5.4
. For rigid bodies the corresponding third order temperature equation (
64
) was first proposed by Joseph and Preziosi in
[
7
]
based on the linearization of the Gurtin-Pipkin model.

Finally, linear and nonlinear Guyer-Krumhansl heat conduction models are derived using the Representation Lemma.
Their thermodynamic consistency with the Second Law in the nonlocal form (
12
) is established by exhibiting explicit expressions of the specific free energy
\psi
, entropy production rate
\zeta
and extra entropy flux
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
.
We believe this is the first demonstration that the Guyer-Krumhansl equation can be derived in the framework of classical thermodynamics provided that
material parameters depend on temperature. It is worth noting that necessary and sufficient conditions for entropy production
\zeta
to be non-negative imply their mutual functional dependence (see (
80
) and (
84
) in the linear and nonlinear case, respectively).
Notably, this dependence can also be obtained within different frameworks (see, e.g.,
[
61
]
).
Restrictions imposed on
{\bf q}
and its gradient by the no-flow boundary condition (
9
) for
{\bf k}_{\scriptscriptstyle R}
are discussed. The resulting analysis carries important suggestions for the choice of the most appropriate boundary conditions in applications.
In Remark
6.1
, a connection with the evolution equation of the heat flux in the framework of extended irreversible
thermodynamics is established (see
[
35
,
55
]
).

A final remark is in order. In connection with the same heat conduction model (local or non-local) different schemes are possible.
As shown with the Jeffreys-type equation (
32
), for instance, different free energy functions
\psi
and entropy productions
\sigma
are allowed with the same conclusion (see Remark
5.1
). This in turn means that the free energy and the entropy production need not be unique for a given rate equation.
