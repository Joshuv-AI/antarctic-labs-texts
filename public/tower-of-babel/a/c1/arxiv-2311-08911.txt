Connection Incentives in Cost Sharing Mechanisms with Budgets
Tianyi Zhang, Dengji Zhao, Junyu Zhang, and Sizhe Gu

Abstract
. In a cost sharing problem on a weighted undirected graph, all other nodes want to connect to the source node for some service. Each edge has a cost denoted by a weight and all the connected nodes should share the total cost for the connectivity. The goal of the existing solutions (e.g. folk solution and cycle-complete solution) is to design cost sharing rules with nice properties, e.g. budget balance and cost monotonicity. However, they did not consider the cases that each non-source node has a budget which is the maximum it can pay for its cost share and may cut its adjacent edges to reduce its cost share. In this paper, we design two cost sharing mechanisms taking into account the nodes’ budgets and incentivizing all nodes to report all their adjacent edges so that we can minimize the total cost for the connectivity.



1.
Introduction

Cost sharing problems are popular in economics and computer science
(
Moulin and Shenker 2001
;
Leonardi and Schäfer 2004
;
Lorenzo and Lorenzo-Freire 2009
;
Trudeau and Vidal-Puga 2020
)
. There are a group of agents located at different points and a source. The agents need to connect to the source directly or indirectly in order to get a service or information
(
Moulin 1999
;
Gómez-Rúa and Vidal-Puga 2017
;
Todo and Yokoo 2020
;
Bergantiños et al. 2022
)
. The cost of connecting any pair of agents is known and the total cost of connectivity has to be shared among all connected agents. This setting models many real-world applications from telephone and cable TV to water supply networks
(
Gómez-Rúa and Vidal-Puga 2011
;
Trudeau and Vidal-Puga 2017
;
Trudeau 2023
)
.

To connect with the source, an agent may need to go through other agents. The intermediate agents can strategically block the connection if their cost share is reduced by doing so. However, such strategic behavior will potentially increase the total cost of connecting all agents. Therefore, it is important to design cost sharing mechanisms to prevent such strategic behavior so that we can minimize the total cost of the connectivity.

To solve the problem, we propose a cost sharing mechanism where each agent pays an average marginal cost to connect it to the source under the assumption that each agent’s budget is unlimited. The reason for this assumption is that in some scenarios all the agents have enough budgets to pay their cost share. Since there exist some scenarios where the amount each agent can pay is limited, we also consider the situation where each agent has a limited budget for the share. Then we need to make sure that each agent can afford its cost share. One intuitive idea is that agents with larger budgets cover more costs than the agents with lower budgets. We need to give the agents with larger budgets incentives to do so, otherwise they will disconnect the agents with lower budgets. To that end, we determine their cost share in terms of their savings which are the difference between their budgets and the total cost.

Our contribution:
For cost sharing problems, to incentivize agents to share all their connections, we first design a mechanism where each agent’s budget is unlimited that satisfies truthfulness (each agent is incentivized to offer all its connections) and other desirable properties studied in the literature. However, this mechanism does not satisfy budget feasibility (each agent’s budget is larger than its cost share) when each agent’s budget is limited. Therefore, we propose another mechanism that satisfies budget feasibility, truthfulness and other desirable properties.

2.
Related Work

There is a rich literature for the cost sharing problem
(
Bergantios and Vidal-Puga 2020
;
Bergantiños and Lorenzo 2021
;
Hernández et al. 2023
)
(see the paper
(
Bergantios and Vidal-Puga 2021
)
for a comprehensive overview).

Some treated the problem from the non-cooperative game perspective. Tijs and Driessen
(
Tijs and Driessen 1986
)
proposed the cost gap allocation (CGA) method based on marginal contribution. Bird
(
Bird 1976
)
, Dutta and Kar
(
Dutta and Kar 2004
)
, Norde
et al.

(
Norde et al. 2004
)
, Tijs
et al.

(
Tijs et al. 2006
)
and Hougaard
et al.

(
Hougaard et al. 2010
)
provided cost sharing policies based on the minimum spanning tree
(
Prim 1957
)
of a graph. However, they all cannot prevent the strategic behavior of agents since the agents can change the minimum spanning tree to reduce their cost share.

Others treated the problem from the cooperative game perspective. They are all based on the Shapley value and differ in the definition of the coalition value. Kar
(
Kar 2002
)
proposed the Kar solution and defined the value of a coalition
S
as the minimal cost of connecting all agents of
S
to the source without going through the agents outside of
S
. Bergantiños and Vidal-Puga
(
Bergantiños and Vidal-Puga 2007a
)
proposed the folk solution. They first computed the irreducible cost matrix, and then based on it, they defined the value of a coalition
S
in the same way as the Kar solution. However, the folk solution throws away much information of the original graph. To look for a way to obtain a solution without throwing away as much information as the folk solution, Trudeau
(
Trudeau 2012
)
proposed the cycle-complete solution. They made a less extreme transformation of the cost matrix.

These solutions all assumed that the agents in a coalition cannot use others outside of the coalition to connect to the source. Furthermore, Bergantiños and Vidal-Puga
(
Bergantiños and Vidal-Puga 2007b
)
introduced the optimistic game-based solution, where the value of a coalition
S
is defined as the minimal cost of connecting all agents of
S
to the source given that agents outside of
S
are already connected to the source.

In summary, existing solutions are limited to complete graphs and do not consider the scenario where each agent has a budget to pay its cost share and has a strategic behavior. Therefore, they are inadequate for solving our problem.

3.
The Model

We consider a cost sharing problem to connect all the nodes on a weighted undirected graph
G=\langle V\cup\{s\},E\rangle
. Node
s
is the source to which all the other nodes want to connect. The weight of each edge
(i,j)\in E
denoted by
c_{(i,j)}\geq 0
represents the cost to use the edge to connect
i
with
j
. The total cost of the connectivity has to be shared among all connected nodes except for
s
. Each node
i\in V
has a public budget
B_{i}>0
which is the maximum cost that
i
can pay.

Consider a real scenario of the graph
G=\langle V\cup\{s\},E\rangle
, where
s
represents a power station and each node
i\in V
represents a village. A village can have electricity if there exists an electric transmission line connecting
s
to the village. All villages require electricity and they need to share the total cost of the connectivity. Each village has a budget for the connectivity.

Given the graph, the minimum cost of connecting all the nodes is the weight of a minimum spanning tree. The question here is how they share the minimum cost. We consider a natural strategic behavior that each node except for the source can cut its adjacent edges to share less cost. An edge
(i,j)
cannot be used for the connectivity if either
i
or
j
cuts it. Our goal is to design cost sharing mechanisms to incentivize nodes to share all their adjacent edges so that we can use all the edges to minimize the total cost of the connectivity.

Formally, let
\theta_{i}

(i\in V\cup\{s\})
be the set of
i
’s adjacent edges and it is called
i
’s
type
. Let
\theta=(\theta_{s},\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{|V|})
be the type profile of all nodes including the source
s
. We also write
\theta=(\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})
, where
\theta_{-i}=(\theta_{s},\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{i-1},\theta_{i+1},\cdots,\theta_{|V|})
is the type profile of all nodes except for
i
. Let
\Theta_{i}
be the type space of
i
and
\Theta=\Theta_{s}\times\Theta_{1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{|V|}
be the type profile space of all nodes.

We design cost sharing mechanisms that ask each node to report the set of its adjacent edges that can be used for the connectivity. Let
\theta_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\theta_{i}
be the
report
of
i
, and
\theta^{\prime}=(\theta_{s},\theta_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\theta_{|V|}^{\prime})
be the report profile of all nodes. Note that the source does not behave strategically in this setting. Given a report profile
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, the graph induced by
\theta^{\prime}
is denoted by
G_{\theta^{\prime}}=\langle V\cup\{s\},E_{\theta^{\prime}}\rangle\subseteq\langle V\cup\{s\},E\rangle
, where
E_{\theta^{\prime}}=\{(i,j)|(i,j)\in(\theta_{i}^{\prime}\cap\theta_{j}^{\prime})\}\subseteq E
.

Definition 3.1.

A cost sharing mechanism consists of a node selection policy
g:\Theta\rightarrow 2^{V}
, an edge selection policy
f:\Theta\rightarrow 2^{E}
, and a cost sharing policy
x=(x_{i})_{i\in V}
where
x_{i}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Given a report profile
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
,
g(\theta^{\prime})\subseteq V
is the set of connected nodes,
f(\theta^{\prime})\subseteq E_{\theta^{\prime}}
is the set of selected edges connecting the nodes in
g(\theta^{\prime})
, and
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})
is the cost share of
i
(it will be zero if
i\notin g(\theta^{\prime})
).

For simplicity, we use
(g,f,x)
to denote a cost sharing mechanism. In the following, we introduce the desirable properties of a cost sharing mechanism.

Truthfulness requires that for each node, its cost share for the connectivity is not decreased by cutting its adjacent edges. That is, offering all its adjacent edges minimizes its cost share.

Definition 3.2.

A cost sharing mechanism
(g,f,x)
satisfies
truthfulness
if

x_{i}((\theta_{i},\theta^{\prime}_{-i}))\leq x_{i}((\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}_{-i})),

for all
i\in g(\theta_{i},\theta^{\prime}_{-i})\cap g(\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}_{-i})
, for all
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
, for all
\theta_{i}^{\prime}\in\Theta_{i}
, and for all
\theta_{-i}^{\prime}\in\Theta_{-i}=\Theta_{s}\times\Theta_{1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{i-1}\times\Theta_{i+1}\times\cdots\times\Theta_{|V|}
.

Budget feasibility requires that each node’s cost share is not over its budget.

Definition 3.3.

A cost sharing mechanism
(g,f,x)
satisfies
budget feasibility (BF)
if

x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\leq B_{i}

for all
i\in V
, for all
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
.

Cost monotonicity states that the cost share of a selected node will weakly increase if the cost of one of its adjacent edges increases within the same report profile.

Definition 3.4.

A cost sharing mechanism
(g,f,x)
satisfies
cost monotonicity (CM)
if

x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\leq x_{i}^{+}(\theta^{\prime})

for all
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and for all
i\in g(\theta^{\prime})
, where
x_{i}^{+}(\theta^{\prime})
is the cost share of
i
when the cost of one edge
(i,j)\in\theta_{i}^{\prime}
increases and
i
is still selected.

Budget balance states that the sum of all nodes’ cost share equals the total cost of the selected edges for all report profiles. That is, the mechanism has no profit or loss.

Definition 3.5.

A cost sharing mechanism
(g,f,x)
satisfies
budget balance (BB)
if

\sum_{i\in V}x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{(i,j)\in f(\theta^{\prime})}c_{(i,j)}

for all
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
.

Finally, positiveness states that each node’s cost share should be non-negative.

Definition 3.6.

A cost sharing mechanism
(g,f,x)
satisfies
positiveness
if

x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\geq 0

for all
i\in V
, for all
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
.

In the rest of the paper, we design cost sharing mechanisms to satisfy the above properties.

4.
Cost Sharing with Unlimited Budget

In this section, we first consider the case where each agent’s budget is unlimited, i.e. they can always afford the cost share. We propose a mechanism that satisfies truthfulness, cost monotonicity, budget balance and positiveness.

The key ideas of the mechanism are as follows. First, we compute the minimum cost of connecting any subset of agents to the source. Then we get the marginal cost of adding each agent to a subset of other agents. The cost share of each agent is set to be the average marginal cost considering all possible joining sequences. The mechanism is formally described in Algorithm
1
. A running example is given in Example
4.1
.

4.1.
Properties of AMCM

Theorem 4.2.

The average marginal cost mechanism satisfies truthfulness.

Proof.

When
i
truthfully reports its type, its cost share is

(2)

x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime})\backslash\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(|g(\theta^{\prime})|-|S|-1)!}{|g(\theta^{\prime})|!}\cdot(v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)),

where
\theta^{\prime}=(\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}^{\prime})
.

When
i
misreports its type, its cost share is

(3)

x_{i}(\theta^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime\prime})\backslash\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(|g(\theta^{\prime\prime})|-|S|-1)!}{|g(\theta^{\prime\prime})|!}\cdot(v^{\prime}(S\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(S)),

where
\theta^{\prime\prime}=(\theta_{i}^{\prime},\theta_{-i}^{\prime})
and
v^{\prime}(S)
is the value function of
S
.

According to the definition of truthfulness, we need to show
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\leq x_{i}(\theta^{\prime\prime})
.

(1)

When
g(\theta^{\prime})=g(\theta^{\prime\prime})
,
for any given set
S\subseteq V
, we have

(4)

\frac{|S|!(|g(\theta^{\prime})|-|S|-1)!}{|g(\theta^{\prime})|!}=\frac{|S|!(|g(\theta^{\prime\prime})|-|S|-1)!}{|g(\theta^{\prime\prime})|!}.

When
i
truthfully reports its type, there are two cases for computing
v(S)
.

•

Node
i
is not visited. If
i
misreports its type (i.e.
\theta_{i}^{\prime}\neq\theta_{i}
), then it is still not visited. So we have
v(S)=v^{\prime}(S)
. According to the definition of
v
, we have
v^{\prime}(S\cup\{i\})\geq v(S\cup\{i\})
. Thus it holds that
v^{\prime}(S\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(S)\geq v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
.

•

Node
i
is visited. Then we have
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)=0
. If
i
misreports its type, there are two possibilities.

–

If it is still visited, then
v^{\prime}(S\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(S)=0
.

–

If it is not visited, by the monotonicity of value function, we have
v^{\prime}(S\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(S)\geq 0
.

Therefore, we have
v^{\prime}(S\cup\{i\})-v^{\prime}(S)\geq v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
. Recalling to Equation (
4
), we infer that
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\leq x_{i}(\theta^{\prime\prime})
.

(2)

When
g(\theta^{\prime})\neq g(\theta^{\prime\prime})
, then
g(\theta^{\prime\prime})
is a proper subset of
g(\theta^{\prime})
. For each
S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime\prime})
, an argument similar to the one used in the case of
g(\theta^{\prime})=g(\theta^{\prime\prime})
shows that the corresponding term in Equation (
2
) is less than or equal to that in Equation (
3
). Whereas for each
S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime})\backslash g(\theta^{\prime\prime})
, the corresponding term in Equation (
2
) equals 0. So we have
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})\leq x_{i}(\theta^{\prime\prime})
.

∎

Theorem 4.3.

The average marginal cost mechanism satisfies budget balance.

Proof.

According to the edge selection policy, our mechanism outputs a spanning tree of the graph induced by
g(\theta^{\prime})\cup\{s\}
. Given a report profile
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, by Equation (
1
) and the property of Shapley value, the sum of all nodes’ cost share equals
v(g(\theta^{\prime}))
, i.e.

\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=v(g(\theta^{\prime})).

From the definition of value function,
v(g(\theta^{\prime}))
equals the total cost of minimum spanning tree of the graph induced by
g(\theta^{\prime})\cup\{s\}
, i.e.

v(g(\theta^{\prime}))=\sum_{(i,j)\in f(\theta^{\prime})}c_{(i,j)}.

Thus,

\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{(i,j)\in f(\theta^{\prime})}c_{(i,j)}.

So the proposed mechanism satisfies budget balance.
∎

Theorem 4.4.

The average marginal cost mechanism satisfies cost monotonicity.

Proof.

Given
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
and nodes
i,j\in g(\theta^{\prime})
, the cost share of
i
is

(5)

x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime})\backslash\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(|g(\theta^{\prime})|-|S|-1)!}{|g(\theta^{\prime})|!}\cdot(v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)).

When
c_{(i,j)}
does not change, there are two cases for computing
v(S)
.

•

Node
i
is not visited. If
c_{(i,j)}
increases,
v(S)
does not change, and
v(S\cup\{i\})
increases or does not change. Thus
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
will increase or not change.

•

Node
i
is visited. If
c_{(i,j)}
increases,

–

node
i
is still visited, then
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
does not change.

–

node
i
is not visited, then
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
increases.

In summary, when
c_{(i,j)}
increases,
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)
increases or does not change, and so does
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})
.
∎

Theorem 4.5.

The average marginal cost mechanism satisfies positiveness.

Proof.

Given a report profile
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, for each
i\in V\backslash g(\theta^{\prime})
, we have
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=0
. For each
i\in g(\theta^{\prime})
, by the definition of value function in Algorithm
1
, we get
v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)\geq 0
for each
S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime})
. Therefore, according to Equation (
1
), each node’s cost share
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})
is non-negative.
∎

For the mechanism to satisfy budget feasibility (i.e. the budget of each node should cover its cost share), we design a special node selection function defined in Algorithm

In other words, a node is added to the group of nodes that are connected to the source if its budget covers the minimal cost of the edges connecting to the group. Following this principle, starting from

Next, we compare the AMCM with the SCSM. Under AMCM, each node should share the total cost of the path from itself to the source. Since the AMCM does not consider its budget at all, its cost share might be larger than its budget. Under SCSM, each node’s savings (the difference between its budget and the cost of the edge connecting it to its parent) should be shared among all its ancestors. In the worst case, the savings allocated to a node is zero. Therefore, each node’s cost share is not more than its budget.

Theorem 5.3.

The saving-based cost sharing mechanism satisfies truthfulness.

Proof.

When node

\theta_{i}
(i.e.

.

(1)

When

,

Next, we compare

Theorem 5.5.

The saving-based cost sharing mechanism satisfies budget balance.

Proof.

According to the edge selection policy, our mechanism outputs a spanning tree of the graph induced by
g(\theta^{\prime})\cup\{s\}
. Given a report profile
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, by
x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=B_{i}-\phi_{i}
, we have

\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}B_{i}-\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}\phi_{i}.

By the property of Shapley value, we have

\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}\phi_{i}=v(g(\theta^{\prime})).

Therefore,

\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})=\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}B_{i}-v(g(\theta^{\prime})).

By the definition of mechanism, we get

v(g(\theta^{\prime}))=\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}B_{i}-C(g(\theta^{\prime})).

Thus, we get

\begin{split}\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}x_{i}(\theta^{\prime})&=\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}B_{i}-(\sum_{i\in g(\theta^{\prime})}B_{i}-C(g(\theta^{\prime})))\\
&=C(g(\theta^{\prime}))\\
&=\sum_{(i,j)\in f(\theta^{\prime})}c_{(i,j)},\end{split}

which states that our mechanism satisfies budget balance.

\phi_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq g(\theta^{\prime})\backslash\{i\}}\frac{|S|!(|g(\theta^{\prime})|-|S|-1)!}{|g(\theta^{\prime})|!}\cdot(v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)),

it suffices to show that

decreases or does not change.

•

If
i\notin g(\theta^{\prime})
, then
i
is still not selected by the mechanism. Thus

•

If
i\in g(\theta^{\prime})
, for node

Node
i

–

Node
i

g(\theta^{\prime})

If

6.
Conclusions

For cost sharing problems on graphs, we considered an important strategic behavior of agents, i.e. cutting their adjacent edges. We proposed two mechanisms where each agent has an unlimited budget or a limited budget respectively. Our mechanisms can incentivize all agents to offer all their adjacent edges so that we can minimize the total cost of connectivity by using all the connections. One meaningful future work is to characterize all possible such mechanisms.
