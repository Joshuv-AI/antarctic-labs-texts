Extracting human interpretable structure-property relationships in chemistry using XAI and large language models
Geemi P. Wellawatte, Philippe Schwaller

Abstract
Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is an emerging field in AI that aims to address the opaque nature of machine learning models. Furthermore, it has been shown that XAI can be used to extract input-output relationships, making them a useful tool in chemistry to understand structure-property relationships. However, one of the main limitations of XAI methods is that they are developed for technically oriented users. We propose the XpertAI framework that integrates XAI methods with large language models (LLMs) accessing scientific literature to generate accessible natural language explanations of raw chemical data automatically. We conducted 5 case studies to evaluate the performance of XpertAI. Our results show that XpertAI combines the strengths of LLMs and XAI tools in generating specific, scientific, and interpretable explanations.

Method

We begin the workflow by training an ML model using the initial raw data. This model serves as a surrogate for mapping input to output.
The initial dataframe includes featurized molecular structures and target labels for training.
Note that these features must be human-interpretable (e.g., molecular descriptors/properties or MACCS keys).
We currently employ gradient-boosting decision trees with the XGBoost framework, utilizing the Scikit-learn API for regression and classification tasks.
33
,
34

Once the model is trained, users can select from SHAP
35
, LIME
36
, or both to estimate the “most impactful” features.
SHAP and LIME are commonly used to generate local explanations.
Therefore, we compute mean SHAP values and Z-scores for LIME explanations to extract globally impactful features.
For the LIME analysis, we only use a sample of the initial dataset due to time and resource constraints.
The default sample size is either 500 or the entire dataset if its length is less than 500.
After identifying impactful features, we draw knowledge from the literature to elucidate physicochemical relationships between these features and the target property.
As seen in the overview of our proposed workflow (Figure
1
) LLMs are used to unite the backend modules generating human interpretable explanations.
We leverage on LangChain python package (
https://github.com/langchain-ai/langchain
), OpenAI’s GPT-4
37
language model, and Chroma vector database (
https://github.com/chroma-core/chroma
) to query multiple literature publications and generate intelligent explanations.
Users have the flexibility to upload a literature dataset or scrape
arxiv.org
to gather relevant literature information.
The latter is enabled via the arXiv python API.

Once the literature is collected, we select the most related literature excerpts using maximal marginal relevance search (MMR) to validate XAI observations.
Following a similar approach as the “StuffDocumentsChain” in LangChain, we stuff these excerpts to a specialized prompt to generate the complete explanation.
We utilize the chain-of-thought prompting approach
38
to create the NLEs – we provide a series of intermediate steps and examples within the prompt to improve the output NLE’s interpretability.
XpertAI also generates and adds citations in the final NLEs to improve the accountability of the explanations.
We would like to highlight that, in addition to the NLEs, XpertAI also provides the surrogate model’s evaluation plot (error plot) and XAI analysis plots for the users.
To streamline this complete workflow, we have deployed a Streamlit App (
https://xpert-ai.streamlit.app/
) that can be used with an OpenAI API key.
The GitHub repository of our work can be found at:
https://github.com/geemi725/XpertAI
.

Results

We used the XpertAI tool to suggest structure-property relationships for five case studies in chemistry; 1) the presence of open metal sites in metal-organic frameworks (classification), 2) pore limiting diameter in metal-organic frameworks (regression), 3) toxicity of small molecules (classification), 4) solubility of small molecules (regression), and 5) upper flammability limit of organic molecules (regression).
Please note that we used the SHAP method as the chosen XAI method, and the default hyperparameters to generate NLEs in the following case studies. We chose the SHAP method due to its consistency in generating global explanations in comparison to LIME.
Complete NLEs and SHAP plots for each case study from XpertAI are provided in Appendices A and D respectively.

Evaluation

Human expert consensus. 3 human experts were asked to evaluate explanations from XpertAI, ChatGPT, and SHAP plots for each case study based on accuracy and interpretability. Expert consensus is given as a percentage.

Firstly, to evaluate the explanations for the listed case studies, we compared three different explanations from; 1) XpertAI 2) ChatGPT (GPT-4), and 3) Graphical plots from the XAI analysis (See Appendix D for SHAP plots).
We selected these three setups to evaluate if combining LLMs with XAI can harness the advantages of both approaches rather than using one alone.
We asked three expert chemists to score 15 explanations in total (5 tasks
\times
3 explanations). The experts were given a scorecard (given in Appendix C) to evaluate each answer based on accuracy and interpretability.
As seen in Figure
3
, evaluators associated XpertAI NLEs with being specific to a given dataset/task, providing scientific explanations, and having high accessibility to non-technical experts.
However, LLMs by themselves (ChatGPT) were associated with a lack of specificity to the given data, and XAI with a lack of interpretability.
As summarized in Figure
2
, the evaluations conclude that XpertAI effectively leverages the advantages of both ChatGPT and XAI to provide a complete explanation.
These results validate the accomplishment of our goal to extract accessible and interpretable structure-property relationships in chemistry from raw data.

Based on the expert scores, we note that XpertAI’s explanations are considered “accurate” or “somewhat accurate”.
Although ChatGPT scored higher in terms of accuracy, we argue this is because human experts excel at validating generalized scientific hypotheses.
However, XpertAI’s explanations are specific to relationships established from provided datasets which may be different from familiar concepts in chemistry.
Therefore, we conclude that XpertAI shows promise in generating interpretable and accurate natural language descriptions of structure-function relationships.
Furthermore, we noted that the explanations remained consistent, indicating less probability of hallucinations over multiple generations.
However, it should be emphasized that the accuracy and specificity of the generated explanations are dependent on the quality of the literature dataset provided.
To maintain the quality of explanations, we only used specifically curated publications in case studies 1-5.
The lists of references used can be found in our GitHub repository.

Next, we asked Claude AI assistant (
https://claude.ai/
) by Anthropic
https://www.anthropic.com/
to compare the two explanations from XpertAI and ChatGPT based on accuracy and interpretability. The responses from Claude are given in Appendix B.
Based on the responses, Claude rates the explanations from XpertAI higher than ChatGPT’s for 4/5 tasks. We list a few reasons for Claude’s higher ratings for XpertAI below.
Note that we anonymized the two explanations during the scoring; Explanation A is by XpertAI and Explanation B is by ChatGPT.

•

The language and terminology used in Explanation A is more accessible and easier to understand
for a non-expert compared to Explanation B.

•

Explanation A provides more details on the specific features that impact the target property, including both the top features from XAI analysis and other correlated features (from literature). In contrast, Explanation B only broadly refers to a few features.

•

Explanation A provides mechanistic explanations and states how the target property can be altered by changing each feature in the desired direction. Explanation B lacks this level of mechanistic detail and only generally mentions that the features can be changed, but not how.

However, Claude rates XpertAI’s explanation in case study 5 less than ChatGPT’s.
A similar observation was made from the expert evaluations, where 1 out of 3 evaluators indicated the XpertAI’s NLE as not accurate.
According to Claude “Explanation B demonstrates strong domain knowledge by linking features to concepts like vapor pressure, steric hindrance, and flame retardants. Explanation A does not connect to these chemistry concepts.”
We hypothesize this is due to the underperformance of the trained XGBoost model, as evidenced by a higher root-mean-square error (RMSE) during testing, which results in XpertAI’s lower-rated explanation.
This possibly stems from the limited size of the training dataset (only 63 data points for training). As a result, the XAI analysis reveals the model could have learned non-causal correlations within the dataset.
Therefore, the essential features may not be faithfully represented, thereby leading XpertAI to generate a flawed explanation.

5
Conclusion & Outlook

XAI is becoming increasingly important in ML workflows due to developmental, scientific, and regulatory needs. With the recent growth of XAI, more tools and techniques are emerging. In this context, we addressed a key challenge in applying XAI to chemistry - the lack of interpretability of explanations generated by XAI tools. Generally, XAI tools target technical experts, reducing usability.
We proposed “XpertAI”, a framework leveraging XAI and LLMs to generate intelligent natural language explanations of structure-property relationships from raw chemistry data.
XpertAI produces readily interpretable and specific explanations while uncovering structure-property relationships.
XpertAI works for both regression and classification tasks. We showed combining XAI and LLMs is more powerful than using either alone and demonstrated that XpertAI can accurately explain input-output relationships, not just model predictions.

Importantly, XpertAI’s performance depends on the surrogate model used by the XAI methods. If the surrogate model has acquired spurious data relationships, it will inevitably yield an inaccurate explanation.
In the current version of XpertAI, hyperparameters are hardcoded to enhahnce non-expert usability.
However, technically oriented users can implement XpertAI locally using our GitHub repository.

We aim to incorporate other ML models and enable user-provided models, giving more flexibility and making XpertAI model-agnostic.
Upcoming work will integrate automated hyperparameter optimization. This will further increase accessibility for non-technical users to generate accurate explanations.
Moreover, future work will incorporate additional open-source XAI methods and LLMs into a fully open-source XpertAI without GPT4 dependencies.

Despite current limitations, XpertAI demonstrates promising potential as an interpretable tool for uncovering novel structure-property relationships and generating scientific insights in chemistry. By leveraging AI’s strengths in explanation and language, XpertAI aims to democratize chemical knowledge extraction to accelerate scientific progress. With ongoing improvements to the framework, XpertAI represents an exciting advancement in elucidating meaningful chemical structure-property relationships to propel discovery.

The features identified by the XAI analysis affecting the Presence of open metal sites in MOFs are Fraction of Metals, Density of Solid, and Average Cationic Radius. In summary, the Presence of open metal sites in MOFs is influenced by various structural and chemical features of the MOFs, including the <Fraction of Metals>, Density of Solid, and Average Cationic Radius. ChatGPT Explanation

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are a class of compounds consisting of metal ions or clusters coordinated to organic ligands to form one-, two-, or three-dimensional structures. The presence of open metal sites (OMS) in MOFs is a crucial factor that determines their properties and applications, especially in gas storage, separation, and catalysis.

The presence of OMS in MOFs can be altered by several factors:

•

Choice of Metal Ions: The type of metal ions used in the synthesis of MOFs can influence the presence of OMS. Some metal ions have a higher tendency to form OMS due to their coordination preferences. For example, MOFs made with transition metals often have OMS because these metals typically have a higher coordination number.

•

Choice of Organic Ligands: The type and structure of the organic ligands used can also affect the presence of OMS. Ligands with larger sizes or more complex structures can create more space in the MOF structure, leading to the formation of OMS. Additionally, ligands that can form strong bonds with metal ions can stabilize the OMS.

•

Synthetic Conditions: The conditions under which the MOFs are synthesized, such as temperature, pressure, and solvent, can also influence the formation of OMS. For example, lower synthesis temperatures may favor the formation of OMS because they slow down the reaction rate, allowing for more control over the MOF structure.

•

Post-Synthetic Modifications: After the MOFs are synthesized, their structures can be further modified to create or remove OMS. This can be done through methods such as solvent exchange, thermal treatment, or chemical modification.

•

Use of Modulators: Modulators are additives used during the synthesis of MOFs to control their growth and structure. By choosing appropriate modulators, the presence of OMS can be controlled.

In summary, the presence of open metal sites in metal-organic frameworks can be altered by manipulating the choice of metal ions and organic ligands, the synthetic conditions, post-synthetic modifications, and the use of modulators. These factors can be adjusted to tailor the properties of MOFs for specific applications.

Similarly, altering the electronic properties of the MOF by changing the Unoccupied Energy Levels at Conduction Band Minimum may also affect the Pore-limiting diameter.

•

In summary, the Pore-limiting diameter in MOFs is influenced by several features including the Volume Per Atom, symmetry function G, and Unoccupied Energy Levels at Conduction Band Minimum. These features can be manipulated to alter the Pore-limiting diameter, which can have significant implications for the performance of MOFs in various applications.

In summary, the pore limiting diameter in MOFs is a tunable property that can be controlled by carefully selecting the organic linkers and metal ions or clusters, adjusting the synthesis conditions, and performing post-synthetic modifications.

Case study 3: Toxicity of small drug-like molecules

XpertAI Explanation

•

The features identified by the XAI analysis that affect the toxicity of small molecules include the presence of a heteroatom bonded to three Oxygen atoms, the presence of an aminomethyl group, and the presence of a Tertiary Amine. Additional features that may be correlated with the toxicity of small molecules include the solubility of the molecule, the presence of aromatic rings, and the presence of nucleophilic N, O, and aryl containing substructures (Sharma et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2023).

•

The presence of a heteroatom bonded to three Oxygen atoms can increase the reactivity of the molecule, potentially leading to increased toxicity. The aminomethyl group and the Tertiary Amine can also increase the reactivity of the molecule, potentially leading to increased toxicity (Kalgutkar et al., 2005). The solubility of the molecule can affect its absorption, with molecules with poor solubility showing limited absorption and hence reduced toxicity (Sharma et al., 2017). The presence of aromatic rings and nucleophilic N, O, and aryl containing substructures can also influence the toxicity of the molecule (Sharma et al., 2023).
