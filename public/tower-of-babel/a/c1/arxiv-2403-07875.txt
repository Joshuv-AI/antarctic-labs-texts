Factorizations and fast diagonalization for the heat equation
A. Bressan \orcidID 0000-0003-3730-668X and A. Kushova \orcidID 0000-0002-5243-2249 and G. Loli \orcidID 0000-0002-2192-3889 and M. Montardini \orcidID 0000-0003-0729-063X and G. Sangalli \orcidID 0000-0002-5642-1969 and M. Tani \orcidID 0000-0002-7070-6776

Abstract
This work investigates diagonalization-based methods for efficiently solving linear evolution problems, with a particular focus on the heat equation. The plain diagonalization of the differential operator, though effective for elliptic problems where fast diagonalization can be used, exhibits instability when applied to the heat equation. To address this difficulty, we examine three alternative approaches, based on LU factorization, a suitable arrowhead factorization, and a low-rank modification. These methods introduce more robust factorizations of the time derivative, ensuring both computational efficiency and stability.



0.1
Introduction

Solutions of linear evolution problems are typically computed numerically by sequential time stepping methods.
Maday and Rønquist
maday2008parallelization
proposed a method to parallelize this sequential computation by diagonalizing the time derivative matrix and then solving all time steps concurrently resulting in a Parallel-in-Time (PinT) direct solver.
Their strategy has since been further investigated and an overview of several variants, collectively known as
ParaDiag
algorithms, is provided in
gander2020paradiag
.
This approach relies on the Cartesian structure of the space-time domain and the tensor product structure of the discretization space, which induces a Kronecker structure in the differential operator.
In this it is similar to earlier methods like
alternating direction implicit
(ADI)
birkhoff1962alternating
and
fast diagonalization
(FD)
tensormethods1964
, originally developed for efficiently solving finite difference discretizations of the Poisson problem.
This chapter recalls and compares three methods exploiting the Kronecker structure for the heat equation and highlighting their common structure that is also shared by the original approach of Maday and Rønquist.
These methods primarily differ in how they handle the inversion of the time derivative operator.

The model problem considered is the heat equation. Assuming homogeneous Dirichlet boundary and initial conditions, it is formulated as follows: find
u
such that

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\Delta_{s}u=f&\quad\text{in }\,\phantom{\partial}\Omega_{s}\times\Omega_{t},\\
u=0&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{s}\times\Omega_{t},\\
u=0&\quad\text{in }\,\phantom{\partial}\Omega_{s}\times\{0\},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\Omega_{s}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, with boundary
\partial\Omega_{s}
, and
\Omega_{t}=[0,T]\subset\mathbb{R}
is the time interval. For
f\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\Omega_{s})
problem (
1
) has a unique weak solution
u
in the Bochner space of functions in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{s}))
with time derivative in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega_{s}))
satisfying the initial condition, see
evans-book
for details.

System (
1
) is a linear problem of the form
Au=f
where the operator
A
exhibits a tensor product structure:

A=A_{t}\otimes M_{s}+M_{t}\otimes A_{s},

(2)

with
M_{s}
and
M_{t}
the identity operators seen as the Riesz operators of the
L^{2}
-scalar product.
Here,
A_{t}
is the time derivative, and
A_{s}
is the space-Laplacian operator, which is elliptic and
M_{s}
-self-adjoint.
Consequently, a generalized eigendecomposition of the pair
(A_{s},M_{s})
is possible and stable, which forms a crucial assumption in the methods discussed in this chapter.

If such a problem is discretized with a finite dimensional space
V=V_{s}\otimes V_{t}
, the structure is preserved leading to the linear system

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{A}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{s}+\mathbf{M}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{A}_{s})\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f},

(3)

where the correspondence with the continuous operators is given by matching letter.
Introducing
N_{\square}=\dim V_{\square}
and
N=\dim V=N_{s}N_{t}
, then

\mathbf{u},\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,
\mathbf{M}_{s},\mathbf{A}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{t},\mathbf{A}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}
.

Efficient solvers for problems of the form (
3
),
are well established in literature (see
simoncini2016computational
for a survey of methods requiring loose assumptions on the Kronecker factors).
In this chapter, we consider methods that are based on diagonalization and to illustrate the core concept we assume that
the generalized eigendecompositions of both pairs
(\mathbf{A}_{t},\mathbf{M}_{t})
and
(\mathbf{A}_{s},\mathbf{M}_{s})
are available:
this means that there are diagonal matrices
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}
,
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}
and invertible matrices
\mathbf{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}},\mathbf{U}_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{s}\mathbf{U}_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{s}\mathbf{U}_{s}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}.

(5)

By introducing the matrices
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}\right)^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s}=\left(\mathbf{M}_{s}\mathbf{U}_{s}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain the factorizations

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{t}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{t}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}\mathbf{U}_{t}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{s}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}\mathbf{U}_{s}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{s}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}\mathbf{U}_{s}^{-1},

with
\mathbf{I}_{\square}\in\mathbb{R}^{\square\times\square}
the identity matrices.
The above expressions lead to the following factorization of
\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{A}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s})^{-1}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}+\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}\otimes\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s})(\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{s})^{-1}.

(6)

The factorization (
6
) permits to solve (
3
) by applying the inverse of each factor in turn. This approach is here called
Diagonalization in Time
(DT) method.
Furthermore, the Kronecker structure of
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s}
and
{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\otimes{\mathbf{U}}_{s}
allows for matrix-vector multiplication with a reduced computational cost of

O(N_{s}N+N_{t}N)
, in contrast to the
O(N^{2})
complexity, and
(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}+\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}\otimes\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s})
is diagonal allowing for inversion at a cost of
O(N)
operations.
This highlights how the tensor/Kronecker structure facilitates the solution of the system. However, it hides important details about the
existence, stability and computational cost of the generalized eigendecompositions (
4
) and (
5
).

The existence and stability of the eigendecomposition of the pair
(\mathbf{A}_{s},\mathbf{M}_{s})
, i.e. (
5
),
follows for the heat equation from
\mathbf{A}_{s}
and
\mathbf{M}_{s}
being symmetric and positive definite. In such a case

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s}=\left(\mathbf{M}_{s}\mathbf{U}_{s}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{U}_{s}^{T}\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\mathbf{U}^{T}_{s}\mathbf{M}_{s}\mathbf{U}_{s}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}.

(7)

Consequently, the condition number of the eigenvector matrix
\mathbf{U}_{s}
satisfies

\kappa_{2}\left(\mathbf{U}_{s}\right):=\|\mathbf{U}_{s}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{U}_{s}^{-1}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\kappa_{2}(\mathbf{M}_{s})},

which is often small or moderate.

Assuming
N_{s}>N_{t}
, which is typically the case when
d>1
, the computational cost of this step is in general
O(N_{s}^{3})
(see e.g.
(
Golub2012
, Chapter 8)
), and results prohibitively high.
However, the generalized eigendecomposition of the pair
(\mathbf{A}_{s},\mathbf{M}_{s})
can be computed with a reduced cost when
\Omega_{s}
is a Cartesian domain. In such a case Fast Diagonalization (FD) can be performed, see Section
0.2
.

Another interesting case is when a generalized eigendecomposition with reduced cost is possible for a suitable approximation

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}_{t}\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{s}+\mathbf{M}_{t}\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{s},

(8)

of
\mathbf{A}
, that is one can use FD on the pair
(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{s},\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{s})
. In such a case
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}
has the role of a preconditioner.
Both cases are exemplified in the literature and have been applied in different
contexts, including finite differences
tensormethods1964
;
Buzbee1970
, finite elements
Patera1986
;
Bjontegaard2009
, spectral elements
Couzy1994
;
Lottes2005
;
Bjontegaard2009
and isogeometric analysis
Montardini2018
.
Section
0.5
reports numerical result of both cases
applied to spline based Galerkin discretizations.

Concerning (
4
), observe that the time derivative, at the continuous level, does not admit eigenfunctions fulfilling the homogeneous initial condition. Therefore, at the discrete level, the generalized eigendecomposition (
4
) of the pair
(\mathbf{A}_{t},\mathbf{M}_{t})
, when computable,
should be seen as a numerical artifact and indeed becomes instable in some cases, e.g. for high-degree spline based Galerkin discretization.
These instabilities are exemplified for both finite differences and Galerkin methods in Section
0.3
, and they
may affect the accuracy of the solution.

This chapter presents three alternative methods, here called:
LU factorization
(LU)

Haidvogel1979
;
Shen1994
;
Wang2013
;
bressan2023space
,
arrowhead factorization
(AR), originally proposed in the context of isogeometric analysis
MR2152382
, and
low-rank modification
(LR), here introduced similarly to the methods in
kressner2023improved
;
gander2024new
. Among them, AR and LR are space-time parallelizable, while LU is only parallelizable in space.
Each method corresponds to a factorization of
\mathbf{A}
, that replaces (
6
), of the form:

\mathbf{A}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s})^{-1}\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{s})^{-1},

(9)

where
\mathbf{U}_{s}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s}
fulfill (
5
), while
\mathbf{U}_{t}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}
and
\mathbf{T}
are method specific.
Then, (
3
) is solved following the template described by Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Common structure for the methods DT, LU, AR, LR.

1:

compute
\mathbf{U}_{s},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s},\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}
as in (
5
)

2:

compute
\mathbf{U}_{t},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t},\mathbf{T}

3:

compute
\mathbf{y}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s})\mathbf{f}

4:

solve
\mathbf{T}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{y}

5:

compute
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{s})\mathbf{z}

Notice that, when the methods are used to apply a preconditioner,
steps 1–2 of Algorithm
1
are computed only once as the setup phase, while steps 3–5 are repeated for each application of the preconditioner and form the application phase.

In what follows, Section
0.2
describes the
FD method. Section
0.3
describes the diagonalization in time method introduced in
maday2008parallelization
and reports specific examples of numerical instabilities
for both finite difference and Galerkin discretizations of the heat equation.
The stable methods LU, AR and LR are described in details in Section
0.4
and numerical results are reported in Section
0.5
.
Finally in Section
0.6
the theoretical and numerical results are summarized.

0.2
Fast diagonalization

The generalized eigendecomposition in space (
5
) has computational cost of order
O(N_{s}^{3})
,
that is too demanding to be practical. However, it can be achieved at a reduced cost when the spatial domain has an inner Cartesian structure that is reflected in the function space

\Omega_{s}=\Omega_{s,1}\times\dots\times\Omega_{s,d},\qquad V_{s}=V_{s,1}\otimes\dots\otimes V_{s,d},

(10)

in which case

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}_{s,d}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbf{M}_{s,1},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{K}_{j,d}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbf{K}_{j,1},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{K}_{j,l}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{A}_{s,l}&\text{for }l=j,\\
\mathbf{M}_{s,l}&\text{for }l\neq j,\end{cases}

(12)

with
\mathbf{M}_{s,l},\mathbf{A}_{s,l}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s,l}\times N_{s,l}}
for
l=1,\ldots,d
representing respectively the identity and the Laplace operator on
V_{s,l}
.
The FD method applies more generally when
\mathbf{A}_{s}
is a sums of
kronecker matrices of the form

\mathbf{A}_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}\mathbf{K}_{j,d}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbf{K}_{j,1},

(13)

with
c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{K}_{j,l}\in\{\mathbf{M}_{s,l},\mathbf{A}_{s,l}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{s,l}\times N_{s,l}}
such that
(\mathbf{A}_{s,l},\mathbf{M}_{s,l})
admits a generalized eigendecomposition, for all
l=1,\dots,d
.

In this case, for
l=1,\ldots,d
there are
\mathbf{U}_{s,l}
,
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s,l}

that satisfy

\mathbf{A}_{s,l}\mathbf{U}_{s,l}=\mathbf{M}_{s,l}\mathbf{U}_{s,l}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s,l}.

Then, the generalized eigendecomposition of
the pair
(\mathbf{A}_{s},\mathbf{M}_{s})
of equation (
5
)
is realized with

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{s}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{U}_{s,d}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbf{U}_{s,1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}\mathbf{L}_{j,d}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbf{L}_{j,1},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{L}_{j,l}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{I}_{N_{s,l}}&\text{for }\mathbf{K}_{j,l}=\mathbf{M}_{s,l},\\
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s,l}&\text{for }\mathbf{K}_{j,l}=\mathbf{A}_{s,l}.\end{cases}

Computing the generalized eigendecomposition of
the pair
(\mathbf{A}_{s,l},\mathbf{M}_{s,l})
has a computational cost of order

O(N_{s,l}^{3})
, so that Step 1 in Algorithm
1

has a cost of
O(\sum_{l=1}^{d}N_{s,l}^{3})
.
Notice that, assuming
N_{s,l}=N_{s}^{1/d}
, for
l=1,\dots,d
, then the eigenvalues are computed with cost
O(N_{s}^{3/d})
, that for
d=2
is
O(N_{s}^{3/2})
, and for
d=3
is
O(N_{s})
.

When the domain is not Cartesian, but it is parametrized over a Cartesian domain one can construct a preconditioner based on the technique above as exemplified in Section
0.5.2
.

0.3
Diagonalization in time and numerical instabilities

This method was proposed in
maday2008parallelization
and is based on the generalized eigendecomposition (
4
), further combined with the generalized eigendecomposition (
5
).
In this case, the factorization (
9
) reduces to (
6
), i.e

\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}+\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}\otimes\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s},

and the inverse of
\mathbf{A}
can be applied with Algorithm
1
.

We assume that Step 1 can be computed with FD method.
Notice that, at Step 2 one computes the generalized eigendecomposition of
the pair
(\mathbf{A}_{t},\mathbf{M}_{t})
with cost
O(N_{t}^{3})
and the
diagonal matrix
\mathbf{T}
with cost
O(N)
.
It should be mentioned that in certain cases (see e.g.
Buzbee1970
;
Haidvogel1979
;
Shen1997
;
kressner2023improved
;
Montardini2023

gander2024new
)
the matrix with
\mathbf{U}_{s,l}
,
l=1,\ldots,d
, and/or with
\mathbf{U}_{t}
appearing at steps 3 and 5 can be performed by exploiting the Fast Fourier Transform, resulting in an almost linear computational cost. However, for simplicity here we assume that matrix products are computed in the standard way.

While this method is very effective in some settings, see
maday2008parallelization
, it may in other cases lead to instabilities and inaccurate solutions. In the remainder of this section, we present two distinct time discretizations of (
1
) that highlight these situations.
We first consider a time stepping scheme, and then the Galerkin approximation method.

0.3.1
Finite differences

In
maday2008parallelization
, the authors considered an implicit Euler scheme in time coupled with a finite difference approximation in space.
For a fixed time partition
\{t_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N_{t}}
of the interval
\Omega_{t}=[0,T]
, denote by
\mathbf{u}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}

the unknown numerical approximation of the solution of (
1
) in the
N_{s}
internal grid points
at the time
t_{n}
. Analogously
\mathbf{f}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}}
is the vector
representing the source term. Let
\mathbf{A}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}
be
the symmetric positive definite matrix representing
A_{s}
, then the set of
fully discrete equations is

\frac{\mathbf{u}_{n+1}-\mathbf{u}_{n}}{\tau_{n}}+\mathbf{A}_{s}\mathbf{u}_{n+1}=\mathbf{f}_{n+1},\quad n=0,\dots,N_{t}-1,

where
\tau_{n}:=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
, for
n=0,\dots,N_{t}-1
. Therefore, equation (
3
) holds with

\mathbf{A}_{t}:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}&&\\
-\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}&\frac{1}{\tau_{1}}&&&\\
&-\frac{1}{\tau_{2}}&\frac{1}{\tau_{2}}&&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
&&&-\frac{1}{\tau_{N_{t}-1}}&\frac{1}{\tau_{N_{t}-1}}\end{pmatrix},

(14)

and
\mathbf{M}_{t}
,
\mathbf{M}_{s}
the identity matrices.

The matrix
\mathbf{A}_{t}
in (
14
) is lower triangular and its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries. Hence, there exist an invertible matrix
\mathbf{U}_{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{t}\times N_{t}}
that satisfies (
4
)
only if

\tau_{i}\neq\tau_{j}
for every
i\neq j
.
To ensure this condition, Maday and Rønquist
maday2008parallelization
proposed the use of a geometric partition of the kind

\tau_{n}=\beta^{n-1}\tau_{1},\quad n\geq 1,

where
\beta>1
is a free parameter and
\tau_{1}
is the first time step.

By using instead a time step closer to the uniform one, it leads indeed to bad conditioning and instability. The study of these phenomena, in conjunction with the approximation properties of the scheme, has been explored in the work
gander2016direct
.

0.3.2
Galerkin discretization

Consider
\Omega_{s}=[0,1]^{d}
and let

V_{s}:=\spann{(B_{i}|i=1,\dots,N_{s})}
be the discrete space of tensor product B-splines of degree
p_{s}
and
C^{p_{s}-1}
regularity.
Analogously,
\Omega_{t}=[0,1]
is the time interval and we denote by
V_{t}=\spann{(b_{j}|j=1,\dots,N_{t})}
the spline space of degree
p_{t}
and regularity
C^{p_{t}-1}
. For details on spline functions we refer the reader to
deboor
.We assume that
f\in L^{2}(\Omega_{t};H^{-1}(\Omega_{s}))
and consider the well posed space-time Galerkin discretization method for (
1
) given in
(
loli2020efficient
, Section 3.2)
with the discrete space
V=V_{s}\otimes V_{t}
.

0.4
Stable time factorizations

This section describes the three alternatives to the DT method, denoted LU, AR and LR, considered in Algorithm
1
.
Recall that the generalized eigendecomposition (
5
)
can be computed and the matrix
\mathbf{A}
factorizes as in (
9
), that is

\mathbf{A}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{s})^{-1}\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{s})^{-1}.

Let us recall that Kronecker product is not commutative, but it holds for instance

\mathbf{U}_{s}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}(\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{U}_{s})\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{\top},

with
\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
a
perfect shuffle
matrix
van2000ubiquitous
.
Applying the shuffle matrix corresponds to swapping the positions of time and space in the tensor product structure.
Clearly
\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{\top}
and denoting by

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}:=\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{\top}
the commuted matrix, it holds

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{\top}

(16)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}+\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}\otimes\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s})\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{I}_{N_{s}}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}+\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}\otimes\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}.

The matrix
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}
has a block diagonal structure, where each block

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda_{i}):=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}+\lambda_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}

is associated to an eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}:=[\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}]_{i,i}
, for
i=1,\dots,N_{s}
, that is

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}=\begin{pmatrix}\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda_{1})&&&\\
&\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda_{2})&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda_{N_{s}})\end{pmatrix},

(17)

and the expression of
\mathbf{U}_{t}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}
and
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)
depends on the chosen method.
Moreover, computing
\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{y}
in Step 4 of Algorithm
1
is equivalent to compute

\mathbf{T}^{-1}=\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}}^{\top}\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}\,^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{N_{t},N_{s}},

that is to apply twice a shuffle matrix to a vector, and invert a block diagonal matrix
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}
,
that is, to solve
N_{s}
independent subproblems. For this reason all the methods allow for a space parallel implementation. Time parallelization is achieved when each subproblem allows for additional parallelism.

When reporting the cost of the different methods, it is assumed
the generalized eigendecomposition (
5
) can be computed with the same cost as in the FD method, sufficient conditions
are (
11
) and (
13
).

This approach is well suited if the time matrices
\mathbf{A}_{t}
and
\mathbf{M}_{t}
have small bandwidth
\mathscr{b}
. In this method

\mathbf{U}_{t}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}},

so that

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda):=\mathbf{A}_{t}+\lambda\mathbf{M}_{t}.

(18)

The LU-factorization of
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)
preserves the bandwidth

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}l_{1,1}(\lambda)&&&\\
\vdots&\ddots&&\\
l_{\mathscr{b},1}(\lambda)&&&\\
&\ddots&\quad\quad&l_{N_{t},N_{t}}(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1,1}(\lambda)&\dots&u_{1,\mathscr{b}}(\lambda)&\\
&\ddots&&\ddots\\
&&&\\
&&&u_{N_{t},N_{t}}(\lambda)\end{pmatrix},

\lambda_{i}

i=1,\dots,N_{s}

0.4.2
Arrowhead factorization

The arrowhead factorization was introduced in
loli2020efficient
. For any basis such that
b_{j}(T)=0
,
j=1,\dots,N_{t}-1
,
b_{N_{t}}(T)\neq 0
, the Galerkin time derivative
\mathbf{A}_{t}
and the Gram matrix
\mathbf{M}_{t}
have the block structure:

\mathbf{A}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{\mathbf{A}}_{t}&\mathbf{a}\\[2.0pt]
-\mathbf{a}^{\top}&\alpha\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{M}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{\mathbf{M}}_{t}&\mathbf{m}\\[2.0pt]
\mathbf{m}^{\top}&\mu\end{bmatrix},

where
\mathring{\mathbf{M}}_{t}
is symmetric,
\mathring{\mathbf{A}}_{t}
is skew-symmetric and

\alpha=\int_{0}^{T}b_{N_{t}}^{\prime}\ b_{N_{t}}\ \mathrm{dt}=\frac{b_{N_{t}}(T)^{2}}{2}>0.

Therefore, there exists
\mathring{\mathbf{U}}_{t}
satisfying

\mathring{\mathbf{U}}_{t}^{*}\mathring{\mathbf{A}}_{t}\mathring{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\mathring{\mathbf{D}}_{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}.

Let us define
\mathbf{U}_{t}
as

\mathbf{U}_{t}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{\mathbf{U}}_{t}&-\rho\mathring{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{m}\\
\mathbf{0}^{\top}&\rho\end{bmatrix},

(19)

with
\mathbf{0}
being the null vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{t}-1}
and

\rho:=\left(\mu-\mathbf{m}^{\top}\mathring{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{m}\right)^{-1/2}=\left(\|b_{N_{t}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T)}-\|\Pi(b_{N_{t}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T)}\right)^{-1/2},

where

\Pi:L^{2}(0,T)\rightarrow\spann\{b_{i}:i=1,\ldots,N_{t}-1\}

denotes the
L^{2}
-projection. Since
b_{N_{t}}\notin\spann\{b_{i}:i=1,\ldots,N_{t}-1\}
, we highlight that

\mu-\mathbf{m}^{\top}\mathring{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{m}>0.

Choosing
\mathbf{U}_{t}
as in (
19
), we obtain:

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*},\quad\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}},

where
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}
is a complex arrowhead matrix, characterized by non-zero entries only on the diagonal, in the last row, and in the last column, resembling an arrow pointing down-right. Specifically, the expression for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}
is

\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}:=\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{\mathbf{D}}_{t}&\mathbf{g}\\[2.0pt]
-\mathbf{g}^{*}&\sigma\end{bmatrix},

where

\mathbf{g}:=\mathring{{\mathbf{U}}}_{t}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}\mathring{\mathbf{A}}_{t}&\mathbf{a}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-\rho\mathring{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{m}\\
\rho\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma:=\begin{bmatrix}-\rho\mathbf{m}^{\top}\mathring{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}_{t}&{\rho}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{t}\begin{bmatrix}-\rho\mathring{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{m}\\
\rho\end{bmatrix}.

With this method the blocks of
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}
in (
17
)
have the same arrowhead structure of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}
, and the LU factorization
of
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)
is as follows

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)=\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&1&\\
-\frac{g_{1}^{*}}{\delta_{1}+\lambda}&\dots&-\frac{g_{N_{t}-1}^{*}}{\delta_{N_{t}-1}+\lambda}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(\delta_{1}+\lambda)&&&g_{1}\\
&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&(\delta_{N_{t}-1}+\lambda)&g_{N_{t}-1}\\
&&&s(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}

(20)

where
g_{j}=[\mathbf{g}]_{j}
,
\delta_{j}=[\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}]_{j,j}
for

j=1,\dots,N_{t}-1
,
\sigma=[\mathbf{\Delta}_{t}]_{N_{t},N_{t}}
and

s(\lambda)=(\sigma+\lambda)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{t}-1}g_{j}^{*}(\delta_{j}+\lambda)^{-1}g_{j}.

The application of the inverse of
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}
is now parallelizable in space and time. Parallelization in space is of course achievable by inverting each block independently. Parallelization in time is achieved by backward substitution, inverting first the last row of (
20
), and then the previous
N_{t}-1
rows concurrently.

The setup of the AR method incudes the computation of the LU factors for each
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbf{\Lambda}_{s}
whose computational cost is
O(N)
.
The inversion of
\mathbf{T}
in Step 4 of Algorithm
1

is again of order
O(N)
due to the form of the LU factorization, so that the dominant costs are given by the matrix vector multiplications in Step 3 and Step 5.

0.4.3
Low-rank modification

The arrowhead factorization is a way to cope with the
lack of skew-symmetry in
\mathbf{A}_{t}
due to a single positive coefficient in
the diagonal entries.
In that case a skew-symmetric matrix is obtained from
\mathbf{A}_{t}
by
removing the positive entry, i.e., by a rank-1 modification.

A generalization of the arrowhead factorization that allows for broader set of pencils
(\mathbf{A}_{t},\mathbf{M}_{t})
is to split
\mathbf{A}_{t}
as

\mathbf{A}_{t}=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t}+\mathbf{R}_{t},

where the matrix
\mathbf{R}_{t}
has rank-
\mathscr{r}
,
1\leq\mathscr{r}\leq N_{t}
, and the pair
(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t},\mathbf{M}_{t})
admits the generalized eigendecomposition

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t},

with
\mathbf{U}_{t}
an
\mathbf{M}_{t}
-orthonormal matrix (
7
), or equivalently

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*},\quad{\mathbf{U}}_{t}^{*}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathbf{U}}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{M}_{t}\mathbf{U}_{t}=\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}.

(21)

Let
\mathbf{R}_{t}=\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{G}
, for some
\mathbf{F},\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathscr{r}\times N_{t}}
, then
the blocks of
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}
for this method take the form

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}+\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}{\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{U}_{t},

(22)

that is,
\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)
is a low-rank modification of
a diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}:=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{t}+\lambda\mathbf{I}_{N_{t}}
and

\overset{\text{\tiny$\leftrightarrow$}}{\mathbf{T}}_{t}(\lambda)^{-1}
can be applied
using the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula

\left(\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}+\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{t}\right)^{-1}=\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}^{-1}-\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{I}_{\mathscr{r}}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}^{-1}.

(23)

Thus, in the setup phase of Algorithm
1
,
the following small matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathscr{r}\times\mathscr{r}}
are precomputed
for each
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbf{\Lambda_{s}}

{\mathbf{C}}_{\lambda}:=\left(\mathbf{I}_{\mathscr{r}}+\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{t}\mathbf{D}_{\lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{F}^{\top}\right)^{-1}.

This costs
O(\mathscr{r}N_{t}^{2})
to compute
\mathbf{U}_{t}^{*}\mathbf{F}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{U}_{t}

plus
O(\mathscr{r}^{2}N)
to compute the matrix in parenthesis and
O(N_{s}\mathscr{r}^{3})
to invert it.

Applying the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula is done in few steps as described in Algorithm
2
,
while the overall computational cost of the LR method is reported in Table
6

The steps of Algorithm
2
are sequential but each step is a matrix vector multiplication implying that this method is parallelizable in both time and space.

Application as a preconditioner

In this section, we apply the LU, AR, and LR methods as left preconditioners in the context of isogeometric discretizations.
In isogeometric analysis (IGA), introduced in
MR2152382
,
the space domain
\Omega_{s}
is the
image through a bi-Lipschitz map
G
of a cuboid,
usually
\widehat{\Omega}_{s}:=[0,1]^{d}
.
The discrete space defined on
\Omega_{s}\times\Omega_{t}
is
V:=V_{s}\otimes V_{t}
where
V_{s}
is the push-forward
of a tensor product spline space

\widehat{V}_{s}
defined on
\widehat{\Omega}_{s}

V_{s}=\{\hat{v}(G^{-1}(\mathbf{x})):\hat{v}\in\widehat{V}_{s}\},

see
MR3202239
for more details, and
V_{t}
is a spline space on
\Omega_{t}
.

The domain
\Omega_{s}
, in general, is not a Cartesian product
and consequently the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{A}_{s}
and the Gram
matrix
\mathbf{M}_{s}
for the space
V_{s}
do not satisfy (
11
)
nor (
13
)
and the generalized eigendecomposition of the pair

(\mathbf{A}_{s},\mathbf{M}_{s})
is computationally infeasible.

Nevertheless the stiffness matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{s}
and the Gram
matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{s}
constructed for the space
\widehat{V}_{s}
on the Cartesian domain
\widehat{\Omega}_{s}

satisfy both conditions so that the presented methods (LU, AR, LR) can be
efficiently applied to the heat operator on
\widehat{\Omega}_{s}\times\Omega_{t}
that is

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}_{t}\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{s}+\mathbf{M}_{t}\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{s}.

The matrices
\mathbf{M}_{s}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{s}
are spectrally
equivalent with constants that depend only on
G

loli2022easy

\mathbf{A}_{s}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{s}

igaFD2016
.

loli2020efficient

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}
as
a left preconditioner , i.e. we solve the system

\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}=\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{f},

where the application of
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}

Conclusions

In this work we have investigated several Kronecker-based methods for solving linear evolution problems
with particular emphasis on the heat equation.
The Diagonalization in Time (DT) exhibits significant instabilities when applied to the heat equation.
To address these limitations, we examined three alternative approaches: LU factorization (LU), arrowhead factorization (AR), and low-rank modification (LR).
These methods replace the unstable generalized eigendecomposition in time (
4
) with more robust factorizations,
thereby maintaining computational efficiency while ensuring stability in the solution of the heat equation.
These methods require a tensor product structure of the discrete space
when used as direct solvers. When this assumption fails, but it is satisfied by
an approximate linear system, they can be used as preconditioners as demonstrated in one of the presented numerical experiments.

Future research could build on these results by investigating their applicability to variable coefficient problems, cases beyond the parabolic framework, and nonlinear equations.
In conclusion, while these methods exhibit optimal behavior in academic tests, several challenges remain when addressing real-world applications.
