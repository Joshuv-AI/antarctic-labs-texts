Rational surfaces with a non-arithmetic automorphism group
Jennifer Li, Sebastián Torres

Abstract
. In [ T12 ] , Totaro gave examples of a K3 surface such that its automorphism group is not commensurable with an arithmetic group, answering a question of Mazur [ M93 , §7] . We give examples of rational surfaces with the same property. Our examples Y Y are Looijenga pairs, i.e., there is a connected singular nodal curve D ⊂ Y D\subset Y such that K Y + D = 0 K_{Y}+D=0 .

1.
Introduction

A Looijenga pair is a pair
(Y,D)
in which
Y
is a smooth, complex projective surface and
D\in|-K_{Y}|
is a (reduced) anticanonical divisor given by a connected singular nodal curve. Such boundary
D
must be either a cycle of smooth rational curves or an irreducible rational curve with a single node. Throughout this article we will use some general facts about these surfaces, and more details can be found in
[
GHK15
,
F15
]
. Such a surface
Y
is necessarily rational (see e.g.
[
GHK15
, §1]
). We fix an orientation of the cycle
D
and write
D=D_{1}+\ldots+D_{r}
, where
D_{i}
are the irreducible components of
D
, and where the order is compatible with the orientation. If
r=2
, then
D
consists of a cycle of two smooth rational curves intersecting at two points. In the case
r>2
, for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
the component
D_{i}
intersects each of the
D_{i-1}
and
D_{i+1}
once, and no other component. We denote by
D_{i}^{\circ}
the component
D_{i}
minus the two intersection points with
D_{i\pm 1}
.

We say that the pair
(Y,D)
is negative (semi)definite if the intersection matrix of
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
is so. The sequence
(D_{1}^{2},\ldots,D_{r}^{2})
is called the self-intersection sequence of
(Y,D)
. If
D_{i}^{2}=-2
for every
i
, then
(Y,D)
is negative semidefinite. Assuming that there are no
(-1)
-components in the boundary,
(Y,D)
is negative definite if and only if
D_{i}^{2}\leq-2
for every
i
and
D_{i}^{2}\leq-3
for at least one index
i

[
F15
, §1]
. The group
\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
is isomorphic to
H^{2}(Y,\mathbb{Z})
via the first Chern class. We denote its rank by
\rho(Y)
, which equals the second Betti number of
Y
. That is,
\rho(Y)=b_{2}(Y)=10-D^{2}
, since
K_{Y}=-D
.

When
(Y,D)
is negative definite,
D
can be analytically contracted to a cusp singularity
[
G62
]
, and in this way we obtain a normal complex analytic surface
\hat{Y}
with trivial dualizing sheaf. Thus,
\hat{Y}
can be regarded as a singular analog of a K3 surface.

Definition 1.1
.

An automorphism of a Looijenga pair
(Y,D)
is an automorphism
\varphi:Y\xrightarrow{\sim}Y
such that
\varphi(D_{i})=D_{i}
for every
i
and
\varphi
preserves the orientation if
r\leq 2
. We denote by
\operatorname{Aut}(Y,D)
the group of automorphisms of the pair
(Y,D)
.

Definition 1.2
.

Two groups
G
,
H
are said to be commensurable with each other if they have finite-index subgroups
G^{\prime}\subset G
and
H^{\prime}\subset H
such that
G^{\prime}\cong H^{\prime}
.
In this case, we write
G\doteq H
.

Definition 1.3
.

An arithmetic group is a subgroup
G
of the group of
\mathbb{Q}
-points of some
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic group
H_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that, for some integral structure on
H_{\mathbb{Q}}
, the intersection
G\cap H(\mathbb{Z})
is of finite index in both
G
and
H(\mathbb{Z})
.

In
[
T12
]
, Totaro showed that the automorphism group of a K3 surface need not be commensurable with an arithmetic group, thereby answering a question by Mazur
[
M93
, §7]
. In
[
L18
]
, Lesieutre found a projective variety of dimension six with non-finitely generated (in particular, non-arithmetic) automorphism group. Inspired by Lesieutre’s work, Dinh and Oguiso
[
DO19
]
found projective varieties of each dimension
d\geq 2
with non-finitely generated automorphism group. Remarkably, rational projective varieties with the same property have been found in every dimension
d\geq 3
by Dinh, Oguiso and Yu
[
DOY22
]
. In
[
O20
]
, Oguiso finds a smooth projective surface in odd characteristic, birational to a K3 surface, with non-finitely generated automorphism group.
In the present article, we find a rational surface, namely a Looijenga pair, with discrete and finitely generated yet non-arithmetic automorphism group. As far as the authors know, this is the first rational example in dimension
2
with this property.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists a (negative definite) Looijenga pair
(Y,D)
such that the automorphism group
\operatorname{Aut}(Y,D)
is not commensurable with an arithmetic group.

We note that, when
(Y,D)
is negative definite,
\operatorname{Aut}(Y,D)
is a finite-index subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
. Indeed, in this case, every automorphism of
Y
must permute the components
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
, which gives a homomorphism
\rho:\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to S_{r}
(where
S_{r}
denotes the symmetric group on
r
elements), and
\operatorname{Aut}(Y,D)
is precisely the subgroup of
\ker\rho
consisting of automorphisms that preserve the orientation (if
r\leq 2
), which has finite index in
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
. In particular, both groups are commensurable with each other, and Theorem
1.4
may be considered as a statement about
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
itself.

Since every automorphism of
Y
induces an isometry of the Picard lattice, we have a group homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to O(\operatorname{Pic}(Y))
, the kernel of which is finite (see e.g.
[
DM19
, Proposition 2.1]
).
Using this, the proof of Theorem
1.4
reduces to studying groups that live inside
O(\operatorname{Pic}(Y))
.
In order to do this, we follow the techniques used in
[
T12
, §7]
. Most importantly, we make use of the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

[
T12
, Theorem 7.1]

Let
M
be a lattice of signature
(1,m)
for
m\geq 3
. Let
S
be an infinite-index subgroup in
O(M)
. Suppose that
\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
is an infinite-index subgroup of
S
. Then
S
is not commensurable with an arithmetic group.

We will study
\operatorname{Aut}(Y,D)
through its action on the perpendicular lattice to
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
. In order to construct some infinite subgroups of
\operatorname{Aut}(Y,D)
, we will, as in
[
T12
, §7]
, construct some elliptic fibrations and compute their rank using the Shioda–Tate formula.

2.
Background notions

Throughout the article,
(Y,D)
will denote a Looijenga pair with an anticanonical cycle of length
r>1
, together with an orientation of the cycle, as defined in the introduction.
We need some definitions first.

Definition 2.1
.

An internal
(-2)
-curve is a smooth rational curve of self-intersection
-2
disjoint from
D
. The subgroup
W_{Y}\subset O(\operatorname{Pic}(Y))
generated by the reflections along internal
(-2)
-curves is called the Weyl group of
Y
. The pair
(Y,D)
is said to be generic if it has no internal
(-2)
-curves.

By
[
GHK15
, Lemma 2.1]
, there is a canonical identification
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
, where
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)
is the subgroup of
\operatorname{Pic}(D)
consisting of line bundles
L
on
D
such that
\deg L|_{D_{i}}=0
for each
i
. Under this identification, if
p,q\in D_{i}^{\circ}
and we choose coordinates on
D_{i}^{\circ}\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
such that
p
corresponds to
1
and
q
corresponds to
\lambda
, then
\mathcal{O}_{D}(q-p)\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)
corresponds to
\lambda^{-1}\in\mathbb{G}_{m}

[
F15
, Lemma 1.6]
.

Recall that on
\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
we have an intersection pairing. We use it to define the following sublattice of
\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a Looijenga pair
(Y,D)
, let
\Lambda(Y,D)=\langle D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}\rangle^{\perp}
be the sublattice of classes
\alpha\in\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
such that
\alpha\cdot D_{i}=0
for every
i
.

We have the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
F15
, Lemma 1.5]

Let
s
be the rank of the kernel of the homomorphism
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}[D_{i}]\to\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
. Then
\Lambda(Y,D)
is a lattice of rank
10-D^{2}-r+s
. In particular, if the classes of
D_{1},\ldots,D_{r}
are linearly independent in
\operatorname{Pic}(Y)
, then the rank of
\Lambda(Y,D)
equals
10-D^{2}-r
.

Using the identification
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
above, we can define the following.

Definition 2.4
.

The period point of
(Y,D)
is the morphism

\phi_{Y}:\Lambda(Y,D)\to\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
, given by
L\mapsto L|_{D}

By abuse of notation, we may denote
\phi_{Y}(\mathcal{O}_{Y}(E))
simply by
\phi_{Y}(E)
, where
E
is a divisor.
By
[
GHK15
, §2]
, any given morphism
\phi:\Lambda(Y,D)\to\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)
can be realized as the period point of some deformation equivalent pair
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
. More precisely, we have the following result:

Theorem 2.5
.

[
F15
, Theorem 3.17]

Let
\phi:\Lambda(Y,D)\to\mathbb{G}_{m}
be any morphism. Then there is a deformation equivalent pair
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
and an identification
\Lambda(Y,D)\cong\Lambda(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
induced by parallel transport, such that the period point
\phi_{Y^{\prime}}
of
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
corresponds to
\phi
.

We will use this to construct a Looijenga pair whose period point satisfies certain conditions. We may denote the pair
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
from Theorem
2.5
by
(Y_{\phi},D_{\phi})
.

Definition 2.6
.

We denote by
\Phi
the set of classes in
\Lambda(Y,D)
with square
-2
which are realized by an internal
(-2)
-curve on some deformation equivalent Looijenga pair
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
. An element
\alpha\in\Phi
is called a root.

Remark 2.7
.

By
[
GHK15
, Corollary 3.5]
, the condition that
(Y,D)
is generic is equivalent to having
\phi_{Y}(\alpha)\neq 1
for every
\alpha\in\Phi
. In this case, there are no
(-2)
-curves in
Y\backslash D
.

3.
Elliptic fibrations

Our goal is to use Theorem
1.5
, for which we will need to produce some infinite-order subgroups of the automorphism group of a Looijenga pair. This is achieved by constructing elliptic fibrations with an infinite Mordell–Weil group. In the present section we collect some well-known facts about elliptic fibrations that we will need later.
By an elliptic fibration we will mean a proper morphism
Y\to B
, where
B
is a smooth curve, such that the general fiber is a smooth curve of genus one.
For our purposes, all elliptic fibrations will be over
B=\mathbb{P}^{1}
, since under our assumptions
Y
is a
smooth projective
rational surface.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\pi:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be an elliptic fibration.
We define the Mordell–Weil group of
\pi
to be

\operatorname{MW}(\pi)=\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(Y_{\eta}),

where
Y_{\eta}
is the generic fiber.

The group
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
is abelian and finitely generated, and it acts by translation on each smooth fiber of
\pi
. If
\pi:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
is minimal, that is, if there are no
(-1)
-curves in the fibers of
\pi
, then
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
acts (faithfully) on
Y
by automorphisms. If, in addition,
\pi
has a section, then
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
acts simply transitively on the sections of
\pi
by translation.

Remark 3.2
.

In Definition
3.1
, we do not require
\pi
to have a section. If it does, then
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
can be identified with the set of sections.
The latter is sometimes given as a definition in the literature.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\pi_{i}:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
,
i=1,2
, be two different elliptic fibrations, and suppose there are subgroups
G_{i}\subset\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{i})
such that
G_{i}
acts by automorphisms on
Y
. Then the intersection
G_{1}\cap G_{2}\subset\operatorname{Aut}(Y)
is finite.

Proof.

Equivalently, we may assume that each
G_{i}
is free and show that
G_{1}\cap G_{2}
is trivial.
We follow the proof of
[
T12
, Corollary 7.2]
. By the Hodge Index Theorem, the intersection form has signature
(1,\rho(Y)-1)
on the Picard group of
Y
. Fix an ample divisor
H
. Then the quotient of the positive cone
C=\{\alpha\in\operatorname{Pic}(Y)\mid\alpha^{2}>0,\>\alpha\cdot H>0\}
by
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
can be identified with the
(\rho(Y)-1)
-dimensional hyperbolic space, and its group of isometries contains
O(\operatorname{Pic}(Y))
as a discrete subgroup
(cf.
[
T12
, §5]
).
Let
g\in G_{i}
be non-trivial. Using the homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(Y)\to O(\operatorname{Pic}(Y))\subset O(C/\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, we identify
g
with an infinite-order isometry of the hyperbolic space. Then the subgroup generated by
g
is an infinite discrete subgroup of isometries where each element fixes a point in the boundary of the cone
C
, namely the class of a fiber
F_{i}
, which has
F_{i}^{2}=0
.
Following
[
AVS93
, Theorem 2.6 of Chapter 5]
and its proof, this implies that
g
must be of parabolic type. Since parabolic motions have only one fixed point in the sphere at infinity, the fact that
F_{1}\neq F_{2}
implies that
G_{1}\cap G_{2}
must be trivial, as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
D
be a length
r
cycle of
(-2)
-curves. Identify
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
as above and suppose that
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(D)|_{D}
is torsion of order
m
. Then there is a minimal elliptic fibration
\pi:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
with
\pi^{*}(\infty)=mD
. In addition, if
m=1
then
\pi
has a section.

Proof.

For each
1\leq k\leq m-1
, we twist the tautological short exact sequence by
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(kD)
to obtain

(3.1)

0\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}((k-1)D)\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}(kD)\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}(kD)|_{D}\to 0.

First, we see that
H^{0}(D,\mathcal{O}_{D}(kD))=H^{1}(D,\mathcal{O}_{D}(kD))=0
. Indeed, call
L=\mathcal{O}_{Y}(kD)|_{D}
. Then
L
is torsion in
\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)
and nontrivial. Using Riemann–Roch,

\chi(L)=1-p_{a}(D)+\deg L=\deg L,

since
p_{a}(D)=1
.
But
L
torsion implies
\deg L=0=h^{0}(L)-h^{1}(L)
. Since
L\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)
but
L
is nontrivial,
h^{0}(L)=0
(cf.
[
F15
, Lemma 1.7]
), so we obtain
h^{0}(L)=h^{1}(L)=0
, as desired.

As a consequence, from (
3.1
) we get an isomorphism
H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(k-1)D)\xrightarrow{\sim}H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(kD))
for each
1\leq k\leq m-1
. Since
Y
is rational,
H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y})=0
, and then by induction we get
H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(kD))=0
for every
1\leq k\leq m-1
. In particular,
H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}((m-1)D))=0
.

Now consider (
3.1
) with
k=m
, that is,

0\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}((m-1)D)\to\mathcal{O}_{Y}(mD)\to\mathcal{O}_{D}\to 0.

Since
H^{1}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}((m-1)D))=0
, we get a short exact sequence of global sections. Therefore, the constant section
1\in H^{0}(D,\mathcal{O}_{D})
has a lift
t
to
H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(mD))
that does not vanish anywhere in
D
. On the other hand, the tautological section
s\in H^{0}(Y,\mathcal{O}_{Y}(D))
vanishes precisely along
D
with multiplicity one. Then
(s^{m}:t)
defines a morphism
\pi
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with fiber
mD
over
\infty
.

We see that
\pi
is an elliptic fibration. Let
F
be a fiber. Then
\deg K_{F}=(K_{Y}+F)\cdot F=-D\cdot(mD)=0
since
mD
itself is a fiber. Thus the general fiber has genus one. Furthermore,
\pi
is minimal. Indeed, any
(-1)
-curve in
Y
not contained in
D
must have intersection
1
with
D=-K_{Y}
, but a fiber of
\pi
other than
mD
does not intersect
D
.

Finally, when
m=1
, the
(-1)
-curves of
Y
are precisely the sections. This follows from the fact that a
(-1)
-curve intersects the fiber with
F\cdot C=D\cdot C=1
. Since
Y
is a rational surface of Picard rank
10-D^{2}=10
, it has a
(-1)
-curve by the classification of surfaces.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If
\pi:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a minimal elliptic fibration, then one of the fibers must be
D
with some multiplicity. This follows from the Kodaira canonical bundle formula
[
K64
]
.
Moreover, if
F
is some other reducible fiber,
F
must be a union of internal
(-2)
-curves with some multiplicities (cf. Kodaira’s classification of singular fibers of minimal elliptic fibrations
[
K63
]
).

In order to compute the rank of the Mordell–Weil group of an elliptic fibration, we will use the Shioda–Tate formula
[
S72
, Corollary 1.5]

(cf. also
[
DM22
, Lemma 5.1]
).

Theorem 3.6

(Shioda–Tate formula)
.

Let
\pi:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be an elliptic fibration.
Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
be the locus where the fibers
\pi^{-1}(p)
are singular. For each
p\in\Sigma
, let
m_{p}
be the number of irreducible components of
\pi^{-1}(p)
. Then
\rho(Y)=\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{MW}(\pi)+2+\sum_{p\in\Sigma}(m_{p}-1)
.

3.1.
The case r=7.

When
D
is a cycle of seven
(-2)
-curves, the root system
\Phi
can be described as follows. Let
(Y_{e},D_{e})
be the deformation equivalent pair with a split mixed Hodge structure on
H^{2}(Y_{e}\backslash D_{e})
, or equivalently, such that
\phi_{Y_{e}}=e
is the identity (cf.
[
F15
, Proposition 3.12]
). Then the elliptic fibration
Y_{e}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by Lemma
3.4
has two reducible fibers, which consist of
D
and another
I_{2}
fiber (a cycle of two
(-2)
-curves). Let
\beta
and
F-\beta
denote the two
(-2)
-curves of
I_{2}
, where
F
is the class of a fiber. Then, by

[
F15
, Proposition 9.16 and Corollary 9.20]

\Phi
is the root system of type
\tilde{A}_{1}
(that is, affine
{A}_{1}
) associated to the
I_{2}
fiber, which is given by

(3.2)

\Phi=\{\pm\beta+kF\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(Y,D)
be a Looijenga pair where
D
is a cycle of
(-2)
-curves as above and
\phi_{Y}(D)=1
. If
\phi_{Y}(\beta)\neq 1
, then
(Y,D)
does not contain any internal
(-2)
-curves.

Proof.

By Remark
2.7
, we just need to check that
\phi_{Y}(\alpha)\neq 1
for every
\alpha\in\Phi
. For a root
\alpha=\pm\beta+kF
, we compute

\phi_{Y}(\pm\beta+kF)=\phi_{Y}(\beta)^{\pm 1}\phi_{Y}(F)^{k}.

But
\phi_{Y}(F)=\phi_{Y}(D)=1
, because
D
is a fiber of the minimal elliptic fibration
Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
, so
\phi_{Y}(\alpha)=\phi_{Y}(\beta)^{\pm 1}\neq 1
.
∎

Moreover, (
3.2
) together with Remark
3.5
implies that if
(Y,D)
is given by a cycle of seven
(-2)
-curves, then any minimal elliptic fibration
Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
will have at most one reducible fiber besides
D
, consisting of a union of two
(-2)
-curves.

Corollary 3.8
.

If
D
is a cycle of seven
(-2)
-curves and
\pi:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a minimal elliptic fibration, then the Mordell–Weil group
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
is infinite.

Proof.

The Picard rank is
\rho(Y)=10-D^{2}=10
, because
D
is a cycle of
(-2)
-curves. Using the Shioda–Tate formula (Theorem
3.6
), the rank of
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
is

\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{MW}(\pi)=10-2-\sum_{p\in\Sigma}(m_{p}-1).

The reducible fibers are
D
and at most one extra fiber consisting of two
(-2)
-curves, so

\sum_{p\in\Sigma}(m_{p}-1)\leq(7-1)+(2-1)=7.

Therefore
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{MW}(\pi)\geq 10-2-7=1
and, in particular, the Mordell–Weil group
\operatorname{MW}(\pi)
is infinite.
∎

4.
Main result

In this section, we construct our main example. Consider the toric Looijenga pair
(\bar{Y},\bar{D})=(\bar{Y}_{e},\bar{D}_{e})
, where
\bar{D}_{e}
is a cycle of length
r=7
, with self-intersection cycle
(-1,-2,-1,-1,-1,-1,-2)
, and the period point
\phi_{\bar{Y}}=e
is the identity.
The toric variety
\bar{Y}
is determined by a fan with seven rays in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, so in particular
\rho(\bar{Y})=7-2=5
. Blowing up one interior point on each of the
(-1)
-components of
\bar{D}
yields a pair
(Y,D)
with a cycle of seven
(-2)
-curves
(see Figure
1
)
and
\rho(Y)=10
. We construct a pair
(Y,D)
by choosing the blow-up points in the following way.

Figure 1.
Y=\operatorname{Bl}_{q_{i}}\bar{Y}

Construction 4.1
.

Let
(Y,D)
be the blow-up of
(\bar{Y},\bar{D})
in one point
q_{i}\in\bar{D}_{i}^{\circ}
for each
(-1)
-component
\bar{D}_{i}
, in such a way that
\phi_{Y}:\Lambda(Y,D)\to\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(D)\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}
is torsion,
\phi_{Y}(D)=1
and
\phi_{Y}(\beta)\neq 1
, where
\beta
is the class appearing in (
3.2
).

The existence of
(Y,D)
with such choice for
\phi_{Y}
is guaranteed by Theorem
2.5
.

Remark 4.2
.

There is some flexibility, in that
\phi_{Y}
can be chosen to be any morphism satisfying the conditions stated in Construction
4.1
. Therefore, our main theorem is not strictly a statement about a single Looijenga pair, but rather a collection of examples.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(Y,D)
be as in Construction
4.1
. Then there is a minimal elliptic fibration
\pi_{1}:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
with
\pi_{1}^{*}(\infty)=D
and no other reducible fibers, and admitting a section.

Proof.

The existence of the minimal elliptic fibration with
\pi_{1}^{*}(\infty)=D
and admitting a section is guaranteed by Lemma
3.4
and the fact that
\phi_{Y}(D)=1
, which by definition means that
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(D)|_{D}
is trivial. Any other reducible fiber would consist of a union of internal
(-2)
-curves, with some multiplicities. But by Lemma
3.7
,
(Y,D)
has no internal
(-2)
-curves.
∎

Since
\pi_{1}
has a section,
\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{1})
acts simply transitively on the sections of
\pi_{1}
by translation.
Now let
C
and
C^{\prime}
be two sections of
\pi_{1}
and suppose that
C
and
C^{\prime}
intersect the same component
D_{i}
, that is,
C|_{D}=\mathcal{O}_{D}(p)
and
C^{\prime}|_{D}=\mathcal{O}_{D}(q)
for some
p,q\in D_{i}^{\circ}
. Then
C-C^{\prime}\in\Lambda(Y,D)
and therefore
(C-C^{\prime})|_{D}=\mathcal{O}_{D}(p-q)
must be torsion of order at most the order of
\phi_{Y}
. Hence, there are only finitely many choices for such
\mathcal{O}_{D}(p-q)
. But there are infinitely many sections, as the rank of
\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{1})
is positive by Corollary
3.8
. Then there must be a component
D_{i}
and some point
p\in D_{i}^{\circ}
through which there pass infinitely many such sections of
\pi_{1}
, and each section is a
(-1)
-curve. Thus, we have proved the following proposition.

D_{i}
of
D
and a point
p\in D_{i}^{\circ}

(-1)

Construction 4.5
.

Let
(Y,D)
be as in Construction
4.1
and
\pi_{1}
as in Proposition
4.3
. Let
p
be a point as in Proposition
4.4
, through which there pass infinitely many sections of
\pi_{1}
. Let
(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
be the Looijenga pair where
\widetilde{Y}
is obtained by blowing up
Y
at the point
p
and
\widetilde{D}
is the strict transform of
D
.

Observe that, for each
(-1)
-curve in
Y
passing through
p
, its strict transform becomes a
(-2)
-curve in
\widetilde{Y}
. Moreover, it is disjoint from
\widetilde{D}
. In particular,
\widetilde{Y}
contains infinitely many internal
(-2)
-curves, and therefore its Weyl group
W_{\widetilde{Y}}
is infinite. This fact will be crucial for the proof of Theorem
4.7
.
We also note that
\widetilde{D}
is a cycle of six
(-2)
-curves and one
(-3)
-curve, which implies that
(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
is negative definite and
\widetilde{D}^{2}=-1
.

.

\phi_{\widetilde{Y}}

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
be the Looijenga pair from Construction
4.5
. Then
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
is not commensurable with an arithmetic group.

Proof.

We use Theorem
1.5
as follows. Let
M=\Lambda(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
. Since
\widetilde{D}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{D}_{7}
are linearly independent, by Lemma
2.3
,
M
is a lattice of rank
10+1-7=4
. Moreover, by the Hodge Index Theorem, the signature of
M
is
(1,3)
. Every automorphism
\varphi\in\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
preserves the lattice
M
, so we get a group homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})\to O(M)
, whose image we denote by
S
. Then
S
is isomorphic to the quotient of
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
by a finite subgroup.
Indeed, if an automorphism
\varphi
acts trivially on
M
in addition to fixing each
\widetilde{D}_{i}
, then
\varphi
must act trivially on
\operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{Y})
. But the subgroup of
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y})
that acts trivially on
\operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{Y})
is finite.
As a consequence, we obtain that
S\doteq\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
, since the latter is virtually torsion-free.
In particular, it suffices to show that
S
is not commensurable with an arithmetic group.

First we need to show that
S
has infinite index in
O(M)
. For this, consider the Weyl group
W_{\widetilde{Y}}\subset O(\operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{Y}))
, which is of infinite order as seen earlier. It is not difficult to see that, given a class
\alpha
of an internal
(-2)
-curve, the simple reflection
s_{\alpha}
fixes the class of each
\widetilde{D}_{i}
. Therefore,
W_{\widetilde{Y}}
acts faithfully on
M
, and by abuse of notation we write
W_{\widetilde{Y}}\subset O(M)
. We will see that
W_{\widetilde{Y}}
and
S
intersect trivially.

Let
C^{++}_{D}\subset\operatorname{Pic}(\widetilde{Y})
be the full-dimensional subcone defined in
[
GHK15
, Definition 1.7]
.
By
[
GHK15
, Theorem 3.2]
, the set of internal
(-2)
-curves determines a decomposition of
C^{++}_{D}
into a collection of Weyl chambers on which
W_{\widetilde{Y}}
acts simply transitively. One of these chambers is identified with the ample cone of
\widetilde{Y}
, in the sense that its closure coincides with
\operatorname{Nef}(\widetilde{Y})
. Moreover,
M
intersects the closure of
C^{++}_{D}
in a face
F
. Hence, intersecting with
M
we get a chamber decomposition of
F
, where
W_{\widetilde{Y}}
acts simply transitively on the set of chambers, whereas
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
(and therefore also
S
) preserves the chamber
F\cap\operatorname{Nef}(\widetilde{Y})
. We conclude that
W_{\widetilde{Y}}\cap S
is trivial, so
S
has infinite index in
O(M)
.

Now consider the minimal elliptic fibration
\pi_{1}:Y\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by Proposition
4.3
and let
\widetilde{\pi}_{1}:\widetilde{Y}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be its composition with the blow-up
\widetilde{Y}=\operatorname{Bl}_{p}Y\to Y
. The only reducible fiber of
\pi_{1}
is
D
. Using the Shioda–Tate formula, we find that
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{1})=10-2-(7-1)=2
.
The group
\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{1})=\operatorname{MW}(\widetilde{\pi}_{1})
acts on
Y
by automorphisms, and we let
G
be (the free part of) the subgroup of
\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{1})
consisting of automorphisms that
fix the point
p
. Then
G
has finite index in
\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{1})
because
\phi_{Y}
is torsion (cf. the argument for Proposition
4.4
), and therefore
G\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Since the automorphisms in
G
lift to
\widetilde{Y}
, we have
G\subset\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y})
, and since
(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
is negative definite, passing to a finite-index subgroup we can assume
G\subset\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
. Recall that the homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})\to S\subset O(M)
has finite kernel, so it sends
G
into an isomorphic copy
G\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}\subset S
.

We claim that
G
has infinite index in
S
. To see this, we construct a second elliptic fibration as follows. Recall the Construction
4.5
of
(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
from
(Y,D)
. Let
q\in D_{i}^{\circ}
be another point in the same component as
p
, such that
\mathcal{O}_{Y}(q-p)
is a primitive
m
-th root of unity
\xi_{m}
where
m\neq 1
, and such that through
q
there passes another section
C:=\sigma(\mathbb{P}^{1})
of
\pi_{1}
. Such point
q
exists because we have chosen
\phi_{Y}
to be torsion and not the identity. Then
C
is a
(-1)
-curve in
Y
, and its strict transform
\widetilde{C}\subset\widetilde{Y}
is a
(-1)
-curve in
\widetilde{Y}
. Let
(Y^{\prime},D^{\prime})
be the Looijenga pair obtained by blowing down
\widetilde{C}
. Then
D^{\prime}
is a cycle of seven
(-2)
-curves, and by construction
\phi_{Y^{\prime}}(D^{\prime})=\xi_{m}
. By Lemma
3.4
, there is a minimal elliptic fibration
\pi_{2}:Y^{\prime}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
with
mD
as a non-reduced fiber. Let
\widetilde{\pi}_{2}:\widetilde{Y}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be its composition with the blow-up
\widetilde{Y}=\operatorname{Bl}_{q}Y^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
. By Corollary
3.8
,
\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{2})
is infinite. Similarly as before, there is a finite-index subgroup
H\subset\operatorname{MW}(\pi_{2})=\operatorname{MW}(\widetilde{\pi}_{2})
such that
H\subset\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
, and
by Lemma
3.3

we see that
G\cap H
is finite
(and in fact it must be trivial since
G
has been chosen to be torsion-free).
This proves that
G
has infinite index as a subgroup of
S
. By Theorem
1.5
, we conclude that
S
is not commensurable with an arithmetic group, and then the same is true for
\operatorname{Aut}(\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{D})
.
∎
