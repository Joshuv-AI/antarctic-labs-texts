Lattice calculation of the π 0 \pi^{0} , η \eta and η ′ \eta^{\prime} transition form factors and the hadronic light-by-light contribution to the muon g − 2 g-2
Antoine Gérardin, Willem E. A. Verplanke, Gen Wang, Zoltan Fodor, Jana N. Guenther, Laurent Lellouch, Kalman K. Szabo, Lukas Varnhorst

Abstract
In this paper we present a first ab-initio calculation of the π 0 \pi^{0} , η \eta and η ′ \eta^{\prime} transition form factors performed with physical light-quark masses. We provide a complete parametrization of the form factors that includes both single and double-virtual kinematics. Our results are compared with experimental measurements of the form factors in the space-like region and with the measured two-photon decay widths. In a second step, our parametrizations of the transition form factors are used to compute the dominant pseudoscalar-pole contributions to the hadronic light-by-light scattering in the muon g − 2 g-2 . Our final result reads a μ hlbl , ps − pole = ( 85.1 ± 5.2 ) × 10 − 11 a_{\mu}^{\rm hlbl,ps-pole}=(85.1\pm 5.2)\times 10^{-11} . Although the pion-pole is dominant, we confirm that, together, the η \eta and η ′ \eta^{\prime} provide roughly half of its contribution.

Introduction

The combined experimental result for the anomalous magnetic moment of the muon, from the Fermilab collaboration

The theory error is completely dominated by effects of the strong interaction which can be separated into two distinct contributions: the hadronic vacuum polarization (HVP) which enters at order

;
Abe et al. 2019

Grange et al. 2015
and a new experiment at J-PARC
Abe et al. 2019
is under construction.
To fully benefit from these two new experiments, a precision of 0.2% for the HVP and below 10% for the HLbL is required.

On the lattice, the main challenge consists in reducing the statistical error that increases rapidly at long distances and to control possibly large finite-volume corrections. Fortunately, both sources of uncertainty can be strongly suppressed if one knows the pion transition form factor (TFF) on the same set of gauge ensembles

. The knowledge of the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}

An alternative to the direct lattice calculation, is the data driven approach
Colangelo et al. 2014a
;
Colangelo et al. 2014b
;
Colangelo et al. 2015
;
Colangelo et al. 2017a
;
Colangelo et al. 2017b
. It is based on a dispersive framework, similar to the one used for the HVP, but is much more complicated due to the complex analytical structure of the four-point function involved in the HLbL diagram. It is expected that, among all possible intermediate states, the largest contributions are given by a handful of states: the light pseudoscalar mesons (
\pi^{0}
,
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
). In this dispersive framework, the contribution from the pseudoscalar states can be unambiguously computed once the TFFs, describing the interaction of the mesons with two virtual photons, are known (see Fig.
1
).

Although the form factors are, in principle, accessible through experiments, their measurement in the whole kinematic range relevant for the

, CLEO
Gronberg et al. 1998
and Belle

\mathrm{GeV}^{2}
. Recently, the BES III collaboration has presented preliminary results down to
0.3\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
.
The single-virtual
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
form factors have been measured in the space-like region by CELLO
Behrend et al. 1991
, CLEO
Gronberg et al. 1998
, BaBar
del Amo Sanchez et al. 2011
and the L3 experiment at LEP
Acciarri et al. 1998
, covering the region from
0.3\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
up to
40\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
. The BaBar collaboration has published first results for the double-virtual TFF associated with the
\eta^{\prime}
meson, but only for virtualities larger than
Q^{2}\approx 6.5\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}

Lees et al. 2018
.
When both photons are real, the form factors are related to the two-photon decay width. The latter has been measured for all three mesons
Tanabashi et al. 2018
;
Larin et al. 2020
and is an important constraint to the low-energy behavior of the TFFs.

In
Gérardin et al. 2016
;
Gérardin et al. 2019b
it has been shown that a precise lattice calculation of the pion TFF in the kinematical range relevant for the

a_{\mu}^{\mathrm{HLbL};\pi^{0}}=(59.7\pm 3.6)\times 10^{-11}
. This result is in good agreement with the data-driven estimate
Hoferichter et al. 2018a
;
Hoferichter et al. 2018b
.
Using similar techniques, the
\eta

In this work, we provide a new estimate of the pion-pole contribution, using a different fermionic action as compared to
Gérardin et al. 2019b
, and we extend the calculation to the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
mesons. We provide parametrizations of the form factors at the physical point that are then used to estimate the light pseudoscalar-pole contributions to the HLbL diagram.
To this end, we are preparing a dedicated spectroscopy analysis of the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
mesons using staggered quarks on the same set of ensembles
BMW
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
we describe our strategy to extract the TFFs in Euclidean spacetime using lattice QCD simulations. In Section
III
we present our lattice setup and explain how the relevant correlation functions are computed with staggered quarks. Then, in Section
IV
, we present our analysis and we discuss the main sources of systematic uncertainties.
Finally, in Section
V

Tables with the coefficients of the TFF parametrizations in the continuum limit, and the associated covariance matrices, are provided in Appendix
A

B

Methodology

II.1
Form factors

In this section, we follow the notations introduced in
Gérardin et al. 2016
;
Gérardin et al. 2019b
. The TFF describing the interaction between a pseudoscalar meson
P=\pi^{0},\eta,\eta^{\prime}
, with momentum
p
, and two off-shell photons, with momenta
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, such that
p=q_{1}+q_{2}
, is defined via the following matrix element

M_{\mu\nu}(p,q_{1})=i\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,e^{iq_{1}\cdot x}\,\langle\Omega|T\{J_{\mu}(x)J_{\nu}(0)\}|P(p)\rangle=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\,q_{1}^{\alpha}\,q_{2}^{\beta}\,{\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}(q_{1}^{2},q_{2}^{2})\,.

(1)

In this equation,
J_{\mu}
is the hadronic component of the electromagnetic current and
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}
is the fully antisymmetric tensor with
\epsilon^{0123}=1
.
We have implicitly treated the meson as an asymptotic state.
This is true for the pion but also for the
\eta
meson in the isospin limit of QCD (the decays
\eta\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}\pi^{0}
and
\eta\to 3\pi^{0}
break isospin symmetry).
On the other hand, the
\eta^{\prime}
meson can decay via the strong interaction, even in the isospin limit, and its main decay modes are
\eta^{\prime}\to\eta\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
and
\eta^{\prime}\to\eta\pi^{0}\pi^{0}
.
However, the smallness of the decay width justifies the narrow-width approximation in which the meson is treated as stable particle.

\displaystyle P_{3}(x)

(4c)

\eta_{8}
and
\eta_{0}

To extract the matrix element in Eq. (
2
), we consider the following three-point correlation functions

C^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P})=\sum_{\vec{x},\vec{z}}\langle J_{\mu}(\vec{z},t_{i})J_{\nu}(\vec{0},t_{f})\mathcal{O}_{i}(\vec{x},t_{0})\rangle\,e^{i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}e^{-i\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\vec{z}}\,,

(5)

where
t_{P}={\rm min}(t_{f}-t_{0},t_{i}-t_{0})
is the minimal time separation between the pseudoscalar interpolating operator and either of the two vector currents and
\tau=t_{i}-t_{f}
. The lattice operators
\mathcal{O}_{i}
have the same quantum numbers as the pseudoscalar densities
P_{i}
. The precise definition of those operators will be given in Section
III.2
.
For the pion, neglecting excited states and wrap around contributions, we obtain the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle C^{(3)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{z}}\langle 0|J_{\mu}(\vec{z},\tau)J_{\nu}(\vec{0},0)|\pi(\vec{p})\rangle e^{-i\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\vec{z}}\times\frac{1}{2E_{\pi}}\langle\pi(\vec{p})|\mathcal{O}_{3}|0\rangle e^{E_{\pi}(t_{0}-t_{f})}

(6)

where
E_{\pi}
is the energy of the pion and
Z_{\pi}=\langle\pi|\mathcal{O}_{3}|0\rangle
is the overlap factor of the pseudoscalar operator with the pion state. It leads to

\widetilde{A}^{(\pi)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau)=\lim_{t_{P}\rightarrow+\infty}\widetilde{A}_{\mu\nu}^{(\pi);{\rm eff}}(\tau,t_{P})\,,\quad\widetilde{A}_{\mu\nu}^{(\pi);{\rm eff}}(\tau,t_{P})=\frac{2E_{\pi}}{Z_{\pi}}e^{E_{\pi}(t_{f}-t_{0})}C^{(3)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P})\,.

(7)

A similar expression could be used for the
\eta
meson, looking at the correlator
C^{(8)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P})
. Indeed, the correlation function
C^{(8)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P})
is expected to be dominated by the
\eta

\eta^{\prime}
that can be seen as an excited state. This is the procedure followed in
Alexandrou et al. 2022
. However, this method has some disadvantages. First, the long time asymptotic is reached at large values of
t_{P}
where the signal-to-noise ratio deteriorates rapidly. Second, this method is not applicable to the
\eta^{\prime}
state. A comparison of two approaches for the
\eta

Instead, the
\eta

\eta^{\prime}

2\times 2

i=0,8

\displaystyle C^{(i)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{z}}\langle 0|J_{\mu}(\vec{z},\tau)J_{\nu}(\vec{0},0)|\eta(\vec{p})\rangle e^{-i\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\vec{z}}\times\frac{1}{2E_{\eta}}\langle\eta(\vec{p})|\mathcal{O}_{i}|0\rangle e^{E_{\eta}(t_{0}-t_{f})}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{z}}\langle 0|J_{\mu}(\vec{z},\tau)J_{\nu}(\vec{0},0)|\eta^{\prime}(\vec{p})\rangle e^{-i\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\vec{z}}\times\frac{1}{2E_{\eta^{\prime}}}\langle\eta^{\prime}(\vec{p})|\mathcal{O}_{i}|0\rangle e^{E_{\eta}^{\prime}(t_{0}-t_{f})}\,.

(8)

Or, in a more compact matrix notation

\begin{pmatrix}C_{\mu\nu}^{(8)}\\[4.2679pt]
C_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}T^{(8)}_{\eta}&T^{(8)}_{\eta^{\prime}}\\
T^{(0)}_{\eta}&T^{(0)}_{\eta^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{A}^{(\eta)}_{\mu\nu}\\[3.69885pt]
\widetilde{A}^{(\eta^{\prime})}_{\mu\nu}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(9)

with

T_{n}^{(i)}=\frac{Z_{n}^{(i)}}{2E_{n}}e^{-E_{n}(t_{f}-t_{0})}\,,\quad\widetilde{A}^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(\tau)=\sum_{\vec{z}}\langle 0|J_{\mu}(\vec{z},\tau)J_{\nu}(\vec{0},0)|n(\vec{p})\rangle\,e^{-i\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\vec{z}}\,.

(10)

. Inverting Eq. (

\eta

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}^{(\eta)}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}\phi_{I}\ \frac{C_{\mu\nu}^{(8)}}{T_{\eta}^{(8)}}+\sin^{2}\phi_{I}\ \frac{C_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}}{T_{\eta}^{(0)}}

(11a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}^{(\eta^{\prime})}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\sin^{2}\phi_{I}\ \frac{C_{\mu\nu}^{(8)}}{T_{\eta^{\prime}}^{(8)}}+\cos^{2}\phi_{I}\ \frac{C_{\mu\nu}^{(0)}}{T_{\eta^{\prime}}^{(0)}}

The TFFs are the key ingredients to compute the pseudoscalar-pole contribution to the HLbL diagram in the dispersive framework introduced in
Colangelo et al. 2014a
;
Colangelo et al. 2014b
;
Colangelo et al. 2015
;
Colangelo et al. 2017a
;
Colangelo et al. 2017b
.
The master equation, first derived in Ref.
Jegerlehner and Nyffeler 2009
, holds exactly for the pion and the
\eta
in the isospin limit and for the
\eta^{\prime}
in the narrow-width approximation.
Starting from the two-loop integral (see Fig.
1
), all angular integrals, except one, are performed using the Gegenbauer polynomial technique leading to

a_{\mu}^{\mathrm{HLbL};P}=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\right)^{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!dQ_{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!dQ_{2}\!\!\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!d\beta\,\Big(w_{1}(Q_{1},Q_{2},\beta)\,{\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}(-Q_{1}^{2},-(Q_{1}+Q_{2})^{2})\times\\
{\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}(-Q_{2}^{2},0)+w_{2}(Q_{1},Q_{2},\beta)\,{\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}(-Q_{1}^{2},-Q_{2}^{2})\,{\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}(-(Q_{1}+Q_{2})^{2},0)\Big)\,,

(16)

where
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are two model-independent weight functions,
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are the norms of the two Euclidean four-momentum vectors and
\beta=\cos\theta
is related to the angle between them,
Q_{1}\cdot Q_{2}=Q_{1}Q_{2}\cos\theta
.
The weight functions and the shape of the integrand will be further discussed in Section
V.4
, along with the presentation of the lattice data.

Lattice ensembles

This work is based on a subset of gauge ensembles generated by the Budapest-Marseille-Wuppertal collaboration

III.2
Staggered correlation functions

The extraction of the TFFs requires the calculation of the three-point functions in Eq. (
5
) and the corresponding pseudoscalar two-point functions to extract the pseudoscalar masses and overlap factors that appear in Eq. (
11
). We now describe the construction of those correlators.

In the staggered formalism, the operators are chosen such that the overall correlation function is a taste singlet.
Since we are interested in the extraction of the
\eta^{(\prime)}
TFFs, both the two- and three-point correlation functions involve Wick contractions with quark-disconnected contributions for the pseudoscalar density. Such contributions require taste-singlet pseudoscalar operators.
Using the notations of
Follana et al. 2007
, where operators are given in the staggered spin-taste basis as
\Gamma_{S}\otimes\Gamma_{T}
, the operator would correspond to a
\gamma_{5}\otimes 1
operator.
In
BMW
we consider two operators
Altmeyer et al. 1993
: a time-local 3-link operator and a time non-local 4-link operator where the operator has support on two timeslices.
In this work, we use the 4-link operator that has the advantage to highly suppress the parity partner state such that no oscillation in time is observed in the analysis of the two-point correlation functions.
It makes the subsequent analysis of the two and three point functions much simpler.

For the vector current, we also use a taste singlet operator. More precisely, we use the conserved one-link operators

J_{\mu}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu}(x)\left[\overline{\chi}(x+a\hat{\mu})U_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(x)\chi(x)+\overline{\chi}(x)U_{\mu}(x)\chi(x+a\hat{\mu})\right]\,,

(17)

where
\chi
is a single component staggered fermion field and
\eta_{\mu}
is a phase factor (we use the convention of
Follana et al. 2007
, also used in
BMW
). The gauge links
U_{\mu}(x)
ensure that correlation functions are gauge invariant.
In Eq. (
17
), the current is defined for a single flavor and the electromagnetic charge factors are not included.
Our choice is motivated by the presence of disconnected contributions that involve a single vector loop and also require a taste-singlet operator. Moreover, this current does not require multiplicative renormalization. We note that this operator was already used in
Borsanyi et al. 2021
to compute the LO-HVP contribution.

Among the 16 tastes, the taste-singlet pion is the heaviest. Taste splitting is expected to decrease as O(
a^{2}
) as we approach the continuum limit
Lepage 1998
;
Lepage 1999
.
Between our coarsest and finest lattice spacings the taste-singlet pion mass decreases from about 400 MeV to 200 MeV while the goldstone pion
\gamma_{5}\otimes\gamma_{5}
is kept at the physical pion mass: a strong dependence on the lattice spacing is possible.

III.3
Computational strategy

Figure 2:
The four different Wick contraction topologies that are present in the three-point correlation function. Some topologies contains several diagrams that are not shown for brevity. The blue and green blobs represent a pseudoscalar or a vector current insertion respectively. Only the first two contractions contribute to the pion TFF.

The three-point correlation function in Eq. (
5
) receives potential contributions from four Wick contractions, shown in Fig.
2
. In this section, the pseudoscalar and vector currents are defined for a single flavor and the correct charges factors associated with the operators in Eq. (
4
) need to be included when building the
\pi^{0}
,
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
correlators.

The connected contribution to the three-point function is computed using similar techniques to the one used in
Gérardin et al. 2016
;
Gérardin et al. 2019b
. First, a point source is created at a random position on the timslice
t_{f}
. For each value of the meson momentum
\vec{p}
, the pseudoscalar operator is applied to the solution vector, restricted to the timeslice
t_{0}
, and then used as a source for a sequential inversion.
The correlation function is finally obtained by contracting the original and the sequential solution vectors with appropriate phase factors and shifts on timeslice
t_{i}
.
This method allows us to access many photon virtualities
\vec{q}_{1}
and
\vec{q}_{2}
at no additional cost. Only additional pion momenta, or additional values of
t_{P}
, defined after Eq. (
5
), require new evaluations of the quark propagator.

The main computational challenge is to obtain a good signal for the quark-disconnected contributions.
First, we need to compute the pseudoscalar and vector loop functions

\displaystyle L_{P}^{(f)}(x_{0},\vec{p})

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{a}{L}\right)^{3}\sum_{\vec{x}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathcal{O}_{P}\,S_{f}(x,x)\,\right]\,e^{i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}

(18a)

\displaystyle L_{V;\mu}^{(f)}(x_{0};\vec{q}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a}{L}\right)^{3}\sum_{\vec{x}}\,\eta_{\mu}(x)\,\mathrm{Tr}\left[S_{f}(x,x+a\hat{\mu})U_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}(x)+S_{f}(x+a\hat{\mu},x)U_{\mu}(x)\right]\,e^{i\vec{q}_{1}\cdot\vec{x}}

(18b)

with
S_{f}
the quark propagator of flavor
f=(l,s)
,
\mathcal{O}_{P}
the pseudoscalar taste-singlet 4-link operator presented in
BMW
and where the trace runs over color indices. Those functions have been stored on disk for all time slices and all required values of the momenta.
The pseudoscalar loops have been computed using a combination of low-mode averaging (LMA)
DeGrand and Schaefer 2005
;
Giusti et al. 2004
and all-mode-averaging (AMA)
Bali et al. 2010
;
Blum et al. 2013
;
Shintani et al. 2015
combined with the Venkataraman-Kilcup variance reduction (VKVR) trick
Gregory et al. 2008
to reach the intrinsic gauge noise. More details are provided in Ref.
BMW
. For the vector loops, the VKVR trick is not applicable. Instead, since we are only interested in the light minus strange combination
L_{V}^{(l-s)}
(this is the only combination that appears in the isospin limit of QCD), we adopt the split-even stochastic estimator introduced in
Giusti et al. 2019b
combined with LMA and AMA to reach the gauge noise (see Appendix
B
for more details).

Since we want to access many photon virtualities, the vector-vector two-point correlation functions with flavor
f
, and denoted
D^{(f)}_{VV;\mu\nu}(t_{f},t_{i};\vec{p},\vec{q}_{1})
(third diagram in Fig.
2
), has been computed using point sources for all source (
t_{i}
) and sink (
t_{f}
) time slices.
It is a noisy contribution and we found that it was optimal to use AMA with 64 sloppy inversions per exact solve for the light quark and 16 for the strange quark. Sloppy inversions are defined by a fixed number of iterations, chosen such that the bias correction is small as compared to the statistical error. In practice, we used 1, 2 or 3 exact solves per time slice depending on the ensemble.
The corresponding three-point correlators, with all flavor combinations, are finally obtained through

C^{(3)}_{\mathrm{P}_{f_{2}}-\mathrm{VV}_{f_{1}};\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P};\vec{p},\vec{q}_{1})=\frac{a}{T}\sum_{t_{i}}\ \langle D^{(f_{1})}_{VV;\mu\nu}(t_{i}+\tau,t_{i};\vec{p},\vec{q}_{1})L^{(f_{2})}_{P}(t_{i}-t_{P},\vec{p})\rangle\,,

(19)

where translation invariance in the time direction has been used.

The computation of the pseudoscalar-vector two-point function (second diagram in Fig.
2
) is similar, except that we use a single stochastic source per time slice, at the pseudoscalar insertion, but with support on the whole time slice. We again use AMA to reduce the cost of the analysis. Since this contribution is much smaller, we use only 16 sloppy inversions per exact solve (2 for the strange quark). In this case, the final correlators for both flavors
(f)
in the PV loop are computed as

C^{(3)}_{\mathrm{PV}_{f}-\mathrm{V};\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P};\vec{p},\vec{q}_{1})=\frac{a}{T}\sum_{t_{i}}\ \langle L^{(l-s)}_{V;\nu}(t_{i};\vec{q}_{1}-\vec{p})D^{(f)}_{V_{\mu}P}(t_{i}+\tau,t_{i}-t_{P};\vec{p},\vec{q}_{1})\\
+L^{(l-s)}_{V;\mu}(t_{i}+\tau;-\vec{q}_{1})D^{(f)}_{V_{\nu}P}(t_{i},t_{i}-t_{P};\vec{p},\vec{p}-\vec{q}_{1})\rangle\,.

The remaining correlation function (last diagram in Fig.
2
) is the purely disconnected contribution,
C^{(3)}_{\mathrm{P}_{f}-\mathrm{V}-\mathrm{V};\mu\nu}(\tau,t_{P};\vec{p},\vec{q}_{1})
. It can be easily computed from the loop functions in Eq. (
18
). Again, more information is provided in Appendix
B
.

Finally, all correlation functions have been computed for two values of the meson momentum (
\vec{p}=\vec{0}
and
\vec{p}=(2\pi/L)\vec{z}
). The number of photon momenta (
\vec{q}_{1}
) is tuned on each ensemble to efficiently probe the (
Q_{1}^{2},Q_{2}^{2}
) plane up to 2 GeV
2
for the pion and 4 GeV
2
for the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}

IV
Analysis

In Ref.
Gérardin et al. 2019b
, the amplitude
\widetilde{A}_{\mu\nu}(\tau)
was written in terms of a single scalar function
\widetilde{A}^{(1)}(\tau)
and its derivative

\widetilde{A}_{\mu\nu}(\tau)=-iQ^{E}_{\mu\nu}\ \widetilde{A}^{(1)}(\tau)+P^{E}_{\mu\nu}\ \frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{A}^{(1)}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}(\tau)\,.

(20)

The two coefficients
P^{E}_{\mu\nu}=iP_{\mu\nu}
and
Q^{E}_{\mu\nu}=(-i)^{n_{0}}Q_{\mu\nu}
do not depend on
\omega_{1}
and are given by

q_{\mu\nu}\equiv\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}q_{1}^{\alpha}q_{2}^{\beta}=P_{\mu\nu}\omega_{1}+Q_{\mu\nu}\,.

(21)

In the analysis, all equivalent contributions to
\widetilde{A}^{(1)}
are averaged over and we explicitly use the Bose symmetry relation

\widetilde{A}_{\mu\nu}(\tau;\vec{q}_{1},\vec{q}_{2})=\widetilde{A}_{\nu\mu}(-\tau;\vec{q}_{2},\vec{q}_{1})\,e^{-E_{P}\tau}\,.

(22)

Once
\widetilde{A}^{(1)}(\tau)
, or equivalently
\widetilde{A}_{\mu\nu}(\tau)
is known, the integration over
\tau
in Eq. (
3
) is done using the Simpson rule.
In Section
IV.1
, we will often display individual contributions to
\widetilde{A}^{(1)}(\tau)
that originate from the different Wick contractions of Fig.
2
.
The scalar function
\widetilde{A}^{(1)}(\tau)
is strongly affected by staggered oscillations due to the contribution of the parity partner state. We stress that these oscillations do not disappear as we take the continuum limit but the contribution of the parity partner state to the form factor does. Thus, for clarity, we will often display the smeared function

f(\tau)|_{\rm smr}=\frac{1}{4}f(\tau-a)+\frac{1}{2}f(\tau)+\frac{1}{4}f(\tau+a)\,,

(23)

instead of
f
itself.

IV.1
Wick contractions

The contributions from the four Wick contractions of Fig.
2
to the amplitude
\widetilde{A}^{(1)}(\tau)
are shown in Fig.
4
for one ensemble at our finest lattice spacing.
In the isospin limit, the last two contractions, that involve a pseudoscalar loop, vanish for the pion and contribute only to the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
transition form factors.
In
Gérardin et al. 2019b
;
Feng et al. 2012
, the authors have shown that the second diagram, with a single vector loop, is numerically sub-dominant.
It is negative and contributes only at the percent level to the pion TFF. In particular, this diagram would exactly vanish in the SU(3) flavor limit.
For the same reason, the last diagram of Fig.
2
, that contains two vector loops, is expected to give a very small contribution to the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
form factors. Thus, for the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
TFFs, most of the signal comes from the purely connected contribution and from the disconnected contribution that contains a single pseudoscalar loop. Both contributions have opposite sign and the cancellation is stronger for the
\eta^{\prime}
.

IV.2
Tail correction

Because of the finite time extent of the lattice and of the increase of the statistical error, the integration over
|\tau|
, the time separation between the two vector currents, is truncated at
\tau_{c}\approx 1.5
fm.
In Ref.
Gérardin et al. 2019b
, the large-
\tau
dependence of the amplitude was fitted assuming a vector meson dominance (VMD) or a lowest meson dominance (LMD) parametrization. This parametrization was then used to estimate the integrand above the cut.
With staggered quarks, this procedure is more complicated due to the contribution of the parity partner state that appears with the usual factor
(-1)^{\tau}
. This contribution, responsible for the presence of oscillations at the level of the integrand, largely cancels between consecutive time slices and vanishes in the TFFs in the continuum limit. However, since we have little knowledge about the parity parter state contribution, and to avoid complicated fits, we decided to follow a different strategy.
First, we compute the TFF neglecting the tail of the integrand above the cut. The resulting form factor is fitted using an LMD parametrization in the range
Q^{2}\leq Q^{2}_{\rm tail}=1
GeV
2
where the model provides an acceptable description of our data.
In a second step, this parametrization is used to estimate the missing tail contribution at the level of the amplitude (we use the equations derived in Appendix A of Ref.
Gérardin et al. 2019b
).
In principle, this method can be applied iteratively until it converges.
In practice, since we use a conservative value of
\tau_{c}
, we find that at most two iterations already suffice at our level of precision.
To avoid any bias due to this tail correction, we remove all points where the tail contributes more than 10% to the integrand in Eq. (
3
).

IV.3
Finite-size effects

Figure 5:
Pion TFF in the double-virtual regime using two different volumes (

The calculation of the disconnected diagram with a single pseudoscalar loop (third diagram in Fig.
2
) is numerically expensive and dominates the cost of our simulations.
In this section we study the possibility of using smaller physical volumes to increase the statistical precision.

When using smaller volumes, two different kind of finite-size effects need to be considered.
First, it was observed in Ref.
Gérardin et al. 2016
that the finite-time extent of the lattice potentially leads to large finite-time effects (FTE) due to backward propagating pions. Fortunately, a simple procedure was proposed to correct for this effect. Second, we need to consider finite-spatial-extent effects.
This can be done by looking at the pion TFF for which a high statistical precision has been achieved on both large (
L=6
fm) and small (
L=3
fm) ensembles.
Since the mass of the taste-singlet pseudoscalar meson decreases as we approach the continuum limit, we focus on our finest lattice spacing where the taste-singlet pion mass is approximately 200 MeV. The results for
L=6
fm and
L=3
fm boxes are depicted on Fig.
5
before and after FTE correction. In fact, when one corrects for FTE, the data for the two different box sizes agree within statistical errors, even at the lowest accessible virtualities
Q^{2}
. For the pion TFF, that is extracted exclusively from
L\approx 6\penalty\
fm ensembles, finite-size effects (FSE) are thus neglected.

Large volumes allow us to probe much smaller virtualities.
This feature is very important for the pion-pole contribution, but to a lesser extent for the
\eta
- and
\eta^{\prime}
-pole contributions. First
a_{\mu}^{\mathrm{HLbL};\eta}
and
a_{\mu}^{\mathrm{HLbL};\eta^{\prime}}
are less sensitive to the region close to the origin, see Table
2
.
Second, as the pseudoscalar mass increases, lower virtualities can be reached, even in small volumes.
To estimate the FSE due to the use of small spatial volumes at fine lattice spacings, the pion-pole contribution is computed from Eq. (
16
) but using the weight functions of the
\eta^{(\prime)}
meson. The relative difference between small and large volumes is then taken as our systematic.

When using Eq. (
7

\eta

\Delta E\,t_{P}\equiv(E_{\eta^{\prime}}-E_{\eta})t_{P}

(24)

where the ellipsis represents higher excited state contributions.
The ratio of overlap factors strongly depends on the choice of interpolating operator used for the
\eta

\Delta E\approx 400
MeV.
With staggered quarks, because of taste breaking effects, the mass gap between the two taste-singlet mesons decreases at large lattice spacings: we find

With our choice of interpolating operator, and at the physical
\eta^{(^{\prime})}
masses, we find that

a_{\mu}^{\rm hlbl}

IV.5
Treatment of statistical and systematic errors

Statistical errors are estimated using the jackknife method after blocking. All observables are computed on the same set of gauge field configurations and using the same block size such that we can easily propagate statistical errors. The lattice spacing was computed in
Borsanyi et al. 2021
, with a relative precision at the permil level, but using a different blocking procedure. The lattice spacing enters as a trivial factor in the conversion of the TFF and the virtualities in physical units but also in the continuum extrapolations. We have generated pseudo-samples with a gaussian distribution to propagate the error on the lattice spacing.
This error is always sub-dominant so that it is safe to neglect the correlation of the lattice spacing with the quantities studied here.

During the analysis several choices are made. First, to extract the form factors on individual ensembles, we need to choose
\Delta t_{P}
(for the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
mesons, see Section
IV.4
), the fitting range
Q^{2}_{\rm tail}
for the LMD or VMD model used for the tail correction, the values of
\omega_{1}
that are used to samples the orbits (see Eq. (
3
) and above) and the strategy to compute the pseudoscalar energies and overlap factors (see Section
V.4.2
).
Second, in the continuum extrapolation, we can choose the order of the
z
-expansion described in Section
V.2
, add higher order terms in the continuum extrapolation or perform cuts in lattice spacings.
Several analyses have been performed by varying all these choices. Since some fits do not include all correlations, the systematic error is estimated assuming a flat weight among these representative variations. An explicit example is given in Section
V.4.2
where we compute the pseudoscalar-pole contributions.

V.2
Parametrization of
{\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}
and extrapolation to the physical point

To extrapolate the form factor to the continuum limit, we fit our lattice data using the modified
z
-expansion introduced in
Gérardin et al. 2019b
;
Boyd et al. 1996

P(Q_{1}^{2},Q_{2}^{2})\ {\cal F}_{P\gamma^{*}\gamma^{*}}(-Q_{1}^{2},-Q_{2}^{2})=\\
\sum_{n,m=0}^{N}c_{nm}(a)\,\left(z_{1}^{n}+(-1)^{N+n}\frac{n}{N+1}\,z_{1}^{N+1}\right)\,\left(z_{2}^{m}+(-1)^{N+m}\frac{m}{N+1}\,z_{2}^{N+1}\right)\,,

(25)

where
z_{k}
are conformal variables

\displaystyle z_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{t_{c}+Q_{k}^{2}}-\sqrt{t_{c}-t_{0}\vphantom{Q_{k}^{2}}}}{\sqrt{t_{c}+Q_{k}^{2}}+\sqrt{t_{c}-t_{0}\vphantom{Q_{k}^{2}}}},\quad k=1,2,

(26)

c_{nm}
are coefficients symmetric in
m
and
n
,
t_{c}=4m_{\pi}^{2}
maps the branch cut of the TFF onto the unit circle
\left|z_{k}\right|=1
and
t_{0}
is a free parameter.
The choice
t_{0}=t_{c}(1-\sqrt{1+Q_{\max}^{2}/t_{c}})

ensures that the maximum value of
\left|z_{k}\right|
is minimized in the momentum range
Q^{2}<Q_{\max}^{2}
.
Finally, the function
P(Q_{1}^{2},Q_{2}^{2})
is an arbitrary analytic function. In
Gérardin et al. 2019b
it was argued that a convenient choice is

P(Q_{1}^{2},Q_{2}^{2})=1+\frac{Q_{1}^{2}+Q_{2}^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}\,,

(27)

such that the form factor parametrization decreases as
1/Q^{2}
at large virtualities, even at finite value of

\delta_{nm}a^{4}

\gamma_{nm}

;

V.3
Normalization of the form factors

When both photons are real, the form factor is related to the decay rate of the pseudoscalar meson to two photons. At leading order in QED

\Gamma(P\to\gamma\gamma)=\frac{\pi\alpha^{2}m_{P}^{3}}{4}\mathcal{F}^{2}_{P\gamma\gamma}(0,0)

(30)

where
\alpha
is the fine-structure constant. The neutral pion decay width has been measured by the PrimEx
Larin et al. 2011
and PrimEx-II
Larin et al. 2020
experiments with a precision of 1.5% leading to the combined result
\Gamma(\pi^{0}\to\gamma\gamma)=7.802(52)_{\rm stat}(105)_{\rm syst}
eV. It translates to a relative precision of 0.75% on the normalization of the form factor.
From the PDG average
Workman et al. 2022
, the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
decay widths are
\Gamma(\eta\to\gamma\gamma)=516\pm 18
eV and
\Gamma(\eta^{\prime}\to\gamma\gamma)=4.28\pm 0.19
keV. It corresponds to a relative precision of 1.7% and 2.2% respectively.
In the case of the
\eta
meson, a significant tension exists between the Primakoff measurement
Browman et al. 1974
and the results from collider experiments
Bartel et al. 1985
;
Williams et al. 1988
;
Roe et al. 1990
;
Baru et al. 1990
;
Babusci et al. 2013
. A new measurement by PrimEx-Eta is expected in the comings years
Liping Gan 2022
.

V.4
Pseudoscalar-pole contribution to HLbL scattering in
(g-2)_{\mu}

The master formula Eq. (
16
) requires the knowledge of the TFF at any spacelike virtuality and the dominant contribution comes from the low-energy region with
Q^{2}\lesssim 2\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
. Since the single-virtual TFF appears explicitly, this specific kinematic is important.
For the pion, it was noticed in
Gérardin et al. 2019b
that the single-virtual TFF is more difficult to extract on the lattice and this observation motivated the inclusion of a moving frame for the pion to improve the covering of large virtualities. The situation tends to be better for the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
mesons due to their heavier masses: we probe larger virtualities and the signal deteriorates more slowly at large virtualities.

A detailed analysis of the weight functions
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
has been done in
Nyffeler 2016
and we simply mention their most relevant properties. First, the second weight function
w_{2}
is typically one order of magnitude smaller than
w_{1}
and, at large virtualities, the weight function
w_{1}
goes to zero as

w_{1}(Q_{1},Q_{2},\tau)\underset{Q_{1}^{2}\to\infty}{\sim}Q_{1}^{-1}\,,\quad w_{1}(Q_{1},Q_{2},\tau)\underset{Q_{2}^{2}\to\infty}{\sim}Q_{2}^{-2}\,,\quad w_{1}(Q,Q,\tau)\underset{Q^{2}\to\infty}{\sim}Q^{-2}\,.

(32)

In particular, there is a long tail at constant value of
Q_{2}^{2}
.
Second, the only dependence on the pseudoscalar mass appears as pre-factors
(Q_{2}^{2}+m_{P}^{2})^{-1}
and
((Q_{1}+Q_{2})^{2}+m_{P}^{2})^{-1}
in
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
respectively. Thus, we expect the saturation of the integrand, as a function of the
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, to be slower for heavier pseudoscalar mesons.
In
Nyffeler 2016
, it was noted that, for the bulk of the contribution, the relation
w_{1}^{\eta}=(1/6)w_{1}^{\pi}
and
w_{1}^{\eta^{\prime}}=(1/2.5)w_{1}^{\eta}
approximately holds.

V.4.2
Discussion of systematic errors

In this section, we discuss the different analysis variations that have been included to estimate the systematic errors using the procedure presented in Section
IV.5
. A summary plot is provided in Fig.
13
.

For the pion, three different procedures have been used to extract the mass and overlap factor with our interpolating operator: we start with a two-exponential fit in a time range that leads to a good chi-square (2exp). In a second step, this fit is used to determine a fit range where the excited state contribution is negligible and the data are fitted by a single-exponential (1exp). Finally, we perform a combined fit where both pion momenta are fitted simultaneously assuming the validity of the dispersion relation (comb). Similarly, for the
\eta^{(\prime)}
mesons, three different procedures, described in
BMW
, have been considered. In this case, the masses and overlap factors are obtained by fitting a correlation matrix with both vanishing and non-vanishing pseudoscalar momenta. At vanishing momentum, it is often advantageous to consider the forward derivative
D(t)=C(t+a)-C(t)
that significantly reduces the statistical noise. Thus, the three variations refer to the combinations
(C,C)
,
(D,C)
and
(D,D)
where
(D,C)
means that the forward derivative
D
is used at vanishing momenta while the original correlator
C
is used for the moving frame.

For all TFFs, three samplings of the orbit shown in Fig.
3
have been considered. They differ by the number of
\omega
-values for each orbit,
N_{\omega}=15,20,25
. We also considered three methods for the tail correction, that differ by the value of
Q^{2}_{\rm tail}=(0.75,0.75,1.25)\penalty\
GeV and/or the model (VMD, LMD, LMD) used to fit the lattice data in the region
Q_{1}^{2},Q_{2}^{2}<Q^{2}_{\rm tail}
.

For the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
TFFs, we also considered two different values of
\Delta t_{P}
. Our optimal choice, from Section
IV.4
, and a more conservative choice.

In the continuum extrapolation of the pion TFFs, we have considered
a^{4}
correction as explained in Section
V.2
. For the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
mesons, the lattice data are not precise enough to test higher order terms in the continuum extrapolation. Instead, we have performed cuts in the lattice spacing by removing all points with
a>0.1\penalty\
fm.

The impact of each particular choice is shown in Fig.
13
where each point is obtained by averaging over all variations that are compatible with this choice. Our total error is dominated by statistics, especially for the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
mesons.

VI
Conclusion

Figure 14:
Overview of recent estimates for the pion,

In this paper, we have presented a first ab-initio calculation of the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
transition form factors at the physical point, in the kinematical range relevant for computing the HLbL contribution to the anomalous magnetic moment of the muon. Results for the pion TFF are also provided.
As compared to the pion TFF, the extraction of the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
TFFs requires a proper treatment of the mixing in the
\eta-\eta^{\prime}
system
BMW
.
Thanks to the implementation of advanced numerical methods, we have been able to compute and get a signal for all disconnected contributions: the diagram with a single pseudoscalar loop is by far dominant, the other contributions are SU(3)
f
suppressed and contribute at the percent level.

Our result for the pion TFF is in good agreement with previous lattice calculations
Gérardin et al. 2016
;
Gérardin et al. 2019b
and with experimental data in the single-virtual regime.
Concerning the
\eta
TFF, our result turns out to be in good agreement with previous estimates based on Canterbury approximants
Masjuan and Sanchez-Puertas 2017
or Dyson-Schwinger equations
Eichmann et al. 2019
;
Raya et al. 2020
for virtualities above
0.4\penalty\ \mathrm{GeV}^{2}
.
However, we find a slight tension, at the level of 1.7
\sigma
, for the normalization of the
\eta
TFF.
In the current setup, extracting the normalization of the TFF is challenging as it requires an extrapolation with data becoming noisier as we approach vanishing virtualities. Additional larger-volume ensembles could further improve the result.
The single-virtual
\eta
form factor is also compatible with experimental measurements by CELLO
Behrend et al. 1991
and CLEO
Gronberg et al. 1998
, with a slight tension for the lowest CELLO bin.
Finally, the result for the
\eta^{\prime}
TFF is in good agreement with other approaches in the double-vritual regime. In the single-virtual regime, our result is compatible (at the level of 1-2
\sigma
) with existing measurements from CELLO
Behrend et al. 1991
, CLEO
Gronberg et al. 1998
, BaBar
Lees et al. 2018
;
del Amo Sanchez et al. 2011
and the L3 experiment at LEP
Acciarri et al. 1998
. We observe a slight tension with the results based on the Dyson-Schwinger equation
Eichmann et al. 2019
.

Using our parametrization of the TFFs at the physical point, we provide an ab-initio estimate of the light-pseudoscalar meson-pole contribution to HLbL.
For the pion, we confirm the previous lattice calculations with Wilson-clover quarks
Gérardin et al. 2019b
where a good agreement was observed with the data-driven estimate of Ref.
Hoferichter et al. 2018b
.
The extraction of the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
pole contributions is much more challenging but a precision of 17% and 27% has been achieved.
Previous estimates of the
\eta^{(^{\prime})}
pseudoscalar-pole were based on Dyson-Schwinger equations
Eichmann et al. 2019
;
Raya et al. 2020
or Canterbury approximants
Masjuan and Sanchez-Puertas 2017
, relying on the availability of experimental data. For the
\eta
meson we find a slightly smaller value, although compatible at the level of 1.9 combined standard deviations. This can be explained by our lower value for the normalization of the form factor as compared to the experimental value quoted in
Tanabashi et al. 2018
.

Summing all contributions in Eq. (
33
), we obtain the main result of this paper

a_{\mu}^{\mathrm{HLbL};P}=(85.1\,\pm 4.7_{\rm stat}\,\pm\,2.3)\times 10^{-11}\,.

(35)

This value is compatible with the White-Paper estimate,
a_{\mu}^{\mathrm{HLbL};P}=93.8^{+4.0}_{-3.6}\times 10^{-11}

Aoyama et al. 2020
at the level of 1.4 combined standard deviations, and with a competitive uncertainty.

In the future, adding large-volume ensembles at smaller lattice spacings for the
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
TFFs would help to better constrain the normalization of the form factor and to clarify the tension on the
\eta\to\gamma\gamma
decay rate. The main advantage of large physical volume is to provide a better sampling of the low-virtuality region close to the origin. As shown on Table
2
, reducing the error on the decay rate has a strong impact on the
\pi^{0}
and
\eta
-pole contributions and, to a lesser extent, on the
\eta^{\prime}
-pole contribution. Another possibility would be to use the framework presented in
Meng et al. 2021
;
Feng and Jin 2019
to directly compute the normalization of the TFF.

Disconnected contributions : estimator for the vector loops

In this appendix we compare different estimators to evaluate the vector loops that appear in the second and fourth disconnected diagrams of Fig.
2
. We also provide estimates of those sub-leading diagrams to the pion-pole contribution to
a_{\mu}^{\rm hlbl}

B.1
Noise reduction techniques

In the isospin limit, we are interested in the vector loop function for the light-minus-strange contribution

\displaystyle L^{(l-s)}_{V;\mu}(x_{0};\vec{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{x}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\gamma_{\mu}D^{-1}_{l}(x,x)-\gamma_{\mu}D^{-1}_{s}(x,x)\right]e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}

(39)

\displaystyle=(m_{s}-m_{l})\sum_{\vec{x}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\gamma_{\mu}D^{-1}_{l}D^{-1}_{s}(x,x)\right]e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}

(40)

where
D_{f}
denotes the Dirac operator for a fermion of mass
m_{f}
. In this appendix we use Wilson-like fermion notations.
A standard estimator is to use the same set of
N
volume stochastic sources for both light and strange contributions
Gülpers et al. 2014

L^{(l-s)}_{V;\mu}(x_{0};\vec{q})=(m_{s}-m_{l})\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\vec{x}}\left[\eta_{n}^{{\dagger}}(x)\gamma_{\mu}D^{-1}_{l}D^{-1}_{s}\eta_{n}(x)\right]e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}

(41)

where the volume sources satisfy
\langle\eta(x)\eta^{{\dagger}}(y)\rangle=\delta_{x,y}
. This estimator is noisy and typically thousands of inversions are required to reach the gauge noise.
In
Giusti et al. 2019b
, the authors introduced the split-even stochastic estimator

L^{(l-s)}_{V;\mu}(x_{0};\vec{q})=(m_{s}-m_{l})\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{\vec{x}}\left[\left(\eta_{n}^{{\dagger}}D_{l}^{-1}\right)(x)\gamma_{\mu}\left(D^{-1}_{s}\eta_{n}\right)(x)\right]e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}

(42)

which drastically reduces the variance of the estimator for a comparable numerical cost. Finally, both estimators can be combined with LMA where the low-mode contribution is computed exactly, without adding stochastic noise. When the low modes of the Dirac operator are used to deflate the lattice Dirac operator, the overhead of LMA is small.

In this appendix, we compare the performances of the four estimators discussed above.
We use the variance of the loop function as a benchmark of the estimators.
Since the error on the loop function is constant in time, we average the variance over all time slices and all directions
\mu=0,1,2,3
.
The results are obtained on the
L=6\penalty\
fm ensemble at a lattice spacing of
a=0.13\penalty\
fm and with
\vec{q}=\vec{0}
. We use 1000 eigenvectors of the even-odd Dirac operator (i.e. 2000 eigenvectors of the Dirac operator) to compute the low-mode contribution. The results are depicted in Fig.
15
.

We see that the split-even estimator outperforms the standard estimator, even when the latter is combined with LMA. Less than 1000 inversions are needed to reach the gauge noise. When the split-even estimator is combined with LMA, only
O(10)
sources are needed to reach the gauge noise. Combining LMA with the split-even stochastic estimator, the computer time spent to compute those disconnected loops is completely sub-dominant.

Square root of the variance of the vector loop function for the four estimators described in the text. The lines represent a perfect scaling of the variance assuming the variance is dominated by the stochastic noise. The horizontal dashed line is the error from the low-mode contribution only and is used as a normalization scale.
