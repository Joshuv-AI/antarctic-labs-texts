Weak and ultrastrong coupling limits of the quantum mean force Gibbs state
J. D. Cresser, J. Anders

Abstract
The Gibbs state is widely taken to be the equilibrium state of a system in contact with an environment at temperature T T . However, non-negligible interactions between system and environment can give rise to an altered state. Here we derive general expressions for this mean force Gibbs state, valid for any system that interacts with a bosonic reservoir. First, we derive the state in the weak coupling limit and find that, in general, it maintains coherences with respect to the bare system Hamiltonian. Second, we develop a new expansion method suited to investigate the ultrastrong coupling regime. This allows us to derive the explicit form for the mean force Gibbs state, and we find that it becomes diagonal in the basis set by the system-reservoir interaction instead of the system Hamiltonian. Several examples are discussed including a single qubit, a three-level V-system and two coupled qubits all interacting with bosonic reservoirs. The results shed light on the presence of coherences in the strong coupling regime, and provide key tools for nanoscale thermodynamics investigations.

Ultrastrong coupling.

To derive the MFG state
\rho_{S}
in the ultrastrong coupling limit
\lambda\to\infty

We here develop such a new approach, see Appendix

Conclusion and open questions.

For general quantum systems coupled to a bosonic reservoir two explicit expressions for the MFG state
\rho_{S}
defined in Eq. (
1
) have been derived.
Results (
7
) and (
3
) are valid for any quantum system, be it single qubits, V-systems, harmonic oscillators or others, and make it possible to immediately identify
\rho_{S}
for a multitude of problems and applications arising in quantum thermodynamics, quantum thermometry, magnetism, quantum biology, and beyond.
Taken together, expressions (
3
) and (
7
) evidence the departure from the text book Gibbs state, diagonal in
H_{S}
, whenever the system-environment coupling is not negligible and the temperature is not large. With increasing coupling, the basis
\{P_{n}\}
of the system’s interaction operator
X
becomes increasingly dominant, culminating in the ultrastrong limit where it is the
only
relevant basis.

To further explore this transition will require finding MFG state expressions for intermediate coupling strengths — a very difficult analytical problem that may first be solved with numerical methods. We highlight that, while it is known that the dynamics of an open quantum system converges to the MFG state in the weak coupling limit
T. Mori and S. Miyashita 2008
;
C. H. Fleming and N. I.
Cummings 2011
;
J. Thingna, J-S Wang, and P.
Hänggi 2012
;
Y. Subaşı, C. H. Fleming, J. M. Taylor, and
B. L. Hu 2012
;
Purkayastha et al. 2020
, the jury is out for the intermediate and ultrastrong coupling limit.
Another open question concerns extensions of the MFG state forms derived here to systems coupled to multiple bosonic reservoirs at the same temperature
Anders et al.
, besides the two qubit example given in Eq. (
10
). Finally, we expect that extensions to fermionic reservoirs can readily be made, as the derivations leading to (
3
) and (
7
) do not centrally depended on the bosonic nature of the reservoir.

Trushechkin 2021
that derives an ultrastrong coupling master equation and confirms dynamical convergence to the ultrastrong MFG state (
7
) derived here.

Appendix A
High temperature limit via a factorization approximation

The high temperature limit corresponds to
\beta\to 0
. We make use of the product approximation obtained via the Zassenhaus formula
Suzuki 1977
to write it in symmetrized form, with
H_{0}=H_{S}^{\prime}+H_{R}
,

e^{-\beta H_{SR}}=e^{-\beta(H_{0}+\lambda V)}=\frac{1}{2}\left[e^{-\beta H_{0}}e^{-\beta\lambda V}+e^{-\beta\lambda V}e^{-\beta H_{0}}\right]\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2})\right).

(11)

With interaction
V=X\,B
with
B=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\sqrt{J(\omega)}\left(b(\omega)+b^{\dagger}(\omega)\right)
, and reservoir thermal state
\tau_{R}(\beta)={e^{-\beta H_{R}}/\mathrm{Tr}_{R}[e^{-\beta H_{R}}]}
, and denoting the un-normalized state with a tilde, one obtains the reduced state

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=\left(e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}\,\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\tau_{R}(\beta)\,e^{-\beta\lambda XB}\right]+\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\tau_{R}(\beta)\,e^{-\beta\lambda XB}\right]\,e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2})\right),

(12)

where any common factors have been ignored as we ultimately will have to normalize this state. Further, given that
\tau_{R}(\beta)
is a Gaussian state, we can immediately evaluate the trace over reservoir states to give

\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[{\tau_{R}(\beta)}\,e^{-\beta{\lambda}XB}\right]=e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}{\lambda^{2}}X^{2}\langle B^{2}\rangle}

(13)

where

\langle B^{2}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,J(\omega)\coth\frac{\beta\omega}{2}.

(14)

Using
\coth(x)=x^{-1}+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
for
x=\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\omega
, we have for small
\beta
, on noting that only the first term will contribute as the exponent is already second order in
\beta
,

\langle B^{2}\rangle=\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{J(\omega)}{\omega}=\frac{2Q}{\beta},

(15)

where
Q
is the reorganization energy. Thus we have

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=\left(e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}e^{\beta{\lambda^{2}}X^{2}Q}+e^{\beta{\lambda^{2}}X^{2}Q}e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2})\right),

(16)

at which point we can reverse the product approximation for small
\beta
. Noting that
H_{S}^{\prime}=H_{S}+{\lambda^{2}}X^{2}Q
and normalizing, one finds
\rho_{S}=\tau_{S}{+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{2})}
i.e., the high temperature mean force state is just the Gibbs state,
\tau_{S}
as stated in the main text. This result is valid for all coupling strengths such that the energy scale set by the temperature is larger than that of the system-reservoir coupling.

Appendix B
Kubo expansion for weak coupling at arbitrary temperature

This is the approach used in Mori and Miyashita
T. Mori and S. Miyashita 2008
, Subaşı
et al.
,
Y. Subaşı, C. H. Fleming, J. M. Taylor, and
B. L. Hu 2012
, Thingna
et al.
,
J. Thingna, J-S Wang, and P.
Hänggi 2012
, and appeared earlier in Geva
et al.

E. Geva, E. Rosenman, and D.
Tannor 2000
, and in the recent paper by Purkayastha
et al.
,
Purkayastha et al. 2020
and involves using the so-called Kubo expansion
Kubo 1957
, analogous to the corresponding expansion for the time evolution operator, albeit for imaginary time

e^{-\beta(H_{0}+\lambda V)}=e^{-\beta H_{0}}\left(1-\lambda\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}e^{\beta_{1}H_{0}}Ve^{-\beta_{1}H_{0}}+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{2}\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}e^{{+}\beta_{2}H_{0}}Ve^{-(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})H_{0}}Ve^{-\beta_{1}H_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})\right).

(17)

B.1
Hermiticity of perturbative result

In the case of the unitary time evolution operator, the expansion cut off at a finite number of terms is no longer unitary. However, here, the operator
\exp[-\beta H]
for
H=H_{0}+\lambda V
is Hermitian, and the finite order expansion is also Hermitian i.e., there is no requirement to symmetrize the expression, cf.,
Purkayastha et al. 2020
. To show this, we can write
e^{-\beta H}=e^{-\beta H_{0}}F(\beta)
where

F(\beta)=1-\lambda\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}e^{\beta_{1}H_{0}}Ve^{-\beta_{1}H_{0}}+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{2}\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}e^{{+}\beta_{2}H_{0}}Ve^{-(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})H_{0}}Ve^{-\beta_{1}H_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3}).

(18)

This quantity is clearly non-Hermitian, but the product
e^{-\beta H_{0}}F(\beta)=e^{-\beta H}
clearly is. The question is whether or not cutting off the expansion of
F(\beta)
to second order breaks this Hermiticity. So we will write
F(\beta)=F^{(2)}(\beta)+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})
. We want to check whether or not the quantity
e^{-\beta H_{0}}F^{(2)}(\beta)
is Hermitian. To do this, take the hermitian conjugate

\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}F^{(2)}(\beta)\right)^{\dagger}=e^{-\beta H_{0}}-{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}e^{-\beta_{1}H_{0}}Ve^{-(\beta-\beta_{1})H_{0}}+{\lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{2}\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}e^{-\beta_{1}H_{0}}Ve^{-(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})H_{0}}Ve^{-(\beta-\beta_{2})H_{0}}.

(19)

Now make the changes of variables
\beta_{1}^{\prime}=\beta-\beta_{1}
in the first integral, and
\beta_{1}^{\prime}=\beta-\beta_{2}
and
\beta-\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}^{\prime}
in the second integral. We immediately get
\left(e^{-\beta H_{0}}F^{(2)}(\beta)\right)^{\dagger}=e^{-\beta H_{0}}F^{(2)}(\beta)
.

B.2
Perturbative result for general system operator
X

We now want to expand the Mori and Miyashita result making it more explicit for practical application. Using
V=XB
,
H_{0}=H_{S}^{\prime}+H_{R}
where
H_{S}^{\prime}=H_{S}+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q
, we can define, in a ‘
\beta
-interaction picture’
V(-i\beta)=e^{\beta H_{0}}Ve^{-\beta H_{0}}=X(-i\beta)B(-i\beta)
. We then find, recognizing that
{\tau}_{R}(\beta)=e^{-\beta H_{R}}/Z_{R}
and on taking the trace over
R
, that the unnormalized state of the system (indicated with a tilde,
\tilde{\rho}_{S}
)

\tilde{\rho}_{S}={\mathrm{Tr}_{R}}\left[e^{-\beta H_{SR}}\right]={e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}}\left(1+\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{2}\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}X(-i\beta_{2})X(-i\beta_{1})G(\beta,-i(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}))+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\right),

(20)

where we have used
\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[B\,{\tau}_{R}(\beta)\right]=0
and where

G(\beta,-i(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}))=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[B(-i\beta_{2})B(-i\beta_{1})\,{\tau}_{R}(\beta)\right],

(21)

is the
\beta
-analogue of the temporal reservoir correlation function.

This result (
20
) is useful for finding the high temperature limit
\beta\to 0
, and is discussed later in Section
B.4
, but as it stands, this result is not in a form for practical application. To achieve this necessitates rewriting
X
in terms of the energy eigenoperators for an arbitrary multilevel system.

The system operator
X
can be expressed in terms of the energy eigenoperators for the system

X=\sum_{n=-N}^{+N}X_{n}

(22)

where
\left[H_{S},X_{n}\right]=\omega_{n}X_{n}
, and where the
\omega_{n}
are Bohr frequencies, where
m
ranges over
2N+1
positive and negative values, and
\omega_{n}=-\omega_{-n}
. Finally, since
X=X^{\dagger}
we have
X_{n}=X_{-n}^{\dagger}
, and
\omega_{0}=0
, though in general,
X_{0}\neq 0
.

The role of the
X_{n}
can be seen by considering, for an eigenstate
|\omega\rangle
of
H_{S}
,
\left[H_{S},X_{n}\right]|\omega\rangle=H_{S}\left(X_{n}|\omega\rangle\right)-\omega X_{n}|\omega\rangle=\omega_{n}X_{n}|\omega\rangle
so
H_{S}(X_{n}|\omega\rangle)=(\omega+\omega_{n})X_{n}|\omega\rangle
. Provided
X_{n}|\omega\rangle\neq 0
, the effect of
X_{n}
on the state
|\omega\rangle
is to map it to a new state
X_{n}|\omega\rangle
with energy
\omega+\omega_{n}
. So, if
n>0
,
X_{n}
is a raising operator, while if
n<0
it is a lowering operator.
We then have

e^{\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}Xe^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}=X(-i\beta)=e^{\beta H_{S}}Xe^{-\beta H_{S}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})=\sum_{n}X_{n}e^{\beta\omega_{n}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}).

(23)

Note that here and in the following, the sum will be over the
2N+1
integers
-N
to
N
, but the limits of the sum will be implied.

Feeding (
23
) into expression (
20
) we get, correct to second order, for the unnormalized density operator

\tilde{\rho}^{\,(2)}_{S}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}e^{-\beta H_{S}}\sum_{mn}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{2}\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\,e^{\beta_{2}\omega_{m}}e^{-\beta_{1}\omega_{n}}G(\beta,-i(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})).

(24)

A simple change of variable, and swapping the order of integration puts this in the form

\tilde{\rho}^{\,(2)}_{S}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}e^{-\beta H_{S}}\sum_{mn}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\int_{\beta_{1}}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{2}\,e^{\beta_{2}\omega_{mn}}e^{\beta_{1}\omega_{n}}G(\beta,-i\beta_{1}),

(25)

with
\omega_{mn}=\omega_{m}-\omega_{n}
, which after carrying out the
\beta_{2}
integral gives

\tilde{\rho}^{\,(2)}_{S}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}e^{-\beta H_{S}}\sum_{mn}\omega_{mn}^{-1}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\left(e^{\beta\omega_{mn}}e^{\beta_{1}\omega_{n}}-e^{\beta_{1}\omega_{m}}\right)G(\beta,-i\beta_{1}).

(26)

Appearing here is the correlation function
G(\beta,-i\beta)
defined in (
21
), which is the
t\to-i\beta
form of the temporal correlation function
G(\beta,t)=\mathrm{Tr}[\tau_{R}(\beta)B(t)B(0)]
where

B(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\sqrt{J(\omega)}\left(b(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}+b^{\dagger}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\right)

(27)

from which it readily follows that

G(\beta,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega J(\omega)\left((n_{\beta}(\omega)+1)e^{-i\omega t}+n_{\beta}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\right)

(28)

with
n_{\beta}(\omega)=1/(e^{\beta\omega}-1)
. Hence for the correlation function
G(\beta,-i\beta_{1})
we have

G(\beta,-i\beta_{1})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega J(\omega)\left((n_{\beta}(\omega)+1)e^{-\beta_{1}\omega}+n_{\beta}(\omega)e^{\beta_{1}\omega}\right).

(29)

An integral transform of this,

\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\,e^{\beta_{1}\omega_{n}}G(\beta,-i\beta_{1})=A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})+e^{\beta\omega_{n}}A_{\beta}(-\omega_{n}),

(30)

defines the function
A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})
, generally to be understood as a principal part integral, given by

A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega J(\omega)\left(\frac{n_{\beta}(\omega)+1}{\omega-\omega_{n}}-\frac{n_{\beta}(\omega)}{\omega+\omega_{n}}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,J(\omega)\left(\frac{\omega_{n}\coth(\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\omega)}{\omega^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}}+\frac{\omega}{\omega^{2}-\omega_{n}^{2}}\right),

(31)

where we have used
2n_{\beta}(\omega)+1=\coth(\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\omega)
. This expression can be recognized as appearing in the second order energy level ‘Lamb shift’ contribution to the system Hamiltonian, given by
\Delta H_{LS}=-\sum_{n}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}
.
We can now write

\tilde{\rho}^{\,(2)}_{S}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}+\lambda^{2}e^{-\beta H_{S}}\sum_{mn}\omega_{mn}^{-1}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}\left(e^{\beta\omega_{mn}}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-A_{\beta}(\omega_{m})+e^{\beta\omega_{m}}\left(A_{\beta}(-\omega_{n})-A_{\beta}(-\omega_{m})\right)\right).

(32)

It is convenient at this stage to separate out the
m=n
contribution to the double sum, so we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}e^{-\beta H_{S}}\sum_{n}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\left(\beta A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{n})}{d\omega_{n}}+e^{\beta\omega_{n}}\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{-n})}{d\omega_{-n}}\right)

(33)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}e^{-\beta H_{S}}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}\left[e^{\beta\omega_{mn}}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})+e^{\beta\omega_{m}}A_{\beta}(-\omega_{n})-\left(A_{\beta}(\omega_{m})+e^{\beta\omega_{m}}A_{\beta}(-\omega_{m})\right)\right],

where
\sum_{m\neq n}
is a sum over all ordered pairs
(m,n)
with

.
The exponential factors appearing in Eq. (
33
) can now be removed by use of
e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{m}=X_{m}e^{-\beta H_{S}}e^{-\beta\omega_{m}}
so that

e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}=X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}e^{-\beta\omega_{mn}}.

(34)

This yields

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\left(e^{-\beta H_{S}}\beta X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{n})}{d\omega_{n}}+X_{n}e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}^{\dagger}\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{-n})}{d\omega_{-n}}\right)

(35)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}\left(X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}(\omega_{m})+X_{m}e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}^{\dagger}\left(A_{\beta}(-\omega_{n})-A_{\beta}(-\omega_{m})\right)\right).

We now relabel the summation

indices, so that
A_{\beta}(\omega_{-n})\to A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})
, and similarly for the derivatives, recalling when doing so that the sums are symmetric from

to

, that

and

, to give

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\left(e^{-\beta H_{S}}\beta X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-\left(e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}-X_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}\right)\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{n})}{d\omega_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}\left(X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}(\omega_{m})-X_{m}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}\left(A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})-A_{\beta}(\omega_{m})\right)\right).

Next, relabel the indices

for all terms involving

, giving

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\left(e^{-\beta H_{S}}\beta X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})+\left[X_{n}^{\dagger},e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}\right]\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{n})}{d\omega_{n}}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}\left(X_{m}X_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}+e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}X_{m}^{\dagger}-X_{m}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{n}-X_{n}^{\dagger}e^{-\beta H_{S}}X_{m}\right)A_{\beta}(\omega_{n}).

Regrouping everything into commutators and using

then gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}Z_{S}\sum_{n}\left(\beta\tau_{S}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}A_{\beta}(\omega_{n})+\left[X_{n}^{\dagger},\tau_{S}X_{n}\right]\frac{dA_{\beta}(\omega_{n})}{d\omega_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}Z_{S}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}\left(\left[X_{m},X_{n}^{\dagger}\tau_{S}\right]+\left[\tau_{S}X_{n},X_{m}^{\dagger}\right]\right)A_{\beta}(\omega_{n}).

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}\left(\left[X_{m},X_{n}^{\dagger}\tau_{S}\right]+\left[\tau_{S}X_{n},X_{m}^{\dagger}\right]\right){\cal D}_{\beta}(\omega_{n}),

B.3
Normalization

Normalization requires calculating the trace of
\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}
. We find

Z_{S}^{(2)}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}\right]=1+\beta\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\left[\tau_{S}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\right]{\cal D}_{\beta}(\omega_{n}).

(43)

Then provided

{\left|\beta\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\left[\tau_{S}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\right]{\cal D}_{\beta}(\omega_{n})\right|}\ll 1,

(44)

which defines an upper limit to

, we can use the binomial approximation and expand

\frac{1}{Z_{S}^{(2)}}=1-\beta\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\left[\tau_{S}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\right]{\cal D}_{\beta}(\omega_{n}).

(45)

This leads to the following expression, correct to second order in the interaction, for the normalized density operator

\displaystyle\rho_{S}^{(2)}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{(2)}}{Z_{S}^{(2)}}=\tau_{S}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{n}\left[X_{n}^{\dagger},\tau_{S}X_{n}\right]\frac{d{{\cal D}}_{\beta}(\omega_{n})}{d\omega_{n}}+\lambda^{2}\beta\sum_{n}\tau_{S}\left(X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}-\mathrm{Tr}_{S}\left[\tau_{S}X_{n}X_{n}^{\dagger}\right]\right){\cal D}_{\beta}(\omega_{n})

(46)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\omega_{mn}^{-1}\left(\left[X_{m},X_{n}^{\dagger}\tau_{S}\right]+\left[\tau_{S}X_{n},X_{m}^{\dagger}\right]\right){\cal D}_{\beta}(\omega_{n}).

This is result Eq. (

B.4
High temperature limit

In the high temperature limit
\beta\to 0
, it is easiest to work with the earlier form (
20
), this requiring an expansion to second order in
\beta
, from which we find

\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}\left(1+\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right).

(47)

Expanding
e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}
to second order in the interaction in a similar manner to (
20
) (or using (
39
)) and taking the high temperature limit
\beta\to 0
yields

e^{-\beta H_{S}^{\prime}}=e^{-\beta(H_{S}+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}}-\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}e^{-(\beta-\beta^{\prime})H_{S}}X^{2}Qe^{-\beta^{\prime}H_{S}}\sim e^{-\beta H_{S}}\left(1-\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right),

(48)

so that to second order we get

\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}}\left(1-\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)\left(1+\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)=e^{-\beta H_{S}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4}).

(49)

Hence, here the reorganization energy
\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q
is cancelled and we find
\tilde{\rho}_{S}^{\,(2)}=e^{-\beta H_{S}}
to second order, which is just the result obtained by the factorization method.

Alternatively, and as a sanity check, the same limit can be taken for (
46
). This involves expanding all the
\lambda
-dependent terms in (
46

\beta
. The term with the prefactor
\beta^{-1}
is found to vanish in the limit of
\beta\to 0
. Constant terms (i.e., those independent of
\beta
) also cancel, as do all those terms proportional to
Q
. The remaining terms, all of higher order in
\beta
will then vanish in the limit of
\beta\to 0
. What is left in the high temperature limit is thus once again
e^{-\beta H_{S}}
.

Appendix D
Ultrastrong coupling limit

The approach taken here is based on a straightforward idea that allows the description of the quantum Zeno effect, historically first analysed for a system subject to rapidly repeated measurement
Misra and Sudarshan 1977
, but later shown to be equivalent to a strong coupling model
Facchi and Pascazio 2003
. The method is built around standard perturbation theory
Facchi 2003
, but can be put on a more formal basis including the estimation of errors
Burgarth et al. 2019
.

The idea is to find an approximate form for the time evolution operator
U_{\lambda}(t)=\exp(-itH_{\lambda})
with total Hamiltonian
H_{\lambda}=H_{S}+\lambda V
, where
H_{S}
is strongly perturbed by the term
\lambda V
, where
\lambda
is a parameter that scales the strength of the perturbation. The desired limit is the strong perturbation limit,

U_{\infty}(t)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}e^{-it(H_{S}+\lambda V)}.

(66)

The analysis of the case of unitary dynamics is outlined in the following Section for the purposes of illustrating the approach used. Further developments needed to deal with the strong coupling limit of the MFG state are presented in Section
D.2
. Specifically, we will transition to the thermal equilibrium case via the ‘Wick rotation’,
t\to-i\beta
, as well as a trace over the reservoir states.

D.1
Strong coupling for unitary dynamics

The idea is to write the evolution operator as
U_{\lambda}(t)=\exp\left[-it\lambda\left(V+\lambda^{-1}H_{S}\right)\right]
and work with the eigenstates and eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}=V+\lambda^{-1}H_{S}
where now
V
is the ‘unperturbed system’ and
\lambda^{-1}H_{S}
is the ‘perturbation’, an appropriate description for
\lambda
large.

The procedure then is to identify the ‘unperturbed’ eigenstates of
V
,
|v_{n}\rangle
say, and corresponding eigenvalues
v_{n}
(ignoring here any considerations concerning degeneracies), from which by first order perturbation theory the perturbed eigenvalues

\tilde{v}_{n}=v_{n}+\lambda^{-1}\langle v_{n}|H_{S}|v_{n}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2})

(67)

are determined. The eigenstates
|\tilde{v}_{n}\rangle
of
V+\lambda^{-1}H_{S}
can also be calculated to lowest order, but are not needed for determining the leading contribution to
U_{\infty}(t)
, except to note that
|\tilde{v}_{n}\rangle=|v_{n}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
. We then write
U_{\lambda}(t)=\sum_{n}U_{\lambda}(t)\tilde{P}_{n}
where
\tilde{P}_{n}=|\tilde{v}_{n}\rangle\langle\tilde{v}_{n}|
. Further

\sum_{n}U_{\lambda}(t)\tilde{P}_{n}=\sum_{n}\exp\left(-i\lambda t\tilde{H}_{\lambda}\right)\tilde{P}_{n}=\sum_{n}\exp\left(-it\lambda\tilde{v}_{n}\right)\tilde{P}_{n}.

(68)

Substituting for
\tilde{v}_{n}
from Eq. (
67
), and noting that
\tilde{P}_{n}=P_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
where
P_{n}=|v_{n}\rangle\langle v_{n}|
, we find, after a little manipulation

U_{\lambda}(t)=\sum_{n}\exp\left[-it\left(\langle v_{n}|H_{S}|v_{n}\rangle+\lambda v_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\right)\right]P_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}).

(69)

There are expressions available for the
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
corrections, but they are unfeasibly complicated to evaluate, so the results reported here will be for the ultrastrong ‘
\lambda\to\infty
’ limit, for which we can write

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}U_{\lambda}(t)\,e^{it\lambda V}=\exp\left[-it\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}\right]

(70)

Thus the effect of the strong coupling is to replace the original system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
by a ‘partitioned’ Hamiltonian, i.e.,

H_{S}\to\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}.

(71)

.

We note that for a system with Hamiltonian
H_{S}
(and no coupling to an environment) following unitary evolution with
H_{S}
but experiencing perturbations
\epsilon V_{S}
with
||V_{S}||=1
, some of the authors of the unitary Zeno effect techniques
Facchi and Pascazio 2003
;
Facchi 2003
;
Burgarth et al. 2019
have recently shown that the unique robust stationary state at long times is the Gibbs state
Burgarth et al. 2020
, i.e.,

e^{-it(H_{S}+\epsilon V_{S})}\,e^{-\beta H_{S}}\,e^{+it(H_{S}+\epsilon V_{S})}\sim e^{-\beta H_{S}}+{\cal O}(\epsilon).

(72)

D.2
Mean force Gibbs state at ultrastrong coupling to a reservoir

The aim here is to evaluate the MFG state in the strong coupling limit, i.e., determining the large
\lambda
limit of

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[e^{-\beta\left(H_{S}+H_{R}+\lambda BX+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)}\right],

(73)

where as before,
H_{R}
is the Hamiltonian of the reservoir, and
V=\lambda BX
is the coupling between a reservoir operator
B
and a system operator
X
. In general, the reservoir will have a quasi-continuous energy spectrum, necessary for the system-reservoir to evolve to a steady state. The interaction
XB
must be such that energy is exchanged between the system and the reservoir so that an energetic equilibrium state can be established. As before, the reservoir is assumed to be bosonic, with
B
of the form

B=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\sqrt{J(\omega)}\left(b(\omega)+b^{\dagger}(\omega)\right),

(74)

where
\left[b(\omega),b^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime})\right]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
and where
J(\omega)
is the spectral density.

As well as the replacement
t\to-i\beta
as compared to the unitary case, an extra step required here is to carry out the trace over the reservoir states.
The starting point is the full expression for the unnormalized joint system-reservoir Gibbs state

\tilde{\rho}_{SR}=e^{-\beta(H_{S}+H_{R}+\lambda BX+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q)}.

(75)

We first separate out the reservoir Hamiltonian by moving to an interaction picture:

\tilde{\rho}_{SR}=e^{-\beta H_{R}}W(\beta),

(76)

where

W(\beta)=e^{\beta H_{R}}e^{-\beta(H_{S}+H_{R}+\lambda BX+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q)}.

(77)

Differentiating with respect to
\beta
then gives

W^{\prime}(\beta)=-(H_{S}+\lambda XB(-i\beta)+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q)W(\beta),

(78)

where we have introduced the ‘interaction picture operator’

B(-i\beta)=e^{\beta H_{R}}Be^{-\beta H_{R}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\sqrt{J(\omega)}\left(b(\omega)e^{-\beta\omega}+b^{\dagger}(\omega)e^{\beta\omega}\right).

(79)

Eq. (
78
) has as its solution a ‘time ordered’ or better, a ‘beta ordered’, exponential, with
W(0)=1

W(\beta)=\mathbb{T}\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}(H_{S}+\lambda XB(-i\beta^{\prime})+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q)\right]\equiv\mathbb{T}\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}\lambda^{-1}\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta^{\prime})\right].

(80)

Here we have defined the Hamiltonian

\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta)=V_{\lambda}+\lambda^{-1}H_{S}

(81)

with
V_{\lambda}=B(-i\beta)X+\lambda X^{2}Q
being the ‘unperturbed Hamiltonian’ and
\lambda^{-1}H_{S}
the ‘perturbation’.
We will now work with the eigenstates and eigenvalues of
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta)
.

Note that
B(-i\beta)
commutes with all the system operators appearing in the above expression for
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta)
, and hence will be treated as a c-number parameter in what follows. Thus, the eigenstates of the unperturbed Hamiltonian
V_{\lambda}
will be simply the eigenstates of
X
, that is
|x_{n}\rangle
, with eigenvalue
v_{\lambda}
, i.e.,

V_{\lambda}|x_{n}\rangle=v_{\lambda}|x_{n}\rangle=\left(B(-i\beta)x_{n}+\lambda x_{n}^{2}Q\right)|x_{n}\rangle.

(82)

Substituting (
81
) into (
80
) and expanding we have

W(\beta)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}\ldots\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\,(-\lambda)^{j}\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta_{j})\ldots\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta_{1}).

(83)

If we let
|\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)\rangle
be the eigenstates of
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta)
with eigenvalues
\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)
, and define
\tilde{P}_{n}(\beta)
as the projection operator onto these eigenstates

\tilde{P}_{n}(\beta)=|\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)\rangle\langle\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)|,

(84)

then we can write

\tilde{H}_{\lambda}(\beta)=\sum_{n}\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)\tilde{P}_{n}(\beta).

(85)

The expansion of
W(\beta)
then becomes

W(\beta)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}\ldots\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\,\left(-\lambda\right)^{j}\sum_{n_{j}}\ldots\sum_{n_{1}}\tilde{v}_{n_{j}}(\beta_{j})\ldots\tilde{v}_{n_{1}}(\beta_{1})\tilde{P}_{n_{j}}(\beta_{j})\ldots\tilde{P}_{n_{1}}(\beta_{1}).

(86)

By first order perturbation theory, given that the unperturbed eigenstates are the eigenstates of
X
, and the perturbation
\lambda^{-1}H_{S}
is
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
, it follows that
|\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)\rangle=|x_{n}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
. Hence
\tilde{P}_{n}(\beta)=P_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})

where
P_{n}=|x_{n}\rangle\langle x_{n}|
are the projectors onto the eigenstates of
X
, so that the product of projection operators appearing in (
86
) will become

\tilde{P}_{n_{j}}(\beta_{j})\ldots\tilde{P}_{n_{1}}(\beta_{1})=P_{n_{j}}\ldots P_{n_{1}}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}).

(87)

As the eigenstates of
X
will be orthornormal, the product of projection operators will collapse to a single operator, so the sum over
(n_{j}\ldots n_{1})
in (
86
) will reduce to

W(\beta)=1+\sum_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}\ldots\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\,\left(-\lambda\right)^{j}\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta_{j})\ldots\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta_{1})P_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}).

(88)

Further, by first order perturbation theory, the eigenvalue
\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)
of
\tilde{H}_{\lambda}
can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalue
v_{n}(\beta)=x_{n}B(-i\beta)+\lambda x_{n}^{2}Q
of the unperturbed Hamiltonian, plus a first order correction i.e.,

\tilde{v}_{n}(\beta)=x_{n}B(-i\beta)+\lambda x_{n}^{2}Q+\lambda^{-1}\langle x_{n}|H_{S}|x_{n}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-2}),

(89)

and we now have

\displaystyle W(\beta)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}\ldots\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\left(\langle x_{n}|H_{S}|x_{n}\rangle+\lambda x_{n}B(-i\beta_{j})+\lambda^{2}x_{n}^{2}Q+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\right)P_{n}\ldots

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\left(\langle x_{n}|H_{S}|x_{n}\rangle+\lambda x_{n}B(-i\beta_{1})+\lambda^{2}x_{n}^{2}Q+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\right)P_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{j}\ldots\int_{0}^{\beta_{2}}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{1}\left(P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}+\lambda XB(-i\beta_{j})+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\right)P_{n}\ldots

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\times\left(P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}+\lambda XB(-i\beta_{1})+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\right)P_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}\left(\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}+XB(-i\beta^{\prime})+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1}).

(90)

If we now assume
\lambda
is sufficiently large that terms
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{-1})
can be ignored, then we can write for
\tilde{\rho}_{SR}
from Eq. (
76
)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{SR}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\beta H_{R}}\,\,\mathbb{T}\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}\left(\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}+XB(-i\beta^{\prime})+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)\right]

(91)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\beta H_{R}}\,\,\mathbb{T}\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{\beta}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{\prime}\,e^{\beta^{\prime}H_{R}}\left(\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}+XB+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)e^{-\beta^{\prime}H_{R}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\beta\left(\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}+H_{R}+\lambda XB+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)\right].

(92)

As the pair of exponents
H_{R}+\lambda XB
and
P_{n}\left(H_{S}+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)P_{n}
in (
91
) commute, we can write

\tilde{\rho}_{SR}=\exp\left[-\beta\left(H_{R}+\lambda XB\right)\right]\,\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}\left(H_{S}+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)P_{n}\right),

(93)

which now makes it possible to carry out the trace over the reservoir states, i.e.,

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=\mathrm{Tr}_{R}\left[\exp\left(-\beta\left(H_{R}+\lambda XB\right)\right)\right]\,\exp\left(-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}\left(H_{S}+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)P_{n}\right).

(94)

The first factor here involving the trace can be evaluated directly by rewriting, in the exponent,
H_{R}+\lambda XB=H_{R}+\lambda XB+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q-\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q=H_{SR}-H_{S}-\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q
where
H_{SR}
is the total Hamiltonian of the combined system and reservoir, equation (
2
), and
Q=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)/\omega
is the reorganization energy. From equation (
2
) we can read off the expression for the difference
H_{SR}-H_{S}
so that this trace can then be written

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{R}\left[\exp\left({-\beta\left(H_{R}+\lambda XB\right)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\left(p^{2}(\omega)+\left(\omega q(\omega)+\lambda\sqrt{\frac{2J(\omega)}{\omega}}X\right)^{2}\right)\right)\right]\,e^{\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q}

\displaystyle=Z_{R}\,e^{\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q}.

(95)

For large
\lambda
of course this expression arising from taking the trace over the reservoir diverges. However, when multiplying it with the other
\lambda
-dependent term in (
94
), the divergent factor is exactly cancelled since
X^{2}=\sum_{n}P_{n}X^{2}P_{n}
. This leaves a finite expression already before normalisation of the state, i.e.
the unnormalised system state is, from Eq. (
94
) and dropping the constant
Z_{R}
,

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=e^{\beta\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q}\,e^{-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}\left(H_{S}+\lambda^{2}X^{2}Q\right)P_{n}}{=e^{-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}\,H_{S}\,P_{n}}}.

(96)

Thus all dependence on
\lambda
is removed, and we get the strong coupling limit for the normalised MFG state

\rho_{S}=\frac{e^{-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left[e^{-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}}\right]}.

(97)

This is result (
7
) in the main text.

Our derived MFG state can be compared to the steady state
conjectured
by Goyal and Kawai
K. Goyal and R. Kawai 2019
. They propose that in the strong coupling limit a system relaxes to a stationary state
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}{\varrho}^{ss}_{S}(t\to\infty)
given by

\varrho_{S}^{conj}=\sum_{n}P_{n}\,\tau_{S}(\beta)\,P_{n},

(98)

where
\tau_{S}(\beta)
is the Gibbs state with respect to the bare system Hamiltonian
H_{S}
. Note that for both states, (
97
) and (
98
), the projection operators
P_{n}
appear.
For comparison, equation (
97
) can be rewritten as

\rho_{S}\propto e^{-\beta\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}}=\sum_{n}P_{n}\,e^{-\beta\langle x_{n}|H_{S}|x_{n}\rangle}

(99)

which gives a weighting of
e^{-\beta\langle x_{n}|H_{S}|x_{n}\rangle}
accorded to each of the subspaces defined by the projection operator
P_{n}
. This can be compared to steady state conjecture (
98
)

\varrho_{S}^{conj}\propto\sum_{n}P_{n}e^{-\beta H_{S}}P_{n}=\sum_{n}P_{n}\,\langle x_{n}|e^{-\beta H_{S}}|x_{n}\rangle.

(100)

While similar, there are clearly some differences. The interpretation of the derived ultrastrong coupling expression (
97
) is that the equilibrium state is a Gibbs state w.r.t. a modified Hamiltonian,
\sum_{n}P_{n}H_{S}P_{n}
. The effect of the ultrastrong coupling is to force the system to equilibrate according to the eigenstates of the now dominant system interaction operator
X
, rather than with respect to the system Hamiltonian, with a weighting according to the mean value of the system Hamiltonian with respect to each such eigenstate.

Below the two states will be compared for two specific situations, that of a single qubit strongly coupled to a single reservoir Section
E.1
, and the more general case of two coupled qubits which are individually coupled to two independent reservoirs, Section
E.3
.

D.3
Two systems and two reservoirs

This generalization of the previous case can take two forms, one in which a single system is coupled to more than one reservoir, such as a spin system coupled to a three dimensional reservoir
Anders et al.
, the other in which separate systems (that may interact with each other) each individually couple to a separate reservoir. Within these two possibilities there is a further generalization to reservoirs held at different temperatures. This last case would lead to non-equilibrium steady states, a different class of problem, and will not be considered here. The first case will also not be considered as it has been found to introduce difficulties that also require further development of the theory. Thus here we will focus on the case of two interacting systems,
S_{\alpha}
with
\alpha=1,2
, each individually interacting with a separate reservoir,
R_{\alpha}
, and these reservoirs are at the same inverse temperature
\beta
.

In this case, we will have a Hamiltonian of the form

H_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}=H_{S_{1}}+H_{S_{2}}+H^{int}_{12}+H_{R_{1}}+H_{R_{2}}+\lambda_{1}B_{1}X_{1}+\lambda_{2}B_{2}X_{2}+\lambda_{1}^{2}X_{1}^{2}Q_{1}+\lambda_{2}^{2}X_{2}^{2}Q_{2},

(101)

where the interaction between the systems is given by
H^{int}_{12}
, and the two reservoirs are independent, so that
\left[B_{1},B_{2}\right]=0
. The system Hamiltonian is now
H_{S}=H_{S_{1}}+H_{S_{2}}+H^{int}_{12}
, and the
Q_{\alpha}
are the reorganization energies for the coupling of each system to its respective reservoir, i.e.,

Q_{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\frac{J_{\alpha}(\omega)}{\omega},

(102)

where
J_{n}(\omega)
is the spectral density of the coupling between
S_{\alpha}
and
B_{\alpha}
.

Taking the global thermal state of systems and reservoirs at inverse temperature
\beta
, the MFG state of the combined system
S\equiv S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
is formally,

\rho_{S}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{B_{1}B_{2}}\left[e^{-\beta H_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}\right]}{\mathrm{Tr}_{S_{1}S_{2}}\left[\mathrm{Tr}_{B_{1}B_{2}}\left[e^{-\beta H_{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}\right]\right]}.

(103)

The strong coupling limit can now be performed as in the single reservoir case, but sequentially for the two couplings and in either order. E.g., first with respect to the parameter
\lambda_{1}
, giving rise to a partitioning of the Hamiltonian in terms of the projection operators
P_{1m}
onto the eigenstates of
X_{1}
, and then repeated with respect to the parameter
\lambda_{2}
, and the concomitant projection operators onto the eigenstates of
X_{2}
. Note that
\left[P_{1m},P_{2n}\right]=0
as the systems
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are independent.

The traces over the two independent reservoir states can each be done as in the single reservoir case. The result is that the unnormalized joint state for the combined system
S
is given by

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{mn}P_{1m}P_{2n}H_{S}P_{2n}P_{1m}\right],

(104)

where
P_{\alpha n}=|x_{\alpha n}\rangle\langle x_{\alpha n}|
, and
X_{\alpha}|x_{\alpha m}\rangle=x_{\alpha m}|x_{\alpha m}\rangle
,
\alpha=1,2
. This is the unnormalised version of result Eq. (
9
) in the main text.

To see the different contributions of the bare Hamiltonians
H_{S\alpha}
and the inter-system coupling
H^{int}_{12}
, one may further expand

\sum_{mn}P_{1m}P_{2n}H_{S}P_{2n}P_{1m}=\sum_{\alpha,m}P_{\alpha m}H_{S_{\alpha}}P_{\alpha m}+\sum_{mn}P_{1m}P_{2n}H^{int}_{12}P_{2n}P_{1m},

(105)

and as the various terms in this expression all mutually commute, we have

\tilde{\rho}_{S}=\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{\alpha,m}P_{\alpha m}H_{S_{\alpha}}P_{\alpha m}\right]\exp\left[-\beta\sum_{mn}P_{1m}P_{2n}H^{int}_{12}P_{2n}P_{1m}\right].

(106)

Interestingly, the structure of
\tilde{\rho}_{S}
in (
104
) is exactly the same as that for the single reservoir case, cf. (
7
), but with the single reservoir projectors
P_{n}
replaced by the tensor product
P_{1m}\,P_{2n}
.
It can be noted that for two coupled systems interacting with a common reservoir, as studied in, for instance
Orth et al. 2010
;
F. Benatti, R. Floreanini, and U.
Marzolino 2010
;
Deng et al. 2016
the same result, (
106
) will follow.

E.2
V-system

For the three-level V-system, see Appendix

|x_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|1\rangle-|2\rangle\right),\quad|x_{\pm}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\pm\sqrt{2}|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle\right),

from which we can construct the relevant projection operators
P_{0}=|x_{0}\rangle\langle x_{0}|

H_{S}=\omega_{2}|2\rangle\langle 2|+\omega_{1}|1\rangle\langle 1|

\begin{split}\rho_{S}=\left(2+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\omega_{q}}\right)^{-1}\Big[|0\rangle\langle 0|\Big.&+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\omega_{q}}\right)\left(|1\rangle\langle 1|+|2\rangle\langle 2|\right)\\

(112)

Thus, in the strong coupling limit coherence persists between the pair of excited states

\beta\omega_{q}\ll 1
.

E.3
Two interacting qubits, two reservoirs

Here we will apply the strong coupling result (
104
) to the example of two identical qubits that are coupled to each other, and individually coupled to separate reservoirs at the same temperature.
The system Hamiltonian is given by

H_{S}=\tfrac{1}{2}\omega_{q}\left(\sigma_{1z}+\sigma_{2z}\right)+\lambda_{S}\left(\sigma_{1+}\sigma_{2-}+\sigma_{1-}\sigma_{2+}\right),

(113)

where for qubit
\alpha=1,2
, the operators are
\sigma_{\alpha z}=|e_{\alpha}\rangle\langle e_{\alpha}|-|g_{\alpha}\rangle\langle g_{\alpha}|
and
H^{int}_{12}=\lambda_{S}\left(\sigma_{1+}\sigma_{2-}+\sigma_{1-}\sigma_{2+}\right)
is the qubit interaction term with
\lambda_{S}
the inter-qubit coupling of arbitrary strength and
\sigma_{\alpha-}=|g_{\alpha}\rangle\langle e_{\alpha}|=\sigma_{\alpha+}^{\dagger}

The coupling of the qubits to the two reservoirs is given by

\lambda_{1}B_{1}X_{1}+\lambda_{2}B_{2}X_{2},

(114)

with
X_{\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha x}=\sigma_{\alpha+}+\sigma_{\alpha-}
and
\lambda_{\alpha}
both large coupling constants. The normalized eigenstates of
X_{\alpha}

|\pm_{\alpha}\rangle=\left(|e_{\alpha}\rangle\pm|g_{\alpha}\rangle\right)/\sqrt{2}
in terms of which can be defined the projection operators
P_{\alpha\pm}=|\pm_{\alpha}\rangle\langle\pm_{\alpha}|
.

Consequently we find that

P_{1m}P_{2n}H_{S}P_{2n}P_{1m}=P_{1m}P_{2n}H^{int}_{12}P_{2n}P_{1m}=\lambda_{S}\left(P_{1m}\sigma_{1+}P_{1m}\,P_{2n}\sigma_{2-}P_{2n}+\text{H.c.}\right),

(115)

for
m=\pm
and
n=\pm
.
With
P_{\alpha\pm}\sigma_{\alpha+}P_{\alpha\pm}=P_{\alpha\pm}\sigma_{\alpha-}P_{\alpha\pm}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_{\alpha x}
it follows directly that
