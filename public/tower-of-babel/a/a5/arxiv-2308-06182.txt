Noise-Resilient Designs for Optical Neural Networks
Gianluca Kosmella, Ripalta Stabile, Jaron Sanders

Abstract
All analog signal processing is fundamentally subject to noise, and this is also the case in modern implementations of Optical Neural Networks. Therefore, to mitigate noise in Optical Neural Networks, we propose two designs that are constructed from a given, possibly trained, Neural Networkthat one wishes to implement. Both designs have the capability that the resulting Optical Neural Networksgives outputs close to the desired Neural Network. To establish the latter, we analyze the designs mathematically. Specifically, we investigate a probabilistic framework for the first design that establishes that the design is correct, i.e., for any feed-forward Neural Networkwith Lipschitz continuous activation functions, an Optical Neural Networkcan be constructed that produces output arbitrarily close to the original. Optical Neural Networksconstructed with the first design thus also inherit the universal approximation property of Neural Networks. For the second design, we restrict the analysis to Neural Networkswith linear activation functions and characterize the Optical Neural Networks’ output distribution using exact formulas. Finally, we report on numerical experiments with LeNet Optical Neural Networksthat give insight into the number of components required in these designs for certain accuracy gains. We specifically study the effect of noise as a function of the depth of an Optical Neural Network. The results indicate that in practice, adding just a few components in the manner of the first or the second design can already be expected to increase the accuracy of Optical Neural Networksconsiderably.

We follow this assumption and will model an Optical Neural Networkas having been built up from interconnected nodes with noise in between them. This generic approach does not restrict us to any specific device that may be used in practice.

The model also applies to the two alternative designs of an All-Opticalimplementation of a Neural Network(see for example

In an All-OpticalNeural Network, the activation function is applied by manipulating an incoming electromagnetic wave.
Modulation (and the Additive White Gaussian Noiseit causes) only occurs prior to entering an All-Optical Neural Network(or equivalently, in the first layer).
For the remainder of the network the signal remains in the optical domain.
Here, when applying the optical activation function a new source of noise is introduced as Additive White Gaussian Noiseat the end of each layer.
Using the optical/electrical/opticalnetwork architecture, the weighted addition is performed in the optical realm, but the light is captured soon after each layer, where it is converted into an electrical and digital signal and the activation function is applied via software on a computer.
The operation on the computer can be assumed to be noiseless.
However, since the result again needs to be modulated (to be able to act as input to the next layer), modulation noise is added.
We can further abstract from the specifics of the All-Opticaland optical/electrical/opticaldesign and see that in either implementation noise occurs at the same locations within the mathematical modeling, namely Additive White Gaussian Noisefor weighted addition and afterwards Additive White Gaussian Noisefrom an optical activation function or from modulation, respectively.
This means that we do not need to distinguish between the two design choices in our modeling; we only need to choose the corresponding Additive White Gaussian Noiseterm after activation.

1.3
Noise-resistant designs for Optical Neural Networks

The main contribution of this paper lies in analyzing two noise reduction mechanisms for feed-forward Optical Neural Networks.
The mechanisms are derived from the insight that noise can be mitigated through averaging because of the law of large numbers, and they are aimed at using the enormous bandwidth that photonics offer.
The first design (Design A) and its analysis are inspired by recent advancements for Neural Networkswith random edges in
[
33
]
;
the second design (Design B) is new and simpler to implement, but comes without a theoretical guarantee of correctness for nonlinear Optical Neural Networks, specifically.

Both designs—illustrated in
Figure

2
—are built from a given Neural Networkfor which an optical implementation is desired.
Each design proposes a larger Optical Neural Networkby taking parts of the original Neural Network, and duplicating and arranging them in a certain way.
If noise is absent, then this larger Optical Neural Networkproduces the same output as the original Neural Network; and, if noise is present, then this Optical Neural Networkproduces an output closer to the desired Neural Networkthan the direct implementation of the Neural Networkas an Optical Neural Networkwithout modifications would give.

The first mechanism to construct a larger Optical Neural Networksuppressing inherent noise of analog systems starts with a certain number of copies
N
of the input data.
The copies are all processed independently by (in parallel arranged copies of) the layers.
Each copy of a layer takes in multiple input copies to produce the result of weighted addition, to which the activation mechanism is applied.
The copies that are transmitted to each layer (or set of parallel arrayed layers) are independent of each other.
The independent outputs function as inputs to the upcoming (copies of the) layers, and so on and so forth.

The idea of the second design is to use multiple copies of the input, on which the weighted addition is performed.
The noisy products of the weighted addition are averaged to a single number/light beam.
This average is then copied and the multiple copies are fed through the activation function, creating multiple noisy activations to be used as the next layer’s input, and so on.

1.4
Summary of results

Using Design A, we are able to establish that Optical Neural Networksposses the same theoretical properties as Neural Networks.
Specifically, we can prove that
any
Neural Networkcan be approximated arbitrarily well by an Optical Neural Networkbuilt using Design A (
Theorem

1
).
Similar considerations for Neural Networkswith random edges can be found in
[
33
]
, but the noise model and proof method are different.
Here, we first bound the deviation of an Optical Neural Networkand a noiseless Neural Network. To this bound Hoeffding’s inequality is then applied.

Establishing this theoretical guarantee, however, is done by increasing the number of components exponentially as the depth of the network increases.
The current proof shows that for an Optical Neural Networkwith Design A meant to approximate a Neural Networkwith
L
layers arbitrarily well (and thus reduce the noise to negligible levels), a sufficient number of components is
\omega(K^{L(L+1)}L^{L})
for some constant
K>0
. This is however not to say that such a large number is necessary: it is merely sufficient.

From a practical viewpoint, however, having to use as few components as possible would be more attractive.
We therefore also investigate Design B, in which the number of components increases only linearly with the depth of the network.
Because Design A already allows us to establish the approximation property of Optical Neural Networks, we limit our analysis of Design B to
linear
Neural Networksfor simplicity.
We specifically establish in
Theorem

2
for any linear Neural Networkthe exact output distribution of an Optical Neural Networkbuilt using Design B.
Similar to the guarantee for Design A in
Theorem

1
, but more restrictively, this implies that any linear Neural Networkcan be approximated arbitrarily well by some Optical Neural Networkbuilt using Design B. Strictly speaking, Design B now has no guarantee of correctness for nonlinear Neural Networks, but this should practically not withhold us (especially when activations, for instance, are close to linear).

We conduct numerical experiments with Designs A and B by constructing to LeNet Optical Neural Networks.
The numerical results indicate that in practice, adding some components for noise negation is already sufficient to increase the accuracy of an Optical Neural Network; an exponential number does not appear not to be necessary (see
Figures

3
to
4
).

Finally, we want to remark that the high bandwidth of photonic circuits can be exploited to implement the designs as efficiently as possible.

Discussion

One way to think of the Additive White Gaussian Noisemodel in
Section

2.1
is to take a step back from the microscopic analysis of individual devices, and consider an Optical Neural Networkas a series of black box devices (recall also
Figure

1
).
Each black box device performs their designated task and acts as communication channels with Additive White Gaussian Noise.
This way of modeling in order to analyze the impact of noise can also be seen in
[
31
]
; and other papers modeling optical channels include
[
28
,
27
]
.
Further papers considering noise in optical systems with similar noise assumptions are
[
35
,
36
]
, where furthermore multiplicative noise is considered when an amplifier is present within the circuit
[
35
]
. Qualitatively the results for Design A also apply for multiplicative noise, the scaling however may differ.

Limitations of the model

We note firstly that modeling the noise in Optical Neural Networksas Additive White Gaussian Noiseis warranted only in an operating regime with many photons, and is thus unlikely to be a good model for Optical Neural Networksthat operate in a regime with just a few photons.

Secondly, due to physical device features and operation conditions, weights, activations, and outputs can only be realized in Optical Neural Networksif their values lie in certain ranges.
Such constraints are no part of the model in
Section

2
.
Fortunately, however, the implied range restrictions are usually not a problem in practice.
For example, activation functions like sigmoid and
\tanh
map into
[0,1]
and
[-1,1]
, respectively.
Additional regularization rules like weight decay also move the entries of weight matrices in Neural Networkstowards smaller values.
In case physical constraints were met one can increase the weight decay parameter to further penalize large weights during training, leading to smaller weights so that the Optical Neural Networkis again applicable.

Observe that in Design A, the number of copies utilized in each layer, the
n_{\ell}
, are fixed.
There is however only a single copy in the last layer.
Its output is the unique output of the Optical Neural Network.
Each other layer receives multiple independent inputs.
With each of the independent copies weighted addition is performed, and the results are averaged to produce the layer’s single output.
Having independent incoming copies is achieved by having multiple independent branches of the prior partial networks incoming into a given layer.
This means that the single layer
L

n_{L-1}
independent inputs of
n_{L-1}
independent layers
L-1

Each of the
n_{L-1}

n_{L-2}

L-2

Generally, let

\ell-1

\ell

Observe that all copies are created upfront.
That means there are
\prod_{\ell=0}^{L-1}n_{\ell}
copies of the data.
By
Algorithm

\prod_{\ell=1}^{L-1}n_{\ell}

The outputs of the

arrayed copies of the first layer are the input to the
\prod_{\ell=2}^{L-1}n_{\ell}
arrayed copies of the second layer, and so on.

Notice that noise stemming from applying the activation function is subject to a linear transformation in the next layer.
The activation function noise can therefore be considered as weight-noise by inserting an identity layer with
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
,
W=I
and

3.2
Idea behind Design A

Having the law of large numbers in mind it seems reasonable that the average of multiple experiments would help in achieving a more precise output in the presence of noise.
However, it would typically not be correct to just input
n
identical, deterministic copies of
x
into
n
independent Optical Neural Networks—thus producing
n
noisy realizations
\Psi^{\mathrm{ONN},1}(x,w),\ldots,\Psi^{\mathrm{ONN},n}(x,w)
say—and then calculate their average in the hope to recover
\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}(x,w)
.
This is because while by the law of large numbers it is true that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Psi^{\mathrm{ONN},i}(x,w)=\mathbbm{E}\left[\Psi^{\mathrm{ONN}}(x,w)\right],

(25)

it is not necessarily true that the expectation

\mathbbm{E}\left[\Psi^{\mathrm{ONN}}(x,w)\right]

equals

\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}(x,w)
.
The reason is that activation functions in Neural Networksare typically nonlinear.

We can circumvent the issue by modifying the approach and instead exploit the law of large numbers layer-wise.
Recall that in the noiseless Neural Network, layer
\ell
maps a fixed input

\displaystyle x\mapsto\sigma^{(\ell)}(W^{(\ell)}x+b^{(\ell)}),

(26)

and that the same layer in the Optical Neural Networkmaps the same fixed input

\displaystyle x\mapsto\sigma^{(\ell)}(W^{(\ell)}x+b^{(\ell)}+{N}_{\mathrm{w}}^{(\ell)})

(27)

instead.
If we let
(N^{(i)})_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
be independent realizations of the distribution of
{N}_{\mathrm{w}}^{(\ell)}
(which has mean zero), we can expect by the law of large numbers that for sufficiently large
n
, the realized quantities

\frac{1}{n}\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}W^{(\ell)}x+b^{(\ell)}+N^{(i)}\bigr)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad W^{(\ell)}x+b^{(\ell)}

(28)

are close to each other.
If
\sigma^{(\ell)}
is moreover sufficiently regular, then we may expect that the realized quantity

\displaystyle\sigma^{(\ell)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}W^{(\ell)}x+b^{(\ell)}+N^{(i)}\Bigr)\Bigr)

(29)

is close to (
26
) for sufficiently large
n
, i.e., close to the unperturbed output of the original layer.

The implementation in (
29
) can be realized by using
n
times as many nodes in the hidden layer; thus to essentially create
n
copies of the original hidden layer.
These independent copies are then averaged.
Furthermore, one can allow for different inputs
(x^{i})_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
, assuming some statistical properties of their distribution.
This will be formalized next in the proof of
Theorem

1
in
Section

3.3
.

3.3.1
An upper-bound for the Optical Neural Network— Neural Networkdeviation

The output of the Design A network is

\displaystyle\tilde{x}=\sigma^{(L)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{L-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\bigl(W^{(L)}\tilde{x}^{i}+b^{(L)}+N^{(i)}\bigr)\Bigr),

(30)

where each
\tilde{x}^{i}
is recursively calculated as

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{i}=\sigma^{(L-1)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{{L-2}}}\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{L-2}}\Bigl(W^{(L-1)}\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}+b^{({L-1})}+N^{(j_{i})}\Bigr)\Bigr),

(31)

the
\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}
are calculated as

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}=\sigma^{(L-2)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{{L-3}}}\sum_{k_{j_{i}}=1}^{n_{L-3}}\Bigl(W^{(L-2)}\tilde{x}^{k_{j_{i}}}+b^{({L-2})}+N^{(k_{j_{i}})}\Bigr)\Bigr),

(32)

and so on and so forth.
The difference in
L_{2}
-norm of (
30
) and the noiseless Neural Network

\displaystyle\sigma^{(L)}\bigl(W^{(L)}\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(W^{(L-1)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-1)}\bigr)+b^{(L)}\bigr)

(33)

can iteratively be bounded by using the Lipschitz property of the activation functions, triangle inequality, and submultiplicativity of the norms.

We start the iteration by bounding

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\Bigl\|\sigma^{(L)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{L-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl(W^{(L)}\tilde{x}^{i}+b^{(L)}+N^{(i)}\Bigr)\Bigr)

(34)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}-\sigma^{(L)}\bigl(W^{(L)}\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(W^{(L-1)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-1)}\bigr)+b^{(L)}\bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\leq a^{(L)}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n_{L-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}

(36)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\Bigl(W^{(L)}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{i}-\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(W^{(L-1)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-1)}\bigr)\Bigr)+N^{(i)}\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(37)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a^{(L)}\|W^{(L)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{n_{L-1}}

(38)

\displaystyle\phantom{leq}\times\Bigl\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{i}-\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(W^{(L-1)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-1)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(39)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+a^{(L)}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n_{L-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}N^{(i)}\Bigr\|_{2}.

(40)

In the next iteration step the term

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{i}-\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(W^{(L-1)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-1)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(41)

is further bounded by first using the triangle inequality and thereafter bounding in the same way as we did in the first layer:

\displaystyle\Bigl\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl(\sigma^{(L-1)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{{L-2}}}\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{L-2}}\Bigl(W^{(L-1)}\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}+b^{({L-1})}+N^{(j_{i})}\Bigr)\Bigr)

(42)

\displaystyle-\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(W^{(L-1)}\bigl(\sigma^{(L-2)}\bigl(W^{(L-2)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-2)}\bigr)\bigr)

(43)

\displaystyle\phantom{-\sigma^{(L-1)}\bigl(}+b^{(L-1)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a^{(L-1)}\|W^{(L-1)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{n_{L-2}}

(45)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\times\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{L-2}}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}-\sigma^{(L-2)}\bigl(W^{(L-2)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-2)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(46)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+a^{(L-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n_{L-2}}\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{L-2}}N^{(j_{i})}\Bigr\|_{2}.

(47)

Here,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{L-1}}\Bigl\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{L-2}}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}-\sigma^{(L-2)}\bigl(W^{(L-2)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L-2)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(48)

may again be bounded in the same fashion. This leads to the following recursive argument.

Let
\mathscr{F}^{(\ell)}
be the sum of the differences between—loosely speaking—the ends of the remaining Design A “subtrees” and noiseless Neural Networks“subtrees” at layer
\ell
. More specifically, let

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}^{(L)}

\displaystyle:=\Big\|\tilde{x}-\sigma^{(L)}\bigl(W^{(L)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(L)}\bigr)\Big\|;

(49)

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i_{L}=1}^{n_{L-1}}\sum_{i_{{L-1}_{L}}=1}^{n_{L-2}}\dots\sum_{i_{{{\ell+2}_{\dots{}_{{L-1}_{L}}}}}=1}^{n_{\ell+1}}\Big\|\sum_{i_{{\ell+1}_{{\ell+2}_{\dots{}_{{L-1}_{L}}}}}=1}^{n_{\ell}}

(50)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\qquad\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{i_{{\ell+1}_{{\ell+2}_{\dots{}_{{L-1}_{L}}}}}}-\sigma^{(\ell)}\bigl(W^{(\ell)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(\ell)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Big\|_{2},

(51)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall\ell=1,\dots,L-1,

(52)

the special case of
\mathscr{F}^{(0)}
will be considered in detail later.
For simplicity, we join the sums outside the norm into one. Notice that because
n_{L}=1
, we have
\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L-1}n_{k}=\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}n_{k}
, and we can write

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}n_{k}}\Big\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell}}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}-\sigma^{(\ell)}\bigl(W^{(\ell)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(\ell)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Big\|_{2},

(53)

where specifically

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{j_{i}}=\sigma^{(\ell)}\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_{{\ell-1}}}\sum_{k_{j_{i}}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\Bigl(W^{(\ell)}\tilde{x}^{k_{j_{i}}}+b^{({\ell})}+N^{(k_{j_{i}})}\Bigr)\Bigr),

(54)

and the
j_{i}
and
k_{j_{i}}
are nothing more than relabelings.

Bounding
\mathscr{F}^{(\ell)}
using the triangle inequality, Lipschitz-property, and submultiplicativity yields

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle\leq a^{(\ell)}\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}n_{k}}\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell}}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n_{\ell-1}}\sum_{k_{j_{i}}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}\Bigl(N^{(k_{j_{i}})}

(55)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+W^{(\ell)}\Bigl(\tilde{x}^{k_{j_{i}}}-\sigma^{(\ell-1)}\bigl(W^{(\ell-1)}\bigl(\dots\bigr)+b^{(\ell-1)}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr\|_{2}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a^{(\ell)}\|W^{(\ell)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{n_{\ell-1}}\mathscr{F}^{(\ell-1)}

(57)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+a^{(\ell)}\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}n_{k}}\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell}}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n_{\ell-1}}\sum_{k_{j_{i}}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(k_{j_{i}})}\Bigr\|_{2}.

(58)

We thus found a recursive formula for the bound.

The recursion ends at
\mathscr{F}^{(0)}
. The noiseless Neural Networkreceives
x
as input, while the Optical Neural Networkreceives modulated input
x+N^{(j_{i})}
, where
N^{(j_{i})}
is the modulation noise, i.e., Additive White Gaussian Noise. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=1}^{L}n_{k}}\Big\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{0}}((x+N^{(j_{i})})-x)\Big\|_{2}

(59)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=1}^{L}n_{k}}\Big\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{0}}N^{(j_{i})}\Big\|_{2}.

(60)

Observe that the
x
-dependence disappeared.

Readily iterating (
58
) leads to the bound

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}\mathscr{F}^{(L)}

(61)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=L,L-1,\dots,1}\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(62)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\sum_{\ell=L,L-1,\dots,1}}\times\frac{1}{\prod_{k=\ell}^{L}n_{k}}\frac{1}{n_{\ell-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=\ell}^{L}n_{k}}\Bigl\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(j_{i})}\Bigr\|_{2}.

(63)

Therefore, if all the
L_{2}
-norms of the sums of the Gaussians are small at the same time, the network is close to the noiseless Neural Network. Let

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{\ell}:=\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}

(64)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\sum_{\ell=L,L-1,\dots,1}}\times\frac{1}{\prod_{k=\ell}^{L}n_{k}}\frac{1}{n_{\ell-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\prod_{k=\ell}^{L}n_{k}}\Bigl\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(j_{i})}\Bigr\|_{2}.

(65)

If for all
\ell

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathscr{S}_{\ell}\leq\delta_{\ell}\bigr]>1-\kappa_{\ell},

(66)

and moreover
\sum\delta_{\ell}\leq D_{L}
as well as
\prod(1-\kappa_{\ell})>1-C_{L}
,
then (
19
) holds.
This can be seen by bounding

\displaystyle\phantom{\geq}\mathbbm{P}\Bigl[\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big\|\Psi^{\mathrm{NN}}(x,w)-\tilde{\Psi}^{\mathrm{ONN}}(x,w)\big\|_{2}<D_{L}\Bigr]

(67)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbbm{P}\Bigl[\sum_{\ell}\mathscr{S}_{\ell}<D_{L}\Bigr]\geq\mathbbm{P}\Bigl[\bigcap_{\ell}\bigl\{\mathscr{S}_{\ell}<\delta_{\ell}\bigr\}\Bigr]

(68)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\ell}\mathbbm{P}\Bigl[\mathscr{S}_{\ell}<\delta_{\ell}\Bigr]>\prod_{\ell}(1-\kappa_{\ell})>1-C_{L}.

(69)

Here, in the first inequality the dependence on
x
disappears due to (
60
).

3.3.2
Bound for deviations

We next consider the
\mathscr{S}_{\ell}
for which we want to guarantee that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl[\mathscr{S}_{\ell}<\delta_{\ell}\bigr]>1-\kappa_{\ell}.

(70)

Let
m_{\ell}=\prod_{k=\ell}^{L}n_{k}
.
By assumption the
N^{(j_{i})}_{k}
are independent and identically
\mathrm{Normal}(0,\sigma^{2}_{k})
distributed, where
\sigma^{2}_{k}\leq\sigma^{2}
, for some common
\sigma^{2}
.
We are lower bounding the number of copies required, therefore using Additive White Gaussian Noisewith higher variance only increases the lower bound, as the calculations below show.
We calculate the bound exemplary for
N^{(j_{i})}
distributed according to
\mathrm{Normal}(0,\sigma^{2})
, re-substituting
\sigma_{k}^{2}
below in (
91
) (which is the bound given in
Theorem

1
) thus covers the case of
N^{(j_{i})}_{k}\overset{\textnormal{(d)}}{=}\mathrm{Normal}(0,\sigma^{2}_{k})
.

Each component of the vector

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(j_{i})}=\Bigl(\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(j_{i})}_{1},\dots,\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(j_{i})}_{d_{\ell}}\Bigr)^{\intercal}

(71)

is assumed to be
\mathrm{Normal}(0,{n_{\ell-1}}\sigma^{2})=\sqrt{{n_{\ell-1}}}\sigma\mathrm{Normal}(0,1)
distributed.
It then holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}\Bigl\|\sum_{j_{i}=1}^{n_{\ell-1}}N^{(j_{i})}\Bigr\|_{2}\overset{\textnormal{(d)}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}\sigma\|\mathrm{Normal}(0,I_{d})\|_{2}.

(72)

This is a sum of independent chi-distributed random variables, which means they are sub-gaussian (see below that we can calculate the sub-gaussian norm and it is indeed finite). Thus Hoeffding’s inequality applies, according to which, for
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
independent, mean zero, sub-gaussian random variables, for every
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl[\Bigl|\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\Bigr|<t\Bigr]>1-2\exp\Bigl(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|X_{i}\|^{2}_{\psi_{2}}}\Bigr)

(73)

holds;
see e.g.
[
37
, Theorem 2.6.2]
.
Here
c>0
is an absolute constant (see
[
37
, Theorem 2.6.2]
) and

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\bigl\{t>0:\mathbb{E}[\exp(X^{2}/t^{2})]\leq 2\bigr\}.

(74)

To apply Hoeffding’s inequality in our setting, we need to center the occurring random variables.
For
N^{(i)}\sim\mathrm{Normal}(0,I_{d})
, the term
\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}
is chi distributed with mean

\displaystyle\mu_{d}=\sqrt{2}\frac{\Gamma((d+1)/2)}{\Gamma(d/2)},

(75)

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function, see e.g.
[
38
, p.238]
.

Consider

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\frac{\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{m_{\ell}\sqrt{{n_{\ell-1}}}}\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}<\delta_{\ell}\Bigg]

(76)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\frac{\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{m_{\ell}\sqrt{{n_{\ell-1}}}}\sigma\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}\Bigl(\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}-\mu_{d_{\ell}}\Bigr)

(77)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\mathbb{P}\Bigg[}\qquad\quad<\delta_{\ell}-\frac{\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}{m_{\ell}\sqrt{{n_{\ell-1}}}}\sigma m_{\ell}\mu_{d_{\ell}}\Bigg]

(78)

which equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}\Bigl(\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}-\mu\Bigr)

(79)

\displaystyle\phantom{\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}}<\frac{m_{\ell}\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}\delta_{\ell}}{\sigma\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}-m_{\ell}\mu\Bigg]

(80)

and is lower bounded (compare to (
73
)) by

\displaystyle 1-2\exp\left(\frac{-c\Bigl(\frac{m_{\ell}\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}\delta_{\ell}}{\sigma\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}-m_{\ell}\mu_{d_{\ell}}\Bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}\Big\|\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}-\mu\Big\|^{2}_{\psi_{2}}}\right),

(81)

which in turn is lower bounded by

\displaystyle 1-2\exp\left(\frac{-c\Bigl(\frac{m_{\ell}\sqrt{n_{\ell-1}}\delta_{\ell}}{\sigma\prod_{i=\ell}^{L}a^{(i)}\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{L}\|W^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}}-m_{\ell}\mu_{d_{\ell}}\Bigr)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\ell}}C^{2}\Big\|\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}\Big\|^{2}_{\psi_{2}}}\right),

(82)

where
C>0
is an absolute constant (see
[
37
, Lemma 2.6.8]
).
For a chi distributed random variable
\mathbf{X}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mathbf{X}^{2}/t^{2})]=M_{\mathbf{X}^{2}}(1/t^{2})

(83)

where
M_{\mathbf{X}^{2}}(s)
is the moment generating function of
\mathbf{X}^{2}
—a chi-squared distributed random variable. It is known (see e.g.
[
39
, Appendix 13]
) that

\displaystyle M_{\mathbf{X}^{2}}(s)=(1-2s)^{-d_{\ell}/2}

(84)

for
s<\frac{1}{2}
. Accordingly for
2<t^{2}
, the property in the definition of the sub-gaussian norm

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\exp(\mathbf{X}^{2}/t^{2})]=\Bigl(1-2\frac{1}{t^{2}}\Bigr)^{-d_{\ell}/2}\leq 2

(85)

is satisfied for all
t
for which

\displaystyle t\geq\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{4\sqrt[d_{\ell}]{4}}{2\sqrt[d_{\ell}]{4}-2}},\sqrt{2}\right\}=\sqrt{\frac{4\sqrt[d_{\ell}]{4}}{2\sqrt[d_{\ell}]{4}-2}}

(86)

holds.
The square of the sub-gaussian norm of the chi distributed random variables is thus

\displaystyle\Big\|\|N^{(i)}\|_{2}\Big\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}=\frac{4\sqrt[d_{\ell}]{4}}{2\sqrt[d_{\ell}]{4}-2}.

(87)

Substituting the norm into the lower bound yields

3.4
Conclusion

Within the context of the model described in
Section

2
, we have established that any feed-forward Neural Networkcan be approximated arbitrarily well by Optical Neural Networksconstructed using Design A. This is
Theorem

1
in essence.

This result has two consequences when it comes to the physical implementation of Optical Neural Networks.
On the one hand, it is guaranteed that the theoretical expressiveness of Neural Networkscan be retained in practice.
On the other hand, Design A allows one to improve the accuracy of a noisy Optical Neural Networkto a desired level, and in fact bring the accuracy arbitrarily close to that of any state-of-the-art feed-forward noiseless Neural Networks.
Let us finally remark that the high bandwidth of photonic circuits may be of use when implementing Design A.

Discussion & Conclusion

Compared to

While the result holds more restrictively, it is more insightful.
For example, it allows us to consider the limit of the covariance matrix in an extremely deep Optical Neural Networks(see

Corollary

These results essentially mean that in a physical implementation of an increasingly deep and linear Optical Neural Network, the covariance matrix can be reduced (and thus remain bounded) by applying Design B with multiple copies.
The quality of the Optical Neural Network’s output increases as the number of copies in Design B (or Design A for that matter) is increased.
Finally, it is worth mentioning that Design B could potentially be implemented such that it leverages the enormous bandwidth of optics.

6
Discussion & Conclusion

Design A, introduced in
Section

3
, guarantees an approximation property (
Theorem

1
).
This is achieved through technical machinery to control the noise, even though there are nonlinear activation functions involved.
This method is powerful enough to yield the universal approximation property, as Neural Networkscan be approximated arbitrarily well with Optical Neural Networksthat are constructed through the first design, and Neural Networksthemselves can approximate any continuous function arbitrarily well
[
43
, Theorem 1]
.
Our mathematical guarantee however, only states a sufficient number of copies required, and this number grows exponentially as the number of layers increases.

We then introduced Design B in
Section

4
, in which the growth of number of copies is much more benign.
However, the analysis of Design B was restricted to linear Neural Networks, and Design B might therefore not be expressive enough to have the universal approximation property.
Linear Neural Networks, or Neural Networkswith algebraic polynomials as activation functions for that matter, namely do not posses the universal approximation property.
The assumption of linear activation functions did allow us to characterize the distribution of the output exactly on the flipside (
Theorem

2
).

In short, in this paper, we have discussed the noise present in Optical Neural Networksand described a mathematical model for the noise.
We also investigate the numerical implications of the the mathematical model, with a specific focus on the effects of depth (
Figure

5
).
The proposed noise reduction schemes yield greater accuracy and the theoretical results (
Theorem

1
and
Corollary

3
) guarantee that Optical Neural Networkswork just as noiseless Neural Networksin the many copies limit.
With the designs and findings of
Sections

3
to
4
we have a framework to exploit known Neural Networkwisdom, as no new training is required.
Further research should address optimization algorithms that take the noise of Optical Neural Networksinto account to investigate the regularization, generalization and minimization properties of trained Optical Neural Networks.
