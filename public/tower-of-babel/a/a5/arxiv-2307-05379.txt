Three-stage thermalisation of a quasi-integrable system Preprint: APS/123-QED
Leonardo Biagetti, Guillaume Cecile, Jacopo De Nardis

Abstract
We consider a system of classical hard rods or billiard balls in one dimension, initially prepared in a Bragg-pulse state at a given temperature and subjected to external periodic fields. We show that at late times the system always thermalises in the thermodynamic limit via a 3-stages process characterised by: an early phase where the dynamics is well described by Euler hydrodynamics, a subsequent where a (weak) turbulent phase is observed and where hydrodynamic gradient expansion can be broken, and a final one where the gas thermalises according to a viscous hydrodynamics. As the hard rod gas shares the same large-scale hydrodynamics as other quantum and classical integrable systems, we expect these features to universally characterise all many-body integrable systems in generic external potentials.



Introduction.—

Interacting many-particle systems are notorious for bringing up new emergent physical behaviours at large scales that would be impossible to observe at the level of their elementary constituents. Understanding and classifying the emergent laws of non-equilibrium many-body dynamics is, therefore, one of the main focuses of present-day physics. In the past years, a lot of effort has been put on understanding the equilibration and thermalisation mechanism of isolated many-body interacting systems, at theoretical
Rigol et al. 2006
;
Rigol et al. 2007
;
Rigol et al. 2008
;
Abanin et al. 2019
;
Eisert et al. 2014
and experimental level
Kinoshita et al. 2006
;
Bloch et al. 2008
;
Tang et al. 2018
;
Schemmer et al. 2018
;
Møller et al. 2021
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Malvania et al. 2021
;
Le et al. 2023
. Indeed, while the postulates of statistical physics indicate that eventually, the system thermalises, or at least it explores equally all the phase space at its disposal, much less is known about the approach to equilibrium, especially with regard to quantum systems
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Rigol et al. 2007
;
Essler and Fagotti 2016
. Violation of canonical thermalisation on the other hand, can be observed in the presence of extra symmetries, for example, local symmetries, or extensive ones, as it is the case for localised or integrable systems
Altman 2018
;
Srednicki 1994
;
Vidmar and Rigol 2016a
;
Essler and Fagotti 2016
.
There, despite conserved quantities are typically broken at large times in real settings, the dynamics of quasi-integrable systems is of extreme interest, as numerous experimental settings are much better described by integrable systems than fully non-integrable ones
Kinoshita et al. 2006
;
Bloch et al. 2008
;
Tang et al. 2018
;
Schemmer et al. 2018
;
Møller et al. 2021
;
Wei et al. 2022
;
Malvania et al. 2021
. Global integrability is typically broken by other Hamiltonian terms, such as the harmonic trap in a cold atomic setting, or an inhomogeneous magnetic field. As a full quantum problem is hard to numerically simulate for a long time, it is advisable to introduce toy models that are expected to display the same physics as the real quantum ones.

One of them is the
hard rod gas
, namely billiard balls with a fixed diameter
d
in one dimension that scatters elastically at each collision. The model is clearly integrable
Spohn 1991
;
C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
;
Percus 1969
as all the initial momenta of the rods are preserved by dynamics, and it has been shown
Spohn 1991
;
Doyon and Spohn 2017a
to be described at large scale by the same hydrodynamics characterizing generic integrable systems
Doyon and Spohn 2017b
;
Doyon et al. 2017
, i.e. generalised hydrodynamics (GHD)
Castro-Alvaredo et al. 2016
;
Bertini et al. 2016
(see also various applications to cold atomic as well as spin and fermionic systems
Bulchandani et al. 2018
;
De Nardis et al. 2018
;
Bastianello et al. 2017
;
Doyon et al. 2017
;
Ilievski and De Nardis 2017
;
Bulchandani 2017
;
Fava et al. 2021
;
Bertini and Piroli 2018
;
Pozsgay 2020
;
Bastianello et al. 2019
;
Bastianello et al. 2020a
;
Koch et al. 2021
;
Bastianello and De Luca 2019
). Moreover, the dynamics with integrability breaking terms in the hard rod gas has been the subject of numerous studies recently
Lopez-Piqueres and Vasseur 2023
;
Ferrari and Olla 2023
as indeed it is expected to display many similarities with the one observable in quantum gases.

In this letter, we study a model of hard rods in the presence of external inhomogeneous potentials. We consider both a trapping potential
V(x)
and a spacial dependent mass
m(x)
. While the latter may sound artificial, it actually mimics the dynamics of quasiparticles in spin chains under inhomogeneous magnetic fields (as the magnetic field gives an effective mass to the magnonic degrees of freedom, see for example
Scopa et al. 2022
;
Senaratne et al. 2022
). We here show that the system thermalises as long as external fields are finite and inhomogeneous by means of an effective
viscous hydrodynamics
. We observe that the thermalisation dynamics can be split into three main phases in time: I) an
Euler phase
where the dynamics almost follow the one of the Euler GHD equation, II) a
turbulent phase
, in close agreement with 2-dimensional wave turbulence
Zakharov et al. 1992
phenomenology, where the state of the system deviates from the viscous hydrodynamics prediction and where the Fourier transform of the distribution of momenta shows a power law decay and III) a final
diffusively thermalising phase
where the thermal state is approached exponentially with a timescale set by the diffusion constant of the final thermal state, fixed by the viscous GHD. While in phase I and III, viscous GHD equation correctly capture
quantitatively
the dynamics of the gas, it can fail to give correct predictions in phase II, due to a turbulence-induced
gradient catastrophe
. However, if the initial momentum distribution is smooth, as in the case of initial high temperature, the effect of such a turbulent phase is reduced and viscous GHD captures the whole thermalisation dynamics.

The hard rod gas.—

We consider a system of
N
billiard balls of diameter
d
on a circle of length
L
with Hamiltonian given by

H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[\frac{\theta_{i}^{2}}{2m(x_{i})}+V(x_{i})\Big]+\sum_{i<j}U(x_{i}-x_{j}),

(1)

where
\theta_{i}^{2}
is the momentum of each particle with its centre positioned in
x_{i}
, and where the interaction potential is the one describing hard spheres (Tonks gas),
U(x)=\infty
for
|x|\leq d
and
U(x)=0
for
|x|>d
. In this letter we consider time-independent periodically varying external potentials, with a wavelength
\ell
, namely, we consider

m(x)=m\Big(1+\frac{m_{0}}{m}{\cos(2\pi x/\ell)}\Big)^{-1};\,V(x)=V_{0}\cos(2\pi x/\ell),

(2)

and we set
m=1
generically.

The Hamiltonian (
SE.31
) with
V_{0}=m_{0}=0
is integrable and is well studied in the literature
Lebowitz et al. 1968
;
Spohn 1982
;
Tonks 1936
;
Robledo and Rowlinson 1986
;
Mendl and Spohn 2014
. As scatterings are all elastic, the initial values of the momenta
\theta_{i}
of the rods are conserved, giving
N
integrals of motions.
A generic stationary state of the integrable model, with zero external fields, is given by a so-called Generalised Gibbs ensemble (GGE)
Vidmar and Rigol 2016b
, namely a thermodynamic state where entropy is maximized given a distribution of momenta
\rho(\theta)
. One can simply use the mapping from free particles to hard-core ones to initialise the system in such a state, in particular, the spatial distribution of the particles is taken to be a Poisson point process
C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
;
Boldrighini and Sukhov 1997
. Such an initial state, when averaged over different realisations, has all the correct statistical properties of a GGE, i.e. the average number of particles in an interval
\Lambda
is
\langle dN(\theta,\Lambda)\rangle=\rho(\theta)\Lambda
and their fluctuations are given by the correct susceptibility matrix (for its explicit definition see, for example,
Doyon and Spohn 2017a
)
C({\theta,\theta^{\prime}})\Lambda=\langle dN(\theta,\Lambda)dN(\theta^{\prime},\Lambda)\rangle^{c}
.
In this work, simulations are performed always on a ring of length
L=10^{3}
, with
N=L/2
particles and by averaging over
3\times 10^{3}
initial configurations.

Viscous GHD.—

The hard rod gas, despite being a purely classical model, shares exactly the same large-scale hydrodynamics with other quantum and classical integrable models, i.e. the GHD, where the distribution of the rods momenta
\rho(\theta,x,t)
at position
x
and time
t
is taken as the effective hydrodynamic fluid density. In the purely integrable case, there is no flow in the
\theta
space, as all the momenta are conserved. However, in the presence of external forces, the flow in
\theta
space gets activated.
Indeed, switching on external potentials generically leads to integrability breaking. The momenta are not conserved any more and the resulting equations of motions for the rods, whenever the rod
i
is not in contact with any of the other rods, result in
\dot{x}_{i}(t)=\frac{\theta_{i}(t)}{m(x_{i})}
and
\dot{\theta}_{i}(t)=a_{i}(t)
,
with acceleration
a_{i}(t)=\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x_{i})+{\theta^{2}_{i}}\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x_{i})/2
, where we introduced the forces
\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x)=-\partial_{x}V
and
\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)=-\partial_{x}\big(m(x)^{-1}\big)
. Including exact viscous dissipative terms, the relevant hydrodynamic equation takes the form of a two-dimensional fluid in the effective space
(\theta,x)
, with associated gradient
\nabla=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x},&\partial_{\theta}\end{pmatrix}
, flow vector
\bm{J}^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}=\begin{pmatrix}v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]},&a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}\end{pmatrix}
and diffusion matrix
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}
(whose definition is given in eq. (
5
)),
reading as

\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\bm{J}^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}\nabla{\rho}).

(3)

This equation is conjectured to describe generic integrable systems under external fields, and it was first derived in full generality in
Durnin et al. 2021
. In
SM
is given an intuitive derivation in the case of the hard rod gas only based on the kinetic picture, in the same spirit as
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018a
;
Gopalakrishnan and Vasseur 2018
.

In order to define the flow vector and the diffusive matrix, it is useful to move from the elementary rods to the quasiparticle tracers. The tracers are defined as labels associated with the rods. During a scattering, the two rods exchange the tracer label. Such tracers, therefore, move throughout the system, experiencing jumps in space and momenta at each scattering with other particles.
Therefore, at the mesoscopic scale, the phase space dynamics depends on the local density
\rho(\theta,x)
and is described by the flow vector

\bm{J}^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}=\begin{pmatrix}v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}=(\theta-\overline{\rho\theta}d)/\big(m(x)(1-\bar{\rho}d)\big)\\
a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}=\mathfrak{f}_{1}+\mathfrak{f}_{2}(\theta^{2}/2-\overline{\rho\theta^{2}}d/2)/(1-\bar{\rho}d)\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

where over-lined quantities denote integral over momenta
\bar{f}=\int{\rm d}\theta f(\theta)
.
Since the hydrodynamic flow (
4
) depends non-trivially on the local particle density
\rho(\theta,x)
, it is subjected to a diffusive broadening due to its local finite thermodynamic fluctuations defined by the susceptibility matrix
C({\theta,\theta^{\prime}})
. Notice that in non-integrable systems such quadratic fluctuations of convective currents give generically only a lower bound to the diffusion constants
Prosen 2014
, while in integrable ones they fully saturate them
Doyon 2022
. Here we show that the latter remains true also in quasi-integrable systems.

The diffusion coefficients
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}
are then defined as generators of reversible Markov jump processes induced by such density fluctuations
Spohn 1991
;
Doyon and Spohn 2017b
and are equal to

\displaystyle\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}=\delta_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x^{\prime})\bm{g}(\theta,\theta^{\prime\prime})-\rho(\theta^{\prime},x)\bm{g}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}),

(5)

where
\delta_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}
is the Dirac delta in the momenta space and the kernel matrix
\bm{g}
is defined by

\frac{\bm{g}}{d^{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}|v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})|&\xi_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\\
\xi_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)&\frac{\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)^{2}}{|v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})|}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(6)

with
\xi_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\equiv{\rm sgn}\big(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)
(for a full derivation from kinetic theory see
SM
).
While the left-hand side of (
3
), which corresponds to the Euler GHD equation, has numerous integrals of motion
Caux et al. 2019
;
Cao et al. 2017
and does not thermalise, one can show (see
SM
) that the viscous terms on the right-hand side break most of the conservation laws, and the fixed point of eq. (
3
) is a thermal LDA (local density approximation) state, (as discussed also in
Bastianello et al. 2020b
;
Durnin et al. 2021
) determined only by the inverse temperature
\beta
and chemical potential
\mu
, fixed by the only conserved quantities: the integrated density
N_{0}=\int{\rm d}x\int{\rm d}\theta\rho(\theta,x,0)
and energy
E_{0}=\int{\rm d}x\int{\rm d}\theta\rho(\theta,x,0)(\theta^{2}/(2m(x))+V(x))
. In terms of these parameters, the thermal distribution reads

{}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta;x)\sim e^{-\beta(m(x)\theta^{2}/2+V(x)-\mu)}.

(7)

We here shall consider the integrated distribution in space

\hat{\rho}(\theta,t)=\int{\rm d}x\rho(\theta,x,t)

and show that
at late times thermalisation is always achieved

\hat{\rho}(\theta,t)\to\hat{\rho}_{\rm th}(\theta)
under the effect of the external forces and internal viscosities, by means of the mechanism we describe below.

The three-stage thermalisation.—

We now focus on a concrete example of dynamics from an initial state. We mimic the state created by a strong Bragg pulse in cold atomic gas, as done for example in
Kinoshita et al. 2006
;
Le et al. 2023
. In the context of the hard rods, we prepare a spatially homogenous gas with a distribution of momenta given by two Gaussian peaks with a given initial temperature
T_{0}

\rho(\theta,x,t=0)\sim e^{-\frac{(\theta-\theta_{\rm Bragg})^{2}}{2T_{0}}}+e^{-\frac{(\theta+\theta_{\rm Bragg})^{2}}{2T_{0}}},

(8)

with normalisation fixed by density
\bar{\rho}=1/2
and with
\theta_{\rm Bragg}=1
here for convenience. We then let the system evolve under an external field, either with
V_{0}
or
m_{0}
finite. We solve the GHD equation (
3
) by means of a combined 4th order Runge-Kutta method as defined in
Møller et al. 2022
and an implicit mid-point method and by rescaling space
x
and time
t
by
\ell
, which allows solving it in
x\in[0,1]
, by adding a factor
1/\ell
in front of the diffusive terms. We compare the GHD prediction with exact numerical simulations of hard rods, see
SM
for details on the algorithm. We focus on the integrated density
\hat{\rho}(\theta)
(which can be obtained by a trap-release protocol in a cold atomic setting
Malvania et al. 2021
;
Bezzaz et al. 2023
) and in particular on its kurtosis
{\rm Ku}(t)=\bar{\theta^{4}}/(\bar{\theta}^{2})^{2}-3
with the overline average taken with
\hat{\rho}(\theta,t)
as a measure, see Fig.
1
. When
m_{0}=0
, thermalisation is manifested by
{\rm Ku}(t)\to 0
at large times, signalling that the integrated distribution converges to its thermal value (
7
). However, when
m_{0}\neq 0
the integrated thermal distribution is not a Gaussian, as can be seen from expression (
7
) and a different value of non-zero kurtosis is therefore expected at large times. For values of
\ell
relatively large to ensure the validity of the fluid cell approximation, at times
t\lesssim t_{\rm Eul}\equiv\ell m/\theta_{\rm Bragg}
we always observe agreement between numerical hard rods simulations and viscous GHD, where the latter can also be simplified to its Euler formulation with no dramatic change of behaviour, see Fig.
1
where the Euler GHD prediction is reported (up to the time after which the numerical simulation becomes unstable). We denote this phase as the Euler phase, where the dynamics is purely given by an adiabatic Euler flow. As soon as time becomes comparable with
\sim t_{\rm Eul}
something dramatic happens: when the gas initial temperature is small
T_{0}/(\theta_{\rm bragg}^{2})\ll 1
, the exact numerical simulations and the hydrodynamic predictions do not agree any more, even for large
\ell
(actually larger is
\ell
, stronger are the deviations from hydrodynamics). We denote this phase as the
turbulent phase
. This phase is characterised by a proliferation of discontinuities in the derivative of the momentum distribution
\rho(\theta,x,t)
, causing the hydrodynamic gradient expansion to break down (gradient catastrophe), whenever the gas is interacting (a similar mechanism for free fermion gases was discussed in
Kulkarni et al. 2018
). In order to quantify such a discontinuous behaviour, we consider the absolute value of the Fourier transform in
\theta
of the integrated momenta distribution
|\mathfrak{F}[\hat{\rho}](k_{\theta})|=|\int{\rm d}\theta e^{ik_{\theta}\theta}\hat{\rho}(\theta,t)|/2\pi
, see Fig.
2
. The latter decays exponentially in
k_{\theta}
at short times (being the Fourier transform of two Gaussian peaks) to then develop a power law decay as
k_{\theta}^{-2}
, compatible with the presence of cusps. For momenta larger than a dissipation scale
k_{\theta}\gtrsim 1/\Lambda_{\rm sink}
, (the latter fixed by the viscosity
\Lambda_{\rm sink}\sim\bm{{\mathfrak{D}}}/\theta_{\rm Bragg}\sim d^{2}\bar{\rho}
and by the initial temperature
T_{0}
, the power law decay transients to an exponential one, as expected from typical turbulent spectra. As the system under consideration here is not driven, eventually viscous terms take over as time progresses and redistribute smoothly momenta in the
(\theta,x)
space. At times
t\sim t_{\rm Diff}
, with (see
SM
for a derivation)

\displaystyle t_{\rm Diff}\equiv\ell^{2}\ m\theta_{\rm Bragg}/(d^{2}\bar{\rho}V_{0}),

\displaystyle t_{\rm Diff}\equiv\ell^{2}\ m^{2}(m-m_{0})^{2}/(m_{0}^{3}d^{2}\bar{\rho}\theta_{\rm Bragg}),

(9)

respectively in the presence of space-dependent trapping potential or space-dependent mass (see also
SM
for rescaled plots in these units), there is no trace of turbulent behaviour any more and the system thermalizes by redistributing a remaining low-momentum density ripple (in
x
direction) on a timescale set by the minimal diffusion constant in the system. In this phase, denoted as
diffusively thermalising phase
, hydrodynamics is restored and gives quantitatively correct prediction for the decay time and the momenta distributions. To clarify such mechanism we show also the free particle case
d=0
in Fig.
2
(see also
SM
for additional data on this case): the same proliferation of discontinuities in the single particle density
\hat{\rho}(\theta)
occurs, but being Euler hydrodynamics there an exact description of the system, with no higher derivative terms, no breaking of hydrodynamics expansion occurs. In the interacting case
d>0
instead, Euler and diffusive GHD are approximate theories and higher order derivatives in
x
and
\theta
are generically present, and they are expected to become relevant at times
t/t_{\rm Eul}\sim 1
. Therefore, the inset of the turbulent phase does not signal the breakdown of Euler GHD but rather the
breakdown of Hydrodynamic gradient expansion
, so that viscous GHD fails to provide a complete description of the state.

Finally, we emphasise that the relevance of such a turbulent regime depends on the initial state. Initial states of the type of eq. (
8
) with larger
T_{0}/\theta_{\rm Bragg}^{2}
ratio, have larger
\Lambda_{\rm sink}
and thus a less pronounced turbulent phase and diffusive GHD well reproduces the dynamics at all times, see Fig.
3
. Moreover, we also stress the surprising consequence of our results: the GHD description is actually more accurate when
\ell
is small, i.e. for strongly broken integrability, as compared with large
\ell
, i.e. for weakly broken integrability. Clearly, if
\ell
is taken to be very large, the timescale for turbulence
t\sim t_{\rm Eul}\propto\ell
and for thermalisation
t\sim t_{\rm Diff}\propto\ell^{2}
does increase, but the turbulence phase will be even stronger as
\ell
is increased, as diffusion needs longer times to fully redistribute momenta throughout the system.

Conclusion.—

In this letter, we have studied how under the influence of external fields, a system of interacting hard rods thermalises, a question that has received contradictory answers in the past few years
Cao et al. 2017
;
Bagchi et al. 2023
, and which is extremely relevant in order to understand the thermalisation mechanisms for quasi-integrable models.

Our findings are summarised as the following: in the case of smooth (in momentum space) initial states, the evolution of the distribution of momenta remain also smooth both in space and momentum direction, and viscous GHD provides an exact description of the thermalisation dynamics. When instead the distribution develops cusps, a turbulent phase kicks in and the hydrodynamic gradient expansion fails: viscous GHD is not enough to predict the whole thermalisation dynamics, yet it provides a good description of the system at early and, surprisingly enough, late times.
Therefore, quite counter-intuitively, viscous GHD predicts more accurately the behavior of a gas with strong external forces rather than one with weak ones.

Our work opens new unexplored and exciting directions, in particular on the nature of turbulence and hydrodynamic breaking and restoration in quasi-integrable systems.
Indeed, as their effective hydrodynamics is two-dimensional, weaker forms of turbulence, as for example the wave turbulent phase, recently observed in a two-dimensional weakly interacting Bose gas
Navon et al. 2016
;
Navon et al. 2019
;
Galka et al. 2022
and in other quasi-integrable systems in
Gallone et al. 2022
, are therefore possible. This paper provides a first example and open the way for new exiting developments for example within the Lieb-Liniger gas or the Gross-Pitaevskii equation
Koch et al. 2022
;
Bezzaz et al. 2023
;
Vecchio et al. 2020
, which would open the way to a possible experimental observation of such a turbulent behaviour.

Appendix B
The hard rods algorithm

In this appendix, we describe the algorithm used to simulate the dynamics of a hard rod gas.
We consider a system of
N
particles
\{P_{j},j=1,...,N\}
having length
d
and coordinates
\{(x_{j},\theta_{j}),j=1,...,N\}
. They move freely in a one-dimensional space of length
L
, except for elastic collisions that conserve energy and momentum. The particles are also ordered from left to right
x_{j+1}\geq x_{j}+d
.
The single-particle Hamiltonian of the system is

H(\theta,x)=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2m(x)}+V_{1}(x),

(SB.1)

where
V_{1}(x)
is an external trapping potentials and
m(x)
is a space dependent mass. The hard rods algorithm for the time evolution of the system is the following
Mestyán and Alba 2020
:

0.

Initialize the system in a GGE state (see subsection
B.1
).

1.

Find the pair of particles
\{P_{j},P_{j+1}\}
that scatter first and their scattering time
t_{\rm coll}
.

2.

Let all the particles evolve without interaction until
t_{\rm coll}
.

3.

Perform the scattering between particles
\{P_{j},P_{j+1}\}
.

4.

Iterate
1
-
3
.

B.1
Initialization of the system

We discuss here how to initialize the rods in a GGE state, homogeneous in space and with an arbitrary momentum distribution
h(\theta)
.

In a hard rod GGE state, the distribution of particles in space is a Poisson point process
C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
;
Boldrighini and Sukhov 1997
. In particular, the probability of finding the edges of two neighbouring particles at distance
y
is equal to

\mathbb{P}(y)=\frac{\bar{\rho}}{1-d\bar{\rho}}{\rm e}^{-y\frac{\bar{\rho}}{1-d\bar{\rho}}},

(SB.2)

with
\bar{\rho}=N/L
. In order to impose such statistics, we initialize the system with the following procedure: we place the particle with
j=1
at distance
y_{1}+d
from the left border, where
y_{1}
is extracted from a distribution
SB.2
. We repeat for particles with
j>1
, placing them at position
x_{j}=x_{j-1}+y_{j}+d
until we reach the right border. Then we extract the momentum of particles from the distribution
h(\theta)
.
It can be proven that the mean number of particles in the system is actually
N
.

B.2
Free particle dynamics and collisional time

The equations of motion for the Hamiltonian defined in eq.
SB.1
are

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=\frac{\theta}{m(x)},\\
\dot{\theta}=\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x)+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\ \mathfrak{f}_{2}(x),\end{cases}

(SB.3)

with
\mathfrak{f}_{1}\equiv-\partial_{x}V_{1}(x)
and
\mathfrak{f}_{2}\equiv-\partial_{x}m^{-1}(x)
. Considering non-trivial potentials, the system
SE.32
of nonlinear differential equations is not solvable analytically. Thus, we use a second-order explicit Runge-Kutta method to compute the time evolution of a free particle.

We adopt a time discretization with time step
{\rm d}t
and evolve the particle coordinates
\{x_{[n-1]},\theta_{[n-1]}\}\rightarrow\{x_{[n]},\theta_{[n]}\}
according to the equations

\begin{split}x_{[n]}&=x_{[n-1]}+\frac{K_{v,n}}{m(K_{x,n})}{\rm d}t,\\
\theta_{[n]}&=\theta_{[n-1]}+\big(\mathfrak{f}_{1}(K_{x,n})+\mathfrak{f}_{2}(K_{x,n})\frac{K_{v,n}^{2}}{2}\big){\rm d}t,\\
K_{x,n}&\equiv x_{[n-1]}+\frac{\theta_{[n-1]}}{m(x_{[n-1]})}\frac{{\rm d}t}{2},\\
K_{v,n}&\equiv\theta_{[n-1]}+\big(\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x_{[n-1]})+\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x_{[n-1]})\frac{\theta_{[n-1]}^{2}}{2}\big)\frac{{\rm d}t}{2},\end{split}

(SB.4)

where
\{x_{[n]},\theta_{[n]}\}
is the Runge-Kutta approximation of
\{x(t_{n}),\theta(t_{n})\}
with
t_{n}=n\,{\rm d}t
.
According to equation
SB.4
, the collisional time between two neighbouring particles
\{P_{j},P_{j+1}\}
is

t^{j}_{\rm coll}=\frac{d-(x_{j+1}-x_{j})}{\theta_{j+1}/m(x_{j+1})-\theta_{j}/m(x_{j})}+\mathcal{O}({\rm d}t^{2}).

(SB.5)

Hence, at each time step, we evaluate the minimal collisional time
t_{\rm coll}=\{{\rm min}\,t^{j}_{\rm coll},j=1,...,N-1\}
between particles. As long as
t_{\rm coll}>{\rm d}t
is valid, we perform the Runge-Kutta time evolution using equations
SB.4
with time step
{\rm d}t
. When
t_{\rm coll}<{\rm d}t
, we perform the Runge-Kutta time evolution with time step
t_{\rm coll}
and then we perform the collision.

B.3
Collisions between particles

In the hard rod gas, the particles experience contact interaction that conserves energy and momentum. Hence, two rods
\{P_{j},P_{j+1}\}
interact only if
x_{j+1}=x_{j}+d
.

Considering the scattering between particles
\{P_{j},P_{j+1}\}
with initial coordinates
\{x_{j},\theta_{j}\}
,
\{x_{j+1}=x_{j}+d,\theta_{j+1}\}
, their momenta after the collision will be

\theta_{j+1}^{\rm f}=\frac{2m(x_{j+1})\theta_{j}-m(x_{j})\theta_{j+1}+m(x_{j+1})\theta_{j+1}}{m(x_{j})+m(x_{j+1})},\quad\theta_{j}^{\rm f}=\frac{2m(x_{j})\theta_{j+1}-m(x_{j+1})\theta_{j}+m(x_{j})\theta_{j}}{m(x_{j})+m(x_{j+1})}.

(SB.6)

Thus, in the case of constant mass
m(x)=m_{0}
, the particles simply exchange their momenta through collisions.

Appendix C
The GHD equation for a hard rod gas with forces

In this appendix, we introduce the GHD theory for a hard rod gas in the presence of external forces.
In particular, we consider a one-dimensional gas of hard rods with length
d
and in the presence of the external trapping potential
V_{1}(x)
and with a space-dependent mass
m(x)
such that the bare energy and momentum of a single particle are defined as

\epsilon(\theta,x)=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2m(x)}+V_{1}(x)\quad\mbox{ , }\quad p(\theta,x)=\theta.

(SC.7)

In the absence of external potentials, the system is clearly integrable
Spohn 1991
;
C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
;
Percus 1969
and it has been proven that at large scale it is described by GHD
Doyon and Spohn 2017b
;
Doyon et al. 2017
.
In section
C.1
we define the dressing operator for the hard rod gas, and we describe the GHD at the Euler scale. In section
C.2
we define the full GHD equation with viscosities and force terms of the system.

C.1
The GHD equation at Euler scale

We consider an ensemble of hard rods moving in one dimension with velocities
v
. The quasiparticles are identified with trajectory tracers, following the centres of particles. Whenever two particles collide, the two tracers associated with them, exchange their position. Thus, they compute an instantaneous displacement of
d
in space, with
d
equal to the length of the rods. The momenta
\theta
of the quasiparticles can be identified with the velocities
v
of the particles. Hence, we can define a local stationary state via the local density of quasiparticles
\rho(\theta,x,t)
so that
\rho(\theta,x,t){\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta
is equal to the number of particles in the phase space interval
[(x,x+{\rm d}x),(\theta,\theta+{\rm d}\theta)]
.

Since the spatial displacement of the quasiparticles at each collision is not dependent on their momenta, the scattering kernel (in real space) is particularly simple

T_{x}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=-\frac{d}{2\pi}.

(SC.8)

Given the scattering kernel
SC.8
, we can define the dressing operation for single-particle functions
f(\theta,x)

f^{\rm dr}(\theta,x)=f(\theta,x)-d\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta\rho(\theta,x,t)f(\theta,x).

(SC.9)

Thus, if we use the dressing operation on the identity we have

1^{\rm dr}=1-\bar{\rho}(x,t)d\quad\mbox{ with, }\quad\bar{\rho}(x,t)\equiv\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta\rho(\theta,x,t).

(SC.10)

The effective velocity
v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x)
of a quasiparticle is defined by
Doyon 2020

v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta,x)\equiv\frac{(\partial_{\theta}\epsilon)^{\rm dr}}{(\partial_{\theta}p)^{\rm dr}},

(SC.11)

and the effective acceleration
a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x)
is defined by

a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta,x)\equiv\frac{(-\partial_{x}\epsilon)^{\rm dr}}{(\partial_{\theta}p)^{\rm dr}}.

(SC.12)

Using the explicit form of the single-particle energy and momentum
SC.7
and the dressing operation
SC.9
, we get the expressions for the effective velocity and acceleration for a hard rod gas

\displaystyle v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta,x,t)=\frac{\theta-d\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}{m(x)\big(1-\bar{\rho}(x,t)d\big)},

(SC.13)

\displaystyle a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta,x,t)=\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x)+\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)\frac{\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-d\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\frac{\theta^{\prime 2}}{2}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}{1-\bar{\rho}(x,t)d},

(SC.14)

where we defined
\mathfrak{f}_{1}=-\partial_{x}V_{1}(x)
and
\mathfrak{f}_{2}=-\partial_{x}m^{-1}(x)
. The expressions
SC.13
and
SC.14
are equivalent to the components of the flow vector
\bm{J}^{\rm eff}
defined in the main text.

Thus, the GHD equation at the Euler scale reads

\partial_{t}\rho(\theta,x,t)+\partial_{x}\big(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta,x,t)\rho(\theta,x,t)\big)+\partial_{\theta}\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta,x,t)\rho(\theta,x,t)\big)=0.

(SC.15)

C.2
The diffusive GHD equation with forces

In this section, we take into consideration the second-order terms in the hydrodynamic expansion.
The diffusive GHD equation in the main text can be written as
Durnin et al. 2021
;
Nardis et al. 2019

\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}\rho)+\partial_{\theta}(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\partial_{x}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,1)}\partial_{x}\rho)+\partial_{x}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho)+\partial_{\theta}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(2,1)}\partial_{x}\rho)+\partial_{\theta}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(2,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho)\Big],

(SC.16)

where the diffusion matrices
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}
is a
2\times 2
matrix of operators defined as

\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=d^{2}\Big(\delta_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\kappa\rho(\kappa,x,t)\bm{g}(\theta,\kappa)-\rho(\theta,x,t)\bm{g}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big),

(SC.17)

with
\delta_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}
Dirac delta.
We also defined the kernels
\bm{g}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
as

\bm{g}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=d^{2}\begin{pmatrix}|v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})|&{\rm sgn}\big(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\\
{\rm sgn}\big(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)&\frac{\big(a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})\big)^{2}}{|v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho]}(\theta^{\prime})|}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(SC.18)

C.3
Euler and diffusive time scales

Here we consider a system of hard rods prepared in a homogeneous state with distribution of momenta given by two two Gaussian peaks centered in
\theta=\pm\theta_{\rm Bragg}
and with a given initial temperature
T_{0}
. Since rapidities have the same units as momentum, the unit of velocity is
v\sim\theta_{\rm Bragg}/m
. The macroscopic length scale of the system is the parameter
\ell
, defining the potential trap and the space dependent mass. Then, the Euler time scale is given by the macroscopic unit of length and velocity :
t_{\rm Eul}=m\ell/\theta_{\rm Bragg}
.

To compute the diffusive time scale we firstly consider the case with
m_{0}=0
and
V_{0}\neq 0
. In this case, from GHD equation, we know that the diffusive time scale is inversely proportional to the force
F\sim V_{0}/\ell
and to the diffusive kernel
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(x,x)}\sim d^{2}\bar{\rho}/m
. Since
[\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(x,x)}V_{0}/\ell]^{-1}=[t^{2}/\ell^{2}]
, then the only time scale that we can define using these quantities, together with the unit of velocity and length, is

t_{\rm Diff}=\frac{\theta_{\rm Bragg}\ell^{2}}{d^{2}\bar{\rho}V_{0}}\,.

(SC.19)

Now we consider the case with
V_{0}=0
and
m_{0}\neq 0
. Again, the diffusive time scale is known to be inversely proportional to the to the force
F\sim\mathfrak{f}_{2}\theta^{2}
and to the diffusive kernel
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(\theta,\theta)}\sim d^{2}\bar{\rho}m(x)\mathfrak{f}^{2}_{2}\theta^{2}_{\rm Bragg}
. Since
[\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(x,\theta)}\mathfrak{f}_{2}\theta^{2}]^{-1}=[t^{4}/m^{2}\ell^{2}]
, the only time scale that we can define using this quantity, together with the unit of velocity and length, is
t_{\rm Diff}=(d^{2}\bar{\rho}\ell m(x)^{2}\mathfrak{f}_{2}^{3}\theta_{\rm Bragg})^{-1}
. Using that
(m^{2}(x)\mathfrak{f}^{3}_{2})^{-1}\sim\ell^{3}m^{2}(m-m_{0})^{2}/m_{0}^{3}
we can express the diffusive time scale in terms of
m_{0}

t_{\rm Diff}=\frac{\ell^{2}m^{2}(m-m_{0})^{2}}{d^{2}\bar{\rho}\theta_{\rm Bragg}m_{0}^{3}}\,.

(SC.20)

Appendix D
Stationarity of thermal distribution under diffusive GHD

In this appendix, we show that the thermal distribution is stationary under diffusive GHD. Namely, we prove that the thermal distribution
\rho_{\rm th}(\theta,x)
is a solution of the equation,

-\partial_{x}(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}\rho_{\rm th})-\partial_{\theta}(a_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{\rm eff}\rho_{\rm th})+\frac{1}{2}\Big[\partial_{x}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th})+\partial_{x}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th})+\partial_{\theta}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th})+\partial_{\theta}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th})\Big]=0.

(SD.21)

In order to do that we introduce some explicit expressions for the derivatives of a thermal distribution. This latter is defined via the function

\epsilon_{\rm th}(\theta)=\beta w(\theta;x)-\mu+\frac{d}{2\pi}\int d\theta e^{-\epsilon_{\rm th}(\theta)},\quad\quad w(\theta,x)=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2m(x)}+V_{1}(x),

(SD.22)

as
n_{\rm th}(\theta)=e^{-\epsilon_{\rm th}(\theta)}
at each point
x
. It satisfies the relations

\begin{split}\partial_{x}n_{\rm th}&=(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th})\beta\mathfrak{f}_{1}n_{\rm th}+\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}n_{\rm th}\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-d\bar{e}_{\rm th}\bigg),\\
\partial_{\theta}n_{\rm th}&=-\beta\theta n_{\rm th}/m(x),\\
\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}&=-\frac{d\partial_{x}\bar{\rho}_{\rm th}}{(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th})}\rho_{\rm th}+(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th})\beta\mathfrak{f}_{1}\rho_{\rm th}+\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}\rho_{\rm th}\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-d\bar{e}_{\rm th}\bigg),\\
\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}&=-\beta\theta\rho_{\rm th}/m(x),\\
\partial_{x}\bar{\rho}_{\rm th}&=(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th})^{2}\Big(\beta\mathfrak{f}_{1}\bar{\rho}_{\rm th}+\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}\bar{e}_{\rm th}\Big),\end{split}

(SD.23)

with
\bar{e}_{\rm th}\equiv\int\mbox{d}\theta\,\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\theta^{2}/2
and
\bar{u}_{\rm th}\equiv\int\mbox{d}\theta\,\theta\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)=0
.

Firstly, we derive the explicit expressions for the Euler terms in eq.
SD.21
using the formulas
SD.23

\begin{split}\partial_{x}(v^{\rm eff}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}\rho_{\rm th})&=\partial_{x}(\theta n_{\rm th}/m(x))=\theta(\partial_{x}n_{\rm th})/m(x)-\theta\mathfrak{f}_{2}n_{\rm th},\\
&=-\mathfrak{f}_{1}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}+\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}\theta\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-d\bar{e}_{\rm th}\bigg)n_{\rm th}/m(x)-\theta\mathfrak{f}_{2}n_{\rm th}.\end{split}

(SD.24)

\begin{split}\partial_{\theta}(a^{\rm{eff}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}\rho_{\rm th})&=\mathfrak{f}_{1}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}-\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}\theta\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-d\bar{e}_{\rm th}\bigg)n_{\rm th}/m(x)+\theta\mathfrak{f}_{2}n_{\rm th}\\
&=-\partial_{x}(\rho_{\rm th}v^{\rm{eff}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}),\end{split}

(SD.25)

hence, the thermal distribution is stationary under Euler GHD. Then we take into consideration the diffusive terms of equation
SD.21
, as follows

\begin{split}\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\bigg[\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bm{g}^{(1,1)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)-\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\bm{g}^{(1,1)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bigg]\\
&=\frac{d^{2}\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}}{m(x)\big(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th}\big)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta^{\prime 2}}{2}\bigg)\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime}),\end{split}

(SD.26)

\begin{split}\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\bigg[\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bm{g}^{(1,2)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)-\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\bm{g}^{(1,2)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bigg]\\
&=-\frac{d^{2}\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}}{m(x)\big(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th}\big)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta^{\prime 2}}{2}\bigg)\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\\
&=-\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th},\end{split}

(SD.27)

\begin{split}\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\bigg[\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bm{g}^{(2,1)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)-\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\bm{g}^{(2,1)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bigg]\\
&=\frac{d^{2}\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}^{2}}{(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rm{sgn}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta^{\prime 2}}{2}\bigg)^{2}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime}),\end{split}

(SD.28)

\begin{split}\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\bigg[\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bm{g}^{(2,2)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)-\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\bm{g}^{(2,2)}_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta^{\prime})\bigg]\\
&=-\frac{d^{2}\beta\mathfrak{f}_{2}^{2}}{(1-d\bar{\rho}_{\rm th})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rm{sgn}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta^{\prime 2}}{2}\bigg)^{2}\rho_{\rm th}(\theta)\rho_{th}(\theta^{\prime})\\
&=-\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th},\end{split}

(SD.29)

where we used the relations
SD.23
and the symmetry of the operators
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(i,j)}
with
i,j\in\{1,2\}
.

Thus, we proved that the thermal distribution is stationary also under the diffusive terms of GHD equation

\partial_{x}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}+\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(1,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th})+\partial_{\theta}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,1)}\partial_{x}\rho_{\rm th}+\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho_{\rm th}]}^{(2,2)}\partial_{\theta}\rho_{\rm th})=0.

(SD.30)

Appendix E
Kinetic picture of GHD equation

The aim of this appendix is to give a kinetic interpretation of GHD equation. In section
E.1
, we derive the Euler GHD equation in the presence of external forces using only kinetic arguments. Next, in section
E.2
, we give a kinetic interpretation of the diffusive terms of equation
SC.16
as diffusive corrections to ballistic quasiparticle spreading.

E.1
Kinetic derivation of Euler GHD equation

In this section, we extend the argument presented in
C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
to derive the Euler GHD equation in the presence of external forces. We consider a system of hard rods of length
d
. The one-particle Hamiltonian of the system is

H(\theta,x)=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2m(x)}+V_{1}(x),

(SE.31)

with
V_{1}(x)
external trapping potential and
m(x)
space dependent mass.
The Hamilton equations associated to
SE.31
are

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=\frac{\theta}{m(x)},\\
\dot{\theta}=\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x)+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\ \mathfrak{f}_{2}(x),\end{cases}

(SE.32)

where we defined
\mathfrak{f}_{1}\equiv-\partial_{x}V_{1}(x)
and
\mathfrak{f}_{2}\equiv-\partial_{x}m^{-1}(x)
.
Considering the system in the thermodynamic limit, we introduce
\rho(\theta,x,t)
as the average particle density of the gas, namely
\rho(\theta,x,t){\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}t
is equal to the number of particles in the interval
[(x,x+{\rm d}x),(\theta,\theta+{\rm d}\theta),(t,t+{\rm d}t)]
. The total particle density is given by

\bar{\rho}(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta\rho(\theta,x,t).

(SE.33)

The particles are also labelled to exchange their position during the elastic collisions. Namely, each particle moves freely, according to equations
SE.32
, except for jumps in position and momentum at each collision
C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
;
Doyon and Spohn 2017b
.
On a timescale much larger than the mean free time, the particles will move with an effective velocity and effective acceleration that depend on all the collisions that they make. In particular, each time a particle collides from the left (right) with another one, its position is shifted to the right (left) by
d
.
Hence, the effective velocity of a particle having momentum
\theta
must be equal to,

v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)=\frac{\theta}{m(x)}+d\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\Big(n_{+}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-n_{-}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big),

(SE.34)

where
n_{+(-)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}){\rm d}t{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}
is the probability of a particle with momentum
\theta
making a scattering with a particle with momentum in
[\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}+{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}]
from right (left) in the time step
[t,t+{\rm d}t]
.
The average distance
\bar{d}(x,t)
between particles in the interval
[(x,x+{\rm d}x),(t,t+{\rm d}t)]
is given by the ratio between the total free space and the total number of particles,

\bar{d}(x,t)=\frac{{\rm d}x{\rm d}t-d\bar{\rho}(x,t){\rm d}x{\rm d}t}{\bar{\rho}(x,t){\rm d}x{\rm d}t}=\frac{1-d\bar{\rho}(x,t)}{\bar{\rho}(x,t)}.

(SE.35)

The probability of the particle firstly colliding with another one having velocity in the range
[\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}+{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}]
is
\big(\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)/\bar{\rho}(x,t)\big){\rm d}\theta^{\prime}
.
Thus, assuming that the space-dependent mass
m(x)
is slowly varying compared to the dimension
d
of the rods, the probability of having this scattering in the time interval
[t,t+{\rm d}t]
is equal to

\begin{split}d\Big(n_{+}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-n_{-}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big){\rm d}t{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}&=d\frac{\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}{\bar{\rho}(x,t)}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\frac{\big(\theta/m(x)-\theta^{\prime}/m(x+d)))}{\bar{d}(x,t)}{\rm d}t\\
&=d\frac{\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}{m(x)\big(1-d\bar{\rho}(x,t)\big)}(\theta-\theta^{\prime}){\rm d}t{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(d\partial_{x}m^{-1}(x)).\end{split}

(SE.36)

Using the probability
SE.36
in eq.
SE.34
we get the explicit expression for the effective velocity

v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)=\frac{\theta}{m(x)}+\frac{d}{m(x)\big(1-d\bar{\rho}(x,t)\big)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)(\theta-\theta^{\prime}),

(SE.37)

which is equal to the one previously shown in eq.
SC.13
.
The effective acceleration is expected to have a similar form as
SE.34
, but with a different kernel
T_{\theta}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
defined as the jump of momenta at each scattering,

\begin{split}a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)&=a^{\rm br}(\theta,x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}T_{\theta}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big(n_{+}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})-n_{-}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\Big)\\
&=a^{\rm br}(\theta,x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}T_{\theta}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\frac{\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}{m(x)\big(1-d\bar{\rho}(x,t)\big)}(\theta-\theta^{\prime}),\end{split}

(SE.38)

where
a^{\rm br}(\theta,x)\equiv\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x)+\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)
is the bare acceleration shown in eq.
SE.32
. We now derive an explicit expression for the kernel
T_{\theta}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})
.
Since the collisions are elastic, they conserve energy and momentum of the two particles that are colliding. Thus, if a particle of momentum
\theta
collides with a particle with
\theta^{\prime}
, its final momentum will be

\theta^{\rm f}=\frac{2m(x+d)\theta-m(x)\theta^{\prime}+m(x+d)\theta^{\prime}}{m(x+d)+m(x)}.

(SE.39)

Assuming that
m^{-1}(x)
is slow varying compared to the dimension
d
of the rods, we can expand it in derivatives:
m^{-1}(x+d)=m^{-1}(x)-d\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)+\mathcal{O}\big(d^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}m^{-1}(x)\big)
. Hence, the series expansion of
m(x+d)
around
d=0
is

\frac{m(x+d)}{m(x)}=1+d\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)m(x)+\mathcal{O}\big(d^{2}(\partial_{x}m^{-1})^{2}/m(x),d^{2}(\partial^{2}_{x}m^{-1})/m(x)\big).

(SE.40)

Expanding up to first order the equation
SE.39
we get

\theta^{f}=\theta+d\mathfrak{f}_{2}m(x)(\theta^{\prime}+\theta)+\mathcal{O}\big(d^{2}(\partial_{x}m^{-1})^{2}/m,d^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}m^{-1}/m\big).

(SE.41)

Thus, from the linearization
SE.41
of equation
SE.39
we can express the scattering kernel in momentum space as

T_{\theta}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\equiv\theta^{f}-\theta\simeq d\mathfrak{f}_{2}m(x)\frac{\theta^{\prime}+\theta}{2}.

(SE.42)

Finally, using the formula
SE.42
in the effective acceleration
SE.38
we get

\begin{split}a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)&=a^{\rm br}(\theta,x)+d\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\frac{\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}{1-d\bar{\rho}(x,t)}\bigg(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta^{\prime 2}}{2}\bigg)\\
&=\mathfrak{f}_{1}(x)+\mathfrak{f}_{2}(x)\frac{\theta^{2}/2-d\int{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\theta^{\prime 2}/2}{1-d\bar{\rho}(x,t)},\end{split}

(SE.43)

which is equal to the formula
SC.14
.

Now we impose the local conservation of mass on a fluid cell which is initially at time
t
in the interval
[(x,x+{\rm d}x),(\theta,\theta+{\rm d}\theta)]

C. Boldrighini and Sukhov 1983
.
After a time step
{\rm d}t
, the initial interval is transformed into
\big[\big(x+v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\,,x+{\rm d}x+v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\big),\big(\theta+a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\,,\theta+{\rm d}\theta+a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\big)\big]
.
Imposing the conservation of mass, we find the equation

\rho(\theta,x,t){\rm d}x{\rm d}\theta=\rho\big(\theta+a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\,,x+v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\,,t+{\rm d}t\big)\times\\
\times\big[{\rm d}x+v^{\rm{\rm eff}}(x+{\rm d}\theta,x,t){\rm d}t-v^{\rm{\rm eff}}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\big]\big[{\rm d}\theta+a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x+{\rm d}\theta,t){\rm d}t-a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t\big],

(SE.44)

where
v^{\rm eff}
and
a^{\rm eff}
are defined in eq.
SE.37
and
SE.43
.
Taking into consideration only first-order terms we get

\frac{\rho(\theta,x,t+{\rm d}t)-\rho(\theta,x,t)}{{\rm d}t}+v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)\frac{\rho(\theta,x+v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t,t)-\rho(\theta,x,t)}{v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t}+\\
+a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)\frac{\rho(\theta+a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t,x,t)-\rho(\theta,x,t)}{a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t){\rm d}t}+\\
+\rho(\theta,x,t)\bigg(\frac{v^{\rm eff}(\theta+{\rm d}\theta,x,t)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)}{{\rm d}x}+\frac{a^{\rm eff}(\theta+{\rm d}\theta,x,t)-a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)}{{\rm d}\theta}\bigg)=0.

(SE.45)

Hence, the equation
SE.45
is formally identical to the Euler GHD equation

\partial_{t}\rho(\theta,x,t)+\partial_{x}\big(v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)\rho(\theta,x,t)\big)+\partial_{\theta}\big(a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)\rho(\theta,x,t)\big)=0.

(SE.46)

E.2
Diffusive corrections to ballistic quasiparticle spreading

The purpose of this section is to give a kinetic interpretation of the diffusive terms of equation
SC.16
as diffusive corrections to ballistic particle spreading. In particular, we will generalize the argument presented in
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018b
in the case of a system with free particle Hamiltonian
SE.31
.

We consider the trajectory
(x(t),\theta(t))
of a single particle on the phase space. At the leading order, the particle moves ballistically with
(\dot{x}(t),\dot{\theta}(t))\simeq(v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x),a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x))
, where the physical interpretation of these relations is explained in appendix
E.1
. Since this ballistic spreading depends on the interaction with the other particles in the system, it is subjected to a diffusive broadening due to the fluctuations of the statistical ensemble
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018b
.

Our aim is to compute the variance of the fluctuations
\bm{R}\equiv(\delta x,\delta\theta)
of the quasiparticle’s trajectory in the phase space induced by
a fluctuation in the density of particles at
(\theta^{\prime},x)
. Namely, we want to compute

\langle\bm{R}\otimes\bm{R}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}\big|_{\theta^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\delta x^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}&\langle\delta x\delta\theta(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}\\
\langle\delta x\delta\theta(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}&\langle\delta\theta^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix},

(SE.47)

where we defined
\langle\bullet\rangle^{c}
as the connected part of the average over the GGE.
Let us first consider the space variance and express it in terms of density fluctuations

\langle\delta x^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=t^{2}\langle(\delta v^{\rm{eff}})^{2}\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=t^{2}\bigg(\frac{\delta v^{\rm{eff}}(\theta)}{\delta n(\theta^{\prime})}\bigg)^{2}\langle\delta n(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\rangle^{c},

(SE.48)

where
n(\theta,x,t)
denote the generalized Fermi factor, that for the Hard Rod system is
n(\theta,x,t)=\rho(\theta,x,t)/(1-\bar{\rho}(x,t)d)

Doyon 2020
.
The density fluctuations of a GGE state, computed over an interval of length
\Lambda
, are diagonal in position and momentum
Gopalakrishnan et al. 2018b
;
Fendley and Saleur 1996
and for the hard rod gas are equal to
Doyon and Spohn 2017b

\langle\delta n(\theta,x)n(\theta^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle^{c}\equiv\langle n(\theta,x)n(\theta^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle-\langle n(\theta,x)\rangle\langle n(\theta^{\prime},x^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\frac{n(\theta,x)}{\Lambda\big(1-a\bar{\rho}(x)\big)}.

(SE.49)

Since the collisions between quasiparticles with momenta
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
can happen only inside the light-cone defined by the velocity
v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})
, the fluctuations must be computed over the region of length
\Lambda=t|v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})|
.
The derivative of the effective velocity with respect to the generalized Fermi factor can be computed from equation
SC.11
and is equal to

\frac{\delta v^{\rm eff}(x,\theta,t)}{\delta n(x,\theta^{\prime},t)}=d\big(1-\bar{\rho}(x,t)d\big)\big(v^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\big).

(SE.50)

Hence, using the relations
SE.49
and
SE.50
inside equation
SE.48
we get

\langle\delta x^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=td^{2}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)|v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})|=t\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\bm{g}_{1,1}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})

(SE.51)

where
\bm{g}
is the matrix defined in the main text. Integrating over the fluctuations of all quasiparticles, we get the full variance of the quasiparticle trajectory,

\langle\delta x^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}=t\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\bm{g}_{1,1}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=t{\rm diag}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,1)}),

(SE.52)

where
{\rm diag}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,1)})
is the diagonal part of the
(1,1)
component of the diffusion matrix
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}
.

Now we take into consideration the variance of the trajectories in the momentum space. Using the same argument presented above, we can express it in terms of density fluctuations,

\langle\delta\theta^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=t^{2}\langle(\delta a^{\rm{eff}})^{2}\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=t^{2}\bigg(\frac{\delta a^{\rm{eff}}(\theta)}{\delta n(\theta^{\prime})}\bigg)^{2}\langle\delta n(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\rangle^{c}

(SE.53)

where the fluctuations
\langle\delta n(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\rangle^{c}
are evaluated again over an interval of length
\Lambda=|v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})|t
.
The derivative of the effective acceleration with respect to the generalized Fermi factor can be computed from equation
SC.12
as is

\frac{\delta a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)}{\delta n(\theta^{\prime},x,t)}=d\big(1-\bar{\rho}(x,t)d\big)\big(a^{\rm eff}(\theta,x,t)-a^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\big).

(SE.54)

Using the relations
SE.49
and
SE.54
in eq.
SE.53
we get

\langle\delta\theta^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=td^{2}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\frac{\big(a^{\rm eff}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})\big)^{2}}{|v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})|}=t\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\bm{g}_{2,2}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(SE.55)

Integrating the quantity computed in eq.
SE.55
over all the quasiparticles’ fluctuations, we get the full variance of the trajectory in momentum space

\langle\delta\theta^{2}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}=t\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\bm{g}_{2,2}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=t{\rm diag}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(2,2)}).

(SE.56)

Finally, we compute the transverse variance
\langle\delta x\delta\theta(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}
expressing it in terms of density fluctuations

\langle\delta x\delta\theta(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=t^{2}\langle\delta v^{\rm eff}(\theta)\delta a^{\rm eff}(\theta)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=t^{2}\bigg(\frac{\delta v^{\rm eff}(\theta)}{\delta n(\theta^{\prime})}\frac{\delta a^{\rm eff}(\theta)}{\delta n(\theta^{\prime})}\bigg)\langle\delta n(\theta^{\prime})^{2}\rangle^{c}.

(SE.57)

Using relations
SE.50
,
SE.54
and
SE.49
into equation
SE.57
we get

\langle\delta x\delta\theta(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}|_{\theta^{\prime}}=td^{2}\rho(\theta,x,t){\rm sgn}\big(v^{\rm eff}(\theta)-v^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\big(a^{\rm eff}(\theta)-a^{\rm eff}(\theta^{\prime})\big)=t\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\bm{g}_{1,2}(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

(SE.58)

Integrating equation
SE.58
over all possible momenta
\theta^{\prime}
we get the full variance of the trajectory in the transverse direction

\langle\delta x\delta\theta(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}=t\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}\theta^{\prime}\rho(\theta^{\prime},x,t)\bm{g}_{1,2}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=t{\rm diag}(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,2)}).

(SE.59)

Thus, from equations
SE.52
,
SE.56
and
SE.59
, we conclude that the quasiparticles’ trajectories broaden diffusively in phase space. Namely, the variance of a single particle’s probability distribution in phase space grows linearly in time with a coefficient equal to the diagonal part of the diffusion matrices
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}
previously defined
SC.17
:

\langle\bm{R}\otimes\bm{R}(\theta,x,t)\rangle^{c}=t\begin{pmatrix}{\rm diag}\big(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,1)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\big)&{\rm diag}\big(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\big)\\
{\rm diag}\big(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(1,2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\big)&{\rm diag}\big(\bm{\mathfrak{D}}_{[\rho]}^{(2,2)}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\big)\end{pmatrix}.

(SE.60)
