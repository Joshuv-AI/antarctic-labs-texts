The “Power of Few” phenomenon: the sparse case
BaoLinh Tran, Van Vu

Abstract
. The “majority dynamics” process on a social network begins with an initial phase, where the individuals are split into two competing parties, Red and Blue. Every day, everyone updates their affiliation to match the majority among those of their friends. While studying this process on Erdös-Rényi G ⁡ ( n , p ) G(n,p) random graph (with constant density), the authors discovered the “Power of Few” phenomenon, showing that a very small advantage to one side already guarantees that everybody will unanimously join that side after just a few days with overwhelming probability. For example, when p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 , then 10 extra members guarantee this unanimity with a 90% chance, regardless of the value of n n . In this paper, we study this phenomenon for sparse random graphs. It is clear that below the connectivity threshold, the phenomenon ceases to hold, as the isolated vertices never change their colors. We show that it holds for every density above the threshold. To make the process more realistic, we also assume that individuals can randomly activate their accounts to post their opinions and observe their neighbors (just as we do on social media). We prove that the phenomenon is robust under this assumption. To motivate further research, we also state a number of open questions with supportive numerical experiments.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Majority Dynamics

The study of processes on a social network where individuals form and change their opinions based on those around them (opinion dynamics) is an active research area with applications in various fields.
The 2017 survey
[
15
]
by Mossel and Tamuz discussed applications in Biology, Economics, Statistical Physics. More recent applications have been found in Computer Science, particularly in network security
[
2
,
1
,
20
,
11
,
14
]
.

In this paper, we focus on a process called
majority dynamics
, where each individual starts with a color (Red or Blue, representing different opinions), and updates their color each day to match the one the majority of their friends hold on the previous day.

Definition 1.1

(Majority Dynamics)
.

Given simple undirected graphs
G=(V,E)
, and a partition
V=R\cup B
, the
majority dynamics
on
G
with initial coloring
(R,B)
is a process that begins with Day 0, where vertices in
R
and
B
are respectively colored Red and Blue.
For each
t\geq 0
, on day
t+1
,
each vertex adopts the color the majority of its neighbors hold on day
t
, or remains unchanged in case of a tie.

1.2.
Unanimity and the “Power of Few” phenomenon

How the process terminates depends strongly on the structure of the network.
In this paper, we consider
Erdös-Rényi

G(n,p)
random graphs and focus on the question of
unanimity
: Given an initial coloring, what is the probability for each color to
win
, meaning it covers the whole graph at some point?

This natural question has been tackled in many recent works by various authors
[
5
,
10
,
20
,
6
,
17
,
8
]
.

Consider the balanced case, when both color classes begin with
n/2
vertices, then it is clear that the winning probabilities (of Red and Blue) are equal, by symmetry.
When an initial bias, or
gap

\Delta>0
, is introduced (say Red begins with
n/2+\Delta
vertices), the winning odds shift in favor of Red. But how fast?

In a previous paper, the authors discovered a surprising “Power of Few” phenomenon: when
p
is a constant,
\Delta
only needs to be a constant for Red to win within 4 days with probability arbitrarily close to
1

[
19
]
, regardless of the size
n
of the population. For instance, when
p=1/2
,
\Delta=5
already guarantees that Red wins with a probability of at least
.9

[
19
]
.
It is thus natural to ask: can we extend this phenomenon to smaller values of
p
?

It is clear that when
p
is below the connectivity threshold,
n^{-1}\log n
, the phenomenon fails, since there are, with probability
1-o(1)
, isolated vertices that never change their colors.
Under what assumption can we ensure that the threshold for connectivity is also the threshold for unanimity?

1.3.
Main result

Our first main theorem
establishes an assumption guaranteeing unanimity for every
p
above the connectivity threshold, resolving this question.

Theorem 1.2

(The Power of Few for sparse graphs)
.

Consider a set
V
of
n
vertices with an arbitrary partition
V=R\cup B
where
|R|=n/2+\Delta
, then draw a
G(n,p)
random graph over
V
.
If
n-10\geq pn\geq(1+\lambda)\log n
and
p\Delta\geq 10
, where
\lambda>0
is a constant, then majority dynamics on
G
with initial coloring
(R,B)
achieves a Red unanimity within time
O(\log_{pn}n)
with probability at least

1-O\left(\frac{1}{\min\{n,p\Delta^{2}\}}+n^{-\lambda/2}\right).

The key point here is that
\Delta
only depends directly on
p
and not
n
.
In the special case when
p
is a constant,
\Delta
needs only to be a constant for Red to win with probability arbitrarily close to
1
, thus this theorem extends the “Power of Few” phenomenon in
[
19
]
.

Notation for asymptotics

Throughout the paper, we write
a=O(b)
for two
quantities
a,b>0
that depend on the same set of variables, which includes the variable
m
,
if
a\leq Cb
for a universal constant
C
, and
a=O_{m}(b)
if

only depends on

We write
a=o_{m}(b)
if
a\leq c_{m}b
, where

only depends on
m
and
\lim_{m\to\infty}c_{m}=0
.
Note that the second asymptotics has to involve a variable that grows to
+\infty
, however, since
the order of the graph

plays this role most of the time, we often use
a=o(b)
in place of
a=o_{n}(b)
.
This semantics apply analogously for
a=\Omega(b)
,
a=\Omega_{m}(b)
,
a=\Theta(b)
,
a=\Theta_{m}(b)
,
a=\omega_{m}(b)
and

.

Remark 1.3
.

Note that, while we assume the initial coloring is fixed before the graph is drawn, we can study the process in the following equivalent models:

Model 1.
First draw a
G(n,p)
random graph over
V
, then uniformly sample a
(n/2+\Delta)
-subset of
V
to color Red and color the rest Blue.

Model 2.
(For even

) First fix an arbitrary even partition

of

, namely one such that

,
then draw a

\Delta
-subset of “defectors” from

.

Model 3.
(For even

) First draw a

random graph over

, then uniformly sample an even partition

of V and color them accordingly, finally uniformly sample a
\Delta
-subset of “defectors” from

and move them to

.

In the next three subsections, we will discuss the applications of Theorem
1.2
in proving and strengthening some recent results on unanimity, its generalization to a more realistic setting, and some open questions and conjectures.

1.4.
Relations with the random coloring scheme

Another process considered in the literature
involves coloring every vertex of a random graph Red or Blue independently with probability
1/2
each
[
5
,
10
,
8
]
, and then run the majority dynamics.
We refer to this as the
random
1/2
coloring scheme
.
Benjamini, Chan, O’Donnell, Tamuz and Tan
[
5
]
proved that in this coloring scheme, the side with the initial majority wins with probability at least
.4
when
p\geq\lambda n^{-1/2}
, where
\lambda
is a sufficiently large constant.
Fountoulakis, Kang and Makai
[
10
]
improved the winning probability to
1-{\varepsilon}
for any constant
{\varepsilon}>0
, provided that
\lambda=\lambda_{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow{{\varepsilon}\to 0}+\infty
, and unanimity is achieved only in 4 days.

We can use Theorem
1.2
to give a quick proof for this result, with some “delay”.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any fixed
{\varepsilon}>0
, there is a constant
\lambda>0
such that: in majority dynamics on a
G(n,p)
random graph with
p\geq\lambda n^{-1/2}
and a random
1/2
initial coloring, the larger side wins in
O(1)
days with probability at least
1-{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
R
and
B
respectively be the Red and Blue camps after the initial coloring and
\Delta
the initial gap.
The quantity
2\Delta=||R|-|B||
is a sum of independent Rademacher variables, with mean
0
and variance
n
, so by the central limit theorem (see Corollary
A.2
), for any
{\varepsilon}>0
,
\Delta\geq C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{n}
for some constant
C_{\varepsilon}>0
with probability at least
1-{\varepsilon}/2
.
Conditioned on a fixed instance of
(R,B)
satisfying this, Therem
1.2
implies that if
p\geq 10\Delta^{-1}
and
p\Delta^{2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\infty
, the majority side wins in
O(\log_{pn}n)
days with probability
1-o(1)
.
Both conditions are satisfied when
p\geq(10/C_{\varepsilon})n^{-1/2}
. The winning time is
O(\log_{pn}n)=O(1)
, so by choosing
\lambda_{\varepsilon}=10/C_{\varepsilon}
we complete the proof.
∎

A careful inspection of Theorem
1.2
’s proof reveals that the winning time can be taken to be at most 5 days, which is very close to the original theorem in
[
10
]
.

If one considers a more general
random
(p_{r},p_{b})
coloring scheme
, where the probability for a vertex to be Red is
p_{r}
and Blue
p_{b}=1-p_{r}
, then the initial gap is larger for which unanimity can be proven for lower
p
. Zehmakan in 2019
[
20
]
reached the threshold
p=(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
for such a biased random coloring, proving that Red wins with probability
1-o(1)
if
p_{r}=1/2+\omega((pn)^{-1/2})
, i.e. Red has a bias
\omega((pn)^{-1/2})
. Our theorem
1.2
implies that a smaller bias

\omega((pn)^{-1})
suffices.

Theorem 1.5
.

On a
G(n,p)
random graph where
p\geq(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
for a constant
\lambda>0
, majority dynamics with a random
(p_{r},p_{b})
initial coloring with
p_{r}=1/2+\omega((pn)^{-1})
terminates with a Red unanimity with probability
1-o(1)
.

By the Central Limit Theorem or Chernoff bound, it is easy to see that with probability
1-o(1)
,
|R|>|B|
and the initial gap
\Delta=(|R|-|B|)/2
is
\omega(1/p)
.
Conditioned on a fixed instance of
(R,B)
satisfying this, we have
p\Delta>10
and
p\Delta^{2}=\omega(1/p)=\omega(1)
, so we deduce by Theorem
1.2
that Red wins with probability
1-O((p\Delta^{2})^{-1})=1-o(1)
, proving the theorem.

1.5.
“Power of Few” with random activations and updates

In real-life situations, not everyone on a social network activates or updates their account on a daily basis. To make the model more realistic, we introduce random
activation rate

p_{\textup{ac}}
and
updating rate

p_{\textup{up}}
.

Definition 1.6

(Majority Dynamics with random activations and updates)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
,
majority dynamics with activation rate
p_{\textup{ac}}
and updating rate
p_{\textup{up}}
on
G
is a process that works as follows. Beginning with Day 0, the vertices are colored either Red or Blue. For each
t\geq 0
, on day
t+1
, the following takes place:

\bullet

Each vertex activates with probability
p_{\textup{ac}}
, independently.
Neighboring vertices that are both active observe each other’s color on day
t
.
Inactive vertices neither observe nor are observed by anyone.

\bullet

Each active vertices then chooses to update its status with probability
p_{\textup{up}}
, again independently.
Inactive vertices do not update by default.

\bullet

Each updating vertex adopts the majority among the colors it observed or retains its day
t
color in case of a tie.

It is clear that in this more general setting, unanimity is still permanent once it occurs.
We show that the “Power of Few” phenomenon remains robust here.

Theorem 1.7

(The Power of Few with random activations and updates)
.

Consider the majority dynamics process with fixed activation rate
p_{\textup{ac}}
and updating rate
p_{\textup{up}}
on a
G(n,p)
random graph, with
n/2+\Delta
initial Red vertices.
Assume
p=p(n)
satisfies
pp_{\textup{ac}}n\geq(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
for a constant
\lambda>0
.
Then there is a constant
C
depending only on
p_{\textup{ac}}
and
p_{\textup{up}}
such that when
p\Delta\geq C
, a Red unanimity is reached within time
O_{p_{\textup{ac}},p_{\textup{up}}}(\log n)
with probability at least

1-O\left(\frac{1}{\min\{n,p\Delta^{2}\}}\right)-O(n^{-\lambda/4})-n^{-\Omega_{p_{\textup{ac}},p_{\textup{up}}}(1)}.

Remark 1.8
.

Despite seeming more general, Theorem
1.7
does not directly imply
1.2
, due to different convergent times.

If we let
p_{\textup{up}}=p_{\textup{ac}}=1
in Theorem
1.7
, then the setting is identical to Theorem
1.2
’s. However, the former only implies convergence to unanimity in
O(\log n)
days, while the latter gives a shorter time
O(\log_{pn}(n))=o(\log n)
.
In particular, when
pn\geq n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, Theorem
1.2
shows unanimity in
O(1/\alpha)=O(1)
days, while the convergence time in Theorem
1.7
remains stuck at
O(\log n)
.

1.6.1.
“Optimal” condition for unanimity

The most interesting open question here is to determine the best value of “Few”:

Question 1.9
.

Given a density

(Optimal Power of Few)

There is a function

This conjecture is related to a question concerning the random

What is the smallest

n/2-\Omega(n)
after the second day.

(3)

With probability at least
1-O(n^{-\lambda/2})
,
G
has “nice” properties that allow Blue to shrink repeatedly until no Blue nodes are left.

We begin with Step 1 in the next section.

2.2.
Step 1 - First day

Below is the main technical result of this section.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
0<c\leq\min\bigl\{p\Delta,\sqrt{p(1-p)n}\bigr\}
and
D\in\mathbb{R}
define

(2)

D_{c}:=\Phi_{0}\left(2-\frac{1}{c}\right)-\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{c}\quad\text{ and }\quad C_{\ref{lem:day1}}(c,D):=\frac{7}{12\left(D_{c}-D\right)^{2}}.

For every
0<D<D_{c}
, if
|B_{0}|\leq n/2-\Delta
then

{\mathbf{P}}\left(|B_{1}|\leq\frac{n}{2}-D\min\{n,\Delta\sqrt{pn}\}\right)\geq 1-\frac{C_{\ref{lem:day1}}(c,D)}{\min\{n,p\Delta^{2}\}}.

As mentioned in the overview, we can prove Lemma
2.1
by combining Chebyshev’s inequality with the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

For each
0<c\leq\min\{p\Delta,\sqrt{p(1-p)n}\}
we have

\mathbf{E}\left[|B_{1}|\right]\leq n/2-D_{c}\min\{n,\Delta\sqrt{pn}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{Var}\left[|B_{1}|\right]\leq 7n/12.

Assume, for now, Proposition
2.2
.
Let
m:=\min\{n,\Delta\sqrt{pn}\}
.
For any
D<D_{c}
,
Chebyshev’s inequality implies

(3)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{P}}\left(|B_{1}|\geq\frac{n}{2}-Dm\right)\leq{\mathbf{P}}\left(|B_{1}|\geq\mathbf{E}\left[|B_{1}|\right]+(D_{c}-D)m\right)\leq\frac{\mathbf{Var}\left[|B_{1}|\right]}{(D_{c}-D)^{2}m^{2}}.

The right-most expression can be simplified to
\frac{C_{\ref{lem:day1}}(c,D)}{\min\{n,p\Delta^{2}\}}
, giving Lemma
2.1
.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Write
|B_{1}|=\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)
.
We will bound
|B_{1}|
using Chebyshev’s inequality.
We proceed by bounding
\mathbf{E}\left[|B_{1}|\right]
and
\mathbf{Var}\left[|B_{1}|\right]
.

Step 1. Bounding the expectation.

We have

(4)

\mathbf{E}\left[|B_{1}|\right]=\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]\leq\sum_{v\in V}{\mathbf{P}}(\delta_{0}(v)\leq 0).

Recall that by Eq.
1
,

\delta_{0}(v)=\sum_{u\in V}\ell_{0}(v)\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}
,
where
\{\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}\}_{u\in V}
are i.i.d.
\operatorname{Ber}(p)
variables.
By Gaussian approximation (Corollary
A.2
in Appendix
A
), we have

{\mathbf{P}}(\delta_{0}(v)\leq 0)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\Phi_{0}\left(\frac{\mathbf{E}\left[\delta_{0}(v)\right]}{\sqrt{\mathbf{Var}\left[\delta_{0}(v)\right]}}\right)+\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{\sqrt{\mathbf{Var}\left[\delta_{0}(v)\right]}}.

Calculations show

\mathbf{E}\left[\delta_{0}(v)\right]\geq p(2\Delta-1)
and
\mathbf{Var}\left[\delta_{0}(v)\right]=p(1-p)(n-1)
.
Using the inequality
\Phi_{0}(x)\geq\Phi_{0}(y)\min\{x/y,1\}
, we have for arbitrary
y>0
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}-\min\left\{1,\frac{p(2\Delta-1)}{y\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}\right\}\Phi_{0}(y)+\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\min\left\{\Phi_{0}(y)-\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}},\ \frac{2\frac{\Phi_{0}(y)}{y}p\Delta-\bigl(p\frac{\Phi_{0}(y)}{y}+C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}\bigr)}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}\right\}.

Using the fact

c\leq\min\{p\Delta,\sqrt{p(1-p)n}\}
,
the RHS can be bounded further:

\Phi_{0}(y)-\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}\geq\Phi_{0}(y)-\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{c},

and

\frac{2\frac{\Phi_{0}(y)}{y}p\Delta-\bigl(p\frac{\Phi_{0}(y)}{y}+C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}\bigr)}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}\geq\left(2\frac{\Phi_{0}(y)}{y}-\frac{\Phi_{0}(y)/y+C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{c}\right)\frac{p\Delta}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}

Now let
y=2-c^{-1}
so that the RHSs of the above two equations become
D_{c}
and
D_{c}\frac{p\Delta}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}
respectively.
We have

\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}-D_{c}\min\left\{1,\frac{p\Delta}{\sqrt{p(1-p)n}}\right\}\leq\frac{1}{2}-D_{c}\min\left\{1,\frac{\Delta\sqrt{p}}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}

Using Eq.
4
, we get the desired bound on
\mathbf{E}\left[|B_{1}|\right]
.

Step 2. Upper-bounding the variance.

Firstly write

\mathbf{Var}\left[|B_{1}|\right]=\sum_{v}\mathbf{Var}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]+2\sum_{u<v}\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(u),\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right].

Now that
\mathbf{Var}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right](1-\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right])\leq 1/4
for each
v
, we are left to bound
\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(u),\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]
for each pair
u<v
.
Let us write

(5)

\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(u),\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]={\mathbf{P}}(u,v\in B_{1})-{\mathbf{P}}(u\in B_{1}){\mathbf{P}}(v\in B_{1}).

We can break up these three events into independent ones as follows.

(6)

{\mathbf{P}}\bigl(u,v\in B_{1}\bigr)=p{\mathbf{P}}\bigl(u,v\in B_{1}\mid u\sim v\bigr)+(1-p){\mathbf{P}}\bigl(u,v\in B_{1}\mid u\not\sim v\bigr).

Let
F_{u}:=\bigl\{u\in B_{1}\big|u\sim v\bigr\}
and
similarly
for
F_{v}
.

F_{u}
and
F_{v}
are independent since
u
and
v
have no influence over the other’s neighbors in
V\setminus\{u,v\}
.

The second component in the RHS of Eq.
6
is thus

{\mathbf{P}}(F_{u}){\mathbf{P}}(F_{v})
.
By the same reasoning, let
E_{u}:=\bigl\{u\in B_{1}\big|u\not\sim v\bigr\}
and
similarly
for
E_{v}
, then
E_{u}
and
E_{v}
are independent and the first component in the RHS of Eq.
6
is
{\mathbf{P}}(E_{u}){\mathbf{P}}(E_{v})
.

The probabilities
{\mathbf{P}}(u\in B_{1})
and
{\mathbf{P}}(v\in B_{1})
can be broken down easily.
We have
{\mathbf{P}}(u\in B_{1})=p{\mathbf{P}}(E_{u})+(1-p){\mathbf{P}}(F_{u})
and
analogously
for
{\mathbf{P}}(v\in B_{1})
.
Substituting these back into
6
and into
5
gives, after some algebraic manipulations:

\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(u),\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]=p\left(1-p\right)\left({\mathbf{P}}(E_{u})-{\mathbf{P}}(F_{u})\right)\left({\mathbf{P}}(E_{v})-{\mathbf{P}}(F_{v})\right).

The term
{\mathbf{P}}(E_{v})-{\mathbf{P}}(F_{v})
can be handled by looking at their raw meanings.
Letting

X=\delta_{0}(v)-\ell_{0}(u)W_{uv}+\mathbf{1}_{B_{0}}(v),

we see that
E_{v}\Leftrightarrow X\leq 0
and
F_{v}\Leftrightarrow X\geq-\ell_{0}(u)
, so

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(E_{v})-{\mathbf{P}}(F_{v})={\mathbf{P}}(X\leq 0)-{\mathbf{P}}(X+\ell_{0}(u)\leq 0)=\ell_{0}(u){\mathbf{P}}\left(X=\frac{\ell_{0}(u)-1}{2}\right),

where the last equality can be reached by case analysis on
u
’s color.
By Lemma
A.3
in Appendix
A
,

{\mathbf{P}}(X=\ell_{0}(u)-1)\leq\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}}{\sqrt{p(1-p)(n-2)}}.

We get the same inequality for
u
, therefore

\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(u),\mathbf{1}_{B_{1}}(v)\right]\leq\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}^{2}}{n-2}<\frac{1}{3n}.

Finally we get:

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{Var}\left[|B_{1}|\right]<\sum_{v}\frac{1}{4}+\sum_{u<v}\frac{1}{3n}<\frac{n}{4}+\frac{n}{3}=\frac{7n}{12}.

∎

The proof of Lemma
2.1
is now complete and we consider Step 1 done.

2.3.
Step 2 - Second day

We first introduce one of the two main technical lemmas of this step (the other one being Lemma
2.5
).
It shows that if the gap is at least
C\sqrt{n}
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, Blue will shrink with a factor less than one on the next day, regardless of which vertices are Blue today.

Lemma 2.3
.

For fixed
x,y\in(0,1/2)
and
d>0
, define

(7)

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle:=x\log x+(1-x)\log(1-x),

(8)

\displaystyle F(d,x,y)

\displaystyle:=dx\left(1-\frac{1}{2}y-\frac{3}{2}y^{1/3}(1-y)^{2/3}\right)+2h(x).

Then for any
0\leq b_{2}\leq b_{1}\leq 1/2-7/n
,

G
is such that for any coloring with at most
b_{1}n
Blue nodes, there are at most
b_{2}n
nodes that can turn Blue the next day,
except with probability at most
b_{2}^{-1}\exp\bigl[-nF(pn,b_{2},b_{1})\bigr]
.

Proof.

Fix two subsets
B\in\binom{V}{\lfloor xn\rfloor}
and
S\in\binom{V}{\lfloor yn\rfloor+1}
.
Since the Blue camp on the next day is fully determined from the current Blue camp and the graph
G
, we can let
\mathcal{E}(B,S)
be the event that
S
is contained in the Blue camp on the next day if
B
is the Blue camp today.
Note that this is an event on the space of all simple, undirected graphs over
n
vertices, endowed with the
G(n,p)
measure.
Let
\delta(S):=\sum_{v\in S}\delta(v)
, which is a random variable on the same space, and notice that
\mathcal{E}(B,S)
implies
\delta(S)\leq 0
.
We apply a Chernoff argument: for any
t>0
,

{\mathbf{P}}(\mathcal{E}(B,S))\leq{\mathbf{P}}(\delta(S)\leq 0)\leq{\mathbf{P}}(e^{-t\delta(S)}\geq 1)\leq\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-t\delta(S)}\right].

To proceed, we need to split
\delta(S)
into independent random variables.

\displaystyle\delta(S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}\Bigl[\chi_{S}(v)\sum_{u\in V}\ell(u)\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}\Bigr]=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset V}\Bigl[\ell(u)\chi_{S}(v)+\ell(v)\chi_{S}(u)\Bigr]\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset V}X(u,v)\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v},\quad\quad\text{ for }X(u,v):=\ell(u)\chi_{S}(v)+\ell(v)\chi_{S}(u).

We have

(9)

\displaystyle\log\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-t\delta(S)}\right]=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset V}\log\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-tX(u,v)\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset V}\log\Bigl(1-p+p\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-tX(u,v)}\right]\Bigr)\leq p\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset V}\Bigl(\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-tX(u,v)}\right]-1\Bigr).

Table
1
sums up respective values for
X(u,v)
when
u
and
v
vary among the four sets:

R\cap S
,
R\cap S^{c}
,
B\cap S
,
B\cap S^{c}
(where
R=B^{c}
is the set of Red nodes).

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c}u\setminus v&R\cap S&B\cap S&R\cap S^{c}&B\cap S^{c}\\
\hline\cr R\cap S&2&0&\hbox{\multirowsetup$1$}&\hbox{\multirowsetup$-1$}\\

\hline\cr R\cap S^{c}&\vrule\lx@intercol\hfil 1\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol&\vrule\lx@intercol\hfil\hbox{\multirowsetup 0}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\cline{1-3}\cr B\cap S^{c}&\vrule\lx@intercol\hfil-1\hfil\lx@intercol\vrule\lx@intercol&\vrule\hfil\hfil\lx@intercol\end{array}

Table 1.
Values of
X(u,v)

Using this table, we get

(10)

\displaystyle\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset V}\Bigl(\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-tX(u,v)}\right]-1\Bigr)=

\displaystyle\binom{|S\cap R|}{2}\left(e^{-2t}-1\right)+\binom{|S\cap B|}{2}\left(e^{2t}-1\right)

\displaystyle+|S||R\cap S^{c}|(e^{-t}-1)+|S||B\cap S^{c}|(e^{t}-1)

We aim to find a simple upper bound for this expression with an appropriate
t
.
It can be viewed as
(1-e^{-t})f(|S\cap R|)
, where

f(x)=\binom{|S|-x}{2}e^{t}\left(e^{t}+1\right)-\binom{x}{2}\left(e^{-t}+1\right)-|S|(|R|-x)+|S|(|B|-|S|+x)e^{t},

is a quadratic polynomial of
x
, with quadratic coefficient

\frac{1}{2}(e^{t}-e^{-t})(e^{t}+1)>0
.
Thus
f
is convex and
f(x)\leq\max\{f(0),f(|S|)\}
for
x\in[0,|S|]
.
Let

T=(1/2)|S|n\left(x\left(e^{2t}+e^{t}+2\right)-2\right),

we prove
\max\{f(0),f(|S|)\}\leq T
for an appropriately chosen
t
.
Replacing
|B|
with
\lfloor xn\rfloor
and
|S|
with
\lfloor yn\rfloor+1
, we have after a few computations,

T-f(0)=|S|\Bigr(e^{t}+(xn-\lfloor xn\rfloor)+(xn-\lfloor yn\rfloor)e^{t}(e^{t}-1)/2\Bigr)>0\ \text{ for all }t>0,

On the other hand,

\displaystyle T-f(|S|)

\displaystyle=|S|\Bigl((xne^{t}-\lfloor yn\rfloor e^{-t})(e^{t}-1)/2+(xn-\lfloor xn\rfloor)(e^{t}+1)-1\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}|S|(xn(e^{t}-1)^{2}(1+e^{-t})-2)\geq\frac{1}{2}|S|(xn(e^{t}-1)^{2}-2),

which is not always positive. Let us call a
t
“nice” if
xn(e^{t}-1)^{2}\geq 2
.
Assuming we have a nice
t
, then by Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
),

\log\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-t\delta(S)}\right]\leq pn|S|g_{x}(t),\ \text{ where }g_{x}(t):=(1-e^{-t})\left(x(e^{2t}+e^{t}+2)/2-1\right)

If we were to choose
t
freely, the best choice would be
t_{x}=\log(1/x-1)/3
, which can be shown easily to minimize
g_{x}(t)
.
Fortunately, we can show that this
t_{x}
is nice.
Using the fact

(y-1)(y^{2}+y+1)=y^{3}-1\quad\text{ for all }y,

specifically at
y=e^{t_{x}}
, we have

\displaystyle xn(e^{t_{x}}-1)^{2}\geq 2

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow xn(e^{3t_{x}}-1)^{2}\geq 2(e^{2t_{x}}+e^{t_{x}}+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow n(1-2x)^{2}\geq 2x(e^{2t_{x}}+e^{t_{x}}+1)^{2},

where the last inequality is obtained by plugging the definition of
t_{x}
into the left-hand side.
Now consider two cases:
If
x<1/9
, we have
e^{2t_{x}}+e^{t_{x}}+1\leq 3e^{2t_{x}}<3x^{-2/3}
,
and
1-2x>7/9
, so it suffices to show that

n(7/9)^{2}\geq 18x^{-1/3}
, or
xn^{3}\geq(1458/49)^{3}
, which holds when
x\geq 1/n
and
n
is large enough.

If
x\geq 1/9
,
e^{t_{x}}\leq 2
, and
2x\leq 1
so it suffices to show that

n(1-2x)^{2}\geq 7
.
By hypothesis,
x\leq 1/2-2n^{-1/2}
so
1-2x\geq 4n^{-1/2}
, thus
n(1-2x)^{2}\geq 16>7
.

Therefore we can use
t_{x}=\log(1/x-1)/3
and obtain the bound

(11)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(\mathcal{E}(B,S))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-t\delta(S)}\right]\leq e^{-pn|S|g(t_{x})}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-pn^{2}y\left(1-\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{3}{2}x^{1/3}(1-x)^{2/3}\right)\right].

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the event that for any choice of at most
xn
Blue nodes, there are at most
yn
Blue nodes the next day.

\mathcal{G}^{c}
is the event that there are
B\in\binom{V}{\lfloor xn\rfloor}
and
S\in\binom{V}{\lfloor yn\rfloor+1}
satisfying
\mathcal{E}(B,S)
.
By a double union bound,
{\mathbf{P}}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\leq\binom{n}{\lfloor xn\rfloor}\binom{n}{\lfloor yn\rfloor+1}{\mathbf{P}}(\mathcal{E}(B,S))
.
A routine Stirling bound shows that with
h(x)=x\log x+(1-x)\log(1-x)
,
\binom{n}{k}\leq e^{-nh(k/n)}
.
Therefore we have

(12)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\lfloor xn\rfloor}\binom{n}{\lfloor yn\rfloor+1}\leq\exp\left[-n\left(h\Bigl(\frac{\lfloor xn\rfloor}{n}\Bigr)+h\Bigl(\frac{\lfloor yn\rfloor+1}{n}\Bigr)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-n\left(h(x)+h\Bigl(y+\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right)\right].\quad(h\text{ is decreasing on }\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right))

Since
h
is convex,
h(y+{\varepsilon})\geq h(y)+{\varepsilon}h^{\prime}(y)
, so

(13)

\displaystyle\exp\left[-n\left(h(x)+h\Bigl(y+\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-n\left(h(x)+h(y)+\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{y}{1-y}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-y}{y}e^{-n(h(x)+h(y))}\leq\frac{1}{y}e^{-2nh(x)}.

Combining Eqs. (
11
), (
12
) and (
13
), we have

{\mathbf{P}}(\mathcal{G}^{c})\leq\frac{1}{y}\exp\left[-pn^{2}y\left(1-\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{3}{2}x^{1/3}(1-x)^{2/3}\right)-2nh(x)\right].

The right-hand side is precisely
(1/y)\exp\bigl[-nF(pn,y,x)\bigr]
, as desired.
∎

Assuming Lemma
2.3
, the following holds.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
a>0
and
b\in(0,1/2)
satisfying:

(14)

ba^{2}>3(\log 2)/2,

if
|B_{1}|\leq\frac{n}{2}-\frac{an}{\sqrt{pn}}
,
then
|B_{2}|\leq bn
with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
.

Proof.

Let
b_{1}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{a}{\sqrt{pn}}
.
Conditioned on
|B_{1}|\leq b_{1}n
, Lemma
2.3
implies

{\mathbf{P}}\left(|B_{2}|\leq bn\right)\geq 1-b^{-1}\exp\bigl[-nF(pn,b,b_{1})\bigr].

We want to show
F(pn,b,b_{1})=\Omega(1)
, where by Eq.
8
, we have

F(pn,b,b_{1})=pnb\left(1-\frac{1}{2}b_{1}-\frac{3}{2}b_{1}^{1/3}(1-b_{1})^{2/3}\right)+2h(b_{1}).

A routine Taylor expansion around
1/2
shows that

(15)

1-\frac{1}{2}b_{1}-\frac{3}{2}b_{1}^{1/3}(1-b_{1})^{2/3}\geq\frac{1}{3}(1-2b_{1})^{2}=\frac{4a^{2}}{3pn},\ \text{ and }\ h(b_{1})\geq-\log 2.

Thus

F(pn,b,b_{1})\geq\frac{4}{3}a^{2}b-2\log 2=\Omega(1)
due to (
14
).
The proof is complete.
∎

2.4.
Step 3 - Third Day onwards

Note that although Lemma
2.3
can be used to prove Blue repeatedly shrinks until it vanishes,
some calculations show that the minimum
p
for which this argument works is
2n^{-1}\log n
.
We use a different argument in this section specifically to include the case
p=(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
, which is also the smallest possible threshold.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
A_{G}
denote the adjacency matrix of
G
.
For any choice of
B_{t}
and any
1/2>b\geq|B_{t}|/n
, we have either
B_{t+1}=\varnothing
or

|B_{t+1}|\Bigl(\frac{1}{|B_{t+1}|}\sum_{v\in B_{t+1}}d(v)\Bigr)^{2}\leq\inf_{x}\|A_{G}-x\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\|_{op}^{2}\frac{1-b}{(1/2-b)^{2}}|B_{t}|,

where
\|\cdot\|_{op}
denotes the matrix operator norm.

This lemma works best when
|B_{t}|\leq bn
for a constant
b<1/2
, hence the need for the argument in Step 2.
The full proof is presented in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Consider the majority dynamics process on
G\sim G(n,p)
where
pn\geq(1+\lambda)\log n
.
Assume that
|B_{2}|\leq bn
for some constant
b<1/2
.
Then with probability at least
1-2n^{-\lambda/2}
, there is a time
t^{*}=O(\log_{pn}n)
such that
|B_{t^{*}}|=\varnothing
.

Proof.

Lemma
A.7
implies that with probability at least
1-n^{-\lambda}
,

(16)

\|A_{G}-p\mathbf{1}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{n}^{T}\|_{op}\leq\|A_{G}-\mathbf{E}\left[A_{G}\right]\|_{op}+\|pI_{n}\|_{op}<(C+1)\sqrt{pn},

for the constant
C=C_{\lambda+1,\lambda}
from Lemma
A.7
.
Lemma
A.4
implies that for
\alpha=\alpha_{\lambda}
in Lemma
A.4
, with probabiliy at least
1-n^{-\lambda/2}
,

(17)

\min{\{d(v):v\in V\}}\geq\alpha pn.

Thus with probability
1-2n^{-\lambda/2}
, we can assume
G
satisfies Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
).
The latter also implies the average degree in
B_{t+1}
is at least
\alpha pn
.
Assuming
|B_{t}|\leq bn
, Lemma
2.5
implies that

|B_{t+1}|(\alpha pn)^{2}\leq(C+1)^{2}pn\cdot\frac{1-b}{(1/2-b)^{2}}|B_{t}|,

or, equivalently,

|B_{t+1}|\leq\frac{A}{pn}|B_{t}|\quad\text{ where }A=\frac{(C+1)^{2}(1-b)}{\alpha^{2}(1/2-b)^{2}}.

By choosing
n
large enough so that
pn\geq\log n>A
, we also have
|B_{t+1}|<bn
.
This ensures the inequality above holds for
t+1
and so on.
After
\lceil\log_{pn/A}{bn}\rceil=O(\log_{pn}n)
days, Blue has less than
1
vertex and thus is empty.
∎

2.5.
Wrapping up the proof of Theorem
1.2

Consider the majority dynamics process on
G\sim G(n,p)
, where

3.
Some Results with Sub-connectivity Density

In this section, we state and prove some results about Majority Dynamics on a
G(n,p)
random graph for
p<n^{-1}\log n
, using techniques developed in the case
p\geq(1+\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
.
Note that when
p<(1-\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
for any constant
\lambda>0
, the number of isolated vertices in
B_{0}
has expectation

|B_{0}|(1-p)^{n-1}=(1\pm O(p^{2}))|B_{0}|e^{-pn}=\Theta(|B_{0}|n^{\lambda-1}),

and variance

2\binom{|B_{0}|}{2}p(1-p)^{2n-3}+|B_{0}|(1-p)^{n-1}-|B_{0}|(1-p)^{2n-2}=\Theta(|B_{0}|n^{\lambda-1}).

Both quantities are
\Theta(n^{\lambda})
for any setting with
|B_{0}|=\Omega(n)
, so there are isolated vertices in
B_{0}
with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
by a simple Chebyshev bound.
Clearly the same applies for
R_{0}
, so unanimity is unreachable with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
when the density
p
is below the connectivity threshold.
However, with a fixed initial gap
\Delta
satisfying
p\Delta\geq 10
, we show that the number of Blue vertices at the end is at most a small fraction of
n
, which decreases as
p
increases, with high probability.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given any constant
0<\lambda<1
, consider Majority Dynamics on a
G(n,p)
random graph with
p=(1-\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
and a fixed initial gap
\Delta
such that
p\Delta\geq 10
.
With probability at least

1-O\left(\frac{1}{\min\{n,p\Delta^{2}\}}+n^{-(1-\lambda)}\right),

there is some

such that

or
B_{t}=B_{t^{*}+1}
for all

|B_{t^{*}+1}|\leq|B_{t^{*}}|\leq(\log n)^{O_{\lambda}(\log^{1/2}n)}n^{\lambda},

and the average degree of vertices in

is
O_{\lambda}(\sqrt{pn})
.

Proof.

Since we only use the assumption that

in the proof of Theorem
1.2
at Step 3 (Section

), in particular, to show that the minimum degree in

is
\Omega(pn)
with high probability, every other argument still works.
The arguments in Steps 1 and 2 imply that

{\mathbf{P}}\left(|B_{2}|\leq.25n\right)\geq 1-O\left(\frac{1}{\min\{n,p\Delta^{2}\}}\right).

Assuming
|B_{2}|\leq.25n
, the first half of the proof of Lemma
2.6
gives for all
t\geq 3
,
Assuming this, we have, with probability at least
1-n^{-(1-\lambda)}
,

has a property that implies for all

,

(20)

|B_{t+1}|\Bigl(\frac{1}{|B_{t+1}|}\sum_{v\in B_{t+1}}d(v)\Bigr)^{2}\leq M_{\lambda}pn|B_{t}|,

for some constant
M_{\lambda}
depending only on
\lambda
.
Recall that the next step in Lemma

is to use the fact that the average degree in
B_{t+1}
is at least the minimum degree in
G
and is thus
\Omega(pn)
with high probability (Lemma

until Red wins.
When

states that if we can choose

Plugging this bound into Eq. (

To satisfy the condition

∎

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem

is optimal up to a factor of

o(pn)

If

contains mostly centers of flowers, while

contains mostly leaves, the average degree in

\Omega(pn)
.

Appendix A
Some Preliminary Results in Probability

We provide some supporting results that are used throughout the proof of the main theorems in
Sections
2
and
4
.

Berry-Esseen theorem in its classical form establishes an explicit bound for the convergence rate of sums of random variables to the normal distribution.

Theorem A.1

(Berry-Esseen)
.

There is a universal constant
C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},\cdots,X_{n}
are random variables with zero means, variances
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{n}>0
, and absolute third moments
\mathbf{E}\left[|X_{i}|^{3}\right]=\rho_{i}<\infty
, we have:

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|{\mathbf{P}}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{\sigma_{X}}\leq x\right)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)^{-3/2}

The original proof by Esseen
[
9
]
yielded
C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}=7.59
, and this constant has been improved a number of times. The latest work by Shevtsova
[
18
, Theorem 1]
achieved
C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}=.56
, which
is used throughout this
paper.
We will be interested in the setting where
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are r.v.s taking values in either
\{0,1\}
or
\{0,-1\}
. The following corollary and lemmas naturally follow from this theorem.

Corollary A.2
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}
be Bernoulli random variables such that for all
i
, either

X_{i}\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p)
or
X_{i}\sim-\operatorname{Ber}(p)
. Let

X=X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n}
and
\mu_{X}=\mathbf{E}\left[X\right]
.
Then,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|{\mathbf{P}}\left(X-\mu_{X}\leq x\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\textup{BE}}(1-2\sigma^{2})}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\quad\text{ where }\sigma=\sqrt{p(1-p)}.

This implies the following lemma, which bounds the probability that two binomial variables are
exactly
some units apart.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
be a constant and
\sigma=\sqrt{p(1-p)}
,
X_{1}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{1},p)
and
X_{2}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n_{2},p)
be independent
random variables defined on the same probability space.
Then for any positive integer
d<(n_{1}+n_{2})/2
,

{\mathbf{P}}\left(X_{1}=X_{2}+d\right)\leq\frac{1.12\left(1-2\sigma^{2}\right)}{\sigma\sqrt{n_{1}+n_{2}}}.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
{\varepsilon}
be an arbitrary positive constant,
N_{\varepsilon}=4/{\varepsilon}+4
,
and
\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\exp(-(8/{\varepsilon}+7))
.
Then with probability at least
1-n^{-{\varepsilon}/2}
, a
G(n,p)
random graph with
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}
and
pn\geq(1+{\varepsilon})\log n
has minimum degree at least
\alpha_{\varepsilon}pn
.

Proof.

First, let
\alpha
be arbitrary.
Using a Chernoff bound, given that

d(v)\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n-1,p)
, we have

{\mathbf{P}}(d(v)\leq\alpha p(n-1))\leq\exp\Bigl[-(n-1)D(\alpha p\ \|\ p)\Bigr],

where

D(x\ \|\ y):=x\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)+(1-x)\log\left(\frac{1-x}{1-y}\right).

It can be shown easily that

D(\alpha p\ \|\ p)=p\left[\alpha\log\alpha+\frac{1-\alpha p}{p}\log\left(\frac{1-\alpha p}{1-p}\right)\right]\geq p(1-\alpha+\alpha\log\alpha).

Therefore

{\mathbf{P}}(d(v)\leq\alpha p(n-1))\leq\exp\Bigl[-p(n-1)(1-\alpha+\alpha\log\alpha)\Bigr].

Replacing
\alpha
with
\alpha\frac{n}{n-1}
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(d(v)\leq\alpha pn)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-pn\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{n}-\alpha+\alpha\log\left(\frac{\alpha n}{n-1}\right)\Bigr)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-pn\Bigl(1-\alpha+\alpha\log\alpha-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right].

Applying a union bound over all choices of
v
, we get

{\mathbf{P}}(\exists v,d(v)\leq\alpha pn)\leq n\cdot\exp\left[-pn\Bigl(1-\alpha+\alpha\log\alpha-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)\right]=e^{-T},

where

T:=pn\bigl(1-\alpha+\alpha\log\alpha-\frac{1}{n}\bigr)-\log n\geq T^{\prime}\log n
, where

T^{\prime}:=(1+{\varepsilon})\bigl(1-\alpha+\alpha\log\alpha-n^{-1}\bigr)-1={\varepsilon}-(1+{\varepsilon})\alpha(1-\log\alpha)-(1+{\varepsilon})/n.

Now assume
\alpha=\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\exp(-(8/{\varepsilon}+7))
, and let
t=-\log\alpha\geq 8/{\varepsilon}+7
.
Then

\alpha(1-\log\alpha)=e^{-t}(1+t)\leq 8(1/{\varepsilon}+1)\exp\left(-8/{\varepsilon}-7\right)<\tfrac{1}{4}{\varepsilon}/(1+{\varepsilon}).

Assuming
n\geq N_{\varepsilon}=4/{\varepsilon}+4
, we get
(1+{\varepsilon})n^{-1}\leq{\varepsilon}/4
.
The two inequalities above give
T^{\prime}\geq{\varepsilon}-{\varepsilon}/4-{\varepsilon}/4={\varepsilon}/2
.
By a union bound, the probability that
d(v)\leq\alpha_{\varepsilon}pn
for some
v
is at most

e^{-T^{\prime}\log n}\leq n^{-{\varepsilon}/2}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

When
p
is below the connectivity threshold, we have a weaker version of the above lemma, which gives a lower bound on the average degree of sufficiently large subsets of
V
.

Lemma A.5
.

Consider a
G(n,p)
random graph with
p=(1-\lambda)n^{-1}\log n
.
For any
0<\delta<1-\lambda
and any
0<{\varepsilon}<1
such that
{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{e}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\delta/4
, we have: with probability at least
1-n^{-\delta n^{\lambda+\delta}/2}
, any subset
U\subset V
such that
|U|\geq n^{\lambda+\delta}
has average degree at least
{\varepsilon}pn
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary subset
U
with

|U|=\lceil n^{\lambda+\delta}\rceil

.
Consider

X:=\sum_{u\in U}d(u)=\sum_{u\in U}\sum_{v\in V}\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}=2\sum_{\{u,v\}\subset U}\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}+\sum_{u\in U}\sum_{v\in U^{c}}\mathbf{1}_{u\sim v}.

Let
X_{1}
denote the first sum, and
X_{2}
denote the second double sum.
Then
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are indpendent binomial variables, where
X_{1}\sim\operatorname{Bin}\bigl(\binom{|U|}{2},p\bigr)
and
X_{2}\sim\operatorname{Bin}\left(|U||U^{c}|,p\right)
.
In the context of this proof, let
f_{Y}(t):=t{\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}\left[Y\right]+\log\mathbf{E}\left[e^{-tY}\right]
for a random variable
Y
.
A Chernoff bound gives

{\mathbf{P}}\left(X\leq{\varepsilon}\mathbf{E}\left[X\right]\right)\leq e^{f_{X}(t)}=e^{f_{2X_{1}+X_{2}}(t)}=e^{f_{X_{1}}(2t)+f_{X_{2}}(t)}.

Let
f(t):=f_{\operatorname{Ber}(p)}(t)=t{\varepsilon}p+\log(pe^{-t}+1-p)
.
When
Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,p)
, we have
f_{Y}(t)=mf(t)
, so

f_{X_{1}}(2t)+f_{X_{2}}(t)=\binom{|U|}{2}f(2t)+|U||U^{c}|f(t).

It is well-known in the proof of Chernoff’s inequality that
t_{*}=\log\left(\frac{1-p{\varepsilon}}{{\varepsilon}(1-p)}\right)
minimizes
f(t)
at

f(t_{*})=-D(p{\varepsilon}\ \|\ p)=-p{\varepsilon}\log{\varepsilon}-(1-p{\varepsilon})\log\left(\frac{1-p{\varepsilon}}{1-p}\right)
.
We have

\displaystyle f(2t_{*})-2f(t_{*})=\log\left(p\frac{{\varepsilon}^{2}(1-p)^{2}}{(1-p{\varepsilon})^{2}}+1-p\right)-2\log\left(p\frac{{\varepsilon}(1-p)}{1-p{\varepsilon}}+1-p\right),

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{(1-p)(1-2p{\varepsilon}+p{\varepsilon}^{2})}{(1-p{\varepsilon})^{2}}\right)-2\log\left(\frac{1-p}{1-p{\varepsilon}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{1-2p{\varepsilon}+p{\varepsilon}^{2}}{1-p}\right)=O(p).

Thus

\displaystyle\binom{|U|}{2}f(2t_{*})+|U||U^{c}|f(t_{*})=\left[2\binom{|U|}{2}+|U||U^{c}|\right]f(t_{*})+O(p|U|^{2})

\displaystyle=|U|\left[-(n-1)f(t_{*})+O(p|U|)\right]=-|U|\left[nD(p{\varepsilon}\ \|\ p)-O(p|U|)\right],

which is close to the true minimum of the expression.
We can replace
|U|
directly with
n^{\lambda+\delta}
without significantly affecting the computations that follows.
The last expression becomes

-n^{\lambda+\delta}\bigl[nD(p{\varepsilon}\|p)-O(pn^{\lambda+\delta})\bigr]=-n^{1+\lambda+\delta}\bigl[D(p{\varepsilon}\|p)-O(pn^{\lambda+\delta-1})\bigr].

Let
\mathfrak{E}
be the event that there is a subset
U
with at least
n^{\lambda+\delta}
vertices whose average degree is at least
{\varepsilon}pn
.
Then a union bound gives

(28)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbf{P}}(\mathfrak{E})\leq\binom{n}{n^{\lambda+\delta}}\exp\Bigl[-n^{1+\lambda+\delta}\bigl(D(p{\varepsilon}\|p)-O(pn^{\lambda+\delta-1})\bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl[-nh(n^{\lambda+\delta-1})-n^{1+\lambda+\delta}\bigl(D(p{\varepsilon}\|p)-O(pn^{\lambda+\delta-1})\bigr)\Bigr],

where we again replace
\lceil n^{\lambda+\delta}\rceil
with just
n^{\lambda+\delta}
without significantly changing the calculations.
We use the following estimates:

D(p{\varepsilon}\|p)=p{\varepsilon}\log{\varepsilon}-(1-p{\varepsilon})\log\left(1-\frac{p(1-{\varepsilon})}{1-p{\varepsilon}}\right)=p\bigl({\varepsilon}\log{\varepsilon}-{\varepsilon}+1\bigr)+O(p^{2}),

and

h(x)=x\log x-(1-x)\log\left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=x\log x-x+O(x^{2})\text{ for }|x|<1.

We have

nh(n^{\lambda+\delta-1})=n^{\lambda+\delta}(\lambda+\delta-1)\log n-n^{\lambda+\delta}+O(n^{2(\lambda+\delta)-1})

and

\displaystyle n^{1+\lambda+\delta}\bigl(D(p{\varepsilon}\|p)-O(pn^{\lambda+\delta-1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=pn^{1+\lambda+\delta}(1-{\varepsilon}+{\varepsilon}\log{\varepsilon})+O(p^{2}n^{1+\lambda+\delta})-O(pn^{2(\lambda+\delta)})

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda)n^{\lambda+\delta}\left(1-{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{e}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)\log n-O(n^{2(\lambda+\delta)-1}\log n).

The exponent in Eq. (
28
) becomes

\displaystyle-n^{\lambda+\delta}\left[\left((1-\lambda)\left(1-{\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{e}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)-1+\lambda+\delta\right)\log n-1\right]-O(n^{2(\lambda+\delta)-1}\log n)

\displaystyle=-n^{\lambda+\delta}\left[\left(\delta-(1-\lambda){\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{e}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\right)\log n-1\right]-O(n^{2(\lambda+\delta)-1}\log n).

As given,
{\varepsilon}
is small enough so that
(1-\lambda){\varepsilon}\log\left(\frac{e}{{\varepsilon}}\right)<\delta/4
, so the exponent in Eq. (
28
) is at most

-n^{\lambda+\delta}\left(\frac{3\delta}{4}-O(n^{-(1-\lambda-\delta)})\right)\log n\leq-\frac{1}{2}n^{\lambda+\delta}\delta\log n

Therefore all subsets of size at least
n^{\lambda+\delta}
has average degree at least
{\varepsilon}pn
with probability at least
1-n^{-\delta n^{\lambda}/2}
.
∎

The next lemma is an extension of
the Chernoff bounds
for binomial random variables whose size is not fixed.

Lemma A.6
.

Given
p\in(0,1)
,
m\in(0,\infty)
, if
Y
be a random variable supported on
[0,m]\cap\mathbb{Z}

and
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(Y,p)
, then for all
t>0
,

{\mathbf{P}}(X\geq pm+t)\leq\exp\left[-\max\Bigl\{\frac{t^{2}}{2(pm+t)},\frac{t^{2}}{2(1-p)m}\Bigr\}\right].

Proof.

Recall the usual Chernoff bounds for
Z\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,p)
:

(29)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(Z\geq T)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-\frac{(T-pm)^{2}}{2T}\right]\quad\text{ for any }T>pm,

(30)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(Z\leq T)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-\frac{(pm-T)^{2}}{2pm}\right]\quad\text{ for any }T<pm.

Using Eq.
30
, one can get an extension of Eq.
29
as follows:
For
T>pm
, consider
Z^{\prime}:=m-Z\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,1-p)
, then
(1-p)m-(m-T)=T-pm
, so we have

(31)

{\mathbf{P}}(Z\geq T)={\mathbf{P}}(Z^{\prime}\leq m-T)\leq\exp\left[-\frac{(T-pm)^{2}}{2(1-p)m}\right].

Combining Eeqs. (
29
) and (
31
), we get the desired inequality for the random variable
Z
.
It now suffices to show that
Z

stochastic dominates

X
, in the sense that for all
t\geq 0
, we have

{\mathbf{P}}(Z\leq t)\leq{\mathbf{P}}(X\leq t).

To prove this, it suffices to couple
X
and
Z
in a probability space such that
X^{\prime}\leq Z^{\prime}
always holds, where
X^{\prime}
and
Z^{\prime}
are respectively copies of
X
and
Z
.
We can use the following straightforward coupling:

Let
X^{\prime}
be an arbitrary copy of
X
.
Let
Y_{1}:=m-Y
and let
X_{1}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(Y_{1},p)
, independently of
Y
and
X^{\prime}
.
Let
Z^{\prime}:=X^{\prime}+X_{1}
.
For any
k\in[0,m]\cap\mathbb{Z}
,
Z^{\prime}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,p)
on the subspace conditioned on
Y=k
.
For each
t\in[0,m]\cap\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{\prime}=t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}{\mathbf{P}}(Z^{\prime}=t\mid Y=k){\mathbf{P}}(Y=k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{t}p^{t}(1-p)^{m-t}{\mathbf{P}}(Y=k)=\binom{m}{t}p^{t}(1-p)^{m-t}.

Therefore
Z^{\prime}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,p)
.
Since
Z^{\prime}\geq X^{\prime}
always from their definitions, the proof is complete.
∎

The final lemma comes from a breakthrough work by Georges, Bordenave and Knowles
[
4
]
, which provides a tight bound on the operator norm of a random matrix with independent but arbitrary, potentially non-identical cell distributions.
Their result extends another pioneering work by Bandeira and van Handel
[
3
]
for the case of non-sub-Gaussian cell distribution.
It can be proven using
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
and some techniques in
[
3
]
.

Lemma A.7
.

Let
c,a>0
be constants.
Assume
pn\geq c\log n
.
With probability at least
1-n^{-a}
, the adjacency matrix
A
of a random graph
G\sim G(n,p)
satisfies

\|A-\mathbf{E}\left[A\right]\|_{op}\leq C_{c,a}\sqrt{pn}
for a constant
C_{c,a}
depending on
c
and
a
.

Proof.

Let
X:=A-\mathbf{E}\left[A\right]
.
Applying
[
4
, Theorem 3.2]
for the simple case
p_{ij}=p\mathbf{1}_{\{i\neq j\}}
for all
i,j\in[n]
, we get

\mathbf{E}\left[\|X\|_{op}\right]\leq\left(2+C\eta\right)\sqrt{d},

where
C
is a universal constant,
d:=p(1-p)(n-1)\leq pn
, and

\eta:=\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{\min\{\sqrt{d},n^{.1}\}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{\sqrt{c\log n}}=c^{-1/2}.

Therefore, we obtain a simpler bound:

\mathbf{E}\left[\|X\|_{op}\right]\leq(2+Cc^{-1/2})\sqrt{pn}.

Finally, a Talagrand-like inequality
[
7
, Theorem 6.10]
for the convex
1
-Lipschitz function
g:[0,1]^{n(n-1)/2}\to\mathbb{R}
:
\{Z_{ij}\}_{i<j}\mapsto 2^{-1/2}\|Z-\mathbf{E}\left[A\right]\|_{op}
gives

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\|X\|_{op}\geq\mathbf{E}\left[\|X\|_{op}\right]+t\right)\leq e^{-t^{2}/4}.

Combining the three inequalities, we get

{\mathbf{P}}\left(\|X\|_{op}\geq(2+Cc^{-1/2})\sqrt{pn}+t\right)\leq e^{-t^{2}/4}.

Letting
t=2\sqrt{apn}
, we get the desired bound for
C_{c,a}:=2+Cc^{-1/2}+2\sqrt{a}
.
∎

, we have
