Tubal Matrices
Liqun Qi, and, Ziyan Luo

Abstract
It was shown recently that the f-diagonal tensor in the T-SVD factorization must satisfy some special properties. Such f-diagonal tensors are called s-diagonal tensors. In this paper, we show that such a discussion can be extended to any real invertible linear transformation. We show that two Eckart-Young like theorems hold for a third order real tensor, under any doubly real-preserving unitary transformation. The normalized Discrete Fourier Transformation (DFT) matrix, an arbitrary orthogonal matrix, the product of the normalized DFT matrix and an arbitrary orthogonal matrix are examples of doubly real-preserving unitary transformations. We use tubal matrices as a tool for our study. We feel that the tubal matrix language makes this approach more natural. Key words. Tubal matrix, tensor, T-SVD factorization, tubal rank, B-rank, Eckart-Young like theorems AMS subject classifications. 15A69, 15A18

The followings are two further theoretical issues.

First, can we characterize the f-diagonal tensor in the T-SVD factorization for the

Second, the Eckart-Young like theorem (Theorem 4.3 of

We characterize the f-diagonal tensor in the T-SVD factorization of a third order real tensor for a doubly

Note that the real-preserving and doubly real-preserving properties may be neglected in

We use tubal matrices as a tool for this discussion. We feel that the tubal matrix language makes this approach more natural.

2
T-SVD Factorization and s-Diagonal Tubal Matrices

2.1
Real-Preserving Transformation and Tubal Scalar Module

Let
L=(L_{ij})\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
be an invertible matrix. Denote
L^{-1}\equiv H=(H_{ij})
. For
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
,
define a mapping
\phi_{L}:\mathbb{C}^{p}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}
by
\phi_{L}({\bf a}):=L{\bf a}
, and its inverse mapping
\phi_{L}^{-1}:\mathbb{C}^{p}\to\mathbb{C}^{p}
by
\phi_{L}^{-1}({\bf a}):=L^{-1}{\bf a}
. For
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, define

{\bf a}\odot_{L}{\bf b}=\phi_{L}^{-1}[(\phi_{L}({\bf a}))\circ(\phi_{L}({\bf b}))],

(2.1)

where
\circ
is the Hadamard product. If
L
is real, then
\odot_{L}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In general, even if
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
{\bf a}\odot_{L}{\bf b}
may not be real.

For
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, denote its
i
th component by
{\bf a}(i)
.

Let
F\equiv F_{p}
be the
p\times p
Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) matrix with the form

F=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1&1\\
1&\omega&\cdots&\omega^{p-2}&\omega^{p-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&\omega^{p-2}&\cdots&\omega^{(p-2)(p-2)}&\omega^{(p-2)(p-1)}\\
1&\omega^{p-1}&\cdots&\omega^{(p-1)(p-2)}&\omega^{(p-1)(p-1)}\end{bmatrix},

where

\omega=e^{-{2\pi{\bf i}\over p}},

and
{\bf i}=\sqrt{-1}
is the imaginary unit. Then
F^{-1}={1\over p}F^{H}
, where
F^{H}
is the conjugate transpose of
F
, and for any
{\bf a},{\bf c}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, we have

\phi_{F}({\bf a})(l)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega^{(i-1)(l-1)}{\bf a}(i),

(2.2)

for
l=1,\cdots,p
, and

\phi_{F}^{-1}({\bf c})(k)={1\over p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\bar{\omega}^{(l-1)(k-1)}{\bf c}(l),

(2.3)

for
k=1,\cdots,p
.

Proposition 2.1

For any
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

({\bf a}\odot_{F}{\bf b})(k)=\sum\left\{{\bf a}(i){\bf b}(j):i+j-k-1=0\ {\rm mod}(p),i,j=1,\cdots,p\right\},

(2.4)

for
k=1,\cdots,p
.
Thus,
\odot_{F}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Proof
Let
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
{\bf c}=\phi_{F}({\bf a})\circ\phi_{F}({\bf b})
. For
k=1,\cdots,p
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{F}^{-1}({\bf c})(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\bar{\omega}^{(l-1)(k-1)}{\bf c}(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bar{\omega}^{(l-1)(k-1)}\omega^{(i-1)(l-1)}\omega^{(j-1)(l-1)}{\bf a}(i){\bf b}(j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\bf a}(i){\bf b}(j)\left[\sum_{l=1}^{p}\omega^{(l-1)(i+j-k-1)}\right].

Note that

\sum_{l=1}^{p}\omega^{(l-1)(i+j-k-1)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} p,&\ \ {\rm if\ }i+j-k-1=0,\ {\rm mod}(p)\\
0,&\ \ {\rm otherwise}.\end{aligned}\right.

Thus,

\phi_{F}^{-1}({\bf c})(k)=\sum\left\{{\bf a}(i){\bf b}(j):i+j-k-1=0\ {\rm mod}(p),i,j=1,\cdots,p\right\}.

This proves (
2.4
). The last conclusion follows.

.

\Box

We call a
p\times p
invertible matrix
L
a
real-preserving transformation
if
\odot_{L}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The DFT matrix
F
and any
p\times p
real invertible matrices are real-preserving transformations. Not all complex invertible matrices are real-preserving. For example, let

L=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf i}&0\\
0&{\bf i}\end{bmatrix},{\bf a}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
1\\
\end{array}\right],{\bf b}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\\
\end{array}\right].

Then
L^{-1}=-L
,
L{\bf a}={\bf i}{\bf a}
,
L^{-1}{\bf a}=-{\bf i}{\bf a}
, and

{\bf a}\odot_{L}{\bf b}=L^{-1}(L{\bf a}\circ L{\bf b})=-{\bf i}(-{\bf a}\circ{\bf b})=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bf i}\\
0\\
\end{array}\right],

which is not real.

Proposition 2.2

The product of a
p\times p
real-preserving matrix
L
and any
p\times p
invertible matrix
P
is real-preserving.

Proof
Suppose that
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then,

\displaystyle{\bf a}\odot_{LP}{\bf b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(LP)^{-1}\left((LP{\bf a})\circ(LP{\bf b})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{-1}L^{-1}\left((LP{\bf a})\circ(LP{\bf b})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{-1}\left((P{\bf a})\odot_{L}(P{\bf b})\right).

Since
L
is real-preserving,
(P{\bf a})\odot_{L}(P{\bf b})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Thus,

{\bf a}\odot_{LP}{\bf b}=P^{-1}\left((P{\bf a})\odot_{L}(P{\bf b})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Hence,
LP
is real-preserving.

.

\Box

This shows that the set of
p\times p
real-preserving matrices is invariant for multiplying right with any
p\times p
invertible matrix. In particular, the product of the DFT matrix and any invertible matrix is real-preserving. However, it is worth pointing out that
PL
may not be real-preserving even if
P
is an orthogonal matrix and
L
is real-preserving, as illustrated in the following example.

Example 2.3

Let
P=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&0\\
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right]
and
F=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
1&w&w^{2}\\
1&w^{2}&w^{4}\\
\end{array}\right]
. Obviously,
P
is orthogonal and
F
is the
3\times 3
DFT matrix. Choose
{\bf a}=(1,2,3)^{\top}
, and
{\bf b}=(3,4,5)^{\top}
. Direct calculations lead to

{\bf a}\odot_{PF}{\bf b}=(PF)^{-1}\left((PF{\bf a})\circ(PF{\bf b})\right)\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}11.4602+3.9279{\bf i}\\
17.8241-8.8269{\bf i}\\
22.6881+3.0619{\bf i}\\
\end{array}\right],

(2.5)

which indicates that
PF
is not real-preserving.

Suppose
L
is a real-preserving transformation. Then
\mathbb{K}_{p}\equiv\mathbb{K}_{p}(L)=(\mathbb{R}^{p},+,\odot_{L})
is a commutative ring with unity, with
+
as the usual addition of vectors. As discussed in
[
1
]
,
\mathbb{K}_{p}
is also a module. As in
[
8
]
, we call an element
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
a tubal scalar, and call
\mathbb{K}_{p}
the
p
-dimensional tubal scalar module. We use small bold letters
{\bf a},{\bf b},\cdots
, to denote tubal scalars.

The zero tubal scalar
{\bf 0}
has components
{\bf 0}(k)=0
for
k=1,\cdots,p
.
Let the tubal scalar
{\bf 1}
be defined by
{\bf 1}(k)=1
for
k=1,\cdots,p
.
Denote the unit tubal scalar of
\mathbb{K}_{p}
by
{\bf e}_{L}
.

Theorem 2.4

For
k=1,\cdots,p
, we have

{\bf e}_{L}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}H_{kj}.

(2.6)

In particular, we have
{\bf e}_{F}(1)=1
,
{\bf e}_{F}(k)=0
, for
k=2,\cdots,p
.
For
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
, it is invertible if and only if
\phi_{L}({\bf a})(j)\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{ji}{\bf a}(i)\not=0
for
j=1,\cdots,p
. In this case, we have

{\bf a}^{-1}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}{H_{kj}\over\sum_{i=1}^{p}L_{ji}{\bf a}(i)},

(2.7)

for
k=1,\cdots,p
.

Proof
Suppose that
{\bf e}_{L}
is defined by (
2.6
). Then
\phi_{L}({\bf e}_{L})={\bf 1}
. For any
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
,

{\bf a}\odot_{L}{\bf e}_{L}=\phi_{L}^{-1}[(\phi_{L}({\bf a}))\circ(\phi_{L}({\bf e}_{L}))]=\phi_{L}^{-1}[(\phi_{L}({\bf a}))\circ{\bf 1}]=\phi_{L}^{-1}[(\phi_{L}({\bf a}))]={\bf a}.

Similarly,
{\bf e}_{L}\odot_{L}{\bf a}={\bf a}
. Thus,
{\bf e}_{L}
is the unit element of
\mathbb{K}_{p}
.

Therefore, we have the formula of
{\bf e}_{L}\equiv\phi_{L}^{-1}\left({\bf 1}\right)
.

Suppose that
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
is invertible. Then

{\bf a}\odot_{L}{\bf a}^{-1}={\bf e}_{L},

i.e.,

\phi_{L}^{-1}\left(\phi_{L}({\bf a})\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{-1})\right)=\phi_{L}^{-1}\left({\bf 1}\right).

Thus,

\phi_{L}({\bf a})\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{-1})={\bf 1}.

This is equivalent to

\phi_{L}({\bf a})(k)\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{-1})(k)=1,

for
k=1,\cdots,p
. The last two conclusions follow from this.

.

\Box

If
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
is not invertible, we may still derive the formula of
{\bf a}^{\dagger}
, the generalized inverse of
{\bf a}
. We do not go to details for this.

For
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
, define its modulus as

|{\bf a}|=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{p}{\bf a}(k)^{2}}.

2.2
Doubly Real-Preserving Transformation and the Transpose of a Tubal Scalar

Suppose that
L
is a real-preserving transformation.

For
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
, define a mapping
\psi_{L}
as

\psi_{L}({\bf a})=\phi_{L}^{-1}(\overline{\phi_{L}({\bf a})}).

(2.8)

If
L
is real, then
\psi_{L}({\bf a})={\bf a}
. In general,
\psi_{L}({\bf a})
is not real.

Proposition 2.5

The mapping
\psi_{F}:\mathbb{K}_{p}\to\mathbb{K}_{p}
. For
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
, we have
\psi_{F}({\bf a})(1)={\bf a}(1)
, and

\psi_{F}({\bf a})(k)={\bf a}(p+2-k)
for
k=2,\cdots,p
.

Proof

Let
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
and
{\bf c}=\overline{\phi_{F}({\bf a})}
. Then for
k=1,\cdots,p
,

\displaystyle\psi_{F}({\bf a})(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{F}^{-1}({\bf c})(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\bar{\omega}^{(l-1)(k-1)}{\bf c}(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over p}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\bar{\omega}^{(l-1)(k-1)}\overline{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega^{(i-1)(l-1)}{\bf a}(i)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\bf a}(i)\left[\sum_{l=1}^{p}\omega^{(1-l)(i+k-2)}\right].

Since

\sum_{l=1}^{p}\omega^{(1-l)(i+k-2)}=\left\{\begin{aligned} p,&\ \ {\rm if\ }i+k-2=0,\ {\rm mod}(p)\\
0,&\ \ {\rm otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

the conclusion follows.

.

\Box

We say that a real-preserving transformation
L
is a
doubly real–preserving transformation
if

\psi_{L}:\mathbb{K}_{p}\to\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Then DFT matrix
F
and any
p\times p
real invertible matrices are doubly real-preserving transformations.

Proposition 2.6

The product of a
p\times p
doubly real-preserving matrix
L
and any
p\times p
invertible matrix
P
is doubly real-preserving.

Proof
By Proposition
2.2
,
LP
is real-preserving.

Let
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Then,

\displaystyle\psi_{LP}({\bf a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{LP}^{-1}(\overline{\phi_{LP}({\bf a})})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{-1}L^{-1}(\overline{LP{\bf a}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{-1}\left(L^{-1}(\overline{L(P{\bf a})})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P^{-1}\psi_{L}(P{\bf a}).

Since
L
is doubly real-preserving,
\psi_{L}(P{\bf a})\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Thus,

\psi_{LP}({\bf a})=P^{-1}\psi_{L}(P{\bf a})\in\mathbb{K}_{p}.

This implies that
LP
is doubly real-preserving.

.

\Box

Thus, the set of
p\times p
doubly real-preserving matrices is invariant for multiplying right with any
p\times p
invertible matrix. In particular, the product of the DFT matrix and any invertible matrix is doubly real-preserving.

Suppose that
L
is a doubly real-preserving transformation. For any
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
,
its transpose
{\bf a}^{\top}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
is defined by
{\bf a}^{\top}=\psi_{L}({\bf a})
.
If
{\bf a}^{\top}={\bf a}
, then we say that
{\bf a}
is symmetric. Thus, if
L
is real, any
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
is symmetric. The following proposition can be proved by definition.

Proposition 2.7

Suppose that
L
is a doubly real-preserving transformation. Let
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Then
({\bf a}^{\top})^{\top}={\bf a}
and
({\bf a}\odot_{L}{\bf b})^{\top}={\bf b}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a}^{\top}
.

The symmetric tubal scalars in
\mathbb{K}_{p}
form a subspace of
\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Denote it as
\mathbb{S}_{p}
.

In general, even if
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
,
\phi_{L}({\bf a})
is not real. However, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.8

Suppose that
L
is a doubly real-preserving transformation.
The mapping
\phi_{L}
defined above maps
\mathbb{S}_{p}
to
\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Furthermore, we have

\phi_{L}:\mathbb{S}_{p}\to\mathbb{S}_{p}
if
L
is real or
L=F
.

Proof
Let
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{S}_{p}
. Then

\phi_{L}({\bf a})=\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{\top})=\phi_{L}\left(\phi_{L}^{-1}(\overline{\phi_{L}({\bf a})})\right)=\overline{\phi_{L}({\bf a})}.

Thus,
\phi_{L}({\bf a})
is real. If
L
is real, then
\mathbb{K}_{p}(L)\equiv\mathbb{S}_{p}(L)
.
We also have

\phi_{F}({\bf a})(1)=\sum_{l=1}^{p}\omega^{(l-1)(1-1)}{\bf a}(l)=\sum_{l=1}^{p}{\bf a}(l),

and for
k=2,\cdots,p
,

\displaystyle\phi_{F}({\bf a})(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf a}(1)+{1\over 2}\sum_{l=2}^{p}\left[\omega^{(l-1)(k-1)}+\bar{\omega}^{(l-1)(k-1)}\right]{\bf a}(l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{F}({\bf a})(p-k+2).

Thus,
\phi_{F}({\bf a})\in\mathbb{S}_{p}
.

.

\Box

We say that
{\bf a}\in\mathbb{S}_{p}
is positive semi-definite if
\phi_{L}({\bf a})(k)\geq 0
for
k=1,\cdots,p
.

Theorem 2.9

Suppose that
L\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
is a doubly real-preserving transformation, and

{\bf a}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
. Then
{\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a}
is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

Proof

By Proposition
2.7
,

({\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a})^{\top}={\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}({\bf a}^{\top})^{\top}={\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a}.

Hence,
{\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a}
is symmetric. Also,

\displaystyle\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{L}\left(\phi_{L}^{-1}[\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{\top})\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a})]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{L}({\bf a}^{\top})\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{L}(\psi_{L}({\bf a}))\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\phi_{L}(\phi_{L}^{-1}(\overline{\phi_{L}({\bf a})})\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\overline{\phi_{L}({\bf a})}\circ\phi_{L}({\bf a})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\bf 0}.

This shows that
{\bf a}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf a}
is positive semi-definite.

.

\Box

2.3
Tubal Vectors

Suppose that
L
is a doubly real-preserving transformation.

As in
[
8
]
, an element
X\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n}\equiv\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n}(L)
is an
n
-dimensional tubal vector with
p
-dimensional tubal scalar as its components:

X=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}_{1}\\
{\bf x}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf x}_{n}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

We call
X
an
n
-dimensional column tubal vector, and

X^{\top}=({\bf x}_{1}^{\top},{\bf x}_{2}^{\top},\cdots,{\bf x}_{n}^{\top})

an
n
-dimensional row tubal vector. Then
\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n}
is a linear vector space for addition and multiplication with real numbers. We use capital letters
X,Y,Z,\cdots
, to denote tubal vectors.

For
X\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n}
, if

X^{\top}*_{L}X\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf x}_{k}^{\top}\odot_{L}{\bf x}_{k}={\bf e}_{L},

then we say that
X
is normalized.

For
X,Y\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n}
, if

X^{\top}*Y\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bf x}_{k}^{\top}\odot{\bf y}_{k}={\bf 0},

then we say that
X
and
Y
are orthogonal.

For
X\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n}
, define

{\rm unfold}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}^{(1)}\\
{\bf x}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf x}^{(p)}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where

{\bf x}^{(k)}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}_{1}(k)\\
{\bf x}_{2}(k)\\
\vdots\\
{\bf x}_{n}(k)\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Thus,
{\rm unfold}(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{np}
. Note here
{\bf x}^{(k)}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, not a tubal scalar. Then we define
X={\rm fold}({\rm unfold}(X))
.

2.4
Tubal Matrices

Suppose that
L
is a doubly real-preserving transformation.

As in
[
8
]
, an element
{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{m\times n}\equiv\mathbb{K}_{p}^{m\times n}(L)
is an
(m\times n)
-dimensional tubal matrix with
p
-dimensional tubal scalar as its components:

{\mathcal{A}}=({\bf a}_{ij})=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}_{11}&{\bf a}_{12}&\cdots&{\bf a}_{1n}\\
{\bf a}_{21}&{\bf a}_{22}&\cdots&{\bf a}_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
{\bf a}_{m1}&{\bf a}_{m2}&\cdots&{\bf a}_{mn}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

We say that

A_{i}=({\bf a}_{i1},{\bf a}_{i2},\cdots,{\bf a}_{in})

is the

, the DFT matrix is not a unitary matrix, but a unitary matrix multiplied with a positive constant. But it is not difficult to extend the discussion in this section to doubly real-preserving transformation multiplied with a positive constant.

B-Rank and The Second Eckart-Young Like Theorem

For
{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{m\times n}
, suppose
\mathcal{S}=G({\mathcal{A}})
. Then
\mathcal{S}
is an s-diagonal tubal matrix. Let the diagonal entries of
\mathcal{S}
be
{\bf s}_{11},\cdots,{\bf s}_{\min\{m,n\},\min\{m,n\}}
, and
{\bf d}_{ii}=\phi_{L}({\bf s}_{ii})
, for
i=1,\cdots,\min\{m,n\}
. Then the
j

B-singular value

\mu_{j}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
is defined as
j
th largest number of
\{{\bf d}_{11}(1),\cdots,{\bf d}_{11}(p),{\bf d}_{22}(1),\cdots,{\bf d}_{\min\{m,n\},\min\{m,n\}}(p)\}
. The
B-rank
of
{\mathcal{A}}
is equal to the number of its nonzero B-singular values, and denoted as
{\rm Rank}_{b}({\mathcal{A}})
. The
i
th B-tail energy of
{\mathcal{A}}
is defined as
\nu_{i}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=i+1}^{p\min\{m,n\}}\mu_{j}^{2}}
. Here, the letter B means block diagonalization.
It involves a block diagonal matrix below.

Denote
J_{1}=\{1,\cdots,\min\{m,n\}\},J_{2}=\{1,\cdots,p\}
and
J=\{1,\cdots,p\min\{m,n\}\}
. Now, let
\eta:J_{1}\times J_{2}\to J
be a function such that
{\bf d}_{ii}(k)=\mu_{\eta(i,k)}
. Such a function
\eta

\eta
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{m\times n}
. Define
{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}}))
as a
block diagonal matrix

{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}})):={\rm diag}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}})(1),\cdots,\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}})(p)).

Then, the B-singular values of
{\mathcal{A}}
are the singular values of the matrix
{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}}))
, the B-rank of
{\mathcal{A}}
is the rank of
{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}}))
. Since
L
is unitary, we have

\|{\mathcal{A}}\|_{F}=\|\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}})\|_{F}=\|{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}}))\|_{F}.

Also, if
{\mathcal{A}}
has T-SVD factorization (
2.13

\|{\mathcal{A}}\|_{F}=\|\mathcal{S}\|_{F}
.

4
]
,
we have the second Eckart-Young like theorem for
*_{L}

Theorem 3.6

Suppose that
L
is a doubly real-preserving unitary transformation, and

{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}^{m,n}
has a T-SVD factorization (
2.13
), where
\mathcal{S}
as defined in (
3.16
) is an s-diagonal tubal matrix. For
1\leq j<p\min\{m,n\}
, let
{\bf d}_{ii}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{K}_{p}
be defined as
{\bf d}_{ii}^{(j)}(k)={\bf d}_{ii}(k)
if
\eta(i,k)\leq j
; otherwise we have
{\bf d}_{ii}^{(j)}(k)=0
. Let
{\bf s}_{ii}^{(j)}=\phi_{L}^{-1}({\bf d}_{ii}^{(j)})
for
i=1,\cdots,\min\{m,n\}
,

\mathcal{S}_{j}={\rm diag}\left({\bf s}_{11}^{(j)},\cdots,{\bf s}_{\min\{m,n\},\min\{m,n\}}^{(j)}\right)
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{j}=\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{S}_{j}*\mathcal{V}^{\top}
. Then
{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
is the best B-rank
j
approximation of
{\mathcal{A}}
, with

\|{\mathcal{A}}-{\mathcal{A}}_{j}\|_{F}^{2}=\nu_{j}^{2}.

Proof
Denote the set of all tubal matrices in
\mathbb{K}_{p}^{m\times n}
with B-rank no more than
j
by
\mathbb{B}_{j}
.
Let

\Xi:={\rm diag}(\Xi(1),\cdots,\Xi(p))\in\mathbb{C}^{mp\times mp},

D:={\rm diag}(D(1),\cdots,D(p))\in\mathbb{R}^{mp\times np}

and

\Theta:={\rm diag}(\Theta(1),\cdots,\Theta(p))\in\mathbb{C}^{np\times np},

where
\Xi(k),D(k)
and
\Theta(k)
for
k=1,\cdots,p
, are defined by (
2.11
).
It is easy to verify that
\Xi
and
\Theta
are unitary matrices, and

{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}}))=\Xi D\Theta^{H}.

Direct manipulation yields

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}_{j}}\|{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{B}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}_{j}}\|{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}({\mathcal{A}}))-{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}(\mathcal{B}))\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}_{j}}\|\Xi D\Theta^{H}-{\rm Bldg}(\Phi_{L}(\mathcal{B}))\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\|(\hat{\bf d}_{11}(1),\cdots,\hat{\bf d}_{11}(p),\hat{\bf d}_{22}(1),\cdots,\hat{\bf d}_{\min\{m,n\},\min\{m,n\}}(p))\|_{0}\leq j}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{m,n\}}\left({\bf d}_{ii}(k)-\hat{\bf d}_{ii}(k)\right)^{2}\right\}.

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{0}
denotes the number of nonzero members of an argument. Thus,

({\bf d}_{11}^{(j)}(1),\cdots,{\bf d}_{11}^{(j)}(p),{\bf d}_{22}^{(j)}(1),\cdots,{\bf d}^{(j)}_{\min\{m,n\},\min\{m,n\}}(p))

is an optimal solution of the last minimization problem in the above expressions. This further implies that
{\mathcal{A}}_{j}\equiv\mathcal{U}*_{L}\mathcal{S}_{j}*_{L}\mathcal{V}^{\top}
is an optimal solution of
\min_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}_{j}}\|{\mathcal{A}}-\mathcal{B}\|_{F}^{2}
. This completes the proof.

4
Further Questions

There are some further questions on this topic.

Question 1
What is the algebraic structure of the set of
p\times p
doubly real-preserving unitary transformations? Example
2.3
indicates that if
P
is an orthogonal matrix and
F
is the DFT matrix,
PF
may not be real-preserving. Thus, the set of
p\times p
doubly real-preserving unitary transformations is not a group. Proposition
3.1
partially identified the algebraic structure of this set. However, it remains an interesting problem to fully characterize the algebraic structure of this set. Similar questions can be raised for the set of
p\times p
real-preserving matrices, and the set of
p\times p
doubly real-preserving matrices.

Question 2
Can we extend eigenvalue analysis to tubal matrices in
\mathbb{K}_{p}^{n\times n}
for a doubly real-preserving unitary transformation?

Question 3
What part of matrix theory can be extended to tubal matrices? What part of matrix theory can be extended to tubal matrices with substantial differences? What part of matrix theory cannot be extended to tubal matrices?
