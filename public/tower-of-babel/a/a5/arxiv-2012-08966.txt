Limit distribution of the least square estimator with observations sampled at random times driven by standard Brownian motion
Tania Roa, Soledad Torres, Ciprian Tudor

Abstract
In this article, we study the limit distribution of the least square estimator, properly normalized, from a regression model in which observations are assumed to be finite ( α ​ N \alpha N ) and sampled under two different random times. Based on the limit behavior of the characteristic function and convergence result we prove the asymptotic normality for the least square estimator. We present simulations results to illustrate our theoretical results.



2010 AMS Classification Numbers:
60G22; 62J86; 62M09.

Key Words and Phrases
: least squares estimator, random times, regression model, asymptotic normality.

1
Introduction

The estimation of the parameters of a stochastic process on the basis of its random sampling (i.e. the process is observed at random times) received a wide attention in the past. Such a problem is well motivated by practical aspects. Indeed, the measuring instruments (classical or modern, such as sattelites) may introduce random disturbances to the data. For examples, transaction data in finance arrive in irregular time intervals (see e.g.
Engle and Russell 1998
), and the same happens with biological signals in medicine, such as heart rate (see e.g.
Bardet and Bertrand 2010
). Other examples of appearances of random models observed at unequally, possibly random, times can be found, among many others, in climatology (see
Max-Moerbeck et al. 2014
,
Olafsdöttir, Schulz and Mudelsee 2016
) or computer science.
Although quite natural, the hypothesis of random sampling for stochastic models leads to more complex estimators and, in general, some particular choices for the random observation times are considered in the literature. For instance,
Dalayan and Yoshida 2011
, the authors studied a diffusion process observed at independent Poisson times,
Jacod 1993
the situation when the
i
th observation depends on the previous
i-1
observations is considered, while
Vilar 1995
,
Vilar and Vilar 2000
the authors used the so-called jittered and renewal sampling.

Our purpose is to analyse the asymptotic properties of the least squares estimator (LSE in the sequel) for a simple regression model driven by a standard Wiener process, i.e.

Y_{\tau_{i+1}}=a\tau_{i+1}+W_{\tau_{i+1}}-W_{\tau_{i}},\hskip 14.22636pti=0,...,N-1

(1)

where
\tau_{i},i=0,..,N
are random times, independent of
W
, with
\tau_{0}:=0
. We choose to work with the random sampling proposed by
Vilar 1995
,
Vilar and Vilar 2000
which includes two types of randomness: the jittered sampling (the observation times are
\frac{i}{N}
,
i=1,...,N
perturbed by a “small” uniform random variable) or the renewal sampling (the
i
th observation times is a sum of
i
independent positive random variables, so the randomness is somehow progressive).

We construct a least squares estimator (LSE) for the drift parameter
a
of the model (
1
) and then we analyse its asymptotic properties. Our proofs are based on a sharp calculation of the mean square of the estimator and of its conditional distribution given the random times, which is Gaussian. We also use some results given by
Araya et al. 2019
where the asymptotic behavior of the denominator of the LSE estimator is obtained.

We organized our paper as follows. In Section 2 we describe the model and we include a discussion about the number of random observations used to define to estimator. In Section 3 we calculate exactly the mean square norm of the estimator when the number of observations is large enough while in Section 4 we give the asymptotic distribution for the LSE. Many of our theoretical results are illustrated by numerical simulations in Section 5.

2
Preliminaries

Let us now introduced the random times considered in our model (
1
). Our examples are inspired from
Araya et al. 2019
and
Vilar 1995
.

2.1
Random times

Let
T=1
and
\tau=\{\tau_{i};i=0,\ldots,N\}
a strictly increasing sequence of random points over time, where
N
is the last integer such that
\tau_{N-1}\leq 1
, which exhibits one of the following two features.

1.

Jittered sampling (JS)
. First, we assume that we observed a certain process at regular times
\tau
with period
\delta=1/N>0
but contaminated by an additive noise
\nu
which represents possible measurement errors. Then the sequence of random times
\tau_{i},\quad 0\leq i\leq N
is defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{i,N}=:\tau_{i}=\dfrac{i}{N}+\nu_{i,N},\quad i=1,\dots,N\;and\;\tau_{0}:=0,\dots,

(2)

where
\{\nu_{i,N};\quad 1\leq i\leq N\}
constitutes a triangular array of independent and identically distributed set of random variables with common density function depending on
N
, called
g_{N}(t)
, which is assumed to be symmetric in
\left[-\frac{1}{2N},\frac{1}{2N}\right]
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. From now on, we state the following about
\nu_{i,N}

•

\mathbb{E}\left[\nu_{i,N}\right]=0
, and

•

\mathbb{E}\left[\nu^{2}_{i,N}\right]
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\nu^{2}_{i,N}\right]=c_{1}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mbox{ with }c_{1}>0.

(3)

Some distributions that satisfy the latter statement are the uniform distribution in
\left[-\frac{1}{2N},\frac{1}{2N}\right]
, triangular distribution with parameters
\left(-\frac{1}{2N},0,\frac{1}{2N}\right)
and the raised cosine distribution with parameters
\mu=0
and
s=\frac{1}{2N}
. For instance,
c_{1}=\frac{1}{12}
when
g_{N}
is the uniform distribution over the interval
\left[-\frac{1}{2N},\frac{1}{2N}\right]
and
c_{1}=\frac{1}{24}
when
g_{N}
is the triangular distribution with parameters
\left(-\frac{1}{2N},0,\frac{1}{2N}\right)
.

2.

Renewal sampling (RP)
. In this case, the sequence
\tau
satisfies the following property

\displaystyle\tau_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}t_{j}\ \ \ \ i=1,2,...\ \ \ \mbox{and}\,\tau_{0}:=0,

(4)

where
\{t_{j},1\leq j\}
is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables, with a common distribution function
G
with support in
[0,\infty)
. In this work we consider that
G
is an exponential distribution with parameter
\lambda=N
, i.e. it has density function
g(t)=Ne^{-Nt}1_{(0,\infty)}(t).
The random times
\tau_{i}
given by (
4
) actually depend also on
N
but we still use the notation
\tau_{i,N}=:\tau_{i}
, for simplicity.

2.2
The number of observations

Assume that we observe a stochastic process
Y
at times
\tau_{1},...,\tau_{[\alpha N]}
with
\tau_{i}<\tau_{i+1}
for every
i\geq 1
and with
0<\alpha\leq 1
. We want to ensure that our observation period remains, almost surely, inside the interval
[0,T]
with
T=1
. That is, we would like to have that the last observation
\tau_{[\alpha N]}
is almost surely less that
1
for
N
sufficiently large. In the case of jittered sampling, this is always true for
\alpha=1
, due to our hypothesis (
3
). Indeed,
\tau_{N-1}=\frac{N-1}{N}+\nu_{N-1,N}
and
\tau_{N}=1+\nu_{N,N}
and then
P(\tau_{N-1}>1)=0
while
P(\tau_{N}>1)=\frac{1}{2}
, so
\tau_{N-1}
is almost surely in the observation interval
[0,1]
. In this case we assume
\alpha=1
and
Y_{\tau_{N}}=0
.

On the other hand, in the situation of the renewal sampling, we have
\tau_{N}\sim G(N,N)
(by
(G(a,\lambda)
we denote the Gamma law with parameters
a>0,\lambda>0
)) and by a result of
Gautschi 1977
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N}>1)=\mathbb{P}(G(N,N)>1)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{2}.

In order to be sure that our observation period remains inside the interval
[0,1]
, the price to pay is to consider a slightly less number of observations, i. e. to take
\alpha\approx 1
(which means
\alpha<1
is arbitrary close to
1
). Then,
\tau_{\alpha N}\sim G(\alpha N,N)
, which can be written as
\tau_{\alpha N}\sim G(M,\tilde{\alpha}M)
, where
M=\alpha N
and
\tilde{\alpha}=1/\alpha
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{\alpha N}>1\right)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(\tilde{\alpha}M)^{M}}{\Gamma(M)}x^{M-1}e^{-\tilde{\alpha}Mx}dx,

and by the change of variable
y=\tilde{\alpha}Mx
, the last equation can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{\alpha N}>1\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(\tilde{\alpha}M)^{M}}{\Gamma(M)}\int_{\tilde{\alpha}M}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{y}{\tilde{\alpha}M}\right)^{M-1}e^{-y}dy

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}M}{\Gamma(M)}\int_{\tilde{\alpha}M}^{\infty}y^{M-1}e^{-y}dy=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}M}{\Gamma(M)}\Gamma(M,\tilde{\alpha}M).

By the result obtained by
Gautschi 1977
on the limit behavior of
\Gamma(N,\alpha N)
for
\tilde{\alpha}>1
, the last result can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{\alpha N}>1\right)

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}M}{\Gamma(M)}\cdot\dfrac{(\tilde{\alpha}M)^{M}e^{-\tilde{\alpha}M}}{(1+\tilde{\alpha})M}=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}^{M+1}M^{M+1}e^{-\tilde{\alpha}M}}{(1+\tilde{\alpha})M!},

from Stirling approximation we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{\alpha N}>1\right)

\displaystyle\sim\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}^{M+1}M^{M+1}e^{-\tilde{\alpha}M}}{(1+\tilde{\alpha})\sqrt{2\pi M}\left(\frac{M}{e}\right)^{M}}=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}^{M+1}M^{1-1/2}e^{-\tilde{\alpha}M}e^{M}}{(1+\tilde{\alpha})\sqrt{2\pi}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}^{M+1}M^{1/2}e^{-M(\tilde{\alpha}+1)}}{(1+\tilde{\alpha})\sqrt{2\pi}}=\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}^{\alpha N+1}M^{1/2}e^{-\alpha N(\tilde{\alpha}+1)}}{(1+\tilde{\alpha})\sqrt{2\pi}},

and recalling that
M=\alpha N
, the last quantity goes to zero as
N\to\infty
for
\alpha<1
.

Then, for every
\alpha\approx 1
and for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a set
\Omega_{\alpha,\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
such that
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\alpha,\varepsilon})>1-\varepsilon
and for every
\omega\in\Omega_{\alpha,\varepsilon}
we have
\tau_{[\alpha N]}(\omega)\leq 1
when
N
is sufficiently large. We then assume that, in the renewal case,
\alpha\approx 1
and we always work on the space
\Omega_{\alpha,\varepsilon}.
A similar procedure was considered by
Mishura 2008
, Theorem 3.4.1.

Notice that several authors (see e.g.
Vilar 1995
), consider that the model (
1
) is at times
\tau_{1},...,\tau_{N(1)}
where
N(1)
is the last time contained in the interval
[0,1]
. Instead, we prefer to work on a smaller probability space (but still very close to
\Omega)
which guaranties that the entire observation period is contained in the unit interval
[0,1]
.

3
Least squares estimator

Let us fix
0<\alpha\leq 1
and denote
N_{\alpha}=[\alpha N]
, the number of observations. Consider the model

Y_{\tau_{i+1}}=a\tau_{i+1}+\Delta W_{\tau_{i+1}},\hskip 14.22636pti=0,\ldots N_{\alpha}-1

(5)

where
\Delta W_{\tau_{i+1}}=W_{\tau_{i+1}}-W_{\tau_{i}}
, with
0<\alpha\leq 1
. Actually, throughout this work we assume
\alpha=1
in the jittered sampling case and
\alpha\approx 1
in the renewal sampling case, see the discussion in Section
2.2
. In Figures
1
and
2
we illustrate the behavior of the noise in (
5
) at the random times (
2
) and (
4
) (which appears to be similar to the behavior of the Brownian increment itself). The LSE for the drift parameter
a
in the model (
1
) is obtained in a standard way, by minimizing the function
f(a)=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\left(Y_{\tau_{i+1}}-a\tau_{i+1}\right)^{2}
giving

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{i+1}Y_{\tau_{i+1}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{i+1}^{2}}

(6)

for both jittered sampling (JS) and renewal sampling (RS) cases. From (
5
), (
6
) we immediately have,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{N}-a

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{i+1}\Delta W_{\tau_{i+1}}}{\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{i+1}^{2}}:=\dfrac{A_{N}}{D_{N}},\quad

(7)

for every
N\geq 1
,

A_{N}=\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{i+1}Y_{\tau_{i+1}}\mbox{ and }D_{N}=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{i+1}^{2}.

(8)

Our purpose is to analyze the asymptotic properties of the LSE (
6
), in particular its asymptotic normality in distribution. The denominator of the expression (
7
) has been already studied by
Araya et al. 2019
. Let us recall their results (see Lemma 3.2 of
Araya et al. 2019
).

Proposition 1
.

Let
D_{N}
be given by (
8
). Then
D_{N}
converges almost surely, as
N\to\infty
to
\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3}
.

Actually, the result of
Araya et al. 2019
has been obtained for
\alpha=1
, but after inspecting the proof, it is clear that the same arguments holds for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Therefore, in order to obtain the asymptotic behavior of the LSE, we need to analyse the sequence
A_{N}
in (
7
). A first step in this direction is to evaluate the
L^{2}(\Omega)
- norm of
A_{N}
when
N
is large.

Lemma 1
.

Let
A_{N}
given by (
7
), for every
N\geq 1
, then either if
\tau
is defined as (
2
) or (
4
), it holds

\mathbb{E}\left|NA_{N}\right|^{2}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{3}\alpha^{3}.

Although the above result is the same in the JS and RS cases, the proof is different. While in the JS case, the limit if given by the “deterministic part” of the times (
2
), in the RS case there is no deterministic part and both summands in (
19
) contribute to the limit. The prroofs can be found in the appendix.

4
Limit distribution of the LSE

Lemma
1
shows that the sequence
(A_{N})_{N\geq 1}
given by (
8
) converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)
to zero as
N\to\infty
. We can also show that
A_{N}
converges to zero in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for every
p\geq 2
and by a Borel-Cantelli argument, we get its almost sure convergence to zero. Indeed, via conditioning on
\nu
,

\mathbb{E}|A_{N}|^{p}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[|A_{N}|^{p}|\nu\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N})\right]

with, for
x_{1}<x_{2}<\ldots x_{N}
,

g(x_{1},...,x_{N})=\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x_{i+1}(W_{x_{i+1}}-W_{x_{i}})\right|^{p}\leq C_{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}x_{i+1}(W_{x_{i+1}}-W_{x_{i}})\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}.

This implies, together with Lemma
1

\mathbb{E}|A_{N}|^{p}\leq C_{p}N^{-2p}

and thus for every
\gamma>0
and for every
p
integer such
2p-\gamma>1
,

\sum_{N\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(A_{N}>N^{-\gamma})\leq\sum_{N\geq 1}N^{\gamma-2p}<\infty.

Then, via Proposition
1
, we obtain the consistency of the LSE (
6
).

Let us now study the asymptotic limit in distribution of
A_{N}
. To this end, we need to study the sequence
(Q_{N})_{N\geq 1}
defined, for every
N\geq 1
, by

\displaystyle Q_{N}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\alpha}-1}\tau_{j+1}^{2}\left(\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j}\right).

(9)

This plays the role of the “bracket” of
A_{N}
. Before, let us introduce some notation: If
\tau_{j}
is given by (
2
), then we can write them as

\tau_{j}=\frac{j}{N}+\frac{X_{j}}{N},

(10)

where
X_{j},j=1,..,.
are independent random variables and
X_{j}
follows a symmetric probability distribution with support in
\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]
and with density denoted by
g
. If
\tau_{j}
is given by (
4
), then

\tau_{j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{j}X_{i},

(11)

where
X_{i}
follows the Gamma law
G(1,1)
. Moreover, for every
i\geq 1
,
X_{i+1}-X_{i}
is independent of
X_{l}
if
l\leq i
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
Q_{N}
be given by (
9
). Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(Q_{N}-\frac{1}{3}\right)^{2}\right]\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.

We now give the limit in distribution of the sequence
A_{N}
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
A_{N}
given in (
7
), then the following convergence in distribution holds

NA_{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty^{+}]{\mathcal{L}}N\left(0,\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3}\right),

either if
\tau_{i}
are defined either by (
2
) or by (
4
).

Proof:
We analyse the asymptotic distribution of the characteristic function of
NA_{N}
, denoted
\varphi_{NA_{N}}
in the sequel. Via conditioning, with
X_{j}
given by (
10
) or by (
11
), with
N^{\prime}_{\alpha}=N-1
in the JS case and
N^{\prime}_{\alpha}=N_{\alpha}
in the RP case,

\displaystyle\varphi_{NA_{N}}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{itNA_{N}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sum_{j=0}^{N^{\prime}_{\alpha}-1}\left(\frac{j+1}{N}+\frac{X_{j+1}}{N}\right)\left(W_{\frac{j+1}{N}+\frac{X_{j+1}}{N}}-W_{\frac{j}{N}+\frac{X_{j}}{N}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left.\left[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{it\sum_{j=0}^{N^{\prime}_{\alpha}-1}\left(\frac{j+1}{N}+\frac{X_{j+1}}{N}\right)\left(W_{\frac{j+1}{N}+\frac{X_{j+1}}{N}}-W_{\frac{j}{N}+\frac{X_{j}}{N}}\right)}\right|X\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{N^{\prime}_{\alpha}-1}\left(\frac{j+1}{N}+\frac{X_{j+1}}{N}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{X_{j+1}}{N}-\frac{X_{j}}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}Q_{N}}\right]

(12)

with
Q_{N}
from (
9
). Now, by Propostion
2
, the sequence
Q_{N}
converges, in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, thus in probability, to
\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3}
. By Dominated convergence theorem, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\varphi_{NA_{N}}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3}}\right]

and this gives the conclusion.

By Propositions
1
and
3
, we immediately obtain the asymptotic normality of the LSE. We denote by
\xrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}
the convergence in law.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the LSE
\hat{a}_{N}
given by (
6
). Then

N(\hat{a}_{N}-a)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty^{+}]{\mathcal{L}}N(0,\frac{3}{\alpha^{3}}).

Remark 1
.

Let us give some heuristics that explain the convergence in law of the sequence
(NA_{N})_{N\geq 1}
. Consider the JS case and
\alpha=1
. Then we can write

\displaystyle NA_{N}=\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\tau_{i+1}(W_{\tau_{i+1}}-W_{\tau_{i}})=\int_{0}^{1}H_{N}(s)dW_{s}

with
H_{N}(s)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-2}\tau_{i+1}1_{(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]}(s)
, for
s\in[0,1]
. Intuitively,
H_{N}(t)

converges to
t
in
L^{2}([0,1]\times\Omega)
since
|\tau_{i}-\frac{i}{N}|\leq\frac{1}{N}
. Therefore

NA_{N}
would converge in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, as
N\to\infty
, to
\int_{0}^{1}sdW_{s}
whose law in
N(0,\frac{1}{3})
.

Let us finish this theoretical part with some comment on the distance between the law of the sequence
(NA_{N})_{N\geq 1}
and its limit. Recall that the distance between the laws of two random variables
X
and
Y
is defined as

d(X,Y)=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(Y)\right|

where
\mathcal{A}
is a class of functions (its choice defines specific distances, such as Kolmogorov, total variation or Wasserstein or other distances). Let
h
be a function such that the all expectations below exist. Consider for simplicity
\alpha=1
as in the JS case. Then, by taking the conditional expectation as in the proof of Lemma
1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}h(NA_{N})=\mathbb{E}h\left(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(NA_{N})^{2}}Z\right)

with
Z\sim N(0,1)
. This implies that, for
N
large (see Proposition 3.6.1 of
Nourdin and Peccati 2012
)

d\left(NA_{N},\sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}Z\right)\leq C\left|\mathbb{\mathbb{E}}(NA_{N})^{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right|\leq C\frac{1}{N}

(13)

where the last bound can be obtained easily from the proof of Lemma
1
. A similar bound as in (
13
) can be obtained when we replace
NA_{N}
by
\hat{a}_{N}-a
with
\hat{a}_{N}
given by (
6
).
