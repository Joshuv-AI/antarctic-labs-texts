Alberto Dayan

Abstract
. We study interpolating sequences of d d -tuples of matrices, by looking at the commuting and the non-commuting case separately. In both cases, we will give a characterization of such sequences in terms of separation conditions on suitable reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, and we will give sufficient conditions stated in terms of separation via analytic functions. Examples of such interpolating sequences will also be given.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
be the algebra of bounded analytic functions on the unit disc, normed with

||\phi||_{\infty}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|\phi(z)|\qquad\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}.

A sequence
Z:=(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
is interpolating if, for any bounded sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{C}
, there exists a bounded analytic function
\phi
such that
\phi(z_{n})=w_{n}
, for any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
. Intuitively, an interpolating sequence is a separated sequence, as we want to specify the values of a bounded holomorphic functions
arbitrarily
at the nodes. Since we work with analytic functions in the unit disc, a natural way to measure the distance between the points of a sequence is by using the
pseudo-hyperbolic distance

\rho(z,w)=|b_{w}(z)|:=\left|\frac{w-z}{1-\overline{w}z}\right|\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}.

Here
b_{w}
denotes the involutive Blaschke factor at
w
.

The sequence
Z
is said to be
strongly separated
if

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\prod_{j\neq n}\rho(z_{n},z_{j})>0

whereas
z
is
weakly separated
if

\inf_{n\neq j}\rho(z_{n},z_{j})>0.

Since any function
\phi
in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
with zeros of multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{l}
at the points
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{l}
in
\mathbb{D}
is divisible in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
by a Blaschke product

\phi=g\penalty\ \prod_{j=1}^{l}b_{\lambda_{j}}^{m_{j}},

where
||g||_{\infty}=||\phi||_{\infty}
, being weakly separated is equivalent to the existence, for any pair of distinct indices
n
and
j
, of a function
\phi_{n,j}
in the unit ball of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
that vanishes at
z_{j}
and such that
|\phi_{n,j}(z_{n})|\geq\delta
, for some positive
\delta
independent of
n
and
j
. Analogously,
Z
is strongly separated if and only if there exists a positive
\delta
such that, for any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
, there exists a function
\phi_{n}
in the unit ball of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
that vanishes at all points of the sequence except
z_{n}
and
|\phi_{n}(z_{n})|\geq\delta
.

Let
\mathrm{H}^{2}
be the Hardy space, that is, the Hilbert space of analytic functions on the unit disc with square summable Taylor coefficients. Carleson characterized interpolating sequences in the unit disc in
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
Z
be a sequence in the unit disc. The following are equivalent:

(i):

Z
is interpolating;

(ii):

Z
is strongly separated;

(iii):

Z
is weakly separated and the measure

\mu_{Z}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(1-|z_{n}|^{2})\delta_{z_{n}}

satisfies the embedding condition

(1.1)

||f||_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{D},\mu_{Z})}\leq C\penalty\ ||f||_{\mathrm{H}^{2}}\qquad f\in\mathrm{H}^{2}.

Condition (
1.1
) can be extended to more general settings, as it can be expressed in terms of the Euclidean geometry of
reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
. A reproducing kernel Hilbert space on a domain
X
of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of functions on
X
such that point evaluation at any
x
in
X
is continuous. Thanks to Riesz representation Theorem, this implies that, for any
x
in
X
, there exists a function
k_{x}
in
\mathcal{H}
such that

(1.2)

f(x)=\braket{f,k_{x}}_{\mathcal{H}}\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}.

This define a function
k\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{C}
by

k(x,y)=k_{y}(x)

which is referred as a
kernel
on
X
. To emphasize the dependence on the kernel
k
of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we will denote the associated reproducing kernel Hilbert space by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where necessary. Each reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
has a so called
multiplier algebra

\mathcal{M}_{k}:=\{\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{C}\,|\,\phi f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\,f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}

defined as the algebra of those functions on
X
that multiplies
\mathcal{H}_{k}
into itself. Thanks to the closed graph Theorem, any function
\phi
in
\mathcal{M}_{k}
induces a bounded linear operator
M_{\phi}
on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
by

M_{\phi}(f):=\phi\penalty\ f\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

In particular,
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is normed by

||\phi||_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}:=||M_{\phi}||_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})}.

The Hardy space
\mathrm{H}^{2}
is one of the most studied examples of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, together with its Szegö kernel

s_{w}(z):=\frac{1}{1-\overline{w}z}\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}.

Its multiplier algebra
\mathcal{M}_{s}
coincides isometrically with
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
.

A measure
\mu
on
X
is a
Carleson measure
for
\mathcal{H}
if
\mathrm{L}^{2}(X,\mu)
embeds continuously in
\mathcal{H}
. Hence (
1.1
) says precisely that
\mu_{Z}
is a Carleson measure for
\mathrm{H}^{2}
.

It turns out that separation conditions on a sequence
Z
correspond to separation conditions on the kernel functions at the point of such sequence. More precisely, let

\hat{h}:=\frac{h}{||h||}

be the normalization of any element in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and recall that a sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of unit vectors in
\mathcal{H}
is a
Riesz system
if there exists a positive
C
such that, for any
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
l^{2}
,

(1.3)

\frac{1}{C^{2}}\penalty\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|a_{n}|^{2}\leq\left|\left|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}h_{n}\right|\right|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq C^{2}\penalty\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|a_{n}|^{2}.

If the right inequality holds, we say that
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
Bessel system
. The least constant
C
in (
1.3
) is denoted by the
Riesz constant
of the sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Shapiro and Shields proved in
[
14
]
that a sequence
Z
in
\mathbb{D}
is interpolating if and only if the sequence
(\hat{s}_{z_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of normalized Szegö kernels at the points of the sequence is a Riesz system. Moreover, one can find in
[
3
, Th. 9.5]
a proof that, given any reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
on a domain
X
and given a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of points in
X
, then the measure

\mu_{X}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}||k_{x_{n}}||^{-2}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\delta_{x_{n}}

is a Carleson measure for
\mathcal{H}
if and only if the sequence
(\hat{k}_{x_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Bessel system in
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, (
1.1
) can be restated as the sequence of normalized Szegö kernels at the points of
Z
being a Bessel system in the Hardy space. Such results, other than constitute a valuable correspondence between separation of points via holomorphic functions on a domain
X
and separation of the respective kernel functions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, are extremely useful for extending Carleson’s Theorem to more general settings.

One of the various efforts meant to extend Theorem
1.1
can be found in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
, where the author extended Carleson interpolation Theorem to sequences of square matrices (of any dimensions). A holomorphic function
f
on the unit disc is evaluated at a square matrix
M
via the following extension of Cauchy integral formula:

(1.4)

f(M):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(\xi)(\xi\penalty\ Id-M)^{-1}\,d\xi.

In particular, for
f(M)
to be defined, the eigenvalues of
M
must lay in
\mathbb{D}
. Given then any sequence
A:=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of square matrices (of possibly different and not uniformly bounded dimensions) with spectra in the unit disc, one can extend the classic definition of interpolating sequences by identifying a sequence of bounded targets with a bounded sequence in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
:

Definition 1.2
.

A
is interpolating if, for any bounded sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
there exists a bounded analytic function
\phi
such that

\phi(A_{n})=\phi_{n}(A_{n})\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

As for separating the matrices in
A
, one can follow the same outline as for scalars, and define
A
to be
weakly separated
if there exists a
\delta>0
such that, for any pair of distinct indices
j
and
n
, there exits a function
\phi_{n,j}
in the unit ball of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
such that

\phi_{n,j}(A_{j})=0\quad\phi(A_{n})=\delta\penalty\ Id.

In the same fashion,
A
is said to be
strongly separated
if there exists a bounded sequence of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
functions
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\phi_{n}(A_{j})=\delta_{n,j}\penalty\ Id.

In order to extend the Carleson measure condition in (
1.1
) to the sequence of square matrices
A
, one has to consider separation conditions on finite dimensional subspaces of
\mathrm{H}^{2}
. For any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
, let

(1.5)

H_{n}:=\mathrm{H}^{2}\ominus\{f\in\mathrm{H}^{2}\,|\,f(A_{n})=0\}

be the orthogonal complement in
\mathrm{H}^{2}
of the subspace of all those
\mathrm{H}^{2}
functions that vanish at
A_{n}
. Observe that if
A_{n}=z_{n}
is a
1\times 1
scalar in
\mathbb{D}
, then
H_{n}
is the line spanned by the Szegö kernel at
z_{n}
, thanks to the reproducing property in (
1.2
). With this in mind, one can extend the notion of Riesz and Bessel system to any sequence
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of closed subspaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, by asking that there exists a positive
C
so that, for any sequence of unit vectors
(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
chosen in
\mathcal{H}
so that each
h_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, (
1.3
) holds (or, in the case of a Bessel system, the right inequality in (
1.3
) holds). Carleson’s Theorem and Shapiro and Shields result in
[
14
]
can be then extended to this matrix node interpolation problem as follows,
[
9
]

[
10
]
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
A
be a sequence of square matrices with spectra in the unit disc. The following are equivalent:

(i):

A
is interpolating;

(ii):

A
is strongly separated;

(iii):

A
is weakly separated and the sequence
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (
1.5
) is a Bessel system in
\mathrm{H}^{2}
;

(iv):

(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Riesz system in
\mathrm{H}^{2}
.

The aim of this note is to consider sequences of
d
-tuples
of matrices (of any dimensions), and to determine whether some well known results on interpolating sequences in
multi-variable
function theory extend to a matrix interpolation problem. The main distinction for such a multi-variable matrix interpolation problem from the scalar case is that a
d
-tuple

M=(M^{1},\dots,M^{d})

of
m\times m
matrices might commute or not. If the matrices in
M
commute, then (
1.4
) has a multi-variable analogue, and a bounded holomorphic function on the polydisc can be applied to
M
, provided that its joint spectrum belongs to
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. On the other hand, not even a polynomial can be applied to
M
if its components do not commute, and therefore in order to study interpolating properties of sequences of
d
-tuples of non commuting matrices one has to consider a whole different class of functions. Given this intrinsic difference, this introduction will treat those two cases separately.

1.1.
Interpolating
d
-tuples of Commuting Matrices

Let
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
be the Banach algebra of bounded analytic functions on the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. A sequence
Z=(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is interpolating for
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
if, given any bounded
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{C}
there exists a bounded holomorphic function
f
on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
so that
f(z_{n})=w_{n}
, for all
n
. Berndtsson, Chang and Lin proved in
[
6
]
the following analogue of Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem 1.4

(Berndtsson, Chang and Lin)
.

Let
Z=(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, and let
(a)
,
(b)
and
(c)
denote the following statements:

(a)

(1.6)

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\prod_{j\neq n}\rho_{G}(z_{n},z_{j})>0;

(b)

Z
is interpolating for
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
;

(c)

The measure

\mu_{Z}:=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}(1-|z_{n}^{i}|^{2})\right)\delta_{z_{n}}

is a Carleson measure for
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{d}
and

(1.7)

\inf_{n\neq j}\rho_{G}(z_{n},z_{j})>0.

Then
(a)
\implies
(b)
\implies
(c)
, and if
d\geq 2
none of the converse implications hold.

\mathrm{H}^{2}_{d}
will be here the
Hardy space
on the polydisc, that is, the reproducing kernel Hilbert space on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
with kernel

s_{d}(z,w):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-\overline{w^{i}}z^{i}}\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}^{d}.

Conditions (
1.6
) and (
1.7
) are separation conditions, both stated in terms of the so called
Gleason distance
on the polydisc:

\rho_{G}(z,w):=\max_{i=1,\dots d}\rho(z^{i},w^{i})\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}^{d}.

It turns out that
\rho_{G}
corresponds to separating points via bounded analytic function, since

(1.8)

\rho_{G}(z,w)=\sup\{|\phi(z)|\,|\,||\phi||_{\infty}\leq 1,\phi(w)=0\}.

Throughout this note, (
1.6
) will refer to
strong separation
on the polydisc, while (
1.7
) defines a
weakly separated
sequence on the polydisc.

Even if Theorem
1.4
does not characterize interpolating sequences in the polydisc, the case
d=2
provides a characterization of interpolating sequences by looking at the Euclidean geometry of those reproducing kernels Hilbert spaces whose multiplier algebra is
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
. The main difference with the one-variable case is the fact that, rather than working with just the Szegö kernel, in order to obtain interpolating properties one has to consider a whole class of different kernels on the polydisc. In
[
2
]
, Agler and McCarthy characterized interpolating sequences in the bi-disc in terms of separation conditions on the class of so called
admissible kernels
. A kernel
k
on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is admissibile if the multiplications by the coordinates

M_{z^{1}},\dots,M_{z^{d}}

form a set of commuting contractions on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{d}
be the set of all admissible kernels on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, and let
\mathcal{B}_{d}
be the set of all kernels
k
on the
d
-dimensional polydisc whose multiplier algebra coincide with
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
. Since the coordinate functions are clearly in the unit ball of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
, we have that
\mathcal{B}_{d}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_{d}
. Conversely, thanks to Ando’s inequality
[
4
]
, for any kernel in
\mathcal{B}_{2}

||\phi(T_{1},T_{2})||_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})}\leq||\phi||_{\infty}

for any
\phi
in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
and for any pair
(T_{1},T_{2})
of commuting contractions on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Since

M_{\phi}=\phi(M_{z^{1}},M_{z^{2}})\qquad\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}

we have that

\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{B}_{2}.

Namely, the class of admissible kernels coincides with the class of kernels on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
whose multiplier algebra is
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
.

Example 1.5
.

Let
\alpha:=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
be a
d
-tuple of positive integers. Then the kernel

s^{\alpha}_{w}(z):=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{(1-\overline{w^{i}}z^{i})^{\alpha_{i}}}\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}^{d}

is admissible on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. Indeed, multiplication by the coordinates being a set of contractions is equivalent to assert that, for any
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, the matrices

[(1-\overline{z_{n}^{i}}z_{j}^{i})k_{z_{j}}(z_{n})]_{n,j=1}^{N}\qquad i=1,\dots,d

are positive semi-definite. Therefore, since the Szegö kernel is admissible and the Schur (point-wise) product of positive semi-definite matrices is positive semi-definite,
s^{\alpha}
belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{d}
for any
\alpha
.

Let, for any kernel
k
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}

S^{k}_{Z}:=\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{k_{z_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}.

Given a bounded sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, if there exits a function
\phi
in the unit ball
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
such that

\phi(z_{n})=w_{n}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

then, for any
k
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, the operator

T^{k}\colon S^{k}_{Z}\to S^{k}_{Z}

such that

T^{k}(k_{z_{n}})=\overline{w_{n}}k_{z_{n}}

is a contraction in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Indeed,
T^{k}
is the restriction of
M^{*}_{\phi}
to
S^{k}_{Z}
since, for any reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
on domain
X
, the reproducing property of the kernel

implies that every kernel function is an eigenvector of the adjoint of multiplication by any multiplier:

\mathcal{M}_{\phi}^{*}(k_{x})=\overline{\phi(x)}\penalty\ k_{x}\qquad x\in X,\,\phi\in\mathcal{M}_{k}.

Conversely,
[
1
]
, if

\sup_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}||T^{k}||_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{k})}\leq 1,

then
each

T^{k}
is in fact the restriction of the adjoint of the multiplication by a function
\phi
in the unit ball of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
, and
\phi(z_{n})=w_{n}
. This, together with Theroem
1.7
below, gives the following characterization for interpolating sequences for the bidisk, which is part of a result in
[
2
]
:

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
Z=(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Then
Z
is interpolating for
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
if and only if there exists a constant

such that, for any
k
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, the sequence of normalized kernels
(\hat{k}_{z_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Reisz sequence in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
with Riesz bound

.

As we will see, the key for the identification between Riesz systems of kernels functions and interpolating sequences for those reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces with extension properties is the following characterization of the Riesz system condition
[
12
, Th. 3.1.4 ]
:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
H=(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of closed sub-spaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The following are equivalent:

(i):

H
is a Riesz system with Riesz bound
C
;

(ii):

For any sequence of linear functions
(\chi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\chi_{n}\colon H_{n}\to H_{n}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}

and
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}||\chi_{n}||\leq 1

\chi\colon\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{n}\}\to\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{n}\}

\chi_{|H_{n}}=\chi_{n}

is bounded by
C
.

(iii):

For any sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the unit ball of

\mu\colon\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{n}\}\to\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{n}\}

\mu_{|H_{n}}=w_{n}\penalty\ Id_{H_{n}}
is bounded by

.

(iv):

For any finite subset
\sigma

\mathbb{N}
, the linear function
P_{\sigma}\colon\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{n}\}\to\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{n}\}
such that

P_{\sigma}(x):=\begin{cases}x\quad\text{if}&\quad x\in H_{j},j\in\sigma\\

is bounded by
\frac{1}{6}\penalty\ C^{2}
.

Let
M=(M^{1},\dots,M^{d})
be a
d
-tuple of matrices of size
m
, and let
\phi
be a function in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
. In order for

\phi(M)=\phi(M^{1},\dots,M^{d})

to makes sense the matrices in
M
must be
commuting
, so that the value of a bounded analytic function at
M
can be expressed via the matrix version of Cauchy integral formula:

(1.9)

\phi(M):=\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{d})(\xi^{1}\penalty\ Id-M^{1})^{-1}\dots(\xi^{d}\penalty\ Id-M^{d})^{-1}\,d\xi^{1}\dots d\xi^{d},

where
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is the
d
-dimensional torus. As for the one dimensional case, (
1.9
) makes sense provided that the
joint spectrum
of
M
belongs to
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. In order to define the joint spectrum of
M
, recall that commuting matrices preserve each others invariant sub-spaces: consequently,
M
can be
jointly upper-triangularizable
, that is, there exits an
m\times m
non singular matrix
P
such that

(1.10)

N^{i}=P^{-1}M^{i}P

is upper triangular, for any
i=1,\dots,d
. The joint spectrum of
M
is the (multi)-set

\sigma(M)=\{(N^{1}_{j,j},\dots,N^{d}_{j,j})\,|\,j=1,\dots,d\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}.

In particular,
\lambda=(\lambda^{1},\dots,\lambda^{d})
belongs to
\sigma(M)
if and only if there exists a vector
x
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
that is a joint eigenvector for the components of
M
:

M^{i}x=\lambda^{i}x\qquad i=1,\dots,d.

Definition 1.8
.

A sequence
A=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
d
-tuples of commuting matrices with joint spectra in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is
interpolating
for
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
if for any bounded target sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
there exists a bounded analytic function
\phi
on the polydisc such that

\phi(A_{n})=\phi_{n}(A_{n})\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

In order to separate the matrices in
A
we will extend (
1.8
) to this matrix setting by defining the Gleason distance
\rho_{G}
between two
d
-tuples of commuting matrices
M
and
N
(of eventual different dimension) to be the supremum of all
|r|>0
such that there exists a function
\phi
in the unit ball of
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
so that

\phi(N)=0,\qquad\phi(M)=r\penalty\ Id.

We will say then that the sequence
A
is
weakly separated
if

\inf_{n\neq j}\rho_{G}(A_{n},A_{j})>0,

whereas (
1.6
) extends to define
strongly separated
sequences of commuting
d
-tuples by

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\prod_{j\neq n}\rho_{G}(A_{n},A_{j})>0.

If
\tau
is any subset of
\mathbb{N}
not containing
n
, then the distance between
A_{n}
and
A_{\tau}:=(A_{j})_{j\in\tau}
is the inverse of the least norm of a function in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
that vanishes on
(A_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and it’s the identity on
A_{n}
:

(1.11)

\rho_{G}(A_{n},A_{\tau}):=\frac{1}{\inf\{||\phi||_{\infty}\,|\,\phi(A_{n})=Id,\phi(A_{j})=0,j\in\tau\}}.

As for the one dimensional case, one can relate interpolation conditions for the sequence
A
to separation condition on well chosen sub-spaces of some reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, and the main difference with the theory in one variable is that one has to consider
all
the kernels in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, rather than only the Szegö kernel. Let then
k
be a kernel in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, and let
M
be a
d
-tuple of commuting matrices with joint spectra in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. Assume that a function
f
in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
vanishes at
M
: without loss of generality, we can assume thanks to (
1.10
) that each
M^{i}
is upper triangular. Let
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\}
be the joint spectrum of
M
. For any
\xi
in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,

(1.12)

(\xi^{i}\penalty\ Id-M^{i})^{-1}

has on its main diagonal multiples of

\frac{1}{(\xi^{i}-z^{i}_{j})}\qquad j=1,\dots,d

while on its second to main diagonal has a linear combination of factors of the form

\frac{1}{(\xi^{i}-z^{i}_{j})^{2}}\qquad j=1,\dots,d,

where such linear combination depends exclusively on the algebraic structure of
M
, and not on the kernel
k
. In this fashion, the
l
-th-to-main diagonal of (
1.12
) will contain linear combinations of terms of the form

\frac{1}{(\xi^{i}-z^{i}_{j})^{l}}\qquad j=1,\dots,d.

Hence, thanks to (
1.9
),
f(M)=0
if and only if
f
vanishes to its joint spectrum, and so do some linear combinations of its partial derivatives at the points of its joint spectrum.

Example 1.9
.

Let
\lambda
and
\gamma
be in
\mathbb{D}
, and set

M=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&2\\
0&\lambda\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\gamma&1\\
0&\gamma\end{bmatrix}\right).

Then the joint spectrum of
M
consists in just the point
z=(\lambda,\gamma)
in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
and, for any
f
holomorphic in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
,

f(M)=\begin{bmatrix}f(z)&2\partial_{1}f(z)+\partial_{2}f(z)\\
0&f(z)\end{bmatrix}.

Thus
f(M)=0
if and only both
f(z)=0
and
2\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^{1}}(z)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^{2}}(z)=0.

Let
A=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of commuting
d
-tuples with joint spectra in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. For any positive integer
n
and for any kernel
k
in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, define

(1.13)

H_{n}^{k}:=\mathcal{H}_{k}\ominus\{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}|f(A_{n})=0\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}.

By the above discussion
H^{k}_{n}
is a finite dimensional sub-space of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
spanned by the kernels at the points of the joint spectrum of
A_{n}
, together with some linear combinations of kernel functions that represent partial derivatives at the joint spectrum of
A_{n}
. Moreover, if the matrices in
A_{n}
have size
s_{n}
, then

H^{k}_{n}=\{K_{A_{n}}(u,v)\,|\,u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{s_{n}}\},

where, if
(e_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is any orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
,
K_{A_{n}}(u,v)
is defined by

(1.14)

K_{A_{n}}(u,v):=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\braket{v,e_{j}(A_{n})u}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}e_{j}.

In particular, the function
K_{A_{n}}(u,v)
satisfies

(1.15)

\begin{split}\braket{f,K_{A_{n}}(u,v)}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}&=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\braket{e_{j}(A_{n})u,v}_{\mathbb{C}^{s_{n}}}\braket{f,e_{j}}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\\
&=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\braket{\braket{f,e_{j}}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}e_{j}(A_{n})u,v}_{\mathbb{C}^{s_{n}}}\\
&=\left<\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\braket{f,e_{j}}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}e_{j}(A_{n})\right)u,v\right>_{\mathbb{C}^{s_{n}}}\\
&=\braket{f(A_{n})u,v}_{\mathbb{C}^{s_{n}}}\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\end{split}

which says that (
1.14
) does not depend on the choice of the basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Equation (
1.15
) works as a
reproducing property
for
K_{A_{n}}(u,v)
and implies that
K_{A_{n}}(u,v)
is linear in
v
and conjugate-linear in
u
. Most importantly, (
1.15
) implies that

(1.16)

M_{\phi}^{*}(K_{A_{n}}(u,v))=K_{A_{n}}(u,\phi(A_{n})^{*}v)\qquad\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}.

It is worth noticing that, for any positive integer
n
, some of the
K_{A_{n}}(u,v)
might repeat while varying
u
and
v
. The following Lemma describes the relation on the pairs
(u_{1},v_{1})
and
(u_{2},v_{2})
in order for this to happen. If
l
is a multi-index in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, and
N
is a
d
-tuple of commuting matrices, we will write

N^{l}:=(N^{1})^{l_{1}}\dots(N^{d})^{l_{d}}.

Lemma 1.10
.

Let
k
be a kernel in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, and let
N
be a
d
-tuple of
m\times m
commuting matrices with spectra in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
. Let
u_{1},v_{1},u_{2}
and
v_{2}
be in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
. Then
K_{N}(u_{1},v_{1})=K_{N}(u_{2},v_{2})
if and only if

(1.17)

\braket{N^{l}u_{1},v_{1}}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\braket{N^{l}u_{2},v_{2}}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\qquad l\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
K_{N}(u_{1},v_{1})=K_{N}(u_{2},v_{2})
. Thus, since each monic monomial
z^{l}
belongs to
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{d}
and thanks to (
1.16
),

K_{N}(u_{1},(N^{l})^{*}v_{1})=M^{*}_{z^{l}}(K_{N}(u_{1},v_{1}))=M^{*}_{z^{l}}(K_{N}(u_{2},v_{2}))=K_{N}(u_{2},(N^{l})^{*}v_{2}).

Therefore, if
\phi
is a polynomial such that
\phi(N)=Id
, the reproducing property in (
1.15
) implies that

\braket{u_{1},(N^{l})^{*}v_{1}}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\braket{\phi,K_{N}(u_{1},(N^{l})^{*}v_{1})}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\braket{\phi,K_{N}(u_{2},(N^{l})^{*}v_{2})}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\braket{u_{2},(N^{l})^{*}v_{2}}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}.

Conversely, assume that (
1.17
) holds. We have that
K_{N}(u_{1},v_{1})=K_{N}(u_{2},v_{2})
if and only if their inner product with any
f
in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
coincide:

\braket{u_{1},f(N)^{*}v_{1}}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}=\braket{f,K_{N}(u_{1},v_{1})}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\braket{f,K_{N}(u_{2},v_{2})}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\braket{u_{2},f(N)^{*}v_{2}}_{\mathbb{C}^{m}}\qquad f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

The rightmost and the leftmost hand side of the above equation coincide, thanks to (
1.17
) and modulo writing
f
in its power series.
∎

Observe that (
1.17
) does
not
depend on the kernel
k
that we chose in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, but it depends exclusively on the algebraic structure of
M
.

Example 1.11
.

Let
M
be the pair of commuting matrices in Example
1.9
. If
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are the elements of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, then

\begin{split}&K_{M}(e_{1},e_{1})=K_{M}(e_{2},e_{2})=k_{z},\\
&K_{M}(e_{1},e_{2})=2k^{(1,0)}_{z}+k^{(0,1)}_{z}\\
&K_{M}(e_{2},e_{1})=0,\end{split}

where

k_{z}^{(1,0)}:=\frac{\partial k_{z}}{\partial{\overline{z}^{1}}}

and

k_{z}^{(0,1)}:=\frac{\partial k_{z}}{\partial{\overline{z}^{2}}}

are the kernels in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
that represent, respectively, one derivative in the first variable and one derivative in the second variable.

In Section
2
, we will extend Theorem
1.6
to pairs of commuting matrices by replacing normalized admissible kernels with the collections
(H^{k}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
:

Theorem 1.12
.

A sequence of pairs of commuting matrices is interpolating if and only if there exists a positive
C
such that, for any admissible kernel
k
on the bi-disc, the sequence

H^{k}=(H^{k}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is a Riesz system in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
with Riesz bound
C
.

Since the approach we use to study interpolating sequences of matrices is mainly defined by looking at the Euclidean geometry of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, and since we do not know whether
\mathcal{A}_{d}=\mathcal{B}_{d}
for
d\geq 3
, we can only give a (stronger) sufficient condition to replace (
1.6
) in the case of the bi-disc:

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
A=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of pairs of commuting matrices with spectra in

such that

(1.18)

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{G}(A_{n},A_{\mathbb{N}\setminus n})>0.

Then
A
is interpolating.

Let us observe that (
1.18
) is a rather strong separation condition on
A=(A_{n})_{n\in N}
. In order to extend the sufficient condition in Theorem
1.4
, one has would have to give a positive answer to the following question:

Question 1
.

Is
A
interpolating, provided that

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\prod_{j\neq n}\rho_{G}(A_{n},A_{j})>0?

This remain, for us, open.

will show that, given any sequence
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
and any

, there exists an interpolating sequence

of

-tuples of commuting matrices such that each

has

coordinates.

1.2.
Interpolating
d
-tuples of Non-Commuting Matrices

In order to study interpolating properties of a sequence of eventually non-commuting
d
-tuples of matrices, one has to change the class of functions to apply to such a sequence. Specifically, the robust and highly active field of
non-commutative (NC) functions
is the environment in which the discussion of Section
3
will take place.

Fix
d
in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. For any positive integer
n
, let
\mathbb{M}^{1}_{n}
be the set of all
n\times n
matrices with coefficient in
\mathbb{C}
and, more generally, let
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{n}
be the set of all
d
-tuples of square matrices of size
n
. If
d=\infty
, we require that the row norm

(1.19)

||X||:=\left|\left|\sum_{i}X^{i}(X^{i})^{*}\right|\right|

is bounded for any
X
in
\mathbb{M}^{\infty}_{n}
. Each
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{n}
is normed with (
1.19
) and endowed with the induced topology. Let us consider now arbitrarily large sizes by defining

\mathbb{M}_{d}:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{M}^{d}_{n}

as the disjoint union in
n
of
d
-tuples of
n\times n
matrices. A topology of interest for us is the so-called
disjoint union (DU) topology
: a subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{M}_{d}
is open if and only if all of its
n
components

\Omega(n):=\Omega\cap\mathbb{M}^{d}_{n}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}

are open in
\mathbb{M}^{d}_{n}
. Moreover,
\Omega
is a
non-commutative (NC) set
if it is closed under direct sums:

Z,W\in\Omega\implies Z\oplus W\in\Omega.

A class of functions that can be defined on such an
\Omega
is
\mathbb{C}\braket{z_{1},\dots,z_{d}}
, the set of all
free polynomials
in
d
non-commuting variables. More generally, a function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{M}_{1}
is a
non-commutative (NC) function
if

•

f
is
graded
: if
Z
is in
\Omega(n)
, then
f(Z)
belongs to
\mathbb{M}^{1}_{n}
;

•

f

respects direct sums
: for any
Z
and
W
in
\Omega
, then
f(Z\oplus W)=f(Z)\oplus f(W)
;

•

f

respects similarities
: for any
Z
in
\Omega(n)
and for any invertible
P
in
\mathbb{M}^{1}_{n}
, then
f(P^{-1}ZP)=P^{-1}f(Z)P
, provided that
P^{-1}ZP
belongs to
\Omega
.

One can easily check that any NC polynomial is an NC function on
\Omega
. An NC function
f
is
holomorphic
on
\Omega
if it is locally bounded.

The domain of interest for us in order to extend interpolation results to this NC setting is the
NC unit ball

\mathcal{B}_{d}:=\left\{Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}\,\big|\,||Z||<1\right\},

and the interpolating functions will be chosen from the algebra of
bounded NC analytic functions

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}:=\{f\quad\text{NC holomorphic on}\,\mathcal{B}_{d}\quad|\quad||f||_{\infty}<\infty\},

where

||f||_{\infty}:=\sup_{Z\in\mathcal{B}_{d}}||f(Z)||.

Definition
1.2
adapts also to this non-commutative setting: a sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
(of eventually non-constant dimensions) is
interpolating
for
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
if for any bounded sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
there exists a function
\phi
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
such that

\phi(Z_{n})=\phi_{n}(Z_{n})\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

As for the well-known scalar case, the understanding of NC interpolating sequences goes through the study of related Hilbert spaces. Let
\mathbb{W}_{d}
be the set of words with
d
generators, and let

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}:=\left\{f=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{W}_{d}}a_{l}Z^{l}\quad\bigg|\quad||f||_{2}^{2}:=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{W}_{d}}|a_{l}|^{2}<\infty\right\}

be the so-called
NC Drury Arveson space
of those formal NC power series with square-summable coefficients. It turns out that
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
can be seen as an
NC reproducing kernel Hilbert space
. More specifically, for any
W
in
\mathcal{B}_{d}(n)
and for any pairs of vectors
u
and
v
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, let

(1.20)

K_{W}(u,v)(Z):=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{W}_{d}}\braket{v,W^{l}u}Z^{l}\qquad Z\in\mathcal{B}.

The function
K_{W}(u,v)
is linear in
v
and conjugate-linear in
u
. Salomon, Shalit and Shamovich showed in
[
13
, Prop. 3.2]
that
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
is generated by the NC functions in (
1.20
), and that for any
f
in
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{2}
and for any
W
in
\mathcal{B}_{d}(n)

(1.21)

\braket{f,K_{W}(u,z)}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}}=\braket{f(W)u,v}_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}.

Moreover, the multiplier algebra of
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{2}

\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}}:=\{\phi\,\,\text{NC holomorphic function on}\,\mathcal{B}_{d}\,|\,\phi f\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{2},f\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{2}\}

is isometrically identifiable with
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
,
[
13
, Coro. 3.6]
, and the reproducing property in (
1.21
) implies that

M_{\phi}^{*}(K_{W}(u,v))=K_{W}(u,\phi(W)^{*}v)\qquad\phi\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}.

Similarly to Section
2
, in Section
3
we will describe interpolating sequences in terms of
separated sequences
. Moreover, we will see how to separated elements in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
correspond separated sub-spaces of
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
. Specifically, the
NC Gleason distance
between two elements
Z
and
W
of
\mathcal{B}_{d}
is the inverse of the least norm of an
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
NC function that vanishes at
W
and that is the identity at
Z
:

\rho_{NC}(Z,W):=\frac{1}{\inf\{||\phi||_{\infty}\,|\,\phi(Z)=Id,\phi(W)=0\}}.

As in (
1.11
), we might also want to separate
Z
from a whole sequence
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
via

\rho_{NC}(Z,(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}):=\frac{1}{\inf\{||\phi||_{\infty}\,|\,\phi(Z)=Id,\phi(W_{n})=0,n\in\mathbb{N}\}}.

We will see in Section
3
how an analogue of the Pick property for the NC Drury-Arveson space implies that separation conditions on a sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
correspond to separated sequences of sub-spaces of
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
defined as

(1.22)

\mathcal{H}_{n}:=\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}\ominus\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}\,|\,f(Z_{n})=0\}.

Since the right hand side of the reproducing property (
1.21
) vanishes for any
u
and
v
if and only if
f(W)=0
, we have that,

(1.23)

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\{K_{Z_{n}}(u,v)\,|\,u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{n}}\},

where we assume that
Z_{n}
belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{d}(m_{n})
for any
n
.

Interpolating sequences can be then characterized via separation conditions in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
:

Theorem 1.14
.

Z
is interpolating if and only if the sequence
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Riesz system in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
.

As a consequence, we will show that a separation condition on the sequence
Z
is sufficient for it to be interpolating:

Theorem 1.15
.

If

(1.24)

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{NC}(Z_{n},(Z_{j})_{j\neq n})>0

then
Z
is interpolating.

Condition (
1.24
) is a rather strong separation condition on the sequence
Z
. It would be interesting for us to know whether such condition can be relaxed:

Question 2
.

Is any sequence
Z
in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
satisfying

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\prod_{j\neq n}\rho_{NC}(Z_{n},Z_{j})>0

an interpolating sequence?

A positive answer to Question
2
would extend a result from Berndtsson in
[
5
]
to this non commutative setting.

An even more ambitious goal would be to prove an analogue of Carleson interpolation Theorem for the noncommutative setting. In particular, we ask whether strongly separated sequences are interpolating, and whether weakly separated sequences whose sequence
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Bessel system are interpolating:

Question 3
.

Is
Z
interpolating, provided that

\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\rho_{NC}(Z_{n},(Z_{j})_{j\neq n})>0?

Question 4
.

Is
Z
interpolating, provided that

\inf_{n\neq j}\rho_{NC}(Z_{n},Z_{j})>0

and
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Bessel system?

An example of an interpolating sequence of pairs of non-commuting matrices in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
will be given in Section
3.2
.

2.1.
Admissible Kernels

The key step for the proof of Theorem
1.12
is to extend Agler’s argument in
[
1
]
to the matrix case. Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
be finitely many
d
-tuples of commuting matrices with spectra in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, of eventually different sizes
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
. Given the formal sets

K_{j}:=\{K_{j}(u,v)|u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{s_{j}}\}\qquad j=1,\dots,N

define an equivalence relation
\sim_{j}
on
K_{j}
that makes the elements in
K_{j}
linear in
v
, conjugate linear in
u
, and that sets
K_{j}(u_{1},v_{1})\sim_{j}K_{j}(u_{2},v_{2})
if and only if

\braket{A_{j}^{l}u_{1},v_{1}}_{\mathbb{C}^{s_{j}}}=\braket{A_{j}^{l}u_{2},v_{2}}_{\mathbb{C}^{s_{j}}}\qquad l\in\mathbb{N}^{d}.

Let

F_{j}:=K_{j}/\sim_{j}\qquad j=1,\dots,N

and let
F
be the set of formal linear combinations of the elements of
\cup_{j=1}^{N}F_{j}
. Since each
F_{j}
is a finite dimensional vector space, so is
F
. A
kernel

\tilde{k}
on
F
is a choice of a strictly positive scalar product on
F
. Let
F_{\tilde{k}}
be the Hilbert space
(F,\tilde{k})
. In this finite-nodes setting,
\tilde{k}
is
admissible
if, for any
i=1,\dots,d
, the linear map
T^{\tilde{k}}_{i}\colon F_{\tilde{k}}\to F_{\tilde{k}}
defined by

(2.1)

T^{\tilde{k}}_{i}([K_{j}(u,v)]_{\sim_{j}}):=[K_{j}(u,(A_{j}^{i})^{*}v)]_{\sim_{j}}

is a contraction. Observe that each
T^{\tilde{k}}_{i}
is well defined thanks to Lemma
1.10
. In particular, if
k
is an admissible kernel on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, then

\mathrm{span}\{H^{k}_{A_{j}}\,|\,j=1\dots,N\}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}

is isometric to some
F_{\tilde{k}}
. In other words,
F_{\tilde{k}}
works as a
restriction
of an admissible kernel to the finite nodes
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
. For the sake of notation,
k
will indicate both a kernel on
\mathbb{D}^{d}
and the scalar product on
F
which restricts
k
to our finite nodes of
d
-tuples of matrices.

Let
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}
be targets in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
, and suppose that there exists a contraction
\phi
in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
such that

\phi(A_{j})=\phi_{j}(A_{j})\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

Then, thanks to (
1.16
), for any kernel in
k
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}
the map
R^{k}\colon\mathrm{span}\{H^{k}_{A_{j}}\,|\,j=1,\dots,N\}\to\mathrm{span}\{H^{k}_{A_{j}}\,|\,j=1,\dots,N\}
given by

(2.2)

R^{k}(K_{A_{j}}(u,v))=K_{A_{j}}(u,\phi_{j}(A_{j})^{*}v)\qquad j=1,\dots,N

is a restriction of
M^{*}_{\phi}
, and therefore is a contraction. Conversely,

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N}
be in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
such that, if
R^{k}
is defined as in (
2.2
),

(2.3)

\sup_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{2}}||R^{k}||\leq 1.

Then there exists a function
\phi
in
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{2}
such that
||\phi||_{\infty}\leq 1
and

\phi(A_{j})=\phi_{j}(A_{j})\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

Theorem
2.1
works as an extension property for all admissible kernels, provided that the necessary bounds are uniform in

The proof of Theorem

has a strong operator theory flavour: the first main tool is the following Lemma, due to Parrot:

Lemma 2.2

(Parrot’s Lemma)
.

Let

and
\mathcal{K}_{i}

, be Hilbert spaces, Let

C&D\end{bmatrix}\colon\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathcal{K}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{2}.

\sup_{D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{2})}||W_{D}||=\max\left\{\left|\left|\begin{bmatrix}A\\
C\end{bmatrix}\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{2})}\,,\,\left|\left|\begin{bmatrix}A&B\end{bmatrix}\right|\right|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{1})}\right\}.

For a proof, see
[

The second technical tool can be proved for any

is strictly contained in

be the restriction on

of the orthogonal projection

onto

As we already pointed out,

is isometric to a finite admissible kernel structure

Observe that

is an admissible kernel structure on the

\tilde{T}^{k}_{i}

For any

follows then from Theorem

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

&\mathcal{F}:=H_{1}\ominus H_{0}.\end{split}

Remark 2.5
.

As a main consequence of Theorem
2.1
, the Gleason distance

between two pairs of commuting matrices corresponds to the sine of the least angle between

and

, where

ranges among all admissible kernels in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Indeed, if

in

has norm

and separates

and

then for any admissible kernel

the operator

acts like the identity on

and like the zero operator on

. Since

, the angle between

and

.

Conversely, if

2.1
(

) implies that there exists a function
\phi
whose

that separates

and

.

2.3.
A More Explicit Sufficient Condition

In this Section we will prove Theorem

. Thanks to Remark

, condition (

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sin\left(H^{k}_{n},\underset{j\neq n}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{j}^{k}\}\right)

is uniformly bounded below for any
k
in
A_{2}
. Thanks to Theorem
1.12
, it suffices then to show that any sequence
H=(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of closed subspaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
satisfying (
2.7
) is a Riesz system. In order to do so, we will use Theorem
1.7
, (i)
\iff
(iv). Fix a finite subset
\sigma
of
\mathbb{N}
, and enumerate the elements in
\sigma^{c}
by
\{j_{1},j_{2},\dots\}
. For the sake of brevity, let
H_{\sigma}:=\underset{j\in\sigma}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{H_{j}\}
and

S_{i}:=\mathrm{span}\{H_{\sigma},H_{j_{1}},\dots,H_{j_{i}}\}\qquad i\in\mathbb{N}.

Namely, if
S_{0}=H_{\sigma}
, then each
S_{i}
is obtained by adding a subspace not labeled in
\sigma
to the linear span of
S_{i-1}
. Let
P_{0}
be the identity on
H_{\sigma}
, and define
P_{i}\colon S_{i}\to S_{i}
by

P_{i}(x):=\begin{cases}P_{i-1}(x)\quad&\text{if}\quad x\in S_{i-1}\\
0\quad&\text{if}\quad x\in H_{j_{i}}\end{cases}.

Thanks to Theorem
1.7
, (i)
\iff
(iv), we need to prove the following

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of closed sub-spaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that

(2.8)

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sin(H_{n},H_{\mathbb{N}\setminus\{n\}})>0

Then

(2.9)

\sup_{\sigma\,\text{finite}}\lim_{i\to\infty}||P_{i}||<\infty.

This will be done by analyzing how much does the operator norm increase when extending
P_{i}
to
P_{i+1}
:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
H
and
F
be two closed subspaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
that intersect trivially. Let
d
be the distance (equivalently, the sine of the angle) between
H
and
K
, and let
T
be any bounded operator from
H
to itself. Then the operator
\tilde{T}\colon\mathrm{span}\{H,F\}\to\mathrm{span}\{H,F\}
such that

\tilde{T}(x):=\begin{cases}T(x)\quad\text{if}&\quad x\in H\\
0\quad\text{if}&\quad x\in F\end{cases}

extends
T
and has norm

||\tilde{T}||\leq\frac{1}{d}||T||.

Proof.

Let
G:=\mathrm{span}\{H,F\}\ominus H
be the orthogonal complement of
H
in
\mathrm{span}\{H,F\}
, and fix a unit vector
x
in
\mathrm{span}\{H,F\}
. Then
x
can be written uniquely as

x=\alpha y+\beta z\qquad y\in G\quad z\in H,

where
y
and
z
are unit vectors and
|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
, thanks to orthogonality. Moreover,
y
can be written as

y=h+f\qquad h\in H\quad f\in F,

where

||h||^{2}=\frac{1-\sin^{2}(f,H)}{\sin^{2}(f,H)}\leq\frac{1-d^{2}}{d^{2}}

and
\tilde{T}(y)=\tilde{T}(h)=T(h)
. Therefore, if
r:=\sqrt{1-d^{2}}/d
, we have

\begin{split}||\tilde{T}(x)||^{2}=&||T(\alpha h+\beta z)||^{2}\\
\leq&||T||^{2}\penalty\ ||\alpha h+\beta z||^{2}\\
=&||T||^{2}\penalty\ (\alpha^{2}r^{2}+\beta^{2}+2\alpha\overline{\beta}\mathrm{Re}\braket{h,z})\\
\leq&||T||^{2}\penalty\ (\alpha^{2}r^{2}+\beta^{2}+2|\alpha||\beta|r)\\
=&||T||^{2}(r|\alpha|+|\beta|)^{2}.\end{split}

Thus

||\tilde{T}||\leq||T||\penalty\ \sup_{|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1}(r|\alpha|+|\beta|)=||T||\penalty\ \sqrt{1+r^{2}}=\frac{||T||}{d}.

∎

In particular,

||P_{n}||^{2}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(H_{j_{i}},S_{i})}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(H_{i},H_{\mathbb{N}\setminus\{i\}})}

is uniformly bounded in

(and
\sigma
) if

is bounded below, and Proposition
2.7
follows. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.13
.

2.4.
An Example via Random Interpolating Sequences in the Polydisc

In certain instances, considering a random sequence (of scalars) in the polydisc can help to understand the conditions in Theorem
1.4
and their relation. This is the motivation that led the authors in
[
11
]
to study
random interpolating sequences
. A random sequence in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is a sequence with pre-fixed deterministic radii and random arguments in
\mathbb{T}^{d}
: let
(\theta^{1}_{n},\dots,\theta^{d}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent and indentically distributed random variables taking values on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, all distributed uniformly and defined on the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. Let
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
[0,1)^{d}
, and define a random sequence
\Lambda=(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{D}^{d}
as

\lambda_{n}(\omega)=\left(r^{1}_{n}e^{i\theta^{1}_{n}(\omega)},\dots,r^{d}_{n}e^{i\theta^{d}_{n}(\omega)}\right),\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

Looking for separation conditions on
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that yield almost sure interpolating properties for
\Lambda
, partition
\mathbb{D}^{d}
into the following dyadic radial rectangles

I_{m}:=\{z\in\mathbb{D}^{d}:1-2^{-m_{i}}\leq|z^{i}|<1-2^{-(m_{i}+1)},i=1,\dots d\}

and count the number of points of
\Lambda
that fall into each
I_{m}
:

N_{m}=|\Lambda\cap I_{m}|,

for any multi-index
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})
in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. Observe that
(N_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
depends exclusively on the sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and therefore it is a deterministic quantity. The smaller the sequence
(N_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
is, the fastest the random sequence
\Lambda
escapes any compact subset of the polydisc, and therefore (at least intuitively) the most chances it has to be interpolating. This is the idea behind
[
11
, Coro 1.4]
( here
|m|=m_{1}+\dots+m_{d}
will denote the length of
m
) :

Theorem 2.9
.

If

(2.10)

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}N_{m}^{1+\frac{1}{d}}2^{-\frac{|m|}{d}}<\infty,

then
\Lambda
is interpolating almost surely. If

\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}N_{m}^{2}2^{-|m|}=\infty,

then
\Lambda
is not interpolating almost surely.

Random interpolating sequences help to construct examples of interpolating sequences of
d
-tuples of commuting matrices
of any dimensions
. Let indeed
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive integeres, and choose, for any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
,
m_{n}
points
\tau_{n,1},\dots,\tau_{n,m_{n}}
on the
d
-torus. A sequence of
d
-tuples of commuting matrices having those points as their joint spectra is
W=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

W_{n}^{i}:=\mathrm{diag}(\tau_{n,1}^{i},\dots,\tau_{n,m_{n}}^{i}).

In order for the joint spectra to belong to
\mathbb{D}^{d}
, let us re-scale the matrices in
W
via a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
[0,1)^{d}
:

A_{n}:=(r_{n}^{1}W_{n}^{1},\dots,r^{d}_{n}W_{n}^{d})\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

The more sparse the sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the more separated the joint spectra of the matrices in
A:=(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are. It is natural then to ask if there is a choice of the radii
(r_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that makes the sequence
A
interpolating, for some choice of the sequence
T:=(\tau_{n,j})
. Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
and let

r_{n}^{i}=1-2^{-\alpha_{n}^{i}}.

If the point in
T
are uniformly randomly chosen
\mathbb{T}^{d}
independently one from the other and if

(2.11)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}m_{n}^{1+\frac{1}{d}}\penalty\ 2^{-\frac{(\alpha_{n}^{i}+\dots+\alpha_{n}^{d})}{d}}<\infty

then the collection
Z
of all joint eigenvalues of
A
satisfies (
2.10
) and therefore Z is interpolating almost surely, thanks to Theorem
2.9
. Fixed
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, it will suffice then to choose the sequence
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverging fast enough in order for (
2.11
) to hold. This will give a whole family of possible choices of the parameters in
T
for which
Z
(and hence
A
) is interpolating. In particular, there exists a sequence of interpolating
d
-tuples of commuting matrices given any choice for their dimensions.

3.
Non Commuting
d
-tuples

The aim of this Section is to prove Theorem
1.14
and Theorem
1.15
.

This will be done in Section
3.1
by showing that the NC Drury-Arveson space has an extension property which works as analogue of the Pick property of the classic Drury-Arveson space.

Section
3.2
will then consider an explicit example of a class of sequences of pairs of
2\times 2
non commuting matrices and it will characterize those that are interpolating.

3.1.
The NC Pick Property

Salomon, Shalit and Shamovich proved in
[
13
, Th. 4.7]
that
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
has the following non-commutative version of the Pick property: let
\Omega
be a subset of
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, and let
\overline{\Omega}^{nc}
be its NC-envelop, that is, the smallest NC set containing
\Omega
that is also closed under left intertwiners:

W\in\overline{\Omega}^{nc}\implies P^{-1}WP\in\overline{\Omega}^{nc}.

Suppose
f_{0}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{M}_{1}
is an NC function that extends to an NC function on
\overline{\Omega}^{nc}
and suppose that the map

R_{0}\colon\mathrm{span}\{K_{W}(u,v)\,|\,W\in\Omega\}\to\mathrm{span}\{K_{W}(u,v)\,|\,W\in\Omega\}

such that, for any
W
in
\Omega(n)
,

R_{0}(K_{W}(u,v))=K_{W}(u,f_{0}(W)^{*}v)\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

is a contraction. Then
f_{0}
extends to a contractive multiplier on
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, that is, there exists a function
f
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
whose norm doesn’t exceed
1
such that
f_{|\Omega}=f_{0}
.

With this in mind we can partially extend Theorem
2.6
to this non-commutative setting, and prove Theorem
1.14
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Z=(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{B}_{d}
. The following are equivalent:

(i):

Z
is interpolating;

(ii):

For any bounded
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{C}
there exists a function
\phi
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
such that

(3.1)

\phi(Z_{n})=w_{n}\penalty\ Id\qquad n\in\mathbb{N};

(iii):

The sequence
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (
1.22
) is a Riesz System.

Proof.

The implication (i)
\implies
(ii) is trivial, as constant functions belong to
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
. Moreover, (ii)
\implies
(iii) follows from Theorem
1.7
, since for any
\phi
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
satisfying (
3.1
) the restriction of
M^{*}_{\phi}
to
\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{H}_{n}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a bounded linear operator that acts like
w_{n}\penalty\ Id
on each
\mathcal{H}_{n}
. To conclude, it suffices to show then that (iii)
\implies
(i). To do so, let
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
. There exists an NC function
\phi_{0}\colon\overline{Z}^{nc}\to\mathbb{M}_{1}
such that

\phi_{0}(Z_{n})=\phi_{n}(Z_{n})\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

since each
\phi_{n}
respects direct sums and left intertwiners. Since
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, so is the sequence of operator norms
(||R_{n}||)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

R_{n}:=(M^{*}_{\phi_{n}})_{|\mathcal{H}_{n}}

is the linear map from
\mathcal{H}_{n}
to itself such that

R_{n}(K_{Z_{n}}(u,v))=K_{Z_{n}}(u,\phi_{n}(Z_{n})^{*}v)=K_{Z_{n}}(u,\phi_{0}^{*}(Z_{n})v).

Thanks to Theorem
1.7
, the map
R_{0}
from
\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{H}_{n}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
to itself such that

R_{0}(K_{Z_{n}}(u,v))=K_{Z_{n}}(u,\phi_{0}^{*}(Z_{n})v)

is bounded, and by the NC Pick property there exists a multiplier
\phi
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
such that

\phi(Z_{n})=\phi_{0}(Z_{n})=\phi_{n}(Z_{n})\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},

concluding the proof.
∎

As for the commuting case, we would like to point out how the a-priori weaker interpolation property in (ii) is in fact equivalent for
Z
to be interpolating.

Theorem
1.14
is not the only main consequence of the NC Pick property of the NC Drury-Arveson space. Indeed, since the existence of a function
\phi
in
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{d}
of norm
M
separating two points in
\mathcal{B}_{d}

\phi(Z)=Id\qquad\phi(W)=0

is equivalent to the operator
R\colon\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{H}_{W},\mathcal{H}_{Z}\}\to\mathrm{span}\{\mathcal{H}_{W},\mathcal{H}_{Z}\}
such that

R_{|\mathcal{H}_{Z}}=Id\qquad R_{|\mathcal{H}_{W}}=0

being bounded by
M
, one has that the Gleason NC distance between
Z
and
W
coincide with the sine of the angle between
\mathcal{H}_{Z}
and
\mathcal{H}_{W}
in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{d}
:

\rho_{NC}(Z,W)=\sin(\mathcal{H}_{Z},\mathcal{H}_{W}).

As a consequence, (
1.24
) can be re-written as

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sin(\mathcal{H}_{n},\underset{j\neq n}{\overline{\mathrm{span}}}\{\mathcal{H}_{j}\})>0,

and Theorem
1.15
follows from Theorem
1.14
and Proposition
2.7
.

3.2.
An Example

We give here an example of an interpolating sequence of pairs of non-commuting matrices. Let
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be two sequences of non-zero complex numbers, and define the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{M}_{2}
as

Z_{n}^{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\alpha_{n}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\quad Z_{n}^{2}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
\beta_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then
Z_{n}^{1}
and
Z_{n}^{2}
do not commute for any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
, and since

Z_{n}^{1}(Z_{n}^{1})^{*}+Z_{n}^{2}(Z_{n}^{2})^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}|\alpha_{n}|^{2}&0\\
0&|\beta_{n}|^{2}\end{bmatrix}

we have that
Z
belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{2}
if and only if any
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})
belongs to the bi-disc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. We claim that a separation condition on the sequence
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
encodes all the cases in which
Z
is an NC interpolating sequence:

Theorem 3.2
.

Z
is interpolating if and only if
(\alpha_{n}\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is interpolating in
\mathbb{D}
.

Thanks to Theorem
3.1
, we need to study separation conditions of the subspaces
(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{2}
, and according to (
1.23
) this is equivalent to studying the NC kernel functions of the form
(K_{Z_{n}}(u_{n},v_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, for any sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Observe that

(Z_{n}^{i})^{2}=0\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},i=1,2

and therefore, out of all the coefficients of the NC function

(3.2)

K_{Z_{n}}(u_{n},v_{n})(Z)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{W}_{2}}\braket{v_{n},Z_{n}^{l}u_{n}}Z^{l}\qquad Z\in\mathcal{B}_{2},

the only non zero ones are the ones associated to a word which alternates its two letters
a
and
b
. Such words can be written in four different ways:

\begin{split}\omega&=(ab)^{l}\qquad l\in\mathbb{N}\\
\omega&=(ba)^{l}\qquad l\in\mathbb{N}\\
\omega&=a(ba)^{l}\qquad l\in\mathbb{N}\\
\omega&=b(ab)^{l}\qquad l\in\mathbb{N},\end{split}

depending on its lenght and its first and last letter. Since

\begin{split}Z_{n}^{1}Z_{n}^{2}&=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\quad Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n}^{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}\end{bmatrix}\\
Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n}^{1}Z_{n}^{2}&=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
\beta_{n}\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}\quad Z_{n}^{1}Z_{n}^{2}Z_{n}^{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}\alpha_{n}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

we can split the NC power series in (
3.2
) into four
orthogonal
pieces

\begin{split}K_{Z_{n}}(u_{n},v_{n})(Z)=&\braket{v_{n},u_{n}}+v_{n}^{1}\overline{u_{n}^{1}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}(Z^{1}Z^{2})^{l}+v_{n}^{2}\overline{u_{n}^{2}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}(Z^{2}Z^{1})^{l}+\\
+&\overline{\alpha_{n}}v_{n}^{1}\overline{u_{n}^{2}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}Z^{1}(Z^{2}Z^{1})^{l}+\overline{\beta_{n}}v_{n}^{2}\overline{u_{n}^{1}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}Z^{2}(Z^{1}Z^{2})^{l}\\
=&v_{n}^{1}\overline{u_{n}^{1}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}(Z^{1}Z^{2})^{l}+v_{n}^{2}\overline{u_{n}^{2}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}(Z^{2}Z^{1})^{l}+\\
+&\overline{\alpha_{n}}v_{n}^{1}\overline{u_{n}^{2}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}Z^{1}(Z^{2}Z^{1})^{l}+\overline{\beta_{n}}v_{n}^{2}\overline{u_{n}^{1}}\penalty\ \sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(\overline{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})^{l}Z^{2}(Z^{1}Z^{2})^{l}.\\
=&\sum_{m,j=1}^{2}v^{m}_{n}\overline{u_{n}^{j}}K_{Z_{n}}(e^{m},e^{j})(Z),\end{split}

where
\{e^{1},e^{2}\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

The sequence
(\hat{K}_{n}(e^{1},e^{1}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of normalized NC kernels can be unitarily identified with a multiple of the sequence
(s_{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Szegö kernels at the points of
(\alpha_{n}\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{D}
. Thus if
(\alpha_{n}\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not interpolating, the sequence
(\hat{s}_{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not a Reisz system, and therefore
(\hat{K}_{n}(e^{1},e^{1}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not a Riesz system either. Hence, thanks to Theorem
3.1
,
Z
is not interpolating.

Conversely, assume that
(\alpha_{n}\beta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an interpolating sequences in
\mathbb{D}
or, equivalently, that the associated sequence
(\hat{s}_{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Szegö kernels in
\mathrm{H}^{2}
is a Riesz system. Both the sequences
(\hat{K}_{Z_{n}}(e^{1},e^{1}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\hat{K}_{Z_{n}}(e^{2},e^{2}))
are untarly equivalent to
(\hat{s}_{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathrm{H}^{2}
, and hence are Riesz systems. The same holds for
(\hat{K}_{Z_{n}}(e^{1},e^{2}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\hat{K}_{z_{n}}(e^{2},e^{1}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, since they are both unitarly equivalent to a multiple of the sequence of shifted Szegö kernels
(zs_{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since the four sequences

(K_{Z_{n}}(u^{m},u^{j}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\qquad m,j=1,2

are pairwise orthogonal in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{2}
, we can conclude that the sequence of subspaces

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\{K_{Z_{n}}(u_{n},v_{n})\,|\,u_{n},v_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}

is a Riesz system in
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{2}
, thanks to Lemma
3.3
below.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
m
be a finite positive integer, and let
X^{i}:=(x^{i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
i=1,\dots,m
be
m
pairwise orthogonal sequences in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. If each
X^{i}
is a Riesz system, then so is the sequence
X=(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined as

x_{n}:=x^{1}_{n}+\dots+x^{m}_{n}\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
||x_{n}||=1
for any
n
in
\mathbb{N}
. Thus

x_{n}=t^{1}_{n}\hat{x}^{1}_{n}+\dots+t^{m}_{n}\hat{x}^{m}_{n},

where thanks to orthogonality

(3.3)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|t^{i}_{n}|^{2}=1\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Fix then an arbitrary
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
l^{2}
, and observe that

\left|\left|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}x_{n}\right|\right|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|\left|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}t^{i}_{n}\hat{x}^{i}_{n}\right|\right|^{2}.

Therefore, if
C_{i}
is the Riesz bound of each
X^{i}
,

\left|\left|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}x_{n}\right|\right|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}^{2}\penalty\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|t^{i}_{n}|^{2}|a_{n}|^{2}\leq\max_{i=1,\dots,m}C_{i}^{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|a_{n}|^{2}

and

\frac{1}{\underset{i=1,\dots,m}{\max}C_{i}^{2}}\penalty\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|a_{n}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{C_{i}^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|t_{n}^{i}|^{2}|a_{n}|^{2}\leq\left|\left|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a_{n}x_{n}\right|\right|^{2},

thanks to (
3.3
).
∎
