Mirror partner for a Klein quartic polynomial
Alexey Basalaev

Abstract
. The results of A. Chiodo, Y. Ruan and M. Krawitz associate the mirror partner Calabi–Yau variety X X to a Landau–Ginzburg orbifold ( f , G ) (f,G) if f f is an invertible polynomial satisfying Calabi–Yau condition and the group G G is a diagonal symmetry group of f f . In this paper we investigate the Landau–Ginzburg orbifolds with a Klein quartic polynomial f = x 1 3 ​ x 2 + x 2 3 ​ x 3 + x 3 3 ​ x 1 f=x_{1}^{3}x_{2}+x_{2}^{3}x_{3}+x_{3}^{3}x_{1} and G G being all possible subgroups of GL ⁡ ( 3 , ℂ ) {\mathrm{GL}}(3,{\mathbb{C}}) , preserving the polynomial f f and also the pairing in its Jacobian algebra. In particular, G G is not necessarily abelian or diagonal. The zero–set of polynomial f f , called Klein quartic, is a genus 3 3 smooth compact Riemann surface. We show that its mirror Landau–Ginzburg orbifold is ( f , G ) (f,G) with G G being a ℤ / 2 ​ ℤ {\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}} –extension of a Klein four–group.



1.
Introduction

Mirror symmetry, in one of its formulations, relates a complex variety
X
to a so–called
Landau–Ginzburg orbifold

(f,G)
(
[
IV90
,
V89
,
W93
]
). In the latter
f
defines a quasihomogeneous isolated singularity and
G
is its symmetry group. In particular
{f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]}
is a polynomial, whose partial derivatives all vanish simultaneously if and only if
x_{1}=\dots=x_{N}=0
and there is a set of natural numbers
d_{f},d_{1},\dots,d_{N}
, such that
f(\lambda^{d_{1}}x_{1},\dots,\lambda^{d_{N}}x_{N})=\lambda^{d_{f}}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
for any
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
.

The symmetry group
G
is a subgroup of
{{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}:=\{g\in{\mathrm{GL}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N})\ |\ f(g\cdot x)=f(x)\}}
. The diagonal operator
{j_{f}=\text{diag}(\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}\frac{d_{1}}{d_{f}}),\dots,\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}\frac{d_{N}}{d_{f}}))}
belongs to
{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
, showing that
{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
is never trivial. The variety
X
is assumed to be either quasismooth or a smooth orbifold.

Both
X
and
(f,G)
have some essential
{\mathbb{C}}
–algebras associated to them. These are the cohomology ring
H^{*}(X)
if
X
is smooth or orbifold cohomology ring
H_{orb}^{*}(X)
otherwise (cf.
[
AGV08
]
). Note that
H^{*}_{orb}(X)=H^{*}(X)
if
X
is smooth. To a pair
(f,G)
one associates the Hochschild cohomology ring
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
of the category of
G
–equivariant matrix factorizations
{\mathrm{MF}}_{G}(f)
. The variety
X
is said to be mirror to the Landau–Ginzburg orbifold
(f,G)
if there is a
{\mathbb{C}}
–algebra homomorphism
{H_{orb}^{*}(X)\cong{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)}
.

1.1.
Landau-Ginzburg – Calabi-Yau mirror symmetry

Large class of mirror pairs was discovered by Chiodo,Ruan and Krawitz in
[
CR11
,
K09
]
. In particular, let
(f,G)
be such that

(1)

f
is the so-called invertible polynomial. Namely
f
is a sum of exactly
N
monomials;

(2)

Calabi–Yau condition holds:
d_{1}+\dots+d_{N}=d_{f}
;

(3)

G
acts diagonally on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and is a subgroup of
{\mathrm{SL}}(N,{\mathbb{C}})
.

Then the Berglund–Hübsch–Henningson dual Landau-Ginzburg orbifold
(\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G})
(see
[
BH93
,
BH95
,
Kreu94
]
) can be used to construct the mirror variety by

X_{\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G}}:=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{P}}(d_{1},\dots,d_{N})\ |\ \widetilde{f}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=0\right\}\Big/\penalty(\widetilde{G}/\langle j_{\widetilde{f}}\rangle).

Theorem 14 of
[
CR11
]
together with Theorem 2.4 of
[
K09
]
give that there is a
{\mathbb{C}}
–vector space isomorphism
\tau:{H_{orb}^{*}(X_{\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G}})\cong{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)}
. This suggests calling
X_{\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G}}
a mirror pair of
(f,G)
. Up to now
\tau
is proved to be an algebra homomorphism only for the certain examples (cf.
[
MR11
,
MS12
,
BT3
]
). The major complication here lies in computing the algebra structure of
H_{orb}^{*}(X_{\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G}})
while the algebra structure of
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
was computed explicitly in
[
BT2
,
BTW16
,
BTW17
,
HLL20
,
BI22
]
using the technique developed by D.Shklyarov in
[
Sh20
]
.

Let’s comment on the conditions above. Condition
1
restricts us to work with the invertible polynomials only, for which the construction of Berglund–Hübsch–Henningson can be applied. Condition
2
has the name
Calabi–Yau condition
because it guarantees that the first Chern class of
X_{\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G}}
vanishes. It follows from Condition
3
that
\langle j_{\widetilde{f}}\rangle\subseteq\widetilde{G}
— this is the special property of the dual group construction.

1.2.
Admissible symmetry groups

The algebra
H_{orb}^{*}(X_{\widetilde{f},\widetilde{G}})
is endowed with a pairing for any
f
. However one can introduce an essential pairing on
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
only if
f
satisfies the Calabi–Yau condition (cf.
[
PV12
]
).
Generally in mirror symmetry one considers the groups
G
, such that
G\subseteq{\mathrm{SL}}(N,{\mathbb{C}})
. These groups are distinguished by the fact that they preserve volume form of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
and therefore the pairing of both algebras.

1.3.
Mirror symmetry for curves

More general mirror symmetry results were established in the context toric degenerations and homological algebra. In particular, Ruddat in
[
R17
]
and Gross-Katzarkov-Ruddat in
[
GKR17
]
investigated the mirrors of the varieties of general type from the point of view of toric degenerations via the relations on the Hodge numbers.

P.Acosta investigated in
[
A14
]
the vector space structure of the algebras above for the polynomials
f
that do not satisfy the Calabi–Yau condition. His result shows that
H^{\ast}(X_{f,G})
and
{\mathsf{HH}}^{\ast}(f,G)
are of different dimension if
f
does not satisfy the Calabi–Yau condition.

A smooth genus
g
surface
S_{g}
is a Calabi–Yau manifolds only if
g=1
. However, this is essential to ask what is a mirror Landau–Ginzburg model for
S_{g}
with
g\geq 2
.

Efimov and Seidel
[
E12
,
S11
]
established an equivalence between the (suitably defined) triangulated categories of a genus
g\geq 2
surface
S_{g}
and Landau–Ginzburg orbifold
(f_{g},G)
with
f_{g}=x_{1}^{2g+1}+x_{2}^{2g+1}+x_{3}^{2g+1}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
. Here the group
G
is generated by the diagonal operator
\text{diag}(\zeta,\zeta,\zeta^{2g-1})
, such that
\zeta=\exp(\frac{2\pi\sqrt{-1}}{2g+1})
. Without giving the details of this mirror theorem we quote the following consequence: there is a
{\mathbb{C}}
–algebra isomorphism
H^{*}(S_{g})\cong{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f_{g},G)
(cf.
[
E12
,
S11
,
Sh20
]
).

The difference between the approaches of Acosta and Efimov–Seidel is that in the first case the variety
X=X_{f,G}
is given by a Landau–Ginzburg model
(f,G)
whereas in the seconds case the Landau–Ginzburg model is constituted by the variety
X=S_{g}
. Another important difference is that
H^{\ast}(S_{g})
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
–commutative algebra while Acosta works in the context of FJRW theory (c.f.
[
FJR
]
) that is always commutative.

In this note we investigate the homological mirror symmetry result of Efimov and Seidel from the point of view of Berglund–Hübsch–Henningson duality.

1.4.
Klein quartic

Consider the polynomial
f=x_{1}^{3}x_{2}+x_{2}^{3}x_{3}+x_{3}^{3}x_{1}
. We have
d_{1}=d_{2}=d_{3}=1
and
d_{f}=4
. It is invertible with the Berglund–Hübsch dual
\widetilde{f}=f
. Take
J:=\langle j_{f}\rangle
. Then the variety
X_{\widetilde{f},J}
is a smooth genus
3
curve
S_{3}
. Its symmetry group is isomorphic to
\mathrm{PSL}(2,7)
— simple nonabelian of order
168
(cf.
[
K79
,
E98
]
).

Denote
{\mathrm{SL}}_{f}:={\mathrm{GL}}_{f}\cap{\mathrm{SL}}(N,{\mathbb{C}})
.
For any
G\subseteq{\mathrm{SL}}_{f}
denote by
\widehat{G}
the central extension of
G
by
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
obtained by adding the
-\mathrm{id}\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
to
G
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f=x_{1}^{3}x_{2}+x_{2}^{3}x_{3}+x_{3}^{3}x_{1}
be a Klein quartic polynomial and
S_{3}
stand for the smooth genus
3
Riemann surface. Then

(a)

\dim{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)>\dim H^{\ast}(S_{3})
for any
G\subseteq{\mathrm{SL}}_{f}
.

(b)

\dim{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,\widehat{G})=\dim H^{\ast}(S_{3})
for
\widehat{G}\subseteq\widehat{\mathrm{SL}}_{f}
if and only if
G\cong V_{4}
is conjugate to a Klein four–group.

Proof of the theorem occupies Section
4
along with some preparational propositions given in the preceding sections.

This theorem suggests that the mirror partner of
X_{\widetilde{f},J}
is
(f,\widehat{V}_{4})
.
A very important consequence of this theorem is the following observation.

In mirror symmetry it’s not enough to consider only the symmetry groups acting diagonally even for invertible polynomials.

This is important to stress that the theorem above considers not only diagonal symmetry groups. It also covers some symmetry groups
G
that are not abelian. Such symmetry groups are not widely considered from the point of view of Landau–Ginzburg orbifolds. The following list of citations is pretty much the full list of publications assuming nonabelian orbifolds
[
M
,
BI21
,
BI22
,
BI24
,
WWP
,
CJMPW23
,
EGZ20
,
EGZ18
]
. And even in this list (except
[
BI24
]
) one considers only the semidirect product groups
G^{d}\rtimes S
with
G^{d}
acting diagonally and
S
being a subgroup of a symmetric group. To our knowledge, no investigation of a simple group was made before except
[
BI24
]
.

Comparing our theorem to the mirror theorem of Efimov and Seidel it’s important to note that their Landau–Ginzburg orbifold was suitably chosen, while our investigation is in the setup of the general approach of the Landau–Ginzburg orbifolds given by the invertible polynomials. The polynomial
f_{g}
of Efimov and Seidel is not invertible whatever
g
one takes.

2.
Notations and terminologies

For a non-negative integer
N
and a polynomial
f=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
,
the
Jacobian algebra

\mathrm{Jac}(f)
of
f
is a
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra defined as

(1)

\mathrm{Jac}(f)=\left.\raisebox{2.15277pt}{${\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]$}\mkern-3.0mu\middle/\mkern-3.0mu\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{$\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{N}}\right)$}\right..

The polynomial
f
is said to define an isolated singularity if
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
is a finite-dimensional
{\mathbb{C}}
–vector space. In this case set
\mu_{f}:=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{Jac}(f)
and call it the
Milnor number
of
f
.
For
N=0
set
\mathrm{Jac}(f):={\mathbb{C}}
with
\mu_{f}=1
.

Let the
Hessian
of
f
be defined as the following determinant:

(2)

\mathrm{hess}(f):=\det\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,N}.

Its class is nonzero in
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
giving the
{\mathbb{C}}
–bilinear nondegenerate pairing
\eta_{f}
called
the residue pairing
(see
[
GH94
, Chapter 5]
,
[
AGV85
, Section 5.11]
).
The value
\eta_{f}([u],[v])
is taken as the projection of the product
[u][v]
to the
{\mathbb{C}}
–span of
[\mathrm{hess}(f)]
. In particular, we fix

\eta_{f}([1],[\mathrm{hess}(f)])=1.

Remark 2
.

One notes immediately that under the coordinate–wise action of
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
we have
g\cdot\mathrm{hess}(f)=(\det g)^{2}\mathrm{hess}(f)
. This shows that the groups
{G\subseteq\{g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}\ |\ \det g=\pm 1\}}
preserve the pairing
\eta_{f}
. We will comment more on this in Proposition
7
.

2.1.
Symmetries

Given a quasihomogeneous polynomial
f=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
consider the

maximal group of linear symmetries
of
f
defined by

{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}:=\left\{g\in{\mathrm{GL}}(N,{\mathbb{C}})\ |\ f(g\cdot{\bf x})=f({\bf x})\right\}.

Remark 3
.

This group contains the so–called maximal group of
diagonal
symmetries of
f
defined by

G_{f}^{d}:=\left\{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{N}\ |\ f(\lambda_{1}x_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}x_{N})=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\right\}.

For each
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
, denote by
\mathrm{Fix}(g)

the fixed locus of
g

(3)

\mathrm{Fix}(g):=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N}\ |\ g\cdot(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\right\}.

This is an eigenvalue
1
subspace of
g
and therefore a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.
By
N_{g}:=\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathrm{Fix}(g)
denote its dimension and by
f^{g}:=f|_{\mathrm{Fix}(g)}
the restriction of
f
to the fixed locus of
g
.
For
g\in G_{f}^{d}
this linear subspace is furthermore a span of a collection of standard basis vectors.

Proposition 4
.

For any
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
with
N_{g}>0
there is a choice of coordinates on
\mathrm{Fix}(g)
linear in
x_{i}
, such that the polynomial
f^{g}
defines a quasihomogeneous singularity.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{x}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}},\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}+1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{N}
be the coordinates of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
, such that
\widetilde{x}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}}
are dual to the eigenspace
1
basis vectors of
g
. In this coordinates we have
f^{g}=f\mid_{\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}+1}=\dots=\widetilde{x}_{N}=0}
. The proof follows now by the same argument as in Proposition 5 of
[
ET13
]
.
∎

Denote also

{\mathrm{SL}}_{f}:={\mathrm{GL}}_{f}\cap{\mathrm{SL}}(N,{\mathbb{C}}).

This group will be important later on because it preserves the volume form of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
.

3.
Phase spaces

This section is devoted to the definition of the state spaces and also the computational aspects of them. These are the A model state space
H^{\ast}(S_{3})
and the B model state space
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
.

3.1.
A–model total space

Recall that
{H^{\ast}_{orb}(S_{g})=H^{\ast}(S_{g})}
because
S_{g}
is a smooth manifold.
The dimension of
H^{\ast}(S_{g})
is
2+2g
. This is a
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
–graded algebra. In particular, we have
H^{\ast}(S_{g})=H^{even}\oplus H^{odd}
with

H^{even}={\mathbb{C}}\cdot\delta\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\cdot\gamma\quad H^{odd}=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{g}({\mathbb{C}}\cdot\alpha_{k}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}\cdot\beta_{k}).

Here
\delta\in H^{0}(S_{g})
,
\gamma\in H^{2}(S_{g})
and
\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}\in H^{1}(S_{g})
. We can also assume the generators above to be such that the only non–zero products are

\alpha_{k}\circ\beta_{k}=-\beta_{k}\circ\alpha_{k}=\gamma,\quad\delta\circ a=a,\quad a\in H^{\ast}(S_{g}).

Such an algebra has a unique up to a
{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
–multiple Frobenius algebra structure, however this is not a subject of the current note.

3.2.
B–model total space

For each
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
fix a generator of a one-dimensional vector space
\Lambda(g):=\bigwedge^{N-N_{g}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N}/\mathrm{Fix}(g))
. Denote it by
\xi_{g}
.

Define
{\mathcal{B}}_{tot}(f)
to be the
{\mathbb{C}}
–vector spaces of dimension
\sum_{g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}}\dim\mathrm{Jac}(f^{g})

(4)

{\mathcal{B}}_{tot}(f):=\bigoplus_{g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}}\mathrm{Jac}(f^{g})\xi_{g},

Each direct summand
\mathrm{Jac}(f^{g})\xi_{g}
will be called the
g
–th sector
and denoted by
{\mathcal{B}}_{g}^{\prime}
.
Any
h\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
induces a map

h\colon\mathrm{Fix}(g)\to\mathrm{Fix}(hgh^{-1}).

and hence

h\colon\Lambda(g)\to\Lambda(hgh^{-1}).

Since we have fixed the generators
\xi_{\bullet}
, the latter map provides a constant
\rho_{h,g}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
such that

h\left(\xi_{g}\right)=\rho_{h,g}\xi_{hgh^{-1}}.

Then
\rho_{h_{2},h_{1}gh_{1}^{-1}}\rho_{h_{1},g}=\rho_{h_{2}h_{1},g}
. Define the action of
{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
on
{\mathcal{B}}_{tot}
by

(5)

\displaystyle h^{*}([p({\bf x})]\xi_{g})=\rho_{h,g}[p(h^{-1}\cdot{\bf x})]\xi_{hgh^{-1}}.

For a finite
G\subseteq{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
put

{\mathcal{B}}_{tot,G}:=\bigoplus_{g\in G}{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{g}=\bigoplus_{g\in G}\mathrm{Jac}(f^{g})\xi_{g}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{tot}

and define as a vector space

(6)

{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G):=\left({\mathcal{B}}_{tot,G}\right)^{G}.

Namely, the linear span of the
{\mathcal{B}}_{tot}
vectors that are invariant with respect to the action of all elements of
G
.

The latter direct sum and the group action can be intertwined as follows. Let
{\mathcal{C}}^{G}
stand for the set of representatives of the conjugacy classes of
G
and
Z(g)
for the centralizers of
g\in G
. Denote
{\mathcal{B}}_{g}:=\left({\mathcal{B}}_{g}^{\prime}\right)^{Z(g)}
. Then we have

(7)

{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)\cong\bigoplus_{g\in{\mathcal{C}}^{G}}{\mathcal{B}}_{g}=\bigoplus_{g\in{\mathcal{C}}^{G}}\left({\mathcal{B}}_{g}^{\prime}\right)^{Z(g)}.

cf.
[
BI22
, Proposition 42]
for the proof.

It’s much more complicated to define the algebra structure of
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
and we don’t do it here because it will not be needed later on. We will only need that the product
\circ
of
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
respects the
G
–grading:
\circ:{\mathcal{B}}_{g}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{B}}_{gh}
, its unit is
1\xi_{\mathrm{id}}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{id}}
and
\circ:{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{id}}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{id}}\to{\mathcal{B}}_{\mathrm{id}}
coincides with the product of
(\mathrm{Jac}(f))^{G}
.

3.3.
Computational statements

The following proposition follows immediately from the definition but will be very important later on.

Proposition 5
.

Let
f
define an isolated singularity. Then we have

(i):

Let
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be such that
\mathrm{Fix}(g^{k})=\mathrm{Fix}(g)
then
g^{\ast}\left(\xi_{g^{k}}\right)=\det g\cdot\xi_{g^{k}}
.

(ii):

Let
G\subseteq{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
be abelian. Then
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)=\bigoplus_{g\in G}\left(\mathrm{Jac}(f^{g})\xi_{g}\right)^{G}
.

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{x}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}}
be the coordinates dual to the eigenvalue
1
subspace of
g
. Then
(d\widetilde{x}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge d\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}})\wedge\xi_{g^{k}}
is a volume form of
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
. We get

\displaystyle\det g\cdot(d\widetilde{x}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge d\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}})\wedge\xi_{g^{k}}=g\left((d\widetilde{x}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge d\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}})\wedge\xi_{g^{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\rho_{g,g^{k}}\cdot(d\widetilde{x}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge d\widetilde{x}_{N_{g}})\wedge\xi_{g^{k}}.

This gives the first claim.

In order to show the second claim consider Eq. (
7
). We have
{\mathcal{C}}^{G}=G
and
Z(g)=G
for any
g\in G
, what completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let
G_{1},G_{2}\subseteq{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
be conjugate. Then
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G_{1})\cong{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
G_{2}=\widetilde{g}G_{1}\widetilde{g}^{-1}
for some
\widetilde{g}\in{\mathrm{GL}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.
Consider the decomposition of Eq.(
7
) for both groups. We show that

\Psi:[\phi(x)]\xi_{h}\mapsto[\phi(g\cdot x)]\xi_{\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1}},\quad\forall h\in G_{1}

establishes the isomorphism
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G_{1})\xrightarrow{\sim}{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G_{2})
.

Obviously
{\mathcal{C}}^{G_{2}}=\{\widetilde{g}r\widetilde{g}^{-1}\ |\ r\in{\mathcal{C}}^{G_{1}}\}
and
Z(\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1})=\widetilde{g}Z(h)\widetilde{g}^{-1}
.
Note that we have the isomorphism
\mathrm{Fix}(h)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathrm{Fix}(\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1})
. This gives
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{h}={\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1}}
.

In order to finish the proof it remains to show that the invariants under the action of
Z(h)
and
Z(\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1})
agree.
Let
r=\widetilde{g}s\widetilde{g}^{-1}\in\widetilde{g}Z(h)\widetilde{g}^{-1}
. Then
we have

r^{\ast}\xi_{\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1}}=\rho\cdot\xi_{\widetilde{g}s\widetilde{g}^{-1}}\quad\text{and}\quad s^{\ast}\xi_{\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1}}=\rho^{\prime}\cdot\xi_{s}

for some
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
. The following computation shows that
\rho=\rho^{\prime}
.

r^{\ast}\xi_{\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1}}=(\widetilde{g}s\widetilde{g}^{-1})^{\ast}\xi_{\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1}}=s^{\ast}\xi_{h}

where the last equality follows from the fact that
s
acts on
\Lambda(h)
by the same multiple as
\widetilde{g}s\widetilde{g}^{-1}
acts on
\Lambda(\widetilde{g}h\widetilde{g}^{-1})
.
∎

The following proposition shows that the pairing restricts to the invariants of
{G\subseteq\widehat{\mathrm{SL}}_{f}}
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
G\subset{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
be such that
\det g=\pm 1
for any
g\in G
. Consider the polynomials
\phi_{1}({\bf x}),\phi_{2}({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
satisfying
\eta_{f}([\phi_{1}],[\phi_{2}])\neq 0
.

If
[\phi_{1}]\neq 0
in
(\mathrm{Jac}(f))^{G}
, then
[\phi_{2}]\neq 0
in
(\mathrm{Jac}(f))^{G}
as well.

Proof.

Any
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
acts on the hessian polynomial by
g\left(\mathrm{hess}(f)\right)=(\det g)^{2}\mathrm{hess}(f)
. Let
\eta_{f}([\phi_{1}],[\phi_{2}])=\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
. The following equality holds in
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]
for some polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}

\phi_{1}({\bf x})\cdot\phi_{2}({\bf x})-\alpha\mathrm{hess}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}p_{k}({\bf x})\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{k}}.

The Jacobian ideal of
f
is preserved under the
G
–action. Hence under the assumption of the proposition we have

[\phi_{1}({\bf x})]\cdot[\phi_{2}({\bf x})]=\alpha[\mathrm{hess}(f)]\neq 0\in(\mathrm{Jac}(f))^{G}

what completes the proof.

∎

3.4.
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
for a Klein quartic

The following propositions are specific for
f
being the Klein quartic polynomial.
We consider in details the cases of
G\subseteq{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
with
\det g=1
and
(\det g)^{2}=1
.

3.4.1.
Consider
g\in G\backslash\{\mathrm{id}\}
with
\det g=1

The fixed locus of
g
corresponds to the eigenvalue
1
subspace and could be either
0
or
1
–dimensional. If
\mathrm{Fix}(g)=0
the element
\xi_{g}
is a non–zero multiple of a volume form on
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
(see Section
3.2
). Then any element
h\in Z(g)
acts on
\xi_{g}
by multiplication by
\det h
. In particular,
\xi_{g}
is
G
–invartiant if
G\subset{\mathrm{SL}}(3,{\mathbb{C}})
and is not
G
–invariant if
-\mathrm{id}\in G
.

If
\mathrm{Fix}(g)\cong{\mathbb{C}}
, we have
f^{g}=\widetilde{x}^{4}
where
\widetilde{x}
is a coordinate of
\mathrm{Fix}(g)
. This gives
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{g}\cong{\mathbb{C}}\langle[1],[\widetilde{x}],[\widetilde{x}^{2}]\rangle\xi_{g}
. Let
h\in Z(g)
act on
\widetilde{x}
by
\rho\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\ast}
. Namely
h(\widetilde{x})=\rho\widetilde{x}
. Then we have
\rho_{h,g}=\det h/\rho
by the same argument as in proof of Proposition
5
. This gives

(8)

h^{\ast}([\widetilde{x}^{k}]\xi_{g})=\frac{\det h}{\rho^{k+1}}\cdot[\widetilde{x}^{k}]\xi_{g}.

In order to compute the direct sum contribution of
g
to
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
it remains to check the multiples
\rho
of all the centralizers
Z(g)
.

3.4.2.
Consider
g\in G
with
\det g=-1

The fixed locus of
g
can be either
0
,
1
–dimensional or
2
–dimensional. The first two cases are treated exactly in the same way as above and we concentrate on the last one. Let
\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2}
be the coordinates on
\mathrm{Fix}(g)
. Then
f^{g}
is quasihomogeneous with weights
(1/4,1/4)
. After some rescaling of the variables
f^{g}
can either be
\widetilde{x}_{1}^{4}+\widetilde{x}_{2}^{4}
,
\widetilde{x}_{1}^{3}\widetilde{x}_{2}+\widetilde{x}_{2}^{4}
or
\widetilde{x}_{1}^{3}\widetilde{x}_{2}+\widetilde{x}_{2}^{3}\widetilde{x}_{1}
.

We have
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{g}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{9}
generated by the classes
[\widetilde{x}_{1}^{a_{1}}\widetilde{x}_{2}^{a_{2}}]
for some pairs
(a_{1},a_{2})
. Since
\det g=-1
, we have
g^{\ast}(\xi_{g})=-\xi_{g}
and
g^{\ast}([\widetilde{x}_{1}^{a_{1}}\widetilde{x}_{2}^{a_{2}}]\xi_{g})=-[\widetilde{x}_{1}^{a_{1}}\widetilde{x}_{2}^{a_{2}}]\xi_{g}
.
This shows that
({\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}_{g})^{G}=0
.

3.4.3.
Consider
g=\mathrm{id}

While computing the vector space
{\mathsf{HH}}^{*}(f,G)
one has to find the
G
–invariants of
\mathrm{Jac}(f)\xi_{\mathrm{id}}
. We have
h^{\ast}(\xi_{\mathrm{id}})=\xi_{\mathrm{id}}
and it remains to compute the
G
–invariants of
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
itself. This goal can be achieved by symmetrization procedure. Namely, if
\{\phi_{k}({\bf x})\}_{k=1}^{\mu}
are the monomials whose classes generate
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
, then the classes of
\{\sum_{g\in G}\phi_{k}(g\cdot{\bf x})\}_{k=1}^{\mu}
generate
(\mathrm{Jac}(f))^{G}
. However, some of these classes might be zero or linearly dependant.

The basis monomials of
\phi_{\bullet}
can be taken to be
x_{1}^{a_{1}}x_{2}^{a_{2}}x_{3}^{a_{3}}
, such that
\sum_{k}a_{k}\leq 6
and
0\leq a_{k}\leq 2
. Also
[\mathrm{hess}(f)]=756[(x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})^{2}]
.
Assign the grading
\sum_{k}a_{k}
to each of these monomials. The vector space
\mathrm{Jac}(f)
decomposes into the direct sum of the graded pieces. The dimensions of these pieces can be composed into the following vector

1,\ 3,\ 6,\ 7,\ 6,\ 3,\ 1.

This vector is symmetric by its middle point — the
4
–th component because the pairing
\eta_{f}
is non–degenerate.

The grading introduced is preserved under the coordinate-wise action of
g\in{\mathrm{GL}}_{f}
. Similarly
(\mathrm{Jac}(f))^{G}
decomposes into the direct sum of the graded pieces and the dimensions of these pieces can be written by a vector. This vector will be symmetric by its middle point due to Proposition
7
.

The group

\displaystyle R=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{\sqrt{7}}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\zeta-\zeta^{6}&\zeta^{2}-\zeta^{5}&\zeta^{4}-\zeta^{3}\\
