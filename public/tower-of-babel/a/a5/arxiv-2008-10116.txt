Octonionic Brownian windings
Gunhee Cho, Guang Yang

Abstract
. We define and study windings along Brownian paths on octonionic, Euclidean, projective, and hyperbolic spaces which are isometric to 8 8 -dimensional Riemannian model spaces. In particular, the asymptotic laws of these windings are shown to be Gaussian for flat and spherical geometries while the hyperbolic winding exhibits different long-time behavior.



1.
Introduction

A normed division algebra
A
over a real field
\mathbb{R}
is a division algebra over
\mathbb{R}
which is also a normed vector space, with norm
||\cdot||
satisfying

||x\cdot y||=||x||\cdot||y||,

for all
x
and
y
in
A
. A theorem of Hurwitz says that every normed united finite-dimensional algebra over
\mathbb{R}
is isomorphic to one of
\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{Q}
, or
\mathbb{O}
.

Hence with the norms
||{\cdot}||
of those four algebras
A
, one can talk about the spherical part
\frac{x}{||x||}
of the element
x\in A
.

One situation where the spherical part works nicely is the angular motion of the path on the punctured complex plane
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. We have a nice polar decomposition of any smooth path
\gamma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}

\gamma(t)=|\gamma(t)|\exp\left(i\int_{\gamma[0,t]}\frac{xdy-ydx}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right),t\geq 0.

Thus we call
\alpha=\frac{xdy-ydx}{x^{2}+y^{2}}
the winding form around
0
since this form provides the angular motion of the path
\gamma
. Based on this representation, one can also take the integral of the winding form along the path of a Brownian motion
\left(B(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
that does not start from
0
which becomes the Brownian winding functional:

\zeta(t)=\int_{B[0,t]}\alpha.

This functional has been studied in the literature and we refer the reader to
[
19
,
16
,
17
,
5
]
, and references therein for more details. The goal of this paper is to introduce the octonionic winding form in
8
-dimensional Riemannian homogeneous spaces equipped with an octonionic structure and study Brownian paths.

Studying the winding process in the octonionic setting is indeed important from several perspectives. Of course, the winding process in the complex and quaternionic settings could be extended to the last possible octonionic setting. Although defining the winding process in an octonionic setting is a natural analog from the previous setting, obtaining practical calculations based on Yor’s methods
[
19
]
is indeed non-trivial, especially for
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
. Moreover, the winding number process is closely related to the stochastic area process. In particular, in the case of the Heisenberg group, which is a flat-model space of the sub-Riemannian geometry, one can find that the Markov process consists of a Brownian motion and an area process on a horizontal distribution corresponding to the canonical sub-Laplacian. Under the complex field and the quaternion division algebra, the same results could be found by explicit calculation of the sub-Laplacian in the case of the Hopf fibration and the anti-de Sitter space; which are curved model spaces of sub-Riemannian geometry. However, in this explicit calculation, the fact that under the complex field and the quaternion division algebra, both the Hopf fibration and the anti-de Sitter space have contact structures and the fiber has a Lie-group structure can be found
[
5
,
4
]
. In contrast, in the case of the octionic setting, structures crucially used for calculation in the complex field and the quaternionic setting are lost. But one can still obtain the radial part of the sub-Laplacian by using the symmetries of octonion
[
2
,
3
]
. Therefore, in order to find out the relationship between the sub-Laplacian and the Markov process, a new calculation and verification method is required that does not depend on the structure given only to the complex and quaternionic settings.

While the octonionic winding process is directly related by definition to the area process, we study the winding process on
8
-dimensional model spaces of Riemannian geometry. This study shows how the octonionic structure can still be used, while also providing a stepping stone for studying the area process and its sub-Riemannian geometry in an octonionic setting.

Our main results are the following: let
\zeta(t)
be the octonionic Brownian winding functional. Then

-

on
\mathbb{O}
, the following convergence holds in distribution:

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{\log t}}\zeta(t)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{7}).

Here, the right-hand side means the normal distribution.

-

on
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
, the following convergence holds in distribution:

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\zeta(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{14}{3}I_{7}).

-

on
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
, we have the following asymptotic result:

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}-3}\bigg(1+\frac{6\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}-18}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}A(\lambda)\bigg),

where
A(\lambda)
is given by

\frac{\cosh^{4}(r(0))}{12}+\frac{(\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-2)\cosh^{2}(r(0))}{60}+\frac{|\lambda|^{2}-3\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}+11}{720}.

2.2.
Asymptotic winding of octonionic Brownian motion

We got the following definition from the previous section:

Definition 2
.

The winding number of an octonionic Brownian motion
W=\sum_{i=0}^{7}W_{i}e_{i}
, not started from
0
, is defined by the Stratonovitch stochastic line integral:

\zeta(t):=\int_{W[0,t]}\eta,t\geq 0.

From a well-known consequence of the skew-product decomposition of Euclidean Brownian motions, we have the following lemma (see
[
15
]
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
W=\sum_{i=0}^{7}W_{i}e_{i}
be an octonionic Brownian motion not started from
0
. There exists a Bessel process
(R(t))_{t\geq 0}
of dimension
8
(or equivalently index one) and a
\mathbb{S}^{7}
-valued Brownian motion
\Theta(t)_{t\geq 0}
independent from the process
(R(t))_{t\geq 0}
such that

W(t)=R(t)\Theta(A_{t}),

where

A_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{ds}{R^{2}(s)}.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\zeta(t)
be the winding process of a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}
. Then we have the following asymptotic result

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\sqrt{\log t}}\zeta(t)=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{7}).

Proof.

We provide two different proofs. The first one relies on properties of Bessel processes in Euclidean spaces. The second one is Yor’s method.

Proof 1:
By Hartman-Watson’s law, for
\mid W_{0}\mid=\rho
we have

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(e^{-\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{2}A_{t}}\mid R(t)=r)=\frac{I_{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}}(\frac{\rho r}{t})}{I_{3}(\frac{\rho r}{t})}

(for example, see
[
18
]
), where
I_{\alpha}
is the modified Bessel function

I_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+j+\alpha)j!}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2j+\alpha}.

(2.3)

Recall that the transition density of Bessel process with index
\delta
is given by

p^{\delta}_{t}(x,y)=t^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{\delta}\exp\left\{\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{2t}\right\}I_{\delta}\left(\frac{xy}{t}\right).

Now by using the density of the Bessel process with index
3
and performing a change of variables
r\rightarrow\frac{r}{\sqrt{t}}
, it is readily checked that

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(e^{-\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{2}A_{t}})=\frac{e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2t}}}{\rho^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{4}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}t^{\frac{3}{2}}I_{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}}\left(\frac{\rho r}{\sqrt{t}}\right)dr.

By (
2.3
), we have

I_{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}}\left(\frac{\rho r}{\sqrt{t}}\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+j+\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}})j!}\left(\frac{\rho r}{2\sqrt{t}}\right)^{2j+\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}}.

To get the asymptotic behavior, one only needs to work with the first term of the series. Performing the transformation
\lambda(t)=\sqrt{\frac{6}{\log t}}\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(e^{-\frac{\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}}{2}A_{t}})

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2t}}}{\rho^{3}\Gamma(1+\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}})}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}r^{4}t^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{\rho r}{2\sqrt{t}}\right)^{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}}}dr.

(2.5)

Since
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\Gamma(1+\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}})}=\Gamma(4)=6,
we see that the right-hand side of (
2.5
) is equal to

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{6\rho^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{\rho r}{2\sqrt{t}}\right)^{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}}}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}r^{4}dr=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{48}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\rho r}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}}-3}e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{2}}r^{7}dr.

By L’Hôpital’s rule, we have
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\rho r}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}}-3}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}
. Finally, by using integration by parts repeatedly we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(e^{-\frac{\mid\lambda(t)\mid^{2}}{2}A_{t}})=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}.

Proof 2:
First note that, the
8
-dimensional Bessel process solves the following stochastic differential equation

dR(t)=\frac{7}{2R(t)}dt+dB_{t},\;R(0)=\rho,

where
B_{t}
is a real-valued standard Brownian motion. We define a local martingale

D^{\mu}_{t}=\exp\bigg(\mu\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{R(s)}dB_{s}-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{R^{2}(s)}ds\bigg).

By Itô’s formula, we have

D^{\mu}_{t}=\bigg(\frac{R(t)}{R(0)}\bigg)^{\mu}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\mu^{2}+6\mu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{R^{2}(s)}ds\bigg).

Now we define a new probability measure

\mathbb{P}^{\mu}\mid_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}=D^{\mu}_{t}\mathbb{P}\mid_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}.

Under this new probability,
R(t)
is given by

dR(t)=\frac{7+2\mu}{2R(t)}dt+dB_{t},\;R(0)=\rho.

Let
\mu=\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3
. Then we have

\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(e^{-\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2}A_{t}})=\mathbb{E}^{\mu}_{\rho}\bigg(\frac{\rho}{R(t)}\bigg)^{\mu}.

By applying the scaling
\lambda(t)=\sqrt{\frac{6}{\log t}}\lambda
and the density of
R(t)
, one can recover (
2.5
), hence the desired result.

∎

3.
Windings of Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}P^{1}

3.1.
Winding form on
\mathbb{O}P^{1}

The unit sphere in
\mathbb{O}^{2}
is given by

\mathbb{S}^{15}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{O}^{2},||(x,y)||=1\}.

We have a Riemannian submersion
\pi:\mathbb{S}^{15}\rightarrow\mathbb{O}P^{1}
, given by
(x,y)\mapsto[x:y]
, where
[x:y]=y^{-1}x
. Then the vertical distribution
\mathcal{V}
and the horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
of
T\mathbb{S}^{15}
are defined by
\ker d\pi
and the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{V}
respectively so that
T\mathbb{S}^{15}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{V}
. Note that
\pi:\mathbb{S}^{15}\rightarrow\mathbb{O}P^{1}
has totally geodesic fibers and for each
b\in\mathbb{O}P^{1}
, the fiber
{\pi}^{-1}(\{b\})
is isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{7}
with the standard sphere metric
g_{\mathbb{S}^{7}}
.

This submersion
\pi
yields the octonionic Hopf fibration:

\mathbb{S}^{7}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{15}\rightarrow\mathbb{O}P^{1}

(see
[
2
]
). In particular, viewing the fiber
\mathbb{S}^{7}
as the set of unit octonions, one can take the quotient space

\mathbb{S}^{15}/\mathbb{S}^{7}

which is the octonionic projective space
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
. Note that
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
is isometric to the
8
-dimensional Euclidean sphere with radius
\frac{1}{2}
(see Theorem 3.5 in
[
10
]
).

Since we identify
\mathbb{S}^{15}
as a subset of
\mathbb{O}^{2}
and
\mathbb{S}^{7}
as the unit sphere in
\mathbb{O}
, its quotient space
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
consists of elements written by the homogeneous coordinate
[x:y]=\left\{(\lambda x,\lambda y):(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{15},\lambda\in\mathbb{S}^{7}\right\}
. Thus we can parametrize
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
using the octonionic inhomogeneous coordinate:

w={y}^{-1}x,(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{15}

with the convention
0^{-1}x=\infty
. This coordinate allows us to identify
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
with its one-point compactification
\mathbb{O}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
. With this inhomogeneous coordinate, the Riemannian distance from
0
is given by

r=\arctan|w|.

(3.1)

Let
\gamma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{O}P^{1}\backslash\left\{0,\infty\right\}
be a
C^{1}
-path. We have its polar decomposition:

\gamma(t)=|\gamma(t)|\Theta(t),

with
\Theta(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{7}
and defining the winding path
\theta(t)\in T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{7}
as

\theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\Theta(s)}^{-1}d\Theta(s),

where
p
is the north pole of
\mathbb{S}^{7}
.

The octonionic winding form on
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
is then the
T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{7}
-valued one-form
\eta
such that

\theta(t)=\int_{\gamma[0,t]}\eta=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\overline{\gamma}(s)d\gamma(s)-d\overline{\gamma}(s)\gamma(s)}{|\gamma(s)|^{2}}ds,t\geq 0.

3.2.
Asymptotic winding of Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}P^{1}

Let us first consider a general Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
. With the geodesic polar coordinate
(r,\theta^{1},\cdots,\theta^{n-1})
, the Riemannian metric
g
is given by

g=dr^{2}+\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}d\theta^{i}d\theta^{j}.

Then the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\triangle_{M}
with this coordinate is written as

\triangle_{M}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\sqrt{\det g}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{i}}\left(g^{ij}\sqrt{\det g}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{j}}\right)\\

We can decompose the Laplace-Beltrami operator as:

\triangle_{M}:=\mathcal{L}+\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(r)},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\sqrt{\det g}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial r},

\displaystyle\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{i}}\left(g^{ij}\sqrt{\det g}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{j}}\right).

Here,
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}(r)
is the geodesic sphere of radius
r
.
\mathcal{L}
is called the radial part of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. General formulas for the radial parts of Laplacian-Beltrami operators on rank one symmetric spaces are well-known (Chapter 3 in
[
9
]
, also p171 in
[
13
]
and
[
12
]
). Notice that as a Riemannian manifold,
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
is a compact rank one symmetric space. Moreover, it is isometric to the 8-dimensional euclidean sphere of sectional curvature
\frac{1}{4}
.

Now, let
(M,g)
be the
8
-dimensional euclidean sphere of the sectional curvature
\frac{1}{4}
. Then with the inhomogeneous coordinate (
3.1
), those operators must be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+14\cot(2r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}(r)}=\frac{2}{\sin^{2}2r}\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}},.

Here
\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{7}
(
[
7
,
8
,
13
]
).

Equivalently,
\triangle_{\mathbb{O}P^{1}}
with an inhomogeneous coordinate
w\in\mathbb{O}
is given by

\triangle_{\mathbb{O}P^{1}}=4(1+|w|^{2})^{2}\text{Re}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{w}\partial w}\right)-24(1+|w|^{2})\text{Re}\left({w\frac{\partial}{\partial w}}\right).

Here, with
w=\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}e_{i}
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}e_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{7}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}e_{j}\right).

In real coordinates, we have

\triangle_{\mathbb{O}P^{1}}=\sec^{4}r\left(\sum_{i=0}^{7}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x_{i}}^{2}}\right)-12\sec^{2}r\left(\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right).

Thus, the Brownian motion
\left(w(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
solves the stochastic differential equation:

dw(t)=\sec^{2}r(t)dW(t)-6\sec^{2}r(t)w(t)dt,

where
\tan r(t)=|w(t)|
and
\left(W(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{O}
. Thus we can write the winding process as

\zeta(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\overline{w}(s)dw(s)-d\overline{w}(s)w(s)}{|w(s)|^{2}},

or equivalently,

\zeta(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\overline{w}(s)dW(s)-d\overline{W}(s)w(s)}{\sin^{2}r(s)}.

As in the flat setting, the study of
\zeta
makes use of the following skew-product decomposition.

Lemma 5
.

Let
w
be a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
not started from
0
or
\infty
. Then there exists a Jacobi process
(r(t))_{t\geq 0}
with generator

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+14\cot(2r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)

and a Brownian motion
\Theta(t)
on
\mathbb{S}^{7}
independent from the process
(r(t))
such that

w(t)=\tan r(t)\Theta_{A_{t}},

where

A_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{4ds}{\sin^{2}(2r(s))}.

Proof.

This follows from
[
15
]
and with the fact from (
3.2
),
\frac{1}{2}\triangle_{\mathbb{O}P^{1}}
may be decomposed in polar coordinates as:

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+14\cot 2r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{4}{\sin^{2}2r}\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}}\right).

∎

Theorem 6
.

We have

\frac{\zeta(t)}{\sqrt{t}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{14}{3}I_{7}),

in distribution.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
. From the previous lemma, we have

\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=\mathbb{E}(e^{-\frac{|\lambda|^{2}}{2}A_{t}})=e^{-2|\lambda|^{2}t}\mathbb{E}(e^{-2|\lambda|^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\cot^{2}2r(s)ds}).

Note that
r(t)
solves the following stochastic differential equation

r(t)=r(0)+7\int_{0}^{t}\cot 2r(s)ds+B_{t},

where
B_{t}
is a real-valued standard Brownian motion. We consider the following local martingale

D^{\mu}_{t}=\exp\bigg(2\mu\int_{0}^{t}\cot 2r(s)dB_{s}-2\mu^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\cot^{2}2r(s)ds\bigg).

By Itô’s formula, we have

D^{\mu}_{t}=e^{2\mu t}\left(\frac{\sin 2r(t)}{\sin 2r(0)}\right)^{\mu}\exp\bigg(-2(\mu^{2}+6\mu)\int_{0}^{t}\cot^{2}2r(s)ds\bigg).

Let
\mu=\sqrt{|\lambda|^{2}+9}-3
. Then by Girsanov’s theorem, we have

\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=(\sin 2r(0))^{\sqrt{|\lambda|^{2}+9}-3}e^{-2t(|\lambda|^{2}+\sqrt{|\lambda|^{2}+9}-3)}\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left(\sin 2r(t)^{3-\sqrt{|\lambda|^{2}+9}}\right).

Applying the scaling
\lambda\rightarrow\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{t}}
, we arrive at

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\frac{\zeta(t)}{\sqrt{t}}})=(\sin 2r(0))^{\sqrt{\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{t}+9}-3}e^{-2t\left(\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{t}+\sqrt{\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{t}+9}-3\right)}\mathbb{E}^{\mu}\left(\sin 2r(t)^{3-\sqrt{\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{t}+9}}\right)

Taking the limit gives

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\frac{\zeta(t)}{\sqrt{t}}})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}e^{-2t\left(\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{t}+\sqrt{\frac{\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}{t}+9}-3\right)}=e^{-\frac{7}{3}|\lambda|^{2}}

∎

4.
Windings of Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}H^{1}

4.1.
Winding form on
\mathbb{O}H^{1}

Similarly, the octonionic anti-de Sitter space
AdS^{15}(\mathbb{O})
is defined as the pseudo-hyperbolic space by:

AdS^{15}(\mathbb{O})=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{O}^{2},||(x,y)||^{2}_{\mathbb{O}}=-1\},

where

||(x,y)||^{2}_{\mathbb{O}}:=||x||^{2}-||y||^{2}.

The map
\pi:AdS^{15}(\mathbb{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{O}H^{1}
, given by
(x,y)\mapsto[x:y]=y^{-1}x
is a pseudo-Riemannian submersion with totally geodesic fibers isometric to
\mathbb{S}^{7}
. The pseudo-Riemannian submersion
\pi
yields the octonionic anti-de Sitter fibration

\mathbb{S}^{7}\hookrightarrow{AdS}^{15}(\mathbb{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{O}H^{1},

(see
[
3
]
).
Viewing the fiber
\mathbb{S}^{7}
as a set of unit octonions, one can take the quotient space

AdS^{15}(\mathbb{O})/\mathbb{S}^{7}.

The quotient space is the octonionic hyperbolic space
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
. As in the
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
case, one can parametrize
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
using the octonionic inhomogeneous coordinate:

w={y}^{-1}x,(x,y)\in AdS^{15}(\mathbb{O}),

with the convention
0^{-1}x=\infty
. This coordinate allows us to identify
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
with the open unit ball
\{w\in\mathbb{O}:|w|<1\}
. With this inhomogeneous coordinate, the Riemannian distance from
0
is given by

\tanh r=|w|.

(4.1)

Let
\gamma:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{O}P^{1}\backslash\left\{0,\infty\right\}
be a
C^{1}
-path. Then we have its polar decomposition:

\gamma(t)=|\gamma(t)|\Theta(t),

with
\Theta(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{7}
and defining the winding path
\theta(t)\in T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{7}
as

\theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}{\Theta(s)}^{-1}d\Theta(s),

where
p
is the north pole of
\mathbb{S}^{7}
. The octonionic winding form on
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
is then the
T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{7}
-valued one-form
\eta
such that

\theta(t):=\int_{w[0,t]}\eta=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{dw(s)\overline{w}(s)-d\overline{w}(s)w(s)}{|w(s)|^{2}}.

4.2.
Asymptotic winding of Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}H^{1}

As we did in a previous section, a similar analogue holds for
(M,g)
in the
8
-dimensional euclidean unit ball as the Riemannian homogeneous space. With the inhomogeneous coordinate (
4.1
), once one writes
\triangle_{\mathbb{O}H^{1}}=\mathcal{L}+\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}(r)}
, then those operators must be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+14\coth(2r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}(r)}=\frac{2}{\sinh^{2}2r}\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}},

(for example, see
[
3
,
8
,
13
]
).

Equivalently,
\triangle_{\mathbb{O}H^{1}}
with a coordinate
w\in\mathbb{O}
is given by

\triangle_{\mathbb{O}P^{1}}=4(1-|w|^{2})^{2}\text{Re}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\overline{w}\partial w}\right)+24(1-|w|^{2})\text{Re}\left({w\frac{\partial}{\partial w}}\right),

here, with
w=\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}e_{i}
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial w}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{0}}e_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{7}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}e_{j}\right).

In real coordinates, we have

\triangle_{\mathbb{O}H^{1}}=\textup{sech}\,^{4}r\left(\sum_{i=0}^{7}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{x_{i}}^{2}}\right)+12\text{}\textup{sech}\,^{2}r\left(\sum_{i=0}^{7}x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\right).

Thus, the Brownian motion
\left(w(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
solves the stochastic differential equation:

dw(t)=\textup{sech}\,^{2}r(t)dW(t)+6\textup{sech}\,^{2}r(t)w(t)dt,

where
\tanh r(t)=|w(t)|
and
\left(W(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{O}
. Thus we can write the winding process as

\zeta(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\overline{w}(s)dw(s)-d\overline{w}(s)w(s)}{|w(s)|^{2}},

or equivalently,

\zeta(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\overline{w}(s)dW(s)-d\overline{W}(s)w(s)}{\sinh^{2}r(s)}.

As before, to study
\zeta
, we shall make use of a skew-product decomposition.

Lemma 7
.

Let
w
be a Brownian motion on
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
not started from
0
. Then there exists a Jacobi process
(r(t))_{t\geq 0}
with generator

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+14\coth(2r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)

and a Brownian motion
\Theta(t)
on
\mathbb{S}^{7}
independent from the process
(r(t))
such that

w(t)=\tan r(t)\Theta_{A_{t}},

where

A_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{4ds}{\sinh^{2}(2r(s))}.

Proof.

This follows from
[
15
]
and with the fact from (
3.2
),
\frac{1}{2}\triangle_{\mathbb{O}P^{1}}
may be decomposed in polar coordinates as:

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r^{2}}+14\coth 2r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{4}{\sinh^{2}2r}\triangle_{\mathbb{S}^{7}}\right).

∎

Theorem 8
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
, we have the following asymptotic result

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}\bigg(1+\frac{6\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-18}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}A(\lambda)\bigg),

where
A(\lambda)
is given by

\frac{\cosh^{4}(r(0))}{12}+\frac{(\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-2)\cosh^{2}(r(0))}{60}+\frac{|\lambda|^{2}-3\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}+11}{720}.

Proof.

Let
\beta
be a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{7}
. Since
\zeta(t)=\beta_{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{4ds}{\sinh^{2}(2r(s))}}
in distribution, the characteristic function of
\zeta(t)
is given by

\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=\mathbb{E}(e^{-|\lambda|^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{2ds}{\sinh^{2}(2r(s))}}).

In order to apply Yor’s method, we introduce the following local martingale

D^{(a,b)}_{t}=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}a\coth(r(s))+b\tanh(r(s))dB_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(a\coth(r(s))+b\tanh(r(s)))^{2}ds\right\}.

By Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle D^{(a,b)}_{t}=e^{-4(a+b+\frac{ab}{4})t}

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\sinh(r(t))}{\sinh(r(0))}\bigg)^{a}\bigg(\frac{\cosh(r(t))}{\cosh(r(0))}\bigg)^{b}

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{a^{2}+6a}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\coth^{2}(r(s))ds-\frac{b^{2}+6b}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tanh^{2}(r(s))ds\right\}.

Note that

\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}(2r(s))}=\frac{\coth^{2}(r(s))+\tanh^{2}(r(s))-2}{4}.

Let
\hat{a},\hat{b}
be the solution of

x^{2}+6x-|\lambda|^{2}=0.

More precisely,

\hat{a}=-3+\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}\;,\;\hat{b}=-3-\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}.

Then

\displaystyle D^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}_{t}

\displaystyle=e^{24t}\bigg(\frac{\sinh(r(t))}{\sinh(r(0))}\bigg)^{-3+\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{\cosh(r(t))}{\cosh(r(0))}\bigg)^{-3-\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{-2|\lambda|^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(2r(s))}ds\right\}

\displaystyle=e^{24t}\bigg(\frac{\tanh(r(t))}{\tanh(r(0))}\bigg)^{-3+\sqrt{9+\mid\lambda\mid^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{\cosh(r(0))}{\cosh(r(t))}\bigg)^{6}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{-2|\lambda|^{2}}{\sinh^{2}(2r(s))}ds\right\}.

Since
D^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}_{t}
is bounded, it is of class DL and hence a martingale. We can now define a new probability

\mathbb{P}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}\mid_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}=D^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}_{t}\mathbb{P}\mid_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}.

By Girsanov’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}\left(e^{-24t}\bigg(\frac{\tanh(r(t))}{\tanh(r(0))}\bigg)^{3-\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}}\bigg(\frac{\cosh(r(t))}{\cosh(r(0))}\bigg)^{6}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-24t}\frac{(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}\left(\frac{\cosh^{6}(r(t))}{\tanh(r(t))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-24t}\frac{(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}\left((1-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(r(t))})^{-\frac{\hat{a}}{2}}\cosh^{6}(r(t))\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-24t}\frac{(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\hat{a}/2)_{k}}{k!}\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2k-6}(r(t))}\right).

As before, the asymptotic behavior is determined by the lowest order term. Thus,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=\frac{(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}e^{-24t}\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{6}(r(t))).

Under this new probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}
,
r(t)
solves the following stochastic differential equation

r(t)=r(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg[(\hat{a}+\frac{7}{2})\coth(r(s))+(\hat{b}+\frac{7}{2})\tanh(r(s))\bigg]ds+d\hat{B}_{t},

where
\hat{B}_{t}
is a standard Brownian motion under
\mathbb{P}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}
. By Itô’s formula, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{6}(r(t)))

\displaystyle=(6\hat{a}+6\hat{b}+18)\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{6}(r(t)))-(6\hat{b}+\frac{5}{2})\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{4}(r(t)))

\displaystyle=24\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{6}(r(t)))-(6\hat{b}+36)\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{4}(r(t))).

By solving this differential equation, we have

\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{6}(r(t)))=e^{24t}\bigg(\cosh^{6}(r(0))-(6\hat{b}+36)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{4}(r(s)))}{e^{24s}}ds\bigg).

By using a similar argument, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{4}(r(t)))

\displaystyle=e^{12t}\bigg(\cosh^{4}(r(0))-(4\hat{b}+20)\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{2}(r(s)))}{e^{12s}}ds\bigg),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{(\hat{a},\hat{b})}(\cosh^{2}(r(t)))

\displaystyle=e^{4t}\bigg(\cosh^{2}(r(0))-\frac{2\hat{b}+8}{4}\bigg)+\frac{2\hat{b}+8}{4}.

Putting everything together, we end up with

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}(e^{i\lambda\cdot\zeta(t)})=\frac{(\tanh(r(0)))^{\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-3}}{\cosh^{6}(r(0))}\bigg(\cosh^{6}(r(0))+(6\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-18)A(\lambda)\bigg),

where
A(\lambda)
is given by

\frac{\cosh^{4}(r(0))}{12}+\frac{(\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}-2)\cosh^{2}(r(0))}{60}+\frac{|\lambda|^{2}-3\sqrt{9+|\lambda|^{2}}+11}{720}.

∎

Remark 9
.

Unlike in the case of
\mathbb{O}
or
\mathbb{O}P^{1}
, the formula of the limiting behavior of the Brownian winding functional of
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
is different from its counterpart of
4
-dimensional quaternionic hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}H^{1}
. This is due to the fact that limit behaviour of the Brownian winding in
\mathbb{H}H^{1}
and
\mathbb{O}H^{1}
are sensitive to the parameter of the corresponding Bessel process, while in the first two cases, different parameters only give different scaling.
