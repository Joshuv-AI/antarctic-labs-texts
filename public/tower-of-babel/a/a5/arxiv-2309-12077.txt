Stochastic compressible Navier–Stokes equations under location uncertainty and its approximations for ocean modelling Journal:
Gilles Tissot, Étienne Mémin, Quentin Jamet

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to provide a stochastic version under location uncertainty of the compressible Navier–Stokes equations. The modelling under location uncertainty setting is used here to derive a physically consistent stochastic dynamical system for compressible flows. It relies on an extended stochastic version of the Reynolds transport theorem involving stochastic source terms. In a similar way as in the deterministic case, this conservation theorem is applied to density, momentum and total energy in order to obtain a transport equation of the primitive variables, i.e. density, velocity and temperature. For the modelling of ocean dynamics, the transport of mass fraction of species, such as salinity, is also considered. We show that performing low Mach and Boussinesq approximations to this more general set of equations allows us to recover previous versions of incompressible stochastic Navier–Stokes equations and the stochastic Boussinesq equations, respectively. Finally, we provide some research directions on the use of this general set of equations in the perspective of relaxing the Boussinesq and hydrostatic approximation for ocean modelling.



1
Introduction

Stochastic modelling under location uncertainty (LU) relies on the decomposition of the displacement of fluid particles into a time-differentiable velocity field, and a highly fluctuating component represented by a Brownian motion.
It was proposed in
Mémin 2014
to apply this principle to fluid flows, leading to a stochastic version of the Navier–Stokes equations. The ability of this model to represent at coarse-scale the fine-scale deterministic Navier-Stokes equation has been recently analysed
(
Debussche et al. 2023
)
. Beyond the fact that the existence of weak (probabilistic) solutions has been demonstrated in 3D – with unicity in 2D –, it was shown that, for vanishing noise, this stochastic model tends to the deterministic equation, providing hence in consequence a strong physical consistency to this formal setting.
Similar derivations have been devised within the same framework for various ocean models, such as Boussinesq models
(
Resseguier et al. 2017a
)
, quasi-geostrophic (QG) models
(
Bauer et al. 2020a
;
Li et al. 2023a
;
Resseguier et al. 2017b
)
, surface quasi-geostrophic models (SQG)
(
Resseguier et al. 2017c
)
and shallow water equations
(
Brecht et al. 2021
)
.

In the ocean, variations of density, temperature and salinity as well as compressibility effects are key drivers of the flow dynamics.
However, in the previously cited stochastic models the Boussinesq approximation of small compressibility has been assumed from the start.
This is a fair approximation, but it can become limiting, for instance when accurate energy budget are considered
(
Young 2010
)
, or for the representation by the noise of non hydrostatic processes such as deep convection.
For example, a wrong representation of such effects may potentially lead to strong biases in climate models of the thermohaline circulation and deep currents such as the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC).
Some intensive research efforts have been performed to account for compressibility in deterministic oceanic flow models
(
Tailleux 2012
;
Eden 2015
;
Dewar et al. 2015
)
.
It has been shown
(
McDougall 2003
;
Graham and McDougall 2013
)
, that under the Boussinesq approximation works of compression/dilatation are not negligible due to density variations.
In this situation, potential enthalpy is a conserved quantity, leading to the definition of a variable named conservative temperature.
This conservation is exact when molecular/eddy dissipation is discarded, but the topic became controversial when conservation of quantities is expressed in an averaged sense for large-scales modelling, and when energy is transferred toward unresolved (small) scales by mixing
(
McDougall et al. 2021
)
.

Tailleux 2015
for instance explored the differences between the non-conservation of potential and conservative temperature.
In the present study, we propose to consider the stochastic LU framework in order to postulate conservation of total energy (kinetic, potential and internal) with an explicit separation between resolved slow scales and unresolved fast scales.
An abuse of language, inherited from the large eddies simulation (LES) community, often associates large/slow (respectively fast/small) components. Here large/slow scales are related to resolved processes while fast/small scales are unresolved and represented by random processes uncorrelated in time but spatially correlated. From this decomposition, an evolution equation of non conservative quantities, such as temperature, can be deduced in a compressible setting.

Another key aspect is that, Boussinesq models cannot sustain acoustic waves, which is relevant for two major applications:
i)
ocean acoustics, which is important for instance in tsunami detection
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
and
ii)
numerical simulations of non-Boussinesq models, where pseudo-compressibility strategies
(
Chorin 1967
)
are employed to compute the pressure with dynamical schemes to avoid solving a numerically costly 3D Poisson equation
(
Auclair et al. 2018
)
.
To be able to propose numerical oceanic stochastic LU models incorporating non-hydrostatic pressure correction, a derivation from the compressible equations appears necessary.

The derivation of a compressible stochastic system cannot be derived from the incompressible stochastic system, since the latter is supposed to be to a limiting system of the former.
We propose in this paper to start from the classical physical conservation laws to derive a general stochastic compressible Navier–Stokes system.
As will be seen, we will verify that the provided set of equations remains consistent with the incompressible stochastic models previously devised and through Boussinesq approximation, we will derive a stochastic Boussinesq model incorporating thermodynamic effects.
We will moreover theoretically show some potential benefits of the stochastic modelling enabling to perform a relaxation of the Boussinesq approximation.
Such a procedure will allow us to propose stochastic systems of increasing complexity lying in between Boussinesq hydrostatic systems and a fully compressible flow dynamics.

In this paper, all the derivations will be treated discarding the Coriolis effects, but it could be straightforwardly taken into account.
In section
2
, we briefly recall the LU formalism and provide a convenient form of the stochastic Reynolds transport theorem when the budget of conserved quantities is balanced by external sources or flux terms of stochastic nature.
In section
3
we develop the stochastic compressible Navier–Stokes equations.
In sections
4
and
5
, the low Mach number and Boussinesq approximations are performed respectively.
We verify in these two sections the consistency with stochastic models previously derived from stochastic isochoric models
(
Resseguier et al. 2017a
)
.
This stochastic Boussinesq model is generalised by incorporating thermodynamic effects.
In section
6
these approximations are relaxed and we propose a model which can be integrated explicitly in time, similarly as in
Auclair et al. 2018
.
In section
7
some concluding remarks are given.
In appendix, technical calculation rules and important details to perform energy budgets are provided.

2
Stochastic Reynolds transport theorem

The transport of conserved quantities subject to a stochastic displacement of fluid parcels is described by the stochastic Reynolds transport theorem (SRTT) introduced in
Mémin 2014
.
When stochastic source terms are involved in the budget, additional covariation terms have to be taken into account
(
Resseguier et al. 2017a
)
.
In the present section, we briefly present the modelling under location uncertainty, and we rewrite the SRTT with stochastic source terms in a convenient form for further developments.

In the modelling under location uncertainty
(
Mémin 2014
)
, the displacement
\bm{X}(\bm{x},t)
of a particle is written in a differential form as

\mathrm{d}\bm{X}(\bm{x},t)=\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}(\bm{x}),

(1)

where
\bm{u}=(u,v,w)^{T}
is a time-differentiable velocity component, and
\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
is the increment of a cylindrical Wiener process
(
Prato and Zabczyk 1992
)
, whose aim is to model unresolved time-decorrelated velocity contributions.
The correlation operator
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
is an integral operator which involves a spatial convolution in the domain
\Omega
with a user-defined symmetric
1
1

1

Note that for a non-symmetric kernel, a noise with the same (Gaussian) law can be defined from a symmetric kernel associated to the symmetric square root of the covariance operator.
correlation kernel
\bm{\check{\sigma}}
, such that

\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)^{i}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\check{\sigma}}^{ij}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}^{j}(\bm{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}^{\prime},

(2)

where the convention of summation over repeated indices is used.
For a bounded kernel in time and space, the operator
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
is Hilbert-Schmidt and the noise above can be shown to be well defined.
Associated with
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
, we define the (matrix) variance tensor
\bm{\mathsf{a}}

\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{ij}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\check{\sigma}}^{ik}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\bm{\check{\sigma}}^{kj}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x},t)\mathrm{d}\bm{x}^{\prime}.

(3)

This quantity corresponds in the general case to the quadratic variation of the noise

\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{ij}\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{i},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{j}\right\rangle_{t}.

(4)

The quadratic variation (whose definition is briefly recap in
A
) is a finite variation process when the correlation operator,
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
, is random. For deterministic operator, it can be understood as the one point covariance tensor):

\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{ij}(\bm{x})\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)^{i}(\bm{x})\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)^{j}(\bm{x})\right),

(5)

and we refer to it as the variance tensor by abuse of language.
In the following for sake of simplicity, we will assume that the correlation operator is deterministic.
However, immediate extensions can be considered with random correlation tensor (see for instance
Li et al. 2023a
where the
\bm{\mathsf{a}}
, and
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
are random and defined from a dynamical mode decomposition).
Let us note that these operators being assumed Hilbert-Schmidt a convenient spectral representation of the noise on the eigenfunction basis of the noise correlation can be considered:

\begin{split}&\left(\bm{\sigma}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}(t)^{1/2}\bm{\varphi}_{i}(\bm{x},t)\mathrm{d}\beta_{i,t}\\
&\text{ and }\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{ij}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(t)\bm{\varphi}_{k}^{i}(\bm{x},t)\bm{\varphi}_{k}^{j}(\bm{x},t),\end{split}

(6)

where
\lambda_{i}
and
\bm{\varphi}_{i}
denotes respectively the positive (decreasing) eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the noise correlation operator
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
, and
\mathrm{d}\beta_{i,t}
are increments of independent scalar Wiener processes.

Within this framework, the stochastic transport operator of a scalar quantity
q
is defined by
(
Mémin 2014
– see also
Bauer et al. 2020a
for a simplified and complete exposition dedicated to geophysical fluids)

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{t}q\triangleq&\mathrm{d}_{t}q+\left(\underbrace{\left(\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)}_{{\bm{u}^{\star}}}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)q\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)q-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla q)\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(7)

where
{\bm{u}^{\star}}
, called
drift velocity
, is the resolved velocity corrected by the inhomogeneity and divergence of the noise correlation tensor, respectively.
The correction drift,
\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{\star}
, is usually referred to as the Ito-Stokes drift due to its relation with the wave induced Stokes drift
(
Bauer et al. 2020a
)
. The second term of the drift velocity involves the product
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})
defined as the kernel product

\bigl(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\bigr)^{i}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\check{\sigma}}^{ik}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime},t)\partial_{x^{j}}\bm{\check{\sigma}}^{jk}(\bm{x}^{\prime},\bm{x},t)\mathrm{d}\bm{x}^{\prime},

(8)

and is associated with the divergence of the noise.

In the stochastic transport operator, the noise term takes the form of an advection term often referred to as transport noise in the literature.
It can be remarked upon applying Itô integration by part formula (see
A
for a brief recapitulation on covariation and Itô integration by parts) that for suitable boundary conditions and divergent-free noise, the
L_{2}
norm of
q
is preserved path-wise (
i.e.
for any realisation).
The energy brought by the noise term exactly compensates the loss of energy by the stochastic diffusion
(
Bauer et al. 2020a
;
Resseguier et al. 2017a
)
.
This balance can be interpreted as an immediate instance of a fluctuation-dissipation relationship between the transport noise (whose energy is expressed through its covariation) and the stochastic diffusion.
This balance is seen only in the Itô setting.
For Stratonovich integral it is implicit and reveals itself only when expressing expectation and variance – which requires to go back to Itô integral.
Physical relevance of the drift velocity and the stochastic diffusion
\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla q)\,\mathrm{d}t
has been extensively highlighted in previous studies
(
Bauer et al. 2020a
;
Chandramouli et al. 2018
;
Pinier et al. 2019
,
e.g.
)
.
Mathematically, the emergence of such a term together with a transport noise has been also verified as a suitable limit of a coupled system of 3D slow/fast Navier-Stokes equation with additive Brownian forcing on the fast component
(
Debussche and Pappalettera 2023
;
Debussche and Hofmanová 2023
)
.
In the wake of F. Flandoli program, transport noise has been recently the subject of intensive research works due to well posedness properties
(
Agresti et al. 2022
;
Brzeźniak and Slavík 2021
;
Crisan et al. 2019
;
Debussche et al. 2023
;
Flandoli et al. 2010
;
Flandoli et al. 2021
;
Flandoli and Luo 2021
;
Goodair et al. 2022
;
Lang et al. 2023
;
Mikulevicius and Rozovskii 2005
)
of several fluid dynamics models as well as due to the emergence of enhanced dissipation and mixing
(
Flandoli et al. 2022
;
Flandoli and Russo 2023
)
.
Transport noise has been also exploited with variational formulation
(
Holm 2015
;
Street and Crisan 2021
)
. Compressible stochastic system has been recently studied for barotropic fluids
(
Breit et al. 2021
)
.
The system analysed is comparable to ours.
Nervertheless, we will deal with general baroclinic flows with hence additional thermodynamic evolution equations for active tracer.
These evolution equations will be obtained from stochastic Reynolds transport expression and evaluations of the works involved. The derivation of such expression is described below.

We now aim at deriving evolution equations of conserved quantities undergoing forcing terms.
Variation of
q
integrated over a transported volume
\mathcal{V}(t)
can be written as the follows budget

\mathrm{d}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}q\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\left(Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(9)

These right-hand side (RHS) terms correspond to forces (resp. work) when this general expression is associated with the momentum (resp. energy/heat fluxes) equation.
The source terms
Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
(which can be issued from physical sources or divergence of fluxes), are composed by a time-differentiable and a martingale contribution where the product of correlation operators,
\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, must be interpreted as the kernel product:

(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})^{i}(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}\check{\bm{Q}}^{ij}(\bm{x},\bm{y},t)\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}^{j}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\bm{y}.

(10)

Even if in the applications of the present paper the operator
\bm{Q}_{\sigma}
is assumed to be deterministic, it could be a stochastic process under the condition the
\mathrm{d}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}
is differentiable in the Largangian frame.
This leads to the explicit expression of the stochastic Reynolds transport theorem

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}q+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)q\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla q)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.\end{split}

(11)

The demonstration is provided in
B
.
The absence of the term
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})
in the modified drift is an important feature of this expression.
It has been cancelled (not neglected) by expressing
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
in Itô form.
As it will be detailed further, this term will reappear when we will transform the conservative form of the equations to their associated non-conservative form,
i.e.
writing a transport equation for the primitive variables.

From the modelling point of view, let us mention that several approaches to specify the noise have been recently proposed in the literature.
We describe hereafter a brief description of the state-of-the-art.
Noises based on homogeneous stationary turbulence assumption have been first proposed
(
Resseguier et al. 2017b
;
Resseguier et al. 2017c
)
.
Based on the modal description of the noise (
6
), spectral representations through proper orthogonal decomposition to deal with stationary inhomogeneous turbulence have been elaborated for different flows
(
Bauer et al. 2020b
;
Brecht et al. 2021
;
Cotter et al. 2020
;
Gugole and Franzke 2019
)
. Projection techniques to enforce a steering of the small scales by the resolved scales have been also proposed to introduce a temporal evolution of the noise
(
Li et al. 2023a
;
Tucciarone et al. 2023
)
. Advanced models for waves relying on resolvent and spectral proper decomposition
(
Towne et al. 2018
)
have been derived in
Tissot et al. 2021
;
Tissot et al. 2023
. For the shallow water model, the interaction of large waves (such as internal waves) with noisy waves has been studied in
Mémin et al. 2023
.
Methods relying on dynamic mode decomposition has been been proposed in
Li et al. 2023b
.
A technique based on the Girsanov change of measure has been devised in order to deal with non centered noise incorporating a slow component
(
Li et al. 2023a
;
Li et al. 2023b
)
or to drive the dynamics toward an available observation
(
Dufée et al. 2023
)
.
Efficient noise models relying on a self similarity assumption has been proposed in
Brecht et al. 2021
;
Kadri Harouna and Mémin 2017
.
Studies to compare with different statistical criteria the different noises on simple ocean dynamics models is available in
Brecht et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023a
;
Resseguier et al. 2021
.
The possibility to represent different unresolved physical processes (waves, turbulence, deep convection,
etc.
) through the combination of different noise models has to be further explored and is the subject of ongoing studies.

3
Stochastic compressible Navier–Stokes equations

To obtain the stochastic compressible Navier–Stokes equations we will need to apply the SRTT equation (
11
) to the mass, momentum and total energy.
This requires at first to properly define the physical variables.

3.1
Non-dimensioning

We consider the time
t
,
\bm{x}=(x,y,z)^{T}
the space coordinates of
\Omega
, and
(\bm{e}_{x},\bm{e}_{y},\bm{e}_{z})
the associated canonical basis.
Physical quantities are marked by
\bullet^{\phi}
and the other quantities are non-dimensional.
We adimensionalise by reference conditions (noted
\bullet_{\text{\tiny ref}}
), and introduce a reference distance
L_{\text{\tiny ref}}
, velocity
u_{\text{\tiny ref}}
, density
\rho_{\text{\tiny ref}}
, sound speed
c_{\text{\tiny ref}}
as well as viscosity
\mu_{\text{\tiny ref}}
.
We get

\begin{split}&\bm{x}=\frac{\bm{x}^{\phi}}{L_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad t=\frac{t^{\phi}{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}}}{L_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad\bm{u}=\frac{\bm{u}^{\phi}}{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad c=\frac{c^{\phi}}{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad M=\frac{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}}{c_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\\
&\rho=\frac{\rho^{\phi}}{\rho_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad\mu=\frac{\mu^{\phi}}{\mu_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad p=\frac{p^{\phi}}{\rho_{\text{\tiny ref}}u_{\text{\tiny ref}}^{2}}\quad;\quad T=\frac{T^{\phi}c_{p}^{\phi}}{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}^{2}}\quad;\\
&\gamma=\frac{c_{p}^{\phi}}{c_{v}^{\phi}}\quad;\quad e=\frac{e^{\phi}}{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}^{2}}=\frac{T}{\gamma}\quad;\quad\bm{g}=\frac{\bm{g}^{\phi}L_{\text{\tiny ref}}}{u_{\text{\tiny ref}}^{2}},\end{split}

(12)

with
\bm{u}
the velocity vector,
c
the speed of sound,
M
the Mach number (
i.e.
the ratio of typical particle velocity to typical sound speed),
\rho
the density,
p
the pressure,
\mu
de dynamic viscosity,
T
the temperature,
\gamma
the heat capacity ratio,
(c_{p},c_{v})
the heat capacities at constant pressure/volume,
e
the internal energy and
\bm{g}=-g\bm{e}_{z}
the acceleration vector due to gravity.
We consider the mass fraction
Y_{i}
of the species
i
.
Salinity
S
considered in section
5
is a special case of a mass fraction transported by the flow.
We introduce as well the Reynolds, Prandtl ans Schmidt numbers

Re=\frac{\rho_{\text{\tiny ref}}u_{\text{\tiny ref}}L_{\text{\tiny ref}}}{\mu_{\text{\tiny ref}}}\quad;\quad Pr=\frac{c_{p}^{\phi}\mu^{\phi}}{k_{T}^{\phi}}\quad;\quad Sc_{i}=\frac{\mu^{\phi}}{\rho^{\phi}D_{i}^{\phi}},

(13)

with
k_{T}^{\phi}
the thermal conductivity and
D_{i}
the mass diffusivity associated with the species
i
.

3.2
Continuity

Mass conservation ensues upon applying the SRTT on density,
i.e.

q=\rho
with no mass source of any kind:

\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\rho\right)=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla\rho)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(14)

where we have moved the stochastic-diffusion term on the RHS to reflect its contribution as a sink term that aims to balance the source induced by the transport by
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
through quadratic variations.
As already noted before, it can be checked with Itô integration by part formula that the mass is preserved path-wise (
i.e.
for any realisation) for suitable boundary conditions.

Similarly, the conservation of the mass fraction of the species
i
can be written

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}(\rho Y_{i})+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\rho Y_{i}\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\left(\rho\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{Y_{i}}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}t=\\
&\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla\rho Y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{ReSc_{i}}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\nabla(\rho Y_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\rho Q_{t}^{Y_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\rho\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{Y_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.\end{split}

(15)

A semi-martingale source
Q_{t}^{Y_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{Y_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, with
\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{Y_{i}}
a kernel correlation operator, is considered.
This source ensues potentially from physico-chemical reactions or dilution effects.
For sake of simplicity, we do not consider additional diffusive mass flux related to differences of molecular properties between species, such as Soret effect
(
Mortimer and Eyring 1980
, induced by the difference of thermal diffusivity between species submitted to a temperature gradient;)
.
But they could be considered without further difficulties.
Combining equations (
14
) and (
15
) and using the stochastic distributivity rule (
89
) given in
C.1
, leads to the stochastic advection-diffusion-reaction equation

\begin{split}&\mathbb{D}_{t}Y_{i}+\sum_{k}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right)^{k},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{k}}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{Y_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&=\frac{1}{ReSc_{i}}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\nabla Y_{i})\,\mathrm{d}t+Q_{Y_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{Y_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.\end{split}

(16)

To obtain this non-conservative form, the steps are the same as the ones detailed in
D
for the momentum equation (section
3.3
).

3.3
Momentum

The SRTT is now applied to the momentum
\rho u_{i}
balanced by forces, with
u_{i}\in\{u,v,w\}
:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}(\rho u_{i})+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\rho u_{i}\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho u_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}-\rho g\delta_{i,\bm{e}_{z}}\\
&+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{u})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla(\rho u_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(17)

with
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\tau_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}))
.
Throughout the paper, we use the following notation convention: the martingale RHS of a transport equation of any variable
q
is written
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{q}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
,
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{q}
is a correlation operator expressed through a (possibly time dependent) kernel as previously and the product of correlation tensor (
\bm{\sigma}_{t}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho u_{i}}
) must be still understood in the same way as in (
10
).
The forces involved here are caused by pressure gradient, viscous stresses
\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}
and gravity.
The pressure gradient is decomposed in a time-differentiable part
p\,\mathrm{d}t
and a martingale component
\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}
.
For sake of generality, we consider the molecular viscosity stress tensor

\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{u})=\mu\left(\nabla\bm{u}+\left(\nabla\bm{u}\right)^{T}\right)+\left(\mu_{b}-\frac{2}{3}\mu\right)\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{u}\,\mathbb{I},

(18)

with
\mu_{b}
the bulk viscosity.
Similarly to the pressure, there is a finite variation friction contribution due to
\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}t
and a martingale contribution due to
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
.

After some manipulations detailed in
D
, and using the general form of transport distributivity (as the precise form of the martingale pressure is in particular unknown) (
90
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\rho\,\mathbb{D}_{t}u_{i}+\rho\sum_{k}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right)^{k},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{k}}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right\rangle_{t}\\
=&-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{u})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}-\rho g\delta_{i,\bm{e}_{z}},\end{split}

(19)

with
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\mathrm{d}p_{s}^{\sigma}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}\right)
the martingale part of the RHS.
The expression (
19
) is very similar to the momentum equation of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
(
Mémin 2014
, eq. 41 with incompressibility assumption)
.
We only have as an additional term, the covariation between (the martingale part of) forces and the small scale component
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
.
This term, usually difficult to evaluate analytically is generally neglected, as for instance in
Resseguier et al. 2017a
.

3.4
Energy

As in the deterministic framework
(
Woods 1975
;
Anderson and Wendt 1995
;
Landau and Lifshitz 2013
)
, we now consider conservation of the total energy and deduce a transport equation for the temperature.

General formulation

Work of forces and heat fluxes acting on a transported control volume induce variations of total energy
E
such that

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}(\rho E)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\rho E\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{F}^{\rho E}_{\sigma})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla(\rho E))\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W-\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\mathrm{d}\bm{q}),\end{split}

(20)

with
\mathrm{d}W
and
\mathrm{d}\bm{q}
the elementary work of the forces and heat fluxes detailed later.
The martingale part of these RHS terms is written
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho E}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, where
\bm{F}^{\rho E}_{\sigma}
denotes as previously a correlation operator.

Similarly as in
D
, using (
90
) and the continuity equation (
14
), we obtain

\rho\mathbb{D}_{t}(E)+\rho\sum_{k}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s})^{k},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{k}}}\left(\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{E}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right)\right\rangle_{t}=\mathrm{d}W-\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\mathrm{d}\bm{q}),

(21)

with
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{E}=\frac{1}{\rho}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho E}
.

Definition of the energy

At this point, the form of the total energy has to be specified.
It is strongly related to the physical mechanisms at play.
In the present study, we consider the total energy
\rho E=\rho(e+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{u}\|^{2}+gz)
, as the sum of internal energy
e=\frac{T}{\gamma}
, kinetic energy and potential energy due to gravity.
In this definition we considered the kinetic energy of the resolved scales only. The kinetic energy of the small scale component is not considered since it is ill-defined path-wise (possibly infinite). As will be explained in the following we will assume that the work component associated with the noise is in balance with its kinetic energy and corresponds to highly oscillating components of higher order terms. However, let us note that the contribution of the small-scale velocity to all the components of the total energy are well taken into account through the SRTT and in the work calculation through the interaction of the martingale forces with the resolved component.

Definition of the work of forces and heat fluxes

We now detail the heat fluxes and the works associated to the different forces.

The work of the time-differentiable pressure represents how pressure is working with the displacement of the control surface.
The expression can be obtained by integrating the force multiplied by the surface displacement over a transported control volume and applying Green’s formulae.
The procedure is similar to the deterministic framework, with the additional implication of the drift velocity, as demonstrated in
E
.
We have for the pressure work:

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega(t)}\mathrm{d}W_{p}\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=&\int_{\partial\Omega(t)}\left(-p\,\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}S\right)\bm{\cdot}({\bm{u}^{\star}}\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})\\
=&-\int_{\Omega(t)}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bigl(p\,({\bm{u}^{\star}}\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.\end{split}

(22)

The minus sign comes from the outward normal
\bm{n}
convention.
We can then identify

\mathrm{d}W_{p}=-\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bigl(p\,({\bm{u}^{\star}}\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})\bigr).

(23)

In the same way, the viscous stress of the resolved component can be written

\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}=\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bigl(\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{u})\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\bigr).

(24)

Following
C.2
, we take as well into account the work of the random pressure:

\mathrm{d}W_{rp}=-\nabla\bm{\cdot}{\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}\right)},

(25)

and the work of the random viscous stress

\mathrm{d}W_{r\tau}=\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}{\left(\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}){\bm{u}^{\star}}\right)}.

(26)

As rigorously detailed in
C.2
, we do not consider work of random forces associated with
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, as such a highly irregular (in time) work and should be in balance with variations of kinetic energy of
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, which is possibly infinite.

There is no work contribution of gravity on total energy, since the gain in kinetic energy directly associated with the gravity force is compensated by the loss in potential energy.

The summation of all the above works gives us the global work:

\mathrm{d}W=\mathrm{d}W_{p}+\mathrm{d}W_{rp}+\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}+\mathrm{d}W_{r\tau}.

(27)

In presence of several species, energy budget is impacted by mass diffusion when the various species have different partial heat capacities
c_{v,i}
(such that
c_{v}=\sum_{i}Y_{i}c_{v,i}=\frac{1}{\gamma}
).
This induces a flux

\mathrm{d}\bm{q}_{Y_{i}}=-\sum_{i}\frac{1}{ReSc_{i}}(\rho Y_{i}e_{i})\nabla Y_{i}\mathrm{d}t,

(28)

with the internal energy of the species
e_{i}=c_{v,i}T
.
The Dufour effect
(
Mortimer and Eyring 1980
,
i.e.
heat flux due to a chemical potential gradient;)
is neglected in the present work, but could be straightforwardly considered.

Finally, we obtain the thermal conductivity by expressing the thermal fluxes by the Fourier law

\mathrm{d}\bm{q}=-\frac{\rho}{RePr}\nabla T\mathrm{d}t.

(29)

Transport equation of temperature

By replacing the energy by the contributions of internal, kinetic and potential energy, and by expressing the contribution of the kinetic energy using the momentum equation (
19
) and the transport product distributivity rule (
90
) (steps to
107
to
109
), we obtain after rearrangements (steps
110
to
113
) the transport equation for the temperature

\begin{split}&\frac{\rho}{\gamma}\mathbb{D}_{t}T+\underbrace{\frac{\rho}{\gamma}\sum_{k}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}}^{k},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{k}}}\left(\bm{F}^{T}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}{\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}}\right)\right\rangle_{t}}_{A_{T}}\\
&+\underbrace{\frac{\rho}{2}\sum_{i}{\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}{\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}{\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}}\right\rangle_{t}}}_{A_{u}}\\
&=\underbrace{-p\nabla\bm{\cdot}({\bm{u}^{\star}}\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})}_{P_{t}}\underbrace{-\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}\nabla\bm{\cdot}{\bm{u}^{\star}}}_{P_{\sigma}}\\
&+\underbrace{\frac{1}{Re}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{u}):\nabla\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)}_{V_{t}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{Re}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}):\nabla{\bm{u}^{\star}}}_{V_{\sigma}}\\
&+\underbrace{{\left(({\bm{u}^{\star}}-\bm{u})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(-\nabla p+\rho\bm{g}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{u})\right)}}_{D_{t}}\\
&+\underbrace{{\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}-\bm{u}\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(-\nabla\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})\right)}}_{D_{\sigma}}\\
&+\sum_{i}\frac{1}{ReSc_{i}}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left((\rho Y_{i}c_{v,i}T)\nabla Y_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\rho}{RePr}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\nabla T)\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(30)

with
\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}
defined in (
19
), and

\begin{split}\frac{\rho}{\gamma}\bm{F}^{T}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=&-p\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}-\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}\nabla\bm{\cdot}{\bm{u}^{\star}}\\
&+\frac{1}{Re}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{u}):\nabla\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}+\frac{1}{Re}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}):\nabla{\bm{u}^{\star}}\\
&+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\left(-\nabla p+\rho\bm{g}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{u})\right)\\
&+\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}-\bm{u}\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(-\nabla\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\tau}}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})\right),\end{split}

(31)

the sum of all martingale terms of the RHS of equation (
30
).
Details of the calculation are given in
F
.
In equation (
30
), we recover the terms present in the deterministic framework, with the stochastic transport operator instead of the usual material derivative.
Nevertheless, some covariation terms are now arising.
In particular the term
A_{T}
is induced by the work of the martingale part of the works/heat fluxes, the term
A_{u}
is induced by the increase of kinetic energy through covariations of the forces in the momentum equation. On the RHS, we remark that the drift velocity is involved in the compression/dilatation work of the time-differentiable pressure (in
P_{t}
) and martingale pressure (in
P_{\sigma}
), consistently with
E
.
We can remark that in case of isochoric flow, the condition
(\nabla\bm{\cdot}{\bm{u}^{\star}}=0\,;\,\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=0)
leads consistently to no compression/dilatation work.
The terms
V_{t}
and
V_{\sigma}
are works induced by smooth in time and martingale viscous stresses, respectively.
In addition, the terms
D_{t}
and
D_{\sigma}
correspond to works caused by the alignment between the drift and small-scale martingale velocities with the forces.
We call
D_{t}
and
D_{\sigma}

drift works
.
Focusing on
-({\bm{u}^{\star}}-\bm{u})\bm{\cdot}\nabla p
, we interpret this drift work to be related to baropycnal work
(
Aluie 2013
)
, present in the compressible large eddy simulation (LES) framework.
Indeed in standard compressible LES, baropycnal work corresponds to a contribution caused by the alignment between the large scale pressure gradient and the Reynolds stresses induced by product between the small scales contributions of
\rho
and
\bm{u}
(
i.e.

\frac{1}{\overline{\rho}}\nabla\overline{p}\bm{\cdot}\overline{\rho^{\prime}\bm{u}^{\prime}}
, with
\bm{\cdot}^{\prime}
denoting here small scale components and
\overline{\bm{\cdot}}
large scale filtering).
This Reynolds stress
\frac{1}{\overline{\rho}}\overline{\rho^{\prime}\bm{u}^{\prime}}
has the dimension of a velocity.
In our case, the interpretation of the effective displacement associated with small scales is directly the drift velocity over a time interval.
Similar interpretations can be made for the other drift work terms, associated with viscous stresses and random variables.

In presence of gravity, the vertical direction drift work is of the form
((w^{\star}-w)\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)_{z})\left(-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{z}}-\rho g\right)
.
In case of oceanic applications, decomposing the pressure into its hydrostatic
p_{H}
and non-hydrostatic
p_{NH}
contributions and the density as such
\rho=\rho_{0}+\rho^{\prime}
, this vertical drift-work component becomes

\begin{split}&((w^{\star}-w)\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)_{z})\left(-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p_{NH}}}{\displaystyle\partial{z}}-\rho^{\prime}g\right)\\
&=\rho_{0}((w^{\star}-w)\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)_{z})\left(-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p_{NH}}}{\displaystyle\partial{z}}+b\right),\end{split}

(32)

with the buoyancy
b=-\rho^{\prime}g/\rho_{0}
.
This makes appear explicitly the vertical small-scale mass flux times non-hydrostatic pressure effects, plus the buoyancy.
In other words, the hydrostatic pressure component is not involved in the drift works.
A finer decomposition of the pressure and density will be considered in section
5
.

It can be noticed that for a divergence-free homogeneous noise (for which the variance tensor is constant in space) the drift work is null as
{\bm{u}^{\star}}-\bm{u}
cancels.

3.5
Equation of state

In order to close the system, we have to specify the equation of state.
We keep generality and write the equation of state formally as follows

p=f(\rho,T,Y_{i}).

(33)

As in the deterministic framework, since we have an evolution equation of density and temperature, the pressure can be determined explicitly, at the price of a Courant-Friedrichs-Lewi (CFL) condition constrained by the speed of sound.

The martingale pressure can be identified by differentiating the equation of state.
Indeed, we have an explicit evolution equation of the pressure, which can be expressed through Itô formulae (the equation of state
f
being deterministic – i.e the state map does not depend on the random events) as

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}_{t}p=&\frac{\displaystyle\partial{f}}{\displaystyle\partial{\rho}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho+\frac{\displaystyle\partial{f}}{\displaystyle\partial{T}}\mathrm{d}_{t}T+\sum_{i}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{f}}{\displaystyle\partial{Y_{i}}}\mathrm{d}_{t}Y_{i}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\displaystyle\partial^{2}{f}}{\displaystyle\partial{\rho}^{2}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle\rho,\rho\rangle_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\displaystyle\partial^{2}{f}}{\displaystyle\partial{T}^{2}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle T,T\rangle_{t}+\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{{}^{2}f}}{\displaystyle\partial{Y_{i}\partial Y_{j}}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle Y_{i},Y_{j}\rangle_{t}\\
&+\frac{\displaystyle\partial{{}^{2}f}}{\displaystyle\partial{\rho\partial T}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle\rho,T\rangle_{t}+\sum_{i}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{{}^{2}f}}{\displaystyle\partial{\rho\partial Y_{i}}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle\rho,Y_{i}\rangle_{t}+\sum_{i}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{{}^{2}f}}{\displaystyle\partial{T\partial Y_{i}}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle T,Y_{i}\rangle_{t}\\
=&\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\widetilde{p}}}{\displaystyle\partial{t}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}}{{\tau}},\end{split}

(34)

where
\widetilde{p}
is the time-differentiable part of the pressure (more precisely the finite variation part) which contains, among other things, all covariation terms.
The martingale part of
\mathrm{d}_{t}p
is
\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}{/\tau}
, with
\tau
a decorrelation time.
This decorrelation time represents the typical time during which the martingale pressure acts in a coherent manner to produce a change of momentum.
It is assumed to be the same decorrelation time than the one introduced in previous works
(
Kadri Harouna and Mémin 2017
;
Bauer et al. 2020a
,
e.g.
)
to relate in practice the definition of the variance tensor to velocity fluctuations variance: (
i.e.

\bm{\mathsf{a}}=\tau\,\mathbb{E}(\bm{u}^{\prime}{\bm{u}^{\prime}}^{T})
).
The term
\mathrm{d}p^{\sigma}
is identified from (
34
) to be the martingale pressure acting on the momentum equation.

If we assume that the pressure ensues from an isentropic process,
i.e.
of acoustic nature, we can write

\mathrm{d}_{t}p=\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{\rho}}\Big|_{s}\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho=c^{2}\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho,

(35)

with
c
the speed of sound and
s
the entropy.
We can then identify from (
14
)

\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}=-{\tau}c^{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\rho\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}).

(36)

It can be remarked that this expression can be obtained by substituting the transport equations in equation (
34
), and by assuming an isentropic transformation.

For oceanic flows, the equation of state is often expressed in terms of density rather than pressure.
A specific treatment adapted to oceanic flows is detailed in section
6
.

4
Low Mach approximation

To perform the low Mach approximation, we follow the same steps as
Hoff 1998
, but applied here to the compressible stochastic Navier–Stokes equations.
With our non-dimensioning, we have at the reference conditions for isentropic transformations,

\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{\rho}}\Big|_{s}=c_{\text{\tiny ref}}^{2}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}.

(37)

This suggests for small
M
the following asymptotic expansion

\begin{split}\rho&=\rho_{0}+M^{2}\rho_{1}+o(M^{2}),\\
\bm{u}&=\bm{u}_{0}+o(1),\\
T&=\frac{1}{M^{2}}T_{0}+T_{1}+o(1).\\
\end{split}

(38)

and
p=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\rho_{1}}{M^{2}})=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
Similarly, the martingale pressure
\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}
follows the same scaling as the time-differentiable pressure.

Collecting
\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathcal{O}(M^{2})
terms in the continuity equation, we obtain respectively

\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{u}^{\star}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})=0\quad;\quad\mathbb{D}_{t}\rho_{1}=0.

(39)

We obtain then a divergence-free drift velocity and operator
\bm{\sigma}_{t}
.
The momentum equation (
19
) is unchanged, but considering constant density
\rho=\rho_{0}
.

5
Boussinesq-hydrostatic approximation

In this section, starting from the stochastic compressible Navier–Stokes equations, we perform the Boussinesq approximation by considering small density fluctuations.
These fluctuations are neglected, when they are not multiplied by gravity
\bm{g}
, which leads to the classical definition of the buoyancy.
We perform as well the hydrostatic approximation through the classical aspect ratio scaling
D=H/L_{\text{\tiny ref}}\ll 1
, with
H
the water depth.
At the scales of interest, we neglect molecular diffusions of mass momentum and temperature.
For simplicity, we do not consider a rotating frame.
Coriolis correction could be straightforwardly considered as in
Tucciarone et al. 2023
.
The vertical coordinate
z\in[-H,\eta]
is bounded by the bottom (
-H
) and the free surface (
\eta
).

Density

The density is decomposed through the following asymptotic expansion

\rho=\rho_{0}+\epsilon\rho_{1}(z)+\epsilon\rho_{2}(x,y,z,t)+o(\epsilon),

(40)

with
\rho_{1}(z)
the horizontally and time averaged stratification term, and
\epsilon\ll 1

and we do not need to assume that
\rho_{1}>\rho_{2}
.
We obtain hence

\nabla\bm{\cdot}{\bm{u}^{\star}}=0\quad;\quad\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=0\quad;\quad\mathbb{D}_{t}\left(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\right)=0.

(41)

The drift velocity and the noise are divergence free.
Density perturbations undergo a stochastic transport by the flow.
We remark that since
\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=0
, then the transport operator
\mathbb{D}_{t}(\bm{\cdot})
can be directly used in the SRTT (
11
).

The terms of order
\epsilon
of equation (
41
) can be expressed in terms of buoyancy
b=-\epsilon\,g\rho_{2}/\rho_{0}
:

\frac{\rho_{0}}{g}\mathbb{D}_{t}b=\left(w^{*}\,\mathrm{d}t+(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})_{z}\right)\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\rho_{1}}}{\displaystyle\partial{z}}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{\bullet z}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\rho_{1}}}{\displaystyle\partial{z}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(42)

with

\bm{\mathsf{a}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{HH^{T}}&\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{Hz}\\
\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{zH^{T}}&a_{zz}\end{pmatrix},

(43)

and
\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{zH^{T}}=\bm{\mathsf{a}}_{Hz}^{T}
, for
H=(x\ y)^{T}
.

Thermodynamic effects

Equation (
42
) is part of the stochastic version of what is often referred to as the
simple Boussinesq
equations (which includes in addition the so-called traditional approximation of the Coriolis term with no influence of the horizontal Coriolis component).
In the ocean, thermodynamic effects can be important, and we propose to incorporate these effects by combining the buoyancy and the energy equation, following the steps of
Vallis 2017
.
In particular, we consider an additional time-differentiable radiative source term
Q_{\text{\tiny rad}}\,\mathrm{d}t
in the energy equation, which plays an important role in the energy budget.

Assuming a linear equation of state for sea water around the state (
\rho_{0}
,
T_{0}
,
S_{0}
,
p_{0}
), we have

\rho=\rho_{0}\Big(1-\beta_{T}(T-T_{0})+\beta_{S}(S-S_{0})+\beta_{p}(p-p_{0})\Big),

(44)

with
S
the salinity,
\beta_{T}=1/\rho_{0}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\rho}}{\displaystyle\partial{T}}
,
\beta_{S}=1/\rho_{0}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\rho}}{\displaystyle\partial{S}}
and
\beta_{p}=1/\rho_{0}c^{2}
the coefficients of the Taylor expansion.
Equation (
16
) (for mass fraction) is written for
Y_{i}=S

\mathbb{D}_{t}S=Q_{S}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{S}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.

(45)

Source terms are considered in (
45
) in order to model physical phenomenon, such as evaporation/precipitations at the sea surface, dilution with fresh water or change of salinity at the vicinity of iceberg formation/melting.
Especially, the ocean-atmosphere exchanges could be fast compared to the ocean dynamics, which motivates keeping a martingale source term
\bm{Q}_{S}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
.
Similarly as in section
4
, the covariation term is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, and then is neglected.

We apply the stochastic transport operator to eq. (
44
).
We obtain

\begin{split}&\mathbb{D}_{t}\rho=-\rho_{0}\beta_{T}\mathbb{D}_{t}T+\rho_{0}\beta_{S}\mathbb{D}_{t}S+\frac{1}{c^{2}}\mathbb{D}_{t}p,\\
&\mathbb{D}_{t}\left(\rho-\frac{1}{c^{2}}p\right)=-\gamma\beta_{T}\mathrm{d}Q_{T}+\rho_{0}\beta_{S}\mathrm{d}Q_{S},\end{split}

(46)

where
\mathrm{d}Q_{T}
contains all work, source and quadratic variation terms, respectively, detailed in equation (
30
) and
\mathrm{d}Q_{S}=Q_{S}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{S}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
.
We introduce the potential buoyancy
b_{\phi}
, which is directly sensitive to works and heat sources:

b_{\phi}\triangleq-\frac{g}{\rho_{0}}\left(\epsilon(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})+\frac{\rho_{0}gz}{c^{2}}\right)=b_{N}+b-g\frac{z}{H_{p}},

(47)

where
b_{N}=-\epsilon\,g\rho_{1}/\rho_{0}
is related to stratification, and with
H_{p}=c^{2}/g
.
We obtain

\mathbb{D}_{t}b_{\phi}=\frac{\gamma g\beta_{T}}{\rho_{0}}\mathrm{d}Q_{T}-g\beta_{S}\mathrm{d}Q_{S}.

(48)

(49)

The term

can be interpreted as an alternative to the so-called eddy diffusivity mass flux (EDMF) scheme proposed recently for atmospheric and oceanic penetrative convection parameterization (see for instance

Giordani et al. 2020

Indeed, in EDMF, the subgrid stress in the transport equation of temperature is modelled as a standard eddy-viscosity Boussinesq assumption term plus an empirical term: the mass flux induced by a given number of plumes, multiplied by the difference of temperature between the plume and the ambient flow.
This term is built based on phenomenological considerations, and in this framework the small scale component is assumed smooth in time.
In the present derivation as mentioned previously, the vertical drift work can have the form
\rho_{0}(w^{\star}-w)b\,\mathrm{d}t
,
i.e.
the mass flux multiplied by the buoyancy.
With a linear equation of state, the buoyancy can be considered closely related to temperature, which suggests that the vertical drift work plays the role of EDMF.
Drift works are rigorously derived from conservation laws under the hypothesis of a time decorrelated small-scale component.
Interestingly, the pressure work provides a natural non-local (horizontal and vertical) forcing term while standard EDMF is a local upward/downward vertical statistical stress.
EDMF-like schemes could be obtained in the present framework by specifying the noise in terms of velocity fluctuations between the mean velocity and non-convective environment, upward plumes and downward plumes.
Such an interpretation needs to be tested with numerical simulations, and will be the focus of a future dedicated study.

Extension to non-Boussinesq

The aim of this section is to propose a formulation to relax now the Boussinesq approximation in the LU stochastic framework while avoiding the resolution of a 3D Poisson equation.
We consider now an intermediate model between the fully non-Boussinesq non-hydrostatic formulation and the system (
59

For sea water, the equation of state is formulated in terms of
\rho_{\text{\tiny BQ}}(T,p,S)

To take this aspect into consideration, we follow

This is at the cost of resolving in time acoustic waves, or more precisely a (pseudo-)compressibility information propagating at the velocity

The density is decomposed as

(60)

The term

This pseudo-compressibility aims at numerically resolve the pressure explicitly during the time marching, instead of solving an expensive 3D Poisson equation.

7
Conclusion

This paper proposes a stochastic representation under location uncertainty of the compressible Navier–Stokes equations.
It has been obtained from conservation of mass, mass fraction, momentum and total energy, undergoing a stochastic transport.
The structure of equations remains similar to the compressible deterministic case.
Nevertheless, because of the specificities related to stochastic transport, we have identified meaningful additional terms such as work induced by the alignment between the time-differentiable pressure gradient and the drift velocity.
This small-scale induced work is alike the baropycnal work known in compressible large eddy simulations and includes also terms reminiscent to mass flux parameterization of atmospheric and oceanic penetrative convection phenomenon.
These terms are obtained by the mean of a rigorous derivation from the conservation laws coupled with stochastic calculus rules associated to stochastic transport, instead of phenomenological arguments. This constitutes hence a justification of their shape, of their role and of their nature as they mainly arise from statistical correlations between small-scale velocity and forces.

We have checked that applying low-Mach and Boussinesq approximations on the stochastic compressible system enabled us to recover the known incompressible and simple Boussinesq stochastic systems respectively. From the modelling point of view, these systems constitutes a nested dynamics of increasing complexity.
The general set of stochastic compressible equations allowed us in particular to incorporate thermodynamic effects on the Boussinesq system.
Finally, this formulation has lead us to propose a natural way to relax the Boussinesq and hydrostatic approximation.
We believe this study brings a clear modelling framework and opens some new research directions to exploit the potential of stochastic modelling for the numerical simulations of oceanic flows.

Appendix A
Quadratic (co-)variation

In stochastic calculus, quadratic covariation of two real-valued processes
X
and
Y
plays a fundamental role. Quadratic variation is a bounded variation process defined as:

\langle X,Y\rangle_{t}=\lim_{n\rightarrow 0}\sum_{i=1}^{p_{n}}(X_{i}^{n}-X_{i-1}^{n})(Y_{i}^{n}-Y_{i-1}^{n}),

(70)

where
0=t_{0}^{n}<t_{1}^{n}<\cdots<t_{p_{n}}^{n}=t
is a partition of the interval
[0,t]
and this limit, if it exists, is defined in the sense of convergence in probability.

Assuming that
X
and
Y
are two real-valued continuous semimartingales, defined as
X_{t}=X_{0}+A_{t}+M_{t},Y_{t}=Y_{0}+B_{t}+N_{t}
with
M,N
martingales and
A,B
finite variation processes, then their quadratic covariation (
70
) exists, and is given by

\langle X,Y\rangle_{t}=\langle M,N\rangle_{t}.

(71)

In particular, the quadratic variation of a standard Brownian motion
B
(as a martingale) is given by
\langle B\rangle_{t}=t
, the quadratic variation of two bounded variation processes
f
, and
g
(such as deterministic functions) can be shown to be zero (
\langle f,g\rangle_{t}=0
), as well as the covariation between a martingale and a bounded variation process (
\langle f,M\rangle_{t}=0
).

The quadratic (co-)variations play an important role in the Itô calculus and its generalization of the chain rule. In particular, they are involved in the Itô integration by parts formula:

\mathrm{d}(XY)=X\mathrm{d}Y+Y\mathrm{d}X+\mathrm{d}\langle X,Y\rangle_{t}.

(72)

The quadratic variation of the Itô integrals of two adapted processes with respect to martingale,
M
and
N
, respectively, is provided by the following important formula:

\biggl\langle\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Theta_{s}\mathrm{d}M_{s},\int_{0}^{\cdot}\Theta^{\prime}_{s}\mathrm{d}N_{s}\biggr\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\Theta_{s}\Theta^{\prime}_{s}\mathrm{d}\langle M,N\rangle_{s}.

(73)

This property is involved in the Itô isometry, allowing to express the covariance of two Itô integrals:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}f\mathrm{d}M_{s}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t}g\mathrm{d}N_{s}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}fg\,\mathrm{d}\langle M,N\rangle_{s}\right],

(74)

where
f
and
g
are two adapted processes such that
\int_{0}^{t}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\langle M,M\rangle_{s}
and
\int_{0}^{t}g^{2}\mathrm{d}\langle N,N\rangle_{s}
are integrable.

The aim of this section is to demonstrate the Stochastic Reynolds transport theorem with source terms presented in (

To that end, we follow the steps of

The bracket in the above expression stands for the martingale covariation. The definition of the quadratic covariation process is recalled in
A
.
Stratonovich integral has the advantage to be associated to a classical Leibniz product rule, while Itô calculus involves quadratic variation terms – see
A
.
Otherwise, Stratonovich integral is not anymore a martingale opposite to Itô integral. Formal computation are much more easier in Stratonovich setting, however the analytical specification of expectation can only be done in the Itô representation.

Using the above conversion rule, it can be shown that the flow (
1
) reads in Stratonovich form
(
Bauer et al. 2020a
)
as

\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{t}=\left(\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.

(76)

We define the characteristic function
\phi(x,t)
transported by the flow, such that

\phi\left(\bm{X}_{t}(\bm{x}_{0})\right)=g(\bm{x}_{0}),

(77)

within a compact spatial support
\mathcal{V}(t)
of non-zero values that does not include points on the domain boundary.
We can then write

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}(q\phi)(\bm{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=&\mathrm{d}\int_{\Omega}(q\phi)(\bm{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
=&\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ q\,\phi+q\,\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.\end{split}

(78)

Since
\phi
is transported, we have

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}\phi(\bm{X}_{t},t)=\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ\phi+\nabla{\phi}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{t}=0,\\
&\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ\phi+\left(u-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)\bm{\cdot}\nabla{\phi}\,\mathrm{d}t+(\nabla{\phi}{\,\bm{\cdot}\,}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=0.\end{split}

(79)

We have then

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}(q\phi)(\bm{x},t)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ q\,\phi\\
&-q\left(\left(u-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)\bm{\cdot}\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}t+(\nabla{\phi}{\,\bm{\cdot}\,}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ q+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(q\left(\bigl(u-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\right)\right]\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.\end{split}

(80)

We add now a force and obtain the SRTT in Stratonovich form:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ q+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(q\left(\left(u-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\right)\\
&=Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}.\end{split}

(81)

Let us now write this expression in Itô form:

\begin{split}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(q\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}^{j}\right)=&\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(q\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}^{j}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(q{\bm{\sigma}_{s}^{ij}}\right)\right),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{j}\right\rangle_{t}}_{J}.\end{split}

(82)

Since
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}
is time differentiable in the Eulerian grid, we have

\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\bm{\sigma}_{s}^{ij},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{j}\right\rangle_{t}=0.

Then,

\begin{split}J=&\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(\mathrm{d}_{t}q{\bm{\sigma}_{s}^{ij}}\right)\right),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{j}\right\rangle_{t}\\
=&\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(\left(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{k}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{k}-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{l}}}\left(q\bm{\sigma}_{s}^{lm}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{m}\right)\right){\bm{\sigma}_{s}^{ij}}\right)\right),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{j}\right\rangle_{t}\\
=&\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{j}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{l}}}\left(q\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{lj}\right)\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{j}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{q}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{l}}}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{lj}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\left(q\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{lj}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{l}}}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ij}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t-\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla q)\,\mathrm{d}t-\nabla\bm{\cdot}(q\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(83)

In addition, we make the hypothesis that
\mathrm{d}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}
is time-differentiable in the Lagrangian frame, such that we have

\begin{split}{\mathrm{d}\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\bm{Q}^{j}_{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}&=\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}\mathrm{d}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{j}+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{Q}^{j}_{\sigma}({\bm{u}^{\star}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}))+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla\bm{Q}^{j}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
&=\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}F\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.\end{split}

(84)

We can then write

\begin{split}{\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\,{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}=&{\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{i},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{i}\right\rangle_{t}\,{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}\\
=&{\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{i}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{i}\right\rangle_{t}\,{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}\\
=&{\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{i}\bm{\sigma}_{s}^{jk}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{k}),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}^{i}\right\rangle_{t}\,{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}\\
=&{\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}(\bm{Q}_{\sigma}^{i}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{ji})\,\mathrm{d}t\,{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}\\
=&{\int_{\mathcal{V}(t)}}\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t\,{\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}},\end{split}

(85)

where the correlation operator product
(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})
should be understood in terms of kernel product.
Assembling everything and dropping the space integral, we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}\circ q+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(q\left(\left(\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\right)\\
&-Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t-\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\circ\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\\
=&\mathrm{d}_{t}q+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(q\left(\left(\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t})\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t-\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla q)\,\mathrm{d}t-\nabla\bm{\cdot}(q\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&-Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t-\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(86)

After simplification, we obtain

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}q+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(q\left(\left(\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{Q}_{\sigma})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla q)\,\mathrm{d}t+Q_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{Q}_{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t},\end{split}

(87)

which is exactly equation (
11
).
It can be shown after some algebraic manipulations, that this expression is consistent with the implicit form provided by
Resseguier et al. 2017a
.

Appendix C
Calculation rules

C.1
Distributivity of the stochastic transport operator

The distributivity of the stochastic transport operator is detailed in this section, in the case where the stochastic transport operator is balanced by random RHS.
If the evolution of two variables
f
and
g
are given by

\begin{split}&\mathbb{D}_{t}f=F_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\\
&\mathbb{D}_{t}g=G_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t},\end{split}

(88)

we have then

\mathbb{D}_{t}(fg)=f\,\mathbb{D}_{t}g+g\,\mathbb{D}_{t}f+\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\bm{G}_{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}t-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\bm{F}_{\sigma})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g\,\mathrm{d}t-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\bm{G}_{\sigma})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\,\mathrm{d}t,

(89)

or less formally:

\begin{split}\mathbb{D}_{t}(fg)=&f\,\mathbb{D}_{t}g+g\,\mathbb{D}_{t}f\\
&+\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g\right\rangle_{t}\\
&-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\right\rangle_{t},\end{split}

(90)

where Itô integration by part formula (
72
) has been used.
This relation reveals itself very useful to transform the conservative form of the Navier-Stokes equations into non-conservative form.

Proof

\begin{split}&\mathbb{D}_{t}(fg)=\\
&\mathrm{d}_{t}(fg)+\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)(fg)\,\mathrm{d}t+\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)(fg)-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla(fg))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=f\mathrm{d}_{t}g+g\mathrm{d}_{t}f+\mathrm{d}_{t}\langle f,g\rangle+f\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g\,\mathrm{d}t+g\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+f\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g+g\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}{(f\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{g}+g\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{f})}\\
&=f\mathrm{d}_{t}g+g\mathrm{d}_{t}f\\
&+\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f+\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g+\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&+f\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g\,\mathrm{d}t+g\left({\bm{u}^{\star}}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\,\mathrm{d}t+f\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g+g\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\Big(f\nabla\bm{\cdot}{(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{g})}+{\left((\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{g})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f}+g\nabla\bm{\cdot}{(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{f})}+{\left((\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{f})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(91)

Developing only the covariation term:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f+\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g+\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right\rangle_{t}=\\
&{\left((\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{f})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g\right\rangle_{t}\\
&-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{G}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\right\rangle_{t},\end{split}

(92)

whose right hand side is written formally

{\left(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla{f}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g}\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{F}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\bm{G}_{\sigma}\,\mathrm{d}t-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\bm{F}_{\sigma})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)g\,\mathrm{d}t-\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}(\bm{G}_{\sigma})\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)f\,\mathrm{d}t.

(93)

Substituting (
93
) into (
91
), we obtain equation (
89
).

C.2
Work of martingale forces

For sake of clarity, we first detail calculation rules in the context of point mechanics to define the work of martingale forces.
We consider a force, whose impulse is a martingale
\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}
.
We write its elementary work in a weak sense for any differentiable function
\phi(t)
, which respects
\phi(0)=\phi(T)=0
:

\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma}=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{t}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{X}.

(94)

The random force
\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{t}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}
is written here formally, since
\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}
is a martingale and cannot be differentiated in time.
The work in equation (
94
) can be split in two contributions:
\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma,u}
associated with the displacement
\bm{u}\,\mathrm{d}t
, and
\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma,\sigma}
associated with the displacement
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
.
We treat them separately.

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma,u}&=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\left(\frac{\displaystyle\partial{}}{\displaystyle\partial{t}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)\bm{\cdot}\bm{u}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t.\\
&=-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(\phi(t)\bm{u}(x,t)\right)^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(95)

The last expression is well defined and is a proper way to write this term.

To go further, we note that
\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}
is homogeneous to a Brownian.
Let us remark, that if we expand the following expression for a time-differentiable function
\bm{\psi}(x,t)
, we have the following relationship

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}\left(\bm{\psi}(x,t)\bm{\cdot}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)&=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{\psi}(x,t)+\int_{0}^{T}\bm{\psi}(x,t)\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)\bm{\cdot}\bm{\psi}^{\prime}(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\bm{\psi}(x,t)\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}.\end{split}

(96)

By taking
\bm{\psi}(x,t)=\phi(t)\bm{u}(x,t)
, we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma,u}&=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\bm{u}(x,t)\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}-\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}\left(\phi(t)\bm{u}(x,t)\bm{\cdot}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}\right)\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\bm{u}(x,t)\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}-\phi(T)\bm{u}(x,T)\bm{\cdot}\bm{F}_{\sigma}(x,T)\\
&=\int_{0}^{T}\phi(t)\bm{u}(x,t)\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}.\end{split}

(97)

We can identify

\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma,u}=\bm{u}(x,t)\mathrm{d}\bm{F}_{\sigma}.

(98)

The second term
\mathrm{d}W_{\sigma,\sigma}
is not well defined, even in weak form.
Informally, it should balance with kinetic energy of
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
, which is not well defined (possibly infinite), and which has not been considered in the definition of total energy.
Discarding this term is similar and consistent with the derivation of the momentum in
Mémin 2014
, where the acceleration associated with
\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}
being highly irregular is assumed to be in balance with some forces components of the same nature (
i.e.
of the order of the (weak) acceleration of white noise).
As a consequence in our model, there is no work of the random forces associated with the Brownian motion displacement of the control surface.

Appendix D
Non-conservative form of the momentum equation

The aim of this appendix is to detail the passage from the conservative form of the momentum equation to the non-conservative form,
i.e.
a transport equation for the velocity.
Conservation of the moment eq. (
17
) is

\begin{split}&\mathrm{d}_{t}(\rho u_{i})+\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\rho u_{i}\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho u_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}-\rho g\delta_{i,\bm{e}_{z}}\\
&+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{u})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{Re}\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\tau_{ij}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{j}}}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla(\rho u_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(99)

Using the distributivity rule (
90
) with
\bm{F}^{\rho}_{\sigma}\bm{\cdot}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}=-\rho\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\rho\mathrm{d}_{t}u_{i}+u_{i}\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\rho\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}{\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&+\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\rho\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)u_{i}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}{\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}},\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)\rho\right\rangle_{t}\\
&+\rho\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)u_{i}\\
&+u_{i}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\left(\rho\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\right)\right)+\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{\rho u_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&-\rho\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla(u_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}t-u_{i}\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{a}}\nabla(\rho))\,\mathrm{d}t\\
=&-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{p}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}t-\frac{\displaystyle\partial{\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\sigma}}}{\displaystyle\partial{x_{i}}}-\rho g\delta_{i,\bm{e}_{z}}+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\tau_{i}(\bm{u}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{Re}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\tau_{i}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t})),\end{split}

(100)

which simplifies using the continuity equation (
14
) to

\begin{split}&\rho\mathrm{d}_{t}u_{i}+\rho\left((\bm{u}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{\mathsf{a}})\,\mathrm{d}t+\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{t}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{t}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)u_{i}\\
&-\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\rho\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\bm{F}_{\sigma}^{u_{i}}\bm{\cdot}{\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}}\right\rangle_{t}\\
&+\mathrm{d}_{t}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\rho\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}),\int_{0}^{\bm{\cdot}}\left(\bm{\mathsf{\sigma}}_{s}\mathrm{d}\bm{B}_{s}\bm{\cdot}\nabla\right)u_{i}\right\rangle_{t}\\
