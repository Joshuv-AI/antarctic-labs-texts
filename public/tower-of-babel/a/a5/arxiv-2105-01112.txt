Tuple Interpretations for Higher-Order Complexity
Cynthia Kop, Deivid Vale

Abstract
We develop a class of algebraic interpretations for many-sorted and higher-order term rewriting systems that takes type information into account. Specifically, base-type terms are mapped to tuples of natural numbers and higher-order terms to functions between those tuples. Tuples may carry information relevant to the type; for instance, a term of type Nature {\rm Nature} may be associated to a pair ⟨ 𝖼𝗈𝗌𝗍 , 𝗌𝗂𝗓𝖾 ⟩ \langle\mathsf{cost},\mathsf{size}\rangle representing its evaluation cost and size. This class of interpretations results in a more fine-grained notion of complexity than runtime or derivational complexity, which makes it particularly useful to obtain complexity bounds for higher-order rewriting systems. We show that rewriting systems compatible with tuple interpretations admit finite bounds on derivation height. Furthermore, we demonstrate how to mechanically construct tuple interpretations and how to orient β \beta and η \eta reductions within our technique. Finally, we relate our method to runtime complexity and prove that specific interpretation shapes imply certain runtime complexity bounds.

Introduction

Term rewriting systems (TRSs) are a conceptually simple but powerful computational model.
It is simple because computation is modelled straightforwardly by step-by-step applications of transformation rules.
It is powerful in the sense that any algorithm can be expressed in it (Turing Completeness).
These characteristics make TRSs a formalism well-suited as an abstract analysis language,
for instance to study properties of functional programs.
We can then define specific analysis techniques for each property of interest.

One such property is
complexity
. The study of complexity has long been a topic of interest
in term rewriting

[
11
,
27
,
25
,
7
,
24
,
34
]
,
as it both holds relations to computational complexity
[
3
,
11
,
12
]

and resource analysis
[
6
,
13
]

and is highly challenging.
Most commonly studied are the notions of runtime and derivational complexity,
which capture the number of steps that may be taken when starting with terms of a given size and shape.
In essence, this is a form of resource analysis which abstracts away from the true machine cost of reduction in a rewriting engine but still has a close relation to it

[
8
,
18
,
1
,
12
]
.

These notions do not obviously extend to the
higher-order
setting, however.
In higher-order term rewriting, a term may represent a function;
yet, the size of a function does not tell us much
about its behaviour.
Rather, properties such as “the function is size-increasing” may be more relevant.
Clearly a more sophisticated complexity notion is needed.

In this paper we will propose a new method to analyse many-sorted and higher-order term rewriting
systems, which can be used as a foundation to obtain a variety of complexity results.
This method is based on
interpretations
in a monotonic algebra as also used for
termination analysis
[
38
,
22
]
, where a term of function type is mapped to a
monotonic function.
Unlike
[
38
,
22
]
, we map a term of base type not to an integer,
but rather to a vector of integers describing different values of interest in the term.
This will allow us to reason separately about—for instance—the length of a list and the size
of its greatest element, and to describe the behaviour of a term of function type in a fine-grained
way.

This method is also relevant for termination analysis, since we essentially generalise and extend

matrix interpretations

[
34
]
to higher-order rewriting.
In addition, the technique may add some power to the arsenal
of a complexity or termination analysis tool for first-order term rewriting; in particular

many-sorted
term rewriting due to the way we use type information.

A note on terminology.

We use the word “complexity” as it is commonly used in
term rewriting: a worst-case measure of the number of steps in a reduction. In this paper we do
not address the question of true resource use or connections to computational complexity. In
particular, we do not address the true cost of beta-reduction. This is left to future work.

Outline of the paper.

We will start by recalling the definition of and fixing notation for many-sorted and higher-order
term rewriting (§
2
).
Then, we will define tuple interpretations for many-sorted first-order rewriting to explore the
idea (§
3
), discuss our primary objective of
higher-order
tuple
interpretations (§
4
), and relate our method to runtime complexity
(§
5
). Finally, we will discuss related work (§
6
)
and end with conclusions and future work (§
7
).

2.3
Functions and orderings

An extended well-founded set is a tuple
(A,>,\geq)
such that

>
is a well-founded ordering on
A
;

\geq
is a quasi-ordering on
A
;

x>y
implies
x\geq y
; and

x>y\geq z
implies
x>z
.
Hence, it is permitted, but not required, that
\geq
is the reflexive closure of
>
.

For sets
A,B
, the notation
A\Longrightarrow B
denotes the set of functions from
A
to
B
.
Function equality is extensional: for
f,g\in A\Longrightarrow B
we say
f=g
iff
f(x)=g(x)
for all
x\in A
.

If
(A,>,\geq)
and
(B,\succ,\succeq)
are extended well-founded sets,
we say that
f\in A\Longrightarrow B
is
weakly monotonic
if
x\geq y
implies
f(x)\succeq f(y)
.
In addition, if
(A_{1},>_{1},\geq_{1}),\dots,(A_{n},>_{n},\geq_{n})
are all well-founded sets, we say that

f\in A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{n}\Longrightarrow B
is weakly monotonic if we have

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\succeq f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
whenever
x_{i}\geq_{i}y_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
We say that
f
is
strict
in argument
j
if
x_{j}>_{j}y_{j}
(and also
x_{i}\geq_{i}y_{i}
for
all
i
) implies
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\succ f(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
.

We say that
f\in A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{n}\Longrightarrow B
is
strongly monotonic
if
f
is
weakly monotonic and strict in all its arguments (and similar for
f\in A\Longrightarrow B
).

3
First-Order tuple interpretation

In this section, we will introduce the concept of tuple interpretations for many-sorted term rewriting.
This is the core methodology which the higher-order theory is built on top of.
This theory also has value by itself as a first-order termination and complexity technique.

It is common in the rewriting literature to use termination proofs to assess the
difficulty
of rewriting a term to normal form
[
7
,
27
]
.
The intuition comes from the idea that by ordering rewriting rules in descending order we gauge the order of magnitude of reduction.
The same principle applies for syntactic
[
24
,
25
,
33
]
and semantic
[
27
,
26
,
34
]
termination proofs.

On the semantic side there is a natural strategy:
given an extended well-founded set
\mathcal{A}=(A,>,\geq)
find an interpretation from terms
to elements of
A
so that
\llbracket s\rrbracket>\llbracket t\rrbracket
whenever
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
. (This can
typically be done by showing that
\llbracket\ell\rrbracket>\llbracket r\rrbracket
for all rules
\ell\to r
).
This interpretation holds information about the complexity of
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
since the maximum length of a
reduction starting in a term
s
is bounded by number of
>
steps that may be done starting in

\llbracket s\rrbracket
. If
\llbracket s\rrbracket
is a natural number, this gives a bound immediately.

In the setting of many-sorted term rewriting, we may formally define this as follows.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of sorts and
\mathcal{F}
an
\mathcal{S}
-signature.
A many-sorted monotonic algebra
\mathcal{A}
consists of a family of extended well-founded sets

(A_{\iota},>_{\iota},\geq_{\iota})_{\iota\in\mathcal{S}}
together with an interpretation
\mathcal{J}

which associates to each
\mathsf{\mathsf{f}::[\iota_{1}\times\dots\times\iota_{k}]\Rightarrow\kappa}
in

\mathcal{F}
a strongly monotonic function
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}\in A_{\iota_{1}}\times\dots\times A_{\iota_{k}}\Longrightarrow A_{\kappa}
.
Let
\alpha
be a function that maps variables of sort
\iota
to elements of
A_{\iota}
.
We extend
\mathcal{J}
to a function
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\alpha}
that maps terms of sort
\iota
to elements of
A_{\iota}
,
by letting

\llbracket x\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\alpha(x)
if
x
is a variable of sort
\iota
, and

\llbracket\mathsf{f}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}(\llbracket s_{1}\rrbracket_{\alpha},\dots,\llbracket s_{k}\rrbracket_{\alpha})
.
We say that a TRS
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
is
compatible
with
\mathcal{A}
if
\llbracket\ell\rrbracket_{\alpha}>\llbracket r\rrbracket_{\alpha}

for all
\alpha
and all
\ell\to r\in\mathcal{R}
.

We will generally omit the subscript
\alpha
when it is clear from
context, writing

\llbracket s\rrbracket
instead of
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}
.
In examples, we may write something like
\llbracket s\rrbracket=x+y
to mean
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\alpha(x)+\alpha(y)
.

Theorem 4
.

If
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
is compatible with
\mathcal{A}
then for all
\alpha
:

\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}>\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\alpha}
whenever
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
.

Proof Sketch.

By induction on the size of
s
using strong monotonicity of each
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}
.
∎

A common notion in the literature on complexity of term rewriting is
derivation height
:

\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(t):=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid\exists s.\,t\to^{n}s\}.

Intuitively,
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(t)
describes the worst-case number of steps for all possible reductions starting in
t
.
If
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
is terminating, then
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot)
is a total function.
If
(A_{\iota},>_{\iota})=(\mathbb{N},>)
then we easily see that
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\leq\llbracket t\rrbracket
for any term
t:\iota
.
Hence,
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket{}
can be used to bound the derivation height function.
However, this may give a severe overestimation,
as demonstrated below.

Example 5
.

Let
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}_{\mathsf{ab}}
be the TRS with only a rule
\mathsf{a}(\mathsf{b}(x))\to\mathsf{b}(\mathsf{a}(x))

and signature
\mathsf{a},\mathsf{b}:[\mathsf{string}]\Rightarrow\mathsf{string}
and
\mathsf{\epsilon}:\mathsf{string}
.
We can prove termination by the following interpretation:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{a}(x)\rrbracket

\displaystyle=2*x

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{b}(x)\rrbracket

\displaystyle=x+1

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{\epsilon}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=0

Indeed, we have
\llbracket\ell\rrbracket>\llbracket r\rrbracket
for the only rule as

\llbracket\mathsf{a}(\mathsf{b}(x))\rrbracket=2*x+2>2*x+1=\llbracket\mathsf{b}(\mathsf{a}(x))\rrbracket
.
Now consider a term
t=\mathsf{a}^{n}(\mathsf{b}^{m}(\mathsf{\epsilon}))
.
Then
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(t)=n*m
whereas
\llbracket t\rrbracket{}=2^{n}m
; an exponential difference!
Such an overestimation is problematic if we want to use
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket{}
to bound
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot)
.

We could find a tight bound for the system of Example
5
by a reasoning like the
following: for every term
s
, let
\mathit{\#bs}(s)
be the number of
\mathsf{b}
occurrences in
s
. For a
term
t
, let
\mathit{cost}(t)
denote
\sum\{\!\{\mathit{\#bs}(s)\mid\mathsf{a}(s)
is a subterm of
t\}\!\}
.
Then, the cost of a term decreases exactly by
1
in each step.
As the normal form has cost
0
, we find the tight bound
\mathit{cost}(\mathsf{a}^{n}(\mathsf{b}^{m}(\epsilon)))=n*m
.

This reasoning relies on tracking more than one value.
We can formalise this reasoning using an algebra
interpretation (and will do so in Example
8
), by choosing the right
\mathcal{A}
:

Definition 6
.

A
tuple algebra
is an algebra
\mathcal{A}=(A,\mathcal{J})
with
A=(A_{\iota},>_{\iota},\geq_{\iota})_{\iota\in\mathcal{S}}
such that each
A_{\iota}
has the form

\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota]}
(for an integer
K[\iota]\geq 1
) and
we let
\langle n_{1},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}\rangle\geq_{\iota}\langle n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}^{\prime}\rangle
if each
n_{i}\geq n_{i}^{\prime}
, and
\langle n_{1},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}\rangle>_{\iota}\langle n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}^{\prime}\rangle
if additionally
n_{1}>n_{1}^{\prime}
.

Intuitively, the first component always indicates “cost”: the number of steps needed to reduce a term
to normal form. This is the component that needs to decrease in each rewrite step to have
\llbracket s\rrbracket>\llbracket t\rrbracket
whenever
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
. The remaining components represent some value of interest for the
sort. This could for example be the size of the term (or its normal form), the length of a list,
or following Example
5
, the number of occurrences of a specific symbol.
For these components, we only require that they do not increase in a reduction step.

By the definition of
>_{\iota}
, and using Theorem
4
, we can
conclude:

Corollary 7
.

If a TRS
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
is compatible with a tuple algebra
then it is terminating and
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\leq\llbracket t\rrbracket_{1}
, for all terms
t
.
(Here,
\llbracket t\rrbracket_{1}
indicates the first component of the tuple
\llbracket t\rrbracket

Using this, we obtain a tight bound on the derivation height of

Example 8

The TRS

To build strongly monotonic functions we can for instance use the following observation:

Lemma 9
.

A function

F:\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota_{1}]}\times\dots\times\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota_{k}]}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}^{K[\kappa]}

is strongly monotonic if we can write

F(x^{1},\dots,x^{k})=\langle x^{1}_{1}+\dots+x^{k}_{1}+S_{1}(x^{1},\dots,x^{k}),\ S_{2}(x^{1},\dots,x^{k}),\dots,\ S_{K[\kappa]}(x^{1},\dots,x^{k})\rangle
,
where each
S_{i}
is a weakly monotonic function in
\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota_{1}]}\times\dots\times\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota_{k}]}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

Moreover, a function

S:\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota_{1}]}\times\dots\times\mathbb{N}^{K[\iota_{k}]}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

is weakly monotonic if it is built from constants in
\mathbb{N}
,
variable components
x_{j}^{n}
and weakly monotonic functions in
\mathbb{N}^{n}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

For the “weakly monotonic functions in
\mathbb{N}^{n}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
” we could for instance use
+
,
*
or
\max
.

To determine the length
K[\iota]
of the tuple for a sort
\iota
,
we use a semantic approach,
similar to one used in
[
19
]
in the context of functional languages:
the elements of the tuple are values of interest for the sort.
The two prominent examples in this paper are the sort
\mathsf{nat}
of natural numbers—which is constructed from the symbols

\mathsf{0::nat}
and
\mathsf{s::[nat]\Rightarrow nat}
—and the sort
\mathsf{list}
of lists of natural numbers—which is constructed using

\mathsf{nil::list}
and
\mathsf{cons::[nat\times list]\Rightarrow list}
.
For natural numbers, we
consider their size, so the number of
\mathsf{s}
s.
For lists, we consider both their length and an upper bound on the size of their elements.
This gives
K[\mathsf{nat}]=2
(cost of reducing the term, size of its normal form) and

K[\mathsf{list}]=3
(cost of reducing, length of normal form, maximum element size).
In the remainder of this paper, we will use
x_{\mathsf{c}}
as syntactic sugar for
x_{1}

(the cost component of
x
),
x_{\mathsf{s}}
and
x_{\mathsf{l}}
as
x_{2}
and
x_{\mathsf{m}}
as
x_{3}
.

Example 10
.

Consider the TRS defined in Example

.
We start by giving an interpretation for the type constructors: the symbols
\mathsf{0},\mathsf{nil},\mathsf{s}

and
\mathsf{cons}
which are used to construct natural numbers and lists. To be in line with the
semantics for the type interpretation, we let:

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{0}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\langle 0,0\rangle

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{s}(x)\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle

\displaystyle\llbracket\mathsf{nil}\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\langle 0,0,0\rangle

\displaystyle\llbracket x:xs\rrbracket

\displaystyle=\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{c}},xs_{\mathsf{l}}+1,\max(x_{\mathsf{s}},xs_{\mathsf{m}})\rangle

This expresses that
\mathsf{0}
has no evaluation cost and size
0
;
analogously,
\mathsf{nil}
has no evaluation cost and
0
as length and maximum element.
The cost of evaluating a term
\mathsf{s}(t)
depends entirely on the cost of the term’s argument
t
;
the size component counts the number of
\mathsf{s}
s.
The cost component for
\mathsf{cons}
similarly sums the costs of its arguments,
while the length is increased by 1, and the maximum element is the maximum between its head and tail.

For the remaining symbols we choose the following interpretations:

\begin{array}[]{rcllrcl}\llbracket x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}y\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{sum}(xs)\rrbracket&=&\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}}+2*xs_{\mathsf{l}}+xs_{\mathsf{l}}*xs_{\mathsf{m}}+1,xs_{\mathsf{l}}*xs_{\mathsf{m}}\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{rev}(xs)\rrbracket&=&\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{l}}+\frac{xs_{\mathsf{l}}*(xs_{\mathsf{l}}+1)}{2}+1,xs_{\mathsf{l}},xs_{\mathsf{m}}\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{append}(xs,ys)\rrbracket&=&\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}}+ys_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{l}}+1,xs_{\mathsf{l}}+ys_{\mathsf{l}},\max(xs_{\mathsf{m}},ys_{\mathsf{m}})\rangle\end{array}

Checking compatibility is easily done for the interpretation above, and strong monotonicity follows by
Lemma
9
(as
n\mapsto\frac{n*(n+1)}{2}\in\mathbb{N}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
is weakly monotonic).
We see that the cost of evaluating
\mathsf{append}
is linear in the first list length and independent of the size of the list elements,
while evaluating
\mathsf{sum}
gives a quadratic dependency on length and size combined.

Our tuple interpretations have some similarities with matrix interpretations

[
21
]
, where also each term is associated to an
n
-tuple. In essence, matrix
interpretations
are
tuple interpretations, for systems with only one sort. However, the
shape of the interpretation functions
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}
in matrix interpretations is limited
to functions following Lemma

where each

is a linear multivariate polynomial.
Hence, our interpretations are a strict generalisation, which also admits interpretations such as
those used for
\mathsf{sum}
,
\mathsf{rev}
and
\mathsf{append}
in Example

.

For the purpose of termination, tuple interpretations strictly extend the power of both
polynomial interpretations and matrix interpretations already in the first-order case.

Example 11
.

A TRS that implements division in
[
4
]
shows a limitation of polynomial interpretations:
it contains a rule
\mathsf{quot}(\mathsf{s}(x),\mathsf{s}(y))\to\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{quot}(\mathsf{minus}(x,y),\mathsf{s}(y)))
which cannot
be oriented by any polynomial interpretation, because
\llbracket\mathsf{minus}(x,\mathsf{s}(x))\rrbracket>\llbracket\mathsf{s}(x)\rrbracket
for any strongly monotonic polynomial
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{minus}}
. Due to the duplication of

, this rule also cannot be handled by a matrix interpretation.
However, we do have a compatible tuple interpretation:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\llbracket\mathsf{0}\rrbracket&=&\langle 0,0\rangle&&\llbracket\mathsf{minus}(x,y)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{s}(x)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle&&\llbracket\mathsf{quot}(x,y)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}*y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}*y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\\
\end{array}

In practice, in first-order termination or complexity analysis one would not exclusively use
interpretations, but rather a combination of different techniques.
In that context, tuple interpretations may be used as one part of a large toolbox.
They are likely to offer a simple complexity proof in many cases,
but they are unlikely to be an essential technique since so many other methods have already been developed.
Indeed, all examples in this section can be handled with previously established theory.
For instance, Example
5
can be handled with matrix interpretations,
while
\mathsf{sum}
and
\mathsf{rev}
may be analysed using ideas from
[
24
]
and
[
34
]
.

However, developing a new technique for first-order termination and traditional complexity analysis
is not our goal.
Our method
does
provide a more fine-grained notion of complexity, which may
consider information such as the length of a list.
Moreover, the first-order case is an important
stepping stone towards higher-order analysis, where far fewer methods exist.

4
Higher-order tuple interpretations

In this section, we will extend the ideas from Section
3
to the higher-order setting,
and hence define the core notion of this paper: higher-order tuple interpretations. To do this, we will
build on the notion of
strongly monotonic algebras
originating in
[
38
]
.

4.1
Strongly monotonic algebras

In first-order term rewriting, the complexity of a TRS is often measured as
runtime
or

derivational
complexity.
Both measures consider initial terms
s
of a certain shape,
and supply a bound on
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)
given the size of
s
.
However, this is not a good approach for higher-order terms: the behaviour of a term of higher
type generally cannot be captured in an integer.

Example 12
.

Consider the AFS obtained by combining Examples
1
and
.
The evaluation cost of a term
\mathsf{foldl}(F,n,q)
depends almost completely on the behaviour of the
functional subterm
F
, and not only on its evaluation cost.
To see this, consider two cases:

F_{1}:=\lambda x.\lambda y.y\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}x
, and

F_{2}:=\lambda x.\lambda y.x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}x
.
For natural numbers
n,m
, the evaluation cost of both
F_{1}(n,m)
and
F_{2}(n,m)
is the same:

n+1
. However, the
size
of the result is different. Hence, the number of
steps needed to compute
\mathsf{foldl}(F_{1},n,q)
for a number
n
and list
q
is quadratic in
the size of
n
and
q
, while the number of steps needed for
\mathsf{foldl}(F_{2},n,q)
is exponential.

As Example
12
shows, higher-order rewriting is a natural place to separate cost and size.
But more than that, we need to know what a function does with its arguments: whether it is
size-increasing, how long it takes to evaluate them, and more.

This is naturally captured by the notion of (weakly or strongly) monotonic algebras for higher-order
rewriting introduced by v.d. Pol
[
38
]
: here, a term of arrow type is interpreted as a
function, which allows the interpretation to retain all relevant information.

Monotonic interpretations were originally defined for a different higher-order rewriting
formalism, which does make some difference in the way abstraction and application is handled.

Weakly
monotonic algebras were transposed to AFSs in
[
22
]
;
however, here we extend the more natural notion of
hereditarily
monotonic algebras
which v.d. Pol only briefly considered.
1
1

1

In
[
38
]
, v.d. Pol rejects hereditarily (or: strongly) monotonic algebras because
they are not so well-suited for analysing the HRS format
[
35
]
where reasoning is modulo

\to_{\beta}
: it is impossible to both interpret all terms of function type to strongly monotonic
functions and have
\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\ t\rrbracket=\llbracket s[x:=t]\rrbracket
.
In the AFS format, we do not have the latter requirement.
In
[
22
]
, where the authors considered the AFS format like we do here (but for interpretations to
\mathbb{N}
rather than to tuples), weakly monotonic
algebras were used because they are a more natural choice
in the context of dependency pairs.

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of sorts and
\mathcal{F}
a higher-order signature.
We assume given for every sort
\iota
an extended well-founded set

(A_{\iota},>_{\iota},\geq_{\iota})
.
From this, we define the set of
strongly monotonic functionals
, as follows:

•

For all sorts
\iota
:
\mathcal{M}_{\iota}:=A_{\iota}
and
\sqsupset_{\iota}\,:=\,>_{\iota}

and
\sqsupseteq_{\iota}\,:=\,\geq_{\iota}
.

•

For an arrow type
\sigma\Rightarrow\tau
:

–

\mathcal{M}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}:=\{F\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}\mid F
is strongly monotoic
\}

–

F\sqsupset_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}G
iff
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
is non-empty and

\forall x\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}.F(x)\sqsupset_{\tau}G(x)
, and

F\sqsupseteq_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}G
iff
\forall x\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}.F(x)\sqsupseteq_{\tau}G(x)
.

That is,
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}
contains strongly monotonic functions from
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
to

\mathcal{M}_{\tau}
and both
\sqsupset_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}
and
\sqsupseteq_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}
do a point-wise comparison.
By a straightforward induction on types we have:

Lemma 14
.

For all types
\sigma
,
(\mathcal{M}_{\sigma},\sqsupset_{\sigma},\sqsupseteq_{\sigma})
is an extended well-founded set;
that is:

•

\sqsupset_{\sigma}
is well-founded and
\sqsupseteq_{\sigma}
is reflexive;

•

both
\sqsupset_{\sigma}
and
\sqsupseteq_{\sigma}
are transitive;

•

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
,
x\sqsupset_{\sigma}y
implies
x\sqsupseteq_{\sigma}y
and

x\sqsupset_{\sigma}y\sqsupseteq_{\sigma}z
implies
x\sqsupset_{\sigma}z
.

We will define
higher-order strongly monotonic algebras
as an extension of Definition
3
,
mapping a term of type
\sigma
to an element of
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
.
Functional terms
\mathsf{f}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
and variables can be handled as before, but we now also have to deal with application and abstraction.
Application is straightforward: since terms of higher type are mapped to functions, we can interpret application as function application, i.e.,

\llbracket s\cdot t\rrbracket_{\alpha}:=\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}(\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\alpha})
.
However, abstraction is more difficult.
The natural choice would be to view abstraction as defining a function; i.e.,
let
\llbracket\lambda x.s\rrbracket_{\alpha}
be the function
d\mapsto\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=d]}
.
Unfortunately, this is not necessarily monotonic:
d\mapsto\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=d]}
is strongly monotonic only if
x
occurs freely in
s
.
For example
\lambda x.\mathsf{0}
would be mapped to a constant function, which is not in
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}}
.
Moreover, this definition would give
\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\cdot t\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\llbracket s[x:=t]\rrbracket_{\alpha}
, so
\beta
-steps would not be counted toward the evaluation cost.

We handle both problems by using a choosable function
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}
, which takes a function that may be strongly monotonic or constant, and turns it strongly monotonic.

Definition 15
.

A
(\sigma,\tau)
-monotonicity function
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}
is a
strongly monotonic function in
C_{\sigma,\tau}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\sigma\Longrightarrow\tau}
,
where the set
C_{\sigma,\tau}
is defined as
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}\cup\{F\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}\mid F(x)=F(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}\}
. (Here, the set
C_{\sigma,\tau}
is ordered by point-wise comparison.)

With this definition, we are ready to define strongly monotonic algebras.

Definition 16
.

A strongly monotonic algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
consists of a family

(\mathcal{M}_{\sigma},\sqsupset_{\sigma},\sqsupseteq_{\sigma})_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{T}}
,
an interpretation function
\mathcal{J}
which associates to each

\mathsf{\mathsf{f}::[\sigma_{1}\times\dots\times\sigma_{k}]\Rightarrow\tau}

in
\mathcal{F}
an element of

\mathcal{M}_{\sigma_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\sigma_{k}\Rightarrow\tau}
,
and a
(\sigma,\tau)
-monotonicity function
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}
,
for each
\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{T}
.

Let
\alpha
be a function that maps variables of type
\sigma

to elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
.
We extend
\mathcal{J}
to a function
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{\alpha}
that
maps terms of type
\sigma
to elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
, as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\llbracket x\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\alpha(x)\ \text{for variables}\ x&&\llbracket\mathsf{f}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}(\llbracket s_{1}\rrbracket_{\alpha},\dots,\llbracket s_{k}\rrbracket_{\alpha})\\
\llbracket s\cdot t\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}(\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\alpha})&&\llbracket\lambda x.s\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}(d\mapsto\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=d]})\ \text{if}\ x::\sigma\ \text{and}\ s::\tau\end{array}

We can see by induction on
s
that for
s::\sigma
indeed
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
.
We say that an AFS
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
is
compatible
with
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
if for all valuations
\alpha
both
(1)
\llbracket\ell\rrbracket_{\alpha}\sqsupset\llbracket r\rrbracket_{\alpha}
,
for all
\ell\to r\in\mathcal{R}
; and
(2)
\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\,t\rrbracket_{\alpha}\sqsupset\llbracket s[x:=t]\rrbracket_{\alpha}
,
for any
s::\sigma
,

and
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\tau}
.

As before, we will typically omit the
\alpha
subscript and use notation like
\llbracket s\rrbracket=F(x+3)

to denote
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\alpha(F)(\alpha(x)+3)
.
When types are not relevant, we will denote
\sqsupset
instead of specifying
\sqsupset_{\sigma}
, and
we may write
f\in\mathcal{M}
to mean

for some
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}\!\mathcal{T}
.

We extend Theorem
4
into the following compatibility result.

Theorem 17
.

If
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
is compatible with

,
then for all
\alpha

\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha}\sqsupset\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\alpha}
when
s\to_{\mathcal{R}}t
.

For Definition
13
and Theorem
17
, we can choose the well-founded sets

(A_{\iota},>_{\iota},\geq_{\iota})
for each sort, and the functions
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}

A
higher-order tuple algebra
is a strongly monotonic algebra where each

(A_{\iota},>_{\iota},\geq_{\iota})
follows Definition

.

Example 18
.

Let
A_{\mathsf{nat}}=\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
A_{\mathsf{list}}=\mathbb{N}^{3}
as before, and assume
\mathsf{cons}
and
\mathsf{nil}
are interpreted as in Example
10
.
Consider the rules for
\mathsf{map}
in Example

We let:

\llbracket\mathsf{map}(F,xs)\rrbracket=\langle(xs_{\mathsf{l}}+1)*(F(\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}},xs_{\mathsf{m}}\rangle)_{\mathsf{c}}+1),\,xs_{\mathsf{l}},\,F(xs_{\mathsf{c}},xs_{\mathsf{m}})_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle

This expresses that
\mathsf{map}
does not increase the list length (as the length component is just
xs_{\mathsf{l}}
),
the greatest element of the result is bounded by the value of
F
on the greatest element of
xs
,
and the evaluation cost is mostly expressed by a number of
F

xs
.
We will see in Lemma

that
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{map}}
is indeed strongly monotonic.

To prove compatibility of the AFS with
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{M}}
, we must first see that
\llbracket\ell\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket r\rrbracket
for all rules
\ell\to_{\mathcal{R}}r
. For the first
\mathsf{map}
rule this is easy:

\llbracket\mathsf{map}(F,\mathsf{nil})\rrbracket=\langle F(\langle 0,0\rangle)_{\mathsf{c}}+1,0,F(\langle 0,0\rangle)_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\sqsupset_{\mathsf{list}}\langle 0,0,0\rangle=\llbracket\mathsf{nil}\rrbracket
.
For the second
\mathsf{map}

\langle\text{cost-}\ell,\text{len-}\ell,\text{max-}\ell\rangle\sqsupset_{\mathsf{list}}\langle\text{cost-}r,\text{len-}r,\text{max-}r\rangle
; that is,

\text{cost-}\ell>\text{cost-}r
and
\text{len-}\ell\geq\text{len-}r
and
\text{max-}\ell\geq\text{max-}r
, where:

\begin{array}[]{lclclcccl}\text{cost-}\ell&=&\llbracket\mathsf{map}(F,x:xs)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{c}}&=&\lx@intercol(xs_{\mathsf{l}}+2)*(F(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{c}},\max(x_{\mathsf{s}},xs_{\mathsf{m}})\rangle)_{\mathsf{c}}+1)\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\text{cost-}r&=&\llbracket F(x):\mathsf{map}(F,xs)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{c}}&=&\lx@intercol F(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle)_{\mathsf{c}}+(xs_{\mathsf{l}}+1)*(F(\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}},xs_{\mathsf{m}}\rangle)_{\mathsf{c}}+1)\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\text{len-}\ell&=&\llbracket\mathsf{map}(F,x:xs)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{l}}&=&xs_{\mathsf{l}}+1&=&\llbracket F(x):\mathsf{map}(F,xs)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{l}}&=&\text{len-}r\\
\text{max-}\ell&=&\llbracket\mathsf{map}(F,x:xs)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{m}}&=&\lx@intercol F(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{c}},\max(x_{\mathsf{s}},xs_{\mathsf{m}})\rangle)_{\mathsf{s}}\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\text{max-}r&=&\llbracket F(x):\mathsf{map}(F,xs)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{m}}&=&\lx@intercol\max(F(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle)_{\mathsf{s}},F(\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}},xs_{\mathsf{m}}\rangle)_{\mathsf{s}})\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\end{array}

To see why

observe that for all

\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{c}},\max(x_{\mathsf{s}}+xs_{\mathsf{m}})\rangle\sqsupseteq_{{\rm Nature}}

and
\langle xs_{\mathsf{c}},xs_{\mathsf{m}}\rangle
.
Since

therefore

both

and

We find

by a similar reasoning.

4.2
Interpreting abstractions

Example
18

\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}

\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\ t\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket s[x:=t]\rrbracket

To achieve this, we will define some standard functions to build elements of
\mathcal{M}
.
This allows us to easily construct strongly monotonic functionals, both to build
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}

and to create interpretation functions
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}

Definition 19
.

For every type
\sigma

\mathtt{0}_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}

\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

\mathtt{addc}_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}

by mutual recursion on
\sigma

\displaystyle\mathtt{0}_{\iota}=\langle 0,\dots,0\rangle

\displaystyle\mathtt{0}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}=d\mapsto\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}(d),\mathtt{0}_{\tau})

\displaystyle\mathtt{costof}_{\iota}(\langle n_{1},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}\rangle)=n_{1}

\displaystyle\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}(F)=\mathtt{costof}_{\tau}(F(\mathtt{0}_{\sigma}))

\displaystyle\mathtt{addc}_{\iota}(c,\langle n_{1},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}\rangle)=\langle c+n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{K[\iota]}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathtt{addc}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}(c,F)=d\mapsto\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(c,F(d))

Here,
\mathtt{0}_{\sigma}
defines the
minimal element
of
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
.
The function
\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}
maps every

to the cost component of

; hence, if
F\sqsupset_{\sigma}G
we have

\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}(F)>\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}(G)
.
The function
\mathtt{addc}_{\sigma}
pointwise increases an element of
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
by adding to the cost component:
if

\mathtt{addc}(c,F)(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\langle c+n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}\rangle
.

It is easy to see that
\mathtt{0}_{\sigma}
and
\mathtt{addc}_{\sigma}(n,X)
are in
\mathcal{M}
for all
\sigma
(by

\sigma

\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}
and
\mathtt{addc}_{\sigma}
are

Various properties of these functions are detailed in the appendix
(Lemmas

–

).
We will particularly use that always

F(\mathtt{addc}(n,x))\sqsupseteq\mathtt{addc}(n,F(x))
(Lemma

) and

\mathtt{costof}(F(x))\geq\mathtt{costof}(x)
(Lemma

).

We can use these functions to for instance create candidates for
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}
.
While many suitable definitions are possible, we will particularly consider the following:

Definition 20
.

For types
\sigma,\tau
, and
F
a weakly monotonic function in
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}
, let:

\Phi_{\sigma,\tau}(F)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}d\mapsto\mathtt{addc}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}(1,F(d))&\text{if}\ F\ \text{is in}\ \mathcal{M}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}\\

\end{array}\right.

Then

-monotonicity function.
To see this, the most challenging part is proving that

if

and
F\in\mathcal{M}_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}

while

is a constant function. We can prove this using the result that

\mathtt{addc}(1,x)\sqsupseteq y
for all

.
We have:

Lemma 21
.

If
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}=\Phi_{\sigma,\tau}
then

\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\,t\rrbracket\sqsupset_{\tau}\llbracket s[x:=t]\rrbracket
, for

s::\tau
,
t::\sigma
,
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\sigma}
.

Proof Sketch.

We expand
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}
to achieve

\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\,t\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(\mathtt{costof}_{\sigma}(\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\alpha})+1,\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=\llbracket t\rrbracket]})

or

\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\,t\rrbracket_{\alpha}=\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(1,\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=\llbracket t\rrbracket]})
.
By induction on
\tau
we prove that
\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(n,F)\sqsupset_{\tau}F
for all

.
So either way,
\llbracket(\lambda x.s)\,t\rrbracket_{\alpha}\sqsupset_{\tau}\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=\llbracket t\rrbracket]}
.
Finally, we prove a substitution lemma,

\llbracket s\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\alpha}]}=\llbracket s[x:=t]\rrbracket_{\alpha}

by induction on

.
∎

In examples in the remainder of this paper, we will assume that

With these choices we do not only orient the

(and thus satisfy item (2) of the compatibility conditions),
but also the

Lemma 22
.

If
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\sigma,\tau}=\Phi_{\sigma,\tau}

then for any

\llbracket\lambda x.F\,x\rrbracket\sqsupset_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}\llbracket F\rrbracket

Proof Sketch.

Since

\llbracket F\rrbracket_{\alpha[x:=d]}=\alpha(F)

Consequently,

\llbracket\lambda x.F\,x\rrbracket\sqsupseteq_{\sigma\Rightarrow\tau}d\mapsto\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(1,F(d))

\mathtt{addc}_{\tau}(1,F(d))\sqsupset_{\tau}F(d)

∎

5
Finding complexity bounds

A key notion in complexity analysis of first-order rewriting is
runtime complexity
. In
this section, we will define a conservative notion of runtime complexity for higher-order term
rewriting, and show how our interpretations can be used to find runtime complexity bounds.

In first-order (and many-sorted) term rewriting, a
defined symbol
is any function symbol

\mathsf{f}
such that there is a rule
\mathsf{f}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})\to r
in the system;
all other symbols are called
constructors
.
A
ground constructor term
is a ground term without defined symbols.
A
basic term
has the form
\mathsf{f}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
with
\mathsf{f}
a defined
symbol and
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
all ground constructor terms.
The
runtime complexity
of a TRS is then a function
\varphi
in
(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\})\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
that maps each
n
to
a number
\varphi(n)
so that for every basic term
s
of size at most
n
:
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)\leq\varphi(n)
.

The comparable notion of
derivational complexity
considers the derivation height for
arbitrary ground terms of size
n
, but we will not use that here, since it can often give very
high bounds that are not necessarily representative for realistic use of the system.
In practice, a computation with a TRS would typically start with a main function,
which takes
data
(e.g., natural numbers, lists) as input.
This is exactly a basic term.
Hence, the notion of runtime complexity roughly captures the worst-case number of steps for a realistic computation.

It is not obvious how this notion translates to the higher-order setting.
It may be tempting to literally apply the definition to an AFS,
but a “ground constructor term” (or perhaps “closed constructor term”)
is not a natural concept in higher-order rewriting; it does not intuitively capture data.
Moreover, we would like to create a
robust
notion which can be extended to simple functional programming languages,
so which is not subject to minor language difference like whether partial application of function symbols is allowed.

Instead, there are two obvious ways to capture the idea of input in higher-order rewriting:

•

closed irreducible terms
; this includes all ground constructor terms, but also
for instance
\lambda x.\mathsf{0}\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}x
(but not
\lambda x.x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{0}
, since this can be
rewritten following the rules in Example
1
);

•

data
: this includes only ground constructor terms with no higher-order subterms.

As we observed in Example
12
, the size of a higher-order term does not capture its
behaviour.
Hence, a notion of runtime complexity using closed irreducible terms is not obviously meaningful—and might be closer to
derivational
complexity due to defined symbols inside abstractions.
Therefore, we here take the conservative choice and consider
data
.

Definition 28
.

In an AFS
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
,
a
data constructor
is a function symbol
\mathsf{c}::[\iota_{1}\times\dots\times\iota_{k}]\Rightarrow\iota_{0}
with each
\iota_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
, such that there is no rule of the form

\mathsf{c}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k})\to r
.
A
data term
is a term
\mathsf{c}(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
such that
\mathsf{c}
is a constructor and all

d_{i}
are also data terms.

In practice, a sort is defined by its data constructors.
For example,
{\rm Nature}
is defined by
\mathsf{0}
and
\mathsf{s}
, and
\mathsf{list}
by
\mathsf{nil}
and
\mathsf{cons}
.
In typical examples of first- and higher-order term rewriting systems,
rules are defined to exhaustively pattern match on all constructors for a sort.

With this definition, we can conservatively extend the original notion of runtime complexity to be
applicable to both many-sorted and higher-order term rewriting.

Definition 29
.

A
basic term
is a term of the form
\mathsf{f}(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
with
all
d_{i}
data terms and
\mathsf{f}
not a data constructor.
We let
|d|
denote the total number of symbols in a basic term
d
.

The
runtime complexity
of an AFS is a function

\varphi\in(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\})\Longrightarrow\mathbb{N}

so that for all
n

and basic terms
d
, with
|d|\leq n
:
\mathtt{dh}_{\mathcal{R}}(d)\leq\varphi(n)
.

Note that if
\mathsf{f}(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})
is a basic term, then
\mathsf{f}::[\iota_{1}\times\dots\times\iota_{k}]\Rightarrow\tau
with all
\iota_{i}
sorts. Hence,
higher-order runtime complexity considers the same (first-order) notion of basic terms
as the first-order case;
terms such as
\mathsf{map}(F,s)
or even
\mathsf{map}(\lambda x.\mathsf{s}(x),\mathsf{nil})
are not basic.
One might reasonably question whether such a first-order notion is useful when studying the
complexity of
higher-order
term rewriting.
However, we argue that it is:
runtime complexity aims to address the length of computations that begin at a typical starting point.
When performing a
full program
analysis of an AFS,
the computation will still typically start in a basic term, for instance;
the entry-point symbol
\mathsf{main}
applied to some user input
d_{1},\dots,d_{k}
.

Example 30
.

We consider an AFS from the Termination Problem Database, v11.0
[
16
]
.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{0}&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&x&&\mathsf{rec}(\mathsf{0},y,F)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&y\\
x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{s}(y)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\mathsf{s}(x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}y)&&\mathsf{rec}(\mathsf{s}(x),y,F)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&F\cdot x\cdot\mathsf{rec}(x,y,F)\\
&&&&x\otimes y&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\mathsf{rec}(y,\mathsf{0},\lambda n.\lambda m.x\oplus m)\\
\end{array}

Here,
\mathsf{rec}::[{\rm Nature}\times{\rm Nature}\times({\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow{\rm Nature})]\Rightarrow{\rm Nature}
.
The only basic terms have the form
\mathsf{s}^{n}(\mathsf{0})\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{s}^{m}(\mathsf{0})
or
\mathsf{s}^{n}(\mathsf{0})\otimes\mathsf{s}^{m}(\mathsf{0})
. Using our method, we obtain cubic runtime complexity; to be precise:

\mathcal{O}(m^{2}*n)
. The interpretation functions are found in Appendix
A
.

To derive runtime complexity for both first- and higher-order rewriting,
our approach is to consider bounds for the functions
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}
;
we only need to consider the first-order symbols
\mathsf{f}
.

Definition 31
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{M}_{\iota_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\iota_{m}\Rightarrow\kappa}
be of the form

P(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})=\langle P_{1}(x^{1},\dots,x^{m}),\linebreak\dots,P_{K[\kappa]}(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})\rangle
.
Then
P
is
linearly bounded
if each component function
P_{l}
of
P
is upper-bounded by a positive
linear polynomial, i.e., there is a constant
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that

P_{l}(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})\leq a*(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{K[\iota_{i}]}x^{i}_{j})
.
We say that
P
is
additive
if
there exists a constant
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{l=1}^{K[\kappa]}P_{l}(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})\leq a+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{K[\iota_{i}]}x^{i}_{j}
.

By this definition,
P_{l}
is not required to be a linear function, only to be bounded by one.
This means that for instance
\min(x^{i}_{j},2*x^{a}_{b})
can be used, but
x^{i}_{j}*x^{a}_{b}
cannot.
It is easily checked that all the data constructors in this paper have an additive interpretation.
For example, for
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{cons}}
:
(x_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{c}})+(x_{\mathsf{l}}+1)+\max(x_{\mathsf{s}},xs_{\mathsf{m}})\leq 1+x_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+xs_{\mathsf{c}}+xs_{\mathsf{l}}+xs_{\mathsf{s}}
.

Lemma 32
.

Let
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
be an AFS or TRS that is compatible with a strongly monotonic algebra with interpretation
function
\mathcal{J}
.
Then:

1.

if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
is additive for all data constructors
\mathsf{c}
, then there exists
a constant
b>0
in
\mathbb{N}
so that for all data terms
s
: if
|s|\leq n
then

\llbracket s\rrbracket_{l}\leq b*n
, for each component
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{l}
of
\llbracket s\rrbracket
;

2.

if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
is linearly bounded for all data constructors
\mathsf{c}
, then there exists
a constant
b>0
in
\mathbb{N}
so that for all data terms
s
: if
|s|\leq n
then
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{l}\leq 2^{b*n}
, for each component
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{l}
of
\llbracket s\rrbracket
.

By using Lemma
32
, we quickly obtain some ways to bound runtime complexity:

Corollary 33
.

Let
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
be an AFS or TRS that is compatible with a strongly monotonic algebra with interpretation
function
\mathcal{J}
, and let
\mathcal{F}_{C}
denote its set of data constructors, and

\mathcal{F}_{B}
the set of all other symbols
\mathsf{f}
with a signature
\mathsf{f}::[\iota_{1}\times\dots\times\iota_{m}]\Rightarrow\tau
.
Then:

•

if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}
is additive for all
\mathsf{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{C}\cup\mathcal{F}_{B}
, then
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
has linear runtime complexity;

•

if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}
is additive for all
\mathsf{c}\in\mathcal{F}_{C}
and
for all
\mathsf{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{B}
,
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}(\vec{x})=(P_{1}(\vec{x}),\dots,P_{k}(\vec{x}))
where
P_{1}
is bounded by a polynomial, then
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
has
polynomial runtime complexity;

•

if
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{f}}
is linearly bounded for all
\mathsf{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{C}\cup\mathcal{F}_{B}
, then
{(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{R})}
has exponential runtime complexity.

We could easily use these results as part of an automatic complexity tool—and indeed, combine
them with other methods for complexity analysis. However, this is not truly our goal: runtime
complexity is only a part of the picture, especially in higher-order term rewriting where we may
want to analyse modules that get much more hairy input. Our technique aims to give more fine-grained
information, where we consider the impact of input with certain properties—like the length of a
list or the depth of a tree. For this, the person interested in the analysis should be the one to
decide on the interpretations of the constructors.

With this information given, though, it should be possible to automatically find interpretations
for the other functions.
The search for the best strategy requires dedicated research,
which we leave to future work; however, we expect Lemmas
23
and
25
to play a
large role. We also note that while the cost component may depend on the other components, the
other components (which represent a kind of size property) typically do not depend on the cost.

6
On Related Work

Rewriting.

There are several first-order complexity techniques based on interpretations.
For example, in
[
11
]
, the consequences of using additive, linear,
and polynomial interpretations to the natural numbers are investigated;
and in
[
26
]
, context-dependent interpretations are introduced, which map terms to real numbers to obtain tighter bounds.
Most closely related to our approach are
matrix interpretations

[
21
,
34
]
, and a technique by the first author
for complexity analysis of conditional term rewriting
[
31
]
.
In both cases, terms are mapped to tuples as they are in our approach, although neither considers sort information,
and matrix interpretations use linear interpretation functions.
Our technique is a generalisation of both.

Higher-order Rewriting.

In
higher-order
term rewriting (but a formalism without
\lambda
-abstraction),
Baillot and Dal Lago
[
10
]
develop a version of higher-order polynomial interpretations which,
like the present work, is based on v.d. Pol’s higher-order interpretations
[
38
]
.
In similar ways to our Section
5
, the authors enforce polynomial bounds on derivational complexity
by imposing restrictions on the shape of interpretations.
Their method differs from ours in various ways, most importantly by mapping terms to
\mathbb{N}
rather than tuples.
In addition, the interpretations are limited to higher-order polynomials.
This yields an ordering with the subterm property (i.e.,
\mathsf{f}(\dots,s,\dots)\sqsupset s
), which means that
TRSs like Example
11
cannot be handled. Moreover, it is not possible to find a general
interpretation for functions like
\mathsf{foldl}
or
\mathsf{rec}
; the method can only handle instances of

\mathsf{foldl}
with a linear function.

Beyond this, it unfortunately seems that relatively little work has thus far been done on complexity analysis of higher-order term rewriting.
However, complexity of
functional programs
is an active field of research with a close relation to higher-order term rewriting.

Functional Programming.

There are various techniques to statically analyse resource use of functional programs.
These
may be fully automated
[
5
,
9
,
41
]
,
semi-automated designed to reason about programmer specified-bounds
[
44
,
15
,
23
]
,
or even manual techniques, integrated with type system or program logic semantics
[
14
,
17
]
.
We discuss the most pertinent ones.

An approach using rewriting for full-program analysis is to translate functional programs to TRSs
[
6
]
,
which can be analysed using first-order complexity techniques.
This takes advantage of the large body of work on first-order complexity, but loses
information; the transformation often yields a system that is harder to analyse than the original.

The research methodology in most studies in functional programming differs significantly from rewriting
techniques.
Nevertheless,
there are some studies with clear connections to our approach;
in particular our separation of cost and size (and other structural properties).
Most relevant, in
[
19
]
the authors use a similar approach by giving semantics to a complexity-aware
intermediate language allowing arbitrary user-defined notions for size—such as list length or maximum
element size; recurrence relations are then extracted to represent the complexity.

Additionally, most modern complexity analysis is done via enhancements at the type system level

[
2
,
5
,
28
,
39
,
23
,
20
]
.
For example, types may be annotated with a counter, the heap size or a data type’s size measure.
Notably, a line of work on Resource-Aware ML
[
28
,
36
,
30
]

studies resource use of OCaml programs with methods based on Tarjan’s amortized analysis
[
42
]
.
Types are annotated with
potentials
(a cost measure), and type inference generates a set of
linear constraints which is sent over to an external solver.
For Haskell, Liquid Haskell
[
40
,
43
]
provides a language to annotate types, which can be
used to prove properties of the program; this was recently extended to include complexity
[
23
]
.
Unlike RAML, this approach is not fully automatic: type annotations are checked, not derived.

These works in functional programming have a different purpose from ours: they study the resource
use in a specific language, typically with a fixed evaluation strategy.
Our method, in contrast, allows for arbitrary evaluation, which could be specified to various
strategies in future work.
Moreover, most of these works limit interest to full-program analysis. We do this for runtime
complexity, but our method offers more, by providing general interpretations for individual functions
like
\mathsf{map}
or
\mathsf{foldl}
. Similarly, most of these works impose additive type annotations for the
constructors; we do not restrict the constructor interpretations outside Lemma
32
.
On the other hand, many do consider (shallow) polymorphism, which we do not.

While in functional programming one considers resource usage
[
39
,
28
]
,
rewriting is concerned with the number of steps,
which can be translated to a form of resource measure if the true cost of each step is kept low.
This is achieved by imposing restrictions on reduction strategy and term representation
[
1
,
18
]
.
Our approach carries the blessing of being general and machine independent
and the curse of not necessarily being a reasonable cost model.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we have introduced tuple interpretations for many-sorted and higher-order term rewriting.
This includes providing a new definition of strongly monotonic algebras,
a compatibility theorem, a function
\mathit{MakeSM}

that orients
\beta
- and
\eta
-reductions,
and several lemmas to prove monotonicity of interpretation functions.
We also show that for certain restrictions on interpretation functions, we find linear,
polynomial or exponential bounds on runtime complexity
(for a simple but natural definition of higher-order runtime complexity).

Our type-based, semantical approach allows us to relate various “size” notions (e.g., list length,
tree depth, term size. etc.) to reduction cost,
and thus offers a more fine-grained analysis than traditional notions like runtime complexity.
Most importantly, we can express the complexity of a higher-order function in terms of the behaviour of its
(function) arguments. In the future, we hope that this could be used towards a truly higher-order complexity notion.

Some further examples and weaknesses.

Aside from the three higher-order examples in this paper, we have successfully applied our method
to a variety of higher-order benchmarks in the Termination Problem Database
[
16
]
, all with
additive interpretations for the constructors. Two additional examples
(
filter
and
deriv
) are included in Appendix
A
.

A clear weakness we discovered was that our method can only handle “plain function-passing”
systems
[
32
]
. That is, we typically do not succeed on systems where a
variable of function type occurs inside a subterm of base type, and occurs outside this subterm
in the right-hand side.
Examples of such systems are
ordrec
, which has a rule
\mathsf{ordrec}(\mathsf{lim}(F),x,G,H)\to_{\mathcal{R}}H\cdot F\cdot(\lambda n.\mathsf{ordrec}(F\cdot n,x,G,H))
with
\mathsf{lim}::[{\rm Nature}\Rightarrow\mathsf{ord}]\Rightarrow\mathsf{ord}
, and
apply
, which has a rule
\mathsf{lapply}(x,\mathsf{fcons}(F,xs))\to_{\mathcal{R}}F\cdot\mathsf{lapply}(x,xs)
with
\mathsf{fcons}::[(\mathsf{a}\Rightarrow\mathsf{a})\times\mathsf{listf}]\Rightarrow\mathsf{listf}
.

Future work.

We intend to consider the effect of different evaluation strategies, such
as innermost evaluation, weak-innermost evaluation (where rewriting below an abstraction is not allowed, as
is commonly the case in functional programming) or outermost evaluation.
This extension is likely to be an important step towards another goal: to more closely relate our complexity
notion to a reasonable measure of resource consumption in a rewriting engine.

In addition, we plan to extend first-order complexity techniques like dependency tuples
[
24
]
,
which may allow us to overcome the weakness described above.
Another goal is to enrich our type system to support a notion of polymorphism and
add polymorphic interpretations into the play.
We also aim to develop a tool to automatically find suitable tuple interpretations.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\llbracket\mathsf{0}\rrbracket&=&\langle 0,0\rangle\\

\llbracket x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}y\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\\
\llbracket x\otimes y\rrbracket&=&\langle 1+y_{\mathsf{s}}*(x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}*(y_{\mathsf{s}}+1)/2+3),x_{\mathsf{s}}*y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{rec}(x,y,F)\rrbracket&=&\mathit{Helper}(x,F)^{x_{\mathsf{s}}}(\ \langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ )\\
\mathit{Helper}(x,F)&=&z\mapsto\langle F(x,z)_{\mathsf{c}},\max(z_{\mathsf{s}},F(x,z)_{\mathsf{s}})\rangle\\
\end{array}

Then we always have (*A)
\mathit{Helper}(x,F)(z)\sqsupseteq_{{\rm Nature}}z
because

F(x,z)_{\mathsf{c}}\geq z_{\mathsf{c}}
which we will see in Lemma
41
,
and clearly
\max(z_{\mathsf{s}},F(x,z)_{\mathsf{s}})\geq z_{\mathsf{s}}
.
Hence, the monotonicity requirements are satisfied.
We also clearly have (*B)
\mathit{Helper}(x,F)(z)\sqsupseteq_{{\rm Nature}}F(x,z)
,
since clearly
\max(z_{\mathsf{s}},F(x,z)_{\mathsf{s}})\geq F(x,z)_{\mathsf{s}}
.
We have:

•

\llbracket x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{0}\rrbracket\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}\llbracket x\rrbracket
:

\llbracket x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{0}\rrbracket=\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+0+0+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle=\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle>\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle=\llbracket x\rrbracket
.

•

\llbracket x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{s}(y)\rrbracket\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}\llbracket\mathsf{s}(x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}y)\rrbracket
:

\llbracket x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}\mathsf{s}(y)\rrbracket=\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+(y_{\mathsf{s}}+1)+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}+(y_{\mathsf{s}}+1)\rangle>\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle=\llbracket\mathsf{s}(x\mathrel{\mathsf{\oplus}}y)\rrbracket

•

\llbracket\mathsf{rec}(\mathsf{0},y,F)\rrbracket\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}\llbracket y\rrbracket
:

\llbracket\mathsf{rec}(\mathsf{0},y,F)\rrbracket=\mathit{Helper}(\langle 0,0\rangle,F)^{0}(\ \langle 1+0+y_{\mathsf{c}}+0+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ )=\langle 1+y_{\mathsf{c}}+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}\langle y_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle=\llbracket y\rrbracket
.

•

\llbracket\mathsf{rec}(\mathsf{s}(x),y,F)\rrbracket\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}\llbracket F\cdot x\cdot\mathsf{rec}(x,y,F)\rrbracket
:

\llbracket\mathsf{rec}(\mathsf{s}(x),y,F)\rrbracket=\mathit{Helper}(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle,F)^{x_{\mathsf{s}}+1}(\ \langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+(x_{\mathsf{s}}+1)+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ )=\mathit{Helper}(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle,F)(\mathit{Helper}(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle,F)^{x_{\mathsf{s}}}(\ \langle 2+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ ))\sqsupseteq_{{\rm Nature}}F(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle,\mathit{Helper}(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle,F)^{x_{\mathsf{s}}}(\ \langle 2+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ ))
by (*B),
\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}F(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle,\mathit{Helper}(\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle,F)^{x_{\mathsf{s}}}(\ \langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ ))
by monotonicity,
=F(x,\mathit{Helper}(x,F)^{x_{\mathsf{s}}}(\ \langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}+F(\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}},\mathtt{0}_{{\rm Nature}})_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle\ ))=\llbracket F\cdot x\cdot\mathsf{rec}(x,y,F)\rrbracket
.

•

\llbracket x\otimes y\rrbracket\sqsupset_{{\rm Nature}}\llbracket\mathsf{rec}(y,\mathsf{0},\lambda n.\lambda m.x\oplus m)\rrbracket
:

–

\llbracket\lambda n.\lambda m.x\oplus m\rrbracket=n\mapsto m\mapsto\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+n_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}+3,x_{\mathsf{s}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle

–

\mathit{Helper}(y,\llbracket\lambda n.\lambda m.x\oplus m\rrbracket)=m\mapsto\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}+3,x_{\mathsf{s}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle

–

For given

\mathit{Helper}(y,\llbracket\lambda n.\lambda m.x\oplus m\rrbracket)^{i}(m)_{\mathsf{s}}=(\sum_{j=0}^{i}x_{\mathsf{s}})+m_{\mathsf{s}}=x_{\mathsf{s}}*i+m_{\mathsf{s}}

–

\mathit{Helper}(y,\llbracket\lambda n.\lambda m.x\oplus m\rrbracket)^{y_{\mathsf{s}}}=m\mapsto\langle\sum_{i=1}^{y_{\mathsf{s}}}(x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+(x_{\mathsf{s}}*i+m_{\mathsf{s}})+3)+m_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}*x_{\mathsf{s}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle=\langle y_{\mathsf{s}}*(x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}+3)+x_{\mathsf{s}}*\sum_{i=1}^{y_{\mathsf{s}}}(i)+m_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}*x_{\mathsf{s}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle=\langle y_{\mathsf{s}}*(x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}+3)+x_{\mathsf{s}}*(y_{\mathsf{s}}*(y_{\mathsf{s}}+1)/2)+m_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}*x_{\mathsf{s}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle=\langle y_{\mathsf{s}}*(x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}+x_{\mathsf{s}}*(y_{\mathsf{s}}+1)/2+3)+m_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}}*x_{\mathsf{s}}+m_{\mathsf{s}}\rangle

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsf{rand}(x)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&x&&\mathsf{filter}(F,\mathsf{nil})&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\mathsf{nil}\\

\mathsf{bool}(\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{0}))&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\mathsf{true}&&\mathsf{consif}(\mathsf{false},x,xs)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&xs\\
\end{array}

As we did in Section
A.1
, we will use the notation
q
instead of
xs
to
avoid clutter in the proof.
We let
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm Nature}}=\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{list}}=\mathbb{N}^{3}
as before, and additionally
let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathsf{boolean}}=\mathbb{N}
(so only a cost component and no size components). We let:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrclcrclcrcl}\llbracket\mathsf{true}\rrbracket&=&\langle 0\rangle&&\llbracket\mathsf{s}(x)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1\rangle&&\llbracket\mathsf{bool}(x)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+1\rangle\\

A.5
Deriv

Our final example also comes from the termination problem database.
This example seems to be designed to calculate a function’s derivative.
It is worth noting that all symbols other than
\mathsf{der}
are constructors.

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.y)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lambda z.\mathsf{0}&&\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{sin}(x))&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lambda z.\mathsf{cos}(z)\\
\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.x)&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lambda z.\mathsf{1}&&\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{cos}(x))&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lambda z.\mathsf{min}(\mathsf{cos}(z))\\
\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{plus}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x))&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lx@intercol\lambda z.\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z)\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{times}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x))&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lx@intercol\lambda z.\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,G\cdot z),\mathsf{times}(F\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z))\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{ln}(F\cdot x))&\to_{\mathcal{R}}&\lx@intercol\lambda z.\mathsf{div}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,F\cdot z)\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\end{array}

With
\mathsf{der}::[\real\Rightarrow\real]\Rightarrow\real\Rightarrow\real
.
We let
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{N}^{3}
where the first component indicates cost, and the
second and third component roughly indicate the number of plus/times/ln occurrences and
the number of times/ln occurrences respectively.
We will denote
x_{\mathsf{s}}
for
x_{2}
, and
x_{\star}
for
x_{3}
.
We use the following interpretation:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\llbracket\mathsf{0}\rrbracket&=&\langle 0,0,0\rangle&&\hskip-20.0pt\llbracket\mathsf{plus}(x,y)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\star}+y_{\star}\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{1}\rrbracket&=&\langle 0,0,0\rangle&&\hskip-20.0pt\llbracket\mathsf{times}(x,y)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\star}+y_{\star}+1\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{cos}(x)\rrbracket&=&x&&\llbracket\mathsf{ln}(x)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}}+1,x_{\star}+1\rangle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{sin}(x)\rrbracket&=&x&&\llbracket\mathsf{der}(F)\rrbracket&=&z\mapsto\langle\\
\llbracket\mathsf{min}(x)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}},0,0\rangle&&&&\phantom{A}1+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+2*F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+F(z)_{\star}*F(z)_{\mathsf{c}},\\
\llbracket\mathsf{div}(x,y)\rrbracket&=&\langle x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}},0,0\rangle&&&&\phantom{A}F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(F(z)_{\star}+1),\\
&&&&&&\phantom{A}F(z)_{\star}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)\ \rangle\\
\end{array}

It is easy to see that monotonicity requirements are satisfied.
In addition, all the rules are oriented by this interpretation:

•

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.y)\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{0}\rrbracket

–

Note that by choice of
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\real,\real}
, we have
\llbracket\lambda x.y\rrbracket=x\mapsto\langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}},y_{\mathsf{s}},y_{\star}\rangle
.

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.y)\rrbracket=z\mapsto\langle 1+(1+z_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}})+2*y_{\mathsf{s}}+y_{\star}*(1+z_{\mathsf{c}}+y_{\mathsf{c}}),y_{\mathsf{s}}*(y_{\star}+1),y_{\star}*(y_{\star}+1)\rangle\sqsupset z\mapsto\langle 1+z_{\mathsf{c}},0,0\rangle=\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{0}\rrbracket

•

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.y)\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{0}\rrbracket

–

Note that by choice of
\mathit{MakeSM}_{\real,\real}
, we have
\llbracket\lambda x.x\rrbracket=x\mapsto\langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}},x_{\star}\rangle
.

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.x)\rrbracket=z\mapsto\langle 1+(1+z_{\mathsf{c}})+\dots,z_{\mathsf{s}}*(z_{\star}+1),z_{\star}*(z_{\star}+1)\rangle\sqsupset z\mapsto\langle 1+z_{\mathsf{c}},0,0\rangle=\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{1}\rrbracket

•

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{sin}(x))\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{cos}(z)\rrbracket

–

Note that
\llbracket\lambda x.\mathsf{sin}(x)\rrbracket=x\mapsto\langle 1+x_{\mathsf{c}},x_{\mathsf{s}},x_{\star}\rangle

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{sin}(x))\rrbracket=z\mapsto\langle 1+(1+z_{\mathsf{c}})+2*z_{\mathsf{s}}+z_{\star}*(1+z_{\mathsf{c}}),z_{\mathsf{s}}*(z_{\star}+1),z_{\star}*(z_{\star}+1)\rangle\sqsupset z\mapsto\langle 1+z_{\mathsf{c}},z_{\mathsf{s}},z_{\star}\rangle=\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{cos}(z)\rrbracket

•

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{cos}(x))\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{min}(\mathsf{cos}(z))\rrbracket

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{cos}(x))\rrbracket=z\mapsto\langle 1+(1+z_{\mathsf{c}})+\dots,z_{\mathsf{s}}*(z_{\star}+1),z_{\star}*(z_{\star}+1)\rangle\sqsupset z\mapsto\langle 1+z_{\mathsf{c}},0,0\rangle=\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{min}(\mathsf{cos}(z))\rrbracket
.

•

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{plus}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x))\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z)\rrbracket

–

\llbracket\lambda x.\mathsf{plus}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x)\rrbracket=x\mapsto\langle 1+F(x)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(x)_{\mathsf{c}},F(x)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(x)_{\mathsf{s}}+1,F(x)_{\star}+G(x)_{\star}\rangle

z\mapsto\langle 1+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+2*F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+2*G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+2+F(z)_{\star}*F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(z)_{\star}*G(z)_{\mathsf{c}}),F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(G(z)_{\star}+1)+1,F(z)_{\star}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)+G(z)_{\star}*(G(z)_{\star}+1)\rangle=\\
z\mapsto\langle 1+(1+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+2*F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+F(z)_{\star}*F(z)_{\mathsf{c}})+(1+G(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+2*G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\star}*G(z)_{\mathsf{c}}),\llbracket\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z)\rrbracket_{\mathsf{s}},\llbracket\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z)\rrbracket_{\star}\rangle=\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z)\rrbracket

•

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{times}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x))\rrbracket\sqsupset\llbracket\lambda z.\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,G\cdot z),\mathsf{times}(F\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z))\rrbracket

–

\llbracket\lambda x.\mathsf{times}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x))\rrbracket=x\mapsto\langle 1+F(x)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(x)_{\mathsf{c}},F(x)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(x)_{\mathsf{s}}+1,F(x)_{\star}+G(x)_{\star}+1\rangle

–

\llbracket\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,G\cdot z)\rrbracket=\langle 1+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+2*F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+F(z)_{\star}*F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{c}},F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1,F(z)_{\star}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)+G(z)_{\star}+1\rangle

–

\llbracket\mathsf{times}(F\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z))\rrbracket=\langle 1+G(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+2*G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\star}*G(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}},G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(G(z)_{\star}+1)+F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1,G(z)_{\star}*(G(z)_{\star}+1)+F(z)_{\star}+1\rangle

\llbracket\mathsf{der}(\lambda x.\mathsf{times}(F\cdot x,G\cdot x))\rrbracket=z\mapsto\langle 1+\text{cost},\text{size},\text{star}\rangle
, where:

–

\text{cost}=(1+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{c}})+2*(F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1)+(F(z)_{\star}+G(z)_{\star}+1)*(1+F(z)_{\mathsf{c}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{c}})
;

–

\text{size}=(F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1)*(F(z)_{\star}+G(z)_{\star}+2)
;

–

\text{star}=(F(z)_{\star}+G(z)_{\star}+1)*(F(z)_{\star}+G(z)_{\star}+2)
.

We have
\text{size}=F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1+(F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1)*(F(z)_{\star}+G(z)_{\star}+1)\geq F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1+F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(G(z)_{\star}+1)+1*1=(F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(F(z)_{\star}+1)+G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1)+(G(z)_{\mathsf{s}}*(G(z)_{\star}+1)+F(z)_{\mathsf{s}}+1)=\llbracket\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,G\cdot z),\mathsf{times}(F\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z))\rrbracket_{\mathsf{s}}
.

The proof that
\text{star}\geq\llbracket\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{der}(F)\cdot z,G\cdot z),\mathsf{times}(F\cdot z,\mathsf{der}(G)\cdot z))\rrbracket_{\star}
is the same, just with
\cdot_{\mathsf{s}}
replaced by
\cdot_{\star}
.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on all types
\sigma

For a base type

For

•

\sqsupset_{\tau\Rightarrow\rho}

\sqsupseteq_{\tau\Rightarrow\rho}

Suppose, by contradiction, that there is an infinite chain

\sqsupset_{\rho}

For reflexivity, notice that

iff for all
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\tau}

•

Both relations are transitive.

For
\sqsupset_{\tau\Rightarrow\rho}

then for all

For all

Let

2.

The proof follows the same structure as before:
by Definition
31
,
each
\mathcal{J}_{\mathsf{c}}=\langle P_{1},\dots,P_{K[\kappa]}\rangle
is now linearly bounded;
that is, for each
\mathsf{c}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a constant
a_{\mathsf{c}}
such that for all
(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})

we have

P_{l}(x^{1},\dots,x^{m})\leq a_{\mathsf{c}}*(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{K[\iota_{i}]}x^{i}_{j})
.
Let us set, as before,
a
to be the maximum of such
a_{\mathsf{c}}
and define
k=\max(2,\max_{i}K[\iota_{i}])
.
Notice that
k
is determined when we define the interpretation’s domain, so it does not depend on the size of
s
.

We prove by induction on the size of
s
that
\llbracket s\rrbracket_{l}\leq 2^{(a*k)*|s|}
,
for each component
P_{l}
of
\llbracket s\rrbracket
.
In the base case, where
s
is a constant constructor,
we have that

\llbracket\mathsf{c}\rrbracket_{l}\leq a_{\mathsf{c}}\leq a*k<2^{a*k}
follows trivially.
For the inductive step, we have
s=\mathsf{c}(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
.
Then:

\displaystyle P_{l}(\llbracket d_{1}\rrbracket,\dots,\llbracket d_{m}\rrbracket)

\displaystyle\leq a_{\mathsf{c}}*(1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K[\iota_{i}]}\llbracket d_{i}\rrbracket_{j})

\displaystyle\leq a*(2*\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K[\iota_{i}]}\llbracket d_{i}\rrbracket_{j})\ \text{because}\ 1+z\leq 2z\ \text{for}\ z\geq 1

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(IH)}}{{\leq}}2*a*\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K[\iota_{i}]}2^{a*k*|d_{i}|}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2*a*k*\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}2^{a*k*|d_{i}|}

\displaystyle\leq(2*a*k)*\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}2^{a*k*|d_{i}|}\ \text{by claim (\ref{claim:bounding-finite-pols})}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{a*k}*\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\left(2^{a*k*|d_{i}|}\right)\ \text{because}\ 2z\leq 2^{z}\ \text{if}\ z\geq 2

\displaystyle=2^{(a*k)}*2^{(a*k)*\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}|d_{i}|}

\displaystyle=2^{a*k\left(1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}|d_{i}|\right)}

\displaystyle=2^{(a*k)*|s|}.

Hence, we are done choosing
b:=a*k
.
