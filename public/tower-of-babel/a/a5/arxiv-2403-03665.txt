Robust radial basis function interpolation based on geodesic distance for the numerical coupling of multiphysics problems
Michele Bucelli, Francesco Regazzoni, Luca Dede’, Alfio Quarteroni

Abstract
Multiphysics simulations frequently require transferring solution fields between subproblems with non-matching spatial discretizations, typically using interpolation techniques. Standard methods are usually based on measuring the closeness between points by means of the Euclidean distance, which does not account for curvature, cuts, cavities or other non-trivial geometrical or topological features of the domain. This may lead to spurious oscillations in the interpolant in proximity to these features. To overcome this issue, we propose a modification to rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation to account for the geometry of the interpolation domain, by yielding conformity and fidelity to geometrical and topological fieatures. The proposed method, referred to as RL-RBF-G, relies on measuring the geodesic distance between data points. RL-RBF-G removes all oscillations appearing in the rescaled localized radial basis function interpolant, resulting in increased accuracy in domains with complex geometries. We demonstrate the effectiveness of RL-RBF-G interpolation through a convergence study in an idealized setting. Furthermore, we discuss the algorithmic aspects and the implementation of RL-RBF-G interpolation in a distributed-memory parallel framework, and present the results of a strong scalability test yielding nearly ideal results. Finally, we show the effectiveness of RL-RBF-G interpolation in multiphysics simulations by considering an application to a whole-heart cardiac electromecanics model.



Keywords:
intergrid interpolation, radial basis functions, geodesic distance, multiphysics modeling, cardiac electromechanics

Introduction

We consider the problem of transferring data between two different meshes of the same spatial domain. This problem often arises in the context of finite elements multiphysics simulations

[
25

, or to allow the coupling of independent solvers
[
15
]
. In all these cases, it is therefore crucial to introduce suitable intergrid transfer operators to realize the coupling. Operators of this kind are relevant also for remeshing techniques or geometric multigrid methods
[

,

To this purpose, we consider rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation, introduced in
[
23

[

and volume-coupled
[
55
,
14
]

This may prevent from capturing the correct geometry of the domain under consideration. Indeed, in realistic applications, disconnected regions of the domain may be close in the Euclidean norm (see e.g.
Figure

1(a)
). Therefore, an interpolant constructed by relying exclusively on the Euclidean distance will not account for the fact that those regions are disconnected. When considering data representing the solution of a physical problem, the points in regions of the domain that are not connected may feature very different solution values, which can lead to large artificial oscillations in the interpolant. These oscillations mostly appear in proximity to non-trivial geometrical features such as holes or cuts in the domain, as showcased by the examples of this paper.

We propose a modification to rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation that relies on an approximation of the geodesic distance within the domain of interest to take its geometry into account. The geodesic distance is used to threshold the Euclidean distance, so that points that belong to disconnected regions of the domain are regarded as infinitely distant. We discuss the computational aspects of the proposed method by describing in detail the algorithms used to construct and evaluate the interpolant. We focus in particular on a distributed-memory parallel implementation, in which the interpolation and evaluation points are distributed across several processes.

Finally, we present numerical examples that illustrate the issues of rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation in topologically complex domains, and we show that our geodesic distance thresholding method can overcome those issues. We consider both an idealized example and a realistic multiphysics application to three-dimensional whole-heart electromechanics

2
RBF intergrid transfer with geodesic thresholding

We briefly recall the rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation procedure, and present our novel geodesic-based distance thresholding method to interpolate in complex domains accounting for their geometry.

2.1
Intergrid transfer using rescaled, localized RBF interpolation

Let
\displaystyle\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded domain, and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\text{src}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\text{dst}}
be two meshes of
\displaystyle\Omega
. The two meshes are not necessarily conforming or nested, and may feature different resolutions and different element types (tetrahedral or hexahedral). In other words, the two meshes represent two different approximations of the domain
\displaystyle\Omega
, which we will denote by
\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{src}}
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{dst}}
respectively. We remark that, in general, the boundaries of
\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{src}}
and
\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{dst}}
do not necessarily coincide.

Let
\displaystyle f:\Omega^{\text{src}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a known function. Let
\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{\text{src}}}
, with
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i}\in\Omega^{\text{src}}
, be a set of interpolation points, and
\displaystyle f^{\text{src}}_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i})
for
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,N^{\text{src}}
. The radial basis function interpolant of the function
\displaystyle f
at points
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i}
is a function
\displaystyle\Pi_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
in the form

\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\text{src}}}\gamma_{j}\,\phi(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}),r_{j})\;,

(1)

wherein
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are unknown interpolation coefficients,
\displaystyle d:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,+\infty)
measures the distance between two points, and
\displaystyle\phi:\mathbb{R}\times[0,+\infty)\to[0,1]
is the compactly supported
\displaystyle C^{2}
-continuous Wendland basis function radial basis function
[
66
]
with support radius
\displaystyle r_{j}>0
, defined as

\phi(t,r)=\max\left\{1-\frac{t}{r},0\right\}^{4}\left(1+4\frac{t}{r}\right)\quad\text{for }r\geq 0\;.

(2)

The distance is typically measured in the Euclidean norm
[
23
,
64
,
67
,
43
,
28
,
15
]
, that is

d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\;.

(3)

However, as discussed in the following, this may introduce artificial oscillations when the domain features a complex geometry.

The coefficients
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}
in (
1
) are determined by imposing the interpolation constraints
\displaystyle\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i})=f^{\text{src}}_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,N^{\text{src}}
, which is equivalent to solving the following linear system:

\Phi_{\text{int}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{f}^{\text{src}}\;,

(4)

wherein
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{N^{\text{src}}})^{T}
is the vector of interpolation coefficients, the vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\text{src}}=(f^{\text{src}}_{1},\allowbreak f^{\text{src}}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f^{\text{src}}_{N^{\text{src}}})^{T}
collects the evaluations of
\displaystyle f
on the interpolation points, and
\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{int}}
is an
\displaystyle N^{\text{src}}\times N^{\text{src}}
matrix of entries

(\Phi_{\text{int}})_{ij}=\phi(d(\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}),r_{j})\;.

(5)

The evaluation of the interpolant
\displaystyle\Pi_{f}
onto a set of points
\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{\text{dst}}}
, with
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{i}\in\Omega^{\text{dst}}
, can be obtained by computing the matrix-vector product
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\text{dst}}=\Phi_{\text{eval}}\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{f}^{\text{dst}}=(\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{1}),\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{2}),\dots,\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{N^{\text{dst}}}))^{T}
and
\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{eval}}
is an
\displaystyle N^{\text{dst}}\times N^{\text{src}}
matrix of entries

(\Phi_{\text{eval}})_{ij}=\phi(d(\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}),r_{j})\;.

(6)

The radial basis function interpolant may yield oscillations even if the interpolated data is constant
[
23
]
. To avoid this, we adopt the rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation introduced in
[
23
]
. The radial basis function support radius
\displaystyle r_{j}
associated with interpolation node
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}
is chosen as the distance between
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}
and the
\displaystyle M
-th nearest neighbor in the set
\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N^{\text{src}}}
, magnified by a factor
\displaystyle\alpha

[
14
,
64
]
, where
\displaystyle M\in\mathbb{N}
and
\displaystyle\alpha\in[1,\infty)
are user-defined parameters. Furthermore, introducing the constant function
\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x})=1
, we rescale
\displaystyle\Pi_{f}
by the interpolant
\displaystyle\Pi_{g}
of
\displaystyle g
, leading to the following definition of the rescaled interpolant
\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{res}}_{f}
:

\Pi^{\text{res}}_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x})}{\Pi_{g}(\mathbf{x})}\;,

(7)

To keep the notation light, we shall drop the superscript “res” from here on, and denote the rescaled interpolant simply by
\displaystyle\Pi_{f}
.

The definition of the interpolant and its evaluation generalize to vectorial functions by interpolating every component independently. We refer to
[
23
,
14
]
for further details on rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation, and to
[
20
]
for a discussion of its convergence properties.

2.2
Geodesic distance thresholding

The radial basis function interpolant is a linear combination of the interpolation coefficients
\displaystyle\gamma_{j}
, where the weights of the combination are larger the closer the evaluation point
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
is to the interpolation point
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}
. In qualitative terms, this means that the value of
\displaystyle\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x})
is influenced by the value of
\displaystyle f
at the interpolation points that are close to
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
.

This may not be appropriate for complex domains that are characterized by disconnected regions, high curvature or cavities, such as those represented in
Figure

1(a)
. In those cases, the Euclidean distance may not be an adequate measure of the closeness between two points of
\displaystyle\Omega
. This may lead to large artificial oscillations in the interpolant.

To avoid this issue, we need to replace the Euclidean distance (
3
) with a notion of distance that better accounts for the geometry of
\displaystyle\Omega
. As a starting point, we consider the geodesic distance within
\displaystyle\Omega
.

Definition 1

(Geodesic distance)
.

Given two points
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\Omega
, their geodesic distance
\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is the length of the shortest curve contained in
\displaystyle\Omega
and featuring
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
as its endpoints.

Since we consider discrete approximations of the domain
\displaystyle\Omega
, we replace the geodesic distance defined above with a discrete counterpart. We begin by defining the distance between mesh vertices.

Definition 2

(Discrete geodesic distance)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
be a mesh representing an approximation
\displaystyle\widetilde{\Omega}
of
\displaystyle\Omega
. Given two points
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\widetilde{\Omega}
, vertices of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, we define their discrete geodesic distance
\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
as the length of the shortest path from
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
to
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
traveling along edges and element diagonals of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
.

Applications of the interpolation procedure often require the interpolation or evaluation points to be internal to mesh elements (such as Gauss-Legendre quadrature points
[
14
]
). Furthermore, the evaluation of the interpolant requires us to compute the distance between interpolation points in
\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{src}}
and evaluation points in
\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{dst}}
. Therefore, we extend the above definition to arbitrary points in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows.

Definition 3

(Discrete geodesic distance)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
be a reference mesh approximating
\displaystyle\Omega
. Given two points
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we set
\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=g_{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
, where
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
are the vertices of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
closest to
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
, respectively.

Figure

1(b)
displays a schematic representation of the above notions. The computation of the discrete geodesic distance
\displaystyle g_{h}
is computationally efficient and easy to implement
[
24
,
38
]
(see also
Section

3.4
). However,
\displaystyle g_{h}
may be a poor approximation of the exact geodesic distance
\displaystyle g

[
39
,
38
]
. Furthermore, as shown in
Figure

1(c)
, it may happen that
\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0
even if
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}
, which may hinder the well-posedness of the interpolation problem
[
64
]
. Instead of resorting to more accurate yet more complex (and computationally demanding) discretization methods, we use
\displaystyle g_{h}
to apply a threshold to the Euclidean distance, replacing (
3
) with the following.

Definition 4

(Thresholded Euclidean distance)
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
be a reference mesh approximating
\displaystyle\Omega
. Given two points
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};R)=\begin{cases}+\infty&\text{if }g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})>R\;,\\
g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})&\text{if }\beta h_{\text{max}}+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|<g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq R\;,\\
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|&\text{if }g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\beta h_{\text{max}}+\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\;,\\
\end{cases}

(8)

where the discrete geodesic distance
\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is evaluated with
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
as a reference mesh,
\displaystyle h_{\text{max}}
is the maximum diameter of the elements of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
, and
\displaystyle\beta>0
is a constant coefficient.

The coefficient
\displaystyle\beta
has the role of detecting regions of high curvature, where the discrete geodesic distance between points is significantly larger than the Euclidean distance. In those regions it is preferrable to compute
\displaystyle d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
through
\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, instead of the Euclidean distance
\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|
. Indeed, although
\displaystyle g_{h}
is a potentially inaccurate approximation of
\displaystyle g
, this approximation will generally be better than the Euclidean distance. Conversely, in regions of low curvature, the Euclidean distance enjoys better smoothness properties than
\displaystyle g_{h}
, and is therefore a better choice for evaluating
\displaystyle d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

Finally, we replace the definition of the interpolant in (
1
) with

\Pi_{f}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\text{src}}}\gamma_{j}\,\phi(d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j};r_{j}),r_{j})\;,

(9)

where the threshold
\displaystyle R
of (
8
) is chosen equal to the radial basis function support radius. The entries of
\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{int}}
and
\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{eval}}
are updated accordingly. To evaluate the discrete geodesic distance, we use the finest of the two meshes
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\text{src}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\text{dst}}
, to obtain the best approximation. We refer to the resulting method as RL-RBF-G interpolation.

Since we only use

\displaystyle R

2

2

\displaystyle R

The loop of
Algorithm

1
, lines 16–35, requires to evaluate
\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j},\mathbf{y})
multiple times with
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}
as first argument and varying the second argument
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
. To avoid unnecessary computations, the sets
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
and the tentative distances
\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{z}}
are reused between calls. If the point
\displaystyle\mathbf{y}
was already added to the set
\displaystyle B
during a previous evaluation of the distance, we reuse the already computed distance
\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{y}}
.

When running in parallel, the mesh
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
is distributed over multiple processes, and each of the processes stores only a subset of the mesh vertices. However, each process needs to evaluate the discrete geodesic distance between arbitrary mesh vertices, regardless of whether they are owned by that process or not. A naive solution would be to send all the vertices and their adjacency information to every process. This however does not scale efficiently over large meshes or large number of processes.

To avoid this issue, we observe that, when evaluating
\displaystyle g_{h}
, each process
\displaystyle r
always passes a locally owned point
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\text{src}}_{j}\in X^{\text{src}}_{r}
as first argument (see
Algorithm

1

\displaystyle r_{\max}{}
. Therefore, it is sufficient that each process stores the points that are within
\displaystyle r_{\max}{}
of any locally owned point, together with the associated adjacency information.

4.1.1
Convergence test

We assess the convergence of the proposed method by constructing the interpolant of
\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\text{tet}}_{\text{ring},i}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega)

e_{\infty}=\frac{\max_{i=1,\dots,N^{\text{dst}}}\left|\mathbf{f}^{\text{dst}}_{i}-f(\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{i})\right|}{\max_{i=1,\dots,N^{\text{dst}}}\left|f(\mathbf{x}^{\text{dst}}_{i})\right|}\;.

(16)

Figure

3
reports the error obtained for
\displaystyle M=1
and
\displaystyle M=4
with different choices of
\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle h^{\text{src}}_{\max}
, RL-RBF-G interpolation reduces the interpolation error approximately by one order of magnitude with respect to rescaled localized radial basis function. Indeed, the latter is largely affected by the oscillations appearing near the slit.

The coefficient
\displaystyle\alpha
rescales the radial basis function support radius. For large values of
\displaystyle h^{\text{src}}_{\max}
, increasing
\displaystyle\alpha
without geodesic thresholding allows points on one side of the slit to interact more with points on the other, leading to larger errors. Conversely, increasing
\displaystyle\alpha
with geodesic thresholding reduces the error, since a larger radial basis function support radius removes small spurious oscillations in the interpolant, improving its accuracy.

For a sufficiently small
\displaystyle h^{\text{src}}_{\max}
, rescaled localized radial basis function and RL-RBF-G lead to approximately equal interpolation errors. This happens because the interpolation radii
\displaystyle r_{j}
are progressively reduced as the mesh is refined, so that for a sufficiently fine mesh the interpolation radius is small enough that points on one side of the slit do not interact with points on the other side. In that case, geodesic thresholding has no effect in practice, and the two methods yield almost identical results.

Finally, our numerical results indicate that the RL-RBF-G interpolant converges to
\displaystyle f
with approximate order
\displaystyle 1
with respect to
\displaystyle h^{\text{src}}_{\max}
. This is in agreement with the analysis presented in
[
20

for rescaled localized radial basis function interpolation.

\displaystyle\beta=$\displaystyle 0.5$

An intuitive explanation for this behavior is that the condition

\displaystyle g_{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|

\displaystyle g_{h}

5
Conclusions

We proposed an interpolation method based on rescaled localized radial basis function that uses a discrete approximation of the geodesic distance to take into account the geometry of the interpolation domain. The interpolation method, referred to as RL-RBF-G, allows to transfer variables between non-matching meshes, and prevents the onset of spurious oscillations associated with holes, cuts and otherwise complex geometrical features. Our numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed method overcomes limitations of rescaled localized radial basis function, while retaining good convergence properties.

Furthermore, we focused on the distributed-memory parallel implementation of RL-RBF-G interpolation, a non-trivial task due to the two meshes having independent parallel partitionings. The scalability tests carried out in this work indicate that the proposed implementation, available as open source within
life
x

[
1
]
, has nearly ideal scalability properties, and is therefore well suited to large scale applications.

Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of RL-RBF-G interpolation in a realistic application to a whole-heart cardiac electromechanics model. This application is a particularly challenging setting for the proposed method, given the geometrical complexity of the domain. This test shows that the RL-RBF-G method is capable of transferring data between arbitrarily refined and even non-nested meshes, with an acceptable impact on the computational costs, thereby providing an effective tool in developing multiphysics computational models in geometrically complex domains.
