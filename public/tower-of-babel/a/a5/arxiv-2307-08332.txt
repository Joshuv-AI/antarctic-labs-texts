Components of domino tilings under flips in quadriculated tori
Qianqian Liu, Yaxian Zhang, Heping Zhang

Abstract




Abstract
:
In a region
R
consisting of unit squares, a (domino) tiling is a collection of dominoes (the union of two adjacent squares) which pave fully the region. The flip graph of
R
is defined on the set of all tilings of
R
where two tilings are adjacent if we change one from the other by a flip (a
90^{\circ}
rotation of a pair of side-by-side dominoes).
If
R
is simply-connected, then its flip graph is connected.
By using homology and cohomology, Saldanha, Tomei, Casarin and Romualdo obtained a criterion to decide if two tilings are in
the same component of flip graph of quadriculated surface. By a graph-theoretic method, we obtain that the flip graph of a non-bipartite quadriculated torus consists of two isomorphic components. As an application, we obtain that the forcing numbers of all perfect matchings of each non-bipartite quadriculated torus form an integer-interval. For a bipartite quadriculated torus, the components of the flip graph is more complicated, and we use homology to obtain a general lower bound for the number of components of its flip graph.

Keywords
: Domino tiling; Flip; Perfect matching; Resonance graph; Quadriculated torus; Homology group.

1
Introduction

In a quadriculated region
R
consisting of unit squares with vertices in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, a
domino
(or
dimer
) is the union of two adjacent squares, and a (
domino
)
tiling
is a covering of
R
by dominoes with disjoint interiors. In another language,
R
can be transformed into a graph
D(R)
called
inner dual
of
R
: vertices of
D(R)
are unit squares in
R
and two vertices are adjacent if their corresponding squares have an edge in common. Then a domino of
R
corresponds to an edge of
D(R)
and a tiling corresponds to a perfect matching of
D(R)
.

A classical model is to determine the number of ways of an
n\times m
chessboard (
nm
even) can be covered by dimers, known as the
dimer problem

[
9
]
, which
has important applications in both statistical mechanics and physical system. In other words, the dimer problem is to compute the number of perfect matchings in its inner dual. For rectangle lattices, the dimer problem was solved independently by Fisher
[
4
]
, Kasteleyn
[
9
]
, Temperley and Fisher
[
19
]
. Further, Cohn et al.
[
3
]
studied domino tilings of simply-connected regions of arbitrary shape on the plane by a variational method. Especially,
Thurston
[
21
]
produced a necessary and sufficient condition for a simply-connected region in the plane to be tileable by dominoes.
There are also some results of dimer problem for 3-dimensional lattices
[
16
,
17
,
14
,
12
]
.

Given a tiling of a region
R
, a
flip
is a local move: lifting two parallel dominoes and then placing them back in a unique different position. Define the
flip graph

\mathcal{T}(R)
on the set of all tilings of
R
where two tilings are adjacent if we convert one from another by a flip. If
R
is simply-connected, then
\mathcal{T}(R)
is connected
[
21
,
18
]
. However, the result is not necessarily hold for other regions. Saldanha, Tomei, Casarin and Romualdo
[
18
]
gave three characterizations (combinational, homology and height section versions) for two tilings of a plane region in the same component of the flip graph. By homology and cohomology with local coefficients, they
[
18
]
provided a necessary and sufficient condition for two tilings in the same component of flip graphs of quadriculated surfaces.
By an elementary graph theory, we can obtain clearly the two components of flip graphs of non-bipartite quadriculated tori.

Theorem 1.1
.

The flip graph of a non-bipartite quadriculated torus consists of two isomorphic components.

Let
G
be a graph with the vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
. A
perfect matching

M
of
G
is a subset of
E(G)
such that each vertex is adjacent to exactly one edge in
M
. The
forcing number
of
M
is the smallest cardinality of a subset of
M
that is contained in no other perfect matching of
G
. This concept was first introduced by Klein and Randić
[
10
]
in chemical literatures, which has important applications in resonance theory. The
forcing spectrum
of
G
is the set of forcing numbers over all perfect matchings of
G
. As an application of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

The forcing spectrum of a non-bipartite quadriculated torus is continuous (forming an integer-interval).

For a bipartite quadriculated torus, we associate it to a CW-complex
\boldsymbol{T}
. By using the flux of a tiling i.e. homology class
[t-t_{\oplus}]
in 1-dimensional homology group
H_{1}(\boldsymbol{T},Z)
, where
t_{\oplus}
is a fixed base tiling and
t
is any tiling of the quadriculated torus, we obtain that the flip graph of
T(n,m,r)
(defined in Section 2) has at least
2\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor-1
non-trivial components. In particular, the flip graph of usual
2n\times 2m
quadriculated torus contains at least
2n+2m-3
non-trivial components.

We organize the rest of paper as follows. In Section 2 we present some basic properties of quadriculated tori. In Section 3 we give the concept of resonance graphs, and prove Theorem
1.1
using graph theory. In Section 4, we discuss the number of components of flip graphs for bipartite quadriculated tori. In Section 5, we prove Theorem
1.2
and give two examples whose forcing spectra are not continuous for bipartite quadriculated tori.

A graph

Noting that

Theorem 2.4
.

T(n,m,r)
is bipartite if and only if both

are even.

Proof.

If both

and

are even, then the quadriculated cylinder obtained by identifying the left and right sides of the

chessboard is bipartite,
whose vertices can be colored with white and black such that two end vertices of all edges have different colors. Since

and

have the same parity, white (resp. black) vertices are glued with white (resp. black) vertices when identifying the top and bottom sides with a torsion of
r

have different colors and

is bipartite.

Conversely, we will prove it by contradiction. If
m
is odd, then the cycle induced by all vertices lying in a row contains
m

If

is odd, then
v_{0,0}v_{1,0}\cdots v_{n-1,0}v_{n-1,m-1}\cdots\\
v_{n-1,m-r}v_{0,0}
is a cycle of length

contains
an odd cycle (a cycle containing odd number of vertices), which contradicts that

is bipartite.
∎

Different from the

Proof.

(
mod

(

Since

(r^{\prime},m^{\prime})=(n(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k),\frac{mn}{(r,m)})=n(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k,\frac{m}{(r,m)})=n(\frac{m}{(r,m)},-k),

Noting that

n^{\prime}(\frac{m^{\prime}}{(r^{\prime},m^{\prime})}-k^{\prime})=(r,m)(\frac{\frac{mn}{(r,m)}}{n}-k^{\prime})=(r,m)(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k^{\prime})=m-(r,m)k^{\prime},

we need to prove that
k^{\prime}=\frac{m-r}{(r,m)}
. By definition, we have
r^{\prime}k^{\prime}\equiv(r^{\prime},m^{\prime})
(mod
m^{\prime}
). That is,
k^{\prime}
is a solution of

(mod
\frac{mn}{(r,m)}
). Dividing by

, we obtain that
(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k)x\equiv 1
(mod
\frac{m}{(r,m)}
). By the above proof, we have
(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k,\frac{m}{(r,m)})=1
. Thus
(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k)x\equiv 1
(mod
\frac{m}{(r,m)}
) has a unique solution (see Theorem 57 in
[
8
]
). Finally, we will check that
k^{\prime}=\frac{m-r}{(r,m)}
is a solution of above equation. Recall that
\frac{rk}{(r,m)}\equiv 1~(\text{mod }\frac{m}{(r,m)}),
we have

(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k)\frac{m-r}{(r,m)}\equiv(-k)\frac{m-r}{(r,m)}\equiv\frac{rk}{(r,m)}\equiv 1~(\text{mod }\frac{m}{(r,m)}).

Thus
k^{\prime}=\frac{m-r}{(r,m)}
is the unique solution of
(\frac{m}{(r,m)}-k)x\equiv 1
(mod
\frac{m}{(r,m)}
), and we finish the proof.
∎

3
Non-bipartite quadriculated tori

In this section, we give the concept of resonance graphs and prove Theorem
1.1
by using the theory of resonance graphs.

3.1
Resonance graphs

For a perfect matching
M
of a graph
G
, a cycle
C
of
G
is
M
-
alternating
if its edges appear alternately in
M
and
E(C)\setminus M
. For an
M
-alternating cycle
C
of
G
, the
symmetric difference

M\oplus E(C):=(M-E(C))\cup(E(C)-M)
is another perfect matching of
G
and such an operation is called a
rotation
of
M
(along
C
).

Let
G
be a graph 2-cell embedded on a closed surface, i.e. each face is homeomorphic to an open disc in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For convenience, sometimes we do not distinguish a face with its boundary, and a cycle with its edge set.
The
resonance graph
of
G
, denoted by
R_{t}(G)
, is defined on the set of all perfect matchings of
G
where two perfect matchings
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are adjacent if and only if
M_{1}\oplus M_{2}
forms the boundary of a face, that is,
M_{2}
is obtained from a rotation of
M_{1}
along the boundary of a face, simply a flip of
M_{1}
.
The resonance graph was introduced independently many times under different names by Grümdler
[
7
]
, Zhang et al.
[
24
]
, Randić
[
15
]
and Fournier
[
5
]
. The study of resonance graphs mainly concentrates on some special plane graphs such as hexagonal systems
[
7
,
24
,
15
]
, polyominoes
[
22
,
26
]
and plane bipartite graphs
[
5
,
25
]
. Readers may refer to a survey
[
23
]
.

Note that the flip graph of a quadriculated region corresponds to the resonance graph of its inner dual. Since
T(n,m,r)
is a 4-regular quadriculated graph on torus, so is its (inner) dual. By Theorem
2.2
,
D(T(n,m,r))
is also a quadriculated torus. Analyzing carefully, we find that inner dual of
T(n,m,r)
is itself. Hence, we have the following observation.

Observation 3.1
.

The flip graph of
T(n,m,r)
is the same as its resonance graph.

Since an

is an even number. Label the vertices of

Without loss of generality, assume that all edges of

By Cases 1 and 2, there exists two consecutive horizontal edges of

on the same side. By Lemma

In the last section, we have proved that flip graphs of non-bipartite quadriculated tori consist of two isomorphic components. Whereas, flip graphs of bipartite quadriculated tori have more complicated cases.

A

[

See the first 8 tilings in Figure

In fact, Saldanha, Tomei, Casarin and Romualdo

[

Furthermore, Saldanha et al.

Freire et al.

5
An application to forcing spectra of quadriculated tori

We now prove Theorem
1.2
by using a lemma due to Afshani et al.
[
1
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

[
1
]
If
M
is a perfect matching of a graph
G
and
C
is an
M
-alternating cycle of length 4, then the symmetric difference on
M
along
C
does not change the forcing number by more than 1.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By Theorem
2.4
, it suffices to prove that Theorem
1.2
holds separately for quadriculated torus
T(2n+1,2m,2r)
,

T(2n,2m,2r-1)
and
T(2m,2n+1,r)
.

By Theorem
3.7
and Remark
3.9
, simple quadriculated torus
R_{t}(T(2n+1,2m,2r))
has exactly two components
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
where
n\geq 0
,
m\geq 2
and
1\leq r\leq m
. By the definition of resonance graph, any two perfect matchings of
H_{i}
can
be obtained from each other by some flips along some faces of
T(2n+1,2m,2r)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. By Lemma
5.1
, the forcing numbers of all perfect matchings in
H_{i}

form an integer-interval.

Two perfect matchings
M^{1}
and
M^{2}
are called
equivalent
if there is an automorphism of the graph which maps
M^{1}
to
M^{2}
. By Lemma
2.3
,

\varphi
is an automorphism of
T(2n+1,2m,2r)
. Since
\varphi(M_{1})=M_{2}
,
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are equivalent. By the proof of Theorem
3.7
,
\varphi
induces an
isomorphism from
H_{1}
to
H_{2}
. Thus, for any perfect matching
M
of
H_{1}
, there exists a perfect matching
\varphi(M)
of
H_{2}
such that
M
and
\varphi(M)
are equivalent.
Since two equivalent perfect matchings have the same forcing number, the sets of forcing numbers of all perfect matchings in
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are the same. Thus forcing numbers
of all perfect matchings of
T(2n+1,2m,2r)
form an integer-interval. That is, the forcing spectrum of
T(2n+1,2m,2r)
is continuous.

By Lemma
3.8
,
T(2m,2n+1,r)
and
T(2n,2m,2r-1)
can be reduced into some quadriculated torus
T(2n^{\prime}+1,2m^{\prime},2r^{\prime})
where
n\geq 0
,
m\geq 2
and
1\leq r\leq m
. By above arguments, we obtain that Theorem
1.2
holds for all non-bipartite quadriculated tori.
\square

For bipartite quadriculated tori, their forcing spectra are not necessarily continuous.
