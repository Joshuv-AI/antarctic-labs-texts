Preserving Besov (fractional Sobolev) energies under sphericalization and flattening
Anders Björn

Abstract




Abstract
.
We introduce a new sphericalization mapping for metric spaces that is applicable
in very general situations, including totally disconnected fractal type sets.
For an unbounded complete metric space
which is uniformly perfect at a base point
for large radii and
equipped
with a doubling measure, we make a more specific construction based on the measure and
equip it with a weighted measure.
This mapping is then shown to preserve the doubling property of the measure and
the Besov (fractional Sobolev) energy.
The corresponding results for flattening of bounded complete metric spaces are also
obtained. Finally, it is shown that for the composition of
a sphericalization with a flattening, or
vice versa, the obtained space is biLipschitz equivalent with the original space
and the resulting measure is comparable to the original measure.

Key words and phrases
:
Besov energy,
doubling measure,
flattening,
fractional Sobolev energy,
metric space,
sphericalization.

(2020):

Introduction

The stereographic projection and its inverse between the Riemann sphere
and the complex plane are very useful mappings in many situations.
Similar mappings on metric spaces have been studied and used during the last 25 years.
Since the inverse of the stereographic projection “sphericalizes” the complex plane, such mappings
from an unbounded to a bounded metric space are called
sphericalizations
.
In the other direction, from a bounded to an unbounded space, they are
called
flattenings
.

These mappings (with suitably chosen parameters) can preserve the Dirichlet energy and the notion
of
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions, and thus make it possible to transform boundary value problems
in unbounded domains to problems in bounded domains, see e.g. Björn–Björn–Li
[
5
]
and
Gibara–Korte–Shanmugalingam
[
12
]
.
Natural spaces for
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions are the Sobolev spaces,
whose traces on the boundary of sufficiently nice domains
\Omega

are the Besov (fractional Sobolev) spaces
B^{\theta}_{p}(\partial\Omega)
,
see e.g.
[
6
]
,
[
17
]
and
[
22
]
.
Besov spaces are also important for
nonlocal (fractional) partial differential equations and in other situations.

In this paper we therefore study preservation of the Besov
(fractional Sobolev)
energy

[u]_{\theta,p}^{p}=[u]_{\theta,p,Z,d,\nu}^{p}:=\int_{Z}\int_{Z}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{d(x,y)^{\theta p}}\frac{d\nu(y)\,d\nu(x)}{\nu(B(x,d(x,y)))}

(1.1)

under suitably defined sphericalizations and flattenings in rather general
complete metric measure spaces
(Z,d,\nu)
.
In particular, our general results apply to
closed uniformly perfect subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, including many fractals.

Roughly speaking, by a
sphericalization
we mean a
topology-preserving change of metric which makes a complete
unbounded metric space into a bounded space whose completion adds
exactly one point (denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
)
which is the accumulation point of every unbounded set in the original space.
Similarly, a
flattening
is a topology-preserving change
of metric
which makes a punctured complete bounded metric space
into an unbounded complete metric space such that the puncture
at the base point is sent to infinity.
See Section
2.1
for precise definitions.

The following are some of our main results.
In order to treat sphericalization and flattening simultaneously
we introduce the parameter

m_{0}=0
for flattening and
m_{0}=1
for sphericalizing.
The resulting space is denoted
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d})
.
To be precise, a point
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
is added to the sphericalized space
to make it complete,
while a base point
b
is removed from the original space during flattening.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
(Z,d)

is a complete metric space that is
uniformly perfect at a base point
b\in Z

for radii
r\geq m_{0}
and that the measure
\nu
is doubling.
Let the deformed space
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d},{\hat{\nu}})
be defined
using (
1.3
)–(
1.5
) below.

1.

In both cases, sphericalization and flattening, the transformed
measure
{\hat{\nu}}
is doubling.

2.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
\theta>0
.
If the parameter
\sigma

in (
1.4
)–(
1.5
)
satisfies
\sigma=p\theta
,
then for every measurable function
u
on
Z
,
the Besov
(
fractional Sobolev
)
energies (
1.1
)
with respect to
(Z,d,\nu)
and
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d},{\hat{\nu}})

are comparable,
i.e.

C_{1}[u]_{\theta,p,Z,d,\nu}^{p}\leq[u]_{\theta,p,\widehat{Z},\hat{d},{\hat{\nu}}}^{p}\leq C_{2}[u]_{\theta,p,Z,d,\nu}^{p}

where the comparison constants
0<C_{1}\leq C_{2}

depend only on
\sigma=p\theta
, the doubling constant
C_{\nu}
and
the uniform perfectness constant
\kappa
.

Also inversions are covered by our results, see Remark
4.1
.
Roughly speaking, an inversion is a topology-preserving change of metric
between two punctured complete unbounded metric spaces
such that the puncture at the base point
is sent to infinity while infinity is sent to a new puncture (denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
).
See Section
2.1
for the precise definition.

In Section
7
we end the paper by showing that
sphericalization and flattening are each other’s inverses, up to
a biLipschitz equivalence of the metric and the measure.

As far as we know, Bonk–Kleiner
[
7
]
were the first
to study sphericalization of general metric spaces.
They used the sphericalized distance

\hat{d}(x,y):=\inf\biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho(x_{j})\rho(x_{j-1})d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\biggr\},

(1.2)

where the infimum is taken over all chains
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
, and with the specific choice

\rho(x)=\frac{1}{1+d(x,b)},

where
b
is a fixed base point.

Some years later, Buckley–Herron–Xie
[
8
]

used (
1.2
) with
\rho(x)=1/d(x,b)
,
which led them to
inversions and flattenings (both of which were called inversions
in
[
8
]
).
Balogh–Buckley
[
1
]

used general sphericalizing/flattening functions
and defined
the new metric by integrating and taking the infimum
over rectifiable paths between two points.
This way they showed that the quasihyperbolic metrics corresponding
to the
spherical and the flat
metrics are biLipschitz equivalent.
Such an approach requires pathconnected spaces.

We have taken a different route, using the sum
\rho(x_{j})+\rho(x_{j-1})

in (
1.3
) below
rather than the product
\rho(x_{j})\rho(x_{j-1})
in (
1.2
),
since the product definition does not work with all the parameters we need,
see Remark
3.10
.
Starting with a general nonincreasing
metric density
function

\rho:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty]

and a complete metric space
Z=(Z,d)

we define a deformed metric
\hat{d}
, by letting

\hat{d}(x,y):=\inf\biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho(x_{j})+\rho(x_{j-1}))d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\biggr\},

(1.3)

where the infimum is taken over all chains
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
.
Unlike
[
1
]
, which also considers general sphericalizing functions,
we do not use any curves and therefore allow disconnected spaces.
Even in connected spaces, our transformations differ from
[
1
]
.
For example, the completion of the sphericalized
\mathbf{R}
in
[
1
]

adds two points (corresponding to
\pm\infty
)
and is therefore not a sphericalization in our sense,
while in our construction it only adds one point
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
,
by the following result for general metric density functions
\rho
.

Proposition 1.2
.

1.

(Sphericalization)

Let
(Z,d)
be complete, unbounded and uniformly perfect at large scales at
b

and
\rho(0)<\infty
.
Then
(Z,\hat{d})
is bounded and its
completion adds exactly one point, called
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
, if and only if

\int_{1}^{\infty}\rho\,dt<\infty.

2.

(Flattening)

Let
(Z,d)
be complete, bounded and uniformly perfect at
b
.
Then

(Z\setminus\{b\},\hat{d})
is unbounded and complete
if and only if

\int_{0}^{1}\rho\,dt=\infty.

For most of our results, we assume that
Z

is uniformly perfect at the base point
b\in Z
.
This assumption is needed to guarantee that the distances in the deformed space
are not larger than expected.
Propositions
3.8
and
3.9
show that uniform
perfectness is also essentially preserved by our
general transformations.

In order to show the preservation of measure-theoretic properties,
as in Theorem
1.1
,
we consider particular choices of the metric density function
\rho

in (
1.3
), namely

\rho(t)=\frac{1}{(t+m_{0})\nu(B(b,t+m_{0}))^{1/\sigma}},

(1.4)

with
m_{0}=0
for flattening and
m_{0}=1
for sphericalizing.
In both cases,
\sigma>0
is a fixed but arbitrary
parameter and the deformed space
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d})

is equipped with the measure
{\hat{\nu}}
defined by

d{\hat{\nu}}=\rho^{\sigma}\,d\nu.

(1.5)

The papers
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
8
]

studied purely metric notions, and did not consider any measure.
For the metric transformation (
1.2
) considered in
[
7
]
,
Wildrick
[
27
, Proposition 6.13]
showed that if
(Z,d)
is connected and
Ahlfors
Q
-regular (with respect to the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure),
then its sphericalization
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d})

is also Ahlfors
Q
-regular.
Li–Shanmugalingam
[
21
]
considered more general measures
in connection with sphericalization and flattening of metric spaces and showed that (under suitable conditions) these transformations preserve the doubling property, Ahlfors regularity and Poincaré inequalities.
Further results in this direction were obtained by Durand-Cartagena–Li
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
.

Using a weighted version of the measure
from
[
21
]
, Björn–Björn–Li
[
5
]
showed that (again under suitable assumptions) sphericalization preserves
p\mspace{1mu}
-harmonic functions.
This was then applied to study the Dirichlet problem and boundary regularity for such functions on unbounded open sets.
All the above papers
(
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
21
]
and
[
27
]
)
used the sphericalization and flattening functions from
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
.

Gibara–Shanmugalingam
[
13
]
considered a sphericalization similar
to the one in
[
1
]
, but with
the sphericalizing function depending on the distance to the boundary
of a uniform domain, instead of
a single base point. They showed that uniformity is preserved in this type of sphericalization.
This same idea was further developed by
Gibara–Korte–Shanmugalingam
[
12
]
, where they also defined a new measure on the deformed space.
They showed that the transformed measure is doubling and the transformed domain supports a Poincaré inequality, and used this to solve the
p\mspace{1mu}
-Dirichlet problem for
Besov boundary data.

Most of these papers rely on pathconnectedness of the original space
(or even stronger assumptions
such as Poincaré inequalities).
Due to the nonlocal nature of the Besov
space this is not required here.
We instead assume the much weaker assumption of uniform perfectness at
b

(for radii
r\geq m_{0}
).
In fact, many
fractal type sets are natural objects for our study.
The smoothness exponent
\theta
in the Besov
energy (
1.1
) on
such sets can even be greater than 1.
In doubling metric measure spaces supporting a certain Sobolev–Poincaré inequality,
such as
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, this would force functions in
B^{\theta}_{p}

(defined by (
2.7
))
to be constant (see Theorem 1.6 and Corollary 1.8 in Kumagai–Shanmugalingam–Shimizu
[
20
]
).

At the same time, there are plenty of spaces (even rectifiably connected ones)
for which the Besov spaces
B^{\theta}_{p}
are nontrivial for some
\theta>1
.
More precisely, letting

\displaystyle\theta_{p}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\theta:B^{\theta}_{p}\text{ contains nonconstant functions}\}

and

\displaystyle s_{p}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\theta:B^{\theta}_{p,\infty}\text{ contains nonconstant functions}\},

where
B^{\theta}_{p,\infty}
is the Besov–Lipschitz (Korevaar–Schoen) space,
it follows from (
2.8
) below that

\theta_{p}=s_{p}
.
For the Sierpiński carpet,
Theorem 1.4 in Murugan–Shimizu
[
23
]
shows that

s_{p}=\frac{d_{w,p}}{p},\quad\text{where $d_{w,p}$ is the {$p\mspace{1mu}$}-walk dimension.}

At the same time,
d_{w,p}>p
by
Theorem 2.27 in Shimizu
[
26
]

(or the more general
Theorem 9.8 in Kajino–Shimizu
[
18
]
), and hence
\theta_{p}>1

for the Sierpiński carpet.
Similar observations for the Sierpiński gasket follow
from Kajino–Shimizu
[
19
, Theorem 5.26]

and
[
18
, Theorem 9.13]
.

Other examples of spaces carrying nontrivial Besov spaces with
\theta>1
can be
obtained by a general snowflaking process, such as the von Koch snowflake.
(Note that on e.g.
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
,
Besov spaces with
\theta>1
are usually defined in a different way.
Here we ignore such Besov spaces, and only consider
Besov spaces defined as in (
2.7
).)
See Kumagai–Shanmugalingam–Shimizu
[
20
]
for recent
results about potential theoretic implications
of finite dimensionality of Besov spaces when
\theta>1
.

The walk dimension mentioned above
is an important concept in the differential calculus on fractal sets.
For
p=2
, it describes the scaling
\text{time}\simeq\text{space}^{\beta}
for random walks or diffusion processes,
based on Dirichlet forms.
In
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
the walk dimension
\beta=2
, but on fractal sets typically
\beta>2
.

The outline of the paper is as follows.
In Section
2
we introduce some basic notions essential in this paper.
In particular uniform perfectness, doubling measures and the Besov (semi)norm
are defined here.
In Section
3
we prove results about the metric
\hat{d}
defined
with a general metric density function
\rho
in (
1.3
).
In particular we prove Proposition
1.2
.
Here the measure does not play any role.

In Section
4
we turn to the more specific choices of the metric density
function
\rho
given by (
1.4
)
for the sphericalization and flattening.
Using some additional notation, we are able to treat both cases simultaneously in
most of our estimates.
These are mainly carried out in Sections
4
(where uniform perfectness is not assumed) and
5
(where uniform perfectness is made a standing assumption)
leading to the proof of Theorem
1.1
1

(= Theorem
5.9
).
In Section
6
we deduce
Theorem
1.1
2
(= Theorem
6.1
).
We
end the paper, in Section
7
, by showing that
if we flatten the sphericalized space, or sphericalize the flattened space,
then the resulting space is biLipschitz equivalent to the original space and the resulting
measure is comparable to the original measure.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper we let
Z=(Z,d)
be a complete metric space
with
\diam Z>0
.
We also fix a base point
b\in Z
.
Further standing assumptions are added at the beginning of
various sections.

We will use the following notation:

|x|=d(x,b)\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in Z}|x|,

(2.1)

and for balls,

B(x,r)=\{y\in Z:d(x,y)<r\}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{r}=B(b,r).

All balls considered in this paper are open.
It will be convenient to use this notation also with
r=0
, i.e.
B_{0}=B(b,0)=\varnothing
.

Throughout the paper, we write
a\lesssim b
and
b\gtrsim a

if there is an implicit comparison constant
C>0
such that
a\leq Cb
,
and
a\simeq b
if
a\lesssim b\lesssim a
.
The implicit comparison constants are allowed to depend on the
fixed data.
We will carefully explain the dependence in each case.

2.1
Sphericalizations, flattenings and inversions

Our main aim in this paper is to study certain
sphericalizations and flattenings given by a gauge function
as in (
3.1
),
but we start by defining what we mean by sphericalizations and flattenings in general.

By a
sphericalization
we mean a
topology-preserving change of metric from an unbounded complete space
(Z,d)
to
a bounded space
(Z,d^{\prime})
whose completion adds
exactly one point
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
,
so that for any sequence
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
Z
,

\lim_{j\to\infty}d^{\prime}(x_{j},{\boldsymbol{\infty}})=0\quad\textrm{if and only if}\quad\lim_{j\to\infty}d(x_{j},b)=\infty.

(2.2)

The metric
d^{\prime}
extends directly to
Z\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\}
.
With a slight abuse of terminology,
we call also the completion
(Z\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\},d^{\prime})

a
sphericalization
of
(Z,d)
.

Next, let us continue to assume that
(Z,d)
is a complete metric space and let

Z_{0}=Z\setminus\{b\}
.
A
flattening
is a topology-preserving change
of metric from a bounded punctured space
(Z_{0},d)

to an unbounded complete space
(Z_{0},d^{\prime})

(called a
flattening
of
(Z,d)
)
such that for any
b_{0}\in Z_{0}
and any sequence
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
in
Z_{0}
,

\lim_{j\to\infty}d^{\prime}(x_{j},b_{0})=\infty\quad\textrm{if and only if}\quad\lim_{j\to\infty}d(x_{j},b)=0.

(2.3)

Similarly, an
inversion
is a topology-preserving change
of metric from an unbounded punctured
space
(Z_{0},d)
to an unbounded space
(Z_{0},d^{\prime})

whose completion adds exactly one point
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}

such that
(
2.2
) and (
2.3
) hold
for all
sequences in
Z_{0}
.

Note that
(Z,d)
is assumed to be complete but not necessarily
proper (i.e. its closed bounded subsets need not be compact),
in which case
(Z\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\},d^{\prime})
will not be compact.
However, from Section
4
onwards we assume that
(Z,d)
is
equipped with a doubling measure
\nu
, which together with the completeness
implies that

(Z,d)
is proper, by e.g. Proposition 3.1 in Björn–Björn
[
2
]
.
For the results in Section
3
it is not even required
that
(Z,d)
is separable.

2.2
Uniform perfectness at a point

The space
Z
is
uniformly perfect at
x

if there is a constant
\kappa>1
such that

B(x,\kappa r)\setminus B(x,r)\neq\varnothing\quad\text{whenever }B(x,r)\neq Z\text{ and }r>0.

(2.4)

Note that the condition
B(x,r)\neq Z
in
(
2.4
)
can equivalently be replaced by
B(x,\kappa r)\neq Z
.
The space
Z
is
uniformly perfect
if it is uniformly
perfect at every
x
with the same constant
\kappa
.
See Heinonen
[
16
, Chapter 11]

and Björn–Björn
[
4
, Section 2]

for further discussion and history.
Note that
Z
is uniformly perfect with any
\kappa>1
if it is connected.
We will also need the following notions,
which may not have been considered before.

Let

m_{0}\geq 0
.
Then
Z
is
uniformly perfect at
x

for radii
r\geq m_{0}
if there is a constant
\kappa>1
such that

B(x,\kappa r)\setminus B(x,r)\neq\varnothing\quad\text{whenever }B(x,r)\neq Z\text{ and }0\neq r\geq m_{0}.

Note that
Z
is uniformly perfect at
x
if and only if it is
uniformly perfect at
x
for radii
r\geq 0
.

We also say that
Z
is
uniformly perfect at large scales at
x
, if it is unbounded and uniformly perfect at
x
for radii
r\geq 1
.
It is easy to see that the constant
1
in
r\geq 1
can equivalently be replaced by any other positive number, provided that
\kappa
is allowed to change.

2.3
Doubling measures

In this section, we will
provide some preliminary estimates for a doubling
measure under the assumption that the space is uniformly perfect
at a fixed base point
b\in Z
.

A complete Borel regular measure
\nu
is
doubling

if there is a
doubling constant

C_{\nu}>1
such that

0<\nu(B(x,2r))\leq C_{\nu}\nu(B(x,r))<\infty\quad\text{for all $x\in Z$ and $r>0$}.

The following simple lemma from Björn–Björn
[
2
, Lemma 3.6]

will be used several times.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume that
\nu
is a doubling measure on
Z
.
Let
B=B(x,r)
and
B^{\prime}=B(x^{\prime},r^{\prime})
be two balls such that

d(x,x^{\prime})\leq ar\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{r}{a}\leq r^{\prime}\leq ar.

Then
\nu(B)\simeq\nu(B^{\prime})
with comparison constants
depending only on
a\geq 1
and
C_{\nu}
.

The following consequence of the doubling property and uniform perfectness is a (strong) type of reverse-doubling.
Recall from (
2.1
)
that
R_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in Z}|x|
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
\nu
is a doubling measure on
Z

and that
Z
is
uniformly perfect at
b
for radii
r\geq m_{0}

with constant
\kappa
.
Then there are constants
\alpha>0
and
\Lambda\geq 1
,
only depending on
C_{\nu}
and
\kappa
, such that

\frac{\nu(B_{r})}{\nu(B_{R})}\leq\Lambda\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\quad\text{when }m_{0}\leq r<R<2R_{\infty}.

In particular
\nu(\{b\})=0
if
m_{0}=0
.

If
R_{\infty}\leq 2m_{0}
, then

\frac{\nu(B_{r})}{\nu(B_{R})}\leq 1\leq\frac{4r}{R},

so the claim holds with
\alpha=1
and
\Lambda=4
.

Now assume that
R_{\infty}>2m_{0}
.
The claim is trivial if
r=0
since
B_{0}=\varnothing
, so we may assume that

r>0
.
Let
t>0
be such that

m_{0}<t<\tfrac{1}{2}R_{\infty}
.
By the uniform perfectness, there is
x\in B_{2\kappa t}\setminus B_{2t}
.
Then

\nu(B_{t})\leq\nu(B(x,3\kappa t))\lesssim\nu(B(x,t)).

Thus

\nu(B_{4\kappa t})\geq\nu(B_{t})+\nu(B(x,t))\geq\Lambda\nu(B_{t}),

(2.5)

where
\Lambda>1
.

For
j=0,1,\dots
, let
r_{j}=(4\kappa)^{-j}R
and find
n
such that

r_{n+1}\leq r<r_{n}
.
Note that
r_{n}>m_{0}
and
r_{1}<\tfrac{1}{2}R_{\infty}
.
Then, by (
2.5
),

\frac{\nu(B_{r})}{\nu(B_{R})}\leq\frac{\nu(B_{r_{n}})}{\nu(B_{R})}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\nu(B_{r_{j}})}{\nu(B_{r_{j-1}})}\leq\Lambda^{-n}=\Lambda\Bigl(\frac{r_{n+1}}{R}\Bigr)^{\alpha}\leq\Lambda\Bigl(\frac{r}{R}\Bigr)^{\alpha},

where

\alpha=\frac{\log\Lambda}{\log 4\kappa}.\qed

2.4
The Besov (fractional Sobolev) energy

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and
\theta>0
.
For a measurable function
u:Z\to[-\infty,\infty]
(which is
finite
\nu
-a.e.) we define the

Besov
(
fractional Sobolev
)

energy
(
seminorm
)

by

[u]_{\theta,p}^{p}=[u]_{\theta,p,Z}^{p}=\int_{Z}\int_{Z}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{d(x,y)^{\theta p}}\frac{d\nu(y)\,d\nu(x)}{\nu(B(x,d(x,y)))}.

The

We restrict our attention to Besov spaces with two indices (i.e. “

”).

The above spaces and (semi)norms go under various names,

Besov spaces is the usual name in the metric space literature,

3
Deformation of the metric

Recall the standing assumptions from the beginning of Section

In particular,

In this section we deal with purely metric notions, and do not need any

Let
\rho:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)

\rho(0)=\lim_{t\to 0{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}}\rho(t)

We define

is not a metric on

We therefore introduce

Balls with respect to the metric

are denoted by

Proposition 3.1
.

\hat{d}
is a metric on

.

For proving this proposition,

Lemma 3.2

Let

be such that

Then

0<d(x,y)\inf_{B(x,d(x,y))}\rho\leq\hat{d}(x,y)\leq(\rho(x)+\rho(y))d(x,y).

(3.2)

Let

x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
be any chain from
x
to
y
and
let

be the smallest index such that
x_{j_{0}}\notin B(x,d(x,y))

(Such a

always exists as
x_{n}=y\notin B(x,d(x,y))

Let

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho(x_{j})+\rho(x_{j-1}))d(x_{j},x_{j-1})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{j_{0}}\rho(x_{j-1})d(x_{j},x_{j-1})

\displaystyle\geq m\sum_{j=1}^{j_{0}}d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\geq md(x,x_{j_{0}})\geq md(x,y).

Taking the infimum over all chains from

to

For the second inequality, we instead use the trivial chain

\hat{d}(x,y)\leq(\rho(x)+\rho(y))d(x,y).\qed

Clearly

for each

, and
\hat{d}
is symmetric and satisfies

Finally,

for all

by Lemma

.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
x,y\in Z
and that
|x|\leq|y|
.
Then

\hat{d}(x,y)\geq\int_{|x|}^{|y|}\rho\,dt.

Let

x=x_{0},x_{1},\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.},x_{n}=y
be a chain.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho(x_{j})+\rho(x_{j-1}))d(x_{j},x_{j-1})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{|x_{j}|\geq|x_{j-1}|}\rho(x_{j-1})d(x_{j},x_{j-1})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{|x_{j}|\geq|x_{j-1}|}\int_{|x_{j-1}|}^{|x_{j}|}\rho\,dt\geq\int_{|x|}^{|y|}\rho\,dt.

The claim then follows by taking the infimum over all chains from

to

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

The deformation
(Z^{\prime},d)\mapsto(Z^{\prime},\hat{d})
is topology-preserving, i.e. for any sequence
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
in

and

\lim_{j\to\infty}d(x_{j},x)=0\quad\textrm{if and only if}\quad\lim_{j\to\infty}\hat{d}(x_{j},x)=0.

If

, then clearly

and so for big enough

.

Conversely, if

.
So for large enough

Thus, Lemma

yielding

.

(Z^{\prime},\hat{d})

is uniformly perfect at large scales at

with a constant
\hat{\kappa}>1
that depends only on

,
\kappa

and
|b^{\prime}|\rho(b^{\prime})
.

Note that by (

) and the monotonicity
of
\rho
, we have
\rho(0)=\infty
and therefore

.
By the uniform perfectness at

, there are

\kappa^{-j-1}|b^{\prime}|\leq|x_{j}|\leq\kappa^{-j}|b^{\prime}|,\quad j=1,2,\dots.

By Lemmas

\int_{|x_{j}|/\kappa}^{|x_{j}|}\rho\,dt\simeq\rho(x_{j})|x_{j}|\lesssim\int_{|x_{j}|}^{|b^{\prime}|}\rho\,dt

Then for some

Remark 3.10
.

We will now
explain why it is important in
this paper to define
sphericalization and flattening using
a sum as in (
3.1
) rather than
a product.
Suppose that one would like to sphericalize an unbounded metric space
(Z,d)
using
the formula

\tilde{d}(x,y):=\inf\biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{\rho}(x_{j})\tilde{\rho}(x_{j-1})d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\biggr\},

(3.9)

where the infimum is taken over all chains
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
,
and with a general positive nonincreasing function
\tilde{\rho}(x)=\tilde{\rho}(|x|)

such that
\tilde{\rho}(0)<\infty
.
Note that a transformation
using products as in (
3.9
)
that is equivalent to a transformation
using sums as in (
3.1
),
or path integrals,
would typically have
\tilde{\rho}\simeq\sqrt{\rho}
.

The main problem with the product definition is that

\tilde{\rho}(x_{j})\tilde{\rho}(x_{j-1})
can be small even when
\tilde{\rho}(x_{j})

is large, provided that
x_{j-1}
is very far away and
\tilde{\rho}
is fast decreasing.
We can see this by considering a transformation with

\tilde{\rho}
such that
\lim_{t\to\infty}t\tilde{\rho}(t)=0
.
Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in Z
.
Since
(Z,d)
is unbounded, there is
a sequence
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that
|y_{j}|\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
.
We then get (using the chain

x_{0}=z_{1},x_{1}=y_{j},x_{2}=z_{2}
) that
for sufficiently large
j
,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\rho}(z_{1})\tilde{\rho}(y_{j})d(y_{j},z_{1})+\tilde{\rho}(z_{2})\tilde{\rho}(y_{j})d(y_{j},z_{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2(\tilde{\rho}(z_{1})+\tilde{\rho}(z_{2}))\tilde{\rho}(y_{j})|y_{j}|\to 0,

\displaystyle\text{as }j\to\infty.

So in order for
\tilde{d}
to be a metric, we need

\liminf_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x|\tilde{\rho}(|x|)>0
.
If this type of sphericalization of e.g.
Z={\mathbf{R}^{n}}
is equipped
with the measure
d\tilde{\nu}=\tilde{\rho}^{\sigma}\,dx
, then

\tilde{\nu}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})=\infty
whenever
\sigma\leq n
, and thus
\tilde{\nu}
is not
a doubling measure (since the sphericalized space is required to be bounded).
In conclusion,
using sums as in (
3.1
), rather than products as in (
3.9
),
gives much more flexibility.

If one instead uses the approach by
Balogh–Buckley
[
1
]
then one is
limited to pathconnected spaces with additional connectivity at
\infty
.
In this case Lemma
3.3
holds.
However, with such an approach,
the completion of

(\mathbf{R},\hat{d})
will add two points, corresponding to
\pm\infty
, and
is thus
not
a sphericalization in our sense.
Hence additional connectivity at
\infty
is required,
which would severely
restrict the spaces under consideration.

Sphericalization and flattening

In the rest of the paper,

\nu
.

In order to treat sphericalization and flattening simultaneously it will be convenient to introduce the function

For

where the comparison constants depend only on

,
M
and
\sigma
.
In particular,

satisfies condition (

).

Equip

with the metric
\hat{d}
defined as
in (

) with the above

.
Recall that

R_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in Z}|x|\quad\text{and}\quad Z^{\prime}=\begin{cases}Z,&\text{if }m_{0}=1,\\
Z\setminus\{b\},&\text{if }m_{0}=0.\end{cases}

Note that
m(x)>0
for every
x\in Z^{\prime}
.
Recall also that
\widehat{Z}
is the metric completion of
(Z^{\prime},\hat{d})
and that we denote balls with respect to
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d})
by
\widehat{B}
.
We equip

.

Remark 4.1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
x,y\in Z^{\prime}
and
M>1
.

1.

If

, then

\hat{d}(x,y)\gtrsim\rho(x)m(x)=\frac{1}{\nu(B_{m(x)})^{1/\sigma}}\geq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{m(y)})^{1/\sigma}}.

(4.4)

2.

If
M^{-1}m(x)\leq m(y)\leq Mm(x)
, then

\hat{d}(x,y)\simeq\rho(x)d(x,y).

In both cases, the comparison constants depend only on
C_{\nu}
,
M
and
\sigma
.

1

Lemma
3.3
and the doubling property of
\nu
yield

\displaystyle\hat{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{|x|}^{|y|}\rho\,dt=\int_{|x|}^{|y|}\frac{1}{m(t)\nu(B_{m(t)})^{1/\sigma}}\,dt=\int_{m(x)}^{m(y)}\frac{1}{s\nu(B_{s})^{1/\sigma}}\,ds

\displaystyle\geq\int_{m(x)}^{Mm(x)}\frac{1}{s\nu(B_{s})^{1/\sigma}}\,ds\simeq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{m(x)})^{1/\sigma}},

which proves (
4.4
).

2

The
\lesssim
inequality
follows directly from (
4.3
)
by choosing the trivial chain
x=x_{0},x_{1}=y
.

For the
\gtrsim
inequality, let
x=x_{0},x_{1},\mathinner{.}\nonscript\!.\nonscript\!\mathinner{.},x_{n}=y
be a chain.
If
m(x_{j})\leq Mm(x)
for all

, then
\rho(x_{j})\gtrsim\rho(x)

by (

), and hence

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho(x_{j})+\rho(x_{j-1}))d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\gtrsim\rho(x)\sum_{j=1}^{n}d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\geq\rho(x)d(x,y).

On the other hand, if

)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho(x_{j})+\rho(x_{j-1}))d(x_{j},x_{j-1})\geq\hat{d}(x,x_{k})\gtrsim\rho(x)m(x).

Since also

to

.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

There are constants

and
a_{2}\geq a_{1}>0
,
depending only on

and

Moreover,

{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(x,r))\simeq\rho(x)^{\sigma}\nu\biggl(B\biggl(x,\frac{r}{\rho(x)}\biggr)\biggr).

In this lemma the comparison constants depend only on
C_{\nu}
and
\sigma
.

First note that
it follows from Lemma
4.2
2

with
M=2
that there are
C_{1}\geq c_{1}>0
such that

c_{1}\rho(x)d(x,y)\leq\hat{d}(x,y)\leq C_{1}\rho(x)d(x,y)\quad\text{when }\tfrac{1}{2}m(x)\leq m(y)\leq 2m(x).

(4.5)

Assume next that
y\in\widehat{B}(x,r)
.
If
m(y)\geq 2m(x)
or
m(x)\geq 2m(y)
, then Lemma

1

\frac{1}{\nu(B_{m(x)})^{1/\sigma}}\lesssim\hat{d}(x,y)<r\leq\frac{c_{0}}{\nu(B_{m(x)})^{1/\sigma}}.

This leads to a contradiction if

is sufficiently small.

.
Then

It then follows from (

\tilde{d}(x,y)\simeq{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}({\boldsymbol{\infty}},\hat{d}(y,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})))^{-1/\sigma}\simeq{\hat{\nu}}\biggl(\widehat{B}\biggl(y,\frac{1}{\nu(B_{m(y)})^{1/\sigma}}\biggr)\biggr)^{-1/\sigma}\simeq m(y),

where the last comparison follows from Proposition
5.6

with
r=\nu(B_{m(y)})^{-1/\sigma}
.

If
m(y)\geq 2m(x)
then as in (
6.5
),
also
\tfrac{1}{2}m(y)\leq d(x,y)\leq\tfrac{3}{2}m(y)
and the result follows.
On the other hand, if
m(y)\leq 2m(x)
then

\nu(B_{m(y)})\lesssim\nu(B_{m(x)})
by the doubling
property of
\nu
,
and thus
Corollary
5.4
implies that we are in the following case:

Case 2:

\hat{d}(x,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})\lesssim\hat{d}(y,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})\leq\hat{d}(x,{\boldsymbol{\infty}})
.
In particular,
\widehat{m}(x)\simeq\widehat{m}(y)
.
It also follows from Corollary
5.4
that

\nu(B_{m(x)})\simeq\nu(B_{m(y)})
,
and thus
m(x)\simeq m(y)
by Lemma
2.2
.
Using also Lemma
4.2
2
(applied first to
\tilde{d}
and then to
\hat{d}
) and Lemma
7.1
we get that

\tilde{d}(x,y)\simeq\hat{\rho}(x)\hat{d}(x,y)\simeq\hat{\rho}(x)\rho(x)d(x,y)\simeq d(x,y).\qed

7.2
The sphericalization of a flattened space

In this section we assume that
(Z,d)
is a bounded complete metric space
equipped with a doubling measure
\nu
with a doubling constant
C_{\nu}
.
We also assume that
Z
is uniformly perfect at a base point
b
with constant
\kappa>1
.

We denote the flattening of this space by
(Z^{\prime},\hat{d},{\hat{\nu}})
as defined
in Section
4
with the gauge function
m(x)=|x|

and
Z^{\prime}=Z\setminus\{b\}
.
By Lemma
5.1
2
the space
(Z^{\prime},\hat{d})
is unbounded,
and by Proposition
3.7
it
is complete, so
\widehat{Z}=Z^{\prime}
.
Theorem
5.9
shows that the measure
{\hat{\nu}}
is
doubling on
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d})
with a doubling constant
depending only on
C_{\nu}
,
\kappa
and
\sigma
.

Note that
\rho
is doubling and the
doubling constant
A
for
\rho

in (
3.5
) can be chosen so that
A\leq 2C_{\nu}^{1/\sigma}
.
Therefore by Proposition
3.9
, the space
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d})

is uniformly perfect at large scales at
a base point
\hat{b}\in\widehat{Z}

with a constant
\hat{\kappa}>1
that depends only on

C_{\nu}
,
\kappa
,
\sigma
and
|\hat{b}|\rho(\hat{b})=\nu(B_{\hat{b}})^{-1/\sigma}
, where we let

B_{z}=B(b,|z|)\quad\text{for }z\in Z

in this section.

Then we sphericalize the space
(\widehat{Z},\hat{d},{\hat{\nu}})

at the base point
\hat{b}
, as defined in Section
4

with the function
\widehat{m}(x)=\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})+1
.
After sphericalization, we get a new space, which we denote by
(\widetilde{Z},\tilde{d},{\tilde{\nu}})
.
The act of sphericalization adds one point to
\widehat{Z}
, which is denoted
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
.
It follows from Case 3 in the proof of Theorem
7.4
below
that
we can
identify
{\boldsymbol{\infty}}
with the point
b
that was removed in the flattening.
Thus
\widetilde{Z}=(Z\setminus\{b\})\cup\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\}=Z
.

Denote by
\rho
the metric density function used in the flattening and by
\hat{\rho}
the metric density function used in the sphericalization, i.e.

\rho(x)=\frac{1}{|x|\nu(B_{x})^{1/\sigma}}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{\rho}(x)=\frac{1}{\widehat{m}(x){\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(\hat{b},\widehat{m}(x)))^{1/\sigma}}.

Recall that

R_{\infty}:=\sup_{x\in Z}|x|<\infty.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
x\in Z\setminus\{b\}
.
Then
\rho(x)\hat{\rho}(x)\simeq 1
.
In particular, we have
d{\tilde{\nu}}(x)\simeq d\nu(x)
for every

x\in Z\setminus\{b\}
.
The comparison constants depend only on
C_{\nu}
,
\kappa
,
\sigma
,
R_{\infty}
,
\nu(Z)
and
|\hat{b}|
.

In this case we have

\frac{1}{\rho(x)\hat{\rho}(x)}=|x|\nu(B_{x})^{1/\sigma}\widehat{m}(x){\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(\hat{b},\widehat{m}(x)))^{1/\sigma},

(7.3)

where
\widehat{m}(x)=\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})+1
.
Note that
|\hat{b}|\simeq 1

and
\nu(B_{\hat{b}})\simeq 1

(since the comparison constants are allowed to depend on

C_{\nu}
,
R_{\infty}
,
\nu(Z)
and
|\hat{b}|
).

If
|x|\geq\tfrac{1}{2}|\hat{b}|
then also
|x|\simeq 1
.
Moreover, Lemma
4.2
2
implies that

\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})\simeq\rho(\hat{b})d(x,\hat{b})\simeq d(x,\hat{b})\leq d(x,b)+d(b,\hat{b})\leq 2R_{\infty}.

Hence

\widehat{m}(x)=\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})+1\simeq 1\quad\text{and consequently}\quad{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(\hat{b},\widehat{m}(x)))\simeq 1,

where we also used Lemma
4.3
.
Thus all the four factors in (
7.3
) are
\simeq 1
,
which gives the desired estimate.

On the other hand, if
|x|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}|\hat{b}|
then
Corollary
5.4
with
y=\hat{b}

gives that

\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})\simeq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{x})^{1/\sigma}}\geq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{\hat{b}})^{1/\sigma}}\simeq 1

and hence

\widehat{m}(x)=\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})+1\simeq\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})\simeq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{x})^{1/\sigma}}.

Thus, the two middle factors in (
7.3
) cancel.
Then Lemma
2.1

and Proposition
5.6
with
r=\widehat{m}(x)
and
m(x)=|x|
yield that

{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(\hat{b},\widehat{m}(x)))^{1/\sigma}\simeq{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(x,\widehat{m}(x)))^{1/\sigma}\simeq(|x|^{-\sigma})^{1/\sigma}=|x|^{-1}.

Inserting this into (
7.3
) concludes the proof.
∎

Theorem 7.4
.

The sphericalization of the flattened space is equivalent to the original
space in the following sense:
The metrics
\tilde{d}
and
d
are equivalent and the measures
{\tilde{\nu}}
and
\nu

are comparable, that is, for all
x,y\in Z
,

\tilde{d}(x,y)\simeq d(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad d{\tilde{\nu}}(x)\simeq d\nu(x),

where the comparison constants depend only on
C_{\nu}
,
\kappa
,
\sigma
,
R_{\infty}
,
\nu(Z)
and
|\hat{b}|
.

Since
{\tilde{\nu}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\infty}}\})=\nu(\{b\})=0
by Lemma
2.2
, the comparability of the measures follows from Lemma
7.3
.
For the metrics we may assume by symmetry that
|x|\leq|y|
and
y\neq b
.
Let

a\leq\tfrac{1}{2}|\hat{b}|/R_{\infty}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Case 1:

0<|x|\leq a|y|
.
By Lemma
2.2
,

\nu(B_{x})\leq\Lambda a^{\alpha}\nu(B_{y})\leq\Lambda a^{\alpha}\nu(Z).

(7.4)

|x|\leq a|y|\leq aR_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{2}|\hat{b}|

and hence by Corollary
5.4
with

\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})\simeq\frac{1}{\nu(B_{x})^{1/\sigma}}\gtrsim a^{-\alpha/\sigma},

(7.5)

where by (
7.4
) the comparison constants do not depend on
a
.
Since
|y|\leq R_{\infty}\lesssim|\hat{b}|
, Lemma
5.5
,
(
7.4
) and (
7.5

\hat{d}(y,\hat{b})\lesssim\frac{1}{\nu(B_{y})^{1/\sigma}}\lesssim\frac{a^{\alpha/\sigma}}{\nu(B_{x})^{1/\sigma}}\simeq a^{\alpha/\sigma}\hat{d}(x,\hat{b}),

where the comparison constants do not depend on
a
.
Thus by choosing

small enough,
we see that it follows from (

|x|\leq a|y|

\widehat{m}(x)=\hat{d}(x,\hat{b})+1\geq 2(\hat{d}(y,\hat{b})+1)=2\widehat{m}(y)\quad\text{and}\quad d(x,y)\simeq|y|.

5.4

and Lemma
2.1

\tilde{d}(x,y)\simeq\frac{1}{{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(\hat{b},\widehat{m}(y)))^{1/\sigma}}\simeq\frac{1}{{\hat{\nu}}(\widehat{B}(y,\widehat{m}(y)))^{1/\sigma}}.

(7.8)

Case 2:
