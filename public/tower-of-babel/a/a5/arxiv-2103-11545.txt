1.
Introduction

In this paper, a meromorphic function always means a function meromorphic in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. We assume that the readers are familiar with the standard notation and basic results of Nevanlinna theory; see, e.g.,
[
15
,
20
]
. Moreover, we say that a function
\gamma(z)\not\equiv\infty
is a
small
function of
f
if
T(r,\gamma)=S(r,f)
, where
S(r,f)
denotes any quantity satisfying
S(r,f)=o(T(r,f))
,
r\rightarrow{\infty}
, possibly outside an exceptional set of finite linear measure. For a
differential polynomial

P(z,f)
in
f
, we mean a finite sum of monomials in
f
and its derivatives with the form

P(z,f)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}f^{n_{j0}}(f^{\prime})^{n_{j1}}\cdots(f^{(k)})^{n_{jk}},

where
n_{j0},\cdots,n_{jk}\in\mathbb{N}
and the coefficients
a_{j}
are small functions of
f
. We define the
degree
of
P(z,f)
to be the greatest integer of
d_{j}:=\sum_{l=0}^{k}n_{jl}
,
1\leq j\leq n
, and denote it by
\deg_{f}(P(z,f))
.

A generalization of the theorem of Tumura–Clunie
[
11
,
26
]
given by Hayman
[
15
, p. 69]
states that: If a nonconstant meromorphic function
f(z)
satisfies
f(z)^{n}+P(z,f)=g(z)
, where
n\geq 2
is an integer and
P(z,f)
is a differential polynomial in
f
of degree
\leq n-1
with small functions of
f
as coefficients, and
N(r,f)+N(r,1/g)=S(r,f)
, then there is a small function
\gamma(z)
of
f(z)
such that
(f(z)-\gamma(z))^{n}=g(z)
. In
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, Li and his coauthor considered the equation

(1.1)

f^{n}+P(z,f)=b_{1}e^{\alpha_{1}z}+b_{2}e^{\alpha_{2}z},

where
P(z,f)
is a differential polynomial in
f
of degree
\leq n-1
with small functions of
f
,
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are nonzero constants. They solved meromorphic solutions such that
N(r,f)=S(r,f)
of (
1.1
) under assumptions that
\deg_{f}(P(z,f))\leq n-2
or that
\deg_{f}(P(z,f))=n-1
and
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=0
or
0<\alpha_{2}/\alpha_{1}\leq(n-1)/n
. Li
[
22
]
further asked how to solve the general solutions of (
1.1
). Denote by
\rho(f)
the order of a meromorphic function
f(z)
. Assuming that in (
1.1
) all coefficients of
P(z,f)
have order less than
\rho(f)
, Zhang, Gao and Zhang
[
29
]
showed that if
\alpha_{2}/\alpha_{1}
is real and (
1.1
) has an entire solution
f
, then
\alpha_{2}/\alpha_{1}
must be equal to
-1
or a positive rational number and in either case
f
is a linear combination of exponential functions with certain coefficients plus some entire function of order less than
1
. We note that an application of the method in
[
19
]
to equation (
1.1
) will yield that (
1.1
) cannot have entire solutions when
\alpha_{2}/\alpha_{1}
is not real and thus
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
actually gives a complete answer to Li’s question in the entire solution case.

The main purpose of this paper is to provide a generalization of
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
by solving entire solutions of (
1.1
) when the right-hand side is replaced by
b_{1}(z)e^{p_{1}(z)}+b_{2}(z)e^{p_{2}(z)}
, where
b_{1}(z)
and
b_{2}(z)
are now nonzero polynomials,
p_{1}(z)
and
p_{2}(z)
are two nonconstant polynomials of the same degree
k
with distinct leading coefficients, respectively; see Theorem
2.1
in section
2
. To this end, we first need to give a generalization of
[
29
, Lemma 2.3]
on first-order differential equations. Consider the differential equation

(1.2)

f^{\prime}-\kappa f=\beta,

where
\kappa
is a nonzero polynomial and
\beta
is an entire small function of
e^{p(z)}
, where
p(z)
is a primitive function of
\kappa
. By elementary integration, the general solution of (
1.2
) is
f=ce^{p(z)}+H(z)
with

(1.3)

H(z)=e^{p(z)}\int_{0}^{z}\beta(t)e^{-p(t)}dt.

The function
H(z)
with
\beta
being a polynomial has frequently appeared in the study of
differential equations and complex dynamics; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
1
,
7
,
8
,
14
,
27
]
. Below we describe the growth behavior of
H(z)
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
.

We write
p(z)=\alpha z^{k}+p_{k-1}(z)
, where
\alpha=a+ib\not=0
with
a,b
real and
p_{k-1}(z)
is a polynomial of degree at most
k-1
. Denote

(1.4)

\delta(p,\theta)=a\cos k\theta-b\sin k\theta,\quad\theta\in[0,2\pi).

Let
\varepsilon>0
be given and small. Then on the ray
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\geq 0
, we have: (1) if
\delta(p,\theta)>0
, then there exists an
r_{0}=r_{0}(\theta)
such that
\log|e^{p(re^{i\theta})}|
is increasing on
[r_{0},\infty)
and
|e^{p(re^{i\theta})}|\geq e^{(1-\varepsilon)\delta(p,\theta)r^{k}}
there; (2) if
\delta(p,\theta)<0
, then there exists an
r_{0}=r_{0}(\theta)
such that
\log|e^{p(re^{i\theta})}|
is decreasing on
[r_{0},\infty)
and
|e^{p(re^{i\theta})}|\leq e^{(1-\varepsilon)\delta(p,\theta)r^{k}}
there; see
[
6
]
or
[
20
, Lemma 5.14]
. Let
\theta_{j}\in[0,2\pi)
,
j=1,2,\cdots,2k
, be such that
\delta(p,\theta_{j})=0
. We may suppose that
\theta_{1}<\pi
and
\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\cdots<\theta_{2k}
. Then
\theta_{j}=\theta_{1}+(j-1)\pi/k
. Moreover, denoting
\theta_{2k+1}=\theta_{1}+2\pi
, we have
2k
sectors
S_{j}
defined as

(1.5)

S_{j}=\left\{re^{i\theta}:\,0\leq r<\infty,\quad\theta_{j}<\theta<\theta_{j+1}\right\},\quad j=1,\cdots,2k.

Moreover, for any
\epsilon>0
, we denote

(1.6)

\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}=\{re^{i\theta}:\,0\leq r<\infty,\quad\theta_{j}+\epsilon\leq\theta\leq\theta_{j+1}-\epsilon\},\quad j=1,\cdots,2k.

Denote by
\overline{S}_{j}
and
\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
the closure of
S_{j}
and
S_{j,\epsilon}
, respectively. We have

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
p(z)
is polynomial with degree
k\geq 1
and that
\beta
is a small entire function of
e^{p(z)}
.
Then for each
S_{j}
where
\delta(p,\theta)>0
, there is a constant
a_{j}
such that
|H(re^{i\theta})-a_{j}e^{p(re^{i\theta})}|\leq e^{o(1)r^{k}}
uniformly as
r\to\infty
in
\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
; for each
S_{j}
where
\delta(p,\theta)<0
,
|H(re^{i\theta})-ae^{p(re^{i\theta})}|\leq e^{o(1)r^{k}}
uniformly as
r\to\infty
in
\overline{S}_{j}
for any constant
a
.

By the same arguments as in
[
29
, Lemma 2.3]
together with the growth properties of
e^{p(z)}
mentioned before, we easily prove Theorem
1.1
. We omit the proof. Here we give two remarks: First, if
\rho=\max\{\rho(\beta),k-1\}<k
, then the error term
e^{o(1)r^{k}}
in Theorem
1.1
can be replaced by
e^{r^{\eta}}
for a constant
\eta>\rho
. Second, Theorem
1.1
easily extends to the case where the function
\beta(z)
in (
1.2
) is meromorphic and has at most finitely many poles. If the solution
f
of equation (
1.2
) is meromorphic, then there is a rational function
R(z)
such that
R(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
and
h(z)=f(z)-R(z)
is entire. It follows that
f(z)=h(z)+R(z)
and
h
satisfies
h^{\prime}-\kappa h=\beta-(R^{\prime}-\kappa R)
and
\beta_{1}=\beta-(R^{\prime}-\kappa R)
is a small entire function of
e^{p(z)}
.

The rest of this paper is organized in the following way. In section

, we apply Theorem
1.1
to Tumura–Clunie type differential equations and provide a generalization of
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
. Our results also improve
[
24
, Theorem 1]
. Then we use our results to determine all solutions with relatively few zeros of the second-order linear differential equation:

, where
b_{j}(z)
,

, are polynomials,
p_{1}(z)
and
p_{2}(z)
are two polynomials of the same degree with distinct leading coefficients. In section

, we consider the first-order linear differential equation

with a polynomial

and two rational functions

and

and show that

must be a constant when

is of finite order. This equation is related to a class of meromorphic functions appearing in complex dynamics.

2.
Tumura–Clunie differential equations

Let
b_{1}(z)
and
b_{2}(z)
be two nonzero polynomials and
p_{1}(z)
and
p_{2}(z)
be two polynomials of the same degree
k\geq 1
with distinct leading coefficients
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, respectively. In this section, we solve entire solutions of the Tumura–Clunie type differential equation

(2.1)

f^{n}+P(z,f)=b_{1}e^{p_{1}}+b_{2}e^{p_{2}},

where
n\geq 2
and
P(z,f)
is a differential polynomial in
f
of degree
\leq n-1
with meromorphic functions of order less than
k
as coefficients. In the following, a differential polynomial in
f
will always have meromorphic functions of order less than
k
as coefficients and thus we will omit mentioning this. By doing a linear transformation
z\to z/\alpha_{1}^{1/k}
or
z\to z/\alpha_{2}^{1/k}
, if necessary, we may suppose that the leading coefficients of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are
\alpha_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{2}=\alpha
, respectively, and
|\alpha|\leq 1
. With these settings, we prove the following

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
be an integer and
P(z,f)
be a differential polynomial in
f
of degree
\leq{n-1}
. Let
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
be two nonzero polynomials and
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
be two nonconstant polynomials of degree
k
with distinct leading coefficients
1
and
\alpha
, respectively, and
p_{1}(0)=p_{2}(0)=0
. Suppose that (
2.1
) has an entire solution
f
. Then
\alpha
is real and rational. Moreover,

(1)

if
\alpha<0
, then
\alpha=-1
and
f=\gamma+\gamma_{1}e^{p_{1}/n}+\gamma_{2}e^{p_{2}/n}
,
where
\gamma
is an entire function of order less than
k
and
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
are
two polynomials such that
\gamma_{1}^{n}=b_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}^{n}=b_{2}
;

(2)

if
0<\alpha<1
, letting
m
be the smallest integer such that
\alpha\leq[(m+1)n-1]/[(m+1)n]
,
then
f=\gamma+\gamma_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}(b_{2}/b_{1})^{j}e^{[jn(\alpha-1)+1]p_{1}/n}
, where
\gamma
is a function of order less than
k
and
\gamma_{1}
is a polynomial such that
\gamma_{1}^{n}=b_{1}
and
c_{0}
,
\cdots
,
c_{m}
are constants such that
c_{0}^{n}=1
when
m=0
, and
c_{0}^{n}=nc_{0}^{n-1}c_{1}=1
when
m=1
, and
c_{0}^{n}=nc_{0}^{n-1}c_{1}=1
and
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{0}+\cdots+j_{m}=n,\\
j_{1}+\cdots+mj_{m}=j\end{subarray}}\frac{n!}{j_{0}!j_{1}!\cdots j_{m}!}c_{0}^{j_{0}}c_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots c_{m}^{j_{m}}=0
,
j=2,\cdots,m
, when
m\geq 2
. Moreover, we have
p_{2}=\alpha p_{1}
when
m\geq 1
.

We now apply Theorem
2.1
to linear differential equations. In the second-order case, the linear differential equation

(2.2)

g^{\prime\prime}+Ag=0,

where
A
is an entire function, has attracted much interest; see
[
20
, chapter 5]
and references therein. A famous conjecture concerning the zero distribution of solutions of (
2.2
) is known as the
Bank–Laine conjecture

[
3
,
4
]
: Let
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
be two linearly independent solutions of (
2.2
) and denote by
\lambda(g)
the exponent of convergence of zeros
of
g
. Is
\max\{\lambda(g_{1}),\lambda(g_{2})\}=\infty
whenever
\rho(A)
is not an integer? Recently, this conjecture was disproved by Bergweiler and Eremenko
[
9
,
10
]
. In their construction of the counterexamples, they started from the solutions of (
2.2
) when
A
is a polynomial of
e^{z}
of degree 2.

Suppose that the coefficient
A
in (
2.2
) has the form
A=-[b_{1}(z)e^{p_{1}(z)}+b_{2}(z)e^{p_{2}(z)}+b_{3}(z)]
, where
b_{1}(z)
,
b_{2}(z)
and
p_{1}(z)
,
p_{2}(z)
are as in Theorem
2.1
and
b_{3}(z)
is a polynomial. If
\alpha
is non-real or is real negative, then all non-trivial solutions of (
2.2
) satisfy
\lambda(f)=\infty
; see
[
5
,
19
]
. When
\alpha
is positive, Ishizaki
[
18
, Theorem 1]
proved: If
0<\alpha<1/2
or if
b_{3}\equiv 0
and
3/4<\alpha<1
, then all non-trivial solutions of (
2.2
) satisfy
\lambda(g)\geq k
. We will improve Ishizaki’s result by showing that the condition
b_{3}\equiv 0
can be removed. In fact, with Theorem
2.1
at our disposal, we are able to determine all solutions such that
\lambda(g)<k
of equation (
2.2
) with
A
above.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
and
b_{3}
be polynomials such that
b_{1}b_{2}\not\equiv 0
and
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
be two polynomials of degree
k\geq 1
with distinct leading coefficients
1
and
\alpha
, respectively, and
p_{1}(0)=p_{2}(0)=0
. Let
A=-(b_{1}e^{p_{1}}+b_{2}e^{p_{2}}+b_{3})
. Suppose that (
2.2
) has a non-trivial solution such that
\lambda(g)<k
. Then
\alpha=1/2
or
\alpha=3/4
. Moreover,

(1)

if
\alpha=1/2
, then
p_{2}=p_{1}/2
,
g=\kappa e^{h}
, where
\kappa
is a polynomial with simple roots only and
h
satisfies
h^{\prime}=\gamma_{1}e^{p_{1}/2}+\gamma
with
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma
being two polynomials such that
\gamma_{1}^{2}=b_{1}
,
2\gamma_{1}\gamma+\gamma_{1}^{\prime}+\gamma_{1}p_{1}^{\prime}/2+2\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa\gamma_{1}=b_{2}
and
\gamma^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}+2\gamma\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa+\kappa^{\prime\prime}/\kappa=b_{3}
;

(2)

if
\alpha=3/4
, then
p_{1}=z
,
p_{2}=3z/4
and
g=e^{h}
, where
h
satisfies
h^{\prime}=-4c^{2}e^{z/2}+ce^{z/4}-1/8
and
A=-(16c^{2}e^{z}-8c^{3}e^{3z/4}+1/64)
, where
c
is a nonzero constant.

Proof.

We write
g=\kappa e^{h}
, where
h
is an entire function and
\kappa
is the canonical product from the zeros of
g
and satisfies
\rho(\kappa)=\lambda(\kappa)<k
. By denoting
f=h^{\prime}
, from (
2.2
) we have

(2.3)

f^{2}+f^{\prime}+2\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}f+\frac{\kappa^{\prime\prime}}{\kappa}=b_{1}(z)e^{p_{1}(z)}+b_{2}(z)e^{p_{2}(z)}+b_{3}(z).

By Theorem
2.1
together with previous discussions we know that
\alpha
is a positive rational number. Below we consider the two cases where
0<\alpha\leq 1/2
and
1/2<\alpha<1
, respectively.

When
0<\alpha\leq 1/2
, by Theorem
2.1
we may write
f=\gamma_{1}e^{p_{1}/2}+\gamma
, where
\gamma_{1}
is a polynomial such that
\gamma_{1}^{2}=b_{1}
and
\gamma
is an entire function of order less than
k
. Substitution into equation (
2.3
) gives

(2.4)

\begin{split}2\gamma_{1}\left(\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\gamma_{1}}+\frac{p_{1}^{\prime}}{4}+\gamma\right)e^{p_{1}/2}-b_{2}e^{p_{2}}+\gamma^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}+2\gamma\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}+\frac{\kappa^{\prime\prime}}{\kappa}-b_{3}=0.\end{split}

If
\alpha\not=1/2
, then by Borel’s lemma (see
[
28
, pp. 69–70]
) we get
b_{2}\equiv 0
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\alpha=1/2
. Then by rewriting equation (
2.4
) and applying Borel’s lemma to the resulting equation again, we get
\gamma^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}+2\gamma\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa+\kappa^{\prime\prime}/\kappa=b_{3}
and
2\gamma_{1}\gamma+\gamma_{1}^{\prime}+\gamma_{1}p_{1}^{\prime}/2+2\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa\gamma_{1}=b_{2}e^{p_{2}-p_{1}/2}
. From the second relation we see that
\kappa
has only finitely many zeros and thus we may suppose that
\kappa
is a polynomial. Then from the first relation we see that
\gamma
is also a polynomial and all zeros of
\kappa
are simple. This implies that
p_{2}=p_{1}/2
.

When
1/2<\alpha<1
, by Theorem
2.1
we may write
f=\gamma+\gamma_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}(b_{2}/b_{1})^{j}e^{[2j(\alpha-1)+1]p_{1}/2}
, where
m\geq 1
is an integer,
\gamma_{1}
is a polynomial such that
\gamma_{1}^{2}=b_{1}
and
\gamma
is a meromorphic function with finitely many poles and of order less than
k
, and
c_{0}
,
\cdots
,
c_{m}
are constants such that
c_{0}^{2}=2c_{0}c_{1}=1
when
m=1
, and
c_{0}^{2}=2c_{0}c_{1}=1
and
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{0}+\cdots+j_{m}=2,\\
j_{1}+\cdots+mj_{m}=j\end{subarray}}\frac{2}{j_{0}!j_{1}!\cdots j_{m}!}c_{0}^{j_{0}}c_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots c_{m}^{j_{m}}=0
,
j=2,\cdots,m
, when
m\geq 2
. Note that
(2m-1)/(2m)<\alpha\leq[2(m+1)-1]/[2(m+1)]
and
(m+j+1)(\alpha-1)+1=j(\alpha-1)+1/2
,
j=0,1,\cdots,m-1
, when
\alpha=[2(m+1)-1]/[2(m+1)]
. By substituting this expression together with
p_{2}=\alpha p_{1}
into equation (
2.3
), we get

(2.5)

\begin{split}&\gamma_{1}^{2}\sum_{k_{0}=m+1}^{2m}C_{k_{0}}\left(\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}}\right)^{k_{0}}e^{(k_{0}\alpha-k_{0}+1)p_{1}}+\gamma^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}+2\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}\gamma+\frac{\kappa^{\prime\prime}}{\kappa}-b_{3}\\
&+\gamma_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{m}c_{j}\left(\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}}\right)^{j}\left[2\gamma+\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\prime}}{\gamma_{1}}+2\frac{\kappa^{\prime}}{\kappa}+j\frac{(b_{2}/b_{1})^{\prime}}{b_{2}/b_{1}}+\frac{2j(\alpha-1)+1}{2}p_{1}^{\prime}\right]e^{\frac{2j(\alpha-1)+1}{2}p_{1}}=0,\end{split}

where
C_{k_{0}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{0}+\cdots+j_{m}=2,\\
j_{1}+\cdots+mj_{m}=k_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{2!}{j_{0}!j_{1}!\cdots j_{m}!}c_{0}^{j_{0}}c_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots c_{m}^{j_{m}}
,
k_{0}=m+1,\cdots,2m
. We may rewrite the left-hand side of equation (
2.5
) by combining the same exponential terms together. By Borel’s lemma, all coefficients of the exponential terms in the resulting equation vanish identically. Therefore, we have
\gamma^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}+2\gamma\kappa^{\prime}/\kappa+\kappa^{\prime\prime}/\kappa=b_{3}
and, by looking at the coefficients of the terms
e^{[2m(\alpha-1)+1]p_{1}/2}
and
e^{p_{1}/2}
in (

\hat{C}_{k_{0}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{0}+\cdots+j_{m}=n,\\

for

.
For each

, since

, it is easy to see that there is an

,

\omega_{l,i}=1

Since
f=b_{1}^{1/n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}c_{i}(b_{2}/b_{1})^{i}e^{t_{i}p_{1}}+H_{0}
, then by considering the growth of

. Further, by substituting

as

. Since
\alpha<1
, we

e^{jp_{2}}/f^{jn}=(c_{0}^{n}b_{1})^{-j}e^{-j(1-\alpha^{\prime})r^{k}}(1+o(1))
for some

,

. Then we substitute

3.
Further results about linear differential equations

In this section, we use the Phragmén–Lindelöf theorem to prove a theorem on a class of meromorphic functions appearing in complex dynamics, namely
class
N
; see
[
1
,
7
,
8
]
. For a meromorphic function
f
, we say that a point
z_{0}
is a
superattracting
fixed point of
f
if
f(z_{0})=z_{0}
and
f^{\prime}(z_{0})=0
. A meromorphic function
f
in
N
has the following properties:
f
has finitely many poles;
f^{\prime}
has finitely many multiple zeros; the superattracting fixed points of
f
are zeros of
f^{\prime}
and vice versa, with finitely many exceptions;
f
has finite order. Then a meromorphic function is in
N
if and only if
f
satisfies the first-order differential equation:

(3.1)

f^{\prime}(z)=\frac{q_{1}(z)}{q_{2}(z)}e^{q_{3}(z)}(f(z)-z),

for some polynomials
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
and
q_{3}
. By denoting
F(z)=f(z)-z
, then (
3.1
) becomes
F^{\prime}=(q_{1}/q_{2})e^{q_{3}}F-1
. We claim that
q_{3}
must be a constant. More generally, consider the equation

(3.2)

F^{\prime}=R_{1}e^{q(z)}F+R_{2},

where
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are two rational functions and
q(z)
is a polynomial. We prove

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that equation (
3.2
) has a nonconstant meromorphic solution of finite order. Then
q
is a constant.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
..

The proof is a revised version of that in
[
1
]
or in
[
13
]
since we need to deal with the poles of
f
. On the contrary, we suppose that
q(z)
is a polynomial with degree
n\geq 1
. Then from (
3.2
) we see that
F
is transcendental and has order of growth at least
n
. Now we recall the notation in section
1
. Denote the leading coefficient of
q(z)
by
\alpha
and write
\alpha=a+ib
with
a,b
real. Let
\theta_{j}
be such that
\delta(q,\theta_{j})=a\cos n\theta_{j}-b\sin n\theta_{j}=0
. Then
\theta_{j}
divides the plane
\mathbb{C}
into
2n
sectors defined as in (
1.5
). Without loss of generality, we may suppose that in the sectors
S_{j}
, where
j
is odd, we have
\delta(q,\theta)>0
. Moreover, we suppose that all zeros and poles of
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
are contained in the disc
D:=\{z:|z|\leq r_{0}\}
for some
r_{0}>0
. Then on the circle
|z|=r_{0}
,
g(z)
is bounded. Let
\epsilon>0
be given. We also recall the definition
S_{j,\epsilon}
in (
1.6
).

First, we consider
F(z)
in a sector
\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
, where
j
is odd. Let
\Gamma
be a curve not intersecting the corresponding
R
–set of
F
and contained in
\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}\setminus D
. From the proof of Theorem
1.1
we know that
|e^{q(z)}|\geq e^{c|z|^{n}}
uniformly for all
z\in\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
and some
c=c(j,\epsilon)>0
and
|z|
is large. By
[
20
, Proposition 5.12]
we know that
|F^{\prime}/F|\leq r^{n_{1}}
for some integer
n_{1}
outside an
R
-set. We write equation (
3.2
) as

(3.3)

e^{q}=\frac{1}{R_{1}}\frac{F^{\prime}}{F}-\frac{R_{2}}{R_{1}}\frac{1}{F}.

Then, for
z
on the curve
\Gamma
and
|z|=r
,

(3.4)

\left|e^{q(z)}\right|\leq\left|\frac{1}{R_{1}(z)}\right|\left|\frac{F^{\prime}(z)}{F(z)}\right|+|R_{2}(z)|\left|\frac{1}{F(z)}\right|\leq|z|^{n_{1}}+\left|\frac{R_{2}(z)}{R_{1}(z)}\right|\left|\frac{1}{F(z)}\right|.

Therefore, for all
z
on the curve
\Gamma
, we must have that
g(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
since otherwise the above inequality would imply that
e^{cr_{k}^{n}}\leq|r_{k}|^{n_{1}}+O(r_{k}^{m})
as
r_{k}\to\infty
for some infinite sequence
z_{k}=r_{k}e^{i\theta_{k}}
, where
m
is an integer, which is impossible. Since
F(z)
is analytic in the
R
–set corresponding to
F
and outside
D
, we conclude by the maximum modulus principle that
F(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
for all
z\in\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}\setminus D
, where
j
is odd.

Second, we consider
F(z)
in a sector
\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
, where
j
is even. We write
R_{2}=P_{1}/P_{2}+P_{3}
with three polynomials
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and
P_{3}
such that
\deg(P_{1})<\deg(P_{2})
and denote
\deg(P_{3})=n_{2}
. It should be understood that
P_{3}\equiv 0
when
n_{2}=0
. Then along each ray
z=re^{i\theta}
contained in the sector
\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
, we must have
|F^{\prime}(z)|\leq|z|^{n_{2}+1}
for all
z=re^{i\theta}
and
|z|
is large. In fact, if this is not true, then there is an infinite sequence
z_{l}=r_{l}e^{i\theta_{l}}
such that
|F^{\prime}(z_{l})|>|z_{l}|^{n_{2}+1}
as
z_{l}\to\infty
and moreover,

(3.5)

|F(z_{l})|=\left|\int_{z_{0}}^{z_{l}}F^{\prime}(t)dt-F(z_{0})\right|\leq|F^{\prime}(z_{l})||z_{l}-z_{0}|+|F(z_{0})|,

which gives
|F(z_{l})/F^{\prime}(z_{l})|\leq|z_{l}|+O(1)
. But then it follows by dividing
F^{\prime}(z)
on both sides of (
3.2
) that

1=R_{1}e^{q}\frac{F}{F^{\prime}}+R_{2}\frac{1}{F^{\prime}},

and thus by the basic properties of
e^{q(z)}
mentioned in the introduction we have

(3.6)

1\leq\left|R_{1}(z_{l})e^{q(z_{l})}\right|\left|\frac{F(z_{l})}{F^{\prime}(z_{l})}\right|+|R_{2}|\left|\frac{1}{F^{\prime}(z_{l})}\right|=o(1),

a contradiction. Therefore, for all
z\in\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}\setminus D
,
F^{\prime}(z)
is uniformly bounded by
|z|^{n_{2}+1}
when
|z|
is large. An elementary integration again shows that
|F(z)|\leq C|z|^{n_{2}+2}+O(1)
holds uniformly for some
C
and all
z\in\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}\setminus D
.

From the above reasoning, we conclude that for a given
\epsilon>0
and for all
z\in\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}\setminus D
,
j=1,2,\cdots,2n
, and
|z|
is large, we have
|F(z)|\leq|z|^{n_{3}+1}
and
n_{3}=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}+2\}
. Since
F(z)
has only poles in the plane, there is some rational function
R(z)
such that
R(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
and that
h(z):=F(z)-R(z)
is an entire function. Then the previous reasoning yields that
|h(z)|\leq|z|^{n_{3}}
for all
z\in\overline{S}_{j,\epsilon}
,
j=1,\cdots,2n
, and
|z|
is large. Since
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small, then the Phragmén–Lindelöf theorem (see
[
16
, Theorem 7.3]
) implies that
h
is a polynomial and so
F
is a rational function, a contradiction. Therefore,
q
must be a constant.

∎
