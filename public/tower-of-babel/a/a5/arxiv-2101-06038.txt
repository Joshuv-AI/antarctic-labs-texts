Spectral representations of characteristic functions of discrete probability laws
I. A. Alexeev, A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We consider discrete probability laws on the real line, whose characteristic functions are separated from zero. This class includes arbitrary discrete infinitely divisible laws and lattice probability laws, whose characteristic functions have no zeroes on the real line. We show that characteristic functions of such laws admit spectral Lévy–Khinchine type representation with non-monotonic Lévy spectral function. We also apply the representations of such laws to obtain limit and compactness theorems with convergence in variation to probability laws from this class.



Keywords and phrases
: discrete probability laws, characteristic functions, spectral Lévy-Khinchine type representations, quasi-infinitely divisible laws, convergence in variation, relative compactness, stochastic compactness.

1
Introduction

This paper is devoted to the Lévy–Khinchine type representations for characteristic functions of univariate discrete probability laws from rather wide class.

Let
F
be a distribution function of a probability law on the real line
\mathbb{R}
. Recall that
F
is called
infinitely divisible
if for every positive integer
n
there exists a distribution function
F_{1/n}
such that
F=(F_{1/n})^{*n}
, where “
*
” is the convolution, i.e.
F
is
n
-fold convolution power of
F_{1/n}
. It is well known that
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if its characteristic function

\displaystyle f(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

admits the following representation

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\Biggr\{it\gamma_{\tau}-\frac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(e^{itu}-1-\tfrac{it}{\tau}\sin(\tau u)\bigr)d\Lambda(u)\Biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

for any
\tau>0
and some
\gamma_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\geqslant 0
, and function
\Lambda
(the
Lévy spectral function
), which is non-decreasing on every interval
(-\infty,0)
and
(0,+\infty)
, and it satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{u\to-\infty}\Lambda(u)=\lim_{u\to+\infty}\Lambda(u)=0,

(2)

and also

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0<|u|<\delta}x^{2}d\Lambda(u)<+\infty,\quad\delta>0.

Here
\gamma_{\tau}
depend on
\tau
, but
\sigma
and
\Lambda
are not. We use
u\mapsto\tfrac{1}{\tau}\sin(\tau u)
as the “centering function” in the integral in (
1
) following to Zolotarev
[
26
]
and
[
27
]
. This function can be replaced by any bounded Borel function
c:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, which satysfies

c(0)=0
and
(c(x)-x)/x^{2}\to 0
as
x\to 0
(see
[
17
]
).

It is well known that the properties of infinitely divisible
F
(continuity, moments, and support) can be described in terms of its triplet
(\gamma_{\tau},\sigma^{2},\Lambda)
(see
[
24
]
).

It turns out that there are probability laws, whose characteristic functions admit representation of type (
1
) but with non-monotonic spectral function
\Lambda
. The examples of such laws can be found in the classical monographs
[
10
]
,
[
18
]
, and
[
19
]
. The corresponding class was introduced by Lindner and Sato
[
16
]
. Following them, a distribution function
F
is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
if its characteristic function
f
admits the representation (
1
) with some (any)
\tau>0
and some
\gamma_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma\geqslant 0
, and function
\Lambda
, which has finite total variation on every interval
(-\infty,-r]
and
[r,\infty)
,
r>0
, and it satisfies (
2
) and

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0<|u|<\delta}x^{2}d|\Lambda|(u)<+\infty,\quad\delta>0.

Such probability laws find interesting applications in theory of stochastic processes (see
[
16
]
and
[
21
]
), number theory (see
[
20
]
), physics (see
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
), insurance mathematics (see
[
25
]
).

The first detailed analysis of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions on
\mathbb{R}
was performed in
[
17
]
(the multivariate case is considered in the recent papers
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
).
In these works the authors studied questions concerning supports, moments, continuity, and weak convergence. The most complete results were obtained for probability laws on the set of integers
\mathbb{Z}
(with extension for lattice laws). The following important fact was stated in
[
17
]
.

Theorem 1

Let
F
be the distribution function of probability law on
\mathbb{Z}
with characteristic function
f
. Then
F
is quasi-infinitely divisible if and only if
f
does not have zeroes on the real line. In that case,
f
admits the representation

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{k}(e^{itk}-1)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(3)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{k}|<\infty
.

Here we omitted some detailes from the original formulation of this theorem. It is not difficult to check that (
3
) can be written in the form (
1
) (we will do the similar derivation in the next section).

In
[
17
]
the authors also obtained a criterion of weak convergence for sequences of quasi-infinitely divisible laws to a fixed law from this class in terms of parameters of (
3
). These results were complemented by compactness theorems in
[
11
]
. It is known that weak and total variation convergences are equivalent for probability laws on
\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore these results can be considered within the convergence in variation.

In this paper we generalize the above mentioned results. Namely, we state similar facts for rather wide class of discrete probability laws on
\mathbb{R}
, whose characteristic functions are separated from zero. In particular, this class includes arbitrary discrete infinitely divisible laws and lattice probability laws, whose characteristic functions have no zeroes on the real line. The first step in this direction was made in
[
12
]
.

2
Representation theorem

Let us consider an arbitrary discrete distribution function

\displaystyle F(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}}I_{x_{k}}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
x_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
(the set of positive integers), are distinct real numbers,
p_{x_{k}}\geqslant 0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}}=1
. Here and below
I_{c}(x)=1
if
x\geqslant c
and
I_{c}(x)=0
if
x<c
for any fixed
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
X
be the set of all points of growth of
F
, i.e.
X\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\{x_{k}:p_{x_{k}}>0,\,k\in\mathbb{N}\}\neq\varnothing
. So we can write

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{x_{k}\in X}p_{x_{k}}I_{x_{k}}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus
F
corresponds to a discrete probability law concentrated on the countable set
X
of real numbers.

Let
f
be the characteristic function of
F
. Then

\displaystyle f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}}=\sum_{x_{k}\in X}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

So
f
is an almost periodic function with absolutely convergent Fourier series.

We focus on the case, when
f
is separated from zero. Namely, we always assume that

\text{{there exists}\quad}\mu>0\quad\text{{such that}}\quad|f(t)|\geqslant\mu>0\text{\quad{for all}\quad}t\in\mathbb{R}.

[formula]

Condition
(S)
is equivalent to the relation
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
.

Let
\boldsymbol{D}
denote the class of all discrete distribution functions. Let
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
be the subclass of
\boldsymbol{D}
, which consists of distribution functions, whose characteristic functions satisfy condition
(S)
. The class
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
is rather wide. For example, if there exists
x_{k_{0}}
such that
p_{x_{k_{0}}}>1/2
, then
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. This is seen from the following estimate:

\displaystyle|f(t)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\biggl|p_{x_{k_{0}}}e^{itx_{k_{0}}}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}:\,k\neq k_{0}}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}}\biggr|\geqslant p_{x_{k_{0}}}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}:\,k\neq k_{0}}p_{x_{k}}

\displaystyle{}\qquad=p_{x_{k_{0}}}-(1-p_{x_{k_{0}}})=2p_{x_{k_{0}}}-1>0.

Moreover,
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
contains all distribution functions of lattice probability laws, whose characteristic functions have no zeroes on the real line, i.e. if

X\subset\{a+bl:l\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with some
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b>0
and also
f(t)\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Indeed, in this case
|f(\cdot)|
is a continuous
2\pi/b
-periodic function and its infimum is attained at some point in the period segment. Thus to have no zeroes on the real line means that
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
.

It is important to note that all discrete infinitely divisible distribution functions belongs to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Let us show this. It is known (see
[
4
]
) that discrete distribution function
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if its characteristic function

f
admits the following representation

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggr\{it\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{u_{k}}(e^{itu_{k}}-1)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(4)

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda_{u_{k}}\geqslant 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{u_{k}}<\infty
. Therefore we have the estimate

\displaystyle|f(t)|=\exp\biggr\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{u_{k}}(\cos(tu_{k})-1)\biggr\}\geqslant\exp\biggl\{-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{u_{k}}\biggr\}>0,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We see that
f
satisfies
(S)
, i.e.
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
.

There exist examples of characteristic functions of probability laws admitting representation (
4
), where some of
\lambda_{u_{k}}
can be negative within condition
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{u_{k}}|<\infty
(see
[
10
]
pp. 81–83,
[
18
]
p. 165, and
[
19
]
p. 123–124). For such characteristic functions we similarly get the estimate

\displaystyle|f(t)|\geqslant\exp\biggl\{-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{u_{k}}|\biggr\}>0,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus the corresponding distribution functions belong to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. In the result below we show that the converse fact is also true, i.e. the characteristic function of every
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
admits representation of type (
4
) with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda_{u_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{u_{k}}|<\infty
.

Howeever, it is clear that
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}\neq\boldsymbol{D}
. For example, the distribution function
G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\tfrac{1}{2}I_{0}(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}I_{1}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, does not belong to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Indeed, its characteristic function
g(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}e^{it}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, has zeroes in the points
\pi(2k+1)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. There are exist more interesting examples. Namely, let us consider the distribution function
H(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\tfrac{1}{2}I_{0}+p_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}(x)+p_{1}I_{1}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\alpha
is an irrational number from
(0,1)
, the weights
p_{\alpha}
and
p_{1}
are strictly positive, and
p_{\alpha}+p_{1}=1/2
. Its characteristic function
h(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}+p_{\alpha}e^{it\alpha}+p_{1}e^{it}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, does not have zeroes, and it is not separated from zero, i.e.
h(t)\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|h(t)|=0
. Thus we have
H\notin\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. This and other similar particular results were obtained in
[
12
]
.

For sharp formulation of the result below we need to introduce the set of all finite
\mathbb{Z}
–linear combinations of elements from a set
Y\subset\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\langle Y\rangle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\biggl\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}y_{k}:\,c_{k}\in\mathbb{Z},\,y_{k}\in Y,\,m\in\mathbb{N}\biggr\}.

In other words,
\langle Y\rangle
is a module over the ring
\mathbb{Z}
with the generating set
Y
. It easily seen that, in particular,
Y\subset\langle Y\rangle
,
0\in\langle Y\rangle
, and
ky\in\langle Y\rangle
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
y\in Y
. If
Y\neq\{0\}
, then
\langle Y\rangle
is an infinite countable set.

We always set below that sums over the null set equal zero.

Theorem 2

Under the assumption
(S)
, the characteristic function
f
admits the following representation

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggr\{it\gamma+\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}(e^{itu}-1)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(5)

where
\gamma\in\langle X\rangle
,
\lambda_{u}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{u}|<\infty
.

Before the proof of Theorem
2
we will do some remarks.

According to this theorem, if
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
, i.e.
f
satisfies
(S)
, then
f
admits representation (
5
). Let
\mathrm{Arg}\,f(t)
be the argument of
f(t)
(i.e.
f(t)=\exp\{i\mathrm{Arg}\,f(t)\}
) that is uniquely defined for
t\in\mathbb{R}
by continuity with the condition
\mathrm{Arg}\,f(0)=0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Arg}\,f(t)=t\gamma+\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}\sin(tu),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Due to
\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{u}|<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathrm{Arg}\,f(T)}{T}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathrm{Arg}\,f(T)-\mathrm{Arg}\,f(0)}{T-0}.

(6)

Thus
\gamma
is a “mean motion” of the argument of
f
on
[0,\infty)
.

Let us fix arbitrarily
\tau>0
and introduce “mean motion” on the interval
[0,\tau]
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\tau}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dfrac{\mathrm{Arg}\,f(\tau)}{\tau}=\gamma+\tfrac{1}{\tau}\!\!\!\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}\sin(\tau u).

(7)

Then we write

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma_{\tau}+\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}(e^{itu}-1-i\tfrac{t}{\tau}\sin(\tau u))\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us define

\displaystyle\Lambda(u)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}\leqslant u}\lambda_{u_{k}},\quad u<0,\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda(u)=-\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{N}:u_{k}>u}\lambda_{u_{k}},\quad u>0.

Hence we have

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\Biggr\{it\gamma_{\tau}+\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(e^{itu}-1-\tfrac{it}{\tau}\sin(\tau u)\bigr)d\Lambda(u)\Biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

So we proved the following fact.

Remark 1

If
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
, then it is quasi-infinitely divisible with the triplet
(\gamma_{\tau},0,\Lambda)
.

It is easily seen that the function
\Lambda
satisfies all the required conditions.

Note that the centering by
u\mapsto\tfrac{1}{\tau}\sin(\tau u)
in the integral has the advantage that the shift parameter
\gamma_{\tau}
of the triplet gets additional meaning according to formulas (
6
) and (
7
).

For fixed
\tau>0
the parameter
\gamma_{\tau}
and the spectral function
\Lambda
are uniquely determined from
f
(see
[
10
]
, p. 80). Therefore
\gamma
and the sequence
\lambda_{u}
,
u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}
, are also uniquely determined in (
5
).

According to Remark
1
and comments above concerning lattice probability laws, Theorem
2
is a generalization of Theorem
1
.

It is very interesting to know whether or not every discrete quasi-infinitely divisible distribution function belongs to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. It is known that characteristic functions of quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions have no zeroes on the real line (see
[
17
]
). So the problem is reduced to the following question. Are there exist discrete quasi-infinitely divisible distribution functions with characteristic functions that have no zeroes on the real line and that are not separated from zero? There are only particular results. For example, the above mentioned distribution function
H
is not quasi-infinitely divisible that was shown in
[
12
]
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.
The proof is devided into two steps: for the case of finite
X
and for the general case.

1. Suppose that
X
is finite. Without loss of generality we can assume that
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Hence
f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The case
n=1
and
x_{1}=0
is trivial:
\langle X\rangle=\{0\}
,
\gamma=0
. We next exclude this case. Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}\in X
be a basis in
X\neq\{0\}
over
\mathbb{Z}
, i.e. for every
k=1,\ldots,n
there is the unique representation
x_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}c_{k,j}\alpha_{j}
with
c_{k,j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Here the numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Z}
, that is the equation
l_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+l_{1}\alpha_{d}=0
with
l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
holds if and only if
l_{1}=l_{2}=\ldots=l_{d}=0
. It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\langle X\rangle=\biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}l_{j}\alpha_{j}:\,l_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\biggr\}.

(8)

Using the representations for
x_{k}
, we consider the function

\displaystyle\varphi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}\exp\biggl\{i\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}c_{k,j}\alpha_{j}t_{j}\biggr\},\quad t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}.

The function
\varphi
is a continuous periodic function with periods
2\pi/\alpha_{1},\ldots,2\pi/\alpha_{d}
over variables
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}
, correspondingly. The function
f
is the diagonal function for
\varphi
:

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\ldots,t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}\exp\biggl\{it\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}c_{k,j}\alpha_{j}\biggr\}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}}=f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore the set of values
\{f(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is everywhere dense in the set
\{\varphi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}):t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}\}
(see
[
15
]
, Theorem 5, p. 46). Hence, by the assumption
(S)
, we have

\displaystyle|\varphi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})|\geqslant\mu>0,\quad t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}.

(9)

We now consider the following function

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\varphi(t_{1}/\alpha_{1},\ldots,t_{d}/\alpha_{d})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}\exp\biggl\{i\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}c_{k,j}t_{j}\biggr\},\quad t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}.

It is the characteristic function for the probabilty law on the vectors
(c_{k,1},\ldots,c_{k,d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with weights
p_{x_{k}}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
. Due to (
9
), we have
|\tilde{\varphi}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})|\geqslant\mu>0
,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
. By Theorem 3.2 in
[
2
]
, the function
\tilde{\varphi}
admits the following representation for any
t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\biggl\{i(t_{1}\gamma_{1}+\ldots+t_{d}\gamma_{d})

\displaystyle{}\qquad+\!\!\!\sum_{(l_{1},\ldots,l_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\!\!\!\lambda_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}}\Bigr(\exp\bigl\{i(t_{1}l_{1}+\ldots+t_{d}l_{d})\bigr\}-1\Bigr)\biggr\},

where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\lambda_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}
for
(l_{1},\ldots,l_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{(l_{1},\ldots,l_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}}|<\infty
.

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(t\alpha_{1},\ldots,t\alpha_{d})=\varphi(t,\ldots,t)=f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Then for
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it(\gamma_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\gamma_{d}\alpha_{d})+\!\!\!\sum_{(l_{1},\ldots,l_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\!\!\!\lambda_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}}\Bigr(\exp\bigl\{it(l_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+l_{d}\alpha_{d})\bigr\}-1\Bigr)\biggr\}.

Due to (
8
), this representation we can write in the form (
5
), where
\gamma\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\gamma_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\gamma_{d}\alpha_{d}\in\langle X\rangle
, and
\lambda_{u}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lambda_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{d}}
for
u=l_{1}\alpha_{1}+\ldots+l_{d}\alpha_{d}\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}
with
(l_{1},\ldots,l_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
.

2. We now turn to the general case for
X
. So
f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
X\neq\varnothing
, without loss of generality we can suppose that
x_{1}\in X
, i.e.
p_{x_{1}}>0
. We will uniformly approximate
f
by the following characteristic functions:

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{n,x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where

\displaystyle p_{n,x_{k}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\dfrac{p_{x_{k}}}{\sum_{m=1}^{n}p_{x_{m}}},\quad k=1,\ldots,n,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Here
\sum_{m=1}^{n}p_{x_{m}}\geqslant p_{x_{1}}>0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us estimate the approximation error for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\sum_{k=1}^{n}(p_{x_{k}}-p_{n,x_{k}})e^{itx_{k}}+\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}}e^{itx_{k}}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}|p_{x_{k}}-p_{n,x_{k}}|+\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}},

where, due to the equality
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}p_{x_{m}}=1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}|p_{x_{k}}-p_{n,x_{k}}|=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}\biggl(\dfrac{1}{\sum_{m=1}^{n}p_{x_{m}}}-1\biggr)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{x_{k}}\biggl(\dfrac{\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}p_{x_{m}}}{\sum_{m=1}^{n}p_{x_{m}}}\biggr)=\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}p_{x_{m}}.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|\leqslant 2\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}p_{x_{k}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

From this we conclude that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

Let us take
\mu>0
from
(S)
, and fix arbitrarily
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
. Then there exists
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|\leqslant\varepsilon\mu,\quad n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}.

(10)

From this we see that
f_{n}(t)\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, for every
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle|f_{n}(t)|\geqslant|f(t)|-|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|\geqslant\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|-\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|,

and, due to
(S)
and (
10
), we have

\displaystyle|f_{n}(t)|\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\mu>0,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(11)

Choose
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
and represent
f(t)=f_{n}(t)\cdot R_{n}(t)
with
R_{n}(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}f(t)/f_{n}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
f
,
f_{n}
,
R_{n}
are continuous functions without zeroes and they equal
1
at
t=0
, we can proceed to
the distinguished logarithms
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)=\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{n}(t)+\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(12)

which are uniquely defined by continuity with the conditions that they equal
0
at
t=0
(see
[
13
]
p. 14–16 or
[
22
]
p. 2). We next consider
\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{n}
and
\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}
separately.

By the part 1., for the function
f_{n}
we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma_{n}+\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}(e^{itu}-1)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where
X_{n}=\bigl\{x_{k}:p_{x_{k}}>0,\,k=1,\ldots,n\bigr\}
,
\gamma_{n}\in\langle X_{n}\rangle
,
\lambda_{n,u}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n,u}|<\infty
. Since the function in the exponent is continuous and it equals
0
at
t=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{n}(t)=it\gamma_{n}+\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}(e^{itu}-1),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Setting
\lambda_{n,0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}
, we represent
f_{n}
in the following form

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{n}(t)=it\gamma_{n}+\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that
X_{n}\subset X
, and hence
\langle X_{n}\rangle\subset\langle X\rangle
. Then we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{n}(t)=it\gamma_{n}+\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(13)

where for every
u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\langle X_{n}\rangle
we define
\lambda_{n,u}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
if
\langle X\rangle\setminus\langle X_{n}\rangle\neq\varnothing
.

Next, we consider the function
\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)=\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{f(t)-f_{n}(t)}{f_{n}(t)}\biggr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(14)

(the latter is the principal value of the logarithm) because due to (
10
) and (
11
)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\dfrac{f(t)-f_{n}(t)}{f_{n}(t)}\biggr|\leqslant\dfrac{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)-f_{n}(t)|}{\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)|}\leqslant\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}<1,

(15)

and the function in the right-hand side of (
14
) is continuous and equals
0
at
t=0
.
Using this, we get the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m}\biggl(\dfrac{f(t)-f_{n}(t)}{f_{n}(t)}\biggr)^{m},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It yields the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)|\leqslant\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{m}\,\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\dfrac{f(t)-f_{n}(t)}{f_{n}(t)}\biggr|^{m}\leqslant\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{m}\biggl(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\biggr)^{m}.

Since
\varepsilon\in(0,1/4)
, here we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{m}\biggl(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\biggr)^{m}\leqslant\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\biggl(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\biggr)^{m}=\dfrac{\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}}{1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}}=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}<2\varepsilon.

Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)|<2\varepsilon.

(16)

Let us consider the function
(f-f_{n})/f_{n}
from (
14
). It is clear that
f-f_{n}
is an almost periodic function with absolutely convergent Fourier series. Due to (
11
), the function
1/f_{n}
is also almost periodic (see
[
5
]
p. 35 or
[
14
]
p. 21), and it has absolutely convergent Fourier series (see
[
9
]
p. 175). These facts can be also shown using (
13
). Therefore the function
(f-f_{n})/f_{n}
is almost periodic with absolutely convergent Fourier series.

Let us return to (
14
). Since the function
z\mapsto\ln(1+z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
(the set of complex numbers), is analytic on the unit disk, due to (
15
) and the above remark, we get that
\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}
is almost periodic with absolute convergent Fourier series (see
[
9
]
p. 175):

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)=\sum\limits_{u\in\Delta_{n}}\beta_{n,u}e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(17)

where
\Delta_{n}
is a countable set of real numbers (and, probably,
\Delta_{n}\neq\langle X\rangle
),
\beta_{n,u}\in\mathbb{C}
for
u\in\Delta_{n}
, and
\sum_{u\in\Delta_{n}}|\beta_{n,u}|<\infty
. Without loss of generality we can assume that either
\Delta_{n}=\varnothing
(the case
f=f_{n}
) and the sum equals zero in (
17
), or
\Delta_{n}\neq\varnothing
and
\beta_{n,u}\neq 0
,
u\in\Delta_{n}
.

We now return to the function
\mathrm{Ln}\,f
and (
12
). The formulas (
13
) and (
17
) yield

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)=it\gamma_{n}+\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}e^{itu}+\sum\limits_{u\in\Delta_{n}}\beta_{n,u}e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that this is valid for every
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}|\lambda_{n,u}|<\infty
and
\sum_{u\in\Delta_{n}}|\beta_{n,u}|<\infty
,
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
, it is true that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathrm{Ln}\,f(T)}{iT}=\gamma_{n},\quad n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}.

Consequently,
\gamma_{n}
are equal for
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
, and we denote their value by
\gamma
. Since
\gamma_{n}\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\subset\langle X\rangle
, we have
\gamma\in\langle X\rangle
.

We next consider the function

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)-it\gamma=\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}e^{itu}+\sum\limits_{u\in\Delta_{n}}\beta_{n,u}e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}.

(18)

It is an almost periodic function with absolutely convergent Fourier series. We define
\Delta=\cup_{n=n_{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}\Delta_{n}
, and we introduce the Fourier coefficients

\displaystyle\lambda_{v}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}\bigl(\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)-it\gamma\bigr)e^{-itv}dt,\quad v\in\langle X\rangle\cup\Delta.

(19)

According to (
17
) and (
18
), for any
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{v}=\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}\biggl(\,\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}e^{itu}\biggr)e^{-itv}dt+\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)e^{-itv}dt,\quad v\in\langle X\rangle\cup\Delta.

Due to (
16
), for any
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\Biggl|\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)e^{-itv}dt\Biggr|\leqslant\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}|\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)|dt\leqslant\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathrm{Ln}\,R_{n}(t)|\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
can be choosen arbitrarily small, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{v}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}\biggl(\,\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}e^{itu}\biggr)e^{-itv}dt,\quad v\in\langle X\rangle\cup\Delta.

The series in the integral is absolutely convergent. Hence

\displaystyle\lambda_{v}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{n,u}\biggl(\,\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}e^{itu}e^{-itv}dt\biggr),\quad v\in\langle X\rangle\cup\Delta,

(20)

where

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{2T}\int\limits_{-T}^{T}e^{itu}e^{-itv}dt=\begin{cases}1,&u=v,\\
0,&u\neq v.\end{cases}

(21)

Suppose that
\Delta\setminus\langle X\rangle\neq\varnothing
, and
v\in\Delta\setminus\langle X\rangle
. Then, on the one hand, from (
20
) and (
21
) we have
\lambda_{v}=0
. On the other hand,
v\in\Delta_{n}\setminus\langle X\rangle
for some
n
. So from (
18
), (
19
), and (
21
) we have
\lambda_{v}=\beta_{n,v}
for such
n
, where
\beta_{n,v}\neq 0
according to the convention above. Thus we obtain a contradiction. Hence
\Delta\subset\langle X\rangle
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)-it\gamma=\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}(\lambda_{n,u}+\beta_{n,u})e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon},

where
\beta_{n,u}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
,
u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\Delta_{n}
. So
\lambda_{u}=\lambda_{n,u}+\beta_{n,u}
,
u\in\langle X\rangle
,
n\geqslant n_{\varepsilon}
, and, from (
20
) and (
21
), we have
\lambda_{u}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n,u}
,
u\in\langle X\rangle
. Since
\lambda_{n,u}\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude that
\lambda_{u}\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)-it\gamma=\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}\lambda_{u}e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

with
\lambda_{u}\in\mathbb{R}
for
u\in\langle X\rangle
, and
\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle}|\lambda_{u}|<\infty
.
Since
\bigl(\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t)-it\gamma\bigr)\bigr|_{t=0}=0
, we get
\lambda_{0}=-\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}
. From this we obtain (
5
).
\Box

3
Limit and compactness theorems

In this section we will apply the representations (
5
) to obtain
limit and compactness theorems with convergence in variation to distribution functions from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
.

Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of distribution functions and let
F
be a distribution function. Recall that, by definition, the sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converges in variation
to
F
if
\|F_{n}-F\|\to 0
,
n\to\infty
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the total variation on
\mathbb{R}
. We denote this as
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
.
Here we are interested in the case, when
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. So it is naturally to consider the following notions of compactness with class
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. We say that
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation
if every its subsequence contains a further subsequence converging in variation to a distribution function from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. We say that
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastically compact in variation
if every its subsequence contains a further subsequence converging in variation to a nondegenerate distribution function from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. In particular, if a sequence of distribution functions converges in variation to some distribution function from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
, then the sequence is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation. If we know that the limit is nondegenerate, then the sequence is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastically compact in variation. The introduced notions of compactness are modified versions of well known relative (weak) compactness (see
[
3
]
) and stochastic compactness (introduced by Feller in
[
8
]
), which are based on weak convergence.

It should be noted that the class
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
is not closed under convergence in variation. Indeed, it is easily seen that the sequence

\displaystyle G_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2+n}\bigr)\,I_{0}(x)+\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2+n}\bigr)\,I_{1}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

converges in variation to the law

\displaystyle G(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\tfrac{1}{2}\,I_{0}(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,I_{1}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

However,
G_{n}\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, but
G\notin\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
(see the examples from Section 2). Here we also see that
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively (and hence
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastically) compact in variation.

We begin with the following observation.

Theorem 3

Suppose that
F_{n}\in\boldsymbol{D}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation, then
F_{n}\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Due to this theorem, the conditions of
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relative compactness of
F_{n}\in\boldsymbol{D}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, can be formulated in terms of parameters from existing representations of type (
5
) for characteristic functions of
F_{n}
with all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the theorem and this remark are valid for the sequences from
\boldsymbol{D}
converging in variation to some
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
.

We fix
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Let
f
be its characteristic function with representation (
5
). Without loss of generality we suppose that the whole sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Let
X_{n}
be the set of all points of growth of
F_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f_{n}
be the characteristic function of
F_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. According to Theorem
2
, the functions
f_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, admit representation of type (
5
). We set

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma_{n}+\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}(e^{itu}-1)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\gamma_{n}\in\langle X_{n}\rangle
,
\lambda_{n,u}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}
, and
\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n,u}|<\infty
.

We adopt the convention:

\displaystyle\lambda_{u}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0,\quad u\notin\langle X\rangle,\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{n,u}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0,\quad u\notin\langle X_{n}\rangle,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(22)

We propose the following criterion of convergence in variation.

Theorem 4

F_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
,
n\to\infty
, if and only if the following couple of conditions holds:

(i)

there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\gamma_{n}=\gamma
,
n\geqslant n_{0}
;

(ii)

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u}|\to 0
,
n\to\infty
.

This criterion generalizes the corresponding result from
[
17
]
for probability laws on
\mathbb{Z}
.

We next turn to compactness theorems. We need to introduce the following set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle X_{n}\rangle=\{u_{0},u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\}.

Since always
0\in\langle X_{n}\rangle
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
u_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}0
.

Theorem 5

(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation if and only if the following ensemble of conditions holds:

(i)

\gamma_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, have finite number of values from
\mathcal{U}
;

(ii)

\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum\limits_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n,u}|<\infty
;

(iii)

\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{k}\in\,\mathcal{U}\setminus\{0\}:\\
k>N\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n,u_{k}}|=0
.

Theorem 6

(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastically compact in variation if and only if it is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation, and

\displaystyle\varliminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\sum\limits_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n,u}|>0.

(23)

Theorems
5
and
6
generalize the corresponding results from
[
11
]
concerning compactness for probability laws on
\mathbb{Z}
.

We now turn to the proofs of proposed theorems.

Proof of Theorem
3
.
Suppose, contrary to our claim, that there exists a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that every
F_{n_{j}}
does not belong to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Let us choose from
(F_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a subsequence
(F_{n_{j}^{\prime}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges in variation to some distribution function
F
from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Let
f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}
be the characteristic function of
F_{n_{j}^{\prime}}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and
f
be the characteristic function of
F
. Then there exists
\mu>0
such that
|f(t)|\geqslant\mu>0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Due to the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(t)-f(t)|=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\,\biggl|\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}d(F_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(x)-F(x))\biggr|\leqslant\|F_{n_{j}^{\prime}}-F\|,\quad j\in\mathbb{N},

(24)

we obtain
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(t)-f(t)|\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Since

\displaystyle|f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(t)|\geqslant|f(t)|-|f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(t)-f(t)|\geqslant\mu-\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(s)-f(s)|,\quad j\in\mathbb{N},

it is true that
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n_{j}^{\prime}}(t)|\geqslant\mu/2>0
for all sufficiently large
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence
F_{n_{j}^{\prime}}\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
for these
j
, that is a contradiction.
\Box

Proof of Theorem
4
.
We will adopt here the following convention. Let
G
be a function of bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}
with characteristic function
g
, i.e.
g(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}dG(x)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since the uniqueness theorem is valid for functions of bounded variation, we set
\|g\|\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\|G\|
, where the latter is the total variation of
G
on
\mathbb{R}
. So
\|g\|=0
if and only if
g(t)=0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. It is easy to check that
\|c\cdot g\|=|c|\cdot\|g\|
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be functions of bounded variation with characteristic functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, correspondingly. From the inequalities
\|G_{1}+G_{2}\|\leqslant\|G_{1}\|+\|G_{2}\|
and
\|G_{1}*G_{2}\|\leqslant\|G_{1}\|\cdot\|G_{2}\|
, we correspondingly have
\|g_{1}+g_{2}\|\leqslant\|g_{1}\|+\|g_{2}\|
and
\|g_{1}\cdot g_{2}\|\leqslant\|g_{1}\|\cdot\|g_{2}\|
. Moreover, such normed space of functions
g
will be complete (see
[
9
]
p. 165).

Sufficiency
. Suppose that
(i)
and
(ii)
hold. We define

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathrm{Ln}\,f_{n}(t)-\mathrm{Ln}\,f(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(25)

For
n\geqslant n_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}(e^{itu}-1)-\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}(e^{itu}-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}(\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u})(e^{itu}-1),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Define

\displaystyle\lambda_{n,0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}-\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}.

(26)

So we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t)=\sum\limits_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle}(\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u})e^{itu},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\geqslant n_{0}.

(27)

Therefore

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{n}\|=\sum\limits_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle}|\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u}|,\quad n\geqslant n_{0}.

(28)

According to (
26
), observe that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle|\lambda_{n,0}-\lambda_{0}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Biggl|\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}-\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}\Biggr|

\displaystyle{}=\Biggl|\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}(\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u})\Biggr|\leqslant\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u}|.

Then

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{n}\|=|\lambda_{n,0}-\lambda_{0}|+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u}|\leqslant 2\cdot\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u}|,\quad n\geqslant n_{0}.

Due to
(ii)
, we have
\|\Delta_{n}\|\to 0
,
n\to\infty
.

We next write

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)-f(t)=f(t)\bigl(e^{\Delta_{n}(t)}-1\bigr)=f(t)\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Delta_{n}(t)^{k}}{k!},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\geqslant n_{0}.

Hence

\displaystyle\|F_{n}-F\|=\|f_{n}-f\|=\biggl\|f\cdot\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Delta_{n}^{k}}{k!}\biggr\|\leqslant\|f\|\cdot\biggl\|\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Delta_{n}^{k}}{k!}\biggr\|,\quad n\geqslant n_{0}.

Here
\|f\|=1
and

\displaystyle\biggl\|\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Delta_{n}^{k}}{k!}\biggr\|\leqslant\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\|\Delta_{n}\|^{k}}{k!}=e^{\|\Delta_{n}\|}-1,\quad n\geqslant n_{0}.

Thus
\|F_{n}-F\|\leqslant e^{\|\Delta_{n}\|}-1
,
n\geqslant n_{0}
. Since
\|\Delta_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we obtain
\|F_{n}-F\|\to 0
, i.e.
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
.

Necessity
. Suppose that
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
,
n\to\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\|f_{n}-f\|=\|F_{n}-F\|\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

(29)

Due to the estimate
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)-f(t)|\leqslant\|F_{n}-F\|
(as in (
24
)),
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)-f(t)|\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

(30)

We now consider the functions
\Delta_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, defined by (
25
):

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t)=it(\gamma_{n}-\gamma)+\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{n,u}(e^{itu}-1)-\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\lambda_{u}(e^{itu}-1),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the function
\Delta_{n}
is continuous and
\Delta_{n}(0)=0
, it is the distinguished logarithm of the function
f_{n}/f
. Moreover, for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t)=\ln\bigl(f_{n}(t)/f(t)\bigr)=\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{f_{n}(t)-f(t)}{f(t)}\biggr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(31)

Indeed, by assumption,
f\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
, i.e.
f
satisties
(S)
with some
\mu>0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\dfrac{f_{n}(t)-f(t)}{f(t)}\biggr|\leqslant\dfrac{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)-f(t)|}{\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|}\leqslant\dfrac{1}{\mu}\,\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)-f(t)|,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Due to (
30
), the first supremum goes to zero. In particular, there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\dfrac{f_{n}(t)-f(t)}{f(t)}\biggr|<1,\quad n\geqslant n_{1}.

(32)

Consequently, for
n\geqslant n_{1}
the function in the right-hand side of (
31
) is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, and it equals zero for
t=0
. By uniqueness, this function is the distinguished logarithm of
f_{n}/f
, i.e. (
31
) is valid.

We first check
(i)
. According to the above remark, from representation (
25
), on the one hand, we see that
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\Delta_{n}(t)|\to 0
,
n\to\infty
. On the other hand, we have the lower estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\Delta_{n}(t)|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|t(\gamma_{n}-\gamma)|-\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\bigl|\lambda_{n,u}(e^{itu}-1)\bigr|-\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}\bigl|\lambda_{u}(e^{itu}-1)\bigr|

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|t(\gamma_{n}-\gamma)|-2\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n,u}|-2\sum_{u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{u}|,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence if there exists a strongly increasing sequence of numbers
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\gamma_{n_{k}}-\gamma\neq 0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|t(\gamma_{n_{k}}-\gamma)|=|\gamma_{n_{k}}-\gamma|\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|t|=\infty
and, consequently,
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\Delta_{n_{k}}(t)|=\infty
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In this case we have a contradiction. Therefore
\gamma_{n}=\gamma
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
except a finite number, i.e.
(i)
is valid with some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
.

We now check
(ii)
. Let us fix positive integer
n\geqslant\max\{n_{0},n_{1}\}
.
Then (
27
) and (
28
) are valid for
\Delta_{n}
, where we use (
26
). Also, due to (
31
) and (
32
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}(t)=\ln\biggl(1+\dfrac{f_{n}(t)-f(t)}{f(t)}\biggr)=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m}\biggl(\dfrac{f_{n}(t)-f(t)}{f(t)}\biggr)^{m},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us consider the function
(f_{n}-f)/f
. It is evident that
f_{n}-f
is almost periodic function with absolutely convergent Fourier series. Since
f
satisfies
(S)
the function
1/f
is also almost periodic (see
[
5
]
p. 35 or
[
14
]
p. 21) and it has absolutely convergent Fourier series (see
[
9
]
p. 175). Therefore the function
(f_{n}-f)/f
is almost periodic with absolutely convergent Fourier series. For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
the same is true for the function
((f_{n}-f)/f)^{m}
, and

\displaystyle\biggl\|\biggl(\dfrac{f_{n}-f}{f}\biggr)^{m}\biggr\|\leqslant\biggl\|\dfrac{f_{n}-f}{f}\biggr\|^{m}\leqslant\bigl(\|f_{n}-f\|\cdot\|1/f\|\bigr)^{m}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{n}\|=\biggl\|\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-1)^{m-1}}{m}\biggl(\dfrac{f_{n}-f}{f}\biggr)^{m}\biggr\|\leqslant\sum\limits_{m=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{m}\cdot\bigl(\|f_{n}-f\|\cdot\|1/f\|\bigr)^{m}.

From this and (
29
) we see that
\|\Delta_{n}\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{n}\|=\sum\limits_{u\in\langle X_{n}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle}|\lambda_{n,u}-\lambda_{u}|\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

that yields
(ii)
.
\Box

Proof of Theorem
5
.
Due to the convention (
22
), we rewrite
(ii)
in the following form

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n,u_{k}}|<\infty.

This and
(iii)
together are equivalent to the relative compactness in
\ell^{1}
of the sequence
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
V_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}(\lambda_{n,u_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. that
every subsequence of
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contains a further subsequence
(V_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n_{j},u_{k}}-\lambda_{u_{k}}|\to 0
as
j\to\infty
, where
(\lambda_{u_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is some sequence of real numbers with
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{u_{k}}|<\infty
(see
[
23
]
, p. 271 and p. 278).

Suppose that
(i)
holds and
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is relatively compact in
\ell^{1}
. We show that
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation. Let us choose any subsequence
(F_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. We first choose from
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a subsequence
(n_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
(V_{n^{\prime}_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in
\ell^{1}
to some sequence
V=(\lambda_{u_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Next, since
\gamma_{n_{j}^{\prime}}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, have the finite number of values from
\mathcal{U}
, we can choose from
(n_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a subsequence
(n_{j}^{\prime\prime})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\gamma_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}}
is constant for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. there exists
\gamma\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\gamma_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}}=\gamma
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
. It is easily seen that
f_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}}(t)\to f(t)
,
j\to\infty
, where

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{u_{k}}(e^{itu_{k}}-1)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

This function is continuous at
t=0
. By the continuity theorem,
f
is the characteristic function for some distribution function
F
. It is discrete (because there is the absolutely convergent series in the exponent) and its characteristic function
f
satisfies
(S)
(see Section 2). So
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
and, by Theorem
4
,
F_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
,
j\to\infty
.
Thus we chosen from the arbitrary subsequence
(F_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
the further subsequence
(F_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, which converges in variation to a distribution function from
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. This yields
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relative compactness in variation for
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

We now suppose that
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation. We show that
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is relatively compact in
\ell^{1}
. Let us choose any subsequence
(V_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. We next choose from
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a subsequence
(n_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
F_{n_{j}^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
,
j\to\infty
, where
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
.
Let
f
be its characteristic function with representation (
5
). According to Theorem
4
, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n_{j}^{\prime}}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n_{j}^{\prime},u}-\lambda_{u}|\to 0,\quad j\to\infty.

Due to the convention (
22
), we can write this in the following form

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}\cup\langle X\rangle,\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n_{j}^{\prime},u}-\lambda_{u}|\to 0,\quad j\to\infty.

It is clear that if
\langle X\rangle\setminus\mathcal{U}\neq\varnothing
then
\lambda_{u,n_{j}^{\prime}}=0
, and hence
\lambda_{u}=0
for all
u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\mathcal{U}
. Therefore we have
\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n_{j}^{\prime},u}-\lambda_{u}|=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|\lambda_{n_{j}^{\prime},u_{k}}-\lambda_{u_{k}}|\to 0
,
j\to\infty
. This proves the relative compactness
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\ell^{1}
.

We now show that
(i)
holds. Suppose, contrary to our claim, that there exists subsequence
(\gamma_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with distinct values. We choose from
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a subsequence
(n_{j}^{\prime\prime})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
F_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
,
j\to\infty
with some
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. According to Theorem
4
,
\gamma_{n_{j}^{\prime\prime}}
is constant for all sufficiently large
j\in\mathbb{N}
, a contradiction.
\Box

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Sufficiency
. Let us choose any subsequence
(F_{n_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation, we can choose futher subsequence
(F_{n_{j}^{\prime}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, which converges in variation to some distribution function
F\in\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
. Let
f
be its characteristic function with representation (
5
). If
F
is degenerate, then
f(t)=e^{it\gamma}
, i.e.
\lambda_{u}=0
,
u\in\langle X\rangle\setminus\{0\}
. By Theorem
4
, we have that
\gamma_{n_{j}^{\prime}}=\gamma
for all sufficiently large
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\langle X_{n_{j}^{\prime}}\rangle\cup\langle X\rangle\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\lambda_{n_{j}^{\prime},u}|\to 0,\quad j\to\infty.

This contradicts (
23
). Hence
F
is always nondegenerate distribution function. Thus we have
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastic compactness in variation of
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Necessity
. Suppose that
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastically compact in variation. Then, by definition, it is
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-relatively compact in variation. Suppose that (
23
) is not true, i.e. there exists a sequence
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\sum_{u\in\langle X_{n_{j}}\rangle\setminus\{0\}}|\lambda_{n_{j},u}|\to 0
,
j\to\infty
. By Theorem
5
,
\gamma_{n_{j}}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, have finite number of values. Let us choose from
(n_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
a subsequence
(n_{j}^{\prime})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\gamma_{n_{j}^{\prime}}=\gamma
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, according to Theorem
4
,
F_{n_{j}^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{var}}F
,
j\to\infty
, where
F
is degenerate distribution function with unique growth point
\gamma
. This contradicts to
\boldsymbol{D}_{\!S}
-stochastic compactness in variation for
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
\Box
