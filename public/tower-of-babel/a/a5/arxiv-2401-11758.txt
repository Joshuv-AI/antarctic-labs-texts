Qubit fidelity under stochastic Schrödinger equations driven by colored noise
R.J.P.T. de Keijzer, L.Y. Visser, O. Tse, S.J.J.M.F. Kokkelmans

Abstract
Environmental noise on a controlled quantum system is generally modeled by a dissipative Lindblad equation. This equation describes the average state of the system via the density matrix ρ . \rho. One way of deriving this Lindblad equation is by introducing a stochastic operator evolving under white noise in the Schrödinger equation. However, white noise, where all noise frequencies contribute equally in the power spectral density, is not a realistic noise profile as lower frequencies generally dominate the spectrum. Furthermore, the Lindblad equation does not fully describe the system as a density matrix ρ \rho does not uniquely describe a probabilistic ensemble of pure states { ψ j } j \{\psi_{j}\}_{j} . In this work, we introduce a method for solving for the full distribution of qubit fidelity driven by important stochastic Schrödinger equation cases, where qubits evolve under more realistic noise profiles, e.g. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck noise . This allows for predictions of the mean, variance, and higher-order moments of the fidelities of these qubits, which can be of value when deciding on the allowed noise levels for future quantum computing systems, e.g. deciding what quality of control systems to procure. Furthermore, these methods will prove to be integral in the optimal control of qubit states under (classical) control system noise.



I
Introduction

For quantum computers to become important tools in highly complex simulation/optimization problems, they should be able to perform many successive operations faithfully, i.e. be able to manipulate the qubits with high fidelity. In the current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era
Preskill 2018
, quantum computers suffer from noise stemming from both uncontrollable sources such as radiative decay, and from sources in control systems, such as frequency and intensity noises
Day et al. 2022
;
Nakav et al. 2023
;
Yan et al. 2015
. One way of addressing the latter is by designing control operations that are robust against these types of noise
Mohan et al. 2023
;
Jandura et al. 2023
. Alternatively, these controllable noise sources can be diminished by increasing the quality of the systems, which often comes at both monetary and technological complexity costs. It is therefore important to know in what way noise is determinantal to the fidelity of the qubit operations. A model that predicts fidelities based on noise spectra can be used to ensure that a system meets certain criteria for qubit fidelities, before being acquired. Furthermore, such a model can be used to
control
qubit systems
de Keijzer et al. 2023
;
Meitei et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2022
, where the noise affecting each state preparation is accounted for.

Commonly, noisy quantum systems can be regarded as open quantum systems, and are often modeled by the Lindblad master equation
Brasil et al. 2013
. These equations describe the effective Markovian behavior of a quantum state and can be derived by various approaches
Manzano 2020
(cf. Sec.
II
for an example). However, Markovianity is in general an unrealistic assumption for a quantum system subjected to control noise, where lower frequencies often dominate the power spectral density (PSD)
Welch 1967
. Moreover, a density matrix does not completely characterize the state of the system, as two ensembles of states
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j}
might share a common density matrix
\rho
. For example, consider two ensembles
\{\{\psi_{1}=|0\rangle,\ p_{1}=1/2\},\{\psi_{2}=|1\rangle,\ p_{2}=1/2\}\}
and
\{\{\psi_{1}=|+\rangle,\ p_{1}=1/2\},\{\psi_{2}=|-\rangle,\ p_{2}=1/2\}\}
(where
|+\rangle
and
|-\rangle
are the eigenstates of the Pauli
X
operator, then these would be described by the same density matrix
\rho
but would behave differently in subsequent evolution. Therefore, one might not only be interested in the effective (or average) state of the system but also in the variances of the evolution, which indicate the variety of states being prepared.

. The SSE, which implies the Lindblad equation for white noise, can be used to treat colored noise and to find a distribution of fidelities. Conversely, the Lindblad equation method is only valid for white noise and results in an average fidelity rather than a full distribution.

Because of these shortcomings in the Lindblad equation, we instead consider the
stochastic Schrödinger equation
(SSE) as a starting point for our analysis, allowing us to model the evolution of a quantum state under more general noise profiles. Using this model, we derive stochastic ODE systems for the qubit fidelity distribution
{\sf F}
of qubit states under (classical) control system noise that we verify numerically with Monte-Carlo methods. These ODE systems are substantially simpler and less computationally demanding than their Monte Carlo counterparts.

In line with this work, other studies have been performed to understand the influence of noise on qubit fidelity
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Day et al. 2022
;
Green et al. 2013
, where the Lindblad equation is considered primarily in the small noise regime, without providing the full qubit fidelity distribution. Similarly, past works on the SSE for quantum computing
Carballeira et al. 2021
;
Semin 2020
;
Kobayashi and Yamamoto 2019
;
Müller et al. 2022
;
Li 2021
only provide lower bounds or approximations on the achievable fidelities of qubit state preparations. As only white noise and mean fidelities are considered, these calculations could also have been performed using the Lindblad equation. To go beyond the Lindblad equation, these SSE methods are expanded in this work to obtain exact results for more realistic noise acting on both single and multiple qubits. For this, we extend the colored noise methods for the SSE developed in
Semina et al. 2014
to the class of
Itô processes
having finite quadratic variation
Street and Crisan 2021
;
Wong 1971
.

The layout of this paper is as follows. Sec.
II
describes the SSE, its relation to noise induced by the control mechanism, and the Lindblad equation in the case of white noise. Sec.
III
details our ODE approach and the numerical scheme used to solve the SSE equations. In Sec.
IV
, we show initial results for our model on single and multiple qubits, using white and colored noise.

Stochastic Schrödinger Equation

The standard Schrödinger equation describing the evolution of a pure state
\phi

H_{coup}=e^{i\varphi}|0\rangle\langle 1|+e^{-i\varphi}|1\rangle\langle 0|,

H_{det}=(I-\sigma_{Z})/2=|1\rangle\langle 1|,

\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega+{\rm d}X^{\Omega}

[X]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}^{2}\,ds.

Here,

In this work, we distinguish between three classes of noise operators
S

SSE
),

Pauli noise:

\displaystyle\quad[H,S]=0,\quad S^{2}=I

\displaystyle\quad\text{(e.g. }S=\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{Z}\text{),}

Projection noise:

\displaystyle\quad[H,S]=0,\quad S^{2}=S

\displaystyle\quad\text{(e.g. }S=(I-\sigma_{Z})/2\text{).}

Non-commuting:

\displaystyle\quad H=\alpha\sigma_{1},\quad S=\sigma_{2},

\displaystyle\quad[H,S]=2i\alpha\sigma_{3},

Thus, following (
2

For our simulation results, we consider the following two examples of noise processes:

White noise (WN) process.

Here,
X
takes the form

X_{t}=\gamma\int_{0}^{t}{\rm d}W_{s},\qquad\gamma\geq 0,

(3)

is the standard Brownian motion, and has the quadratic variation
[X]_{t}=\gamma\,{\rm d}t
. Hence, the corresponding SSE reads

{\rm d}\psi=-iH\psi\,{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}S^{2}\,{\rm d}t-i\gamma S\psi\,{\rm d}W_{t}.

(WN)

Using (
WN

\psi\psi^{\dagger}
and taking the expectation results in the Lindblad equation
Brasil et al. 2013
;
Weinberg 2016
(see App.
A
)

\partial_{t}\rho=-i\left[H,\rho\right]+S\rho S^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl\{S^{\dagger}S,\rho\bigr\},

(4)

where
\rho=\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}]
.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process.

For any constant
k>0
, the (OU) process is given by

{\rm d}X_{t}=-kX_{t}\,{\rm d}t+\gamma\,{\rm d}W_{t},

(5)

subjected to either the calibrated initial data,
X_{0}=0
, or distributed according to the stationary distribution of the (OU) process, i.e.
X_{0}\sim\gamma\mathcal{N}/\sqrt{2k}

\mathcal{N}

k\downarrow 0

{\rm d}\psi=-iH\psi\,{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}S^{2}\psi\,{\rm d}t-iS\psi\,{\rm d}X_{t}.

(6)

Note that it has the same quadratic variation as (WN).

III
Analytical methods

In this section, we describe in detail the methods used to solve stochastic Schrödinger equations for qubit systems. Since the numerical integration of the SSE for
\psi
can be computationally expensive and unstable due to the non-Euclidean state space of the qubit and the possible non-linearity introduced by a general noise process, we derive full distribution solutions, which in turn lead to explicit formulas for the expectation and variance instead of approximations and/or bounds.

III.1
System of ODEs

Similar to
Kobayashi and Yamamoto 2019
, the quantity of interest in quantum computing is the fidelity
{\sf F}=|\phi^{\dagger}\psi|^{2}
, where
\phi
is the desired state without noise, i.e.
\phi
satisfies (
1
), and
\psi
is the state evolving under noise, i.e.
\psi
satisfies (
SSE
).

To derive an explicit formula for
{\sf F}
, we recursively derive a system of real-valued stochastic differential equations for a vector
{\sf V}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m\geq 1
, as

{\rm d}{\sf V}=A{\sf V}\,{\rm d}t+B{\sf V}\,{\rm d}X_{t}+a\,{\rm d}t+b\,{\rm d}X_{t},

(7)

where the first component of
{\sf V}
is the fidelity
{\sf F}=|\phi^{\dagger}\psi|^{2}
. The size of the system varies depending on the properties of the noise operator
S
. In the case of Pauli and projection noise,
m=3
, while for the non-commuting case,
m=10
.

For white noise (WN), we can solve these equations by considering
{\sf Y}=\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}]
and solving for the deterministic equation
{\rm d}{\sf Y}=A{\sf Y}\,{\rm d}t+a\,{\rm d}t
, which is equivalent to the Lindbladian approach (see App.
A
). However, for a general noise (SM), this can no longer be done because
\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}\,{\rm d}X_{t}]\neq\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}]\mathbb{E}[{\rm d}X_{t}]
. Specifically, for (OU), we see terms of the form
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}{\sf V}]
appearing. The physically most obvious way of incurring a solution, as performed in
Armaroli et al. 2022
, is to extend the vector
{\sf V}
with new entries of the form
X^{2n}{\sf V}
, and derive an elaborate system which will then again include
\mathbb{E}[X^{2n+1}{\sf V}]
. Eventually, an approximation has to be made for these terms to keep the system finite. In App.
B
, we detail on the approximation
\mathbb{E}[X^{2n}{\sf V}]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2n}]\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}]
. The
n
at which the truncation is done is referred to as the
order
of the approximation, for which an analytical solution for
\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}]
can be found. As shown in Sec.
IV.1
, solutions become better with increasing order of approximation, but eventually become nonphysical as fidelities will leave the interval
[0,1]
.

Instead, we argue for the less intuitive approach of solving for the full distribution of
{\sf V}
, from which the expectation and higher-order moments can be inferred. For all cases considered in this work—except for the non-commuting case—we obtain a diagonalizable ODE system (
7
), allowing us to obtain

{\rm d}{\sf Z}=\left(-\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}\Lambda^{\dagger}\Lambda\,{\rm d}t+\Lambda\,{\rm d}X_{t}\right)\bigl({\sf Z}-c\bigr),

(8)

where
\{\Lambda,P\}
is the eigensystem diagnonalizing both
A
and
B
,
{\sf Z}=P^{-1}{\sf V}
, and
c
is a vector of constants depending on
a
and
b
. The solution of this stochastic differential equation can, by Ito’s formula
Lawler 2016
, be seen to be

{\sf Z}=\exp(\Lambda(X_{t}-X_{0}))({\sf Z}_{0}-c)+c,

(9)

consequently giving,

{\sf V}=P(\exp(\Lambda(X_{t}-X_{0}))(z_{0}-c)+c),

from which we obtain an expression for the fidelity
{\sf F}
.

III.2
Pauli noise

We consider the case for Pauli noise. Using stochastic calculus, we can derive the ODE system (
7
) for the vector

{\sf V}=\begin{bmatrix}{\sf F}\\
|\phi^{\dagger}S\psi|^{2}\\
i(\phi^{\dagger}S\psi\psi^{\dagger}\phi-\phi^{\dagger}S\psi\psi^{\dagger}\phi)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

obtaining
a=b=0
,

A=\gamma^{2}\begin{bmatrix}-1&1&0\\
1&-1&0\\
0&0&-2\end{bmatrix},\;\;B=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\
0&0&1\\
2&-2&0\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Notice that
A=\gamma^{2}B^{2}/2
. After applying the diagonalization method, we deduce the following expression for the fidelity

\displaystyle{\sf F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+{\sf S}_{0}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2})\cos(2(X-X_{0}))

(11)

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}(X-X_{0})+{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\sin^{2}(X-X_{0}),

where
{\sf S}_{0}=\phi_{0}^{\dagger}S\phi_{0}
. For the (WN) and (OU) noise process, we deduce explicit formulas for the expectation and variance for the fidelity (cf. App.
C
).

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process.

For calibrated initial data (i.e.
X_{0}=0
), we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2\gamma^{2}\tau_{k}(t)},

(12)

\displaystyle\text{Var}({\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}\left[1-e^{-4\gamma^{2}\tau_{k}(t)}\right]^{2},

with
\tau_{k}(t):=e^{-kt}\sinh(kt)/{k}
,
while for stationary initial data (i.e.
X_{0}\sim\gamma\mathcal{N}/\sqrt{2k}
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2(1-e^{-kt})\frac{\gamma^{2}}{k}},

(13)

\displaystyle\text{Var}({\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}\left[1-e^{-4(1-e^{-kt})\frac{\gamma^{2}}{k}}\right]^{2}.

White noise (WN) process.

Passing
k\downarrow 0
in (
12
) and using the fact that
\lim_{k\downarrow 0}\tau_{k}(t)=t
, we recover the expectation and variance for the (WN) case:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{WN}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-2\gamma^{2}t},

(14)

\displaystyle\text{Var}({\sf F}_{t}^{\text{WN}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}\left[1-e^{-4\gamma^{2}t}\right]^{2}.

Notice the difference between the large-time asymptotic fidelity values for the (WN) and (OU) processes:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)e^{-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{k}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\right)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{WN}}],

and

\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Var}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}}]\leq\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Var}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{WN}}],

indicating a better limit fidelity for the (OU) process, which is logical given that the (OU) noise is inherently damped.

Projection noise

Next, we consider projection noise. For the vector

{\sf V}=\begin{bmatrix}{\sf F}\\
\phi^{\dagger}S\psi\psi^{\dagger}\phi+\phi^{\dagger}\psi\psi^{\dagger}S\phi\\

we find the ODE system (

\displaystyle A=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\

0&0&1\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle,\;\;B=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle a=\gamma^{2}{\sf S}_{0}^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}_{t}^{\text{OU}}]

Non-commuting

III.5
Multi-qubit

In a work by Kobayashi and Yamamoto
Kobayashi and Yamamoto 2019
, noise acting on multiple qubits is analyzed for multi-qubit operators satisfying
S^{2}=I
. These are important cases, as noise on the
S=Z\otimes Z
operator could model noise during an entanglement procedure, while
S=X\otimes I
or
S=I\otimes X
represents noise acting on one of the qubits in the system. However, it is specifically mentioned in
Kobayashi and Yamamoto 2019
that operators of the form
I\otimes Q+Q\otimes I
, with
Q^{2}=I
, cannot be treated using their methods. These types of operators are also important cases, as they model noise on a global control addressing all qubits simultaneously, which is prevalent in many quantum computing systems
Wintersperger et al. 2023
.

With our methods, we can solve these systems by defining
R:=Q\otimes Q
, such that

\displaystyle S^{2}=I\otimes Q^{2}+Q^{2}\otimes I+2R.

(22)

For Pauli noise (
Q^{2}=I
) we find

\displaystyle{\sf F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left({\sf S}_{0}^{2}+({\sf R}_{0}-1)^{2}+2\left(1-{\sf R}_{0}^{2}\right)\cos(2(X_{t}-X_{0}))\right.

(23)

\displaystyle+\left.(1-{\sf S}_{0}+{\sf R}_{0})(1+{\sf S}_{0}+{\sf R}_{0})\cos^{2}(2(X_{t}-X_{0}))\right),

with
{\sf S}_{0}=\phi_{0}^{\dagger}S\phi_{0}
and
{\sf R}_{0}=\phi_{0}^{\dagger}R\phi_{0}
.

For projection noise (
Q^{2}=Q
), we find

\displaystyle{\sf F}

\displaystyle=1-2\big(\cos(X_{t}-X_{0})-1\big)\times

(24)

\displaystyle\big({\sf S}_{0}^{2}+{\sf S}_{0}(2{\sf R}_{0}(\cos(X_{t}-X_{0})-1)-1)

\displaystyle-2{\sf R}_{0}[{\sf R}_{0}(\cos(X_{t}-X_{0})-1)+\cos(X_{t}-X_{0})]\big).

If we assume that the two-qubit system starts in a product state
\phi_{0}=\phi_{0}^{(0)}\otimes\phi_{0}^{(1)}
, then the values
{\sf S}_{0}
and
{\sf R}_{0}

{\sf S}_{0}={\sf S}_{0}^{(0)}+{\sf S}_{0}^{(1)}
and
{\sf R}_{0}={\sf S}_{0}^{(0)}{\sf S}_{0}^{(1)}
, where the superscript
(i)
refers to the
i
-th qubit. For both Pauli and projection noise, the fidelity distribution then factors as

{\sf F}={\sf F}^{(0)}{\sf F}^{(1)}.

(25)

In App.
D
, this is generalized to
n
-qubit systems. Physically, this result is intuitive, as the two qubits evolve independently but under the same noise. This means that their fidelities are only dependent on the noise process and thus factor on the level of the distribution. Note that this does not mean that the expectation of the fidelity will factor as
{\sf F}^{(0)}
and
{\sf F}^{(1)}
are not independent. The full expectation
\mathbb{E}[{\sf F}]
can still be calculated according to the methods of App.
C
. Moreover, the two-qubit method can also treat entangled ground states for which the distributions do not factor as described by Eq. (
25
).

Lastly, we analyze a two-qubit system with the noise operator

k\ll 1

U(X_{T})

|00\rangle\rightarrow|\eta_{T}\rangle
and
|11\rangle\rightarrow|\zeta_{T}\rangle.
The overlap of the final states with
\phi_{T}

\phi_{0}
since
H=0
, are given for the case
\phi_{0}=|00\rangle
by

\langle\psi_{T}|\phi_{T}\rangle=\langle 00|\eta_{T}\rangle,

and for the case
\phi_{0}=\text{GHZ}
by

\langle\psi_{T}|\phi_{T}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\langle 00|\psi_{T}\rangle+\langle 11|\zeta_{T}\rangle+\langle 00|\zeta_{T}\rangle+\langle 11|\psi_{T}\rangle\bigr).

For both small and large noises we can, by symmetry, consider
\langle 00|\psi_{T}\rangle=\langle 11|\zeta_{T}\rangle

k\gg 1

|\psi_{T}\rangle\approx|00\rangle
and
|\zeta_{T}\rangle\approx|11\rangle
, and the cross terms barely contribute to the fidelity. Conversely, for small
k\ll 1
, the noise is larger, and the cross terms become large, resulting in higher fidelities. Note that this does not always have to be the case. Indeed, if
S=Y\otimes I+I\otimes Y
, then GHZ is an eigenstate with eigenvalue 0 and does not evolve under this noise at all.

V
Conclusion

This work describes a novel method for expressing the full distribution of qubit fidelity under SSEs. These solutions contain more information than the standard Lindblad equation, which can in turn be used for noise characterization and optimal control problems. Furthermore, our work is the first to predict the long-term behavior of fidelities evolving under realistic noise types, such as (OU) noise stemming from fluctuation-dissipation processes. This long-time behavior is especially important in control processes, where one seeks to maintain a state faithfully for a long period. The methods developed here are significantly less computationally demanding and more accurate than standard Monte Carlo methods for SSEs. All these predictions can further be used to decide on the allowed levels of noise in a qubit control system, e.g. a driving laser or a cavity resonator, thus, resulting in an accurate benchmark for control system noise on a qubit system.

In future work, we hope to extend this work to predictions on noises with arbitrary power spectral densities. Furthermore, we hope to generalize our method to
N
qubits and extend the results to non-commuting Hamiltonians. Lastly, we hypothesize that these methods on SSEs can be used to introduce optimal control for noise mitigation on open quantum systems, which so far has failed to be realized for Lindblad equations.

Appendix A
Formal derivation of the Stochastic Schrödinger equation

In this section, we follow the derivation of the SSE as in
Semina et al. 2014
. Consider an (OU) process
X(t)
and operators
C,D
, and
R
on the Hilbert space. The basic linear Schrödinger equation with noise is given by

{\rm d}\psi=(C+DX_{t})\psi\,{\rm d}t+R\psi\,{\rm d}X_{t}.

(27)

Using
{\rm d}X_{t}=-kX_{t}+\gamma{\rm d}W_{t}
, this can be rewritten as

{\rm d}\psi=(C+XD-kXR)\psi\,{\rm d}t+\gamma R\psi\,{\rm d}W_{t}.

(28)

For normalization,
{\rm d}|\psi|^{2}=({\rm d}\psi^{\dagger})\psi+\psi^{\dagger}({\rm d}\psi)+({\rm d}\psi^{\dagger})({\rm d}\psi)=0
is required at all times. Expanding this using Ito calculus (
{\rm d}t^{2}=0,{\rm d}W_{t}{\rm d}t=0,{\rm d}W_{t}^{2}={\rm d}t)

Lawler 2016
gives

{\rm d}|\psi|^{2}=\psi^{\dagger}\left[C^{\dagger}+C+X_{t}\left(D^{\dagger}+D-kR-kR^{\dagger}\right)+\gamma^{2}R^{\dagger}R\right]\psi\,{\rm d}t+\gamma\psi^{\dagger}(R^{\dagger}+R)\psi\,{\rm d}W_{t}=0.

(29)

Thus,
C^{\dagger}+C+R^{\dagger}R=0
,
D^{\dagger}+D-kR-kR^{\dagger}=0
and
R^{\dagger}+R=0
. One can choose
R=iS
with
S=S^{\dagger}
Hermitian. Furthermore,
C=-iH-\frac{1}{2}S^{\dagger}S=-iH-\frac{1}{2}S^{2}
, with
H=H^{\dagger}
and
D=0
to finally get the SSE for an (OU) process as

\text{(OU)}:\quad{\rm d}\psi=-iH\psi\,{\rm d}t+ikX_{t}S\psi\,{\rm d}t-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}S^{\dagger}S\psi\,{\rm d}t-i\gamma S\psi\,{\rm d}W_{t}.\\

(30)

Letting
k\rightarrow 0
results in the SSE for the (WN) process as

\text{(WN)}:\quad{\rm d}\psi=-iH\psi\,{\rm d}t-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}S^{\dagger}S\psi\,{\rm d}t-i\gamma S\psi\,{\rm d}W_{t}.\\

(31)

Note that Ito calculus holds for any semimartingale
Lawler 2016
, and thus for general semimartingale noise we can write

\text{(SM)}:\quad{\rm d}\psi=-iH\psi\,{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{2}S^{\dagger}S\psi\,{\rm d}[X]_{t}-iS\psi\,{\rm d}X_{t},\\

(32)

where
[X]_{t}
is the quadratic variation of the process
Kulasiri and Verwoerd 2002
. By defining
\rho=\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}]
and noting that
\mathbb{E}[{\rm d}W_{t}]=0

\displaystyle{\rm d}\psi\psi^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=-i[H,\psi\psi^{\dagger}]\,{\rm d}t+(S\psi\psi^{\dagger}S^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{S^{\dagger}S,\psi\psi^{\dagger}\})\,{\rm d}[X]_{t}-i[S,\psi\psi^{\dagger}]\,{\rm d}X_{t}

(33)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=-i[H,\rho]+\gamma^{2}(S\rho^{\dagger}S^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{S^{\dagger}S,\rho\})-i[S,\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}\,{\rm d}X_{t}]],

which for white noise reduces to the Lindblad equation since
\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}{\rm d}X_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}{\rm d}W_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}]\mathbb{E}[{\rm d}W_{t}]=0
. Note that for general noise, there is no independence of the state and the noise increments (i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\psi\psi^{\dagger}{\rm d}X_{t}]\neq\mathbb{E}[{\rm d}X_{t}]\rho
), and we do not get the standard Lindblad equation.

Appendix B
Ito’s Isometry

We detail the approximation method for terms of the form
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}{\sf V}]
as described in Sec.
III.1
. The integral solution of the (OU) process
Särkkä and Solin 2019
can be written as

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma e^{k(s-t)}{\rm d}W_{s}.

(34)

By positivity of
{\sf V}

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}{\sf V}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\gamma e^{k(s-t)}{\rm d}W_{s}\right)^{2}{\sf V}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\gamma\sqrt{{\sf V}}e^{k(s-t)}{\rm d}W_{s}\right)^{2}\right].

(35)

If
{\sf V}
were to be adapted to the natural filtration of the Wiener process
W_{t}
, Ito’s isometry
Øksendal 2010
can be used to write

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}{\sf V}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\gamma^{2}{\sf V}e^{2k(s-t)}{\rm d}t\right]=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2k}(1-e^{-2kt})\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2}]\mathbb{E}[{\sf V}].

(36)

However,
{\sf V}
could for instance be the fidelity at time
t
which is dependent on the Wiener process in the interval
[0,t]
. Therefore, Ito’s isometry does not hold. Nevertheless, for small values of
t
, the
{\sf V}
values are roughly equal to their respective initial values, and Ito’s isometry can be used as an approximation.

Appendix C
Moment calculation

For the expectation and variance of the fidelity distributions, expressions for the expectations of terms
\cos(\alpha(X_{t}-X_{0}))
for
\alpha>0
have to be calculated (note that for square terms
\cos(x)^{2}=(\cos(2x)+1)/2
). To do so, the cosines are expanded into their power series representation, and both linearity and conditional expectations are used to get

\mathbb{E}[\cos(\alpha(X_{t}-X_{0}))]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2n!}\alpha^{2n}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}-X_{0})^{2n}|X_{0}]\big].

(37)

The (OU) process, by Ito’s formula
Øksendal 2010
, can be shown to obey

{\rm d}X_{t}^{n}=-nkX^{n}{\rm d}t+n\gamma X^{n-1}{\rm d}W_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}-n)\gamma^{2}X^{n-2}{\rm d}t,\quad X^{n}(0)\sim X_{0}^{n},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(38)

Calibrated initial data
X_{0}=0

For calibrated initial data (e.g.
X_{0}=0
), it is easily proven by induction that

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}-X_{0})^{2n}|X_{0}]\big]=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2n}]=\frac{\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{k}e^{-kt}\sinh(kt)\right)^{n}.

(39)

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. Resulting from Eq. (
37
) in

\mathbb{E}[\cos(\alpha(X_{t}-X_{0}))]=\exp\left(-\alpha^{2}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2k}e^{-kt}\sinh(kt)\right).

(40)

Stationary initial data
X_{0}\sim\gamma\mathcal{N}/\sqrt{2k}

For stationary initial data, a slightly more involved approach is necessary, where the binomial theorem is employed to get

\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}-X_{0})^{2n}|X_{0}]=\sum_{m=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{m}(-1)^{2n-m}X_{0}^{2n-m}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}|X_{0}].

(41)

From the differential equations in Eq. (
38
)

d\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}|X_{0}]=-mk\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}|X_{0}]{\rm d}t+\frac{1}{2}(m^{2}-m)\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m-2}|X_{0}]{\rm d}t,

(42)

with initial conditions
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{0}|X_{0}]=1,\,\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{1}|X_{0}]=X_{0}e^{-kt}.
This can be shown to have the solution

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}|X_{0}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}e^{-2wkt}(2k)^{-w}\sum_{l=0}^{w}a[l,w](e^{2kt}-1)^{w-l}\gamma^{2(w-l)}k^{l}X_{0}^{2l},\quad\,\,\,\,\,w=m/2,\qquad\,\,\,\text{ for }m\text{ even}\\
e^{-(2w+1)kt}(2k)^{-w}\sum_{l=0}^{w}b[l,w](e^{2kt}-1)^{w-l}\gamma^{2(w-l)}k^{l}X_{0}^{2l+1},\quad w=(m-1)/2,\text{ for }m\text{ odd}\end{cases},

(43)

\displaystyle a[l,w]:=2^{l}\prod_{q=l+1}^{w}\frac{q(2q-1)}{q-l},\quad b[l,w]:=2^{l}\prod_{q=l}^{w-1}\frac{(1+q)(3+2q)}{(q-l+1)}.

For verification, the expressions for
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{2m}]
in the case of calibrated initial data (Eq. (
39
)) are retrieved when taking
X_{0}=0
. Using conditional expectation on Eq. (
41
) gives

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}-X_{0})^{2n}|X_{0}]\big]=\sum_{m=0}^{2n}\binom{2n}{m}(-1)^{2n-m}\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{2n-m}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}^{m}|X_{0}]].

(44)

Furthermore, the normal distribution of
X_{0}
gives

\mathbb{E}[X_{0}^{q}]=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{2k}}\right)^{q}(q-1)!!,\quad q\text{ even }\\
0,\quad q\text{ odd }.\end{cases}

(45)

Combining Eq. (
43
) and Eq. (
45
), and filling into Eq. (
44
) gives

\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[(X_{t}-X_{0})^{2n}|X_{0}]]=(2n-1)!!\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{k}e^{-kt}(e^{kt}-1)\right)^{n},

(46)

which, when used in Eq. (
37
), results in

\mathbb{E}[\cos(\alpha(X_{t}-X_{0}))]=\exp\left(-\alpha^{2}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2k}e^{-kt}(e^{kt}-1)\right).

(47)

Appendix D
Multi-qubit noise

This section proves a lemma regarding the factoring of fidelity distributions of
n
-qubit systems, which have a product initial state and evolve under the same noise source.

Lemma

(Factoring fidelity for pure states)
.

Let the state
\psi_{(n)}
of a
n
-qubit system evolve according to

{\rm d}\psi_{(n)}=-iH_{n}\psi_{(n)}{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{2}S_{n}^{2}\psi_{(n)}{\rm d}[X]_{t}-iS_{n}\psi_{(n)}{\rm d}X_{t},\quad\psi_{(n)}(0)=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{j0},

(48)

where the Hamiltonian
H_{n}
and the noise operator
S_{n}
take the sum form

\displaystyle H_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{j},\quad A_{j}=I^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes\tilde{A}_{j}\otimes I^{\otimes(n-j)},\quad\tilde{A_{j}}=\tilde{A_{j}}^{\dagger},

(49)

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j},\quad Q_{j}=I^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes\tilde{Q}_{j}\otimes I^{\otimes(n-j)},\quad\tilde{Q_{j}}=\tilde{Q_{j}}^{\dagger}.

Let
\phi_{(n)}
be the noiseless target state. Then

|\phi_{(n)}^{\dagger}\psi_{(n)}|^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\phi_{j}^{\dagger}\psi_{j}|^{2},

where
\psi_{j}
is a 1-qubit state evolving according to the SSE

{\rm d}\psi_{j}=-i\tilde{A}_{j}\psi_{j}\,{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{Q}_{j}^{2}\psi_{j}\,{\rm d}[X]_{t}-i\tilde{Q}_{j}\psi_{j}\,{\rm d}X_{t},\quad\psi_{j}(0)=\psi_{j0},

(50)

and
\phi_{j}
is its corresponding noiseless target state.

Proof.

Squaring
S_{n}
, we find

S_{n}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k\neq j}^{n}Q_{jk},\quad Q_{jk}=I^{\otimes(j-1)}\otimes\tilde{Q}_{j}\otimes I^{\otimes(k-j-1)}\otimes\tilde{Q}_{k}\otimes I^{\otimes(n-k)}.

(51)

By induction we prove the
\psi_{(n)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{j}
.

This holds trivially for
n=1
. Now, assume the statement holds for
n
. Then for
n+1
, we use Itô’s formula to obtain

\displaystyle{\rm d}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n+1}\psi_{j}=-iH_{n+1}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n+1}\psi_{j}\,{\rm d}t-iS_{n+1}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n+1}\psi_{j}\,{\rm d}X_{t}-\frac{1}{2}S_{n+1}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n+1}\psi_{j}\,{\rm d}[X]_{t}.

(52)

As we have equal initial conditions, we indeed find
\psi_{(n+1)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n+1}\psi_{j}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Analogously, we find
\phi_{(n)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{j}
. For the fidelity, we then deduce

|\phi_{(n)}^{\dagger}\psi_{(n)}|^{2}=\phi_{(n)}^{\dagger}\psi_{(n)}\psi_{(n)}^{\dagger}\phi_{(n)}=\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{j}^{\dagger}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{j}\right)\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\psi_{j}^{\dagger}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\phi_{j}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\phi_{j}^{\dagger}\psi_{j}|^{2}.

(53)

∎

Note that these results do not only hold for qubits, but for any finite ensemble of finite dimensional quantum systems.

Appendix E
Stochastic Integration

Numerical verification of the analytic results is performed using stochastic integration. To solve for the noise and state simultaneously, we define
{\sf Y}:=(\psi,X)
. For the (OU) process, this gives the differential equation

{\rm d}{\sf Y}=a({\sf Y})\,{\rm d}t+b({\sf Y})\,{\rm d}W_{t},\quad a({\sf Y})=\begin{pmatrix}-iH+ikXS-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}S^{\dagger}S&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\mathbf{0}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}^{\text{T}}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&-k\end{pmatrix}{\sf Y},\quad b({\sf Y})=\begin{pmatrix}-i\gamma S&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\mathbf{0}\\
\hline\cr\mathbf{0}^{\text{T}}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\frac{1}{X}\end{pmatrix}{\sf Y}.

(54)

These equations can be solved discretely over time steps
\Delta t
using a numerical integration scheme. One possible scheme is the explicit (weak) first-order Euler-Maruyama scheme
Bayram et al. 2018
as

{\sf Y}_{n+1}={\sf Y}_{n}+a({\sf Y}_{n})\Delta t+b({\sf Y}_{n})\mathcal{N}\sqrt{\Delta t},

(55)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a standard normal distribution. Throughout this work, convergences issues persisted using stochastic integration schemes for (OU) noise at higher evolution times, likely due to the non-Lipschitz
1/X
dependence
Kloeden and Platen 1999
and possibly the non-Euclidean space in which the states live. These convergence issues are absent for white noise and always occur below fidelities of
F=0.95
, which is not a relevant regime for pragmatic quantum computing and therefore not a deliberating issue. We have found that these issues are mitigated (but not resolved) when using a higher-order scheme such as the explicit (weak) second-order scheme due to Platen
Breuer et al. 2000
;
Kloeden and Platen 1999
, which is used throughout this work. This scheme is given by

\displaystyle\sf{\displaystyle Y}_{n+1}={\sf Y}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\big(a(\bar{\Upsilon})+a({\sf Y}_{n})\big)\Delta t+\frac{1}{4}\big(b\left(\bar{\Upsilon}^{+}\right)+b\left(\bar{\Upsilon}^{-}\right)+2b({\sf Y}_{n})\big)\mathcal{N}\sqrt{\Delta t}+\frac{1}{4}\big(b(\tilde{\Upsilon}^{+})-b(\widetilde{\Upsilon}^{-})\big)\left(\mathcal{N}^{2}-1\right)\sqrt{\Delta t},

with supporting values
\bar{\Upsilon}={\sf Y}_{n}+a({\sf Y}_{n})\Delta t+b({\sf Y}_{n})\mathcal{N}\sqrt{\Delta t}
and
\bar{\Upsilon}^{\pm}={\sf Y}_{n}+a({\sf Y}_{n})\Delta t\pm b({\sf Y}_{n})\sqrt{\Delta t}
.

Non-commuting

For the non-commuting system we consider
H=\alpha\sigma_{1}
,
S=\sigma_{2}
, giving
[H,S]=2i\alpha\sigma_{3}
, where
\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\}
can be any cyclic permutation of
\{\sigma_{X},\sigma_{Y},\sigma_{Z}\}
. The group structure of the Pauli matrices allows us to close the system of ODEs for the vector

\displaystyle{\sf V}=\begin{bmatrix}{\sf F}\\
|\phi^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}\psi|^{2}\\
|\phi^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}\psi|^{2}\\
|\phi^{\dagger}\sigma_{3}\psi|^{2}\\

A

A_{c}

Solving this system as in Sec.
III.1
is not possible since
[A_{c},B]\neq 0
. However, a perturbation technique can be used when the Hamiltonian strength is much larger than the noise, i.e.
\varepsilon^{2}:=\gamma^{2}/\alpha\ll 1
, which holds for many realistic systems. Rescaling time
\widehat{t}=t/\alpha
, we obtain a rescaled system of equations for
\widehat{\sf V}={\sf V}_{t/\alpha}
and
\widehat{X}=X_{t/\alpha}
,

{\rm d}\widehat{\sf V}=A_{c}\widehat{\sf V}\,{\rm d}\widehat{t}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}B^{2}\widehat{\sf V}\,{\rm d}\widehat{t}+B\widehat{\sf V}\,{\rm d}\widehat{X}_{t},

(61)

where
\widehat{X}
has the quadratic variation
[\widehat{X}]_{\widehat{t}}=\varepsilon^{2}\widehat{t}
.

In the following, we assume w.l.o.g. that
\alpha=1
and
\gamma^{2}\ll 1
. Otherwise, we simply rescale time as above and write
{\sf V}
in place of
\widehat{\sf V}
. Setting
{\sf U}:=\exp(-A_{c}t){\sf V}
to get

{\rm d}{\sf U}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}D^{2}(t){\sf U}\,{\rm d}t+D(t){\sf U}\,{\rm d}X_{t},

(62)

with

D(t)=\exp(-A_{c}t)B\exp(A_{c}t)=\cos(2t)B-\frac{1}{2}\sin(2t)[A_{c},B],

(63)

where the second equality can be established according to a generalized harmonic oscillator
Parthasarathy 1992
. Although (
62
) cannot be solved explicitly, an approximation to its solution can be found via the stochastic Magnus expansion
Kamm et al. 2021
, which states that

{\sf U}=\exp({\sf L})=\exp\left(\sum_{n,r}\gamma^{2n-r}{\sf L}^{(r,n-r)}\right),

(64)

where
{\sf L}^{(r,n-r)}
are the expansion terms up to order
\gamma^{2}
in
{\sf L}
. For general processes we find

{\sf U}_{t}=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}D(s)\,{\rm d}X_{s}+O(\gamma^{3})\right),

(65)

which, for white noise, (WN) gives

\mathbb{E}[{\sf U}_{t}]\approx\exp\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}D^{2}(s)\,ds\right).

(66)

For the fidelity under white noise, we obtain the approximation

{\sf F}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[{\sf U}_{t}]\approx\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\\
C_{1}^{2}e^{-2\gamma^{2}t}+\frac{1}{2}e^{-\gamma^{2}t}\Bigl(\sinh(u)(({\sf C}_{2}^{2}-{\sf C}_{3}^{2})\cos(2at)-2{\sf C}_{2}{\sf C}_{3}\sin(2at))+\left({\sf C}_{2}^{2}+{\sf C}_{3}^{2}\right)\cosh(u)\Bigr).

(67)

For the (OU) process with
k>0
, the exponential can not be expressed analytically. Instead, one could approximate it by expanding the exponential up to the second order to find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\sf U}_{t}]

\displaystyle\approx I+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}D^{2}(s)ds

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}k\int_{0}^{t}\left\{e^{-ks}D(s),\int_{0}^{s}e^{ks^{\prime}}D(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}\right\}ds+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}k\int_{0}^{t}\left\{e^{-2ks}D(s),\int_{0}^{s}e^{2ks^{\prime}}D(s^{\prime})ds^{\prime}\right\}ds.
