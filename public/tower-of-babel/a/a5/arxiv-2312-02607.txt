Kevin Carrier, Valerian Hatey, Jean-Pierre Tillich

Abstract
We show that here standard decoding algorithms for generic linear codes over a finite field can speeded up by a factor which is essentially the size of the finite field by reducing it to a low weight codeword problem and working in the relevant projective space. We apply this technique to SDitH and show that the parameters of both the original submission and the updated version fall short of meeting the security requirements asked by the NIST.

Introduction

Code-based cryptography is based on the hardness of the decoding problem. In its syndrome (and fixed weight) version it is given for the Hamming metric
(where we denote the Hamming weight of a vector
{\mathbf{x}}
by
\left|{\mathbf{x}}\right|
) by

Problem 1.1

(Syndrome Decoding
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
)
.

Given a matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
, a syndrome
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
and a weight
t\in\left\llbracket 0,n\right\rrbracket
, find a vector
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{s}}
and
\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t
.

In other words, it consists in solving a linear system with a constraint on the weight of the solution. This non-linear constraint is commonly believed to make the problem difficult on average over
{\mathbf{H}}
for suitable values of
t
.
Despite that many efforts have been spent over the last 60 years
[

,
Ste88
,
Dum91
,
MMT11
,
BJMM12
,
MO15
,
BM17
,
BM18
,
CDMT22
]
, the problem remains hard in the range of parameters given above, even with the help of a quantum computer

[
Ber10
,
KT17
]
.
Thus, the decoding problem has raised interest among cryptosystem designers. It is today the heart of the security of PKE and signature schemes submitted to the NIST competitions

such as
Classic McEliece

[

[

,

[

]
and

.
It is quite common to study the binary version of the decoding problem but the non-binary case also aroused interest

,

]
or more recently with the signature schemes

[

]

. In this article, we focus on the case where

The best known decoding algorithm are Information Set Decoding (ISD) initiated by Prange in
[

]
. The idea of Prange basically consists in

is zero on an

{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{s}}

t

There have been numerous improvements to the Prange algorithm. One of the first breakthrough in this domain uses the
birthday paradox

[

Dum91
]
. Basically, the ISD template is used here to reduce the decoding problem to a collision search. Later, other techniques were introduced to improve ISD. For instance,

[

]
where he shows that the MMT
[

SDitH
focuses on is Stern
[

]
which is considered optimal by the authors of
SDitH
in their particular context.

1.1
Our contribution

Our main observation here is that decoding over a big field can basically be speeded up by a factor which is the size of the field by a simple homogenizing trick (or what is the same by a reduction to the low weight codeword search problem). The idea is that instead of looking for a vector
{\mathbf{e}}
of weight
t
satisfying
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{s}}
we look for a vector
{\mathbf{x}}
of weight

t
such that
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}
is
proportional
to
{\mathbf{s}}
,
i.e.
is such that
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{x}}=\lambda{\mathbf{s}}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. If we find such a vector (and if
\lambda\neq 0
which will happen with large probability as we will see in what follows) then we get from such an
{\mathbf{x}}
our
{\mathbf{e}}
by taking
{\mathbf{e}}=\lambda^{-1}{\mathbf{x}}
. The point is that basically all the collision search techniques used for solving the decoding problem get speeded by essentially a factor
q-1
by identifying all vectors which are proportional. This is particularly helpful in the case where we work
with big field sizes as is the case for the NIST submission
SDitH

[
AMFG+23
]
.
We will adapt this idea to one of the simplest collision decoding technique, namely Stern’s decoding algorithm over
\mathbb{F}_{q}

[
Pet10
]
.
We call this variant,
projective Stern’s algorithm
since we work here essentially in the projective space.
We provide here a clean counting of the complexity of this variant of Stern’s algorithm
in the spirit of
[
Pet11
, Ch. 6]
. This precise complexity counting
includes the use of the Canteaut-Chabaud technique
[
CC98
]
to gain in the
complexity of Gaussian elimination. This part can not be neglected at all for giving tight security estimates in the case of
SDitH
because the list sizes in an optimal Stern algorithm are in this case really small. In
SDitH
there is also a variant of the decoding which is considered, which is the
d
-split variant: the support of the error is split into
d
equal parts and it asked to find
an error
{\mathbf{e}}
of weight
t/d
on each of the part. We give an adaptation of our projective Stern’s algorithm to this case too.

We will study in detail the impact of this technique to
SDitH
both for the initial submission
[
AMFG+23
]
and for the recent update that can be found on

https://sdith.org/
. The initial submission was unfortunately affected by a mistake in the choice of parameters that corresponded to a region where there were several hundred of solutions to the decoding problem whereas the analysis implicitly assumed that there was just one. The security claims made in
[
AMFG+23
]
were incorrect because of this.
The new algorithm presented here also reduces the security of this proposal. All in all, this shows that the security of the initial submission
[
AMFG+23
]
is below the NIST requirements by 9 to 14 bits depending on the
SDitH
variant. Three days after preliminary results of this work were made public
[
CTH23
]
, new parameters of
SDitH
were released and announced on the NIST forum
2
2

2

See
https://groups.google.com/a/list.nist.gov/g/pqc-forum/c/OOnB655mCN8/m/rL4bPD20AAAJ
. This new parameter set corrected the initial error in the parameter choice (now the parameters are chosen such that there is typically just one solution to the decoding problem). The authors took a
4
bit security margin between the NIST security requirements and the
estimate for the best attack provided in
https://sdith.org/
. We show here that this is still a little bit short of meeting the NIST requirements by roughly one bit. It should be noted that
contrarily to
[
AMFG+23
]
which uses (i) a non tight reduction from standard decoding to
d
-split decoding which gives an overestimate on the attacks, (ii) neglects the cost
of Gaussian elimination in the attack, our security estimate is based on a precise count of the complexity of the attack which does not neglect the cost of Gaussian elimination. It turns out that the optimal parameters for the projective Stern algorithm are in the regime where the cost of Gaussian elimination is non negligible.

Vectors and matrices.
Vectors and matrices are respectively denoted in bold letters
and bold capital letters such as

The notation

The double square brackets stand for a set of consecutive integers. For instance,
\left\llbracket a,b\right\rrbracket

a

Coding background.

A linear code
\mathcal{C}
of length
n
and dimension
k
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of dimension
k
. We say that it is an
[n,k]_{q}
-code.
It can be defined by a
generator matrix

{\mathbf{G}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
whose rows form a basis of the code:

\mathcal{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{{\mathbf{G}}^{\intercal}{\mathbf{u}}\;:\;{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}\right\}.

(2.1)

A
parity-check matrix
for
\mathcal{C}
is a matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
whose right kernel is
\mathcal{C}
:

\mathcal{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\;:\;{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{c}}={\mathbf{0}}\right\}.

(2.2)

A set of
k
positions that fully defines a code
\mathcal{C}
is called an
information set
. In other words, for
I\subseteq\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
such that
\left|I\right|=k
and
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket\setminus I
, the subset
I
is an information set if and only if
{\mathbf{G}}_{I}
is invertible or equivalently
{\mathbf{H}}_{J}
is invertible. In that case,
J
is called
redundancy set
.

In this paper, we address the decoding problem
1.1
. We focus on the case where the decoding distance
t
is lower than
n-\frac{n}{q}
. We distinguish two particular regimes: when the decoding problem has typically less than one solution and when it has more. For
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
that are drawn uniformly at random, the greatest distance
t
for which the decoding problem has less than one solution on expectation is called the
Gilbert-Varshamov distance
and it is denoted by:

d_{\mathsf{GV}}(n,k)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\sup\left(\left\{t\in\left\llbracket 0,n-\tfrac{n}{q}\right\rrbracket\;:\;\binom{n}{t}(q-1)^{t}\leqslant q^{n-k}\right\}\right)

(2.3)

How we measure complexities.
Because one of our goals is to compare our results to those of the
SDitH
specifications, we measure the complexities in the same way as they do. In particular, we assume that the additions and multiplications in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are implemented using lookup tables and that these two operations therefore have the same cost. In the
SDitH
specifications, this cost is considered as
\log_{2}(q)
which is the estimated cost of a memory access. In the following, we count the complexities in number of additions/multiplications and therefore we ignore the factor
\log_{2}(q)
. However, the results of Section
7
are given with this factor.

Stern’s decoding algorithm is an iterative algorithm that is parametrized by two integers
p

I\subseteq\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket

k

\left|{\mathbf{P}}{\mathbf{x}}-{\mathbf{y}}\right|=t-2p

{\mathbf{P}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}{\mathbf{H}}_{J}^{-1}{\mathbf{H}}_{I}\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;{\mathbf{y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}{\mathbf{H}}_{J}^{-1}{\mathbf{s}}

We can easily verify that if we find such an

defined by

is zero on its second half (resp.

is zero on its first half),

Algorithm 1

Stern’s algorithm to solve
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)

Note that if a particular error vector
{\mathbf{e}}
has the good weight distribution – that is
{\mathbf{e}}_{I}
is of weight

{\mathbf{e}}_{J}
is
{\mathbf{0}}
on its

p_{\mathrm{part}}=\dfrac{\binom{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}{p}\binom{k-\lfloor k/2\rfloor}{p}\binom{n-k-\ell}{t-2p}}{\binom{n}{t}}

Moreover, in the case where
{\mathbf{s}}
has been produced as the syndrome of an error
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
of weight

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{sol}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\left\{{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\;:\;\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t\text{ and }{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\right\}\right|\right)

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\}\\

Reducing the decoding problem to the low weight codeword search

Working in projective spaces is only interesting if the syndrome is zero. In that case, we are actually looking for a low weight codeword instead of an error vector.
This can be readily achieved by a well known reduction from decoding in an
[n,k]_{q}
linear code to a low weight codeword search in an
[n,k+1]_{q}
linear code
that we now recall.
Let
{\mathbf{H}}
be a parity check matrix of a code
\mathcal{C}
. Without loss of generality, we can consider that
{\mathbf{H}}
is in systematic form
3
3

3

The first operation of Stern’s algorithm precisely consists in putting the parity-check matrix in systematic form (up to a permutation). And therefore making this assumption does not induce any additional cost.
:

{\mathbf{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left[{\mathbf{A}}|{\mathbf{I}}_{n-k}\right]\;\;\;\;\;\text{ where }{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times k}

(4.1)

To solve the decoding problem
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
, one can find a low weight codeword in the new code

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\langle\mathcal{C},{\mathbf{z}}\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{{\mathbf{c}}+\alpha{\mathbf{z}}\;:\;{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathcal{C}\text{ and }\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\right\}

(4.2)

where
{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is any solution of the equation
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{s}}
(without any weight constraint on
{\mathbf{z}}
). Because
{\mathbf{H}}
is in systematic form, we can take
{\mathbf{z}}^{\intercal}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}({\mathbf{0}}^{\intercal},{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Then a generator matrix of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is

{\mathbf{G}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{I}}_{k}&-{\mathbf{A}}^{\intercal}\\
{\mathbf{0}}^{\intercal}&{\mathbf{s}}^{\intercal}\end{matrix}\right]

(4.3)

By only one step of a Gaussian elimination (one column to eliminate), we can find a parity-check matrix
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}
of the augmented code
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

By looking for a low weight codeword in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
–
i.e.
a vector
{\mathbf{e}}
such that
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{0}}
–, we actually find a low weight error
{\mathbf{e}}
of
\mathcal{C}
that has syndrome
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}=\alpha{\mathbf{s}}
where
\alpha
can be any scalar in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. There are two possible situations: either
\alpha=0
or
\alpha\neq 0
. If
\alpha=0
then we have actually found a codeword in
\mathcal{C}
instead of an error vector (we want to avoid this situation). On the contrary, if
\alpha\neq 0
then a solution to our original decoding problem is simply
\alpha^{-1}{\mathbf{e}}
. We now claim that the probability to get
\alpha=0
is lower than
\frac{1}{q}
:

Theorem 4.1

Let a code
\mathcal{C}
be the right kernel of a parity-check matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and let
{\mathbf{s}}\in\left\{{\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\;:\;\left|\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\right|=t\right\}
for
t\in\left\llbracket 0,n\right\rrbracket
.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\langle\mathcal{C},{\mathbf{z}}\rangle
be the code generated by the codewords in
\mathcal{C}
and any word
{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{s}}
. We denote by
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}
a parity-check matrix of this augmented code.
Then we can solve the decoding problem
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
by solving, on average over
{\mathbf{H}}
, at most
\frac{q}{q-1}
low weight codeword searches
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{0}},t)
.

Proof.

First, by construction of
{\mathbf{s}}
and
{\mathbf{z}}
, we know there exists a codeword
{\mathbf{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
and an error vector
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of weight
t
such that
{\mathbf{z}}={\mathbf{c}}+\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
. So we have
\langle\mathcal{C},{\mathbf{z}}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{C},\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\rangle
and
{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
.

Let
{\mathbf{e}}
be any solution of the low weight codeword search problem
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{0}},t)
. Then, as said before,
{\mathbf{e}}
is an error vector of weight
t
such that
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}=\alpha{\mathbf{s}}
for a scalar
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and
{\mathbf{e}}
induces a solution of the original decoding problem if and only if
\alpha\neq 0
. On average over
{\mathbf{H}}
and for
{\mathbf{e}}
drawn uniformly at random in the solutions of
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{0}},t)
, the probability that
\alpha=0
is

\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{e}}}\left(\alpha=0\right)=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(t)\right|\right)}{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t)\right|\right)}

where

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{C}(t)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{C}\;:\;\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t\right\}\\
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}&\left\{{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\;:\;\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t\right\}\end{array}

We already know that

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(t)\right|\right)=\frac{\binom{n}{t}(q-1)^{t}}{q^{n-k}}.

Let us count
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t)
. We remark that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of the cosets
\mathcal{C}+\alpha\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
for all the
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. So

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t)\right|\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\right|\right)

where

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{{\mathbf{e}}+\alpha\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\;:\;{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{C}\text{ and }\left|{\mathbf{e}}+\alpha\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\right|=t\right\}.

On one hand, we have that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{0}(t)=\mathcal{C}(t)
. On another hand, for all non-zero
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, the map

{\mathbf{x}}\longmapsto\alpha^{-1}\alpha^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}
is a bijection from
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)
to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\alpha^{\prime}}(t)
; so for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
,
\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{\alpha}(t)\right|=\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}(t)\right|.

Thus, we have

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t)\right|\right)=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(t)\right|\right)+(q-1)\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}(t)\right|\right)

By doing a calculation similar to that of the Equation (
3.6
), we can show that

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}(t)\right|\right)&=&1+\frac{\binom{n}{t}(q-1)^{t}-1}{q^{n-k}}\\[5.69046pt]
&=&1-\frac{1}{q^{n-k}}+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(t)\right|\right).\end{array}

and so

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}(t)\right|\right)=q\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(t)\right|\right)+(q-1)\left(1-\tfrac{1}{q^{n-k}}\right).

Finally, the probability that the reduction succeeds is

1-\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{e}}}\left(\alpha=0\right)=1-\frac{1}{q+\tfrac{(q-1)(1-1/q^{n-k})}{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(t)\right|\right)}}\geqslant\frac{q-1}{q}.

∎

5
Stern’s algorithm in projective space

In Section
4
, we gave a reduction of decoding to low weight codeword searching. In this section, we address the second problem, that is given a parity-check matrix
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k-1)\times n}
of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, we want to find
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t
and
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{0}}
.

We have to be careful about the distribution of
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}
which has not been drawn uniformly at random in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k-1)\times n}
. Indeed, for an error
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
of weight
t
,
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}
has been drawn such that
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
is a codeword in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, so
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}
verifies
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}={\mathbf{0}}
.

Essentially, our method consists in running a Stern procedure in the projective space
{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}
. In particular, we show that Peters’ improvements of Stern
[
Pet11
, Ch. 6]
are still applicable in the projective space.

5.1
The algorithm

When the syndrome is zero, Stern’s algorithm essentially consists in finding pairs
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}
such that
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
(resp.
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
) is of weight
p
on its first
\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor
(resp. last
k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor
) positions, zero elsewhere and

{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{1}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{2}

(5.1)

where
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}
are the
\ell
first rows of
{\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime-1}_{J}{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}_{I}
. Each pair
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
that collides gives a candidate codeword
{\mathbf{e}}
defined by

{\mathbf{e}}_{I}={\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;{\mathbf{e}}_{J}={\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{x}}_{2}).

(5.2)

One can remark that if the pair
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
is a solution to the collision search, then for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
,
\alpha{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
\alpha{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
also collide.

Remark 1
.

Note that this trick is specific to the fact that the syndrome is zero. If the syndrome is non-zero, then given a pair
({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2})
that is such that
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
, we can no longer guarantee that for any non-zero
\alpha
, we still have
\alpha{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}=\alpha{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
. That is the reason why the reduction in Section
4
is essential.

Moreover,
\alpha{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
\alpha{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
respectively share the same support as
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
so the Stern procedure enumerates all the collinear equivalents of
{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
and consequently, it explores all the candidate codewords that are collinear to
{\mathbf{e}}
. However, we only need one of them. Indeed, if
{\mathbf{e}}
is in
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
but not in
\mathcal{C}
– that is
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{0}}
but
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}\neq{\mathbf{0}}
– then there is a unique
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that the syndrome
\alpha{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}
is exactly
{\mathbf{s}}
and not a multiple of it. The solution
\alpha{\mathbf{e}}
can be found from any vector that is collinear to
{\mathbf{e}}
and so, we only need to find one of them.

From the discussion above, we remark that when the syndrome is zero, Stern’s algorithm can be run in the projective space. For a space
\mathcal{E}
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the projective space
{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathcal{E}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}
is the quotient set of
\mathcal{E}
by the
equivalence relation

\sim
:

\forall{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{E},\;\;\;{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{y}}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*},\;{\mathbf{x}}=\alpha{\mathbf{y}}.

(5.3)

The
equivalence class
of a vector
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{E}
is denoted by:

\left[{\mathbf{x}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{E}\;:\;{\mathbf{y}}\sim{\mathbf{x}}\right\}.

(5.4)

And so

{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathcal{E}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\left[{\mathbf{x}}\right]\;:\;{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{E}\right\}.

(5.5)

Now, if
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}
are such that
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{1}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
then we also have
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
where
\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]
and
\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
live in
{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}
.
But we have to note that if
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
then we do not necessarily have
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{1}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{2}
. So we need to choose representatives
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\in\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]
and
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}\in\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
which guarantee

{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}

(5.6)

To do that, we distinguish a particular class representative:

Definition 1

(Particular class representative)
.

Let
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\ell\times(k+1)}
. For all
\left[{\mathbf{x}}\right]\in{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}
, if
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{0}}
then the vector
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}
is any representative of
\left[{\mathbf{x}}\right]
, otherwise it is the unique representative of
\left[{\mathbf{x}}\right]
such that the first non-zero symbol of
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}}
is
1
.

Lemma 1

For any
{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}
,

{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]\;\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\;{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}.

(5.7)

Proof.

If
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{1}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{2}={\mathbf{0}}
then the proof is trivial. Otherwise, for either
i=1
or
2
,
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right]
is made of all the vectors that are collinear to
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
. Thus, all the elements in
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right]
share the same support, in particular they have the same first non-zero position, and there is a unique vector in
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right]
for which this first non-zero position contains a
1
. So, we first notice that
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
and
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}
exist and they are unique.

Assume
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
. That means
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
. On another hand, the first non-zero symbol in
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
is a one and the only element of this kind in
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
is
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}
, so we necessarily have
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}
.

Conversely,
{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}\;\Rightarrow\;\left[{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\right]=\left[{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}\right]\;\Rightarrow\;{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]={\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]
.

∎

5.2
Reducing the cost of Gaussian elimination

For large
q
and
p
,
the Gaussian elimination step is negligible and so, we can afford to perform it on
n-k-1
columns drawn independently at each iteration. Thus, Gaussian elimination needs
(n-k-1)^{2}(n+k+2)
operations. However, in the context of
SDitH
, we are far away from this regime and the Gaussian elimination is actually one of the most expensive operation we have to perform. In this sub-section, we present two modifications of our original projective Stern algorithm that allow to reduce the impact of the Gaussian elimination step. Those tricks are inspired by
[
Pet10
]
and
[
BLP08
]
and have been adapted to our situation.

Factorizing the Gaussian elimination step.
An iteration of Algorithm
2
begins with selecting an information set
I
and a window of size
\ell
. Let denote by
I_{1}
(resp.
I_{2}
) the first half of
I
(resp. the second half of
I
) and
J_{\ell}
the
\ell
first positions of
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket\setminus I
. The iteration succeeds in finding the particular error vector
{\mathbf{e}}
of weight
t
if it verifies

\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{I_{1}}\right|=\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{I_{2}}\right|=p\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{J_{\ell}}\right|=0.

(5.8)

To save some Gaussian elimination steps, we can test several partitions
(I_{1},I_{2},J_{\ell})
for one given information set. In other words, the main loop in Algorithm
2
can be divided into an
outer loop
and an
inner loop
. The
outer loop
consists in selecting an information set
I
and performing a Gaussian elimination on it (steps
2
-
2
). The
inner loop
starts by partitioning
I
into
(I_{1},I_{2})
and selecting a window
J_{\ell}\subset J
of size
\ell
, then it performs the steps
2
-
2
with

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}

The rows of
{\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}
indexed by
J_{\ell}

(5.9)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\;:\;\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right]\in{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}\text{ and }\mathrm{supp}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right)\subseteq I_{1}\text{ and }\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right|=p\right\}

(5.10)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\{{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{2}}\;:\;\left[{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right]\in{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}\text{ and }\mathrm{supp}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right)\subseteq I_{2}\text{ and }\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\right|=p\right\}

For a given information set
I
, we choose the partition
(I_{1},I_{2},J_{\ell})
uniformly at random and independently from one iteration to another. Assuming we are looking for a
t
-weight codeword
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
that verifies

\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{I}\right|=2p
, then the success probability of finding this particular codeword during an iteration of the inner loop is

q_{\mathrm{in}}=\dfrac{\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\binom{n-k-1-\ell}{t-2p}}{\binom{k+1}{2p}\binom{n-k-1}{t-2p}}.

(5.12)

So the number of trials needed to get
{\mathbf{e}}
follows a geometric distribution of parameter
q_{\mathrm{in}}
and so, by iterating
N_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}
times the inner loop, we will find
{\mathbf{e}}
with probability

p_{\mathrm{in}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}1-(1-q_{\mathrm{in}})^{N_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}}

(5.13)

Note that taking

N_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{q_{\mathrm{in}}}

(5.14)

allows to achieve a success probability
p_{\mathrm{in}}
for the inner loop that is exponentially close to
1
.

Reusing pivots in the Gaussian elimination.
In
[
CC98
]
, Canteaut and Chabaud propose to simplify the Gaussian elimination step by changing only one index in the information set
I
. Thus, only one pivot is necessary from one iteration of the outer loop to another. This idea is generalized in
[
BLP08
]
where this time, the number of columns to eliminate from one iteration to another can be greater than
1
. By doing this, we reduce the cost of Gaussian elimination but we also induce some dependencies between the selected information sets that impact the number of iterations of the outer loop that is needed.

To estimate the impact of the technique described above, we lean on the analysis in
[
BLP08
,
Pet10
]
. We first introduce the parameter
c
which represents the number of columns to eliminate in each iteration
4
4

4

In
[
BLP08
]
, another parameter
r
is introduced but its interest is only for small field.
. Thus, the cost of Gaussian elimination per iteration of the outer loop is

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{Gauss}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{c}(n-k-1)(k+1+i)

(5.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c(n-k-1)(2k+c+3)

(5.16)

Note that if a
t
-weight codeword
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is such that
\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{I}\right|=2p
, then the corresponding iteration of the outer loop will find a representative of
\left[{\mathbf{e}}\right]
with probability
p_{\mathrm{in}}
. So we need to count the average number of iterations of the outer loop that we need for having this particular weight distribution. However, there are some dependencies between the iterations that must be taken into account. Indeed, we do not draw the
k+1
positions of the information set independently from one iteration to another (
k+1-c
positions are kept).

The situation can be modeled by a
(t+2)
-state absorbing Markov chain. Given a
t
-weight codeword
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, let
X_{i}
be the random variable that represents the weight of
{\mathbf{e}}_{I}
at iteration
i\in\mathbb{N}
of the outer loop or “
\mathsf{Done}
” if the previous iteration succeeds. For the first iteration, the information set
I
is chosen uniformly at random as a subset of
\left\llbracket 1,n\right\rrbracket
of size
k
. So the distribution of
X_{0}
is given by

\forall v\in\left\llbracket 0,t\right\rrbracket,\;\;\;\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}=v\right)=\frac{\binom{k+1}{v}\binom{n-k-1}{t-v}}{\binom{n}{t}}\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}=\mathsf{Done}\right)=0.

(5.17)

Let
\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}
be the transition matrix of the Markov chain. It is defined as the following stochastic matrix:

\forall(u,v)\in\{\mathsf{Done},0,\cdots,t\}^{2},\;\;\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[u,v]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{i+1}=v\;|\;X_{i}=u\right)

(5.18)

The state
\mathsf{Done}
is the absorbing state, that means when we are in this state, we cannot get out anymore. So we have

\forall v\in\left\llbracket 0,t\right\rrbracket,\;\;\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[\mathsf{Done},v]=0\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[\mathsf{Done},\mathsf{Done}]=1.

(5.19)

From an iteration to another, the information set
I
is updated by swapping
c
indexes drawn uniformly at random in
I
with
c
indexes drawn uniformly at random in
J
. So an iteration moves from state
u
to state
v
with probability

\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[u,v]=\sum_{j}\dfrac{\binom{u}{j}\binom{k+1-u}{c-j}\binom{t-u}{v-u+j}\binom{n-k-1-t+u}{c-v+u-j}}{\binom{k+1}{c}\binom{n-k-1}{c}}

(5.20)

except for
u=2p
because then the algorithm succeeds with probability:

\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[2p,\mathsf{Done}]=p_{\mathrm{in}}.

(5.21)

So for all
v\in\left\llbracket 0,t\right\rrbracket
:

\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[2p,v]=\left(1-p_{\mathrm{in}}\right)\cdot\sum_{j}\dfrac{\binom{2p}{j}\binom{k+1-2p}{c-j}\binom{t-2p}{v-2p+j}\binom{n-k-1-t+2p}{c-v+2p-j}}{\binom{k+1}{c}\binom{n-k-1}{c}}.

(5.22)

Finally, to determine the number of iterations needed to get the first success, one only has to compute the fundamental matrix associated to
\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}
:

\boldsymbol{\rm F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}

(5.23)

where
{\mathbf{I}}_{t+1}
is the identity matrix of size
t+1
and
\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi^{\prime}}
is the
(t+1)\times(t+1)
sub-matrix of
\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}
such that

\forall(u,v)\in\{0,\cdots,t\}^{2},\;\;\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi^{\prime}}[u,v]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\rm\Pi}[u,v].

(5.24)

Then, the average number of iterations of the outer loop needed to find a representative of
\left[{\mathbf{e}}\right]
is

N_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}=\sum_{u=0}^{t}\sum_{v=0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{0}=v\right)\boldsymbol{\rm F}[u,v].

(5.25)

Finding one solution from many.
With
N_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}\cdot N_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}
repetitions of the inner loop, we are able to find one particular
t
-weight projective codeword
\left[{\mathbf{e}}\right]\in{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathcal{C}^{\prime}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}
. But there is potentially more than one such projective codeword since this number is

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{sol}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}}\left(\left|\left\{\left[{\mathbf{e}}\right]\in{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}\;:\;\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t\text{ and }{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\left[{\mathbf{e}}\right]=\left[{\mathbf{0}}\right]\right\}\right|\right)

(5.26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\binom{n}{t}(q-1)^{t-1}-1}{q^{n-k-1}}

(5.27)

Because we want to find only one solution from the
N_{\mathrm{sol}}
ones, we actually need to approximately iterate the outer loop
N_{\mathrm{out}}
times and for each iteration of the outer loop, we iterate the inner loop
N_{\mathrm{in}}
times where

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{out}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\max\left(1,\frac{N_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}}{N_{\mathrm{sol}}}\right)

(5.28)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\max\left(1,N_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}\cdot\min\left(1,\frac{N_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{tmp}}}{N_{\mathrm{sol}}}\right)\right).

(5.29)

5.3
Complexity of our projective Stern decoding

Finally, considering the modifications of the previous sub-section and using the implementation tricks of Peters
[
Pet10
]
, we are able to state the following Theorem
5.1
that gives the complexity of Stern’s algorithm in projective space.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be such that
\left|{\mathbf{e}}\right|=t
. On average over the choice of
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k-1)\times n}
that is such that
{\mathbf{H}}^{\prime}\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}={\mathbf{0}}
, we can solve the low weight codeword search problem
\textsf{SD}({\mathbf{H}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{0}},t)
with a running time of order

T_{\mathrm{Stern-proj}}=N_{\mathrm{out}}\Big(T_{\mathrm{Gauss}}+N_{\mathrm{in}}\big(T_{\mathrm{lists}}+T_{\mathrm{check}}\big)\Big)

(5.30)

where
N_{\mathrm{out}}
,
N_{\mathrm{in}}
and
T_{\mathrm{Gauss}}
are given by Equations (
5.28
), (
5.29
) and (
5.16
), and

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}(q-1)^{p-1}

(5.31)

\displaystyle L_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}(q-1)^{p-1}

(5.32)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{lists}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell\left(k+2p-1+2\left(L_{1}+L_{2}\right)\right)

(5.33)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{collisions}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(q-1)L_{1}L_{2}}{q^{\ell}}

(5.34)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{check}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2p+\tfrac{q}{q-1}(t-2p+1)2p\left(1+\tfrac{q-2}{q-1}\right)\right)\cdot N_{\mathrm{collision}}

(5.35)

Proof.

According to the Sub-section
5.2
, we have to iterate
N_{\mathrm{out}}
times the outer loop which consists in a Gaussian elimination over
c
columns and
N_{\mathrm{in}}
iterations of the inner loop. All that remains is to determine the cost of one iteration of the inner loop. To perform this iteration optimally, we will use Peters’ implementation tricks
[
Pet10
]
.

To build the lists
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{L}}_{2}
, we need to define another representative of an equivalence class in
{\raisebox{1.49994pt}{$\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}$}\mathord{\left/\raisebox{-1.49994pt}{$\sim$}\right.}}
. Let
{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k+1}
, we denote by
\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}
, the representative of
\left[{\mathbf{x}}\right]
whose first non-zero symbol is
1
. Thus, for
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
, we produce successively the representatives
\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
that have a weight
p
on the first half and zero elsewhere using exactly the same trick as Peters (except we fix the first non-zero symbol to
1
and that there is no syndrome to add). So we can compute successively
{\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
by only adding one column or two consecutive columns (except for the first element that needs
2p-2
column additions). The single column additions allow to browse all the vectors of a same support and the two columns additions allow to move from one support to another. The two columns additions can actually be replaced by single column additions if we pre-compute all the sums of two consecutive columns in
{\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}
. This costs
k+1
column additions. By fixing the first non zero symbol to one, we browse only one representative per equivalence class. However, it is not the good representative that we defined in Definition
1
. But it is quite easy to find the factor
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}=\alpha\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
by multiplying one column by a scalar. Then, we do not compute
\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}
but we save the pair
(\alpha,\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}})
instead.
We proceed similarly for the list
{\mathscr{L}}_{2}
. So finally, the cost of producing
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{L}}_{2}
is

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{lists}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell(k+1)+\ell(2p-2)+2\ell\left(\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}+\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\right)(q-1)^{p-1}

(5.36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell\left(k+2p-1+2\left(\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}+\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\right)(q-1)^{p-1}\right)

(5.37)

To deal with the collisions we first need to count them.
On average, there are

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{collision}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}(q-1)^{2p-2}}{1+(q^{\ell}-1)/(q-1)}

(5.39)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\cdot\left((q-1)\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}={\mathbf{0}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left({\mathbf{P}}^{\prime}\overline{{\mathbf{x}}_{1}}\neq{\mathbf{0}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}(q-1)^{2p-2}}{1+(q^{\ell}-1)/(q-1)}\cdot\left(\frac{q-1}{q^{\ell}}+1-\frac{1}{q^{\ell}}\right)

(5.40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\binom{\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}\binom{k+1-\lfloor\frac{k+1}{2}\rfloor}{p}(q-1)^{2p-1}}{q^{\ell}}

(5.41)

For each collision, we must first apply the scalar multiplication to change the representative of the equivalence class. This costs
2p
multiplications. Then we can apply the same trick as Peters and check for only
\frac{q}{q-1}(t-2p+1)
rows on average. The cost to treat a row is
2p
additions and
2p\frac{q-2}{q-1}
multiplications. So the cost to check all the candidates (each coming from a collision) is

T_{\mathrm{check}}=\left(2p+\tfrac{q}{q-1}(t-2p+1)2p\left(1+\tfrac{q-2}{q-1}\right)\right)\cdot N_{\mathrm{collision}}.

(5.42)

∎

6
The
d
-split decoding problem

The security of
SDitH
is actually based on the
d
-split decoding problem. Before stating this problem, let us bring in the following notation:

Notation 6.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. For
d
that divides
n
and for all
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
, we denote by
{\mathbf{v}}_{[i]}
the
i^{\text{th}}
piece of
{\mathbf{v}}
of length
\frac{n}{d}
. More formally,

{\mathbf{v}}_{[i]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}{\mathbf{v}}_{\left\llbracket(i-1)\frac{n}{d}+1,i\frac{n}{d}\right\rrbracket}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}(v_{j})_{j\in\left\llbracket(i-1)\frac{n}{d}+1,i\frac{n}{d}\right\rrbracket}

(6.1)

Then the
d
-split syndrome decoding problem can be stated as follows:

Problem 6.2

(
d
-split Syndrome Decoding
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
)
.

Given a matrix
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
, a syndrome
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n-k}
and a distance
t\in\left\llbracket 0,n\right\rrbracket
, find a vector
{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{e}}={\mathbf{s}}
and
\forall i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
,
\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{[i]}\right|=t/d
.

The
d
-split decoding problem is quite similar to the standard decoding problem but with the additional constraint that the error weight must be regularly distributed over
d
blocks. Note that the syndrome
{\mathbf{s}}
was actually produced using an injected solution
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
whose weight is precisely regularly distributed. In other word, there exists at least one
\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{H}}\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}
and
\forall i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
,
\left|\widetilde{{\mathbf{e}}}_{[i]}\right|=t/d
.

In the
SDitH
specifications, the hardness of the
d
-split syndrome decoding problem is lower bounded by a quantity that depends on the complexity to solve the standard decoding problem. This lower bound is based on the following theorem:

Theorem 6.3

(
[
FJR22
]
)

Let
{\mathbf{H}}
be drawn uniformly at random in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and let
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
If an algorithm can find any particular solution of the
d
-split syndrome decoding problem
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
in time
T
with probability
\varepsilon_{d}
, then there is an algorithm that can find any particular solution of the syndrome decoding problem
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
in time
T
with probability
\varepsilon_{1}
with

\varepsilon_{1}\geqslant\frac{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}}{\binom{n}{t}}\varepsilon_{d}

(6.2)

Using Theorem
6.3
, it can be argued that the best average complexity to solve
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
cannot be lower than
{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}}/{\binom{n}{t}}
times the best average complexity to solve
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
.
SDitH
measures the difficulty of the
d
-split decoding problem with this lower bound on the complexity
together with the complexity of the best known attack on
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
. However, this bound is not tight and gives optimistic security levels. Indeed, it is considered here that we are only looking for a particular solution; the number of solutions to the problem is not taken into account. But recall that if there are many solutions, we just want to find one of them. We therefore state the following theorem that gives a more precise lower bound on the complexity we can expect to achieve:

Theorem 6.4

Let
{\mathbf{H}}
be drawn uniformly at random in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
and let
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
If an algorithm can solve
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
in time
T
with probability
p_{d}
, then there is an algorithm that can solve
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
in time
T
with probability
p_{1}
with

p_{d}\leqslant 1-\left(1-\frac{\binom{n}{t}}{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}}\left(1-(1-p_{1})^{1/N_{1}}\right)\right)^{N_{d}}\approx\frac{\binom{n}{t}}{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}}\frac{N_{d}}{N_{1}}p_{1}

(6.3)

where
N_{1}
is the expected number of solutions to the problem
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
and
N_{d}
is the expected number of solutions to the problem
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathscr{A}}_{d}
be an algorithm which finds a particular solution in
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
in times
T
with probability
\varepsilon_{d}
. From Theorem
6.3
, there exists an algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}_{1}
which finds a particular solution in
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
in times
T
with probability
\varepsilon_{1}
with

\begin{array}[]{lcl}1-\left(1-\varepsilon_{d}\right)^{N_{d}}&\leqslant&1-\left(1-\frac{\binom{n}{t}}{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}}\varepsilon_{1}\right)^{N_{d}}\end{array}

We end the proof by noticing that

p_{d}=1-\left(1-\varepsilon_{d}\right)^{N_{d}}\;\;\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\;\;p_{1}=1-\left(1-\varepsilon_{1}\right)^{N_{1}}

∎

In Theorem
6.4
, since we address the
d
-split syndrome decoding problem where
{\mathbf{s}}
is the syndrome of a
d
-split error vector of weight
t
, we have:

N_{d}=1+\frac{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}(q-1)^{t}-1}{q^{n-k}}.

(6.4)

Moreover, the algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}_{1}
consists essentially in repeating the algorithm
{\mathscr{A}}_{d}
by permuting the code randomly so this algorithm solves the standard decoding problem where
{\mathbf{s}}
is the syndrome of any
t
-weight error vector. So we have:

N_{1}=1+\frac{\binom{n}{t}^{d}(q-1)^{t}-1}{q^{n-k}}.

(6.5)

Remark 2
.

Note that when
t
is smaller than the Gilbert-Varshamov distance, then the only solution to
\textsf{SD}(1,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
is the injected solution and has a probability
{\binom{n/d}{t/d}^{d}}/{\binom{n}{t}}
to be a solution for
\textsf{SD}(d,{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{s}},t)
. So we get the same lower bound as in
SDitH
since
p_{1}\approx\varepsilon_{1}
and
p_{d}\approx\varepsilon_{d}
. On the contrary, if
t
is such that we have many solutions, then the injected solution has little impact and so we only have
p_{d}\leqslant p_{1}(1+\mathop{}\mathopen{}o\mathopen{}\left(1\right))
. Note that when the number of solutions is large,
p_{d}
is close to
p_{1}
because it is simpler to find a particular solution to the
d
-split decoding problem but there are also less solutions in proportion.

Adapting the projective Stern algorithm for d-split.
Theorem
6.4
induces a lower bound on the complexity of
d
-split decoding, but we cannot guarantee that it is actually possible to reach this bound. It is possible to give an actual algorithm to solve the
d
-split decoding problem. Indeed, we can apply the reduction of Section
4
and adapt our projective Stern algorithm to take into account the regularity of the weight of the solution we are looking for. More precisely, at each iteration of Algorithm
2
, one can choose the information set as

I\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{def}}}{{=}}I_{1}\cup\dots\cup I_{d}

(6.6)

where each
I_{i}
is a subset of
\left\llbracket(i-1)\frac{n}{d}+1,i\frac{n}{d}\right\rrbracket
of size
\frac{k}{d}
. At each iteration, we bet that at least one sought solution
{\mathbf{e}}
is such that for all
i\in\left\llbracket 1,d\right\rrbracket
,
\left|{\mathbf{e}}_{I_{i}}\right|=\frac{p}{d}
.

Note that, in the context of
SDitH
, when adapting projective Stern to
d
-split, the optimal parameter
p
increases: it goes from
1
to
2
. So the cost to produce the lists
{\mathscr{L}}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{L}}_{2}
increases quadratically. Consequently, the Gaussian elimination step becomes negligible, especially if we factorize it. It follows that the Canteaut-Chabaud technique is not relevant because it increases the number of iterations but it does not substantially reduce their cost. This is why finally, for
d>1
, we do not use the Canteaut-Chabaud’s trick.
Appendix
0.A
, gives the formulas for the
2
-split projective Stern algorithm.
