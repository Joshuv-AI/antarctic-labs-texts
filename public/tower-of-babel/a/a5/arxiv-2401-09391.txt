Different theoretical aspects of the intrinsic decoherence in the Milburn formalism
S. V. Mousavi, S. Miret-Artés

Abstract
In this work, we consider different theoretical aspects and simple applications of the Milburn equation which is governed by a parameter controlling what is known as intrinsic decoherence. The main goal is to show some similarities also observed when external decoherence is considered. Linear entropy, Ehrenfest relations, probability density current, the Wigner representation as well as the relation to a Lindbladian master equation are analyzed in terms of this intrinsic decoherence, leading to new insights on the Milburn dynamics. Interference of two wave packets, tunneling and the bouncing ball problem are also studied under this perspective.



Keywords
: Intrinsic decoherence; Linear entropy; Ehrenfest relations; Arrival time; Wigner function; Tunneling; Bouncing ball.

I
Introduction

Quantum mechanics is a linear theory, and thus it admits coherent superposition of distinct physical states.
This property is the basis of the measurement problem in this theory. However, this superposition principle does
not act at the macroscopic scale, although nothing shows this in the formulation of the quantum mechanics.
The problem of the standard quantum mechanics is to explain non-observability of macroscopic distinct states.
Two routes have been tackled in the literature to overcome this problem. First, the role played by the environment.
In this way, the whole system, the system of interest and the environment, is considered a
closed system evolving on the standard Schrödinger equation. Then, by integrating out the environment
degrees of freedom, an equation known as the master equation is derived for the reduced density matrix of
the system of interest. This formalism is known as environment-induced decoherence started by the work of
Zeh
Ze-FP-1970
and developed by Zurek
Zu-RMP-2003
. Decoherence is important, not only from
the foundational point of view but also in the realm of quantum information theory, since quantum
information processing and quantum technologies depend on our ability in creation, maintenance and
manipulation of non-classical superposed states
Sc-PR-2019
.
In the second approach, the Schrödinger equation is itself modified by adding some additional terms which
induce decoherence within the quantum mechanics itself. Among the various models have been proposed in
this regard, the famous one is maybe the model of Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, the so-called GRW
model
GRW-PRD-1986
. In another approach, decoherence has been related to the gravity effects
ElMoNa-PLB-1989
.

Over the years, several different paths have been taken to break up with the standard quantum mechanics; some of the well-known attempts include the postulation of extra nonlinear terms or stochastic terms in the Schrödinger equation; and gravitational effects invalidates this equation
St-PTRSA-2012
.
Several years ago, Milburn
Mi-PRA-1991
modified the von Neumann equation of the density operator
in a way to include the decoherence, what is known as intrinsic decoherence. He postulated that, at
sufficiently short time steps, the system does not evolve continuously under unitary evolution but
by a stochastic sequence of identical unitary transformations. This means that a minimum time step in
the universe is introduced. The inverse of this time step is the mean frequency of the unitary
steps. The system is then considered isolated and described by a state whose evolution is governed
by a generalized von Neumann equation having this mean frequency as a parameter.
For free particles, it can be seen that the Milburn equation is a Lindbladian master equation,
where the only Lindblad operator is proportional to the square of linear momentum
MoMi-JPA-2022
.
It is notable that in studying a Jaynes-Cummings model with phase damping, Kuang et. al
Kuetal-PRA-1997
have introduced a master equation describing phase damping under the Markovian approximation which has the same form as the Milburn equation.
If the frequency of time steps is sufficiently high, evolution in the experimental time-scales seems
approximately continuous. In the zeroth order of the inverse of this frequency, the usual von Neumann equation is recovered. The first order
correction yields decoherence in the energy eigenbasis. One aspect of this model is that constants of motion
in the standard quantum mechanics approach remain constants of motion of the model and thus, stationary
states remain stationary. Dynamics of open quantum systems has been considered within the Milburn
framework
BuKo-PRA-1998
. Furthermore, a Lorentz invariant model of intrinsic decoherence has
been presented
Mi-NJP-2006
.

The Milburn equation has attracted many attentions within the quantum information community in recent years.
Effect of the intrinsic decoherence on quantum communications and various quantum correlations have been
studied
LiShZo-PLA-2010
;
JaNa-IJQI-2016
;
ZhZh-EPJD-2017
;
NaJa-IJTP-2019
;
AbMu-PS-2021
;
EsKhMaDa-OQE-2022
;
NaMuCh-PA-2022
;
OuChMa-IJTP-2023
;
KhMu-APB-2023
;
ChMa-APB-2023
.
Decaying dynamics of a displaced harmonic oscillator
UrMC-Pramana-2022
and in the mirror-field
interaction
UrMC-arxiv-2023
have also been studied within this framework.
In spite of these studies, only two works, to the best of our knowledge, have applied the Milburn equation in
the coordinate representation: (i) Milburn himself investigated, in his original paper, the effect of the
intrinsic decoherence on the interference fringes of the position probability density for a harmonic oscillator
prepared in an initial superposition state of two coherent states
Mi-PRA-1991
and (ii) this equation has been
used by the same authors in the context of the quantum backflow
MoMi-JPA-2022
. Thus, we think the
Milburn equation deserves further attention by analyzing different theoretical aspects. In this paper, we are going
to examine this equation in the coordinate representation.
Since, we sought to examine the first order correction to the customary von Neumann equation throughout the work, it is the first order approximation of the Milburn’s equation that is used.

This work is organized as follows. Section II discusses some new aspects of the Milburn equation and the role played
by the intrinsic decoherence by analyzing linear entropy, Ehrenfest relations, the probability current density, arrival
times, the Wigner representation, the relation to a Lindbladian master equation
and transmission through a localized barrier. In section III, three simple problems are analyzed by comparing
the von Neumann and Milburn dynamics: interference of two Gaussian wave packet, tunneling through a
rectangular barrier and the so-called bouncing ball problem. Finally, in the last section, some conclusions are
reported.

II
Intrinsic decoherence in the Milburn formalism. Connection to the Wigner representation and the Lindbladian formalism

The intrinsic decoherence within the Milburn formalism is based on the equation of motion of the density operator
Mi-PRA-1991
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left(e^{-i\hat{H}/\hbar\gamma}\hat{\rho}~e^{i\hat{H}/\hbar\gamma}-\hat{\rho}\right).

(1)

In this equation of motion,
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian of the system and
\gamma
a parameter which is the inverse
of the minimum time step in the universe responsible for a stochastic sequence of identical unitary transformations
at sufficiently short time steps.
Expansion of Eq. (
1
) up to first order in
\gamma^{-1}
reads as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}[\hat{H},[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}]],

(2)

which reduces to the well-known von Neumann equation in the limit
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
.
In the energy representation, this equation can be written

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}(E-E^{\prime})\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t)-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t),

(3)

\{|E\rangle\}
being the energy eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}|E\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E|E\rangle,

(4)

where
\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t)=\langle E|\hat{\rho}(t)|E^{\prime}\rangle
are the density matrix elements in the energy eigenbasis. Eq. (
3
)
is easily solved leading to

\displaystyle\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}(E-E^{\prime})t-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}(E-E^{\prime})^{2}t\right]\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(0).

(5)

Thus, for the density operator one has that

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t)|E\rangle\langle E^{\prime}|

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})t/\hbar-(E-E^{\prime})^{2}t/(2\hbar^{2}\gamma)}\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(0)|E\rangle\langle E^{\prime}|,

(7)

which shows that if the initial state is an energy eigenstate then it does not evolve in time i.e., stationary states
of the von Neumann equation remains so in the Milburn framework.
Note that sums over
E
and
E^{\prime}
turn into integrals when the energy spectrum is continuous.
From Eq. (
6
), in the position representation one has that

\displaystyle\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})t/\hbar-(E-E^{\prime})^{2}t/(2\hbar^{2}\gamma)}\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(0)u_{E}(x)u_{E^{\prime}}^{*}(x^{\prime}),

(8)

where the corresponding diagonal elements of the density operator give us the probability density

\displaystyle P(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})t/\hbar-(E-E^{\prime})^{2}t/(2\hbar^{2}\gamma)}\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(0)u_{E}(x)u_{E^{\prime}}^{*}(x),

(9)

where
u_{E}(x)=\langle x|E\rangle
. Now, if the initial state is considered to be a pure state

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|,

(10)

then

\displaystyle P(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})t/\hbar-(E-E^{\prime})^{2}t/(2\hbar^{2}\gamma)}C_{E}C^{*}_{E^{\prime}}u_{E}(x)u_{E^{\prime}}^{*}(x),

(11)

where the expansion coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C_{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle E|\psi_{0}\rangle=\int dx~u^{*}_{E}(x)\psi_{0}(x).

(12)

Due to the time-dependent damping exponential factor, the
long time behavior
of the probability density is
given by

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}P(x,t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{E}|C_{E}|^{2}|u_{E}(x)|^{2}

(13)

which is independent on time. In this limit, non-diagonal elements of density matrix decay
exponentially. This is the way the intrinsic decoherence works in the Milburn framework. It is a gradual and continuous process
depending on the
\gamma^{-1}
value. This behavior is the same observed for the external decoherence.

In the context of the standard quantum mechanics, when the Hamiltonian of a many body system
has the additive form

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\hat{H}_{i},

(14)

a possible solution of the Schrödinger equation is written as a product of single particle states,

\displaystyle\Psi(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots x_{N},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pi_{i}\psi_{i}(x_{i},t),

(15)

i.e., provided that the state is in the product form in an instant of time, this property is maintained over the time
evolution. However, this is not valid in the context of the Milburn framework.
In fact, one can show that if the one-particle state
\hat{\rho}_{i}
evolves under the Milburn equation
with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{i}
, then the two-particle separable state

\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}
is not evolved under the Milburn equation with the
two-particle Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}
since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}_{1}}{\partial t}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}+\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}_{2}}{\partial t}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{1},\hat{\rho}_{1}]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}[\hat{H}_{1},[\hat{H}_{1},\hat{\rho}_{1}]]\right)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}+\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{2},\hat{\rho}_{2}]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}[\hat{H}_{2},[\hat{H}_{2},\hat{\rho}_{2}]]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2},\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}[\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2},[\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2},\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{2}]]+\frac{1}{\gamma\hbar^{2}}[\hat{H}_{1},\hat{\rho}_{1}]\otimes[\hat{H}_{2},\hat{\rho}_{2}].

The last term in the third line is responsible for this different behavior. Note that for convenience we have
written
\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}
instead of

\hat{H}_{1}\otimes\hat{\mathds{1}}_{2}+\hat{\mathds{1}}_{1}\otimes\hat{H}_{2}
;
\hat{\mathds{1}}_{i}

being the identity operator in the vector space of particle
i
.

II.1
Connection to the Wigner representation

The Wigner function is defined as the
partial
Fourier transform of the density matrix in the
(R,r)
representation with respect to the relative coordinate
r

MoMi-Sym-2023

\displaystyle W(R,u,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int dr~e^{-iur/\hbar}\rho(R,r,t).

(17)

From Eq. (
47
) one obtains an equation of motion for the Wigner function expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}W(R,u,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{u}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}W(R,u,t)+\int du^{\prime}~K(R,u^{\prime}-u,t)~W(R,u^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\frac{-1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\int du^{\prime}~\int dr~e^{-iur/\hbar}\left(\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R\partial r}+V(R+r/2)-V(R-r/2)\right)^{2}e^{iu^{\prime}r/\hbar}W(R,u^{\prime},t),

where the kernel of the first integral is defined as

\displaystyle K(R,q,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int dr~e^{iqr/\hbar}\frac{V(R/2+r)-V(R/2-r)}{\hbar}.

(19)

Note that the first line of Eq. (
) is the equation of motion for the Wigner distribution function within the von Neumann formalism; the second line is the contribution due to the intrinsic decoherence. As an illustration, let us consider now two simple examples, the motion for a free particle and under a linear potential or constant force.

For free particles, Eq. (
) is reduced to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}W(x,p,t)+\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}W(x,p,t)-\frac{p^{2}}{2m^{2}\gamma}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}W(x,p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(20)

Apart from the last term which is responsible for the intrinsic decoherence, this equation has the same formal expression as that of the classical Liouville equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(x,p,t)+\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f(x,p,t)+\dot{p}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}f(x,p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(21)

where
f(x,p,t)
is the phase space distribution function. Note that in this case
\dot{p}=0
and thus the last term of Eq. (
21
) is zero.
In the second example, the motion under the presence of a linear potential

\displaystyle V(x)=c_{1}~x,

(22)

one obtains from Eq. (
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}W(x,p,t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}W(x,p,t)+c_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}W(x,p,t)

(23)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\frac{p^{2}}{2m^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{c_{1}}{2m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-c_{1}\frac{p}{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}\right)W(x,p,t)=0.

which again, apart from the last term, one recovers the classical Liouville equation (
21
).

II.2
Connection to the Lindblad equation

In the context of open quantum systems by taking into account the role played by the environment one obtains the Lindbladian master equation
Sch-book-2007

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}^{\prime},\hat{\rho}(t)]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\kappa_{i}[\hat{L}_{i},[\hat{L}_{i},\hat{\rho}(t)]],

(24)

for the evolution of the reduced density operator describing the system of interest where we have
assumed Hermitian Lindblad operators
\hat{L}_{i}
and parameters
\kappa_{i}>0
. The so-called
Lamb-shifted Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{\prime}
is not generally identical to the self Hamiltonian
\hat{H}

of the system containing additional terms caused by the interaction with the environment.
For free particles with
\hat{H}^{\prime}=\hat{p}^{2}/2m
, this master equation with only one Lindblad operator reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m},\hat{\rho}(t)]-\frac{1}{2}\kappa[\hat{L},[\hat{L},\hat{\rho}(t)]].

(25)

Comparison of Eq. (
25
) with the Milburn equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m},\hat{\rho}(t)]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}[\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m},[\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m},\hat{\rho}(t)]],

(26)

for free particles shows that the Milburn equation (
26
) has the same mathematical structure
than the Lindbladian equation (
25
) with a Lindblad operator proportional to
\hat{p}^{2}
. Let us
consider two simple cases.
If we consider
\hat{L}=f(\hat{p})
,
f
being an arbitrary well-defined function, from Eq. (
25
)
in the momentum representation we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(p,p^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\frac{p^{2}-p^{\prime 2}}{2m}-\frac{1}{2}\kappa(f(p)-f(p^{\prime}))^{2}\right\}\rho(p,p^{\prime},t),

(27)

whose solution reads as

\displaystyle\rho(p,p^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\frac{p^{2}-p^{\prime 2}}{2m}-\frac{1}{2}\kappa(f(p)-f(p^{\prime}))^{2}\right)t\right]\rho(p,p^{\prime},0),

(28)

revealing the probability density
\rho(p,p,t)
is time independent.
Now, with the choice
\hat{L}=\hat{x}
, Eq. (
25
) can be recast as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{\prime 2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\kappa(x-x^{\prime})^{2}\right]\rho(x,x^{\prime},t),

(29)

in the coordinate representation being the environmental scattering
Sch-book-2007
equation of motion.
Again, comparison of the high temperature limit of the well-known Caldeira-Leggett master equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{\prime 2}}\right)-\lambda(x-x^{\prime})\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)-\frac{D}{\hbar^{2}}(x-x^{\prime})^{2}\right]\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

(30)

with Eq. (
29
) reveals that the former is Lindbladian only in the negligible dissipation limit i.e,
when the second term can be neglected. Note that
\lambda
is the relaxation rate or damping constant
and
D=2m\lambda k_{B}T_{e}
plays the role of the diffusion coefficient (
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant
and
T_{e}
is the temperature of the environment).

Finally, when comparing Eq. (
2
) to Eq.(
24
), one can see that the Milburn equation is just a master equation with the Hamiltonian as the only Lindblad operator.

III
First order approximation of Milburn’s equation. Some analytical results

By using the first order approximation of Milburn’s equation (
2
), in this section we analyze
the effect of the intrinsic decoherence on various important theoretical issues such as the linear entropy,
Ehrenfest relations, arrival time distribution and transmission probability through a localized barrier
providing some analytical results.

III.1
Linear entropy

Once it is well established this gradual intrinsic decoherence process, a standard quantity used in this context is the
so-called linear entropy
DoTaPaWa-PRA-1999

\displaystyle S_{L}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\text{tr}(\hat{\rho}(t)^{2})

(31)

being a measure of the mixedness of the state which is easier to calculate than the von
Neumann entropy. In other words, the second term of Eq. (
31
) quantifies the purity of
the state. Change of mixedness is a result of the non-unitary part of the evolution equation.
There are other applications beyond its main application as a measure of mixedness of the quantum state. For instance, in the field of quantum information theory, it has been used as a measure of quantum entanglement
BuBoBe-PRA-2007
.

Since the trace operation is a basis independent quantity, from Eq. (
1
), and writing the
density matrix elements in the energy representation as
\rho(E,E^{\prime},t)=\langle E|\hat{\rho}(t)|E^{\prime}\rangle
, one has that

\displaystyle\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{EE^{\prime}}(0)~\exp\left[\gamma t\left(e^{-i(E-E^{\prime})/\hbar\gamma}-1\right)\right],

(32)

and the linear entropy is expressed as

\displaystyle S_{L}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{E}~\langle E|\hat{\rho}(t)^{2}|E\rangle=1-\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}~\langle E|\hat{\rho}(t)|E^{\prime}\rangle\langle E^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}(t)|E\rangle

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}~\langle E|\hat{\rho}(0)|E^{\prime}\rangle\langle E^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}(0)|E\rangle\exp\left[-4\gamma t\sin^{2}\left(\frac{E-E^{\prime}}{2\hbar\gamma}\right)\right],

where in the second line of this equation we have used Eq. (
32
). Thus, linear entropy decays also exponentially in time.
Its short linear time behavior is then given by

\displaystyle S_{L}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle S_{L}(0)+4\gamma t\sum_{E}\sum_{E^{\prime}}~|\langle E|\hat{\rho}(0)|E^{\prime}\rangle|^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{E-E^{\prime}}{2\hbar\gamma}\right).

(34)

Here, we have used the Hermiticity property of the density operator.
This linear behaviour in time is a typical feature of master equations (Markov approximation)

Joosetal-book-2003
. Note that as we mentioned in subsection
II.2
,
the Milburn equation is a master equation with a single Lindblad operator taken to be the Hamiltonian
of the system.
The other interesting limit of linear entropy is its behavior when
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
, i.e., the limit of the standard
quantum mechanics. In this limit, to first order in
\gamma^{-1}
, one obtains from Eq. (
33
)

\displaystyle S_{L}(t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle S_{L}(0)+\frac{t}{\gamma\hbar^{2}}\text{tr}([\hat{H},\hat{\rho}(0)]^{2}).

(35)

Note that the operator
[\hat{H},\hat{\rho}(0)]
is anti-Hermitian and thus its eigenvalues are imaginary.
Thus, since the second term in Eq. (
35
) is negative, the intrinsic decoherence reduces
entropy following a linear behavior in time.

III.2
Ehrenfest relations

In the standard quantum mechanics, the Ehrenfest relations are

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{x}\rangle=\frac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle}{m}

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{p}\rangle=-\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\hat{x}}\right\rangle.

(37)

It is instructive to see the effect of the intrinsic decoherence on these relations.
From Eq. (
1
), one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left\langle e^{i\hat{H}/\hbar\gamma}\hat{A}e^{-i\hat{H}/\hbar\gamma}-\hat{A}\right\rangle

(38)

for the rate of change of the expectation value of an observable
\hat{A}
. To first order in
\gamma^{-1}
,
one has that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat{H},\hat{A}]\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\langle[\hat{H},[\hat{H},\hat{A}]]\rangle_{t}.

(39)

The rates of change of the expectation values of position and momentum operators are then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}}{m}-\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\hat{x}}\right\rangle_{t}

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\hat{x}}\right\rangle_{t}-\frac{1}{4m\gamma}\left\langle\left\{\hat{p},\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial\hat{x}^{2}}\right\}\right\rangle_{t},

(41)

where
\{\hat{A},\hat{B}\}=\hat{A}\hat{B}+\hat{B}\hat{A}
is the anti-commutator of observables
\hat{A}

and
\hat{B}
. It is assumed here that the interaction potential is only a function of the position operator i.e.,

V=V(\hat{x})
. These relations show that only for non-interacting (free) particles the first Ehrenfest relation
(
40
) has the same formal expression as in the standard QM while for potentials, at most linear
in the position operator, the second Ehrenfest relation (
41
) also does.
Then, one concludes that, only for free particles,
\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{t}
follows a classical trajectory. In fact, the
intrinsic decoherence does not affect the first moments
\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{t}
and
\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}

(
Mi-PRA-1991
)
.

As an illustration, let us consider the motion in a uniform gravity field for which
V(\hat{x})=mg\hat{x}
.
In this case, solutions of Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
) yield

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{t}\approx\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{0}+\left(\frac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}}{m}-\frac{g}{2\gamma}\right)t-\frac{1}{2}gt^{2},

(42)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}\approx\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}-mgt.

(43)

Note that while the expectation value of momentum is not affected by the intrinsic decoherence, the rate of change
of the expectation value of position is reduced by the amount of
g/2\gamma
.
In the upward motion of a projectile, the peak height is

\dfrac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}^{2}}{2m^{2}g}\left(1-\frac{mg}{\gamma\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}}\right)
.
Note that in order to reach this height, one needs
\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}>mg/\gamma
.
If a harmonic potential
V(\hat{x})=m\omega^{2}\hat{x}^{2}/2
is now considered, solutions of
Eqs. (
40
) and (
41
) are given by

\displaystyle\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{t}\approx e^{-\omega^{2}t/2\gamma}\left(\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{0}\cos(\omega t)+\frac{\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}}{m\omega}\sin(\omega t)\right),

(44)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{t}\approx e^{-\omega^{2}t/2\gamma}(-m\omega\langle\hat{x}\rangle_{0}\sin(\omega t)+\langle\hat{p}\rangle_{0}\cos(\omega t)).

(45)

Although the appearance of these equations suggests that the coefficient
\gamma
acts like a friction
coefficient, this perception is misleading. Since the Milburn correction cannot change the energy,
\gamma
cannot be the friction coefficient.
The decay of the first order moments is not due to friction but to phase diffusion
Mi-PRA-1991
;
Sa-PRA-1989
;
BaStPe-OC-1989
arising from the second term in Eq. (
2
).

III.3
First moments of the arrival time distribution

The Milburn equation (
2
) in the position representation reads as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{2m}(\partial_{x}^{2}-\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{2})+V(x)-V(x^{\prime})\right]\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

(46)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\left[\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{2m}(\partial_{x}^{2}-\partial_{x^{\prime}}^{2})+V(x)-V(x^{\prime})\right]^{2}\rho(x,x^{\prime},t).

This equation in the center of mass and relative coordinates, defined by
R=(x+x^{\prime})/2
and
r=x-x^{\prime}
, is
written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(R,r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R\partial r}+V(R+r/2)-V(R-r/2)\right]\rho(R,r,t)

(47)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\left[\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial R\partial r}+V(R+r/2)-V(R-r/2)\right]^{2}\rho(R,r,t),

which in the absence of interactions i.e., for free particles, it can be written as a continuity equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(R,r,t)+\frac{\partial}{\partial R}j(R,r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(48)

where we have introduced the probability current density matrix
j(R,r,t)
as

\displaystyle j(R,r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{i\hbar}{m}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\gamma m^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r\partial R}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\rho(R,r,t).

(49)

On the other hand, the density matrix in the position representation can be seen as the inverse Fourier transform
of its representation in the momentum space,

\displaystyle\rho(R,r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp^{\prime}e^{i(p-p^{\prime})R/\hbar}e^{i(p+p^{\prime})r/2\hbar}\langle p|\hat{\rho}(t)|p^{\prime}\rangle,

(50)

where, from the solution of Eq. (
2
) for free particles, we have that the matrix elements in this
representation are written as

\displaystyle\langle p|\hat{\rho}(t)|p^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\frac{p^{2}-p^{\prime 2}}{2m}t-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}\gamma}\left(\frac{p^{2}-p^{\prime 2}}{2m}\right)^{2}t\right]\langle p|\hat{\rho}(0)|p^{\prime}\rangle,

(51)

where the standard result is again reached when
\gamma^{-1}\rightarrow 0
.

By substituting now Eq. (
51
) into Eq. (
49
), the diagonal elements
of the probability current density are given by

\displaystyle J(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi m\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp^{\prime}e^{i(p-p^{\prime})x/\hbar}(p+p^{\prime})\left(1-i\frac{p^{2}-p^{\prime 2}}{4\hbar m\gamma}\right)\rho(p,p^{\prime},t).

(52)

Note that, since the density operator is Hermitian, one has that

\langle p|\hat{\rho}(t)|p^{\prime}\rangle^{*}=\langle p^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}(t)|p\rangle
which, as expected,
J(x,t)
is real.

As a particular case, let us see if the product of states in the context of the standard quantum mechanics is
kept in this formalism. If the wave function of a system is initially factorized as follows

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(x)\psi_{2}(y)\psi_{3}(z),

(53)

one sees that it will be preserved along time when the potential is additive such as

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{1}(x)+V_{2}(y)+V_{3}(z),

(54)

(including
V=0
). However, this result is not longer valid in the context of the Milburn equation. If one considers
free propagation, Eq. (
51
) explicitly shows, even if the initial state is factorized as

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p^{\prime}},0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(p_{x},p^{\prime}_{x},0)\rho_{2}(p_{y},p^{\prime}_{y},0)\rho_{3}(p_{z},p^{\prime}_{z},0),

(55)

that the exponential term
e^{-[(\mathbf{p}^{2}-\mathbf{p^{\prime}}^{2})/2m]^{2}t/2\hbar^{2}\gamma}
appears in
Eq. (
51
) and this factorization is not maintained at later times.

Within the framework of the Bohmian mechanics, it is proved that the
ideal
(measurement-independent)
arrival time distribution at the detector location
x_{d}
is uniquely given by the modulus of the probability
current density when starting from the continuity equation
Leavens

\displaystyle\Pi(x_{d},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|J(x_{d},t)|}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~|J(x_{d},t^{\prime})|}.

(56)

Here, we use this formalism to see how arrival times are affected by the intrinsic decoherence.
For a given state, the probability current density is positive for all times at the location of the detector
which, by assumption, is taken to be at the origin. Then, the normalized arrival time distribution is

\displaystyle\Pi(0,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J(0,t)}{\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~J(0,t^{\prime})}.

(57)

The mean arrival time at the detector location
x_{d}=0
and its variance are then given by

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~t^{\prime}~\Pi(0,t^{\prime}),

(58)

\displaystyle(\Delta t)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle t^{2}\rangle-\langle t\rangle^{2},

(59)

respectively.
The standard deviation and the square root of the variance can be used as a measure of the width of the arrival
time distribution, i.e., the rms width. Then, from Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
), we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~J(0,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv\frac{\rho(u,v,0)}{v},

(60)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~t^{\prime}~J(0,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{m\hbar}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv\frac{\rho(u,v,0)}{uv^{2}\left(1-\frac{iuv}{2m\hbar\gamma}\right)},

(61)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}~t^{\prime 2}~J(0,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{m^{2}\hbar^{2}}{i\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}du\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv\frac{\rho(u,v,0)}{u^{2}v^{3}\left(1-\frac{iuv}{2m\hbar\gamma}\right)^{3}},

(62)

where we have used the new variables

\displaystyle u=\frac{p+p^{\prime}}{2}

(63)

\displaystyle v=p-p^{\prime}.

(64)

Eq. (
60
) shows that the normalization factor (or time integral of this current) is independent
on
\gamma^{-1}
i.e., the intrinsic decoherence does not affect this quantity. The first and second moment
have the same functional dependence of the intrinsic decoherence. From Eqs. (
60
), (
61
) and
(
62
), one writes to first order in
\gamma^{-1}

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\langle t\rangle_{\text{q}}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}

(65)

\displaystyle(\Delta t)^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle(\Delta t)^{2}_{\text{q}}+\frac{2}{\gamma}\langle t\rangle_{\text{q}},

(66)

where the sub-index “q” stands for the corresponding quantity within the standard quantum mechanics.
Eqs. (
65
) and (
66
) confirm that both the mean arrival time and the rms
width of the arrival time distribution are increased by the intrinsic decoherence and the amount of increment
is linear in
\gamma^{-1}
. We emphasize that the above results are only valid for states where the current
density is positive.
It is noteable that a master equation in the same form of the Milburn equation has been derived to study errors in measurement of time by a clock
Maetal-LNP-2008
. Treating the arrival time model as a kind of clock, Eqs. (
65
) and (
66
) can be interpreted as a limit on clock precision.

III.4
Transmission probability through a localized barrier

Consider now the solution of the time independent Schrödinger equation for a localized barrier
V(x)
,

\displaystyle u_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\begin{cases}e^{ikx}+R(k)e^{-ikx}&x<0,\\
A(k)f(x)+B(k)g(x)&0\leq x<L,\\
T(k)e^{ikx}&L\leq x,\end{cases}

(67)

where
k=\sqrt{2mE/\hbar^{2}}
is the wave number,
[0,L]
is the support of the barrier and

R(k)
and
T(k)
are the reflection and transmission coefficients, respectively. When
E<V_{\max}
,
tunneling is present. Finally,
f(x)
and
g(x)
are two independent solutions of the Scrödinger equation
in the barrier region. Note that here we have preferred to use
u_{k}(x)
instead of
u_{E}(x)
.

Let us now consider transmission of a localized wave packet through the barrier. If the system is initially
prepared in the state
\psi_{0}(x)
in the far left side of the barrier and the wavepacket includes only
positive momenta, the solution of the Schrödinger equation is given by

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dk~\phi(k)e^{-iE_{k}t/\hbar}u_{k}(x),\qquad E_{k}=\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m},

(68)

where
\phi(k)=\langle k|\psi_{0}\rangle
denotes the momentum amplitude (the Fourier transform of
\psi_{0}(x)
)
of initial wave packet in the absence of the barrier.

From Eq. (
8
), the solution of the corresponding problem in the Milburn framework is given by

\displaystyle\rho(x,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dk~\int_{0}^{\infty}dk^{\prime}~e^{-i(E_{k}-E_{k^{\prime}})t/\hbar-(E_{k}-E_{k^{\prime}})^{2}t/(2\hbar^{2}\gamma)}\phi(k)\phi^{*}(k^{\prime})u_{k}(x)u_{k^{\prime}}^{*}(x^{\prime}).

(69)

The time-dependent transmission probability is defined as

\displaystyle P_{\text{tr}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{L}^{\infty}dx~\rho(x,x,t)

(70)

and the stationary value of this quantity is independent on the lower limit of the corresponding integral.
The transmission probability for a monochromatic beam with momentum
p=\hbar k
is
|T(k)|^{2}
.
Due to the fact that the initial probability density in momentum is
|\phi(k)|^{2}
, then the stationary
transmission probability for a wave packet is

\displaystyle P_{\text{tr}}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dk~|\phi(k)|^{2}|T(k)|^{2}.

(71)

This is applicable even in the framework of the Milburn equation.
In this way, one sees that
P_{\text{tr}}(\infty)
is independent on the intrinsic decoherence parameter
\gamma
.

(2\hbar/mg^{2})^{1/3}
.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have analyzed several theoretical and practical aspects of the Milburn equation which comes from
a first order expansion on the parameter
\gamma^{-1}
governing the decoherence, the so-called intrinsic
decoherence. In the limit of
\gamma^{-1}\to 0
, one recovers the von Neumann equation for the
density operator. Linear entropy is reduced following a linear behavior in time. Ehrenfest relations and the
probability current density and arrival times are also modified by the presence of the intrinsic decoherence.
In particular, the extension of the Milburn formalism to include the Wigner representation of the
corresponding equation and the relation to a Lindbladian master equation are important steps to better
understand the importance of the corresponding quantum dynamics.
Moreover, the interference displayed by two Gaussian wave packets leads to a decreasing
function of the visibility as the intrinsic decoherence increases. The same is observed for the tunneling
transmission probability. For the bouncing ball problem, the oscillations displayed by the average value of
the height are clearly attenuated. In a certain sense, this behavior is the one should expect when the
external decoherence is present; a gradual and continuous process. This work stresses thus new insights
in the Milburn dynamics compared to the von Neumann dynamics of the density operator.
