Improving Expressivity of Graph Neural Networks using Localization
Anant Kumar kumar_anant@iitgn.ac.in, Shrutimoy Das shrutimoydas@iitgn.ac.in, Shubhajit Roy royshubhajit@iitgn.ac.in, Binita Maity binitamaity@iitgn.ac.in, Anirban Dasgupta anirbandg@iitgn.ac.in

Abstract
In this paper, we propose localized versions of Weisfeiler-Leman (WL) algorithms in an effort to both increase the expressivity, as well as decrease the computational overhead. We focus on the specific problem of subgraph counting and give localized versions of k − k- WL for any k k . We analyze the power of Local k − k- WL and prove that it is more expressive than k − k- WL and at most as expressive as ( k + 1 ) − (k+1)- WL. We give a characterization of patterns whose count as a subgraph and induced subgraph are invariant if two graphs are Local k − k- WL equivalent. We also introduce two variants of k − k- WL: Layer k − k- WL and recursive k − k- WL. These methods are more time and space efficient than applying k − k- WL on the whole graph. We also propose a fragmentation technique that guarantees the exact count of all induced subgraphs of size at most 4 using just 1 − 1- WL. The same idea can be extended further for larger patterns using k > 1 k>1 . We also compare the expressive power of Local k − k- WL with other GNN hierarchies and show that given a bound on the time complexity, our methods are more expressive than the ones mentioned in Papp and Wattenhofer 2022a .

Introduction

Graphs have been used for representing relational and structural data that appear in a variety of domains, ranging from social network analysis and combinatorial optimization to particle physics and protein folding

In order to learn representations of these data for various downstream learning tasks such as graph classification, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as very effective models. Given the various types of GNN-based models developed in recent years

A count of specific patterns is very useful in determining the similarity between two graphs. Weisfeiler Leman Algorithm

Weisfeiler-Leman (WL) is a well known combinatorial algorithm that has many theoretical and practical applications. Color refinement(
1-

•

Initially, color all the vertices as color 1.

•

In the next iteration

C_{i}(v)=(C_{i-1}(v),\{\{C_{i}(w)\}\}_{w\in N_{G}(v)})

We assign a new color to the vertices, according to the unique tuple it belongs to. This partitions the vertex set in every iteration according to their color classes.

•

The algorithm terminates if there is no further partition. We call the color set a
stable
color set.

•

We can also observe that if two vertices get different colors at any stage
i,
then they will never get the same color in the later iterations. We can observe that the number of iterations is at most
n
as a vertex set ,
V(G),
can be partitioned at most
n

•

The color class of any vertex

can appear at most
1+\log n

\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log n)

Immerman and Sengupta 2019
.

In case we need to run only

The same idea was extended by Weisfeiler and Leman in which instead of coloring vertex, they colored all the two tuples based on edge, non-edge and
(v,v)

Similar approach was extended later for coloring

Spoiler and Duplicator are two players.

2.

3.

In this section, we theoretically prove the expressive power of GNN models that we proposed in

-pebble bijective game.

Theoretical Comparison of Graph Neural Networks

In this section we give a comparison of the time and expressiveness between the GNN hierarchies proposed in
Papp and Wattenhofer 2022a
and our methods. From
Theorem

3
, it is clear that
k-
WL is less expressive than Local
k-
WL. Also, we have shown that the space and time required by Local
k-
WL are less compared to
k-
WL.

S_{k}
is a model in which a vertex is given an attribute based on the number of connected induced subgraphs of size at most
k,
the key vertex. Even though it searches locally, the number of non-isomorphic graphs may be too many for a small value of
k
. Suppose the radius of the induced subgraph is
r
; then it has to search up to the
r
-hop neighborhood. Using brute force would require
O(n_{1}^{k})
-time to count the number of induced subgraphs of size
k,
for each individual induced subgraph. To improve the time complexity, it either stores the previous computation, which requires a lot of space or further recomputes from scratch. Thus, it would require
O(t_{k}\times n_{1}^{k})
, where
t_{k}
is the number of distinct graphs upto isomorphism of at most
k
-vertices. Using
Theorem

6
, one can easily see that running
k-
WL locally is more expressive than
S_{k+2}.

The
N_{k}
model has a preprocessing step in which it takes the
k
-hop neighborhood around vertex
v,
and gives attributes based on the isomorphism type. In a dense graph, it is not feasible to solve the isomorphism problem in general, as the size of the induced subgraph may be some function of
n
. Also, using the best known algorithm for graph isomorphism by
Babai 2016
, the time required is
O(n_{1}^{O(\log n_{1})}).
However, running Local
k-
WL would require
O(n_{1}^{k})
. Also, there are rare examples of graphs that are
3-
WL equivalent and non-isomorphic. So, if we run
3-
WL locally, then most of times expressive power matches with
N_{k}.

The
M_{k}
model deletes a vertex
v
and then runs
1-WL.

Papp and Wattenhofer 2022a
proposed that instead of deleting the vertices,
k
vertices are marked in the local neighborhood and showed that it is more expressive than deletion. It identifies
k
set of vertices in the local
r
-hop neighborhood of the graph. It would require
O(n_{1}^{(k+2)}\log(n_{1}))
time as it has
O(n_{1}^{k})
many possibilities of choosing
k
many vertices. It requires
O(n^{2}\log n)
time to run
1-
WL. The same time is required for Local
(k+1)-
WL. That’s why we compare
M_{k-1}
with Local
k-
WL in
Table

3
. Also, it is known that with any
l
markings and running
k-
WL is less expressive than running
(k+l)-
WL on the graph
Fürer 2017
. So, if we plug in the value, we can see that running Local
k-
WL is more expressive than doing
l
marking and running
1-
WL. One can get an intuition by comparing with the
(k+1)
bijective pebble game. If we mark the vertices, then the marking pebbles get fixed and give less power to spoiler. However, just running
k-
WL the spoiler is free to move all the pebbles. We present a simple proof that Local
k-
WL is at least as expressive as
M_{k-1}
.

Theorem 12
.

Local
k-
WL is atleast as expressive as
M_{k-1}
.

Proof.

Let
G_{v}^{r}
and

be the induced subgraphs around the

-hop neighborhood for vertices
v
and
u

M_{k}
distinguish

and
G_{u}^{r}

k-

G_{v}^{r}
and
G_{u}^{r}

G_{v}^{r}

(v_{1},v_{2},....v_{k-1})

Now, consider two games. One game corresponds to
1-

After that, in both games, there are two pebbles and the position of both the games are same. Let

S_{2}

D_{2}
follows the strategy of
D_{1}.

will propose the same bijection. Now,

Similarly,

has not won till round

S_{2}

u,

S_{2}

Proposed Architecture

In this section, we propose two GNN architectures for subgraph as well as induced subgraph counting. The design of these networks follows from the discussions about subgraph and induced subgraph counting in the previous sections. Given a graph
G
as an input, we extract the rooted subgraphs
G_{v}^{r}
for each vertex
v.
Each of these subgraphs is rooted at
v.
The value of
r
depends on the structure to be counted. For example, we extract
1-
hop induced subgraphs for counting triangles. These subgraphs are then given as inputs to the proposed models.

9.1
InsideOut GNN

We conducted the experiments using two different architectures,
InsideOutGNN
and
InSigGNN
.

Figure 3:
Schematic of the InsideOutGNN Model

The InsideOut GNN model (
Figure

3
) consists of an internal network and an external network. The internal network learns the local substructure counts for each rooted subgraph,
G_{v}^{r}.
A subgraph are given as input pass through an Internal GNN model. This model consists of
GINConv

Xu et al. 2019
layers. The Internal GNN outputs the embeddings of the nodes present in a given subgraph. These node embeddings are then aggregated using a GlobalAddPool layer to get the embedding of the root node
v
of the subgraph. This root node embedding is the feature vector for
v,
as given in line
7
of Algorithm
1
. The
GINConv
layers simulate the WL iterations on the nodes of the subgraph. This root node embedding,
emb_{v},
is then passed through a linear transformation layer to learn the local counts of the required substructure in the subgraph.

Now, we take a union of all the subgraphs for a given graph. Using the node embeddings,
emb_{v},
learnt from the Internal GNN model, the External GNN model learns the global count of the required substructure. The External GNN model also comprises of
GINConv
layers.

The intuition behind this design is to make the models learn the local counts well. Once these local counts are learned, the global count is just a linear function of the local counts, which can be learnt easily by the External GNN model.

9.2
InSigGNN

For the InSig model, the Internal GNN component is similar to that of the InsideOut GNN model. The architecture is shown in
Figure

4
. However, in this model, we do not transfer the node embeddings learnt from the Internal GNN part of the model. Instead, we add all the local counts predicted by the internal part of the model and pass the sum as an input to the external model, which is just a linear transformation layer. The weights learned in the Internal GNN model depend only on the local counts of the substructures in the rooted subgraphs. The external linear transformation is learned based on the global count of a substructure in the entire input graph.

Schematic of the InSigGNN model

9.3
Implementation Details

We experimented with both
InsideOutGNN
and
InsigGNN
models to predict the count of structures such as triangles, 3stars, 2stars, and Chordal cycles. For each of these structures, the true local counts are computed using various methods. For example, if our task is to count the number of triangles in a particular subgraph, for the root node of the subgraph, we label its local count to be the number of edges present in a 1-hop neighbouring subgraph without the root node. Using these local counts, from Equation
Lemma

5
, we assign the global counts to be
\frac{1}{3}\sum\limits_{u\in V(G_{v}^{1})}\prescript{x\mkern-0.5mu}{}{C}_{2}
, where
x
is the local count corresponding to the root vertex
v.
Here,
3
is the orbit of a node in a triangle. Similarly, we assign the local counts and the global counts for different substructures.

When counting larger substructures such as
K_{4}
and
C_{4},
, we use the fragmentation technique with the help of the
InSignGNN
model. For calculating
K_{4}
, we construct a set of subgraphs. For every node in the graph, a 1-hop neighbouring subgraph is generated, from which we remove the root node. In this subgraph, we count the number of triangles and process accordingly. Therefore, we use the model already trained for counting triangles in order to count
K_{4}.
During the inference, we apply a rounding function, as the counts are integer numbers.

2

2

GINConv
layers in the external part of the model as well.

We use a learning rate of
1e-4

1.
We also experimented with different hidden dimensions for the node embeddings and obtained the best results when we used

Conclusion

In this work, we progressed toward a more precise characterization of the localized versions of WL algorithms. We showed how the Local-
k-
WL lies between the global
k
and
k+1-
WL in terms of expressiveness. We also developed strategies to make the Local-
k-
WL algorithm more efficient by introducing techniques such as layered WL, recursive WL, and pattern fragmentation. The hope is that such generalizations of the WL algorithm will lead to a finer subdivision of the WL hierarchy as well as more efficient and expressive graph neural networks.
