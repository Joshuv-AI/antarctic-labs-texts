The Photon Sphere and Response Functions in Holography Preprint: today
Marcos Riojas a, Hao-Yu Sun a

Abstract
In this letter, we show the Unruh temperature of the photon sphere for an AdS 4 -Schwarzschild black hole can be determined holographically from the retarded Green’s function and is proportional to its circumference according to a boundary observer. We then sharpen the conjecture that the photon sphere, as seen by a boundary observer, is the spatial Fourier transform of the response function. The conjecture is found to be in excellent agreement with our numerics after certain long-lived excitations – associated with geodesics traveling between boundary points – are removed from the response, which is then controlled by the short-lived excitations of the AdS black hole.



I
Introduction

The significance of the photon sphere, also called the Einstein ring, was initially recognized by Bardeen
Bardeen et al. 1972
, who used it to investigate the dynamics of marginally trapped light rays near black holes.
Its importance in holography is beginning to be recognized. In particular, the photon sphere plays a crucial role in the imaging of Einstein rings of black holes in anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime, as elucidated in
Hashimoto et al. 2020
;
Hashimoto et al. 2019a
. They possess emergence conformal symmetries associated with enhanced bright regions, sometimes referred to as “photon (sub)rings”, from gravitational lensing by a Kerr black hole in asymptotically flat spacetime, as discussed in
Johnson et al. 2020
;
Gralla and Lupsasca 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Hadar et al. 2022
. Furthermore, photon spheres have implications in the context of charged
Liu et al. 2022
and warped
Kapec et al. 2023
black holes in AdS
3
.

The imaging of astrophysical black hole shadows, such as those of M87* and Sgr A* by the Event Horizon Telescope
Akiyama et al. 2019

Akiyama et al. 2022
, has also sparked renewed interest in the properties of the photon sphere. An extensive review on analytical techniques for calculating black hole photon rings and shadows can be found in
Perlick and Tsupko 2022a
.

One critical aspect for astrophysical and holographic applications is the concept of quasi-normal modes (QNMs). These are the frequencies that solve the Klein–Gordon equation, for specific boundary conditions, and play a foundational role in our understanding of the behavior of perturbations around black holes.
Various techniques have been developed to study QNMs over the past few decades, such as the WKB method
Schutz and Will 1985
;
Iyer and Will 1987
, the monodromy method
Iyer 1987
, continued fraction
Leaver 1985
;
Leaver 1986a
;
Leaver 1986b
;
Daghigh et al. 2023
, Regge poles
Decanini and Folacci 2009
and purely numerical techniques.

QNMs offer a powerful approach for understanding perturbations around black holes, with their applications extending to AdS/CMT (condensed matter theory) correspondence
Hartnoll et al. 2018
. QNMs have connections to physical quantities in the dual strongly coupled field theory and play a role in describing charge transport in the quantum critical theory. Furthermore, the functional determinant for semiclassical gravity in asymptotically (A)dS can be written as a product over QNMs
Denef et al. 2010
;
Keeler and Ng 2014
;
Keeler et al. 2016
. Similarly, thermal two-point functions in chaotic systems in general are also conjectured to be products over QNMs
Dodelson et al. 2023a
.

Overall, the precise relationship between QNMs and black hole photon spheres (or shadows) remains a complex challenge. Recently, Bardeen
Bardeen 2018
stressed the predominant generation of Hawking radiation near the photon sphere of a black hole in asymptotically flat spacetime, emphasizing the importance of understanding such relationship.

One of the most appealing aspects of the imaging program of Hashimoto et al.
Hashimoto et al. 2020
;
Hashimoto et al. 2019a
is the detection of the potential existence of a gravitational dual of a given
3
d quantum system by observing the temperature-dependence of
4
d planar AdS-Schwarzschild black hole photon rings. Their thermal properties are encoded in the retarded Green’s function, which describes the linear response to the external monochromatic source.

II
The Critical Transition

This section discusses a critical phase transition that occurs for black holes of intermediate size. It plays a fundamental role in the behavior of geodesics and Klein–Gordon modes, which are known to be related through the eikonal approximation at large angular momentum
l
.

The same critical transition occurs for the retarded Greens function for the dual theory, also known as the response.
1
1

1

We thank the authors of
Dodelson et al. 2023b
for pointing out that Festuccia and Liu have already performed a similar analysis
Festuccia and Liu 2009
. They are apparently related to boundary states which do not thermalize
Berenstein et al. 2021

We discuss the consequences of this critical behavior, highlight the connections between these phase transitions, and show some new results. One of these is that the Unruh temperature of the photon sphere is related to its angular extent, according to a boundary observer, and to the Lyapunov exponent
\lambda
of the orbiting geodesics. We also show that
\lambda
controls the critical behavior for the geodesics, Klein–Gordon modes, and the retarded Greens function.

II.1
The Critical Transition for Geodesics

Spherically symmetric and static metrics, on which we focus in this letter, can be placed in the form:

g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-A(r)dt^{2}+B(r)dr^{2}+D(r)d\Omega_{d-2}.

(1)

For a massless particle, it is customary to study the first integral of its null geodesic equation:

-A(r)\dot{t}^{2}+B(r)\dot{r}^{2}+D(r)\dot{\varphi}^{2}=0.

(2)

The Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(x,\dot{x})=(1/2)g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}
does not depend on
\varphi
or
t
, so Eq.(
2
) admits conserved energy and angular momentum:

\omega=A(r)\dot{t},\quad l=D(r)\dot{\varphi}.

(3)

Their ratio
b:=l/\omega
, the
impact parameter
, determines the behavior of a particle emitted from the asymptotic boundary. Specializing to an AdS
4
-Schwarzschild black hole with blackening function
F(r)
, and choosing AdS radius
L=1
for convenience:

F(r)=1+r^{2}-2M/r,

(4)

we then obtain equations of motion :

\hskip-5.69046pt\left(\frac{dr}{d\varphi}\right)^{2}=r^{2}\left(\frac{1}{b^{2}}-h(r)\right),

(5)

where
h(r):=F(r)/r^{2}
is the effective potential.

The boundary conditions are given by initializing
r=r_{0}
and determining the momentum:

p=\frac{dr}{d\tau}=\left(\frac{dr}{d\varphi}\right)\left(\frac{d\varphi}{d\tau}\right)=\sqrt{\omega^{2}-\frac{l^{2}F(r)}{r^{2}}}.

(6)

One then solves (
5
) to obtain the null geodesics with manifest critical behavior in the impact parameter, as shown in Figure
1
. Note the unstable maximum at
r_{\text{ps}}=3M
where massless particles can orbit around the black hole; their trajectories define the
photon sphere
.

For the geodesic to travel between two points on the boundary, its impact parameter must lie in the range:

b_{c}^{2}\leq b^{2}\leq 1,

(7)

where
b_{c}
is the maximum value of the effective potential:

h(r_{\text{ps}})=1+\frac{1}{27M^{2}}:=\frac{1}{b_{c}^{2}}=\lambda^{2}.

(8)

The value of
\lambda
, the Lyapunov exponent of orbiting null geodesics, was determined in
Cardoso et al. 2009
. It is fundamental in describing the QNMs of perturbed black holes in asymptotically flat spacetime, and given by:

\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{-\frac{r_{\text{ps}}^{2}}{F(r_{\text{ps}})}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{dr_{*}^{2}}\frac{F(r)}{r^{2}}\right)\bigg|_{r=r_{\text{ps}}}}.

(9)

This yields
Riojas and Sun 2023
the critical impact parameter (
7
):

\lambda^{2}=1+\frac{1}{27M^{2}}=h(r_{\text{ps}})=\frac{1}{b_{\text{c}}^{2}}.

(10)

We will soon show it plays a key role in the behavior of the retarded Green’s function
G_{R}
.

II.1.1
The Angular Extent of the Photon Sphere

The extent of the shadow cast by the photon sphere of a black hole was first understood by Bardeen in 1973
Bardeen 1973
.
2
2

2

See
Perlick and Tsupko 2022b
for an excellent review.
For an observer at
r_{0}
, its angular extent
\alpha
is:

\sin(\alpha)=\sqrt{\frac{h(r_{0})}{h(r_{\text{ps}})}}

(11)

For a boundary observer
h(r_{0})=1
, so if the lens used to observe the photon sphere has unit length, the radius of the photon sphere will be
\sin(\alpha)=\lambda^{-1}
.

Intuitively, the Lyapunov exponent gives the local acceleration of the massless particles at the photon sphere. This has already been explored by Raffaelli
Raffaelli 2022
, who showed the Unruh temperature of the photon sphere is:

T_{\text{PS}}=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}

(12)

One of our results follows immediately from (
11
); the temperature of the photon sphere is proportional to its circumference, according to a boundary observer. This will be of interest later when we explore the implications of our results for photon sphere imaging and show that the system can be understood by extracting
\lambda
from the retarded Green’s function
G_{R}
(or retarded response function, or just “response function” for short from now on).

II.2
The Critical Transition for Klein–Gordon Modes

Here we show that the same critical transition occurs for bulk fields
\Phi
satisfying the Klein–Gordon equation:

g_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\mu}\nabla^{\nu}\Phi(t,r,\theta,\phi)=0.

(13)

For a Schwarzschild black hole, (
4
) gives:

\frac{1}{F}\partial_{t}^{2}\Phi+F\partial^{2}_{r}\Phi+\frac{\partial_{r}(r^{2}F)}{r^{2}}\partial_{r}\Phi+\frac{\nabla^{2}}{r^{2}}\Phi=0,

(14)

where
\nabla^{2}
is the scalar Laplacian on
S^{d-2}
. Since this problem has an azimuthal symmetry, we can solve this equation by expanding its solution in spherical harmonics
Y_{lm}
with
m=0
:

\Phi(t,r,\theta,\phi)=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{l}c_{l}\phi_{l}(r)Y_{l\,0}(\theta).

(15)

The equations of motion simplify in tortoise coordinates:

r_{*}:=\int\frac{1}{F(r)}dr\Leftrightarrow\frac{d}{dr_{*}}:=F(r)\frac{d}{dr}.

(16)

Since the blackening function
F(r)
is evaluated in the original
r
-coordinates,
r(r_{*})
must be obtained from an inverse tortoise coordinate. The modes
\phi_{l}
satisfy:

\begin{split}\left(\frac{d}{dr_{*}}\right)^{2}\phi_{l}(r_{*})&+\left(\frac{2F(r)}{r}\right)\left(\frac{d}{dr_{*}}\right)\phi_{l}(r_{*})\\
&+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{l(l+1)F(r)}{r^{2}}\right)\phi_{l}(r_{*})=0,\end{split}

(17)

resembling the radial Helmholtz equation in flat spacetime. Its effective potential
F(r)/r^{2}
achieves a local maximum at the photon sphere, consistent with the massless particle picture.

II.2.1
An Approximate Formula for the Bulk Field

An approximate formula for the bulk field satisfying (
19
) can be obtained using the WKB approximation
Riojas and Sun 2023
. First, one converts (
17
) to Schrödinger form using the field redefinition:

\psi_{l}(r):=r\phi_{l}(r).

(18)

The result resembles the equations of motion for massless particles (
5
):

\psi_{l}^{\prime\prime}(r_{*})+\left(\omega^{2}-\frac{l(l+1)F(r)}{r^{2}}-\frac{F(r)F^{\prime}(r)}{r}\right)\psi_{l}(r_{*})=0.

(19)

It was recently pointed out
Riojas and Sun 2023
;
Hashimoto et al. 2023
that, for large
l
, the
F(r)F^{\prime}(r)/r
term acts as a hard wall on the AdS boundary and can be neglected inside the bulk. From here, the WKB approximation gives
Riojas and Sun 2023
:

|\psi_{l}(r_{*})|\approx\frac{\left(\omega^{2}-l(l+1)\right)^{\frac{3}{4}}}{\sqrt[4]{\omega^{2}-l(l+1)\frac{f(r)}{r^{2}}}}.

(20)

Note
r=r(r_{*})
. The bulk field
\phi
can be recovered from the field redefinition (
18
), so the formula describes the amplification of the source as it propagates toward the black hole. The field
\psi
must tunnel through a potential barrier at the photon sphere to reach the horizon when:

\frac{\omega^{2}}{l(l+1)}\geq\frac{f(r_{\text{ps}})}{r_{\text{ps}}^{2}}=\frac{1}{b_{c}^{2}}=\lambda^{2}.

(21)

The approximation is accurate before the critical phase transition (
8
) in the bulk field occurs, which causes the field to decay as it propagates. This behavior is exactly analogous to the behavior of bulk geodesics; when (
21
) is satisfied, the bulk field does not propagate beyond the photon sphere, and the field “orbits”.

II.3
The Critical Transition for the Response

It is well known
Klebanov and Witten 1999
that the AdS/CFT correspondence relates the response function
G_{R}
for boundary operators to the bulk field (
13
) through an asymptotic expansion near the AdS boundary.
For a scalar field of mass
m
the conformal dimension
\Delta
in an AdS
d
bulk can be determined from:

\Delta(\Delta-d+1)=m^{2}.

(22)

In our case
d=4
and
m=0
, so we have:

\Delta_{-}=0,\quad\Delta_{+}=3.

(23)

According to the AdS/CFT dictionary, the field
\Phi
near the asymptotic boundary expanded in terms of
1/r
can be written as
Klebanov and Witten 1999
;
Hashimoto et al. 2020
:

\Phi(t,r,\theta,\phi)=J_{\mathcal{O}}(t,\theta,\phi)+\dots+\frac{\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}(t, \theta, \phi)}}{r^{3}}+\dots,

(24)

where
J_{\mathcal{O}}
is the oscillating source (with frequency
\omega
) for the scalar operator
\mathcal{O}
dual to
\Phi
in the boundary CFT. The source
J_{\mathcal{O}}
is the boundary value of the field at the UV cutoff, while the response function
\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}}
corresponds to the coefficient of the
1/r^{3}
term.
3
3

3

The retarded Green’s function is given by the ratio of the leading and subleading terms:
G_{R}=-\frac{\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}(t, \theta, \phi)}}{J_{\mathcal{O}}(t,\theta,\phi)}

The quasi-normal modes of the AdS black hole are usually believed to be dual to the poles in the response function, but black holes of intermediate size are more complicated. The phase transition in the amplification formula is directly connected to the resonance of the quasinormal modes of the AdS Schwarzschild black hole
Riojas and Sun 2023
, which activate when the phase transition (
8
) occurs. Still, below the critical transition, there is a line of poles near the real line roughly obeying the rule:

\omega\approx l+(d_{\text{CFT}})+2n,

(25)

where
d_{\text{CFT}}
is the spatial dimension of the holographic theory. These are the normal modes of AdS, see Fig.
2
for an illustration. Once the critical phase transition (
6
) occurs, the QNMs related to Schwarzschild black holes become active and move into the lower half-plane. The imaginary part of the retarded Greens function (the spectral function) for a real frequency also moves into the lower half-plane. This is consistent with
Jokela et al. 2016
, which confirmed the predictions of
Horowitz and Hubeny 2000
.

We are not the first to point out the significance of the potential well, or the existence of the long-lived excitations
Festuccia and Liu 2009
. Our point is that these long-lived modes have consequences for sources of real frequency, which strafe the poles near the real line.

III
The Photon Sphere Image Conjecture

The existence of the long-lived modes (
25
) leads us to sharpen a well-known conjecture concerning the photon sphere
Hashimoto et al. 2020
;
Hashimoto et al. 2019b
. The authors used wave optics to convert the response function
\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}}
(
24
), into an image of the dual black hole on a virtual screen:

\Psi_{S}\left(\vec{x}_{S}\right)=\int_{|\vec{x}|<d}d^{2}x\langle\mathcal{O}(\vec{x})\rangle e^{-\frac{i\omega}{f}\vec{x}\cdot\vec{x}_{S}}.

(26)

The image is given by
\left|\Psi_{\mathrm{S}}\left(\vec{x}_{\mathrm{S}}\right)\right|^{2}
, where
\vec{x}
is the location where the response is measured and
\vec{x}_{S}
is the location on the screen. Intuitively, (
26
) is integrated over the lens used to observe the image. The situation is illustrated in Figure

III.1
Determining the Response Function

We follow the standard procedure to numerically determine the response function
\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}}
, which has already been explained in detail in Section A of the reference
Hashimoto et al. 2020
. First the bulk scalar field
\Phi
, satisfying (
17
), is decomposed as:

\Phi(t,r,\theta,\varphi)=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}c_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(r)Y_{\ell 0}(\theta)

(27)

The response to an individual mode
\phi_{l}
,
q_{3}
, is determined by performing an asymptotic expansion of the numerically obtained solutions. Following
Hashimoto et al. 2020
, we assume a Gaussian source:

J_{\mathcal{O}}(t,\theta,\varphi)=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\exp\left[-\frac{(\pi-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right],

(28)

and decompose it using spherical harmonics. The response function is then given by:

\langle\mathcal{O}(\theta)\rangle=-\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}q_{3}Y_{\ell 0}(\theta),

(29)

where
c_{\ell}\simeq(-1)^{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{\ell+1/2}{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\ell+1/2)^{2}\sigma^{2}\right)
.

Here our analysis diverges from
Hashimoto et al. 2020
;
Hashimoto et al. 2019b
. It can be seen in Fig.
4
that the long-lived modes, which activate after the critical phase transition (
21
) is crossed, will lead to an essentially random response for the long-lived modes.

from the sum (

According to (

Discussion.
In this letter, we have analyzed the critical phase transition for massless geodesics, massless bulk fields
\Phi
, and the holographically dual response function
\expectationvalue{\mathcal{O}}
. All three transitions are controlled by the Lyapunov exponent of the null geodesics,
\lambda
, and occur for the same value of the impact parameter
b=l/\omega
.

In each case, there is a transition from a long-lived to short-lived behavior. For massless geodesics, the particles transition from orbiting the black hole to falling in; for massless bulk fields, the field transitions from probing the horizon to remaining outside the photon sphere; for the response function, the modes transition from the long-lived modes of empty AdS to the short-lived modes of an AdS black hole.

By carefully considering this critical phase transition, we sharpened the conjecture of
Hashimoto et al. 2020
;
Hashimoto et al. 2019b
– the conjecture has enhanced predictive power after the long-lived response is excised from (
26
). By determining the response function for a system with a holographically dual AdS black hole, one can extract
\lambda
from the critical transition in Fig.
2
, thereby determining the temperature of the black hole
Riojas and Sun 2023
and other properties. Similarly,
\lambda
can be obtained directly from the image of the photon sphere (
26
) using the response. Finally, using
Raffaelli 2022
and (
11
), the temperature of the photon sphere can be determined from the circumference of the images in Fig.
5
.
