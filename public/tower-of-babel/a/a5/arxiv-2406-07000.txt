Strong decays of the isovector-scalar D ∗ ​ D ¯ ∗ D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast} hadronic molecule
Jin-Cheng Deng, Bo Wang

Abstract
We adopt the effective Lagrangian approach to study the strong decays of the 1 − ​ ( 0 + ⁣ + ) 1^{-}(0^{++}) D ∗ ​ D ¯ ∗ D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast} molecular state [denoted as T ψ ​ 0 a ​ ( 4010 ) T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010) according to the LHCb naming convention] through triangle diagrams. The decay channels include the open-charm D ​ D ¯ D\bar{D} , and the hidden-charm η c ​ π \eta_{c}\pi , J / ψ ​ ρ J/\psi\rho , and χ c ​ 1 ​ π \chi_{c1}\pi . The coupling between the T ψ ​ 0 a ​ ( 4010 ) T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010) and its constituents D ∗ ​ D ¯ ∗ D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast} is obtained by solving for the residue of the scattering T T -matrix at the pole. Our calculations yield a total width of few tens MeV for the T ψ ​ 0 a ​ ( 4010 ) T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010) state using three different form factors, with its main decay channels being η c ​ π \eta_{c}\pi and χ c ​ 1 ​ π \chi_{c1}\pi . The X ⁡ ( 4100 ) X(4100) and X ⁡ ( 4050 ) X(4050) have similar masses and widths, with both masses being close to the D ∗ ​ D ¯ ∗ D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast} threshold. Additionally, their decay final states are consistent with those of the T ψ ​ 0 a ​ ( 4010 ) T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010) . Therefore, it is likely that they represent the same state and both potentially correspond to the T ψ ​ 0 a ​ ( 4010 ) T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010) . We suggest that future experiments focus on searching for the T ψ ​ 0 a ​ ( 4010 ) T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010) signal in the final states η c ​ π − \eta_{c}\pi^{-} , χ c ​ 1 ​ π − \chi_{c1}\pi^{-} and D 0 ​ D − D^{0}D^{-} of the B 0 → η c ​ π − ​ K + B^{0}\to\eta_{c}\pi^{-}K^{+} , χ c ​ 1 ​ π − ​ K + \chi_{c1}\pi^{-}K^{+} and D 0 ​ D − ​ K + D^{0}D^{-}K^{+} processes, respectively, as well as further investigating its resonance parameters with Flatté-like formula.

II.2
Determination of the coupling constant
g_{0}

In this section, we will demonstrate how to determine the coupling constant
g_{0}
in Eq. (
1
) using the residue of the scattering
T
-matrix at the pole. To begin, we consider the scattering of particles
A
and
B
(with masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, respectively) within the framework of field theory. Assuming that their interaction is strong enough, allowing for the formation of bound states, we must treat their scattering in a nonperturbative manner, which means using the Bethe-Salpeter (BS) equation,

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{FT}}=V^{\mathrm{FT}}+V^{\mathrm{FT}}G^{\mathrm{FT}}T^{\mathrm{FT}},

(24)

where the superscript “
\mathrm{FT}
” means that we are working in a relativistic field theoretical approach. The two-body relativistic propagator
G^{\mathrm{FT}}
reads

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{FT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{q^{2}-m_{1}^{2}+i\epsilon}\frac{1}{(P-q)^{2}-m_{2}^{2}+i\epsilon}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{q^{2}dq}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}\frac{1}{P_{0}^{2}-(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})^{2}+i\epsilon},

where
P=p_{1}+p_{2}
, with
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
the momentum of
A
and
B
particles, respectively, and
P_{0}
denotes the center of mass energy of
AB
system. The result in the second line of Eq. (
25
) is obtained with the residue theorem. The
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\omega_{1}^{2}=\bm{q}^{2}+m_{1}^{2},\qquad\omega_{2}^{2}=\bm{q}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}.

(26)

Then, we perform a nonrelativistic reduction

\displaystyle\omega_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{i}+\frac{\bm{q}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\dots,\quad(i=1,2),

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{\omega_{1}\omega_{2}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{1}}+\frac{1}{m_{2}}=\frac{1}{\mu},

(28)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P_{0}^{2}-(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P_{0}-(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}\frac{1}{P_{0}+(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}

(29)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{P_{0}-m_{1}-m_{2}-\frac{\bm{q}^{2}}{2\mu}}\frac{1}{2P_{0}}.

With the nonrelativistic reduction, the
G^{\mathrm{FT}}
becomes

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{FT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{\mu\sqrt{s}}G,

(30)

where

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{q^{2}dq}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}-m_{1}-m_{2}-\frac{\bm{q}^{2}}{2\mu}}

(31)

represents the nonrelativistic two-body propagator. Note that we have replaced the
P_{0}
with
\sqrt{s}
, where
s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}
is the invariant mass of the
AB
system, and it equals to the
P_{0}^{2}
in the center of mass frame.

For nonrelativistic scattering, such as the interaction of the
AB
system near the threshold energy, we can use the Lippmann-Schwinger (LS) equation to handle it, i.e,

\displaystyle t=v+vGt,

(32)

where
G
is given in Eq. (
31
).

The integral equation in Eqs. (
24
) and (
32
) can be transformed into algebraic equations with, e.g., the on-shell factorization approach
[
50
]
,

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{FT}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-V^{\mathrm{FT}}G^{\mathrm{FT}})^{-1}V^{\mathrm{FT}},

(33)

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-vG)^{-1}v.

(34)

It can be seen that the position of the pole in the
T
-matrix is determined by the product of
V^{\mathrm{FT}}
(
v
) and
G^{\mathrm{FT}}
(
G
). The following condition ensures that the pole position remains unchanged after the reduction,

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{FT}}G^{\mathrm{FT}}=vG.

(35)

With the relation in Eq. (
30
), one can obtain that
V^{\mathrm{FT}}=\frac{\mu\sqrt{s}}{\pi}V
. Similarly, the
T^{\mathrm{FT}}
and
t
will satisfy the same relationship, i.e.,

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{FT}}=\frac{\mu\sqrt{s}}{\pi}t.

(36)

Now, we assume that there exists a pole with the squared mass
s_{R}
in the
T
-matrix, e.g., corresponding to a bound state. Then, in the vicinity of this pole, the
T
-matrix can be approximately written as

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{FT}}=\frac{(g^{\mathrm{FT}})^{2}}{s-s_{R}},

(37)

where
g^{\mathrm{FT}}
represents the coupling constant of the bound state
R
to its component
AB
. The relation in Eq. (
37
) allows us to extract the coupling constant from the residue of
T
-matrix,

\displaystyle(g^{\mathrm{FT}})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to s_{R}}(s-s_{R})T^{\mathrm{FT}}

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to s_{R}}(\sqrt{s}+\sqrt{s_{R}})\frac{\mu\sqrt{s}}{\pi}(\sqrt{s}-\sqrt{s_{R}})t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu s_{R}}{\pi}\lim_{s\to s_{R}}(\sqrt{s}-\sqrt{s_{R}})t,

and

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to s_{R}}(\sqrt{s}-\sqrt{s_{R}})t=\lim_{s\to s_{R}}\left[\frac{d}{d\sqrt{s}}t^{-1}(\sqrt{s})\right]^{-1},

(39)

where we have used the l’Hôpital rule.

We adopt sharp cutoff to regularize the integral in Eq. (
31
). For the bound state case, i.e.,

, its expression reads

\displaystyle G_{\Lambda}(\sqrt{s})=\frac{\mu}{4\pi^{3}}\left[\gamma_{b}\arctan\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\gamma_{b}}\right)-\Lambda\right],

(40)

where
\gamma_{b}=\sqrt{2\mu(m_{1}+m_{2}-\sqrt{s})}
denotes the binding momentum, and

is the cutoff parameter.

If the effective potential

does not depend on energy, as in the case given in Ref.
[
32
]
. Then the Eq. (
39
) will equal to

\displaystyle g^{\prime 2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to s_{R}}\left[-\frac{d}{d\sqrt{s}}G_{\Lambda}(\sqrt{s})\right]^{-1}

(41)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{b}}{\frac{\mu^{2}}{4\pi^{3}}\left[\arctan\left(\frac{\Lambda}{\gamma_{b}}\right)-\frac{\gamma_{b}\Lambda}{\gamma_{b}^{2}+\Lambda^{2}}\right]}.

The
g^{\mathrm{FT}}
is obtained by combining the results in Eqs. (
38
)-(

and using

. This is consistent with the relation used in Ref.

]

To ensure that the loop integrals in Eqs. (
47
)-(
52
) converge and yield finite results, we employ different form factors to regularize the integrals. Theoretically, the most commonly used form factors can be categorized into three types: i) the Heaviside form factor (step function), ii) the Gaussian form factor, and iii) the multipole form factor. Their expressions are respectively given as

\displaystyle\text{Heaviside form factor: }\mathfrak{F}_{1}(|\bm{p}|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(\Lambda_{1}-|\bm{p}|),

(64)

\displaystyle\text{Gaussian form factor: }\mathfrak{F}_{2}(\bm{p}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left({-\frac{\bm{p}^{2}}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}}\right),

(65)

\displaystyle\text{Multipole form factor: }\mathfrak{F}_{3}(p^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m_{M}^{2}-\Lambda_{3}^{2}}{p^{2}-\Lambda_{3}^{2}}\right)^{n},

(66)

where
m_{M}
denotes the mass of the exchanged particle, and
\Lambda_{3}=m_{M}+\alpha_{\Lambda}\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}
, with
\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}=220
MeV.
\alpha_{\Lambda}
is a dimensionless phenomenological parameter, typically taken to be around
1

[
40
]
.

We then demonstrate how to regularize the loop integrals in Eqs. (
47
)-(
52
) using these three form factors, beginning with the Heaviside and Gaussian form factors as examples. Since we are dealing with the decays of a bound state, the constituent particles
D^{\ast}
and
\bar{D}^{\ast}
are off-shell, meaning that there are no right-hand cuts in the loop integrals. Thus, for instance, for a scalar integral with the following form,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{(p_{1}+q)^{2}-m_{D^{\ast}}^{2}}\frac{1}{(p_{2}-q)^{2}-m_{\bar{D}^{\ast}}^{2}}\frac{1}{q^{2}-m_{M}^{2}},

it can be regularized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i}(\Lambda_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3i}{16\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx\int_{0}^{1-x}dy\mathscr{I}_{i}(\Lambda_{i}).

(68)

The integrands
\mathscr{I}_{i}(\Lambda_{i})
respectively read

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{1}(\Lambda_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\frac{\bm{\ell}^{2}\Theta(\Lambda_{1}-|\bm{\ell}|)}{\left(\bm{\ell}^{2}+\Delta\right)^{5/2}}=\frac{\Lambda^{3}_{1}}{3\Delta\left(\Delta+\Lambda^{2}_{1}\right)^{3/2}},

(69)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{2}(\Lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}d\ell\frac{\bm{\ell}^{2}e^{-\bm{\ell}^{2}/\Lambda_{2}^{2}}}{\left(\bm{\ell}^{2}+\Delta\right)^{5/2}}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4\Delta}U\left(\frac{3}{2},0,\frac{\Delta}{\Lambda_{2}^{2}}\right).

(70)

We have used the Feynman parameterization to combine the denominators of the propagators, and employed the residue theorem to integrate out the
\ell_{0}
component.
The
\ell=q+xp_{1}-yp_{2}
, and
\Delta=xm_{D^{\ast}}^{2}+y(m_{\bar{D}^{\ast}}^{2}-m_{f_{2}}^{2})+xy(m_{f_{1}}^{2}+m_{f_{2}}^{2}-m_{T_{\psi 0}^{a}}^{2})+x^{2}m_{f_{1}}^{2}-xm_{f_{1}}^{2}+y^{2}m_{f_{2}}^{2}-(x+y-1)m_{M}^{2}
(where
m_{M}
,
m_{f_{1}}
and
m_{f_{2}}
denote the masses of the exchanged particles, the final states
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, respectively).
U(a,b,z)
represents the Tricomi’s (confluent hypergeometric) function. It can be easily proven that when
\Lambda_{1,2}
tends to infinity, the result of
\mathcal{I}_{i}(\Lambda_{i})
is equivalent to that obtained within the dimensional regularization.

Using the Heaviside and Gaussian form factors ensures that all integral terms are convergent. However, when employing multipole form factors, it is necessary to set the power

n
to at least
4
to guarantee the convergence of all terms in the integral. This choice, however, significantly suppresses the contribution of lower-order terms in
q
. Therefore, we adopt a strategy of incrementally increasing

n
as the power of
q
in the numerator increases. For example, we multiply the integrand similar to that in Eq. (
II.3
) by the multipole form factor, and then, by using the Feynman parametrization, we can obtain an integral in the following form,

\displaystyle\left(m_{M}^{2}-\Lambda_{3}^{2}\right)^{n}\frac{\Gamma(3+n)}{\Gamma(n)}\int_{0}^{1}dxdydzdw\delta(x+y+z+w-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad~\times\frac{w^{n-1}\left(\ell^{2}\right)^{n^{\prime}}}{\left(\ell^{2}-\Delta^{\prime}\right)^{3+n}},

(71)

where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}n=1,&n^{\prime}=0,1\\
n=n^{\prime},&n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N},n^{\prime}\geq 2\end{cases},

(72)

and

\Delta^{\prime}=-xy\left(m_{T_{\psi 0}^{a}}^{2}-m_{f_{1}}^{2}-m_{f_{2}}^{2}\right)+m_{D^{\ast}}^{2}x+m_{\bar{D}^{\ast}}^{2}y+m_{M}^{2}z+m_{f_{1}}^{2}x^{2}-m_{f_{1}}^{2}x+m_{f_{2}}^{2}y^{2}-m_{f_{2}}^{2}y+\Lambda_{3}^{2}w
.

In Sec.
III
, we will demonstrate the effects of these three form factors on the results.

As shown in Tables
2
,
3
and
4
, the results obtained using the Heaviside and Gaussian form factors are very similar. In contrast, when using the multipole form factor, the width of the
D\bar{D}
channel is significantly smaller than in the previous two cases, while the widths of the other channels remain relatively close.
It is evident that the
\eta_{c}\pi
channel is the dominant decay mode of the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
state, with a decay width approximately twice that of the
\chi_{c1}\pi
channel. However, the width of
\chi_{c1}\pi
channel is still larger than the remaining two channels, namely
D\bar{D}
and
J/\psi\rho
. Therefore,
\eta_{c}\pi
and
\chi_{c1}\pi
are considered the golden channels for detecting the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
state. Furthermore, these two channels respectively correspond to the final states in which the
X(4100)
and
X(4050)
resonances were observed, which may suggest, to some extent, that
X(4100)
and
X(4050)
are molecular state of the
1^{-}(0^{++})

D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
system.

The future experiments can also explore the charged
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
state by studying the invariant mass spectrum of
D^{+}\bar{D}^{0}+c.c
. For example, with the weak decay processes

B^{0}\to D^{-}D^{0}K^{+}
, and
\bar{B}^{0}\to D^{+}\bar{D}^{0}K^{-}
.
The angular distribution of
D^{+}\bar{D}^{0}+c.c
will exhibit the characteristic of a flat S-wave distribution. The
D^{+}\bar{D}^{0}+c.c
channel can effectively exclude contributions from conventional charmonia, such as the
\psi(4040)
. Therefore, the signal observed in the
D^{+}\bar{D}^{0}+c.c
channel may be cleaner and provide a clearer indication of the presence of the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
state. This makes the
D^{+}\bar{D}^{0}+c.c
channel a promising avenue for studying the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
state and distinguishing it from other resonances.

Lastly, it is important to note that our calculations are based on the configuration that
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
is a bound state of
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
. This primarily stems from our previous work
[
32
]
, which employed an energy-independent contact potential, where
Z_{c}(3900)
is treated as a virtual state. If an energy-dependent contact potential were used, resonance solutions could be obtained
[
53
]
. However, the current experimental data is insufficient to definitively determine whether
Z_{c}(3900)
is a virtual state or a resonance
[
14
]
. If
T_{\psi 0}^{a}
becomes a resonance state of
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
, the approaches outlined in this paper could still apply, but the conclusions would change accordingly. For instance, while the coupling constant
g_{0}
could still be extracted from the residue of the
T
-matrix at the pole, it would now have an imaginary part due to the resonance mass lying above the
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
threshold. Consequently, the
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
in the loop diagram could be on-shell, and the small imaginary part
i\epsilon
in the denominator of the propagator cannot be discarded. The resulting scattering amplitude would also contain an imaginary part. In addition to the decay channels listed in this paper, a resonance state could also decay into its constituents, specifically
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
. Therefore, the width might be larger than that obtained in the bound state scenario. Moreover, it is very likely that the dominant decay channel is into
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
rather than the hidden-charm decay channel
\eta_{c}\pi
, as the decay into
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
can occur via tree-level process.

IV
Summary

Recently, we investigated the interactions of the
D^{(\ast)}\bar{D}^{(\ast)}
systems based on a quark-level potential model
[
32
]
. We found that if
X(3872)
and
Z_{c}(3900)
are the isoscalar and isovector molecular states of the
D\bar{D}^{\ast}
system, respectively, then there must exist a bound state in the
1^{-}(0^{++})

D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
system, denoted as
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
. This state would decay into the
D\bar{D}
,
\eta_{c}\pi
,
J/\psi\rho
and
\chi_{c1}\pi
channels. It is noteworthy that the LHCb and Belle Collaborations have observed the
X(4100)

[
2
]
and
X(4050)

[
10
]
in the final states of
\eta_{c}\pi
and
\chi_{c1}\pi
, respectively. The masses and widths of these two states are of similar size within the experimental uncertainties, and their masses are close to the
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
threshold. Furthermore, the final states of their decays are consistent with the decay channels of
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
. Therefore, it is significant to investigate whether these two states are the same one and whether they correspond to the molecular state of
D^{\ast}\bar{D}^{\ast}
.

In this work, we used the effective Lagrangian approach to investigate the strong decays of the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
state through triangle loop diagrams. We found that its main decay channels are
\eta_{c}\pi
and
\chi_{c1}\pi
, which may explain why the LHCb and Belle Collaborations reported the signals of
X(4100)
and
X(4050)
in these two channels, respectively. This also suggests that
X(4100)
and
X(4050)
might be identified as the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
state. We also investigated the dependence of the total (partial) width(s) and branching fractions (ratios) on the cutoff parameters in the loop integrals. We noticed that the width shows a strong dependence on the cutoff, while the branching fractions (ratios) exhibit a very weak dependence. Therefore, experimental measurements of the branching fractions (ratios) would help to identify the properties of
X(4100)
and
X(4050)
and their relationship with the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
. Our calculations predict a total width of few tens MeV for the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
within three different form factors, which is consistent with the experimentally measured widths of its partners
Z_{c}(3900)
and
Z_{c}(4020)
.

We propose that future experiments focus on the study of the resonance parameters of the
T_{\psi 0}^{a}(4010)
state in the
\eta_{c}\pi^{-}
,
\chi_{c1}\pi^{-}
and
D^{0}D^{-}
invariant mass spectra of the
B^{0}\to\eta_{c}\pi^{-}K^{+}
,
\chi_{c1}\pi^{-}K^{+}
and
D^{0}D^{-}K^{+}
processes (or the charge conjugate channels). This would be crucial for constructing the mass spectrum of the hadronic molecules in the
D^{(\ast)}\bar{D}^{(\ast)}
systems and, in turn, understanding the properties of the exotic hadrons such as
X(3872)
and
Z_{c}(3900)
.
