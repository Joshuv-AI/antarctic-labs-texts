Noise sensitivity on affine Weyl groups
Ryokichi Tanaka

Abstract
. We show that on every affine Weyl group natural random walks are noise sensitive in total variation.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Gamma
be a countable group and
\mu
be a probability measure on it.
A
\mu
-
random walk

\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
starting from the identity
{\rm id}
is defined by
w_{n}:=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
and
w_{0}:={\rm id}
for an independent, identically distributed sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
with the common law
\mu
.
The distribution of
w_{n}
is the
n
-fold convolution
\mu_{n}:=\mu^{\ast n}
.
The noise sensitivity problem for random walks on groups asks:
Does resampling a small fraction of increments
x_{1},x_{2},\dots
produce an almost independent copy of
w_{n}
or a highly correlated copy of
w_{n}
?

The precise definition is as follows.
For a real
\rho\in[0,1]
,
let

\pi^{\rho}:=\rho(\mu\times\mu)+(1-\rho)\mu_{{\rm diag}}\quad\text{on $\Gamma\times\Gamma$},

where
\mu\times\mu
denotes the product measure and
\mu_{{\rm diag}}((x,y)):=\mu(x)
if
x=y
and
0
otherwise.
Let us consider a
\pi^{\rho}
-random walk
\{{\bm{w}}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
starting from the identity on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
.
We say that the
\mu
-random walk is
noise sensitive
in total variation if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=0\quad\text{for all $\rho\in(0,1]$}.

In the above, the total variation distance coincides with the half of
\ell^{1}
-norm,

\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|_{\rm TV}=\max_{A\subset\Gamma\times\Gamma}|\nu_{1}(A)-\nu_{2}(A)|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{x}}\in\Gamma\times\Gamma}|\nu_{1}({\bm{x}})-\nu_{2}({\bm{x}})|,

where
\nu_{i}
are probability measures on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
,
i=1,2
.
Since we use only total variation distance in the definitions, let us simply say noise sensitive if there is no danger of confusion.

If a
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is noise sensitive,
then informally speaking, the situation is as in the following: For each fixed
\rho\in(0,1]
even though it is close to
0
,
resampling a
\rho
-portion of increments produces an asymptotically independent copy of the original
\mu
-random walk.

The definition of noise sensitivity for random walks on groups was introduced by Benjamini and Brieussel in
[
BB23
, Definition 2.1]
.
In their paper, they discuss the notion and the variants not only in total variation (there it is called
\ell^{1}
-noise sensitivity) but also in other distances or in terms of entropy.
See also the related discussion in
[
Kal18
, Section 3.3.4]
.
It has been observed that on finite groups random walks are noise sensitive in all natural definitions
[
BB23
, Proposition 5.1]
.
This raises a challenge to find finitely generated
infinite
groups on which random walks are noise sensitive.
Further the problem becomes more restrictive by measuring the distance in total variation.
A simple observation using the central limit theorem shows that standard random walks on finite rank free abelian groups
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
are
not
noise sensitive
[
BB23
, Theorem 1.1 (1)]
.
Benjamini and Brieussel have shown that on the infinite dihedral group some lazy simple random walk is noise sensitive
[
BB23
, Theorem 1.4]
.
So far, this has been the only known random walk which is noise sensitive in total variation on a finitely generated infinite group.
We provide a class of such groups on which natural random walks are noise sensitive.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be an affine Weyl group with the standard set of generators
S
, and
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
such that
the support of
\mu
equals
S\cup\{{\rm id}\}
.
For all
\rho\in(0,1]
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=0,

i.e., the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is noise sensitive in total variation.

For the definition of affine Weyl groups, see Section
2.1
.
The infinite dihedral group

D_{\infty}:=\langle s_{1},s_{2}\mid s_{1}^{2}=s_{2}^{2}={\rm id}\rangle\quad\text{with $S=\{s_{1},s_{2}\}$}

is an example of affine Weyl group called type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
.
See more examples of affine Weyl groups in Section
4.2
.
In fact, there exist a constant
C>0
and an integer
m>0
such that for all integer
n>1
,

\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{C(\log n)^{m}}{\sqrt{n}}.

This strengthening of Theorem
1.1
is stated below as Theorem
4.1
.

In Theorem
1.1
,
the laziness (i.e.,
\mu({\rm id})>0
) is crucial since otherwise the random walk on that group is not necessarily noise sensitive.
This in particular shows that the noise sensitivity is a property of the random walk rather than the group as was pointed out in
[
BB23
]
.
Let us note that the
\mu
-random walk on the infinite dihedral group considered there has a particular form:
\mu(s_{1})=\mu(s_{2})=\mu({\rm id})=1/3
.
It is not clear from their proof whether changing the laziness (i.e., the measure on the identity element) would still provide a noise sensitive random walk or not.
We show that this is indeed the case, furthermore,
\mu
is allowed to be a non-uniform distribution on
S\cup\{{\rm id}\}
.

In general,
the following is known for a finitely supported probability measure
\mu
:
If either the group
\Gamma
admits a surjective homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
, or
(\Gamma,\mu)
is
non-Liouville
, i.e., there exists a non-constant bounded
\mu
-harmonic function on
\Gamma
,
then a
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is
not
noise sensitive
[
BB23
, Theorem 1.1 (2)]
.
In a more specific class of
(\Gamma,\mu)
(where possibly
\Gamma
does or does not admit a surjective homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
),
a strong negation of noise sensitivity has been shown for non-elementary word hyperbolic groups, e.g., free groups of rank at least
2
.
If
\mu
is non-elementary and has a finite first moment,
then there exists a
\rho_{0}\in(0,1]
such that

\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
for all
\rho\in[0,\rho_{0})

[
Tan24
, Theorem 1.3]
.
In this generality, it is not known as to whether
\rho_{0}=1
or not.
Note that it is rather straightforward to check that
\rho_{0}=1
for simple random walks on free
semi
-groups of rank at least
2
, cf.
[
Tan24
, Introduction]
.

Outline of the proof of Theorem
1.1

An affine Weyl group
(\Gamma,S)
with the standard set of generators
S
is associated with some Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
The group
\Gamma
acts on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
isometrically and generators act as reflections relative to hyperplanes.
The group
\Gamma
has the form
\Lambda\rtimes W
where
\Lambda
is identified with a lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
W
is a finite group (called a spherical Weyl group).
There is a point
o
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that the orbit map
\Phi:x\mapsto x.o
is injective.
Taking a conjugate by a translation if necessary, we assume that
o
is the origin in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

(see Section
2.1
for the precise discussion).
A main ingredient is to establish a local central limit theorem (Theorem
3.6
).
We define a discrete normal distribution
{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{n\Sigma}
on
\Gamma
induced from the normalized restriction on
\Phi(\Gamma)
of the
m
-dimensional Gaussian density function with a covariance matrix
n\Sigma
. Further we show the following:
The distribution
\mu_{n}
is approximated by
{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{n\Sigma}
uniformly on
\Gamma
within an error of order
n^{-\frac{m+1}{2}}
as
n
tends to infinity.
The local central limit theorem itself follows from a classical argument based on characteristic functions.
Some more general results (other than the Cayley graphs of affine Weyl groups) have been proved, e.g., in
[
KS83
]
,
[
PS94
]
,
[
KSS98
]
and
[
Sun13
]
.
We provide the proof of the local central limit theorem in our setting with an error estimate.
Furthermore, we use an explicit form of matrix
\Sigma
in terms of harmonic
1
-forms on a finite quotient graph of the Cayley graph by the lattice
\Lambda
.
The matrix
\Sigma
is obtained as some limiting form, which has previously appeared in the literature of symbolic dynamics, see e.g.
[
PS94
]
; however, the explicit form in Theorem
3.6
plays an important role.

We apply this discussion to
(\Gamma\times\Gamma,\pi^{\rho})
for
\rho\in(0,1]
.
The local central limit theorem enables us to show that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
for all large enough
n
,

\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{C(\log n)^{m}}{\sqrt{n}}.

See Theorem
3.8
.
(For the discussion on the sharpness of this bound, see Remark
3.9
.)
If
\mu
has support
S\cup\{{\rm id}\}
, then
\pi^{\rho}
has support
(S\cup\{{\rm id}\})^{2}
consisting of elements of order at most
2
.
The explicit formula of the covariance matrix implies that
\Sigma^{\rho}
has a block diagonal form and
\Sigma^{\rho}=\Sigma^{1}
for all
\rho\in(0,1]
.
(This is the only part where we use the particular structure of the generating set.)
Thus by the triangle inequality we conclude Theorem
1.1
(in Theorem
4.1
).
Let us note that if the support of
\mu
does not contain
{\rm id}
, then the support of
\pi^{\rho}
does not generate the group
\Gamma\times\Gamma
(cf. Section
4.1
).

2
,
we introduce affine Weyl groups and discuss background.
In Section
3
,

3.6
).
In Section

A
, we include the result (Theorem

) on

for the sake of convenience.

Notations

For a constant
C
, we write
C=C_{\Sigma}

to indicate its dependence on
\Sigma
.
For non-negative real valued functions
f
and
g
on a common (sub-)set of non-negative integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,
we write
f(n)=O(g(n))
or
f\ll g
if there exists a constant
C
such that
f(n)\leq Cg(n)
for all large enough
n
.
We also write
f(n)=O_{\Sigma}(g(n))
if
C=C_{\Sigma}
in the above notation.
Further we write
f(n)=\Omega(g(n))
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
f(n)\geq cg(n)
for all large enough
n
, and
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))
if
f\ll g
and
g\ll f
.
For a set
A
, we denote by
|A|
the cardinality.

2.
Preliminaries

For a group
\Gamma
and for a subset
A
in
\Gamma
,
we write
\Gamma=\langle A\rangle
if
\Gamma
is generated by
A
as a
semigroup
, i.e., every element in
\Gamma
is obtained as a product of some finite sequence of elements from
A
.
Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group with a finite symmetric set of generators
S
, i.e.,
\Gamma=\langle S\rangle
and
S
is invariant under the map
s\mapsto s^{-1}
.
It holds that, in fact,
s=s^{-1}
for all
s\in S
if
S
consists of involutions.
(This is the case of an (affine) Weyl group
(\Gamma,S)
in the following discussion.)
Let
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
be the (right) Cayley graph of
\Gamma
with respect to
S
, i.e., the set of vertices is
\Gamma
and an edge
\{x,y\}
is defined if and only if
x^{-1}y\in S
.
Since
S
is invariant under
s\mapsto s^{-1}
,
the Cayley graph is defined as an undirected graph.
For
x\in\Gamma
, let
|x|_{S}
denote the word norm with respect to
S
, i.e., the graph distance between
{\rm id}
and
x
in
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
.

2.1.
Affine Weyl groups

Let
(\Gamma,S)
be an
affine Weyl group
where
S
is a canonical finite set of generators,
consisting of involutions, i.e.,
s^{2}={\rm id}
for every
s\in S
.
The group
\Gamma
admits a semi-direct product structure
\Gamma=\Lambda\rtimes W
where
the subgroup
W
called the
spherical Weyl group
is finite and the normal subgroup
\Lambda
is isomorphic to a free abelian group of finite rank.
For a thorough background on the subject, we refer to
[
AB08
]
.
We also refer to Section
4.2
for the examples most relevant to the present discussion.

The group
\Gamma
is equipped with an isometric action on the standard Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for some
m\geq 1
,
where
each generator
s\in S
acts as a reflection with respect to an affine hyperplane.
The action is properly discontinuous and admits a relatively compact convex fundamental domain with nonempty interior
C_{0}
called a
chamber
.
The group
\Gamma
acts on the set of chambers
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{xC_{0}\}_{x\in\Gamma}
simply transitively, i.e., for all
C_{1},C_{2}\in{\mathcal{C}}
there exists
x\in\Gamma
such that
C_{1}=xC_{2}
, and if
xC_{0}=C_{0}
, then
x={\rm id}
.
The normal subgroup
\Lambda
acts freely (i.e., without fixed points) as translations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
The
\Lambda
-orbit of the origin is a lattice:

\big\{a_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}v_{m}:a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\big\},

where
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
form a basis in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
We identify
\Lambda
with the lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Note that the action of
W
preserves
\Lambda
.

The affine Weyl group
(\Gamma,S)
is called
reducible
if there exist nontrivial affine Weyl groups generated by
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
respectively with
S=S_{1}\times\left\{{\rm id}\right\}\cup\left\{{\rm id}\right\}\times S_{2}
for which
\Gamma=\langle S_{1}\rangle\times\langle S_{2}\rangle
, and
irreducible
otherwise.
The group
\Gamma
we consider is possibly (and basically) reducible.
Irreducible ones are completely classified in terms of root systems.
For example, the affine Weyl group of type
\widetilde{A}_{1}
is the infinite dihedral group

\langle s_{1},s_{2}\mid s_{1}^{2}=s_{2}^{2}={\rm id}\rangle,

where
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
act as reflections with respect to
0
(the origin) and
1
respectively in
{\mathbb{R}}
.
A chamber has the form of interval
[0,1)
.

Let us fix a point
o
in the interior of a chamber and define

\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\quad x\mapsto x.o.

The map
\Phi
is injective since
\Gamma
acts on the set of chambers simply transitively, and is
\Gamma
-equivariant, i.e.,
\Phi(xy)=x.\Phi(y)
for all
x,y\in\Gamma
.
Let us call
\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
an associated
equivariant embedding
.
Since the generators act as reflections with respect to affine hyperplanes,
it is illustrative to consider that
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
is realized in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
via the map
\Phi
.
Namely, the vertices are placed inside of the chambers as the orbit
x.o
for
x\in\Gamma
and an edge is a line segment connecting two vertices for which one is obtained from the other by a reflection of the form
xsx^{-1}
for
s\in S
and
x\in\Gamma
.

The group
\Lambda
itself acts on
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
from left freely as automorphisms of the graph.
Let us consider the quotient graph

G=\Lambda\backslash{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
.
The graph
G=(V(G),E^{un}(G))
is finite, the set of vertices
V(G)
is
W
and the set of edges
E^{un}(G)
consists of
undirected
edges.
Note, however, that
G
is
not
the right Cayley graph of
W
with respect to the image
\overline{S}
of
S
under the quotient map
\Lambda\rtimes W\to W
.
This is because the quotient map restricted on
S
is not bijective onto
\overline{S}
.
The graph
G
has possibly multiple edges.

2.2.
Pointed finite networks as quotients

The main interest is on an affine Weyl group
\Gamma
and the canonical set of generators
S
.
It is, however, useful to discuss a slightly more general setting.
Let
\Gamma
be a virtually finite rank free abelian group, i.e.,
\Gamma
admits a finite rank free abelian group
\Lambda
as a finite index subgroup.
We assume that
\Gamma
acts on an
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
isometrically with a relatively compact fundamental domain with nonempty interior, and that
\Lambda
acts as translations and is identified with a lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Let us fix a point
o
in the interior of such a fundamental domain of
\Gamma
and define
\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
by
x\mapsto x.o
.
The map
\Phi
is equivariant with
\Gamma
-actions and injective.
For a finite symmetric set of generators
S
in
\Gamma
,
let

G:=\Lambda\backslash{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S).

The quotient
G=(V(G),E^{un}(G))
is a finite (undirected) graph possibly with multiple edges (whence a multi-graph) and with loops.
It holds that
V(G)=\Lambda\backslash\Gamma

and

E^{un}(G)=\{\{x,x.s\}\ :\ x\in\Lambda\backslash\Gamma,\ s\in S\},

where
\{x,x.s\}
and
\{x.s,x\}
are identified.
For the examples, see Section
4.2
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
such that the support
{\rm supp}\,\mu
of
\mu
is finite, that
\Gamma=\langle{\rm supp}\,\mu\rangle
, and that
\mu
is
symmetric
, i.e.,
\mu(s)=\mu(s^{-1})
for every
s\in{\rm supp}\,\mu
.
If we define
S={\rm supp}\,\mu
, then
S
is a finite symmetric set of generators.
Let

p(\{x,x.s\}):=\mu(s)\quad\text{for $\{x,x.s\}\in E^{un}(G)$}.

This defines a Markov chain on
G=\Lambda\backslash{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
with transition probabilities

\sum_{s\in S,y=x.s}\mu(s)\quad\text{for $x,y\in\Lambda\backslash\Gamma$}.

Note that this Markov chain is irreducible, i.e., it visits every vertex from every other vertex after some time since
\Gamma=\langle{\rm supp}\,\mu\rangle
.
Furthermore, it
is reversible with respect to the uniform distribution
\pi
on the set of vertices
V(G)=\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
.
Indeed, since
\mu
is symmetric,
it holds that

\pi(x)p(\{x,x.s\})=\pi(x.s)p(\{x.s,x\})\quad\text{for $\{x,x.s\}\in E^{un}(G)$},

(2.1)

where
\pi(x)=1/|V(G)|
for
x\in V(G)
.
Note that if
\mu({\rm id})>0
, then
p(x,x)\geq\mu({\rm id})>0
for every
x\in V(G)
.
For each
\{x,y\}\in E^{un}(G)
, let us define the
conductance
by

c(\{x,y\}):=\pi(x)p(\{x,y\}).

This is well-defined since
c(\{x,y\})=c(\{y,x\})
by (
2.1
).
Note that
c(\{x,y\})>0
for all
\{x,y\}\in E^{un}(G)
.
Let
x_{0}\in\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
denote the coset containing
{\rm id}
.
Let us call
(G,c,x_{0})
the pointed (finite)
network
as the finite multi-graph
G
equipped with the conductance
c:E^{un}(G)\to(0,\infty)
and the point
x_{0}
.

3.
Local central limit theorems

3.1.
Harmonic
1
-forms on finite graphs

Let
(G,c,x_{0})
be the pointed finite network.
Henceforth it is convenient to consider
G
as a
graph with orientations
where
each edge (and loop) has both possible orientations.
Let

E(G):=\big\{(x,y),(y,x)\ :\ \{x,y\}\in E^{un}(G)\big\}.

For
e=(x,y)
, we write
\overline{e}=(y,x)
.
The “reversing direction” operation
\overline{\ \cdot\ }:E(G)\to E(G)
,
e\mapsto\overline{e}
, defines a bijection and
\overline{\overline{e}}=e
for
e\in E(G)
.
For
e=(x,y)\in E(G)
, let us denote by
oe:=x
the
origin
and by
te:=y
the
terminus
of
e
respectively.
We have that
o\overline{e}=te
for
e\in E(G)
.
Let us also consider
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
as a graph with orientations, defining both possible orientations for each edge and loop.
Letting
c(e):=c(x,y)
and
p(e):=p(x,y)
for
e=(x,y)
,
we have that

c(e)=c(\overline{e})
and
c(e)=\pi(oe)p(e)
for
e\in E(G)
.
It holds that by the definition of conductance,

\pi(x)=\sum_{e\in E_{x}}c(e),\quad\text{where $E_{x}:=\big\{e\in E(G)\ :\ oe=x\big\}$ for $x\in V(G)$}.

Let us define the
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear space of complex-valued functions on
V(G)
by

C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}}):=\big\{f:V(G)\to{\mathbb{C}}\big\}

equipped with the inner product

\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\pi}:=\sum_{x\in V(G)}f_{1}(x)\overline{f_{2}(x)}\pi(x)
,
where
\overline{a}
stands for the complex-conjugate of
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Similarly, let
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
be the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear space of real-valued functions on
V(G)
endowed with the inner product as the restriction of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
.
Further let us define the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear space of real-valued
1
-forms
on
E(G)
by

C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}}):=\big\{\omega:E(G)\to{\mathbb{R}}\ :\ \text{$\omega(\overline{e})=-\omega(e)$ for $e\in E(G)$}\big\}

equipped with the inner product

\langle\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\rangle_{c}:=(1/2)\sum_{e\in E(G)}\omega_{1}(e)\omega_{2}(e)c(e)
.
The
differential

d:C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\to C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
is the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear map defined by

df(e):=f(te)-f(oe)\quad\text{for $e\in E(G)$}.

Moreover, the
adjoint

d^{\ast}:C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\to C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
with respect to the inner products is obtained by

d^{\ast}\omega(x):=-\sum_{e\in E_{x}}\frac{1}{\pi(x)}c(e)\omega(e)\quad\text{for $x\in V(G)$}.

It holds that for
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,

\langle df,\omega\rangle_{c}=\langle f,d^{\ast}\omega\rangle_{\pi}.

(3.1)

Note that if we define the transition operator
P
on
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
to itself by

Pf(x):=\sum_{e\in E_{x}}\frac{1}{\pi(x)}c(e)f(te)\quad\text{for $x\in V(G)$},

then

d^{\ast}d=I-P
where
I
is the identity operator.
Let us define the space of
harmonic
1
-forms
by

H^{1}:=\big\{\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\ :\ d^{\ast}\omega=0\big\}.

Note that
H^{1}=({\rm Im}\,d)^{\bot}
the orthogonal complement of the image
{\rm Im}\,d
by (
3.1
).
The fact that we use in the sequel is that for every
1
-form
\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
there exists a
unique
harmonic
1
-form
u\in H^{1}
and some
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
such that

u+df=\omega.

The
u
is obtained as the
H^{1}
-part in the orthogonal decomposition
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})=H^{1}\oplus{\rm Im}\,d
.
Note that
f
is not unique since every
f
added a constant function satisfies the relation.

Remark 3.1
.

If we endow
G
with a structure of
1
-dimensional CW complex, then the
1
-cohomology group with real coefficient is defined as
H^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}}):=C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})/{\rm Im}\,d
.
The fact mentioned above means that every
1
-cohomology class is represented by a unique harmonic
1
-form.
Although all these notions are not needed in our discussion, it might be useful to grasp an idea behind some of our computations.

3.2.
Perturbations of transfer operators

For each
1
-form
\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
let us define the
transfer operator
on
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
by

{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}f(x):=\sum_{e\in E_{x}}p(e)e^{2\pi i\omega(e)}f(te)\quad\text{for $x\in V(G)$}.

Here
i=\sqrt{-1}
.
In this particular setting where
\mu
is symmetric,
the transfer operator is self-adjoint on
(C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}}),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\pi})
.
Hence it has real eigenvalues.
Let
\lambda(\omega)
be the largest eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
.
If
\omega=0
, then
\lambda(0)=1
and this is a simple eigenvalue by the Perron-Frobenius theorem since
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=P
and
P
is irreducible.
We apply to an analytic perturbation in
\omega
:
for a small enough neighborhood
U
of
0
in
C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
the function
U\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
\omega\mapsto\lambda(\omega)
is real analytic.
Moreover, corresponding eigenvectors
f_{\omega}
depend analytically in
\omega\in U
with
f_{0}={\bf 1}
(the constant vector with all
1
’s).
This follows from the implicit function theorem for
\det(tI-{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega})=0
around
(t,\omega)=(1,0)
in this finite graph setting.
Note that if we consider
\omega+d\varphi
for
\varphi\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
in place of
\omega
,
then
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega+d\varphi}=e^{-2\pi i\varphi}{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}e^{2\pi i\varphi}
, where
(e^{\varphi}f)(x):=e^{\varphi(x)}f(x)
for
x\in V(G)
, and thus

\lambda(\omega+d\varphi)=\lambda(\omega).

This shows that
\lambda(\omega)
depends only on the harmonic
H^{1}
-part of
\omega
.
Let

\beta(\omega):=\log\lambda(\omega).

Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=P
which has a simple eigenvalue
\lambda(0)=1
, there exists
a small enough open neighborhood
U
of
0
in
C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\lambda(\omega)
is a simple eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
and
\beta(\omega)=\log\lambda(\omega)
is well-defined for all
\omega\in U
.
It holds that
\beta(0)=0
since
\lambda(0)=1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\lambda(\omega)=e^{\beta(\omega)}
and
f_{\omega}
be the eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenvector of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
such that
f_{0}={\bf 1}
and
\langle f_{\omega},{\bf 1}\rangle_{\pi}=1
for
\omega\in U
where
U
is a neighborhood of
0
in
C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
For all harmonic
1
-forms
u,u_{i}\in H^{1}
on
G
and real parameters
r,r_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
, the following holds:

\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}\beta(ru)=0,

(3.2)

\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x)=0\quad\text{for all $x\in V(G)$},

(3.3)

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta(r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2})=-4\pi^{2}\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e),

(3.4)

and

\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}\partial r_{3}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2},r_{3})=(0,0,0)}\beta(r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2}+r_{3}u_{3})=0.

(3.5)

Proof.

Let
f_{\omega}
be the eigenvector normalized as stated.
Since
\overline{{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}f_{\omega}}={\mathcal{L}}_{-\omega}\overline{f_{\omega}}
holds for all
\omega
and
\lambda(\omega)
is real for all
\omega
in a small enough neighborhood of
0
in
C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
it holds that
\lambda(-\omega)=\overline{\lambda(\omega)}=\lambda(\omega)
.
Thus
\beta(-\omega)=\beta(\omega)
for all
\omega\in U
and all odd time derivatives of
\beta
at
0
vanish.
This in particular implies (
3.2
) and (
3.5
).

For every
1
-form
u
, it holds that
ru\in U
for all small enough real
r
and

\left\langle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=0.

(3.6)

Moreover, since
P
is self-adjoint with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\pi}
and
P{\bf 1}={\bf 1}
, by (
3.6
) it holds that

\left\langle P\left(\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}\right),{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=\left\langle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru},P{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=\left\langle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=0.

(3.7)

We will also use the analogous identities to (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) for the second derivatives of the normalization:
\langle f_{\omega},{\bf 1}\rangle_{\pi}=1
for
\omega\in U
.

First
differentiating
{\mathcal{L}}_{ru}f_{ru}=e^{\beta(ru)}f_{ru}
at
r=0
yields for each
x\in V(G)
,

\sum_{e\in E_{x}}\left(p(e)(2\pi iu(e))+p(e)\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(te)\right)=\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}\beta(ru)+\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x),

(3.8)

where we have used
f_{0}={\bf 1}
.
Let us note that (
3.8
) above yields by (
3.2
) which we have just shown and by that
d^{\ast}u=0
,

P\left(\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}\right)(x)=\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x)\quad\text{for each $x\in V(G)$}.

Since
P
has the simple eigenvalue
1
, this implies that
(d/dr)|_{r=0}f_{ru}
is constant.
By (
3.6
), for every harmonic
1
-from
u
,
it holds that

(d/dr)|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x)=0
for all
x\in V(G)
,
showing (
3.3
).

For all
1
-forms
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
and for all small enough reals
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
,
it holds that

{\mathcal{L}}_{r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2}}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}=e^{\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}\quad\text{where $\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}:=\beta(r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2})$ and $f_{r_{1},r_{2}}:=f_{r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2}}$}.

Taking the second derivatives at
(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)
of both terms yields by (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and that
f_{0}={\bf 1}
, for each
x\in V(G)
,

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E_{x}}\Bigg(p(e)(-4\pi^{2}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e))+p(e)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}(te)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}(x).

Evaluating the inner products of the above terms with
{\bf 1}
leads

\displaystyle-4\pi^{2}\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)+\left\langle P\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}\right),{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}+\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}f_{r_{1},r_{2}},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}.

The second terms in the left hand side and in the right hand side respectively are
0

since they are the second derivatives on the normalization (cf. (
3.6
) and (
3.7
)).
This proves (
3.4
).
∎

3.3.
An explicit Hessian formula in terms of harmonic
1
-forms

Recall that
\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
x\mapsto x.o
.
Taking a conjugate to the action of
\Gamma
by a translation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we assume that
o
is the origin, whence
\Phi({\rm id})=0
.
The function
(x,y)\mapsto\Phi(y)-\Phi(x)
for (oriented) edges
(x,y)
in
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
is invariant under the action by
\Lambda
.
Indeed, this follows since
\Lambda
is identified with a lattice and acts as translations in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Therefore this descends to an
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
-valued function on
E(G)
, which we denote by

e\mapsto\Phi_{e}
for
e\in E(G)
.
Note that
\Phi_{\overline{e}}=-\Phi_{e}
for each
e\in E(G)
.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be the standard inner product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
For each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
let

\widehat{v}(e):=\langle v,\Phi_{e}\rangle\quad\text{for $e\in E(G)$}.

This
\widehat{v}
defines a
1
-form on
G
.
For
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
near
0
,
let
\beta(v):=\beta(\widehat{v})
where
e^{\beta(v)}
is the largest eigenvalue of the transfer operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}
.
Let us define the Hessian of
\beta
at
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with the standard coordinate
(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})
by

\Hess_{0}\beta:=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{k}\partial r_{l}}\Big|_{(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})=(0,\dots,0)}\beta(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\right)_{k,l=1,\dots,m}.

For the pointed finite network
(G,c,x_{0})
and
\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we compute
\Hess_{0}\beta
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The Hessian
\Hess_{0}\beta
of
\beta
at
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is non-degenerate and negative definite.
Moreover, it holds that

\langle v_{1},\Hess_{0}\beta\,v_{2}\rangle=-4\pi^{2}\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e),

(3.9)

where
u_{i}
is defined as the harmonic part of
\widehat{v}_{i}
for
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
i=1,2
.

Proof.

For every
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
we have
\widehat{v}(e)=\langle v,\Phi_{e}\rangle
for
e\in E(G)
,
and
u
is the harmonic part of
\widehat{v}
, i.e., the unique
u\in H^{1}
such that

u+df=\widehat{v}
for some
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
Note that the resulting map
v\mapsto u
is
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear.
Since
\beta(v)
depends only on the harmonic part of
v
, Lemma
3.2
(
3.4
) implies that

\displaystyle\langle v_{1},\Hess_{0}\beta\,v_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta(r_{1}v_{1}+r_{2}v_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta(r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2})=-4\pi^{2}\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e).

This shows (
3.9
).

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
be a basis of the lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
:

\Lambda=\big\{a_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}v_{m}:a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\big\}
.
For each
v_{k}=\Phi(v_{k})\in\Lambda
under the identification of
\Lambda
with the lattice,
there exists a path
(\widetilde{e}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{e}_{n})
from
{\rm id}
to
v_{k}
in
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)

since the Cayley graph is connected.
Let
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
be the image in
G
of that path under the covering map from
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
.
Note that the image is a cycle:
x_{0}=oe_{1}
,
te_{i}=oe_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
and
te_{n}=x_{0}
.
Thus
\sum_{l=1}^{n}df(e_{l})=0
and

\sum_{l=1}^{n}u(e_{l})=\sum_{l=1}^{n}(u(e_{l})+df(e_{l}))=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{v}(e_{l})
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{v}(e_{l})=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\langle v,\Phi_{e_{l}}\rangle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{l})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{l})\rangle=\langle v,\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{l})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{l}))\rangle.

The last term equals
\langle v,v_{k}\rangle

since
\sum_{l=1}^{n}(\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{l})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{l}))=\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{n})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{1})=v_{k}
.
This shows the following:
For each
k=1,\dots,m
there exists a cycle
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
in
G
with
x_{0}=oe_{1}
and
te_{n}=x_{0}
such that

\sum_{l=1}^{n}u(e_{l})=\langle v,v_{k}\rangle.

(3.10)

For
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
let us assume that
\langle v,\Hess_{0}\beta\,v\rangle=0
.
It holds that
u=0
by (
3.9
), and thus
\langle v,v_{k}\rangle=0
for every
k=1,\dots,m
by (
3.10
). Hence
v=0
since
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
form a basis of a lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
This shows that
\Hess_{0}\beta
is non-degenerate.
Furthermore
\Hess_{0}\beta
is negative definite by (
3.9
).
∎

.

at

, i.e.,

(

.

.

3.4.
Local central limit theorems

For every positive integer
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, it holds that

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}(x_{0})=\sum_{(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})}p(e_{1})\cdots p(e_{n})e^{2\pi i(\widehat{v}(e_{1})+\cdots+\widehat{v}(e_{n}))}.

(3.11)

In the above the summation runs over all directed paths
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
starting from
x_{0}
in
G
, i.e.,

oe_{1}=x_{0}
and
te_{k}=oe_{k+1}
for each
k=1,\dots,n-1
.
For each such path
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
,
there exists a unique path
(\widetilde{e}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{e}_{n})
which is a lift of the path, starting from
{\rm id}
in
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
.
By a lift we mean that the path
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
is the image of
(\widetilde{e}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{e}_{n})
under the covering map
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)\to G=\Lambda\backslash{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
.
The definition of the
1
-form
\widehat{v}
on
G
implies the following:

\displaystyle\widehat{v}(e_{1})+\cdots+\widehat{v}(e_{n})

\displaystyle=\langle v,\Phi_{e_{1}}\rangle+\cdots+\langle v,\Phi_{e_{n}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{1})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{1})\rangle+\cdots+\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{n})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{n})\rangle=\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{n})\rangle,

where
\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{1})=\Phi({\rm id})=0
.
Recall that
p(e)=\mu(s)
for
e=(x,x.s)\in E(G)
and
s\in S
in the pointed finite network
(G,c,x_{0})
.
By (
3.11
), it holds that

{\mathcal{L}}^{n}_{\widehat{v}}{\bf 1}(x_{0})=\sum_{(\widetilde{e}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{e}_{n})}\mu(s_{1})\cdots\mu(s_{n})e^{2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{n})\rangle}=\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\mu_{n}(x)e^{2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle}.

In the above, the (edge) path
(\widetilde{e}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{e}_{n})
is represented as the (vertex) path on
{\rm id}
,
s_{1}
,
s_{1}s_{2}
,
\dots
,
s_{1}\cdots s_{n}
in
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
.
Therefore letting

\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v):=\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\mu_{n}(x)e^{2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle}\quad\text{for $v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$},

we have the following:
For all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}(x_{0})=\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v).

(3.12)

Let
\Lambda^{\ast}
be the dual lattice of
\Lambda
, i.e.,

\Lambda^{\ast}:=\Big\{a_{1}v_{1}^{\ast}+\cdots+a_{m}v_{m}^{\ast}\ :\ a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\Big\},

where
v_{1}^{\ast},\dots,v_{m}^{\ast}
form the dual basis of
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
:
\langle v_{k}^{\ast},v_{l}\rangle=1
if
k=l
and
0
else.
The fundamental parallelotope of
\Lambda^{\ast}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is denoted by

D:=\Big\{r_{1}v_{1}^{\ast}+\cdots+r_{m}v_{m}^{\ast}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\ :\ |r_{i}|\leq 1/2,\ i=1,\dots,m\Big\}.

The volume of
D
is assumed to be
1
up to a homothety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
The Fourier inversion formula shows that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\mu_{n}(x)=\int_{D}\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle}\,dv\quad\text{for $x\in\Gamma$}.

(3.13)

For
\delta>0
,
let

D_{\delta}:=\Big\{r_{1}v_{1}^{\ast}+\cdots+r_{m}v_{m}^{\ast}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\ :\ |r_{i}|<\delta,\ i=1,\dots,m\Big\}.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\mu({\rm id})>0
, then
for all small enough
\delta>0
,
there exists a constant
c_{\delta}>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

|\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v)|\leq\sqrt{|V(G)|}\cdot e^{-c_{\delta}n}\quad\text{for all $v\in D\setminus D_{\delta}$}.

Proof.

This uses a standard perturbation argument; we provide a proof for the sake of completeness.
For
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, let
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}\|:=\max_{\|f\|_{\pi}=1}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}f\|_{\pi}
where
\|\cdot\|_{\pi}
is the associated norm in
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
. Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}
is self-adjoint, the eigenvalues are real and the operator norm
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}\|
is the spectral radius
|\lambda(v)|
, i.e., the largest eigenvalue in absolute value.
Note that
|\lambda(v)|\leq 1
.
For
v\in D
, if
|\lambda(v)|=1
, then the condition
\mu({\rm id})>0
implies that
v=0
(in which case in fact
\lambda(0)\neq-1
).
Indeed, for the maximal eigenvalue
\lambda(v)
in absolute value and a corresponding eigenvector
f_{v}
,
we have
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}f_{v}=\lambda(v)f_{v}
.
Taking absolute values shows that
|f_{v}|\leq P|f_{v}|
, implying that
|f_{v}|
is a non-zero constant since
P
is irreducible.
Further since
f_{v}
is an eigenvector with the eigenvalue
1
in absolute value,

\lambda(v)f_{v}(x)
and
e^{2\pi i\langle v,\Phi_{e}\rangle}f_{v}(te)
for
e\in E_{x}
are on a common circle in the complex plane for each
x\in V(G)
.
Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}f_{v}=\lambda(v)f_{v}
,
it holds that for all
x\in V(G)
and for all
e\in E_{x}
,

\lambda(v)f_{v}(x)=e^{2\pi i\langle v,\Phi_{e}\rangle}f_{v}(te).

(3.14)

If
\mu({\rm id})>0
, then for each
v_{k}\in\Lambda
there exists an edge path from
{\rm id}
to
v_{k}
in
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
of length with a given (in particular, even) parity.
Applying to (
3.14
) along the image of the path in
G
successively yields
\langle v,v_{k}\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
This holds for a basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
of
\Lambda
, implying that

v\in\Lambda^{\ast}
.
Therefore if
v\in D
, then
v=0
.

We have shown that
|\lambda(v)|<1
for all
v\in D\setminus\{0\}
, and
in this finite dimensional setting,
v\mapsto\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}\|=|\lambda(v)|
is continuous.
Thus for a small enough
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\delta}>0
such that
|\lambda(v)|\leq e^{-c_{\delta}}
on a compact set
D\setminus D_{\delta}
.
Since

|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}(x_{0})|\sqrt{\pi(x_{0})}\leq\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}\|_{\pi}\leq|\lambda(v)|^{n}\|{\bf 1}\|_{\pi},

\|{\bf 1}\|_{\pi}=1
and
\pi(x_{0})=1/|V(G)|
,
by (
3.12
), we conclude the claim.
∎

For an associated
\Gamma
-equivariant embedding
\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
x\mapsto x.o
and a non-degenerate positive definite matrix
\Sigma
,
let

\xi_{\Sigma}(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\Phi(x),\Sigma^{-1}(\Phi(x))\rangle}\quad\text{for $x\in\Gamma$}.

Theorem 3.6

(Local central limit theorem)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a virtually finite rank free abelian group acting on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
isometrically with a relatively compact fundamental domain which contains the origin in the interior.
Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
such that the support
{\rm supp}\,\mu
is finite,
\Gamma=\langle{\rm supp}\,\mu\rangle
and
\mu
is symmetric.
If
\mu({\rm id})>0
,
then the following holds:
There exist a non-degenerate positive definite matrix
\Sigma
and a constant
C>0

such that

\sup_{x\in\Gamma}|\mu_{n}(x)-\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}\quad\text{
for all $n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}$}.

Moreover, the matrix
\Sigma
is obtained by

\langle v_{1},\Sigma v_{2}\rangle=\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e),

(3.15)

where
u_{i}
is the harmonic part of
\widehat{v}_{i}
for
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for
i=1,2
.

Proof.

For all
\delta>0
, the Fourier inversion formula (
3.13
) and the change of variables
v\mapsto v/\sqrt{n}
yield the following:
For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for
x\in\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{\delta}}\varphi_{\mu_{n}}\left(v\right)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle}\,dv+\int_{D\setminus D_{\delta}}\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle}\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}\varphi_{\mu_{n}}\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}}\right)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle/\sqrt{n}}\,dv+\int_{D\setminus D_{\delta}}\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle}\,dv.

Since
\mu({\rm id})>0
, Lemma
3.5
shows that
for all small enough
\delta>0
,
there exists a constant
c_{\delta}>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\sup_{v\in D\setminus D_{\delta}}|\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v)|=O\left(e^{-c_{\delta}n}\right).

We will analyze the first integral in the last displayed equation.
For
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
let
\widehat{v}=u+d\varphi
and
u
be the harmonic
H^{1}
-part of
\widehat{v}
for some
\varphi\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
There exists a constant
C
such that such
\varphi
can be chosen to satisfy

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq C\|v\|
.
Indeed, letting
v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}v_{i}
for the standard basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{m}
, and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
we choose
\varphi_{i}
such that
\widehat{v}_{i}=u_{i}+d\varphi_{i}
, and define
\varphi:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\varphi_{i}
.
This yields the inequality with
C=\sqrt{m}\max_{i=1,\dots,m}\|\varphi_{i}\|_{\infty}
.

We have
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}=e^{-2\pi i\varphi}{\mathcal{L}}_{u}e^{2\pi i\varphi}
.
By (
3.12
), up to replacing
c_{\delta}
by a smaller positive value,

\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v)=e^{n\beta(v)}\left(\langle e^{2\pi\varphi}{\bf 1},f_{u}\rangle_{\pi}e^{-2\pi i\varphi(x_{0})}f_{u}(x_{0})+O(e^{-c_{\delta}n})\right)\quad\text{for $v\in D_{\delta}$},

where
f_{u}
is the normalized eigenvector of
{\mathcal{L}}_{u}
of eigenvalue
e^{\beta(v)}
.
By Lemma
3.3
, the Hessian of
\beta
at
0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is obtained by

\Hess_{0}\beta=-4\pi^{2}\Sigma
.
Lemma
3.2
implies that by the Taylor theorem,

\beta(v)=-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle+O(\|v\|^{4})\quad\text{for $v\in D_{\delta}$}.

Therefore for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all
v\in D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}
,

\beta\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{n}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle+O\left(\frac{\|v\|^{4}}{n^{2}}\right).

Replacing by
\delta
a smaller positive constant if necessary,
we have that for all
v\in D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}
,

e^{n\beta\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}}\right)}\leq\left(1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{n}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle\right)^{n}\leq e^{-\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}.

Since
|\varphi_{\mu_{n}}(v/\sqrt{n})|\leq C_{\delta}e^{-\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}
for all
v\in D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}
,
for all large enough
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}\setminus D_{n^{1/8}}}\Big|\varphi_{\mu_{n}}\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}}\right)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle/\sqrt{n}}\Big|\,dv\leq\frac{C_{\delta}}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}\setminus D_{n^{1/8}}}e^{-\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\,dv

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\delta}^{\prime}}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{c_{0}n^{1/8}}^{c_{1}\delta\sqrt{n}}e^{-cr^{2}}r^{m-1}\,dr\leq\frac{C_{\delta}^{\prime}}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{c_{0}n^{1/8}}^{c_{1}\delta\sqrt{n}}re^{-\alpha r^{2}}\,dr=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}e^{-\alpha c_{0}^{2}n^{1/4}}\right)\ll\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}.

In the above,
C_{\delta},C_{\delta}^{\prime},c,c_{0},c_{1}
and
\alpha
(where
c>\alpha
) are positive constants;
we have used the polar coordinate and the positive definiteness of
\Sigma
in the second inequality,
and that
e^{-cr^{2}}r^{m-1}\leq e^{-\alpha r^{2}}r
for all large
r
in the third inequality.

Furthermore for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all
v\in D_{n^{1/8}}
,
since
\|v\|^{4}/n\ll 1/\sqrt{n}
,

\varphi_{\mu_{n}}\left(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}(1+R_{n}(v))\left(\langle e^{2\pi i\varphi/\sqrt{n}}{\bf 1},f_{u/\sqrt{n}}\rangle_{\pi}e^{-2\pi i\varphi(x_{0})/\sqrt{n}}f_{u/\sqrt{n}}(x_{0})+O(e^{-c_{\delta}n})\right),

where
R_{n}(v)=O\left(\|v\|^{4}/n\right)
.
For the normalized eigenvector
f_{u}
of
e^{\beta(v)}
for
u
near
0
,
since
f_{0}={\bf 1}
, by Lemma
3.2
(
3.3
)
the Taylor theorem shows the following: for each
x\in V(G)
,

f_{u/\sqrt{n}}(x)=1+O\left(\frac{\|u\|^{2}}{n}\right).

Furthermore, since
\|u\|\leq\|v\|
and
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq C\|v\|
,
for
v\in D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}
,

\displaystyle\langle e^{2\pi i\varphi/\sqrt{n}}{\bf 1},f_{u/\sqrt{n}}\rangle_{\pi}e^{-2\pi i\varphi(x_{0})/\sqrt{n}}f_{u/\sqrt{n}}(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(\frac{\|\varphi\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\left(1+O\left(\frac{\|v\|^{2}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=1+O\left(\frac{\|v\|}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Note that the change of variable
v\mapsto v/\sqrt{n}
yields
u\mapsto u/\sqrt{n}
and
\varphi\mapsto\varphi/\sqrt{n}
.
For each
k\geq 0
,

\int_{D_{n^{1/8}}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\frac{\|v\|^{k}}{\sqrt{n}}\,dv\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\|v\|^{k}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\,dv=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Summarizing the above estimates with
e^{-c_{\delta}n}\ll n^{-\frac{m+1}{2}}
yields for
x\in\Gamma
and for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{n^{1/8}}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle/\sqrt{n}}\,dv+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}\right).

(3.16)

A direct computation on the Fourier transform yields for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all
x\in\Gamma
,

\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle/\sqrt{n}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\,dv.

Abusing notations, we have a constant
\alpha>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and uniformly in

,

\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{n^{1/8}}}e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(x)\rangle/\sqrt{n}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\,dv+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}e^{-\alpha n^{1/4}}\right).

(3.17)

Therefore by (
3.16
) and (
3.17
),
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and uniformly in
x\in\Gamma
,

\mu_{n}(x)=\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}\right).

Furthermore the explicit form of
\Sigma
is obtained by Lemma

, as claimed.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
such that
{\rm supp}\,\mu
is finite and
\Gamma=\langle{\rm supp}\,\mu\rangle
, and
\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
be a
\mu
-random walk with
w_{0}={\rm id}
.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all real
r>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\big(|w_{n}|_{S}\geq r\big)\leq C\exp\Big(-\frac{r^{2}}{Cn}\Big).

Proof.

The proof follows from the Gaussian estimates established in a more general setting (
[
HSC93
, the proof of Theorem 9.1]
and
[
Woe00
, Chapter 14]
).
We provide an alternative proof adapted to this setting for the sake of completeness.

Note that if
\{\Phi(w_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
is a martingale with respect to the filtration associated with
\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
, then the proof follows from a concentration inequality.
In general,
\{\Phi(w_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
is not a martingale.
What we do below is to replace
\Phi
by another
\Lambda
-equivariant map which makes the images of
w_{n}
form a martingale.

First we claim that there exists a
\Lambda
-
equivariant harmonic map

\Phi_{H}:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
This is a map satisfying the following:
\Phi_{H}(gx)=\Phi_{H}(x)+g
for all
x\in\Gamma
and for all
g\in\Lambda

under the identification between
\Lambda
and a lattice in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
and

\sum_{s\in{\rm supp}\,\mu}\left(\Phi_{H}(xs)-\Phi_{H}(x)\right)\mu(s)=0\quad\text{for each $x\in\Gamma$}.

This map is obtained from a
\Lambda
-equivariant lift of a Dirichlet energy minimizing map from
G=(V(G),E(G))
with weights on edges
c(e)
for
e\in E(G)
into the flat torus
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/\Lambda
equipped with metric as a quotient of the standard Euclidean space.
The existence of such a map is shown by a simple variational calculus
[
KS01
, Theorem 2.3]
(see also
[
Sun13
, Chapter 7]
).
(In general,
\Phi_{H}
is not necessarily injective, but this does not affect the following discussion.)
For a
\Lambda
-equivariant harmonic map
\Phi_{H}
, we have a martingale
\{\Phi_{H}(w_{n})\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
with respect to the natural filtration.

Next note that the map
\Phi_{H}
yields a quasi-isometry between
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
In particular, there exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that

\|\Phi_{H}(x)\|_{\infty}\geq c_{0}|x|_{S}-c_{1}\quad\text{for all $x\in\Gamma$}.

(3.18)

In the above,
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Finally, each component of
\Phi_{H}(w_{n})
in the coordinate of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a martingale with a uniformly bounded difference
B
for some
B>0
.
Hence a union bound and the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality show that by (
3.18
),
for all
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(|w_{n}|_{S}\geq(r+c_{1})/c_{0}\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\Phi_{H}(w_{n})\|_{\infty}\geq r\right)\leq 2m\exp\Big(-\frac{r^{2}}{2B^{2}n}\Big).

Therefore taking a large enough constant
C>0
concludes the inequality as claimed.
∎

For the
\xi_{\Sigma}
in Theorem
3.6
,
let us define a
discrete normal distribution

{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{\Sigma}
on
\Gamma
by

{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{\Sigma}(x):=\frac{1}{Z}\xi_{\Sigma}(x)\quad\text{where $Z:=\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\xi_{\Sigma}(x)$ for $x\in\Gamma$}.

Theorem 3.8
.

In the same setting and assumption as in Theorem
3.6
,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all integers
n>1
,

\|\mu_{n}-{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{n\Sigma}\|_{\rm TV}\leq\frac{C(\log n)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

The local central limit theorem (Theorem
3.6
) implies that there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\sup_{x\in\Gamma}|\mu_{n}(x)-\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}.

(3.19)

For a
\mu
-random walk
\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
with
w_{0}={\rm id}
on
\Gamma
,
Lemma
3.7
,
there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all
r>0
,

{\mathbb{P}}\big(|w_{n}|_{S}\geq r\big)\leq C\exp\Big(-\frac{r^{2}}{Cn}\Big).

(3.20)

A direct computation on
\xi_{n\Sigma}
yields for a (possibly different) constant
C>0
,
for all
n>0
and for all
r>C\sqrt{n}
,

\sum_{|x|_{S}\geq r}\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)\leq C\exp\Big(-\frac{r^{2}}{Cn}\Big).

(3.21)

Indeed, this follows from an approximation by a Gaussian
f_{\Sigma}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
for which
\xi_{\Sigma}=f_{\Sigma}\circ\Phi
, and that
\Phi
yields a quasi-isometry between
{\rm Cay}(\Gamma,S)
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Note that
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}=\bigcup_{x\in\Gamma}x\overline{C}_{0}
for the closure
\overline{C}_{0}
of a relatively compact fundamental domain
C_{0}
of
\Gamma
.
For
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
i=1,2
,
the following holds:

\displaystyle|\langle v_{1},\Sigma^{-1}v_{1}\rangle-\langle v_{2},\Sigma^{-1}v_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\langle v_{1}+t(v_{2}-v_{1}),\Sigma^{-1}(v_{1}+t(v_{2}-v_{1}))\rangle\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}|\langle v_{2}-v_{1},\Sigma^{-1}(v_{1}+t(v_{2}-v_{1}))\rangle|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Sigma^{-1}\|\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|\max\{\|v_{1}\|,\|v_{2}\|\}.

Letting
{\rm diam\,}C_{0}
denote the diameter of
\overline{C}_{0}
,
we have that by the above inequality, if
v_{i}\in x\overline{C}_{0}
and
\|v_{i}\|\geq{\rm diam\,}C_{0}
,
i=1,2
,
then
\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|\leq\|v_{2}\|
and

|\langle v_{1},\Sigma^{-1}v_{1}\rangle-\langle v_{2},\Sigma^{-1}v_{2}\rangle|\leq 2\|\Sigma^{-1}\|\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|(\|v_{2}\|+\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|)\leq 4\|\Sigma^{-1}\|{\rm diam\,}C_{0}\|v_{2}\|.

Thus, for all
v_{i}\in x\overline{C}_{0}
,
i=1,2
,

f_{n\Sigma}(v_{1})\leq f_{n\Sigma}(v_{2})e^{\frac{c}{n}\|v_{2}\|},\quad\text{where $c:=2\|\Sigma^{-1}\|{\rm diam\,}C_{0}$}.

Since
\Sigma^{-1}
is positive definite, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that

\langle v,\Sigma^{-1}v\rangle\geq\alpha\|v\|^{2}
for
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Therefore noting that
\|\Phi(x)\|\geq c_{0}|x|_{S}-c_{1}
for all
x\in\Gamma
,
we estimate

\sum_{|x|_{S}\geq r}\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)\leq\frac{1}{{\rm vol}(C_{0})}\int_{\|v\|\geq c_{0}r-c_{1}}\frac{1}{(2\pi n)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2n}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{c}{n}\|v\|}\,dv,

for
r\geq({\rm diam\,}C_{0}+c_{1})/c_{0}
, where
{\rm vol}(C_{0})
is the volume of
C_{0}
.
By the change of variables
v\mapsto\sqrt{n}v
, the right hand side equals the following:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm vol}(C_{0})}\int_{\|v\|\geq(c_{0}r-c_{1})/\sqrt{n}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\|v\|^{2}+\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}\|v\|}\,dv

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=c_{m,\Sigma}\int_{(c_{0}r-c_{1})/\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}s^{2}+\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}s}s^{m-1}\,ds,

where
c_{m,\Sigma}
is a constant depending only on
\Sigma
and
m
in the polar coordinate.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{(c_{0}r-c_{1})/\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(s-\frac{c}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{2\alpha n}}s^{m-1}\,ds=e^{\frac{c^{2}}{2\alpha n}}\int_{R}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}s^{2}}\left(s+\frac{c}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}\right)^{m-1}\,ds,

where
R:=(c_{0}r-c_{1})/\sqrt{n}-c/(\alpha\sqrt{n})
by change of variables.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left(s+c/(\alpha\sqrt{n})\right)^{m-1}\leq se^{\frac{\alpha}{4}s^{2}}
for all
s>C
.
Hence there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n>0
and
r>C\sqrt{n}
,
the last term is at most

e^{\frac{c^{2}}{2\alpha n}}\int_{R}^{\infty}se^{-\frac{\alpha}{4}s^{2}}\,ds=e^{\frac{c^{2}}{2\alpha n}}\frac{2}{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{4}R^{2}}\leq Ce^{-\frac{r^{2}}{Cn}}.

This shows (
3.21
).

Combining (
3.19
), (
3.20
) and (
3.21
) yields for all
n>0
and for all
r>C\sqrt{n}
,

\displaystyle\|\mu_{n}-\xi_{n\Sigma}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|x|_{S}\leq r}|\mu_{n}(x)-\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)|+\sum_{|x|_{S}>r}|\mu_{n}(x)-\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|B_{S}(r)|\frac{C}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}+2C\exp\Big(-\frac{r^{2}}{Cn}\Big).

In the above
B_{S}(r):=\{x\in\Gamma\ :\ |x|_{S}\leq r\}
.
Note that
|B_{S}(r)|=\Theta(r^{m})
, in particular,
|B_{S}(r)|\leq Cr^{m}
for all
r\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
for a constant
C>0
independent of
r
since
\Phi
is a
\Lambda
-equivariant injective map from
\Gamma
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Letting
r=A\sqrt{n\log n}
for a constant
A>0
, we have
r>C\sqrt{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,
and

\|\mu_{n}-\xi_{n\Sigma}\|_{1}\leq\frac{C^{2}A^{m}(\log n)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{2C}{n^{A^{2}/C}}.

Fixing a large enough constant
A
such that
A^{2}/C>1/2
shows that there exists a constant
C_{1}
such that for all
n>1
,

\|\mu_{n}-\xi_{n\Sigma}\|_{1}\leq\frac{C_{1}(\log n)^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\sqrt{n}}.

(3.22)

Note that there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)=1+\sum_{x\in\Gamma\setminus\{{\rm id}\}}\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)=1+O(e^{-cn}).

(3.23)

Indeed, since
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
is a finite index subgroup of

and

is a

-equivariant embedding with a discrete image in

,

(cf. (

) in Appendix

) on finitely many orbits of

).
This implies that for all

,

\|\xi_{n\Sigma}-{\mathcal{N}}^{\Phi}_{n\Sigma}\|_{1}=\sum_{x\in\Gamma}\Big|\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)-\frac{1}{\sum_{z\in\Gamma}\xi_{n\Sigma}(z)}\xi_{n\Sigma}(x)\Big|=O(e^{-cn}).

Therefore this together with (
3.22
), adjusting a constant factor

yields the claim.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

) suffices for our purpose in Theorem

Noise sensitivity on affine Weyl groups

as in Section

.
For a probability measure
\mu

\Gamma

\pi^{\rho}=\rho(\mu\times\mu)+(1-\rho)\mu_{\rm diag}
,
where

\mu_{\rm diag}((x,y))=\mu(x)

Appendix A
Noise sensitivity problem on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}

Theorem A.1
.

Let
m
be a positive integer and
\mu
be a probability measure on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.
If
\mu
has a finite second moment and the support generates the group as a semigroup,
then

\lim_{\rho\to 1}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=0\quad{and}\quad\lim_{\rho\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=1.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-degenerate positive definite (covariance) matrix of size
m
and

f_{\Sigma}(v):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{m}\det\Sigma}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle v,\Sigma^{-1}v\rangle}\quad\text{for $v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$}.

Let us define the function

F(v):=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}f_{\Sigma}(x+v)
for
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Note that since
F
is
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
-periodic on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, it is regarded as a function on
[0,1)^{m}
.
Let

\widehat{F}(x):=\int_{[0,1)^{m}}F(v)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,x\rangle}\,dv\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{f}_{\Sigma}(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}f_{\Sigma}(v)e^{-2\pi i\langle v,x\rangle}\,dv\quad\text{for $x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}$}.

Since
f_{\Sigma}
is in the Schwartz class on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we have
\widehat{F}(x)=\widehat{f}_{\Sigma}(x)
for
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
and

F(v)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\widehat{f}_{\Sigma}(x)e^{2\pi i\langle x,v\rangle},

where the right hand side is absolutely convergent.
In the case when
v=0
, the Poisson summation formula is obtained by a direct computation,

\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}f_{\Sigma}(x)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\widehat{f}_{\Sigma}(x)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle x,\Sigma x\rangle}.

Thus there exists a constant
c_{\Sigma}>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}f_{n\Sigma}(x)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}e^{-2\pi^{2}n\langle x,\Sigma x\rangle}=1+O_{\Sigma}(e^{-c_{\Sigma}n}).

(A.1)

Let
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
be covariance matrices of size
m
.
First we consider the upper bound.
For every real
\lambda>0
and for every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\|x\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)-f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\|x\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}\left|f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}}f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)\right|+\sum_{\|x\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}\left|1-\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}}\right|f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x).

(A.2)

The first sum in (
A.2
) is estimated as follows:
For
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\Big|1-e^{-\frac{1}{2n}\langle x,(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1})x\rangle}\Big|\leq e^{\frac{1}{2n}|\langle x,(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1})x\rangle|}-e^{-\frac{1}{2n}|\langle x,(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1})x\rangle|}.

Since
|\langle x,(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1})x\rangle|\leq\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|\|x\|^{2}
,
for
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
with
\|x\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}
,

\Big|1-e^{-\frac{1}{2n}\langle x,(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1})x\rangle}\Big|\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|}-e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|}=2\sinh\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|\right).

Therefore the first sum in the right hand side of (
A.2
) is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{\|x\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}\Big|1-e^{-\frac{1}{2n}\langle x,(\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1})x\rangle}\Big|f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)\leq 3\lambda^{2}\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|\left(1+O_{\Sigma_{1}}(e^{-c_{\Sigma_{1}}n})\right),

if
\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|\leq 2/\lambda^{2}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all
\lambda>0
by (
A.1
).
The second sum in the right hand side of (
A.2
) is at most, for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, by (
A.1
),

\displaystyle\left|1-\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}}\right|\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)=\left|1-\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}}\right|\left(1+O_{\Sigma_{2}}(e^{-c_{\Sigma_{2}}n})\right).

Summarizing the above estimates yields for every
\lambda>0
,
there exist constants
C_{1,2,\lambda}=C_{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\lambda}>0
and
c_{1,2}=c_{\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}}>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\|x\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)-f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)|\leq 3\lambda^{2}\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1}-\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\|+\left|1-\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma_{2}}{\det\Sigma_{1}}}\right|+C_{1,2,\lambda}e^{-c_{1,2}n}.

(A.3)

Next let us consider the lower bound.
Noting that
\sqrt{f_{1}-f_{2}}\geq\sqrt{f_{1}}-\sqrt{f_{2}}
for
f_{1}\geq f_{2}\geq 0
,
we have by squaring both sides and summing over
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,

\displaystyle\|f_{n\Sigma_{1}}-f_{n\Sigma_{2}}\|_{1}\geq\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)+\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)-2\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\sqrt{f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)}.

In the following, we assume that
\Sigma_{1}^{-1}+\Sigma_{2}^{-1}
is invertible.
In that case, by (
A.1
),

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\sqrt{f_{n\Sigma_{1}}(x)f_{n\Sigma_{2}}(x)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{m}{2}}(\det(n\Sigma_{1})\det(n\Sigma_{2}))^{\frac{1}{4}}}e^{-\frac{1}{4n}\langle x,(\Sigma_{1}^{-1}+\Sigma_{2}^{-1})x\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\frac{m}{2}}(\det\left((\Sigma_{1}^{-1}+\Sigma_{2}^{-1})^{-1}\right))^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(\det\Sigma_{1}\det\Sigma_{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}e^{-4\pi^{2}n\langle x,(\Sigma_{1}^{-1}+\Sigma_{2}^{-1})^{-1}x\rangle}.

Let us focus on the special case when
m=2
, and

\Sigma^{1}=\sigma^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma^{\rho}=\sigma^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1-\rho\\
1-\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{for $\sigma>0$ and $0<\rho\leq 1$}.

In this case,

\displaystyle(\Sigma^{1})^{-1}+(\Sigma^{\rho})^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1+\frac{1}{\rho(2-\rho)}&-\frac{1-\rho}{\rho(2-\rho)}\\
-\frac{1-\rho}{\rho(2-\rho)}&1+\frac{1}{\rho(2-\rho)}\end{pmatrix}.

Furthermore,

\det\Sigma^{1}=\sigma^{4}
,
\det\Sigma^{\rho}=\sigma^{4}\rho(2-\rho)
and

\det\left((\Sigma^{1})^{-1}+(\Sigma^{\rho})^{-1}\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho(2-\rho)}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1-\rho}{\rho(2-\rho)}\right)^{2}\right).

Hence one computes for all small enough
\rho>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(\rho(2-\rho))^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho(2-\rho)}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{1-\rho}{\rho(2-\rho)}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{2(\rho(2-\rho))^{\frac{3}{4}}}{\sqrt{(\rho(2-\rho)+1)^{2}-(1-\rho)^{2}}}<2\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Summarizing the above computations and letting
n\to\infty
yield, for all small enough
\rho>0
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}-f_{n\Sigma^{1}}\|_{1}\geq 2-4\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}.

(A.4)

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

Noting that
{\mathbb{E}}\,_{\pi^{\rho}_{n}}{\bm{w}}_{n}
is independent of
\rho\in[0,1]
,
we assume that
\mu
has mean zero up to shifting by the mean.
Further, we assume that
\mu
is aperiodic (i.e., for each
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
for all large enough
n
one has
\mu_{n}(x)>0
) and the general case is reduced to this case by dividing the times according to the period.
For
0<\rho\leq 1
, we note that
\pi^{\rho}
is aperiodic since
\pi^{\rho}
and
\mu\times\mu
have the same support in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2m}
.
Let
\Sigma^{\rho}
and
\Sigma^{1}
denote the covariance matrices of size
2m
for
\pi^{\rho}
and
\mu\times\mu
respectively.

First let us show the upper bound in the claim.
For all real
\lambda>0
and integers
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|>\lambda n^{1/2}}\pi^{\rho}_{n}({\bm{x}})={\mathbb{P}}_{\pi^{\rho}}\left(\|{\bm{w}}_{n}\|>\lambda n^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}n}{\mathbb{E}}\,_{\pi^{\rho}_{n}}\|{\bm{w}}_{n}\|^{2}=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\,_{\mu}|x|^{2},

by the Chebyshev inequality.
This shows that (recalling that
\pi^{1}=\mu\times\mu
)

\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|>\lambda n^{1/2}}|\pi^{\rho}_{n}({\bm{x}})-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}({\bm{x}})|\leq\frac{4}{\lambda^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\,_{\mu}|x|^{2}.

(A.5)

Since
\pi^{\rho}
is aperiodic for
\rho\in(0,1]
,
then the local central limit theorem
[
LL10
, Theorem 2.3.9]
shows the following:
There exists a sequence
\delta_{n}>0
such that
\delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
and for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2m}
,

|\pi^{\rho}_{n}({\bm{x}})-f_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}({\bm{x}})|\leq\frac{\delta_{n}}{n^{m}}\quad\text{and}\quad|\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}({\bm{x}})-f_{n\Sigma^{1}}({\bm{x}})|\leq\frac{\delta_{n}}{n^{m}}.

(A.6)

Note that there exists a constant
C_{m}>0
such that for all real
\lambda>0
and all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}
, the number of
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2m}
with
\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}
is at most
C_{m}\lambda^{2m}n^{m}
.
Therefore it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|\pi^{\rho}_{n}({\bm{x}})-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}({\bm{x}})|\leq C_{m}\lambda^{2m}n^{m}\cdot\frac{2\delta_{n}}{n^{m}}+\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|f_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}({\bm{x}})-f_{n\Sigma^{1}}({\bm{x}})|.

(A.7)

Hence by (
A.3
), (
A.5
) and (
A.7
), for every
\lambda>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{1}\leq 3\lambda^{2}\|(\Sigma^{\rho})^{-1}-(\Sigma^{1})^{-1}\|+\left|1-\sqrt{\frac{\det\Sigma^{\rho}}{\det\Sigma^{1}}}\right|+\frac{4}{\lambda^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\,_{\mu}|x|^{2}.

Since
\Sigma^{\rho}\to\Sigma^{1}
as
\rho\to 1
,
we obtain

\limsup_{\rho\to 1}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{1}\leq\frac{4}{\lambda^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\,_{\mu}|x|^{2}.

This holds for all
\lambda>0
, and thus
we obtain in the total variation distance

\lim_{\rho\to 1}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=0.

This shows the upper bound in the claim.

Next let us show the lower bound in the claim.
The general case reduces to the case when
m=1
since a projection
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{Z}}
(whence
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2m}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
) to a coordinate only decreases the total variation distance.
Let
\Sigma=\Sigma^{\rho}
.
For
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
i=1,2
,

\displaystyle|\langle{\bm{x}}_{1},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}_{1}\rangle-\langle{\bm{x}}_{2},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}_{2}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}|\langle{\bm{x}}_{2}-{\bm{x}}_{1},\Sigma^{-1}({\bm{x}}_{1}+t({\bm{x}}_{2}-{\bm{x}}_{1}))\rangle|\,dt

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\Sigma^{-1}\|\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|\max\{\|{\bm{x}}_{1}\|,\|{\bm{x}}_{2}\|\}.

Thus, if
\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
(where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm) and
\|{\bm{x}}_{i}\|\geq\sqrt{2}
,
i=1,2
,
then
\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|\leq\|{\bm{x}}_{2}\|
and

|\langle{\bm{x}}_{1},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}_{1}\rangle-\langle{\bm{x}}_{2},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}_{2}\rangle|\leq 2\|\Sigma^{-1}\|\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|(\|{\bm{x}}_{2}\|+\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|)\leq 4\sqrt{2}\|\Sigma^{-1}\|\|{\bm{x}}_{2}\|.

This shows that for
{\bm{x}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that
\|{\bm{x}}_{1}-{\bm{x}}_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\|{\bm{x}}_{i}\|\geq\sqrt{2}
,
i=1,2
,

f_{n\Sigma}({\bm{x}}_{1})\leq f_{n\Sigma}({\bm{x}}_{2})e^{\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{n}\|{\bm{x}}_{2}\|},\quad\text{where $c_{\Sigma}:=2\sqrt{2}\|\Sigma^{-1}\|$}.

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|>\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}}f_{n\Sigma}({\bm{x}})\leq\int_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\geq\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2}\det(n\Sigma)}}e^{-\frac{1}{2n}\langle{\bm{x}},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}\rangle+\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{n}\|{\bm{x}}\|}\,d{\bm{x}}.

Since
\Sigma^{-1}
is positive definite, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that
\langle{\bm{x}},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}\rangle\geq\alpha\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2}
for all
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and

-\frac{1}{2n}\langle{\bm{x}},\Sigma^{-1}{\bm{x}}\rangle+\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{n}\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq-\frac{\alpha}{2n}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2}+\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{n}\|{\bm{x}}\|=-\frac{\alpha}{2n}\left(\|{\bm{x}}\|-\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{\alpha}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}.

This shows that the last integral is at most, by the change of variables
{\bm{x}}\mapsto\sqrt{n}{\bm{x}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\geq\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{2}\det(n\Sigma)}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2n}\left(\|{\bm{x}}\|-\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{\alpha}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}}\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}}}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\int_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\geq(\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{n}}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\|{\bm{x}}\|-\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}}\,d{\bm{x}}=\frac{e^{\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\int_{(\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{n}}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(s-\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}}s\,ds.

In the above, the last equality has used the polar coordinate.
There exists a constant
C_{\Sigma}>0
such that

s+c_{\Sigma}/(\alpha\sqrt{n})\leq se^{\frac{\alpha}{4}s^{2}}

for all
s>C_{\Sigma}
and all
n\geq 1
.
The last term above equals by the change of variables, for
R:=(\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{n}-c_{\Sigma}/(\alpha\sqrt{n})
,

\frac{e^{\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\int_{R}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}s^{2}}\left(s+\frac{c_{\Sigma}}{\alpha\sqrt{n}}\right)\,ds\leq\frac{e^{\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\int_{R}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{4}s^{2}}s\,ds=\frac{e^{\frac{c_{\Sigma}^{2}}{2\alpha n}}}{\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\frac{2}{\alpha}e^{-\frac{\alpha}{4}R^{2}}.

Note that for all
n>(c_{\Sigma}/\alpha)^{2}+2
,

R=(\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{n}-c_{\Sigma}/(\alpha\sqrt{n})\geq\lambda-\sqrt{2}/\sqrt{n}-1\geq\lambda-2.

Hence there exist constants
c_{\rho},C_{\rho}>0
such that for all
n>C_{\rho}
and all
\lambda\geq C_{\rho}
,

\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|>\lambda n^{\frac{1}{2}}}f_{n\Sigma}({\bm{x}})\leq C_{\rho}e^{-c_{\rho}\lambda^{2}}.

Therefore together with (
A.6
),
there exist (possibly different) constants
c_{\rho},C_{\rho}>0
depending only on
\rho

such that the following holds: For all
n>C_{\rho}
and all
\lambda\geq C_{\rho}
,

\displaystyle\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{1}\geq\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|\pi_{n}^{\rho}({\bm{x}})-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}({\bm{x}})|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\|{\bm{x}}\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|f_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}({\bm{x}})-f_{n\Sigma^{1}}({\bm{x}})|-2C_{m}\lambda^{2m}\delta_{n}\geq\|f_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}-f_{n\Sigma^{1}}\|_{1}-2C_{\rho}e^{-c_{\rho}\lambda^{2}}-2C_{m}\lambda^{2m}\delta_{n}.

Thus by (
A.4
), letting
n\to\infty
, we obtain for all small enough
\rho>0
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{1}\geq 2-4\rho^{\frac{1}{4}}-2C_{\rho}e^{-c_{\rho}\lambda^{2}}.

In total variation distance, letting
\lambda\to\infty
and then
\rho\to 0
yields

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=1,

as required.
∎
