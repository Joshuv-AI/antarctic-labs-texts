The camel-banana problem The proof of Lemma B has been replaced by a shorter proof on February 10, 2024.
Michiel de Bondt

Abstract




A camel can carry one banana at a time on its back. It is on a diet and
therefore can only have one banana at a time in its stomach. As soon as it
has eaten a banana it walks a mile and then it needs a new banana (in
order to be able to continue its itinerary).

Let there be a stock of
N
bananas at the border of the desert. How far
can the camel penetrate into the desert, starting at this point? (Of
course it can form new stocks with transported bananas.)

This was problem number 10 of the “Universitaire Wiskunde
Competitie” of 1992-93, a Dutch mathematical competition for students.
A partial solution then was given by the author. Here he presents a full
solution.

It is easy to see that the camel can penetrate
1
mile into the
desert with
1
banana,
2
miles with
2
bananas and
2\frac{1}{3}

miles with
3
bananas. But how far can the camel get with

73083734
bananas? We shall give the answer in the following pages.

We can assume that the camel will use only one line of the desert. It is
natural to take a real co-ordinate along this line, with the starting
point at the origin and a mile as the unit length. Positions along the
line will be given by their co-ordinate.
An ‘Ultra Wise Camel’ might apply the following strategy, since it is
optimal, as we shall prove.

UWC-STRATEGY: Repeat the following until there are
3
bananas left:

(1)

If the number of bananas left is
even
: eat a banana, carry
another banana as far as possible and return to the banana(s)
closest to the origin.

(2)

If the number of bananas left is
odd
: eat a banana, carry
another banana to the furthest banana, then carry both bananas
(each in turn) as far as possible, and finally return to the
banana(s) closest to the origin.

When there are
2
or
3
bananas left, do the above without returning to
the banana(s) closest to the origin.

Figure 3 shows the last
3

this strategy. Steps (1) and (2) are illustrated in Figures
1 and 2. The figures show that the following properties holding at the
beginning are not affected by applying the UWC-strategy:

(a)

During meal-time, the camel is with the banana(s) closest to the
origin and there is an interval of length
\frac{1}{2}
containing
all bananas.

(b)

Just before step (1), the bananas can be divided into
pairs
, such that the bananas of each pair are at the same
position (but different pairs may also be at the same position).
Figure 1 makes clear that the camel can transform the hindmost
pair into one furthest banana (with one of the two bananas being
eaten and the other being transported) and leave all other
pairs where they are in step (1).

(c)

Just before step (2), the bananas can be divided
into pairs in the same way as in (b), except one furthest
banana. Figure 2 makes clear that the camel can transform the
hindmost pair and the sole furthest banana into a new furthest
pair and leave all other pairs where they are in step (2).

Suppose that the camel applies the UWC-strategy. Since pairs of
bananas as described in properties (b) and (c)
are destructed only during meal-time, each position of a pair is an
eating position
: a position where a banana is eaten during the strategy.

Let

denote the eating position where the camel eats its

-th

0=e_{N}\leq e_{N-1}\leq\ldots\leq e_{2}\leq e_{1}=c(N)-1

2n

e_{n}=\frac{1}{4}e_{2n-2}+\frac{1}{2}e_{2n-1}+\frac{1}{4}e_{2n}+\frac{1}{2}\quad(n\geq 2)

(1)

Note that for
n=2

e_{2}=\frac{2}{3}e_{3}+\frac{1}{3}e_{4}+\frac{2}{3}

This implies that for each

,

,

The same

Note also that

.

Theorem I.

If

, then

\displaystyle c(2n+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(2n)+\frac{1}{2}c(2n+2)

\displaystyle c(2n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(n)+\frac{1}{2}c(n+1)+\frac{1}{2}

Proof:

The examples above show that the first equality holds for
n=1
,

n>1
.
Suppose that there are
2n
bananas left and the camel is about to eat its
next banana, while applying the UWC-strategy for
N
bananas
(
N\geq 2n
). Then:

•

if
N=2n
then there are
2n
bananas at
0
, so

e_{2n}=\ldots=e_{n+1}=0
, and
c(2n)=\lambda_{n,0}
,

•

if
N=2n+1
then there are
2n-2
bananas at
0
and
2
bananas
at
\frac{1}{4}
, so
e_{2n}=\ldots=e_{n+2}=0
,

e_{n+1}=\frac{1}{4}
, and
c(2n+1)=\frac{1}{4}\lambda_{n,1}+\lambda_{n,0}
,

•

if

then there are
2n-2
bananas at
0
and
2
bananas
at
\frac{1}{2}
, so
e_{2n}=\ldots=e_{n+2}=0
,

e_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}
, and
c(2n+2)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{n,1}+\lambda_{n,0}
.

The first equality of the theorem thus is proved.
The proof of the second equality is slightly more complicated. We
split it into two cases,
n=2m
and
n=2m+1
:

\displaystyle c(4m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(2m)+\frac{1}{2}c(2m+1)+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle c(4m+2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(2m+1)+\frac{1}{2}c(2m+2)+\frac{1}{2}

The above examples show that these equalities hold for

We apply the UWC-strategy for

•

If

\frac{1}{2}

2

\frac{5}{8}

\displaystyle c(4m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=2}^{m}\lambda_{m,i}+\frac{5}{8}\lambda_{m,1}+\lambda_{m,0}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\lambda_{m,1}+\lambda_{m,0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}c(2m)+\frac{1}{2}c(2m+1)+\frac{1}{2}

•

Although the equalities of theorem I and the initial value
c(1)=1

completely determine the numbers
c(n)
for all

, to compute, say,

, it is more convenient to have an explicit formula. We
now shall derive one.

Define

F(n,m)=\frac{{}^{2}\!\log m}{2}+g(\frac{n-1}{m})+\frac{(n-1)\pmod{2}}{6m^{2}}\quad(n,m>0)

(2)

where

g(x)=-\frac{1}{6}x^{2}+x+1

Then it is a trivial exercise to verify the following equalities:

\displaystyle F(2m,2m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F(2m,m)

(3)

\displaystyle F(2n+1,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(2n,m)+\frac{1}{2}F(2n+2,m)

(4)

\displaystyle F(2n,2m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(n,m)+\frac{1}{2}F(n+1,m)+\frac{1}{2}

(5)

Define

h(n)=2^{\lfloor{{}^{2}\!\log n}\rfloor}\quad(n\geq 1)

h(n)
is the largest power of

.

Theorem II.

c(n)=F(n,h(n))\quad(n\geq 1)

Proof:

The case
n=1
is trivial, so let
n>1

\displaystyle F(2n+1,h(2n+1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(2n,h(2n))+\frac{1}{2}F(2n+2,h(2n+2))\quad(n\geq 1)

\displaystyle F(2n,h(2n))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(n,h(n))+\frac{1}{2}F(n+1,h(n+1))+\frac{1}{2}\quad(n\geq 1)

To do so, we consider two cases:

•

n+1=2^{k}

Then

,

h(2n+2)=2^{k+1}
. By
(

\displaystyle F(2n+1,n+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(2n,n+1)+\frac{1}{2}F(2n+2,2n+2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(2n,n+1)+\frac{1}{2}F(2n+2,n+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}F(n,\frac{n+1}{2})+\frac{1}{2}F(n+1,n+1)+\frac{1}{2}

c(73083734)=14\frac{1003590240076691}{1125899906842624}

Before proving the optimality of the UWC-strategy, we first
derive two more properties of the UWC-strategy. Let

s_{n}=e_{1}+2\sum_{k=2}^{n}e_{k}

(which by definition also includes
s_{1}=e_{1}
).

Lemma A.

\begin{array}[]{ll}s_{n}=N-1&\quad(n\geq N/2)\\
s_{n}=\frac{1}{4}s_{2n-1}+\frac{1}{4}s_{2n}+\frac{2n-1}{2}&\quad(n\leq N/2)\end{array}

Proof:

Suppose that the camel has just eaten
its last banana in the UWC-strategy.
It has walked
N-1
miles in total, of which, say,
f
miles in forward
direction, and
b

miles backward. Then
f+b=N-1
and
f-b=e_{1}
, so
f=(N-1+e_{1})/2
.
Since the camel always had a banana on its back when it
walked forward, the sum
\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}
of the positions of the banana
skins equals
(N-1+e_{1})/2
. Since
e_{n}=0
if
n\geq N/2+1
,
it follows that
s_{n}=N-1
for all
n\geq N/2
.

To prove the remaining part of the lemma, we note that by equation
(
1
)

\displaystyle 2e_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e_{2k-2}+e_{2k-1}+\frac{1}{2}e_{2k}+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(2e_{2k-2}+2e_{2k-1})+\frac{1}{4}(2e_{2k-1}+2e_{2k})+1

for
k=2
,
3
,
\ldots
,
n
. Since also

e_{1}=\frac{1}{2}e_{1}+\frac{1}{2}e_{2}+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}s_{1}+\frac{1}{4}s_{2}+\frac{1}{2}

the desired result follows by addition.

Now let
S
be any optimal strategy for
N
bananas.
Let
e_{1}^{\prime}
,
e_{2}^{\prime}
,
\ldots
,
e_{N}^{\prime}

be the eating positions of the
N
bananas
(which are all used), numbered in such a way that

0=e_{N}^{\prime}\leq e_{N-1}^{\prime}\leq\ldots\leq e_{2}^{\prime}\leq e_{1}^{\prime}

Note that we do not suppose that the bananas are eaten in the order

e_{N}^{\prime}
,
e_{N-1}^{\prime}
,
\ldots
,
e_{1}^{\prime}
, although we
may (and shall) assume that the first banana is eaten at

e_{N}^{\prime}
, and the last one at
e_{1}^{\prime}
. In between, however, the
order might be different.

As above, we define

s_{n}^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}+2\sum_{k=2}^{n}e_{k}^{\prime}

and as in the proof of Lemma A, we suppose that the camel has just eaten
its last banana. Again, it has walked
N-1
miles,
of which
(N-1+e_{1}^{\prime})/2
miles in forward direction. Since it
always had at most one banana on its back when it walked forward, the
sum
\sum_{n=1}^{N}e_{n}^{\prime}
of the positions of the banana skins is at
most
(N-1+e_{1}^{\prime})/2
. This implies that
s_{N}^{\prime}\leq N-1
, and since

s_{n}^{\prime}\leq s_{N}^{\prime}
for all

, we also have
s_{n}^{\prime}\leq N-1
for
all

.

As an analogue of Lemma A, we formulate:

Lemma B.

\begin{array}[]{ll}s_{n}^{\prime}\leq N-1&\quad(n\geq N/2)\\
s_{n}^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{4}s_{2n-1}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{4}s_{2n}^{\prime}+\frac{2n-1}{2}&\quad(n\leq N/2)\end{array}

The second inequality of Lemma B is somewhat more difficult to prove.
Before giving a proof of lemma B, we note that lemmas A
and B together yield
s_{n}^{\prime}\leq s_{n}
for all

(start with

and
proceed in descending order), so in particular

e_{1}^{\prime}+1=s_{1}^{\prime}+1\leq s_{1}+1=e_{1}+1=c(N)
. Since

e_{1}^{\prime}+1\geq c(N)
in

. So
e_{1}^{\prime}+1=c(N)
, which amounts to the optimality of the
UWC-strategy.

Theorem III.

The UWC-strategy is optimal.

Proof:

We are done when we shall have proved lemma B.

Let
\beta=e^{\prime}_{2n}+\frac{1}{2}
. During the
\frac{1}{2}
mile before eating a banana on position
e^{\prime}_{i}
, the camel can maximize the portion of its walk that is done in the interval
[\beta,\infty)
by walking from position
e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}
to position
e^{\prime}_{i}
. It walks at most
\max\{e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2},\beta\}-\max\{e^{\prime}_{i},\beta\}
miles in
[\beta,\infty)
. During the
\frac{1}{2}
mile after eating a banana on position
e^{\prime}_{i}
, the camel can maximize the portion of its walk that is done in the interval
[\beta,\infty)
by walking from position
e^{\prime}_{i}
to position
e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}
. Again, it walks at most
\max\{e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2},\beta\}-\max\{e^{\prime}_{i},\beta\}
miles in
[\beta,\infty)
.

For
2\leq i\leq n
, we estimate
\max\{e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2},\beta\}-\max\{e^{\prime}_{i},\beta\}
by
\frac{1}{2}
. For
n+1\leq i\leq 2n
, we estimate
\max\{e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2},\beta\}-\max\{e^{\prime}_{i},\beta\}
by
\max\{e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2},\beta\}-\beta=e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-\beta
. So before eating its last banana on position
e^{\prime}_{1}
, the camel walks

\alpha\leq(e^{\prime}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}-\max\{e^{\prime}_{1},\beta\})+2\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{2}+2\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}(e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-\beta)

miles in
[\beta,\infty)
. These
\alpha
miles add up to a walk from position
\beta
to position
\max\{e^{\prime}_{1},\beta\}
, of which

\frac{\alpha-(\max\{e^{\prime}_{1},\beta\}-\beta)}{2}

miles are in backward direction and

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha+(\max\{e^{\prime}_{1},\beta\}-\beta)}{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big(e^{\prime}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}-\beta\big)+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}(e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}-\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(e^{\prime}_{1}-e^{\prime}_{2n})+\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{2n-1}{2}-n\cdot\beta

miles are in forward direction.

The camel carries bananas to positions
e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{n}
, so

\alpha^{\prime}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\max\{e^{\prime}_{i},\beta\}-\beta)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\prime}_{i}-n\cdot\beta

Combining the upper bound and the lower bound of
\alpha^{\prime}
, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\prime}_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}(e^{\prime}_{1}-e^{\prime}_{2n})+\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}e^{\prime}_{i}+\frac{2n-1}{2}

Consequently

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}s^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}e^{\prime}_{2n}+\sum_{i=n+1}^{2n}e_{i}+\frac{2n-1}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{4}s^{\prime}_{2n-1}-\frac{1}{4}s^{\prime}_{2n})+(\frac{1}{2}s^{\prime}_{2n}-\frac{1}{2}s^{\prime}_{n})+\frac{2n-1}{2}

This proves lemma B, and, as a consequence, theorem III.
