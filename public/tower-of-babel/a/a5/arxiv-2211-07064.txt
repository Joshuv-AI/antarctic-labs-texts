Wilson Area Law formula on ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4}
Adrian P. C. Lim

Abstract
Let 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} be the Lie Algebra of a compact semi-simple gauge group. For a 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} -valued 1-form A A , consider the Yang-Mills action S YM ​ ( A ) = ∫ ℝ 4 | 𝑑 A + A ∧ A | 2 ​ 𝑑 ω , S_{{\rm YM}}(A)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\left|dA+A\wedge A\right|^{2}\ d\omega, using the Euclidean metric on T ​ ℝ 4 T\mathbb{R}^{4} . We want to make sense of the following path integral, Tr ​ ∫ A ∈ 𝒜 ℝ 4 , 𝔤 / 𝒢 exp ⁡ [ c ​ ∫ S 𝑑 A ] ​ e − 1 2 ​ S YM ​ ( A ) ​ D ​ A , {\rm Tr}\ \int_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}^{4},\mathfrak{g}}/\mathcal{G}}\exp\left[c\int_{S}dA\right]e^{-\frac{1}{2}S_{{\rm YM}}(A)}\ DA, whereby D ​ A DA is some Lebesgue type of measure on the space of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} -valued 1-forms, modulo gauge transformations 𝒜 ℝ 4 , 𝔤 / 𝒢 \mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{R}^{4},\mathfrak{g}}/\mathcal{G} . Here, S S is some compact flat rectangular surface. Using an Abstract Wiener space, we can define a Yang-Mills path integral rigorously, for a compact semi-simple gauge group. Subsequently, we will then derive the Wilson area law formula from the definition, using renormalization techniques and asymptotic freedom. One of the most important applications of the Area Law formula will be to explain why the potential measured between a quark and antiquark is a linear function of its distance.

Lemma 3.1

Refer to Definition
2.13
for
\mathcal{Y}^{\kappa}
. Consider the probability space
{\mathbb{B}}\otimes\mathfrak{g}
equipped with a product Wiener measure
\tilde{\mu}_{\kappa^{2}}^{\times^{N}}
. For any
\kappa>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Y}^{\kappa}\right]:=\int_{{\mathbb{B}}\otimes\mathfrak{g}}\mathcal{Y}^{\kappa}d\tilde{\mu}_{\kappa^{2}}^{\times^{N}}

is finite.

Proof.
Observe that
Y_{1}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)+Y_{2}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)+Y_{3}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)

is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma=1}^{N}\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\ \Bigg[\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\Big|\left(B,\kappa\xi_{ij,\gamma}(w)\right)_{\sharp}+\sum_{\alpha,\beta}c_{\gamma}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\left(B^{\otimes^{2}},\pi_{i,\alpha,w}\otimes\pi_{j,\beta,w}\right)_{\sharp}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\left|\left(B,\kappa\xi_{ij,\gamma}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\Bigg].

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)=

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg[-\frac{1}{2}\left[Y_{1}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)+Y_{2}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)+Y_{3}^{\kappa}\left(B\right)\right]\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\left|\left(B,\kappa\xi_{ij,\alpha}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right],

whereby
\{\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij,\alpha}(w)\right)_{\sharp}:\ 1\leq\alpha\leq N,1\leq i<j\leq 3\}
is a set of i.i.d. complex random variables with mean 0. Hence, it suffices to show

\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\left|\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right]

is integrable on
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Using Jensen’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\exp

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\left|\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left|\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right].

Now, we can write the complex-valued random variable

\displaystyle\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij,\alpha}(w)\right)_{\sharp}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{-1}\left(\cdot,f\right)_{\sharp}+\left(\cdot,g\right)_{\sharp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\langle f,g\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}+\sqrt{-1}\right]\left(\cdot,f\right)_{\sharp}+\left(\cdot,g-\frac{\langle f,g\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}f\right)_{\sharp},

(3.1)

whereby
f,g\in[{\mathbb{B}}\otimes\mathfrak{g}]^{\ast}
. Thus
|\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij,\alpha}(w)\right)_{\sharp}|^{2}
is equal to
|Z_{1}+\sqrt{-1}Z_{2}|^{2}
in distribution, each
Z_{i}
is a real normal random variable with mean 0.

From Equation (
3.1
), write

Then

, so by Jenson’s inequality,

|(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w))_{\sharp}|^{2}\leq 4\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(|c||W_{1}|+|W_{2}|\right)\right]^{2}\leq 2[|c|^{2}|W_{1}|^{2}+|W_{2}|^{2}].

From Remark
2.12
,
{{\mathbb{E}}}[|(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w))_{\sharp}|^{2}]=1/2\pi
. Because
W_{1}
is independent of
W_{2}
, note that
{{\mathbb{E}}}[|(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w))_{\sharp}|^{2}]=|c|^{2}{{\mathbb{E}}}[|W_{1}|^{2}]+{{\mathbb{E}}}[|W_{2}|^{2}]
. Hence, for some constants

, which depend on

, we have

2\pi|(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w))_{\sharp}|^{2}\leq 2[a^{2}\tilde{W}_{1}^{2}+b^{2}\tilde{W}_{2}^{2}],

whereby for each

,

•

\tilde{W}_{i}
is a (real) random variable on
\left({\mathbb{B}},\tilde{\mu}_{\kappa^{2}}\right)
,

•

\tilde{W}_{i}
is equal in distribution to a standard normal
Z_{i}
and

•

Z_{1}
is independent of
Z_{2}
.

Using Fubini’s Theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left|\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left|\left(\cdot,\kappa\xi_{ij}(w)\right)_{\sharp}\right|^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{w\in{{\mathbb{C}}}^{4}}d\lambda_{4}(w)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}[a^{2}\tilde{W}_{1}^{2}+b^{2}\tilde{W}_{2}^{2}]\right]\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}[Z_{1}^{2}+Z_{2}^{2}]\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{x\in{{\mathbb{R}}}}e^{x^{2}/2\pi}e^{-x^{2}/2}dx\cdot\int_{y\in{{\mathbb{R}}}}e^{y^{2}/2\pi}e^{-y^{2}/2}dy

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(1/\pi)}}\right]^{2}<\infty.

Proposition 4.1

We have that
\{Y_{3}^{1}>0\}
with probability 1, on the Wiener space
({\mathbb{B}}\otimes\mathfrak{g},\tilde{\mu}_{1}^{\times^{N}})

Proof.
Since
\mathfrak{g}
is semi-simple, without any loss of generality,
c^{1,\beta_{0}}_{\gamma}=-c_{\gamma}^{\beta_{0},1}\neq 0
for some
\beta_{0},\gamma\neq 1
. By Definition
2.13

\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}c_{\gamma}^{\alpha\beta}(\cdot,\pi_{i,\alpha}(w))_{\sharp}(\cdot,\pi_{j,\beta}(w))_{\sharp}\neq 0

\{(\cdot,\pi_{i,\alpha}(w))_{\sharp},(\cdot,\pi_{j,\beta}(w))_{\sharp}:\ 1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq N\}
is equal in distribution to

2N

\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{N}c_{\gamma}^{\alpha,\beta}\tilde{N}_{\alpha}N_{\beta}=0

5
Application

The main purpose of computing the Yang-Mills path integral is to derive the Wilson Area Law formula. A quark is never observed in isolation due to quark confinement. A quark and an antiquark are bounded together in a meson via the strong force. Unlike the Coulomb potential, which decays inversely proportional to the distance, the strong force actually gets stronger as the separation between the quarks increases. It is confirmed experimentally that the potential varies proportionally to the distance between quarks.

The path integral given by Expression
1.8
can be used to explain why this potential is linear. However, to compute it is highly nontrivial and difficult. Note that perturbative methods using Feynman diagrams cannot be used to compute it, lest derive the area formula.

Let
S
be a flat rectangular surface, whose boundary is a rectangular contour
C
with spatial length
R
and timelike length
T
, thus
S
has area
RT
. Then, we will write

W(R,T;\rho):=\lim_{\kappa\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\tilde{N}}{{\mathbb{E}}}_{{\rm YM}}^{\kappa}\left[{{\rm{Tr}}}\ \exp\left[(\cdot,\nu_{S}^{\kappa,\rho})_{\sharp}\right]\right].

From Theorem
4.8
, we have the average trace

W(R,T;\rho)=\frac{1}{\tilde{N}}{{\rm{Tr}}}\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n)!}\sum_{\tilde{k}_{n}\in K_{n}}\sum_{f_{n}\in S_{n}}\left[\frac{RT}{4}\right]^{n}G_{\rho}(\tilde{k}_{n},f_{n}).

A meson is made up of a quark and antiquark. Now, these 2 quarks go in opposite direction along the spatial length
R
and after time
T
, are attracted to each other along the spatial length
R
, hence tracing a rectangular contour
C
described in the previous paragraph. We want to calculate the potential energy between the quark and antiquark, as a function of distance
R
. Using the path integral computed on the surface
S
with boundary
C
, the potential is given by

V(R)=-\lim_{T\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{T}\log W(R,T;\rho).

When
n=1
, note that
\sum_{\tilde{k}_{1}\in K_{1}}\sum_{f_{1}\in S_{1}}G_{\rho}(\tilde{k}_{1},f_{1})=-\mathscr{E}(\rho)
, the negative of the Casimir operator. When
RT
is small, we have that
W(R,T;\rho)=1-\frac{RT}{8}\frac{1}{\tilde{N}}{{\rm{Tr}}}\ \mathscr{E}(\rho)+O(R^{2}T^{2})
and thus

V(R)=\frac{R}{8\tilde{N}}{{\rm{Tr}}}\ \mathscr{E}(\rho).

In the case of the standard representation of
{\rm SU}(N)
, we can see that

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N^{2}-1}E^{\alpha}E^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{1}{N}-N\right)\mathbb{I},

\mathbb{I}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix. See
[
12
]
. Therefore,

V(R)=\frac{R}{8}\left(N-\frac{1}{N}\right).

In the case of the standard representation of
{\rm SO}(N)
, we see that

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N(N-1)/2}E^{\alpha}E^{\alpha}=\frac{1-N}{2}\mathbb{I},

hence

V(R)=\frac{R}{16}\left(N-1\right).

Compare with Equation (19.6) in
[
13
]
. Thus, we see that quark confinement implies that the potential energy between the quarks is linear in nature.

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}z_{0}^{m},-\frac{1}{c}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\left(\frac{y}{2c}\right)^{n}\frac{z_{0}^{n}}{n!}\right\rangle=\left\langle g,\theta_{y/2c}\right\rangle.

Suppose

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{c}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{p_{r}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}c_{p_{r}}\frac{i}{(2c)^{r}}\frac{\mathbf{x}^{p_{r}}}{\sqrt{p_{r}!}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{c}\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{p_{r}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}|c_{p_{r}}|\frac{r}{(2c)^{r}}\frac{|\mathbf{x}^{p_{r}}|}{\sqrt{p_{r}!}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R^{M}}{c}\sum_{r=0}^{M}\sum_{p_{r}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}r|c_{p_{r}}|\frac{|\mathbf{x}^{p_{r}}|}{(2c)^{r}}\frac{1}{R^{r}}+\frac{1}{c}\sum_{r=M+1}^{\infty}\sum_{p_{r}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}}r|c_{p_{r}}|\frac{|\mathbf{x}^{p_{r}}|}{(2c)^{r}}\frac{1}{R^{r}}\frac{R^{r}}{\sqrt{p_{r}!}}
