Emergent gravity at all scales
SAURYA DAS, SOURAV SUR

Abstract
It has recently been shown that any observed potential can in principle be generated via quantum mechanics using a suitable wavefunction. In this work, we consider the concrete example of the gravitational potential experienced by a test particle at length scales spanning from the planetary to the cosmological, and determine the wavefunction that would produce it as its quantum potential. In other words, the observed gravitational interaction at all length scales can be generated by an underlying wavefunction. We discuss implications of our result.

Can all perceived potentials in nature have a simple quantum mechanical origin?
We asked this question recently, and showed that a quantum particle sees the sum
of the background classical potential and a wavefunction dependent quantum
potential, and there is no way of separating the two in any experiment

[
1
,
2
]
.
In other words, potentials around us can be attributed to quantum mechanics,
either partially or wholly, and in the case of the latter, the background
spacetime is flat and with no classical interactions, but ‘filled’ with suitable wavefunctions.

We would test this idea in this paper in the context of gravity, considering in
particular,
the effective gravitational potential
V(r)
, on a test particle of mass
m
,
to be given by

\displaystyle V(r)\,=\,\left\{\begin{aligned} &V_{1}=-\dfrac{k_{1}}{r}\,;~&r\leq r_{1}\,,\\
&V_{2}=k_{2}\,\ln\left(\dfrac{r}{r_{0}}\right);~&r_{1}<r\leq r_{2}\,,\\
&V_{3}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\,k_{3}\,r^{2}\,;~&r_{2}<r<\infty\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

where
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are respectively the galactic and the cosmological length scales,
k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}
and
r_{0}
are dimensionful constants with
r_{0}
being arbitrary.
Note that this effective potential can explain observations at all scales,
without
making any specific allusion to dark matter or dark energy. A similar potential
stretching across length scales has also been proposed in the literature

[
3
,
4
]
.

Now, in an earlier set of works, it was shown that
practically any potential experienced by a particle
can in principle
have its origin in quantum mechanics in terms of its wavefunction
\Psi
.
The latter gives rise to its own ‘quantum potential’, the particle sees the sum of the background classical and the new wavefunction dependent quantum potential, and
there is no way of distinguishing between the two

[
1
,
2
]
.
In other words, in principle the potential (
1
) may entirely be the quantum potential of a suitable wavefunction of the particle. Before we compute that wavefunction, we first present a brief review of the derivation of the quantum potential.

Consider the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for a point particle of mass
m

-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}\Psi+V\Psi=i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}.

(2)

We write the wave function as

\Psi(\vec{r},t)=\,{\cal R}(\vec{r},t)\,e^{iS(\vec{r},t)}~,

(3)

where
{\cal R}(\vec{r},t)
and
S(\vec{r},t)
are real functions. Substituting this in Eq.(
2
) and extracting the real and imaginary parts, we obtain

-\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}=\,\frac{(\nabla S)^{2}}{2m}+V(r)-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\nabla^{2}{\cal R}}{{\cal R}}~,

(4)

and the continuity equation

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla.(\rho\frac{\nabla S}{m})=0~,

(5)

where
\rho={\cal R}^{2}
.
In the absence of the last term in Eq.(
4
), namely

V_{Q}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\nabla^{2}{\cal R}}{{\cal R}}

(6)

we are left with the classical Hamilton-Jacobi equation,
giving rise to
the standard Newtonian trajectories for the constituent particles.
However, if we define the ‘velocity field’ as

v(\vec{r},t)\equiv\,\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\vec{\nabla}S~,

(7)

Eq.(
4
) can simply be written as Newton’s second law of motion with the standard classical potential (or force) augmented by the quantum potential (and a corresponding wavefunction dependent force) as follows

[
5
,
6
,
7
]

\displaystyle m\,\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}=\,-\,\vec{\nabla}\left(V+\,V_{Q}\right)~.

(8)

Evolution via
(V+V_{Q})
yields the so-called
‘Bohmian trajectories’ or
‘quantal trajectories’, which can be thought of as classical trajectories plus the
{\cal O}(\hbar^{2})
quantum corrections.
As expected, these become important at short distances
(when quantum effects are expected to dominate) and indeed reproduce
the results of all quantum experiments, such as the interference patterns
in a double slit experiment. It is implicit of course that no intermediate measurements are done on the quantal trajectories until the particle reaches its desired destination and standard quantum mechanical measurements are made at that point.
The Bohmian interpretation of quantum mechanics,
albeit not the most popular one, is perfectly equivalent to the standard ones,
offers a novel and useful picture in many situations,
such as the current one and
elsewhere
[
8
,
9
]
.
In other words, all predictions from the (Bohmian) approach are producible from the
standard formalism of quantum mechanics, albeit in a slightly
different way. The generalization of the above method to relativistic scenarios raises some interesting interpretational issues regarding the quantal trajectories, but the procedure as such is straightforward and once again yields identical predictions as from the conventional approach.

With the above, we now return to
the question as to
whether it is plausible to have a wave-function
\Psi
that
can give rise to the above potential (
1
) as a
quantum potential

over the entire range of
r
. Or, in other words, can one have the following?

\displaystyle\Psi\,=\,\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Psi_{1}\rightarrow V_{Q}=V_{1}\,;~&r\leq r_{1}\,,\\
&\Psi_{2}\rightarrow V_{Q}=V_{2}\,;~&r_{1}<r\leq r_{2}\,,\\
&\Psi_{3}\rightarrow V_{Q}=V_{3}\,;~&r_{2}<r<\infty\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(9)

If so, then one can indeed say that the wavefunction ‘induces’ the observed
potential and that gravity ‘emerges’ from an underlying quantum theory in flat
spacetime.

Re-writing Eq.(
6
) as

\displaystyle{\nabla^{2}\mathcal{R}}+\frac{2m\,V_{Q}}{\hbar^{2}}\,{\cal R}=0~,

(10)

a number of important points may be noted:

(i)

V_{Q}
is a local quantity.

(ii)

V_{Q}
depends on the amplitude
\mathcal{R}
, but not on the phase
\mathcal{S}
, of
the wavefunction
\Psi
.

(iii)

V_{Q}
does not depend on the overall normalization of the wavefunction

\Psi
. Thus, for example, if
\Psi
is suddenly magnified by an arbitrary
factor in a region of space, there will be no effect on the quantum potential.

(iv)

Despite the similarity, Eq.(
10
) should not be confused with the

free
time-independent Schrödinger equation for stationary states,
namely,

\nabla^{2}\,\Psi_{s}+\frac{2mE}{\hbar^{2}}\,\Psi_{s}=0\,,

(11)

which does not involve any background classical potential. Here,
E
is a
constant,
\Psi_{s}
is a stationary state wavefunction, and the full
wavefunction
\Psi
can be considered as a superposition of a requisite
number of
\Psi_{s}
.

It follows from the point (ii) that the phase
\mathcal{S}
remains undetermined.

Next, we solve Eq.(
10
) by substituting for
V_{Q}
in the three regions,
given by Eq.(
1
).
Since the potential
is spherically symmetric, we assume, for simplicity, the wavefunction to be
spherically symmetric as well, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(r)
. We also assume the phase
of the wavefunction
\mathcal{S}=0
. Eq.(
10
) then simplifies to

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^{2}\,\frac{d\mathcal{R}}{dr}\right)+\frac{2m\,V_{Q}}{\hbar^{2}}\,{\cal R}=\,0\,.

(12)

Solutions which are not spherically symmetric and for which the phase
\mathcal{S}
is
non-vanishing will be investigated in a future work.

For the potential given by Eq.(
1
),
the above Eq.(
12
) translates to the following set of equations

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0pt\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Big[r^{2}\,\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(r)\Big]^{\prime}\!-\frac{1}{\ell_{1}\,r}\,\mathcal{R}(r)=\,0\,;~~\quad\quad\quad r\leq r_{1}\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0pt\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Big[r^{2}\,\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(r)\Big]^{\prime}\!+\frac{1}{\ell_{2}^{2}}\ln\!\left(\!\frac{r}{r_{0}}\!\right)\mathcal{R}(r)=0\,;\quad r_{1}<r\leq r_{2}\,,

(14)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0pt\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Big[r^{2}\,\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(r)\Big]^{\prime}\!-\frac{r^{2}}{\ell_{3}^{4}}\,\mathcal{R}(r)=\,0\,;\qquad\quad\quad r_{2}<r<\infty\,,

(15)

where the prime
\{^{\prime}\}\equiv d/dr\,
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{1}}=\,\frac{2m\,k_{1}}{\hbar^{2}}\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{2}^{2}}=\,\frac{2m\,k_{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{3}^{4}}=\,\frac{mk_{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\,.

(18)

Note that
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}
all have dimensions of length.

The general solutions in the three regions can be written symbolically as

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(r)=\,c_{1}\,f_{1}(r)\,;\qquad\qquad\qquad r\leq r_{1}\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(r)=\,c_{2}\,f_{2}(r)+\,d_{2}\,g_{2}(r)\,;~\quad r_{1}\leq r\leq r_{2}\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\Psi_{3}(r)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{3}(r)=\,d_{3}\,g_{3}(r)\,;\qquad\qquad\qquad r_{2}<r<\infty\,,

(21)

where
c_{1},c_{2},d_{2}
and
d_{3}
are constant coefficients of
the respective linearly independent solutions of relevance, denoted by

c_{1},c_{2},d_{2}
and
d_{3}
.
Note that in either of the Eqs.(
19
) and (
21
), only one of
the two linearly independent solutions is of relevance, namely
f_{1}(r)
and

g_{3}(r)
, which remain finite respectively at
r=0
and as
r\rightarrow\infty
.
Eq.(
20
), on the other hand, involves both the linearly independent
solutions,
f_{2}(r)
and
g_{2}(r)
.
While the exact forms of
f_{1}(r)
and
g_{3}(r)
can be derived from the
respective Eqs.(
19
) and (
21
) in a straightforward way,
solving Eq.(
13
) to obtain
f_{2}(r)
and
g_{2}(r)
is not quite simple,
because of the presence of the logarithmic term.
We therefore solve Eq.(
13
) by approximating the logarithm to a
polynomial, i.e.,

\ln\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)=\,\ln\left(1+\,\frac{r-r_{0}}{r_{0}}\right)=\,\frac{r-r_{0}}{r_{0}}+\,\dots\,,

(22)

and retaining only the leading order term, after choosing the arbitrary
constant
r_{0}
such that
r\ll r_{0}
. One can of course obtain the
solution to any desired accuracy by retaining more and more terms, or
by solving numerically.
We have, finally

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0ptf_{1}=\,\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{1}}{r}}\,I_{1}\left(2\,\sqrt{\frac{r}{\ell_{1}}}\right)\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0ptf_{2}=\,\frac{1}{r}\,Ai\left((-1)^{1/3}\,\left[\frac{r_{0}}{\ell_{2}}\right]^{2/3}\left[\frac{r}{r_{0}}-1\right]\right)+\,\text{c.c.}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0ptg_{2}=\,\frac{1}{r}\,Bi\left((-1)^{1/3}\,\left[\frac{r_{0}}{\ell_{2}}\right]^{2/3}\left[\frac{r}{r_{0}}-1\right]\right)+\,\text{c.c.}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\hskip-25.0ptg_{3}=\,\frac{e^{-r^{2}/\ell_{3}^{2}}}{r}\,H_{-1/2}\left(\frac{r}{\ell_{3}}\right)\,,

(26)

where
I_{n},Ai,Bi,H_{n}
are the Bessel function, Airy
Ai
and
Bi

functions and Hermite polynomials, respectively, and c.c. denotes the
complex conjugation.

Next, we impose the following matching conditions for the wavefunction

\Psi(r)
and its derivative
\Psi^{\prime}(r)
at two points
r=r_{1}
and
r=r_{2}
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{I}(r_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{II}(r_{1})\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\Psi_{I}^{\prime}(r_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{II}^{\prime}(r_{1})\,,

(28)

\displaystyle\Psi_{II}(r_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{III}(r_{2})\,,

(29)

\displaystyle\Psi_{II}^{\prime}(r_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{III}^{\prime}(r_{2})\,.

(30)

These translate, respectively, to the equations

\displaystyle c_{1}\,f_{1}(r_{1})=\,c_{2}\,f_{2}(r_{1})+\,d_{2}\,g_{2}(r_{1})\,,

(31)

\displaystyle c_{1}\,f_{1}^{\prime}(r_{1})=\,c_{2}\,f_{2}^{\prime}(r_{1})+\,d_{2}\,g_{2}^{\prime}(r_{1})\,,

(32)

\displaystyle c_{2}\,f_{2}(r_{2})+\,d_{2}\,g_{2}(r_{2})=\,\,d_{3}\,g_{3}(r_{2})\,,

(33)

\displaystyle c_{2}\,f_{2}^{\prime}(r_{2})+\,d_{2}\,g_{2}^{\prime}(r_{2})=\,d_{3}\,g_{3}^{\prime}(r_{2})\,.

(34)

Since there are four equations involving four constants, one can in
principle determine these constants. As mentioned before, the overall
normalization is not important for our purposes. However, if required,
the wavefunction
\Psi
can be easily normalized upon dividing it by

4\pi\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\,dr\,r^{2}|\Psi|^{2}
.

To summarize, we have shown in this paper that the gravitational potential
which accounts for most observations,
from the planetary to the cosmological scales, can be correctly reproduced
by a suitable wavefunction via its quantum potential. The wavefunction is
continuous and finite everywhere, and is normalizable. This supports the
proposal that what appears as classical potential may in fact be the
quantum potential of a suitable wavefunction in
flat and free space
.
The symmetries of the background spacetime is that of the vacuum, namely
the usual Poincaré symmetry and the potential and force is experienced
by a particle purely due to its wavefunction. It is hard to think of a
simpler physical picture, in which classical potentials and forces are
emergent and completely producible from an underlying quantum picture.

present approach with that in related works on gravitational and other

One would need to study other wavefunctions compatible with the given
potentials, for e.g., the ones with a non-trivial angular dependence, or
phase. Moreover, one would need to generalize this formalism to make it
manifestly (special) relativistic, as well as study the decoherence of the
said wavefunction.
However, given that there is no ‘environment’ as such, it is unclear what
would cause such a decoherence. In any case, a set of concrete predictions, testable in the astrophysical or cosmological scenario, would help strengthen
our formalism here. We hope to report on these elsewhere.
