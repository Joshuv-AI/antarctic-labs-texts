General Distance Balancing for Quantum Locally Testable Codes
Adam Wills, Ting-Chun Lin, Min-Hsiu Hsieh

Abstract
In this paper, we prove a lower bound on the soundness of quantum locally testable codes under the distance balancing construction of Evra et al. [ 1 ] . Our technical contribution is that the new soundness of the quantum code is at least the old soundness divided by the classical code length (up to a constant factor). This allows us to use any classical code with independent checks when distance balancing, where previously only the repetition code had been considered for qLTCs. By using a good classical LDPC code, we are able to grow the dimension of the hypersphere product codes [ 2 ] and the hemicubic codes [ 3 ] while maintaining their distance and locality, but at the expense of soundness. From this, and also by distance balancing a chain complex of Cross et al. [ 4 ] , we obtain quantum locally testable codes of new parameters.

In the present work, we hope to leverage a construction that was used in the search for better quantum LDPC codes for the benefit of constructing better quantum locally testable codes, namely the distance balancing construction of

[

Let us note that the primary goal behind this paper was to reduce the locality of quantum codes, and the distance balancing construction was a bi-product of one of these methods. Let us also note the paper

. The idea is, to balance the distances of a code with a poor X - distance
d_{X}
and a good Z - distance
d_{Z}

l\approx\frac{d_{Z}}{d_{X}}
, to obtain a 4-term chain complex, from which the last three terms may be extracted to define a new quantum code. It may then be shown that the distances change as
d_{X}\mapsto l\cdot d_{X}
and
d_{Z}\mapsto d_{Z}
, the number of physical qubits scales as
n\mapsto nl+n_{X}(l-1)
, where
n_{X}
is the number of X - checks, the dimension of the quantum code is unchanged and the locality is unchanged up to a constant. In order to balance distances when the X - distance is good, but the Z - distance is poor, one may simply first take the co-complex of the quantum code, perform the above procedure, and then take the co-complex again. A disadvantage of Hastings’ method is that, because the repetition code itself has dimension 1, the resultant quantum code has the same dimension as the old quantum code but, because the new quantum code has more physical qubits, the dimension scaling worsens due to this procedure. This disadvantage is addressed by the updated distance balancing method of Evra et al.
[
1
]
(see their Theorem 4.2). The procedure is the same, except the repetition code is replaced by any
[t,k,d]

d_{X}\mapsto d\cdot d_{X}
,
d_{Z}\mapsto d_{Z}
,
n\mapsto nt+n_{X}s
and the dimension of the quantum code scales as
K\mapsto k\cdot K
. It therefore makes sense to let the classical code be a good classical LDPC code, at which point the locality of the quantum code will also be unchanged up to a constant.

The authors do also ask if there may be other, more general product constructions under which soundness is preserved. Given our main result, a very natural question to ask is whether there exist other classical codes for which the distance balancing construction allows for the preservation of soundness, rather than causing it to fall by a factor of

2.1
Quantum CSS Codes

We present a brief review of the relevant details of constructing quantum CSS codes from chain complexes, but we recommend the review,

A quantum CSS code on qubits may be thought of as a three-term chain complex

C=\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{Z}}\overset{H_{Z}^{T}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\overset{H_{X}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{X}}\right),

H_{X}\cdot H_{Z}^{T}=0

\dim(\ker(H_{X})/\text{im}(H_{Z}^{T}))

2.2
Classical Locally Testable Codes and Quantum Locally Testable Codes

A (linear) classical code with
t
bits and
s
checks and parity-check matrix
H
may be thought of as a two-term chain complex over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
:

R=\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}\overset{H}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{s}\right).

(8)

To say that
H
has independent checks is to say that the rows of the matrix
H
are linearly independent - note that this is equivalent to saying that the matrix is surjective. This will be an important property for us later on. The classical code is then said to be a locally testable code (LTC) with soundness
\rho
if for all words
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{t}
,
\frac{|Hx|}{s}\geq\rho\frac{d\left(x,\ker(H)\right)}{t}
. Roughly speaking, this says that words far from the code must have a high-weight syndrome.

The most informative definition of soundness for quantum locally testable codes is more involved, but for CSS codes there is a condition that simplifies it considerably. First, we give the full definition for a stabiliser code as in
[
10
]

m

g_{1},...,g_{m}

\Pi_{i}=(I-g_{i})/2

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Pi_{i}
. Given this codespace
C
, the t-fattening of

such that
\left|u_{i}+z_{i}\right|=d\left(u_{i},\ker(H_{X})\right)
. Adding the 1-cycle
\left(\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}z_{i}\otimes v_{i},0\right)
to
x
shows that, without loss of generality, we have
|u_{i}|=d\left(u_{i},\ker(H_{X})\right)
for all
i\in\{s+1,...,t\}
.

Now let us write

x=\left(\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}u_{i}\otimes v_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}\otimes e_{j}\right)

(51)

for some
y_{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{X}}
. From this we get

\partial_{1}(x)=\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}(H_{X}u_{i})\otimes v_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}y_{j}\otimes(H^{T}e_{j}).

(52)

Now for
i\in\{1,...,s\}
, we define

y_{i}^{\prime}=\sum_{j\in supp\left(H^{T}\right)_{i,:}}y_{j}

(53)

and for
i\in\{s+1,...,t\}
, we define

y_{i}^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{j\in supp\left(H^{T}\right)_{i,:}}y_{j}

(54)

where
\left(H^{T}\right)_{i,:}
denotes the i-th row of
H^{T}
. Then

\partial_{1}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}y_{i}^{\prime}\otimes v_{i}+\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\otimes v_{i}+\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}(H_{X}u_{i})\otimes v_{i}

(55)

and so

\left|\partial_{1}(x)\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|+\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left(\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|+\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|-2\left|H_{X}u_{i}\cap y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|\right)

(56)

using that for binary vectors
a
and
b
of the same length,
|a+b|=|a|+|b|-2|a\cap b|
.

First, let us consider just the term
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|
. Using the triangle inequality, we can add together any desired combination of the
y_{i}^{\prime}
. For example,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|y_{1}^{\prime}\right|+\left|y_{2}^{\prime}\right|+...+\left|y_{s}^{\prime}\right|

(57)

\displaystyle\geq\left|y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{2}^{\prime}\right|+\left|y_{3}^{\prime}\right|+...+\left|y_{s}^{\prime}\right|

(58)

\displaystyle\geq\left|y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{2}^{\prime}\right|.

(59)

We see that we have, for any subset of the indices
\{i_{1},...,i_{k}\}\subseteq[s]
,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|y_{i_{1}}^{\prime}+...+y_{i_{k}}^{\prime}\right|.

(60)

Moreover, the matrix
H^{\prime}
is full rank, and since the
y_{i}^{\prime}
represent combinations of the
y_{i}
’s according to the rows of
H^{\prime}
, we have that for any subset of the indices
\{j_{1},...,j_{l}\}\subseteq[s]
,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|y_{j_{1}}+...+y_{j_{l}}\right|.

(61)

In particular,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|\geq\left|y_{j}\right|\;\;\forall j\in[s].

(62)

Then, by Fact
3.1
,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|\geq\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left|y_{j}\right|}{s}.

(63)

In the exact same way, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}^{\prime}\right|\geq\frac{\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|}{t-s}

(64)

from which we get

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|+\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left(\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|+\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|-2\left|H_{X}u_{i}\cap y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|\right)

(65)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|+\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left(\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|+\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|-2\left|H_{X}u_{i}\cap y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|\right)

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left(\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|+2\left|y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|-2\left|H_{X}u_{i}\cap y_{i}^{\prime\prime}\right|\right)

(67)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|

(68)

where we have used the fact that for any binary vectors
a
and
b
of the same length,
|a\cap b|\leq|a|
. Then, combining Equations (
63
) and (
68
) and using Fact
3.1
gives us

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s}{t}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left|y_{i}\right|}{s}+\left(1-\frac{s}{t}\right)\frac{1}{t-s}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left|y_{j}\right|+\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|H_{X}u_{i}\right|\right).

(70)

Then, using the soundness of
H_{X}
,

\left|\partial_{1}(x)\right|\geq\frac{1}{t}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left|y_{j}\right|+\frac{n_{X}\rho_{X}}{n}\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|u_{i}\right|\right)

(71)

where we recall that
\left|u_{i}\right|=d\left(u_{i},\ker(H_{X})\right)
for
i\in\{s+1,...,t\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{1}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{t}\min\left(\frac{n_{X}\rho_{X}}{n},1\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{s}\left|y_{j}\right|+\sum_{i=s+1}^{t}\left|u_{i}\right|\right)

(72)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\min\left(\frac{n_{X}\rho_{X}}{n},1\right)\left|x\right|

(73)

Finally,

\frac{\left|\partial_{1}(x)\right|}{n_{X}t}\geq\frac{1}{t}\min\left(\frac{n_{X}\rho_{X}}{n},1\right)\left(\frac{nt+n_{X}s}{n_{X}t}\right)\frac{d\left(x,\ker(\partial_{1})\right)}{nt+n_{X}s}

(74)

as required.
∎

We are now in a position that we can prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The result follows from the preliminary Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
and by applying our Lemmas
19
and
3.2
with a good classical LDPC code, with independent checks.
∎
