\Shorttitle
On Nonparanormal Likelihoods

\Abstract
Nonparanormal models describe the joint distribution of multivariate responses via latent Gaussian, and thus parametric, copulae while allowing flexible nonparametric marginals. Some aspects of such distributions, for example conditional independence, are formulated parametrically. Other features, such as marginal distributions, can be formulated non- or semiparametrically. Such models are attractive when multivariate normality is questionable but interpretability paramount.
Most estimation procedures perform two steps, first estimating the nonparametric part. The copula parameters come second, treating the marginal estimates as known. This is sufficient for some applications. For other applications, e.g. when a semiparametric margin features parameters of interest or when standard errors are important, a simultaneous estimation of all parameters might be more advantageous.
We present suitable parameterisations of nonparanormal models, possibly including semiparametric effects, and define four novel nonparanormal log-likelihood functions. In general, the corresponding one-step optimisation problems are shown to be non-convex. In some cases, however, biconvex problems emerge. Several convex approximations are discussed. From a low-level computational point of view, the core contribution is the score function for multivariate normal log-probabilities computed via Genz’ procedure.

As a demonstration for the versatility of the theoretical and computational framework, we present a series of nonparanormal models for transformation discriminant analysis when some biomarkers are subject to limit-of-detection problems. Possible empirical gains of full maximum likelihood estimation compared to two-step approaches are illustrated in a simulation study targeting semiparametric efficient polychoric correlation analysis where a theoretical benchmark is
available.

The multivariate normal distribution comes with a high potential for addiction
due to its covariance and precision matrix containing information about
marginal and conditional independence, respectively. The fact that many
foundations of classical and contemporary multivariate statistics, such as
linear or quadratic discriminant analysis, graphical models, or structural
equation models, have been defined in terms of this distribution can be
explained by these favourable properties.
However, normality is the exception rather than the rule in the real world.
As an alternative to a full
normality detox, statisticians may sacrifice marginal normality while retaining joint
normality on some latent scale. This idea has been popularised under
different terms, for example as “nonparanormal models”

(
Liu et al., 2009
)
or “coordinatewise Gaussianisation”
(
Mai et al., 2023
)
, but its roots go
deeper. For multivariate ordinal variables,
Jöreskog (1994)

suggested the estimation of “polychoric correlations” defined by a latent
bivariate normal distribution coupled with marginal ordinal probit models.
Corresponding concepts in multivariate analysis are discussed in

Joe (1997)
and similar principles have been applied in semiparametric copula estimation

(
Klaassen and Wellner, 1997
;
Joe, 2005
)
, where marginal parameters are
estimated first, followed by a second step of estimating the copula
parameters
conditionally
on margins.
The rank likelihood
(
Hoff, 2007
;
Hermes et al., 2024
)
does not
condition on marginal ranks but treats the marginal distributions as
nuisance parameters and focuses on the sole estimation of Gaussian copula parameters.

While such ideas have been very successfully applied for the estimation of
dependency structures also in high-dimensional multivariate
data, more complex models for both marginal and joint distributions are
necessary in many applications. Very much in the spirit of

Chen et al. (2006)
, simultaneous likelihood estimation of and inference
for marginal
and
copula parameters in more complex models is our main interest here.
The motivation comes from a wide range of applications of the
nonparanormal model where the application of a “normalise and forget” scheme is not
adequate. For general discrete
(
Popovic et al., 2018
)
or mixes of
continuous and discrete variables
(
Pritikin et al., 2018
;
Christoffersen et al., 2021
;
Göbler et al., 2024
, potentially allowing missing observations
in some responses,)
,
rank-based approaches are more difficult to justify. Access to the full likelihood
covering all model parameters is required in semiparametric discriminant analysis

(
Mai and Zou, 2015
)
or for analysing multivariate interval-censored survival data

(
Ding and Sun, 2022
)
. Most interesting are nonparanormal models where
the marginal distributions, for example in multivariate regression models

(
Lesaffre and Kaufmann, 1992
;
Song et al., 2009
;
Nikoloulopoulos, 2023
,
e.g.
in multivariate GLMs or other linear models,)
, or the copula
parameters
(
Lu et al., 2018
,
e.g.
in time-varying graphical models,)

feature parameters capturing covariate effects. The most striking example necessitating the
joint estimation of marginal and copula parameters is a model class for
survival analysis under dependent censoring

(
Deresa and Keilegom, 2023
)
. Here, marginally estimated
distributions for time-to-event and time-to-censoring are biased and only
the joint model leads to properly identified and estimable parameters. In
Section
6
, a selection of special applications and models with potential
benefits from the contributions to likelihood-based inference made in this work is discussed.

We proceed by suggesting parameterisations of the nonparanormal
model for discrete, continuous, and mixed discrete-continuous multivariate
responses and derive the several variants of nonparanormal
log-likelihoods, including “empirical” or “nonparametric” and
“parametric” flavours, and
the corresponding score functions. In general, maximum likelihood estimation in this model
class is shown to be non-convex. We discuss convex
approximations, which might be useful at least for the computation of
starting values. The theory and computational framework presented here
allows implementation of a rather general likelihood estimation toolbox for many interesting
applications. For some areas in multivariate analysis discussed in
Section
6
and beyond, this toolbox helps to setup
likelihood and score functions. Coupled with suitable optimisation
routines, joint maximum likelihood estimation of all model parameters
becomes computationally feasible in a range of interesting problems.
As an illustration highlighting the practical potential of this framework,
we introduce novel forms of
discriminant analyses for evaluating the diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma
based on only partially observed non-normal biomarker data.

Regarding the statistical properties of maximum nonparanormal likelihood
estimation, it is
demonstrated empirically that copula parameters obtained from optimising the nonparanormal
log-likelihood
attain the semiparametric efficiency bound
derived by
Klaassen and Wellner (1997)
. A more in-depth analysis of
theoretical properties of these estimators is beyond the scope of this
paper.

1
The Nonparanormal Model

We jointly observe
J
response variables
\text{\boldmath$Y$}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{J})^{\top}

from at least ordered sample spaces
Y_{j}\in\mathcal{Y}_{j},j=1,\dots,J
.
The nonparanormal (NPN) model
\text{\boldmath$Y$}\sim\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$},\mathbf{\Sigma})
features

J
monotonically non-decreasing transformation functions

\text{\boldmath$h$}=(h_{1},\dots,h_{J})^{\top}
, one for each dimension

h_{j}:\mathcal{Y}_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and, in addition, a positive
semidefinite
J\times J
covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
such that the joint
cumulative distribution function can be written in terms of normal
probabilities
\mathbb{P}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}\leq\text{\boldmath$y$})=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}))
,
where
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
is the joint cumulative distribution function of

\ND_{J}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})
. In case all elements of
Y
are
continuous, each
h_{j}
is bijective and one typically

(
Liu et al., 2009
)
writes
\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$}^{-1},\mathbf{\Sigma})
for
the absolutely continuous distribution of
\text{\boldmath$Y$}=\text{\boldmath$h$}^{-1}(\text{\boldmath$Z$})
generated
by a latent multivariate normal variable
\text{\boldmath$Z$}=\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$Y$})\sim\ND_{J}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})
.
The copula parameters
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are identified as a consequence of the uniqueness of
the copula in Sklar’s theorem.
We allow more general sample spaces for binary, ordered, count, or otherwise discrete variables
and mixed continuous-discrete variables and thus neither require
\mathcal{Y}_{j}=\mathbb{R}
for

j=1,\dots,J
nor the existence of
\text{\boldmath$h$}^{-1}
.
In this discrete situation, identifiability issues arise with two-step
copula estimators, however, maximum likelihood estimation has been demonstrated
to work in special cases
(
Genest and Nešlehová, 2007
, Section 6.2.,)
.

The model is invariant with respect to rescaling, that is

\displaystyle\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$},\mathbf{\Sigma})=\text{NPN}\left(\diag(\text{\boldmath$d$})^{-1}\text{\boldmath$h$},\diag(\text{\boldmath$d$})\mathbf{\Sigma}\diag(\text{\boldmath$d$})\right)

for all
\text{\boldmath$d$}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{J})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
with
d_{j}>0
for all
j=1,\dots,J

and
\text{diag}(\text{\boldmath$d$})
the
J\times J
diagonal matrix.
Thus, identifiability constraints on
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are needed. One option is to require

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{jj}\equiv 1
for
j=1,\dots,J
leading to the interpretation
of
h_{j}
as probit-transformed marginal distribution function

\mathbb{P}(Y_{j}\leq y_{j})=\Phi(h_{j}(y_{j}))
for all
y_{j}\in\mathcal{Y}_{j},j=1,\dots,J
.

Alternatively, we write
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top}
in terms of the
inverse lower triangular Cholesky factor
\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}
of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}

and require
\mathbf{\Omega}_{jj}\equiv 1
for
j=1,\dots,J
.
This implies
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{11}\equiv 1
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{jj}\geq 1
for
j=2,\dots,J

and we define
\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Omega}}:=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top}}
.
In the absolutely continuous case with
\text{\boldmath$Y$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
, the model

\text{\boldmath$Y$}\sim\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$},\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top})
is identical to a structural equation model defined by a series
of additive transformation models beginning with the marginal model

\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\leq y_{1})=\Phi(h_{1}(y_{1}))
and proceeding
with conditional models

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{j}\leq y_{j}\mid Y_{1}=y_{1},\dots,Y_{j-1}=y_{j-1})=\Phi\left(\sum_{\jmath=1}^{j}\mathbf{\Omega}_{j\jmath}h_{\jmath}(y_{\jmath})\right),\quad j=2,\dots,J

for any
(y_{1},\dots,y_{J})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
. In other words,
the rows of
\mathbf{\Omega}
contain regression coefficients
for the conditional distribution of
Y_{j}
given its predecessors and
the transformation
h_{j}(Y_{j})
is
conditionally
standard normal.
For exclusively binary outcomes
\mathcal{Y}_{j}=\{0,1\},j=1,\dots,J
we have

\mathbb{P}(Y_{j}=0\,\forall j=1,\dots,J)=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
,
where
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{J})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
with
\theta_{j}=\sqrt{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{jj}}\Phi^{-1}(\mathbb{P}(Y_{j}=0))
.

In the presence of covariates
\text{\boldmath$X$}=\text{\boldmath$x$}\in\mathcal{X}
, one can characterise
the model via the conditional joint cumulative distribution function

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}\leq\text{\boldmath$y$}\mid\text{\boldmath$X$}=\text{\boldmath$x$})=\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}(\text{\boldmath$x$})}(\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$}))

(1)

where the covariates impact the transformation functions
\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})
, the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\text{\boldmath$x$})
, or both. For example, simple probit
linear transformation models

\displaystyle h_{j}(y_{j}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=h_{j}(y_{j})-\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{j},\quad j\in\{1,\dots,J\}

(2)

feature linear covariate effects. More elaborate choices include
transformation functions
h_{j}(y_{j}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})
of the form
\Phi^{-1}(F_{j}(h_{j}(y_{j})-\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{j}))
,
where
F_{j}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,1]
denotes an absolutely continuous
distribution function with log-concave density. For example, a marginal Weibull model can be
formulated via the inverse complementary log-log (
F_{j}=\text{cloglog}^{-1}
)
and a log-linear function
h_{j}(y_{j})

(
Hothorn et al., 2018
, see Table 1 in)
.
Also the joint distribution might change with
x
, for example via linear models
for the off-diagonal elements of the inverse Cholesky factor

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}_{j\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$x$})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&1\leq j=\jmath\leq J\\
\alpha_{j\jmath}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\gamma$}_{j\jmath}&1\leq\jmath<j\leq J.\end{array}\right.

In the context of multivariate transformation models, such a
parameterisation has been proposed by
Klein et al. (2022)
.
For multivariate normal distributions, the same idea was applied by
Barratt and Boyd (2023)
.
For the sake of notational simplicity, we will consider the unconditional
case in Sections
2
to
5
and comment on such conditional extensions
in Section
6
.

2
Parameterisation

The term “nonparanormal” insinuates a combination of nonparametrically
parameterised marginal distributions with a parametric Gaussian copula.
As a gold standard, we therefore first derive the “nonparanormal”
log-likelihood via nonparametric margins from
N
independent samples
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{N}\sim\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$},\mathbf{\Sigma})

with realisations
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i}=(Y_{i1},\dots,Y_{iJ})^{\top}
whose elements
were drawn from the
corresponding sample space
Y_{ij}\in\mathcal{Y}_{j},i=1,\dots,N;j=1,\dots,J
.

In the model
\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$},\mathbf{\Sigma})
, we first parameterise the
j=1,\dots,J
transformation functions

h_{j}
. In each dimension
j=1,\dots,J
, we consider the “empirical” sample
space given by the ordered unique realisations
\text{\boldmath$\upsilon$}_{j}=\{\upsilon_{j1},\dots,\upsilon_{jK(j)}\}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{j}
with
\upsilon_{j,k-1}<\upsilon_{j,k}
for
k=2,\dots,K(j)
. For the
i
th observation in the
j
th variable, write

r(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,K(j)\}
such that
Y_{ij}=\upsilon_{j,r(i,j)}
.
In the absence of ties,
r(i,j)
is the rank of the
i
th observation in
the sample
Y_{1j},\dots,Y_{Nj}
. We can now parameterise the
transformation function
h_{j}
as a step function
h_{j}(\upsilon_{jk})=\theta_{jk}\in\mathbb{R}
for
k=0,\dots,K(j)
with
values
\theta_{j0}\equiv-\infty
and
\theta_{jK(j)}\equiv\infty
at
the boundaries. The
j
th marginal parameter vector
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}=(\theta_{j1},\dots,\theta_{j,K(j)-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K(j)-1}
comes
with a monotonicity constraint
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j}\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}\geq\mathbf{0}_{K(j)-2}

defined by the
(K(j)-2)\times(K(j)-1)
first order difference matrix
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j}
.
Finally, we collect all marginal parameters in the parameter vector
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{j=1}^{J}(K(j)-1)}
fully specifying
h
. Because the number of parameters
K(j)
grows
with the sample size whenever the sample space is infinite, the
situation here is typically referred to as being “nonparametric”,
although the vector
\theta
contains parameters, in fact, potentially many of
them. The connection to the “parametric” situation will be made in
the next section.

Second, we parameterise the inverse Cholesky factor
\mathbf{\Omega}
of the
covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top}
by defining
a lower triangular unit matrix
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

in terms of its unconstrained lower triangular elements

\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}=(\lambda_{21},\lambda_{31},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
.
The first option to ensure parameter identifiability is to write

\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{\Omega}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\mathbf{\Lambda}
to obtain
\mathbf{\Omega}_{jj}\equiv 1

for
j=1,\dots,J
was already mentioned. As a second option, we can write
\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\mathbf{\Lambda}\diag(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-\top})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
such that

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top}
is equal to
\diag(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-\top})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-\top}\diag(\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-\top})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
ensuring the condition
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{jj}\equiv 1,j=1,\dots,J
.
Consequently,
h_{j}(Y_{j})
is now
marginally
standard normal in the
absolute continuous case. The transformation to conditional standard
normality arises from
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}_{jj}h_{j}(Y_{j})
.
In the following we refer to these two options as
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}
for
s=1,2
.
In either case,
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is parameterised in terms of the
\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}
lower
triangular parameters
\lambda
of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
and is, for all values of

\lambda
, symmetric and positive semidefinite. Furthermore,
if
\lambda_{j\jmath}=0
, then
Y_{j}
and
Y_{\jmath}
are conditionally
independent given the remaining variables in
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{j}
in both
parameterisations
(the reverse might not be true when some margins are
discrete, see
Genest and Nešlehová, 2007
, Section 4.1.)
.

3
Nonparanormal Log-likelihoods

In the following, we use the term “likelihood” in its broadest
sense, including Fishers original
definition
of it as being
proportional to the probability of the observed data and

approximations
thereof in terms of absolutely continuous densities

(
Lindsey, 1999
, Section 5)
. This point of view allows us to choose
between “empirical” or “nonparametric” and “parametric” variants (or
combinations thereof) motivated by practical necessity and computational convenience.

Before deriving the joint log-likelihood for all
J
variables, we consider the
likelihood for
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}
, that is, the parameters defining the
j
th marginal distribution. For the absolutely
continuous case recall that the empirical or nonparametric log-likelihood

(
Owen, 2001
)
given by

\displaystyle\ell_{j}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\Phi(\theta_{j,r(i,j)})-\Phi(\theta_{j,r(i,j)-1})\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\,\int\limits_{\theta_{j,r(i,j)-1}}^{\theta_{j,r(i,j)}}\phi(z)\,dz\right)

(6)

leads to a convex problem whose analytical solution
\Phi(\hat{\theta}_{j,r(i,j)})=r(i,j)/N

is identical to the empirical cumulative distribution function evaluated that

\upsilon_{j,r(i,j)}
. From this perspective, many “nonparametric”
estimators can be motivated as maximum
empirical
likelihood
estimators and we will follow this path with the nonparanormal
log-likelihood to be introduced in Formula (
8
).

Furthermore, assume we had directly observed the latent
multivariate normal variables
\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\sim\ND_{J}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top})

with absolute continuous density

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$z$}\mid\mathbf{\Omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{J}{2}\log(2\pi)+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log(\mathbf{\Omega}_{jj})-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}\text{\boldmath$z$}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\diag(\mathbf{\Omega})>\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times J}\,\text{lower triangular}

for
i=1,\dots,N
. Then, the parametric negative log-likelihood

-\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(0)}(\mathbf{\Omega})=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\ell}_{J,i}^{(0)}(\mathbf{\Omega})
with

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{J,i}^{(0)}(\mathbf{\Omega})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\mid\mathbf{\Omega}))\propto-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}\text{\boldmath$Z$}_{i}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log(\mathbf{\Omega}_{jj})

is convex in
\mathbf{\Omega}

(
Barratt and Boyd, 2023
)
. In this section, we leverage
both principles to define a novel log-likelihood for the NPN model.

The Nonparanormal Log-likelihood.

The nonparanormal log-likelihood for all
J
variables is a direct extension
of the bivariate log-likelihood for ordinal data proposed by

Jöreskog (1994)
. By replacing the univariate standard normal
density
\phi
in the nonparametric log-likelihood (
6
) with the
J
-dimensional density

\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$z$}\mid\mathbf{\Omega})
of
\ND_{J}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top})
while keeping the integration limits for the
j
th
dimension in a
J
-dimensional integral, we define the nonparanormal log-likelihood by

\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell^{(s)}_{J,i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
with

\displaystyle\ell^{(s)}_{J,i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathbb{P}_{\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j,r(i,j)-1}<h_{j}(Y_{ij})\leq\theta_{j,r(i,j)}\right)\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}\left(\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{i})\in\mathcal{B}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}\left(\text{\boldmath$z$}\mid\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\right)\,d\text{\boldmath$z$}\right),\quad s\in\{1,2\}

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})=\{\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}\mid\theta_{j,r(i,j)-1}<z_{j}\leq\theta_{j,r(i,j)};j=1,\dots,J\}
. We refer to this nonparanormal log-likelihood
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
as “NPN log-likelihood”.

The Smooth Nonparanormal Log-likelihood.

The number of parameters
K(j)
grows with
N
for infinite sample spaces

\mathcal{Y}_{j}
and one might want to reduce the number of parameters in such cases.
For
\mathcal{Y}_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, define
\theta_{jk}=h_{j}(\upsilon_{jk}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j})=\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(\upsilon_{jk})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}
in terms of a spline basis
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}:\mathcal{Y}_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{P(j)}
and
corresponding coefficients
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{P(j)}
, potentially under some constraint
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}\geq\mathbf{0}
. Typically,
P(j)<K(j)
. For finite discrete
sample spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{j}
, we use the same notation with
\vartheta_{jk}=\theta_{jk}
and
h_{j}(\upsilon_{jk}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j})=\text{\boldmath$e$}_{K(i)}(k)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}
, where

\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(\upsilon_{jk})=\text{\boldmath$e$}_{K(i)}(k)
denotes the unit vector of
length
K(j)
with non-zero element
k
and
P(j)=K(j)
. For
a fixed sample size, the empirical sample space discussed in
Section
2
is finite discrete and we can thus establish a one-to-one
connection between discrete and nonparametrically parameterised continuous
models. Motivations for and examples of such parameterisations can be found in

Hothorn et al. (2018)
.
Let
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}=\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j})
and

\text{\boldmath$\theta$}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$})=(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{1}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1})^{\top},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J})^{\top})^{\top}

for
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}=(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J})^{\top}
and define the
log-likelihood
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}):=\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}),\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
.
Because the bases
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}
and thus the transformations

\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(y_{j})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}

are smooth in
y_{j}
, we refer to the log-likelihood
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
as “smooth NPN log-likelihood”, allowing us to estimate a fully parametric model
featuring a fixed number of parameters by maximising an empirical likelihood.

The Flow Nonparanormal Log-likelihood.

When all response variables are absolutely continuous, we can approximate
the smooth NPN log-likelihood defined through log-probabilities by the corresponding multivariate
log-densities. The density in the distribution function

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{\boldmath$Y$}\leq\text{\boldmath$y$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}_{\mathbf{\Omega}}(\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$y$}))=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$y$})}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$z$}\mid\mathbf{\Omega})\,d\text{\boldmath$z$}=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\text{\boldmath$y$}}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$h$}(\tilde{\text{\boldmath$y$}})\mid\mathbf{\Omega})\text{det}(\text{\boldmath$h$}^{\prime}(\tilde{\text{\boldmath$y$}}))\,d\tilde{\text{\boldmath$y$}}

motivates the approximate log-likelihood

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\text{\boldmath$\phi$}\left((h_{1}(Y_{i1}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1}),\dots,h_{J}(Y_{iJ}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J}))^{\top}\mid\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\right)\right)+

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log(h_{j}^{\prime}(Y_{ij}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j})),\quad s\in\{1,2\}

where
h_{j}(Y_{ij}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j})=\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(Y_{ij})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}

and
h_{j}^{\prime}(Y_{ij}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j})=\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}_{j}(Y_{ij})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}

(
Hothorn et al., 2018
)
. Because
\mathbf{\Omega}\text{\boldmath$h$}(\text{\boldmath$Y$})\sim\ND_{J}(\mathbf{0},\text{\boldmath$I$})
is a simple
normalising flow
(
Papamakarios et al., 2021
)
, we use the term “flow NPN log-likelihood” for
\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
.

The Mixed Nonparanormal Log-likelihood.

If some response variables are discrete and some absolutely continuous, one can approximate
the absolutely continuous parts by the corresponding flow NPN log-likelihood in a mixed continuous-discrete
log-likelihood.
Without loss of generality, assume that the first
1\leq\jmath<J

variables
Y_{\jmath}
are absolutely continuous and the remaining
J-\jmath

variables are discrete. We first partition the inverse Cholesky factor

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{A}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{B}}&\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}\end{pmatrix}

with the continuous
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\jmath\times\jmath}
and discrete
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(J-\jmath)\times(J-\jmath)}
parts being lower triangular and the full
matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(J-\jmath)\times\jmath}
representing the interplay
between continuous and discrete variables. We then obtain
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{\jmath})^{\top}\sim\text{NPN}((h_{1},\dots,h_{\jmath})^{\top},\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{A}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{A}}^{-\top})
, a NPN model for the
continuous part with flow NPN log-likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{\jmath}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\lambda_{21},\dots,\lambda_{\jmath,\jmath-1})
, and
the conditional distribution of discrete given continuous variables

\displaystyle Y_{\jmath+1},\dots,Y_{J}\mid Y_{1}=y_{1},\dots,Y_{\jmath}=y_{\jmath}\sim\text{NPN}\left((h_{\jmath+1},\dots,h_{J})^{\top}-\text{\boldmath$\mu$},\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}^{-\top}\right)

with
\text{\boldmath$\mu$}=-\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{B}}(h_{1}(y_{1}),\dots,h_{\jmath}(y_{\jmath}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J-\jmath}
, that is, a NPN model for the discrete part given the realisations
of the continuous variables.
The log-likelihood contribution of all variables is then the sum of

\tilde{\ell}_{\jmath,i}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\lambda_{21},\dots,\lambda_{\jmath,\jmath-1})

and the term

\displaystyle\ell_{J,i\mid\jmath}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath+1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J},\lambda_{\jmath+1,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})=

\displaystyle\quad\log\left(\int_{\mathcal{B}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath+1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J})}\text{\boldmath$\phi$}\left(\text{\boldmath$z$}-\text{\boldmath$\mu$}\mid\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}^{(s)}\right)\,d\text{\boldmath$z$}\right),\quad s\in\{1,2\}

where
\mathcal{B}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath+1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J})=\{\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J-\jmath}\mid\theta_{j,r(i,j)-1}<z_{j}\leq\theta_{j,r(i,j)};j=\jmath+1,\dots,J\}
. Here,

\text{\boldmath$\mu$}=\text{\boldmath$\mu$}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\lambda_{\jmath+1,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})=-\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}^{{(s)}^{-1}}\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{B}}^{(s)}(h_{1}(y_{1}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1}),\dots,h_{\jmath}(y_{\jmath}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J-\jmath}
depends on
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{B}}^{(s)}
and

\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}^{(s)}
which, in turn, depend on
(\lambda_{\jmath+1,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})
. In total, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}^{(s)}_{J\mid\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\ell}_{\jmath,i}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\lambda_{21},\dots,\lambda_{\jmath,\jmath-1})+

\displaystyle\quad\ell_{J,i\mid\jmath}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath+1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J},\lambda_{\jmath+1,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})

and we refer to this form of the log-likelihood as “mixed NPN log-likelihood”.

In summary, we defined the NPN log-likelihood
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
and three approximations thereof. The smooth NPN log-likelihood
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}),\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
computes
log-probabilities based on smooth transformations, the flow NPN log-likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
for absolute continuous responses approximates log-probabilities
by log-densities, and the mixed NPN log-likelihood, given by the term

\tilde{\ell}^{(s)}_{J\mid\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
, mixes
the flow NPN log-likelihood defined by log-densities of the
marginal distribution for
j=1,\dots,\jmath
with log-probabilities for
the remaining elements (which again can be in form of a NPN log-likelihood or a smooth NPN log-likelihood). Each of these log-likelihoods
can be coupled with either constraint
s=1
(unit diagonal in
\mathbf{\Omega}
) or

s=2
(
\mathbf{\Sigma}
being a correlation matrix). Ways to enhance these log-likelihoods to covariate effects
in
h
or
\mathbf{\Omega}
are discussed in Section
6
.

Evaluation of Log-likelihood and Score Functions.

Computing the flow NPN log-likelihood involves simple matrix multiplications whose
gradient with respect to
\vartheta
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\ell}_{J,i}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{\top}\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})(\text{\boldmath$a$}_{1}(Y_{i1})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$a$}_{J}(Y_{iJ})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(Y_{ij})^{\top}+\frac{\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}(Y_{ij})^{\top}}{\text{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}_{j}(Y_{ij})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}}

both for
s=1
and
s=2
. For
s=1
, the score
function with respect to
\lambda
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\ell}_{J,i}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}{\partial\lambda_{j\jmath}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\mathbf{\Lambda}(\text{\boldmath$a$}_{1}(Y_{i1})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$a$}_{J}(Y_{iJ})^{\top})(\text{\boldmath$a$}_{1}(Y_{i1})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$a$}_{J}(Y_{iJ})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J})^{\top}\right)_{j\jmath}

for
1<\jmath<j<J
. The case of
s=2
is more elaborate, the score
function is derived in
Hothorn (2026)
listed in the supplementary
material, see Section
9
.

The NPN log-likelihood, smooth NPN log-likelihood, and mixed NPN log-likelihood require the evaluation
of multivariate normal probabilities over boxes
\mathcal{B}_{i}
and the algorithm by

Genz (1992)
has been widely applied to approximate such probabilities
by quasi-Monte-Carlo integration. The only attempt to also approximate the score function
for these log-probabilities we are aware of was described for the special case of
binary outcomes by
Christoffersen et al. (2021)
.

In this simplest form, for
\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}\mid\underaccent{\bar}{\bvec}<\text{\boldmath$z$}\leq\bar{\text{\boldmath$b$}}\}
, the probability defining the NPN log-likelihood contribution is approximated as

\displaystyle\exp\left(\ell_{J,i}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\text{\boldmath$W$}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}(e_{j}(\text{\boldmath$W$})-d_{j}(\text{\boldmath$W$}))\quad\text{with}

\displaystyle d_{j}(\text{\boldmath$W$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}\left(\underaccent{\bar}{b}_{j}-\sum_{\jmath=1}^{j-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}_{\jmath j}\Phi^{-1}(d_{\jmath}+W_{\jmath}(e_{\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$W$})-d_{\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$W$})))\right);\quad d_{1}(\text{\boldmath$W$})=\Phi(\underaccent{\bar}{b}_{1})

\displaystyle e_{j}(\text{\boldmath$W$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}\left(\bar{b}_{j}-\sum_{\jmath=1}^{j-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}_{\jmath j}\Phi^{-1}(d_{\jmath}+W_{\jmath}(e_{\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$W$})-d_{\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$W$})))\right);\quad e_{1}(\text{\boldmath$W$})=\Phi(\bar{b}_{1})

and the expectation is over
\text{\boldmath$W$}=(W_{1},\dots,W_{J-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J-1}
,

W_{j}\sim\UD(0,1)
whose elements are independent. The expectation in turn is approximated by the mean
over
M
independent draws of
W
. The question arises how many samples

M
are required when searching for the maximiser of the sample log-likelihood

\displaystyle N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{J,i}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}).

Conceptually, because
\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$Y$}_{N}
were assumed to be independent, the variance
of this mean of log-probabilities
\ell_{J,i}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

converges to zero as the number of observations
N
increases, regardless of

M
. For finite samples, of course, one should assess the impact of
M
on
the maximum likelihood estimators for
\theta
and
\lambda
and choose

M
large enough.
Graz et al. (2026)
reports extensive simulation results
indicating that, depending on context, maximum likelihood estimation
may being to stabilise with values as small as
M=250
or
M=500
.

Instead of approximating both
the log-likelihood and the corresponding score function, we propose to approximate the
log-likelihood by Genz’ method in a first step and, in a second step,
to derive the exact score function of this approximation rather than an
approximate score function of the true log-likelihood.
Conditionally on
M
realisations of
W
, the score function
with respect to
\underaccent{\bar}{\bvec}
and
\bar{\text{\boldmath$b$}}
and the score function
with respect to the lower off-diagonal elements of
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}
can
be computed deterministically
by the chain-rule. Scores with respect to
\mathbf{\Lambda}
are
then given by
-\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-\top}\otimes\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}
.
A modular re-implementation of
Genz (1992)
algorithm and its score function, also for
s=2
, is given in
Hothorn (2026)
as
listed in Section
9
.

4
Properties and Convex Approximations

Unfortunately, neither of these nonparanormal log-likelihoods leads to a convex
optimisation problem. We study the properties of each of the four
log-likelihoods in the following theorems. These disappointing results,
however, lead to some insights allowing to suggest some convex
approximations to these problems.

We first consider the flow NPN log-likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
,
which only involves multivariate normal densities, transformation
functions, and derivatives thereof.

Theorem 1
.

Minimising
-\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}\geq\mathbf{0}
for
j=1,\dots,J

is a biconvex problem in
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{j=1}^{J}(P(j)-1)}
and
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
for
s=1,2
.

The negative NPN log-likelihood, defined by log-probabilities, is not necessarily convex in
\lambda
.

Theorem 2
.

Minimising
-\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j}\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}\geq\mathbf{0}
for
j=1,\dots,J

is a convex problem in
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{j=1}^{J}(K(j)-1)}
for given
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
for
s=1,2
. It is not necessarily
convex in
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
.

Consequently, the negative smooth NPN log-likelihood is also not necessarily convex in

\lambda
. Under independence (that is, for
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}=\mathbf{0}
), the NPN log-likelihood
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\mathbf{0})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\ell_{j}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j})

is the sum of
J
marginal empirical log-likelihoods and therefore
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J}
are orthogonal. This property is lost whenever
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\neq\mathbf{0}

and therefore
\ell_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
has to be maximised with respect to both
\theta
and

\lambda
simultaneously for proper estimation and uncertainty
assessment. We conclude with a statement about the mixed NPN log-likelihood.

Corollary 1
.

Minimising
-\tilde{\ell}^{(s)}_{J\mid\jmath}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\jmath},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{\jmath},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{j}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}\geq\mathbf{0}
for
j=1,\dots,J-1
and
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{J}\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J}\geq\mathbf{0}

is a biconvex problem for
s=1
and
\jmath=J-1
, that is in
(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J-1},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{J})\in\mathbb{R}^{K(J)-1+\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}(P(j)-1)}
and
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
for
s=1,2
.

The proofs are given in Appendix
A
.

Given the malign nature of the optimisation problems involved, we discuss
three convex approximations which, at the very least, help to derive
good starting values:

1.

Minimise
-\ell_{j}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j})
, or
-\ell_{j}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}))
, in (
6
),
and obtain the empirical marginal estimate
\hat{\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,J
and get

\hat{\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}=(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\text{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{J})^{\top}
.
One typically tries to avoid
\theta_{j,N}=\infty
by changing the
estimator to the normal score
r(i,j)(N+1)^{-1}=\Phi(\hat{\theta}_{j,r(i,j)})

(
Mai et al., 2023
, or a winsorised version,)

when estimating
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{j}
(this problem is not present when a smoothly
parameterised model is given by
\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{j}
). Define

\hat{\text{\boldmath$Z$}}_{i}=(\hat{\theta}_{1,r(i,1)},\dots,\hat{\theta}_{J,r(i,J)})^{\top}
and
minimise
-\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(0)}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}))

with respect to
\lambda
. This maintains the interpretation of

\Phi(\hat{\theta}_{j,r(i,j)})
as
j
th marginal distribution function
evaluated at
Y_{ij}
.

2.

For the flow NPN log-likelihood, an iterative version with alternating estimation of

\lambda
or
\vartheta
, that is, switching between the two target functions

\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
and

\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\hat{\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}})
,
is known as alternate convex search (ACS) which, under certain
conditions, might converge
(
Gorski et al., 2007
)
.

3.

(a)

Solve the convex problem (in
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1}
) and
minimise
-\tilde{\ell}^{(s)}_{1}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1})
subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{1}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1}\geq\mathbf{0}
.

(b)

Solve the convex problem (in
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{2}
and
\lambda_{21}
) and
minimise
-\tilde{\ell}_{2}^{(s)}(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{1},\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{2},\lambda_{21})

subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{2}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{2}\geq\mathbf{0}
.

(c)

Solve the convex problem (in
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{3}
and
\lambda_{3\cdot}
) and
minimise
-\tilde{\ell}_{3}^{(s)}(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{1},\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{2},\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{3},\hat{\lambda}_{21},\lambda_{3\cdot})
subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{3}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{3}\geq\mathbf{0}
.

(d)

Repeat until
j=J
. Solve the convex problem (in
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J}
and
\lambda_{J\cdot}
)
and minimise
-\tilde{\ell}_{J}^{(s)}(\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{1},\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{2},\dots,\hat{\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}}_{J-1},\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J},\hat{\lambda}_{21},\dots,\hat{\lambda}_{(J-1)\cdot},\lambda_{J\cdot})
subject to
\text{\boldmath$D$}_{J}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J}\geq\mathbf{0}
.

The approaches in
1
. are variants of the two-step maximum pseudo
likelihood estimator, where the parameters of the marginal models are fitted
first. Treating these parameters as known, the copula parameters are
then estimated in a second step. Two-step maximum pseudo likelihood
estimators are used as starting values for the simultaneous maximisation of
NPN log-likelihood functions with respect to all model parameters in
Section
5
.

The sequential approximation
3
. fits a series of linear transformation
models to regressors
\hat{h}_{1},\dots,\hat{h}_{j-1}
, where only
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}
and the
j
th row

\lambda_{j\cdot}
of
\mathbf{\Lambda}
are updated.
This works for
s=1,2
and also allows penalisation of the
\mathbf{\Lambda}

parameters for high(er)-dimensional data as suggested for normal models by
Khare et al. (2019)
.
Variants
2
. and
3
. could also be combined with the smooth and mixed NPN log-likelihood.

5
Empirical Comparisons

The theoretical and computational framework presented in
Sections
1
–
4
is too broad to be
empirically evaluated in an exhaustive way. We therefore focus on one
application highlighting the versatility of the framework
and a simple simulation setup to illustrate potential practical
merits. A more comprehensive picture of the empirical performance of
maximum nonparanormal likelihood estimation emerges from simulation results
published elsewhere:
Klein et al. (2022)
investigate the
flow NPN log-likelihood with covariate-dependent
\mathbf{\Lambda}
,
Graz et al. (2026)
estimate and
evaluate multivariate count models obtained by maximisation of the NPN log-likelihood, and the
mixed NPN log-likelihood was evaluated for the estimation of time-dependent receiver
operating characteristic curves
(
Sewak et al., 2026a
)
and in
covariate adjustment procedures
(
Dandl and Hothorn, 2026
)
.

5.1
Transformation Discriminant Analysis

We discuss a discrimination function for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) diagnosis
based on four biomarkers (DKK: Dickkopf-1, OPN:
osteopontin, PIV: protein induced by vitamin K absence or
antagonist-II, and AFP: alpha-fetoprotein). Based on data reported
from a retrospective case-control study of
403
subjects by

Jang et al. (2016)
,

Sewak et al. (2026b)
proposed the log-likelihood ratio function of a
transformation discriminant analysis (TDA) model as optimal discrimination function. The
empirical biomarker distributions presented for HCC cases and non-HCC
controls in Figure
1
show that PIV and AFP readings are affected by a limit-of-detection
problem. For these subjects, it is only known that PIV (or AFP) is larger
than a specific detection limit, in other words, these observations are
right-censored.

For the analysis of this dataset, we show how one can put together different models playing
with different parameterisations of the marginal and copula parameters and
then picking the ‘right’ likelihood based on properties of the observations
(the mixed NPN log-likelihood because of right-censoring). The same case is made by the

R
code snippet presented in Section
8
, demonstrating
how the theoretical concepts translate into software.

We fit three models to the data. First, a classical linear discriminant
analysis (LDA) assuming a linear transformation function (and thus a linear basis
function
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}
) for each of the four
biomarkers and a common covariance, resulting in a joint normal distribution
of the biomarker values with class-specific means. Second, we replace
the linear transformation functions with potentially non-linear
ones (
2
)
featuring a location term differentiating between classes. As
a third option, we introduce a scale term such that the marginal variability
may differ between classes. In all models, we restrict our attention to a
common correlation matrix.
With
x=1
for HCC case and
x=0
for a non-HCC control and
\text{\boldmath$Y$}=(Y_{\text{AFP}},Y_{\text{DKK}},Y_{\text{OPN}},Y_{\text{PIV}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
the LDA model is equivalent to

\text{\boldmath$Y$}\sim\ND_{J}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\text{\boldmath$\eta$}(x),\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}^{-\top})
. Because this problem
is convex in both
\text{\boldmath$\eta$}(x)_{j}=\beta_{j}x
and
\mathbf{\Omega}

(
Barratt and Boyd, 2023
, Section 5.2.1. in)
, we use a convex solver
as a benchmark for later method comparison.

Figure 1:
Case-control study for Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by

Jang et al. (2016)
: Empirical cumulative distribution functions
(CDFs) for
log-transformed biomarkers alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), protein induced by
vitamin K absence or
antagonist-II (PIV), osteopontin (OPN), and Dickkopf-1 (DKK) in HCC cases
and non-HCC controls.

All NPN models feature variants of the transformation function
implementing a location-scale model
(
Siegfried et al., 2023
)
with

h_{j}(y_{j}\mid x)=\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(y_{j})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}\exp(\xi_{j}x)-\beta_{j}x
.
The LDA model can be formulated by choosing linear bases
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}^{\top}=(1,y_{j})
and
location-only part (
\xi_{j}=0
for all
j\in\{\text{DKK},\text{OPN},\text{PIV},\text{AFP}\}
), however, this parameterisation leads to a non-convex
optimisation problem when minimising the
negative flow NPN log-likelihood
-\tilde{\ell}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
simultaneously in
all model parameters
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}=(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\text{DKK}}^{\top},\beta_{\text{DKK}},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{\text{AFP}}^{\top},\beta_{\text{APF}})^{\top}
and
\lambda
.

The log-likelihoods (normal negative convex and flow NPN log-likelihood) obtained by both optimisation routines are equivalent
(
-2373.492
), this also applies to the log-likelihood ratios.
After dividing each column of
\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}
obtained from the convex solver with the
square-root of the corresponding diagonal element, the estimated values of

\lambda
are also identical, see Table
1
. This means that
the simultaneous one-step non-convex optimisation of the flow NPN log-likelihood resulted in the
same model as the optimisation of the corresponding convex negative normal

The location-only transformation discriminant analysis model (lTDA) is

5.2
Polychoric Correlations

For bivariate Gaussian copulas, the semiparametric efficiency bound is known
and the performance of several estimators against this
theoretical benchmark is studied in this section.
We sample
N\in\{10,20,50\}
observations from
\text{\boldmath$Y$}=(Y_{1},Y_{2})\sim\text{NPN}(\text{\boldmath$h$},\mathbf{\Sigma}(\rho))
with
h_{1}(y_{1})=\Phi^{-1}(\chi^{2}_{2}(y_{1}))
and
h_{2}=h_{1}
, that is,
Y_{j}\sim\chi^{2}_{2}
for
j=1,2
. The latent correlation
between both variables is given by

\mathbf{\Sigma}(\rho)=((1,\rho)^{\top}\mid(\rho,1)^{\top})
for
\rho\in\{0,0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9\}
.

Klaassen and Wellner (1997)
established the semiparametric efficiency bound
(1-\rho^{2})/\sqrt{N}

for the correlation, that is, the standard error of semiparametric efficient
estimators
\hat{\rho}
. It is identical to the standard error of the
Pearson correlation coefficient computed for the
transformed

bivariate observations
(\Phi^{-1}(\chi^{2}_{2}(Y_{1i})),\Phi^{-1}(\chi^{2}_{2}(Y_{2i}))),i=1,\dots,N
, that is, in the oracle case
where the marginal
\chi^{2}_{2}
distribution functions are known.

For both responses being absolutely continuous, we
estimate
\rho
by the two-step maximum pseudo likelihood estimator
(which is,
according to
Klaassen and Wellner, 1997
, semiparametric efficient in this simple
case)
, with two different ways to obtain standard
errors (labelled “ML” and “MPL”, see Section
8
).

We compare the performance of this estimator to maximum
likelihood-based estimation of
\rho
using the NPN log-likelihood, smooth NPN log-likelihood, and
flow NPN log-likelihood, that is, by employing the transformation
\hat{\rho}=\nicefrac{{-\hat{\lambda}_{21}}}{{\sqrt{1+\hat{\lambda}_{21}^{2}}}}
. In addition, we also report estimators of the corresponding
standard errors of
\hat{\rho}
, obtained via the
\Delta
-method for the
procedures described in this paper.

To study the performance for non-continuous data, we transform each variable
to binary and ordinal (five categories) measurements using random empirical
quantiles between
20\%
and
80\%
as cut-offs. As a competitor for binary
or ordinal variables, we use the composite likelihood

(“cML”,
Nikoloulopoulos, 2023
, which in the binary case is identical to the NPN log-likelihood)
.
In absence of a direct competitor for
mixed continuous-discrete responses, we only report the results obtained via
the mixed NPN log-likelihood (that is, a mix of NPN log-likelihood, smooth NPN log-likelihood, or flow NPN log-likelihood for the continuous
variable and NPN log-likelihood for the categorical variable). For each combination of
measurement scales, we repeat the simulation
100
times.

\rho=0.5
, the distribution of the estimators and their
standard errors are given in Figures

and

.
For continuous variables, all three flavours of the NPN log-likelihood attain the
semiparametric efficiency bound for all sample sizes and the corresponding

\Delta
standard errors are very close to the theoretical value. In
comparison for small
sample sizes, the classical two-step maximum pseudo likelihood
copula estimators are slightly more biased, this
also applies to their standard errors. For larger sample sizes, the
estimated correlations are identical, unlike the corresponding standard
errors.

When at least one variable is
categorical, the NPN estimators are still unbiased but the variance of the
estimators increases slightly. Especially for small sample sizes, the standard errors by the
competing procedures under- or over-estimate the true variation, whereas the
standard errors obtained from inverting the Hessian of some NPN log-likelihood reflect the
variability of the corresponding estimates closer. For larger sample sizes,
these differences become very small. For smaller and larger values of

\rho
in the data generating process, results are given in
Appendix
B
. Especially for very high correlations and when both
variables are binary, the estimation performance as well as the quality of
the standard errors degrades.

In the multivariate setup with
J=3,6,9
ordinal outcomes, we
compared the polychoric correlations estimated by maximising the composite
likelihood with nonparanormal estimates. The data generating process was the
same as in the bivariate case, except that we randomly sampled
\lambda

from uncorrelated uniform distributions on
[-1,1]
for each of the
100
simulation
runs. We report the root mean squared error between the true and estimated

\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}
polychoric correlations as well as computing times
in Appendix
C
. With increasing dimension and increasing sample
size, the number of convergence failures increases for cML, whereas
optimisation of the NPN log-likelihood was always possible. In case of successful
convergence, the root mean squared errors were comparable. Availability of
the score function for the NPN log-likelihood resulted in faster computing times compared to
optimisation of the composite likelihood relying on numerically approximated
scores.

6
Application Domains

The range of applications of the NPN model, especially with
covariate-adjustment, is quite broad and we sketch possible parameterisations
for some interesting applications in this section. We start with probit
models for multivariate binary outcomes
(
Lesaffre and Kaufmann, 1992
)
, that
is,
Y_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for all
j=1,\dots,J
. The marginal
distributions are given by

h_{j}(y_{j}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=\theta_{j1}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{j}
, where

\theta_{j1}
is the intercept term for the
j
th binary response.
This concept was later generalised to “Copula regression” via a multivariate GLM
formulation
(
Song et al., 2009
;
Masarotto and Varin, 2012
)
. For example,
marginal binary logistic models feature

h_{j}(y_{j}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=\Phi^{-1}(\text{expit}(\theta_{j1}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{j}))

as marginal transformation functions.
The notion of “polychoric correlations”
(
Jöreskog, 1994
)
was
extended to multivariate proportional-odds models

(
Hirk et al., 2019
,
e.g.
)
. For ordered sample spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{j}=\{\upsilon_{j1}<\cdots<\upsilon_{jK(j)}\}
, a marginal proportional-odds model corresponds to the
transformation

h_{j}(y_{j}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=\Phi^{-1}(\text{expit}(\theta_{jk}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{j}))
. In all these models, the NPN log-likelihood
\ell(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
can be maximised simultaneously in all model
parameters. This also allows likelihood inference for contrasts of
marginal parameters, for example when the hypothesis
\beta_{j1}=0

for all
j=1,\dots,J
is of interest.

For counts
Y_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
,
Siegfried and Hothorn (2020)
suggested marginal
proportional-odds models
h_{j}(y_{j}\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=\Phi^{-1}(\text{expit}(\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(\lfloor y_{j}\rfloor)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{j}))
which, for multiple count outcomes, can be
estimated jointly by maximising the smooth NPN log-likelihood
\ell(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}),\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

(
Graz et al., 2026
)
. This also generalises the bivariate count models by

Niehaus et al. (2024)
. For continuous outcomes,
Mai and Zou (2015)

and
Sewak et al. (2026b)
studied transformation discriminant analysis models,
where marginal transformations include shift and possibly scale effects
differentiating between two (or more) classes, see also
Section
5.1
for a worked example involving both the flow NPN log-likelihood and
mixed NPN log-likelihood. The latter likelihood is relevant for the estimation of graphical
models
(
Göbler et al., 2024
)
or structural equation models

(
Pritikin et al., 2018
)
for mixed outcomes, for the generation
of synthetic data from such models
(
Zhao and Udell, 2020
;
Christoffersen et al., 2021
,
e.g.
for missing value
imputation,)
, and for prognostic
covariate adjustment under non-collapsibility
(
Dandl and Hothorn, 2026
)
.

In survival analysis, multivariate survival times can be analysed by
NPN models with Cox-type margins. Independent censoring
requires the application of the mixed NPN log-likelihood. Such models
have been suggested for the case
J=2

(
Marra and Radice, 2020
;
Ding and Sun, 2022
)
,
NPN models also allow
J>2
. A very important topic are recently suggested models
for dependent censoring. In addition to some time to event of interest
T>0
, one
also observes drop-out times
C>0
and times of administrative independent
censoring
A>0
. For each subject, only
\min(T,C,A)
can be observed.

Czado and Van Keilegom (2023)
and
Deresa and Keilegom (2023)
proved that the
parameters of a suitably defined NPN model are identified even
under this partial information. More specifically, with

h_{T}(t\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=\Phi^{-1}(\text{cloglog}^{-1}(\text{\boldmath$a$}_{T}(t)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{T}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{T}))

and
h_{C}(c\mid\text{\boldmath$x$})=\Phi^{-1}(\text{cloglog}^{-1}(\text{\boldmath$a$}_{C}(c)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{C}+\text{\boldmath$x$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}_{C}))

one can estimate the latent correlation
\rho=\nicefrac{{-\lambda_{21}}}{{\sqrt{1+\lambda_{21}^{2}}}}
if
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{C}(c)^{\top}=(1,\log(c))
, that is, when the marginal drop-out
time follows a Weibull model. The marginal time to event might even follow a
Cox proportional hazards model, for example with marginal log-baseline cumulative
hazard function
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{T}(t)^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{T}
parameterised in terms of a
polynomial in Bernstein form. Both marginal distributions ensure that covariate effects are
interpretable as marginal
log-hazard ratios. The log-likelihood for an observed event time
(
i.e.

T=t,C>t
)
is the mixed NPN log-likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{C\mid T}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{T},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{C}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{C}),\lambda_{21})
. For
a drop-out (
i.e.

T>c,C=c
), the log-likelihood is the mixed NPN log-likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{T\mid C}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{C},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{T}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{T}),\lambda_{21})
.
Administratively censored subjects (
i.e.

T>a,C>a
) further add
the NPN log-likelihood
\tilde{\ell}_{2}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{T}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{T}),\text{\boldmath$\theta$}_{C}(\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{C}),\lambda_{21})
, an illustration is given by
Siegfried et al. (2026)
.

Finally, all the models above can be coupled with covariate-dependent copula
parameters (
1
) as explained in

Klein et al. (2022)
and
Barratt and Boyd (2023)
, for example when
estimating time-varying graphical models
(
Lu et al., 2018
)
.
Unfortunately, and unlike models with constant
\lambda
parameters,
such models are in general not invariant to the order in which responses
enter the model.

7
Discussion

Given the plethora of inference procedures for many special cases of the
NPN model, one might wonder in which cases
optimisation of the NPN log-likelihood, or any of the approximations discussed in this
paper, is beneficial. From a methodological point of view, the NPN log-likelihood provides a benchmark against which other approximations, for example the
composite likelihood in multivariate regression models
(
Nikoloulopoulos, 2023
)
,
can be evaluated. We present a simple
version of such a benchmark comparison in Section
5
,
comparing the two-step pseudo and composite maximum likelihood approaches to several
flavours of the NPN log-likelihood, both in terms of their estimation accuracy and
corresponding variability assessment. The exercise shows that NPN log-likelihood estimators exhibit the variability of semiparametric efficient
estimators for at least ordered response variables. For small samples, the
estimation accuracy of NPN log-likelihood estimators is better, compared to two-step
estimators. Practically
even more relevant is the improved accuracy of standard
errors obtained from the NPN log-likelihood approaches. On these grounds, the application of standard
maximum-likelihood inference procedures to more complex models, for example
when one is interested in supplementing bootstrap procedures by
simple Wald tests and confidence intervals in dependent censoring models

(
Deresa and Keilegom, 2023
)
, seems promising.

An important contribution is the ability to estimate models when the
response types are mixed, that is, some variables can be considered
continuous while others are clearly discrete. The same applies to missing
values in some of the response variables.
The NPN log-likelihood allows a straightforward handling of observations missing at
random. We simply use the datum
(-\infty,\infty)
when computing the contribution of the

j
th, missing, covariate to the likelihood. Imprecise
measurements can be handled via interval-censoring.

From a more theoretical point of view, the consistency of the two-step pseudo maximum likelihood approach,
based on normal or winsorised scores, in combination with the graphical lasso was recently demonstrated
in ultra-high dimensions by
Mai et al. (2023)
. So far, such a result is
only available when all responses are absolutely continuous and in the
absence of any additional parameters in the marginal or joint distributions.
The non-convexity of the negative nonparanormal log-likelihoods
studied here renders them unattractive for penalisation approaches in higher
dimensions. However, the contribution might still be
useful for the estimation of graphical models for non-normal and potentially
discrete responses in high-dimensions. Following
Xue and Zou (2012)
or

Suggala et al. (2017)
, bivariate NPN models could be employed to estimate the polychoric
correlations
\rho_{j\jmath}
. The matrix
(\hat{\rho}_{j\jmath})_{1\leq j<\jmath\leq J}
with
\hat{\rho}_{jj}\equiv 1
can then replace the sample covariance matrix in
a graphical lasso, neighbourhood Dantzig selector, or CLIME.
Xue and Zou (2012)

demonstrated that their “rank-based” versions are consistent with the same
rates of convergence as the original versions based on the sample covariance
matrix of normal data.

Efficiency results on such two-step estimators are sparse.

Klaassen and Wellner (1997)
demonstrated efficiency of the correlation
parameter in a bivariate Gaussian copula, and we utilised this ground truth in the
simulation experiments in Section
5
. Even in this simple
case, the two-step marginal distributions are inefficient.
The flow NPN log-likelihood is conceptually very similar to the semiparametric efficient
sieve maximum-likelihood estimators studied by

Chen et al. (2006)
. The main difference lies in their choice
of a sieve approximation for marginal densities whereas we utilise
polynomials in Bernstein form
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(y_{j})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}
to
approximate marginal transformation functions. If one allows the number of
basis functions
K(j)
in
\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}
, and therefore the number of
coefficients
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}
, to depend on the same size
N
, the sieve space
proposed and analysed by
McLain and Ghosh (2013)
emerges.
Chen et al. (2006)

also proved that semiparametric efficiency carries over to models where some
of the marginal distributions are fully parametric. These results make the
NPN model, its smooth parameterisation and the corresponding
flow NPN log-likelihood, especially when coupled with the ACS optimisation method,
promising candidates for future research.

Appendix A
Proofs

Theorem
1
.

Proof.

As a function of
\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
,
\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}^{(s)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\text{\boldmath$z$}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is convex for
s=1,2
and each fixed
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
.
The argument is completed noting that
z_{j}=\text{\boldmath$a$}_{j}(y_{j})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}

is linear in
\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{j}
.

As a function of
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nicefrac{{J(J-1)}}{{2}}}
,

\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\text{\boldmath$z$}\rVert_{2}^{2}=\lVert\mathbf{\Lambda}\text{\boldmath$z$}\rVert_{2}^{2}
is convex
for each fixed
\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
. For
s=2
, we follow
Khare et al. (2019)

and write

\displaystyle-\tilde{\ell}_{J,i}^{(0)}(\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)})=-\left(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\text{\boldmath$z$}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left(\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}_{jj}\right)\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{j\cdot}\text{\boldmath$z$}\rVert_{2}^{2}-\log\left(\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}_{jj}\right)

as a sum of
J
independent terms.

For
j=1
,
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{11}=1
. For
j=2
, we add the
constraint
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{22}=\lVert\lambda_{2,\cdot}\lVert_{2}=\sqrt{1+\lambda_{21}^{2}}
. This constraint is convex in
\lambda_{21}
and can be
relaxed to the convex inequality constraint
\lVert\lambda_{2,\cdot}\lVert_{2}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{22}\leq 0
.
For
j>3
, we write
\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{j,\jmath}=\lambda_{j,\jmath}\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{\jmath,\jmath}
and note that

\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{\jmath,\jmath}
only depends on
\lambda_{11},\dots,\lambda_{\jmath,\jmath-1}
for
\jmath=1,\dots,j-1
.
Thus, the constraint

\lVert\lambda_{j,\cdot}\rVert_{2}-\mathbf{\Omega}^{(2)}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})_{jj}\leq 0
is a
convex inequality constraint.
∎

Theorem
2
.

Proof.

The normal density
\text{\boldmath$\phi$}(\text{\boldmath$z$}\mid\mathbf{\Omega})
is log-concave in
\text{\boldmath$z$}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}

for fixed
\mathbf{\Omega}
and probabilities thereof are again log-concave
(
Prékopa, 1973
)
.

In a counter-example with
J=2
and
s=1
, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(\ell^{(s)}_{J,i}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\theta_{1,r(i,1)-1}}^{\theta_{1,r(i,1)}}\int\limits_{\theta_{2,r(i,2)-1}}^{\theta_{2,r(i,2)}}\underbrace{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}z_{1}^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(z_{2}+\lambda z_{1})^{2}\right)}_{\psi(\lambda)}\,dz_{2}dz_{1}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\psi(\lambda)}{\partial^{2}\lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{1}^{2}((z_{2}+\lambda z_{1})^{2}-1)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}z_{1}^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(z_{2}+\lambda z_{1})^{2}\right)

The integral of the latter expression is larger than zero for example for
\lambda=0
and

\theta_{2,r(i,2)-1}>1
and thus not concave in
\lambda
for all
configurations of
\theta
. Similar issues have been noted by

Lesaffre and Kaufmann (1992)
in the multivariate probit model.
∎

Corollary
1
follows from Theorem
1
, noting that
\mathbf{\Omega}_{\text{C}}=1
and

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\theta_{J,r(i,J)}+(\lambda_{J,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})^{\top}(h_{1}(y_{1}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1}),\dots,h_{\jmath}(y_{J-1}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J-1})\right)-

\displaystyle\quad\Phi\left(\theta_{J,r(i,J)-1}+(\lambda_{J,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})^{\top}(h_{1}(y_{1}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{1}),\dots,h_{\jmath}(y_{J-1}\mid\text{\boldmath$\vartheta$}_{J-1})\right)

is log-concave (a probability of a log-concave density and linearity in
\lambda_{J,1},\dots,\lambda_{J,J-1})^{\top}
).
