Optimistic Estimate Uncovers the Potential of Nonlinear Models
Yaoyu Zhang, Zhongwang Zhang, Leyang Zhang, Zhiwei Bai, Tao Luo, Zhi-Qin John Xu

Abstract
We propose an optimistic estimate to evaluate the best possible fitting performance of nonlinear models. It yields an optimistic sample size that quantifies the smallest possible sample size to fit/recover a target function using a nonlinear model. We estimate the optimistic sample sizes for matrix factorization models, deep models, and deep neural networks (DNNs) with fully-connected or convolutional architecture. For each nonlinear model, our estimates predict a specific subset of targets that can be fitted at overparameterization, which are confirmed by our experiments. Our optimistic estimate reveals two special properties of the DNN models—free expressiveness in width and costly expressiveness in connection. These properties suggest the following architecture design principles of DNNs: (i) feel free to add neurons/kernels; (ii) restrain from connecting neurons. Overall, our optimistic estimate theoretically unveils the vast potential of nonlinear models in fitting at overparameterization. Based on this framework, we anticipate gaining a deeper understanding of how and why numerous nonlinear models such as DNNs can effectively realize their potential in practice in the near future.

Introduction

In recent years, large nonlinear models like deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved unprecedented success, powering amazing applications like AlphaGo
[
1
]
, AlphaFold
[
2
]
, and GPT
[
3
,
4
]
. However, for many decades, the community had severely underestimated their potential. One major factor is that traditional theoretical frameworks for regression and generalization excessively focus on the worst-case scenarios in which overfitting easily happens for overparameterized models. Currently, the faith of the community in DNNs stems from their practical achievements, which are produced in a way analogous to alchemy. The danger lies in the tendency to overestimate the capability of DNNs. This may lead to the misallocation of resources toward unattainable goals, reminiscent of the pursuits of transmuting copper into gold or creating a perpetual motion machine. In this work, to uncover the true potential of nonlinear models like DNNs, we establish a framework of optimistic estimate for nonlinear regression to evaluate their best possible fitting performance.

We demonstrate the idea and significance of an optimistic estimate using a chemical reaction analogy. By applying the law of element conservation, we derive the following optimistic estimates for chemical reactions: graphite has the potential to be converted to diamond, while gold cannot be obtained from copper. These optimistic estimates inform the potential of a chemical reaction, leading to the abandonment of alchemy and subsequently the invention of synthetic diamonds. Likewise, our optimistic estimate for nonlinear regression aims to uncover the potential of nonlinear models in fitting, which could serve as a foundation for their theoretical understanding.

To assess how well a model fits a target function, the sample size necessary for fitting serves as a natural quantity. In linear regression, the sample size is in general determined by the size of model parameters. Therefore, linear models with fewer parameters are preferred in practice. In the case of nonlinear regression, although theoretical understanding is limited, it has been empirically demonstrated that DNN models can effectively fit target functions even at overparameterization
[
5
]
. For these models, our optimistic estimate provides the smallest possible sample size, referred to as the optimistic sample size, for fitting each target function. It is influenced by both the target function and the model architecture. Moreover, the optimistic sample size as a functional of the target function exhibits rich structures, especially in the case of DNNs.

Remark that, our theory does not guarantee that a target function
f^{*}
with optimistic sample size
R^{*}
can be fitted from
R^{*}
samples in practice. However, our experiments in matrix factorization models and DNNs show that these models can achieve near-optimism fitting performance with hyperparameter tuning. In this context, “near-optimism” means the empirical sample size necessary for fitting
f^{*}
in experiments can approach or even match
R^{*}
. In particular, when
R^{*}
is much smaller than the total number of model parameters
M
, the empirical sample size is generally much closer to
R^{*}
than to
M
. The potential mechanism for this phenomenon is discussed in
Conclusions and discussion
.

Our optimistic estimate reveals the vast potential of nonlinear models in fitting at overparameterization. In particular, our estimates uncover the free expressiveness in width and costly expressiveness in connection properties of DNN models. These properties suggest two architecture design principles: (i) feel free to add neurons/kernels; (ii) restrain from connecting neurons. These principles effectively justify the common preference of researchers to enhance the expressive capacity of DNNs by increasing their widths rather than adding connections. Our estimates also suggest quantitative answers to five long-standing problems in nonlinear regression discussed in
Conclusions and Discussion
. These problems include determining which model can fit at overparameterization, the effective size of parameters of a model, the implicit bias of a model, the advantage of neural network models, and the superiority of convolutional architectures.

(1)

Here the target set
\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}:=\left\{\bm{\theta}\mid f_{\bm{\theta}}=f^{*},\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\right\}

\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}

Above optimistic initialization leads to a best possible fitting scenario. It is possible because any neighbourhood
N(\bm{\theta}^{*},\epsilon)\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
has a positive measure. It is “best” because any point in

\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}

We can analyze the sample size necessary for fitting in a small neighborhood
N(\bm{\theta}^{\prime},\epsilon)
with
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}
using the linear approximation
f(\cdot;\bm{\theta})\approx f^{\mathrm{lin}}\left(\cdot;\bm{\theta}\right):=f^{*}(\cdot)+\nabla f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}(\cdot)^{\text{T}}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime})

\min_{\bm{\theta}\in N(\bm{\theta}^{\prime},\epsilon)}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f^{\mathrm{lin}}\left(\bm{x}_{i};\bm{\theta}\right)-f^{*}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)^{2}.

(2)

This is a linear problem with a well-known result: we need
n=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\partial_{\theta_{i}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\right\}_{i=1}^{M}\right)
samples to recover
f^{*}

\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
. Inspired by the rank in differential topology, we refer to this quantity as the model rank defined for any
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
as

R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}):=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\partial_{\theta_{i}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{\prime})\right\}_{i=1}^{M}\right).

(3)

Model rank in a nonlinear model can vary across different points in the target set. In the optimistic scenario, the model rank is defined for any function
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
as the minimum rank among
\bm{\Theta}_{f^{\prime}}
, i.e.,
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{\prime})
is given by

R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{\prime}):=\min_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}_{f^{\prime}}}R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}).

(4)

Note that the notation
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}
is slightly abused for convenience, representing the model rank of either a parameter point or a function depending on its input. Under optimistic initialization, the sample size necessary for fitting
f^{*}
is
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})
.
We refer to it as the optimistic sample size of
f^{*}
, which determines the minimum sample size needed to fit
f^{*}
using the model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
.

The optimistic sample size exhibits the following properties: (a)
0\leqslant R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*})\leqslant M
; and (b) it is influenced by both the model architecture and the target function. Estimating the optimistic sample sizes across
\mathcal{F}
allows us to tackle a fundamental problem in nonlinear regression: determining whether a model is capable of fitting at overparameterization. We note that overparameterization is not a well defined notion in machine learning. In particular, naively characterizing the scenario where the sample size
n<M
as overparameterization results in trivial cases of fitting at overparameterization. For instance, fitting a linear function with the model
f_{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})x
only need one sample, which is less than
M=2
. In this work, to eliminate such trivial instances, we formally define overparameterization as the scenario of
n<M_{\mathrm{I}}
, where
M_{\mathrm{I}}:=\max_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
counts the maximum effective number of parameters of model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
. If the optimistic sample size
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{\prime})=M_{\mathrm{I}}
for any
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, we can infer that model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
is incapable of fitting at overparameterization. In the subsequent section, we estimate the optimistic sample sizes for two simple models for illustration. We also demonstrate through experiments that the best possible performance estimated under optimistic initialization can be approached under generic random initialization with finely-tuned hyperparameters.

3
Optimistic estimate on simple models

Initially, we apply our optimistic estimate to a basic linear model
f_{\rm{L}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}x_{1}+\theta_{2}x_{2}
featuring
M=3
parameters. Considering any
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}=[\theta^{\prime}_{0},\theta^{\prime}_{1},\theta^{\prime}_{2}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

R_{f_{\textnormal{L}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{1,x_{1},x_{2}\right\}\right)=3
. Consequently, for any
f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{L}}:=\{f_{\rm{L}}(\cdot;\bm{\theta})|\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}
, we have
R_{f_{\textnormal{L}}}(f^{\prime})=M_{\mathrm{I}}=3
. Therefore, we conclude that
f_{\rm{L}}
is unable to fit at overparameterization. Analogously, we can demonstrate that every linear model possesses a fixed optimistic sample size. As a result, all linear models are unable to fit at overparameterization. This finding aligns with the principles of linear regression theory.

The optimistic estimate is subject to change when we make a slight reparameterization of the linear model as

R_{f_{\mathrm{NL}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\{1,x_{1},\theta_{3}^{\prime}x_{2},\theta_{2}^{\prime}x_{2}\}\right)=\begin{cases}2,&\theta^{\prime}_{2}=\theta^{\prime}_{3}=0,\\
3,&\textrm{others}.\end{cases}

5
Optimistic estimate on DNNs

The DNN family stands out as the most important group of nonlinear models for our optimistic estimate. Nevertheless, determining their optimistic sample sizes proves to be considerably more challenging compared to the aforementioned models. In this section, we present exact results for two-layer tanh-NNs with fully-connected or convolutional architectures. Additionally, we demonstrate via experiments that the empirical sample sizes can approach the optimistic sample sizes for DNNs. To address deeper NNs, we establish an upper bound on their optimistic sample sizes.

5.1
Optimistic sample sizes of two-layer fully-connected and convolutional NNs

We first consider a two-layer fully-connected neural network with hyperbolic tangent activation function (tanh-NN) with
m
hidden neurons, represented by
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}\tanh(\bm{w}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{x})
, where
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{\theta}=(a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})_{i=1}^{m}
. This network has a total of
M=m(d+1)
parameters. By our optimistic estimate (see Appendix for details), the optimistic sample size is a function of the intrinsic width of the target function
k(f^{*})
. Here, the intrinsic width
k(f^{*})
is defined as the minimum width of an NN that can express
f^{*}
, i.e.,
f^{*}
can be expressed by a width-
k(f^{*})
NN but not any narrower NN. For any
f^{*}
expressible by the width-
m
NN, its intrinsic width
0\leqslant k(f^{*})\leqslant m
. The optimistic sample size of
f^{*}
is

R_{\mathrm{NN}_{m}}(f^{*})=k(f^{*})(d+1),

(12)

which increases linearly with the intrinsic width. Therefore, a function with intrinsic width
k(f^{*})\ll m
can be fitted optimistically at heavy overparameterization.

We then consider a two-layer tanh-CNN with weight-sharing represented by

\displaystyle f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{C}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1-s}a_{ij}\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{i;\alpha}\right),

(13)

with
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This CNN with
1

M={m_{\mathrm{C}}}(d+1)
free parameters, where
{m_{\mathrm{C}}}
is the number of convolution kernels analogous to the width of a fully-connected NN. By our optimistic estimate (see Appendix for details), similar to the fully-connected case, the optimistic sample size is a function of the intrinsic number of kernels
k_{\textnormal{C}}(f^{*})
of the target function
f^{*}
. Specifically, for any
f^{*}
expressible by the
m_{\textnormal{C}}
-kernel CNN, its optimistic sample size is

R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\mathrm{C}}}}(f^{*})=k_{\textnormal{C}}(f^{*})(d+1).

Therefore, a function with intrinsic number of kernel

5.2
Optimistic properties of two-layer NNs

Our optimistic estimate provides a means to theoretically quantifies the best possible performance of different models in fitting a target function. It is useful for model selection and architecture design. For example, given a target function
f^{*}
, we can trace the change of its optimistic sample size with the design of model architecture to study how model architecture influences the fitting performance.

We first use this approach to study the impact of width for two-layer tanh-NNs. Given a target function with intrinsic width
k
, it can be expressed by all NNs with width
m\geqslant k
. According to Eq. (
12
), all of them yield the same optimistic sample size
k(d+1)
. Therefore, their best possible fitting performance remains the same. A similar result applies to two-layer tanh-CNNs, in which a target function with intrinsic number of kernels
k
can be fitted optimistically from
k(d+1)
samples for all CNNs with
m\geqslant k
kernels. Clearly, for both two-layer fully-connected and convolutional NNs, an increase in the width (or number of kernels) enhances their expressiveness without impairing the optimistic fitting performance.
We call this property
free expressiveness in width
. This property appears to conflict with the Occam Razor principle. However, it is worth noting that the Occam Razor accounts for the worst-case scenario, while the free expressiveness property accounts for the best possible scenario. As demonstrated by our experiments, performance estimated at the best possible scenario is often close to the empiricial performance especially with finely tuned hyperparameters.

We then investigate how interlayer connectivity between neurons affects the fitting performance. For instance, a two-layer fully-connected neural network connects every input neuron to a hidden neuron, while a two-layer CNN connects only a local group of input neurons to a hidden neuron. Let
f^{*}
be a target function with intrinsic number of kernels
k
(each kernel consist of
d+1-s
neurons). For the convenience of discussion, we further assume the intrinsic width of
f^{*}
is
k(d+1-s)
. Then, for all CNNs with
m_{\mathrm{C}}>k
kernels,
R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}}(f^{*})=k(d+1)
. For all fully-connected NNs with
m>k(d+1-s)
neurons,
R_{\mathrm{NN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}}(f^{*})=k(d+1-s)(d+1)
. In practice, because input dimension
d
is often much larger than the convolution kernel size
s
, we have
k(d+1)\ll k(d+1-s)(d+1)
indicating that CNNs are superior to fully-connected NNs regarding the CNN functions.

Note that both reduced connectivity and weight-sharing contribute to the smaller optimistic sample sizes of CNNs. To identify the contribution purely from the reduced connectivity between neurons, we also estimate the optimistic sample size for a no-sharing CNN (see Appendix for details). For all no-sharing CNNs with kernel number
m_{\mathrm{C}}\geqslant k
, the optimistic sample size is
R_{\mathrm{CNN}^{\mathrm{NS}}_{m_{\mathrm{C}}}}(f^{*})=k(s+1)(d+1-s)
. Therefore, increasing the number of connections per hidden neuron from
s
to
d
multiplies the optimistic sample size by
\frac{d+1}{s+1}
. To illustrate the difference, we consider a target function expressed by a
k

3\times 3

685k
for a CNN with
3\times 3

6760k

In general, we refer to the properties of models obtained from optimistic estimates as their optimistic properties. Above, we uncover two optimistic properties of two-layer tanh-NNs—free expressiveness in width and costly expressiveness in connection. These properties suggest two architecture design principles for NNs: (i) feel free to add neurons/kernels, and (ii) restrain from connecting neurons. These two principles justifies the common practice that one would rather expanding the width (or number of kernels) than adding connections to increase the expressiveness of NNs.

5.4
Free expressiveness in width of general DNNs

Estimating
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})
across the parameter space for a general DNN model presents a challenge. Additionally, identifying the minimum model rank within a target set
\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}
is difficult due to its intricate geometry. The exact estimate of optimistic sample sizes for general DNNs is left for future study. Nevertheless, by employing the powerful tool of critical embeddings
[
12
,
13
]
, we can derive an upper bound estimate of the optimistic sample size (see Appendix for details):

R_{\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(f^{*})\leqslant R_{\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{narr}}}(f^{*})\leqslant M_{\textnormal{narr}},

(16)

for any
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{narr}}}\subset\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{wide}}}
, where
\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{wide}}
denotes a wider DNN with no fewer neurons in each layer than
\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{narr}}
.

The above upper bound estimate establishes the property of free expressiveness in width for general DNNs, which states:

Free expressiveness in width:
Expanding the width of a DNN does not increase the optimistic sample size.

Consequently, the optimistic sample size for a target function
f^{*}
within a wide DNN does not exceed the size of the minimum-width DNN capable of expressing
f^{*}
. Hence, the optimistic sample size for a general DNN adapts to the intrinsic complexity of the target function, rather than the complexity of the DNN model employed for fitting.

6
Conclusions and discussion

In this work, we establish an optimistic estimate framework for nonlinear regression, which uncovers the best possible fitting performance of nonlinear models at overparameterization. We apply this framework to matrix factorization models, deep models and NNs with fully-connected or convolutional architectures. Our results predict the target functions that these models can fit at overparameterization, which have been empirically confirmed through experiments.

6.1
Optimistic estimate sheds light to long-standing problems

Based on our estimates and experimental findings, we propose an optimistic hypothesis that many nonlinear models, including deep neural networks (DNNs), can achieve near-optimism fitting performance. However, to validate and support this hypothesis, further comprehensive experiments and theoretical studies are required. To inspire further research in this direction, we note that our optimistic estimate suggests quantitative answers to five long-standing problems in nonlinear regression as follows.

(1) Models capable of fitting at overparameterization:
nonlinear models characterized by non-constant optimistic sample sizes.

(2) Effective size of parameters of a nonlinear model
f_{\bm{\theta}}

]
.
:

R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f)
for any target function
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

(3) Implicit bias of a nonlinear model:
biased towards interpolations with lower optimistic sample sizes.

(4) Advantage of NN models:
free expressiveness in width, i.e., expressiveness of a NN can be improved through widening without increasing the optimistic sample size.

(5) Superiority of the local connection in CNNs:
costly expressiveness in connection, i.e., removing unnecessary connections between neurons substantially reduces the optimistic sample size.

6.2
Potential mechanism to the near-optimism performance

The near-optimism fitting performance of DNNs is closely related to the condensation phenomenon
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
observed during the nonlinear training process beyond the neural tangent kernel regime
[
18
]
. Specifically, in a two-layer ReLU-NN, condensation happens when the input weights of hidden neurons become concentrated on isolated orientations. The model rank of a condensed ReLU network is significantly lower than the number of network parameters. Ref.
[
15
]
demonstrated that condensation happens commonly in the nonlinear regime of the phase diagram for both synthetic and real datasets for wide two-layer ReLU networks. Similar observations have been reported for three-layer ReLU NNs
[
17
]
and networks employing different activation functions
[
16
]
. Additionally, it has been shown that condensation can be facilitated by employing large learning rates
[
19
]
and dropout techniques
[
20
]
. Theoretical research regarding condensation has provided insights into its occurrence during the initial stage of training
[
21
,
22
,
16
]
. In future investigations, we aim to delve deeper into the role of condensation in enabling DNNs to achieve near-optimism fitting performance.

6.3
Implicit bias of nonlinear models

Investigating the implicit bias present at overparameterization constitutes a significant challenge in the fields of deep learning and nonlinear regression. Our optimistic estimate reveals an inherent bias towards interpolations characterized by lower optimistic sample sizes, as elaborated below. When provided with
n
training samples, a nonlinear model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
tends to acquire an interpolation
f^{\prime}
with an optimistic sample size
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{\prime})\leqslant n
, which may not match the target function. For instance, in a matrix completion problem with
2rd-r^{2}
observed entries, a matrix factorization model tends to find a completion characterized by a matrix rank no more than
r
. This implicit bias highlights the inherent adaptability of nonlinear models, wherein the complexity of the acquired solution adjusts to match the complexity of the training dataset. As suggested by our prior studies
[
16
,
12
]
, this bias may be implicitly attained through an adaptive training process during which the model’s complexity, measured by the model rank, gradually increases. Further elaborations regarding this implicit bias will be provided in our subsequent research works.

Appendix A
Estimate of the optimistic sample sizes

Definition

(model rank)
.

Given any differentiable (in parameters) model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
, the model rank for any
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is defined as

R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*}):=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\partial_{\theta_{i}}f(\cdot;\bm{\theta}^{*})\right\}_{i=1}^{M}\right),

(17)

where
\textnormal{span}\left\{\phi_{i}(\cdot)\right\}_{i=1}^{M}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{M}a_{i}\phi_{i}(\cdot)|a_{1},\cdots,a_{M}\in\mathbb{R}\}
and
\textnormal{dim}(\cdot)
returns the dimension of a linear function space. Then the model rank for any function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
with model function space
\mathcal{F}:=\{f(\cdot;\bm{\theta})|\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
is defined as

R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f^{*}):=\min_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}}R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}),

(18)

where the target set
\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}:=\{\bm{\theta}|f(\cdot;\bm{\theta})=f^{*};\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}\}
.

Given a differentiable model
f_{\bm{\theta}}
, the optimistic estimate is obtained by the following two steps: estimate
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})
for
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
; and estimate
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(f)
for
f\in\mathcal{F}
. The difficulty of optimistic estimate depends on the complexity of model architecture. In the following subsections, we present details of optimistic estimates for deep models, matrix factorization models and two-layer tanh-NNs with various architectures with an upper bound for general DNNs.

A.1
Deep model

f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(a_{j}^{[1]}\cdots a_{j}^{[L]}\right)x_{j}
with
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{d})
,
\bm{a}_{j}=[a_{j}^{[1]},\cdots,a_{j}^{[L]}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
,
L\geqslant 2
.

Step 1: Estimate
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})
.

For any parameter point
\bm{\theta}
, the tangent space is

\operatorname{span}\left\{\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial a_{j}^{[l]}}\right\}_{j\in[d],l\in[L]}=\operatorname{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{s\in[L]\backslash\{l\}}a_{j}^{[s]}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{j\in[d],l\in[L]}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\operatorname{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{s\in[L]\backslash\{l\}}a_{j}^{[s]}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}.

We observe that, for any
j\in[d]
, if
\bm{a}_{j}
has more than one zeros, then
\operatorname{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{s\in[L]\backslash\{l\}}a_{j}^{[s]}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}=\operatorname{span}\{0(\cdot)\}
. Otherwise,
\operatorname{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{s\in[L]\backslash\{l\}}a_{j}^{[s]}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}=\operatorname{span}\{x_{j}\}
. We define a function
g(\cdot)
as
g(\bm{v})=0
for vector
\bm{v}
having more than one zeros, and
g(\bm{v})=1
for vector
\bm{v}
having no more than one zero. Then, we obtain

\textnormal{dim}\left(\operatorname{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{s\in[L]\backslash\{l\}}a_{j}^{[s]}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}\right)=g(\bm{a}_{j})

and

R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})=\textnormal{dim}\left\{\bigoplus_{j=1}^{d}\operatorname{span}\left\{\left(\prod_{s\in[L]\backslash\{l\}}a_{j}^{[s]}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}\right\}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}g(\bm{a}_{j}).

Step 2: Estimate
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{f})
.

Given any target function
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}=\{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}x_{j}|\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathbb{R}\}
, we suppose it is
k
-sparse, i.e., it has
k
nonzero coefficients. Then, for any
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{f^{*}}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{d}g(\bm{a}_{j})\geqslant k
. Specifically,
\sum_{j=1}^{d}g(\bm{a}_{j})
attains
k
when
\bm{a}_{j}=\bm{0}
for all dimension
j
with zero coefficients. Therefore, we have

R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{f}^{*})=k.

A.2
Matrix factorization model

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
with
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})
,
\bm{A}=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\bm{B}=[b_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Step 1: Estimate
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})
.

For any parameter point
\bm{\theta}
, the tangent space is

\operatorname{span}\big\{\bm{P}^{ij},\bm{Q}^{ij}\big\}_{i,j=1}^{d},

where
\bm{P}^{ij}=\dfrac{\partial\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial a_{ij}}=[\delta_{ki}b_{jl}]_{k,l=1}^{d}
,
\bm{Q}^{ij}=\dfrac{\partial\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial b_{ij}}=[a_{ki}\delta_{jl}]_{k,l=1}^{d}
. Here
\delta_{i}j
is the Kronecker delta. By vectorizing
\bm{P}^{ij}_{d\times d}
and
\bm{Q}^{ij}_{d\times d}
, we get

\mathrm{vec}(\bm{P}^{ij})=[P^{ij}_{11},\cdots,P^{ij}_{1d},P^{ij}_{21},\cdots,P^{ij}_{2d},\cdots,P^{ij}_{d1},\cdots,P^{ij}_{dd}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}},

\mathrm{vec}(\bm{Q}^{ij})=[Q^{ij}_{11},\cdots,Q^{ij}_{1d},Q^{ij}_{21},\cdots,Q^{ij}_{2d},\cdots,Q^{ij}_{d1},\cdots,Q^{ij}_{dd}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}.

Then, we have

R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})=\operatorname{dim}\left(\operatorname{span}\big\{\bm{P}^{ij},\bm{Q}^{ij}\big\}_{i,j=1}^{d}\right)=\operatorname{dim}\left(\operatorname{span}\big\{\mathrm{vec}(\bm{P}^{ij}),\mathrm{vec}(\bm{Q}^{ij})\big\}_{i,j=1}^{d}\right)=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{\Gamma}),

where

\bm{\Gamma}=\left[\begin{matrix}\mathrm{vec}(\bm{Q}^{11}),&\cdots,\mathrm{vec}(\bm{Q}^{dd}),&\mathrm{vec}(\bm{P}^{11}),&\cdots,&\mathrm{vec}(\bm{P}^{dd})\end{matrix}\right]^{\text{T}}

is a
2d^{2}\times d^{2}
matrix.
Using the Kronecker product
\otimes
of matrices, we obtain

\bm{\Gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\bm{B}&&&\\
&\bm{B}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\bm{B}\\
a_{11}\bm{I}&a_{21}\bm{I}&\cdots&a_{d1}\bm{I}\\
a_{12}\bm{I}&a_{22}\bm{I}&\cdots&a_{d2}\bm{I}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1d}\bm{I}&a_{2d}\bm{I}&\cdots&a_{dd}\bm{I}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{I}\otimes\bm{B}\\
\bm{A}^{\text{T}}\otimes\bm{I}\\
\end{matrix}\right].

Then,

\operatorname{rank}(\bm{\Gamma})=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{\Gamma}^{\text{T}}\bm{\Gamma})=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{I}\otimes\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\text{T}}\otimes\bm{I})=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\bm{B}\oplus\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\text{T}}),

where
\oplus
indicates the Kronecker sum. Let
r_{\bm{A}}:=\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A}),r_{\bm{B}}:=\textnormal{rank}(\bm{B})
. Because the eigenvalues of
\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\bm{B}\oplus\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\text{T}}
are the sums of all possible pairs of eigenvalues of
\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\bm{B}
and
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\text{T}}
, there are
(d-r_{\bm{A}})(d-r_{\bm{B}})
zero eigenvalues. Therefore, we have

R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta})=\operatorname{rank}(\bm{B}^{\text{T}}\bm{B}\oplus\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\text{T}})=d^{2}-(d-r_{\bm{A}})(d-r_{\bm{B}}).

(19)

Step 2: Estimate
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{f})
.

Given any target matrix
\bm{f}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, let
r=\textnormal{rank}(\bm{f}^{*})
. Because

\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A}\bm{B})\leq\min\{\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A}),\textnormal{rank}(\bm{B})\},

any factorization
\bm{f}^{*}=\bm{A}^{*}\bm{B}^{*}
satisfies
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A}^{*})\geqslant r
and
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{B}^{*})\geqslant r
. By Eq. (
19
),
R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})\geqslant d^{2}-(d-r)^{2}=2rd-r^{2}
. This lower bound can be obtained using singular value decomposition
\bm{f}^{*}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\text{T}}
, and let
\bm{\theta}^{*}=(\bm{A}^{*},\bm{B}^{*})
with
\bm{A}^{*}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\bm{B}^{*}=\bm{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{V}^{\text{T}}
. Then,

R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{f}^{*})=R_{\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=2rd-r^{2}.

(20)

i\neq j
. Then

\bm{\theta}^{*}=(\bm{a}_{1}^{\prime},\bm{K}_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\bm{a}_{k}^{\prime},\bm{K}_{k}^{\prime},0,\bm{0},\cdots,0,\bm{0})

(25)

satisfies
R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=k(d+1)
. Therefore, we obtain

R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}}(f^{*})=k_{\textnormal{C}}(f^{*})(d+1).

(26)

Following a similar procedure, we can also obtain the optimistic sample size for two-layer tanh-CNNs with
2
-d convolution. Suppose the input dimension is
d\times d
and the kernel size is
s\times s
with stride
1
, the optimistic sample size

R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}}(f^{*})=k_{\textnormal{C}}(f^{*})(s^{2}+(d+1-s)^{2}).

(27)

A.5
Two-layer no-sharing convolutional tanh-NN

f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\mathrm{C}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1-s}a_{ij}\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right),

with
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1-s},\bm{K}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{s})_{i=1}^{m_{\textnormal{C}}}
.

Step 1: Estimate
R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}(\bm{\theta})
.

For any parameter point
\bm{\theta}
, the tangent space is

\displaystyle\textnormal{span}\left\{\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial a_{ij}},\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial K_{ij;1}},\cdots,\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial K_{ij;s}}\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{span}\left\{\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right),a_{ij}\tanh^{\prime}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)x_{j+s-1},\cdots,\right.

\displaystyle\left.a_{ij}\tanh^{\prime}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textnormal{span}\left\{\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{1+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}\bigoplus

\displaystyle\textnormal{span}\left\{a_{ij}\tanh^{\prime}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)x_{j+s-1}\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}\bigoplus\cdots\bigoplus

\displaystyle\textnormal{span}\left\{a_{ij}\tanh^{\prime}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}.

Let

m_{\bm{K}}=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{1+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}\right),

and

m_{a}=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{a_{ij}\tanh^{\prime}\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}\right)x_{j}\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}\right),

we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}(\bm{\theta})=\textnormal{dim}\left(\textnormal{span}\left\{\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial a_{ij}},\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial K_{ij;1}},\cdots,\dfrac{\partial f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})}{\partial K_{ij;s}}\right\}_{i\in[m_{\textnormal{C}}],j\in[d+1-s]}\right)=m_{\bm{w}}+m_{a}s.

(28)

Step 2: Estimate
R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}(\bm{f})
for a subset of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{CNN-NS}}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}
.

Given any
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{CNN-NS}}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}
, we suppose
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{CNN-NS}}_{k}/\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{CNN-NS}}_{k-1}
, i.e., the intrinsic number of kernels
k_{\textnormal{C}}(f^{*})=k
. Then
f^{*}
can be expressed as
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1-s}a_{ij}^{\prime}\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}^{\prime}\right)
. If
f^{*}
further satisfies (i)
a_{ij}^{\prime}\neq 0
and
\bm{K}_{ij}^{\prime}\neq\bm{0}
for all
i\in[k],j\in[d+1-s]
and (ii)
\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{j+s-\alpha}K_{ij;\alpha}^{\prime}\right)\neq\pm\tanh\left(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{s}x_{h+s-\alpha}K_{lh;\alpha}^{\prime}\right)
for all pairs
(i,j)\neq(l,h)
, then
m_{\bm{K}}\geqslant k(d+1-s)
and
m_{a}\geqslant k(d+1-s)
. Therefore,
R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}(\bm{\theta})\geqslant k(d+1-s)(s+1)
.
R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}
attains
k(d+1)
for

\bm{\theta}^{*}=(\bm{a}_{11}^{\prime},\bm{K}_{11}^{\prime},\cdots,\bm{a}_{k(d+1-s)}^{\prime},\bm{K}_{k(d+1-s)}^{\prime},0,\bm{0},\cdots,0,\bm{0}).

(29)

Therefore, we obtain

R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}(f^{*})=k(d+1-s)(s+1).

(30)

Following a similar procedure, we can also obtain the optimistic sample size for two-layer no-sharing tanh-CNNs with
2
-d convolution. Suppose the input dimension is
d\times d
and the kernel size is
s\times s
with stride
1
, the optimistic sample size

R_{\mathrm{CNN}_{m_{\textnormal{C}}}^{\mathrm{NS}}}(f^{*})=k(d+1-s)^{2}(s^{2}+1).

(31)

A.6
Deep fully-connected NNs

Estimating
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f)
for deep NNs with more than
2
layers is challenging due to their complex composition structure. However, we note that it is not difficult to obtain a good upper bound estimate of
R_{f_{\bm{\theta}}}(f)
.

For a
L
-layer (
L\geq 2
) fully-connected NNs with a general differentiable activation function,
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot)
is defined recursively:
\bm{f}^{[0]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; for
l\in[L-1]
,

\bm{f}^{[l]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\sigma(\bm{W}^{[l]}\bm{f}^{[l-1]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})+\bm{b}^{[l]})
; and the output function

f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})=f^{[L]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})=\bm{W}^{[L]}\bm{f}^{[L-1]}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})+b^{[L]}.

(32)

Here
W^{[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}\times m_{l-1}}
, bias
b^{[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}}
for
l\in[L]
.
m_{l}
is the number of neurons in the
l
-th layer with
m_{0}=d
and
m_{L}=1
. The parameters

\bm{\theta}=\Big(\bm{\theta}|_{1},\cdots,\bm{\theta}|_{L}\Big)=\Big(\bm{W}^{[1]},\bm{b}^{[1]},\ldots,\bm{W}^{[L]},\bm{b}^{[L]}\Big).

(33)

where
\bm{\theta}|_{l}=\Big(\bm{W}^{[l]},\bm{b}^{[l]}\Big)
. The total number of parameters
M=\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}(m_{l}+1)m_{l+1}
. For any
f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}_{\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}\subseteq\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{NN}}_{\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}
with
m^{\prime}_{l}\leqslant m_{l}
for
l\in[L-1]
, we will show that the following upper bounds can be obtained

R_{\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(f^{*})\leqslant R_{\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(f^{*})\leqslant M^{\prime}.

(34)

Suppose
f^{*}=f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}
, where
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}|_{l}=\left(\bm{W}^{\prime[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}_{l}\times m^{\prime}_{l-1}},\bm{b}^{\prime[l]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}^{\prime}}\right)
and
R_{\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(f^{*})=R_{\{m^{\prime}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
. We construct
\bm{\theta}^{*}=\Big(\bm{\theta}^{*}|_{1},\cdots,\bm{\theta}^{*}|_{L}\Big)=\mathcal{T}_{\textnormal{null}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})
as

\bm{\theta}^{*}|_{l}=\mathcal{T}_{\textnormal{null}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})|_{l}=\left(\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{W}^{\prime[l]}&\bm{0}_{m^{\prime}_{l}\times(m_{l-1}-m^{\prime}_{l-1})}\\
\bm{0}_{(m_{l}-m^{\prime}_{l})\times m^{\prime}_{l-1}}&\bm{0}_{(m_{l}-m^{\prime}_{l})\times(m_{l}-m^{\prime}_{l})}\end{matrix}\right],\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{b}^{\prime[l]}\\
\bm{0}_{(m_{l}-m^{\prime}_{l})}\end{matrix}\right]\right)

(35)

for all
l\in[L]
. Remark that
\mathcal{T}_{\textnormal{null}}
is a null embedding operator with output preserving and criticality preserving properties
[
13
]
. Then, we have

R_{\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{l-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(f^{*})\leqslant R_{\{m_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(\mathcal{T}_{\textnormal{null}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}))=R_{\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{L-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime})=R_{\{m_{l}^{\prime}\}_{l=1}^{L-1}}^{\mathrm{NN}}(f^{*})\leqslant M^{\prime}.

Above upper bound estimate shows the free expressiveness property in width for general deep NNs that the optimistic fitting performance never deteriorates as the NN becomes wider. We elaborate on this property in the main text.

A.7
Remark on embedding the parameters of a narrow NN to a wider NN

A key step in above optimistic estimates is to embed any
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{\prime}}
of a narrower NN to
\bm{\theta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

of a wide NN such that
f_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}=f_{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}}
and

R_{\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{wide}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})=R_{\mathrm{NN}_{\textnormal{narr}}}(\bm{\theta}^{*})
. We remark that all critical embedding operators
\mathcal{T}_{\textnormal{c}}:\mathbb{R}^{M^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
proposed in Refs.
[
12
,
13
]
satisfy the above requirements.
In particular, Eq. (
22
), Eq. (
25
),
Eq. (
29
) and Eq. (
35
) are essentially the null embedding in Ref.
[
13
]
. These critical embedding operators are initially proposed to study the embedding principle of the loss landscape of DNNs, i.e., the loss landscape of a wide NN inherits all critical points from that of the narrower NNs
[
12
,
13
]
. Other studies about this embedding relation can be found in Refs.
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. It is clear from the above estimates that these critical embedding operators are important tools for the optimistic estimates of general DNNs. We will elaborate on the intimate relation between the optimistic estimate and the embedding principle in subsequent works.
