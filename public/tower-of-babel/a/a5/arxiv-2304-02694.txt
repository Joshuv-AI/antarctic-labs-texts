Zeros Transfer For Recursively defined Polynomials
Bernhard Heim, Markus Neuhauser, Robert Tröger

Abstract
. The zeros of D’Arcais polynomials, also known as Nekrasov–Okounkov polynomials, dictate the vanishing of the Fourier coefficients of powers of the Dedekind functions. These polynomials satisfy difference equations of hereditary type with non-constant coefficients. We relate the D’Arcais polynomials to polynomials satisying a Volterra difference equation of convolution type. We obtain results on the transfer of the location of the zeros. As an application, we obtain an identity between Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind and 1 1 -associated Laguerre polynomials. We obtain a new version of the Lehmer conjecture and bounds for the zeros of the Hermite polynomials.

Introduction

Powers of the Dedekind
\eta
-function
\eta\left(\omega\right)^{r}
and properties of the
Fourier coefficients, especially their non-vanishing, play an important role in
the theory of numbers
[
Se85
,
On04
,
BCO22
]
, combinatorics
[
An99
,
Ha10
]
, and physics
[
NO06
,
DDMP05
,
DD22
]
.
Let
q:=\textrm{e}^{2\pi\textrm{i}\omega}
, where
\omega
is in the complex upper half-plane. Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}(r)\,q^{n}=q^{-\frac{r}{24}}\,\,\eta\left(\omega\right)^{r}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{r}.

Let
r
be even. Then Serre
[
Se85
]
proved that
\{a_{n}(r)\}_{n}
is lacunary if and only if

r\in S:=\{2,4,6,8,10,14,26\}.

It is conjectured by Lehmer
[
Le47
,
On08
,
BCO22
]

that
a_{n}(24)

never vanishes.
Numerical experiments
[
HNW18
]
suggest that
a_{n}(r)\neq 0
for all
n,r\in\mathbb{N}

with
r
even and
r\not\in S
, generalizing the Lehmer conjecture.

It is known that the coefficients
a_{n}(r)
, are special values of polynomials

P_{n}(z)
at
z=-r
of degree
n
,
the D’Arcais polynomials
[
DA13
,
Ne55
,
HN20a
]
,
also known as
the Nekrasov–Okounkov polynomials
[
Ha10
,
Zh22
]

in combinatorics. We have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(z)\,q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{n}\right)^{-z}=\exp\left(z\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{1}(n)\,\frac{q^{n}}{n}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

where
\sigma_{d}(n):=\sum_{\ell\mid n}\ell^{d}
.
Also,

P_{n}(z)
is integer-valued and

n!\,P_{n}(z)
is monic of degree
n
with non-negative integer coefficients and therefore,
zeros are algebraic integers.
The polynomials can also be defined
recursively
[
Ne55
,
HNT20
]
,
which enables us
to study them
using methods from difference equations:

(1.1)

P_{n}(z)=\frac{z}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{1}(k)\,P_{n-k}(z),\quad n\geq 1,

with initial value
P_{0}(z)=1
. This generalizes the well known
recurrence relation for
partition numbers
p(n)
. Since the times of Euler, it is known that

n\,p(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{1}(k)\,p(n-k).

Serre’s results
[
Se85
]
imply that for each
z\in S
,
there are infinitely many
n
, such that
P_{n}(-z)=0
.
It would be interesting to devise
a combinatorial proof utilizing (
1.1
).
Consider
z
as a parameter and (
1.1
) as a difference equation. We refer to Elaydi’s excellent introduction to difference equations

[
El05
]
. The equation (
1.1
) has non-constant coefficients and is of hereditary type.

Poincaré and Perron (
[
El05
]
,
section 8.2) offered a method to solve difference equations of fixed order with non-constant coefficients,
to obtain the asymptotic behavior of the solutions. On the other hand, for some difference equations of hereditary type with constant coefficients,
called Volterra difference equations of convolution type, one can utilize the discrete Laplace transform, also
called Z-transform (
[
El05
]
, section 6.3).

In this paper,
we develop a new method to study the solutions, and especially the zero
distribution of (
1.1
). We
associate

\{P_{n}(z)\}_{n}
with another family of polynomials

\{Q_{n}(z)\}
. These polynomials satisfy a Volterra difference equation. Then we transfer properties of
Q_{n}(z)

to the D’Arcais polynomials
P_{n}(z)
. In this paper,
we
provide evidence that
Q_{n}(z)
is easier to study,
rather than
P_{n}(z)
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
g
be a normalized arithmetic function with non-negative values and
let
h
be a normalized arithmetic function with positive values.
Then,

(1.2)

P_{n}^{g,h}(z):=\frac{z}{h(n)}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(k)\,P_{n-k}^{g,h}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

with initial value
P_{0}^{g,h}(z)=1
.

Examples of arithmetic functions are provided by
g\left(n\right)=\sigma_{d}(n),\psi_{d}(n)=n^{d}
,

h_{s}(n)=n^{s}
for
s\in[0,1]
and
\mathop{\rm id}(n)=n
. Note,
that
P_{n}(z)=P_{n}^{\sigma_{1},h_{1}}\left(z\right)
. To simplify notation and to highlight the special roles of
s=0
and
s=1
,
we put
P_{n}^{g}(z):=P_{n}^{g,h_{1}}(z)
and

Q_{n}^{g}(z):=P_{n}^{g,h_{0}}(z)
.

Although, we
address the general case in this paper, we begin to illustrate our results
with the following explicit example related to orthogonal polynomials
[
Sz75
,
Ch11
,
Do16
]
related to

g(n)=\mathop{\rm id}(n)
.

1.3.
Main Results

We describe the impact of the smallest and the
largest real zeros of
\{Q_{n}^{g}(z)\}_{n}

on the location of the real zeros of
\{P_{n}^{g,h}(z)\}_{n}
. Let
H(n):=\max\{0,h(k)\,:\,1\leq k\leq n\}
and
H(0):=0
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
g
and
h
be normalized arithmetic functions
with
g,h:\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Let us fix
n\geq 1
and suppose there exists
\kappa_{n}>0
, such that

(-1)^{m}\,Q_{m}^{g}(x)>0
for all real

and
1\leq m\leq n

Let

y\leq x\,H(n-1)

(-1)^{m}P_{m}^{g,h}(y)\geq(-1)^{m}\frac{y}{x\,h\left(m\right)}\,Q_{m}^{g}(x)>0.

Let already

instead of (

(-1)^{m}P_{m}^{g,h}(y)>0.

Further, we obtain a result on the influence of the largest non-trivial zeros of

for

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
g
and
h
be normalized positive valued arithmetic functions,

h(n)\geq 1
for all

.
Let

be fixed.
Suppose there exists a
\mu_{n}<0
, such that for all

Lemma 1.6

(Fundamental transfer lemma)
.

Let
g
and
h
be normalized positive valued arithmetic functions.
Let

\frac{h\left(n\right)}{y}\,P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)-\frac{1}{x}\,Q_{n}^{g}\left(x\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{h\left(k\right)}{y}\right)P_{k}^{g,h}\left(y\right)Q_{n-k}^{g}\left(x\right).

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}g(k)\,\left(P_{n-k}^{g,h}(y)-Q_{n-k}^{g}\left(x\right)\right).

\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}\,P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)-Q_{n}^{g}\left(x\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{x\,h\left(k\right)}{y}\right)P_{k}^{g,h}\left(y\right)Q_{n-k}^{g}\left(x\right)

or

Proof of Lemma

.

To make the proof transparent, we first assume that all involved sums are regular at

We have

\displaystyle x\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}g\left(k\right)q^{k}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}P_{m}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1,m\geq 0\\
k+m=n\end{subarray}}g\left(k\right)P_{m}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{k+m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}\frac{y}{h\left(n\right)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}g\left(k\right)P_{n-k}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{n}.

Therefore,

\left(1-x\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g\left(n\right)q^{n}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}\right)P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{n}.

Here, we extended the arithmetic

.
Since

\left(1-x\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g\left(n\right)q^{n}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{n}^{g}\left(x\right)q^{n}=1,

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}\right)P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)q^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}Q_{m}^{g}\left(x\right)q^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-\frac{xh\left(k\right)}{y}\right)P_{k}^{g,h}\left(y\right)Q_{n-k}^{g}\left(x\right)q^{n}.

Comparing coefficients of the last power series yields

P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(1-\frac{xh\left(k\right)}{y}\right)P_{k}^{g,h}\left(y\right)Q_{n-k}^{g}\left(x\right).

Subtracting the terms in the last sum for
k=0
and
k=n
yields
(
1.7
)

\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)-Q_{n}^{g}\left(x\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{xh\left(k\right)}{y}\right)P_{k}^{g,h}\left(y\right)Q_{n-k}^{g}\left(x\right).

Let
n\geq 1
. We could truncate all involved sums to obtain (
1.7
).
Therefore, the regularity of
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g(n)\,q^{n}
at
q=0
is not needed.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We prove
the
theorem by mathematical induction. The basic case
n=1
is already proven.
Next, let
n>1
and let the
theorem be true for all
1\leq n_{0}<n
.
Suppose
\kappa_{n_{0}}>0

is given, such that for each
1\leq m_{0}\leq n_{0}
and

x<-\kappa_{n_{0}}
:
(-1)^{m_{0}}Q_{m_{0}}^{g}(x)>0
.
By induction hypothesis we have for
y<-\kappa_{n_{0}}\,H(n_{0}-1)
and
1\leq m_{0}\leq n_{0}
:

(-1)^{m_{0}}\,P_{m_{0}}^{g,h}(y)>0.

By (
1.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{n}\left(\frac{xh\left(n\right)}{y}P_{n}^{g,h}\left(y\right)-Q_{n}^{g}\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{0}=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{xh\left(n_{0}\right)}{y}\right)\left(-1\right)^{n_{0}}P_{n_{0}}^{g,h}\left(y\right)\left(-1\right)^{n-n_{0}}Q_{n-n_{0}}^{g}\left(x\right)\geq 0

for
x<-\kappa_{n}\leq 0
and
y\leq xH\left(n-1\right)\leq xH\left(n_{0}\right)
for all
n_{0}\leq n-1
as

H\left(n_{0}\right)=\max\left\{h\left(n_{0}\right),H\left(n_{0}-1\right)\right\}.

This implies

(-1)^{n}\frac{x\,h(n)}{y}\,P_{n}^{g,h}(y)\geq(-1)^{n}\,Q_{n}^{g}(x)>0,

and finally, the
theorem is proven.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
n\geq 2
be given. Let
\frac{x\,h(k)}{y}>1
for
1\leq k\leq n-1
.
Then for each
1\leq k\leq n-1
, the term
1-\frac{x\,h(k)}{y}
is always negative. Since
h(n)\geq 1
,
this is guaranteed
since
\left|x\right|>\left|y\right|
.
We prove the
theorem by mathematical induction. Theorem
1.5

holds true for
n=1
and
n=2
. Therefore, let
n\geq 3
and
\mu_{n}<0
be given, such that for
\mu_{n}<x<0
:

Q_{k}^{g}(x)<0
for all
1\leq k\leq n
.
Then
P_{k}^{g,h}(x)<0
for
1\leq k\leq n-1
by induction hypothesis.
We examine (
1.7
). Let
\mu_{n}<y<0
. Let
x
be given,
such that
\mu_{n}<x<y<0
. Then we obtain from (
1.7
) that

\frac{x\,h(n)}{y}P_{n}^{g,h}(y)-Q_{n}^{g}(x)\leq 0.

Since
Q_{n}^{g}\left(x\right)<0
, the theorem is proven.
∎

3.4.
Bounds on the Zeros of Hermite Polynomials

We denote by
H_{n}(x)
the
n
th Hermite polynomials defined by (
[
Sz75
]
, (5.5.4)):

H_{n}(x):=n!\,\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\,\frac{(2x)^{n-2k}}{(n-2k)!}.

We have
H_{0}(x)=1
,
H_{1}(x)=2x
,
H_{2}(x)=4x^{2}-2
, and
H_{3}(x)=8x^{3}-12x
.
The Hermite polynomials are orthogonal. The zeros are real, simple, and interlacing.
Theorem
1.4
leads to the following result. We also refer to
(
[
Sz75
]
, (6.326)).

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
. Then for the zeros of
H_{n}\left(x\right)

holds

\left|x\right|\leq\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1}\right)\sqrt{2n-2}.

Proof.

Let
g\left(1\right)=g\left(2\right)=1
and
g\left(n\right)=0
for

n\geq 3
. Then

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{n}^{g}\left(-x^{2}\right)q^{n}=\left(1-2\left(x/2\right)\left(-xq\right)+\left(-xq\right)^{2}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}U_{n}\left(x/2\right)\left(-x\right)^{n}q^{n}.

Therefore,

Q_{n}^{g}\left(-x^{2}\right)=\left(-x\right)^{n}U_{n}\left(x/2\right)
.
Note, that
U_{m}\left(x/2\right)=0
implies

\left|x\right|\leq 2\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1}\right)

for all
m\leq n
.
This leads to

x^{2}\leq 4\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1}\right)\right)^{2}
.

We obtain

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}^{g}\left(-2x^{2}\right)q^{n}=\exp\left(2x\left(-xq\right)-\left(-xq\right)^{2}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{H_{n}\left(x\right)\left(-x\right)^{n}}{n!}q^{n}.

Therefore,

P_{n}^{g}\left(-2x^{2}\right)=\left(-x\right)^{n}H_{n}\left(x\right)/\left(n!\right)
.
Theorem
1.4

now ensures that

2x^{2}\leq 4\left(\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1}\right)\right)^{2}\left(n-1\right).

Finally,

\left|x\right|\leq\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1}\right)\sqrt{2n-2}
.
∎

Remark
.

It follows from (
[
Sz75
]
, (6.326)) that the largest
zero of
H_{n}\left(x\right)
is bounded by

\sqrt{2n+1}-6^{-1/2}\left(2n+1\right)^{-1/6}i_{1}

with
i_{1}
the smallest positive zero of Airy’s function.

In our case, we have

\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n+1}\right)\sqrt{2n-2}=\sqrt{2n}-\left(2n\right)^{-1/2}+O\left(n^{-3/2}\right)

with Landau’s
O
notation.
The estimate we obtain from the transfer lemma
is
slightly weaker, since the second order term has exponent
-1/2

instead of
-1/6
.
