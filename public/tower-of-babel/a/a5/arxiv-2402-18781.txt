Conjectural Online Learning with First-order Beliefs in Asymmetric Information Stochastic Games
Tao Li, Kim Hammar, Rolf Stadler, Quanyan Zhu

Abstract
Asymmetric information stochastic games ( aisg s) arise in many complex socio-technical systems, such as cyber-physical systems and IT infrastructures. Existing computational methods for aisg s are primarily offline and can not adapt to equilibrium deviations. Further, current methods are limited to particular information structures to avoid belief hierarchies. Considering these limitations, we propose conjectural online learning ( col ), an online learning method under generic information structures in aisg s. col uses a forecaster-actor-critic ( fac ) architecture, where subjective forecasts are used to conjecture the opponents’ strategies within a lookahead horizon, and Bayesian learning is used to calibrate the conjectures. To adapt strategies to nonstationary environments based on information feedback, col uses online rollout with cost function approximation (actor-critic). We prove that the conjectures produced by col are asymptotically consistent with the information feedback in the sense of a relaxed Bayesian consistency. We also prove that the empirical strategy profile induced by col converges to the Berk-Nash equilibrium, a solution concept characterizing rationality under subjectivity. Experimental results from an intrusion response use case demonstrate col ’s faster convergence over state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods against nonstationary attacks.



I
Introduction

Stochastic game theory provides an analytical framework for automated and resilient management of complex socio-technical systems (
sts
s)
[
1
]
, such as cyber-physical systems and IT infrastructures
[
2

, where decision-making entities (players) jointly control the system’s evolution. Due to the complex nature of

asymmetric
information structures (feedback), i.e., players acquire different information over time.

Information asymmetry poses a significant computational challenge since each player has to reason recursively about the other players’ (opponents’) private information. In particular, information asymmetry causes players’ beliefs to bifurcate, which means that each player has to form a belief about the opponents’ belief (a second-order belief), a belief about the opponents’ second-order belief (a third-order belief), etc., leading to infinite belief hierarchies
[
3
]
.

To avoid belief hierarchies, prior work focuses on special classes of asymmetric information stochastic games (
aisg

[
4
,
5

, stochastic games with public observations
[

, hidden stochastic games
[
8
]
, and common-information-based equilibria
[

10

Moreover, prior work focuses on offline methods for equilibrium computation, which means that the obtained strategies become irrelevant if opponents deviate from the equilibrium path during online execution.

This paper presents an attempt at a unified online learning framework under generic information structures. We study the following question:
how should a player reason about the opponent’s private information and update its strategy online in generic

To answer this question, we present conjectural online learning (

II
Asymmetric Information Stochastic Game

Consider an infinite-horizon, discrete-time (indexed by
t\in\mathbb{N}
), finite, stochastic game
\Gamma
with asymmetric information where players do not perfectly observe the states or actions:

\displaystyle\Gamma\triangleq\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{S},\{\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{k}},\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{k}},z^{\mathrm{k}},c^{\mathrm{k}}\}_{\mathrm{k}\in\mathcal{N}},f,\mathbf{b}_{1},\gamma\rangle.

(1)

\mathcal{N}
is the set of players, indexed by
\mathrm{k}\in\mathcal{N}
.
\mathcal{S}
is the set of states, unobservable to all players.
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{k}},\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{k}}
are the sets of actions and observations, respectively. All the sets mentioned above are assumed to be finite and endowed with the discrete topology.
z^{\mathrm{k}}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{k}})
is the observation kernel, where
\Delta(\cdot)
is the set of Borel probability measures over the underlying set.
c^{\mathrm{k}}:\mathcal{S}\times\prod_{\mathrm{k}\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the cost function,
f:\mathcal{S}\times\prod_{\mathrm{k}\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{k}}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the transition function, and
\mathbf{b}_{1}\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})
is the initial state distribution.
\gamma\in[0,1)
is a discounting factor.

In addition to the above, some helpful notations are as follows. Elements of the aforementioned sets are denoted by the corresponding lowercase letters. Boldface lowercase letters (e.g.,
\mathbf{x}
) denote vectors. A random variable is written in upper case (e.g.,
X
), a random vector in boldface (e.g.,
\mathbf{X}
).
|\mathcal{S}|
denotes the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{S}
.

The game begins with a randomly sampled initial state
s_{1}\sim\mathbf{b}_{1}
. At each time step, each player observes a private partial observation
o^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\sim z(\cdot|s_{t})
. We assume perfect recall, i.e., players perfectly recollect the play history. Denote by
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}\triangleq\{a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}},o^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\}
the information feedback revealed to player
\mathrm{k}
before its decision-making at time
t

[
12
]
. The history can then be recursively defined as
\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\triangleq(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{k}}_{l-1},\mathbf{i}_{l}^{\mathrm{k}})_{l=2,\ldots,t}\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{k}}
, with
\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\triangleq\{\mathbf{b}_{1},o^{\mathrm{k}}_{1}\}
. After observing the partial observation and updating the history, the player selects an action
a_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
according to its strategy
\pi_{\mathrm{k}}:\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{k}}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{k}})
, which incurs a stage cost
c_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}(s_{t},a_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},a_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}})
determined by the joint actions. Finally, the game transitions to a new state
s_{t+1}
, after which the above procedure is repeated.

Each player aims to minimize its expected cumulative cost, defined below

J_{\mathrm{k}}^{(\pi_{\mathrm{k}},\pi_{-\mathrm{k}})}(\mathbf{b}_{1})\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}c^{\mathrm{k}}(S_{t},A^{\mathrm{k}}_{t},A^{-\mathrm{k}}_{t})\mid\mathbf{b}_{1}\right].

(2)

A strategy
\pi_{\mathrm{k}}
is a
best response
against
\pi_{-\mathrm{k}}
if it minimizes
J_{\mathrm{k}}^{(\pi_{\mathrm{k}},\pi_{-\mathrm{k}})}
. Such a best response may not be unique in general, and hence, we use
\operatorname{BR}_{\mathrm{k}}(\pi_{-\mathrm{k}})\triangleq\argmin_{\pi_{\mathrm{k}}}J_{\mathrm{k}}^{(\pi_{\mathrm{k}},\pi_{-\mathrm{k}})}
to represent the best response correspondence. If each player follows the best response strategy against the opponents’ strategies, then no player has an incentive to deviate from their strategy, and the resulting strategy profile is referred to as a Nash equilibrium, defined as

\bm{\pi}^{\star}=(\pi_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star},\pi_{-\mathrm{k}}^{\star})\in\operatorname{BR}_{\mathrm{k}}(\pi_{-\mathrm{k}}^{\star})\times\operatorname{BR}_{-\mathrm{k}}(\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{k}}).

(3)

II-A

Asymmetric Information and Belief Hierarchy

Player
\mathrm{k}
’s belief about the hidden state at time
t
is the conditional probability of the underlying state given the realized history:
\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\triangleq\mathbb{P}[s_{t}|\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}]
. The belief state
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
is a sufficient statistic for
\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}

[
13
]
. Consequently, the strategy can be defined on the belief space
\mathcal{B}\triangleq\Delta(S)
, i.e.,
\pi_{\mathrm{k}}:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{k}})
. For simplicity, we consider the two-player case and refer to player
-\mathrm{k}
as the opponent in the sequel.

Suppose that the opponent is aware of player
\mathrm{k}
’s belief state and uses the same belief to determine its actions as
\pi_{-\mathrm{k}}(\cdot|\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}})
, then each player can compute
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
recursively through (
4
). While this assumption makes the computation of
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
tractable, it is unrealistic since, in practice, each player has their own private information, leading to separate belief states for each player. That is, even though players share the initial belief state
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\mathrm{k}}=\mathbf{b}_{1}
, their belief evolutions bifurcate as the information feedbacks
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
and
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}}
differ. For this reason, each player also has a belief over the opponents’ beliefs, leading to second-order beliefs from
\Delta(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{B})
. The second-order beliefs also bifurcate, leading to third-order beliefs and so on, creating infinite hierarchies of beliefs.

\mathscr{B}(\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1},\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},\pi_{-\mathrm{k}})(s_{t}=s)\triangleq\frac{z^{\mathrm{k}}(o^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid s_{t}=s)\sum_{\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathrm{k}}}\pi_{-\mathrm{k}}(a^{-\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}=a\mid\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1})\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}(s_{t-1}=\tilde{s})f(s\mid\tilde{s},a^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1},a^{-\mathrm{k}}_{t-1})}{\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{-\mathrm{k}}}\sum_{s^{\prime},\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S}}z^{\mathrm{k}}(o^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid s_{t}=s^{\prime})\pi_{-\mathrm{k}}(a^{-\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}=a\mid\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1})\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}(s_{t-1}=\tilde{s})f(s^{\prime}\mid\tilde{s},a^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1},a^{-\mathrm{k}}_{t-1})}.

(4)

Even though prior works bear distinct motivations and theoretical treatments, most of them focus on subclasses of
aisg
where a common belief is shared among all players, allowing for the avoidance of belief hierarchies. For example,
[
4
,
5
]
considers one-sided partial observability, where one informed player, say
\mathrm{k}
, can observe the state, the other players’ observations, as well as the other player’s actions, i.e., it has full observability. The information structures in this type of game are
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}=\{a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}},a_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}},o_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}},s_{t}\}
and
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}}=\{a_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}},o_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}}\}
. The informed player reconstructs the belief of the lesser informed, and thus both players share the same belief. Following a similar approach,
[
6
,
8
]
assume public observations that are shared across players, i.e.,
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}=\{o_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}\}
,
o_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}=o_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}}=o_{t}
. In this type of game, the public observations enable each player to reconstruct the opponent’s belief. Consequently, players reach a consensus over the joint belief state
(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},\mathbf{b}_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}})
. In a separate line of work,
[
9
,
10
]
explore common-information-based beliefs. The common information refers to the non-empty intersection of information structures
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{c}\triangleq\cap_{\mathrm{k}\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
, which subsequently leads to common beliefs among players given that only
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{c}
is used to form beliefs. The typical solution method for the
aisg
s described above is dynamic programming aided by heuristic search
[
14
]
. In generic
aisg
s, however, this solution method can not be applied due to belief hierarchies.

II-B

Subjective Rationality

To avoid handling belief hierarchies,
col
takes inspiration from subjective rationality
[
15
]
, which mandates the player to behave optimally given available information feedback and (possibly incorrect) subjective perception of the environment. In addition to Berk-Nash equilibrium (
Definition

1
)
[
11
]
considered in this work, many other equilibrium notions have been developed to characterize rationality under subjectivity. Among them, conceptually related to
col
are self-confirming equilibrium
[
16
,
17
]
and subjective equilibrium
[
18
,
19
]
, which require players’ subjective conjecture to coincide with objective probability over realizable history. In contrast,
col
uses a relaxed consistency notion that only requires the equilibrium subjective conjecture to be the closest to the objective one among all admissible conjectures.

III
Conjectural Online Learning

Conjectural online learning (
col
) is based on a forecaster-actor-critic (
fac
) architecture where the forecaster first conjectures the opponent’s strategy
\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t}
to be used within a lookahead horizon
\ell_{\mathrm{k}}
at each time step, against which the critic evaluates the player
\mathrm{k}
’s previous strategy
\pi_{\mathrm{k},t-1}
by estimating the value function
\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{k}}^{(\bm{\pi}_{t})}
,
\bm{\pi}_{t}=(\pi_{\mathrm{k},t-1},\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t})
. Finally, the actor updates the strategy through a
\ell_{\mathrm{k}}
-step rollout operation as

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathrm{k},t}(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}})=\mathscr{R}(\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t},\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{k}},\ell_{\mathrm{k}})\triangleq\argmin_{a_{t}^{(\mathrm{k})},a^{(\mathrm{k})}_{t+1},\ldots,a^{(\mathrm{k})}_{t+\ell_{\mathrm{k}}-1}}

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\pi}_{t}}\left[\sum_{j=t}^{t+\ell_{\mathrm{k}}-1}\gamma^{j-t}c^{\mathrm{k}}(S_{j},A_{j}^{\mathrm{k}},A_{j}^{-\mathrm{k}})+\gamma^{\ell_{\mathrm{k}}}\widehat{J}_{\mathrm{k}}(\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t+\ell_{\mathrm{k}}})\mid\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\right],

where the random vector
\mathbf{B}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t+\ell_{\mathrm{k}}}
denotes the reachable belief state at the future time step
t+\ell_{\mathrm{k}}
under the strategy profile
\bm{\pi}_{t}
. Its realization depends on the actions, the conjecture
\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t}
, and the Bayesian update (
4
).

When
\pi_{\mathrm{k},t}
is obtained from (
5

4

\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
through Bayesian learning, which ensures that its conjecture is asymptotically consistent with the observation. Subsequently, the actor and the critic repeat the rollout (
5

\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t}

III-A

Bayesian Forecaster and Consistent Conjecture

The Bayesian forecaster in
col
begins with a prior probability measure
\mu^{\mathrm{k}}_{1}
over a set of candidate opponent strategies
\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k}}\in\Pi_{-\mathrm{k}}\triangleq\{\pi|\pi:\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A}^{-\mathrm{k}})\}
. We assume that the opponent’s strategy is parameterized by
\ell_{-\mathrm{k}}
, which means that it suffices to conjecture the parameter from
\mathrm{k}
’s candidate set:
\widehat{\ell}_{-\mathrm{k}}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{k}}
. We remark that the set of candidate strategies can be obtained from opponent modeling
[
20
]
or prior knowledge of the opponent. For example, suppose that both players employ rollout strategies, then the parameter set
\Theta_{\mathrm{k}}
simply includes all possible lookahead horizons that the opponent could use (this is the parameterization used in Alg.
1
). Note that the candidate set may not include the actual opponent’s strategy, meaning that the player’s subjective modeling of its opponent can be misspecified
[
11
]
.

Upon receiving the information feedback
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
, the forecaster calculates the Bayesian posterior through (
6
), from which a new conjecture is sampled
\widehat{\ell}_{-\mathrm{k},t}\sim\mu_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
.

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}({\widehat{\ell}})\triangleq\frac{\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid{\widehat{\ell}},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}]\mu_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}({\widehat{\ell}})}{\sum_{\overline{\ell}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{k}}}\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid\overline{\ell},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}]\mu^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}(\widehat{\ell})},

(6)

where
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid{\widehat{\ell}},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}]
is the conditional probability of observing
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
given the conjecture
\widehat{\ell}
selected from the candidate set and the current belief state. For
\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}=\{o_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}\}
, the conditional probability is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid{\widehat{\ell}},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}]=\sum_{a}\sum_{s,\tilde{s}}z^{\mathrm{k}}(o_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}|s)\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}(a_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}}=a|\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}})

\displaystyle\times\pi_{\mathrm{k},t-1}(a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}|\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}})f(s|\tilde{s},a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}},a_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}})\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}(\tilde{s}).

(7)

(

[

, while the last one ensures that players’ conjecture sets are rich enough to make objective events also subjectively realizable.

Assumption 1

(i)
\Theta_{\mathrm{k}}
is finite subset of an Euclidean space; (ii)
\mu_{1}^{\mathrm{k}}
has full support; and (iii) for all feasible
(\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t})
, there exists
\widehat{\ell}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{k}}
such that

\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}|\widehat{\ell},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}]>0
.

One natural question arises: does the Bayesian posterior concentrate on the actual opponent’s strategy when
\ell_{-\mathrm{k}}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{k}}
, achieving Bayesian consistency
[
21
]
? The answer is negative due to the belief bifurcation:
\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}\neq\mathbf{b}_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}}
. In
col
, each player
\mathrm{k}
computes the conditional probability in (
7
) using its conjecture
\widehat{\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}
parameterized by
\widehat{\ell}_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}
, which acts on its own belief state
\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}
. Hence,
\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid{\widehat{\ell}_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}]
is a subjective conditional probability. In stark contrast, the objective conditional probability under the actual strategy
\pi_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}
(parameterized by
\ell_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid\ell_{-\mathrm{k},t-1},\mathbf{h}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}\cup\mathbf{h}_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}}]=\mathbb{P}[\mathbf{i}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t}\mid\ell_{-\mathrm{k},t-1},\mathbf{b}^{\mathrm{k}}_{t-1},\mathbf{b}^{-\mathrm{k}}_{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\sum_{s,\tilde{s}}z^{\mathrm{k}}(o_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}|s){\pi}_{-\mathrm{k},t-1}(a_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}}=a|\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}})

(8)

\displaystyle\times\pi_{\mathrm{k},t-1}(a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}|\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}})f(s|\tilde{s},a_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}},a_{t-1}^{-\mathrm{k}})\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}(\tilde{s}).

Therefore, the Bayesian learning (
6
) assigns more probability mass to conjectures under which
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
is more likely to be observed under the player’s subjective belief state, which may deviate from its opponent’s. Consequently, even though some conjecture
\widehat{\ell}
may well approximate the actual
\ell

\mathbf{b}_{t-1}^{\mathrm{k}}
is less likely to induce
\mathbf{i}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}}
than other parameters.

One may wonder what asymptotic behaviors (
6
) displays if the traditional Bayesian consistency does not hold. We propose a new consistency metric based on the Kullback-Leibler (
kl

kl

Denote by
\mathbf{b}_{t}\triangleq(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\mathrm{k}},\mathbf{b}_{t}^{-\mathrm{k}})\in\Delta(\mathcal{S})\times\Delta(\mathcal{S})\triangleq\mathcal{B}^{2}
the joint belief state. Define a product measure
\bm{\nu}=(\nu^{\mathrm{k}},\nu^{-\mathrm{k}})

Since

Compared with the

V
Conclusion

We propose conjectural online learning (
col
), an online method for stochastic games with information asymmetry. By utilizing first-order beliefs,
col
spares learning agents from nested beliefs, ensures conjecture consistency with information feedback, and adapts efficiently to the conjectured opponent. The empirical strategy profile of
col
, shall it stabilize, converges to the Berk-Nash equilibrium. The numerical results indicate the fast convergence of
col
against online-rollout-based attacks. A promising future direction for this online learning paradigm is to design and influence players’ subjective perceptions for incentive provision purposes.
