Generalized Markov-Bernstein inequalities and stability of dynamical systems Thanks: The research is supported by the RFBR grant 20-01-00469.
Vladimir Yu. Protasov

Abstract
The Markov-Bernstein type inequalities between the norms of functions and of their derivatives are analysed for complex exponential polynomials. We establish a relation between the sharp constants in those inequalities and the stability problem for linear switching systems. In particular, the maximal discretization step is estimated. We prove the monotonicity of the sharp constants with respect to the exponents, provided those exponents are real. This gives asymptotically tight uniform bounds and the general form of the extremal polynomial. The case of complex exponent is left as an open problem. Key words: exponential polynomial, quasipolunomial, Bernstein inequality, inequality between derivative, Chebyshev sysstem, stability, Lyapunov exponent, Lyapunov functions, dynamical switching system AMS 41A50, 93D20, 41A17, 37N35



1. Introduction

The Markov-Bernstein type inequalities associate the norm of a polynomial
to the norm of its derivative of
\ell
-th order. The Bernstein inequality
states that for a trigonometric polynomial of degree
n
, it holds that
\|p^{\prime}\|\,\leq\,n\,\|p\|
, where
\|p\|=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{T}}|p(t)|
is
the uniform norm on the period. The Markov inequality states that
for an algebraic polynomial of degree
n
on the segment
[-1,1]
, it holds
\|p^{\prime}\|\,\leq\,n^{2}\,\|p\|
, and the equality is attained
precisely when

p
is proportional to the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{n}
.
We are interested in generalizations of those inequalities
to the exponential polynomials, i.e., to the functions of the form

p(t)\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}e^{-h_{k}t}
, where

h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
are given complex numbers
(in case of multiplicity
m
the corresponding exponent is multiplied by the
powers
t^{k},\,k=0,\ldots,m-1
).
For every
\ell\geq 1
, for
arbitrary
h_{k}
and for a segment (domain), we consider the
inequality

\|p^{(\ell)}\|\,\leq\,C\,\|p\|

with the sharp constant
C
. The case when
\ell=1
,
the exponents are purely imaginary and form an arithmetic progression,
corresponds (after a proper change of variables) to the Bernstein inequality.
If the exponents are real and form an arithmetic progression, then this case corresponds to the Markov inequality.

Note that in the case of real rational powers
h_{k}

the exponential polynomial
p(t)

is transferred by the change of variables
x=e^{-t/N}
to
an algebraic polynomial
P(x)
. However, this does not solve the problem
of finding the sharp constant, since the polynomial
P(x)

is not arbitrary: its degree can significantly exceed the number of its
nonzero coefficients. Therefore, the constant
obtained this way can be much bigger than the sharp constant.

We consider inequalities for derivatives on the positive half-axis
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

naturally assuming that
{\rm Im}\,h_{k}\,>\,0
and, therefore,
all the polynomials
p(t)
tend to zero as
t\to+\infty
.
The main problem is to find the precise value or at least to estimate
the sharp constant
C
in the inequality

\|p^{(\ell)}\|\leq C\,\|p\|
. For the derivative
p^{(\ell)}
, we always choose
the norm in
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, while the norm
\|p\|
of the polynomial
can be taken more general. In particular, a part of the results
are obtained for an arbitrary monotone norm on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

(the monotonicity means that
\|f\|\geq\|g\|
if
|f(t)|\geq|g(t)|

at every point
\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
).

We are interested in the values of the constants
C=C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})

in the Markov-Bernstein inequality for fixed exponents
{\boldsymbol{h}}

and in the uniform constants for all values of
{\boldsymbol{h}}

from a given domain. This problem is motivated
by applications in the study of trajectories of linear systems.

The problem of the maximal initial velocity of a bounded trajectory.

If a function

x\in C^{n}[0,+\infty)
is a solution of a linear ODE with constant coefficients

x^{(n)}\,=\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}x^{(k)}(t)
, then how large its initial
derivative
x^{\prime}(0)
can be under the condition
\|x\|_{C[0,+\infty)}\leq R
,
where

R>0
is given? In other words, the trajectory never leaves the ball
of radius
R
. The same question can be formulated for the
higher order derivatives
x^{(\ell)}(0)
.
The answer is that
|x^{(\ell)}(0)|\,\leq\,R\,C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})
,
where

C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})
is the sharp constant in the Markov-Bernstein inequality for
exponential polynomials over
the system
\{e^{-h_{k}t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, where
\{-h_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}

are the roots of the characteristic polynomial of the ODE.
Similar problems are considered for other norms, for example, for
\|x\|_{L_{p}}\leq R
.

The stability problem for linear switching systems
.
This is probably the most important application and the main motivation of this research.
We realize it in Section 7. The linear switching system is a linear ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\,=\,A(t){\boldsymbol{x}}(t),\ {\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
A(\cdot)
is an arbitrary measurable function
taking values on some compact set of
n\times n
matrices
{\cal{A}}
.
Such systems regularly arise in engineering applications. Their systematic study
began in late 70-s, see
[
29
,
26
,
27
,
21
]
and references therein.
A system is asymptotically stable if its trajectories
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)

tend to zero as
t\to+\infty
for all functions
A(\cdot)
.
One of the methods of proving stability is the discretization of the system, i.e.,
its approximation by the discrete-time system:

{\boldsymbol{x}}(k+1)\,=\,\bigl(I\,+\,\tau A(k)\bigr){\boldsymbol{x}}(k),\ k=0,1,\ldots
,
where
I
is the identity matrix. It is known
[
27
]
that
if the system becomes stable after the discretization with
some step
\tau
, then it does
for all smaller steps and it is stable (as a continuous-time system).
The problem consists of finding the longest possible step
\tau
for which
the converse is also true with some precision
\varepsilon>0
. This means that
if the system
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,A{\boldsymbol{x}}

is stable, then the discrete system
{\boldsymbol{x}}(k+1)\,=\,\bigl(I\,+\,\tau\,(A(k)\,-\,\varepsilon I)\bigr)\,{\boldsymbol{x}}(k)
is stable as well.
Since there are efficient methods for deciding the discrete-time stability
[
15
,
17
,
32
,
23
,
24
]
, it follows that an efficient estimate of the discretization step
\tau

makes those methods applicable also for continuous-time systems.
This idea was developed in
[
2
,
10
,
30
,
31
]
, etc.
In Section 7 we estimate
\tau
in terms of the sharp constant
C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})

in the Markov-Bernstein inequality for
\ell=2
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}=-{\rm sp}\,(A),\ A\in{\cal{A}}
,
where
{\rm sp}\,(A)
denotes the set of eigenvalues of the matrix
A
.
Those results are formulated in Theorems
4
and
5
.

The fundamental results
. We obtain the estimates for the step size
\tau

by the constants
C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})
for arbitrary vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}
, while
the estimates and the sharp values of these constants are found only for the
real vectors. In the latter case the system of exponents is a Chebyshev system
and, therefore, for finding the extremal function we can apply the
alternance. A similar idea for estimating the constants in the Markov-Bernstein inequality
for real exponential polynomials have been exploited in
[
6
]
–
[
9
]
,
[
25
,
28
,
33
]
. We establish the comparison theorem (Theorem
1
)
according to which the constant
C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})
strictly decreases in
each component
h_{k}
. This is true not only for a uniform norm in
C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})

but also for each
L_{p}
-norm, and, more generally, for all monotone norms
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
Therefore, this constant reaches its biggest value on the set
h_{k}\leq\alpha_{k},\ k=1,\ldots,n
, at the point
{\boldsymbol{h}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
.
This result allows us to obtain uniform estimates for the constant
C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})

for various ranges of the vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}
.
For example, under the conditions
h_{k}\leq 1,\ k=1,\ldots,n
,
the maximal values are attained for the polynomial
p(t)=e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)
,
where
R_{n-1}
is the Chebyshev polynomial with the Laguerre
weight
e^{-t}
(Theorem
2
). Applying known estimates on the derivative of those polynomials
[
11
,
14
,
25
,
36
]
and especially the asymptotically sharp bound
of V.Sklyarov
[
35
]
, we obtain uniform estimates
for the constant in the Markov-Bernstein inequality (Theorem
3
).
They lead to practically applicable estimates for the discretization step
\tau

in the problem of stability of linear switching systems.
Those estimates, however, can be used only for systems of real exponents, i.e.,
for switching systems defined by matrices with purely real spectra.

The problem of generalization to arbitrary exponents
.
A significant disadvantage of the obtained results is that they hold
for real exponents. This restriction looks especially strange
in the problem of the discretization steplength, where our estimates
are true only when all the matrices of the system have real spectrum.
This phenomenon does not cause problems for a concrete switching system since
the value
C(\ell,{\boldsymbol{h}})
can be found for every
{\boldsymbol{h}}
as
the solution of a convex problem
p^{(\ell)}(0)\to\max,\ \|p\|\leq 1
.
However, to derive uniform estimates one needs the comparison theorem, whose proof
cannot be extended to arbitrary systems of exponents since they do not form
a Chebyshev system. We leave the proof of the comparison theorem
for complex exponents as an open problem (Conjecture 1). If the answer is affirmative,
then all estimates obtained for real exponents are extended to the complex case.

Notation
.
We denote vectors by bold letters and scalars by standard letters. Thus,

{\boldsymbol{h}}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})
.
We use the standard notation

{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{++}
for the set of nonnegative numbers and of positive numbers
respectively; similarly
{\mathbb{C}}_{++}=\bigl\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\ |\ {\rm Re}\,z\,>\,0\bigr\}

is an open right half-plane of the complex plane;

L_{p}
-norm on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
will be denoted as
\|\cdot\|_{p}
.
In particular, if
f\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, then
\|f\|_{\infty}=\|f\|_{C({\mathbb{R}}_{+})}
.

2. Statement of the problem

For a given vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}_{++}
, we
consider the system of exponent

E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}=\{e^{-h_{k}t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
. Some of the numbers
h_{k}
can coincide,
in this case the corresponding exponents are multiplied by powers of
t
.
If, for instance, the components
h_{1},\ldots,h_{r}
are equal and are different from all others, i.e.,
the exponent
h_{1}
has multiplicity
r
,
then the functions
e^{-h_{1}t},\ldots,e^{-h_{r}t}

are replaced by
e^{-h_{1}t},te^{-h_{1}t},\ldots,t^{r-1}e^{-h_{1}t}
respectively.
A
polynomial
by the system
\{e^{-h_{k}t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, or a
quasipolynomial

is an arbitrary linear combination of those exponents with complex coefficients.
The linear space by a given system on the half-line
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

is denoted by
\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.
This is an
n
-dimensional subspace

C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
of functions that are continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

an tend to zero as
t\to+\infty
.
The map
{\boldsymbol{h}}\mapsto\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
is well-defined and continuous
[
18
,
20
]
.

The real part of a quasipolynomial is a real linear combination of the
functions
t^{m}e^{-\alpha_{k}t}\cos\beta_{k}t
,
\ t^{m}e^{-\alpha_{k}t}\sin\beta_{k}t,\ k=1,\ldots,n
, where

\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}
are the real and the imaginary part of
h_{k}
, and the degree
m

does not exceed the multiplicity of
h_{k}
. Linear combinations of those functions
with real coefficients form the
space of real quasipolynomials
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.

Consider an arbitrary
monotone norm

\|\cdot\|
in the space
\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.
The monotonicity means that if

|f_{1}(t)|\geq|f_{2}(t)|
for all
t\geq 0
, then
\|f_{1}\|\geq\|f_{2}\|
.
For example, the
L_{p}
-norms,
p\in[1,+\infty]
, the weighted

L_{p}
-norms are monotone. For every monotone norm and for an arbitrary functional

F:{\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we consider the following problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}F(p)\ \to\ \min\\
p\in{\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}\,,\ \|p\|\leq 1\,.\end{array}\right.

(1)

The value of this problem (the minimal value of the objective function)
will be denoted by
\Phi({\boldsymbol{h}})
.
Since this problem is convex,
\Phi({\boldsymbol{h}})
can be found by standard tools of
convex programming. We, however, are interested not in the
numerical solution but in the description of the extremal polynomial
and in uniform estimates for
\Phi({\boldsymbol{h}})

over all vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}
from a given set. We deal with the sets
{\cal{D}}_{n}\ =\,\bigl\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\ |\ |z_{k}|\leq 1,\ {\rm Re}\,z_{k}\,>\,0,\ \ k=1,\ldots,n\bigr\}\,
and
\,{\cal{I}}_{n}\,=\,\bigl\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ |\ 0<\,x_{k}\,\leq 1,\ k=1,\ldots,n\bigr\}
.
We also use simplified notation:

{\cal{D}}_{1}={\cal{D}}
(half-disc) and
{\cal{I}}_{1}={\cal{I}}
(half-interval
(0,1]
).

In this section we consider the problem (
1
)
in the uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}

on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

for the functional
F(p)\,=\,-\,p^{(\ell)}(0)
, where
p^{(\ell)}

is the
\ell
-th derivative of
p
,
\ell\geq 1
.
Thus, one finds the value of the
\ell
-th derivative of the polynomial
p

at zero under the assumption
\|p\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
This is equivalent to finding the maximal norm
\|p^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty}

on the unit ball, which is equal to the sharp constant in the Markov-Bernstein inequality
for polynomials from
\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.

Definition 1

For a given vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{++}^{n}
, we set

M_{\,\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\ =\ \max_{\|p\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\ p\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}\ \bigl\|\,p^{\,(\ell)}\,\bigr\|_{\infty},

and

M_{\,\ell,n}\ =\ \max_{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\cal{D}}_{n}}M_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,.

If we search the extremal polynomial among the real exponential
polynomials with real coefficients
h_{i}
, then

{\boldsymbol{h}}
is the real positive vector and the set
{\cal{D}}_{n}

is the non-closed unit cube

{\cal{I}}_{n}\,=\,(0,1]^{n}
:

m_{\,\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\ =\ \max_{\|p\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\ p\in{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}}\ \bigl\|\,p^{\,(\ell)}\,\bigr\|_{\infty},\qquad m_{\,\ell,n}\ =\ \max_{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\cal{I}}_{n}}m_{\,\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,.

Clearly,

m_{\,\ell,n}\,\leq\,M_{\,\ell,n}
.
Our main conjecture is that those two numbers are actually equal:

Conjecture 1

For all
\ell,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
m_{\,\ell,n}\,=\,M_{\,\ell,n}
.

The value
M_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
is the sharp constant in the Markov-Bernstein type inequality
for exponential polynomials:

\|p^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty}\ \leq\ M_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,\|p\|_{\infty},\ p\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.
In fact, the same constant
M_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
is sharp in the same inequality
also for the class of real exponential polynomials
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.
For the proof, it suffices to observe that by multiplying
the polynomial
p
by a proper number
\,e^{i\alpha},\ \alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
and by translating we obtain the equality
\|p^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty}\,=\,{\rm Re}\,p^{(\ell)}(0)
.
Thus,
M_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
is the value of the problem
{\rm Re}\,p^{(\ell)}(0)\to\max\,,\ \|p\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. The replacement of the polynomial
p
by its real part, which is a real
quasipolynomial
q
, does not change the value
{\rm Re}\,p^{(\ell)}(0)\,=\,q^{(\ell)}(0)
and does not increase the norm. Therefore, the restriction of our problem to the class of real
quasipolynomials does not change its value.

Then we will return to an arbitrary monotone norm in the problem (
1
)
and will focus on real exponents
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
.
We will prove the comparison theorem (theorem
1
)
according to which the value of the problem strictly increases in each component
h_{i}
.
This yields that the value
m_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
also increases in each component
h_{i}
.
This allows us to find the optimal value
over various domains of the parameter
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and obtain uniform bounds over those
domains. Further we apply those estimates to the
problem of stability
of linear switching systems.

3. The case of real exponents

A system
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}=\{e^{-h_{k}t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is called real if

h_{k}
are all real. In this case all those numbers are strictly positive and
are assumed to be arranged in ascending order:
0<h_{1}\leq\cdots\leq h_{n}
.
The space of polynomials by the system
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
with real coefficients
is called the space of real exponential polynomials (or real quasipolynomials)
and is denoted by
{\cal{RP}}({\boldsymbol{h}})
. The main results of this section are easily generalized
to polynomials with complex coefficients (but still with real exponents
h_{i}
).
For the sake of simplicity, we consider only the case of real coefficients.
We need several basic properties of the space
{\cal{RP}}({\boldsymbol{h}})
.

1)
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
is a Cartesian system, i.e.,
it satisfies the Cartesian rule of counting zeros, see, for example,
[
12
]
.
This implies in particular that if the polynomial
p\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
has
n-1

zeros on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
counting multiplicities, then all its
coefficients are nonzero and their signs alternate.

The Cartesian property can be proved by approximating all the numbers

h_{i}
by rational numbers
\tilde{h}_{i}
and by the change of variables

x=e^{-t/N}
, where
N
is such that

\tilde{h}_{i}N\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,\,i=1,\ldots,n
.
Thus,
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
is approximated with arbitrary precision
by algebraic polynomials, which, as we know, form a Cartesian system.

2) Every Cartesian system is also a Chebyshev system
(or Haar system), i.e., every nontrivial polynomial from
\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}

has at most
n-1
zeros
[
19
,
20
,
12
]
. Thus,
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}

is a Chebyshev system on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
For every
n
, for arbitrary
t_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, and for arbitrary numbers

c_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},\,i=1,\ldots,n
, there exists a polynomial
p\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}

for which
p(t_{i})=c_{i}
.

3) For each
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
\ell
-th derivative of the polynomial
p\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}

has at most
n-\ell
zeros on

{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
counting multiplicities.

Indeed, if we denote all zeros of
p
by
\,a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}<+\infty
,
assuming now that they are all different, and adding an extra zero

a_{k+1}=+\infty
(since
p(+\infty)=0
)
we come to the conclusion that each interval

(a_{i},a_{i+1}),\,\,i=1,\ldots,k
, contains at least one zero of the
derivative
p\,^{\prime}
.
Therefore,
p\,^{\prime}
has at least
k

zeros on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Applying induction we extend this property for all derivatives
p^{(\ell)}
.
The case of multiple roots follows by a limit passage.

4. The comparison theorem

For a system of real exponents
{\boldsymbol{h}}
, it is possible
not only to compute the value
m_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
but also to
analyse its behaviour as a function of the arguments
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
.
In other words, one can find the asymptotics of the
\ell
-th derivative
of the polynomial in the unit ball of the space
\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
depending on the vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}
.
Moreover, this can be done not only for the unit ball in the uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, but also for every monotone norm in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, in particular, for the
L_{p}
-norm.
As we mentioned in the previous section, it is sufficient to solve this problem
the space of quasipolynomials with real coefficients
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.

Consider the problem (
1
) for the functional
F(p)=-p^{(\ell)}(0)

and for a fixed monotone norm
\|\cdot\|
in
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.
Thus, it suffices to find the maximum of the
\ell
-th derivative
at zero under the assumption
\|\cdot\|\leq 1
. Denote by
\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})

the value of this problem. The polynomial
p

on which this maximum is attained has

n-1
zeros (counting multiplicities) on the ray
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Otherwise,
there would exist a polynomial

q\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
that has the same roots as
p
does and such that

p(t)q(t)<0
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
different from the roots of
p

and for which
p^{(\ell)}(0)q^{(\ell)}(0)>0
.
Then for sufficiently small
\lambda
, we have

\|p+q\|<1
and
F(p+q)>F(p)
, which contradicts to the optimality of
p
.

Theorem 1

(
comparison theorem
)
Let
\|\cdot\|
be an arbitrary monotone norm in
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
and

\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
be the maximal value of
p^{(\ell)}(0)
for all possible
p\in{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}

such that
\|p\|\leq 1
.
Then, if the vectors
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{h}}\,\in\,{\mathbb{R}}_{++}
are such that

\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\geq{\boldsymbol{h}}\,
and
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}
, then for all
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
we have
\ \Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime})>\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
.

We see that the value of the problem (
1
)
increases in every variable
h_{i}
.
Applying this theorem for the
L_{\infty}
-norm, we obtain

Corollary 1

If
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\geq{\boldsymbol{h}}\,
and
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}\neq{\boldsymbol{h}}
, then for each
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\ m_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime})\,>\,m_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
.

For
\ell\,\,=1
, this corollary is analogous to the comparison theorem
for hyperbolic sines
[
8
]
, but the method of the proof is
different and is based on the following key fact:
a small perturbation of a Chebyshev system
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}

generates a bigger Chebyshev system (i.e.,
a system of a larger number of functions). This provides an additional
degree of freedom for the choice of the parameter and makes it possible to reduce the
norm of the polynomial and to simultaneously increase the value of the objective function
F
.

We will use the following fact that is simply derived from the convexity of a norm.

Lemma 1

If
p
and
q
are elements of a normed space,
\|p\|=1
and

\|p+q\|<1
, then there exists a positive constant
c
such that
\|p+\lambda q\|\,<\,1\,-\,c\lambda

for all
\,\lambda\in(0,1]
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.
Without loss of generality we assume that

{p^{(\ell)}(0)>0}
. It suffices to consider the case
when all coordinates of the vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}
, except for one of them,
say, the
r
th one, coincide:
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{h}}+\tau{\boldsymbol{e}}_{r}\,,\ \tau>0
.
After this the theorem is proved by step-by-step changing the coordinates.
This, in turn, is sufficient to prove for small variations of

\tau
, because locally increasing functions increase globally.
Further, it can be assumed that the exponent
h_{\,r}
is simple.
Indeed, if it is multiple, then the inequality
F(p_{\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}})\geq F(p_{\,{\boldsymbol{h}}})

follows from the continuity,
and the strict inequality
F(p_{\,{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}})>F(p_{\,{\boldsymbol{h}}})

follows by replacing the shift
h_{\,r}\to h_{\,r}^{\prime}

by two consecutive shifts
h_{\,r}\to h_{\,r}^{\prime\prime}\to h_{\,r}^{\prime}
with simple
exponent
h_{\,r}^{\prime\prime}
. The first shift does not reduce
F
and the second one
increases it. Thus, the exponent
h_{\,r}
is simple. Assume that so are all other
h_{k}

(in the general case the proof is similar).

The maximum of
F
is attained at some polynomial
p(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}e^{-h_{k}t}
,
that has

n-1
roots on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Suppose all of them are simple:
0<\mu_{1}<\ldots<\mu_{n-1}<\infty
. The general case is treated in the same way.
Consider the vector
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and a number
\delta>0

which will be specified later.
For arbitrary
\lambda>0
, take a
(\lambda{\boldsymbol{y}},\lambda\delta)
-variation
of the polynomial
p
:

p_{\lambda}(t)\ =\ (p_{r}\,+\,\lambda y_{r})\,e^{-(h_{\,r}+\lambda\delta)t}\,+\,\sum_{k\neq r}(p_{k}\,+\,\lambda y_{k})\,e^{-h_{k}t}

Thus,
p_{\lambda}\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}}
with
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{h}}+\lambda\delta{\boldsymbol{e}}_{r}
.
We aim to find
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
\delta
such that
p_{\lambda}
has the small norm and
a large value
F
for sufficiently small
\lambda>0
.
Then
e^{-(h_{r}+\lambda\delta)t}\,=\,e^{-h_{r}t}(1-\lambda\delta t)\,+\,O(\lambda^{2})
, and therefore

p_{\lambda}(t)\,-\,p(t)\ =\ \lambda\,\Bigl[\,-\,y_{r}\,\delta\,te^{-h_{r}t}\ +\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}e^{-h_{k}t}\,\Bigr]\ +\ O(\lambda^{2})\,,\qquad\lambda\to 0\,.

(2)

Since
\,te^{-h_{r}t}\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
,
the latter term in (
2
) is
O(\lambda^{2})
uniformly for all
t\in[0,+\infty)
.
Hence, the polynomial
p_{\lambda}-p
can be
approximated with precision
O(\lambda^{2})
by a polynomial
that consists of
n+1
exponents (the left-hand side of (
2
)).
Now we choose the coefficients of those polynomials so that
\|p_{\lambda}\|<1
and
F(p_{\lambda})>F(p)
.
Consider the polynomial
q(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}e^{-h_{k}t}\,+\,q_{r,1}te^{-h_{r}t}
,
that consists of
n+1
exponents (
h_{\,r}
has multiplicity
2
),
and that vanishes in
n
points
-\alpha=\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n-1}
, where
\alpha<0
, at all points
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
different from the roots
\mu_{k}

and, therefore,
|p(t)+q(t)|<1
, hence
\|p+q\|<1
.
Furthermore, the derivative
q^{(\ell)}(t)

changes its sign
n
times on the interval
[-\alpha,+\infty)
,
while
p^{(\ell)}(0)
does

n-1
times and the signs of those polynomials are different as
t\to+\infty
.
Therefore, they have the same sign at the point
t=-\alpha
.
Thus,
q^{(\ell)}(-\alpha)>0
. Choosing a small
\alpha
we can assume that
q^{(\ell)}(0)>0
, and so
F(q)>0
.

Now we choose the coefficients
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},\delta
so that the polynomials
in the right-hand side of (
2
) will coincide with
q
.
In this case
y_{k}=q_{k}
for all
k
and

-y_{r}\delta=q_{r,1}
. Thus,
\delta=-\frac{q_{r,1}}{q_{r}}
,
and this number is positive. Indeed, the polynomial

q
consists of
n+1
exponents and has
n
zeros, hence,
all its coefficients are nonzero and have alternating signs. Since
q_{r,1}
and
q_{r}

are two consecutive coefficients, we have
\frac{q_{r,1}}{q_{r}}<0
.
Substituting to (
2
) we obtain

p_{\lambda}(t)\ =\ p(t)\ +\ \lambda\,q(t)\ +\ O(\lambda^{2})\,,\qquad\lambda\to 0\,.

On the other hand,

\|p+q\|<1
, and consequently
\|p+\lambda q\|<1-c\lambda\,,\ \lambda\in(0,1]
. Therefore,
\|p_{\lambda}\|<1-c\lambda
for all positive
\lambda

small enough. Moreover, since

F(p+\lambda q)=F(p)+\lambda F(q)
with
F(q)>0
, it follows that
F(p_{\lambda})>F(p)+c_{1}\lambda

for all positive
\lambda
small enough, where
c_{1}>0
is a constant.
This contradicts the optimality of
p
.

\Box

5. Corollaries and special cases

Theorem
1
claims that the maximal value of the
\ell
th
derivative of a real quasipolynomial on the unit ball (in the monotone norm)
strictly increases in each component
h_{i}
. This, in particular, gives us the
largest value of the
\ell
th derivative over all polynomials when the vector

{\boldsymbol{h}}
fills a rectangle in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Corollary 2

If the parameter
h_{i}
belongs to the rectangle
\{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{++}\ |\ h_{i}\in(0,\alpha_{i}]\}
, then the maximal value of
\Phi_{\ell}({{\boldsymbol{h}}})
on the unit ball of an arbitrary monotone norm is achieved at a unique point, which is the vertex
{\boldsymbol{h}}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
.

Let us now apply the comparison theorem to some concrete norms.
We also call a monotone norm on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

shift-monotone
,
if
\bigl\|f(\cdot+a)\bigl|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\bigr\|\,\leq\,\|f(\cdot)\|

for every positive
a
.
All the
L_{p}
-norms possess this property.
For an arbitrary shift-monotone norm
\|\cdot\|
,
the maximal value of

\|p^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty}
under the constraint
\|p\|\leq 1
is equal to
|p^{(\ell)}(0)|
,
i.e., it is attained at zero. To prove this is suffices to note that this function is coercive
and hence attains its maximum at some point and then
shift that point to zero. Thus,

Corollary 3

Suppose that the monotone norm
\|\cdot\|
is also a shift-monotone;
then for every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the maximal value of
\|p^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty}
on the unit ball
p\in{\cal{RP}},\ \|p\|\leq 1
, is an increasing function in
{\boldsymbol{h}}\,
.

As we know, the
L_{\infty}
-norm of a polynomial over an arbitrary Chebyshev system
takes the largest value
p^{(\ell)}(0)

on the ball
\|p\|_{\infty}\leq 1
at a unique polynomial, which possesses an alternanse and is called the
Chebyshev polynomial for that system. In our case this is the Chebyshev polynomial
on the system
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
that has an alternance on
[0,+\infty)
of
n
points including
the point
0
. We denote this polynomial by

T_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
. For every
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{++}
, the polynomial

T_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
can be found numerically by the Remez algorithm
[
33
]
, which makes it possible to compute
\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,=\,m_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
.
We are interested in the uniform sharp constant
\,m_{\,\ell,n}
.

6. The Markov-Bernstein inequality for exponential polynomials

For an arbitrary vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{C}}_{++}
and for every
\ell\geq 0
, the value

\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
of the problem (
1
) for the functional
F(p)=-p^{(\ell)}(0)
, is also the sharp constant in the Markov-Bernstein inequality for exponential polynomials
over the system
E_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
:

\bigl\|p^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_{\infty}\quad\leq\quad\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,\bigl\|p\bigr\|\,.

(3)

Let us note that each shift-monotone norm
\|\cdot\|

on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
has its own constant
\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
,
therefore, the prober notation for it is
\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}},\,\|\cdot\|)
.
We, however,
use the previous short notation. If
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is a real vector
with components from the unit cube
{\cal{I}}_{n}\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ 0<h_{i}\leq 1,\,i=1,\ldots,n\}
, then the
maximal value of the constant
\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{h}})
, according to Corollary
2
,
is attained if
{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{e}}=(1,\ldots,1)
.
Therefore, the maximum is always achieved at the polynomial
\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
, i.e.,
at a function of the form

p(t)\,=\,e^{-t}q(t)
, where
q(t)
is an arbitrary
algebraic polynomial of degree
n-1
. In particular,
for
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, the extremal polynomial is
p(t)\,=\,e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)
,
where
R_{n-1}
is a Chebyshev algebraic polynomial of degree
n-1
with the Laguerre
weight
e^{-t}
, for which
the function
e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)
possesses
n
points of alternance on
the positive half-line
[
11
,
36
]
.
Let us collect all these facts in the following theorem:

Theorem 2

For an arbitrary shift-invariant norm

\|\cdot\|
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, arbitrary
\ell\geq 1
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\cal{I}}_{n}
, we have

\bigl\|p^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_{\infty}\quad\leq\quad\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{e}})\,\bigl\|p\bigr\|\ ,\qquad p\in{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}\,.

(4)

This inequality becomes an equality at a unique, up to multiplication by a constant,
polynomial
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
. For
\|\cdot\|=\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, one has
\Phi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{e}})=m_{\ell,n}
, and a unique extremal polynomial
is the exponential Chebyshev polynomial
T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}=e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)
,
where
R_{n-1}
is an algebraic Chebyshev polynomial of degree
n-1

with the Laguerre weight
e^{-t}
.
Moreover,
m_{\ell,n}\,=\,|T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}^{(\ell)}(0)|
.

Thus,
m_{\ell,n}
is equal to the
the
\ell
-th derivative of the polynomial
e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)
at zero.
For every
n
, this polynomial can be explicitly found, hence, the value
m_{\ell,n}

is efficiently computable. However, a natural question arises
how fast does it grow in
n
and in
\ell
.
This problem is reduced to the estimation of the
\ell
-th derivative of the polynomial

R_{n-1}
at zero. The first upper bound was presented in 1964 by
Sege
[
36
]
, who proved that
|R_{n-1}^{\prime}(0)|\leq Cn
. Then this result was successively
improved in
[
11
,
14
,
25
]
. Asymptotically sharp estimates
for all
\ell
have been obtained by V.Sklyarov
[
35
]
:

Theorem A
(V.Sklyarov, 2009).
For arbitrary natural
n\geq 2
, let
R_{n-1}
denote the algebraic
Chebyshev polynomial of degree
n-1
with the Laguerre weight
e^{-t}
.
Then for each
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\frac{8^{\ell}(n-1)\,!\,{\ell}\,!}{(n-1-{\ell})\,!\,(2{\ell})\,!}\Bigl(1-\frac{{\ell}}{2(n-1)}\Bigr)\quad\leq\quad\Bigl|\,R_{n-1}^{(\ell)}(0)\,\Bigr|\quad\leq\quad\frac{8^{{\ell}}(n-1)\,!\,{\ell}\,!}{(n-1-{\ell})\,!\,(2{\ell})\,!}.

(5)

Thus, the ratio between the upper and the lower bound (
5
) tends to one as
n\to\infty
when
\ell
is fixed.
To estimate
m_{\,\ell,n}
one needs to evaluate the derivatives of the polynomial
T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}
.
They can easily be obtained with the derivatives of the polynomial
R_{n-1}
:

\Bigl[\,e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)\,\Bigr]^{(\ell)}\quad=\quad e^{-t}\,\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\ (-1)^{\ell-j}\,{k\choose j}\ R_{n-1}^{(j)}(t)\,.

Taking into account that the signs of
R_{n-1}^{(j)}(0)
alternate in
j
, we get

m_{\,\ell,n}\quad=\quad 1\ +\ \sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\,\,{\ell\choose j}\ \,\bigl|\,R_{n-1}^{(\ell)}(0)\,\bigr|.

(6)

Now invoke (
5
) and obtain after elementary simplifications:

\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\,\left(1-\frac{{j}}{2(n-1)}\right)\frac{8^{j}\ {n-1\choose j}\ {\ell\choose j}}{{2j\choose j}}\quad\leq\quad m_{\ell,n}\quad\leq\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\,\frac{8^{j}\ {n-1\choose j}\ {\ell\choose j}}{{2j\choose j}}\,

(7)

(all the terms with
j\geq d
are zeros;
{n\choose 0}=1
for each
n\geq 0
).
We have not succeeded in simplifying these expressions. Combining with
Theorem
2
, we obtain

Theorem 3

For all
\ell,n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,n\geq 2,
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\cal{I}}_{n}
, we have

\max_{p\in{\cal{RP}}({\boldsymbol{h}})}\ \bigl\|p^{(\ell)}\bigr\|_{\infty}\quad\leq\quad m_{\,\ell,n}\,\bigl\|p\bigr\|_{\infty}\,,

(8)

where the equality is attained at the polynomial
T_{{\boldsymbol{e}}}=e^{-t}R_{n-1}
, and

\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\,\left(1-\frac{{j}}{2(n-1)}\right)\frac{8^{j}\ {n-1\choose j}\ {\ell\choose j}}{{2j\choose j}}\quad\leq\quad m_{\,\ell,n}\quad\leq\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\,\,\frac{8^{j}{n-1\choose j}{\ell\choose j}}{{2j\choose j}}

(9)

(all the terms with
j\geq n
are zeros).

Corollary 4

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
,
if the half-interval
(0,1]
for
h_{i}
is replaced by
(0,\alpha]
,
then the estimate (
9
) is replaced by
\alpha^{\ell}
.
This inequality is asymptotically sharp with the extremal polynomials
e^{-t/\alpha}R_{n}\bigl(t/\alpha\bigr)
.

Thus, for every
\ell,n
, we have an upper bound for the sharp constant
m_{\ell,n}

in the Markov-Bernstein inequality for real exponential polynomials.
In the next section we deal with applications to dynamical systems, where
we need only the case
\ell=2
, for which the inequality (
9
)
gets the following form:

m_{2,n}\ \leq\ \frac{16\,n^{\,2}\,-\,24\,n\,+\,11}{3}\,.

(10)

This estimate is sharp
asymptotically as
n\to\infty
. For small

, it can be computed precisely.

7. The stability of linear switching systems

Linear switching system is a linear ODE
\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\,=\,A(t){\boldsymbol{x}}(t)

on the vector-function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\,:\,{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\,\to\,{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with the initial
condition
{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)={\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
with a matrix function
A(t)

taking values from a given compact set
{\cal{A}}
called

control set
. The
control function
,
or the

switching law
is an arbitrary measurable function

A:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\cal{A}}
. Linear switching systems naturally appear
in problems of robotics, electronic engineering, mechanics, planning etc.
[
21
]
.
One of the main issues is to find or estimate the fastest possible growth of trajectories
of the stability of the system. The
Lyapunov exponent

\sigma({\cal{A}})
of the system is the infimum of the numbers
\alpha
for which

\|{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\|\,\leq\,C\,e^{\alpha t}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|,\ \ t\in[0,+\infty)
.
We shall identify the linear switching system with the corresponding
matrix family (control set)
{\cal{A}}
. The Lyapunov exponent
does not change after replacing the control set by its convex hull.
Therefore, without lost of generality we assume that
{\cal{A}}
is convex.
Moreover, it can also be assumed that the system is irreducible, i.e., its matrices
do not share common invariant nontrivial subspaces.

A system is called
asymptotically stable
(in short, “stable”), if all its trajectories tend to zero.
If
\sigma<0
, then the system is obviously stable. The converse is
less obvious: the stability implies that
\sigma<0

[
27
]
.
For one-element control sets
{\cal{A}}=\{A\}
, the stability is equivalent to that
the matrix
A
is Hurwitz, i.e., all its
eigenvalues have negative real parts.
If
{\cal{A}}
contains more than one matrix, the stability problem becomes
much harder. It is well known
[
26
,
29
]
that the stability
is equivalent to the existence of

Lyapunov norm
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, i.e., a norm for which there exists
\delta>0

such that

\|{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\|\,\leq\,e^{-\,\delta t}\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|\,,\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
for all
trajectories
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
. N.Barabanov
[
1
]
showed that
for an arbitrary convex control set
{\cal{A}}
, there exists an

invariant Lyapunov norm
such that

\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\|\,\leq\,e^{\,\sigma\,t}\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|
for every
trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
, and for every point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
there exists a trajectory

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
such that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(0)\,=\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
and
\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\|\,=\,e^{\,\sigma\,t}\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|
.

The problem of computation of the Lyapunov exponent of the system is equivalent to the solution of the
stability problem. Indeed, for an arbitrary
s
, the inequality
\sigma({\cal{A}})<s

is equivalent to that
\sigma({\cal{A}}-sI)<0
, i.e., is equivalent to the stability of the
system
{\cal{A}}-sI
, where
I
is a unit matrix and
{\cal{A}}-sI\,=\,\{A-sI\ |\ A\in{\cal{A}}\}
. Consequently, if we are able to solve the stability problem for every matrix
family, then we can compare the Lyapunov exponent with an arbitrary number,
which allows us to compute the Lyapunov exponent merely by the double division.
Unfortunately, the general stability problem is quite
difficult; the existing methods either work in low dimensions
(as a rule, at most
4-5
), or give too rough estimates.
For example the method of common quadratic Lyapunov function (CQLF)
gives only necessary conditions for stability,
which is far from being necessary
[
21
,
22
]
.
Other methods, for example, by piecewise-linear or by piecewise-quadratic
Lyapunov functions
[
3
,
4
]
, the extremal polytope method
[
16
]
, etc.,
are sharper but, as a rule, are realizable
only in small dimensions. The discretization method considered below is well known and
looks quite prospective.

7.1. Discrete vs continuous

The disctetisation method reduces the stability of the linear switching system
to the stability of a suitable discrete-time system:

{\boldsymbol{x}}(k+1)=B(k){\boldsymbol{x}}(k),\ B(k)\in{\cal{B}},\ k\geq 0
, where
{\cal{B}}

is a compact matrix set, see
[
2
,
10
,
30
,
31
]
.
The following key statement has been established in
[
27
]
:

Fact 1.

If for some
\tau_{0}>0
, the discrete
system generated by the family of matrices
{\cal{B}}=I+\tau_{0}{\cal{A}}
is stable,
then it is stable for all

\tau<\tau_{0}
, and, moreover, so is the continuous time system
{\cal{A}}
.

Since

e^{\tau A}\,=\,I+\tau A+O(\tau^{2})
, the matrix
B=I+\tau A

gives a linear approximation of
e^{\tau A}
.
In fact, we approximate every trajectory of the system
{\cal{A}}

by the Euler method with the step size
\tau
and analyse the
corresponding piecewise-linear trajectories. If the step size
tends to zero, then, obviously, the stability of piecewise-linear approximation
implies the stability of the original system.
Fact 1, however, asserts more: if the piecewise-linear approximation is stable
for some step size
\tau_{0}
, not necessarily small,
then it is stable for every step size and the system
{\cal{A}}
is stable.

Thus, the Euler method with the step
\tau
not only approximates the trajectories of the
system, but also gives sufficient conditions for stability.
The main issue is to what extend that condition is necessary? The fundamental
problem is formulated as follows:

Problem 1.

For a given continuous-time system
{\cal{A}}
and for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, find
\tau_{0}>0
such that the inequality
\sigma({\cal{A}})<-\varepsilon

implies instability of the discrete-time system

{\cal{B}}=I+\tau{\cal{A}}
for all
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
.

In other words, how small the discretisation step
\tau
should be to guarantee that
the stability of the obtained discrete system implies the stability of the
continuous-time system with precision
\varepsilon
?
The stability of the discrete system
{\cal{B}}
is decided in terms of

the joint spectral radius
:

\rho({\cal{B}})\quad=\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}\ \max_{B(j)\in{\cal{B}},j=1,\ldots,k}\ \bigl\|B(k)\cdots B(1)\bigr\|^{1/k}\,.

A discrete-time system
{\cal{B}}
is stable precisely when
\rho({\cal{B}})<1
, see.
[
26
]
.
Thus, if for some
\tau_{0}
, we have
\rho(I+\tau_{0}{\cal{A}})<1
, then

\rho(I+\tau{\cal{A}})<1
for all
\tau<\tau_{0}
and, moreover,
\sigma({\cal{A}})<0
.
The inequality
\rho({\cal{B}})<1
is equivalent to
the existence of the norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
in which
\|B\|<1,\,B\in{\cal{B}}
,
i.e., the operators
B\subset{\cal{B}}
all contractions in the corresponding norm.
Considering the unit ball in that norm gives an equivalent condition:
there exists a symmetrized convex bodies
G\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for which

B\,(G)\,\subset\,{\rm int}\,G
for all
B\in{\cal{B}}
.
For a one-matrix family
{\cal{B}}
, the joint spectral radius becomes
the usual spectral radius, i.e., the largest modulus of eigenvalues of the matrix.
If
{\cal{B}}
contains at least two matrices, then the computation of its joint spectral radius
becomes an NP-hard problem
[
5
]
. Nevertheless, recently
several efficient methods of computation of
\rho({\cal{B}})

for generic matrix families were presented. They work well in dimensions
n\leq 25
,
and for nonnegative families, the dimension can be increased to several thousands
[
17
,
23
,
24
]
. Hence, the stability problem for discrete-time systems can be efficiently solved
by computing the joint spectral radius. The solution of the Problem 1 makes it possible
to extend this method to the continuous-time system.
To this end, the discretisation step
\tau
should not be too small.
Otherwise, all the matrices of the family
I+\tau{\cal{A}}

will be close to the identity matrix, in which case all known methods of the
joint spectral radius computation suffer.

Definition 2

For a given compact family of matrices,
{\cal{A}}

and for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
, let

S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}+\infty&\ ,&\sigma({\cal{A}})\,\geq\,-\varepsilon\,,\\
\sup\,\bigl\{\tau>0\ |\ \rho(I+\tau{\cal{A}})<1\,\bigr\}&\ ,&\sigma({\cal{A}})\,<\,-\varepsilon\,.\end{array}\right.

(11)

We use the simplified notation
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)=S
.
Thus, if
\sigma({\cal{A}})<-\varepsilon
, then

\rho(I+\tau{\cal{A}})<1
for all
\tau\,<\,S
.
The value
S
has the following meaning: if we do not know the value of
\sigma({\cal{A}})
,
but have an upper bound for
S
, then we choose an arbitrary value

\tau<S
and compute the joint spectral radius of the operator
\rho(I+\tau{\cal{A}})
.
If it is larger than or equal to one, then
\sigma\geq-\varepsilon
;
otherwise
\sigma<0
. Denote also

S_{r}(\varepsilon)\ =\ \inf\,\Bigl\{\ S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)\ \Bigl|\ \max_{A\in{\cal{A}}}\rho(A)\,\leq\,r\ \Bigr\}\,.

Thus,
S_{r}(\varepsilon)
is a lower bound for
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)
which is
uniform over all families
{\cal{A}}
of
n
matrices with the spectral radii not exceeding
r
.
In Theorems
1
and
5
we express
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)
and
S_{r}(\varepsilon)

by the values
M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})
and

M_{2,n}
for
{\boldsymbol{h}}\,=\,-\,{\rm sp}\,(A),\ A\in{\cal{A}}
.

Now our plan is to estimate the value
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)
from below by applying the Lyapunov norm
of the family
{\cal{A}}
(Propositions
1
and
2
).
After this the problem will be reduced to the solution of extremal problem (
12
),
which is equivalent to finding the sharp constant in the Markow-Bernstein type
inequality for exponential polynomials
(Proposition
3
). Then deriving a uniform bound for
S_{r}(\varepsilon)

will be equivalent to the minimization of the value
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)

over all families of matrices
{\cal{A}}

with spectral radii at most
r
. This will be done in Theorem
5
.

7.2. Reduction to an extremal problem

We are going to estimate the discretization step
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)
from below
by solving a special extremal problem.
Let
{\boldsymbol{h}}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and

{\cal{RP}}_{\,{\boldsymbol{h}}}
be the corresponding space of real quasipolynomials.
For given
\varepsilon>0
, we denote by
s\,({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)

the value of the following problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1-p(0)}{p^{\prime}(0)-\varepsilon p(0)}\ \to\ \min,\\
\\
p\in{{\cal{RP}}}_{h}\\
\|p\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\leq 1\,,\ p\,^{\prime}(0)\,>\,\varepsilon p(0)\,.\end{array}\right.

(12)

The geometric sense of this problem becomes obvious by Proposition
1
.
To formulate it we need to introduce some further notation. For a Hutwitz
matrix
B
and for a vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we set
G_{B}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\rm co}\,\{\,\pm\,e^{\,t\,B}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,,\ t\in[0,+\infty)\}
.
Thus,
G_{B}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the convex hull
of the curve

\{\,e^{\,t\,B}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,,\ t\in[0,+\infty)\,\bigr\}\,\bigcup\,\{\,-\,e^{\,t\,B}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,,\ t\in[0,+\infty)\,\bigr\}
, which connects the points

{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\,-{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Proposition 1

Let
B
be a Hurwitz matrix,
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is an arbitrary point and
\varepsilon>0
; then the largest number
\tau
for which
{\boldsymbol{x}}+\tau\,(B\,-\,\varepsilon I)\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\in\,G_{B}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is equal to

s\,({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)
, where
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}\,=\,-{\rm sp}(B)\
(Fig. 1)

Figure 1:
The largest number
\tau
for which
{\boldsymbol{x}}+\tau\,(B\,-\,\varepsilon I)\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\in\,G({\boldsymbol{x}})

Proof.
Denote
G_{B}({\boldsymbol{x}})=G
. Assume that
B
possesses

n
different eigenvalues; the general case then follows by the limit passage.
By the Caratheodory theorem, an arbitrary point of the set
G

is a convex combination of at most

n+1
its extreme points of the form, i.e., points of the form

\pm\,e^{\,t\,B}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,,\ t\geq 0
.
Hence, there are
n+1
nonnegative numbers

\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
and numbers
\{q_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}

such that

{\boldsymbol{x}}\ +\ \tau\,(B\,-\,\varepsilon I)\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\quad=\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,q_{k}\,e^{\,t_{k}\,B}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}\,,\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}|q_{k}|=1\,.

(13)

In the basis of eigenvectors of
B
,
we obtain the diagonal matrix

\tilde{B}\,=\,{\rm diag}\,(-h_{1},\ldots,-h_{n})\,
and the point

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,\bigl(\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Moreover,

\,{\rm Re\,}\,h_{k}>0\,,k=1,\ldots,n
.
We assume that all coordinates of the vector
\tilde{x}_{k}
are nonzero;
the general case will then follow by the limit passage.
Equality (
13
) has the following form in the new basis:

\Bigl(\,I\ -\ \tau\,{\rm diag}\,\bigl(h_{1}+\varepsilon,\ldots,h_{n}+\varepsilon)\ \Bigr)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,q_{k}\,e^{\,-t_{k}\,h_{j}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\,.

For each component
\tilde{x}_{j}
, we obtain:

\bigl(\,1\,-\,\tau\,(h_{j}\,+\,\varepsilon\,)\,\bigr)\,\tilde{x}_{j}\,\ =\ \sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,q_{k}\,e^{\,-t_{k}\,h_{j}}\tilde{x}_{j}\,,

then we divide by
\tilde{x}_{j}
:

1\,-\,\tau\,(h_{j}\,+\,\varepsilon\,)\quad=\quad\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,q_{k}\,e^{\,-t_{k}\,h_{j}}\,\quad j=1,\ldots,n\,.

(14)

This equation does not contain coordinates of the vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
The maximal
\tau
for which there are coefficients
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
and

\{q_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
such that (
14
) holds, must be the same for all

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq 0
. We set
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{e}}

(the vector of ones), and denote by
\tilde{G}

the set of all values of the sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,q_{k}\,e^{\,-t_{k}\tilde{B}}{\boldsymbol{e}}

for all suitable
\{t_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
and
\{q_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n+1}
.
This is a convex body in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
over the field of real numbers, i.e.,
a convex body in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. By the convex separation theorem, for an arbitrary
\delta>0
, the point
{\boldsymbol{e}}\ +\ \tau\,(\tilde{B}\,-\,\delta I)\,{\boldsymbol{e}}
does not belong to
\tilde{G}
precisely when it can be strictly separated from it with a linear functional, i.e., when there exists a nonzero vector

{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, for which

{\rm Re\,}\ \Bigl(\,{\boldsymbol{e}}\,+\,\delta\,(\tilde{B}\,-\,\varepsilon I\,)\,{\boldsymbol{e}}\,,\,{\boldsymbol{z}}\Bigr)\quad>\quad\max_{t\geq 0}\ \left|\,{\rm Re\,}\left(\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\bar{z}_{j}\,e^{\,-t\,h_{j}}\,\right)\,\right|\,.

(15)

Denote by
\,q(t)\,=\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,\bar{z}_{j}e^{\,-\,t\,h_{j}}\in\cal P_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
a complex polynomial and by
p(t)\,=\,{\rm Re}\,q(t)
the corresponding real quasipolynomial. Then the right-hand side of the inequality (
15
) is equal to
\bigl\|\,p\,\bigr\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
.
Then we modify the left-hand side as follows:

\Bigl(\,{\boldsymbol{e}}\,+\,\delta(\tilde{B}\,-\,\varepsilon I)\,{\boldsymbol{e}}\,,\,{\boldsymbol{z}}\,\Bigr)\ =\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{z}_{j}\,+\,\delta\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-h_{j}-\varepsilon)\,\bar{z}_{j}\ =\ q(0)\,+\,\delta\,q\,^{\prime}(0)\,-\,\delta\,\varepsilon\,q(0)\,.

Thus, the left-hand side of (
15
) is equal to

p(0)+\delta\,p^{\prime}(0)-\delta\,\varepsilon\,p(0)\,,

and we arrive at the inequality

p(0)\,+\,\delta\,p\,^{\prime}(0)\,-\,\delta\,\varepsilon\,p(0)\quad>\quad\|p\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\,,

hence,

\delta\,\bigl(\,p\,^{\prime}(0)\,-\,\varepsilon\,p(0)\,\bigr)\quad>\quad\|p\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\,-\,p(0)\,.

The right-hand side is obviously nonnegative, consequently
p^{\prime}(0)-\varepsilon\,p(0)>0
.
Normalising the polynomial
p
so that
\|p\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}=1
, we obtain

\delta>\frac{1-p(0)}{p\,^{\prime}(0)-\varepsilon p(0)}
.Thus, the point
{{\boldsymbol{e}}\,+\,\delta\,(B\,-\,\varepsilon I)\,{\boldsymbol{e}}}
does not belong to the convex body

G_{B}({\boldsymbol{e}})
if and only if there exists a convex body
p\in{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}},\,\|p\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\leq 1
satisfying that inequality.

\Box

Remark 2

In fact we prove more: if
\tau<s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq 0
, then the point

{\boldsymbol{x}}+\tau\,(B\,-\,\varepsilon I)\,{\boldsymbol{x}}
lies inside
G_{B}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

The lower bound for the estimate
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)

is provided by the maximal possible value of
s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)

over all vectors

{\boldsymbol{h}}\,=\,-\,{\rm sp}\,(A)\,-\,\varepsilon\,{\boldsymbol{e}},\ A\in{\cal{A}}
.
We prove this in the following proposition by applying the invariant norm of the matrix family
{\cal{A}}
.

Proposition 2

For an arbitrary matrix family
{\cal{A}}
, we have

\,S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)\quad\geq\quad\min_{A\in{\cal{A}}}\,s\,({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)\,,\

(16)

\mbox{where}\ {\boldsymbol{h}}\ =\ -{\rm sp}(A)\,-\,\varepsilon\,{\boldsymbol{e}}\,.

Proof.
It is needed to show that if
\sigma({\cal{A}})<-\varepsilon
, then

\rho(I+\tau A)<1
, for all
\tau
which are smaller than the right-hand side of the inequality (
16
).
Since
\sigma({\cal{A}})<-\varepsilon
,
It follows that there exists a Lyapunov norm in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
for which
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)\|\,<\,e^{-\varepsilon t}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|,\ t>0,
for every trajectory
{\boldsymbol{x}}(\cdot)
.
This is true, in particular, for trajectories without switches, i.e.,
for the stationary control.
Thus, for an arbitrary matrix
A\in{\cal{A}}
, we have

\|e^{\,tA}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|\,<\,e^{-\varepsilon t}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|
, and therefore

\|e^{\,t\,(A+\varepsilon I)}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|\,<\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|
for all
t>0
.
Hence, for each point
{\boldsymbol{y}}
from the symmetrised convex hull of the set

\,\bigl\{\,e^{\,t\,(A+\varepsilon I)}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\ \bigl|\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\,\bigr\}
,
we have
\|{\boldsymbol{y}}\|\leq\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|
. Applying Proposition
1
to the matrix
B=A+\varepsilon I
and taking into account Remark
2
,
we conclude that the point

\,(I\,+\,\tau A){\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
belongs to the interior of the set
G_{A+\varepsilon I}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
,
and therefore,
\,\|(I\,+\,\tau A){\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|\,<\,\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\|
. Thus,
for each
A\in{\cal{A}}
, the operator norm of
I\,+\,\tau A

is strictly smaller than one. Consequently,
\rho(I+\tau{\cal{A}})<1
.

\Box

Remark 3

The value
s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)

of the problem (
12
) is inversely proportional to its parameters: for every

\lambda>0
, we have

s\bigl(\lambda{\boldsymbol{h}}\,,\,\lambda\varepsilon\bigr)\ =\ \lambda^{-1}\,s\bigl({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon\bigr)\,.

(17)

For the proof, it suffices to change the variable
u=\lambda t
and
observe that

{p_{t}}\,^{\prime}(0)=\lambda\,{p_{u}}^{\prime}(0)
.
Therefore, the computation or estimation of the value
s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)
is reduced to the case

{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Delta
, i.e.,
|h_{n}|\leq 1
.

It remains to compute
s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)
, i.e., to solve the problem (
12
). This can be done numerically applying the convex optimization tools, since the set of real quasipolynomials

q
satisfying all the constraints of the problem is convex and the
objective functions quasiconvex (all its level sets are convex).
Therefore, for a concrete vector

{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, the problem can be efficiently solved, for instance, by the gradient relaxation methods. We, however, need a general lower bound for the value
s\,({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)
.

Proposition 3

For each
\varepsilon>0
and
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\Delta
, we have

s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)\quad>\quad\frac{2\,\varepsilon}{M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,+\,2\,\varepsilon^{2}}

where
s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)
is the value of the problem (
12
).

Proof.

Let us first forget for a moment that
p
is a quasipolynomial,
we leave the only assumption that
\|p^{\prime\prime}\|\leq\ M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})\|p\|
.
For the sake of simplicity we also denote
M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,=\,m
.
Thus, we need to compute the minimum of the value
\frac{1-p(0)}{p^{\prime}(0)-\varepsilon p(0)}

under the assumptions
p\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}),\,\|p\|\leq 1
and
\|p^{\prime\prime}\|<m
.
Set
x=1-p(0)
, then the objective function in the problem (
12
)
gets the form
f(x)=\frac{x}{p^{\prime}(0)-\varepsilon(1-x)}
.
The numerator and the denominator are both positive, hence
the minimum of this fraction is achieved when
p^{\prime}(0)
is maximal.
Denote
p^{\prime}(0)=\alpha
.
Since
p^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq-m
, we have for each
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
:

p(t)\quad\geq\quad p(0)\,+\,p^{\prime}(0)t\,-\,\frac{mt^{2}}{2}\quad=\quad 1\,-\,x\,+\,\alpha t\,-\,\frac{mt^{2}}{2}\,.

On the other hand,
p(t)\leq 1
because
\|p\|_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\leq 1
.
Therefore, the maximum of the quadratic function

1\,-\,x\,+\,\alpha t\,-\,\frac{mt^{2}}{2}
over
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

does not exceed
1
. Computing the value at the vertex of the parabola, we obtain
\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2m}\,+\,1-x\leq 1
, and so

\alpha\leq\sqrt{2mx}
. Hence,

f(x)\ \geq\ \frac{x}{\sqrt{2mx}-\varepsilon(1-x)},.

The minimum of the left-hand side of this inequality under the positivity constraints
for the numerator and denominator is attained at the point
x_{\min}=\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{m}
,
therefore

f(x)\ \geq\ f(x_{\min})\ =\ \frac{2\varepsilon}{m+2\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

Thus, the minimum of the objective function
\frac{1-p(0)}{p^{\prime}(0)-\varepsilon p(0)}

is equal to
\frac{2\varepsilon}{m+2\varepsilon^{2}}
and is achieved at the polynomial
p
defined above. Since the quadratic function does not belong to
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
and
the unit ball in the space
{\cal{RP}}_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
is compact, it follows that the minimum of
the objective function is larger than
\frac{2\varepsilon}{m+2\varepsilon^{2}}
.

\Box

Combining Propositions
2
and
3
we obtain the estimate for the
length o the discretization interval
S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)
.

Theorem 4

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
r>\varepsilon
,
for every system of operators
{\cal{A}}
such that
\max_{A\in{\cal{A}}}\rho(A)\,\leq\,r
,
we have

S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)\quad\geq\quad\max_{A\in{\cal{A}}}\ \frac{2\,\varepsilon}{M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,+\,2\,\varepsilon^{2}}\ ,

(18)

where
{\boldsymbol{h}}=\,-\,{\rm sp}\,(A)\,-\,\varepsilon\,{\boldsymbol{e}}
.

The estimate (
18
) is computed individually for all systems of matrices
{\cal{A}}
.
A uniform estimate
S_{r}(\varepsilon)
over all families of matrices is given by the following

Theorem 5

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
r>\varepsilon
,
for every system of operators
{\cal{A}}
such that
\max_{A\in{\cal{A}}}\rho(A)\,\leq\,r
,
we have

S_{r}(\varepsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{2\,\varepsilon}{r^{2}M_{2,n}}

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Applying Propositions
1
and
2
we conclude that the value

S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)
is not smaller than the minimal value
s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)

over all vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}=-{\rm sp}(A)-\varepsilon{\boldsymbol{e}},\ A\in{\cal{A}}
.
Proposition
3
yields

S_{{\cal{A}}}(\varepsilon)\quad\geq\quad\min_{{\boldsymbol{h}}=-{\rm sp}(A)-\varepsilon{\boldsymbol{e}},\ A\in{\cal{A}}}\quad\frac{2\,\varepsilon}{M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})\,+\,2\,\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

On the other hand

s({\boldsymbol{h}},\varepsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{2\,\varepsilon}{(\rho(A)-\varepsilon)M_{2,n}\,+\,\frac{2\varepsilon^{2}}{\rho(A)-\varepsilon}}\,.

If
\rho(A)\leq r
for all
A\in{\cal{A}}
, then

S_{r}(\varepsilon)\ \geq\ \frac{2\,\varepsilon}{(r-\varepsilon)^{2}M_{2,n}\,+\,2\,\varepsilon^{2}}

Since
M_{2,n}>2
and
r\geq\varepsilon
,
we see that the denominator of that fraction is at least

r^{\,2}M_{2,n}
, which completes the proof.

\Box

Theorems
4
and
5
show that
the lower bound for the discretization step is linear in
\varepsilon
,
which is much better than one might expect. As a rule, such estimates are exponential in
\varepsilon
and can hardly be applied in dimensions bigger that three.
The estimate provided by Theorem
5
depends on the dimension
only in the coefficient of the linear function. This makes it applicable even in relatively high dimensions. However, there is one difficulty on this way: we cannot compute
M_{2,n}
. Only the families
{\cal{A}}
that consist of matrices with real eigenvalues the constant
M_{2,n}
in Theorem
5
becomes efficiently computable because it can be replaced by
m_{2,n}

(Theorem
2
).

Corollary 5

If all the matrices of the family
{\cal{A}}
have real spectra, then the value
M_{2,n}

in Theorem
5
is replaced by
m_{2,n}
and
the value
M_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})
in Theorem
4
is replaced by
m_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})
.

The value
m_{2}({\boldsymbol{h}})
can be found with arbitrary precision by applying the Remez algorithm.
It is attained at the corresponding Chebyshev polynomial
T_{{\boldsymbol{h}}}
.
The value
m_{2,n}
, as it follows from Theorem
2
, is attained at the polynomial
e^{-t}R_{n-1}(t)
and can be efficiently computed as well. It can be estimated by
inequality (
9
) and, for small dimensions, can be evaluated precisely by
computing the Chebyshev polynomial
R_{n-1}

with the Laguerre weight. For small dimensions, the results are presented in Table
1
. We see that
m_{2,n}
grows in
n
not very fast, therefore, the estimate
from Theorem
5
, is applicable even for relatively high dimensions
(few dozens).

Certainly, the real spectrum case is rather exceptional.
In the general case one needs to compute or at east to estimate the value of
M_{2,n}

from above. We are not aware of any method of its computation with arbitrary precision.
All known estimates are rough and can hardly be applicable. If Conjecture
1
is true, then
M_{2,n}=m_{2,n}
, and the problem will be solved.
Otherwise, we have an open problem of finding satisfactory
upper bounds for
M_{2,n}
.
