Religious Competition, Culture and Domestic Violence: Evidence from Colombia
Hector Galindo-Silva, Guy Tchuente

Abstract
This paper studies how religious competition, as measured by the emergence of religious organizations with innovative worship styles and cultural practices, impacts domestic violence. Using data from Colombia, the study estimates a two-way fixed-effects model and reveals that the establishment of the first non-Catholic church in a predominantly Catholic municipality leads to a significant decrease in reported cases of domestic violence. This effect persists in the long run, indicating that religious competition introduces values and practices that discourage domestic violence, such as household stability and reduced male dominance. Additionally, the effect is more pronounced in municipalities with less clustered social networks, suggesting the diffusion of these values and practices through social connections. This research contributes to the understanding of how culture influences domestic violence, emphasizing the role of religious competition as a catalyst for cultural change. Keywords: Marketplace for Religion, Domestic violence. JEL classification : H41, J13, O17.

This paper seeks to contribute to the understanding of the interplay between domestic violence and culture by examining the consequences of a significant phenomenon in the recent history of several countries: the profound transformation of their religious landscape.
7
7

7

In this transformation, established churches encounter competition from other religious organizations that often introduce new cultural practices. If these practices are associated with a decrease in domestic violence and if they spread and gain acceptance among the population, the phenomenon of religious competition holds the potential to substantially reduce this critical form of violence.

This study focuses on Colombia, a deeply religious country which in the last few decades has experienced a significant increase in religious competition between the Catholic Church, which held a near-monopoly on religion for four centuries, and new evangelical Pentecostal churches. To measure the establishment of new religious organizations in areas where traditional religious entities have historically held a monopoly, we use data on the instalment of a first non-Catholic church in a typically deeply Catholic Colombian municipality. Then, we study how these events of increased religious competition impact domestic violence.

To estimate this impact, we employ a two-way fixed-effects model that incorporates both geographical (municipality) and temporal (year) dimensions. We use (linear) dynamic and static specifications. In the dynamic specifications, our central assumption for identification is that “control" municipalities, referring to those where no non-Catholic church has been established yet, as well as those where such churches were established a long time ago, serve as valid reference points to measure the evolving patterns of domestic violence in adopting municipalities. This comparison allows us to estimate what the dynamics of domestic violence would have been in these adopting municipalities if they had never experienced the arrival of a non-Catholic church. Under a similar assumption, we estimate the static versions of the two-way fixed-effect model using a difference-in-differences estimator. We interpret these estimates as capturing the long-term effects of religious competition on domestic violence.

Our analysis shows that the establishment of a first non-Catholic church in a municipality leads to a reduction in domestic violence incidents. Specifically, during the period that followed the first non-Catholic church establishment, there is a decrease of about 14 reported cases of domestic violence per 100,000 inhabitant and per year. Furthermore, this effect endures over the long term, resulting in a sustained decrease of approximately 14 reported cases of domestic violence per 100,000 inhabitants. These findings are robust to the use of alternative datasets, econometric specifications and estimators (such as
de Chaisemartin and
DHaultfoeuille 2020
,
Sun and Abraham 2021
and
Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021
’ heterogeneity-robust DID estimators).

We explain the above findings by proposing the following rationale. When a non-Catholic church is established for the first time in a Colombian municipality, it introduces new styles of worship, notably those with a ‘Pentecostal-Charismatic’ nature, which resonate effectively with the local population. Amidst religious competition for followers and due to the greater efficiency of these worship styles, they are adopted by other religious organizations, notably the Catholic Church. Importantly, these worship styles are associated with values and norms that discourage domestic violence, such as household stability, reduced alcohol consumption, sexual restraint, and decreased male dominance. The consequence of this phenomenon is a substantial and widespread reduction in domestic violence in the areas where it occurs.

This mechanism aligns with a substantial body of existing anecdotal evidence derived from numerous interviews with members of non-Catholic churches, as well as reports in newspapers and official statements from the Catholic Church. Furthermore, this rationale is consistent with additional quantitative evidence grounded in established models of innovation diffusion within social networks. These models, as exemplified by
Morris 2000
and particularly by
Acemoglu et al. 2011
, underscore the pivotal role of network structure in the diffusion of innovations. Specifically, they show that in networks with less clustering and/or cohesion, and with longer connections between sub-communities, the diffusion of innovations is faster and more extensive, particularly when these innovations originate within a select few sub-communities.

In the context of our study, ‘innovation’ denotes the introduction of new cultural practices which contribute to the reduction of domestic violence. These innovative practices were initially introduced in Colombian municipalities by the first non-Catholic church. If the spread of these practices through social networks accounts for the extent of the reduction in domestic violence, we expect a more significant reduction where this innovation spreads more, particularly in municipalities with greater population homogeneity, characterized by lower ethnic fragmentation, fewer civil society organizations and reduced religious diversity. Our empirical evidence supports this hypothesis, showing a more pronounced reduction in domestic violence in those municipalities

In the last part of the paper, we assess the plausibility of alternative explanations by examining the link between domestic violence and other types of violence, particularly those connected to the Colombian armed conflict. We also investigate whether there is a plausible economic phenomenon coinciding with religious competition that may impact domestic violence. None of the available evidence appears to lend support to these alternative mechanisms.

As our data is confined to Colombia, we are cautious about making claims regarding external validity. Nevertheless, we believe that the mechanisms and evidence in this paper can be extended to other countries due to the global nature of domestic violence and the role of cultural factors in its occurrence. The results of this paper may be most applicable in areas with deeply religious populations that are also undergoing significant changes in the religious landscape, such as Brazil, Guatemala, Bolivia, Kenya, and the Philippines
(
Miller et al. 2013
, in this respect, see for instance)
.

In this context, our research identifies one such ‘shock’: the establishment of new religious organizations introducing novel and appealing worship styles in areas previously dominated by older religious organizations with less attractive worship styles. This represents both an institutional change, with the transformation of informal institutions in these areas, and a technological change, as the introduction of innovative and attractive worship styles can be seen as a form of technological innovation. Our paper delves into the effects of these changes on domestic violence, a culturally rooted and pertinent phenomenon. To our knowledge, no existing studies in the literature have examined the consequences of this type of ‘shock’ on this specific social outcome.

This suggests that competition, particularly among religious organizations, plays a pivotal role in the dynamics of cultural change, which we consider to be an additional contribution of this paper to the field of cultural economics.

Background

In this section, we provide a brief description of the Colombian context, focusing on the evolution of the religious landscape and domestic violence.

2.1
Religious Landscape

In recent decades, Colombia has undergone a significant transformation in its religious composition, departing from the long-standing Catholic dominance that had been upheld for four centuries through state-imposed barriers to alternative religious affiliations. During the period from 1950 to 1970, the proportion of Colombians identifying as Catholic remained relatively stable, fluctuating between 91% and 95%. However, by 2014, this figure had declined to 79%
(see
Pew Research Center 2014
, p. 27)
. This decline in Catholic adherence was accompanied by a noteworthy increase in the prevalence of Protestantism. In 2014, while Catholicism experienced a net decrease of 13 percentage points, Protestantism witnessed an 8-percentage-point rise.

8

8

Data from 2019 and 2021, as reported by Colombia’s
National Administrative Department of Statistics
(DANE), is consistent with these figures: around 78.4% of the Colombian population identifies as Catholic, while approximately 10.1% identifies as Protestant (including Evangelical, Pentecostal, and Traditional Protestant non-evangelical denominations)
(
DANE 2021
, see)
.
This pattern of religious change, albeit with slight temporal variations, has been observed consistently in other countries (see
Pew Research Center 2014
and
Somma et al. 2017
for evidence of this phenomenon in Latin America countries).

Pentecostalism has been the fastest-growing Protestant denomination in Colombia, with at least 56% of Colombian Protestants identifying as Pentecostal in 2014
(see
Pew Research Center 2014
, p. 62)
.
9
9

9

These figures are also consistent with what was reported by the DANE for the years 2019 and 2021: among those who identify as non-Catholic Christians, approximately 58.4% specifically identify as Evangelical or Pentecostal
(
DANE 2021
, see)
.
Several factors contributing to Pentecostalism’s success in Colombia include urbanization, the need for hope amid urban challenges, perceived shortcomings of the Catholic Church, and the efficiency of Pentecostal organizations in addressing these needs
(

;

, see)

2.2
Domestic Violence

The significant transformation of religious identities in Colombia in recent decades has coincided with significant fluctuations in various societal indicators, some of which may be directly associated with this phenomenon. In this study, we propose that one such indicator is domestic violence.

Domestic violence, defined as any form of verbal, psychological, physical, or other abuse inflicted by a family member on another family member, has exhibited notable variations in recent decades in Colombia, with a slight overall trend toward reduction. Figure

When considering the factors influencing domestic violence, previous studies in Colombia have identified factors such as poverty, the educational levels of household members, the number of children, the presence of women in the household, the presence of young mothers, and women’s participation in the labor force

The notion that cultural factors may be contributory factors in domestic violence has also been a recurring theme in the discourse and initiatives of social and political entities in Colombia. Non-governmental actors, particularly religious organizations like the Catholic Church and non-Catholic Christian churches, have also expressed concerns about fostering cultural change in households to reduce domestic violence. Data

Our primary data focuses on the establishment of non-Catholic churches in Colombian municipalities. As mentioned in the introduction, this is the main measure proposed in this paper to assess the presence of a first non-Catholic Christian religious organization in a municipality.

Given the deeply Catholic historical context of Colombia, we interpret this establishment as a measure of the existence of religious competition. The data on the establishment of these churches is obtained from the ‘‘Public Registry of Religious Organizations," provided by the Colombian Ministry of the Interior.
14

14

In Spanish, this registry is called the
Registro Público de Entidades Religiosas

https://www.mininterior.gov.co/registro-publico-de-entidades-religiosas/

This registry documents the exact timing and location at which a non-Catholic church attains legal ‘‘personhood." In Colombia, non-Catholic churches are required to apply for legal status, whereas the Catholic Church already possesses this status. The application process typically takes a minimum of 60 working days to receive a response from the government, and approval is the usual outcome. Gaining legal personhood status enables a church to engage in various activities, such as entering contracts (e.g., opening bank accounts, hiring employees, compensating organizational leaders, purchasing properties, and qualifying for tax incentives). It also grants churches the ability to collect contributions from their members, as outlined in Law 133 of 1994. This dataset covers the years from 1995 to 2021 and includes information regarding the date of legal recognition and the primary municipality in which each church operates.
15
15

15

Upon obtaining legal recognition, a church is permitted to operate throughout the entire country under the law. It’s essential to note that this circumstance could introduce potential bias into our measurement of religious competition. This is because a church that becomes the first to acquire legal personhood in a specific municipality may not necessarily be the first church to commence its activities there. It’s possible that another church in the same municipality had already obtained its legal status elsewhere. Therefore, we should exercise caution when interpreting our estimates, as they may tend to underestimate the true level of religious competition or exhibit a bias toward lower competition. However, there is evidence showing that Colombian non-Catholic churches are very fragmented (see

In the period of our focus, from 2005 to 2019, on average, 28.4 new non-Catholic churches established annually (see Table

3.2
Empirical Strategy

To estimate the impact religious competition on domestic violence we use a linear two-way (unit and time) fixed effects (FE) estimator. Our main specification models a given outcome
v_{i,t}
(e.g. number of reported cases of domestic violence) in municipality
i
and year
t
as a function of the arrival of the first non-Catholic church:

v_{i,t}=\alpha_{i}+\beta_{t}+\sum_{\tau=-K}^{-2}\gamma_{\tau}D_{i,t}^{\tau}+\sum_{\tau=0}^{L}\gamma_{\tau}D_{i,t}^{\tau}+\epsilon_{i,t}

(1)

where
D_{i,t}^{\tau}
is an indicator for
\tau
years for municipality
i
’s treatment (which in our case is the establishment of the first non-Catholic church in municipality
i
, occurring at
\tau=0
).
21
21

21

Note that negative values of
\tau
indicate the arrival of the first non-Catholic church
|\tau|
years in the future, representing the “lead” effect denoted by
K
, while positive values indicate its arrival
\tau
years in the past, denoting the “lag” effect represented by
L
). In this dynamic specification, we allow the treatment to have different effects in the adoption year than in subsequent years. It is important to note that we do not include all possible relative time indicators in Eq. (
1
) due to multicollinearity, as discussed by
Laporte and Windmeijer 2005
,
Borusyak and Jaravel 2017
, and
Sun and Abraham 2021
. Regarding which relative time to exclude, we follow common practice and normalize relative to the period prior to treatment.

\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{t}
represent municipality and year fixed effects, respectively, while
\epsilon_{i,t}
is the error term.

The primary identifying assumption of the dynamic two-way fixed-effects model in Equation (
1
) is that changes in “control" municipalities (those that have not yet experienced the arrival of a non-Catholic church and those that experienced it
L
periods ago) provide a valid counterfactual to measure the dynamics of domestic violence that would have occurred in adopting municipalities if they had never experienced the arrival of a non-Catholic church. In other words, this assumption presupposes the absence of time-varying unobserved effects. It is crucial for interpreting our estimates as causal effects but may be invalid if, for example, the arrival of a non-Catholic church was most of the time preceded (and explained) by an unobserved event (which is not observed by the econometrician).

We also assume no reverse causality between domestic violence and the establishment of a municipality’s first non-Catholic church. This implies that it is the establishment of the non-Catholic church that leads to changes in domestic violence, not the other way around.

The main specification further assumes that the treatment effects are homogeneous, meaning that the change in domestic violence associated with the establishment of a non-Catholic church is the same for all municipalities, particularly for those treated at different times. If the assumption of homogeneous treatment effects is not satisfied, the estimates obtained using a dynamic two-way fixed-effects model may not be causally interpretable.

We offer a variety of evidence consistent with our assumptions. First, in Table
II
, we show that the level of domestic violence, as well as related factors (such as poverty, homicide rates, internally displaced populations, and political outcomes), prior to the arrival for the first time of a non-Catholic church in a municipality, are not correlated with the church’s arrival. This evidence indicates the absence of reverse causality and supports our parallel trends assumption. Second, we show that our findings are robust to the inclusion of various potentially confounding municipality-year variables. Third, we show that our estimations are robust to employing alternative heterogeneity-robust DID estimators (such as those proposed by
de Chaisemartin and
DHaultfoeuille 2020
,
Sun and Abraham 2021
, and
Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021
). All this evidence supports our interpretation of the estimates from Equation (
1
) as causal average treatment effects.

4.1
Effects of Religious Competition on Domestic Violence

The primary findings from our analysis are presented in Table

The results reveal that the establishment of the first non-Catholic church in a municipality causes a reduction in domestic violence. The findings presented in this section can be explained in multiple ways. In the following section, we provide what we believe to be the most likely explanation for these findings and present additional evidence to support this interpretation. Additionally, we explore alternative explanations, acknowledging their potential but ultimately deeming them less likely.

4.2
Mechanisms

Our preferred explanation for the observed reduction in domestic violence following the establishment of a first non-Catholic church in a municipality is grounded in the notion that this event introduces into the municipality a new and more efficient ‘cultural’ technology for the adoption of practices that lead to results that are widely recognized as beneficial in the community. Less domestic violence is one of these results. That technology is rapidly adopted by a significant number of households due to its enhanced efficiency. The net result is a substantial reduction in domestic violence.

To render this explanation more plausible, we offer now a range of evidence, both quantitative and anecdotal, that aligns with it.

The first set of evidence supports the assertion that major religious organizations in Colombia actively endorse specific practices aimed at improving interpersonal dynamics within households. As stated in Section
2
the main types of religious organizations in Colombia are the Catholic Church and non-Catholic churches (mostly Pentecostal denominations).

Concerning non-Catholic churches,
Beltrán 2013a

(see
Beltrán 2013a

“the new religious movements condemn all addictions, especially alcohol and cigarette consumption, and promote changes in consumption habits […] Additionally, thanks to their participatory dynamics, the new religious movements—especially the Pentecostal movement—promote female leadership, turning into spaces of empowerment for women."
(see
Beltrán 2013a
, p. 220)

To complement this evidence, we now introduce additional quantitative evidence that considerably strengthens the plausibility of our hypothesis. This evidence is based on the theoretical idea, from

This diffusion is faster and more extensive when there is less clustering and/or cohesion and more connections between sub-communities (assuming that innovations start in a few subcommunities).

Based on the previous idea but applying it to what we referred to earlier as ‘cultural innovations,’ particularly within the Colombian context of religious competition, we present the following empirical hypothesis: If there is a process of diffusion of cultural practices (which contribute to the reduction of domestic violence), initiated in Colombian municipalities by the arrival of new non-Catholic churches, and if the extent of this reduction is associated with the diffusion process, then we expect a more significant reduction in areas where these practices spread more extensively. In line with the theory we discussed earlier, these areas should correspond to municipalities with a higher degree of population homogeneity. We hypothesize that these municipalities have lower levels of ethnic diversity, fewer civil society organizations, and reduced religious diversity. In such municipalities, it is plausible that there are fewer sub-communities, fostering increased interactions among all individuals within the municipality. Consequently, in these municipalities, we expect a higher likelihood of widespread adoption of novel and appealing social practices, which should result in a more pronounced reduction in domestic violence.

Alternative explanations

There may be alternative explanations for the results presented in Section
4
. One potential alternative explanation is that domestic violence decreases due to changes in other, more significant factors often associated with this type of violence. One example of such factors is the internal armed conflict. If the establishment of a first non-Catholic church in a municipality leads to a decrease in armed violence, and if armed violence is related to domestic violence, then the decline in domestic violence might be viewed as a byproduct of a presumed reduction in armed violence. While this explanation may seem plausible, recent evidence from
Galindo-Silva and Tchuente 2021
contradicts it. According to their findings, the establishment of the first non-Catholic church in a municipality actually increases conflict-related violence rather than reducing it. Therefore, based on this result, we consider this alternative explanation to be unlikely.

Another alternative explanation, which shares similarities with our favoured one but is somewhat broader, suggests that the arrival of a first non-Catholic church in a municipality reduces domestic violence by altering people’s moral values, leading them to exhibit more moral behavior in general. While this hypothesis aligns with our preferred explanation, it’s worth noting that this effect may not be universally applicable. Evidence from
Galindo-Silva and Tchuente 2021
indicates that the establishment of a first non-Catholic church in a municipality does not influence homicide or robbery rates in those municipalities.

A third alternative explanation suggests that the establishment of the first non-Catholic church in a municipality coincided with a positive economic boost in that area, potentially leading to a decrease in domestic violence. It’s important to note that this alternative explanation can complement our primary hypothesis, as long as it doesn’t imply that the economic boost preceded the church’s arrival, as we have argued in Section
4
with supporting evidence. In this scenario, the cultural transformation initiated by the first non-Catholic church may have positively influenced household economic conditions, discouraging costly behaviors like alcohol consumption and promoting greater female participation in the job market. This economic improvement may have significantly contributed to the reduction in domestic violence.
33
33

33

Since there is no household-level data in Colombia that encompasses a significant number of municipalities, and because we have found no anecdotal support for this explanation, we consider it plausible but have opted not to emphasize it.

However, note that this third explanation can also be viewed in a way that diverges from our primary hypothesis. It’s possible that the establishment of the first non-Catholic church had a direct and positive impact on the local economy, and the improved economic conditions in these municipalities may have played a role in reducing domestic violence. One potential mechanism for this impact is the investment in public goods, such as education or childcare, by religious organizations to attract followers.

Empirically testing this hypothesis presents a challenge due to the absence of municipal-level data in Colombia that can capture these investments. To address this challenge, we propose examining poverty data at the municipal level. Our hypothesis is that if the local economy in a municipality improved as a result of the introduction of the first non-Catholic church, poverty levels may have been affected. Table

It also points to a potential extension of this work: What could have been the economic consequences (at the household level or at a slightly more aggregated level) of the cultural change in Colombia resulting from the increase in religious competition?

5
Conclusion

Violence among members of the same family occurs in all countries and cultures. The consequences and costs of these acts are both private and social. This paper documents the causal link between a change in cultural practices (induced by changes in religious competition) and domestic violence using data from Colombia. We posit that in contexts where multiple religions can peacefully coexist, increased religious competition will lead to less domestic violence.

Colombia, which has a long history of Catholic dominance upheld by state-imposed barriers, has undergone a significant transformation in its internal religious composition. The liberalization of the religious services marketplace has led to an increase in the population’s adherence to new non-Catholic churches. Our results show that, in the Colombian context, more religious competition (measured by the arrival of the first non-Catholic church in a municipality) leads to fewer domestic violence events. To address the problem of endogeneity, we use a two-way fixed effects model and allow for a dynamic and static specification. We also control for an exhaustive set of observable contemporary, and historical, characteristics. We demonstrate that our estimates are robust compared to alternative estimators proposed in the literature.

We provide evidence supporting the following mechanism: new non-Catholic churches, to gain followers, develop new and more efficient cultural practices that result in beneficial outcomes for the community. The reduction in domestic violence serves as an example of these benefits. These benefits then diffuse throughout society via the social network. Our findings suggest that in municipalities where the social network is less clustered, the benefits of religious competition are greater. Indeed, we show that in municipalities that have lower levels of ethnic diversity, fewer civil society organizations, and reduced religious diversity, the arrival of a new non-Catholic church further reduces domestic violence.

This paper contributes to the literature examining the role played by religious institutions in determining various social, economic, and political outcomes. In the context of broader cultural change, this paper also relates to the literature that examines the production and diffusion of new technologies and the role played by market conditions. Although the evidence presented in our paper is based on the Colombian experience, we believe that the mechanism presented here could apply to other developing nations with deeply religious populations undergoing significant peaceful changes in religious composition.
