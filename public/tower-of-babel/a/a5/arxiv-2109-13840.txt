Stable nickel production in Type Ia supernovae: A smoking gun for the progenitor mass?
S. Blondin, E. Bravo, F. X. Timmes, L. Dessart, D. J. Hillier

Abstract
Context. At present, there are strong indications that white dwarf (WD) stars with masses well below the Chandrasekhar limit ( M Ch ≈ 1.4 M_{\mathrm{Ch}}\approx 1.4 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} ) contribute a significant fraction of SN Ia progenitors. The relative fraction of stable iron-group elements synthesized in the explosion has been suggested as a possible discriminant between M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} and sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} events. In particular, it is thought that the higher-density ejecta of M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} WDs, which favours the synthesis of stable isotopes of nickel, results in prominent [Ni ii ] lines in late-time spectra ( ≳ 150 \gtrsim 150 d past explosion). Aims. We study the explosive nucleosynthesis of stable nickel in SNe Ia resulting from M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} and sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} progenitors. We explore the potential for lines of [Ni ii ] in the optical an near-infrared (at 7378 Å and 1.94 μ \mu m) in late-time spectra to serve as a diagnostic of the exploding WD mass. Methods. We reviewed stable Ni yields across a large variety of published SN Ia models. Using 1D M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} delayed-detonation and sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} detonation models, we studied the synthesis of stable Ni isotopes (in particular, Ni 58 {}^{58}\mathrm{Ni} ) and investigated the formation of [Ni ii ] lines using non-local thermodynamic equilibrium radiative-transfer simulations with the CMFGEN code. Results. We confirm that stable Ni production is generally more efficient in M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} explosions at solar metallicity (typically 0.02–0.08 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} for the Ni 58 {}^{58}\mathrm{Ni} isotope), but we note that the Ni 58 {}^{58}\mathrm{Ni} yield in sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} events systematically exceeds 0.01 M ⊙ \mathrm{M}_{\odot} for WDs that are more massive than one solar mass. We find that the radiative proton-capture reaction Co 57 ​ ( p , γ ) 58 ​ Ni {}^{57}\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{p},\gamma)^{58}\mathrm{Ni} is the dominant production mode for Ni 58 {}^{58}\mathrm{Ni} in both M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} and sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} models, while the α \alpha -capture reaction on Fe 54 {}^{54}\mathrm{Fe} has a negligible impact on the final Ni 58 {}^{58}\mathrm{Ni} yield. More importantly, we demonstrate that the lack of [Ni ii ] lines in late-time spectra of sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} events is not always due to an under-abundance of stable Ni; rather, it results from the higher ionization of Ni in the inner ejecta. Conversely, the strong [Ni ii ] lines predicted in our 1D M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} models are completely suppressed when Ni 56 {}^{56}\mathrm{Ni} is sufficiently mixed with the innermost layers, which are rich in stable iron-group elements. Conclusions. [Ni ii ] lines in late-time SN Ia spectra have a complex dependency on the abundance of stable Ni, which limits their use in distinguishing among M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} and sub- M Ch M_{\mathrm{Ch}} progenitors. However, we argue that a low-luminosity SN Ia displaying strong [Ni ii ] lines would most likely result from a Chandrasekhar-mass progenitor.

Introduction

In the long-standing model for Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia), a
carbon-oxygen white dwarf (CO WD) star accretes material from a binary
companion until it approaches the Chandrasekhar-mass limit for a
relativistic degenerate electron plasma (
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}\approx 1.4

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
). While this model provides a robust ignition mechanism
for runaway carbon fusion, it is in tension with the observed
SN Ia rate (see e.g.
Maoz & Mannucci 2012
;

Livio & Mazzali 2018
;
Wang 2018
;

Soker 2019
for reviews). Moreover, there is growing evidence
that it cannot explain the full range of observed SN Ia properties

(
Jha et al. 2019
, e.g.)
. For instance,
Flörs et al. 2020

find that the Ni/Fe abundance ratio inferred from late-time
spectroscopy is consistent with the predictions of sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models
for 85% of normal SNe Ia.

There are multiple paths leading to the explosion of a WD
significantly below the Chandrasekhar-mass limit. In double-detonation
models

(
Shen et al. 2018
;
Townsley et al. 2019
;
Magee et al. 2021
;
Gronow et al. 2021
, e.g.)
,
a sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
WD accretes a thin He-rich layer from a non-degenerate
binary companion, triggering a detonation at its base which leads to a
secondary detonation of the CO core. Modern incarnations of this model
consider modestly CO-enriched low-mass He layers (
\lesssim 10^{-2}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
), whose detonation does not lead to spurious
spectroscopic features from iron-group elements (IGEs) at early times

(
Shen & Moore 2014
;
Townsley et al. 2019
)
. Furthermore, the predicted
rate of double-detonation models matches the observed SN Ia rate

(
Ruiter et al. 2011
;
Ruiter et al. 2014
, e.g.)
.

Other sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
progenitor models involve double-WD systems. In the
classical double-degenerate model of
Webbink 1984
, two
unequal-mass WDs in a close binary system merge through loss of energy
and angular momentum via gravitational-wave radiation. The more
massive WD tidally disrupts and accretes the lower-mass object,
resulting in an off-centre carbon ignition in the merger remnant and
the formation of an oxygen-neon (ONe) WD

(
Saio & Nomoto 1985
;
Timmes et al. 1994
;
Shen et al. 2012
, e.g.)
. If
the remnant mass exceeds the Chandrasekhar limit, accretion-induced
collapse to a neutron star ensues, associated with a weak explosion,
but with no SN Ia event
(
Nomoto & Kondo 1991
)
.

This model was later revised by
Pakmor et al. 2010
, who
considered the merger of two nearly equal-mass WDs, in which the less
massive WD is rapidly accreted onto the primary WD, resulting in
compressional heating of the accreted material and subsequent carbon
ignition. Such violent merger models have been successful in
reproducing the observed properties of both sub-luminous and normal
SNe Ia
(
Pakmor et al. 2010
;
Pakmor et al. 2012
)
, as well as more
peculiar events
(
Kromer et al. 2013
;
Kromer et al. 2016
)
.

In addition to mergers, several authors have explored collisions
between two WDs as a potential SN Ia progenitor scenario

(
Raskin et al. 2009
;
Rosswog et al. 2009
, e.g.)
. Such collisions
are expected to occur in dense stellar environments such as globular
clusters
(
Hut & Inagaki 1985
;
Sigurdsson & Phinney 1993
, e.g.)
; it
was more recently suggested that they may efficiently occur in triple
systems to explain SNe Ia
(
Katz & Dong 2012
;
Kushnir et al. 2013
)
,
although the predicted rates vary significantly
(
Toonen et al. 2018
, see
e.g.)
. Furthermore,
Dong et al. 2015
argued
that the doubly peaked line profiles observed in late-time spectra of
several SNe Ia result from a bimodal
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
distribution produced in
WD-WD collisions.

It should be possible to identify SNe Ia resulting from

M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
vs. sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
progenitors observationally, since
variations in the ejecta mass have an impact on the radiative display

(
Pinto & Eastman 2000
, e.g.)
. The typical photon diffusion
time depends on the mean opacity,
\kappa
, of the ejecta, its mass,

M_{\mathrm{ej}}
, and characteristic velocity,
\varv
, following

t_{\mathrm{diff}}\propto\kappa^{1/2}M_{\mathrm{ej}}^{1/2}\varv^{-1/2}

(
Arnett 1982
;
Woosley et al. 2007
;
Piro et al. 2010
;
Khatami & Kasen 2019
, e.g.)
. The
photon diffusion time is thus shorter for a sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
ejecta compared
to a
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
ejecta, resulting in shorter bolometric rise times

(
Blondin et al. 2017
, see e.g.)
. The post-maximum bolometric
decline is also faster, as the lower density of sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
ejecta
favours the earlier escape of
\gamma
-rays

(
Kushnir et al. 2020
;
Sharon & Kushnir 2020
, e.g.)

We
note, however, that both of these studies argue that observed

.

A more robust signature of the WD mass should therefore be sought in
the spectroscopic signatures of distinct abundance patterns predicted
by different explosion models; in particular, the density at which the
CO fuel is ignited affects the resulting nucleosynthesis. More
specifically, Chandrasekhar-mass WDs have central densities

\rho_{\mathrm{c}}\gtrsim 10^{9}

\mathrm{g\,cm}^{-3}
, where explosion models involving
sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
WDs (including WD mergers and collisions) detonate the CO
core in regions with
\rho\lesssim 10^{8}

\mathrm{g\,cm}^{-3}
. The higher densities
in
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models result in a higher electron-capture rate during the
explosion, which enhances the production of neutron-rich stable
isotopes of iron-group elements compared to sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models.

Of particular interest are the stable isotopes of nickel, the most
abundant of which is
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
. At sufficiently late times (
\gtrsim 150
d past explosion), SN Ia spectra are dominated by forbidden
lines of singly and doubly ionized Ni, Co, and Fe. By then, the only
nickel left in the ejecta is stable Ni synthesized in the explosion,
whereas most of the Co is
{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}
from
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
decay, and Fe is a
mixture of primordial stable Fe and
{}^{56}\mathrm{Fe}
from
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
decay. The
larger abundance of stable Ni in
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models is thus expected to
manifest itself in the form of forbidden lines of [Ni
ii
], which have
been detected in late-time spectra of several SNe Ia to date

(
Dhawan et al. 2018
;
Maguire et al. 2018
;
Flörs et al. 2018
;
Flörs et al. 2020
, e.g.)
. These lines ought to be largely suppressed if
not completely absent from sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models due to the lower abundance
of stable Ni.

In principle, we thus have a clear prediction in terms of stable Ni
production that depends on the mass of the exploding WD and an
associated spectroscopic diagnostic to distinguish between
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
and
sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models. This was partly confirmed by

Blondin et al. 2018
for low-luminosity SN Ia models: the

M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
model displayed a prominent line due to [Ni
ii
] 1.94
\mu
m,
where the sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
model showed no such line.

In this paper, we test whether this prediction holds for
higher-luminosity models, which correspond to the bulk of the
SN Ia population, and to what extent the abundance of stable Ni is
the determining factor in explaining the strength of [Ni
ii
] lines in
late-time SN Ia spectra. We first briefly present the SN Ia models
and numerical methods in Sect.
2
. We investigate the
dominant nuclear reactions responsible for stable Ni production and
present a census of
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
yields in
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
versus sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models in
Sect.
3
. We discuss the relative impact of Ni abundance
and ionization on nebular [Ni
ii
] lines in Sect.
4
, as
well as the impact of mixing in Sect.
5
. Our conclusions
follow in Sect.
6
.

Explosive burning at sufficiently high temperatures (

3.2
Freeze-out yields

\rho_{\mathrm{peak}}

{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
(
10^{7}\mathrm{-}10^{8}

\mathrm{g\,cm}^{-3}

3.5.1
Chandrasekhar-mass models

Deflagrations:

Laminar flames in SNe Ia quickly become turbulent as buoyant hot
ashes rise through overlying cold fuel, generating Rayleigh-Taylor and
Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities that increase the flame surface and
hence the rate of fuel consumption. Deflagrations are thus best
studied in 3D, and we base our discussion on the models of

Fink et al. 2014
9
9

9

For models that fail to completely
unbind the WD, the reported yields also include the
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
and

{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
synthesized in the remnant core.
. Since the precise initial
conditions at the onset of thermonuclear runaway remain unknown to a
large extent, the deflagration is artificially ignited in a number

N_{k}
of spherical ignition spots (or ‘kernels’) simultaneously. In
their study,
Fink et al. 2014
consider
N_{k}=1,3,5,10,20,40,100,150,200,300,\ \mathrm{and}\ 1600
such kernels distributed at
random around the WD centre.

As
N_{k}
increases, the rate of fuel consumption (and hence nuclear
energy release) also increases, resulting in a more rapid flame growth
and more material being burnt. Thus, increasing
N_{k}
results in a
higher production of both stable
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
and radioactive
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
, which
explains the monotonic sequence in Fig.
5
(open squares)
up until
N_{k}=150
. For higher
N_{k}
, the nuclear energy release is
so high early on that the resulting WD expansion quenches nuclear
reactions, resulting in less material being burnt. Thus, while the

{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
yield continues to increase with
N_{k}
(as this stable isotope
is mainly produced during the initial stages of the deflagration), the

{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
yield remains more or less constant, even decreasing slightly
for
N_{k}=1600
(model N1600def).

The models of
Fink et al. 2014
enable us to study the impact of
variations in the central density of the progenitor WD for model
N100def (dashed line in Fig.
5
). A lower central density of

\rho_{\mathrm{c}}=1.0\times 10^{9}

\mathrm{g\,cm}^{-3}

\sim 50

{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
yield

\rho_{\mathrm{c}}=2.9\times 10^{9}

\mathrm{g\,cm}^{-3}
,
but the yield of heavier stable Ni isotopes more than doubles (see

Gravitationally confined detonation (GCD):

In this model, originally proposed by
Plewa et al. 2004
, burning
is initiated as a weak central deflagration which drives a buoyant
bubble of hot ash that breaks out at the stellar surface, causing a
lateral acceleration and convergence of the flow of material at the
opposite end. Provided the density of the compressed material is high
enough, a detonation is triggered which incinerates the remainder of
the WD.

In Fig.
5

{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
are synthesized during
the detonation phase. However, the WD does expand during the flow
convergence phase, so less
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
is synthesized compared to
delayed-detonation models with similar
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
yield.

3.5.2
Sub-Chandrasekhar-mass models

Detonations:

For detonations of sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
WDs the main parameter that determines
the final yields is the mass of the exploding WD. The propagation of
the detonation front is so fast compared to the WD expansion timescale
that the density at which material is burnt is close to the original
density profile of the progenitor WD. Lower-mass WDs have lower
densities at a given mass (or radial) coordinate, so the detonation
produces less electron-capture isotopes than for more massive WDs. For
the 1D sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models at solar metallicity shown here (SCH series of

Blondin et al. 2017
; filled diamonds in Fig.
5
),
only the highest-mass WDs (
M_{\mathrm{WD}}>1.1

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) have a

{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
yield comparable to 1D delayed-detonation models (DDC series of

Blondin et al. 2013
).
For WD masses below 1
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, the
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
yield is significantly lower
(
<0.025

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) yet not vanishingly small. Stable
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
is still

5
Impact of mixing on nebular [Ni
ii
] lines

5.1
Macroscopic versus microscopic mixing

Macroscopic mixing in SNe Ia occurs during the deflagration phase of
3D
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
delayed-detonation models, due to rising bubbles of buoyant
hot nuclear ash and downward mixing of nuclear fuel

(
Seitenzahl et al. 2013
, e.g.)
. In the innermost ejecta, stable
IGEs can be transported outwards while
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
synthesized at higher
velocities is mixed inwards. As a result, there is no radial chemical
segregation between stable IGEs and
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
-rich layers as in the 1D

M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models studied here. While it is not possible to simulate such
macroscopic mixing in 1D, where the composition is fixed at a given
radial (or velocity) coordinate, various numerical techniques have been
developed to approximate this and other multi-dimensional effects

(
Duffell 2016
;
Zhou 2017
;
Mabanta & Murphy 2018
;
Mabanta et al. 2019
;
Dessart & Hillier 2020
, see
e.g.)
.

Instead, a commonly used expedient in 1D consists in homogenizing the
composition in successive mass shells by applying a running boxcar
average
(
Woosley et al. 2007
;
Dessart et al. 2014
, e.g.)
. In this
approach, the mixing is both macroscopic (material is effectively
advected to larger and lower velocities) and microscopic (the composition
is completely homogenized within each mass shell at each step of the
running average). The method is convenient but results in non-physical
composition profiles that affect the spectral properties

(
Dessart & Hillier 2020
, e.g.)
. We note that numerical
diffusion causes some level of microscopic mixing even in 3D
simulations.

Here, we simply wish to illustrate the impact of mixing on the strength
of [Ni
ii
] lines in late-time spectra of the
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
delayed-detonation
model DDC15 of
Blondin et al. 2015
. For this, we adopt a fully
microscopic mixing approach by homogenizing the composition in the
inner ejecta below some cutoff velocity
\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}
. In what
follows, we refer to this as ‘uniform’ mixing. The mass fraction of a
given species
i
is set to its mass-weighted-average for
\varv\leq\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}
, and is left unchanged for
\varv>\varv_{\mathrm{orig}}=\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}+\Delta\varv_{\mathrm{trans}}
, where
\Delta\varv_{\mathrm{trans}}=\{500,1000\}

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
. To avoid strong compositional discontinuities at

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}
, we use a cosine function to smoothly transition
from the uniform to the unchanged composition over the interval

[\varv_{\mathrm{mix}},\varv_{\mathrm{orig}}]
. Formally, in each mass
shell with velocity coordinate
\varv
:

X_{i}(\varv)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sum\limits_{\varv^{\prime}<\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}}X_{i}(\varv^{\prime})\Delta M(\varv^{\prime})}{\sum\limits_{\varv^{\prime}<\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}}\Delta M(\varv^{\prime})}&\text{for $\varv<\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}$},\\
X_{i}(\varv_{\mathrm{mix}})+f_{\mathrm{cos}}\ X_{i}(\varv_{\mathrm{orig}})&\text{for $\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}\leq\varv<\varv_{\mathrm{orig}}$},\\
X_{i}(\varv)\ \ \text{(unchanged)}&\text{for $\varv\geq\varv_{\mathrm{orig}}$}.\end{cases}

(3)

where

f_{\mathrm{cos}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\{1-\cos\left[\left(\dfrac{\varv-\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}}{\Delta\varv_{\mathrm{trans}}}\right)\pi\right]\right\}.

(4)

This procedure conserves the total mass of each species as
the density profile is left unchanged.

5.2
Impact of mixing on ionization and [Ni
ii
] lines

The uniform mixing we apply not only affects the abundance profiles,
but the ionization as well (Fig.
8
, bottom panel). In the
inner 3000
\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
, the Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio systematically decreases
with increasing
\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}
. This increase in ionization
simply traces the increase in deposited energy from radioactive
decays, through inward mixing of
{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}
(see inset). Unlike the
comparison between
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
and sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models in the previous section,
here the density profile is identical for all uniformly mixed models,
such that the
{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}
radioactive decay heating (20-25% of which is
due to local deposition of positron kinetic energy below
\sim 3000

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
) predominantly determines the ionization state.

Variations in the amount of cooling through line emission further
affect the energy balance. This is best seen in the mixed model with

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}=15000

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
, where the Ni
ii
/Ni

ratio
below
\sim 4000

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
is comparable to the
\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}=7500

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
model despite the
\sim
20% lower decay
heating
13

The mass fraction of
{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}

\sim 0.08

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}

for the

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}=7500

. This is
due to the less efficient line cooling in the

] collisional cooling dominates due
to the larger Ca mass fraction in these layers) compared to the

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}=7500

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
model, in which cooling via
[Fe

] and [Fe

] line profiles are shown in

. As expected, the [Ni

] 1.94
\mu

/Ni

and

\sim

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}=5000

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
, the [Ni

] 1.94
\mu

\sim 3500

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
. However, its FWHM is similar despite

] 7300 Å doublet progressively

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}
. A weak [Ni

]

\varv_{\mathrm{mix}}=3750

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
models, where the Ca mass fraction
is
<10^{-2}
below
\sim 3000

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
. In the other models, inward
mixing of Ca results in a mass fraction of a few times
10^{-2}
which
is sufficient to swamp the [Ni
ii
] 7378 Å line, as [Ca
ii
]
becomes a dominant coolant. The overabundance of Ca in the inner
ejecta illustrates a severe limitation of our 1D approach to mixing:
in the 3D delayed-detonation model N100 of

Seitenzahl et al. 2013
, the Ca mass fraction remains almost
systematically
\lesssim 10^{-5}
below 5000
\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
in all directions
(Seitenzahl 2021, private communication).

Furthermore, aside from low-luminosity 91bg-like events, the presence
of [Ca
ii
] 7300 Å in late-time spectra is not compatible with
observations of SNe Ia, as illustrated with SN 2017bzc in the left
panel of Fig.
9
(grey line,
Flörs et al. 2020
;
see also
Maguire et al. 2018
). Our original (unmixed) DDC15
model does not predict significant [Ca
ii
] 7300 Å emission
(dotted line): The broad double-humped feature around 7300 Å is
dominated by [Ni
ii
] 7378 Å to the red and [Fe
ii
] 7155 Å to
the blue (as noted by
Flörs et al. 2020
). However, our model
clearly overestimates the strength of [Ni
ii
] 1.94
\mu
m, and while
we can adjust the level of mixing to match its strength, this
inevitably results in an overestimation of [Ca
ii
] 7300 Å in the
optical.

Our results nonetheless suggest that inward mixing of
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
can
completely wash out otherwise strong [Ni
ii
] lines in late-time
spectra of
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models. A more physical treatment of mixing could
result in pockets rich in stable nickel being physically isolated from
regions rich in
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
(as in the 3D delayed-detonation models of

Bravo & García-Senz 2008
). This would suppress decay heating
of the stable Ni pockets by local positron kinetic energy deposition
from
{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}
decay, and compensate in part for the increase in
ionization. Moreover, such stable Ni pockets could be moderately
compressed through the
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
bubble effect

(
Wang 2005
;
Dessart et al. 2021
, e.g.)
, enhancing the Ni
recombination rate. Whatever the exact effect, mixing complicates the
use of [Ni
ii
] lines to constrain the stable Ni abundance, and the
absence of these lines cannot be unambiguously associated with a
sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
explosion.

6
Conclusions

We studied the explosive nucleosynthesis of stable nickel and its
dominant isotope
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
in SNe Ia to test its use as a diagnostic of
the progenitor WD mass. Among all reactions ending in
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
, we find
that the radiative proton-capture reaction

{}^{57}\mathrm{Co}(\mathrm{p},\gamma)^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
mostly
determines the final
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
abundance in both
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
and
sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models. Contrary to expectations, direct
\alpha
captures
on
{}^{54}\mathrm{Fe}
only contribute at the percent level to the net
nucleosynthetic flux to
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
, even in the
\alpha
-rich freeze-out
regime from nuclear statistical equilibrium.

At solar metallicity and for a given
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
yield, sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models
synthesize less
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
(
\sim
0–0.03
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) compared to
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models
(
\sim
0.02–0.08
\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
), although this difference is reduced for WD
masses
\gtrsim 1

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
or for super-solar metallicities. The
trend remains the same when considering the total stable nickel yield
as opposed to only
{}^{58}\mathrm{Ni}
, although some double-detonation models
synthesize 30–90% of stable Ni in the form of heavier isotopes (in
particular
60
Ni), causing an overlap with the 1D

M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
delayed-detonation models studied here.

The systematic absence of [Ni
ii
] lines in late-time spectra of
sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models is due to the higher Ni ionization in the inner
ejecta, where Ni
iii
dominates over Ni
ii
. This higher ionization
results from the lower density of the inner ejecta compared to

M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models, which limits the Ni
iii
\rightarrow
ii

recombination rate and increases the deposited decay energy per unit
mass. In 1D
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models, the difference in ionization is exacerbated
by the under-abundance of
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
in the inner ejecta, which results in
lower local kinetic energy deposition by positrons from
{}^{56}\mathrm{Co}
decay
at late times.

Artificially reducing the Ni ionization of the sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models (while
maintaining the same Ni abundance) results in the emergence of
[Ni
ii
] lines, although these remain fairly weak in low-luminosity
models where the stable Ni yield is
<10^{-2}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, even when the
Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio is increased by a factor of 100. Any mechanism
that reduces the ionization state of the inner ejecta in
sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models could thus in principle lead to the formation of
[Ni
ii
] lines, thereby invalidating the use of this line as a
fool-proof discriminant between
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
and sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
explosions. One
such mechanism is clumping, although a recent study by

Wilk et al. 2020
showed that the ionization level was not lowered
sufficiently to produce a favourable Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio, at least
in their 1D implementation of clumping via volume-filling factors.

Likewise, an increase in the Ni ionization of the
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models through
a tenfold reduction of the Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio completely
suppresses both optical and near-infrared [Ni
ii
] lines, despite a
relatively large abundance of stable Ni (
3\mathrm{-}5\times 10^{-2}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
). This again demonstrates the importance of ionization
over abundance in determining the presence of [Ni
ii
] lines in
late-time spectra of
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models.

Conversely, mixing can completely wash out otherwise strong [Ni
ii
]
lines in
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
models. Our investigation of this effect in 1D is
artificial, but nonetheless captures the main effect of the inward
microscopic mixing of
{}^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
and the resulting increase in decay
energy deposition and, hence, the Ni ionization state, in the inner
ejecta. At the same time, stable Ni is mixed outwards, reducing its
abundance in the [Ni
ii
] line-formation region. A more elaborate
treatment of mixing could mitigate in part this increase in Ni
ionization.

In summary, the presence of [Ni
ii
] lines in late-time spectra of
SNe Ia is largely the result of a favourable Ni ionization state in
the inner ejecta and it is not guaranteed solely based on a large
abundance of stable nickel. This sensitivity to ionization complicates
the use of these lines alone as a diagnostic of the progenitor WD mass
(or simply differentiating between
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
and sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
ejecta). It is
possible that [Ni
ii
] lines in combination with other lines of
[Co
ii
/
iii
] and [Fe
ii
/
iii
] present in late-time spectra
could help constrain the Ni ionization state. In that case, a low Ni
ionization combined with an absence of [Ni
ii
] lines would point to a
very low abundance of stable nickel (
\lesssim 10^{-3}

\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
) and,
in turn, to a sub-
M_{\mathrm{Ch}}
progenitor. Conversely, the presence of strong
[Ni
ii
] lines in a low-luminosity SN Ia would likely be the result
of a Chandrasekhar-mass explosion.

Appendix C
Modifying the Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio in CMFGEN

In CMFGEN, the population density
n_{l}
(in cm
-3
) of any given
state (level)
l
is determined via a solution to the time-dependent
statistical equilibrium equations
(
Hillier & Dessart 2012
)
. The
population density of the ionization state
i+
(
i=0
for neutral,

i=1
for once-ionized etc.) for some species is then simply the sum
of
n_{l}
over all
N
levels of that ionization state:

n^{i+}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}{n_{l}}^{i+}.

(5)

We then define the Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio as:

I_{23}=\frac{n^{+}}{n^{2+}}.

(6)

In Sect.
4
we artificially modify the
Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio to test the impact on the resulting [Ni
ii
]
lines in late-time SN Ia spectra. At any given depth in the ejecta
(denoted by index
j
, which corresponds to a given radius or velocity
coordinate in our 1D spatial grid), we scale all the Ni
ii
and
Ni
iii
level population densities by a factor of
a_{1,j}
and

a_{2,j}
, respectively, to obtain new population densities:

\widetilde{n_{j}}^{+}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{1,j}\ {n_{l,j}}^{+}=a_{1,j}\ {n_{j}}^{+}

(7)

and

\widetilde{n_{j}}^{2+}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}a_{2,j}\ {n_{l,j}}^{2+}=a_{2,j}\ {n_{j}}^{2+}

(8)

in order to achieve a new Ni
ii
/Ni
iii
ratio

\widetilde{I_{23}}
related to the original ratio

I_{23}
by some pre-determined factor:

\mathcal{R}_{23}=\frac{\widetilde{I_{23}}}{I_{23}}=\frac{\widetilde{n_{j}}^{+}}{\widetilde{n_{j}}^{2+}}\frac{{n_{j}}^{2+}}{{n_{j}}^{+}}=\frac{a_{1,j}}{a_{2,j}}.

(9)

). We note that

a_{1}/a_{2}=\mathcal{R}_{23}=0.1
at all depths by definition
(Eq.

). When the original Ni

/Ni

ratio becomes
small enough (
\lesssim 10^{-1}

\mathrm{km\,s}^{-1}
),

(=\mathcal{R}_{23})
and

, as

Since we wish to preserve the total species population density at each
depth, we further require that:

\widetilde{n_{j}}^{+}+\widetilde{n_{j}}^{2+}={n_{j}}^{+}+{n_{j}}^{2+},

(10)

from which we derive an equation for the scale factor
for the Ni

a_{2,j}=\frac{{n_{j}}^{+}(1-a_{1,j})+{n_{j}}^{2+}}{{n_{j}}^{2+}},

(11)

which we then plug into Eq.
9
to derive the scale

a_{1,j}=\frac{({n_{j}}^{+}+{n_{j}}^{2+})\ \mathcal{R}_{23}}{{n_{j}}^{+}\ \mathcal{R}_{23}+{n_{j}}^{2+}},

(12)

from which we trivially compute
a_{2,j}=a_{1,j}\ \mathcal{R}_{23}
using Eq.
9
(or Eq.
11

When
{n_{j}}^{+}\ll{n_{j}}^{2+}

\mathcal{R}_{23}<1

and
