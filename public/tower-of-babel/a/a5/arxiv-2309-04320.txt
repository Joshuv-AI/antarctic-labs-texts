Determination of stable branches of relative equilibria of the N N -vortex problem on the sphere
Constantineau, K, García-Azpeitia, C, García-Naranjo, L. C, J.-P. Lessard

Abstract
We consider the N N -vortex problem on the sphere assuming that all vorticities have equal strength. We investigate relative equilibria (RE) consisting of n n latitudinal rings which are uniformly rotating about the vertical axis with angular velocity ω \omega . Each such ring contains m m vortices placed at the vertices of a concentric regular polygon and we allow the presence of additional vortices at the poles. We develop a framework to prove existence and orbital stability of branches of RE of this type parametrised by ω \omega . Such framework is implemented to rigorously determine and prove stability of segments of branches using computer-assisted proofs. This approach circumvents the analytical complexities that arise when the number of rings n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 and allows us to give several new rigorous results. We exemplify our method providing new contributions consisting in the determination of enclosures and proofs of stability of several equilibria and RE for 5 ≤ N ≤ 12 5\leq N\leq 12 .



Keywords:

N
-vortex problem, relative equilibrium, stability, interval arithmetic, computer-assisted proofs.

Stability.

Our nonlinear stability analysis relies on the
energy-momentum
method
of Patrick
[
56
]
. Such method concludes Lyapunov stability modulo a subgroup, which
in our case generically translates into orbital stability of a periodic orbit (see Proposition
2.7
).
The method examines positivity of a certain Hessian matrix which we
block-diagonalise exploiting the
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-symmetries following closely the construction of Laurent-Polz, Montaldi and Roberts
[
40
]
.
Our approach differs from
[
40
]
in two ways. On the one hand, instead of using spherical coordinates,
we work with the extrinsic geometry induced by our embedding of
M
on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
. This allows us to interpret
tangent vectors to
M
as vectors in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
, which is convenient for the implementation of CAPs.
Secondly, we refine the block diagonalisation of
[
40
]
by exploiting the complex
structure of some blocks as determined by Theorem
4.8
.
The great technicality involved in this block diagonalisation and its refinement is compensated
by obtaining larger
ranges of application of CAPs, since the method prevents clustering of eigenvalues of large matrices.

A summary of the stability test which indicates the matrix blocks which need to be computed, and their dimension,
according to the values of
m
,
n
and
p
is given in subsection
4.5
.

Preliminaries

In this section we recall the equations of motion and basic known properties of the equations of motion
of the
N
-vortex on the sphere. This allows us to introduce the notation and concepts used ahead. In particular
our working definition of RE is given in Definition

2.1
Equations of motion

The equations of motion of the
N
-vortex problem on the sphere are a
Hamiltonian system on the phase space
M
obtained as the cartesian product of
N
copies of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
minus the
collision set

\Delta
. That is,
M:=(S^{2}\times\dots\times S^{2})\setminus\Delta
where

S^{2}:=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,\|w\|=1\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\Delta:=\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in M\,:\,v_{i}=v_{j}\,\,\mbox{for some $i\neq j$}\}.

The Hamiltonian
function
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
and symplectic form
\Omega
on
M
are given by

H(v):=-\frac{\Gamma_{i}\Gamma_{j}}{4\pi}\sum_{i<j}\ln\left(\left\|v_{i}-v_{j}\right\|^{2}\right),\qquad\Omega:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\pi^{*}_{i}\omega_{S^{2}}.

Here
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in M
specifies the position of each of the vortices whose respective (constant) intensities are denoted by the scalars
\Gamma_{i}\neq 0
.
The map

\pi_{i}:M\to S^{2}
denotes the projection onto the
i^{th}
factor of the cartesian product
S^{2}\times\dots\times S^{2}
and
\omega_{S^{2}}
is the standard area form on

S^{2}
. The resulting equations of motion are

\dot{v}_{j}=-\frac{1}{\Gamma_{j}}v_{j}\times\nabla_{v_{j}}H=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{i\neq j}\Gamma_{i}\frac{v_{i}\times v_{j}}{\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|^{2}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N,

(2.1)

where
\times
denotes the cross product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Owing to the Hamiltonian structure, the Hamiltonian function
H
is a first integral.

2.2
Rotational symmetries, center of vorticity, equilibria and relative equilibria

Rotational symmetries.

The position of the vortices in the sphere is specified by
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in M
. This tacitly assumes a choice of an inertial frame
whose origin lies at the center of the sphere
which defines cartesian coordinates for the
v_{j}
’s. There is freedom in the choice of orientation of this inertial frame and this is reflected by
the invariance of the problem under the
SO(3)
action that
simultaneously rotates all vortices. Specifically we consider the
SO(3)
action on
M
defined by

(g,v)\mapsto g.v:=(gv_{1},\dots,gv_{N})\in M,\qquad g\in SO(3),\;v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in M.

For
N\geq 3
this action is free since the collisions do not belong to
M
. It is also a proper action since
SO(3)
is compact.

It is easily checked that both the Hamiltonian and the symplectic form are invariant under this action and as a consequence, the equations of motion (
2.1
)
are
SO(3)
-equivariant.

Center of vorticity.

One may easily show that the components of the map

\Phi:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\qquad\Phi(v)=\Gamma_{1}v_{1}+\dots+\Gamma_{N}v_{N},

(2.2)

are first integrals of the equations of motion (
2.1
). It is common to refer to
\Phi(v)
as the
center of vorticity

of the vortex configuration
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
. The existence of these first integrals is in fact an instance of Noether’s theorem.
Upon the standard identification of the dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(3)^{*}
with
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the function
\Phi
can be geometrically interpreted as the
momentum map
associated to the
symplectic action of
SO(3)
on the symplectic manifold
(M,\Omega)
.

For
N\geq 3
, the freeness of the action guarantees that

\Phi
is a submersion onto its image. As a consequence, if
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is such that
\Phi(v)=\mu
for some
v\in M
, then

\Phi^{-1}(\mu)
is a smooth submanifold of
M
of codimension 3 and
T_{v}\Phi^{-1}(\mu)=\ker d\Phi(v)
.

The momentum map
\Phi
plays a fundamental role in the study of relative equilibria.
An important property of
\Phi
is its equivariance with respect to the coadjoint representation which, in our interpretation, is simply the
standard linear action of
SO(3)
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, namely, one has

\Phi(g.v)=g\Phi(v),\quad\forall v\in M,\;g\in SO(3).

(2.3)

Equilibrium points and ground states.

The simplest solutions of the equations of motion are the equilibria which are in one-to-one correspondence with the critical points of
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
.
Due to the
SO(3)
invariance of the problem, equilibrium points are never isolated: if
v_{0}\in M
is an equilibrium of (
2.1
)
so is
g.v_{0}
for any
g\in SO(3)
. In other words, the orbit
SO(3).v_{0}\subset M
is comprised of equilibrium points.

If all the vortex strengths
\Gamma_{i}
have the same sign, then
H
is bounded from below and it assumes a global minimum on
M
. The
minimising configurations are called
ground states
and
form a very important kind of equilibria from the physical point of view. Again, the
SO(3)
-invariance of
H
implies that if
v_{0}\in M
is a ground state then the orbit
SO(3).v_{0}\subset M
consists of ground states.
The ground states of the system are stable in a sense that will be made precise below.
As mentioned in the introduction, proving that a given equilibrium is the ground state of the system is a very difficult problem for which very little is known.

Relative equilibria.

The next type of fundamental solutions are the so-called
relative equilibria
(RE) which are the main subject of this work. This kind of solutions
may exist for any system of differential equations which is equivariant with respect to the action of a continuous symmetry group. There are several equivalent definitions
of relative equilibria. For our purposes, it suffices to define them as
solutions of the equations that at the same time are orbits of a one-parameter subgroup of the symmetry group. Thus, in our case, relative equilibria have the form

v(t)=e^{\xi t}.a
for a fixed
a\in M
and a skew-symmetric matrix
\xi\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
. This is a periodic solution in which
the vortices steadily rotate along the axis passing through the center of the sphere in the direction of the vector
\xi_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying
\langle\xi_{e}\rangle=\ker\xi

and whose angular velocity
\omega_{e}
satisfies
2\omega_{e}^{2}=\mbox{trace}(\xi\xi^{T})
.

Just like equilibrium points, relative equilibria are never isolated:
if
v(t)=e^{\xi t}.a
is a relative equilibrium and
g\in SO(3)
then, by equivariance of (
2.1
),

\tilde{v}(t):=g.v(t)
is also a solution. However, we may
write

\tilde{v}(t)=g.v(t)=(ge^{\xi t}g^{-1}).(g.a)=e^{(g\xi g^{-1})t}.(g.a)=e^{\tilde{\xi}t}.\tilde{a},

where
\tilde{\xi}:=g\xi g^{-1}\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
and
\tilde{a}:=g.a\in M
, which shows that
\tilde{v}(t)
is also a relative equilibrium
with axis of rotation
\tilde{\xi}_{e}=g\xi_{e}
and the same angular frequency
\tilde{\omega}_{e}=\omega_{e}
. Physically, this means that the orientation
of the axis of rotation of a relative equilibrium is unessential and may be arbitrarily chosen via a suitable rotation of the inertial frame. On the other hand, relative
equilibria having distinct angular frequencies are not related by a rotation. Therefore, for the rest of the paper, we will
restrict our attention to relative equilibria for which the axis of rotation is the
z
-axis and we will give a prominent role to the angular velocity
\omega
.
In accordance with this, we search for solutions of (
2.1
) of the form

v(t)=e^{\omega J_{3}t}.a=(e^{\omega J_{3}t}a_{1},\dots,e^{\omega J_{3}t}a_{N}),\qquad J_{3}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},

(2.4)

for a certain frequency
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and a configuration
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})\in M
. Our discussion leads to the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

A
relative equilibrium
(RE) of the equations of motion (
2.1
) is a pair
(a,\omega)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
such
that
v(t)
given by (
2.4
) is a solution of
(
2.1
). We say that
\omega
is the
angular velocity
and
a\in M
is the
configuration

of the relative equilibrium.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

Equilibrium points may be interpreted as RE
with zero angular velocity.

(ii)

If
(a,\omega)
is a RE with
\omega\neq 0
, then so is
(e^{J_{3}\theta}.a,\omega)
for any
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. This is a consequence
of the autonomous nature of the equations of motion (
2.1
): if
v(t)
is a solution, then so is
v(t+t_{0})
for any
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. In other words,

a
is determined up to a time-shift along the solution with initial condition
a
.
Considering that the solution is periodic, this time-shift corresponds to
a certain
SO(2)
action. Such
SO(2)
action is precisely the one-parameter subgroup of
SO(3)
of rotations about the
z
axis. Namely, if we
denote
e_{3}:=(0,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and define

SO(2):=\{g\in SO(3)\,:\,ge_{3}=e_{3}\}=\left\{e^{J_{3}\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\,:\,\theta\in[0,2\pi)\right\},

(2.5)

then the orbit
SO(2).a
coincides with the dynamical orbit of the solution
v(t)=e^{\omega J_{3}t}.a
, and is comprised of relative equilibrium configurations with the same angular velocity
\omega
.

(iii)

Some authors instead define the whole orbit
SO(3).a\subset M
of the relative equilibrium configuration
a
as
the
relative equilibrium since it projects to
a single equilibrium point of the reduced system on the quotient space
M/SO(3)
.
As explained in item (ii), our approach selects instead
an
SO(2)
-orbit of representatives. This is essential for the continuation of relative equilibria as a function of the angular velocity
\omega

considered ahead.

For any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
define the
augmented Hamiltonian

H_{\omega}:M\to\mathbb{R},\qquad H_{\omega}(v):=H(v)-\omega\Phi_{3}(v),

(2.6)

where
\Phi_{3}:M\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the third component of the momentum map

given by (

). Namely,

\Phi_{3}(v)=e_{3}\cdot\Phi(v)
where
e_{3}=(0,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and
\cdot
denotes the standard euclidean product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The following proposition is a particular instance of general results on relative equilibria on symplectic manifolds (see e.g.
[
42
]
).
We present an elementary proof for completeness.

Proposition 2.3
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

(a,\omega)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
is a relative equilibrium if and only if
a\in M
is a critical point of
H_{\omega}
.

(ii)

If
(a,\omega)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
is a relative equilibrium with
\omega\neq 0
then
e_{3}\times\Phi(a)=0
.

Proof.

(i) Let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and introduce the time dependent change of variables,

y_{j}(t)=e^{-\omega J_{3}t}v_{j}(t)\text{,}\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

According to Definition
2.1
, it is clear that
(a,\omega)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
is a relative equilibrium if and only if
a\in M
is
an equilibrium solution for the equations satisfied by
y(t)=(y_{1}(t),\dots,y_{N}(t))
. A direct calculation shows that

\dot{y}_{j}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\sum_{i\neq j)}^{N}\Gamma_{i}\frac{y_{i}\times y_{j}}{\left\|y_{j}-y_{i}\right\|^{2}}-\omega e_{3}\times y_{j}=-y_{j}\times\left(\nabla_{y_{j}}H_{\omega}(y)\right).

This shows that the equations for
y(t)
are Hamiltonian on the symplectic manifold
(M,\Omega)
with respect to the Hamiltonian
function
H_{\omega}
. As a consequence, the equilibrium points of
y(t)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the critical points of
H_{\omega}
.

(ii) By conservation of
\Phi
we have
\Phi(a)=\Phi(v(t))
where
v(t)
is given by (
2.4
).
Therefore,

\begin{split}\Gamma_{1}a_{1}+\dots+\Gamma_{N}a_{N}&=\Gamma_{1}e^{\omega J_{3}t}a_{1}+\dots+\Gamma_{N}e^{\omega J_{3}t}a_{N}\\
&=e^{\omega J_{3}t}(\Gamma_{1}a_{1}+\dots+\Gamma_{N}a_{N})=e^{\omega J_{3}t}\Phi(a).\end{split}

Differentiating with respect to
t
and evaluating at
t=0
gives
\omega J_{3}\Phi(a)=0
, which
for
\omega\neq 0
is equivalent to
e_{3}\times\Phi(a)=0
.

∎

A fundamental observation which follows directly from (
2.3
) and the definition of the augmented Hamiltonian
H_{\omega}
is that, for
\omega\neq 0
,

H_{\omega}
is no longer
SO(3)
-invariant but only

SO(2)
-invariant with
SO(2)
given by (
2.5
). As a consequence of this

-symmetry, if
a\in M
is a critical point of

so is
e^{J_{3}\theta}.a

for
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. This
observation (together with Proposition
2.3
) is consistent with item
(ii) of Remark
2.2
.

Another useful interpretation of RE which follows from the Proposition
2.3
and the
Lagrange multiplier theorem is stated next. We refer the reader to

for details.

Proposition 2.4
.

(a,\omega)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
is a relative equilibrium if and only if
a\in M
is a critical point of the restriction of

H
to the level set
\Phi^{-1}(\mu)\subset M
where
\mu=(0,0,\Phi_{3}(a))\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Recall from our previous discussion on the ground states, that if all the vortex strengths
\Gamma_{i}
have equal sign, then the Hamiltonian

is bounded from below.
Therefore, given
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
belonging to the image of
\Phi
, there exists a minimal energy relative equilibrium
(a,\omega)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
satisfying that
H(a)
is the minimum value of the restriction
of

to
\Phi^{-1}(\mu)\subset M
. These minimising RE are stable (in a sense that we make precise below). The
results in section

to

2.3
The case of identical vortex strengths.

Most of the results of this paper assume that the
vortex strengths
\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma_{j}
for all

. With an appropriate scaling of units, we can assume that

H(v)=-\sum_{i<j}\ln\left(\left\|v_{i}-v_{j}\right\|^{2}\right),\qquad\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ \pi^{*}_{i}\omega_{S^{2}},

\dot{v}_{j}=-v_{j}\times\nabla_{v_{j}}H=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{v_{i}\times v_{j}}{\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|^{2}},\qquad i=1,\dots,N.

\Phi:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\qquad\Phi(v)=v_{1}+\dots+v_{N}.

(2.9)

(2.10)

The above action is symplectic, preserves the Hamiltonian
H
, and the equations (

equivariant. However, in contrast with the

-action on

Energy momentum method

The stability analysis of RE that we employ in this work relies on the

Let

From our discussion above,

2.5
computer-assisted proofs

As mentioned in the introduction, the CAPs in this paper are obtained via a finite dimensional Newton-Kantorovich
like theorem (see
[

]

[

48
]
and Krawczyk’s operator approach
[

]

[
31

21
]
) together with the uniform contraction principle (e.g. see
[
17
]
) to obtain one-dimensional branches of solutions. Let us give some details.

Consider a smooth map
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(x,\omega)\mapsto F(x,\omega)
,
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Now consider
two numerical approximations
(\bar{x}_{0},\omega_{0}),(\bar{x}_{1},\omega_{1})

such that
F(\bar{x}_{i},\omega_{i})\approx 0
(for
i=0,1
).
Given
s\in[0,1]
, let
\bar{x}_{s}:=s\bar{x}_{1}+(1-s)\bar{x}_{0}
and
\omega_{s}:=s\omega_{1}+(1-s)\omega_{0}
.

Choose a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and given a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the closed ball of radius
r>0
by
\overline{B_{r}(x)}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\xi-x\|\leq r\}
.

Theorem 2.8

(Uniform Newton-Kantorovich-like theorem)
.

Let
F
be as above and
A\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
A\approx D_{x}F(\bar{x}_{0},\omega_{0})^{-1}
. Consider the non-negative bounds

,
\hat{Y}
,

and the positive number
r_{*}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|AF(\bar{x}_{0},\omega_{0})\|

\displaystyle\leq Y,

\displaystyle\|A[F(\bar{x}_{s},\omega_{s})-F(\bar{x}_{0},\omega_{0})]\|

\displaystyle\leq\hat{Y},\quad\textit{for s}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\|A[I-D_{x}F(c,\omega_{s})]\|

\displaystyle\leq Z,\quad\textit{for all c}\in\overline{B_{r_{*}}(\bar{x}_{s})}\textit{and s}\in[0,1].

Define the radii polynomial

p(r):=(Z-1)r+Y+\hat{Y}.

(2.12)

If there exists
r_{0}
such that

satisfies

, then there exists a function

\tilde{x}:\{\omega_{s}:s\in[0,1]\}\rightarrow\bigcup\limits_{s\in[0,1]}\overline{B_{r_{*}}(\bar{x}_{s})}

such that

The above Newton-Kantorovich-like theorem will be used to show existence of branches of relative equilibria (see Section

Proof.

v\in U

v=(g,x)\in SO(3)\times\mathcal{N}_{0}
. In particular, the point

a_{0}=(e,0)
(where

denotes the identity element in
SO(3)
) and elements of the orbit
SO(3).a_{0}
have the form
(g,0)
. Our hypothesis that the orbit of
a_{0}
is a nondegenerate critical manifold
of
H_{0}
implies that for all

we have

In particular, for fixed
g_{0}\in SO(3)
and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
f_{g_{0},\omega}:\mathcal{N}_{0}\to\mathcal{N}^{*}
given by
f_{g_{0},\omega}(x)=d_{x}H_{\omega}(g_{0},x)

satisfies

f_{g_{0},0}(0)=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad D_{x}f_{g_{0},0}(0)\quad\mbox{is invertible}.

Therefore, by the implicit function theorem we have

d_{x}H_{\omega}(g,\tilde{x}_{g_{0}}(g,\omega))=0,

for a unique function
\tilde{x}_{g_{0}}:U_{g_{0}}\times I_{g_{0}}\to\mathcal{N}_{0}
, where
U_{g_{0}}
is a neighbourhood of
g_{0}\in SO(3)
,
I_{g_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}
is
a small interval around zero, and
\tilde{x}_{g_{0}}
satisfies
\tilde{x}_{g_{0}}(g_{0},0)=0
.

The above argument may be repeated varying the group element
g_{0}\in SO(3)
. Using compactness of
SO(3)
, one may obtain (see
[
23
]
for details)
a uniform version of the implicit
function theorem, namely, the existence of
\omega_{0}>0
and a unique function
\tilde{x}:SO(3)\times(-\omega_{0},\omega_{0})\to\mathcal{N}_{0}
satisfying

\tilde{x}(g,0)=0
such that

d_{x}H_{\omega}(g,\tilde{x}(g,\omega))=0,\qquad\mbox{for all $g\in SO(3)$, $\omega\in(-\omega_{0},\omega_{0})$}.

For any
\omega\in(-\omega_{0},\omega_{0})
define
\psi_{\omega}:SO(3)\to\mathbb{R}
by

\psi_{\omega}(g)=H_{\omega}(g,\tilde{x}(g,\omega))
.
By compactness of
SO(3)
the function
\psi_{\omega}
attains its maximum and its minimum at certain

and hence

H_{\omega}
has an extremum at
a_{1}:=(g_{1},\psi_{\omega}(g_{1}))
and
a_{2}:=(g_{2},\psi_{\omega}(g_{2}))
. Therefore,
(a_{1},\omega)
and
(a_{2},\omega)
are the desired relative equilibria.
∎

and
m\geq 3
. In the former case we have in view of (
4.19
),

\begin{split}d\Phi(a)\hat{B}_{j,1}=J_{3}(A+\overline{A})u_{j}=2(-y_{j},x_{j},0).\end{split}

For
m\geq 3
using again (
4.19
) and

\overline{A}e_{3}=0
, we have instead,

\begin{split}d\Phi(a)\hat{B}_{j,1}=\frac{m}{2}J_{3}Au_{j}+\frac{m}{2}J_{3}\overline{A}e_{3}e_{3}^{T}u_{j}=\frac{m}{2}(-i\eta_{j},\eta_{j},0)=-i\frac{m}{2}\eta_{j}(1,i,0).\end{split}

The above calculations show that all given formulas for
d\Phi(a)\hat{B}_{j,l}

in (
4.17
) indeed hold. In order to prove those for

d\Phi(a)\hat{C}_{j,l}
we proceed analogously. We first notice that

\begin{split}d\Phi(a)C_{j,k}&=c_{j,k}=\left(J_{3}a_{j,k}\right)\times a_{j,k}=\left(J_{3}e^{kJ_{3}\zeta}u_{j}\right)\times\left(e^{kJ_{3}\zeta}u_{j}\right)=e^{kJ_{3}\zeta}\left(J_{3}u_{j}\right)\times u_{j}=e^{kJ_{3}\zeta}(z_{j}u_{j}-e_{3})\\
&=z_{j}e^{kJ_{3}\zeta}u_{j}-e_{3}.\end{split}

Therefore,

\begin{split}d\Phi(a)\hat{C}_{j,l}&=z_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{ikl\zeta}e^{kJ_{3}\zeta}u_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{ikl\zeta}e_{3}.\end{split}

Using (
4.18
) we obtain

d\Phi(a)\hat{C}_{j,l}=\frac{z_{j}}{2}A\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{k(l-1)J_{3}\zeta}\right)u_{j}+\frac{z_{j}}{2}\overline{A}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{k(l+1)J_{3}\zeta}\right)u_{j}+(z_{j}^{2}-1)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{ikl\zeta}\right)e_{3}.

The last term on the right hand side equals

m(z_{j}^{2}-1)e_{3}=-m|\eta_{j}|^{2}e_{3}
if
l=0
, and it instead
vanishes if

1\leq l\leq\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]
. Indeed, this follows from

\sum_{k=1}^{m}e^{ikl\zeta}=\begin{cases}m&\mbox{if $l=0$},\\
0&\mbox{if $1\leq l\leq\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]$}\end{cases}.

Thus, in view of (
4.19
), and since
Ae_{3}=\overline{A}e_{3}=0
,
we conclude that the given formulas
for
d\Phi(a)\hat{C}_{j,l}
indeed hold for
l=0,2,\dots,\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]
. We now
treat the case
l=1
. Using again (
4.19
) and

\overline{A}e_{3}=0
, we find that
for
m\geq 3
we have

d\Phi(a)\hat{C}_{j,1}=\frac{m}{2}z_{j}Au_{j}=\frac{m}{2}z_{j}\eta_{j}\left(1,i,0\right).

On the other hand, if
m=2
we obtain, again in view of (
4.19
),

d\Phi(a)\hat{C}_{j,1}=z_{j}(A+\overline{A})u_{j}=2z_{j}\left(x_{j},y_{j},0\right).

Finally, it is obvious from their definition, that
d\Phi(a)\delta x_{s}=e_{1}
and
d\Phi(a)\delta y_{s}=e_{2}
which immediately yields the last identity in (
4.17
).
∎

4.3
Block diagonalisation of
\left.d^{2}H_{\omega}(a)\right|_{\mathcal{N}_{a}}
and construction of the block matrices

As a consequence of item (i) of Lemma
4.2

and the definition of the spaces

, the symmetric bilinear
form
\left.d^{2}H_{\omega}(a)\right|_{\mathcal{N}_{a}}

block diagonalises with respect to the symmetric decomposition (
4.22
).

In order to obtain a matrix representation of each of the blocks it is necessary to use Lagrange multipliers as explained
in Appendix
A
. Namely, given that
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{N})\in M
is a critical point of the augmented Hamiltonian
H_{\omega}:M\to\mathbb{R}
,
there exist Lagrange multipliers
(c_{1},\dots,c_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
(a,c)
,
interpreted as a point
in
(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
, is a critical point of the function

H^{*}_{\omega}:(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad H^{*}_{\omega}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})=H_{\omega}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})+\sum\lambda_{j}R_{j}(x).

and on the right hand side we think of
H_{\omega}
as a function whose domain is
(an open subset of )
(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}
in the natural way. We have,

(4.25)

where

denotes the standard Hessian matrix of the function

(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\ni x\mapsto H_{\omega}^{*}(x,c)\in\mathbb{R}
evaluated at
a
, and
{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in V_{a}\subset(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}
are column vectors
(see Appendix
A
).

Now, in order to give a matrix form for the restriction of

to the block
U_{l}
we need a basis for
U_{l}
.

\mathcal{B}_{0}
for

4.23
). For

4.15
) or

) (according to whether

2
). Finally, considering that

, we can
simply set
\mathcal{B}_{l}:=\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{l}}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{B}_{l}}
is the basis of

given in the statement of item (ii) of Lemma

representing

4.4.2
The signature of
\mathcal{P}_{l}
is twice the signature of
\mathcal{Q}_{l}

The relation between the signature of the complex Hermitian matrices
\mathcal{Q}_{l}

and the real symmetric matrices
\mathcal{P}_{l}
is given by the following.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
l
as in (
4.28
) and consider
the block matrices
\mathcal{P}_{l}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{l}
defined above. Then

i_{+}(\mathcal{P}_{l})=2i_{+}(\mathcal{Q}_{l}),\qquad i_{-}(\mathcal{P}_{l})=2i_{-}(\mathcal{Q}_{l}),\qquad\dim\ker\mathcal{P}_{l}=2\dim\ker\mathcal{Q}_{l},

where
i_{+}
and
i_{-}
denote the positive and negative indices of inertia of the corresponding
matrices. In particular,
\mbox{signature}(\mathcal{P}_{l})=2\mbox{signature}(\mathcal{Q}_{l})
, and
\mathcal{P}_{l}
is positive definite
if and only if
\mathcal{Q}_{l}
is positive definite.

The proof of Theorem
4.8
relies on a general linear
algebra result, which we state as Lemma
4.9
below and whose proof is given in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
V
be a real vector space of dimension
2d
and
f:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
a symmetric bilinear
form. Suppose that there exists a basis
\{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d},\beta_{d}\}
of

V
satisfying

f(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k})=f(\beta_{j},\beta_{k}),\qquad f(\alpha_{j},\beta_{k})=-f(\alpha_{k},\beta_{j}),\qquad\forall j,k=1,\dots,d.

(4.32)

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the
d\times d
complex matrix with entries

\mathcal{Q}_{jk}=f(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k})+f(\beta_{j},\beta_{k})+i\left(f(\alpha_{j},\beta_{k})-f(\beta_{j},\alpha_{k})\right),\qquad\forall j,k=1,\dots,d.

Then
\mathcal{Q}
is Hermitian and

i_{+}(f)=2i_{+}(\mathcal{Q}),\qquad i_{-}(f)=2i_{-}(\mathcal{Q}),\qquad\dim\ker f=2\dim\ker\mathcal{Q},

where
i_{+}
and
i_{-}
denote the positive and negative indices of inertia of the corresponding
form/matrix. In particular,
\mbox{signature}(f)=2\mbox{signature}(\mathcal{Q})
, and

is positive definite
if and only if
\mathcal{Q}
is positive definite.

5
CAPs of existence and stability of branches of relative equilibria

We now explain how CAPs are implemented using the setting developed in sections
3

4
to
establish existence and stability of branches of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-symmetric RE of (
2.8
).

5.1
Existence

We start with a a non-degenerate
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-symmetric RE
(a_{0},\omega_{0})\in M\times\mathbb{R}
approximated numerically
by
(\overline{b}_{0},\overline{\omega}_{0})\in M_{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
a_{0}\approx\rho(\overline{b}_{0})
.
Based on the setting of subsection
3.2
, we determine a (local) continuation branch of
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-symmetric RE,
m\geq 1
, parametrised
by
\omega
, as unique zeros of the function
F
defined by (
3.15
) as follows.
Consider a small interval
[\omega_{0},\omega_{1}]
and set
\omega_{s}:=s\omega_{1}+(1-s)\omega_{0}
for
s\in[0,1]
. Let
\overline{x}_{s}=(\overline{b}_{s},\overline{\lambda}_{s},\overline{\alpha}_{s})
be a numerical branch segment such that
F(\overline{b}_{s},\overline{\lambda}_{s},\overline{\alpha}_{s},\omega_{s})\approx 0
for
s\in[0,1]
. Using Theorem
2.8
, we obtain existence and rigorous bounds of a unique
\tilde{x}(\omega_{s})=(\tilde{b}(\omega_{s}),\tilde{\lambda}(\omega_{s}),\tilde{\alpha}(\omega_{s}))
satisfying

F(\tilde{b}(\omega_{s}),\tilde{\lambda}(\omega_{s}),\tilde{\alpha}(\omega_{s}),\omega_{s})=0,\quad\forall s\in[0,1].

Such branch is indeed unique in view of Theorem
3.10
. This implementation requires explicit expressions for the
gradient and hessian matrix of
F
which are easily determined except perhaps for the terms coming from the derivatives of the reduced
Hamiltonian
h
which are given in Appendix
B
.

Using interval arithmetic, we then obtain existence and rigorous bounds of the RE
(\tilde{a}(\omega_{s}),\omega_{s})\in M\times\mathbb{R}
with
\tilde{a}(\omega_{s}):=\rho(\tilde{b}(\omega_{s}))
. As one might expect, such rigorous bounds are better as the interval
[\omega_{0},\omega_{1}]
is shrunk.

5.2
Stability

Our strategy to prove that the matrix representation,
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{s})
, of
\left.d^{2}H_{\omega}(\tilde{a}(\omega_{s}))\right|_{\mathcal{N}_{a}}
is positive definite for all
s\in[0,1]
is to first determine
positive enclosures for the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{0})
. Once this is
done, we prove that
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{s})
is invertible for all
s\in[0,1]
, which is easily done by
applying interval arithmetic and the function
inv
in INTLab
to all of the blocks described in section
4.5
.
This proves that
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{s})
is positive definite for all
s\in[0,1]
as a simple continuity argument shows.
This approach avoids the computationally expensive validation of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{s})
for values of
s\neq 0
.

The framework summarised in section
4.5
gives a convenient block diagonalisation of
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{0})
with blocks

\mathcal{M}_{l}(\omega_{0})
,
l=0,\dots,\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]
, constructed in terms of
\tilde{a}(\omega_{0})
and given
in Table
4.3
.
The calculation of rigorous enclosures of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{0})
is then performed on each of the blocks
\mathcal{M}_{l}(\omega_{0})
as we explain below. We emphasise that the
block diagonalisation is useful to avoid clustering of eigenvalues. Moreover, the complexification of certain blocks is essential to deal with eigenvalues which
would appear with double multiplicity in their real form. Such clusterings or multiplicities of eigenvalues would otherwise present significant
computational challenges.

5.2.1
Validation of simple eigenvalues

To begin, we repeat the existence procedure outlined above to obtain a stronger
rigorous error bound on
\tilde{a}(\omega_{0})
. More precisely, we use Theorem
2.8
letting
s=0
instead of
s\in[0,1]
. This makes the bound
\hat{Y}
in such theorem disappear and provides a tighter enclosure on a point instead of a branch. Using this tighter bound, we compute an enclosure
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})

of the block
{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})
according to the prescription of section
4.5
.
We then compute approximate eigenvalues
(\bar{\lambda}_{1},\bar{\lambda}_{2},...,\bar{\lambda}_{\mathcal{D}_{l}})
and associated eigenvectors
(\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2},...,\bar{v}_{\mathcal{D}_{l}})
of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})
, where
\mathcal{D}_{l}
is the
size of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})
(given explicitly in Table
4.3
in terms of
m
,
n
,
p
).

For each
k\in\{1,\dots,\mathcal{D}_{l}\}
, we enclose rigorously the value of the true eigenpair
(\lambda_{k},v_{k})
close to the numerical approximation
(\bar{\lambda}_{k},\bar{v}_{k})
as follows. Let
j_{k}
be such that
|\bar{v}_{k}\cdot e_{j_{k}}|=\max_{j=1,\dots,\mathcal{D}_{l}}\left(|\bar{v}_{k}\cdot e_{j}|\right)
(note that
j_{k}
need not be unique) and consider the map

\mathcal{G}_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}+1},\quad\mathcal{G}_{l}(\lambda,v):=\begin{pmatrix}(\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})-\lambda I)v\\
(v-\bar{v}_{k})\cdot e_{j_{k}}\end{pmatrix},

(5.1)

where
e_{j_{k}}
is the
j_{k}^{th}
canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{D}_{l}}
. The last component of (
5.1
) is included to remove the scaling invariance of eigenvectors. With this definition,
an isolated zero of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
corresponds
to a simple eigenpair
(\lambda,v)
with the prescribed
j_{k}
component of the eigenvector
v
(which by
construction is guaranteed to be non zero). The requirement that the zero of
\mathcal{G}_{l}
is isolated
is only satisfied if
\lambda
is a simple eigenvalue. Under this condition, we can apply a (non uniform) version of Theorem
2.8
with
F=\mathcal{G}_{l}
and
d=\mathcal{D}_{l}+1
, to obtain rigorous bounds on the eigenpair of
(\lambda,v)
. The implementation of the method will fail in the presence of other eigenvalues close to
\lambda

(clustering).

5.2.2
Validation of clustered eigenvalues

To handle the case of clustered eigenvalues, we follow the
argument principle and validated winding number computation
technique presented in Section 3.2 of
[
18
]
.
Suppose that
\lambda_{0}
is a clustered
eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})

and consider the following small rectangle centred around
\lambda_{0}
in the complex plane

R_{\epsilon}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|Re(z)-\lambda_{0}|\leq\epsilon,\quad|Im(z)|\leq 1\},

where
\epsilon>0
will be chosen small enough to obtain tight bounds and
such that no other eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(\omega_{0})
lies on the boundary
\Gamma
of
R_{\epsilon}
. Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be the complex polynomial
\mathcal{G}(z):=\det(\mathcal{M}_{l}(\omega_{0})-zI)
. By Cauchy’s argument principle,

\#zeros=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}\frac{{\mathcal{G}}^{\prime}(z)}{{\mathcal{G}}(z)}dz,

where
\#zeros
denotes the number of zeros of
{\mathcal{G}}
inside the contour
\Gamma
. Considering that these
zeros are the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{l}(\omega_{0})
, applying the substitution
u={\mathcal{G}}(z)
, we obtain

\#eigenvalues=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{{\mathcal{G}}(\Gamma)}\frac{du}{u}.

Therefore, the number of eigenvalues inside
R_{\epsilon}
is exactly the winding number of the curve
{\mathcal{G}}(\Gamma)
about 0. To rigorously compute this, we first naturally separate
\Gamma
into its 4 linear segments
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\Gamma_{3},\Gamma_{4}
, each parameterized by a parameter
s
. Then we create a fine mesh of each
\Gamma_{i}
. For every segment of these meshes, denoted by
\Gamma^{*}
, we use interval arithmetic to compute a rigorous enclosure
{\tilde{\mathcal{G}}(\Gamma^{*})}
of
{\mathcal{G}}(\Gamma^{*})
. With all of these enclosures, we then count the number of times they intersect the positive real axis of the complex plane. To determine if the count goes up or down by 1 at
an intersection, we use interval arithmetic to compute an enclosure of the imaginary part of

\frac{d}{ds}\mathcal{G}(\gamma(s))
where
\gamma(s)
is the parametrisation of
\Gamma^{*}
.

For

Our conjectured ground state for

\pm z_{0}\approx\pm 0.703111

makes an angle of

For

\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{2\sqrt{106}-19}

Appendix A
Geometric preliminaries

The phase space
M
of the system is the open subset of
(S^{2})^{N}=S^{2}\times\dots\times S^{2}
(
N
copies) obtained by
removing the collision configurations.
The component
v_{j}
of the element
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in M
specifies the position of the

j^{th}
vortex. Throughout the paper we represent
(S^{2})^{N}
as the embedded submanifold
in
(S^{2})^{N}
specified by
(S^{2})^{N}:=R^{-1}(0)
where
R:=(R_{1},\dots,R_{N}):(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

R_{j}:(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(\|x_{j}\|^{2}-1).

This embedding induces a convenient identification of tangent vectors to
M
with vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
.
Specifically, if
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})\in M
we identify

T_{v}M=V_{v}:=\{\,{\bf w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\,:\,v_{j}\cdot w_{j}=0\,\},

where
\cdot
is the euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Let
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
. The differential of
f
at
v\in M
is the linear map
df(v):V_{v}\to\mathbb{R}

defined intrinsically by

df(v)({\bf w})=L_{X}f(v),\qquad{\bf w}\in V_{v},

where
X
is any locally defined vector field on
M
satisfying

X(v)={\bf w}
and
L_{X}
denotes the Lie derivative along
X
. Note that
df(v)\in V_{v}^{*}
.
If
\tilde{f}:(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is any smooth extension of
f
, then

df(v)({\bf w})=\langle\nabla_{x}\tilde{f}(v),{\bf w}\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the euclidean inner product in
(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}
and
\nabla_{x}\tilde{f}(v)
is
the standard gradient of
\tilde{f}
evaluated at
v\in(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}
.

A point
a\in M
is a critical point of
f
if
df(a)=0
. Hence,

a
is a critical point of

f
if and only if for any smooth extension
\tilde{f}:(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
of
f
one has

\langle\nabla_{x}\tilde{f}(a),{\bf w}\rangle=0\qquad\forall{\bf w}\in V_{a}.

In other words,
\nabla_{x}\tilde{f}(a)\in V_{a}^{\perp}
.
Considering that
V_{a}^{\perp}=\mathrm{span}\{\nabla_{x}R_{i}(a)\,:\,i=1,\dots,N\}
, we conclude that
if
a\in M
is a critical point of
f
and
\tilde{f}
is an extension then:

(a)

There
exist Lagrange multipliers
c_{1},\dots,c_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\nabla_{x}\tilde{f}(a)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}\nabla_{x}R_{i}(a)=0.

(b)

Define the function
\tilde{f}^{*}:(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\tilde{f}^{*}\left(x,\lambda\right):=\tilde{f}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}R_{i}(x),\qquad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{N},\;\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Then
\tilde{f}^{*}
has a critical point at
(a,c)
, with
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{N})
.

Continue to assume that
a\in M
is a critical point of
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
. The second derivative of

f
at
a
is the symmetric bilinear form
d^{2}f(a):V_{a}\times V_{a}\to\mathbb{R}
defined intrinsically by

d^{2}f(a)({\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2})=L_{X_{1}}L_{X_{2}}f(a)=L_{X_{2}}L_{X_{1}}f(a),

where
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are any locally defined vector fields on
M
satisfying

X_{1}(a)={\bf w}_{1}
,
X_{2}(a)={\bf w}_{2}
.
With the definitions introduced above we have

d^{2}f(a)({\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2})={\bf w}_{1}^{T}\nabla^{2}_{x}\tilde{f}^{*}(a,c){\bf w}_{2},\qquad\forall{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in V_{a},

where
\nabla^{2}_{x}\tilde{f}^{*}(a,c)
denotes the standard Hessian matrix
of
x\mapsto\tilde{f}^{*}(x,c)
evaluated at
x=a
.

Appendix B
Computation of the gradient and Hessian matrix of the reduced
Hamiltonian

Below we give explicit expressions for the gradient and Hessian matrix of
the reduced Hamiltonian
h
given by (
3.4
) interpreted as function

h:\mathbb{R}^{3n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Throughout this appendix we simplify the notation and
write
g:=g_{m}\in SO(3)
and denote by

the
3\times 3
identity matrix.

B.1
Gradient
\nabla_{u}h(u)
.

The gradient of the reduced Hamiltonian
h
has components

\nabla_{u_{j}}h(u)=-m\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j}}{\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}}-m\sum_{j^{\prime}=1(j^{\prime}\neq j)}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}}{\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}}-m\sum_{f\in F}\frac{u_{j}-f}{\left\|u_{j}-f\right\|^{2}}.

(B.1)

To see this, write
M:=I-g^{i}
and compute:

\nabla_{u}\ln\left\|Mu\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\left\|Mu\right\|^{2}}\nabla_{u}\left\langle Mu,Mu\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\left\|Mu\right\|^{2}}\nabla_{u}\left\langle M^{T}Mu,u\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\left\|Mu\right\|^{2}}\left(2M^{T}M\right)u.

Since
M^{T}M=(I-g^{-i})+(I-g^{i})
, we conclude

\nabla_{u_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\ln\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}=2\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{(I-g^{-i})u+(I-g^{i})u}{\left\|\left(I-g^{i}\right)u\right\|^{2}}=4\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{(I-g^{i})u}{\left\|\left(I-g^{i}\right)u\right\|^{2}}.

Combining these identities with (
3.4
) proves (
B.1
).

B.2
Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}h(u)
.

We now describe the Hessian matrix

in terms of the

blocks

In this case we have:

(B.2)

To prove (
B.2
), start with (

) and use the

\begin{split}D_{u_{j^{\prime}}}\nabla_{u_{j}}h(u)&=m\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{i}D_{u_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{-i}u_{j}}{\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{-i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}}=m\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{-i}D_{u_{j^{\prime}}}\frac{u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}}{\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}}\\
&=m\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{-i}\left(\frac{I}{\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}}-\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{-4}\left(u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right)\left(D_{u_{j^{\prime}}}\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}\right)\right).\end{split}

Since

\displaystyle D_{u_{j^{\prime}}}\nabla_{u_{j}}h(u)

\displaystyle m\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{-i}\frac{1}{\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{4}}\left(I\left\|u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right\|^{2}-2\left(u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right)\left(u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{i}u_{j}\right)^{T}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{i}\frac{1}{\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}\right\|^{4}}\left(I\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}-2\left(u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{-i}u_{j}\right)\left(u_{j^{\prime}}-g^{-i}u_{j}\right)^{T}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\sum_{i=1}^{m}g^{i}\frac{1}{\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}\right\|^{4}}\left(I\left\|u_{j}-g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}-2g^{-i}\left(g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}-u_{j}\right)\left(g^{i}u_{j^{\prime}}-u_{j}\right)^{T}g^{i}\right),

which simplifies to (

Case
