Geometrical torque on magnetic moments coupled to a correlated antiferromagnet
Nicolas Lenzing, David Krüger, Michael Potthoff

Abstract
The geometrical spin torque mediates an indirect interaction of magnetic moments, which are weakly exchange coupled to a system of itinerant electrons. It originates from a finite spin-Berry curvature and leads to a non-Hamiltonian magnetic-moment dynamics. We demonstrate that there is an unprecedentedly strong geometrical spin torque in case of an electron system, where correlations cause antiferromagnetic long-range order. The key observation is that the anomalous torque is strongly boosted by low-energy magnon modes emerging in the two-electron spin-excitation spectrum due to spontaneous breaking of SU(2) spin-rotation symmetry. As long as single-electron excitations are gapped out, the effect is largely universal, i.e., essentially independent of the details of the electronic structure, but decisively dependent on the lattice dimension and spatial and spin anisotropies. Analogous to the reasoning that leads to the Mermin-Wagner theorem, there is a lower critical dimension at and below which the spin-Berry curvature diverges.



Introduction.

A magnetic moment coupled to a system of itinerant electrons via a local exchange interaction of strength
J
experiences a spin torque which leads to precession dynamics.
For several magnetic moments
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
(with
m=1,...,M
), usually described as classical fixed-length spins, there are further torques caused by, e.g., indirect exchange interactions mediated by the electron system.
These
Hamiltonian
spin torques, well known in micromagnetics
Bertotti et al. 2009
and in the theory of coupled spin-electron dynamics
Koshibae et al. 2009
;
Bhattacharjee et al. 2012
;
Evans et al. 2014
;
Sayad and Potthoff 2015
;
Sayad et al. 2016
;
Chern et al. 2018
;
Bajpai and Nikolic 2019
, all derive from interaction terms in the quantum-classical Hamiltonian
Elze 2012
for the spin and electron degrees of freedom.
In addition, there is a non-Hamiltonian spin torque that has a purely
geometric
nature.
This geometrical spin torque represents the feedback of the Berry physics
Berry 1984
on the classical magnetic-moment dynamics.

Generally, such feedback effects have been pointed out early
Kuratsuji and Iida 1985
;
Moody et al. 1986
;
Zygelman 1987
but have not been studied in spin dynamics theory until recently
Stahl and Potthoff 2017
.
For weak
J
compared to the typical energy scales of the electron system, the classical spin dynamics is slow, such that the electron system accumulates a geometrical phase which is gauge independent in case of a cyclic motion
Berry 1984
;
Simon 1983
;
Wilczek and Zee 1984
.
This Berry phase is closely related to the Berry curvature, a two-form which, when integrated in classical parameter space over a two-dimensional surface bounded by a closed path
{\cal C}
yields the Berry phase associated with
{\cal C}
.
For example, in molecular physics
Bohm et al. 2003
and when treating the coordinates of the nuclei classically, the feedback of the Berry physics produces an additional geometrical force, where the Berry curvature plays the role of a magnetic field in the nuclei equations of motion.
This effect is known as “geometrical magnetism”
Berry and Robbins 1993
;
Campisi et al. 2012
.

The geometrical spin torque resulting from the spin-Berry curvature (SBC)
Stahl and Potthoff 2017
is the analogous concept in the field of atomistic spin dynamics
Skubic et al. 2008
;
Evans et al. 2014
.
As opposed to the closely related geometrical friction term
Berry and Robbins 1993
;
Campisi et al. 2012
, i.e., Gilbert damping
llg
, it is energy conserving.
But, importantly, the SBC is non-Hamiltonian and emerges for weak
J
, i.e., in the limit of slow classical spin dynamics.
However, the effects are typically weak
Michel and Potthoff 2022
for a solid
Ihm 1991
, such that it appears difficult to disentangle the effect of the geometrical spin torque from other contributions
Bajpai and Nikolić 2020
.

In this Letter we study the geometrical spin torque for magnetic moments coupled to a magnetic solid: a correlated
D
-dimensional antiferromagnetic (AF) insulator.
This is a generic situation realized, e.g., by magnetic impurities in the bulk or by magnetic adatoms on the surface of the antiferromagnet.
We demonstrate that the magnitude of the SBC is governed by the magnon-excitation spectrum.
This has very general consequences:
the SBC must diverge for
D=1
but is regular for
D\geq 3
, see Tab.
1
.
For
D=2
the SBC generically exhibits a logarithmic divergence as a function of any perturbation causing a gap in the magnon dispersion, such as magnetic anisotropies or external magnetic fields.
The magnitude of the SBC and thus the impact on the magnetic-moment dynamics is studied for the Hubbard model at half-filling and zero temperature as a prototype of a correlation-induced insulator.

Time-reversal symmetry (TRS).

Within adiabatic spin-dynamics theory
Stahl and Potthoff 2017
;
Michel and Potthoff 2022
, the geometrical spin torque is obtained from the SBC of the electron system, see Eq. (
2
) below.
Importantly, a finite SBC generally requires TRS breaking in the electron system
Michel and Potthoff 2022
.
If
J
is strong, as assumed in Ref.
Stahl and Potthoff 2017
, TRS is broken by the classical spin moment itself, as this acts like a local symmetry-breaking field.
TRS breaking can be waived only at the cost of working with a non-Abelian extension of the theory well beyond the adiabatic limit
Lenzing et al. 2022
, where the dynamics is governed by the generically finite non-Abelian spin-Berry curvature.
Another approach is to replace the electron system by an entirely classical model composed of “slow” and “fast” spin moments
Elbracht et al. 2020
;
Michel and Potthoff 2021
.
This circumvents the necessity of TRS breaking altogether but still exhibits the feedback of holonomy effects in purely classical systems
Hannay 1985
.
For magnetic moments coupled to
quantum
systems and in the physically relevant weak-
J
regime, a finite SBC can be achieved with an external magnetic field, or with a (staggered) orbital field as considered recently
Michel and Potthoff 2022
with the Haldane model
Haldane 1988
as a prototype of a TRS-breaking Chern insulator
Bernevig 2013
.
However, fine tuning of the parameters is required to achieve considerable effects
Michel and Potthoff 2022
.
Here we consider an electron system in which correlations induce a TRS-breaking AF state.
The AF order not only enables a finite SBC but also strongly boosts its magnitude due to magnon modes in the spin-excitation spectrum.

Dynamics of magnetic moments.

We are interested in the slow dynamics of
M
magnetic moments, described as classical spins
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
of unit length, which are coupled to a correlated electron system with Hamiltonian
H_{\rm el}
via a local exchange interaction

H_{\rm int}=J\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
.
Here,
i_{m}
is the site, at which the
m
-th moment is coupled to, and
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}=1/2\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}c_{i\sigma^{\prime}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is the vector of Pauli matrices, is the local spin moment at site
i
of the electron system.
The total Hamiltonian is
H=H({\boldsymbol{S}})=H_{\rm el}+H_{\rm int}({\boldsymbol{S}})
and depends on the configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}=({\boldsymbol{S}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m})
of the magnetic moments.

Assuming that the electron system at any instant of time
t
is in its instantaneous ground state for the spin configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}(t)
, i.e.,
|\Psi(t)\rangle=|\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\rangle
, the equation of motion of adiabatic spin dynamics is given by
Stahl and Potthoff 2017
;
Michel and Potthoff 2022

\dot{{\boldsymbol{S}}}_{m}=({\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{(\rm H)}+{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(geo)})\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}\>.

(1)

Here
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(H)}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
with
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(H)}=\partial\langle H({\boldsymbol{S}})\rangle/\partial{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}=J\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i_{m}}\rangle
is the conventional (Hamiltonian) spin torque, where
\langle\cdots\rangle
is the instantaneous ground-state expectation value.

Geometrical spin torque.

The second term, the geometrical spin torque
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(geo)}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
is necessary to enforce the constraint
|\Psi(t)\rangle=|\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{S}}(t))\rangle
and has been derived within a quantum-classical Lagrange formalism in Refs.
Stahl and Potthoff 2017
;
Michel and Potthoff 2022
.
This assumes that the ground state is non-degenerate (otherwise non-Abelian spin-dynamics theory
Lenzing et al. 2022
must be used) and that
J
is sufficiently weak so that the classical spin dynamics is much slower than typical relaxation time scales of the quantum system
H_{\rm el}
.
Alternatively, the term may be derived within adiabatic response theory
Berry and Robbins 1992
;
Berry and Robbins 1993
;
Campisi et al. 2012
as the first nontrivial correction in a systematic expansion of the response of a driven system with respect to the driving speed, when applied to spin dynamics
Lenzing and Potthoff 2023
.
It is given by

{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(geo)}=\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}({\boldsymbol{S}})\dot{S}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\alpha}\;,

(2)

with
\alpha=x,y,z
and the
\alpha
-th unit vector
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\alpha}
, and where

\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{S}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{m\alpha}}A_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{S}})-\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}}A_{m\alpha}({\boldsymbol{S}})

(3)

is the spin-Berry curvature. At each spin configuration
{\boldsymbol{S}}
, this is a real antisymmetric tensor
(
\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}=-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}
), which is invariant under local gauge transformations of the ground states
|\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{S}})\rangle\mapsto e^{i\phi({\boldsymbol{S}})}|\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{S}})\rangle
.
It is the exterior derivative of the spin-Berry connection
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{m}=i\langle\Psi_{0}|\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, which describes parallel transport of the ground state
|\Psi_{0}({\boldsymbol{S}})\rangle
on the manifold of spin configurations
{\cal M}
.
For
M
classical spins
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}\in S^{2}
, this is given by the
M
-fold Cartesian product of 2-spheres
{\cal M}\equiv S^{2}\times\cdots\times S^{2}
.

Spontaneous antiferromagnetic order.

We consider a coupling of the magnetic spin moments to the single-band Hubbard model
Gebhard 1997
;
Essler et al. 2005
on a
D
-dimensional hypercubic lattice as a prototypical model for itinerant magnetic order.
Its Hamiltonian is
H_{\rm el}=-t\sum_{ij}^{\rm{n.n.}}\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{i\sigma}c_{j\sigma}+U\sum_{i}n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}
, where the nearest-neighbor hopping
t=1
fixes the energy and (with
\hbar\equiv 1
) the time scales.

c_{i\sigma}
annihilates an electron at site
i
with spin projection
\sigma
, and
n_{i\sigma}=c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\sigma}
.
The sums over
i,j
are restricted to nearest neighbors, and
L
is the total number of sites.
It is well known
Anderson 1950
;
Schrieffer et al. 1989
;
Manousakis 1991
;
Auerbach 1994
;
Schäfer et al. 2015

that at half-filling, repulsive Hubbard-
U
and for
D\geq 2
, the ground state of the system in the thermodynamical limit
L\to\infty
develops long-range AF correlations.
SU(2) spin-rotation symmetry and therewith TRS are spontaneously broken, and the ordered state is characterized by a finite staggered magnetization
{\boldsymbol{m}}=m{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
with
m=L^{-1}\sum_{i}z_{i}\langle n_{i\uparrow}-n_{i\downarrow}\rangle
and
z_{i}=\pm 1
for
i
in sublattice A or B, respectively.
We assume
m>0
for sublattice A.

At weak
U
, AF order is driven by the Slater mechanism and perturbatively accessible
Schrieffer et al. 1989
;
Auerbach 1994
.
Within self-consistent Hartree-Fock theory
Moriya 1985
, the one-electron excitation spectrum displays a gap
\Delta=Um
at wave vector
Q=(\pi,\pi,...)
in the conventional Brillouin zone.
The two-electron spin-excitation spectrum is well described by standard random-phase approximation (RPA) but for the symmetry-broken AF state
Rowe et al. 2012
;
Del Re and Toschi 2021
;
Luttinger and Ward 1960
;
Rohringer et al. 2018
.

In the strong-
U
limit, the one-electron spectrum is dominated by a large Hubbard gap
\Delta\sim U
and well developed local spin moments, coupled via Anderson’s superexchange
Anderson 1950
;
Auerbach 1994
.
Here, the model maps onto the Heisenberg spin-1/2 Hamiltonian with AF exchange
J_{\rm H}=4t^{2}/U
and AF long-range order, see Refs.
Chakravarty et al. 1989
;
Manousakis 1991
;
Sandvik 1997
, for example.
To compute the low-energy magnon dispersion and states, we can apply spin-wave theory (SWT)
Anderson 1952
to the AF Heisenberg model and use the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
Holstein and Primakoff 1940
at linear order.
Linear SWT is motivated by the fact that single-magnon decay requires overlap with the two-magnon continuum, so that the picture of a stable magnon gas is protected by kinematic restrictions at low energies
Hamer et al. 1992
;
Chernyshev and Maksimov 2016
;
McClarty et al. 2018
;
McClarty 2022
.

Spin-Berry curvature of an antiferromagnet.

To compute the geometrical spin torque, we make use of a Lehmann-type representation of the SBC starting from Eq. (
3
).
This is straightforwardly derived
Michel and Potthoff 2022
using a resolution of the the unity,
{\boldsymbol{1}}=\sum_{n}|\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{S}})\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}({\boldsymbol{S}})|
, with an orthonormal basis of instantaneous eigenstates of
H_{\rm el}+H_{\rm int}({\boldsymbol{S}})
:

\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-2J^{2}\mbox{Im}\!\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i_{m}}^{\alpha}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\,\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i_{m^{\prime}}}^{\alpha^{\prime}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{(E_{n}-E_{0})^{2}}\>.

(4)

Note that, due to the
J^{2}
prefactor, the
{\boldsymbol{S}}
dependence of the eigenenergies and eigenstates will provide corrections to Eq. (
4
) only at order
J^{3}
.
As we refer to the weak-
J
limit, these will be neglected in the following.

In the AF phase and assuming that the order parameter is aligned to the
z
axis,
\langle{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}\rangle=(-1)^{i}m{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
, there is a remaining SO(2) symmetry of the energy eigenstates under spin rotations around
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
.
This unbroken spin-rotation symmetry, together with the spatial inversion and translation symmetries of
H_{\rm el}
, and the antisymmetry
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}
[see Eq. (
3
)] imply that the spin-Berry curvature tensor is entirely fixed by a single real number
\Omega\equiv\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},yx}
for each fixed pair of sites
i_{m}
,
i_{m^{\prime}}
.
All other elements must vanish, as is detailed by the symmetry analysis in Sections A and B of the Supplemental Material (SM)
SM
.

In a first step, for weak
U
, we compute the SBC via

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}=-iJ^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\chi_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}},xy}(\omega)\Big|_{\omega=0}+{\cal O}(J^{3})\>,

(5)

where
\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)=L^{-1}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i^{\prime}})}\chi_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)
is the real-space retarded susceptibility, obtained by the RPA (see SM, Sec. C
SM
).
The relation Eq. (
5
) is easily derived by comparing the representation Eq. (
4
) of the SBC with the Lehmann representation of the susceptibility (SM, Secs. A, B
SM
).
Therewith, the susceptibility in the symmetry-broken AF state is seen to play a dual role for the spin dynamics:
(i) via Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
2
) its frequency derivative at
\omega=0
yields the geometrical spin torque
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(geo)}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
, and
(ii) the static susceptibility yields, in the weak-
J
regime, the conventional RKKY spin torque

{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(H)}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
with
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{m}^{\rm(H)}=\partial H_{\rm RKKY}/\partial{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}
,
where
H_{\rm RKKY}=J^{2}\sum\chi_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega=0)S_{m\alpha}S_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}
is the perturbative RKKY Hamiltonian of the AF state.

Goldstone theorem, implications.

In our second step, we exploit the fact that the spin-excitation spectrum of an AF insulator has a universal structure at low frequencies.
This is due to Goldstone’s theorem which enforces the presence of gapless magnon modes
Goldstone 1961
;
Nambu and Jona-Lasinio 1961
;
Brauner 2010
.
In the collinear AF state and corresponding to the two broken generators of the spin SU(2) symmetry, there are two degenerate modes with a linear and isotropic dispersion in the vicinity of the
\Gamma
point in the magnetic Brillouin zone (mBz).
Linear SWT applied to the Heisenberg model that emerges in the strong
U
limit captures this physics, i.e., the dispersion close to
\Gamma
is given by
\frac{1}{2}J_{\rm H}\omega({\boldsymbol{k}})=c_{\rm s}k+{\cal O}(k^{2})
, where
c_{\rm s}
is the spin-wave velocity.
Using the magnon energies and eigenstates, we can compute the SBC in this limit from Eq. (
4
) directly (SM, Secs. D, E
SM
), ending up with

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}=\mp\frac{2J^{2}}{J_{\rm H}^{2}}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D}}\int_{\rm mBz}\!\!d^{D}k\frac{\cos(\boldsymbol{k}({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}\!-\!{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}))}{\omega({\boldsymbol{k}})^{2}}\>,

(6)

if both,
i_{m},i_{m^{\prime}}
belong to sublattice A (
-
sign) or B (
+
sign), and
\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}=0
else.

For
D=2
, the linear dispersion close to
\Gamma
then implies a
1/k^{2}
singularity of the integrand and thus a logarithmic infrared divergence.
For
D\geq 3
, the local (
m=m^{\prime}
) SBC is finite.
We note that the same arguments as invoked for the Mermin-Wagner theorem
Anderson 1952
;
Mermin and Wagner 1966
, i.e., a divergence due to the low-energy spin excitations, here lead to a lower critical dimension (
D_{\rm c}=3
) that is shifted by one, see Tab.

.
The numerical value for the

local SBC is
\Omega_{\rm loc}\approx-0.084\,J^{2}/J_{\rm H}^{2}=-0.084\,J^{2}U^{2}/16t^{4}
.
When scaling the hopping as

with
t^{\ast}=\mbox{const}

;
Müller-Hartmann 1989

, and the SBC approaches a finite mean-field value

for

(SM, Sec. F

).

Magnitude of the SBC.

SWT predicts a
U^{2}
dependence of the SBC in the Heisenberg limit for strong
U
.
For
U=0
, on the other hand, TRS of the resulting paramagnetic state implies that it must vanish.
For
U\to 0
, there is an intricate competition between the exponential suppression of the order parameter
m\propto e^{-1/U}
, i.e., of the “strength” of TRS breaking and thus of the SBC and, on the other hand, the exponential closure of the single-electron Slater gap
\Delta=Um
and thus of the onset of the continuum in the spin-excitation spectrum resulting in continuum contributions that favor a large SBC.
Our numerical results for the local SBC in
D=3
, as obtained from weak-coupling RPA and strong-coupling SWT are displayed in Fig.
2
.
With increasing
U
we find a smooth crossover from the Slater to the Heisenberg limit with a monotonically increasing
|\Omega_{\rm loc}|
.

The nonlocal SBC at large distances
R\equiv\|{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}\|
is again governed by the linear dispersion at low frequencies.
Carrying out the integration in Eq. (
6
) for

\Omega(R)\propto 1/R^{D-2}
(see Tab.
1
and SM, Sec. F
SM
).
For

Geometrical spin dynamics.

For the AF ordered phase, Eq. (
1
) tells us that the dominating effect in the magnetic-moment dynamics is a precession around the staggered magnetization
{\boldsymbol{m}}
on a time scale
1/J
.
This effect dominates the weaker (and slower) anisotropic RKKY-type exchange on the scale
J^{2}
.
Importantly, the SBC
\Omega\sim J^{2}
enters the equations of motion as a renormalization
factor
(for
M>1
classical spins as a matrix factor) rather than a summand and thus does
not compete
with the stronger direct exchange of order
J

(SM, Sec. G
SM
).
For
M=1
this factor amounts to
1/(1-\Omega_{\rm loc}S_{z})
, such that the most pronounced effects are found for a SBC of intermediate strength,
\Omega_{\rm loc}={\cal O}(1)
.
This holds true for
M=2
as well, as is detailed in the SM, Sec. G
SM
.
Note that a singular renormalization indicates a breakdown of the theory as this is the point where the condition for nearly adiabatic spin dynamics is invalidated.
Note further that the precession comes with an inverted orientation beyond the singular point.

Conclusions.

A hitherto unknown but generic interplay of electron correlations, spontaneous symmetry breaking, gapless Goldstone bosons, and a holonomy on the configuration space of classical spin degrees of freedom leads to non-Hamiltonian effects, such as renormalization of precession frequencies, inverted orientation of the precessional motion, or long-range interactions, in the spin dynamics.
This is due to a geometrical spin torque which is finite for correlated AF ground states in lattice models with dimension
D\geq 3
and diverges for
D\leq 2
, caused by the same mechanism that leads to the Mermin-Wagner theorem, however, shifted by one dimension.
With a SBC
\Omega_{\rm loc}={\cal O}(1)
for typical parameters, the effect is unexpectedly large.
It is boosted by electron correlations and further enhanced by spatial and by spin anisotropies.
We expect a strong overall impact on the phenomenology of atomistic spin dynamics.

Section A: SO(2) symmetry analysis.

The retarded spin susceptibility is defined as

\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(t)=-i\Theta(t)\langle[s_{i\alpha}(t),s_{i^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}(0)]\rangle\>,

(7)

where
\Theta
is the step function,
s_{i\alpha}(t)=e^{iH_{\rm el}t}s_{i\alpha}e^{-iH_{\rm el}t}
, and
\langle\cdots\rangle
is the ground-state expectation value.
Fourier transformation to frequency space yields the Lehmann representation in terms of an energy eigenbasis
\{|\Psi_{n}\rangle\}
:

\displaystyle\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\Bigg(\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i\alpha}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{\omega+i\eta-(E_{n}-E_{0})}

(8)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i\alpha}|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{\omega+i\eta-(E_{0}-E_{n})}\Bigg)\>.

We have the relation
\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)^{\ast}=\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(-\omega)
.
The spectral density
-(1/\pi)\mbox{Im}\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)=\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)-\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(-\omega)/2i
is an antisymmetric function of
\omega
.

In the AF phase with order parameter
{\boldsymbol{m}}=m{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
, there is a remaining SO(2) symmetry of the nondegenerate energy eigenstates under spin rotations around
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
, which is unitarily represented by
U_{\rm R}=e^{-is_{\rm tot,z}\varphi}
on the Fock space with the
z
-component of the total spin
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\rm tot}=\sum_{i}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
as the unbroken generator and the rotation angle
\varphi
.
We have
U_{\rm R}^{\dagger}|\Psi_{n}\rangle=e^{i\phi_{n}}|\Psi_{n}\rangle
with phases
\phi_{n}
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{i}
is a vector operator, we have
U_{\rm R}^{\dagger}s_{i\alpha}U_{\rm R}=\sum_{\beta}R_{\alpha\beta}s_{i\beta}
, where
\underline{R}=\underline{R}(\varphi)
is the standard real
3\times 3
matrix representation of SO(2) rotations around
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}
.
Hence, the first matrix element in Eq. (
4
) can be written as

\langle\Psi_{0}|U_{\rm R}U_{\rm R}^{\dagger}s_{i\alpha}U_{\rm R}U_{\rm R}^{\dagger}|\Psi_{n}\rangle=\sum_{\beta}R_{\alpha\beta}(\varphi)e^{i\phi_{0}}\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i\beta}|\Psi_{n}\rangle e^{-i\phi_{n}}
.
The phase factors cancel with those from the second matrix element, and we thus find:

\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)=\sum_{\beta\beta^{\prime}}R_{\alpha\beta}(\varphi)\chi_{ii^{\prime},\beta\beta^{\prime}}(\omega)R^{T}_{\beta^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}(\varphi)
, i.e.,
[\underline{\chi}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega),\underline{R}(\varphi)]=0
for all
i,i^{\prime}
and
\varphi
.
It is easily verified directly that this implies

\underline{\chi}_{ii^{\prime}}(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{ii^{\prime},xx}(\omega)&\chi_{ii^{\prime},xy}(\omega)&0\\
-\chi_{ii^{\prime},xy}(\omega)&\chi_{ii^{\prime},xx}(\omega)&0\\
0&0&\chi_{ii^{\prime},zz}(\omega)\\
\end{pmatrix}\>,

(9)

i.e., there are only 3 independent entries for each pair
i,i^{\prime}

(note that
R
is reducible, and furthermore Schur’s lemma does not apply to representations over
\mathbb{R}
).

Section B: Spatial symmetries.

With Eq. (
8
), we immediately see that the spin-Berry curvature is related to the spin susceptibility via

\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-iJ^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\chi_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)\Big|_{\omega=0}

(10)

up to correction terms of order
J^{3}
, see Eq. (
5
).
Therefore, the same reasoning as above can be applied to the spin-Berry curvature tensor and yields the same result for its structure:

\underline{\Omega}_{mm^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xx}&\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}&0\\
-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}&\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xx}&0\\
0&0&\Omega_{mm^{\prime},zz}\\
\end{pmatrix}\>.

(11)

In addition, for a given pair of sites
i_{m}
and
i_{m^{\prime}}
, we may consider a combined transformation
T\circ I
, composed of the space inversion
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}
with respect to
i_{m}
followed by the translation
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}\mapsto{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}+({\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})
with the translation vector
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}
.

T\circ I
is a discrete symmetry of the hypercubic lattice and interchanges
i_{m}
with
i_{m^{\prime}}
.
This implies that the Hamiltonian commutes with the standard unitary (and also Hermitian) representation
U_{\rm TI}
of
T\circ I
.

For
i_{m}
and
i_{m^{\prime}}
in the same sublattice, the symmetry-broken ground state is an eigenstate of
U_{\rm TI}
as well.
Analogously to the SO(2) spin-rotation symmetry discussed above, we can thus immediately see from the analysis of the matrix elements in Eq. (
4
) that
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}
.
For the spin-Berry curvature we have the additional antisymmetry,
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}
, which follows from Eq. (
3
).
With this we get
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}
, i.e., for each pair
m,m^{\prime}
, the spin-Berry curvature tensor is antisymmetric in the indices
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
separately.
Hence, with Eq. (
11
), we see that only the elements
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},yx}
can be nonzero.
Analogously, for the spin susceptibility, the
T\circ I
symmetry of the Hamiltonian implies
\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)=\chi_{i^{\prime}i,\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(\omega)
.
With the additional symmetry,
\chi_{ii^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}(0)=\chi_{i^{\prime}i,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}(0)
, which follows from Eq. (
8
), this implies that the susceptibility matrix Eq. (
9
) is diagonal for
\omega=0
.

For
i_{m}
and
i_{m^{\prime}}
in different sublattices, we concatenate the transformation
T\circ I
with a flip
F
of the
z
-component of all spins.
This is unitarily represented by
U_{\rm F}
, which is defined via
U^{\dagger}_{\rm F}c_{i\uparrow}U_{\rm F}=c_{i\downarrow}
and
U^{\dagger}_{\rm F}c_{i\downarrow}U_{\rm F}=c_{i\uparrow}
.
We have
[U_{\rm F},U_{\rm TI}]=0
and
[U_{\rm F},H_{\rm el}]=0
.
The symmetry-broken ground state and the corresponding excited states are eigenstates of
U\equiv U_{\rm TI}U_{\rm F}
.
Hence, we find for the
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
matrix elements

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i_{m}\alpha}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i_{m^{\prime}}\alpha}|\Psi_{0}\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|U^{\dagger}s_{i_{m}\alpha}U|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|U^{\dagger}s_{i_{m^{\prime}}\alpha}U|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i_{m^{\prime}}\alpha}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i_{m}\alpha}|\Psi_{0}\rangle\>,

and thus
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha}=\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha\alpha}
.
With the antisymmetry of the full tensor,
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}
, we thus find

\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha}=0
.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i_{m}x}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i_{m^{\prime}}y}|\Psi_{0}\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|U^{\dagger}s_{i_{m}x}U|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|U^{\dagger}s_{i_{m^{\prime}}y}U|\Psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle\Psi_{0}|s_{i_{m^{\prime}}x}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|s_{i_{m}y}|\Psi_{0}\rangle\>,

since
U_{\rm F}^{\dagger}s_{ix}U_{\rm F}=s_{ix}
but
U_{\rm F}^{\dagger}s_{iy}U_{\rm F}=-s_{iy}
.
This implies
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=-\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,xy}
, and with the antisymmetry of the full tensor we find
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=\Omega_{mm^{\prime},yx}
.
Together with Eq. (
11
), we see that
\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=0
, and hence the matrix
\underline{\Omega}_{mm^{\prime}}=0
in Eq. (
11
).

Summing up, for arbitrary sites
i_{m}
and
i_{m^{\prime}}
we have

\underline{\Omega}_{mm^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Omega&0\\
-\Omega&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\>,

(14)

and hence the spin-Berry curvature is fixed by a single real number
\Omega\equiv\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,xy}
.
Furthermore,
\Omega=0
if
i_{m},i_{m^{\prime}}
belong to different sublattices.

Section C: Random phase approximation.

The random phase approximation (RPA) represents a standard weak-coupling approach to the magnetic susceptibility, see, e.g., Refs.
Rowe et al. 2012
;
Del Re and Toschi 2021
.
It can be motivated in various ways, for example, via a partial diagrammatic summation.
In general, the RPA Luttinger-Ward functional
\Phi[{\boldsymbol{G}}]

Luttinger and Ward 1960
;
Rohringer et al. 2018
is given as the sum of the two closed and self-consistently renormalized first-order diagrams, i.e., by the Hartree and the Fock diagram.
For the Hubbard model the Fock diagram vanishes such that we are left with

\Phi[{\boldsymbol{G}}]=U\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}G_{ii,\uparrow}(i\omega_{n})G_{ii,\downarrow}(i\omega_{n^{\prime}})\>.

(15)

Here,
i
runs over the sites of the hypercubic lattice,
\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow
refers to the spin projection relative to the
z
axis,
n
labels the fermionic Matsubara frequencies
i\omega_{n}
, and
\beta
is the inverse temperature.
Computations are done in the zero-temperature limit
1/\beta\to 0
, which is taken at the end.
Furthermore,
G_{ii,\sigma}
denotes the local one-particle Green’s function at site
i
in the symmetry-broken AF state, as obtained within the self-consistent Hartree-Fock approximation.
The Hartree-Fock self-energy is generated by the Luttinger-Ward functional:

\Sigma_{ii,\sigma}(i\omega_{n})=\beta\delta\Phi/\delta G_{ii\sigma}(i\omega_{n})=U\beta^{-1}\sum_{n}G_{ii,-\sigma}(i\omega_{n})=U\langle c^{\dagger}_{i-\sigma}c_{i-\sigma}\rangle
.

On the two-particle level, the RPA yields a local and frequency-independent irreducible vertex

\Gamma^{\rm(loc)}_{\sigma,-\sigma}(i\omega_{n},i\omega_{n^{\prime}})=\beta^{2}\frac{\delta^{2}\Phi}{\delta G_{ii,-\sigma}(i\omega_{n})\delta G_{ii,\sigma}(i\omega_{n^{\prime}})}=U\>.

(16)

This means that there is no feedback of two-particle correlations on the single-particle Green’s function.
The structureless vertex allows us to easily get the transversal magnetic susceptibility

\chi_{rs,+-}({\boldsymbol{k}},\omega)=\langle\langle s_{r,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{+};s_{s,{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{-}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}

as the solution of a strongly simplified Bethe-Salpether equation in the particle-hole channel:

\underline{\chi}_{+-}({\boldsymbol{k}},i\nu_{n})=\underline{\chi}^{(0)}_{+-}({\boldsymbol{k}},i\nu_{n})+\underline{\chi}^{(0)}_{+-}({\boldsymbol{k}},i\nu_{n})U\underline{\chi}_{+-}({\boldsymbol{k}},i\nu_{n})\>.

(17)

Here,
\underline{\chi}_{+-}
is a
2\times 2
matrix in the sublattice degrees of freedom, and
\underline{\chi}^{(0)}_{+-}
the bare susceptibility matrix, which is computed with the Hartree-Fock one-particle propagators.
The equation is diagonal in the wave vectors
{\boldsymbol{k}}
of the first magnetic Brillouin zone and in the bosonic Matsubara frequencies
i\nu_{n}
.
The transversal susceptibility
\chi_{+-}
is related to the susceptibility tensor
\chi_{\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}
introduced in Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
), via
\chi_{+-}=2(\chi_{xx}-i\chi_{xy})
.
From the renormalized zeroth-order diagram, we get the Hartree-Fock susceptibility in Eq. (
17
) as

\displaystyle{\chi}^{(0)}_{rs,+-}({\boldsymbol{k}},i\nu_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{L}\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{q}},n^{\prime}}G_{sr,\uparrow}({\boldsymbol{q}},i\omega_{n^{\prime}})

(18)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle G_{rs,\downarrow}({\boldsymbol{q}}+{\boldsymbol{k}},i\nu_{n}+i\omega_{n^{\prime}})\>.

After performing the summation over the fermionic frequencies
i\omega_{n}
analytically, we can replace
i\nu_{n}\mapsto\nu+i\eta
to find the retarded susceptibility on the real-frequency axis.
The frequency derivative in Eq. (
10
) is done numerically.
In the thermodynamical limit, the
{\boldsymbol{q}}
sum over the magnetic Brillouin zone in Eq. (
18
) can be converted into a
{\boldsymbol{q}}
-space integration.
The latter is computed in two or three dimensions via a standard adaptive
{\boldsymbol{q}}
-space integration technique for arbitrary
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
and for each allowed wave vector
{\boldsymbol{k}}
in the magnetic Brillouin zone of a finite lattice with
L
sites and periodic boundary conditions.
Practical computations are performed at a finite Lorentzian broadening parameter
\eta>0
replacing the infinitesimal
\eta
, and convergence with respect to
L
is controlled by runs for different system sizes
L
.
The main numerical error is due to extrapolation of the data for
\eta\to 0
.

Section D: Magnon spectrum of an antiferromagnet.

In the strong-
U
limit of the Hubbard model, the low-energy physics is captured by the
s=1/2
antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model

H=J_{\rm H}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(\frac{1}{2}(s^{+}_{i}s^{-}_{j}+s^{-}_{i}s^{+}_{j})+\Delta s^{z}_{i}s^{z}_{j}\right)

(19)

with
J_{\rm H}=4t^{2}/U
and
\Delta=1
.
An anisotropy parameter
\Delta>1
can be used to discuss the effect of opening a gap in the dispersion.
The sum runs over all nearest-neighbor pairs
\langle ij\rangle
.

We apply the standard Holstein-Primakoff transfomation for the model on the bipartite hypercubic lattice with dimension
D
.
For sites
i
in sublattice A, the spin operators are expressed in terms of bosonic annihilators and creators, i.e.,

\displaystyle s^{z}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s-a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i}\>,

\displaystyle s^{+}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{n}_{i}}{2s}}a_{i}\>,

\displaystyle s^{-}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}a^{\dagger}_{i}\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{n}_{i}}{2s}}\>,

(20)

while for sites
j\in B

\displaystyle s^{z}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-s+b^{\dagger}_{j}b_{j}\>,

\displaystyle s^{+}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}b^{\dagger}_{j}\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{n}_{j}}{2s}}\>,

\displaystyle s^{-}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}\sqrt{1-\frac{\hat{n}_{j}}{2s}}b_{j}\>.

(21)

The transformed Hamiltonian reads

H=J_{\rm H}s\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}[(a_{i}b_{j}+a^{\dagger}_{i}b^{\dagger}_{j})+\Delta(a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{i}+b^{\dagger}_{j}b_{j})]-\frac{L}{2}zJ_{\rm H}\Delta s^{2}\;,

(22)

where
z=2D
is the coordination number, and
L
is the number of lattice sites.
Quartic and higher-order magnon interaction terms resulting for the expansion of the square root have been disregarded.

We drop the additive energy constant and block-diagonalize
H
via Fourier transformation:

\displaystyle a_{i}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{i}}a_{\boldsymbol{k}}\>,\quad b_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}\>.

Here,
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i}
are the translation vectors of the magnetic A sublattice (the same for
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{j}
and the B sublattice), consisting of
L/2
unit cells, and
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is an allowed wave vector of the first magnetic Brillouin zone (mBz).
Defining
\gamma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\equiv\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\cos({\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}})
with nearest-neighbor vectors
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
, the Fourier-transformed model reads as

H=J_{\rm H}s\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}[\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}+b^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}a^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}})+z\Delta(a_{\boldsymbol{k}}a^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}+b^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}})]

(24)

and can be diagonalized by Bogoliubov transformation

a_{\boldsymbol{k}}=u_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\>,\quad b_{\boldsymbol{k}}=u_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

(25)

with real coefficients
u_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and
v_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.
We require

u^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-v^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=1\>,

(26)

to ensure that
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}
satisfy bosonic commutation relations, as well as

2z\Delta u_{\boldsymbol{k}}v_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}(u^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0\>,

(27)

as usual, to get the Hamiltonian to the form

H=J_{\rm H}s\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\omega(\boldsymbol{k})(\alpha^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\beta^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}})\>,

(28)

where we again dropped an unimportant constant energy term.
The magnon spectrum consists of two degenerate branches with dispersion
J_{\rm H}s\,\omega({\boldsymbol{k}})
given by

\omega(\boldsymbol{k})=\sqrt{(z\Delta)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}=\sqrt{z^{2}\Delta^{2}-\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\cos({\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{\delta}})\right)^{2}}\>.

(29)

Close to
{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
and in the isotropic case
\Delta=1
, the dispersion
J_{\rm H}s\,\omega({\boldsymbol{k}})
is linear,

\omega({\boldsymbol{k}})=2\sqrt{D}k+{\cal O}(k^{3})\>,

(30)

while for
\Delta>1
the spectrum is gapped, and
\omega({\boldsymbol{k}})=2S\sqrt{\Delta^{2}-1}+{\cal O}(k^{2})
.

From the conditions Eq. (
26
) and Eq. (
27
), we can deduce the well-known results

\displaystyle u^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z\Delta}{\sqrt{(z\Delta)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}}+1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z\Delta}{\omega(\boldsymbol{k})}+1\right)\>,

\displaystyle v^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z\Delta}{\sqrt{(z\Delta)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}}-1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{z\Delta}{\omega(\boldsymbol{k})}-1\right)

(31)

and

u_{\boldsymbol{k}}v_{\boldsymbol{k}}=-\frac{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{2\sqrt{(z\Delta)^{2}-\gamma^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}}=-\frac{\gamma_{\boldsymbol{k}}}{2\omega(\boldsymbol{k})}\>,

(32)

see Refs.
Anderson 1952
;
Holstein and Primakoff 1940
.

Section E: Computing the spin-Berry curvature from the magnon Hamiltonian.

The contribution of the magnon excitations to the spin-Berry curvature is obtained from

\Omega_{mm^{\prime},\alpha\alpha^{\prime}}=-2J^{2}\mbox{Im}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\eta=1,2}\frac{\langle 0|s^{\alpha}_{i_{m}}|\boldsymbol{k},\eta\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},\eta|s^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}|0\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{\boldsymbol{k}})^{2}}\>,

(33)

where
|\boldsymbol{k},1\rangle\equiv\alpha^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|0\rangle
and
|\boldsymbol{k},2\rangle\equiv\beta^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}|0\rangle
are the single-magnon states.
Following Eq. (
14
), it is sufficient to compute
\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}=\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},yx}
.
Furthermore,
\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}\neq 0
only for
i_{m}
and
i_{m^{\prime}}
in the same sublattice, as also argued in section B.
Expressing the spin components in terms of the Bogoliubov operators,

\displaystyle s^{+}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{L/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{i}}(u_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle s^{-}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{L/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{i}}(u_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle s^{+}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{L/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}(u_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle s^{-}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2s}\frac{1}{\sqrt{L/2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{R}_{j}}(u_{\boldsymbol{k}}\beta_{\boldsymbol{k}}+v_{\boldsymbol{k}}\alpha^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\>,

(34)

we find

\displaystyle\langle 0|s^{x}_{i}|\boldsymbol{k},1\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},1|s^{y}_{i^{\prime}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{s}{L}u^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i^{\prime}})}\>,

\displaystyle\langle 0|s^{x}_{i}|\boldsymbol{k},2\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},2|s^{y}_{i^{\prime}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\frac{s}{L}v^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i^{\prime}})}

(35)

for
i
,
i^{\prime}
in sublattice A, and

\displaystyle\langle 0|s^{x}_{j}|\boldsymbol{k},1\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},1|s^{y}_{j^{\prime}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\frac{s}{L}v^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j^{\prime}})}\>,

\displaystyle\langle 0|s^{x}_{j}|\boldsymbol{k},2\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},2|s^{y}_{j^{\prime}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\frac{s}{L}u^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j^{\prime}})}

(36)

for
j
,
j^{\prime}
in sublattice B.
This implies

\displaystyle\mbox{Im}{\sum_{\eta=1,2}\langle 0|s^{x}_{i}|\boldsymbol{k},\eta\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},\eta|s^{y}_{i^{\prime}}|0\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{s}{L}\cos(\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle\mbox{Im}{\sum_{\eta=1,2}\langle 0|s^{x}_{j}|\boldsymbol{k},\eta\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{k},\eta|s^{y}_{j^{\prime}}|0\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{s}{L}\cos(\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j^{\prime}}))\>,

and finally we get

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}=\mp\frac{1}{s}\frac{J^{2}}{J_{\rm H}^{2}}\frac{2}{L}\sum^{\rm mBz}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\cos(\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i_{m}}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}))}{\omega(\boldsymbol{k})^{2}}\>,

(38)

where the upper sign refers to
i_{m},i_{m^{\prime}}
in sublattice A and the lower for
i_{m},i_{m^{\prime}}
in sublattice B.
Recall that
\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}=0
if
i_{m},i_{m^{\prime}}
belong to different sublattices.

Eq. (
38
) can be evaluated numerically.
For
D=3
, for example, we find

\Omega_{\rm loc}\approx-0.084\frac{J^{2}}{J_{\rm H}^{2}}\>

(39)

for the local element of the SBC with
i_{m}=i_{m^{\prime}}
in sublattice A.

Section F: Different dimensions and distance dependence.

In the thermodynamic limit
L\to\infty
(and in the isotropic case
\Delta=1
), the convergence of the resulting integral in Eq. (
38
) over the magnetic Brillouin zone decisively depends on the lattice dimension
D
.
We consider the critical contribution of the long-wave-length magnons by integrating over a
D
-dimensional ball around
{\boldsymbol{k}}=0
with small cutoff radius
k_{\rm c}
, such that we can make use of Eq. (
30
), i.e., of the linearity and isotropy of the magnon dispersion for
k\to 0
:

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}\sim\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\int_{\kappa}^{k_{\rm c}}dk\,k^{D-1}\frac{1}{\omega(\boldsymbol{k})^{2}}\propto\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\int_{\kappa}^{k_{\rm c}}dk\,k^{D-1}\frac{1}{k^{2}}\>.

(40)

This yields

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}\sim\begin{cases}\kappa^{D-2}\quad\text{ for }D\geq 3\\
\ln\kappa\quad\text{ for }D=2\\
1/\kappa\quad\text{ for }D=1\end{cases}\>.

(41)

For
\kappa\to 0
, the spin-Berry curvature diverges for
D=1
and
D=2
.
We conclude that a meaningful theory is obtained in dimensions
D\geq 3
only.

The magnitude of the spin-Berry curvature decreases with increasing distance
{\boldsymbol{R}}\equiv{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m}}-{\boldsymbol{R}}_{i_{m^{\prime}}}
.
For
D\geq 3
its dependence in the large-
R
limit is governed by long-wave-length magnon excitations, and we have:

\Omega(R)\propto\int_{0}^{k_{c}}dk\,k^{D-1}\int d\Omega\frac{\cos(kR\cos\theta)}{k^{2}}\>,

(42)

where
\int d\Omega
denotes the surface integral over the
(D-1)
-dimensional unit sphere, and
\theta
the angle between
{\boldsymbol{k}}
and
{\boldsymbol{R}}
.
Furthermore, we made use of Eq. (
30
) for
k
smaller than the cutoff
k_{\rm c}
.
We note that the distance dependence at large
R
is isotropic.
Substituting
kR\to k
in the one-dimensional
k
integral immediately yields

\Omega(R)\propto\frac{1}{R^{D-2}}\>.

(43)

For
D=3
, we have
\Omega(R)\propto 1/R
.
In the infinite-
D
limit, we expect a local spin-Berry curvature

To compute the local element
m=m^{\prime}
of the spin-Berry curvature Eq. (
38
) in this limit, we start from the representation

\Omega_{\rm loc}=-\frac{1}{s}\frac{J^{2}}{J_{\rm H}^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\rho_{D}(x)\frac{1}{z^{2}\Delta^{2}-Dx^{2}}\>,

(44)

where, for dimension
D
, we have defined the density function

\rho_{D}(x)=\frac{2}{L}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\rm mBz}\delta(x-\gamma_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}/\sqrt{D})\>,

(45)

and where we have used Eq. (
29
).
We have
z=2D
for the
D
-dimensional hypercubic lattice and, in the Heisenberg limit of the Hubbard model,
J_{\rm H}=4t^{2}/U=4t^{\ast}{}^{2}/DU
, when using the scaling
t=t^{\ast}/\sqrt{D}
with
t^{\ast}=\mbox{const}
.
In the limit
D\to\infty
, this scaling of the hopping ensures that the kinetic energy of the Hubbard model remains nontrivial and balances the interaction term
Müller-Hartmann 1989
.
Moreover, the density function converges to a Gaussian
Müller-Hartmann 1989
:

\rho_{D}(x)\to\rho_{\infty}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{4}\right)\>.

(46)

In the Heisenberg limit and with the scaled hopping, we thus have

\Omega_{\rm loc}(D)=-\frac{1}{s}\frac{J^{2}U^{2}}{16t^{\ast}{}^{4}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx\rho_{D}(x)}{4\Delta^{2}-x^{2}/D}\>,

(47)

which for
D\to\infty
, and assuming
s=1/2
and
\Delta=1
converges to

\Omega_{\rm loc}(\infty)=-\frac{1}{32t^{\ast}{}^{4}}J^{2}U^{2}\>.

(48)

This represents the mean-field value of the (lcoal) spin-Berry curvature in the antiferromagnetic state at large
U
.

To compare with the result obtained for
D=3
, we must use the same scaling of the hopping.
This yields

\Omega_{\rm loc}(3)\approx-0.084\frac{J^{2}U^{2}}{16{t^{\ast}}^{4}}D^{2}\Bigg|_{D=3}\approx 1.51\cdot\Omega(\infty).

(49)

For lattice dimensions
D>3
we find:

\Omega_{\rm loc}(4)\approx 1.22\,\Omega(\infty)
,

\Omega_{\rm loc}(5)\approx 1.16\,\Omega(\infty)
,

\Omega_{\rm loc}(6)\approx 1.12\,\Omega(\infty)
.
Hence, given the standard scaling of the hopping with
D
, the absolute value of
\Omega_{\rm loc}(D)
increases with decreasing
D
and finally, for
D=2
diverges.

Finally, when addressing the dimensional crossover
Liu 1990
;
Majlis et al. 1992
;
Majlis et al. 1993
, we consider the Heisenberg model given by Eq. (
19
) again, but with spatially anisotropic nearest-neighbor exchange couplings
J_{\rm H}\equiv J_{\rm H,x}=J_{\rm H,y}\geq J_{\rm H,z}
.
Proceeding analogously to Sec. C, one ends up with a modified magnon dispersion only:

\omega(\boldsymbol{k})=\sqrt{(z_{\text{eff}}\Delta)^{2}-\gamma^{\prime 2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\>.

(50)

Here, we have defined an effective coordination number
z_{\text{eff}}=2(J_{\rm H,x}+J_{\rm H,y}+J_{\rm H,z})/J_{\rm H,x}
.
Furthermore,
\gamma^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{k}}:=2(J_{\rm H,x}\cos k_{x}+J_{\rm H,y}\cos k_{y}+J_{\rm H,z}\cos k_{z})/J_{\rm H,x}
.

Section G: Spin dynamics.

The equations of motion Eq. (
1
) for the classical spins comprise the conventional (Hamiltonian) and the geometrical spin torque, see Eq. (
2
).
In the weak-
J
limit, the former results from the local direct exchange
J
as well as from the indirect RKKY-type exchange.
We have:

\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{m}=J\langle\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{m}}\rangle^{(0)}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{m}+J^{2}\sum_{m^{\prime}}\underline{\chi}_{i_{m}i_{m^{\prime}}}(0)\boldsymbol{S}_{m^{\prime}}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{m}+\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}\Omega_{m^{\prime}m,\alpha^{\prime}\alpha}({\boldsymbol{S}})\dot{S}_{m^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime}}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{\alpha}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{m}\;,

(51)

where
\langle\dots\rangle^{(0)}
denotes the expectation value at
J=0
.
For the non-vanishing components of the spin susceptibility and of the spin-Berry curvature on sublattice A we have

\chi_{ii^{\prime}}\equiv\chi_{ii^{\prime},xx}(0)=\chi_{ii^{\prime},yy}(0)=-\frac{z\Delta}{J_{\rm H}}\frac{2}{L}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\rm mBz}\frac{\cos{\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i^{\prime}})}}{\omega(\boldsymbol{k})^{2}}

(52)

and

\Omega_{mm^{\prime}}\equiv\Omega_{mm^{\prime},xy}=-\Omega_{mm^{\prime},yx}=-\frac{1}{s}\frac{J^{2}}{J_{\rm H}^{2}}\frac{2}{L}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\rm mBz}\frac{\cos(\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i_{m}}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i_{m^{\prime}}})}{\omega(\boldsymbol{k})^{2}}\>.

(53)

Specializing Eq. (
51
) for
M=1
, i.e., for a single classical spin, we get

\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}={\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(H)}_{1}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}+\Omega_{11}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1})\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\>,

(54)

where

{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(H)}_{1}=J\langle\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{1}}\rangle^{(0)}+J^{2}\chi_{i_{1}i_{1}}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{m})\times{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\>.

(55)

With

\underline{T}^{\rm(H)}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-T^{\rm(H)}_{1,z}&T^{\rm(H)}_{1,y}\\
T^{\rm(H)}_{1,z}&0&-T^{\rm(H)}_{1,x}\\
-T^{\rm(H)}_{1,y}&T^{\rm(H)}_{1,x}&0\end{pmatrix}

(56)

the cross product can be written as a matrix-vector product,

{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(H)}_{1}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}=\underline{T}^{\rm(H)}_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}
, and the equation of motion reads:

\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}=\frac{1}{1-\Omega_{11}S_{1z}}\underline{T}^{\rm(H)}_{1}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\>.

(57)

The classical spin undergoes a purely precessional dynamics around the
z
axis, but with a renormalized precession frequency.
The renormalization is due to the local spin-Berry curvature
\Omega_{\rm loc}=\Omega_{11}
and is the strongest for
\Omega_{\rm loc}={\cal O}(1)
.
Right at
\Omega_{\rm loc}=1/S_{1z}
, the precession frequency diverges.
This implies that the spin dynamics is no longer adiabatic and the theory breaks down.

In case of two classical spins,
M=2
, the equations of motion (
51
) can be cast into the form

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{\text{(H)}}_{1}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}+\boldsymbol{T}^{\text{(geo)}}_{1}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\>,

\displaystyle\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{\text{(H)}}_{2}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}+\boldsymbol{T}^{\text{(geo)}}_{2}\times{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}\>,

(58)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{\text{(H)}}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\langle\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{1}}\rangle^{(0)}+J^{2}\chi_{i_{1}i_{1}}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1})\times{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}+J^{2}\chi_{i_{1}i_{2}}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{2})\times{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\>,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{\text{(H)}}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J\langle\boldsymbol{s}_{i_{2}}\rangle^{(0)}+J^{2}\chi_{i_{2}i_{2}}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{2})\times{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}+J^{2}\chi_{i_{2}i_{1}}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1})\times{\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\>.

(59)

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{\rm(geo)}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{11}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1})\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}+\Omega_{12}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2})\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}^{\rm(geo)}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Omega_{22}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2})\times\boldsymbol{S}_{2}+\Omega_{12}({\boldsymbol{e}}_{z}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1})\times\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\>.

(60)

Here, we have assumed that the two spins couple to sites in the same sublattice, as otherwise the spin-Berry curvature vanishes.
The local spin-Berry curvature term can be treated in the same way as in the
M=1
case, while the nonlocal term can be written as a matrix-vector product:

\displaystyle(1-\Omega_{11}S_{1z})\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(H)}_{1}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}-\Omega_{12}\underline{\mathcal{A}}^{(z)}_{1}\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}\;,

\displaystyle(1-\Omega_{22}S_{2z})\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{T}}^{\rm(H)}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{2}-\Omega_{12}\underline{\mathcal{A}}^{(z)}_{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\;,

(61)

with

\underline{\mathcal{A}}^{(z)}_{m}=\begin{pmatrix}-S_{mz}&0&0\\
0&-S_{mz}&0\\
S_{mx}&S_{my}&0\end{pmatrix}\>.

(62)

This allows us to cast the equations of motion into an explicit system of ordinary differential equations:

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{1}\\
\dot{\boldsymbol{S}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}={\cal M}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}{\boldsymbol{T}}_{1}^{\rm(H)}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\\
{\boldsymbol{T}}_{2}^{\rm(H)}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\>.

(63)

Here, the
6\times 6
matrix

{\cal M}=\begin{pmatrix}(1-\Omega_{11}S_{1z}){{\boldsymbol{1}}}&\Omega_{12}\underline{\mathcal{A}}^{(z)}_{1}\\
\Omega_{12}\underline{\mathcal{A}}^{(z)}_{2}&(1-\Omega_{22}S_{2z}){{\boldsymbol{1}}}\end{pmatrix}

(64)

is given in terms of the components of the spin-Berry curvature tensor.
Eq. (
63
) demonstrates that the effect of the geometrical spin torque is not simply additive and hence does not directly compete with the conventional spin torque, but enters the spin dynamics as a multiplicative (matrix) factor.

The determinant of
\mathcal{M}
can be computed analytically:

\det\mathcal{M}=(1-\Omega_{11}S_{1z})(1-\Omega_{11}S_{2z})\big[(1-\Omega_{11}S_{1z})(1-\Omega_{22}S_{2z})-\Omega_{12}^{2}S_{1z}S_{2z}\big]^{2}\>.

(65)

The theory breaks down if
\det\mathcal{M}=0
.
We consider
\det{\cal M}
as a function of the local elements
\Omega_{\rm loc}=\Omega_{11}=\Omega_{22}
and assume that the nonlocal elements are small,
\Omega_{\rm nonloc}=|\Omega_{12}|\ll\Omega_{\rm loc}
.
We immediately see that the zeros of
\det{\cal M}
are of the order of unity.
This implies that anomalous spin dynamics, which is substantially affected by the geometrical spin torque, is expected if
\Omega_{\rm loc}={\cal O}(1)
and thus close to, but yet different from the zeros of
{\cal M}
.
