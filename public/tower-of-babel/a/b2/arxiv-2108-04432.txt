Revisit the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra
Jun Lu

Abstract
This survey is meant to provide an introduction to the fundamental theorem of linear algebra and the theories behind them. Our goal is to give a rigorous introduction to the readers with prior exposure to linear algebra. Specifically, we provide some details and proofs of some results from ( Strang 1993 ) . We then describe the fundamental theorem of linear algebra from different views and find the properties and relationships behind the views. The fundamental theorem of linear algebra is essential in many fields, such as electrical engineering, computer science, machine learning and, deep learning. This survey is primarily a summary of purpose, significance of important theories behind it. The sole aim of this survey is to give a self-contained introduction to concepts and mathematical tools in theory behind the fundamental theorem of linear algebra and rigorous analysis in order to seamlessly introduce its properties in four subspaces in subsequent sections. However, we clearly realize our inability to cover all the useful and interesting results and given the paucity of scope to present this discussion, e.g., the separated analysis of the (orthogonal) projection matrices. We refer the reader to literature in the field of linear algebra for a more detailed introduction to the related fields. Some excellent examples include ( Rose 1982 ; Strang 2009 ; Trefethen and Bau III 1997 ; Strang 2019 ; Strang 2021 ) .

Definition 1

(Eigenvalue)
.

Given any vector space
E
and any linear map
A:E\rightarrow E
, a scalar
\lambda\in K
is called an eigenvalue, or proper value, or characteristic value of
\bm{A}
if there is some nonzero vector
\bm{u}\in E
such that

\bm{A}\bm{u}=\lambda\bm{u}.

Definition 2

(Eigenvector)
.

A vector
\bm{u}\in E
is called an eigenvector, or proper vector, or characteristic vector of
\bm{A}
if
\bm{u}\neq 0
and if there is some
\lambda\in K
such that

\bm{A}\bm{u}=\lambda\bm{u},

where the scalar
\lambda
is then an eigenvalue. And we say that
\bm{u}
is an eigenvector associated with
\lambda
.

Definition 3

(Linearly Independent)
.

A set of vectors
\{\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}\}
is called linearly independent if there is no combination can get
x_{1}\bm{a}_{1}+x_{2}\bm{a}_{2}+\cdots+x_{n}\bm{a}_{n}=0
except all
x_{i}
’s are zero. An equivalent definition is that
\bm{a}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, and for every
k>1
, the vector
\bm{a}_{k}
does not belong to the span of
\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\cdots,\bm{a}_{k-1}
.

In the study of linear algebra, every vector space has a basis and every vector is a linear combination of members of the basis. We then define span and dimension of subspace via the basis.

Definition 4

(Span)
.

If every vector
\bm{v}
in subspace
\mathcal{V}
can be expressed as a linear combination of
\{\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\cdots,\bm{a}_{k}\}
, then
\{\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\cdots,\bm{a}_{k}\}
is said to span
\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 5

(Basis and Dimension)
.

A set of vectors
\{\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\cdots,\bm{a}_{k}\}
is called a basis of
\mathcal{V}
if they are linearly independent and span
\mathcal{V}
.
Every basis of a given subspace has the same number of vectors, and the number of vectors in any basis is called the dimension of the subspace
\mathcal{V}
. By convention, the subspace
\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
is said to have dimension zero. Furthermore, every subspace of nonzero dimension has a basis that is orthogonal, i.e., the basis of a subspace can be chosen orthogonal.

Definition 6

(Column Space (Range))
.

If
\bm{A}
is an
n\times p
real matrix, we define the column space (or range) of
\bm{A}
to be the set spanned by its columns:

\mathcal{C}(\bm{A})=\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\exists\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\,\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{x}\}.

And the row space of
\bm{A}
is the set spanned by its rows, which is equal to the column space of
\bm{A}^{\top}
:

\mathcal{C}(\bm{A}^{\top})=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\exists\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\,\bm{x}=\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{y}\}.

Definition 7

(Null Space (Kernel))
.

If
\bm{A}
is an
n\times p
real matrix, we define the null space (or kernel) of
\bm{A}
to be the set:

\mathcal{N}(\bm{A})=\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,\bm{A}\bm{y}=\boldsymbol{0}\}.

And the null space of
\bm{A}^{\top}
is defined as

\mathcal{N}(\bm{A}^{\top})=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{x}=\boldsymbol{0}\}.

Both the column space of
\bm{A}
and the null space of
\bm{A}^{\top}
are subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In fact, every vector in
\mathcal{N}(\bm{A}^{\top})
is perpendicular to
\mathcal{C}(\bm{A})
and vice versa.

Definition 8

(Rank)
.

The
rank
of a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the dimension of the column space of
\bm{A}
. The rank of
\bm{A}
is equal to the maximal number of linearly independent columns of
\bm{A}
, and is also the maximal number of linearly independent rows of
\bm{A}
. The matrix
\bm{A}
and its transpose
\bm{A}^{\top}
have the same rank. We say that
\bm{A}
has full rank, if its rank is equal to
min\{n,p\}
. In another word, this is true if and only if either all the columns of
\bm{A}
are linearly independent, or all the rows of
\bm{A}
are linearly independent. Specifically, given a vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, then the
n\times p
matrix
\bm{u}\bm{v}^{\top}
is of rank 1.

Definition 9

(Orthogonal Complement in General)
.

The orthogonal complement
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}
of a subspace
\mathcal{V}
contains every vector that is perpendicular to
\mathcal{V}
.
That is,

\mathcal{V}^{\perp}=\{\bm{v}|\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{u}=0,\,\,\,\forall\bm{u}\in\mathcal{V}\}.

The two subspaces are disjoint that span the entire space. The dimensions of
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\perp}
add to the dimension of the whole space. Furthermore,
(\mathcal{V}^{\perp})^{\perp}=\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 10

(Orthogonal Complement of Column Space)
.

If
\bm{A}
is an
n\times p
real matrix, the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{C}(\bm{A})
,
\mathcal{C}^{\bot}(\bm{A})
is the subspace defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\bot}(\bm{A})

\displaystyle=\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}=\boldsymbol{0},\,\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\,\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{v}=\boldsymbol{0},\,\forall\bm{v}\in\mathcal{C}(\bm{A})\}.

Definition 11

(Orthogonal Matrix)

A real square matrix
\bm{Q}
is an orthogonal matrix if the inverse of
\bm{Q}
equals to the transpose of
\bm{Q}
, that is
\bm{Q}^{-1}=\bm{Q}^{\top}
and
\bm{Q}\bm{Q}^{\top}=\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{Q}=\bm{I}

\bm{Q}=[\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\cdots,\bm{q}_{n}]
where
\bm{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all

\bm{q}_{i}^{\top}\bm{q}_{j}=\delta(i,j)

\bm{Q}

Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra

2.1
Dimension of Column Space and Row Space

In this section, we prove Lemma
that the dimension of the column space of a matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is equal to the dimension of its row space, i.e., the row rank and the column rank of a matrix
\bm{X}
are equal.

Lemma 12

(Dimension of Column Space and Row Space)
.

The dimension of the column space of a matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is equal to the dimension of its
row space, i.e., the row rank and the column rank of a matrix
\bm{X}
are equal.

of Lemma
.

We first notice that the null space of
\bm{X}
is orthogonal complementary to the row space of
\bm{X}
:
\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})\bot\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
(where the row space of
\bm{X}
is exactly the column space of
\bm{X}^{\top}
), that is, vectors in the null space of
\bm{X}
are orthogonal to vectors in the row space of
\bm{X}
. To see this, suppose
\bm{X}
has rows
\bm{a}_{1}^{\top},\bm{a}_{2}^{\top},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}^{\top}
and
\bm{X}=[\bm{a}_{1}^{\top};\bm{a}_{2}^{\top};\cdots;\bm{a}_{n}^{\top}]
. For any vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})
, we have
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}
, that is,
[\bm{a}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta};\bm{a}_{2}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta};\cdots;\bm{a}_{n}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}]=\boldsymbol{0}
. And since the row space of
\bm{X}
is spanned by
\bm{a}_{1}^{\top},\bm{a}_{2}^{\top},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}^{\top}
. Then
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is perpendicular to any vectors from
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
which means
\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})\bot\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
.

Now suppose, the dimension of the row space of
\bm{X}
is
r
.
Let
\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r}
be a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and form a basis for the row space
. Then the
r
vectors
\bm{X}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{r}_{r}
are in the column space of
\bm{X}
, furthermore, they are linearly independent. To see this, suppose we have a linear combination of the
r
vectors:
\beta_{1}\bm{A}\bm{r}_{1}+\beta_{2}\bm{A}\bm{r}_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{r}\bm{A}\bm{r}_{r}=0
, that is,
\bm{X}(\beta_{1}\bm{r}_{1}+\beta_{2}\bm{r}_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{r}\bm{r}_{r})=0
and the vector
\bm{v}=\beta_{1}\bm{r}_{1}+\beta_{2}\bm{r}_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{r}\bm{r}_{r}
is in the null space of
\bm{X}
. But since
\{\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r}\}
is a basis for the row space of
\bm{X}
,
\bm{v}
is thus also in the row space of
\bm{X}
. We have shown that vectors from the null space of
\bm{X}
is perpendicular to vectors from row space of
\bm{X}
, thus
\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{v}=0
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\cdots=\beta_{r}=0
. Then
\bm{X}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{r}_{r}
are in the column space of
\bm{X}
and they are independent
which means column space of
\bm{X}
is larger than
r
. This result shows that
row rank of
\bm{X}\leq
column rank of
\bm{X}
.

If we apply this process again for
\bm{X}^{\top}
. We will have
column rank of
\bm{X}\leq
row rank of
\bm{X}
. We complete the proof.
∎

A further information can be drawn from this proof is that if
\{\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r}\}
is a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
that forms a basis for the row space, then
\{\bm{X}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{r}_{r}\}
is a basis for the column space of
\bm{X}
. We formulate this finding into the following lemma.

Lemma 13

(Column Basis from Row Basis)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, let
\{\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r}\}
be a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
which forms a basis for the row space, then
\{\bm{X}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{r}_{r}\}
is a basis for the column space of
\bm{X}
.

\bm{X}

The fundamental theorem contains two parts, the dimension of the subspaces and the orthogonality of the subspaces. The orthogonality can be easily verified as we have shown in the beginning of this section. When the row space has dimension
r

p-r

Theorem 14

(Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra)
.

Orthogonal Complement and Rank-Nullity Theorem: for any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, we have

\bullet

\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})
is orthogonal complement to the row space
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
:
dim(\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}))+dim(\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}))=p
;

\bullet

\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}^{\top})
is orthogonal complement to the column space
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:
dim(\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}^{\top}))+dim(\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}))=n
;

\bullet
For rank-
r
matrix
\bm{X}
,
dim(\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}))=dim(\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}))=r
, that is,
dim(\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}))=p-r
and
dim(\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}^{\top}))=n-r
.

.

Follow from the proof of Lemma

. Let
\{\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r}\}
be a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
that form a basis for the row space, then
\{\bm{X}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{r}_{r}\}
is a basis for the column space of
\bm{X}

\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\cdots,\bm{n}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
form a basis for the null space of
\bm{X}

\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})\bot\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})

\{\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r},\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\cdots,\bm{n}_{k}\}

\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

For any vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
is in the column space of
\bm{X}
. Then it can be expressed by a combination of
\bm{X}\bm{r}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{r}_{r}
:
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\bm{X}\bm{r}_{i}
which states that
\bm{X}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\bm{r}_{i})=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\bm{r}_{i}
is thus in
\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})
. Since
\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\cdots,\bm{n}_{k}
is a basis for the null space of
\bm{X}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\bm{r}_{i}
can be expressed by a combination of
\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\cdots,\bm{n}_{k}

\boldsymbol{\beta}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\bm{r}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\bm{n}_{j}

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\bm{r}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\bm{n}_{j}
. That is, any vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be expressed by
\{\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\cdots,\bm{r}_{r},\bm{n}_{1},\bm{n}_{2},\cdots,\bm{n}_{k}\}
and the set forms a basis for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Thus the dimension sum to
p

r+k=p

dim(\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}))+dim(\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}))=p
. Similarly, we can prove

.

The fundamental theorem of linear algebra tells us there exists a basis for each subspace.
Specifically, from SVD, we can find an orthonormal basis for each subspace.

Lemma 16

(Four Orthonormal Basis in SVD)
.

Given the full SVD of matrix
\bm{X}=\bm{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
, where
\bm{U}=[\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\cdots,\bm{u}_{n}]
and
\bm{V}=[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{p}]
are the column partitions of
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
. Then, we have the following property:

\bullet

\{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{r}\}
is an orthonormal basis of the row space,
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
;

\bullet

\{\bm{v}_{r+1},\bm{v}_{r+2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{p}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})
;

\bullet

\{\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\cdots,\bm{u}_{r}\}
is an orthonormal basis of the column space,
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X})
;

\bullet

\{\bm{u}_{r+1},\bm{u}_{r+2},\cdots,\bm{u}_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{N}(\bm{X}^{\top})
.

of Lemma
.

From Lemma
, for symmetric matrix
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
,
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})
is spanned by the eigenvectors, thus
\{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\cdots,\bm{v}_{r}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})
.

Since,

1.
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
is symmetric, then the row space of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
is equal to the column space of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
.

2. All rows of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
are combination of rows of
\bm{X}
, so the row space of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}

\subseteq
row space of
\bm{X}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})\subseteq\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
.

3. Since
rank(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X})
by Lemma
, we then have

The row space of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
= the column space of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
= the row space of
\bm{X}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})=\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
. Thus
\{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{r}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{C}(\bm{X}^{\top})
.

Further, the space spanned by
\{\bm{v}_{r+1},\bm{v}_{r+2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{n}\}
is an orthogonal complement to the space spanned by
\{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{r}\}
, so
\{\bm{v}_{r+1},\bm{v}_{r+2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{n}\}

\mathcal{N}(\bm{X})
.

If we apply this process to
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
, we will prove the rest claims in the lemma. As an alternative way, we realize that
\{\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\cdots,\bm{u}_{r}\}
is a basis for the column space of
\bm{X}
by Lemma
, since
\bm{u}_{i}=\frac{\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}}{\sigma_{i}},\,\forall i\in\{1,2,\cdots,r\}
.
∎

5
Least Squares via the Full SVD for Rank Deficient Matrices

In the previous section, we assume
\bm{X}
has full rank. However, if
\bm{X}
does not have full column rank,
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
is not invertible. We thus can use SVD decomposition of
\bm{X}
to solve the least squares problem with rank-deficient
\bm{X}
. And we illustrate this rank-deficient least squares method in the following theorem.

Theorem 17

(LS via SVD for Rank Deficient Matrix)
.

Let
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
,
\bm{X}=\bm{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
is its full SVD decomposition with
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
being orthogonal matrices and
rank(\bm{X})=r\leq\min\{n,p\}
. Suppose
\bm{U}=[\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\cdots,\bm{u}_{n}]
,
\bm{V}=[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}_{p}]
and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then the LS solution to
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bm{y}
is given by

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{y}}{\sigma_{i}}\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{y},

where the upper-left side of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is a diagonal matrix
diag(\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}},\frac{1}{\sigma_{2}},\cdots,\frac{1}{\sigma_{r}})
.

of Theorem
.

Write out the loss to be minimized

\displaystyle||\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{y}||^{2}

\displaystyle=(\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{y})^{\top}(\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{y})

\displaystyle=(\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{y})^{\top}\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}(\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{y})\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{Since $\bm{U}$ is an orthogonal matrix})

\displaystyle=||\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{y}||^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{Invariant under orthogonal})

\displaystyle=||\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{y}||^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{Since $\bm{V}$ is an orthogonal matrix})

\displaystyle=||\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{y}||^{2}\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{Let $\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\bm{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(\sigma_{i}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}-\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{y})^{2}+\sum_{i=r+1}^{n}(\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{y})^{2}.\qquad

\displaystyle(\text{Since $\sigma_{r+1}=\sigma_{r+2}=\cdots=\sigma_{n}=0$})

Since
\boldsymbol{\beta}
only appears in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we just need to set
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{y}}{\sigma_{i}}
for all
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,r\}
to minimize the above equation. For any value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r+1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r+2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{p}
, it won’t change the result. From the regularization point of view we can set them to be 0 (to get minimal length). This gives us the LS solution via SVD:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\top}\bm{y}}{\sigma_{i}}\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}\bm{U}^{\top}\bm{y}=\bm{X}^{+}\bm{y},

where
\bm{X}^{+}=\bm{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{+}\bm{U}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is known as the
pseudo-inverse
of
\bm{X}
. Please refer to Section
for a detailed discussion about pseudo-inverse where we also prove that
the column space of
\bm{X}^{+}
is equal to the row space of
\bm{X}
, and the row space of
\bm{X}^{+}
is equal to the column space of
\bm{X}
.
∎

6
Pseudo-Inverse in the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra

If the matrix
\bm{X}
is nonsingular, then the linear system
\bm{y}=\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be easily solved by the inverse of
\bm{X}
such that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bm{X}^{-1}\bm{y}
. However, the inverse of an
n\times p
matrix
\bm{X}
does not exist if
\bm{X}
is not square or
\bm{X}
is singular. But we can still find its pseudo-inverse, a
p\times n
matrix denoted by
\bm{X}^{+}
.
Before the discussion of pseudo-inverse, we firstly introduce one-sided inverse, generalized inverse, and reflexive generalized inverse that are prerequisites of pseudo-inverse and some the properties of them.

6.1
One-sided Inverse

Definition 19

(One-sided Inverse)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, if there is a matrix
\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}
such that

\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}\bm{X}=\bm{I}_{p},

then
\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}
is a
left inverse
of
\bm{X}
, and
\bm{X}
is called
left-invertible
.
Similarly, if there is a matrix
\bm{X}_{R}^{-1}
such that

\bm{X}\bm{X}_{R}^{-1}=\bm{I}_{n},

then
\bm{X}_{R}^{-1}
is a
right inverse
of
\bm{X}
, and
\bm{X}
is called
right-invertible
.

A word on the notation
: Note here the superscript
-1
in
\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}
and
\bm{X}_{R}^{-1}
does not mean the inverse of
\bm{X}_{L}
or
\bm{X}_{R}
but the one-sided inverse of
\bm{X}
.

Lemma 20

(One-sided Invertible)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, we have

\bullet

\bm{X}
is left-invertible if and only if
\bm{X}
has full column rank (which implies
n\geq p
);

\bullet

\bm{X}
is right-invertible if and only if
\bm{X}
has full row rank (which implies
n\leq p
).

of Lemma
.

Suppose
\bm{X}
has full column rank, then
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
has full rank (see Lemma
). Therefore,
(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})=\bm{I}_{p}
. That is
(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}
is a left inverse of
\bm{X}
.

For the reverse, now suppose
\bm{X}
is left-invertible such that
\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}\bm{X}=\bm{I}_{p}
. Since all rows of
\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}\bm{X}
are the combinations of the rows of
\bm{X}
, that is, the row space of
\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}\bm{X}
is a subset of the row space of
\bm{X}
. We then have
rank(\bm{X})\geq rank(\bm{X}_{L}^{-1}\bm{X})=rank(\bm{I}_{p})=p
which implies
rank(\bm{X})=p
and
\bm{X}
has full column rank.

Similarly, we can show
\bm{X}
is right-invertible if and only if
\bm{X}
has full row rank and
\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top})^{-1}
is a right inverse of
\bm{X}
.
∎

We have shown in the above proof that
(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}
is a specific left inverse of
\bm{X}
if
\bm{X}
has full column rank, and
\bm{X}^{\top}(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top})^{-1}
is a specific right inverse of
\bm{X}
if
\bm{X}
has full row rank.
However, the inverse of a
p\times p
nonsingular matrix requires
2p^{3}
floating points operations (flops) which is a complex procedure
(
Lu 2021a
)
.
In our case, the inverse of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
requires
2p^{3}
flops and the inverse of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
requires
2n^{3}
flops. A simpler way to find a one-sided inverse is through elementary operations.

Suppose
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
has full column rank and we apply
row elementary operations

\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
on
[\bm{X},\bm{I}_{n}]
such that

\bm{E}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{X}&\bm{I}_{n}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{p}&\bm{G}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\bm{Z}\end{bmatrix},

(1)

where
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}

\bm{I}_{n}
is an
n\times n
identity matrix,
\bm{I}_{p}
is a
p\times p
identity matrix, and
\bm{Z}
is an
(n-p)\times n
matrix.
Then, it can be easily verified that
\bm{G}\bm{X}=\bm{I}_{p}
and
\bm{G}
is a left inverse of
\bm{X}
.

Similarly, suppose
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
has full row rank and we apply
column elementary operations

\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
on
[\bm{X}^{\top},\bm{I}_{p}]^{\top}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{X}\\
\bm{I}_{p}\end{bmatrix}\bm{E}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{n}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\bm{G}&\bm{Z}\end{bmatrix},

(2)

where
\bm{Z}
is a
p\times(p-n)
matrix.
Then,
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
is a right inverse of
\bm{X}
.

6.2
Generalized Inverse (g-inverse)

We mentioned previously that if matrix
\bm{X}
is nonsingular, then the linear system
\bm{y}=\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
can be easily solved by the inverse of
\bm{X}
such that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bm{X}^{-1}\bm{y}
. However, the inverse of an
n\times p
matrix
\bm{X}
does not exist if
\bm{X}
is not square or
\bm{X}
is singular. But still, when
\bm{y}
is in the column space of
\bm{X}
, we can find the solution of the linear system as well. The relationship between the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
\bm{y}
is given by the generalized inverse of
\bm{X}
:
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bm{X}^{-}\bm{y}
.

Definition 25

(Generalized Inverse)
.

Any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
has rank
r
with
r\leq p\leq n
. Then a generalized inverse
\bm{X}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
of
\bm{X}
is a matrix that satisfies

(C1)\qquad\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}=\bm{X},

or equivalently,

(C1.1)\qquad\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{y}=\bm{y}

for any
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\bm{X})
.

To see the equivalence between
(C1)
and
(C1.1)
, that is, we want to show
\bm{X}
satisfies
(C1)
if and only if it satisfies
(C1.1)
. For any
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{C}(\bm{X})
, we can find a
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bm{y}
. If
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{-}
satisfy
(C1)
, then

\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{y}=\bm{y},

which implies
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{-}
also satisfy
(C1.1)
. For the reverse, suppose
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{-}
satisfy
(C1.1)
, then

\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{y}=\bm{y}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta},

which implies
\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}^{-}
also satisfy
(C1)
.

Multiply on the left of
(C1)
by
\bm{X}^{-}
and follow the definition of projection matrix, we obtain
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}=\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
such that
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
is idempotent, which implies
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
is a projection matrix (not necessarily to be an orthogonal projection).

Lemma 26

(Projection Matrix from Generalized Inverse)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}
, and its generalized inverse
\bm{X}^{-}
,
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
is a projection matrix but not necessarily an orthogonal projection. Same claim can be applied to
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}
as well.

Lemma 27

(Rank of Generalized Inverse)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, and its generalized inverse
\bm{X}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
, then

rank(\bm{X}^{-})\geq rank(\bm{X}).

Specifically, we also have
rank(\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-})=rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})
.

of Lemma
.

From
(C1)
, we notice that
rank(\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})
. And

rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})\leq rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-})\leq rank(\bm{X}^{-}),

where the first inequality comes from the fact that the columns of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
are combinations of columns of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}
, and the second inequality comes from the fact that the rows of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}
are combinations of rows of
\bm{X}^{-}
.

For the second part, we have

rank(\bm{X})\geq rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-})\geq rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}),

where the first inequality is from the fact that the columns of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}
are combinations of the columns of
\bm{X}
, and the second inequality is from the fact that the columns of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
are combinations of the columns of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}
. From
(C1)
again,
rank(\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})
which implies by ”sandwiching” that

rank(\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}).

Similarly, we also have

rank(\bm{X})\geq rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})\geq rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}),

where the first inequality is from the fact that the rows of
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
are combinations of the rows of
\bm{X}
, and the second inequality is from the fact that the rows of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
are combinations of the rows of
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}
. By ”sandwiching” again, we have

rank(\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}),

which completes the proof.
∎

In Lemma
, we have shown that the left inverse exists if and only if
\bm{X}
has full column rank, and the right inverse exists if and only if
\bm{X}
has full row rank. However, this is not required in generalized inverse. When this full rank condition is satisfied, we have another property as follows.

Lemma 28

(Full Rank Generalized Inverse)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, and its generalized inverse
\bm{X}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
, then we have

1).
\bm{X}
has full column rank if and only if
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}=\bm{I}_{p}
;

2).
\bm{X}
has full row rank if and only if
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}=\bm{I}_{n}
.

of Lemma
.

For 1). Suppose
\bm{X}
has full column rank, and we have shown in Lemma
that
rank(\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-})=rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})

rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X})=p
and
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is nonsingular. We have

\bm{I}_{p}=(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})^{-1}=\bm{X}^{-}(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})^{-1}=\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}.

For the reverse, suppose
\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X}=\bm{I}_{p}
which implies
rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})=p

rank(\bm{X}^{-}\bm{X})=rank(\bm{X})

rank(\bm{X})=p
such that
\bm{X}
has full column rank.

Similarly, we can show
\bm{X}
has full row rank if and only if
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{-}=\bm{I}_{n}
.
∎

Definition 31

(Reflexive Generalized Inverse)
.

Any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
has rank
r
with
r\leq\min\{p,n\}
. Then a reflexive generalized inverse
\bm{X}_{r}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
of
\bm{X}
is a matrix that satisfies

(C1)\qquad\bm{X}\bm{X}_{r}^{-}\bm{X}=\bm{X},

and

(C2)\qquad\bm{X}_{r}^{-}\bm{X}\bm{X}_{r}^{-}=\bm{X}_{r}^{-}.

That is
\bm{X}_{r}^{-}
is a g-inverse of
\bm{X}
, and
\bm{X}
is a g-inverse of
\bm{X}_{r}^{-}
.

Let
\bm{X}
be of rank
r
, it can be factored as
\bm{X}=\bm{E}_{1}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}\bm{E}_{2}
, where
\bm{E}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
\bm{E}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
are elementary transformations on
\bm{X}
. Then, we can construct such a reflexive generalized inverse of
\bm{X}
as

\bm{X}_{r}^{-}=\bm{E}_{2}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{r}&\bm{A}\\
\bm{B}&\bm{B}\bm{A}\end{bmatrix}\bm{E}_{1}^{-1},

where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times(n-r)}
,
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-r)\times r}
can be any matrix so that the reflexive generalized inverse is
not unique
. This construction of the reflexive generalized inverse also shows that reflexive generalized inverse exists for any matrix. This implies reflexive generalized inverse is a more general inverse of
\bm{X}
compared to the one-sided inverse which may not exist.

(Reflexive Generalized Inverse from G-Inverse)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}

\bm{A}

\bm{X}

\bm{X}

It can be easily verified that
\bm{X}\bm{Z}\bm{X}=\bm{X}
and
\bm{Z}\bm{X}\bm{Z}=\bm{Z}

6.4
Pseudo-Inverse

As we mentioned previously, for a matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, we can find its pseudo-inverse, a
p\times n
matrix denoted by
\bm{X}^{+}
.
In words, when
\bm{X}
multiplies a vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in its row space, this produces
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
in the column space (see Figure
). Those two spaces have equal dimension
r
, i.e., the rank.
\bm{X}
is always invertible when restricted to these spaces and
\bm{X}^{+}
inverts
\bm{X}
. That is,
\bm{X}^{+}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
when
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is in the row space of
\bm{X}
. And
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{+}\bm{y}=\bm{y}
when
\bm{y}
is in the column space of
\bm{X}
(see Figure
).

The null space of
\bm{X}^{+}
is the null space of
\bm{X}^{\top}
. It contains the vectors
\bm{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{y}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Those vectors
\bm{y}
are perpendicular to every
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
in the column space. We delay the proof of this property in Lemma
.

More formally, the pseudo-inverse, or also known as Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse,
\bm{X}^{+}
, is defined by the unique
p\times n
matrix satisfying the following four criteria

\boxed{\begin{aligned} &(C1)\qquad\bm{X}\bm{X}^{+}\bm{X}&=&\bm{X}\qquad&(\bm{X}^{+}\text{ is a g-inverse of }\bm{X})\\
&(C2)\qquad\bm{X}^{+}\bm{X}\bm{X}^{+}&=&\bm{X}^{+}\qquad&(\bm{X}\text{ is a g-inverse of }\bm{X}^{+})\\
&(C3)\qquad(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{+})^{\top}&=&\bm{X}\bm{X}^{+}\\
&(C4)\qquad(\bm{X}^{+}\bm{X})^{\top}&=&\bm{X}^{+}\bm{X}\end{aligned}}

(6)

In Lemma
, we claimed that
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{+}
and
\bm{X}^{+}\bm{X}
are idempotent if
\bm{X}^{+}
is a g-inverse of
\bm{X}
, and thus they are both projection matrix. For
\bm{X}^{+}
to be pseudo-inverse, by
(C3),(C4)
conditions, they are symmetric such that they are orthogonal projection as well (again, please refer to
(
Lu 2021b
)
for more details about orthogonal projection matrices).

From the pseudo-inverse of the matrix from CR decomposition, we can also claim that any matrix has a pseudo-inverse.

Appendix D
Spectral Decomposition (Theorem)

In this section, we provide the proof for Theorem
which is essential for the existence of singular value decomposition in the sequel.

Theorem 49

(Spectral Decomposition)
.

A real matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is symmetric if and only if there exists an orthogonal matrix
\bm{Q}
and a diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
such that

\bm{A}=\bm{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bm{Q}^{\top},

where the columns of
\bm{Q}=[\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\cdots,\bm{q}_{n}]
are eigenvectors of
\bm{A}
and orthonormal, and the entries of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
are the corresponding eigenvalues of
\bm{A}
, which are real. Specifically, we have the following properties:

1. A symmetric matrix has only
real eigenvalues
;

2. The eigenvectors can be chosen
orthonormal
;

3. The rank of
\bm{A}
is the number of nonzero eigenvalues;

4. If the eigenvalues are distinct, the eigenvectors are unique as well.

We prove the theorem in several steps.

Symmetric Matrix Property 1 of 4

The eigenvalues of any symmetric matrix are all real.

All the eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real
.

Suppose eigenvalue
\lambda
is a complex number
\lambda=a+ib
where
a,b
are real. Its complex conjugate is
\bar{\lambda}=a-ib
. Same for complex eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bm{c}+i\bm{d}
and its complex conjugate
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bm{c}-i\bm{d}
where
\bm{c},\bm{d}
are real vectors. We then have the following property

\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{ leads to }}\qquad\bm{X}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bar{\lambda}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{ transpose to }}\qquad\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{X}=\bar{\lambda}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}.

We take dot product of the first equation with
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and the last equation with
\boldsymbol{\beta}
:

\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta},\qquad\text{and }\qquad\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bar{\lambda}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Then we have the equality
\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bar{\lambda}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\bm{c}-i\bm{d})^{\top}(\bm{c}+i\bm{d})=\bm{c}^{\top}\bm{c}+\bm{d}^{\top}\bm{d}
is a real number. Therefore the imaginary part of
\lambda
is zero and
\lambda
is real.
∎

The eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of any symmetric matrix are orthogonal so that we can normalize eigenvectors to make them orthonormal since
\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}\underrightarrow{\text{ leads to }}\bm{X}\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{||\boldsymbol{\beta}||}=\lambda\frac{\boldsymbol{\beta}}{||\boldsymbol{\beta}||}
which corresponds to the same eigenvalue.

The eigenvectors are orthogonal

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}

\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
and

\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}=\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}=\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}=\lambda_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2},

\bm{Y}_{1}\bm{n}
is an eigenvector of
\bm{X}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
(same eigenvalue corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}
). Since
\bm{Y}_{1}\bm{n}
is a combination of
\bm{y}_{2},\bm{y}_{3},\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}
which are orthonormal to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}
, the
\bm{Y}_{1}\bm{n}
can be chosen to be orthonormal to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}
.

To conclude, if we have one eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}
corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
whose multiplicity is
k\geq 2
, we could construct the second eigenvector by choosing one vector from null space of
(\bm{B}-\lambda_{i}\bm{I}_{n-1})
constructed above. Suppose now, we have constructed the second eigenvector
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2}
which is orthonormal to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}
.
For such eigenvectors
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2}
, we can always find additional
n-2
orthonormal vectors
\bm{y}_{3},\bm{y}_{4},\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}
so that
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2},\bm{y}_{3},\bm{y}_{4},\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}\}
forms an orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Put the
\bm{y}_{3},\bm{y}_{4},\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}
into matrix
\bm{Y}_{2}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2},\bm{y}_{3},\bm{y}_{4},\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}\}
into matrix
\bm{P}_{2}
:

\bm{Y}_{2}=[\bm{y}_{3},\bm{y}_{4},\cdots,\bm{y}_{n}]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{P}_{2}=[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2},\bm{Y}_{1}].

We then have

\bm{P}_{2}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{P}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}&0&\boldsymbol{0}\\
0&\lambda_{i}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\bm{Y}_{2}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{Y}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}&0&\boldsymbol{0}\\
0&\lambda_{i}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\bm{C}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{C}=\bm{Y}_{2}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{Y}_{2}
such that
\det(\bm{P}_{2}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{P}_{2}-\lambda\bm{I}_{n})=(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)^{2}\det(\bm{C}-\lambda\bm{I}_{n-2})
. If the multiplicity of
\lambda_{i}
is
k\geq 3
,
\det(\bm{C}-\lambda_{i}\bm{I}_{n-2})=0
and the null space of
\bm{C}-\lambda_{i}\bm{I}_{n-2}
is not none so that we can still find a vector from null space of
\bm{C}-\lambda_{i}\bm{I}_{n-2}
and
\bm{C}\bm{n}=\lambda_{i}\bm{n}
. Now we can construct a vector
\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\\
\bm{n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
z_{1},z_{2}
are any scalar values, such that

\bm{X}\bm{P}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\\
\bm{n}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{P}_{2}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{i}&0&\boldsymbol{0}\\
0&\lambda_{i}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}&\bm{C}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}\\
z_{2}\\
\bm{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Similarly, from the left side of above equation we will get
\lambda_{i}z_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}+\lambda_{i}z_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2}+\bm{X}\bm{Y}_{2}\bm{n}
. From the right side of above equation we will get
\lambda_{i}z_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1}+\lambda_{i}z_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2}+\lambda_{i}\bm{Y}_{2}\bm{n}
. As a result,

\bm{X}\bm{Y}_{2}\bm{n}=\lambda_{i}\bm{Y}_{2}\bm{n},

where
\bm{Y}_{2}\bm{n}
is an eigenvector of
\bm{X}
and orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i2}
. And it is easy to construct the eigenvector to be orthonormal to the first two.

The process can go on, and finally, we will find
k
orthonormal eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
.

Actually, the dimension of the null space of
\bm{P}_{1}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{P}_{1}-\lambda_{i}\bm{I}_{n}
is equal to the multiplicity
k
. It also follows that if the multiplicity of
\lambda_{i}
is
k
, there cannot be more than
k
orthogonal eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
. Otherwise, it will come to the conclusion that we could find more than
n
orthogonal eigenvectors which leads to a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 53

(Reduced SVD for Rectangular Matrices)
.

For every real
n\times p
matrix
\bm{X}
with rank
r
, then matrix
\bm{X}
can be factored as

\bm{X}=\bm{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=diag(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\cdots,\sigma_{r})
with
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{r}
and

\bullet

\sigma_{i}
’s are the nonzero
singular values
of
\bm{X}
, in the meantime, they are the (positive) square roots of the nonzero
eigenvalues
of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
and
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
.

\bullet
Columns of
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
contain the
r
eigenvectors of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
corresponding to the
r
nonzero eigenvalues of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
.

\bullet
Columns of
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
contain the
r
eigenvectors of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
corresponding to the
r
nonzero eigenvalues of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
.

\bullet
Moreover, the columns of
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
are called the

of
\bm{X}
, respectively.

\bullet
Further, the columns of
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
are orthonormal (by Spectral Theorem
).

In particular, we can write out the matrix decomposition
\bm{X}=\bm{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
, which is a sum of
r
rank-one matrices.

If we append additional
n-r
silent columns that are orthonormal to the
r
eigenvectors of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
, we will have an orthogonal matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Similar for the columns of
\bm{V}
.

To prove the existence of the SVD, we need to use the following lemmas. We mentioned that the singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
. While, negative values do not have square roots such that the eigenvalues must be nonnegative.

Lemma 54

(Nonnegative Eigenvalues of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
)
.

For any matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
,
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
has nonnegative eigenvalues.

of Lemma
.

For eigenvalue and its corresponding eigenvector
\lambda,\boldsymbol{\beta}
of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
, we have

\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}.

Since
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=||\bm{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}||^{2}\geq 0
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\geq 0
. We then have
\lambda\geq 0
.
∎

Since
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
has nonnegative eigenvalues, we then can define the singular value
\sigma\geq 0
of
\bm{X}
such that
\sigma^{2}
is the eigenvalue of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
, i.e.,
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{v}=\sigma^{2}\bm{v}
. This is essential to SVD.

We have shown in Lemma
that
rank
(
\bm{X}\bm{B}
)
\leq
min(
rank
(
\bm{X}
),
rank
(
\bm{B}

However, the symmetric matrix
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}

.

In the form of SVD, we claimed the matrix
\bm{X}
is a sum of
r

r
is the number of nonzero singular values. And the number of nonzero singular values is actually the rank of the matrix.

Lemma 55

(The Number of Nonzero Singular Values Equals to the Rank)
.

The number of nonzero singular values of matrix
\bm{X}
equals the rank of
\bm{X}
.

of Lemma
.

The rank of any symmetric matrix (here
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
) equals the number of nonzero eigenvalues (with repetitions) by Lemma

We are now ready to prove the existence of SVD.

of Theorem
: Existence of the SVD
.

Since
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
is a symmetric matrix, by Spectral Theorem
and Lemma
, there exists an orthogonal matrix
\bm{V}
such that

\boxed{\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}=\bm{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\bm{V}^{\top}},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values of
\bm{A}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}
contains the eigenvalues of
\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}
.
Specifically,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=diag(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\cdots,\sigma_{r})
and
\{\sigma_{1}^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2},\cdots,\sigma_{r}^{2}\}
are the nonzero eigenvalues of
\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{A}
with
r
being the rank of
\bm{A}
. I.e.,
\{\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{r}\}
are the singular values of
\bm{A}
. In this case,
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
.
Now we are into the central part.

Start from
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\bm{v}_{i}
,
\forall i\in\{1,2,\cdots,r\}
, i.e., the eigenvector
\bm{v}_{i}
of
\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}
corresponding to
\sigma_{i}^{2}
:

1. Multiply both sides by
\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
:

\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{v}_{i}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad||\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}||^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad||\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}||=\sigma_{i}.

2. Multiply both sides by
\bm{X}
:

\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}\,\,\,\,\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\,\,\,\,\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\frac{\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\frac{\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\,\,\,\,\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\,\,\,\,\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{u}_{i}=\sigma_{i}^{2}\bm{u}_{i},

where we notice this form can find the eigenvector of
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
corresponding to
\sigma_{i}^{2}
which is
\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}
. Since the length of
\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}
is
\sigma_{i}
, we then define
\bm{u}_{i}=\frac{\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}
with norm 1.

These
\bm{u}_{i}

(\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i})^{\top}(\bm{X}\bm{v}_{j})=\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}\bm{v}_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{2}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{v}_{j}=0
. That is

\boxed{\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}=\bm{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\bm{U}^{\top}}.

Since
\bm{X}\bm{v}_{i}=\sigma_{i}\bm{u}_{i}
, we have

[\bm{X}\bm{v}_{1},\bm{X}\bm{v}_{2},\cdots,\bm{X}\bm{v}_{r}]=[\sigma_{1}\bm{u}_{1},\sigma_{2}\bm{u}_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{r}\bm{u}_{r}]\qquad\underrightarrow{\text{leads to}}\qquad\bm{X}\bm{V}=\bm{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma},

which completes the proof.
∎

By appending silent columns in
