Error Analysis of an HDG Method with Impedance Traces for the Helmholtz Equation
Michael Leumüller, Scientific Computing, Austria, Joachim Schöberl, Scientific Computing, Austria

Abstract
In this work, a novel analysis of a hybrid discontinuous Galerkin method for the Helmholtz equation is presented. It uses wavenumber, mesh size and polynomial degree independent stabilisation parameters leading to impedance traces between elements. With analysis techniques based on projection operators unique discrete solvability without a resolution condition and optimal convergence rates with respect to the mesh size are proven. The considered method is tailored towards enabling static condensation and the usage of iterative solvers.

1
Introduction

The numerical solution of the Helmholtz equation with impedance boundary conditions is challenging.
For large wavenumbers the finite element method (FEM) yields, due to the necessary small mesh size, large systems of linear equations.
The computational costs for direct solvers are too expensive and due to the indefinite structure of the Helmholtz equation,
iterative solvers developed for elliptic problems cannot be applied.
Domain decomposition preconditioners seem promising, but sub-blocks of finite element (FE) system matrices need to be inverted.
For conforming FE spaces these blocks represent local Dirichlet problems which may be singular.

Discontinuous spaces can be applied to circumvent this issue leading to absolutely stable local methods, e.g.
[
FX13
]
.
In
[
MPS13
]
a discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method for the Helmholtz equation has been proposed and analysed.
It is remarkable that therein the existence of a discrete solution is proven without the need for a resolution condition of the discrete space.
Quasi-optimality has been established in the asymptotic regime.
Other studies of DG methods have been dedicated to giving explicit pre-asymptotic estimates, e.g.
[
FW09
,
FW11
,
FX13
,
Wu14
]
.
The analysis is based on carefully chosen test functions contained in DG spaces.
The same technique cannot be applied to conforming FE spaces.

A disadvantage of DG methods compared to standard FEM is the larger number of coupling unknowns.
Hybrid discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) methods have been developed to counteract this issue.
Applying static condensation leads to smaller systems of linear equations for skeleton unknowns.
A requirement is the invertibility of element matrices which is closely related to local absolute stability.
In
[
LCQ17
]
an absolutely stable HDG method for Maxwell’s equations has been analysed
via a new method based on
L^{2}
-projections proving discrete absolute stability.

DG and HDG methods depend on stabilisations of jumps over facets, which usually are
h
-dependent stemming from the analysis of the elliptic Poison equation.
Contrary to that approach, the experiments in
[
Hub13
,
HPS13
,
HS14
]
suggests that iterative solvers for the Helmholtz equation
require
h
-independent stabilisations representing impedance traces between sub-domains.
In
[
GM11
]
, an HDG method with such a stabilisation has been analysed.
The used technique is based on results from
[
CGS10
]
and utilises special projections tailored to the therein employed HDG formulation.
The authors were able to prove optimal convergence rates in the asymptotic range.

The main contribution of this work is the analysis of the HDG formulation introduced in
[
MSS10
]

and further studied in
[
Hub13
,
HPS13
,
HS14
]
.
The authors explored various promising iterative solvers, motivating the favourable properties of this HDG formulation.
Numerical experiments therein suggest for solvers the necessity of a second variable per facet representing the flux,
which is similar to the discontinuous Petrov Galerkin method in
[
DGMZ12
,
GMO14
]
.
This work establishes the analytical foundation for the HDG method with two variables per facet introduced in
[
MSS10
]
.
It utilises a mesh size, domain and wave number independent stabilisation which is apriori known for all Helmholtz problems.
In Section
2
, the HDG formulation is introduced and the main results, consisting of discrete absolute stability and optimal convergence rates, are stated.
The consecutive sections are dedicated to proving these statements, starting with the discrete absolute stability in Section
3
,
followed by the optimal convergence for the pressure in Section
4
and of the flux in Section
5
.
In Section
6
, simulations for problems with plane waves as analytical solutions are shown and
to illustrate the potential of an iterative solver, a 2D example with heterogeneous materials and a 3D example with scattering objects are considered.

2
Hybrid Discontinuous Galerkin Method

This study considers the following mixed Helmholtz problem with an impedance boundary condition on a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 1

(Mixed Helmholtz Problem)
.

For given
\kappa>0
and
g\in L^{2}({\partial\Omega})
, let
(\sigma,u)
be the solution of the boundary value problem (BVP)

\displaystyle j\kappa\sigma-\grad u=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1a)

\displaystyle-\diver\sigma+j\kappa u=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(1b)

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n}+u=g

\displaystyle\text{ on }{\partial\Omega}.

(1c)

The outward pointing normal vector is denoted by
\mathbf{n}
, the imaginary unit is
j:=\sqrt{-1}
,
g
represents a given excitation on
{\partial\Omega}
and
\kappa
is a prescribed positive wavenumber.
The flux and pressure are symbolised by
\sigma
and
u
.
The corresponding adjoint problem is defined as follows.

Definition 2

(Adjoint Mixed Helmholtz Problem)
.

For given
\kappa>0
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, let
(\phi,w)
be the solution of the BVP

\displaystyle j\kappa\phi+\grad w=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2a)

\displaystyle\diver\phi+j\kappa w=f

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2b)

\displaystyle\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}+w=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }{\partial\Omega}.

(2c)

The adjoint BVP has a homogenous Robin-boundary condition and a volume excitation
f
.
The existence and uniqueness of the continuous BVPs have been proven in
[
Mel95
, Proposition 8.1.3]
.

Remark 3
.

The mixed adjoint problem is equivalent to

\displaystyle-\laplace w-\kappa^{2}w=j\kappa f

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(3a)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{j\kappa}\grad w\cdot\mathbf{n}+w=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }{\partial\Omega}

(3b)

with
\kappa
-dependent right-hand side.

To be able to achieve
\kappa
-explicit analysis, regularity estimates of the Helmholtz equation are necessary.
For convex, or smooth and star-shaped domains, the adjoint Helmholtz problem in Definition
2
satisfies the regularity

\displaystyle\lVert\grad w\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa\lVert w\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(4)

\displaystyle\lVert\grad^{2}w\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\kappa\lVert\grad\phi\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\kappa)\kappa\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(5)

with a constant
C>0
independent of
\kappa
, see
[
MPS13
, Remark 2.6]
.
Throughout this study, the
H^{2}
regularity of both BVPs is assumed.

The domain is split into disjoint finite elements
T
. The set of all finite elements is called
\mathcal{T}
and there holds
\cup_{T\in\mathcal{T}}T=\Omega
.
The facets between elements inside of
\Omega
are denoted by
F_{\rm I}
and the facets on
{\partial\Omega}
by
F_{\rm O}
.
If a distinction between
F_{\rm{I}}
and
F_{\rm{O}}
is not necessary, then a facet will be called
F
and the set of all facets is defined as
\mathcal{F}
.
The following short notations for
L^{2}
integrals on volume and facet terms are used,

\displaystyle(u,v)_{T}:=\int_{T}u\cdot\overline{v}d\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle(u,v)_{F}:=\int_{F}u\cdot\overline{v}ds,

(6)

and the accordingly induced norms are

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{T}:=\sqrt{(u,u)_{T}},

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{F}:=\sqrt{(u,u)_{F}}.

(7)

It is noteworthy that the second argument is complex conjugated.
The used discontinuous, complex-valued spaces are defined by:

\displaystyle H(\diver,T)

\displaystyle:=\{\sigma\in[L^{2}(T)]^{d}:\diver\sigma\in L^{2}(T)\},

(8)

\displaystyle H(\diver,\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{T\in\mathcal{T}}H(\diver,T),

(9)

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}}L^{2}(F).

(10)

The respective discrete spaces, of the polynomial degree
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, are

\displaystyle\mathcal{RT}^{p}(T)

\displaystyle:=\{\sigma+\mathbf{x}\ q:\sigma\in[\mathcal{P}^{p}(T)]^{d},q\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(T)\}\subset H(\diver,T),

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{RT}^{p}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{RT}^{p}(T)\subset H(\diver,\mathcal{T}),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{p}(\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{P}^{p}(F)\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{F}),

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{p}(\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle:=\prod_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{P}^{p}(T)\subset L^{2}(\Omega),

(14)

where
\mathbf{x}:=(x,y,z)^{\top}
and
\mathcal{P}^{p}
is the space of polynomials with degrees smaller or equal to
p
.
For the discrete, discontinuous compound space the short notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{h}:=\mathcal{RT}^{p}(\mathcal{T})\times\mathcal{P}^{p}(\mathcal{T})\times\mathcal{P}^{p}(\mathcal{F})\times\mathcal{P}^{p}(\mathcal{F})

(15)

is used. The discrete space
\mathcal{RT}
is commonly known as the Raviart-Thomas space, see
[
SZ05
]
.

The consistent HDG formulation introduced in
[
MSS10
]
and considered in this work is defined as follows.

Definition 4

(HDG Formulation)
.

For given
g\in L^{2}({\partial\Omega})
and
\kappa>0
find
\sigma\in H(\diver,\mathcal{T})
,
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\hat{u}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F})
,
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F})
such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}B(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}},u,\hat{u},\tau,\hat{\tau}_{\mathbf{n}},v,\hat{v})=-(g,\hat{v})_{\partial\Omega}\end{split}

(16)

holds for all
\tau\in H(\diver,\mathcal{T})
,
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\hat{v}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F})
,
\hat{\tau}_{\mathbf{n}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{F})

with the sesquilinear form (SLF)

\displaystyle\begin{split}B(\sigma,\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}},u,\hat{u},\tau,\hat{\tau}_{\mathbf{n}},v,\hat{v}):=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\Big(j\kappa(\sigma,\tau)_{T}+(u,\diver\tau)_{T}+(\diver\sigma,v)_{T}-j\kappa(u,v)_{T}\\
&-(\hat{u},\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial T}-(\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n},\hat{v})_{\partial T}-\alpha([u],[v])_{\partial T}+\beta(\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket,\llbracket\tau\rrbracket)_{\partial T}\Big)-(\hat{u},\hat{v})_{\partial\Omega}.\end{split}

(17)

The jumps are defined as

\displaystyle[u]:=u-\hat{u},

\displaystyle\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket:=\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n}-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}},

\displaystyle\text{ on }{\partial T}

(18)

and the stabilisation parameters are
\alpha:=1
,
\beta:=1
.

The normal vector
\mathbf{n}
is per default outward-oriented on
T

and the use as the subscript for the facet variables
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n}}
and
\hat{\tau}_{\mathbf{n}}
indicates that the variables are scalar-valued and their sign changes with respect to the adjacent elements.

Remark 5
.

An interesting property of the HDG formulation is the choice of the
h
-independent parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
.
The reason for the choices in this study is due to the favourable properties for iterative solvers and their preconditioning established in
[
Hub13
,
HPS13
,
HS14
]
.

For methods with static condensation, the sub-problems defined on individual elements must be uniquely and stably solvable.
The HDG formulation (
16
) satisfies that condition and the following discrete absolute stability result holds.

Theorem 6
.

Assuming
H^{2}
-regularity of the adjoint problem, there exists a constant
\tilde{C}>0
, independent of
\kappa,h,\alpha,\beta
,
so that there holds for the discrete solution of the HDG formulation (
16
)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha\lVert[u_{h}]\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}({\partial T})}+\beta\lVert\llbracket\sigma_{h}\rrbracket\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}({\partial T})}+\lVert\hat{u}_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}({\partial\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert g\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}({\partial\Omega})},

(19)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa\lVert\sigma_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa\lVert u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(T)}+\lVert g\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}({\partial\Omega})},

(20)

\displaystyle\lVert u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\kappa,h,\alpha,\beta)\lVert g\rVert_{L^{2}({\partial\Omega})},

(21)

with the constant

\displaystyle C(\kappa,h,\alpha,\beta)=\tilde{C}\Bigg(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta\right)(1+\kappa)^{2}h+2\alpha(1+\kappa)^{2}\kappa^{2}h^{3}+1\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(22)

This theorem implies the existence and uniqueness of the discrete solution for the HDG formulation, without a resolution condition on the discrete space.
The proof uses a similar approach as introduced in
[
LCQ17
]
for Maxwell’s equations.
Two major differences are that only a single facet space is used in the mentioned work and the stabilisation parameter therein is
h
-dependent.
The method in
[
LCQ17
]
is related to the case of choosing
\alpha\approx\frac{1}{h}
,
\beta=0
and omitting the second facet variable in the HDG formulation (
16
).
The main idea of the proof is to use an Aubin-Nitsche technique along with
L^{2}
-projections of
\sigma
and
u
so that volume terms vanish and only facet terms remain.
The real part of the HDG formulation controls those facet terms.
With similar techniques and arguments, the following optimal convergence rates for jumps and the pressure are proven.

Theorem 7
.

Assuming
H^{p+2}
-regularity of the Helmholtz problem, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\kappa,h,\alpha,\beta
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha\lVert[u-u_{h}]\rVert_{L^{2}({\partial T})}^{2}+\beta\|\llbracket\sigma-\sigma_{h}\rrbracket\rVert_{L^{2}({\partial T})}^{2}+\lVert u&-\hat{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}({\partial\Omega})}^{2}\\
&\leq Ch^{2p+1}\Bigg(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\alpha\lVert u\rVert_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}\Bigg).\end{split}

(23)

Theorem 8
.

Assuming
H^{2}
-regularity of the adjoint problem and
H^{p+2}
-regularity of the Helmholtz problem,
there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
\kappa,h,\alpha,\beta
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert u-&u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq Ch^{p+1}\left(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\left(1+\kappa\right)^{2}+2\alpha(1+\kappa)^{2}\kappa^{2}h^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}+2\alpha\lVert u\rVert_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

(24)

Due to the
h
-independent stabilisation, the optimal convergence rate for the flux cannot be straightforwardly derived.
For the following result, a more refined technique based on projections is needed.

Theorem 9
.

Assuming
H^{2}
-regularity of the adjoint problem and
H^{p+2}
-regularity of the Helmholtz problem,
there exists a constant
C(\kappa,\alpha,\beta)>0
, independent of
h
, such that

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma-\sigma_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}{(\Omega)}}\leq C(\kappa,\alpha,\beta)h^{p+1}\left(\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}+\lVert u\rVert_{H^{p+1}(\Omega)}\right).

(25)

The explicit form of
C(\kappa,\alpha,\beta)
can be seen in Section
5
.
The contribution of
[
GM11
]
, which is based upon the techniques developed in
[
CGS10
]
, motivates the proof of this theorem.
A comprehensive and thorough explanation of the later work can be found in
[
Say13
]
.
In these studies, HDG methods with
h
-independent stabilisation are analysed.
The HDG formulation in
[
GM11
]
is similar to the formulation in this work.
The first major difference is that only a single facet variable with a comparable
\alpha
-stabilisation is used
and the second is that existence, uniqueness and optimal convergence rates are proven under the assumption of a resolution condition.
Proving optimal rates for
h
-independently stabilised HDG formulation is challenging,
because element boundary terms need to be estimated via an inverse estimate with volume terms leading to sub-optimal rates.
The idea is to use a, to the HDG formulation tailored, projection into the discrete space, so that boundary terms vanish.
For that purpose, a special projection has been established and analysed in
[
CGS10
]
.
In this study, due to the second
\beta
-stabilisation, that projection cannot be applied.
A generalisation needs to be established for the proof.

3
Discrete Absolute Stability

Usually, for finite element discretisations of the Helmholtz equation, a resolution condition is required to prove the existence and uniqueness of discrete solutions as well as quasi-optimality.
These results hold in the asymptotic regime. Several publications have established the existence and uniqueness for DG and HDG methods without such a condition,
e.g.
[
FW09
,
FW11
,
FX13
,
MPS13
,
MS14
,
Wu14
,
FLX16
]
.
The idea is to use the discrete test function
\mathbf{x}\cdot\grad u_{h}
which is contained in the discrete discontinuous space and leads to positive volume terms.
The disadvantage is that the pre-asymptotic and the asymptotic analysis require different techniques.

A new approach based upon
L^{2}
-projections, which leads to results viable in the pre-asymptotic as well as the asymptotic regime, has been developed in
[
LCQ17
]
.
In this section, the technique therein is applied to the HDG formulation (
16
), with the additional consideration of a second facet variable and
\beta
-stabilisation as well as
h
-independent
\alpha
-stabilisation.

The following lemma establishes the consistency of the adjoint Helmholtz problem in Definition
2
.

Lemma 10

(Weak Adjoint Formulation)
.

The adjoint solution
(\phi,w)
of (
2a
) - (
2c
), satisfies

\displaystyle B(\tau_{h},\hat{\tau}_{\mathbf{n},h},v_{h},\hat{v}_{h},\phi,\phi\cdot\mathbf{n},w,w)=(v_{h},f)_{\Omega},

(26)

for all
(\tau_{h},\hat{\tau}_{\mathbf{n},h},v_{h},\hat{v}_{h})\in\mathcal{X}_{h}
.

Proof.

Conjugating (
2a
) and (
2b
) leads to

\displaystyle-j\kappa\overline{\phi}+\grad\overline{w}

\displaystyle=0,

(27)

\displaystyle-\diver\overline{\phi}+j\kappa\overline{w}

\displaystyle=-\overline{f},

(28)

further multiplying by
\tau_{h}
,
v_{h}
respectively and integrating over
T
gives

\displaystyle-j\kappa(\tau_{h},\phi)_{T}+(\tau_{h},\grad w)_{T}

\displaystyle=0,

(29)

\displaystyle-(v_{h},\diver\phi)_{T}+j\kappa(v_{h},w)_{T}

\displaystyle=-(v_{h},f)_{T}.

(30)

Element wise partial integration of
(\tau_{h},\grad w)_{T}
and summation over all elements results in

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}j\kappa(\tau_{h},\phi)_{T}+(\diver\tau_{h},w)_{T}+(v_{h},\diver\phi)_{T}-j\kappa(v_{h},w)_{T}-(\tau_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n},w)_{\partial T}=(v_{h},f)_{\Omega}.

(31)

Whenever jumps between variables on adjacent elements over facets are considered, then one side is marked by the
+
-subscript and the other by
-
.
The normal continuity of
\phi
on
F_{\rm I}
implies

\displaystyle 0=-(\hat{v}_{h},\phi_{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{+}-\phi_{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{+})_{F_{\rm I}}=-(\hat{v}_{h},\phi_{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{+})_{F_{\rm I}}-(\hat{v}_{h},\phi_{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{-})_{F_{\rm I}}

(32)

and due to the boundary condition (
2c
) there holds

\displaystyle 0=-(\hat{v}_{h},\phi\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}-(\hat{v}_{h},w)_{\partial\Omega}.

(33)

Adding these terms and considering that the
\alpha
- and
\beta
-stabilisations vanish due to the continuity of
\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}
and
w
on element boundaries concludes the proof.
∎

The following first stability results follow by considering the real (
\Re
) and imaginary (
\Im
) part of the SLF
B
separately.
This lemma and the proof of it are similar to
[
LCQ17
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 11
.

For the discrete solution of (
16
) there holds

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha\lVert[u_{h}]\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\beta\lVert\llbracket\sigma_{h}\rrbracket\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\lVert\hat{u}_{h}\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert g\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega},

(34)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa\lVert\sigma_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa\lVert u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}+\lVert g\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}.

(35)

Proof.

By choosing
v_{h}=-u_{h},\tau_{h}=\sigma_{h},\hat{v}_{h}=-\hat{u}_{h},\hat{\tau}_{n,h}=\hat{\sigma}_{n,h}
as test functions
B
has the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}B(\sigma_{h},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n},h},u_{h},\hat{u}_{h},\sigma_{h},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n},h},-u_{h},-\hat{u}_{h})=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}j\kappa\lVert\sigma_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}+2\Im(u_{h},\diver\sigma_{h})_{T}+j\kappa\lVert u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}\\
&-2\Im(\hat{u}_{h},\sigma_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial T}+\alpha\lVert[u_{h}]\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\beta\lVert\llbracket\sigma_{h}\rrbracket\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\lVert\hat{u}_{h}\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}.\end{split}

(36)

Taking the real part leads to

\displaystyle\alpha\lVert[u_{h}]\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\beta\lVert\llbracket\sigma_{h}\rrbracket\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\lVert\hat{u}_{h}\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}=\Re(g,\hat{u}_{h})_{\partial\Omega}

(37)

directly implying (
34
).
Similarly, choosing
v_{h}=u_{h},\tau_{h}=\sigma_{h},\hat{v}_{h}=\hat{u}_{h},\hat{\tau}_{n,h}=\hat{\sigma}_{n,h}
as test functions gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}B(\sigma_{h},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n},h},u_{h},\hat{u}_{h},\sigma_{h},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n},h},u_{h},\hat{u}_{h})=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}j\kappa\lVert\sigma_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}+2\Re(u_{h},\diver\sigma_{h})_{T}-j\kappa\lVert u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}\\
&-2\Re(\hat{u}_{h},\sigma_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial T}-\alpha\lVert[u_{h}]\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}+\beta\lVert\llbracket\sigma_{h}\rrbracket\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}-\lVert\hat{u}_{h}\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}.\end{split}

(38)

Taking the imaginary part yields

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\kappa\lVert\sigma_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}-\kappa\lVert u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{T}=-\Im(g,\hat{u}_{h})_{\partial\Omega},

(39)

and with (
34
), which implies
\lVert\hat{u}_{h}\rVert_{\partial\Omega}\leq\lVert g\rVert_{\partial\Omega}
, gives (
35
).
∎

Lemma
11
shows that
\hat{u}_{h}
and the discrete jumps are bounded by the excitation,
and if
u_{h}
can be bounded then the same follows for
\sigma_{h}
.
The result highlights the favourable choice of real, positive
\alpha
and
\beta
.
The adjoint problem in combination with an Aubin-Nitsche type technique is used to bound
u_{h}
.

Lemma 12
.

For the solution
(\phi,w)
of (
2a
) - (
2c
) there holds

\displaystyle\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}=\lVert\phi\cdot\mathbf{n}\rVert^{2}_{\partial\Omega}\leq C\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{\Omega},

(40)

with a constant
C>0
independent of
\kappa
.

Proof.

The first equality immediately follows from (
2c
).
Multiplying (
2a
) and (
2b
) by
\overline{\phi}
and
\overline{w}
respectively gives

\displaystyle j\kappa\lVert\phi\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}+(\grad w,\phi)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(41)

\displaystyle j\kappa\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}+(\diver\phi,w)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=(f,w)_{\Omega}.

(42)

Partial integration of
(\grad w,\phi)_{\Omega}
leads to

\displaystyle j\kappa\lVert\phi\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}-(w,\diver\phi)_{\Omega}+(w,\phi\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(43)

By conjugating and adding the equations the terms
(\diver\phi,w)_{\Omega}
cancel out and

\displaystyle j\kappa\lVert w\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}-j\kappa\lVert\phi\rVert^{2}_{\Omega}+(\phi\cdot\mathbf{n},w)_{\partial\Omega}=(f,w)_{\Omega}

(44)

remains.
Using (
2c

In the case of coercive weak formulations optimal convergence rates are proven with coercivity, Galerkin orthogonality and continuity.
The Helmholtz equation does not satisfy coercivity, therefore other techniques need to be established.
Usually, a Schatz argument is applied to derive asymptotic results, but these depend on a resolution condition.
The
L^{2}
-projections circumvent this necessity.

For the analysis, the following projected errors, contained in the discontinuous space
\mathcal{X}_{h}

Definition 15
.

\displaystyle e_{\sigma}:=\Pi\sigma-\sigma_{h},

\displaystyle e_{u}:=\Pi u-u_{h},

\displaystyle e_{\hat{u}}:=\Pi_{F}u-\hat{u}_{h},

\displaystyle e_{\hat{\sigma}}:=\Pi_{F}\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n}-\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n},h}.

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{\Omega}=\lVert u-\Pi u+\Pi u-u_{h}\rVert_{\Omega}\leq\lVert u-\Pi u\rVert_{\Omega}+\lVert\Pi u-u_{h}\rVert_{\Omega}.

(67)

The first part only depends on the approximation properties of the projection.
The second part holds the advantage that
\Pi u-u_{h}
is a viable choice as a discrete test function.
Therefore, if the projection has optimal approximation properties and if the projected error has an optimal convergence rate, then the discrete solution has an optimal rate as well.

For the projected errors the Galerkin orthogonality does not hold, but they satisfy the following discrete weak formulation.

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{n},h}

\displaystyle\text{ on }F_{\rm O}

\displaystyle=\{u_{h}\}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}[\sigma_{h}]_{\mathbf{n}}:=\frac{1}{2}(u_{h,+}+u_{h,-})-\frac{1}{2\alpha}(\sigma_{h,+}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{+}+\sigma_{h,-}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{-})

\displaystyle\text{ on }F_{\rm I},

(84a)

\displaystyle\hat{u}_{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(\Pi_{F}g-\sigma_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}+\alpha u_{h})

(84b)

The same holds for the continuous facet variables

Proof.

With the previous lemma the facet variables can be eliminated from the HDG formulation and the volume variables satisfy the following DG formulation.

Lemma 18

(DG Formulation)
.

For given

and
\kappa>0
find
\sigma\in H(\diver,\mathcal{T})
,
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{DG}(\sigma,u,\tau,v)=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(g,\tau\cdot\mathbf{n}-\alpha v)_{\partial\Omega}\end{split}

(90)

holds for all
\tau\in H(\diver,\mathcal{T})
,

with

,
\beta:=1
and the SLF

\displaystyle\begin{split}B_{DG}(\sigma,u,\tau,v):=&\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}j\kappa(\sigma,\tau)_{T}+(u,\diver\tau)_{T}+(\diver\sigma,v)_{T}-j\kappa(u,v)_{T}\\
+&\sum_{F_{\rm I}\in\mathcal{F}}-(\{u\},[\tau]_{\mathbf{n}})_{F_{\rm I}}-([\sigma]_{\mathbf{n}},\{v\})_{F_{\rm I}}-\frac{\alpha}{2}([u]_{+},[v]_{+})_{F_{\rm I}}+\left(\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\frac{\beta}{2}\right)([\sigma]_{\mathbf{n}},[\tau]_{\mathbf{n}})_{F_{\rm I}}\\
+&\ \frac{1}{1+\alpha}\left((\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n},\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}-\alpha\left((u,\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}+(\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n},v)_{\partial\Omega}+(u,v)_{\partial\Omega}\right)\right).\end{split}

(91)

The scalar jump on inner facets is defined as
[u]_{+}:=u_{+}-u_{-}

Proof.

With Lemma
17
the facet variables on inner facets are eliminated. Terms on adjacent elements are combined into DG form:

\displaystyle\beta(\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket_{+},\tau_{+}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{+})_{F_{\rm I}}+\beta(\llbracket\sigma\rrbracket_{-},\tau_{-}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{-})_{F_{\rm I}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2}([\sigma]_{\mathbf{n}},[\tau]_{\mathbf{n}})_{F_{\rm I}},

(92)

\displaystyle-(\hat{u},[\tau]_{\mathbf{n}})_{F_{\rm I}}

\displaystyle=-(\{u\}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}[\sigma]_{\mathbf{n}},[\tau]_{\mathbf{n}})_{F_{\rm I}},

(93)

\displaystyle-\alpha([u]_{+},v_{+})_{F}-\alpha([u]_{-},v_{-})_{F_{\rm I}}

\displaystyle=-([\sigma]_{\mathbf{n}},\{v\})_{F_{\rm I}}-\frac{\alpha}{2}([u]_{+},[v]_{+})_{F_{\rm I}}.

(94)

Replacing
\hat{u}
on the domain boundary leads to

\displaystyle\begin{split}-(\hat{u},\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}-\alpha(u-\hat{u},v)_{\partial\Omega}=&-\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(g-\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n}+\alpha u),\tau\cdot\mathbf{n}\right)_{\partial\Omega}\\
&-\alpha\left(u-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(g-\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n}+\alpha u),v\right)_{\partial\Omega}\\
=&\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n},\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}(u,\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(g,\tau\cdot\mathbf{n})_{\partial\Omega}\\
&-\alpha(u,v)_{\partial\Omega}-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}(\sigma\cdot\mathbf{n},v)_{\partial\Omega}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha}(u,v)_{\partial\Omega}+\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}(g,v)_{\partial\Omega}\\

&-\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(g,\tau\cdot\mathbf{n}-\alpha v)_{\partial\Omega}.\end{split}

(95)

C
changes in each line, but stays independent of
h,\kappa,\alpha,\beta
.
Due to (
125
) Lemma
23
can be applied yielding

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\lVert\delta_{\sigma}\rVert^{2}_{T}\leq&\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\beta\right)Ch\lVert\delta_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rVert^{2}_{\partial T}\\
=&\ Ch((u-P_{u},\delta_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n})_{{\partial T}\setminus{\partial\Omega}}+\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}([u-P_{u}],\delta_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n})_{{\partial T}\setminus{\partial\Omega}}\\
&+(1+\alpha\beta)(u-P_{u},\delta_{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n})_{{\partial T}\cap{\partial\Omega}})\\
\leq&\ C(1+\alpha\beta)h^{p+1}|u|_{H^{p+1}}\lVert\delta_{\sigma}\rVert_{T},\end{split}

(129)

which implies

\displaystyle\lVert\delta_{\sigma}\rVert_{T}\leq C\frac{1+\alpha\beta}{\alpha^{-1}+\beta}h^{p+1}|u|_{H^{p+1}}

(130)

and finally gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\sigma-P_{\sigma}(\sigma,u)\rVert_{\Omega}&\leq\lVert\sigma-\mathcal{RT}(\sigma)\rVert_{\Omega}+\lVert\delta_{\sigma}\rVert_{\Omega}\\
&\leq Ch^{p+1}\left(|\sigma|_{H^{p+1}}+\frac{1+\alpha\beta}{\alpha^{-1}+\beta}|u|_{H^{p+1}}\right).\end{split}

(131)

∎

With this result, the error estimate for the flux can be finalised.

Proof of Theorem
9
.

According to Lemma
19
in combination with the projection in Definition
20
there holds

\displaystyle\begin{split}\kappa\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\lVert e_{\sigma}^{P}\rVert_{T}^{2}=&-\Im\Big(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}j\kappa(\sigma-P_{\sigma}(\sigma,u),e_{\sigma}^{P})_{T}-j\kappa\lVert e_{u}^{P}\rVert^{2}_{T}\Big)\\
\leq&\ \kappa\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}|(\sigma-P_{\sigma}(\sigma,u),e_{\sigma}^{P})_{T}|+\lVert e_{u}^{P}\rVert^{2}_{T}\\
\leq&\ \kappa\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\lVert\sigma-P_{\sigma}(\sigma,u)\rVert_{T}\lVert e_{\sigma}^{P}\rVert_{T}+\lVert e_{u}^{P}\rVert^{2}_{T}.\end{split}

(132)

Applying Young’s inequality, Lemma
24
for
\sigma-P_{\sigma}
and Theorem
8
for
e_{u}^{P}
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\kappa\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\lVert e_{\sigma}^{P}\rVert_{T}^{2}&\leq\kappa\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\lVert\sigma-P_{\sigma}(\sigma,u)\rVert_{T}^{2}+2\lVert e_{u}^{P}\rVert^{2}_{T}\\
&\leq\kappa Ch^{2p+2}\left(\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H^{p+1}}^{2}+\left(\frac{1+\alpha\beta}{\alpha^{-1}+\beta}\right)^{2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{p+1}}^{2}\right)\\
&+\kappa Ch^{2p+2}\left(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\left(1+\kappa\right)^{2}+2\alpha(1+\kappa)^{2}\kappa^{2}h^{2}\right)\left(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta\right)\lVert\sigma\rVert_{H^{p+1}}^{2}+2\alpha\lVert u\rVert_{H^{p+1}}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(133)

Considering

\displaystyle\lVert\sigma-\sigma_{h}\rVert_{\Omega}\leq\lVert\sigma-P_{\sigma}(\sigma,u)\rVert_{\Omega}+\lVert e_{\sigma}^{P}\rVert_{\Omega}

(134)

and applying Lemma
24

concludes the proof.
∎
