Integrability of Nonabelian Differential-Difference Equations: the Symmetry Approach
Vladimir Novikov, Jing Ping Wang

Abstract
We propose a novel approach to tackle integrability problem for evolutionary differential-difference equations (D Δ \Delta Es) on free associative algebras, also referred to as nonabelian D Δ \Delta Es. This approach enables us to derive necessary integrability conditions, determine the integrability of a given equation, and make progress in the classification of integrable nonabelian D Δ \Delta Es. This work involves establishing symbolic representations for the nonabelian difference algebra, difference operators, and formal series, as well as introducing a novel quasi-local extension for the algebra of formal series within the context of symbolic representations. Applying this formalism, we solve the classification problem of integrable skew-symmetric quasi-linear nonabelian equations of orders ( − 1 , 1 ) (-1,1) , ( − 2 , 2 ) (-2,2) , and ( − 3 , 3 ) (-3,3) , consequently revealing some new equations in the process.



1
Introduction

In the recent paper
[
1
]
, Mikhailov, Novikov and Wang presented the perturbative symmetry approach for evolutionary differential-difference equations (D
\Delta
Es), which is based on establishing a symbolic representation for the difference polynomial ring and introducing its quasi-local extension. This approach enabled the derivation of necessary integrability conditions for evolutionary D
\Delta
Es of arbitrary order and consequently allowed to solve some classification problems of integrable equations for any given order.
In this work, the existence of higher symmetries of a D
\Delta
Es, or more precisely, an infinite hierarchy of higher symmetries, is taken as the criterion for its integrability.

This paper can be considered as an extension of this research, transitioning from the commutative (abelian) case to the noncommutative (nonabelian) case.
Our focus lies in exploring the integrability conditions for evolutionary D
\Delta
Es when field variables take values in an associative, but noncommutative algebra.
Typical examples of associative algebras include matrix or operator algebras. Matrix formulations of integrable equations trace back to the very beginning of integrable systems theory. The first matrix integrable equation, known as the matrix KdV equation, was introduced by P. Lax in
[
2
]
. One striking feature observed early on in integrable matrix equations is that their algebraic structures such as symmetries, conservation laws, and Lax representations are independent of individual matrix entries and the sizes of matrices. Instead, they are expressed as polynomials or formal series in field variables. Consequently, field variables can be treated as elements of an associative, noncommutative algebra. When considering nonabelian D
\Delta
Es, we exclusively employ the intrinsic algebraic operations of multiplication, addition and
scalar multiplication, irrespective of any specific concrete realisation.

The systematic classification of integrable partial differential equations (PDEs) with field variables in an associative algebra was initiated by
Olver and Sokolov
[
3
,
4
]
. They provided a comprehensive list of integrable scalar evolution PDEs of this type, characterised by higher order symmetries. The completeness of this classification was demonstrated in
[
5
]
. In 2000, Mikhailov and Sokolov
[
6
]
successfully developed the theory of integrable ordinary differential equations on associative algebras. They solved a number of classification problems using the existence of hierarchies of first integrals and/or symmetries as a criterion for integrability.
In a recent contribution
[
7
]
, Adler and Sokolov extended classifications to nonabelian generalisations of integrable systems of nonlinear Schrödinger and Boussinesq types. Their approach relied on both the existence of higher conservation laws and higher symmetries, which are determined by their corresponding abelian integrable systems.
Additionally, the classification of non-abelian Painlevé type systems was addressed in
[
8
]
, introducing non-abelian constants into the equations.

Nonabelian integrable D
\Delta
Es have historically emerged as direct matrix generalisations and discretisations of matrix integrable PDEs. The matrix Volterra chain was initially introduced in
[
9
]
. Further examples of integrable D
\Delta
Es on associative algebras, including the nonabelian Bogoyavlensky equation, were obtained in
[
10
]
. The matrix Toda lattice initially originated from a discrete version of the principal chiral field model
[
11
,
12
]
and has recently appeared in the study of Matrix-valued Hermite polynomials
[
13
]
. The algebraic structures underlying these equations, such as recursion operators and Hamiltonian structures, have been extensively investigated in
[
14
,
15
]
.

The symmetry approach based on a concept of formal recursion operator has been formulated and developed in works of Shabat and co-authors
(see for example review papers
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
). It has become one of powerful tools for addressing the integrability problem.
Formal recursion operator carries information
about integrability, remaining robust even in the presence of gaps within the infinite hierarchy of symmetries or conservation laws.
There have been numerous exhaustive classification results of abelian integrable PDEs including nonlocal and/or non-evolutionary equations such as the Benjamin–Ono equation and the Camassa–Holm equation. The symmetry approach has recently been extended to D
\Delta
Es in
[
1
]
following the introduction of symbolic representation of the difference polynomial ring and its proper ring extension. However, the exploration of this approach in the realm of nonabelian integrable systems remains uncharted territory.

In this paper, we develop the perturbative symmetry approach, built upon the recent work in
[
1
]
, to investigate the integrability problem concerning evolutionary D
\Delta
Es

u_{t}=F(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q}),\quad p\leq q\in\mathbb{Z}

(1)

of order
(p,q)
, where
u=u(n,t)
is a function of a discrete variable
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and a continuous variable
t\in{\mathbb{C}}
, taking values in an associative algebra. We adopt standard notation:

u_{k}=S^{k}u(n,t)=u(n+k,t),\quad u_{t}=\partial_{t}u,

and
S
is the shift operator. One main challenge from the abelian to nonabelian case is to distinguish between the noncommutative multiplication on the
left and on the right.
This requires building a symbolic representation, taking into account of the noncommutative multiplication, for nonabelian difference polynomial ring, a ring of difference operators, quasi-local extensions of rings and formal pseudo-difference series with quasi-local coefficients.

Similar to the abelian scenario in
[
1
]
, the integrability conditions for (
1
) are given in terms of the coefficients of a canonical formal recursion operator. We prove that if an equation possesses an infinite dimensional algebra of symmetries, irrespective of their orders, then it possesses a unique canonical formal recursion operator. The coefficients of the canonical formal recursion operator can be explicitly determined in terms of the symbolic representation of the equation. Moreover, if the equation is integrable, these coefficients must be quasi-local. We apply the developed formalism to classify integrable quasi-linear skew-symmetric equations (
1
) of orders
(-1,1),(-2,2)
and
(-3,3)
.

We start the paper with the fundamental algebraic framework necessary for investigating nonabelian evolutionary D
\Delta
Es. In Section
2
we introduce essential definitions and concepts of nonabelian difference algebra. This includes the grading of the algebra, derivations, evolutionary derivations, as well as algebras of local difference operators and formal series.
We then define algebras of symmetries and approximate symmetries as well as the notion of integrability and approximate integrability in Section
3
.

The symbolic representation serves as the primary computational tool. In Section
4
we define the symbolic representation of the nonabelian difference algebra, difference operators and formal series. We formulate criteria of approximate integrability in the symbolic representation. For a given evolutionary equation we provide a recursive formula for the coefficients of its symmetries (Theorem
1
). We show that these coefficients are uniquely determined by the linear part of the symmetry. This result can be used to determine the existence of
fixed-order symmetries as well as to derive the integrability conditions if the orders of symmetries are known.

The main result on universal integrability conditions for evolutionary D
\Delta
Es on free associative algebras is Theorem
3
presented in Section
5
.
The universal integrability conditions are the conditions on the coefficients of the canonical formal recursion operator: the coefficients must be quasi-local.
The proof is based on computation of fractional powers of formal series with local coefficients. It is shown that coefficients of fractional powers of local formal series are quasi-local, i.e. belong to the appropriate algebraic extension of the algebra of formal series.
Furthermore, for a given equation these coefficients are recursively determined in terms of the symbolic representation of the equation (Theorem
2
).

In Section
6
we apply the developed theory to classify integrable quasi-linear skew-symmetric equations (
1
) of orders
(-1,1),(-2,2)
and
(-3,3)
. We list only equations which do not possess symmetries of lower orders. We prove integrability of each of these equations either by providing their Lax representations or by giving an explicit Miura type transformation to a known integrable equation. We present below two examples of new equations, to the best of our knowledge, resulting from the classification:

u_{t}=uu_{1}\cdots u_{n}(u+\alpha)-(u+\alpha)u_{-n}\cdots u_{1}u,\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}}

(2)

where
\alpha
is constant, and

u_{t}=(uu_{-1}+1)(uu_{1}+1)u_{2}-u_{-2}(u_{-1}u+1)(u_{1}u+1).

(3)

Equation (
2
) possesses the Lax representation
L_{t}=[A,L]
,

L=(u+\alpha)S^{-n}+\lambda uS,\quad A=uu_{1}\cdots u_{n}(1+\lambda S^{n+1}),

where
\alpha
is an essential constant and cannot be removed via invertible transformations.
The reduction
\alpha=0
of this equation appears in
[
10
]
.

Equation (
3
) possesses the Lax representation

L=P^{-1}Q,\quad P=(1-S^{-2})u_{1}^{-1},\quad Q=\left((uu_{1}+1)(u_{2}u_{1}+1)S^{2}-1\right)u_{-1}^{-1}

A=(u_{1}u+1)(u_{1}u_{2}+1)S^{2}-u_{-1}\left((uu_{1}+1)(u_{2}u_{1}+1)-1\right)u_{1}^{-1}-S^{-2}.

The abelian version of this equation was found and studied in
[

]
.

We conclude the paper by provide a brief summary of our results and concluding remarks on the implications and potential directions for future studies.

2
Derivations, difference operators and formal series

In this section, we introduce basic definitions and notations of objects and constructions for nonabelian difference algebras required for the aims of this paper. The detailed abelian analogue can be found, for example, in
[
1
]
. Further details regarding Hamiltonian structures of nonabelian D
\Delta
Es, as well as their connection to the problem of quantization of integrable systems, can be found in
[
14
,
20
]
.

2.1
Difference algebra

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}=\langle u_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\rangle
be a free associative unital algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
generated by an infinite set of non-commuting variables
u_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and a unit element
e
, defined as

e\cdot f(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q})=f(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q})\cdot e=f(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q}),\quad f(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q})\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

Elements of algebra are polynomials (over
{\mathbb{C}}
) in variables
u_{n},\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We use the notation
\alpha\cdot e\equiv\alpha,\,\,\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}},

and often write
u
instead of
u_{0}
without causing a confusion.

We define on
{\mathfrak{A}}
an automorphism
S:\,{\mathfrak{A}}\to{\mathfrak{A}}
by the action

S(f(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q}))=f(u_{p+1},\ldots,u_{q+1}),\quad S(e)=e,\quad f(u_{p},\ldots,u_{q})\in{\mathfrak{A}},

and call the automorphism
S
the
shift operator
. The algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
together with the shift operator
S
is a difference algebra.

Let
T
and
I
denote the transposition and involution automorphisms respectively, defined as follows:

T(u_{n})=u_{n},\quad T(ab)=T(b)T(a),\quad T(e)=e,\quad a,b,\in{\mathfrak{A}}

(4)

and

I(u_{n})=u_{-n},\quad I(ab)=I(a)I(b),\quad I(e)=e,\quad a,b,\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

Thus their composition
{\cal T}=TI
yields:

{\cal T}(u_{n})=u_{-n},\quad{\cal T}(ab)={\cal T}(b){\cal T}(a),\quad{\cal T}(e)=e,\quad a,b,\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

(5)

The automorphism
{\cal T}
induces a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
grading on
{\mathfrak{A}}
:

{\mathfrak{A}}={\mathfrak{A}}^{0}\oplus{\mathfrak{A}}^{1},\quad{\mathfrak{A}}^{0}\cdot{\mathfrak{A}}^{0}={\mathfrak{A}}^{0},\quad{\mathfrak{A}}^{0}\cdot{\mathfrak{A}}^{1}={\mathfrak{A}}^{1}\cdot{\mathfrak{A}}^{0}={\mathfrak{A}}^{1},\quad{\mathfrak{A}}^{1}\cdot{\mathfrak{A}}^{1}={\mathfrak{A}}^{0},

where

{\mathfrak{A}}^{i}=\{a\in{\mathfrak{A}}\,|\,{\cal T}(a)=(-1)^{i}a\},\quad i=0,1.

We refer to elements of
{\mathfrak{A}}^{0}
as
symmetric
and elements of
{\mathfrak{A}}^{1}
as
skew-symmetric
.

2.2
Derivations

A derivation
{\cal D}
of the algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear map satisfying the Leibnitz’s rule

{\cal D}(\alpha f+\beta g)=\alpha{\cal D}(f)+\beta{\cal D}(g),\quad{\cal D}(fg)={\cal D}(f)g+f{\cal D}(g),\quad f,g\in{\mathfrak{A}},\quad\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}.

For any derivation
{\cal D}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
we have
{\cal D}(e)=0
for the derivation of the unit in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Each derivation can be uniquely defined by its action on the generators
u_{n}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
.

For any two derivations
{\cal D},{\cal D}^{\prime}
their commutator
[{\cal D},{\cal D}^{\prime}]={\cal D}\circ{\cal D}^{\prime}-{\cal D}^{\prime}\circ{\cal D}
is also a derivation on
{\mathfrak{A}}
. Any
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear combination of derivations is a derivation, and any triple of derivations
{\cal D},{\cal D}^{\prime},{\cal D}^{\prime\prime}
satisfies the Jacobi identity

[{\cal D},[{\cal D}^{\prime},{\cal D}^{\prime\prime}]]+[{\cal D}^{\prime\prime},[{\cal D},{\cal D}^{\prime}]]+[{\cal D}^{\prime},[{\cal D}^{\prime\prime},{\cal D}]]=0,

and thus the set of derivations of
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a Lie algebra.

Define derivations
X_{k},\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
as

X_{k}(u_{j})=\delta_{kj}u_{k},

and let

X=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}X_{k}

The difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
has a natural grading

{\mathfrak{A}}=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}{\mathfrak{A}}_{p},\quad{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}=\{f\in{\mathfrak{A}}\,|\,X(f)=pf\}.

(6)

Every element
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}
can be uniquely represented as a sum of homogeneous components
f=\sum_{p\geq 0}f_{p},\,\,f_{p}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}
(some components may be equal to zero).
The subspace
{\mathfrak{A}}_{0}
consists of elements of the form
\alpha\cdot e\equiv\alpha,\,\,\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
, while the subspaces
{\mathfrak{A}}_{1},{\mathfrak{A}}_{2},\ldots
consist of linear, quadratic etc polynomials in generators
u_{k}
.
The projection
\pi_{k}:{\mathfrak{A}}\to{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}
selecting
k
-th homogeneous component is defined as

\pi_{k}(f)=\pi_{k}(\sum_{p\geq 0}f_{p})=f_{k}.

(7)

An important class of derivations of a difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
are
evolutionary
derivations:

Definition 1
.

A derivation
{\cal D}
is called evolutionary if it commutes with the shift operator
S
.

An evolutionary derivation is completely defined by its action on the generator
u
, that is

{\cal D}(u)=a,\quad{\cal D}(u_{k})=S^{k}(a),\quad a\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

The element
a\in{\mathfrak{A}}
is called the characteristic of an evolutionary derivation. We denote by
{\cal D}_{a}
an evolutionary derivation with the characteristic
a
.

A commutator of two evolutionary derivations
{\cal D}_{a}
and
{\cal D}_{b}
is also an evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{c}=[{\cal D}_{a},{\cal D}_{b}]
with the characteristic
c={\cal D}_{a}(b)-{\cal D}_{b}(a)
. Evolutionary derivations form a Lie subalgebra of the Lie algebra of derivations on
{\mathfrak{A}}
.

The notion of an evolutionary derivation allows to introduce a Lie algebra structure on
{\mathfrak{A}}
: for every
a,b\in{\mathfrak{A}}
, we define a Lie bracket as

[a,b]={\cal D}_{a}(b)-{\cal D}_{b}(a).

It can be rewritten in terms of the Fréchet derivatives.

Definition 2
.

A Fréchet derivative of an element
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}
is defined as

f_{*}(a)={\cal D}_{a}(f),\quad\forall a\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

(8)

Thus, the Lie bracket becomes

[a,b]=b_{*}(a)-a_{*}(b),\quad a,b\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

(9)

It is easy to see that the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
is graded with respect to the natural grading (
6
):

[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n},{\mathfrak{A}}_{m}]\subset{\mathfrak{A}}_{n+m-1}.

2.3
Difference operators and formal difference series

In this section, we extend the notion of difference operators/formal series (for the abelian case, refer to
[
1
]
) to the non-commutative case and introduce the algebra of formal series
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
.

Let
{\cal L}_{a}
and
{\cal R}_{a}
be the left and right multiplication operators on the algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
, defined as:

{\cal L}_{a}(f)=af\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\cal R}_{a}(f)=fa,

where
a,f\in{\mathfrak{A}}.
These operators satisfy the following relations:

{\cal L}_{a}{\cal R}_{b}={\cal R}_{b}{\cal L}_{a},\quad{\cal L}_{a}{\cal L}_{b}={\cal L}_{ab}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\cal R}_{a}{\cal R}_{b}={\cal R}_{ba}

for all
a,b\in{\mathfrak{A}}
. We denote by
{\cal M}
the algebra of the left and right multiplication operators.
This algebra inherits the natural grading (
6
) on
{\mathfrak{A}}
:

{\cal M}=\bigoplus_{p,q\geq 0}{\cal M}_{p,q},\quad{\cal M}_{p,q}{\cal M}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}\subset{\cal M}_{p+p^{\prime},q+q^{\prime}},

(10)

where each element in the
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear subspace
{\cal M}_{p,q}
is a finite linear combination of terms of the form

{\cal L}_{a}{\cal R}_{b},\,a\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{p},b\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{q}
. We represent it as

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{p,q}=\Big\{\sum_{\gamma=1}^{k}{\cal L}_{a^{(\gamma)}}{\cal R}_{b^{(\gamma)}}\,|\,a^{(\gamma)}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{p},b^{(\gamma)}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{q},k\in{\mathbb{N}}\Big\}.

(11)

Definition 3
.

A local difference operator of order
\,\mbox{ord}\,B:=(p,q)
is of the form

B=\sum_{i=p}^{q}b^{(i)}S^{i},\quad b^{(i)}\in{\cal M},\quad b^{(p)}\neq 0,\ b^{(q)}\neq 0,\quad p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}},\quad p\leq q

and its total order is
\,\mbox{Ord}\,B:=p-q
.
A local formal difference series
B
is defined as

B=\sum_{i\leq p}b^{(i)}S^{i},\quad b^{(i)}=\sum_{j,k\geq 0}b_{jk}^{(i)}\in{\cal M},\quad b_{jk}^{(i)}\in{\cal M}_{j,k}\quad b^{(p)}\neq 0,\quad p\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

(12)

The action of a local difference operator on
{\mathfrak{A}}
is well-defined if we set

{\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{n}(h)=fS^{n}(h)g,\quad f,g,h\in{\mathfrak{A}},\ n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

The action of a formal series on
{\mathfrak{A}}
is generally not well-defined.

The Fréchet derivative of any element in
{\mathfrak{A}}
, as defined by (
8
), serves as an example of a local difference operator. For a non-constant element
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}
, we define its order and total order as those for
f_{*}
.

Example 1
.

Consider the nonabelian Volterra equation

u_{t}=uu_{1}-u_{-1}u=f.

(13)

Note that

f_{*}={\cal L}_{u}S+{\cal R}_{u_{1}}-{\cal L}_{u_{-1}}-{\cal R}_{u}S^{-1}.

Thus the nonabelian Volterra equation has the order
(-1,1)
and the total order
2
.

Local formal series on
{\mathfrak{A}}
form a unital associative non-commutative algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
with the standard addition
and the multiplication rule (defined on monomials) given by

{\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{n}\cdot{\cal L}_{f^{\prime}}{\cal R}_{g^{\prime}}S^{m}={\cal L}_{fS^{n}(f^{\prime})}{\cal R}_{S^{n}(g^{\prime})g}S^{n+m},\quad f,g,f^{\prime},g^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{A}},\quad\ n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

The action of an evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{a}
on an element
{\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i}
in
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
is defined as

{\cal D}_{a}({\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i})={\cal L}_{{\cal D}_{a}(f)}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i}+{\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{{\cal D}_{a}(g)}S^{i}={\cal L}_{f_{*}(a)}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i}+{\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g_{*}(a)}S^{i}.

(14)

Thus it is well-defined on any formal series in
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
.

The algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
inherits the natural grading (
10
) on
{\cal M}
:

{\mathfrak{A}}((S))=\bigoplus_{p,q\geq 0}{\mathfrak{A}}_{p,q}((S)),\quad{\mathfrak{A}}_{p,q}((S))\cdot{\mathfrak{A}}_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}((S))\subset{\mathfrak{A}}_{p+p^{\prime},q+q^{\prime}}((S)),

(15)

where each
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear subspace
{\mathfrak{A}}_{p,q}((S))
consists of formal series defined by (
12
) when
b^{(i)}\in{\cal M}_{p,q}
.

3
Symmetries and approximate symmetries

We begin with defining an evolutionary D
\Delta
Es on free associative algebras.
Assume that the generators
u_{k}
of the free associative algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
depend on
t\in{\mathbb{C}}
. We identify every evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{f}
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
with a D
\Delta
E on
{\mathfrak{A}}
, namely,

u_{t}={\cal D}_{f}(u)=f,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{A}}.

(16)

In particular, we have
u_{n,t}=S^{n}(f),\,\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. The order of a D
\Delta
E is the order of
f
, that is, the order of its Fréchet derivative
f_{*}
.
Evolution with respect to
t
of any element
a\in{\mathfrak{A}}
is therefore given by
a_{t}={\cal D}_{f}(a)=a_{*}(f)
.

We call an evolutionary equation (
16
)
skew-symmetric
if
f
is skew-symmetric, i.e.,
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{1}

satisfying
{\cal T}(f)=-f
under the action of automorphism
{\cal T}
defined by (
5
).

3.1
Symmetries of evolutionary D
\Delta
Es

Definition 4
.

We say that
g\in{\mathfrak{A}}
is a symmetry of a D
\Delta
E (
16
) if the Lie bracket (
9
) between
f
and
g
vanishes, that is,
[g,f]=0
.

For every element
g\in{\mathfrak{A}}
, we associate an evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{g}
. The condition that
g
is a symmetry of (
16
) is equivalent to the evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{g}
commuting with the evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{f}
, i.e.
[{\cal D}_{f},{\cal D}_{g}]=0
. If we assume that the generators
u_{k}
also depend on
\tau\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the conventional way of representing a symmetry of equation (
16
) is to associate the evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{g}
with a D
\Delta
E:

u_{\tau}={\cal D}_{g}(u)=g.

A linear combination
\alpha g_{1}+\beta g_{2}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
, of two symmetries
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, as well as their Lie bracket
[g_{1},g_{2}]
are also symmetries of the equation (
16
). Thus the set of all symmetries of the equation (
16
), denoted by

{\cal C}_{f}=\{g\in{\mathfrak{A}}\,|\,[g,f]=0\},

forms a Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{A}}
. This subalgebra is the centraliser of
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}
.

The characteristic property of integrable systems is the existence of an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra of symmetries. Therefore, we adopt the following definition of integrability:

Definition 5
.

A nonabelian D
\Delta
E (
16
) is called integrable if its Lie algebra of symmetries
{\cal C}_{f}
is infinite dimensional and contains symmetries of arbitrarily high total order, in the sense that for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the equation possesses a symmetry
g
of total order
\,\mbox{Ord}\,\,g\geq N
.

Example 2
.

For the nonabelian Volterra equation (
13
),
one can directly verify that

u_{\tau}=uu_{1}u_{2}-u_{-2}u_{-1}u+uu_{1}^{2}+u^{2}u_{1}-u_{-1}^{2}u-u_{-1}u^{2}

is a symmetry of order
(-2,2)
and the total order
4
. It is known that the nonabelian Volterra equation possesses infinitely many symmetries of order
(-n,n),\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. These can be explicitly found either from the Lax representation
[
10
]
or by means of the recursion operator
[
15
]
.

3.2
Approximate symmetries

Based on the gradation on
{\mathfrak{A}}
, we shall introduce the notion of approximate symmetries.

Consider a nonabelian D
\Delta
E (
16
)
and represent
f
as a sum of homogeneous components

f=f_{0}+\cdots+f_{N},\quad f_{k}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{k},\quad k=0,\ldots,N.

According to Definition
4
the element
g=g_{0}+\cdots+g_{M},\,\,g_{i}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{i},\,i=0,\ldots,M
is a symmetry if
[f,g]=0
. Due to the natural gradation on the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
, the vanishing of the Lie bracket is equivalent to

\sum_{k=0}^{p}[f_{k},g_{p-k}]=0,\quad p=0,1,\ldots,N+M,

(17)

where we assume
f_{k}=0,\,k>N
and
g_{k^{\prime}}=0,\,k^{\prime}>M
. We call
g

an approximate symmetry of degree
s
or
s
-approximate symmetry
if the first
s+2
relations of (
17
) with
0\leq p\leq s+1
are satisfied. In other words,
g
is an approximate symmetry of degree
s
if

\pi_{k}([f,g])=0,\quad k=0,\ldots,s.

Similar to symmetries, all
s
-approximate symmetries form a Lie subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{A}}
:

{\cal C}_{f}^{s}=\{g\in{\mathfrak{A}}\,|\,\pi_{k}([g,f])=0,\,\,k=0,1,\ldots,s\}.

Note that there is no obstruction to replace the free associative algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
by an algebra of formal series defined as

\overline{{\mathfrak{A}}}=\{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f_{k}\,|\,f_{k}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}\}.

One can then consider equations and their symmetries defined by formal series in
\overline{{\mathfrak{A}}}
. The definition of integrability, identical to the abelian case
[
1
]
, for formal series is as follows:

Definition 6
.

A nonabelian D
\Delta
E (
16
) for
f\in\overline{{\mathfrak{A}}}
is called
s
-approximate integrable if its Lie algebra
{\cal C}_{f}^{s}
is infinite dimensional and contains
s
-approximate symmetries of arbitrarily high total order. The equation is called formally integrable if it is
s
-approximate integrable for arbitrarily large
s
.

Any integrable equation is formally integrable, while the converse is not necessarily true as there is no assumption regarding the convergence of formal series and their approximate symmetries. Conditions of
s
-approximate integrability can be explicitly written and verified for a given equation. Moreover, these are necessary integrability conditions suitable for classification of integrable equations. The natural approach for formulating these conditions is through symbolic representation, a concept that will be elaborated upon in the subsequent sections.

In what follows we will consider nonabelian evolutionary D
\Delta
Es without a constant term.
A constant term in the equation can often be removed by an invertible change of variables. Moreover, if an equation possesses
N
symmetries with constant terms, then taking their linear combination one can always construct
N-1
symmetries without a constant term.
We denote the difference algebra without constant terms by
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{k\geq 1}{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}
, and similarly the difference algebra of formal series without constant terms by
\overline{{\mathfrak{A}}}^{\prime}=\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}\,|\,f_{k}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}\}
. Hereafter, we restrict ourselves to nonabelian D
\Delta
Es of the form

u_{t}=f,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\ \mbox{or}\ f\in\overline{{\mathfrak{A}}}^{\prime}.

(18)

4
Symbolic representation

Symbolic representation was first applied in the area of integrable systems by Gel’fand and Dickey
[
21
]
and later further developed in the framework of the symmetry approach in works of Beukers, Sanders, Wang, Mikhailov, Novikov and van der Kamp
[
22
]
-
[
33
]
. In
[
1
]

the method of symbolic representation was extended to the abelian difference algebra as well as difference operators and formal series. Now we extend the approach to the nonabelian case.

4.1
Symbolic representation of difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}

To define the symbolic representation
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p}
of the naturally graded difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}=\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}
, we first define an isomorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear spaces
\phi:{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}\to\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p}
, and then extend it to difference algebra and Lie algebra isomorphism by defining the multiplication, the action of the shift operator, derivations and Lie bracket on
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}
. The isomorphism of linear spaces is uniquely determined by its action on monomials.

Definition 7
.

The symbolic representation of difference monomial terms is defined as

\phi:\,e\mapsto 1,\quad\quad\phi:\,u_{i_{1}}u_{i_{2}}\cdots u_{i_{n}}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\mapsto{\hat{u}}^{n}\xi_{1}^{i_{1}}\xi_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\xi_{n}^{i_{n}}\in\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{n}.

Notations
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
are reserved for the variables in the symbolic representation, while
{\hat{u}}^{n}
indicates the appropriate linear space
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{n}
to which the symbol belongs to. We note here that unlike the situation in the abelian case
[
1
]
the nonabelian symbolic representation does not require symmetrisation operation.

Example 3
.

Here we give a few concrete examples:

\displaystyle u_{k}{\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}\xi_{1}^{k},\quad u^{m}{\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{m},\quad u_{1}u_{-1}{\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{2}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}^{-1},

\displaystyle\alpha uu_{1}u_{2}+\beta u_{-2}u_{-1}u{\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{3}(\alpha\xi_{2}\xi_{3}^{2}+\beta\xi_{1}^{-2}\xi_{2}^{-1}),\quad\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Under the action of the isomorphism
\phi
a homogeneous polynomial
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
is in one-to-one correspondence with a symbol
\hat{f}={\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
, where the coefficient function
a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
is a Laurent polynomial in variables
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
.

The action of automorphisms
S,T,I
and
{\cal T}
, presented in Section
2.1
, on
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle S(f){\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n};\quad T(f){\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{n},\ldots,\xi_{1});

\displaystyle I(f){\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\xi_{n}^{-1});\qquad\qquad{\cal T}(f){\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}{\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{n}^{-1},\ldots,\xi_{1}^{-1}).

The projector
\pi_{k}
selects the
k
-th homogeneous component of
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}
. Its symbolic representation
\hat{\pi}_{k}
is induced by the condition
\phi\pi_{k}=\hat{\pi}_{k}\phi
. If

f=\sum_{l\geq 0}f_{l}\in\overline{{\mathfrak{A}}},\quad\pi_{k}(f)=f_{k}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{k},\quad\phi(f_{k})={\hat{u}}^{k}a_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}),

then
\hat{f}:=\phi(f)=\sum_{l\geq 0}{\hat{u}}^{l}a_{l}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{l})
and

\hat{\pi}_{k}(\hat{f})=\hat{\pi}_{k}\left(\sum_{l\geq 0}{\hat{u}}^{l}a_{l}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{l})\right)={\hat{u}}^{k}a_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}).

We now define the multiplication in the symbolic representation.

Definition 8
.

Let
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{n},\,g\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{m}
and
\phi(f)={\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\,\,\phi(g)={\hat{u}}^{m}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})
. Then
\phi(fg)=\phi(f)*\phi(g)
, where

\phi(f)*\phi(g)={\hat{u}}^{n+m}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})b(\xi_{n+1},\ldots,\xi_{n+m}).

(19)

Note that the symbolic representation of difference monomials (Definition
7
) can be deduced from the representation of linear monomials
\phi(u_{k})={\hat{u}}\xi_{1}^{k}
and the multiplication rule (
19
).
The commutative algebra
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}=\oplus\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{k}
equipped with the
*
multiplication is isomorphic to the difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
.

4.2
Symbolic representation of difference operators and formal series

We extend the symbolic representation to difference operators and formal series, thereby obtaining the symbolic representation
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}((\eta))
for the algebra of formal series
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
.

We assign the symbol
\eta
to the symbolic representation of the shift operator
S
with the action rule given by

\eta({\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}))={\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}

and the composition rule, corresponding to
S\circ f=S(f)S
, defined as

\eta\circ{\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})={\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{n}\eta.

Consider
A={\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{n,m}((S))
, where the grading of
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
is defined by (
15
) and

\phi(f)=\hat{f}={\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}),\quad\phi(g)=\hat{g}={\hat{u}}^{m}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}).

Then its symbolic representation
\hat{A}
is

A={\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i}\,\,{\,\xmapsto{\ \phi\ }\,}\,\,\hat{A}={\hat{u}}^{n}_{l}{\hat{u}}^{m}_{r}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})b(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})\eta^{i}.

Here we reserve the notation
{\hat{u}}^{n}_{l}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
for the symbol of the left multiplication operator
{\cal L}_{f}
, while
{\hat{u}}^{m}_{r}b(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})
denotes the symbol of the right multiplication operator
{\cal R}_{g}
. Referring to (
11
), when dealing with an operator
b^{(i)}S^{i}
, where
b^{(i)}\in{\cal M}_{p,q}
, we adopt a shortened notation for its symbolic representation:

\sum_{\gamma=1}^{k}{\hat{u}}^{n}_{l}{\hat{u}}^{m}_{r}a^{(\gamma)}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})b^{(\gamma)}(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})\eta^{i}=:{\hat{u}}^{n}_{l}{\hat{u}}^{m}_{r}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})\eta^{i}.

The symbolic representation
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p,q}((\eta))
of a subspace
{\mathfrak{A}}_{p,q}((S))
is the sum of the terms in the form

{\hat{u}}^{p}_{l}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}a^{(s)}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q})\eta^{s},

where
s\leq N_{pq}
and
N_{pq}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We simply denote such element in
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p,q}((\eta))
by

{\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q})
.
Thus, for a generic formal series
B\in{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
, following from (
15
), its symbolic representation
\hat{B}
can be written as

\hat{B}=\sum_{p\geq 0}\sum_{q\geq 0}{\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}a_{pq}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q}),

(20)

where the summation indices
p,q
represent the summation over elements belonging to different subspaces
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p,q}((\eta))
, and

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}a_{pq}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q})={\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}\!\sum_{s\leq N_{pq}}a_{pq}^{(s)}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q})\eta^{s}\!\in\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p,q}((\eta)),N_{pq}\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
a_{pq}^{(s)}
are Laurent polynomials in
{\mathbb{C}}[\xi_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,\xi_{p}^{\pm 1},\zeta_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,\zeta_{q}^{\pm 1}]
.

Naturally, the projector
\hat{\pi}_{p,q}:\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}((\eta))\to\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}_{p,q}((\eta))
on formal series in the symbolic representation becomes

\hat{\pi}_{p,q}(\hat{B})={\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}a_{pq}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q}).

We now define the operations in the symbolic representation
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}((\eta))
.
To the sum of formal series in
{\mathfrak{A}}((S))
corresponds the sum of their symbolic representations in
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}((\eta))
.
The composition rule
{\cal L}_{f}{\cal R}_{g}S^{i}\circ{\cal L}_{f^{\prime}}{\cal R}_{g^{\prime}}S^{i^{\prime}}={\cal L}_{fS^{i}(f^{\prime})}{\cal R}_{S^{i}(g^{\prime})g}S^{i+i^{\prime}}
in the symbolic representation takes the form

\displaystyle{\hat{u}}^{n}_{l}{\hat{u}}^{m}_{r}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})\circ{\hat{u}}^{n^{\prime}}_{l}{\hat{u}}^{m^{\prime}}_{r}a^{\prime}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n^{\prime}},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m^{\prime}})

(21)

\displaystyle={\hat{u}}_{l}^{n+n^{\prime}}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{m+m^{\prime}}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\eta\prod_{j=n+1}^{n+n^{\prime}}\!\!\xi_{j}\prod_{k=1}^{m^{\prime}}\!\zeta_{k},\zeta_{m^{\prime}+1},\ldots,\zeta_{m+m^{\prime}})a^{\prime}(\xi_{n+1},\ldots,\xi_{n+n^{\prime}},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m^{\prime}}).

By linearity we extend this rule to the composition rule of formal series in
\hat{{\mathfrak{A}}}((\eta))
.

Next we state the symbolic representations for the Fréchet derivative and the action of difference operators on
{{\mathfrak{A}}}
.
Due to linearity of these operations, we only represent them for an element in subspace
{\mathfrak{A}}_{p},0\leq p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}
with a symbol
\hat{f}={\hat{u}}^{p}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p})
. The symbolic representation of its Fréchet derivative is given by

\hat{f}_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{l}^{i-1}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{p-i}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{p-i}),

(22)

The action rule of a difference operator on
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}
is derived from the action

\displaystyle\quad{\hat{u}}_{l}^{n}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{m}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})\left({\hat{u}}^{p}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p})\right)

\displaystyle={\hat{u}}^{n+m+p}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\prod_{i=n+1}^{n+p}\xi_{i},\xi_{n+p+1},\ldots,\xi_{n+m+p})b(\xi_{n+1},\ldots,\xi_{n+p}).

(23)

Example 4
.

Let
f=uu_{1}u_{2}-u_{-2}u_{-1}u
. We know that
\hat{f}={\hat{u}}^{3}(\xi_{2}\xi_{3}^{2}-\xi_{1}^{-2}\xi_{2}^{-1})
from Example

\hat{f}_{*}=\phi(f_{*})={\hat{u}}^{2}_{r}\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}^{2}+{\hat{u}}_{l}{\hat{u}}_{r}\zeta_{1}^{2}\eta+{\hat{u}}_{l}^{2}\xi_{2}\eta^{2}-{\hat{u}}_{r}^{2}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\eta^{-2}-{\hat{u}}_{l}{\hat{u}}_{r}\xi_{1}^{-2}\eta^{-1}-{\hat{u}}_{l}^{2}\xi_{1}^{-2}\xi_{2}^{-1},

which is the symbol of

f_{*}={\cal R}_{u_{1}u_{2}}+{\cal L}_{u}{\cal R}_{u_{2}}S+{\cal L}_{uu_{1}}S^{2}-{\cal R}_{u_{-1}u}S^{-2}-{\cal L}_{u_{-2}}{\cal R}_{u}S^{-1}-{\cal L}_{u_{-2}u_{-1}}.

By combining the symbolic representation of Fréchet derivative (
22
) with (

), we obtain the symbolic representation of the Lie bracket (
9
).

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}
with the symbol
\hat{f}={\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
and
g\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{m}
with the symbol
\hat{g}={\hat{u}}^{m}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})
. Then

\displaystyle\phi([f,g])=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\hat{u}}^{m+n-1}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\prod_{j=i}^{i+n-1}\xi_{j},\xi_{i+n},\ldots,\xi_{m+n-1})a(\xi_{i},\ldots,\xi_{i+n-1})

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\hat{u}}^{m+n-1}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\prod_{j=i}^{i+m-1}\xi_{j},\xi_{i+m},\ldots,\xi_{m+n-1})b(\xi_{i},\ldots,\xi_{i+m-1}).

(24)

Proof.

Directly applying (
22
) and (
23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\phi(g_{*}(f))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\hat{u}}_{l}^{i-1}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{m-i}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m-i})\big({\hat{u}}^{n}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\hat{u}}^{m+n-1}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\prod_{j=i}^{i+n-1}\xi_{j},\xi_{i+n},\ldots,\xi_{m+n-1})a(\xi_{i},\ldots,\xi_{i+n-1}).

(25)

Then formula (
24
) follows from the definition of Lie bracket (
9
).
∎

In particular, if
f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}
with the symbol
\hat{f}={\hat{u}}\omega(\xi_{1})
, then

\phi([f,g])={\hat{u}}^{n}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})\Big(\omega(\xi_{1})+\cdots+\omega(\xi_{m})-\omega(\xi_{1}\cdots\xi_{m})\Big).

(26)

We conclude this section with the symbolic representation for
the action of an evolutionary derivation
{\cal D}_{f}
on an element

defined by (

Let
\hat{f}={\hat{u}}^{p}a(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p})
and

with
\phi(B)={\hat{u}}^{n}_{l}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{m}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})
.
Using (

\displaystyle\phi({\cal D}_{f}(B))\!=\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\hat{u}}_{l}^{n+p-1}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{m}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\!\prod_{j=i}^{i+p-1}\!\!\xi_{j},\xi_{i+p},\ldots,\xi_{n+p-1},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{m})a(\xi_{i},\ldots,\xi_{i+p-1})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\hat{u}}_{l}^{n}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{m+p-1}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{i-1},\!\prod_{j=i}^{i+p-1}\!\!\zeta_{j},\zeta_{i+p},\ldots,\zeta_{m+p-1})a(\zeta_{i},\ldots,\zeta_{i+p-1}).

(27)

4.3
Symmetries in the symbolic representation

We proceed to describe the symmetry algebra of a given equation in symbolic representation. We remind the reader that we consider nonabelian difference equations (
18
) without a constant term. The findings presented in this section bear similarities to those in the abelian case
[
1
]
, albeit accounting for the distinction in symbolic representation between the abelian and nonabelian cases.

Proposition 2
.

Let
g\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
be a symmetry of equation (
18
) with a non-zero linear term,i.e.
f_{1}=\pi_{1}(f)\neq 0
. Then the symmetry
g
also possesses a non-zero linear term
g_{1}=\pi_{1}(g)\neq 0
.

Proof.

Assume that the symmetry
g=g_{k}+g_{k+1}+\cdots,\,g_{k}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}
and
g_{k}\neq 0
for some
k>1
, which implies
\pi_{k}([f,\ g])=[f_{1},\ g_{k}]=0
. Let the symbolic representation of
f_{1}
be
{\hat{u}}\omega(\xi_{1})
and the symbolic representation of
g_{k}
be
{\hat{u}}^{k}b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
. Then it follows from (
26
) that
b(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})=0
. Thus
g_{k}=0
contradicting with the assumption.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Let
u_{t}=f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}
be a linear equation. Then the algebra of its symmetries coincides with
{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}
, that is,
{\cal C}_{f}={\mathfrak{A}}_{1}
.

Proof.

Let
g=\sum_{k\geq 1}g_{k},\,g_{k}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{k}
be a symmetry of the equation
u_{t}=f\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}
. Then we must have
\pi_{k}([f,g])=0,\,k=2,3,\ldots
. Let
{\hat{u}}^{k}b_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
be the symbol of
g_{k}
. Then from (
26
) it follows that
b_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})=0
, which implies
g_{k}=0
for all
k\geq 2
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

The Lie algebra of symmetries of equation
u_{t}=f\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
with a non-zero linear term
f_{1}=\pi_{1}(f)\neq 0
is commutative.

Proof.

By Proposition
2
a symmetry of equation
u_{t}=f\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
with a non-zero linear term
f_{1}=\pi_{1}(f)\neq 0
must contain a linear term. Let
g,h
be two symmetries. Then
[g,h]
is a symmetry without a linear term implying that
[g,h]=0
.
∎

For a given D
\Delta
E, using symbolic representation, we are able to explicitly present its symmetries as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Consider a nonabelian D
\Delta
E

u_{t}=f,\quad f\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime},\quad\hat{f}={\hat{u}}\omega(\xi_{1})+\sum_{k\geq 2}{\hat{u}}^{k}a_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}),\quad\omega(\xi_{1})\neq 0.

(28)

Let
g\in{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
be a symmetry. Then the coefficients
A_{k}
of its symbolic representation

\hat{g}={\hat{u}}\Omega(\xi_{1})+\sum_{k\geq 2}{\hat{u}}^{k}A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})

(29)

can be determined recursively:

\displaystyle A_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})=\frac{G^{\Omega}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}{G^{\omega}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})}a_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),

(30)

\displaystyle A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})=\frac{1}{G^{\omega}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})}\left(G^{\Omega}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})a_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})\right.

(31)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j}A_{j}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\prod_{s=i}^{i+k-j}\xi_{s},\xi_{i+k+1-j},\ldots,\xi_{k})a_{k+1-j}(\xi_{i},\ldots,\xi_{i+k-j})

\displaystyle\qquad\left.-\sum_{j=2}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{j}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1},\prod_{s=i}^{i+k-j}\xi_{s},\xi_{i+k+1-j},\ldots,\xi_{k})A_{k+1-j}(\xi_{i},\ldots,\xi_{i+k-j})\right),

where
k\geq 3
and

G^{\varkappa}(\xi_{1},...,\xi_{m})=\varkappa(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varkappa(\xi_{i}),\quad\varkappa=\omega,\ \Omega.

(32)

Proof.

Since
g
is a symmetry of equation (
28
), we have
[f,g]=0
, which is equivalent to
\pi_{k}([f,g])=0
, i.e.,

\hat{\pi}_{k}(\phi([f,g]))=0,\,\,k=1,2,\ldots

(33)

in symbolic representation. When
k=1
, it is zero for any choices of
\omega(\xi_{1})
and
\Omega(\xi_{1})
. When
k=2
, vanishing of
\hat{\pi}_{2}\big(\phi([f,g])\big)=[\omega(\xi_{1}),A_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})]+[a_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}),\Omega(\xi_{1})]
is equivalent to

{\hat{u}}^{2}\bigg(A_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\big(\omega(\xi_{1})+\omega(\xi_{2})-\omega(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})\big)-a_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\big(\Omega(\xi_{1})+\Omega(\xi_{2}))-\Omega(\xi_{1}\xi_{2})\big)\bigg)=0

using (
26
). Taking into account the notation defined by (
32
), we obtain the expression of
A_{2}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
as in (
30
).
Vanishing of
\hat{\pi}_{k}(\phi([f,g]))

for
k\geq 3
is equivalent to the expression of
A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
as in (
31
).
∎

From Theorem
1
it follows that a symmetry
g
is uniquely determined by its linear part
\Omega(\xi_{1})
. For a given
\Omega(\xi_{1})
, all subsequent coefficients
A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
can be found recursively by formulae (
30
) and (
31
) in terms of the symbolic representation of the equation. However, it does not imply that every evolutionary equation possesses symmetries. To represent an element of the difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
coefficients
A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
must be Laurent polynomials. For a generic
\Omega(\xi_{1})
, coefficients
A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
given by (
30
)-(
31
) are rational functions as these expressions contain denominators
G^{\omega}
(unless
\omega(\xi_{1})=const
– see Example
7
below). In order to define symbols of elements of the difference algebra
{\mathfrak{A}}
, these denominators must cancel with appropriate factors in the numerators.

We call a linear term
{\hat{u}}\Omega(\xi_{1}),\,\Omega(\xi_{1})\in{\mathbb{C}}[\xi_{1},\xi_{1}^{-1}]

admissible
for the equation (
28
) if it is a linear term of a symmetry of the equation, i.e., all the coefficients
A_{k}
given by (
30
)-(
31
) are Laurent polynomials. As a
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear combination of symmetries is again a symmetry, the set of all admissible linear terms is a vector space over
{\mathbb{C}}
. We denote this space by
V_{f}
. For an integrable equation, the algebra of its symmetries and the vector space

are infinite dimensional.

We say that
g
is an approximate symmetry of degree
s
if coefficients
A_{k}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k})
,

are Laurent polynomials. When

5
The symmetry approach and integrability conditions

The goal of the symmetry approach is to find necessary conditions for the existence of an infinite-dimensional algebra of symmetries for a given equation. This formalism was originally proposed and subsequently developed by Shabat and his team for the case of abelian PDEs (see e.g.,
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
). The integrability conditions were formulated in terms of a formal recursion operator. Namely, the existence of an infinite-dimensional algebra of symmetries for an equation implies the existence of a formal pseudo-differential series of an arbitrary order, particularly of order 1, with coefficients belonging to an appropriate differential field/ring or its extension. This approach allows us to obtain necessary integrability conditions explicitly, which can be used not only as an integrability test for a given equation but also to solve classification problems by producing complete lists of integrable PDEs.
Later, the formalism was extended to integro-differential equations
[
26
,
27
]
, some non-evolutionary PDEs
[
28
]
, and partial-difference and D
\Delta
Es
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
1
]
.

The universality of these integrability conditions means that the existence of the formal recursion operator follows from the existence of an infinite-dimensional algebra of symmetries and does not depend on the structure of the symmetry algebra. Deriving these conditions requires the existence of fractional powers of formal series. It is known that in both abelian and nonabelian cases of formal difference series, their fractional powers with coefficients in
{\mathfrak{A}}
or
{\cal M}
correspondingly may not exist. To address this issue in the abelian case, the quasi-local extension of the difference algebra was introduced
[
1
]
. In this section, we first provide this extension in the nonabelian case and subsequently formulate the integrability conditions in universal form, i.e., independent of the structure of the symmetry algebra.

Proof.

7
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have developed a new approach that enables the derivation of explicit and easily verifiable necessary integrability conditions for scalar evolutionary D
\Delta
Es on associative algebras. We prove that if an equation

u_{t}=f(u_{-n},\ldots,u_{n}),\quad f=\sum_{p\geq 1}f_{p},\quad f_{p}\in{\mathfrak{A}}_{p}

possesses an infinite dimensional algebra of symmetries, then there exists a canonical formal recursion operator

\Lambda=\eta+\sum_{p+q\geq 1}{\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}\phi_{pq}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q}),

where the coefficients are explicitly expressed in terms of the symbolic representation of the equation. The requirement of quasi-locality of all terms
{\hat{u}}_{l}^{p}{\hat{u}}_{r}^{q}\phi_{pq}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p},\eta,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{q})
provides explicit necessary integrability conditions for the equation. Moreover, these conditions are independent on the symmetry structure of the equation.

We applied our methodology to classify integrable quasi-linear skew-symmetric equations of the form (
59
) of orders
(-1,1)
,
(-2,2)
, and
(-3,3)
. For each equation on the list, we presented either its Lax representation or a Miura transformation to a known integrable equation. We conjecture that each equation on the list is a member of an infinite family of integrable equations of arbitrarily high order. For example, equations (

2

) are members of a family of multiplicative nonabelian Bogoyavlensky equations of the form (
65
). The description of such families is outside the scope of this paper.

The requirement of quasi-linearity and skew-symmetry in the classification problem may be removed.
While the classification of non-quasi-linear equations remains unresolved, the approach developed in this paper is suitable to tackle this problem.
Indeed, the derived integrability conditions are applicable in the case of a generic equation. It is worth noting that all presently known integrable nonabelian non-quasi-linear equations are related to the quasi-linear ones through either invertible or non-invertible Miura-type transformations.
This observation prompts speculation that such a relationship may universally apply to integrable non-quasi-linear equations.
Additionally, all non-skew-symmetric equations known to the authors are either connected to linear ones or to non-linearisable skew-symmetric equations. The verification (or refutation) of these hypotheses remains a challenge for further investigations.

Integrable D
\Delta
Es serve as generalized symmetries for integrable discrete equations and as Bäcklund transformations for integrable PDEs. Although extensively studied in the abelian case since the inception of integrable systems theory, investigations in the nonabelian setting remain notably limited. The construction of nonabelian integrable discrete systems corresponding to the D
\Delta

The nonabelian equations can also be interpreted as quantised versions of classical systems. For instance, the nonabelian KdV equation was derived by transitioning classical fields to a quantum framework and substituting Poisson brackets with commutators

Recently, a new approach to the problem of quantisation based on the notion of quantisation ideals was proposed by Mikhailov in

In this new approach it is proposed to start from a dynamical system defined on a free associative algebra. The nonabelian integrable D
\Delta
