Testing with Non-identically Distributed Samples
Shivam Garg, Chirag Pabbaraju, Kirankumar Shiragur, Gregory Valiant

Abstract
We examine the extent to which sublinear-sample property testing and estimation applies to settings where samples are independently but not identically distributed. Specifically, we consider the following distributional property testing framework: Suppose there is a set of distributions over a discrete support of size k k , p 1 , p 2 , … , p T \textbf{p}_{1},\textbf{p}_{2},\ldots,\textbf{p}_{T} , and we obtain c c independent draws from each distribution. Suppose the goal is to learn or test a property of the average distribution, p avg \textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}} . This setup models a number of important practical settings where the individual distributions correspond to heterogeneous entities — either individuals, chronologically distinct time periods, spatially separated data sources, etc. From a learning standpoint, even with c = 1 c=1 samples from each distribution, Θ ⁡ ( k / ε 2 ) \Theta(k/\varepsilon^{2}) samples are necessary and sufficient to learn p avg \textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}} to within error ε \varepsilon in TV distance. To test uniformity or identity — distinguishing the case that p avg \textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}} is equal to some reference distribution, versus has ℓ 1 \ell_{1} distance at least ε \varepsilon from the reference distribution, we show that a linear number of samples in k k is necessary given c = 1 c=1 samples from each distribution. In contrast, for c ≥ 2 c\geq 2 , we recover the usual sublinear sample testing of the i.i.d. setting: we show that O ⁡ ( k / ε 2 + 1 / ε 4 ) O(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2}+1/\varepsilon^{4}) samples are sufficient, matching the optimal sample complexity in the i.i.d. case in the regime where ε ≥ k − 1 / 4 \varepsilon\geq k^{-1/4} . Additionally, we show that in the c = 2 c=2 case, there is a constant ρ > 0 \rho>0 such that even in the linear regime with ρ ​ k \rho k samples, no tester that considers the multiset of samples (ignoring which samples were drawn from the same p i \textbf{p}_{i} ) can perform uniformity testing.

Introduction

The problem of estimating statistics of an unknown distribution, or determining whether the distribution in question possesses a property of interest has been studied by the Statistics community for well over a century. Interest in these problems from the TCS community was sparked by the seminal paper of Goldreich
[
GR00
]
, demonstrating the surprising result that such tasks might be accomplished given a sample size that scales
sublinearly
with the support of the underlying distribution. Over the subsequent 20+ years, the lay of the land of sublinear sample property testing and estimation has largely been worked out: for a number of natural properties of distributions (or pairs of distributions), testing or estimation can be accomplished with a sample size that is sublinear in the support size of the distributions in question, and in most settings, the optimal sample complexities are now known to constant or subconstant factors.

More recently, these questions have been revisited with an eye towards relaxing the assumption that samples are drawn i.i.d. from the distribution in question. The majority of such departures focused on relaxing the independence assumption; this includes work from the robust statistics perspective where some portion of samples may be adversarially corrupted, as well as other models of dependent sampling.

In this work, we consider the implications of relaxing the more innocuous seeming assumption of identical samples. Given samples drawn independently from a collection of distributions,
\textbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{p}_{T}
, there are a number of different questions one can ask, including estimating properties of how the various distribution are related. Here, however, we focus on the practically motivated question of testing or estimating properties of the
average
distribution
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum\textbf{p}_{i}.
To what extent is sublinear sample testing or estimation still possible in this setting, and do existing estimators suffice? Below, we describe several concrete practical settings that motivate these questions.

Example 1
.

The “Federated” Setting:
Consider the setting where there are a number of users/individuals, each with a corresponding distribution (over online purchases, language, biometrics, etc.) and the goal is to test whether the average distribution is close to some reference. There might be significant heterogeneity between users, and the number of samples (i.e. amount of data) collected from each user might be very modest, due to privacy, bandwidth, or power considerations.

Example 2
.

Chronological Heterogeneity:
Consider a setting where samples are collected chronologically, and the underlying distribution varies over time. Consider a wearable technology that periodically samples biometric statistics, or language spoken. The goal may be to test whether the average distribution is close to some reference (with the goal of providing an early warning of illness in the case of biometrics, or for early detection of dementia in the case of sampling words spoken). Ideally, this test can be successfully accomplished with far less data than would be required to
learn
. There is significant heterogeneity across time but samples collected close together can be regarded as being sampled from the same distribution,
\textbf{p}_{i}.

Example 3
.

Spatially Segregated Sampling:
Consider a setting where the goal is to sample species (of animals, bacteria, etc.) and test whether the overall distribution is close to a reference distribution. In many such settings, the distribution of species has significant spatial variation — the distribution of soil bacteria in a grassy spot is quite different from the distribution in dense forest, similarly, the distribution of wildlife observed by trail-cameras in national parks varies according to the location of the cameras. Again, the goal is to accomplish this statistical test with less data than would be required to learn the underlying average distribution.

In some of these motivating settings, one may assume some structure in the various distributions—for example in chronological settings, for most indices,
i
,
\textbf{p}_{i}
is similar to
\textbf{p}_{i-1}.
Nevertheless, it is still worth understanding whether such assumptions
must
be leveraged. Indeed, for
learning
the average distribution,
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}
, to within total variation distance
\varepsilon
, the sample complexity is the same as in the i.i.d. case,
O(k/\varepsilon^{2})
where
k
is the support size,
without any assumptions
on the
\textbf{p}_{i}
s. At the highest level, our results demonstrate that as soon as one draws at least
c=2
samples from each distribution, this non-identically distributed setting recovers much of the power of sublinear-sample property testing. The caveat is that one must design new algorithms for this setting that are cognizant of which distribution each sample was drawn from; as we show, testers that return a function of the multiset of examples do not suffice.

Beyond the specific problems addressed in this work, there are many obvious directions for future work, including mapping the sample complexities for other natural property testing and estimation problems within the framework of non-identically distributed samples. This includes considering properties of pairs of distributions, such as “closeness” testing, and estimating distance metrics between distributions. Also, while studying properties of the average distribution is a natural starting point, other summary statistics of a collection of distributions are also worth considering. For example, in chronological settings, one might be interested in how certain properties change over time and properties of recently seen distributions. More broadly, probing the robustness of sublinear sample testing from other perspectives may yield further surprises with practical implications.

Claim 1.2

(Impossibility result with

Given access to

The above impossibility of sublinear-sample testing in the case of

Consider the instance where
T=k/2

is a point mass on a distinct element, versus the instance where each
\textbf{p}_{i}
is a uniform distribution over two distinct domain elements that are disjoint from the supports of
\textbf{p}_{j}
for

. In both instances, given

Our main result is that with just

The key intuition is that with two samples from each distribution, we can count both the collision statistics within distributions, as well as the collisions across distributions, and build an unbiased estimator for
\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}
. We then leverage techniques from
[
DGPP16
]
and adapt them to this setting of non-identical samples to bound the variance of our estimator to get the following sublinear sample complexity for uniformity testing.

Theorem 1.3

(Uniformity testing, proof in
Section

3
)
.

There is an absolute constant,
\alpha
, such that given access to
T

\textbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{p}_{T}
, each supported on a common domain of size
\leq k
, for any
\varepsilon>0,
provided
T\geq\alpha(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2}+1/\varepsilon^{4})
given
c=2
samples drawn from each
\textbf{p}_{i}
, there exists a testing algorithm that succeeds with probability
2/3
and distinguishes whether,

\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\mathrm{Unif}(k)\quad\text{ versus }\quad\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}},\mathrm{Unif}(k))\geq\varepsilon~.

Note that when
\varepsilon\geq k^{-1/4}
, our sample complexity for uniformity testing simplifies to
O(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
, which matches the optimal sample complexity for testing uniformity in the i.i.d setting. Since the setting of non-identical samples is a generalization of the i.i.d setting, the lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
for testing uniformity in the i.i.d setting also holds in our setting, hence we get optimal sample complexity in the regime where
\varepsilon\geq k^{-1/4}
.

Our results also hold for identity testing, through standard reduction techniques. In particular, we can adapt the reduction from identity testing to uniformity testing of
[
Gol16
]
to our setting with non-identical distributions, yielding the following result on the reduction from an instance of identity testing to an instance of uniformity testing.

Lemma 1.4

(Identity to uniformity testing, proof in
Section

)
.

Given an arbitrary reference distribution

\leq k
elements, there exists a pair

[4k]

•

For a sequence of distributions

, the map
\Phi_{\textbf{q}}

\mathrm{avg}(\Phi_{\textbf{q}}(\textbf{q}_{1}),\dots,\Phi_{\textbf{q}}(\textbf{q}_{T}))=\textbf{u}_{4k}~.

•

For two sequences of distributions
\textbf{p}^{1}_{1},\dots,\textbf{p}^{1}_{T}
and
\textbf{p}^{2}_{1},\dots,\textbf{p}^{2}_{T}

The above result combined with the result for uniformity testing, immediately yields the following result for identity testing. Our bounds for identity testing are optimal in the worst case for large values of

Corollary 1.5

(Identity testing)
.

There is an absolute constant,
\alpha
, such that given a reference distribution
q
supported on
\leq k

\textbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{p}_{T}

and distinguishes whether,

\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\textbf{q}\quad\text{ versus }\quad\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}},\textbf{q})\geq\varepsilon~,

We note that our sublinear sample testing algorithms leverage the knowledge of which distribution each sample was drawn from. As the following theorem asserts, this is necessary — there is a constant
\rho>0
such that even in the linear regime with
\rho k
samples, there is no testing algorithm that simply returns a function of the multiset of samples, throwing away the information about which samples were drawn from the same distributions.

Theorem 1.6

(Lower bound for “pooled” estimators, proof in
Section

5
)
.

There is an absolute constant,
\rho>0
, such that given access to distributions
\textbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{p}_{\rho k}
supported on a domain of size
k
, given
c=2
samples from each of
\textbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{p}_{\rho k}
, no tester that returns a function of the multiset of samples (i.e. ignoring the information about which samples were drawn from the same distributions) can distinguish the case that
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\mathrm{Unif}(k)\quad\text{ versus }\quad\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}},\mathrm{Unif}(k))\geq 0.01~
with success probability at least
2/3
.

Finally, we note that this interesting phase shift for different values of
c
holds only in the non-Poisson setting. Results in the Poisson setting remain the same as that of the i.i.d setting. In particular, if instead of
c
samples, we are given
Poi(c)
samples from each distribution, then even in the case of
c=1
, we recover all testing results known in the i.i.d setting. This follows from the following basic fact: receiving
Poi(c)
samples from every distribution is equivalent to receiving
Poi(cT)
samples from the average distribution. A more formal version of this result is stated in
Appendix

C
for identity testing, but such a result also holds for testing other properties.

The techniques used to prove our upper bounds mostly involve adapting the techniques from the i.i.d. case to our setting. Establishing the lower bound for pooled samples is more complex, involving a new construction that might be of independent interest. Nonetheless, more than the techniques, our main contribution is the problem definition and the high-level takeaways. Commonly, when collecting samples, heterogeneity invariably arises, particularly when these samples are gathered over various time points, geographical locations, or from different users, as highlighted in the examples discussed above. Given this, it is natural to ask if it is okay to apply standard testers which assume identically distributed samples. We demonstrate that while standard testers are not directly applicable (as evidenced by our lower bound for pooled samples), they can be effectively adapted. For example, for identity testing, a standard estimator involves counting the number of collisions. In contrast, our modified estimator employs a weighted collision count, assigning different weights to collisions depending on whether they occur within samples from identical or non-identical distributions.

Related Work

Interest from the theoretical computer science community on distribution testing, given i.i.d sample access, was sparked by the problem of distinguishing whether an unknown probability distribution was the uniform distribution over support
k
, versus has total variation distance at least
\epsilon
from this uniform distribution. The early work
[
GR00
]
proposed a collision-based estimator, demonstrating that testing this property could be accomplished given significantly fewer samples than would be required to learn the distribution in question. It was used for testing whether a bounded degree graph is an expander. Since then, a line of work eventually pinned down the exact sample complexity of
\Theta(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
— optimal to constant factors including the dependence on the probability of the tester failing
[
Pan08
,
VV17
,
DKN14
,
DGPP16
,
DGPP17
]
. For a comprehensive exposition of this line of work, we refer the reader to Chapters 2 and 3 in
[
Can22
]
.

A closely related problem to uniformity testing is that of
identity
testing, where the task is to determine if an unknown probability distribution,
p
, is equal to versus far from a fixed reference distribution,
q
, given i.i.d sample access
[
BFF+01
]
. While “instance-optimal” estimators — estimators that are optimal for
every

q
— are known
[
VV17
]
, among reference distributions supported on
\leq k
elements, the uniform distribution is the most difficult to test. Beyond this, building off
[
DK16
]
,
[
Gol16
]
gave a reduction from identity testing to uniformity testing, showing that any uniformity testing algorithm can be leveraged in a black-box fashion for identity testing. We leverage both the high-level design of the optimal uniformity testing algorithms, and this reduction from identity testing to uniformity testing.

Beyond uniformity testing and identity testing, there is an enormous body of work on testing or estimating other distribution properties, including properties of pairs of distributions, such as total variation distance and other distance metrics. Broadly speaking, the optimal sample complexities for these tasks are now pinned down, at least to constant factors, in this setting where one obtains samples drawn i.i.d. from the distribution in question.

Beyond i.i.d samples:

The questions of learning, property testing and estimation have also been considered in various settings that deviate from the idealized setting of samples drawn i.i.d. from a fixed distribution. These include work from the perspective of robust statistics, where some portion of the samples may be adversarially corrupted (see e.g.
[
DKK+19
,
DK19
,
CSV17
]
, as well as the broad area of time-series analysis where observations are drawn from time-varying distributions (e.g.
[
CZ64
,
KSAX10
,
Lam15
]
).

Most similar to the present paper are works that explore learning and testing questions in various settings where there are a number of data sources, each providing a batch of samples. In
[
QV18
]
and the followup papers
[
JO20
,
CLM20
]
, it is assumed that an unknown
1-\epsilon

c

c

O(\varepsilon/\sqrt{c})
. The papers
[
TKV17

VKVK19

explore the setting where batches of size
c
are drawn i.i.d. from a collection of heterogeneous distributions, and the goal is to learn the multiset of distributions. The punchline here is that the multiset of distributions can be learned given asymptotically fewer draws from each distribution than would be necessary to learn them in isolation. While the focus in these works is on the setting where the distributions have support size 2 (i.e. the setting corresponds to flipping coins with heterogeneous probabilities), the results apply more broadly. The paper of Levi, Ron, and Rubinfeld
[
LRR13
]
considered the setting of a collection of
T
distributions over large support size, and explored the question of testing whether all distributions in the collection are the same, versus have significant variation. They considered two sampling models — a “query” model where one can adaptively choose which distribution to draw a sample from, and a weaker model where each sample is drawn from a distribution selected uniformly at random from the
T

We note that in this latter model, testing properties of the average distribution trivially reduces to the i.i.d. setting. Later works
[
DK16

ABR16

improve the results obtained by
[
LRR13
]

[
AS19
]
considers the setting with a collection of distributions, and given one sample (in expectation) from each distribution, the task is to distinguish whether all the distributions are equal to a reference distribution, or all distributions are far from it. In contrast, our focus is on distinguishing whether the average distribution is equal to or far from a reference distribution. They also consider the case where we only have sample access to the reference distribution. General non-vacuous results are not possible in their setting given a single sample from each distribution. To bypass this, they impose a structural condition on the collection of distributions. We also face a similar issue and show that it can be bypassed in our setting by drawing multiple samples from each distribution.

are all distinct, the random variables
\hat{Y}_{st}
and
\hat{Y}_{ab}
are independent, and hence
\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{st}\hat{Y}_{ab}]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{st}]\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{ab}]=0

\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{st}]=0
. Therefore all that remains is to only bound the second term. Observe that for any 3 fixed distinct values, there are 6 possible orderings of the indices

and
a<b
such that the multiset
\{s,t,a,b\}|
has exactly these distinct values. Further, for each of these orderings, the random variable
\hat{Y}_{st}\hat{Y}_{ab}
is non-zero if and only if the first sample at the three distinct indices is the same. Thus, we have that,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s<t\\
a<b\\
|\{s,t,a,b\}|=3\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}[\hat{Y}_{st}\hat{Y}_{ab}]

\displaystyle=6\sum_{a<b<c}\sum_{l=1}^{k}p^{l}_{a}p^{l}_{b}p^{l}_{c}=6\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{a<b<c}p^{l}_{a}p^{l}_{b}p^{l}_{c}\leq 6\sum_{l=1}^{k}\Big(\sum_{a}p^{l}_{a}\Big)^{3}=6\Big\|\sum_{a}p_{a}\Big\|^{3}_{3}~.

(5)

Putting it all together, that is substituting

Equation

5

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{s<t}\hat{Y}_{st}\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s<t}\langle\textbf{p}_{t},\textbf{p}_{s}\rangle+6\Big\|\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}\Big\|^{3}_{3}.

Substituting the above inequality, combined with
Section

3
into

3

,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Var}}[Z]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{T^{4}}\Big(\sum_{t\in[T]}\|\textbf{p}_{t}\|^{2}_{2}+4\sum_{s<t}\langle\textbf{p}_{t},\textbf{p}_{s}\rangle+24\Big\|\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}\Big\|^{3}_{3}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{T^{4}}\Big(2\sum_{t\in[T]}\|\textbf{p}_{t}\|^{2}_{2}+4\sum_{s<t}\langle\textbf{p}_{t},\textbf{p}_{s}\rangle+24\Big\|\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}\Big\|^{3}_{3}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{T^{4}}\Big\|\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}\Big\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{48}{T^{4}}\Big\|\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}\Big\|^{3}_{3}=\frac{4}{T^{2}}\Big\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\Big\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{48}{T}\Big\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\Big\|^{3}_{3}.

(6)

In the third equality, we used
2\sum_{t\in[T]}\|\textbf{p}_{t}\|^{2}_{2}+4\sum_{s<t}\langle\textbf{p}_{t},\textbf{p}_{s}\rangle=2\Big\|\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}\Big\|^{2}_{2}

\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}
. In the remainder of the proof, we use the variance bound obtained above to solve the uniformity testing problem. We divide our remaining proof into two cases.

Uniform case:

In this case, we are promised that the
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\textbf{u}_{k}
. Therefore, it is immediate that
\mathbb{E}[Z]=\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[Z\geq\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\Big)\frac{1}{k}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Pr\Big[Z\geq\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\Big)\mathbb{E}[Z]\Big]\leq\frac{9}{\varepsilon^{4}\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}\cdot\operatorname{{Var}}[Z]=\frac{9k^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\cdot\operatorname{{Var}}[Z]~,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9k^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\Big(\frac{4}{T^{2}k}+\frac{48}{Tk^{2}}\Big)=\frac{36k}{\varepsilon^{4}T^{2}}+\frac{432}{T\varepsilon^{4}}~.

In the first inequality, we used that
\mathbb{E}[Z]=1/k

3

Far from uniform case:

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[Z\leq\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\Big)\frac{1}{k}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Pr\Big[Z\leq\Big(\frac{1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\Big)\Big(\frac{1+\alpha^{2}}{k}\Big)\Big]=\Pr\Big[Z\leq\Big(\frac{1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}[Z]\Big]

\displaystyle=\Pr\Big[Z\leq\Big(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}[Z]\Big]\leq\Pr\Big[Z\leq\Big(1-\frac{\alpha^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{3}}{1+\alpha^{2}}\Big)\mathbb{E}[Z]\Big]

\displaystyle=\Pr\Big[Z\leq\Big(1-\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{3(1+\alpha^{2})}\Big)\mathbb{E}[Z]\Big]\leq\frac{9(1+\alpha^{2})^{2}}{4\alpha^{4}\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}\cdot\operatorname{{Var}}[Z]=\frac{9(1+\alpha^{2})^{2}}{4\alpha^{4}\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{4}}\cdot\operatorname{{Var}}[Z]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9(1+\alpha^{2})^{2}}{T^{2}\alpha^{4}\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}}+\frac{108(1+\alpha^{2})^{2}}{T\alpha^{4}}\cdot\frac{\Big\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\Big\|^{3}_{3}}{\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|^{4}_{2}}=\frac{9k(1+\alpha^{2})}{T^{2}\alpha^{4}}+\frac{108k^{2}}{T\alpha^{4}}\cdot\Big\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\Big\|^{3}_{3}.

(7)

In the first equality, we multiplied and divided by the term
(1+\alpha^{2})
. In the second equality, we used the definition of
\alpha

and in the fifth equality, we simplified the expression. In the sixth inequality, we used Chebyshev’s inequality. In the seventh and eighth inequality, we used
\mathbb{E}[Z]=\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{2}^{2}
and substituted the bound on the variance of

from
Section

3
. In the final expression, we used the definition of
\alpha
. To upper bound the above probability term, we next bound each of the terms in the final expression separately. The first term above is bounded by,

\displaystyle\frac{9k(1+\alpha^{2})}{T^{2}\alpha^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9k(1+\varepsilon^{2})}{T^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}\leq\frac{18k}{T^{2}\varepsilon^{4}}~.

(8)

In the first inequality, we used the fact that for

\frac{1+x}{x^{2}}

.
The final inequality follows because

. To upper bound the second term in

\displaystyle\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\|_{3}^{3}

\displaystyle=\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}-u+u\|_{3}^{3}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}((\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-u(i))+u(i))^{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big[(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-u(i))^{3}+u(i)^{3}+3(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(i)-u(i))u(i)\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(i)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}-u\|_{2}^{3}+\frac{3}{k}\|\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}-u\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}=\frac{\alpha^{3}}{k^{3/2}}+\frac{3\alpha^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

\alpha

Thus, the second term is bounded as,

We now prove our result which reduces the problem of identity testing to uniformity testing. Such a reduction for the i.i.d setting is already known

In particular, we use the same reduction functions from that of the i.i.d case and show that they work even in the case of a sequence of changing distributions. A more formal statement of this reduction, along with its proof for a sequence of distributions is provided below.

(11)

(14)

In the second equality, we substituted the value

Sampling:

5
Lower bound for pooling-based estimators

In this section, we prove the indistinguishability result for pooling-based estimators given in
1.6
. First, observe that uniformity of the average distribution is a symmetric property, and thus, labels of the samples do not matter for a testing algorithm. Thus, without loss of generality, we can assume that the testing algorithm takes as input the pooled multiset of samples and operates only on the
fingerprint
of the pooled multiset (refer to
Appendix

E
for a formal discussion on this). Here, the fingerprint is the count of elements that occur exactly zero times, once, twice etc. In what follows, we will construct two sequences of distributions: one whose average is the uniform distribution and another whose average is far from uniform. However, we show that the total variation distance between the distributions of their corresponding fingerprints is small, implying impossibility of uniformity testing with pooled samples. Below, we describe the steps involved in the proof.

Step 1:

We construct two sequences of distributions
a
and
b
(Definition
5.2
) such that
\mathrm{avg}(\textbf{a})
is uniform and
\mathrm{avg}(\textbf{b})
is far from uniform (Claim
5.3
). Moreover, these sequences are chosen in such a way that when we draw
2
samples from each distribution in the sequence, with good probability no element will be observed more than
4
times in the pooled multiset. The building blocks needed for defining these sequences of distributions are introduced in Lemma
5.1
.

Step 2:

Next, we show that when we draw 2 samples from each distribution in the sequences, the fingerprints of the pooled multisets have the same mean (Claim
5.4
), and similar covariances (Claim
5.5
). A minor detail here is that we show this for the collision counts rather than fingerprints, but this is not an issue, as one can use an invertible linear transform to convert one to the other.

Step 3:

We want to eventually show that the distributions of fingerprints are close, and hence can not be distinguished. For this, we will first show that the distributions of these fingerprints are close, in total variation distance, to the multivariate Gaussian distributions
G_{\textbf{a}}
and
G_{\textbf{b}}
of corresponding mean and covariance, that have been discretized by rounding all probability mass to the nearest point in the integer lattice (Lemma
5.7
). We show this via a CLT (Lemma
5.6
). One slight complication in proving this CLT is that in our setting, the fingerprints no longer correspond to a generalized multinomial distribution, and hence we cannot directly leverage the multivariate CLT for such distributions in
[
VV11
]
. Instead, we will show that the distribution of fingerprints is represented as a sum of independent samples, supported on the all-zero vector, the basis vectors, and the vector
[2,0,0,\ldots].
Our proof essentially “splits” each distribution into the component supported on the zero vector and the basis vectors, and a component supported on the zero vector and the
[2,0,0,\ldots]
vector. The portion supported on the basis vectors
is
a generalized multinomial, and hence is close to the corresponding discretized Gaussian by the CLT in
[
VV11
]
, and the remaining portion is simply a binomial, scaled by a factor of
2
. We then argue that the convolution of these distributions is close to a discretized Gaussian.

Step 4:

Having shown that the distributions of fingerprints are close to corresponding discretized multivariate Gaussian distributions, we are left with the tractable task of showing that the TV distance between these two (discretized) Gaussians is small. This is shown in Lemma
5.8
and relies on certain technical results proved in Lemma
5.10
and Lemma
5.11
. Here, we use the fact that the two collision-count distributions have the same mean and similar covariances as discussed in Step 2 above. Finally, a triangle inequality yields the desired result (Lemma
5.9
).

We now prove a CLT which helps us approximate the distributions of the fingerprints by multivariate Gaussians having the same mean and covariance.

Lemma 5.6

(CLT)
.

Consider two distributions supported on
[0,0,0]
, the basis vectors, and
[2,0,0]
, such that the probability of
[0,0,0]
is at least
0.9999
. Then for constant
\alpha
, and
n
large enough, the distribution of the sum of
\alpha n
i.i.d samples from the first distribution and
(1-\alpha)n
i.i.d samples from the second distribution has total variation distance at most
0.01
from a Gaussian, where the mass has been discretized to the nearest point in the integer lattice.

Proof.

We begin by showing that the distribution of the sum of
\ell
i.i.d. samples from each distribution is close, in total variation distance to the multivariate Gaussian of corresponding mean and covariance, discretized to the integer lattice. Consider one of the distributions, and let
u_{0}=\Pr([0,0,0]),
and
u_{1}=\Pr([1,0,0]),u_{2}=\Pr([0,1,0]),u_{3}=\Pr([0,0,1]),
and
v=\Pr([2,0,0])
.
We draw a sample from this distribution as follows: first flip a fair coin, and if the coin lands heads, sample from the distribution over
[0,0,0],[1,0,0],[0,1,0],[0,0,1],
given by
respective probabilities
1-2(u_{1}+u_{2}+u_{3}),2u_{1},2u_{2},2u_{3}.
If the coin lands tails, we sample from the distribution over
[0,0,0],[2,0,0]
with respective probability
1-2v,2v.
Note that this sampling procedure yields a sample from the original distribution.

Given
\ell
independent samples from this distribution sampled in this way, let us consider the distribution of the sum of the samples, conditioned on exactly
t
of the
\ell
fair coins having landed heads. Going forward, we will assume that
t
and
\ell-t
are at least
\ell/4
— by the Chernoff bound, each of these events individually happen with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Omega(l))
, and so by having
l
be large enough and applying a union bound, we lose at most a probability mass of
0.0001
in our total variation distance bound.
Conditioned on a value of
t
, the distribution corresponds to the convolution of
t
independent draws from the multinomial distribution given by the distribution corresponding to the coin landing heads, and the distribution corresponding to the
\ell-t
samples from the distribution supported on
[0,0,0]
and
[2,0,0]
, which is a binomial random variable in the first coordinate, scaled by a factor of 2.

Leveraging a multivariate CLT (e.g. the multivariate CLT for generalized multinomial distributions in
[
VV11
, Theorem 4]
), the distribution of the sum of the
t
draws from the multinomial will have total variation distance at most
O\left(\frac{\log t}{\sigma^{1/3}}\right)
from the multivariate Gaussian of corresponding mean and covariance, discretized to the nearest point in the integer lattice. Here,
\sigma^{2}
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance of the sum vector of the
t
draws. Since each of the
t
independent draws has covariance matrix with smallest eigenvalue bounded below by a constant,
\sigma=\Theta(\sqrt{t})
and this distance goes to zero as
t
gets large.

Since the binomial is supported on lattice points, the convolution of a binomial with a discretized Gaussian is identical to discretizing the convolution of the binomial and (non-discretized) Gaussian. Since we have assumed that both
t
and
\ell-t
are
\Theta(n)
, the convolution of the binomial and (non-discretized) Gaussian is itself total variation distance
o(1)
from the distribution of the Gaussian of corresponding mean and covariance.

Note that for large
\ell
, with probability at least
0.999
,
t\in[\ell/2-4\sqrt{\ell},\ell/2+4\sqrt{\ell}].
We now claim that for any
t
in this range, the Gaussian distributions of mean and covariance corresponding to the mean and covariance of
\ell
samples from our distribution
conditioned on this value of
t
, have total variation distance at most
0.005
from each other. This follows from elementary bounds on the distance between shifted multivariate Gaussians, and the assumption that the probability of sampling
[0,0,0]
is at least
u_{0}>0.9999
. Indeed, for each of the coordinates, the mean shifts by at most
p\cdot 8\sqrt{\ell}
where
p=1-u_{0}<0.0001
denotes the expectation of that coordinate, whereas the standard deviation in this direction is
\approx\sqrt{p}\sqrt{\ell}
, which is significantly greater as long as
\sqrt{p}\gg p
.

Since we have shown that the distribution of the sum from each distribution is close to a discretized Gaussian, all that remains, is to prove that the convolution of two discretized Gaussians is close, in total variation distance, to the discretization of the convolution of the two Gaussians. This is trivially true for any Gaussians in constant dimension with minimum eigenvalue of the covariance super-constant.

∎

Let
FP(\textbf{a})
and
FP(\textbf{b})
be 3 dimensional random variables containing the number of elements observed exactly 2, 3 and 4 times, for samples drawn from
a
and
b
respectively.
Let
G_{\textbf{a}}
be a random variable corresponding to picking a sample from gaussian with same mean and covariance as
FP(\textbf{a})
and rounding each coordinate to the nearest integer. Similarly,
G_{\textbf{b}}
corresponds to
FP(\textbf{b})
.

Lemma 5.7

(Fingerprints close to discretized Gaussians)
.

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(FP(\textbf{a}),G_{\textbf{a}})\leq 0.01
and
\mathrm{d_{TV}}(FP(\textbf{b}),G_{\textbf{b}})\leq 0.01
.

Proof.

Recall that
FP(\textbf{a})

\displaystyle FP(\textbf{a})=\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha n}FP(\textbf{p}^{i})+\sum_{i=\alpha n+1}^{n}FP(\textbf{r}^{i}),

\displaystyle FP(\textbf{b})=\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha n}FP(\textbf{q}^{i})+\sum_{i=\alpha n+1}^{n}FP(\textbf{s}^{i}).

Here,

Lemma 5.8

(Discretized Gaussians are close)
.

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(G_{\textbf{a}},G_{\textbf{b}})\leq 0.02
.

Proof.

Let
G^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}
be a random variable corresponding to picking a sample from gaussian with same mean and covariance as

(without any rounding). Similarly,
G^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}
corresponds to

. By data processing inequality, rounding each coordinate can only decrease the total variation distance,

.

By Lemma
D.1
, given 4 samples from a finite support, there exists an invertible linear map, say

, such that we can go from a vector of fingerprints to vector of collisions by applying

to the vector of fingerprints. Since we can decompose
a
and
b
into disjoint supports from each of which we are getting 4 samples, applying

also lets us go from vector of fingerprints to vector of collisions for samples obtained from
a
and
b
.

We apply
A
to random variables
G^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}
and
G^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}
.
Since
A
is invertible,
\mathrm{d_{TV}}(AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}},AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}})=\mathrm{d_{TV}}(G^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}},G^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}})
. Here
AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}
is a 3 dimensional Gaussian random variable with mean and covariance same as the mean and covariance of the vector containing number of 2-way, 3-way and 4-way collisions in the samples produced by
a
.
AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}
is related to
b
in the same fashion.

From Claim
5.4
, we know that expectations of
AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}}
and
AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}
match. Let us denote the expectation by
\mu
. From Claim
5.5
, we know that covariances of
AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}
and
AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}}
are as follows:

\Sigma(AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{a}})=\Sigma_{1}+n\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{11}/m^{2}&\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{13}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{22}/m^{3}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{13}/m^{4}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}&\alpha_{33}/m^{4}\end{bmatrix},~~~~\Sigma(AG^{\prime}_{\textbf{b}})=\Sigma_{1}+n\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{\prime}_{11}/m^{2}&\alpha^{\prime}_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha^{\prime}_{13}/m^{4}\\
\alpha^{\prime}_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha^{\prime}_{22}/m^{3}&\alpha^{\prime}_{23}/m^{4}\\
\alpha^{\prime}_{13}/m^{4}&\alpha^{\prime}_{23}/m^{4}&\alpha^{\prime}_{33}/m^{4}\end{bmatrix},

where

\Sigma_{1}=n\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{11}/m&\gamma_{12}/m^{2}&\gamma_{13}/m^{3}\\
\gamma_{12}/m^{2}&\gamma_{22}/m^{2}&\gamma_{23}/m^{3}\\

Let

, which is a technical lemma that bounds the TV distance between Gaussians having the same mean and similar covariances, and is proved at the end of this section, we know

. Since

∎

Lemma 5.9

(Fingerprints are close)
.

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(FP(\textbf{a}),FP(\textbf{b}))\leq 0.04
.

Proof.

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(FP(\textbf{a}),FP(\textbf{b}))\leq\mathrm{d_{TV}}(FP(\textbf{a}),G_{a})+\mathrm{d_{TV}}(FP(\textbf{b}),G_{b})+\mathrm{d_{TV}}(G_{a},G_{b})

0.04

5.7
and Lemma
5.8
.
∎

With this, we have shown that the TV distance between the distribution of fingerprints of the two sequences is small, even when the total variation distance between the corresponding average distributions is at least some constant. We can then invoke the Neyman-Pearson lemma
[

]
,
[

to say that no testing algorithm can distinguish between these two cases with high probability. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
A
and
D
be defined as follows:

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{11}/m^{2}&\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{13}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{22}/m^{3}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{13}/m^{4}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}&\alpha_{33}/m^{4}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and matrix }D=\begin{bmatrix}1/m^{2}&0&0\\
0&1/m^{3}&0\\
0&0&1/m^{4}\end{bmatrix},

where
\alpha_{ij}
are bounded by constants independent of
m
. For large enough
m
, there exists a positive constant
\alpha
such that,

-\alpha D\preceq A\preceq\alpha D.

Proof.

Let
v_{1}=[1,1,0]
,
v_{2}=[1,0,1]
and
v_{3}=[0,1,1]
.
Note that,

A=\frac{\alpha_{12}}{m^{3}}v_{1}v_{1}^{T}+\frac{\alpha_{13}}{m^{4}}v_{2}v_{2}^{T}+\frac{\alpha_{23}}{m^{4}}v_{3}v_{3}^{T}+D^{\prime},

where
D^{\prime}
is a diagonal matrix, whose entries are as follows:

D^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\alpha_{11}}{m^{2}}-\frac{\alpha_{12}}{m^{3}}-\frac{\alpha_{13}}{m^{4}}&0&0\\
0&\frac{\alpha_{22}}{m^{3}}-\frac{\alpha_{12}}{m^{3}}-\frac{\alpha_{23}}{m^{4}}&0\\
0&0&\frac{\alpha_{33}}{m^{4}}-\frac{\alpha_{13}}{m^{4}}-\frac{\alpha_{23}}{m^{4}}\end{bmatrix}

(21)

Note that, it is immediate that,
v_{1}v_{1}^{T}\preceq 2D_{1}
,
v_{2}v_{2}^{T}\preceq 2D_{2}
and
v_{3}v_{3}^{T}\preceq 2D_{3}
, where matrices
D_{1},D_{2}
and
D_{3}
are defined as follows,

D_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},~~D_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }D_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, as all
\alpha_{ij}
s are bounded by constants, we have that the matrix
D^{\prime}
satisfies,
D^{\prime}\preceq c\cdot D
for some positive constant

.

Combining all the above inequalities we get that,

A=\frac{\alpha_{12}}{m^{3}}v_{1}v_{1}^{T}+\frac{\alpha_{13}}{m^{4}}v_{2}v_{2}^{T}+\frac{\alpha_{23}}{m^{4}}v_{3}v_{3}^{T}+D^{\prime}\preceq\frac{2|\alpha_{12}|}{m^{3}}D_{1}+\frac{2|\alpha_{13}|}{m^{4}}D_{2}+\frac{2|\alpha_{23}|}{m^{4}}D_{3}+c\cdot D~.

The matrix in the final expression simplifies to,

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{c}{m^{2}}+\frac{2|\alpha_{12}|}{m^{3}}+\frac{2|\alpha_{13}|}{m^{4}}&0&0\\

As all
\alpha_{ij}
s are bounded by constants, we have that the above matrix is upper bounded by
\alpha\cdot D
for some large constant

. Therefore

.

The proof for the other side, that is

follows exactly the same argument when applied to

and we conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
G_{1}
be distributed as

and
G_{2}
be distributed as
N(\mu,\Sigma_{2})
where

\Sigma_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{11}/m&\gamma_{12}/m^{2}&\gamma_{13}/m^{3}\\
\gamma_{12}/m^{2}&\gamma_{22}/m^{2}&\gamma_{23}/m^{3}\\
\gamma_{13}/m^{3}&\gamma_{23}/m^{3}&\gamma_{33}/m^{3}\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }\Sigma_{2}=\Sigma_{1}+\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{11}/m^{2}&\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{13}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{22}/m^{3}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{13}/m^{4}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}&\alpha_{33}/m^{4}\end{bmatrix},

for
\gamma_{ij}
and
\alpha_{ij}
such that
|\gamma_{ij}|
and

are bounded by constants independent of

\mathrm{d_{TV}}(G_{1},G_{2})\leq 0.01

In our proof, we show that, for a sufficiently large enough

and

[

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{TV}}(G_{1},G_{2})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda^{-1}_{i})-1}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{O(1/m)}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}\leq O(1/m)~.

The above inequality holds because both the distributions
G_{1}
and

have the same mean. Now note that, for a sufficiently large

Therefore to prove our lemma, all that remains is to show that,
(1-\alpha/m)\Sigma_{1}\preceq\Sigma_{2}\preceq(1+\alpha/m)\Sigma_{1}

\alpha>0
. In the remainder of the proof, we prove this claim. First we show invertibility of

to the determinant, while all other permutations have absolute value that is bounded by

\Omega(1/m^{6})
and matrix
\Sigma_{1}
is invertible.
Let,

E=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{11}/m^{2}&\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{13}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{12}/m^{3}&\alpha_{22}/m^{3}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}\\
\alpha_{13}/m^{4}&\alpha_{23}/m^{4}&\alpha_{33}/m^{4}\end{bmatrix}

(22)

To prove the claim, we in turn show that,
-\frac{\alpha}{m}\Sigma_{1}\preceq E\preceq\frac{\alpha}{m}\Sigma_{1}
.

By
Lemma

5.10
, we know that there exist constants
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
such that,
-\alpha_{1}D\preceq E\preceq\alpha_{1}D
and
0\preceq\Sigma_{1}\preceq\alpha_{2}mD
, where

D=\begin{bmatrix}1/m^{2}&0&0\\
0&1/m^{3}&0\\
0&0&1/m^{4}\end{bmatrix}

In the following, we prove
E\preceq\frac{\alpha}{m}\Sigma_{1}
for some constant
\alpha>0
. We first show that
D\preceq\frac{\gamma}{m}\Sigma_{1}
for some constant
\gamma
, as

, we conclude that

. Towards this end, we now prove that

for some constant
\gamma
.
Equivalently, we wish to show that the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
F=D^{-1/2}\frac{1}{m}\Sigma_{1}D^{-1/2}
is lower bounded by some constant. Note that
F
is equal to,

F=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{11}&\gamma_{12}/m^{1/2}&\gamma_{13}/m\\
\gamma_{12}/m^{1/2}&\gamma_{22}&\gamma_{23}/m^{1/2}\\
\gamma_{13}/m&\gamma_{23}/m^{1/2}&\gamma_{33}\end{bmatrix}~.

Note that

is a PSD matrix as
\Sigma_{1}
is a PSD matrix. The trace of matrix
F
is given by,
\mathrm{Tr}(F)=\gamma_{11}+\gamma_{22}+\gamma_{33}\in\Omega(1)
. Also, note that for large enough
m
, the determinant of
F
is lower bounded by
\gamma_{11}\gamma_{22}\gamma_{33}/2\in\Omega(1)
because the permutation correpsonding to the diagonal entries contributes
\gamma_{11}\gamma_{22}\gamma_{33}
and for a large enough
m
all the other permutations contribute at most
\gamma_{11}\gamma_{22}\gamma_{33}/2
in absolute value.

Recall that trace is the sum of eigenvalues and determinant is the product of eigenvalues, and we have just shown that both the trace and determinant are lower bounded by positive constants. Consequently, as all the eigenvalues of
F
are non-negative, we immediately conclude that all the eigenvalues are additionally lower bounded by a positive constant and we conclude that
E\preceq\frac{\alpha}{m}\Sigma_{1}
.

The proof for the other side, that is
-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot\Sigma_{1}\preceq E
follows exactly the same argument when applied to
-E
and we conclude the proof.

Appendix A
Learning the average distribution

In this section, we show that the sample complexity of
learning
the average distribution in the setting of non-identical samples is asymptotically equal to that of the i.i.d.
See
1.1

Proof.

The lower bound for the setting of non-identical samples follows directly from the lower bound of the standard i.i.d setting, that is if we let
\textbf{p}_{t}=\textbf{p},\forall t\in[T]
. For the upper bound, we follow the proof in
[
Can20
, Theorem 1]
. Consider the empirical estimator given by,

\displaystyle\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(i)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}1[X_{t}=i].

Observe that by definition,
\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}},\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}})>\varepsilon
implies that there exists some
S\subseteq[k]
such that
\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)>\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)+\varepsilon
. Fix any
S\subseteq[k]
and we have,

\displaystyle\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i\in S}\sum_{t\in T}1[X_{t}=i].

Define
Y_{t}=\sum_{i\in S}1[X_{t}=i]
so that
\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}
. Note that, each
Y_{t}
is a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=\textbf{p}_{t}(S)
. Furthermore, since samples from different distributions are independent, all the
Y_{t}
’s are independent and we have that,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}>\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}\right]+\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)>\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in T}\textbf{p}_{t}(S)+\varepsilon\right]=\Pr[\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)>\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)+\varepsilon],

and hence, letting
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}\right]
, we get

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)>\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)+\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}>\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}\right]+\varepsilon\right]=\Pr\left[\sum_{t\in[T]}Y_{t}>\left(1+\frac{T\varepsilon}{\mu}\right)\mu\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{\mu(2+T\varepsilon/\mu)}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{T^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{2\mu+T\varepsilon}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{T\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\right),

where in the first inequality, we used the Chernoff bound, and in the last inequality, we used the facts that
\varepsilon\leq 1,\mu\leq T
.
Setting this failure probability to
\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{k}}
along with the union bound over
2^{k}
possible values of
S
would give us the required result, namely

\displaystyle\Pr[\exists S\in[k]\text{ s.t. }\tilde{\textbf{p}}_{\mathrm{avg}}(S)>\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}+\varepsilon]\leq\frac{1}{3}.

Correspondingly,

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{T\varepsilon^{2}}{3}\right)=\frac{1}{3\cdot 2^{k}}\implies T=O(k/\varepsilon^{2}).

We conclude the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Poissonized setting

In this section, we state and prove some results for the non-i.i.d setting, where we are also allowed to use Poissonization. We will use the following standard facts about Poisson distributions.

Let
\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda)
denote a Poisson random variable with mean parameter
\lambda\geq 0
.

Fact C.1
.

For any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0
, if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent random variables distributed as

X_{1}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1})
and
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{2})
, then
X_{1}+X_{2}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})~.

Fact C.2
.

Given
\mathrm{Poi}(c)
samples from a distribution
\textbf{p}\in\Delta_{k}
, the frequency of any element
j\in[k]
follows the distribution
\mathrm{Poi}(c\cdot\textbf{p}(j))
.

We are now ready to prove the following theorem.

Claim C.3

(Poisson identity testing)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
and reference distribution
\textbf{q}\in\Delta_{k}
, with
\mathrm{Poi}(c)
samples from each of
\textbf{p}_{1},\dots,\textbf{p}_{T}
and
T=O(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
, there exists a testing algorithm that succeeds with probability at least
2/3
and tests whether,

\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\textbf{q}\quad\text{ versus }\quad\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}},\textbf{q})\geq\varepsilon~,

for any
c\geq 1
.

Proof.

Note that we are given
\mathrm{Poi}(c)
samples from each distribution
\textbf{p}_{t}
for all
t\in[T]
. For each
t\in[T]
, let
N_{t}
denote the number of samples drawn from
\textbf{p}_{t}
. Note that
N_{t}\sim\mathrm{Poi}(c)
. For
i\in[k]
, let
N_{t}(i)
be the random variable that denotes the frequency of element
i
in samples
X_{t}
, that is,
N_{t}(i)=|\{j\in[N_{t}]~:~X_{t}(j)=i\}|
. By
C.2
, we know that

N_{t}(i)\sim\mathrm{Poi}(c\cdot\textbf{p}_{t}(i))~.

Furthermore, if we let
N(i)=\sum_{t\in[T]}N_{t}(i)
, then by
C.1
, we get that,

N(i)\sim\mathrm{Poi}\Big(c\cdot\sum_{t\in[T]}\textbf{p}_{t}(i)\Big)=\mathrm{Poi}(cT\cdot\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}(i))~.

Therefore,
N(i)
has the same distribution as the frequency of element
i
when we are given
\mathrm{Poi}(cT)
i.i.d samples from distribution
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}
. As
cT\in O(\sqrt{k}/\varepsilon^{2})
, and since we just argued that we are under the setting of having drawn
\mathrm{Poi}(cT)
samples from
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}
, we can invoke existing identity testing algorithms that use Poissonization for the i.i.d setting
to solve the problem with success probability
2/3
, even with
c=1
.
∎

Appendix D
Collisions and Fingerprints

We relate the collision statistics and the fingerprint of a sample by an invertible linear map. To recall, the number of
m
-way collisions in a sample
S=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
is equal to
\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}}^{n}1[X_{i_{1}}=\dots=X_{i_{m}]}]
. The fingerprint of the sample is a count array, where for each
i
, we list the count of the number of support elements that appeared exactly
i
times in
S
.

Lemma D.1

(Collisions to fingerprint)
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in[m]^{4}
for
m>4
. Let
c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
denote the number of
2
-way, 3-way and
4
-way collisions amongst
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
. Further, let
n_{2},n_{3},n_{4}
denote the number of elements in
[m]
occurring exactly 2 times, 3 times and 4 times respectively in
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
. Then, the vectors
\begin{bmatrix}c_{2}\\
c_{3}\\
c_{4}\end{bmatrix}
and
\begin{bmatrix}n_{2}\\
n_{3}\\
n_{4}\end{bmatrix}
are related by an invertible linear map. In particular,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}n_{2}\\
n_{3}\\
n_{4}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-3&6\\
0&1&-4\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_{2}\\
c_{3}\\
c_{4}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle n_{4}=c_{4},~~n_{3}=c_{3}-\binom{4}{3}n_{4}=c_{3}-4c_{4}

\displaystyle n_{2}=c_{2}-\binom{3}{2}n_{3}-\binom{4}{2}n_{4}=c_{2}-3(c_{3}-4c_{4})-6c_{4}=c_{2}-3c_{3}+6c_{4}.

The matrix associated with the linear map has determinant
1
, therefore the map is invertible.
∎

Appendix E
Sufficiency of fingerprint for pooling-based testing algorithms

We first formally state the definition of a pooling-based testing algorithm.

Definition E.1

(Pooling-based testing algorithm)
.

Fix
c

\pi\sim\textrm{Perm}(cT)
denote drawing a uniformly random permutation
\pi

cT

\mathcal{A}
for property
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{k}

\textbf{p}_{1},\dots,\textbf{p}_{T}

\mathcal{A}(S)\in\{0,1\}

•

If
\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}}\in\mathcal{P}
, then
\Pr_{S,\pi,\mathcal{A}}[\mathcal{A}[S]=1]\geq 2/3
.

•

If
\mathrm{d_{TV}}(\textbf{p}_{\mathrm{avg}},\mathcal{P})\geq\varepsilon
, then
\Pr_{S,\pi,\mathcal{A}}[\mathcal{A}[S]=0]\geq 2/3
.

The following lemma states that the fingerprint of the combined sample captures all the information required for a pooling-based testing algorithm to test a symmetric property of the average distribution.

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a pooling-based testing algorithm having sample complexity

that tests a symmetric property

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

Let the fingerprint of the combined sample
S
be denoted by

f_{i}
is the number of elements in
[k]
that occur exactly
i

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}

S=\phi
,

2.

\displaystyle\qquad\text{While }f_{i}>0:

Draw
\pi_{k}\sim\textrm{Perm}(k)
.

4.

For every

\displaystyle\qquad\text{Set }s\leftarrow\pi_{k}(s).

Draw
\pi_{S}\sim\textrm{Perm}(cT)

Set
