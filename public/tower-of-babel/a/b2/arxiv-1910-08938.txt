Versatile stabilized finite element formulations for nearly and fully incompressible solid mechanics Journal: Computational Mechanics
Elias Karabelas, Gundolf Haase, Gernot Plank and Christoph M. Augustin

Abstract
Computational formulations for large strain, polyconvex, nearly incompressible elasticity have been extensively studied, but research on enhancing solution schemes that offer better tradeoffs between accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency remains to be highly relevant. In this paper, we present two methods to overcome locking phenomena, one based on a displacement-pressure formulation using a stable finite element pairing with bubble functions, and another one using a simple pressure-projection stabilized ℙ 1 − ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}_{1}-\mathbb{P}_{1} finite element pair. A key advantage is the versatility of the proposed methods: with minor adjustments they are applicable to all kinds of finite elements and generalize easily to transient dynamics. The proposed methods are compared to and verified with standard benchmarks previously reported in the literature. Benchmark results demonstrate that both approaches provide a robust and computationally efficient way of simulating nearly and fully incompressible materials.



1
Introduction

Locking phenomena, caused by ill-conditioned global stiffness matrices in
finite element analyses, are an often observed and extensively studied issue
when modeling nearly incompressible, hyperelastic materials
Babuška & Suri 1992
;
Braess 2007
;
Hughes 1987
;
Wriggers 2008
;
Zienkiewicz et al. 2000
.
Typically, methods based on Lagrange multipliers are applied to enforce
incompressibility. A common approach is the split of the deformation gradient
into a volumetric and an isochoric part
Flory 1961
.
Here, locking commonly arises when unstable standard displacement formulations
are used that rely on linear shape functions to approximate the displacement
field
{{\mathbf{u}}}
and piecewise-constant finite elements combined with static
condensation of the hydrostatic pressure
p
,
e.g.,
\mathbb{P}_{1}-\mathbb{P}_{0}
elements.
It is well known that in such cases solution algorithms may exhibit very low
convergence rates and that variables of interest such as stresses can be
inaccurate
Gültekin et al. 2018
.

From mathematical theory it is well known that approximation spaces for the
primal variable
{{\mathbf{u}}}
and
p
have to be well chosen to fulfill
the Ladyzhenskaya-Babuŝka–Brezzi (LBB) or
inf-sup

condition
Babuška 1973
;
Brezzi 1974
;
Chapelle & Bathe 1993

to guarantee stability.
A classical stable approximation pair is the
Taylor–Hood element
Taylor & Hood 1973
,
however, this requires quadratic ansatz functions for the displacement part.

For certain types of problems higher order interpolations
can improve efficiency as higher accuracy is already reached with coarser
discretizations
Chamberland et al. 2010
;
Land et al. 2015
.
In many applications though, where geometries are fitted to, e.g.,
capture fine structural features, this is not beneficial due to a
possible increase in degrees of freedom and consequently a higher
computational burden.
Also for coupled problems such as electromechanical or
fluid-structure-interaction models high-resolution grids for mechanical
problems are sometimes required when interpolations between grids are not
desired
Augustin et al. 2016
;
Karabelas et al. 2018
.
As a remedy for these kind of applications quasi
Taylor–Hood elements with an order of
\tfrac{3}{2}
have been considered,
see
Quaglino et al. 2017
, as well as equal order linear pairs of ansatz
functions which has been a field of intensive research in the last decades,
see
Auricchio et al. 2017
;
Hughes et al. 2017
and references therein.
Unfortunately, equal order pairings do not fulfill the LBB
conditions and hence a stabilization of the element is of crucial importance.

There is a significant body of literature devoted to
stabilized finite elements for the Stokes and Navier–Stokes equations.
Many of those methods were extended to incompressible elasticity, amongst other approaches by Hughes, Franca,
Balestra, and collaborators
Franca et al. 1988
;
Hughes et al. 1986
.
Masud and co-authors followed an idea by means of variational multiscale (VMS)
methods
Masud & Truster 2013
;
Masud & Xia 2005
;
Nakshatrala et al. 2007
;
Xia & Masud 2009
,
a technique that was recently extended to dynamic problems
(D-VMS)
Scovazzi et al. 2016
;
Rossi et al. 2016
.
Further stabilizations of equal order finite elements include orthogonal sub-scale
methods
Chiumenti et al. 2015
;
Codina 2000
;
Lafontaine et al. 2015
;
Cervera et al. 2003

and methods based on pressure projections
Dohrmann & Bochev 2004
;
Zienkiewicz et al. 1998
.
Different classes of methods to avoid locking for nearly incompressible elasticity
were conceived by introducing nonconforming finite elements such as the
Crouzeix–Raviart element
Di Pietro & Lemaire 2014
;
Falk 1991
and Discontinuous Galerkin methods
Kabaria et al. 2015
;
Ten Eyck & Lew 2006
.
Enhanced strain formulations
Reese et al. 1998
;
Taylor 2000
have been considered as well as
formulations based on multi-field variational
principles
Bonet et al. 2015
;
Schröder et al. 2016
;
Schröder et al. 2011
.

In this study we introduce a novel variant of the MINI
element for accurately solving nearly and fully
incompressible elasticity problems.
The MINI element was originally established for computational fluid dynamics
problems
Arnold et al. 1984
and pure tetrahedral meshes
and previously used in the large strain regime, e.g. in

Chamberland et al. 2010
;
Lamichhane 2009
.
We extend the MINI element definition for hexahedral meshes by
introducing two bubble functions in the element and provide a novel
proof of stability and well-posedness in the case of linear elasticity.
The support of the bubble functions is restricted to the element and can thus
be eliminated from the system using static condensation.
This also allows for a straightforward inclusion in
combination with existing finite element codes since all
required implementations are purely on the element level.
Additionally, we introduce a pressure-projection stabilization
method originally published for the
Stokes equations
Bochev et al. 2006
;
Dohrmann & Bochev 2004

and previously used for large strain nearly incompressible elasticity in the
field of particle finite element methods and plasticity
Rodriguez et al. 2016
;
Cante et al. 2014
.
Due to its
simplicity
, this type of stabilization is especially attractive
from an implementation point of view.

Robustness and performance of both the MINI element and the
pressure-projection approach are verified and compared to standard benchmarks
reported previously in literature.
A key advantage of the proposed methods is their
high versatility
:
first, they are readily applicable to nearly and fully incompressible solid
mechanics; second, with little adjustments the stabilization techniques can be
applied to all kinds of finite elements, in this study we investigate the
performance for hexahedral and tetrahedral meshes;
and third, the methods generalize easily to transient dynamics.

Real world applications often require highly-resolved meshes and thus
efficient and massively parallel solution algorithms for the
linearized system of equations become an important factor to deal with the
resulting computational load. We solve the arising saddle-point systems
by using a GMRES method with a block preconditioner based on an
algebraic multigrid (AMG) approach. Extending our previous
implementations
Augustin et al. 2016
we
performed the numerical simulations with the software

Cardiac Arrhythmia Research Package

(CARP)
Vigmond et al. 2008
which relies on the MPI based library

PETSc

Balay et al. 2018
and the incorporated solver suite

hypre/BoomerAMG

Henson & Yang 2002
. The combination of these
advanced solving algorithms with the proposed stable elements which only rely on
linear shape functions proves to be
very efficient
and renders feasible
simulations on grids with high structural detail.

The paper is outlined as follows:
Section
2
summarizes in brief the background on the methods.
In Section
3
, we introduce the finite element discretization
and discuss stability.
Subsequently, Section
4
documents benchmark problems
where our proposed elements are applied and compared to results published in the
literature.
Finally, Section
5
concludes the paper with a discussion of
the results and a brief summary.

2
Continuum mechanics

2.1
Nearly incompressible nonlinear elasticity

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the reference configuration and
let
\Omega_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the current configuration
of the domain of interest. Assume that the boundary of
\Omega_{0}
is decomposed into

\partial\Omega_{0}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{D},0}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},0}

with
|\Gamma_{D,0}|>0
.
Here,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D},0}
describes the Dirichlet part of the boundary
and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},0}
describes the Neumann part of the boundary, respectively.
Further, let
{{\mathbf{n}}}_{0}
be the unit outward normal on
\partial\Omega_{0}
.
The nonlinear mapping
\bm{\phi}\colon\bm{\mathbf{X}}\in\Omega_{0}\rightarrow{{\mathbf{x}}}\in\Omega_{t}
,
defined by
\bm{\phi}:=\bm{\mathbf{X}}+{{\mathbf{u}}}(\bm{\mathbf{X}},t)
, with displacement
{{\mathbf{u}}}
,
maps points in the reference configuration to points in the current configuration.
Following standard notation we introduce the
deformation gradient

\bm{F}

and the Jacobian
J
as

\bm{F}:=\operatorname{Grad}\bm{\phi}=\bm{I}+\operatorname{Grad}{{\mathbf{u}}},\quad J:=\det(\bm{F}),

and the
left Cauchy-Green tensor
as
\bm{C}:=\bm{F}^{\top}\bm{F}
.
Here,
\operatorname{Grad}(\bullet)
denotes the gradient with respect to the reference
coordinates
\underline{X}\in\Omega_{0}
. The displacement field
{{\mathbf{u}}}

is sought as infimizer of the functional

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mathrm{tot}}({{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle:=\Pi({{\mathbf{u}}})+\Pi^{\mathrm{ext}}({{\mathbf{u}}}),

\displaystyle\Pi({{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\Psi(\bm{F}({{\mathbf{u}}}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mathrm{ext}}({{\mathbf{u}}})

\displaystyle:=-\rho_{0}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}{{\mathbf{f}}}({{\mathbf{x}}})\cdot{{\mathbf{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}-\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N,0}}}{{\mathbf{h}}}({{\mathbf{x}}})\cdot{{\mathbf{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{\bm{\mathbf{X}}},

(1)

over all admissible fields
{{\mathbf{u}}}
with
{{\mathbf{u}}}={{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}
on

\Gamma_{\mathrm{D,0}}
, where,
\Psi
denotes the strain energy function;

\rho_{0}
denotes the material density in reference configuration;

{{\mathbf{f}}}
denotes a volumetric body force;

{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}
denotes a given boundary displacement;
and
{{\mathbf{h}}}
denotes a given surface traction.
For ease of presentation it is assumed that
\rho_{0}
is constant and

{{\mathbf{f}}}
,
{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}
, and
{{\mathbf{h}}}
do not depend on
{{\mathbf{u}}}
.
Existence of infimizers is, under suitable assumptions,
guaranteed by the pioneering works of Ball, see
Ball 1976
.

In this study we consider nearly incompressible materials,
meaning that
J\approx 1
. A possibility to model this behavior was
originally proposed by
Flory 1961

using a split of the deformation gradient
\bm{F}
such that

\bm{F}=\bm{F}_{\mathrm{vol}}\overline{\bm{F}}.

(2)

Here,
\bm{F}_{\mathrm{vol}}
describes the volumetric change
while
\overline{\bm{F}}
describes the isochoric change.
By setting
\bm{F}_{\mathrm{vol}}:=J^{\frac{1}{3}}\bm{I}
and

\overline{\bm{F}}:=J^{-\frac{1}{3}}\bm{F}

we get
\det(\overline{\bm{F}})=1
and
\det(\bm{F}_{\mathrm{vol}})=J
.
Analogously, by setting
\bm{C}_{\mathrm{vol}}:=J^{\frac{2}{3}}\bm{I}
and

\overline{\bm{C}}:=J^{-\frac{2}{3}}\bm{C}
,
we have
\bm{C}=\bm{C}_{\mathrm{vol}}\overline{\bm{C}}
.
Assuming a hyperelastic material, the Flory split also postulates an additive
decomposition of the strain energy function

\Psi=\Psi(\bm{C})=\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}})+\kappa U(J),

(3)

where
\kappa
is the
bulk modulus
. The function
U(J)

acts as a penalization of incompressibility and we require that it is
strictly convex and twice continuously differentiable. Additionally,
a constitutive model for
U(J)
should fulfill that (i) it vanishes in the reference
configuration and that (ii) an infinite amount of energy is required to shrink
the body to a point or to expand it indefinitely, i.e.,

\mbox{(i)}\ U(1)=0,\ \mbox{(ii)}\lim_{J\to 0+}U(J)=\infty,\ \lim_{J\to\infty}U(J)=\infty.

For the remainder of this work we will focus on functions
U(J)
that can be written as

\displaystyle U(J):=\frac{1}{2}{(\Theta(J))}^{2}.

In the literature many different choices for the function
\Theta(J)
are
proposed, see, e.g.,
Doll & Schweizerhof 2000
;
Hartmann & Neff 2003
;
Rossi et al. 2016

for examples and related discussion.

As we also want to study the case of full incompressibility,
meaning
\kappa\to\infty
, we need a reformulation of the system.
In this work we will use a perturbed Lagrange-multiplier functional,
see
Sussman & Bathe 1987
;
Atluri & Reissner 1989
;
Brink & Stein 1996
for details,
and we introduce

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mathrm{PL}}({{\mathbf{u}}},q):=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}}({{\mathbf{u}}}))+q\Theta(J({{\mathbf{u}}}))-\frac{1}{2\kappa}q^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}.

We will now seek infimizers
({{\mathbf{u}}},p)\in V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}}\times Q

of the modified functional

\displaystyle\Pi^{\mathrm{tot}}({{\mathbf{u}}},q):=\Pi^{\mathrm{PL}}({{\mathbf{u}}},q)+\Pi^{\mathrm{ext}}({{\mathbf{u}}}).

(4)

To guarantee that the discretization of (
4
) is well defined,
we assume that

V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}}=\{{{\mathbf{v}}}\in{[H^{1}(\Omega_{0})]}^{3}:\left.{{\mathbf{v}}}\right|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D},0}}={{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}\},

with
H^{1}(\Omega_{0})
being the standard Sobolev space consisting of all
square integrable functions with square integrable gradient, and
Q=L^{2}(\Omega_{0})
.
Existence of infimizers in
V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}}
cannot be guaranteed in general.
However, assuming suitable growth conditions on the strain energy function

\Psi
, and assuming that the initial data keeps the material in the hyperelastic range,
one can conclude that the space
V
for the infimizer
{{\mathbf{u}}}
contains

V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}}
as a subset, see
Ball 1976
for details.

To solve for the infimizers of (
4
) we require to compute
the variations of (
4
) with respect to
\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}
and
\Delta p

\displaystyle D_{\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}}\Pi^{\mathrm{PL}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\left(\bm{S}_{\mathrm{isc}}+p\bm{S}_{\mathrm{vol}}\right):\bm{\Sigma}({{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle-\rho_{0}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}{{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}-\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},0}}{{\mathbf{h}}}\cdot\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{\bm{\mathbf{X}}},

(5)

\displaystyle D_{\Delta q}\Pi^{\mathrm{PL}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\left(\Theta(J)-\frac{1}{\kappa}p\right)\Delta q\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

(6)

with abbreviations as, e.g., in
Holzapfel 2000

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\mathrm{isc}}

\displaystyle:=J^{-\frac{2}{3}}\mathrm{Dev}(\overline{\bm{S}}),\quad\text{where~}\overline{\bm{S}}:=\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}})}{\partial\overline{\bm{C}}}

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\mathrm{vol}}

\displaystyle:=\pi(J)\bm{C}^{-1},\quad\text{with~}\pi(J):=J\Theta^{\prime}(J),

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}({{\mathbf{u}}},{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{sym}(\bm{F}^{\top}({{\mathbf{u}}})\operatorname{Grad}{{\mathbf{v}}}).

(9)

Next, with notations

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{isc}}({{\mathbf{u}}};\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\bm{S}_{\mathrm{isc}}({{\mathbf{u}}}):\bm{\Sigma}({{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

(10)

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{vol}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}p\bm{S}_{\mathrm{vol}}({{\mathbf{u}}}):\bm{\Sigma}({{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

(11)

\displaystyle b_{\mathrm{vol}}({{\mathbf{u}}};\Delta q)

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\Theta(J({{\mathbf{u}}}))\Delta q\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

(12)

\displaystyle c(p,\Delta q)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}p\Delta q\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

(13)

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{body}}(\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{0}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}{{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

(14)

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{surface}}(\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},0}}{{\mathbf{h}}}\cdot\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}s_{\bm{\mathbf{X}}},

(15)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{upper}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=a_{\mathrm{isc}}({{\mathbf{u}}};\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})+a_{\mathrm{vol}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle-l_{\mathrm{body}}(\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})-l_{\mathrm{surface}}(\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}),

(16)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta q)

\displaystyle:=b_{\mathrm{vol}}({{\mathbf{u}}};\Delta q)-c(p,\Delta q),

(17)

we formulate the mixed boundary value problem of nearly incompressible
nonlinear elasticity via a nonlinear system of equations.
This yields a nonlinear saddle-point problem,
find
({{\mathbf{u}}},p)\in V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}\times Q
such that

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{upper}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta q)

\displaystyle=0,

(19)

for all
(\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}},\Delta q)\in V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}\times Q
.

2.2
Consistent linearization

To solve the nonlinear variational equations (
18
)–(
19
),
with a finite element approach we first apply a Newton–Raphson scheme,
for details we refer to
Deuflhard 2011
.
Given a nonlinear and continuously differentiable operator
F\colon X\to Y

a solution to
F(x)=0
can be approximated by

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=x^{k}+\Delta x,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\right|_{x=x^{k}}\Delta x

\displaystyle=-F(x^{k}),

which is looped until convergence.
In our case, we have
X=V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}\times Q
,
Y=\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\Delta x={(\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta p)}^{\top}
,
x^{k}={({{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},p^{k})}^{\top}
,
and
F={(R_{\mathrm{upper}},R_{\mathrm{lower}})}^{\top}
.
We obtain the following linear saddle-point problem for each

({{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},p^{k})\in V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}\times Q
,
find
(\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta p)\in V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}\times Q
such that

\displaystyle a_{k}(\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})+b_{k}(\Delta p,\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=-R_{\mathrm{upper}}({{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},p^{k};\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}),

(20)

\displaystyle b_{k}(\Delta q,\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}})-c(\Delta p,\Delta q)

\displaystyle=-R_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},p^{k};\Delta q),

(21)

where

\displaystyle a_{k}(\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}},\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\operatorname{Grad}\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\bm{S}_{\mathrm{tot},k}:\operatorname{Grad}\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\bm{\Sigma}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{k},\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}):\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{tot},k}:\bm{\Sigma}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{k},\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

\displaystyle b_{k}(\Delta p,\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\Delta p\pi(J_{k})\bm{F}^{-\top}_{k}:\operatorname{Grad}\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}},

with abbreviations

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bm{F}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}),

\displaystyle J_{k}

\displaystyle:=\det(\bm{F}_{k}),

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\mathrm{tot},k}

\displaystyle:=\left.\bm{S}_{\mathrm{isc}}\right|_{{{\mathbf{u}}}={{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}}+p_{k}\left.\bm{S}_{\mathrm{vol}}\right|_{{{\mathbf{u}}}={{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{tot},k}

\displaystyle:=\left.\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{isc}}\right|_{{{\mathbf{u}}}={{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}}+p_{k}\left.\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{vol}}\right|_{{{\mathbf{u}}}={{\mathbf{u}}}_{k}},

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{vol}}

\displaystyle:=k(J)\bm{C}^{-1}\otimes\bm{C}^{-1}-2\pi(J)\bm{C}^{-1}\odot\bm{C}^{-1},

\displaystyle k(J)

\displaystyle:=J^{2}\Theta^{\prime\prime}(J)+J\Theta^{\prime}(J),

where
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{isc}}
is given in (
57
).

The derivation of the consistent linearization is lengthy but standard,
we refer to
Holzapfel 2000
, Chapter 8
for details.
The definition of the higher order tensor and other abbreviations are given
in the Appendix.

2.3
Review on solvability of the linearized problem

Since (
20
)–(
21
)
is a linear saddle-point problem for each given
({{\mathbf{u}}}^{k},p^{k})

we can rely on the well-established Babuška–Brezzi theory,
see
Boffi et al. 2013
;
Ern & Guermond 2013
;
Rüter & Stein 2000
;
Schwab 1998
.
The crucial properties to guarantee that the
problem (
20
)–(
21
) is well-posed
are continuity of all involved bilinear forms and the following three conditions:

(i)

The
inf-sup condition
:
there exists
c_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\underset{q\in Q}{\inf}\ \underset{{{\mathbf{v}}}\in V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}}{\sup}\frac{b_{k}(q,{{\mathbf{v}}})}{{\left\lVert{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\right\rVert}_{V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}}{\left\lVert{q}\right\rVert}_{Q}}\geq c_{1}.

(24)

(ii)

The
coercivity on the kernel condition
:
there exists
c_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle a_{k}({{\mathbf{v}}},{{\mathbf{v}}})\geq c_{2}{\left\lVert{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\right\rVert}_{V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{for all }{{\mathbf{v}}}\in\ker B,

(25)

where

\displaystyle\ker B:=\left\{{{\mathbf{v}}}\in V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}:b_{k}(q,{{\mathbf{v}}})=0~\text{for all }q\in Q\right\}.

(iii)

Positivity of
c
: it holds

\displaystyle c(q,q)\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{for all }q\in Q.

(26)

Upon observing that
\bm{F}^{-\top}:\operatorname{Grad}{{\mathbf{v}}}=\operatorname{div}{{{\mathbf{v}}}}
,
see
Holzapfel 2000
, we rewrite the bilinear form
b_{k}(q,{{\mathbf{v}}})
as

\displaystyle b_{k}(q,{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}q\pi(J_{k})\bm{F}^{-\top}_{k}:\operatorname{Grad}{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}q\pi(J_{k})\operatorname{div}{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{t}}q\Theta^{\prime}(J_{k})\operatorname{div}{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}.

(27)

Assuming that
\Theta^{\prime}(J)\geq 1
, we can conclude the

inf-sup
condition from standard arguments, see
Weise 2014
, Section 5.2
.
The positivity of the bilinear form
c
is always fulfilled.
However, it is not possible to show the coercivity condition (
25
)
for a general hyperelastic material or load configuration.
Nevertheless, for some special cases it is possible to establish a result.
We refer to
Weise 2014
;
Auricchio et al. 2005
;
Auricchio et al. 2010
for a more detailed discussion.
Henceforth, we will assume that our given input data is such that we stay
in the range of stability of the problem.
Examples for cases in which bilinear form
a_{k}
lacks coercivity can be found
in
Weise 2014
, Chapter 9
and
Auricchio et al. 2010
, Section 4
.

3
Finite element approximation and stabilization

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a finite element partitioning of
\overline{\Omega}

into subdomains, in our case either tetrahedral or convex hexahedral elements.
The partitioning is assumed to fulfill standard regularity conditions, see
Ciarlet 2002
.
Let
\hat{K}
be the reference element,
and for
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
denote by
F_{\mathrm{K}}
the affine,
or trilinear mapping from
\hat{K}
onto
K
.
We assume that
F_{\mathrm{K}}
is a bijection. For a tetrahedral element
K
this can
be assured whenever
K
is non-degenerate, however, for hexahedral elements
this may not necessary be the case, see
Knabner et al. 2003
for details.
Further, let
\hat{\mathbb{V}}
and
\hat{\mathbb{Y}}
denote two polynomial
spaces defined over
\hat{K}
. We denote by

\displaystyle V_{h,0}

\displaystyle:=\left\{{{\mathbf{v}}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega_{0}):{{\mathbf{v}}}=\hat{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\circ F_{\mathrm{K}}^{-1},\hat{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\in{[\hat{\mathbb{V}}]}^{3},\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

(28)

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle:=\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega_{0}):p=\hat{p}\circ F_{\mathrm{K}}^{-1},\hat{p}\in\hat{\mathbb{Y}},\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\},

(29)

\displaystyle V_{h,{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}}

\displaystyle:=H^{1}_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{\mathrm{D}}}(\Omega_{0})\cap V_{h,0},

(30)

the spaces needed for further analysis in the following sections.

3.1
Nearly incompressible linear elasticity

Boffi & Stenberg 2017
;
Shariff 1997
;
Shariff & Parker 2000

As a model problem we study the well-known equations for
nearly incompressible linear elasticity.

In this case it is assumed that
\Omega:=\Omega_{0}\approx\Omega_{t}
.
Then, the linear elasticity problem reads: find
({{\mathbf{u}}},p)\in V_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}\times Q
such that

\displaystyle 2\mu\int\limits_{\Omega}\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{u}}}):\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\int\limits_{\Omega}p\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}{{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

(31)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{u}}}q\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int\limits_{\Omega}pq\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=0

(32)

for all
({{\mathbf{v}}},q)\in V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}\times Q
.
Here,
\mu>0
and
\lambda
denote the Lamé parameters, and

\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{v}}}):=\mathrm{sym}(\operatorname{grad}{{\mathbf{v}}})
.

The regularity of (
31
)–(
32
)
is a classical result
Steinbach 2008

and follows with the same arguments as for the Stokes equations.
The discretized analogue of (
31
)–(
32
) is:
find
({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h},p_{h})\in V_{h,{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}\times Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle 2\mu\int\limits_{\Omega}\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h}):\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{v}}}_{h})\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\int\limits_{\Omega}p_{h}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

(33)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{u}}}_{h}q_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int\limits_{\Omega}p_{h}q_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=0

(34)

for all
({{\mathbf{v}}}_{h},q_{h})\in V_{h,{{\mathbf{0}}}}\times Q_{h}
.
Coercivity on the kernel condition (
25
)
is a standard result for the case of nearly incompressible linear
elasticity posed in the form (
31
)-(
32
) and (
33
)-(
34
).
In the nonlinear case this is not true in general and will be adressed
in Section
3.4
.

The crucial point for checking well-posedness of the discrete
equations (
33
)–(
34
)
is the fulfillment of the
discrete inf-sup condition
, reading

\displaystyle\underset{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}{\inf}\underset{{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\in V_{h,{{\mathbf{0}}}}}{\sup}\frac{\int\limits_{\Omega}q_{h}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}}{{\left\lVert{{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}}\right\rVert}_{V_{{{\mathbf{0}}}}}{\left\lVert{q_{h}}\right\rVert}_{Q}}>0.

(35)

The discrete
inf-sup
condition puts constraints on the choice of the spaces

V_{h,0}
and
Q_{h}
. A finite element pairing fulfilling (
35
)
is called a
stable pair
.
A classic example for tetrahedral meshes would be the Taylor–Hood element.
In this paper, we will focus on two different finite element pairings,
the MINI element and a stabilized equal order element.

The stabilized equal order pairing has been used in this context for pure tetrahedral meshes, see
Cante et al. 2014
;
Rodriguez et al. 2016
. To the best of the authors knowledge those elements have not been used in the present context for general tesselations.

3.2
The pressure-projection stabilized equal order pair

In the following, we present a stabilized lowest equal order finite
element pairing, adapted to nonlinear elasticity from the pairing originally
introduced by
Dohrmann & Bochev 2004
;
Bochev et al. 2006
for the Stokes
equations.

We choose
\hat{\mathbb{V}}
and
\hat{\mathbb{Y}}
in (
28
)–(
29
)
as the space of linear (or trilinear) functions over
\hat{K}
.
This choice of spaces is a textbook example of an unstable element, however,
following
Dohrmann & Bochev 2004
, we can introduce a stabilized formulation
of (
33
)–(
34
) by:
find
({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h},p_{h})\in V_{h,{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}\times Q_{h}
such that

\displaystyle\mu\int\limits_{\Omega}\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h}):\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{v}}}_{h})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\int\limits_{\Omega}p_{h}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}},

(36)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{u}}}_{h}q_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\int\limits_{\Omega}p_{h}q_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}\quad\qquad

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mu^{\ast}}s_{h}(p_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle=0,

(37)

for all
({{\mathbf{v}}}_{h},q_{h})\in V_{h,0}\times Q_{h}
, where

\displaystyle s_{h}(p_{h},q_{h}):=\int\limits_{\Omega}(p_{h}-\Pi_{h}p_{h})(q_{h}-\Pi_{h}q_{h})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

(38)

and
\mu^{\ast}>0
a suitable parameter. We note that the integral in (
38
) has to be understood as sum over integrals of elements of the tessellation.
The projection operator
\Pi_{h}
is defined element-wise for each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}

\displaystyle\left.\Pi_{h}p_{h}\right|_{K}:=\frac{1}{{\lvert{K}\rvert}}\int\limits_{K}p_{h}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}.

We can state the following results for this discrete problem:

Theorem 3.1

There exists a unique bounded solution to the discrete
problem (
36
).

Theorem 3.2

Assume that
{{\mathbf{u}}}\in{[H^{1}_{{{\mathbf{g}}}_{D}}(\Omega)]}^{3}\cap{[H^{2}(\Omega)]}^{3}
and

p\in{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\cap{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}
solve the
problem (
31
)–(
32
).
Further, assume that
({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h},p_{h})
are the solutions to the stabilized
problem (
36
).
Then there exists a constant
c_{3}
independent of the mesh size
h
and it holds:

\displaystyle{\left\lVert{{{\mathbf{u}}}-{{\mathbf{u}}}_{h}}\right\rVert}_{V}+{\left\lVert{p-p_{h}}\right\rVert}_{Q}\leq c_{3}h({\left\|{{{\mathbf{u}}}}\right\|_{{\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)}}}+{\left\|{p}\right\|_{{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}}})

(39)

Proof

Due to the similarity of the linear elasticity and the Stokes problem the proof
follows from
Bochev et al. 2006
, Theorem 4.1, Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2
.

3.3
Discretization with MINI-elements

3.3.1
Tetrahedral elements

One of the earliest strategies in constructing a stable finite element pairing
for discrete saddle-point problems arising from Stokes Equations is the
MINI-Element, dating back to the works of Brezzi et al, see for
example
Arnold et al. 1984
;
Brezzi et al. 1992
.
In the case of Stokes the velocity ansatz space is enriched by suitable
polynomial bubble functions.
More precisely, if we denote by
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
the space of polynomials
with degree
\leq 1
over the reference tetrahedron
\hat{K}
, we will choose

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{V}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{1}\oplus\{\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{B}}\},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{Y}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{1},

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle:=256\xi_{0}\xi_{1}\xi_{2}(1-\xi_{0}-\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}),

where
(\xi_{0},\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\in\hat{K}
, see also
Boffi et al. 2013
.

Classical results
Boffi et al. 2013
guarantee the stability of the
MINI-Element for tetrahedral meshes.
Due to compact support of the bubble functions, static condensation can be
applied to remove the interior degrees of freedom during assembly. A short review on the static condensation process is given in the Appendix. Hence, these degrees of freedom are not needed to be
considered in the full global stiffness matrix assembly which is a key
advantage of the MINI element.

3.3.2
Hexahedral meshes

In the literature mostly two dimensional quadrilateral tessellations,
see for example
Boffi et al. 2013
;
Bai 1997
;
Lamichhane 2017
, were considered for MINI element discretizations.
In this case, the proof of stability relies on the so-called

macro-element technique
proposed by
Stenberg 1990
.

To motivate our novel ansatz for hexahedral bubble functions,
we will first give an overview of Stenbergs main results.
A macro-element
M
is a connected set of elements in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Moreover, two macro-elements
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are said to be equivalent
if and only if they can be mapped continuously onto each other.
Additionally, for a macro element
M
we define the spaces

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}}&:=\left\{{{\mathbf{v}}}\in{[H^{1}_{0}(M)]}^{3}:{{\mathbf{v}}}=\hat{{{\mathbf{v}}}}\circ F_{\mathrm{K}}^{-1},\right.\\
&\left.\quad\quad\hat{v}\in{[\hat{\mathbb{V}}]}^{3},~K\subset M\right\},\\
P_{\mathrm{M}}&:=\left\{p\in L^{2}(M):p=\hat{p}\circ F_{\mathrm{K}}^{-1},\hat{p}\in\hat{\mathbb{Y}},~K\subset M\right\},\\
N_{\mathrm{M}}&:=\left\{p\in P_{\mathrm{M}}:\int\limits_{M}p\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}=0,\forall{{\mathbf{v}}}\in\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}}\right\}.\end{split}

(40)

Denote by
\Gamma_{h}
the set of all edges in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
interior
to
\Omega
. The macro-element partition
\mathcal{M}_{h}
of

\Omega
then consists of a (not necessarily disjoint) partitioning into
macro-elements
{\{M_{i}\}}_{i=1}^{M}
with

\overline{\Omega}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\overline{M}_{i}
.
The macro element technique is then described by the following theorem,
see
Stenberg 1990
.

Theorem 3.3

Suppose that there is a fixed set of equivalence classes

\mathcal{E}_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,q
, of macro-elements,
a positive integer
L
,
and a macro-element partition
\mathcal{M}_{h}
such that

(M1)

for each
M_{i}\in\mathcal{E}_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,q
,
the space
N_{\mathrm{M}}
is one-dimensional consisting of functions that
are constant on
M
;

(M2)

each
M\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
belongs to one of the classes

\mathcal{E}_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,q
;

(M3)

each
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
is contained in at least one and not
more than
L
macro-elements of
\mathcal{M}_{h}
;

(M4)

each
E\in\Gamma_{h}
is contained in the interior of at least
one and not more than
L
macro-elements of
\mathcal{M}_{h}
.

Then the discrete
\inf
-
\sup
-condition (
35
) holds.

Conditions (M2)–(M4) are valid for a quasi-uniform tessellation
of
\Omega
into hexahedral elements and, thus, it remains to show (M1).
To this end, we consider a macro-element
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{h}
consisting of
eight hexahedrons that share a common vertex
{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}\in\Omega
, see
Figure
1
.
A macro-element partitioning of this type fulfills conditions (M1)–(M3)
from Theorem
3.3
.
We will next show, that Assumption (M1) depends on the choice of the bubble
functions inside every
K\in M_{i}
.
For ease of presentation and with no loss of generality we will assume that

M_{i}
is a parallelepiped.
This means that the mapping
F_{\mathrm{M}_{i}}
from
\hat{K}
onto
M_{i}

is affine, so there exists an invertible matrix

\bm{J}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
such that

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{x}}}=F_{\mathrm{M}_{i}}(\bm{\xi})=\bm{J}_{i}\bm{\xi}+{{\mathbf{x}}}_{0},

where
\bm{\xi}\in\hat{K}={[-1,1]}^{3}
and
{{\mathbf{x}}}_{0}
is a given node
of
M_{i}
.
The case of
M_{i}
not being the image of an affine mapping of

\hat{K}
can be handled analogously, however, there are constraints on the
invertibility of
F_{\mathrm{M}_{i}}
, see
Knabner et al. 2003
.
Let
{\{\psi_{j}\}}_{j=1}^{8}
denote the standard trilinear basis functions on the
unit hexahedron. These functions will serve as a basis for
P_{\mathrm{M}_{i}}
.
For the space
\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}
we will chose one piecewise
continuous trilinear ansatz function defined in
{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
and for
each sub-hexahedron we will add two bubble functions as degrees of freedom.
The distribution of the degrees of freedom is depicted in
Figure
2
.
On
\hat{K}
we will define the following two bubble functions

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{B}}^{1}

\displaystyle:={(1-\xi_{0})}^{2}{(1-\xi_{1})}^{2}{(1-\xi_{2})}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{\alpha},

(41)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{\mathrm{B}}^{2}

\displaystyle:={(1-\xi_{0})}^{2}{(1-\xi_{1})}^{2}{(1-\xi_{2})}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{\beta},

(42)

where the indices
\alpha,\beta
are chosen such that
\hat{\psi}_{\alpha}

and
\hat{\psi}_{\beta}
are two ansatz functions belonging to two diagonally
opposite nodes.
Having this, we will form a basis for
\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}

by gluing together the images of the basis functions of each sub-hexahedron.
So we can write a basis for
\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}
as

\displaystyle V_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{span}\{\psi_{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}},\phi_{\mathrm{B},1}^{1},\phi_{\mathrm{B},2}^{1},\ldots,\phi_{\mathrm{B},1}^{8},\phi_{\mathrm{B},2}^{8}\},

(43)

\displaystyle\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}

\displaystyle:={[V_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}]}^{3}.

Here,
\psi_{{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}}
corresponds to a piecewise trilinear ansatz function
that has unit value in
{{\mathbf{x}}}_{i}
and zero in all other nodes of
M_{i}
.
Thus, we can calculate that
\dim(P_{\mathrm{M}_{i}})=27

and
\dim(\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}})=51
.
For ease of presentation we will rename the elements of (
43
)
as
{\{\phi_{i}\}}_{i=1}^{17}
.
Now, for
q_{h}\in P_{\mathrm{M}_{i}}
and

{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\in\bm{V}_{0,\mathrm{M}_{i}}
we can write

\displaystyle\int\limits_{M_{i}}q_{h}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{17}\sum_{l=1}^{27}\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{k}^{j}q_{l}\int\limits_{M_{i}}\operatorname{grad}_{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\phi_{k}[j]\psi_{l}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}.

Next, we use the chain rule to get

\operatorname{grad}_{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\phi_{k}=\bm{J}_{i}^{-\top}\hat{\operatorname{grad}}_{\bm{\xi}}

and a change of variables to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{17}\sum_{l=1}^{27}\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{k}^{j}q_{l}\int\limits_{M_{i}}\operatorname{grad}_{{{\mathbf{x}}}}\phi_{k}[j]\psi_{l}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}},

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{17}\sum_{l=1}^{27}\sum_{j=1}^{3}v_{k}^{j}q_{l}\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\bm{J}_{i}^{-\top}\hat{\operatorname{grad}}_{\bm{\xi}}\hat{\phi}_{k}[j]\hat{\psi}_{j}\lvert\det{\bm{J}_{i}}\rvert\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}.

This means we can find a matrix
\widetilde{\bm{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{27\times 51}
such that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{M_{i}}q_{h}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}={{\mathbf{q}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{D}}{{\mathbf{v}}},

where
{{\mathbf{q}}}
and
{{\mathbf{v}}}
encode the nodal values of
q_{h}
and
{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}
.
The following ordering will be employed for
{{\mathbf{v}}}

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{v}}}={\left(v_{1}^{1},v_{1}^{2},v_{1}^{3},\ldots,v_{17}^{1},v_{17}^{2},v_{17}^{3}\right)}^{\top}.

To proof (M1) we need to show that the rank of the matrix
\widetilde{\bm{D}}
is 26.
Due to the invertibility of
\bm{J}_{i}
the rank of the matrix
\widetilde{\bm{D}}
will remain unchanged by replacing
M_{i}
by
\hat{K}
.
Thus, it suffices to compute the rank of the matrix
\bm{D}

whose
j^{\mathrm{th}}
row is defined by

\left(\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\partial_{\xi_{1}}\phi_{1}\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\partial_{\xi_{2}}\phi_{1}\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\partial_{\xi_{3}}\phi_{1}\psi_{1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},\right.\\
\left.,\ldots,\right.\\
\left.\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\partial_{\xi_{1}}\phi_{17}\psi_{j}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\partial_{\xi_{2}}\phi_{17}\psi_{j}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi},\int\limits_{\hat{K}}\partial_{\xi_{3}}\phi_{17}\psi_{j}\mathrm{d}\bm{\xi}\right).

By this formula the matrix
\bm{D}
can be explicitly calculated, e.g., by
using software packages like
Mathematica
TM
and further
analyzed. We can conclude that the rank of
\bm{D}
is 26 and thus (M1)
holds and we can apply Theorem
3.3
.

3.4
Changes and limitations in the nonlinear case

One of the main differences between the linear and nonlinear case stems
from the definition of the pressure
p
as remarked in
Boffi & Stenberg 2017
.
Consider, as an example, the strain energy function for a nearly
incompressible neo-Hookean material where

\displaystyle\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}}):=\frac{\mu}{2}\left(\mathrm{tr}(\overline{\bm{C}})-3\right),

with
\mu>0
a material parameter.
Then,
\bm{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}
and
\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{tot}}
,
evaluated at
({{\mathbf{u}}}_{k},p_{k})=({{\mathbf{0}}},0)
,
are given by

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\bm{0},\quad\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{tot}}=2\mu\bm{I}\odot\bm{I}-\frac{2\mu}{3}\bm{I}\otimes\bm{I},

independent of the choice of
\Theta(J)
.
Assuming that
\Omega:=\Omega_{0}\approx\Omega_{t}
we obtain from
Eqs. (
20
)–(
21
) the following
linear system

\displaystyle 2\mu\int\limits_{\Omega}\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}}({{\mathbf{u}}}):\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}}({{\mathbf{v}}})\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}+\int\limits_{\Omega}p\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega}{{\mathbf{f}}}\cdot{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}},

(44)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{u}}}q\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\int\limits_{\Omega}pq\,\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=0,

(45)

where

\bm{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{d}}({{\mathbf{u}}}):=\bm{\varepsilon}({{\mathbf{u}}})-\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{div}({{\mathbf{u}}})\bm{I}
.
While the pressure in formulation (
31
)–(
32
)
is usually denoted as
Herrmann pressure

Herrmann 1965
,
above formulation (
44
)–(
45
)
uses the so-called
hydrostatic pressure
.

The arguments to prove the
inf-suf
condition for this linear problem
remains the same as for (
31
)–(
32
).
For the extension of the
inf-suf
condition to the nonlinear case we
already stated earlier in Eq. (
27
) that

\displaystyle b_{k}(q_{h},{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h})=\int\limits_{\Omega_{t,h}}q_{h}\Theta^{\prime}(J_{h})\operatorname{div}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}.

Here,
\Omega_{t,h}
is the approximation of the real current configuration

\Omega_{t}
. Our conjecture is that stability of the chosen elements is given
provided sufficient fine discretizations and volumetric functions

\Theta(J)
fulfilling
\Theta^{\prime}(J)\geq 1
.
However, we can not offer a rigorous proof of this, and rely on our numerical
results which showed no sign of numerical instabilities.

Concerning well-posedness of (
44
)–(
45
),
it was noted in
Boffi & Stenberg 2017
,
that the coercivity on the kernel condition (
25
)
does not hold in general, which makes the formulation with hydrostatic pressure
not well-posed in general.
However, it remains well-posed for strictly divergence-free finite elements or pure
Dirichlet boundary conditions.
This has also been observed by other authors, see
Khan et al. 2019
;
Viebahn et al. 2018
.
Even if the coercivity on the kernel condition can be shown for the hydrostatic,
nearly incompressible linear elastic case this result may not transfer
to the nonlinear case.
Here, this condition is highly dependent on the chosen nonlinear material law and
for the presented benchmark examples (Section
4
)
we did not observe any numerical instabilities.

For an in-depth discussion we refer the interested reader
to
Auricchio et al. 2010
;
Auricchio et al. 2005
.
A detailed discussion on Herrmann-type pressure in
the nonlinear case is presented in
Shariff 1997
;
Shariff & Parker 2000
.

To show well-posedness for the special case of the presented MINI element
discretizations
we rely on results given in
Boffi & Stenberg 2017
, Section 4
.
There it is shown, that discrete coercivity on the kernel holds,
provided that a rigid body mode is the only function that renders

\displaystyle a({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h},{{\mathbf{v}}}_{h}):=\int\limits_{\Omega_{h}}\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h}):\bm{\varepsilon}_{d}({{\mathbf{v}}}_{h})\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}

from (
44
)–(
45
) zero.
We could obtain this result following the same procedure outlined in

Boffi & Stenberg 2017
for both hexahedral and tetrahedral MINI elements.
A
Mathematica
TM
notebook
containing the computations discussed is available upon request.

In the case of the pressure-projection stabilization
we will modify Equation (
17
) using the stabilization
term (
38
)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h},p_{h};\Delta q_{h})

\displaystyle:=b_{\mathrm{vol}}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{h};\Delta q_{h})-c(p_{h},\Delta q_{h})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\mu^{*}}s_{h}(p_{h},q_{h}).

Here, the stabilization parameter
\mu^{*}>0
is supposed to be large enough and
will be specifically defined for each nonlinear material considered.
Note, that by modifying the definition of the lower residual,
we introduced a mesh dependent perturbation of the original residual.
An estimate of the consistency error caused by this is not readily
available and will be the topic of future research. However, results and
comparisons to benchmarks in Section
4
suggest that
this error is negligible for the considered problems as long as
\mu^{\ast}
is
well-chosen.

If not specified otherwise we chose

•

\mu^{\ast}=\mu
for neo-Hookean materials and

•

\mu^{\ast}=c_{1}
for Mooney–Rivlin materials

in the results section.
For the pressure-projection stabilized equal order pair we can not transfer
the results from the linear elastic case to the non-linear case,
as the proof of well-posedness relies on the coercivity of

a_{k}({{\mathbf{u}}},{{\mathbf{v}}})
which can not be concluded for this formulation.
However, no convergence issues occured in the numerical examples given
in Section
4
.

The considerable advantage of the MINI element is that there are no
modifications needed and that no additional stabilization parameters are
introduced into the system.

3.5
Changes and limitations in the transient case

The equations presented in Section
2
are not yet suitable
for transient simulations. To include this feature we modify the nonlinear
variational problem (
18
) in the following way:

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{upper}}^{\mathrm{trans}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle:=\rho_{0}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\ddot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}\cdot\Delta{{\mathbf{v}}}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}+R_{\mathrm{upper}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta v),

(46)

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{lower}}^{\mathrm{trans}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta q)

\displaystyle:=R_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{u}}},p;\Delta q).

(47)

For time discretization we considered a generalized-
\alpha
method,
see
Chung & Hulbert 1993
and also the Appendix for a short summary.
Due to the selected formulation, the resulting ODE system turns out to be of
degenerate hyperbolic type. Hence, we implemented a variant of the
generalized-
\alpha
method as proposed in
Kadapa et al. 2017
and using that we
did not observe any numerical issues in our simulations.
Note, that other groups have proposed a different treatment of the
incompressibility constraints in the case of transient problems,
see
Rossi et al. 2016
;
Scovazzi et al. 2016

4
Numerical examples

While benchmark cases presented in this section are fairly simple,
mechanical applications often require highly resolved meshes. Thus,
efficient and massively parallel solution algorithms for the
linearized system of equations become an important factor to deal with the
resulting computational load.
After discretization, at each Newton–Raphson step a block system of the form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}_{h}&\bm{B}_{h}^{\top}\\
\bm{B}_{h}&\bm{C}_{h}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}\\
\Delta{{\mathbf{p}}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{upper}}\\
-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{lower}}\end{pmatrix}

has to be solved.
In that regard, we used a generalized minimal residual method (GMRES)
and efficient preconditioning based
on the
PCFIELDSPLIT

5
Conclusion

In this study we described methodology for modeling nearly and fully
incompressible solid mechanics for a large variety of different scenarios.
A stable MINI element was presented which can serve as an
excellent choice for applied problems where the use of higher order
element types is not desired, e.g., due to fitting accuracy of the problem
domain.

We also proposed an easily implementable and computationally cheap technique based on a local
pressure projection. Both approaches can be applied to stationary as well as
transient problems without modifications and perform excellent with both
hexahedral and tetrahedral grids.

Both approaches allow a straightforward inclusion in combination with existing
finite element codes since all required implementations are purely on the element
level and are well-suited for simple single-core simulations as well as HPC computing.
Numerical results demonstrate the robustness of the formulations,
exhibiting a great accuracy for selected benchmark problems from the literature.

While the proposed projection method works well for relatively
stiff materials as considered in this paper,
the setting of the parameter
\mu^{\ast}
has to be adjusted for
soft materials such as biological tissues.
A further limitation is that both formulations render the need of solving a
block system, which is computationally more demanding and suitable
preconditioning is not trivial.
However, the MINI element approach can be used without further tweaking of
artificial stabilization coefficients and preliminary results suggested robustness,
even for very soft materials.
Consistent linearization as presented ensures that quadratic convergence of
the Newton–Raphson algorithm was achieved for all the problems considered.
Note that all computations for forming the tangent matrices and also the right
hand side residual vectors are kept local to each element.
This benefits scaling properties of parallel codes and also enables
seamless implementation in standard finite element software.

The excellent performance of the methods along with their high versatility
ensure that this framework serves as a solid platform for simulating
nearly and fully incompressible phenomena in stationary and transient solid
mechanics.
In future studies, we plan to extend the formulation to anisotropic materials
with stiff fibers as they appear for example in the simulation of cardiac
tissue and arterial walls.

where
\bm{M}_{h}
denotes the mass matrix;

\bm{C}_{h}
denotes an optional damping matrix;

\ddot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}(t)
denote the unknown nodal accelerations;

\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}(t)
denote the unknown nodal velocities;

{{\mathbf{u}}}(t)
denote the unknown nodal displacements; and

{{\mathbf{p}}}(t)
denote the unknown nodal pressure values.
We will use the modified generalized-
\alpha
method proposed in
Kadapa et al. 2017
.
To this end we introduce the auxiliary velocity
{{\mathbf{v}}}=\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}
.
Then, applying the standard generalized-
\alpha
integrator from
Chung & Hulbert 1993

we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{h}\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}-\bm{M}_{h}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle={{\mathbf{0}}},

(48)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}\bm{M}_{h}\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}+\bm{C}_{h}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}+\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{upper}}^{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle={{\mathbf{0}}},

(49)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{lower}}^{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle={{\mathbf{0}}},

(50)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{upper}}^{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{upper}}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1},{{\mathbf{p}}}_{n+1}),

\displaystyle+(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{upper}}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{n},{{\mathbf{p}}}_{n}),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{lower}}^{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1},{{\mathbf{p}}}_{n+1}),

\displaystyle+(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{lower}}({{\mathbf{d}}}_{n},{{\mathbf{p}}}_{n}),

and

\displaystyle\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n+1}+(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}})\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n},

(51)

\displaystyle\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n+1}+(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}})\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n},

(52)

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\ {{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+1}+(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{f}})\ {{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}.

(53)

Moreover, we employ Newmark’s approximations,
Newmark 1959
,

\displaystyle\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma\Delta t}\left({{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1}-{{\mathbf{u}}}_{n}\right)+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n},

(54)

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma\Delta t}\left({{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+1}-{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}\right)+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n}

(55)

Using (
48
) we observe

\displaystyle\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}={{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}

and combining this with (
49
)–(
55
) we conclude

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\gamma\Delta t}\left({{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1}-{{\mathbf{u}}}_{n}\right)+\frac{\gamma-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\gamma\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n}+\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}-1}{\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n},

\displaystyle\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\gamma^{2}\Delta t^{2}}\left({{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1}-{{\mathbf{u}}}_{n}\right)-\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\gamma\Delta t}{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n}

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma-\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}}{\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}\gamma^{2}\Delta t}\dot{{{\mathbf{u}}}}_{n}.

Thus, a dependence of
{{\mathbf{v}}}_{n+1}
and
\dot{{{\mathbf{v}}}}_{n+1}
on
{{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1}
can be established.
Having this the unknown values
{{\mathbf{u}}}_{n+1},{{\mathbf{p}}}_{n+1}
can be computed with the Newton–Raphson method.
Based on
Kadapa et al. 2017
we set the parameters depending only on

\rho_{\infty}\in[0,1)
by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{1+\rho_{\infty}},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{3-\rho_{\infty}}{2(1+\rho_{\infty})},

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}+\alpha_{\mathrm{m}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{f}}.

In all our simulations we used a value of
\rho_{\infty}=0.5
.

Remark on the implementation of the pressure-projection
stabilized equal order pair

Considering the bilinear form
s_{h}(p_{h},q_{h})
defined in (
38
) we can rewrite this with a simple calculation into

\displaystyle s_{h}(p_{h},q_{h}):=\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\mathrm{el}}}\left(\int\limits_{K_{l}}p_{h}q_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\frac{1}{\lvert\tau_{l}\rvert}\int\limits_{K_{l}}p_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}\int\limits_{K_{l}}q_{h}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}\right).

Denoting by
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
the chosen ansatz functions the element contribution for an arbitrary element
K
to the matrix
\bm{C}_{h}
is given by

\displaystyle\int\limits_{K}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}-\frac{1}{\lvert K\rvert}\int\limits_{K}\phi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}\int\limits_{K}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}{{\mathbf{x}}}.

This corresponds to an element mass matrix minus a rank-one correction.

Static condensation

For completeness we provide a summary for the static condensation used for
the MINI element.
Consider a finite element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
with a local ordering of the
unknowns
{{\mathbf{u}}}

{{\mathbf{u}}}=\left(u_{x}^{1},u_{y}^{1},u_{z}^{1},\ldots,u_{x}^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{N}},u_{y}^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{N}}},u_{z}^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{N}}},\right.\\
\left.u_{x,\mathrm{B}}^{1},u_{y,\mathrm{B}}^{1},u_{z,\mathrm{B}}^{1},\ldots,u_{x,\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{B}}},u_{y,\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{B}}},u_{z,\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{B}}}\right)

and
{{\mathbf{p}}}
as

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{p}}}=\left(p^{1},p^{2},\ldots,p^{\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{N}}}\right).

Here,
\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{N}}
corresponds to the nodal degrees of freedom
per element and
\mathrm{ndofs}_{\mathrm{B}}
to the bubble degrees of freedom
(one for tetrahedral elements and two for hexahedral elements).
Then the element contribution to the global saddle-point system can be written
as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{NN}}&\bm{K}_{\mathrm{NB}}&\bm{B}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\top}\\
\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BN}}&\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}&\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\top}\\
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{N}}&\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}&\bm{C}_{\mathrm{N}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\mathrm{N}}\\
\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\mathrm{B}}\\
\Delta{{\mathbf{p}}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{upper}}\\
-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{upper}}\\
-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{lower}}\end{pmatrix}.

The bubble part of the stiffness matrix,
\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}
is local to
the element and can be directly inverted. This gives the condensed system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{eff}}&\bm{B}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{T}\\
\bm{B}_{\mathrm{eff}}&\bm{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\mathrm{N}}\\
\Delta{{\mathbf{p}}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{upper}}\\
-\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{lower}}\end{pmatrix},

where the effective matrices and vectors are given as

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{K}_{\mathrm{NN}}-\bm{K}_{\mathrm{NB}}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}^{-1}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BN}}

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{B}_{\mathrm{N}}-\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}^{-1}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BN}},

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{C}_{\mathrm{N}}-\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{upper}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{upper}}-\bm{K}_{\mathrm{NB}}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{upper}},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{lower}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{N}}^{\mathrm{lower}}-\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{upper}}.

The effective matrices and vectors can then be assembled in a standard way into the global system.
The bubble update contributions can be calculated once
\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}_{N}
and
\Delta{{\mathbf{p}}}_{N}
are know as

\displaystyle\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}_{B}=-\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BB}}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{upper}}+\bm{K}_{\mathrm{BN}}\Delta{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\mathrm{N}}+\bm{B}_{\mathrm{B}}^{\top}\Delta{{\mathbf{p}}}_{\mathrm{N}}\right).

Tensor calculus

We use the following results from tensor calculus,
for more details we refer to,
e.g.,
Holzapfel 2000
;
Wriggers 2008
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{\bm{C}}}{\partial\bm{C}}

\displaystyle=J^{-\frac{2}{3}}\mathbb{P}=J^{-\frac{2}{3}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{C}^{-1}\otimes\bm{C}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{C}^{-1}}{\partial\bm{C}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{C}^{-1}\odot\bm{C}^{-1},

\displaystyle{(\bm{A}\odot\bm{A})}_{ijkl}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(A_{ik}A_{jl}+A_{il}A_{jk}\right).

For symmetric
\bm{A}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}:\bm{A}=\mathrm{Dev}(\bm{A})=\bm{A}-\frac{1}{3}(\bm{A}:\bm{C})\bm{C}^{-1}.

The isochoric part of the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor as well as the
isochoric part of the fourth order elasticity tensor are given as

\displaystyle\bm{S}_{\mathrm{isc}}

\displaystyle:=2\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}})}{\partial\bm{C}}=J^{-\frac{2}{3}}\mathrm{Dev}(\overline{\bm{S}}),

(56)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{S}}

\displaystyle:=2\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}})}{\partial\overline{\bm{C}}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{\mathrm{isc}}

\displaystyle:=4\frac{\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}})}{\partial\bm{C}\partial\bm{C}}

(57)

\displaystyle=J^{-\frac{4}{3}}\mathbb{P}\overline{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{\top}+J^{-\frac{2}{3}}\frac{2}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\bm{C}\overline{\bm{S}})\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{3}\bm{S}_{\mathrm{isc}}\overset{\mathrm{S}}{\otimes}\bm{C}^{-1},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle:=4\frac{\partial\overline{\Psi}(\overline{\bm{C}})}{\partial\overline{\bm{C}}\partial\overline{\bm{C}}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{C}^{-1}\odot\bm{C}^{-1}-\frac{1}{3}\bm{C}^{-1}\otimes\bm{C}^{-1},

\displaystyle\bm{A}\overset{\mathrm{S}}{\otimes}\bm{B}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{A}\otimes\bm{B}+\bm{B}\otimes\bm{A}\right).
