Remark 1.8

(Physically realistic potentials)
.

The mildly repulsive regime
\alpha>\beta\geq 2
which we address may be unphysical in several respects: the potentials

grow rapidly as

(meaning long range forces increase without bound), yet remain bounded at

, which permits a positive fraction of the particles to condense on the same point.
These may or may not be desirable features, depending on what one is trying to model.
It is perhaps worth pointing out the global energy minimizers

we identify for these potentials will remain

-local minimizers (see
[
17
]
and (
4.1
))
for any other potential

which agrees with

in a neighourhood of

and of
|x|=1
when
\alpha>\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)
(or of

more generally). This includes potentials which need not be spherically symmetric, nor grow at infinity. On the other hand, our techniques say nothing obvious about potentials with singularities at the origin that prevent condensation onto points, such as
W_{\alpha,\beta}

with
\beta<2
or bond order potentials more generally.

Finally, taking the limit
\beta\to\alpha
for the rescaled potential

(which has minimum value
-1
), leads us to introduce the following new class of interaction kernels,

. This family continuously extends of the two-parameter family of rescaled potentials

to the portion of the boundary of the mildly repulsive regime which lies on the diagonal line

n\times n
matrix given by

(2.1)

I(\mu)=\int x\otimes x\,d\mu(x)=\bigg(\int x_{i}x_{j}d\mu(x)\bigg)_{i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}.

Lemma 2.2

(Moment criteria for strict convexity)
.

For any
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in{\cal P}_{0}({\mathbf{R}}^{n})
having finite fourth moments, set
a(t):=\mathcal{E}_{W_{4}}(\mu_{t})
where
\mu_{t}:=(1-t)\mu_{0}+t\mu_{1}
.
Then
a(t)
is convex, and depends affinely on
t\in[0,1]
if and only if
I(\mu_{0})=I(\mu_{1})
.

Proof.

Fix
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in{\cal P}_{0}({\mathbf{R}}^{n})
with fourth moments. Since
\mathcal{E}_{W_{4}}(\mu)
is a quadratic function of
\mu
, we see
a^{\prime\prime}(t)=2\mathcal{E}_{W_{4}}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})
.
Thus convexity and affinity of
a(t)
on
t\in[0,1]
depend on the sign of

8\mathcal{E}_{W_{4}}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{4}d(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})(x)d(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})(y).

Vanishing of the zeroth and first moments of
\eta:=\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}

allows us to express

{\cal E}_{W_{4}}(\eta)

as the following sum of squares involving the second moment tensors
I(\eta):=I(\mu_{0})-I(\mu_{1})

from (
2.1
)

\displaystyle 8\mathcal{E}_{W_{4}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}[4(x\cdot y)^{2}+2|x|^{2}|y|^{2}]d\eta(x)d\eta(y)

\displaystyle=4\mathop{\rm Tr}(I(\eta)^{2})+2(\mathop{\rm Tr}I(\eta))^{2}.

Thus
{\cal E}_{W_{4}}(\eta)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
I(\mu_{0})=I(\mu_{1})
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Second moments for measures on centered spheres)
.

Let
{\bf S}_{r}
be the centered sphere of radius r in
{\mathbf{R}}^{n}
, and let
\mu\in{\cal P}({\bf S}_{r})
. If
I(\mu)=\lambda{\rm Id}
for some
\lambda>0
, then
I(\mu)=I(\sigma_{r})
where
\sigma_{r}
is the uniform probability on
{\bf S}_{r}
.

Proof.

If
I(\mu)=\lambda{\rm Id}
, any rotation
R\mu
of
\mu
has the same second moment tensor
I(R\mu)=I(\mu)
.
Now if we uniformize
\mu
by averaging over its rotations, the resulting measure
\sigma_{r}
will have the same second moment tensor as
\mu
due to the linearity of
I
.
∎

It is plausible that the following lemma is known, but lacking a reference we provide a proof for the sake of clarity and completeness.

Lemma 2.4

(Minimizing moments under moment constraints)
.

Let
0<p<q<\infty
,
C>0
and
\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}({\mathbf{R}}^{n})
. Then

\mu_{0}\in\argmin\bigg\{\int|x|^{q}d\mu(x)\ \bigg|\ \mu\in{\cal P}({\mathbf{R}}^{n}),\ \int|x|^{p}d\mu(x)=C\bigg\}

if and only if
\mu_{0}
is concentrated on the centered sphere of radius
C^{1/p}
.

Proof.

Let
m(x)=|x|
be the modulus map for
x\in{\mathbf{R}}^{n}
, and let
\eta:=m_{\#}(\mu)\in{\cal P}({[0,\infty)})
be the push-forward of
\mu\in{\cal P}({\mathbf{R}}^{n})
by the map
m
. Then
\int_{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}|x|^{p}d\mu(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{p}d\eta(r)
for any
p>0
. Hence from now on we assume
\eta\in{\cal P}({[0,\infty)})
and
\int r^{p}d\eta(r)=C
. Recall Jensen’s inequality, which states that
if
f:{\mathbf{R}}\to{\mathbf{R}}
is convex and
X
is a real-valued random variable
with average value
E[X]
, then
E[f(X)]\geq f(E[X])
, and equality holds if and only if
f
is linear on the interval
[\inf X,\sup X]
.
With
f(r)=r^{q/p}
, Jensen’s inequality yields
\int r^{q}d\eta(x)\geq\bigg(\int r^{p}d\eta(x)\bigg)^{q/p}=C^{q/p}
, and moreover equality holds if and only if
\eta
is supported at a point in
{[0,\infty)}
, since
f
is strictly convex on
{[0,\infty)}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Remark 2.6

(Second moments for the uniform measure on the vertices of a regular simplex)
.

Let
\nu_{d}\in{\cal P}_{0}({\mathbf{R}}^{n})
denote the uniform measure on the
n+1
vertices of a regular simplex with center of mass at the origin and diameter

I(\nu_{d})=\frac{d^{2}}{2n+2}\mathop{\rm Id}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathds{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in{\mathbf{R}}^{n+1}

. Its vertices coincide with the standard basis vectors

for
{\mathbf{R}}^{n+1}

We compute the second moments

of the uniform measure

over these vertices, and its translation
T_{\lambda}\nu=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\delta_{e_{i}-\lambda\mathds{1}}

along the principal diagonal
\mathds{1}
for each

\displaystyle I_{jk}(T_{\lambda}\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}(e_{i}-\lambda\mathds{1})_{j}(e_{i}-\lambda\mathds{1})_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}({\mathop{\rm Id}}_{jk}-2\lambda+(n+1)\lambda^{2}),

Note that the choice

makes

for any orthonormal basis

For each

Direct computation yields

From this, the lemma follows from the fact that the function

for

4.

In this section, we establish the existence of a transition threshold

4.1.
Threshold upper bound independent of dimension
n\geq 2

We now establish an upper bound
\alpha_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
for the threshold
\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)
.
Note that this upper bound and the quantities
\beta_{\infty}^{*}
and
f_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
defining it become independent of dimension as soon as
n\geq 2
.
The asterisk on these quantities reminds us of their implicit dependence on
\min\{n,2\}
, however.

Definition 4.1

(Threshold upper bound)
.

Set

\beta_{\infty}^{*}:=\frac{4^{*}-2}{\log(4^{*}/2)}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\log(3/2)}&\ {\rm if}\ n=1,\\
\frac{2}{\log 2}&\ {\rm if}\ n\geq 2.\end{cases}

For
\beta\geq 2
,
define
\alpha_{\infty}^{*}=\alpha_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
as the largest solution of

(4.4)

\frac{e^{\alpha/\beta_{\infty}^{*}}}{\alpha}=\frac{e^{\beta/\beta_{\infty}^{*}}}{\beta}.

Remark 4.2

(Number of solutions)
.

For any given
\beta\geq 2
and
n\in\{1,2\}
, there are at most two solutions to equation (
4.4
), which follows from the fact that
f_{\infty}^{*}(t):=1-\frac{e^{t/\beta_{\infty}^{*}}}{t}
is unimodal on
(0,\infty),

i.e. has a unique global maximum and no local minima.
In particular, we see

t^{2}\beta_{\infty}^{*}e^{-t/\beta_{\infty}^{*}}\frac{df_{\infty}^{*}}{dt}=t-\beta_{\infty}^{*}

is positive on
(0,\beta_{\infty}^{*}),
zero at
\beta_{\infty}^{*}
, and negative on
(\beta_{\infty}^{*},\infty)
.
Thus
\alpha_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)=\beta
if and only if
\beta\geq\beta_{\infty}^{*}
.

Remark 4.3

(Alternative interpretation)
.

Set

\bar{w}_{\beta,\beta}(r):=\lim_{\alpha\to\beta}\bar{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(r)=r^{\beta}(\beta\log r-1),

and let
z_{\alpha,\beta}

denote the positive zero of
\bar{w}_{\alpha,\beta}
, where
z_{\alpha,\beta}=(\frac{\alpha}{\beta})^{\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta}}
for
\alpha\neq\beta
and
z_{\beta,\beta}:=e^{1/\beta}
. Notice that
z_{4^{*},2}=\frac{3}{2},
if
n=1
, and
z_{4^{*},2}=\sqrt{2},
if
n\geq 2.
Hence, after some rearranging, we obtain
\beta_{\infty}^{*}
from the equation
z_{\beta_{\infty}^{*},\beta_{\infty}^{*}}=z_{4^{*},2}
and
\alpha_{\infty}^{*}
as the largest solution of
z_{\alpha,\beta}=z_{4^{*},2},
or rather
w_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{4^{*},2})=0
.

The following lemma and corollary demonstrate that
\alpha_{\infty}^{*}
is indeed an upper bound for the threshold function:

Lemma 4.4

(Comparing pair potentials)
.

Let
2<\beta<\alpha<4^{*}
. Then

\bar{w}_{4^{*},2}(r)\leq\bar{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(r)
for all
r\in[0,z_{\alpha,\beta}]
if and only if
z_{\alpha,\beta}\leq z_{4^{*},2}
.

Proof.

One direction is trivial, as if
\bar{w}_{4^{*},2}(r)\leq\bar{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(r)
for all
r\in[0,z_{\alpha,\beta}]
, then in particular
\bar{w}_{4^{*},2}(z_{\alpha,\beta})\leq\bar{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(z_{\alpha,\beta})=0,
hence
z_{\alpha,\beta}\leq z_{4^{*},2}
. For the proof of the other direction, we begin by defining

g(r):=\bar{w}_{4^{*},2}(r)-\bar{w}_{\alpha,\beta}(r)=\frac{2r^{4^{*}}-4^{*}r^{2}}{4^{*}-2}-\frac{\beta r^{\alpha}-\alpha r^{\beta}}{\alpha-\beta}.

We may divine the behaviour of
g
from its fifth derivative

g^{(5)}(r)=\frac{\alpha\beta r^{\beta-5}}{\alpha-\beta}\left[-r^{\alpha-\beta}\prod_{i=1}^{4}(\alpha-i)+\prod_{i=1}^{4}(\beta-i)\right]

for
r\in(0,\infty).

Written in this form, we see that
g^{(5)}(r)
is the product of a positive function of
r
and a monotone function of
r
, and hence has at most one sign change. More precisely,
g^{(3)}(r)
is either convex-concave (if
\alpha<3
), concave-convex (if
\beta>3
), or strictly convex (if
\beta\leq 3\leq\alpha\neq\beta
) on
(0,\infty).
Moreover, we may write

g^{(3)}(r)=2\cdot 4^{*}(4^{*}-1)r^{4^{*}-3}+\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha-\beta}\left[-(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)r^{\alpha-3}+(\beta-1)(\beta-2)r^{\beta-3}\right].

Here, both the highest order term
r^{4^{*}-3}
and the lowest order term
r^{\beta-3}
have positive coefficients, which implies that
g^{(3)}
is positive outside a compact subinterval of
(0,\infty)
. This, combined with the convex/concave structure of
g^{(3)}
, implies that
g^{(3)}
can have at most two zeros on
(0,\infty)
and, in particular, may change signs at most twice — from positive to negative to positive.

This implies either
g^{\prime}
is convex-concave-convex on
(0,\infty)
or just convex. We may assume
g^{\prime}
is convex-concave-convex as, if it is simply convex, an easier argument than what follows will yield the desired conclusion. Notice that

g^{\prime}(r)=\frac{4^{*}\cdot 2}{4^{*}-2}(r^{4^{*}-1}-r)-\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha-\beta}(r^{\alpha-1}-r^{\beta-1})

is negative near zero and hence, the convex-concave-convexity implies
g^{\prime}
changes sign at most thrice on
(0,\infty).
Note
g^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime}(1)=0=g(0)=g(1)
. Since
g^{\prime}
is negative near zero, we see that
g^{\prime}
must change from negative to positive somewhere in
(0,1)
, implying the existence of a zero of
g^{\prime}
on this interval. Hence
g^{\prime}
has at most one zero on
(1,\infty)
. But if there is no zero on
(1,\infty)
, then the shape of
g^{\prime}
and
g(1)=g^{\prime}(1)=0
implies
g^{\prime}>0
hence
g>0
on
(1,\infty)
, yielding
z_{\alpha,\beta}>z_{4^{*},2}
, a contradiction. Hence we deduce that, on
(1,\infty)
,
g^{\prime}
changes sign from negative to positive. With
g(1)=0
, this implies
g
also changes sign from negative to positive on
(1,\infty)
. Now since the condition
z_{\alpha,\beta}\leq z_{4^{*},2}
clearly implies
g(z_{\alpha,\beta})\leq 0
, this allows us to conclude that
g\leq 0
on
[1,z_{\alpha,\beta}].

It remains to show
g\leq 0
on
[0,1]
. Assume
g
is positive somewhere in
(0,1)
. Then
g^{\prime}
would have to change signs (at least) twice on the interval
(0,1)
, from negative to positive to negative. With
g^{\prime}(1)=0
all three zeros of
g^{\prime}
are in
(0,1]
, thus no zero on
(1,\infty)
, contradiction as before. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Threshold lower bound for each dimension

We now derive a dimension dependent lower bound
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}
for
\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}
from the Euler-Lagrange equation (
3.3
) for minimizers.

Definition 4.6

(Threshold lower bound)
.

Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{\Delta^{n}}
. For each
\beta\geq 2
, define
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)\in[\beta,\infty)
to be

(4.5)

\displaystyle\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{\alpha>\beta\ |\ \mathop{\rm spt}\nu\subseteq\argmin_{{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}}(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{\alpha\in{\mathbf{R}}\ |\ \mathop{\rm spt}\nu\not\subseteq\argmin_{{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}}(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)\}.

Proposition 4.7

(Threshold lower bound)
.

Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{\Delta^{n}}
.
If
\alpha>\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)
for some
\beta\geq 2
, then
\mathop{\rm spt}\nu=\argmin_{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)
.
In particular,

(4.6)

\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)=\inf\{\alpha>\beta\ |\ \mathop{\rm spt}\nu=\argmin_{{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}}(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)\},

and
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}\leq\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}
.

Proof.

For any
\alpha>\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)
, notice Lemma
3.1
yields
\mathop{\rm spt}\nu=\argmin_{{{\mathbf{R}}^{n}}}(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)
, which gives (
4.6
). The fact that

\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}\leq\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}
is a direct consequence of the Euler-Lagrange equation satisfied by a minimizer: i.e. if
\alpha\geq\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}
, so that
\nu\in{\cal P}_{\Delta^{n}}
minimizes
{\cal E}_{W_{\alpha,\beta}}
, then
\nu
satisfies (
3.3
) hence
\alpha\geq\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}
.
∎

Although the value of
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)
is not very explicit, it is possible to estimate it explicitly from below by evaluating the potential
W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu

at points chosen judiciously to expose potential violations of the Euler-Lagrange equation.
The resulting estimates
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}\leq\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}
provide weaker but explicit lower bounds for the threshold.
This requires the following family of functions and their unimodality:

Definition 4.8

(A family of unimodal functions)
.

Define
f_{n}:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbf{R}}
by

(4.7)

f_{n}(t):=\begin{cases}\frac{2^{-1}-2^{-t}}{t}&\text{ if }n=1\\
\frac{n-(\frac{2n}{n+1})^{t/2}-n(\frac{n-1}{n+1})^{t/2}}{t}&\text{ if }n\geq 2.\end{cases}

Using this family of functions, we define a new family of lower bounds:

Definition 4.9

(A weaker threshold lower bound)
.

For
\beta\geq 2,
define
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)
by

(4.8)

\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)=\max\{\alpha\geq 2\ |\ f_{n}(\alpha)=f_{n}(\beta)\}.

In particular, the set over which we take the maximum in the previous definition has at most two elements, as the following lemma shows:

Lemma 4.10

(Unimodality of
f_{n}
)
.

For any
n\geq 1
, the function
f_{n}(t)
is unimodal on
t\in(0,\infty)
.
Indeed,
f_{n}
admits a unique global maximum
\underline{\beta}_{n}:=\argmax_{t>0}f_{n}(t)
and
no other critical points.

Proof.

We first treat the case
n=1
separately. Here, notice that
f_{1}^{\prime}(t)
has the same sign as
g_{1}(t):=t^{2}f_{1}^{\prime}(t)=(t\log 2+1)2^{-t}-2^{-1}
.
Since
g_{1}^{\prime}(t)=-t2^{-t}\log^{2}2
is always negative, and since
g_{1}(0)=\frac{1}{2}
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}g_{1}(t)=-\frac{1}{2},
we conclude that
f_{1}^{\prime}
switches sign from positive to negative at its unique zero in
(0,\infty),
and has no other sign changes. We denote the unique zero of
f_{1}^{\prime}
by
\underline{\beta}_{1}.

The
n\geq 2
case proceeds in a similar manner. Here, we notice that

\displaystyle g_{n}(t):=t^{2}f_{n}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{t}{2}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}\right)^{t/2}\log\frac{2n}{n+1}+n\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)^{t/2}\log\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right]

\displaystyle-n+\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}\right)^{t/2}+n\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)^{t/2},

and compute

g_{n}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{t}{4}\left[\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}\right)^{t/2}\log^{2}\frac{2n}{n+1}+n\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)^{t/2}\log^{2}\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right].

Since
g_{n}^{\prime}(t)
is negative everywhere,
g_{n}(0)=1,
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}g_{n}(t)=-\infty
, we may apply an identical argument to the one employed in the
n=1
case to show the existence of
\underline{\beta}_{n}
with all desired properties.
∎

Remark 4.11

(Diagonal intersects bound)
.

Notice
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)>\beta
if and only if
\beta<\underline{\beta}_{n}
. That is, the graph of
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}
intersects the line
\alpha=\beta
at the point
(\underline{\beta}_{n},\underline{\beta}_{n})
.

Proposition 4.12

(Estimating threshold lower bound)
.

For
\beta\geq 2
, the thresholds of Definitions
4.9
,
4.6
and
Theorem
1.5

satisfy

\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)\leq\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)\leq\alpha_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)
.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
4.7
we need only show
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)\leq\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}^{+}(\beta)
.
We proceed by relating the defining equations for
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}
to the Euler-Lagrange equation for a unit simplex
\nu\in{\cal P}_{\Delta^{n}}
. As in the introduction, we denote the vertices of the unit
n
-simplex by
\{x_{0},....,x_{n}\}.
We divide the proof into two cases,
n=1
and
n\geq 2
. Notice that, in either case, the inequality is trivial for any

for which
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)=\beta
, so we are free to assume that
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)>\beta
.

If

notice that the Euler-Lagrange equation requires that

(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)(x_{0})\leq(W_{\alpha,\beta}*\nu)(0).

More explicitly, as

this inequality reads

\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\beta}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\alpha 2^{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{\beta 2^{\beta}},

or,

(4.9)

f_{1}(\alpha)=\frac{2^{-1}-2^{-\alpha}}{\alpha}\leq\frac{2^{-1}-2^{-\beta}}{\beta}=f_{1}(\beta).

By definition,
\alpha=\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{1}}(\beta)

saturates this inequality. Our assumption

Our proof proceeds analogously for

or equivalently,

Since

is still unimodal for

, the remainder of the proof proceeds in an identical manner to the proof for

following (

), hence is omitted.
∎

Proposition 4.13

(Bounds converge in the high dimensional limit)
.

For all
\beta\geq 2
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)=\alpha_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)\qquad(n\neq 1).

Proof.

For
\beta\geq 2
, observe that the unimodal functions
f_{n}(\beta)
of Lemma
4.10
converge to the unimodal limit
f_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
of Remark
4.2
:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(\beta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n-\left(\frac{2n}{n+1}\right)^{\beta/2}-n\left(\frac{n-1}{n+1}\right)^{\beta/2}}{\beta}=1-\frac{2^{\beta/2}}{\beta}=f_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)\qquad(n\neq 1).

Since
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)
and
\alpha_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
are defined as the largest
\alpha
satisfying
f_{n}(\alpha)=f_{n}(\beta)
and
f_{\infty}^{*}(\alpha)=f_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
respectively,
it follows that
\underline{\alpha}_{\Delta^{n}}(\beta)\to\alpha_{\infty}^{*}(\beta)
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 4.14
