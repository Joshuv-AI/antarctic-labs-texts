Bruhat intervals, subword complexes and brick polyhedra for finite Coxeter groups Thanks: The authors are supported by the DFG Heisenberg grant STU 563/4-1 “Noncrossing phenomena in Algebra and Geometry”.
Dennis Jahn, Christian Stump

Abstract
. We study the interplay between the discrete geometry of Bruhat poset intervals and subword complexes of finite Coxeter systems. We establish connections between the cones generated by cover labels for Bruhat intervals and of root configurations for subword complexes, culminating in the notion of brick polyhedra for general subword complexes.



1.
Introduction

This paper develops the relationship between Bruhat intervals in finite Coxeter groups and subword complexes by describing the interplay between Bruhat interval cones and root configurations.
Based on this newly developed relationship, properties of brick polytopes for spherical root-independent subword complexes are extended towards general subword complexes by introducing
brick polyhedra
.

Bruhat interval cones were introduced and studied by Dyer in the study of positivity properties of Kazhdan-Lusztig and Stanley polynomials
[
Dye94
]
.
Subword complexes are simplicial complexes that have been introduced by Knutson and Miller in the context of Gröbner geometry of Schubert varieties
[
KM04
,
KM05
]
.
Based on the notion of root configurations from
[
CLS14
]
, this paper reconsiders the combinatorial and discrete-geometric understanding of subword complexes by closely tightening them to Dyer’s Bruhat interval cones.

Brick polytopes for spherical root-independent subword complexes were introduced and studied by Pilaud and Stump
[
PS15
]
.
These generalized brick polytopes for sorting networks by Pilaud and Santos
[
PS12
]
.
As shown by Ceballos, Labbé and Stump in
[
CLS14
]
, cluster complexes of finite type cluster algebras can be realized as spherical root-independent subword complexes and one main motivation for studying brick polytopes in
[
PS15
]
was to show that generalized associahedra for cluster algebras previously constructed by Chapoton, Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
CFZ02
]
and by Hohlweg, Lange and Thomas
[
HLT11
]
can be realized as brick polytopes.

Motivated by the conjecture that important properties of brick polytopes for spherical root-independent subword complexes hold for all spherical subword complexes
[
PS15
, Conjecture 7.1]
, this paper introduces and studies brick polyhedra for general (spherical and non-spherical) subword complexes of finite type.
It turns out that subtle properties of Bruhat interval cones are at the core of their understanding.

Subword complexes have a canonical recursive decomposition into smaller subword complexes.
Previous considerations in
[
CLS14
,
PS15
]
were mainly developed for spherical subword complexes which are not closed und this recursive structure.
The presented constructions still rely on elementary properties of the two fundamental notions
root
and
weight functions
for subword complexes.
Otherwise, all constructions are based on newly developed properties of Bruhat interval cones that are then applied to general subword complexes.

This newly presented approach allows inductive arguments using the recursive structure as done in the proof of
Proposition

3.14
.
This is then used to deduce the uniqueness properties of certain facets constructed in
3.16
that in turn are the central ingredient in the structural understanding of the construction of brick polyhedra.

2.
Background on Coxeter systems and subword complexes

This section recalls standard notions for finite Coxeter systems.
These are mostly following the notions from
[
CLS14
,
PS15
]
.
We also refer to
[
BB05
]
for further background material.
Ongoing examples are collected in
Section

2.5
.

Let
{(W,\mathcal{S})}
be a finite type Coxeter system of rank
n=|\mathcal{S}|
acting on a Euclidean vector space
V\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
with inner form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Let
\Delta\subseteq\Phi^{+}\subseteq\Phi\subseteq V
be a root system for
{(W,\mathcal{S})}
with simple roots
\Delta=\left\{\alpha_{s}\;|\;s\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
, positive roots
\Phi^{+}
, and negative roots
\Phi^{-}=-\Phi^{+}
.
For
\beta\in\Phi
we write
|\beta|\in\Phi^{+}
for the positive root in
\{\pm\beta\}
.
The reflections in
W
are
\mathcal{R}=\left\{s_{\beta}\;|\;\beta\in\Phi^{+}\right\}
where
s_{\beta}
denotes the reflection sending
\beta
to its negative while fixing pointwise its orthogonal complement
\beta^{\perp}=\left\{v\in V\;|\;\langle\beta,v\rangle=0\right\}
.

The corresponding Cartan matrix
(a_{st})_{s,t\in\mathcal{S}}
is given by
s(\alpha_{t})=\alpha_{t}-a_{st}\alpha_{s}
.
The fundamental weights
\nabla=\left\{\omega_{s}\;|\;s\in\mathcal{S}\right\}\subseteq V
are then
\alpha_{s}=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}a_{ts}\omega_{t}
and
W
acts on the fundamental weights by
s(\omega_{t})=\omega_{t}-\delta_{s=t}\alpha_{s}
for
s,t\in\mathcal{S}
.

2.1.
Reduced words and weak order

The
length

\ell(w)
of an element
w\in W
is the smallest length of a word
{\rm s}_{1}\dots{\rm s}_{\ell(w)}
such that its product is an expression
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{\ell(w)}
for
w
.
Words (and also expressions) of smallest length are called
reduced
.
Here and below, we mildly distinguish between words in
\mathcal{S}
and the corresponding expressions for elements in
W
by writing
{\rm s}_{1}\dots{\rm s}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
for the word and
s_{1}\cdots s_{\ell}\in W
for the expression.
It is well-known that for
w\in W,s\in\mathcal{S}
we have

(2.1)

\ell(ws)=\begin{cases}\ell(w)+1&\text{ if }w(\alpha_{s})\in\Phi^{+}\\
\ell(w)-1&\text{ if }w(\alpha_{s})\in\Phi^{-}\end{cases}\ .

This in particular means that a word
{\rm s}_{1}\dots{\rm s}_{\ell}
is reduced if and only if for all
1<i\leq\ell
we have
s_{1}\cdots s_{i-1}(\alpha_{s_{i}})\in\Phi^{+}
.
The
(right) weak order

\leq_{\mathsf{R}}
on
W
is the partial order defined by the cover relations
w\prec_{\mathsf{R}}ws
for
w\in W,s\in\mathcal{S}
with
\ell(ws)=\ell(w)+1
.
The
inversion set
of an element
w\in W
with reduced expression
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{\ell}
is given by

(2.2)

\operatorname{Inv}(w)=\left\{s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}(\alpha_{s_{k}})\;\big|\;1\leq k\leq\ell\right\}.

This is independent of the chosen reduced word and it is not hard to see that
\operatorname{Inv}(w)=\Phi^{+}\,\cap\,w(\Phi^{-})
.
In particular,
\ell(w)=|\operatorname{Inv}(w)|
and
x\leq_{\mathsf{R}}y\Leftrightarrow\operatorname{Inv}(x)\subseteq\operatorname{Inv}(y)
.
The
longest element

{w_{\circ}}\in W
is the unique element of longest length
|\Phi^{+}|
which is equivalently described as the unique element with
\operatorname{Inv}({w_{\circ}})=\Phi^{+}
.
For any
w\in W
, we have a decomposition

(2.3)

\Phi^{+}=\operatorname{Inv}(w)\sqcup\operatorname{Inv}(w\cdot{w_{\circ}}),

and a subset
X\subseteq\Phi^{+}
is the inversion set of an element if and only if
X
and
\Phi^{+}\setminus X
are separated by a hyperplane,
i.e.
, there is a linear functional
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
, we have

(2.4)

f(\beta)>0\Longleftrightarrow\beta\in X.

We record the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
BB05
, Lemma 1.4.4]
)
.

Let
w\in W
with reduced expression
w=s_{1}\cdots s_{\ell}
and let
s_{\beta}\in\mathcal{R}
.
Then the following properties are equivalent:

(a)

\ell(s_{\beta}w)<\ell(w)
,

(b)

s_{\beta}w=s_{1}\cdots\widehat{s}_{k}\cdots s_{\ell}
for some index
k
,

(c)

\beta=s_{1}\cdots s_{k-1}(\alpha_{s_{k}})
for some index
k
.

Moreover, the index
k
is unique in both cases.

Since
(c)
is the defining property for
\beta
to be an inversion of
w
, we obtain from this lemma that

(2.5)

\operatorname{Inv}(w)=\left\{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\;\big|\;\ell(s_{\beta}w)<\ell(w)\right\}.

2.2.
Bruhat order

The
Bruhat order

\leq
on
W
is the partial order defined by the cover relations
w\prec rw
for
w\in W,r\in\mathcal{R}
with
\ell(rw)=\ell(w)+1
.
Observe that
w\prec_{\mathsf{R}}ws\Rightarrow w\prec ws
because
ws=(wsw^{-1})w
for
wsw^{-1}\in\mathcal{R}
.
For the positive root
\beta\in\Phi^{+}
with
r=s_{\beta}
, we say that the cover relation
w\prec rw
is
labelled by

\beta
.
In particular, the cover
w\prec ws
is labelled by
w(\alpha_{s})
which is a positive root by (
2.1
).
A
Bruhat interval

[x,y]
is defined for
x\leq y
in Bruhat order by

[x,y]=\left\{z\in W\;\big|\;x\leq z\leq y\right\}.

The following properties of the Bruhat order are needed in subsequent sections.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
BB05
, Proposition 2.2.7]
)
.

Let
x,y\in W
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
such that
x\leq y
,
x\prec sx
and
sy\prec y
.
Then
x\leq sy
and
sx\leq y
.

This lemma immediately yields the following known lifting property for Bruhat intervals.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
x,y\in W
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
such that
x\leq y
,
x\prec sx
,
sy\prec y
and
sx\not\leq sy
.
Then
[x,sy]\cong[sx,y]
and the isomorphism is given by
w\mapsto sw
.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
BB05
, Corollary 2.2.8]
)
.

Let
w\in W
,
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
r\in\mathcal{R}
such that
s\neq r
and
w\prec sw,rw
.
Then
sw,rw\prec srw
.

Putting these properties together, we obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
x,y\in W
,
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
r\in\mathcal{R}
such that
s\neq r
and
x\prec sx,rx\leq y
.
If moreover
srx\not\leq y
then
y\prec sy
,
srx\leq sy
and
[rx,y]\cong[srx,sy]
.

Proof.

Lemma

2.4
yields
x\prec sx,rx\prec srx
.
Assuming
sy\prec y
and applying
Lemma

2.2
to
rx\prec srx
and to
sy\prec y
would imply
srx\leq y
, a contradiction.
Hence
y\prec sy
.
Again by
Lemma

2.2
applied to
rx\prec srx
and to
y\prec sy
we obtain
srx\leq sy
and by
Corollary

2.3
we get
[rx,y]\cong[srx,sy]
.
∎

For later reference, we further recollect the following property.

Lemma 2.6

(
[
BB05
, Lemma 2.2.10]
)
.

Let
x,y\in W
and
r\in\mathcal{R}
.
If both,
x\leq xr
and
y\leq ry
, then
xy\leq xry
.

2.3.
The Demazure product

For a (not necessarily reduced) word
{\rm Q}={\rm s}_{1}\dots{\rm s}_{m}
in
\mathcal{S}
and a subset
X\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
, we write
{\rm Q}_{X}
to be the subword of
{\rm Q}
of the letters
{\rm s}_{i}
with
i\in X
.
The
Demazure product

{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})\in W
may be defined in terms of the weak order recursively by

{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})=\begin{cases}{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q}_{\{1,\dots,m-1\}})\cdot s_{m}&\text{ if }{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q}_{\{1,\dots,m-1\}})(\alpha_{s_{m}})\in\Phi^{+}\\
{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q}_{\{1,\dots,m-1\}})&\text{ if }{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q}_{\{1,\dots,m-1\}})(\alpha_{s_{m}})\in\Phi^{-}\end{cases}\ ,

with initial condition
{\operatorname{Dem}}({\varepsilon})={e}\in W
for the empty word
{\varepsilon}
.
By (
2.1
), these conditions say that the Demazure product of
{\rm Q}
is obtained by scanning through
{\rm Q}
from left to right, starting at the identity and going upwards in weak order whenever possible.
One may equivalently define the Demazure product in terms of the Bruhat order as the unique maximal element in Bruhat order among all expressions obtained from the word
{\rm Q}={\rm s}_{1}\dots{\rm s}_{m}
by removing letters,

(2.6)

{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})=\max{}_{\leq}\left\{\Pi{{\rm Q}}_{X}\;\big|\;X\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}\right\}.

Here and below,
\Pi{{\rm Q}}_{X}\in W
denotes the product of the simple reflections
s_{i}
with
i\in X
in the given order.

2.4.
Subword complexes for Coxeter systems

For
w\in W
and
{\rm Q}={\rm s}_{1}\dots{\rm s}_{m}
, the
subword complex

\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
is the simplicial complex of sets of (positions of) letters in
{\rm Q}
whose complements contain reduced words for
w
.
Knutson and Miller introduced subword complexes in the context of Gröbner geometry of Schubert varieties
[
KM04
,
KM05
]
.
By (
2.6
),
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
is non-empty if and only if
w\leq{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})
.
Facets of
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
are subwords of
{\rm Q}
whose complements are reduced words for
w
.
As shown in
[
KM04
, Theorem 3.7, Corollary 3.8]
the non-empty complex
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
is a topological sphere if and only if
w={\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})
and a topological ball otherwise.
In these cases we call
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)

spherical
and
non-spherical
, respectively.

For two facets
I\neq J
of
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
we say
I
and
J
are
adjacent
if there are
i\in I
and
j\in J
such that
I\setminus\{i\}=J\setminus\{j\}
.
We call the transition from
I
to
J
the
flip
of
i
in
I
and furthermore say the flip is
increasing
if
i<j
and
decreasing
otherwise.
The
root function

{{\sf r}}(I,\cdot):[m]\rightarrow\Phi=W(\Delta)\subseteq V
is defined by

{{\sf r}}(I,k)=\Pi{{\rm Q}}_{\{1,\dots,k-1\}\setminus I}(\alpha_{s_{k}}).

As above,
\Pi{{\rm Q}}_{X}
denotes the product of the simple reflections
s_{i}
, for
i\in X
in the given order.
Observe here that the root
{{\sf r}}(I,k)
only depends on
I\cap\{1,\dots,k-1\}
and not on the complete facet
I
.
The ordered multiset
1
1

1

We think of facets of subword complexes as being
ordered sets
(sorted lists of indices written in set notation) and we think of root configurations of facets as
ordered multisets
(lists of roots written in multiset notation with ordering inherited from the order of the facet).

{{\sf R}}(I)=\left\{\!\!\left\{{{\sf r}}(I,i)\;|\;i\in I\right\}\!\!\right\}
is called
root configuration
of the facet
I
.

The following lemma recalls several properties of flips in subword complexes in terms of root configurations.
These can be found in
[
PS13
, Proposition 2]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
I
be a facet of the non-empty subword complex
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
.

(a)

The map
{{\sf r}}(I,\cdot):k\mapsto{{\sf r}}(I,k)
is a bijection between the complement of
I
and
\operatorname{Inv}(w)
.

(b)

For
i\in I
, there exists a facet
J
and an index
j\in J
such that
I\setminus\{i\}=J\setminus\{j\}
if and only if
|{{\sf r}}(I,i)|\in\operatorname{Inv}(w)
.
In this case, the index
j
is the unique position in the complement of
I
for which
{{\sf r}}(I,j)=|{{\sf r}}(I,i)|
and the sign is determined by

{{\sf r}}(I,j)=\begin{cases}{{\sf r}}(I,i)&\text{if }i<j,\\
-{{\sf r}}(I,i)&\text{if }i>j.\end{cases}

(c)

In the situation of
(b)
, the map
{{\sf r}}(J,\cdot)
is obtained from
{{\sf r}}(I,\cdot)
by

{{\sf r}}(J,k)=\begin{cases}s_{{{\sf r}}(I,i)}\big({{\sf r}}(I,k)\big)&\text{if }\min(i,j)<k\leq\max(i,j),\\
{{\sf r}}(I,k)&\text{otherwise. }\end{cases}

Non-empty subword complexes come with two extremal facets.
Considering facets as ordered sets
1
, the
greedy facet

I_{\operatorname{g}}
is the lexicographically first facet and the
antigreedy facet

I_{\operatorname{ag}}
is the lexicographically last facet.
Using
Lemma

2.7
, one can describe these greedy facets by their root configurations.
We also refer to
[
PS13
, Section 4.2]
for a discussion of greedy facets.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
be a non-empty subword complex.
Its greedy facet
I_{\operatorname{g}}
is the unique facet with
{{\sf R}}(I_{\operatorname{g}})\subseteq\Phi^{+}
and its antigreedy facet
I_{\operatorname{ag}}
is the unique facet with
{{\sf R}}(I_{\operatorname{ag}})\subseteq w(\Phi^{+})
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
PS13
, Proposition 6]
, the greedy facet is the unique facet for which all flips are increasing, and the antigreedy facet is the unique facet for which all flips are decreasing.
These properties are equivalent to the respective descriptions in terms of root configurations:
For the greedy facet, this immediately follows from
Lemma

2.7
(b)
, while it follows for the antigreedy facet from this lemma together with the computation

\displaystyle{{\sf R}}(I_{\operatorname{ag}})

\displaystyle\subseteq\big(\Phi^{+}\setminus\operatorname{Inv}(w)\big)\cup-\operatorname{Inv}(w)

\displaystyle=\big(\Phi^{+}\setminus\big(\Phi^{+}\cap-w(\Phi^{+})\big)\big)\cup\big(\Phi^{-}\cap w(\Phi^{+})\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\Phi^{+}\cap w(\Phi^{+})\big)\cup\big(\Phi^{-}\cap w(\Phi^{+})\big)

\displaystyle=w(\Phi^{+}),

where the first inclusion is the describing property that all flips are decreasing and the others are elementary reformulations.
∎

3.
The interplay between Bruhat intervals and subword complexes

This section studies the interplay between Bruhat intervals and subword complexes via root configurations.
To this end, we recall the
(closed) cone
over a finite set
X\subseteq V
to be

\cone(X)=\textstyle\sum_{v\in X}\mathbb{R}_{+}v=\left\{\textstyle\sum_{v\in X}\lambda_{v}v\;|\;\lambda_{v}\geq 0\right\}.

The following notions of Bruhat cones were studied by Dyer in the context of Kazhdan-Lusztig polynomials
[
Dye94
]
.
For a Bruhat interval
[x,y]
, the
upper Bruhat cone

{\mathcal{C}^{+}(x,y)}
and the
lower Bruhat cone

{\mathcal{C}^{-}(x,y)}
are defined by
{\mathcal{C}^{+}(x,y)}=\cone{\mathcal{E}^{+}(x,y)}
and
{\mathcal{C}^{-}(x,y)}=\cone{\mathcal{E}^{-}(x,y)}
for

{\mathcal{E}^{+}(x,y)}=\left\{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\;\big|\;x\prec s_{\beta}x\leq y\right\},\quad{\mathcal{E}^{-}(x,y)}=\left\{\beta\in\Phi^{+}\;\big|\;x\leq s_{\beta}y\prec y\right\}.

This is, the upper Bruhat cone is the cone spanned by the labels of the atoms in the Bruhat interval
[x,y]
and the lower Bruhat cone is the cone spanned by the labels of the coatoms.
Since we mainly discuss upper Bruhat cones, we simply call these
Bruhat cones
.

The goal of this section is to develop tools connecting Bruhat cones and root configurations, culminating in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\rm Q}
be a word in
\mathcal{S}
and let
w\in W
with
w\leq{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})

We first recall some elementary notions for polyhedra in the vector space

A subset

and scalars

P=\left\{v\in V\;\big|\;f_{i}(v)+b_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k\right\}.

A bounded polyhedron is furthermore called

A

of a polyhedron

is a subset

such that there exists a linear functional

and a scalar

with
f+b

P
and such that
F=\left\{v\in P\;|\;f(v)+b=0\right\}
.
The linear functional
f
is called
defining functional
for the face
F
and the pair
(f,b)
is called
defining hyperplane
.
The
local cone
of a polyhedron

seen from the point

\operatorname{cone}^{(q)}(P)=\cone\left\{p-q\;|\;p\in P\right\}.

Moreover, the
(inner) normal cone

of a face

is the cone of defining functionals of
F
and the
(inner) normal fan

is the collection of normal cones of faces of
P
,

{\mathcal{N}(P)}=\left\{{\operatorname{C}^{\diamond}(F)}\;\big|\;F\text{ a face of }P\right\}.

Finally, the
Minskowski sum

of two polyhedra

and

is given by pointwise vector addition.
Observe that every

and a
j

Define the

4.3.
Containment properties of brick polyhedra for a fixed word

The considerations of brick polyhedra also for non-spherical subword complexes makes it possible to discuss how the various brick polyhedra for a fixed word

are related.
In this section, we use the map
\iota:\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},s_{\beta}w)\rightarrow\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
from (
3.1
), to show how brick vectors change between the subword complexes
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},w)
and
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},s_{\beta}w)
for a cover

in Bruhat order.

3.6
, we then obtain the following containment statement of brick polyhedra.

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
w\in W
and

such that

.
Then
\mathcal{B}({\rm Q},ws)\subseteq\mathcal{B}({\rm Q},w)
.

We first show the following result for brick vectors and extract the key ingredient into a lemma.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
w\prec s_{\beta}w\leq{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q})
and
I\in\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},s_{\beta}w)
be a facet.
Then
{{\sf b}({I})}\in\mathcal{B}({\rm Q},w)
.

Lemma 4.23
.

In the situation of the proposition, we have
{{\sf b}({I})}\in{{\sf b}({\iota(I)})}+\mathbb{R}_{+}(\beta)
.

Proof.

The facet
\iota(I)
is the set
I\cup\{j\}
where
j
is the unique position in the complement of
I
, such that
{{\sf r}}(I,j)=\beta
in
\mathcal{SC}({\rm Q},s_{\beta}w)
.
Similar to
Lemma

4.7
(b)
, the weight function
{{\sf w}}(\iota(I),\cdot)
is obtained from the weight function
{{\sf w}}(I,\cdot)
by

{{\sf w}}(\iota(I),k)=\begin{cases}{{\sf w}}(I,k)&\text{ if }k\leq j,\\
s_{\beta}({{\sf w}}(I,k))&\text{ if }k>j.\end{cases}

Let
k>j
.
Since
\beta={{\sf r}}(I,j)
and
j\notin I
,
Lemma

4.7
(c)
yields that
\langle\beta,{{\sf w}}(I,k)\rangle
is non-positive.
Therefore,
s_{\beta}({{\sf w}}(I,k))\in{{\sf w}}(I,k)+\mathbb{R}_{+}(\beta)
, and we obtain

{{\sf b}({I})}={{\sf b}({\iota(I)})}+\mathbb{R}_{+}(\beta)
.

Proof of
Proposition

4.22
.

The previous lemma shows that
{{\sf b}({I})}\in{{\sf b}({\iota(I)})}+\mathbb{R}_{+}(\beta)
.
Since
\beta\in{\mathcal{C}^{+}(w,{\operatorname{Dem}}({\rm Q}))}

Proof of
Theorem

4.21
.

This follows from

3.6
.
