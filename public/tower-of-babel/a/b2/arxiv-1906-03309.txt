An optimal transport problem with backward martingale constraints motivated by insider trading.
Dmitry Kramkov, Yan Xu

Abstract
We study a single-period optimal transport problem on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} with a covariance-type cost function c ⁡ ( x , y ) = ( x 1 − y 1 ) ​ ( x 2 − y 2 ) c(x,y)=(x_{1}-y_{1})(x_{2}-y_{2}) and a backward martingale constraint. We show that a transport plan γ \gamma is optimal if and only if there is a maximal monotone set G G that supports the x x -marginal of γ \gamma and such that c ⁡ ( x , y ) = min z ∈ G ⁡ c ⁡ ( z , y ) c(x,y)=\min_{z\in G}c(z,y) for every ( x , y ) ∈ supp ⁡ γ (x,y)\in\supp\gamma . We obtain sharp regularity conditions for the uniqueness of an optimal plan and for its representation in terms of a map. Our study is motivated by a variant of the classical Kyle model of insider trading from Rochet and Vila 1994 .

Introduction

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and

Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
be a
2

second moment:
Y\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Our goal
is to

\text{minimize}\quad\mathbb{E}\left(c(X,Y)\right)\quad\text{over}\quad X\in\mathcal{X}(Y),

(1)

for the cost function
c(x,y)=(x_{1}-y_{1})(x_{2}-y_{2})
,

x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and the domain
\mathcal{X}(Y)
that consists of

Y
-measurable random variables
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
such that
(X,Y)
is a
martingale:
\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=X
. A relaxation of the
Y
-measurability
constraint on

leads to the optimal transport problem:

\text{minimize}\int c(x,y)d\gamma\quad\text{over}\quad\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu),

(2)

where
\nu=\Law(Y)
and
\Gamma(\nu)
is the family of probability
measures
\gamma=\gamma(dx,dy)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
that have

\nu
as their

\gamma(\mathbb{R}^{2},dy)=\nu(dy)
, and make a
martingale out of the canonical process:
\gamma(y|x)=x
. In view

equivalent formulation:

\displaystyle\text{maximize}\quad\int x_{1}x_{2}d\gamma\quad\text{over}\quad\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu),

and thus, has a natural connection to the classical
Fréchet-Hoeffding inequality and the Wasserstein
2
-distance.

Problem (

structure in the sense

Beiglböck et al. 2017

Henry-Labordère and Touzi 2016
,
and
Ghoussoub et al. 2019

martingale measure
\gamma=\gamma(dx,dy)
with given marginals

and
\nu=\nu(dy)
produces the same average cost:

\int c(x,y)d\gamma=\int y_{1}y_{2}d\nu-\int x_{1}x_{2}d\mu.

Our work is motivated by the classical

with insider from financial economics. More precisely, we consider the

Setting

Let

Remark 2.1
.

Problem (

\displaystyle\int c(x,y)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int y_{1}y_{2}d\nu-\int x_{1}x_{2}d\gamma,

For a Borel probability measure
\gamma

For

,

Lemma 2.7
.

The family
\Gamma(\nu)
is a convex compact set in

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
under the Wasserstein metric
W_{2}
.

Proof.

The martingale property
\gamma(y|x)=x
of
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)

is equivalent to the identity

\int f(x)(y-x)d\gamma=0

for every bounded and continuous function
f=f(x)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The
convexity and the closedness of
\Gamma(\nu)
under
W_{2}
readily
follow. It only remains to be shown that
\Gamma(\nu)
is
pre-compact under
W_{2}
or, equivalently, that

\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\int(\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2})1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma\to 0,\quad K\to\infty.

For
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int(\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2})1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert+\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq 2K}\right\}}d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int\left(\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}+\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}\right)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int\left\lvert\gamma(y|x)\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\nu

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\nu

and then that

\displaystyle\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K}\right\}}d\gamma

\displaystyle\leq\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K>\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}}\right\}}d\gamma+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}\geq K}\right\}}d\nu

\displaystyle\leq K\gamma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K)+\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}1_{\left\{{\left\lvert y\right\rvert\geq\sqrt{K}}\right\}}d\nu.

Finally, we obtain that

\displaystyle K\gamma(\left\lvert x\right\rvert\geq K)\leq\frac{1}{K}\int\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{2}d\gamma=\frac{1}{K}\int\left\lvert\gamma(y|x)\right\rvert^{2}d\gamma\leq\frac{1}{K}\int\left\lvert y\right\rvert^{2}d\nu,

and the result follows.
∎

is continuous and has quadratic growth, we
deduce that

\int c(x,y)d\gamma=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int c(x,y)d\gamma_{n_{k}}=\inf_{\zeta\in\Gamma}\int c(x,y)d\zeta.

Thus,
\gamma
is an optimal plan.
∎

The implication
(a)
\implies
(b)
of
Theorem
2.2
is proved in Lemma
2.10
and relies on the
following first-order optimality condition.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\gamma
be an optimal plan for (
5
). Then

\int c(x,y)d\eta\leq\int y_{1}y_{2}d\eta-\int y_{1}d\eta\int y_{2}d\eta,

(12)

for every
\eta\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that

\supp\eta\subset\supp\gamma
.

Proof.

We first establish (
12
) for a Borel probability measure

\eta
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
that has a bounded density with respect
to
\gamma
:

V(x,y)=\frac{d\eta}{d\gamma}\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}).

We choose a non-atom
q\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
of
\mu(dx)=\gamma(dx,\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
define the probability measure

\zeta(dx,dy)=\delta_{q}(dx)\eta(\mathbb{R}^{2},dy),

where
\delta_{q}
is the Dirac measure concentrated at
q
. For
sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
the probability measure

\widetilde{\gamma}=\gamma+\varepsilon(\zeta-\eta)

is well-defined and has the same
y
-marginal
\nu
as
\gamma
. We
define the conditional expectation

\widetilde{X}(x)=\widetilde{\gamma}(y|x)
and observe that the law
of
(\widetilde{X},y)
under
\widetilde{\gamma}
belongs to

\Gamma(\nu)
. The optimality of
\gamma
for (
5
) or,
equivalently, for (
6
) implies that

\int\widetilde{X}_{1}\widetilde{X}_{2}d\widetilde{\gamma}\leq\int x_{1}x_{2}d\gamma.

(13)

Standard computations based on Bayes formula show that

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}(x)=1_{\left\{{x\not=q}\right\}}\frac{x-\varepsilon R(x)}{1-\varepsilon U(x)}+1_{\left\{{x=q}\right\}}m,

where
U(x)=\gamma(V|x)
,
R(x)=\gamma(Vy|x)
, and

m=\int yd\eta
. Since
\left\lvert V\right\rvert\leq K
for some constant
K>0
,
we deduce that
\left\lvert U\right\rvert\leq K
and

\left\lvert R\right\rvert\leq K\gamma(\left\lvert y\right\rvert|x)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\int\widetilde{X}_{1}\widetilde{X}_{2}d\widetilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(x_{1}-\varepsilon R_{1})(x_{2}-\varepsilon R_{2})}{(1-\varepsilon U)^{2}}(1-\varepsilon V)d\gamma+\varepsilon m_{1}m_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(x_{1}-\varepsilon R_{1})(x_{2}-\varepsilon R_{2})}{1-\varepsilon U}d\gamma+\varepsilon m_{1}m_{2}

\displaystyle=\int x_{1}x_{2}d\gamma+\varepsilon\left(m_{1}m_{2}+\int(x_{1}x_{2}U-x_{1}R_{2}-x_{2}R_{1})d\gamma\right)+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

In view of (
13
), the first-order term is negative. It can
be written as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq m_{1}m_{2}+\int(x_{1}x_{2}U-x_{1}R_{2}-x_{2}R_{1})d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int y_{1}d\eta\int y_{2}d\eta+\int(x_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})Vd\gamma

\displaystyle=\int y_{1}d\eta\int y_{2}d\eta+\int(x_{1}x_{2}-x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1})d\eta

\displaystyle=\int y_{1}d\eta\int y_{2}d\eta+\int(c(x,y)-y_{1}y_{2})d\eta

and the result follows.

In the general case, where
\eta\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})

and
\supp{\eta}\subset\supp{\gamma}
, we use the approximation
result from Appendix
A
. By
Theorem
A.1
, there are Borel probability measures
(\eta_{n})

on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
that have bounded densities with respect to

\gamma
and converge to
\eta
under
W_{2}
. By what we have already
proved,

\int c(x,y)d\eta_{n}\leq\int y_{1}y_{2}d\eta_{n}-\int y_{1}d\eta_{n}\int y_{2}d\eta_{n},\quad n\geq 1.

Since the integrands are continuous functions with quadratic growth,
we can pass to the limit as
n\to\infty
and obtain (
12
).
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\gamma
be an optimal plan for (
5
). Then
condition
(b)
of Theorem
2.2
holds.

Proof.

Lemma
2.9
yields inequality (
12
) for the
probability measure

\eta(dx,dy)=(1-t)\delta_{(x^{0},y^{0})}(dx,dy)+t\delta_{(x^{1},y^{1})}(dx,dy),

where
t\in[0,1]
,
(x^{i},y^{i})\in\supp{\gamma}
, and

\delta_{(x^{i},y^{i})}
is the Dirac measure concentrated at

,

. Elementary computations show that for such

).
∎

The equivalence of assertions
(b)
and
(c)
of
Theorem
2.2
is a special case of Lemma
2.12
, whose proof
relies on the following geometric interpretation
of (
7
). Figure

visualizes the arguments.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
x^{i}
and
y^{i}
,
i=0,1
, be points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

y^{0}_{1}<y^{1}_{1}
. Then (
7
) holds if and only if for all

a_{i}<c(x^{i},y^{i})
,
i=0,1
, the graphs of the hyperbolas

\displaystyle h^{0}(s)

\displaystyle=y^{0}_{2}+\frac{a_{0}}{s-y^{0}_{1}},\quad s>y^{0}_{1},

\displaystyle h^{1}(s)

\displaystyle=y^{1}_{2}+\frac{a_{1}}{s-y^{1}_{1}},\quad s<y^{1}_{1},

do not intersect:

h^{0}(s)<h^{1}(s),\quad s\in(y^{0}_{1},y^{1}_{1}).

Proof.

The result follows from the identity:

\displaystyle(h^{1}(s)-h^{0}(s))(s-y^{0}_{1})(y^{1}_{1}-s)

\displaystyle\qquad=(y^{1}_{2}-y^{0}_{2})(s-y^{0}_{1})(y^{1}_{1}-s)-a_{0}(y^{1}_{1}-s)-a_{1}(s-y^{0}_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad=(y^{1}_{1}-y^{0}_{1})\left(t(1-t)c(y^{0},y^{1})-(1-t)a_{0}-ta_{1}\right),

where
s\in(y^{0}_{1},y^{1}_{1})
and
t=(s-y^{0}_{1})/(y^{1}_{1}-y^{0}_{1})
.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
the following conditions are
equivalent:

(i)

If points
(x^{0},y^{0})
and
(x^{1},y^{1})
belong
to
A
, then (
7
) holds.

(ii)

There is
G\in\mathfrak{M}
such that

c(x,y)\leq\phi_{G}(y),\quad(x,y)\in A.

Proof.

We observe first that under either
(i)
or
(ii)
,

c(x,y)\leq 0,\quad(x,y)\in A.

Indeed, under
(i)
this inequality follows
from (
7
), while under
(ii)
it holds because

\phi_{G}\leq 0
. We define the open sets

\displaystyle B^{i}=\cup_{(x,y)\in A}B^{i}(x,y),\quad i=0,1,

where, for
(x,y)\in A
,

\displaystyle B^{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;c(z,y)<c(x,y),\;z_{1}>y_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;z_{2}<y_{2}+\frac{c(x,y)}{z_{1}-y_{1}},\;z_{1}>y_{1}\right\},

\displaystyle B^{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left\{{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;c(z,y)<c(x,y),\;z_{1}<y_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;z_{2}>y_{2}+\frac{c(x,y)}{z_{1}-y_{1}},\;z_{1}<y_{1}\right\}.

The boundaries of
B^{i}
,
i=0,1
, are, respectively, upper and lower
envelopes of the graphs of increasing hyperbolas and thus, are
maximal monotone sets.

By Lemma
2.11
, item
(i)
holds if and only if the
sets
B^{0}
and
B^{1}
are disjoint:

B^{0}\cap B^{1}=\emptyset.

(14)

On the other hand, item
(ii)
holds if and only if the closed
set

C=\cap_{(x,y)\in A}\left\{{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;c(x,y)\leq c(z,y)\right\}

contains a maximal monotone set
G
. As

C=\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\left(B^{0}\cup B^{1}\right),

every such set
G
separates
B^{0}
and
B^{1}
. Hence, its existence
yields (
14
) and then
(i)
. Conversely, if the sets

B^{0}
and
B^{1}
are disjoint, then their boundaries belong to

C
. As the boundaries are maximal monotone sets, we
obtain
(ii)
.
∎

The remaining assertions of the theorem follow from
Lemma
2.14
. A key role is played by inequality (
15
).

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
and
\mu
be its
x
-marginal. For
every
G\in\mathfrak{M}
we have that

\gamma(\phi_{G}(y)-c(x,y)|x)\leq\phi_{G}(x)\leq 0,\quad\gamma\text{-a.s.},

and then that

\int\phi_{G}(y)d\nu-\int c(x,y)d\gamma\leq\int\phi_{G}(x)d\mu\leq 0.

(15)

Proof.

We only need to prove the inequality with conditional
expectations. From Lemma
B.1
we know that
\phi_{G}\leq 0
.
As
x=\gamma(y|x)
, we deduce that for every
r\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\gamma(c(y,r)-c(x,y)|x)=c(x,r),\quad\gamma\text{-a.s.},

and taking
\inf
over a dense countable set of
r\in G
obtain the
result.
∎

The following lemma completes the proof of the theorem.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
,
\mu
be its
x
-marginal, and

G\in\mathfrak{M}
be such that

\int c(x,y)d\gamma\leq\int\phi_{G}(y)d\nu.

(16)

Then in (
16
) we actually have the equality,
\gamma
is an
optimal plan for (
5
), the set
G
contains
\supp{\mu}
,
and

c(x,y)=\phi_{G}(y)=\min_{r\in G}c(r,y),\quad(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}.

(17)

Proof.

We shall write
\phi
for
\phi_{G}
. From (
15
)
and (
16
) we deduce that
\gamma
is a solution
to (
5
), that in (
16
) we have an equality, and that

\int\phi(x)d\mu=0.

Lemma
B.1
states that
\phi\leq 0
and

G=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\phi(x)=0\right\}
. It follows that
\mu(G)=1
.
Being a closed set,
G
contains
\supp{\mu}
. In particular, if

(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}
, then
x\in G
. It follows that

c(x,y)\geq\inf_{r\in G}c(r,y)=\phi(y),\quad(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}.

Accounting for (
16
), we deduce that

c(x,y)=\phi(y),\quad\gamma\text{-a.s.}.

Hence, for every
(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}
we can find a sequence

\left\{{(x_{n},y_{n})}\right\}\subset\supp{\gamma}
that converges to
(x,y)

and such that
c(x_{n},y_{n})=\phi(y_{n})
,
n\geq 1
. Being a
pointwise infinum of continuous functions, the function
\phi
is
upper semi-continuous. It follows that

c(x,y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}c(x_{n},y_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(y_{n})\leq\phi(y),

and we obtain (
17
).
∎

3
Dual problem

In view of Theorem
2.2
and Lemma
2.13
, a natural dual
problem to (
5
) is to

\text{maximize}\quad\int\phi_{G}d\nu\quad\text{over}\quad G\in\mathfrak{M}.

(18)

Such problem appears in
Rochet and Vila 1994
in connection to their
study of Kyle-type equilibrium with insider;
see Section
6
. They use a direct method based
on the properties of the space of closed graph correspondences and
assume that
\nu
has a compactly supported density.

We recall that
G=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\phi_{G}(x)=0\right\}
,

G\in\mathfrak{M}
, and thus the family
\mathfrak{M}
of all maximal
monotone sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is in one-to-one correspondence with the
family of functions

\Phi\triangleq\left\{{\phi_{G}}:\;G\in\mathfrak{M}\right\}.

Hence, (
18
) is equivalent to the problem, where we

\text{maximize}\quad\int\phi d\nu\quad\text{over}\quad\phi\in\Phi.

A technical inconvenience of the set
\Phi
is the absence of
convexity. It turns out that the set of functions dominated by the
elements of
\Phi
is not only convex, but also admits a
self-contained description related to item
(b)
of
Theorem
2.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\phi:\;\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow[-\infty,0]
be a Borel function. Then

\phi\leq\phi_{G}
for some
G\in\mathfrak{M}
if and only if

(1-t)\phi(y^{0})+t\phi(y^{1})\leq t(1-t)c(y^{0},y^{1}),\quad y^{0},y^{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\;t\in[0,1].

(19)

The set of such functions
\phi
is convex.

Proof.

The result follows directly from Lemma
2.12
, where we take

A=\left\{{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;c(x,y)=\phi(y)\right\}.

Clearly, the family of functions
\phi
satisfying (
19
) is
convex.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We have that

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\int c(x,y)d\gamma=\max_{G\in\mathfrak{M}}\int\phi_{G}d\nu,

where the lower and upper bounds are attained at respective
solutions to (
5
) and (
18
). A probability measure

\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
and a maximal monotone set
G
are such
solutions if and only if

c(x,y)=\phi_{G}(y),\quad(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}.

(20)

In this case,
G
contains the support of the
x
-marginal of

\gamma
. Moreover,
\phi_{G}
and
G
are uniquely defined on

\supp{\nu}
, that is,

\displaystyle\phi_{G}(y)

\displaystyle=\phi_{\widetilde{G}}(y),\quad y\in\supp{\nu},

\displaystyle G\cap\supp{\nu}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{G}\cap\supp{\nu},

for any other solution
\widetilde{G}
to (
18
). In
particular,
\phi_{G}
and
G
are unique if
\supp{\nu}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

With an exception of the uniqueness part, all other assertions
follow directly from Theorem
2.2
and Lemmas
2.13

and
2.14
.

Let
\gamma
be a solution to (
5
),
G
and
\widetilde{G}

be solutions to (
18
) and denote
\phi=\phi_{G}
and

\widetilde{\phi}=\phi_{\widetilde{G}}
. From (
20
) we
deduce that the functions
\phi
and
\widetilde{\phi}
coincide on

P_{y}
, the projection of
\supp{\gamma}
on
y
-coordinates. Since
every
y\in\supp{\nu}
is the limit of a sequence

(y_{n})\subset P_{y}
, Lemma
B.3
yields that

\phi(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(y_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\widetilde{\phi}(y_{n})=\widetilde{\phi}(y).

We have proved the uniqueness of
\phi_{G}
on
\supp{\nu}
. The
uniqueness of
G
on
\supp{\nu}
holds as

G=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\phi_{G}(x)=0\right\}
.
∎

4
Optimal maps

For simplicity of notations, we slightly modify the setup. We start
with a 2-dimensional random variable
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
having a finite
second moment:

Y\in\mathcal{L}^{2}=\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
As usual, we identify random variables that differ only on a set of
measure zero. Our goal is to

\text{minimize}\quad\mathbb{E}\left(c(X,Y)\right)\quad\text{over}\quad X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)

(21)

for the same cost function
c(x,y)=(x_{1}-y_{1})(x_{2}-y_{2})
and the
domain

\mathcal{X}(Y)\triangleq\left\{{X=(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathcal{L}^{2}}:\;X\text{ is
$Y$-measurable and }\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=X\right\}.

We denote
\nu=\Law(Y)
and observe that
\Law(X,Y)\in\Gamma(\nu)

for every
X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)
. Thus, optimal
plan

problem (
5
) may be viewed as a Kantorovich-type relaxation of
optimal
map
problem (
21
). In general,

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\int c(x,y)d\gamma\leq\inf_{X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)}\mathbb{E}\left(c(X,Y)\right),

(22)

and the inequality may be strict and an optimal map may not exist as
Examples
5.2
and
5.3
show.

The main results of this sections are Theorems
4.1
,
4.5
,
and
4.6
. Theorem
4.1
yields the equality
in (
22
) provided that
\nu=\Law(Y)
is atomless.
Theorem
4.5
shows the existence of an optimal map if
\nu
is

\mathcal{D}
-regular in the sense of
Definition
4.4
. Theorem
4.6
establishes the uniqueness
of optimal plan and map if, in addition, every component
Y_{i}
has a
continuous distribution function. The last two theorems play a key
role in the study of equilibrium in
Section
6
.

We shall use the notations from
Appendix
B
related to the function

\phi=\phi_{G}
, where
G\in\mathfrak{M}
. In particular,

D^{c}=(D^{c}_{1},D^{c}_{2})
stands for the differential operator associated
with the cost function
c=c(x,y)
:

\displaystyle D^{c}_{1}\phi(y)=y_{1}-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y_{2}}(y),\quad D^{c}_{2}\phi(y)=y_{2}-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y_{1}}(y),\quad y\in\dom{\nabla\phi},

where
\dom{\nabla\phi}
is the set of points where
\phi
is
differentiable. We denote by
E^{G}=E_{1}^{G}\cup E_{2}^{G}
the union of
the vertical and horizontal line segments of
G
:

\begin{split}E_{i}^{G}(t)&=\left\{{x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in G}:\;x_{i}=t\right\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\\
\mathcal{T}_{i}^{G}&=\left\{{t\in\mathbb{R}}:\;{E_{i}^{G}(t)}\;\text{has more
than one point}\right\},\\
E_{i}^{G}&=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{i}^{G}}E_{i}^{G}(t),\quad i=1,2.\end{split}

(23)

Clearly, the sets
(\mathcal{T}^{G}_{i})
are countable. Finally, we
define

\displaystyle\Arg_{G}(y)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{x\in G}c(x,y)=\left\{{x\in G}:\;\phi_{G}(y)=c(x,y)\right\},

\displaystyle\dom{\Arg_{G}}

\displaystyle=\left\{{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\Arg_{G}(y)\not=\emptyset\right\}.

The following result is similar to that of
Pratelli 2007
obtained
for the classical unconstrained optimal transport problem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and suppose that
\nu=\Law(Y)

is atomless. Then plan and map problems (
5
) and
(
21
) have identical values:

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)}\int c(x,y)d\gamma=\inf_{X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)}\mathbb{E}\left(c(X,Y)\right).

The proof of the theorem relies on some lemmas.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
be an
optimal plan for (
5
), and
G\in\mathfrak{M}
be a
maximizer for (
18
). If
\nu(G)>0
, then the probability
measure

\eta(dx,dy)=\frac{1}{\nu(G)}1_{\left\{{y\in G}\right\}}\gamma(dx,dy)

has the martingale property:
\eta(y|x)=x
.

Proof.

We write
\phi
for
\phi_{G}
. We shall show that

\eta(y_{1}|x)=x_{1}
, that is, that

\int f(x)(y_{1}-x_{1})d\eta=\frac{1}{\nu(G)}\int f(x)(y_{1}-x_{1})1_{\left\{{y\in G}\right\}}d\gamma=0,

(24)

for every bounded Borel function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The martingale
property for the second coordinate has a similar proof.

Let
E_{2}=E_{2}^{G}=\cup_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}}E_{2}(t)
be the union of
the horizontal line segments of
G
. If
(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}
,
then Theorem
3.2
yields that
x\in G
and
c(x,y)=\phi(y)
. If, in addition,
y\in G\setminus E_{2}
, then

c(x,y)=\phi(y)=0
and, subsequently,

x_{1}=y_{1}
. Hence, (
24
) holds if

\int f(x)(y_{1}-x_{1})1_{\left\{{y\in E_{2}(t)}\right\}}d\gamma=0,\quad t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}.

(25)

Hereafter, we fix
t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
. Let

(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}
. If
y\in E_{2}(t)
, then

c(x,y)=\phi(y)=0
and thus,
x\in E_{2}(t)
. Conversely, if

x\in\ri{E_{2}(t)}
, the relative interior of
E_{2}(t)
, then
Lemma
B.4
yields that
y\in G
and then, as

c(x,y)=\phi(y)=0
, that
y\in E_{2}(t)
. Hence,

\displaystyle 1_{\left\{{y\in E_{2}(t)}\right\}}

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{{x,y\in E_{2}(t)}\right\}}

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{{x\in\ri{E_{2}(t)}}\right\}}+1_{\left\{{x=a(t),y\in E_{2}(t)}\right\}}+1_{\left\{{x=b(t),y\in E_{2}(t)}\right\}},

where
a(t)
and
b(t)
are the boundary points of
E_{2}(t)
such
that
a_{1}(t)<b_{1}(t)
. Accounting for the martingale property of

\gamma
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int f(x)(y_{1}-x_{1})1_{\left\{{x\in\ri{E_{2}(t)}}\right\}}d\gamma=0.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be such that
\phi(y)=c(b(t),y)
. If

y\not\in G
, then Lemma
B.4
yields that
b_{1}(t)>y_{1}
. If

y\in G\setminus E_{2}(t)
, then
c(b(t),y)=\phi(y)=0
and thus,

b_{1}(t)=y_{1}
. Finally, if
y\in E_{2}(t)
, then
b_{1}(t)\geq y_{1}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\int\left\lvert x_{1}-y_{1}\right\rvert 1_{\left\{{x=b(t),y\in E_{2}(t)}\right\}}d\gamma\leq\int(x_{1}-y_{1})1_{\left\{{x=b(t)}\right\}}d\gamma=0,

where at the last step we used the martingale property of

\gamma
. The case of the left boundary

is similar. We have
proved (

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G\in\mathfrak{M}
and
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
and
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
be
random variables such that
X
takes values in
G
,

X1_{\left\{{Y\in G}\right\}}=Y1_{\left\{{Y\in G}\right\}}
, and
c(X,Y)=\phi_{G}(Y)
. If the
law of
Y
is atomless, then for every
\epsilon>0
there is a
random variable
Z=Z(\epsilon)
such that
\Law(Z)=\Law(Y)
,

\left\lvert Z-Y\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
, and
X
is
Z
-measurable.

Proof.

We fix
\epsilon>0
and denote
\phi=\phi_{G}
,
\Arg=\Arg_{G}
, and

D=\dom{\Arg}\setminus(\dom{\nabla\phi}\cup G).

Theorems
B.6
and
B.12
show that
D=\cup_{n}D_{n}
,
where
D_{n}
is either a point or the graph of a strictly decreasing
function. Of course, we can choose the sets
(D_{n})
so that

\diam{D_{n}}\triangleq\sup_{x,y\in D_{n}}\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert\leq\epsilon,\quad n\geq 1.

For every
n\geq 1
we shall construct a two-dimensional random
variable
Z^{n}=(Z^{n}_{1},Z^{n}_{2})
and a Borel function

f^{n}:\;D_{n}\rightarrow G
such that

\begin{split}\Law(Z^{n}1_{\left\{{Y\in D_{n}}\right\}})&=\Law(Y1_{\left\{{Y\in D_{n}}\right\}}),\\
X1_{\left\{{Y\in D_{n}}\right\}}&=f^{n}(Z^{n})1_{\left\{{Y\in D_{n}}\right\}}.\end{split}

(26)

Given the sequence of such pairs
(Z^{n},f^{n})
,
n\geq 1
, we define

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=Y1_{\left\{{Y\in\dom{\nabla\phi}\cup G}\right\}}+\sum_{n}Z^{n}1_{\left\{{Y\in F_{n}}\right\}},

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=y1_{\left\{{y\in G}\right\}}+D^{c}\phi(y)1_{\left\{{y\in\dom{\nabla\phi}\setminus G}\right\}}+\sum_{n}f^{n}(y)1_{\left\{{y\in F_{n}}\right\}}.

where

. We have that

\Law(Z)=\Law(Y)=\nu
and
\left\lvert Z-Y\right\rvert\leq\epsilon
. Moreover,
in view of Theorem
B.6
,
X=f(Z)
. Hence, (
26
) is
all we need to obtain.

Using the conditional probabilities with respect to events

\left\{{Y\in D_{n}}\right\}
, we can reduce the general case to the situation
where

Y\in D=\left\{{(t,h(t))}:\;t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\right\},

for some strictly decreasing function
h=h(t)
. Since
\nu
is
atomless, every component
Y_{i}
has a continuous distribution
function
a_{i}(t)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{i}\leq t\right)
,
i=1,2
. It follows that

has the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and

Y_{1}=a_{1}^{-1}(U)
, where

is the pseudo-inverse function
to

a_{1}^{-1}(t)=\inf\left\{{s\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}:\;a_{1}(s)\geq t\right\},\quad t\in[0,1].

In particular,

is
U
-measurable.

Lemma
B.16
yields Borel functions
g_{i}:\;D\rightarrow G
,

, such that either

or

. As the

\displaystyle b_{i}(t)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(U\leq t,X=g_{i}(Y)\right),\quad t\in[0,1],\;i=1,2,

are continuous and increasing, the random variable

V=b_{1}(U)1_{\left\{{X=g_{1}(Y)}\right\}}+(b_{1}(1)+b_{2}(U))1_{\left\{{X=g_{2}(Y)}\right\}}

has the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. Clearly,

and the

(1_{\left\{{X=g_{i}(Y)}\right\}})
are
V
-measurable. It follows that

Y
and

are also

-measurable. Setting

we obtain that

, and that

is
Z
-measurable.
∎

.

and thus,

.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the family of graphs of strictly decreasing
functions

defined on closed intervals of
\mathbb{R}
such that both

f
and its inverse
f^{-1}
are Lipschitz functions:

\frac{1}{K}(t-s)\leq f(s)-f(t)\leq K(t-s),\quad s<t,

for some constant

. To make statements shorter we allow for
a degenerate case where the domain of

is just a point. Thus,

\mathbb{R}^{2}\subset\mathcal{D}
.

Definition 4.4
.

A Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is

-regular
if
\mu(D)=0
,

.

The following theorem establishes the existence of optimal maps that
induce optimal plans.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and suppose that
\nu=\Law(Y)

is
\mathcal{D}
-regular. Let
G\in\mathfrak{M}
be a maximizer
for (
18
) and denote
\phi=\phi_{G}
and

. Then

is an optimal map for (
21
),
\gamma=\Law(X,Y)
is an
optimal plan for (
5
), and the law of
X
is

\mathcal{D}
-regular. Moreover, if
\widetilde{X}
is an optimal map
and
\widetilde{\gamma}
is an optimal plan, then

\displaystyle X1_{\left\{{Y\not\in E}\right\}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{X}1_{\left\{{Y\not\in E}\right\}},

(27)

\displaystyle 1_{\left\{{y\not\in E}\right\}}\gamma(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=1_{\left\{{y\not\in E}\right\}}\widetilde{\gamma}(dx,dy).

(28)

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma(\nu)
be an optimal plan. From
Theorem
3.2
we deduce that if

(x,y)\in\supp{\widetilde{\gamma}}
, then

y\in\dom{\Arg}=\dom{\Arg_{G}}
. In particular,

\nu(\dom{\Arg})=1
. By Theorem
B.12
, the exception set

\dom{\Arg}\setminus\dom{\nabla\phi}
belongs to the union of

E=E^{G}
and of a countable family of sets from
\mathcal{D}
. Since

\nu=\Law(Y)
is
\mathcal{D}
-regular, we have that

\nu(\dom{\nabla\phi}\setminus E)=\nu(\dom{\Arg}\setminus E)=1-\nu(E).

(29)

It follows that the random variable
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
is well-defined.

Theorem
B.6
shows that if

y\in\dom{\nabla\phi}\setminus E
, then
D^{c}\phi(y)
is the only
element of
\Arg(y)
. It follows that
X\in G
and

\phi(Y)=c(X,Y)
. In view of Theorem
3.2
,

\gamma\triangleq\Law(X,Y)
is an optimal plan if it has the martingale
property:
\gamma(y|x)=x
.

If
(x,y)\in\supp{\widetilde{\gamma}}
and

y\in\dom{\nabla\phi}\setminus E
, then Theorems
3.2

and
B.6
yield that
x=D^{c}\phi(y)
. Since
\gamma
and

\widetilde{\gamma}
have common
y
-marginal
\nu

satisfying (
29
), they coincide outside of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times E
,
that is, (
28
) holds.

Let
f=f(x)
be a bounded Borel function on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. As

(Y-X)1_{\left\{{Y\in G}\right\}}=0
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\int(y-x)f(x)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int(y-x)f(x)1_{\left\{{y\not\in G}\right\}}d\gamma=\int(y-x)f(x)1_{\left\{{y\not\in G}\right\}}d\widetilde{\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\int(y-x)f(x)1_{\left\{{y\in G}\right\}}d\widetilde{\gamma}=0,

where the last two equalities follow from the martingale property of

\widetilde{\gamma}
and Lemma
4.2
, respectively. Thus,

\gamma(y|x)=x
. We have proved that
\gamma
is an optimal plan
and, in particular, that
X
is an optimal map. The uniqueness
property (

) for optimal maps follows directly from the
corresponding property (
28
) for optimal plans.

It only remains to be shown that
\mu\triangleq\Law(X)
is

\mathcal{D}
-regular. As
\supp{\mu}\subset G
and the intersection
of

with any set from

is a point,
\mu
is

-regular if and only if it is atomless. Assume to the

\mu(\left\{{r}\right\})>0
for some

and define the

Being

-regular, the measure

is atomless. Hence,

\displaystyle\mu(\left\{{r}\right\})\eta(G)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\left\{{r}\right\}\times G)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(X=r,Y\in G\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y=r\right)=\nu(\left\{{r}\right\})=0.

From the optimality of

By Lemma

. As

We now state the main uniqueness result of the paper, which can be
viewed as an adaptation of the classical Brenier theorem to our
setting. We point out that our regularity assumption on

\nu
is weaker than the standard condition of the Brenier theorem,
which requires
\nu
to assign zero mass to rotations of the graphs of
Lipschitz functions.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and suppose that
\nu=\Law(Y)

is
\mathcal{D}
-regular and the (one-dimensional) laws of
Y_{1}
and

Y_{2}
are atomless. Let
G\in\mathfrak{M}
be a maximizer
for (
18
) and denote
\phi=\phi_{G}
. Then
X=D^{c}\phi(Y)

or, in more detail,

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=D^{c}_{1}\phi(Y)=Y_{1}-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y_{2}}(Y),

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=D^{c}_{2}\phi(Y)=Y_{2}-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y_{1}}(Y),

is the unique optimal map for (
21
) and the law of
(X,Y)

is the unique optimal plan for (
5
). Moreover, the law of

X
is
\mathcal{D}
-regular and the laws of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are
atomless.

Proof.

We omit
G
from the notations (
23
) related to its vertical
and horizontal line segments. As the law of
Y_{i}
is atomless and
the set
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is countable, we deduce that

\nu(E)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y\in E\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y\in E_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}\right)=0.

Except the continuity of the distribution functions for
X_{1}
and

X_{2}
, all other assertions follow directly from Theorem
4.5
.

We shall prove that the law of
X_{2}
is atomless. If

t\not\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
, then the set
{E}_{2}(t)
is a singleton:

E_{2}(t)=\left\{{z}\right\}
. By Theorem
4.5
, the law of
X
is

\mathcal{D}
-regular and, in particular, atomless. It follows that

\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{2}=t\right)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(X=z\right)=0
.

Let
t\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
. Lemma
B.4
shows that if

and

. As

,

, and

is atomless, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{2}=t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(X\in E_{2}(t)\right)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(X\in\ri{E_{2}(t)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y\in E_{2}(t)\right)=\mathbb{\mathbb{P}}\left(Y_{2}=t\right)=0,

where the last step holds by the continuity of the law of

.
∎

5
Examples

Example 5.1

(Linear optimal map)
.

Let
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
be a random variable in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{i}\right)=0
,
\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{i}^{2}\right)=\sigma_{i}^{2}>0
, and

\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y_{i}\right\lvert{Z}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{i}Z\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}Z\quad\text{for all}\quad Z=a_{1}Y_{1}+a_{2}Y_{2},\;a_{j}\in\mathbb{R}.

The latter property holds if the distribution of
Y
is Gaussian or,
more generally, elliptically contoured.

We denote
\lambda=\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}>0
and define

G=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;x_{2}=\lambda x_{1}\right\}
and

\displaystyle X_{1}=\frac{1}{2}Y_{1}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}Y_{2},\quad X_{2}=\lambda X_{1}=\frac{\lambda}{2}Y_{1}+\frac{1}{2}Y_{2}.

Elementary computations show that
\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)=X=(X_{1},X_{2})
and

\phi_{G}(Y)=\inf_{x\in G}c(x,Y)=\inf_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}(Y_{1}-x_{1})(Y_{2}-\lambda x_{1})=c(X,Y).

Being the graph of an increasing linear function,

G\in\mathfrak{M}
. Setting
\nu=\Law(Y)
, we deduce from
Theorem
3.2
that
G
and
\gamma=\Law(X,Y)
are respective
solutions to (
18
) and (
5
). Moreover, as
X
is
the only element of
G
such that
\phi_{G}(Y)=c(X,Y)
, the
characteristic property (
20
) yields that
\gamma
is the
unique optimal plan. In particular,
X
is the unique optimal map
for (
21
).

Example 5.2

(Optimal map may not yield optimal plan)
.

Let
Y
be a random variable taking values in
y^{0}=(-1,1)
,

y^{1}=(0,-1)
, and
y^{2}=(1,0)
with probability
\frac{1}{3}
. Direct
computations show that the points

z^{i}=\frac{1}{3}y^{0}+\frac{2}{3}y^{i}=(-1)^{i}\big(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}\big),\quad i=1,2,

belong to the set

G=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;c(x,y^{0})=-\frac{8}{9},\;x_{1}>y_{1}^{0}\right\}

and that the probability measure

\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}\delta_{(z^{i},y^{i})}+\frac{1}{6}\delta_{(z^{i},y^{0})}\right)

belongs to
\Gamma(\nu)
, where
\nu=\Law(Y)
. Being the graph of
an increasing hyperbola,
G\in\mathfrak{M}
. By Theorem
2.2
,

\gamma
is an optimal plan for (
5
). The value of this

\int c(x,y)d\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}c(z^{i},y^{i})+\frac{1}{6}c(z^{i},y^{0})\right)=-\frac{4}{9}.

On the other hand, let
X\in\mathcal{X}(Y)
, that is,
X
is

Y
-measurable and
X=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Y\right\lvert{X}\right)
. We write
x^{i}=X(y^{i})
,

i=1,2,3
. If all
(x^{i})
are distinct, then

and

.
If they are the same point, then

and

\mathbb{E}\left(c(X,Y)\right)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=0}^{2}c(0,y^{i})=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=0}^{2}y^{i}_{1}y^{i}_{2}=-\frac{1}{3}.

Finally, if precisely two of the elements of
(x^{i})
coincide:

is a permutation of

(0,1,2)
, then

,

, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(c(X,Y)\right)=\frac{1}{3}\left(c(y^{k},\frac{1}{2}(y^{k}+y^{l}))+c(y^{l},\frac{1}{2}(y^{k}+y^{l}))\right)=\frac{1}{6}c(y^{k},y^{l}).

As

and

, the value
function of the optimal map problem (

) is given by

, which is strictly less than

, the
value of the optimal plan problem (

).

Example 5.3

(Optimal map may not exist)
.

Let
U
and

be independent symmetric random variables in

with

having a continuous distribution function

To this end, we define a 2-dimensional random variable

\displaystyle X=(\frac{1}{3}U,U)1_{\left\{{U<0}\right\}}+(U,\frac{1}{3}U)1_{\left\{{U\geq 0}\right\}}.

From the construction of

\left\{{V=-1}\right\}=\left\{{Y_{1}=-Y_{2}}\right\}=\left\{{Y=(-\left\lvert U\right\rvert,\left\lvert U\right\rvert)}\right\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left.X\right\lvert{Y}\right)1_{\left\{{Y_{1}=-Y_{2}}\right\}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.X\right\lvert{\left\lvert U\right\rvert}\right)1_{\left\{{Y_{1}=-Y_{2}}\right\}}=\frac{1}{3}(\left\lvert U\right\rvert,-\left\lvert U\right\rvert)1_{\left\{{Y_{1}=-Y_{2}}\right\}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}Y1_{\left\{{Y_{1}=-Y_{2}}\right\}}\not=X1_{\left\{{Y_{1}=-Y_{2}}\right\}}.

Hence,
X
is not
Y
-measurable.

Let
\gamma\in\Gamma(\nu)
be an optimal plan and
\mu
be its

x
-marginal. By Theorem
3.2
,
\supp{\mu}\subset G
and

c(x,y)=\phi(y),\quad(x,y)\in\supp{\gamma}.

The random variable
Y
takes values in
F=F_{1}\cup F_{2}
, where

\displaystyle F_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{{y_{2}=-y_{1},\;y_{1}<0}\right\},

\displaystyle F_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{{y_{2}=-9y_{1}\text{ or }y_{2}=-\frac{1}{9}y_{1},\;y_{1}\geq 0}\right\}.

Elementary computations show that for
x\in G
the set of
y\in F

such that
c(x,y)=\phi_{G}(y)
consists of two points
g(x)
and

f(x)
such that

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=(-x_{1},9x_{1})1_{\left\{{x_{1}<0}\right\}}+(3x_{1},-\frac{1}{3}x_{1})1_{\left\{{x_{1}\geq 0}\right\}},

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=(3x_{1},-3x_{1})1_{\left\{{x_{1}<0}\right\}}+(-x_{1},x_{1})1_{\left\{{x_{1}\geq 0}\right\}}.

For
x\not=0
,

, the three points
\left\{{g(x),x,f(x)}\right\}
are
distinct and

x=\frac{1}{2}(g(x)+f(x)).

On the other hand, by the martingale property of
\gamma
and the
fact that
\nu(\left\{{0}\right\})=0
, we have that

x=\gamma(y|x)=f(x)\gamma(y=f(x)|x)+g(x)\gamma(y=g(x)|x),\quad\gamma\text{-a.s.},

and therefore, the conditional probabilities

\gamma(y=f(x)|x)=\gamma(y=g(x)|x)=\frac{1}{2},\quad\gamma\text{-a.s.}.

For a bounded Borel function

on

we then
obtain that

\displaystyle\int h(x,y)d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int(h(x,g(x))1_{\left\{{y=g(x)}\right\}}+h(x,f(x))1_{\left\{{y=f(x)}\right\}})d\gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int(h(x,g(x))+h(x,f(x)))d\mu.

Hence,

is unique if and only if
\mu
is unique. We observe
now that the map

is one-to-one. Thus, for a Borel

,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mu(B)=\int 1_{\left\{{x\in B}\right\}}1_{\left\{{y=f(x)}\right\}}d\gamma=\int 1_{\left\{{y\in f(B)}\right\}}d\gamma=\nu(f(B)),

and the uniqueness of

follows.

6
Equilibrium with insider

We consider a single-period financial market. There are a bank account
with zero interest rate and a stock. The stock value at maturity
t=1

is represented by a random variable
V
. The stock price
S
at
initial time
t=0
is the result of the interaction between

noise traders
, an
insider
, and
market makers
,
where

1.

The noise traders place an order for
U
stocks;
U
is a random
variable.

2.

The insider knows the value of both
U
and
V
and places an
order for
Q
stocks. The
trading strategy

Q
is a

(U,V)
-measurable random variable.

3.

The market makers observe only the total order
R=Q+U
. They
quote the price
S=f(R)
according to a
pricing rule

f=f(r)
, which is a Borel function

f:\;\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}\triangleq\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{{-\infty}\right\}\cup\left\{{\infty}\right\}
.

Definition 6.1
.

An
equilibrium

(Q,f)
is defined by a trading strategy
Q

and a pricing rule
f=f(r)
such that

1.

Given the total order
R=Q+U
, the price
S=f(R)
is

efficient
in the sense that

S=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.V\right\lvert{R}\right).

2.

Given the pricing rule
f=f(r)
, the order
Q

maximizes
insider’s profit:

Q(V-f(Q+U))=\max_{q\in\mathbb{R}}q(V-f(q+U)),

with the convention
0\times\infty=0
.

Remark 6.2
.

Up to minor technical differences, our notion of equilibrium
coincides with the one in
Rochet and Vila 1994
. It differs from the
classical equilibrium from
Kyle 1985
in the ability of the
insider to observe noise traders’ order flow
U
. In the model of

Kyle 1985
, the insider maximizes
\mathbb{E}\left(\left.Q(V-f(Q+U)\right\lvert{V}\right)
over
all
V
-measurable random variable
Q
.

The following result links the existence of equilibrium with the
existence of an optimal map for (
21
) that induces an optimal
plan for (
5
).

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
Y=(U,V)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
and denote
\nu=\Law(Y)
. An
equilibrium
(Q,f)
exists if and only if there is an optimal map

X
for (
21
) such that the law of
(X,Y)
is an optimal
plan for (
5
). Insider’s profit is unique and given by

Q(V-f(Q+U))=-c(X,Y)=-\phi_{G}(U,V),

where

is a maximizer for (
18
).

Moreover, there are equilibrium
(Q,f)
and optimal map

such that the pricing rule

is an
increasing function, the total order
Q+U=R
, and the price

f(Q+U)=S
.

We divide the proof of the theorem into lemmas.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
Y=(U,V)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
,
\nu=\Law(Y)
, and

be an
optimal map for (
21
) such that
S
is
R
-measurable and
the law of
(X,Y)
is an optimal plan for (

). Then there
is an increasing function
f:\;\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
such that

S=f(R)
and
(Q,f)
is an equilibrium with
Q=R-U
.

Proof.

By construction,
S=\mathbb{E}\left(\left.V\right\lvert{R}\right)
. Hence, we only need to verify the
profit maximization condition for the order
Q=R-U
.
Theorem
2.2
yields
G\in\mathfrak{M}
such that
(R,S)\in G

and

(U-R)(V-S)=\min_{(r,s)\in G}(U-r)(V-s)=\phi_{G}(U,V).

Let
P_{1}
be the projection of

on the first or

r
-coordinate. Clearly,
P_{1}
is an interval. As
S
is

R
-measurable, there is an
increasing
function
f=f(r)
on

P_{1}
such that
S=f(R)
and
(r,f(r))\in G
for

. By

We now extend

to an increasing function from
\mathbb{R}
to

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
by setting its values to

on the left and
to

on the right of

. As
\phi_{G}(U,V)>-\infty

yields that

takes values in the closure of

. Under the standing convention:

, we
obtain that

\phi_{G}(U,V)=(U-R)(V-S)=\min_{r\in\mathbb{R}}(U-r)(V-f(r)).

Hence,
(Q,f)
is an equilibrium with

.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
f:\;\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a Borel function and

\phi(y)=\inf_{r\in\mathbb{R}}(y_{1}-r)(y_{2}-f(r))\in[-\infty,0],\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

with the convention:
0\times\infty=0
. Then there is

such that
\phi\leq\phi_{G}
.

Proof.

Given

and deduce that

\displaystyle(1-t)\phi(y^{0})+t\phi(y^{1})

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\min((y^{0}_{1}-r)(y^{0}_{2}-f(r)),0)

\displaystyle\quad+t\min((y^{1}_{1}-r)(y^{1}_{2}-f(r)),0)

\displaystyle\leq t(1-t)(y^{1}_{1}-y^{0}_{1})(y^{1}_{2}-y^{0}_{2}),

where in the middle we used the negative parts to account for the

from Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
Y=(U,V)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
,
\nu=\Law(Y)
, and
(Q,f)
be an

Q(f(Q+U)-V)=(R-U)(S-V)=(\widetilde{R}-U)(S-V).

Proof.

From the definition of the equilibrium we obtain that

\phi(U,V)=Q(f(Q+U)-V)=(R-U)(S-V),

where
\phi(u,v)=\inf_{r\in\mathbb{R}}(u-r)(v-f(r))
,

We claim that

(31)

As the integrability properties of

are unknown, we use a
localization argument. For

from the martingale

\mathbb{E}\left(\left.V\right\lvert{R}\right)=S

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\phi(U,V)1_{\left\{{\left\lvert R\right\rvert\leq n}\right\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(U(V-S)1_{\left\{{\left\lvert R\right\rvert\leq n}\right\}}\right)

Lemma

yields

It follows that

is an optimal plan,

∎

Lemma 6.8

Let

\phi(u,v)=\inf_{r\in\mathbb{R}}(u-r)(v-f(r))\in[-\infty,0],\quad(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

Appendix A
Closure of probability measures with bounded densities in

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Let
p\geq 1
. We denote by
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of Borel
probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite
p
-th moments equipped
with the Wasserstein metric:

W_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\left\{\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int|x-y|^{p}d\gamma\right\}^{1/p},

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the family of Borel probability measures

\gamma
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}=\left\{{(x,y)}:\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}
with

x
-marginal
\mu
and
y
-marginal
\nu
. We recall that

\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a complete separable metric space and that

\mu_{n}\rightarrow\mu
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if and only if

\int f(x)d\mu_{n}\rightarrow\int f(x)d\mu
for every continuous
function
f
with polynomial
p
-th growth:

|f(x)|\leq K(1+|x|^{p}),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
be the
family of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that have bounded
densities with respect to
\nu
:

\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)\triangleq\left\{{\mu\ll\nu}:\;\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\in\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)\subset\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The
following result, used in the proof of our main Theorem
2.2
,
describes the closure of
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
under
W_{p}
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
\nu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then the closure of

\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has the form:

\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)=\left\{{\mu\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:\;\supp{\mu}\subset\supp{\nu}\right\}.

Proof.

If
\mu_{n}\to\mu
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\mu_{n}\to\mu

weakly and thus,

\mu(C)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(C),

for every closed set
C
. In particular, if

(\mu_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
, then

\mu(\supp{\nu})\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}(\supp{\nu})=1

and hence,
\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
. It follows that

\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
is closed in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Clearly,

\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
is convex.

If
\supp{\nu}
is compact, then restricted to
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)

the convergence under
W_{p}
is equivalent to the weak convergence
and thus, the family of probability measures in
\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)

with
finite
support is dense. Being a closed convex set,

\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
is then the closure of

\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if and only if
every Dirac measure
\delta_{y}=\delta_{y}(dx)
concentrated at

y\in\supp{\nu}
is the weak limit of a sequence

(\mu_{n})\subset\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
. The sequence
(\mu_{n})

with

\frac{d\mu_{n}}{d\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{\nu(B_{1/n}(y))}1_{\left\{{x\in B_{1/n}(y)}\right\}},\quad n\geq 1,

where
B_{r}(y)
is the ball of radius
r>0
centered at
y
, has the
required properties.

If
\supp{\nu}
is not compact, we approximate

\mu\in\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
by the sequence
(\mu_{n})
given by

\frac{d\mu_{n}}{d\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(B_{n}(y))}1_{\left\{{x\in B_{n}(y)}\right\}},\quad n\geq 1,

for some
y\in\supp{\mu}
. We have that

(\mu_{n})\subset\mathcal{S}_{p}(\nu)
and
\mu_{n}\to\mu
under

W_{p}
. By what we have already proved, each
\mu_{n}
belongs to the
closure of
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu_{n})
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where

\frac{d\nu_{n}}{d\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{\nu(B_{n}(y))}1_{\left\{{x\in B_{n}(y)}\right\}},\quad n\geq 1.

As
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu_{n})\subset\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
,

n\geq 1
, we deduce that the sequence
(\mu_{n})
belongs to the
closure of
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}(\nu)
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Same
property holds for its
W_{p}
-limit
\mu
and the result follows.
∎

Appendix B
Properties of the function
\phi_{G}

Let
G
be a maximal monotone set:
G\in\mathfrak{M}
. In this
appendix, we collect the properties of the function

\phi=\phi_{G}(y)\triangleq\inf_{x\in G}c(x,y)=\inf_{x\in G}(x_{1}-y_{1})(x_{2}-y_{2}),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

used throughout the paper.

Lemma B.1
.

The function
\phi=\phi_{G}
and its
c
-conjugate

\phi^{c}(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}(c(x,y)-\phi(y)),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

take values in
[-\infty,0]
,
\phi^{c}\leq\phi
, and

G=\left\{{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\phi(y)=0\right\}=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\phi^{c}(x)=0\right\}.

Proof.

Let
y\in G
. As
G\in\mathfrak{M}
, we have that
c(x,y)\geq 0
,

x\in G
, and thus,
\phi(y)=0
. Conversely, if
y\not\in G
, then
the maximal monotone set
G
crosses the interior of either the
upper-left or the lower-right quadrants relative to
y
. If
z\in G

belongs to such intersection, then

\phi(y)\leq c(z,y)=(z_{1}-y_{1})(z_{2}-y_{2})<0.

We have shown that
\phi<0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus G
and
\phi=0
on

G
. It follows that

\phi^{c}(x)\leq\inf_{y\in G}(c(x,y)-\phi(y))=\inf_{y\in G}c(x,y)=\phi(x)\leq 0.

If
\phi^{c}(x)=0
, then
\phi(x)=0
and thus,
x\in G
. Conversely,
if
x\in G
, then
c(x,y)-\phi(y)\geq 0
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
therefore,
\phi^{c}(x)=0
.
∎

We associate with
\phi
the closed convex function

\psi(y)=\psi_{G}(y)=y_{1}y_{2}-\phi(y)=\sup_{x\in G}(x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1}-x_{1}x_{2}),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Clearly,
\phi
and
\psi
have same domains:

\dom{\phi}=\left\{{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\phi(y)>-\infty\right\}=\left\{{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;\psi(y)<\infty\right\}=\dom{\psi}.

For a convex set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\closure{A}
,

Lemma B.2
.

The domain of
\phi
is convex. If

is either horizontal or
vertical line, then
\dom{\phi}=G
. Otherwise,
\dom{\phi}
has a
non-empty interior:

\interior{\dom{\phi}}=\interior{P_{1}}\times\interior{P_{2}},

(32)

where
P_{i}
is the projection of
G
on
x_{i}
-coordinate,

relative interiors of the horizontal and vertical parts of

\boundary{\dom{\phi}}
containing

\dom{\phi}

Proof.

Being convex, the function
\psi=\psi_{G}
has convex domain. As

\dom{\phi}=\dom{\psi}
, the domain of
\phi
is also convex.

We observe that
P_{i}
is either a point or an interval. If

P_{1}=\left\{{a_{1}}\right\}
, then

is a vertical line:

G=\left\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}:\;x_{1}=a_{1}\right\}
. For

\left\lvert y_{1}-a_{1}\right\rvert>0

\phi(y)=\inf_{x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}(y_{1}-a_{1})(y_{2}-x_{2})=-\infty.

Thus,
\dom\phi=G

P_{2}
is a point and thus,
G

is a horizontal line is identical.

We assume now that
\interior{P_{i}}=(a_{i},b_{i})

\phi(y)=\inf_{x\in G}c(x,y)=\inf_{x\in C}c(x,y)>-\infty.

Conversely, suppose that

does not belong to the closure of

We have proved (

For the last assertion of the lemma, we assume that

y=(a_{1},y_{2})
and

\phi(z)>-\infty
. Indeed,
otherwise there is a sequence
(x^{n})\subset G
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(a_{1}-x_{1}^{n})(z_{2}-x_{2}^{n})=-\infty.

Since
z_{2}<b_{2}
, the sequence
(x_{1}^{n})
is bounded and

x_{2}^{n}\rightarrow a_{2}=-\infty
. It follows that

\phi(y)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}(a_{1}-x_{1}^{n})(y_{2}-z_{2})+\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(a_{1}-x_{1}^{n})(z_{2}-x_{2}^{n})=-\infty,

and we obtain a contradiction.
