Near-optimal inference in adaptive linear regression
Koulik Khamarulabel=e1]kk1241@stat.rutgers.edu, Yash Deshpandelabel=e2, mark]ydeshpande@voleon.com, Tor Lattimorelabel=e3, mark]tor.lattimore@gmail.com, Lester Mackeylabel=e4, mark]lmackey@microsoft.com, Martin J. Wainwrightlabel=e5, mark]wainwrigwork@gmal.com

Abstract
When data is collected in an adaptive manner, even simple methods like ordinary least squares can exhibit non-normal asymptotic behavior. As an undesirable consequence, hypothesis tests and confidence intervals based on asymptotic normality can lead to erroneous results. We propose a family of online debiasing estimators to correct these distributional anomalies in least squares estimation. Our proposed methods take advantage of the covariance structure present in the dataset and provide sharper estimates in directions for which more information has accrued. We establish an asymptotic normality property for our proposed online debiasing estimators under mild conditions on the data collection process and provide asymptotically exact confidence intervals. We additionally prove a minimax lower bound for the adaptive linear regression problem, thereby providing a baseline by which to compare estimators. There are various conditions under which our proposed estimators achieve the minimax lower bound. We demonstrate the usefulness of our theory via applications to multi-armed bandit, autoregressive time series estimation, and active learning with exploration.

Introduction

Consider a prediction problem in which we observe
n
datapoints
of the form
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\bf{R}^{d}\times\bf{R}
with covariate
vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and response
y_{i}
linked via the linear model

\displaystyle y_{i}=\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\big\rangle+\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\quad\mbox{for $i=1,\ldots,n$.}

(1)

Here the vector
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\in\bf{R}^{d}
is an unknown parameter of
interest, and
\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}
is additive noise. When the datapoints are
generated via some i.i.d. sampling process, this model, and in
particular the behavior of the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimate

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}
, is very well-understood. The focus of this paper is the
more challenging setting in which the covariate vectors
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

have been
adaptively
collected, meaning that the choice of

\mathbf{x}_{i}
can depend on the entire set of previous observations
\{\mathbf{x}_{j},y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
.

More precisely, given a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, assume
that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
-measurable and
that the additive error
\{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a martingale difference
sequence with respect to
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, with

\displaystyle\Exs[\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Exs[\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}^{2}\lvert\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=\sigma^{2},

(2)

for some non-random scalar
\sigma^{2}>0
. We refer to the
combination of the linear observation
model (
1
) with such (potentially) adaptive
collection procedures as the
adaptive linear regression model
.
Instances of adaptive linear regression arise in a variety of
applications, including multi-armed
bandits
[
20
]
, active
learning
[
9
]
, times series
modeling
[
4
]
, stochastic
control
[
1
]
, and adaptive stochastic
approximation schemes
[
18
,
6
]
.

Let us discuss some known results for the OLS estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}
.
The estimate can be expanded in the form

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}=\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{n}^{\top}y_{n}={\bm{\theta}^{*}}+\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=11}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i},\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{S}_{n}:\,=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}.

(3)

This decomposition reveals that the statistical properties of the OLS
estimate depend on the martingale transform
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}
,
along with the random matrix
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. There is a lengthy
literature on conditions under which the OLS estimate is
consistent
[
18
,
1
,
4
,
10
,
16
,
17
]
.
Notably,
Lai and Wei 1982
show that the OLS estimate is
strongly consistent, meaning that
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{a.s.}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
,
whenever

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{a.s.}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\log\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}_{n})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S}_{n})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{a.s.}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(4)

Arguably, these conditions for consistency are quite mild. In
contrast,
Lai and Wei 1982
also show that asymptotic
normality of the least squares estimator in the adaptive linear
regression model holds under a stability condition that is
substantially more restrictive—namely, the existence of a sequence

\{\mathbf{B}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of
non-random
strictly
positive definite matrices such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{p}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{I}.

(5)

Moreover,
Lai and Wei 1982
demonstrate through the
example of a unit root autoregressive model that the OLS estimator
fails to be asymptotically normal in absence of the stability
property (
5
). In such cases, confidence
intervals and other forms of inference performed using Gaussian limit
theory are no longer valid.

Contributions

In this paper, we propose and analyze a new family of estimators for
the parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
(or linear functionals thereof)
based on online debiasing techniques. We show that, under mild
conditions, our proposed estimators are both asymptotically unbiased
and asymptotically normal. The underlying assumptions are less
stringent than the stability condition (
5
)
and are satisfied by a large class of models for data generation and
protocols for choosing covariate vectors. We provide a detailed
discussion of three such example classes in
Section

4
.
By deriving minimax lower bounds on the performance of any estimator,
we show that our estimators are minimax optimal. We also show that
the asymptotic performance of these estimators are near-optimal in an
instance dependent sense, in that they match the performance of the
best problem-specific behavior up to a logarithmic factor.

Related work

The broader literature on bandit algorithms and experimentation
focuses mostly on a single statistical objective, with standard
examples being minimizing regret or selecting an optimal arm with high
probability. In the papers
[
29
,
26
]
,
the authors empirically observed that bandit algorithms induce bias,
which can be problematic for ex-post inference. Later
works
[
21
,
22
,
23
]

characterize the sign and bound the magnitude of this bias. In the
paper
[
11
]
, the authors develop estimators that
use propensity scores for the multi-armed bandit setting, a special
case of the stochastic regression model (
1
)
in which the covariate vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are restricted to standard basis
vectors. However, it is not clear how to extend this approach to
general designs. Also in the bandit setting, Zhang et
al.
[
30
]
develop a least squares estimator that
exploits an assumed batch structure, meaning that only a fixed, finite
number of adaptive decisions are made. This approach, however, does
not apply to more general schemes that make adaptive decisions at each
round. Recently, Zhang et al.
[
31
]
proposed a
weighted
M-estimator
for contextual bandit problems where the
bandit algorithm is known. It is also not clear how to generalize this
approach to a more general data collection scheme or to the case when
the data collection algorithm is not completely known.

There is also a parallel line of work that exploits concentration of
measure results (e.g., see the papers
[
3
,
27
]
) to
develop confidence regions that are valid uniformly in time. This
approach has its roots in the bandits
literature
[
2
,
13
]
and has been
refined in more recent
work
[
12
,
14
]
. An advantage of
this approach is that it yields bounds that are uniform in time. On
the flip side, it requires very strong exponential tail conditions on
the error sequence in contrast to the relatively mild moment conditions
that we impose. Overall, we view this line of work as being
complementary to our goal of developing corrected estimators that obey
asymptotic normality.

This paper builds upon and extends past work, due to a subset of the
current authors
[
6
,
5
]
,
using online debiasing techniques. In
2
,
we prove a lower bound that shows how the matrix sequence

\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}
controls the fundamental difficulty of the
problem, and this lower bound also motivates the particular form of
debiasing proposed in this paper. The construction used in past
work
[
6
,
5
]
is based on a
non-adaptive upper bound of the form
\lambda_{*}\mathbf{I}
, where the
scalar
\lambda_{*}
is chosen to be much larger than

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1})
with high probability. By
sharp contrast, our analysis instead makes use of an adaptive upper
bound that simultaneously respects the structure of

\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}
and leads to a stable martingale transform;
this particular construction and our analysis thereof allows us to
obtain sharper guarantees than past
work
[
6
,
5
]
.

Notation

Let us summarize some notation used throughout
the remainder of the paper. For a positive integer
n
, we make use
of the convenient shorthand
[n]:\,=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. We use

\mathbf{e}_{j}
to denote the
j
th standard basis vector in

\bf{R}^{d}
. For a matrix
\mathbf{M}
, we use the notation

|\!|\!|\mathbf{M}|\!|\!|_{{\scriptsize{\operatorname{op}}}}
and
|\!|\!|\mathbf{M}|\!|\!|_{{\scriptsize{\operatorname{F}}}}
to denote the
operator norm (maximum singular value) and the Frobenius norm of the
matrix
\mathbf{M}
, respectively; similarly, we use the notation

\|\mathbf{M}\|_{\scriptsize{\operatorname{max}}}
to denote the maximum entry in absolute
value. For a square matrix
\mathbf{S}
, the quantities

\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S})
and
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S})

respectively denote the maximum and minimum eigenvalue of the matrix

\mathbf{S}
. The quantity
\tr(\mathbf{S})
denotes the sum of
diagonal entries of the square matrix
\mathbf{S}
. For a pair of
squares matrices
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
of compatible
dimensions, we use the notation
\mathbf{A}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{B}
to indicate that the difference matrix
\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}
is positive semidefinite; we use the notation

\mathbf{A}\succ\mathbf{B}
when the difference matrix

\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}
is positive definite. The relations

\mathbf{A}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{A}\prec\mathbf{B}
are defined analogously. For a symmetric positive
semidefinite matrix
\mathbf{S}
, we use
\mathbf{S}^{\frac{1}{2}}
to
denote its symmetric matrix square root.

For a sequence of random variables
\{Z_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

and a random variable
Z
, we write
Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{p}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z
to mean that the sequence
of random variables
\{Z_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
converges to

Z
in probability; the notation
Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z

indicates convergence in distribution. For a sequence of real-valued
random variables
\{Z_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and a sequence of
non-zero real numbers
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, we
write
Z_{n}=o_{p}(a_{n})
to mean that the
ratio
\tfrac{Z_{n}}{a_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{p}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. We write
Z_{n}=O_{p}(a_{n})
to mean that the ratio

Z_{n}/a_{n}
is stochastically bounded. More
precisely, for every scalar
\epsilon>0
, there exits a positive real
number
C_{\epsilon}
such that
\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\,\mathbb{P}[Z_{n}/a_{n}>C_{\epsilon}]<\epsilon
.

classical theory about ordinary least squares estimate can break down
when data is collected in an adaptive manner. We then introduce an
online debiasing approach to computing alternative estimates.

[
20
]
.

In Section

2.2
Online debiasing estimator

In this section, we propose and analyze an estimator based on an
online debiasing technique motivated by the
work of
Deshpande
et al. 2018
. At a high-level, the estimator
involves a specific perturbation of the ordinary least squares
estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}
. This perturbation is constructed via a linear
combination of the prediction errors
\{y_{i}-\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}\big\rangle\}_{i=1}^{n}
along with a carefully chosen
sequence of weight vectors
\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The key property ensured by the
construction is that the weight vector
\mathbf{w}_{i}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i-1}

measurable for each
i\in[n]
.

Concretely, for weight vectors
\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we
compute the
online debiasing estimate

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}

\displaystyle:\,=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}+\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{w}_{i}(y_{i}-\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}\big\rangle).

(6)

Here the reader should recall our earlier definition

\mathbf{S}_{n}:\,=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
, and
throughout, we assume that the sample covariance
\mathbf{S}_{n}

is invertible. The matrix
\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
denotes a
symmetric matrix square root of
\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}
.

Of course, there is an infinite family of estimators of the
form (
6
), and the key question is how to
define the weight vectors. In this paper, we propose an estimator in
which the sequence
\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is obtained by solving an
optimization problem that takes three inputs:

(i)

the original data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,

(ii)

a non-random scalar
\gamma_{n}\in(0,1]
,
and

(iii)

a sequence of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices

\{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
for each
i\in[n]:\,=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

In order to simplify notation, we adopt the shorthand
\mathbf{z}_{i}:\,=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Moreover, for each index
i\in[n]
,
we define the matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}:\,=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}_{1}&\mathbf{z}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{z}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{W}_{i}:\,=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{1}&\mathbf{w}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{w}_{i}\end{bmatrix}

We also define
\mathbf{W}_{0}=0
and
\mathbf{Z}_{0}=0
. With these definitions,
the vectors
\{\mathbf{w}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are obtained recursively by solving the
following convex program

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{i}

\displaystyle:\,=\arg\min_{\mathbf{w}\in\bf{R}^{d}}\left\{|\!|\!|\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{i-1}\mathbf{Z}_{i-1}-\mathbf{w}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}|\!|\!|_{{\scriptsize{\operatorname{F}}}}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\quad\mbox{for $i=1,2,\ldots,n$.}

(7a)

Conveniently, this optimization problem has the explicit solution

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{i-1}\mathbf{Z}_{i-1})\mathbf{z}_{i}}{(\gamma_{n}/2)+\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(7b)

Main results

Having motivated and introduced the online debiasing approach, we now
turn to some theoretical guarantees that can be given for these
methods. We begin in
Section

3.1
by providing sufficient
conditions for the online debiasing estimator of
Section

2.2

to exhibit asymptotically Gaussian behavior
(
1
). In
Section

3.2
, we
provide an asymptotically exact confidence region for
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
as
well as an asymptotically exact confidence interval
(Proposition
1
) for
{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
, where

{\mathbf{v}}
is an arbitrary fixed direction
{\mathbf{v}}\in\bf{R}^{d}
.
In
Section

3.3
—in particular,
see
2
—we complement these results by
providing minimax lower bounds on a family of Mahalanobis errors and
the length of confidence intervals. These lower bounds apply to any
estimator for the stochastic regression model which does not know the

{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
but may have the full
knowledge of how the data was collected. Finally,
in
Section

we provide general strategies which can
be used to verify the conditions of

. All
of our asymptotic statements assume that the dimension

is fixed
(constant) while the sample size

grows.

3.1
Asymptotic normality guarantees

The main result of this section is an asymptotic normality guarantee
for the proposed estimator (
6
), where the
weight vectors are defined via the
recursion (
7
).

We begin by stating our assumptions and providing some intuition about
their role in the theorem.

Assumption A

(A1)

There are positive scalars
\sigma
and

\Delta
such that the noise sequence
\{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
satisfies
the conditions
\Exs[\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=0
and
\Exs[\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]=\sigma^{2}
for all

i\in[n]
and moreover

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n]}\;\Exs[\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}^{2+\Delta}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1}]<\infty.

(A2)

The sequence of matrices
\{\mathbf{S}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

satisfy the conditions
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S}_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{a.s.}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
and
\frac{\log\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}_{n})}{\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S}_{n})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{a.s.}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

(A3)

For each
n
, the scalar
\gamma_{n}>0
and
positive semidefinite matrices
\{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with

\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
are chosen such that:

\displaystyle\max\limits_{i\in[n]}\;\;\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\big\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{p}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

(a) Asymptotic negligibility:

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{n}\log\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{S}_{n})}\cdot|\!|\!|\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{n}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\!|\!|_{{\scriptsize{\operatorname{op}}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{p}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,\quad\mbox{and}

(b) Vanishing bias:

\displaystyle|\!|\!|\mathbf{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{w}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\!|\!|_{{\scriptsize{\operatorname{op}}}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{p}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(c) Variance stability:

Let us provide some intuition for the role of each of these
assumptions in the theorem. First, Assumption
(A1)
is
quite simple: it imposes relatively mild moment conditions on the
noise variables. Second, as discussed in the introduction,
Assumption
(A2)
is standard in guaranteeing the
consistency of the least squares estimate. Both
Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
are viewed as
mild conditions in the stochastic linear regression literature and are
satisfied by many practical models including those studied in the
papers
[
16
,
18
,
15
,
6
]
.
Note that Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)

concern the regression model itself as opposed to the method: in
particular, they do not depend on the algorithm parameters

\gamma_{n}
and
\{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

The more subtle requirements for our theorem to apply, which
do
depend
on the algorithm parameters, are stated in
Assumption
(A3)
. We discuss the technical role of
these conditions in the comments after
1
, to
be stated momentarily. In
Section

3.4
to follow, we
provide concrete choices of the algorithm parameters

\gamma_{n}
and
\{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
that ensure that
Assumption
(A3)
holds.

Finally, it should be noted that Assumption
(A3)
is
weaker than the stability condition (
5
).
Indeed, if the stability condition (
5
) and
the growth condition
(A2)
are satisfied, then we may
take
\gamma_{n}=1
,
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i}=\mathbf{B}_{n}
and
\mathbf{w}_{i}=\mathbf{B}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}_{i}
. With these choices, the
conditions
(A3)
are automatically satisfied; moreover,
in this particular case, the online debiased estimator reduces to
ordinary least squares; see
3
for
details.

With these preliminaries in
place, we are now equipped to state our main theorem on the online
debiasing estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}
:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}

Let us now discuss how Assumption
(A3)
enters the proof of

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}})

(10a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{n}

\displaystyle:\,=\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}\cdot\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{W}_{n}\mathbf{X}_{n}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}})\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{n}

The random vector

3.2
Obtaining confidence regions and intervals

In this section, we use the online
debiasing procedure to obtain asymptotically exact confidence regions
and intervals.

3.2.1
Confidence region for
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}

First, for some user-defined level
\alpha\in(0,1)

problem of finding a confidence region for
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
—that is, a
(random) set
\mathbf{A}_{1-\alpha}

with
probability at least

small as possible, asymptotically exact in the sense that its coverage
converges to
1-\alpha

Theorem
1
allows us to construct such a set in
the following straightforward way. For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
consider the subset of
\bf{R}^{d}
given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{1-\alpha}=\left\{\mathbf{\theta}\in\bf{R}^{d}\mid\frac{\gamma_{n}}{{\widehat{\sigma}}^{2}}\cdot(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}-\theta)^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{n}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}-\theta)\leq\chi^{2}_{d,1-\alpha}\right\}

where

denotes the
(1-\alpha

for a standard chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom

From the result of Theorem

guarantee

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}({\bm{\theta}^{*}}\in\mathbf{A}_{1-\alpha})=1-\alpha,

In many applications, however, instead of a confidence region for the
full vector
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
, we are instead interested in obtaining a
confidence interval for the scalar quantity
{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
,
where
{\mathbf{v}}\in\bf{R}^{d}
is a fixed direction. It turns out that
Theorem
1
no longer provides a straightforward
answer to this question. In order to understand why, it is useful to
begin by following a naive line of reasoning that is incorrect
and then show how it can be fixed.

3.2.2
An incorrect argument

In order to obtain a confidence interval for
{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
,
it might be tempting to “directly invert” the distributional
property (
9
). In particular, letting

z_{1-(\alpha/2)}:\,=\Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2})
denote the
1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}
quantile of
the standard Gaussian distribution, we might claim that the interval

\displaystyle\left[\big\langle{\mathbf{v}},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}\big\rangle-\tfrac{{\widehat{\sigma}}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}(\big\langle{\mathbf{v}},\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{v}}\big\rangle)^{\frac{1}{2}}z_{1-\alpha/2},\qquad\big\langle{\mathbf{v}},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}\big\rangle+\tfrac{{\widehat{\sigma}}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}(\big\langle{\mathbf{v}},\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{v}}\big\rangle)^{\frac{1}{2}}z_{1-\alpha/2}\right],

{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}

Unfortunately, the conclusion (

logic, namely the assertion that the asymptotic
guarantee (

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}{{\widehat{\sigma}}}\cdot(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}})\;-\;\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(12)

It is now interesting to understand when the implication above follows from Theorem

Under the stability condition

for a sequence of deterministic matrices

by Slutsky’s theorem.
However, in the absence of this stability condition, the sample covariance matrix remains random and
may depend on
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}
.

The following counterexample shows that when a sequence of random vectors
\mathbf{b}_{n}
and matrices
\mathbf{A}_{n}
are dependent,
\mathbf{A}_{n}\mathbf{b}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
does not imply
\mathbf{b}_{n}-\mathcal{N}(0,(\mathbf{A}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{n})^{-1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\operatorname{d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
in general.
Let
X,Y
be independent standard Gaussian random variables. Consider the vector
\mathbf{b}=(\textrm{sign}(X),\textrm{sign}(Y))^{\top}
, and a
2\times 2
matrix
\mathbf{A}
with
\mathbf{A}_{11}=|X|/\sqrt{2},\mathbf{A}_{12}=|Y|/\sqrt{2},A_{21}=-|X|/\sqrt{2},\mathbf{A}_{22}=|Y|/\sqrt{2}

\mathbf{Ab}=(X+Y,\;X-Y)/\sqrt{2}\sim\mathcal{N}\left((0,0)^{\top},\mathbf{I}\right)

\mathbf{b}
is
\pm 1
with probability
1/2
. Additionally,
the variable
\mathcal{N}((0,0)^{\top}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A})^{-1})
is a scale mixture of Gaussians — a continuous distribution —- and therefore does not match the distribution of
\mathbf{b}
.
In summary, we conclude that additional justification is needed to guarantee
the asymptotic validity of the confidence interval (
11

{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
in general.

Nonetheless, there are certain special cases in which the
interval (
11

{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{e}_{j}
is one of the standard coordinate basis vectors and that

\mathbf{S}_{n}
is diagonal as in the multi-armed bandit setting
studied in
Section

of
Section

A.1
show that the interval (
11
)
is valid. More generally, given an arbitrary direction
{\mathbf{v}}
, our
strategy will be to run a variant of online debiasing that effectively
reduces the problem to this favorable case.

3.2.4
Comparison with least squares confidence intervals

An interesting consequence of the results so far, is that we can construct a confidence intervals based on any consistent estimator
{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}
of
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}

\widehat{\theta}_{1}
for

Comments on part (a): instance-dependent lower bound on MSE

In order to gain intuition for the MSE bound in part (a), it is
helpful to begin with the simplest case—that is, the non-adaptive
setting. Consider the classical problem of fixed design linear
regression, in which the covariates (and hence
\mathbf{S}_{n}

are viewed as fixed, and the additive noise is zero-mean Gaussian with
variance
\sigma^{2}

{\mathcal{N}}({\bm{\theta}^{*}},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1})

Consequently, for any fixed matrix
{\mathbf{M}}

\displaystyle{\Exs}\lVert{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rVert^{2}_{{\mathbf{M}}}=\sigma^{2}\Exs[\tr(\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}{\mathbf{M}})].

(22)

This simple calculation shows that the lower
bound (
21a

Of course, the more substantive content of
Theorem

(a) lies in the fact that it
allows for adaptive data collection, along with potentially random
choices of
{\mathbf{M}}
. One interesting choice is the random matrix

{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{S}_{n}
, for which the
bound (
21a
) guarantees that
{\Exs}[\lVert{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rVert_{\mathbf{S}_{n}}^{2}]\geq\sigma^{2}d

It is worth comparing this lower bound to
1
.
From the arguments used to prove this theorem, and under a

mildly stronger
version of

(A3)
—which the convergence in distribution
conditions are replaced by convergence in

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\gamma_{n}\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{OD}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{n}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}d.

(23)

See
Section

5.1.1
for the details of this argument.

As discussed in
Section

4
to follow, in many practical
problems of interest, the tuning parameter
\gamma_{n}

also our choice of the tuning parameters ensure that the
aforementioned stronger version of assumption

satisfied. Consequently, the result (

combined with the lower bound (

Comments on part (b): minimax lower bound on MSE

Part (b) of Theorem
2
provides a minimax
lower bound on the MSE. It shows that in the adaptive setting, the
scaling of
\gamma_{n}
needs to at least
\log(n)

this logarithmic scaling is unavoidable when dimension

discussed in the last comment, for many problems, we can take

\log(n)

not
true
when

. In this case the logarithmic dependence on

becomes doubly logarithmic, as is consistent with the law of the
iterated logarithm that underlies this behavior in that case.

Comments on part (c): bounds on lengths of CIs

2

of any confidence interval for the scalar
{\mathbf{v}}^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
that
is valid when the data set is collected in an adaptive manner. To the
best of our knowledge, this is the first result providing a lower
bound on the width of confidence intervals in an adaptive setting.

3.4
Choices of the tuning parameters

\gamma_{n}

In the absence of any additional assumption on the data collection

4
Applications

We next illustrate the concrete consequences of our results.
Sections
4.1
and
4.2
are devoted to
multi-armed bandit problems and autoregressive time series models,
respectively, while
Section

4.3
discusses active
learning with exploration. We end each section with an empirical
evaluation of online debiasing. Specifically, we compare the
confidence interval (CI) coverage and width of four methods: our
online debiasing estimator (
6
),
OLS (
3
) with standard but potentially invalid
Gaussian intervals, the
W
-decorrelation estimator

of
Deshpande
et al. 2018
, and a valid CI based on the
concentration inequality of
Abbasi-Yadkori, Pál and
Szepesvári 2011
. We highlight
that the CIs for OLS are based on the distributional assumption

\mathbf{S}_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}-{\bm{\theta}^{*}})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
. This property, while true when the covariates
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are selected in a non-adaptive manner, need not hold for
adaptively collected
covariates
[
6

30
]
, and as a
consequence, the corresponding CIs need not give the correct coverage.
Meanwhile, the valid concentration inequality-based
intervals
[
2
]
are guaranteed to provide at least
the nominal coverage but are often unnecessarily wide.

4.1
Multi-armed bandits

Consider a multi-armed bandit with
d

At each time

5

6

20

5

[
30

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{i}

(29)

\displaystyle N_{\min}\geq(\log n)^{2}.

(30)

6

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{(\log n)\cdot\log\log(n)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{\Gamma}_{i,n}=\max\left\{\mathbf{S}_{i},\;\;(\log n)^{2}\cdot\mathbf{I}_{d}\right\},

Our next example involves estimating the parameters of an autoregressive

versions of such processes that are unstable

18

More precisely, given the initial point
y_{0}=0

\theta^{*}\in(-1,1]

\displaystyle y_{i}=\theta^{*}y_{i-1}+\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\quad\mbox{for $i=1,\ldots,n$.}

33

\theta^{*}=1

[
4

With the choice
\theta^{*}=1

33

denotes the standard Wiener process (see the

[
28

for details). Put simply, in the autoregressive time series

4.3
Active learning with exploration

In our third example, we focus on the case where the covariates

\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are generated using any algorithm satisfying a sufficient exploration property.

Definition 4.1

(Selection algorithms with
\varepsilon
-exploration)
.

We say that a selection algorithm
\Psi_{n}=\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
admits a
\varepsilon
-exploration property
if

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}:\,=\begin{cases}u_{i}&\text{with probability $1-\varepsilon_{i}$ for some $u_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$, and}\\
v_{i}&\mbox{with probability $\varepsilon_{i}$ for some $v_{i}$
independent of $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$.}\end{cases}

(37)

Here the exploration probability sequence
\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

consists of nonnegative scalars in the interval
(0,1)
, and the
vectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are i.i.d. random vectors such that

\displaystyle\Exs[v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}]\succeq\mathbf{G}\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{G}\succeq\mathbf{0}

(38)

In words, the selection algorithm
\psi_{i}
behaves as follows:
with probability
1-\varepsilon_{i}
, it chooses vector
u_{i}

based on the previous data points
\{(\mathbf{x}_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
, and
with probability
\varepsilon_{i}
, it chooses a random direction

v_{i}
, independent of the previous data points.

Example:
\varepsilon
-greedy linear bandits

Let us briefly consider a concrete instance of a selection algorithm

\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
that is of the
\varepsilon
-greedy type. In
the linearly parameterized bandit problem, at each time
i\in[n]
, an algorithm
\psi_{i}
chooses an action vector

\mathbf{x}_{i}
, usually lying within some bounded set
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, and
obtains a reward
y_{i}=\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\big\rangle+\epsilon_{i}
.
A popular and simple strategy for regret minimization is a special
case of the
\varepsilon
-greedy selection
algorithm
[
20
]
. For linearly parameterized
bandits, the selection algorithm
\psi_{i}
chooses

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}\;\;

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\in\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\quad\big\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\text{ridge}}^{(i-1)}\big\rangle&\mbox{with probability
$1-\varepsilon_{i}$, and}\\
\sim{\mathrm{Unif}}(\mathcal{A}_{i})&\mbox{with probability
$\varepsilon_{i}$.}\end{cases}

(39)

where
\hat{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\text{ridge}}^{(i-1)}
denotes the ridge regression estimator
based on all data observed up to stage
i-1
, i.e., the collection
of covariate-response pairs
\{(\mathbf{x}_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{i-1}
. Put
simply, with probability
1-\varepsilon_{i}
, the selection algorithm

\psi_{i}
chooses an optimal action given data collected so far
(exploitation), and with probability
\varepsilon_{i}
, the algorithm
randomizes uniformly amongst its choices (exploration). In the more
general setting (
37
) considered
here, it is not necessary to select the optimal action in the
exploitation step. Rather, our result holds also when an arbitrary,

\mathcal{F}_{i-1}
-measurable choice is made in the first part
of
Equation

39
, as in the EXP3 or UCB algorithms with
exploration. See the book
[
20
]
for more
details.
\diamondsuit

Returning to our general
setting (
37
), we now state a
guarantee for selection algorithms with
\varepsilon
-exploration. As
is standard in the bandit literature, we assume that the covariates
are uniformly bounded, so that there exists a scalar
K
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq K\quad\mbox{for all $i\in[n]$.}

(40a)

See our discussion following the corollary for how this condition can
be relaxed. In addition, we impose a
sufficient exploration

Our proof follows a standard Bayesian argument.
Consider a randomly generated vector
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
that is equal to

\bm{0}
with probability
1/2
, and otherwise sampled from a multivariate
Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu=(\bm{0},1)^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and degenerate
covariance

\displaystyle\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}\,.

Lower bounding the supremum by an expectation over this prior, the
minimax risk can be lower bounded as

\displaystyle\inf_{{\widehat{U}}}\sup_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}}{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}}\left[\frac{(\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle-{\widehat{U}})^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}}\right]\geq\inf_{{\widehat{U}}}{\Exs}\left[\frac{(\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle-{\widehat{U}})^{2}}{\|{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}}\right]\,.

(52)

where the second expectation integrates over randomness in

{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
as well as the observations
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
.

We now provide a sequential definition of the selection algorithm that
yields the claimed lower bound. Each covariate
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is supported on
the last coordinate as well as one of the first
d-1
coordinates, chosen in round-robin fashion.
Let
u_{i}=1+(i\operatorname{mod}(d-1))
and
v_{i}=\left\lceil i/(d-1)\right\rceil
, which are chosen so that

\displaystyle\{u_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}=\{1,2,\ldots,d-1,1,2,\ldots\}\text{ and }\{v_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}=\{\underbrace{1,1,\ldots,1}_{d-1\text{ times}},2,2,\ldots\}\,.

In other words,
u_{i}
is the index of the first non-zero coordinate in
\mathbf{x}_{i}

and
v_{i}
is the number of times coordinate
u_{i}
was non-zero in rounds
j\leq i
.
The first
d-1
coordinates of the covariate process are deterministic and the last coordinate is chosen adaptively to maximize the difficulty of estimation. Precisely,
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}:\,=b_{v_{i}}e_{u_{i}}+a_{u_{i},v_{i}}e_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,,

where

b_{v}:\,=v^{-1/4}/\sqrt{d}
, and the random sequence
\{a_{u,v}\}_{u\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\},v\geq 1}
is to
defined momentarily.
Let
y_{u,w}:\,=y_{u+(w-1)(d-1)}
, which is the observed response in the round
i
where coordinate
u
was non-zero for the
v
th time. Define

\displaystyle m_{u,v}:\,=\sum_{w=1}^{v}b_{w}(y_{u,w}-a_{u,w}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad d_{v}:\,=1+\sum_{w=1}^{v}b_{w}^{2}.

Then
a_{u,v}:\,=-b_{v}m_{u,v-1}/d_{v-1}
, noting that
a_{u,1}=0
for all
u
.

To provide some intuition, our construction is designed so as to make
estimation challenging. Let
\mathbf{\epsilon}_{u,v}=\mathbf{\epsilon}_{u+(v-1)(d-1)}
. On the event that
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq\bm{0}
, we
have the equality
y_{u,v}=a_{u,v}+\theta^{*}_{u}b_{v}+\epsilon_{u,v}
, and
the ratio
m_{u,v}/d_{v}
is the ridge regression estimate of

\theta^{*}_{u}
. For any vector
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq 0
, the choice
\mathbf{x}_{i}
ensures that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle=a_{u_{i},v_{i}}+b_{v_{i}}\theta^{*}_{u_{i}}\approx 0=\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\bm{0}\rangle\,,

so that the observed responses are extremely similarly under either of
the events
\{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}=\bm{0}\}
or
\{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq\bm{0}\}
.
But
\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle=\bm{1}({\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq\bm{0})
, which means that any estimator of
\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle
must have large
error in expectation. What is missing is to formalize the above claims
and show that
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}^{-1}_{n}}
shrinks suitably fast.

Returning the proof, since
\|{\mathbf{v}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}
is

\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable, the infimum on the right-hand side of
equation (
52
) is achieved by the estimator

\displaystyle{\widehat{U}}_{n}:\,={\Exs}[\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle|\mathcal{F}_{n}]=\mathbb{P}\left({\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq\bm{0}|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right).

Therefore, introducing the event
\mathcal{E}=\{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq\bm{0}\}
,
we have the lower bound

Risk

\displaystyle\geq{\Exs}\Biggr[\frac{\big(\mathbb{P}\big[{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\neq\bm{0}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}\big]-\langle e_{d},{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\rangle\big)^{2}}{\|e_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}}\Biggr]={\Exs}\Biggr[\frac{{\widehat{U}}_{n}(1-{\widehat{U}}_{n})}{\|e_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}}\Biggr]\geq\frac{1}{2}{\Exs}\Biggr[\frac{{\widehat{U}}_{n}(1-{\widehat{U}}_{n})}{\|e_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}}\Bigg|\mathcal{E}\Biggr]\,.

In the remainder of the proof, we study the laws of
{\widehat{U}}_{n}
and

\smash{\|e_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}}
under the measure

\mathbb{P}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{E})
. For a sequence of random variables
X_{n}
,
we use the notation
X_{n}=\Omega_{p}(a_{n})
to mean that
\sup_{n}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}/a_{n}<C_{\epsilon}\mid\mathcal{E})<\epsilon
. We will show below that

\displaystyle{\widehat{U}}_{n}(1-{\widehat{U}}_{n})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{p}(1),\qquad\mbox{and}

(53a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|e_{d}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{p}(d\log(n/d^{3}))\,.

(53b)

Therefore, there exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for
n\geq d^{3}/C
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(1/\|e_{d}\|_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}^{2}\geq Cd\log(n)\right)\geq 3/4\text{ and }\mathbb{P}\left({\widehat{U}}_{n}(1-{\widehat{U}}_{n})\geq C\right)\geq 3/4\,.

By a union bound and the positivity in the integrand of the risk,

Risk

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C^{2}d\log(n/d^{3})}{4}\,.

It remains to show that the lower bounds (
53a
)
and (
53b
) hold, which we prove in Appendix
F
.

5.2.3
Proof of
2
(c)

Let
\Exs_{\bm{\theta}}
denote expectation over a data set drawn from the
distribution indexed by
\bm{\theta}
, and define
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}
as the
analogous probability. For a given dataset based on
n

samples, consider a confidence interval of the form
[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]
. Introducing the shorthand
\xi_{n}^{2}:\,=v^{T}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}v
, we then define the minimax risk

\displaystyle{\sf Risk}

\displaystyle:\,=\inf_{[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]}\sup_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\bigg\{\frac{\widehat{u}_{n}-\widehat{\ell}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}\bigg\},

where we take the infimum over all estimators
[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]
such that

\mathbb{P}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}}\big([\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]\ni v^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\big)\geq 1-\alpha
for each value of
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
.

By the usual Bayesian argument, for any prior distribution
\pi
on
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
, we have the lower
bound

\displaystyle{\sf Risk}\geq\inf_{[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]}{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\bigg[\frac{\widehat{u}_{n}-\widehat{\ell}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}\bigg].

We now obtain a further lower bound by enlarging the space of possible
estimators
[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]
, in particular requiring only that
[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]

belong to the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle:\,=\Big\{[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]\;\mid\;\Exs_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\big([\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]\ni v^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\big)\geq 1-\alpha\Big\}.

Since this allows for a larger collection of possible estimators, we
have the lower bound

\displaystyle{\sf Risk}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]\in\mathcal{A}}{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\bigg\{\frac{\widehat{u}_{n}-\widehat{\ell}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}\bigg\}.

We are now free to choose the prior. In particular, we set
\pi

equal to the density
\phi_{\rho}
of the Gaussian random vector

\mathcal{N}(0,\rho^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d})
. From our previous calculations (
5
) we have that conditional on the observed
data, the random vector
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
is Gaussian with
covariance
(\mathbf{S}_{n}/\sigma^{2}+\mathbf{I}_{d}/\rho^{2})^{-1}
. With this
choice, the random variable
v^{\top}{\bm{\theta}^{*}}
, conditioned on
the observed data, is a Gaussian random variable with variance
\tilde{v}_{n}^{2}:\,=v^{T}(\mathbf{S}_{n}/\sigma^{2}+\mathbf{I}_{d}/\rho^{2})^{-1}v
. Therefore, the width of
any confidence interval
[\widehat{\ell}_{n},\widehat{u}_{n}]\in\mathcal{A}
is lower bounded by

2z_{1-\alpha/2}\tilde{v}_{n}
, and we have

\displaystyle{\sf Risk}

\displaystyle\geq 2z_{1-\alpha/2}{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\Big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}\Big].

It remains to show that
\lim_{\rho\to\infty}{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}\big]=1
. Recalling that

\phi_{\rho}
denotes the Gaussian density with zero mean and covariance

\rho^{2}I_{d}
, we have

\displaystyle{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\Big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}-1\Big]

\displaystyle=\int\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\Big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}-1\Big]\phi_{\rho}({\bm{\theta}^{*}})d{\bm{\theta}^{*}},

By the bounded convergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\Big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}-1\Big]

\displaystyle=0,

pointwise for each
\bm{\theta}
. Consequently, the quantity in the
integral converges point-wise to zero, so that applying the bounded
convergence theorem again yields

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}{\Exs}_{{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\sim\pi}\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\Big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}-1\Big]=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\int\Exs_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\Big[\frac{\tilde{v}_{n}}{\xi_{n}}-1\Big]\phi_{\rho}({\bm{\theta}^{*}})d{\bm{\theta}^{*}}

\displaystyle=0,

which completes the proof of part (c).

7
Discussion

In this paper, we proposed a family of online debiasing estimators for
adaptive linear regression and analyze their asymptotic properties. We
introduced an online debiasing estimator, and proved that it admits a
Gaussian limit under considerably weaker conditions than the OLS estimator. We highlighted its practical behavior using examples from
multi-armed bandits, time series modeling, and active learning in
which online debiasing yields asymptotic normality while OLS does
not. We also proved a minimax lower bound for the adaptive linear
regression model; in conjunction with our upper bounds, our results
reveal that the online debiasing estimator is minimax optimal.

This work opens up a number of directions for future research. For
example, it would be interesting to characterize the non-asymptotic
behavior of estimators based on online debiasing. Concretely, we
would like to investigate the rate of distributional convergence of
the online debiasing estimators to the appropriate Gaussian
distributions.

Appendix D
Comparison to the least squares estimator

In this section, we provide a more fine-grained comparison between the least squares estimator and the online debiased estimator. We start with a lower bound on the MSE of the ordinary least squares estimator, which is based on the law of iterated logarithm.

Law of the iterated logarithm lower bound

Let
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of independent
standard Gaussian random variables and and
d=1
and
{\bm{\theta}^{*}}=0
, which means the least squares estimator is

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{n}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\epsilon_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}}\,.

We will choose an adaptive design with
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
and for which

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n}>1)\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\,\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mathcal{E}_{n}=\frac{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{1/2}\hat{\theta}_{n}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\log\log n}}\,.

The construction is essentially based on using the adaptive covariates to derandomize the law of the iterated logarithm.
Let
M_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\epsilon_{i}
be a Gaussian random walk.
Define a sequence of intervals
\{m_{k},m_{k}+\ell_{k}-1\}
of length
\ell_{k}
inductively as follows.
Let
m_{0}=0
and
m_{k}=m_{k-1}+\ell_{k}
where

\displaystyle\ell_{k}=\min\left\{\ell:\mathbb{P}\Big(\text{exists }1\leq s\leq\ell:M_{s}\geq\sqrt{(m_{k-1}+s)\log\log m_{k-1}}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\right\}\,.

By the same tedious argument used in the proof of the law of the iterated logarithm one can also show
that
\log\log m_{k+1}\leq 2\log\log m_{k}
for suitably large
k
.
By symmetry,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{exists }m_{k-1}+1\leq t\leq m_{k-1}+\ell_{k}:|M_{t}|\geq\sqrt{t\log\log m_{k-1}}\Big|M_{m_{k-1}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\,\,a.s.\,.

Let
\mathbb{N}=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}
where
\{I_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
are disjoint consecutive intervals and

I_{k}
has length
2\ell_{k}
.
We choose an adaptive design so that
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for all
i
and
\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\ell_{k}
for all
k
. That is,

\mathbf{x}_{i}=1
in exactly half of the time in each interval.
The adaptivity within each interval is as follows. The feature
\mathbf{x}_{i}=1
for either
\ell_{k}
time-steps or until
{\widehat{U}}_{i-1}\geq 1
.
Then
\mathbf{x}_{i}=0
until a final block of the interval where
\mathbf{x}_{i}=1
to guarantee that
\sum_{i\in I_{k}}\mathbf{x}_{i}=\ell_{k}
holds.
Formally,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }m(i-1)=m_{k}\\
1&\text{if }\mathcal{E}_{i-1}\leq 1\\
1&\text{if }m(i-1)+|I_{k}\setminus\{1,2,\ldots,i-1\}|=m_{k}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

By construction, if
s_{k}
is the
\ell_{k}
th time-step in interval
I_{k}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({\widehat{U}}_{s_{k}}>1\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s_{k}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\epsilon_{i}\geq\sqrt{\frac{m(s_{k})}{2}\log\log s_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s_{k}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\epsilon_{i}\geq\sqrt{m(s_{k})\log\log m_{k-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\,.

\Exs[\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}^{2}]=1
. We show that this modified estimator can have less bias for certain multiarmed bandit problems, and thereby improving upon the performance of the least squares estimators. We also show that this improved estimator has the same performnace as online debiased estimator (
6
) in the worst case. We start with the following modified version of the online debiased estimtor

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{OD}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\widehat{\gamma}_{n}}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{w}_{i}\left(y_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\operatorname{LS}}}\right)

(67)

Proof of the bound (
53a
)

Let
v=n/(d-1)
. By Bayes’ rule,

\displaystyle\frac{{\widehat{U}}_{n}}{1-{\widehat{U}}_{n}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{u=1}^{d-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=1}^{v}(a_{u,w}+b_{w}\varphi-y_{u,w})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{u,w}^{2}-\frac{\varphi^{2}}{2}\right)d\varphi

\displaystyle=\prod_{u=1}^{d-1}\exp\left(\sum_{w=1}^{v}a_{u,w}y_{u,w}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=1}^{v}a_{u,w}^{2}+\frac{m_{u,v}^{2}}{2d_{v}}-\frac{1}{2}\log d_{v}\right)

\displaystyle:\,=\exp\left(\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}Z_{u,w}\right)\,.

Note that
{\widehat{U}}_{n}(1-{\widehat{U}}_{n})=\Omega_{p}(1)
is equivalent
to
|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}Z_{u,w}|=O_{p}(1)
, which we now establish. To begin,

\displaystyle\frac{m_{u,w+1}^{2}}{d_{w+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{u,w}^{2}}{d_{w}}+\frac{a_{u,w+1}^{2}d_{w}}{d_{w+1}}+\frac{b_{w+1}^{2}(y_{u,w+1}-a_{u,w+1})^{2}}{d_{w+1}}-\frac{2a_{u,w+1}d_{w}}{d_{w+1}}y_{u,w+1}\,.

Telescoping shows that

\displaystyle Z_{u,v}=\underbracket{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{2}a_{u,w}^{2}}{d_{w}}}_{\textrm{A}_{u,v}}+\underbracket{\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{2}a_{u,w}y_{u,w}}{d_{w}}}_{\textrm{B}_{u,v}}+\underbracket{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{2}(y_{u,w}-a_{u,w})^{2}}{d_{w}}-\frac{1}{2}\log d_{v}}_{\textrm{C}_{u,v}}\,.

By definition,
b_{w}=w^{-1/4}/d^{1/2}
and
d_{w}=\Omega(w^{1/2}/d)
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle{\Exs}[a_{u,w}^{2}]=b_{w}^{2}\left(\left(\frac{d_{w-1}-1}{d_{w-1}}\right)^{2}+\frac{d_{w-1}-1}{d_{w-1}^{2}}\right)=O(b_{w}^{2})\text{ and }{\Exs}[y_{u,w}^{2}]=O(1)\,.

Therefore, by Cauchy-Schwarz and Chebyshev’s inequality,
|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\textrm{A}_{u,v}|=O_{p}(1)

and
|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\textrm{B}_{u,v}|=O_{p}(1)
and

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\textrm{C}_{u,v}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{2}(y_{u,w}-a_{u,w})^{2}}{d_{w}}-\log d_{v}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{2}(b_{w}+\mathbf{\epsilon}_{u,w})^{2}}{d_{w}}-\log d_{v}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{4}+b_{w}^{2}\mathbf{\epsilon}_{u,w}^{2}+2b_{w}^{3}\mathbf{\epsilon}_{u,w}}{d_{w}}-\log d_{v}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}\sum_{w=1}^{v}\frac{b_{w}^{2}}{d_{w}}-\log d_{v}\right|+O_{p}(1)

\displaystyle=O_{p}(1)\,,

where the second last equality follows from Chebyshev’s inequality and the last by an elementary integral approximation.
Therefore
|\sum_{u=1}^{d-1}Z_{u,v}|=O_{p}(1)
, which
completes the proof of (
53a
).

Proof of bound

By definition,

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{n}=\left[\begin{matrix}\sum_{w=1}^{v}b_{w}^{2}&0&\cdots&\sum_{w=1}^{v}a_{1,w}b_{w}\\
0&\ddots&0&\vdots\\
\vdots&0&\sum_{w=1}^{v}b_{w}^{2}&\sum_{w=1}^{v}a_{d-1,w}b_{w}\\

Using the formula for the inverse of a block matrix,

\displaystyle 1/\|e_{d}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1}}

\displaystyle=1/(\mathbf{S}_{n}^{-1})_{d,d}
