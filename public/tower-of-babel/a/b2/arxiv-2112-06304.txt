Abstract
In this article, we study the mean field limit of weakly interacting diffusions for confining and interaction potentials that are not necessarily convex. We explore the relationship between the large
N
limit of the constant in the logarithmic Sobolev inequality (LSI) for the
N
-particle system and the presence or absence of phase transitions for the mean field limit. The non-degeneracy of the LSI constant is shown to have far reaching consequences, especially in the context of uniform-in-time propagation of chaos and the behaviour of equilibrium fluctuations. Our results extend previous results related to unbounded spin systems and recent results on propagation of chaos using novel coupling methods. As incidentals, we provide concise and, to our knowledge, new proofs of a generalised form of Talagrand’s inequality and of quantitative propagation of chaos by employing techniques from the theory of gradient flows, specifically the Riemannian calculus on the space of probability measures.

Introduction

Interacting particle systems have attracted a lot of attention in recent years since they appear in diverse areas ranging from plasma physics and galactic dynamics to machine learning and optimization. For systems of identical (or exchangeable) particles in which the pair-wise interactions scale like the inverse of the number of particles, it is possible to pass to the mean field limit and obtain a coarse-grained description of the system via a nonlinear nonlocal PDE that governs the evolution of the one-particle density. In this paper, we consider systems of weakly interacting diffusions driven by pair-wise interactions, confinement and independent Brownian motions (see (
2.1
) ). In this case the mean field PDE is the so-called McKean–Vlasov equation.

A natural problem that one would like to address is how to obtain sharp quantitative estimates on the rate at which the empirical measure of the particle system converges to the mean field limit, as the number of particles
N
goes to infinity. When considering arbitrarily long time scales, this problem is intimately connected to the rate of convergence to steady states as time
t
goes to infinity. For the study of such quantitative results, a crucial role is played by the Poincaré (PI) and logarithmic Sobolev (LSI) inequalities. Our focus in this paper is to elucidate the connection between the validity of the LSI for the
N
-particle Gibbs measure uniformly in the number of particles
N
and the properties of the mean field limit. We establish connections with uniform-in-time propagation of chaos, (non-)uniqueness of steady states of the mean field equation, exponential convergence to equilibrium, and the behaviour of equilibrium fluctuations. We show the effect that the presence of multiple steady states for the mean field equation, which correspond to invariant measures of the associated nonlinear McKean SDE, has on the various quantitative and qualitative features of the underlying interacting particle system.

In the equilibrium statistical mechanics of lattice systems, the non-uniqueness of the infinite-volume grand canonical Gibbs measure is referred to as a phase transition
[
30
]
. The presence of such a phase transition can then be detected with the help of some order parameter, for example the thermodynamic pressure for the nearest-neighbour Ising model, or the average magnetisation of the ensemble for the mean-field Curie–Weiss model. Based on the behaviour of these quantities (or rather on the behaviour of the infinite-volume partition function), one can then characterise the phase transitions as either continuous (second-order) or discontinuous (first-order).

The situation in our setting is more complicated but closely mirrors the one for lattice systems. Indeed, the system we consider can be thought of as spin system with mean field interaction and the “spins” taking values in some uncountable state space
\Omega
. The difference between the system we consider and the Ising and Curie–Weiss model lies in the fact that, except in a small number of specific examples, it is extremely hard to specify an order-parameter or understand the exact behaviour of the infinite-volume partition function. An additional important difference is that for our system we will be more interested in the non-uniqueness of critical points of the free energy, which correspond to important changes in the features of the system, as opposed to non-uniqueness of its minimisers. In either case, the similarity with spin systems is instructive enough that it will serve the reader well to remember this analogy as we discuss the notion of phase transition we work with.

A detailed characterisation of phase transitions for McKean-Vlasov PDEs on the torus without a confining potential was given in
[
11
]
. In particular, the presence of phase transitions for this setting as it relates to non-uniqueness of minimisers of the free energy functional was discussed in detail. However, in this paper, we are more interested in characterising these phase transitions as they relate to non-uniqueness of critical points of the free energy.

For convex confining and interaction potentials (when the state space is Euclidean), the system does not undergo phase transitions. In fact, uniform-in-time propagation of chaos and uniqueness of the steady state for the mean field PDE have been established, for e.g. in
[
44
]
. Moreover, in
[
13
]
under a uniform convexity assumption of the potentials, the authors show exponentially fast relaxation to the unique steady state of the mean field system. Our focus in this paper is to deal with non-convex potentials which may exhibit phase transitions and thus could not be expected to always (for all temperatures) exhibit uniform-in-time propagation of chaos. In terms of the LSI, we show that the existence of a non-minimising steady state implies the (quantitative) degeneracy of the constant in the LSI of the
N
-particle Gibbs measure in the limit as
N\rightarrow+\infty
. On the other hand, we show that the non-degeneracy of the LSI, implies uniform-in-time propagation of chaos and Gaussianity of the fluctuations around the mean field limit at equilibrium. Furthermore, we conjecture that the limit of the LSI constant for the
N
-particle system can be uniquely characterised in terms of the dissipation inequality for the mean field system.

The relation between the non-degeneracy of the constant in the PI or the LSI, the absence of phase transitions, and the exponentially fast decay of correlations has been studied extensively for unbounded spin systems
[
63
]
. Conversely, in these works the equivalence between the slow decay of correlations and the fact that the constant in the LSI becomes degenerate at the phase transition has been established. Uniform estimates on the constant in the LSI beyond the convex case have been established recently
[
32
]
, under the Lipschitzian spectral gap condition for the single particle. We remark that this assumption is reminiscent to the assumptions on the conditional measures for the two-scale LSI
[
49
,
31
,
41
]
. We utilize the latter approach to show the non-degeneracy of the LSI for our
N
-particle Gibbs measure in the high temperature/weak interaction regime.

In the probability literature, the study of the LSI in the context of linear Fokker–Planck equations goes back to the classical
\Gamma_{2}
functional introduced by Bakry and Emery
[

[

Our approach in this paper exploits the fact that both the
N

-Wasserstein distance. We can use this structural feature of the system to study the limit of all the relevant quantities as

The advantage of this approach is that we can often make minimal assumptions on the regularity of the confining and interaction potentials: we will essentially assume that they are both only semi-convex, which is natural for 2-Wasserstein gradient flows (see
[

\begin{cases}\displaystyle dX_{t}^{i}=-\nabla V(X_{t}^{i})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{1}W(X_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{j})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}dB_{t}^{i}\\

,

2.1
The Fokker–Planck equation.

It is well known that the curve
\rho^{N}:[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})
which describes the evolution of the law of the process
(X_{t}^{1},...,X_{t}^{N})\in\Omega^{N}
satisfies the following linear Fokker–Planck equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho^{N}=\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho^{N}+\nabla\cdot(\rho^{N}\;\nabla H_{N})&\mbox{in $(0,\infty)\times\Omega^{N}$},\\
(\nabla\rho^{N}+\rho^{N}\nabla H_{N})\cdot\vec{n}_{\Omega^{N}}=0&\mbox{on $(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega^{N}$}\\
\rho^{N}(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^{\otimes N},\end{cases}

(2.3)

where the Hamiltonian
H_{N}
is given by

H_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W(x_{i},x_{j}),

\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, and
\vec{n}_{\Omega^{N}}
is the unit normal to
\Omega^{N}
.

2.2
de Finetti/Hewitt–Savage.

To take the limit as
N\to\infty

is symmetric for all times, that is

\rho^{N}(t)\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega)\quad\textrm{ for all }t\geq 0.

The main idea is that we can characterise the limit
N\to\infty
of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})
as
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))
, which denotes the Borel probability measures with bounded second moment defined over the metric space
(\mathcal{P}(\Omega),d_{2})

is the 2-Wasserstein distance.

Following de Finetti
[

]
and Hewitt–Savage
[

(\rho^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

\rho^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})

P_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))
along a subsequence which we do not relabel such that

\rho^{N}\rightharpoonup P_{\infty},

where weak convergence is given by duality with cylindrical test functions. That is, for any

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\Omega^{l}}\varphi(y)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho^{N}_{l}(y)=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}\left(\int_{\Omega^{l}}\varphi(y)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho^{\otimes l}(y)\right)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P_{\infty}(\rho),

(2.4)

where

\rho^{N}_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{l})\qquad\mbox{is the $l$-th marginal of $\rho^{N}$.}

In essence, this means that in the limit
N\to\infty
of symmetric probability measures can be characterised as convex combinations of chaotic measures. For more details, we refer the reader to

with

\overline{d}_{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}d_{2},

2.3
The mean field limit and nonlinear behaviour

Within this formalism, the limit
N\to\infty
of the equation (
2.3
) can be written as

\rho^{N}(t)\rightharpoonup\delta_{\rho(t)}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))\qquad\mbox{for all $t\geq 0$,}

(2.7)

where
\delta_{\rho(t)}
denotes the delta measure concentrated on
\rho(t)
, the unique solution of the nonlinear McKean equation

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho=\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\left(\nabla V+\nabla W\star\rho\right)\right)&\mbox{on $(0,\infty)\times\Omega$}\\
(\nabla\rho+\rho(\nabla V+\nabla W\star\rho)\cdot\vec{n}_{\Omega}=0&\mbox{on $(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega$}\\
\rho(0)=\rho_{\mathrm{in}},\end{cases}

(2.8)

with

W\star\rho(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W(x,y)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(y)\,.

(2.9)

One of the most salient differences between the particle dynamics (
2.3
) and the mean field dynamics (
2.8
) is that, whereas the Fokker-Planck equation governing the evolution of the
N
-particle system is linear, the mean field PDE (
2.8
) is nonlinear. As is well known, a consequence of this is that for non-convex confining/interaction potentials the mean field dynamics might have more than one stationary state, in contrast to the particle dynamics. Indeed, the unique steady state of the
N
-particle Fokker–Planck equation (
2.3
) is given by the Gibbs measure

M_{N}:=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{N}(x)}}{Z_{N}}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad Z_{N}:=\int_{\Omega^{N}}e^{-\beta H_{N}(y)}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}y.

(2.10)

On the other hand, the mean field limit can admit more than a single steady state, with the full characterisation being the set of solutions to the self-consistency equation

\beta^{-1}\log\rho_{*}+W\star\rho_{*}+V=C_{*}\qquad\mbox{on $\Omega$ for some $C_{*}\in\mathbb{R}$,}

(2.11)

This is discussed in detail in
Proposition

4.1
. See also
[
11
,
18
]
and the references therein.

2.4
Phase transitions

The uniqueness/non-uniqueness of steady states of the mean field system (
2.8
) depends both the temperature of the system (or, equivalently, the strength of the interaction) and on the convexity properties of the confining and interaction potentials
V
and
W
. At sufficiently high temperatures, the diffusion is strong enough that the expected escape time of particles from local minima of the potentials is bounded uniformly in the number of particles. Indeed, a perturbation argument shows that, at sufficiently high temperatures, the self-consistency equation (
2.11
) has a unique solution, see Proposition
4.2
. When we cool the system and the potentials are non-convex, particles can get trapped for arbitrarily long time scales in local minima and condense
[
4
]
. In statistical physics terms, the system changes from a gaseous state to a liquid or solid state. In the mean field limit, this change of behavior can be characterised by the local or global instability of the minimisers of the mean field energy (see (
2.15
)), see for instance
[
11
,
15
]
and
Fig.

1
for a loose picture of the change in the mean field energy landscape as the temperature of the system is varied. For more details on the possible definitions of a phase transition, see Section
4
.

2.5
Qualitative long time behavior

Due to the linearity of the
N
-particle system, we know that (
2.3
) admits a unique steady state (given by the Gibbs measure
M_{N}
). If the potentials prevent mass from escaping to infinity, by the La-Salle’s principle for graident flows
[
12
, Theorem 2.13]
, we know that independent of the initial condition

\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho^{N}(t)=M_{N}\,,

(2.12)

in the sense of weak convergence of probability measures. Furthermore, entropy methods and the LSI provide us with exponentially fast convergence to the steady state
[
45
]
. On the other hand, if the McKean–Vlasov equation admits multiple steady states, then the limit
t\to\infty
for the mean field dynamics will depend on the choice of initial condition. In this case, the limiting dynamics (
2.8
) do not approximate the particle dynamics (
2.1
) for arbitrarily long times. In this paper we provide evidence that phase transitions constitute the natural obstruction to obtaining uniform-in-time propagation of chaos estimates. See Theorem
3.7
for sufficient conditions for the mean field approximation to be valid uniformly in time.

2.6
\Gamma
-convergence

Taking advantage of the 2-Wasserstein gradient flow structure of (
2.3
) and (
2.8
) (see
[
1
,
10
,
53
]
), the main tool that we will use to obtain a quantitative understanding of the limit

is

-convergence with respect to the topology introduced by de Finetti/Hewitt-Savage-type convergence (

). To illustrate this technique, we use it to characterise the limit

of the Gibbs measure

. Following the pioneering work of Messer and Spohn
[
47
]
, we notice that

is the unique minimiser over probability measures of the energy per unit particle

E^{N}[\rho^{N}]=\frac{1}{N}\left(\beta^{-1}\int_{\Omega^{N}}\rho^{N}\log\rho^{N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+\int_{\Omega^{N}}H_{N}\rho^{N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\right).

(2.13)

Taking

, we can characterise the thermodynamic limit of the energy
E^{N}
as
E^{N}\to^{\Gamma}E^{\infty}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, where

\displaystyle E^{\infty}[P]:=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}[\rho]\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P(\rho),

(2.14)

with the mean field energy
E^{MF}:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
given by

E^{MF}[\rho]:=\beta^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\rho\log(\rho)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega^{2}}W(x,y)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(x)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(y)+\int_{\Omega}V(x)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(x)\,,

(2.15)

see
[
35
]
or
Theorem

5.6
for a more modern proof. Using the fact that

is the minimiser of (

), we know that any accumulation point of the sequence
M_{N}
(in the sense of de Finetti/Hewitt–Savage)

needs to be a minimiser of (

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{N}\right)=\lim_{N}E^{N}[M_{N}]=E^{\infty}[P_{\infty}]=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}E^{\infty}[P]=\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}E^{MF}[\rho],

. This convergence and related results are discussed in further detail in

Theorem A
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
,

Main results

To quantify the convergence as
t\to\infty

M_{N}
:

E^{N}[\rho^{N}(t)]-E^{N}[M_{N}]=\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N}(t)|M_{N}):=\frac{1}{N}\int_{\Omega^{N}}\log\left(\frac{\rho^{N}(t)}{M_{N}}\right)\rho^{N}(t)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x,

(3.1)

where we use the notation
\overline{\mathcal{E}}:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty]
to denote the scaled relative entropy. Taking a time derivative and using the PDE (
2.3
) we obtain the scaled relative Fisher information
\overline{\mathcal{I}}:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to[0,\infty]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N}(t)|M_{N})=-\beta^{-1}\frac{1}{N}\int_{\Omega^{N}}\left|\nabla\log\left(\frac{\rho^{N}(t)}{M_{N}}\right)\right|^{2}\rho^{N}(t)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=:-\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N}(t)|M_{N}).

(3.2)

The convergence (
2.12
) in relative entropy is exponential whenever we can show that the
N
-particle log Sobolev constant is bounded away from zero:

0<\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}:=\inf_{\rho^{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega^{N})\setminus\{M_{N}\}}\frac{\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})}.

(3.3)

Following the classical work of Bakry–Emery
[
3
,
40
,
36
]
, we can find mild conditions for the positivity of the log Sobolev constant whenever the domain
\Omega
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem C
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, if there exists
R>1
and
\lambda>0
such that

D^{2}H^{N}(x)\geq\lambda I^{Nd\times Nd}\qquad\mbox{for every $|x|>R$,}

(3.4)

then we have that
\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}>0
.

Remark 3.1
.

The convexity condition (
3.4
) in the far field can arise from either the convexity of the interaction or the confining potential. We expect that the sharp condition for the Gibbs measure
M_{N}
to satisfy
\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}>0
uniformly in
N
is related to the behavior of the mean field limit dissipation inequality (
3.6
). We will discuss this in more detail in
1
.

For the mean field limit, we can perform a similar analysis with the relative mean field energy. More specifically, given
\rho(t)
the solution to (
2.8
) we can differentiate to obtain the dissipation

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t}E^{MF}[\rho(t)]-\inf E^{MF}=-\int_{\Omega}|\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho(t)+\nabla W\star\rho(t)+\nabla V|^{2}\rho(t)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=:-D(\rho(t)).

(3.5)

Hence, we obtain exponential decay of the mean field energy to its minimum value, as long as the so-called infinite colume log Sobolev constant, given by

0<\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\\
\rho\notin\mathcal{K}\end{subarray}}\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho(t)]-\inf E^{MF}},

(3.6)

is positive, where

\mathcal{K}=\{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\;:\;E^{MF}[\rho]=\inf E^{MF}\}.

(3.7)

In both cases, when the log Sobolev constant is positive we can show that the relative energy behaves quadratically with respect to the 2-Wasserstein distance. This is essentially the content of Talagrand’s inequality
[
58
]
. One of our contributions in this paper is a new proof of a generalised version of this inequality using gradient flow techniques.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
,

E^{N}[\rho^{N}]-E^{N}[M_{N}]\geq\frac{\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}}{2}\overline{d}^{2}_{2}(\rho^{N},M_{N})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf E^{MF}\geq\frac{\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}}{2}d^{2}_{2}(\rho,\mathcal{K}),

(3.8)

where
\mathcal{K}
is the set of minimisers of
E^{MF}
and
d^{2}_{2}(\rho,\mathcal{K})=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{K}}d_{2}^{2}(\rho,\mu).

An optimal transport-based proof of inequality (
3.8
) for
E^{N}
and
E^{MF}
can be found in
[
61
, Theorem 22.17]
. We also refer to
[
16
]
for a proof using entropic interpolation. In
Section

6
, we provide a different more intuitive proof of (
3.8
) for general energies
E:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Our strategy is to use the associated gradient flow structure and what is sometimes referred to as Otto calculus
[
48
]
, a formal Riemannian calculus on
(\mathcal{P}(\Omega),d_{2})
. We should note that one of the main differences in Talagrand’s inequality for the
N
-particle energy and for the mean field energy is that the set of minimisers
\mathcal{K}
does not need to be a single point in the mean field case.

Having established the need for understanding the behavior of the log Sobolev constant, our first result relates the limit of the particle system log Sobolev constant (
3.3
) with the mean field or infinite volume log Sobolev constant(
3.6
):

Theorem 3.3
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}\leq\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}.

(3.9)

Moreover, if the mean field energy
E^{MF}
(
2.15
) admits a critical point that is not a minimiser, then
\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}=0
, and there exists
C>0
such that

\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}\leq\frac{C}{N}.

(3.10)

Our result complements similar results that have been obtained for unbounded spin systems
[
63
]
and the references therein. On the other hand, when
\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}>0
, we can show that the
regularized log Sobolev constant

\lambda_{\LSI}^{N,\varepsilon}:=\inf_{\rho^{N}:\;\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})>\varepsilon}\frac{\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})}

(3.11)

does not degenerate. More specifically,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{\LSI}^{N,\varepsilon}\geq\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}>0.

(3.12)

This result implies that relaxation to neighborhoods of the stationary state of the particle dynamics
(
2.3
)
happens exponentially fast, uniformly in
N
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, assume that
\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}>0
, and that
\rho_{\mathrm{\mathrm{in}}}
in (
2.1
) has finite energy and bounded higher order moments,

E^{MF}[\rho_{\mathrm{\mathrm{in}}}]<\infty\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\int_{\Omega}|x|^{2+\delta}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho_{\mathrm{\mathrm{in}}}<\infty,\qquad\mbox{for some $\delta>0$.}

(3.13)

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for every
N>N_{0}
we have

\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N}(t)|M_{N})\leq\max\left\{\varepsilon,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}t}\;\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{\otimes N}_{\mathrm{in}}|M_{N})\right\}.

Remark 3.5
.

For chaotic measures, we can take the limit
N\to\infty
of the relative entropy obtaining

\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{\otimes N}_{\mathrm{in}}|M_{N})=E^{MF}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]-\inf E^{MF}\,.

This is discussed in
Theorem

5.6
.

Unfortunately, we are not able to fully characterise the limit of
\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}
in terms of the mean field limit. Despite this, Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.4
, provide us with evidence which is convincing enough to make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, we have the equality

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}=\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}.

The results of our paper provide us with a strong indication that the absence of phase transitions (loosely defined to mean that the mean field limit has a unique stationary state), the non-degeneracy of the infinite volume log Sobolev constant, and the validity uniform-in-time propagation of chaos are all equivalent.

3.1
Consequences of the non-degeneracy of the log Sobolev inequality

Bearing
1
in mind, we now explore the implications of the non-degeneracy of the LSI constant in the limit
N\rightarrow+\infty
. We begin by noticing that if the log Sobolev constant does not degenerate in
N
, then the invariant Gibbs measure
M_{N}
of the
N
-particle system is well approximated by the unique minimiser of the mean field energy.

Theorem 3.6
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, assume that
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}>0
. Then, there exists a unique steady state
\rho_{\beta}
to (
2.8
). Moreover, there exists
C>0
, such that

\overline{d}^{2}_{2}(\rho_{\beta}^{\otimes N},M_{N})\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{\beta}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})\leq\frac{2}{(\lambda_{\LSI}^{N})^{2}}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{\beta}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})\leq\frac{C}{N}.

(3.14)

Interpolating the previous result with more standard propagation of chaos estimates that depend on the convexity constant of the potentials, we obtain the following uniform-in-time propagation of chaos result.

Theorem 3.7
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, let
\rho^{N}
and
\rho
denote the unique solutions to the particle (
2.3
)
and mean field (
2.8
) dynamics given by
B
. Assume that
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
has finite energy

E^{MF}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]<\infty\,,

that the gradient of the square of the interaction potential is uniformly integrable

\sup_{t\in[0,\infty]}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{1}W|^{2}\star\rho(t)\rho(t)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x<\infty\,,

(3.15)

and that
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}=:\lambda^{\infty}>0
. Then,

\overline{d}_{2}(\rho^{N}(t),\rho^{\otimes N}(t))\leq\frac{C}{N^{\theta}}\qquad\mbox{for all $t>0$,}

(3.16)

where

\theta=\begin{cases}1/2&\mbox{if $K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N)>0$}\\
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{\infty}}{\lambda^{\infty}-2(K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N))}&\mbox{if $K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N)<0$}\end{cases}

with
K_{V}
and
K_{W}
the convexity constants of
V
and
W
in
2.1
and
2.2
. In the case where
K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N)=0
, we can pick any
\theta<1/2
.

Remark 3.8
.

The integrability assumption (
3.15
) is trivially true when
W
is uniformly Lipschitz. Also, this assumption is satisfied when the potentials are attractive enough in the far field, i.e. outside a ball of radius
R
, that we can obtain uniform exponential bounds for the tail behavior of the mean field solution.

Remark 3.9
.

We note that for convex potentials
K_{V},

K_{W}\geq 0
the uniform propagation of chaos with
\theta=1/2
has already been shown in
[
44
]
. The main difference with this work is that our approach utilizes the convexity of the entropy along the 2-Wasserstein distance to obtain a contraction estimate. This approach can be easily extended to manifolds with Ricci curvature bounded from below
[
43
]
, where we need to consider the sign of

K_{\mathrm{Ric}}+K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N)

with
K_{\mathrm{Ric}}
the lower bound on the Ricci curvature of the underlying Riemannian manifold.

Remark 3.10
.

We do not expect
\theta
in the above theorem to be sharp for
K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N)<0
. At sufficiently high temperatures and
K_{V}+K_{W}(1-1/N)<0
, a comparable result for the 1-Wasserstein distance is shown by coupling methods in
[
25
]
with
\theta=1/2
.

We can also use the non-degeneracy of the LSI constant to identify the fluctuations at equilibrium.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
and assume that
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}>0
, then the fluctuations process

\eta^{N}(t)=\sqrt{N}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}-\rho_{\beta}\right)\,,

(3.17)

where
(X_{t}^{1},...,X_{t}^{N})
are solutions to (
2.1
) with initial law given by the invariant Gibbs measure
M_{N}
, satisfies

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\eta^{N}(t)\|_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\right]<\infty,

(3.18)

for any
T>0,\,s>d/2+1
.

Moreover, assume that
V
,
W
are smooth and that the linearised operator (
2.8
) around
\rho_{\beta}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta=\beta^{-1}\Delta\eta+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\beta}\nabla W\star\eta)+\nabla\cdot(\eta\nabla W\star\rho_{\beta})+\nabla\cdot(\nabla V\eta)\,,

(3.19)

satisfies, for all
\phi\in\textrm{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the following coercivity inequality

\langle-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\phi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\geq c\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

(3.20)

for some
c>0
.

Then, for any
m>d/2+3
,
\eta^{N}
converges in law, as a
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
-valued random variable, to the unique stationary solution
\eta^{\infty}
of the following linear SPDE

\partial_{t}\eta^{\infty}=\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta^{\infty}+\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}\xi)

(3.21)

where
\xi
is space-time white noise.

Remark 3.12
.

We mention the result of Fernandez and Meleard
[
26
]
(see also
[
59
,
55
]
) which characterises the fluctuations of the particle dynamics with respect to the mean field limit for finite time horizons, under a stronger closeness assumption for the initial data. We also mention the recent preprint
[
62
]
which studies fluctuations for singular potentials.

To our knowledge, the first available results for fluctuations at equilibrium is due to Dawson
[
18
]
in which he shows that, for the specific case of Desai–Zwanzig model, at the phase transition temperature, equilibrium fluctuations are non-Gaussian. It is an interesting open problem if this behavior is universal for any system which undergoes a (continuous) phase transition. The different notions of phase transition will be discussed in
Section

4
.

Remark 3.13
.

For The smoothness assumptions on
V
and
W
are used to have a well-defined semigroup associated to the linearised operator
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}
which regularizes instantaneously arbitrary initial data in
H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Up to technical results, this can be quantified by requiring
V
and
W\in W^{m+\epsilon,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for any
\epsilon>0
. For fluctuation results with singular potentials we refer the reader to the recent preprint
[
62
]
.

The unique invariant measure of the SPDE (
3.21
)
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{P}(H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is a centred Gaussian measure with covariance operator
Q_{\mathcal{G}}
given by

Q_{\mathcal{G}}(\varphi,\psi):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}}\varphi\cdot\nabla e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}}\psi\,\rho_{\beta}\,\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t\,,

(3.22)

for any mean-zero
\varphi,\;\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and where
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}
denotes the flat
L^{2}
-adjoint.

In the specific case that
V\equiv 0
and
W(x,y)=W(x-y)
, we can obtain a more explicit characterisation of
\mathcal{G}
. Indeed, since
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}>0
, we know from
Proposition

4.2
(and the discussion following it) that
E^{MF}
has a unique critical point which is given by
\rho_{\infty}(\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x)=\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
. We can then write down an explicit representation of the action of the semigroup
e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x}}
in Fourier space as follows

\hat{\varphi}_{t}(k)=e^{-4\pi^{2}\lvert k\rvert^{2}(\beta^{-1}+\hat{W}(k))t}\hat{\varphi}(k)\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},k\neq 0\,,

(3.23)

where
\varphi_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x}}\varphi
for some mean-zero
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. This leaves us with the formula

Q_{\mathcal{G}}(\varphi,\psi)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},k\neq 0}\frac{\hat{\varphi}(k)\hat{\psi}(k)}{8\pi^{2}(\beta^{-1}+\hat{W}(k))}\,,

(3.24)

where we have used the fact that the coercivity inequality (
3.20
) is equivalent to the fact that
\beta^{-1}+\hat{W}(k)>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},k\neq 0
, which is also equivalent to the condition
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
, see
Proposition

4.3
.

Another way of rewriting this is that
\mathcal{G}
is the unique centred Gaussian measure with Cameron–Martin space given by the closure of all smooth mean-zero functions
\varphi
under the norm

\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{G}}}^{2}=8\pi^{2}\left(\beta^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\varphi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(W*\varphi)\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\right)\,.

(3.25)

Since,
\beta^{-1}+\hat{W}(k)>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},k\neq 0
, the above norm is equivalent to the standard
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
norm. The above norm is also the same, up to a multiplicative constant, as the norm introduced in
A
.

One can further use the structure of the covariance operator
Q_{\mathcal{G}}
to read off that
\mathcal{G}
is supported on
H^{-\frac{d}{2}-}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
distributions. Thus, the limiting equilibrium fluctuations have the regularity of spatial white noise, which is not surprising considering the fact that their Cameron–Martin space is “
basically
”
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

3.2
Non-degeneracy of the LSI constant in specific cases

Putting aside for the time being the validity of
1
, we show that the LSI constant
\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}
does not degenerate in the high temperature regime when
\Omega
is compact, or when the confinement
V
satisfies an LSI inequality and the interaction strength is small enough.

Theorem 3.14
.

Assume that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\lVert W\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})},\,\lVert D^{2}_{xy}W\rVert_{{L}^{\infty}(\Omega^{2})}<C\,.

(3.26)

We then have the following two scenarios:

(a)

Compact case
: Assume
\Omega
is compact and its normalised Lebesgue measure
\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
satisfies a log Sobolev inequality. Then, there exists a
0<\beta_{\LSI}=\beta_{\LSI}(C)
such that for all
\beta<\beta_{\LSI}
, we have

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}>0\,.

(3.27)

(b)

weak interaction case
: Assume
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and that the one-particle measure
Z_{V}^{-1}e^{-V}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
satisfies a log Sobolev inequality with constant
\lambda^{V}_{\LSI}>0
. Then there exists an
\epsilon_{\LSI}=\epsilon_{\LSI}(C,\lambda^{V}_{\LSI},\beta)>0
, such that for any
0\leq\epsilon<\epsilon_{\LSI}
we have

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{\epsilon,N}_{\LSI}>0\,,

(3.28)

where
\lambda^{\epsilon,N}_{\LSI}
is the log Sobolev constant of the Gibbs measure
\tilde{M}_{N}=Z_{N}^{-1}e^{-\beta H^{\epsilon}_{N}}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
with

H^{\epsilon}_{N}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(x_{i})+\frac{\epsilon}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W(x_{i},x_{j})\,.

(3.29)

The above result relies crucially on the two-scale approach to log Sobolev inequalities introduced in
[
49
]
, which is based on the analysis of the marginal and conditional measures of
M_{N}
. For the convenience of the reader, we describe the main result of
[
49
]
in
Theorem

11.2
. In addition to the above high temperature result, it is also possible to obtain a sharper result in certain specific scenarios. For instance, in the case in which
\Omega=\mathbb{T}
,
W(x,y)=-\cos(2\pi(x-y))
, and
V\equiv 0
. The corresponding system is referred to by many names: the noisy Kuramoto model, the mean field classical
XY
model, the mean field
O(2)
model or the Brownian mean field model
[
14
]
. It is known that this system exhibits a phase transition (of type A, B, and C, see
Definition

4.6
). Due to the particularly simple nature of the model, it is possible to show that the
N
particle log Sobolev constant
\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}
is asymptotically non-degenerate all the way up to the critical inverse temperature
\beta_{c}=2
. We state without proof the following result due to Bauerschmidt and Bodineau
[
5
]
, see also
[
6
]
.

Theorem D
(
[
5
, Theorem 1]
).

Consider the Gibbs measure
M_{N}
of the mean field
O(2)
model and denote by
\lambda_{\LSI}^{N}
its log Sobolev constant. Then, for all
0<\beta<\beta_{c}=2
, we have that

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}>0\,.

(3.30)

Remark 3.15
.

An essentially similar argument as in
[
5
, Theorem 1]
, can be used to show that the system with
\Omega=\mathbb{T}
,
W(x,y)=-\cos(2\pi k(x-y))
, and

.

4
Phase transitions

We start our discussion by stating and proving the following result which provides us with a particularly useful characterisation of steady states.

Proposition 4.1
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is a critical point of the mean field free energy
E^{MF}
, that is to say

\lvert\partial E^{MF}\rvert(\rho)=D(\rho)=0.

2.

\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is a steady state of the mean field equation (
2.8
), i.e. it is distributional weak solution of the PDE

\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho(\nabla W\star\rho+\nabla V))=0\,\quad x\in\Omega\,.

(4.1)

3.

\rho
solves the self-consistency equation:

\displaystyle\rho-\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta(W\star\rho+V)}=0\,,\qquad Z_{\beta}=\int_{\Omega}e^{-\beta(W\star\rho+V)}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\,.

(4.2)

Furthermore, for all
\beta>0
,
E^{MF}
has at least one global minimiser which is a critical point, and any critical point
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
of
E^{MF}
is Lipschitz, strictly positive, and has moments of all orders.

The proof of the equivalence of the three characterisations follows from similar arguments to
[
11
, Proposition 2.4]
and so we omit the proof. The fact that
E^{MF}
has a critical point follows from
A
.

Now, using (
4.2
), we know that any critical point
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is of the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta(W\star\rho+V)}\,,

(4.3)

which is Lipschitz and strictly positive. We now use
Eq.

2.2
to assert that, since
W
is bounded below,

\displaystyle\rho\leq Z_{\beta}^{-1}e^{\beta C}e^{-\beta V}\,.

(4.4)

Thus, by
2.1
, any critical point
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
of
E^{MF}
has moments of all orders.
∎Furthermore, we have the following result regarding uniqueness of critical points.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

3.14
there exists a unique critical point
\rho_{\beta}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
of the mean field free energy
E^{MF}
.

The proof of this result follows by combining the results of
Theorems

3.6
and
3.14
. We know from
Theorem

3.14
that the logarithmic Sobolev inequality holds uniformly for
M_{N}
. We can then use
Theorem

3.6
to argue that for
\beta
sufficiently small, the mean field free energy
E^{MF}
must have a unique critical point
\rho_{\beta}
.
∎

When we discuss non-uniqueness of critical points, we will often restrict ourselves to the case in which
V=0,W(x,y)=W(x-y)
, and
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
. This case lends itself particularly well to analysis as the Lebesgue measure
\rho_{\infty}(\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x)=\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
, what we shall hereafter refer to as the “
flat state
”, is always a critical point of
E^{MF}
for all
\beta>0
. We shall see later that in this setting it is possible to provide relatively clean examples of the different types of phase transitions that we consider in this paper. For an example of what a typical phase transition looks like, see
Fig.

1
for a schematic of the free energy landscape in the vicinity of a phase transition.

We now introduce and motivate a list of properties that may serve as a proxy for the absence of well-defined order parameter. We discuss how these properties relate to each other, specifically in the “
flat case
”. We start by looking at the linearisation of the mean field dynamics (
2.8
):

Property A
.

Fix
\beta>0
. We say that the system (
2.8
) satisfies
Property A
if
E^{MF}
has a unique critical point
\rho_{\beta}
and the linearised operator associated to right hand side of (
2.8
) has a spectral gap under the interaction weighted inner product. More specifically, we have that

\displaystyle\inf_{\eta\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),\int_{\Omega}\eta\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=0}\frac{\langle-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{W,\rho_{\beta}}}{\lVert\eta\rVert_{W,\rho_{\beta}}^{2}}>0\,,

(A)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta=\beta^{-1}\Delta\eta+\nabla\cdot(\nabla V\eta)+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\beta}\nabla W\star\eta)+\nabla\cdot(\eta\nabla W\star\rho_{\beta})\,,

(4.5)

and

\displaystyle\langle\eta,\nu\rangle_{W,\rho_{\beta}}=\frac{\beta^{-1}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\eta\nu\rho_{\beta}^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(W\star\eta)\nu\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

(4.6)

with
\lVert\eta\rVert_{W,\rho_{\beta}}^{2}=\langle\eta,\eta\rangle_{W,\rho_{\beta}}
.

The above property captures essentially the local stability of the critical point
\rho_{\beta}
. Loss of the local stability of
\rho_{\beta}
is a precursor to a phase transition. In the setting of a continuous phase transition (see the upper half of
Fig.

1
), one expects this property to fail exactly at the critical temperature
\beta=\beta_{c}
. The weighted inner product arises naturally through the linearisation of the mean field log Sobolev constant, or more specifically through the linearisation of the mean field energy
E^{MF}
, see (
4.8
). We note that the bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{W,\rho_{\beta}}
is positive semi-definite when
\rho_{\beta}
is the unique critical point. For the classical
XY
model (see the discussion before
Eq.

3.30
), it is known that the system exhibits a continuous phase transition
[
11
, Proposition 6.1]
. In this case, the non-local inner product above it is related to the inner product that was introduced in
[
8
]
to understand the spectral gap ahead of the phase transition. In the absence of the interaction term, it reduces to the standard weighted inner product that symmetrises the Fokker-Planck operator, see
[
50
]
. In the periodic translation invariant case
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
W(x,y)=W(x-y)
, and
V\equiv 0
, when
\rho_{\infty}=\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
is the unique critical point of
E^{MF}
, the weighted inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{W,\rho_{\infty}}
is equivalent to the standard
L^{2}
inner product. Thus, in this situation, checking
A
is satisfied is equivalent to checking that
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\infty}}
has a spectral gap in the standard
L^{2}
inner product. This relationship will be made clearer in
Proposition

4.3
. Before discussing
A
in more detail, we introduce the next property which measures the local degeneracy of the self-consistency equation (
4.2
).

Property B
.

Fix
\beta>0
. We denote by
T:{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to{L}^{2}(\Omega)
the map
associated to the self-consistency equation (
4.2
):

\displaystyle T(\rho)=\rho-\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta(W\star\rho+V)}=0\,,\qquad Z_{\beta}=\int_{\Omega}e^{-\beta(W\star\rho+V)}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\,.

(B)

We say that the system (
2.8
) satisfies
Property B
if
E^{MF}
has a unique critical point
\rho_{\beta}
and the Fréchet derivative
D_{\rho}T
of
T
at
\rho_{\beta}
is non-degenerate, that is to say it has a trivial kernel consisting only of constant functions.

Under additional conditions, see
[
11
]
, the above property being violated implies the presence of a local bifurcation around the minimiser
\rho_{\beta}
. A simple condition that implies the presence of a local bifurcation is when the algebraic multiplicity of the
0
eigenvalue of
D_{\rho}T
at
\rho_{\beta}
is odd. Local bifurcations also arise, if the map
T
can be rewritten as a so-called potential operator and its Frechét derivative
D_{\rho}T
has a non-zero crossing number, see
[
39
]
. The above property exactly captures the second-order degeneracy of the mean field free energy around the unique critical point
\rho_{\beta}
. Indeed, formally expanding
E^{MF}[\rho]
about
\rho_{\beta}
we obtain

\displaystyle E^{MF}[\rho]=

\displaystyle E^{MF}[\rho_{\beta}]+\int_{\Omega}\beta^{-1}\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\rho_{\beta}^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}W\star\eta\eta\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+O(\eta^{3})\,

(4.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E^{MF}[\rho_{\beta}]+\beta^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\left(D_{\rho}T[\rho_{\beta}]\eta\right)\eta\rho_{\beta}^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x+O(\eta^{3})\,,

(4.8)

with
\eta=\rho-\rho_{\beta}
. Thus, if
B
is satisfied the mean field free energy is non-degenerate at second order near the critical point
\rho_{\beta}
.

The third and final property considers the validity of the Dissipation Inequality.

Property C
.

Fix
\beta>0
. We say that the system (
2.8
) satisfies
Property C
if the infinite-volume log Sobolev constant is positive. More precisely, if we have that

\displaystyle\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}:=\inf_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega),\rho\not\in\mathcal{K}}\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho]-\min_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}}>0\,.

(C)

The third and final property captures global aspects of the free energy landscape. Indeed, one would expect it to be violated in both the situations described in
Fig.

1
. For the case of the discontinuous phase transition, the lower half of
Fig.

1
,
C
would be violated because of the presence of a non-minimising critical point of
E^{MF}
which is represented by the red circles. Thus, the numerator of (
C
) vanishes while the denominator is strictly positive. As an explicit example of a system which exhibits such a phase transition, one can consider
\Omega=\mathbb{T}
, with the bi-chromatic interaction potential
W(x,y)=-\cos(2\pi(x-y))-\cos(4\pi(x-y))
, and
V\equiv 0
(cf.
[
11
, Theorem 5.11]
). On the other hand, in the case of a continuous phase transition, the upper half of
Fig.

1
, the fact that
C
should be violated is more subtle. To observe this, we linearise the right hand side of (
C
) about the unique minimiser
\rho_{\beta}
at
\beta=\beta_{c}
. For the dissipation, we have that

\displaystyle D(\rho)=

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\beta^{-1}\nabla\frac{\eta}{\rho_{\beta}}+\nabla W\star\eta\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho_{\beta}+O(\eta^{3})\,,

(4.9)

with
\eta=\rho-\rho_{\beta}
. Combining the above expression with (
4.8
), we obtain to leading order

\displaystyle\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho]-E^{MF}[\rho_{\beta}]}\approx\frac{4\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\beta^{-1}\nabla\frac{\eta}{\rho_{\beta}}+\nabla W\star\eta\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho_{\beta}}{\int_{\Omega}\beta^{-1}\eta^{2}+W\star\eta\eta\rho_{\beta}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho_{\beta}^{-1}}\,.

(4.10)

Moreover, we notice that

\displaystyle(-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta,\eta)_{W,\rho_{\beta}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\beta^{-1}\nabla\frac{\eta}{\rho_{\beta}}+\nabla W\star\eta\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho_{\beta}\,.

(4.11)

Using (
4.10
), it follows that to leading order

\displaystyle\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho]-E^{MF}[\rho_{\beta}]}\approx 4\frac{\langle-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta,\eta\rangle^{2}_{W,\rho_{\beta}}}{\lVert\eta\rVert_{W,\rho_{\beta}}^{2}}\,.

(4.12)

Hence, in the setting of a continuous phase transition the infinite volume log Sobolev constant captures the spectral gap of
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}
, and thus also captures the loss of local stability of
\rho_{\beta}
.

We now present the following result which characterises how the various properties we have discussed relate to each other in the periodic spatially homogeneous case.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
V\equiv 0,

W(x,y)=W(x-y)
, and denote

\beta_{\sharp}:=\frac{1}{\min(0,-\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\hat{W}(k))}\in(0,\infty].

Then, for
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
the linearised operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x}
satisfies the spectral gap property (
10.4
), and the kernel of the Fréchet derivative of the self-consistency equation (
10.5
) is non degenerate at the “
flat state
”. For
\beta\geq\beta_{\sharp}
,
A
and
B
are violated.

Furthermore, for all
\beta\neq\beta_{\sharp}
,
C
implies
A
and
B
.

Remark 4.4
.

We emphasize that
Proposition

4.3
does not ensure that
A
and
B
are satisfied for
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
. In fact, for the bi-chromatic potential case
W(x,y)=-\cos(2\pi(x-y))-\cos(4\pi(x-y))
, we know that the “
flat state
” is not the unique steady state for some
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
violating the uniqueness requirement of
A
and
B
, see
[
11
, Theorem 5.11]
.

We will first argue that
A
and
B
are equivalent. It follows from the fact that
\rho_{\infty}(\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x)\equiv\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x
is always a critical point of
E^{MF}
(in the flat case) that if
E^{MF}
has a unique critical point, it must be
\rho_{\infty}
. For any mean-free
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have from the previous calculation, that

\displaystyle\frac{\langle-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\infty}}\eta,\eta\rangle_{W,\rho_{\beta}}}{\lVert\eta\rVert_{W,\rho_{\beta}}^{2}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\beta^{-1}\nabla\eta+\nabla W*\eta\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x}{\int_{\Omega}(\beta^{-1}\eta+W*\eta)\eta\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x}\,,

(4.13)

where we have used the fact that
\rho_{\beta}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. It is easy to check that the above expression is strictly positive if and only if
\beta<\frac{1}{\min(0,-\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\hat{W}(k))}=\beta_{\sharp}
. Similarly, computing the Fréchet derivative of
T
at
\rho_{\infty}
, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{\rho}T[\rho_{\infty}]\eta=\eta+\beta W*\eta\,.

(4.14)

Again, the above linear operator has a trivial kernel if and only if
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
. It follows that
A
and
B
are equivalent.

We will now show that
C
implies
B
for
\beta\neq\beta_{\sharp}
. We know that
B
is satisfied if and only if
E^{MF}
has unique critical point and
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
. Assume it is violated. Then, either
E^{MF}
has more than one critical point or
\beta\geq\beta_{\sharp}
(or both).

Consider the case in which
\beta<\beta_{\sharp}
but
E^{MF}
has more than one critical point one of which is
\rho_{\infty}
. Furthermore, we know from
[
33
, Lemma 5.6]
that
\rho_{\infty}
is a strict local minimum. If at least one of the other critical points is not a minimiser then clearly
C
is violated since the numerator can be chosen to be zero while the denominator remains positive. We will now argue that this is the case. Assume it is not, that is all other critical points are minimisers. Then, we can apply the mountain pass theorem in
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
(using the fact that
\rho_{\infty}
is a strict local minimum)
[
33
, Theorem 1.1]
to construct a new non-minimising critical point thus obtaining a contradiction.

Consider now the case in which
\beta>\beta_{\sharp}
. In this situation, we note from
[
11
, Proposition 5.3]
that
\rho_{\infty}
is a non-minimising critical point of
E^{MF}
. Thus,
C
is again violated.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

We note here that one would expect the
A
,
B
and
C
to be equivalent to each other. However, we unable to show that this holds true in general.

Given the above result, we are now finally in a position to present our definition of a phase transition in the “

”.

Except for the Brownian mean field model see

5
The limit
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))

In this section, we recall some useful results that allows us the characterise the various relevant objects in the limit as
N\to\infty
. We follow the approach taken by Hauray and Mischler in
[
35
]
. We start by defining the empirical measure.

Definition 5.1
.

Given some
\rho^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})
, the empirical measure is the
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
-valued random variable which is given by

\mu^{(N)}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}}\qquad\mbox{where $(x_{1},...,x_{N})$ is distributed according $\rho^{N}$.}

We denote the law of the empirical measure
\mu^{(N)}
on
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
by

\hat{\rho}^{N}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))\,.

The topology we consider throughout most of the manuscript is the one induced by the scaled 2-Wasserstein distance.

Definition 5.2
.

Given
\rho_{1}^{N},\,\rho_{2}^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})
, the scaled 2-Wassertein distance between them is given by

\overline{d}_{2}(\rho_{1}^{N},\rho_{2}^{N})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\sim\rho_{1}^{N}\\
Y\sim\rho_{2}^{N}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[|X-Y|^{2}]\right)^{1/2},

where
X,\,Y
are
\Omega^{N}
-valued random variables.

Similarly, given
P_{1},\,P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))
, the 2-Wasserstein distance between them is given by

\mathfrak{D}_{2}(P_{1},P_{2})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{X}\sim P_{1}\\
\mathcal{Y}\sim P_{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{2}^{2}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})]\right)^{1/2}

where
\mathcal{X},\,\mathcal{Y}
are
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
-valued random variables.

A fundamental result we need to understand the convergence
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))
is the fact that the mapping
\rho^{N}\mapsto\hat{\rho}^{N}
is an isometry for the appropriately scaled 2-Wasserstein distance defined in
Definition

5.2
. Indeed, we have the following result.

Theorem 5.3
(
[
35
, Proposition 2.14]
).

For each
\mu^{N},\,\nu^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{sym}}}(\Omega^{N})
, we have

\overline{d}_{2}(\mu^{N},\nu^{N})=\mathfrak{D}_{2}(\hat{\mu}^{N},\hat{\nu}^{N})\,.

We present only a sketch of the proof of this result. The first inequality
\overline{d}_{2}(\mu^{N},\nu^{N})\geq\mathfrak{D}_{2}(\hat{\mu}^{N},\hat{\nu}^{N})
follows from the mapping in
Definition

5.1

X\mapsto\mathcal{X}
, i.e. given
X=(X_{1},...,X_{N})
an
\Omega^{N}
-valued random variable with law
\mu^{N}
, we define

\mathcal{X}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}}\,,

is a
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
-valued random variable with law
\hat{\mu}^{N}
. The converse inequality follows from taking the inverse mapping and exploiting the symmetry of
\mu^{N}
and
\nu^{N}
.
∎

Using this result, we can provide a metric notion of the de Finetti/Hewitt–Savage convergence.

Definition 5.4
.

We say that the sequence
(\rho^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\rho^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{\mathrm{sym}}}}(\Omega^{N})
converges in the 2-Wasserstein distance to
P_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))
if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathfrak{D}_{2}(\hat{\rho}^{N},P_{\infty})=0,

where
\hat{\rho}^{N}
is the law of the empirical measure (as
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
-valued random variable) associated to
\rho^{N}
.

We now have the following result which connects the above convergence to the one introduced in (
2.4
).

Proposition 5.5
.

For any sequence
(\rho^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\rho^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})
and

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Omega}|x|^{\gamma}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho_{1}^{N}(x)<\infty

(5.1)

for some
\gamma>2
, the metric convergence in Definition
5.4
is equivalent to the original marginal convergence of (
2.4
).

We first notice that by the results of Diaconis and Freedman
[
21
]
, for a fixed
l\in\mathbb{N}
, the marginal
\rho^{N}_{l}
coincides in the limit as
N\to\infty
with the
l
th
product of the empirical measure. More specifically, for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega^{l})
we have that

\left|\int_{\Omega^{l}}\varphi\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho^{N}_{l}-\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}\left(\int_{\Omega^{l}}\varphi\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\mu^{\otimes l}\right)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\hat{\rho}^{N}(\mu)\right|\leq l^{2}\frac{\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{l})}}{N}\,.

(5.2)

Furthermore, (
5.1
) implies that
\hat{\rho}^{N}
is tight in
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}d_{2}^{2\gamma}(\rho,\delta_{0})\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\hat{\rho}^{N}(\rho)=\int_{\Omega}|x|^{\gamma}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho^{N}_{1}\,.

(5.3)

Now if
\hat{\rho}^{N}
converges in the sense of
Definition

5.4
to
P_{\infty}
we know that it must converge when tested against every
C_{b}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))
. Since cylindrical test functions are a subset of

, it is clear from (

) that
\rho^{N}
must also converge to
P_{\infty}
in the sense of (
2.4
).

On the other hand if
\rho^{N}
converges to
P_{\infty}
in the sense of (
2.4
) we know that, under (
5.1
), the sequence
\hat{\rho}^{N}
is relatively compact in
(\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega)),\mathfrak{D}_{2})

(see
[

), which tells us that the limits in (

5.4
coincide.
∎Next, we discuss the limit of the associated free energy and dissipation functionals.

Theorem 5.6
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2

(\rho^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

\rho^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})

\liminf_{N\to\infty}E^{N}[\rho^{N}]\geq E^{\infty}[P_{\infty}]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\liminf_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})\geq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}D(\rho)\;dP_{\infty}(\rho)

where

\lim_{N\to\infty}E^{N}[\rho_{*}^{N}]=E^{\infty}[P_{\infty}]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{*}^{N}|M_{N})=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}D(\rho)\;dP_{\infty}(\rho)

The convergence of the relative entropy functional follows directly from the classical arguments in

]

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N},M_{N})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\int_{\Omega^{N}}|\nabla\log\rho^{N}-\nabla H^{N}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho^{N}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{N}}(|\nabla\log\rho^{N}|^{2}+2\Delta H^{N}+|\nabla H^{N}|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho^{N}\,.

[

[
47
]

\overline{\mathcal{I}}^{1/2}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})=\sup_{\nu^{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Omega^{N})}\left(\frac{E^{N}[\rho^{N}]-E^{N}[\nu^{N}]}{\overline{d}_{2}(\rho^{N},\nu^{N})}-\frac{K_{V}+K_{W}}{2}\overline{d}_{2}(\rho^{N},\nu^{N})\right)_{+}\,,

see
[

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{I}}^{1/2}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})\geq\sup_{Q_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))}\left(\frac{E^{\infty}[P_{\infty}]-E^{\infty}[Q_{\infty}]}{\mathfrak{D}_{2}(P_{\infty},Q_{\infty})}-\frac{K_{V}+K_{W}}{2}\mathfrak{D}_{2}(P_{\infty},Q_{\infty})\right)_{+}.

To obtain equality, we consider
S_{t}:\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\to\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
the solution operator associated to (

and consider the curve
Q_{\infty}=S_{t}\#P_{\infty}
, which coincides with the unique gradient flow of
E^{\infty}
with respect to
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
(see
[
10
, Lemma 19]
). Taking
t\to 0^{+}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{E^{\infty}[P_{\infty}]-E^{\infty}[S_{t}\#P_{\infty}]}{\mathfrak{D}_{2}(P_{\infty},S_{t}\#P_{\infty})}-\frac{K_{V}+K_{W}}{2}\mathfrak{D}_{2}(P_{\infty},S_{t}\#P_{\infty})\right)_{+}

(5.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}D(\rho)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P_{\infty}(\rho)\right)^{1/2}\,,

(5.10)

obtaining the desired lower semicontinuity result.

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(\rho^{N}|M_{N})\geq\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}D(\rho)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P_{\infty}(\rho)

Now we focus on showing that given

, the sequence

\rho_{*}^{N}=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}\rho^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P(\rho)

(5.12)

attains the limit (
5.5
). For convenience, we consider the auxiliary functional
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}:\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\Omega))\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, which is given by

\mathcal{I}^{\prime}[P]=\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{*}^{N}|M_{N}),

where
\rho_{*}^{N}
is given by (
5.12
). Next, we show that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is well defined and that it is weakly lower semicontinuous.

To show this, we regularize
\rho_{*}^{N}
by using the
N
-particle dynamics (
2.3
). Using the convexity in
2.1
and
2.2
, we obtain that

e^{t(\lambda_{V}+\lambda_{W})}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(S^{N}_{t}\rho_{*}^{N}|M_{N})\leq\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{*}^{N}|M_{N})\,,

where

is the solution operator associated to the Fokker–Planck equation (
2.3
). Using the weak lower semicontinuity of
\overline{\mathcal{I}}
from the expression (
5.8
), we obtain

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(S^{N}_{t}\rho_{*}^{N}|M_{N})=\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{*}^{N}|M_{N})\,.

(5.13)

Using the fact that
S_{t}^{N}
is immediately regularizing, we can use the formal expression (
5.7
) freely for the measure

. By using the sub-additivity of the Fisher information, we can notice that for any

(5.14)

where
(S^{N}_{t}\rho_{*}^{N})_{j}
is the
j

marginal of

which implies that we can characterise

By the weak lower semicontinuity of
\overline{\mathcal{I}}
, we obtain the weak lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.

Using (
5.14
) and the propagation of chaos result of
[

]
, we obtain that

where the equality in the left hand side follows from using the expression (
5.7
) and the result for the standard Fisher information in
[
35
]
. Taking
t\to 0^{+}
and using the lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
we have showed above, we obtain the desired conclusion (

)

∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.2

We prove the result only for

, as the proof for

and even more general energies

is analogous, see
Remark

6.1
. We consider
\rho:[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, the unique 2-Wasserstein gradient flow of
E^{MF}
with initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, see
B
.

We notice that if
\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}=0
, then there is nothing left to prove. Hence, we can assume without loss of generality that
\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}>0
, which implies (cf.
C
and
4.1
) that
E^{MF}
does not admit non-minimising steady state. Using the version of LaSalle’s invariance principle for gradient flows proved in
[
12
, Theorem 4.11]
, we know that
\rho(t)
accumulates on the set of steady states of
E^{MF}
, as
t\to\infty
. Using the fact that all steady states are minimisers, we can find a sequence of times
t_{n}\to\infty
such that

\rho(t_{n})\rightharpoonup\rho_{\infty}

(6.1)

for some
\rho_{\infty}\in\mathcal{K}
. Differentiating
E^{MF}[\rho(t)]
with respect to time and using that
\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}>0
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t}\left(E^{MF}[\rho(t)]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho(t)+\nabla V+\nabla W\star\rho(t)\right\rvert^{2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(t)

(6.2)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}}\left(E^{MF}[\rho(t)]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}\right)^{1/2}

(6.3)

\displaystyle\times\left(\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho(t)+\nabla V+\nabla W\star\rho(t)\right\rvert^{2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(t)\right)^{1/2}.

(6.4)

Integrating from
0
to
t_{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(E^{MF}[\rho(t_{n})]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}\right)^{1/2}-\left(E^{MF}[\rho_{0}]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}\right)^{1/2}

(6.5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}}{2}\right)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{t_{n}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\beta^{-1}\nabla\log\rho(s)+\nabla V+\nabla W\star\rho(s)|^{2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\rho(s)\right)^{1/2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s

(6.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}}{2}\right)^{1/2}d_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho(t_{n})),

(6.7)

where the last inequality follows from the Benamou–Brenier
[
7
]
formulation of the 2-Wasserstein distance. Taking
t_{n}\to\infty
, applying (
6.1
), and rearranging, we end up with

\displaystyle\left(E^{MF}[\rho_{0}]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}\right)^{1/2}\geq\left(\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}}{2}\right)^{1/2}d_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{\infty})\geq\left(\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}}{2}\right)^{1/2}d_{2}(\rho_{0},\mathcal{K}).

(6.8)

The desired inequality (
3.8
) follows by squaring both sides.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3

For the proof of the first part of the theorem, we consider some
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
such that
E^{MF}[\rho]>\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}>-\infty
, see
A
. By
Theorem

5.6
and
Corollary

5.9
, we can find a recovery sequence
P^{N}=\rho^{\otimes N}
, such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(P^{N}|M_{N})=E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(P^{N}|M_{N})=D(\rho).

(7.1)

Using the definition of the log Sobolev constant and passing to the limit as
N\to\infty
, we observe

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\overline{\mathcal{I}}(P^{N}|M_{N})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(P^{N}|M_{N})}=\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf E^{MF}}.

Taking the infimum over all
\rho\notin\mathcal{K}
, we obtain

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{\LSI}\leq\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}=\inf_{\rho\notin\mathcal{K}}\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}}.

This completes the proof of the first half of the theorem.

Next, we consider the case when there exists
\rho_{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
a non-minimising steady state, that is to say

D(\rho_{*})=0\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad E^{MF}[\rho_{*}]>\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}.

Consider now the sequence
\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{sym}}}(\Omega^{N})
. We have

\displaystyle\lambda_{N}^{\LSI}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})}

(7.2)

By
Theorem

5.6
and
Corollary

5.9
, we can pass to the limit in the denominator to obtain

\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})=E^{MF}[\rho_{*}]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}>0.

Thus, we have that for
N
large enough

\lambda_{N}^{\LSI}\leq\frac{2}{E^{MF}[\rho_{*}]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})\,.

(7.3)

The desired bound

\lambda_{N}^{\LSI}\leq\frac{C}{N}

follows from the decay estimate for the Fisher information proved in
Lemma

7.1
.
∎

Lemma 7.1
.

Under
2.1
and
2.2
, assume that
\rho_{*}
is a critical point of the mean field free energy
E^{MF}
, then we have the following bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla_{1}W|^{2}\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})-\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\right)\rho_{*}(x_{1})\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\int\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\rho_{*}(x_{1})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\,.

We start by expanding
\mathcal{I}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{N}}\left|\nabla\left(\log\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}+\beta\sum_{i}V(x_{i})+\frac{\beta}{2N}\sum_{i,j}W(x_{i},x_{j})\right)\right|^{2}\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

(7.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\int_{\Omega^{N}}\left|\frac{\nabla\rho_{*}(x_{i})}{\rho_{*}(x_{i})}+\beta\nabla V(x_{i})+\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{j}\nabla_{1}W(x_{i},x_{j})\right|^{2}\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

(7.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\int_{\Omega^{N}}\left|-\beta\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})+\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{j}\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{j})\right|^{2}\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

(7.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\beta^{2}\int_{\Omega^{N}}\left(\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}-\frac{2}{N}\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\cdot\sum_{j}\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{j})\right.

(7.7)

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{j})\cdot\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{k})\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\right)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x,

(7.8)

where we have used symmetry of the particle system to write the integrand in terms of the variable
x_{1}
and that
\rho_{*}
is critical point of the mean field energy
E^{MF}
which itself implies that

\beta^{-1}\log\rho_{*}+W\star\rho_{*}+V=C.

Next, proceeding term by term, we notice the following cancellation (for simplicity, we assume that
W(x,x)=0
, otherwise we can change
V
by an additive constant such that this holds):

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{N}}\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\rho_{*}(x_{1})\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1},

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega^{N}}\frac{2}{N}\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\cdot\sum_{j}\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{j})\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(2-\frac{2}{N}\right)\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\rho_{*}(x_{1})\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1},

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{N}}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j,k}\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{j})\cdot\nabla_{1}W(x_{1},x_{k})\rho_{*}^{\otimes N}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)(N-2)}{N^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\rho_{*}(x_{1})\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1}

(7.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{N-1}{N^{2}}\int\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\right\rvert^{2}\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\rho_{*}(x_{1})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1}\,.

(7.10)

Putting the previous identities together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\rho_{\beta}^{\otimes N}|M_{N})=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)\int_{\Omega}\left(\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\right\rvert^{2}\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})-\left\lvert\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{*}(x_{1})\right\rvert^{2}\right)\rho_{*}(x_{1})\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x_{1}

) and the fact that all steady states have finite moments of every order by

4.1
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.4

We prove the statement of the theorem by contradiction. To this end, we assume that there exists a sequence
N_{i}\to\infty
and a sequence of times
t_{i}>0

0<t_{i}<\frac{2}{\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}}\log((E^{N_{i}}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^{\otimes N_{i}}]-E^{N_{i}}[M_{N_{i}}])/\varepsilon)

(8.1)

such that

\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(t_{i})|M_{N_{i}})>\max(\varepsilon,e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}t_{i}}{2}}(E^{N_{i}}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^{\otimes N_{i}}]-E^{N_{i}}[M_{N_{i}}]).

Using the finite energy assumption on the initial condition (
3.13
), and
2.1
and
2.2
which imply that
E^{N}
is uniformly bounded below, we have that

\sup_{i}t_{i}\leq\sup_{i}\frac{2}{\lambda^{\infty}_{LSI}}\log((E^{N_{i}}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^{\otimes N_{i}}]-E^{N_{i}}[M_{N_{i}}])/\varepsilon)=:T(\varepsilon)<\infty.

(8.2)

By the relative entropy dissipation estimate (
3.2
), we obtain

\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(t_{i})|M_{N_{i}})>e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}t_{i}}{2}}(E^{N_{i}}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^{\otimes N_{i}}]-E^{N_{i}}[M_{N_{i}}])

which implies that there exists at least one
s_{i}\in(0,t_{i})
such that

\frac{\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}<\frac{\lambda_{\LSI}^{\infty}}{2}.

The contradiction will arise if we can show that

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\frac{\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}\geq\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}=\inf_{\rho\notin\mathcal{K}}\frac{D(\rho)}{E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf_{\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}E^{MF}}.

First, we consider the case when

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})=\infty\,.

By using the monotonicity of the relative entropy (
3.2
) and the finite energy assumption in the statement of the theorem, we obtain

\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})\leq\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}^{\otimes N_{i}}|M_{N_{i}})=E^{MF}[\rho_{\mathrm{in}}]-E^{N}[M_{N}]<\infty.

Hence, we can conclude

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\frac{\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}=\infty>\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}.

Therefore, we can assume that up to a subsequence, which we do not relabel,

\sup_{i}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})<\infty.

By the bounded higher moment hypothesis in (
3.13
), the uniform boundedness of
s_{i}
(
8.2
), and the propagation of moments along the flow for
K
-convex potentials
[
10
]
, we have that
\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})
has uniformly bounded moments of order
2+\delta
. By
Proposition

5.5
, we obtain that there exists
P_{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, such that up to a (not relabeled) subsequence,
\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})\to P_{*}
in the sense of
Definition

5.4
. By the HWI inequality
Theorem

5.8
and
Corollary

5.9
, we obtain the strong convergence in the relative entropy term, which yields

\varepsilon\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})=\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf E^{MF}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P_{*}(\rho).

Combining this limit with the
\liminf
-inequality of
Theorem

5.6
for the dissipation we obtain that

\liminf_{i\to\infty}\frac{\beta^{-1}\overline{\mathcal{I}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}{\overline{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{N_{i}}(s_{i})|M_{N_{i}})}\geq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}D(\rho)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P_{*}(\rho)}{\int_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf E^{MF}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}P_{*}(\rho)}\geq\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI},

where the last inequality follows from the point-wise inequality

D(\rho)\geq\lambda^{\infty}_{\LSI}(E^{MF}[\rho]-\inf E^{MF})\,.

This is the desired contradiction and the result now follows.
∎

\mu^{(N)},\rho_{\beta}^{(N)}

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mu^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta}^{(N)}\|^{2}_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right]\leq\frac{C}{N}.

(12.4)

Next, we bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\rho_{\beta}^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta}\|^{2}_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right]
. We take
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
to be the orthonormal basis of
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
from (
12.1
). Expanding the square, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\rho_{\beta}^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta}\|^{2}_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left\lvert\langle\rho_{\beta}^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta},\phi_{j}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\right]

(12.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{j}(X_{i})-\int\phi_{j}\rho_{\beta}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\right|^{2}\right]

(12.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int\phi_{j}^{2}\rho_{\beta}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x-\frac{1}{N}\left(\int\phi_{j}\rho_{\beta}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x\right)^{2}

(12.7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|\rho_{\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\phi_{j}\|_{{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

(12.8)

Noticing that
s>d/2
implies that the embedding
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is Hilbert-Schmidt, we can use the boundedness of
\rho_{\beta}
to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\rho_{\beta}^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta}\|^{2}_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right]\leq\frac{C}{N}\,.

(12.9)

Combining (
12.3
) with (
12.4
) and (
12.9
), we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

Remark 12.3
.

An alternative proof of inequality (
12.9
) can be found in
[

, Theorem 5.1]
.

We now consider the implications of having uniform control of the log Sobolev constant the fluctuations of the stationary solutions to (
2.1
), i.e. solutions of (
2.1
) started at stationarity. To this end, we consider the empirical measure process
t\mapsto\mu^{(N)}(t)
defined by

\mu^{(N)}(t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}},

where
X_{t}=(X_{t}^{1},\dots,X_{t}^{N})
is the solution to (
2.1
) started from the unique invariant Gibbs measure
M_{N}
. Our goal is to analyze the corresponding fluctuation process
t\mapsto\eta^{N}(t)
defined by

\eta^{N}(t):=\sqrt{N}(\mu^{(N)}(t)-\rho_{\beta}),

As a direct consequence of the estimates from the previous lemma and using the fact that
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{LS}>0
, we have the uniform bound

\sup_{N,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\eta^{N}(t)\|_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\right]<\infty\qquad\mbox{for any $s>d/2+1$.}

(12.10)

In the sequel, we will use the above estimate together with the classical martingale method, c.f.
[
17
, Chapter 8]
, to establish convergence in law as
N\to\infty
of
\eta^{N}
to the stationary solution
\eta
of the following linear SPDE

\partial_{t}\eta=\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta+\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}\xi),

(12.11)

where
\xi
is a mean-zero space-time white noise on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}
is the linearisation of the McKean-Vlasov operator (
2.8
) around the unique invariant measure
\rho_{\beta}
defined by

\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\psi=\beta^{-1}\Delta\psi+\nabla\cdot(\nabla W\star\psi\,\rho_{\beta})+\nabla\cdot(\nabla W\star\rho_{\beta}\,\psi)+\nabla\cdot(\nabla V\,\psi).

(12.12)

The SPDE (
12.11
) can be solved using classical methods, the three typical notions of solution being the mild, weak, and martingale formulations. As is typical, the martingale formulation is the most convenient for identifying the limiting law of a tight subsequence of
\eta^{N}
, while the mild formulation provides a clearer picture of the uniqueness in law and hence the convergence in law of the full sequence.

Denote
U
as the closed mean-zero subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
H:=H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for
s>\frac{d}{2}
. Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
be a stochastic basis, i.e a complete filtered probability space with a right continuous filtration endowed with an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted
U
-valued cylindrical Wiener process
W
and let
\eta_{0}
be an
H
-valued
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable independent of
W
. The mild formulation of (
12.11
) with initial condition
\eta_{0}
involves stochastic integration in Hilbert spaces, c.f.
[
17
, Chapter 4]
, which we quickly review for our specific case below. Let
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H)
denote the Hilbert–Schmidt operators from
U
to
H
equipped with the standard Hilbert–Schmidt norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbf{L}_{2}^{0}(U;H)}
. Given
T>0
and
\Phi\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H))
, the stochastic integral
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(s)d\textbf{W}(s)
is well defined as a continuous
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale with trajectories in
C([0,T];H)
. We remind the reader that the relevance of
\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H)
in this context is that the Itô isometry takes the following form

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\Phi(s)d\textbf{W}(s)\right\|_{H}^{2}\right]=\int_{0}^{t}\|\Phi(s)\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s.

The mild solution to
t\mapsto\eta(t)\in H
to (
12.11
) with initial condition
\eta_{0}
is then given by the following stochastic convolution:

\eta^{\infty}(t):=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}}\eta_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}}\nabla\cdot\big(\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}d\textbf{W}(s)\big).

(12.13)

Lemma 12.4
.

The mild solution (
12.13
) is well-defined as a stochastic process with trajectories in
C([0,T];H)
.

In light of our remarks in the preceding paragraph, it suffices to show that
s\mapsto\Phi(s)
defined by
U\ni u\mapsto e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}}\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}u)\in H
belongs to
L^{2}([0,T];\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H))
. Given an orthornormal basis
\{e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of
U
, using the definition of the Hilbert–Schmidt norm and integrating by parts leads to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi(s)\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\|e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}}\nabla\cdot\big(\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}e_{k}\big)\|_{H}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle e_{k},\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}\nabla e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}}\phi_{j}\rangle_{U}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\|\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}\nabla e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}}\phi_{j}\|_{U}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s,

(12.14)

where in the last step we used Parseval’s identity in
U
. Finally, we note that the coercivity hypothesis for
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}
(see
Lemma

12.8
) implies that

\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}}\phi_{j}\|_{U}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s\leq C\|\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

Combining this with (
12.14
) and using the fact that
\rho_{\beta}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, yields

\int_{0}^{T}\|\Phi(s)\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}_{0}(U;H)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s\leq C\|\rho_{\beta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\phi_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

(12.15)

Since
s>\frac{d}{2}
, the embedding of
H^{s}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
into
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is Hilbert–Schmidt. Thus, the above series converges, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 12.5
.

We note that the representation (
12.13
) immediately implies that solutions to (
12.11
) are unique in law, i.e. (
12.11
) satisfies weak uniqueness. That is to say, given two different stochastic bases
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}},\textbf{W})
and
(\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}},\widetilde{\textbf{W}})
defining solutions
\eta
and
\tilde{\eta}
to (
12.11
) on their respective probability spaces through the formula (
12.13
), the laws of
\eta
and
\tilde{\eta}
agree on
C([0,T];H)
for any
T>0
, as long as
\eta_{0}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{0}
are equal in law on
H
.

For our purposes, it is easier to work with the martingale formulation of (
12.11
), which is in turn motivated by the weak formulation of (
12.11
). Hence, we note in passing that the mild solution (
12.13
) has the property that for each
t\in[0,T]
the following equality holds in
H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, for
s>\frac{d+2}{2}
,

\eta^{\infty}(t)=\eta_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta^{\infty}(s)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\cdot(\sqrt{\rho_{\beta}}d\textbf{W}_{s}).

(12.16)

Lemma 12.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},{\mathbb{P}})
be a filtered probability space. Assume that
\eta
is a continuous-time
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted
H
-valued stochastic process and define
t\mapsto M(t)
by

M(t):=\eta(t)-\eta_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta(s)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}s.

(12.17)

Assume that the following two conditions hold:

•

For all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
it holds that

t\mapsto\langle M(t),\varphi\rangle

(12.18)

is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale.

•

For all
\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
it holds that

t\mapsto\langle M(t),\varphi\rangle\langle M(t),\psi\rangle-t\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi\rho_{\beta}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}x

(12.19)

is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
martingale.

Then,
\eta
is equal in law on
C([0,T];H)
to the mild solution (
12.13
).

By
[
17
, Theorem 8.2]
, it follows that on a suitable extension of the probability space,
\eta
is a weak solution in the sense of (
12.16
). By
[
17
, Theorem 5.4]
, the weak and mild solutions coincide on that probability space, so equality in law follows from
Remark

12.5
.
∎

Using the above lemma, we are now finally in a position to prove the convergence of the fluctuations.

Theorem 12.7
.

Assume that
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\lambda^{N}_{LS}>0
and that
V
and
W
are smooth. Then, for any
m>d/2+3
the fluctuation process
\eta^{N}
converges in law on
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
to the unique stationary mild solution
\eta^{\infty}
of the SPDE (
12.11
).

The proof has four steps. In Step 1, we apply Itô’s formula to show that
\eta^{N}
satisfies (
12.20
), an approximate version of the weak formulation (
12.16
). In Step 2, we combine Step 1 with the uniform bound (
12.10
) to show that the laws of
(\eta^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
on
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
are uniformly tight for
m
large enough. In Step 3, we pass to the limit in the martingale problem and verify the assumptions of Lemma (
12.6
) to identify the limit along any tight subsequence. In Step 4, we conclude the uniqueness of the limit and hence the proof of the theorem.

Step
1. In this step, we show that for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
it holds

\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\langle\eta^{N},\varphi\rangle=\langle\eta^{N},\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t+\langle R_{N},\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t+\sqrt{2}(\beta N)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla\varphi(X_{t}^{i})\cdot dB_{t}^{i}\,,

(12.20)

with
X_{t}^{i}
the solution to (
2.1
) and where
R_{N}
is defined as

R_{N}:=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\nabla\cdot(\eta^{N}\nabla_{1}W\star\eta^{N})\,.

(12.21)

Indeed, Itô’s formula gives

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\varphi(X_{t}^{i})=

\displaystyle\left(\beta^{-1}\Delta-\nabla V\cdot\nabla\right)\varphi(X_{t}^{i})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\nabla_{1}W(X_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{j})\cdot\nabla\varphi(X_{t}^{i})\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t

(12.22)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2}\beta^{-1}\nabla\varphi(X_{t}^{i})\cdot dB_{t}^{i}\,.

(12.23)

We now sum over
i=1,\dots,N
, divide by
N
, and use the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\nabla_{1}W(X_{t}^{i},X_{t}^{j})\cdot\nabla\varphi(X_{t}^{i})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{1}W*\mu^{(N)}(X_{t}^{i})\cdot\nabla\varphi(X_{t}^{i})

(12.24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mu^{(N)},\nabla_{1}W\star\mu^{(N)}\cdot\nabla\varphi\rangle\,,

(12.25)

to obtain

\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\langle\mu^{(N)},\varphi\rangle=\langle\mu^{(N)},\beta^{-1}\Delta\varphi-(\nabla V+\nabla_{1}W\star\mu^{(N)})\cdot\nabla\varphi\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t+\sqrt{2}\beta^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla\varphi(X^{i}_{t})\cdot dB_{t}^{i}

Next, we insert the identity
\mu^{(N)}=\rho_{\beta}+N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\eta^{N}
, to deduce

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}\langle\eta^{N},\varphi\rangle=

\displaystyle N^{1/2}\underbrace{\langle\rho_{\beta},\beta^{-1}\Delta\varphi-(\nabla V+\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{\beta})\cdot\nabla\varphi\rangle}_{=0}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t

(12.26)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\langle\eta^{N},\beta^{-1}\Delta\varphi-(\nabla V+\nabla_{1}W\star\rho_{\beta})\cdot\nabla\varphi\rangle+\langle\rho_{\beta},\nabla_{1}W\star\eta^{N}\cdot\nabla\varphi\rangle}_{=\langle\eta^{N},\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}\varphi\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t

(12.27)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{N^{-1/2}\langle\eta^{N},\nabla_{1}W\star\eta^{N}\cdot\nabla\varphi\rangle}_{\langle R^{N},\varphi\rangle}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\beta N^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla\varphi(X^{i}_{t})\cdot dB_{t}^{i}

The first identity follows from the fact that
\rho_{\beta}
is a steady state and the second follows from integration by parts and Fubini’s theorem (using the symmetry of
W
).

Step
2.
In this step, we will show that the laws of
(\eta^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
on
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for
m>m_{0}:=d/2+3
are uniformly tight. To this end, we define a decomposition of
t\mapsto\eta^{N}(t)
via the equality
\eta^{N}(t)=Y_{N}(t)+M_{N}(t)
, where

Y_{N}(t):=\eta^{N}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\big(\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta^{N}(r)+R^{N}(r)\big)\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}r.

(12.28)

We claim that for
m>m_{0}
and all
p\geq 1
there exist constants
C,C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y^{N}(t_{1})-Y^{N}(t_{2})\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}

(12.29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|M^{N}(t_{1})-M^{N}(t_{2})\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\frac{p}{2}}.

(12.30)

To obtain (
12.29
), first observe that at each fixed time we have

\displaystyle\|R_{N}\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=

\displaystyle\|\nabla\cdot(\eta^{N}\nabla_{1}W\star(\mu^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta}))\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

(12.31)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\eta^{N}\nabla_{1}W\star(\mu^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta})\|_{H^{1-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{s}\|\eta^{N}\|_{H^{1-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\nabla W\star(\mu^{(N)}-\rho_{\beta})\|_{W^{{m-1},\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}

(12.32)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{s}\|W\|_{W^{m,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\eta^{N}\|_{H^{1-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.

(12.33)

Furthermore, note that the regularity of
V
,
W
, and
\rho_{\beta}
implies the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}
is bounded from
H^{2-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
to
H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Therefore, integrating in time, taking the second moment, and applying the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y^{N}(t_{1})-Y^{N}(t_{2})\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\|\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\eta^{N}(r)\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}+\mathbb{E}\|R_{N}\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}r\right)^{2}

(12.34)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C|t_{2}-t_{1}|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathbb{E}\|\eta^{N}\|_{H^{2-s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\eta^{N}\|_{H^{1-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}r

(12.35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\eta^{N}(0)\|_{H^{2-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\,,

where we used the stationarity of
\eta^{N}
in the last step. The inequality (
12.29
) now follows immediately from the bound (
12.10
), since our choice of
m
implies
m-2>1+\frac{d}{2}
. We now turn our attention to the estimate (
12.30
). Note that for any smooth
\varphi
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lvert\langle M^{N}(t)-M^{N}(s),\varphi\rangle\right\rvert^{p}\right]\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{s}^{t}|\nabla\varphi(X_{t}^{i})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t\bigg)^{\frac{p}{2}}

(12.36)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C|t-s|^{\frac{p}{2}}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.

(12.37)

We take
\varphi
to be elements of a basis of
H^{m}_{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the inequality (
12.30
) holds as long as

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\|\nabla\varphi_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}<\infty.

The above estimate follows when we take
m>d/2+1
.
Finally, we note that by an argument entirely analogous to the one showing (
12.29
) and (
12.30
), we can also show

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\eta^{N}(t)\|_{H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\right]<\infty,

(12.38)

by using that
M^{N}(0)=0
and
Y^{N}(0)=\eta^{N}(0)
together with (
12.10
). Combining (
12.29
), (
12.30
), and (
12.38
) we obtain the tightness of the laws of
(\eta^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
as a consequence of
[
27
, Theorem 2.2]
and Chebyshev’s inequality. Specifically, we use the embedding of
W^{1-\varepsilon,2}([0,T];H^{-m+\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))+W^{\frac{1}{2},p}([0,T];H^{-m+\epsilon}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
into
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
for
p>2
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Step
3.
In light of Step 2 and the Skorokhod representation theorem, passing to a subsequence (which we do not relabel) we can find a new probability space
(\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
, a new sequence
(\tilde{\eta}^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
, and a limiting random variable
\eta
such that
\tilde{\eta}^{N}
is equal in law to
\eta^{N}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and converges
\tilde{{\mathbb{P}}}
-a.s to
\eta
in
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
. In this step, we claim that (
12.18
) and (
12.19
) hold true.

To this end, for each
t>0
, we denote by
r_{t}
the restriction operator from
C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
to
C([0,t];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
and define a filtration
(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by letting
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}=\sigma(r_{t}\eta)
for
t>0
, i.e. the sigma algebra generated by
r_{t}\eta
. Recalling the definition (
12.17
) of
t\mapsto M(t)
, we will show that that for all times
s<t
, functions
\varphi,\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and bounded, continuous functions
\Gamma:C([0,s];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\mapsto\mathbb{R}
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Gamma(r_{s}\eta)\langle M(t)-M(s),\varphi\rangle\right]=0.

(12.39)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma(r_{s}\eta)\big(\langle M(t),\varphi\rangle\langle M(t),\psi\rangle-\langle M(s),\varphi\rangle\langle M(s),\psi\rangle

(12.40)

\displaystyle\qquad-(t-s)\langle\rho_{\beta}\nabla\varphi,\nabla\psi\rangle\big)\big]=0.

By the definition of conditional expectation and Egorov’s theorem, (
12.39
) and (
12.40
) imply (
12.18
) and (
12.19
) hold true with respect to the filtration
(\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. To prove (
12.39
) and (
12.40
), define
t\mapsto\tilde{M}^{N}(t)
as in Step 2 but with
\tilde{\eta}_{N}
in place of
\eta^{N}
.

Since
\tilde{\eta}^{N}
and
\eta^{N}
are equal in law, (
12.20
) implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma(r_{s}\tilde{\eta}_{N})\langle\tilde{M}^{N}(t)-\tilde{M}^{N}(s),\varphi\rangle\big]=0.

(12.41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\Gamma(r_{s}\tilde{\eta}_{N})\big(\langle\tilde{M}_{N}(t),\varphi\rangle\langle\tilde{M}_{N}(t),\psi\rangle-\langle\tilde{M}_{N}(s),\varphi\rangle\langle\tilde{M}_{N}(s),\psi\rangle

(12.42)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(t-s)\langle\rho_{\beta}\nabla\varphi,\nabla\psi\rangle\big)\big]=0.

By a calculation similar to (
12.33
), it follows that for each
t\leq T

\bigg\|\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{R}_{N}(s)ds\bigg\|_{H^{-(m+1)}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq CN^{-1/2}\|\tilde{\eta}_{N}\|_{C([0,T];H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}^{2},

(12.43)

which converges to zero
\tilde{P}
-a.s. As a consequence, we obtain
\langle\tilde{M}^{N}(t),\varphi\rangle
converges
\tilde{P}
-a.s. to
\langle M(t),\varphi\rangle
as a consequence of the a.s. convergence of
\tilde{\eta}^{N}
to
\eta^{\infty}
. In addition, for all
t>0
, the sequence
\langle\tilde{M}^{N}(t),\varphi\rangle
is uniformly bounded in

We finish this section with the implication of the coercivity property, that is used in the proof to show the Hilbert-Schmidt property of the appropriate operators.

Lemma 12.8
.

Assume that
\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}
satisfies

-\langle\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\phi,\phi\rangle\geq c\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}

for some
c>0
, then

\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\nabla e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rho_{\beta}}}\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t\leq\frac{\|\phi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{2c}.

We use the short hand notation
\phi_{t}=e^{t\mathcal{L}^{*}_{\rho_{\beta}}}\phi
. We then have

\partial_{t}\phi_{t}-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}^{*}\phi_{t}=0\,.

Multiplying by
\phi_{t}
and integrating, we obtain the identity

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t}\|\phi_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}-\langle\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{\beta}}\phi_{t},\phi_{t}\rangle=0.

Applying the coercivity bound, we obtain

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t}\|\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}+2c\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq 0.

Integrating in time we obtain

2c\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\nabla\phi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\;\mathop{}\!\mathup{d}t\leq\|\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\,,

which is the desired estimate.
∎
