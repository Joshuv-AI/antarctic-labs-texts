Viscous regularization of the MHD equations Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 7, 2026.
Tuan Anh Dao, Lukas Lundgren, Murtazo Nazarov

Abstract
Nonlinear conservation laws such as the system of ideal magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equations may develop singularities over time. In these situations, viscous regularization is a common approach to regain regularity of the solution. In this paper, we present a new viscous flux to regularize the MHD equations which holds many attractive properties. In particular, we prove that the proposed viscous flux preserves positivity of density and internal energy, satisfies the minimum entropy principle, is consistent with all generalized entropies, and is Galilean and rotationally invariant. We also provide a variation of the viscous flux that conserves angular momentum. To make the analysis more useful for numerical schemes, the divergence of the magnetic field is not assumed to be zero. Using continuous finite elements, we show several numerical experiments including contact waves and magnetic reconnection.



keywords

MHD, viscous regularization, artificial viscosity, entropy principles

Introduction

Dynamics of conducting fluids, such as plasma, when viscosity and magnetic resistivity are neglected can be numerically studied by solving the equations of ideal MHD. Besides accuracy and stability, preserving physical features of numerical solutions is very desirable and sometimes is even essential. For instance, the laws of physics break down when fluid density or internal energy becomes negative. Many difficulties in avoiding such situations come from the complex nonlinearity of the advective MHD flux
[
33
]
. A common solution is to regularize the equations by adding a vanishing viscous term, see e.g.,
[
19
,
23
,
8
]
. For that purpose, a straightforward choice of the viscous regularization would be taking the resistive model of the MHD equations. However, the resistive MHD flux suffers from serious downsides.
For example, unless the thermal diffusivity is zero, the resistive MHD flux is incompatible with the minimum entropy principle
[
16
]
.

One way to overcome these issues is to, instead, consider a monolithic regularization to the MHD equations
[
8
]
. The limitation with the monolithic flux includes that it is incompatible with the divergence-free nature of the magnetic field,
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
; it does not conserve angular momentum; and it is too simplified since an equal amount of viscosity is added to all conserved variables. Therefore, we propose a more general and improved viscous regularization for the ideal MHD equations. Our idea is to combine the Guermond-Popov (GP) viscous flux
[
16
]
for compressible flows with the resistive flux for the magnetic component of the MHD equations. In contrast to the monolithic flux, the GP flux can be physically motivated. On the other hand, the resistive flux for the electromagnetic part is suitable for many numerical schemes with mechanisms to guarantee
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
. Our main focus is to carefully investigate positivity, conservation properties, entropy principles, and Galilean and rotational invariance of the new viscous regularization at the PDE level.

One of the conserved quantities we investigate more closely is angular momentum which is believed by physicists to be an important conserved property of fluid models. It is known that viscous regularizations of compressible flow can cause the model to lose conservation of angular momentum due to added diffusion of mass
[
27
,
32
]
. Additionally, since many numerical methods include so-called artificial mass diffusivity as stabilization, it is challenging to find numerical approximations that conserve angular momentum. In this work, we show that the GP flux
[
16
]
does not conserve angular momentum and our proposed viscous flux conserves angular momentum while also satisfying entropy principles.

When
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\neq 0
, different techniques are needed when solving the MHD equations. To maintain thermodynamic consistency, a nonconservative source term proportional to the divergence is usually added, for example the Powell term
[
28
]
. Unless a specialized mechanism is built into the scheme, it is often necessary to further clean the divergence using, e.g., the generalized Lagrange multiplier (GLM)
[
12
]
. Whether such additions, e.g., of the GLM or the Powell term, take away the good properties of the viscous regularization is also investigated in this work.

. Overall, the theory and the numerical experiments suggest that the proposed viscous fluxes are suitable for artificial viscosity methods as well as
being a viable candidate
for modeling fluid viscosity and magnetic resistivity.

2.1
The nonconservative divergence source term

An important physical property of the magnetic field is that the divergence of it is pointwise zero. However, without any special consideration, numerical solutions to (
2.1
) do not satisfy this property exactly. In that case, several physical properties related to the magnetic field are violated, and numerical methods may converge to wrong solutions, see e.g.,
[
5
]
. When
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\neq 0
is unavoidable, ensuring thermodynamic consistency often involves adding a nonconservative term in the form of,

\Psi(\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}{\boldsymbol{B}}\\
\alpha_{E}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{B}}\\
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}{\boldsymbol{u}}\end{pmatrix}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}),

(2.6)

where
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}
are constants. Popular divergence source terms are
\Psi_{\text{Powell}}

\coloneqq

\Psi(-1,-1,-1)

[
28
]
,
\Psi_{\text{Janhunen}}

\coloneqq

\Psi(0,0,-1)

[
20
]
, and
\Psi_{\text{BB}}

\coloneqq

\Psi(-1,0,0)

[
5
]
. With the Powell source term being the most popular choice, symmetrization
[
15
]
, consistency with the Lorentz force, and entropy principles of the MHD equations are recovered when
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\neq 0
.
For the rest of the paper, we do not assume that
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
.

3
A Guermond-Popov (GP) type viscous flux

Our idea is to regularize the hydrodynamic part of (
2.1
) following the work of
[
16
]
and electromagnetic part using the resistive MHD flux, see e.g.,
[
29
]
. We propose the viscous flux
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\textsf{{U}})
in (
2.5
) to be
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\text{GP}}(\textsf{{U}})
defined as

\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\text{GP}}(\textsf{{U}})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\kappa\nabla\rho\\
\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
\kappa\nabla(\rho e)+\frac{|{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}}{2}\kappa\nabla\rho+\mu\rho(\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+\eta\big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\big){\boldsymbol{B}}\\
\eta\big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\big)\end{pmatrix},

(3.1)

where
\kappa>0,\mu>0,\eta>0
are viscosity coefficients, and
\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right)
. The resulting GP-MHD system reads

{\partial}_{t}\textsf{{U}}+\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\textsf{{U}})+\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\textsf{{U}})=\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\text{GP}}(\textsf{{U}})+\Psi.

(3.2)

This section analyzes several physically relevant properties of the viscosity regularized system (
3.2
).

3.1
Positivity of density

Theorem 3.1
(Positivity of density).

Assume sufficient smoothness of
\kappa
,
\rho
,
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, and boundedness of
{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}
,
\kappa\nabla\rho
,
{\partial}_{t}\rho+\nabla{\cdot\,}(\rho{\boldsymbol{u}})
. With the addition of the viscous term

Since the resulting mass equation coincides with the Euler mass equation regularized by the GP flux, see

]
for the proof of Theorem

Theorem 3.4
(Minimum entropy principle).

Assume sufficient smoothness and that the density and the internal energy uniformly converge to stationary constant states

\square

Proof.

We first prove that

Isolating the signed terms in the equation of Lemma

3.3
Positivity of internal energy

Theorem 3.5
(Positivity of internal energy).

The specific internal energy
e
and the internal energy
\rho e
of the GP-MHD system (
3.2
) always remain positive given that the initial data fulfills
e>0
.

\square

Proof.

From the assumption that the temperature is positive,
T=s_{e}^{-1}>0
, we have
s_{e}>0
where
s=s(e,\rho^{-1})
and

defined by the equation of state (

). Given positivity of density
\rho>0
, we have

[
16
, Appendix A.1]
. The minimum entropy principle implies that

, where

is the specific internal energy at

, which leads to

for all
\rho>0
since

. Therefore, given physically correct initial data, the specific internal energy is positive at all times

, and so is the internal energy

due to the proven positivity of density.

If an ideal gas is considered, Theorems

and

3.4
Generalized entropy inequalities

In this section, we show that the proposed regularized model is compatible with all the generalized entropy inequalities. The generalized entropies in the manner of
[

consider a large class of strictly convex entropies
-\rho\phi(s)
where

Theorem 3.6

[

The generalized entropy

\phi^{\prime}(s)>0\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\phi^{\prime}(s)}{c_{p}}-\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)>0,

\square

The result of Theorem
3.6

[

The main result of this section is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7
(Generalized entropy inequalities).

All smooth solutions to (

\displaystyle+\phi^{\prime}(s)J_{1}=\phi^{\prime}(s)s_{e}{\mathbb{G}}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+\phi^{\prime}(s)s_{e}{\mathbbm{k}}:\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}.

Multiplying the regularized mass equation with

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\partial}_{t}(\rho\phi(s))+\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{u}}\rho\phi(s))-\nabla{\cdot\,}(\kappa\rho\nabla\phi(s)+\kappa\phi(s)\nabla\rho)

(3.11)

Let
J_{2}\coloneqq\kappa\rho c_{p}^{-1}|\nabla s|^{2}+J_{1}
. It is possible to write
J_{2}
in a quadratic form as

J_{2}=\kappa\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\rho\\
\nabla e\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}J_{3}\otimes{\mathbb{I}}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\rho&\nabla e\end{pmatrix},

where
J_{3}
is obtained by applying the chain rule on
\nabla s
,

J_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{p}^{-1}\rho s_{\rho}^{2}+\rho^{-1}{\partial}_{\rho}(\rho^{2}s_{\rho})&c_{p}^{-1}s_{\rho}\rho s_{e}+\rho s_{\rho e}\\
c_{p}^{-1}\rho s_{\rho}s_{e}+\rho s_{\rho e}&c_{p}^{-1}\rho s_{e}^{2}+\rho s_{ee}\end{pmatrix}.

The
2\times 2
matrix
J_{3}
is negative definite due to the strict convexity of
-s
, see
[
8
, Lemma 4]
. We deduce from this and (
3.11
) that

-\kappa\rho\phi^{\prime\prime}(s)|\nabla s|^{2}-\phi^{\prime}(s)J_{1}\geq 0.

Therefore, the right hand side of (
3.10
) is greater than or equal to zero, which leads to the desired conclusion.

3.5
Galilean and rotational invariance

By Galilean relativity, a physical law should stay invariant under a Galilean transformation, which in this case means that the set of equations does not change under a shift of the reference frame. In the following theorem, we state this property of the GP-MHD system.

Theorem 3.8
.

The GP-MHD system (
3.2
) is Galilean invariant.

\square

Proof.

See Section
B.1
.

Rotational invariance is also a physically relevant property. This property says that the laws described by the set of equations remain valid when the observation frame is rotated by an arbitrary angle. Regarding numerical methods, many Riemann solver-based methods rely on rotational invariance, see e.g.,
[
2
]
. We look at this property of the GP-MHD system in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.9
.

The GP-MHD system (
3.2
) is rotationally invariant.

\square

Proof.

See Section
B.2
.

3.6
GLM-GP-MHD systems

Including the Powell term alone is insufficient to reduce
|\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}|
in general. As the solution evolves, it is necessary to clean up the unphysical portion of
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. A preferred approach to divergence cleaning is the hyperbolic cleaning method, see e.g.,
[
4
,
18
,
29
]
, also known as the generalized Lagrange multiplier (GLM)
[
12
]
. In this section, we investigate three variations of the GLM with the most favorable conservative properties. The first one is a classical formulation by
[
12
]
. The second one is an improvement by
[
13
]
. The third one is a modified version of the second one which compromises Galilean invariance in exchange for energy conservation.

3.6.1
The Galilean invariant extended GLM system by
[
12
]

The GLM system is obtained by adding a conserved variable
\Phi
with an extra governing equation,

{\partial}_{t}\Phi+c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=-{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi-\frac{c_{r}c_{h}}{h}\Phi,

where
c_{h}
is the divergence cleaning speed,
c_{r}
is a global constant, and
h
is a mesh size indicator. We note that the GLM system being used here is referred to as the Galilean invariant extended GLM system in
[
12
,
13
]
. The vector of conserved variables is extended to
\textsf{{U}}\coloneqq(\rho,{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\top},E,{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top},\Phi)^{\top}
. The GLM-GP-MHD system reads

{\partial}_{t}\textsf{{U}}+\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\textsf{{U}})+\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\textsf{{U}})-\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\text{GP}}(\textsf{{U}})=\Psi+\Upsilon_{\text{GLM}},

(3.12)

where all the GLM terms are gathered in
\Upsilon_{\text{GLM}}
,

\Upsilon_{\text{GLM}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
-c_{h}{\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi\\
-c_{h}\nabla\Phi\\
-{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi-\frac{c_{r}c_{h}}{h}\Phi-c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 3.10
.

The results of Theorems
3.1
(positivity of density),
3.4
(minimum entropy principle),
3.5
(positivity of internal energy), and
3.7
(generalized entropy inequalities) still hold for the GLM-GP-MHD system (
3.12
).

\square

Proof.

Theorem
3.1
follows because the mass equation remains the same in (
3.12
).

We now revisit a key step in Theorem
3.4
with (
3.12
). Multiplying the
\Upsilon_{\text{GLM}}
components in the momentum equation with
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, the magnetic equation with
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, then subtract them from the
\Upsilon_{\text{GLM}}
component in the energy equation gives zero. Therefore, (
3.5
) also holds for the GLM-GP-MHD system (
3.12
) and so the rest of the proof to Theorem
3.4
, and so Theorem
3.7
. Theorem
3.5
holds since both Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
hold.

Furthermore, the Galilean invariance of
\Upsilon_{\text{GLM}}
is known, see
[
12
]
. Thus, Galilean invariance of the GLM-GP-MHD system (
3.12
) naturally follows.

3.6.2
Nine-wave formulation by
[
13
]

By adding a contribution of
\Phi
into the total energy

E^{*}=\rho e+\frac{1}{2}\rho|{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{2},

a new GLM formulation is derived by
[
13
]
,

\Upsilon_{\text{9W-GLM}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
-c_{h}{\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi-c_{h}\Phi(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}})+\Phi{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi\\
-c_{h}\nabla\Phi\\
-{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi-c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
-c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}(\Phi{\boldsymbol{B}})+\Phi{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi\\
-c_{h}\nabla\Phi\\
-{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla\Phi-c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\end{pmatrix}.

The properties in Theorem
3.10
and Galilean invariance hold true for
\Upsilon_{\text{9W-GLM}}
with
E
being replaced by
E^{*}
. We skip the proofs here because except for the positivity of density,
\Upsilon_{\text{9W-GLM}}
has been thoroughly analyzed in
[
13
]
.

3.6.3
An energy conservative GLM formulation

If conservation of energy is of interest, we propose a new GLM formulation which is a slight modification of the GLM formulation proposed by
[
13
]
,

\Upsilon_{\text{CONS-GLM}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
-c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}(\Phi{\boldsymbol{B}})\\
-c_{h}\nabla\Phi\\
-c_{h}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\end{pmatrix}.

Similar to
\Upsilon_{\text{9W-GLM}}
, the properties in Theorem
3.10
hold true for
\Upsilon_{\text{CONS-GLM}}
with
E
being replaced by
E^{*}
. However,
\Upsilon_{\text{CONS-GLM}}
conserves
E^{*}
but is not Galilean invariant and
\Upsilon_{\text{9W-GLM}}
is Galilean invariant but does not conserve
E^{*}
.

3.7
Conservation

In this section we investigate how the viscous regularization
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\textsf{{U}})
and divergence source term
\Psi(\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}})
effect conservation properties.
To simplify the boundary integrals, we assume that the velocity
{\boldsymbol{u}}
and the magnetic field
{\boldsymbol{B}}
are compactly supported. This assumption has no conflicts with the previous assumptions in Sections
3.1
and
3.4
. The ideal MHD equations (
2.1
) with pointwise
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
conserve mass, momentum, angular momentum, total energy, magnetism and magnetic helicity. These are defined as

\displaystyle\text{Mass}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},\quad

\displaystyle\text{Momentum}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{m}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\text{Total energy}

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}E\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

\displaystyle\text{Angular momentum}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{m}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

\displaystyle\text{Magnetism}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{B}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

\displaystyle\text{Magnetic helicity}\quad

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{B}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}},

where
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a vector potential to
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, i.e.,
\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}}={\boldsymbol{B}}
. For
{\boldsymbol{B}}
to admit a vector potential
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, we require that
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is pointwise divergence-free since the divergence of curl is zero, i.e.,
\nabla{\cdot\,}(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}})=0
. Thus magnetic helicity is only conserved if
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
. The other properties in (
3.13
) can be shown to be conserved for the ideal MHD equations (
2.1
) even if
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}\neq 0
.

The viscous regularization
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\textsf{{U}})
inside (
2.5
) can effect the conserved properties. It is known that zero resistivity in the magnetic equation is required for the MHD equations to conserve magnetic helicity, see Section
C
for more details. It is also known that viscous regularization via mass diffusion, i.e.,
\kappa\neq 0
, can make flow models lose conservation of angular momentum which is a criticism brought forward against such models
[
27
,
32
]
. In Section
3.8
, we propose a variant of the viscous regularization (
3.1
) that conserves angular momentum. Lastly, the divergence source term
\Psi(\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}})
will affect conservation depending on
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}
, see e.g.,
[
28
,
20
]
.

3.8
An angular momentum conserving viscous regularization

By modifying the GP flux such that the viscous flux in the momentum equations is symmetric one can ensure that angular momentum is conserved. It is, however, also important that entropy principles still hold, meaning that the energy equation has to be adequately compensated. To this end, if conservation of angular momentum is of interest, we propose the following
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system

{\partial}_{t}\textsf{{U}}+\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\textsf{{U}})+\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\textsf{{U}})=\nabla{\cdot\,}\textsf{{F}}^{\text{GP}^{s}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\textsf{{U}})+\textsf{{F}}^{E}(\textsf{{U}})+\Psi,

(3.14)

where
\textsf{{F}}^{\text{GP}^{s}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}
is the same as
\textsf{{F}}^{\text{GP}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}
except that the viscous flux in the momentum equations is symmetric,
\textsf{{F}}^{E}
is a nonconservative contribution to the energy equation, and
\Psi
is the nonconservative divergence source term defined in Section
2.1
. The new terms
\textsf{{F}}^{\text{GP}^{s}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}
and
\textsf{{F}}^{E}
are given by

\textsf{{F}}^{\text{GP}^{s}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\textsf{{U}})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\kappa\nabla\rho\\
\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+\frac{1}{2}((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes(\kappa\nabla\rho))\\
\kappa\nabla(\rho e)+\frac{|{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}}{2}\kappa\nabla\rho+\mu\rho(\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+\eta\big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\big){\boldsymbol{B}}\\
\eta\big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\big)\end{pmatrix},

(3.15)

\textsf{{F}}^{E}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes(\kappa\nabla\rho)-(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\right){\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(3.16)

We note that in one spatial dimension the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system (
3.14
) is equivalent to the GP-MHD system (
3.2
). In more than one spatial dimension they are different in the following ways. The
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system conserves angular momentum, but not total energy whereas the GP-MHD system conserves total energy but not angular momentum. Both systems satisfy the same entropy principles. This is summarized in Theorems
3.11
and
3.12
.

Theorem 3.11
.

The results of Theorems
3.1
(positivity of density),
3.4
(minimum entropy principle),
3.5
(positivity of internal energy), and
3.7
(generalized entropy inequalities) still hold for the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system (
3.14
).

\square

Proof.

Theorem
3.1
follows because the mass equation remains the same in (
3.14
).

We now revisit a key step in Lemma
3.2
with (
3.14
). By rewriting the momentum equations as

\displaystyle\rho({\partial}_{t}{\boldsymbol{u}}+{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})+{\boldsymbol{u}}\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{f}}+\nabla p-\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle-\nabla{\cdot\,}(\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}})-\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes\kappa\nabla\rho-\kappa\nabla\rho\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)\right)=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}{\boldsymbol{B}}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}).

Multiplying them with
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, the magnetic equations with
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, then subtracting them from the energy equation gives (
3.5
). Therefore, (
3.5
) also holds for the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system (
3.14
) and so the rest of the proof to Theorem
3.4
, and so Theorem
3.7
. Theorem
3.5
holds since both Theorems
3.1
and
3.4
hold.

Theorem 3.12
.

The GP-MHD and
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system satisfy the following conservation properties:

1.

Both systems conserve mass;

2.

Both systems conserve magnetism if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
;

3.

Both systems conserve momentum if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}=0
;

4.

The GP-MHD system conserves total energy if
\alpha_{E}=0
but the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system does not;

5.

The
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system conserves angular momentum if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}=0
but the GP-MHD system does not.

\square

Proof.

We divide the proof of this theorem into several parts. We follow a similar proof technique as
[
7
,
22
]
. We denote the
L^{2}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\right)
inner product as
(\cdot,\cdot)
.

(i) Conservation of mass.

The mass equation is the same for the GP-MHD and
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD systems. Integrating the mass equation and using the divergence theorem, we obtain

\partial_{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=0.

This shows that mass is conserved.

(ii) Conservation of magnetism.

The GP-MHD and
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system have the same magnetic equation. Taking the inner product of the magnetic equation with a constant unit vector
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
yields

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})+(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})-(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})-\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\eta\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{B}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}).

Performing integration by parts on some of the terms yields

(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})-({\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})+({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})+\left(\eta\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\right),\nabla{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\right)=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{B}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}).

Since
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
is a constant vector we obtain
\partial_{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{B}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})
which shows that magnetism is conserved if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
.

(iii) Conservation of momentum.

We start with the GP-MHD system. Taking the inner product of the momentum equations with a constant unit vector
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
yields

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})-({\boldsymbol{m}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})-(p,\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})+({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2},\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\right)+\left(\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}).

Since
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
is a constant vector we obtain
\partial_{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{m}}_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i})
, which means that momentum is conserved if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}=0
. The same steps can be repeated for the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system.

(iv) Conservation of total energy.

We integrate the energy equation and
use
the divergence theorem to obtain

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\partial}_{t}E\,\mathrm{d}x=\alpha_{E}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}})({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}})\,\mathrm{d}x.

This shows that total energy is conserved if
\alpha_{E}=0
. Repeating the same steps for the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system yields

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\partial}_{t}E\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\alpha_{E}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}})({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes(\kappa\nabla\rho)-(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}})\right)\right){\cdot}{\boldsymbol{u}}\,\mathrm{d}x\neq 0.

This shows that total energy is not conserved for the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system.

(v) Conservation of angular momentum.

We define
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{x}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}
so that angular momentum can be expressed as
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}({\boldsymbol{m}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{x}})_{i}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=({\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})
. We note that
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}
has the property that
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}=0
and
\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}=0
. We start by considering angular momentum conservation of the GP-MHD system. Taking the inner product of the momentum equations with a constant unit vector
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}
and integrating over the domain yields

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{m}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+(\nabla p,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})\\
-\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\right),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}).\end{split}

Expanding the convection operators yields

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+({\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{m}}+{\boldsymbol{m}}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-\left({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}+(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)\\
+\left(\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}),\end{split}

since
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}=0
. Integration by parts on
{\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{m}}
yields

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-({\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{m}})-({\boldsymbol{m}}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+({\boldsymbol{m}}(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{B}})\\
+((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+\left(\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}).\end{split}

(3.17)

By using (
A.1
) it can be shown that

\begin{split}({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{B}})&=\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{B}})+\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{B}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{B}})=\frac{1}{2}((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{B}})+\frac{1}{2}((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{B}})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{B}})+\frac{1}{2}((\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{B}})=0,\end{split}

since
(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}+\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}=0.
The same procedure can be used to show that
({\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{m}})=({\boldsymbol{u}}{\cdot}\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rho)=0
. Inserting this into (
3.17
) gives

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})+\left(\mu\rho\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)+\left((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)=0.\end{split}

(3.18)

Using that the contraction between a symmetric and anti-symmetric matrix with zero diagonal is zero
[
21
, Ch 11.2.1]
, one can show that

\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu\rho\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}})^{\top}\right),\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu\rho\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}+\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)^{\top}\right),\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}\right).

(3.19)

Since
\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}=-\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}^{\top}
we are left with

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-\left((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right)\neq 0,\end{split}

even if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}=0
. Thus the GP-MHD system does not conserve angular momentum.

We now consider the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system. Repeating the same steps as before we are instead left with

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-\frac{1}{2}\left((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}+\left((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)^{\top},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}\right).\end{split}

Next, we use the same trick as performed in (
3.19
) to obtain

\begin{split}(\partial_{t}{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})&=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})-\frac{1}{4}\left((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}+\left((\kappa\nabla\rho)\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}\right)^{\top},\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i})^{\top}\right)\\
&=\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}((\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}){\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}),\end{split}

since
\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}=-\nabla{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{i}^{\top}
. Thus, angular momentum is conserved for the
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD system if
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{m}}=0
.
A numerical validation of this property can be found in Section
F
.

4
Numerical investigation

In this section, we demonstrate
several
numerical properties of the GLM-GP-MHD system (
3.12
) and GLM-
\text{GP}^{s}
-MHD sytem (
3.14
). The continuous Lagrange finite elements are used to discretize space and a fourth-order SSP explicit Runge-Kutta method
[
30
]
is used to discretize time.

4.1
Finite element discretization

Instead of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in the continuous analysis, the computational domain is an open bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We consider a finite partition of the bounded polytope
\overline{\Omega}\approx\Omega
into a finite number of disjoint simplex elements
K
,
\overline{\Omega}=\uplus K
such that no vertex of any element is hanged on an edge of another element. At a fixed time
t
, we aim to find the finite element solution
\textsf{{U}}_{h}(t)\coloneqq(\rho_{h},{\boldsymbol{m}}_{h},E_{h},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{h})^{\top}
in
{\mathbfcal W}_{h}
,

4.2
Residual-based viscosity (RV)

We employ the residual viscosity method
[
24
]
for our finite element discretization where the proposed viscous flux works as a stabilization term. The RV method is used to scale the viscosity coefficients such that the discretization is stable while still being high-order accurate in space. Several benefits of the residual viscosity method are: (i) provable convergence to the unique entropy solution using implicit time-stepping; (ii) arbitrary high-order accuracy for smooth solutions
[
9
]
; (iii) being simple to construct and implement.

At every nodal point
i
, the viscosity coefficient is computed as

{\varepsilon}_{h,i}\coloneqq\min\left(\frac{1}{2}h_{i}|\lambda_{\max,i}|,C_{E}h_{i}^{2}\ |R_{i}|\right),

where
h_{i}
is a local mesh size indicator, that is a nodal value of the mesh function
h_{h}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}
computed by the following projection problem: Find
h_{h}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}
such that

(h_{h},v)+\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}(|K|^{2/d}\nabla h_{h},v)_{K}=(k^{-1}|K|^{1/d},v),\quad\forall v\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{h}

where
|K|
is the
volume
of the element
K
,
k
is the polynomial order. Moreover
\lambda_{\max,i}
is the exact or an estimated upper bound of the local maximum wave speed,
C_{E}
is a scaling number
, and
R_{i}
is the nodal value of the PDE residual. Unless stated,
C_{E}
is set to 1.0. See
[
9
]
for technical details and experimental results of the RV method. The different viscosities are set as
\kappa=\mu=\eta={\varepsilon}_{h}
, see the unit analysis in
[
9
]
.

4.3
1D contact wave problem

Contact discontinuities are an interesting phenomenon in gas dynamics. A contact wave or a contact line is a solution to (
2.1
) in which the velocity
{\boldsymbol{u}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)={\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}
, the magnetic field
{\boldsymbol{B}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)={\boldsymbol{B}}_{0}
, and the pressure
p({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=p_{0}
are constant but the density is a discontinuity. This section demonstrates some advantages of the GP flux over the more commonly used resistive MHD flux. The resistive MHD flux is defined as

\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{\text{r}}(\textsf{{U}})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0\\
2\mu\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+\lambda\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathbb{I}}\\
(2\mu\nabla^{s}{\boldsymbol{u}}+\lambda\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{u}}{\mathbb{I}}){\boldsymbol{u}}+\kappa_{T}\nabla T+\eta\big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\big){\boldsymbol{B}}\\
\eta\big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\big)\end{pmatrix}.

(4.2)

Assume that the contact wave is a solution of (
2.1
) regularized by the resistive MHD flux. The density is a discontinuous function which satisfies the mass equation

{\partial}_{t}\rho+\nabla{\cdot\,}(\rho{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0})=0.

Inserting
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{0},p_{0},\rho
into the energy equation gives

{\partial}_{t}(\rho e)+\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\rho e)-\nabla{\cdot\,}(\kappa_{T}\nabla T)=0.

(4.3)

Consider the simplest case of ideal gases, due to (
4.1
), if the pressure is a constant, then
\rho e
is also a constant. This observation shows that (
4.3
) holds only if the thermal diffusivity
\kappa_{T}\equiv 0
. Apart from having no physical meaning, zero thermal diffusivity leads to spurious oscillations of the numerical solutions, see
[
8
,
25
]
.

We repeat the above analysis on the GP flux. The density solution instead satisfies

{\partial}_{t}\rho+\nabla{\cdot\,}(\rho{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0})=\nabla{\cdot\,}(\kappa\nabla\rho).

Again, we insert
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{0},p_{0},\rho
into the energy equation to obtain

{\partial}_{t}(\rho e)+\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\rho e)-\nabla{\cdot\,}(\kappa\nabla(\rho e))+\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}^{2}({\partial}_{t}\rho+\nabla{\cdot\,}(\rho{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0})-\nabla{\cdot\,}(\kappa\nabla\rho))=0

which holds trivially when substituting the ideal equation of state (
4.1
).

Appendix B
Proof of Galilean and rotational invariance of (
3.2
)

B.1
Galilean invariance

For readability, the analysis is done in 2D. Extension to 3D is trivial. Consider a shift of the time-space frame from
(t,x_{1},x_{2})
to
(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
. The inertial frame
(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
moves with a constant velocity of
V
,
|V|\ll c
along the
x_{1}
-direction relatively to the reference frame
(t,x_{1},x_{2})
. A particle moving at velocity
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2})^{\top}
in
(t,x_{1},x_{2})
is observed as moving at velocity
{\boldsymbol{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\top}
in
(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
,

\begin{array}[]{lll}\tau=t,&\xi_{1}=x_{1}-Vt,&\xi_{2}=x_{2},\\
&v_{1}=u_{1}-V,&v_{2}=u_{2}.\end{array}

Of physical relevance, we consider the magnetic limit of Galilean electromagnetism, see
[
11
]
. The displacement current is neglected in the non-relativistic MHD (
2.1
), yielding that the magnetic field
{\boldsymbol{B}}=(b_{1},b_{2})^{\top}
stays invariant under the Galilean transformation.
By partial derivative rules, we have

\begin{array}[]{lll}{\partial}_{t}={\partial}_{\tau}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}},&{\partial}_{x_{1}}={\partial}_{\xi_{1}},&{\partial}_{x_{2}}={\partial}_{\xi_{2}}.\end{array}

To begin with, we consider the mass equation,

{\partial}_{t}\rho+{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\rho u_{1})+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\rho u_{2})={\partial}_{x_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{x_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{x_{2}}\rho)

Rewrite the equation in the new reference frame,

{\partial}_{\tau}\rho-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho(v_{1}+V))+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{2})={\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho),

which can be simplified as

{\partial}_{\tau}\rho+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{2})={\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho).

The mass equation is Galilean invariant because it stays the same in
(\tau,\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
.

We continue with the momentum equation,

{\partial}_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\rho u_{1}\\
\rho u_{2}\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}\rho u_{1}^{2}+p\\
\rho u_{1}u_{2}\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\rho u_{1}u_{2}\\
\rho u_{2}^{2}+p\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{1}^{2}\\
-b_{1}b_{2}\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-b_{1}b_{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=

{\partial}_{x_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}\mu\rho{\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{1}+\kappa({\partial}_{x_{1}}\rho)u_{1}\\
\mu\rho\frac{{\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{2}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{1}}{2}+\kappa({\partial}_{x_{1}}\rho)u_{2}\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\mu\rho\frac{{\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{2}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{1}}{2}+\kappa({\partial}_{x_{2}}\rho)u_{1}\\
\mu\rho{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{2}+\kappa({\partial}_{x_{2}}\rho)u_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

+\begin{pmatrix}-b_{1}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2})\\
-b_{2}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2})\end{pmatrix}.

The magnetic contribution stays invariant, meaning that

\begin{array}[]{cc}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{1}^{2}\\
-b_{1}b_{2}\end{pmatrix}={\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{1}^{2}\\
-b_{1}b_{2}\end{pmatrix},&{\partial}_{x_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-b_{1}b_{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}={\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}-b_{1}b_{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix},\end{array}

\begin{pmatrix}-b_{1}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2})\\
-b_{2}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-b_{1}({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{2})\\
-b_{2}({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{2})\end{pmatrix}.

The left hand side of the first momentum equation without the magnetic terms reads

\begin{array}[]{c}{\partial}_{t}(\rho u_{1})+{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\rho u_{1}^{2}+p)+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\rho u_{1}u_{2})\\
={\partial}_{\tau}(\rho(v_{1}+V))-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho(v_{1}+V))+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho(v_{1}+V)^{2}+p)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho(v_{1}+V)v_{2})\\
={\partial}_{\tau}(\rho v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho v_{1}^{2})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{1}v_{2})+V({\partial}_{\tau}\rho+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{2}))\\
={\partial}_{\tau}(\rho v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho v_{1}^{2})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{1}v_{2})+V({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho)).\end{array}

The right hand side of the first momentum equation without the magnetic terms is

\begin{array}[]{c}{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\mu\rho{\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{1}+\kappa({\partial}_{x_{1}}\rho)u_{1})+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\left(\mu\rho\frac{{\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{2}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{1}}{2}+\kappa({\partial}_{x_{2}}\rho)u_{1}\right)\\
={\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\mu\rho{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(v_{1}+V)+\kappa({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)(v_{1}+V))\\
+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\left(\mu\rho\frac{{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}v_{2}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(v_{1}+V)}{2}+\kappa({\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho)(v_{1}+V)\right)\\
={\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\mu\rho{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}v_{1}+\kappa({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\left(\mu\rho\frac{{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}v_{2}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}v_{1}}{2}+\kappa({\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho)v_{1}\right)\\
+V({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho)).\end{array}

The term
V({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho))
appearing on both sides of the equation are cancelled. We obtain the same equation in the new coordinates. Similar derivation can be done on the second momentum equation. Therefore, the momentum equation is also Galilean invariant.

For convenience, we split the energy equation into four parts,
\text{LHS}_{1}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

+

\text{LHS}_{2}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

=

\text{RHS}_{1}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

+

\text{RHS}_{2}^{x_{1},x_{2}}
, where

\displaystyle\text{LHS}_{1}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

\displaystyle={\partial}_{t}E+{\partial}_{x_{1}}(u_{1}(E+p))+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(u_{2}(E+p)),

\displaystyle\text{LHS}_{2}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

\displaystyle={\partial}_{x_{1}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{1}^{2}\right)u_{1}-b_{1}b_{2}u_{2}\right)+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\left(-b_{1}b_{2}u_{1}+\left(\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{2}^{2}\right)u_{2}\right),

\displaystyle\text{RHS}_{1}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

\displaystyle={\partial}_{x_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\rho e))+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\rho e))

\displaystyle+{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}\kappa{\partial}_{x_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{u}}|^{2}\kappa{\partial}_{x_{2}}\rho)

\displaystyle+{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\mu\rho u_{1}{\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{1}+\mu\rho\frac{1}{2}({\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{2}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{1})u_{2})

\displaystyle+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\mu\rho\frac{1}{2}({\partial}_{x_{1}}u_{2}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{1})u_{1}+\mu\rho u_{2}{\partial}_{x_{2}}u_{2})

\displaystyle+{\partial}_{x_{1}}(\eta b_{2}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{2}-{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{1}))

\displaystyle+{\partial}_{x_{2}}(\eta b_{1}({\partial}_{x_{x}}b_{1}-{\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{2})),

\displaystyle\text{RHS}_{2}^{x_{1},x_{2}}

\displaystyle=-(u_{1}b_{1}+u_{2}b_{2})({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2}).

Applying (
2.3
) on
E
gives

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{LHS}_{1}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&\text{LHS}_{1}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}V^{2}\left[{\partial}_{\tau}\rho+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{2})\right]\\
&+V\left[{\partial}_{\tau}(\rho v_{1})+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\rho v_{1}^{2}+p)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\rho v_{1}v_{2})\right],\\
\text{LHS}_{2}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&\text{LHS}_{2}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}+V\left[{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{B}}|^{2}-b_{1}^{2})-{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(b_{1}b_{2})\right],\\
\text{RHS}_{1}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&\text{RHS}_{1}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}V^{2}\left[{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho)\right]\\
&+V\big[{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(v_{1}\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\rho)+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(v_{1}\kappa{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\rho)\\
&\quad\quad+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}(\mu\rho{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}v_{1})+\frac{1}{2}{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(\mu\rho{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}v_{1}+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}v_{2})\big],\\
\text{RHS}_{2}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&\text{RHS}_{2}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}-Vb_{1}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2}).\end{array}

The terms associated with
\frac{1}{2}V^{2}
are cancelled due to the mass equation. The terms associated with
V
are canceled due to the momentum equation. Therefore, the energy equation is also Galilean invariant.

Finally, we consider the magnetic equation,

{\partial}_{t}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
u_{1}b_{2}-u_{2}b_{1}\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}u_{2}b_{1}-u_{1}b_{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix}=

{\partial}_{x_{1}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\eta({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{2}-{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{1})\end{pmatrix}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\eta({\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{1}-{\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{2})\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-u_{1}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2})\\
-u_{2}({\partial}_{x_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{x_{2}}b_{2})\end{pmatrix}.

Let us use
\text{LHS}^{x_{1},x_{2}},\text{RHS}^{x_{1},x_{2}}
as common notations to respectively denote the left hand side and the right hand side as functions of the spatial variables
x_{1},x_{2}
. The first magnetic equation can be written as

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{LHS}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&{\partial}_{\tau}b_{1}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}(v_{2}b_{1}-(v_{1}+V)b_{2})\\
=&\text{LHS}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{2},\\
\text{RHS}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&\eta({\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{1}-{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{2})-(v_{1}+V)({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{2})\\
=&\text{RHS}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{2}.\end{array}

The second magnetic equation can be written as

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{LHS}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&{\partial}_{\tau}b_{2}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{2}+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}((v_{1}+V)b_{2}-v_{2}b_{1})\\
=&\text{LHS}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}-V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{2}+V{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{2},\\
\text{RHS}^{x_{1},x_{2}}=&\eta({\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{1}-{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{2})-v_{2}({\partial}_{\xi_{1}}b_{1}+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}b_{2})\\
=&\text{RHS}^{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}.\end{array}

Therefore, the magnetic equation is Galilean invariant. It can be seen that without the Powell term, the system would not be Galilean invariant. This was mentioned earlier by
[
28
]
.

B.2
Rotational invariance

Consider the same equations in a new system of coordinates
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\xi_{3})^{\top}
by the rotation
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\boldsymbol{R}}{\boldsymbol{x}}
. The observation frame is rotated through an angle
\theta_{y}
around the
x_{2}-
axis by the rotation matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\psi}
and through an angle
\theta
around the
x_{3}-
axis by
{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\theta}
,

{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\psi&0&\sin\psi\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\psi&0&\cos\psi\end{pmatrix},\;{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta&0\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.

The full rotation matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}
is

{\boldsymbol{R}}\coloneqq{\boldsymbol{R}}(\psi,\theta)={\boldsymbol{R}}_{\psi}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{\theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\psi\cos\theta&\cos\psi\sin\theta&\sin\psi\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
-\sin\psi\cos\theta&-\sin\psi\sin\theta&\cos\psi\end{pmatrix}.

It is clear that
{\boldsymbol{R}}
is an orthogonal matrix
{\boldsymbol{R}}^{-1}={\boldsymbol{R}}^{\top}
. For a given state, the conserved variable vector
\textsf{{U}}_{0}=(\rho_{0},{\boldsymbol{m}}_{0},E_{0},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{0})^{\top}
is rotated to
{\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}}_{0}
by the transformation matrix

{\boldsymbol{T}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&{\boldsymbol{0}}^{\top}&0&{\boldsymbol{0}}^{\top}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{R}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\mathbb{O}}\\
0&{\boldsymbol{0}}^{\top}&1&{\boldsymbol{0}}^{\top}\\
{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\mathbb{O}}&{\boldsymbol{0}}&{\boldsymbol{R}}\end{pmatrix},

where
{\boldsymbol{0}}
is the zero vector of size 3 and
{\mathbb{O}}
is the
3\times 3
zero matrix.
The relation of the derivatives is

\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{\xi_{3}}\end{pmatrix}={\boldsymbol{R}}\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{3}}\end{pmatrix}\text{ or }\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{3}}\end{pmatrix}={\boldsymbol{R}}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{\xi_{3}}\end{pmatrix}.

Rotational invariance of (
2.1
) means that (
2.1
) does not change under the above rotation,

{\partial}_{t}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\textsf{{F}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\textsf{{F}}_{2}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\partial}_{\xi_{3}}\textsf{{F}}_{3}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})=0,

(B.1)

where
\textsf{{F}}(\textsf{{U}})\coloneqq\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}(\textsf{{U}})+\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{B}}}(\textsf{{U}})
, and
\textsf{{F}}_{1}(\textsf{{U}}),\textsf{{F}}_{2}(\textsf{{U}}),\textsf{{F}}_{3}(\textsf{{U}})
are
x_{1}-
,
x_{2}-
,
x_{3}-
components of
F
.
Because
{\partial}_{t}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})={\boldsymbol{T}}{\partial}_{t}(\textsf{{U}})
, substituting (
2.1
) for
{\partial}_{t}(\textsf{{U}})
in (
B.1
) gives

{\boldsymbol{T}}\left({\boldsymbol{R}}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{\xi_{3}}\end{pmatrix}\right){\cdot}\begin{pmatrix}\textsf{{F}}_{1}(\textsf{{U}})\\
\textsf{{F}}_{2}(\textsf{{U}})\\
\textsf{{F}}_{3}(\textsf{{U}})\end{pmatrix}={\partial}_{\xi_{1}}\textsf{{F}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\partial}_{\xi_{2}}\textsf{{F}}_{2}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\partial}_{\xi_{3}}\textsf{{F}}_{3}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}}).

We now group the terms associating to
{\partial}_{\xi_{1}}
to get an equivalent statement to (
B.1
). Without loss of generality, the following theorem states rotational invariance of (
2.1
) in the form of
[
2
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma B.1
.

The nonlinear advective MHD flux
\textsf{{F}}(\textsf{{U}})
satisfies

{\boldsymbol{R}}_{11}\textsf{{F}}_{1}(\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{12}\textsf{{F}}_{2}(\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{13}\textsf{{F}}_{3}(\textsf{{U}})={\boldsymbol{T}}^{-1}\textsf{{F}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}}).

\square

Direct substitution can be used to verify the equality in Lemma
B.1
. We now consider the case where Lemma
B.1
holds and there is an additional source term
\Psi
. Assume that
\Psi=\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}+\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{2}}}+\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{3}}}
where
\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{i}}}
contains only quasilinear terms of first derivatives with respect to
x_{i}
, the condition of rotational invariance on
\Psi
is

\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}(\textsf{{U}})={\boldsymbol{T}}^{-1}\left({\boldsymbol{R}}_{11}\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{21}\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{2}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{31}\Psi^{{\partial}_{x_{3}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})\right).

(B.2)

The equality (
B.2
) is true if the source term
\Psi
is chosen to be one of the divergence source terms
\Psi(\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}})
for any
\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\alpha_{E},\alpha_{{\boldsymbol{B}}}
. Thus, the Powell term
\Psi_{\mathrm{Powell}}
and the Janhunen term
\Psi_{\mathrm{Janhunen}}
are rotational invariant. Now we consider the regularized equation (
2.5
). The rotational invariance assumption gives an extra condition on the viscous flux,

{\boldsymbol{T}}\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{3}}\end{pmatrix}{\cdot}\;\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\textsf{{U}})=\left({\boldsymbol{R}}\begin{pmatrix}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{2}}\\
{\partial}_{x_{3}}\end{pmatrix}\right){\cdot}\;\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},\xi}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}}),

(B.3)

where all the partial derivatives in
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},\xi}
are with respect to
\xi
. Denote
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1},\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},2},\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},3}
the three
x_{i}-
components of
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}
. The viscous flux
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}
is a quasilinear function of first order spatial derivatives,
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},j}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},j}^{{\partial}_{x_{i}}}
where
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},j}^{{\partial}_{x_{i}}}
contains all the
{\partial}_{x_{i}}
terms of
\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},j}
, for
j=1,2,3
.

The following proposition simplifies (
B.3
) and shows a slightly more convenient way to check if a viscous regularization to (
2.5
) preserves rotational invariance.

Proposition B.2
.

A viscous flux
\textsf{{F}}_{\mathcal{V}}
in (
2.5
) is rotationally invariant if the following statements are true:

\begin{array}[]{rl}(i)&{\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}(\textsf{{U}})={\boldsymbol{R}}_{11}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{21}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},2}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{31}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},3}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}});\\
(ii)&\begin{array}[]{r}{\boldsymbol{T}}\left({\partial}_{x_{1}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1}^{{\partial}_{x_{2}}}(\textsf{{U}})+{\partial}_{x_{2}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}(\textsf{{U}})\right)=\\
\\
\end{array}\begin{array}[]{l}{\boldsymbol{R}}_{11}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1}^{{\partial}_{x_{2}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{12}{\partial}_{x_{2}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},1}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})\\
+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{21}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},2}^{{\partial}_{x_{2}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{22}{\partial}_{x_{2}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},2}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})\\
+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{31}{\partial}_{x_{1}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},3}^{{\partial}_{x_{2}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}})+{\boldsymbol{R}}_{32}{\partial}_{x_{2}}\textsf{{F}}_{{\mathcal{V}},3}^{{\partial}_{x_{1}}}({\boldsymbol{T}}\textsf{{U}}).\end{array}\end{array}

\square

The first condition is derived by matching the
{\partial}_{x_{1}}{\partial}_{x_{1}}
terms in (
B.3
). The second condition matches the
{\partial}_{x_{1}}{\partial}_{x_{2}}
terms. Since the rotation is arbitrary, (
B.3
) follows if
(i)
and
(ii)
hold for all
\psi,\theta
.

Lemma B.3
.

The GP viscous flux
\textsf{{F}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{\mathrm{GP}}
given by (
3.1
) is rotationally invariant.

\square

It can be verified that the equalities in
proposition

B.2
hold for the GP viscous flux
\textsf{{F}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{\mathrm{GP}}
. A symbolic Matlab script to verify
lemma

B.3
is included in
section

B.2.1
.
theorem

3.9
is followed by
lemma

B.1
and
lemma

B.3
.

Appendix C
Magnetic helicity

We follow a similar proof as
[
14
, Sec 2]
.

Proposition C.1
.

Magnetic helicity is conserved if
\nu=0
and if
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
.

\square

Proof.

For
{\boldsymbol{B}}
to admit a vector potential
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, we require that
{\boldsymbol{B}}
is pointwise divergence-free since the divergence of curl is zero (
\nabla{\cdot\,}(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}})=0
). Thus magnetic helicity only exists if
\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0
. To show conservation of magnetic helicity, we start with the definition of magnetic helicity

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{A}}{\cdot}{\boldsymbol{B}}\,\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\partial_{t}({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}})=({\boldsymbol{A}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{B}})+({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t})=({\boldsymbol{A}}_{t},\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}})+({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t})\\
=(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{A}})+({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t})=({\boldsymbol{B}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{A}})+({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}}_{t})\\
=2(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{A}})+2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\\
=2(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{A}})+2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right),\\
\end{split}

(C.1)

where (
A.3
) was used. Using the identity
\nabla{\times}({\boldsymbol{u}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}})=\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}})
yields

\begin{split}\partial_{t}({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}})=2(\nabla{\cdot\,}({\boldsymbol{B}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{u}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{A}})+2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\\
=2(\nabla{\times}({\boldsymbol{u}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{A}})+2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\\
=2({\boldsymbol{u}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}},\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}})+2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\\
=2({\boldsymbol{u}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{B}})+2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)=2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right),\end{split}

(C.2)

where we used (
A.3
) and the fact that
{\boldsymbol{u}}{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}}
is perpendicular to
{\boldsymbol{B}}
. Thus, magnetic helicity is conserved if
\nu=0
.

Remark C.2
.

If
\nu
is constant, the term
2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)
simpifies to

\begin{split}2\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\nu\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)=2\nu\left(\nabla{\cdot\,}\left(\left(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}}-(\nabla{\boldsymbol{B}})^{\top}\right)\right),{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\\
=-2\nu(\nabla{\times}(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}}),{\boldsymbol{A}})=-2\nu(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}},\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{A}})=-2\nu(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{B}})\neq 0,\end{split}

(C.3)

where (
A.3
) and
\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{B}}-\nabla(\nabla{\cdot\,}{\boldsymbol{B}})=-\nabla{\times}(\nabla{\times}{\boldsymbol{B}})
was used.

Appendix E
Brio-Wu problem: GP flux vs resistive MHD flux

We know from the literature that the resistive MHD flux is implemented in various MHD codes. The point of this test is to validate that the proposed viscous flux converges to the same solution, and to compare the two viscous fluxes in presence of shocks. A comparison of the GP flux and the resistive MHD flux using
{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
elements for the Brio-Wu problem is shown in Figure
6
. In the first-order plot, the GP flux solution appears to be more diffusive due to mass diffusion. In the high-order plot, the resistive MHD flux exhibits slightly more oscillative behaviors. The two fluxes produce different solutions but no concrete statement can be drawn from here. Furthermore, both the fluxes yield the same order of convergence in L
1
- and L
2
-norms, as can be seen from Table
5
. Although there exists a contact wave in this test, the inconsistency of the resistive MHD flux is hidden when other waves get involved.
