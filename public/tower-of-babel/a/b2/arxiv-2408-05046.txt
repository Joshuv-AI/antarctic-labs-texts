An activities expansion of the transition polynomial of a multimatroid
Criel Merino, Iain Moffatt, Steven Noble

Abstract
The weighted transition polynomial of a multimatroid is a generalization of the Tutte polynomial. By defining the activity of a skew class with respect to a basis in a multimatroid, we obtain an activities expansion for the weighted transition polynomial. We also decompose the set of all transversals of a multimatroid as a union of subsets of transversals. Each term in the decomposition has the structure of a boolean lattice, and each transversal belongs to a number of terms depending only on the sizes of some of its skew classes. Further expressions for the transition polynomial of a multimatroid are obtained via an equivalence relation on its bases and by extending Kochol’s theory of compatible sets. We apply our multimatroid results to obtain a result of Morse about the transition polynomial of a delta-matroid and get a partition of the boolean lattice of subsets of elements of a delta-matroid determined by the feasible sets. Finally, we describe how multimatroids arise from graphs embedded in surfaces and apply our results to obtain an activities expansion for the topological transition polynomial. Our work extends results for the Tutte polynomial of a matroid.

activities expansion, delta-matroid, multimatroid, ribbon graph, transition polynomial, Tutte polynomial

Introduction

Tutte’s eponymous polynomial is perhaps the most widely studied two variable graph and matroid polynomial due to its many specializations, their
vast breadth and the richness of the underlying theory.
Its specializations include the chromatic, flow and reliability polynomials from graph theory, the partition functions of the Ising and
q
-states Potts models from theoretical physics, and, via Tait graphs, the Jones polynomial of an alternating knot. Discussion of the Tutte polynomial and closely related polynomials fills an entire handbook
[
25
]
.

Tutte first introduced the Tutte polynomial of a graph, as the
dichromate
in
[
38
]
. It is closely related to Whitney’s rank
generating function
[
40
]
which Tutte and Crapo extended from graphs to matroids
[
19
,
37
]
.
See Farr
[
27
]
for more on the early history of the Tutte polynomial.

The simplest definition of the Tutte polynomial
T(M;x,y)
of a matroid
M
is probably in terms of the rank function
r
of
M
. (We assume familiarity with the basic notions of matroids. See
[
35
]

for more information.)

Definition 1
.

For a matroid
M=(E,r)
, we let

T(M;x,y):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(x-1)^{r(E)-r(A)}(y-1)^{|A|-r(A)}.

Tutte’s original definition
[
38
]
used the notion of activities.
Take a matroid
M
with element set
E
and fix a total ordering
\prec
of
E
. Given a basis
B
of
M
, for each element
e
of
E-B
, there is a unique circuit,
C(B,e)
such that
C(B,e)\subseteq B\cup\{e\}
.
The circuit
C(B,e)
is called the
fundamental circuit of
e
with respect to
B
.

An element
e
of
E-B
is
externally active
with respect to
B
if
e
is the least element of
C(B,e)
with respect to
\prec
.
Similarly, for each element
e
of
B
, there is a unique cocircuit,
C^{*}(B,e)
such that
C^{*}(B,e)\subseteq(E-B)\cup\{e\}
.
The cocircuit
C^{*}(B,e)
is called the
fundamental cocircuit of
e
with respect to
B
.

An element
e
of
B
is

internally active
with respect to
B
if
e
is the least element of
C^{*}(B,e)
with respect to
\prec
.
The collections of internally and externally active elements with respect to
B
are denoted by
\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{M,\prec}(B)
and
\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{M,\prec}(B)
, respectively. We usually omit the subscript
M
whenever it is clear from context. Given that,
in general,
|{\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{\prec}(B)}|
and
|{\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{\prec}(B)}|
depend on
\prec
, the following result proved by Tutte
[
38
]
for graphs and extended by Crapo
[
19
]
to matroids is remarkable. Let
\mathcal{B}(M)
denote the collection of bases of a matroid
M
.

Theorem 2
(Tutte
[
38
]
, Crapo
[
19
]
).

Let
M
be a matroid
and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its elements. Then

T(M;x,y)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(M)}x^{|{\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{\prec}(B)}|}y^{|{\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{\prec}(B)}|}.

Setting
x=y=2
, we get the following.

Corollary 3
.

Let
M
be a matroid
and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its elements. Then

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(M)}2^{|{\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{\prec}(B)}|+|\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{\prec}(B)|}=2^{|E|}.

Crapo gave a nice bijective proof of this identity. For any basis
B
of
M
consider the Boolean interval
I(B):=[B-\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{\prec}(B),B\cup\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{\prec}(B)]
, that is,
the family of sets
X
with
B-\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{\prec}(B)\subseteq X\subseteq B\cup\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{\prec}(B)
. Then we have the following theorem of Crapo from
[
19
]
.

Theorem 4
(Crapo
[
19
]
).

Let
M
be a matroid with element set
E

and let
\prec
be a total ordering of
E
. Then the
collection of Boolean intervals
\{I(B):B\in\mathcal{B}\}
forms a partition of
2^{E}
.

Dawson
[
20
]
showed that the existence of such a partition into Boolean intervals does not actually require matroid structure. In fact such a partition exists for any family of sets, although clearly in this more general setting the matroidal description of the Boolean intervals is lost. One of the main aims of this paper is to show that an analogue of Theorem
4
reflecting the underlying structure holds in the setting of multimatroids.

Our interest in multimatroids comes from topological graph theory (i.e., the study of graphs embedded in surfaces) via delta-matroids.
Delta-matroids were introduced independently by several authors
[
4
,
16
,
21
]
but principally studied by
Bouchet who showed that every embedded graph has an associated delta-matroid in the same way as every graph has an associated matroid
[
7
,
17
]
. We give the formal definition of a delta-matroid later in Section
5
, but roughly speaking delta-matroids generalize
matroids by allowing the bases to have differing sizes.
Some important classes of delta-matroids mirror classes of matroids, for example, those coming from embedded graphs and those coming from binary matrices. But, in contrast with graphic matroids, the class of delta-matroids coming from embedded graphs can also be realized as a class defined in terms of Eulerian circuits in
4
-regular graphs
[
7
]
. As the delta-matroid of an embedded graph with one vertex may be obtained from its intersection graph and these graphs are circle graphs, there is also a class of delta-matroids associated with circle graphs and these form a subclass of those coming from embedded graphs. For more information on these classes see
[
32
,
34
]
.

In topological graph theory nonorientable surfaces present additional challenges compared with orientable surfaces.
In
[
18
]
we studied how nonorientability
impacts the delta-matroid of an embedded graph but here we show that to fully understand the basis structure of a graph embedded in a nonorientable surface we need to consider a more general structure, namely, a multimatroid (see Section
2
for a definition). These were introduced by Bouchet in the 1990s

[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, extending and unifying delta-matroids and
isotropic systems
[
5
]
,
another of his earlier combinatorial structures. (Very roughly, isotropic systems generalize circuit spaces of binary matroids.)

Special classes of multimatroids include

1
-matroids which correspond precisely to matroids and
2
-matroids which are equivalent (in a sense we will make precise in Section
5
) to delta-matroids.
Thus, because every matroid is a delta-matroid, matroids are
1
-matroids and can also be seen as
2
-matroids. A subclass of
3
-matroids is equivalent to isotropic systems but of more relevance to us is that
3
-matroids of embedded graphs allow us, in some sense, to capture when edges of graphs embedded in a nonorientable surface go over crosscaps.

In this paper we are interested in basis activities in multimatroids.
Our first two main results extend Theorems
2
and
4
to multimatroids. It is, however, more natural to describe our results in terms of the transition polynomial
[
13
]
rather than the Tutte polynomial.
For classes of objects where these are both defined, they are related by a substitution of variables. Moreover, the transition polynomial of an embedded graph includes Bollobás and Riordan’s 2-variable ribbon graph polynomial
[
2
,
3
]
as a specialization.
Theorem
20
extends Crapo and Tutte’s Theorem
2
by expressing the transition polynomial of a multimatroid as a sum over its bases.
This expansion of the transition polynomial launches us into the study of bases with our second main result, Theorem
26
, extending Crapo’s partition into Boolean intervals in Theorem
4
to multimatroids.

Recent results of Kochol
[
31
]
suggest an alternative approach to obtain an expression for the transition polynomial. In Section
4
, we generalize the main ideas of Kochol by defining a notion of compatibility for multimatroids and obtaining a corresponding expression for the transition polynomial in Theorem
42
, our third main result.

The notion of compatibility for multimatroids allows us to define an equivalence relation
\sim
on the transversals of a multimatroid.
In the second half of Section
4
we relate the equivalence classes of
\sim
to the sets appearing in Theorem
26
which leads to Theorem
50
, our fourth main result, which in turn leads to another expression for the transition polynomial.

In the remainder of the paper we exploit Theorems
20
and
26
. In Section
5
we use the equivalence of
2
-matroids and delta-matroids to derive Theorem
58
from Theorem
20
, recovering the main result of Morse in
[
33
]
. We then apply Theorem
26
to construct a partition of the subsets of the elements of a delta-matroid into Boolean intervals in Theorem
59
, making concrete implicit underlying ideas in
[
33
]
.

Finally, we return to our original motivation by collecting ideas from the literature to show that there is a
3
-matroid associated with every embedded graph, giving Theorem
61
. We use this to prove
an expression for the transition polynomial of an embedded graph
[
22
]
which follows by combining Theorems
20
and
63
with a careful interpretation of how active edges are manifest in an embedded graph.

2
Multimatroids

Multimatroids generalize matroids.
They were introduced by Bouchet in
[
8
]
. They are defined on what he called a
carrier
, a pair

(U,\Omega)
where
U
is a finite set and
\Omega
is a partition of
U
into (non-empty) blocks called
skew classes
. If each skew class
has size
q
, then
(U,\Omega)
is a
q
-carrier
.
A multimatroid is said to be
non-degenerate
if each of its skew classes has cardinality at least two.
The first sentence in each of Examples
13
and
14
offers examples of carriers.

In order to prepare the reader for the definitions which follow, we begin by giving a hint of how multimatroids generalize matroids.
We shall see that
q
-carriers give rise to a class of multimatroids called
q
-matroids. 1-matroids are exactly matroids. But there is also a correspondence between matroids and a class of 2-matroids that turns out to be more useful.
In Example
15
, we describe how to form a
2
-matroid from a matroid. In this example, every element of the matroid gives rise to a skew class in the multimatroid. Thus there is a bijection between the elements of the matroid and the skew classes of the corresponding multimatroid. More generally,
we shall see that given a multimatroid defined on a carrier
(U,\Omega)
, it is helpful to think of the skew classes, rather than the elements of
U
, as playing a role analogous to the elements of a matroid, and the elements of each skew class representing different ‘states’ that the skew class might play.

A pair of distinct elements belonging to the same skew class is called a
skew pair
. A
subtransversal
of
\Omega
is a subset of
U

meeting each skew class at most once. A
transversal

T
of
\Omega
is a subtransversal of
\Omega
with
|T|=|\Omega|
, that is, a set containing precisely one element from each skew class. Given a subtransversal
S
and a skew class
\omega
meeting
S
, we let
S_{\omega}
denote the unique element of
S\cap\omega
.
The sets of
subtransversals and transversals of
\Omega
are denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{T}(\Omega)
respectively.

A
multimatroid
is a triple
Z=(U,\Omega,r_{Z})
so that
(U,\Omega)
is a carrier and
r_{Z}
is a
rank function
,
a non-negative integral function defined on
S(\Omega)

and satisfying the two axioms below.

(R1)

For each transversal
T
of
\Omega
, the pair comprising
T
and the restriction of
r_{Z}
to subsets of
T
forms a matroid.

(R2)

For each subtransversal
S
of
\Omega
and skew pair
\{x,y\}
from a skew class disjoint from
S
, we have

r_{Z}(S\cup\{x\})+r_{Z}(S\cup\{y\})-2r_{Z}(S)\geq 1.

We generally omit the subscript
Z
from
r_{Z}
whenever the context is clear. Axiom
(R1)
implies the following:
r(\emptyset)=0
; for
A\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
and
x\in U
such that
A\cup\{x\}\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
,
r(A)\leq r(A\cup\{x\})\leq r(A)+1
; and for
A
,
B\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
such that
A\cup B\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
,
r(A\cup B)+r(A\cap B)\leq r(A)+r(B)
.

It is convenient to extend the concepts we have defined for
carriers to multimatroids. For example, by a transversal of a multimatroid we mean a transversal of its underlying carrier,
and we sometimes use
\mathcal{T}(Z)
to denote the set of transversals of the carrier of
Z
.
We say that a multimatroid is a
q
-matroid
if its carrier is a
q
-carrier.
The special case of
2
-matroids was first introduced earlier than general multimatroids in
[
4
]
under the name

symmetric matroids
. The
order
of a carrier
(U,\Omega)
, denoted by
|\Omega|
,
is its number of skew classes. If
S
is a subtransversal of
Z
, then we define its
nullity

n_{Z}(S)
by
n_{Z}(S)=|S|-r_{Z}(S)
, again omitting
the subscript when the context is clear.

An
independent set
of
Z
is a subtransversal
S
with
n(S)=0
. Subtransversals that are not independent are
dependent
. A

basis
is a maximal independent subtransversal.
A
circuit
is a minimal dependent subtransversal. Given a carrier
(U,\Omega)
, a multimatroid on
(U,\Omega)
is determined by each of its collections of independent sets, circuits and bases
[
8
]
. The set of bases and circuits of
Z
are denoted by
\mathcal{B}(Z)
and
\mathcal{C}(Z)
, respectively.
Observe that for any subtransversal
S
, we have
r(S)=\max_{B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)}|B\cap S|
.
The following are straightforward consequences of
(R1)
and
(R2)
.

Proposition 5
(
[
8
, Proposition 5.5]
).

The bases of a non-degenerate multimatroid
Z
are transversals of
Z
.

Proposition 6
(
[
8
, Proposition 5.7]
).

Let
B
be a basis and
\omega
be a skew class of a multimatroid. Then
B\cup\omega
contains at most one circuit.

A circuit of the form described in the previous proposition is a
fundamental circuit

[
10
]
with respect to
B
and is denoted by
C(B,\omega)
.

Let
Z=(U,\Omega,r)
and let
u
be in
U
. Then the
elementary minor

[
9
]
of
Z
by
u
, denoted by
Z|u
, is the triple

(U^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime},r^{\prime})
with
\Omega^{\prime}=\{\omega\in\Omega:u\notin\omega\}
,
U^{\prime}=\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}}\omega

and
r^{\prime}(S)=r(S\cup\{u\})-r(\{u\})
,
S\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega^{\prime})
.
It is straightforward to show that
Z|u
is a multimatroid.

Let
Z=(U,\Omega,r)
be a multimatroid and
\omega
be a skew class of
\Omega
.
We let
Z\ba\omega
denote the
restriction
of
Z
to
U-\omega

[
8
]
, comprising the triple

(U-\omega,\Omega-\{\omega\},r_{Z\ba\omega})
,
where

r_{Z\ba\omega}
is the restriction of
r
to subtransversals of
\Omega-\{\omega\}
.
It is straightforward to show that
Z\ba\omega
is a multimatroid.
It follows immediately that if
S
is a subtransversal of
\Omega-\{\omega\}
, then

n_{Z\ba\omega}(S)=n_{Z}(S)
.

An element
e
of a multimatroid
(U,\Omega,r)
is
singular
if
r(\{e\})=0
. A skew class is
singular
if it contains a singular element.
We establish a few simple properties of singular elements and skew classes.

Lemma 7
.

Let
e
be a singular element of a multimatroid
(U,\Omega,r)
.
Suppose that the skew class containing
e
is
\omega
and that
S
is a subtranversal of
\Omega
such that
S\cap\omega=\emptyset
. Then
r(S\cup\{e\})=r(S)
and
r(S\cup\{u\})=r(S)+1
, for all
u\in\omega-\{e\}
.

Proof.

By
(R1)
we have
r(S)\leq r(S\cup\{e\})\leq r(S)+r(\{e\})=r(S)
, giving
r(S\cup\{e\})=r(S)
. Now, by
(R2)
,
r(S\cup\{u\})=r(S)+1
.

The following results of Bouchet follow immediately.

Proposition 8
(
[
9
, Proposition 5.4]
).

A singular skew class contains precisely one singular element.

Proposition 9
(
[
9
, Proposition 5.5]
).

If
\omega
is a singular skew class of a multimatroid
Z
, then for all
x
in
\omega
, we have
Z|x=Z\ba\omega
.

Bouchet established several cryptomorphisms for multimatroids and in particular proved the following.

Proposition 10
(
[
8
, Proposition 5.4]
).

Let
Z
be a multimatroid and
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be circuits of
Z
. Then
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
cannot contain exactly one skew pair.

From this we get an easy proof of the next lemma describing the circuits of a multimatroid with a singular skew class.

Lemma 11
.

Let
Z=(U,\Omega,r)
be a multimatroid with a singular element
e
. Suppose that
\omega
is the skew class containing
e
. Then
\mathcal{C}(Z)=\mathcal{C}(Z\ba\omega)\cup\{\{e\}\}
.

Proof.

First, observe that as circuits are minimal dependent sets, the only circuit of
Z
containing
e
is
\{e\}
. Together with Proposition
10
this implies that the only circuit of
Z
meeting
\omega
is
\{e\}
.
Second, recall that for every transversal
T
of
\Omega-\{\omega\}
, we have
r_{Z}(T)=r_{Z\ba\omega}(T)
. Thus
C
is a circuit of
Z\ba\omega
if and only if it is a circuit of
Z
with
C\cap\omega=\emptyset
. The result follows by combining these two observations.

We record the following lemma for reference later.

Lemma 12
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid with a non-singular skew class
\omega
. Let
S
be a subtransversal of
Z
with
S\cap\omega=\emptyset
and let
u
be in
\omega
. Then
n_{Z}(S\cup\{u\})=n_{Z|u}(S)
.

Proof.

As
\omega
is non-singular and
u\in\omega
,
r_{Z}(\{u\})=1
. Hence

\displaystyle n_{Z|u}(S)

\displaystyle=|S|-r_{Z|u}(S)=|S|-(r_{Z}(S\cup\{u\})-r_{Z}(\{u\}))

\displaystyle=|S\cup\{u\}|-r_{Z}(S\cup\{u\})=n_{Z}(S\cup\{u\}),

as required.

We have noted that every matroid is a

Example 15
.

Let
M
be a matroid with element set
E
and rank function
r_{M}
. We define a
2
-matroid

as follows. We have
U:=\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e},\overline{e}:e\in E\}
and
\Omega:=\{\omega_{e}:e\in E\}
, where for each

we have
\omega_{e}:=\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e},\overline{e}\}
.
For a subset

of

, we define
\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}:=\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}:e\in A\}

\overline{A}:=\{\overline{e}:e\in E\}
.
Then for disjoint subsets
A
and
B
of
E
, we define

, where
M^{*}
is the dual matroid of
M
. It is not difficult to show that that
r_{Z(M)}
satisfies
(R1)
. To see that it satisfies
(R2)
, let
S
be a subtransversal of
\Omega
missing the skew class
\omega_{e}
. Then
S=\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}\cup\overline{B}
for disjoint subsets of
A
and
B
of
E
. Proposition 2.1.11 of
[
35
]
states that a circuit

and a cocircuit

of a matroid satisfy
|C\cap C^{*}|\neq 1
. Thus, in
M
, either
A\cup\{e\}
does not have a circuit containing
e
or
B\cup\{e\}
does not have a cocircuit containing
e
. Hence either
r_{M}(A\cup\{e\})=r_{M}(A)+1
or
r_{M*}(B\cup\{e\})=r_{M^{*}}(B)+1
, giving either

r_{Z(M)}(\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}\cup\overline{B}\cup\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}\})=r_{Z(M)}(\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}\cup\overline{B})+1

r_{Z(M)}(\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}\cup\overline{B}\cup\{\overline{e}\})=r_{Z(M)}(\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}\cup\overline{B})+1
.

We let
\phi:2^{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}(\Omega)
be given by
\phi(A)=\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{A}\cup\overline{E-A}
. Then clearly

is a bijection. We shall see several examples of classical matroid constructions or results which may be translated from a matroid

to the

-matroid

via

. For example,

\mathcal{B}(Z(M))=\{\phi(B):B\in\mathcal{B}(M)\},

which implies that
\phi|_{\mathcal{B}(M)}
is a bijection from
\mathcal{B}(M)
to
\mathcal{B}(Z(M))
.

Next we claim that
C
is a circuit of

if and only if
C=\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{C^{\prime}}
for a circuit
C^{\prime}
of
M
or
C=\overline{C^{\prime}}
for a cocircuit
C^{\prime}
of
M
. It is not difficult to see that this claim is true when

or

, so it remains to show that
Z(M)
has no circuit meeting both
\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{E}
and
\overline{E}
. Suppose, for contradiction, that

is a circuit of

meeting both
\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{E}
and
\overline{E}
. Thus there are non-empty, disjoint subsets

and

of

with

Then, as circuits are minimal dependent sets, both

3.1
An activities expansion

The

of a multimatroid

, introduced in

Notice that if

, then

, so

.
The
(unweighted) transition polynomial
of
Z
, which we denote by

by setting
\mathbf{x}_{u}=1
for all

.
An example of the unweighted transition polynomial can be found at the end of this section.

It follows from Proposition
5
that whenever

is non-degenerate,

is equal to the number of bases of

.

Proposition 16

Let

Lemma 18
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid.
Suppose that
u
is a non-singular element of
Z
and
\omega
is a skew class of

.
Then
B
is a basis of

if and only if

is a basis of
Z
. Moreover if
B
is a basis of
Z|u

then

exists if and only if

C_{Z|u}(B,\omega)
exists, and when these fundamental circuits exist we have
C_{Z|u}(B,\omega)=C_{Z}(B\cup\{u\},\omega)-\{u\}
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows immediately from the definition of the rank function of
Z|u
.
Suppose then that
B
is a basis of
Z|u
.
Then for every
f
in
\omega-B
, by the definition of the rank function of
Z|u
,
(B-\omega)\cup\{f\}
is independent in
Z|u
if and only if
(B-\omega)\cup\{f,u\}
is independent in
Z
. Thus
C_{Z}(B\cup\{u\},\omega)
exists if and only if

C_{Z|u}(B,\omega)
exists. Suppose then that

C_{Z}(B\cup\{u\},\omega)
and
C_{Z|u}(B,\omega)
exist.
Let
C:=C_{Z}(B\cup\{u\},\omega)
.
We next show that
C-\{u\}
is a circuit of
Z|u
.
Suppose first that

. As
C
is the unique circuit in

, for any subset

of

, we have

. Thus

, so

is a circuit of

.
Now suppose that

. Then
r_{Z|u}(C-\{u\})=r_{Z}(C)-1=|C-\{u\}|-1
,
and if
C^{\prime}
is a proper subset of

. Thus

is a circuit in

. But

, and

contains a unique circuit of

, namely

. Hence
C_{Z|u}(B,\omega)=C-\{u\}
, as required.

Let

\inact_{Z,\prec}(B\cup\{u\})=\inact_{Z|u,\prec}(B)\cup\{\gamma\}

Theorem 20
.

Let
Z
be a non-degenerate multimatroid and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes. Then

Q(Z;\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{\prec}(B)}\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(B)}\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{\underline{B}_{\omega}}}{|\omega|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Big)\Bigg).

Proof.

Suppose that
Z=(U,\Omega,r)
. We proceed by induction on
|\Omega|
. If
|\Omega|=0
, then
\mathcal{B}(Z)=\{\emptyset\}
and
\inact_{\prec}(\emptyset)=\act_{\prec}(\emptyset)=\emptyset

So we may suppose that

\gamma

\prec

\gamma
is singular and the element

in

By Lemmas

17

\displaystyle{\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{Z,\prec}(B)}\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{Z,\prec}(B)}\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{\underline{B}_{\omega}}}{|\omega|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Big)\Bigg)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(Z\ba\gamma)}\sum_{u\in\gamma-\{e\}}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{Z\setminus\gamma,\prec}(B^{\prime})}\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{Z\setminus\gamma,\prec}(B^{\prime})}\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{\underline{B}^{\prime}_{\omega}}}{|\omega|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{B^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(Z\ba\omega)}\sum_{u\in\gamma-\{e\}}}\cdot\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{e}}{|\gamma|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{u}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(t\mathbf{x}_{e}+\sum_{u\in\gamma-\{e\}}\mathbf{x}_{u}\Big)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\cdot\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(Z\setminus\gamma)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{Z\setminus\gamma,\prec}(B^{\prime})}\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{Z\setminus\gamma,\prec}(B^{\prime})}\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{\underline{B}^{\prime}_{\omega}}}{|\omega|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{B^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Big)\Bigg).

By using first the induction hypothesis and then Proposition
16
, we see that this is equal to

\Big(t\mathbf{x}_{e}+\sum_{u\in\gamma-\{e\}}\mathbf{x}_{u}\Big)Q(Z\ba\gamma;\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t)=Q(Z;\mathbf{x},t),

where
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is the restriction of
\mathbf{x}
to
Z\ba\gamma
.

Now suppose that
\gamma

\displaystyle{\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{Z,\prec}(B)}\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{Z,\prec}(B)}\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{\underline{B}_{\omega}}}{|\omega|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{B_{\omega}}\Big)\Bigg)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in\gamma}\sum_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(Z|u)}\mathbf{x}_{u}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{Z|u,\prec}(B^{\prime})}\mathbf{x}_{B^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{Z|u,\prec}(B^{\prime})}\Big(\frac{t\mathbf{x}_{\underline{B}^{\prime}_{\omega}}}{|\omega|-1}+\mathbf{x}_{B^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Big)\Bigg).

to

\displaystyle\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(M)}{\sqrt{y}}^{\,r_{M}(E)}{\sqrt{x}}^{\,|E|-r_{M}(E)}(x+1)^{|{\mathop{\mathrm{int}}_{\prec}(B)}|}(y+1)^{|{\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}_{\prec}(B)}|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subseteq E}{\sqrt{y}}^{\,r_{M}(E)}{\sqrt{x}}^{\,|E|-r_{M}(E)}x^{r_{M}(E)-r_{M}(A)}y^{|A|-r_{M}(A)}.

But this is a polynomial identity, so it holds for all

and

From this we deduce that

So we see that Theorem

is a special case of Theorem
20
.
The argument above also shows that

T(M;x+1,y+1)=\sqrt{x}^{\,r_{M}(E)-|E|}\sqrt{y}^{\,-r_{M}(E)}Q(Z(M);\mathbf{x},t),

(5)

where

,

and
x_{\overline{e}}=\sqrt{x}
.
When

is expanded in powers of

,

, and

and

in each monomial is equal to

and the degree of

in each monomial containing

is equal to

.
Thus the weighted transition polynomial generalizes the Tutte polynomial, in the sense that the Tutte polynomial may be obtained from

by an appropriate symbolic substitution.

Our second main result gives a combinatorial interpretation of (

Now let

The following lemma is surely known, but we cannot find an explicit reference. We use

Lemma 25

Let

S-\mce(S,\prec)

4
Compatible transversals

In this section we extend from matroids to multimatroids a more recent expression for the Tutte polynomial introduced by Kochol
[
30
]
. We begin by extending Kochol’s ideas to define a notion of compatibility for the transversals of a multimatroid. This leads to our third main result, Theorem
42
which gives an expression for the transition polynomial as a sum over compatible transversals.
In the second half of the section we explore the links between compatibility and the collection
\mathcal{H}_{Z,\prec}
.
This suggests an equivalence relation on the bases of a non-degenerate multimatroid leading to our fourth main result, Theorem
50
, which in turn leads to another expression for the transition polynomial.

4.1
A compatible transversals expansion of the transition polynomial

Given a matroid
M
with a total ordering
\prec
of its elements, Kochol
[
30
]
defines a subset
A
of the elements of

to be
(M,\prec)
-
compatible
if and only if
M
has no circuit
C
such that
A\cap C=\{\min(C)\}
. The set
\mathcal{D}(M,\prec)
is then defined as follows.

\mathcal{D}(M,\prec)\\
:=\{A\subseteq E(M):\text{$A$ is $(M^{\ast},\prec)$-compatible and $E(M)-A$ is $(M,\prec)$-compatible}\}.

This leads to the following expression for the Tutte polynomial of

Theorem 30
(Kochol
[
30
]
).

Let

be a matroid and let

be a total ordering on

T(M;x,y)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{D}(M,\prec)}x^{r(E)-r(A)}y^{|A|-r(A)}.

Substituting

is equal to the number of bases of

Kochol’s original proof of Theorem
30
used the deletion-contraction formula for the Tutte polynomial, but later proofs by Kochol
[
31
]
and Pierson
[
36
]
have used the activities expansion from Theorem

and established a bijection between the collection of bases and
\mathcal{D}(M,\prec)
. We shall introduce the notion of a compatible transversal of a multimatroid

and a mapping from

to the set of compatible transversals of

we shall see in Example

that this mapping restricts to a bijection from

to the set of compatible transversals equivalent to that of Kochol
[
31
]
and Pierson
[
36
]
via the

described in Example
15

The form of

suggests that it is particularly amenable to an extension to multimatroids, as it mentions both the circuits and cocircuits of

, that is, the entire collection of circuits of the
2
-matroid

Definition 31

Given a multimatroid

and a total ordering

of its skew classes, we say that a transversal

\omega\prec\omega^{\prime}
. By the maximality of
\omega
, there is a circuit
C^{\prime\prime}
with
\min(C^{\prime\prime})\in\omega^{\prime}
,
C^{\prime\prime}-\omega^{\prime}\subseteq T
such that
C^{\prime\prime}
meets no active skew class other than
\omega^{\prime}
. By Proposition
10
,
C^{\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}}=C^{\prime\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}}
, so
C^{\prime}\cup C^{\prime\prime}
is a subtransversal of
Z
.
Hence the strong circuit elimination axiom of matroids
[
35
, Proposition 1.4.12]
, implies that there is a circuit
C^{\prime\prime\prime}
of
Z
with
C^{\prime}_{\omega}\in C^{\prime\prime\prime}\subseteq(C^{\prime}\cup C^{\prime\prime})-\{C^{\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}}\}
. Then
\min(C^{\prime\prime\prime})=\min(C^{\prime})\in\omega
,
C^{\prime\prime\prime}-\omega\subseteq T
and
C^{\prime\prime\prime}
meets fewer active skew classes than
C^{\prime}
contradicting the definition of
C^{\prime}
. So the result follows.

As a consequence of Lemma
34
, if
\omega
is active with respect to the transversal
T
, then we may define

\underline{T}_{\omega}
to denote the common element
\min(C)
of every circuit
C
with
\min(C)\in\omega
and
C-\omega\subseteq T
.
(This extends our earlier definition of
\underline{B}_{\omega}
when
B
is a basis.) When
T
is not a basis it is possible that

\underline{T}_{\omega}\in T
.

The next lemma shows that if we change a transversal by replacing one of its elements from an active skew class by another from that skew class, then we do not change the collection of active skew classes. We recall that
A\bigtriangleup B
denotes the
symmetric difference

(A\cup B)-(A\cap B)
of the sets
A
and
B
.

Lemma 36
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes, and let
T
be a transversal of
Z
.
Suppose that
\omega
is active with respect to
T
and that
\{T_{\omega},y\}
is a skew pair. Let
T^{\prime}:=T\bigtriangleup\{T_{\omega},y\}
. Then
\act_{Z,\prec}(T)=\act_{Z,\prec}(T^{\prime})
. Moreover, if
\omega^{\prime}\in\act_{Z,\prec}(T)
, then

\underline{T}_{\omega^{\prime}}=\underline{T}^{\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\omega^{\prime}
is active with respect to
T
. Then by Lemma
35
, there is a circuit
C
such that
\min(C)\in\omega^{\prime}
and
C-\omega^{\prime}\subseteq T
, meeting no skew class other than
\omega^{\prime}
that is active with respect to
T
. Then
C-\omega^{\prime}\subseteq T^{\prime}
, so
\omega^{\prime}\in\act_{Z,\prec}(T^{\prime})
and
\underline{T}_{\omega^{\prime}}=\underline{T}^{\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}}
.

Now suppose that
\omega^{\prime}
is active with respect to
T^{\prime}
. We may assume that
\omega^{\prime}\neq\omega
.
Then by Lemma
35
, there is a circuit
C
such that
\min(C)\in\omega^{\prime}
and
C-\omega^{\prime}\subseteq T^{\prime}
, meeting no skew class other than
\omega^{\prime}
that is active with respect to
T^{\prime}
. So, from the first part,
C
meets no skew class other than
\omega^{\prime}
that is active with respect to
T
.
Thus, in particular,
C\cap\omega=\emptyset
and
C-\omega^{\prime}\subseteq T
. Hence
\omega^{\prime}\in\act_{Z,\prec}(T)
.

We now see how to associate any transversal of a multimatroid
Z
with
a
(Z,\prec)
-compatible transversal. Given a transversal
T
of

we define its
\prec
-
canonical compatible transversal
, denoted by
\ccl_{\prec}(T)
to be the transversal determined by setting
(\ccl_{\prec}(T))_{\omega}
as follows.

(\ccl_{\prec}(T))_{\omega}=\begin{cases}\underline{T}_{\omega}&\text{if $\omega$ is active,}\\
T_{\omega}&\text{if $\omega$ is inactive.}\end{cases}

We will prove that
\ccl_{\prec}(T)
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible in Proposition
38
.

The next result is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
36
.

Lemma 37
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes, and let
T
be a transversal of
Z
.
Suppose that
\omega
is active with respect to
T
and that
\{T_{\omega},y\}
is a skew pair. Let
T^{\prime}:=T\bigtriangleup\{T_{\omega},y\}
. Then
\ccl_{\prec}(T)=\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})
.

We now give the key properties of
(Z,\prec)
-canonical compatible transversals.

Proposition 38
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes and let
T
be a transversal of
Z
. Then each of the following holds.

1.

\ccl_{\prec}(T)
and
T
have the same active skew classes.

2.

\ccl_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
.

3.

\ccl_{\prec}(T)
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible.

Proof.

The first and second parts follow using induction on the number of active skew classes with respect to
T
and Lemmas
36
and
37
, respectively.

To prove the third part, suppose that
C
is a circuit of
Z
with
C-\{\min(C)\}\subseteq\ccl_{\prec}(T)
. Let
\omega
be the skew class containing
\min(C)
. Then
\omega
is active with respect to
\ccl_{\prec}(T)
. By the second part,
\min(C)=(\ccl_{\prec}(T))_{\omega}
. Hence
C\subseteq\ccl_{\prec}(T)
, as required.

The next result is an easy consequence of the previous one.

Proposition 39
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes and let
T
be a transversal of
Z
.
Then
T
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible if and only if
\ccl_{\prec}(T)=T
.

Proof.

Suppose that
T
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible. Then there is no circuit
C
such that
C-T=\{\min(C)\}
. So for every skew class
\omega
which is active with respect to
T
, we have
T_{\omega}=\underline{T}_{\omega}
. Hence
\ccl_{\prec}(T)=T
.

Conversely, suppose that
T=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
. By Proposition
38
Part
3
,
\ccl_{\prec}(T)
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible, so
T
is too.

Proposition 40
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes
and let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be transversals of
Z
. Then both the following hold.

1.

\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
if and only if
T^{\prime}_{\omega}=T_{\omega}
for every skew class
\omega
that is inactive with respect to
T
.

2.

If
T^{\prime}_{\omega}=T_{\omega}
for every skew class
\omega
that is inactive with respect to
T
, then
\act_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=\act_{\prec}(T)
.

Moreover when
T
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible we have
\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T
if and only if

T^{\prime}_{\omega}=T_{\omega}
for every skew class
\omega
that is inactive with respect to
T
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
T^{\prime}_{\omega}=T_{\omega}
for every skew class
\omega
that is inactive with respect to
T
. We prove by induction on
|T^{\prime}\bigtriangleup T|
that
\ccl_{\prec}(T)=\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})

and
\act_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=\act_{\prec}(T)
.
If
|T^{\prime}\bigtriangleup T|=0
, then
T^{\prime}=T
and there is nothing to prove. Suppose then that
|T^{\prime}\bigtriangleup T|>0
. Let
\omega
be a skew class such that
T^{\prime}_{\omega}\neq T_{\omega}
. Then
\omega
is active with respect to
T
. Let
T^{\prime\prime}:=T\bigtriangleup\{T_{\omega},T^{\prime}_{\omega}\}
. By Lemma
36

\act_{\prec}(T^{\prime\prime})=\act_{\prec}{T}
and by Lemma
37

\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime\prime})=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
. Thus we may apply the inductive hypothesis to
T^{\prime\prime}
and
T^{\prime}
to deduce that
\act_{\prec}(T)=\act_{\prec}(T^{\prime\prime})=\act_{\prec}(T^{\prime})
and
\ccl_{\prec}(T)=\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime\prime})=\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})
, establishing one direction of the first part and the second part.

For the converse of the first part, suppose that
\ccl_{\prec}(T)=\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})
. Let
T^{\prime\prime}:=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
.
Then by applying Proposition
38
Part
1
twice,
\act_{Z,\prec}(T)=\act_{Z,\prec}(T^{\prime\prime})=\act_{Z,\prec}(T^{\prime})
, so it follows from the definition of the canonical compatible closure that
T_{\omega}=T^{\prime}_{\omega}
for every skew class
\omega
that is inactive with respect to
T
.

The last part follows immediately from the first part and Proposition
39
.

We next establish a simple expression for the nullity of a transversal
T
depending on
\ccl_{\prec}(T)
.

Proposition 41
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid, let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes and let
T
be a transversal of
Z
. Then

n(T)=|{\act_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))}|-|T\bigtriangleup\ccl_{\prec}(T)|.

Proof.

Note that
0\leq|{T\bigtriangleup\ccl_{\prec}(T)}|\leq|{\act_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))}|
.
Suppose that
T=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
. Then by Proposition
39
,
T
is
(Z,\prec)
-compatible.
We claim that

\act_{\prec}(T)=\mcs(T,\prec).

To show this, suppose first that
\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)
. Then there is a circuit
C
of
Z
with
\min(C)\in\omega
and
C-\omega\subseteq T
. But as
T
is
\prec
-compatible,
C\subseteq T
and
\omega\in\mcs(T,\prec)
. Conversely, if
\omega\in\mcs(T,\prec)
, then there is a circuit
C
with
C\subseteq T
and
\min(C)\in\omega
, so
\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)
.
Thus, by Lemma
25
,

n(T)=|{\act_{\prec}(T)}|=|{\act_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))}|-|{T\bigtriangleup\ccl_{\prec}(T)}|.

Next suppose that
|{T\bigtriangleup\ccl_{\prec}(T)}|=|{\act_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))}|
. Then for every circuit
C
with
C-\min(C)\subseteq T
, we have
\min(C)\notin T
. Thus
T
is a basis of
Z
. Hence

n(T)=0=|{\act_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))}|-|{T\bigtriangleup\ccl_{\prec}(T)}|.

So the expression for
n(T)
holds in the cases when
T=\ccl_{\prec}(T)
or when

|{T\bigtriangleup\ccl_{\prec}(T)}|

=

|{\act_{\prec}(\ccl_{\prec}(T))}|
.

Axiom
(R1)
implies that if
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are transversals
differing on at most one skew class, then
|n(T_{1})-n(T_{2})|\leq 1
. The result now follows.

It is now easy to obtain an expression for the transition polynomial as a sum over
\prec
-compatible transversals. Given a multimatroid
Z
with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes, we let
\Comp(Z,\prec)
denote the collection of
\prec
-compatible transversals of
Z
.

Theorem 42
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes. Then

Q(Z;\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{T\in\Comp(Z,\prec)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{\prec}(T)}\mathbf{x}_{T_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)}\Bigg(\Big(\sum_{e\in\omega-\{T_{\omega}\}}\mathbf{x}_{e}\Big)+t{\mathbf{x}}_{T_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg).

Proof.

By partitioning the set of transversals of
Z
according to their
(Z,\prec)
-canonical compatible transversal, we obtain

\displaystyle Q(Z;\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(Z)}t^{n(T^{\prime})}\mathbf{x}_{T^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\Comp(Z,\prec)}\sum_{T^{\prime}:\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{\prec}(T)}\mathbf{x}_{T^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)}\mathbf{x}_{T^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Bigg)t^{n(T^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\Comp(Z,\prec)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\inact_{\prec}(T)}\mathbf{x}_{T_{\omega}}\Bigg)\sum_{T^{\prime}:\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)}\mathbf{x}_{T^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Bigg)t^{n(T^{\prime})},

where the final equality follows from Proposition
40
. Moreover it also follows from Proposition
40
that the transversals contributing to the inner sum for a particular
\prec
-compatible transversal
T
may include any member of each active skew class of
T
. Proposition
41
tells us that the nullity of such a transversal
T^{\prime}
is equal to the number of skew classes which are active with respect to
T
and on which
T
and
T^{\prime}
agree. Thus

\sum_{T^{\prime}:\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)}\mathbf{x}_{T^{\prime}_{\omega}}\Bigg)t^{n(T^{\prime})}=\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)}\Bigg(\Big(\sum_{e\in\omega-\{T_{\omega}\}}\mathbf{x}_{e}\Big)+t{\mathbf{x}}_{T_{\omega}}\Bigg)\Bigg)

and the result follows.

Then we obtain the following corollaries.
The first is immediate. The second comes from the previous theorem and by noting that Proposition
41
implies that if
T
is
\prec
-compatible then
n(T)=|{\act_{\prec}(T)}|
.

Corollary 43
.

Let
Z
be a multimatroid and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes. Then the unweighted transition polynomial is given by

Q(Z;t)=\sum_{T\in\Comp(Z,\prec)}\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(T)}(|\omega|-1+t)\Bigg).

Corollary 44

Let

be a
q
-matroid and let
\prec

Q(Z;t)=\sum_{T\in\Comp(Z,\prec)}(q-1+t)^{n(T)}.

4.2
An equivalence relation on transversals

The proof of Theorem
42
worked by partitioning the transversals of
Z
according to their
\prec
-canonical compatible transversal. In the remainder of this section we consider the equivalence relation on the transversals for which this partition is the set of equivalence classes, in particular, its restriction to the set of bases.

Given a multimatroid
Z
and a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes, we define an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{T}(Z)

so that
T_{1}\sim T_{2}
if and only if
\ccl_{\prec}(T_{1})=\ccl_{\prec}(T_{2})
. It is clear that
\sim
is genuinely an equivalence relation. Let
[T]
denote the equivalence class of the transversal
T
according to
\sim
. By Proposition
40
Part
1

[T]=\{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(Z):T^{\prime}_{\omega}=T_{\omega},\ \omega\in\inact_{\prec}(\omega)\}.

Let
\mathcal{T}(Z)/{\sim}
denote the set of equivalence classes of
\mathcal{T}(Z)
according to
\sim
. It follows from Proposition
38
Part
3
that
the image of
\ccl_{\prec}
is
\Comp(Z,\prec)
, so

|\mathcal{T}(Z)/{\sim}|=|{\Comp(Z,\prec)}|
.

In the case where
Z
is non-degenerate, we now relate
\mathcal{T}(Z)/{\sim}
to the collection
\mathcal{H}_{Z,\prec}
from the previous section.

Proposition 47
.

Let
Z
be a non-degenerate multimatroid with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes. Then for every
(Z,\prec)
-compatible transversal
T
, we have

\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}(Z):\\
\ccl_{\prec}(B)=T\end{subarray}}H_{Z,\prec}(B)=\{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(Z):\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T\}.

Proof.

Members of
H_{Z,\prec}(B)
differ only on active skew classes of
B
. Thus if
T^{\prime\prime}\in H_{Z,\prec}(B)
, then by Proposition
40
Part
3
we have
\ccl(T^{\prime\prime})=\ccl(B)
. This gives

\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}(Z):\\
\ccl_{\prec}(B)=T\end{subarray}}H_{Z,\prec}(B)\subseteq\{T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}(Z):\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T\}.

To prove the reverse inclusion, observe that by Theorem
26
, for every transversal
T^{\prime}
with
\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T
, there is at least one basis
B
for which
T^{\prime}\in H_{Z,\prec}(B)
.
As
B\in H_{Z,\prec}(B)
,
by the first part of the proof
\ccl_{\prec}(B)=\ccl_{\prec}(T^{\prime})=T
and the result follows.

Thus
\mathcal{T}(Z)/{\sim}
is closely related to
\mathcal{H}_{Z,\prec}
.
The advantage of locating transversals around
(Z,\prec)
-compatible transversals rather than bases, as we did in Theorem
26
, is that we always get a partition of
\mathcal{T}(Z)
.

Let
Z
be a non-degenerate multimatroid and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes. We now consider the restriction
\sim_{\mathcal{B}}
of
\sim
to
\mathcal{B}(Z)
.
For a basis
B
, we define
[B]_{\mathcal{B}}:=\{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(Z):B^{\prime}\sim_{\mathcal{B}}B\}
and
\mathcal{B}(Z)/{\sim_{\mathcal{B}}}:=\{[B]_{\mathcal{B}}:B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)\}
. For each
(Z,\prec)
-compatible transversal
T
,
we may choose
B
such that
T\in H_{Z,\prec}(B)
. So Proposition
47
implies that there is a basis
B
such that
\ccl_{\prec}(B)=T
. Consequently
|\mathcal{B}(Z)/{\sim_{\mathcal{B}}}|=|\mathcal{T}(Z)/{\sim}|=|{\Comp(Z,\prec)}|
.

The preceding discussion together with Proposition
40
allows us to rewrite Corollary
43
as a sum over the equivalence classes of
{\sim_{\mathcal{B}}}
.

Corollary 48
.

Let
Z
be a non-degenerate multimatroid and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes. Then the unweighted transition polynomial is given by

Q(Z;t)=\sum_{[B]_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}(Z)/\sim_{\mathcal{B}}}\>\Bigg(\prod_{\omega\in\act_{\prec}(B)}(t+|\omega|-1)\Bigg).

Here the sum is over equivalence classes, and the product is over the active skew classes of any equivalence class representative.

The next corollary is immediate.

Corollary 49
.

Let
Z
be a
q
-matroid with
q\geq 2
and let
\prec
be a total ordering of its skew classes. Then the unweighted transition polynomial is given by

Q(Z;t)=\sum_{[B]_{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{B}(Z)/\sim_{\mathcal{B}}}(t+q-1)^{|{\act_{\prec}(B)}|}.

(8)

Here the sum is over equivalence classes, and the exponent is the number of active skew classes of any equivalence class representative.

We now state our fourth main result which gives a succinct description of the members of each equivalence class of
\sim_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Theorem 50
.

Let
Z
be a non-degenerate multimatroid
with a total ordering
\prec
of its skew classes. Let
B
be a basis of
Z
. Then a transversal

Proof.

5
Delta-matroids

In this section we explore the implications of Theorem
20
and Corollary
27
for delta-matroids and connect our results with those of Morse
[
33
]
.
Example
60
(below) provides a running example for the constructions in this section.
Delta-matroids and equivalent structures were introduced in the mid 1980s by several authors
[
4
,
16
,
21
]
, but principally studied by Bouchet.
A delta-matroid is a pair
(E,\mathcal{F})
where
E
is a finite set and
\mathcal{F}
is a non-empty collection of subsets of
E
satisfying the
symmetric exchange axiom
:

for all triples
(X,Y,u)
with
X
and
Y
in
\mathcal{F}

and
u\in X\triangle Y
, there is a
v\in X\triangle Y
(perhaps

u
itself) such that
X\triangle\{u,v\}
is in
\mathcal{F}
.

The elements of
\mathcal{F}
are called
feasible sets
.
A matroid (defined in terms of its bases) is precisely a delta-matroid in which all the feasible sets have the same size.

Bouchet established the relationship between
2
-matroids and delta-matroids in
[
4
]
. Describing and using this relationship places a strain on the notation, so to aid the exposition, we say that a
2
-matroid with carrier
(U,\Omega)
is
indexed
by a finite set
E
if
\Omega=\{\omega_{e}:e\in E\}
.
Now suppose that we have a
2
-matroid with carrier
(U,\Omega)
indexed by
E
. Fix a transversal
T
of
\Omega
. For a subtransversal
S
of
\Omega
, we let

T(S)=\{e\in E:T\cap\omega_{e}=S\cap\omega_{e}\}.

Theorem 54
(Bouchet
[
4
]
).

Let
Z=(U,\Omega,r)
be a
2
-matroid indexed by
E
and let
T
be a transversal of
\Omega
. Let
\mathcal{F}:=\{T(B):B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)\}
.
Then
(E,\mathcal{F})
is a delta-matroid.
Moreover, up to isomorphism, every delta-matroid arises in this way.

We denote the delta-matroid described in the theorem by
D(Z,T)
.
The process is reversible and every
2
-matroid may be constructed from a delta-matroid by reversing the process.
Given a delta-matroid
D=(E,\mathcal{F})
, let
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
denote two disjoint copies of
E
. Now let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be bijections from
E
to
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, respectively. Then we obtain a
2
-matroid from
D
indexed by
E
with carrier
(E_{1}\cup E_{2},\{\omega_{e}:e\in E\})
, so that
for all
e
we have
\omega_{e}=\{\mu_{1}(e),\mu_{2}(e)\}
,
and collection of bases
\{\mu_{1}(F)\cup\mu_{2}(E-F):F\in\mathcal{F}\}
.
Given a delta-matroid
D=(E,\mathcal{F})
we use
Z(D,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
to denote this
2
-matroid. We have
D=D(Z(D,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}),\mu_{1}(E))
.
As every matroid is a delta-matroid,
the construction we have just described extends the one given in Example
15
where we described how to associate a
2
-matroid with a matroid.

As a consequence, definitions and results for
2
-matroids may be translated to delta-matroids and vice versa. We now effect this translation for some of the concepts we have introduced.
In order to simplify the notation, when moving from a delta-matroid
(E,\mathcal{F})
to a
2
-matroid, we will take
E_{1}:=\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{E}:=\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}:e\in E\}
,
E_{2}:=\overline{E}:=\{\overline{e}:e\in E\}
,
\mu_{1}(e)=\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}
and
\mu_{2}(e)=\overline{e}
. We write
Z(D)
for
Z(D,\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
.
In order to translate between subsets of
E
(thought of in the delta-matroid
D
) and the corresponding transversal of
Z(D)
, we extend the scope of the map
\phi
from Example
15
to delta-matroids.
For a subset
A
of
E
we let
\phi(A):=\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}:e\in A\}\cup\{\overline{e}:e\not\in A\}
.

Following
[
13
]
, for a delta-matroid
D=(E,\mathcal{F})

and subset
X
of
E
, we define

d(X):=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}}|F\bigtriangleup X|.

Let
Z=(U,\Omega,r)
be a multimatroid.
Recall that for a transversal
T
of
\Omega
, we have
r_{Z}(T)=\max\{|T\cap B|:B\in\mathcal{B}(Z)\}
.
From this it is easy to deduce that
d(X)=n_{Z(D)}(\phi(X))
.

We define the
transition polynomial
of a delta-matroid

by

Q(D;w,x,t):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}w^{|E-A|}x^{|A|}t^{d(A)}.

Observe that the transition polynomial of
D
can be obtained from the weighted transition polynomial of

by letting

\mathbf{x}_{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}}=x
and
\mathbf{x}_{\overline{e}}=w
for all

in
E
.
This is the polynomial
Q_{w,x,0}(D;t)
of
[
13
]
.

Following
[
33
]
, for a feasible set
F
and element

of a delta-matroid

, we say that

is
F
-
orientable
if
F\bigtriangleup\{e\}
is not feasible. So

is

exists in

. Let

and

be distinct elements of

such that

is

[

]
, we say that

is

is feasible. (Notice that for

[

, interlacement is described more generally, but we will not need that.)

The next lemma is closely related to Proposition 6.1 of
[

]
.
Its translation to delta-matroids will be useful here and it will itself be useful in the final section.

Lemma 55

Let

Proof.

Let

be the skew class containing

Suppose first that

contains a unique circuit which includes

is not. Hence by

for every element

Now suppose that
\omega^{\prime}
has an element

, but

Ribbon graphs and the topological transition polynomial

In this section we apply our results to topological graph polynomials. The
topological transition polynomial
, introduced in
[
22
]
, is a multivariate polynomial of ribbon graphs (or equivalently, of graphs embedded in surfaces). It contains both the 2-variable version of Bollobás and Riordan’s ribbon graph polynomial
[
2
,
3
]
and the Penrose polynomial
[
1
,
24
]
as specializations, and is intimately related to Jaeger’s transition polynomial
[
29
]
and the
generalized transition polynomial of

[
26
]
.
Describing the polynomial requires some background on ribbon graphs. We keep our exposition brief and refer the reader to
[
23
,
25
]
for additional details.

A
ribbon graph

\mathbb{G}=\left(V,E\right)
is a surface with boundary, represented as the union of two sets of discs — a set
V
of
vertices
and a set
E
of
edges
— such that: (1) the vertices and edges intersect in disjoint line segments; (2) each such line segment lies on the boundary of precisely one vertex and precisely one edge; and (3) every edge contains exactly two such line segments.
As every ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
can be regarded as a surface with boundary, it has some number of boundary components, which we shall denote by
b(\mathbb{G})
. The boundary components will be particularly important in what follows.
A ribbon graph is said to be a
quasi-tree
if it has exactly one boundary component. We let
k(\mathbb{G})
denote the number of connected components of
\mathbb{G}
.
As an example, Figure
1a
shows a ribbon graph with two vertices, three edges and one boundary component. It is a quasi-tree.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a ribbon graph and
e\in E
. Then
\mathbb{G}\ba e
denotes the ribbon graph obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by
deleting
the edge
e
. For
A\subseteq E
,
\mathbb{G}\ba A
is the result of deleting each edge in
A
(in any order). Ribbon graphs of the form
\mathbb{G}\ba A
for some
A\subseteq E
are the
spanning ribbon subgraphs
of
\mathbb{G}
.
We use
\mathbb{G}|_{A}
to denote
\mathbb{G}\ba(E-A)
.

The
partial Petrial
with respect to an edge of a ribbon graph
e
, introduced in
[
22
]
and denoted here by
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(e)}
, is, informally, the result of detaching one end of the edge
e
from its incident vertex, giving the edge a half-twist, and reattaching it. Formally, it is obtained by detaching an end of
e
from its incident vertex
v
creating arcs
[a,b]
on
v
, and
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
on
e
(so that
\mathbb{G}
is recovered by identifying
[a,b]
with
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
), then reattaching the end by identifying the arcs antipodally (so that the arc
[a,b]
is identified with the arc
[b^{\prime},a^{\prime}]
).
For
A\subseteq E(G)
, the partial Petrial
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(A)}
is the result of forming the partial Petrial with respect to every element in
A
(in any order).
There is a natural correspondence between the edges of
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(A)}
, and so we can and will assume that they have the same edge set.
We note that
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(E)}
is the Petrie dual, defined in
[
41
]
, of
\mathbb{G}
.
Figure
1
shows a ribbon graph and one of its partial Petrials.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a ribbon graph,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{e}\}_{e\in E}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\{\beta_{e}\}_{e\in E}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{e}\}_{e\in E}
be indexed families of indeterminates, and

be another indeterminate. Then the

topological transition polynomial
,
Q(\mathbb{G};(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}),t)
, can be defined as

Q(\mathbb{G};(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}),t):=\sum_{(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{P}_{3}(E)}\Big(\prod_{e\in X}\alpha_{e}\Big)\Big(\prod_{e\in Y}\beta_{e}\Big)\Big(\prod_{e\in Z}\gamma_{e}\Big)t^{b(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}\ba Y)},

where
\mathcal{P}_{3}(E)
denotes the set of ordered partitions of
E
into three blocks, which may be empty, and where
b(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}\ba Y)
denotes the number of boundary components of
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}\ba Y
.

We shall apply Theorem
20
to obtain an activities expansion for the topological transition polynomial. This expansion is analogous to the activities expansion for the Tutte polynomial (Theorem
2
), and the quasi-tree expansions for the Bollobás–Riordan and Krushkal polynomials
[
14

15

28

39

.

For a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
we set

\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G}):=\{(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{P}_{3}(E):b(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}\ba Y)=k(\mathbb{G})\}.

Thus when
(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
, the ribbon subgraph
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}\ba Y
comprises a spanning quasi-tree of each component of
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}
.

Now let
(X,Y,Z)
be in
\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
and consider the spanning ribbon subgraph
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}\!\ba\!Y
as a subset of the ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}^{\tau(Z)}
.
For each connected component of
\mathbb{G}
the corresponding unique boundary component of

\partial
in

that meets each edge
e

and

If we arbitrarily orient each edge then the arcs

and

are directed.
We use these directed arcs to classify the edges of

as follows.
Arbitrarily orient the curve

If the direction of one arc

Next, if when travelling around

(X,Y,Z)

Now suppose that

has a total ordering

Given

we say that an edge

(Although missing from the notation,

Brijder and Hoogeboom
[

Theorem 63
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a ribbon graph. Then the set

\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{G}):=\{T(X,Y,Z):(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})\}

Proof.

Given a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}

and then the

as described just before the theorem with

A set

if and only if

We denote the

Next suppose

Above we described how to delete an edge

\mathbb{G}
, resulting in a ribbon graph

\mathbb{G}

A full appreciation of the definition of contraction is not necessary here, but for completeness we include one. If

Lemma 65
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a ribbon graph,
e
be an edge of
\mathbb{G}
and
\omega_{e}=\{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e},\bar{e},\hat{e}\}
be its skew class. Then

Z(\mathbb{G}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}},\quad Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\bar{e}},\quad Z(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(e)}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\hat{e}},

and if
\omega_{e}
is singular then

Z(\mathbb{G})\ba\omega_{e}=Z(\mathbb{G}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e)=Z(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(e)}\con e).

Proof.

Let
e
be an edge of a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
.
If
e
is not a bridge, then it is clear that
(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G}\ba e)
if and only if
(X,Y\cup\{e\},Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
, from which it easily follows that
Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\bar{e}}
.
Next, if
e
is not a trivial orientable loop, since
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}\con e
have the same boundary components, we have

(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G}\con e)
if and only if
(X\cup\{e\},Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
, from which it follows that
Z(\mathbb{G}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\dot{e}}
.
Finally, again using the fact that contraction preserves boundary components, it is readily seen that if
e
is not a trivial nonorientable loop, then

(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(e)}\con e)
if and only if
(X,Y,Z\cup\{e\})\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
, and it follows that
Z(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(e)}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\hat{e}}
.

To establish the outstanding cases in the first part of the lemma and to prove the second part, we may suppose that
e
is either a bridge or a trivial loop. Equivalently, by Proposition
64
,
\omega_{e}
is singular in
Z(\mathbb{G})
. We recall from Proposition
9
that if
\omega
is a singular skew class of a multimatroid

, then for all
x
in
\omega
, we have
Z|x=Z\ba\omega
.

If
e
is a bridge, then

(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G}\ba e)
if and only if
(X\cup\{e\},Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
. Thus
Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}}
, and as
\omega_{e}
is singular,

Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}}=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\bar{e}}
, giving

Z(\mathbb{G}\ba e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\bar{e}}
. Similarly,
if

is a trivial orientable loop, then
(X,Y,Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G}\con e)
if and only if
(X,Y\cup\{e\},Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
. Thus

Z(\mathbb{G}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\bar{e}}=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\accentset{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{0.4}{$\bullet$}}}}}{e}}
.
Finally, if

if and only if
(X,Y\cup\{e\},Z)\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathbb{G})
. Thus

Z(\mathbb{G}^{\tau(e)}\con e)=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\bar{e}}=Z(\mathbb{G})|_{\widehat{e}}

Combining this with the first part of the proof completes the first part of the lemma. The second part then follows because

\omega_{e}
is singular, so
Z(\mathbb{G})\ba\omega_{e}=Z|x
for all

in
\omega_{e}
.

As shown in

Q(\mathbb{G})=\begin{cases}t^{|V|}&\text{if $\mathbb{G}=(V,\emptyset)$ is edgeless,}\\
