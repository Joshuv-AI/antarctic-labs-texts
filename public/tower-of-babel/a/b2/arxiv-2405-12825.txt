Classification of translation surfaces in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} with constant sectional curvature
Muhittin Evren Aydin, Rafael López, Adela Mihai

Abstract
. In this paper, we study translation surfaces in the Euclidean space endowed with a canonical semi-symmetric non-metric connection. We completely classify the translation surfaces of constant sectional curvature with respect to this connection, proving that they are generalized cylinders. This consequence is the same as in the case of the Levi-Civita connection, but in this new setting, there are also generalized cylinders whose sectional curvature can be constant or non-constant, in contrast to the Levi-Civita connection, where the Gaussian curvature is zero.

1.
Introduction and statement of the results

Let
(x,y,z)
be the canonical coordinates in the
3
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A surface
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is called a

if

is locally a graph of the form
z=f(x)+g(y)
, for some smooth functions
f
and
g
. If
M
is parametrized by
\psi(x,y)=(x,y,f(x)+g(y))
, then
M
is the sum of two planar curves, namely,
\psi(x,y)=(x,0,f(x))+(0,y,g(y))
. Scherk proved that a translation minimal surface is a plane or
z=\log\cos y-\log\cos x

[
16
]
. Liu showed that circular cylinders are the only translation surfaces with non-zero constant mean curvature
[
9
]
. In
[
9
]
, it was also proved that if the Gaussian curvature of a translation surface
M
is constant then
M
is a generalized cylinder and so the curvature is identically
0
. The notion of translation surfaces can be generalized assuming that the surface is the sum of two spatial curves of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It is known the classification of such surfaces if the Gaussian curvature is constant
[
5
,
10
]
or if the mean curvature is zero
[
4
,
6
,
11
]
or non-zero constant
[
4
]
.

In this paper, we study translation surfaces when
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is endowed with a semi-symmetric non-metric connection. We introduce the definitions. Let
\langle,\rangle
be the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. An affine connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be
semi-symmetric
if there is a non-zero vector field
W\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that its torsion is

(1)

\widetilde{T}(X,Y)=\langle W,Y\rangle X-\langle W,X\rangle Y,\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

If in addition the metric
\langle,\rangle
is not parallel,
\widetilde{\nabla}\langle,\rangle\neq 0
, then
\widetilde{\nabla}
is said to be a
semi-symmetric non-metric connection
. In such a case, there is a relation between
\widetilde{\nabla}
and the Levi-Civita connection
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
, namely,

\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y=\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}_{X}Y+\langle W,Y\rangle X,\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

The definition of a semi-symmetric connection on a Riemannian manifold was introduced in
[
3
]
by Friedmann and Schouten. The study of submanifolds endowed with semi-symmetric connections was initiated in
[
7
]
by Hayden. Following this, the submanifolds in Riemannian manifolds endowed with a semi-symmetric metric connection were studied in
[
8
,
15
,
17
]
. The notion of semi-symmetric non-metric connections on Riemannian manifolds was introduced in
[
1
,
2
]
by Agashe and Chafle, studying their properties and submanifolds.

Many semi-symmetric non-metric connections can be defined by depending on the vector field
W
. The simplest choice for
W
is to consider the canonical vector fields
\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\partial_{z}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Without loss of generality, in this paper we will assume that
W=\partial_{z}
.

Definition 1.1
.

A semi-symmetric non-metric connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is said to be
canonical
if it is defined by

(2)

\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y=\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}_{X}Y+\langle\partial_{z},Y\rangle X,\quad X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

To abbreviate, we will say simply
canonical snm-connection
. Once we have defined the connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
, we ask for the similar problems that were presented at the beginning of the paper. Let
M
be a surface isometrically immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Denote by
\nabla
and
\nabla^{0}
be the induced connections on
M
from
\widetilde{\nabla}
and
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
, respectively. For the problems involving the mean curvature, we point out that the second fundamental forms of
M
with respect to
\widetilde{\nabla}
and
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
coincide (see Eq. (
5
) below). In particular, the mean curvature coincides for both connections and thus questions related with the mean curvature of surfaces are the same than with the Levi-Civita connection.

We now deal with problems about the sectional curvature. Let
K^{0}
denote the sectional curvature of
M
with respect to the induced Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{0}
. Since the dimension of
M
is
2
, the sectional curvature
K^{0}
is simply the Gaussian curvature of
M
. However, the notion of sectional curvature of
\widetilde{\nabla}
, and consequently of
\nabla
, cannot be defined by using the usual way as the Levi-Civita connection. For this, recently the third author of the present paper in collaboration with I. Mihai have defined the sectional curvature of a Riemannian manifold endowed with a snm-connection
[
14
]
. So, if
\widetilde{R}
is the tensor curvature of
\widetilde{\nabla}
, then the sectional curvature
\widetilde{K}(\pi)
of a plane
\pi
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is defined by

\widetilde{K}(\pi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1})+\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2})\right),

where
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
\pi
. This definition does not depend on the basis of
\pi
. Similarly, it is defined the sectional curvature
K
of the tangent plane of a surface of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with respect to
\nabla
.

Motivated by the results of translation surfaces with constant Gauss curvature in the Euclidean space, we consider the problem of classifying the translation surfaces with
K
constant. We are able to provide a successful answer to this problem in the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
M
is a translation surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of constant sectional curvature
K
with respect to the canonical snm-connection then
M
is a generalized cylinder.

Since the canonical snm-connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
is constructed with the vector field
\partial_{z}
, the coordinates
x
and
y
can be interchanged. Thus we can assume that a translation surface can be given in the forms
z=f(x)+g(y)
or
x=f(y)+g(z)
. From Thm.
1.2
, if the surface is a generalized cylinder, then one of the functions
f
or
g
must be linear. It is important to point out a difference with respect to the Levi-Civita connection. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Gaussian curvature of a generalized cylinder is constantly
0
. In contrast, with the canonical snm-connection
\nabla
, the sectional curvature
K
may or may not be a constant but if it is a constant, it is not necessarily
0
.

The organization of the paper is as follows. We first recall some fundamental formulas and equations in Sect.
2

3

4

1.2

5.1
,
5.4
and
5.6

2.
Riemannian manifolds endowed with snm connections

Let
(\widetilde{M},\tilde{g})
be a Riemannian manifold,
\mbox{dim}\widetilde{M}\geq 2
and let
\widetilde{\nabla}
be an affine connection on
\widetilde{M}
. The torsion of
\widetilde{\nabla}
is a
(1,2)
-tensor field
\widetilde{T}
and the curvature of
\widetilde{\nabla}
is a
(1,3)
-tensor field

defined, respectively, by

\begin{split}\widetilde{T}(X,Y)&=\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y-\widetilde{\nabla}_{Y}X-[X,Y],\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{M}),\\
\widetilde{R}(X,Y)Z&=\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}\widetilde{\nabla}_{Y}Z-\widetilde{\nabla}_{Y}\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Z-\widetilde{\nabla}_{[X,Y]}Z,\quad\forall X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{M}).\end{split}

The affine connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
on
\widetilde{M}
is said to be
semi-symmetric
if there is a nonzero vector field
W\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{M})
such that its torsion is

\widetilde{T}(X,Y)=\tilde{g}(Y,W)X-\tilde{g}(W,X)Y,\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{M}).

See
[
3
]
. We also call
\widetilde{\nabla}
a
semi-symmetric non-metric connection
(
snm-connection
) if
\widetilde{\nabla}\tilde{g}\neq 0

[
1
,
2
]
.

Let
\widetilde{\nabla}
be a snm-connection on
(\widetilde{M},\tilde{g})
. If
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
is the Levi-Civita connection, then
\widetilde{\nabla}
and
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
are related by

\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y=\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}_{X}Y+\tilde{g}(W,Y)X,\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\widetilde{M}).

Also, there is a relation between
\widetilde{R}
and the Riemannian curvature tensor
\widetilde{R}^{0}
of
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
. For orthonormal vectors
e_{1},e_{2}\in T_{p}\widetilde{M}
,

, we have

\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1})=\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}^{0}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1})-e_{2}(\tilde{g}(W,e_{2}))+\tilde{g}(W,\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}_{e_{2}}e_{2})+\tilde{g}(W,e_{2})^{2}.

Although the first term at the right side is the sectional curvature of the plane section

with respect to
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
, the term at the left side depends on the choice of the basis of
\pi
. Therefore,
\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1})
does not stand for a sectional curvature of the plane
\pi
. The following geometric quantity was proposed in
[
14
]
as the sectional curvature
\widetilde{K}
with respect to

(3)

\widetilde{K}(\pi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1})+\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2})\right),

which is proved to be independent of the orthonormal basis of
\pi
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\widetilde{\nabla}
be a snm-connection on
(\widetilde{M},\tilde{g})
. If

and

In case that

\widetilde{K}(\pi)=\frac{\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1})+\tilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2})}{2(\tilde{g}(e_{1},e_{1})\tilde{g}(e_{2},e_{2})-\tilde{g}(e_{1},e_{2})^{2})}.

As an example, we particularize to the case that
\widetilde{M}
is the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\widetilde{\nabla}
be the canonical snm-connection on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined in (
2
) and
\{\vec{u},\vec{v}\}
an orthonormal basis of a plane
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then the sectional curvature of

is

K(P)=K(\vec{u},\vec{v})=\frac{1}{2}(u_{3}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}),

where
u_{3}
and
v_{3}
are the third components of
\vec{u}
and
\vec{v}
with respect to the canonical basis.

Proof.

For the covariant derivatives, we use the formula in (
2

\begin{array}[]{lll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{x}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{y}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{x}}\partial_{z}=\partial_{x},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{x}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{y}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}\partial_{z}=\partial_{y},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{z}}\partial_{x}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{z}}\partial_{y}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\partial_{z}}\partial_{z}=\partial_{z}.\\
\end{array}

Set

\vec{u}=u_{1}\partial_{x}+u_{2}\partial_{y}+u_{3}\partial_{z},\quad\vec{v}=v_{1}\partial_{x}+v_{2}\partial_{y}+v_{3}\partial_{z}.

Therefore we calculate

\begin{array}[]{lll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{u}}\vec{u}=u_{3}\vec{u},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{u}}\vec{v}=v_{3}\vec{u},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{v}}\vec{u}=u_{3}\vec{v},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{v}}\vec{v}=v_{3}\vec{v}\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{lll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{u}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{v}}\vec{v}=v_{3}^{2}\vec{u},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{v}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{u}}\vec{v}=u_{3}v_{3}\vec{v},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{v}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{u}}\vec{u}=u_{3}^{2}\vec{v},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{u}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\vec{v}}\vec{u}=u_{3}v_{3}\vec{u}.\end{array}

Moreover, it is obviously
[\vec{u},\vec{v}]=0
. In consequence, the curvature tensors of
\widetilde{\nabla}
are

\begin{array}[]{l}\widetilde{R}(\vec{u},\vec{v})\vec{v}=v_{3}^{2}\vec{u}-u_{3}\vec{v},\\
\widetilde{R}(\vec{v},\vec{u})\vec{u}=u_{3}^{2}\vec{v}-u_{3}\vec{u}.\end{array}

Thus

\widetilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(\vec{u},\vec{v})\vec{v},\vec{u})=v_{3}^{2},\quad\widetilde{g}(\widetilde{R}(\vec{v},\vec{u})\vec{u},\vec{v})=u_{3}^{2}.

This gives the result and concludes the proof.
∎

3.
Sectional curvature of translation surfaces

Let
M
be a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since the dimension of
M
is
2
, there is only one tangent plane and, consequently, we simply write
K
to denote the sectional curvature of
M
. In this section, we compute the sectional curvature
K
of a translation surface with respect to the canonical snm-connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
. In principle, there are three types of translation surfaces depending on the choice of two coordinate planes. Since the canonical snm-connection
\widetilde{\nabla}
utilizes the vector field
\partial_{z}
, then the roles of the coordinates
x
and
y
can be interchanged. Therefore, after renaming the coordinates
x
and
y
, if necessary, there are only two types of translations surfaces, namely, of type
z=f(x)+g(y)
and of type
x=f(y)+g(z)
.

3.1.
Translation surfaces of type
z=f(x)+g(y)

Assume that
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
and
g\colon J\to\mathbb{R}
are two smooth functions. To simplify the notation, we will use
f^{\prime}
,
f^{\prime\prime}
,
g^{\prime}

g^{\prime\prime}
and so on, understanding which is the variable in each case. A basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
\mathfrak{X}(M)
is

e_{1}=\partial_{x}+f^{\prime}\partial_{z},\quad e_{2}=\partial_{y}+g^{\prime}\partial_{z}.

The coefficients of the first fundamental form are

(7)

E=1+f^{\prime 2},\quad F=f^{\prime}g^{\prime},\quad G=1+g^{\prime 2}.

If we set

w=EG-F^{2}=1+f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2},

then the unit normal vector field
\xi
of
M
is

\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{w}}(-f^{\prime}\partial_{x}-g^{\prime}\partial_{y}+\partial_{z}).

By a direct computation from the definition of
\widetilde{\nabla}
in (
2
), the covariant derivatives are

\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{1}=f^{\prime}e_{1}+f^{\prime\prime}\partial_{z},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{2}=g^{\prime}e_{1},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{1}=f^{\prime}e_{2},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{2}=g^{\prime}e_{2}+g^{\prime\prime}\partial_{z}.\end{array}

From (
1
) the torsion is
\widetilde{T}(e_{1},e_{2})=g^{\prime}e_{1}-f^{\prime}e_{2}
and hence we have

[e_{1},e_{2}]=\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{2}-\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{1}-\widetilde{T}(e_{1},e_{2})=0.

The coefficients of the
(0,2)
-tensor field
h
given in the formula of Gauss coincide with the second fundamental form
h^{0}
of the Levi-Civita connection
\widetilde{\nabla}^{0}
. Thus

(8)

h_{11}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{w}},\quad h_{12}=h_{21}=0,\quad h_{22}=\frac{g^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{w}}.

The covariant derivatives of second order are

\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{2}=(g^{\prime\prime}+g^{\prime 2})e_{1},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{1}=(f^{\prime\prime}+f^{\prime 2})e_{2},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{2}=g^{\prime\prime}e_{1}+f^{\prime}g^{\prime}e_{2},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{1}=f^{\prime}g^{\prime}e_{1}+f^{\prime\prime}e_{2}.\end{array}

The curvature tensor
\widetilde{R}
necessary for the computation of
\widetilde{K}
is

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(g^{\prime}e_{1}-f^{\prime}e_{2}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f^{\prime}(g^{\prime}e_{1}-f^{\prime}e_{2}).

Thus

\begin{split}\langle\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle&=g^{\prime 2},\\
\langle\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2}\rangle&=f^{\prime 2}.\end{split}

The Gauss equation given by (
6
) is written by

\begin{split}\langle R(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle&=\langle\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle+h_{11}h_{22}-\frac{h_{22}}{\sqrt{w}}E\\
&=g^{\prime 2}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1)}{w},\\
\langle R(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2}\rangle&=\langle\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2}\rangle+h_{11}h_{22}-\frac{h_{11}}{\sqrt{w}}G\\
&=f^{\prime 2}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)}{w}.\end{split}

Since
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is not an orthonormal basis, we use formula (
4
) for the computation of the sectional curvature
K
of
M
, obtaining

\begin{split}K=K(e_{1},e_{2})&=\frac{\langle R(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle+\langle R(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2})\rangle}{2w}\\
&=\frac{f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}}{2w}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1)+f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)}{2w^{2}}.\end{split}

This identity writes as

(9)

2w^{2}K=w(f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2})+f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)+g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1).

3.2.
Translation surfaces of type
x=f(y)+g(z)

We do calculations in analogy to the previous case. A basis
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
of
\mathfrak{X}(M)
is

e_{1}=f^{\prime}\partial_{x}+\partial_{y},\quad e_{2}=g^{\prime}\partial_{x}+\partial_{z},

and let
w=EG-F^{2}=1+f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}
. The unit normal of
M
is

\xi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{w}}(\partial_{x}-f^{\prime}\partial_{y}-g^{\prime}\partial_{z}).

The covariant derivatives are given by

\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{1}=f^{\prime\prime}\partial_{x},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{2}=e_{1},\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{1}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{2}=e_{2}+g^{\prime\prime}\partial_{x}.\end{array}

The torsion is computed by
\widetilde{T}(e_{1},e_{2})=e_{1}
and so the bracket is
[e_{1},e_{2}]=0.
Also, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{2}=e_{1},&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{2}=0,\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}e_{1}=0,&\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{1}}\widetilde{\nabla}_{e_{2}}e_{1}=0.\end{array}

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{1},

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

By the Gauss equation we get

\begin{split}\langle R(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle&=\langle\widetilde{R}(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle+h_{11}h_{22}-\frac{h_{22}}{\sqrt{w}}E\\
&=1+f^{\prime 2}+\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1)}{w},\\
\langle R(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2}\rangle&=\langle\widetilde{R}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1},e_{2}\rangle+h_{11}h_{22}-\frac{h_{11}}{\sqrt{w}}G\\
&=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)}{w}.\end{split}

Then, the sectional curvature
K
of
M
satisfies the equation

(10)

2w^{2}K=w(1+f^{\prime 2})+f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)+g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1).

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Suppose that
M
is a translation surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with constant sectional curvature
K=K_{0}/2
. We will prove that
f
or
g
is a linear function, which implies that
M
is a generalized cylinder. The proof of the theorem is by contradiction. Suppose that
f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}\not=0
and
f^{\prime}g^{\prime}\not=0
in a subdomain of
I\times J
. Because
M
can be given in two different forms, we separate the proof in two cases.

4.1.
Case
z=f(x)+g(y)
.

Since
f^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime 2}=w-1
, then Eq. (
9
) writes as

(11)

w^{2}K_{0}=w(w-1)+f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)+g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1).

Since the roles of
f
and
g
in (
11
) are symmetric, the arguments for one of
f
and
g
are also valid for the other. We discuss two subcases:

Subcase
K_{0}=1
. Then (
11
) is

w=2f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}-(1+g^{\prime 2})f^{\prime\prime}-(1+f^{\prime 2})g^{\prime\prime}.

Dividing by
f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}
, we write

(12)

\frac{w}{f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}}+\frac{1+g^{\prime 2}}{g^{\prime\prime}}+\frac{1+f^{\prime 2}}{f^{\prime\prime}}=2.

Differentiating identity (
12
) with respect to
x
and next with respect to
y
, we have

\left(\frac{w}{f^{\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime}}\right)_{xy}=0.

This yields

(13)

2f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime 2}g^{\prime\prime\prime}+2g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime 2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}-wg^{\prime\prime\prime}f^{\prime\prime\prime}=0.

If
f^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
and
g^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
identically, then
f^{\prime\prime}=c_{1}^{-1}
and
g^{\prime\prime}=c_{2}^{-1}
, for nonzero constants
c_{1},c_{2}
. This allows us to write Eq. (
12
) as

c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}+c_{2}-2+c_{1}(c_{2}+1)f^{\prime 2}+c_{2}(c_{1}+1)g^{\prime 2}=0.

It implies
c_{1}+1=c_{2}+1=c_{1}c_{2}+c_{1}+c_{2}-2=0
, which is not possible.

Hence we have
f^{\prime\prime\prime}g^{\prime\prime\prime}\neq 0
. If we go back to Eq. (
13
), then

1=\frac{2f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime 2}}{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}-f^{\prime 2}+\frac{2g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime 2}}{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}-g^{\prime 2}.

There are some nonzero constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that
c_{3}+c_{4}=1
and

\frac{2f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime 2}}{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}-f^{\prime 2}=c_{3},\quad\frac{2g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime 2}}{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}-g^{\prime 2}=c_{4},

or equivalently

\frac{2f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}}{c_{3}+f^{\prime 2}}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime\prime}},\quad\frac{2g^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}}{c_{4}+g^{\prime 2}}=\frac{g^{\prime\prime\prime}}{g^{\prime\prime}}.

Integrating once, we get

f^{\prime\prime}=c_{5}(c_{3}+f^{\prime 2}),\quad g^{\prime\prime}=c_{6}(c_{4}+g^{\prime 2}),

for some nonzero constants
c_{5}
and
c_{6}
. Substituting into (
12
), we obtain a polynomial equation of
f^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime}
of the form

A_{0}+A_{1}f^{\prime 2}+A_{2}g^{\prime 2}+A_{3}f^{\prime 2}g^{\prime 2}=0,

where

\begin{split}A_{0}&=1+c_{3}c_{5}+c_{4}c_{6}-2c_{3}c_{4}c_{5}c_{6},\\
A_{1}&=1+c_{5}+c_{4}c_{6}-2c_{4}c_{5}c_{6},\\
A_{2}&=1+c_{3}c_{5}+c_{6}-2c_{3}c_{5}c_{6},\\
A_{3}&=c_{5}+c_{6}-2c_{5}c_{6}.\end{split}

Since all coefficients
A_{i}
must vanish, then the combinations
A_{0}-A_{1}=0
and
A_{0}-A_{2}=0
give
c_{3}c_{5}=\frac{1}{2}=c_{4}c_{6}.
On the other hand,

0=A_{1}+A_{2}=3+c_{5}+c_{6}-2c_{5}c_{6}=3+A_{3}.

This gives
A_{3}=-3
, a contradiction.

Subcase
K_{0}\neq 1
. We introduce new functions

P=P(f)=f^{\prime 2},\quad Q=Q(g)=g^{\prime 2}.

Then
P^{\prime}=2f^{\prime\prime}
and
Q^{\prime}=2g^{\prime\prime}
. Equation (
11
) is now

(14)

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}4\left(K_{0}+(2K_{0}-1)(P+Q)+(K_{0}-1)(P^{2}+Q^{2})+2(K_{0}-1)PQ\right)\\
=P^{\prime}(Q^{\prime}-2Q-2)+Q^{\prime}(P^{\prime}-2P-2).\end{array}\right.

Obviously,
P^{\prime\prime}
cannot be zero identically, because otherwise (
14
) would be a polynomial on
P
of degree
2
whose leading coefficient is
4(K_{0}-1)\not=0
. By the symmetry,
Q^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
. Taking successive derivatives of identity (
14
) with respect to
f
and
g
, we obtain

(15)

8(K_{0}-1)P^{\prime}Q^{\prime}=P^{\prime\prime}(Q^{\prime\prime}-2Q^{\prime})+Q^{\prime\prime}(P^{\prime\prime}-2P^{\prime}).

Claim:
P^{\prime\prime}
is not proportional to
P^{\prime}
. The proof of the claim is by contradiction. If
P^{\prime\prime}=c_{1}P^{\prime}
,
c_{1}\neq 0
, then a first integration follows
P^{\prime}=c_{1}P+d_{1}
, for some constant
d_{1}
. Inserting in (
14
) we have a polynomial on
P
with non-vanishing leading coefficient, which is a contradiction. By the symmetry we also conclude that
Q^{\prime\prime}
is not proportional to
Q^{\prime}
.

Once we have established the claim, let us divide (
15
) by
P^{\prime\prime}Q^{\prime\prime}
, obtaining

8(K_{0}-1)\frac{P^{\prime}Q^{\prime}}{P^{\prime\prime}Q^{\prime\prime}}=P^{\prime\prime}+Q^{\prime\prime}-2(P^{\prime}+Q^{\prime}).

Finally, the successive derivatives with respect to
f
and
g
yield

\left(\frac{P^{\prime}}{P^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{\prime}\left(\frac{Q^{\prime}}{Q^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{\prime}=0,

which implies
P^{\prime}/P^{\prime\prime}
or
Q^{\prime}/Q^{\prime\prime}
are constant functions, contrary to the Claim. This finishes the proof when the translation surface is
z=f(x)+g(y)
.

4.2.
Case
x=f(y)+g(z)
.

Equation (
10
) is now

(16)

w^{2}K_{0}=w(1+f^{\prime 2})+f^{\prime\prime}(g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)+g^{\prime\prime}(f^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime 2}-1).

As opposed to the previous subsection, the roles of
f
and
g
in (
16
) are not symmetric. Dividing (
16
) by
1+f^{\prime 2}
, we have

(17)

w(K_{0}-1)+K_{0}g^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime\prime}+K_{0}\frac{g^{\prime 4}}{1+f^{\prime 2}}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)}{1+f^{\prime 2}}.

We discuss three subcases:

Subcase
K_{0}=0
. Equation (
17
) is

(18)

-1-f^{\prime 2}-g^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime\prime}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)}{1+f^{\prime 2}}.

If we differentiate (
18
) with respect to
y
and
z
, one gets

0=\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{1+f^{\prime 2}}\right)^{\prime}(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}.

If
(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{1+f^{\prime 2}})^{\prime}=0
identically, then (
18
) would be a polynomial on
f^{\prime}
of degree
2
with non-vanishing leading coefficient, which is not possible. Then
(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}=0
identically, that is,
2g^{\prime\prime}=c+g^{\prime 2}
, for some constant
c
. Replacing in Eq. (
18
) we have a polynomial on
g^{\prime}
of degree
2
with non-vanishing leading coefficient. This is a contradiction again.

Subcase
K_{0}=1
. Then, (
17
) is

(19)

g^{\prime 2}+g^{\prime\prime}+\frac{g^{\prime 4}}{1+f^{\prime 2}}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2}-1)}{1+f^{\prime 2}}.

We differentiate (
19
) with respect to
y
and

, obtaining

-8g^{\prime 3}g^{\prime\prime}\frac{f^{\prime}f^{\prime\prime}}{(1+f^{\prime 2})^{2}}=(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{1+f^{\prime 2}}\right)^{\prime}.

Therefore there are nonzero constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that
c_{1}c_{2}=1
and

\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{1+f^{\prime 2}}\right)^{\prime}=c_{1}\left(\frac{1}{1+f^{\prime 2}}\right)^{\prime},\quad(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}=c_{2}(g^{\prime 4})^{\prime}.

Integrating both equations, we deduce

f^{\prime\prime}=c_{1}+d_{1}(1+f^{\prime 2}),\quad 2g^{\prime\prime}=c_{2}g^{\prime 4}+g^{\prime 2}+d_{2},

for some constants
d_{1},d_{2}
. Inserting in (

), we obtain

\frac{d_{2}}{2}+\frac{3}{2}g^{\prime 2}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}g^{\prime 4}=\frac{c_{1}(d_{2}-1)}{1+f^{\prime 2}}+d_{1}(c_{2}g^{\prime 4}+d_{2}-1).

This is a polynomial on
g^{\prime}
whose coefficient of the term of degree

is
\frac{3}{2}
: a contradiction.

Subase
K_{0}\notin\{0,1\}
. By the successive derivatives of (

) with respect to

and

, we obtain

K_{0}\left(\frac{1}{1+f^{\prime 2}}\right)^{\prime}(g^{\prime 4})^{\prime}=\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{1+f^{\prime 2}}\right)^{\prime}(2g^{\prime\prime}-g^{\prime 2})^{\prime}.

In analogy to the previous subcase we may conclude

f^{\prime\prime}=c_{1}+d_{1}(1+f^{\prime 2}),\quad 2g^{\prime\prime}=c_{2}g^{\prime 4}+g^{\prime 2}+d_{2}.

Substituting into (
17
), we have a polynomial equation on
f^{\prime}
of degree
4
with leading coefficient
K_{0}-1\not=0
, which is not possible.

5.
Generalized cylinders with constant sectional curvature

In Thm.
1.2
we have proved that if
M
is a translation surface
z=f(x)+g(y)
or
x=f(y)+g(z)
with
K
constant, then one of the functions
f
or
g
is linear. In particular,
M
is a generalized cylinder whose rulings, depending on the case, are parallel to one of the coordinate axes. In this section we complete the classification of the translation surfaces with
K
constant giving a full description of these cylinders.

For the surface
z=f(x)+g(y)
, we will assume, without loss of generality, that the function
g
is linear. After a vertical translation, which it does not affect the value of
K
, let
g(y)=ay
. However, as previously mentioned, in case
x=f(y)+g(z)
the roles of
f
and
g
are not symmetric. In particular, we have to distinguish if
f
or
g
are linear functions.

5.1.
Generalized cylinders
z=f(x)+ay

Let
M
be a generalized cylinder of type
z=f(x)+ay
and assume that the sectional curvature of
M
is constant,
K=K_{0}/2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

If a generalized cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of type
z=f(x)+ay
has constant sectional curvature
K_{0}/2
with respect to the canonical snm-connection, then it is either a plane
(K_{0}=\frac{a^{2}}{1+a^{2}})
, or a grim reaper cylinder
(K_{0}=1)
, or the function
f
satisfies the integral equation

(20)

x=\pm\int^{f}\left(\frac{cK_{0}-e^{\frac{2u}{1+a^{2}}}-c}{e^{\frac{2u}{1+a^{2}}}-cK_{0}}\right)^{1/2}du,\quad K_{0}\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty),\quad c>0,

where the maximal domain is

(1)

f\in(-\infty,(1+a^{2})\log\sqrt{cK_{0}})
if
0<K_{0}<1
,

(2)

f\in((1+a^{2})\log\sqrt{c(K_{0}-1)},(1+a^{2})\log\sqrt{cK_{0}})
if
K_{0}>1
.

Proof.

Equation (
11
) is now

(21)

(1+a^{2}+f^{\prime 2})^{2}(K_{0}-1)+(1+a^{2}+f^{\prime 2})+(1+a^{2})f^{\prime\prime}=0.

We distinguish the following cases:

(1)

If
f^{\prime}=0
identically, then (
21
) is simply
(1+a^{2})K_{0}=a^{2}
and the resulting surface is a plane parallel with
K=\frac{a^{2}}{1+a^{2}}
.

(2)

If
K_{0}=1
, then Eq. (
21
) is

(1+a^{2})f^{\prime\prime}+1+a^{2}+f^{\prime 2}=0.

The solution of this equation is

f(x)=(1+a^{2})\log(\cos(\frac{x+c}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}}}))+d,\quad c,d\in\mathbb{R}.

This curve is known as grim reaper and the resulting surface
M
becomes a grim reaper cylinder
[
13
]
.

(3)

Suppose
f^{\prime\prime}\not=0
and
K_{0}\not=1
. We introduce
P(f)=f^{\prime 2}
. Denote by
p(f)=P(f)+a^{2}
, then (
21
) is

(1+a^{2})p^{\prime}+2(K_{0}-1)p^{2}+2(2K_{0}-1)p+2K_{0}=0.

A first integral follows

(22)

f^{\prime 2}+a^{2}=\frac{e^{\frac{2f}{1+a^{2}}}-cK_{0}}{cK_{0}-e^{\frac{2f}{1+a^{2}}}-c},\quad c>0,

where
K_{0}
must be always positive because the left hand side is strictly positive. In addition, the numerator and the denominator must be always negative, namely

c(K_{0}-1)<e^{\frac{2f}{1+a^{2}}}<cK_{0},\quad K_{0}\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty).

Consequently, we have the restriction on the domain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}f\in(-\infty,(1+a^{2})\log\sqrt{cK_{0}}),\quad 0<K_{0}<1\\
f\in((1+a^{2})\log\sqrt{c(K_{0}-1)},(1+a^{2})\log\sqrt{cK_{0}}),\quad K_{0}>1.\end{array}\right.

Finally, by integrating the differential equation (
22
), we obtain (
20
).

g^{\prime 2}=-(1+a^{2})\frac{c+(K_{0}-1)e^{2g}}{c+K_{0}e^{2g}},\quad c>0,

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}g\in(\log\frac{c}{1-K_{0}},\log\frac{c}{-K_{0}}),\quad K_{0}<0,\\
g\in(\log\frac{c}{1-K_{0}},\infty),\quad 0<K_{0}<1.\end{array}\right.

Integrating the differential equation (

Therefore we have proved
