Adaptive Collision Sensitivity for Efficient and Safe Human-Robot Collaboration
Lukas Rustler, Matej Misar, Matej Hoffmann

Abstract
What is considered safe for a robot operator during physical human-robot collaboration (HRC) is specified in corresponding HRC standards (e.g., ISO/TS 15066). The regime that allows collisions between the moving robot and the operator, called Power and Force Limiting (PFL), restricts the permissible contact forces. Using the same fixed contact thresholds on the entire robot surface results in significant and unnecessary productivity losses, as the robot needs to stop even when impact forces are within limits. Here we present a framework that decides whether the robot should interrupt or continue its motion based on estimated collision force computed individually for different parts of the robot body and dynamically on the fly, based on the effective mass of each robot link and the link velocity. We performed experiments on simulated and real 6-axis collaborative robot arm (UR10e) with sensitive skin (AIRSKIN) for collision detection and isolation. To demonstrate the generality of our method, we added experiments on the simulated KUKA LBR iiwa robot, where collision detection and isolation draws on joint torque sensing. On a mock pick-and-place scenario with both transient and quasi-static collisions, we demonstrate how sensitivity to collisions influences the task performance and number of stops. We show an increase in productivity over 45% from using the standard approach that interrupts the tasks during every collision. While reducing the cycle time and the number of interruptions, our framework also ensures the safety of human operators. The method is applicable to any robot for which the effective mass can be calculated.



I
Introduction

Robots are leaving safety fences and start to share workspace or even living space with humans. As they leave controlled environments and enter domains that are much less structured, they need to dynamically adapt to unpredictable interactions and guarantee safety at every moment. There has been rapid development in this field of physical human-robot interaction (pHRI) or human-robot collaboration (HRC) in the last decade (see

For PFL, physical contacts with a moving robot are allowed, but the forces, pressures, and energy absorbed during a collision need to be within part-specific limits of the human body. This translates into lightweight structure, soft padding, and no pinch points on the robot side, in combination with robot interaction control methods for the post-impact phase: collision detection and response relying on motor load measurements, force/torque, or joint torque sensing (e.g.,
[
8
]
). However, the speed and payload of robots that comply with this safety requirement is limited.

Furthermore, in practice, exploiting the full potential of the PFL regime, i.e., stopping the robot only if the prescribed force threshold is exceeded, is difficult or impossible. According to
[
7
]
, a collision needs to be 1) detected, 2) isolated (which robot link)), 3) identified (estimating the external torque or force), 4) classified, and 5) then a robot reaction is triggered—see
Fig.
2
. The accuracy or even the possibility of performing some of these steps depends on the type of contact (single or multiple), the contact location, and in particular on the sensory equipment of the robot. Electronic skins covering large parts of the robot surface
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
facilitate detection and isolation. For identification, force/torque or joint torque sensors together with accurate models of the robot dynamics are the best. However, correct estimation of forces, pressures, and energies on the body of the human operator is not possible for several reasons. First, only the first very brief evolution of the collision is used for collision identification. According to PFL, the first
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
should be considered for transient contact and even longer for quasi-static contact. Second, even if external force on the robot body can be estimated, applying Newton’s third law and assuming that the same force acts on the human body is problematic. Third, without additional visual sensors, the robot does not know with which part of the body it is colliding (different limits apply and different tissue stiffness is needed to model the collision). In practice, collaborative robot applications are thus configured too conservatively, i.e., stopping the robot as soon as a collision is detected. Frequent stops of the robot then negatively impact productivity.

In this work, we present a new method that aims to maximize productivity in the PFL regime as illustrated in
Fig.
2
. The idea is to perform collision detection and isolation using a robotic skin (or joint-torque sensors),
“identify”
the force using a simple equation and trigger a robot reaction. We tested the method on two robots, and the collision is detected and isolated using a pressure-sensitive electronic skin (AIRSKIN) covering the entire robot body of the UR10e robot (both real and simulated), or using joint-torque sensors of the KUKA LBR iiwa 7 robotic arm (only simulated). The key innovation here is that we online estimate the collision force from the robot velocity and effective mass and then use that estimate to decide how to react to the collision. If the estimated force is low, the robot will react by continuing the execution of the task. If the estimated impact force is high, the robot will stop.

To our knowledge, this is the first study that adaptively sets collision thresholds on a collaborative robot that brings safety (force limits according to
[
6
]
) and productivity under one hood. Solutions for use in industry such as AIRSKIN normally employ the same threshold in all skin pads and use a highly sensitive setting, resulting in too frequent task interruptions. In research articles employing large-area skins, same thresholds are also typically employed (e.g.,
[
13
]
).

Our recent work on this topic
[
14
]
introduced this idea, but was largely based on simulation, featured only preliminary real robot experiments and was not safety focused. Here, we present systematic experiments on a real robot by utilizing a robust environment allowing for repeatable measurements. In addition, the collision handling has been redesigned to be safer. Previously, the collision was detected only if the pressure applied to the robot was higher than a predefined threshold. Now, every collision is detected, and only the reaction is based on the estimated force. The new experimental evaluation contains not only transient but also quasi-static (clamping) contacts. We added a second robot with different dimensions and physical properties, and different collision-sensing technology. Finally, all experiments were performed with multiple Cartesian velocities.

II
Method

The core of this work is the evaluation of simulated and real-world impacts in a setup that simulated human-robot cooperation with several obstacles during a task performed by the robot. This section describes the theoretical background, setup, and robots used in the experiments.

II-A

Task and Environment

The environment (
Fig.

CBSF-75-Basic

20\text{\,}\mathrm{N}500\text{\,}\mathrm{N}

II-C

Collision Handling Strategies

We employed three different strategies to handle collision events:

(i)

FACTORY
;

(ii)

FIXED MASS
;

(iii)

ADAPTIVE MASS
.

II-C
1
FACTORY

The first option is common (
“industrial”
) strategy used with collaborative robots in industry: Whenever a contact is detected, the robot is stopped immediately. This serves as a baseline to gauge how much productivity can be gained when a better collision reaction strategy is used.

Dynamic collision handling strategies.
The two strategies described below (
FIXED MASS
and
ADAPTIVE MASS
) are dynamic, that is, the robot reacts to a collision by stopping or continuing based on properties computed on the fly.

Both methods are based on equation from ISO/TS 15066
[
6
]
, which defines the maximal allowed robot velocity given the permissible collision force limit as

v\leq\frac{F_{\mathrm{max}}}{\sqrt{k}}\sqrt{m_{R}^{-1}+m_{H}^{-1}},

(1)

where
k
is the spring constant of impacted human body,
m_{R}
is the
ef
(
ef
) of the robot,
m_{H}
is the
ef
of the impacted human body,
v
is the Cartesian velocity of the robot link and
F_{max}
is the maximal permissible force.

For this work, we need to rearrange the equation and express
F_{max}

F_{max}=\frac{v\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{m_{R}^{-1}+m_{H}^{-1}}}.

(2)

We evaluate an instance of this equation for every robot link on the run, plugging in the instantaneous velocity
v
of every robot link. If collision of a particular robot link is detected, the equation is used to estimate the impact force.

The spring constant
k
is fixed in our experiments to
75\,000\text{\,}\mathrm{N}\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
corresponding to the spring constant of the back of the non-dominant hand as suggested by ISO/TS 15066. We selected the mass
m_{H}
as
5.6\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}
(and we made the hanging buckets weigh the same) based on the average weight of human arm defined also in
[
6
]
.

The two methods then differ in how they determine the robot mass
m_{R}
.

The mass

Setting collision force thresholds.

For both of the dynamic collision handling strategies, on collision of a particular robot link, the collision force is estimated based on
Eq.
2
and compared to force thresholds obtained from
[
6
]
:
140\text{\,}\mathrm{N}
and
280\text{\,}\mathrm{N}
for quasi-static and transient contacts, respectively.

The collision reaction is thus defined as:

T_{transient}=\begin{cases}STOP,&\text{if }F\geq$280\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$\\
CONTINUE,&\text{if }F<$280\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$,\end{cases}

(5)

for transient contacts and

T_{quasi-static}=\begin{cases}STOP,&\text{if }F\geq$140\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$\\
CONTINUE,&\text{if }F<$140\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$,\end{cases}

(6)

for quasi-static contacts.

II-D

Transient Contact Force Estimation

In physical HRI, safety is at stake and we need to make sure that the dynamic estimates of the impact forces, which are used to boost productivity here, are accurate or conservative (actual collision forces are always lower than estimated).
In our setup, the certified force-measuring device is used only at the location of the quasi-static contact. In order to empirically evaluate also the forces of the transient collisions with the buckets suspended from the ceiling, we measured their deflection after impact and approximated them as pendulum.
Therefore, we added a large protractor (circa A4 paper) behind the first bucket B1 and captured the swing of the bucket using a camera and extracted the maximal angle of the pendulum
\theta
. We know that potential energy
p.e.=mgh
and kinetic energy
k.e.=\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}L^{2}
, where
m
is the weight of the bucket,
L
is the length of the string,
\omega
is the angular velocity and height
h=L(1-\cos{\theta})
. Using the law of conservation of energy, the energies at the maximal angle should be equal, i.e.,
k.e.=p.e.
, and from there we can solve for
\omega
as

\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{L}(1-cos{\theta})}.

(7)

For this calculation, we used the baseline,
FACTORY
, setting where the robot stops immediately after impact. We assume that all momentum
p_{r}
will be transferred to the bucket, i.e.,
p_{r}=p_{b}
, with momentum of the bucket given by
p_{b}=mv=mL\omega
. From impulse-momentum principle we know that the average force over a small period of time
\Delta t
is equal to the moment, i.e.,
F_{avg}\Delta t=p_{r}
. If we put all together and solve for
F
we get

F_{avg}=\frac{mL\sqrt{\frac{2g}{L}(1-cos{\theta})}}{\Delta t}.

(8)

III
Experiments and Results

We performed several simulated and real-world experiments using our setup that mimics possible collision scenarios in human-robot collaboration. All data collected during the experiments are available at
https://rustlluk.github.io/adaptive-sensitivity/
.

III-A

Experiments Settings

As described in
Section
II-A
, the robot performed a mock pick-and-place task in a controlled environment with three buckets (
5.6\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}
each) suspended from the ceiling that acted as obstacles (simulation of a collision with the human arm). Collisions were detected and isolated and depending on the collision reaction strategy, the robot was commanded to stop or to continue task operation. If stop was triggered, the robot remained stopped for
1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
. If there was no longer a collision after the waiting period, the robot continued its task. If a new collision occurred at the same place (usually the bucket swinged back to the robot), the robot evaluted it, possibly stopping again.
In addition to the buckets, there was an impact measuring device attached to the table, which simulated a clamping scenario between the robot and a human operator. As this contact was not transient, if a stop was triggered, the robot stopped for
1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
and then was commanded to continue the task.

The robots were controlled in joint velocity so that the end-effector moved with a predefined Cartesian velocity. However, we had to make the movement towards the quasi-static collision at max
400\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
in order not to trigger the internal safety controller of the robot.

In simulation, we tested both robots with Cartesian velocity of 200, 400, and
600\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
. For each velocity and each collision strategy (
FACTORY
,
FIXED MASS
,
ADAPTIVE MASS
) we ran 100 repetitions of the experiments. In the real world, we tested the UR10e robot with velocities of
400
and
600\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
and performed 10 repetitions for each velocity and collision reaction strategy.

UR10e with AIRSKIN

IV
Conclusion, Discussion, and Future Work

We introduced three different collision handling strategies: One static and commonly used in the industry, where every detected collision triggers a stop of the robot, and two dynamic strategies that are calculating estimated contact force on the fly to choose how to react to the collisions—stop or continue operation. We tested the strategies using two robots in simulation (UR10e with artificial skin AIRSKIN and KUKA LBR iiwa 7 with joint-torque sensors) and with UR10e robot in the real world. The results are consistent for the two different robots we used, each of them using a different collision detection and isolation strategy (sensitive skin vs. joint torque sensing). Other collision detection methods are used by collaborative robots (a force/torque sensor in the robot base or using motor currents). Our method is potentially applicable to any of them, provided that collision detection and isolation (which robot link collided) is available. For the strategy that increases productivity the most (
ADAPTIVE MASS
