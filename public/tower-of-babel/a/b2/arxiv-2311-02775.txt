AI-TA : Towards an Intelligent Question-Answer Teaching Assistant using Open-Source LLMs
Yann Hicke Cornell UniversityIthaca, USA ylh8@cornell.edu, Anmol Agarwal Microsoft ResearchBangalore, India t-agarwalan@microsoft.com, Qianou (Christina) Ma Carnegie Mellon UniversityPittsburgh, USA qianouma@cmu.edu Paul DennyUniversity of AucklandAuckland, New Zealand p.denny@auckland.ac.nz

Abstract
Responding to the thousands of student questions on online QA platforms each semester has a considerable human cost, particularly in computing courses with rapidly growing enrollments. To address the challenges of scalable and intelligent question-answering (QA), we introduce an innovative solution which leverages open-source Large Language Models (LLMs) from the LLaMA-2 family to ensure data privacy. Our approach combines augmentation techniques such as retrieval augmented generation (RAG), supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and learning from human preferences data using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). Through extensive experimentation on a Piazza dataset from an introductory CS course, comprising 10,000 QA pairs and 1,500 pairs of preference data, we demonstrate a significant 30% improvement in the quality of answers, with RAG being a particularly impactful addition. Our contributions include the development of a novel architecture for educational QA, extensive evaluations of LLM performance utilizing both human assessments and LLM-based metrics, and insights into the challenges and future directions of educational data processing. This work paves the way for the development of AI-TA , an intelligent QA assistant customizable for courses with an online QA platform. 1 1 1 Relevant code and sample snippets of data for this work can be found at https://github.com/aita-ai4ed/AITA.git

Introduction

Online question-answering (QA) platforms like Piazza or EdStem have been commonly used to deliver timely and effective responses to student inquiries regarding course content, assignments, and administrative matters
[
47
]
. However, responding to the thousands of student questions each semester has a considerable human cost.
This challenge is ubiquitous across disciplines, but it is particularly relevant in computing courses due to their rapid enrollment growth
[
32
]
.

There exists a pressing need for innovative and scalable solutions to provide immediate and accurate answers to student queries, thereby enhancing the educational experience without incurring additional costs. Recent approaches for tackling this problem have revealed several limitations. For example, in 2015 GeorgiaTech launched Jill Watson AI
[
14
]
;
this model relies on ontological frameworks and data banks of question-answer pairs
[
13
]
, which limits the type of queries that can be answered (logistics or syllabus-related) and requires significant time to develop and maintain.
More recently,
Harvard’s CS50.AI is exploring the use of proprietary large language models (LLMs) like OpenAI’s GPT-3.5 and GPT-4
[
1
]
. Khan Academy’s Khanmigo is a similar chatbot tutor, also powered by GPT-4
[
5
]
. These tools send student queries to third-party providers, thus raising data privacy concerns.

To overcome the above-mentioned limitations, we leverage the adaptability of open-source LLMs to offer a versatile range of query responses and, crucially, ensure data privacy. Our current implementation uses LLaMA-2
[
48
]
, one of the most advanced open-source LLMs currently available, and serves as the foundation of our pipeline, as shown in
Figure

1
. We enhance the baseline LLaMA-2 model with a novel combination of techniques and test them on an anonymized Piazza QA dataset of a CS1 (introductory-level programming) course (
Section

3
). We apply retrieval augmented generation (RAG)
[
25
]
, supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
[
10
]
, and an alternative to reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)
[
57
]
to improve the quality of the generated answers in response to a student’s question (
Section

4
). We evaluate the performance of different models using GPT-4
[
26
,
50
]
and conduct a manual evaluation of a subset of the model answers (
Section

5
). In this work, we present our preliminary findings and architecture as we progress towards our ultimate objective of developing
AI-TA
, an intelligent QA assistant based on a customized in-house LLM that can be tailored to support any course with an online question-answering platform.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a pipeline that harnesses open-source LLMs for educational QA tasks. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to combine RAG, SFT, and DPO to develop a robust QA system specifically designed for the educational context.

•

We present a comprehensive evaluation of our pipeline, including both LLM-based and rubric-based human evaluations, on a range of models and baselines. Additionally, we analyze the impact of different combinations of augmentation techniques on the performance of the models.

•

We investigate the application of LLM-based solutions in the unique context of educational QA, utilizing a large Piazza QA dataset from an introductory CS course. We also discuss the challenges, limitations, and future directions related to data processing and evaluation in this context.

Related work

Fine-tuning on instruction data:
Self-supervised language models (LMs), when scaled up, are capable of zero-shot
[
38
]
or few-shot task completion
[
7
]
. Performance on specialized tasks and alignment with user goals can be significantly enhanced by fine-tuning them on specific datasets comprising instructions and human-generated text
[
10
]
.

Fine-tuning on human preferences data:
In practice, collecting data based on human preferences for response quality is often more feasible than gathering expert demonstrations for instruction tuning. This involves training a reward model, typically using preference models
to predict scores for generated text. The language model is then fine-tuned to maximize this reward function through reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
43
]
.
This approach has been previously used to improve the LMs’ performance in tasks such as translation
[
22
]
, summarization
[
46
]
, storytelling
[
57
]
, and instruction-following
[
35
]
.
Nonetheless, RLHF is more complex than supervised learning, prone to optimization instability, and is sensitive to hyperparameter adjustments.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
[
39
]
is a more streamlined approach that utilizes a relationship between reward functions and optimal policies to solve the constrained reward optimization at hand in a single stage of policy training. Essentially, it treats the problem as a classification task based on human preference data and is claimed to be more stable and efficient.

Parameter efficient fine-tuning:
Training the smallest LLaMA-2 model, i.e., LLaMA-2-7B, requires over 100GB of memory for just a batch size of four. To address such issues,
[
11
]
introduced LLM.int8(), an 8-bit matrix multiplication method, shrinking model sizes significantly by quantizing parameters. Subsequent work followed to optimize further the required number of parameters to be stored in memory at the same time
[
12
]
which built on top of Low-rank adaptation methods
[
17
]
.

Retrieval Augmented Generation:
Fine-tuning is less suitable for infusing knowledge into the model as: it cannot keep up with frequently changing data, may leak sensitive information, lacks explainability due to lack of references, and risks catastrophic forgetting.
RAG
[
25
,
15
]
addresses these issues by retrieving knowledge through a separate search system and grounding the model’s reasoning and synthesis. With a regularly updated search index, the knowledge used by the LLM is always up-to-date. By examining the documents used by the LLM, users can inspect the source of the generated response and detect potential hallucinations.

AI-based question-answering assistants:

Prior works in different domains have already been taking the approach of fine-tuning open-source LLMs and augmenting them with different techniques such as RAG
[
25
]
and RLHF
[
57
]
. For example, ClinicalGPT
[
49
]
fine-tuned Bloom-7B to the medical and healthcare domain with RAG and RLHF, demonstrating better performance in question-answering in comparison to baseline LLaMA-7B and ChatGLM-6B evaluated using GPT-4. Some of the authors have also preliminarily explored this idea in a Generative AI hackathon
2
2

2

https://genaihackathon2023.github.io
, and the code repo for hackathon can be found

here
.

Dataset

For our experiments, we utilized a pre-anonymized Piazza QA dataset spanning eight semesters (from 2015 to 2022) of a large-scale introductory programming course in Matlab and C, taught at a public research university in New Zealand. The dataset comprises a total of
10,647
QA pairs. We reserved
717
QA pairs from the
2022
semester for evaluation, and we further randomly selected only a subset of
236
pairs due to computational and budget constraints. Additionally, we obtained the instructional materials for the
2022
semester, including the course manual, lab handouts, and assessment dates to perform information retrieval.
See
Appendix

A
for more details about the dataset.

Data Preparation:
In preparation for the QA dataset for SFT, we extracted the single-turn question-answer pairs using a student’s question and the corresponding answer from the instructor or their peers. In case there were multiple answers to a question (e.g., a student answer and an instructor answer), we only kept the final instructor’s answer. Additionally, some posts included various images such as screenshots of code, tables, graphs, IDE, etc. We did some preliminary explorations on object character recognition (OCR) on these images but the quality is still limited because of the diverse image content, so we ended up filtering out posts with images. In
Section

6
, we discuss future work with better quality OCR
[
6
]
and multi-turn and conversational QA support
[
53
]
.

Additionally, upon analysis of the dataset, we observed the presence of near-duplicate queries, even within the same semester. Data duplication and train-test overlap can lead to over-estimate model accuracy in addition to other disadvantages
[
24
,
36
]
. Therefore, we performed deduplication where we created an embedding
3
3

3

using
sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
for each query, applied agglomerative clustering to group potential duplicates in the same cluster, and retained only one member of each cluster. This decreased the size of our dataset by
5.1\%
. In the end, we built a dataset of
10,647
single-turn QA pairs without images or duplicates. See appendix
A.2
for examples of duplicates.

Additionally, we also created
1,549
pairs of answers with proxy human preferences from the Piazza dataset. We used the edit history of Piazza posts: some answers have an original response but are then edited for improvement. We adopted the heuristics of preferring the final answer to the first one to create this human preference dataset. Refer to
Section

A.3
for examples of preference pairs, and
Appendix

B
for more details on the data format for models.

Methods

In our experiment, the primary goal is to evaluate the capabilities of LLMs in the educational domain, especially when they are augmented with RAG, SFT, and DPO. More details about training and inference parameters are included in
Appendix

C
.

Baselines
:
Among many highly proficient LLMs
[
34
,
3
,
9
,
2
,
37
]
, some models are not publicly available, which often demonstrate better performance compared to their open-source counterparts. We used the LLaMA-2 family
[
48
]
as our open-source model baselines due to their widespread adoption and support, specifically LLaMA-2-7B-chat, LLaMA-2-13B-chat, and LLaMA-2-70B-chat. For closed-source model baseline, we used GPT-4
[
34
]
.
4
4

4

Before sending inference queries to the OpenAI API, we ensure that no private or identifiable information is present in the queries.

Supervised Finetuning (SFT):
We used pre-trained LLaMA-2-13B-chat to initialize our model and perform
instruction-tuning
with the previously described QA dataset on a single A100 GPU. We trained the model for three epochs with 100 warmup steps and set the learning rate to
1e-4
with a cosine learning rate scheduler.

Alignment to Human Preferences (DPO):
Because DPO
[
39
]
bypasses the need for sampling from the language model during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter tuning, it is much cheaper computationally than RLHF through PPO. It requires less VRAM to load all three models (policy, value, and reward models) and also hyperparameter optimization is not as expensive.
We used DPO as our fine-tuning algorithm on human preferences data which performs an equivalent of RLHF but on a single A6000 GPU. We also trained the model for three epochs with the DPO objective.

Parameter efficient fine-tuning:
Both our supervised-finetuning and our DPO pipelines make use of the
peft
huggingface library to load our base models before finetuning. We load our models in the 4-bit configuration and then train via the QLora method
[
12
]
.

Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG):

We extracted text from the PDFs (e.g., textbooks and lab manual) using Nougat
[
6
]
, a Visual Transformer model, which converts PDFs to markdown files and is particularly impressive in capturing several instances of LaTeX math, tabular data and code snippets present in the PDFs.

To improve the retrieval efficiency and to account for the limited context length of LLMs, we chunk the individual source documents with a maximum limit of
1000
characters per chunk, and an overlap of
100
characters between adjacent chunks. To keep paragraphs, sentences, and words together, we split the document using predefined delimiters, including double newlines, single newlines, spaces, and empty strings, until the resulting chunks are sufficiently small. We observed that (1) an
embedding-based retriever
is more suitable for queries that require a more nuanced understanding of language such as logistical queries and, (2)
keyword-based retriever
is more robust for queries that make reference to specific coding functions and assignment problem names. To take advantage of both, we constructed the context using a union of the top-
3
documents retrieved by the embedding-based
Dense Passage Retriever

[
19
]
and used the top-
2
documents retrieved by the
BM25 ranking function
. Note that we did not train the retriever end-to-end but instead augment the prompt with the retrieved documents. The prompt construction with the retriever context can be found in
Appendix

B
.

Evaluation:
We adopted two methods to evaluate our models: human evaluation and LLM-based (GPT-4) evaluation.
Previous research
[
44
]
has shown that traditional evaluation metrics such as BLEU, Rouge-L, and Cider are not effective in capturing the usefulness and accuracy of model responses, which are critical in the context of
AI-TA
’s usecase. Nonetheless, for the sake of completeness, we also report scores based on the
BertScore-F1

[
54
]
metric in
Section

5
.

Human Evaluation:
In our study, we utilized a set of rubric items adapted from prior work
[
44
,
55
]
and refined it through an iterative process. Our final rubrics assign scores of
0
,
0.5
, or
1
(with higher scores indicating better quality) to two distinct criteria:
Usefulness
and
Accuracy
.
The Usefulness category measures the practical value of the model answer to a human instructor or teaching assistant (TA), taking into account factors such as the naturalness, coherence, and conciseness of the response. The Accuracy category, on the other hand, assesses the factual correctness and groundedness of the information presented in the model answer.
For instance, a Usefulness score of
1
indicates that the answer is highly useful and would be readily accepted by a TA with minimal or no revisions, while an Accuracy score of
0
denotes that the answer is entirely inaccurate and provides false information.
Please refer to
Table

6
in
Appendix

E
for a detailed description of the evaluation rubrics and examples of their application.

For the human evaluation component of our study, we randomly selected a subset of 20 questions from our evaluation set of
236
questions. Each question was accompanied by 9 distinct model answers, resulting in a total of 180 answers for manual scoring. The 9 models used in this evaluation are listed in
Table

,

5.1
Human evaluation

Baseline model performances are similar, improve with size, and GPT-4 performs the best.

According to human evaluation scores, the performance of baseline models improves with size, with GPT-4 demonstrating the best performance. The LLaMA models of different sizes (7B, 13B, 70B) do not generate significantly different answers, although larger models perform slightly better. However, there is a high standard deviation in the ratings of baseline models, indicating that their performance varies greatly depending on the type of query. For instance, models without retrieved-context perform poorly on logistical questions related to assignments, but can perform well on conceptual questions such as C code syntax.

Different augmentation techniques (SFT, DPO, RAG) have different effects on performance.

SFT alone does not improve performance
, with the
LLaMA-2-13b+SFT
performing 13% worse than the
LLaMA-2-13B
base model. Qualitatively, we observed that
LLaMA-2-13B + SFT
often hallucinates, which makes its answer neither useful nor accurate. Fine-tuning is supposed to teach the model to output correct answers based on it’s knowledge. However, there are some queries (such as logistical queries) which cannot be answered without relevant context and the knowledge regarding such queries was absent in the pre-training data. It is possible that finetuning on such queries infuses the model with the tendency to guess and hence, might explain the hallucinations.

DPO appears to regulate hallucination in models
. The
LLaMA-2-13B+SFT+DPO
model showed a
17
% improvement in human evaluation scores over the
LLaMA-2-13B+SFT
model, with a notable
23
% increase in accuracy. However, its performance was only marginally better than the
LLaMA-2-13B
base model, by
4
%.

RAG has the most significant impact on performance
, with all models incorporating RAG demonstrating improved results compared to those without. For example,
GPT-4+RAG
showed a
35
% improvement over
GPT-4
without RAG, while
LLaMA2-13b+SFT+DPO+RAG
improved by
26
% compared to the
LLaMA2-13b+SFT+DPO
model. Qualitatively, we observed answers in RAG-based models to be much more accurate with less irrelevant information (examples in
Appendix

D
,
Table

4
).

Model answers: generally helpful, may outperform humans or completely fail.

The second-best model as per human evaluation,
LLaMA2-13B+SFT+DPO+RAG
, performs
15
% worse than
GPT-4+RAG
in both usefulness and accuracy metrics. However, when compared to the
LLaMA-2-13B
baseline, its performance improved by 30%. With an overall usefulness of
0.50
and an accuracy rating of
0.58
, it could already be valuable to instructors as a means of reducing their workload while also alleviating privacy concerns in comparison to
GPT-4
.

It is important to note that model answers can sometimes surpass human ground truth answers in terms of comprehensiveness and positive sentiment. For instance, one student asked the question
“Do we get penalties when the code is quite long?”
The human answer was
“Best is to try to simplify as much as possible. If it is too long, it is likely not efficient and can be improved upon”
. In contrast, the response (shown below) from
LLaMA2-13b+SFT+DPO+RAG
was more detailed and encouraging:

“Hello! Regarding your concern about penalties for long codes, I’m happy to clarify that there are no penalties for having long codes in the course.
However, it’s important to note that long codes can sometimes be difficult to read and maintain, so it’s a good idea to strive for conciseness and clarity in your coding. This will not only make your codes more manageable, but it will also help you avoid unnecessary errors and bugs.
So, focus on writing clean, efficient, and well-structured codes! If you have any further questions or concerns, feel free to ask, and I’ll be here to help.”

On the other hand, there are also cases where all models fail to generate any reasonable outputs. For example, given a question that asks the TA to check their submission result on another platform, none of the models respond appropriately. It is not a task models should even be expected of performing.

5.2
Evaluation using GPT-4

As shown in
Table

1
, both human and LLM (GPT-4) evaluations agreed that
GPT-4+RAG
is the strongest model among all. However, the LLM evaluation rated the baseline
GPT-4
as the second-best model, which differs from the human evaluation results. This discrepancy may be attributed to the findings of
[
16
]
, which suggest that GPT-4 has a tendency to favor models trained on data derived from InstructGPT, GPT-4, or ChatGPT, even if the content is less factual or useful.

It is also worth noting that GPT-4 assigned much higher scores to the baseline models than the human evaluation. While the LLM evaluation did capture some differences between models (e.g., it also consistently showed that SFT alone makes
LLaMA2-13b
perform worse), it did not align well enough with human evaluation. To quantify the agreement between the human and LLM evaluations, we calculated the Pearson (
r
) and Spearman (
\rho
) correlation scores. Across 20 queries for the 9 models (180 responses), we found the Pearson coefficient between human and LLM-eval to be
0.662
and Spearman Coefficient to be
0.659
. These scores indicate a moderate positive correlation between the two types of evaluation, suggesting that while there is some agreement between the human and LLM evaluations, there are also many differences. For detailed analysis on the correlation between different types of evaluation methods, please refer to
Section

E.2

6
Limitations and Future work

In this work, our primary goal was to leverage our own in-house infrastructure to generate responses to student queries. Instead of relying on commercial models and prompt engineering through API calls, we aimed to maximize the use of open-source models. This approach enables us to avoid the issues of unreliability and data privacy that come with using third-party service providers. Our experiments have shown that using different augmentation techniques can improve the performance of smaller, in-house models from the LLaMA-2 family, bringing them closer to the more capable GPT-4 model. However, there is still significant potential for further improvement in our future work.

Data:
Our work is limited by the dataset filtering and preparation process. In particular, we may have lost valuable QA data from follow-up interactions that were not included, due to the challenge of separating questions and answers in forum-style data. Furthermore, posts containing images, such as screenshots, were excluded from our dataset. In the future, one could explore multi-turn and conversational QA, as suggested by recent research
[
53
,
40
,
41
,
51
]
. Additionally, one could employ better OCR models
[
6
]
to process images in our data sources.

Training:
Our pipeline consists of several stages - RAG, SFT, and RLHF - and each of these stages can be further developed and enhanced individually. In this work, we only tested the LLaMa-2 models.
In the future, one plan to experiment with specialized domain-specific models, such as CodeLLaMA, which have enhanced coding capabilities
[
42
]
.
We also plan to explore ways to improve performance using an ensemble of models while reducing inference costs
[
8
]
. As already demonstrated in our work, students’ queries and their corresponding answers can often be similar across semesters. Therefore, we intend to retrieve comparable QA-pairs from previous semesters and utilize them as few-shot examples during the inference process, building upon the approaches already explored in
[
30
]
. Additionally, training a retriever end-to-end may further improve the quality of the retrieved context
[
25
]
.

Evaluation:
Our human evaluation is currently limited by the small sample size of 20 responses, which were rated by only one annotator. In the future, we plan to expand our evaluation to include a larger sample size and multiple annotators, in order to establish robust inter-rater reliability for our rubrics. It is important to note that LLMs and existing automatic evaluation methods may not always align with the answering heuristics of human instructors, or accurately capture the pedagogical value of an answer. To address this, we will continue to explore automatic evaluation methods, drawing on insights from existing work
[
20
,
18
,
50
,
28
]
.

Furthermore, our experimentation with LLaMA-2 models has so far been limited to a single course. It has been noted that existing pre-trained models may not perform as well on languages such as Matlab and C, compared to Python and Java
[
33
]
. To address this, we plan to deploy our system across a range of university CS courses, in order to gather feedback from TAs and instructors and experiment with different model configurations.

Deployment & User Study:
We are currently developing the
AI-TA
tool, utilizing APIs from Piazza, Discord, and Chrome extensions to integrate the collection of TA feedback seamlessly into their routine QA workflow. We plan to conduct a user study to assess the productivity and pedagogical value that
AI-TA
brings to classes. Additionally, we will explore interface and interaction design to maximize value and minimize risks in human-AI question answering
[
4
]
. For instance, we could show TAs the uncertainty estimation of the model’s answers
[
23
]
or present the model’s answer as an imperfect peer student’s discussion
[

Supervised Finetuning:
For finetuning the optimizer used was a paged AdamW with a 32-bit precision setting, which helped optimize memory usage and computation efficiency. We employed a per-device training batch size of 4 and an evaluation batch size of 1. To enhance training stability and effectiveness, gradient accumulation steps were set to 2, and gradient checkpointing was enabled. Given the size of the LLaMA-2-13B-chat model and our computational constraints, we leveraged LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) layers with an alpha parameter of 16, a dropout rate of 0.05, and an r parameter of 8. This approach allowed us to adapt the model efficiently without the need for extensive retraining of the entire network, thus saving both time and computational resources. The sequence length was fixed at 1024 tokens to balance between context availability and computational feasibility.

DPO:
