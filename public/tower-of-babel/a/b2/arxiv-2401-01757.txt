Directed polymers in a random environment: a review of the phase transitions
Nikos Zygouras

Abstract
. The model of directed polymer in a random environment is a fundamental model of interaction between a simple random walk and ambient disorder. This interaction gives rise to complex phenomena and transitions from a central limit theory to novel statistical behaviours. Despite its intense study, there are still many aspects and phases which have not yet been identified. In this review we focus on the current status of our understanding of the transition between weak and strong disorder phases, give an account of some of the methods that the study of the model has motivated and highlight some open questions.

1.
Introduction

The model of directed polymer in a random environment was conceived almost forty
years ago as a model of one dimensional interfaces of disordered Ising magnets. The interest
in the model soon went beyond its original purpose. The reason for this was that, on the one hand,
it demonstrated a non trivial interaction between disorder and the underlying simple random walk,
which indicated new exponents different from those dictated by the central limit theorem.
On the other hand, it exhibited links to universality of stochastic growth phenomena, what is nowadays
known as the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class. One of the most intriguing features is the transition
that this model exhibits between the central limit theory behaviour of that of a simple random walk
and new statistical phenomena.
Besides dimension one, where there is now a good anticipation of several aspects of
the novel statistical theory (even if a mathematical theory is still not fully in place),
in higher dimensions the situation as to when the transition happens and
what new statistics emerge is nebulous. In this review we will try to summarise the current status of our
understanding and to present some prominent methods that have emerged during this endeavour, as well
as highlight some questions.

Let us define the model of the directed polymer in random environment (DPRE) on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
:
We assume that
S=(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.
We will denote its law when starting at
S_{0}=x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
by
\mathrm{P}_{x}
and we will often
drop the subscript
x
if this is
0
.
Typically, we will consider the case of
a simple, symmetric random walk but the option of more general lattice random walks, e.g. performing long
jumps, is also available, although less studied in the literature. In addition to
S
, we consider a family of random variables
\omega=(\omega_{n,x})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}},x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
, which plays the role of disorder. We denote its distribution by
{\mathbb{P}}
and the associated expectation by

{\mathbb{E}}
. Again, typically,
\omega
is taken to be a family of independent, identically distributed
(i.i.d.) random variables with

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\omega_{n,x}]=0,\quad{\mathbb{V}}ar(\omega_{n,x})=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda(\beta):=\log{\mathbb{E}}[e^{\beta\omega_{n,x}}]<\infty,\quad\text{for $\beta\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

The possibility of correlated disorder or disorder without exponential moments has also been
considered but unless we assume explicitly such hypotheses, we will be working with the
assumptions of i.i.d. and (
1.1
).
The directed polymer measure is a probability measure on random walk paths
S
defined as

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu_{n,x}^{\beta,\omega}(S)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{Z_{n}^{\beta,\omega}(x)}e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{x}(S)\quad\text{with $\,\,\,Z_{n}^{\beta,\omega}(x):=\mathrm{E}_{x}[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}}]$}.

The normalisation
Z_{n,x}^{\beta,\omega}(x)
is called the partition function. It contains
a significant amount of information and will be discussed extensively in these notes.
Both
\mu_{n,x}^{\beta,\omega}
and
Z_{n,x}^{\beta,\omega}(x)
are random, depending on the
disorder
\omega
, but
to lighten notation we will drop the superscript
\omega
and simply write

\mu_{n,x}^{\beta}
and
Z_{n}^{\beta}(x)
and when the starting point
x=0
, we will simply write

\mu_{n}^{\beta}
and
Z_{n}^{\beta}
.

We will also want to normalise the partition function and the Gibbs factor so that they have mean
1

with respect to
{\mathbb{E}}
and so we subtract from the exponential the term
n\lambda(\beta)
,
thus writing

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu_{n,x}^{\beta,\omega}(S)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{W_{n}^{\beta,\omega}(x)}e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{P}_{x}(S)\quad\text{with $\,\,\,W_{n}^{\beta,\omega}(x):=\mathrm{E}_{x}[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))}]$}.

The measure
\mu_{n,x}^{\beta}
models the situation where a random
walk path
S
moves inside a random “landscape”, which affects its motion. In particular, we see
from its definition that
\mu_{n,x}^{\beta}
gives more weight to trajectories
(S_{1},...,S_{n})
that visit
sites that maximise the “energy”
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}
, i.e. the cumulative
disorder encountered along the random walk trajectory up to time
n
.
This interaction might affect the diffusive behaviour of the random walk since the latter will try
to reach possibly remote and spatially restricted locations, where disorder takes atypically high values. In such a situation we say that the walk becomes
localised
as it tends to move in
narrow corridors, which carry favourable disorder. Of course,
an atypical path trajectory, will have a cost in “entropy”, quantified by
\mathrm{P}_{x}(S)
and so the
problem of determining whether the walk will adopt a different long time asymptotic is a matter
of a complex energy-entropy balance.

Understanding when a phase transition between diffusive and super-diffusive
behaviour takes place has been a matter of study for very long time but still many
questions remain to be settled. This will be the focus of this review.
We plan to start from the classical results that dominated the first 15-20 years of the lifespan of the model
and which demonstrated the existence of a transition between a
weak disorder
phase (where the walk maintains its diffusive behaviour) and a
strong disorder
phase (where deviation from diffusivity is expected).
We will then pass to recent progress in the understanding of this transition both in higher dimensions
as well as at the
critical dimension

2
, where the transition is hidden.
We also discuss phase diagrams when disorder has heavy tails.

The directed polymer model has demanded the deployment of a variety of methods
and has also motivated the development of new ones. The first 15-20 years focused on martingale
methods and a partial characterisation of the weak disorder regime.
At the turn of the century there was the boom of combinatorial and representation theoretic
methods related to the random matrix theory and integrability,
which gave the rigorous derivation of the conjectured
1/3,2/3

exponents for certain one-dimensional,
integrable
polymers at
zero temperature
and the
identification of the limit theory with statistics of eigenvalues of random matrices. The expansion
of what is now named
integrable probability
allowed for the treatment of a few integrable
polymer models at positive temperature, as well as a number of other models in the KPZ universality
class.

Around that time and still continuing till today, methods more grounded to probability are developing
with the purpose of better understanding the transitions in dimensions higher than one and towards
building a universality framework. These methods combine a variety of geometric and probabilist intuition
and tools. Some of the keywords in this direction are: pinning interfaces, fractional moment methods, concentration estimates, coarse graining, Lindeberg principles. Furthermore, the original martingale techniques from the nineties and beginning of the millennium have very recently been revisited and expanded, opening new prospects in the understanding of the phase transitions in higher dimensions.

The above set of methods will be the main focus of this review. Unfortunately, we will not discuss
methods and aspects relating to integrable probability and one-dimensional KPZ asymptotics. This
is a rather separate and extensive topic to which a number of other reviews have been devoted
and to which we refer
[
J05
,
C12
,
QS15
,
BDS16
,
BG16
,
BP14
,
Z22
]
.
We will also not discuss other pertinent polymer models, such as pinning and wetting models or
polymers with self interactions or large deviation type methods.
Some references on this topic are the books
[
G07
,
dH09
,
G11
,
CdHP12
]
and we refer
to them. Complementary features and further references of the topics we discuss here can be found in
Francis Comets’ St. Flour Lectures
[
C16
]
.

A brief historical overview.

2.1.
The physical origins.

The directed polymer model was first introduced in a one-dimensional setting by Huse and Henley

[
HH85
]
as a model of the interface separating the
+
and the
-
states in a two-dimensional
Ising model
with coupling disorder. It is instructive
to see how the model emerges from this angle, so let us briefly review this.

Let
\Lambda_{n}:=[0,n]^{2}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}

and consider the
spin field

\sigma=(\sigma_{x})_{x\in\Lambda_{n}}
with

\sigma_{x}\in\{+1,-1\}
for all
x\in\Lambda_{n}
. On top of this field, consider the disordered field of couplings
(J_{x,y}^{\omega})_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n}}
, which is assumed to be an i.i.d. family of random
variables, which are non-zero only if
x,y
are neighbouring sites, i.e.
|x-y|=1
.
Upon the field
\sigma
, we will impose the probability distribution

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\rm Ising}_{n}(\sigma):=\frac{1}{Z^{\rm Ising}_{n}}e^{\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n},\,|x-y|=1}J^{\omega}_{x,y}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}\quad\text{with}\quad Z^{\rm Ising}_{n}:=\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Lambda_{n}}}e^{\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n},\,|x-y|=1}J^{\omega}_{x,y}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}.

To simplify the situation, we assume that there are just two regions inside
\Lambda_{n}
:
one which has all spin values equal to
+1
and the other with all spins
-1
, and we assume that the two regions are
separated by an interface which does not have loops or overhangs, i.e. it is a path, which we denote by
\pi
, see Figure
1
. To be consistent with this, one should also assign
-
boundary condition on the part of the boundary
above the interface and
+
to the part of the boundary below.
Since
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=1
if
\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y}
have the same sign, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n},\,|x-y|=1}J^{\omega}_{x,y}\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n}}J^{\omega}_{x,y}-2\sum_{x,y\sim\pi}J^{\omega}_{x,y},

where
x,y\sim\pi
means that sites
x,y
are adjacent and on opposite sites of path
\pi
.
The Ising measure, then, takes the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu^{\rm Ising}_{n}:=\frac{1}{\hat{Z}^{\rm Ising}_{n}}e^{-2\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n},\,x,y\sim\pi}J^{\omega}_{x,y}}\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{Z}^{\rm Ising}_{n}(\sigma):=\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{\Lambda_{n}}}e^{-2\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n},\,x,y\sim\pi}J^{\omega}_{x,y}},

since the term
\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{n}}J^{\omega}_{x,y}
cancels out in the ratio.
Even though paths
\pi
can be non-nearest neighbour, the similarity to the directed
polymer measure (where paths
S
are considered to be simple random walks)
is apparent and in fact one should expect similar behaviour given that both

S
and
\pi
fall within the scope of Donsker’s invariance principle.
In fact, Huse and Henley assumed such invariance principle and replaced the discrete interface with
a continuum model to which they performed numerical calculation, which revealed a localisation-delocalisation transition and new scaling (or “roughness”) exponents, which indicated deviations
from diffusive behaviour. Huse and Henley also considered d-dimensional lattices. Then the
connection to the directed polymer is lost, since paths are replaced
(d-1)
-dimensional interfaces.
Predictions of a localisation phase were put forward in
[
HH85
]
for dimensions
5/3<d<5
but
most conclusive and striking were their numerical predictions in
d=2

(where the links to polymer are present). In this case
[
HH85
]
predicted the standard deviations of a polymer path of length

n
are of order
n^{2/3}
as opposed to the
n^{1/2}
deviations of a simple random walk.
Moreover, they predicted that the fluctuations of the logarithm of the partition function
Z_{n}^{\beta}
are of
order
n^{1/3}
.

After the work of Huse and Henley a considerable interest emerged as to the universality of the

1/3,2/3
exponents observed numerically in
[
HH85
]
and further queried
by Kardar
[

]
(see also Huse-Henley-Fischer
[
HHF85
]

The reason that led to querying the universality
of these exponents was that similar exponents had already been observed in the study of hydrodynamics and Burgers turbulence by Foster, Nelson and Stephen
[
FNS77
]

through a more theoretical, dynamic renormalisation approach.
The proposal of the universality of the new exponents
was then put forward by Kardar, Parisi and Zhang in the
famous KPZ paper
[
KPZ86
]
. There they proposed that fluctuations of random (growing)

d
-dimensional interfaces are governed by the stochastic PDE

(2.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h=\frac{1}{2}\Delta h+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla h|^{2}+\xi,\qquad t\geq 0,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
h(t,x)
denotes the height of the interface at time
t
and spatial location
x
and

\xi=\xi(t,x)
is a space-time white noise, that is a Gaussian process with delta correlations
as
{\mathbb{E}}[\xi(t,x)\,\xi(s,y)]=\delta(t-s)\delta(x-y)
.

The relation between the KPZ equation and the directed polymer model arises through
a Cole-Hopf transformation:
h(t,x)=:\log u(t,x)
, which transforms (
2.1
) to the stochastic
heat equation (SHE)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u+\xi u,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

whose solution can be written via the (stochastic version of the) Feynman-Kac formula

[
BC95
]
as

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[\,e^{\int_{0}^{t}\beta\xi(t-s,B(s))\,\mathrm{d}s-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(\int_{0}^{t}\xi(t-s,B(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\big)^{2}\big]}\,\Big],

with
\mathrm{E}_{x}
is the expectation over a Brownian motion starting from
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Even though neither term in the exponential formally makes sense (they are infinities) and
a proper interpretation is required, one can see the connection to the directed polymer model:
one just needs to replace the white noise
\xi
, which is the continuum analog of i.i.d. family,
with i.i.d. variables
(\omega_{n,x})
on the discrete lattice, the Brownian motion with the trajectory
of a simple random walk and

\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(\int_{0}^{t}\xi(t-s,B(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\big)^{2}\big]

with the corresponding log-moment generating function
\lambda(\beta)
, which in the Gaussian case is just
\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}
.

2.2.
Passage into mathematics.

In the mathematical literature the directed polymer model was introduced by Imbrie and Spencer

[
IS88
]
. Analysing certain expansions of
W_{n}^{\beta}
they showed that diffusivity can still persist. More precisely, in dimensions
d\geq 3
and small enough
\beta

it holds that
\mathrm{E}^{\mu_{n}^{\beta}}[|S_{n}|^{2}/n]\to 1
, a.s. with respect to
\omega
.
Diffusivity was then upgraded to a central limit theorem by Bolthausen
[
B89
]
, who also
introduced a martingale approach. In particular,
he showed that in the same regime of
\beta
as
[
IS88
]
,
d\geq 3
and

a.e.
realisation of the disorder
\omega
,

S_{n}/\sqrt{n}
converges to a normal distribution with
covariance matrix
\frac{1}{d}\mathsf{I}
and
\mathsf{I}
the identity matrix.
The martingale approach was further used to upgrade the central limit theorem to an invariance
principle
[
AZ96
]
, while the expansion approach of
[
IS88
]
led Sinai
[
S95
]
to prove a
local limit theorem in
d\geq 3
and small
\beta
.
The diffusive regime that the above works addressed was identified (see also
[
S95
,
SZ96
]
)
as
\{\beta\colon\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)<\log\frac{1}{\pi_{d}}\}
, where
\pi_{d}
is the probability that
a simple
d
-dimensional random walk which starts at
0
returns to
0
in finite time.
We will later see that this condition emerges from the requirement that the second moment
of
W_{n}^{\beta}
remains bounded.

The regime where the polymer exhibits or it is expected to exhibit diffusive behaviour is
called
weak disorder regime
. Opposite to that is the
strong disorder regime
where
localisation and super-diffusivity behaviour is expected to take place. A dichotomy
between weak and strong disorder was identified according to whether
W_{n}^{\beta}

converges a.s. to a random variable
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
, which is a.s. strictly positive (weak disorder)
or a.s. identically equal to
0
(strong disorder). Identifying the critical value
\beta_{c}

(in high dimensions) is still a challenging problem and large part of this review will consider
the progress around this. Below the critical
\beta_{c}
Comets and Yoshida
[
CY06
]
managed
to extend Bolthausen’s central limit theorem, showing that the polymer measure
\mu_{n}^{\beta,\omega}

satisfies a CLT in probability, with respect to
\omega
,
up to the critical temperature as
n\to\infty
. A CLT for
\mu_{n}^{\beta,\omega}

that would hold for
a.e.
disorder
\omega
, for general disorder,
in the whole weak disorder regime is still an open problem. In a Brownian environment such a CLT has been established in

[
J23b
]
. In the strong disorder regime Carmona-Hu
[
CH02
]
,
Comets-Shiga-Yoshida
[
CSY03
]
and Vargas
[
V07
]

demonstrated
*
*

*

the result on the
\sup_{x}\mu_{n}^{\beta,\omega}(S_{n}=x)
not converging to zero
was proved in
[
CH02
,
CSY03
]
in
d=1,2
or under the additional assumption of
“very” strong disorder
,
which we now know that it is equivalent to strong disorder
[

]
localisation phenomena in the form
of
\sup_{x}\mu_{n}^{\beta}(S_{n}=x)
not converging to zero
- contrary to what happens for simple random walks.

2.3.
The rise of integrable probability.

Going beyond the weak disorder regime has been a very challenging problem as a new type
of probability theory, which would cross beyond the central limit theory, is required.
In fact the exponents
1/3
and
2/3
predicted by
[
HH85
,
KPZ86
,
FNS77
]
indicated
a non central limit universality. The first breakthrough in this direction was achieved through
the works of Baik-Deift-Johansson
[
BDJ99
]
, Johansson
[
J00
]
, Prahofer-Spohn
[
PS02
]
,
where the exponents
1/3,2/3
were firmly established and the limiting distribution was discovered
to be identical to distributions arising in random matrix theory. More precisely, it was established that
for the
ground state
of a directed polymer model (more widely known as
last passage percolation
)
in an environment with exponential or geometric distribution the following limit theorem takes place:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\max_{S:(0,0)\to(2n,0)}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}{\omega_{i,S_{i}}}-\mu n}{\sigma n^{1/3}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}{\rm TW}_{\rm GUE},

where the maximum is over all simple random walk paths starting from
0
at time
0
and ending
also at
0
at time
2n
,
\mu
and
\sigma
are model specific constants and
{\rm TW}_{\rm GUE}

is the Tracy-Widom GUE (
Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
), which described (in the same (!)
n^{1/3}

scaling) the fluctuations of the largest eigenvalue of an ensemble of unitary random matrices
with independent (up to unitary symmetry) Gaussian entries.

The
n^{2/3}
exponent emerges through the limit at a process level

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\frac{\max_{S:(0,0)\to(2n,\lfloor n^{2/3}x\rfloor)}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}{\omega_{i,S_{i}}}-\mu n}{\sigma n^{1/3}}\Bigg\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\big\{{\rm Airy}_{2}(x)-x^{2}\big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}},

where now the end point of the path varies through
\lfloor n^{2/3}x\rfloor

(respecting the parity of the walk) and
{\rm Airy}_{2}(\cdot)

is the
Airy-two
process: a continuous, stationary process with one-dimensional marginal given
by the Tracy-Widom GUE distribution. This process was constructed in
[
PS02
,
J03
]
and the
finite dimensional distributions were fully determined.

To achieve these novel limit theorems, novel links to algebraic combinatorics, symmetric function theory
and integrability were required. Some of the key words here are
Robinson-Schensted-Knuth correspondence, Young tableaux, Schur functions, Fredholm determinants, steepest descent asymptotics
.
This endeavour gave rise to the “new” probability theory, which is now known as
Integrable Probability
and which allowed the study of a large number of models in the one-dimensional KPZ universality class. Unfortunately, we will not be able to discuss the methods and results of this exciting field but we can
refer to a number of relevant reviews
[

BP14
,
Z22
]
.

The analogue of (
2.3
) for the directed polymer model:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\log Z_{2n}^{\beta}(0,0)-\mathsf{f}(\beta)n}{\sigma(\beta)n^{1/3}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}{\rm TW}_{\rm GUE},

(the argument
(0,0)

denotes that the path starts and ends at

)
was achieved later through
[

,

]
for an integrable polymer model,
known as the log-gamma polymer. This model was introduced by Seppäläinen
[

where the disorder

been obtained for a semi-discrete, Brownian polymer model, called the O’Connell-Yor

[

,

]
.
The analogue of (
2.4
), i.e.

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\frac{\log Z_{n}^{\beta}(0,\lfloor n^{2/3}x\rfloor)-\mathsf{f}(\beta)n}{\sigma(\beta)n^{1/3}}\Bigg\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\big\{{\rm Airy}_{2}(x)-x^{2}\big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}},

was recently obtained by Arrarwal and Huang (
[
AH23
]
, see Corollary 25.2 therein) employing also geometric and probabilistic methods
on top of integrable structures.

2.4.
Probability strikes back.

Integrable probability has been very successful in obtaining very detailed results for a large range
of models, which possess some specific integrability (exponential last passage percolation, log-gamma polymer, TASEP and ASEP, six vertex and tiling models etc). Aspects of universality, though, cannot be
covered by the integrable probability approach as integrability is lost by any change of parameters, however
small this might be.

Nevertheless, there has recently been efforts to combine the detailed information
that emerges through integrability with more probabilistic methods.

3.
Weak and strong disorder

3.1.
The general framework and some basic results.

Let us start by introducing some notation, complementary to (
1.3
),
encoding when the polymer starts at different times and has fixed end point. To this end, we define,

(3.1)

\displaystyle W_{(m,n)}^{\beta}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}_{x}\bigg[e^{\sum_{i=m+1}^{n-1}\{\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta)\}}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{n}=y\}}\bigg]\,,\qquad\text{and}

(3.2)

\displaystyle W_{(m,n]}^{\beta}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}_{x}\bigg[e^{\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}\{\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta)\}}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{n}=y\}}\bigg]\,,

with the convention that
\sum_{n=m+1}^{n-1}\{\ldots\}:=0
for
n\leq m+1
.
We will also extend the above definition to
W_{(m,\infty)}
, if this quantity is well defined.
If there is only one spatial and/or
temporal argument, then we will always refer to definition (
1.3
), i.e. consider a fixed starting but
free ending point. We will follow similar conventions for the unnormalised partition function
Z^{\beta}
.

The martingales.
Consider the filtration

{\mathcal{F}}_{n}=\sigma\big\{\omega_{i,x}\colon i\leq n,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\big\}
for
n\geq 1
. It is easy to
check that
W_{n}^{\beta}
is a martingale with respect to
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
. Given that it is also non-negative,
it will have, by standard martingale theory, an a.s. limit as
N\to\infty
, which we denote by
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
. The events
\{W_{\infty}^{\beta}>0\}
and
\{W_{\infty}^{\beta}=0\}

belongs to the tail
\sigma
-field because whether the limit of
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
is zero or not
clearly does not dependent on the value of any
\omega_{i,x}
for any finite index
(i,x)
.
By Kolmogorov’s 0-1 law, these events will have probability either equal to
1
or equal to
0
.
The dichotomy
between weak disorder (WD) and strong disorder (SD) is identified with the positivity or triviality
of
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
. In particular, we have

Definition 3.1.

Let
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
be the a.s. limit of
W_{n}^{\beta}
.
If
{\mathbb{P}}(W_{\infty}^{\beta}>0)=1
, we say that weak disorder
(WD)

takes place and if
{\mathbb{P}}(W_{\infty}^{\beta}=0)=1
, we have strong disorder
(SD)
.

Uniform integrability of
(W_{n}^{\beta})_{n\geq 1}
implies weak disorder. This is
straightforward since uniform integrability will allow to interchange limits and expectations and lead
to
{\mathbb{E}}[W_{\infty}^{\beta}]=1
.
It is an interesting fact that
the converse is also true for the directed polymer partition function
W_{n}^{\beta}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
In particular, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 3.2.

(W_{n}^{\beta})_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable if and only if the limit
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
is a.s.
strictly positive.

If
(W_{n}^{\beta})_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly integrable, then we can interchange limit and expectation in

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[W_{\infty}^{\beta}]=\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[W_{N}^{\beta}]=1,

and therefore
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
cannot be a.s. equal to
0
.

For the opposite claim we start by deriving a self-similar identity for
W_{\infty}^{\beta}
.
Using the Markov property and notation (
3.2
), we derive the identity

(3.3)

\displaystyle W_{n+m}^{\beta}=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}W_{n}^{\beta}(0,x)W_{(n,m]}^{\beta}(x),

for any
n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}

and taking the limit
m\to\infty
we obtain that

(3.4)

\displaystyle W_{\infty}^{\beta}=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}W_{n}^{\beta}(0,x)W_{(n,\infty)}^{\beta}(x).

Let us now compute

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[W_{\infty}^{\beta}\,\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}W_{n}^{\beta}(0,x)W_{(n,\infty)}^{\beta}(x)\,\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\Big]={\mathbb{E}}[\,W_{\infty}^{\beta}]\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}W_{n}^{\beta}(x)={\mathbb{E}}[\,W_{\infty}^{\beta}]W_{n}^{\beta},

Taking expectation with respect to disorder
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
on both sides and then the limit
n\to\infty
we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[W_{\infty}^{\beta}\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}[W_{\infty}^{\beta}]W_{\infty}^{\beta}\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[W_{\infty}^{\beta}\Big]^{2}

and if
W_{\infty}>0
a.s. this implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[W_{\infty}^{\beta}]=1
.
This, combined with the fact that
W_{n}^{\beta}
is a non-negative martingale,
implies uniform integrability, see
[
D19
]
, Chapter 4, Theorem 5.2.
∎

Remark 3.3.

It is worth noting that the above equivalence between uniform integrability and positivity of the limit
of the martingale is a more general feature shared among martingales which have a “linear” structure
as that in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
). Systems possessing such property often go by the name “linear systems” and they appear in branching and interacting particle systems,
see
[
L85
]
, Chapter IX.2. We should point out, though, that there are examples of directed polymer models
on more exotic graph structures for which this equivalence fails
[
CoSeZe21
]
.

The dichotomy between weak and strong disorder is marked by a critical value of the temperature.
More precisely, we define

(3.5)

\displaystyle\beta_{c}:=\sup\{\beta>0\colon W_{\infty}^{\beta}>0\}.

In other words,
W_{\infty}^{\beta}>0
for
\beta<\beta_{c}
and

W_{\infty}^{\beta}=0
for
\beta>\beta_{c}
. The fact that this phase transition takes
places only at a single value
\beta_{c}
is a consequence of a monotonicity property.
More precisely, as we will show below, for
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
the function
\beta\to{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]
is non-increasing.
Then, since for
\gamma<1
,
(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}
is uniformly integrable, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]={\mathbb{E}}[(W_{\infty}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]
and by the
mentioned monotonicity,
if
{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{\infty}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]=0
(which would imply that
W_{\infty}^{\beta}=0
a.s.)
for some
\beta
, then it will also be zero for all larger ones.

Let us now prove the monotonicity as part of a more general monotonicity property.

Proposition 3.4.

Consider a function
\phi\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. The function
\beta\to{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(W_{n}^{\beta})]

is non-decreasing if
\phi
is convex and non-increasing if
\phi
is concave.

We prove the statement for convex
\phi
. Let us start by differentiating:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(W_{n}^{\beta})]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathrm{E}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\big]\,\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})\,e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\big]\,\Big].

For a fixed path
S
we define the distribution
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
on
\omega
under which

(\omega_{i,x})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}},x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
are independent with individual laws given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}_{S}(\omega_{i,x})=\exp\Big\{\big(\beta\omega_{i,x}-\lambda(\beta)\big)\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=x\}}\Big\}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}(\omega_{i,x}).

This measure tilts the law of
\omega
on sites visited by the random walk and, thus,
provides a measure of the correlations between walk and disorder. Similar change
of measure will play an important role later in the notes.

Using the tilted measure we can write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})\,e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\big]={\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(\omega_{i,x}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=x\}}\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})\big].

Note that the function
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(\omega_{i,x}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=x\}}
is an increasing function in
\omega_{i,x}
for every
(i,x)

and so is
\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})
, if
\phi
is assumed to be convex. Therefore by FKG
inequality (
[
L85
]
, Chapter 2 or
[
G99
]
, Section 2.2)
and given that
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
is still a product measure we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(\omega_{i,x}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=x\}}\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})\big]\geq{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(\omega_{i,x}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=x\}}\big]\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\phi^{\prime}(W_{n}^{\beta})\big],

which is equal to
0
since

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda^{\prime}(\beta))e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\big]=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\big]=0

since
{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\big]=1
. Therefore,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathbb{E}}[\phi(W_{n}^{\beta})]\geq 0
, and the monotonicity follows.
∎Characterising the critical value
\beta_{c}
has been a problem of major interest since the onset
of the directed polymer model but progress has been slow. However, there has been some important
developments, which we will describe in the next sections.

The equivalence between weak disorder and uniform integrability provides a path to provide
some information on
\beta_{c}
. Uniform integrability can be provided through boundedness
of moments higher than 1 of
W_{n}^{\beta}
. The easiest case is to estimate the second moment
and in fact this is the regime that works
[
IS88
,
B89
,
S95
,
SZ96
]
considered. Let us state:

Theorem 3.5.

In dimensions
d\geq 3
, there exist
\beta_{2}>0
such that

\sup_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}[W_{n}^{2}]<\infty
for
\beta<\beta_{2}
. In particular, this implies
uniform integrability for
\beta<\beta_{2}
and, therefore, that
\beta_{c}>0
.

The proof of this result, which is essentially the original approach of Imbrie-Spencer
[
IS88
]
,
is simple but it contains some elements, including notions of expansions,
whose ramifications will be very useful later on, in particular in Section
4
.

The second moment of
W_{n}^{\beta}
can be easily computed. More precisely, if
S,S^{\prime}
are two
independent simple random walks and
\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}
denotes the joint expectation,
then we have by simple Fubini that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}^{\prime}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\big]\Big]

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\lambda_{2}(\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{i}^{\prime}\}}}\Big],

where we have denoted by

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(\beta):=\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta).

We can deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2}]=\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\lambda_{2}(\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{i}^{\prime}\}}}\Big].

Denoting the right-hand side by
\mathfrak{E}
and
\tau:=\inf\{n\geq 1\colon S_{n}=S_{n}^{\prime}\}
and using the strong Markov property
we have that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}=1+\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}(\tau<\infty)\,e^{\lambda_{2}(\beta)}\,\mathfrak{E},

from which it follows that
\mathfrak{E}<\infty
if
\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}(\tau<\infty)\,e^{\lambda_{2}(\beta)}<1
. Since
\tau
has the same distribution
as that of the return time to
0
of a single random walk, we have that

\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}(\tau=\infty)=\mathrm{P}(S\,\text{does {\it not} return to zero})=:\pi_{d}
, which is strictly positive in
d\geq 3
.
Therefore,
\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}(\tau<\infty)=1-\pi_{d}<1
and, thus, there exists
\beta>0
such that
\mathfrak{E}<\infty
. In particular,
this is the case if
e^{\lambda_{2}(\beta)}<\frac{1}{1-\pi_{d}}
.
∎The question of whether
\beta_{2}<\beta_{c}
, i.e. whether the
L^{2}
regime coincides with the weak
disorder regime concerned the physics literature
[
ED92
,
MG06a
,
MG06b
]
.
We now know that
\beta_{2}<\beta_{c}
in
d\geq 3

[
BGH10
,
BS10
,
BT10
,
BS11
]
but we will detail on this in the next section.
We will also discuss recent characterisations
of the weak disorder regime via moments in Section
3.3
.
For the moment let us record the central
limit behaviour of the polymer path in the weak disorder regime.

Theorem 3.6.

Assume weak disorder, that is,
\beta<\beta_{c}
. Then for every bounded, continuous

f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/\sqrt{n})\,\mu_{n}^{\beta}(x)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{{\mathbb{P}}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}[f({\mathcal{N}}_{d})],

where the convergence is in probability and

{\mathcal{N}}_{d}
is a multivariate normal variable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with covariance matrix
\frac{1}{d}\mathsf{I}
.
Moreover, if
\beta<\beta_{2}
the above convergence is a.s.

The a.s. convergence in the
L^{2}
regime is the original result of Bolthausen
[
B89
]
, while
the extension of the central limit behaviour in the whole weak disorder regime (even if we do not
know
\beta_{c}
itself) was proved by Comets-Yoshida
[
CY06
]
.
An extension to an a.s. central limit theorem in the whole weak disorder regime
for a Brownian disorder has been proved by Junk in
[
J23b
]
.

Let us comment on the existence of a strong disorder regime and a formulation of localisation.
We have

Theorem 3.7.

In dimensions
d=1,2
strong disorder takes place for all
\beta>0
, that is
\beta_{c}=0
.
Moreover, strong disorder takes place also in
d\geq 3
, if
\beta
is large enough so that

\beta\lambda^{\prime}(\beta)-\lambda(\beta)>\log(2d)
.

This result was proved in
[
CH02
,
CSY03
]
(see also
[
CSY04
]
). We will refer there for
the details of the proof. Here we will give a sketch of the main points. In particular we want to
highlight the estimates on fractional moments - an approach that has, more recently,
been developed significantly
and has been very fruitful. The fully developed
fractional moment method will be detailed in Section
3.4
.
Before getting to the sketch of the proof of the above theorem,
let us comment that the set of
\beta
such that
\beta\lambda^{\prime}(\beta)-\lambda(\beta)>\log(2d)
is empty
when the disorder
\omega
has finite support and gives mass
p
to the right boundary of its support such that
\log\frac{1}{p}<\log(2d)
.

A first main point is to establish the existence of numbers
\alpha_{n}
diverging to infinity such that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}\log{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]<0,

for some fractional power
\gamma\in(0,1)
. This would imply that
W_{n}
converges to zero in probability and
since we know that
W_{n}
converges a.s., the a.s. convergence to
0
follows.
The second main point is then to estimate the
fractional moments to obtain the above limiting behaviour.
The use of fractional moment will become a recurring theme in these notes.

The first step here is to
use the linearity (
3.3
) of the partition function and the elementary inequality

(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i})^{\gamma}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(0,1)

and
a_{i}
non-negative numbers, to obtain that

\displaystyle(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{2d}\sum_{x\colon|x|=1}e^{\beta\omega_{1,x}-\lambda(\beta)}\,W^{\beta}_{(1,n]}(x)\Big)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(2d)^{\gamma}}\sum_{x\colon|x|=1}e^{\gamma\beta\omega_{1,x}-\gamma\lambda(\beta)}\,\big(W^{\beta}_{(1,n]}(x)\big)^{\gamma},

and by taking expectation this leads to

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}\big]\leq r(\gamma){\mathbb{E}}\big[(W_{n-1}^{\beta})^{\gamma}\big],\quad\text{with}\quad r(\gamma):=(2d)^{1-\gamma}e^{\lambda(\gamma\beta)-\gamma\lambda(\beta)}.

Condition
\beta\lambda^{\prime}(\beta)-\lambda(\beta)>\log(2d)
implies that

\frac{\mathrm{d}r(\gamma)}{\mathrm{d}\gamma}\Big|_{\gamma=1}>0
and this, together with the log-convexity
of
r(\gamma)
and its continuous differentiability, implies the existence of a
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
such that
r(\gamma)<1
. Then, in this case, (
3.8
) is satisfied with a choice of

\alpha_{n}=n
.

We refer on how to extend this method to all
\beta>0
in dimensions one and two to
[

]
.

Theorem 3.8.

Let

(3.9)

\displaystyle I_{n}:=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\mu_{n-1}^{\beta}(S_{n}=x)^{2}

then a.s. we have that

(3.10)

\displaystyle\big\{W_{\infty}^{\beta}=0\big\}=\Big\{\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}=\infty\Big\}

Moreover, if strong disorder holds, then there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
such that a.s.

(3.11)

\displaystyle-c_{1}\log W_{n}^{\beta}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}I_{k}\leq-c_{2}\log W_{n}^{\beta},

for all large
n
.

Let us observe that
I_{n}
can also be written in the form

I_{n}=\mu_{n-1}^{\beta,\otimes 2}(S_{n}=S_{n}^{\prime})
, where

\displaystyle\mu_{n-1}^{\beta,\otimes 2}(S,S^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{(W_{n-1}^{\beta})^{2}}\,e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\,\mathrm{P}(S)\mathrm{P}(S^{\prime})

is the joint law of two polymer paths run in the same environment
\omega
.
In other words,
I_{n}
is the probability that two independent
polymer paths, which run in the random environment
\omega
, meet at time
n
.
Relation (
3.10
) says that in the strong disorder regime, the number
of times that two independent polymers in the same environment meet is infinite. This is
in contrast to what would happen if considering two independent simple random walks in
d\geq 3
.
This has a flavour of localisation as the two independent paths will tend to meet at sites at the
vicinity of which disorder is favourable.
Relation (
3.11
) can provide a stronger localisation statement if one could
show that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log W_{n}^{\beta}<0
. This would mean that
the Césaro means limit of
I_{n}
is positive, which
could be interpreted (but not directly implying) that two independent
polymers will actually meet a positive fraction of times. This motivates the following definition

Definition 3.9.

We say that very strong disorder
(vSD)
holds if

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}(\beta):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log W_{n}^{\beta}<0.

The fact that the limit in (
3.12
) exists and that it is, in fact, self-averaging i.e. equal to

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}\log W_{n}^{\beta}
, was established in
[
CH02
,
CSY03
]
.
In
[
CV06
]
it was established that in
d=1
very strong disorder holds for all
\beta>0
.
In
[
L10
]
based on an upscaled
fractional moment method
it was proved that very strong
disorder also holds in dimension
2
. Moreover, asymptotics on
\mathsf{f}(\beta)
for
\beta
small
were derived.
The question of whether strong disorder coincides with very strong disorder in dimensions
d\geq 3
, equivalently
whether the decay of
W_{n}
to
0
always happens at an exponential rate, was a longstanding question. In a recent breakthrough
Junk-Lacoin
[

]
have resolved this question in the case of bounded disorder:

Theorem 3.10 (
[

]

In

and for bounded disorder
\omega
, strong disorder and very strong disorder are equivalent.
Furthermore, weak disorder holds at the critical temperature
\beta_{c}
.

One would expect that the boundedness assumption is not necessary but let us state this as a question:

Question 1.

Are strong and very strong disorder equivalent for unbounded disorder
\omega
?

We will further discuss the points of strong and very strong disorder in Section

Under the assumption of very strong disorder and additionally that
\mathsf{f}(\beta)

Carmona-Hu
[

]
. In relation to the discussion above about strong and very strong disorder we can also pose the question:

Question 2.

The most detailed result on localisation is probably due to Comets-Nguyen

]
for the exactly solvable, one-dimensional,
log-gamma polymer. Without presenting the specifics of the log-gamma setting (as this will require a digression) let us state (in rough terms) the main content of the Comets-Nguyen localisation theorem (we refer to
[
CN16
]
for the details and some of the
terminology):

Theorem 3.11.

For the one-dimensional, stationary log-gamma polymer measure
\mu_{n}^{\rm log-gamma}
,
with equilibrium boundary condition, denote by

l_{n}:={\rm argmax}\{\mu_{n}^{\rm log-gamma}(x)\colon x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{l_{n}}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}{\rm argmax}_{t\in[0,1]}B_{t},

where
B
is a one-dimensional Brownian motion with certain diffusion coefficient depending on the parameters of the log-gamma
polymer.

3.2.
Random walk pinning model and non-coincidence of
\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{c}
.

The question of whether the
L^{2}
regime characterises the weak disorder regime has been
a subject of interest since the early directed polymer era. In this section we will see that this is not
the case in
d\geq 3
, through linking to intersection properties of random walks. In particular, in the
second part of this section we will introduce an interesting link to a study of intersections of
random walks, where one path trajectory is considered fixed. This will be the
random walk pinning
model
.

Before proceeding, let us record:

(3.13)

\displaystyle\beta_{2}:=\sup\{\beta\colon\sup_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2}]<\infty\}.

Probably the first study of the relation
between the
L^{2}
and weak disroder regimes is that of Evans-Derrida

[
ED92
]
. There they attempted to estimate fractional moments of

(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2\gamma}
and obtained the following result:

Theorem 3.12.

Let
S,S^{\prime}
be two independent, simple random walks on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and denote by
P^{S,S^{\prime}}
their
joint law. Weak disorder holds for
\beta
such that

(3.14)

\displaystyle e^{\lambda(2\gamma\beta)-2\gamma\lambda(\beta)}\sum_{n\geq 1,\,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}(\text{$S$ and $S^{\prime}$ meet for the {\it first time} at site $(n,x)$})^{\gamma}<1,

for some
\gamma<1
.

The idea is to estimate moments of
W_{n}^{\beta}
between
1
and
2
.
To this end we start by writing

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2\gamma}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\big]\Big)^{\gamma}\Big].

Next, we identify the set of space-time points where
S,S^{\prime}
intersect.
For
(n_{1},x_{1}),...,(n_{k},x_{k})\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, we denote by

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{(n_{1},x_{1}),...,(n_{k},x_{k})}:=\big\{S\cap S^{\prime}=\{(n_{1},x_{1}),...,(n_{k},x_{k})\}\big\},

and by

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}^{(n_{j},x_{j})}:=\{S_{n_{j-1}}=S^{\prime}_{n_{j-1}}=x_{j-1},\,S_{n_{j}}=S^{\prime}_{n_{j}}=x_{j},\,\,\text{and $S_{i}\neq S^{\prime}_{i}$ for $n_{j-1}<i<n_{j}$ }\},

and write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2\gamma}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq n\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\,;\,\mathsf{A}_{(n_{1},x_{1}),...,(n_{k},x_{k})}\big]\Big)^{\gamma}\Big].

Using, again, the fractional moment inequality

(\sum_{i}a_{i})^{\gamma}\leq\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\gamma}
, for
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2\gamma}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq n\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\,;\,\mathsf{A}_{(n_{1},x_{1}),...,(n_{k},x_{k})}\big]^{\gamma}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq n\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}\big[e^{\sum_{i={n_{j-1}+1}}^{n_{j}}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\,;\,\mathsf{A}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}^{(n_{j},x_{j})}\big]^{\gamma}\Big].

Singling out from
E^{S,S^{\prime}}
the disorder
e^{2\beta\omega_{n_{j},x_{j}}-2\lambda(\beta)}

on the sites where the two walks meet, we can write the above as

(3.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq n\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[e^{2\gamma\beta\omega_{n_{j},x_{j}}-2\gamma\lambda(\beta)}\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}\big[e^{\sum_{i={n_{j-1}+1}}^{n_{j}-1}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\,;\,\mathsf{A}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}^{(n_{j},x_{j})}\big]^{\gamma}\Big]

Now compute
{\mathbb{E}}e^{2\gamma\beta\omega_{n_{j},x_{j}}-2\gamma\lambda(\beta)}=e^{\lambda(2\gamma\beta)-2\gamma\lambda(\beta)}

and we use Hölder in

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}\big[\cdots\big]^{\gamma}\big]

and the fact that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}\big[e^{\sum_{i={n_{j-1}+1}}^{n_{j}-1}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}+\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}\neq S^{\prime}_{i},\,\,\,\text{for $n_{j-1}<i<n_{j}\,\,S_{n_{j}}=S^{\prime}_{n_{j}}=x_{j}$\lx@text@rbrace}}\big]\Big]^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}\big(S_{i}\neq S^{\prime}_{i},\,\,\,\text{for $n_{j-1}<i<n_{j}\,\,S_{n_{j}}=S^{\prime}_{n_{j}}=x_{j}$ }\big)^{\gamma}

to obtain that (
3.15
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq n\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}e^{\lambda(2\gamma\beta)-2\gamma\lambda(\beta)}\,\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}_{(n_{j-1},x_{j-1})}\big(S_{i}\neq S^{\prime}_{i},\,\,\,\text{for $n_{j-1}<i<n_{j}\,\,S_{n_{j}}=S^{\prime}_{n_{j}}=x_{j}$}\big)^{\gamma}.

Letting
n\to\infty
we can decouple the spatial and temporal summations and have that in the limit

n\to\infty
the above equals

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}e^{\lambda(2\gamma\beta)-2\gamma\lambda(\beta)}\,\mathrm{P}^{S,S^{\prime}}\big(S_{i}\neq S^{\prime}_{i},\,\,\,\text{for $0<i<n\,\,S_{n}=S^{\prime}_{n}=x$}\big)^{\gamma}\Big)^{k},

which is finite if condition (
3.14
) is satisfied.
∎Evans and Derrida in
[
ED92
]
provided numerical evidence that condition (
3.14
)
extends the
L^{2}
regime. There has also been further discussion in the physics literature

[
MG06a
,
MG06b
]
as to whether the Evans-Derrida condition provides a good estimate on

\beta_{c}
. This is still open but let us remark that just a crude use of fractional inequality
and Hölder without taking into account the correlations between the walk and the disorder
is known in several circumstances not to be sharp.
Below we outline another approach to the comparison between
\beta_{2}
and
\beta_{c}

that was put forward in
[
B04
,
BS10
,
BS11
,
BT10
]
.
This approach share some features to the approach of Evans-Derrida but it more refined.
In particular, it also considers the intersections of two independent random walks, with a reward
assigned each time the walks intersect, but, crucially, the framework is in a “quenched” setting.
This means that averaging is taking place with respect to only one of the two walks, while the other is
fixed and one is interested in determining the growth of a (certain) partition for a.e. trajectory of the fixed
random walk. Proceeding with such estimates, they use the full power of the refined moment method, which we will describe in Section
3.4
. Below, let us
outline the main framework of the approach of
[
B04
,
BS10
,
BS11
,
BT10
]
.

Random walk pinning model.

For a simple random walk
S
denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
the law on
\omega=(\omega_{n,x})_{n\geq 0,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
under which

(\omega_{n,x})_{n\geq 0,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
are independent and with a law for each individual
\omega_{n,x}
given by

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}_{S}(\omega_{n,x})=\exp\Big\{\beta\omega_{n,x}-\lambda(\beta))\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{n}=x\}}\Big\}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}(\omega_{n,x}).

In other words, the environment admits an exponential tilt on the sites which are visited by the
trajectory of the random walk
S
.
Note that we have already encountered this random walk - tilted measure in the proof of the monotonicity properties of the partition functions in Proposition
3.4
.
For a given random walk trajectory
S
,
we will also denote the realisation of the disorder sampled from (
3.16
) by

\omega^{S}:=(\omega^{S}_{n,x})_{n\geq 0,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
. The partition function in a disorder tilted along
S
, will be denoted by

\displaystyle W_{n}^{\beta,S}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega^{S}_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))}\Big]

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}\mathbb{1}_{\{S^{\prime}_{i}\neq S_{i}\}}+\beta\omega^{S}_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}\mathbb{1}_{\{S^{\prime}_{i}=S_{i}\}}-\lambda(\beta)\big)}\Big]

where in the right-hand-side the expectation is with respect to the random walk
S^{\prime}
, which is independent of
S
. We also used the fact that on sites
(i,x)
not visited by
S
, we have

\omega^{S}_{(i,x)}=\omega_{(i,x)}
.

For a random variable
X
with law
{\mathbb{P}}
and mean equal to
1
, we call the distribution
\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}_{X}:=X\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}
the
size-bias law
of
X
.
We will be able to relate the size-bias law of
W_{n}^{\beta}
to the law of
W_{n}^{\beta,S}
as the following simple computation shows:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[W_{n}^{\beta}f(W_{n}^{\beta})]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathrm{E}\big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))}\big]\,f(W_{n}^{\beta})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))}\big]\,f(W_{n}^{\beta})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\,f(W_{n}^{\beta,S})\big]\Big]

(3.18)

\displaystyle=:\mathrm{E}\otimes{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\,f(W_{n}^{\beta,S})\big].

The above identity can then be interpreted as that

the size-bias law of
W_{n}^{\beta}
is identical to the law of
W_{n}^{\beta,S}

under the measure
\mathrm{P}\otimes{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
.

The significance of this fact, with regards to weak disorder, is that it relates,
via choosing functions
f
that grow to infinity, the uniform integrability
of
W_{n}^{\beta}
under
{\mathbb{P}}
to the tightness of
W_{n}^{\beta,S}
under
\mathrm{P}\otimes{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
.
Using this fact, Birkner-Sun
[
BS10
,
BS11
]
were able to show that the weak disorder regime extends beyond the
L^{2}
regime in dimensions
d\geq 3
. A similar result was also derived
in
d=3
by Berger-Toninelli
[
BT10
]
(see also
[
JL24a
]
, Theorem B).
In order to state the result, we need to introduce the
random walk pinning
partition function:
Given a path of a simple random walk
S
, define

(3.19)

\displaystyle W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin}(S)}:=\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\big(\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{i}^{\prime}\}}}\Big].

The random walk pinning partition arises from
W_{n}^{\beta,S}
by integrating out the disorder, with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}

(again, the random walk trajectory
S
is fixed), as:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[W_{n}^{\beta,S}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{S}\Big[\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-\lambda(\beta)\big)}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\Big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}\mathbb{1}_{\{S^{\prime}_{i}\neq S_{i}\}}+\beta\omega^{S}_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}\mathbb{1}_{\{S^{\prime}_{i}=S_{i}\}}-\lambda(\beta)\big)}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\Big[e^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\beta\omega^{S}_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-\lambda(\beta)\big)\mathbb{1}_{\{S^{\prime}_{i}=S_{i}\}}}\Big]\Big]

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}^{S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\big(\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{i}^{\prime}\}}}\Big]=W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin}(S)},

where in the third equality we used that for sites
(i,S^{\prime}_{i})
, which are not visited by
S
, we
have that
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[e^{\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-\lambda(\beta)}\big]=1
, while in the fourth that
on sites
(i,S^{\prime}_{i})
, which are visited by
S
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[e^{(\beta\omega^{S}_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{S_{i}=S^{\prime}_{i}}}\big]={\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{(2\beta\omega_{i,S^{\prime}_{i}}-2\lambda(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S^{\prime}_{i}}\}}\big]=e^{(\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S^{\prime}_{i}\}}}
.

We note that averaging
W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin}(S)}
with respect to
S
gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}^{S}\big[W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin}(S)}\big]=\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\Big[e^{\big(\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)\big)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{i}^{\prime}\}}}\Big],

which coincides with
{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{2}]
.
Averaging with respect to some form of disorder is often called
annealing
and, therefore, the above indicates that
the annealed critical temperature of the random walk pinning partition
coincides with the
L^{2}
critical temperature
\beta_{2}
of
W_{n}^{\beta}
. The results of Birkner-Sun
[
BS10
]
for
d\geq 4
and of
[
BT10
,
BS11
]
for
d=3
are summarised in
the following theorem:

Theorem 3.13.

Let
d\geq 3
,
\beta_{2}
be the
L^{2}
critical temperature as in (
3.13
) and

(3.21)

\displaystyle\beta_{c}^{\rm RWpin}:=\sup\big\{\beta\colon\sup_{n\geq 1}W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin(S)}}<\infty,\quad\text{for $a.e.$ random walk $S$}\big\},

then
\beta_{2}<\beta_{c}^{\rm RWpin}
.

Given the discussion around (
3.2
), we have that
\beta_{c}^{\rm RWpin}\leq\beta_{c}
and the above result
shows that
\beta_{2}<\beta_{c}^{\rm RWpin}\leq\beta_{c}
, thus the weak disorder regime extends beyond the
L^{2}
regime.
The fact that the weak disorder regime extends beyond the
L^{2}
regime in dimensions
d\geq 5
was earlier established
by Birkner-Greven-den Hollander
[
BGH10
]
(see also
[
BGH23
]
),
but in a less quantitative form
and via different methods, based on large deviations.
The approaches of both Birkner-Sun and Berger-Toninelli relied on the fractional moment method. In fact, they were able to establish that
for a
\gamma\in(0,1)
it holds that

(3.22)

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{E}^{S}\big[(W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin}(S)})^{\gamma}\big]<\infty,\qquad\text{for a.e. realisation of $S$.}

Taking
f(x)=x^{\gamma}
in (
3.2
) and via a simple Jensen we obtain via (
3.2
) and (
3.2
)
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(W_{n}^{\beta}\big)^{1+\gamma}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[W_{n}^{\beta}\cdot\big(W_{n}^{\beta}\big)^{\gamma}\big]=\mathrm{E}^{S}\otimes{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\big(W_{n}^{\beta}\big)^{\gamma}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{E}^{S}\big[\big({\mathbb{E}}_{S}W_{n}^{\beta}\big)^{\gamma}\big]=\mathrm{E}^{S}\big[(W_{n}^{\beta,{\rm pin}(S)})^{\gamma}\big]<\infty

for
\beta<\beta_{c}^{\rm RWpin}
.
Therefore, the relation to the random walk pinning model already provides a regime of
\beta
beyond the
L^{2}
,
where
W_{n}^{\beta}
has moments strictly greater than
1
.

Birkner-Sun
[
BS10
,
BS11
]
were also able to give quantitative bounds on the gap between the random walk pinning critical temperature and the
L^{2}
one in the case where the
simple,
d
-dimensional random walks
S,S^{\prime}
are replaced by continuous,
d
-dimensional random walks
{\mathcal{S}}^{(1)},{\mathcal{S}}^{(\varrho)}

with jump intensities
1
and
\varrho>0
, respectively, and identical, symmetric and irreducible transition probabilities. In particular, if
\widetilde{\beta}_{c}^{\,\rm RWpin}
and
\widetilde{\beta}_{2}
are the
corresponding critical temperatures in this continuous setting, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}_{c}^{\,\rm RWpin}-\widetilde{\beta}_{2}\geq\begin{cases}a\varrho,&\qquad\text{if $d\geq 5$ and for some constant $a$},\\
a_{\delta}\varrho^{1+\delta},&\qquad\text{if $d=4$ and for any $\delta>0$ and suitable constant $a_{\delta}$},\\
e^{-a^{\prime}_{\delta}\varrho^{-\delta}},&\qquad\text{if $d=3$ and for any $\delta>2$ and suitable constant $a^{\prime}_{\delta}$}.\end{cases}

Question 3.

Can one obtain upper bounds matching the lower bounds ?

3.3.
Moment characterisation of the weak disorder regime

The understanding of the weak disorder regime beyond the
L^{2}
regime received substantial
boost recently via the very insightful works of Junk
[
J22a
,
J22b
,
J23
]
, Fukushima-Junk
[
FJ23
]
and
more recently Junk-Lacoin
[
JL24a
,
JL24b
]
.
What was first
shown in
[
J22a
]
was that in
d\geq 3
, for any
0<\beta<\beta_{c}
the partition function
W_{n}^{\beta}
admits
moments strictly greater than
1
, which are obviously strictly less than
2
if
\beta>\beta_{2}
.

Before going into details let us define:

(3.23)

\displaystyle p^{*}(\beta):=\sup\big\{p\geq 1\colon\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\big[(Z_{n}^{\beta})^{p}\big]<\infty\big\}.

The original result of Junk
[
J22a
]
was, in rough terms and under certain assumptions,
that for every
\beta<\beta_{c}
, we have
p^{*}(\beta)>1
. This result opened new paths in the
understanding of properties of the directed polymer model beyond the
L^{2}
regime. Some of these
have already been explored in
[
J22b
,
J23
,
JL24a
,
JL24b
]
but the understanding of the
moment properties of the partition function offers tools for further exploration.

The results in this section will be based on one of the following two assumptions.

Assumption 1 (upper-bounded).

Disorder
\omega
is upper bounded. That is, there exists
M>0
, such
{\mathbb{P}}(\omega_{i,x}>M)=0

for every
(i,x)\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Assumption 2 (controlled overshoot).

For all
\beta>0
, there exist
A_{1}=A_{1}(\beta)>1
and
C=C(\beta)>0
such that for all
A>A_{1}

(3.24)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{\beta\omega}\,\big|\,\omega>A\big]\leq Ce^{\beta A}.

Clearly, Assumption
2
implies Assumption
1
but some of the results in this
section currently require the stronger upper bound of Assumption
1
.
The significance of this assumption lies on imposing some control on the overshoot that
certain martingales can have when crossing a level. This plays a role in being able to
obtain certain
maximal inequalities
, i.e. relating to
\sup_{n}M_{n}
, of martingales. We will
come to this point later.

Some of the insights in the first works of Junk came from revisiting the martingale nature of

W_{n}^{\beta}
and drawing inspiration from classical (but forgotten) works from the
onset of martingale theory
[
G69
]
. In particular, the relations between moments
of martingales which possess a linear structure such as the one encountered in (
3.3
)
and the integrability of their supremum. These relations were then applied to reveal the links
between moments of
W_{n}^{\beta}
and integrability of
\sup_{n}W_{n}^{\beta}
, i.e. the understanding of
maximal inequalities
. Let us state
Junk’s theorem
[
J22a
]
:

Theorem 3.14.

Let
\beta
be such that weak disorder holds. Then the following hold:

(i)

{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sup_{n\geq 1}W_{n}^{\beta}\big]<\infty

(ii)

If Assumption
1
holds and
p^{*}(\beta)
is as defined in (
3.23
),
then
p^{*}(\beta)>1
.

We will present the proof of Theorem
3.14
under the more restrictive boundedness
Assumption
1
as originally done in
[
J22a
]
. The proof under the more general
Assumption
2
was provided in
[
FJ23
]
, to which we refer.
Before presenting the proof of the above theorem, let us state some properties of
p^{*}(\beta)

as these were presented and proved in
[
J23
]
. We note that the weak monotonicity part of
Property (ii) is a consequence of the
monotonicity proved in Proposition
3.4
. We will also
discuss the proof of Property (i) after the proof of the main theorem. For the proofs of Properties
(iii) and (iv) we refer to
[
J23
]
, Theorem 1.2.

Theorem 3.15 (Properties of
p^{*}(\beta)
).

Let Assumption
1
hold true and also assume weak disorder. The
L^{p}
-critical exponent
p^{*}(\beta)
has the
following properties:

(i)

it holds that
p^{*}(\beta)\geq 1+\frac{2}{d}
.

(ii)

the function
\beta\to p^{*}(\beta)
is strictly decreasing, if
{\mathbb{P}}
has finite support.

(iii)

if
\beta
is such that
p^{*}(\beta)\in(1+2/d,2]
, then the function
p^{*}(\cdot)

is right-continuous at
\beta
.

(iv)

if
\beta>\beta_{2}
, and
p^{*}(\beta)>1
, then
p^{*}(\cdot)
is left-continuous at
\beta
.

The value of
p^{*}(\beta_{c})
at the critical temperature
\beta_{c}
has been identified more recently in
[
JL24a
]
:

Theorem 3.16 (
[
JL24a
]
, Corollary 2.2).

Under Assumption
1
, it holds that in
d\geq 3
,

\displaystyle p^{*}(\beta_{c})=\lim_{\beta\uparrow\beta_{c}}p^{*}(\beta)=1+\frac{2}{d}.

The last theorem is a consequence of the results summarised in Theorem
3.15
and Theorem
3.10
. Indeed,
by the latter theorem we know that weak disorder holds at
\beta_{c}
and by (i) of Theorem
3.15

that
p^{*}(\beta_{c})\geq 1+\frac{2}{d}
. If

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[f\Big(\frac{M_{k+\ell}}{M_{k}}\Big)\,\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\Big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[f(M_{\ell})].

(i)

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}]<\infty.

The interval of

(iii)

if

(3.28)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(M^{*}_{\infty}>t)>\frac{1}{Kt},\qquad\text{for all $t>1$}.

(iv)

(i) The proof is simple and ingenious. It hinges upon cleverly
choosing a suitable concave test function that takes advantage of property

3.25

\displaystyle f_{\delta,\varepsilon}(x):=\Big(\delta\big(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-1\big)\Big)\wedge 1.

We notice that for all
x\geq 0

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{(\varepsilon,\infty)}\geq f_{\delta,\varepsilon}(x)\geq\mathbb{1}_{[(\delta^{-1}+1)\varepsilon,\infty)}(x)-\delta\mathbb{1}_{[0,\varepsilon)}(x),

hold. For
t>0

\tau:=\inf\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\colon M_{n}>t\}

On the even
\tau<\infty

M_{\tau}\geq t>0
and, thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(M_{n}>t\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq n,\frac{M_{n}}{M_{\tau}}>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{\frac{M_{n}}{M_{k}}>\varepsilon}\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\Big[f_{\delta,\varepsilon}\Big(\frac{M_{n}}{M_{k}}\Big)\Big|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[f_{\delta,\varepsilon}\big(M_{n-k}\big)\big]\Big]

(3.33)

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq n)\,\inf_{k}{\mathbb{E}}[f_{\delta,\varepsilon}\big(M_{k}\big)],

where in the second inequality we used (
3.31
) and in the third (
3.25

Using now the second inequality in (

\displaystyle\inf_{k}{\mathbb{E}}[f_{\delta,\varepsilon}\big(M_{k}\big)]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{E}}[\inf_{k}f_{\delta,\varepsilon}\big(M_{k}\big)]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbb{P}}(\inf_{k}M_{k}\geq(\delta^{-1}+1)\varepsilon))-\delta{\mathbb{P}}(\inf_{k}M_{k}<\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}{\mathbb{P}}(M_{\infty}>0)-\delta{\mathbb{P}}(M_{\infty}=0),

Given the assumption that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[f_{\delta,\varepsilon}(M_{k})\big]=:\eta>0.

Going back to (

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(M_{n}>t\varepsilon)\geq\eta{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq n)=\eta{\mathbb{P}}(M^{*}_{n}>t)

and the monotone convergence theorem.

(ii) For

and, thus,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq t^{p}+{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq n\}}]

\displaystyle\leq t^{p}+(Kt)^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{M_{n}}{M_{\tau}}\Big)^{p}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\Big]

\displaystyle=t^{p}+(Kt)^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\frac{M_{n}}{M_{\tau}}\Big)^{p}\,\Big|\,{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\Big]\,\Big]

\displaystyle\leq t^{p}+(Kt)^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau=k\}}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[\big(M_{n-k}\big)^{p}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq t^{p}+(Kt)^{p}\,{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq n)\,{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}]

\displaystyle\leq t^{p}+(Kt)^{p}\,{\mathbb{P}}(M^{*}_{\infty}>t)\,{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}]

where in the third inequality we used assumption (

and in the last the fact for

is a sub-martingale.
Since by Chebyshev inequality

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(M^{*}_{\infty}>t)\leq\frac{1}{t}{\mathbb{E}}[M^{*}_{\infty};M^{*}_{\infty}>t],

and
{\mathbb{E}}[M^{*}_{\infty}]<\infty
, we can choose, using monotone convergence,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(M^{*}_{\infty}>t)\leq\frac{1}{4K^{p}t}.

Inserting this into

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}]\leq t^{p}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}[M_{n}^{p}],

thus, implying that

for

The fact that the interval of

Let

and it remains to choose

(iii)
Let

(ii)

3.4.
Very strong disorder and the fractional moment method

In this section we will discuss the phenomenon of
very strong disorder
as this was presented in Definition
3.9
.
That is, the question of whether the partition function

W_{n}^{\beta}
decays exponentially fast at a rate given by the free energy

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}(\beta):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log W_{n}^{\beta}.

We talk about very strong disorder if
\mathsf{f}(\beta)<0
. As we already saw in
Theorem
3.8
, very strong disorder is significant as it translates to localisation
estimates.

Very strong disorder was first established by Comets-Vargas
[
CV06
]
in dimension
1
and for every
\beta>0
via computation of fractional moments, in spirit similar to what
we saw in the proof of Theorem
3.7
.
Lacoin
[
L10
]
proved that very strong disorder also holds in dimension 2 by
making use of an upgraded formulation of the fractional moment, which constitutes a method,
now known as
the fractional moment method
.
This method is very powerful, allowing for very precise estimate on free energies and
other critical point shifts and has found several applications.
Recently, it was shown in
[
JL24a
]
that strong disorder is equivalent to very strong disorder in any dimension. For this
result the fractional moment method was combined with martingale tools similar to what were used in Section
3.3

and motivated by those objectives.
Outlining the factional moment method is the main objective of this section.
Before doing so, let us mention the following theorem.

Theorem 3.21.

Very strong disorder holds in dimensions
1,2
for every
\beta>0
. Moreover, the
following asymptotics holds:

(i)

in dimension
1
, we have that

(3.41)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{f}(\beta)}{\beta^{4}}=-\frac{1}{6}.

(ii)

in dimension
2
, we have that

(3.42)

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\beta^{2}\log\mathsf{f}(\beta)=-\pi.

The above theorem is the summary and culmination of several works concerning dimensions
1,2
. Lacoin
[
L10
]

first proved very strong disorder in both dimensions
1
and
2
and obtained upper
bounds on the free energy of order
O(\beta^{4})
and
O(e^{-c/\beta^{4}})
, in
d=1,2
, respectively, using (and developing) the fractional moment method. Lower bounds were also obtained in
[
L10
]
.
In
d=1
and Gaussian disorder the lower bound was
of the form
O(\beta^{4})
and for
d=2
and general disorder it was of the form
O(e^{-c/\beta^{2}})
.
The lower bounds were obtained using the so-called second-to-first moment estimates and concentration.
The very precise asymptotic (
3.41
) was obtained by Nakashima in
[
N19
]

making use of another method, called the intermediate disorder scaling, which we will
discuss in Section
4
. Asymptotic (
3.42
) was obtained by Berger-Lacoin
in
[
BL17
]
, developing further the fractional moment method for the upper bounds
and performing sharp second-to-first moment estimates for the lower bound.

[
BL17
]
refined in the directed polymer setting
previous work of the authors
[
BL16
]
on the study of the critical point shift of another
important polymer model, called the
disordered pinning model
.
It is worth mentioning that the framework of the fractional moment method
was actually born out of the attempt to study the critical points of the disordered pinning model,
in a series of works which started from
[
T08
,
DGLT09
,
GLT11
]
.

In the following, we first outline the more standard fractional moment framework and afterwards we discuss the recent variation of Junk-Lacoin

[
JL24a
]
, which was used in order to establish Theorem
3.10
, that strong and very strong disorder are equivalent in any dimension.

Outline of the fractional moment method
.
The pillars of the fractional moment method are the following:

A.

Estimate fractional moments.

B.

Perform coarse graining by identifying a proper correlation length.

C.

Change of measure.

Let us remark that the change of measure in Step C will share features with the

path tilted measures
we have seen in the discussion of the Random Walk Pinning Model
in Section
3.2
as well as some crucial features with
the material that we will discuss in Section
4
.

The discussion that follows is restricted to dimensions
1
and
2
.

Let us start with the following elementary computation:

(3.43)

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}(\beta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}\log W_{n}^{\beta}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\gamma}\log{\mathbb{E}}\log(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n\gamma}\log\ {\mathbb{E}}(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma},

where first we trivially inserted a fractional power
\gamma\in(0,1)
before applying Jensen’s inequality.
The first crucial idea is to split, in a sense, the length
n
of the polymer into

correlations lengths
. In other words, we will need to identify a particular length scale
\ell=\ell_{\rm corr.}(\beta)

and split
n=m\ell
where
m
will be the number of correlation lengths inside horizon
n
.
The point then will be to identify the correlation length
\ell_{\rm corr.}
so that (roughly)

{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{\ell}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]<e^{-c}
, for some
c>0
and, therefore (roughly, again)

{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]\leq e^{-cm}
, which would then imply via (
3.43
)
that

(3.44)

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}(\beta)\leq-\frac{c}{\gamma\,\ell_{\rm corr.}(\beta)}.

Identifying the correlation length is important in order to obtain a sharp estimate on the free energy.
In
d=1
the correlation length turns out to be of order
\beta^{-4}
, while in dimension
d=2
it is of order
e^{-\pi/\beta^{2}}

[
L10
,
BL17
]
.

Estimating a fractional moment
\gamma
for
\gamma<1
offers, on the one hand,
a more convenient route than estimating the expected value of the logarithm. Moreover, fractional moments
provide sharper estimates as compared to moments of order
p\geq 1
as the latter are dominated by the atypical
high values of the partition function
W_{n}^{\beta}
.
The idea that allows to discount atypical fluctuations in the disorder is to

change the law
of the disorder at locations visited by the polymer path. This change of measure will
also facilitate estimating the fractional moment as then it will be the right moment to make use of Hölder inequality
(any attempt to use Hölder before a suitable change of measure is doomed to fail). However, we also do not want to change the measure
along all paths since this will have a high cost and here comes the idea of
coarse graining
, which is to change the
measure at only macroscopic blocks visited by the path. Let us start going through the details of this strategy.

For
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(for the considerations here
d
will be either
1
or
2
) let us define the spatial coarse-grained blocks

\displaystyle\Lambda_{y}:=y\sqrt{\ell}+\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\ell},\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\ell}\,\Big]^{d}\qquad\text{for $y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}$}.

The scale of the coarse-graining is diffusive in the correlation length
\ell=\ell_{\rm corr.}
(to keep notation simpler
we will drop the “corr.” and just use
\ell
).
Given a vector
{\mathcal{Y}}:=(y_{1},...,y_{m})\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{m}
define the event
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
on the random walk trajectory
S
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}:=\{S_{i\ell}\in\Lambda_{y_{i}},\text{for all $i=1,...m$}\}

In other words,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is the event that the trajectory of the random walk crosses boxes
\Lambda_{y_{i}}

at times
i\ell
for
i=1,...,m
. Let us decompose

(3.45)

\displaystyle W_{n}^{\beta}=\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{m}}\mathrm{E}\big[e^{H^{\omega}_{n}(S)}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}\}}\big]=:\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{m}}W^{\beta}_{n,{\mathcal{Y}}}

Using the inequality
(\sum_{i}a_{i})^{\gamma}\leq\sum_{i}a_{i}^{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
(a_{i})_{i\geq 1}
non-negative numbers,
we have

(3.46)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]\leq\sum_{{\mathcal{Y}}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{m}}{\mathbb{E}}[(W^{\beta}_{n,{\mathcal{Y}}})^{\gamma}]

The use of the elementary fractional inequality is the first technical advantage of working with powers
\gamma\in(0,1)
and we have already seen this in the proof of Theorem
3.7
.
Let us now define the space-time coarse grained blocks over which we will perform the
change of measure. For
R>0
, which will be taken large, we define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lambda}_{y}

\displaystyle:=y\sqrt{\ell}+(-R\sqrt{\ell},R\sqrt{\ell}\,]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{B}_{(n,x)}:=[(n-1)\ell,n\ell\,]\times\widetilde{\Lambda}_{y}

The choice of
R
large enough is in order to guarantee that, once the block is visited, the path will keep
moving, with high probability, within this block where disorder will be modified.

Next, come the details of how the change of measure is performed.
We will need to choose a random variable
\mathsf{X}(\omega)=\mathsf{X}(\{\omega_{n,x}\}_{(n,x)\in\mathsf{B}_{(1,0)}})
,
which depends locally only on the disorder inside box
\mathsf{B}_{(1,0)}
. The random variable

\mathsf{X}
will be chosen so that it has
mean zero and
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{X}^{2}]\leq 1
but the most important feature will be to capture the correlation structure of the interaction
of the polymer path with the environment, which leads to atypical fluctuations.
We will come to this point and the precise choice of
\mathsf{X}
later. For the moment, let us also introduce
the functions
\mathsf{X}_{i,y}:=\mathsf{X}\circ\theta_{(i\ell,y\sqrt{\ell})}
, where
\theta
is the shift operator,
as well as the functions

(3.47)

\displaystyle g_{i,y}(\omega)=e^{-K\,\mathbb{1}_{\{{\mathsf{X}}_{i,y}(\omega)\geq e^{K^{2}}\}}}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega):=\prod_{i=0}^{m}g_{i,y_{i}}(\omega)

where in the left
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is a generic lattice point and
y_{1},...,y_{m}
in the right are the elements of the
vector
{\mathcal{Y}}
. The constant
K>0
will be chosen large so that to penalise atypically large configurations
\mathsf{X}
.
We insert the function
g_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(\omega)
in the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]
and
now is the time to use Hölder to obtain

(3.48)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\big[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}\,g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)^{\gamma}\,g_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(\omega)^{-\gamma}\big]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\big[W_{n}^{\beta}\,g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)\big]^{\gamma}\,{\mathbb{E}}\big[g_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(\omega)^{-\gamma/(1-\gamma)}\big]^{1-\gamma}

Using that
\mathsf{X}
is chosen to satisfy
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{X}^{2}]\leq 1
, we have via Chebyshev that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[g_{{\mathcal{Y}}}(\omega)^{-\gamma/(1-\gamma)}\big]

\displaystyle\leq 1+e^{\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathsf{X}\geq e^{K^{2}})

(3.49)

\displaystyle\leq 1+e^{\tfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}K-2K^{2}}\leq 2,

where the last inequality is obtained by choosing
K
large enough.

Let us prepare for the more subtle analysis of the first term in (
3.48
).
We start by rewriting it as

(3.50)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[W_{n}^{\beta}\,g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)\big]=\mathrm{E}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}\,g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)\big]\,\mathbb{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\,\Big]=\mathrm{E}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\,g_{i,y_{i}}(\omega)\big]\,\big]\,\mathbb{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\,\Big],

where in the last we use again the measure
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
, which tilts
{\mathbb{P}}
by the factor
e^{H_{n}^{\beta}(S)}
as defined in (
3.16
).
We would now like to bring the product outside the expectation. This cannot be done as an identity
since the factors have Markovian dependence. However, we can make use of the Markov property of
S
at the entrance
points of block
\Lambda_{y_{i}}
and dominate by considering the maximum over these entrance points. In this
way, we can obtain the upper bound

(3.51)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[\,g_{i,y_{i}}(\omega)\big]\,\big]\,\mathbb{1}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\,\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{i-1}}}\mathrm{E}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{i,y_{i}}(\omega)\,\big]\mathbb{1}_{S_{i\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y_{i}}}\,\big|\,S_{(i-1)\ell}=x\Big]=\Big(\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{0,0}(\omega)\,\big]\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y_{1}}\}}\Big]\Big)^{m},

where in the last equality we used the shift invariance of
g_{i,y}(\omega)
.
Inserting bounds (
3.50
) and (
3.4
) into (
3.48
) and then into
(
3.46
) we obtain

(3.52)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]\leq\Big(2^{1-\gamma}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{0,0}(\omega)\,\big]\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y}\}}\Big]^{\gamma}\Big)^{m}

it only remains to show that, when
\ell
is suitably chosen, there is a constant
c>0

(3.53)

\displaystyle 2^{1-\gamma}\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{0,0}(\omega)\,\big]\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y}\}}\Big]^{\gamma}\leq e^{-c}.

which then implies the desired estimate (
3.44
).

The main estimate (
3.53
) is obtained in the following way.
First, since
g\leq 1
we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{|y|>A}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{0,0}(\omega)\,\big]\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y}\}}\Big]\leq\sum_{|y|>A}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{P}_{x}\big(S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y}\big)\leq\sum_{|y|>A}e^{-c|y|^{2}},

by standard random walk estimates.
The latter can be made small by choosing
A
large enough.
Choose such an
A
and fix it and then proceed with bounding
the remainder sum. Towards this task, we now drop the indicator
\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y}\}}

(as it does not offer any further help) and so

\displaystyle\sum_{|y|\leq A}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{0,0}(\omega)\,\big]\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{\ell}\,\in\,\Lambda_{y}\}}\Big]\leq A^{d}\max_{x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}}\mathrm{E}_{x}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\big[g_{0,0}(\omega)\,\big]\Big].

It turns out that with high probability with respect to the random walk
S
, the function
\mathsf{X}(\omega)
has high expectation and high concentration with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
. In other words, it turns out that for any
\delta>0
and
R>0
, there exists
\beta_{0}(\delta,K)
such that for
\beta\leq\beta_{0}
, uniformly in

x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}
, we have

(3.54)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{x}\big({\mathbb{E}}_{S}[\mathsf{X}]>2e^{K^{2}}\big)\geq 1-\delta,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{P}_{x}\big({\mathbb{V}}ar_{S}[\mathsf{X}]\ll e^{K^{2}}\big)\geq 1-\delta.

These conditions would then imply that with high probability with respect to the random and uniformly
over its starting point
x\in\Lambda_{y_{0}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{S}[g_{0,0}(\omega)]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-K}+{\mathbb{P}}_{S}\big(\mathsf{X}(\omega)\leq e^{K^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-K}+{\mathbb{P}}_{S}\big(\mathsf{X}(\omega)-{\mathbb{E}}_{S}[\mathsf{X}]\leq-e^{K^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-K}+{\mathbb{P}}_{S}\big(\big|\,\mathsf{X}(\omega)-{\mathbb{E}}_{S}[\mathsf{X}]\,|\geq e^{K^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=e^{-K}+e^{-K^{2}}\,{\mathbb{V}}ar_{S}[\mathsf{X}]

and the above can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
K
large enough. We now remain with the most crucial
task, which is how to choose
\mathsf{X}
in such a way that requirements
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{X}]=0
,

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{X}^{2}]\leq 1
and most crucially (
3.54
) are satisfied.

Choice of
\mathsf{X}
.
As we mentioned, the driving idea is to manage to discount configurations of the disorder
\omega

which cause atypical fluctuations in the partition function
W_{n}^{\beta}
. This is implemented via the function

g_{\mathcal{Y}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\exp\big(-K\mathbb{1}_{\{\mathsf{X}_{i,y_{i}}\geq e^{K^{2}}\}}\big)
and the computation of

{\mathbb{E}}\big[W_{n}^{\beta}g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)\big]
in (
3.50
).

Bearing in mind the coarse-graining that we performed above, it would seem natural to choose
\mathsf{X}
to actually be
the partition function
W^{\beta}_{\ell}
over a single correlation length
\ell
, itself. Then the function
g_{\mathcal{Y}}
would
penalise by a factor
e^{-K}
the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}\big[W_{n}^{\beta}g_{\mathcal{Y}}(\omega)\big]

each time the partition
W^{\beta}_{\ell}\circ\theta_{(i\ell,y_{i}\sqrt{\ell})}
takes exceptionally high values.
However, for such a choice, estimates (
3.54
) are not easy. What has proven useful is to consider, instead,
an approximation of
W^{\beta}_{\ell}
that carries substantial contributions to its total fluctuations. The suitable functional
has proven to be of the form

(3.55)

\displaystyle\mathsf{X}(\omega):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\rm Vol}(B_{1,0})}\,\mathsf{R}_{\ell}^{k/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{k}\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}\\
1\leq n_{0}<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq\ell\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}q_{n_{i}-n_{i-1}}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\,\omega_{n_{i},x_{i}},

(3.56)

\displaystyle\text{with}\quad\mathsf{R}_{\ell}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}q_{i}(x)^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{E}^{S,S^{\prime}}\big[\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{i}^{\prime}\}}\big]

and
\text{Vol}(B_{1,0})
denoting the Euclidean volume of
B_{1,0}
.
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}
is the expected collision time of
two independent simple random walks in a time horizon
\ell
, which by the local limit theorem is known to have the
asymptotic behaviour
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}\sim\sqrt{2\ell/\pi}
is
d=1
and
\mathsf{R}_{\ell}\sim\log\ell/\pi
in
d=2
.

Let us remark that the functional
\mathsf{X}
as defined in (
3.55
) is (essentially) the
k^{th}
term in the so called
chaos expansion

of the partition function, suitably normalised so that it satisfies
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathsf{X}^{2}]\leq 1
. We will discuss more about
chaos expansion and its significance in the analysis of the partition function in Section

4.3
(see Remark
4.9
) but, for the moment,
one can essentially think of
\mathsf{X}
as the expectation with respect to
\mathrm{E}
of
k^{th}
term in the Taylor expansion
of the exponential

\displaystyle e^{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(\beta\omega_{i,S_{i}}-\lambda(\beta))}=e^{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(\beta\omega_{i,x}-\lambda(\beta))\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=x\}}}.

normalised by
\sqrt{{\rm Vol}(B_{1,0})}\,\mathsf{R}_{\ell}^{k/2}
.
It turns out
[
L10
]
that in dimension one,
\mathsf{X}
carries a significant portion of the fluctuations of
W^{\beta}_{\ell}
even for
k=1
, which is eventually a sufficient choice.
However, in dimension two, which is the
critical dimension
, the main contribution is carried by terms that correspond to
k
that grows with
\ell
. We will also discuss this point in Remark
4.9
in the next section.
For a length scale

\ell=\ell(\beta)=\exp\big(\tfrac{1+\varepsilon}{\pi}\beta^{-2}\big)
, which is near the critical correlation length, corresponding to

\varepsilon=0
, it turns out to suffice to take
k=k_{\ell}:=(\log\log\ell)^{2}
and this choice was made in
[
BL17
]
.
To facilitate the estimates, Berger-Lacoin
[
BL17
]
chose, moreover, a tailored version of
\mathsf{X}
.
We denote it by
\mathsf{X}_{\rm BL}
and for

\ell_{\varepsilon}:=\ell^{1-\varepsilon^{2}}
it takes the form

\displaystyle\mathsf{X}_{\rm BL}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\rm Vol}(B_{1,0})}\,\mathsf{R}_{\ell_{\varepsilon}}^{k/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{0},x_{1},...,x_{k}\in\widetilde{\Lambda}_{0}\\
1\leq n_{0}<n_{1}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq\ell_{\varepsilon}\\
n_{j}-n_{j-1}\leq\ell_{\varepsilon}\,,\,j=1,...,k\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\hat{q}_{n_{i}-n_{i-1}}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\,\omega_{n_{i},x_{i}},

\displaystyle\text{with $\hat{q}_{n}(x):=q_{n}(x)\mathbb{1}_{\{|x|\leq\varrho(t)}$ and with $\varrho(t):=\min\big(\tfrac{t}{2},(\log t)\sqrt{t}\big)$}.

The fact that with high probability with respect to
S
, the mean
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}[\mathsf{X}_{\rm BL}]
is high, is a consequence of the fact that
choosing
R
large enough in the definition of
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{y}
, we guarantee that with high probability the random walk path

S
, which lands in
\widetilde{\Lambda}_{y}
will stay there for the whole time
\ell
. Thus, it will be moving inside a tilted
disorder environment, leading to a high value of
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}[\mathsf{X}_{\rm BL}]
. The computations are subtle
and we refer to
[
BL17
]
for the details.

Outline of the Junk-Lacoin variation
[
JL24a
]
.
The starting point in this variation is also to find a scale
n
such that
a fractional moment of
W_{n}^{\beta}
is small. In
[
JL24a
]
they require
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sqrt{W_{n}^{\beta}}\big]<(2n+1)^{-d}
.
A main difference with what we outlined above is at the change of measure required to obtain this estimate. Here the change of measure
is via the partition function
W_{n}^{\beta}
itself instead of a chaos approximation proxy. In particular, they consider the size-biased change of measure

\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{d}\omega)=W_{n}^{\beta}\mathrm{d}{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{d}\omega)
. Lemma 3.2 in
[
JL24a
]
shows that for any event
A
of the disorder
\omega
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\sqrt{W_{N}^{\beta}}\Big]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{P}}(A)}+\sqrt{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(A^{c})}.

The task then is reduced to finding an event
A
which has small probability with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}
but high probability with respect
to
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
. The intuition towards this is something we already discussed at the beginning of the previous change-of-measure outline,
which is to exploit events where the partition function has atypically large fluctuations. The event chosen in
[
JL24b
]
is (we use slightly
different notation)

\displaystyle A_{n,s}:=\Big\{\exists\,(m,y)\in\{1,...,n\}\times\{-n,...,n\}^{d}\colon\theta_{m,y}W_{s}^{\beta}\geq n^{4d}\Big\},

where
\theta
is the shift operator i.e.
\big(\theta_{m,y}\,\omega\big)_{n,x}=\omega_{n+m,x+y}
. It is then proved in Proposition 4.1
in
[
JL24a
]
that

there exist
C>0,n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\forall n\geq n_{0}
there exists
s=s_{n}\in[0,C\log n]
for which

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt{\mathbb{P}}(A_{n,s})\leq\frac{1}{8(2n+1)^{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{P}}(A_{n,s}^{c})\leq\frac{1}{8(2n+1)^{d}}

A further novelty in
[
JL24a
]
is the approach to establishing the existence of this event and its probability estimates. This uses
martingale, stochastic calculus techniques and overshoot probability estimates. We refer directly to
[
JL24a
]
for details.

4.
Intermediate disorder and phase transition in dimension
2

4.1.
The general framework.

We have so far seen that in dimensions
1
and
2
, for every
\beta>0
, strong disorder
takes place, which naturally suggests the absence of a weak disorder regime in these dimensions. However, this is not the full picture. As we will discuss in this section,
in dimension
2
a weak disorder phase can be exhibited but in order to observe it one
has to zoom into the critical temperature
\beta_{c}=0
in a very particular fashion,
which is named
intermediate disorder regime
. The precise zooming is related to the
choice of
\beta
as a function of the polymer length
n
as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}:=\frac{\hat{\beta}}{\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}},\qquad\text{with}\qquad\mathsf{R}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}q_{i}(x)^{2}=\frac{\log n}{\pi}(1+o(1)).

We note that
\mathsf{R}_{n}=\mathrm{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{i}=S^{\prime}_{i}\}}]
and, thus, it represents the
expected number of collisions of two independent simple random walks. A quantity
of this sort plays an important role in other disordered system models and often
goes by the name
“replica overlap”
. The fact that the replica overlap grows like
a slowly varying function is crucial towards seeing a weak-to-strong disorder phase transition.
The phase transition happens when changing the parameter
\hat{\beta}
. This phenomenon
was first observed in
[
CSZ17b
]
and is presented in the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1.

Let
d=2
and
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
be the partition function of the
2d
directed polymer at intermediate
disorder regime with
\beta_{n}=\hat{\beta}/\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}
as in (
4.1
).
We then have

(4.2)

W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}\xrightarrow[\,n\to\infty\,]{d}\begin{cases}\exp\big(\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}\,{\mathcal{N}}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}^{2}\,\big)&\text{if }\hat{\beta}<1\\
0&\text{if }\hat{\beta}\geq 1\end{cases}\,.

where the convergence is in distribution and
{\mathcal{N}}
is a standard normal variable and
\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}^{2}=\log(1-\hat{\beta}^{2})^{-1}
.

The transition between weak and strong disorder phase in the intermediate disorder regime happen at the
critical value
\hat{\beta}_{c}=1
. Below this,
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
converges in distribution to a
strictly positive random variable, while at and above it to
0
. A difference between the
weak and strong disorder, as this was defined earlier is that the limit
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
, is

in distribution
while earlier we were talking about
a.s.
limits.
Another very important difference between weak-to-strong disorder transition in
d\geq 3

and the transition observed in
d=2
is that the critical point
\hat{\beta}_{c}
is
explicitly determined as
\hat{\beta}_{c}=1
and moreover it coincides with the
L^{2}
critical
point. In fact, it is worth drawing a contrast to the moment characterisation described in Section
3.3
: in
d=2
and intermediate disorder regime,
all

moments exist for
\hat{\beta}<\hat{\beta}_{c}=1
. This has been proved in
[
CZ21
,
CZ23
,
LZ22
,
LZ23
]
. Furthermore, in
[
CZ23
]
it has been proved that
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}

has moments even of growing order, up to
C(\hat{\beta})\sqrt{\log n}
for a particular
constant
C(\hat{\beta})
. Such considerations are important towards understanding
of properties of the field of partition functions, indexed by their starting point, as
pointed in
[
CZ23
]
.

On the intermediate disorder regime and (marginal) disorder relevance.

The notion of intermediate disorder was first introduced by Alberts-Khanin-Quastel

[
AKQ14
]
for the one-dimensional polymer, with temperature scaling

\beta_{n}=\hat{\beta}n^{-1/4}
. There it was shown that in
d=1
and with such
intermediate disorder scaling,
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
converges in distribution to
the solution of the one-dimensional stochastic heat equation (SHE)

(4.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u+\sqrt{2}\hat{\beta}\xi u,\qquad t>0,x\in{\mathbb{R}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad u(0,x)\equiv 1,\quad\text{for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

with
\xi
being the space-time white. Contrary to what is observed in dimension two, in
dimension one there no phase transition of weak-to-strong disorder type, as for any
\hat{\beta}>0
the solution to the stochastic heat equation is strictly positive.
The fact that one sees a phase transition in dimension two is a consequence of the fact
that dimension two is a
critical dimension
in the sense of renormalisation.
To get an idea of what this means, consider the rescaled solution to the SHE

u_{\varepsilon}(t,x):=u(\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{2}},\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
in
general dimension
d\geq 1
. Using the scaling
invariance of the white noise,
u_{\varepsilon}
then solves the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\sqrt{2}\hat{\beta}\xi u,

and we see that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the noise term
\varepsilon^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\sqrt{2}\hat{\beta}\xi u
(formally) converges to
0
in
d\geq 3

(indication of weak disorder as the effect of noise is attenuated) and
it blows up when
d=1
(indication of strong disorder), while it remains invariant when

d=2
. The latter indicates that dimension two is critical and
whether disorder is relevant (strong disorder) or irrelevant (weak disorder)
cannot be concluded from a renormalisation procedure as under this transform
the noise term has neither a clearly dominant effect nor it is clearly negligible.
In statistical physics and renormalisation this situation is often termed

marginal disorder

[
H74
,
G11
]
. In a situation like this one has to look at the
system in a much finer detail, in order to draw conclusions, and this is the framework
that the two-dimensional polymer falls into, as also confirmed by Theorem
4.1

Theorem
4.1
was presented in
[
CSZ17b
]
as a particular case of
a more general framework, which concerned
marginally relevant disordered systems
,
with a time direction. Let us record a form of this result here

Theorem 4.2.

Let
Z_{n,\beta_{n}}^{\rm marginal}
be a partition function of a marginally relevant disordered system,
which admits an expansion as a multilinear polynomial of the form

(4.4)

\displaystyle Z_{n,\beta_{n}}^{\rm marginal}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\,\beta_{n}^{k}\,\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n\\
x_{0}=0,x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\,\,\prod_{j=1}^{k}p_{i_{j}-i_{j-1}}(x_{j}-x_{j-1})\,\,\xi_{i_{j},x_{j}},

where

(\xi_{i,x})_{i\in\mathbb{N},x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
is a collection of i.i.d. mean zero, variance one random variables with exponential moments
and the kernel
(p_{i}(x))_{i\in\mathbb{N},x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
satisfies a condition which can be recast as equivalent to marginal relevance:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}p_{i}(x)^{2}\quad\text{grows to infinity as a slowly varying function}.

Assume, also, that the kernel
q_{n}(x)
satisfies a type of local limit theorem, i.e.

(4.6)

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\left\{n^{\gamma}\,p_{n}(x)-g\big(\frac{x}{n^{a}}\big)\right\}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0\,,

for a sufficiently smooth density
g(\cdot)
and exponents
a,\gamma>0

Then, if
\beta_{n}:=\hat{\beta}/\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}
, it holds that

(4.7)

Z_{n,\beta_{n}}^{\rm marginal}\xrightarrow[\,n\to\infty\,]{d}\begin{cases}\exp\big(\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}{\mathcal{N}}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}^{2}\,\big)&\text{if }\hat{\beta}<1\\
0&\text{if }\hat{\beta}\geq 1\end{cases}\,.

where
{\mathcal{N}}
is a standard normal variable and
\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}^{2}=\log(1-\hat{\beta}^{2})^{-1}
.

We will refer to
[
CSZ17b
,
CSZ18+
]

for more discussion about this theorem and its relations
to disorder relevance and the so-called
Harris criterion
,
[
H74
]
.
Here, let us point that (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) imply the relation
\gamma>\frac{1+ad}{2}
, which is equivalent to the
scaling relation proposed by Harris
[
H74
]
.
Let us also mention that the above theorem encompasses, besides the two-dimensional
directed polymer model, a one-dimensional directed polymer where the underlying walk
is in the domain of attraction of the Cauchy distribution, the disordered pinning model
[
G11
]
and the two-dimensional stochastic heat equation. The extension to statistical
mechanics models which do not have a time directions (e.g. Parabolic Anderson model
or Ising type models) is an interesting open problem.

The extension to the two-dimensional KPZ equation has been accomplished in

[
CSZ20
,
G18
]

and there has also been a very important extension of this framework to a certain class of
seminlinear stochastic heat equations by Dunlap-Gu
[
DG22
]
and
Dunlap-Graham
[
DG23
]

where further phenomena have been revealed. We refer to the above works for details.

4.2.
Field correlations

In this subsection we want to outline the properties of the field
(W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(x))_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
, in analogy with what was presented for
d\geq 3
in
Theorem
3.20
. This is important as it reveals the correlation structure
of the polymer and how localisation can emerge. The following theorem was proved
in
[
CSZ17b
]
, Theorem 2.13, in greater generality than what is stated below, that includes fluctuations of a wider class of marginally relevant models.

Theorem 4.3 (Partition function of
2d
polymer as a field).

Let
(W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(x))_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
be the field of partition functions of the directed polymer in
d=2
, indexed by the starting point
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
Let
\beta_{n}
be chosen as

(4.8)

\displaystyle\beta_{n}:=\frac{\hat{\beta}}{\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathsf{R}_{n}:=\sum_{i\leq n\,,\,x\in Z^{2}}q_{i}(x)^{2}=\frac{\log n}{\pi}(1+o(1)).

Let also
\phi\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a test function. Then, for
\hat{\beta}<1
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(\phi):=\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}}{n}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\big(W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(x)-1\big)\phi\big(\tfrac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\big),

converges to a Gaussian variable with mean zero and variance

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\beta},\phi}=\frac{\hat{\beta}^{2}}{1-\hat{\beta}^{2}}\int_{R^{2}}\int_{R^{2}}\phi(x)K(x,y)\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\qquad\text{with}\qquad K(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2\pi t}e^{-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{2t}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The analogue of this theorem in the case of
(\log W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(x))_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
and
for the solution of the KPZ equation has been proved in
[
CSZ20
,
G18
]
.
The analogue for fields coming from solutions to semilinear stochastic her equation has
been achieved in
[
T22
]
.

We remark that the amplification in (
4.9
) by the factor
\sqrt{R_{n}}\approx\sqrt{\log n}
, indicates that for
\hat{\beta}<1
,
the polymer partition functions become asymptotically
uncorrelated. This is also an indication of a delocalisation of the polymer. Strong correlations
start to emerge at the critical temperature
\hat{\beta}_{c}=1
. This is the content of the
next theorem, proved in
[
CSZ23
]
, which essentially says that the polymer partition,
viewed as a random measure, has a non-trivial scaling limit (without need of renormalisation)
at a
critical window
around
\hat{\beta}_{c}=1
, more precisely for
\beta_{n}
such that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\sigma(\beta_{n})^{2}:=e^{\lambda(2\beta_{n})-2\lambda(\beta_{n})}-1=\frac{1}{\mathsf{R}_{n}}\Big(1+\frac{\vartheta+o(1)}{\log n}\Big),\qquad\text{for $\vartheta\in{\mathbb{R}}$}.

To formulate the result, recall the definition of the point-to-point partition function
(
3.1
), (
3.2
).
To deal with parity issues, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

we denote by
[\![x]\!]
the closest point
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}_{\rm even}:=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:z_{1}+z_{2}\mbox{ even}\}
;
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
we define the even approximation

[\![s]\!]:=2\,\lfloor s/2\rfloor\in\mathbb{Z}_{\rm even}:=2\mathbb{Z}
.
We then introduce the field

(4.11)

{\mathcal{W}}^{\beta_{n}}_{n}=\bigg(W^{\beta_{n}}_{n;\,s,t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y):=\frac{n}{4}\,W_{[\![ns]\!],[\![nt]\!]}^{\beta_{n},\,\omega}([\![\sqrt{n}x]\!],[\![\sqrt{n}y]\!])\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\bigg)_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}.

where
\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The rescaling
\frac{n}{4}
is just to take into account the Lebesque scalings
and, thus, it is not of a particular substance.
The next theorem is mainly proved in
[
CSZ23
]
; part (v) is proved in
[
CSZ23b
]
, while (iv)
in
[
CSZ24+
]
.

Theorem 4.4 (Critical 2d Stochastic Heat Flow).

Fix
\beta_{n}
in the critical window (
4.10
), for some
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}
.
As
n\to\infty
, the family of random measures

{\mathcal{W}}^{\beta_{n}}_{n}=(W^{\beta_{n}}_{n;\,s,t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}
defined in (
4.11
)
converges in the sense of measures to a unique limit

\mathscr{Z}^{\vartheta}=(\mathscr{Z}_{s,t}^{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}\,,

which we call the
Critical
2d
Stochastic Heat Flow
. This is a universal process, in that it does not depend on the law of the disorder
\omega
, and
has the following properties:

(i)

it is translation invariant in law:

(\mathscr{Z}_{s+\mathsf{a},t+\mathsf{a}}^{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}(x+\mathsf{b}),\mathrm{d}(y+\mathsf{b})))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm dist}}{{=}}(\mathscr{Z}_{s,t}^{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}\quad\ \forall\mathsf{a}\geq 0,\ \forall\mathsf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

(ii)

it satisfies the scaling covariant relation:

(4.12)

(\mathscr{Z}_{\mathsf{a}s,\mathsf{a}t}^{\vartheta}(\mathrm{d}(\sqrt{\mathsf{a}}x),\mathrm{d}(\sqrt{\mathsf{a}}y)))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm dist}}{{=}}(\mathsf{a}\,\mathscr{Z}_{s,t}^{\vartheta+\log\mathsf{a}}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y))_{0\leq s\leq t<\infty}\quad\ \forall\mathsf{a}>0\,.

(iii)

it is a log-correlated field, with covariance function as in (
4.2
) below,

(iv)

it is a.s. singular with respect to the Lebesque measure,

(v)

it is neither a Gaussian field nor an exponential of a Gaussian field,
i.e. it is not a Gaussian Multiplicative Chaos (GMC - see
[
RV14
]
for a review on this model).

The covariance function of the Stochastic Heat Flow is given by

\displaystyle\bbcov[\mathscr{Z}^{\vartheta}_{s,t}(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y),\mathscr{Z}^{\vartheta}_{s,t}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y^{\prime})]=\tfrac{1}{2}\,K_{t-s}^{\vartheta}(x,x^{\prime};y,y^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,,\qquad\text{with}

\displaystyle K_{t}^{\vartheta}(z,z^{\prime};w,w^{\prime})\,=\,\pi\,\iiiint\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<s<u<t\\
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\end{subarray}}\big\{g_{\frac{s}{2}}(a-z)\,g_{\frac{s}{2}}(a-z^{\prime})\big\}\,G_{\vartheta}(u-s)\,g_{\frac{u-s}{4}}(b-a)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\big\{g_{\frac{t-u}{2}}(w-b)\,g_{\frac{t-u}{2}}(w^{\prime}-b)\big\}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}a\,\mathrm{d}b,\,

\displaystyle G_{\vartheta}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{(\vartheta-\gamma)s}\,s\,t^{s-1}}{\Gamma(s+1)}\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\text{and $g_{t}(x)$ the two-dimensional heat kernel.}

The covariance kernel
K_{t-s}^{\vartheta}(x,x^{\prime};y,y^{\prime})

first appeared in the setting of the
2d
stochastic heat equation
[
BC98
]
.
Explicit but complicated expressions for the higher, mixed moments of the Critical 2d SHF also exist
[
GQT21
,
CSZ19
]
.
A point to be remarked is that the
k^{th}
moment grows too fast in
k
(current lower bounds are of the
form
e^{ck^{2}}

[
CSZ23b
]
,
while the conjectured growth is
e^{e^{k}}
) and, thus, cannot be used to determine the distributional
properties of the field.
A question of interest is:

Question 4.

Provide an axiomatic characterisation of the Critical 2d SHF. Study its fine properties as a random measure and
explore its universality scope.

4.3.
Some points of the methods.

The approach to Theorems
4.2
,
4.3
and
4.4

starts with a representation of the partition function in terms of an expansion in multilinear
polynomials of disorder. Such an expansion often goes by the name
chaos expansion

in stochastic analysis or
high temperature expansion
in statistical physics. Let us start describing it.
Denote by

(4.14)

\displaystyle\xi_{t,x}:=\sigma(\beta)^{-1}\big(e^{\beta\omega_{t,x}-\lambda(\beta)}-1\big)\quad\text{with}\quad\sigma(\beta):=\sqrt{e^{\lambda(2\beta)-2\lambda(\beta)}-1},

with
\sigma(\beta)
chosen so that
{\mathbb{V}}ar(\xi_{t,x})=1
.
Using the fact that

\displaystyle e^{(\beta\omega_{t,x}-\lambda(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{t}=x\}}}-1=\Big(e^{\beta\omega_{t,x}-\lambda(\beta)}-1\Big)\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{t}=x\}},

we can then write

\displaystyle\prod_{t=1}^{n}e^{(\beta\omega_{t,x}-\lambda(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{t}=x\}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\Big(1+\big(e^{(\beta\omega_{t,x}-\lambda(\beta))\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{t}=x\}}}-1\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{n}\Big(1+\sigma(\beta)\xi_{t,x}\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{t}=x\}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}<n\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\end{subarray}}\,\sigma(\beta)^{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\xi_{t_{i},x_{i}}\,\mathbb{1}_{\{S_{t_{i}}=x_{i}\}}.

Inserting this into the original expression for
W_{n}^{\beta}(x)
, we obtain its
chaos or high temperature expansion:

(4.15)

\begin{split}W_{n}^{\beta}(x)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sigma(\beta)^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{k}\leq n\\
x_{0}=x,\ x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\,\xi_{t_{i},x_{i}},\end{split}

The above expansion for
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
has the form of a multilinear polynomial.
This structure plays an important role in the approach as it allows for a robust limit theory.
In particular, it allows for the use of two powerful tools, the
Lindeberg principle
and the

Fourth Moment Theorem
. We will expose these below but first let us introduce the more
general form of a multilinear polynomial (a particular case of which is expansion (
4.15
)).

Definition 4.5 (Multilinear polynomials).

Let
S
be an index set and
\xi:=(\xi_{x}\colon x\in S)
be a family of i.i.d. random variables.
Consider also a kernel
\psi
defined as a real function on the subsets of
S
.
We define the multilinear polynomial
\Psi
associated to the family
\xi
and the kernel
\psi
, to be

\displaystyle\Psi(\xi):=\sum_{I\subset S,\,|I|<\infty}\psi(I)\prod_{x\in I}\xi_{x}.

We also define the degree of
\Psi
,
{\rm deg}(\Psi):=\max\{|I|\colon I\subset S\,\,\text{such that $\psi(I)\neq 0$}\}
.

We also need the important notion of
influence
, which roughly quantifies how much a function
of several random variables depends on any individual one of them.

Definition 4.6 (Influence).

Let
S
be an index set,
\xi=(\xi_{x}\colon x\in S)
be a family of i.i.d., real random variables and

f\colon\mathbb{R}^{S}\to\mathbb{R}
a function of
\xi
. The influence of the individual random variable
\xi_{x},x\in S

on
f
is defined as

\displaystyle{\rm Inf}_{x}(f):={\mathbb{E}}\big[{\mathbb{V}}ar(f(\xi))\,\big|\,\{\xi_{y}\}_{y\neq x}\,\big].

In the case that
f
is a multilinear polynomial
\Psi
with kernel
\psi
, it is easy to conclude that
the influence takes the form

\displaystyle{\rm Inf}_{x}(\Psi)={\mathbb{V}}ar\Big(\frac{\partial\Psi(\xi)}{\partial\xi_{x}}\Big)=\sum_{I\ni x}\psi(I)^{2}.

Controlling the influence allows to control how much the distribution of a multilinear polynomial

\Psi
will change, if the input changes from one family of random variables
\xi
to another one
\zeta
.
The relevant result is the Lindeberg principle.

Theorem 4.7 (Lindeberg principle).

Let
\Psi
a multilinear polynomial and
\xi=(\xi_{x}\colon x\in S)
and
\zeta=(\zeta_{x}\colon x\in S)

two independent families of i.i.d. random variables with mean
0
, variance
1
and uniformly
integrable second moments ( i.e.
\xi_{x}^{2}
and
\eta_{x}^{2}
are uniformly integrable).
Then for any function
f\in C_{b}^{3}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
and every
\varepsilon>0

there exist
C_{\varepsilon}
, depending on the sup-norm of the derivatives of
f
and the degree of
\Psi
,
{\rm deg}(\Psi)

(see Definition
4.5
), such that

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(\Psi(\xi))\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[f(\Psi(\zeta))\big]\big|\leq\varepsilon+C_{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\max_{x\in S}{\rm Inf}_{x}(\Psi)}.

Lindeberg principles have a long history. The philosophy was introduced by Lindeberg as direct way to
prove the central limit theorem. More recently this principle has found a wide range of applications.
The above theorem was established in
[
CSZ17a
]
, Theorem 2.6 extending relevant work from
[
MOO10
]
.
The significance of the result is that, if one has a sequence of multilinear functionals
\Psi_{n}
and
one can show that asymptotically the influence is negligible, i.e.
\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{x}{\rm Inf}_{x}(\Psi)\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
, then the asymptotic distribution of
\Psi_{n}(\xi)
is stable and, thus, one can replace

\xi
by another set of variables that have more convenient distribution to work with, e.g. normal variables.

We also need to record the following powerful theorem, which goes under the name
Fourth Moment Theorem
and identifies when asymptotic limits of multilinear polynomials are Gaussian.
The origins of the fourth moment theorem can be traced to
[
dJ87
,
dJ90
,
NP05
]
but there has
been a huge number of extensions and applications
(see also
[
CSZ17b
]
, Theorem 4.2 for bit more general setting than what is presented below).

Theorem 4.8 (Fourth moment theorem).

Let
\Psi_{n}
be a sequence of
multilinear polynomials on a family
\xi=(\xi_{x}\colon x\in S)
of i.i.d. random variables
with mean
0
. Assume also
\Psi_{n}
has variance
1
and the each variable
\xi_{x}
has asymptotically
negligible influence on
\Psi_{n}
.
Then the sequence
\Psi_{n}
converges in distribution to a
standard normal random variable
{\mathcal{N}}
if
{\mathbb{E}}\big[\Psi_{n}(\xi)^{4}\big]\to 3
, as
n\to\infty
.
If, moreover, the family
\xi
is a family of normal random variables, then this is an if-and-only-if statement.

Let us now use the above toolbox to give a sketch of the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
An alternative proof has been provided by
Caravenna-Cottini
[
CC22
]
and Cosco-Donadini
[
CD24
]
, who looked directly at
\log W_{N}^{\hat{\beta}}
and provided
approximations for which the classical Lindeberg central limit theorem could be applied.

Let us first discuss why the limit is
0
for
\hat{\beta}\geq 1
. This will follow by approximating the partition function at
critical value through its log-normal limit for
\hat{\beta}<1
and using the monotonicity of the fractional moments as this follows from Proposition
3.4
.
More precisely, let
\gamma\in(0,1)
. By the fact that for
\gamma\in(0,1)
the fractional moment
\beta\to{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\beta})^{\gamma}]
is decreasing as a function of
\beta
, we have that for

\beta_{n}=\hat{\beta}/\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}
with
\hat{\beta}<1
:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{1/\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}})^{\gamma}]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{\hat{\beta}/\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}})^{\gamma}]={\mathbb{E}}\big[e^{\gamma\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}{\mathcal{N}}-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}^{2}}\big]=e^{\tfrac{(\gamma-1)\gamma}{2}\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\beta}}},

where in the first equality we used the fact that
(W_{N}^{\beta_{n}})^{\gamma}
is uniformly integrable for

\gamma\in(0,1)
(since
{\mathbb{E}}\big[W_{N}^{\beta_{n}}\big]=1
) and, thus, we can interchange limits and expectations. The last equality is then an explicit computation of the moment generating function of the normal distribution. Letting now
\hat{\beta}\uparrow 1
and using the fact that
\sigma_{\hat{\beta}}\to\infty
as
\hat{\beta}\uparrow 1
, we obtain that
\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[(W_{n}^{1/\sqrt{\mathsf{R}_{n}}})^{\gamma})]=0
,
By monotonicity, we obtain that also the fractional moment of any
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
corresponding to
\hat{\beta}\geq 1
converges to
0
.
The convergence of the fractional moment to
0
implies that
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
tends to
0
in distribution for all
\beta_{n}
corresponding
to
\hat{\beta}\geq 1
.

Let us now sketch the log-normal convergence in the regime
\hat{\beta}<1
.
An important first observation has to do with the correct
time scale
upon which one observes a change in the
fluctuations. To determine this time scale, one may look at the partition function of a system of length
tn
for arbitrary
t>0
.
Computing the variance of
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
using the chaos expansion (
4.15
) and the orthogonality of its terms, one obtains that it is asymptotically independent of
t
when
n\to\infty
.
One can be easily convinced about this fact by looking, for example, at the variance of the first
term in the chaos expansion, which behaves as

\displaystyle\sigma(\beta_{n})^{2}\,\bbvar\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t_{1}\leq tn\\
x_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}q_{t_{1}}(x_{1})\xi_{t_{1},x_{1}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\beta}^{2}}{\mathsf{R}_{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t_{1}\leq tn\\
x_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\end{subarray}}q_{t_{1}}(x_{1})^{2}=\hat{\beta}^{2}\frac{R_{tn}}{R_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\hat{\beta}^{2},

which is independent of
t
, since
R_{n}
is a slowly varying function.
Moreover, a similar computation shows that the contribution to the fluctuations from disorder
\xi_{i,x}
sampled in the time interval

[tn,n]
is negligible, for any
t>0
fixed.
On the other hand, a similar computation shows a change in the fluctuations when at time scales

n^{t}
, with
t>0
. These facts dictate that the meaningful time scale is not
tn
but
n^{t}
and that the partition function

W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
essentially depends only on disorder
\xi_{i,x}
with
i/n\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.
We remark that this observation plays a crucial role in the study of asymptotic limits below the critical
temperature, not only for the directed polymer model but also for the other marginal models or stochastic
PDEs that fall in this framework
[
CSZ18+
,
CSZ20
,
G18
,
DG22
,
DG23
]
.

To quantify the observation on the time scale,
we decompose the summations over
t_{1},...,t_{k}
in the multilinear expansion (
4.15
), over intervals

t_{j}-t_{j-1}\in I_{i_{j}}
, with
I_{i_{j}}=\big(n^{\frac{i_{j}-1}{M}},n^{\frac{i_{j}}{M}}\big]
,
i_{j}\in\{1,...,M\}
with
M
being
a coarse graining parameter (which will eventually tend to infinity).
We can then rewrite the
k
-th term in the expansion (
4.15
) as

(4.16)

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\beta}^{k}}{M^{k/2}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1},...,i_{k}\leq M}\Theta^{n,M}_{i_{1},...,i_{k}}\qquad\text{where}

\displaystyle\Theta^{n,M}_{i_{1},...,i_{k}}:=\left(\frac{M}{\mathsf{R}_{N}}\right)^{k/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{j}-t_{j-1}\in\,I_{i_{j}}\,\,\text{for }\,j=1,...,k\\
x_{1},...,x_{k}\in Z^{d}\end{subarray}}\,\,\,\prod_{j=1}^{k}q_{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}(x_{j}-x_{j-1})\,\xi_{t_{j},x_{j}}.

For technical reasons, that should become obvious below, we are led to restrict the summation in (
4.16
) to the subset

\displaystyle i_{1},...,i_{k}\in\{1,...,M\}_{\sharp}\qquad\text{with}\qquad

\displaystyle\{1,...,M\}_{\sharp}:=\{\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{k})\colon|i_{j}-i_{j^{\prime}}|\geq 2,\,\,\text{for all $j\neq j^{\prime}$}\}.

It is not difficult to justify, via an
L^{2}({\mathbb{P}})
estimate, that this restriction has negligible error but we will omit the details.

We now observe that if an index
i_{j}
is a running maximum for the
k
-tuple
\boldsymbol{i}:=(i_{1},...,i_{k})
, i.e.
i_{j}>\max\{i_{1},...,i_{j-1}\}

then
\big(n^{\frac{i_{j}-1}{M}},n^{\frac{i_{j}}{M}}\big]\ni t_{j}\gg t_{r}\in\big(n^{\frac{i_{r}-1}{M}},n^{\frac{i_{r}}{M}}\big]
, for all

r<j
, when
n\to\infty
.
This is the point where we also use the restriction into
\{1,...,M\}_{\sharp}
, which imposes that indices

i_{j}
are well separated.
This implies that

q_{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}(x_{j}-x_{j-1})\approx q_{t_{j}}(x_{j})
for
t_{j}\in I_{i_{j}}
and
t_{j-1}\in I_{i_{j-1}}
, where the
drop out of the spatial term
x_{j-1}
makes use of the diffusive properties of the random walk.
Thus, we will decompose the sequence
\boldsymbol{i}:=(i_{1},...,i_{k})
according to its running maxima, i.e.

\boldsymbol{i}=(\boldsymbol{i}^{(1)},...,\boldsymbol{i}^{(\mathfrak{m})})
identified by the properties:

•

\boldsymbol{i}^{(r)}:=(i_{\ell_{r}},...,i_{\ell_{r+1}-1})
with

i_{1}=i_{\ell_{1}}<i_{\ell_{2}}<\cdots<i_{\ell_{\mathfrak{m}}}
being the successive running maxima,

•

i_{\ell_{r}}>i_{\ell_{r}+1},...,i_{\ell_{r+1}-1}
, for
r=1,...,\mathfrak{m}
.

It can then be shown
that (
4.16
) asymptotically factorizes for large
n
and takes the form

(4.17)

\displaystyle\frac{{\hat{\beta}}^{k}}{M^{\frac{k}{2}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{k}_{\sharp}}\Theta^{n;M}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{(1)}}\Theta^{n;M}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{(2)}}\cdots\Theta^{n;M}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{(\mathfrak{m})}}.

The heart of the matter is to show that all the
\Theta^{n;M}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{(j)}}
converge jointly,
when
n\to\infty
to standard normal variables. This is where we use the Fourth Moment Theorem

4.8
.
Assuming this, let us see how we can obtain the convergence to the log-normal distribution in (
4.7
) when
\hat{\beta}<1
.

We can start by replacing, using the Lindeberg Principle
4.7
,
the
\Theta^{N;M}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{(j)}}
variables in (
4.17
) by standard normals, which we denote by
\zeta_{\boldsymbol{i}^{(j)}}
. Recall that
\boldsymbol{i}^{(j)}=(i_{\ell_{j}},...,i_{\ell_{j+1}-1})
and since
i_{\ell_{j}},j\geq 1
are
the running maxima we have
i_{\ell_{j}}>i_{\ell_{j}+1},...,i_{\ell_{j+1}-1}
.
Then denoting by

\displaystyle\zeta_{r}(a):=\sum_{(a_{2},...,a_{r})\in\{1,...,a-1\}^{r-1}}\zeta_{(a,a_{2},...,a_{r})},

we have that
W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
is approximately (in the large
n
limit)

\displaystyle W_{n}^{\beta_{n}}\approx 1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{\hat{\beta}^{k}}{M^{\tfrac{k}{2}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell_{1}<\cdots<\ell_{m}\leq k\\
1\leq a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{m}\leq M\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\zeta_{\ell_{j+1}-\ell_{j}}(a_{j}),

where
m
denotes the number of running maxima in the sequence
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{k})
in (
4.16
) (thus determining the number of dominated sequences),

\ell_{1},...,\ell_{m}
denotes the location of the running maxima in
\boldsymbol{i}
and
a_{1},...,a_{m}
denote the values of
i_{\ell_{1}},...,i_{\ell_{m}}

Heuristic derivation of the phase diagram.
We can distinguish three mechanisms that
drive fluctuations, which we will detail. The terminology was coined in the very interesting paper
[
GDBR16
]
where we refer for conjectures, heuristics and numerics that go beyond what we present here.
We will discuss the mechanisms that drive fluctuations and give rise to the conjectured asymptotics
afterwards. The three regimes are:

I. Collective optimisation.

In this case, the polymer is attracted to narrow corridors where
the energy
H_{n}^{\beta}(S)
is large. In this case, KPZ and Airy process mechanisms prevail.

II. Elitist.

This mechanism emerges when heavy tails of the disorder start having a
distinguishing effect and the polymer is attracted to a relatively small (but still significant)
fraction of sites where disorder is exceptionally
high. In this situation energy starts being balanced by entropy.

III. Individual.

Heavy tails become dominant and the polymer is attracted to a few
(finite number of) sites where disorder takes large values.

Heuristics on the Collective regime.

Let us present the heuristic argument from
[
AKQ10
]
.
As already mentioned, this is the situation that no sites carry exceedingly large disorder and fluctuations
are driven by a collective optimisation of the energy. This is very difficult to track and one
resorts to the detailed information coming from one-dimensional KPZ asymptotics, which
provide the process limit

(5.4)

\displaystyle\Big\{\frac{\log Z_{n}^{\beta}(0,xn^{2/3})-n\mathsf{f}(\beta)}{c(\beta)n^{1/3}}\Big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\big\{{\rm Airy}_{2}(x)-x^{2}\big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}},

where the argument
(0,xn^{2/3})
denotes a starting point
0
and an end point
xn^{2/3}

and

is the Airy-2 process.
This asymptotic limit is largely open for polymer models at positive temperature, even in the integrable cases.
Currently, only the 1-point asymptotics have been established for the log-gamma and
O’Connell-Yor polymers
[
COSZ14
,
OC12
,
BCR13
]
and formal asymptotics only for the

[

]
(see also
[

]

Let us assume (

Then we can expect

\displaystyle\log Z_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(0,x)-\log Z_{n}^{\beta_{n}}(0,0)\approx\beta_{n}\,n^{1/3}\big({\rm Airy}_{2}\big(\frac{x}{n^{2/3}}\big)-{\rm Airy}_{2}(0)\big)-\frac{x^{2}}{n}.

We have looked at the difference

Taking advantage of the fact that the Airy process has locally the regularity of Brownian motion, we can
deduce that

\displaystyle\log Z_{n}^{\beta}(x)-\log Z_{n}^{\beta}(0)\approx\beta_{n}\,x^{1/2}-\frac{x^{2}}{n}=n^{-\gamma}x^{1/2}-\frac{x^{2}}{n}.

5.2.
Heavy tails and weak-to-strong disorder

In this section we report on an interesting phase transition for a variation of the directed polymer
model with disorder in the domain of attraction of stable laws. The result here is from the work of
Viveros
[
V21
]
. Similar phase transition was observed earlier in the case of the disordered pinning
model
[
L16
]
.

More precisely let us consider the partition function

(5.13)

\displaystyle\hat{Z}_{n}^{\beta}:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\beta\xi_{i,S_{i}}).

It is worth comparing
\hat{Z}_{n}^{\beta}
with (

) and the derivation there.
The assumptions on the disorder

(\xi_{i,x}\colon i\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d})
are that they form an i.i.d. field and that

(5.14)

\begin{split}\xi_{i,x}\geq-1,\quad{\mathbb{E}}[\xi_{i,x}]=0\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{P}}(\xi_{i,x}>u)\sim Cx^{-\gamma},\quad\text{for $\gamma\in(1,2)$}.\end{split}

The assumption that the tail exponent
\gamma
is strictly less than
2

components in a chaos decomposition (

) do not posses second moment.

The interesting phenomenon observed is that there is a critical value of the parameter

equal to

, below which there is no weak disorder phase (in the same sense as
we have encountered so far in Definition

) while a weak disorder phase exists for

. The relevant theorem from
[

]
is the following:

Theorem 5.6.

Assume (

) for the partition function
\hat{Z}_{n}^{\beta}
in (

and
\hat{\mathsf{f}}(\beta)
the

\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0}\frac{\log\hat{\mathsf{f}}(\beta)}{\log\beta}=\frac{2}{d}\frac{\gamma}{1+\frac{2}{d}-\gamma},

6.
polymers on more general graphs

In this section we will mainly give pointers to studies of the directed polymer model on graph structures
other than
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Most of the graphs that we will discuss have a built-in renormalisation structure,
which allows for more independence and, thus, more exact computations.

Directed polymer on trees.

We can consider the case of discrete,
d
-regular trees or continuous, Galton-Watson trees.
Tree structures can be thought of as a mean-filed limit of directed polymer on the lattice, when the
lattice dimension tends to infinity. One feature that trees have and which makes the study of polymers
easier is that two independent copies of the random walk will not meet again once they split and follow
different branches.

Directed polymers on trees were studied by Derrida-Spohn
[
DS88
]
and Chauvin-Rouault
[
CR88
]
.
For a
b
-ary tree, i.e. a tree where every vertex branches out to
b
subsequent vertices,
the precise critical value
\beta_{c}
has been determined as the solution to the equation

\beta\lambda^{\prime}(\beta)-\lambda(\beta)=\log b
, see
[
C16
]
, Section 4.3. Chapter 4 in
[
C16
]
also
discusses polymers on more general tree structures.

In the setting of continuous trees with binary branching at rate
\lambda
,
the partition function of the directed polymer is related to Branching Brownian motion.
The Laplace transform of the corresponding
partition function
Z^{\beta,{\rm branch}}_{t}(x)
,

u_{t}(x):={\mathbb{E}}\big[\exp\big(-e^{-\beta x}Z^{\beta}_{t}(x)\big)\big]
,
solves the Kolmogorov-Petrovsky-Piscounov (KPP) equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u+\lambda(u^{2}-u),\quad t>0,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\qquad u(0,x)=e^{-\beta x},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

The KPP equation admits travelling wave solutions and it turns out the speed of the wavefront is related to the
free energy of the polymer partition function. We refer to
[
DS88
]
for details.

Directed polymer on hierarchical lattice.

Hierarchical lattices present a graph structure which lies in between the solvable tree structure (where
independence is very pronounced) and the more complex
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
lattice structure. It was originally introduced in the physics literature as a model amenable to renormalisation analysis in spin systems

[
M75
,
K76
]
.
Hierarchical lattices are constructed recursively in the following way.
We start with two parameters: the branching parameter
b\in\{2,3,...\}
and the segmenting parameter
s\in\{2,3,...\}
. The
0^{th}
lattice
D_{0}^{b,s}
is just an edge connecting two vertices, say
A,B
.

D_{1}^{b,s}
is obtained by replacing the singe edge in
D_{0}^{b,s}
by
b
branches from
A
to
B

and then splitting each branch into
s
segments.
Assume we have constructed lattices
D_{n-1}^{b,s}
. The lattice
D_{n}^{b,s}
is constructed
by replacing each edge in
D_{n-1}^{b,s}
by a copy of
D_{1}^{b,s}
.
An example of a hierarchical lattice with
b=s=2
is shown in Figure

.
The partition function
W_{n}^{\beta}
is defined analogously to the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
case but now with the
polymer running from
A
to
B
on lattice

.

The study of directed polymer on hierarchical lattices was initiated in works of

[
CD89
]
and Derrida-Griffiths

]

[

and
(ii) a weak-to-strong phase transition takes place if
b>s
. The analogy between the relations between
hierarchical lattices with parameters

In a very interesting series of work by Clark
