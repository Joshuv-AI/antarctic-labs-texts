Piecewise Nonlinear Materials and Monotonicity Principle
Antonio Corbo Esposito 1 , Luisa Faella 1 , Vincenzo Mottola 1 , Gianpaolo Piscitelli 2 , Ravi Prakash 3 , Antonello Tamburrino 1,4

Abstract
. This paper deals with the Monotonicity Principle (MP) for nonlinear materials with piecewise growth exponent. The results obtained are relevant because they enable the use of a fast imaging method based on MP, applied to a wide class of problems with two or more materials, at least one of which is nonlinear. The treatment is very general and makes it possible to model a wide range of practical configurations such as Superconducting (SC), Perfect Electrical Conducting (PEC) or Perfect Electrical Insulating (PEI) materials. A key role is played by the average Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator, introduced in [ 17 ] , where the MP for a single type of nonlinearity was treated. Realistic numerical examples confirm the theoretical findings.

1.
Introduction

This contribution falls within the framework of a nonlinear generalization of the Calderon problem
[
4
,
12
,
13
]
. Specifically, it consists in retrieving the electrical conductivity
\sigma=\sigma(x,|\nabla u(x)|)
appearing in the nonlinear Laplace type equation:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\dive\Big(\sigma(x,|\nabla u(x)|)\nabla u(x)\Big)=0\ &\text{in }\Omega\\
u(x)=f(x)\qquad\ &\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
f
is the boundary data and
\Omega
is a region filled by an electrical conducting material. The nonlinear electrical conductivity
\sigma
in
\Omega
is retrieved tomographically from the knowledge of boundary data evaluated on
\partial\Omega
. Specifically, the electrical conductivity is retrieved from the knowledge of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator

\Lambda_{\sigma}:f(x)\ \mapsto\ \sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)|)\partial_{n}u^{f}(x),

that is the operator mapping the applied boundary potential
f
on
\partial\Omega
, into the electrical current density, entering the domain
\Omega
through its boundary
\partial\Omega
. As is well known, this is a strongly ill-posed inverse problem that, moreover, presents nonlinear constitutive relationships.

In the case of linear materials, i.e.
\sigma=\sigma(x)
, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) operator
\Lambda_{\sigma}
is of paramount importance for retrieving
\sigma
from boundary data. In this framework, a relevant Monotonicity Property was found by Gisser, Isaacson and Newell in
[
29
]
:

(1.2)

\sigma_{1}(x)\leq\sigma_{2}(x)\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left\langle{\Lambda}_{\sigma_{1}}f,f\right\rangle\leq\left\langle{\Lambda}_{\sigma_{2}}f,f\right\rangle\quad\forall f.

Then, Tamburrino and Rubinacci
[
62
,
63
]
exploited the negation of (
1.2
):

\exists f\ :\ \left\langle{\Lambda}_{\sigma_{1}}f,f\right\rangle\not\leq\left\langle{\Lambda}_{\sigma_{2}}f,f\right\rangle\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sigma_{1}(x)\not\leq\sigma_{2}(x)\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega

to develop a real-time imaging method, the so-called Monotonicity Principle Method (MPM), to solve inverse obstacle problems, where the aim is to reconstruct the shape of the support of anomalies in a given background, from boundary data.

The four features rendering the MPM highly suitable for solving inverse problems are: (i) the computational cost is negligible making the method suitable for online and real-time applications, (ii) the processing can be carried out in parallel, (iii) under proper assumptions, the MPM provides rigorous upper and lower bounds to the regions occupied by the unknown anomalies, even in the presence of noise and a finite number of measurements (see
[
68

[

62

[
2

20
,
30
,
34
]

The original content of the present contribution is twofold and consists in the treatment of a much larger class of materials. Specifically, we consider materials made up of different phases where, in each phase, the material has either a different growth exponent, or is a Perfect Electric Conductor (PEC), or is a Perfect Electric Insulator (PEI). 2.
A unified treatment for Nonlinear Inverse Problems

This section presents a general and unified framework for treating the steady ohmic conduction, i.e. problem (
1.1
), in the presence of materials comprising different phases. For the sake of simplicity we refer to materials comprising two different phases, however the treatment is general and can be extended to materials consisting of multiple phases.

We first introduce the basic notations followed by the formal statement of the problem and the required assumptions. Finally, we prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution.

2.1.
Notations

Throughout this paper,
\Omega
is the region occupied by the conducting material. We assume
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, to be an open bounded domain (i.e. an open and connected set) with Lipschitz continuous boundary.

Hereafter we assume that the first material occupies the open bounded set

, with a Lipschitz continuous boundary and made up of a finite number of connected components

We denote by

and by

Moreover, we set

L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega):=\{\theta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\ |\ \theta\geq c_{0}\ \text{a.e. in}\ \Omega,\ \text{for a positive constant}\ c_{0}\}.

Furthermore, the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is the closure set of

with respect to the

The applied boundary voltage

belongs to the abstract trace space

where

|u|_{B^{1-\frac{1}{p},p}(\partial\Omega)}=\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{||x-y||^{N-1+(1-\frac{1}{p})p}}dS(y)dS(x)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

see Definition 18.32, Definition 18.36 and Exercise 18.37 in

This guarantees the existence of a function in

whose trace is

[

For the sake of brevity, we denote this space by

Finally, we denote by

2.2.
Statement of the Problem

Our aim is to prove a Monotonicity Principle for two-phase materials operating in steady ohmic conduction.

In this regime, the electric field
\bf E
is expressed in terms of the electric scalar potential as
{\bf E}(x)=-\nabla u(x)
.

For a quasilinear material, Ohm’s law
{\bf J}(x)=\sigma(x,E(x)){\bf E}(x)
is expressed as

(2.1)

{\bf J}(x)=-\sigma(x,|\nabla u(x)|)\nabla u(x)\quad\text{in }\Omega,

where
\sigma
is the electrical conductivity, and
{\bf J}
is the electric current density. A quasilinear material reduces to a linear material when
\sigma
does not depend on the electric field, i.e.
\sigma=\sigma(x)
. For a PEC we have
\bf E=\bf 0
, i.e.
\nabla u=\bf 0
, whereas for a PEI we have
\bf J=\bf 0
, i.e.
\sigma(x,|\nabla u(x)|)\nabla u(x)=\bf 0
. It is worth noting that a PEC corresponds to
\sigma=+\infty

\sigma=0
.

We assume that the material in
B
has a
p-
growth (
1<p<+\infty
) and that the material in
A
either has a
q-
growth, or is a PEC, or is a PEI.

2.3.
The assumptions

The well-posedness of the forward problem in the sense of Hadamard is the minimal requirement to formulate the associated inverse problem. This is guaranteed by the following assumptions on
\sigma_{B}:B\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{A}:A\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
.

Firstly, we recall the definition of the Carathéodory function.

Definition 2.1
.

\sigma:\Omega\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function iff:

•

x\in\overline{\Omega}\mapsto\sigma(x,E)
is Lebesgue-measurable for every
E\in[0,+\infty)
,

•

E\in[0,+\infty)\mapsto\sigma(x,E)
is continuous for almost every
x\in\Omega
.

(P1)

\sigma_{B}
and
\sigma_{A}
are Carathéodory function.

(P2)

For fixed
1<p<+\infty
, there exists three positive constants
\underline{\sigma}\leq\overline{\sigma}
and
E_{0}

such that:

\begin{split}&\underline{\sigma}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{p-2}\leq\sigma_{B}(x,E)\leq\overline{\sigma}\left[1+\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{p-2}\right]\qquad\text{if}\ p\geq 2,\\
&\underline{\sigma}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{p-2}\leq\sigma_{B}(x,E)\leq\overline{\sigma}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{p-2}\qquad\qquad\quad\ \text{if}\ 1<p<2,\\
\end{split}

\text{for a.e.}\ x\in{\overline{B}}\ \text{and}\ \forall E\geq 0
.

(P3)

For fixed
1<p<+\infty
, there exists
\kappa>0
such that:

\begin{split}(\sigma_{B}(x,E_{2})&{\bf E}_{2}-\sigma_{B}(x,E_{1}){\bf E}_{1})\cdot({\bf E}_{2}-{\bf E}_{1})\\
&\geq\begin{cases}\kappa|{\bf E}_{2}-{\bf E}_{1}|^{p}\ &\text{if}\ p\geq 2\\
\kappa(1+|{\bf E}_{2}|^{2}+|{\bf E}_{1}|^{2})^{\frac{p-2}{2}}|{\bf E}_{2}-{\bf E}_{1}|^{2}\ &\text{if}\ 1<p<2\end{cases}\\
\end{split}

\text{for a.e.}\ x\in B
, and for any
{\bf E}_{1},{\bf E}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(Q1)

For fixed
1<q<+\infty
, there exists three positive constants
\underline{\sigma}\leq\overline{\sigma}
and
E_{0}

such that:

\begin{split}&\underline{\sigma}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{q-2}\leq\sigma_{A}(x,E)\leq\overline{\sigma}\left[1+\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{q-2}\right]\qquad\text{if}\ q\geq 2,\\

\text{for a.e.}\ x\in A\ \text{and}\ \forall E>0
.

(Q2)

For fixed
1<q<+\infty
, there exists a positive constant
\kappa

such that:

\begin{split}(\sigma_{A}(x,E_{2})&{\bf E}_{2}-\sigma_{A}(x,E_{1}){\bf E}_{1})\cdot({\bf E}_{2}-{\bf E}_{1})\\

\end{split}

\ \text{for a.e.}\ x\in A,

for any

,

Remark 2.3

Assumptions (P2) and (Q1) imply that

2.4.
The mathematical model

The electric scalar potential
u

solves the steady current problem (
1.1
), where
f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
. Problem (
1.1
) is meant in the weak sense, that is

\int_{\Omega}\sigma\left(x,|\nabla u(x)|\right)\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\ \text{d}x=0\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),

and
u
restricted to
B
belongs to
W^{1,p}(B)
, whereas
u
restricted to
A
belongs to
W^{1,q}(A)
. Moreover, the solution
u
, as a whole, is an element of the larger functional space
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W^{1,q}(\Omega)
. Hereafter, we will exploit (i) if
p\leq q
then
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W^{1,q}(\Omega)=W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, and (ii) if
p\geq q
then
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W^{1,q}(\Omega)=W^{1,q}(\Omega)
. The solution
u
satisfies the boundary condition in the sense that
u-f\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
and we write
u|_{\partial\Omega}=f
.

The solution
u
is variationally characterized as

(2.3)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W^{1,q}(\Omega)\\
u=f\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left(u\right).

In (
2.3
), the functional
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left(u\right)
is the Dirichlet Energy

(2.4)

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left(u\right)=\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}(x,|\nabla u(x)|)\ \text{d}x=\int_{B}Q_{B}(x,|\nabla u(x)|)\ \text{d}x+\int_{A}Q_{A}(x,|\nabla u(x)|)\ \text{d}x

where
Q_{B}
and
Q_{A}
are the (nonnegative) Dirichlet Energy densities in
B
and in
A
, respectively:

\displaystyle Q_{B}\left(x,E\right):=\int_{0}^{E}\sigma_{B}\left(x,\xi\right)\xi\text{d}\xi\quad\text{for a.e.}\ x\in B\ \text{and}\ \forall E\geq 0,

\displaystyle Q_{A}\left(x,E\right):=\int_{0}^{E}\sigma_{A}\left(x,\xi\right)\xi\text{d}\xi\quad\text{for a.e.}\ x\in A\ \text{and}\ \forall E\geq 0,

and
\sigma_{B}
and
\sigma_{A}
are the restriction of the electrical conductivity
\sigma
in
B
and
A
.

At this point let us stress that when
A=\emptyset
, we fall into the case treated in
[
17
]
for
p\geq 2
.

Moreover, if
\sigma_{A}(x,E)=0
, we have a PEI in
A
. The corresponding problem is

(2.5)

\begin{cases}\dive(\sigma_{B}(x,|\nabla v|)\nabla v)=0&\text{in }B\\
\sigma_{B}(x,|\nabla v|)\partial_{\nu}v=0&\text{on }\partial A\\
v\in W^{1,p}(B),\ v=f&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The solution
u
is variationally defined as the minimum of the following Dirichlet Energy

(2.6)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(B)\\
u=f\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u),

where

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u)=\int_{B}Q_{B}(x,|\nabla u(x)|)dx.

Similarly, if
\sigma_{A}(x,E)=+\infty
, we have a PEC in
A
. The corresponding problem is

(2.7)

\begin{cases}\dive(\sigma_{B}(x,|\nabla v|)\nabla v)=0&\text{in }B\\
|\nabla v|=0&\text{a.e. in }A\\
\int_{\partial A_{i}}\sigma(x,|\nabla v(x)|)\partial_{\nu}v(x)dS=0&i=1,...,M\\
v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega),\ v=f&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The solution
u
is variationally defined as the minimum of the following Dirichlet Energy

(2.8)

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
u=f\text{ on }\partial\Omega\\
|\nabla u|=0\text{ in }A\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u),

where

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u)=\int_{B}Q_{B}(x,|\nabla u(x)|)dx.

Let us stress that, since the solution in (
2.8
) assumes a constant value in
A
, then we can equivalently consider
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}
equal to (
2.9
) instead of (
2.8
).

Throughout the paper, we will use the following notation to denote the solutions of the previously described minimum problems.

Definition 2.6
.

We denote by
u^{g}
the unique solution arising from problems (
2.3
), (
2.6
) and (
2.8
), corresponding to the boundary data
g\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
.

It is worth noting that (i) if
u^{g}
solves problem (
2.3
), then it is in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W^{1,q}(\Omega)
, (ii) if
u^{g}
solves problem (
2.6
), then it is in
W^{1,p}(B)
and (iii) if
u^{g}
solves problem (
2.8
), then is in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
.

The proof of the existence and uniqueness of the solution for (
2.3
), (
2.6
) and (
2.8
) in its variational form, relies on standard methods of the Calculus of Variations, when the Dirichlet Energy Density presents the same growth exponent in any point of the domain
\Omega
. The case treated in this work is nonstandard, because the Dirichlet Energy Density presents different growth exponents in
B
and
A
and, hence, we provide a proof in the following.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
1<p,q<+\infty
,
f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
and
\sigma
satisfies (P1), (P2) and (P3).

•

If
A=\emptyset
, then there exists a unique solution of problem (
2.3
);

•

If (Q1)-(Q2) hold, then there exists a unique solution of problem (
2.3
);

•

If (R) hold, then there exists a unique solution of problem (
2.6
);

•

If (S) hold, then there exists a unique solution of problem (
2.8
).

Proof.

A simple integration of the l.h.s. of the assumption (P2) provides the coercitivity of
Q_{B}(x,\cdot)
a.e. in
B
.

Furthermore, if we consider two parallel vectors
\bf E_{1}
and
\bf E_{2}
in (P3), we easily deduce that
\sigma_{B}(x,E_{2}){E}_{2}-\sigma_{B}(x,E_{1}){E}_{1}>0
for any
E_{1},E_{2}>0
. This means that
E\in\ [0,+\infty)\mapsto\sigma_{B}(x,E)E
is strictly increasing for a.e.
x\in B
, that is
E\in\ [0,+\infty)\mapsto Q_{B}(\cdot,E)
is strictly convex a.e. in
B
.

When (Q1) holds, (
2.2
) implies that there exist
\underline{Q}>0
such that

Q_{\sigma}(x,E)\geq\underline{Q}\left(\frac{E}{E_{0}}\right)^{r}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega\text{ and }\forall E>0.

Hence, the function
Q_{\sigma}(x,\cdot)
is coercive a.e. in
\Omega
.

The assumption (Q2) implies that
E\in\ [0,+\infty)\mapsto Q_{A}(\cdot,E)
is strictly convex a.e. in
A
and hence
E\in\ [0,+\infty)\mapsto Q_{\sigma}(\cdot,E)
is strictly convex a.e. in
\Omega
.

Similarly, when (R) or (S) holds, the convexity of
Q_{B}
follows from assumption (P3) whereas the coercitivity follows from the left hand side of assumption (P2).

In all four cases, standard direct methods of the calculus of variations
[
19
, Th. 3.30]
provide the existence and uniqueness of the solution.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

The condition
f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
is a necessary assumption because it guarantees the existence of a function in
f+W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(f)<+\infty
(see
[
19
, Th. 3.30]
for details).

2.5.
The DtN operator.

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DtN) operator maps the Dirichlet data into the corresponding Neumann data:

\Lambda_{\sigma}:f\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)\mapsto\sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f}|)\ \partial_{n}u^{f}|_{\partial\Omega}\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)^{\prime},

where
X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)^{\prime}
is the dual space of
X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)
and
u^{f}
is the solution of (
1.1
). From the physical standpoint, the DtN operator maps the boundary electric scalar potential into the normal component of the electrical current density entering
\partial\Omega
(see
2.1
).

In weak form, the DtN operator is

(2.10)

\langle\Lambda_{\sigma}\left(f\right),\varphi\rangle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi(x){\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right)\partial_{{{n}}}u^{f}(x)\text{d}S\quad\forall\varphi\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega).

Furthermore, by testing the DtN operator (
2.10
) with the solution
u^{f}
of (
1.1
) and using a divergence Theorem, we obtain the ohmic power dissipated by the conducting material:

\langle\Lambda_{\sigma}\left(f\right),f\rangle=\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}(x,\nabla u^{f}(x)){|\nabla u^{f}}(x)|^{2}\ \text{d}x.

If
\varphi\neq f
in (
2.10
), we have the so-called

virtual power
product that plays an important role since it is equal to the Gâteaux derivative of the Dirichlet Energy, when evaluated at the solution
u^{f}
, as shown in Section
4
.

The injectivity of the DtN operator is guaranteed by the assumption of zero average of
f
.

3.
Convergence of the Scalar Potential w.r.t. the Boundary Data

The aim of this section is to prove a fundamental convergence result of the solutions with respect to the boundary data. Specifically, it is possible to prove that the operator
f\mapsto\nabla u^{f}
is continuous with respect to the boundary data
f
, along any arbitrary direction (see Proposition
3.2
). This generalizes the result of
[
17
, Lem. 3.1]
, which proved a convergence result for
p\geq 2
and
q=p
. Here we generalize these previous results to arbitrary
p
and
q
with
1<p,q<+\infty
.

Firstly, we prove the following Lemma that provides an upper bound to the norm of the solutions of problems (
2.3
), (
2.6
), (
2.8
), through the norm of the boundary data. The proof, based on well-established methods, is given explicitly because it refers to an atypical configuration.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
1<p,q<+\infty
,
f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
,
{\sigma}
satisfying (P1), (P2), (P3). Then, there exists
C=C(p,q,\Omega)
such that

(i)

If
A=\emptyset
, then

(3.1)

\begin{split}||\nabla u^{f}||_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C(1+||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}),\end{split}

where
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
is the minimizer of (
2.3
).

(ii)

If
{\sigma}
satisfies (QX), then

(3.2)

\begin{split}||\nabla u^{f}||_{L^{p}(B)},||\nabla u^{f}||^{\frac{p}{q}}_{L^{q}(A)}&\leq\left(||\nabla u^{f}||^{p}_{L^{p}(B)}+||\nabla u^{f}||^{q}_{L^{q}(A)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\\
&\leq C(1+||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}),\end{split}

where
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
is the minimizer of (
2.3
).

(iii)

If
{\sigma}
satisfies (R), then

(3.3)

||\nabla u^{f}||_{L^{p}(B)}\leq C(1+||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}),

where
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
is the minimizer of (
2.6
).

(iv)

If
{\sigma}
satisfies (S), then

(3.4)

||\nabla u^{f}||_{L^{p}(B)}\leq C(1+||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}),

where
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
is the minimizer of (
2.8
).

Proof.

We give the proof for each individual case.

\bullet

Claim (i)
.
Let us assume
p\geq 2
. Since
u^{f}
realizes the minimum in (
2.3
) with
A=\emptyset
, by using (P2), we have

(3.5)

\underline{c}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{p}\text{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}(x,|\nabla{u^{f}}(x)|)\text{d}x\leq\overline{c}\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla u(x)|^{2}+|\nabla u(x)|^{p}\right)\text{d}x

for any
u\in f+W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, and where the two constants
\overline{c}
and
\underline{c}
are defined as

(3.6)

\overline{c}=\frac{\overline{\sigma}}{pE_{0}^{p-2}},\ \underline{c}=\frac{\underline{\sigma}}{pE_{0}^{p-2}}.

The Inverse Trace inequality in Besov spaces
[
41
, Th. 18.34]
assures the existence of a function
w\in f+W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
||\nabla w||_{p}\leq K(p,\Omega)||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}
.
Therefore, we have

\begin{split}||\nabla u^{f}||^{p}_{p}\leq C_{1}(||\nabla w||^{2}_{2}+||\nabla w||^{p}_{p})\leq C_{1}(C_{2}||\nabla w||^{2}_{p}+||\nabla w||^{p}_{p})\\
\leq C_{1}(C_{2}+(C_{2}+1)||\nabla w||^{p}_{p})\leq C_{1}(C_{2}+(C_{2}+1)K^{p}_{p}||f||^{p}_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}).\end{split}

where in the first inequality we have used (
3.5
) with
u=w
and
C_{1}=\overline{c}/\underline{c}
; in the second inequality that, for
p\geq 2
there exists a constant
C
such that
||\nabla w||_{2}\leq C_{2}||\nabla w||_{p}
by the continuous embedding of the Sobolev spaces; in the third inequality that
||\nabla w||_{p}^{2}\leq 1+||\nabla w||_{p}^{p}
and in the fourth inequality the inverse trace inequality. Therefore, (
3.1
) follows by setting
C=C_{1}\max\{C_{2},(C_{2}+1)K_{p}^{p}\}
.

The inequality for
1<p<2
analogously follows (in easier way) using the second line of the assumption (P2).

\bullet

Claim (ii)
.
We have

\begin{split}\underline{c}\left(\int_{B}|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{p}dx+\int_{A}|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{q}dx\right)&\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u^{f})\\
&\hskip-142.26378pt\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u_{\infty}^{f})=\int_{B}Q_{B}(x,|\nabla{u^{f}_{\infty}}(x)|)\text{d}x\leq\overline{c}C\left(1+||f||^{p}_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\right),\end{split}

where
\overline{c}
and
\underline{c}
are the constants defined in (
3.6
) and
u_{\infty}^{f}
is the solution of the PEC problem
(
2.8
), the first inequality follows from assumption (P2)-left and (Q1)-left, the second inequality follows from using
u_{\infty}^{f}
as the test function, and the third inequality follows by using the argument of the previous case.

\bullet

Claim (iii)
.
Regarding the case where (R) holds, that is the PEI case, let us consider the solution
\hat{u}^{f}
of the problem
\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)\\
u=f\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\hat{Q}(x,|\nabla{u}(x)|)\text{d}x
, where

\hat{Q}(x,E)=\begin{cases}Q_{B}(x,E)\ &\text{on}\ B,\\
E^{p}\ &\text{on}\ A.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that there exist two positive constants
\underline{c}
and
\overline{c}
such that

\begin{split}\underline{c}\int_{B}|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{p}dx&\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(u^{f})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}(\hat{u}^{f})\leq\overline{c}\left(\int_{B}|\nabla\hat{u}^{f}(x)|^{p}\text{d}x+\int_{A}|\nabla\hat{u}^{f}(x)|^{p}\text{d}x\right)\\
&\leq C||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}.\end{split}

where in the first inequality we have used the l.h.s. of (P2), in the second inequality we have
\hat{u}^{f}
as the admissible test function, in the third inequality we have used the r.h.s of assumption (P2) and the last inequality follows as in Claim
(i)
.

\bullet

Claim (iv)
.
The conclusion for the PEC case follows, arguing as in Claim
(i)
.
∎

Lemma
3.1
underpins the following fundamental convergence result.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
1<p,q<+\infty
,
f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
and
{\sigma}
satisfying (P1), (P2), (P3).

(i)

If
A=\emptyset
, then

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}\ \text{in}\ {L^{p}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{as}\ {\varepsilon\to 0^{+}},\end{split}

for any
\varphi\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
, where
u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(resp.
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
) is the minimizer of (
2.3
) with boundary data
f+\varepsilon\varphi
(resp.
f
).

(ii)

If
{\sigma}
satisfies (Q1)-(Q2), then

(3.8)

\begin{split}&\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}\ \text{in}\ {L^{p}(B)}\qquad\text{as}\ {\varepsilon\to 0^{+}},\\
&\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}\ \text{in}\ {L^{q}(A)}\qquad\text{as}\ {\varepsilon\to 0^{+}},\\
&\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}\ \text{in}\ {L^{r}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{as}\ {\varepsilon\to 0^{+}},\end{split}

for any
\varphi\in(W^{1,p}(\Omega)\cup W^{1,q}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,p}(B)\cap W^{1,q}(A)
, where
r=\min\{p,q\}
and
u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(resp.
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
) is the minimizer of (
2.3
) with boundary data
f+\varepsilon\varphi
(resp.
f
).

(iii)

If
{\sigma}
satisfies (R), then

(3.9)

\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}\ \text{in}\ {L^{p}(B)}\qquad\text{as}\ {\varepsilon\to 0^{+}},

where
u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(resp.
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
) is the minimizer of (
2.6
) with boundary data
f+\varepsilon\varphi
(resp.
f
).

(iv)

If
{\sigma}
satisfies (S), then

(3.10)

\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}\ \text{in}\ {L^{p}(B)}\qquad\text{as}\ {\varepsilon\to 0^{+}},

where
u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(resp.
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
) is the minimizer of (
2.8
) with boundary data
f+\varepsilon\varphi
(resp.
f
).

Proof.

For any fixed
0<\varepsilon<1
, we denote by
I
and
II
the following quantities:

(3.11)

\displaystyle I:

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|\right){\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)}\cdot(\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\nabla u^{f}(x))\text{d}x,

(3.12)

\displaystyle II:

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right){\nabla u^{f}(x)}\cdot(\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\nabla u^{f}(x))\text{d}x.

\bullet

Claim (i)
. We distinguish the proof according to the value of the exponent
p
.

For
p\geq 2
, the divergence Theorem gives

(3.13)

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\text{d}x

(3.14)

\displaystyle II

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\text{d}x.

By subtracting (
3.14
) from (
3.13
), we have

\begin{split}I-II&=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}\nabla\varphi(x)\left[{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f}(x)\right]\ \text{d}x\\
&\leq\varepsilon D_{1}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi(x)|\left[\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|+\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|^{p-1}+\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|+\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|^{p-1}\right]\ \text{d}x,\\
\end{split}

where in the second line we have used (P2)-right with
D_{1}=\overline{\sigma}\max\left\{1,{E_{0}^{2-p}}\right\}
. Then, we have

(3.15)

\begin{split}I-II&\leq\varepsilon D_{1}\left[\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left|\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\left|\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\left.+\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left|\left|\nabla u^{f}\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\left|\left|\nabla u^{f}\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}\right]\\
&\leq\varepsilon CD_{1}\left[\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(1+\left|\left|f+\varepsilon\varphi\right|\right|_{X^{2}(\partial\Omega)})+\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}(1+||f+\varepsilon\varphi||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)})^{p-1}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\left.+\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(1+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{X^{2}(\partial\Omega)})+\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}(1+||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)})^{p-1}\right]\\
&\leq\varepsilon CD_{1}\bigg[\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(1+\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{X^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\left|\left|\varphi\right|\right|_{X^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\left.\left|\left|\nabla\varphi\right|\right|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\left(1+||f||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}+||\varphi||_{X^{p}(\partial\Omega)}\right)^{p-1}\right],\end{split}

where in the first inequality we used the Hölder inequality, in the second inequality we used (
3.1
) from Lemma
3.1
, in the third inequality we used the elementary properties of the norm and the fact that
\varepsilon<1
.

The quantity in the squared bracket of the last term in (
3.15
) is finite because
f,\varphi\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)
. Hence,
I-II
is upper bounded:

(3.16)

I-II\leq\varepsilon F,

where
F
is the product of
C
,
D_{1}
and the quantity in the squared brackets on the r.h.s. of (
3.15
).

On the other hand, by using (P3), we have:

(3.17)

||\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon g}-\nabla u^{f}||^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}(I-II).

By joining (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we have

||\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon g}-\nabla u^{f}||^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}(I-II)\leq\frac{F}{\kappa}\varepsilon.

This gives (
3.7
) by passing the to limit as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

If
1<p<2
, the inequality (
3.16
) follows analogously but with slightly different constants.

Regarding the lower bound, we have

(3.18)

\begin{split}&||\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}-\nabla u^{f}||^{p}_{p}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{\Omega}(1+|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon g}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{2})^{\frac{2-p}{4}}\\
&\qquad\qquad((\sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|)-\sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)|))(\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\nabla u^{f}(x)))^{\frac{p}{2}}\text{d}x\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\kappa}\left(\int_{\Omega}(1+|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{d}x\right)^{\frac{2-p}{2}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\left(\int_{\Omega}(\sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|)-\sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)|))(\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\nabla u^{f}(x))\text{d}x\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\\
&=\frac{G}{\kappa}(I-II)^{\frac{p}{2}},\end{split}

where in the first inequality we used the assumption (P3) raised to the power
p/2
, in the second inequality we used the Hölder inequality with exponents
2/p
and
2/(2-p)
, and
G=\left(\int_{\Omega}(1+|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla u^{f}(x)|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}\text{d}x\right)^{\frac{2-p}{2}}
is a finite quantity.

By taking into account (
3.16
) and (
3.18
), we have

||\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon g}-\nabla u^{f}||^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{G}{\kappa}(I-II)^{\frac{p}{2}}\leq\frac{GF^{\frac{p}{2}}}{\kappa}\varepsilon^{\frac{p}{2}},

which gives (
3.7
) by passing the to limit as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

\bullet

Claim (ii)
. First, we notice that the divergence Theorem gives (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), also in this case.
For

4.
The connection between the Dirichlet Energy and the DtN

The main aim of this Section is to prove that, in each of the four cases of nonlinearity we are interested in, there is a connection between the Dirichlet Energy and the DtN operator.

The DtN operator is of paramount relevance in inverse problems, because it can be measured from boundary data, unlike the Dirichlet Energy which is an integral quantity that requires the knowledge of the solution within the domain
\Omega
. On the other hand, the Dirichlet Energy is a relevant quantity because it satisfies a Monotonicity Principle, as shown in Section
5.2
.

The intimate connection between the Dirichlet Energy and the DtN operator is given by the the Gâteaux derivative. Specifically, it turns out that the Gâteaux derivative of the mapping
f\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\circ\mathbb{U}_{\sigma}
is equal to
\Lambda_{\sigma}
, where
\mathbb{U}_{\sigma}
is the operator mapping the boundary data
f
into the corresponding solution
u^{f}
:

\mathbb{U}_{\sigma}:f\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)\mapsto u^{f}\in W,

where
W
is a proper functional space (see Proposition
4.1
). In line with
[
17
]
, it is possible to prove the following Proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
1<p,q<+\infty
,
f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega)
and
{\sigma}
satisfying (P1), (P2), (P3). Then

\text{\emph{d}}(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\circ\mathbb{U_{\sigma}})(f)=\Lambda_{\sigma}(f).

Proof.

In the following we prove that

(4.1)

\text{\emph{d}}(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\circ\mathbb{U_{\sigma}})(f;\varphi)=\langle\Lambda_{\sigma}(f),\varphi\rangle\quad\forall\varphi\in W

where

(i)

if
A=\emptyset
, then
W=W^{1,p}(\Omega)
;

(ii)

if
{\sigma}
satisfies (Q1)-(Q2), then
W=W^{1,r}(\Omega)
where
r=\min\{p,q\}
;

(iii)

if
{\sigma}
satisfies (R), then
W=W^{1,p}(B)
;

(iv)

if
{\sigma}
satisfies (S), then
W=W^{1,p}(B)
.

Since the
\sigma(x,E)E
product is strictly increasing w.r.t.
E>0
and
\partial_{E}Q_{\sigma}(x,E)=\sigma(x,E)E
, we have

(4.2)

\begin{split}0\leq\sigma(x,E_{1})E_{1}(E_{2}-E_{1})&\leq Q_{\sigma}(x,E_{2})-Q_{\sigma}(x,E_{1})\leq\sigma(x,E_{2})E_{2}(E_{2}-E_{1}),\end{split}

for a.e.
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and for any
0<E_{1}\leq E_{2}
.

\bullet

Case (i)
. Let
u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
(resp.
u^{f}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
) be the minimizer of (
2.3
) corresponding to
f+\varepsilon\varphi
(resp.
f
).
For the sake of simplicity, we set

(4.3)

\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f):=(\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\circ\mathbb{U}_{\sigma})(f)=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left(u^{f}\right).

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we consider the following finite quantities

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)|)\text{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|\right)\text{d}x,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|\right)\text{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)+\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|\right)\text{d}x,

where we have used the minimality of the Dirichlet Energy on the solutions.

Let us consider the incremental ratio
\left[\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)\right]/\varepsilon
. From (
4.5
), we have

(4.6)

\frac{\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f}+\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)|\right)-Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)|\right)\text{d}x.

The magnitude of the integrand in (
4.6
) can be easily bounded from above. Indeed, from (
4.2
), we have

(4.7)

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla{u}(x)+\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)|\right)-Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u(x)|\right)}{\varepsilon}\right|\\
&\qquad\leq\frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\sigma\left(x,\left|\nabla{u}(x)|+|\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|\right)(\left|\nabla u(x)|+|\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|)|\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)|\\
&\qquad\leq\sigma\left(x,\left|\nabla u(x)|+|\nabla\varphi(x)\right|\right)(\left|\nabla u(x)|+|\nabla\varphi(x)\right|)|\nabla\varphi(x)|,\end{split}

for any
\varepsilon<1
. From the assumption (P2), the last term in (
4.7
) is a
L^{1}
function and hence, through the Lebesgue Dominate Convergence Theorem, we can pass to the limit in (
4.6
):

(4.8)

\begin{split}&\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)}{\varepsilon}\\
&\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)+\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)|\right)-Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f}(x)|\right)\text{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\ \text{\emph{d}}x.\end{split}

To prove that the limit of the incremental ratio on the l.h.s. of (
4.6
) exists, for
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, it is sufficient to prove that the
\liminf
is greater than or equal to the r.h.s. of (
4.8
), that is

(4.9)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)}{\varepsilon}\geq\int_{\Omega}{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)\ \text{\emph{d}}x.

Specifically, let
\{\varepsilon_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence such that
\varepsilon_{j}\to 0^{+}
and

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)}{\varepsilon}=\lim_{j\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon_{j}\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)}{\varepsilon_{j}}.

By using (
4.4
), we have

(4.10)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f+\varepsilon_{j}\varphi)-\mathbb{G}_{\sigma}(f)}{\varepsilon_{j}}&\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{j}}\int_{\Omega}Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon_{j}\varphi}(x)|\right)-Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon_{j}\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon_{j}\nabla\varphi(x)|\right)\text{d}x.\end{split}

To show that it is possible to pass to the limit, as
j\to+\infty
, in the r.h.s of (
4.10
), we prove that (i) the integrand is convergent and (ii) there exists a dominating summable function for the integrand. Then, the limit exists thanks to the Dominated Convergence Theorem.

In order to prove the pointwise convergence of the argument of the r.h.s. of (
4.10
), we observe that
Q_{\sigma}(x,\cdot)
is in
C^{1}([0,+\infty[)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and, therefore, the integrand of the r.h.s. of (
4.10
) can be written as

(4.11)

\begin{split}&\frac{Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|\right)-Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)|\right)}{\varepsilon}\\
&\qquad\qquad=\sigma\left(x,\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\right)\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\frac{\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|-\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|}{\varepsilon}\\
\end{split}

with

\begin{split}\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\in&[\min\{\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|\},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\max\{\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\right|,\left|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)\right|\}].\end{split}

The r.h.s. in (
4.11
) is equal to

(4.12)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\sigma\left(x,\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\right)\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\dfrac{2\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)-\varepsilon\left|\nabla\varphi(x)\right|^{2}}{|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|+|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon\nabla\varphi(x)|}\quad\text{if}\ |\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}(x)|\neq 0,\\
\sigma\left(x,\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\right)\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\sign(-\varepsilon)|\nabla\varphi(x)|\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Exploiting the fact that
\xi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\to|\nabla u^{f}(x)|
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
thanks to (
3.7
), we find that both expression appearing in (

) tend to
{\sigma}\left(x,\left|\nabla u^{f}(x)\right|\right)\nabla u^{f}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(x)
, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

To prove the existence of a summable dominating function for the integrand of (
4.10
), we recall from Proposition
3.2
(see the convergence result (

)), that
\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon\varphi}\to\nabla u^{f}
strongly in

By standard arguments, we can say that there exists a subsequence

a.e. in
\Omega

\psi

(4.13)

When

, for any
\varepsilon_{j_{h}}<1
, we have

(4.14)

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\varphi}(x)-\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\nabla\varphi(x)|\right)-Q_{\sigma}\left(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\varphi}(x)|\right)}{\varepsilon_{j_{h}}}\right|\\
&\leq\sigma(x,|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\varphi}(x)|+|\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\nabla\varphi(x)|)(|\nabla u^{f+\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\varphi}(x)|+|\varepsilon_{j_{h}}\nabla\varphi(x)|)|\nabla\varphi(x)|\\

5.
The Monotonicity Principle

Here we prove the Monotonicity Principle of the average-DtN, with respect to the electrical conductivity.

Specifically, first we demonstrate a Monotonicity Principle for the Dirichlet Energy (Theorem
5.2
)

\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}_{\sigma_{1}}(u_{1}^{f})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\sigma_{2}}(u_{2}^{f})\quad\forall f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega),

where
u_{i}^{f}
is the minimizer of the Dirichlet Energy with
\sigma=\sigma_{i}
, for
i=1,2
and
f
is the applied boundary voltage and we subsequently “translate” the Dirichlet Energy in terms of boundary data via the following fundamental relationship (see Theorem
5.3
)

(5.1)

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\left(u^{f}\right)=\left\langle\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}\left(f\right),f\right\rangle\quad\forall f\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega).

The operator
\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}
appearing in (
5.1
) is called Average DtN and is defined as

\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}:f\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}\Lambda_{\sigma}\left(\alpha f\right)\text{d}\alpha\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega)^{\prime},

where

\langle\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}(f),\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\Lambda_{\sigma}\left(\alpha f\right),\varphi\right\rangle\text{d}\alpha\quad\forall\varphi\in X^{p}_{\diamond}(\partial\Omega).

Operator
\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}
gives the average flow of the electrical current density through
\partial\Omega
for an applied boundary potential of the type
\alpha f
, for
\alpha\in\left[0,1\right]
. It is the key “tool” for transferring the Monotonicity Principle for the Dirichlet Energy to the boundary data and it replaces the usual DtN operator
\Lambda_{\sigma}
when treating nonlinear problems (see
[
17
]
for its very first introduction).

Finally, as a byproduct of Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
, we get the Monotonicity Principle for the operator
\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}
(see Theorem
5.4
), i.e.

(5.2)

\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left\langle\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma_{1}}\left(f\right),f\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma_{2}}\left(f\right),f\right\rangle\quad\forall f\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega).

In (
5.2
)
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}
means that

(5.3)

\sigma_{1}(x,E)\leq\sigma_{2}(x,E)\quad\text{for a.e.}\ x\in\overline{\Omega}\ \text{and}\ \forall\ E>0.

Furthermore, let us also observe that there exist four possibly different subsets
B_{i},A_{i}
of
\Omega
,
i=1,2
, such that
\sigma_{i}
is defined on
\Omega=B_{i}\cup A_{i}
,
i=1,2
, with the same growth exponent
p
on
B_{i}
and possibly different growth exponents
q_{i}
on
A_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Therefore, no inclusion relation is imposed between
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
but only the validity of relation (
5.3
).

Remark 5.1
.

It is worth noting that the operator

(5.4)

\Pi:\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega)\mapsto\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}

is monotonic in the sense of the MP stated in (
5.2
), where
\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}
the average DtN operator defined as in (
1.3
). On the hand, for a prescribed
\sigma
, the average DtN is a (non linear) monotonic operator in the classical sense stated in the books of Brezis
[

and Zeidler
[

]
, i.e.

(5.5)

\left\langle\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}\left(f_{1}\right)-\overline{\Lambda}_{\sigma}\left(f_{2}\right),f_{1}-f_{2}\right\rangle\geq 0\quad\forall f_{1},f_{2}\in X_{\diamond}^{p}(\partial\Omega).

7.
Conclusions

The contribution of this paper is focused on the Monotonicity Principle for nonlinear materials. Specifically, it covers the cases of (i) nonlinear materials with piecewise growth exponents and (ii) nonlinear materials with a growth exponent in
(1,2)
.

These results make it possible to extend a fast imaging method based on the Monotonicity Principle, to the wide class of problems with two or more materials, where at least one is nonlinear.

The treatment is very general and makes it possible to model a wide variety of practical configurations, such as Superconductors (SC), or Perfect Electrical Conductors(PEC), or Perfect Electrical Insulators (PEI).

The key results for proving the Monotonicity Principle in this setting are the convergence of the scalar potential with respect to the boundary data
f
(Proposition
3.1
), and
the Proposition
4.1
, which gives the connection between the Dirichlet Energy and the measured boundary data.

Numerical results related to a superconducting cable confirm the validity of the Monotonicity Principle.
