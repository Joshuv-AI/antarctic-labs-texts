From loom spaces to veering triangulations
Saul Schleimer, Henry Segerman

Abstract
. We introduce loom spaces , a generalisation of both the leaf spaces associated to pseudo-Anosov flows and the link spaces associated to veering triangulations. Following work of Guéritaud, we prove that there is a locally veering triangulation canonically associated to every loom space, and that the realisation of this triangulation is homeomorphic to ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} .

1.
Introduction

One-dimensional foliations, for example orbits of a flow, appeared early in the history of dynamical systems.
More delicate applications, such as foliations stable for, or transverse to, a flow, arrived in due course.
We refer to
[
17
]
for a readable and well-illustrated introduction to this area.

Suppose that
S
is a closed, connected, oriented surface.
Suppose that
f\colon S\to S
is a surface homeomorphism.
The
mapping torus
for
f
is the manifold
M(f)
obtained from
S\times[0,1]
by gluing, for every
x\in S
, the point
(x,1)
to the point
(f(x),0)
.
Then
M(f)
is equipped with a
suspension flow

\Phi(f)
along the intervals;
this flow has a transverse foliation given by the copies of
S
.
For an example in genus one, see Figure
2.18
.
Suspension flows associated to surface homeomorphisms are particularly important, for example due to the work of Thurston
[
29
, Theorem 5.6]
.

When
f
is (pseudo-)Anosov then we also have the
stable
and
unstable
(singular) foliations of
M(f)
associated to
\Phi(f)
;
the two-dimensional leaves of these foliations are obtained by taking suitable unions of flow-lines.
See Examples
2.15
and
2.17
;
for more detail we refer to
[
7
, Chapter 1]
.
Unfortunately, the
leaf space
of
\Phi(f)
is highly non-Hausdorff.
To obtain a somewhat calmer object, we define
\mathcal{L}(f)
to be the leaf space of the lift of
\Phi(f)
to the universal cover of
M(f)
.
Since
S
intersects every orbit of
\Phi(f)
, it follows that
\mathcal{L}(f)
is homeomorphic to
{\widetilde{S}}
, the universal cover of
S
.
Furthermore, the stable and unstable foliations for
\Phi(f)
induce singular transverse foliations
F^{f}
and
F_{f}
of
\mathcal{L}(f)
.

Following Agol and Guéritaud
[
1
,
16
]
, we form
S^{\circ}
by removing the singular points from
S
.
Let
f^{\circ}
be the restriction of
f
to
S^{\circ}
.
Thus
M(f^{\circ})
is obtained from
M(f)
by
drilling
.
The transverse foliations in
\mathcal{L}(f^{\circ})
are now non-singular.

The goal of this paper is to generalise leaf spaces to what we call
loom spaces
.
We provide axioms, discuss a number of examples, and draw out connections to
veering triangulations

[
1
]
.

1.2.
Future work

The functor
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}
given by Guéritaud’s construction is, in fact, an
equivalence
from
\operatorname{\mathsf{Loom}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

.
That is, there is a functor
\operatorname{\mathsf{L}}\colon\operatorname{\mathsf{Veer}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{Loom}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
so that the
\operatorname{\mathsf{L}}\circ\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}\circ\operatorname{\mathsf{L}}
admit natural transformations to the identities on
\operatorname{\mathsf{Loom}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{Veer}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

In joint work with Steven Frankel
[

]
, we will prove this by building, from a veering triangulation

of

\operatorname{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{V})
.
After proving that
\operatorname{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{V})
is a loom space we check naturality.

In other work, joint with Jason Manning, we will show how the veering circle for

compactifies the link space
\operatorname{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{V})
to give the

\operatorname{\mathsf{D}}(\mathcal{V})

We will then use the astroid lemma (Lemma

) to give a careful description of various Hausdorff limits in

.
Further work will prove, when

gives a finite-volume cusped hyperbolic three-manifold

, that the

is equivariently homeomorphic to the Bowditch boundary of

We will then use naturality to obtain new examples of Cannon-Thurston maps.

1.3.
Constructions of veering triangulations

The definition and first construction of veering triangulations in the fibred case are due to Agol
[
1
]
.
The second author and collaborators generalised the definition
[
18
]
; they also answered a question of Agol, using a computer search to find the first non-fibred examples.
Guéritaud
[
16
]
gave an alternative construction in the fibred case, which has inspired much later work, including this paper. We
[
26
]
announced a procedure to perform Dehn surgery along horizontal annuli or Möbius strips in veering triangulations.
We gave an implementation of a special case of this in the file
veering_dehn_surgery.py
in our codebase
[
25
]
.
Shortly afterwards, Agol and Guéritaud
[
2
]
announced an extension of Guéritaud’s construction to drillings of manifolds admitting pseudo-Anosov flows without perfect fits.

A computer-generated census of all transverse veering triangulations with up to 16 tetrahedra was found by Giannopolous and ourselves
[
15
]
.
Chi Cheuk Tsang
[
31
]
announced a procedure very similar to our veering Dehn surgery, which he calls
horizontal surgery
.
He also introduced
vertical surgery
along strictly ascending loops in the stable branched surface.
Landry, Minsky, and Taylor
[
22
]
gave an exposition of the Agol-Guéritaud construction.
Moreover, they proved that the veering triangulation can be made smoothly transverse to the pseudo-Anosov flow.

2.1.
Rectangles

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}

Remark 2.2
.

The foliations

and

have no singularities in

Thus, by the Poincaré–Hopf theorem
[
23

We deduce that every leaf is properly embedded in

.
Thus, by the Jordan curve theorem
[
32

\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Definition 2.3

A

We require that

sends intervals parallel to the

and sends intervals parallel to the

–axis to arcs of
F^{\mathcal{L}}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

With

as above, the rectangles give a basis for the topology of

. ∎

Remark 2.5

Since

When we do this, we also assume that all rectangle maps

2.10.
Loom spaces

We are now ready to state our main definition.

Definition 2.11
.

A
loom space

\mathcal{L}
is a copy of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
equipped with two transverse foliations
F^{\mathcal{L}}
and
F_{\mathcal{L}}
satisfying the following axioms.

(1)

For every cusp side
s
of every cusp rectangle, some initial open interval of
s
is contained in some rectangle.
(See Figure
2.13
.)

(2)

Every rectangle is contained in some tetrahedron rectangle. ∎

To explain the name
loom space
we recall that the warp and weft of a fabric, as produced by a loom, give a pair of transverse foliations.
See Figure
1.1
.

Definition 2.12
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
are loom spaces.
We say that
f\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{M}
is a
loom isomorphism
if

•

f
is a homeomorphism and

•

f
sends leaves to leaves. ∎

Note that a loom isomorphism

Lemma 2.30
.

•

Every tetrahedron rectangle contains exactly four face rectangles.

•

Every tetrahedron rectangle contains exactly six edge rectangles.

•

Every face rectangle contains exactly three edge rectangles.

Proof.

Suppose that

be the given parametrisation.
There are at most four face rectangles in
R
;
the sides of each intersect three of the four sides of
R
.

Let
(a,0)
and
(c,1)
be the missing points on the southern and northern sides.
Appealing to Lemma
2.25
and breaking symmetry, suppose that
a<c
.
Then

F=f_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{(x,y)\in(0,1)^{2}\mathbin{\mid}x>a\}}}\right)

is one of the desired face rectangles.
The remaining three are formed similarly.

The other two statements are proved similarly.
∎

Lemma 2.31
.

Every face rectangle is contained in exactly two tetrahedron rectangles.

Proof.

Breaking symmetry, suppose that
F
is a north-west face rectangle.
See Figure
5.13a
.
Let

be the northern side of
F
.
Suppose that
C
be a small rectangle contained in
F
;
suppose that the north and west sides of
C
are contained in north and west sides of
F
.
Let
\delta_{C}\subset\delta_{F}
be the northern side of

.

Since

we deduce that

is a cusp rectangle and that
\delta_{C}
is a cusp side.
By Definition
2.11
(
1
) we have that an initial segment of

is contained in a rectangle, say

.
Note that
\epsilon=\delta_{F}-D
is compact in
\mathcal{L}
.
Applying Lemma
2.4

We deduce that there is a rectangle

so that

contains both

and
\delta_{F}

We appeal to Definition
2.11
(
2
) to obtain a tetrahedron rectangle

containing

Repeating the argument with the western side of

containing

Note that

lies in the interior of

Remark 4.11

Loom spaces associated to pseudo-Anosov homeomorphisms have various natural non-complete euclidean metrics.
Each such metric has a definite injectivity radius;
the astroid lemma is immediate in these cases.

[

[

Loom spaces associated to pseudo-Anosov flows (without perfect fits) on finite volume hyperbolic three-manifolds need not have a natural choice of metric.
However, in this setting the action of the fundamental group still gives a local finiteness that can replace the lower bound on injectivity radius.
This, in slightly different language, is carried out in

is the figure-eight knot complement;
the taut triangulation
\mathcal{V}
lifts to give the one shown in Figure

Thus

∎

Locally veering triangulations are “almost” veering, in the following sense.

Proposition 5.7
.

Suppose that
M
is a three-manifold.
Suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is a taut ideal triangulation of
M
.
Suppose that
(M,\mathcal{V})
is locally veering.
Then
(M,\mathcal{V})
admits a veering structure if and only if
M
is orientable.

Proof.

Suppose that
(M,\mathcal{V})
admits a veering structure.
Then each model tetrahedron is veering, thus oriented.
These orientations disagree across faces (because of the colours of the edges).
Thus they define an orientation of
M
.

For the converse we fix an orientation of
M
.
This induces an orientation on all tetrahedra and thus on all model tetrahedra.
The taut structure picks four of the six model edges to be equatorial.
The orientation of the model tetrahedron and the axioms of a veering triangulation determines the colours of the equatorial model edges.
The hypothesis of local veering tells us that all equatorial models of a given edge have the same colour.
∎

We use
\operatorname{\mathsf{Veer}}
to denote the full subcategory of
\operatorname{\mathsf{Taut}}
consisting of locally veering triangulations.
We use
\operatorname{\mathsf{Veer}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
to denote the further full subcategory of those triangulations whose realisation is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

5.18.
Functorial

We summarise this section as follows.

Proposition 5.19
.

Guéritaud’s construction

\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}\colon\operatorname{\mathsf{Loom}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\operatorname{\mathsf{Veer}}

is a functor from the category of loom spaces to the category of locally veering triangulations.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{L}
is a loom space.
By Lemma
5.12
its induced triangulation
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})
is an ideal triangulation of a non-compact, connected, orientable three-manifold.
By Corollary
5.16
the induced dihedral angle makes
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})
into a taut triangulation.
By Corollary
5.17
we have that
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})
is locally veering.

Suppose now that
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are also loom spaces.
Suppose that
f\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{M}
and
g\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{N}
are loom isomorphisms.
Recall that we use the notations
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}_{f}=\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(f)
to represent the induced map.

Suppose that
P\subset\mathcal{L}
is a skeletal rectangle.
If
f=\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{L}}
then, appealing to Definition
5.10
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}_{f}(\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(P))=\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(f(P))=\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(P)
.
Thus
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal{L}})=\operatorname{Id}_{\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})}
, as desired.

For general loom isomorphisms
f
and
g
, and again appealing to Definition
5.10
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}_{g\circ f}(\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(P))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(g(f(P)))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}_{g}(\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(f(P)))

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}_{g}\circ\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}_{f})(\operatorname{\mathsf{c}}(P))

Thus
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(g\circ f)=\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(g)\circ\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(f)
.

Note that
f^{-1}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{L}
is a loom isomorphism;
thus
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(f^{-1})
is an inverse for
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(f)
.
We deduce that
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(f)
is a bijection.
Note that the triangulations
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{M})
are defined solely in terms of the combinatorics of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{M}
.
Thus the cell structures, angle assignments, and local veering structures are also preserved by
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(f)
.
∎

We deduce that
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}\colon\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{L})\to\operatorname{Aut}(\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L}))
is a group homomorphism.
In fact it is an isomorphism;
we do not prove this here.
See the discussion in Section
1.2
.

6.
Convexity

In this section we prove Theorem
6.50
: the realisation of the triangulation
\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Remark 6.1
.

One proof of Theorem
6.50
runs along the following lines.
Choose transverse measures of full support for
F^{\mathcal{L}}
and
F_{\mathcal{L}}
.
This gives
\mathcal{L}
an incomplete, locally euclidean, metric.
Suppose that
t\in\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})
is a model tetrahedron.
By sending its vertices to the associated cusps (in the completed metric),
and then extending to all of
t
via barycentric coordinates, we obtain a linear map from
t
to
\mathcal{L}
.
We glue these linear maps together to obtain a piecewise-linear map
\pi\colon|\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})|\to\mathcal{L}
.
We now claim the following.

•

The map
\pi
is continuous and surjective.

•

Point preimages under
\pi
are copies of
\mathbb{R}
.

•

The map
\pi
is a fibre bundle map.

The proof of surjectivity requires the astroid lemma (Lemma
4.10
(
1
)).
Since
\mathcal{L}
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
we deduce that
|\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})|
is isomorphic to a product.
Thus the claims imply Theorem
6.50
.

In their work, Landry, Minsky, and Taylor
[
22
, Proposition 5.11]
carry this strategy out but in a more delicate setting.
They begin with a pseudo-Anosov flow without perfect fits.
After drilling, the leaf space is a loom space, equipped with an action by the fundamental group of the drilled three-manifold.
Here one cannot simply choose measures since, in general, measures invariant under the action need not exist.
∎

In the remainder of Section
6
we give a very different proof.
We develop synthetic notions of geodesicity and convexity in
\mathcal{L}
.
We use these to prove that
|\operatorname{\mathsf{V}}(\mathcal{L})|
admits an exhaustion by three-balls.
The synthetic approach is longer than that of Remark
6.1
;
however it is constructive
and is more revealing of the structure of
\mathcal{L}
.
In future work we use this combinatorial structure to give an algorithm for drilling veering triangulations along flow loops.
