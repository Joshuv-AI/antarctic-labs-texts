On the twisted octonionic eigenvalue problem and some sextics hypersurfaces related to the Cartan cubic
Roland Abuaf

Abstract
We revisit the octonionic eigenvalue problem from a geometric perspective. In particular, we study a tautological sheaf defined on a sextic related to this problem, the Ogievetskiî-Dray-Manogue sextic. We then define and study a twisted version of the octonionic eigenvalue problem. A new sextic arises in this setting and we study the corresponding tautological sheaf supported on it. This twisted version of the octonionic eigenvalue problem is eminently more symmetric than the original one, as reflected by the last result we prove in this paper : the automorphism group of the twisted octonionic eigenvalue problem, though not isomorphic to E 6 \displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6} , acts prehomogeneously on the exceptional Jordan algebra J 3 ​ ( 𝕆 ) \displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}) . This is in sharp contrast with the fact that the generic orbit for the action of the automorphism group of the classical octonionic eigenvalue problem has (at least) codimension 6 \displaystyle 6 in J 3 ​ ( 𝕆 ) \displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}) .

\displaystyle U,V

\displaystyle X

&\ (a\otimes v,x)\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76228pt}(t_{U,V}(x)(a\otimes v),x).\end{split}

The algebra

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}

\displaystyle\mathbb{O}

is of the form:

\begin{split}t_{U,V}\ :&\ \mathbb{O}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\left\{U\cap V\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76228pt}}}\mathbb{O}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\left\{U\cap V\right\}\\

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle X

-equivariant.

It should be noted that the explicit equation of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
has been first found by Jonathan Hauenstein using the
Bertini
software. The embedding of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{24}
we consider does not factor through
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
. As a matter of fact, we haven’t been able to identify the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{24}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}(\mathbb{C})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
. We can however show the:

Theorem 3

Let
\displaystyle G
be the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{24}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}(\mathbb{C})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
. The action of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{*}\times G
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
is prehomogeneous.

Theorem
3
is slightly surprising as the subgroup
\displaystyle G
of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{24}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}(\mathbb{C})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
is
not

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
(and is even not a subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
). We will prove this fact in details in section 3.3, but less us have a glimpse at the argument. Any element of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
can be diagonalized under the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
. Hence, any vector of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
can be transformed, under the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
, to a vector whose corresponding
\displaystyle 24*24
matrix has rank
\displaystyle 0,8,16
or
\displaystyle 24
. On the other hand, the action of
\displaystyle G
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
preserves the rank. Since there are vectors in
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
whose corresponding
\displaystyle 24*24
matrix have rank
\displaystyle 22
, we conclude that
\displaystyle G
is not
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
. The prehomogeneous space
\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}),\mathbb{C}^{*}\times G)
does not appear in the list of Kimura-Sato
[
KS77
]
. This is not surprising as it is most likely not irreducible as a prohomogeneous space. It thus provides an interesting example of a non-irreducible prehomogeneous vector space which does not split as a sum of lower dimensional prehomogeneous vector spaces.

We prove Theorems
2
and
3
in the second section of this paper. There, we introduce a variant of the octonionic eigenvalue problem, which we call
the twisted octonionic eigenvalue problem
. We deduce the aforementioned results from both algebraic and geometric features of this twisted problem. We note that the geometry of the twisted octonionic eigenvalue problem differs strongly from that of the corresponding untwisted problem. Indeed, one easily notices that the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{GL}_{3}\times\mathrm{SO}_{7}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
is far from being homogeneous (the generic orbit has codimension at least
\displaystyle 6
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
). Theorem
3
thus shows that the twisted problem is eminently more symmetric than the untwisted problem.

The sheaf
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
appearing in Theorem
2
may be considered as a quasi-tautological sheaf on
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{O}}
as it is the cokernel of a matrix whose tautological interpretation is clear. However, the description of
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}|_{A}
for generic
\displaystyle A\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{O}}
is not completely obvious. Furthermore, the restriction of
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
to the smooth locus of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{O}}
is not a vector bundle, so that
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
is not the Fourier-Mukai transform, by the conormal diagram, of a vector bundle on
\displaystyle\mathbb{OP}^{2}
. One can however expect that it is the Fourier-Mukai transform of a rank
\displaystyle 4
coherent sheaf on
\displaystyle\mathbb{OP}^{2}
which could be conceived as quasi-tautological sheaf on
\displaystyle\mathbb{OP}^{2}
. The tautological interpretation of this sheaf is again not completely transparent. Further investigations should reveal many more fascinating features associated to the twisted octonionic eigenvalue problem. In a forthcoming paper with Jonathan Hauenstein
[
AH
]
, we will we study in more details the geometry and singularities of
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
.

2.1
The octonionic eigenvalue problem

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
be
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
or
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
(the algebra
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
being the
complexified
quaternions and octonions). The left (complex) eigenvalue problem for
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{A})
is the following. Given
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{A})
, what are the
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{C}
such that there exists a non zero vector
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{A}^{3}
with
\displaystyle A\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=\mu.\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}
? So that there is no confusion due to the non-commutativity (and possibly non associativity) of
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
, the equality
\displaystyle A\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=\mu.\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}
is meant to be:

\left\{\begin{split}\lambda_{1}x+cy+\overline{a}_{2}z&\ =\mu x\\
\overline{c}x+\lambda_{2}y+az&\ =\mu y\\
bx+\overline{a}y+\lambda_{2}z&\ =\mu z.\\
\end{split}\right.

Let us say that
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{A})
is
singular
or
degenerate
if
\displaystyle 0
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle A
. In case
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
is associative, the following proposition is well-known. We recall a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 2.1.1

Let
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
be an associative composition algebra which is finite dimensional over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
. Let
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{A})
and
\displaystyle\mu\in\mathbb{C}
. Then, there exists a non zero vector
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{A}^{3}
with
\displaystyle A\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=\mu.\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}
if and only if
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3})=0
.

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

If
\displaystyle A=0
the result is obvious. We now assume that
\displaystyle A\neq 0
, for instance
\displaystyle c\neq 0
(the non-vanishing of any other coefficient in
\displaystyle A
is dealt with exactly in the same way). Assume that
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3})=0
. If
\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(A-\mu.I_{3})\neq 0
then, the equation:

(A-\mu.I_{3})\times\mathrm{Com}(A-\mu.I_{3})=\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3}).I_{3}=0

shows that any non-zero column vector of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(A-\mu.I_{3})
is an eigenvector for
\displaystyle A
with respect to
\displaystyle\mu
. If
\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(A-\mu.I_{3})=0
, then one easily checks that
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{2}-\mu\\
-\overline{c}\\
0\end{pmatrix}
is a non-zero eigenvector for
\displaystyle A
with respect to
\displaystyle\mu
.

Assume on the other side that there exists
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\neq 0
such that
\displaystyle(A-\mu.I_{3})\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}=0
. Let
\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}x&x&x\\
y&y&y\\
z&z&z\end{pmatrix}
. We have
\displaystyle(A-\mu.I_{3})\times B=0
and since
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
is associative, we also have
\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(A-\mu.I_{3})\times(\mathbb{A}-\mu.I_{3})\times B=0
. But
\displaystyle\mathrm{Com}(A-\mu.I_{3})\times(A-\mu.I_{3})=\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3}).I_{3}
, so that we get:

\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3}).B=0.

The matrix
\displaystyle B
is non-zero by hypothesis and
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3})\in\mathbb{C}
imply that
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{A}}(A-\mu.I_{3})=0
.

\displaystyle\blacktriangleleft

The algebra
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
being not associative, the above result does not apply in this case. Nevertheless, Ogievetskiî found out a characteristic equation that determines the complex eigenvalues of
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
:

Theorem 2.1.2
(see
[
Ogi81
,
DM98
]
)

1.

Let
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
. The matrix
\displaystyle A
is singular if and only if the
\displaystyle 24*24
matrix
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
is singular.

2.

The degeneracy locus of the matrix
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
is given by the equation:

(\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A))^{2}-4\phi(c,b,a)\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)-\Big|[c,b,a]\Big|^{2}=0.

As we will build upon it when dealing with the twisted version of the octonionic eigenvalue problem, we recall the main steps of the proof from
[
Ogi81
]
:

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

The first assertion in the Theorem is obvious and follows from the definitions. We prove the second assertion. For
\displaystyle a,b,c\in\mathbb{O}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\displaystyle|a|,|b|,|c|,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\neq 0
and
\displaystyle|c|^{2}\lambda_{1}-\dfrac{|c|^{4}}{\lambda_{2}}\neq 0
, we define the following
\displaystyle 24*24
matrices in block form:

R_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{|c|^{2}}L_{c}&0_{8,8}&0_{8,8}\\
0_{8,8}&I_{8}&0_{8,8}\\
0_{8,8}&0_{8,8}&\frac{1}{|a|^{2}}{}^{t}L_{a}\end{pmatrix}\ \ \textrm{and}\ \ R_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{8}&\frac{|c|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}&0_{8,8}\\
0_{8,8}&I_{8}&0_{8,8}\\
\frac{1}{\delta}B&\frac{|a|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}I_{8}&I_{8}\end{pmatrix},

where
\displaystyle B=L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}-\dfrac{|a|^{2}|c|^{2}}{\lambda_{2}}.I_{8}
,
\displaystyle\delta=|c|^{2}\lambda_{1}-\dfrac{|c|^{4}}{\lambda_{2}}
,
\displaystyle 0_{8,8}
is the zero
\displaystyle 8*8
matrix and
\displaystyle I_{8}
the identity matrix of size
\displaystyle 8
.
One easily checks that:

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}=R_{1}R_{2}\begin{pmatrix}\delta.I_{8}&0_{8,8}&0_{8,8}\\
0_{8,8}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&0_{8,8}\\
0_{8,8}&0_{8,8}&\nu.I_{8}-\dfrac{1}{\delta}B{}^{t}B\end{pmatrix}{}^{t}R_{2}{}^{t}R_{1},

where
\displaystyle\nu=\lambda_{3}|a|^{2}-\frac{|a|^{4}}{\lambda_{2}}
. Since
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}(R_{1})=\dfrac{1}{|a|^{8}|c|^{8}}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}(R_{2})=1
, we get:

\begin{split}\mathrm{det}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}&=\dfrac{1}{|a|^{16}|c|^{16}}\mathrm{det}\left(\delta\nu\lambda_{2}.I_{8}-\lambda_{2}.B{}^{t}B\right)\\
&=\mathrm{det}\left((\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{1}|a|^{2}-\lambda_{2}|b|^{2}-\lambda_{3}|c|^{2}).I_{8}+(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})\right)\\
&=\mathrm{det}(-(2\mathrm{Re}(cab)-\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)).I_{8}+(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})),\end{split}

where
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

The expression
\displaystyle\mathrm{det}(-(2\mathrm{Re}(cab)-\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)).I_{8}+(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a}))
has no denominators so that the above equality holds for all
\displaystyle a,b,c\in\mathbb{O}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{C}
. We deduce the following:

The determinant of
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
vanishes if and only if
\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Re}(cab)-\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)
is a (complex) eigenvalue of
\displaystyle(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})
. We shall now determine the (complex) eigenvalues of
\displaystyle(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})
. This means we are looking for the
\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
such that:

\exists x\in\mathbb{O},\ x\neq 0\ \ a.(b.(c.x))+\overline{c}.(\overline{b}.(\overline{a}.x))=\kappa.x

(4)

Let us consider a four dimensional quaternionic subalgebra of
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
which contains
\displaystyle a
and
\displaystyle c
(its existence is granted by a Theorem of Hurwitz). Let
\displaystyle e\in\mathbb{O}
be a vector orthogonal to
\displaystyle M
such that
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}=M\oplus M.e
. We write
\displaystyle b=b_{0}+b_{1}.e
and
\displaystyle x=x_{0}+x_{1}.e
, with
\displaystyle b_{0},b_{1},x_{0},x_{1}\in M
. Using the relations:

u.(v.e)=(v.u).e,\ (u.e).(v.e)=-\overline{v}.u,\ u.e=e.\overline{u}\ \textrm{and}\ (u.e).v=(u.\overline{v}).e,

valid for all
\displaystyle(u,v)\in M^{2}
, we find that equation
4
is equivalent to the system:

\left\{\begin{split}2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0}).x_{0}+(\overline{c}.a-a.\overline{c}).\overline{x_{1}}.b_{1}&=\kappa.x_{0}\\
(\overline{a}.c-c.\overline{a}).x_{0}.\overline{b_{1}}+2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a).\overline{x_{1}}&=\kappa.\overline{x_{1}}.\end{split}\right.

This system can be written in matrix form:

\begin{pmatrix}(2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})-\kappa).I_{4}&({}^{t}L_{c}L_{a}-L_{a}{}^{t}L_{c})R_{b_{1}}\\
({}^{t}L_{a}L_{c}-L_{c}{}^{t}L_{a}){}^{t}R_{b_{1}}&(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)-\kappa).I_{4}\end{pmatrix}\times\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}\\
\overline{x_{1}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{4,1}\\
0_{4,1}\end{pmatrix}.

The existence of such an
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}\\
\overline{x_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\neq 0
is then equivalent to:

\mathrm{det}\begin{pmatrix}(2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})-\kappa).I_{4}&({}^{t}L_{c}L_{a}-L_{a}{}^{t}L_{c})R_{b_{1}}\\
({}^{t}L_{a}L_{c}-L_{c}{}^{t}L_{a}){}^{t}R_{b_{1}}&(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)-\kappa).I_{4}\end{pmatrix}=0.

The bottom right entry of the above block-matrix commutes with every matrix, so that the block-computation of the determinant gives:

\begin{split}\mathrm{det}&\begin{pmatrix}(2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})-\kappa).I_{4}&({}^{t}L_{c}L_{a}-L_{a}{}^{t}L_{c})R_{b_{1}}\\
({}^{t}L_{a}L_{c}-L_{c}{}^{t}L_{a}){}^{t}R_{b_{1}}&(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)-\kappa).I_{4}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\mathrm{det}((2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})-\kappa)(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)-\kappa).I_{4}-(({}^{t}L_{c}L_{a}-L_{a}{}^{t}L_{c})R_{b_{1}})\times(({}^{t}L_{a}L_{c}-L_{c}{}^{t}L_{a}){}^{t}R_{b_{1}}))\\
&=\mathrm{det}(\left((2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})-\kappa)(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)-\kappa)-|\overline{c}.a-a.\overline{c}|^{2}.|b_{1}|^{2}\right).I_{4}).\end{split}

We deduce that:

\displaystyle\kappa
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})\ \Leftrightarrow\ (2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})-\kappa)(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)-\kappa)-|\overline{c}.a-a.\overline{c}|^{2}.|b_{1}|^{2}=0
.

We note that
\displaystyle b=b_{0}+b_{1}.e
and
\displaystyle e\perp M
, hence:

\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b_{0})=\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b),\ \textrm{Re}(c.b_{0}.a)=\mathrm{Re}(c.b.a)\ \textrm{and}\ |\overline{c}.a-a.\overline{c}|^{2}.|b_{1}|^{2}=|[a,b_{1}.e,c]|^{2}=|[c,b,a]|^{2}.

We furthermore recall that
\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b)-\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)
is an eigenvalue of
\displaystyle(L_{a}L_{b}L_{c}+{}^{t}L_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})
if and only if the matrix
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
is singular. We finally find that the degeneracy locus of the matrix
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&L_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}L_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
is defined by the equation:

\begin{split}&\ (2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b)-\kappa)(2\mathrm{Re}(c.b.a)-\kappa)-|[c,b,a]|^{2}=0\\
\Leftrightarrow\ &\ \mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}(2\mathrm{Re}(c.a.b)-2\mathrm{Re}(c.b.a)+\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}})-|[c,b,a]|^{2}=0\\
\Leftrightarrow\ &\ \mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}^{2}+2\mathrm{Re}(c(ab-ba))\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}-|[c,b,a]|^{2}=0\\
\Leftrightarrow\ &\ \mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}^{2}+4\phi(a,\overline{b},c)\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}-|[c,b,a]|^{2}=0\\
\Leftrightarrow\ &\ \mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}^{2}-4\phi(a,b,c)\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}-|[c,b,a]|^{2}=0.\\
\end{split}

\displaystyle\blacktriangleleft

2.2
The geometry of the Ogievetskiî-Dray-Manogue sextic

We will now describe elementary geometric features of the sextic
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{26}
defined by the equation:

(\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A))^{2}-4\phi(c,b,a)\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)-\Big|[c,b,a]\Big|^{2}=0.

Some of them have been obtained with the help of
Macaulay2
(
[
GS
]
):

Theorem 2.2.1

1.

The sextic
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{26}
is irreducible and its singular locus is of codimension
\displaystyle 4
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{26}
.

2.

The neutral component the automorphism group of
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}
is
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO_{7}}\times\mathrm{SL}_{3}
.

3.

The map:

\mathrm{Gr}(6,\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}))/\!\!/\mathrm{SO_{7}}\times\mathrm{SL}_{3}\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76228pt}\mathrm{S}^{6}\mathbb{C}^{6}/\!\!/\mathrm{GL}_{6}

which sends a six dimensional subspace
\displaystyle L\subset\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
to the sextic
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}\cap L
is generically étale onto its image.

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

The codimension of the singular locus of
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}\subset\mathbb{P}^{26}
is obtained computationally with the help of
Macaulay2
. The only <<challenge>> is to find a way to write explicitly the equation
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}
as, to the best of my knowledge, there is no obvious package to simulate the octonion algebra in
Macaulay2
. We refer to the first appendix for a description of our algorithm. The irreducibility of
\displaystyle\mathrm{S_{ODM}}
follows as it is then a normal hypersurface in
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{26}
.

We now prove the second and third assertion. We first describe the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO_{7}}\times\mathrm{SL}_{3}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
. Let
\displaystyle T_{1}\in\mathrm{SO_{7}}
and
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
, we put:

T_{1}.A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&T_{1}(c)&(\overline{T_{1}(b)})\\
(\overline{T_{1}(c)})&\lambda_{2}&T_{1}(a)\\
T_{1}(b)&(\overline{T_{1}(a)})&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}),

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO_{7}}\subset\mathrm{SO_{8}}
is the isotropy group of
\displaystyle 1\in\mathbb{O}
.
On the other hand, for
\displaystyle C\in\mathrm{SL_{3}}
and
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
, we put:

C.A={}^{t}C\times A\times C\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}),

where
\displaystyle\times
denotes here the product of
\displaystyle 3*3
matrices (there is no associativity issues since all coefficients of
\displaystyle C
are complex and thus commute with all coefficients of
\displaystyle A
). Since
\displaystyle T_{1}(x)=x
, for all
\displaystyle T_{1}\in\mathrm{SO_{7}}
and for all
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{O}
with zero imaginary part, we deduce that:

C.(T_{1}.A)=T_{1}.(C.A),

for all
\displaystyle T_{1}\in\mathrm{SO_{7}},\ C\in\mathrm{SL_{3}}
and
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
. Furthermore, we notice that
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO_{7}}\cap\mathrm{SL_{3}}=\{Id\}

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{27}

\mathrm{det}\bigg(\kappa.I_{8}-L_{a}L_{b}R_{c}+{}^{t}R_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a}\bigg)=(\kappa-2.\mathrm{Re}(\overline{c}ba))^{4}.T(a,b,c,\kappa),

where
\displaystyle T(a,b,c,\kappa)
is weighted-homogeneous of degree
\displaystyle 12
with weights
\displaystyle(1,1,1,3)
.

We can now conclude the proof of the Theorem as we remember that the determinant of
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&R_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}R_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
vanishes if and only if
\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Re}(cab)-\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)
is a (complex) eigenvalue of
\displaystyle(L_{a}L_{b}R_{c}+{}^{t}R_{c}{}^{t}L_{b}{}^{t}L_{a})
. Hence the degeneracy locus of
\displaystyle N_{A}
is scheme-theoretically given by the equation:

\bigg(\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)-2\mathrm{Re}(cab)+2\mathrm{Re}(\overline{c}ba)\bigg)^{4}\times\mathfrak{T}=0,

where
\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}
is the degree
\displaystyle 12
homogeneous polynomial in
\displaystyle a,b,c
defined by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}=T(a,b,c,2\mathrm{Re}(cab)-\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A))
. One easily proves that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}=\mathfrak{S}^{2}
is a square and the explicit equation:

\begin{split}\mathfrak{S}&=\left(\lambda_{1}|a|^{2}+\lambda_{2}|b|^{2}+\lambda_{3}|c|^{2}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\right)^{2}\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt-4\left(\lambda_{1}|a|^{2}+\lambda_{2}|b|^{2}+\lambda_{3}|c|^{2}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}\right)\mathrm{Re}(c)\mathrm{Re}(ab)\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt+4\left(|b|^{2}|a|^{2}\mathrm{Re}(c)^{2}+|c|^{2}\mathrm{Re}(ab)^{2}-|a|^{2}|b|^{2}|c|^{2}\right),\end{split}

has been first found by Jonathan Hauenstein using the software
Bertini
.

\displaystyle\blacktriangleleft

3.2
A quasi-tautological rank
\displaystyle 4
sheaf on the Cartan cubic

We shall first explain how
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
act on
\displaystyle N_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&R_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}R_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}.
The action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
is again by transpose-conjugation and is the same as in section 2.2. To describe the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
, we shall need a few more facts on triality (we refer to
[
DeS01
]
for more details on the triality principle). We recall that there is an embedding (the triality embedding) of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO}_{8}\times\mathrm{SO}_{8}\times\mathrm{SO}_{8}
defined as follows:

\mathrm{Spin}_{8}=\{(T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})\in\big(\mathrm{SO}_{8}\big)^{3},\ T_{1}(x)T_{2}(y)=T_{3}(xy),\ \textrm{for all}\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}\}.

There are three copies of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
inside
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
which have remarkable group-theoretic properties with respect to the triality embedding. We are interested in one of them:

\mathrm{Spin}_{7}=\{(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\big(\mathrm{SO}_{8}\big)^{3},\ T_{1}(x)T_{2}(y)=T_{1}(xy),\ \textrm{for all}\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}\}.

The projection map:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Spin}_{7}\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76228pt}\mathrm{SO}_{8}\\
&(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\longrightarrow T_{2}\end{split}

is a double cover of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO}_{7}
embedded in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO_{8}}
as the stabilizer of
\displaystyle 1\in\mathbb{O}
. For any
\displaystyle T\in\mathrm{SO}_{8}
, we denote by
\displaystyle\mathrm{K}_{T}
the element of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO}_{8}
defined by
\displaystyle\mathrm{K}_{T}(x)=\overline{T(\overline{x})}
. It is easily checked that if
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})
is a triality, then
\displaystyle(\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}},T_{1},T_{2})
and
\displaystyle(T_{2},\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}},\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}})
are equally trialities (but, in general, they lie in different copies
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}\subset\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
).

We now come back to the twisted eigenvalue problem. Let
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
and let
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\\
z\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{O}^{3}
is in the twisted kernel of
\displaystyle A
, namely:

\left\{\begin{split}\lambda_{1}x+yc+\overline{b}z&\ =0\\
x\overline{c}+\lambda_{2}y+az&\ =0\\
bx+\overline{a}y+\lambda_{3}z&\ =0\\
\end{split}\right.

A quick computation shows that for any
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
, the vector
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}T_{1}(x)\\
T_{1}(y)\\
T_{2}(z)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{O}^{3}
is in the twisted kernel of
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&T_{2}(c)&\overline{\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)}\\
\overline{T_{2}(c)}&\lambda_{2}&T_{1}(a)\\
\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)&\overline{T_{1}(a)}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}
, that is:

\left\{\begin{split}\lambda_{1}T_{1}(x)+T_{1}(y)T_{2}(c)+T_{1}(\overline{b})T_{2}(z)&\ =0\\
T_{1}(x)\overline{T_{2}(c)}+\lambda_{2}T_{1}(y)+T_{1}(a)T_{2}(z)&\ =0\\
\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)T_{1}(x)+\overline{T_{1}(a)}y+\lambda_{3}T_{2}(z)&\ =0\\
\end{split}\right.

For any
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
and any
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
, we then define:

(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1}).A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&T_{2}(c)&\overline{\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)}\\
\overline{T_{2}(c)}&\lambda_{2}&T_{1}(a)\\
\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)&\overline{T_{1}(a)}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

This defines an action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
which preserves the twisted eigenvalue problem. The main result of this section is related to the cokernel of
\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&R_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}R_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
. As in section 2.3, we work over the polynomial ring
\displaystyle R=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{27}]
. We let
\displaystyle c=x_{1}e_{1}+\ldots+x_{8}e_{8}
,
\displaystyle b=x_{9}e_{1}+\ldots+x_{16}e_{n}
,
\displaystyle a=x_{17}e_{1}+\ldots+x_{24}e_{n}
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=x_{25}e_{1}
,
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=x_{26}e_{1}
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{3}=x_{27}e_{1}
, where
\displaystyle e_{1},\ldots,e_{8}
is the canonical basis of
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
.

Theorem 3.2.1

Let
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{O}}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}.I_{8}&R_{c}&{}^{t}L_{b}\\
{}^{t}R_{c}&\lambda_{2}.I_{8}&L_{a}\\
L_{b}&{}^{t}L_{a}&\lambda_{3}.I_{8}\end{pmatrix}
in
\displaystyle\mathscr{M}_{24,24}(R)
and let
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
be the cokernel of:

\mathbb{C}^{24}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{26}}(-1)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N_{\mathbb{O}}}}{{\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76228pt}}}\mathbb{C}^{24}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{26}}.

1.

The sheaf
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
is
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
-equivariant (where
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin_{7}},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
is the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{24}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
as above).

2.

It is supported on the union of a cubic hypersurface isomorphic to
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{\mathbb{O}}
and the degree
\displaystyle 6
hypersurface given by the equation
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}=0
. Both hypersurfaces are
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
-invariant.

3.

The restriction of
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
to the cubic hypersurface has generically rank
\displaystyle 4
while its restriction to the sextic
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}=0
has generically rank
\displaystyle 2
.

In the following, we shall also denote by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
the hypersurface defined by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}=0
.

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

Theorem
3.1.1
insures that the degeneracy locus of
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{O}}
is scheme-theoretically given by
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}-\mathrm{Re}(cab)+\mathrm{Re}(\overline{c}ba))^{4}\times\mathfrak{S}^{2}=0
. We already noted that
\displaystyle(\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}(A)-\mathrm{Re}(cab)+\mathrm{Re}(\overline{c}ba))=\mathrm{det}_{\mathbb{O}}A^{\prime}
, where
\displaystyle A^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\overline{c}&\overline{a}\\
c&\lambda_{2}&b\\
a&\overline{b}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}
. As a consequence, the equation defines a cubic isomorphic to the Cartan cubic, which we denote by
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{O}}
. The sheaf
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
is then supported on the union of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{O}}
and the degree
\displaystyle 6
hypersurface
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
. The rank of the restriction of
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
to
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{O}}
is exactly
\displaystyle 4
as
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Det}_{\mathbb{O}}-\mathrm{Re}(cab)+\mathrm{Re}(\overline{c}ba))
does not divide
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
. The same argument shows that the rank of
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
restricted to
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
is
\displaystyle 2
.

The
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
-equivariance of
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{O}}
follows from the definition of the
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
action (by transpose-conjugation). Let us prove that
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{O}}
is also
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
-equivariant. For any
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
, we know that
\displaystyle(\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}},T_{1},T_{2})
is also in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
. Hence, for any
\displaystyle(x,y,z,w)\in\mathbb{O}^{4}
and any
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
, we have:

R_{\overline{T_{2}(x)}}=T_{1}{}^{t}R_{x}T_{1}^{-1},\ L_{\overline{\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(y)}}=T_{1}{}^{t}L_{y}T_{2}^{-1},\ L_{T_{1}(z)}=T_{1}L_{z}T_{2}^{-1}\ \textrm{and}\ L_{\overline{T_{1}}(w)}=T_{2}{}^{t}L_{w}T_{1}^{-1}.

As a consequence, for any
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
, we find:

(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1}).N_{\mathbb{O}}=\begin{pmatrix}T_{1}&&\\
&T_{1}&\\
&&T_{2}\end{pmatrix}N_{\mathbb{O}}\begin{pmatrix}T_{1}^{-1}&&\\
&T_{1}^{-1}&\\
&&T_{2}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

which proves the
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
-equivariance of
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{O}}
. In fine, we can conclude that
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
, the cokernel of
\displaystyle N_{\mathbb{O}}
, is
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin_{7}},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
-equivariant, where
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin_{7}},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
is the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{24}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
. Since
\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{O}}
is
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
-equivariant, its support must be
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
-invariant. This proves that the sextic
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}
is
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
-invariant.

3.3
On the automorphism group of twisted eigenvalue problem

It is well known (see the section 14 of
[
Har90
]
) that the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin_{8}}
is
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
itself. In fact, one can prove that the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
is also
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
. The following argument has been communicated to me by Robert Bryant. Let
\displaystyle h\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}
defined by:

h=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Let us denote by
\displaystyle K_{2}
the copy of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
embedded in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
we considered in the previous section:

K_{2}=\{(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\big(\mathrm{SO}_{8}\big)^{3},\ T_{1}(x)T_{2}(y)=T_{1}(xy),\ \textrm{for all}\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}\}.

It is easily checked that we have
\displaystyle hK_{2}h^{-1}=K_{1}
as subgroups of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
, where
\displaystyle K_{1}
is the copy of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
embedded in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
as:

K_{1}=\{(T_{2},T_{1},T_{1})\in\big(\mathrm{SO}_{8}\big)^{3},\ T_{2}(x)T_{1}(y)=T_{1}(xy),\ \textrm{for all}\ (x,y)\in\mathbb{O}^{2}\}.

It is known that these two copies of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
are sufficient to generate
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{8}
(see the section
\displaystyle 14
of
[
Har90
]
) and we are done.

Proposition 3.3.1

The action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
we considered in Theorem
3.2.1
does not factor through the action
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
.

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

Assume by absurd that it does. Then the remark above shows that
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle=\mathrm{E}_{6}
. One immediately checks that the action of
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Spin}_{7},\mathrm{SL}_{3}\rangle
we defined on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
preserves the rank, when we see elements of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
as
\displaystyle 24*24
matrices. Since any element of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
can be diagonalized by the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
(see the chapter 14 of
[
Har90
]
), this would prove that any element of
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
has rank
\displaystyle 0,8,16
or
\displaystyle 24
as a
\displaystyle 24*24
matrix. But we know that a generic point on
\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}=0
has rank
\displaystyle 22
(see Theorem
3.2.1
), a contradiction.

\displaystyle\blacktriangleleft

We now state the main result of this section:

Theorem 3.3.2

The action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aut}^{0}(\mathfrak{S})\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
is prehomogeneous.

This result is slightly surprising as the subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{27}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}(\mathbb{C})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
is not
\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{6}
(see the above proposition). One also notices that the prehomogeneous space
\displaystyle(\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O}),\mathrm{Aut}^{0}(\mathfrak{S}))\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
does not appear in the list of Kimura-Sato
[
KS77
]
. This is not surprising as it is unlikely to be an irreducible prehomogeneous space. It thus provides an interesting example of a non-irreducible prehomogeneous vector space which does not split as a sum of lower dimensional prehomoegeneous vector spaces. Theorem
3.3.2
also shows that the geometry of the twisted octonionic eigenvalue problem differes eminently from that of the untwisted eigenvalue problem : the former is much more <<symmetric>> than the latter. Indeed, we noticed in section 2.2 that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aut}^{0}(\mathrm{S_{ODM}})\times\mathbb{C}^{*}=\mathrm{SO}_{7}\times\mathrm{SL}_{3}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
does not act prehomogeneously on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
.

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

We have shown in the previous section that the subgroup
\displaystyle G
of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{27}
generated by
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
is included in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Aut}^{0}(\mathfrak{S})
. We are going to prove that the action of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{*}\times G
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
is prehomogeneous. We first start with:

Lemma 3.3.3

Let
\displaystyle\{1,i,j,k,l,m,n,o\}
be a basis of
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}
over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
. We fix a splitting
\displaystyle\mathbb{O}=\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}.l
, with
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}=\mathrm{Vect}(1,i,j,k)
. The group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
acts transitively on the following varieties:

\begin{split}&S^{6}\times\{(x+y.l)\in\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}.l,\ \langle x,y\rangle=0,x\neq 0,y\neq 0\}\subset\mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{O})\times\mathbb{O},\\
&S^{6}\times\left(\mathbb{H}\backslash\{0\}\oplus\{0\}\right)\subset\mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{O})\times\mathbb{O},\\
&S^{6}\times\left(\{0\}\oplus\mathbb{H}.l\backslash\{0\}\right)\subset\mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{O})\times\mathbb{O},\end{split}

where
\displaystyle S^{6}
denotes the subvariety of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{O})
defined by
\displaystyle|z|^{2}=1
.

This lemma is well-known, we refer to corollary 5.4 in
[
Ada96
]
. We recall a proof for the convenience of the reader since the results of
[
Ada96
]
are stated over
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
and there are some minor modifications to be made over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}
.

Proof
:

\displaystyle\blacktriangleright\;

The projection map:

\begin{split}&\mathrm{Spin}_{7}\xrightarrow{\hskip 22.76228pt}\mathrm{SO}_{8}\\
&(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\longrightarrow T_{2}\end{split}

is a double cover of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO}_{7}
embedded in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SO_{8}}
as the stabilizer of
\displaystyle 1\in\mathbb{O}
. Hence,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
acts transitively on
\displaystyle S^{6}
and the stabilizer of any point in
\displaystyle S^{6}
is
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{6}
. We know that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{6}\simeq\mathrm{SL}_{4}
when we make the identification
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}.l=\mathbb{C}^{4}\oplus(\mathbb{C}^{4})^{*}
. Furthermore, for any
\displaystyle(x+y.l)\in\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}.l
, the scalar product
\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle
is the perfect pairing between
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{4}
and
\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{4})^{*}
. This pairing is obviously invariant for the product of the natural and the contragedient representation of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{4}
. The action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{4}
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{4}\backslash\{0\}
is transitive and the stabilizer in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{4}
of any non-zero
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{C}^{4}
contains
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
, which acts on
\displaystyle x^{\perp}\subset(\mathbb{C}^{4})^{*}
. The transitive action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{3}\backslash\{0\}
allows us to conclude the proof of the lemma.

\displaystyle\blacktriangleleft

We can now proceed to the proof of Theorem
3.3.2
. Recall that the action of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Spin}_{7}
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
is given as follows. For any
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
and any
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
, we have:

(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1}).A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&T_{2}(c)&\overline{\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)}\\
\overline{T_{2}(c)}&\lambda_{2}&T_{1}(a)\\
\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)&\overline{T_{1}(a)}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

We start with
\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&c&\overline{b}\\
\overline{c}&\lambda_{2}&a\\
b&\overline{a}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix}
generic in
\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{3}(\mathbb{O})
. By lemma
3.3.3
, we can find
\displaystyle(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1})\in\mathrm{Spin_{7}}
such that
\displaystyle T_{2}(c)=r_{1}+r_{2}.i
, with
\displaystyle r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
non zeros (by genericity of
\displaystyle A
). Hence, we have:

A_{1}=(T_{1},T_{2},T_{1}).A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&r_{1}+r_{2}.i&\overline{b_{1}}\\
r_{1}-r_{2}.i&\lambda_{2}&a^{\prime}\\
b_{1}&\overline{a^{\prime}}&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix},

with
\displaystyle a^{\prime}=T_{1}(a)
and
\displaystyle b_{1}=\mathrm{K}_{T_{1}}(b)
. Let us write
\displaystyle a^{\prime}=a_{0}^{\prime}+a_{1}^{\prime}.l
with
\displaystyle a_{0}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}
. Since
\displaystyle A
is assumed generic, we can arrange
\displaystyle T_{1}
in the previous transformation so that
\displaystyle\langle r_{1}-r_{2}.i,a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle\neq 0
. We plan to make the transformation
\displaystyle a^{\prime}\longleftarrow a^{\prime}-\frac{\langle a_{0}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle r_{1}-r_{2}.i,a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}(r_{1}-r_{2}.i)
. We thus consider
\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\frac{-\langle a_{0}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle r_{1}-r_{2}.i,a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{3}
and we have:

A_{2}={}^{t}HA_{1}H=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&r_{1}+r_{2}.i&\overline{b_{2}}\\
r_{1}-r_{2}.i&\lambda_{2}&a^{\prime\prime}\\
b_{2}&\overline{a^{\prime\prime}}&{\lambda_{3}}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

with
\displaystyle a^{\prime\prime}=a^{\prime}-\frac{\langle a_{0}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}{\langle r_{1}-r_{2}.i,a_{1}^{\prime}\rangle}(r_{1}-r_{2}.i)
,
\displaystyle b_{2}\in\mathbb{O}
and
\displaystyle{\lambda_{3}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
