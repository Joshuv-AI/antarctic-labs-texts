Stiefel Whitney Classes for Real representations of GL 2 ⁡ ( 𝔽 q ) \GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
Jyotirmoy Ganguly, Rohit Joshi

Abstract
. We compute the total Stiefel Whitney class for a real representation π \pi of GL 2 ⁡ ( 𝔽 q ) \GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}) , where q q is odd. The obstruction class of π \pi is defined to be the Stiefel Whitney class of lowest positive degree that does not vanish. We provide an expression for the obstruction class of π \pi in terms of its character values if det π = 1 \det\pi=1 .



1.
Introduction

Let
\pi
be a real representation of a finite group
G
. One can associate
Stiefel Whitney classes
w_{i}(\pi)\in H^{i}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
(see
[

, Section
2.6
, page no.
50
]
) to it such that
w_{i}(\pi)=0
if
i>\deg\pi
. We define the “obstruction class” to be
w_{i}(\pi)
where the degree

, called the obstruction degree of
\pi
, is minimal with
w_{i}(\pi)\neq 0
. In fact the obstruction degree of
\pi
turns out to be a power of
2
(see
[
MS16
, Problem
8
-B]
).
In this paper we calculate the total Stiefel Whitney class of a real representaton
\pi
of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, where
q
is odd. Moreover, if
w_{1}(\pi)=0
we compute the obstruction class of
\pi
in terms of its character values.

Let
G=\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be the general linear group for a vector space of dimension 2 over the field with
q
elements where
q
is odd. Let
\pi
be a real representation of
G
.
Take
z_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{pmatrix}
and
t_{(x,y)}=\begin{pmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{pmatrix}
. Consider the terms

(1)

m_{10}=\frac{1}{8}(\chi_{\pi}(z_{1})-\chi_{\pi}(z_{-1}))

(2)

m_{11}=\frac{1}{8}(\chi_{\pi}(z_{1})-2\chi_{\pi}(t_{(1,-1)})+\chi_{\pi}(z_{-1}))

It turns out that

.

From

we have for

H^{*}(\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}),\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[e_{1},e_{2},c_{1},c_{2}]/(e_{1}^{2}=0,e_{2}^{2}=0),

are elements of degree

A real representation
\pi
of

is called achiral if

. Otherwise it is called chiral.
We define

For

. Now we state our main results.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\pi
be a real representation of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})

w(\varphi)=(1+\delta e_{1})(1+c_{1}+c_{2})^{m_{10}}(1+c_{1})^{m_{11}}.

\\
\left(\dfrac{m_{10}+m_{11}}{2^{k}}\right)c_{1}^{2^{k}}+\left(\dfrac{m_{10}}{2^{k-1}}\right)c_{2}^{2^{k-1}},\quad\text{if $k\geq 1$}.\end{cases}

We also have a similar results for a real representation
\pi
of

where

(See Theorem
6
and Theorem

). We obtain explicit expressions for

2.
Notation and Preliminaries

2.1.
Orthogonal Representations and Spinoriality

A complex representation
(\pi,V)
of a finite group
G
is called orthogonal if it preserves a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form. An orthogonal representation
\pi
is called spinorial if it can be lifted to
\mathrm{Pin}(V)
, the topological double cover of
\mathrm{O}(V)
.
See
[
GS20
]
for reference.

Here we present a brief review on real and complex representations of a finite group
G
. For details and proofs we refer the reader to
[
BtD95
, Section II.6]
.
A real (or complex) representation means the underlying vector space is real (or complex).
For a complex representation
(\pi,V)
of
G
we write
(\pi_{\mathbb{R}},V_{\mathbb{R}})
for the realization of
\pi
. This simply means that we forget the complex structure on
V
and regard it as a real representation. When
\pi
is orthogonal, there is a unique real representation
(\pi_{0},V_{0})
, up to isomorphism, so that
\pi\cong\pi_{0}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. Observe that a real representation
\pi
of a finite group is equivalent to an orthogonal representation.

Any orthogonal complex representation
\Pi
of
G
can be decomposed as

\Pi=S(\pi)\oplus\bigoplus_{j}\varphi_{j},

where
S(\pi)=\pi\oplus\pi^{\vee}
and each
\varphi_{j}
is irreducible orthogonal and
\pi
is arbitrary. For details see
[
JS21
, Section
2.1
]
.

We say a complex representation
\pi
is
orthogonally irreducible
, provided
\pi
is orthogonal, and
\pi
does not decompose into a direct sum of
orthogonal
representations. Thus, an orthogonal representation
\pi
is orthogonally irreducible iff
\pi
is irreducible, or of the form
S(\phi)
where
\phi
is irreducible but not orthogonal. We write ‘OIR’ for “orthogonally irreducible representation”.

For an even integer
n
, write
C_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
for the additive cyclic group and multiplicative cyclic group of order
n
respectively. Let
\zeta_{n}=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{n}}
be the primitive
n^{th}
root of unity. Let
\chi^{j}:C_{n}\to\mu_{n}
denote the representation where
\chi^{j}(1)=\zeta_{n}^{j}
and
sgn=\chi^{n/2}
.
We write
\epsilon_{\chi^{j}}
to denote the parity of
j
. We say
\chi^{j}
is odd (resp. even) if
\epsilon_{\chi^{j}}
is odd (resp. even).

2.2.
Some Results on Binomial Coefficients

For non-negative integers
m,n
and a prime
p
one gets base
p
expansions for
m
and
n
as
m=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}m_{i}p^{i}
and
n=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}n_{i}p^{i}
. Here we state Lucas theorem (
[
Fin47
]
) which we use extensively in Section
4
.

Theorem 3

(Lucas Theorem)
.

For non-negative integers
m,n
and a prime
p
we have

\binom{m}{n}\equiv\prod_{i=0}^{k}\binom{m_{i}}{n_{i}}\pmod{p}.

Proposition 1
.

If
\upsilon_{2}(n)\geq k
, then
\dbinom{n}{2^{k}}\equiv\dfrac{n}{2^{k}}\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

The proof follows from Theorem
3
.
∎

The Vandermonde’s identity for binomial coefficients states that

\binom{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{l}}{m}=\sum_{k_{1}+\cdots+k_{l}=m}\binom{n_{1}}{k_{1}}\binom{n_{2}}{k_{2}}\cdots\binom{n_{l}}{k_{l}}.

(1)

We write
\upsilon_{2}(n)
to denote the
2
-adic valuation of the
n
.

Proposition 2
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, if
\upsilon_{2}(n)\geq k
and
a<2^{k}
, then
\binom{n}{a}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
.

Proof.

The proof is immediate from Lucas Theorem
3
.
∎

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
m
is a positive integer. Then
\binom{m}{2^{i}}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
for
i=0,1,2,\ldots,k
, if and only if,
2^{k+1}\mid m
.

Proof.

The if part follows from Theorem
3
. For the only if part, we prove the result by indution on
k
. The
k=0
case is trivial.
Suppose the statement holds for
k=l-1
i.e. if
\binom{m}{2^{i}}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,l-1
then
2^{l}\mid m
.
Now if moreover
\binom{m}{2^{l}}\equiv 0\pmod{2}
, then using Lucas Theorem
3
we obtain that the
(l+1)^{th}
digit in the binary expansion (counting from the right end) of
m
should be
0
. Hence we get
2^{l+1}\mid m
.
∎

3.
Review of Stiefel Whitney Classes

3.1.
Group Cohomology

For
R
a ring, write
H^{*}(G,R)
for the usual group cohomology ring, with
R
regarded as a trivial
G
-module.

If
\varphi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
is a group homomorphism, we have an induced map
\varphi^{*}:H^{*}(G_{2},R)\to H^{*}(G_{1},R)
on cohomology.

Write

\kappa:H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z})\to H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})

(2)

for the coefficient map of cohomology.

3.2.
Characteristic Classes

In this section we review the theory of characteristic classes of representations of a finite group
G
. Our reference is
[
GKT89
]
.
Associated to complex representations
\pi
of
G
are cohomology classes
c_{i}(\pi)\in H^{2i}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, for
0\leq i\leq\deg\pi
, called Chern classes.
We have
c_{0}(\pi)=1
. The first Chern class, applied to linear characters, gives an isomorphism

c_{1}:\mathrm{Hom}(G,S^{1})\overset{\sim}{\to}H^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}).

This extends to arbitrary complex representations
\pi
by
c_{1}(\pi)=c_{1}(\det\pi)
.

Definition 1
.

If
\pi
is an orthogonal complex representation of
G
, put

w^{\mathbb{C}}_{i}(\pi)=w^{\mathbb{R}}_{i}(\pi_{0}),

for
0\leq i\leq\deg\pi
.

Thus if
\rho
is a real representation, we have
w^{\mathbb{R}}(\rho)=w^{\mathbb{C}}(\rho\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 1
.

If
\pi
is a complex representation, then

w^{\mathbb{C}}(S(\pi))=\kappa(c(\pi)).

Proof.

We have

\begin{split}w^{\mathbb{C}}(S(\pi))&=w^{\mathbb{R}}(S(\pi)_{0})\\
&=w^{\mathbb{R}}(\pi_{\mathbb{R}})\\
&=\kappa(c(\pi)),\\
\end{split}

the last equality by
[
MS16
, Problem 14-B]
.
∎

Henceforth all representations will be complex representations, and we will drop the superscript ‘
\mathbb{C}
’ from
w^{\mathbb{C}}
.

Let
\pi
be an orthogonal (complex) representation. Again one has
w_{0}(\pi)=1
, and the first Stiefel Whitney class, applied to linear characters
G\to\{\pm 1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, is the well-known isomorphism

w_{1}:\mathrm{Hom}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\overset{\sim}{\to}H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}).

Therefore
w_{1}(\pi)=\det\pi
.

Definition 2
.

A real representation
\pi
of
G
is called achiral if
w_{1}(\pi)=0
. It is called chiral otherwise.

Proposition 4
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and
\pi
an orthogonal representation of
G
. Then
\varphi
is spinorial iff

w_{2}(\pi)=w_{1}(\pi)\cup w_{1}(\pi).

Proof.

See, for instance,
[
GKT89
]
.
∎

3.3.
Detection

Let
G^{\prime}
be a subgroup of a finite group
G
.
We say that
G^{\prime}

detects
the (mod
2
) cohomology of
G
, provided that the restriction map

\res:H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\to H^{*}(G^{\prime},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})

is injective.
A special case of
[
DF04
, Section 17.2, Exercise 19]
gives:

Proposition 5
.

If
P
is a
2
-Sylow subgroup of
G
, then
P
detects the (mod
2
) cohomology of
G
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
P
denote the
2
-Sylow subgroup of
C_{n}
. Then

H^{*}(C_{n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})=H^{*}(P,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}).

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
C_{n}=P\times H
, where
H
is a cyclic group of odd order.
∎

The following theorem is found in
[
AM13
, theorem
4.4
, page no.
227
]
:

Theorem 4
.

If
q
is odd, then the subgroup
D
of diagonal matrices in
G=\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
detects the (mod
2
) cohomology of
G
.

For an orthogonal representation
\pi
of
G
, we will often write ‘
w_{i}(\pi)
’ as an abbreviation for

\iota^{*}(w_{i}(\pi))=w_{i}(\pi|_{D}).

4.1.
Results for the Bicyclic Group

(3)

Consider a real representation

(5)

Let us define

S_{xy}=\{(j_{1},j_{2})\in S\mid j_{1}\equiv x\pmod{2},j_{2}\equiv y\pmod{2}\},

(6)

(7)

Lemma 2
.

For

We get the result by solving the following equations:

\displaystyle=m^{\prime}+2(m_{00}+m_{10}+m_{01}+m_{11}),

\pi
of

Consider a real representation

Proof.

According to

\det(\Ind_{H}^{G}(\rho))=\det(\Ind_{H}^{G}{\mathbb{1}})^{\dim\rho}\cdot(\det\rho\circ\ver_{G/H}),

We recall that given

\ver_{G/H}(g\cdot D(G))=\prod_{x\in G/H}h_{x,g}\cdot D(H).

Given

Lemma 8
.

The transfer map corresponding to the subgroup

\ver_{G/B}(g\>\Mod D(G))=\begin{pmatrix}\det g&0\\
0&\det g\end{pmatrix}\>\Mod D(B).

It is well-known that

.

Define

x&1\end{pmatrix}\text{ if $\alpha\in\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x&1\end{pmatrix}B$},\\
\\
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\text{ if $\alpha\in\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}B$}.\\
\end{cases}

(21)

Since

(1)

7.1.
Steenrod Squares

For a finite group
G
one can define Steenrod Square operator
\Sq^{i}:H^{j}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\to H^{i+j}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
(see
[
AM13
, page
52
]
for details). For
x\in H^{j}(G,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
one has

\Sq(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\Sq^{i}(x).

Note that
\Sq^{i}(x)=0
if
i>j
. Here we mention some basic results of the Steenrod square operator on the cohomolohy ring
H^{*}(C_{n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
where
n
is even.
We follow
the note
by B. I. Chetard closely.

We know
H^{*}(C_{2},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}[v]
, where
\deg(v)=1
.
We have

\Sq(v^{k})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}v^{k+i}.

(24)

We know
H^{*}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\cong\dfrac{\mathbb{F}_{2}[s,t]}{(s^{2})}
, where
s
is of degree
1
and
t
is of degree
2
.

Note that
\Sq^{1}
is the Bockstein homomorphism associated to the exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow 0.

This gives the following long exact sequence
H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})
H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
H^{3}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
\cdots
\alpha
\beta

Here
\beta=\Sq^{1}
. We have
\Sq^{1}(t)=0
if
t=\alpha(x)
for some
x\in H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. Since
\Sq^{1}(s)=0
the image of the map
\Sq^{1}:H^{1}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\to H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is zero. Moreover we have
H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
and
H^{2}(C_{4n},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore the exact sequence becomes

0
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
\alpha
\beta

The map
\alpha
is surjective. Thus
\ker(\beta)=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and this gives
\Sq^{1}(t)=0
.
We obtain

\Sq(s)=s,\quad\quad\Sq(t^{k})=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}t^{k+i}.

(25)

Lemma 12
.

Consider a real representation of
C_{n}\times C_{n}
where
n
is even. Let
2^{l}
be the obstruction degree for
\pi
. Then

w_{2^{l}+i}(\pi)=Sq^{i}(w_{2^{l}}(\pi)),

for
1\leq i<2^{l}
.

Proof.

By Wu’s formula we have

Sq^{i}(w_{j})=\sum_{t=0}^{i}\binom{j+t-i-1}{t}w_{i-t}w_{j+t}.

We put
j=2^{l}
and use the fact that
w_{i-t}=0
unless
i=t
. For
i=t
we obtain

Sq^{i}(w_{2^{l}})=\binom{2^{l}-1}{i}w_{2^{l}+i}=w_{2^{k}+i}.

∎

Theorem 10
.

Consider a real representation
\pi
of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
where
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Let the obstruction degree of
\pi
is
2^{k+1}
where
k=\min\{\upsilon_{2}(m_{10}+m_{11}),\upsilon_{2}(m_{10})+1\}
. Then we have

w_{2^{k+1}+i}(\pi)=\begin{cases}0,\quad\text{if}\,\,1\leq i<2^{k+1},i\neq 2^{k},\\
\\
\left(\dfrac{m_{10}}{2^{k-1}}\right)(t_{1}^{2^{k}}t_{2}^{2^{k-1}}+t_{1}^{2^{k-1}}t_{2}^{2^{k}}),\quad\text{if}\,\,i=2^{k}.\end{cases}

In particular
w_{r}(\pi)=0
for
r>\deg\pi
.

Proof.

For
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the obstruction class of a representation
\pi
of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is given by

w_{2^{k+1}}(\pi)=\frac{m_{10}+m_{11}}{2^{k}}(t_{1}^{2^{k}}+t_{2}^{2^{k}})+\frac{m_{10}}{2^{k-1}}t_{1}^{2^{k-1}}t_{2}^{2^{k-1}},

where
k=\min\{\upsilon_{2}(m_{10}+m_{11}),\upsilon_{2}(m_{10})+1\}
. From Lemma
12
we know that
w_{2^{k+1}+i}(\pi)=\Sq^{i}(w_{2^{k+1}}(\pi))
.
Note that the expression for obstruction class is actually
\res(w_{2^{k+1}}(\pi))
,
where
\res
denotes the restriction of the representation to the diagonal subgroup. Since
\Sq\res=\res\Sq
, we directly compute
\Sq^{i}(w_{2^{k+1}}(\pi))
for
i<2^{k+1}
. For
j\in\{1,2\}
we have

\Sq^{i}(t_{j}^{2^{k}})=\begin{cases}0,\quad\text{if $i$ is odd},\\
\binom{2^{k}}{i/2}t_{j}^{2^{k}+i/2},\quad\text{if $i$ is even}.\end{cases}

Observe that
\binom{2^{k}}{i/2}=0
, unless
i=0
or
2^{k+1}
. Since
0<i<2^{k+1}
we obtain
\Sq^{i}(t_{j}^{2^{k}})=0
.
Using Cartan’s formula we have

\displaystyle\Sq^{i}(t_{1}^{2^{k-1}}t_{2}^{2^{k-1}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}+l_{2}=i}\Sq^{l_{1}}(t_{1}^{2^{k-1}})\Sq^{l_{2}}(t_{2}^{2^{k-1}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l_{1}+l_{2}=i,l_{1},\l_{2}\,\text{even}}\binom{2^{k-1}}{l_{1}/2}t_{1}^{2^{k-1}+l_{1}/2}\binom{2^{k-1}}{l_{2}/2}t_{1}^{2^{k-1}+l_{2}/2}.

Note that the expression in the right hand side survives if
l_{1}=2^{k},l_{2}=0
or
l_{1}=0,l_{2}=2^{k}
. This gives

w_{3\cdot 2^{k}}(\pi)=\left(\dfrac{m_{10}}{2^{k-1}}\right)(t_{1}^{2^{k}}t_{2}^{2^{k-1}}+t_{1}^{2^{k-1}}t_{2}^{2^{k}}).

∎

7.3.
Anisotropic Torus

Let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
,
G=\GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. If we identify
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{q}
then the map
x\to a\cdot x
gives a linear map
M_{a}\in G
. Then
M=\{M_{a},a\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\}
is a cyclic group of order
q^{2}-1
and is called an anisotropic torus.

Theorem 12
.

The anisotropic torus
M
does not detect the mod-2 cohomology of
G
.

Proof.

Take representation
\pi=S(\chi)
, where
\epsilon_{\chi}=1
. Then
w_{2}(\pi)\neq 0
.
Let
\hat{g}
be the generator of
M
. Now we have

w_{2}(\pi\mid_{M})=\kappa(c_{1}(\chi\circ\det\mid_{M})).

Since the map
\chi\circ\det\mid_{M}
factors through
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
we obtain
\chi\circ\det(\hat{g})=\zeta_{q-1}^{j}=\zeta_{q^{2}-1}^{(q+1)j}
. Let
u=c_{1}(\dot{\chi})
where
\dot{\chi}\in\mathrm{Hom}(M,\mathbb{C}^{\times})
such that
\dot{\chi}(\hat{g})=\zeta_{q^{2}-1}
. As a result we get

\displaystyle\kappa(c_{1}(\chi\circ\det\mid_{M}))

\displaystyle=\kappa((q+1)ju)

\displaystyle=(q+1)j\kappa(u)

\displaystyle=0.

Hence the proof.

∎
