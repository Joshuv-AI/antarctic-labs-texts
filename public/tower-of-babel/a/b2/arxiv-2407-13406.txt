Quasi-stratified Order Semantics of Concurrency
Maciej Koutny, Łukasz Mikulski

Abstract
In the development of operational semantics of concurrent systems, a key decision concerns the adoption of a suitable notion of execution model, which basically amounts to choosing a class of partial orders according to which events are arranged along the execution line. Typical kinds of such partial orders are the total, stratified and interval orders. In this paper, we introduce quasi-stratified orders — positioned in-between the stratified and interval orders — which are tailored for transaction-like or hierarchical concurrent executions. Dealing directly with the vast number of executions of concurrent system is far from being practical. It was realised long time ago that it can be much more effective to consider behaviours at a more abstract level of behavioural specifications (often based on intrinsic relationships between events such as those represented by causal partial orders), each such specification — typically, a relational structure — encompassing a (large) number of executions. In this paper, we introduce and investigate suitable specifications for behaviours represented by quasi-stratified orders. The proposed model of quasi-stratified relational structures is based on two relationships between events — the ‘before’ and ‘not later’ relationships — which can be used to express and analyse causality, independence, and simultaneity between events.

1
Introduction

When designing a sound semantics of a class of concurrent systems,
it is paramount to address the following two aspects: (i)
faithful formal modelling of the executed behaviours; and (ii)
providing support for effective validation techniques of
system behaviour.
The former basically amounts to choosing a class of partial orders
(e.g., total, stratified or interval orders)
according to which events are arranged along the execution line.
The latter is often realised through devising suitable relational structures which
are more abstract and encompassing a (large) number
of executed behaviours.
Typically, such relational structures capture intrinsic relationships
between events (such as those represented by causal partial orders),
and can be used to express and analyse
causality, independence, and simultaneity between events.

Each of the existing ways of modelling
concurrent behaviours using partial orders comes with its own merits.
We feel, however, that neither addresses in a satisfactory manner the behavioural features
of systems which support hierarchical executions, such as
those built on transaction-like executions.
In this paper, we aim at providing some initial ideas and solutions to
rectify this.

1.1
Background

Virtually any approach to modelling operational semantics of concurrent systems
is fundamentally based on a model for representing individual system behaviours.
In such representations, two events (executed actions)
can be observed as either happening one after another or simultaneously.
This, in particular, means that such a behaviour can be represented as
a partially ordered set of events as precedence is a transitive relationship, and the
simultaneity between events can be understood as the relationship of being unordered.
In the literature, one can identify three main kinds of
partial orders modelling concurrent behaviours:
(i) total orders modelling sequential executions;
(ii) stratified orders modelling behaviours in which sets of simultaneous events are executed sequentially;
and
(iii) interval orders modelling behaviours where events can take time and the corresponding time intervals can
arbitrarily overlap.
In this paper we are concerned with modelling of executions where events can have duration,
and so can be modelled by interval orders.

The relevance of interval orders follows from an observation, credited to Wiener
[
21
]
,
that any execution of a physical system that can be observed by a single observer
must be an interval order. It implies that the most precise observational
semantics should be defined in terms of interval orders (cf.
[
9
]
).
In the area of concurrency theory,
the use of interval orders
can be traced back to
[
15
,
18
,
9
]
, and
processes of concurrent systems with interval order semantics were studied in
[
10
,
11
]
.
Interval orders were used to investigate communication protocols in
[
1
]
, using the approach of
[
14
]
.
Interval semantics (ST-semantics) was investigated for Petri nets with read arcs
[
20
]
and discussions on distributability of concurrent systems
[

]
.

As an example, consider four transactions in the distributed environment:

In this paper, we consider a model of interval order executions tailored for concurrent systems
such as distributed systems with nested transaction, and concurrent systems employing action
refinement. In such cases, the underlying assumption is that
an execution can be seen as a sequence of generalised strata,
where each generalised stratum is composed, e.g., of a base event

1.2
The approach followed in this paper

Dealing directly with the vast number of actual behaviours of a concurrent system is far from being practical.
It was realised long time ago that it is more effective to discuss behaviours at more abstract level of
behavioural specifications (often based on intrinsic relationships
between events such as those represented by causal partial orders),
each such specification — typically, a relational structure — encompassing a (large) number
of behaviours.

During the operation of a
concurrent or distributed system, system actions
may be executed sequentially or simultaneously. To faithfully reflect the simultaneity of actions, one
may choose step sequences (or stratified orders of events) where
actions are regarded as happening instantaneously in sets (called steps),
or interval orders where actions are executed over time intervals.
In this paper, we investigate a novel representation of concurrent behaviours
(such as those exhibited by transactional distributed systems),
called quasi-stratified orders, which lie in-between the stratified and interval orders.

Dealing with the semantics of concurrent systems purely in terms of their individual executions, such as total orders (sequences), stratified orders (step sequences),
quasi-stratified orders,
or interval orders is far from being computationally efficient in terms both of behaviour modelling and of
property validation.
To address this shortcoming, more involved relational structures have been introduced, aiming at a succinct and faithful
representation
\mathit{rs}
of (often exponentially large) sets
\mathit{RS}
of closely related individual executions.
Examples include causal partial orders for sequences, and
invariant structures for step sequences.
Succinctness is usually achieved by
retaining in
\mathit{rs}
(through intersection) only those relationships which are common to all executions in
\mathit{RS}
.
Faithfulness, on the other hand, requires that all potential executions which are extensions of
\mathit{rs}
belong to
\mathit{RS}
.
Structures like
\mathit{rs}
,
referred to as invariant structures
represent concurrent histories
and both desired properties
(succinctness and faithfulness)
follow from generalisations of Szpilrajn’s Theorem (i.e., a partial order is the intersection of all
its total order extensions).
Although
\mathit{rs}
provides a clean theoretical capture of the set
\mathit{RS}
, to turn them into a practical tool (as, e.g., in
[
17
]
)
one needs to be able to derive them directly from single executions using
the relevant structural properties of the concurrent system.
This brings into focus relational structures with acyclic relations on events (e.g., dependence graphs introduced
in
[
16
]
and analysed in detail in
[
5
]
), which
yield invariant structures after applying a suitable closure operation (e.g., the transitive closure
for acyclic relations).

The approach sketched above has been introduced and investigated
in
[
7
,
6
]

as a generic model
that provides general recipes for building analytic frameworks based on
relational structures.
It starts from
a class
\mathcal{R}
of relational structures (such as acyclic relations),
which are compared w.r.t. the information they convey
(expressed by the relation
\trianglelefteq
with
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\trianglelefteq\mathit{rs}
if

\mathit{rs}
is obtained from
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
by adding
new relationships, i.e.,
\mathit{rs}
is more
concrete
than
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
).
Then the
maximal
relational structures
(such as total orders)

\mathcal{R}^{\mathit{max}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
represent individual executions.
Then
closed

relational structures (like causal partial orders)
\mathcal{R}^{\mathit{clo}}\subseteq\mathcal{R}

can be used to analyse intrinsic relationships between executed events.

2
Modelling concurrent behaviours

There is a natural distinction between specifications of concurrent
system behavior (which may be given as sets of constraints about
the ordering of actions to be executed), and actual observed behaviours
(executions of actions) which should adhere to these specifications.
Usually, a single specification can be realised by (very) many actual behaviours.

In this paper, we will address both specifications and behaviours within a single framework.
We will consider events (i.e., executed actions)
as activities taking time which can therefore be understood as
time intervals. We are not interested in the precise timings of such intervals,
but only whether they are ordered in time
(i.e., that one interval precedes another interval) or simultaneous
(i.e., that two time intervals overlap).

2.1
Partial order models of system executions

We adopt the view that observed behaviours of concurrent systems
are represented by partial orders (as acyclicity of events
is a very minimal requirement resulting from physical considerations, and event precedence
is transitive).
Note that for partial order system executions, simultaneity
of events
(corresponding to the overlapping of time intervals)
amounts to the lack of ordering between events.

There are different partial order models of concurrent
behaviours, e.g., reflecting the
underlying hardware and/of software.
In the literature, there are three main kinds of such models,
namely the total, stratified, and interval orders, as recalled below.

Let
\Delta
be a finite set (of events) and
\prec
be
a binary (precedence) relation over
\Delta
.
Then
\mathit{po}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
is called (below
x,y,z,w\in\Delta
):

•

partial order
if

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~}l@{~~}l@{~~~~~~~}l@{~~}l@{~~}l}\textsc{po{:}1}&:&\makebox[85.35826pt][l]{\mbox{$x\not\prec x$}}&\textsc{po{:}2}&:&x\prec y\prec z\Longrightarrow x\prec z\end{array}

•

total order
if

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~}l@{~~}l@{~~~~~~~}l@{~~}l@{~~}l}\textsc{to{:}1}&:&\makebox[85.35826pt][l]{\mbox{$x\not\prec x$}}&\textsc{to{:}3}&:&x\neq y\Longrightarrow x\prec y~\vee~y\prec x\\
\textsc{to{:}2}&:&x\prec y\prec z\Longrightarrow x\prec z\end{array}

•

stratified order
if

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~}l@{~~}l@{~~~~~~~}l@{~~}l@{~~}l}\textsc{so{:}1}&:&\makebox[85.35826pt][l]{\mbox{$x\not\prec x$}}&\textsc{so{:}3}&:&x\not\prec y\not\prec x~\wedge~x\prec z\Longrightarrow y\prec z\\
\textsc{so{:}2}&:&x\prec y\prec z\Longrightarrow x\prec z&\textsc{so{:}4}&:&x\not\prec y\not\prec x~\wedge~z\prec x\Longrightarrow z\prec y\end{array}

•

interval order
if

\begin{array}[]{l@{~~}l@{~~}l@{~~~~~~~}l@{~~}l@{~~}l}\textsc{io{:}1}&:&\makebox[85.35826pt][l]{\mbox{$x\not\prec x$}}&\textsc{io{:}2}&:&x\prec y~\wedge~z\prec w\Longrightarrow x\prec w~\vee~z\prec y\end{array}

All total orders are stratified, all stratified orders are interval,
and all interval orders are partial.

The following result
provides a useful characterisation of stratified orders.

Proposition 2.1

\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
is a stratified order iff
there is a partition
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
of
\Delta
such that

\prec=\bigcup_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j}
.

Proof.

(
\Longleftarrow
)
Clearly,
so:1,2
are satisfied.
Moreover,
so:3,4
hold
as
x\not\prec y\not\prec x
implies that
there is
\Delta_{i}
such that
x,y\in\Delta_{i}
.

(
\Longrightarrow
)
We first observe that the relation

S=\{\langle x,y\rangle\in\Delta\times\Delta\mid x\not\prec y\not\prec x\}

is an equivalence relation.
Indeed,
\{\langle x,x\rangle\mid x\in\Delta\}\subseteq S
by
so:1
.
Suppose now that

x\not\prec y\not\prec x
and
z\not\prec y\not\prec z
.
If
x\prec z
or
z\prec x
, then, by
so:3,4
, we obtain that

y\prec z
or
z\prec y
, yielding
a contradiction with
z\not\prec y\not\prec z
.
Hence, there is a partition
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
of
\Delta
into the equivalence classes of
S
.

Suppose that
i\neq j
,
x,y\in\Delta_{i}
and
z\in\Delta_{j}
. Then, as
\Delta_{i}
and
\Delta_{j}

are equivalence classes of
S
,

x\prec z
or
z\prec x
. If
x\prec z
, then, by
so:3
,
y\prec z
.
Hence,
\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j}\subseteq\prec
. Moreover, as
\prec
is acyclic (by
so:1,2
),
we have

(\Delta_{j}\times\Delta_{i})\cap\prec=\varnothing
. Similarly, if
z\prec x
, then

\Delta_{j}\times\Delta_{i}\subseteq\prec
and
(\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j})\cap\prec=\varnothing
.

As a result, for all different
\Delta_{i}
and
\Delta_{j}
,

\prec|_{(\Delta_{i}\cup\Delta_{j})\times(\Delta_{j}\cup\Delta_{i})}

is equal either to
\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j}
or
\Delta_{j}\times\Delta_{i}
.
Therefore, because
\prec
is acyclic, there is a re-ordering of the
equivalence classes of
S
such that

\prec=\bigcup_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j}
.

Remark 2.3
(models of execution)

Thinking about partial orders as representations of behaviours,
leads to the following observations.

•

The adjective in ‘total order’ derives from an alternative
definition:

\mathit{po}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
is a total order
if
\Delta
can be enumerated as
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
so that

\prec=\{\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}
.

Intuitively, this means that the intervals corresponding to events are linearly
ordered, with no overlapping (no two events are simultaneous).

•

The adjective in ‘stratified order’ derives from an alternative
definition (see Proposition
2.1
):

\mathit{po}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
is a stratified order
if there are sets (strata)

\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
forming a partition of
\Delta
so that

\prec=\bigcup_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j}
.

Intuitively, this means that the intervals corresponding to events can be
partitioned into non-overlapping totally ordered strata (or steps) such that within each stratum
all the intervals overlap.

•

The adjective in ‘interval order’ derives from an alternative
definition (see
[
3
]
):

\mathit{po}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
is an interval order
if there exist real-valued mappings,

\beta
and
\epsilon
, such that, for
all
x,y\in\Delta
,
\beta(x)<\epsilon(x)
and

x\prec y\iff\epsilon(x)<\beta(y)
.

Intuitively,
\beta
and
\epsilon
represent
the ‘beginnings’ and ‘endings’, respectively, of the intervals corresponding to
events in
\Delta
.
A remark, credited to Wiener
[
21
]
, states
that any execution of a physical system that can
be observed by a single observer is an interval order.
Interval order executions are typically generated
after splitting each action in the model of a
concurrent system into a ‘begin’ and an ‘end’ actions
and, after executing the modified model
using sequential semantics, deriving
the corresponding interval orders.
However, this can also be done directly, i.e., without
splitting actions, as shown in
[
13
]
.

\diamond

In this paper, we will introduce a new partial order model of concurrent behaviours,
called quasi-stratified orders, which
lies in-between the stratified and interval orders (in the sense presented in Examples
2.2
and
3.5
).
The motivation comes from
a realisation that in many systems (such as distributed transaction systems) a base activity
can exist so that other (sub-activities) are executed and completed over its lifetime.

2.2
Relational structures for specifying system executions

Concurrent system behaviours can be specified using relational structures.
Here we consider relational structures with two relationships ‘generating’ behaviours
consistent with these two relations.

Throughout the paper, a
structure
is a triple
\mathit{rs}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle
,
where
\prec
and
\sqsubset

are two binary relations over a finite domain
\Delta
.
Moreover, if both relations are irreflexive,
then
\mathit{rs}
is a
relational structure
.
We also use

\Delta_{\mathit{rs}}
,
\prec_{\mathit{rs}}
, and
\sqsubset_{\mathit{rs}}
to denote the components of
\mathit{rs}
.
The notations below can be used to add new domain elements and relationships: c

\mathit{rs}[x]=\langle\Delta\cup\{x\},\prec,\sqsubset\rangle~~~~~~~~\mathit{rs}{[x\prec y]}=\langle\Delta,\prec\cup\{\langle x,y\rangle\},\sqsubset\rangle~~~~~~~~\mathit{rs}{[x\sqsubset y]}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\cup\{\langle x,y\rangle\}\rangle

where in the first case
x\notin\Delta
while in the other two cases
x,y\in\Delta
.

In the approach followed in this paper, relational structures are
used to specify some relevant
relationships between events represented by domain elements.
Intuitively,
x\prec y
can be interpreted as stating that the execution of
x
precedes the
execution of
y
, whereas
x\sqsubset y
can be interpreted as stating that the execution of
x
precedes or is simultaneous with the
execution of
y
(i.e.,
y
does not precede
x
)

1

1

It is never the case that
x
precedes
y
while
y
weakly precedes
x
. Hence,
\sqsubset
can be treated as the negation of the reversed
\prec
.
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a set of relational structures, and
\mathit{rs},\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
be relational structures:

•

\mathit{rs}\trianglelefteq\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
means that
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
is an extension of
\mathit{rs}
,
i.e.,
\Delta_{\mathit{rs}}=\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}}
,

\prec_{\mathit{rs}}\subseteq\prec_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}}
, and

\sqsubset_{\mathit{rs}}\subseteq\sqsubset_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}}
.

•

\operatorname{ext}_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathit{rs})=\{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mid\mathit{rs}\trianglelefteq\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\}

are the extensions of
\mathit{rs}
in
\mathcal{R}
.

•

\mathcal{R}^{\mathit{max}}=\{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}\mid\operatorname{ext}_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathit{rs}^{\prime})=\{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\}\}
are the maximal structures in
\mathcal{R}
.

•

\satmap_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathit{rs})=\operatorname{ext}_{\mathcal{R}^{\mathit{max}}}(\mathit{rs})
are
the maximal extensions (or saturations) of
\mathit{rs}
in
\mathcal{R}
.

•

\mathit{rs}|_{\Phi}=\langle\Phi,\prec|_{\Phi\times\Phi},\sqsubset|_{\Phi\times\Phi}\rangle

is the projection of
\mathit{rs}
onto
\Phi\subseteq\Delta_{\mathit{rs}}
.

•

\mathcal{R}
is projection-closed if
\mathit{rs}|_{\Phi}\in\mathcal{R}
,
for all
\mathit{rs}\in\mathcal{R}
and
\Phi\subseteq\Delta_{\mathit{rs}}
.

•

\mathcal{R}
is intersection-closed if

\langle\Delta_{\mathit{rs}}\cap\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}},\prec_{\mathit{rs}}\cap\prec_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}},\sqsubset_{\mathit{rs}}\cap\sqsubset_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathcal{R}
,

A partial order
\mathit{po}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
can be represented
in a natural way as a relational structure

\rho(\mathit{po})=\langle\Delta,\prec,\{\langle x,y\rangle\in\Delta\times\Delta\mid y\not\prec x\neq y\}\rangle,

which means that

As

are intended to represent executions specified by relational

\mathcal{R}

a key modelling question:

What relational structures
\mathcal{R}
provide a
suitable specification model for partial orders
\mathcal{X}

The answer is rather straightforward, namely all one needs to require
is that
\mathcal{R}^{\mathit{max}}=\rho(\mathcal{X})

Note that in such a case,
\mathit{rs}\in\mathcal{R}
iff

\satmap_{\mathcal{R}}(\mathit{rs})\cap\rho(\mathcal{X})\neq\varnothing
. The latter
implies that no specification is vacuous as it generates

Remark 2.4
(acyclity in execution)

For the partial order models of behaviours in Section
2.1
,
suitable relational structures
\mathit{rs}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle
satisfy
the following:

•

Total orders:
there are no events
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}(=x_{1})

such that
x_{i}(\prec\cup\sqsubset)x_{i+1}
,
for every
1\leq i<n
.

•

Stratified orders:
there are no events
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}(=x_{1})

such that: (i)
x_{i}(\prec\cup\sqsubset)x_{i+1}
,
for every
1\leq i<n
; and (ii)
there is

such that
x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}
.

•

Interval orders:
there are no events
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}(=x_{1})

such that: (i)
x_{i}(\prec\cup\sqsubset)x_{i+1}
,
for every
1\leq i<n
;
and (ii)

x_{i-1}\prec x_{i}~\vee~x_{i}\prec x_{i+1}
, for all
1<i\leq n
,
and
x_{n-1}\prec x_{n}~\vee~x_{1}\prec x_{2}
.

Each case above captures some kind of ‘acyclicity’,
or perhaps more accurately, ‘constrained cyclicity’.
Such a property is linked to the absence of substructures of
\mathit{rs}

which are deemed ‘unacceptable’ or ‘forbidden’ in a given execution model. For example,
if

then no total order exists in which
x
does not precede
y

and, at the same time,
y
does not precede
x
. However, such a cyclic relationship
is allowed in the stratified order case as one can execute
x
and
y
simultaneously.

Quasi-stratified orders

As far as we are aware
the next definition introduces a new class of partial
orders, positioned in-between stratified and
interval orders.

Definition 3.1
(
qs
-order)

Quasi-stratified orders
(or
qs
-orders)
are the smallest set
QSO
such that
\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\in\textit{QSO}
and the following hold,
for all
\textit{qso}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
and
\textit{qso}^{\prime}=\langle\Delta^{\prime},\prec^{\prime}\rangle
in
QSO
:

•

If
x\notin\Delta
,
then
\textit{qso}[x]=\langle\Delta\cup\{x\},\prec\rangle
belongs to
QSO
.

•

If
\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}=\varnothing
, then

\textit{qso}\circ\textit{qso}^{\prime}=\langle\Delta\cup\Delta^{\prime},(\prec\cup\prec^{\prime})\cup(\Delta\times\Delta^{\prime})\rangle
belongs to
QSO
.

Moreover,
\textit{qso}\in\textit{QSO}
is a
qso
-stratum
if there are
\textit{qso}^{\prime}\in\textit{QSO}
and
x
such that
\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}[x]
.

\diamond

Proposition 3.2

Let
\textit{qso}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle\in\textit{QSO}
. Then:

1.

\textit{qso}\circ\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\circ\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}
.

2.

\textit{qso}|_{\Phi}=\langle\Phi,\prec|_{\Phi\times\Phi}\rangle\in\textit{QSO}
, for every
\Phi\subseteq\Delta
;
i.e.,
qs
-orders are projection-closed.

3.

qso
is a
qso
-stratum iff

\textit{qso}\neq\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
and there is

x\in\Delta_{\textit{qso}}
such that

x\not\prec_{\textit{qso}}z\not\prec_{\textit{qso}}x
, for every
z\in\Delta_{\textit{qso}}\setminus\{x\}
.

4.

If
\textit{qso}\neq\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
, then there is a unique
sequence
\textit{qso}_{1},\dots,\textit{qso}_{n}
(
n\geq 1
)
of
qso
-strata
such that
\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}_{1}\circ\dots\circ\textit{qso}_{n}
.

Proof.

All parts follows directly from Definition
3.1
. We only note that
‘
\circ
’ is an associative operation, and

(\textit{qso}^{\prime}\circ\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime})|_{\Psi}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}|_{\Psi\cap\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime}}}\circ\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}|_{\Psi\cap\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}}}
,
for all
\textit{qso}^{\prime},\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}\in\textit{QSO}
with disjoint domains and

\Psi\subseteq\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime}}\cup\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}}
.

\Box

The following result provides an axiomatic characterisation of
qs
-orders,
similar to those given for stratified and interval orders.

Theorem 3.3

Let
\mathit{rs}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
, where
\prec
is a binary relation over
a finite set
\Delta
.
Then
\mathit{rs}\in\textit{QSO}
iff the following hold, for all
x,y,z,t\in\Delta
:

\begin{array}[]{lll@{~~~}c@{~~~}l@{~~~~~}l@{~~~}lr@{~~~}c@{~~~}l@{~~~~~}l@{~~~}l}\textsc{qso{:}1}&:&x\not\prec x\\
\textsc{qso{:}2}&:&x\prec y\land z\prec t&\Longrightarrow&x\prec t\land z\prec y\,~\lor~\,x\prec z\land x\prec t\,~\lor~\,z\prec x\land z\prec y\,~\lor~\,t\prec y\land z\prec y\,~\lor~\,y\prec t\land x\prec t\end{array}

Proof.

(
\Longrightarrow
)
We proceed by induction on the derivation of
\mathit{rs}\in\textit{QSO}
(see Definition
3.1
).

First, we observe that
\mathit{rs}=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
trivially satisfies
qso:1,2
.
In the inductive case, we consider the following two cases.

Case 1:

\mathit{rs}=\textit{qso}[x]
, where
\textit{qso}\in\textit{QSO}
and
x\notin\Delta
. Then, by the induction
hypothesis,
qso
satisfies
qso:1,2
. Hence,

\mathit{rs}
also satisfies
qso:1,2
as we neither introduced nor
removed any relationships.

Case 2:

\mathit{rs}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}\circ\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\textit{qso}^{\prime}=\langle\Delta^{\prime},\prec^{\prime}\rangle\in\textit{QSO}
and

\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}=\langle\Delta^{\prime\prime},\prec^{\prime\prime}\rangle\in\textit{QSO}
satisfy

\Delta^{\prime}\cap\Delta^{\prime\prime}=\varnothing
.
Then we may additionally assume that
\Delta^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\neq\Delta^{\prime\prime}
as
\textit{qso}^{\prime}=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle

(or
\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
) implies
\mathit{rs}=\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}
(resp.
\mathit{rs}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}
). As a result, by the
induction hypothesis, both
\textit{qso}^{\prime}
and
\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}
satisfy
qso:1,2
.
Hence,
\mathit{rs}
satisfies
qso:1
as we introduced new relationships only between disjoint sets
\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime\prime}
.

To show that
qso:2
is satisfied by
\mathit{rs}
, we take any
x,y,z,t\in\Delta^{\prime}\cup\Delta^{\prime\prime}
such that
x\prec y
and
z\prec t
.
As

y\in\Delta^{\prime}
/
t\in\Delta^{\prime}
/
x\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
/
z\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}

implies

x\in\Delta^{\prime}
/
z\in\Delta^{\prime}
/
y\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
/
t\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
, respectively,
we have the following cases to consider (after omitting the symmetric ones):

•

x,y,z,t\in\Delta^{\prime}
or
x,y,z,t\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
. Then

qso:2
is satisfied as
it holds for both
\textit{qso}^{\prime}
and
\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}
.

•

x\in\Delta^{\prime}
and
y,z,t\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
. Then we have
x\prec z
and
x\prec t
.

•

x,y,z\in\Delta^{\prime}
and
t\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
. Then
we have
z\prec y
and
t\prec y
.

•

x,y\in\Delta^{\prime}
and
z,t\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
. Then
we have
x\prec z
and
x\prec t
.

•

x,z\in\Delta^{\prime}
and
y,t\in\Delta^{\prime\prime}
. Then

x\prec t
and
z\prec y
.

Hence
\mathit{rs}
satisfies
qso:2
.

(
\Longleftarrow
)
Suppose that
\mathit{rs}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
satisfies
qso:1,2
.
We first observe that
\mathit{rs}
is an interval order (and so also a partial order).
Indeed, since
qso:1

holds for
\mathit{rs}
,
all we need to show is that
x\prec y\wedge z\prec t
implies
x\prec t\wedge z\prec y
.
And this, clearly, follows from
qso:2
.

We then prove
the (
\Longleftarrow
) implication by induction on
|\Delta|
.
To start with, if
|\Delta|\leq 1
then, by
qso:1
,
\mathit{rs}\in\textit{QSO}
.
In the inductive case
|\Delta|>1
and we consider the following two cases.

Case 1:

There is
x\in\Delta
such that
x\not\prec y\not\prec x
,
for every
y\in\Delta
.
Then, by the induction hypothesis,

\mathit{rs}^{\prime}=\langle\Delta\setminus\{x\},\prec\rangle
is an
qs
-order.
Moreover,
\mathit{rs}=\mathit{rs}^{\prime}[x]
and so,
by Definition
3.1
,
\mathit{rs}
also is an
qs
-order.

Case 2:

For every
x\in\Delta
there is
y\in\Delta
such that
x\prec y
or
y\prec x
(
\dagger
).
Then, let

M=\{x\in\Delta\mid\neg\exists y\in\Delta:~y\prec x\}~~\mbox{and}~~B=\{x\in M\mid\forall z\in M:~|N_{x}|\leq|N_{z}|\}\;,

where
N_{z}=\{w\in\Delta\mid z\prec w\}
, for every
z\in\Delta
.
We observe that
M\neq\varnothing
as
\mathit{rs}
is a finite partial
order (note that
po:2
follows from
qso:1,2
),
and so also
B\neq\varnothing
.
Moreover, for every
z\in M
, we have
N_{z}\neq\varnothing
, by

z\in M
and (
\dagger
).
(Intuitively, from the set
M
of topologically minimal elements
of
\mathit{rs}
we distinguished the minimal ones w.r.t. the out-degree; the set of such
elements is the root of the
qs
-sequence corresponding to
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
defined below.)

We first observe that
N_{x}\subseteq N_{w}
, for all
w\in M
and
x\in B
(
\ddagger
).
Indeed, if this is not true, then
N_{x}\setminus N_{w}\neq\varnothing
. Moreover, by the definition of
B
,

|N_{x}|\leq|N_{w}|
. Hence,
N_{w}\setminus N_{x}\neq\varnothing
.
Therefore, there are
y,t\in\Delta
such that

x\prec y
,
w\prec t
,
x\not\prec t
, and
w\not\prec y
,
yielding to a contradiction with
qso:2
.

Let
x
be any element of
B\neq\varnothing
,
N=N_{x}
, and
P=\Delta\setminus N_{x}
.

Moreover,
(N\times P)\cap\prec=\varnothing
(
\dagger\dagger
). Indeed, if
a\in N
and
b\in P

such that
a\prec b
, then
x\prec a\prec b
, and so
x\prec b
.
This, however, means that
b\in N
, a contradiction.

Both
P
and
N
are nonempty disjoint sets (
P\neq\varnothing
as
x\notin N_{x}
).
Hence,
by the induction hypothesis,

\mathit{rs}^{\prime}=\mathit{rs}|_{P}
and
\mathit{rs}^{\prime\prime}=\mathit{rs}|_{N}
are
qs
-orders.

Suppose that
\mathit{rs}\neq\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\circ\mathit{rs}^{\prime\prime}
.
Then, by Definition
3.1
and (
\dagger\dagger
), there are
a\in P
and
b\in N
such that
a\not\prec b
.
By (
\ddagger
),
a\in P\setminus M
and, since
\prec
is transitive, there is
c\in M
such that
c\prec a
.
Also,
x\prec b
. Therefore, by
qso:2
, one of the following five cases must hold.

•

x\prec a
and
c\prec b
. Then
a\in N
, a contradiction.

•

x\prec c
and
x\prec a
. Then
c\notin M
, a contradiction.

•

c\prec x
and
c\prec b
. Then
x\notin M
, a contradiction.

•

b\prec a
and
x\prec a
. Then
a\in N
, a contradiction.

•

a\prec b
and
c\prec b
. Then we obtain a contradiction as we assumed
a\not\prec b
.

As a result,
\mathit{rs}=\mathit{rs}^{\prime}\circ\mathit{rs}^{\prime\prime}
and so, by Definition
3.1
,
\mathit{rs}
is a
qs
-order.

\Box

We can now show that

Proposition 3.4

All stratified orders are

qs
-orders, and all
qs
-orders are interval orders.

Proof.

To show the first inclusion,
let
\mathit{rs}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle\neq\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
be a stratified order and

\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{n}
(
n\geq 1
) be a partition of
\Delta
such that

\prec
is equal to
\bigcup_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\Delta_{i}\times\Delta_{j}
.
Let
\mathit{rs}_{i}=\langle\Delta_{i},\varnothing\rangle
, for every
1\leq i\leq n
.
By Definition
3.1
,
\mathit{rs}_{i}\in\textit{QSO}
, for every
1\leq i\leq n
.
Hence, if
n=1
, we obtain
\mathit{rs}=\mathit{rs}_{1}\in\textit{QSO}
.
Otherwise, again by Definition
3.1
,

\mathit{rs}=(\dots(\mathit{rs}_{1}\circ\mathit{rs}_{2})\circ\dots)\circ\mathit{rs}_{n}\in\textit{QSO}
.

The second inclusion
follows directly from Theorem
3.3
(
\Rightarrow
) and
the observation made at the beginning of the proof of
Theorem
3.3
(
\Leftarrow
).

\Box

In this section, we introduced quasi-stratified orders which, we believe,
provide a faithful representation of behaviours for a range of concurrent computing
systems. In fact, we provided two different ways of defining
qs
-orders, and
the decision which one to use will typically depend on
the context.

4
Quasi-stratified sequences

To explain the rationale behind the adjective in ‘quasi-stratified order’,
we need an auxiliary notion of
a
quasi-stratified sequences
(or
qs
-sequences).

Definition 4.1
(
qs
-sequence and
qss
-stratum)

Quasi-stratified sequences
(or
qs
-sequences)
and
quasi-stratified sequence strata
(or
qss
-strata)
are the smallest sets,
QSS
and
QSSS
, respectively,
such that the following hold
(below we also define the domain
\delta_{\textit{qss}}
of each
\textit{qss}\in\textit{QSS}
):

•

\Delta\in\textit{QSS}\cap\textit{QSSS}
, for every finite nonempty set
\Delta
.
Moreover,
\delta_{\Delta}=\Delta
.

•

\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}\in\textit{QSS}
,
for every
n\geq 2
and all
qss
-strata
\textit{qss}_{1},\dots,\textit{qss}_{n}

with mutually disjoint domains.
Moreover,

\delta_{\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}}=\delta_{\textit{qsss}_{1}}\cup\dots\cup\delta_{\textit{qss}_{n}}
.

•

\langle\Delta,\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}\rangle\in\textit{QSS}\cap\textit{QSSS}
,
for every
n\geq 2
, every finite nonempty set
\Delta
,
and all
qss
-strata
\textit{qss}_{1},\dots,\textit{qss}_{n}

with mutually disjoint domains satisfying

\Delta\cap\delta_{\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}}=\varnothing
.
Moreover,

\delta_{\langle\Delta,\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}\rangle}=\Delta\cup\delta_{\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}}
.

\diamond

Clearly,
qss
-strata are also
qs
-sequences.
Using a ‘tree-based methapor’, each
qs
-sequence can be thought of as an ordered forest of trees whose
nodes are disjoint nonempty finite sets; moreover,
each node either has no direct descendants or the direct descendants form an ordered sequence of
at least two such trees.

One can verify that
qs
-sequences are in a strict correspondence with
nonempty
qs
-orders, through the mapping
\mathfrak{G}
defined thus
(see Definitions
3.1
and
4.1
):

1.

\mathfrak{G}(\Delta)=\langle\Delta,\varnothing\rangle
.

2.

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n})=\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{1})\circ\dots\circ\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{n})
.

3.

\mathfrak{G}(\langle\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\},\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}\rangle)=\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n})[x_{1}]\dots[x_{k}]
.

Note that in (2) and (3) above the order of application of the operations
on the rhs does not matter.

We first show the soundness of the mapping
\mathfrak{G}
.

Proposition 4.2

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})\in\textit{QSO}
and
\delta_{\textit{qss}}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
,
for every
qs
-sequence
qss
.
Moreover,

qss
is a
qss
-stratum iff

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})
is a
qso
-stratum.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the syntax of
qs
-sequences.

In the base case,
\textit{qss}=\Delta=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
(
n\geq 1
). Then

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})=(\Delta,\varnothing)=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle[x_{1}]\dots[x_{n}]\in\textit{QSO}
.
Moreover,
\delta_{\textit{qss}}=\Delta=\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
.

In the inductive case we have the following:

Case 1:

\textit{qss}=\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}
(
n\geq 2
).
Then, by the induction hypothesis,
\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{i})\in\textit{QSO}

and
\delta_{\textit{qsss}_{i}}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{i})}
,
for every
1\leq i\leq n
. Hence, we also have

\delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{i})}\cap\delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{j})}
,
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
.
Therefore, by
Definitions
3.1
and
4.1
,

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})=\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{1})\circ\dots\circ\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qsss}_{n})\in\textit{QSO}

and
\delta_{\textit{qss}}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
.

Case 2:

\textit{qss}=\langle\Delta,\textit{qss}^{\prime}\rangle

and
\Delta=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
, where
k\geq 1
.
Then, by the induction hypothesis,
\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime})\in\textit{QSO}

and
\delta_{\textit{qss}^{\prime}}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime})}
.
Hence,
\Delta\cap\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime})}=\varnothing
.
Therefore, by Definition
3.1
,

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})=\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime})[x_{1}]\dots[x_{k}]\in\textit{QSO}

and
\delta_{\textit{qss}}=\Delta\cup\delta_{\textit{qss}^{\prime}}=\Delta\cup\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime})}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
.

To show the second part, suppose that
qss
is a
qss
-stratum.
Then there is
x\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
such that

x\not\prec_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}z\not\prec_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}x
, for all

z\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}\setminus\{x\}
.
Hence
\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})
is a
qso
-stratum.
Conversely, if
\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})
is a
qso
-stratum, then
there is
x\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
such that

x\not\prec_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}z\not\prec_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}x
, for all

z\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}\setminus\{x\}
.
Hence,
qss
has to be of the form
\langle\Delta,\textit{qss}^{\prime}\rangle
, and so

qss
is a
qss
-stratum.

\Box

Proposition 4.3

\mathfrak{G}:\textit{QSS}\to\textit{QSO}\setminus\{\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\}

is an injective mapping.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.2
,
\mathfrak{G}
is well-defined
(note that
\Delta_{\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle}=\varnothing
).

Suppose that
\textit{qss},\textit{qss}^{\prime}\in\textit{QSS}
are such that

\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})=\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime})=\textit{qso}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle\in\textit{QSO}
. To show

\textit{qss}=\textit{qss}^{\prime}
we proceed by induction of
|\Delta|
.

In the base case,
|\Delta|=1
, we have
\textit{qss}=\textit{qss}^{\prime}=\Delta
.
In the inductive case, suppose that
|\Delta|>1
and consider two cases.

Case 1:
There is

x\in\Delta
such that

x\not\prec z\not\prec x
, for all

z\in\Delta\setminus\{x\}
.
Then,
\textit{qss}=\langle\Delta_{o},\textit{qss}_{o}\rangle
and
\textit{qss}^{\prime}=\langle\Delta^{\prime}_{o},\textit{qss}^{\prime}_{o}\rangle
.
Moreover,

\Delta_{o}=\{y\in\Delta\mid\forall z\in\Delta\setminus\{y\}:y\not\prec z\not\prec\}=\Delta^{\prime}_{o}
.
We therefore have
\Delta_{o}=\Delta^{\prime}_{o}
and
\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}_{o})=\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}^{\prime}_{o})
.
Thus, by the induction hypothesis,
\textit{qss}_{o}=\textit{qss}^{\prime}_{o}
, and so
\textit{qss}=\textit{qss}^{\prime}
.

\Box

To show a one-to-one relationship between nonempty
qso
-orders
and
qs
-sequences, we introduce a mapping

\mathfrak{I}:\textit{QSO}\setminus\{\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\}\to\textit{QSS}

such that,
for every
\textit{qso}\in\textit{QSO}\setminus\{\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\}
,
\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso})

is defined as follows:

•

If
qso
is a
qs
-stratum then

\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}[x_{1}]\dots[x_{n}]
, where
n\geq 1
.
Then
\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso})=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
if
\textit{qso}^{\prime}=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
,
and

\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso})=\langle\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso}^{\prime})\rangle
if
\textit{qso}^{\prime}\neq\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
.

•

If
qso
is not a
qs
-stratum, then there are
qso
-strata

\textit{qso}_{1},\dots,\textit{qso}_{n}
(
n\geq 2
) such that

\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}_{1}\circ\dots\circ\textit{qso}_{n}
.
Then

\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso})=\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso}_{1})\dots\mathfrak{I}(\textit{qso}_{n})
.

Theorem 4.4

\mathfrak{G}:\textit{QSS}\to\textit{QSO}\setminus\{\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\}
and

\mathfrak{I}:\textit{QSO}\setminus\{\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\}\to\textit{QSS}

are inverse bijections.

Proof.

By Propositions
4.2
and
4.3
, it suffices to show
that
\mathfrak{I}(\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss}))=\textit{qss}
, for every
\textit{qss}\in\textit{QSS}
.

\Box

Remark 4.5
(

Using the notion of

\textit{qss}=\textit{qsss}_{1}\dots\textit{qsss}_{n}
(

) such that each
\textit{qsss}_{i}
is a

,

\prec=\prec_{\mathfrak{G}(\textit{qss})}
.

Intuitively, this means that the intervals corresponding to events can be
partitioned into
qs
-strata (each being represented by
a tree whose root is the ‘base’ of a stratum).
Note that if
\textit{qsss}_{1}=\Delta_{1},\dots,\textit{qsss}_{n}=\Delta_{n}
, then the above
coincides with
the alternative definition of stratified orders (see Section
2.1
).

Quasi-stratified structures

We are now ready to introduce relational structures which will be suitable
as specifications of concurrent behaviours modelled by
qs
-orders.
Before that we introduce auxiliary notions.

Definition 5.1
(
csc
-subset)

Let
\mathit{rs}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle
be a structure and
\Phi\subseteq\Delta
be a nonempty set.

•

\Phi
is a
combined strongly connected subset
(or
csc
-subset)
if

\langle\Phi,(\sqsubset{\cup}\prec)|_{\Phi\times\Phi}\rangle

is a strongly connected directed graph.

•

A
pre-dominant
of
\Phi

is any

such that
there is no
y\in\Phi
satisfying
x\prec y
or
y\prec x
.
We denote this by
x\in\textit{predmt}_{\mathit{rs}}(\Phi)
.

All
csc
-subsets of
\mathit{rs}
are denoted by
\textit{CSCS}(\mathit{rs})
, and
all
csc
-subsets of
\mathit{rs}
without
any pre-dominants by
\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})
.
\diamond

Intuitively, a
csc
-subset with pre-dominant
x
is a potential
candidate for a nested stratum where
x
belongs to the base (cf. Remark
4.5
).

Definition 5.2
(
qsa
-structure)

A structure
is
quasi-stratified acyclic
(or
qs
-acyclic) if it has no

csc
-subset without pre-dominant.
Then, a
quasi-stratified acyclic structure
(or
qsa
-structure)
is a relational structure which is
qs
-acyclic.
All
qsa
-structures are denoted by
QSAS
.

\diamond

In the context of the discussion on ‘acyclicity’ in Remark
2.4
,
in the case of
qsa
-structures, the role of ‘forbidden’
cyclic substructures is played by

csc
-subsets without pre-dominants. The reason why such substructures are
inadmissible can be explained in the following way.
Suppose that
\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})
and
qso
is a
qs
-order execution
in which all events
\Phi

By definition,
qso
is either of the form
\textit{qso}^{\prime}[x]
or of the form

\textit{qso}^{\prime}\circ\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}
. The first case is not possible as then

would be

\Phi
, contradicting
\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})
.
In the second case,
all the events in
\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime}}
must precede all the events in
\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}}
,
but then, by
\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})
and the strong connectivity property,
there is a pair of events,
x\in\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime}}
and
y\in\Delta_{\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}}
,
such that
y\sqsubset_{\mathit{rs}}x
or
y\prec_{\mathit{rs}}x
, yielding to a contradiction with
x\prec_{\textit{qso}}y
.

We end this section providing some basic properties of relational structures related with quasi-stratified acyclicity.

Proposition 5.3

Let
\mathit{rs}
and
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}

\mathit{rs}\trianglelefteq\mathit{rs}^{\prime}

\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})\subseteq\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs}^{\prime})

\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs}|_{\Phi})\subseteq\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})

(1) follows directly from the definitions and the fact that
adding additional arcs maintains the property of being a strongly connected subgraph,

(2) follows from from the fact the all relationships
between the elements of
\Phi

in

\mathit{rs}
.

\Box

Proposition 5.4

qsa

It follows directly from Proposition

.

Definition 6.1

A

Theorem 6.4

\textit{QSMS}=\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{max}}
.

Proof.

(\subseteq)

Let
\textit{qsms}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle\in\textit{QSMS}
and
\textit{qso}=\langle\Delta,\prec\rangle
.
By Proposition
6.3
,
\textit{qso}\in\textit{QSO}
.
We first show that
\textit{qsms}\in\textit{QSAS}
, proceeding by induction on structure of

qso
(see Definition
3.1
).
If
\textit{qso}=\langle\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle
then, clearly,
\textit{qsms}\in\textit{QSAS}
.
In the inductive step, we consider two cases.

Case 1:

\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}[x]
, where
\textit{qso}^{\prime}\in\textit{QSO}
. Then, by the induction hypothesis,

\textit{qsms}^{\prime}=\textit{qsms}|_{\Delta\setminus\{x\}}\in\textit{QSAS}
.
We then observe that, by Proposition
6.3
,

\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms})\setminus\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms}^{\prime})=\{\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms})\mid x\in\Phi\}
.
And so,
x\in\textit{predmt}_{\textit{qsms}}(\Phi)
, for every

\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms})\setminus\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms}^{\prime})
. Hence
\textit{qsms}\in\textit{QSAS}

Case 2:

\textit{qso}=\textit{qso}^{\prime}\circ\textit{qso}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}}\neq\varnothing\neq\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime\prime}}
.
Then, by the induction hypothesis, we have
\textit{qsms}^{\prime}=\textit{qsms}|_{\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}}}\in\textit{QSAS}

and
\textit{qsms}^{\prime\prime}=\textit{qsms}|_{\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime\prime}}}\in\textit{QSAS}
.
Moreover,
(\prec\cup\sqsubset)\cap(\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime\prime}}\times\Delta_{\mathit{rs}^{\prime}})=\varnothing
,
which means that

\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms})=\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms}^{\prime})\cup\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsms}^{\prime\prime})
.

Hence
\textit{qsms}\in\textit{QSAS}
. Moreover, by Proposition
6.3
, for all
x\neq y\in\Delta
,

x\not\prec y
implies
\{x,y\}\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\textit{qsms}{[x\prec y]})
and

x\not\sqsubset y
implies
\{x,y\}\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\textit{qsms}{[x\sqsubset y]})
.
Hence
\textit{qsms}\in\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{max}}
.

(\supseteq)

Since
qsa
-structures are finite and
we have already proved that
\textit{QSMS}\subseteq\textit{QSAS}
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\textit{qsas})\neq\varnothing
,
for every
\textit{qsas}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle\in\textit{QSAS}
.
We proceed by induction on the size of the domain of
qsas
.
Clearly,
\langle\varnothing,\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\in\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\langle\varnothing,\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle)

and
\langle\{x\},\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle\in\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\langle\{x\},\varnothing,\varnothing\rangle)
. In
the inductive step
|\Delta|>1
and we consider two cases.

Case 1:

\Delta\in\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsas})
. Then, by
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
, there is
x\in\textit{predmt}_{\textit{qsas}}(\Delta)
.
Let
\Delta^{\prime}=\Delta\setminus\{x\}
.
As
qsa
-structures are projection-closed, by Proposition
5.4
,
\textit{qsas}|_{\Delta^{\prime}}\in\textit{QSAS}
.
Hence, by the induction hypothesis, there is

\langle\Delta^{\prime},\prec^{\prime},\sqsubset^{\prime}\rangle\in\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\textit{qsas}|_{\Delta^{\prime}})
.
It is straightforward to check that

\langle\Delta,\prec^{\prime},\sqsubset^{\prime}\cup(\Delta^{\prime}\times\{x\})\cup(\{x\}\times\Delta^{\prime})\rangle

belongs to
\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\textit{qsas})
.

Case 2:

\Delta\notin\textit{CSCS}(\textit{qsas})
. Then
\Delta
can be partitioned
as
\Delta^{\prime}\uplus\Delta^{\prime\prime}
in such a way that
we have
(\Delta^{\prime\prime}\times\Delta^{\prime})\cap(\prec\cup\sqsubset)=\varnothing
.
As
qsa
-structures are projection-closed,
\textit{qsas}|_{\Delta^{\prime}}\in\textit{QSAS}
and
\textit{qsas}|_{\Delta^{\prime\prime}}\in\textit{QSAS}
.
Hence, by the induction hypothesis, there are

\textit{qsms}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\textit{qsas}|_{\Delta^{\prime}})
and

\textit{qsms}^{\prime\prime}\in\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\textit{qsas}|_{\Delta^{\prime\prime}})
.
It is straightforward to check that

\langle\Delta,(\prec_{\textit{qsms}^{\prime}}\cup\prec_{\textit{qsms}^{\prime\prime}})\cup(\Delta^{\prime}\times\Delta^{\prime\prime}),(\sqsubset_{\textit{qsms}^{\prime}}\cup\sqsubset_{\textit{qsms}^{\prime\prime}})\cup(\Delta^{\prime}\times\Delta^{\prime\prime})\rangle

belongs to
\operatorname{ext}_{\textit{QSMS}}(\textit{qsas})
.

\Box

)

)

Proposition 6.6

\textit{qsas}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle\in\textit{QSAS}
and

. Then:

1.

implies
\textit{qsas}{[y\sqsubset x]}\notin\textit{QSAS}
.

2.

x\sqsubset y
implies
\textit{qsas}{[y\prec x]}\notin\textit{QSAS}
.

3.

\textit{qsas}{[x\prec y]}\in\textit{QSAS}

or

\textit{qsas}{[y\sqsubset x]}\in\textit{QSAS}
.

4.

\textit{qsas}{[x\prec y]}\notin\textit{QSAS}

\textit{qsas}{[y\sqsubset x]}\in\textit{QSAS}

\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}{[y\sqsubset x]})=\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})
.

\textit{qsas}{[x\sqsubset y]}\notin\textit{QSAS}

implies

\textit{qsas}{[y\prec x]}\in\textit{QSAS}

and
\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}{[y\prec x]})=\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})
.

Proof.

(1) follows from
\{x,y\}\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\textit{qsas}{[y\sqsubset x]})
.

(2) follows from
\{x,y\}\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\textit{qsas}{[y\prec x]})
.

(3)
We will proceed by contradiction. Suppose that

\mathit{rs}=\textit{qsas}{[x\prec y]}\notin\textit{QSAS}
and

\mathit{rs}^{\prime}=\textit{qsas}{[y\sqsubset x]}\notin\textit{QSAS}
.
As both
\mathit{rs}
and
\mathit{rs}^{\prime}
are relational structures,
there are
\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})
and
\Psi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs}^{\prime})
.
From
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
it follows that
x,y\in\Phi\cap\Psi
. Moreover,

\Phi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs})
implies
that there is a path, using
\prec
and
\sqsubset
, from
y
to
x
in
qsas
going through the elements of
\Phi
,
and

\Psi\in\textit{CSCS}^{\dagger}(\mathit{rs}^{\prime})
implies

\prec
and
\sqsubset
, from
y
to
x
in
qsas
going through the elements of
\Psi
.

qsa
-structures can be regarded as
suitable specifications of

In this section, we will look closer at

which are particularly suitable for, e.g., property verification or

In the general theory expounded in

Definition 7.1
(closed
qsa
-structure)

A
qsa
-structure
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
is
closed
if

\textit{qsas}=\langle\Delta_{\textit{qsas}},\prec_{\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})},\sqsubset_{\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})}\rangle\}\;

where

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\makebox[85.35826pt][r]{\mbox{$\prec_{\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})}$}}&=&\bigcap\{\prec_{\textit{qsms}}\mid\textit{qsms}\in\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})\}\\
\sqsubset_{\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})}&=&\bigcap\{\sqsubset_{\textit{qsms}}\mid\textit{qsms}\in\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})\}\;.\end{array}

All closed
qsa
-structures are denoted by
\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{clo}}
.

\diamond

From the general results presented in
[
7
]
(Prop.7.5),
and the fact that
QSAS
is a
set of intersection closed relational structures, it follows that there is a
structure closure

mapping
\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}:\textit{QSAS}\to\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{clo}}

such that the following hold.

•

A closed
qsa
-structure is the largest
qsa
-structure
which generates a given set of
qsm
-structures, i.e., for all
\mathit{rs}\in\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{clo}}
and
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
:

\mathit{rs}\trianglelefteq\textit{qsas}~\wedge~\mathit{rs}\neq\textit{qsas}\implies\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})\neq\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\mathit{rs})\;.

(2)

•

\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}
is a closure mapping in the usual sense, i.e., for every
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
:

\textit{qsas}\trianglelefteq\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})=\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}))\;.

(3)

•

Structure closure preserves the
generated
qsm
-structures, i.e., for every
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
:

\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})=\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}))\;.

(4)

•

Generating the same
qsm
-structures is equivalent to having the same closure, i.e., for all
\textit{qsas},\textit{qsas}^{\prime}\in\textit{QSAS}
:

\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})=\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}^{\prime})\iff\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})=\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}^{\prime})\;.

(5)

•

Structure closure is monotonic, i.e., for all
\textit{qsas},\textit{qsas}^{\prime}\in\textit{QSAS}
:

\textit{qsas}\trianglelefteq\textit{qsas}^{\prime}\implies\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})\trianglelefteq\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas}^{\prime})\;.

(6)

•

The structure closure of a given
\textit{qsas}\in\textit{QSAS}
is given by:

\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})=\langle\Delta_{\textit{qsas}},\prec_{\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})},\sqsubset_{\satmap_{\textit{QSAS}}(\textit{qsas})}\rangle\;.

(7)

The above, generic, characterisation of closed
qsa
-structures
is not a practical one because it directly refers
to potentially (very) large set of maximal extensions of
qsa
-structures.
To address this, we will now present an alternative algebraic characterisation
of
\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{clo}}
and
\cls_{\textit{QSAS}}
.
We start by introducing an alternative definition of
\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{clo}}

which will then be shown to coincide with
\textit{QSAS}^{\mathit{clo}}
.

Definition 7.2
(
qsc
-structure)

A
quasi-stratified closed structure
(or
qsc
-structure)
is a structure

\textit{qscs}=\langle\Delta,\prec,\sqsubset\rangle
such that the following hold, for all
x\neq y\in\Delta
:

\begin{array}[]{llr@{~~~}c@{~~~}r@{~~~~~}l@{~~~}lr@{~~~}c@{~~~}l@{~~~~~}l@{~~~}l}\textsc{qscs{:}1}&:&x\not\sqsubset x\not\prec x\\
\textsc{qscs{:}2}&:&x\prec y&\Longrightarrow&y\not\sqsubset x\\

Theorem 7.7

\operatorname{qsas2qscs}(\textit{qsas})\in\textit{QSCS}

\operatorname{qsas2qscs}

Concluding remarks

In this paper, we extended the general approach
elaborated in
[
6
,
7
]

to allow
dealing with the semantics of concurrent systems with, e.g., nested transactions.
The specification of such behaviours
was provided by relationships between events, namely
precedence

and
weak precedence
, intuitively corresponding to the ‘earlier than’ and
‘not later than’ positions in individual executions.

As far as applications are concerned,
the resulting framework has the potential of, e.g., alleviating the state space explosion problem,
which is one of the most challenging problems in the
verification of concurrent systems or optimal
(re)scheduling of transaction-based systems.

Another direction of the planned work is related to the theory of
traces
[
2
]
.
Traces that can be interpreted as sets of interval orders and
represent concurrent histories —
called
interval traces
— were
proposed and discussed in
[
11
]
.
Though
[
11
]

analysed their relationship with interval
relational structures from
[
9
,
10
]
,
the relationship with the
qsa
-structures introduced in this paper is yet to be developed.
