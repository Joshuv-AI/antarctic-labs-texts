Exact Training of Restricted Boltzmann Machines on Intrinsically Low Dimensional Data
A. Decelle, C. Furtlehner

Abstract
The restricted Boltzmann machine is a basic machine learning tool able, in principle, to model the distribution of some arbitrary dataset. Its standard training procedure appears however delicate and obscure in many respects. We bring some new insights to it by considering the situation where the data have low intrinsic dimension, offering the possibility of an exact treatment and revealing a fundamental failure of the standard training procedure. The reasons for this failure — like the occurrence of first-order phase transitions during training — are clarified thanks to a Coulomb interactions reformulation of the model. In addition a convex relaxation of the original optimization problem is formulated thereby resulting in a unique solution, obtained in precise numerical form on d = 1 , 2 d=1,2 study cases, while a constrained linear regression solution can be conjectured on the basis of an information theory argument.

Discussion.

— The physical picture of the RBM emerging here, in addition to identifying and
disentangling via Eqs. (
11
,
12
) the role played by some key factors, underlines the importance
of two distinct aspects of learning a high dimensional distribution : the ordered part corresponding
to global statistical patterns and the fluctuations around these patterns encoding possibly short range correlations or corresponding to noise.
Under a flat intrinsic space hypothesis our formalism decouples them and gives indications of how to learn them separately in order to obtain high quality models that are needed in scientific applications,
when default RBM algorithms are thwarted by low dimensional global patterns as we see in our experiments. Among many possible developments we foresee
that the “Coulomb” convex relaxation could be used to fine-tune some otherwise poorly trained RBM, and opens the intriguing
possibility of tackling unsupervised learning via regularized linear regressions.

\displaystyle m_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\lim_{N_{v}\to\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{v}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}}u_{i}^{\alpha}\tanh\Bigl(\sqrt{N_{v}}\sum_{\beta=1}^{d}u_{i}^{\beta}\mu_{\beta}\bigr),

From the small

\displaystyle=-{\cal I}[{\bf m}]+\log(2)+\frac{d}{2N_{v}}\log(2\pi),

\displaystyle=\Phi[\bm{\mu}[{\bf m}]]-{\bf m}^{T}\bm{\mu}[{\bf m}]+\log(2)+{\cal O}\Bigl(\frac{1}{N_{v}}\Bigr).

Note that practically speaking we use finite

estimates of

so that the preceding relation is in fact valid up to some

Appendix B
Longitudinal and transverse free energy

In order to disentangle the contributions of the collective modes materialized by some magnetization
{\bf m}
along some directions

after introducing the canonical free energy
\mathcal{F}[{\bf m}|\Theta]

Let us denote by

(22)

the “conditional” Hamiltonian, where

{\bf m}
independent of
{\bf s}^{\perp}

We chose it as to contain only contributions from the longitudinal magnetization, i.e. coincides with the constant part of
{\mathcal{H}}[{\bf m},{\bf s}^{\perp}|\Theta]

{\bf s}^{\perp}

Rewriting the Hamiltonian in terms of the SVD components

\eta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}[{\bf s}|\Theta]=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}}\eta_{i}s_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{h}}\log\cosh\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{v}}W_{ij}s_{i}-\theta_{j}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=N_{v}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{v}}\eta_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}-\sum_{j=1}^{N_{h}}\log\cosh\Bigl(\sqrt{N_{v}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{v}}w_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}v_{j}^{\alpha}-\theta_{j}\Bigr),

(23)

with

{\mathcal{H}}_{0}[{\bf m}|\Theta]

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[{\bf m}|\Theta]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{v}}\Bigl[{\mathcal{H}}_{0}[{\bf m}|\Theta]-\log\Bigl(\sum_{{\bf s}^{\perp}}e^{-{\mathcal{H}}[{\bf s}^{\perp}|{\bf m},\Theta]}\Bigr)\Bigr],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{v}}\Bigl[{\mathcal{H}}_{0}[{\bf m}|\Theta]-\mathcal{S}[{\bf m}]-\log\Bigl(\frac{\sum_{{\bf s}^{\perp}}e^{-{\mathcal{H}}[{\bf s}^{\perp}|{\bf m},\Theta]}}{2^{\mathcal{N}[{\bf m}]}}\Bigr)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\parallel}[{\bf m}|\Theta]

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\perp}[{\bf m}|\Theta]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{N_{v}}\log\Bigl(\frac{1}{2^{\mathcal{N}[{\bf m}]}}\sum_{{\bf s}^{\perp}}e^{-{\mathcal{H}}[{\bf s}^{\perp}|{\bf m},\Theta]}\Bigr).

The longitudinal free energy of the system is the free energy of the system for a given magnetization
{\bf m}

when the transverse magnetization and interactions among the
s_{\ell}^{\perp}
are neglected. These indeed are expected to

Appendix E
Exact Coulomb charges interpolation

In order to interpolate exactly the empirical distribution
\hat{p}
with a generalized Coulomb charges RBM based distribution it is needed to
regularize
\log(\hat{p}({\bf m}))
. This can be done in many different ways.
Consider for instance

\delta_{\epsilon}({\bf m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{\exp\bigl(-\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{2\epsilon}\bigr)}{(2\pi\epsilon)^{d/2}}

with infinitesimal
\epsilon
to approximate our point-like distribution as

\hat{p}({\bf m})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\delta_{\epsilon}({\bf m}-{\bf m}_{k}),

and let

q_{\epsilon}(k|{\bf m})=\frac{\delta_{\epsilon}({\bf m}-{\bf m}_{k})}{\sum_{l}\delta_{\epsilon}({\bf m}-{\bf m}_{l})}.

These probability weights realize a smooth partition of the space at finite
\epsilon
with Voronoi cells
{\cal R}_{k}
centered at each data point,
q_{\epsilon}(k|{\bf m})

representing the probability that
{\bf m}
belongs to
k
th cell. Equipped with this notation we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf m}}\delta_{\epsilon}({\bf m}-{\bf m}_{k})

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{k}}{\epsilon}\delta_{\epsilon}({\bf m}-{\bf m}_{k})

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf m}}q_{\epsilon}(k|{\bf m})

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{k}}{\epsilon}q_{\epsilon}(k|{\bf m})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\frac{{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{\ell}}{\epsilon}q_{\epsilon}(k|{\bf m})q_{\epsilon}(\ell|{\bf m}).

As a result we get

\nabla_{d}^{2}\log\hat{p}({\bf m})=-\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\text{Var}_{k\sim q_{\epsilon}(k|{\bf m})}[{\bf m}_{k}].

When
\epsilon
becomes small compared to nearest neighbour distances this quantity becomes constant (
=-1/\epsilon
) except on the intersections between Voronoi cells, in particular on
common faces
\mathcal{R}_{k}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\ell}

between two cells
\mathcal{R}_{k}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
it is

\nabla_{d}^{2}\log\hat{p}({\bf m})\msim_{\epsilon\to 0}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}+\frac{|{\bf m}_{k}-{\bf m}_{\ell}|}{2\epsilon}\delta({\bf m}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\ell}).

Indeed, let

\theta_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}({\bf m}_{k}+{\bf m}_{k+1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Delta_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}({\bf m}_{k+1}-{\bf m}_{k}).

For
\delta{\bf m}={\bf m}-\theta_{k}
small compared to
\Delta_{k}
we have

q_{k}(\theta_{k}+\delta{\bf m})=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[1-\tanh\Bigl(\frac{\Delta_{k}^{T}\delta{\bf m}}{2\epsilon}\Bigr)\Bigr],

leading to

\text{Var}_{k\sim q_{k}({\bf m})}[{\bf m}_{k}]=|\Delta_{k}|^{2}\Bigl[1-\tanh^{2}\Bigl(\frac{\Delta_{k}^{T}\delta{\bf m}}{2\epsilon}\bigr)\Bigr].

Since
\frac{\nu}{2}\bigl[1-\tanh^{2}(\nu x)\bigr]
tends to
\delta(x)
when
\nu\to\infty
we arrive at the statement.
As a result the distribution of charges is composed of a constant background
+
surface distribution on Voronoi cells intersections:

\rho_{\rm bulk}({\bf m})=-\frac{1}{N_{v}\epsilon}+\frac{|{\bf m}_{k}-{\bf m}_{\ell}|}{2N_{v}\epsilon}\delta({\bf m}\in\mathcal{R}_{k}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\ell}).

The Voronoi cells intersecting with the boundary of the
{\bf m}
domain induce additional surface charges which can be directly taken care of with visible bias.
Let us show how this works in
1
-d.
Let us call

V(m)=\frac{1}{N_{v}}\log\hat{p}(m)

which when regularized reads

V(m)=-\frac{1}{N_{v}}\min_{k}\frac{(m-m_{k})^{2}}{2\epsilon}.

From what precedes, this potential can be exactly decomposed onto a set of features as

V(m)=-\frac{m^{2}}{2N_{v}\epsilon}+\eta m+\sum_{j}q_{j}|m-z_{j}|

with

q_{j}=\frac{1}{2N_{v}\epsilon}(m_{j+1}-m_{j}),

while from the limit behaviour
V^{\prime}(1)
and
V^{\prime}(-1)
of
V^{\prime}(m)
we get

\eta=\frac{m_{1}+m_{N_{v}}}{2\epsilon}.

Appendix F
RBM optimization seen as a linear regression

The projection of the empirical distribution onto the space of RBM with finite number of features is classically done
by minimizing the Kullback Liebler divergence (
D_{\rm KL}
). If however our RBM space is chosen
with a high number of relevant features, we may expect the solution to be close enough to the empirical distribution so that a Fisher
metric, i.e. the infinitesimal counterpart of the
D_{\rm KL}
, evaluated from the solution or from the empirical distribution should coincide.
In that case it might be pertinent to use it instead of the
D_{\rm KL}
. Let us formalize more precisely this projection problem.
On one hand we have the empirical measure approximated by a Coulomb based RBM model of the form

p({\bf m}|\hat{\rho})=\frac{1}{Z[\hat{\rho}]}e^{-N_{v}\mathcal{F}({\bf m}|\hat{\rho})}

with

\mathcal{F}({\bf m}|\hat{\rho})=\mathcal{F}^{\perp}[{\bf m}]-\mathcal{S}[{\bf m}]-\int d{\bf m}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}({\bf m}^{\prime})K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{\prime}|),

where
\hat{\rho}({\bf m})
is, as seen in the previous Section,
the charge density concentrated on the Voronoi cells faces coming from the empirical part
\log\hat{p}({\bf m})
(including surface terms at the edge of the domain of
{\bf m}
).
On the other hand we have an RBM with a pointwise distribution of features
q({\bf n},z)
yielding a free energy of the form

\mathcal{F}({\bf m}|\Theta)=\mathcal{F}^{\perp}[{\bf m}]-\mathcal{S}[{\bf m}]-\int d{\bf m}^{\prime}\rho({\bf m}^{\prime}|\Theta)K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{\prime}|),

with

\rho({\bf m}|\Theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{h}}q_{j}\delta\bigl({\bf n}_{j}^{T}{\bf m}-z_{j}\bigr).

Our goal is to find the (positive) weights
\{q_{j},j=1,\ldots N_{h}\}
such that the following distance

D(\rho,\rho^{\prime})=\int d{\bf m}_{1}d{\bf m}_{2}\rho({\bf m}_{1})J({\bf m}_{1},{\bf m}_{2})\rho^{\prime}({\bf m}_{2}),

(29)

between
\hat{\rho}
and
\rho
is minimized. Here the relevant metric
J
is the Fisher metric defined as

\displaystyle J\bigl[{\bf m}_{1},{\bf m}_{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}_{{\bf m}\sim p({\bf m}|\Theta)}\Bigl[K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{1}|),K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{2}|)\Bigr],

\displaystyle\simeq\text{Cov}_{{\bf m}\sim\hat{p}({\bf m})}\Bigl[K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{1}|),K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}_{2}|)\Bigr],

(30)

approximated at the empirical point in last equation.
As we shall see this projection turns out to be a linear regression of the centered random variable

\displaystyle V({\bf m}|\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=\int d{\bf m}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}({\bf m}^{\prime})K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{{\bf m}\sim\hat{p}({\bf m})}\Bigl[\int d{\bf m}^{\prime}\hat{\rho}({\bf m}^{\prime})K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{\prime}|)\Bigr]

onto the set of centered random variables (the score variables associated with
\bm{q}
)

\displaystyle V_{j}({\bf m})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\int d{\bf m}^{\prime}\delta\bigl({\bf n}_{j}^{T}{\bf m}^{\prime}-z_{j}\bigr)K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{{\bf m}\sim\hat{p}({\bf m})}\Bigl[\int d{\bf m}^{\prime}\delta\bigl({\bf n}_{j}^{T}{\bf m}^{\prime}-z_{j}\bigr)K_{d}(|{\bf m}-{\bf m}^{\prime}|)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=|{\bf n}_{j}^{T}{\bf m}-z_{j}|-\mathbb{E}_{{\bf m}\sim\hat{p}({\bf m})}\Bigl[|{\bf n}_{j}^{T}{\bf m}-z_{j}|\Bigr].

\mathbb{E}_{{\bf m}\sim\hat{p}({\bf m})}
and
\text{Cov}_{{\bf m}\sim\hat{p}({\bf m})}
denote respectively empirical expectation and covariance, according to our assumption that the solution is close to
\hat{p}
.
Indeed, from elementary linear algebra, the orthogonal projection
V^{\parallel}
of a given vector
\hat{V}
