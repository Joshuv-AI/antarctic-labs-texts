On the Small Jumps of Lévy Processes and the Multivariate Dickman Distribution
Michael Grabchak, Xingnan Zhang

Abstract
We develop a Dickman approximation to the small jumps of multivariate Lévy processes and related stochastic integral processes. Further, we show that the multivariate Dickman distribution is the unique distribution satisfying a certain stability property and that a related stochastic process is self-similar. Along the way we develop applications to non-Gaussian OU-Processes, to the class of generalized multivariate gamma distributions, and to the popular Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard stochastic volatility model.



1
Introduction

Lévy and related infinitely divisible processes are used in many application areas. When simulating a Lévy process, the difficulty typically lies in the simulation of the small jumps, as
the large jumps follow a compound Poisson process, which is often easier to simulate. This has led to the study of approximations of small jumps by simpler processes. In the univariate case, an approximation of small jumps by Brownian motion was developed in
[
1
]
. This was extended to the multivariate case in
[
8
]
, see also the discussion of applications to finance in Chapter 6 of
[
9
]
. For important situations where the Brownian motion approximation fails,
[
10
]
developed an approximation by a Dickman Lévy process in the univariate case. Recently, a multivariate generalization of the Dickman distribution was introduced in
[
6
]
and
[
15
]
.

The goal of the current paper is three-fold. First, we develop a Dickman approximation to the small jumps of multivariate Lévy processes. Second, we extend these results to stochastic integral processes. Third, we show that the multivariate Dickman distribution is the unique distribution satisfying a certain stability property related to the stability of its jumps under scaling and that a related stochastic process is self-similar. To the best of our knowledge, the second and third of these were previously unknown even in the univariate case. Along the way we develop applications to non-Gaussian processes of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck-type (OU-processes), to the class of generalized multivariate gamma distributions, and to the popular Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (BNS) stochastic volatility model introduced in
[
5
]
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we review infinitely divisible distributions, Lévy processes, and stochastic integral processes and we prove a transfer theorem that allows one to transfer convergence results about sequences of Lévy processes to sequences of stochastic integral processes. This will be needed to prove our main results and may be of independent interest. In Section
3
we recall basic facts about Dickman and multivariate Dickman distributions and introduce the more general class of
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions. We then show that these are the only distributions that satisfy a certain stability property and that a related stochastic process is self-similar. In Section
4
we give our main results for approximating the small jumps of certain multivariate Lévy processes by Dickman Lévy processes and we extend these results to stochastic integral processes. In Section
5
we give an application to the simulation of generalized multivariate gamma distributions. In Section
6
we give an application to the BNS stochastic volatility model. Proofs are postponed to Section
7
.

2
Infinitely Divisible Distributions, Lévy Processes, Stochastic Integrals, and a Transfer Theorem

In which we review basic properties of infinitely divisible distributions and their associated Lévy processes, discuss stochastic integral processes, and prove a transfer theorem that allows one to transfer convergence results about sequences of Lévy processes to sequences of stochastic integral processes. The transfer theorem is needed to prove our main results and may be of independent interest.

The characteristic function of an infinitely divisible distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be written in the form
\hat{\mu}(z)=\exp\{C_{\mu}(z)\}
, where

\displaystyle C_{\mu}(z)=-\langle z,Az\rangle+i\left\langle b,z\right\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(e^{i\left\langle z,x\right\rangle}-1-i\left\langle z,x\right\rangle 1_{[|x|\leq 1]}\right)M(\mathrm{d}x),\ \ \ z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1)

A
is a
d\times d
-dimensional covariance matrix called the Gaussian part,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the shift, and
M
is the Lévy measure, which is a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

M(\{0\})=0\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|x|^{2}\wedge 1)M(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

The parameters
A
,
M
, and
b
uniquely determine this distribution and we write
\mu=\mathrm{ID}(A,M,b)
. We call
C_{\mu}
the cumulant generating function (cgf) of
\mu
. The normal distribution corresponds to the case where
M=0
and is denoted
N(b,A)
.

Associated with every infinitely divisible distribution
\mu
is a Lévy process
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
, where
X_{1}\sim\mu
. Lévy processes are characterized by independent and stationary increments, càdlàg paths, stochastic continuity, and the initial condition
X_{0}=0
with probability
1
. Standard Brownian motion is the Lévy process
\{W_{t}:t\geq 0\}
with
W_{1}\sim N(0,I_{d})
, where
I_{d}
is the
d\times d
-dimensional identity matrix. The jumps of a Lévy process are governed by its Lévy measure. Specifically, if
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is a Lévy process with
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}(A,M,b)
, then, for any
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
M(B)
is the expected number of jumps that the process has in the time interval
[0,1]
that fall inside set
B
.

A Lévy process has finite variation if and only if
A=0
and
M
satisfies the additional condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(|x|\wedge 1)M(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

(2)

Through a slight abuse of terminology, we also say that the associated distribution
\mu
has finite variation. In this case, the cgf
can be written in the form

\displaystyle C_{\mu}(z)=i\left\langle\gamma,z\right\rangle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(e^{i\left\langle z,x\right\rangle}-1\right)M(\mathrm{d}x),\ \ \ z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(3)

where
\gamma=b-\int_{|x|\leq 1}xM(\mathrm{d}x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the drift, and we write
\mu=\mathrm{ID}_{0}(M,\gamma)
. For more on infinitely divisible distributions and Lévy processes see
[
27
]
or
[
9
]
.

When discussing convergence in distribution of infinitely divisible distributions and Lévy processes, the concept of vague convergence is fundamental. A portmanteau theorem giving several statements that are equivalent to vague convergence can be found in, e.g.,
[
2
]
. The definition is as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
M_{0},M_{1},M_{2},\dots
be a sequence of Lévy measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that
M_{n}
converges vaguely to
M_{0}
and write
M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\rightarrow}}M_{0}
as
n\to\infty
if
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)M_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)M_{0}(\mathrm{d}x)
, for every
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that is bounded, continuous, and vanishing on a neighborhood of
0
.

We now turn to stochastic integral processes. Let
\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
be the space
\ell\times d
-dimensional matrices. Let
F(\cdot,\cdot):[0,\infty)^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
such that
F(\cdot,t)
is a Borel function for each
t\geq 0
. We write
F_{i,j}(\cdot,\cdot)
to denote the
i,j
th component of
F
. Assume that, for
t\geq 0
and each
i,j
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|F_{i,j}(s,t)|\vee|F_{i,j}(s,t)|^{2}\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(4)

In this case we say that
F
is an integrable kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
. Let
Z=\{Z_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a
d
-dimensional Lévy process with
Z_{1}\sim\mu=\mathrm{ID}(A,M,b)
and set

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}Z_{s},

where the stochastic integral is defined in the sense of Definition 2.20 in
[
28
]
. Many properties of the stochastic integral are given in
[
28
]
. We call
X=\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
a stochastic integral process with background driving Lévy process (BDLP)
Z
and integrable kernel
F
. For each
t\geq 0
, the random variable
X_{t}
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. Applying Proposition 2.7 in
[
28
]
and dominated convergence, shows that the characteristic function of
X_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[e^{i\langle z,X_{t}\rangle}\right]=\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}C_{\mu}(F^{\top}(s,t)z)\mathrm{d}s\right\},\ \ z\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},

(5)

where
C_{\mu}
is the cgf of
Z_{1}
. It follows that
X_{t}\sim\mathrm{ID}(A_{t},M_{t},b_{t})
, where
A_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s,t)AF^{\top}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s
,

M_{t}(B)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{B\setminus\{0\}}(F(s,t)x)M(\mathrm{d}x)\mathrm{d}s,\ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and

b_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(F(s,t)b+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(s,t)x\left(1_{[|F(s,t)x|\leq 1]}-1_{[|x|\leq 1]}\right)M(\mathrm{d}x)\right)\mathrm{d}s.

A common situation is when
\ell=d
and
F(s,t)=f(s,t)I_{d}
, where
I_{d}
is the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
f:[0,\infty)^{2}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is such that
f(\cdot,t)
is a Borel function for each
t\geq 0
satisfying appropriate integrability conditions. In this case we often write

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(s,t)\mathrm{d}Z_{s}

instead of
\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}Z_{s}
. Perhaps the most famous stochastic integral process is when
f(s,t)=e^{-c(t-s)}1_{[0\leq s\leq t]}
for some
c>0
. This corresponds to an OU-process, see
[
9
]
or
[
24
]
, and the references therein for details. Another important, although somewhat trivial, example is when
f(s,t)=1_{[0\leq s\leq t]}
. Here the stochastic integral process is the same as the original BDLP.

We now give our transfer theorem. It shows continuity of the stochastic integral process with respect to the BDLP. We have not seen a result of this type in the literature, although related results can be found in
[
23
]
,
[
29
]
, Proposition 8.2 in
[
4
]
, and Proposition 3.7 in
[
28
]
.

Theorem 1
.

For
n=0,1,2,\dots
let
\{Z_{t}^{(n)}:t\geq 0\}
be a sequence of Lévy processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
Z_{1}^{(n)}\sim\mu^{(n)}=\mathrm{ID}(A^{(n)},M^{(n)},b^{(n)})
and let
X^{(n)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}Z_{s}^{(n)}
for
t\geq 0
, where
F
is an integrable kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
. Assume that there is a sequence of real numbers
\{c_{n}\}
with
c_{n}Z_{1}^{(n)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}Z_{1}^{(0)}
. If either:

(i) there exists an
h\geq 0
with
M_{h}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|c_{n}|\int_{|c_{n}x|>h}|x|M^{(n)}(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty
, or

(ii) there exist a function
a:[0,\infty)\mapsto[0,\infty)
with
F(s,t)=0
for all
s>a(t)
,

then
\{c_{n}X_{t}^{(n)}:t\geq 0\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle fdd}}{{\rightarrow}}\{X_{t}^{(0)}:t\geq 0\}
.

Note that (ii) always holds when
X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}Z_{s}=\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{[0\leq s\leq t]}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}Z_{s}
. In particular, it always holds for OU-processes.

3
\epsilon
-Multivariate Dickman Distributions, Stability, and Self-Similarity

In which Dickman and multivariate Dickman distributions are reviewed, the more general class of
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions is introduced and shown to be characterized by a certain stability property, and a related process is shown to be self-similar.

A random variable
X
on
\mathbb{R}
is said to have a generalized Dickman distribution if

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U^{1/\theta}(X+1),

where
\theta>0
and
U\sim U(0,1)
is independent of
X
on the right side. We denote this distribution by
\mathrm{GD}(\theta)
. When
\theta=1
, it is just called the Dickman distribution. Many properties of this distribution are discussed in the surveys
[
21
]
,
[
20
]
, and
[
14
]
. A multivariate generalization of the Dickman distribution was recently introduced in
[
6
]
. It was further studied in
[
15
]
, where many properties were derived and several approaches for simulation were studied. A random variable
X
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to have a multivariate Dickman (MD) distribution if

\displaystyle X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U^{1/\theta}(X+\xi),

(6)

where
\theta>0
and
X,\xi,U
are independent on the right side with
U\sim U(0,1)
and
\xi\sim\sigma_{1}
for some probability distribution
\sigma_{1}
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Let
\sigma=\theta\sigma_{1}
and note that
\theta=\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma/\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Thus, there is no loss of information when working with
\sigma
instead of
\theta
and
\sigma_{1}
. We write
\mathrm{MD}(\sigma)
to denote this distribution. The generalized Dickman distribution
\mathrm{GD}(\theta)
corresponds to the multivariate Dickman distribution
\mathrm{MD}(\sigma)
when the dimension
d=1
and
\sigma=\theta\delta_{1}
. In
[
15
]
it is shown that the multivariate Dickman distribution is infinitely divisible. Before giving its Lévy measure, we introduce the more general class of
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions.

For
\epsilon>0
, an infinitely divisible distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be an
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distribution if
\mu=\mathrm{ID}_{0}(D^{\epsilon},0)
, where

\displaystyle D^{\epsilon}(B)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}1_{B}(rs)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\sigma(\mathrm{d}s),\ \ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(7)

for some finite Borel measure
\sigma
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. We denote this distribution by
\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon}(\sigma)
and we refer to
\sigma
as the spectral measure. The multivariate Dickman distribution corresponds to the case where
\epsilon=1
, i.e.
\mathrm{MD}(\sigma)=\mathrm{MD}^{1}(\sigma)
, see
[
15
]
. The characteristic function of
\mu=\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon}(\sigma)
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(z)=\exp\left\{\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}\left(e^{ir\left\langle z,s\right\rangle}-1\right)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)\right\},\ \ \ z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(8)

Taking partial derivatives of the exponent in (
8
) shows that the mean vector and the covariance matrix of
X\sim\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon}(\sigma)
are given, respectively, by

\mathrm{E}[X]=\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}s\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)\ \mbox{ and }\ \mathrm{cov}(X)=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}ss^{\top}\sigma(\mathrm{d}s).

One reason for the importance of
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions is that they satisfy a certain stability property, which stems from the following property of the Lévy measure
D^{\epsilon}
defined in (
7
). A simple change of variables shows that for any
\epsilon,\delta>0
and
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

D^{1}(B)=D^{\epsilon}(\epsilon B)\ \mbox{ and }\ D^{\epsilon}(\delta B)=D^{\epsilon/\delta}(B).

It follows that, for any
\epsilon,\gamma>0
, if
X^{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon}(\sigma)
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}X^{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon/\gamma}(\sigma).

(9)

Remark 1
.

Combining (
6
) with (
9
) show that
X^{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon}(\sigma)
if and only if

\displaystyle X^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}U^{1/\theta}(X^{\epsilon}+\epsilon\xi),

where
\theta=\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
X,\xi,U
are independent on the right side with
U\sim U(0,1)
and
\xi\sim\sigma/\theta
. In particular, this means that
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions belong to the class of multivariate Vervaat perpetuities, see
[
15
]
.

We now show that
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions are the only ones that satisfy a scaling property like (
9
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
M\neq 0
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
M(\{0\})=0
and for each
h\in(0,\infty)

\int_{|x|\leq h}|x|^{2}M(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

Let
M^{\delta}(\mathrm{d}x)=1_{[|x|\leq\delta]}M(\mathrm{d}x)
for any
\delta>0
. This is a Lévy measure for every
\delta>0
and we write
X^{\delta}
to denote a random variable with distribution
\mathrm{ID}(0,M^{\delta},0)
. Assume that there exists a function
\psi:(0,\infty)\mapsto(0,\infty)
such that for any
\epsilon,\gamma>0
there exists a non-random
b_{*}(\epsilon,\gamma)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi(\gamma)}X^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X^{\epsilon/\gamma}+b_{*}(\epsilon,\gamma).

(10)

Then
\psi(\gamma)=\gamma
and there exists a spectral measure
\sigma\neq 0
and a
b_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
X^{\epsilon}+b_{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{MD}^{\epsilon}(\sigma)
.

Note that a measure
M
satisfies the assumption of Theorem
2
if and only if
M^{\delta}(\mathrm{d}x)=1_{[|x|\leq\delta]}M(\mathrm{d}x)
is a Lévy measure for each
\delta>0
. We now show the self-similarity of a process related to
\epsilon
-multivariate Dickman distributions. Let
\sigma
be a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, let
\theta=\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, let
E_{1},E_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Exp}(\theta)
, let
\Gamma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}E_{i}
, and let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\sigma_{1}:=\sigma/\theta
be independent of the
E_{i}
’s. In
[
15
]
it is shown that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e^{-\Gamma_{i}}\xi_{i}\sim\mathrm{MD}(\sigma).

(11)

This can be used to simulate from a multivariate Dickman distribution. However, to do so we must truncate the infinite series. It has been argued, see e.g.
[
9
]
, that the proper way to truncate is not at a deterministic point. Instead, we should choose some
\tau>0
and truncate at the random time
N(\tau)
, where
N(\tau)=\max\{i:\Gamma_{i}\leq\tau\}
. This way we have control on the magnitudes of the jumps that we are removing. The remainder in this approximation can be written as

R_{\tau}=\sum_{i=N(\tau)+1}^{\infty}e^{-\Gamma_{i}}\xi_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1_{[\Gamma_{i}>\tau]}e^{-\Gamma_{i}}\xi_{i}.

We select
\tau=\tau(t)=\log(1/t)
for
t\in[0,1]
and set

\displaystyle X_{t}=R_{\tau(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1_{[\Gamma_{i}>\log(1/t)]}e^{-\Gamma_{i}}\xi_{i}

(12)

to be the remainder process. Here we have
X_{0}=0
a.s. The reason for our choice of
\tau
is that
\Gamma_{i}>\log(1/t)
is equivalent to
e^{-\Gamma_{i}}<t
. We now show that
X=\{X_{t}:t\in[0,1]\}
is a self-similar process.

Theorem 3
.

Let
X_{t}
be as in (
12
). We have
X_{t}\sim\mathrm{MD}^{t}(\sigma)
and for any
a\in[0,1]
, we have

\{X_{at}:t\in[0,1]\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{aX_{t}:t\in[0,1]\}.

4
Approximating Small Jumps of Lévy processes

In which a limit theorem showing that the small jumps of a large class of Lévy processes can be well approximated by a Dickman Lévy process is proved and a related limit theorem for stochastic integral processes is given.

Here and throughout, when applied to Lévy processes
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}
refers to weak convergence on the space
D([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is the space of càdlàg functions from
[0,\infty)
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the Skorokhod topology. For any
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
\gamma^{*}=\{t\gamma:t\geq 0\}
to denote the element of
D([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}^{d})
that maps
t
to
t\gamma
. In particular,
0^{*}
denotes the function that is identically zero.

Let
X=\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a Lévy process with
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu,0)
. Note that here, for simplicity, we set the drift to zero. Fix
\epsilon>0
and consider the truncated Lévy process
X^{\epsilon}=\{X_{t}^{\epsilon}:t\geq 0\}
obtained by removing the jumps of the process
X
, whose magnitudes exceed
\epsilon
. In this case,
X^{\epsilon}_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu^{\epsilon},0)
, where
\nu^{\epsilon}(B)=\int_{|x|\leq\epsilon}1_{B}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
,
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Next, consider the scaled truncated process
\epsilon^{-1}X^{\epsilon}=\{\epsilon^{-1}X_{t}^{\epsilon}:t\geq 0\}
and note that all of its jumps are bounded by
1
. It is easily checked that
\epsilon^{-1}X_{1}^{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(M^{\epsilon},0)
, where

M^{\epsilon}(B)=\int_{|x|\leq\epsilon}1_{B}\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}1_{B}\left(\frac{x}{\epsilon}\right)\nu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=\nu^{\epsilon}(\epsilon B),\ \ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Now, consider the multivariate Dickman Lévy process
Y^{1}=\{Y_{t}^{1}:t\geq 0\}
with
Y_{1}^{1}\sim\mathrm{MD}(\sigma)
. We now give conditions for the scaled truncated process
\epsilon^{-1}X^{\epsilon}
to converge to
Y^{1}
in distribution. For the convergence to hold, we need
M^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\rightarrow}}D^{1}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, where
M^{\epsilon}
is the Lévy measure of
\epsilon^{-1}X^{\epsilon}_{1}
and
D^{1}
is the Lévy measure of
Y^{1}_{1}
. We now give several statement that are equivalent to this. In the univariate case, a version of this result is given in Proposition 2.1 of
[
10
]
. As usual, for a set
C\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, we write
\partial C
to denote its boundary.

Proposition 1
.

Let
M^{\epsilon}
,
D^{1}
, and
\sigma
be as described above. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

M^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle v}}{{\rightarrow}}D^{1}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

2.

For all
0<h<1
and all
C\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
\sigma(\partial C)=0
,
\nu((\epsilon h,\epsilon]C)\rightarrow\sigma(C)\log\frac{1}{h}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

3.

For all
p>0
and all
C\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
\sigma(\partial C)=0
,
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{p}}\int_{(0,\epsilon]C}|x|^{p}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\rightarrow\frac{\sigma(C)}{p}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

4.

For some
p>0
and all
C\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
\sigma(\partial C)=0
,
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{p}}\int_{(0,\epsilon]C}|x|^{p}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\rightarrow\frac{\sigma(C)}{p}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Note that we allow
\sigma=0
in the above. Part of the result is the fact that, so long as any of the equivalent conditions in Proposition
1
hold, for every
p>0
we have

\int_{(0,\epsilon]C}|x|^{p}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

We now give our main result for approximating the small jumps of Lévy processes by a Dickman Lévy process. This extends the univariate result in
[
10
]
to the multivariate case.

Theorem 4
.

We have
\epsilon^{-1}{X^{\epsilon}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}Y^{1}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
if and only if any of the equivalent conditions in Proposition
1
hold. Further, when
\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})=0
, this is equivalent to
\epsilon^{-1}X^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}0^{*}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, and when

\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})>0
, it is equivalent to

\left(\frac{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}{p\int_{|x|\leq\epsilon}|x|^{p}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)}\right)^{1/p}X^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}Y^{1}\mbox{ as }\epsilon\downarrow 0

for any
p>0
.

We now give conditions that are easily checked in an important special case. Let
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
be the space of Borel functions
g:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
with
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|g(s)|\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)<\infty
. For any nonnegative
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
define the finite measure
\sigma_{g}
by
\sigma_{g}(B)=\int_{B}g(s)\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)
for
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
\nu
is of the form

\nu(B)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{B}(rs)\rho(r,s)\mathrm{d}r\sigma(\mathrm{d}s),\ \ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),

where
\sigma
is a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\rho:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mapsto[0,\infty)
is a Borel function. If there is a nonnegative
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
with
r\rho(r,\cdot)\to g(\cdot)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
as
r\downarrow 0
, i.e.
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|r\rho(r,s)-g(s)|\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)\to 0
as
r\downarrow 0
, then the equivalent conditions in Proposition
1
hold with
\sigma_{g}
in place of
\sigma
and
\frac{X^{\epsilon}}{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}Y^{1}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
, where
Y^{1}_{1}\sim\mathrm{MD}(\sigma_{g})
.

We now give two ways of checking that convergence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
holds.

Remark 2
.

Assume that, for each
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
r\rho(r,s)\to g(s)
as
r\downarrow 0
.

1. If
g
is bounded and
r\rho(r,s)
is bounded for small enough
r
, then we have convergence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
. This follows by dominated convergence and the fact that
\sigma
is a finite measure.

2. If
r\rho(r,s)\to g(s)
uniformly in
s
, then we have convergence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
. This is trivial when
\sigma=0
. To see that it holds when
\sigma\neq 0
note that, in this case, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a
\delta>0
such that for every
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and every
r\in(0,\delta)
we have
|r\rho(r,s)-g(s)|<\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}
. Thus, for such
r
,
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}|r\rho(r,s)-g(s)|\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)=\epsilon
.

Example 1
.

In
[
25
]
the class of so-called generalized tempered stable distributions was introduced. This class consists of infinitely divisible distributions with no Gaussian part and a Lévy measure of the form

\nu(B)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{B}(rs)q(r,s)r^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}r\sigma(\mathrm{d}s),\ \ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),

where
\alpha\in(0,2)
,
\sigma
is a finite measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
q:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mapsto(0,\infty)
is a Borel function such that
q(r,\cdot)\to q_{*}(\cdot)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
as
r\downarrow 0
for some nonnegative
q_{*}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\sigma)
. In the limiting case when
\alpha=0
, these immediately satisfy the assumptions of Corollary
1
. This class contains all gamma distributions and many of their extensions, see Section
5
below for a detailed discussion.

Theorem
4
tells us that, under appropriate conditions, we can approximately simulate from a Lévy process by approximating the small jumps by a Dickman Lévy process and the large jumps by a compound Poisson process. To see this let
X=\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a Lévy process with
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu,\gamma)
. Note that we now allow for a nonzero drift. Set
\epsilon>0
and let
\nu^{\epsilon}(B)=\int_{B}1_{|x|\leq\epsilon}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
and
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}(B)=\int_{B}1_{|x|>\epsilon}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)
for every
B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. It follows that

\displaystyle X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X^{\epsilon}+\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}+\gamma^{*},

(13)

where
X^{\epsilon}=\{X^{\epsilon}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is a Lévy process with
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu^{\epsilon},0)
,
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}=\{\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is a Lévy process with
\tilde{X}_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon},0)
,
X^{\epsilon}
and
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
are independent, and
\gamma^{*}=\{t\gamma:t\geq 0\}
. Here, we separated
X
into:
X^{\epsilon}
, the process of small jumps,
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
, the process of large jumps, and
\gamma^{*}
, the deterministic drift process. Under appropriate conditions, when
\epsilon>0
is small, we can approximate
X^{\epsilon}
by
\epsilon
times a Dickman Lévy process
Y^{1}
, which gives

\displaystyle X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\approx}}\epsilon Y^{1}+\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}+\gamma^{*},

(14)

where
Y^{1}
is independent of
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
. Following ideas in
[
8
]
about approximations of small jumps by Brownian motion, we now verify that the error in this approximation approaches
0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a Lévy process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu,\gamma)
and let
Y^{1}
,
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
, and
\gamma^{*}
be as above. If any of the equivalent conditions in Proposition
1
hold, then
for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a càdlàg process
Z^{\epsilon}=\{Z_{t}^{\epsilon}:t\geq 0\}
such that

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\epsilon Y^{1}+\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}+\gamma^{*}+Z^{\epsilon},

and, for each
T>0
,

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}|\epsilon^{-1}Z_{t}^{\epsilon}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0\ \text{as}\ \epsilon\downarrow 0.

(15)

This suggest that, for small
\epsilon
, we can approximate
Z^{\epsilon}
by
0
and we can approximately simulate from
X
by independently simulating
Y^{1}
and
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
and then applying (
14
). Several methods for simulating
Y^{1}
are discussed in
[
15
]
. For instance, we can simulate up to a finite time horizon
T>0
as follows. Let
E_{1},E_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Exp}(\theta)
, let
U_{1},U_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
, and let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\sigma/\theta
, where
\theta=\sigma(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, be independent sequences of random variables. If
\Gamma_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}E_{i}
for
i=1,2,\dots
, then

\displaystyle\left\{Y^{1}_{t}:0\leq t\leq T\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}e^{-\Gamma_{i}/T}\xi_{i}1_{[0,t/T]}(U_{i}):0\leq t\leq T\right\}.

(16)

In practice, of course, the infinite sum must be truncated at some finite (possibly random) value. We now turn to the simulation of
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
. Toward this end, note that
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}
is a finite measure, set
\lambda^{\epsilon}=\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}=\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}/\lambda^{\epsilon}
. It follows that
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
is a probability measure. Let
W_{1},W_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
and, independent of this, let
\{N(t):t\geq 0\}
be a Poisson process with rate
\lambda^{\epsilon}
. It is readily checked that
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
is a compound Poisson process and that

\displaystyle\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}=\left\{\tilde{X}_{t}^{\epsilon}:t\geq 0\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}W_{i}:t\geq 0\right\}.

(17)

When simulating until a finite time horizon
T>0
, it is often more convenient to use the following representation. Let
W_{1},W_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
and
U_{1},U_{2},\dots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
be independent sequences, and, independent of these, let
N\sim\mathrm{Pois}(T\lambda_{\epsilon})
. We have

\displaystyle\left\{\tilde{X}_{t}^{\epsilon}:0\leq t\leq T\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}1_{[0,t/T]}(U_{i}):0\leq t\leq T\right\},

(18)

see, e.g., Section 6.1 in
[
9
]
for details. Thus, the problem reduces to simulation from
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
. In the next section we discuss this in an important situation. Now, we give a version of Theorem
4
for stochastic integral processes. The proof is based on combining Theorems
1
and
4
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
X^{\epsilon}
and
Y^{1}
be as in Theorem
4
and let
F
be any integrable kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times d}
. If any of the equivalent condition in Proposition
1
hold, then

\left\{\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}X^{\epsilon}_{s}:t\geq 0\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle fdd}}{{\rightarrow}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}F(s,t)\mathrm{d}Y_{s}:t\geq 0\right\}\ \ \mbox{as }\ \epsilon\downarrow 0.

In particular, this holds for OU-processes.

5
Simulation from General Multivariate Gamma Distributions

In which Theorem
4
is applied to develop an approximate simulation method for a large class of distributions and a small simulation study is given to verify the performance of this approximation.

In this section we focus on distributions of the form
\mu=\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu,\gamma)
, where

\nu(B)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}1_{B}(rs)q(r^{p},s)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\sigma(\mathrm{d}s),\ \ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(19)

p>0
,
\sigma
is a finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
q:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mapsto[0,\infty)
is a Borel function such that for each
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
we have
\lim_{r\downarrow 0}q(r,s)=1
and
q(\cdot,s)
is completely monotone. In
[
11
]
such distributions are called proper
p
-tempered
0
-stable distributions, see also
[
12
]
for many properties. The complete monotonicity of
q
along with our other assumptions implies that
q(r^{p},s)=\int_{(0,\infty)}e^{-r^{p}s}Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}v)
for some measurable family
\{Q_{s}\}_{s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
of probability measures on
(0,\infty)
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\log(1/v)Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}v)\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)<\infty,

see Remark 1 and Corollary 1 in
[
11
]
. If we take
p=1
and
Q_{s}=\delta_{b(s)}
for some Borel function
b:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mapsto(0,\infty)
, these distributions reduce to the class of multivariate gamma distributions introduced in
[
22
]
. Motivated by this, we refer to our class as the class of generalized multivariate gamma distributions (GMGD).

Remark 3
.

We can consider a slight generalization of GMGD. Specifically, we can allow for a Gaussian part and remove the requirement that
\lim_{r\downarrow 0}q(r,s)=1
holds for each
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. The resulting distributions are denoted
J_{0,p}
in
[
18
]
and are called extended
p
-tempered
0
-stable distributions in
[
12
]
. When
p=2
they are called class
M
, see
[
17
]
and the references therein. When
p=1
we get the Thorin class, which is the smallest class of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is closed under convolution and weak convergence and contains the distributions of all so-called elementary gamma random variables on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see
[
3
]
. A related characterization for every
p>0
is given in Theorem 4.18 of
[
12
]
.

Note that
q(r^{p},s)\leq 1
, thus Remark
2
guarantees that the assumptions of Theorem
4
and Corollary
2
are satisfied and that these distributions fall into the more general class discussed in Example
1
. This means that we can approximately simulate from the GMGD Lévy process
X=\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
with
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu,\gamma)
and
\nu
as in (
19
) by using the approximation in (
14
). Here, to simulate
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}=\{\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}_{t}:t\geq 0\},
the process of large jumps, we can either use (
17
) or (
18
). Either way, we need a way to simulate from
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
. We now develop an approach to do this.

Fix
\epsilon>0
, let
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}
be the finite Borel measure defined by

\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}(B)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}1_{B}(rs)q(r^{p},s)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r\sigma(\mathrm{d}s),\ \ \ B\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

let
\lambda^{\epsilon}=\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}=\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}/\lambda^{\epsilon}
be a probability measure. Our goal is to develop an approach to simulate from
\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
. We begin by defining several quantities and distributions. First, for
u>0
define

\displaystyle\ell(u)=\int_{u}^{\infty}r^{-1}e^{-r^{p}}\mathrm{d}r=\frac{1}{p}\int_{u^{p}}^{\infty}y^{-1}e^{-y}\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{p}\Gamma(0,u^{p}),

where
\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
is the upper incomplete gamma function. Next, for
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, define

\displaystyle k_{\epsilon}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}q(r^{p},s)r^{-1}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\epsilon}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{-1}e^{-r^{p}v}Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}v)\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\epsilon v^{1/p}}^{\infty}r^{-1}e^{-r^{p}}\mathrm{d}rQ_{s}(\mathrm{d}v)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell(\epsilon v^{1/p})Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}v).

Now, define a probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}(\mathrm{d}s)=\frac{k_{\epsilon}(s)}{\lambda^{\epsilon}}\sigma(\mathrm{d}s)

and a family of probability measures on
(0,\infty)
by

G_{V}(\mathrm{d}v;s)=\frac{\ell(\epsilon v^{1/p})}{k_{\epsilon}(s)}Q_{s}(\mathrm{d}v),

where
s\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is a parameter. Finally, define a family of probability measures on
(0,\infty)
by
G_{R}(\mathrm{d}r;a)=g_{R}(r;a)\mathrm{d}r
, where
a>0
is a parameter and

g_{R}(r;a)=\frac{1}{\ell(a)}r^{-1}e^{-r^{p}}1_{[r\geq a]}

(20)

is the probability density function (pdf).

Proposition 2
.

Let
S\sim\sigma_{p}
. Given
S
, let
V\sim G_{V}(\cdot;S)
and, given
S
and
V
, let
R\sim G_{R}(\cdot;V^{1/p}\epsilon)
. If
W=RV^{-1/p}S
, then
W\sim\tilde{\nu}^{\epsilon}_{p}
.

There is no general approach for simulating from
\sigma_{p}
and
G_{V}
as they depend on the measures
\sigma
and
Q_{s}
. However, see
[
13
]
,
[
30
]
, and the references therein for discussions of simulation from distributions on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. We now develop a rejection sampling method to simulate from
G_{R}
. Toward this end we introduce two distributions that will serve as our proposal distributions. Let

h_{1}(x;a,p)=px^{p-1}e^{a^{p}-x^{p}}1_{[x\geq a]}

be a pdf, where
a,p>0
are parameters. Next, let

h_{2}(x;a,p,\beta)=\beta\frac{x^{-1}}{\log(1/a)}1_{[a\leq x<1]}+(1-\beta)px^{p-1}e^{1-x^{p}}1_{[x\geq 1]}

be a pdf, where
a,\beta\in(0,1)
, and
p>0
are parameters. We can simulate from these distributions as follows.

Lemma 1
.

Fix
a>0
,
p>0
,
\beta\in(0,1)
, and let
U\sim U(0,1)
. We have

\left(a^{p}-\log(U)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\sim h_{1}(\cdot;a,p).

Further, if
a\in(0,1)
, then

a^{(1-U/\beta)}1_{[U\leq\beta]}+\left(1-\log(1-U)+\log(1-\beta)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}1_{[U>\beta]}\sim h_{2}(\cdot;a,p,\beta).

If
a\geq 1
, we can check that

g_{R}(x)\leq C_{1}h_{1}(x;a,p),\ \mbox{ where }\ C_{1}=\frac{1}{e^{a^{p}}p\ell(a)}.

Similarly, if
a\in(0,1)
, we can check that

g_{R}(x)\leq C_{2}h_{2}(x;a,p,\beta),\ \mbox{ where }\ C_{2}=\frac{1}{\ell(a)}\max\left\{\frac{1}{ep(1-\beta)},\frac{\log(1/a)}{\beta}\right\}.

From here we can derive the following rejection sampling algorithms. Let

\phi_{1}(x)=x^{-1}1_{[x\geq a]}

and let

\phi_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{\max\left\{\frac{1}{ep(1-\beta)},\frac{\log(1/a)}{\beta}\right\}\left(\beta\frac{e^{x^{p}}}{\log(1/a)}1_{[a\leq x<1]}+(1-\beta)epx^{p}1_{[x\geq 1]}\right)}.

Algorithm 1
: Simulation from
G_{R}
when
p>0
and
a\geq 1
.

Step 1.
Simulate
U_{1},U_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
and set
X=\left(a^{p}-\log(U_{1})\right)
.

Step 2.
If
U_{2}\leq\phi_{1}(X)
return
X^{1/p}
, otherwise go back to Step 1.

In this case, the probability of rejection on a given iteration is
1/C_{1}=e^{a^{p}}p\ell(a)
.

Algorithm 2
: Simulation from
G_{R}
when
p>0
and
a\in(0,1)
;
\beta\in(0,1)
is a tuning parameter.

Step 1.
Simulate
U_{1},U_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
and set

X=a^{1-U_{1}/\beta}1_{[U_{1}\leq\beta]}+\left(1-\log(1-U_{1})+\log(1-\beta)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}1_{[U_{1}>\beta]}.

Step 2.
If
U_{2}\leq\phi_{2}(X)
return
X
, otherwise go back to Step 1.

In this case, the probability of rejection on a given iteration is
1/C_{2}
. We will generally take
a=\epsilon V^{1/p}
with
\epsilon
small. Thus, we are most interested in the case when
a\to 0
. By l’Hôpital’s rule, we have

\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{1}{C_{2}}=\beta\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{\ell(a)}{\log(1/a)}=\beta\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{\int_{a}^{\infty}r^{-1}e^{-r^{p}}\mathrm{d}r}{-\log(a)}=\beta\lim_{a\to 0}\frac{-a^{-1}e^{-a^{p}}}{-a^{-1}}=\beta.

Thus, for small
a
we can select a large
\beta
to get a good performance. In general, one can select whichever value of
\beta
maximizes the acceptance probability in a given situation. For simplicity, throughout this paper we take
\beta=1/2
, which leads to a reasonable performance in the situations considered. We now summarize our algorithm to simulate
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
, the compound Poisson process of large jumps. It combines (
18
) with Algorithms 1 and 2.

Algorithm 3:
For
\epsilon>0
, simulate
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}
, the compound Poisson process of large jumps, up to time
T>0
. Fix the tuning parameter
\beta\in(0,1)
.

I.

Simulate
N\sim\mathrm{Pois}(T\lambda^{\epsilon})
.

II.

Simulate
U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
.

III.

For
i=1,2,\dots,N
:

1.

Simulate
S_{i}\sim\sigma_{p}
.

2.

Given
S_{i}
, simulate
V_{i}\sim G_{V}(\cdot;S)
.

3.

Given
S_{i}
and
V_{i}
, simulate
R_{i}\sim G_{R}(\cdot;V^{1/p}\epsilon)
as follows:

(a)

If
\epsilon V_{i}^{1/p}\geq 1
:

(a.1)

Generate
U^{\prime}_{1},U^{\prime}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
and set
X_{i}=\left(\epsilon^{p}V_{i}-\log U^{\prime}_{1}\right)
.

(a.2)

If
U^{\prime}_{2}\leq X_{i}^{-1}
set
R_{i}=X_{i}^{1/p}
, otherwise go back to (a.1).

(b)

If
\epsilon V_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}<1
:

(b.1)

Generate
U^{\prime}_{1},U^{\prime}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}U(0,1)
.

(b.2)

If
U^{\prime}_{1}\leq\beta
set
X_{i}=\left(\epsilon V_{i}^{1/p}\right)^{1-U^{\prime}_{1}/\beta}
, otherwise set

X_{i}=\left[1-\log(1-U^{\prime}_{1})+\log(1-\beta)\right]^{1/p}.

(b.3)

If
U^{\prime}_{2}\leq\phi_{2}(X_{i})
set
R_{i}=X_{i}
, otherwise go back to (b.1).

4.

Set
W_{i}=R_{i}V_{i}^{-1/p}S_{i}
.

IV.

For any
t\in[0,T]
, set
\tilde{X}^{\epsilon}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}W_{i}1_{[0,t/T]}(U)

6
Extension to the BNS Stochastic Volatility Model

In which our approximation results are extended to the Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard (BNS) stochastic volatility model.

The BNS model is one of the best known continuous time stochastic volatility models and is often used to model financial returns. It was introduced by Barndorff-Nielsen and Shephard in
[
5
]
. Our discussion mainly follows Chapter 15 in
[
9
]
. The results in this section do not aim for generality, but to demonstrate an approach to deriving approximation results that can be applied in a variety of other contexts. In this section, unless otherwise specified, all processes are assumed to be one dimensional.

Let
\{S_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be the stock price process. It is typically assumed that
S_{t}=S_{0}e^{X_{t}}
, where
S_{0}
is the (known) price at time
0
and
\{X_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is the process of log returns. The BNS model assumes that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta h_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{h_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\rho\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\ \ \ X_{0}=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ch_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\ \ \ h_{0}\geq 0,

where
\beta>0
,
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,
c>0
,
W=\{W_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is standard Brownian motion, and
Z=\{Z_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is a Lévy process independent of
W
with
Z_{1}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu,0)
and
\nu((-\infty,0])=0
. We refer to
Z
as the background driving Lévy process (BDLP). The process
\{h_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is called the volatility process and our assumptions ensure that
h_{t}\geq 0
for each
t\geq 0
. We typically take
\rho\leq 0
, which models the so-called leverage effect. This is the empirically observed fact that prices tend to fall when volatility increases, see
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
, and the references therein. The system of stochastic differential equations implies that

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{h_{t}}\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\rho Z_{t}

and

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}-c\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}\mathrm{d}s+Z_{t}.

The process
\{h_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is an OU-process, see, e.g.,
[
9
]
or
[
24
]
. It can be equivalently represented as

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}e^{-ct}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-c(t-s)}\mathrm{d}Z_{s}.

Equating the two representations of
h_{t}
and solving gives the useful identity

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{0}\frac{1-e^{-ct}}{c}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1-e^{-c(t-s)}}{c}\mathrm{d}Z_{s}.

(21)

It follows that

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta h_{0}\frac{1-e^{-ct}}{c}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\beta\frac{1-e^{-c(t-s)}}{c}+\rho\right)\mathrm{d}Z_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{h_{s}}\mathrm{d}W_{s}.

Now consider the truncated version of the process
Z
given by
Z^{\epsilon}=\{Z^{\epsilon}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
with
Z_{1}^{\epsilon}\sim\mathrm{ID}_{0}(\nu^{\epsilon},0)
and the truncated BNS model
given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta h_{t}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{h_{t}^{\epsilon}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\rho\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\epsilon}\ \ \ X^{\epsilon}_{0}=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h_{t}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ch_{t}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\epsilon},\ \ \ h_{0}^{\epsilon}=\epsilon h_{0},

where
Z^{\epsilon}
is independent of
W
. We will show that for small
\epsilon>0
this can be approximated by a BNS model with a Dickman BDLP. Specifically, let
Z^{*}=\{Z^{*}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
be a Lévy process with
Z^{*}_{1}\sim\mathrm{MD}^{1}(\sigma)
. Assume that
Z^{*}
is independent of
W
and take

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta h_{t}^{*}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{h_{t}^{*}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\rho\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{*}\ \ \ X_{0}^{*}=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}h_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ch_{t}^{*}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{*},\ \ \ h_{0}^{*}=h_{0}.

We now give the joint convergence for the various components of the model. We note that here different components have different rates of convergence.

Proposition 3
.

Fix
t>0
and let
V_{t}^{\epsilon}=(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{d}s,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{h_{s}^{\epsilon}}\mathrm{d}W_{s},\frac{1}{\epsilon}Z_{t}^{\epsilon})
and
V^{*}_{t}=(\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}^{*}\mathrm{d}s,

\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{h_{s}^{*}}\mathrm{d}W_{s},Z_{t}^{*})
. If any of the equivalent conditions in Proposition
1
hold, then for any
t\geq 0
, we have
V_{t}^{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\rightarrow}}V_{t}^{*}
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

We note that this only gives convergence at one
t
at a time. Extending it to a stronger form of convergence is an important direction for future work. The result suggests that, when the assumptions hold, for small
\epsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\approx}}

\displaystyle\epsilon\beta\int_{0}^{t}h_{s}^{*}\mathrm{d}s+\sqrt{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{h_{s}^{*}}\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\epsilon\rho Z_{t}^{*}.
