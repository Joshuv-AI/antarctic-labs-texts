Abstract
Goemans and Williamson designed a 0.878-approximation algorithm for Max-Cut in undirected graphs [JACM’95]. Khot, Kindler, Mosel, and O’Donnel showed that the approximation ratio of the Goemans-Williamson algorithm is optimal assuming Khot’s Unique Games Conjecture [SICOMP’07]. In the problem of maximum cuts in directed graphs ( Max-DiCut ), in which we seek as many edges going from one particular side of the cut to the other, the situation is more complicated but the recent work of Brakensiek, Huang, Potechin, and Zwick showed that their 0.874-approximation algorithm is tight under the Unique Games Conjecture (up to a small delta) [FOCS’23]. We consider a promise version of the problem and design an SDP-based algorithm which, if given a directed graph G G that has a directed cut of value ρ \rho , finds an undirected cut in G G (ignoring edge directions) with value at least ρ \rho .

Introduction

The maximum cut problem in

1/2

[

Under Khot’s Unique Games Conjecture (

[

]

,

]
. The currently best known inapproximability result not relying on

is

16/17\approx 0.941

[
TSSW00
]
(obtained by a gadget from Håstad’s optimal inapproximability result
[
Hås01
]

The maximum cut problem in

[

]

In recent work, Brakensiek, Huang, Potechin, and Zwick gave an

We now give a brief, informal overview of our algorithm from
Theorem

1
.
Given a directed graph
G=(V,E)
with a directed cut of value
\rho
, we will try to place each vertex

\mathbf{x}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, for some large enough

||\mathbf{x}_{u}||_{2}^{2}=1
. In
\mathbb{R}^{N}
we select a distinguished direction, given by a unit vector
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. We will place these points so as to maximise an objective function that is a sum, over
(u,v)\in E
, of a linear combination between
||\mathbf{x}_{u}-\mathbf{x}_{v}||_{2}^{2}=2-\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}
and
\mathbf{x}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{v}-\mathbf{x}_{u})
; in addition the placement must satisfy some extra constraints known as the “triangle inequalities”. This the standard SDP relaxation of the problem with triangle inequalities. The main novelty comes from our rounding procedure and its proof of correctness: if a vertex
u
has
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
large, then we round it to
\sgn(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})
; whereas if it has
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
small, we round it according to the sides of a uniformly random hyperplane (as in the Goemans-Williamson rounding
[
GW95
]
).

The justification for this idea is the following. The vector
\mathbf{x}_{0}
represents the value
+1
in our SDP. Thus, if
\mathbf{x}_{u}
is very close to
\mathbf{x}_{0}
or
-\mathbf{x}_{0}

|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
is large, then we ought to prioritise the
\mathbf{x}_{0}
component in
\mathbf{x}_{u}
and round according to
\sgn(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Conversely, consider an edge
(u,v)
where
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
and
|\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
are both small. Then the contribution of
\mathbf{x}_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{v}-\mathbf{x}_{u})
to the objective will likewise be small, and we care more about the contribution of
||\mathbf{x}_{u}-\mathbf{x}_{v}||_{2}^{2}
. But this is precisely the contribution given by the vectors in the SDP relaxation for
Max-Cut
; we intuitively want, therefore, to round as dictated by the best known algorithm
[
GW95
]
, i.e., to round according to a random hyperplane. In the case where we mix the sizes of
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
and
|\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
, it can be seen that the contribution of the edge to the objective is approximately
1/2
. Also, the probability of properly cutting the edge is
1/2
. We have not specified the meaning of “large” or “small” in this description: it turns out to be sufficient to simply select the threshold between “large” and “small” values uniformly at random in
[0,1]
!

Related work

Max-Cut
is one of the simplest examples of a Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs)
[
KSTW00
]
. A celebrated result of Raghavendra showed that the basic SDP relaxation gives an optimal approximation algorithm to every CSP (assuming

)
[
Rag08

In particular, the universal rounding scheme of Raghavendra can only go arbitrarily close to the integrality gap (in polynomial time), not match it exactly — thus it would only be able to have an approximation ratio of

and

[

]

Indeed, the algorithm of Raghavendra and Steurer
[

computes a gap instance for an SDP to error

The studied

Problem definitions

Consider a directed graph
G=(V,E)
. A

cut
in
G
is a partition of
V
into two parts
A,B
. The
undirected value
of this cut is
|(A\times B\cup B\times A)\cap E|/|E|
. The
directed value
of the cut is
|(A\times B)\cap E|/|E|
. In other words, the directed value of a cut is the proportion of edges from
A
to
B
, and the undirected value of a cut is the proportion of edges between
A
and
B

While these definitions are the most natural, it will be more convenient (and is standard in the literature) to work with the following equivalent ones instead. We define, for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\cut(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-xy}{2},

\displaystyle\dicut(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-xy+y-x}{4}.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, a cut is a function
c:V\to\{\pm 1\}
. The undirected value of
c
is

\cut(c)=\Exp_{(u,v)\in E}[\cut(c(u),c(v))]=\frac{1}{2}\Exp_{(u,v)\in E}[1-c(u)c(v)].

The directed value of
c
is

\dicut(c)=\Exp_{(u,v)\in E}[\dicut(c(u),c(v))]=\frac{1}{4}\Exp_{(u,v)\in E}[1-c(u)c(v)+c(v)-c(u)].

Now, we define the (directed/undirected) value of a directed graph
G
as follows

\displaystyle\cut(G)

\displaystyle=\max_{c:V\to\{\pm 1\}}\cut(c).

\displaystyle\dicut(G)

\displaystyle=\max_{c:V\to\{\pm 1\}}\dicut(c).

The

\mathbf{x}_{u}
were equal exactly to
\mathbf{x}_{0}
or
-\mathbf{x}_{0}
, then intuitively we should round it to
\pm 1
accordingly. Now, similarly, if
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
is large enough, then we should also round
\mathbf{x}_{u}
according to
\sgn(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})
. On the other hand, if
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
is small enough, then we may as well throw away the component of
\mathbf{x}_{u}
parallel to
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Thus, we set
\mathbf{y}_{u}
to be the normalised projection of
\mathbf{x}_{u}
onto
\langle\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle^{\perp}
. In the case
\mathbf{x}_{u}\in\langle\mathbf{x}_{0}\rangle
, then of course
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
is large, so the value we assign
\mathbf{y}_{u}
shouldn’t matter; we set it to some vector perpendicular to all other vectors seen so far for simplicity. (This requires increasing the dimension of all the vectors by
|V|
, but this does not affect the complexity.)

Consider now an edge
(u,v)
where both
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
and
|\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
are small. In this case, we see that
\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}
does not contribute much to the objective function. Indeed, the major contribution is by
1-\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}=2\cut(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})
. Thus, it intuitively makes sense to round these vectors by a random hyperplane.

We are now ready to describe precisely the operation of the algorithm. First we sample a threshold
a\in[0,1]
uniformly at random. (This threshold separates “large” from “small” values in the informal discussion above.) If
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|\geq a
then set
c(u)=\sgn(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})
. For all
u
, compute
\mathbf{y}_{u}
as described above, namely set

\mathbf{y}_{u}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{u}-(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot t)\mathbf{x}_{0}}{\sqrt{1-(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}}}

whenever
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|<1
, and otherwise
\mathbf{y}_{u}
is set perpendicular to all other vectors seen so far. Sample a hyperplane
H
uniformly at random such that
H(\mathbf{x}_{0})=1
. Now, for all
u
for which
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|<a
, we set
c(u)=H(\mathbf{y}_{u})
.

For any edge
(u,v)\in E
, what is the probability that this edge is correctly cut by our method? We observe that the rounding method we described is exactly the same as the one mentioned in
Lemma

4
, hence

\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]\geq\frac{1-\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}+\mathbf{x}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{u}}{4}.

Thus, noting that
\Exp_{a,H}[\cut(c(u),c(v))]=\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]
, we have

\Exp_{a,H}[\cut(c)]\geq\Exp_{(u,v)\in E}\left[\frac{1-\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}+\mathbf{x}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{u}}{4}\right]\geq\rho-\varepsilon,

as required.
∎

We now derandomise our algorithm. This derandomisation scheme essentially uses the fact that our rounding scheme is a family of
\mathcal{ROT}
rounding schemes i.e. schemes that essentially rotate our vectors by some angle towards/away from
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

We first slightly modify our algorithm. For every vector
\mathbf{x}_{u}
where
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|<a
we set
\mathbf{z}_{u}=\mathbf{y}_{u}
; otherwise we set
\mathbf{z}_{u}=\sgn(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

For any hyperplane where
H(\mathbf{x}_{0})=1
and any
u\in V
where
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot t|\geq a
, we have that
H(\mathbf{z}_{u})=\sgn(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Thus, the following algorithm has the exact same characteristics as the algorithm from
Theorem

2
: compute an SDP solution
\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{u}
, then select a uniformly random threshold
a\in[0,1]
and compute
\mathbf{y}_{u},\mathbf{z}_{u}
as described. Now, select a uniformly random hyperplane
H
, with
H(\mathbf{x}_{0})=1
. Finally, set
c(u)=H(\mathbf{z}_{u})
. This algorithm will in fact output exactly the same cut
c
as our original one as long as the same values of
a,H
are selected.

Now, observe that the performance bound on our original algorithm says that
the expected value of a cut
c
created by selecting
a
uniformly at random from
[0,1]
and setting
c(u)=H(\mathbf{z}_{u})
is at
least
\rho-\varepsilon
. First, we observe that the exact value of
a
doesn’t matter, only its value relative to
|\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|
for
u\in V
. Thus there are essentially
O(|V|)
choices for
a
, and we can try all of them. Now, for the best
a
, we know that the expected value of the cut given by
c(u)=H(\mathbf{z}_{u})
is at least
\rho-\varepsilon
.
It is well known that given such vectors
\mathbf{z}_{u}

we can compute a colouring of
G
with value at least
\rho-\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}
in polynomial time in
|G|
and
\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
— indeed this amounts to derandomising the original Goemans-Williamson
Max-Cut
algorithm. One classic algorithm for this is given in
[
MR99
]
, whereas a simpler and more optimised algorithm is given in
[
BK05
]
.

Thus, by setting
\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{\prime}=1/2|E|
, we get a cut
c
with
value at least
\rho-1/2|E|=\dicut(G)-1/2|E|
. But observe that

\dicut(G)=k/|E|
, where
k
is the number of edges in the optimal
directed cut in
G
, and also
\cut(c)=k^{\prime}/|E|
, where
k^{\prime}
is the number of edges properly cut by
c
. It follows therefore, as
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
and
k^{\prime}/|E|\geq k/|E|-1/2|E|
i.e.
k^{\prime}\geq k-1/2
that
k^{\prime}\geq k
, and hence
\cut(c)\geq\dicut(G)
.
∎

In this section we prove the following lemma.

See

We define the value
\rho_{xyz}
. When
x=\pm 1
or
y=\pm 1
, we define
\rho_{xyz}=0
; otherwise,

\rho_{xyz}=\frac{z-xy}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}.

In order to prove
Lemma

4
, we will need the following fact.

Lemma 5
.

Consider
x,y\in[-1,1]
with
|x|\leq y
, and take
z\in[x+y-1,1-y+x]
. Then

\frac{1-y}{\pi}\arccos\rho_{xyz}\geq\frac{1-z+x-y}{4}.

(4)

To prove this fact we define the following functions:

\displaystyle F(x,y,z)

\displaystyle=\arccos\rho_{xyz}-\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{1-z+x-y}{1-y},

\displaystyle\Delta_{xy}

\displaystyle=4\pi^{4}(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})-64\pi^{2}(1-y)^{2},

\displaystyle\alpha_{xy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{1-y},

\displaystyle G(x,y)

\displaystyle=\pi-\arccsc\alpha_{xy}-\frac{\pi}{4}(1+x)-\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}^{2}-1}.

For the function
G(x,y)
to be defined (over the reals), it is necessary for
|\alpha_{xy}|\geq 1
; in particular this is certainly true when
\Delta_{xy}\geq 0
, which is the only case when we will use this function. For many other of these functions, we require
y<1
; this will also always be the case when they are invoked.

Lemma 6
.

Assume
x,y\in(-1,1)
,
y\geq|x|
,
\Delta_{xy}\geq 0
and
xy-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}\geq x+y-1
. Then
G(x,y)\geq 0
.

Proof.

We observe that for
xy-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}\geq x+y-1
to be true, we must have

0\leq 1+xy-x-y-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}=(1-x)(1-y)-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}=\\
\frac{1}{(1-x)(1-y)+\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}}\left({(1-x)^{2}(1-y)^{2}-\frac{\Delta_{xy}}{4\pi^{4}}}\right)=\\
\frac{2(1-y)}{\pi^{2}(1-x)(1-y)+\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2}}\left(8(1-y)-\pi^{2}(1-x)(x+y)\right).

This is true if and only if
8(1-y)\geq\pi^{2}(1-x)(x+y)
. Thus
y\leq(\pi^{2}x-\pi^{2}x^{2}-8)/(\pi^{2}x-8-\pi^{2})
.

We observe now that
\alpha_{xy}
is an increasing function of
y
on
(-1,1)
, hence by taking
y=(\pi^{2}x-\pi^{2}x^{2}-8)/(\pi^{2}x-8-\pi^{2})
, we find that

\alpha_{xy}\leq\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}(1-x)^{2}}.

Furthermore, note that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{xy}}G(x,y)=-\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}^{2}-1}}{\alpha_{xy}}<0.

Hence we minimise
G
by selecting
y
so as to maximise
\alpha_{xy}
. Thus, taking
y
so that
\alpha_{xy}=\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}(1-x)^{2}}
, we find that

G(x,y)\geq\pi-\arccsc\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}(1-x)^{2}}\right)-\frac{\pi}{4}(1+x)-\sqrt{\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}(1-x)^{2}}^{2}-1}=\\
\pi-\arccsc\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}(1-x)^{2}}\right)-\frac{\pi}{2}\geq 0,

as required.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Assume
x,y\in(-1,1)
and
\Delta_{xy}\geq 0
. If
z=xy-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}
, then
\arccos\rho_{xyz}=\pi-\arccsc\alpha_{xy}
.

Proof.

Observe that
z-xy=-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}
. So

\rho_{xyz}=-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}=-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi^{4}(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})-64\pi^{2}(1-y)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}}=\\
-\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\sqrt{4\pi^{4}-64\pi^{2}\frac{(1-y)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}}=-\sqrt{1-\frac{16}{\pi^{2}}\frac{(1-y)^{2}}{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}}=-\sqrt{1-1/\alpha_{xy}^{2}}.

Thus recalling the trigonometric identity
\arccos(-\sqrt{1-1/\alpha^{2}})+\arccsc\alpha=\pi
, we have

\arccos\rho_{xyz}=\arccos(-\sqrt{1-1/\alpha_{xy}^{2}})=\pi-\arccsc\alpha_{xy}.\qed

Lemma 8
.

Assume
x,y\in(-1,1)
and
\Delta_{xy}\geq 0
. If
z=xy-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}
, then

\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{1-z+x-y}{1-y}=\frac{\pi}{4}(1+x)+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}_{xy}-1}.

Proof.

We have

\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{1-z+x-y}{1-y}=\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{1-xy+x-y}{1-y}+\frac{1}{8\pi(1-y)}\sqrt{4\pi^{4}(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})-64\pi^{2}(1-y)^{2}}=\\
\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{(1+x)(1-y)}{1-y}+\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}\frac{(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})}{(1-y)^{2}}-1}=\frac{\pi}{4}(1+x)+\sqrt{\alpha_{xy}^{2}-1},

as required.
∎

x,y\in(-1,1)

Proof.

Fixing

. We consider the extreme values of
z
, then inspect the local minima.

Case 1:
\bm{z=x+y-1}
.

In this case,
1-z+x-y=2-2y
, so

\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{1-z+x-y}{1-y}=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Furthermore,
z-xy=-1+x+y-xy=-(1-x)(1-y)
, so

\rho_{xyz}=-\frac{(1-x)(1-y)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}<0.

Thus in this case
\arccos\rho_{xyz}>\pi/2
, so
F(x,y,z)>0
.

Case 2:
\bm{z=1-y+x}
.

In this case
1-z+x-y=0
, so

F(x,y,z)=\arccos\rho_{xyz}-0\geq 0.

Case 3:
\bm{x+y-1<z<1-y+x}
.

We are now looking for local minima of
F(x,y,z)
with respect to
z
. Thus, we compute

\frac{\partial}{\partial z}F(x,y,z)=\frac{\pi^{2}(1-x^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2}+2xyz)-16(1-y)^{2}}{4(1-y)\sqrt{1-x^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2}+2xyz}(\pi\sqrt{1-x^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2}+2xyz}+4(1-y))}.

We observe that the denominator of this fraction is always positive, and thus the sign of
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}F(x,y,z)
is given be the numerator, namely

\pi^{2}(1-x^{2}-y^{2}-z^{2}+2xyz)-16(1-y)^{2}.

We compute the discriminant of this quadratic, and observe that it equals
\Delta_{xy}
. Thus, if
\Delta_{xy}<0
there are no stationary points; if
\Delta_{xy}=0
then there is one stationary point but it is neither a local minimum nor a local maximum; and if
\Delta_{xy}>0
there are two stationary points, located at

z=xy\pm\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}.

The first of these is a local minimum, and the second is a local maximum. Thus we are interested in
z=xy-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}
. Of course if this value does not satisfy our boundary conditions, then there are no local minima; so we can assume that
xy-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta_{xy}}}{2\pi^{2}}\geq x+y-1
. By

8

F(x,y,z)=\pi-\arccsc\alpha_{xy}-\frac{\pi}{4}(1+x)-\sqrt{\alpha^{2}_{xy}-1}=G(x,y).

By
Lemma

G(x,y)\geq 0
, so in this case it follows that
F(x,y,z)\geq 0

Thus, our conclusion follows in all cases.

Proof of
Lemma

.

We first deal with the edge cases, when
x,y=\pm 1
, then with the typical case. As
y\geq|x|\geq 0
, for either
x
or
y
to be
\pm 1
, it must be the case that
y=1
, so suppose this is the case. Then the left side of (
4
) is
0
, whereas the right side is
(x-z)/4
. By assumption
x+y-1\leq z
, but since
y=1
this is the same as
x\leq z
, hence
(x-z)/4\leq 0
as required. We can thus assume that
y<1
and thus
x,y\in(-1,1)
.
In this case, (
4
) is equivalent to

, which is implied by
Lemma

.
∎

Having proved
Lemma

, we return to the proof of
Lemma

.

Proof of
Lemma

4
.

Suppose we let
x=\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
y=\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
z=\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}
. Then
(\mathbf{x}_{0}\pm\mathbf{x}_{u})\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{0}\pm\mathbf{x}_{v})\geq 0
amounts to

\displaystyle 1+x+y+z

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle 1-x-y+z

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle 1-x+y-z

\displaystyle\geq 0,

\displaystyle 1+x-y-z

\displaystyle\geq 0,

or equivalently
|x+y|-1\leq z\leq 1-|y-x|
. With this reformulation, we must show that

\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]\geq\frac{1-z+y-x}{4}.

(5)

We first consider the effect of swapping
u
and
v
, and/or negating
\mathbf{x}_{0}
has on the problem. Observe that all of these transformations maintain the requirement
(\mathbf{x}_{0}\pm\mathbf{x}_{u})\cdot(\mathbf{x}_{0}\pm\mathbf{x}_{v})\geq 0
. These transformations do not change
z
, but they have the effect of mapping
(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
(for swapping) and
(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)
(for negating). Thus, by swapping we can achieve
(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
, and by swapping and negating we can achieve
(x,y)\mapsto(-y,-x)
.

We claim that these transformations mean we can consider only the case
x\leq y
,
|x|\leq|y|
. Indeed, if
x>y
then by swapping
\mathbf{x}_{u}
and
\mathbf{x}_{v}
we can reduce to the case
x\leq y
while increasing the right side of (
5
) (i.e. reducing to a necessary subcase). Furthermore, once we are in this case, if
|x|>|y|
, then by both swapping and negating we can get to the case
|x|\leq|y|
while remaining in the case
x\leq y
(and not changing the values in (
5
)). Henceforth we assume that
x\leq y
and
|x|\leq|y|
.

Observe that
x\leq y
and
|x|\leq|y|
are equivalent to
|x|\leq y
; hence we know that
y-x\geq 0
and
x+y\geq 0
, so
|x+y|-1\leq z\leq 1-|y-x|
becomes
x+y-1\leq z\leq 1-y+x
.

Now, what is the inner product of
\mathbf{y}_{u}
and
\mathbf{y}_{v}
? We observe that, as long as
|\mathbf{x}_{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}|<1
, we have

\mathbf{y}_{w}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{w}-(\mathbf{x}_{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathbf{x}_{0}}{\sqrt{1-(\mathbf{x}_{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}}}.

Hence in this case we have

\mathbf{y}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{v}=\frac{(\mathbf{x}_{u}-(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathbf{x}_{0})(\mathbf{x}_{v}-(\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\sqrt{1-(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}}\sqrt{1-(\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}}}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{v}-(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})(\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\sqrt{1-(\mathbf{x}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}}\sqrt{1-(\mathbf{x}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0})^{2}}}=\\
\frac{z-xy}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}.

Conversely if
|\mathbf{x}_{w}\cdot t|=1
, then
\mathbf{y}_{w}
is perpendicular to all other vectors
\mathbf{y}_{w^{\prime}}
, so we have
\mathbf{y}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{v}=0
. Hence we observe that in all cases
\mathbf{y}_{u}\cdot\mathbf{y}_{v}=\rho_{xyz}
.

Now, let us investigate what
\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]
is. There are two main cases, depending on the sign of
x
.

Case 1:
\bm{x\leq 0}
.

In this case, with probability
-x
we have that
a\leq|x|\leq|y|
, and hence
c(u)=-1,c(v)=1
. With probability
|y|-|x|=x+y
, we have
|x|<a\leq|y|
, in which case
c(v)=1
but
c(u)
is uniformly randomly distributed in
\{\pm 1\}
. With probability
1-|y|=1-y
we have
a>|y|\geq|x|
, in which case
\mathbf{y}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{v}
are separated by the random hyperplane
H
with probability
(1/\pi)\arccos\rho_{xyz}
. So

\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]=-x+\frac{x+y}{2}+\frac{1-y}{\pi}\arccos\rho_{xyz}.

By
Lemma

5
, we have
((1-y)/\pi)\arccos\rho_{xyz}\geq(1-z+x-y)/4
, so

\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]\geq-x+\frac{x+y}{2}+\frac{1-z+x-y}{4}=\frac{1-z+y-x}{4}.

Case 2:
\bm{x>0}
.

In this case, with probability
x
we have
a\leq|x|\leq|y|
, and hence
c(u)=1,c(v)=1
. With probability
|y|-|x|=y-x
, we have
|x|<a\leq|y|
, in which case
c(v)=1
but
c(u)
is uniformly randomly distributed in
\{\pm 1\}
. With probability
1-|y|=1-y
we have
a>|y|\geq|x|
, in which case
\mathbf{y}_{u},\mathbf{y}_{v}
are separated by
H
with probability
(1/\pi)\arccos\rho_{xyz}
. So, applying
Lemma

5
as before, we have

\Pr_{a,H}[c(u)\neq c(v)]=\frac{y-x}{2}+\frac{1-y}{\pi}\arccos\rho_{xyz}\geq\frac{y-x}{2}+\frac{1-z+x-y}{4}\geq\frac{1-z+y-x}{4}.

Hence our conclusion follows in all cases.
∎
