The Induced Bipartite Ramsey Theorem: An Exposition
William Gasarch, Gary Peng

Abstract




1
Introduction

This paper presents a proof of the Induced Bipartite Ramsey Theorem. Although based on the treatment in Diestel
[
1
]
, our treatment differs from his in the following ways:

1.

We provide more detail.

2.

We provide examples on small graphs.

3.

We include the Ramsey Theorem for general (i.e., not necessarily induced) bipartite graphs for contrast.

4.

We view the Induced Bipartite Ramsey Theorem as a theorem of interest in and of itself. (Diestel presents the Induced Bipartite Ramsey Theorem as a lemma for the Induced Ramsey Theorem for general graphs.)

5.

We do not prove the Induced Ramsey Theorem for general graphs.

To summarize, our goal is pedagogical.

In both our treatment and Diestel’s, we only deal with the case of 2 colors.
Extending the results to
c
colors is a straightforward but tedious exercise.

3.1
The Bipartite Ramsey Theorem

The following theorem is folklore:

Theorem 3.5
.

For all
a,b\in\sf N
, there exists
n,k\in\sf N
such that for all
2
-colorings of the edges of
K_{n,k},
there exists a monochromatic
K_{a,b}.
In particular, we can take
k=2b
and

n=a2^{2b}
.

Proof.

Let
\text{COL}:[n]\times[k]\rightarrow[2]
be a
2
-coloring of the edges of

K_{n,k},
where
n,k\in\sf N
are constants to be determined later, and define
\text{COL}^{\prime}:[n]\rightarrow[2^{k}]
as

\text{COL}^{\prime}(x)=(\text{COL}(x,1),\ldots,\text{COL}(x,k)).

Then, since there are
n
elements in the domain and
2^{k}
elements in the co-domain of
\text{COL}^{\prime}
, some color
\vec{c}=(c_{1},...,c_{k})
is mapped to by at least
\frac{n}{2^{k}}
left-vertices in
K_{n,k}
. (We will later pick
n,k
such that
2^{k}
divides
n
.) Thus, since
c_{i}\in\{\hbox{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} RED},\hbox{\color[rgb]{0,0,1} BLUE}\}
, there exists
c^{*}\in\{\hbox{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} RED},\hbox{\color[rgb]{0,0,1} BLUE}\}
that appears at least
\frac{k}{2}
times in
\vec{c}.
(We will later pick
k
such that 2 divides
k
.) Hence,
\vec{c}
looks something like

\vec{c}=(\cdots c^{*}\cdots c^{*}\cdots c^{*}\cdots\cdots\cdots c^{*}\cdots).

Now, suppose that the
c^{*}
occur at positions
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\frac{k}{2}}
. Then, the
\frac{n}{2^{k}}
left-vertices that map to
\vec{c}
and the right-vertices
p_{1},\ldots,p_{\frac{k}{2}}
induce a monochromatic complete bipartite graph.

To finish the proof, we will now pick
n,k
. We need the following:

1.

\frac{n}{2^{k}}\geq a
and
2^{k}|n

2.

\frac{k}{2}\geq b
and
2|k

Therefore, we can take
k=2b
and
n=a2^{k}=a2^{2b}
.
∎

5
The Induced
B_{n,k}
Ramsey Theorem

We now generalize Theorem
4.1
to apply to
B_{n,k}
for any
n,k\in\sf N
. Most of the ideas used were already in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
a,b\in\sf N
with
b\leq a.

Then, there exists
n,k\in\sf N
such that for all 2-colorings of the edges of
B_{n,k}
, there exists an induced monochromatic
B_{a,b}.

Proof.

Let
s\in\sf N
be a constant to be determined later, and let

n=R_{2b-1,2\binom{2b-1}{b}}(s).

We will show that any 2-coloring of the edges of
\displaystyle B_{n,2b-1}=\left([n],\binom{[n]}{2b-1},E\right),
where

E=\left\{(x,X)\in[n]\times\binom{[n]}{2b-1}\colon x\in X\right\}

has an induced monochromatic
B_{a,b}
(i.e.,
k=2b-1
).

Let
\displaystyle\text{COL}:E\rightarrow[2]
be a
2
-coloring of the edges of
B_{n,2b-1}
. We will define
\displaystyle\text{COL}^{\prime}\colon\binom{[n]}{2b-1}\rightarrow\left[2\binom{2b-1}{b}\right]
for
\displaystyle X=\{z_{1}<\cdots<z_{2b-1}\}\in\binom{[n]}{2b-1}
. In particular, since there are edges of the form
(z_{1},X)
,
\cdots
,
(z_{2b-1},X)
in
B_{n,2b-1}
,
b
of these edges – say
(z_{i_{1}},X),\cdots,(z_{i_{b}},X)
– have the same color
c
. We define

\text{COL}^{\prime}(X)=(c,\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{b}\})
.

Now, by the definition of
n,
there exists a homogeneous set
H
(relative to
\text{COL}^{\prime}
) of size
s
and color
(c,\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{b}\})
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
H=\{1,\ldots,s\}
and that
c=\hbox{\color[rgb]{1,0,0} RED}
. Thus, for all right-vertices
X\subseteq H
in
B_{n,2b-1}
, the
i_{1}
th,
\ldots
,
i_{b}
th edges coming into
X
are
RED
.

We will now choose
s
. Recall that in the proof of
Theorem
4.1
, we wanted an induced
B_{4,2}
and chose
s=9
.
We then took the 4 (4 was the first number in
B_{4,2}
) left-vertices
2,4,6,8
because there was ONE (ONE was one less than 2, the second number in
B_{4,2}
) invisible vertex between any pair of adjacent vertices, ONE invisible vertex above the top vertex, and ONE invisible vertex below the bottom vertex. Hence, we choose
s=ab+b-1
so that we can take the left-vertices
b,2b,\ldots,ab
and have
b-1
vertices after
ab
. We now leave the rest of the proof to the reader.
∎

Lemma 6.2

Let

Let

We will now show that

2.

For the right-vertices, we will form a

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
G

There exists
n,k\in\sf N
such that for all 2-colorings of the edges of

, there exists an induced monochromatic
G
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.2
, there exists
a,b\in\sf N
such that there exists an induced monochromatic
G
in

and by Theorem

, there exists

such that for all 2-colorings of the edges of

B_{a,b}
. Now, let

. Then, there is an induced monochromatic
B_{a,b}
