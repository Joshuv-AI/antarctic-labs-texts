Scale-invariant multilevel Monte Carlo method and application to linear elasticity
Sharana Kumar Shivanand, Bojana Rosić

Abstract
We propose a novel scale-invariant version of the mean and variance multi-level Monte Carlo estimate. The computation cost across grid levels is optimised using a normalized error based on t-statistics. By doing so, the algorithm achieves convergence independent of the physical scale at which the estimate is computed. The effectiveness of this algorithm is demonstrated through testing on a linear elastic example, where material uncertainty incorporating both heterogeneity and anisotropy is considered in the constitutive law.



Keywords:

Monte Carlo, multilevel Monte Carlo, normalized error, uncertainty quantification, linear elasticity

1
Introduction

In forward problems, estimation of statistics to characterize a stochastic solution of a stochastic partial differential equation or an ordinary differential equation is of utmost importance in uncertainty quantification (UQ). In this regard, one of the fundamental objectives of UQ is to determine second-order statistical moments, notably the mean and variance. Unlike skewness and kurtosis (the third and fourth moments, respectively), which are dimensionless, the mean and variance are scale-dependent measures, which are commonly determined using absolute error estimates such as the mean square error (MSE) or the root mean square error (RMSE). This further means that the statistical convergence of the algorithm is dependent on the scale or magnitude of the quantity of interest (QoI). Hence, a performance comparison between different estimators (here, mean and variance) or individual estimators on different scales cannot be done. To overcome this, normalized error estimates play a crucial role in statistics by providing a standardised measure of the accuracy or precision of statistical estimates or predictions.

In the last few decades, numerous algorithms—to name a few such as surrogates, direct integration, and variance reduction methods—have been developed for performing efficient forward uncertainty propagation
[
38
,
35
,
26
]
. Among these algorithms, the Monte Carlo method (MC) has traditionally been regarded as the gold standard for solving stochastic problems, due to its resilience against the curse of dimensionality
[
27
,
12
,
16
]
. However, despite its appeal, the practical implementation of MC often faces challenges due to slow convergence and the substantial computational effort it demands. To address these limitations, recent scientific attention has shifted towards a more computationally efficient approach known as the sampling-based multilevel Monte Carlo method (MLMC). This algorithm has emerged as a promising alternative to MC, offering improved computational adequacy. The key to MLMC’s success lies in spreading out the sampling strategy across a hierarchy of different fidelity levels such that the number of stochastic samples drastically decays with the increment in fidelity of the model. Under the right conditions, this results in an overall reduction of the computational cost as compared to MC, making MLMC an attractive option for practical applications.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the MLMC was first introduced by
[
20
]
in the context of estimating multi-dimensional parameter-dependent integrals and is further employed by
[
14
]
for solving Itô’s stochastic ordinary differential equations in the computational finance application. Following this, the MLMC is extended to solve the linear elliptic PDEs describing the subsurface flow with inhomogeneous stochastic parameters
[
7
,
2
]
, whereas the extension to the random field scenario is studied in
[
7
,
36
]
. However, previously mentioned works focused only on the approximation of the output response sample mean and therefore lacked full characterization of the probabilistic solution. Consequently,
[
28
]
studies the unbiased MLMC sample variance estimator, which is further analysed in
[
3
]
on a class of elliptic random obstacle problems. An alternative MLMC variance estimator based on h-statistics
[
9
,
29
]
is introduced in
[
25
]
. Though the estimation of other higher-order moments such as skewness and kurtosis through MLMC is out of the scope of this paper, more literature on this can be found
[
4
,
25
]
.

In the context of assessing the computation efficiency of the MLMC algorithm in a standardised manner, the authors in
[
3
]
theoretically prove, with specific assumptions on the deterministic solution, two error bounds for the multilevel sample mean and variance that may be directly compared. But no normalized error estimates are defined for practical interpretation of the complexities between the moments. On the other hand, relative error estimates, where the total MSE of a given moment is normalized by the square of its own statistic, are considered in
[
25
]
. However, such ratios are not fully non-dimensional, especially for linear transformations involving an operation of addition by a scalar with the QoI. Therefore, to tackle this issue, in this article, we propose novel scale-invariant or normalized mean square error (NMSE) estimates for the MLMC mean and variance estimations, based on t-statistics
[
34
,
11
]
. The newly introduced relative errors are statistically defined, with chosen normalizing factors that are finite, unbiased, and of minimal variance. They also ensure the total MSE of the MLMC algorithm is normalized under any linear transformation (scaling and addition) of QoI. Furthermore, we also define standardised errors for the MC mean and variance estimators. Thereby, the dimensionless error estimates can be used for an easier comparison of not only the MLMC mean and variance estimates but also the corresponding MC ones, thus improving the overall interpretability of the MC and MLMC algorithms.

The other objective of this paper is to investigate the applicability of the scale-invariant MLMC method to linear elliptic problems described by stochastic parameters representing both heterogeneity and uncertain material symmetries. As an example, we consider the linear-elastic material model of the human femoral bone, whose constitutive law is assumed to be uncertain. In particular, the bone material symmetry is described as uncertain due to the lack of conclusive verification of the class of elastic symmetry the human femur belongs to
[
31
,
23
]
. Hence, the entire elasticity tensor is constructed as random with a predefined elastic symmetry in the mean (orthotropy
[
13
]
in this case) and purely arbitrary symmetries in each stochastic realization. Consequently, the positive-definite random elasticity matrices are modelled as matrix-valued random fields, as proposed in
[
32
,
33
]
. In this work, we restrict ourselves to material uncertainties only, assuming that the remaining model parametrization is deterministic. However, the numerical approach is general enough to be employed for other types of uncertainties as well.

2
Problem description

Let us consider a physical system occupying the spatial domain
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a d-dimensional Euclidean space, modelled by an abstract equilibrium equation:

\mathcal{A}(q(x),u(x))=f(x).

(1)

Here,
u(x)\in\mathcal{U}
describes the state of the system at a spatial point
x\in\mathcal{G}
lying in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
(for the sake of simplicity),

\mathcal{A}
is a (possibly non-linear) operator modelling the physics of the system, and
f\in\mathcal{U}^{*}
is some external influence (action/excitation/loading). Furthermore, we assume that the model depends on the parameter set
q\in\mathcal{Q}
and that it is accompanied by appropriate boundary and/or initial conditions.
Note that, for brevity, the model in the previous equation describes the physical system only in a spatial domain
x
, whereas one may also generalise this to time-dependent processes.

The uncertainty in the previous equation may arise due to the randomness in external influence
f
, initial or boundary conditions, geometry
\mathcal{G}
, as well as the coefficient of operator
\mathcal{A},
i.e., parameter
q
. Although the theory presented further does not depend on this choice and is general enough to cover all of the mentioned (single or combination of) cases, this article focuses on incorporating stochasticity only in coefficient
q
. In the theory of continuum solid mechanics, the parameter
q
represents one of the very well-known physical phenomena, such as elasticity, which is detailed as an example in Section
5
. Here, we assume that
q

is modelled as a
random field
q(x,\omega)
with finite second-order moments on a probability space
(\varOmega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P})
, where
\varOmega
denotes the set of elementary events,
\mathfrak{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra of events, and
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure.
Following this, Eq. (
1
)
rewrites to a stochastic form:

\mathcal{A}(q(x,\omega),u(x,\omega))=f(x),

(2)

which further is to be solved for
u(x,\omega)\in\mathcal{U}\otimes L_{2}(\varOmega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P})
.

Often, when the response of a model is uncertain, one is interested in attaining its relevant information via its corresponding statistics.
Analytically, these statistical moments of the solution
u\equiv u(x,\omega)
can be represented as

r
-th,
r\in\mathbb{N}
, central moment:

\mathbb{E}\left((u-\mathbb{E}(u))^{r}\right)=\int_{\varOmega}(u-\mathbb{E}(u))^{r}\mathbb{P}(\text{d}\omega)

(3)

with
{\mu}(u):=\mathbb{E}(u)
being the mean or the first raw moment and
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u):=\mathbb{E}\left((u-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}\right)
being the second central moment or the variance. Similarly, the central moments at values
r=3
and
r=4
represent the skewness and kurtosis, respectively. However, the objective of this study is to determine the first two moments (i.e., mean and variance) only after an appropriate deterministic discretization of the problem in Eq. (
2
) is presented.

Due to spatial and stochastic dependence, the solution
u\equiv u(x,\omega)
in Eq. (
2
) is first discretized in a spatial domain, i.e., we search for the solution in a finite subspace
\mathcal{U}_{h}\subset\mathcal{U}
. After rewriting the problem in Eq. (
2
) in a variational form, the spatial discretization
u_{h}(x,\omega)\in\mathcal{U}_{h}
—
h
being the discretization parameter—can take the finite element form on a sufficiently fine spatial grid
\mathcal{T}_{h}

1
1

1

Other types of discretization techniques can be considered as well
. The expectation functional in Eq. (
3
) then rewrites to:

\mathbb{E}((u_{h}-\mathbb{E}(u_{h}))^{r})=\int_{\varOmega}(u_{h}-\mathbb{E}(u_{h}))^{r}\mathbb{P}(\text{d}\omega),

(4)

in which the exact solution
u
is substituted by a semi-discretized solution
u_{h}\equiv u_{h}(x,\omega)
.

Due to the complexity of integrating

) analytically, in this paper, the focus is on the sampling-based Monte Carlo (MC) method

27
,
12
]
. The MC estimators further employ multi-level approximation of the finite element solution
u_{h}
, also known as the multi-level Monte Carlo (MLMC) method
[

,

,

, in Section

.

3.1
Monte Carlo estimation of mean

For an unbiased estimate of the statistic
g(\omega)
, one may take a symmetric function

\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(g)=\frac{1}{N}(g(\omega_{1})+g(\omega_{2})+...+g(\omega_{N})),

(5)

meaning that the estimate does not depend on the order in which observations were taken. The existence and uniqueness of such a choice are given in
[
19
]
. Under the assumption that each sample
u_{h}(x,\omega_{i})
comes from the identical distribution as
u_{h}(x,\omega)
and by use of Eq. (
5
), one may reformulate
the
MC
estimate of the mean
\mu(u_{h})
as
[
27
,
12
]

\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h,N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{h}(x,{\omega}_{i}),

(6)

where
N
is the sample size of the random field
u_{h}(x,{\omega})
at a spatial location
x
.
Following this, the approximation error of the MC-based mean estimate
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}\equiv\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h,N})
compared to the exact mean reads

{e}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})=\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{E}(u),

(7)

which further can be rewritten as

e(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})=\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{E}(u_{h})+\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u).

(8)

Thus, the
mean square error (MSE)
reads

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}):=\mathbb{E}({e}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{E}(u_{h})+\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2})

(9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{E}(u_{h}))^{2})+(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}+

\displaystyle 2(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})-\mathbb{E}(u_{h})),

in which
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}
is assumed to be an unbiased estimator; meaning that

\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})=\mathbb{E}(u_{h})
,
and also,

\mathbb{E}((\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{E}(u_{h}))^{2})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})={\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})}/{N}
. Therefore, the previous equation reduces to:

\varepsilon(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})=\frac{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})}{N}+(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}.

(10)

The first term in the previous equation is the sampling error
\varepsilon_{s}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})
, which varies inversely to the sample size
N
. On the other hand, the second term describes square of the spatial discretization error i.e.

{\varepsilon_{d}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})}:=(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}
, whose change is proportional to the element size
h
.

3.1.1
Scale-invariant error estimator for Monte Carlo mean estimation

The MSE in Eq. (
10
) is an absolute error estimate and, hence, scale-dependent. Meaning that,
if each observation
u_{h}(x,\omega_{i})
is
linearly transformed in the form

a\,u_{h}(x,{\omega}_{i})+b,

in which
\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}
are constants,
then the Monte Carlo error in Eq. (
10
) will be affected in a square proportional manner—as

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(a\,u_{h}+b)=a^{2}\,\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})

(11)

and

(\mathbb{E}(a\,u_{h}+b)-\mathbb{E}(a\,u+b))^{2}=a^{2}(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}.

(12)

For instance, consider a scenario of transforming the units of temperature (e.g., in heat transfer) from Kelvin (K) to degree Fahrenheit (
∘
F), i.e.,
∘
F=9/5(K-273.15)+32 or the units of the displacement field—in mechanics—from a millimetre (mm) to a metre (m), i.e., m=
10^{-3}
mm. Consequently, the absolute error defined in Eq. (
10
) is now read in newly transformed units (
∘
F or m) instead of the original units (K or mm).

This leads to an issue of non-standardised proficiency comparison of MC complexity between such two differently scaled entities, which is not suitable in practical applications. Moreover, a further complication arises when one wants to compare convergence between two different MC moments for a given magnitude of MSE. As an example, under linear transformation as shown in Eqs. (
11
) and (
12
), the MSE in the MC mean estimator is scaled by a factor of
a^{2}
, whereas in the MC variance estimate, the scaling unit
a
would be set to the power of four; more information on this will follow in Section
3.2
.

To overcome this issue, one must define a scale-invariant version of MSE as defined in Eq. (
10
). To this end, we introduce a statistical normalizing function for the MC mean estimate
\zeta_{m}(u_{h})
that must inhibit the following properties:

1.

It should satisfy the condition
\zeta_{m}(a\,u_{h}+b)=a^{2}\,\zeta_{m}(u_{h})
.

2.

It must be finite and greater than zero, i.e.,
0<\zeta_{m}(u_{h})<\infty
.

3.

It should be unbiased and of minimum variance.

Keeping this in mind, as per t-statistics, we consider
\zeta_{m}(u_{h})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})
as a normalization entity
[
34
,
11
]
, given that
0<\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})<\infty
(meets the second requirement).
Clearly,
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})
satisfies the first condition (see Eq. (
11
)), unlike the common practise of taking the square of the mean value
\mathbb{E}(u_{h})^{2}
as a standardising constant, which violates this requirement.
Following this, the new type of standardised error estimate is defined as

\mathring{e}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}):=\frac{e(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})}{\sqrt{\zeta_{m}(u_{h})}}.

(13)

Finally, by normalizing Eq. (
10
), one obtains the squared error estimate

{\mathring{\varepsilon}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}):=\mathbb{E}({\mathring{e}}^{2})=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}}{\zeta_{m}(u_{h})},

(14)

in which the first term is the scale-invariant sampling accuracy
\mathring{\varepsilon}_{s}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})
, and the second term represents the square of the new normalized discretization error,

{{\mathring{\varepsilon}_{d}}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})}:={\varepsilon_{d}^{2}}/{{\zeta_{m}(u_{h})}}
.

3.1.2
Unbiased estimation of MC mean normalizing function

As in general, the variance
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})
is not known; it is estimated. For this purpose, one may use the estimator
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\text{ar}^{\textrm{MC}}(u_{h,N})
, which is determined by the
MC
procedure with
N
random draws. However,
in such a case, the use of a symmetric function as in Eq. (
5
) does not lead to an unbiased estimator anymore.

Following
[
19
]
, of all unbiased estimators of variance, the only one that is symmetric and has minimum variance is

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})\approx\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h,N})=\frac{NS_{2}-S_{1}^{2}}{N(N-1)}.

(15)

This estimator is known as the second h-statistic
[
9
,
29
]
. As this satisfies the third condition listed in the previous section, we consider
\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h,N}):=\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h,N})
as the estimated normalizing function.
In Eq. (
15
),
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are the power sums, given as
S_{r}\equiv S_{r}(u_{h,N}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{h}(\omega_{i})^{r}
for
r=1,2
.
Therefore, rewriting Eq. (
13
), where
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})
is estimated by
\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}
as

{\widehat{\mathring{e}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})}=\frac{e(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})}{\sqrt{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h,N})}},

(16)

one obtains a new formulation on the scale-invariant MSE (from Eq. (
14
)) in the form:

{\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{(\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}}{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h,N})}.

(17)

Certainly, the computational complexity of the scale-invariant MC mean estimate is similar to the conventional procedure (as shown in
[
7
,
25
]
), but it must be redefined with respect to the total NMSE, which is shown below.

Proposition 3.1

Let us consider
c_{\alpha},c_{\gamma},\alpha
and
\gamma
as positive constants, and then one may define the error bounds:

\begin{split}&(i)\quad\frac{|\mathbb{E}(u_{h})-\mathbb{E}(u)|}{\sqrt{\zeta_{m}(u_{h})}}\leq c_{\alpha}h^{\alpha},\\
&(ii)\quad\mathcal{C}(u_{h})\leq c_{\gamma}h^{-\gamma}.\end{split}

Note that, here, the second relation denotes the cost of computing one sample of
u_{h,N}
.
Then, for any
0<\mathring{\epsilon}<e^{-1}
, the MC mean estimator
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}
with
N=\mathcal{O}(\mathring{\epsilon}^{-2})
and
h=\mathcal{O}(\mathring{\epsilon}^{-1/\alpha})
satisfies the NMSE
{\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})<\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}
. Therefore, the corresponding computational cost is given as

\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}})=N\,\mathcal{C}(u_{h})\leq c\,\mathring{\epsilon}^{-2-\gamma/\alpha},

where,
c>0
.

3.2
Monte Carlo estimation of variance

The previously derived estimate in Eq. (
15
) is characterised by an approximation error given as

{e}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})=\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u),

(18)

in which
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u)
denotes the exact variance of the solution
u
.
Following this, the MSE of the variance estimator reads:

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}):=\mathbb{E}({e}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})+\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u))^{2})

(19)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h}))^{2})+(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u))^{2}+

\displaystyle 2(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u))(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})).

Having that
\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}
is an unbiased estimator, meaning that
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h}),

one may further rewrite the previous equation to

{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})+({\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(u_{h})-{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(u))^{2},

(20)

in which the first and second terms are the sampling
\varepsilon_{s}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})
and the square of discretization errors
\varepsilon_{d}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u)
, respectively.
In the previous equation, the variance of the second-order statistic is derived by
[
6
]
and reads

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}):=\frac{1}{N}\left(\mu_{4}(u_{h})-\frac{\mu_{2}(u_{h})^{2}(N-3)}{(N-1)}\right),

(21)

where
\mu_{2}(u_{h})
and
\mu_{4}(u_{h})
represent the second and fourth central population moments, respectively.
For brevity, one may further rewrite the previous equation as

\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}):=\frac{{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h})}{N},

(22)

where

{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h}):=\mu_{4}(u_{h})-\frac{\mu_{2}(u_{h})^{2}(N-3)}{(N-1)}.

(23)

Consequently, by substituting Eq. (
22
) in Eq. (
20
), one obtains

{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})=\frac{{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h})}{N}+({\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(u_{h})-{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(u))^{2}.

(24)

Similar to Eq. (
10
), here the first term defines the statistical error, which is inversely proportional to the number of samples
N
, and the second part represents the square of the discretization error, directly proportional to parameter
h
.

3.2.1
Scale-invariant error estimator for Monte Carlo variance estimation

As explained in Section
3.1
, for any linear transformation of
u_{h}
, the MSE in the above equation would change to bi-quadratic form. This is because of the characteristics of entities in Eq. (
23
):

\mu_{4}(a\,u_{h}+b)=a^{4}\,\mu_{4}(u_{h})

(25)

and

{\mu_{2}}^{2}(a\,u_{h}+b)=a^{4}\,{\mu_{2}}^{2}(u_{h}).

(26)

Therefore, it is clear that

{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(a\,u_{h}+b)=a^{4}\,{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h})

(27)

and also that

(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(a\,u_{h}+b)-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(a\,u+b))^{2}=a^{4}(\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h})-\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u))^{2}.

(28)

Similar to the standardising function
\zeta_{m}(u_{h})
in Section
3.1.1
, the normalization entity for the MC variance estimation
\zeta_{v}(u_{h})\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
must satisfy the condition
\zeta_{v}(a\,u_{h}+b)=a^{4}\,\zeta_{v}(u_{h})
. Also, the other two requirements, i.e.,
0<\zeta_{v}<\infty
and
\zeta_{v}
is an unbiased estimate and of minimum variance, must be met.
From Eq. (
27
), it is clear that
{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h})
holds the first listed requirement, and further assuming that it also follows the second condition, i.e.,
0<{{\mathbb{V}}_{2}}(u_{h})<\infty
, we consider
\zeta_{v}(u_{h})={\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h})
.
Following this, the new error estimate is given by normalizing Eq. (
18
) in the form:

\mathring{e}=\frac{e}{\sqrt{\zeta_{v}(u_{h})}}.

(29)

This further leads to a new scale-invariant MSE of the MC variance estimate

\mathring{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}):=\mathbb{E}({\mathring{e}}^{2})=\frac{1}{N}+\frac{({\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(u_{h})-{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}}(u))^{2}}{{\zeta_{v}}(u_{h})},

(30)

in which the first term is the scale-invariant sampling error
\mathring{\varepsilon}_{s}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}})
and the second term represents the square of the dimensionless discretization error,
{\mathring{\varepsilon}_{d}}^{2}(\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}):={{\varepsilon_{d}^{2}}}/{{{\zeta_{v}}(u_{h})}}
.

3.2.2
Unbiased estimation of MC variance normalizing function

The quantity
{\mathbb{V}}_{2}(u_{h})
in Eq. (
30
) is analytical and not known. To determine the unbiased estimate of
{\mathbb{V}}_{2}
, one must obtain unbiased estimates of entities
\mu_{4}(u_{h})
and
\mu_{2}(u_{h})^{2}
in Eq. (
23
).
To this end, the unbiased approximation of the fourth central moment
\mu_{4}(u_{h})
is computed by the fourth h-statistic
[
9
]
:

\mu_{4}(u_{h})\approx\text{h}_{4}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h,N}):=\frac{1}{N(N-1)(N-2)(N-3)}\bigg(-3S_{1}^{4}+6NS_{1}^{2}S_{2}\\
+(9-6N)S_{2}^{2}+(-4N^{2}+8N-12)S_{1}S_{3}+(N^{3}-2N^{2}+3N)S_{4}\bigg),

(31)

whereas the unbiased estimator of
\mu_{2}(u_{h})^{2}
is the polyache
\text{h}_{\left\{2,2\right\}}
according to
[
37
,

4
Multilevel Monte Carlo method

The Eqs. (
10
), (
14
), (
24
) and (
30
)
signify that to attain an overall higher level of accuracy, one requires a very fine resolution of the finite element mesh grid and a very large number of
MC
samples. This demands a tremendous amount of computational effort, making the algorithm practically infeasible. Therefore, the desired moments are further estimated by a variance reduction technique in a multilevel fashion following
[
20
,
14
,
15
,
10
,
7
]
.

4.1
Multilevel Monte Carlo estimation of mean

Let
\left\{l=0,1,2...,L\right\}
be a generalised increasing sequence—in the context of decreasing element size
h
—of nested mesh-grids
\mathcal{P}_{l}
, a regular (non-degenerate) partition of the computational domain
\mathcal{G}
of the problem described in Eq. (
1
).
Here
l
denotes the mesh level, and
L
represents the finest mesh.
The goal of the
MLMC
method is to determine the statistics (such as the mean in this case) of the solution
u_{h_{L}}(\omega)
on the finest level
L
.
To this end, by exploiting the linearity of the expectation operator, one may express the
MLMC
(also denoted by ML) mean estimate of the mean
\mu(u_{h_{L}})
using a set of samples
\left\{N_{l}\right\}:=\left\{N_{0},N_{1},\!...,N_{L}\right\}
as
[
20
]

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}(u_{h_{L},\left\{N_{l}\right\}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})+\sum_{l=1}^{{L}}\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h_{l},N_{l}}-u_{h_{l-1},N_{l}})

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}}).

Here
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})
is the
MC
estimator of mean
\mu(u_{h_{0}})
on level
l=0
using
N_{0}
samples, and
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h_{l},N_{l}}-u_{h_{l-1},N_{l}})
represents the approximation of mean
\mu(u_{h_{l},N_{l}}-u_{h_{l-1},N_{l}})
with
l>0
and
N_{l}
samples. Furthermore,
\mu^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})
is the
MC
estimator of
\mu(Y_{h_{l}})
such that for
l=0
,
Y_{h_{0},N_{0}}=u_{h_{0},N_{0}}
; otherwise,
Y_{h_{l},N_{l}}:=u_{h_{l},N_{l}}-u_{h_{l-1},N_{l}}
. Note that each term
Y_{h_{l},N_{l}},\ l\geq{0}
, is sampled independently, whereas when
l>0
, the quantities
u_{h_{l},N_{l}}
and
u_{h_{l-1},N_{l}}
in
Y_{h_{l},N_{l}}
are considered to be strongly correlated—meaning that
u_{h_{l},N_{l}}
and
u_{h_{l-1},N_{l}}
are sampled from the same random seed.

As the mean estimate on the finest grid
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}\equiv\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}(u_{h_{L},\left\{N_{l}\right\}})
is obtained as the telescopic sum of the difference of
MC
mean estimates on the coarser grids, the MSE corresponding to Eq. (
10
) takes the form:

\varepsilon(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})=\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\frac{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(Y_{h_{l}})}{N_{l}}+(\mathbb{E}(u_{h_{L}})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}.

(35)

The above error consists of two terms: the variance of the estimator
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}(u_{h_{L},\left\{N_{l}\right\}})
, given as
\varepsilon_{s}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})
, and the square of the
spatial discretization error, i.e.,
\varepsilon_{d}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}):=(\mathbb{E}(u_{h_{L}})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}
.

4.1.1
Scale-invariant multilevel Monte Carlo estimation of mean

Similar to the MC error estimate for the mean in Eq. (
10
), Eq. (
35
) depicts a classical absolute error estimate. As a result, the convergence of the MLMC algorithm strongly depends on the solution magnitude.
Hence, by assuming
\zeta_{m}(u_{h_{L}})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{L}})
as a standardising function (the properties of which are listed in Section
3.1.1
), we suggest a new scale-invariant MSE estimate following Eq. (
13
):

\mathring{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})=\frac{1}{\zeta_{m}(u_{h_{L}})}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\frac{\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(Y_{h_{l}})}{N_{l}}\right)+\frac{(\mathbb{E}(u_{h_{L}})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}}{\zeta_{m}(u_{h_{L}})}.

(36)

However, the normalization term
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{L}})
here is generally not known. Hence, under the assumption that for any value of
l
,
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{l}})
will be approximately constant
[
15
,
7
]
, one may substitute
\zeta_{m}(u_{h_{L}})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{L}})
with
\zeta_{m}(u_{h_{0}})=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{0}})
. Furthermore, under the consideration that
\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}}):=\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})
is the MC estimator of
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{0}})
, and by defining the MC variance estimate of
\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(Y_{h_{l}}):=\mathbb{V}\text{ar}(u_{h_{l}}-u_{h_{l-1}})
via
\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})
, Eq. (
36
) therefore transforms to

{\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})=\frac{1}{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\frac{\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})}{N_{l}}\right)+\frac{(\mathbb{E}(u_{h_{L}})-\mathbb{E}(u))^{2}}{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})}.

(37)

The first entity here stands for the estimated scale-invariant multi-level sampling error
\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}_{s}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})
, whereas the second term defines the square of the dimensionless discretization error
\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}}_{d}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})
. Therefore, to attain an overall normalized mean square error (NMSE)
\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}
, it is adequate, for instance, to satisfy conditions such as
\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}_{s}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})<\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}/2
and
{\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}}_{d}^{2}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}})}<\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}/2
. Note that the equal splitting of
\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}

is not a requirement and can also be set otherwise—for more information on this, see
[
18
]
.

If
\mathcal{C}_{l}\equiv\mathcal{C}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})
is the computational cost of determining a single
MC
sample of
Y_{h_{l},N_{l}}
, then the overall cost of the estimator
\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}
is given as
\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\mu}^{\text{ML}}):=\sum_{l=0}^{L}N_{l}\mathcal{C}_{l}
.
Here, the optimum number of samples
N_{l}
on each level
l
is evaluated by solving an optimisation problem such that the condition
\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}_{s}}<\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}/2
is satisfied. As a result, the cost function

f(N_{l})=\text{arg}\min\limits_{N_{l}}\sum_{l=0}^{L}\left(N_{l}\mathcal{C}_{l}+\tau\frac{\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})}{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})N_{l}}\right)

(38)

is minimised, due to which the optimal samples
N_{l}
are calculated as

N_{l}=\tau\left({\frac{\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})}{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})\mathcal{C}_{l}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(39)

Here,
\tau
is the Lagrange multiplier, determined by

\tau\geq{{\frac{2}{\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}}}}\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\left(\frac{{\widehat{\text{h}}_{2}^{\text{MC}}(Y_{h_{l},N_{l}})\mathcal{C}_{l}}}{\widehat{\zeta}_{m}(u_{h_{0},N_{0}})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(40)

On the other hand, the set of meshlevels
\left\{l=0,1,2...,L\right\}
maybe optimally chosen (in a geometric or non-geometric sequence) for a given deterministic error s.t.
\widehat{\mathring{\varepsilon}}_{d}^{2}<\mathring{\epsilon}^{2}/2
. Typically, for PDE-based applications, the choice of coarse mesh
l=0
depends on the regularity of the solution
u(x)

[
7
]
. Following this, the finer mesh selection is based on an apriori mesh convergence study, where the finest mesh grid
l=L
can be fixed or can be adaptively selected
[
15
]
during the MLMC mean computation. For a more detailed discussion on the optimal selection of mesh hierarchies, see
[
18
]
.

The arguments for determining the total computational cost of the MLMC mean estimator follow the classical MLMC procedure; for example, see
[
14
,
20
]
. However, the difference is that the cost is described with respect to a given NMSE instead of the MSE.

Proposition 4.1

Let us consider the positive constants
c_{\alpha},c_{\beta},c_{\gamma},\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, given that

and

\displaystyle-\textrm{div}\,\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}(x),\quad\forall\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{G},

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}},\quad\forall\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Gamma_{D},

(56)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(x)\cdot\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{n}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{t}}(x),\quad\forall\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Gamma_{N},

describing the linear-elastic behaviour. Here,
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(x)
is the Cauchy stress tensor, which belongs to the space of second-order symmetric tensors
\mathrm{Sym}(d):=\left\{\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d})\ |\ \mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{T}}}\right\}
;
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the body force;
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{t}}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the surface tension on the Neumann boundary
\Gamma_{N}\subset\Gamma
, and
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{n}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the outward unit normal to
\Gamma_{N}
. For simplicity, a homogeneous boundary condition
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is considered on the Dirichlet boundary
\Gamma_{D}\subset\Gamma
. It is also possible to assume that
\Gamma_{D}\cap\Gamma_{N}=0
.

One may further describe the strain-displacement relation as

\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}+\nabla\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}^{T}\right),\quad\forall\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{G},

(57)

where
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x)\in\mathrm{Sym}(d)
denotes an infinitesimal second-order symmetric strain tensor. Finally, the material constitutive equation is of the linear form

\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(x)=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}):\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x),\quad\forall\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{G},

(58)

in which
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
represents a spatially varying fourth-order positive-definite symmetric elasticity tensor. Here, the notion of symmetry signifies the major (
C_{{ijkl}}=C_{klij}
) and minor symmetries (
C_{ijkl}=C_{jikl}=C_{ijlk}
). As a result, one can map a
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
tensor to a second-order tensor
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
, more generally known as the elasticity matrix.
The reduced
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
matrix belongs to a family of
(n\times n)
, where
n=d(d+1)/2
, real-valued positive-definite symmetric matrices:

\mathrm{Sym}^{+}(n)=\{C\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n})\mid C=C^{T},\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{z}}^{T}C\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{z}}>\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}},\forall\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{z}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}\}.

(59)

Accordingly, conforming to Voigt notation, Eq. (
58
) transforms to
\sigma(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})=C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\varepsilon(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
, with
\sigma(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varepsilon(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denoting the stress and strain vectors, respectively.

5.2
Reduced parametric modelling of material uncertainty

Material law as described previously is complicated when highly heterogeneous and anisotropic materials—such as bone tissue, see Section
6
—are to be modelled. To include aleatoric uncertainty with heterogeneity in Eq. (
58
), the material properties have to be modelled as random and spatially dependent. In this paper, a probabilistic point of view is studied, in which the
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
matrix is modelled as a matrix-valued second-order random field
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
on a probability space
(\varOmega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. In other words, the random elasticity matrix can be modelled as a mapping:

C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega):\mathcal{G}\times\varOmega\rightarrow\mathrm{Sym}^{+}(n).

(60)

Furthermore, in practise, the usual assumption is that the elasticity matrix
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
globally follows a certain type of symmetry (e.g., isotropic, orthotropic, etc.; see
[
8
,
5
]
for invariance classification of elasticity matrix
C
), even though the experiments today cannot provide this information with full certainty.
Therefore, to include uncertainty in the type of material symmetry, in this paper, we model the elasticity matrix
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}})
as per the reduced parametric approach (also commonly known as the non-parametric method)
[
32
,
33
,
17
]
.
That is, the random matrix
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
follows a specific type of symmetry only in the mean, whereas each of the realisations belongs to the triclinic system, which is the lowest order of symmetry for elasticity-type tensors.
This gives our model a full degree of freedom in a case where the predefined mean symmetry turns out to be incorrect; one would still be able to identify other types of invariances given experimental data. This is, however, not the case if we assume that each of the realisations is constrained.

Following this, we model the homogeneous mean matrix as
\mathbb{E}(C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega))={Q}^{T}{Q}
,
in which the term
{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
represents an upper triangular matrix—this square-type (Cholesky) factorization ensures the positive-definiteness of the mean matrix.
Then, to allow uncertainty into the model, the mean formulation is extended to

C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)={Q}^{T}{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega){Q},

(61)

such that

\mathbb{E}({{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)})={I}

(62)

holds. Here,
{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
is a matrix-valued random variable that also resides in
\mathrm{Sym}^{+}(n)
, the mean value of which is an identity matrix
{I}\in\mathrm{Sym}^{+}(n)
. In this manner, the mean behaviour is controlled by
\mathbb{E}(C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega))
, and the random fluctuations are governed by
{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
. To construct a random ensemble
{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
, one may use the maximum entropy optimisation principle under the constraints mentioned in the previous list (but with a mean defined in Eq. (
62
)).
Henceforth,
{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
can further be factorised as

{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)={U}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)^{T}{U}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega),

(63)

and by substitution in Eq. (
61
), one obtains

C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)={Q}^{T}{U}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)^{T}{U}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega){Q}.

(64)

Here,
{U}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the upper triangular random matrix with entries

{V}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{\delta_{{T}}}{\sqrt{d+1}}\theta_{ij}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega),\text{if}\ i<j\\
\frac{\delta_{{T}}}{\sqrt{d+1}}\sqrt{N_{c}\Gamma_{\alpha_{j}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)},\text{if}\ i=j,\end{cases}

(65)

given that the elements
{V}_{ij},i\leq j
are independent.
Observe that the non-diagonal upper triangular elements are modelled as
independent Gaussian random fields
\theta_{ij}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega),1\leq i\leq j\leq n
with zero mean and unit variance. On the other hand, the diagonal entries are gamma-distributed positive-definite random fields:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\alpha_{j}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)=F_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}^{-1}\circ\text{erf}(\theta_{ij}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)).

(66)

Here,

\alpha_{j}=(d+1)/(2\delta_{{T}}^{2})+(1-j)/2

(67)

is a positive real number;
erf
is the standard Gaussian distribution function; and
F_{\Gamma_{\alpha}}^{-1}
is the inverse gamma cumulative distribution function. This assures that the diagonal elements are strictly positive, and therefore, the random matrix
{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
also remains positive-definite.
Reverting to the above description in Eq. (
65
), where
N_{c}
is a normalisation constant of
the resultant function (given in Eq. (
66
)), the value of which equals 2.
Furthermore,
\delta_{{T}}:=[0,1]\in\mathbb{R}
defined as Eq. (
68
), is a scalar value that controls the dispersion of
{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
:

\delta_{{T}}=\left\{\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\parallel{T}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)-{{I}}\parallel^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}.

(68)

The coefficient of dispersion parameter
\delta_{{T}}
is chosen such that

\delta_{C}=\left[\frac{\mathbb{E}\left\{\lVert C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)-\mathbb{E}(C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega))\rVert^{2}\right\}}{\lVert\mathbb{E}(C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega))\rVert^{2}}\right]^{1/2}=\frac{\delta_{{T}}}{\sqrt{d+1}}\left[1+\frac{(\text{tr}(\mathbb{E}(C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega))))^{2}}{\text{tr}(\mathbb{E}(C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)))^{2}}\right]^{1/2}

(69)

holds. Here,
\delta_{C}:=[0,1]\in\mathbb{R}
is the coefficient of dispersion of the random matrix
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
.

5.3
Stochastic setting: linear elasticity

The description of the random elasticity matrix field
C(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)
leads to the apparent transformation of a linear-elastic material model to a stochastic model. The aim is to determine the random displacement vector field
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega):\mathcal{G}\times\varOmega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, the equilibrium equations are rewritten in the form

\displaystyle-\textrm{div}\,\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{G},\omega\in\varOmega,

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}(x,\omega)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{0}},\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{D},\omega\in\varOmega,

(70)

\displaystyle\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(x,\omega)\cdot\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{n}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{t}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{t}}(x),\quad\forall x\in\Gamma_{N},\omega\in\varOmega,

in which the boundary conditions and body forces
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{f}}(x)
remain deterministic.
Further, the linearized kinematics relationship is transformed to

\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x,\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}(x,\omega)+\nabla\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}(x,\omega)^{T}\right),\forall x\in\mathcal{G},\omega\in\varOmega,

(71)

where the
\nabla(\cdot)
operator is taken in a weak sense. Finally,
the constitutive law is represented by

\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(x,\omega)=\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{C}}(x,\omega):\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}(x,\omega),\forall x\in\mathcal{G},\omega\in\varOmega.

(72)

By carrying out the variational formulation of the above stochastic partial differential equations on
\mathcal{G}
and further discretizing in a finite element setting, one searches for the solution
\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega):\mathcal{U}_{h}\times\varOmega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
in a finite subspace,
\mathcal{U}_{h}
, as described in
[
30
,
22
]
.
More precisely, the goal of this study is to determine the total displacement scalar field in Euclidean norm, i.e.,
{u}_{h}^{(t)}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)=\|\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)\|
, and estimate the second-order statistics such as the mean and variance of sampled response
u_{h,N}^{(t)}:=[{u}_{h}^{(t)}(\mathchoice{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\textstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}}{\scriptscriptstyle\boldsymbol{x}},\omega_{i})]_{i=1}^{N}
using the scale-invariant MC and MLMC as detailed in Sections (
3
) and (
4
).

7
Conclusion

We present a novel scale-invariant approach for estimating the MLMC mean and variance. This is accomplished by using t-statistics to normalise the usual absolute error estimates used in classical MLMC approaches. The normalizing factors for mean and variance estimates are formulated to be finite, unbiased, have minimum variance, and remain robust under any linear transformation (such as scaling and addition) of the quantity of interest. We also introduce standardised errors for the MC mean and variance estimators. As a result, the technique lessens ambiguity and facilitates the interpretation of performance analysis between estimations and across various scales.

The scale-invariant MLMC (and MC) method is applied to a linear-elastic material model of a two-dimensional proximal human femur. The analysis considers uncertainties in the material description, which are modelled using a reduced parametric approach that includes heterogeneity and random anisotropy. The method propagates these uncertainties to estimate statistics like the mean and variance of the total displacement.

Through normalized error estimates, we compared the computational efficiencies of MLMC and MC estimators for both mean and variance. MLMC outperforms MC in terms of computational cost for both estimates. However, the variance estimate in MLMC requires a higher number of samples, making it more computationally expensive than the mean estimate. The complete normalization of sampling error reveals non-asymptotic behaviour in the MC cost between mean and variance, while MLMC costs exhibit an asymptotic relationship. Additionally, the difference in accuracy between mean and variance estimates is found to be small between the MLMC and MC methods.
