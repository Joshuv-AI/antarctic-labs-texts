Kac–Moody Symmetric Spaces: arbitary symmetrizable complex or almost split real type
Ralf Köhl, Christian Vock

Abstract
We revisit the theory of Kac–Moody symmetric spaces in order to include the affine case, complex numbers, and Galois descent to almost split real Kac–Moody symmetric spaces.



1
Introduction

Kac–Moody groups have been studied extensively since the 1980s, initiated by
[
KP85
]
and
[
Tit87
]
. However, for some reason Kac–Moody symmetric spaces have only been formally introduced in
[
FHHK20
]
, although the heuristic idea that Kac–Moody symmetric spaces might be useful has been around in particular in mathematical physics for some time.

The focus of
[
FHHK20
]
was on symmetric spaces for split real Kac–Moody groups modelled with maximal flats of minimal possible dimension in order to propose geometries that might allow a primitive action as in
[
Cap05
]
– as this might then help prove abstract simplicity of the acting group. It is thus not surprising that the methods of
[
FHHK20
]
failed for affine Kac–Moody groups.

In the present article we vary on the theme of Kac–Moody symmetric spaces and introduce models with higher-dimensional flats that finally provide examples of Kac–Moody symmetric spaces also in the affine case. The key difference in the modelling can actually be found already on Kac–Moody algebra level and is described in Remark
3.3
and Convention
3.4
. Not surprising, it hence turns out that the Kac–Moody symmetric spaces introduced here are identical to the ones introduced in
[
FHHK20
]
if and only if the generalized Cartan matrix is invertible (see Section
7
).

Moreover, in the present article we also include the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers (see Convention
3.5
), and we conduct Galois descent based on
[
Rém02
]
, in order to model Kac–Moody symmetric spaces also in the almost split real case; see Section
9
.

We took quite some effort to make the present article accessible independent from
[
FHHK20
]
, but it is quite natural that we need to borrow and cite a wealth of results from
[
FHHK20
]
; on top of that, also a detailed comparison of our results with the results from
[
FHHK20
]
is called for.

We stress again that the purpose of this article is to provide an alternative definition in order to approach Kac–Moody symmetric spaces, that allows to also cover the affine case. On top of that we deal with the complex case and the almost split real case.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem
9.2

Let
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
and let

be an (almost) split Kac–Moody

2
Basics on Symmetric Spaces

In
[
Loo69
]
, Loos analyzes Riemannian and pseudo-Riemannian symmetric spaces and states an abstract definition without any use of differential geometry. This makes the definition amenable to situations in which one tries to generalize symmetric spaces into a context without a differential structure.

Definition 2.1

(Definition 1, Chapter II in
[
Loo69
]
)

A topological space
X
with a continuous map, which is called
reflection
,
\mu\colon X\times X\to X
,
(x,y)\mapsto\mu(x,y)\coloneqq x.y
is called a
symmetric space

(X,\mu)
if the map satisfies the following properties:

S.1

x.x=x
for all
x\in X
,

S.2

x.(x.y)=y
for all
x,y\in X
,

S.3

x.(y.z)=(x.y).(x.z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
, and

S.4

for every
x\in X
there is a neighborhood
U
such that
x.y=y
implies
x=y
for all
y\in U
.

If the Riemannian symmetric space is of non-compact type, i.e.
X
is non-compact, one can use the global condition instead of the local condition
S.4
:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\textbf{S.4}_{\text{global}}&\forall x,y\in X\;\;y=y\Longrightarrow x=y.\end{matrix}

Since we are interested in generalizations of Riemannian symmetric spaces of non-compact type, the class of symmetric spaces that we consider in this article will automatically satisfy
\textbf{S.4}_{\text{global}}
instead of just
S.4
. Note that this in fact allows one to model symmetric spaces on sets instead of topological spaces.

A
morphism of symmetric spaces
is a (continuous) map
f\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
between two symmetric spaces
(X_{1},\mu_{1})
and
(X_{2},\mu_{2})
satisfying
f(\mu_{1}(x,y))=\mu_{2}(f(x),f(y))
. A pair
((X,\mu),\beta)
consisting of a symmetric space
(X,\mu)
and a point
\beta\in X
is called a
pointed symmetric space
. A morphism between pointed symmetric spaces is a morphism between their underlying symmetric spaces with the additional property that the base point is preserved.

In the rest of the article, if the map
\mu
is clear from the context, we will write
X
for the symmetric space pair
(X,\mu)
for short.

Example 2.2

I.

One of the first examples that comes to mind of a symmetric space is the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{n}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, the symmetric space is given by the pair
\mathbb{R}^{n}
together with the point reflection on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e. the symmetric space map
\mu_{\text{Eucl}}
is defined as
\mu_{\text{Eucl}}(x,y)\coloneqq 2x-y
.

II.

Considering the sphere with the corresponding geodesic reflection leads to an example, which satisfies only
S.4
.

III.

An example of a Riemannian symmetric space of non-compact type is the hyperbolic space. Since there are no closed geodesics, the property
\textbf{S.4}_{\text{global}}
is satisfied.

IV.

Another example is any topological group
G
with the reflection
\mu\colon G\times G\to G
,
\mu(g,h)\coloneqq hgh^{-1}
. Here
S.4
is in general not satisfied, but one can easily check that
S.1
-
S.3
are satisfied. For a detailed calculation of the first three axioms for topological groups, we refer to the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Porposition 4.2]
. There, an assumption is made that guarantees the fourth axiom, and this assumption also holds for the Kac–Moody situation, as we will see in
4.3
.

Define the
point reflection
at
x\in X
as the map

s_{x}\colon X\to X\,,\;y\mapsto\mu(x,y).

The product of two point reflections is called
transvection
and the group

\operatorname{Trans}(X)\coloneqq\langle s_{x}\circ s_{y}\mid x,y\in X\rangle

is called the
transvection group
of
X
.

Lemma 2.3

(p.64, line 15 of
[
Loo69
]
)

Let
(X,\mu)
be a symmetric space,
x
a point in
X
and let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(X)
be an automorphism of the symmetric space. Then

\alpha\circ s_{x}\circ\alpha^{-1}=s_{\alpha(x)}.

Proof
:

For each
p\in X
one calculates

\left(\alpha\circ s_{x}\circ\alpha^{-1}\right)\hskip-2.0pt(p)=\alpha\hskip-2.0pt\left(s_{x}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\alpha^{-1}(p)\right)\right)=\alpha\hskip-2.0pt\left(\mu\hskip-2.0pt\left(x,\alpha^{-1}(p)\right)\right)=\mu(\alpha(x),p)=s_{\alpha(x)}\hskip-1.0pt(p).

∎

By setting
\alpha=s_{y}
for
y\in X
in the previous lemma, we obtain the useful identity

\displaystyle s_{y}\circ s_{x}\circ s_{y}^{-1}=s_{y}\circ s_{x}\circ s_{y}=s_{s_{y}(x)}.

Remark 2.4

Consider a pointed symmetric space
(X,\mu)
with base point
b\in X
. We now want to describe the symmetric space via the
quadratic representation
, see
[
Loo69
, Section II.1]
or
[
FHHK20
, Remark 2.10]
. For
x\in X
define

t_{x}\coloneqq s_{x}\circ s_{b}\in\operatorname{Trans}(X)

and

T(X,b)\coloneqq\{t_{x}\mid x\in X\}.

Evidently, the map

\eta\colon X\to T(X,b)\,,\;x\mapsto t_{x}

is surjective. Since
t_{x}\circ s_{b}=s_{x}
and since
s_{x}=s_{y}
implies
x=y
, the map
\eta
is also injective.

Model a symmetric space on the set
T(X,b)
by defining the reflection map

\mu_{T}\colon T(X,b)\times T(X,b)\to T(X,b)\,,\;(s,t)\mapsto s\circ t^{-1}\circ s.

A direct calculation shows that
\eta:(X,\mu)\to(T(X,b),\mu_{T})
is an isomorphism of symmetric spaces. Therefore, one has to check that the reflection map of
(X,\mu)
is preserved, i.e.
\eta(\mu(x,y))=\mu_{T}(t_{x},t_{y})
. On the right-hand side one can calculate

\displaystyle\mu_{T}(t_{x},t_{y})

\displaystyle=t_{x}\circ t_{y}^{-1}\circ t_{x}

\displaystyle=(s_{x}\circ s_{b})\circ(s_{y}\circ s_{b})^{-1}\circ(s_{x}\circ s_{b})

\displaystyle=s_{x}\circ s_{b}\circ s_{b}\circ s_{y}\circ s_{x}\circ s_{b}

\displaystyle=s_{x}\circ s_{y}\circ s_{x}\circ s_{b}

\displaystyle\overset{\ref{Eq:Verkettung-SxSy}}{=}s_{s_{x}(y)}\circ s_{b}=t_{s_{x}(y)}.

And on the left-hand side one has

\eta(\mu(x,y))=\eta(s_{x}(y))=t_{s_{x}(y)}.

Recall from
[
FHHK20
, Section 2.15]
the following useful definitions and properties of an abstract symmetric space
(X,\mu)
:

•

A
midpoint
of two points
x,y\in X
is a point
m\in X
, such that
\mu(m,x)=y
and
\mu(m,y)=x
.

•

Call a subspace
U\subseteq X

–

a
reflection subspace
, if for all pair of points
x,y\in U
the image
\mu(x,y)
is contained in
U
.

–

midpoint convex
, if for all
x,y\in U
there is a midpoint of
x
and
y
in
U
.

•

Two points
x,y\in X

weakly commute
, if for every point
p\in X
applies
x.(p.(y.p))=y.(p.(x.p))
.

•

Two points
x,y\in X

commute
, if for all points
p,q\in X
applies
x.(p.(y.q))=y.(p.(x.q))
.

•

A reflection subspace
F\subseteq X
is a
(weak) flat
, if it is closed, midpoint convex, contains at least two points and if all points
x,y\in F
(weakly) commute.

Definition 2.5

(cp. Definition 2.23 from
[
FHHK20
]
)

A
Euclidean flat of rank
n
is a closed symmetric subspace
F\subseteq X
isomorphic to the
n
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{E}^{n}
as a symmetric space. For
n=1
, the flat is called a
geodesic
.

,

3.1
Kac–Moody Algebra

Definition 3.1

Let
\mathbb{A}=\left(a_{ij}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
be a square matrix over the integers. The matrix
\mathbb{A}
is called a
generalized Cartan matrix
if it satisfies the following properties

•

a_{ii}=2
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,

•

a_{ij}\leq 0
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
,
i\neq j
, and

•

a_{ij}=0
if and only if
a_{ji}=0
.

The generalized Cartan matrix
\mathbb{A}
is
indecomposable
if it cannot be decomposed into a non-trivial direct sum after rearranging the indices, cf.
[
Kac90
, §1.1]
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{A}
is
symmetrizable
if there is a symmetric matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and an invertible diagonal matrix
D\in(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{n\times n}
such that
\mathbb{A}=DB
, cf.
[
Kac90
, §2.1]
.

If a generalized Cartan matrix is the Cartan matrix of a finite-dimensional semisimple complex Lie algebra, then
\mathbb{A}
is of
spherical type
; otherwise,
\mathbb{A}
is
non-spherical
.

In
[
Kac90
]
the notion of a realization of
\mathbb{A}
is introduced, which assigns a triple to a generalized Cartan matrix, allowing the construction of a Kac–Moody algebra. As in the finite-dimensional situation, a Kac–Moody algebra is naturally related to several different groups; therefore, in order to define a Kac–Moody group associated to the algebra one needs a more subtle definition (see
[
Rém02
, Chapitre 8]
or
[
Mar18
, Definition 7.9]
).

Definition 3.2

Let
\mathbb{A}=\left(a_{ij}\right)_{i,j\in I}
be a generalized Cartan matrix with index set
I
, let
\Lambda
be a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module of finite rank and
\Lambda^{\vee}
the
\mathbb{Z}
-dual of
\Lambda
, and let
(c_{i})_{i\in I}\subset\Lambda
and
(h_{i})_{i\in I}\subset\Lambda^{\vee}
such that
c_{j}(h_{i})=a_{ij}
. The quintuple
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\left(I,\mathbb{A},\Lambda,(c_{i})_{i\in I},(h_{i})_{i\in I}\right)
is called a
Kac–Moody root datum
.

Consider a generalized Cartan matrix
\mathbb{A}
of arbitrary type and a corresponding Kac–Moody root datum
\mathcal{D}
. Define the
Cartan subalgebra

\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\Lambda^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}

with elements,
\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\coloneqq h_{i}\otimes 1
and define the dual space

\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{D}}^{*}\coloneqq\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}

with elements
\alpha_{i}\coloneqq c_{i}\otimes 1
. Call the elements
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\mid i\in I\}\subset\mathfrak{h}^{*}

simple roots
,
\Pi^{\vee}=\{\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\mid i\in I\}\subset\mathfrak{h}

simple coroots
and denote with
\Delta^{re}
the set of real roots (cf.
[
Kac90
, §5.1]
or
[
Mar18
, Chapter 6.1]
).

Remark 3.3

According to the approach proposed in Kac’s book
[
Kac90
, §1.1]
, one wants the sets
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\vee}
to be linearly independent. Therefore, in the case that
\mathbb{A}
is of affine type it follows from the relation
\langle\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}^{\vee}\rangle=a_{ij}\in\mathbb{A}
, that
\Pi
is linearly dependent. Hence, extending the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
solves this problem, as done in
[
Kac90
, Proof of Proposition 1.1]
and
[
Mar18
, Chapter 3.5 and Example 7.10]
: Let
\mathbb{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
and
l
be the rank of
\mathbb{A}
, then the extended Cartan subalgebra must be
\mathfrak{h}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2n-l}
. The dual space is also extended and constructed so that the roots still satisfy the relation to
\mathbb{A}
.

Let us now denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\mathbb{A}}
the root datum where the elements
c_{i}
and
h_{i}
are linearly independent and denote by
\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}}
the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}
-module, which has minimum rank with respect to these properties, i.e. the rank of
\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}}
is equal to
2n-l
. Similarly, we use
\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\vee}
to denote the
\mathbb{Z}
-dual. Moreover, denote by
\{v_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq 2n-l\}
the
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of the extended
\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}}
, where
h_{i}=v_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. We follow here the notation and idea of
[
Mar18
, Example 7.10]
.

Following
[
Rém02
, Chapter 7]
or
[
Mar18
, Definition 7. 13]
, one can define a
Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{D}}
of type
\mathcal{D}
as a Lie algebra with generators
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{D}}
,
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, where
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
are to be symbols satisfying the following relations.

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{D}},\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{D}}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle[h,e_{i}]

\displaystyle=c_{i}(h)e_{i}

\displaystyle[h,f_{i}]

\displaystyle=-c_{i}(h)f_{i}

\displaystyle[e_{i},f_{j}]

\displaystyle=-\delta^{i}_{j}h_{i}

\displaystyle(\operatorname{ad}e_{i})^{1-a_{ij}}e_{j}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{ad}f_{i})^{1-a_{ij}}f_{j}=0\;(i\neq j)

for
h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
i,j\in I
. In the following, we denote with
\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{A})
the Kac–Moody algebra of type
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\mathbb{A}}
.

Convention 3.4

For the rest of the article we consider the root datum
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\mathbb{A}}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is a indecomposable, symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix of size
n

.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a generalized Cartan matrix and consider
\Pi=\{\alpha_{i}\mid i\in I\}
, then define the

as

Q\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{i}

(2)

and call an element
\alpha\neq 0\in Q

Denote by

. Furthermore, a root

is called

Next, recall the

of

\mathcal{W}<\operatorname{GL}(\mathfrak{h})

A root

3.2
The Tits Functor

Let
R
be a ring and recall that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\mathbb{A}}=\left(I,\mathbb{A},\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}},(c_{i})_{i\in I},(h_{i})_{i\in I}\right)
. Then a split torus scheme (cp.
[
Mar18
, Section 7.4]
) is given by

T\colon\mathbb{Z}\text{-alg}\to\textbf{Grp}\,,\;T(R)\coloneqq\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}R^{\times},

alternatively, one can consider
T(R)
as
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{grp}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}},R^{\times})
:

\Lambda^{\vee}\otimes R^{\times}\to\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{grp}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}},R^{\times})\,,\;h\otimes r\mapsto\left(\lambda\mapsto r^{\lambda(h)}\right).

Since
\{v_{i}\}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\Lambda_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\vee}
(see
3.3
) and the rank is equal
2n-l
, the set

\{r^{v_{i}}\mid r\in R^{\times}\,,\;1\leq i\leq 2n-l\}

generates
T(R)
, cf.
[
Mar18
, Example 7.55]
. If
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, one can define an exponential map between
\mathfrak{h}
and
T
as follows

\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}\colon\mathfrak{h}\to T(\mathbb{K})\,,\;(v_{i}\otimes r)\mapsto(v_{i}\otimes\exp(r)),

where
\exp\colon\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{K}^{\times}
denotes the usual exponential map
\exp:(\mathbb{R},+)\to(\mathbb{R}^{\times},\cdot)
or
\exp:(\mathbb{C},+)\to(\mathbb{C}^{\times},\cdot)
.
Since
T(\mathbb{K})
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{2n-l}
and
\mathfrak{h}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}^{2n-l}
, the exponential map
\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}
corresponds to the natural exponential map
\exp\colon\mathbb{K}^{2n-l}\to(\mathbb{K}^{\times})^{2n-l}
.

A
basis of type
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\mathbb{A}}
(as in
[
Tit87
, Section 2]
, also
[
Mar18
, Definition 7.77]
) is defined as a triple
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{G},(\varphi_{i})_{i\in I},\eta\right)
where
\mathcal{G}\colon\mathbb{Z}\text{-alg}\to\textbf{Grp}
is a group functor,
(\varphi_{i})_{i\in I}
is a assortment of morphisms of functors
\varphi_{i}\colon\operatorname{SL}_{2}\to\mathcal{G}
and a morphism of functors
\eta\colon T\to\mathcal{G}
. One calls the group functor
\mathcal{G}

Tits functor of type
\mathcal{D}
if its satisfies the following axioms:

KMG.1

If
\mathbb{K}
is a field,
\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{K})
is generated by
\varphi_{i}(\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{K}))
and by
\eta(T(\mathbb{K}))
.

KMG.2

For every ring
R
, the homomorphism
\eta\colon\mathfrak{T}_{\Lambda}(R)\to\mathcal{G}(R)
is injective.

KMG.3

For
i\in I
and
r\in R^{\times}
, one has

\varphi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}r&0\\
0&r^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=\eta\left(r^{h_{i}}\right),

where
r^{h_{i}}
denotes the element
\lambda\mapsto r^{\langle\lambda,h_{i}\rangle}
of the torus for each
h_{i}\in\Lambda^{\vee}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

KMG.4

If
\iota\colon R\to\mathbb{K}
is an injective morphism of a ring
R
in a field
\mathbb{K}
, then
\mathcal{G}(\iota)\colon\mathcal{G}(R)\to\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{K})
is injective.

KMG.5

There is a homomorphism

\operatorname{Ad}\colon\mathcal{G}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{Aut}([\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{A}),\mathfrak{g}(\mathbb{A})])

whose kernel is contained in
\eta(\mathfrak{T}_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{C}))
, such that for
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
i\in I
,

\operatorname{Ad}\left(\varphi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}1&c\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\right)=\exp(\operatorname{ad}ce_{i}),\;\operatorname{Ad}\left(\varphi_{i}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
c&1\end{pmatrix}\right)=\exp(\operatorname{ad}-cf_{i}),

and for
t\in\mathfrak{T}_{\Lambda}(\mathbb{C})
and
i\in I
,

\operatorname{Ad}(\eta(t))(e_{i})=t(c_{i})\cdot e_{i},\;\operatorname{Ad}(\eta(t))(f_{i})=t(-c_{i})\cdot f_{i}.

Note, that on fields, the Tits functor is unique up to isomorphism, see
[
Mar18
, Theorem 7.82]
.

Convention 3.5

For the remainder of this article, let
\mathbb{K}\in{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}}
and denote by

This section provides a short overview of the basic notions of a (twin) building. For more details see
[

(cp. Definition 5.1 from

3.4
A Characteristic Subgroup

A very useful subgroup to study symmetric spaces which belongs to a Kac–Moody group is the
centered subgroup
, that is the subgroup only generated by the root groups.

Definition 3.11

Let
G
be a split Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
. Denote with
G^{\dagger}<G
the subgroup of
G
generated by the root subgroups of
G
, i.e.

G^{\dagger}\coloneqq\langle U_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Delta^{re}\rangle.

The group is called a
centered subgroup
.

Note, that from
3.10
it is clear, that
G^{\dagger}
is a normal subgroup of
G
. From
[
AB08
, Corollary 8.79]
it follows that the torus of a Kac–Moody group can be described as the intersection of all normalizers of the root groups. We use this fact to define a torus for
G^{\dagger}
:

T^{\dagger}\coloneqq\bigcap_{\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}}N_{G^{\dagger}}(U_{\alpha})=T\cap G^{\dagger}.

Since the RGD axioms are manly using root groups, it follows that
(G^{\dagger},(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}},T^{\dagger})
forms an RGD system. Moreover, according to
[
AB08
, Proposition 8.82]
, the kernel of the action of
G
on the associated twin building
\Delta_{\pm}
is contained in the torus
T
, so that

N_{G^{\dagger}}(T^{\dagger})/T^{\dagger}\cong\mathcal{W}.

Hence the type of the twin building associated to
G^{\dagger}
is the same as the type of the twin building associated to
G
.

Lemma 3.12

Let
G
be a split Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
. Then
G^{\dagger}
is the derived subgroup of
G
, in particular a characteristic subgroup, i.e.

f(G^{\dagger})\subseteq G^{\dagger}

for all
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
.

Proof
:

By the RGD axioms one has
G=TG^{\dagger}
(see
[
AB08
, Chapter 8]
). Moreover,
G^{\dagger}
is a normal subgroup of
G
, since

T=\bigcap_{\alpha\in\Delta^{re}}{N_{G}(U_{\alpha})},

cp.
[
AB08
, Corollary 8.79]
. Furthermore
G/G^{\dagger}
is abelian, therefore
[G,G]\subseteq G^{\dagger}
.

On the other hand, by definition
G^{\dagger}
is generated by the root subgroups, in particular
G^{\dagger}
is generated by the standard rank one subgroups

G_{i}=\langle U_{\alpha_{i}},U_{-\alpha_{i}}\rangle\cong\operatorname{SL}(2,k).

Due to the fact that
\operatorname{SL}(2,k)
is perfect for
k\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
, one deduces that
G^{\dagger}
is perfect. Thus

[G,G]\subseteq G^{\dagger}=[G^{\dagger},G^{\dagger}]\subseteq[G,G]

which finishes the proof.
∎

Note, that there is also an action of
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
on
G^{\dagger}
given by the homomorphism
\operatorname{Aut}(G)\to\operatorname{Aut}(G^{\dagger})
which restricts any
f\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
on
G^{\dagger}
.

Remark 3.13

Since we are interested in a generalization of
[
FHHK20
]
, it is useful to know that the centered subgroup
G^{\dagger}
corresponds to the type of Kac-Moody group used by the authors in
[
FHHK20
]
to develop the theory of symmetric Kac–Moody spaces. In particular, the type of Kac–Moody groups used in
[
FHHK20
]
is called
centered
, which means that the group is generated only by its root subgroups, without an additional torus. In the language of root data, the group is generated by a simply connected root datum, cf.
[
Mar18
, Example 7.11]
.

3.5
The Chevalley Involution

The first goal of this article is to construct a symmetric space with respect to a Kac–Moody group. For this purpose, it is useful and important to define an involution. Therefore let
G
be a Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
of type
\mathbb{A}
. Then

denotes a continuous involution which is given for

for

and

. Since the complex conjugation acts trivially on the reals, it can be omitted in the case

. In
[

, §8.2]
it is described how this involution is obtained by lifting the Chevalley involution of a Kac–Moody algebra

(cf.
[
Kac90
, §1.3]
) to a continuous involution on
G
. In particular, this means that
\Theta
maps
U_{\alpha}
to
U_{-\alpha}
and stabilizes the torus
T
.

Denote by
K=G^{\Theta}
the set of fixed points of
\Theta
and define by
\tau\colon G\to G
,
g\mapsto g\Theta(g)^{-1}
the
twist map
of
G
. In the following lemma, we define useful subgroups and analyze their properties. The statements are based on
[
FHHK20
, Lemma 3.24]
and
[
FHHK20
, Lemma 3.26]
.

Lemma 3.14

Let
G
be a split Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
. Define the subgroups
A\coloneqq\tau(T)
and
M\coloneqq K\cap T
. Then it follows that
T\cong M\times A
and the following properties are satisfied

(A1)

For
g,h\in G
one has
hK=gK\Leftrightarrow\tau(g)=\tau(h)
and
\tau^{-1}(e)=K
.

(A2)

The subgroup
A
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2n-l}
.

(A3)

For
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
the subgroup
M
is isomorphic to
(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{2n-l}
and it is called the
maximal compact subgroup
and in the case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}

M
is the
torsion subgroup
, i.e. unique maximal finite subgroup of order
2^{2n-l}
.

(A4)

M\cap A=\{e\}=K\cap A

(A5)

K\cap B_{\pm}=M
.

Proof.

For
(A1)
let
g,h\in G
and it follows from
gK=hK
that there is a
k\in K
such that
g=hK
. For one direction one calculates

gK=hK\Rightarrow\tau(g)=\tau(hk)=\tau(h),

and for the other direction

\displaystyle\tau(g)=\tau(h)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow g\Theta(g)^{-1}=h\Theta(h)^{-1}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\Theta(h^{-1}g)=h^{-1}g

\displaystyle\Rightarrow h^{-1}g\in K

\displaystyle\Rightarrow gK=hK.

To see that
\tau^{-1}(e)=K
, let
g\in G
be an element with
\tau(g)=e
. Then calculate

\tau(g)=e\Leftrightarrow\Theta(g)=g\Leftrightarrow g\in K.

In order to prove
(A2)
, we first check, that for an element
t\in T
holds

\tau(t)=\begin{cases}t^{2}&\text{for }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}\\
|t|^{2}&\text{for }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}.\end{cases}

This follows directly from the definition of the action of
\Theta
on
T
, to be precise, for
t\in T
applies
\Theta(t)=\overline{t}^{-1}
for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and
\Theta(t)=t^{-1}
for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
. Hence, it follows that
A=\tau(T)\cong(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2n-l}
, since the dimension of
T
is
2n-l
.

Next, we want to check
(A4)
. Let
g\in K\cap A\subseteq T
. Then by definition of
K
and
\Theta
it follows that

g=\Theta(g)=\overline{g}^{-1}.

Thus
g\overline{g}=e
and from the fact that
A=\tau(T)\cong(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2n-l}
it follows that
g=e
. In particular, the statement that
M\cap A=\{e\}
is true follows then directly from
M\subset K
.

Finally, we prove the statement of
(A5)
. Therefore recall, that by the definition of
B_{\pm}
it follows that
\Theta(B_{\pm})=B_{\mp}
and that
T=B_{+}\cap B_{-}
. Note, that this is based on the fact that
U_{+}\cap U_{-}=\{e\}
. Thus, it follows that
K\cap B_{\pm}=K\cap T
.
Now let
g\in K\cap B_{\pm}
, then follows that
g\in T
. Using the decomposition
T\cong M\times A
we can write
g=ma
for
m\in M
and
a\in A
., one calculates

\tau(g)=\begin{cases}g^{2}=m^{2}a^{2}=a^{2}&\text{for }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}\\
|g|^{2}=|m|^{2}|a|^{2}=|a|^{2}=a^{2}&\text{for }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}.\end{cases}

On the other hand, by the fact that
g\in K
follows that
\tau(g)=e
. This leads to
a^{2}=e
and since
a\in A\cong(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2n-l}
it follows that
\tau(g)=e
if and only if
a=e
.

The fact that
T\cong M\times A
follows from the polar decomposition:

T\cong\begin{cases}(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^{2n-l}\cong(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2n-l}\times(\mathbb{S}^{1})^{2n-l}&\text{for }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}\\
(\mathbb{R}^{\times})^{2n-l}\cong(\mathbb{R}_{>0})^{2n-l}\times\{\pm 1\}^{2n-l}&\text{for }\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

∎

3.6
The Iwasawa Decomposition

In the theory of symmetric spaces, it is necessary to define flats because they are the most important principles for understanding actions and causal boundaries. To do so, one needs the topological Iwasawa decomposition, i.e. that the multiplication induces a homeomorphism

m_{\pm}\colon U_{\pm}\times A\times K\to G.

In general, this is known for centered Kac–Moody groups, i.e. Kac–Moody groups generated only by their root groups without any additional torus, see
[
KP85
, Section 3]
.

First we will prove the algebraic statement that for a Kac–Moody group
G
there is a decomposition
G=U_{\pm}AK
which induces a bijection. After establishing the algebraic Iwasawa decomposition, we will use the same strategy as in the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 3.31 (ii)]
to prove the topological assertion for Kac–Moody groups over
\mathbb{K}
of arbitrary type
\mathbb{A}
.

The following section is mainly based on
[
FHHK20
, Section 3.30]
. Denote by
\mathbb{K}
the field of real or complex numbers and with
\mathbb{A}
a symmetrizable, indecomposable generalized Cartan matrix.

Proposition 3.15

Let
G
be a Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
and of arbitrary type
\mathbb{A}
. Then
G
decomposes into

G=U_{\pm}AK=KAU_{\pm}.

Moreover, the maps,

m_{\pm}\colon U_{\pm}\times A\times K\to G

given by multiplication, are bijective.

Proof
:

By
3.10
and
Section

3.4
, one can decompose
G
into
TG^{\dagger}
, where
T
is the torus and
G^{\dagger}
is the centered subgroup of
G
. Since
\mathbb{K}
plays no role in the algebraic decomposition, and
G^{\dagger}
is a centered Kac–Moody group, one can use
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 3.31 (ii)]
. Therefore denote by
K^{\dagger}
the fixed point set
(G^{\dagger})^{\Theta}
and by
B_{\pm}^{\dagger}
the (standard) Borel subgroup
B_{\pm}^{\dagger}=T^{\dagger}\ltimes U_{\pm}
. Then there is the decomposition

G^{\dagger}=B_{\pm}^{\dagger}K_{\pm}^{\dagger}.

Recall that
T=MA
,
M\subseteq K
by
3.14
and that
T
normalizes
U_{\pm}
. Also denote with
A^{\dagger}=\tau(T^{\dagger})
and with
M^{\dagger}=K\cap T
. All properties of
3.14
are also true for the corresponding subgroups in the context of
G^{\dagger}
. It follows

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=TG^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=TB_{\pm}^{\dagger}K^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=TU_{\pm}A^{\dagger}M^{\dagger}K^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=U_{\pm}(MA)A^{\dagger}K^{\dagger}=U_{\pm}AK

since
K\setminus K^{\dagger}
can by only contained in
T
and by
3.14

(A5)
it is contained in
M
. The other order of the decomposition results from the symmetry arguments.

To see that these maps
m_{\pm}
are bijective, we need to check injectivity. To do this, we assume that
u_{1}a_{1}k_{1}=u_{2}a_{2}k_{2}
. By reordering follows,

K\ni k_{1}k_{2}^{-1}=a_{1}^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}u_{2}a_{2}\in AU_{\pm}A\subseteq B_{\pm}.

Hence
a_{1}^{-1}u_{1}^{-1}u_{2}a_{2}\in K
and
B_{\pm}\cap K=M\subseteq T
, see
3.14
. It follows that

u_{1}^{-1}u_{2}\in U_{\pm}\cap T=\{1\}\Longrightarrow u_{1}=u_{2},

(cp.
[
AB08
, Lemma 7.62]
) and from this, that

a_{1}^{-1}a_{2}\in M\cap A=\{1\}\Longrightarrow a_{1}=a_{2}.

It follows that
k_{1}=k_{2}
and thus
m_{\pm}
is injective.
∎

In the following we consider only Kac–Moody groups of non-spherical type
\mathbb{A}
. In the case where
\mathbb{A}
is of spherical type, the Kac–Moody group of type
\mathbb{A}
is a finite-dimensional Lie group and the topological Iwasawa decomposition is a well-known fact, see for example
[
Kna02
, Theorem 6.46]
. Further, there are also proofs of the topological Iwasawa decomposition with respect to other groups, e.g. for
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
(
[
PR94
, Proposition 3.12]
) or in greater generality for reductive
\mathbb{R}
-groups (
[
PR94
, Theorem 3.9]
).

Now for the topological version, the idea is to use the same strategy as in the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 3.31 (ii)]
, to be precise we will construct the inverse functions and show that they are open. That we can use the same strategy as in
[
FHHK20
]
is based on the statement that every Kac–Moody group has a strong topological building, as shown by Grüning and the first-named author of this article in
[
HK23
, Appendix B]
for centered Kac–Moody groups. The very technical generalization for arbitrary split Kac–Moody groups can be found in
[
Voc23
, Appendix C]
. For an introduction to topological buildings see
[
HKM13
, Section 3.1]
and for strong topological buildings in particular see
[
HKM13
, Definition 3.21]
.

Lemma 3.16

Let
G
be a Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
and of type
\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is non-spherical. Then the fibration

p_{\pm}\colon\nicefrac{{G}}{{AU_{\pm}}}\to\nicefrac{{G}}{{B_{\pm}}}

is trivial.

Proof
:

First, note that
G
leads to a non-spherical strong topological twin building, according to a result by Grüning and the first-named author of this article, see
[
HK23
, Corollary B.8]
(or
[
Voc23
, Appendix C]
). Furthermore, note that
T\cong A\times M
as topological groups and that
U_{\mp}\times T\times U_{\pm}
is open. In particular the map
U_{\mp}\times T\times U_{\pm}\to B_{\mp}B_{\pm}
is a homeomorphism (cf.
[
AB08
, Section 8.8]
and
[
HK23
, Corollary B.7]
) and
B_{\mp}B_{\pm}
is open (cf.
[
HKM13
, Lemma 6.1]
). Hence

\{e\}\times M\times\{e\}\to U_{\mp}\times T\times U_{\pm}\to U_{\mp}\times A\times U_{\pm}

provides a local trivialization of the fibration

M\to\nicefrac{{G}}{{AU_{\pm}}}\to\nicefrac{{G}}{{B_{\pm}}},

in particular, every point in
G/AU_{\pm}
has a neighborhood that looks like an open set in
G/B_{\pm}
times
M
.

Since the non-spherical twin building associated to
G
is a strong topological twin building, it is contractible (cf.
[
HKM13
, Theorem 5.13]
) and thus the fibration
p_{\pm}
is trivial.
∎

For the next lemma we need the notion of
k_{\omega}
-spaces
: a Hausdorff topological space
X
is called a
k_{\omega}
-space, if there is a family of compact subspaces
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, called
k_{\omega}
-sequence, such that

X=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}X_{n}

and any open subset
Y\subseteq X
is open in
X
if and only if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

Y\cap X_{n}
is open in
X_{n}
. Call the pair
(X,(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})

k_{\omega}
-pair
. For more details about
k_{\omega}
-spaces see
[
FT77
]
or
[
FHHK20
, Definition 3.33 et seq.]
. Here we recall two lemmas about
k_{\omega}
-spaces for the reader’s convenience that we use in order to derive the topological Iwasawa decomposition.

Lemma 3.17

(Lemma 3.34 from
[
FHHK20
]
)

Let
(X,(X_{n}))
and
(Y,(Y_{n}))
be
k_{\omega}
pairs and let
f\colon X\to Y
be a continuous bijection such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Y_{n}\subset f(X_{m})
.

Then
f(X_{n})
is a
k_{\omega}
-sequence for
Y
and
f
is a homeomorphism.

Lemma 3.18

(Lemma 3.35 from
[
FHHK20
]
)

Let
(X,(X_{n}))
be a
k_{\omega}
pair and let
\pi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
be a finite-sheeted covering. Then
(\widetilde{X},\pi^{-1}(X_{n}))
is a
k_{\omega}
pair.

Now, we can prove the following, which is a version of
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 3.36]
adapted to the context of this article.

Lemma 3.19

Let
G
be a Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is non-spherical and define the maps

\displaystyle\iota_{\pm}\colon K

\displaystyle\to G/AU_{\pm}

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\mapsto kAU_{\pm}.

These maps are homeomorphisms.

Proof
:

The goal is to use
3.17
. According to the algebraic Iwasawa decomposition (cf.
3.15
), the maps
\iota_{\pm}
are bijections, since multiplication is continuous, these maps are also continuous.

Next, we recall that
G
acts strongly transitively on the associated twin building (cf.
3.10
) and we can therefore use the Birkhoff decomposition according to
[
AB08
, Proposition 6.75]
. Based on the Birkhoff decomposition we can define the following:

G_{l}^{\pm}\coloneqq\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{W},\,l(w)\leq l}B_{\pm}wB_{\pm},

and denote with
\widetilde{D}_{l,\pm}
the image of
G_{l}^{\pm}
in
G/AU_{\pm}
and with
D_{l,\pm}
the image of
G_{l}^{\pm}
in
G/B_{\pm}
. By
[
HKM13
, Corollary 3.13]
follows that

\left(\nicefrac{{G}}{{B_{\pm}}},\left(D_{l,\pm}\right)\right)

is a
k_{\omega}
-pair. Note that the preimage of
D_{l,\pm}
under the fibration
p_{\pm}
is exactly
\widetilde{D}_{l,\pm}
and that the fibration by
3.16
is trivial, so we can apply
3.18
, which works here, since the statement is just a topological assertion about
k_{\omega}
pairs, so we observe that

\left(\nicefrac{{G}}{{AU_{\pm}}},\left(\widetilde{D}_{l,\pm}\right)\right)

is also a
k_{\omega}
-pair.

In order to apply
3.17
, it remains to prove that
(K,(K_{l}^{\pm}))
is a
k_{\omega}
-pair, where
K_{l}^{\pm}\coloneqq K\cap G_{l}^{\pm}
. Therefore by
[
HKM13
, Corollary 7.11]
one knows that

G=\varinjlim G_{l}^{\pm}

and in particular that

K=\varinjlim K_{l}^{\pm}.

To conclude that
(K,(K_{l}^{\pm}))
is a
k_{\omega}
pair, we must show that the subsets
(K_{l}^{\pm})
are compact. Therefore, recall that
G
can be written as
KB_{\pm}
using
3.15
, and hence
K
acts chamber-transitive on the to
G
associated twin building
\Delta_{\pm}
. By the construction of
G_{l}^{\pm}
and
K_{l}^{\pm}
it follows that

G_{l}^{\pm}=K_{l}^{\pm}(G_{l}^{\pm}\cap B_{\pm}).

Denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\pm}
the set of chambers of
\Delta_{\pm}
, then by using the Bruhat order (
[
AB08
, Exercise 3.59 (c)]
) we can define the set

E_{w}(C)=\{D\in\mathcal{C}_{\pm}\mid\delta_{\pm}(C,D)=w\}\subseteq\Delta_{\pm},

where
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\pm}
denotes a chamber in
\Delta_{\pm}
,
w\in\mathcal{W}
an element of the Weyl group and
\delta_{\pm}
the Weyl distance in relation to the two halves of the twin building. By the previous discussion
K_{l}^{\pm}
acts chamber-transitive on

\bigcup_{w\in\mathcal{W}\,,\;l(w)\leq l}E_{w}(C).

Moreover, this is a finite union of compact sets, see
[
HKM13
, Corollary 3.10]
.

Using again the chamber-transitivity it follows that we can rewrite the union as the quotient

K_{l}^{\pm}/(K_{l}^{\pm}\cap B_{\pm}).

Recall that
M
is the compact subgroup of
T
, in particular
M
is a Lie group, and that
K_{l}^{\pm}\cap B_{\pm}\subseteq M
. Thus, by the discussion around
[
Pal61
, Corollary 4.1]
, it follows that

K\to K/(K\cap B_{\pm})

are fibers.
K_{l}^{\pm}
are closed sets so it follows that

\pi\colon K_{l}^{\pm}\to K_{l}^{\pm}/(K_{l}^{\pm}\cap B_{\pm})

are also fibers and the corresponding fiber bundles are compact. Thus
K_{l}^{\pm}
is a compact set for every
l\in\mathbb{N}
, therefore, the pair
(K,(K_{l}^{\pm}))
is a
k_{\omega}
pair. Together with the fact that

\iota_{\pm}(K_{l}^{\pm})=\widetilde{D}_{l,\pm}

we can apply
3.17
.
∎

At this point, we are able to prove the topological Iwasawa decomposition for Kac–Moody groups of arbitrary type over
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem 3.20

Let
G
be a split Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is non-spherical. Then each of the multiplication maps

\displaystyle m_{\pm}

\displaystyle\colon U_{\pm}\times A\times K\to G

is a homeomorphism.

Proof
:

By the algebraic Iwasawa decomposition
3.15
we know that
m_{\pm}
is a bijection, so it follows from the continuity of multiplication in
G
that
m_{\pm}
is continuous with respect to the Kac–Peterson topology. Thus, all that remains is to construct an inverse continuous map as in the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 3.31]
.

Let
g\in G
and define the two elements

k(g)\coloneqq\iota_{\pm}^{-1}\hskip-2.0pt\left(gAU_{\pm}\right)\;\text{ and }\;h(g)\coloneqq k(g)^{-1}g,

where the maps
\iota_{\pm}
are the same as in
3.19
. Therefore we define the map

\displaystyle n_{\pm}\colon G

\displaystyle\to K\times AU_{\pm}

\displaystyle g=k(g)h(g)

\displaystyle\mapsto(k(g),h(g)),

which are by construction inverse to
m_{p}m
and they are continuous, since
G
is a topological group and
\iota_{\pm}
is a homeomorphisms. It is left to check, that the multiplication maps
A\times U_{\pm}\to AU_{\pm}
are open, but this follows from
[
HKM13
, Proposition 7.29]
.
∎

A useful application of the Iwasawa decomposition is the following lemma, which can be found for centered Kac–Moody groups over
\mathbb{R}
in
[
FHHK20
, Lemma 3.27]
.

Lemma 3.21

Let
G
be a Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
. Then
N_{G}(T)\cap\tau(G)=A
.

Proof.

The following applies:
\tau(t)=A
and
T\subseteq N_{G}(T)
, i.e.
A\subseteq N_{G}(T)\cap\tau(G)
. For the converse implication let
g\in N_{G}(T)\cap\tau(G)
. By
3.15
there exists elements
u\in U_{+}
,
t\in T
and
k\in K
, such that

g=\tau(utk)=u\tau(t)\Theta(u)^{-1}\in U_{+}\tau(t)U_{-}.

Since the RGD system obtained from a Kac–Moody group is refined (see
[
Rém02
, Theorem 1.5.4]
), we can use the refined Birkhoff decomposition

G=\bigsqcup_{n\in N_{G}(T)}U_{+}nU_{-},

see
[
Kum02
, Theorem 5.2.3 (g)]
.

Since
g\in N_{G}(T)
it follows that
g=\tau(t)
and hence
g\in A
.
∎

4
Kac–Moody Symmetric Spaces

4.1
A criterion for symmetric spaces

In the following section we prove a statement in the context of buildings that allows us to show that
K\cap\tau(G)=\{e\}
. This result will be a very powerful statement to show that a Kac–Moody group can be transformed into a symmetric space in the sense of Loos. The following lemma is very technical and uses building theory arguments, for a brief introduction see
Section

3.3
. Let
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
and
\mathbb{A}
be a symmetrizable, indecomposable generalized Cartan matrix.

In order to state the lemma, call an element
g\in G
of a Kac–Moody group
G
with the property

\Theta(g)=g^{-1}

symmetric
.

Lemma 4.1

(Lemma 4.2 in
[
Hor17
]
)

Let
G
be a Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
and denote with
\Delta_{\pm}
the associated twin building. For a symmetric element
g\in G
the statements below are equivalent:

I.

The element
g
fixes a
\Theta
-stable twin apartment chamberwise.
\Theta
-stable means that a twin apartment of
\Delta_{\pm}
is invariant as a set under the action of
\Theta
.

II.

The element
g
fixes a twin apartment chamberwise.

III.

The element
g
stabilizes a chamber.

IV.

For all chambers
D
in the twin building
\Delta_{\pm}
, the length of any minimal gallery from
D
to a chamber in the set of
G
-orbits
\{g^{n}.D\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, is bounded.

V.

For some chamber
D
in the twin building
\Delta_{\pm}
, the length of any minimal gallery from
D
to a chamber in the set of
G
-orbits
\{g^{n}.D\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, is bounded.

VI.

The element
g
stabilizes a spherical residue in either half of the twin building.

Remark 4.2

This statement was formulated and proven by Max Horn in
[
Hor17
]
. We reproduce the proof here and, in fact, add some details for the reader’s convenience.

Proof
:

Let
g
be a symmetric element of
G
. The implications
\ref{itm:fir}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:sec}
,
\ref{itm:sec}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:thr}
, and
\ref{itm:for}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:fiv}
are clear.

The idea now is to prove that
\ref{itm:thr}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:fir}
, thus the first three points are equivalent. Then we show that

\ref{itm:thr}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:for}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:fiv}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:six}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:sec},

which gives us the desired statement.

\ref{itm:thr}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:fir}

Let
D
be a chamber in
\Delta_{\pm}
which is stabilized by
g
, i.e.
g.D=D
. If
g
stabilizes
D
, then also
g^{-1}
stabilizes
D
:

D=(g^{-1}g).D=g^{-1}.(g.D)=g^{-1}.D.

Hence one obtains by a straight forward calculation,

\Theta(D)=\Theta(g.D)=g^{-1}.\Theta(D),

that
g
stabilizes the
\Theta
-stable twin apartment
\Sigma(D,\Theta(D))
, since
\Theta(D)
is opposite of
D
.

\ref{itm:thr}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:for}

First, recall that the building
\Delta_{\pm}
associated with the Kac–Moody group
G
is of type
(\mathcal{W},S)
.

Let
D^{\prime}\in\Delta_{+}
be a chamber in the positive half of the twin building which is stabilized by
g
, also let
D\in\Delta_{+}
be an arbitrary chamber and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then we know that

\delta_{+}(D^{\prime},D)=\delta_{+}(g^{n}.D^{\prime},g^{n}.D)=\delta_{+}(D^{\prime},g^{n}.D).

Next, denote with
l\colon\mathcal{W}\to\mathbb{N}
the length function of the Weyl group
\mathcal{W}
with respect to the generating set
S
. By
[
AB08
, Definition 5.1 (WD3)]
one derives a triangle inequality, which is given for the length function
l
, so that one can compute

4.2
Kac–Moody Symmetric Space

Loos’ abstract definition of a symmetric space, see
2.1
, requires a set and a multiplication map
\mu
that encodes point reflections within the symmetric space. It turns out that the Chevalley involution composed with the complex conjugation
\Theta
(cf.
Section

3.5
) is essential to define such a map
\mu
. Since the Kac–Moody group
G
is a topological group, see
Section

3.2
, we deduce that
K
is a closed subgroup, since
\Theta
is continuous and thus the quotient

is a topological Hausdorff space.

Proposition 4.4

Let
G
be a split Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
. Define the reflection map

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu\colon G/K\times G/K&\to G/K\\
(gK,hK)&\mapsto\tau(g)\Theta(h)K.\end{split}

(5)

The map
\mu
is continuous, and if
\tau(G)\cap K=\{e\}
, then the pair
(G/K,\mu)
is a symmetric space and there is a natural action by automorphisms

G\to\operatorname{Sym}(G/K)\,,\;g\mapsto(hK\mapsto ghK).

Proof
:

The proof is a straightforward calculation, independent of the choice of
\mathbb{A}
and
\mathbb{K}
, and can be found in
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 4.2]
or
[
Voc23
, Proposition 4.3]
.
∎

Definition 4.5

The symmetric space
(G/K,\mu)
is the
Kac–Moody symmetric space
over
\mathbb{K}
of type
\mathbb{A}
.

Remark 4.6

Note that this definition of the reflection map
\mu
also holds for any topological group with an involution. Moreover, the first three properties in
2.1
can be proved without any assumption about the group, see the calculation in the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 4.2]
. Thus any topological group with an involution that satisfies the last property gives rise to a symmetric space.

I.

The first example which comes to mind is the group given by
(\mathbb{R}^{n},+)
with involution
\Theta\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\Theta(x)=-x
. Then one computes for
\tau(x)=x-\Theta(x)=2x
and hence the reflection of the symmetric space is given by
\mu(x,y)=\tau(x)+\Theta(y)+K=2x-y
and
K
is equal to
\{0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

II.

Another example is the Lie group
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
with involution
\Theta(A)=A^{-T}
. Clearly the fixed point set
K
equals the special orthogonal group
\operatorname{SO}(n)
. Note, that
\tau(A)=AA^{T}
for a matrix

, hence the matrices in the image of the twist map are symmetric and thus diagonalizable over
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the image
\tau(G)
consists of positive semi-definite matrices.

To check if
\tau(G)\cap K
is trivial, we use that
\tau(A)
can be diagonalized: let
S
be a orthogonal real matrix such that

S(AA^{T})S^{-1}=D

is a diagonal matrix. Assume now, that
AA^{T}\in K=\operatorname{SO}(n)
, then it is true that

(AA^{T})(AA^{T})^{T}=\mathbb{E}_{n},

where
\mathbb{E}_{n}
stands for the
n\times n
-identity matrix. Thus we can compute

\displaystyle\left(S(AA^{T})S^{-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=D^{2}

\displaystyle S(AA^{T})^{2}S^{-1}

\displaystyle=D^{2}

\displaystyle SS^{-1}

\displaystyle=D^{2}

and it follows that the entries of

are
\pm 1
. But by the positive semi-definite property only
+1
is possible.

4.3
The Group Model

So far, we can describe a symmetric space associated with a Kac-Moody group by
(G/K,\mu)
, which is called the
coset model
. In this section we want to set up another model to describe the same symmetric space with
\tau(G)
using the quadratic representation of an abstract symmetric space, see
2.4
. The following section is based on
[
FHHK20
, Section 4.12]
.

In order to use the quadratic representation, we need a pointed symmetric space. Therefore choose
eK
as the base point. Then, by
2.4
, there is an isomorphism of symmetric spaces

(G/K,\mu)\cong(T(G/K,eK),\mu_{T}).

Next, we take a closer look at
T(G/K,eK)
. Therefore, we first compute the kernel of the group action of
G
on
G/K
, which is given by left multiplication:

G\times G/K\to G/K\,,\;(g,hK)\mapsto ghK.

If
g\in G
is an element of the kernel, then
ghK=hK
for all
h\in G
, in particular for
h\in K
follows
ghK=K
which implies that
g\in K
. Now assume that
g\in K
acts trivially on
hK
for an element
h\in G
. Then we can compute
ghK=hK\Leftrightarrow h^{-1}gh\in K
and by using
3.14

(A1)
it follows that
\tau(h^{-1}gh)=e
. By a direct calculation this leads to
g\tau(h)g^{-1}=\tau(h)
, and thus the kernel of the action is given by
C_{K}(\tau(G))
.

Next, denote by
\ell_{g}\colon G/K\to G/K
the left multiplication with an element
g\in G
, i.e.
\ell_{g}(hK)=ghK
. It follows from the latter calculation that
\ell_{g}=\ell_{h}
if
g
and
h
are in the same coset of
C_{K}(\tau(G))
. Then we can calculate

\displaystyle s_{gK}(hK)

\displaystyle=\tau(g)\Theta(h)K

\displaystyle=\ell_{g}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\Theta(g^{-1}h)K\right)

\displaystyle=\ell_{g}\hskip-2.0pt\left(s_{eK}\hskip-2.0pt\left(g^{-1}hK\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\ell_{g}\circ s_{eK}\circ\ell_{g^{-1}}\right)\hskip-2.0pt(hK).

Using this, we can rewrite a transvection:

\displaystyle s_{gK}\circ s_{eK}

\displaystyle=\ell_{g}\circ s_{eK}\circ\ell_{g^{-1}}\circ s_{eK}=\ell_{\tau(g)}

and thus

\displaystyle T(G/K,eK)

\displaystyle=\{s_{gK}\circ s_{eK}\mid gK\in G/K\}

\displaystyle=\{\ell_{\tau(g)}\mid g\in G\}

\displaystyle=\{\tau(g)C_{K}(\tau(G))\mid g\in G\}.

This leads to the observation, that the map

\zeta\colon\tau(G)\to T(G/K,eK)

is surjective. Furthermore, if an element is contained in the kernel, it must be contained in
K
by the previous calculation. Since
\tau(G)\cap K=\{e\}
, see
4.3
, it follows that the kernel of
\zeta
is trivial. By transport of structure, the map establishes a symmetric space structure on
\tau(G)
, where the reflection map is given by
\mu_{\tau}(g,h)=gh^{-1}g
.

Altogether there are the following isomorphism of symmetric spaces

(G/K,\mu)\underset{\eta}{\cong}(T(G/K,eK),\mu_{T})\underset{\zeta}{\cong}(\tau(G),\mu_{\tau}).

The symmetric space
(\tau(G),\mu_{\tau})
is called
group model
of the Kac–Moody symmetric space. The reflection map
\mu_{\tau}
is the same as the reflection map given in
2.2

IV.
for a general group.

Remark 4.7

As in
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 4.11 (iii)]
, the transvection group of the Kac–Moody symmetric space can also be written as a quotient of the Kac–Moody group. But in the general case of arbitrary split Kac–Moody groups, we have to deal with the enlarged torus. We adopt the strategy used in the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 4.11]
, but we have to pay attention to the torus.

Define the following quotient, which, as we will see, acts faithfully on a Kac–Moody symmetric space:

\left(G^{\dagger}A\right)_{\operatorname{eff}}\coloneqq\nicefrac{{G^{\dagger}A}}{{C_{K}(G^{\dagger}A)}}.

Since
M\subseteq K
and
\tau(T)=A
, see
3.14
, one concludes that

\langle\tau(G)\rangle=\langle\tau(G^{\dagger}T)\rangle\subseteq G^{\dagger}A.

On the other hand, one knows that
G^{\dagger}=\langle\tau(G^{\dagger})\rangle
(cp.
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 3.39]
) as well as
\tau(T)=A
.
Note that
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 3.39]
is formulated for centered Kac–Moody groups with an RGD system, and
G^{\dagger}
is a centered Kac–Moody group with an RGD system, see the discussion after

. Thus

\langle\tau(G)\rangle=G^{\dagger}A.

It follows from the previous observations that an element
g\in G
acts trivially on a Kac–Moody symmetric space

if and only if it centralizes
G^{\dagger}A
. In terms of the notation of left multiplication used above,
l(g_{1})=l(g_{2})
only if
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are in the same coset
C_{K}(G^{\dagger}A)
.

We now want to associate
\left(G^{\dagger}A\right)_{\operatorname{eff}}
with the transvection group of
G/K
,

\operatorname{Trans}(G/K)\coloneqq\langle s_{gK}\circ s_{hK}\mid gK,hK\in G/K\rangle.

The strategy is the same as in the previous discussion and based on the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 4.11]
. Let
g\in G
be an arbitrary element,
s_{gK}
the corresponding point reflection and denote by
b=eK
the base point of
G/K
. Then as done before, one calculates

\displaystyle\begin{split}s_{gK}\circ s_{b}=l(\tau(g)).\end{split}

Thus, for the generators of the transvection group, we observe the following presentation

\displaystyle s_{gK}\circ s_{hK}

\displaystyle=\left(s_{gK}\circ s_{b}\right)\circ\left(s_{hK}\circ s_{b}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=l(\tau(g)\tau(h)^{-1}).

Further,
\tau(h)^{-1}=\tau(\Theta(h))
, hence the transvection group is generated by elements of the form

\tau(G)^{2}C_{K}\hskip-2.0pt\left(G^{\dagger}A\right).

Together with
4.7

\left(G^{\dagger}A\right)_{\operatorname{eff}}=\operatorname{Trans}(\nicefrac{{G^{\dagger}A}}{{K}})=\operatorname{Trans}(G/K).

On the basis of this section, we have two isomorphic perspectives when we consider a symmetric Kac–Moody space. One is the coset model
G/K
, which is the natural way to construct the symmetric space according to Loos. The other is the group model
\tau(G)
, which allows us to view the symmetric space as a subset of the original Kac–Moody group

7
Comparing Symmetric Spaces

In the previous chapters we established a symmetric space associated to Kac–Moody groups over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
and with respect to arbitrary generalized Cartan matrices. Now we can give an summary and compare it with the symmetric space developed in
[
FHHK20
]
, which was set up for non-affine Kac–Moody groups over
\mathbb{R}
.

In
[
FHHK20
]
the authors consider only real centered Kac–Moody groups with respect to the root datum
\mathcal{D}_{sc}^{\mathbb{A}}
(see
[
Mar18
, Example 7.11]
). This choice implies that their coroots are linearly independent, but this does not mean that the roots are linearly independent. For this reason, the authors in
[
FHHK20
]
work with the derived Kac–Moody algebra (see
[
Mar18
, Definition 7.13]
), and use the Cartan subalgebra of this Kac–Moody algebra to construct the standard flat of the associated symmetric space.

If we now consider Kac–Moody groups with repsect to arbitrary generalized Cartan matrices, one problem we encounter is that Cartan matrices do not have to be invertible. In the case of a non-invertible generalized Cartan matrix, there is a non-trivial kernel of bilinear form the Cartan subalgebra of the derived Kac–Moody algebra, which is equal to the center (
[
Kac90
, Lemma 2.1 a)]
).

To work around this problem for non-affine Cartan matrices, the authors of
[
FHHK20
]
use the semisimple adjoint quotient of a Kac–Moody group: this is the quotient of the group by the center of the Kac–Moody algebra that is lifted onto the group, cf.
[
FHHK20
, Definition 3.8]
. This quotient allows them to prove, for example, the useful statements
[
FHHK20
, Proposition 6.4]
and
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 6.12 (ii)]
.

If we also consider Kac–Moody symmetric spaces over
\mathbb{C}
, we need a topological Iwasawa decomposition to define flats. At the time
[
FHHK20
]
was created, no complex version of the topological Iwasawa decomposition was known.

Here, we are talking about Kac–Moody groups over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
with respect to the root datum
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{Kac}}^{\mathbb{A}}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is an arbitrary generalized Cartan matrix. In this case the linear independence of the roots is given by enlarging the Cartan subalgebra, which leads to an enlarged torus at the group level.

This larger torus turns out to be a problem, because for the analysis of the global and local action of the Kac–Moody group on the corresponding symmetric space it is important that the two automorphism groups are related (see
5.5
). The reason for this problem is Caprace’s theorem: It is the basis for understanding the automorphisms of a particular type of Kac–Moody group. But it only describes the automorphisms of a Kac–Moody group generated by its root groups, see
[
Cap09
, Theorem 4.2]
. On the extended torus one essentially asks for the automorphism group of
(\mathbb{R},+)
. This group is too big to classify, so it is not possible to say anything about the automorphisms on the torus outside of
G^{\dagger}
.

Therefore, in order to study the local and global action, this paper is restricted to the subgroup
G^{\dagger}
of a Kac–Moody group
G
, which is generated only by its root groups. Caprace’s theorem applies to this centered subgroup
G^{\dagger}
and leads to
5.5
.

Regarding the case of generalizing the topological Iwasawa decomposition to complex split Kac–Moody groups, there is now a way to do this thanks to
[
HK23
, Corollary B.8]
. The quoted statement allows us to use the same methods as in
[
FHHK20
]
to prove the topological Iwasawa decomposition in the complex case, see
3.20
.

So far, we have achieved a generalization of the Kac–Moody symmetric space described in
[
FHHK20
]
.

8.2
The Galois Descent

In linear algebra it is well known that a real form of a complex vector space can be identified with the fixed point set of an action given via Galois automorphism. The same idea uses Rémy to develop further the theory of almost split Kac–Moody

As the Kac–Peterson topology is defined for a Kac–Moody group

Let

G^{\musNatural}_{\alpha_{i}}=\left\langle V_{\alpha_{i}}^{\musNatural},V_{-\alpha_{i}}^{\musNatural}\right\rangle,

Now we have all the ingredients to define the relative Kac–Peterson topology in the same way as the Kac–Peterson topology, comparing to

3.2

Definition 8.5

Let

In the proof of

, Porposition B.3]

\mathbb{R}
-group

. To define the notion of a restricted subgroup in

Definition 8.6

Proposition 8.8

Let
G
be a split Kac–Moody group over
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
of type
\mathbb{A}
, where
\mathbb{A}
is an indecomposable, symmetrizable generalized Cartan matrix. Equip the standard torus with the Lie topology induced by the exponential map, see

equals the maximal bounded subgroup topology

.

Proof
:

We follow the strategy of the proof given for
[
HK23
, Proposition B.3]
. Since the cited proposition is given for centered Kac–Moody groups and in general a Kac–Moody group
G
can be decomposed in

, see
3.10

is a centered Kac–Moody group, see
3.13
, we has to take a closer look at the tours.

Note, that by definition the torus is a bounded subgroup,

T=B_{+}\cap B_{-},

and carries a Lie group topology, see
3.7
. Thus the torus embeds as a closed subgroup into a maximal bounded subgroup and by the closed-subgroup theorem (
[

, Theorem 9.3.7]

As a next step, we apply
[
Mar15
, Lemma 4.3]

on the maximal bounded subgroups is equal to the Lie group topology. Although the statement is given for centered split Kac–Moody groups, it also applies to all split Kac–Moody groups, since the statement is based on building theory arguments.

Recall, that the Kac–Peterson topology induces the Lie group topology on the rank one subgroups

[

[

Overall it follows that

\tau_{\text{MB}}\subseteq\tau_{\text{KP}}.

To see the other direction, consider the maximal bounded subgroups in
(G,\tau_{\text{KP}})
. Here, each maximal bounded subgroup has the Lie group topology induced by the subspace topology (cf.
[
Mar15
, Lemma 4.3]
).
The embedded relative rank one subgroups and the embedded torus also have the induced subspace Lie topology and one concludes that the embedding of the maximal bounded subgroups in

is continuous with respect to the topology
\tau_{\text{KP}}
. Therefore, the topology
\tau_{\text{KP}}
is coarser than
\tau_{\text{MB}}
.
∎

A parabolic
\mathbb{R}
-subgroup of

is also a parabolic subgroup in the surrounding split Kac–Moody group

To keep the notation simple, we denote the set of maximal bounded subgroups in

by

The action of an almost connected Lie group

[
Mar15

. Further, recall that

, where

From the closed-subgroup Theorem (cf.

\operatorname{MBS}(\mathbb{C})
. Note that the subspace topology induced by

on a maximal bounded group of

.

Now, we consider the topology that can be defined by the embedding of the maximal bounded subgroups. Recall the definition of

.

III.
. Now given a generating set
\{r^{v_{i}}\mid r\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\,,\;1\leq i\leq 2n-l\}
of
T
. Then we calculate for any
\alpha\in\Delta^{\mathrm{re}}

\overline{\sigma}\left(r^{v_{i}}\right)=\overline{r}^{v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}}.

In
Section

3.5
we have defined the action of
\Theta
on
T
and hence we can compute

\displaystyle\left(\Theta\circ\overline{\sigma}\right)\hskip-2.0pt\left(r^{v_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\overline{r}^{v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}}\right)=r^{-v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}},

and on the other hand

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\sigma}\circ\Theta\right)\hskip-2.0pt\left(r^{v_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}\left(\overline{r}^{-v_{i}}\right)=r^{-v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}}.

∎

Based on the previous lemma, we observe that

\Theta\hskip-2.0pt\left(G^{\Gamma}\right)=\Theta(G)^{\Gamma},

where we denote for simplicity reasons with
\Gamma
the group of rectified and conjugated Galois automorphism
\widehat{\sigma}
.

It follows that
\Theta
is a continuous involution on the topological group
G(\mathbb{R})
and we define

K(\mathbb{R})\coloneqq K^{\Gamma}=\left(G^{\Theta}\right)^{\Gamma}=\left(G^{\Gamma}\right)^{\Theta}.

Similar to the approach for split Kac–Moody groups, we can define the twist map on
G(\mathbb{R})
by

\displaystyle\tau^{\operatorname{as}}\colon G(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to G(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto g\Theta\left(g^{-1}\right),

and also a reflection map by

\begin{split}\mu^{\operatorname{as}}\colon G(\mathbb{R})/K(\mathbb{R})\times G(\mathbb{R})/K(\mathbb{R})&\to G(\mathbb{R})/K(\mathbb{R})\\
(gK(\mathbb{R}),hK(\mathbb{R}))&\mapsto\tau^{\operatorname{as}}(g)\Theta(h)K(\mathbb{R}).\end{split}

(13)

Note, that
\tau^{\operatorname{as}}
equals
\tau
since
G(\mathbb{R})
is a subgroup of
G
. Moreover, since
G(\mathbb{R})
is closed with respect to
\Theta
and
\tau^{\operatorname{as}}
, the map
\mu^{\operatorname{as}}
satisfies all properties of an abstract symmetric space (cp.
2.1
). Even the fourth property
\textbf{S.4}_{\text{global}}
is true, because

\tau^{\operatorname{as}}(G(\mathbb{R}))\cap K(\mathbb{R})\subseteq\tau(G)\cap K=\{e\}.

This results in the following theorem, which is a generalization of the split case.

Theorem 9.2

Let
G
be an almost split Kac–Moody
\mathbb{R}
-group satisfying (
8.2
) and denote by
G(\mathbb{R})
the rational points of
G
. The quotient

X(\mathbb{R})\coloneqq\nicefrac{{G(\mathbb{R})}}{{K(\mathbb{R})}}

is a symmetric space in the sense of Loos with the reflection map
\mu^{\operatorname{as}}
. Moreover, there is a natural action of
G(\mathbb{R})
on the symmetric space given by automorphisms

\displaystyle G(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Sym}(X(\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\mapsto(hK(\mathbb{R})\mapsto ghK(\mathbb{R})).

Since
\mathcal{F}(\mathbb{C})=G
if
G
is a complex (split) Kac–Moody group, it follows that the theorem is in this particular case just
4.4
for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, if we consider a real split Kac–Moody group in the theorem, the following theorem corresponds to
4.4
for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
.

Proof
:

Since the properties of an abstract symmetric space due to Loos are satisfied, we need to check that

G(\mathbb{R})\to\operatorname{Sym}(X(\mathbb{R}))\,,\;g\mapsto(hK(\mathbb{R})\mapsto ghK(\mathbb{R}))

is an action. Thus, let
g,h,d\in G(\mathbb{R})
and do the following calculation

\displaystyle\mu^{\operatorname{as}}(ghK(\mathbb{R}),gdK(\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle=\tau^{\operatorname{as}}(gh)\Theta(gd)K(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=gh\Theta(gh)^{-1}\Theta(g)\Theta(d)K(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=gh\Theta(h)^{-1}\Theta(g)^{-1}\Theta(g)\Theta(d)K(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle=g(\tau^{\operatorname{as}}(h)\Theta(d)K(\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle=g\mu^{\operatorname{as}}(hK(\mathbb{R}),dK(\mathbb{R})).

∎

9.1
Flats in Almost Split Kac–Moody Symmetric Spaces

After establishing the notion of a symmetric space for almost split Kac–Moody groups, we can define flats. As we in the proof of
4.8
, we define a symmetric Euclidean space in the fixed point set of the Cartan subalgebra and use the exponential map to obtain an isomorphism.

Let
G
be an almost split Kac–Moody
\mathbb{R}
-group. Then, based on
Section

8.1

III.
, we know that the Galois action on
G
also acts on
\mathfrak{h}
. Further, a rectified Galois action stabilizes the standard torus
T
of
G
and since
G
satisfies the condition (
8.2
), we can define

\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}\coloneqq\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{h}^{\Gamma}.

Note, that
\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}
is exactly the fixed point set of the real form
\mathfrak{a}
under the Galois action. Recall, that
G(\mathbb{R})\leq G
and by
\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}
we can use the exponential map given in
Section

3.2
: restricting the map onto
\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}
leads to

\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}\hskip-3.0pt\mid_{\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}}\colon\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}\to A.

The next step is to analyze the image of
\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}(\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural})
. Therefore, let

h=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-l}r_{i}v_{i}\in\mathfrak{h}=\Lambda^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}

be an element of the Cartan algebra, where
r_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
are scalars and
v_{i}\in\Lambda^{\vee}
are the
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
\Lambda^{\vee}
(cp.
Section

3.2
). Now we can compute

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}(\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}(h))

\displaystyle=\overline{\sigma}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2n-l}\left(e^{r_{i}}\right)^{v_{i}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{2n-l}\sigma(e^{r_{i}})^{v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}}=\prod_{i=1}^{2n-l}(e^{\overline{r_{i}}})^{v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}}

\displaystyle\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}(\overline{\sigma}(h))

\displaystyle=\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2n-l}\overline{r_{i}}v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{2n-l}(e^{\overline{r_{i}}})^{v_{\overline{\sigma}(i)}}.

The image of the exponential map restricted to
\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}
is given by
A(\mathbb{R})\coloneqq A\cap G(\mathbb{R})
, since the exponential map is Galois-equivariant.

Note that
\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}\cong\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m<2n-l
, but the actual size of
m
is not clear. It depends on how exactly the torus of
G(\mathbb{R})
looks and splits.

Proposition 9.3

Let
X(\mathbb{R})=G(\mathbb{R})/K(\mathbb{R})
be the symmetric space attached to the group of rational points of an almost split Kac–Moody
\mathbb{R}
-group
G
. Moreover, equip
\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}
with the Euclidean symmetric space structure. Then for any
g\in G(\mathbb{R})
there is an isomorphism of symmetric spaces given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi^{\operatorname{as}}_{g}\colon\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}&\to gA(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})\\
X&\mapsto g\left(\exp_{\operatorname{KM}}\hskip-3.0pt\mid_{\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}}\hskip-4.0pt(X)\right)K(\mathbb{R}).\end{split}

(14)

The subset
gA(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
of
X(\mathbb{R})
is a Euclidean flat of dimension
\dim\mathfrak{a}^{\musNatural}
. Call for any
g\in G(\mathbb{R})
the set
gA(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
a
standard
\mathbb{R}
-flat
.

Proof
:

Since
\varphi^{\operatorname{as}}_{g}
is obtained from the map of
4.8
by restricting the exponential map to a subset of
\mathfrak{a}
, the assertion that
\varphi^{\operatorname{as}}_{g}
is an isomorphism of symmetric spaces follows with the same arguments as in the proof of
4.8
.

Assume that
g=e
, then it remains to check that
A(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
is a closed subset, since the multiplication is continuous. To do this, we can use the Iwasawa decomposition on
G
and the fact, that
AK
is a closed subset in the symmetric space associated to
G
. By
8.11
, the induced subspace topology on
G(\mathbb{R})
is the same as the relative Kac–Peterson topology on
G(\mathbb{R})
. Hence, if
AK\cap G(\mathbb{R})=A(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
, the desired statement follows.

Recall from
3.20
that an element
g\in AK
can be written as
ak
for a unique
a\in A
and a unique
k\in K
. Assume that
ak\in AK\cap G(\mathbb{R})\subseteq G
and let
\overline{\sigma}
a rectified Galois automorphism. Then it follows that
\overline{\sigma}(A)\subseteq A
and
\overline{\sigma}(K)\subseteq K
. From the condition (
8.2
) it further follows that

\overline{\sigma}(ak)=ak=\overline{\sigma}(a)\overline{\sigma}(k).

By the uniqueness of the decomposition,
AK\cap G(\mathbb{R})=A(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
follows. Hence the set
A(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
, and so
gA(\mathbb{R})K(\mathbb{R})
for all
g\in G(\mathbb{R})
, is closed in
X(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

As for symmetric spaces of split Kac–Moody groups, we can give the characterization of flats. This is possible, since almost split Kac–Moody
\mathbb{R}
-groups posses an RGD system and the root groups has a good algebraic group structure.

In the proof of the characterization, one uses that the group model of the symmetric spaces is isomorphic to the coset model. But, the isomorphism between the coset model and the group model established in
Section

4.3
is independent of the Kac–Moody group. It is essentially based on the isomorphism specified in
2.4
for abstract symmetric spaces according to Loos and on the existence of the twist map
\tau
. Hence, it follows that

(X(\mathbb{R}),\mu^{\operatorname{as}})\cong(\tau^{\operatorname{as}}(G(\mathbb{R})),\mu^{\operatorname{as}}_{\tau}).

Theorem 9.4

Let
G
be almost split Kac–Moody
\mathbb{R}
-group and denote by
G(\mathbb{R})
the rational points of
G
. Denote by
X(\mathbb{R})=G(\mathbb{R})/K(\mathbb{R})
the symmetric space attached to
G
. Then every weak flat is contained in a standard flat. Especially

I.

standard flats are exactly the maximal flats;

II.

all weak flats are Euclidean;

III.

all weak flats are flats;

IV.

G(\mathbb{R})
acts transitively on maximal flats.

Proof
:

As in the general split case, the proof works verbatim as the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 5.17]
, except claim
5
. There it is stated that the stabilizers of opposite spherical residues are reductive Lie groups. In the case of the group of rational points of an almost split Kac–Moody
\mathbb{R}
-group we get the same:

From
[
AB08
, Proposition 6.27]
it follows that the stabilizer of a spherical residue is equal to a spherical parabolic subgroup. From
[
Rém02
, Definition 12.1.3]
follows that a parabolic subgroup in
G(\mathbb{R})
is equal to a parabolic subgroup in
G
. Using the same arguments as in the last step of the proof of
4.1
, it follows that this can be interpreted as a subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

As in the proof of
4.10
, we have to embed the involution
\Theta
into the matrix group
\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{R})
. According to
9.1
we know that
\Theta
commutes with every rectified Galois automorphism
\widehat{\sigma}
and therefore we can embed
\Theta
on these parabolic
\mathbb{R}
-groups as transpose inverses. Thus, the arguments of claim
5
from the proof of
[
FHHK20
, Theorem 5.17]
apply verbatim.
∎
