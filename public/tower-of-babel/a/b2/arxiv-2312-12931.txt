Spectral synthesis of the invariant Laplacian and complexified spherical harmonics Thanks: † {}^{\dagger}\, Partially supported by the Alexander von Humboldt Stiftung
Annika Moucha †

Abstract
. We show that the space ℋ ⁡ ( Ω ) \mathcal{H}(\Omega) of holomorphic functions F : Ω → ℂ F:\Omega\to\mathbb{C} , where Ω = { ( z , w ) ∈ ℂ ^ 2 : z ⋅ w ≠ 1 } {\Omega=\{(z,w)\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\,:\,z\cdot w\neq 1\}} , possesses an orthogonal Schauder basis consisting of distinguished eigenfunctions of the canonical Laplacian on Ω \Omega . Mapping Ω \Omega biholomorphically onto the complex two-sphere, we use the Schauder basis result in order to identify the classical three-dimensional spherical harmonics as restrictions of the elements in ℋ ⁡ ( Ω ) \mathcal{H}(\Omega) to the real two-sphere analogue in Ω \Omega . In particular, we show that the zonal harmonics correspond to those functions in ℋ ⁡ ( Ω ) \mathcal{H}(\Omega) that are invariant under automorphisms of Ω \Omega induced by Möbius transformations. The proof of the Schauder basis result is based on a curious combinatorial identity which we prove with the help of generalized hypergeometric functions.

\mathcal{H}(\Omega)

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})

\displaystyle:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})\,:\,f={F|}_{\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}}\text{ for some }F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\}\quad\text{and}

(1.6)

\mathbb{D}

. Roughly speaking, the goal in

[

]
is the construction of a so-called star product, a distinguished non-commutative product, on
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
resp.
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
. It turns out that, in this context, a pivotal tool are basic invariant differential operators on
C^{\infty}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
and
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
, the so-called Peschl-Minda operators, which were already studied by Peschl
[
24
]
, Minda

[

. In particular, they show that these Peschl-Minda operators are, also, “shadows” of invariant differential operators on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. This gives a partial explanation for the appearance of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
in strict deformation quantization of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
resp.
\mathbb{D}
: the Peschl-Minda operators (on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
) and the corresponding star product on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
(see

\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
.

Another fruitful connection between
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
with its “shadow spaces” and smooth functions is given by the spectral theory of the
canonical Laplace operator
\Delta_{zw}
on
\Omega
which is defined by

(1.7)

\Delta_{zw}F(z,w)\coloneqq 4(1-zw)^{2}\mathop{}\!\partial_{z}\mathop{}\!\partial_{w}F(z,w)\,,\qquad(z,w)\in\Omega

for
F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
:
the Laplacian
\Delta_{zw}
is closely related to the
spherical and hyperbolic Laplacians

(1.8)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}f(z):=\left(1+|z|^{2}\right)^{2}\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{{z}}}f(z)\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{D}}f(z):=\left(1-|z|^{2}\right)^{2}\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{{z}}}f(z)\,,\quad z\in\mathbb{D}\,,

acting on
C^{\infty}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
resp.
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
. More precisely, given a holomorphic eigenfunction of
\Delta_{zw}
, its “shadows” on the (rotated) diagonal are smooth eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
and
\Delta_{\mathbb{D}}
. Motivated by work of Rudin
[
29
]
on the structure of the eigenspaces of
\Delta_{\mathbb{D}}
with regard to invariance under unit disk automorphisms, Heins, Roth and the author
[
16
]
investigate the function-theoretical approach described above. Using real methods, Rudin gave a classification of the invariant eigenspaces of
\Delta_{\mathbb{D}}
by distinguishing between exceptional and non-exceptional eigenvalues. In
[
16
]
it is shown that in the exceptional case there always exists a finite dimensional subspace such that the eigenfunctions in this particular space admit a “holomorphic extension” to
\Omega
, i.e. belong to
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
. In fact, this “extension” is natural in the sense that
\Omega
is indeed the maximal domain where these functions are holomorphic (see
[
16
, Def. 2.4]
). The crucial tool for this purpose is the identification of specific eigenfunctions, so-called
Poisson Fourier modes
, which were already implicitly used in
[
29
]
.

The purpose of the present paper is a deeper analysis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and its offspring,
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
. We deal with the following two aspects: first, we identify the Poisson Fourier modes as building blocks of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. Second, based on the first aspect, we provide a complex analytic framework for the spectral theory of the spherical Laplacian with an emphasis on the three-dimensional spherical harmonics. Altogether, our results give an intrinsic description of
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
in terms of the exceptional (in the sense of Rudin
[
29
]
) eigenspaces of the natural Laplacians on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{D}
: our results link the spaces
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
precisely with the set of exceptional eigenfunctions singled out by Rudin as well as with the space of three-dimensional spherical harmonics.

More specifically, the first objective of this paper is to show that the Poisson Fourier modes form a Schauder basis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
, or, in other words,
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
admits a Schauder basis consisting of exceptional eigenfunctions. Our proof will use the fact that another Schauder basis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
is already known (see
[
20
, Th. 3.16]
and
[
32
, Prop. 6.4]
, see also
[
14
]
for a more conceptual approach). This enables us to perform a “change of Schauder basis” whose key ingredient is the verification of a curious combinatorial identity. For the latter, some rather deep results about hypergeometric functions based on identities obtained by Whipple
[
36
]
will be required.

The second objective of this paper is a function-theoretic description of the spectral theory of
\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
by developing a version of
complexified spherical harmonics
based on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. This is motivated by two observations: first, the (point) spectrum of
\Delta_{zw}
and
\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
equal each other.

Notation

We denote the open unit disk by
\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|z|<1\}
, the punctured plane by
\mathbb{C}^{*}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the Riemann sphere by
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
and the punctured sphere by
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\coloneqq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover, we write
\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots\}
for the set of positive integers,
\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
\mathbb{Z}
for the set of all integers. For an open subset
U
of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
or
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, we write
\partial U
for its boundary and
\overline{{U}}
for its closure. The set of all infinitely (real) differentiable functions
{f:U\to\mathbb{C}}
is denoted
C^{\infty}(U)
, the set of all holomorphic functions
{F:U\to\mathbb{C}}
is denoted
\mathcal{H}(U)
and
{\sf Aut}(U)
is the set of all biholomorphic mappings or automorphisms
T:U\to U
. Further, we write
\partial_{z}
and
\partial_{w}
resp.
\partial_{\overline{{z}}}
for the complex Wirtinger derivatives for functions of two resp. one complex variable. Finally, note that the set
\Omega
from (
1.1
) is a complex manifold of complex dimension
2
and an open submanifold of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

Further, we will use the
hypergeometric function

{}_{2}F_{1}
, which is defined as the power series

(2.1)

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a,b;c;t\right):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\,,\qquad\quad t\in\mathbb{D}\,,\;a,b,c\in\mathbb{C},\;-c\not\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

where
(a)_{k}:=a\cdot(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1)
denotes the
(rising) Pochhammer symbol
.

3.
Poisson Fourier modes

We now introduce the functions of interest of the present work:

Definition 3.1

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
n
-th Poisson Fourier mode (PFM) of its
(-m)
-th power
is the function
P_{n}^{-m}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
given by

(3.1)

P_{n}^{-m}(z,w):=(-1)^{n}\binom{m}{|n|}(1-zw)^{-m}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-m,|n|-m;|n|+1;zw\right)\cdot\begin{cases}w^{n}\quad&\textrm{if }n\geq 0\,,\\
z^{|n|}\quad&\textrm{if }n\leq 0\,.\end{cases}

Here, we define
\binom{m}{|n|}:=0
, if
|n|>m
.

Remark 1
.

(a)

The name stems from the characterization as Fourier coefficients of powers of a generalized Poisson kernel, i.e. using
[
8
, Sec. 2.5.1, Formula (10), p. 81]
it holds that

(3.2)

P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{1-zw}{(1-ze^{-it})(1-we^{it})}\right)^{-m}e^{-int}\,dt\,,\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}\,.

In fact, this characterization serves as the definition of the PFM in
[
16
, Def. 4.1]
. However, for our purpose the interpretation of
P_{n}^{-m}
as hypergeometric function is more important. Further note that in
[
16
]
PFM of arbitrary complex powers
\mu
are considered. However, we do not need this generality.

(b)

Note that if
|n|>m
, then
P_{n}^{-m}=0
. Otherwise, using (
2.1
),
P_{n}^{-m}
is a rational function:

(3.3)

P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{m-n}\binom{m}{k+n}\binom{m}{k}\frac{z^{k}w^{k+n}}{(1-zw)^{m}}=P_{-n}^{-m}(w,z)\qquad(n\geq 0)\,.

(c)

Since the PFM are symmetric in the sense that
P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)=P_{-n}^{-m}(w,z)
, we can simplify our proofs in the following: we will often prove identities for
P^{-m}_{n}
only which then implies the corresponding result for
P^{-m}_{-n}
.

The specific form of the
{}_{2}F_{1}
function in (
3.1
) allows us to relate the PFM to the
Jacobi polynomials
(we will make this precise in Section
4
) which are orthogonal polynomials. As a consequence, the PFM are mutually orthogonal (in sense of Proposition
3.2
), too.

Proposition 3.2

Let
m,p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n,q\in\mathbb{Z}
. The Poisson Fourier modes fulfil the orthogonality property

(3.4)

\frac{i}{\pi}\int\limits_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}P_{n}^{-m}(z,-\overline{{z}})\overline{{P_{q}^{-p}(z,-\overline{{z}})}}\frac{dz\,d\overline{{z}}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}=\frac{\binom{m}{|n|}\delta_{m,p}\delta_{n,q}}{(2m+1)\binom{m+|n|}{|n|}}\,.

Here,
\delta_{m,p}:=1
resp.
\delta_{n,q}:=1
if
m=p
resp.
n=q
and zero otherwise.

By definition, the functions
P_{n}^{-m}
are
-n
-homogeneous, that is

(3.5)

P_{n}^{-m}\left(\xi z,\frac{w}{\xi}\right)=\xi^{-n}P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{D}\,,\;\xi\in\partial\mathbb{D}\,.

Moreover, the PFM are holomorphic on
\Omega
by (
3.3
). These two properties are essential for the significant role that the PFM play for the spectral theory of the canonical Laplace operator
\Delta_{zw}
on
\Omega
from (
1.7
). Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we denote the
\lambda
-eigenspace of
\Delta_{zw}
on
\Omega
by

(3.6)

X_{\lambda}(\Omega):=\{F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\,:\,\Delta_{zw}F=\lambda F\}\,.

A computation shows that
P_{n}^{-m}\in X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Moreover, every
(-n)
-homogeneous function in
X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
is (up to a multiplicative constant) given by
P_{n}^{-m}
(see
[
16
, Th. 4.2 combined with (4.18)]
), and the set
\{P_{n}^{-m}\,:\,|n|\leq m\}
forms a basis of
X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
(see
[
16
, Th. 8.1]
). In combination with Proposition
3.2
this implies the following result.

Corollary 3.3

Define the map
\langle\cdot{,}\cdot\rangle_{\Omega}:\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
by

(3.7)

\langle F,G\rangle_{\Omega}:=\frac{i}{\pi}\int\limits_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}F(z,-\overline{{z}})\overline{{G(z,-\overline{{z}})}}\frac{dz\,d\overline{{z}}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}\,.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then
(X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega),\langle\cdot{,}\cdot\rangle_{\Omega})
is a Hilbert space with orthogonal basis
{\{P_{n}^{-m}\,:\,|n|\leq m\}}
.

Remark 2
.

By our identification of
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
with the rotated diagonal
\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}\subseteq\Omega
(see (
1.2
)) we see that (
3.7
) is well-defined as integral of continuous functions over a compact set. Further note that (
3.7
) already defines an inner product on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. This will follow from Theorem
3.4
below. Alternatively, one can argue directly with a variant of the identity principle (
[
26
, p. 18]
): if a holomorphic function
F:U\to\mathbb{C}
on a domain
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2}
containing a point of the form
(z,-\overline{{z}})
vanishes on
\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}\cap U
, then
F\equiv 0
on
U
.

In Remark 15 in
[
16
]
it is explained that the closure (w.r.t. the compact open topology of
\Omega
) of the linear hull of all PFM equals
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. Having this in mind, the first main goal of this paper shows that even more is true:

Theorem 3.4

The set
\{P_{n}^{-m}\,:\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,n\in\mathbb{Z},\,|n|\leq m\}
is a Schauder basis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. In particular, each
F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
has a unique representation as

(3.8)

F(z,w)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=-m}^{m}c_{n,m}P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)\,.

This series converges absolutely and locally uniformly in
\Omega
, and the Schauder coefficients
c_{\pm n,m}
(
n\geq 0
) of
F
are given by

(3.9)

c_{\pm n,m}=(2m+1)\binom{m+n}{n}\binom{m}{n}^{-1}\langle F,P_{\pm n}^{-m}\rangle_{\Omega}\,.

Remark 3
.

(a)

We can interpret Theorem
3.4
as a spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
in terms of eigenfunctions of
\Delta_{zw}
. In this sense, the PFM form a natural Schauder basis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
.

(b)

In view of Proposition
3.2
the PFM are an “orthogonal Schauder basis”. Moreover, Theorem
3.4
gives another justification for the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Omega}
on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
in (
3.7
), see Remark
2
.

Theorem
3.4
immediately implies for the spaces
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
from (
1.5
) and (
1.6
):

Corollary 3.5

(a)

The set
\{{P_{n}^{-m}|}_{\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}}\,:\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,n\in\mathbb{Z},\,|n|\leq m\}
is a Schauder basis of
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
.

(b)

The set
\{{P_{n}^{-m}|}_{\mathrm{L}_{\mathbb{D}}}\,:\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,n\in\mathbb{Z},\,|n|\leq m\}
is a Schauder basis of
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
.

4.
Proof of Proposition
3.2
: Poisson Fourier modes and orthogonality

The
Jacobi polynomials
of parameters
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
are defined by

(4.1)

\mathtt{P}_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x):=\binom{\alpha+k}{k}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-k,k+\alpha+\beta+1;\alpha+1;\frac{1-x}{2}\right),\qquad x\in[-1,1]\,.

These functions are related to the PFM as follows:

Lemma 4.1

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|n|\leq m
. Then

(4.2)

P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{(1-zw)^{|n|}}\mathtt{P}_{m-|n|}^{(|n|,|n|)}\left(\frac{zw+1}{zw-1}\right)\cdot\begin{cases}w^{n}\quad&\mathrm{if}\,\,n\geq 0\,,\\
z^{|n|}\quad&\mathrm{if}\,\,n\leq 0\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
. Using a transformation formula for
{}_{2}F_{1}
functions, see
[
1
, Eq. 15.3.4]
, we have

(4.3)

P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)=\binom{m}{n}(-1)^{n}w^{n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-m,1+m;n+1;\frac{zw}{zw-1}\right)\,.

Hence,

(4.4)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{n}\binom{m+n}{n}^{-1}(-1)^{n}w^{n}\mathtt{P}_{m}^{(n,-n)}\left(\frac{1+zw}{1-zw}\right)\,.

The Jacobi polynomials fulfil the symmetry property
\mathtt{P}_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=(-1)^{k}\mathtt{P}_{k}^{(\beta,\alpha)}(-x)
as well as

\binom{k}{\ell}\mathtt{P}_{k}^{(-\ell,\beta)}(x)=\binom{k+\beta}{\ell}\left(\frac{x-1}{2}\right)^{\ell}\mathtt{P}_{k-\ell}^{(\ell,\beta)}(x)

for all
\ell=1,2,...,k
, see
[
34
, p. 59, 64]
. Inserting both identities into (
4.4
) yields (
4.2
).
∎

The Jacobi polynomials are called orthogonal polynomials due to the following property:

(4.5)

\int\limits_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}\mathtt{P}_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\mathtt{P}_{\ell}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\,dx=\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\beta,k}\delta_{k,\ell}

with
\alpha,\beta>-1
, see
[
1
, Eq. 22.2.1]
. Here,
\delta_{k,\ell}:=1
if
k=\ell
and
\delta_{k,\ell}:=0
otherwise, and

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha,\beta,k}:=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(k+\alpha+1)\Gamma(k+\beta+1)}{k!(2k+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(k+\alpha+\beta+1)}\,.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Let
n,q\geq 0
. Using polar coordinates and Lemma
4.1
we can rewrite the left-hand side of (
3.4
) and compute

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}P_{n}^{-m}(e^{it}\sqrt{s},-e^{-it}\sqrt{s})\overline{{P_{q}^{-p}(e^{it}\sqrt{s},-e^{-it}\sqrt{s})}}\frac{dtds}{(1+s)^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lem. }\ref{lem:Jacobipolynomials}}{=}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}(-1)^{m+p}e^{-i(n-q)t}\left(\frac{\sqrt{s}}{1+s}\right)^{n+q}\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)\mathtt{P}_{p-q}^{(q,q)}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)\frac{dtds}{(1+s)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+p}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-i(n-q)t}\,dt}_{=\delta_{n,q}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{s}}{1+s}\right)^{n+q}\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)\mathtt{P}_{p-q}^{(q,q)}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)\frac{ds}{(1+s)^{2}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+p}\delta_{n,q}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{s}{(1+s)^{2}}\right)^{n}\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)\mathtt{P}_{p-n}^{(n,n)}\left(\frac{s-1}{s+1}\right)\frac{ds}{(1+s)^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{x=\tfrac{s-1}{s+1}}{=}(-1)^{m+p}\frac{\delta_{n,q}}{2}\int\limits_{-1}^{1}\frac{(1+x)^{n}(1-x)^{n}}{4^{n}}\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}\left(x\right)\mathtt{P}_{p-n}^{(n,n)}\left(x\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Jacobiintegral}}{=}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{n,n,m-n}}{4^{n}}\frac{\delta_{n,q}\delta_{m,p}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m+1}\binom{m}{n}\binom{m+n}{n}^{-1}\delta_{n,q}\delta_{m,p}\,.

For
n,q<0
only the global signs of the exponential functions in the second and third line change. Similarly, one shows that the integral always vanishes if the subscripts of the PFM in (
3.4
) have different signs.
∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
3.4
: Poisson Fourier modes as Schauder basis of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}(\Omega)}

The proof of Theorem
3.4
is divided into several steps because there are some technical considerations involved. However, as this mainly concerns the existence part, we give a proof of uniqueness right away.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
: uniqueness.

By linearity it suffices to show that
H:=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=-m}^{m}c_{n,m}P_{n}^{-m}=0
implies
c_{n,m}=0
for all
n,m
. Applying Proposition
3.2
yields

\displaystyle 0=\frac{i}{\pi}\int\limits_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}H(z,-\overline{{z}})\overline{{P_{n}^{-m}(z,-\overline{{z}})}}\frac{dz\,d\overline{{z}}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\sum_{q=-p}^{p}c_{q,p}\frac{i}{\pi}\int\limits_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}P_{q}^{-p}(z,-\overline{{z}})\overline{{P_{n}^{-m}(z,-\overline{{z}})}}\frac{dz\,d\overline{{z}}}{(1+|z|^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\sum_{q=-p}^{p}c_{q,p}\frac{1}{2m+1}\binom{m}{n}\binom{m+n}{n}^{-1}\delta_{m,p}\delta_{n,q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m+1}\binom{m}{n}\binom{m+n}{n}^{-1}c_{n,m}\,.

Hence
c_{n,m}=0
. Note that the computation also shows (
3.9
).
∎

In
[
14
]
the structure of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
is investigated and a Schauder basis is identified.

Lemma 5.1

(Corollary 4.8 in
[
14
]
)

The set
(f_{p,q})_{p,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of functions where

f_{p,q}(z,w)=\frac{z^{p}w^{q}}{\left(1-zw\right)^{\max\{p,q\}}}\,,\quad p,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

is a Schauder basis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. In particular, each
F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
has a unique representation as

(5.1)

F(z,w)=\sum\limits_{p,q=0}^{\infty}b_{p,q}f_{p,q}(z,w)\,.

This series converges absolutely and locally uniformly in
\Omega
, and the Schauder coefficients
b_{p,q}
of
F
are given by

(5.2)

b_{p,q}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{D}}F\left(z,\frac{w}{1+zw}\right)\frac{dzdw}{z^{p+1}w^{q+1}}&p<q\,,\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{D}}F\left(\frac{z}{1+zw},w\right)\frac{dzdw}{z^{p+1}w^{q+1}}&p\geq q\,.\end{cases}

Note that similar to the PFM the functions
f_{p,q}
also have a symmetry property, namely
f_{p,q}(z,w)=f_{q,p}(w,z)
. The following result will not be needed in the remainder of this paper. However, it is interesting in its own right: we already know by definition of a Schauder basis, that every PFM can be expressed using the functions
(f_{p,q})_{p,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, but, in fact, every PFM can be expressed using only finitely many of the functions
(f_{p,q})_{p,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Lemma 5.2

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|n|\leq m
. We have

(5.3)

P_{n}^{-m}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m-|n|}\binom{m}{|n|+j}\binom{m+|n|+j}{j}\cdot\begin{cases}f_{j,j+n}\quad&\mathrm{if}\,\,n\geq 0\,,\\
f_{j+|n|,j}\quad&\mathrm{if}\,\,n\leq 0\,.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
n\geq 0
and fix
(z,w)\in\Omega\cap(\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C})
. In view of the global factor
(1-zw)^{-m}
in (
3.3
), we rewrite each of the
f_{j,j+n}
as

f_{j,j+n}(z,w)=\frac{z^{j}w^{j+n}}{(1-zw)^{m}}(1-zw)^{m-n-j}=\frac{1}{(1-zw)^{m}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-n-j}\binom{m-n-j}{k}(-1)^{k}z^{j+k}w^{j+n+k}.

Dropping this common prefactor, (
5.3
) is equivalent to

\sum_{j=0}^{m-n}\binom{m}{j}\binom{m}{n+j}z^{j}w^{j+n}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-n}c_{j,m,n}\sum_{k=0}^{m-n-j}\binom{m-n-j}{k}(-1)^{k}z^{j+k}w^{j+n+k}\,,

where
c_{j,m,n}=\binom{m}{n+j}\binom{m+n+j}{j}
. Using the linear independence of the polynomials
z^{j}w^{j+n}
thus yields a system of linear equations for the coefficients
c_{j,m,n}
. Comparing coefficients for
z^{\ell}w^{\ell+n}
leads to the identity

\binom{m}{\ell}\binom{m}{n+\ell}=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}c_{j,m,n}\binom{m-n-j}{\ell-j}(-1)^{\ell-j}\,.

Our choice of
c_{j,m,n}
indeed satisfies this identity: first, we rewrite

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}c_{j,m,n}\binom{m-n-j}{\ell-j}(-1)^{\ell-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{m}{n+j}\binom{m+n+j}{j}\binom{m-n-j}{\ell-j}(-1)^{\ell-j}

\displaystyle=\binom{m}{n+\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{m+n+j}{j}\binom{n+\ell}{n+j}(-1)^{\ell-j}.

Thus, it remains to show that the sum on the right-hand side equals
\binom{m}{\ell}
. Note that we can replace the sum by a hypergeometric
{}_{2}F_{1}
function. Moreover, investing the
Chu-Vandermonde identity

[
4
, Cor. 2.2.3]

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-k,b;c;1\right)=\frac{(c-b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}\,,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,\;b,c\in\mathbb{C}\,,\;-c\not\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{m+n+j}{j}\binom{n+\ell}{n+j}(-1)^{\ell-j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\binom{n+\ell}{n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\ell,m+n+1;n+1;1\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\binom{n+\ell}{n}\frac{(-m)_{\ell}}{(n+1)_{\ell}}=\binom{m}{\ell}\,.\qed

An essential observation that allows us to prove the existence part of Theorem
3.4
is that Lemma
5.2
has a converse.

Lemma 5.3

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n\leq m
. For all
(z,w)\in\Omega
we have

(5.4)

\displaystyle f_{n,m}(z,w)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-n}\sum_{s=0}^{n}a_{s,m,n}P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}(z,w)=f_{m,n}(w,z)

where

(5.5)

a_{s,m,n}=(-1)^{s}\binom{m}{s}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}\,.

Proof.

The denominator degree of each term of the sum in (
5.4
) differs. Therefore, we rewrite

\displaystyle P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{z^{k}w^{k+m-n}}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{z^{k}w^{k+m-n}}{(1-zw)^{m}}(1-zw)^{s}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m-n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{z^{k}w^{k+m-n}}{(1-zw)^{m}}\sum_{t=0}^{s}(-1)^{t}\binom{s}{t}(zw)^{t}\,.

Hence, we can drop the factor
w^{m-n}(1-zw)^{-m}
in (
5.4
), and we have to prove

\displaystyle(zw)^{n}=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\sum_{t=0}^{s}(-1)^{s+t}\binom{m}{s}\binom{s}{t}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}(zw)^{k+t}\,.

Now, we reorder the right-hand side

\displaystyle(zw)^{n}=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\sum_{d=0}^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{d}(-1)^{s+t}\binom{m}{s}\binom{s}{t}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\binom{m-s}{d-t+m-n}\binom{m-s}{d-t}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}(zw)^{d}\,.

Thus, it suffices to show

(CID)

\displaystyle\delta_{n,d}=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{d}(-1)^{s+t}\binom{m}{s}\binom{s}{t}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\binom{m-s}{d-t+m-n}\binom{m-s}{d-t}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}\,.

Since (
CID
) is “just” a combinatorial identity, we postpone its proof to Section
8
, because the techniques needed for its proof are not required elsewhere in this section.
∎

Corollary 5.4

The absolute values of the coefficients
a_{s,m,n}
from (
5.5
) are less than or equal to 1.

Proof.

For
d=n
we know from (
CID
) that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{n}(-1)^{s+t}\binom{m}{s}\binom{s}{t}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\binom{m-s}{m-t}\binom{m-s}{n-t}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom{m}{s}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\binom{m-s}{n-s}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom{m-s}{n-s}|a_{s,m,n}|\,.

Thus, as a sum of non-negative scalars and with

\binom{m-s}{n-s}\geq 1
it follows that
|a_{s,m,n}|\leq 1
for all
s=0,...,n
.
∎

Now we can turn to the proof of Theorem
3.4
. Our strategy is as follows: we use the Schauder representation of
F
with respect to the “old” Schauder basis
(f_{m,n})_{m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
given by (
5.1
), i.e.

F(z,w)=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}b_{m,n}f_{m,n}(z,w)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}b_{m,n}f_{m,n}(z,w)+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}b_{n,m}f_{n,m}(z,w)\right]\,.

Here, we can replace every
f_{m,n}
function with a sum of PFM by Lemma
5.3
which yields

(5.6)

\displaystyle F(z,w)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-n}b_{m,n}\sum_{s=0}^{n}a_{s,m,n}

\displaystyle P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}(z,w)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-n}b_{n,m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}a_{s,m,n}P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}(z,w)\Bigg]\,.

Thus, we show (
3.8
) if we show absolute and uniform convergence on compact subsets of the series (
5.6
), because this fact allows us to interchange the order of summation. In the following, given a set
K\subseteq\Omega
we write
\|F\|_{K}:=\sup\{|F(z)|\,:\,z\in K\}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
: existence.

Let
K\subseteq\Omega
be compact. Without loss of generality, we can assume that there exists
R>1
such that
K
is contained in the intersection of
\Omega
with one of the sets
K_{i}
,
i=a,b,c,d
, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{(a)}\,\,K_{a}

\displaystyle:=\overline{{K_{R}(0)}}\times\overline{{K_{R}(0)}}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{(b)}\,\,K_{b}

\displaystyle:=\left(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K_{R}(0)\right)\times\left(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K_{R}(0)\right)\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

\displaystyle\mathrm{(c)}\,\,K_{c}

\displaystyle:=\overline{{K_{R}(0)}}\times\left(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K_{R}(0)\right)\subseteq\mathbb{C}\times\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}

\displaystyle\mathrm{(d)}\,\,K_{d}

\displaystyle:=\left(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus K_{R}(0)\right)\times\overline{{K_{R}(0)}}\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times\mathbb{C}\,.

Here,
{K_{R}(0):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|z|<R\}}
. To see that this is true note that we can cover
K
with a finite collection of sets of the form
K_{i}^{\circ}
,
i=a,b,c,d
, where
K_{i}^{\circ}
denotes the interior of
K_{i}
.

Using (
5.6
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|F\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{m,n}||a_{s,m,n}|\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{n,m}||a_{s,m,n}|\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}\right]

(5.7)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Corollary}\ref{cor:coefficientsleq1}}{\leq}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{m,n}|\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{n,m}|\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}\right]\,.

In order to show absolute and uniform convergence of (
5.6
) on
K
, we need to estimate the absolute values of the coefficients
b_{n,m}
and the values of the PFM
P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}
on
K
.

Let
r_{1},r_{2}>0
. Cauchy’s integral formula applied to (
5.2
) implies for
n<m
that

\displaystyle|b_{n,m}|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{-1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{D}}F\left(z,\frac{w}{1+zw}\right)\frac{dzdw}{z^{n+1}w^{m+1}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{|z|=r_{1}}\int\limits_{|w|=r_{2}}F\left(z,\frac{w}{1+zw}\right)\frac{dzdw}{z^{n+1}w^{m+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r_{1}^{n}r_{2}^{m}}\sup\limits_{|z|=r_{1},\,|w|=r_{2}}\left|F\left(z,\frac{w}{1+zw}\right)\right|\,.

Analogously, it holds for
n\leq m
that

\displaystyle|b_{m,n}|\leq\frac{1}{r_{1}^{m}r_{2}^{n}}\sup\limits_{|z|=r_{1},\,|w|=r_{2}}\left|F\left(\frac{z}{1+zw},w\right)\right|\,.

Note that in both cases we used that the functions

(z,w)\mapsto F\left(z,\frac{w}{1+zw}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad(z,w)\mapsto F\left(\frac{z}{1+zw},w\right)

define holomorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Since
F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
, it follows that
F
is continuous on
\Omega
and attains its maximum modulus on every compact set. Thus, there is some
M>0
(depending on
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
) such that

(5.8)

|b_{n,m}|\leq\frac{1}{r_{1}^{n}r_{2}^{m}}\cdot M\quad\text{and}\quad|b_{m,n}|\leq\frac{1}{r_{1}^{m}r_{2}^{n}}\cdot M\,.

(a)

Assume
K\subseteq K_{a}
. Since
K
is compact, we know that

\delta_{K}:=\inf\{|1-zw|\,:\,(z,w)\in K\}>0\,.

Thus, also
\delta:=\min\{\delta_{K},4\}>0
. Recall that we can express
P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}
as a rational function by (
3.3
), i.e.

(5.9)

P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}(z,w)=(-1)^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{z^{k+m-n}w^{k}}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}\,.

Hence, we compute

\displaystyle\left\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\right\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\left|\frac{z^{k+m-n}w^{k}}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{\left|z^{k+m-n}w^{k}\right|}{\delta^{m-s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\frac{R^{2k+m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}(R^{2})^{k}=\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n-s+1}}{1-R^{2}}

and the same estimate holds for
\left\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\right\|_{K}
. Together, (
5.8
) and (
a
) imply for (
5
)

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{m,n}|\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{n,m}|\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\frac{M}{r_{1}^{m}r_{2}^{n}}\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n-s+1}}{1-R^{2}}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\frac{M}{r_{1}^{n}r_{2}^{m}}\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n-s+1}}{1-R^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{1}\delta}\right)^{m}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n+1}}{1-R^{2}}\frac{1}{r_{2}^{n}}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{s}

\displaystyle\hskip 199.16928pt+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{2}\delta}\right)^{m}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n+1}}{1-R^{2}}\frac{1}{r_{1}^{n}}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{s}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq M\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{1}\delta}\right)^{m}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n+1}}{1-R^{2}}\frac{n+1}{r_{2}^{n}}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{2}\delta}\right)^{m}\frac{1-(R^{2})^{n+1}}{1-R^{2}}\frac{n+1}{r_{1}^{n}}\right]\,.

Our assumption
R>1
implies

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{m,n}|\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{n,m}|\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{MR^{2}}{R^{2}-1}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{1}\delta}\right)^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{R^{2}}{r_{2}}\right)^{n}(n+1)+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{2}\delta}\right)^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{R^{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{n}(n+1)\right]\,.

Now choose
r_{1},r_{2}>\max\{4R/\delta,R^{2}\}>1
to guarantee convergence.

(b)

Assume
K\subseteq K_{b}
. Then
\widetilde{K}:=\{(1/w,1/z)\,:\,(z,w)\in K\}
is a compact set and contained in
K_{R}(0)\times K_{R}(0)
. Now, it follows immediately from (
3.3
) that

(5.11)

P_{n}^{-m}(z,w)=(-1)^{m}P_{n}^{-m}\left(\frac{1}{w},\frac{1}{z}\right)

for all
(z,w)\in\Omega
. Hence,

\left\|P_{\pm(n-m)}^{-(m-s)}\right\|_{K}=\left\|P_{\pm(n-m)}^{-(m-s)}\right\|_{\widetilde{K}}\,.

Therefore, we can apply Case (a).

(c)

Assume
K\subseteq K_{c}
. Again, since
K
is compact, we have

\delta_{K}:=\inf\{|1-zw|\,:\,(z,w)\in K\}>0\,.

It follows that

\delta_{K}^{\prime}:=\inf\left\{\left|\frac{1}{w}-z\right|\,:\,(z,w)\in K\right\}>0\,.

Moreover,
\widetilde{K}=\{(1/w,1/z)\,:\,(z,w)\in K\}
is compact, and thus also

\delta_{\widetilde{K}}:=\inf\left\{\left|1-\frac{1}{zw}\right|\,:\,(z,w)\in K\right\}>0\,.

Define

\left|\frac{zw}{1-zw}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{zw}}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\delta_{\widetilde{K}}}

we compute

\displaystyle\left\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\right\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\left|\frac{z^{k+m-n}w^{k}}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\cdot\begin{cases}\left|\frac{(zw)^{m-s}}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}\right|\cdot R^{m-n},\quad\text{if }|zw|>1\\
\left|\frac{1}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}\right|\cdot R^{m-n},\quad\text{if }|zw|\leq 1\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\delta_{\widetilde{K}}^{m-s}}R^{m-n},\quad\text{if }|zw|>1\\
\frac{1}{\delta_{K}^{m-s}}R^{m-n},\quad\text{if }|zw|\leq 1\\
\end{cases}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}

(5.12)

\displaystyle\leq n\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}\,.

Similarly, we compute

\displaystyle\left\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\right\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\left|\frac{z^{k}w^{k+m-n}}{(1-zw)^{m-s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}\left|\frac{z^{k}}{w^{n-s-k}(1/w-z)^{m-s}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in K}\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\binom{m-s}{k+m-n}\binom{m-s}{k}R^{n-s}\frac{1}{\delta^{m-s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-s}\frac{4^{m-s}R^{n-s}}{\delta^{m-s}}

(5.13)

\displaystyle\leq n\frac{4^{m-s}R^{n-s}}{\delta^{m-s}}\,.

Inserting (
5.8
), (
c

c

5

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{m,n}|\|P_{n-m}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}|b_{n,m}|\|P_{m-n}^{-(m-s)}\|_{K}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\frac{M}{r_{1}^{m}r_{2}^{n}}n\frac{4^{m-s}R^{m-n}}{\delta^{m-s}}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\frac{M}{r_{1}^{n}r_{2}^{m}}n\frac{4^{m-s}R^{n-s}}{\delta^{m-s}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{1}\delta}\right)^{m}\frac{n}{(r_{2}R)^{n}}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{s}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{4}{r_{2}\delta}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{R}{r_{1}}\right)^{n}n\sum_{s=0}^{n}\left(\frac{\delta}{4}\right)^{s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq M\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\left(\frac{4R}{r_{1}\delta}\right)^{m}\frac{n(n+1)}{(r_{2}R)^{n}}+\sum_{n=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{4}{r_{2}\delta}\right)^{m}\left(\frac{R}{r_{1}}\right)^{n}n(n+1)\right]

(d)

6.
Complex spherical harmonics

Before we introduce the
complex spherical harmonics
in Section
6.3
, we discuss a different model of
\Omega
first: the complex two-sphere
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

(6.1)

\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:=\left\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\,\big|\,z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}=1\right\}

from (
1.10
). After that, in Section
6.2
, we give some background about classical spherical harmonics. In general, for a solid introduction to that theory, see e.g.
[
5
, Ch. 5]
and
[
33
, Ch. 3]
.

6.1.
The complex two-sphere

The complex two-sphere
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is a closed but unbounded subset of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
, thus not compact. The domain
\Omega
is biholomorphically equivalent to
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
by way of the biholomorphic map
{S\colon\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}
,

(6.2)

S(z,w)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\frac{z-w}{1-zw},-i\frac{z+w}{1-zw},-\frac{1+zw}{1-zw}\right)&\phantom{if\quad}(z,w)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\,,\;zw\not=1\,,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\left(1/z,i/z,1\right)&\text{ if \quad}z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\,,\;w=\infty\,,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\left(-1/w,i/w,1\right)&\phantom{if\quad}w\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\,,\;z=\infty\,.\end{cases}

Note the difference in our notation between
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. The inverse map of
S\colon\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is given by
S^{-1}\colon\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to\Omega
,

(6.3)

S^{-1}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\frac{z_{1}+iz_{2}}{1-z_{3}},-\frac{z_{1}-iz_{2}}{1-z_{3}}\right)&\phantom{if\quad}z_{3}\not=1\,,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\left(1/z_{1},\infty\right)&\text{ if \quad}z_{3}=1\,,\;z_{2}=iz_{1}\,,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\left(\infty,-1/z_{1}\right)&\phantom{if\quad}z_{3}=1\,,\;z_{2}=-iz_{1}\,.\end{cases}

It is natural to call
S^{-1}\colon\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to\Omega
the “complex stereographic projection”, since if we denote by

\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{S}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{3}=\big\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{2}^{3}=1\big\}

the euclidean two-sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then

S^{-1}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\frac{x_{1}+ix_{2}}{1-x_{3}},-\frac{x_{1}-ix_{2}}{1-x_{3}}\right)\quad\text{ for all }\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,

and

\mathbb{S}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\,,\qquad\mathbf{x}\mapsto\frac{x_{1}+ix_{2}}{1-x_{3}}

is the standard stereographic projection of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
onto
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. In particular,

S^{-1}
maps
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
onto the “rotated diagonal”
\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
(see (
1.2
)).

Remark 5
.

Let
H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be the image of the hyperboloid
{\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}=1\}}
under the biholomorphic map given by
(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\mapsto(iz_{1},iz_{2},z_{3})
. Then
S^{-1}
maps “half of”
H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
onto the diagonal
\mathrm{L}_{\mathbb{D}}
. More precisely, the lower half
\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,:\,x_{3}\leq-1\}

is mapped onto
\mathrm{L}_{\mathbb{D}}
and the upper half
\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in H_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,:\,x_{3}\geq 1\}

onto
\{(z,\overline{z})\,:\,z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}\}
.

Moreover, for
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
with
z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
we define the “complexified length of vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
” by

|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}:=\sqrt{z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+z_{3}^{2}}\,.

Here, the square root is to be understood as the principal branch of the square root with branch cut along the non-positive real axis. It follows that for every such
\mathbf{z}
we have
\mathbf{z}/|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

6.2.
From real to complex spherical harmonics

When studying the real euclidean Laplace equation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, i.e

(6.4)

\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{k}^{2}}f(\mathbf{x})=0\,\quad\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is a twice differentiable function, one common approach is to work with spherical harmonics. The name “spherical” stems from the fact that spherical harmonics “live” on the real
d-1
sphere, i.e. they are functions defined on

\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}:=\left\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\big|\,x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{d}^{2}=1\right\}\,.

More precisely, for
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and an
harmonic
and
m
-homogeneous
polynomial
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, i.e.
p
is a polynomial fulfilling (
6.4
) and

(6.5)

p(r\mathbf{x})=r^{m}p(\mathbf{x})\,,\quad\text{for all }r>0\;,

the restriction
q:=p|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\mathbb{R}}}
is called a (real or classical)
spherical harmonic
of degree
m
.

Remark 6
.

Recall (
3.5
) where we identified the PFM as
n
-homogeneous functions. Homogeneity in the sense of (
6.5
) is different from the understanding in (
3.5
). Throughout this section and the following we will use the term
homogeneous function
for functions fulfilling (
6.5
) because this definition is standard in the theory of (real) spherical harmonics. Moreover, homogeneity in the sense of (
3.5
) will be referred to as
\Omega
-homogeneity
.

For functions defined on the real sphere, it is useful to work in spherical coordinates by which we mean identifying each
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
with a tuple
(r,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{\mathbb{R}}
. This point of view enables one to always construct a harmonic
m
-homogeneous polynomial
p
to a given spherical harmonic
q
of degree
m
by setting
p(r,\sigma)=r^{m}q(\sigma)
. In particular, if we consider the spherical part of the real euclidean Laplacian, i.e. the restriction of
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
to
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
which we denote by
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}
, then it is an easy calculation to verify the following characterization:

(6.6)

\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p=0\quad\iff\quad\Delta_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}q=-m(m+d-2)q\,.

Now note that for the special case
d=3
a different description of
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is given by means of the stereographic projection, i.e. we can identify the real two-sphere with the Riemann sphere
\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
. It follows immediately that
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
translates to
\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
from (
1.8
). Since there actually is a counterpart of the two-sphere as well as the stereographic projection in our complexified theory, see the previous Section
6.1
, we can use this approach together with (
6.6
) to define complexified, or in short complex, spherical harmonics.

6.3.
Complex spherical harmonics

Let
F\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and fix
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. By means of the inverse of the complex stereographic projection we define a holomorphic function by

(6.7)

G:\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\,,\quad G:=F\circ S^{-1}\,.

Using this function we define another holomorphic function

(6.8)

\hat{G}:\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\,:\,\mathbf{z}=t\sigma,\,t\in\mathbb{C},\,\mathrm{Re}\,t>0,\,\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\,,\quad\hat{G}(\mathbf{z})=|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}^{m}(F\circ S^{-1})\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}}\right)\,.

Note that the domain of
\hat{G}
is biholomorphically equivalent to the cartesian product of the right half plane with
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Furthermore,
\hat{G}
fulfils

\hat{G}(r\sigma)=r^{m}\hat{G}(\sigma)\quad\text{for all }r>0\,,

and a computation yields

(6.9)

\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\hat{G}(\mathbf{z}):=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{k}^{2}}\hat{G}(\mathbf{z})=|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}^{2-m}\left(\left(m(m+1)F-\left(1-\,zw\right)^{2}\partial_{z}\partial_{w}F\right)\circ S^{-1}\right)\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}}{|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}}\right)\,.

This identity immediately implies

\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\hat{G}=0\quad\iff\quad\Delta_{zw}F=4m(m+1)F\,.

By the definition of the eigenspaces
X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
of
\Delta_{zw}
in (
3.6
), both statements are also equivalent to
F\in X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
. Moreover, in view of Lemma
4.1
we can deduce the following.

Lemma 6.1

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
F\in X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
and define
\hat{G}
as in (
6.8
). Then
\hat{G}
is an
m
-homogeneous polynomial in
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
. In particular, if
F=P_{\pm n}^{-m}
for
0\leq n\leq m
, then it holds that

(6.10)

\displaystyle\hat{G}\Big|_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\left(\frac{\mp z_{1}+iz_{2}}{2}\right)^{n}\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}\left(z_{3}\right)\,.

Here,
\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}
denotes the Jacobi polynomials from (
4.1
).

Proof.

Denote the domain of
\hat{G}
given in (
6.8
) by
\Lambda
. By definition,
\hat{G}
is
m
-homogeneous and holomorphic on
\Lambda
. In view of the PFM being a basis of the finite dimensional space
X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
it suffices to show that
\hat{G}
coincides with a polynomial on
{\Lambda}
whenever
F=P_{\pm n}^{-m}
. However, since this requires some tedious computations, we only stress the important ideas: one verifies (
6.10
) by rewriting
P_{\pm n}^{-m}
as a Jacobi polynomial (see Lemma
4.1
) and using the explicit formula for
S^{-1}
. Then by induction over
m
one can check that

(-1)^{m}|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}^{m-n}\left(\frac{\mp z_{1}+iz_{2}}{2}\right)^{n}\mathtt{P}_{m-n}^{(n,n)}\left(\frac{z_{3}}{|\mathbf{z}|_{\mathbb{C}}}\right)

is indeed a polynomial. Here, the recursion formula
[
1
, Eq. 22.7.1]
for Jacobi polynomials is useful.
∎

Therefore, the functions
F
and
\hat{G}
fulfil all the properties that we have singled out about (real) spherical harmonics
q
and the corresponding harmonic homogeneous polynomials
p
in Section
6.2
. More precisely,

(i)

if
\hat{G}
is an
m
-homogeneous polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
fulfilling
\Delta_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}\hat{G}=0
, then
\hat{G}|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
is an
m
-homogeneous
polynomial in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
fulfilling
\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hat{G}=0

(ii)

if
F\in X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
, then
f(z):=F(z,-\overline{z})
fulfils
\Delta_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}f=4m(m+1)f
. Moreover,
S^{-1}
maps
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
bijectively onto the “rotated diagonal”
\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}
as was noted in Section
6.1

This justifies the following definition.

Definition 6.2

(Complex spherical harmonics)

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
F\in X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
. Let
G_{F}:=G\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
where
G
is the function induced by
F
in (
6.7
). We define the
space of complex spherical harmonics of degree
m
as the linear hull of all
G_{F}
, i.e.

\mathrm{CSH}(m):=\mathrm{span}\{G_{F}\,:\,F\in X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)\}\,.

Every
Q\in\mathrm{CSH}(m)
is called
complex spherical harmonic of degree
m
. Further, define

\mathrm{CSH}:=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}F_{k}\,:\,N\in\mathbb{N},\,F_{k}\in\mathrm{CSH}(m)\text{ for some }m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}\,.

Every function
Q\in\mathrm{CSH}
is called
complex spherical harmonic
. Moreover, we denote by
\mathrm{CSH}^{-}
the closure of
\mathrm{CSH}
with respect to the locally uniform topology on
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

Essentially, since
S^{-1}
is bijective, we can now use our knowledge about
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
and, in particular, the PFM in order to understand the complex spherical harmonics. Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
|n|\leq m
. Motivated by
[
16
]
where it is shown that the PFM play a special role for the eigenspace theory of
\Delta_{zw}
we introduce their spherical counterpart.

Definition 6.3

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
n
-th spherical Poisson Fourier mode (sPFM) of its
(-m)
-th power
is the function
Q_{n}^{-m}:\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
given by

Q_{n}^{-m}=P_{n}^{-m}\circ S^{-1}\;.

Since the PFM
\{P_{n}^{-m}\,:\,|n|\leq m\}
span
X_{4m(m+1)}(\Omega)
, see
[
16
, Th. 8.1]
, we conclude that
\mathrm{CSH}(m)
is spanned by the sPFM
\{Q_{n}^{-m}\,:\,|n|\leq m\}
and has dimension
2m+1
. Note that this matches precisely the dimension of the space of real spherical harmonics of degree
m
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, see
[
33
, Cor. 3.5.6]
. Further, we can again translate the orthogonality of the Jacobi polynomials to our setting.

Corollary 6.4

The sPFM
Q_{n}^{-m}
fulfil the following orthogonality property

\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}Q_{n}^{-m}(\mathbf{x})\overline{{Q_{q}^{-p}(\mathbf{x})}}\,d\mathbf{x}=\binom{m}{n}\binom{m+n}{n}^{-1}\frac{\delta_{m,p}\delta_{n,q}}{2m+1}\,.

Here,
d\mathbf{x}
denotes the normalized area measure on
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Proof.

This is the spherical version of Proposition
3.2
since
S(\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}})=\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
∎

This result allows us to explicitly describe the space(s) of complex spherical harmonics.

Theorem 6.5

(a)

The set
\{Q_{n}^{-m}\,:\,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\,n\in\mathbb{Z},\,|n|\leq m\}
is a Schauder basis of
\mathrm{CSH}^{-}
. In particular,
\mathrm{CSH}^{-}=\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
and each
Q\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
has a unique representation as

Q(\mathbf{z})=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{n=-m}^{m}c_{n,m}Q_{n}^{-m}(\mathbf{z})\,.

This series converges absolutely and locally uniformly in
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, and the Schauder coefficients
c_{\pm n,m}
of
Q
are given by

c_{\pm n,m}=\frac{2m+1}{2}\binom{m}{n}^{-1}\binom{m+n}{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}Q(\mathbf{x})\overline{{Q_{\pm n}^{-m}(\mathbf{x})}}\,d\mathbf{x}\,.

(b)

The map
\langle\cdot{,}\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\colon\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\times\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

(6.11)

\langle Q,P\rangle_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}Q(\mathbf{x})\overline{{P(\mathbf{x})}}\,d\mathbf{x}

is an inner product on
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. In particular,
(\mathrm{CSH}(m),\langle\cdot{,}\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}})
is a
2m+1
-dimensional Hilbert space for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and the sPFM
\{Q_{n}^{-m}\,:\,|n|\leq m\}
form an orthogonal basis. Moreover, the spaces
\mathrm{CSH}(m)
and
\mathrm{CSH}(p)
,
m,p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, are mutually orthogonal with respect to
\langle\cdot{,}\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
.

Proof.

Part (a) is a consequence of the PFM being a Schauder basis of
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
(Theorem
3.4
), the sPFM being orthogonal (Corollary
6.4
) and
S^{-1}
being a bijection. Part (b) is the spherical version of Corollary
3.3
. Since
S(\mathrm{L}_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}})=\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we can again (see Remark
2
) argue with the identity principle to see that (
6.11
) indeed defines an inner product on
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
. The orthogonality properties follow from Corollary
6.4
.
∎

Theorem
6.5
immediately implies for the space
\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
from (

Corollary 6.6

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
. Then
f\in\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
if and only if
f\circ S^{-1}=Q|_{\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}
for some

is uniquely determined.

Remark 7
.

Recall (

[

) every function that is holomorphic on

\widetilde{G}\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{C}^{3})

.
Now suppose that

is an eigenfunction of

. Then

is given by a linear combination of PFM. In view of Lemma

being a homogeneous polynomial hence, in particular,

which shows that there is a

[
22

and
[
23
]
. However, Morimoto’s theory strongly depends on the Lie sphere which does not show up in our theory.

Remark 8
.

By Theorem

7.
Complex zonal harmonics

Consider the matrix group
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
of real orthogonal matrices or, in short, rotations of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}
. Note that
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
consists precisely of the isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e. the linear transformations which preserve the standard inner product

\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle:=\sum_{j=1}^{3}x_{j}y_{j}\,,\qquad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,.

These matrices arise naturally in the theory of (real) spherical harmonics because the space of (real) spherical harmonics of fixed degree
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is invariant under the action of
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, there exists a unique (up to a multiplicative factor) function
\mathrm{z}_{m}\colon\mathbb{S}^{2}_{\mathbb{R}}\times\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with the properties that
z_{m}
is a (real) spherical harmonic of degree
m
if either of its arguments is fixed, and such that
z_{m}
is invariant under rotations applied to both variables, i.e.

(7.1)

z_{m}(R\mathbf{x},R\mathbf{y})=z_{m}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\qquad R\in\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}),\,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\,.

Hence,
\mathrm{z}_{m}
depends only on the inner product of its arguments. This function
\mathrm{z}_{m}
is called
(real) zonal harmonic of degree
m
. In order to find a counterpart of
z_{m}
in our complexified theory, we will first investigate the counterpart of
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
: the “rotations” of
\mathbb{S}_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, that is

\text{SO}(3,\mathbb{C}):=\{R\in\mathbb{C}^{3\times 3}\,:\,R^{T}R=I,\,\det R=1\}\,.

Note that for our purpose it will be easier to work in the
\Omega
-setting again. By way of the inverse complex stereographic projection,
\text{SO}(3,\mathbb{C})
corresponds to the
Möbius group of
\Omega
defined by

(7.2)

\mathcal{M}:=\bigcup\limits_{\psi\in{\sf Aut}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})}\left\{(z,w)\mapsto T_{\psi}(z,w):=\left(\psi(z),\frac{1}{\psi(1/w)}\right)\,,\,(z,w)\mapsto\left(\psi(w),\frac{1}{\psi(1/z)}\right)\right\}\,.

We say that
T_{\psi}\in\mathcal{M}
is induced by
\psi\in{\sf Aut}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}})
. The significance of
\mathcal{M}
for the spectral theory of
\Delta_{zw}
3
3

3

Here, we consider
\Delta_{zw}
as operator on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. For the significance of
\mathcal{M}\cap{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D})

as operator on
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D})

[
16
]
.
(and thus for the complex spherical harmonics) is that

consists precisely of the biholomorphic automorphisms of

satisfying

Lemma 7.3

Let

8.1.
The Hypergeometric and the Beta function

For
p,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the
generalized hypergeometric function

{}_{p}F_{q}
is defined by

(8.1)

{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\dots,a_p}{b_1,\dots,b_q};z\right]}:=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{k}\cdots(a_{p})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k}\cdots(b_{q})_{k}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}

for
a_{1},\dots,a_{p},b_{1},\dots,b_{q},z\in\mathbb{C}
,
|z|<1
and none of
b_{1},\dots,b_{q}
being a negative integer or zero. In many cases it is possible to extend the definition to more values of
z
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{q}
. In particular, if some
a_{j}=-n
for an integer
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
{}_{p}F_{q}
is a polynomial of degree
n
and all values of
z\in\mathbb{C}
as well as
b_{j}\leq-n
are permitted. This will be the situation in our considerations, because when proving a combinatorial identity, one way how hypergeometric series could come into play is identifying the finite combinatorial sum as a (terminating)
{}_{p}F_{q}
series at the point 1. Further, note that
{}_{2}F_{1}
coincides with (
2.1
).

Changing the order of the
a_{1},\dots,a_{p}
does not change the hypergeometric series. The same is true for the
b_{1},\dots,b_{q}
. Further, whenever there is some
a_{j}
that coincides with some
b_{k}
, both parameters cancel out and the
{}_{p}F_{q}
series is in fact a
{}_{p-1}F_{q-1}
series, i.e.

{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\dots,a_p,c}{b_1,\dots,b_q,c};z\right]}={}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\dots,a_p}{b_1,\dots,b_q};z\right]}\,.

This phenomenon can be generalized to parameters coinciding up to a positive integer shift, i.e.

(8.2)

{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_1,\dots,a_p,c+n}{b_1,\dots,b_q,c};z\right]}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\frac{(a_{1})_{j}\cdots(a_{p})_{j}}{(b_{1})_{j}\cdots(b_{q})_{j}(c)_{j}}{}_{p}F_{q}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a_1+j,\dots,a_p+j}{b_1+j,\dots,b_q+j};z\right]}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, see
[
25
, p. 439, 15.]
.

In view of (

) we also have to deal with an inverse binomial coefficient. Here, the

(8.3)

B(a,b)=\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{a-1}(1-y)^{b-1}\,dy\,,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb{C}\,,\;{\sf Re}\,(a),{\sf Re}\,(b)>0\,.

The connection to inverse binomial coefficients is the following

1

8.2.
Proof of Theorem
8.1

We compute

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{n}\sum_{t=0}^{d}(-1)^{s+t}\binom{m}{s}\binom{s}{t}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\binom{m-s}{d-t+m-n}\binom{m-s}{d-t}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:tsum}}{=}\sum_{s=0}^{n}(-1)^{s}\binom{m}{s}\binom{2m-s}{n}^{-1}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}(-1)^{d-n+s}\binom{m-s}{n-s}\binom{n}{d}\frac{s!(2m-s+1)!}{n!(2m-n+1)!}

\displaystyle\hskip 192.05609pt\times{}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{s-n,2m-n-s+1,m-d+1}{2m-n+2,m-n+1};1\right]}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{n}(2m-2s+1)\frac{m!(2m-s-n)!}{(n-s)!(2m-n+1)!(m-n)!}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{n}(2m-2s+1)\frac{m!(2m-s-n)!}{(n-s)!(2m-n+1)!(m-n)!}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}\binom{n-d}{k}\frac{(s-n)_{k}(2m-n-s+1)_{k}}{(2m-n+2)_{k}(m-n+1)_{k}}{}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{s-n+k,2m-n-s+1+k}{2m-n+2+k};1\right]}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{n}(2m-2s+1)m!\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}\binom{n-d}{k}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2m-n-s+k)!}{(n-s-k)!(m-n+k)!(2m-n+1+k)!}

\displaystyle\hskip 213.39566pt\times{}_{2}F_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{s-n+k,2m-n-s+1+k}{2m-n+2+k};1\right]}\,.

Now we use the Gauss identity
[

in order to evaluate the

\displaystyle\phantom{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{n}(2m-2s+1)m!\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}\binom{n-d}{k}\frac{(-1)^{k}(2m-n-s+k)!}{(n-s-k)!(m-n+k)!(2m-n+1+k)!}

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\times\frac{(2m-n+1+k)!(n-k)!}{s!(2m-s+1)!}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\frac{2m-2s+1}{2m-s+1}\binom{2m-s}{n-k}^{-1}\binom{n-k}{s}\binom{m}{n-k}

The inverse binomial coefficients can be identified with Beta functions, see (
8.4
),

\displaystyle\phantom{\overset{\phantom{\eqref{eq:Beta}}}{=}}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\times\sum_{s=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{s}(2m-2s+1)B(2m-s-n+k+1,n-k+1)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:Beta}}{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\times\sum_{s=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{s}(2m-2s+1)\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{2m-s-n+k}(1-y)^{n-k}\,dy

\displaystyle\overset{\phantom{\eqref{eq:Beta}}}{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\times\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{2m-n+k}(1-y)^{n-k}\Bigg[(2m+1)\sum_{s=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{s}y^{-s}dy-2\sum_{s=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{s}sy^{-s}\Bigg]\,dy\,.

We now compute the
s
-sums using the Binomial theorem in its standard and differentiated form

\displaystyle\phantom{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{2m-n+k}(1-y)^{n-k}\Bigg[(2m+1)y^{k-n}(1+y)^{n-k}-2(n-k)y^{k-n}(1+y)^{n-k-1}\Bigg]\,dy

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}\Bigg[(2m+1)\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{2(m-n+k)}(1-y^{2})^{n-k}\,dy

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-2(n-k)\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{2(m-n+k)}(1-y^{2})^{n-k-1}\,dy+2(n-k)\int\limits_{0}^{1}y^{2(m-n+k)+1}(1-y^{2})^{n-k-1}\,dy\Bigg]\,.

Using the Legendre duplication formula (
8.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle\phantom{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}\Bigg[\frac{2m+1}{2}B\left(m-n+k+\frac{1}{2},n-k+1\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt-(n-k)B\left(m-n+k+\frac{1}{2},n-k\right)+(n-k)B\left(m-n+k+1,n-k\right)\Bigg]\,.

We can rewrite the Beta functions using Gamma functions, see (
8.6
), and proceed with the recursion identity for Gamma functions, that is
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\phantom{=}(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}\Bigg[\frac{2m+1}{2}\frac{\Gamma\left(m-n+k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(n-k+1)}{\Gamma\left(m+\frac{3}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\hskip 78.24507pt-(n-k)\frac{\Gamma\left(m-n+k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(n-k)}{\Gamma\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}+(n-k)\frac{\Gamma\left(m-n+k+1\right)\Gamma(n-k)}{\Gamma\left(m+1\right)}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}\Bigg[\frac{\Gamma\left(m-n+k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(n-k+1)}{\Gamma\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-\frac{\Gamma\left(m-n+k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(n-k+1)}{\Gamma\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}+\frac{\Gamma\left(m-n+k+1\right)\Gamma(n-k+1)}{\Gamma\left(m+1\right)}\Bigg]\,.

Finally, by recognizing the fractions as binomial coefficients and using the Binomial theorem once again we obtain

\displaystyle(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-d}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-d}{k}\binom{m}{n-k}\binom{m}{n-k}^{-1}=(-1)^{d-n}\binom{n}{d}(1-1)^{n-d}=\delta_{n,d}\,.\qquad\square

8.3.3.
Step 3: Whipple’s theory

Rewriting (
8.3.1
) requires some deeper results about

functions which go back to Whipple
[
36
]
. Note that in the literature, many identities concerning
{}_{3}F_{2}

[

all those identities contain two
{}_{3}F_{2}
functions depending on (sums and differences) of these parameters and (usually) six Gamma functions which also depend on the parameters. Whipple used this observation and developed a “recipe” how to obtain a large number of transformations of this type. For convenience of the reader, we briefly introduce Whipple’s theory following
[
36
]
and
[
6
, Sec. 3.5-3.9]
as far as it is needed for proving Lemma
8.2
.

The notation

We want to identify
{}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a,b,c}{e,f};1\right]}
as a special case of
{}_{3}F_{2}
functions with other combinations, i.e. sums and differences, of the parameters
a,b,c,e,f
. For this define

(Fp)

\displaystyle\textit{Fp}(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_{xyz})\Gamma(\beta_{vu})\Gamma(\beta_{wu})}{}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha_{vwx},\alpha_{vwy},\alpha_{vwz}}{\beta_{vu},\beta_{wu}};1\right]}

(Fn)

\displaystyle\textit{Fn}(u;v,w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_{uvw})\Gamma(\beta_{uv})\Gamma(\beta_{uw})}{}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha_{uyz},\alpha_{uzx},\alpha_{uxy}}{\beta_{uv},\beta_{uw}};1\right]}

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\ell mn}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}+r_{\ell}+r_{m}+r_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{mn}:=1+r_{m}-r_{n}

for some parameters
r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4},r_{5}
such that
\sum_{k=0}^{5}r_{k}=0
. We always assume the indices to be pairwise distinct, i.e.
\{u,v,w,x,y,z\}=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}
.

At first glance, this might seem rather complicated. However, it is only important to note three things: first, the
\alpha
-coefficients are invariant under permutation of the indices whereas the
\beta
-coefficients are not. Second, we will not work with the
r
-coefficients, we just need to know that the
r
-coefficients can be chosen to depend on
a,b,c,e,f
only. In particular, in the following we choose

\displaystyle\alpha_{145}=a,\quad\alpha_{245}=b,\quad\alpha_{345}=c,\quad\beta_{40}=e,\quad\beta_{50}=f,\quad\alpha_{123}=e+f-a-b-c=s\,.

Third, in this case the
Fp
and
Fn
functions correspond to
{}_{3}F_{2}
functions depending on sums and differences of
a,b,c,e,f
and

\textit{Fp}(0;4,5)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)\Gamma(e)\Gamma(f)}{}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{a,b,c}{e,f};1\right]}\,.

Whipple
[
36
, Tab. I, IIA, IIB]
provides tables with all
\alpha
- and
\beta
-coefficients in terms of

and some examples of explicit

and

General relations

The first observation that we need is that, in fact, the parameter

The same is true for the
