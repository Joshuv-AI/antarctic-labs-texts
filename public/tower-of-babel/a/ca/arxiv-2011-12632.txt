Lower regularity assumption for an Euler-Lagrange equation on the contact line of the phase dependent Helfrich energy.
Sascha Eichmann

Abstract
We examine the phase dependent Helfrich energy and show an Euler-Lagrange equation on the phase seperation line. This result has already been observed by e.g. Jülicher-Lipowski and later Elliot-Stinner. Here we are able to lower the regularity assumption for this result down to C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} for the seperation line. In the proof we employ a carefully choosen test function utilising the signed distance function.



Keywords: Canham-Helfrich energy, Euler-Lagrange equation, phase dependency

Introduction

The Canham-Helfrich energy (or short Helfrich energy) is defined for a two dimensional smooth, orientable surface
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with mean curvature
H
by

W_{H_{0}}(S)=\int_{S}(H-H_{0})^{2}\,dA.

(1.1)

Here
H_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is called spontaneous curvature. This energy is used in e.g. modelling the shape of lipid bilayers, see e.g.
[
18
]
or red blood cells, see
[
6
]
. In more layman’s terms lipid bilayers form the boundary of depots in biological cells. These kind of depots are called vesicles. The thickness of these boundaries is usually small compared to the whole vesicle, hence modelling the shape of it by a two-dimensional surface is feasible (see figure
1
).

Figure 1:
Sketch of a vesicle with homogeneous lipid bilayer.

The parameter
H_{0}
represents an asymmetry of the lipid bilayer. I.e. it has been observed, that it has a prefered curvature, which is dependent on the material the bilayer consists of (see e.g.
[
27
]
and the references therein).

Since the bilayer itself does not consist of a homogeneous material, it may happen that the spontaneous curvature differs throughout the bilayer. Then it has been observed that two phases of the bilayer form, each having their own prefered spontaneous curvature. These different phases usually do not mingle, but rather form a sharp contact line (see e.g.
[
3
]
for some experimental results or
[
22
]
for some numerical simulations).
The Helfrich energy has been adapted to model this kind of behaviour in
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
.

Let us describe this model now (cf. figure
2
).

Figure 2:
Sketch of a surface with domain/phase seperation.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an oriented compact two dimensional surface with scalar mean curvature
H
. Furthermore we describe the two different lipid bilayers by a function
\phi:S\rightarrow\{0,1\}
, i.e.
x\in S
belongs to the bilayer of type
i\in\{0,1\}
, iff
\phi(x)=i
. We call this type phase or domain. The spontaneous curvature is now a function depending on the phase, i.e.
H_{0}:\{0,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Since the phases have been seperated, it is natural to assume, that their contact is minimal. Hence the length of the contact line should be minimised as well (see e.g.
[
23
]
and the references therein). For that let
J\subset S
be the jump set of
\phi
and let it be a one-dimensional curve (in the Hausdorff sense, see e.g.
[
13
, §2.1]
and
[
33
, §2]
for additional informations). Then the domain dependend Helfrich energy (or phase dependent Helfrich energy) is defined by

W_{H_{0},\sigma}(S,\phi)=\int_{S}(H(x)-H_{0}(\phi(x)))^{2}\,dA+\sigma\mathcal{H}^{1}(J).

(1.2)

We call the term
\sigma\mathcal{H}^{1}(J)
line tension. Here
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter describing the contribution of the length of
J
.

The aim of this article is to calculate an Euler-Lagrange equation on
J
. The theorem for
S
being in
C^{1}
is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
S\subset R^{3}
be an oriented surface with mean curvature
H
and phase seperation
\phi:S\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. We assume
S
to be a
C^{1}
surface.
Furthermore
S|_{\{\phi=i\}}
is supposed to be in
C^{4}
, such that the derivatives up to third order can be extended to the contact line
J
(i.e. the jump set of
\phi
) for
i=0,1
.
Now we additionally assume
(S,\phi)
to be critical for
W_{H_{0},\sigma}
, i.e. for any smooth vectorfield
V\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{R}^{3})
and their associated flow
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
we have

0=\frac{d}{dt}W_{H_{0},\sigma}(\Phi(S,t),\phi(\Phi(\cdot,t))).

Finally we assume
J
to be of class
C^{1,1}
. We call
H^{i}
the extension of the scalar mean curvature of
S|_{\{\phi=i\}}
to
J
. This extension satisfies

H^{0}(x)-H_{0}(0)=H^{1}(x)-H_{0}(1)

(1.3)

for all
x\in J
.

Unfortunately there are some experimental results in
[
3
]
, which indicate, that
S
is not necessarily
C^{1}
, but only
C^{0}
. In this case (
1.3
) becomes:

H^{i}(x)-H_{0}(i)=0\mbox{ for all }x\in J\mbox{ and }i=0,1.

(1.4)

A precise statements for
S

1.3
.

The equalities (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) have first been observed in

Unfortunately as seen in the experiments conducted in

Strategy of the proof

Since equation (

) is of local nature, we can assume without loss of generality
S
to be a graph of a function
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
a smooth domain. Let us further refine the phase seperation
\phi
in this case: Let
A_{i}\subset\Omega
be the maximal open set on which
\phi(\operatorname{graph}u|_{A_{i}})=i
. For the proof let
E
be the contact line in the parameter region
\Omega
, i.e.
E:=\Omega\setminus(A_{0}\cup A_{1})
(or the projection of
E
onto
\Omega
). Then
E
is a
C^{1,1}
curve by our assumptions on
J
in Theorem
1.1
.
In this case we call
A_{0},A_{1}
a phase seperation of
\Omega
with contact line
E
.
The phase dependend Helfrich energy for graphs is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&W_{H_{0},\sigma}(u,(A_{0},A_{1}))\\
:=&\int_{\Omega}(H_{u}(x)-H_{0}(\phi(u(x),x))^{2}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u(x)|^{2}}\,dx+\sigma\mathcal{H}^{1}(\operatorname{graph}u|_{E}).\end{split}

(1.5)

We reformulate Theorem
1.1
for graphs:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open bounded set. Furthermore let
A_{0},A_{1}\subset\Omega
be a phase seperation of
\Omega
with contact line

. Now let
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and

u|_{A_{i}}\in C^{4}(A_{i})\cap C^{3}(\overline{A_{i}})
be a critical point of

, i.e. for all
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
we have

0=\frac{d}{dt}W_{H_{0},\sigma}(u+t\varphi,(A_{0},A_{1}))|_{t=0}.

Furthermore we assume
E
to be of class
C^{1,1}
.

Then the derivatives of

up to the order of

can be extended continuously to

. We call this extension

and the corresponding extension of the mean curvature of

is

. Then for every

we have

H_{u_{A_{0}}}(x)-H_{0}(0)=H_{u_{A_{1}}}(x)-H_{0}(1).

The formulation of (
1.4
) as a Theorem for graphs is as follows:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be open and bounded. Furthermore let
A_{0},A_{1}\subset\Omega
be a phase seperation of
\Omega
with contact line

. Now let

be a critical point of

, i.e. for all

\displaystyle\varphi\in

\displaystyle\{\psi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}|\ \psi|_{A_{i}}\in C^{\infty}(A_{i})\mbox{ s.t. }D^{k}\psi

\displaystyle\mbox{ is uniformely continuous for }k=1,2,3\mbox{ and }\operatorname{supp}\psi\subset\Omega\}

we have

0=\frac{d}{dt}W_{H_{0},\sigma}(u+t\varphi,(A_{0},A_{1}))|_{t=0}.

Furthermore we assume

to be of class

.

Then the derivatives of

up to the order of

can be extended continuously to

. We call this extension

and the corresponding extension of the mean curvature of

2
A necessary condition for criticality

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
, we will show the following Euler-Lagrange equation on
E
(see e.g.
[
12
]
) for a function
\varphi\in C^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, which is zero on
E
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&0=\int_{E}\left(\langle\nabla\varphi,n_{E}\rangle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{(n_{E})_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)\\
&\cdot\left(H_{u_{A_{0}}}-H_{0}(0)-(H_{u_{A_{1}}}-H_{0}(1))\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.\end{split}

(2.1)

Here
n_{E}
is a normal of
E
. In case the
A_{i}
are only Caccioppoli it is the corresponding measure theoretic normal (see
[
13
, p. 169]
).
By Remark
1.4
we have
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus(A_{0}\cup A_{1}))=0
and
A_{0}\cap A_{1}=\emptyset
.
Hence

\displaystyle W_{H_{0},\sigma}(u,(A_{0},A_{1}))=

\displaystyle\sigma\mathcal{H}^{1}(\operatorname{graph}u|_{E})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{A_{i}}\left(H_{u}-H_{0}(i)\right)^{2}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\,dx

and therefore we only need to calculate the derivative of the bulk term on
A_{i}
.
First we assume
\varphi
to be smooth. Afterwards we get our result by approximation.
Let us start with the mean curvature of the variation (see e.g.
[
10
, Eq. (8)]
for a formula for the mean curvature of graphs):

\displaystyle H_{u+t\varphi}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{\partial_{i}(u+t\varphi)}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{j}(\partial_{j}u+t\partial_{j}\varphi)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\partial_{i}^{2}(u+t\varphi)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla(u+t\varphi)|^{2}}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{\partial_{i}(u+t\varphi)\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}u+t\partial_{j}\varphi)(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u+t\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi)}{\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}u+t\partial_{j}\varphi)^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H_{u+t\varphi}|_{t=0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\partial_{i}^{2}\varphi}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}-\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial_{i}^{2}u\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{j}u}{(1+|\nabla u|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial_{i}\varphi\partial_{j}u\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u+\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u+\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi}{(1+|\nabla u|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j,k}\frac{3\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u\partial_{k}\varphi\partial_{k}u}{(1+|\nabla u|^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}}.

Since we assume boundedness for
u
and the derivatives, the dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{A_{i}}|H_{u+t\varphi}-H_{0}(i)|^{2}\sqrt{1+|\nabla(u+t\varphi)|^{2}}\,dx|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{A_{i}}2(H_{u}-H_{0}(i))\frac{d}{dt}(H_{u}+t\varphi)|_{t=0}\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}

\displaystyle+|H_{u}-H_{0}(i)|^{2}\frac{\langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\,dx

Now using partial integration twice, we obtain as the bulk term the usual Helfrich equation, which is zero by only utilising test functions from
C^{\infty}_{0}(A_{i})
, and the rest of the boundary terms. Almost all of these boundary terms are zero, because
\varphi=0
on
E
. Only the ones remain, where originally the second derivatives of
\varphi
were present:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{A_{i}}|H_{u+t\varphi}-H_{0}(i)|^{2}\sqrt{1+|\nabla(u+t\varphi)|^{2}}\,dx|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{E}\left(\langle\nabla\varphi,n_{E}\rangle-\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u(n_{E})_{i}}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)(H_{u_{A_{i}}}-H_{0}(i))\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\lfloor E.

Since we have this term twice with opposite signs (we use the same normal
n_{E}
for both
A_{0}
and
A_{1}
in the partial integration), we obtain (
2.1
).

3
The signed distance function

In this section let
c:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an injective, regular curve with
c\in C^{1,1}
and being parametrised by arclength.
We will show, that there exists a neighbourhood of
c
, such that the signed distance function is in
C^{1}
.
We will follow the presentation of
[
15
]
. First we start by showing that the projection is well defined:

Lemma 3.1
.

For all
x\in c((a,b))
exists a
\delta>0
such that the Projection

P:B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)\rightarrow c((a,b))\cap B_{\delta}(x)

is well defined, i.e. for all
y\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)
the nearest point on the curve
c
is unique.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction and assume we find a sequence
y_{n}\in B_{\frac{1}{2n}}(x)
and
t_{n}^{1}<t_{n}^{2}\in(a,b)
, such that

\operatorname{dist}(y_{n},c((a,b))\cap B_{\frac{1}{n}}(x))=|c(t_{n}^{1})-y_{n}|=|c(t^{2}_{n})-y_{n}|

For
i=1,2
we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}0=&\partial_{t}|c(t)-y_{n}|^{2}|_{t=t_{n}^{i}}\\
=&2\langle\dot{c}(t_{n}^{i}),c(t_{n}^{i})\rangle-2\langle\dot{c}(t_{n}^{i}),y_{n}\rangle\end{split}

(3.1)

and therefore
\dot{c}(t_{n}^{i})
is orthogonal to
c(t_{n}^{i})-y_{n}
.
Let
\nu:(a,b)\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a choosen lipschitz continuous unit normal of
c
with Lipschitz constant
L>0
.
Then we find
\lambda_{n}^{i}
, satisfying

c(t_{n}^{i})-y_{n}=\lambda_{n}^{i}\nu(t_{n}^{i})

and therefore

\lambda_{n}^{i}=\pm\operatorname{dist}(y_{n},c((a,b))\cap B_{\frac{1}{n}}(x)).

If we choose
n
big enough, the curve
c
seperates
B_{\frac{1}{n}}(x)
into two connected regions (see Figure
3
), since
c\in C^{1,1}
.

Figure 3:
Sketch of nonuniqueness situation with normals.

Hence we can assume without loss of generality

\lambda_{n}^{i}=\operatorname{dist}(y_{n},c((a,b))\cap B_{\frac{1}{n}}(x)).

Hence
0<\lambda_{n}^{i}\leq\frac{1}{n}
, independent of
i
(i.e.
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{n}^{1}=\lambda_{n}^{2}
) and furthermore

c(t_{n}^{i})-y_{n}=\lambda_{n}\nu(t_{n}^{i}).

Subtracting these two equations yields

c(t_{n}^{1})-c(t_{n}^{2})=\lambda_{n}\nu(t_{n}^{1})-\lambda_{n}\nu(t_{n}^{2}).

By the mean value theorem we find
\xi_{n,1},\xi_{n,2}\in(t_{n}^{1},t_{n}^{2})
with

\left|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{c}^{1}(\xi_{n,1})\\
\dot{c}^{2}(\xi_{n,2})\end{array}\right)\right||t_{n}^{1}-t_{n}^{2}|=\lambda_{n}|\nu(t_{n}^{1})-\nu(t_{n}^{2})|\leq L\lambda_{n}|t_{n}^{1}-t_{n}^{2}|.

Since
c
is parametrised by arclength and
|t_{n}^{1}-t_{n}^{2}|\rightarrow 0
, we have for
n\rightarrow\infty

\left|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{c}^{1}(\xi_{n,1})\\
\dot{c}^{2}(\xi_{n,2})\end{array}\right)\right|\rightarrow 1.

Hence there is a constant
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq n_{0}
we have

\left|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{c}^{1}(\xi_{n,1})\\
\dot{c}^{2}(\xi_{n,2})\end{array}\right)\right|\geq 1-\varepsilon.

All in all we get

1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{n}L

for every
n\geq n_{0}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Sets on which the projection is locally unique are called sets with positive reach. This notion goes back to
[
14
]
and has been characterised very well recently in
[
32
]
(see also the references therein). In our case the arguments are a lot simpler, hence we provided them for the sake of completeness in Lemma
3.1
.

The characterisation in
[
32
]
is essentially a
C^{1,1}
regularity assumption for the set in question. In this sense our method is optimal, though we do not know, whether the regularity assumption of
E
in our theorems is optimal as well.

The next lemma shows, that the projection
P
is continuous. The proof given here is by Federer
[
14
, 4.8(4)]

Lemma 3.3

(see
[
14
]
)
.

Let
x\in c((a,b))
and
\delta>0
as in Lemma
3.1
. Then the projection
P
on the curve
c

P:B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)\rightarrow c((a,b))\cap B_{\delta}(x)

is continuous.

Proof.

Let us assume the opposite. Thereby we find a sequence
y_{i}\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)
with
y_{i}\rightarrow y\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)

and an
\varepsilon>0
such that

|P(y)-P(y_{i})|\geq\varepsilon

for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
P(y_{i})\in B_{\delta}(x)
the sequence
P(y_{i})
is bounded. After choosing a subsequence and relabeling, we get an
a\in\overline{B_{\delta}(x)}\cap c([a,b])
with
P(y_{i})\rightarrow a
.
Furthermore the distance function is Lipschitz and therefore we obtain

\frac{\delta}{2}>\operatorname{dist}(y,c((a,b)))=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{dist}(y_{i},c((a,b)))=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}|P(y_{i})-y_{i}|=|a-y|.

Hence
a\in B_{\delta}(x)
.
By Lemma
3.1
the projection is unique and we get

a=P(y).

This yields by the convergence of
P(y_{i})\rightarrow a

|P(y)-P(y_{i})|=|a-P(y_{i})|\rightarrow 0

and therefore the desired contradiction.
∎

Let us now define a signed distance function:

Definition 3.4
.

Let

and
\delta>0

-\operatorname{dist}(y,c((a,b))),&\mbox{ if }\lambda_{y}<0.\end{array}\right.

The sign of

Theorem 3.6

Let

. Furthermore the derivative satisfies

\nabla d_{\pm}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{y-P(y)}{|y-P(y)|},&\mbox{ if }\lambda_{y}>0\\
-\frac{y-P(y)}{|y-P(y)|},&\mbox{ if }\lambda_{y}<0\\
\nu(y),&\mbox{ if }y\in c((a,b)).\end{array}\right.

Here
\lambda_{y}
is defined as in Definition
3.4
.

Proof.

We show the theorem first for
y\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)\setminus c((a,b))
. Let
d:=(d_{\pm})^{2}
and
now we proceed by contradiction and assume there exists a
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\liminf_{t\searrow 0}\frac{d(y+tv)-d(y)}{t}<2\langle y-P(y),v\rangle

(3.2)

or

\limsup_{t\searrow 0}\frac{d(y+tv)-d(y)}{t}>2\langle y-P(y),v\rangle.

(3.3)

Let us work through case (
3.2
) first:
Hence we find an
\varepsilon>0
, a
t>0
arbitrarily small with

d(y+tv)<d(y)+2\langle y-P(y),tv\rangle-t\varepsilon

Hence

\displaystyle|y-P(y+tv)|^{2}=|y+tv-P(y+tv)-tv|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(y+tv)-2\langle y+tv-P(y+tv),tv\rangle+t^{2}|v|^{2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle d(y)+2\langle P(y+tv)-P(y),tv\rangle-t^{2}|v|^{2}-t\varepsilon

Since
P
is continuous we have
2\langle P(y+tv)-P(y),tv\rangle=o(|t|)
and hence this term can be absorbed for small
t
and we get

|y-P(y+tv)|^{2}<d(y)-t^{2}|v|^{2}-t\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<d(y)=|y-P(y)|^{2}

and therefore
P(y+tv)
has a strictly smaller distance to
y
than
P(y)
, which is a contradiction.

Now we assume (
3.3
): As in the preceeding case, there is an
\varepsilon>0
and
t>0
arbitrarily small, such that

d(y+tv)>d(y)+2\langle y-P(y),tv\rangle+t\varepsilon.

Hence

\displaystyle|y+tv-P(y)|^{2}=

\displaystyle|y-P(y)|^{2}+t^{2}|v|^{2}+2\langle y-P(y),tv\rangle

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle d(y+tv)-t\varepsilon+t^{2}|v|^{2}.

By choosing
t
small enough, we obtain

|y+tv-P(y)|^{2}<d(y+tv).

Therefore
P(y)
is a strictly better projection than
P(y+tv)
, i.e. a contradiction.
Hence for all
y\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)\setminus c((a,b))
we have

\nabla d(y)=2(y-P(y)).

Since
d_{\pm}(y)=\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_{y})\sqrt{d(y)}
we obtain by chain rule for these
y
:

\nabla d_{\pm}(y)=\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_{y})\frac{\nabla d(y)}{2\sqrt{d(y)}}=\lambda_{y}\frac{y-P(y)}{|y-P(y)|}.

Let us now examine
y\in c((a,b))
:
Let
y_{i}\in B_{\frac{\delta}{2}}(x)\setminus c((a,b))
with
y_{i}\rightarrow y
. Then for each
y_{i}
exists a neighbourhood, such that
\lambda_{(\cdot)}
does not change sign. Then by
|\lambda_{y_{i}}|=|y_{i}-P(y_{i})|
we get

\displaystyle\nabla d_{\pm}(y_{i})=

\displaystyle\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_{y_{i}})\frac{y_{i}-P(y_{i})}{|y_{i}-P(y_{i})|}=\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_{y_{i}})\frac{\lambda_{y_{i}}}{|y_{i}-P(y_{i})|}\nu(P(y_{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{sign}(\lambda_{y_{i}})^{2}\nu(P(y_{i}))\rightarrow\nu(y),

since
P
is continuous and
P(y)=y
. Hence
d_{\pm}
is continuously differentiable.
∎

4
Finishing the proofs

In the first part of this section we show Theorem
1.2
. After that we highlight the changes we need to make to obtain a proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
be arbitrary. Since it has compact support and
E
is relatively compact in
\Omega
(because
E
is a
C^{1,1}
curve) the intersection
\operatorname{spt}\varphi\cap E
is compact as well.
Using a partition of zero and Theorem
3.6
we find
\sigma_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{\delta_{i}}(x_{i}))
(
i=1,\ldots,N
) with
x_{i}\in\operatorname{spt}\varphi\cap E
,
\delta_{i}>0
, such that
the signed distance function
d_{\pm}
with respect to
E
and the choosen normal
n_{E}
satisfies
d_{\pm}\in C^{1}(B_{\delta_{i}}(x_{i}))
.
Furthermore
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}=1
.
Then we can define

\Phi:=\varphi\sum_{i}d_{\pm}\sigma_{i}\in C^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

(4.1)

where we implicitly continue
d_{\pm}
by zero to the whole of
\Omega
. Furthermore
\Phi
can be plugged into (
2.1
), because
d_{\pm}
is zero on
E
. We also obtain for any
y\in E
:

\nabla\Phi(y)=\varphi(y)n_{E}(y)\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}(y)=\varphi(y)n_{E}(y).

Hence (
2.1
) yields

0=\int_{E}\varphi\left(1-\sum_{ij}\frac{(n_{E})_{i}(n_{E})_{j}\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\right)(H_{u_{A_{0}}}-H_{0}(0)-(H_{u_{A_{1}}}-H_{0}(1))\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

Let us now analyse
1-\sum_{ij}\frac{(n_{E})_{i}(n_{E})_{j}\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}(n_{E})_{i}(n_{E})_{j}\partial_{i}u\partial_{j}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n_{E})_{1})^{2}(\partial_{1}u)^{2}+2(n_{E})_{1}(n_{E})_{2}\partial_{1}u\partial_{2}u+((n_{E})_{2})^{2}(\partial_{2}u)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((n_{E})_{1}\partial_{1}u+(n_{E})_{2}\partial_{2}u)^{2}=(\langle n_{E},\nabla u\rangle)^{2}\leq|n_{E}|^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}=|\nabla u|^{2}

and hence

1-\sum_{ij}\frac{(n_{E})_{i}(n_{E})_{j}\partial_{i}u\partial u_{j}}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}>0.

Since
\varphi
itself is arbitrary, the fundamental lemma of variational calculus yields

H_{u_{A_{0}}}-H_{0}(0)-(H_{u_{A_{1}}}-H_{0}(1))=0\quad\mathcal{H}^{1}\lfloor E\mbox{-a.e.}

Since the mean curvatures on
E
are continuous continuations, we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Adding
\lambda_{1}\operatorname{area}(S)+\lambda_{2}\operatorname{vol}(S)
to the phase dependend Helfrich energy (
1.2
) to account for additional conditions like prescribing the area and/or enclosed volume of
S
, does not change the result of Theorem
1.1
.
Since these terms do not produce terms with derivatives of the test function in the first variation, the proof of Theorem
1.1
remains valid.

Now we explain the changes to the proof to obtain Theorem
1.3

Proof.

Since
u\in C^{0}(\Omega)
and
C^{4}(A_{i})\cap C^{3}(\overline{A_{i}})
, we can choose from a greater set of testfunctions
\psi
. We employ a function, which is zero on
A_{1}
and
E
, i.e. we take
\psi\in C^{0}_{0}(\Omega)
with
\psi\in C^{1}(A_{i})
, such that the derivative can be extended to
E
.
Then the same calculations to obtain (
2.1
) yield

\int_{E}\left(\langle\nabla\psi,n_{E}\rangle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{(n_{E})_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{j}u_{0}}{1+|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}}\right)\left(H_{u_{A_{0}}}-H_{0}(0)\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=0.

(4.2)

Here
\nabla u_{0}
refers to the continuation of
\nabla u|_{A_{0}}
to
E
.
Instead of using the signed distance function to construct a suitable
\psi
, we employ the following function instead

d_{0}(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\operatorname{dist}(y,E),&y\in A_{0}\cup E\\
0,&\mbox{else.}\end{array}\right.

This
d_{0}
is continuous and the same techniques employed in Lemma
3.6
show
d_{0}
to be
C^{1}
close to
E
with continued derivative

The sign depends on whether
n_{E}
points inward or outward of
A_{0}
.
As in (
4.1
) we can now construct a suitable test function
\psi
with an arbitrary
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
. This we can plug in (
4.2
) and obtain

0=\int_{E}\varphi\left(\pm 1\mp\sum_{ij}\frac{(n_{E})_{i}(n_{E})_{j}\partial_{i}u_{0}\partial_{j}u_{0}}{1+|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}}\right)(H_{u_{A_{0}}}-H_{0}(0))\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

As in the proof of Theorem

the prefactor is never zero and then the fundamental lemma of variational calculus yields (
1.4
).
The same arguments can be employed to show the result for the continuation of

on

∎
