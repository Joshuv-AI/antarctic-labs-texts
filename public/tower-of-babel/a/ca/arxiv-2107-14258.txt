Imprint of Early Dark Energy in Stochastic Gravitational Wave Background
Chia-Feng Chang

Abstract
Early dark energy that relieves Hubble tension leaves a fingerprint in the primordial stochastic gravitational wave (GW) background that originates from cosmic string network. The signal is not only detectable with future planned GW experiments, but also distinguishable from other astrophysical and cosmological signals in the GW frequency spectrum. We find that the cosmic string GW spectrum can probe other new physics that influence the universe in post-Big-Bang-Nucleosynthesis with mid-band GW detection, which extends GW cosmic archaeology search region.

separation of a detected SGWB signal (if exist other GW signal that comparable to or stronger than cosmic string one), and precisely detect the cosmic string GW. For comparing signal difference and noise spectrum, we use Fluctuation-signal-to-Noise Ratio (FNR) as
(i={\rm BBO},{\rm DECIGO},{\rm LISA},{\rm SKA})

\displaystyle\hbox{FNR}=\sqrt{T_{i}\int^{f_{\rm max}}_{f_{\rm min}}df\left(\frac{\Delta\Omega_{\rm GW}(f)}{\Omega^{\rm eff}_{{\rm noise},i}(f)}\right)^{2}},

V
Conclusion

This paper proposed that SGWB originating from cosmic string network can be used to test the Hubble tension solution EDE. EDE behaves as dark energy in the early universe, then begins to dilute at the critical scaling factor
a_{c}\sim 10^{-4}
with total energy density fraction
f_{\rm EDE}\gtrsim 1\%
. It influences cosmic string SGWB by accelerating and decelerating universe expansion rate
\dot{a}(t)
due to a dark energy like equation of state
\omega_{\phi}=-1
and diluting faster than or equal to radiation-like component
\omega_{\phi}\geq 1/3
, respectively. The decelerated universe expansion rate locally increases cosmic string GW frequency spectra with magnitude
0.1\%
to
1\%
in the frequency range
10^{-5}
to
10\,
Hz which is within interferometer experiment such as BBO/DECIGO and LISA sensitivity. And on the other side, the acceleration reduces spectra with magnitude
\sim 5\%
in the lower frequency region
f\sim 10^{-9}
Hz, within the SKA search region. We also showed that the EDE influenced signals were stronger than the interferometer experiments and SKA (with
\gtrsim 10^{3}
monitoring pulsars) noise spectrum, and hence detectable. Such spectral shapes are distinguishable from other SGWB sourced by cosmological or astrophysical objects. Thus, GW detection provides a possibility to probe EDE.

The introduced analysis method and new mechanism provide a new window for probing new physics influences in post-BBN cosmic history with mid-band GW detectors. This novel technique can test early physics not only for Hubble tension solutions, but also other physics that hide in cosmic history; in the words, it extends GW cosmic archaeology to CMB period. It can also be complementary to current early universe observations such as CMB and BBN.

C.3
Pulsar Timing Array

Pulsar timing arrays are monitoring an array of pulsars for timing residuals with quadrupole correlation i.e. the Hellings-Downs curve. Increase regular observations of pulsar number can significantly enhance the pulsar timing precision
Janssen et al. 2015
, as we will discuss as follows. We follow the calculations that have been studied in
Anholm et al. 2009
, see also
Hazboun et al. 2019
;
Chamberlin et al. 2015
;
van Haasteren and Levin 2013
;
Lee et al. 2012
;
Siemens et al. 2013
. The overlap reduction function for a pulsar pair
I
and
J
reads

\displaystyle\Gamma_{IJ}(f)=\mathcal{R}_{\rm PTA}(f)\zeta_{IJ}(\psi),

(60)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\rm PTA}(f)=1/(12\pi^{2}f^{2})
is the averaged timing residual response function for a single pulsar
Hazboun et al. 2019
;
Chamberlin et al. 2015
, and
\zeta_{IJ}(\psi)
is the Hellings-Downs factor
Hellings and Downs 1983
with a separating angle
\psi
between two pulsars in the sky, it reads

\displaystyle\zeta_{IJ}(\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\delta_{IJ}+1+c_{\psi}\left(3\,\hbox{ln}c_{\psi}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right],

(61)

where
c_{\psi}\equiv(1-\cos\psi)/2
, and
\zeta_{II}=1
for a single pulsar. Due to unknown pulsar distribution in future experiment, we used the averaged (for angle
\psi
) value
\zeta_{\rm rms}=0.147

Schmitz 2021
in our numerical. Then we average the means for each set of measurements as

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}=\frac{\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\sum\limits_{J>I}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\lambda_{IJ}\,\mu_{IJ}}{\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\sum\limits_{J>I}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\lambda_{IJ}}\simeq\mu,

(62)

and the corresponding averaged variance

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\hat{\mu}}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\hat{\mu}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{\mu}\rangle^{2}=\frac{\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\sum\limits_{J>I}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\lambda_{IJ}^{2}\,\sigma_{IJ}^{2}}{\left(\sum\limits_{I=1}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\sum\limits_{J>I}^{N_{\rm SKA}}\lambda_{IJ}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{2}{N_{\rm SKA}(N_{\rm SKA}-1)}\sigma^{2}

(63)

where
\lambda_{IJ}
are weighted constant, and
N_{\rm SKA}
is a number of monitoring pulsars. The approximation is assuming that all the observation properties are the same, i.e. identical Hellings-Down factor,
\lambda_{IJ}
, and

\displaystyle\mu_{IJ}\equiv\mu,\;\;\;\;\hbox{and}\;\;\;\;\;\sigma_{IJ}\equiv\sigma

(64)

for each pulsar pair
IJ
. Since we are considering a strong stochastic GW background, the self-noise dominates (or approximately equals to) the noise contributions in our study in SKA frequency sensitivity region (so-called strong signal regime), i.e.

\displaystyle S_{\rm signal}(f)R_{\rm PTA}(f)\gg D_{\rm noise}(f).

(65)

In such a scenario, the variance Eq.(
C.2
) for a pulsar pair with pulsar
I
and
J
simplifies to

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{IJ}\simeq

\displaystyle\;\frac{t_{\rm obs}}{2}\int^{\infty}_{0}df\,\left[\Gamma_{I}(f)\Gamma_{J}(f)+\Gamma_{IJ}^{2}(f)\right]S^{2}_{\rm signal}(f)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\;\frac{t_{\rm obs}}{2}\int^{\infty}_{0}df\,\Gamma_{I}^{2}(f)S^{2}_{\rm signal}(f)

(66)

where we ignore the
D_{\rm noise}(f)
terms from Eq.(
C.2
) at first approximation, and the second approximation is due to
\bar{\zeta}_{\rm rms}^{2}\ll 1
. Following the steps in Eq.(
C.2
), we obtain the GW spectrum uncertainty for each frequency bin
f_{i}
for pulsar timing array experiment

\displaystyle\Delta\Omega_{\rm gw}(f_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}\frac{f_{i}^{3}}{\Gamma_{IJ}(f_{i})t_{\rm obs}\Delta f_{i}}\sigma(f_{i})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\rm SKA}(N_{\rm SKA}-1)}}\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}\frac{f_{i}^{5}}{\zeta_{\rm rms}\sqrt{t_{\rm obs}\Delta f_{i}}}

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{1}{\Delta f_{i}}\int_{F_{i}}df\,f^{-4}S_{\rm signal}^{2}(f)\right]^{1/2},

(67)

where we have used Eq.(
39
). The effective GW noise spectrum in the limit
\Delta f_{i}\to df
(see calculations around Eq.(
47
)) for a given GW spectrum
\Omega_{\rm gw}(f)
reads,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm noise}^{\rm eff}(f)\simeq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\zeta_{\rm rms}\sqrt{N_{\rm SKA}(N_{\rm SKA}-1)}}\Omega_{\rm gw}(f).

(68)

The FNR estimation is given in Eq.(
48
) with
n_{\rm obs}=2
for a pulsar pair. We also include both self noise and instrumental noise in our numerical.

C.3.1
SKA parameters

The Square Kilometre Array (SKA) is a planned next generation intergovernmental radio telescope

Janssen et al. 2015
;
Carilli and Rawlings 2004
;
Weltman et al. 2020
. It has expected to detect nano-Hertz frequency band with pulsar time array observation. We consider the operating time
t_{\rm SKA}=20\,
yrs, cadence
t_{c}=1\,
week
Janssen et al. 2015
;
Moore et al. 2015
, and a constant SKA instrumental noise spectrum
D_{\rm noise}^{\rm SKA}(f)\simeq 1.1\times 10^{-9}\,\hbox{Hz}^{-3}

Schmitz 2021
;
Thrane and Romano 2013
. The sensitive frequency range is between the operating period
f_{\rm min}=1/t_{\rm SKA}
and the cadence of the timing observation,
f_{\rm max}=1/t_{c}
. As discussed in the main letter, we chase a signal that is about
1\%
of the observation GW background, i.e.
\Delta\Omega_{\rm gw}^{\rm th}(f)/\Omega^{\rm st}_{\rm gw}(f)\sim 1\%
(see Eq.(
43
)). In order to observe such a small signal, we need

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\zeta_{\rm rms}\sqrt{N_{\rm SKA}(N_{\rm SKA}-1)}}\lesssim 1\%,

(69)

for ensuing a small self-noise spectrum from Eq.(
68
). This implies a large monitoring pulsar number,

\displaystyle N_{\rm SKA}\gtrsim\mathcal{O}(10^{3}).

(70)

Such a large
N_{\rm SKA}
is an optimistic estimation (maybe unrealistically optimistic). For comparison, Ref.
Moore et al. 2015
discussed that
N_{\rm SKA}\simeq 50
is a conservative estimation, and this value has been widely quoted in literature, see e.g.
Schmitz 2021
;
Siemens et al. 2013
. Moreover, Ref.
Combes 2021
mentioned that SKA will detect more pulsars in the sky, but it is unknown how many pulsars will be monitored for high-precision GW observation.

C.4
Auto-Correlated Detector

We analyze the statistical quantities in this subsection with targeting the auto-correlated detectors such as LISA. Follow Eq.(
23
) and Eq.(
25
) with considering a single detector, we have the mean
\mu
as

\displaystyle\mu\equiv\langle s^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}df\,\langle\tilde{s}(f)\tilde{s}^{*}(f)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{t_{\rm obs}}{2}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}df\,\mathcal{R}(f)S_{\rm signal}(f),

(71)

where
\tilde{s}(f)
is the GW signal as given in Eq.(
19
),
\mathcal{R}(f)
is the detector polarization- and sky-averaged response function, and the ensemble averaged signal production is

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{s}(f)\tilde{s}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\delta(f-f^{\prime})\mathcal{R}(f)S_{\rm signal}(f).

(72)

Its variance is (similar to Eq.(
C.2
)),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=

\displaystyle\,\langle(\tilde{S}(f)\tilde{S}^{*}(f)^{2}\rangle-\langle\tilde{S}(f)\tilde{S}^{*}(f)\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dfdf^{\prime}\,\Bigg[\langle\tilde{S}^{*}(f)\tilde{S}(f)\tilde{S}^{*}(f^{\prime})\tilde{S}(f^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle\tilde{S}^{*}(f)\tilde{S}(f)\rangle\langle\tilde{S}^{*}(f^{\prime})\tilde{S}(f^{\prime})\rangle\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{t_{\rm obs}}{4}\int^{+\infty}_{-\infty}df\Bigg[2R^{2}(f)S_{\rm signal}^{2}(f)

\displaystyle+2\mathcal{R}(f)S_{\rm signal}(f)D_{\rm noise}(f)+2D_{\rm noise}^{2}(f)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,t_{\rm obs}\int_{0}^{\infty}df\Bigg[\mathcal{R}^{2}(f)S_{\rm signal}^{2}(f)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{R}(f)S_{\rm signal}(f)D_{\rm noise}(f)+D_{\rm noise}^{2}(f)\Bigg]

(73)

where for the noise in Eq.(
19
), we have production

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{n}(f)\tilde{n}^{*}(f^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\delta(f-f^{\prime})D_{\rm noise}(f).

(74)

Follow the procedure as given on above Eq.(
48
), the corresponding uncertainty at frequency bin
\{f_{i}\}
for a given GW spectrum
\Omega_{\rm gw}(f)
is

\displaystyle\Delta\Omega_{\rm noise}^{\rm eff}(f_{i})\equiv\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}\frac{f_{i}^{3}}{\mathcal{R}(f_{i})\sqrt{t_{\rm obs}\Delta f_{i}}}\sqrt{\bar{A}_{\rm auto}(f_{i})}

(75)

with

\displaystyle A_{\rm auto}(f)\equiv

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{2}(f)S_{\rm signal}^{2}(f)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{R}(f)S_{\rm signal}(f)D_{\rm noise}(f)+D_{\rm noise}^{2}(f),

(76)

and

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\rm auto}(f_{i})\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta f_{i}}\int_{F_{i}}df\,A_{\rm auto}(f),

(77)

where
F_{i}=[f_{i}-\Delta f_{i}/2,f_{i}+\Delta f_{i}/2]
. Comparing the signal difference as in Eq.(
43
) with a limitation
\Delta f_{i}\to df
, we obtain the effective GW noise spectrum

\displaystyle\Omega^{\rm eff}_{\rm noise}(f)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}f^{3}\sqrt{\frac{A_{\rm auto}(f)}{\mathcal{R}^{2}(f)}},

(78)

and the fluctuation-to-noise ratio (FNR) for the detectability of the given GW spectrum difference
\Delta\Omega^{\rm th}_{\rm gw}(f)
in auto-correlated detection,

\displaystyle\hbox{FNR}\equiv\left[t_{\rm obs}\int df\,\left(\frac{\Delta\Omega_{\rm gw}^{\rm th}(f)}{\Omega^{\rm eff}_{\rm noise}(f)}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

(79)
