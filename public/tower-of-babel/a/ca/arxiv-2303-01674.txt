Image Coding via Perceptually Inspired Graph Learning
Samuel Fernández-Menduiña, Eduardo Pavez, and Antonio Ortega

Abstract
Most codec designs rely on the mean squared error (MSE) as a fidelity metric in rate-distortion optimization, which allows to choose the optimal parameters in the transform domain but may fail to reflect perceptual quality. Alternative distortion metrics, such as the structural similarity index (SSIM), can be computed only pixel-wise, so they cannot be used directly for transform-domain bit allocation. Recently, the irregularity-aware graph Fourier transform (IAGFT) emerged as a means to include pixel-wise perceptual information in the transform design. This paper extends this idea by also learning a graph (and corresponding transform) for sets of blocks that share similar perceptual characteristics and are observed to differ statistically, leading to different learned graphs. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method with both SSIM- and saliency-based criteria. We also propose a framework to derive separable transforms, including separable IAGFTs. An empirical evaluation based on the 5th CLIC dataset shows that our approach achieves improvements in terms of MS-SSIM with respect to existing methods.



keywords

Transform coding, perceptual image coding, image compression, graph learning, transform learning.

Introduction

Mean squared error (MSE) remains popular as a fidelity criterion for codec design because it simplifies transform-domain parameter optimization, even though it often fails to reflect perceptual quality
[
1
]
.
In recent years, research has focused on studying alternatives to include perceptually-driven metrics
[
2
,
3
]
in the coding pipeline. Some of these ideas have succeeded from a practical viewpoint, e.g., quantization tables that can better reflect the human visual system
[
4
,
5
]
. Nonetheless, in the theoretical front, including perceptual metrics as part of the rate-distortion optimization (RDO) used by most codecs remains an open problem.

In this sense, the main challenge is the widespread adoption of transform coding
[
6
]
for image and video compression. Designing these codecs becomes simpler when using the MSE: by virtue of Parseval’s identity, the distortion is the same in the pixel and the transform domain, allowing to perform RDO in the transform domain. On the other hand, many widely used perceptual metrics (e.g., SSIM and its variations) can only be computed in the pixel domain. Moreover, these metrics usually require a complete decoded image to be computed; therefore, codec optimization becomes an iterative process based on evaluations
a posteriori
(encode, decode, estimate the metric and repeat the process). For instance, Pergament
et al.
[
7
]
adjusted the quantization parameter (QP) of modern video compression systems, such as HEVC, to account for perceptual importance. Yet, this method cannot work at the pixel level and the mapping between perceptual importance and QP follows no optimality criterion. Recently, deep-learning methods
[
8
]
have succeeded in optimizing perceptual metrics like the SSIM,
but these models require millions of floating point operations per pixel and end-to-end training
[
9
]
.

To address these challenges, Lu
et al.
[
10
]
designed transforms based on an inner product that gives different weights to different pixels. The corresponding weighted MSE (WMSE) can be designed so that the weights capture the relative perceptual importance of each pixel, as estimated by metrics such as SSIM. This work relied on graph signal processing (GSP)
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
principles, and in particular on the interpretation of transforms such as the discrete cosine transform (DCT)
[
14
]
or the asymmetric discrete sine transform (ADST)
[
15
]
as graph Fourier transforms (GFT) of specific path
graphs.
In particular,
[
10
]
uses the irregularity-aware graph Fourier transform (IAGFT) to adapt the perceptual quality pixel-wise while using the DCT. The WMSE weights are chosen with the SSIM
[
2
]
as a distortion metric after assuming an additive noise model for the transform coefficients. Experimental results show improvements in the multi-scale SSIM
[
3
]
with respect to JPEG.

Despite its conceptual novelty, the method of
[
10
]
has limitations. First, the authors chose the
2
D-DCT graph, modifying the transform by changing the definition of inner product via the IAGFT. Yet, the optimal graph may differ from the
2
D-DCT graph. In this sense, previous works in a different context
[
16
]
suggest that accounting for the statistics of the image via graph learning
[
17
]
leads to compression gains. Moreover, the solution of Lu
et al.
[
10
]
focuses on non-separable transforms, which are too complex for practical implementations. Also, the empirical validation is limited to a database of four images.

In this paper, we improve significantly on the performance of
[
10
]
for perceptually inspired image coding by introducing a graph learning stage
[
17
,
18
]
to design the transforms. We use the fact that different perceptual regions of an image have different statistical properties
[
19
]
; thus, the graphs modeling the correlation between pixels with each of these regions are different. We propose to use a perceptual metric to classify the blocks of each image into several classes, so that different graphs can be learned within each class. To provide an empirical validation, we modify a JPEG encoder using these perceptually learned graphs for transform coding.

Since our method is based on the properties of different perceptual regions, we can use a family of fixed transforms chosen based on perceptual criteria. These transforms are available at both the encoder and the decoder so the side information is the index for a set of predefined transforms, as in
[
10
]
. As perceptual criteria, we consider both the SSIM and saliency
[
20
]
. Although some papers have already combined graph learning and compression
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
, to the best of our knowledge, none of them include perceptual criteria in the estimation process.

In a practical setup, low-complexity solutions are often preferred. Thus, we also show that perceptually inspired graph learning can be constrained to obtain separable transforms. To do so, we estimate a covariance model based on combinatorial graph Laplacians using bi-convex optimization
[
18
]
, leading to a novel, learned separable IAGFT. Finally, we assess the performance of both the separable and the non-separable methods in a set of
30
images from the
5
th CLIC dataset
[
24
]
.
As distortion metric, we choose the MS-SSIM since 1) it aligns better with the human visual system
[
3
]
and 2) the method in
[
10
]
obtains the best results with this quantity. Our algorithm using non-separable transforms requires
6
% less bit rate on average than the method proposed in
[
10
]
, while the method using separable transforms reduces the bit rate more than
5
%. Introducing structural constraints reduces the performance of the system. However, for separable transforms, we obtain computational gains: they are
30
% faster than their non-separable counterparts.

Preliminaries

Notation.
Uppercase bold letters, such as
\bf A
, denote
matrices. Lowercase bold letters, such as
\bf a
, denote vectors.
The
n
th component of the vector
\bf a
is
a_{n}
, and the
(i,j)
th component of the matrix
\bf A
is
a_{ij}
. We denote the vector of all-ones by
\bf 1
. Regular letters denote
scalar values.

2.1
Graph Signal Processing

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mbox{$\bf W$})
denote a weighted undirected graph, where
\mathcal{V}
is the vertex set,
\mathcal{E}
is the edge set, and
\bf W
denotes the weighted adjacency matrix. The entries of the matrix
\bf W
satisfy
w_{ij}>0
; that is, every edge
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
has positive weights. We define the combinatorial graph Laplacian (CGL) as
\mbox{$\bf L$}=\mbox{$\bf D$}-\mbox{$\bf W$}
, where
\bf D
is the degree matrix such that
d_{ii}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\,w_{ij}
. We use the CGL to define the graph Fourier transform (GFT). Let
\mbox{$\bf L$}=\mbox{$\bf U$}\mbox{$\bf\Lambda$}\mbox{$\bf U$}^{\top}
be the eigendecomposition of the CGL, where
\bf\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues and
\bf U
is an orthonormal matrix with the corresponding unit norm eigenvectors. These eigenvectors are the basis for the GFT.

In a recent work, Girault
et al.
[
25
]
proposed the irregularity-aware graph Fourier transform (IAGFT). Under this framework, the Fourier modes are defined not only in terms of the signal variation operator but also introducing a positive definite matrix
\bf Q
that leads to the
\bf Q
-inner product,
\langle\mbox{$\bf x$},\mbox{$\bf y$}\rangle_{\tiny\mbox{$\bf Q$}}=\mbox{$\bf x$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf Q$}\mbox{$\bf y$}
. This inner product induces a new definition of orthogonality:
\bf x
and
\bf y
are orthogonal if and only if
\mbox{$\bf x$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf Q$}\mbox{$\bf y$}=\mbox{$\bf 0$}
. In our work, the matrix
\bf Q
is diagonal; therefore, the energy of the signal is given by
||\mbox{$\bf x$}||^{2}_{\tiny\mbox{$\bf Q$}}=\langle\mbox{$\bf x$},\mbox{$\bf x$}\rangle_{\tiny\mbox{$\bf Q$}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\,q_{i}x_{i}^{2}
, where
\mbox{$\bf Q$}=\text{diag}\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\right)
. The
(\mbox{$\bf L$},\mbox{$\bf Q$})
-GFT basis vectors are the columns of
\mbox{$\bf U$}=\left[\mbox{$\bf u$}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{$\bf u$}_{n}\right]
, which solve the generalized eigenvalue problem

\mbox{$\bf L$}\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\,\mbox{$\bf Q$}\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i},\,\text{where }\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}.

(1)

Note that the eigenvectors are
\bf Q
-orthonormal:
\mbox{$\bf U$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf Q$}\mbox{$\bf U$}=\mbox{$\bf I$}
. We let
\mbox{$\bf F$}=\mbox{$\bf U$}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf Q$}
be the forward transform and
\mbox{$\bf F$}^{-1}=\mbox{$\bf U$}
be its inverse.

2.2
Weighted MSE Optimization via IAGFT

The IAGFT modifies the definition of graph signal energy, weighting the contribution of each node. In
[
10
]
, the authors rely on this property to adapt the perceptual quality pixel-wise. Mathematically, let
\bf z
be the reference signal and
\bf x
be its distorted version. Also, let their IAGFTs be
\hat{\mbox{$\bf z$}}
and
\hat{\mbox{$\bf x$}}
, respectively. Then, we can define the WMSE as

\text{WMSE}(\mbox{$\bf z$},\mbox{$\bf x$},\mbox{$\bf q$})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}(z_{i}-x_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{z}_{i}-\hat{x}_{i})^{2},

where the last equality follows from the Generalized Parseval’s Theorem
[
25
]
. Let us assume a uniform quantizer in the transform domain. With the GFT, the variance of the error in the pixel domain is uniform and equal to
\Delta^{2}/12
, where
\Delta
is the quantization step. Nonetheless, the
\bf Q
-orthogonality of the IAGFT introduces a weighting factor, modifying the variance to
\Delta^{2}/(12q_{i})
. From this model, the authors of
[
10
]
derive the matrix
\bf Q
that optimizes the SSIM
[
2
]
:

q_{i}=\frac{(n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i})\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{\gamma_{i}}}-\gamma_{i},

(2)

where
\gamma_{i}=\Delta^{2}/(12(2\sigma^{2}_{x_{i}}+c_{2}))
,
\sigma^{2}_{x_{i}}
is the local variance, and
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{diag}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
.

2.3
Graph learning

In this work, we consider both separable and non-separable transforms. Regarding non-separable transforms, we follow the formulation in
[
26
]
: to estimate the combinatorial graph Laplacian, we minimize the objective function

J(\mbox{$\bf L$})=-\log\det(\mbox{$\bf L$}+\mbox{$\bf 1$}\mbox{$\bf 1$}^{\top}/n)+\text{tr}(\mbox{$\bf L$}\mbox{$\bf S$}),

(3)

Given a graph topology
\mathcal{E}
, we only consider matrices from the set of CGLs:

\mbox{$\bf L$}=\{\mbox{$\bf L$}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{+}:\mbox{$\bf L$}\mbox{$\bf 1$}=\mbox{$\bf 0$},\left[\mbox{$\bf L$}\right]_{i,j}\leq 0\text{ for }i\not=j\},

(4)

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{+}
is the set of semi-positive definite matrices of size
n\times n
. To solve the optimization problem, we follow the block-coordinate descent method proposed in
[
26
, Algorithm 2]
.

In a separable transform, for a given
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
image block
\mbox{$\bf X$}_{b}
, the transform coefficients are given by
\hat{\mbox{$\bf X$}}_{b}=\mbox{$\bf U$}_{c}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf X$}_{b}\mbox{$\bf U$}_{r}

[
18
]
. Different design options are possible for finding
\mbox{$\bf U$}_{c}
and
\mbox{$\bf U$}_{r}
. In this work, we rely on the bi-convex formulation by Pavez
et al.
[
18
]
: we assume that
\mbox{$\bf U$}_{r}
and
\mbox{$\bf U$}_{c}
are the eigenvector matrices of two generalized Laplacians,
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}
and
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}
, respectively. Both matrices are
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
. Then, by minimizing

-\log\det(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}\otimes\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c})+\text{tr}((\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}\otimes\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c})\mbox{$\bf S$}),

(5)

we can obtain
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}
and
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}
. In essence, we follow an iterative approach fixing either
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}
or
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}
and optimizing the expression in terms of the other matrix. We refer the reader to
[
18
]
for details. In Sec.
3.3
, we will modify Eq. (
5
) to obtain CGLs.

3
Perceptual graph learning

Perceptual importance is linked to the statistical properties of an image
[
19
]
. For instance, the human visual system is less sensitive to noise in textured regions, which contain mainly high-frequency information. In such regions, pixels are barely correlated to their neighbors. From a GSP perspective, correlation between pixels can be modeled using a graph. Therefore, regions with different levels of perceptual importance should be modeled using different graphs.

In this section, we leverage this property to propose transforms for image compression. To do so, first we show in Sec.
3.1
how to segment an image to obtain different perceptual regions. Then, in Sec.
3.2
, we discuss the graph-learning process, while in Sec.
3.3
, we present our separable IAGFT.

3.1
Perceptual image segmentation

We start from a set of
T
training images,
\{\mbox{$\bf X$}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
. Although size may differ, for simplicity, we assume all of them are
M\times M
arrays. We let the vectorized version of these images be
\mbox{$\bf x$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{2}}
for
t=1,\ldots,T
. For each of these vectors, we compute the perceptual weights
\mbox{$\bf q$}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{M^{2}}
using, for instance, Eq. (
2
). The computation of these weights involves the whole image, but most codecs compute transforms block-wise. Therefore, we need to identify the perceptual weights associated with each block. Assume we have
N_{b}
blocks in each image. Then, we associate to the
k
th (vectorized) block of each training image
\mbox{$\bf x$}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the corresponding weights
\mbox{$\bf q$}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from
\mbox{$\bf q$}_{t}
, for
k=1,\ldots,N_{b}
and
t=1,\ldots,T
.

In a practical setup, coding the perceptual weights of the image to compress requires an excessive overhead. In
[
10
]
, the authors train a vector quantizer via an entropy-constrained vector quantizer (ECVQ)
[
27
]
, although simpler algorithms like
k
-means perform correctly. We denote as
N_{c}
the number of codewords after training, and let
\{\mbox{$\bf q$}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{c}}
be these codewords. Typically, codewords represent different levels of perceptual importance.

Now, we leverage this information to segment the training images perceptually. For each
\mbox{$\bf q$}_{t,k}
in the training set, we find the closest codeword in our dictionary,
\tilde{\mbox{$\bf q$}}_{t,k}\in\{\mbox{$\bf q$}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{c}}
. By doing so, we are assigning a perceptually-inspired label to each block of the training set
\mbox{$\bf x$}_{t,k}
. That is, we are able to segment the training images perceptually by linking each block with one of the
N_{c}
codewords. In the following, we denote as
i_{t,k}
the class assigned to each block.

3.2
Graph learning stage

Once we have segmented the image perceptually, we can perform graph learning on each category. The first step is to compute the covariance matrix. Define the scalar
m_{t,k}
as the average intensity of the block
\mbox{$\bf x$}_{t,k}
. We approximate the expectation of each block by
\mbox{$\bf m$}_{t,k}=m_{t,k}\mbox{$\bf 1$}
.

Including the perceptual information we obtained in the previous section, we can write the covariance estimator for the
i
th class as

\mbox{$\bf S$}_{i}=\frac{1}{K_{i}-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}I(i_{t,k}=i)(\mbox{$\bf x$}_{t,k}-\mbox{$\bf m$}_{t,k})(\mbox{$\bf x$}_{t,k}-\mbox{$\bf m$}_{t,k})^{\top},

where
K_{i}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{b}}I(i_{t,k}=i)
is the number of blocks in the training set for each class and
I(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

By using the methods we described in Sec.
2.3
, we can compute
N_{c}
graph Laplacians,
\{\mbox{$\bf L$}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{c}}
. Given these Laplacians, we obtain the basis of the
(\mbox{$\bf L$}_{i},\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{i})
-GFT by setting
\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{i}=\text{diag}(\mbox{$\bf q$}^{(i)})
. By making these bases available for both the encoder and the decoder, we can tailor the transforms to each perceptual region.

3.3
Separable IAGFT design

For image compression, transforms with a DC component usually perform better. However, the separable learning method proposed on Eq. (
5
) cannot handle CGLs. To circumvent this issue, we modify the learning problem as follows. Let
\mbox{$\bf M$}=(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}+\mbox{$\bf J$})\otimes(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}+\mbox{$\bf J$})
. Then, we can minimize

-\log\det(\mbox{$\bf M$})+\text{tr}(\mbox{$\bf M$}\mbox{$\bf S$}),

(6)

where
\mbox{$\bf J$}=\mbox{$\bf 1$}\mbox{$\bf 1$}^{\top}/\sqrt{n}
, for all
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}
and
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}
in the set of CGL. This expression has a probabilistic interpetation in terms of Gauss-Markov random processes
[
26
]
. The problem is still bi-convex; therefore, we can solve it using conventional optimization routines. In particular, it can be shown that, keeping
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}
fixed, Eq. (
6
) simplifies to

-\log\det(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{tr}((\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}+\mbox{$\bf J$})\mbox{$\bf x$}_{i})\text{tr}(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}\mbox{$\bf x$}_{i}^{\top}),

(7)

that is, a weighted version of the optimization problem for non-separable graphs, which is easier to optimize than Eq. (
6
). The equation keeping
\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}
fixed is analogous.

Although the formulation above is sufficient for the conventional GFT, extending the same idea to the IAGFT is not straightforward. One option is applying the transforms row- and column-wise using the appropriate perceptual weights. This approach requires a different transform for each column and each row. As an alternative, if the perceptual matrix has structure, we can use only one transform for all columns and one transform for all rows.

Let
\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}}
be the weight matrix in block form, where
\mbox{$\bf q$}_{b}=\text{vec}(\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{b})
. Assume
\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{b}=\mbox{$\bf q$}_{r}\mbox{$\bf q$}_{c}^{\top}
and
\mbox{$\bf M$}=\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r}\otimes\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c}
. Let us define
\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{c}=\text{diag}(\mbox{$\bf q$}_{c})
and
\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{r}=\text{diag}(\mbox{$\bf q$}_{r})
. Then, the eigenvector matrix
\bf U
satisfying

\mbox{$\bf M$}\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i}=\lambda\,\text{diag}(\mbox{$\bf q$}_{b})\mbox{$\bf u$}_{i},\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n,

(8)

decomposes as
\mbox{$\bf U$}=\mbox{$\bf U$}_{r}\otimes\mbox{$\bf U$}_{c}
, where
\mbox{$\bf U$}_{r}
is the basis for the
(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{r},\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{r})
-GFT and
\mbox{$\bf U$}_{c}
is the basis for the
(\mbox{$\bf M$}_{c},\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{c})
-GFT. Therefore, we can use our conventional definition of separable transform,
\hat{\mbox{$\bf X$}}=\mbox{$\bf U$}_{c}^{\top}\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{c}\mbox{$\bf X$}\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{r}\mbox{$\bf U$}_{r}
.

In practice, we have to approximate the matrix
\mbox{$\bf Q$}_{b}
by a rank
1
matrix. We can do this by relying on the Eckart–Young theorem
[
28
]
. To assess both options, in Sec.
4.2
we will evaluate each of them.

4
Empirical Evaluation

We used images from the
5
th Challenge on Learned Image Compression dataset
[
24
]
. Since the database has no training set, we trained the vector quantizer and estimated the graphs with the thirty images in the validation set. For each of the thirty images in the testing set, we compute the perceptual weights, quantize them and identify the class of each block. Our encoder is depicted in Fig.
1
.

All images are cropped to
1024\times 1024
pixels and converted to gray-scale before compression. We use two codewords in the vector quantizer
[
27
]
since the method in
[
10
]
reaches its best performance with this configuration.

4.1
Computational Complexity

We consider only the complexity of the codec since the training step is performed only once. First, we need to compute and quantize the perceptual matrix
\bf Q
. This operation adds a constant overhead. Now, we distinguish between separable and non-separable transforms. For non-separable transforms, the complexity of the method is dominated by a matrix-vector product of size
n
. Therefore, we need
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
products. For separable transforms, we need two matrix-matrix products of size
\sqrt{n}
; therefore the complexity is
\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2})
.

4.3
Saliency-inspired transforms

Conclusion

In this paper, we considered the problem of perceptual graph learning for image compression. Our idea is based on the fact that regions with different perceptual importance often differ statistically, so the graphs modeling the relationships between pixels on each of these regions are also different. Then, we can segment an image using perceptual criteria, like the SSIM, and apply graph learning methods on each region separately. By relying on this property, we can design perceptual transforms that adapt to the statistics of particular images. We also consider separable transforms and different perceptual criteria, such as saliency. When integrated into a JPEG codec, experiments in a large database show that our method requires
