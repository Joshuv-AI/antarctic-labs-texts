Orthogonal systems for time-dependent spectral methods
Arieh Iserles

Abstract
This paper is concerned with orthonormal systems in real intervals, given with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions. More specifically, our interest is in systems with a skew-symmetric differentiation matrix (this excludes orthonormal polynomials). We consider a simple construction of such systems and pursue its ramifications. In general, given any C 1 ​ ( a , b ) \mathrm{C}^{1}(a,b) weight function such that w ⁡ ( a ) = w ⁡ ( b ) = 0 w(a)=w(b)=0 , we can generate an orthonormal system with a skew-symmetric differentiation matrix. Except for the case a = − ∞ a=-\infty , b = + ∞ b=+\infty , only a limited number of powers of that matrix is bounded and we establish a connection between properties of the weight function and boundedness. In particular, we examine in detail two weight functions: the Laguerre weight function x α ​ e − x x^{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-x} for x > 0 x>0 and α > 0 \alpha>0 and the ultraspherical weight function ( 1 − x 2 ) α (1-x^{2})^{\alpha} , x ∈ ( − 1 , 1 ) x\in(-1,1) , α > 0 \alpha>0 , and establish their properties. Both weights share a most welcome feature of separability, which allows for fast computation. The quality of approximation is highly sensitive to the choice of α \alpha and we discuss how to choose optimally this parameter, depending on the number of zero boundary conditions.

Introduction and motivation

This work is motivated by spectral methods for time-dependent partial differential equations (PDEs) of the form

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}u+f(x,u),\qquad t\geq 0,\quad x\in\Omega,

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a well-posed linear operator and
\Omega\subseteq\mbox{\Bbb R}^{d}
, given with an initial condition for
u(x,0)
and appropriate boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
. Standard examples are

with

(the

\mathcal{L}={\mathrm{i}}\Delta

(the
linear Schrödinger
equation) or

(the
nonlinear Schrödinger
equation with standard cubic nonlinearity).

In this paper we are concerned by spectral methods applied in tandem with a splitting approach. As an example, we commence by approximating locally the solution of (
1.1

u^{n+1}=\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathcal{L}}\mathrm{e}^{\Delta tf}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\mathcal{L}}u^{n},\qquad n\geq 0,

(1.2)

where
u^{n}(x)
is an approximation to

. Here
\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{L}}v
is a shorthand for a numerical solution at

\partial u/\partial t=\mathcal{L}u
,

, while
\mathrm{e}^{tf}v
denotes a numerical solution of the ordinary differential equation (ODE)

,

. The splitting (

[

,

]

Another benefit of (

) and of similar splittings is that it is consistent with conservation of the

energy. Many

norm of the solution. This often represents a highly significant physical feature and it is vital to respect it under discretisation. (Note that conservation of

[

]

norm). It is more challenging to ensure that

for every

is

In this paper we are concerned with spectral methods for time-dependent problems
[

,
hesthaven07std
,
trefethen00smm
]

\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
, where each

is defined in

inner product,

\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{m}(x)\varphi_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{m,n},\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

and complete in
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\Omega)

where the

In a practical method we truncate the expansion and the range of

, thereby obtaining a finite-dimensional linear system of ODEs.

Substantive advantage of spectral methods is that expansions in orthonormal bases typically converge very rapidly indeed: for example, orthogonal polynomials converge in a finite interval to analytic (in the interval and its neighbourhood) functions at an exponential rate. Therefore, the number of degrees of freedom, compared to the more usual finite difference or finite element methods, is substantially small. While this is not the entire truth – finite differences and finite elements produce sparse linear algebraic systems while spectral elements yield dense matrices which sometimes can be also ill conditioned and , moreover, expanding a function in an orthonormal basis can be potentially costly – spectral methods are often the approach of choice in numerical computations. In the specific context of time-dependent problems, however, naive spectral methods are unstable
[
hesthaven07std
]
. This motivates us to consider the major concept of a
differentiation matrix.

In the sequel we restrict our narrative to the univariate case,
\Omega\subseteq\mbox{\Bbb R}

Secondly, it lays the foundations to a multivariate case, whether by tensorial extension to parallelepipeds or by more advanced means which we intend to explore in a future paper.

The

The Cauchy case

W-functions

2.1
The definition and few of its consequences

Let
(a,b)
be a non-empty real interval,
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
, and
s\in\mbox{\Bbb N}\cup\{\infty\}
. We denote by
{\mathrm{H}^{\hskip-6.0pt\raisebox{4.0pt}{\footnotesize$\circ$}}_{2}}^{\hskip-4.0pt\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}[a,b]
the Sobolev space of
\mathrm{H}_{2}^{s}[a,b]

such that

f^{(k)}(a)=f^{(k)}(b)=0,\qquad k=0,\ldots,s-1,

(note that
\mathrm{C}^{s-1}[a,b]\subset\mathrm{H}_{2}^{s}[a,b]
, therefore the derivatives are well defined) equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{s}=\sum_{k=0}^{s}\int_{a}^{b}f^{(k)}(x)g^{(k)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

A
weight function

w\in\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)\cap\mathrm{C}^{1}(a,b)
is a positive function with all its moments

\mu_{k}=\int_{a}^{b}x^{k}w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad k\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

bounded. Given a weight functions, we can define (e.g. using a Gram–Schmidt process) a set of orthonormal polynomials
\mbox{\curly P}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
such that

\int_{a}^{b}p_{m}(x)p_{n}(x)w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{m,n},\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}.

(2.1)

Such a set is unique once we require, for example, that the coefficient of
x^{n}
in
p_{n}
is always positive. We say that
\varphi_{n}
is the
n
th
W-function
once

\varphi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{w(x)}p_{n}(x),\qquad n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+},

and let
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
. It follows at once from (
2.1
) that
\Phi
is an orthonormal set with respect to the standard
\mathrm{L}_{2}
inner product.

Remark 1
The functions
\varphi_{n}
inherit some features of orthonormal polynomials, in particular they obey the same three-term recurrence relation. However, the expansion coefficients of an arbitrary function are different:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}^{P}p_{n},\quad\hat{f}_{n}^{P}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)p_{n}(x)w(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad f\in\mathrm{L}_{2}((a,b),w\,\mathrm{d}x),

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}_{n}^{\Phi}\varphi_{n},\quad\hat{f}_{n}^{\Phi}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)p_{n}(x)\sqrt{w(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad f\in\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b).

Moreover, while convergence theory of orthogonal polynomials is well understood, at any rate in compact intervals, the condition for convergence of the
\hat{f}_{n}^{\Phi}
s are currently a matter for further research.

Remark 2
An important difference between

and
\Phi

\pm 1

We let

stand for the infinite-dimensional

(2.2)

We say that

is of

Theorem 2

The differentiation matrix

Proof

Separable systems

In this section we consider two families of weight functions that share a hugely beneficial feature of

We say that a weight function

is

if there exist real sequences
\mbox{\gothic a}=\{\mbox{\gothic a}_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
and
\mbox{\gothic b}=\{\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
such that

\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}=\left\{\hskip-9.0pt\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n},&m\geq n+1,\\[4.0pt]

-\mbox{\gothic a}_{n}\mbox{\gothic b}_{m},&m\leq n-1,\end{array}\right.\hskip-9.0pt\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}

(3.1)

and it is
symmetrically separable
subject to the existence of real sequences
\mbox{\gothic a}=\{\mbox{\gothic a}_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
and
\mbox{\gothic b}=\{\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}

\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}=\left\{\hskip-9.0pt\begin{array}[]{ll}-\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}&m+n\mbox{\ odd,}\;m\geq n+1,\\[4.0pt]

\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n},&m+n\mbox{\ odd,}\;m\leq n-1,\end{array}\right.\hskip-9.0pt\qquad m,n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}

(3.2)

It will be demonstrated in Section 4 that separability or symmetric separability allow for very rapid computation of products of the form
\mbox{\curly D}_{N}\mbox{\boldmath$v$\unboldmath}
for
\mbox{\boldmath$v$\unboldmath}\in\mbox{\Bbb R}^{N+1}
.

In this section we consider two families of measures, one separable and the other symmetrically separable: the

In both Laguerre and ultraspherical cases we are able to present comprehensive analysis, deriving the sequences

The matrix

\searrow

Computational aspects

4.1
A product of
D
by a vector

Consider a separable weight function, e.g. a Laguerre weight. A major task in practical implementation of the ideas of this paper to spectral methods is to form a product of the form
\mbox{\boldmath$h$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\curly D}\,\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}
, where
f
is a (real or complex) infinite-dimensional vector. In most applications
f
is likely to be the vector of expansion coefficients of a function
f
in the basis
\Phi
, which is likely to decay rapidly. For example, if
f
is analytic in an ellipse enclosing
(a,b)
, we expect the
|f_{k}|
s to decay at an exponential rate.

Forming
h
and, with greater generality, products of the form
\mbox{\curly D}^{\,r}\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}
for
r\in\mbox{\Bbb N}
, is important for the obvious reason that approximating partial differential equations is likely to entail forming derivatives. Perhaps less trivial reason is the formation of matrix functions. Thus, let
g
be a (typically analytic) function and we wish to form
g(\mbox{\curly D})\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}
: standard examples are the exponential
g(z)=\mathrm{e}^{z}
, the cosine
g(z)=\cos z
and the sinc function
g(z)=z^{-1}\sin z
. Two of the most popular methods are the Krylov subspace algorithm and a quadrature of an integral representation of
g

[
higham08fom
]
, and both require the formation of products of the kind considered in this subsection.

Tridiagonal differentiation matrices, of the form considered in
[
iserles19oss
]
, seemingly enjoy strong advantage in this context, because the formation of
\mbox{\curly D}_{M}\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}_{\!M}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}\in\mbox{\Bbb R}^{M+1}
, entails just
\approx 3M
floating-point operations (flops).
7
7

7

Products of the form
\mbox{\smallcurly{D}}_{M}^{\,r}\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle f$\unboldmath}_{\!M}
can thus be formed consecutively in
\approx 3rM
flops.
We demonstrate that this ‘fast product’ property is shared by differentiation matrices formed by separable or symmetrically separable weights. Actually, the structure of these matrices allows for infinite-dimensional computation: the starting point is an integer
N
, typically much larger than
M
, such that
|f_{m}|
is negligible (in practical terms, smaller than a user-provided error tolerance) for
m>N
, and we wish to form

h_{m}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\mbox{\curly D}_{m,n}f_{n},\qquad m=0,\ldots,M.

(4.1)

We commence by assuming that a weight is separable, whereby (
3.1
) yields

h_{m}=-\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}f_{n}+\mbox{\gothic b}_{m}\!\!\sum_{n=m+1}^{N}\mbox{\gothic a}_{n}f_{n}=\sigma_{m}+\rho_{m},\qquad m=0,\ldots,M,

where

\sigma_{m}=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}f_{n},\quad\rho_{m}=\sum_{n=m+1}^{N}\mbox{\gothic a}_{n}f_{n},\qquad m=0,\ldots,M.

Then

\displaystyle h_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\gothic b}_{0}\rho_{0},

\displaystyle h_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\sigma_{m}+\mbox{\gothic b}_{m}\rho_{m},\qquad m=1,\ldots,M,

\displaystyle\mbox{where}\quad\sigma_{m}=\sigma_{m-1}+\mbox{\gothic b}_{m-1}f_{m-1},\quad\rho_{m}=\rho_{m-1}-\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}f_{m}.

Assuming that the
\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}
s and
\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}
s have been precomputed (and this need be done only once, no matter how many products are required), the calculation (
4.1
) takes just
\approx N+4M
flops – and by the same token, computing the first
M+1
entries of
\mbox{\curly D}_{N}^{\,r}\!\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}_{\!N}
takes
\approx r(N+4M)
flops.

Similar operations count applies to symmetrically separable weight, whereby the entires of
D
obey (
3.2
). Assuming that both
M
and
N
are even, we have

\displaystyle h_{2m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N/2}\mbox{\curly D}_{2m,2n+1}f_{2n+1},

\displaystyle h_{2m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N/2}\mbox{\curly D}_{2m+1,2n}f_{2n},\qquad m=0,\ldots,\frac{M}{2}.

Therefore

\displaystyle h_{2m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\gothic a}_{2m}\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{2n+1}f_{2n+1}-\mbox{\gothic b}_{2m}\sum_{n=m+1}^{N/2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{2n+1}f_{2n+1},

\displaystyle h_{2m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\gothic a}_{2m+1}\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{2n}f_{2n}-\mbox{\gothic b}_{2m+1}\sum_{n=m+1}^{N/2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{2n}f_{2n},\qquad m=0,\ldots,\frac{M}{2}.

Set

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}^{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{2n}f_{2n},\qquad

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}^{\mathrm{O}}=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{2n+1}f_{2n+1},

\displaystyle\rho_{m}^{\mathrm{E}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{n=m+1}^{N/2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{2n}f_{2n},\qquad

\displaystyle\rho_{m}^{\mathrm{O}}=\sum_{n=m+1}^{N/2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{2n+1}f_{2n+1},

hence

h_{2m}=\mbox{\gothic a}_{2m}\sigma_{m}^{\mathrm{O}}-\mbox{\gothic b}_{2m}\rho_{m}^{\mathrm{O}},\qquad h_{2m+1}=\mbox{\gothic a}_{2m+1}\sigma_{m}^{\mathrm{E}}-\mbox{\gothic b}_{2m+1}\rho_{m}^{\mathrm{E}},\qquad m=0,\ldots,\frac{M}{2}.

However,

\sigma_{0}^{\mathrm{E}}=\sigma_{0}^{\mathrm{O}}=0,\qquad\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{E}}=\sum_{m=1}^{N/2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{2n}f_{2n},\qquad\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{O}}=\sum_{m=1}^{N/2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{2n+1}f_{2n+1},

\sigma_{m}^{\mathrm{O}}=\sigma_{m-1}^{\mathrm{O}}+\mbox{\gothic b}_{2m-1}f_{2m-1}
,

\rho_{m}^{\mathrm{E}}=\rho_{m-1}^{\mathrm{E}}-\mbox{\gothic a}_{2m}f_{2m},

\displaystyle\rho_{m}^{\mathrm{O}}=\rho_{m-1}^{\mathrm{O}}-\mbox{\gothic a}_{2m+1}f_{2m+1},\qquad m=1,\ldots,\frac{M}{2}.

Thus, again, we need just

4.2
Speed of convergence

While the convergence of orthogonal polynomials to ‘nice’ (in particular, analytic) functions is well understood, this is not the case for W-functions. Comprehensive analysis of their convergence and its speed is a matter for future work, yet it is of an interest to present preliminary computational results, not least as a preamble for a discussion on the choice of the optimal value of
\alpha
in the context of either Laguerre or ultraspherical weights, while comparing them to standard approximation by the underlying orthogonal polynomials.

It rapidly becomes apparent that we have a competition between different imperatives:

•

The number of zero boundary conditions:
This determines the value of
\alpha
and, according to Theorems 7 and 8, we need
\alpha>s-1
in
{\mathrm{H}^{\hskip-6.0pt\raisebox{4.0pt}{\footnotesize$\circ$}}_{2}}^{\hskip-4.0pt\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\scriptstyle s$}}(a,b)
.

•

Regularity of approximating functions:
While
P
consists of polynomials, hence analytic functions, this is not the case with
\Phi
, whether in the context of ultraspherical or Laguerre weights: it all depends on the value of
\alpha
. If
\alpha
is an even integer then the
\varphi_{n}
s are analytic, otherwise analyticity fails at the endpoints.

•

The underlying function space:
Much depends on how the error is measured. Among the many possibilities, we single out two: the
{\mathrm{H}^{\hskip-6.0pt\raisebox{4.0pt}{\footnotesize$\circ$}}_{2}}^{\hskip-4.0pt\raisebox{-3.0pt}{$\scriptstyle p$}}(a,b)
norm for a suitable value of

(in particular, the
\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)
norm) and the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}[a,b]
(and, more generally,
\mathrm{H}_{\infty}^{p}[a,b]
) norm. The choice of a norm depends on the underlying application.

As things stand, we cannot report any ‘hard’ results. Yet, even preliminary numerical experimentation reveals a remarkable state of affairs.

4.3
Computational and theoretical challenges

This is the first paper to consider W-functions in an organised way, although of course Hermite functions have been used and investigated extensively and W-functions associated with Freud weights (and which are special because of Theorem 2) have been introduced in
[
luong23apw
]
. Needless to say, this work neither resolves all the mathematical and computational issues associated with W-functions nor claims to do so. While there are important theoretical questions, e.g. to characterise all separable or symmetrically separable weight functions, perhaps the most urgent issues are related to the applications of W-functions to spectral methods. This concerns issues in approximation theory (speed of convergence in different function classes), as well as purely computational questions. The speed of approximation points out to an imperfect duality between W-functions and the functions
\Psi=\{\psi_{n}\}_{n\in\mbox{\sBbb Z}_{+}}
from Section 1. Recalling the
\hat{f}_{n}^{\Phi}
, the
n
th expansion coefficient in
P
and letting
\check{w}(x)=w(x)\chi_{(a,b)}(x)
, the Plancherel theorem yields at once for every
n\in\mbox{\Bbb Z}_{+}

\hat{f}_{n}^{\Phi}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\phi_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{\check{w}(x)}f(x)p_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=(-{\mathrm{i}})^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(\xi)\overline{\psi_{n}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x,

and we recover an expansion in
\Psi
of the Fourier transform of
f
. This duality, though, is imperfect because, unless
(a,b)=\mbox{\Bbb R}
, it is valid (insofar as
\Psi
is concerned) only in the Paley–Wiener space
\mathcal{P}_{(a,b)}(\mbox{\Bbb R})
rather than in
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mbox{\Bbb R})

[
iserles19oss
]
. Moreover, comprehensive convergence theory for functions of the form
\Psi
is also lacking. Yet, even an imperfect duality might potentially lead to useful observations.

The final issue we wish to mention is fast computation. For example, while Subsection 4.1 provides a viable approach toward the computation of
g(\mbox{\curly D}_{N})\mbox{\boldmath$v$\unboldmath}
using Krylov subspaces, ideally it would have been useful to have other fast methods, in particular for
g(z)=\mathrm{e}^{z}
. Another critical issue is rapid expansion in a W-function basis, similarly perhaps to fast expansion algorithms in polynomial bases
[
olver20fau
]
. All this is a matter for future research.
