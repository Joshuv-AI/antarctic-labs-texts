Norm Orthogonal Bases and Invariants of
p

Abstract




Abstract

In 2018, the Longest Vector Problem (LVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP) in
p
-adic lattices were introduced. These problems are closely linked to the orthogonalization process. In this paper, we first prove that every
p
-adic lattice has an orthogonal basis and give definition to the successive maxima and the escape distance, as the
p
-adic analogues of the successive minima and the covering radius in Euclidean lattices. Then, we present deterministic polynomial time algorithms designed for the orthogonalization process, addressing both the LVP and the CVP with the help of an orthogonal basis of the whole vector space. Finally, we conclude that orthogonalization and the CVP are polynomially equivalent.

2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 11F85.

Key words and phrases: Orthogonalization,
p
-adic lattice, successive maxima, CVP, LVP.

1
Introduction

Lattices were investigated since the late
18
th century. There are two famous computational problems in Euclidean lattices: the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP). Many researches have been done to study these problems. Van Emde Boas
[
7
]
proved that the SVP is NP-hard in the
l_{\infty}
norm and the CVP is NP-hard in the
l_{2}
norm by reducing them to the weak partition problem. Ajtai
[
8
]
proved that the SVP is NP-hard under randomized reductions in the
l_{2}
norm. These hard problems are useful in constructing cryptographic primitives. The GGH scheme
[
9
]
, the NTRU scheme
[
10
]
and the LWE scheme
[
11
]
are based on these problems.

Lattices can also be defined in local fields such as
p
-adic fields, see
[
6
]
. Lattices of local fields are demonstrated to exhibit a similar structural form to those in Euclidean spaces. Motivated by lattice-based cryptosystems, one of the most promising post quantum cryptosystems, Deng et al.
[
1
,
2
]
introduced two new computational problems in
p
-adic lattices of local fields, the Longest Vector Problem (LVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), which are the
p
-adic analogues of the Shortest Vector Problem and the Closest Vector Problem in lattices of Euclidean spaces. They considered these new problems to be challenging and potentially applicable for constructing public-key cryptosystems.

In order to construct a public-key cryptosystem based on
p
-adic lattices, Deng et al.
[
3
]
proved that the LVP can be solved efficiently with the help of an orthogonal basis of the
p
-adic lattice. Moreover, if this orthogonal basis can be extended to an orthogonal basis of the whole vector space, then the CVP can be solved efficiently. Hence the orthogonal bases can be used to construct trapdoor information for cryptographic schemes. Then, Deng et al.
[
3
]
constructed the first signature scheme and public-key encryption cryptosystem based on
p
-adic lattices. They achieved this by introducing a trapdoor function with an orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice.

Therefore, the LVP and the CVP are closely linked to the orthogonalization process. Weil
[
6
]
proved that every finite-dimensional vector space over a local field has an orthogonal basis. However, his proof is not constructive, and he did not give an algorithm to find out an orthogonal basis. Deng
[
4
]
turned Weil’s proof into an algorithm and proved that every
p
-adic lattice of rank two has an orthogonal basis. He asked whether this is true for lattices with rank greater than or equal to three, which was left as an open problem.

This paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we recall some definitions and properties. We give an affirmative answer to the above question in Section
3
. Subsequently, we prove that the sorted norm sequence of orthogonal bases of a lattice is unique in Section
4
. We also give the definitions to the successive maxima and the escape distance in Section
4
. In section
5
we proved that any two orthogonal bases of a lattice can be obtained from each other by some operations. Then, we present a deterministic polynomial time algorithm to find orthogonal bases of
p
-adic lattices with the help of an orthogonal basis of the whole vector space in Section
6
. Next, we introduce new deterministic polynomial time algorithms to solve the CVP and the LVP with the help of orthogonal bases in Section
7
and Section
8
. The new algorithms only need an orthogonal basis of the whole vector space. Finally, we conclude that the orthogonalization process and the CVP are polynomially equivalent.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Norm and Orthogonal Basis

Let
p
be a prime. Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. A norm
N
on
V
is a function

N:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

such that:

1.

N(v)\geq 0
for any
v\in V
, and
N(v)=0
if and only if
v=0
;

2.

N(xv)=\left|x\right|_{p}\cdot N(v)
for any
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
v\in V
;

3.

N(v+w)\leq\max{\left\{N(v),N(w)\right\}}
for any
v,w\in V
.

Here,
\left|x\right|_{p}
is the
p
-adic absolute value for a
p
-adic number
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

If
N
is a norm on
V
, and if
N(v)\neq N(w)
for
v,w\in V
, then we must have
N(v+w)=\max{\{N(v),N(w)\}}
. Weil (
[
6
]
page 26) proved the following proposition:

Proposition 2.1

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of finite dimension
n>0
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Then there is a decomposition
V=V_{1}+\cdots+V_{n}
of
V
into a direct sum of subspaces
V_{i}
of dimension
1
, such that

N\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{v_{i}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{N(v_{i})}

for any
v_{i}\in V_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

Weil proved the above proposition for finite-dimensional vector spaces over a
p
-field (commutative or not). For simplicity, we only consider the case
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Thus, we can define the orthogonal basis.

Definition 2.2

(
N
-orthogonal basis
[
6
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of finite dimension
n>0
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. We call
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
an
N
-orthogonal basis of
V
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
if
V
can be decomposed into the direct sum of
n

1
-dimensional subspaces
V_{i}
’s
(1\leq i\leq n)
, such that

N\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{v_{i}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{N\left(v_{i}\right)}

for any
v_{i}\in V_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, where
V_{i}
is spanned by
\alpha_{i}
.
Two subspaces
U
,
W
of
V
is said to be
N
-orthogonal if the sum
U+W
is a direct sum and it holds that
N\left(u+w\right)=\max\left\{N(u),N(w)\right\}
for all
u\in U
,
w\in W
.

2.2
p
-adic Lattice

We first recall the definition of a
p
-adic lattice.

Definition 2.3

(
p
-adic lattice
[
1
,
2
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of finite dimension
n>0
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}

(1\leq m\leq n)
be
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linearly independent vectors of
V
. A
p
-adic lattice in
V
is the set

\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}):=\left\{\sum^{m}_{i=1}{a_{i}\alpha_{i}}:a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p},1\leq i\leq m\right\}

of all
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-linear combinations of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}
. The sequence of vectors
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}
is called a basis of the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
. The integers
m
and
n
are called the rank and dimension of the lattice, respectively. When
n=m
, we say that the lattice is of full rank.

p
-adic lattices are compact subsets of
V
. The following proposition can be found in
[
5
]
page 72, prop.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset V
be a compact subset.

(a)

For every
a\in V\setminus\Omega
, the set of norms
\{N(x-a):x\in\Omega\}
is finite.

(b)

For every
a\in\Omega
, the set of norms
\{N(x-a):x\in\Omega\setminus\{a\}\}
is discrete in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

We can also define the orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice.

Definition 2.5

(
N
-orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice
[
4
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of finite dimension
n>0
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. If
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space spanned by a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\mathbb{Z}_{p}\alpha_{i}}
, then we call
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}
an
N
-orthogonal basis of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
.

2.3
LVP and CVP

Deng et al.
[
1
,
2
]
introduced two new computational problems in
p
-adic lattices. They are the Longest Vector Problem (LVP) and the Closest Vector Problem (CVP). We first review them briefly.

Definition 2.6

(
[
1
,
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
be a lattice in
V
. We define recursively a sequence of positive real numbers:
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\dots
as follows.

\lambda_{1}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}{N(\alpha_{i})},

\lambda_{j+1}=\max{\left\{N(v):v\in\mathcal{L},N(v)<\lambda_{j}\right\}}\mbox{ for }j\geq 1.

We have
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}>\dots
and
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{j}=0
. The Longest Vector Problem is defined as follows:

Definition 2.7

(
[
1
,
2
]
)
.

Given a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
in
V
, the Longest Vector Problem is to find a lattice vector
v\in\mathcal{L}
such that
N(v)=\lambda_{2}
.

The Closest Vector Problem is defined as follows:

Definition 2.8

(
[
1
,
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
be a lattice in
V
and let
t\in V
be a target vector. The Closest Vector Problem is to find a lattice vector
v\in\mathcal{L}
such that
N(t-v)=\min_{w\in\mathcal{L}}{N(t-w)}
.

Thanks to Proposition
2.4
, these definitions are established. Deng et al.
[
2
]
provided deterministic exponential time algorithms to solve the LVP and the CVP. Additionally, Deng et al.
[
3
]
presented deterministic polynomial time algorithms for solving the LVP and the CVP specifically with the help of orthogonal bases.

3
Orthogonalization of
p
-adic Lattices

In this section, we prove that every
p
-adic lattice has an
N
-orthogonal basis. Firstly, we need some lemmas. Lemma
3.1
, Corollary
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
can be found in
[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
v,w\in V
. Then we have
N(v+w)=\max\{N(v),N(w)\}
if and only if
N(v+w)\geq N(v)
.

Corollary 3.2

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}

(n>1)
be
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linearly independent vectors of
V
. Set
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})
. Then we have:

N(\alpha_{1}+w)=\max\{N(\alpha_{1}),N(w)\}\mbox{ for all }w\in\mathcal{L}

if and only if

N(\alpha_{1})=\min\{N(\alpha_{1}+w):w\in\mathcal{L}\}.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of finite dimension
n>1
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linearly independent vectors of
V
. Then
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
V
if and only if it holds that

N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}N(a_{i}\alpha_{i}),

where one of the
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is
1
and the others are in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

The next lemma shows that we can find an
N
-orthogonal basis of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
by solving a CVP-instance if
\mathcal{L}
has some special structure.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}

(n>1)
be
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linearly independent vectors of
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
. Assume that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1})
and
N(\alpha_{n})\leq N(\alpha_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Let
w_{0}\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1})
be such that

N(\alpha_{n}+w_{0})=\min\{N(\alpha_{n}+w):w\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1})\}.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}_{n}=\alpha_{n}+w_{0}
. Then
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Since
w_{0}\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1})
and
\alpha^{\prime}_{n}=\alpha_{n}+w_{0}
, we have

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{n}).

Next, we will prove that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

By Lemma
3.3
, we only need to consider vectors
\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}+a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
such that one of the
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is
1
and the others are in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. If
a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, then
\left|a_{n}\right|_{p}=1
, hence it is invertible. By Corollary
3.2
, we have

\begin{split}N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}+a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n}\right)&=N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{n}}\alpha_{i}+\alpha^{\prime}_{n}\right)\\
&=\max\left\{N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}\frac{a_{i}}{a_{n}}\alpha_{i}\right),N(\alpha^{\prime}_{n})\right\}\\
&=\max\left\{N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}\right),N(a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n})\right\}\\
&=\max\{N\left(a_{1}\alpha_{1}),\dots,N(a_{n-1}\alpha_{n-1}\right),N(a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n}))\}.\end{split}

On the other hand, if
a_{n}\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, then
\left|a_{n}\right|_{p}<1
and there is a
1
among
a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}
. We may assume that
a_{1}=1
. Since

N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}N(a_{i}\alpha_{i})\geq N(\alpha_{1})>N(a_{n}\alpha_{n})\geq N(a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n}),

we have

\begin{split}N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}+a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n}\right)&=N\left(\sum^{n-1}_{i=1}a_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)\\
&=\max\{N(a_{1}\alpha_{1}),\dots,N(a_{n-1}\alpha_{n-1}),N(a_{n}\alpha^{\prime}_{n})\}.\end{split}

Hence
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Now we can prove the main result of this section. This result gives an affirmative answer to the question in
[
4
]
, asking whether lattices with rank greater than or equal to three has orthogonal bases.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-linearly independent vectors of
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
. Then there is a deterministic algorithm to find out an
N
-orthogonal basis of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
if we can compute efficiently the norm
N(v)
of any vector
v\in V
.

Proof.

Define
CVP(t,\mathcal{L})=w_{0}
such that

N(t-w_{0})=\min\{N(t-w):w\in\mathcal{L}\}.

The algorithm for performing the orthogonalization process is presented as follows:

Algorithm
(orthogonalization).

Input:
a basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
.

Output:
an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

1.

for
i=1
to
n
do:

2.

rearrange
\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{n}
such that
N(\alpha_{i})=\max_{i\leq k\leq n}{N(\alpha_{k})}
,

3.

for
j=i+1
to
n
do:

4.

\alpha_{j}\leftarrow\alpha_{j}-CVP(\alpha_{j},\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i})).

Return
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.

This algorithm calls the CVP algorithm
O(n^{2})
times. We may assume that the order of
\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{n}
never changes in the step
2
. This can be done by giving the input vectors a proper order initially. For example, let the input be
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
. Suppose that
N(\alpha_{2})=\max{\{N(\alpha_{1}),N(\alpha_{2}),N(\alpha_{3})\}}
,
CVP(\alpha_{1},\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{2}))=w_{1}
,
CVP(\alpha_{3},\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{2}))=w_{3}
and
N(\alpha_{3}-w_{3})\geq N(\alpha_{1}-w_{1})
, then a proper order is
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{1}
.

Moreover, based on this assumption, the vector
\alpha_{i}
will be fixed after the
(i-1)
th iteration of the outer loop (where the
0
th iteration of the outer loop means the beginning of the algorithm). In order to distinguish from the input vectors, let us denote this vector as
\beta_{i}
, then the input lattice of the CVP algorithm in the step
4
will be
\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{i})
and the output will be
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
.

First, we prove that
N(\beta_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\beta_{n})
. For any
1\leq i\leq n-1
, suppose that we have obtained
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{i},\alpha^{\prime}_{i+1},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
after the
(i-1)
th iteration of the outer loop. According to the above assumptions, we have
N(\beta_{i})\geq N(\alpha^{\prime}_{i+1})
. Since

\beta_{i+1}=\alpha^{\prime}_{i+1}-CVP(\alpha^{\prime}_{i+1},\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{i})),

we have
N(\beta_{i})\geq N(\alpha^{\prime}_{i+1})\geq N(\beta_{i+1})
. Hence
N(\beta_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\beta_{n})
.

Next, let us prove by induction that
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{i}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i})

(1\leq i\leq n)
. When
i=1
, according to our assumptions, we have
\beta_{1}=\alpha_{1}
. It is clear that
\beta_{1}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1})=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1})
. Suppose that the conclusion holds for
i=k
, then
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})
by induction hypothesis. Suppose that we have obtained
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k},\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{k+1},\dots,\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{n}
after the
(k-1)
th iteration of the outer loop. Since

N(\beta_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\beta_{k})\geq N(\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{k+1})

and

\beta_{k+1}=\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{k+1}-CVP(\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{k+1},\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})),

by Lemma
3.4
, we conclude that
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k+1}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of

\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k},\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{k+1})=\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k},\alpha_{k+1})=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1}),

where the first equation holds because
\alpha^{\prime\prime}_{k+1}=\alpha_{k+1}-w
for some
w\in\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})
. Hence
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.
∎

Here is a toy example for illustrating the orthogonalization process in Theorem
3.5
.

Example 3.6
.

Let
V=\mathbb{Q}_{2}(\zeta)
where
\zeta
is a primitive
5
th root of unity. Let
N
be the unique absolute value extended by the
2
-adic absolute value of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
(see
[

]
). Since

N(a_{1}+a_{2}\zeta+a_{3}\zeta^{2}+a_{4}\zeta^{3})=\max\{N(a_{1}),N(a_{2}\zeta),N(a_{3}\zeta^{2}),N(a_{4}\zeta^{3})\},

where one of the
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
is
1
and the others are in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. We conclude that
1,\zeta,\zeta^{2},\zeta^{3}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
V
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
by Lemma
3.3
.

Let

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(1,1+2\zeta,2+8\zeta+16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}).

Since
N(1)=N(1+2\zeta)=1
and
N(2+8\zeta+16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3})=\frac{1}{2}
, by solving the CVP-instances with the lattice
\mathcal{L}(1)
and the target vectors

and

, we obtain closest vectors
1
and

, respectively. Notice that closest vector is not unique in general. Here the lattice vector
3
in the lattice
\mathcal{L}(1)
is also a closest vector of the target vector

.

Next, since
N(2\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}
and

, by solving the CVP-instance with the lattice
\mathcal{L}(1,2\zeta)
and the target vector

, we obtain a closest vector
8\zeta
. Hence
1,2\zeta,16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

4
Successive Maxima

In the realm of Euclidean lattices, the notion of successive minima is an important concept, which denotes the lengths of the shortest yet linearly independent vectors. We aim to explore the
p
-adic analogue of this concept. However, a straightforward substitution of “minima” with “maxima” results in a trivial definition, which does not capture the essence of the concept in the
p
-adic context. Consider a left vector space
V
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
of rank
n
in
V
such that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
and
N(\alpha_{1})>\cdots>N(\alpha_{n})
. Then the longest vector can be chosen as
\alpha_{1}
.

However, we can choose
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{n}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and all possess a common length of
N(\alpha_{1})
. In order to avoid this trivial situation, we may require the vectors to be
N
-orthogonal rather than just linearly independent. This introduces the problem of determining the uniqueness of the sorted norm sequence of
N
-orthogonal bases of a lattice.

In this section, we firstly establish the uniqueness of the sorted norm sequence for
N
-orthogonal bases of a lattice. Subsequently, we provide definitions for the successive maxima and escape distance. Finally, we prove several properties associated with them.

4.1
Proof of the Uniqueness

Let us begin with a simple observation.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice of rank
2
in
V
. Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
and
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
are two
N
-orthogonal bases of
\mathcal{L}
such that
N(\alpha_{1})\geq N(\alpha_{2})
and
N(\beta_{1})\geq N(\beta_{2})
. Then we have
N(\alpha_{1})=N(\beta_{1})
and
N(\alpha_{2})=N(\beta_{2})
.

Proof.

If
N(\alpha_{1})\neq N(\beta_{1})
, then we may assume that
N(\alpha_{1})<N(\beta_{1})
. Write

\beta_{1}=a_{11}\alpha_{1}+a_{12}\alpha_{2},

where
a_{11},a_{12}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Since
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
, we have

N(\beta_{1})=\max{\{N(a_{11}\alpha_{1}),N(a_{12}\alpha_{2})\}}\leq N(\alpha_{1}).

This is a contradiction. Hence
N(\alpha_{1})=N(\beta_{1})
.

If
N(\alpha_{2})\neq N(\beta_{2})
, then we may assume that
N(\alpha_{2})<N(\beta_{2})
. Now we have

N(\alpha_{1})=N(\beta_{1})\geq N(\beta_{2})>N(\alpha_{2}).

Write

\beta_{1}=a_{11}\alpha_{1}+a_{12}\alpha_{2},

\beta_{2}=a_{21}\alpha_{1}+a_{22}\alpha_{2},

where
a_{11},a_{12},a_{21},a_{22}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Since
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
and
N(\beta_{1})=N(\alpha_{1})>N(\alpha_{2})
, we have
a_{11}\not\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Therefore,

N(\beta_{2}-a_{12}a^{-1}_{11}\beta_{1})=N\big((a_{22}-a_{12}a^{-1}_{11})\alpha_{2}\big)\leq N(\alpha_{2}).

On the other hand, since
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
, we have

N(\beta_{2}-a_{12}a^{-1}_{11}\beta_{1})=\max{\left\{N(\beta_{2}),N(a_{12}a^{-1}_{11}\beta_{1})\right\}}\geq N(\beta_{2}).

This is a contradiction. Hence
N(\alpha_{2})=N(\beta_{2})
.
∎

Now we prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
. Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
are two
N
-orthogonal bases of
\mathcal{L}
such that
N(\alpha_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\alpha_{n})
and
N(\beta_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\beta_{n})
. Then we have
N(\alpha_{i})=N(\beta_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

If
N(\alpha_{1})<N(\beta_{1})
, then we can write

\beta_{1}=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{a_{1j}\alpha_{j}},

where
a_{1j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for
1\leq j\leq n
. Since
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
, we have

N(\beta_{1})=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}{N(a_{1j}\alpha_{j})}\leq N(\alpha_{1}).

This is a contradiction.

If
N(\alpha_{j})=N(\beta_{j})
for
1\leq j\leq m<n
and
N(\alpha_{m+1})<N(\beta_{m+1})
, then we can write

\beta_{i}=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{a_{ij}\alpha_{j}},

where
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for
1\leq i\leq m+1
and
1\leq j\leq n
. Suppose that for
i=s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k}\leq m
, we have
N(\alpha_{i})>N(\alpha_{i+1})
and
N(\alpha_{i})=N(\alpha_{i+1})
for the remaining
1\leq i\leq m
. Set
s_{0}=0
, then for
1\leq t\leq k
, we can conclude that
a_{ij}\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
if
s_{t-1}+1\leq i\leq s_{t}
and
1\leq j\leq s_{t-1}
. Otherwise, there exists an
a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\not\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
whose indices satisfy the above condition. Hence,

\begin{split}N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=N(\beta_{i^{\prime}})&=N\left(\sum^{n}_{j=1}{a_{i^{\prime}j}\alpha_{j}}\right)\\
&=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}{N(a_{i^{\prime}j}\alpha_{j})}\geq N(a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\alpha_{j^{\prime}})=N(\alpha_{j^{\prime}})>N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}).\end{split}

This is a contradiction.

Let
u_{i}
be the
(s_{t}-s_{t-1})
-tuple
(\overline{a_{i(s_{t-1}+1)}},\overline{a_{i(s_{t-1}+2)}},\dots,\overline{a_{is_{t}}})
for
s_{t-1}+1\leq i\leq s_{t}
,
1\leq t\leq k
, where
\overline{a_{ij}}
denote the image of
a_{ij}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. We claim that
u_{s_{t-1}+1},u_{s_{t-1}+2},\dots,u_{s_{t}}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for each
1\leq t\leq k
. We prove this claim by induction.

First, when
t=1
, if there exist coefficients
\overline{b_{1}},\overline{b_{2}},\dots,\overline{b_{s_{1}}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, not all
\overline{0}
, such that

\overline{b_{1}}u_{1}+\overline{b_{2}}u_{2}+\cdots+\overline{b_{s_{1}}}u_{s_{1}}=\overline{0},

then the coefficients of
\alpha_{j}

(1\leq j\leq s_{1})
in
\sum^{s_{1}}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}
all belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Hence

N\left(\sum^{s_{1}}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}\right)=N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=1}{c_{i}\alpha_{i}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{N(c_{i}\alpha_{i})}<N(\alpha_{1})=N(\beta_{1}).

On the other hand, since
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
is also an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
and not all
b_{1},\dots,b_{s_{1}}
belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we have

N\left(\sum^{s_{1}}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq s_{1}}{N(b_{i}\beta_{i})}=N(\beta_{1}).

This is a contradiction.

Moreover, we conclude that the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\overline{a_{11}}&\cdots&\overline{a_{1s_{1}}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\overline{a_{s_{1}1}}&\cdots&\overline{a_{s_{1}s_{1}}}\end{pmatrix}

is invertible over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Consequently, it can be transformed into an identity matrix by a sequence of elementary row operations. The preimage of this identity matrix is of the form

\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}_{11}&\cdots&p\mathbb{Z}_{p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}&\cdots&a^{\prime}_{s_{1}s_{1}}\end{pmatrix},

where the diagonal elements are not in
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, while all other entries belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Hence we can multiply the first row by
(a^{\prime}_{11})^{-1}
and use it to eliminate the other entries of the first column. It can be observed that, during the execution of these elementary row transformations, we consistently multiply the first row by multiples of
p
and add the result to other rows. Consequently, even after these transformations, the diagonal elements still do not belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Hence, we can repeat the same procedure for the second row and proceed iteratively. Finally, we get an identity matrix, which means that we obtain a new basis
b^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{s_{1}}
of
\mathcal{L}(b_{1},\dots,b_{s_{1}})
. The first
s_{1}
coefficients of
b^{\prime}_{1},\dots,b^{\prime}_{s_{1}}
make up an identity matrix.

Now we can continue our induction. Suppose that
u_{s_{t-1}+1},u_{s_{t-1}+2},\dots,u_{s_{t}}
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for each
1\leq t\leq l-1
. According to the induction hypothesis and the previous conclusion, there is a basis of
\mathcal{L}(b_{1},\dots,b_{s_{l-1}})
of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&\cdots&*&\cdots&*\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}&\cdots&\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&\cdots&*\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly, we can eliminate those
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
entries and obtain a basis of
\mathcal{L}(b_{1},\dots,b_{s_{l-1}})
of the form

\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&\cdots&*&\cdots&*\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&\cdots&*\\
\end{pmatrix}.

When
t=l
, if there are coefficients
\overline{b_{s_{l-1}+1}},\overline{b_{s_{l-1}+2}},\dots,\overline{b_{s_{l}}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, not all
\overline{0}
, such that

\overline{b_{s_{l-1}+1}}u_{s_{l-1}+1}+\overline{b_{s_{l-1}+2}}u_{s_{l-1}+2}+\cdots+\overline{b_{s_{l}}}u_{s_{l}}=\overline{0},

then the coefficients of
\alpha_{j}

({s_{l-1}+1}\leq j\leq{s_{l}})
in

v=\sum^{s_{l}}_{i={s_{l-1}+1}}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}

all belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Combining with the previous conclusion, we obtain that the coefficients of
\alpha_{j}

(1\leq j\leq{s_{l}})
in the above sum all belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then we can use
b_{1},\dots,b_{s_{l-1}}
to eliminate the first
s_{l-1}
columns of
v
. Meanwhile, the
(s_{l-1}+1)
th to the
s_{l}
th columns of
v
still belong to
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, i.e., the following matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&\cdots&*&*&\cdots&*&*&\cdots&*\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&*&\cdots&*&*&\cdots&*\\
p\mathbb{Z}_{p}&\cdots&p\mathbb{Z}_{p}&p\mathbb{Z}_{p}&\cdots&p\mathbb{Z}_{p}&*&\cdots&*\\
\end{pmatrix}

becomes

\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&\cdots&*&*&\cdots&*&*&\cdots&*\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\begin{matrix}1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
\end{matrix}&*&\cdots&*&*&\cdots&*\\
0&\cdots&0&c_{s_{l-1}+1}&\cdots&c_{s_{l}}&c_{s_{l}+1}&\cdots&c_{n}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
c_{s_{l-1}+1},\dots,c_{s_{l}}\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Hence there exist
b_{1},\dots,b_{s_{l-1}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that

N\left(\sum^{s_{l-1}}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}+v\right)=N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=s_{l-1}+1}{c_{i}\alpha_{i}}\right)=\max_{s_{l-1}+1\leq i\leq n}{N(c_{i}\alpha_{i})}<N(\alpha_{s_{l}})=N(\beta_{s_{l}}).

On the other hand,

N\left(\sum^{s_{l-1}}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}+v\right)=N\left(\sum^{s_{l}}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq s_{l}}{N(b_{i}\beta_{i})}\geq N(\beta_{s_{l}}).

This is a contradiction. The proof of the claim is complete.

Let us come back to the proof of the theorem. Notice that
N(\alpha_{m})=N(\beta_{m})\geq N(\beta_{m+1})>N(\alpha_{m+1})
, hence
s_{k}=m
. According to the above results, there exist coefficients
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that

N\left(\sum^{m}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}+\beta_{m+1}\right)=N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=m+1}{c_{i}\alpha_{i}}\right)=\max_{m+1\leq i\leq n}{N(c_{i}\alpha_{i})}\leq N(\alpha_{m+1})<N(\beta_{m+1}).

On the other hand,

N\left(\sum^{m}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}+\beta_{m+1}\right)=\max{\{N(b_{1}\beta_{1}),\dots,N(b_{m}\beta_{m}),N(\beta_{m+1})\}}\geq N(\beta_{m+1}).

This is a contradiction. Hence we must have
N(\alpha_{m+1})=N(\beta_{m+1})
. The proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

4.2
Successive Maxima

Now we can define the successive maxima.

Definition 4.3

(successive maxima)
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
. Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
such that
N(\alpha_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\alpha_{n})
. The
i
th successive maxima of
\mathcal{L}
respect to norm
N
is

\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L}):=N(\alpha_{i}).

By Theorem
4.2
, it is well defined.
We have the following property of the successive maxima.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
such that
N(\alpha_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\alpha_{n})
. Then
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{1})
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})\leq N(\alpha_{i})
for
2\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

Since the longest vector in
\mathcal{L}
is
\alpha_{1}
, we have
N(\alpha_{1})=\tilde{\lambda}_{1}(\mathcal{L})
by definition. We can use the algorithm in Theorem
3.5
to find an
N
-orthogonal basis
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
of
\mathcal{L}
. Notice that the order of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
may be changed during the orthogonalization process. We can initially arrange their order in such a way that this sequence remains unchanged throughout the orthogonalization process, as we mentioned in the proof of Theorem
3.5
. Let
\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
be the new order of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. Then we have

N(\beta_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\beta_{n})

and

N(\beta_{i})\leq N(\alpha^{\prime}_{i})

for
1\leq i\leq n
. Since

N(\alpha_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\alpha_{n}),

we have

N(\beta_{i})\leq N(\alpha_{i})

for
1\leq i\leq n
. Finally, by definition, we have
N(\beta_{i})=\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})
and hence
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})\leq N(\alpha_{i})
for
2\leq i\leq n
.
∎

This bound is tight, as demonstrated by the fact that if
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
, then, by definition,
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
by definition. The subsequent corollary establishes the converse of this statement.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
such that
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Then
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is not an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
. Then there is a subscript
i^{\prime}
such that
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}-1}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}-1})
while
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
is not an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}})
. Since
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
, we have
N(\alpha_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\alpha_{n})
. Let
w_{0}\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}-1})
be such that

N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0})=\min\{N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w):w\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}-1})\}.

Then, we have
N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0})<N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})
, otherwise we can take
w_{0}=0
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}})
by lemma
3.4
. Replace
\alpha_{i^{\prime}}
by
\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0}
in the basis, and we have
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i^{\prime}-1},\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0},\alpha_{i^{\prime}+1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
. By Proposition
4.4
,

\begin{split}\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})&\leq\max\{N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0}),N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}+1}),\dots,N(\alpha_{n})\}\\
&=\max\{N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0}),N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}+1})\}.\end{split}

Since

N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0})<N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L}),

we have

\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})\leq N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}+1})=\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}+1}(\mathcal{L})\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L}).

Hence

N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0})<N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}+1}).

Similarly,

N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0})<N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}+1})=\tilde{\lambda}_{i^{\prime}+1}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}+2}).

Repeat this progress, and eventually we obtain

N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0})<N(\alpha_{n-1})=\tilde{\lambda}_{n-1}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{n})=\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(\mathcal{L})\leq N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0}).

The final inequality follows from Proposition
4.4
, given that
\alpha_{i^{\prime}}+w_{0}
is now the shortest. This is a contradiction.
∎

If we know the successive maxima of a lattice
\mathcal{L}
, then we can compute all possible norms of vectors in
\mathcal{L}
, which is

N(\mathcal{L})=\{N(v):v\in\mathcal{L}\}=\{p^{-i}\tilde{\lambda}_{j}(\mathcal{L}):i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},j=1,\dots,n\}\cup\{0\}.

Hence we can use successive maxima to solve the LVP in
p
-adic lattices.

4.3
Escape Distance

In the context of Euclidean lattices, the concept of covering radius represents the maximal distance between a full-rank lattice and a point lying outside it. By substituting “maximal” with “minimal”, we obtain the
p
-adic analogue of this definition.

Definition 4.6

(escape distance)
.

For a full rank lattice
\mathcal{L}
, define the escape distance of
\mathcal{L}
as

\mu(\mathcal{L}):=\min_{x\in V\setminus\mathcal{L}}{{\rm dist}(x,\mathcal{L})}.

Here is a toy example.

Example 4.7
.

Let
V=\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then
\mu(\mathcal{L})=N(p^{-1})=p
.

We can determine the escape distance by the successive maxima.

Theorem 4.8
.

For a full rank lattice
\mathcal{L}
of rank
n
, we have
\mu(\mathcal{L})=p\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be an
N
–orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
such that
N(\alpha_{i})=\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Then for any lattice vector
v\in\mathcal{L}
and vector
x\in V\setminus\mathcal{L}
, we can write
v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{a_{i}\alpha_{i}}
and
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{b_{i}\alpha_{i}}
where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for
i=1,\dots,n
. Since
x\not\in\mathcal{L}
, at least one
b_{i}
is not in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Suppose that
b_{i^{\prime}}\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Then we have

\begin{split}N(x-v)&=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{N\big((b_{i}-a_{i})\alpha_{i}\big)}\geq N\big((b_{i^{\prime}}-a_{i^{\prime}})\alpha_{i^{\prime}}\big)\\
&=N(b_{i^{\prime}}\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=\left|b_{i^{\prime}}\right|_{p}\cdot N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})\\
&\geq pN(\alpha_{n}).\end{split}

The equality holds when
x=p^{-1}\alpha_{n}
. Hence
\mu(\mathcal{L})=pN(\alpha_{n})=p\tilde{\lambda}_{n}(\mathcal{L})
.
∎

5
Transformation of
N
-orthogonal Basis

We can prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
with an
N
-orthogonal basis
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. If
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
can be obtained from
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
by the following operations:

1.

\alpha_{i}\leftarrow k\alpha_{i}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

2.

\alpha_{i}\leftrightarrow\alpha_{j}
,

3.

\alpha_{i}\leftarrow\alpha_{i}+k\alpha_{j}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
N(k\alpha_{j})\leq N(\alpha_{i})
,

then
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
is also an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
is still a basis of
\mathcal{L}
. Also, it can be observed that
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis if it can be obtained by
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
through operations
1
and
2
. We now prove the operation 3. Suppose that
\beta_{2}=\alpha_{2}+k\alpha_{1}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
N(k\alpha_{1})\leq N(\alpha_{2})
and
\beta_{i}=\alpha_{i}
for
i=1,3,4,\dots,n
. Then for any
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, we have

\begin{split}N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}\right)&=N\left((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}+\sum^{n}_{i=2}{b_{i}\alpha_{i}}\right)\\
&=\max{\left\{N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2}),\dots,N(b_{n}\alpha_{n})\right\}}.\end{split}

And

\begin{split}\max{\{N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}}&\leq\max{\{N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}),N(kb_{2}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}}\\
&=\max{\{N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}}\\
&=\max{\{N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2}+kb_{2}\alpha_{1})\}}.\end{split}

If

\max{\{N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}}<\max{\{N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}},

then

N(kb_{2}\alpha_{1})\leq\max{\{N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}}<N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}).

However,
N(kb_{2}\alpha_{1})<N(b_{1}\alpha_{1})
implies that
N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1})=N(b_{1}\alpha_{1})
. This is a contradiction. Hence

\max{\{N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2})\}}=\max{\{N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2}+kb_{2}\alpha_{1})\}}.

Finally, we have

\begin{split}N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=1}{b_{i}\beta_{i}}\right)&=\max{\left\{N((b_{1}+kb_{2})\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2}),\dots,N(b_{n}\alpha_{n})\right\}}\\
&=\max{\left\{N(b_{1}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{2}\alpha_{2}+kb_{2}\alpha_{1}),N(b_{3}\alpha_{3}),\dots,N(b_{n}\alpha_{n})\right\}}\\
&=\max{\{N(b_{1}\beta_{1}),\dots,N(b_{n}\beta_{n})\}}.\end{split}

This equation means that
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Conversely, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a lattice of rank
n
in
V
. Then any two
N
-orthogonal bases of
\mathcal{L}
can be obtained from each other by the following operations:

1.

\alpha_{i}\leftarrow k\alpha_{i}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

2.

\alpha_{i}\leftrightarrow\alpha_{j}
,

3.

\alpha_{i}\leftarrow\alpha_{i}+k\alpha_{j}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
N(k\alpha_{j})\leq N(\alpha_{i})
.

Proof.

We can write

\beta_{i}=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{a_{ij}\alpha_{j}},

where
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
. Let the matrix
A=(a_{ij})
be the coefficient matrix. Our aim is to demonstrate that matrix
A
can be converted to an identity matrix through these three operations. The transformation is consistent with the steps employed in the proof of Theorem
4.2
. Now we prove that each step in this process corresponds to one of the three defined operations.

First, when we try to obtain the small identity matrix in
(\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{*\times*}
, we use the following operations:

1.

\beta_{i}\leftarrow k\beta_{i}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,

2.

\beta_{i}\leftrightarrow\beta_{j}
,

3.

\beta_{i}\leftarrow\beta_{i}+k\beta_{j}
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

Since
N(\beta_{s_{t-1}+1})=\cdots=N(\beta_{s_{t}})
, we have
N(k\beta_{j})\leq N(\beta_{i})
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
s_{t-1}+1\leq i,j\leq s_{t}
. Therefore, these operations are exactly the same as those in this theorem.

Next, when we attempt to obtain the the small identity matrix in
(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{*\times*}
from the small identity matrix in
(\mathbb{Z}_{p}/p\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{*\times*}
, we use the same kinds of operations as above.

Following this, we seek to eliminate
a_{ij}\in p\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for
s_{t-1}+1\leq i\leq s_{t}
and
1\leq j\leq s_{t-1}
. We need to prove that
N(a_{ij}\beta_{j})\leq N(\beta_{i})
. If
N(a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\beta_{j}^{\prime})>N(\beta_{i}^{\prime})
, then
N(a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\alpha_{j}^{\prime})>N(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})
and

N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}})=N(\beta_{j^{\prime}})=N\left(\sum^{n}_{i=1}{a_{ij^{\prime}}}\alpha_{j^{\prime}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{N(a_{ij^{\prime}}\alpha_{j^{\prime}})}\geq N(a_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\alpha_{j^{\prime}})>N(\alpha_{i^{\prime}}).

This is a contradiction.

Now we obtain an upper triangle matrix with diagonals all equal to
1
. Since
N(k\beta_{j})\leq N(\beta_{i})
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
, we can use operation
3
to transform it to an identity matrix.
∎

6
Orthogonalization with Orthogonal Bases

Efficiently solving the CVP in
p
-adic lattices remains an open challenge without the aid of an
N
-orthogonal basis for the vector space, and the algorithm presented in Theorem
3.5
is not considered efficient. However, possessing an
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space enables us to efficiently determine an
N
-orthogonal basis of a lattice in this vector space.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of dimension
n
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be an
N
-orthogonal (not necessarily orthonormal) basis of
V
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
be a lattice of rank
m\leq n
in
V
. Then we can write

\alpha_{i}=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{a_{ij}e_{j}},

where
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for
i=1,\dots,m
and
j=1,\dots,n
. We have the following orthogonalization process.

Algorithm
(orthogonalization with orthogonal bases).

Input:
an
N
-orthogonal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
V
, a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
in
V
.

Output:
an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

1.

for
i=1
to
m
do:

2.

rearrange
\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{m}
such that
N(\alpha_{i})=\max_{i\leq k\leq m}{N(\alpha_{k})}
,

3.

rearrange
e_{i},\dots,e_{n}
such that
N(a_{ii}e_{i})=\max_{i\leq j\leq m}{N(a_{ij}e_{j})}
,

4.

for
l=i+1
to
m
do:

5.

\alpha_{l}\leftarrow\alpha_{l}-\frac{a_{li}}{a_{ii}}\alpha_{i}.

Return
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
.

This algorithm runs in polynomial time in the input size if we can compute efficiently the norm
N(v)
of any vector
v\in V
. Let us now highlight some crucial observations regarding this algorithm. In the first iteration of the outer loop, we have
N(\alpha_{1})=\max_{1\leq k\leq m}{N(\alpha_{k})}
and
N(a_{11}e_{1})=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}{N(a_{1j}e_{j})}
. Then we use
a_{11}
to eliminate
a_{l1}
for
2\leq l\leq m
. After the first iteration of the outer loop, the matrix of
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}
is of the form

\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}&\cdots&a_{1n}\\
0&a^{\prime}_{22}&\cdots&a^{\prime}_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&a^{\prime}_{m2}&\cdots&a^{\prime}_{mn}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover,
\alpha_{1}
and
e_{1}
are fixed during the rest of the algorithm. Similarly, in the
i
th iteration of the outer loop, the entries in the
(i+1)
th to the
m
th row of the
i
th column are eliminated. Also,
\alpha_{i}
and
e_{i}
are fixed after the
i
th iteration of the outer loop. Finally, we obtain a matrix of the form

\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}&\cdots&b_{1m}&\cdots&b_{1n}\\
0&b_{22}&\cdots&b_{2m}&\cdots&b_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&b_{mm}&\cdots&b_{mn}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Denote the row vectors of this matrix by
\beta_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq m
. Let us prove that it is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

The algorithm above outputs an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

We first prove that the algorithm outputs a basis of
\mathcal{L}
. In the
i
th iteration of the outer loop, we have
N(\alpha_{i})=\max_{i\leq k\leq m}{N(\alpha_{k})}
,
N(a_{ii}e_{i})=\max_{i\leq j\leq m}{N(a_{ij}e_{j})}
and
a_{ij}=0
for
1\leq j\leq i-1
. Since
N(\alpha_{i})=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}{N(a_{ij}e_{j})}
, we can imply that

N(a_{ii}e_{i})=N(\alpha_{i})\geq N(\alpha_{l})\geq N(a_{li}e_{i})

for
i+1\leq l\leq m
. Hence
N(a_{ii})\geq N(a_{li})
and
\frac{a_{li}}{a_{ii}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Therefore, the algorithm outputs a basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

Now we prove by induction that, after the
i
th iteration of the outer loop,
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{i}
constitutes an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{i})
. When
i=1
the conclusion is trivial. Suppose that the conclusion holds for
i=t
. Then
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{t}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{t})
by induction hypothesis. Furthermore, we observe
N(\beta_{1})\geq\cdots\geq N(\beta_{t})\geq N(\beta_{t+1})
, as we consistently select the longest vector at the beginning of each outer loop, and the operations within the inner loop can not augment the length of
\alpha_{l}
beyond that of
\alpha_{i}
. Therefore, according to Lemma
3.4
, we just need to prove that

N(\beta_{t+1})=\min\left\{N(\beta_{t+1}+w):w\in\mathcal{L}(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{t})\right\}.

If there is

w=\sum^{t}_{i=1}{c_{i}\beta_{i}}

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
1\leq i\leq t
, such that
N(\beta_{t+1}+w)<N(\beta_{t+1})
, then we must have
N(w)=N(\beta_{t+1})
. The
e_{j}
coordinate of
\beta_{t+1}+w
, denoted by
d_{j}
, is

\sum^{t}_{i=1}{c_{i}b_{ij}}=\sum^{j}_{i=1}{c_{i}b_{ij}}

for
1\leq j\leq t
and

\sum^{t}_{i=1}{c_{i}b_{ij}}+b_{(t+1)j}

for
t+1\leq j\leq m
. Since

\max_{1\leq j\leq m}{N(d_{j}e_{j})}=N(\beta_{t+1}+w)<N(\beta_{t+1})=\max_{t+1\leq j\leq m}{N\left(b_{(t+1)j}e_{j}\right)},

then for each
t+1\leq j_{0}\leq m
such that

N\left(b_{(t+1)j_{0}}e_{j_{0}}\right)=\max_{t+1\leq j\leq m}{N\left(b_{(t+1)j}e_{j}\right)},

we must have

N(d_{j_{0}}e_{j_{0}})=N\left(\sum^{t}_{i=1}{c_{i}b_{ij_{0}}}e_{j_{0}}+b_{(t+1)j_{0}}e_{j_{0}}\right)<N\left(b_{(t+1)j_{0}}e_{j_{0}}\right).

Hence

N(\beta_{t+1})=N\left(b_{(t+1)j_{0}}e_{j_{0}}\right)=N\left(\sum^{t}_{i=1}{c_{i}b_{ij_{0}}}e_{j_{0}}\right)\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq t}{N(c_{i}b_{ij_{0}}e_{j_{0}})}.

Suppose that
i_{0}
is the first subscript such that
N(c_{i_{0}}b_{i_{0}j_{0}}e_{j_{0}})\geq N(\beta_{t+1})
. Then for
1\leq i<i_{0}
, we have

N(c_{i}b_{ij_{0}}e_{j_{0}})<N(\beta_{t+1})\leq N(c_{i_{0}}b_{i_{0}j_{0}}e_{j_{0}}).

Hence
N(c_{i}b_{ii_{0}}e_{i_{0}})<N(c_{i_{0}}b_{i_{0}i_{0}}e_{i_{0}})
for
1\leq i<i_{0}
. Therefore, the norm of the
e_{i_{0}}
coordinate of
\beta_{t+1}+w
is

N(d_{i_{0}}e_{i_{0}})=N\left(\sum^{i_{0}}_{i=1}{c_{i}b_{ii_{0}}e_{i_{0}}}\right)=N(c_{i_{0}}b_{i_{0}i_{0}}e_{i_{0}})\geq N(\beta_{t+1}).

This is a contradiction.
∎

Here is a toy example to explain the above algorithm.

Example 6.2
.

Keep the notation in Example
3.6
. This time we choose
1+2\zeta
first. Since
N(1)>N(2\zeta)
, we eliminate the
1
coordinate of the vectors
1
and
2+8\zeta+16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}
by the vector
1+2\zeta
. We obtain the vectors
-2\zeta
and
4\zeta+16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}
, respectively.

Next, since
N(-2\zeta)>N(4\zeta+16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3})
, we eliminate the
\zeta
coordinate of the vector
4\zeta+16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}
by the vector
-2\zeta
. We obtain the vector
16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}
. Hence
1+2\zeta,-2\zeta,16\zeta^{2}+16\zeta^{3}
is an
N
-orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{L}
.

7
Solving the CVP with Orthogonal Bases

Given our ability to perform the orthogonalization process using an
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space, a pertinent question arises: Can we solve the CVP utilizing an
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space? The solution presented in Theorem 3.6 of
[
3
]
addresses the CVP specifically with the help of orthogonal bases. However, it requires the lattice basis to be included in the
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space, a more restrictive condition than our current assumption.

In this section, we introduce an algorithm for solving the CVP with the help of an
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space, and we assert that the orthogonalization process and the CVP are polynomially equivalent.

Let
V
be a left vector space over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
of dimension
n
, and let
N
be a norm on
V
. Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be an
N
-orthogonal basis of
V
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
be a lattice of rank
m\leq n
in
V
. Then we can write

\alpha_{i}=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{a_{ij}e_{j}},

where
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}
for
i=1,\dots,m
and
j=1,\dots,n
. Let
t\in V
be a target vector. We present the following algorithm to solve the CVP with orthogonal bases.

Algorithm
(CVP with orthogonal bases).

Input:
an
N
-orthogonal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
V
, a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
in
V
, a target vector
t\in V
.

Output:
a closest lattice vector
v
of
t
.

1.

v\leftarrow 0
,
s\leftarrow t
, write
s=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{s_{j}e_{j}}
,

2.

for
i=1
to
m
do:

3.

rearrange
\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{m}
such that
N(\alpha_{i})=\max_{i\leq k\leq m}{N(\alpha_{k})}
,

4.

if
N(s)>N(\alpha_{i})
then break,

5.

rearrange
e_{i},\dots,e_{n}
such that
N(a_{ii}e_{i})=\max_{i\leq j\leq m}{N(a_{ij}e_{j})}
,

6.

s\leftarrow s-\frac{s_{i}}{a_{ii}}\alpha_{i}
,
v\leftarrow v+\frac{s_{i}}{a_{ii}}\alpha_{i}
,

7.

if
s=0
then break,

8.

for
l=i+1
to
m
do:

9.

\alpha_{l}\leftarrow\alpha_{l}-\frac{a_{li}}{a_{ii}}\alpha_{i}
.

Return
v
.

This algorithm runs in polynomial time in the input size if we can compute efficiently the norm
N(v)
of any vector
v\in V
. Let us prove that
v
is a closest lattice vector of
t
.

Theorem 7.1
.

The algorithm above outputs a closest vector of
t
.

Proof.

We first prove that
v\in\mathcal{L}
. If the algorithm performs step
6
in the
i
th iteration of the outer loop, then we must have
N(s_{i}e_{i})\leq N(s)\leq N(\alpha_{i})=N(a_{ii}e_{i})
. Hence
\frac{s_{i}}{a_{ii}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and
v
is a lattice vector.

We now prove that
v
is a closest lattice vector of
t
. If the algorithm breaks because at step
7
, i.e.,
s=0
, then we have
t\in\mathcal{L}
and
v=t
is the closest lattice vector. Now assume
t\not\in\mathcal{L}
, then the algorithm never breaks at step
7
. Suppose that the algorithm breaks in the
i_{0}
th iteration of the outer loop (if it never breaks, then set
i_{0}=m+1
). Notice that steps
2,3,5,8,9
are exactly steps
1
to
5
in the orthogonalization process in section
6
. Moreover, this algorithm does the same thing to
s
if
N(s)\leq N(\alpha_{i})
. Hence after the
i_{0}
th iteration of the outer loop, we have

N(t-v)=N(s)=\min\left\{N(s+w):w\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i_{0}-1})\right\}.

Since
N(s+w)\geq N(s)>N(\alpha_{i})
for all
w\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i_{0}-1})
and
i_{0}\leq i\leq m
, we have
N(s+w+w^{\prime})=N(s+w)\geq N(s)
for all
w\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i_{0}-1})
and
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{i_{0}},\dots,\alpha_{m})
. Therefore,
v
is a closest lattice vector of
t
.
∎

Finally, we can easily conclude that the orthogonalization process and the CVP are polynomially equivalent.

Theorem 7.2
.

Finding an
N
-orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice and solving the CVP in
p
-adic lattice are polynomially equivalent if we can compute efficiently the norm
N(v)
of any vector
v\in V
.

Proof.

If we can solve CVP-instances, then we can use the algorithm in Therorem
3.5
to find an
N
-orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice in polynomial time. Conversely, if we are able to find an
N
-orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice, then we can first compute an
N
-orthogonal basis of the vector space. Subsequently, according to Theorem
7.1
, we can solve CVP-instances in polynomial time.
∎

8
Solving the LVP with Orthogonal Bases

Certainly, we can first compute an
N
-orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice by the algorithm in Section
6
and then use Theorem 3.5 of
[
3
]
to solve the LVP. Yet, if the goal is solely to identify a (second) longest vector, there is no need to compute the entire
N
-orthogonal basis of a
p
-adic lattice. Consequently, the algorithm can be simplified for this specific purpose.

Algorithm
(LVP with orthogonal bases).

Input:
an
N
-orthogonal basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
of
V
, a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
in
V
.

Output:
a (second) longest vector of
\mathcal{L}
.

1.

for
i=1
to
m
do:

2.

rearrange
\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{m}
such that
N(\alpha_{i})=\max_{i\leq k\leq m}{N(\alpha_{k})}
,

3.

if
i>1
and
N(\alpha_{i-1})>N(\alpha_{i})
then break,

4.

rearrange
e_{i},\dots,e_{n}
such that
N(a_{ii}e_{i})=\max_{i\leq j\leq m}{N(a_{ij}e_{j})}
,

5.

for
l=i+1
to
m
do:

6.

\alpha_{l}\leftarrow\alpha_{l}-\frac{a_{li}}{a_{ii}}\alpha_{i}
,

7.

if
N(p\alpha_{1})>N(\alpha_{i})
then
v\leftarrow p\alpha_{1}
,

8.

else
v\leftarrow\alpha_{i}
.

Return
v
.

This algorithm runs in polynomial time in the input size if we can compute efficiently the norm
N(v)
of any vector
v\in V
. Let us prove that
v
is a
(second) longest vector of
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem 8.1
.

The algorithm above outputs a (second) longest vector of
\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Since the step 6 can not make
N(\alpha_{l})
greater than
N(\alpha_{i})
, when the iteration of the outer loop ends or breaks, we have
N(\alpha_{1})=\cdots=N(\alpha_{i-1})>N(\alpha_{i})
and
i>1
. Moreover, the norms of the remaining vectors in this
N
-orthogonal basis are all less than or equal to
N(\alpha_{i})
. Hence
\tilde{\lambda}_{1}(\mathcal{L})=\cdots=\tilde{\lambda}_{i-1}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{1})
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}(\mathcal{L})=N(\alpha_{i})
. Therefore, the the potential candidates for the (second) longest vector have norm either
N(p\alpha_{1})
or
N(\alpha_{i})
.
∎
