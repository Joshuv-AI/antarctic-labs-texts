Fast Approximate Counting of Cycles
Keren Censor-Hillel, Tomer Even, Virginia Vassilevska Williams

Abstract
We consider the problem of approximate counting of triangles and longer fixed length cycles in directed graphs. For triangles, Tětek [ICALP’22] gave an algorithm that returns a ( 1 ± ε ) (1\pm\varepsilon) -approximation in O ~ ​ ( n ω / t ω − 2 ) \tilde{O}(n^{\omega}/t^{\omega-2}) time, where t t is the unknown number of triangles in the given n n node graph and ω < 2.372 \omega<2.372 is the matrix multiplication exponent. We obtain an improved algorithm whose running time is, within polylogarithmic factors the same as that for multiplying an n × n / t n\times n/t matrix by an n / t × n n/t\times n matrix. We then extend our framework to obtain the first nontrivial ( 1 ± ε ) (1\pm\varepsilon) -approximation algorithms for the number of h h -cycles in a graph, for any constant h ≥ 3 h\geq 3 . Our running time is O ~ ​ ( 𝖬𝖬 ⁡ ( n , n / t 1 / ( h − 2 ) , n ) ) , the time to multiply ​ n × n t 1 / ( h − 2 ) ​ by ​ n t 1 / ( h − 2 ) × n ​ matrices . \tilde{O}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n/t^{1/(h-2)},n)),\textrm{the time to multiply }n\times\frac{n}{t^{1/(h-2)}}\textrm{ by }\frac{n}{t^{1/(h-2)}}\times n\textrm{ matrices}. Finally, we show that under popular fine-grained hypotheses, this running time is optimal.

1.2
Technical Overview

To frame our technical contribution, we first briefly overview the approach of
[
Tět22
]
. The latter gives a randomized approximate counting algorithm for triangles, in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{\omega}/t^{\omega-2})
.
In a nutshell, the algorithm finds a subset of vertices
S
that contains all
\Lambda
-heavy vertices and no
\Lambda/\mathrm{polylog}\left(n\right)
-light vertices — a vertex is called
\Lambda
-heavy if it participates in at least
\Lambda
triangles, and otherwise it is called
\Lambda
-light.
Then, the algorithm approximately counts the number of
\Lambda
-heavy triangles, which are triangles with at least one heavy vertex.
The algorithm then continues by sampling subsets of vertices from the set
V-S
, where each vertex is kept independently uniformly at random with some probability, and processing the sampled graphs by recursion.

Our technical contribution consists of three parts: (I) We simplify the above recursive approach, (II) we improve upon the component that finds heavy vertices, and (III) we improve upon the component that counts the number of cycles that contain heavy vertices. A compelling aspect of our technique is that it applies to any constant-length cycle.
In what follows, we overview each of these aspects.

I. The Recursive Template.

In
[
Tět22
]
, each recursive invocation triggers seven further recursive calls and takes the median of their return values. As the depth of the recursion increases, the algorithm needs to use a precision parameter that becomes exponentially tighter.

In contrast, our algorithm initiates only a single recursive call. This allows us to avoid having to compute the median of several subcalls, which makes it easier
to apply standard amplification tools.
In particular, it allows us to
fix the precision parameter
.

In addition, by reducing the recursive
tree to a “path”, we
simplify the analysis
of the running time.

A prime feature of our simplification of the recursion is that it allows us to present it as a
template
for approximate subgraph counting for any fixed subgraph
\mathbb{H}
, provided one designs the two black boxes (one for finding a superset
S
of the
\Lambda
-heavy vertices with no
\Lambda/\mathrm{polylog}\left(n\right)
-light vertices, and another for approximately counting the number of copies of
\mathbb{H}
that intersect the set
S
).

For triangles, our improvement comes from simplifying the recursion and implementing the black box that finds heavy vertices faster, using rectangular matrix multiplication. Crucially, our implementations of these black boxes are general, in the sense that they apply to constant length
h
-cycle.
Specifically, we find the set
S
in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)},n))
. For such
S
, we find a

(1+\epsilon)
approximation for the number of copies of
\mathbb{H}
that intersect
S
in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{3})
, which is independent of
\Lambda
and of the cardinality of
S
.

II. Finding the Heavy Vertices.

The algorithm in
[
Tět22
]

finds
\Lambda
-heavy vertices by sampling a subset of vertices uniformly at random, and using matrix multiplication to detect triangles inside the induced sampled subgraph. This takes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{\omega}/\Lambda^{\omega-2})
time, by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\Lambda^{2})
repetitions of multiplying
n/\Lambda\times n/\Lambda
matrices.

At the heart of our approach for finding the heavy vertices lies non-uniform sampling, and computing the product of rectangular matrices rather than square ones.
We obtain a running time of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)},n))=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{\omega}/\Lambda^{\gamma_{h}})
for
h
-cycles, where
\gamma_{h}\triangleq\frac{\omega-2}{(1-\alpha)(h-2)}
.
This comes from multiplying an
n\times n/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}
matrix by an
n/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}\times n
matrix.
In
[
VXXZ24
]
it was shown that
\alpha\geq 0.321334
, and therefore

\frac{\omega-2}{1-\alpha}\geq 1.47(\omega-2)
, which establishes that our algorithm is never slower, and is faster (if
\omega>2
), where the gap increases with
\Lambda
(for sufficiently large
\Lambda
, the folklore naïve sampling algorithm is superior).

Our starting point for finding the heavy vertices is the
color-coding
technique of
[
AYZ95
]
, which is widely employed for detecting
h
-cycles for
h=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
This technique colors the vertices using
h
colors uniformly independently at random and looks for
colorful

h
-cycles, which are
h
-cycles with exactly one vertex of each color. This restriction allows for faster detection but suffers some probability of missing
h
-cycles that are colored out of order, which can be overcome with sufficiently large probability by repeating this process.

To find colorful
h
-cycles, we utilize matrix multiplication. However, we do so in a refined manner. Rather than considering all vertices, we sample a subset of vertices from each color class in a nonuniform manner.
To illustrate this, consider the task of finding
\Lambda
-heavy vertices w.r.t. triangles.
We assign a random color to each vertex, and denote the three color classes by
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}
.
We focus on identifying the
\Lambda
-heavy vertices within
V_{1}
.
Fix some
i\in[\log\Lambda]
.
We sample each vertex from
V_{2}
with probability
2^{i}/\Lambda
, and we sample each vertex from
V_{3}
with probability
1/2^{i}
. Let
H_{i}
denote the induced graph obtained by all vertices from
V_{1}
and the sampled vertices from
V_{2}
and
V_{3}
, where we also direct edges from
V_{j}
to
V_{j+1\mod 3}
and discard monochromatic edges.
We show that for every
\Lambda
-heavy vertex
v
, there exists an index
i\in[\log\Lambda]
such that
v
is in a triangle in
H_{i}
with some probability at least
p_{\mathrm{heavy}}
, where
1/p_{\mathrm{heavy}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
.
On the other hand, for
\Lambda/\mathrm{polylog}\left(n\right)
-light vertex
u
, we show that for every
i\in[\log\Lambda]
, the vertex
v
is in a triangle in
H_{i}
with probability at most
p_{\mathrm{heavy}}/2
. Therefore, we can distinguish between these cases.
Checking whether
v
is in a triangle in
H_{i}
can be done in
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n,n/\Lambda))
time.
Using amplification, we approximate the probability that
v
is in a triangle in
H_{i}
for every
v\in V_{1}
, and thus distinguish heavy vertices from lighter ones.

We generalize our approach for
h
-cycles by coloring the vertices with
h
colors, and directing edges and discarding monochromatic edges, as for triangles. We also discard edges between non-consecutive color classes.
To find
\Lambda
-heavy vertices in the first color class, we sample in a nonuniform manner a subset of vertices from the
j
-th color class for
2\leq j\leq h
, where the product of the sampling probabilities of the color classes should be at most
1/\Lambda
, as for triangles. Let
H
denote the obtained random induced subgraph.
The running time of computing the exact number of
h
-cycles each vertex in
H
participates in, which is dominated by the size of the smallest color class in
H
, becomes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n,n/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}))
.
To see why, consider an
h
-partite graph
G
with
n
vertices in each part. Suppose
G
has a vertex
v\in V_{1}
with a neighbor
u\in V_{2}
, such that all
h
-cycles that intersect
v
, also intersect the edge
(u,v)
. Now, suppose each vertex set
V_{j}
, for
3\leq j\leq h
has a subset
W_{j}
of
\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}
vertices, such that any
h
-tuple of the form
(v,u,w_{3},w_{4},\ldots,w_{h})
is an
h
-cycle in
G
, where
w_{j}\in W_{j}
for
3\leq j\leq h
.
This implies that
v
is
\Lambda
-heavy.
Note that if we keep each vertex from the
j
-th color class with a probability of
o(1/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)})
, we are unlikely to sample any vertex from
W_{j}
, and therefore we fail to learn that
v
is
\Lambda
-heavy. On the other hand, if we sample vertices from each class with probability
\Omega\left(1/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}\right)
, the smallest color class is of size
\Omega\left(n/\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}\right)
.

III. Counting the Heavy Copies.

Given a graph
G
and a subset of vertices
S
, where each vertex in
S
participates in at least
a
and at most
b
copies of
h
-cycles for
h=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, we show how to compute a
(1+\epsilon)
approximation for the number of
h
-cycles that intersect the set
S
, in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}b/\varepsilon a)
. In particular, the runtime is independent of size of the set
S
.

Consider a naïve approach, which approximates the average number of
h
-cycles that a vertex from
S
intersects, and let us see why it fails to provide a good approximation for the total number of
h
-cycles intersecting
S
. Suppose
h=3
and
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=3
and each vertex
v\in S
participates in exactly one triangle in
G
.
Based solely on the number of triangles in which a vertex participates, it is impossible to distinguish the case where the set
S
intersects one triangle in
G
from the case in which it intersects three triangles in
G
. The issue here is double counting, as we did not avoid counting the same cycle more than once. For triangles, this obstacle can be avoided by replacing
G
with a tripartite graph
G^{\prime}
, where each of the three parts is a copy of
V
, and for each edge in
G
there are six edges in
G^{\prime}
, one for every ordered pair of parts. It is easy to see that every triangle in
G
corresponds to six triangles in
G^{\prime}
, and thus an estimate on
G^{\prime}
directly gives an estimate on
G
.
That is, we sample a subset
F
of vertices from
S
, and for each copy
v^{\prime}
of
v\in F
in the, say, first part of in the tri-partition
G^{\prime}
,
we compute the number of triangles that go through it in
G^{\prime}
. This avoids double counting, because each triangle in
G^{\prime}
intersects copies of the set
F
from the first part at most once (as vertices in the same part form an independent set and hence cannot be in the same triangle).
To summarize, restricting to triangles would significantly simplify this part to a single Chernoff inequality for independent random variables.
The source of this simplicity is that triangles are cliques. However, larger cycles are not cliques, and therefore we cannot apply this trick.
If we simply repeated every vertex
h
times to create a new graph
G^{\prime}
as in the triangle case above, we could create
h
-cycles in
G^{\prime}
that do not correspond to
h
-cycles in
G
: in fact, every
closed walk
of length
h
would become an
h
-cycle. We use color-coding and a more careful analysis to overcome this issue.

First, we sample a subset of vertices from
S
and for each sampled vertex
v
we approximate the number of
h
-cycles which go through
v
(and therefore intersect the set
S
). The crux of our algorithm is that in order to approximate the above, we approximate the number of
h
-cycles which go through
v
and intersect the set
S
exactly
k
times, for each
1\leq k\leq h
.
The summation of these approximations yields our final result, and it naturally avoids the pitfall of double-counting.

To approximate the number of
h
-cycles intersecting the set
S
exactly
k
times for some
k
, we color the graph with
h-1
colors and color vertex
v
with the color
h
. This ensures that any colorful
h
-cycle intersects
v
.
Then, we choose
k-1
color classes from the first
h-1
classes, and retain only vertices of
S
within those classes. For the remaining color classes, we keep only vertices that are not in
S
. The color class
h
is fixed and always contains only
v
. This promises that each colorful
h
-cycle with
v
in this auxiliary graph intersects
S
exactly
k
times.
The number of ways to choose exactly
k-1
color classes that keep only vertices from
S
is
{h-1\choose k-1}
.
We compute the number of
h
-cycles in each such auxiliary graph.
We prove that the expectation of this number is some fixed constant multiplicative factor off from the number of
h
-cycles intersecting
v
and
S
exactly
k
times.
Finally, we prove that the variance of this random variable is suitably bounded. Therefore, conducting this process
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(b/(a\varepsilon))
times enables us to obtain an
(1\pm\varepsilon)
approximation for its expectation
by Chebyshev’s inequality.
We compute the number of
h
-cycles in this auxiliary graph using rectangular matrix multiplication. Since the auxiliary graph is
h
-partite and one part contains only a single vertex, we get a running time of
{\mathsf{MM}(n,n,1)}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
. Thus, we achieve our claimed running time of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}b/\varepsilon a)
.

We mention that we invoke this procedure on the set of vertices given by the previous component of finding heavy vertices, which is called upon in every recursion step.

The Recursive Template

Organization.

In this section, we present an algorithm for approximating the number of copies of a graph
\mathbb{H}
in a graph
G
, denoted by
t
, which builds upon two black boxes.
The first black box, called
\mathsf{Find}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}
, takes a graph
H
and a parameter
\Lambda
as input and computes a superset of the
\Lambda
-
heavy
vertices, excluding any
\Lambda/\mathrm{polylog}\left(n\right)
-light vertices. We denote the computed superset of heaviest vertices as
S

The second black box, called
\mathsf{Count}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}
, is used to compute an approximation for the number of
heavy-copies
of
\mathbb{H}
in
G
, which is the set of all copies that contain at least one vertex from
S
.
In sections
Sections

4
and
5

Our algorithm for subgraph approximate counting that uses the specified black boxes consists of two parts: a doubling algorithm called
\mathsf{Doubling\mhyphen Template}
, and a recursive algorithm called
\mathsf{Template}
, which is the main focus of this section.

The
\mathsf{Template}
Algorithm.

The
\mathsf{Template}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(G,\Lambda\right)
algorithm takes two parameters: a graph
G
and a heaviness threshold
\Lambda
.
The output of the algorithm is a value
\hat{t}
, which, with a probability of at least
2/3
, is within the range
t\pm\left(t\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}+\Lambda\cdot\mathrm{polylog}\left(n\right)/\varepsilon^{\prime}\right)
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is the fixed precision parameter of the algorithm.

We next explain how the recursive
\mathsf{Template}
algorithm works. The algorithm does the following.

1.

Find the heaviest vertices using the
\mathsf{Find}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}
black box, and denote this set by
V_{\Lambda}
.

2.

Compute an approximation to the number of heavy copies of
\mathbb{H}
, i.e., copies of
\mathbb{H}
with at least one vertex from
V_{\Lambda}
, using the
\mathsf{Count}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}
black box, and denote the output by
\hat{t}_{\Lambda}
.

3.

Let
H=G[V(G)-V_{\Lambda}]
, and let
F\leftarrow H[p]
. That is,
F
is an
induced subgraph of
H
, where each vertex from
H
joins
F
independently with probability
p
.

4.

Make a recursive call to
\mathsf{Template}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(F,\Lambda\cdot p^{\left\lvert\mathbb{H}\right\rvert}\right)
and let
\hat{t}_{F}
denote its output.

5.

Return
\hat{t}_{\Lambda}+\hat{t}_{F}/p^{\left\lvert\mathbb{H}\right\rvert}
.

The analysis of the probability that the algorithm will produce a good approximation appears in the proof of
Lemma

2.3
.

The running time of the algorithm depends on the implementation of the black boxes. In the next section, we analyze the running time of the algorithm.

The
\mathsf{Doubling\mhyphen Template}
Algorithm.

The
\mathsf{Doubling\mhyphen Template}
algorithm is a doubling algorithm, which starts with an initial guess for
t
, denoted by
{\mathsf{W}}_{0}=n^{\left\lvert\mathbb{H}\right\rvert}
. This is the maximal number of copies of
\mathbb{H}
an
n

h!\cdot{n\choose\left\lvert\mathbb{H}\right\rvert}\leq n^{\left\lvert\mathbb{H}\right\rvert}

The algorithm then makes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)

\mathsf{Template}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(G,\Lambda_{0}\right)

\Lambda_{0}\leftarrow{\mathsf{W}}\cdot{\varepsilon}^{2}/8Q

, and computes their median, which we denote by
\hat{t}_{0}
.
If
\hat{t}_{0}\geq{\mathsf{W}}_{0}
the doubling algorithm stops and outputs
\hat{t}_{0}
as its approximation for
t
.
Otherwise, the guess for the value of
t
is decreased by a factor of
2
.
The main point here is that the smaller the value of
\Lambda
given to the recursive algorithm is, the better approximation we get, while simultaneously increasing the running time. The guess
{\mathsf{W}}
is a guess for the highest heaviness threshold the algorithm can start with to output a good approximation, and not an actual guess for the value of
t
(although both quantities are related).

Input:

-heavy vertices.
Overall, we get

∎This completes the proof of

See

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
using

\Psi_{\mathsf{Doubling\mhyphen Template}}(G,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{y}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n,\frac{n}{(\Lambda/2^{i})^{1/(h-2)}})+\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon^{3}})

Next, we bound the running time of the

4
Implementing the Black Box
{\mathsf{Count}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}_{\varepsilon}}

In this section, we implement the following black box.

Input:

A graph
G
, a precision parameter
\varepsilon
, a subset of vertices
S
, and two real numbers
0<a\leq b
, such that

\forall v\in S
we have
t_{G}(v)\in[a,b]
.

Output:

\hat{t}
which satisfies
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\hat{t}=t_{G}(S)(1\pm\varepsilon)\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{4}}
.

In this section, we prove the second part of
Lemma

3.1
, which is stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

There is an algorithm that implements the
{\mathsf{Count}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}_{\varepsilon}}(G,S,a,b)
black box, when
\mathbb{H}
is an
h
-cycles, for
h=\mathcal{O}(1)
, in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{a\cdot\varepsilon})
.

For this entire section, the graph
G
is fixed, and the set
S
is a fixed subset of vertices, where for every
v\in S
we have
t_{G}(v)\in[a,b]
.
We emphasize that
a
is only a lower bound on
\min_{v\in S}t_{G}(v)
and
b
is only an upper bound on
\max_{v\in S}t_{G}(v)
.
Denote
{N_{k}\triangleq\left\lvert S\right\rvert/k}
.
Before implementing the
{\mathsf{Count}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}_{\varepsilon}}
black box, we explain why the following simple idea fails: sample a few vertices from
S
, estimate
t_{G}(v)
for each one, and apply a concentration bound to compute
t_{G}(S)
.
Formally, this approach fails because
\sum_{v\in S}t_{G}(v)\neq t_{G}(S)
.
To see why, note that the value
t_{G}(v)
does not tell us how many copies of
\mathbb{H}
intersect
S
more than once, and are therefore counted multiple times.
We present a simple example to demonstrate this.
Assume
\mathbb{H}
is a triangle,
\left\lvert S\right\rvert=3
and for every
v\in S
we have
t_{G}(v)=1
. Solely based on
t_{G}(v)
, one cannot distinguish between the cases
t_{G}(S)=1
and
t_{G}(S)=3
.

We overcome this issue by sampling a small subset of vertices
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
, and then, for every
v\in S^{\prime}
we approximate the number of copies of
\mathbb{H}
which intersect
v
and exactly
i
additional vertices from
S
for
0\leq i\leq h-1
. This will allow us to estimate the number of multiple countings and therefore get an estimation of
t_{G}(S)
.

Our approach for approximating
\hat{t}
as required by
{\mathsf{Count}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}_{\varepsilon}}
is to approximate the number of cycles that intersect
S
in exactly
k
vertices. To this end, we define the following.

Definition 4
.

Define
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of copies of
\mathbb{H}
in
G
.
For a subset of vertices
U
, we define
\mathcal{C}(U)\triangleq\left\{C\in\mathcal{C}\mid C\cap U\neq\emptyset\right\}
.
We denote
t\triangleq\left\lvert\mathcal{C}\right\rvert
and
t(U)\triangleq\left\lvert\mathcal{C}(U)\right\rvert
.
In general, we replace the symbol
\mathcal{C}
by
t
, to denote the cardinality of a set.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{k}
denote the set of copies
C\in\mathcal{C}
with
\left\lvert C\cap S\right\rvert=k
.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)\triangleq\mathcal{C}^{k}\cap\mathcal{C}(v)
. We let
t^{k}=\left\lvert\mathcal{C}^{k}\right\rvert
.

This definition helps us overcome the double-counting issue because for any
k
we have
\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}t^{k}(v)=t^{k}
, and
\sum_{k\in[h]}t^{k}=t_{G}(S)
. We now claim that we can efficiently approximate
t^{k}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{t^{k}}
)
.

There exists a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{t^{k}}
with the following characteristics.
The input is a graph
G
, a set
S
, a precision parameter
\delta\in[0,1]
, a parameter
k\in[h]
, and a tuple
(a,b)
, such that for every
v\in S
we have
t_{G}(v)\in[a,b]
.
The algorithm produces an output
\hat{t}^{k}
which satisfies
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\hat{t}^{k}=t^{k}\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{4}}
.
The running time of the algorithm is bounded by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{\delta})
.

We now show how to prove
Theorem

4.1
using
Lemma

4.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
using
Lemma

4.2
.
Set
\delta=\varepsilon/2k
.
Compute
\hat{t}^{k}
for each
k\in[h]
using
Lemma

4.2
.
Note that for each
k\in[h]
we have

{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\hat{t}^{k}=t^{k}\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right]\geq 1-\frac{{1}}{n^{4}}
.
Denote
\hat{t}_{G}(S)=\sum_{k\in[h]}\hat{t}^{k}
.
We show that
\hat{t}_{G}(S)=t_{G}(S)(1\pm\varepsilon)
, assuming
\hat{t}^{k}=t^{k}\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta
for every
k
, which happens with probability at least
1-\frac{{k}}{n^{4}}
, by
Lemma

4.2
.

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{G}(S)=\sum_{k\in[h]}\hat{t}^{k}=\sum_{k\in[h]}t^{k}\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta=(\sum_{k\in[h]}t^{k})\pm k\cdot t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta=t_{G}(S)\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=t_{G}(S)(1\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{2})\;.

We make
h=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
calls to the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{t^{k}}
, so we get the desired running time, completing the proof of
Theorem

4.1
.
∎

Thus, it remains to prove
Lemma

4.2
.
To approximate
t^{k}
, we find a value whose expectation is
t^{k}
and whose variance is at most
\mathcal{O}(\left(N_{k}\cdot b\right)^{2})
(recall that
N_{k}=\left\lvert S\right\rvert/k
). The following lemma states that we can do this efficiently.

Lemma 4.3

(Algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
)
.

There is a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
whose input is
G,S,\delta
and
k
. Note that unlike
\mathsf{Approx}_{t^{k}}
, the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
does not require
a
and
b
as part of its input parameters.

\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
computes a value
X
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=t^{k}
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[X\right]\leq C\cdot\left(N_{k}\cdot b\right)^{2}
, where
C
is a constant.
The running time of the algorithm is bounded by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
.

The reason that
Lemma

4.3
is helpful is because we can run the algorithm it provides
r
times and take the median of means of these invocations.
Formally, the proof of
Lemma

4.2
is as follows.

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
using
Lemma

4.3
.
Let
\ell\triangleq\frac{b}{a\cdot\delta}\cdot 3C
.
Let
Y
denote the mean of
\ell
invocations of the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k)
. Note that
Y
is a random variable, where the probability space is over the internal choices of the algorithm.
We claim that
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[Y=t^{k}\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right]\geq 2/3
.
Assuming this is true, we can implement the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{t^{k}}
as follows.
Make
\ell\cdot 400\log n
calls to the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
.
The algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{t^{k}}
gets
a,b,
and
\delta
as inputs and therefore it can compute
\ell
.
Split the outputs into batches of size
\ell
. Compute the mean of each batch, and output the median of means of the batches.
Since the mean of each batch falls inside the desired interval with probability at least
2/3
, we can apply the “median trick” (see
Claim

B.1
) to show that the median of the means falls within this interval with probability at least
1-\frac{{1}}{n^{4}}
.

Therefore, to complete the proof, we are left with proving that
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[Y=t^{k}\pm t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right]\geq 2/3
. We do so by
bounding
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y\right]
and using Chebyshev’s inequality.
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\in\ell}\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k)\right]=t^{k}\;,

where the last equality is by the guarantees of
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
given in

Lemma

4.3
.
In addition,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}\left[Y\right]=\mathsf{Var}\left[\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\in\ell}\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k)\right]=\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}\mathsf{Var}\left[\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k)\right]\leq\frac{C\cdot(N_{k}\cdot b)^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\;.

Where the last equality is again by the guarantees of
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
given in
Lemma

4.3
.
Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\left\lvert Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]\right\rvert\geq t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right]\leq\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[Y\right]}{\left(t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{C\cdot(N_{k}\cdot b)^{2}}{{\left(\ell\cdot t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right)^{2}}}=\frac{C\cdot(N_{k}\cdot b)^{2}}{{\left(\frac{b}{a\delta}\cdot 3C\cdot t_{G}(S)\cdot\delta\right)^{2}}}\leq\frac{(a\cdot N_{k})^{2}}{{\left(3\cdot t_{G}(S)\right)^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{9}.

The last inequality follows because
t_{G}(S)\geq a\cdot N_{k}
.
To see that, note that each vertex in
S
participates in at least
a
copies of
\mathbb{H}
.
Moreover, each copy can contain at most
h
vertices from
S
, therefore, the number of copies which intersect
S
is at least
a\cdot\left\lvert S\right\rvert/k
, which means that
t_{G}(S)\geq a\cdot\left\lvert S\right\rvert/k=a\cdot N_{k}
.

The running time is bounded by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{\delta})
, as we made
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{b}{a\delta})
calls to the
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
algorithm, where each call takes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
time by the guarantees of
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
given in
Lemma

4.3
.
∎

In what follows, we prove
Lemma

4.3
.
To get a sample
X
with
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=t^{k}
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[X\right]\leq C\cdot(b\cdot N_{k})^{2}
, we find samples
Y_{v}
with
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{v}\right]=t^{k}(v)
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{v}\right]\leq C\cdot b^{2}
.
The following lemma states that we can do this efficiently.

Lemma 4.4
.

There is a randomized algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
whose input is
G,S,\delta,k
and a vertex
v\in S
.
Unlike
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
,
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
additionally takes a vertex
v\in S
as input.

\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
computes a value
Y_{v}
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{v}\right]=t^{k}(v)
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{v}\right]\leq C\cdot b^{2}
, where
C
is a constant.
The running time of the algorithm is bounded by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
.

The reason that
Lemma

4.4
is helpful is that we can sample a vertex
v
uniformly at random from
S
, and get, using
Lemma

4.4
, an unbias estimator for the number of copies of
\mathbb{H}
which contains
v
, and intersect
S
exactly
k
times, i.e.,
t^{k}(v)
. By the law of total expectation, we get that the expected value of this quantity, is equal to
\frac{1}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\sum_{u\in S}\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}(u)\right]=t^{k}/N_{k}
. Therefore, we got an unbias estimator for
t^{k}
upto a known value
N_{k}
.
Formally, the proof of
Lemma

4.3
is as follows.

Proof of
Lemma

4.3
using
Lemma

4.4
.
For brevity, we write
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}(v)
, instead of
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k,v)
.
We implement
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
using a single call to
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
.
The algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
samples a vertex
u\in S
uniformly at random, and simulates
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}(u)
.
Let
Y_{u}
denote the output of the simulation.
The algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
then outputs
X\leftarrow Y_{u}\cdot N_{k}
.
We need to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=t^{k}
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[X\right]\leq\mathcal{O}((N_{k}b)^{2})
, given that for all
u\in S
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{u}\right]=t^{k}(u)
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{u}\right]=C\cdot b^{2}
.
For
u\in S
, define

\displaystyle Z_{u}\triangleq\begin{cases}Y_{u}&\text{If $\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}$ samples $u$}\\
0&\text{Otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
X=N_{k}\cdot\sum_{u\in S}Z_{u}
.
We compute the expectation and variance of
X
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=N_{k}\sum_{u\in S}{\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{u}\right]}

\displaystyle=N_{k}\sum_{u\in S}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[u\text{ is sampled}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}(u)\right]=\frac{N_{k}}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\sum_{u\in S}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}(u)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{u\in S}t^{k}(u)=t^{k}\;.

We now prove the claim on the variance.
Recall that, by the guarantees of
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
given in
Lemma

4.4
, we have that for every
u
,
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{u}\right]\leq C\cdot b^{2}
.
Note that
\mathsf{Var}\left[Z_{u}\right]=\frac{\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{u}\right]}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\leq\frac{C\cdot b^{2}}{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}
.
Moreover, note that for any two distinct vertices
u,w\in S
we have
\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{u}\cdot Z_{w}\right]=0
, as only one of them was chosen by
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]}
.
Therefore,
\mathsf{Cov}\left(Z_{u},Z_{w}\right)<0
.
We get

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}\left[X/N_{k}\right]=\mathsf{Var}\left[\sum_{u\in{S}}Z_{u}\right]=\sum_{u\in{S}}\mathsf{Var}\left[Z_{u}\right]+\sum_{u,w\in{S}}\mathsf{Cov}\left(Z_{u},Z_{w}\right)\leq\sum_{u\in{S}}\mathsf{Var}\left[Z_{u}\right]\leq Cb^{2}\;,

and therefore
\mathsf{Var}\left[X\right]=N_{k}^{2}\cdot\mathsf{Var}\left[X/N_{k}\right]\leq C\cdot(N_{k}\cdot b)^{2}
.
∎It remains to prove
Lemma

4.4
, which we do in what follows.

4.1
Implementing the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}

We utilize the color-coding technique introduced by
[
AYZ95
]
. The high level approach of the technique is to randomly color vertices with
h
colors and detect
colorful

h
-cycles that are ordered by, say, increasing colors. This additional structure allows for faster detection, at the cost of some probability of missing
h
-cycles that are colored out of order. The technique overcomes this by repeated experiments.

A pertinent question arises: why are the algorithms
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]},\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
necessary? Why not just choose a random coloring, compute the number of colorful copies of
\mathbb{H}
intersecting a set
S
exactly
k
times, and apply Chebyshev’s inequality to conclude that repeating this process
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2}\cdot\frac{b}{a\delta})
times suffices for a good approximation of
t^{k}
? The answer
lies in the execution time of the matrix multiplication algorithm for counting colorful copies, which is dominated by the sizes of the largest, second largest, and smallest color classes. Roughly speaking, the smaller the product of these sizes, the faster the algorithm runs.
Under random coloring, color classes each have a size of
\Omega(n)
with high probability. Conversely,
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
produces a color class containing just a single vertex, which significantly improves the running time in the worst case, compared to the approach which does not use the algorithms
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[t^{k}\right]},\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
.

We need the following definitions to explain how color-coding works, and how the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
uses rectangular matrix multiplication.

Definition 6

(
\varphi
-Colorful,
t_{\varphi}
)
.

Fix some coloring
\varphi:V\to[\ell]
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
(
\ell
will usually be
h
).
Let
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
denote the set of all copies
C\in\mathcal{C}
, such that
\varphi(C)=[\ell]
. If
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
, we say that
C
is
\varphi
-colorful
.
Also define
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v)=\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}\cap\mathcal{C}(v)
,

t_{\varphi}\triangleq\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}\right\rvert
, and
t_{\varphi}(v)\triangleq\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v)\right\rvert
.

The subsequent definition relates colorful copies of
\mathbb{H}
with the set
S
.

Definition 7

(
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}\triangleq\mathcal{C}^{k}\cap\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
. That is,
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}
is the set of copies of
\mathbb{H}
in
G
, where each such copy is colorful w.r.t.
\varphi
, and additionally intersects the set
S
exactly
k
times. Let
t^{k}_{\varphi}=\left\lvert\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}\right\rvert
.

We use the term
random coloring
, to denote a coloring of
V
that is sampled at random from the uniform distribution. The following definition formally defines this term.

Definition 8

(Random Coloring)
.

Let
A,B
be two finite sets.
We say that a function
\varphi:A\to B
is a random coloring, if the value of each
a\in A
is set to some value
b\in B
, where
b
is chosen uniformly at random from
B
and independently of values chosen for other elements in
A
.

The following definition is used to quantify the complexity of computing
t^{k}_{\varphi}
as a function of the sizes of the color classes that the coloring
\varphi
induces.
Let
\varphi:V\to[h]
.
For
i\in[h]
, we denote by
\varphi^{-1}(i)
the set of all vertices
v
with
\varphi(v)=i
, and call this set the
i
-th color class.
Assume without loss of generality that the color classes are sorted according their cardinalities, in a non-decreasing order. That is, for every
i<h
we have
\varphi^{-1}(i)\geq\varphi^{-1}(i+1)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{h})
denote the cardinality of the largest, second largest, and smallest color classes, respectively.
For any fixed
k\in[h]
, there is a deterministic algorithm for computing
\left\{t^{k}_{\varphi}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
in time
\mathcal{O}((h!)^{2}\cdot h^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{h}))
.

Corollary 1
.

For any fixed
k\in[h]
, there is a deterministic algorithm for computing
t^{k}_{\varphi}
in time
\mathcal{O}((h!)^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{h}))
.

The corollary follows because for every
k\in[h]
, we have
\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{v\in V}t^{k}(v)=t^{k}
.
Next, we explain how to implement the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
given that we can compute the number
t^{k}_{\varphi}
of colorful copies of
\mathbb{H}
.
The algorithm works as follows.
It colors each vertex with a random color from the set
[h-1]
.
It then recolors the input vertex by a new color
h
.
Let
\varphi
denote this coloring. The algorithm then computes
t^{k}_{\varphi}
and outputs
t^{k}_{\varphi}/q
for some constant
q
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[t_{\varphi}/q\right]=t^{k}(v)
. We prove that the expectation and variance of this output satisfy the claimed requirements.

Proof of
Lemma

4.4
using
Lemma

4.5
.
The algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}(G,S,\delta,k,v)
is given a graph
G
, the set
S
, a precision parameter
\delta
, and a vertex
v\in S
.
The algorithm samples a random coloring
\varphi^{\prime}:V\setminus\left\{v\right\}\to[h-1]
.
Define a coloring
\varphi:V\to[h]
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall u\in V\quad\varphi(u)\triangleq\begin{cases}\varphi^{\prime}(u)&\text{If }u\neq v\\
h&\text{Otherwise}\end{cases}

The algorithm then outputs
t_{\varphi}^{k}/q
, for
q\triangleq\frac{(h-1)!}{(h-1)^{h-1}}
. This completes the description of the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
.

Next, we prove the algorithm’s correctness assuming we can exactly compute
t_{\varphi}^{k}/q
.
Denote the output of the algorithm by
Y_{v}
, where
Y_{v}
is a random variable as
t_{\varphi}^{k}
is also a random variable, where the randomness is over the choice of
\varphi
.
We show that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{v}\right]=t^{k}(v)
and
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{v}\right]=\mathcal{O}(b^{2})
.

Consider some copy
C
which is in
\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)
.
The probability that
C\in\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}(v)
is the probability that
C
is colorful given that
C\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)
, which is equal to
q\triangleq\frac{(h-1)!}{(h-1)^{h-1}}
.

Consider some copy
C
which is not in
\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)
. We show that
C\notin\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}
. If
v\notin C
, then
C
is not colorful, and therefore not in
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}
. If
v\in C
but
C\not\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)
, then
C\notin\mathcal{C}^{k}
which means
C
is also not in
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{v}\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[C\text{ is $\varphi$-colorful }\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[C\text{ is $\varphi$-colorful }\right]=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}^{k}(v)}q=t^{k}(v)\;.

For the variance, we know that
t^{k}(v)\leq t_{G}(v)\leq b
and that
Y_{v}\leq(b/q)
, which means that
(Y_{v})^{2}\leq(b/q)^{2}
, and therefore
\mathsf{Var}\left[Y_{v}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{v})^{2}\right]\leq(b/q)^{2}
.
This completes the proof of the correctness of the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
.

The running time of the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
is bounded by the running time of the algorithm specified in
Lemma

4.5
, which is bounded by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n,1))
as the smallest color class has only one vertex, and other color classes has at most
n
vertices. Note that
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{MM}(n,n,1))=n^{2+o(1)}
, which proves the running time of the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
is bounded by
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2})
.
∎

We are left with proving
Lemma

4.5
, which is the final step in the implementation of the algorithm
\mathsf{Approx}_{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{vertex}\mhyphen t^{k}\right]}
.
To prove
Lemma

4.5
, we reduce the problem of computing
t^{k}_{\varphi}
to the problem of computing
t_{\varphi}
on an auxiliary graph, in which every
h
-cycle is colorful and also intersects the set
S
exactly
k
times.
We construct the auxiliary graph by randomly coloring the vertices with
h
colors, then selecting
k
color classes and keeping only vertices from
S
in them, while discarding the rest of the vertices in those classes. For the remaining
h-k
color classes, we retain only vertices that are not part of
S
.
We get a graph in which each color class is either contained in
S
or disjoint from
S
.
This reduces the problem of computing
t^{k}_{\varphi}
on the auxiliary graph, to computing
t_{\varphi}
on it.
The next claim addresses the running time of computing
t_{\varphi}
(on the auxiliary graph) instead of computing
t^{k}_{\varphi}
.

Claim 12
.

Let
G
be a graph and
let
\sigma\triangleq\left(U_{1},\ldots,U_{h}\right)
be an (ordered) sequence of disjoint subsets of vertices of
V(G)
.
Let
t^{\sigma}_{G}
denote the number of copies
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}
where
C=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{h})
and
v_{i}\in U_{i}
for every
i\in[h]
.
Let
U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)}
denote the cardinality of the largest, second largest, and smallest subset, respectively.
Then, there is a deterministic algorithm that outputs
\left\{t^{\sigma}_{G}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
in time
\mathcal{O}(h^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)}))
.

Next, we prove
Lemma

4.5
using
Claim

12
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.5
using
Claim

12
.

We are given a graph
G
, a coloring
\varphi
and a set
S
. Our goal is to compute
\left\{t^{k}_{\varphi}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
using
Claim

12
.
First, we remove all monochromatic edges in
G
, as none of those edges participates in any
\varphi
-colorful copy
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}
.
Then, we build a set of auxiliary graphs
\mathcal{H}
.
For each auxiliary graph
H_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
, we compute
\left\{t_{H_{x}}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
using
Claim

12
. We then claim that
\sum_{H_{x}\in\mathcal{H}}t_{H_{x}}(v)=t_{\varphi}^{k}(v)
. The auxiliary graphs are defined as follows.
For a string
x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{h}
, we define a graph
H_{x}
, which is an induced subgraph of
G
.
For
i\in[h]
, we define a set
U^{x}_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle U_{i}^{x}\triangleq\begin{cases}\varphi^{-1}(i)\cap S,&\text{if }x_{i}=1\\
\varphi^{-1}(i)\setminus S,&\text{if }x_{i}=0\end{cases}

Let
V(H_{x})=\bigcup_{i\in[h]}U_{i}^{x}
and
H_{x}=G[V(H_{x})]
.
Let
w(x)
denote the hamming weight of a binary string.
Let
\mathcal{X}_{\ell}\triangleq\left\{x\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{h}\mid w(x)=\ell\right\}
.
The set of auxiliary graphs is
\mathcal{H}=\left\{H_{x}\mid x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}\right\}
.
Note that
\left\lvert\mathcal{H}\right\rvert\leq 2^{h}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
This completes the description of the algorithm specified in
Lemma

4.5
. We proceed with proving its correctness, and then we analyze its running time.

Fix some auxiliary graph
H_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
.
We write
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})
to denote the number of
\varphi
-colorful copies in
H_{x}
that
v
participates in. We also use
t_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})=\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})\right\rvert
.
We first claim that it suffices to compute
\left\{t_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})\right\}_{v\in V}
for every
H_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
, as

\displaystyle t^{k}_{\varphi}(v)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}t_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})\;.

To show that, we need to show two properties:

(i)

every
C\in\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})
is also in
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}(v)
, and

(ii)

every
C\in\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}(v)
is in
\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})
for a unique
x
.

To see the first, note that every
\varphi
-colorful
C
in
H_{x}
must satisfy
\left\lvert C\cap S\right\rvert=k
;
If it is
\varphi
-colorful, it intersects each color class exactly once.
By the construction of
H_{x}
, there are exactly
k
color classes which are contained in
S
while the other color classes are disjoint from
S
.
Therefore, every copy
C
on the right side of the equation, appears also on the left side of the equation. In other words,
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}(v)\supseteq\bigcup_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})
.

We prove the second property.
Fix
C\in\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\varphi}(v)
, and denote its vertices by
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{h})
, where
\varphi(v_{i})=i
for every
i\in[h]
.
To see that every
C
is
\varphi
-colorful for at least one
x
,
define
\hat{x}
as follows. For
i\in[h]
, let
\hat{x_{i}}
be
1
if
v_{i}\in S
, and otherwise let
\hat{x_{i}}
be
0
.

C
is clearly
\varphi
-colorful in
H_{\hat{x}}
.
To see that
C
is not
\varphi
-colorful in
H_{x^{\prime}}
for any
x^{\prime}\neq\hat{x}
, let
j
be the first index in which
x^{\prime}\neq\hat{x}
. If
C
is also
\varphi
-colorful in
H_{x^{\prime}}
, then
v_{j}
is both in
S
and not in
S
which is a contradiction.

We are left with explaining how to compute
\left\{t_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})\right\}_{v\in V}
for every
H_{x}\in\mathcal{H}
, which we do by using
Claim

12
.
Let
\Pi_{h}
denote the set of all permutations
\pi:[h]\to[h]
.
For each permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{h}
, define an ordering
\sigma(\pi)=\left(\varphi^{-1}(\pi(1)),\ldots,\varphi^{-1}(\pi(h))\right)
, and compute
\left\{t_{H_{x}}^{\sigma(\pi)}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
using
Claim

12
.
To complete the proof, we need to show that for every
v
, and every
x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}
we have

\displaystyle 2h\cdot t_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{h}}t_{H_{x}}^{\sigma(\pi)}(v)\;.

Fix
H_{x}
, a vertex
v
, and a cycle
C=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{h})\in\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})
, with
\varphi(v_{i})=i
for all
i\in[h]
.
We show that there are exactly
2h
permutations in
\Pi_{h}
for which
C\in\mathcal{C}_{H_{x}}^{\sigma(\pi)}(v)
. Take any permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{h}
. It defines a permutation
\pi^{\prime}:V(C)\to V(C)
as follows. The vertex colored by the
i
-th color, is mapped to the vertex colored by the
\pi(i)
-th color.

C
will be in
\mathcal{C}_{H_{x}}^{\sigma(\pi)}(v)
if and only if
\pi^{\prime}
is an isomorphism on
C
. That is, for every edge
(u,v)
in
C
, the edge
(\pi^{\prime}(u),\pi^{\prime}(v))
is also an edge in
C
. This set of automorphism of an
h
-cycle has
2h
elements, which consist of a
shift
permutations, and its composition with itself
i
times for any
1\leq i\leq h
, and a
reflection
permutation, which maps the vertex in the
i
-th position to the
h+1-i
-th position. The composition of each of the shift permutation with a reflection permutation forms another set of
h
different permutation, making the total number of permutation that belong to the set of automorphism of
C
equal to exactly
2h
.
This completes the correctness of the lemma.

Running Time.

For a given
x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}
and a specific permutation
\pi\in\Pi_{h}
, the computation of
\left\{t_{H_{x}}^{\sigma(\pi)}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
as per

12
requires
\mathcal{O}(h^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)}))
time. Consequently, determining
\left\{t_{\varphi}(v;H_{x})\right\}_{v\in V}
demands
\mathcal{O}(h!\cdot h^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)}))
time as
\left\lvert\Pi_{h}\right\rvert=h
.
Extending this to all

leads to a computational time of
\mathcal{O}((h!)^{2}\cdot h^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)}))
, as

.
Thus, the computation of
\left\{t^{k}_{\varphi}(v)\right\}_{v\in V}
also requires
\mathcal{O}((h!)^{2}\cdot h^{2}\cdot\mathsf{MM}(U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)}))

\mathsf{MM}(U_{(1)},U_{(2)},U_{(h)})\leq\mathsf{MM}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{h})
.
∎

We now prove
Claim

12
, which is the final missing piece for the proof of

.

Proof of
Claim

12
.
We assume without loss of generality that

Implementing the
\mathsf{Find}\mhyphen\mathsf{Heavy}

In this section, we prove the first part of

Theorem 5.1
.

is an

An algorithm for

\Lambda

\Lambda
-heavy vertices, which contains no
\Lambda/{\left(\log n\right)^{h^{2}}}

Our algorithm works as follows. The algorithm selects a vector

for

i

.
We emphasize that not all vertices are kept with the same probability.
Let

-colorful copy of

in

is
P
-

-discovered vertices over

For every vertex

denote the probability that

is

P
-discovered.
The randomness is taken over the choice of the coloring and the sampling of vertices. We call this experiment the
P
-discovery experiment
.

We repeat this
P
-discovery experiment
k
times. If

is
P
-discovered more than
k\tau
times, where
\tau
is a threshold that we set later, then
P
adds

to the set of heavy vertices. In this case, we say that

is
P
-added

The final step of our algorithm is to choose a vector

, or rather a set of vectors

, and then to perform the
P
-discovery experiment with each vector in the set
k
times. Before specifying how we should choose
\mathcal{P}
,
k
, and

1.

Each vector

induces a graph

4.5

-discovered vertices, i.e., vertices that are part of
\varphi
-colorful copy of

2.

Each vertex with

has at least one vector

that
P
-adds

3.

For any vertex

and any vector

, with high probability,

-added to the heavy vertex set.

4.

The set
\mathcal{P}
has only

vectors, allowing us to avoid repeating this experiment too many times.

Next, we give an explicit definition of the set of all vectors that our algorithm uses. This set is built in the following way. Take all the vectors
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{h})\in[0,1]^{h}
that satisfy
\prod_{i\in[h]}p_{i}\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{1}{\Lambda})

To do that, we only keep vectors
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{h})\in[0,1]^{h}

for

.

Definition 9

(Vector Set:
\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
)
.

For a non-negative real

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{(p_{1},\ldots,p_{h})\in[0,1]^{h}\mid\forall i\in[h]\;,\quad p_{i}\in\left\{2^{-j}\mid 0\leq j\leq\log(\Lambda)+1\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{(p_{1},\ldots,p_{h})\in[0,1]^{h}\mid\prod_{i=1}^{h}p_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\right\}

The rest of the section is dedicated to proving that this set satisfies Properties (1)–(3).
We first prove the first property, which states that for each

(2)

v

t_{G}(v)\geq\Lambda\cdot(2h\log(n))^{(h-1)^{2}}\cdot 2/s
, where
s\triangleq\frac{1}{h^{h}}
.
Then, there exists a vector
P\in\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
such that
v[P]\geq(1-1/e)^{h-1}\cdot s
.

We prove the proposition by induction on
h
, for which the base case is
h=3
.

The intuition for the proof is as follows. Consider the base case of triangles. Let
V_{i}
denote the
i
-th color class.
Consider finding the heavy vertices in the first color class.
Consider all vectors
P_{i}=(1,2^{-i},\frac{2^{i}}{\Lambda})
, for
0\leq i\leq\log(\Lambda)+1
. These vectors form a subset of
\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
.
Fix some
\Lambda
-heavy vertex
v\in V_{1}
.
We want to prove that for at least one
i\in[\log(\Lambda)]
we have
v[P_{i}]\geq\frac{q(1-1/e)^{2}}{2}
.
Our choice for the vectors is designed to deal with the following two extreme cases.
The first case is that every
C\in\mathcal{C}_{\varphi}(v)
intersects one specific vertex
u\in V_{2}
. For this case, the vector
P_{0}=(1,1,\frac{1}{\Lambda})
is the right choice for discovering
v
because it maximizes the probability we hit
u
and a common neighbor of
v
and
u
, under the constraint that the sampling probability
p_{1}\cdot p_{2}\cdot p_{3}\leq 1/\Lambda
.
The second case is that among each of
V_{2}\cap N(v)
and
V_{3}\cap N(v)
, there are
\sqrt{\Lambda}
vertices that
connected as a complete bipartite graph (contained in
V_{2}\times V_{3}
).
For this case, the vector
P_{i}
for
i=\log(\Lambda)/2
is the right choice for discovering
v
, because
P_{i}=(1,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Lambda}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Lambda}})
.

For a larger
h
, the “hard” case is the following generalization of the above. From each color class
V_{i}
for
i\in\{3,\dots,h\}
we take
k=\Lambda^{1/(h-2)}
vertices and connect them by a complete
(h-2)
-partite graph. We then take
v\in V_{1}
and
u\in V_{2}
, we add an edge between
v
and
u
, and we connect
v
to all the aforementioned
k
vertices from
V_{h}
and we connect
u
to all the aforementioned
k
vertices from
V_{3}
. The vector
(1,1,\frac{1}{k},\dots,\frac{1}{k})
is the right choice for this case.

Roughly speaking, we show the set
\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
gradually shifts from handling one extreme case to another, and therefore “covers” all cases in between, which are the different ways to split vertices in
h
-cycles into
h
pieces whose product of sizes is
\Lambda
.

We need a final technical step before presenting the proof.
First, we construct an
h
-partite graph which is easier to work with.
For this, we sample uniform
h
coloring of the vertices of
G
. For
i\in[h]
let
V_{i}
denote the set of vertices colored in the
i
-th color.
For every
i\in[h]
we keep only edges between the vertices of
V_{i}
and
V_{i+1\mod h}
, and direct those edges from
V_{i}
to
V_{i+1}
.
Let
G_{\varphi}
denote the obtained directed graph.
We emphasize that
t_{G_{\varphi}}(v)\leq t_{\varphi}(v)
, as the latter counts all colorful cycles in which
v
participates in, whereas the former counts only colorful cycles with edges between
V_{i}
and
V_{i+1}
in which
v
participates in.
We prove that a heavy vertex will be
P
-discovered on
G_{\varphi}
, with probability at least
\Omega\left(1\right)
, by some
P\in\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
, whereas light vertices will only be discovered with probability
\mathcal{O}(1/\log n)
. In other words, since the gap between the heavy and light vertices is sufficiently big, we can still distinguish between the two in
G_{\varphi}
.

The rest of this section is dedicated to proving the following proposition

Lemma 5.4
.

Fix a vertex
v
, and an
h
-coloring of the vertices of
G

Suppose

Then, there exists a vector

such that the probability that
v
gets
P
-discovered in

is at least
(1-1/e)^{h-1}
.

Proof of
Lemma

.
We prove

5.4
by induction on

.
We start with the base case, i.e.,

.
We fix some
h
-coloring
\varphi
.
Recall that we denote the
i
-th color class by

.
Consider a vertex
v\in V_{1}
with
t_{G_{\varphi}}(v)\geq\Lambda\cdot(2h\log(n))^{4}
.
To simplify the notation,
we use
G^{\prime}=G_{\varphi}
.
We will prove that there exists a vector
P\in\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
, such that the probability that the vertex
v
is
P
-discovered over
G^{\prime}
is at least
(1-1/e)^{h-1}=(1-1/e)^{2}
.
Let
K_{0}=\log(\Lambda)+1
.
We consider a subset of vectors in
\mathsf{Product}_{h}(\Lambda)
of the form
P_{i}=(1,p_{i},q_{i})
, for

, where
p_{i}\triangleq 2^{-i}\;,q_{i}\triangleq\tfrac{2^{i}}{\Lambda}
.

We partition

into classes as follows. For

, let

\displaystyle Q_{k}=\left\{u\in V_{2}\mid\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(v)\cap\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(u)\right\rvert\in\left[\frac{1}{q_{k}},\frac{2}{q_{k}}\right)\right\}\;,

\displaystyle Q_{0}=\left\{u\in V_{2}\mid\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(v)\cap\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(u)\right\rvert\geq 1/q_{0}\right\}\;.

In words, the set

consist of all vertices

, such that the number of
3
-cycles in
G^{\prime}
that contain both
v
and
u
is at least
1/q_{0}=\Lambda
, and for
k>0
, we have that

if and only if the number of
3
-cycles in

that contain both

and

is at least
1/q_{k}=\Lambda/2^{i}
and less than
2/q_{k}=\Lambda/{2^{i-1}}
.
We claim that there exists

such that
\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\geq 1/p_{k}
, which we will later prove.
Given that this holds, we show that for such
k
, we have
v[P_{k}]\geq(1-1/e)^{2}
.

Fix
k\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,K_{0}\right\}
where
\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\geq 1/p_{k}
.
For any non-empty subset
S\subseteq Q_{k}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)
denote the event that
\left\{Q_{k}\cap V_{2}[p_{k}]=S\right\}
.
That is,
\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)
denotes the event that during the
P_{k}
-discovery experiment, the set of vertices that were sampled from
V_{2}\cap Q_{k}
is exactly the set
S
.
Let
R(S)
denote the subset of vertices in
V_{3}
which participate in a
3
-cycle (in
G^{\prime}
) that contains
v
and some additional vertex from
S
.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)
denote the event that
\left\{V_{3}[q_{k}]\cap R(S)\neq\emptyset\right\}
.
That is,
\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)
denotes the event that during the
P_{k}
-discovery experiment, the set of vertices that were sampled from
V_{3}\cap R(S)
is not empty.
We show that

\displaystyle v[P_{k}]\geq\sum_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]\geq(1-1/e)^{2}\;.

(3)

For every fixed non-empty
S\subseteq Q_{k}
, the events
\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)
are independent, since
\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)
addresses sampling vertices from
V_{2}
, while
\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)
addresses sampling vertices from
V_{3}
, where the two samples are independent of each other.
Also note that
\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)
is contained in the event that

is
P_{k}
-discovered, and since the events
\left\{\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\right\}_{S\subseteq Q_{k}}
are pairwise disjoint, so are the events
\left\{\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right\}_{S\subseteq Q_{k}}
. Therefore, the event that

is
P_{k}
-discovered, contains the union of the following pairwise disjoint events
\{\bigcup_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\}
.
We get

\displaystyle v[P_{k}]

\displaystyle\geq{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\bigcup_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\right]{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]\;.

To complete the proof, we need to show that

1.

\sum_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\right]\geq 1-1/e
, and

2.

for every non-empty
S\subseteq Q_{k}
, we have
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]\geq 1-1/e
.

The first claim follows as

\displaystyle\sum_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq Q_{k}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\right]={\mathrm{Pr}}\left[Q_{k}[p_{k}]\neq\emptyset\right]=1-(1-p_{k})^{\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert}\geq 1-1/e\;,

where the inequalities hold for the following reasons. The first two equalities follows from definition, and the last inequality follows from the assumption that
\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\geq 1/p_{k}
.

The second claim follows as every non-empty subset

satisfies

.
To see this, fix some vertex

, and
\left\lvert R(u)\right\rvert\geq 1/q_{k}

.
Therefore, for any such

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]={\mathrm{Pr}}\left[R(S)[q_{k}]\neq\emptyset\right]=1-(1-q_{k})^{\left\lvert R(S)\right\rvert}\geq 1-(1-q_{k})^{1/q_{k}}\geq 1-1/e\;.

This completes the proof of
Equation

3
.

To complete the proof of the induction base, it remains to show that our claim indeed holds, i.e., that there exists

such that
\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\geq 1/p_{k}
.
Assume towards a contradiction that this is not the case, that is, that

. Note that this implies that

is empty.
Then,

\displaystyle t_{G^{\prime}}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in[V_{2}]}\left\lvert\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(v)\cap\mathcal{C}_{G^{\prime}}(u)\right\rvert

\displaystyle<\sum_{u\in[V_{2}]}\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{0}}\chi_{\left\{u\in Q_{k}\right\}}\cdot\frac{2}{q_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{0}}\sum_{u\in[V_{2}]}\chi_{\left\{u\in Q_{k}\right\}}\cdot\frac{2}{q_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{0}}\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\cdot\frac{2}{q_{k}}

Because we assumed towards a contradiction that for every
k\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,K_{0}\right\}
we have

, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{0}}\left\lvert Q_{k}\right\rvert\cdot\frac{2}{q_{k}}<\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{0}}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\cdot\frac{2}{q_{k}}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq K_{0}}2\Lambda=2K_{0}\cdot\Lambda\;.

This is a contradiction, as
2K_{0}\cdot\Lambda\leq\Lambda\cdot(2h\log(n))^{4}\geq t_{G_{\varphi}}(v)
.
This completes the proof of the base case.

(4)

We’ll prove a stronger claim, which generalizes

.

\displaystyle v[Y^{\prime}]\geq\sum_{S:\emptyset\subsetneq S\subseteq S_{Y}}{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{E}_{1}(S)\cap\mathcal{E}_{2}(S)\right]\geq\alpha\cdot{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[S_{Y}[p_{h}]\neq\emptyset\right]\;.

6
Fine-grained Lower Bound

A popular hardness hypothesis in fine-grained complexity is that detecting a triangle in an
n
node graph requires
n^{\omega-o(1)}
time (see e.g.
[
Vas18
]
). The motivation behind the hypothesis is that the only known subcubic time algorithms for triangle detection use fast matrix multiplication. In fact, it is known that triangle detection and
Boolean
matrix multiplication are subcubically equivalent
[
VW18
]
: any truly subcubic time algorithm for one problem implies a truly subcubic time algorithm for the other.

Suppose now that one is given a tripartite graph whose three partitions have sizes
n,n^{a},n^{b}
respectively. Then the fastest known algorithm for finding a triangle in such a graph is again a reduction to fast matrix multiplication, but in this case it is to
rectangular
matrix multiplication:
n\times n^{a}
by
n^{a}\times n^{b}
.

We will shortly see that a faster algorithm would imply (for instance) that
k
-cliques can be detected faster and would be an important breakthrough.
The fastest known algorithm for
k
-clique detection for any constant
k\geq 3
is a reduction (see
[
NP85
,
Vas18
]
) to detecting a triangle in a tripartite graph with partition sizes
n^{\lfloor k/3\rfloor},n^{\lceil k/3\rceil}
and
n^{k-\lceil k/3\rceil-\lfloor k/3\rfloor}
.

Thus, the fastest running time for
k
-clique is
\mathsf{MM}(n^{\lfloor k/3\rfloor},n^{\lceil k/3\rceil},n^{k-\lceil k/3\rceil-\lfloor k/3\rfloor})
.
In fact, this running time is conjectured to be optimal in several papers (see e.g.
[
ABV18
,
BT17
,
BW17
,
DMVX23
]
). This conjecture is known as the
k
-Clique Hypothesis.

Let’s look at
k
of the form
k=3p-1
for integer
p\geq 2
. Then
k
-clique detection in
N
-node graphs reduces to triangle detection in a tripartite graph with parts of sizes
N^{p},N^{p-1},N^{p}
. Letting
n=N^{p}
, the sizes are
n,n^{1-1/p},n
. Thus, if triangle detection in an unbalanced tripartite graph with parts of size
n,n^{1-1/p},n
can be solved in
\mathsf{MM}(n,n^{1-1/p},n)^{1-\delta}
time for some
\delta>0
, then the
k
-Clique Hypothesis for
k=3p-1
would be false.

This motivates the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 1
.

Let
G
be a tripartite graph with vertex set
A\sqcup B\sqcup C
,
where
\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert C\right\rvert=n
and
\left\lvert B\right\rvert=n^{b}
for
b\leq 1
.
Any randomized algorithm for triangle detection in
G
, in the word-RAM with
O(\log n)
bit words needs

\mathsf{MM}(n,n^{b},n)^{1-o(1)}

time.

For
b=1-1/p
for integer
p\geq 2
the hypothesis is implied by the
k
-Clique Hypothesis, as mentioned above. When
b\leq\alpha
, the hypothesis is actually true since by definition in this case
\mathsf{MM}(n,n^{b},n)\neq n^{2+o(1)}
, and it is not hard to show that to determine whether a tripartite graph with parts of sizes
n,n^{b},n
contains a triangle, any (even randomized) algorithm needs
\Omega(n^{2})
time.

A related hypothesis to Hypothesis
1
was considered by Bringmann and Carmeli
[
BC22
]
who proposed that triangle detection in a tripartite graph with partition sizes
n,n^{a},n^{a}
for
a\leq 1
requires
\omega(n^{1+a})
time. Notably, if
\omega=2
, the respective unbalanced matrix multiplication time is
\mathsf{MM}(n,n^{a},n^{a})=n^{1+a+o(1)}
. The hypothesis of
[
BC22
]
states that not only is
\mathsf{MM}(n,n^{a},n^{a})
time needed, but that the extra
+o(1)
factor in the exponent is necessary when
\omega=2
.

Other related unbalanced versions of triangle detection appear for instance in
[
KV20
]
and
[
VX20
]
, where the tripartite graph is unbalanced not only in the partition sizes but in the number of edges between the pairs of partitions.

We reduce the above unbalanced triangle detection problem for any choice of
b
to the problem of approximating the number of triangles in an
n
node graph that contains
0
triangles if the unbalanced instance had
0
triangles and at least
n^{1-b}
triangles if the unbalanced instance had at least one triangle.

Let
t=n^{1-b}
.
We build a new graph
G^{\prime}
with vertex set
A\cup B^{\prime}\cup C

where

\displaystyle B^{\prime}\triangleq

\displaystyle\left\{(x,i)\mid x\in B\wedge i\in[t]\right\}

\displaystyle E(G^{\prime})\triangleq

\displaystyle E(A,C)\cup\left\{(v,(x,i))\mid v\in A\cup C\;\wedge(x,i)\in B^{\prime}\wedge(v,b)\in E(G)\right\}\;.

In words,
B^{\prime}
contains
t
copies of each vertex
x\in B
, and
E(G^{\prime})
contains all edges between a pair of vertices in
A,C
, and also an edge
(u,(x,i))
for every
u\in A\cup C
if
(u,x)\in E(G)
.
Constructing
G^{\prime}
takes
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time and
G^{\prime}
has
O(n)
vertices.

Claim 15
.

If
G
has no triangles, then neither does
G^{\prime}
. If

G
contains a triangle then
G^{\prime}
contains
\geq t
triangles.

Proof.
If
G
has a triangle, it must be of the form
(a,x,c)\in A\times B\times C
. Such a triangle creates
t
new triangles in
G^{\prime}
, each of the form

(a,(x,i),c)
in
G^{\prime}
, showing
G^{\prime}
has
t
triangles.
For the other direction, note that any triangle
(a,(x,i),c)
in
G^{\prime}
, implies that
(a,x,c)
is a triangle in
G
.
∎Any algorithm that can obtain an estimate
t^{\prime}
of the number of triangles
t
in
G
such that
(1-\varepsilon)t\leq t^{\prime}\leq(1+\varepsilon)t
can distinguish between graphs with no triangles and graphs with at least
t
triangles. Thus we obtain:

Corollary 2
.

Under Hypothesis 1, any randomized algorithm that can
(1\pm\varepsilon)
-approximate the number of triangles in a graph with
t
triangles requires
\mathsf{MM}(n,n/t,n)^{1-o(1)}
time.

Generalizing for Directed and Odd Cycles

We now prove our hardness result for

Assume

1

Proof.
Let

Given a graph

\left\lvert A\right\rvert=\left\lvert C\right\rvert=n

We first define a new set of vertices

obtained by duplicating each vertex in

\ell
times.
We then duplicate
B^{\prime}

h-2
times. We denote the copies by
\mathcal{B}\triangleq B_{1}^{\prime},\dots,B_{h-2}^{\prime}

We also write

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\triangleq\left\{(b,i,j)\mid b\in B\wedge i\in[\ell]\wedge j\in[h-2]\right\}\;.

That is, for any

1.

2.

.

3.

Some Matrix Multiplication Tools

Theorem A.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0,a\geq 0,a+\varepsilon\leq 1
. If
\omega(1,1,a)=r
, then
\omega(1,1-\varepsilon,a+

\varepsilon)\leq r
.

Proof.
Consider multiplying matrices of dimension
n\times n^{1-\varepsilon}
by matrices of dimension
n^{1-\varepsilon}\times n^{a+\varepsilon}
.
Define
N=n^{\varepsilon/(1-a)}
. This is possible since
a<1,\varepsilon>0
.
Further define
q=(1-a-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\geq 0
. This is also fine because
a+\varepsilon\leq 1
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Note that these parameters give that
n=N^{q+1}
.
Then

\displaystyle\left\langle n,n^{1-\varepsilon},n^{a+\varepsilon}\right\rangle=\left\langle N^{q+1},N^{q+a},N^{aq+1}\right\rangle=\left\langle N^{q},N^{q},N^{aq}\right\rangle\otimes\left\langle N,N^{a},N\right\rangle.

Thus the rank of
\left\langle n,n^{1-\varepsilon},n^{a+\varepsilon}\right\rangle
is at most the product of the ranks of
\left\langle N^{q},N^{q},N^{aq}\right\rangle
and
\left\langle N,N^{a},N\right\rangle
.

If
\omega(1,a,1)=r
, then
\omega(1,1,a)=r
as well.
Thus,
\operatorname{Rank}\left(\left\langle N^{q},N^{q},N^{aq}\right\rangle\right)\leq

N^{qr+o(1)}
and
\operatorname{Rank}\left(\left\langle N,N^{a},N\right\rangle\right)\leq N^{r+o(1)}
. Hence,
\operatorname{Rank}\left(\left\langle n,n^{1-\varepsilon},n^{a+\varepsilon}\right\rangle\right)\leq

N^{(q+1)r+o(1)}=n^{r+o(1)}
.
Thus
\omega(1,1-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon)\leq r
.

The Median Trick

Here we prove the median trick as used in the proof of
Lemmas

4.2
and
1
.

Claim B.1
.

Let
Y
be a random variable, and let
[a,b]
be some interval, where
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[Y\in[a,b]\right]\geq 2/3
.
Define
Z
to be the median of
r=400\log n
independent samples of
Y
. Then,
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[Z\in[a,b]\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{5}}
.

Proof.
Let
Y_{i}
be the
i
-th sample. We define an indicator random variable
X_{i}
to be
1
if
Y_{i}\in[a,b]
for
i\in[r]
.
Let
X=\sum_{i\in[r]}X_{i}
.
We claim that if more than half of the samples fall inside the interval
[a,b]
then so is the median of all samples. In other words, the rest of the samples do not affect the median.
Therefore,
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[Z\in[a,b]\right]\geq{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[X>\frac{r}{2}\right]
.
We lower bound the probability that
{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[X>\frac{r}{2}\right]
.
Note that
X
is the sum of
r
independent

-Bernoulli random variables with
p\geq 2/3
. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[X\leq\frac{r}{2}\right]\leq{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}\left({r},{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\leq\frac{r}{2}\right]={\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}\left({r},{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\leq\frac{2r}{3}\cdot(1-\frac{1}{4})\right]\;,

and by the following version of Chernoff’s inequality:

{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[X\leq(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]}{2}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Pr}}\left[\mathsf{Bin}\left({r},{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\leq\frac{2r}{3}\cdot(1-\frac{1}{4})\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{r}{48}\right)\leq\exp\left(-8\log n\right)\leq n^{-5}\;.

∎
