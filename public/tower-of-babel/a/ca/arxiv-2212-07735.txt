Higher-order Games with Dependent Types Journal: Theoretical Computer Science
Martín Escardó, Paulo Oliva

Abstract
In previous work on higher-order games, we accounted for finite games of unbounded length by working with continuous outcome functions, which carry implicit game trees. In this work we make such trees explicit. We use concepts from dependent type theory to capture history-dependent games, where the set of available moves at a given position in the game depends on the moves played up to that point. In particular, games are modelled by a W W -type, which is essentially the same type used by Aczel to model constructive Zermelo-Frankel set theory (CZF). We have also implemented all our definitions, constructions, results and proofs in the dependently-typed programming language Agda, which, in particular, allows us to run concrete examples of computations of optimal strategies, that is, strategies in subgame perfect equillibrium.

1
Introduction

Games of perfect information are normally modelled in economics and game theory via
maximising
players. At the end of each play in the game all players receive a payoff, so that players pick their moves in order to maximise their final payoff. In zero-sum games, usually one considers two players and a single outcome, where one player is trying to maximise the outcome, while the other tries to minimise it.

Higher-order games
[
10
,
12
]
have been introduced as a generalisation of both of these frameworks, where the outcome type of the game can have an arbitrary type
R
, and the goal of each player at a round where
X
is the set of available moves is defined via generalised
quantifiers
, which are higher-order functions of type
(X\to R)\to R
.

Standard examples of sequential games of perfect information, such as Tic-Tac-Toe, checkers and chess, can be modelled by taking
R
to be the linearly ordered set
\{-1<0<1\}
, so that the two players correspond to the quantifiers
\max,\min\colon(X\to R)\to R
(see
[
11
]
). This setup is rather general, in particular allowing to treat sequential games of unbounded length
[
13
]
.

In this paper we account for history-dependent sequential games of perfect information of unbounded length, where the set of available moves at a given position in the game depends on the moves played up to that point. This is achieved by modelling game trees as a certain type of well-founded
dependent type trees
. In previous work this was instead done by considering either a fixed game length, or games of countable length with a continuous outcome function
[
10
]
. In fact, every continuous function carries an implicit well-founded tree, and this goes back to Brouwer. Here we work with explicitly given well-founded trees instead.

We make use of concepts from
dependent type theory

[
5
]
in order to formalise this, and we have implemented all our definitions, results and proofs in Agda
[
3
]
, which is both a programming language and a proof assistant based on dependent type theory.

Related work

Our type of trees is essentially the same as that used by Leversha
[
20
]
to define ordinals, and by Aczel
[
2
]
to model constructive Zermelo-Frankel set theory (CZF). The precise relationship is discussed in Section
5
.

As in previous work
[
10
]
, we think of backward induction in terms of a certain product of selection functions (Definition
4.13
below). The product of selection functions is also a manifestation of bar recursion
[
14
]
, used to interpret classical proofs in analysis
[
8
,
9
]
, and can be used to give computational content to the Tychonoff Theorem from topology
[
7
]
. See our survey paper
[
11
]
where the versatility of the product of selection functions is discussed in further detail.

Several other recent papers also explore the connection between selection functions and computational interpretations of classical analysis, e.g. Powell
[
21
,
22
]
.

Ghani, Hedges, Winschel, and Zahn
[
17
]
introduce a notion of
open game
, which is compositional and hence allows to build larger games from smaller blocks, where quantifiers are generalised to best-response functions.

Hartmann and Gibbons
[
18
]
apply selection functions to algorithm design by considering modified notions of (history dependent) products of selection functions, which allows them to design greedy algorithms in a modular way.

Abadi and Plotkin
[
1
]
consider programming languages for describing systems in terms of choices and their resulting costs and rewards, and use the selection monad to define computationally adequate denotational semantics for them.

2
Dependent type trees

We assume some familiarity with dependent type theory
[
5
]
. However, we reason informally, but rigorously, as in the book
[
23
]
. We write the type
\Pi(x:X),A(x)
as
(x:X)\to A(x)
. The elements of this types are functions that map elements
x:X
to values in
A(x)
. We write the type
\Sigma(x:X),A(x)
as
(x:X)\times A(x)
. The elements of this type are (dependent) pairs
(x,y)
with
x:X
and
y:A(x)
.
We work with a universe
\operatorname{Type}
, which is a large type of small types closed under the type theoretic operations.

We consider sequential game plays of the form
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
where the type of the second move
x_{1}
depends on the first move
x_{0}
, and the type of the third move
x_{2}
depends on the first and second moves
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, and so on. In order to specify such a dependent sequence of moves, we first define dependent-type trees.

Definition 2.1
((Dependent) type trees)

We define the type
\operatorname{Tree}
of
dependent type trees
, or just
type trees
for short, by induction as follows

1.

There is an empty type tree, denoted by
[]
.

2.

If
X
is a type and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}\colon X\to\operatorname{Tree}
is a family of trees indexed by
X
, then there is a type tree with root
X
and subtrees
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
for each
x:X
, denoted by
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
. We think of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
as a
forest
.

So notice that
(-)\operatorname{::}(-)
is a function of type

(X:\operatorname{Type})\times(X\to\operatorname{Tree})\to\operatorname{Tree}.

We adopt the following notational conventions:

1.

The variable
X
ranges over types.

2.

The variable
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
ranges over type trees.

3.

The variable
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
ranges over type forests.

Example 2.2
(Tic-Tac-Toe type tree)

In Tic-Tac-Toe, the types of available moves at each round form a dependent type tree. The root of the tree is the type of the nine positions on the grid. Depending on the move of the first player, the second player then chooses from the type of the eight remaining available positions, and so on. Once one of the players wins or all the positions are filled then the next player does not have any further available move, arriving at an empty subtree.

A dependent sequence of moves as discussed above is given by a path in a dependent type tree.

Definition 2.3
(Paths)

We define the type
\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
of paths on a type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
by induction on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
as follows:

1.

There is one path in the empty tree
[]
, which we denote by
\operatorname{\langle\rangle}
.

2.

For every type
X
, every forest
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}\colon X\to\operatorname{Tree}
, every
x:X
and every path
\vec{x}
in
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
, there is a path
x\operatorname{::}\vec{x}
in the tree
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
. The elements
x
and
\vec{x}
are called the
head
and
tail
of the path
x\operatorname{::}\vec{x}
.

The above path constructors have the following types:

1.

\operatorname{\langle\rangle}:\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}\,[]
.

2.

(-)\operatorname{::}(-):(x:X)\times\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))\to\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f})
.

We let the variable
\vec{x}
range over paths.

Example 2.4
(Tic-Tac-Toe paths)

The paths in the Tic-Tac-Toe tree consists of exactly the sequences of legal moves that end with one of the players winning or a draw.

We will need to add different structures to the nodes of game trees, including a type of quantifiers (Section
3.1
), a type of selection functions (Section
4.1
) and move choices (Section
4
). We accomplish this with a type of structured trees defined as follows, with a parameter
\operatorname{S}
for the structure, where structure on a type
X
is taken to be a type
\operatorname{S}X

Definition 2.5
(Trees with structure)

Given structure
\operatorname{S}:\operatorname{Type}\to\operatorname{Type}
and a tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{Tree}
we define a new type of structured trees

\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{S},X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})

by induction on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
as follows:

1.

There is an empty structured tree

[]:\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{S},[]).

2.

If
X
is a type,
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}\colon X\to\operatorname{Tree}
is a forest and

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}X,

\displaystyle s^{\scriptscriptstyle f}

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle(x:X)\to\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{S},X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)),

then there is a structured tree

s\operatorname{::}s^{\scriptscriptstyle f}:\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{S},X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f})

with root
X
equipped with
s:\operatorname{S}X
and subtrees
s^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
for
x:X
.

Notice that in the notation
s\operatorname{::}s^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
we leave both types
X
and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
implicit, and that the forest
s^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
is indexed by the type
X
rather than the type
\operatorname{S}X
. Although we use the same notation
(-)\operatorname{::}(-)
, the type of type trees is of course not the same as that of structured type trees, and the latter depends on the former:

\mathrm{structure}:(\operatorname{Type}\to\operatorname{Type})\times\operatorname{Tree}\to\operatorname{Type}.

Hence if
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is a type tree, then
\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{S},X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
is the type of structured type trees over
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
. So for
one
type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{Tree}
there are, in general, many structured type trees of type
\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{S},X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
.

3
Higher-order games with dependent types

We start by recalling the notion of a
quantifier
, needed for the definition of a higher-order game (see
[
10
]
).
For the remainder of the paper we fix a type
R
of game outcomes. For example, for Tic-Tac-Toe, as discussed in the Introduction, we take
R=\{-1,0,1\}
, but for other games we can choose
R
to be e.g. the type of real numbers or the type of booleans.

3.1
Quantifiers

Let
\operatorname{K}X
be an abbreviation for the type
(X\to R)\to R
, with
R
arbitrary but fixed, as described above.

Definition 3.1
(Quantifiers)

We call functions of type
\operatorname{K}X

quantifiers
.

If
R
is the type of booleans, the elements of the function type
\operatorname{K}X
include the standard existential and universal quantifiers, but we also allow numerical quantifiers, as in the following example.

Example 3.2
(
\min
and
\max
)

The simplest non-standard examples of quantifiers are the
\min,\max\colon(X\to R)\to R
functions, which we will use in our Tic-Tac-Toe running example. The idea is that the type of outcomes
R
will the linearly ordered type with three values
-1<0<1
, so that given a type of available moves
X
, the quantifiers select the preferred outcome given a local outcome function
X\to R
.

Definition 3.3
(Quantifier tree)

Given a type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, we define the type of
quantifier trees over
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
by

\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{K},X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}).

We adopt the following notational conventions:

1.

The variable
\phi
ranges over the type
\operatorname{K}X
of quantifiers.

2.

The variable
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
ranges over the type
\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
of quantifier trees.

3.

The variable
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
ranges over quantifier forests
(x\colon X)\to\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))
.

Example 3.4
(Tic-Tac-Toe quantifier tree)

We associate a quantifier tree to the Tic-Tac-Toe type tree of Example
2.2
by alternating the
\min
and
\max
quantifiers depending on the level of the tree. Assuming that player X moves first, and that the outcome
-1
indicates that player X has won, the root of the quantifier tree is the
\min
quantifier.

Definition 3.5
(Binary dependent product of quantifiers
[
10
]
)

Given a quantifier

\phi:\operatorname{K}X

on a type
X
and given a family
Y
of types indexed by
X
, and a family of quantifiers

\gamma\colon(x:X)\to\operatorname{K}(Y(x)),

we define a quantifier

\phi\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny K}}\gamma:\operatorname{K}((x:X)\times Y(x))

by

(\phi\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny K}}\gamma)(q)=\phi(\lambda x.\gamma(x)(\lambda y.q(x,y))).

Example 3.6
(Product of
\min
and
\max
)

For instance, for the quantifiers
\min\colon(X\to R)\to R
and
\max\colon(x:X)\to(Y(x)\to R)\to R
, their product corresponds to a
\min
-
\max
operation where the range of
y
depends on
x
:

(\min\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny K}}\max)(q)=\min_{x:X}\max_{y:Y(x)}q(x,y).

We can now adapt the iterated product of quantifiers
[
10
]
to quantifier trees.

Definition 3.7
(K-sequence)

To each quantifier tree

\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}

we associate a single quantifier

\operatorname{K{-}sequence}\left(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\right):\operatorname{K}(\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})

on the type of paths of the tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
by induction as follows.

1.

To the empty tree
[]:\operatorname{Tree}
we associate the quantifier

\lambda q.q\operatorname{\langle\rangle}:\operatorname{K}(\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}[]).

2.

To a quantifier tree of the form
\phi\operatorname{::}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
we associate the quantifier

\phi\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny K}}(\lambda x.\operatorname{K{-}sequence}\left(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)\right)).

This inductive definition can also be written as

\displaystyle\operatorname{K{-}sequence}([])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda q.\operatorname{\langle\rangle},

\displaystyle\operatorname{K{-}sequence}(\phi\operatorname{::}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny K}}(\lambda x.\operatorname{K{-}sequence}\left(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)\right))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda q.\phi(\lambda x.(\operatorname{K{-}sequence}\left(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)\right))(\lambda\vec{y}.q(x,\vec{y}))),

by definition
3.5
.

3.2
Higher-order games

Using the notions of type trees and quantifier trees introduced above, we can formulate finite higher-order games as follows.

Definition 3.8
(Dependent higher-order game)

A finite
higher-order game
is given by a tuple
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
where

1.

X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is a
type tree
of moves available at different stages of the game,

2.

R
is the type of possible
outcomes
of the game,

3.

q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})\to R
is the
outcome function
,

4.

\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is a quantifier tree.

One can think of the type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
as describing the rules of the game, specifying the available moves at each position or stage of the game. The function
q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})\to R
gives the outcome at the end of the game, for a given sequence of moves. And the quantifier tree
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
describes the objective of the game, assigning a quantifier to each position in the game. The following two examples have the same rules and outcome functions, but different objectives.

Example 3.9
(Tic-Tac-Toe)

The game of Tic-Tac-Toe can be modelled as the higher-order game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
where

1.

X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is a type tree of moves defined in Example
2.2
,

2.

R
is the linearly ordered type
-1<0<1
,

3.

q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})\to R
is the
outcome function
that, given a sequence of moves, has values
-1
or
1
if one of the players has won, or
0
if no more moves are available, and

4.

\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is a quantifier tree from Example
3.4
.

Example 3.10
(Anti-Tic-Tac-Toe)

The game of Anti-Tic-Tac-Toe, where players try to avoid making three in a row, can be modelled exactly as Tic-Tac-Toe (Example
3.9
), except that the quantifiers in the quantifier tree
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
from Example
3.4
are swapped. The quantifiers still alternate between
\min
and
\max
, but now the root of the quantifier tree is the
\max
quantifier.

Consider also the following example, where the quantifier tree is not based on
\min
and
\max
, but instead on existential quantifiers.

Example 3.11
(Eight Queens)

The game of Eight Queens, where a player attempts to place eight queens on a chess board in such way that no queen can capture the other ones, can be modelled as the higher-order game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
as follows:

1.

X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is a type tree of moves with depth 8, describing the positions on the chess board that have not been used yet.

2.

R
is the type of Booleans
\mathbb{B}=\{\operatorname{true},\operatorname{false}\}
.

3.

q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})\to R
is the
outcome function
that, given the position of the eight queens on the board, has values
\operatorname{false}
or
\operatorname{true}
depending on whether some queen can capture another or not.

4.

\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the quantifier tree of existential quantifiers
\exists\colon(X\to\mathbb{B})\to\mathbb{B}
at each node, where
X
is the type of available moves at the node, defined by
\exists(p)=\operatorname{true}
if and only if there is
x:X
such that
p(x)=\operatorname{true}
.

Notice that, in the above example, in some sense there is only one player, although the concept of player is not incorporated explicitly in our notion of game.

Definition 3.12
(Optimal outcome)

Given a game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
we call
\operatorname{K{-}sequence}\left(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\right)(q):R
the
optimal outcome
of the game.

Example 3.13

In the case of Tic-Tac-Toe, it is well-known that the optimal outcome of the game is a draw. Perhaps less well-known is that in the case of Anti-Tic-Tac-Toc the optimal outcome is also a draw. It is too laborious to calculate these by hand using our definitions, but the definitions can be directly translated to e.g. Haskell or Agda programs so that optimal outcomes of games can be automatically calculated
[
11
,
16
]
.

4
Optimal strategies

A strategy for a given type tree selects an element of each node of the tree. Using Definition
2.5
, this corresponds to the identity structure
SX=X
. That is, for each type
X
of moves in the game tree, the structure is an element of
SX
, which amounts to saying that we pick an element of
X
.

Definition 4.1
(Type of strategies)

Given a type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, we define the type of
strategies over
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
by

\operatorname{Strategy}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=\mathrm{structure}((\lambda X.X),X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}).

The type
\operatorname{Strategy}[]
has exactly one element
\operatorname{\langle\rangle}
, which we think of as the empty strategy. Given a type tree of the form
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
, an element of the type of strategies

\operatorname{Strategy}(X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f})=X\times((x:X)\to\operatorname{Strategy}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))),

is a pair consisting of a move in
X
together with a function that maps each such move
x:X
to a strategy in the subtree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
.

Definition 4.2
(Strategic path)

Each
\sigma\colon\operatorname{Strategy}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
determines a single path

\operatorname{spath}(\sigma):\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},

which we call the
strategic path of
\sigma
, as follows:

1.

If
\sigma:\operatorname{Strategy}\,[]
then its strategic path is the empty path
\operatorname{\langle\rangle}
.

2.

If
\sigma:\operatorname{Strategy}(X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f})
then
\sigma
is of the form
x\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
and its strategic path is defined as
x\operatorname{::}\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))
.

Definition 4.3
(Optimal strategies)

Given a game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
, we define the notion of optimal strategy by induction on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
as follows.

1.

If
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree
[]
then any
\sigma:\operatorname{strategy}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
is empty, which is considered optimal,

2.

When the game has a type tree of the form
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
with a quantifier tree for the form
\phi\operatorname{::}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
, then a strategy
x_{0}\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
is optimal if the following two conditions hold, where

q_{x}=\lambda\vec{y}.q(x\operatorname{::}\vec{y})
:

(a)

q_{x_{0}}(\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x_{0})))=\phi(\lambda x.q_{x}(\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))))
.

(b)

For each
x:X
, the strategy
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)\colon\operatorname{strategy}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))
is optimal at the subgame
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),R,q_{x},\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))
.

This is a generalisation of the notion of subgame perfect equilibrium, where quantifiers other than
\min
and
\max
are allowed.

Theorem 4.4
(Optimality theorem)

Given a game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
and an optimal strategy
\sigma
for it, we have that the outcome on the strategic path of
\sigma
is indeed the optimal outcome of the game, that is,

q(\operatorname{spath}\sigma)=\operatorname{K{-}sequence}(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})(q).

Proof 1

By a induction on the type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
. The base case when
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree is immediate. When the game is defined over a type tree
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
, let
\phi\operatorname{::}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
be the associated quantifier tree, and
x_{0}\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
the given strategy. Then, by the induction hypothesis, we have that for every
x:X

q_{x}(\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)))=\operatorname{K{-}sequence}(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))(q_{x})

and hence

\begin{array}[]{rcl}q(\operatorname{spath}(x_{0}\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}))&=&q_{x_{0}}(\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x_{0})))\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\phi(\lambda x.q_{x}(\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))))\\[2.84526pt]
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle({\rm IH})}}{{=}}&\phi(\lambda x.(\operatorname{K{-}sequence}\left(\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)\right))(q_{x}))\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\operatorname{K{-}sequence}(\phi\operatorname{::}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f})(q)\end{array}

using in the first step that
x_{0}\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
is an optimal strategy.
\Box

4.1
Selection functions

Let
\operatorname{J}X
be an abbreviation for the type
(X\to R)\to X
. Recall the following definitions from
[
10
]
.

Definition 4.5
(Selection functions)

1.

We call functions of type
\operatorname{J}X

selection functions
.

2.

We say that a selection function
\varepsilon:\operatorname{J}X
attains a quantifier
\phi:\operatorname{K}X
if for all
p\colon X\to R
.

p(\varepsilon(p))=\phi(p).

3.

For each selection function
\varepsilon:\operatorname{J}X
we can associate a quantifier
\overline{\varepsilon}:\operatorname{K}X
defined by

\overline{\varepsilon}(p)=p(\varepsilon(p))

so that by definition the selection function
\varepsilon
attains the quantifier
\overline{\varepsilon}
.

Example 4.6
(
\operatorname{argmin}
and
\operatorname{argmax}
)

Let
X
be a finite non-empty type and
R
a linearly ordered type. Standard examples of selection functions are

\operatorname{argmin},\operatorname{argmax}\colon(X\to R)\to X,

which select an arbitrary point at which the outcome function
q\colon X\to R
attains its minimum or maximum. We will use them in our Tic-Tac-Toe running example. It is easy to see that
\operatorname{argmin}
and
\operatorname{argmax}
attain the
\min
and
\max
quantifiers, respectively.

Definition 4.7
(Selection function tree)

Given a type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, we define the type of
selection function trees over
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
by

\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=\mathrm{structure}(\operatorname{J},X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}).

We adopt the following conventions:

1.

The variable
\varepsilon
ranges over the type
\operatorname{J}X
of selection functions.

2.

The variable
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
ranges over the type
\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
of selection function trees.

3.

The variable
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
ranges over selection function forests
(x\colon X)\to\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))
.

Example 4.8
(Tic-Tac-Toe selection function tree)

We associate a selection function tree to the Tic-Tac-Toe type tree of Example

Definition 4.9
(Attainability on trees)

Given a tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, a quantifier tree
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
and a selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, we define the relation

\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
attains
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}

by induction on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
as follows:

1.

If
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree
[]
then
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
attains
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
.

2.

If the tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is of the form
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
then
\varepsilon\operatorname{::}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
attains
\phi\operatorname{::}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
if
\varepsilon
attains
\phi
and for each
x:X
we have that
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
attains
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
.

Example 4.10

The selection function tree for Tic-Tac-Toe given in Example
4.8
has been defined so that it attains the Tic-Tac-Toe quantifier tree of Example
3.4
.

Definition 4.11
(From selection trees to quantifier trees)

Given any tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
and selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, we define a quantifier tree

\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}}:\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}

by induction on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
as follows:

1.

If
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree
[]
then
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree and so is
\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}}
.

2.

If the tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is of the form
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
then the selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is of the form
\varepsilon\operatorname{::}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
and
\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}}
is the quantifier tree
\overline{\varepsilon}\operatorname{::}(\lambda x.\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)})
.

A simple proof by induction shows that
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
attains
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
if and only if
\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}}=\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
.

Example 4.12

It is easy to see that if
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the argmin-argmax selection function tree for Tic-Tac-Toe given in Example
4.8
then
\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}}
is the min-max quantifier tree of Example
3.4
.

Definition 4.13
(Dependent product of selection functions
[

]
)

For any selection function

\varepsilon:\operatorname{J}X

on a type

of types indexed by

\varepsilon\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny J}}\delta:\operatorname{J}((x:X)\times Y(x))

by

(\varepsilon\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny J}}\delta)(q)=(x_{0},\nu(x_{0}))

where

\displaystyle\nu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(x)(q_{x})

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\lambda x.q_{x}(\nu(x)).

Definition 4.14
(J-sequence)

To each selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
we associate a single selection function

\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}):\operatorname{J}(\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})

on the type of paths of the tree

by induction as follows.

1.

To the empty tree

we associate the selection function
\lambda q.\operatorname{\langle\rangle}
of type

2.

To a selection function tree of the form

we associate the selection function
\varepsilon\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny J}}(\lambda x.\operatorname{J{-}sequence}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))

Note that, by Definition

(\varepsilon\operatorname{\otimes^{\tiny J}}(\lambda x.\operatorname{J{-}sequence}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)))(q)=x_{0}\operatorname{::}\nu(x_{0})

where

\displaystyle\nu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\operatorname{J{-}sequence}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))(q_{x})

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\lambda x.q_{x}(\nu(x)).

4.2
Calculating optimal strategies

Finally, let us now show how the
\operatorname{J}
-sequence construction can be used to calculate optimal strategies in games based on dependent type trees.

Definition 4.16
(Strategy of selection tree)

Given tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, a selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
and an outcome function
q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\to R
, we define a strategy

\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q):\operatorname{Strategy}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}

by induction as follows:

1.

When
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree then
\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q)
is the empty strategy.

2.

When
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is of the form
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
and
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=\varepsilon\operatorname{::}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
then we define

\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q)=(x_{0},\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f})

where

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{head}(\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t})(q)),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),q_{x}).

Lemma 4.17
(Main lemma)

Given a tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, a selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
and an outcome function
q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\to R
, we have

\operatorname{spath}(\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q))=\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t})(q).

Proof 2

By induction on
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
. When
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree, the result is immediate. Let
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
and
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=\varepsilon\operatorname{::}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
and assume, for each
x:X
, the induction hypothesis holds

\operatorname{spath}(\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),q_{x}))=\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))(q_{x})

Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\operatorname{spath}(\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q))&=&\operatorname{spath}(x_{0}\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f})\\[5.69054pt]
&=&x_{0}\operatorname{::}\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x_{0}))\\[2.84526pt]
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\text{IH})}}{{=}}&x_{0}\operatorname{::}\nu(x_{0})\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t})(q)\end{array}

where

\displaystyle\nu(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))(q_{x})

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\lambda x.q_{x}(\nu(x)))

\displaystyle\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),q_{x}),

which concludes the proof.
\Box

Lemma 4.18
(Selection strategy lemma)

Given a type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, an outcome function
q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\to R
, and a selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
,

\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q):\operatorname{Strategy}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}

is optimal for the game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}})
.

Proof 3

By induction on the tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
. When
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
is the empty tree, the result is again immediate. If
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
and
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}=\varepsilon\operatorname{::}\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
, recall that
\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q)=x_{0}\operatorname{::}\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
where

\displaystyle x_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\lambda x.q_{x}(\operatorname{J{-}sequence}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x))(q_{x}))),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),q_{x}).

By induction hypothesis we have that, for all
x:X
, the strategy
\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)
is optimal for the subgame

(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),R,q_{x},\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)})

and since

x_{0}=\varepsilon(\lambda x.q_{x}(\operatorname{spath}(\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x),q_{x})))),

by the Main lemma
4.17
, we indeed have

q(x_{0}\operatorname{::}\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x_{0})))=\overline{\varepsilon}(\lambda x.q(x\operatorname{::}\operatorname{spath}(\sigma^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)))),

which concludes the proof.
\Box

Theorem 4.19
(Selection strategy theorem)

If a selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{J}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
attains a quantifier-free
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{K}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
then, for any outcome function
q\colon\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}\to R
,

\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q)

is optimal for the game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
.

Proof 4

Since
\overline{\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}}=\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
when
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
attains
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, the theorem is an immediate consequence of the Selection strategy lemma
4.18
.

Example 4.20
(Tic-Tac-Toe optimal strategy)

Since the quantifier tree
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
in the Tic-Tac-Toe game
(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t},R,q,\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
(Example
3.9
) is attained by a selection function tree
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
(Definition
4.8
), the theorem above shows that
\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q)
is optimal.

5
Discussion of dependent type trees

Aczel
[
2
]
considers a type
\operatorname{Tree}_{A}
similar to our type
\operatorname{Tree}
, but without our leaf constructor
[]
, for the purpose of modelling CZF. The type
\operatorname{Tree}_{A}
consists of well-founded type trees where leaves are of the form
X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
with
X
empty and
X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}
the unique function on
X
.
In our definition, nodes
X
with
X
empty are allowed in principle.
However, as soon as there is a strategy for a type tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{Tree}
, the internal nodes of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
must all be inhabited.

It is not hard to see that the subtype of
\operatorname{Tree}
consisting of the trees whose internal nodes are all inhabited is in bijection with the subtype of
\operatorname{Tree}_{A}
consisting of trees whose internal nodes are all decidable (that is, either empty or inhabited). So we can work with the type
\operatorname{Tree}_{A}
instead. But, in this case, we need to slightly modify all our constructions on trees.
For example, with our definition of path, the type of paths over a tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}:\operatorname{Tree}
is empty if
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
has no leaves of the form
[]
. However, we can define a correct notion of path for Aczel trees by

\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X\operatorname{::}X^{\scriptscriptstyle f})={\rm is{-}empty}(X)+((x:X)\times\operatorname{\mathrm{Path}}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle f}(x)).

This definition is correct in that paths in our original sense correspond to paths in this modified sense along the bijection discussed above. All other definitions have to be modified in a similar way, by treating the base case differently.

Another observation is that empty internal nodes can be pruned away with an idempotent map
\operatorname{prune}:\operatorname{Tree}\to\operatorname{Tree}
such that any tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
has the same paths as the tree
\operatorname{prune}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
, and this can be done without assuming that the internal nodes of
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
are decidable. Moreover, as soon as there is at least one path in the tree
X^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
, all internal nodes of tree
\operatorname{prune}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle t})
are inhabited.

An extended version of this discussion, with full technical details, is formalised in Agda (see the next section).

6
Proofs and Programs in Agda

A second contribution of the present work is that all the definitions, constructions, theorems, lemmas, proofs and examples presented above have been implemented and computer-verified in Agda
[
16
]
.

As Agda adopts a constructive foundation, the function
\operatorname{strategy}(\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t},q)
, which defines strategies from selection trees
\varepsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle t}
and outcome functions
q
, is automatically a computer program that can be used to calculate optimal strategies in concrete games. In particular, we also implemented Tic-Tac-Toe as outlined in the above examples.

The Agda development discussed above
[
16
]
is organised in the following files:

1.

TypeTrees.
This defines dependent type trees and basic concepts for them, including the general definition of adding “structure” to a type tree.

2.

FiniteHistoryDependent
. This implements this paper, excluding the examples. We took care of presenting the material in the same order as in this paper, and of using the same labels for theorems, definitions, etc.

3.

There are three
TicTacToe
files, which implement our running example in different ways. The first one is the shortest and most transparent, but also the most inefficient, while the other two try to sacrifice clarity for efficiency.

4.

Constructor
. This gives an alternative construction of games, not discussed in this paper, and is used in the more efficient versions of Tic-Tac-Toe.

5.

Discussion
. This formalises an extended version of the discussion of Section
5
.

7
Further work

The work presented here is part of a project to extend the theory of higher-order games so as to model different types of players and strategies. Our aim is to make use of the combination of the selection monad with other monads, as in
[
15
]
, leading to games and strategies parametrised by a monadic structure. In particular, by making use of a probability monad, we intend to model suboptimal or “irrational” players in future work.
