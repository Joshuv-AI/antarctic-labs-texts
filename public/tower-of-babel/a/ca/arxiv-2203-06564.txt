Ternary rings of operators arising from inverse semigroups
Robert Pluta, Bernard Russo

Abstract
. We are interested in properties, especially injectivity (in the sense of category theory), of the ternary rings of operators generated by certain subsets of an inverse semigroup via the regular representation. We determine all subsets of the extended bicyclic semigroup which are closed under the triple product x ​ y ∗ ​ z xy^{*}z (called semiheaps) and show that the weakly closed ternary rings of operators generated by them (W*-TROs) are injective operator spaces.

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

1.1.
Introduction

Ternary rings of operators originated in the work of M. R. Hestenes in 1962
[
3
]
. These are linear spaces of operators from one Hilbert space to another which are stable under the triple product
XY^{*}Z
, which he called
ternary algebras
. By their nature, these spaces satisfied an associativity condition involving five elements, namely,

(1.1)

(XY^{*}Z)U^{*}W=XY^{*}(ZU^{*}W)=X(UZ^{*}Y)^{*}W.

These were subsequently axiomatized and named
associative triple systems

[
9
]
. A milestone in their development in the realm of functional analysis was a Gelfand-Naimark type representation theorem for associative triple systems equipped with an operator type norm
[
13
]
.

At around the same time as Hestenes’ work, unbeknownst to the researchers in the west due partially to the Cold War
[
5
]
, the concept of
semiheap
was introduced in the Soviet Union
[
4
]
. A semiheap is a set together with a single three variable operation satisfying an abstract version of (
1.1
), and akin to the known concepts of
ternary group
and
inverse semigroup
.
Since the concept of semiheap is central to this paper, we provide the formal definition, as stated in
[
4
, p. 56]
.

Definition 1.1
.

By a
semiheap
, we mean a set
K
together with a singled-valued, everywhere defined
ternary operation
[\cdot\cdot\cdot]
, satisfying the condition

[[k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}]k_{4}k_{5}]=[k_{1}[k_{4}k_{3}k_{2}]k_{5}]=[k_{1}k_{2}[k_{3}k_{4}k_{5}]].

Semiheaps and their associated structures are closely related to inverse semigroups. In turn, inverse semigroups, together with groupoids, give rise to operator algebras
[
10
]
. A ubiquitous example of an inverse semigroup is the
bicyclic semigroup

[
10
, p. 188]
.

In this paper, we generalize the bicyclic semigroup in such a way that exhibits its semiheap structure.

1.2.
Inverse semigroups

An
inverse semigroup
is a semigroup
S
in which for every element
x
there exists a unique element
x^{*}
, called the
inverse
or
generalized inverse
of
x
, such that
x=xx^{*}x
and
x^{*}=x^{*}xx^{*}
. The function that sends
x
to
x^{*}
is an antiautomorphism of order two. An
idempotent
in a semigroup is an element that multiplied by itself results in the same element. An inverse semigroup may contain many idempotents, but when it contains precisely one idempotent, it is a group. In fact, the existence of a unique idempotent characterizes groups among inverse semigroups.

Remark 1.2
.

Inverse semigroups are precisely the semigroups
in which every element has at least one generalized inverse
and in which any two idempotents commute.

Example 1.3
.

The
symmetric inverse semigroup

I(X)
,
also denoted by
I_{X}
, which is the
monoid under composition of all partial bijections (or partial symmetries) on a set
X

is an inverse semigroup.
The idempotents of
I(X)
correspond to subsets
A\subseteq X

via
A\leftrightarrow 1_{A}
, where
1_{A}
is the identity function on
A
.
In particular,
1_{\emptyset}
is the empty function (zero element)
and
1_{X}
is the identity element of
I(X)
.
The symmetric group on
X
is a subgroup of
I(X)
.

A classical result originating in the work of Wagner and Preston, known as the Wagner-Preston Representation Theorem, states that every inverse semigroup
S
may be embedded in the symmetric inverse semigroup
I(S)
. In fact, for any
a
in
S
, the mapping

a^{*}S\to aS,\qquad x\mapsto ax\qquad\text{ ($x\in a^{*}S$)}

is a bijection from
a^{*}S
onto
aS
that represents the element
a
.

1.3.
The extended bicyclic semigroup

We use the following notation:
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
;
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
;

\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup-\mathbb{N}
.

Example 1.4
.

Let
E_{22}
be the bicyclic semigroup, as realized by the unilateral shift (
[
10
, p. 188]
). Thus
E_{22}=\{a_{ij}:i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, where for any
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
e_{ij}
is the matrix over
\mathbb{Z}
with 1 in the

i,j
position and zeros elsewhere, and
a_{ij}
is the bounded operator on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,

a_{ij}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e_{i+k,j+k}\in B(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})).

Set

E=E_{11}\cup E_{12}\cup E_{21}\cup E_{22}

where
E_{21}=\{a_{ij}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0},j\in-\mathbb{N}\}
,
E_{11}=\{a_{ij}:i,j\in-\mathbb{N}\}
and
E_{12}=\{a_{ij}:i\in-\mathbb{N},j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
.

We note that for
i,j,p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
,
a_{ij}^{*}=a_{ji}
,
a_{ij}
is a partial isometry in
B(H)
(
H=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
) and

(1.2)

a_{ij}a_{pq}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i,q+j-p},&p\leq j\\
a_{i+p-j,q},&p\geq j\end{array},\right.

equivalently

a_{ij}a_{pq}=a_{i+p-\min(j,p),j+q-\min(j,p)}.

In particular,
a_{ij}a_{pq}\neq 0
,
a_{ij}a_{jq}=a_{iq}
, and
a_{ii}a_{pp}=a_{mm}
with
m=\max(i,p)
.

Thus

E
is an inverse semigroup consisting of partial isometries with “inverse”
a_{ij}^{*}
equal to the adjoint of
a_{ij}
and the idempotents coincide with the elements
a_{ii}
. We shall call
E
the
extended bicyclic semigroup
2
2

2

Ibid.
, and when convenient notationally, represent
a_{ij}
in formulas and diagrams simply by
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

We shall analyze the extended bicyclic semigroup
E
of Example
1.4
toward the aims of finding all of the subsemiheaps of
E
, and showing that the associated W*-TROs are injective operator spaces.

The idempotents of
E
are the elements
a_{ii}
with
i\in\mathbb{Z}
and
a_{ii}\leq a_{jj}
, that is,
a_{ii}a_{jj}=a_{ii}
, if and only if
j\leq i
. From (
1.2
), we calculate and find that for
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}
,

a_{ii}a_{pq}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{i,q+i-p}\quad,&p\leq i\\
a_{pq}\quad,&p\geq i\end{array}\right.,

a_{pq}a_{jj}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{pq}\quad,&j\leq q\\
a_{p+j-q,j}\quad,&j\geq q\end{array}\right.,

and

a_{ii}a_{pq}a_{jj}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{i,q+i-p}\quad,&p\leq i\hbox{ and }j\leq q+i-p\\
a_{j-q+p,q}\quad,&p\leq i\hbox{ and }j\geq q+i-p\\
a_{pq}\quad,&p\geq i\hbox{ and }j\leq q\\
a_{p+j-q,j}\quad,&p\geq i\hbox{ and }j\geq q\end{array}\right.

In particular,
a_{00}E=E_{21}\cup E_{22}
and
Ea_{00}=E_{12}\cup E_{22}
. Also,
a_{ii}E
,
Ea_{jj}
and
a_{ii}Ea_{jj}

are subsemigroups and semiheaps, and
a_{ii}Ea_{jj}
is an inverse semigroup if
i=j
. More generally,

Ea_{jj}=\{a_{pq}:j\leq q\},\quad a_{ii}E=\{a_{pq}:p\geq i\},

and

a_{ii}E\cap Ea_{jj}=a_{ii}Ea_{jj}=\{a_{pq}:p\geq i,q\geq j\}.

Subsemiheaps of the extended bicyclic semigroup

In this section we shall determine all of the subsemiheaps of the extended bicyclic semigroup. We shall proceed as follows. First, for an arbitrary subsemiheap
K
of
E
, we define

\alpha_{0}=\inf\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}:\exists\beta\in\mathbb{Z},a_{\alpha\beta}\in K\},

and

\beta_{0}=\inf\{\beta\in\mathbb{Z}:\exists\alpha\in\mathbb{Z},a_{\alpha\beta}\in K\}.

We have four mutually exclusive and exhaustive cases, namely,

(1)

Quadrant

\alpha_{0}\neq-\infty,\beta_{0}\neq-\infty

(2)

Right Half Plane

\alpha_{0}=-\infty,\beta_{0}\neq-\infty

(3)

Lower Half Plane

\alpha_{0}\neq-\infty,\beta_{0}=-\infty

(4)

Full Plane

\alpha_{0}=-\infty,\beta_{0}=-\infty

Remark 3.1
.

We only need to find all of the subsemiheaps of
E
which are in case (1), since the other cases can be reduced to this case in steps, as follows, which shows that every subsemiheap of the extended bicyclic semigroup is the inductive limit of subsemiheaps in case (1) in the category of semiheaps and semiheap homomorphisms

3

3.19
).

of
