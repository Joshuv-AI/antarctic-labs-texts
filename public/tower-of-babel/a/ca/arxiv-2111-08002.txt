Natural Gradient Variational Inference with Gaussian Mixture Models


Abstract
Bayesian methods estimate a measure of uncertainty by using the posterior distribution: p ⁡ ( 𝐳 | 𝒟 ) = p ⁡ ( 𝒟 | 𝐳 ) ​ p ​ ( 𝐳 ) / p ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|\mathcal{D})=p(\mathcal{D}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})/p(\mathcal{D}) . One source of difficulty in these methods is the computation of the normalizing constant p ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) = ∫ p ⁡ ( 𝒟 | 𝐳 ) ​ p ​ ( 𝐳 ) ​ 𝑑 z p(\mathcal{D})=\int p(\mathcal{D}|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})p(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})dz . Calculating exact posterior is generally intractable and we usually approximate it. Variational Inference (VI) methods approximate the posterior with a distribution q ⁡ ( 𝐳 ) q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}) usually chosen from a simple family using optimization. The main contribution of this work is described in section 3.1 , where we provide a set of update rules for natural gradient variational inference with mixture of Gaussians, which can be run independently for each of the mixture components, potentially in parallel.



1
VI with Gradient Descent

The recent approaches to VI generally avoid model-specific derivations.
Black box variational inference
[
6
]
is one of the pioneer works that provides a recipe to quickly build and explore variety of models.

The idea is to assume a simple family for variational distribution
q_{\phi}(z|x)
over the latent variables
z
with parameters
\phi
, and find the member of that family that is closest in KL divergence to the true posterior. Note that if the generative model
p(z,x)
is a parameterized model, we denote its parameters by
\theta
. Minimizing the KL divergence is an optimization problem, and its complexity is partially controlled by the complexity of the chosen variational family.

\displaystyle D_{KL}(q_{\phi}(z|x)||p_{\theta}(z|x))

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\phi}(z|x)}[\log{q_{\phi}(z|x)\over p_{\theta}(z|x)}]

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\phi}(z|x)}[\log q_{\phi}(z|x)-\log p_{\theta}(z,x)+\log p_{\theta}(x)]

\displaystyle=\log p_{\theta}(x)+E_{q_{\phi}(z|x)}[\log q_{\phi}(z|x)-\log p_{\theta}(z,x)]

The term
E_{q_{\phi}(z|x)}[\log{p_{\theta}(z,x)-\log q_{\phi}(z|x)}]
is called the variational lower bound or alternatively, the Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO). We denote the ELBO by
\mathcal{L}
.
Since the log-likelihood of data,
p(x)
is assumed to be fixed, minimizing the KL divergence is equivalent to maximizing the ELBO.

\displaystyle\log p_{\theta}(x)=D_{KL}(q_{\phi}(z|x)||p_{\theta}(z|x))

\displaystyle+E_{q_{\phi}(z|x)}[\log{p_{\theta}(z,x)-\log q(z|x)}]

(1)

In
[
6
]
, the gradient of ELBO is written as the expectation with respect to
q_{\phi}
. Therefore, we can approximate it using Monte Carlo samples from
q_{\phi}
. This gives us an unbiased estimator for the gradient of ELBO.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\phi}E_{q}{(\log p(x,z)-\log q(z|\phi))}

(2)

\displaystyle=E_{q}[(\log p(x,z)-\log q(z|\phi))\nabla_{\phi}\log q(z|\phi)]

(3)

A noisy unbiased gradient estimator of ELBO with Monte Carlo samples
z^{(l)}
from variational distribution is obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}\approx{1\over L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(\log p_{\theta}(x,z^{(l)})-\log q(z^{(l)}|\phi))\nabla_{\phi}\log q(z^{(l)}|\phi)

(4)

This gradient estimate can be used by a stochastic optimizer to achieve the optimized values for variational parameters
\phi
. In stochastic optimization a function is optimized using noisy estimates of its gradient.
Here Monte Carlo estimates are the noisy estimates of the gradient.
This estimator is unbiased but it exhibits high variance.

2
VI with Natural Gradient Descent

Natural gradient descent assumes the parameter of interest lies on a Rimannian manifold and selects the steepest direction along that manifold.
These methods exploit the Riemannian geometry of the approximate posterior and scale the gradient by the inverse of metric tensor. In case of parametric families, the Fischer Information matrix (FIM) induces a Rimannian manifold.
We usually intend to avoid direct computation of FIM.

Suppose we aim to optimize a variational objective
\mathcal{L}(\eta)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{q_{\eta}(z)}\left[\log p(z)-\log q(z)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log(p(D_{i}|z)\right]\right]

(5)

Exponential Family.
Assume the variational distribution
q_{\eta}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})
has a minimal exponential family form with natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.
Then, there exist a one-to-one mapping between the natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and expectation parameter
\mathbf{m}
.
Therefore, we can rewrite the variational objective
\mathcal{L}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})
as a function of expectation parameters
\mathbf{m}
instead of natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L_{*}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})

(6)

Theorem 1. Variable Transformation.
In exponential family distributions, the natural gradient wrt to natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is equal to gradient wrt expectation parameters
m
.

\displaystyle F(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})^{-1}\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}}\mathcal{L}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})=\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\mathcal{L}_{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})

(7)

Proof:
The proof is based on
[
7
]
. We can write the derivative wrt
\boldsymbol{\eta}
in terms of
\mathbf{m}
using the chain rule:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}}\mathcal{L}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})=\left[\nabla_{\eta}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}\right]\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\mathcal{L}_{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})=F(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\mathcal{L}_{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})

(8)

The second equality comes from the fact that in exponential family distributions, natural parameters
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and expectation parameters
\mathbf{m}
are related through
Legendre transform:
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}=\nabla A(\eta)
where
A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})
is the log-partition function. Therefore,
\nabla_{\eta}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}=\nabla^{2}_{\eta\eta}A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})
. By the definitions of the FIM, we have

F(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})\coloneqq\nabla^{2}_{\eta\eta}A(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})
. So
\nabla_{\eta}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}=F(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})
.

It is worth mentioning that FIM in one space is the inverse of FIM in the other space. This enables computing natural gradient in one space using the gradient in the other.

Theorem 2. Mirror Descent.
Mirror descent induced by a Bregman
divergence proximity functions is equivalent to the natural gradient descent algorithm on the dual Riemannian manifold.

Proof:

Assume we have a strictly convex
G
that induces the Bregman Divergence
B_{G}:\Theta\times\Theta\rightarrow{R}^{+}
, where
B_{G}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle B_{G}(m,m_{t})=G(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})-G(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}^{\prime})-\langle\nabla G(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}^{\prime}),\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}-\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}^{\prime}\rangle

(9)

The mirror descent update is written as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t+1}=\text{argmin}_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\nabla\mathcal{L_{*}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t})\rangle+{1\over\alpha_{t}}B_{G}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t})

(10)

Taking the derivative to find the minimum, we arrive at:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}G({\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}_{t+1})=\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}G({\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}_{t})-\alpha_{t}\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\mathcal{L_{*}}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t})

(11)

In terms of the dual variable
\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}=\nabla G(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$})
and noting that
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}=\nabla H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$})
,

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t+1}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\mathcal{L_{*}}(\nabla H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}))

(12)

Applying chain rule, we have:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\mathcal{L_{*}}(\nabla H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}))=\left[\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}}\nabla H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t})\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}}\mathcal{L_{*}}(\nabla H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}))

(13)

Therefore:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t+1}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}-\alpha_{t}\left[\nabla^{2}H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t})\right]^{-1}\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L_{*}}(\nabla H(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}))

(14)

In our settings, the function
G
is the negative entropy function:
G(m)=\sum{q_{m}(\theta)\log q_{m}(\theta)}
. The mirror descent update can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t+1}=\text{argmin}_{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}}\langle\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$},\nabla_{m}\mathcal{L}_{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t})\rangle+{1\over\beta}\mathcal{D}_{KL}\left[q_{m}(\theta)||q_{m_{t}}(\theta)\right]

(15)

In this case the function
H
which is the convex conjugate of
G
, is the
Mirror descent and natural gradient descent are both generalizations of online gradient descent when the parameter of interest lies on a non-Euclidean manifold.
In this section we showed that mirror descent update in the expectation parameters space is equivalent to the natural gradient update in the natural parameter space.

Each step of this mirror descent update is equivalent to the following natural gradient descent in the natural parameter space.

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t+1}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}+\beta_{t}\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{F}$}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t})^{-1}\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L}(\eta_{t})

(16)

Using Theorem 1, we can now write (
16
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t+1}=\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\eta}$}$}_{t}+\beta_{t}\nabla_{m}\mathcal{L}_{*}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{m}$}$}_{t})

(17)

2.2
Variational Online Newton(VON)

We can reformulate the NGVI updates in terms of gradient and Hessian of negative log-likelihood of data, defined as
f(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(p(D_{i}|z)
as follows
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(z)-\log q(z)+f(z)\right]

(24)

\displaystyle=\lambda\mu+\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}(z)\right]

(25)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Sigma}\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\Sigma}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log p(z)-\log q(z)+f(z)\right]

(26)

\displaystyle=-{1\over 2}\lambda\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$}+{1\over 2}\Sigma^{-1}+{1\over 2}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}(z)\right],

(27)

where
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}(z)
and
\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}(z)
denote the gradient and Hessian of negative log-likelihood respectively, and are obtained using Bonnet’s and Price’s theorem
[
4
]
as shown below:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[f(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\nabla_{z}f(z)\right]\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}(z)\right]

(28)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Sigma}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[f(z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\nabla^{2}_{zz}f(z)\right]\coloneqq{1\over 2}\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}(z)\right]

(29)

We estimate each of the expectations above with one Monte-Carlo sample
z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(z|\mu_{t},\Sigma_{t})
, and arrive at the update rules of VON
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\mu_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{t}-\beta\Sigma_{t+1}\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{g}$}$}(z_{0})+\lambda\mu_{t}\right]

(30)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{-1}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)\Sigma^{-1}_{t}+\beta\left[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{H}$}$}(z_{0})+\lambda\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{I}$}$}\right]

(31)

2.3
Variational Online Gauss-Newton(VOGN)

VOGN is similar to VON, however the Generalized Gauss-Newton approximation is used to estimate the Hessian:

where

is the j’th element of
\mathbf{z}
, and

The following entropy trick will reduce the variance of our estimator:

\displaystyle H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]=-\sum_{c}\pi_{c}\log\sum_{j}\pi_{j}q(\hat{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}}|w=j)+H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]-\hat{H}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w],

where
H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]

\hat{H}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]

\displaystyle H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]

4.1
Proof of the Entropy Trick

We define a variational distribution
q(z)
as a mixture distribution as follows:

\displaystyle q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$})=\sum_{c}\pi_{c}q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w=c)

(76)

\displaystyle q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w=c)=\mbox{${\cal N}$}(\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}$}_{c},\mbox{$\mbox{$\boldsymbol{\sigma}$}$}_{c})

(77)

Using Bayes for entropy, the entropy term can be written as:

\displaystyle H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]=H[w]+H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]-H[w|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]

Each term of the right hand side of equation mentioned above can be computed as shown below.

1)
H[w]
: This entropy term is given by definition as follows:

\displaystyle H[w]

\displaystyle=-\sum_{c}\pi_{c}\log\pi_{c}

(78)

2)
H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]
:

\displaystyle H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}{E}_{q(z,w)}[-\log q(z|w)]

(79)

The last line results from the analytical definition of entropy being the weighted sum of component’s entropy:
\sum\pi_{c}H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w=c]
.

3)
H[w|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]
:

\displaystyle H[w|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]

\displaystyle=H[w]+H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]-H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]

(81)

\displaystyle=H[w]+\sum_{c}\pi_{c}\mbox{$E\;$}_{q(z|w=c)}[-\log q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w=c)]

(82)

\displaystyle+\sum_{c}\pi_{c}E_{q(z|w=c)}[\log\sum_{j}\pi_{j}q(\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w=j)]

(83)

The last two terms will be evaluated by sampling from each component.
Denoting sample estimators
\hat{z}
and
\hat{H}
, we have:

\displaystyle H[w|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]=H[w]+\hat{H}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]+\sum_{c}\pi_{c}\log\sum_{j}\pi_{j}q(\hat{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}}|w=j)

(84)

Therefore by plugging (
84

\displaystyle H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]

\displaystyle=H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]+H[w]-H[w|\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}]

\displaystyle=H[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]+H[w]-H[w]-\hat{H}[\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}|w]-\sum_{c}\pi_{c}\log\sum_{j}\pi_{j}q(\hat{\mbox{$\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}$}}|w=j)

(86)
