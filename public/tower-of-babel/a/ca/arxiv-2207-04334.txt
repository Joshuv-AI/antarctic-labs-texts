Polyhedral Estimation of ℒ 1 \mathcal{L}_{1} and ℒ ∞ \mathcal{L}_{\infty} Incremental Gains of Nonlinear Systems Note: This work was supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC) of the United Kingdom - Project Reference 1950140.
Dimitris Kousoulidis, Fulvio Forni

Abstract
We provide novel dissipativity conditions for bounding the incremental ℒ 1 \mathcal{L}_{1} gain of systems. Moreover, we adapt existing results on the ℒ ∞ \mathcal{L}_{\infty} gain to the incremental setting and relate the incremental ℒ 1 \mathcal{L}_{1} and ℒ ∞ \mathcal{L}_{\infty} gain bounds through system adjoints. Building on work on optimization based approaches to constructing polyhedral Lyapunov functions, we make use of these conditions to obtain a Linear Programming based algorithm that can provide increasingly sharp bounds on the gains as a function of a given candidate polyhedral storage function or polyhedral set. The algorithm is also extended to allow for the design of linear feedback controllers for performance, as measured by the bounds on the incremental gains. We apply the algorithm to a couple of numerical examples to illustrate the power, as well as some limitations, of this approach.

Introduction

When we treat open dynamical systems as operators
\mathcal{G}

that map input signals
w
onto output signals
z=\mathcal{G}w
,
the induced operator norms of
\mathcal{G}

quantitatively measure the amplification factor of
the input-output relationship,
which we call the system gain.
Gains provide a useful tool for system specifications,
allowing us to quantitatively characterize performance
and to pose control design problems
to optimize them in closed loop

[
28
]
.
Gains are also used
for studying the stability of interconnections
through the small gain theorem,
which states that
the feedback interconnection of two systems
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}

will be stable as long as
\|\mathcal{G}_{1}\|\|\mathcal{G}_{2}\|<1

[
12
, Chapter 3]
.
For these purposes,
gains based on various norms have been
extensively studied and used in systems and control theory.
The selection of the norm needs to balance the needs of capturing performance
versus our ability to compute with it

[
8
,
28
]
.
In this paper we will be using incremental gains.
Incremental gains are a classic concept in control literature
[
12
]
.
They bound the incremental amplification between any pair of input signals.
Incremental gains maintain the interpretations and properties of gains discussed above,
including a form of the small gain theorem

[
12
, Chapter 3]
.
For linear systems, gains and incremental gains are equivalent.

We will be computing bounds on
the incremental
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains:

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{1}=\sup_{w_{1}-w_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{1}}{\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{1}},

(1)

and

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{w_{1}-w_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty}}{\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\infty}},

(2)

respectively, where

\displaystyle\|w\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}|w|_{1}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{i}|w_{i}|dt,

\displaystyle\|w\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{t}|w|_{\infty}=\sup_{i,\,t}|w_{i}|.

w\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
if
\|w\|_{1}<\infty
,
and
w\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
if
\|w\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

The
\mathcal{L}_{1}
norm assigns lower magnitude to short, high amplitude signals
over long, low amplitude signals;
while the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
norm assigns lower magnitude to long, low amplitude signals
over short, high amplitude signals.
The
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gain is most suitable in quantifying specifications
in which the signals are each directly related to the consumption of a resource,
for example fuel use in a rocket or transaction fees in a trading system,
while the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain is most suitable in quantifying specifications
about the worse-case amplitudes of signals.
Moreover, for linear systems,
upper bounds on the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains,
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{\infty}
,
provide upper bounds for any
\mathcal{L}_{p}
gain with
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\gamma_{p}

[
12
]

\gamma_{p}\leq\gamma_{1}^{1/p}\gamma_{\infty}^{1/q},\ \text{where }1/p+1/q=1,

(3)

offering another reason to study this combination of gains.

\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains have been mostly studied
in the context of positive systems.
This is
because of the ease with which they can be expressed and
their efficient calculation
[
10
,
23
]
.
This has also led to conditions for
robust stability and stabilization via integral linear constraints,
a linear analogue to integral quadratic constraints

[
10
]
.
Moreover,
weighted
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gains can be used to obtain a complete characterization of stability
for positive interconnections of positive systems
[
14
]
.

Beyond positive systems,

\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains of linear systems are computed
through numerical integration of the impulse response

[
1
,
22
,
26
]
.
Current results for
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
bounds on gains
for nonlinear systems include

[
20
, Theorem 5.1]

where a uniform bound for all
\mathcal{L}_{p}
gains

The work of
[
4
]

and
[
15

presents algorithms for computing the

gain of
linear time/parameter varying (LTV/LPV) systems

The main contributions of this paper are novel conditions
for computing incremental

gain bounds based
on dissipation inequalities with respect to polyhedral storage functions.
Conditions for incremental
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain bounds
using polyhedral set invariance

The

[

,

]

Both sets of conditions are framed as optimization problems

This generalizes the known relationship between

When the polyhedral function/set is fixed

This allows us to adapt our previous work

[

We provide definitions and the conditions at an abstract level

2
Gains and Incremental Gains

2.1
Gains for Linear Systems

For linear systems,
the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains
can be characterized in terms of
the matrix of impulse responses
H
.
We denote
the entry in the
i^{th}
row and
j^{th}
column of
H

as
h_{ij}
.
This corresponds to the impulse response of
the
i^{th}
output to the
j^{th}
input.

The
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains of a system with
m
outputs and
n
inputs are then

[
10
]

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{1}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|h_{ij}(t)|dt\right)\right)

(4)

and

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|h_{ij}(t)|dt\right)\right).

(5)

Note that,
other than for the computation of the
m\times n
integrals
\int_{0}^{\infty}|h_{ij}(t)|dt
,
the remaining computations for (
4
) and (
5
)
amount to summations and maximizations over a finite number of scalars.
As such evaluating
\int_{0}^{\infty}|h_{ij}(t)|dt
numerically is the main computational challenge
for evaluating the two gains
and various methods have been proposed for computing it

[
1
,
22
,
26
]
.

It follows from (
4
) and (
5
) that
the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gain of a system with impulse responses
H

is equivalent to the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain of a system with impulse responses
H^{T}
.
This also means that
the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains
of single-input single-output or self-adjoint linear systems
are equivalent.

Remark 1

For the special case of positive linear systems,
all
h_{ij}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0

and so
\int_{0}^{\infty}|h_{ij}(t)|dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{ij}(t)dt
,
which is also equivalent to the frequency response of
\mathcal{G}
evaluated at
0
.
This enables efficient calculation and design for

\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains
[
10
,
23
]
.

2.2
Incremental Gains for Nonlinear Systems

The approach in
Section

2.1
relies
on concepts from linear time invariant systems theory that are hard to generalize
to other classes of systems.
A more general approach to computing incremental gains
(or, at least, upper bounds on incremental gains)
is through dissipativity theory

[
27
]
,
which we apply to more general,
minimal realization
state-space systems of the form

\dot{x}=f(x)+Bw\qquad z=Cx,

(6)

with
n
states,
n_{w}
disturbance inputs, and
n_{z}
outputs.
To obtain a well defined operator we also set
x(0)=0
.

Under this framework we replace conditions over all signals,
such as those in (
1
) and (
2
),
with point-wise in time conditions over all time, inputs, and state variables
– called
dissipation inequalities
.
This framework then requires finding a positive function of the state,
the
storage function
,
which plays a central role in the dissipation inequalities.
For simplicity,
in this paper we only consider linear input/output system matrices
B
and
C
.
However, the approach can be in principle be further generalized
to nonlinear dependencies as well.

For incremental
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gains
the suitable dissipation inequality for all

w_{1},\,w_{2}
and
x_{1},\,x_{2}

is

\dot{\psi}\leq-|z_{1}-z_{2}|_{1}+\gamma_{1}|w_{1}-w_{2}|_{1},

(7)

where
\psi(x_{1},x_{2})
is a storage function.
To make it simpler to verify (
7
),
we limit ourselves to considering storage functions of the form
\psi(x_{1}-x_{2})

that satisfy
\psi(x_{1}-x_{2})\geq 0
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\psi(0)=0
.
Integrating (
7
)
in time from
0
to
T

gives

\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{1}\leq-\psi(x_{1}(T)-x_{2}(T))+\gamma_{1}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{1}

and so, since
\psi(x_{1}(T)-x_{2}(T))\geq 0
,

\frac{\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{1}}{\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{1}}\leq\gamma_{1},

giving us
\gamma_{1}
as an upper bound for the incremental
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gain of the system.

For
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
there is no need to integrate the signals involved
because they directly correspond to point-wise in time conditions
which are conveniently expressed in terms of set invariance

[
4
, Section 6.4]
.
What we need is a limit on the maximum incremental amplification
of the output relative to the input.
We capture this
by confirming that inputs with bounded incremental norms lead to
states with bounded difference,
i.e. confined to a bounded set
\mathcal{X}
,
for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\,x_{1}(0)

\displaystyle=0,\,x_{2}(0)=0

\displaystyle\implies(x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t))\in\mathcal{X}

(8a)

\displaystyle(x_{1}(t)-x_{2}(t))\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\implies\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma_{\infty},

(8b)

giving
\gamma_{\infty}

as an upper bound for the incremental
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain of the system.
Here the set
\mathcal{X}
plays a role similar to that of a storage function.

We next specialize
(
7
)
and (
8
)
to the family of polyhedral functions and sets respectively.

3
Polyhedral Approximation of Gains

3.1
Polyhedral Preliminaries

To compute gains we use C-sets and gauge functions.
C-sets are compact convex sets that contain
0
in their interior,
and gauge functions are subadditive positively homogeneous functions (of degree
1
).
We can extract a gauge function from a C-set through its Minkowski functional and
a C-set from a gauge function through its unit ball,
so each C-set induces a gauge function and vice versa
[
4
]
.

Polyhedra have
two distinct representations,
which we call V-representation and H-representation,
V-rep and H-rep for short.
A V-rep polyhedral set is represented by its extremal vertices,
while a H-rep polyhedral set
is represented by intersections of affine half-spaces.
We adopt the convention of using

\mathcal{V}
to denote V-rep sets
constructed from numerical matrix
V

Likewise,
we use
\mathcal{H}
to denote H-rep sets
constructed from numerical matrix
H

\mathcal{V}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x=Vp,\,p\geq 0,\,\bm{1}^{T}p=1\}

(9)

(10)

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathcal{V}}(x)=

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\{\bm{1}^{T}p:x=Vp,\,p\geq 0\}

(11a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{h^{T}x:h^{T}V\leq\bm{1}^{T}\}\,

where (

)

[

]

H-rep

given

\mathcal{H}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:Hx\leq\bm{1}\}

\interior\mathcal{H}=\{x:Hx<\bm{1}\}.

3.2
Polyhedral Gain Conditions

To compute bounds on incremental
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains
we will use the variational system of (
6
),
along generic solution pairs
(x,w):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{w}}
of (
6
):

\dot{\delta x}=\partial f(x)\delta x+B\delta w,\,\delta z=C\delta x.

(18)

Along a generic solution
(x,w)
, (
18
)
corresponds to a time-varying linear system with
n
(variational) states,
n_{w}
inputs, and
n_{z}
outputs,
which captures the effect of (infinitesimal) perturbations on
(x,w)

[
11
,
16
,
17
]
.

In what follows, we will take advantage of the fact that a uniform bound
\gamma

that satisfies
\|\delta z\|\leq\gamma\|\delta w\|

for all input/output pairs of the variational system
and uniformly with respect to all solution pairs
(x,w)
of (
6
),
is also a bound on the incremental gain of (
6
).
For instance, consider a generic pair of inputs signal
w_{0}
and
w_{1}
,
and define the parameterized signal
w_{s}=sw_{1}+(1-s)w_{0}
, for
0\leq s\leq 1
.
Using (
6
), compute the state trajectory
x_{s}
and the output
z_{s}

related to the input
w_{s}
and to the initial condition
x_{s}(0)=0
.
For any
0\leq s\leq 1
, the quantities
\frac{d}{ds}w_{s}
and
\frac{d}{ds}z_{s}
correspond to the input and output signals of
(
18
) computed along the solution pair
(x_{s},w_{s})
. Thus,

\begin{split}\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|&=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}z_{s}ds\right\|\ \leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{d}{ds}z_{s}\right\|ds\\
&\leq\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{d}{ds}w_{s}\right\|ds\ =\gamma\int_{0}^{1}\|w_{1}-w_{0}\|ds\\
&=\gamma\|w_{1}-w_{0}\|,\end{split}

(19)

where the second inequalities follows from the uniform bound
\|\delta z\|\leq\gamma\|\delta w\|
.

Together with the use of the variational system,
we assume the
existence of a V-rep polyhedral relaxation of its differential dynamics

\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\}\ \text{such that, for all $x$,}\ \partial f(x)\in\convexhull\mathcal{A},

(20)

where

\convexhull\mathcal{A}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}A_{i}:A_{i}\in\mathcal{A},\,\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

Namely, this means that, for any
x
,
\partial f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(x)A_{i}
where
A_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
,
\lambda_{i}(x)\geq 0
, and

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}(x)=1
.
This relaxation is inspired by a standard step in the literature
for computation of nonlinear gains by
linear matrix inequalities (LMIs)
[
6
]
.
We don’t present a general algorithm for computing such relaxations
since it is a non-trivial problem.
This is related to the trade-off between the
quality of the relaxation and the number of elements in it.
Instead, in our examples,
we work directly with sets
\mathcal{A}
,
with the results then applying to any nonlinear system
that satisfies (
20
).

For a linear system,
(
18
) has the same dynamics
as (
6
) with
f(x)=\partial f(x)=A

and so
\mathcal{A}=\{A\}
.

4.1
Analysis

Because of the connection between (
21
)
and (
22

We choose to design an algorithm for the
\mathcal{L}_{1}
problem
and apply the algorithm to the adjoint system
if we are after
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gains bounds.
A comparison between our algorithm and other approaches is provided in
Section

.

Because having variables multiplying each other makes a problem
nonconvex and nonlinear,
directly solving (
21
) with
the polyhedral candidate storage function represented by
V

as a variable is not computationally tractable.
However,
for a fixed
V
,
(
21
)
can be efficiently solved using linear programming (LP).
This suggests a solution strategy based on an alternating iteration
to produce increasingly tight bounds on the gains
by locally optimizing a candidate
V

(building on
[
21
]

that uses the same approach to find polyhedral Lyapunov functions
for autonomous systems).
We alternate between two steps, which we call
the
gain estimation
step and the
polyhedral modification
step.
Both steps are LP problems.
During the gain estimation step we consider
V
fixed
and compute a bound on the gain.
During the polyhedral modification step,
we introduce a small variation in our candidate storage function
representation
\delta V

with the goal of enabling a smaller gain bound to be verified.

Gain estimation step:
after fixing
V
,
variables
(\eta_{w},P)
and
(\eta_{z},\mathcal{M})
of (
21
)
become independent of each other.
We can then solve (
21
)
by solving two LP sub-problems:
minimize
\eta_{w}
subject to (
21a
)
(with variables
\eta_{w},P
),
and
maximize
\eta_{z}
subject to (
21b
)
(with variables
\eta_{z},\mathcal{M}
).
The optimal
\eta_{w}/\eta_{z}
is then equal to
the solution to (
21
) for the given

.
Moreover,
if we fix
V
such that

is a decaying incremental Lyapunov function for the internal dynamics,
(
21b
) is guaranteed to be feasible
(since there must then exist

such that

and

)
and so the gain estimation step is guaranteed to obtain

(since (
21a
) is always feasible
and implies

).

4.2
Synthesis

After fixing
V

the constraints in (
21b
)
are convex with respect to the relaxed dynamics
\mathcal{A}
.
This makes it easy to adapt the algorithms of

Section

4.1
to linear feedback control synthesis
by adding extra free variables corresponding to the control parameters.

We consider
(
6
)
with added
controlled inputs
u

and
measured outputs
y

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=f(x)+Bw+B_{u}u,\,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=Cx,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=C_{y}x.

State feedback design is obtained by taking
C_{y}=I
but our procedure
works with any output matrix, allowing for the design of
static output feedback controllers of the form
u=Ky
.

The procedure is based on the extension
of the gain estimation step of
Section

4.1
, by replacing
each constraint
A_{i}V=VM_{i}
with

(A_{i}+B_{u}KC_{y})V=VM_{i}.

(30)

Since
K
enters (
30
) linearly (for fixed
V
),
it is also straightforward to consider additional linear constraints on
K

with the goal of enforcing bounds on the control gains or sparsity patterns.
By adding sums of constraints of the form

\Gamma_{l}^{T}K\Gamma_{r}\leq g
, where
\Gamma_{l}
and
\Gamma_{r}
are matrices
of suitable size,
we can limit the range of (linear combinations of) the coefficients of
K
.
Likewise, constraints of the form

\Gamma_{l}^{T}K\Gamma_{r}=0

can be used to enforce specific sparsity patterns on
K
.

The introduction of the additional variable
K
also imposes minor changes to
the polyhedral modification step.
The small perturbations in (
29
) must
take into account the extended solution manifold

(\eta_{w},\eta_{z},V,P,\mathcal{M},K).

This means that we need to add
\delta K
as a new variable to (
29
)
and replace the constraints

A_{i}\delta V=\delta VM_{i}+V\delta M_{i}

with

(A_{i}+B_{u}KC_{y})\delta V+B_{u}\delta KC_{y}V=\delta VM_{i}+V\delta M_{i}.

(31)

We also add constraint
\|\vect(\delta K)\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon

to guarantee that only small perturbations are considered.
Finally, we add constraints

\Gamma_{l}^{T}(K+\delta K)\Gamma_{r}\leq g

and
\Gamma_{l}^{T}(K+\delta K)\Gamma_{r}=0

for each constraint

\Gamma_{l}^{T}K\Gamma_{r}\leq g
and
\Gamma_{l}^{T}K\Gamma_{r}=0
,
respectively.

In
[

the algorithm computes the largest set included in
\|Cx\|_{\infty}\leq\mu

that is invariant under all possible
\|u\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
This is done by starting with polyhedral set

\|Cx\|_{\infty}\leq\mu

and propagating the set backwards in time until the set becomes invariant,
in which case
\mu
is an upper bound for the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain,
or no longer contains
0
,
in which case
\mu
is a lower bound for the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain.
This is repeated for different values of
\mu
to obtain a sharp bound.

The approach of
[
15
]

computes the reachable set from the origin by forward propagation
in time.
After each step, the smallest
\mu
for which set

\|Cx\|_{\infty}\leq\mu
contains the reachable set
is a lower bound on the gain.

[
15
]
also provide bounds on the error
between the computed lower bound and the actual gain of the system,
as a function of the number of forward steps taken and of the
system spectral radius. This allows the algorithm
to terminate in a pre-determined finite number of steps
as a function of the required accuracy.
A drawback of the approach is that it scales worse
for inputs of large dimension.
This is because V-rep polyhedra must be used throughout,
including to represent the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
unit ball
of the image of the input
Bw
,
which by itself requires
2^{n_{w}}
vertices.

The approaches of both
[
4
]
and
[
15
]

can be applied to continuous time systems
through
a forward Euler discretization scheme
(from
\dot{x}=Ax
to
x^{+}=[I+\tau A]x
).
Discretizing the systems in this way
guarantees that the set invariance properties
necessary to bound the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
gain
will hold for the continuous system if they hold for the discretized system

[
4
]
.
By using smaller discretization time constants the bound on the gain
can then be made increasingly tighter, but
at an increasing computational cost.
This is a sharp difference with our approach, which is based on conditions
that apply directly to continuous time systems, without the need of any discretization step.

The approaches of
[
4
]

and
[
15
]

iteratively shrink or grow polyhedra at every step of computation. This may lead to
increasingly large matrices to represent the polyhedra,
with the matrix size growing exponentially with the number of steps taken.
In contrast, our approach does not shrink or grow polyhedra but reshapes them at each step,
keeping constant the size of the representation matrix.
This is important since the computational cost at each
iteration scales with the size of the polyhedral representation.
As such,
while for the approaches of
[
4
]

and
[
15
]

there is guaranteed convergence to the actual LTV/LPV gain in a finite number of steps
for discrete or discretized systems,
each step could become computationally intense, with
no upper bound obtained until the process terminates.
In contrast, there is no guaranteed convergence to the actual LTV/LPV gain
for our approach but
each step always has a fixed cost and produces a
tighter upper bound. The complexity of the polyhedra, characterized by
the number of vertices
m
, is governed by the user.

Another important unique strength of our optimization-based approach
is that it allows for synthesis problems to be tackled directly.

Compartmental System

We next consider a small compartmental system example.
Compartmental systems are naturally suited for pharmacological and epidemiological models,
water reservoirs and queuing systems,

Each compartment can have inflows and outflows, both internal and external.
In our example we consider a basic compartmental system where

6
Conclusions

We have discussed theoretical conditions for the estimation of bounds on the incremental
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}

gains of nonlinear systems. Their computation is performed via a two-step procedure based on linear programming problems.
To the best of our knowledge, the characterization of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
gain bounds is a novel contribution
while the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
bounds are closer to results available in the literature, based on set invariance.
The algorithms outlined in the paper are sound but susceptible to local optimal.
They can be used both for system analysis and for closed-loop control synthesis.
The gains computed can quantify performance specifications
or be used in conjunction with the small gain theorem
to prove stability of interconnections. The practical use of the
algorithms was illustrated by two numerical examples.

More work is needed on optimizing, tweaking, and formally evaluating
the properties of the optimization procedure used in the algorithm.
From the modes of failure observed
in sub-optimal solutions, it seems that explicitly enforcing symmetry
and adding the dual constraints of
the solutions from the gain estimation step
to the polyhedral modification step helps avoid a big class of local optima.
Other extensions include weighted norms and synthesis of nonlinear controllers
[
5
]
.
This is particularly relevant because it is well known that linear state feedback controllers are sub-optimal for the
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
problem
[
13
]
.
