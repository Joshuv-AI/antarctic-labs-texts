Thermal Transport and Non-Mechanical Forces in Metals
J. Amarel 1 , D. Belitz 1,2 , and T.R. Kirkpatrick 3

Abstract
We discuss contributions to the thermopower in an electron fluid. A simple argument based on Newton’s second law with the pressure gradient as the force suggests that the thermopower is given by a thermodynamic derivative, viz., the entropy per particle, rather than being an independent transport coefficient. The resolution is the existence of an entropic force that results from a coupling between the mass current and the heat current in the fluid. We also discuss and clarify some aspects of a recent paper (Phys. Rev. B 102 , 214306 (2020)) that provided a method for exactly solving electronic transport equations in the low-temperature limit.

I.2
A simple argument for the thermopower

An elementary argument for the thermopower can be constructed as follows. The electron mass current density is
{\bm{j}}_{m}=m{\bm{j}}_{n}
,
with

(3)

the system volume and
{\bm{F}}
the total force on the electron system.
cur

One contribution to the force density is the negative pressure gradient

{\bm{F}}_{p}/V=-{\bm{\nabla}}p\ ,

(4)

where
p
is the electronic contribution to the pressure.
Let us assume for now that this is the only contribution to the force, as would be the case in a purely mechanical description of the
fluid. In steady state,
\partial_{t}{\bm{j}}_{m}=0

3

\displaystyle{\bm{j}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tau}{m}\,{\bm{\nabla}}p

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\tau}{m}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V}{\bm{\nabla}}\mu-\frac{\tau}{m}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_{\mu,V}{\bm{\nabla}}T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n\tau}{m}\,{\bm{\nabla}}\mu-\frac{s\tau}{m}\,{\bm{\nabla}}T\ .

Here

and

are the number density and entropy density, respectively, and in going from the second line to the third one we have
used the fact that the pressure derivatives at constant volume are just derivatives of the grand canonical potential
\Omega=-pV
. We note that
the thermodynamics of a Fermi liquid with a short-ranged interaction apply since the effective force on the electrons is the unscreened external
force.
Pines and Nozières 1989

Comparing with Eq. (
1a

\sigma=ne^{2}\tau/m\ ,

(6)

and for the thermopower

-e{\mathfrak{S}}=s/n=S/N\ .

(7)

That is, according to this simple argument the thermopower is given by a thermodynamic derivative, namely, the electronic entropy per particle.
This is because
L_{11}
and
L_{12}
are given by thermodynamic derivatives multiplying the same
relaxation time
\tau

with the fact

and
L_{12}
are independent transport coefficients.

Since
L_{11}
and
L_{12}
both describe the response of the same current to a driving force, there is only one
relaxation time that can appear in Eq. (
5

contribution to the force in Eq. (
3
) that has nothing to do with the pressure gradient. In the next
section we will use kinetic theory to elucidate the nature of this non-mechanical or entropic force.

IV
Summary and Conclusion

In summary, we have identified two physically different contributions to the thermopower in a metal. One is due to
the mechanical force on the electrons, i.e., the gradient of the pressure of the Fermi liquid. The other one
is an entropic force that arises from the mass current coupling to the heat current. This is analogous at some level to the
contributions to the sound velocity in either a classical fluid
Forster 1975
or a fermionic quantum fluid.
Bel

A purely mechanical theory would conclude that the speed of sound is given by the isothermal compressibility
of the fluid; it is the coupling to the heat mode that changes this to the adiabatic compressibility. However, an
important difference is that the thermopower is a transport coefficient, whereas the speed of sound is
a thermodynamic derivative; the only question is which derivative.

We also have clarified some aspects of Ref.
Amarel et al. 2020
, which
gave a method exactly solving electronic transport problems in the low-temperature limit. Specifically,
the integral equations solved exactly in that reference were not quite equivalent to the Boltzmann
equation due to some approximations in the procedure that transforms the Boltzmann equation into
a one-dimensional integral equation. These approximations are not necessary, and eliminating them
leads to the exact solution of the Boltzmann equation proper that is given in Sec.
III.2
.
