1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field and let
\mathbb{F}^{n}
denote the space of all vectors of length
n
over
\mathbb{F}
.
An
\mathbb{F}
-linear code
C
of length
n
is a subspace of the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, see
[
Huffman
]
*Section 1.2, and elements of
C
are called
codewords
.
Denote the dimension of
C
by
k
.
Given a codeword
x\in C
, the
(Hamming) weight
of
x
, denoted
by
\mathrm{wt}(x)
, is the number of non-zero coordinates in
x
.
The
(minimum) distance
of
C
, denoted
d
or
d(C)
, is
d(C)=\min\{d(x,y)\ |\ x,y\in C,x\neq y\}
where
d(x,y)=\mathrm{wt}(x-y)
is the number of coordinate entries where
x
and
y
differ.
An
\mathbb{F}
-linear code of length
n
, dimension
k
and distance
d
is abbreviated
an
[n,k,d]
-linear code.
The
(information) rate
of an
[n,k,d]
-linear code
C
is
R(C)=k/n
.
We henceforth consider only linear codes.

Let
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},\dots
be a sequence of
\mathbb{F}
-linear codes where
C_{i}
has parameters
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]
.
We are interested in the question: How quickly can
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}
approach infinity? It is easiest to understand the growth as a function
of one variable, such as
k_{i}
,
d_{i}
or
n_{i}
. Since
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}\leqslant\min\{k_{i},d_{i}\}\leqslant n_{i}
such functions will be at most linear.
Asymptotically good code sequences are ones which satisfy both
k_{i}/n_{i}\geqslant c
and
d_{i}/n_{i}\geqslant c
for some constant
c>0
, see
[
EKL
]
*(1.1)
and
[
ShiWuSole
]
, and hence these code sequences achieve linear growth in
n_{i}
since
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}\geqslant c^{2}n_{i}
.
Such code sequences have been known
to exist since
[
CPW
]
, but they are not well understood, and the existence of asymptotically good
cyclic
codes has been a long standing open problem
[
MW
,
ShiWuSole
]

related to the uncertainty principle
[
EKL
]
. Other code sequences with reasonably good growth rates include the quadratic residue codes, see
[
Huffman
]
*Section 6.6. These have parameters
[p,(p+1)/2,d]
where
p
is a prime, and
d\geqslant\sqrt{p}
by
[
MacS
]
*p. 483, Theorem 1 so
kd/n
is at least

O(\sqrt{n})
. In Example
2.1
, we describe a subsequence of Reed-Muller codes over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
(
c.f.

[
L
]
) with faster growth

viz.

kd/n=O(n^{\log_{2}(3/2)})
. On the other hand, we might ask about ‘non-examples’, that is, code sequences for which
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}
does not grow. There are such examples even among well-known code families. For example, for any fixed prime-power
q
, there is an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear Reed-Solomon code
[
QBC
]
with parameters
[n,k,n-k+1]
provided
q\geqslant n
. The family of all such codes has
kd/n
bounded as
kd/n\leqslant\min\{k,d\}\leqslant n\leqslant q
.

We want
k
to be large (relative to
n
) to transmit a lot of information, and
d
to be large to correct many errors, see Section
2
for more details.
For linear codes over a finite field, the Singleton bound (
d\leqslant n-k+1
) exhibits the tension between
k
and
d
, and
Maximum Distance Separable (MDS) codes have
d=n-k+1
, see
[
San
]
.
In Theorem
1.1
we exhibit an explicit construction for a sequence
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},\dots
of
\mathbb{F}
-linear codes, where
\mathbb{F}
is a fixed but arbitrary field, and where
C_{i}
has parameters
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]
and
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}=O(\sqrt{k_{i}})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each field
\mathbb{F}
, there exists an infinite sequence
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},\dots
of
\,\mathbb{F}
-linear codes such that
C_{i}
has parameters
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]
that satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{k_{i}d_{i}}{n_{i}}=2i>\sqrt{k_{i}}-1>2i-1\qquad\textup{ for $i\geqslant 1$}.

The codes
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},\dots
in Theorem
1.1
are described in Definitions
4.4
and
4.6
. They involve a novel construction described in Section
3
, and their parameters
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]
are determined in Theorem
4.7
. The codes are
\mathbb{F}
-linear, where
\mathbb{F}
is an arbitrary field, and their limiting properties are similar to the well-known Reed-Muller codes, see Example
2.1
.

New codes can also be constructed from old codes via “direct sums” and “repetition”. Both of these constructions fix the quantity
kd/n
, for
suppose that
C
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear code with parameters
[n,k,d]
, and
s
is a positive integer.
The direct sum code
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}C\leqslant\mathbb{F}^{ns}
has parameters
[ns,ks,d]
, and the quantity
(ks)d/(ns)=kd/n
is independent of
s
. Similarly, the repetition
code
\textup{diag}(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}C)=\{(u,\dots,u)\mid u\in C\}
has parameters
[ns,k,ds]
, and again
k(ds)/(ns)=kd/n
is independent of
s
.
By contrast, repeated application of the construction introduced to prove Theorem
4.7
allows

to grow without bound.

2.
Further remarks

We represent the vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
by
n
-dimensional column vectors over
\mathbb{F}
. Let
C
be an
[n,k,d]
-linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
and let
B=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
be an ordered basis of
C
. Let
H=[a_{1},\dots,a_{k}]
be the
n\times k
matrix formed by this basis. Then the code
C
is equal to the set
\{Hx\mid x\in\mathbb{F}^{k}\}
, and we regard an element
x\in\mathbb{F}^{k}
as an information vector which is encoded as a codeword
Hx
. As
H
has rank
k
, by permuting the coordinates of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
if necessary, we will assume that the

k\times k
sub-matrix formed by the first
k
rows of
H
is invertible. Thus the information vector
x
is uniquely determined by the first
k
entries of the associated codeword
Hx
and the last
n-k
entries of
Hx
may be regarded as redundancy added to enable transmission errors to be corrected.

The proportion
k/n
is called the
rate

R(C)
of
C
, and is a measure of the efficiency of
C
in encoding information. The third parameter, the minimum distance
d
of
C
is directly related to the error correction capability of
C
: if strictly less than
d/2
entries in a codeword are changed during transmission then the received
n
-tuple is closer (in the Hamming metric) to the original codeword transmitted than to any other codeword, and hence these ‘errors’ can be corrected and the correct codeword determined. A high value of
d
relative to
n
denotes high reliability in transmitting information correctly.

Both
k/n
and the
relative distance

d/n
are less than
1
: the larger the rate
k/n
the more efficient is the code, while the larger the relative distance
d/n
the more reliable is the code. The question addressed in this paper is: How quickly can the quantity
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}
approach infinity? Our construction demonstrates that this quantity
kd/n
can grow as fast as
\sqrt{k}
for codes over any field
\mathbb{F}
.

Example 2.1
.

A Reed-Muller code
\textup{RM}(m,r)
is an
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear code, with parameters
[2^{m},\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{m}{j},2^{m-r}]
by
[
Huffman
]
*Theorem 1.10.1.
Thus
k_{r}d_{r}/n_{r}=2^{-r}\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{m}{j}
for
\textup{RM}(m,r)
. The subsequence
(\textup{RM}(2r+1,r))_{r\geqslant 1}

has
\dim(\textup{RM}(2r+1,r))=k_{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\binom{2r+1}{j}=2^{2r}
and parameters
[2^{2r+1},2^{2r},2^{r+1}]
. Therefore
k_{r}d_{r}/n_{r}=\sqrt{k_{r}}=O(\sqrt{n_{r}})
. It turns out that the Reed Muller codes
\textup{RM}(2r+1,r)
, and
the codes
C_{r}
in the Theorem
4.7
have
k_{r}d_{r}/n_{r}=O(\sqrt{k_{r}})
, and the quadratic residue codes have
k_{r}d_{r}/n_{r}
at least
\sqrt{k_{r}}
.

A faster growing subsequence is
(\textup{RM}(m,\lfloor\frac{m}{3}\rfloor+1))_{m\geqslant 1}
. It follows from
[
GP
]
*Theorem 2 that the parameters

[n_{m},k_{m},d_{m}]
of
\textup{RM}(m,\lfloor\frac{m}{3}\rfloor+1)
satisfy
k_{m}d_{m}/n_{m}
is asymptotic to

\frac{3}{\sqrt{\pi m}}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{m}=O\left(\frac{n_{m}^{\log_{2}(3/2)}}{\sqrt{\log_{2}(n_{m})}}\right)\qquad\textup{ where $n_{m}=2^{m}$ and $\log_{2}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\approx 0.585$}.

3.
Bounded linear codes

In this section we present, and investigate, a construction which, given a linear code as input, produces a linear code with strictly larger length, dimension and minimum distance. We will be concerned with the extent to which the construction preserves the property of being bounded, which is defined as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a positive integer,
C
an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n,k,d]
-code, and
B=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
an ordered basis for
C
. Then
C
is said to be
u
-bounded relative to
B
if the following three conditions hold:

(i)

\mathrm{wt}(a_{j})=u
for each
j
;

(ii)

\mathrm{wt}(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}
)=
d
; and

(iii)

u\geqslant d(1+k^{-1})
.

We say that
C
is
bounded
if there exist
u
and
B
such that
C
is
u
-bounded relative to
B
.

3.1.
The construction

Here we present the construction applied to an arbitrary linear code, and examine when the boundedness property is preserved.

Construction 3.2
.

We have the following input an output.

Input:
Let
C
an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n,k,d]
-code in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
with ordered basis
B=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})
.

Output:
The
\mathbb{F}
-linear code
{\sf Code}(B)
in
\mathbb{F}^{n(k+1)}
which is the
\mathbb{F}
-linear span of the sequence
{\sf Basis}(B)=(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots a_{k+1}^{\prime})
, where

a_{1}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k}\end{bmatrix},\ a_{2}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{k}\\
0\\
a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k-1}\end{bmatrix},\ \dots,\ a_{k+1}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k}\\
0\\
\end{bmatrix},

and
0
denotes the zero vector in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
.

We show that the output
{\sf Basis}(B)
from Construction
3.2
is linearly independent, determine the parameters of the code
{\sf Code}(B)
, and find conditions under which
{\sf Code}(B)
is bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{F},C,n,k,d,B
be as in Construction
3.2
.

(a)

Then
{\sf Basis}(B)
is an ordered basis for
{\sf Code}(B)
, and
{\sf Code}(B)
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n^{\prime},k^{\prime},d^{\prime}]
-code, where

n^{\prime}=(k+1)n,\quad k^{\prime}=k+1\quad\text{and $d^{\prime}$ satisfies}\quad d^{\prime}\geqslant kd.

(b)

Further, if
C
is
u
-bounded relative to
B
, then
d^{\prime}=(k+1)d
, and, setting
u^{\prime}=ku
,
{\sf Code}(B)
is
u^{\prime}
-bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B)
if and only if
u\geqslant d(1+2k^{-1})
.

Proof.

(a) By Construction
3.2
,
{\sf Code}(B)
has length
n^{\prime}=(k+1)n
. Further, the fact that the
a_{j}
are linearly independent implies that the
a_{j}^{\prime}
are linearly independent, so
{\sf Basis}(B)
is an ordered basis for
{\sf Code}(B)
, and therefore
{\sf Code}(B)
has dimension
k^{\prime}=k+1
.
It remains to bound the minimum distance
d^{\prime}
.
An arbitrary non-zero codeword
w
in
{\sf Code}(B)
has the form
w=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}c_{j}a_{j}^{\prime}
for elements
c_{j}\in\mathbb{F}
, not all zero.
We will prove that
\mathrm{wt}(w)\geqslant kd
, whence
d^{\prime}\geqslant kd
.
Write

w=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\\
\vdots\\
w_{k+1}\end{bmatrix},

where each
w_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Then by the definition of the
a_{j}^{\prime}
in Construction
3.2
,

(1)

w_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{i-j}a_{j},\quad\mbox{for $i=1,\dots,k+1$, reading subscripts modulo $k+1$}.

In particular each
w_{i}
is a codeword of
C
. Thus if each of the
w_{i}
is nonzero, then

\mathrm{wt}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathrm{wt}(w_{i})\geqslant(k+1)d
.
Suppose now that, for some
i
,
w_{i}=0
. Since the
a_{j}
are linearly independent, it follows from (
1
) that
c_{i-j}=0
for
j=1,\dots,k
. Then since
w\neq 0
, we must have
c_{i}\neq 0
so
w=c_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}
, and hence

(2)

\mathrm{wt}(w)=\mathrm{wt}(a_{i}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{wt}(a_{j})\geqslant kd

since each
a_{i}
is a codeword of
C
and so has weight at least
d
. This proves part (a).

(b) Now assume that
C
is
u
-bounded relative to
B
. Let
w=\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}c_{j}a_{j}^{\prime}
be an arbitrary non-zero codeword in
{\sf Code}(B)
, and define the
w_{i}
as above so that
\mathrm{wt}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathrm{wt}(w_{i})
, and equation (
1
) holds. If each of the
w_{i}
is nonzero then
\mathrm{wt}(w)\geqslant(k+1)d
. On the other hand if some
w_{i}=0
, then we showed above that equation (
2
) holds, so
\mathrm{wt}(w)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathrm{wt}(a_{j})
. In this case, by Definition
3.1
(i), we have
\mathrm{wt}(a_{j})=u
for each
j
, and hence
\mathrm{wt}(w)=ku
. Further, by Definition
3.1
(iii),
u\geqslant d(1+k^{-1})
, and hence
\mathrm{wt}(w)=ku\geqslant(k+1)d
. Thus the minimum distance
d^{\prime}
of
{\sf Code}(B)
satisfies
d^{\prime}\geqslant(k+1)d
. To see that equality holds, consider the
the codeword
w
obtained by taking
c_{1}=\dots=c_{k+1}=1
. For each
i
, equation (
1
) shows that
w_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}
, and hence
\mathrm{wt}(w_{i})=d
by Definition
3.1
(ii). Therefore, for this codeword
w
we have
\mathrm{wt}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mathrm{wt}(w_{i})=(k+1)d
, and we conclude that
d^{\prime}=(k+1)d
.

Now set
u^{\prime}=ku
. First, from the definition of the
a_{j}^{\prime}
we have
\mathrm{wt}(a_{j}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{wt}(a_{i})
, which is equal to
ku=u^{\prime}
by Definition
3.1
(i).
Second, we showed in the previous paragraph that
\mathrm{wt}(\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}a_{i}^{\prime})=(k+1)d=d^{\prime}
. Third, since
u^{\prime}=ku
and
d^{\prime}(1+(k^{\prime})^{-1})=(k+1)d(1+(k+1)^{-1})=d(k+2)
, it follows that
u^{\prime}\geqslant d^{\prime}(1+(k^{\prime})^{-1})
if and only if
u\geqslant d(k+2)/k=d(1+2k^{-1})
. Thus, by Definition
3.2
,
{\sf Code}(B)
is
u^{\prime}
-bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B)
if and only if
u\geqslant d(1+2k^{-1})
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Recursive applications of the construction

By Proposition
3.3
(a), the output code
{\sf Code}(B)
of Construction
3.2
is always a linear code with ordered basis
{\sf Basis}(B)
, and hence Construction
3.2
may be applied repeatedly, producing larger and larger codes. Moreover, Proposition
3.3
(b) implies that, provided the parameter
u
is
sufficiently large,
{\sf Code}(B)
is bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B)
. We investigate how many times we may apply Construction
3.2
recursively and still obtain a bounded code. To keep track of these repeated applications of Construction
3.2
we introduce some natural notation for the output codes and bases.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
i
be a positive integer, and let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}
-linear code with an ordered basis
B
. In terms of the output of Construction
3.2
applied repeatedly to
C,B
, for
i=1
, let

{\sf Code}(B,1)={\sf Code}(B)\quad\text{and}\quad{\sf Basis}(B,1)={\sf Basis}(B),

and for
i\geqslant 2
, define recursively,

{\sf Code}(B,i)={\sf Code}({\sf Basis}(B,i-1))\quad\text{and}\quad{\sf Basis}(B,i)={\sf Basis}({\sf Basis}(B,i-1)).

We examine the parameters and boundedness of
{\sf Code}(B,i)
for various values of
i
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
i
be a positive integer, and let
C
be an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n,k,d]
-code with an ordered basis
B
.

(a)

Then
{\sf Code}(B,i)
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]
-code, where

n_{i}=n\prod_{j=1}^{i}(k+j),\quad k_{i}=k+i,\quad d_{i}\geqslant d\prod_{j=1}^{i}(k+j-1).

(b)

Further, suppose that
C
is
u
-bounded relative to
B
, and
u\geqslant d(1+ik^{-1})
, that is
i\leqslant k(ud^{-1}-1)
. Then the minimum distance
d_{i}
of
{\sf Code}(B,i)
is

d_{i}=d\prod_{j=1}^{i}(k+j),\quad\mbox{and setting}\quad u_{i}=u\prod_{j=1}^{i}(k+j-1),

{\sf Code}(B,i)
is
u_{i}
-bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B,i)
if and only if
u\geqslant d(1+(i+1)k^{-1})
.

Proof.

(a) Our proof is by induction on
i
. It follows from Proposition
3.3
(a) that
{\sf Code}(B,1)
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n_{1},k_{1},d_{1}]
-code
where
n_{1}=n(k+1),k_{1}=k+1
and
d_{1}\geqslant dk
. Suppose that
i\geqslant 2
and assume that the assertions of part (a) hold for
{\sf Code}(B,i-1)
.
Proposition
3.3
(a) implies that
n_{i}=n_{i-1}(k_{i-1}+1),k_{i}=k_{i-1}+1
and
d_{i}\geqslant d_{i-1}k_{i-1}
. Hence the assertions of part (a) hold for also for
{\sf Code}(B,i)
.

(b) Now suppose that
C
is
u
-bounded relative to the ordered basis
B
. Then
u\geqslant d(1+k^{-1})
by Definition
3.1
(iii), and by Proposition
3.3
(b), the minimum distance
d_{1}
of
{\sf Code}(B,1)
is
d_{1}=d(k+1)
; also
{\sf Code}(B,1)
is
u_{1}
-bounded, where
u_{1}=ku
, if and only if
u\geqslant d(1+2k^{-1})
. Thus part (b) holds for
i=1
. Assume now that
i\geqslant 2
and
i\leqslant k(ud^{-1}-1)
, and also that part (b) holds for

, that is,

d_{i-1}=d\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(k+j),\quad\mbox{and setting}\quad u_{i-1}=u\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(k+j-1),

{\sf Code}(B,i-1)
is
u_{i-1}
-bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B,i-1)
if and only if
u\geqslant d(1+ik^{-1})
. Since we are assuming that the inequality

{\sf Code}(B,i-1)
is

-bounded relative to
{\sf Basis}(B,i-1)
. Thus applying Proposition

(b) to
{\sf Code}(B,i-1)
with
{\sf Basis}(B,i-1)
, shows that
d_{i}=d_{i-1}(k_{i-1}+1)

{\sf Code}(B,i)

(u_{i-1}k_{i-1})
-bounded relative to

if and only if
u_{i-1}\geqslant d_{i-1}(1+2k_{i-1}^{-1})
.
Since

by part (a), we conclude that

is as in part (b). Also, since
u_{i-1}k_{i-1}=u\prod_{j=1}^{i}(k+j-1)

,

is

-bounded relative to

u_{i-1},d_{i-1},k_{i-1}
, this inequality is

u\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(k+j-1)\geqslant\left(d\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(k+j)\right)\frac{k+i+1}{k+i-1},

that is,
u\geqslant dk^{-1}(k+i+1)=d(1+(i+1)k^{-1})
. Thus part (b) is proved by induction.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We note that, the quantity mentioned in the introduction, namely the rate times the minimum distance, grows slowly with the number
i
of applications of Construction
3.2
: for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k(ud^{-1}-1)
we have, by Proposition
3.5
, that

\frac{k_{i}d_{i}}{n_{i}}=\frac{(k+i)d}{n}=\frac{kd}{n}+\frac{id}{n}.

4.
Explicit instances of the construction

In this section we construct a family of linear codes which are defined as column spaces of a certain family of square matrices. First we introduce the matrices.

4.1.
A family of matrices

As always
\mathbb{F}
denotes an arbitrary field. We introduce an infinite family of matrices over
\mathbb{F}
, and explore some of their properties. We denote the space of
m\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{F}
by
\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
; we denote the zero matrix in this ring by
0_{m\times n}
, or sometimes just by
0
; and if
m=n
we denote the identity matrix by
I_{m}
, and the determinant of
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times m}
by
\det(\mathcal{A})
. For a matrix
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
, its
ij
-entry is denoted
\mathcal{A}_{ij}
, and its
transpose
obtained by interchanging rows and columns is denoted
\mathcal{A}^{T}
, that is
(\mathcal{A}^{T})_{ij}=\mathcal{A}_{ji}
for all
i,j
. For simplicity of the exposition we will sometimes represent matrices as block matrices where some
(a\times b
)-block may be an
empty matrix
, that is
a=0
or
b=0
are allowed.

Definition 4.1
.

For a positive integer
i
, we define matrices
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathcal{B}_{i}
as follows. For
i=1
, these are

\mathcal{A}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix},

and recursively, for
i\geqslant 1
, if
\mathcal{A}_{i},\mathcal{B}_{i}
have been defined, then we let

\mathcal{A}_{i+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{1}&\mathcal{B}_{i}\\
-\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}&\mathcal{A}_{i}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}_{i+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{B}_{1}&\mathcal{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Our first observations about the family are given in this lemma. The idea behind the proof of Lemma
4.2
we found in
[
Silvester
]
*Section 5, Acknowledgements.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
i
be a positive integer and
\mathcal{A}_{i},\mathcal{B}_{i}
be as in Definition
4.1
. Then

(a)

\mathcal{B}_{i}=[\mathcal{B}_{1}\cdots\mathcal{B}_{1}]\in\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2i}
; for
a\in\{1,2\}
and
b\in\{1,\dots,2i\}
we have
(\mathcal{B}_{i})_{ab}=(-1)^{a+b}
and
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}_{i}=\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{i}=0_{2\times 2}
.

(b)

\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{2i\times 2i}
satisfies
\det(\mathcal{A}_{i})=1
.

Proof.

(a) A straightforward inductive argument, based on the definition of
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
in Definition
4.1
shows that
\mathcal{B}_{i}=[\mathcal{B}_{1}\cdots\mathcal{B}_{1}]\in\mathbb{F}^{2\times 2i}
for each
i\geqslant 1
. Then it is easy to see that
(\mathcal{B}_{i})_{ab}=(-1)^{a+b}
for all
a,b
. It follows from
\mathcal{B}_{i}=[\mathcal{B}_{1}\cdots\mathcal{B}_{1}]
that
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathcal{B}_{1}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}_{1}
, and computing we see that
\mathcal{B}_{1}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{1}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}_{1}=0_{2\times 2}
. Hence
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}\mathcal{B}_{i}=0_{2\times 2}
, and the final assertion
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{i}=0_{2\times 2}
follows from the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}=-\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

(b) It is easy to see that
\det(\mathcal{A}_{1})=1
, and
\mathcal{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{2i\times 2i}
for each
i\geqslant 1
. Assume that
i\geqslant 1
, assume inductively that
\det(\mathcal{A}_{i})=1
, and consider
\mathcal{A}_{i+1}
as defined in Definition
4.1
. Modifying an idea from
[
Silvester
]
*Section 5, we see by direct computation that

\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{1}&\mathcal{B}_{i}\\
-\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}&\mathcal{A}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}&-\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{i}\\
0_{2i\times 2}&I_{2i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{1}&0_{2\times 2i}\\
-\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}&\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{i}+\mathcal{A}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{A}_{1}&0_{2\times 2i}\\
-\mathcal{B}_{i}^{T}&\mathcal{A}_{i}\end{bmatrix}

where the last equality follows from part (a). Since the second matrix on the left side has determinant 1, it follows that
\det(\mathcal{A}_{i+1})=\det(\mathcal{A}_{1})\det(\mathcal{A}_{i})=1
where the last equality uses the inductive hypothesis. Thus part (b) follows by induction.
∎

The next lemma looks at properties of the columns of the
\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
i
be a positive integer, and let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{2i}]

be as in Definition
4.1
, where
a_{j}
is the
j
th column of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Then

(a)

\mathrm{wt}(a_{j})=2i-1
for
j\in\{1,\dots,2i\}
,

(b)

a_{2j-1}+a_{2j}=[0_{1\times 2(j-1)},-1,1,0_{1\times 2(i-j)}]^{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{2i\times 1}

for
j\in\{1,\dots,i\}
,

(c)

\sum_{j=1}^{2i-1}a_{j}=[0,\dots,0,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{2i\times 1}
, and in particular,
\mathrm{wt}(\sum_{j=1}^{2i-1}a_{j})=1
.

Proof.

(a) Part (a) follows immediately from Definition
4.1
.

(b) We use induction on
i
. For
i=1
,
a_{1}+a_{2}=[-1,1]^{T}
by Definition
4.1
, so (b) holds in this case (as
i=j=1
and the first and last entries in this vector in (b) are empty matrices). Now let
i\geqslant 1
and assume inductively that (b) holds for
\mathcal{A}_{i}=[a_{1},\dots,a_{2i}]
, and consider
\mathcal{A}_{i+1}=[a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{2i+2}^{\prime}]
. By Definition
4.1
and the structure of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
given by Lemma
4.2
(a), it follows that
a_{1}^{\prime}+a_{2}^{\prime}=[-1,1,0_{1\times 2i}]^{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{2(i+1)\times 1}
. For
j\in\{2,\dots,i+1\}
, it follows from Definition
4.1
that

a_{2j-1}^{\prime}+a_{2j}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{2\times 1}\\
a_{2j-3}+a_{2j-2}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence the structure of
a_{2j-1}^{\prime}+a_{2j}^{\prime}
follows from the inductive hypothesis. Thus part (b) is proved by induction.

(c) This part is also proved by induction on
i
. The case
i=1
follows from the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
. So assume that
i\geqslant 1
and that part (c) holds for
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, that is
b:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{2i-1}a_{\ell}=[0,\dots,0,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{2i\times 1}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{i+1}
have columns
a_{j}
and
a^{\prime}_{j}
as in the proof of part (b). Using the structure of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
from Lemma
4.2
(a), the form of
a_{1}^{\prime}+a_{2}^{\prime}
above, and the inductive hypothesis, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{2i+1}a_{j}^{\prime}=(a_{1}^{\prime}+a_{2}^{\prime})+\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\\
\sum_{\ell=1}^{2i-1}a_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\
1\\
0_{2i\times 1}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\\
b\end{bmatrix}=[\ 0,\ \dots\ ,\ 0,\ 1\ ]^{T}\in\mathbb{F}^{2(i+1)\times 1}.

Part (c) follows by induction.
∎

4.2.
Codes constructed from matrices

We now construct
\mathbb{F}
-linear codes from the matrices in Subsection
4.1
, noting that the columns of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
are linearly independent by Lemma
4.2
(b) .

Definition 4.4
.

Let
i
be a positive integer, let
\mathcal{A}_{i}=[a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{2i}]\in\mathbb{F}^{2i\times 2i}
be
as in Definition
4.1
, where
a_{j}
is the
j
th column of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, and let

C(i)
be the
\mathbb{F}
-linear code in
\mathbb{F}^{2i}
with ordered basis
B(i)=(a_{1},\dots,a_{2i-1})
.

We use the results from Subsection
4.1
to determine the properties of
C(i)
, and the results from Section
3
to study the codes obtained using Proposition
3.5
.

Proposition 4.5
.

For a positive integer
i
, let
C(i)
and
B(i)
be as in Definition
4.4
.

(a)

For
i\geqslant 2
, the code
C(i)
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[2i,2i-1,1]
-code which is
(2i-1)
-bounded relative to the ordered basis
B(i)
.

(b)

For
1\leqslant j\leqslant 4i^{2}-6i+1
, the code
{\sf Code}(B(i),j)
, as defined in Definition
3.4
, is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n_{j},k_{j},d_{j}]
-code that is
u_{j}
-bounded relative to the ordered basis
{\sf Basis}(B(i),j)
, where

n_{j}=2i\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}(2i-1+\ell),\quad k_{j}=2i-1+j,\quad d_{j}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}(2i-1+\ell),\ \mbox{and}\ u_{j}=(2i-1)\prod_{\ell=1}^{j}(2i-2+\ell).

Proof.

(a)
By Lemma
4.2
(b),
B(i)
is an ordered basis for
C(i)
. Thus
C(i)
has length
2i
and dimension is
2i-1
. Moreover,
d(C(i))=1
as
[0,\dots,0,1]^{T}\in C(i)
by Lemma
4.3
(c).
By Lemma
4.3
(a),
\mathrm{wt}(a_{j})=2i-1
for each
j\leqslant 2i-1
, and
\mathrm{wt}(\sum_{j=1}^{2i-1}a_{j})=1
.
To see that
C(i)
is
(2i-1)
-bounded relative to
B(i)
for each
i\geqslant 2
we use Definition
3.1

and note that
2i-1\geqslant 1+(2i-1)^{-1}
holds (this is true for
i\geqslant 2
). This proves part (a).

(b) We apply Proposition
3.5
(b) to
C(i)
and
B(i)
. By part (a) the parameters
n,u,k,d
of that result are:
n=2i,u=k=2i-1,d=1
, and we wish to apply Proposition
3.5
(b) with a positive integer parameter
j
satisfying
u\geqslant d(1+(j+1)k^{-1})
, or equivalently
j+1\leqslant k(ud^{-1}-1)=(2i-1)(2i-2)=4i^{2}-6i+2
. This latter inequality is our assumption on
j
, so we may
therefore apply Construction
3.2

j
times and conclude, by Proposition
3.5
(b), that
{\sf Code}(B(i),j)
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear
[n_{j},k_{j},d_{j}]
-code that is
u_{j}
-bounded relative to the ordered basis
{\sf Basis}(B(i),j)
, where
the parameters
n_{j},k_{j},d_{j},u_{j}
are as in the statement. This proves part (b).
∎

In Proposition
4.5
(b) we have
n_{j}=2id_{j}
and
u_{j}(2i+j-1)=(2i-1)d_{j}
, respectively.
Note that setting
j=0
in the expressions for
n_{j},k_{j},d_{j},u_{j}
in Proposition
4.5
(b), we obtain the parameters
n,k,d,u
, respectively, for the code
C(i)
examined in Proposition
4.5
(a). Thus if we set
{\sf Code}(B(i),0):=C(i)
, then all the assertions of Proposition
4.5
(b) hold with
j=0
. Thus we
have constructed explicitly a two-parameter family of bounded linear codes over an arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
, namely:

{\sf Code}(B(i),j)
for all
i\geqslant 2
and all integers
j
satisfying
0\leqslant j\leqslant 4i^{2}-6i+1
.

We write
{\sf Code}_{\,\mathbb{F}}(B(i),j)
and
{\sf Basis}_{\,\mathbb{F}}(B(i),j)
if we wish to emphasize the field
\mathbb{F}
of scalars. To prove our main result we investigate a one-parameter subfamily of these codes, choosing, for each value of
i
, the code with the largest value of
j
. We now replace
i
with
i+1
.

Definition 4.6
.

For a field
\mathbb{F}
of scalars, and each
i\geqslant 1
, define

C_{i}:={\sf Code}_{\,\mathbb{F}}(B(i+1),4i^{2}-2i-1)\quad\textup{and}\quad\mathcal{B}_{i}:={\sf Basis}_{\,\mathbb{F}}(B(i+1),4i^{2}-2i-1).

The following theorem follows immediately from Proposition
4.5
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Given a field
\mathbb{F}
and an integer
i\geqslant 1
, the code
C_{i}
is a linear
[N_{i},K_{i},D_{i}]
-code over
\mathbb{F}
, which is
U_{i}
-bounded relative to its ordered basis
\mathcal{B}_{i}
, where

N_{i}=2(i+1)\prod_{\ell=1}^{4i^{2}-2i-1}(2i+1+\ell),\quad K_{i}=4(i+1)i,\quad D_{i}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{4i^{2}-2i-1}(2i+1+\ell),\

and
U_{i}=(2i+1)\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{4i^{2}-2i-1}(2i+\ell)
.
Moreover,
\displaystyle{\frac{K_{i}D_{i}}{N_{i}}=2i}
grows linearly with
i
.

Proof.

The first assertions follow immediately from Proposition
4.5
by replacing
i
with
i+1
. Finally, from these parameters we see that
\displaystyle{\frac{K_{i}D_{i}}{N_{i}}=\frac{4(i+1)i}{2i}=2i}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

By Proposition
4.7
there are
[n_{i},k_{i},d_{i}]
-codes
C_{i}
,
i\geqslant 1
, where
k_{i}d_{i}/n_{i}=2i
and
k_{i}=4(i+1)i
. A simple calculation shows that

2i>\sqrt{4i(i+1)}-1>\sqrt{4i^{2}}-1=2i-1.\qed
