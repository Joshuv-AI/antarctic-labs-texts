Subgroups with a small sum of element orders
Mihai-Silviu Lazorec

Abstract
A finite group G G is said to be a ℬ ψ \mathscr{B}_{\psi} -group if ψ ⁡ ( H ) < | G | \psi(H)<|G| for any proper subgroup H H of G G , where ψ ⁡ ( H ) \psi(H) denotes the sum of element orders of H H . In this paper, we characterize the ℬ ψ \mathscr{B}_{\psi} -groups up to the class of finite nilpotent groups. We also study the above inequality in the case of finite P P -groups and Schmidt groups, respectively.

1
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group and let
n
be a positive integer. We denote by
o(x),exp(G),\pi(G)
and
C_{n}
the order of an element
x\in G
, the exponent of
G
, the set of prime divisors of
|G|
and the cyclic group of order
n
, respectively. By adding all element orders of
G
, we get the quantity

\psi(G)=\sum\limits_{x\in G}o(x)

known as the sum of element orders of
G
. During the past 12 years, this quantity was of some interest especially because it was proved that it can be used to characterize various classes of finite groups. More exactly, for a group
G
of order
n
, it was shown that if
\frac{\psi(G)}{\psi(C_{n})}>\alpha
, for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
G
is cyclic/nilpotent/supersolvable/solvable (see
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
12
,
17
]
). For each case, the best lower bound
\alpha
was precisely determined as being
\alpha=\frac{\psi(H)}{\psi(C_{|H|})}
, where
H
is the smallest non-cyclic/non-nilpotent/non-supersolvable/non-solvable group.

In
[
1
]
, it was proved that the maximum value of the sum of element orders among all groups of order
n
is
\psi(C_{n})
. This means that if
G
is a group of order
n
, then
\psi(G)\leq\psi(C_{n})
and the equality holds if and only if
G\cong C_{n}
. Obviously one may ask if something can be said about the minimum value of the sum of element orders. An answer is given up to nilpotent groups. More exactly, in
[
2
]
, the authors proved the following result:

Theorem 1.1.

Let
G
be a group of order
n
. Then
\psi(G)\leq\psi(H)
for every nilpotent group
H
of order
n
if and only if the Sylow subgroups of
G
are of prime exponent.

In
[
11
]
, the authors classified the finite abelian groups determined by an inequality involving the sum of element orders. They said that a finite group
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group
if the inequality

\psi(H)<|G|

holds for all proper subgroups
H
of
G
. Obviously, in order to inspect if
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group, it suffices to check if the maximal subgroups of
G
satisfy the above mentioned inequality. The classification of the finite abelian
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups is the following one:

Theorem 1.2.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. Then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group if and only if
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
G\cong C_{p}^{n}
, where
p
is a prime and
n\geq 1
.

Intuitively, both theorems conclude that if one wants to lower the sum of element orders of a finite group
G
, then a significant part of the group element orders should be equal to a prime/square-free number/square of a prime. The results of this paper also support this idea. Our main objective is to study the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property beyond finite abelian groups. A first step is to outline a connection between this property and a specific normal Hall
\pi
-subgroup of a finite group
G
. Then, we show that a finite nilpotent group
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group if and only if
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
G
is a
p
-group with
exp(G)=p
. We enumerate the non-nilpotent solvable
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups up to order 2000. Such a group
G
has the property
|\pi(G)|=2
. Inspired by this remark, we study the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of two classes of finite groups whose orders are divisible by two primes:
P
-groups and Schmidt groups.

We recall the definition of a finite
P
-group by following section 2.2 of
[
15
]
. Let
p
be a prime number and
n\geq 2
be an integer. We say that a finite group
G
belongs to the class
P(n,p)
if
G
is either an elementary abelian
p
-group of order
p^{n}
, or a semidirect product of an elementary abelian normal
p
-subgroup
H
of order
p^{n-1}
by a group of prime order
q\neq p
which induces a non-trivial power automorphism on
H
. We call
G
a
P
-group if
G
belongs to
P(n,p)
, for some prime
p
and some integer
n\geq 2
. If
p=2
, it is known that the elementary abelian 2-groups are the only finite
P
-groups, while if
p>2
, besides the elementary abelian
p
-group, one can construct a non-abelian
P
-group containing elements of order
q
, for every prime
q
such that
q|(p-1)
.

We end this section by pointing out that some further research directions are outlined throughout the paper.

2
Main results

We begin this section by recalling some well-known results concerning the multiplicativity property of
\psi
and the computation of the sum of element orders of a finite cyclic
p
-group. Also, we prove an inequality that is going to be used at some point.

Lemma 2.1.

The following statements hold:

i)

\psi(C_{p^{n}})=\frac{p^{2n+1}+1}{p+1}
, where
p
is prime and
n\geq 1
is an integer;

ii)

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be finite groups. Then
\psi(G_{1}\times G_{2})\leq\psi(G_{1})\psi(G_{2})
and the equality holds if and only if
(|G_{1}|,|G_{2}|)=1
;

iii)

Let
p,q
be distinct primes and let
\alpha\geq 1,\beta\geq 2
be some integers. Then
\psi(C_{p}^{\alpha})\psi(C_{q}^{\beta-1})>p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}.

Proof.
For the first two items, the reader may check Lemma 2.1 of
[
2
]
and Lemma 2.9 (1) of
[
12
]
, respectively. For
iii)
, we have

\psi(C_{p}^{\alpha})\psi(C_{q}^{\beta-1})>p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}\Longleftrightarrow(p^{\alpha+1}-p+1)(q^{\beta}-q+1)-p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}>0.

To prove the above inequality, it suffices to show that the function
f:[2,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, given by

f(x)=(px-p+1)(q^{\beta}-q+1)-xq^{\beta},\ \forall\ x\in[2,\infty),

takes positive values. Obviously,
f
is derivable and

f^{\prime}(x)=(p-1)q^{\beta}-pq+p\geq(p-1)q^{2}-pq+p>0,

so
f(x)\geq f(2),\ \forall\ x\in[2,\infty)
. Since

f(2)=(p+1)(q^{\beta}-q+1)-2q^{\beta}\geq q^{\beta}-3q+3\geq q^{2}-3q+3>0,

it follows that
f
takes only positive values.
Hence, the proof is complete.

Since a finite nilpotent group is isomorphic to the direct product of its Sylow subgroups, to investigate the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of such a group, we need to solve the case of finite
p
-groups first.

Proposition 2.2.

Let
G
be a finite
p
-group. Then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group if and only if
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
exp(G)=p
.

Proof.
Let
G
be a
p
-group of order
p^{n}
, where
n\geq 1
. Assume that
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. If
n\in\{1,2\}
, then
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
exp(G)=p
, as desired. Let
n\geq 3
and
exp(G)=p^{m}
, where
m\geq 1
. Clearly
m<n
since otherwise
G
would be a cyclic
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group of order
|G|\geq p^{3}
. Then the conclusion of Theorem 1.2 would not hold, a contradiction. It follows that there is a maximal subgroup
H
of
G
containing an element of order
p^{m}
. We get

\psi(H)\geq 1+p^{m}+(p^{n-1}-2)p=|G|+p^{m}-2p+1.

If
m\geq 2
, we obtain
\psi(H)\geq|G|+(p-1)^{2}>|G|
, contradicting the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of
G
. Hence,
m=1
and
exp(G)=p
.

Conversely, if
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
, then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group by Theorem 1.2. If
exp(G)=p
, then for any maximal subgroup
H
of
G
we have

\psi(H)=1+(p^{n-1}-1)p=|G|-p+1<|G|,

so once again
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group.

In general, the direct product of two
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. For instance, according to Theorem 1.2 we know that
C_{2}
and
C_{3}
are
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups, while
C_{6}
is not. Also, in general the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of a finite group is not inherited by its non-trivial subgroups. The smallest example in this regard is
A_{5}
. Its maximal subgroups are isomorphic to
D_{10},S_{3}
or
A_{4}
. We have
\psi(D_{10})=\psi(A_{4})=31
and
\psi(S_{3})=13
. All these sums of element orders are less than
|A_{5}|=60
, so
A_{5}
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. However,
\psi(C_{5})=21>10=|D_{10}|
, so
D_{10}
is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. In what concerns the inheritance of the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property by non-trivial subgroups, we can use Proposition 2.2 to say more in the case of finite
p
-groups.

Corollary 2.3.

Let
G
be a finite
p
-group. Then all non-trivial subgroups of
G
are
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups if and only if
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
exp(G)=p
.

Proof.
Let
G
be a finite
p
-group. If all non-trivial subgroups of
G
are
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups, then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. By Proposition 2.2, we deduce that
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
exp(G)=p
.

The converse easily follows if one uses Proposition 2.2 and the fact that
exp(G)=p
implies that
exp(H)=p
for any non-trivial subgroup
H
of
G
.

Let
\pi
be a set of prime numbers. We recall that a subgroup
H
of a finite group
G
is a Hall
\pi
-subgroup of
G
if
\pi(H)\subseteq\pi
and there is no prime divisor of
[G:H]
which lies in
\pi
. In what follows, we show that if a finite group
G
has a large normal Hall
\pi
-subgroup, then
G
fails to be a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group.

Proposition 2.4.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
\pi(G)=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
, where
k\geq 2
and
p_{1}<p_{2}<\ldots<p_{k}.
If
G
has a normal Hall
\{p_{2},p_{3},\ldots,p_{k}\}
-subgroup, then
G
is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group.

Proof.
Let
P_{1}
be a Sylow
p_{1}
-subgroup of
G
such that
|P_{1}|=p_{1}^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\geq 1
. Let
H
be a normal Hall
\{p_{2},p_{3},\ldots,p_{k}\}
-subgroup of
G
and
K
be a subgroup of
P_{1}
of order
p_{1}^{\alpha-1}
. Since
H
is normal, it follows that
HK
is a maximal subgroup of
G
of index
p_{1}
. Note that if
x\in H
and
y\in HK\setminus H
, then
o(x)\geq p_{2}
and
o(y)\geq p_{1}
. Then

\psi(HK)\geq 1+(|H|-1)p_{2}+|H|(|K|-1)p_{1}=|G|+(p_{2}-p_{1})|H|+1-p_{2}>|G|

since
(p_{2}-p_{1})|H|\geq p_{2}
. We conclude that
G
is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group, as desired.

We have the necessary tools to generalize Theorem 1.2 up to the class of finite nilpotent groups.

Theorem 2.5.

Let
G
be a finite nilpotent group. Then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group if and only if
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
exp(G)=p
, where
p
is a prime.

Proof.
Let
G
be a finite nilpotent
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. We take
\pi(G)=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\}
, where
k\geq 1
, and denote by
P_{i}
the Sylow
p_{i}
-subgroup of
G
, for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Suppose that
k\geq 2
. Since
G
is the direct product of its Sylow subgroups, we deduce that
P_{2}P_{3}\ldots P_{k}
is a normal Hall
\{p_{2},p_{3},\ldots,p_{k}\}
-subgroup of
G
. According to Proposition 2.4, it follows that
G
is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group, a contradiction. Then,
k=1
and
G
is a finite
p
-group satisfying the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property. By Proposition 2.2, we conclude that
G\cong C_{p^{2}}
or
exp(G)=p
.

As we previously saw, the converse also holds, so the proof is finished.

We write some notes based on our enumeration. The list contains only three non-nilpotent
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group of odd order. Some of the non-solvable examples are simple or quasisimple groups. In what concerns the non-nilpotent solvable examples, we observe a similarity: the orders of these groups have only two prime divisors. Hence, we pose the following open problem:

Question 1.

Let
G
be a finite non-nilpotent solvable
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. Is it true that
|\pi(G)|=2
?

As a consequence of our last observation, one may be interested in studying the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property for specific classes of finite groups of order
p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}
, where
p,q
are primes and
\alpha\geq 1,\beta\geq 1
. Two such remarkable classes are the finite non-abelian
P
-groups and the Schmidt groups (i.e. the finite minimal non-nilpotent groups).

For fixed
p
and
n
, a remarkable property of the finite
P
-groups is that they are lattice-isomorphic (see Theorem 2.2.3 of
[
15
]
). This is an additional reason for which we choose to study the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of the
P
-groups. More exactly, if we have two finite non-isomorphic groups
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that their subgroup lattices are isomorphic and
G_{1}
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group, can we conclude that
G_{2}
has the same property? The answer is negative since we prove that the only
P
-groups which satisfy the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property are the abelian ones.

Corollary 2.6.

Let
G
be a finite
P
-group. Then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group if and only if
G
is elementary abelian.

Proof.
Let
G
be a finite
P
-group. Suppose that
G
is non-abelian and it satisfies the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property. By the description of the non-abelian
P
-groups, it follows that
G=H\langle x\rangle
, where
H
is a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
and
o(x)=q
. Since
q|(p-1)
, it follows that
q<p
so, according to Proposition 2.4,
G
would not be a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group, a contradiction. Hence
G
is an abelian
P
-group, so
G
is elementary abelian, as desired.

The proof is complete since the converse holds by Theorem 1.2.

We move our attention to studying the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of the Schmidt groups. These are the finite minimal non-nilpotent groups and details on their properties and structure are covered in
[
4
,
14
]
. We recall some aspects as well. If
G
is a Schmidt group, then
\pi(G)=\{p,q\}
,
G
has a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup
P
and a cyclic Sylow
q
-subgroup
Q
. We conclude that
G=P\rtimes Q
. Moreover, if
r
is the multiplicative order of
p
modulo
q
, it is known that if
P
is abelian, then
P\cong C_{p}^{r}
, while if
P
is non-abelian, then
Z(P)=P^{\prime}=\Phi(P)
is elementary abelian and
\frac{P}{\Phi(P)}\cong C_{p}^{r}.
In short,
P
is a special
p
-group (p. 183 of
[
10
]
). Some constructions of special
p
-groups are covered in
[
8
]
. Note that since
\Phi(P)
and
\frac{P}{\Phi(P)}
are elementary abelian, it follows that
exp(P)\in\{p,p^{2}\}
. Moreover, according to Theorem 5.2 of
[
13
]
, if
p
is odd, then
exp(P)=p
, while if
p=2
, then
exp(P)=4
. Finally, if
Q=\langle x\rangle
, then
x^{q}\in Z(G)
. Hence
\langle x^{q}\rangle
is a normal subgroup of
G
which trivially intersects
P
. Consequently,
G
has a maximal subgroup
H\cong P\times\langle x^{q}\rangle
of index
q
. All other maximal subgroups of
G
are abelian and are of the form
\Phi(P)Q_{i}
(see the proof of Theorem 1 of
[
16
]
), where
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{q}\}
and
n_{q}
is the number of Sylow
q
-subgroups of
G
. These would be the tools that are necessary to study the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of the Schmidt groups.

Theorem 2.7.

Let
G=P\rtimes Q
be a Schmidt group of order
p^{\alpha}q^{\beta}
, where
p,q
are distinct primes and
\alpha\geq 1,\beta\geq 1
. If
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group, then
G=P\rtimes Q
, where
P
is a special
p
-group of order
p^{\alpha}
,
Q\cong C_{q}
and
p,q
are primes such that
p<q
. Conversely,

i)

if
P
is abelian, then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group;

ii)

if
P
is non-abelian,
p
is odd,
r
is the order of
p
modulo
q
, and the inequality

\displaystyle(p^{\alpha-r+1}-p+1)(q^{2}-q+1)<p^{\alpha}q

(1)

holds, then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group;

iii)

if
P
is non-abelian,
p=2
,
r
is the order of
p
modulo
q
, and the inequalities (
1
) and

\displaystyle\psi(P)<2^{\alpha}q

(2)

hold, then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group.

Proof.
As stated above,
P
is a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup isomorphic to a special
p
-group,
Q=\langle x\rangle\cong C_{q^{\beta}}
and
H=P\times\langle x^{q}\rangle
is a maximal subgroup of
G
.

Assume that
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. Then, by Proposition 2.4, we have that
p<q
. Also, since
exp(P)\in\{p,p^{2}\}
, it follows that
\psi(P)\geq\psi(C_{p}^{\alpha})
. Using this fact and Lemma 2.1
i)
and
ii)
, we have

\psi(H)=\psi(P)\psi(C_{q^{\beta-1}})\geq(p^{\alpha+1}-p+1)\cdot\frac{q^{2\beta-1}+1}{q+1}>p^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{q^{2\beta-1}+1}{q+1}.

If
\beta\geq 3
, then
\frac{q^{2\beta-1}+1}{q+1}>q^{\beta}
, so
\psi(H)>|G|
, contradicting the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of
G
. Hence,
\beta\in\{1,2\}
. If
\beta=2
, according to Lemma 2.1
iii)
, we deduce that

\psi(H)=\psi(P)\psi(C_{q})\geq\psi(C_{p}^{\alpha})\psi(C_{q})>p^{\alpha}q^{2},

a contradiction. Consequently,
G=P\rtimes Q
is a Schmidt group, where
P
is a special
p
-group,
Q\cong C_{q}
and
p<q
.

Conversely, let
G=P\rtimes Q
be a Schmidt group of order
p^{\alpha}q
, where
\alpha\geq 1
,
P
is a special
p
-group,
Q\cong C_{q}
and
p,q
are primes such that
p<q
.

i)
If
P
is abelian, then the maximal subgroups of
G
are
P\cong C_{p}^{\alpha}
and
Q_{i}\cong C_{q}
, where
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{q}\}
. Also,
\alpha
would be the multiplicative order of
p
modulo
q
, so
q\leq p^{\alpha}-1
. We have

\psi(P)=\psi(C_{p}^{\alpha})=p^{\alpha+1}-p+1<p^{\alpha+1}<p^{\alpha}q=|G|

and

\psi(Q_{i})=\psi(C_{q})=q^{2}-q+1<q^{2}\leq(p^{\alpha}-1)q<p^{\alpha}q=|G|,\ \forall\ i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{q}\},

so
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group.

If
P
is non-abelian, then the maximal subgroups of
G
are
P
and
\Phi(P)Q_{i}\cong C_{p}^{\alpha-r}\times C_{q}
, where
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{q}\}
and
r
is the multiplicative order of
p
modulo
q
. We distinguish the following two cases based on the parity of
p
.

ii)
If
p
is odd, then
exp(P)=p
. Hence,
\psi(P)=p^{\alpha+1}-p+1<|G|
, as we previously saw. Assume that
G
is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. Then, there exists
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{q}\}
such that

\psi(\Phi(P)Q_{i})\geq|G|\Longleftrightarrow\psi(C_{p}^{\alpha-r})\psi(C_{q})\geq|G|\Longleftrightarrow(p^{\alpha-r+1}-p+1)(q^{2}-q+1)\geq p^{\alpha}q,

a contradiction. Consequently,
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group.

iii)
If
p=2
, then
exp(P)=4
.
Assume that
G
is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. Then
\psi(P)\geq|G|
or there exists
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{q}\}
such that
\psi(\Phi(P)Q_{i})\geq|G|
. It follows that

\psi(P)\geq 2^{\alpha}q\text{ or }(2^{\alpha-r+1}-1)(q^{2}-q+1)\geq 2^{\alpha}q,

a contradiction. Then
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group and the proof is complete.

The result given by the case
iii)
of the previous theorem can be improved if one explicitly computes
\psi(P)
. Since
P
would be a special
2
-group with
exp(P)=4
and
\Phi(P)\cong C_{2}^{r}
, we can easily get some bounds for
\psi(P)
using the fact that the orders of the elements of
P\setminus\Phi(P)
belong to
\{2,4\}
. More exactly, we get

2^{\alpha+1}-1<\psi(P)\leq 1+2\cdot(2^{\alpha-r}-1)+4\cdot(2^{\alpha}-2^{\alpha-r}).

Also, concerning the cases
ii)
and
iii)
of the previous theorem, we note that there are examples of Schmidt groups for which at least one of the inequalities (
1
) and (
2
) hold or not. For instance,
SL(2,3)\cong Q_{8}\rtimes C_{3}
is a Schmidt group having a non-abelian kernel
P\cong Q_{8}
with
exp(P)=4
. This group is not a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group since (
2
) does not hold: we have
\psi(Q_{8})=27>24=|SL(2,3)|
. Some examples of Schmidt
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups
G=P\rtimes Q
, such that
P
is non-abelian and
exp(P)=4
, were outlined in our previous table: SmallGroup(160, 199), SmallGroup(320, 1012). Hence, both groups satisfy inequalities (
1
) and (
2
). Also, from the same table, we can extract SmallGroup(1215, 68) which is a Schmidt
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group with non-abelian kernel of exponent
3
, so inequality (
1
) holds. Finally, it remains unknown if there are any Schmidt groups, with non-abelian kernel of odd exponent, which are not
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
groups. For instance, if one chooses the quadruple
(p,q,r,\alpha)=(3,13,3,6)
, inequality (1) is not checked, but the only Schmidt group of order
9477
is SmallGroup(9477, 505). However, its structure description is
C_{13}\rtimes C_{729}
, but we would need a non-abelian kernel of order 729 in order to follow the hypotheses of Theorem 2.7. Hence, we pose the following questions related to the last two paragraphs:

Question 2.

Let
G
be a non-abelian special
2
-group. What is the explicit form of
\psi(G)
?

Question 3.

Let
G=P\rtimes Q
a Schmidt group of order
p^{\alpha}q
, where
\alpha\geq 1
and
p,q
are odd primes such that
p<q
. If
P
is non-abelian, is it true that
G
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group?

If we check our previous table, we observe that
PSL(2,q)
is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group for
q\in\{3,4,5,7,8,9,11,13\}
. For a prime power
q>3
, it is known that
PSL(2,q)
is a non-abelian simple group. Hence, it would be interesting to check if the class consisting of these simple groups is contained in the class of
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups. For such a purpose, a main tool would be Dickson’s classification of the maximal subgroups of
PSL(2,q)
(see
[
9
]
). One may ask:

Question 4.

What can be said about the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of the finite simple groups
PSL(2,q)
?

Finally, other potential
\mathscr{B_{\psi}}
-groups are the
CP_{1}
groups. The class of
CP_{1}
groups consists of all finite groups whose element orders are primes. Hence, they are some strong candidates for our investigation. A complete classification of the
CP_{1}
groups is highlighted in the main theorem of
[
7
]
. Therefore, we suggest a final open problem:

Question 5.

What can be said about the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of
CP_{1}
groups?

A partial answer for this question is given in our paper since the nilpotent
CP_{1}
groups are the finite
p
-groups of exponent
p
. These are
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-groups according to Proposition 2.2. The only non-solvable
CP_{1}
group is
A_{5}
which also is a
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
-group. Hence, it remains to study the
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
property of the non-nilpotent solvable
CP_{1}
groups. According to
[
7
]
, such a group is a Frobenius group
G=P\rtimes Q
of order
p^{\alpha}q
, where
p,q
are primes and
\alpha\geq 1
. In addition, the kernel
P
is of exponent
p
.
