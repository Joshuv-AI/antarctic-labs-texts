Asymptotic regimes of an integro-difference equation with discontinuous kernel Mohammad El Smaily acknowledges partial support from the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada through the NSERC Discovery Grant RGPIN-2017-04313.
Omar Abdul Halim, Mohammad El Smaily

Abstract
. This paper is concerned with an integral equation that models discrete time dynamics of a population in a patchy landscape. The patches in the domain are reflected through the discontinuity of the kernel of the integral operator at a finite number of points in the whole domain. We prove the existence and uniqueness of a stationary state under certain assumptions on the principal eigenvalue of the linearized integral operator and the growth term as well. We also derive criteria under which the population undergoes extinction (in which case the stationary solution is 0 everywhere).

1.
Introduction and main results

In this paper, we study the long term dynamics of a population in a heterogeneous landscape. The density function of this population obeys an integral equation, which we describe in what follows. The population density in the
\displaystyle n
th generation, at a location
\displaystyle x,
is denoted by
\displaystyle u_{n}(x).
The growth phase is described by some non-negative function
\displaystyle F,
and the dispersal phase is described by a dispersal kernel
\displaystyle k(x,y).
The probability that an individual, who started its dispersal process at
\displaystyle x,
will settle in
\displaystyle[y,y+dy)
is then given by the product
\displaystyle k(x,y)dy.
The population density in the next generation, at a location
\displaystyle x,
is then obtained by summing up arrivals at
\displaystyle x
from all possible locations
\displaystyle y.
This yields the integral equation

u_{n+1}(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(u_{n}(y))\,{\rm d}y,

(1)

where
\displaystyle u_{n}(x)
stands for the density of the population in the
\displaystyle n^{\text{th}}
generation at a position
\displaystyle x.

Our model (

Theorem A

(Krein-Rutman Theorem,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0},
where we let the Banach space
\displaystyle E

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)

Proposition 1
.

The operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
maps
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
into itself. Moreover,
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
is a positive compact operator and there exists an eigenfunction
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\in K\setminus\{0\}
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}:=r(\mathcal{T}_{0}).

Proof.

We know that
\displaystyle K
is a cone and the set
\displaystyle K-K
is dense in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega).
The map
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
maps
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
into itself because

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\mathcal{T}_{0}(u)\|_{2}^{2}&=r_{0}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)u(y)dy\right)^{2}{\rm d}x\\
&\leq r_{0}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\|k(x,\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}\,{\rm d}x\\
&=r_{0}^{2}\|k\|_{2}^{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}<\infty.\end{array}

Since
\displaystyle k\in L^{2}(\Omega^{2}),
it then follows (see Lax
[
7
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
is a compact linear operator. Moreover, if
\displaystyle u\in K
and

and
\displaystyle u\not\equiv 0
. Hence,
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}(u)\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}(u)\in K

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
is positive.

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
is a bounded linear operator, the spectral radius of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
is (see
[
7
]

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\mathcal{T}_{0}^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}=r(\mathcal{T}_{0})

For
\displaystyle v(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Omega|}},
we have
\displaystyle\|v\|_{2}=1.
Using
\displaystyle v

\begin{array}[]{c}\|\mathcal{T}_{0}^{n}\|=\sup_{\|u\|_{2}\leq 1}\|\mathcal{T}_{0}^{n}(u)\|_{2}\geq\|\mathcal{T}_{0}^{n}(v)\|_{2}\\
\geq\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y_{1})\mathcal{T}_{0}^{n-1}(v)(y_{1}){\rm d}y_{1}\right)^{2}{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\

\geq r_{0}^{n}\left(\delta^{2n}\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\Omega}\cdots\ \int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\Omega|}}{\rm d}y_{n}\cdots{\rm d}y_{1}\right)^{2}{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
=r_{0}^{n}\delta^{n}\frac{|\Omega|}{|\Omega|}^{n+1}=r_{0}^{n}\delta^{n}|\Omega|^{n}.\end{array}

Taking the
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle n
goes to
\displaystyle\infty
we get that

r(\mathcal{T}_{0})\geq r_{0}\delta|\Omega|>0.

(9)

This proves that the spectral radius is positive ( i.e.
\displaystyle r(\mathcal{T}_{0})>0
).
We can then apply Theorem

to obtain the existence of an eigenfunction

\displaystyle\phi_{0}\in K
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
that corresponds to the principal eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}:=r(\mathcal{T}_{0}).

We start with a series of Lemmas that will be used in the proofs of the theorems that we announced in Section

Lemma 1
.

Let
\displaystyle w
be a stationary solution of (
1
). Then,
\displaystyle w\geq 0
. Moreover, if
\displaystyle w\not\equiv 0
, then
\displaystyle w(x)>0
for all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
.

Proof.

First we mention that, since
\displaystyle F
is nonnegative and
\displaystyle k>0,
then the function
\displaystyle w
is nonnegative. We prove the claim according to the value of
\displaystyle F(0).
If
\displaystyle F(0)>0,
then

\forall x\in\Omega,\quad w(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y>2a\delta F(0)>0.

If
\displaystyle F(0)=0,
then for
\displaystyle x\in\Omega,
using (
4
) we have

w(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y\geq\delta\int_{\Omega}F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y>0,

because if
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y=0,
then
\displaystyle F(w(y))=0
a.e in
\displaystyle\Omega.
The latter implies that
\displaystyle w(y)=0
a.e in
\displaystyle\Omega.
However, as
\displaystyle w\not\equiv 0
, there exists
\displaystyle z\in\Omega
such that
\displaystyle w(z)>0.
Then,

0<w(z)=\int_{\Omega}k(z,y)F(w(y))\,dy=0

as
\displaystyle w(y)=0
a.e in
\displaystyle\Omega
. This is a contradiction.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\displaystyle u,v\in X
(defined in (
8
)), such that
\displaystyle u\geq\mathcal{T}(u)
and
\displaystyle v\leq\mathcal{T}(v)
. If
\displaystyle u>0
, then
\displaystyle u\geq v
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\Omega}u(x)>0.
We will discuss this according to the value of
\displaystyle F(0).
If
\displaystyle F(0)>0,
then
we have

\forall x\in\Omega,\quad u(x)\geq\mathcal{T}(u)(x)\geq 2a\delta F(0)>0.

If
\displaystyle F(0)=0,
then

\forall x\in\Omega,\quad u(x)\geq\mathcal{T}(u)(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(u(y))\,{\rm d}y\geq\delta\int_{\Omega}F(u(y))\,{\rm d}y>0,

Note that
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}F(u(y))\,{\rm d}y>0,
because we can find an interval in
\displaystyle\Omega
where
\displaystyle u>0,
which implies
\displaystyle F(u)>0
on that interval. This proves our claim. Also, we have

v\leq\mathcal{T}(v)=\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)F(v(y))\,dy\leq 2a\Lambda M.

Since
\displaystyle u,v\in X
, we denote by
\displaystyle\{B_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq p}
and
\displaystyle\{C_{j}\}_{~1\leq j\leq q}
the subintervals of
\displaystyle\Omega
such that
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
are continuous on each
\displaystyle B_{i}
and
\displaystyle C_{j}
respectively. Let
\displaystyle A_{ij}=B_{i}\cap C_{j}
and define

\alpha_{ij}:=\inf\left\{\alpha>0,~\alpha u(x)\geq v(x)\text{ for all }x\in A_{ij}\right\}.

The infimum exists as the set
\displaystyle\left\{\alpha>0,~\alpha u_{|A_{ij}}\geq v_{|A{ij}}\right\}
is nonempty,
\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\Omega}u(x)>0
and
\displaystyle v
is bounded above. It follows from the continuity of
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
over
\displaystyle A_{ij}
that
\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}u\geq v
in
\displaystyle A_{ij}
. Moreover, from the definition of
\displaystyle\inf,
there exists
\displaystyle x_{ij}\in A_{ij}
such that
\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}u(x_{ij})=v(x_{ij}).
Let

\alpha_{0}=\max_{ij}(\alpha_{ij})=\alpha_{i_{0}j_{0}}.

Then,
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}u\geq v
in
\displaystyle\Omega.
Denote by
\displaystyle x_{0}:=x_{i_{0}j_{0}}
. We claim that
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}\leq 1,
which would then prove our lemma. Suppose to the contrary that
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}>1
. Then,

\begin{array}[]{lll}0&=&\alpha_{0}u(x_{0})-v(x_{0})\\
&\geq&\alpha_{0}\mathcal{T}(u)(x_{0})-\mathcal{T}(v)(x_{0})\\
&=&\int_{\Omega}k(x_{0},y)\left[\alpha_{0}F(u(y))-F(v(y))\right]{\rm d}y.\end{array}

From assumption (
6
), and since
\displaystyle F
is increasing, we get

\alpha_{0}F(u(y))=\alpha_{0}u(y)\frac{F(u(y))}{u(y)}>\alpha_{0}u(y)\frac{F(\alpha_{0}u(y))}{\alpha_{0}u(y)}\geq F(v(y))\text{ for all }y.

.

The following lemma will be used in proving the convergence in a certain mode of a sequence

satisfying (

Definition 3

(Pointwise equicontinuous)

A sequence of functions
\displaystyle\{f_{n}\}_{n}
is said to be pointwise equicontinuous on a set
\displaystyle J
if

\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\varepsilon>0,~\forall z\in J,~\exists~\delta(z)>0,\text{ such that }|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(z)|<\varepsilon,\\
\text{ whenever }x\in J~\text{ and }~|x-z|<\delta(z).\end{array}

(11)

Definition 4

(Uniformly equicontinuous)
.

A sequence of functions
\displaystyle\{f_{n}\}_{n}
is said to be uniformly equicontinuous on a set
\displaystyle J
if

\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\varepsilon>0,~\exists~\delta>0,\text{ such that }|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)|<\varepsilon\\
\text{ whenever }x,~y\in J\text{ and }|x-y|<\delta.\end{array}

(12)

Lemma 3
.

Let
\displaystyle J
be a compact set. If
\displaystyle\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n}\subset C(J)
is pointwise equicontinuous, then
\displaystyle\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n}
is uniformly equicontinuous.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
. For every
\displaystyle z\in J
, there exists
\displaystyle\delta_{z}>0
such that for all
\displaystyle x\in B_{J}(z,\delta_{z})
, we have
\displaystyle|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(z)|<{\varepsilon}/{2}
for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\displaystyle B(z,\delta_{z})
is the open ball of center
\displaystyle z
and radius
\displaystyle\delta_{z}
and
\displaystyle B_{J}(z,\delta_{z})=J\cap B(z,\delta_{z})
. We have

\displaystyle{\bigcup_{z\in J}B_{J}\left(z,\frac{\delta_{z}}{2}\right)=J}.

By compactness of
\displaystyle J
, there exist
\displaystyle z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in J
such that

J=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}B_{J}\left(z_{i},\frac{\delta_{z_{i}}}{2}\right).

We take
\displaystyle\delta_{0}<\min_{1\leq i\leq m}\left(\frac{\delta_{z_{i}}}{2}\right).

Let
\displaystyle x,y\in J
such that
\displaystyle|x-y|<\delta_{0}
. Then,
\displaystyle x\in B_{J}\left(z_{j},\frac{\delta_{z_{j}}}{2}\right),
for some
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq m
. Now,

|y-z_{j}|\leq|x-y|+|x-z_{j}|<\delta_{0}+\frac{\delta_{z_{j}}}{2}\leq\frac{\delta_{z_{j}}}{2}+\frac{\delta_{z_{j}}}{2}=\delta_{z_{j}}.

Thus,
\displaystyle y\in B_{J}(z_{j},\delta_{z_{j}}).
It then follows that

|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(y)|\leq|f_{n}(x)-f_{n}(z_{j})|+|f_{n}(y)-f_{n}(z_{j})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon,

for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, the set
\displaystyle\left\{f_{n}\right\}_{n}
is uniformly equicontinuous.

∎

Lemma 4
.

If
\displaystyle u_{0}\in X
, then the sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n},
obtained from (
1
) with an initial condition
\displaystyle u_{0},
satisfies
\displaystyle u_{n}\in X
for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}.
Moreover, if
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
\displaystyle w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then
\displaystyle w\in X
.

Proof.

As
\displaystyle u_{0}\in X
, there exists
\displaystyle m
subintervals
\displaystyle\{A_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq m}
in
\displaystyle\Omega,
such that
\displaystyle u_{0}
is continuous over each
\displaystyle A_{i}
.
We claim that for every
\displaystyle n
,
\displaystyle u_{n}
is continuous on
\displaystyle\Omega
except possibly at the points
\displaystyle\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
(introduced in assumption
1
) and at the discontinuity points of
\displaystyle u_{0},
which are finitely many. Let
\displaystyle B_{ij}
be the interval defined by

B_{ij}:=\Omega_{i}\cap A_{j}.

For
\displaystyle x\in B_{ij},
we have

u_{1}(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(u_{0}(y))\,{\rm d}y=\sum_{i,j}\int_{B_{ij}}k(x,y)F(u_{0}(y))\,{\rm d}y.

Since
\displaystyle y\mapsto k(x,y)F(u_{0}(y))
is continuous on each
\displaystyle B_{ij},
it follows that
\displaystyle u_{1}
is continuous at
\displaystyle x
as a sum of a finite number of continuous functions. Hence,
\displaystyle u_{1}
is continuous on
\displaystyle\Omega
except possibly at the points mentioned in the claim. Suppose now that the claim is true for all
\displaystyle u_{i}
such that
\displaystyle i\leq n
. Then,

u_{n+1}(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(u_{n}(y))\,{\rm d}y=\sum_{i,j}\int_{B_{ij}}k(x,y)F(u_{n}(y))\,{\rm d}y.

As the function
\displaystyle y\mapsto k(x,y)F(u_{n}(y))
is continuous on each
\displaystyle B_{ij},
it then follows that
\displaystyle u_{n+1}
is continuous at
\displaystyle x
. This proves the first assertion in our Lemma.

We move now to the proof of the second assertion. Suppose that
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
\displaystyle w\in L^{2}(\Omega).
We claim that
\displaystyle w
is continuous on
\displaystyle\Omega
except possibly at the points
\displaystyle\{a_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and at the points of discontinuity of
\displaystyle u_{0}.
Suppose that there exists
\displaystyle z\in B_{i_{0}j_{0}}
such that
\displaystyle w
is discontinuous at
\displaystyle z
, for some
\displaystyle i_{0}
and
\displaystyle j_{0}.
Since
\displaystyle B_{i_{0}j_{0}}
is open, there exists
\displaystyle r>0
such that

D:=[z-r,z+r]\subset B_{i_{0}j_{0}}.

We have
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
are all continuous on
\displaystyle D.
Let us show that
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is pointwise equicontinuous. Let
\displaystyle x_{0}\in D
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0.
The function
\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto k(x,y)
is uniformly continuous on
\displaystyle D\times B_{ij},
for all
\displaystyle(i,j).
This is because the function
\displaystyle k
can be extended to a continuous and bounded function on each of the compact sets
\displaystyle\overline{D\times B_{ij}}.
Thus, there exist
\displaystyle h_{ij}>0
such that, for all
\displaystyle(x,y)
and
\displaystyle(x_{1},y_{1})
in
\displaystyle D\times B_{ij},
we have

|(x-x_{1},y-y_{1})|<h_{ij}\implies|k(x,y)-k(x_{1},y_{1})|<l:=\frac{\varepsilon}{2aM},

where
\displaystyle M
is an upper bound of
\displaystyle F.
Let
\displaystyle h:=\min_{i,j}\{h_{ij}\}.
Then, for all
\displaystyle i,j
and for all
\displaystyle(x,y)\in D\times B_{ij},
we have

|x-x_{0}|=|(x,y)-(x_{0},y)|<h\implies|k(x,y)-k(x_{0},y)|<l.

Thus, for every
\displaystyle n
and for every
\displaystyle x\in D,
such that
\displaystyle|x-x_{0}|<h,
we have

\begin{array}[]{lll}|u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(x_{0})|&<&M\int_{-a}^{a}|k(x,y)-k(x_{0},y)|\,{\rm d}y\\
&\leq&M\sum_{i,j}\int_{B_{ij}}|k(x,y)-k(x_{0},y)|\,{\rm d}y\\
&<&M\sum_{i,j}\frac{\varepsilon}{2aM}|B_{ij}|=\varepsilon,\end{array}

where
\displaystyle|B_{ij}|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
\displaystyle B_{ij}.

Hence,
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is pointwise equicontinuous. From Lemma
3
, we conclude that
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is uniformly equicontinuous because
\displaystyle D
is compact. Moreover,
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}\subseteq C(D)
converges to
\displaystyle w
pointwise and it is equicontinuous. Therefore,
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges uniformly to
\displaystyle w
; hence,
\displaystyle w
is continuous over
\displaystyle D
.
This contradicts the assumption that
\displaystyle w
is discontinuous at
\displaystyle z\in D
. The claim then follows and
\displaystyle w\in X
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

If
\displaystyle u_{1}\geq u_{0}
(respectively
\displaystyle u_{1}\leq u_{0}
), then
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
is increasing (respectively decreasing).

Proof.

Suppose that, for all
\displaystyle i\leq n-1,
we have
\displaystyle u_{i+1}\geq u_{i}.
Then,

u_{n+1}(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(u_{n}(y))\,{\rm d}y\geq\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(u_{n-1}(y))\,{\rm d}y=u_{n}(x),

because
\displaystyle F
is increasing.
The other case can be proven similarly.
∎

Lemma 6
.

If
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
\displaystyle w
, then
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
\displaystyle w
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega).

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle w
is the limit of
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n},
it is a stationary solution: indeed

\mathcal{T}(w)=\mathcal{T}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{T}(u_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n+1}=w.

In the above equalities, we can interchange the limit and the integral by Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem. Thus,

\|u_{n}-w\|_{2}^{2}=\|u_{n}-\mathcal{T}(w)\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{-a}^{a}k(x,y)(F(u_{n-1}(y))-F(w(y)))\,{\rm d}y\right)^{2}\,{\rm d}x.

We have the following first inequality:

\begin{array}[]{l}\left(\int_{-a}^{a}k(x,y)(F(u_{n-1}(y))-F(w(y)))\,{\rm d}y\right)^{2}\\
\leq\left(\int_{-a}^{a}[\Lambda(M+M)]\,{\rm d}y\right)^{2}\leq 16a^{2}M^{2}\Lambda^{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega).\end{array}

Moreover, we have

k(x,y)\left[F(u_{n-1}(y))-F(w(y))\right]\leq 2\Lambda M\in L^{1}(\Omega).

Hence, using the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|u_{n}-w\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{-a}^{a}\lim_{n\to\infty}k(x,y)(F(u_{n-1}(y))-F(w(y)))\,{\rm d}y\right)^{2}\,{\rm d}x=0.

The proof of Lemma
6
is now complete.
∎

We are now in position to prove the three main theorems announced in Section
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We know that
\displaystyle 0
is a stationary solution of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
. We claim that there is no positive stationary solution. Suppose that there exists a positive stationary solution
\displaystyle w
of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
. It then follows, from Lemma
1
, that
\displaystyle w>0
on
\displaystyle\Omega
. Using assumption (
6
), we have

F(w(y))=w(y)\frac{F(w(y))}{w(y)}<r_{0}w(y),\text{ for all }y\in\Omega,

(13)

where
\displaystyle r_{0}=F^{\prime}(0).

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{ll}w(x)&=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y\\
&<r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)w(y)\,{\rm d}y=\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)(x),\end{array}

(14)

for all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
. We will now prove that there exists
\displaystyle\mu\in(0,1),
such that

w\leq(1-\mu)\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)\text{ in }\Omega.

(15)

Let
\displaystyle\alpha:=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\frac{w(x)}{\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)(x)}.
Then there exists
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega},
such that

\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\frac{w(x)}{\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)(x)}=\alpha.

From (
14
), we get
\displaystyle 0<\alpha\leq 1.
Our goal is to prove that
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<1.
Suppose to the contrary that
\displaystyle\alpha=1.

We then have

\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}{w(x)}=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)(x).

(16)

But (
13
) and Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem lead to

\begin{array}[]{ll}\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}{w(x)}&=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}k(x,y)F(w(y))\,{\rm d}y\\
&<r_{0}\int_{\Omega}\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}k(x,y)w(y)\,{\rm d}y\\
&=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)w(y)\,{\rm d}y\\
&=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)(x).\end{array}

This contradicts (
16
) and thus
\displaystyle\alpha<1.
This proves the inequality (
15
). We then iterate to get

w\leq(1-\mu)r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)w(y)\,{\rm d}y\leq(1-\mu)^{2}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\,2}(w)\leq\text{\ldots}\leq(1-\mu)^{n}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\,n}(w),

for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us show that
\displaystyle\inf_{\Omega}(\phi_{0})>0,
where
\displaystyle\phi_{0}
is the principal eigenfunction of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{0}
associated with the principal eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}.
In fact,
since
\displaystyle k(x,y)>\delta
for all
\displaystyle(x,y),
then, for all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
we have

\phi_{0}(x)=\frac{r_{0}}{\lambda_{0}}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\phi_{0}(y)\,{\rm d}y>\frac{r_{0}\delta}{\lambda_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}(y)\,{\rm d}y>0,

because
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\not\equiv 0
and
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\geq 0.
Hence,

\inf_{\Omega}\phi_{0}\geq\frac{r_{0}\delta}{\lambda_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{0}(y)\,{\rm d}y>0.

We recall that
\displaystyle\phi_{0}>0
in
\displaystyle\Omega
and it is unique up to multiplication by a scalar. Thus, we can assume that
\displaystyle w\leq\phi_{0}
over
\displaystyle\Omega
since
\displaystyle\inf_{\Omega}\phi_{0}>0.

Therefore,

\mathcal{T}_{0}(w)\leq r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)w(y)\,dy\leq r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\phi_{0}(y)\,dy=\mathcal{T}_{0}(\phi_{0}).

Suppose that, for all
\displaystyle i\leq n-1,
we have
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\,i}_{0}(w)\leq\mathcal{T}^{\,i}_{0}(\phi_{0}).
Then,

\mathcal{T}^{\,n}_{0}(w)\leq r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\mathcal{T}^{\,n-1}_{0}(w)(y)\,dy\leq r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\mathcal{T}^{\,n-1}_{0}(\phi_{0})(y)\,dy\leq\mathcal{T}^{\,n}_{0}(\phi_{0}).

By induction, and using the assumption that
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\leq 1,
we then obtain that

w\leq(1-\mu)^{n}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\,n}(w)\leq(1-\mu)^{n}\mathcal{T}_{0}^{\,n}(\phi_{0})=(1-\mu)^{n}\lambda_{0}^{n}\phi_{0}\leq(1-\mu)^{n}\phi_{0},

for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
\displaystyle 0<(1-\mu)<1,
passing to the limit as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow+\infty
in the above inequality yields
\displaystyle w\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle\Omega,
which is contradiction. This completes the proof of the first result in the theorem.

We move now to the proof of the second result in this theorem. Let
\displaystyle u_{0}\in X
and fix
\displaystyle N>2a\Lambda M.
Then,

\mathcal{T}(N)=\int_{-a}^{a}k(x,y)F(N)\,{\rm d}y\leq 2a\Lambda M<N.

From Lemma
5
, it then follows that
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N)\}_{n}
is decreasing. Since
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N)\}
is bounded below by
\displaystyle 0
uniformly,
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N)\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
\displaystyle w.
Lemma
4
yields the existence of
\displaystyle w\in X.
From the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, it follows that

\mathcal{T}(w)=\mathcal{T}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N)\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{T}^{\,n+1}(N)=w.

The above means that

\displaystyle w
is stationary solution of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
. Since
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\leq 1,
it follows, from the first part of Theorem 1, that
\displaystyle w=0
. Now,

0\leq u_{1}=\mathcal{T}(u_{0})\leq 2r\Lambda M<N.

Suppose that, for all
\displaystyle i\leq n,
we have
\displaystyle u_{i}\leq\mathcal{T}^{\,i-1}(N).
Then,

u_{n+1}=\int_{-a}^{a}k(\cdot,y)F(u_{n}(y))\,{\rm d}y\leq\int_{-a}^{a}k(\cdot,y)F(\mathcal{T}^{\,n-1}(N)(y))\,{\rm d}y=\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N).

By induction, it then follows that

0\leq u_{n}\leq\mathcal{T}^{\,n-1}(N)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Taking the limits in the last inequality yields that
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
\displaystyle 0
pointwise. From Lemma
6
, it follows that
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
\displaystyle 0
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1
.

From Theorem
1
, it suffices to prove that
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\leq 1.
First, we claim that
\displaystyle\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega).
We have

\begin{array}[]{l}0<\lambda_{0}\varphi(x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\|k(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\leq 2a\Lambda\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty,~~\text{ as $\displaystyle\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega).$}\end{array}

(17)

Hence, our claim that
\displaystyle\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
follows. Choosing
\displaystyle\varphi
such that
\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=1,
we then obtain

\begin{array}[]{cl}\lambda_{0}=\|\lambda_{0}\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}&=\|\mathcal{T}_{0}(\varphi)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\\
&=\sup_{\Omega}\left|r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\sup_{\Omega}r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\sup_{\Omega}r_{0}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\leq\sup_{\Omega}r_{0}=r_{0}\leq 1.\end{array}

This completes the proof of Corollary
1
.
∎

We are now in position to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Here, we have the assumption that
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}>1
and
\displaystyle F(0)=0.
Appealing to Lemma
1
and Corollary
2
, it suffices to find one positive stationary solution of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}.

Let
\displaystyle h>0
such that
\displaystyle\frac{r_{0}}{1+h}>1
and
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{0}}{1+h}>1.
Note that
\displaystyle h
exists because
\displaystyle r_{0}>1
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}>1.
Since

\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{0}\phi_{0}(x)=\mathcal{T}_{0}(\phi_{0})(x)&=r_{0}\int_{-a}^{a}k(x,y)\phi_{0}(y)\,{\rm d}y\\
&\leq r_{0}\left(\int_{-a}^{a}(k(x,y))^{2}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{-a}^{a}(\phi_{0}(y))^{2}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leq r_{0}\sqrt{2a}\Lambda\|\phi_{0}\|_{2},\end{array}

it follows that
\displaystyle\phi_{0}
is bounded above by some constant
\displaystyle\kappa
for all
\displaystyle x
.

We claim that the limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{F(\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x))}{\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)}=F^{\prime}(0)

is uniform in
\displaystyle x\in\Omega.

From the definition of
\displaystyle r_{0}=F^{\prime}(0),
we have that for any
\displaystyle\zeta>0
, there exists
\displaystyle\delta_{0}>0,
such that

\text{for }|t|<\delta_{0},~\left|\frac{F(t)-F(0)}{t}-r_{0}\right|<\zeta.

Let
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}>0
be such that
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}\phi_{0}(x)<\varepsilon^{*}\kappa<\delta_{0}.
In other words,
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}
is small enough so that
\displaystyle\delta_{0}
is a uniform upper bound of
\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}\phi_{0}
. Hence, the claim follows. Thus, for
\displaystyle\zeta=\frac{hr_{0}}{1+h},
we can find
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}>0,
such that for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
we have

\left|\frac{F(\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x))}{\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)}-r_{0}\right|<\frac{hr_{0}}{1+h},~\text{ for all }x\in\Omega.

Thus,

\frac{F(\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x))}{\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)}>\frac{r_{0}}{1+h},

for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}].
Then, for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0},

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})&=\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)\varepsilon\phi_{0}(y)\frac{F(\varepsilon\phi_{0}(y)}{\varepsilon\phi_{0}(y)}\,{\rm d}y\\
&\geq\frac{r_{0}}{1+h}\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)\varepsilon\phi_{0}(y)\,{\rm d}y=\frac{1}{1+h}\mathcal{T}_{0}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})\\
&=\frac{\lambda_{0}}{1+h}\varepsilon\phi_{0}\\
&>\varepsilon\phi_{0}.\end{array}

(18)

We claim that
\displaystyle\inf_{\Omega}(\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})-\varepsilon\phi_{0})>0
. Suppose that
\displaystyle\inf_{\Omega}(\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})-\varepsilon\phi_{0})=0.
Then, there exists
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\bar{\Omega}

\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})(x)=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)=\alpha.

From (
18
), we obtain

\alpha=\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0}}\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})(x)\geq\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\frac{\lambda_{0}}{1+h}\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)\geq\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)=\alpha.

This implies that

\frac{\lambda_{0}}{1+h}\alpha=\alpha;

hence,
\displaystyle\alpha=0
. But,

\begin{array}[]{lll}0&=\alpha&=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})(x)\\
&&=\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)F(\varepsilon\phi_{0}(y))\,dy\geq\delta\int_{\Omega}F(\varepsilon\phi_{0}(y))\,dy>0,\end{array}

and so we have a contradiction. Therefore,
\displaystyle\inf_{\Omega}(\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})-\varepsilon\phi_{0})>0.

Since the set of simple functions is dense in
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(\Omega),
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon\phi_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence of simple functions
\displaystyle\{f_{n}\}_{n}
that decreases uniformly to
\displaystyle\varepsilon\phi_{0}
. Thus, for

\zeta_{0}:=\inf_{\Omega}(\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})-\varepsilon\phi_{0})>0,

there exists
\displaystyle n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{\varepsilon},
we have
\displaystyle|f_{n}(x)-\varepsilon\phi_{0}(x)|<\zeta_{0}
for all
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
. Then, as
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
is increasing (which follows from the fact that
\displaystyle F
is increasing), we get

\mathcal{T}(f_{n})\geq\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})\geq\varepsilon\phi_{0}+\zeta_{0}>f_{n}

on
\displaystyle\Omega,
for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\displaystyle f_{n}
is a simple function,
\displaystyle f_{n}\in X.
Now, for
\displaystyle n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
we consider the sequence
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n})\}_{m}.
Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(f_{n})\geq\mathcal{T}(\varepsilon\phi_{0})\geq f_{n}
on
\displaystyle\Omega,

Lemma
5
implies that
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n})\}_{m}
is increasing for all
\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{\varepsilon}.
Thus, for
\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0},
we have

\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n})=\int_{-a}^{a}k(\cdot,y)F(\mathcal{T}^{\,m-1}(f_{n})(y))\,{\rm d}y\leq 2a\Lambda M,

where
\displaystyle\Lambda
and
\displaystyle M
are the bounds of
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle F
respectively. Hence,
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n})\}_{m}
is uniformly bounded and increasing. Therefore, there exists
\displaystyle w\in X
(by Lemma
4
) such that
\displaystyle w=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n})
pointwise. The function
\displaystyle w
is a positive stationary solution of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
since

\mathcal{T}(w)=\mathcal{T}\left(\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n})\right)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{T}^{\,m}(f_{n}))=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{T}^{\,m+1}(f_{n})=w.

Note that we were able to interchange the limit and the integral in the above equation because of the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem.

We have

0<\delta\int_{\Omega}F(u_{0}(y)){\rm d}y\leq\mathcal{T}(u_{0})\leq u_{1}\leq 2a\Lambda M.

(19)

We fix
\displaystyle N>2a\Lambda M.
Then,
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(N)\leq 2a\Lambda M<N
. Appealing to Lemma
5
, we get
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N)\}_{n}
is decreasing and it is bounded uniformly by
\displaystyle 0.
From Lemma
4
, there exists
\displaystyle w^{*}\in X
such that
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N)\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
\displaystyle w^{*}.
This means that
\displaystyle w^{*}
is a stationary solution of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
. Then, for
\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0},
we have

\mathcal{T}(f_{n})<N,\text{ for all }n\geq n_{\varepsilon}.

We know that
\displaystyle F
is increasing. By induction, it follows that

\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(f_{n})\leq\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N).

Taking the limit on both sides, we get that
\displaystyle w\leq w^{*}
. From the uniqueness of the non-zero stationary solution of
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}
in
\displaystyle X
, which was established in Corollary
2
, we have
\displaystyle w^{*}=w\not\equiv 0
. From (
19
), we can find
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}]
such that

\varepsilon\phi_{0}\leq\varepsilon\phi_{0}+\nu\leq u_{1}\leq N\text{ in }\Omega,

for some
\displaystyle\nu>0.
Then, there exists
\displaystyle n_{0}
such that, for all
\displaystyle n\geq n_{0},
we have
\displaystyle f_{n}<\varepsilon\phi_{0}+\nu
in
\displaystyle\Omega.
Let
\displaystyle n_{1}>\max\{n_{0},n_{\varepsilon}\}
. Then,
\displaystyle f_{n_{1}}\leq u_{1}\leq N.

Suppose that, for all
\displaystyle i\leq n-1

Then, since
\displaystyle F

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(f_{n_{1}})&=\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)F(\mathcal{T}^{\,n-1}(f_{n_{1}}(y)))\,{\rm d}y\\
&\leq\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)F(u_{n}(y))\,{\rm d}y\\
&\leq\int_{\Omega}k(\cdot,y)F(\mathcal{T}^{\,n-1}(N)(y))\,{\rm d}y.\end{array}

Hence,

\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(f_{n_{1}})\leq\ u_{n+1}\leq\mathcal{T}^{\,n}(N),\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

By passing to the limit as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow+\infty,
we get

w\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n+1}\leq w.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges pointwise to
\displaystyle w,
which is the unique positive stationary solution of
\displaystyle T
over
\displaystyle\Omega.
From Lemma
6
, we obtain that
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}_{n}
converges to
\displaystyle w
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2

∎
