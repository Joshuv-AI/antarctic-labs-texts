Optimal control of interacting active particles on complex landscapes
S. Sinha, V. Krishnan, & L. Mahadevan

Abstract




Active many-body systems composed of many interacting degrees of freedom often operate out of equilibrium, giving rise to non-trivial emergent behaviors which can be functional in both evolved and engineered contexts. This naturally suggests the question of
control
to optimize function. Using navigation as a paradigm of function, we deploy the language of stochastic optimal control theory to formulate the inverse problem of shepherding a system of interacting active particles across a complex landscape. We implement a solution to this high-dimensional problem using an Adjoint-based Path Integral Control (APIC) algorithm that combines the power of recently introduced continuous-time back-propagation methods and automatic differentiation with the classical Feynman-Kac path integral formulation in statistical mechanics. Numerical experiments for controlling individual and interacting particles in complex landscapes show different classes of successful navigation strategies as a function of landscape complexity, as well as the intrinsic noise and drive of the active particles. However, in all cases, we see the emergence of paths that correspond to traversal along the edges of ridges and ravines, which we can understand using a variational analysis. We also show that the work associated with optimal strategies is inversely proportional to the length of the time horizon of optimal control, a result that follows from scaling considerations. All together, our approach serves as a foundational framework to control active non-equilibrium systems optimally to achieve functionality, embodied as a path on a high-dimensional manifold.

For concreteness, we consider a system of
N
active particles whose controlled dynamics is described by the overdamped Langevin equation,

\displaystyle\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)=-\frac{1}{\eta}\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))+\underline{\bf u}(t)+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t),\hskip 22.99988pt\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}

(1)

where
\underline{\bf x}(t)=({\bf x}_{1}(t),{\bf x}_{2}(t),...{\bf x}_{N}(t))
is the configuration (i.e, positions
{\bf x}_{i}(t)\in\real^{d}
) of the
N
particle system at time
t
interacting via a dynamic potential
V(\underline{\bf x}(t))
, subject to an additive control
\underline{\bf u}(t)
that needs to be determined via some extraneous condition, and
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t)
is a delta-correlated, stationary Gaussian process at time
t
, with statistics given by
\langle{\xi}_{i}^{\alpha}\rangle
=0, and
\langle{\xi}_{i}^{\alpha}(t){\xi}_{j}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta^{\alpha\beta}\delta_{ij}
, where
\alpha=1,2,..d
and
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
,
and the gradient operator
\nabla=\frac{\partial}{\partial\underline{\bf x}}
. The parameters
\eta
and
D
in (
1
) are the damping and diffusion constants respectively. While all our numerical results are associated with assuming that the ambient dimension
d=2
, our framework is valid for arbitrary dimension and our equations are generalizable to their appropriately invariant forms on curved manifolds. In Eqn. (
1
), we assume overdamped dynamics and hence neglect inertia, and further postulate that since we are working with non-equilibrium systems,
\eta
and
D
need not satisfy the fluctuation-dissipation relation
[
35
]
; this naturally allows us to also consider the athermal limit,
D=0
corresponding to the deterministic limit
[
36
,
11
]
. For simplicity, we choose all the particles to be identical, and further that the friction factor
\eta=1
unless specified otherwise. The control task involves steering the system across the dynamic landscape generated by
V
for a time interval,
[0,T]
, with the requirement of minimizing the work done by the controller and with the goal of minimizing the target error
\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))
, on the terminal state,
\underline{\bf x}(T)
. The control task can be formulated as the following stochastic optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\underline{\bf u}_{[0,T]}}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\underline{\bf u}_{[0,T]}}}\left[\left.\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\underline{\bf u}(t^{\prime})\right\|^{2}dt^{\prime}+\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))~\right|~\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}\right]

(2)

subject to the dynamics given by Eqn. (
1
). In Eqn. (
2
), the expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\underline{\bf u}_{[0,T]}}}[G(\underline{\bf x})]=\int_{\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}}~\mathcal{D}\underline{\bf x}~G(\underline{\bf x})~\mathbb{Q}_{\underline{\bf u}_{[0,T]}}(\underline{\bf x})
, is over the distribution of all paths
\mathbb{Q}_{\underline{\bf u}_{[0,T]}}
generated by (
1
) with the control
\underline{\bf u}_{[0,T]}

over the time interval
[0,T]
and
\mathcal{D}\underline{\bf x}
is an infinitesimal volume element in the space of paths, and the condition
\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}
, and the parameter
\gamma
captures the relative weight assigned to the total work done,
W=\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\underline{\bf u}\right\|^{2}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}
compared to the terminal penalty,
\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))
.

Using a standard approach first proposed by Bellman
[
24
]
,
we introduce a value function
F(t,\underline{\bf z})
that denotes the optimal cost-to-go at time
t
from state
\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}
and is defined as

\displaystyle F(t,\underline{\bf z})=\min_{\underline{\bf u}_{[t,T]}}~\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{\underline{\bf u}_{[t,T]}}}\left[\left.\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{t}^{T}\left\|\underline{\bf u}(t^{\prime})\right\|^{2}dt^{\prime}+\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))~\right|~\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}\right]

(3)

and reduce the global formulation above to a local condition known as the the
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB)
equation
[
24
]
, a nonlinear partial differential equation (PDE) given by (see SI section S2 for a brief derivation)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+D\Delta F-\nabla F\cdot\nabla V-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left|\nabla F\right|^{2}=0

(4)

along with the temporal boundary condition
F(T,\underline{\bf z})=\Psi(\underline{\bf z})
, i.e. it must be solved in backward time from
t=T
. The HJB equation (
4
) suffers from the curse of dimensionality
[
24
]
owing to the nonlinearity and the state space being of dimension
N>>1
. To circumvent this, we first reformulate it using the Cole-Hopf transformation
F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
, for some
\beta>0
, and then use our freedom in the choice of the parameter
\beta
by imposing the relation
\beta=\frac{1}{2\gamma D}
(analogous to the Stokes-Einstein relation), to reduce the HJB equation to a linear advection-diffusion equation
[
30
,
37
]
(also in backward time):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}+D\Delta\varphi-\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla V=0,

(5)

with the boundary condition
\varphi(T,\underline{\bf z})=e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf z})}
.
The solution to the transformed equation (
5
)
can be expressed as a path integral using the Feynman-Kac formula
[
38
]

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{[t,T]}}\left[\left.e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\;\right|\;\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime}),~\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}\right].

(6)

An important consequence of this approach is that the expectation above is taken with respect to the distribution

\mathbb{P}_{[t,T]}
of paths generated by the
uncontrolled dynamics

\dot{}\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime})
over the time interval
t^{\prime}\in[t,T]
starting at
\underline{\bf z}
, i.e., satisfying the condition
\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}
. Correspondingly, the value function,
F(t,\underline{\bf z})
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{[t,T]}}\left[\left.e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\;\right|\;\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime}),~\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}\right]\right)

(7)

and the optimal control
\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}(t))
. It is not coincidental that the value function (
7
)
takes the form of a free energy, wherein the (path integral) partition function corresponds to a Boltzmann distribution over uncontrolled paths, with the weight factor
e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}
obtained from the cost of an uncontrolled path
\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))
. All together, we note that the value function for the optimal control can be
computed entirely from the uncontrolled dynamics, a property that follows from two assumptions
[
30
,
37
]
(i) the control
\underline{\bf u}
enters linearly in the dynamics
and (ii) the cost is quadratic in
\underline{\bf u}
. While this might seem limiting, there are many problems where these assumptions are entirely reasonable; furthermore, in the context of modern approaches to the control of stochastic systems using model-predictive-control (MPC), these assumptions can be iteratively used to achieve good approximations to more complex forms of the controller and associated costs.

To solve the combination of the forward and backward (adjoint) problems iteratively and derive the optimal control strategy, we now describe an easily implementable Adjoint-Path Integral Control Algorithm. The path integral representation in (
7
) yields the optimal control at time
t
as
\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}(t))
and involves propagating the state
\underline{\bf x}(t)
via the uncontrolled dynamics over the time interval
[t,T]
to a new state
\underline{\bf x}(T)
at which point the gradient is evaluated, carried out via adjoint method, formulated next. From (
6
) and (
7
),
we first note that the gradients of
F(t,\underline{\bf z})
and
\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
are related by

\displaystyle\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{\nabla\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})}{\beta\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})}.

(8)

To compute the gradient of
\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
with respect to
\underline{\bf z}
in (
6
), we note that the uncontrolled dynamics
\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime})
must be propagated starting from
\underline{\bf z}
at time
t
(i.e.,
\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}
) over the time interval
[t,T]
.
Using the calculus of variations, we treat the uncontrolled dynamics as a constraint to construct the Lagrangian for the evaluation of the expectation in (
6
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t,\underline{\bf x},\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\operatorname{x}(T))}-\int_{t}^{T}\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\top}\left(\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})+\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))-\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime})\right)dt^{\prime}\right],

where
\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the Lagrange multiplier for the constraint of uncontrolled dynamics and the expectation above is with respect to
the stochastic process
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. From setting the first variation with respect to
\underline{\bf x}
in the interval
(0,T)
and the terminal
\underline{\bf x}(T)
above to zero, we obtain the
stationary conditions which fix the evolution of the Lagrange multiplier
\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(t^{\prime})=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t^{\prime}),\\
\text{subject~to}\quad&\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(T)=-\beta e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T)).\end{aligned}

(9)

Recalling the relation between the gradients of
F
and
\varphi
given by (
8
) (see SI for details), and making a change of variables
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t^{\prime})=-\frac{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t^{\prime})}{\beta\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\right]},

we obtain the adjoint equations for the evolution of the scaled Lagrange multiplier as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(t^{\prime})=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t^{\prime}),\hskip 22.99988pt\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(T)=\frac{e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}}{\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\right]}\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T)),\end{aligned}

(10)

where
\nabla^{2}V
is the Hessian (matrix) of potential
V
(with
\nabla^{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\underline{\bf x}_{i}\partial\underline{\bf x}_{j}}
) and the optimal control at time
t
is then given by

\displaystyle\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left.\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf z})\right|_{\underline{\bf z}=\underline{\bf x}(t)}=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t)\right]

(11)

For a practical implementation of the algorithm, as outlined in the algorithm above, we see from (
10
) that to evaluate the optimal control
\underline{\bf u}^{*}
at time
t
, we first sample several instances of the uncontrolled forward dynamics from the current state
\underline{\bf x}(t)
up to time
T
for different realizations of the stochastic process
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, evaluate the gradient
\nabla\Psi
at
\underline{\bf x}(T)
for each sampled path and back-propagate
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
from the terminal condition in (
10
) for each sample to evaluate the average
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
across the samples at time
t
. We note that the Boltzmann weight factor in the value function (
7
) essentially reduces to a relative weighting of the terminal gradient of the sampled uncontrolled paths,
as specified in (
10
). Furthermore, note that the Hessian of the potential
V
along the uncontrolled path governs the adjoint back-propagation in (
10
).

Before proceeding to discuss our numerical results, we note that the athermal limit of the optimal control can be obtained by setting
D=0
in the HJB equation (
4
) and computing its gradient with respect
to
\underline{\bf z}
, to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla F-\nabla^{2}F\left(\nabla V+\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla F\right)-\nabla^{2}V\nabla F=0,

(12)

where
\nabla^{2}F
is the Hessian of
F
. The optimal trajectory
\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t)

satisfies
\dot{\underline{\bf x}}^{*}(t)=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))+\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))-\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))
,
and we see that

\displaystyle\frac{D}{Dt}\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))-\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))=0.

(13)

Letting
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t)=\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))
in the equation above,
we get
\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}^{*}(t)=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t)

and the boundary condition
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(T)=\nabla F(T,\underline{\bf x}^{*}(T))=\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(T))
to obtain the following equations of motion for the system
(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t))

[
39
]

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\underline{\bf x}}^{*}(t)&=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))+\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t),\hskip 22.99988pt\underline{\bf x}^{*}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}\\
\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}^{*}(t)&=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t))\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t),\hskip 22.99988pt\hskip 11.49994pt~\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(T)=\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(T))\\
\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)&=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t)\end{aligned}

(14)

This is equivalent to constructing a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}(\underline{\bf z},\underline{\bf v})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|\underline{\bf v}\right\|^{2}+\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}\left(-\nabla V(\underline{\bf z})+\underline{\bf v}\right)
that allows us to rewrite the optimal solution as

\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}^{*}(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{H}(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t),\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t))

and
\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=\arg\min_{\underline{\bf v}}\mathcal{H}(\underline{\bf x}^{*}(t),\underline{\bf v},\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}(t))
.
Thus we see that the optimal control minimizes the Hamiltonian at every point along the optimal trajectory, provides an alternative to the local HJB equation, and is the well-known
Pontryagin Minimum Principle

[
39
]
. This allows us to interpret (
14
) as the Lagrangian formulation
for the corresponding Eulerian HJB formulation (in the terminology of continuum mechanics).

In our implementation, the (first and higher order) derivatives of the interaction potential are computed by automatic differentiation within the framework supplied by JAX
[
34
]
. High-dimensional ODE and SDE integrations are performed in Diffrax
[
33
]
, a JAX-based library for numerical integration of differential equations.
For the implementation of the sampling-based Feynman-Kac path integral adjoint algorithm, we utilize the automatic vectorization functionality provided by
{\rm vmap}
in JAX in combination with ODE/SDE integration in Diffrax.

Before describing the results of our numerical experiments with the APIC algorithm, we use a variational argument to suggest the emergence of a universal strategy. For simplicity, we start with the athermal limit of the stochastic optimal control problem (i.e. Eqn.
2
) which then reduces to

\displaystyle\min_{\underline{\bf u}}~\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\underline{\bf u}\right\|^{2}(t)dt+\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T)),\hskip 22.99988pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 11.49994pt\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))+\underline{\bf u}(t);\hskip 11.49994pt\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}

(15)

Substituting
\underline{\bf u}(t)=\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)+\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))
into the functional (
15
) yields the following unconstrained minimization problem for
\underline{\bf x}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\min_{\underline{\bf x}}~\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)+\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))\right\|^{2}dt+\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))

\displaystyle=\min_{\underline{\bf x}}~\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)\right\|^{2}dt+\gamma\int_{0}^{T}\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))^{T}\cdot\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)dt+\frac{\gamma}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))\right\|^{2}dt+\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T)).

Setting
\delta\mathcal{L}/\delta\underline{\bf x}=0
yields following equation of motion for the optimal trajectory of the state
\underline{\bf x}(t)
, given by

\displaystyle\ddot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t))\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\left(\left|\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t))\right|^{2}\right)

(16)

with
\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}
and the terminal boundary condition
\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(T)=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(T))-\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))
.

The physical interpretation of the
emergent
Eqn. (
16
) is that in flat regions of the landscape, the particle follows the simple geodesic straight-line path (
\ddot{\underline{\bf x}}=0
) whereas in non-flat regions it accelerates in the direction of increasing magnitude of the gradient of
V
. Furthermore, the behavior is symmetric under the transformation
V\rightarrow-V
of the potential, which implies that hills and valleys of the landscape have the same effect.

To verify the predictions of the theoretical analysis, we performed four numerical experiments with (a) a single stochastic particle in a frozen landscape of hills and valleys, (b) Interacting stochastic particles in a frozen landscape, and (c) Interacting athermal particles. To create the mixed landscape, the static potential was chosen as

\displaystyle V_{\rm s}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\eta_{i}\Theta\left(\left(\left\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{x}_{i}^{s}\right\|-d_{0}\right)^{2}\right),

(17)

where

is the Heaviside step function, the number of modes in the frozen landscape

,

was sampled uniformly from the interval

,

were sampled uniformly from a square of side
0.8

d_{0}=0.04
. In the numerical simulations, the time step for numerical integration by the Euler-Maruyama method was chosen to be

and the number of sampled paths in the implementation of the APIC algorithm was
20
. The parameters
\gamma=1
,
D=5\times 10^{-4}
and

We have shown how a combination of concepts from optimal control theory and statistical mechanics along with efficient computational methods provides a framework for the control of functional many-body systems, instantiated in terms of the navigation of a system of particles. Augmenting the dynamics of the system using co-state variables for optimal control and using the Feynman-Kac path integral in a computationally tractable framework that uses automatic differentiation (JAX) for the efficient solution of the forward-backward adjoint problem leads to our A(djoint) based P(ath) I(integral) Control algorithm. Deploying this in a range of situations for interacting particles moving on complex landscapes shows how particles prefer to move along ridges, while the optimal strategies take advantage of the intrinsic dynamics of the particles when the time horizon is large, leading to a gather-move(-spread) strategy, with the sparing use of control only when necessary. We further see that the work done by the controller is consistent with a simple scaling law, and that there is a tradeoff between control of center of mass and variance of the position as a function of interaction strength and noise. Although our study has focused on the continuous-time control of discrete particulate systems, the same approach can be generalized to the case of continuous media in the context of optimal transport
[
42
,
41
]
as well as discrete-time control of both particulate/agent-based models
[
43
,
44
]
and continuous media
[
45
]
, objects of study for the future. Finally, our framework can be used to study the evolutionary dynamics of populations by allowing the landscape itself to change (slowly) in response to the movement of the particles on it, and introducing a selection principle for the population.

Appendix S4
Derivation of Adjoint-based Path Integral Control (APIC)

We first recall the path integral
[
38
]
representation of the optimal value function
F(t,\underline{\bf z})
from the main text

\displaystyle F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{[t,T]}}\left[\left.e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\;\right|\;\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime}),~\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}\right]\right),

where the expectation above is taken with respect to the distribution

\mathbb{P}_{[t,T]}
of paths generated by the
uncontrolled dynamics

\dot{}\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime})
over the time interval
t^{\prime}\in[t,T]
starting at
\underline{\bf z}
, i.e., satisfying the condition
\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}
.
Furthermore, we recall that
F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
, where
\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{[t,T]}}\left[\left.e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\;\right|\;\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime}),~\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}\right].

We see that computing the optimal control

\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf x}(t))

at time
t
involves propagating the state
\underline{\bf x}(t)
through the
uncontrolled dynamics over the time interval
[t,T]

to a new state
\underline{\bf x}(T)
at which point the gradient is evaluated,
i.e., the gradient is evaluated at a distance.
We accomplish this via the adjoint method, which we formulate below.
From the above path integral representations for
F(t,\underline{\bf z})
and
\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
, we first note that their gradients are related as
follows

\displaystyle\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{\nabla\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})}{\beta\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})}

To compute the gradient of
\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})
with respect to
\underline{\bf z}
, we note that the uncontrolled dynamics
\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})=-\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))+\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime})
must be propagated starting from
\underline{\bf z}
at time
t
(i.e.,
\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}
) over the time interval
[t,T]
.
We do this by calculus of variations
[
48
]
, whereby we treat the uncontrolled dynamics as a constraint to construct the Lagrangian for the evaluation of the expectation in (
6
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t,\underline{\bf x},\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\operatorname{x}(T))}-\int_{t}^{T}\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{\top}\left(\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t^{\prime})+\nabla V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))-\sqrt{2D}\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t^{\prime})\right)dt^{\prime}\right],

where
\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the Lagrange multiplier for the constraint of
uncontrolled dynamics and the expectation above is with respect to
the stochastic process
\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
.
Taking the first variation of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}

with respect to
\underline{\bf x}
, we get

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[-\beta\left.e^{-\beta\Psi(z)}\nabla\Psi(z)\right|_{z=\underline{\bf x}(T)}\delta\underline{\bf x}(T)-\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(T)\delta\underline{\bf x}(T)+\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)\delta\underline{\bf x}(t)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\int_{t}^{T}\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(t^{\prime})^{\top}\delta\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}-\int_{t}^{T}\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t^{\prime})^{\top}\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))\delta\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)\delta\underline{\bf x}(t)+\left(-\beta\left.e^{-\beta\Psi(z)}\nabla\Psi(z)\right|_{z=\underline{\bf x}(T)}-\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(T)\right)\delta\underline{\bf x}(T)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\int_{t}^{T}\left(\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(t^{\prime})-\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t^{\prime})\right)^{\top}\delta\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right]

From setting the first variation with respect to
\underline{\bf x}
in the interval
(0,T)
and the terminal
\underline{\bf x}(T)
above to zero, we obtain the
stationary conditions which fix the evolution of the Lagrange multiplier

\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
as follows

\displaystyle\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(t^{\prime})=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\text{subject~to}\quad\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(T)=-\beta e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T)).

Furthermore, taking the first variation of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, we retrieve the uncontrolled dynamics as the governing equation for
\underline{\bf x}
. Once we fix the dynamics of
\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
as above (with the uncontrolled dynamics determining
\underline{\bf x}
), we get
\delta\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)\right]\delta\underline{\bf x}(t)
.
Since
\underline{\bf x}(t)=\underline{\bf z}
, it follows that
\nabla\mathcal{L}(t,\underline{\bf z},\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)\right]
.
Further, we see that the gradient of
\varphi
with respect to
\underline{\bf z}

is related to the gradient of the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
, and
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})=\nabla\mathcal{L}(t,\underline{\bf z},\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}})=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)\right]

Recalling the relation between the gradients of
F
and
\varphi
, we get

\displaystyle\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf z})=-\frac{\nabla\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})}{\beta\varphi(t,\underline{\bf z})}=-\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}[\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)]}{\eta\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\right]}=\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[-\frac{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)}{\beta\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\right]}\right]

We now make a change of variables

\displaystyle\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t^{\prime})=-\frac{\underline{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t^{\prime})}{\beta\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\right]},

to obtain the adjoint equations for the gradient computation of the path integral as follows

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(t^{\prime})=\nabla^{2}V(\underline{\bf x}(t^{\prime}))\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t^{\prime}),\hskip 22.99988pt\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(T)=\frac{e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}}{\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[e^{-\beta\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))}\right]}\nabla\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T)),\end{aligned}

where
\nabla^{2}V
is the Hessian of potential
V
and the optimal control at time
t
is then given by

\displaystyle\underline{\bf u}^{*}(t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\left.\nabla F(t,\underline{\bf z})\right|_{\underline{\bf z}=\underline{\bf x}(t)}=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\left[\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t)\right]

Appendix S5
Shooting method for athermal optimal control

We construct a control Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(\underline{\bf x},\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\|^{2}-\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}\nabla V(\underline{\bf x})
to express the evolution of the optimally controlled system as a Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(\underline{\bf x}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t))

\displaystyle\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\underline{\bf x}}(\underline{\bf x}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t))

The initial state
\underline{\bf x}(0)=\underline{\bf x}_{0}
is given
and the initial co-state
\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(0)=\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}
is chosen to
minimize the terminal cost
\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))
, via the following shooting problem
[
49
]

\displaystyle\min_{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}}\Psi(\underline{\bf x}(T))\hskip 11.49994pt\text{s.t.}~\begin{cases}\dot{\underline{\bf x}}(t)&=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(\underline{\bf x}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t))\\
\dot{\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(t)&=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\underline{\bf x}}(\underline{\bf x}(t),\underline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(t))\\
\underline{\bf x}(0)&=\underline{\bf x}_{0}\end{cases}
