Quantum Otto engine driven by quantum fields
Kensuke Gallock-Yoshimura, Vaishant Thakur, and Robert B. Mann

Abstract
We consider a quantum Otto engine using an Unruh-DeWitt particle detector model which interacts with a quantum scalar field in curved spacetime. We express a generic condition for extracting positive work in terms of the effective temperature of the detector. This condition reduces to the well-known positive work condition in the literature under the circumstances where the detector reaches thermal equilibrium with the field. We then evaluate the amount of work extracted by the detector in two scenarios: an inertial detector in a thermal bath and a circulating detector in the Minkowski vacuum, which is inspired by the Unruh quantum Otto engine.

Introduction

Quantum thermodynamics is a study of thermodynamic phenomena from the perspective of quantum mechanics.
Within this field quantum heat engines, where the working substance is quantum matter that interacts with thermal baths, have emerged as a crucial area of research
Scovil.heat.engine.maser
.
Notable among quantum heat engines are the quantum Carnot engine and the quantum Otto engine (QOE).
The quantum Carnot engine operates through two isothermal processes and two quantum adiabatic processes.
The QOE, on the other hand, is composed of two isochoric thermalization processes and two quantum adiabatic processes
Scovil.heat.engine.maser
;
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
;
Kieu.heat.engine.2006
;
Rostovtsev.Otto.2003
;
Quan.multilevel.engine.2005
;
Quan.multilevel.heat.engine
.
For a comprehensive review, see
Myers.quantumThermo.review
.

Our interest lies in the QOE using a two-level quantum system in the realm of relativistic quantum information (RQI), where relativity is taken into account.
In particular, we consider a specific model for a qubit known as the Unruh-DeWitt (UDW) particle detector model
Unruh1979evaporation
;
DeWitt1979
, which interacts with a quantum field in (curved) spacetime.
The UDW detector model exhibits the essential features of the light-atom interaction as long as angular momenta are not exchanged
PhysRevD.87.064038
;
PhysRevA.89.033835
.
A UDW detector reveals interesting phenomena such as the Unruh effect
Unruh1979evaporation
, where a uniformly accelerating UDW detector perceives a thermal bath at the Unruh temperature
T_{\text{U}}=a/2\pi
(where
a
is the magnitude of the acceleration), whilst an observer at rest sees a vacuum.

Investigations have been carried out previously on the QOE employing the UDW detector model.
In
UnruhOttoEngine
, it was shown that a linearly accelerating UDW detector can be utilized to extract work in a QOE.
This process is facilitated by the Unruh effect, which generates hot and cold thermal baths by varying the magnitude of acceleration
a
.
Such an engine is commonly referred to as the Unruh QOE.
The amount of work extracted is determined by a response function of the UDW detector, which is the probability of a transition from the ground state to the excited state, and vice versa.
Subsequent studies have further expanded on the Unruh QOE in various scenarios, including a fermionic field
Finn.UnruhOtto
, a degenerate detector
Xu.UnruhOtto.degenerate
, entangled detectors
Kane.entangled.Unruh.Otto
;
Barman.entangled.UnruhOtto
, and in the presence of a reflecting boundary
Mukherjee.UnruhOtto.boundary
.
However, these studies focus on a specific acceleration trajectory, the state of a field, and a switching function.
A comprehensive investigation of the QOE in a more general setting still remains largely unexplored.

In this paper, we explore the QOE within the framework of RQI in a generalized setting.
We consider a pointlike UDW detector following an arbitrary trajectory in curved spacetime, interacting with a quantum scalar field in a quasi-free state.
Thus, the thermalization of the detector is not necessarily required.
By employing a perturbative method, we derive general expressions (
35
) and (
40
) for the work extracted through the quantum Otto cycle, which are written in terms of the response function of the detector.
In addition, we identify conditions for extracting positive work in a generalized setting, given by (
36
) and (
42
).
These conditions are expressed in terms of the effective temperature, an estimator for temperature, as perceived by the detector.
Since thermality is not a prerequisite, the expressions for work extracted and the positive work condition are applicable to any situation.
Notably, when the field is in a thermal state, known as the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) state, and the interaction duration of a rapidly decreasing switching function is sufficiently long, the effective temperature becomes the KMS temperature.
In such a case, the condition for positive work (
42
) becomes identical to the original condition found in
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
.

The derived expressions are demonstrated concretely within two scenarios: an inertial detector immersed in a KMS state of a quantum field in Minkowski spacetime and a detector in circular motion in the Minkowski vacuum.
The first example is the most basic instance of thermality within QFT, for which we employ a Gaussian switching function and explore the effects of interaction duration.

The second example is inspired by the Unruh QOE previously examined in the literature, which has some challenges.
A linearly accelerating detector requires an immense amount of acceleration to reach a temperature of 1 Kelvin.
This can be seen from the expression of the Unruh temperature in SI units,
T_{\text{U}}=\hbar a/2\pi k_{\text{B}}c
, where
\hbar,k_{\text{B}}
, and
c
are respectively the reduced Planck constant, the Boltzmann constant, and the speed of light.
It is well known that an acceleration of
a\sim 10^{20}
m/s
2
is required to achieve
T_{\text{U}}\sim 1
K.
This implies that significant work must be performed on the detector to extract a small amount of work from the Unruh QOE.
Moreover, the duration of interaction is almost instantaneous.
Typically, the Unruh QOE assumes that the detector initially travels at a constant speed
v
and then accelerates until it comes back to the same speed in the opposite direction.
The time interval in this process (in terms of the detector’s proper time
\tau
) is given by
\Delta\tau=(2c/a)\text{arctanh}(v/c)
, and inserting
a=10^{20}
m/s
2
, it requires
v\approx c
to yield
\Delta\tau\sim 1
s.
Otherwise, if we demand a slower speed, say
v=c/2
, then the interaction duration amounts to
\Delta\tau\sim 10^{-12}
seconds, which is an extremely short period of time.
The detector certainly cannot thermalize within such a time scale.
Finally, executing such a protocol demands considerable space.
The procedure in the Unruh QOE consists of linear acceleration and uniform motion at speed
v
.
This means that it requires the detector to consistently move in one direction, which cannot be done in a confined laboratory.

Instead, one can consider a UDW detector in circular motion, a concept that is motivated by
Bell.circular.1983
;
Bell.Leinaas.1987
;
BEC_Unruh
, where an experimental setup is proposed to measure the circular Unruh effect.
See also
Retzker.BEC.2008
;
Marino.zero-point.circular
;
CircularTemperaturesUnruh
;
bunney2023sound
.
A circulating UDW detector overcomes the shortcomings described above, though the temperature induced by the acceleration is no longer
T_{\text{U}}
.
In fact, the detector cannot be thermalized.
Instead, one should define the effective temperature
T_{\text{circ}}
as observed by the circulating detector
CircularTemperaturesUnruh
;
Good.Unruh-like.effective.temp
;
Unruh.acceleration.rad.electron.1998.arxiv
.
This is where our proposed framework excels, as our expressions remain valid even in the absence of thermality.

Setup

2.1
Unruh-DeWitt detector model

Consider a pointlike UDW detector, which is a two-level quantum system with an energy gap
\Omega
between ground
\ket{g}
and excited states
\ket{e}
locally interacting with the quantum Klein-Gordon field
\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x})
.
Here, the background spacetime is not restricted to a specific geometry but is assumed to be a (curved) spacetime that accommodates a well-behaved Klein-Gordon equation.

In the Schrödinger picture, the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{S,tot}}
reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S,tot}}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{\text{S,D}}+\hat{H}_{\text{S,$\phi$}}+\hat{H}_{\text{S,int}}\,,

(1)

where
\hat{H}_{\text{S,D}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{S,$\phi$}}
are the free Hamiltonians for the detector and the field, respectively, and
\hat{H}_{\text{S,int}}
is the interaction Hamiltonian.
These are explicitly given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S,D}}

\displaystyle=\Omega\ket{e}\bra{e}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\phi}\,,

(2a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S,$\phi$}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{\text{D}}\otimes\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\text{d}^{n}k\,\omega_{{\bm{k}}}\hat{a}_{{\bm{k}}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{{\bm{k}}}\,,

(2b)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{S,int}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\chi(\tau/\sigma)(\ket{e}\bra{g}+\ket{g}\bra{e})\otimes\hat{\phi}({\bm{x}}(\tau))\,,

(2c)

where
\lambda
is the coupling constant,
\tau
is the proper time of the detector, and
\chi(\tau/\sigma)
is the switching function (with
\sigma
being the typical time scale of interaction), which determines the time dependence of coupling.
In this paper, we assume that the
L^{2}
norm of
\chi(\tau)
is unity:

\displaystyle||\chi||_{2}\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\,|\chi(\tau)|^{2}}=1\,.

(3)

Let
\hat{H}_{\text{I}}^{\tau}(\tau)
be the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture.
Here, the Hamiltonian is the generator of time-translation with respect to the proper time
\tau
, which is indicated by the superscript.
The explicit form is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{I}}^{\tau}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\lambda\chi(\tau/\sigma)\hat{\mu}(\tau)\otimes\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau))\,,

(4)

where
\hat{\mu}(\tau)
is the monopole moment describing the internal dynamics of the detector:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\ket{e}\bra{g}e^{\mathsf{i}\Omega\tau}+\ket{g}\bra{e}e^{-\mathsf{i}\Omega\tau}\,.

(5)

Let us obtain the final state of the detector.
The time-evolution operator in the interaction picture is given by

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\text{I}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\tau}\exp\left(-\mathsf{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\,\hat{H}_{\text{I}}^{\tau}(\tau)\right)\,,

(6)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\tau}
is a time-ordering symbol with respect to the proper time
\tau
.
Carrying out the perturbative method, this operator can be expanded as

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\text{I}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}+\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{(1)}+\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\,\hat{H}_{\text{I}}^{\tau}(\tau)\,,

(7b)

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{d}\tau\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\hat{H}_{\text{I}}^{\tau}(\tau)\hat{H}_{\text{I}}^{\tau}(\tau^{\prime})\,.

(7c)

Consider the initial state of the total system,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot,0}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\text{D,0}}\otimes\rho_{\phi}\,,

(8)

where
\rho_{\phi}
is the initial state of the field and
\rho_{\text{D,0}}
is the detector’s initial state given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{D,0}}

\displaystyle=p\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p)\ket{g}\bra{g}\,.\quad p\in[0,1]

(9)

The final total state
\rho_{\text{tot}}
can be obtained as

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\hat{U}_{\text{I}}\rho_{\text{tot,0}}\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{(m)}\rho_{\text{tot,0}}\hat{U}_{\text{I}}^{(n)\dagger}

(10)

and by tracing out the field degree of freedom we obtain
the final state of the detector,
\rho_{\text{D}}
.
In general,
\rho_{\text{D}}
contains
n
-point correlation functions
\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{1})...\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}_{n})}_{\rho_{\phi}}
, where
\braket{\cdot}_{\rho_{\phi}}
is the expectation value with respect to
\rho_{\phi}
.
In this paper, we are particularly interested in a quantum field in a quasi-free state,
1
1

1

Sometimes quasi-free states are called Gaussian states in the literature.
in which
the one-point correlator
\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x})}_{\rho_{\phi}}
vanishes and every correlation function can be written in terms of two-point correlators
W(\mathsf{x},\mathsf{x}^{\prime})\coloneqq\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x})\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}^{\prime})}_{\rho_{\phi}}
, also known as the Wightman function.
Examples of quasi-free states include the vacuum state
\ket{0}
and the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) state, which we will describe in the next subsection
2.2
.
Assuming that
\rho_{\phi}
is a quasi-free state, the post-interaction state of the detector to the leading order in
\lambda
can be calculated as follows.

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{D}}

\displaystyle=\Tr_{\phi}[\rho_{\text{tot}}]=(p+\delta p)\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p-\delta p)\ket{g}\bra{g}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\,,

(11)

where

\displaystyle\delta p

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\sigma\big[(1-p)\mathcal{F}(\Omega)-p\mathcal{F}(-\Omega)\big]\,,

(12)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\Omega)=\dfrac{1}{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi(\tau/\sigma)\chi(\tau^{\prime}/\sigma)e^{-\mathsf{i}\Omega(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))

(13)

being the response function,
i.e.
the probability to excite the ground state
\ket{g}
to
\ket{e}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{F}(-\Omega)
is the probability for the transition
\ket{e}\to\ket{g}
.
The quantity
W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\coloneqq\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau))\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))}_{\rho_{\phi}}
is the pullback of the Wightman function along the detector’s trajectory
\mathsf{x}(\tau)
.
Note that the odd-powers in
\lambda
vanish since the one-point correlators
\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x})}_{\rho_{\phi}}
are zero.

We remark that
\rho_{\text{D}}
is the state of the detector after interacting with the field once, while in QOE, the detector interacts with the field twice.
Fortunately, Eq. (
11
) tells us that the density matrix
\rho_{\text{D}}
stays diagonal after the interaction, meaning the final density matrix also remains diagonal after the second interaction.
Nevertheless, careful attention must be paid to the switching timings of these two interactions.

2.2
KMS condition

In quantum mechanics, a thermal state is given by the Gibbs state
\rho=e^{-\beta\hat{H}}/Z
, where
\beta
is the inverse temperature,
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian, and
Z\coloneqq\Tr[e^{-\beta\hat{H}}]
is the partition function.
In quantum field theory, the notion of thermality is rigorously defined by the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition
Kubo1957thermality
;
Martin-Schwinger1959thermality
;
haag1967equilibrium
.
The argument given in this section is summarized in Fig.
1
.

Consider a Wightman function in a field state
\rho_{\phi}
:

\displaystyle W(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau))\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))}_{\rho_{\phi}}\,.

(14)

Note that the vacuum Wightman function
\braket{0|\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau))\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))|0}
is a special case corresponding to
\rho_{\phi}=\ket{0}\bra{0}
.
The Wightman function satisfies the KMS condition with respect to time
\tau
at the KMS temperature
\beta_{\text{KMS}}^{-1}\equiv T_{\text{KMS}}
if

\displaystyle W(\tau-\mathsf{i}\beta_{\text{KMS}},\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=W(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\,.

(15)

In addition, if the KMS condition is satisfied, then the Wightman function is stationary, i.e., it only depends on the difference in time:
W(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=W(\Delta\tau)
.
We identify the KMS temperature
T_{\text{KMS}}
as the temperature of the quantum field.

The KMS condition is related to the so-called detailed balance condition.
For any function
f(\omega)
, the detailed balance condition at temperature
\beta^{-1}
is given by

\displaystyle f(-\omega)=e^{\beta\omega}f(\omega)\,.

(16)

It is known
Fewster.Waiting.Unruh
that, assuming the Wightman function is stationary, the KMS condition with respect to
\tau
at temperature
\beta_{\text{KMS}}^{-1}
is satisfied if and only if the Fourier transformed Wightman function,

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(\omega)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\Delta\tau\,W(\Delta\tau)e^{-\mathsf{i}\omega\Delta\tau}\,,

(17)

satisfies the detailed balance condition (
16
).

The detailed balance condition can be implemented into the response function of a UDW detector.
Suppose the Wightman function satisfies the KMS condition and the switching function
\chi(\tau/\sigma)
has a typical interaction duration time scale
\sigma
.
The response function (
13
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{\text{d}\bar{\omega}}{2\pi}\,|\tilde{\chi}(\bar{\omega})|^{2}\tilde{W}(\Omega+\bar{\omega}/\sigma)\,,

(18)

where
\bar{\omega}
is a dimensionless variable and
\tilde{\chi}(\bar{\omega})
is the Fourier transform of the switching function
\chi(\tau/\sigma)
.
One can show that if
\tilde{\chi}(\bar{\omega})
decays sufficiently fast as
\bar{\omega}
increases (such as a Gaussian switching), then the response function satisfies the detailed balance condition in the long interaction limit
Fewster.Waiting.Unruh
;
Garay2016anti-unruh
:

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}(-\Omega)}{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}=e^{\beta_{\text{KMS}}\Omega}\,.

(19)

Inspired by the detailed balance condition for the response function (
19
), one may define the
effective temperature

T_{\text{eff}}
:

\displaystyle T_{\text{eff}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{\Omega}\ln\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}(-\Omega)}{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}(\Omega)}\,.

(20)

The effective temperature is an estimator for the KMS temperature of the field.
In particular, if the Wightman function obeys the KMS condition and the long interaction limit (
\sigma\to\infty
) is taken, then the effective temperature becomes the KMS temperature due to (
19
).

Relativistic quantum Otto engine

3.1
Review: quantum Otto engine

In this subsection, we review the QOE following the paper by Kieu
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
.
Note that the whole system is composed of a qubit and thermal baths in the Gibbs state.
In the next subsection, we will take the following protocol and apply it to the system composed of a UDW detector interacting with a quantum scalar field in a quasi-free state, namely, we will omit the assumption that the baths are in a thermal state.

1.

Prepare a qubit with energy gap
\Omega_{1}
in an initial state
\rho_{0}=p\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p)\ket{g}\bra{g}
, where
p\in[0,1]
, as shown in Fig.
2
.

2.

Let the qubit undergo an adiabatic expansion (zero heat exchange:
\braket{Q_{1}}=0
) to enlarge the energy gap from
\Omega_{1}
to
\Omega_{2}
, where
\Omega_{2}>\Omega_{1}
.
The Hamiltonian of the qubit is given by
\hat{H}(t)=\Omega(t)\ket{e}\bra{e}
and so the work done on the qubit is

\displaystyle\braket{W_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int\text{d}t\,\Tr\left[\rho_{0}\dfrac{\text{d}\hat{H}(t)}{\text{d}t}\right]=p\Delta\Omega\,,

(21)

where
\Delta\Omega\coloneqq\Omega_{2}-\Omega_{1}
.
The qubit’s state remains as
\rho_{0}
due to the quantum adiabatic theorem.

3.

Let the qubit interact with a bath at temperature
T_{\text{H}}
.
The qubit’s Hamiltonian is time-independent
\hat{H}=\Omega_{2}\ket{e}\bra{e}
, and so the work done on the qubit is zero:
\braket{W_{2}}=0
.
After the interaction, the state becomes
\rho_{1}=(p+\delta p_{\text{H}})\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p-\delta p_{\text{H}})\ket{g}\bra{g}
.
The first law of thermodynamics gives the dissipated heat:

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{2}}

\displaystyle=\Tr[\rho_{1}\hat{H}]-\Tr[\rho_{0}\hat{H}]=\Omega_{2}\delta p_{\text{H}}\,.

(22)

This is an isochoric thermalization process.

4.

After isolating the qubit from the bath, perform an adiabatic contraction of the energy gap from
\Omega_{2}
to
\Omega_{1}
, while the state remains to be
\rho_{1}
.
From the same calculation as in step-2, we have

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{3}}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\braket{W_{3}}

\displaystyle=-(p+\delta p_{\text{H}})\Delta\Omega\,.

(24)

The work is extracted from the qubit in this process.

5.

Next another isochoric thermalization process is carried out: the qubit interacts with a bath at a colder temperature
T_{\text{C}}(<T_{\text{H}})
.
At the end of the process, the state becomes
\rho_{2}=(p+\delta p_{\text{H}}+\delta p_{\text{C}})\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p-\delta p_{\text{H}}-\delta p_{\text{C}})\ket{g}\bra{g}
and so

\displaystyle\braket{Q_{4}}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{1}\delta p_{\text{C}}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\braket{W_{4}}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(26)

The total extracted work and dissipated heat read

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{4}\braket{W_{j}}=\delta p_{\text{H}}\Delta\Omega\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\braket{Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\braket{Q_{j}}=\Omega_{2}\delta p_{\text{H}}+\Omega_{1}\delta p_{\text{C}}\,.

(28)

Assuming
\Omega_{2}>\Omega_{1}
, the extracted work is positive if
\delta p_{\text{H}}>0
.

6.

Impose
\delta p_{\text{H}}+\delta p_{\text{C}}=0
to complete a thermodynamic cycle.
From this condition, Eqs. (
27
) and (
28
) yield
\braket{Q}=\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}
, which obeys the first law of thermodynamics.

It is known that the positive work condition reads
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto

T_{\text{C}}/\Omega_{1}<T_{\text{H}}/\Omega_{2}
and the efficiency of the QOE is
\eta_{\text{O}}\coloneqq\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}/Q_{2}=1-\Omega_{1}/\Omega_{2}
.

3.2
Work extracted by a UDW detector

Now let us employ a UDW detector as the qubit in this process.
As mentioned before, we will not assume that the baths are thermal.
Instead, we only assume that the quantum field is in a quasi-free state, i.e., the one-point correlators
\braket{\hat{\phi}(\mathsf{x})}_{\rho_{\phi}}
vanish.
Nevertheless, we will keep using subscripts such as
\delta p_{\text{H}}
and
\delta p_{\text{C}}
for convenience.

In this subsection, we consider a general scenario in which a UDW detector following an arbitrary path in spacetime interacts with a quantum field in a quasi-free state.
Working perturbatively, we derive the extracted work and the condition for it to be positive.

3.2.1
Relativistic quantum Otto engine

We first describe our QOE in the relativistic setting as depicted in Fig.
3
.

•

(step-1)
Before the interaction, the detector with the energy gap
\Omega_{1}
is prepared in
\rho_{\text{D,0}}
in (
9
).

•

(Step-2)
One performs a quantum adiabatic expansion
\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
with
\Omega_{2}>\Omega_{1}
.
During this process, the detector does not interact with the field.

•

(Step-3)
Isochoric process.
The detector interacts with the field with a time-dependence specified by a compactly supported switching function
\chi_{\text{H}}[(\tau-\tau_{\text{H}})/\sigma_{\text{H}}]
, where
\tau_{\text{H}}
is the center of interaction and
\sigma_{\text{H}}(>0)
is the interaction duration; its support is
\text{supp}(\chi_{\text{H}})=[\tau_{\text{H}}-\sigma_{\text{H}}/2,\tau_{\text{H}}+\sigma_{\text{H}}/2]
.
The state changes from
\rho_{\text{D,0}}\to\rho_{\text{D,1}}
, where
\rho_{\text{D,1}}
is given by Eqs. (
11
)-(
13
), that is,

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{D,1}}=(p+\delta p_{\text{H}})\ket{e}\bra{e}+(1-p-\delta p_{\text{H}})\ket{g}\bra{g}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\,,

(29a)

\displaystyle\delta p_{\text{H}}=\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}\big[(1-p)\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})-p\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})\big]\,,

(29b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})=\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{\text{H}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi_{\text{H}}\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{\text{H}}}{\sigma_{\text{H}}}\right)\chi_{\text{H}}\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}-\tau_{\text{H}}}{\sigma_{\text{H}}}\right)e^{-\mathsf{i}\Omega_{2}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\,.

(29c)

While we identified the switching function as a compactly supported function, it is also possible to employ rapidly decreasing functions such as Gaussian or Lorentzian functions.
These are not compactly supported, but the results remain unchanged when the exponentially decreasing tails of these functions are disregarded.
We will make use of these functions later in this subsection.

•

(Step-4)
Adiabatic compression
\Omega_{2}\to\Omega_{1}
.
Again, the detector does not interact during this process and so the state remains as

(Step-5)
Implement another isochoric process using

To ensure that the interaction does not overlap with the previous isochoric process, we make sure that the supports of the two switching functions are disjoint by implementing

The state of the detector changes as

.

(30a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})=\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{\text{C}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{d}\tau^{\prime}\,\chi_{\text{C}}\left(\frac{\tau-\tau_{\text{C}}}{\sigma_{\text{C}}}\right)\chi_{\text{C}}\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}-\tau_{\text{C}}}{\sigma_{\text{C}}}\right)e^{-\mathsf{i}\Omega_{1}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}W(\mathsf{x}(\tau),\mathsf{x}(\tau^{\prime}))\,.

(30b)

\delta p_{\text{C}}

\lambda^{4}

\delta p_{\text{H}}

.
As in the traditional QOE, the work extracted reads
\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}=\delta p_{\text{H}}\Delta\Omega
.

•

(Step-6)
Finally, we impose the condition
\delta p_{\text{H}}+\delta p_{\text{C}}=0
to complete a thermodynamic cycle.

Since
\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}
only depends on
\delta p_{\text{H}}
, it may appear that the cyclicity condition does not affect this value.
However there is a subtlety.
If we assume that an experimenter can freely choose a value of
p\in[0,1]
at the beginning of the cycle, this means that the experimenter has to adjust the response functions
\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\pm\Omega_{2})
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\pm\Omega_{1})
in such a way that the cyclicity condition is satisfied.
On the other hand, if we allow the response functions to take any value, then the population
p
has to be adjusted accordingly.

3.2.2
When
p
is free to choose

We first require the cyclicity condition,
\delta p_{\text{H}}+\delta p_{\text{C}}=0
, to close the thermodynamic cycle.
Since
\delta p_{\text{C}}
contains terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})
, we omit these by imposing
1\gg\delta p_{\text{H}}/p
for a given
p(\neq 0)
.
This reduces to

\displaystyle p\gg\dfrac{\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})}{1+\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}[\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})+\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})]}\,.

(31)

In particular, if the long-interaction limit
\sigma_{\text{H}}\to\infty
is taken, we obtain

\displaystyle p\gg\dfrac{1}{1+\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma_{\text{H}}\to\infty}\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})/\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})}=\dfrac{1}{1+\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma_{\text{H}}\to\infty}e^{\Omega_{2}/T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}}}\,.

(32)

Thus, in the long interaction duration,
T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{2}\ll 1
is required.
Moreover, as a special case of a detector with a rapidly decreasing switching function interacting with the quantum field in the KMS state, this reads

\displaystyle p\gg\dfrac{1}{1+e^{\Omega_{2}/T_{\text{H}}^{\text{KMS}}}}\,.

(33)

Nevertheless, if the interaction duration is finite, then
\lambda\ll 1
is sufficient for our perturbative analysis.

2

To be precise, one has to make
\lambda
a dimensionless quantity since it has units of
[\text{Length}]^{(n-3)/2}
in
(n+1)
dimensions.

Under this restriction, the cyclicity condition for any
p\in(0,1]
leads to

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{C}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})\left(\dfrac{1-p}{p}-\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(-\Omega_{1})}{\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})}\right)=-\sigma_{\text{H}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})\left(\dfrac{1-p}{p}-\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})}{\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})}\right)\,.

(34)

That is, one has to tune the response functions so that this equality is satisfied.

We then consider the positive work condition in the case where the value of
p
is chosen freely by an experimenter.
The work extracted (
27
) is

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}\big[(1-p)\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})-p\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})\big]\Delta\Omega\,,

(35)

and the positive work condition is
(1-p)\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})-p\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})>0
, i.e.,
(1-p)/p>\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})/\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})
.
We note that
\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})/\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})>1
since the de-excitation probability is higher than the excitation probability, thereby implying
p\in(0,1/2)
.
This is consistent with previous work
UnruhOttoEngine
, where the Unruh QOE with a free
p
is able to extract work only when
0<p<1/2
.
The condition can be written in terms of the effective temperature (
20
):

\displaystyle\left(\ln\dfrac{1-p}{p}\right)^{-1}<\dfrac{T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}}{\Omega_{2}}\,,\quad(0<p<1/2)\,.

(36)

It should be recalled that in the long interaction duration,
T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{2}\ll 1
is required, which means that (
36
) tells us that
p\ll 1
needs to be chosen if we wish to extract positive work in the long-interaction limit within the perturbation theory.

Notice that if positive work is extracted while the cyclicity condition is satisfied, then
\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\pm\Omega_{1})
has to obey
(1-p)/p<\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(-\Omega_{1})/\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})
since the response functions are non-negative and the positive work condition requires
(1-p)/p>\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})/\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})
.
Therefore, within the perturbation theory,

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}>0~\text{from QOE}\Rightarrow\dfrac{T_{\text{C}}^{\text{eff}}}{\Omega_{1}}<\left(\ln\dfrac{1-p}{p}\right)^{-1}<\dfrac{T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}}{\Omega_{2}}\,.

(37)

The opposite is not always true since the inequality does not necessarily imply the cyclicity condition (
34
).
In addition, the statement (
37
) does not hold without the cyclicity condition.
Nevertheless, by contraposition, if
T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{2}>T_{\text{C}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{1}
is violated then positive work cannot be extracted
or
the cycle is not closed.

The inequality
T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{2}>T_{\text{C}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{1}
generalizes the positive work condition found in
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
, by replacing bath temperatures with effective temperatures.
Specifically, effective temperatures reduce to the KMS temperatures when the pullback of the Wightman function along a trajectory satisfies the KMS condition and when the detector, with a rapidly decreasing switching function, interacts with the field for a long time.
In this particular scenario, the work extracted becomes

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})\left[1-p\left(1+e^{\Omega_{2}/T_{\text{H}}^{\text{KMS}}}\right)\right]\Delta\Omega\,.

(38)

3.2.3
When
p
is adjusted

Let us focus on the case where
p
is initially adjusted and derive the work extracted.
We again assume that
p\neq 0
and
p\gg\delta p_{\text{H}}
so that the terms higher than
\lambda^{4}
can be omitted in
\delta p_{\text{C}}
.
From the cyclicity condition, we can solve for
p
, which reads

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\sigma_{\text{H}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})+\sigma_{\text{C}}\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})}{\sigma_{\text{H}}[\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})+\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})]+\sigma_{\text{C}}[\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})+\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(-\Omega_{1})]}\,.

(39)

By inserting
p
into (
27
) we obtain

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}=\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}\sigma_{\text{C}}\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(-\Omega_{1})-\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})}{\sigma_{\text{H}}[\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})+\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})]+\sigma_{\text{C}}[\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})+\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(-\Omega_{1})]}\Delta\Omega\,.

(40)

The extract work is positive if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(-\Omega_{1})>\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(-\Omega_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})\,.

(41)

We find that the positive work condition (
41
) can be written in terms of the effective temperature defined by (
20
) as

\displaystyle\dfrac{T_{\text{C}}^{\text{eff}}}{\Omega_{1}}<\dfrac{T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}}{\Omega_{2}}\,.

(42)

Again, we are not necessarily assuming that the field is in the KMS state, and so the effective temperature is not necessarily the KMS temperature.
If the field is in the KMS state and the detector with a rapidly decreasing switching function interacts with the field for a long time (
\sigma_{\text{H}},\sigma_{\text{C}}\to\infty
), the effective temperature becomes the KMS temperature, leading to

\displaystyle\dfrac{T_{\text{C}}^{\text{KMS}}}{\Omega_{1}}<\dfrac{T_{\text{H}}^{\text{KMS}}}{\Omega_{2}}\,,

(43)

which is the condition found in
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
.
The extracted work in this case is

\displaystyle\braket{W_{\text{ext}}}=\lambda^{2}\sigma_{\text{H}}\sigma_{\text{C}}\dfrac{(e^{\Omega_{1}/T_{\text{C}}}-e^{\Omega_{2}/T_{\text{H}}})\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})}{\sigma_{\text{H}}(1+e^{\Omega_{2}/T_{\text{H}}})\mathcal{F}_{\text{H}}(\Omega_{2})+\sigma_{\text{C}}(1+e^{\Omega_{1}/T_{\text{C}}})\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\Omega_{1})}\Delta\Omega\,.

(44)

Therefore, (
43
) is a special case of (
41
) [and equivalently (
42
)] when the response function obeys the detailed balance condition (
19
).
In other words, the condition (
41
) (or alternatively (
42
)) is applicable to any scenario, even when the detector is not thermalized.

In summary, we showed that within the perturbation theory, the positive work condition is associated with
T_{\text{C}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{1}<T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{2}
.
If
p
is not fixed and the cycle is closed by adjusting the response functions,
T_{\text{C}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{1}<T_{\text{H}}^{\text{eff}}/\Omega_{2}
is a necessary condition to extract positive work.
On the other hand, if we instead adjust
p
to close the cycle while the response functions are not fixed, then this condition is necessary and sufficient.
This recovers the traditional QOE positive work condition
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
as a special case.
Note that our results hold not only for a quantum field in the KMS state but also for any quasi-free state in curved spacetime.
The use of the effective temperature is similar to other papers such as
Huang.squeezing.work
.

5
Conclusion

Inspired by the Unruh quantum Otto engines, we considered the quantum Otto engine (QOE) in a general setting utilizing an Unruh-DeWitt (UDW) detector interacting with a quantum scalar field.
While the original QOE considered a qubit thermalized in thermal baths, we assumed (i) the field is in a quasi-free state, not necessarily in a Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) thermal state, and (ii) the detector follows an arbitrary trajectory in curved spacetime.
By carrying out a perturbative method, we managed to derive the extracted work (
35
) and (
40
) in terms of the response function of the UDW detector.
In addition, we found that the condition for extracting positive work can be expressed in terms of the effective temperature perceived by the detector, (
36
) and (
42
).
These formulae are applicable to various scenarios outside (thermal) equilibrium.
As a particular case, if the field is in the KMS state and the detector with a rapidly decreasing switching function interacts with it for a long time, the response function is known to obey the detailed balance condition, which makes the effective temperature identical to the KMS temperature.
The condition (
42
) then becomes the positive work condition (
43
) originally found by
Feldmann.QOE.2000
;
Kieu.secondlaw.demon.otto
.

Using the formula for the extracted work, we explored two concrete examples: an inertial detector in the KMS state and a circulating detector in the Minkowski vacuum.

In the scenario of an inertial detector in thermal baths, we numerically examined the effect of the interaction duration.
The relationship between the extracted work and the interaction duration is drastically changed by the choice of the detector’s initial state.
Nevertheless, the extracted work asymptotes to the value that corresponds to thermal equilibrium as the interaction duration increases.

Another example involves a circulating detector in the Minkowski vacuum.
The motivation for this scenario comes from the previously proposed Unruh QOE
UnruhOttoEngine
, in which the thermal baths are generated by the Unruh effect.
However, utilizing a linearly accelerating UDW detector in the Unruh QOE is not practical for several reasons such as the work necessary for acceleration and the requirement for a large space.
The circulating detector, on the other hand, circumvents these issues.
Moreover, the QOE with a circulating detector can be adequately examined only when our general framework is employed;
The Wightman function pulled back along the circular trajectory does not satisfy the KMS condition, which suggests that the use of the effective temperature is necessary.

The work extracted by the circulating detector showed behavior similar to that of the inertial detector in a thermal bath.
However, we found that there exist optimal values for the interaction duration and radius of the orbit to extract maximum work.
Furthermore, we showed that the detector is unable to extract work with a fixed acceleration due to the violation of the positive work condition.

An interesting extension to our work would involve exploring quantum heat engines using non quasi-free states.
In particular, coherent and squeezed states—which are not quasi-free—are known to be particular interest in a variety of settings.
It would be interesting to see how a non-vanishing one-point correlator contributes to the cycle.
