Hessenberg varieties of codimension one in the flag variety
Laura Escobar, Martha Precup, John Shareshian

Abstract
. We study geometric and topological properties of Hessenberg varieties of codimension one in the type A flag variety. Our main results: (1) give a formula for the Poincaré polynomial, (2) characterize when these varieties are irreducible, and (3) show that all are reduced schemes. We prove that the singular locus of any nilpotent codimension one Hessenberg variety is also a Hessenberg variety. A key tool in our analysis is a new result applying to all (type A) Hessenberg varieties without any restriction on codimension, which states that their Poincaré polynomials can be computed by counting the points in the corresponding variety defined over a finite field. The results below were originally motivated by work of the authors in [arXiv:2107.07929] studying the precise relationship between Hessenberg and Schubert varieties, and we obtain a corollary extending the results from that paper to all codimension one (type A) Schubert varieties.



1.
Introduction

We consider Hessenberg varieties of type A.
Given a positive integer
n
, let
G=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
B\subseteq G
the Borel subgroup of upper triangular matrices. Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
\mathfrak{b}
be the respective Lie algebras of
G
and
B
. We consider the action of
G
on
\mathfrak{g}
by conjugation. Given
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{g}
and a
B
-invariant subspace
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, the type A
Hessenberg variety

\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H)
consists of all
gB
in the flag variety
\mathcal{B}:=G/B
satisfying
g^{-1}\mathsf{x}g\in H
.

We assume throughout that
H
contains the Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
, as was assumed by De Mari and Shayman in
[
DMS88
]
and by De Mari, Procesi and Shayman in
[
DMPS92
]
, seminal works on Hessenberg varieties. Under this assumption, there is a unique
Hessenberg vector

{\mathbf{m}}=({\mathbf{m}}(1),\ldots,{\mathbf{m}}(n))
, that is, a weakly increasing sequence from
[n]
satisfying
{\mathbf{m}}(i)\geq i
for all
i\in[n]
, such that
H
consists of all matrices
\mathsf{a}=(\mathsf{a}_{ij})\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathsf{a}_{ij}=0
if
i>{\mathbf{m}}(j)
. We write
H=H({\mathbf{m}})
in this case. We omit the assumption from
[
DMS88
,
DMPS92
]
that
\mathsf{x}
is regular and semisimple.

Of particular interest herein is the case where
H=H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}})
with

{\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}:=(n-1,n,\ldots,n).

Notice that
H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}})
is the unique maximal proper
B
-invariant subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
, hence our choice of notation.
We observe that if
\mathsf{x}
is not scalar, then
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
has codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
. Conversely, we showed in
[
EPS21
]
that if
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H)
has codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
, then
H=H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}})
or there is some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\mathsf{x}-\lambda I_{n}
has rank one. We continue our study of codimension one Hessenberg varieties in this manuscript, concentrating on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
.

One of our main tools is a result that applies with no condition on codimension and could be of use in other settings.
We define as usual the
Poincaré polynomial
of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H)
by

\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H);q):=\sum_{j}\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(H^{j}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H);{\mathbb{C}}))q^{j}.

Given a Hessenberg vector
{\mathbf{m}}
, the variety
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}))
admits an affine paving, as shown by Tymoczko in
[
Tym06b
]
. It follows that the (singular) cohomology of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}))
is concentrated in even degrees. So
\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}));q^{1/2})
is a polynomial in
q
. It follows from the particulars of Tymoczko’s result that, given any
\mathsf{x}
and
H({\mathbf{m}})
as above, there exists an upper triangular integer matrix
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
such that

\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}));q)=\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x}^{\prime},H({\mathbf{m}}));q).

We may assume that
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
is in what Tymoczko calls highest form and permuted Jordan form, which will be defined later. In addition, we may assume that the eigenvalues of x are integers. In particular,
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
is the sum of a diagonal integer matrix
\semi
and a strictly upper triangular
0-1
matrix
\nilp
. We write
m_{\semi}
for the largest absolute value of an entry of
\semi
. Given any prime
p
, let
\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
be the matrix obtained from
\mathsf{x}^{\prime}
by reducing each entry modulo
p
. Let
\bar{G}=GL_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
and let
\bar{B}
be the subgroup of
\bar{G}
consisting of upper triangular matrices. The Hessenberg vector
{\mathbf{m}}
determines a subspace
\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})
of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
in the same way it determines
H({\mathbf{m}})
. We define

\mathcal{B}_{p}(\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{\prime},\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})):=\{g\bar{B}\in\bar{G}/\bar{B}\mid g^{-1}\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{\prime}g\in\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})\}.

Theorem 1

(The point count heuristic)
.

If
p>2m_{\semi}
, then

(1.1)

\left|\mathcal{B}_{p}(\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{\prime},\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}}))\right|=\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x}^{\prime},H({\mathbf{m}}));p^{1/2}).

We prove Theorem
1
in Section
3
through close examination of Tymoczko’s affine paving construction. The point count heuristic allows us to compute the Poincaré polynomial of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}))
using elementary methods. For example, we obtain a simple formula for the Poincaré polynomial of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
for arbitrary
\mathsf{x}
. It is not hard to enumerate
\mathcal{B}_{p}(\mathsf{y},\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
for arbitrary
\mathsf{y}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
. The following result appears in Section
4
below.

Proposition 2
.

If
\mathsf{y}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})
fixes exactly
k

1
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n}
, then

\left|\mathcal{B}_{p}(\mathsf{y},\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))\right|=[n-2]_{p}!\left([n]_{p}[n-2]_{p}+kp^{n-2}\right).

The next result follows quickly from Theorem
1
and Proposition
2
.

Theorem 3
.

If
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{g}
has exactly
\ell
pairwise distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots\lambda_{\ell}
and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\ker(\mathsf{x}-\lambda_{j}I_{n})=d_{j}
for each
j\in[\ell]
, then the Poincaré polynomial of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is

(1.2)

\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}));q)=[n-2]_{q^{2}}!\left([n]_{q^{2}}[n-2]_{q^{2}}+q^{2n-4}\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}[d_{j}]_{q^{2}}\right)

Our proof of (
1.2
) using the point count heuristic is much simpler than applying previously known Betti number formulas from, e.g.,
[
Tym06b
,
Pre13
]
, which each require multiple cases.
Theorem
1
(through Theorem
3
) is also helpful in understanding the geometry of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
. We obtain the following result in Section
6
.

Theorem 4
.

For
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{g}
, the variety
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is reducible if and only if there is some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
\mathsf{x}-\lambda I_{n}
has rank one.

It follows immediately from Theorem
4
that
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is reducible if and only if
\mathsf{x}
is nilpotent with Jordan decomposition corresponding to the partition
(2,1^{n-2})
or
\mathsf{x}
is semisimple and conjugate to a diagonal matrix
\mathrm{diag}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{1},c_{2})
such that
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
c_{1}\neq c_{2}
.

The proof of Theorem
4
relies on Theorem
1
and the examination of affine patches. Let
B_{-}
be the Borel subgroup consisting of all lower triangular matrices in
G
and let
U_{-}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{{n}\choose{2}}
be the unipotent radical of
B_{-}
, consisting of all matrices
I+\nilp
with
\nilp
strictly lower triangular. The
patch
in
\mathcal{B}
centered at a point
gB
is
{\mathcal{N}}_{g}:=gB_{-}B/B
. Each coset in this patch has a unique representative of the form
guB
with
u\in U_{-}
. It follows that
{\mathcal{N}}_{g}
is open in
\mathcal{B}
and isomorphic with affine space
{\mathbb{C}}^{{n}\choose{2}}
. Now for
gB\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
, we see that
{\mathcal{N}}_{g,\mathsf{x}}^{{\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}}:={\mathcal{N}}_{g}\cap\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is an open neighborhood of
gB
in
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
that is isomorphic to an affine hypersurface in
U_{-}
cut out by a certain determinant. Using this fact, we will show that
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is equidimensional. This allows us to determine whether or not
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is irreducible by calculating its top Betti number, which Theorem
3
renders easy.

Examination of patches enables us to determine the singular locus of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
when
\mathsf{x}
is nilpotent. Indeed, the point
gB
is smooth in
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
if and only if it is smooth in
{\mathcal{N}}_{g,\mathsf{x}}^{{\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}}
. Direct calculation yields the next result, which we prove in Section
5
. We remark that this method was used to determine the singular loci of the members of another class of Hessenberg varieties, the Petersen varieties, by Insko and Yong in
[
IY12
]
.
These patches were also used by Abe, Dedieu, Galetto and Harada in
[
ADGH18
]
, by Abe, Fujita and Zeng in
[
AFZ20
]
, and by Da Silva and Harada in
[
DSH22
]
.

Theorem 5
.

The singular locus of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
is contained in
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H((1,n-1,\ldots,n-1,n)))
. If
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent, then this containment is an equality.

We consider also the scheme theoretic structure of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
defined as in
[
ITW20
, Section 4]
.
Use of patches yields the following result, which we prove in Section
8
.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
n\geq 3
.
For any
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
the scheme
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))\subseteq GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
is reduced.

Combining Theorems
1
,
3
and
5
, we settle a problem we studied in
[
EPS21
]
: we determine which Schubert varieties of codimension one in
\mathcal{B}
are isomorphic with some Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
. Write
w_{0}
for the longest element in the Weyl group
W=S_{n}
of
G
. For
1\leq i\leq n-1
, let
s_{i}
denote the simple reflection
(i,i+1)\in S_{n}
. Given
w\in S_{n}
, the Schubert variety
X_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
has codimension one if and only if
w=s_{i}w_{0}
for some
i\in[n-1]
. It follows from work of Tymoczko in
[
Tym06a
]
that both
X_{s_{1}w_{0}}
and
X_{s_{n-1}w_{0}}
are Hessenberg varieties in
\mathcal{B}
. We showed in
[
EPS21
]
that if
3\leq i\leq n-3
then no Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
is isomorphic with
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
. (Both Tymoczko’s result and ours do not involve the assumption
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq H
.) In Section
7
, we obtain the following extension of these results.

Corollary 7
.

If
i\in\{2,n-2\}
then no Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
is isomorphic with either of
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
. Therefore, for
j\in[n-1]
, the three conditions

•

some Hessenberg variety in
\mathcal{B}
is isomorphic with
X_{s_{j}w_{0}}
;

•

X_{s_{j}w_{0}}
is a Hessenberg variety; and

•

j\in\{1,n-1\}

are equivalent.

One can compute
\mathsf{Poin}(X_{s_{i}w_{0}};q)
directly and use Theorem
3
to show that, for
i\in\{2,n-2\}
,
\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}));q)=\mathsf{Poin}(X_{s_{i}w_{0}};q)
if and only if
\mathsf{x}
is nilpotent of rank two. For such
\mathsf{x}
we compute
|\mathcal{B}_{p}(\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{\prime},\bar{H}((1,n-1,\ldots,n-1,n)))|
and so determine the Poincaré polynomial of the singular locus
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H(1,n-1,\ldots,n-1,n))
of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},{\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}})
using Theorems
1
and
5
. A beautiful result conjectured by Lakshmibai and Sandhya in
[
LS90
]
and proved, independently, by Manivel in
[
Man01
]
, by Kassel-Lascoux-Reutenauer in
[
KLR03
]
, and by Billey-Warrington in
[
BW03
]
allows us to determine the singular loci of the two
X_{s_{i}w_{0}}
in question. We complete the proof of Corollary
7
by comparing the Poincaré polynomials of these singular loci with those of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H(1,n-1,\ldots,n-1,n))
for
\mathsf{x}
nilpotent of rank two.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

We review here various known results about the general linear group, flag varieties, and
Hessenberg varieties. A reader familiar with basic facts about these objects can skip most of this section
and refer back when necessary, but may wish to consult Remark
2.3
which sets notation for the remainder.

2.1.
Basic notation

The symbol
{\mathbb{N}}
will denote the set of positive integers. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
S_{n}
denote the symmetric group on
[n]
. For an integer partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
,
|\lambda|
will denote the value
\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{j}
.

2.2.
The flag variety

Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbb{F}}
be a field. We use two different models for the
flag variety

\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(n,{\mathbb{F}})
. First,
\mathcal{B}
consists of all full flags

V_{\bullet}=\{0=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\ldots\subset V_{n}={\mathbb{F}}^{n}\mid\dim_{{\mathbb{F}}}V_{i}=i\textup{ for all }i\}.

Having fixed a basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
for
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
, we define the flag
E_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{B}
by

E_{j}={\mathbb{F}}\{e_{i}\mid i\leq j\}

for each
j\in[n]
. We use the basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
to coordinatize
GL_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
. So,
a=(a_{ij})\in GL_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
maps
e_{j}
to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ij}e_{i}
.

The natural action on
G_{\mathbb{F}}:=GL_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
on
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
determines a transitive action of
G_{\mathbb{F}}
on the set
\mathcal{B}
of full flags. The stabilizer of
E_{\bullet}
in this action is the Borel subgroup
B_{\mathbb{F}}
of upper triangular matrices in
G_{\mathbb{F}}
. So, the map from the coset space
G_{\mathbb{F}}/B_{\mathbb{F}}
to
\mathcal{B}
sending
gB_{\mathbb{F}}
to
g(E_{\bullet})
is a bijection. We make no distinction between
\mathcal{B}
and
G_{\mathbb{F}}/B_{\mathbb{F}}
, other than referring to the set of flags as the
flag model
for
\mathcal{B}
and
G_{\mathbb{F}}/B_{\mathbb{F}}
as the
coset model
. We use whichever model suits our purposes at any time.

2.3.
The symmetric group and Bruhat order

We denote elements of
S_{n}
using one-line notation,

w=[w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}]\in S_{n}

where
w_{i}=w(i)
in the natural action of
w
on
[n]
. For
i\in[n-1]
, we write
s_{i}
for the
simple reflection
exchanging
i
and
i+1
and fixing all
j\in[n]\setminus\{i,i+1\}
. Each
w\in S_{n}
is a product of simple reflections,

w=s_{i_{1}}\ldots s_{i_{\ell}},

with each
i_{j}\in[n-1]
. Given such a product with
\ell
as small as possible, we say that
w
has
length

\ell
and write
\ell(w)=\ell
.

An
inversion
of
w
is a pair
(i,j)\in[n]\times[n]
such that
i<j
and
w_{i}>w_{j}
. We write
\mathsf{I}\mathsf{n}\mathsf{v}(w)
for the set of inversions of
w
and
\mathsf{i}\mathsf{n}\mathsf{v}(w)
for
|\mathsf{I}\mathsf{n}\mathsf{v}(w)|
. It is well known that
\ell(w)=\mathsf{i}\mathsf{n}\mathsf{v}(w)
.

An expression of
w\in S_{n}
as a product of
\ell(w)
simple reflections is a
reduced word
for
w
. The
Bruhat order

\leq_{\mathsf{br}}
on
S_{n}
is the partial order in which
v\leq_{\mathsf{br}}w
if some reduced word for
v
is a (not necessarily consecutive) subword of some reduced word for
w
.

The
tableau criterion
gives another characterization of the Bruhat order. Given
w\in S_{n}
and
1\leq p\leq q\leq n
, we write
I_{p,q}(w)
for the
p^{th}
smallest element of
\{w_{i}\mid i\in[q]\}
. So, for example, if
w=[5,2,3,4,1]\in S_{5}
, then
I_{2,4}(w)=3
, as
3
is the second smallest element of
\{5,2,3,4\}
. The following result of Ehresmann appears as
[
BB05
, Theorem 2.6.3]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Tableau Criterion)
.

Let
v,w\in S_{n}
. Then
v\leq_{\mathsf{br}}w
if and only if
I_{p,q}(v)\leq I_{p,q}(w)
whenever
1\leq p\leq q\leq n
.

2.4.
Schubert cells and Schubert varieties

Given
w\in S_{n}
, we write
\dot{w}
for the element of
G_{\mathbb{F}}
mapping
e_{j}
to
e_{w_{j}}
for each
j\in[n]
. Now the
Bruhat decomposition
states that
G_{\mathbb{F}}
is the disjoint union of
B_{\mathbb{F}}-B_{\mathbb{F}}
double cosets, each containing exactly one
\dot{w}
,

G_{\mathbb{F}}=\bigsqcup_{w\in S_{n}}B_{\mathbb{F}}\dot{w}B_{\mathbb{F}}.

We obtain a corresponding decomposition of the flag variety

(2.1)

G_{\mathbb{F}}/B_{\mathbb{F}}=\bigsqcup_{w\in S_{n}}(B_{\mathbb{F}}\dot{w}B_{\mathbb{F}})/B_{\mathbb{F}}.

The
Schubert cell
associated to
w\in S_{n}
is

C_{w}:=(B_{\mathbb{F}}\dot{w}B_{\mathbb{F}})/B_{\mathbb{F}}.

The set
C_{w}
is isomorphic with affine space
{\mathbb{F}}^{\ell(w)}
. The
Schubert variety
associated to
w
is

X_{w}:=\bigcup_{v\leq_{\mathsf{br}}w}C_{v}.

If
{\mathbb{F}}
is algebraically closed then
X_{w}
is the Zariski closure of
C_{w}
in
\mathcal{B}
.

2.5.
Hessenberg varieties in the flag and coset models

Having fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{F}}
and
{\mathfrak{b}}_{\mathbb{F}}
, respectively, for the
{\mathbb{F}}
-Lie algebras of all
n\times n
matrices and all
n\times n
upper triangular matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}
. These are the respective Lie algebras of
G_{\mathbb{F}}
and
B_{\mathbb{F}}
.

The
adjoint representation
is the action of
G_{\mathbb{F}}
on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{F}}
by conjugation.
A
Hessenberg space
is a subspace of
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{F}}
that is
B_{\mathbb{F}}
-invariant under this action.
Given
\mathsf{x}\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{F}}
and Hessenberg space
H\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathbb{F}}
, the associated
Hessenberg variety
is

\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H):=\{gB_{\mathbb{F}}\in G_{\mathbb{F}}/B_{\mathbb{F}}\mid g^{-1}\mathsf{x}g\in H\}.

We assume throughout that
{\mathfrak{b}}_{\mathbb{F}}\subseteq H
. As explained in the Introduction, each such Hessenberg space is of the form
H=H({\mathbf{m}})
for some Hessenberg vector
{\mathbf{m}}=({\mathbf{m}}(1),\ldots,{\mathbf{m}}(n))
. It is straightforward to see that in the flag model,

\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}))=\{V_{\bullet}\mid\mathsf{x}V_{i}\subseteq V_{{\mathbf{m}}(i)}\mbox{ for all }i\in[n]\}.

The flag and coset models of
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}))
define the same scheme-theoretic structure on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}))
by
[
ITW20
, Theorem 10]
.

2.6.
Conjugation of Lie algebra elements and translation of Hessenberg varieties

We will use without comment that fact that if
g\in G_{\mathbb{F}}
,
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
, and
H\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}
is a Hessenberg space, then

\mathcal{B}(g^{-1}\mathsf{x}g,H)=g\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H).

In particular,
\mathcal{B}(g^{-1}\mathsf{x}g,H)
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H)
are isomorphic varieties.

2.7.
The number of points in certain finite varieties

We recall some notation and facts that will be useful when
{\mathbb{F}}
is the finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
for some prime power
q
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, write
[n]_{t}
for the polynomial
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}t^{j}
and set

[n]_{t}!:=\prod_{j=1}^{n}[j]_{t}.

It is straightforward to compute that for every prime power
q
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})|=[n]_{q}

and

(2.3)

|G_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}/B_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}|=[n]_{q}!.

A consequence of the Bruhat decomposition (
2.1
) is that

(2.4)

\mathsf{Poin}(G_{{\mathbb{C}}}/B_{{\mathbb{C}}};q^{1/2})=\sum_{w\in S_{n}}q^{\ell(w)}=[n]_{q}!=|G_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}/B_{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}|

where the second equality is well known, see e.g.
[
Sta12
, Corollary 1.3.13]
.
Thus, Theorem
1
can be seen as a generalization of this result.

2.8.
A remark about nilpotent linear transformations

We require the following straightforward result in the proofs of Theorem
5
and Proposition
7.5
below. Here
{\mathbb{F}}
is an arbitrary field.

Remark 2.2
.

If
\mathsf{x}\in M_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
is nilpotent and
V_{n-1}\subseteq{\mathbb{F}}^{n}
is an
\mathsf{x}
-invariant
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace, then
V_{n-1}
contains
\Ima(\mathsf{x})
.

Proof.

Since the hyperplane
V_{n-1}
is
\mathsf{x}
-stable and
\mathsf{x}
is nilpotent, it is also nilpotent as a linear transformation on
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}/V_{n-1}\simeq{\mathbb{F}}
. Thus it acts as the zero matrix on the quotient, and we conclude
\Ima(\mathsf{x})\subseteq V_{n-1}
.
∎

2.9.
The general linear vs. special linear group over
{\mathbb{C}}

Our main results are all stated and proved under the assumption that
G_{\mathbb{C}}=GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. They hold also if we assume
G_{\mathbb{C}}=SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.
Recall that
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
has Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
of
n\times n
matrices with trace
0
. As
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
acts transitively on the set
\{V_{\bullet}\}
of all full flags in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, there is an isomorphism between the flag varieties
GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
and
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))
, where
B
is the group of nonsingular upper triangular complex matrices. Let
{\mathfrak{s}}
be the set of scalar matrices in
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and let
\mathfrak{b}
be the Lie algebra of
B
. The map sending
H
to
H\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is a bijection from the set of
B
-invariant subspaces of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
containing
\mathfrak{b}
to the set of
B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-invariant subspaces of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
containing
\mathfrak{b}\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. Indeed, the inverse map sends
H^{\prime}
to
H^{\prime}+{\mathfrak{s}}
.

One can define Hessenberg varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))
as they were defined in
GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
. Given
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, there is some scalar
\lambda
such that
\mathsf{x}-\lambda I_{n}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
. If
H\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is a Hessenberg space containing
\mathfrak{b}
, then

\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H)=\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x}-\lambda I_{n},H)\cong\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x}-\lambda I_{n},H\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))\subseteq SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})).

Conversely, if
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
and
H^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
is a
B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
-invariant subspace containing
\mathfrak{b}\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})
, then

\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H^{\prime})\cong\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H^{\prime}+{\mathfrak{s}})\subseteq GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B.

The upshot of all of this is that if one considers only Hessenberg spaces containing a Borel subalgebra, then there is a bijection between the set of Hessenberg varieties in
GL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/B
and the set of Hessenberg varieties in
SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}})/(B\cap SL_{n}({\mathbb{C}}))
under which corresponding varieties are isomorphic. A similar statement holds for any reductive algebraic group with irreducible root system of type
A_{n-1}
. Matters are more complicated if one does not assume that Hessenberg spaces contain Borel subalgebras but this will not arise in our work below.

We conclude by choosing shorthand notation for the relevant groups for use in Sections 3-8.

Remark 2.3
.

For the remainder of the paper we use the notation
G
for
G_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\overline{G}
for
G_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
and similarly for their subgroups and respective Lie algebras. In addition,
\mathcal{B}=G_{\mathbb{C}}/B_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{p}:=G_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}/B_{{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}
throughout. In Section
3
below we write
C_{w}
for the Schubert cell
B\dot{w}B/B
in
\mathcal{B}
and
C_{w,p}
for the Schubert cell
\bar{B}\dot{w}\bar{B}/\bar{B}
in
\mathcal{B}_{p}
.

A close examination of Tymoczko’s paper

Given a matrix

Definition 3.1

Suppose

\semi

(2)

We describe how to fix a representative of each conjugacy class of

, as in

\nilp

starting at the bottom of the leftmost column, incrementing by one while moving up, then moving to the lowest box of the next column and so on. The

and

are pivots, and the highest form is an upper triangular representative for the conjugacy class in which the set of columns containing pivots is right justified. That is, if a pivot appears in column

, then a pivot in appears column

.

If
\mathsf{x}=\semi+\nilp\in\mathfrak{g}

. Replacing each

. We say that this representative is in

(abbreviated below using the acronym

We are ready to state and prove a slightly stronger version of Theorem
1
after recalling some notation and introducing some additional objects. Given an integer matrix

for the element of

modulo

for the subgroup of

for the flag variety
\bar{G}/\bar{B}

{\mathbf{m}}
determines a Hessenberg subspace
\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})
of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{F}}_{p})

\bar{\mathfrak{b}}
of
\bar{B}
, and the Hessenberg variety

.

Let
U\leq B
denote the unipotent subgroup of upper triangular matrices with

’s on the diagonal. Set

, the unipotent subgroup of lower triangular matrices with

’s on the diagonal.
Write
\bar{U}
and
\bar{U}_{-}
for the analogous subgroups of

. For each

(3.2)

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{w}\to C_{w,p}=\bar{B}\dot{w}\bar{B}/\bar{B},\;u\mapsto u\dot{w}\bar{B}.

The follow lemma gives us a finite field version of

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
g\in\bar{U}
and
{\mathbf{m}}
be a Hessenberg vector. Suppose
\mathsf{x}=\semi+\nilp\in\mathfrak{g}
has integer entries and is in HFPJF and

satisfies
\nilp\in\dot{w}H({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}
. For each

, set

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left|\left\{(i<k)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})\mid\begin{array}[]{c}\textup{$\semi_{ii}=\semi_{kk}$ and if $\nilp_{kj}$ is a pivot of }\\
\textup{$\nilp_{\semi_{ii}}$, then $w^{-1}(i)\leq{\mathbf{m}}(w^{-1}(j))$}\end{array}\right\}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+|\{(i<k)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})\mid\semi_{ii}\neq\semi_{kk}\textup{ and }{\mathbf{m}}(w^{-1}(k))\geq w^{-1}(i)\}|.

If
p
is a prime such that no two distinct entries of
\mathsf{x}
have difference divisible by
p
, then for each
i
the set

(3.4)

\displaystyle\{u\in\bar{U}_{i}\cap\bar{U}_{w}\mid(u^{-1}g^{-1}\bar{\mathsf{x}}gu)_{ij}=0\;\textup{ for all }j>i,\;{\mathbf{m}}(w^{-1}(j))<w^{-1}(i)\}

has cardinality

.

Proof.

Our assumptions on the prime

guarantee that
\semi_{ii}\neq\semi_{jj}
if and only if
\bar{\semi}_{ii}\neq\bar{\semi}_{jj}
. Since
\nilp
is a

\nilp
and
\bar{\nilp}
have the same pivots, and in particular, that
\nilp_{\semi_{ii}}
and
\bar{\nilp}_{\semi_{ii}}
have pivots that occur in the rightmost columns.
Since

,
g
is upper triangular with
1
’s on the diagonal. We have

g^{-1}\bar{\mathsf{x}}g=g^{-1}(\bar{\semi}+\bar{\nilp})g=\bar{\semi}+\nilp^{\prime}

is a nilpotent matrix.
Since
\bar{\semi}
is diagonal,
(g^{-1}\bar{\semi}g-\bar{\semi})_{ij}=0
whenever
\semi_{ii}=\semi_{jj}
. In particular, we get that nilpotent matrices
\nilp^{\prime}_{\semi_{ii}}
and
\bar{\nilp}_{\semi_{ii}}
and
\nilp_{\semi_{ii}}
have pivots in exactly the same entries (cf.
[
Tym06b

By definition,

\bar{U}_{i}\cap\bar{U}_{w}=\left\{I_{n}+\sum_{(i\neq\ell)}a_{i\ell}E_{i\ell}\mid a_{i,\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{p},\,a_{i,\ell}=0\,\textup{ unless }\,(i<\ell)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})\right\}.

\displaystyle(u^{-1}g^{-1}\bar{\mathsf{x}}gu)_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u^{-1}\bar{\semi}u)_{ij}+(u^{-1}\nilp^{\prime}u)_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\bar{\semi}_{ii}-\bar{\semi}_{jj})a_{ij}+\nilp_{ij}^{\prime}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i<\ell)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})\\
\ell<j\end{subarray}}a_{i\ell}\nilp_{\ell j}^{\prime}.

If
{\mathbf{m}}(w^{-1}(j))<w^{-1}(i)

(i<j)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})
and using the formula above we see that each of the linear equations defining the set (

(3.5)

\displaystyle 0=(\bar{\semi}_{ii}-\bar{\semi}_{jj})a_{ij}+\nilp_{ij}^{\prime}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i<\ell)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})\\

\left|\left\{(i<k)\in\mathsf{Inv}(w^{-1})\mid\begin{array}[]{c}\textup{$\semi_{ii}=\semi_{kk}$ and if $\nilp_{kj}$ is a pivot of }\\
\textup{$\nilp_{\semi_{ii}}$, then $w^{-1}(i)\leq{\mathbf{m}}(w^{-1}(j))$}\end{array}\right\}\right|.

The proof of the lemma is complete.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose
\mathsf{x}=\semi+\nilp\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}({\mathbb{C}})

{\mathbf{m}}
be a Hessenberg vector. If

is a prime such that no two distinct entries of

, then the cardinality of the Hessenberg variety

Proof.

Let
w\in S_{n}
. Since
\nilp
is a

\bar{\nilp}_{ij}\neq 0
if and only if
\nilp_{ij}\neq 0

\nilp\in\dot{w}H({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}
if and only if
\bar{\nilp}\in\dot{w}\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}
. Furthermore, for every

\mathsf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\{E_{ij}\mid\textup{there exists }(k<\ell)\textup{ such that }i<k,j>\ell,\;\nilp_{k\ell}\neq 0\}

\bar{\nilp}\in\dot{w}\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}
since
\bar{\nilp}
and

have disjoint support in

. This proves that

\displaystyle{C}_{w,p}\cap\mathcal{B}_{p}(\bar{\mathsf{x}},\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}}))\neq\emptyset\Leftrightarrow\bar{\nilp}\in\dot{w}\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}\Leftrightarrow\nilp\in\dot{w}H({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}.

To complete the proof, we argue that
|C_{w,p}\cap\mathcal{B}_{p}(\bar{\mathsf{x}},\bar{H}({\mathbf{m}}))|=p^{d_{w,\mathsf{x}}^{{\mathbf{m}}}}
whenever
\nilp\in\dot{w}H({\mathbf{m}})\dot{w}^{-1}
.

We adapt Tymocko’s proof of Theorem

Z_{i}:=\{u\in(\bar{U}_{n}\bar{U}_{n-1}\ldots\bar{U}_{i})\cap\bar{U}_{w}\mid(u^{-1}\bar{\mathsf{x}}u)_{jk}=0\,\mbox{ for all }\,k>j\geq i,\;{\mathbf{m}}(w^{-1}(k))<w^{-1}(j)\}.

\displaystyle u=gu^{\prime}\,\mbox{ with }\,u^{\prime}\in\bar{U}_{i}\cap\bar{U}_{w}\,\mbox{ and }g\in(\bar{U}_{n}\bar{U}_{n-1}\dots\bar{U}_{i+1})\cap\bar{U}_{w}.

Furthermore, since conjugation by an element in

affects only the first

then
g\in Z_{i+1}
. Thus the factorization from (

.

(c)

if

is obtained from

Proposition 7.3
.

Define
v(2)
and
v(n-2)
in
S_{n}
by

v(2)_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n+1-i&1\leq i\leq n-4\\
2&i=n-3\\
1&i=n-2\\
4&i=n-1\\
3&i=n\end{array}\right.

and

v(n-2)_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n-2&i=1\\
n-3&i=2\\
n&i=1\\
n-1&i=4\\
n+1-i&5\leq i\leq n.\end{array}\right.

Then

(1)

the singular locus of
X_{s_{2}w_{0}}
is
X_{v(2)}
, and

(2)

the singular locus of
X_{s_{n-2}w_{0}}
is
X_{v(n-2)}
.

Proof.

We begin with (1). Let
w=s_{2}w_{0}
and write

w=[n,n-1,\ldots,5,4,2,3,1]

and

v(2)=[n,n-1,\ldots,5,2,1,4,3]

in one-line notation. We show first that
v(2)\in Z_{w}
. If we set
(i,j,k,\ell)=(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})=(n-3,n-2,n-1,n)
we see that Lemma
7.2
(2) can be applied, with
v^{\prime}=v=v(2)
and
w^{\prime}=w
.

Now assume
v\in Z_{w}
. We observe that
v,w
must be as in Lemma
7.2
(2), with
(j,k,\ell)=(n-2,n-1,n)
. It follows that

(v^{\prime}_{n-2},v^{\prime}_{n-1},v^{\prime}_{n})=(1,n+1-i,3)

for some
i\in[n-3]
. By the tableau criterion,
v^{\prime}\leq v
implies
1\in\{v_{n-2},v_{n-1},v_{n}\}
. As
v_{j^{\prime}}=w_{\ell}=1
, we see that
j^{\prime}\in\{n-2,n-1,n\}
. It follows that
(j^{\prime},k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})=(n-2,n-1,n)
. Therefore,

(v_{n-2},v_{n-1},v_{n})=(w_{\ell},w_{i},w_{k})=(1,n+1-i,3),

for some
i\in[n-3]
.
Applying the tableau criterion again, we get that
v\leq v(2)
.
Indeed,
I_{p,q}(v)\leq I_{p,q}(w_{0})=I_{p,q}(v(2))
for
(p,q)\notin\{(1,n-3),(1,n-2),(2,n-2),(1,n-1),(2,n-1)\}
and the remaining cases follow from our analysis.
So,
Z_{w,\mathrm{max}}=\{v(2)\}
and (1) follows from Lemma
7.2
.

The proof of (2) is similar: If we set
w=s_{n-2}w_{0}
we conclude that
v(n-2)\in Z_{w}
by applying Lemma
7.2
(2) with
(i,j,k,\ell)=(1,2,3,4)
. If
v\in Z_{w}
, then
v,w
must be as in Lemma
7.2
(2) with
(i,j,k)=(1,2,3)
. Now
(v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2},v^{\prime}_{3})=(n-2,n+1-\ell,n)
for some
4\leq\ell\leq n
. By the tableau criterion,
n\in\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
, hence
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime})=(1,2,3)
and
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})=(n-2,n+1-\ell,n)
. It follows that
v\leq v(n-2)
, and this implies (2).
∎

Corollary 7.4
.

If
i\in\{2,n-2\}
and
v(i)
is as in Proposition
7.3
then the Euler characteristic of
X_{v(i)}
satisfies

\chi(X_{v(i)})=(n-2)!(n^{2}-5n+6).

Proof.

We obtain the Euler characteristic by counting the number of
u\leq v(i)
in the Bruhat order. Fix
u=[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]\in S_{n}
. Using the tableau criterion, we see that
u\not\leq v(2)
if and only if
\{u_{n-1},u_{n}\}\cap\{1,2\}\neq\emptyset
and
u\not\leq v(n-2)
if and only if
\{u_{1},u_{2}\}\cap\{n-1,n\}\neq\emptyset
. The Corollary follows from a straightforward inclusion-exclusion argument.
∎

Proposition 7.5
.

Assume
\mathsf{x}\in\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent of rank two and consider the Hessenberg vector
{\mathbf{m}}=(1,n-1,\ldots,n-1,n)
.

(1)

If
\mathsf{x}^{2}=0
, then
\chi(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}})))=(n-2)!(n^{2}-5n+8)
.

(2)

If
\mathsf{x}^{2}\neq 0
, then
\chi(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}})))=(n-2)!(n^{2}-5n+7)
.

Proof.

We use Theorem
1
, assuming without loss of generality that
\mathsf{x}
is in highest form and permuted Jordan form. So, we fix any prime
p>2
and count flags
0=V_{0}\subset V_{1}\subset\ldots\subset V_{n}={\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n}
satisfying

(a)

V_{1}\subseteq\ker(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
and

(b)

\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})\subseteq V_{n-1}
.

Note that Remark
2.2
allows us to replace
\bar{\mathsf{x}}V_{n-1}\subseteq V_{n-1}
, implied by our choice of
{\mathbf{m}}
, with (b) as stated.

We observe that
\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{2}=0
if and only if
\mathsf{x}^{2}=0
and consider first the case
\mathsf{x}^{2}=0
. We fix
1
-dimensional
Z\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n}
and count flags satisfying (a) and (b) in which
V_{1}=Z
. If
Z\subseteq\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
then we can choose
V_{n-1}
to be any of the
[n-2]_{p}
hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n}
containing the
2
-dimensional subspace
\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
and then choose
V_{2},\ldots,V_{n-2}
in any of
[n-2]_{p}!
ways. Since
\dim\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})=2
there are
[2]_{p}
possibilities for
Z
and it follows that there are

[2]_{p}[n-2]_{p}[n-2]_{p}!

flags satisfying (a) and (b) in which
V_{1}\subset\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
.

Now consider the case of
1
-dimensional
Z\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{n}
such that
Z\subseteq\ker(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
and
Z\not\subseteq\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
. Since
\bar{\mathsf{x}}^{2}=0
, we know that
\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})\subseteq\ker(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
. Therefore, there are
[n-2]_{p}-[2]_{p}
possibilities for
Z
. Having fixed one such
Z
, we can choose
V_{n-1}
containing
Z+\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
in
[n-3]_{p}
ways, and then choose
V_{2},\ldots,V_{n-2}
in
[n-2]_{p}!
ways. We see there are

\left([n-2]_{p}-[2]_{p}\right)[n-3]_{p}[n-2]_{p}!

flags satisfying (a) and (b) in which

. Direct computation, along with Theorem
1
, gives

\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},{\mathbf{m}});q)=[n-2]_{q^{2}}!\left([n-2]_{q^{2}}[n-3]_{q^{2}}+(1+q^{2})q^{2n-6}\right),

and (1) follows.

The case
\mathsf{x}^{2}\neq 0
is handled similarly. We observe first that in this case

\dim(\ker(\bar{\mathsf{x}})\cap\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}}))=1.

Arguing as we did above, consider first the case in which
V_{1}=\ker(\bar{\mathsf{x}})\cap\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
. Then we calculate that there are

[n-2]_{p}[n-2]_{p}!

flags satisfying (a), (b), and
V_{1}=\ker(\bar{\mathsf{x}})\cap\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
.
On the other hand, if
V_{1}\not\subseteq\Ima(\bar{\mathsf{x}})
we get

p[n-3]_{p}[n-3]_{p}[n-2]_{p}!

flags satisfying (a) and (b). As above, Theorem
1
now yields

\mathsf{Poin}(\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},{\mathbf{m}});q)=[n-2]_{q^{2}}!\left([n-2]_{q^{2}}+q^{2}[n-3]_{q^{2}}^{2}\right)

and (2) follows.
∎

Proposition 8.1
.

Assume
n\geq 3
.
If
gB\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{x},H({\mathbf{m}}_{\bf{max}}))
, then
\det(A_{g})
generates a radical ideal in

We will need two preliminary lemmas to prove Proposition
8.1
. Recall that, given a set
S
of variables, a
term order
on
S
is a total ordering
\preceq
of the set
\mathcal{M}(S)
of monomials in the elements of
S
satisfying, for all
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(S)
and all
s\in S
, the conditions

•

\mu_{1}\prec\mu_{1}s
, and

•

\mu_{1}s\preceq\mu_{2}s
whenever
\mu_{1}\preceq\mu_{2}
.

We observe that
1
is the minimum element of
\mathcal{M}(S)
with respect to any term order.

Given a polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[S]
, the
initial term

{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(f)
of
f
is the monomial appearing with nonzero coefficient in
f
that is maximal with respect to
\preceq
. If
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
is an ideal in
{\mathbb{C}}[S]
, the
initial ideal

{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(I)
is
({\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(f_{1}),\ldots,{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(f_{k}))
.

The next result is well known, see e.g.
[
HH11
, Proposition 3.3.7]
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
I
be an ideal in
{\mathbb{C}}[S]
. If
{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(I)
is square-free with respect to some term order on
\mathcal{M}(S)
, then
I
is radical.

Given a total ordering
<_{S}
of the variables in
S
, the associated
lexicographic term order
on
\mathcal{M}(S)
is obtained by setting
\mu_{1}\preceq\mu_{2}
if and only if, when the variables appearing in
\mu_{1}=s_{1}\ldots s_{j}
and
\mu_{2}=t_{1}\ldots t_{k}
are written in weakly decreasing order, either
j\leq k
and
s_{i}=t_{i}
for all
i\in[j]
or there is some
r
such that
s_{i}=t_{i}
for all
i<r
and
s_{r}<_{S}t_{r}
.

The following lemma is the key technical result of this section.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
m\geq n\geq 3
,
{\mathbf{z}}=\{z_{ji}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\}\sqcup\{z_{j1}\mid n+1\leq j\leq m\}
, and
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}[z_{21},\ldots,z_{m1}]
be linear. Let
u\in M_{n,n}({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbf{z}}])
be as in (
5.1
) and define

(8.1)

\displaystyle L:=[\ell|u_{1}|\ldots|u_{n-1}]\in M_{n,n}({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbf{z}}])\,\textup{ where }\,{\ell}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}e_{i}\in M_{n,1}({\mathbb{C}}[z_{21},\ldots,z_{m1}]).

Then

(1)

\det(L)=0
if and only if
\ell=cu_{1}
for some
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

(2)

Moreover, there exists a lexicographic term order
\preceq
on
\mathcal{M}({\mathbf{z}})
such that
{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(\det(L))
is square-free.

Proof.

The backward direction of (1) is obvious. To prove the forward direction and (2), we proceed by induction on
n
.

Suppose
n=3
. Now

(8.2)

\displaystyle\det(L)=\det\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{1}&1&0\\
\ell_{2}&z_{21}&1\\
\ell_{3}&z_{31}&z_{32}\end{bmatrix}=(z_{32}z_{21}-z_{31})\ell_{1}-z_{32}\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}.

Write
\ell_{i}=a_{i1}+\sum_{j=2}^{m}a_{ij}z_{j1}
for
1\leq i\leq 3
. Substituting these expressions into (
8.2
) and collecting like terms we obtain the following formula for
\det(L)
:

(8.3)

\displaystyle a_{12}z_{32}z_{21}^{2}+a_{13}z_{32}z_{31}z_{21}+(a_{11}-a_{22})z_{32}z_{21}-a_{12}z_{31}z_{21}-a_{13}z_{31}^{2}-a_{23}z_{32}z_{31}-a_{21}z_{32}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+a_{32}z_{21}+(a_{33}-a_{11})z_{31}+a_{31}+\left(\sum_{j=4}^{m}a_{1j}z_{32}z_{21}z_{j1}-a_{1j}z_{31}z_{j1}-a_{2j}z_{32}z_{j1}+a_{3j}z_{j1}\right).

If (
8.3
) is the zero polynomial, then
a_{ij}=0
whenever
i\neq j
and
a_{11}=a_{22}=a_{33}
. The forward direction of (1) now follows. Notice that (
8.3
) has only two monomial terms containing squares:
z_{32}z_{21}^{2}
with coefficient
a_{12}
and
z_{31}^{2}
with coefficient
a_{13}
. If
a_{13}=0
and
a_{12}=0
then (2) is immediate, as (
8.3
) is then a sum of square-free monomials.
By inspection, if
a_{13}\neq 0
then the lexicographic term order
\preceq
associated to a total ordering of
{\mathbf{z}}
in which
z_{32}
is the maximal element and
z_{31}
is the maximal element of
{\mathbf{z}}\setminus\{z_{32}\}
yields
{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(\det(L))=z_{32}z_{31}z_{21}
. The final case is
a_{13}=0
and
a_{12}\neq 0
. Taking the lexicographic term order
\preceq
associated to a total ordering of
{\mathbf{z}}
in which
z_{21}
is the maximal element and
z_{31}
is the maximal element of
{\mathbf{z}}\setminus\{z_{21}\}
gives
{\text{i}}{\text{n}}_{\preceq}(\det(L))=z_{31}z_{21}
. This completes the proof of the base case.

Assume now that
n>3
. For
i,j\in[n]
write
L(i,j)
for the matrix obtained from
L
by deleting the
i^{th}
row and the
j^{th}
column. Expanding along the last column, we see that

(8.4)

\det(L)=\pm z_{n,n-1}\det(L(n,n))\mp\det(L(n-1,n))

Let
{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}=\{z_{ji}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n-1\}\sqcup\{z_{j1}\mid n\leq j\leq m\}
and note that

.

Suppose first that

. By the induction hypothesis, there exists

such that
\ell_{1}=c
and
\ell_{i}=cz_{i1}
for

. We observe that, in this case,

. If

then

Next suppose

By the induction hypothesis, there exists a lexicographic term order

on

such that
