Shifted and threshold matroids
Ethan Partida

Abstract
. We characterize the class of threshold matroids by the structure of their defining bases. We also give an example of a shifted matroid which is not threshold, answering a question of Deza and Onn. We conclude by exploring consequences of our characterization of threshold matroids: We give a formula for the number of isomorphism classes of threshold matroids on a groundset of size n n . This enumeration shows that almost all shifted matroids are not threshold. We also present a polynomial time algorithm to check if a matroid is threshold and provide alternative and simplified proofs of some of the main results of Deza and Onn.

Threshold matroids are the class of matroids whose bases can be separated from non-bases by a weight function on their ground set. Almost all shifted matroids are

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\text{$\#$ of isomorphism classes of threshold matroids on a ground set of size $n$}}{\text{$\#$ of isomorphism classes of shifted matroids on a ground set of size $n$}}=0.

Background

This paper studies two special kinds of matroids, shifted matroids and threshold matroids. Both of these classes of matroids are most naturally defined using the
bases
of a matroid. We recall the bases definition of a matroid below. Throughout the paper, we will refer to the basic concepts of matroid theory (such as circuits, independent sets and deletion/contraction) without definition. These can be found in any standard reference on matroid theory, such as
[
Oxl92
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A rank
k
matroid
M
on ground set
E
is a non-empty collection of
k
-element subsets of
E
which satisfy the basis-exchange axiom. The basis-exchange axiom says that for every
B_{1},B_{2}\in M
and
b_{1}\in B_{1}\setminus B_{2}
there exists a
b_{2}\in B_{2}\setminus B_{1}
such that
(B_{1}\setminus b_{1})\cup b_{2}\in M
.

2.1.
Shifted matroids

Consider the set
[n]
with its total ordering
1<2<\ldots<n
. Let
W(n)
be the set of weakly increasing words whose alphabet is
[n]
and let
W(k,n)
be the set of weakly increasing words of length
k
whose alphabet is
[n]
. Formally, a weakly increasing word
T\in W(k,n)
is a function
T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[k]\to[n]
which is weakly monotonically increasing, i.e.,
T(1)\leq T(2)\leq\ldots\leq T(k)
. Throughout this paper, we often identify the word
T
with the string
t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}
where
t_{i}=T(i)
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
component-wise partial order

\prec
on
W(k,n)
declares
S\prec T
if
S(i)\leq T(i)
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. Equivalently,
S\prec T
if the string
s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
is smaller than the string
t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
at every index
i
.

The following lemma shows that the component-wise partial order is invariant under permutation of words. That is, if we can rearrange a word
T
so that it is larger than
S
at every index, then
T
is still larger than
S
at every index after we re-sort
T
into a weakly increasing word.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
S,T\in W(k,n)
. Then
S\prec T
if and only if there is a bijection
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[k]\to[k]
such that
S(i)\leq T\circ\pi(i)
for all
i\in[k]
.

Proof.

Suppose
S\prec T
and let
\pi
be the identity permutation. By definition,
S(i)\leq T\circ\pi(i)=T(i)
for all
i\in[k]
and the forward direction follows. We now focus on the backwards direction.

Suppose there exists a permutation
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[k]\to[k]
such that
S(i)\leq T\circ\pi(i)
for all
i\in[k]
. If
\pi(i)<\pi(i+1)
for all
i\in[k]
, then
\pi
is the identity permutation and we are done. Instead, assume there is some index
i_{0}
where
\pi(i_{0})>\pi(i_{0}+1)
. Let
\tilde{\pi}=(i_{0}\,\,i_{0}+1)\pi
be the permutation composing
\pi
with the adjacent transposition
(i_{0}\,\,i_{0}+1)
. We show that
S(i)\leq T\circ\tilde{\pi}(i)
for all
i\in[k]
. Since we composed
\pi
with an adjacent transposition, we only need to check this inequality in two indices,
i_{0}
and
i_{0}+1
. We can check this using the fact that
T
and
S
are weakly monotonically increasing. Observe that

T\circ\tilde{\pi}(i_{0})=T\circ\pi(i_{0}+1)\geq S(i_{0}+1)\geq S(i_{0})

and

T\circ\tilde{\pi}(i_{0}+1)=T\circ\pi(i_{0})\geq T\circ\pi(i_{0}+1)\geq S(i_{0}+1).

Since
S_{n}
is generated by adjacent transpositions, we can repeatedly do this modification until
\tilde{\pi}
is the identity permutation.
∎

In terms of strings of letters, Lemma
2.1
states that
S\prec T
if there is a one to one assignment
\psi
between the letters of
T=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
and the letters of
S=s_{1}\cdots s_{k}
such that
t_{i}\geq\psi(t_{i})

The set of size
k
subsets of
[n]
, denoted
\binom{[n]}{k}
, can be identified with the subset of
W(k,n)
of strictly increasing words. Hence
\binom{[n]}{k}
inherits a partial ordering from the component-wise partial ordering of
W(k,n)
. We also call this ordering of
\binom{[n]}{k}
the component-wise partial order. For the rest of this paper, we will identify
\binom{[n]}{k}
with its image in
W(k,n)
and refer to sets by their associated strictly increasing word. We can use the component-wise partial ordering of
\binom{[n]}{k}
to define shifted matroids.

Definition 2.3
.

A
shifted matroid

M
of rank
k
is one which is isomorphic to a matroid whose bases form an order ideal in
\binom{[n]}{k}
under the component-wise partial order. Explicitly, in terms of bases,
M
is shifted if for all bases
B_{1},B_{2}\in M
and
b_{1}\in B_{1}\setminus B_{2}
,
b_{2}\in B_{2}\setminus B_{1}
either
(B_{1}\setminus b_{1})\cup b_{2}\in M
or
(B_{2}\setminus b_{2})\cup b_{1}\in M
.

We close this subsection with a technical lemma characterizing the circuits of a shifted matroid in terms of its defining basis. This lemma will be crucial in the proof of our characterization of threshold matroids as well as the proof of Theorem
7
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M=\langle T\rangle
be a shifted matroid and
C=c_{0}c_{1}\cdots c_{m}
be a circuit of
M
. Let
T=T_{1}\cdots T_{\ell}
be the block decomposition of
T
. If
C
is a loop, then
c_{0}
is strictly larger than the last element of
T_{\ell}
. Otherwise, there exists some index
1\leq i\leq\ell
such that:

•

|C|=|T_{i}|+|T_{i+1}|+\ldots+|T_{\ell}|+1
.

•

c_{1}\cdots c_{m}\prec T_{i}T_{i+1}\cdots T_{\ell}
.

•

If
i\neq 1
, then
c_{0}
is strictly larger than the last element of
T_{i-1}
.

Proof.

If
C=c_{0}
is a loop, then
c_{0}
is contained in no bases of
M
. This occurs exactly when
c_{0}
is strictly larger than the last element of
T_{\ell}
. From here on out, assume that
C
is not a loop.

For ease of indexing, suppose that
T=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
and
C=c_{0}c_{1}\cdots c_{m}
with
1\leq m\leq k
. A circuit is a dependent set whose facets are all contained in
\mathcal{I}(M)
. Since
M
is shifted, all facets of
C
are contained in
\mathcal{I}(M)
if and only if
c_{1}\cdots c_{m}
is contained in
\mathcal{I}(M)
. This occurs exactly when
c_{1}\cdots c_{m}\prec t_{k-m+1}\cdots t_{k}
. In order for this condition to be met and
C
to be dependent, we must have that
c_{0}>t_{k-m}
or
|C|=k+1
. If
|C|=k+1
, then
|C|=|T_{1}|+\ldots+|T_{\ell}|+1
and
c_{1}\cdots c_{m}\prec t_{1}\cdots t_{m}=T
.
In this case,
C
satisfies our conclusion.

Now assume that
|C|<k+1
so that
c_{0}>t_{k-m}
. We have the inequalities

t_{k-m}<c_{0}<c_{1}\leq t_{k-m+1}.

Hence
t_{k-m}<t_{k-m+1}-1
and
t_{k-m+1}
is the start of some block of
T
. Let
T_{i}
be the block for which
t_{k-m+1}
is the start of. We now have that:

•

|C|=|T_{i}|+|T_{i+1}|+\ldots+|T_{\ell}|+1
.

•

c_{1}\cdots c_{m}\prec t_{k-m+1}\cdots t_{k}=T_{i}T_{i+1}\cdots T_{\ell}
.

•

c_{0}
is strictly larger than
t_{k-m}
which is the last element of
T_{i-1}
.

∎

2.2.
Threshold matroids

A threshold matroid is one whose bases are determined by a weight function, as follows:

Definition 2.5
.

Let
M
be a rank
k
matroid on
E
. A matroid
M
is a
threshold matroid
if there exists a function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
B\in\binom{E}{k}
,

\text{$B$ is a basis of $M$}\iff w(B)=\sum_{b\in B}w(b)>0.

When this is the case, we say
w
is a weight function for
M
and call
w(B)
the
weight
of the basis
B
.

Remark 2.6
.

In recent work, Deza and Onn
[
DO23
]
introduce the notion of separable matroids. Their definition of a separable matroid is exactly the same as that of a threshold matroid except one only requires the weight of bases to be non-negative instead of positive. It can be shown, by perturbing the weight function slightly, that a matroid is threshold if and only if it is separable. We choose to use the name threshold because it coincides with that of threshold graphs and the earlier works of Bhanu Murthy–Srinivasan
[
BS02
]
and Klivans–Reiner
[
KR08
]
.

Remark 2.7
.

Previously, Giles and Kannan
[
GK80
]
defined and characterized a different notion of threshold matroid. For the rest of this remark, we will refer to their definition as
non-uniform threshold
. A matroid
M
is non-uniform threshold if there exists a weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to\mathbb{R}
and a threshold
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that a set
I\subset E
is independent if and only if
w(I)\leq t
. Non-uniform thresholdness is a strictly stronger condition than thresholdness. For example, the shifted matroid
M=\langle 246\rangle
is threshold but not non-uniform threshold.

We close this subsection with two lemmas which show that thresholdness is preserved under duality and adding/contracting coloops.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
DO23
, Lemma
2.3
]
)
.

Thresholdness is preserved by matroid duality. That is,
M
is threshold if and only if
M^{\perp}
is.

Lemma 2.4
.

Thresholdness is preserved by contracting coloops. That is, if
M
is a rank
k
matroid with a coloop
e
then
M
is threshold if and only if
M/e
is threshold.

Proof.

First suppose that
M/e
is threshold with weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\setminus e\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
\alpha=\max(w)+1
and
\tilde{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\tilde{w}(i)=\begin{cases}(k-1)\alpha&\text{if }i=e\\
w(i)-\alpha&\text{if }i\neq e\end{cases}.

We claim that
\tilde{w}
is a weight function for
M
. Since
w(i)-\alpha<0
for any
i\neq e
, a size
k
subset
A\subset E
which does not contain
e
will have
\tilde{w}(A)<0
. Now suppose that
e\in A
. Then

\tilde{w}(A)=(k-1)\alpha+w(A\setminus e)-(k-1)\alpha=w(A\setminus e).

This means that
\tilde{w}(A)
will have positive weight if and only if
A
contains
e
and
A\setminus e
is a basis of
M/e
. In other words,
\tilde{w}(A)
will have positive weight if and only if
A
is a basis of
M
.

Now suppose that
M
is threshold with weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
\tilde{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\setminus e\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
\tilde{w}(i)=w(i)+\frac{w(e)}{k-1}
. Then for any size
k-1
subset
A\subset E\setminus e
,

\tilde{w}(A)=w(A)+w(e)=w(A\cup e).

Since
w
is a threshold function for
M
, this means that
\tilde{w}(A)
is positive if and only if
A\cup e
is a basis of
M
.
∎

3.
Shifted vs Threshold

Although seemingly distinct classes of matroids, it turns out that all threshold matroids are shifted. The weight function of a threshold matroid
M
induces an ordering on the ground set for which
M
is a shifted matroid. The more general version of this statement for
k
-hypergraphs has been observed many times; see
[
Mur71
, Theorem
5.3.1
]
,
[
DO23
, Lemma
2.1
]
and
[
CH11
, Theorem
9.8
]
. Because its converse statement is central to our study, we include a proof of the fact that all threshold matroids are shifted.

Lemma 3.1
.

All threshold matroids are shifted.

Proof.

Suppose
M
is a threshold matroid with weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
<
be a total ordering of
E
such that
x<y
whenever
w(x)>w(y)
. Now consider the order preserving bijection
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to[n]
defined by
<
. We show that the image of
M
under
\psi
forms an order ideal in the component-wise partial order. Suppose
B\subset[n]
is the image of a basis of
M
. We need to check that if
b\in B
,
d\not\in B
and
d<b
then
(B\setminus b)\cup d
is also the image of a basis of
M
. But because
d<b
,
w(\psi^{-1}d)>w(\psi^{-1}b)
so

w(\psi^{-1}((B\setminus b)\cup d))=w(\psi^{-1}B)+w(\psi^{-1}d)-w(\psi^{-1}b)>0

and our claim follows.
∎

Since all threshold matroids are shifted, it is natural to wonder whether the converse might be true. This question was asked for matroids by Deza and Onn.

However, in the below example, we give an example of a shifted matroid which is not threshold. This is a counterexample to Question

and indicates that thresholdness is a much stronger condition than shiftedness, even in the very special case of matroids.

Theorem/Counterexample 3.2

(Counterexample to Question
1
)
.

There exists a shifted matroid which is not threshold.

Proof.

Let
M=\langle 2468\rangle
be the shifted matroid with defining basis
2468\subset[8]
. Suppose that
M
is threshold with weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[8]\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that

2468,1357\in M\text{ and }1278,3456\not\in M

so

w(2468),w(1357)>0\text{ and }w(1278),w(3456)\leq 0.

But both
2468\sqcup 1357
and
1278\sqcup 3456
partition
[8]
so we must have

\hskip 128.0374pt0<w(2468)+w(1357)=w(1278)+w(3456)\leq 0.\hskip 113.81102pt

∎

Counterexample
3.2
inspired the characterizations of threshold matroids given in Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
. After proving Theorem
2
, we will be able to see that

is not threshold because
2468
has four blocks. After proving Theorem
3
, we will be able to see that
M=\langle 2468\rangle
is not threshold because
2468
and

are bases,
1278
and

are non-bases,

and

.

4.
Uniform Asummability

We now introduce our main tool for analyzing which shifted matroids are threshold. We call this tool
uniform asummability
as it is a uniform analog of asummability from the simple games literature
[
TZ99
]
. To introduce uniform asummability, we first equip the set
W(n)
with a binary operation to turn it into a graded commutative monoid.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
A\in W(k,n)
and
B\in W(k^{\prime},n)
. We define the
sorted concatenation
of
A
and
B
, denoted
A+B
, to be the unique word in
W(k+k^{\prime},n)
such that
|(A+B)^{-1}(i)|=|A^{-1}(i)|+|B^{-1}(i)|
for all
i\in[n]
.

We can think of
A+B
as the length
k+k^{\prime}
weakly increasing word given by concatenating
A
and
B
then rearranging their concatenation into weakly increasing order. For example,
234+134=123344
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\ell>1
. A matroid
M
is
\ell
-uniform assumable
if for all
B_{1},\ldots,B_{\ell}\in M
and
D_{1},\ldots,D_{\ell}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus M
we have

B_{1}+\ldots+B_{\ell}\neq D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}.

If
M
is
\ell
-uniform assumable for all
\ell
, then we say
M
is
uniform assumable
.

Uniform asummability is important to the present work because of the following special case of a theorem of Klivans and Reiner.

Theorem 5

(Theorem 3.1 of
[
KR08
]
)
.

A matroid
M
is threshold if and only if it is uniform assumable.

Remark 4.3
.

In Klivans and Reiner’s work,
\ell
-uniform assumability is referred to as the
CC_{\ell}
property. They attribute Theorem
5
to Taylor and Zwicker
[
TZ99
, Theorem
2.4.2
]
. However Taylor and Zwicker’s result is a different, but related, result. Taylor and Zwicker’s result, proved in the language of simple games, specializes to the following statement for matroids: A matroid
M
is
non-uniform threshold
if and only if it is
non-uniform assumable
. See Remark
2.7
for a discussion of non-uniform thresholdness. The definition of non-uniform asummability is identical to uniform asummability except that we no longer require the
B
’s and
D
’s to be sets of size
k
. Non-uniform asummability is a strictly stronger condition than uniform assumability. For example, the shifted matroid
M=\langle 246\rangle
is uniform assumable but is not
2
non-uniform assumable because
135+246=56+1234
.

If one wants to exhibit a matroid that is not threshold, it suffices to present
\ell
many bases
B_{1},\ldots,B_{\ell}
and
\ell
many non-bases
D_{1},\ldots,D_{\ell}
so that

B_{1}+\ldots+B_{\ell}=D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}.

Example 4.1
.

We show that the shifted matroid
M=\langle 2468\rangle
is not
2
-uniform assumable and hence, as seen previously in Example
3.2
, not threshold. Let

B_{1}=2468,B_{2}=1357,D_{1}=1278\text{ and }D_{2}=3456.

Then
B_{1},B_{2}\in M
and
D_{1},D_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus M
but
B_{1}+B_{2}=[8]=D_{1}+D_{2}
. Thus
M
is
not

2
-uniform assumable and is not threshold.

In fact, as part of our proof of Theorem
2
, we show that a matroid is threshold if and only if it is
2
-uniform asummable.

Lemma 4.1
.

A matroid
M
is
2
-uniform assumable if and only if for all bases
B_{1},B_{2}\in M
and non-bases
D_{1},D_{2}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus M
, we have that

B_{1}\cup B_{2}\neq D_{1}\cup D_{2}\text{ or }B_{1}\cap B_{2}\neq D_{1}\cap D_{2}.

Proof.

The sorted concatenations of two pairs
B_{1},B_{2}
and
D_{1},D_{2}
are equal if and only if
B_{1}\cup B_{2}=D_{1}\cup D_{2}
and
B_{1}\cap B_{2}=D_{1}\cap D_{2}
. Thus our second condition is simply a rephrasing of the definition of
2
-uniform asummability.
∎

It is asked in work of Deza and Onn whether the property outlined in Lemma
4.1
characterizes threshold matroids. In the proof of Theorem
2
, we will show that this is the case.

Theorem
3

(Question
1.1
of
[
DO23
]
)
.

A matroid is threshold if and only if it is
2
-uniform assumable. Equivalently, a rank
k
matroid
M
is threshold if and only if for all bases
B_{1},B_{2}
and size
k
non-bases
D_{1},D_{2}

B_{1}\cup B_{2}\neq D_{1}\cup D_{2}\text{ or }B_{1}\cap B_{2}\neq D_{1}\cap D_{2}.

Remark 4.4
.

The relationship between
2
non-uniform assumability and non-uniform thresholdness has been the subject of much study in the theories of simple games and Boolean functions; see
[
TZ99
, Section
2.7
]
and
[
CH11
, Section
9.3
]
. In general, the class of
2
non-uniform assumable hypergraphs strictly contains the class of non-uniform threshold hypergraphs. However, as a consequence of Giles and Kannan’s result
[
GK80
]
,
2
non-uniform assumable matroids are the same as non-uniform threshold matroids.

Theorem
3
shows that a similar story is true in the uniform setting. Although
2
-uniform assumability does not imply thresholdness for hypergraphs
[
DO23
, Section
6
]
, the class of threshold matroids is equal to the class of
2
-uniform asummable matroids.

The goal of the rest of this section is to further refine uniform-asummability for shifted matroids. We first show that we lose nothing by restricting to shifted matroids. That is, every
\ell
-uniform assumable matroid is shifted.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
M
is an
\ell
-uniform assumable matroid for some
\ell>1
. Then
M
is a shifted matroid.

Proof.

If
M
is
\ell
-uniform assumable for some
\ell>1
, then
M
is
2
-uniform assumable. We now proceed by contrapositive to show that
2
-uniform assumable matroids are shifted. Suppose
M
is not shifted. Then there exists bases
B_{1},B_{2}
and elements
b_{1}\in B_{1}\setminus B_{2}
,
b_{2}\in B_{2}\setminus B_{1}
such that
(B_{1}\setminus b_{1})\cup b_{2}\not\in M
and
(B_{2}\setminus b_{2})\cup b_{1}\not\in M
. But

B_{1}+B_{2}=(B_{1}\setminus b_{1})\cup b_{2}+(B_{2}\setminus b_{2})\cup b_{1}

so
M
is not
2
-uniform assumable.
∎

We now analyze the relationship between sorted concatenation and the component-wise partial order.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
A,A^{\prime}\in W(k_{A},n)
,
B,B^{\prime}\in W(k_{B},n)
,
A\prec A^{\prime}
and
B\prec B^{\prime}
. Then
A+B\prec A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
in
W(k_{A}+k_{B},n)
.

Proof.

Let
\iota_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[k_{A}]\hookrightarrow[k_{A}+k_{B}]
and
\iota_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[k_{B}]\hookrightarrow[k_{A}+k_{B}]
be injections with the following properties: the images of
\iota_{A}
and
\iota_{B}
partition
[k_{A}+k_{B}]
,
A=(A+B)\circ\iota_{A}
and
B=(A+B)\circ\iota_{B}
. Define
\iota_{A^{\prime}}
and
\iota_{B^{\prime}}
similarly for
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
. Let
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[k_{A}+k_{B}]\to[k_{A}+k_{B}]
be the bijection which sends
\iota_{A}(i)\mapsto\iota_{A^{\prime}}(i)
and
\iota_{B}(j)\mapsto\iota_{B^{\prime}}(j)
for all
i\in[k_{A}]
and
j\in[k_{B}]
. Then

(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})\circ\pi(\alpha)=\begin{cases}(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})\circ\iota_{A^{\prime}}(i)={A^{\prime}}(i),&\text{if $\alpha=\iota_{A}(i)$ for $i\in[k_{A}]$}\\
(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})\circ\iota_{B^{\prime}}(j)={B^{\prime}}(j),&\text{if $\alpha=\iota_{B}(j)$ for $j\in[k_{B}]$.}\end{cases}

But
{A^{\prime}}(i)\geq A(i)=(A+B)\circ\iota_{A}(i)
and
{B^{\prime}}(j)\geq B(j)=(A+B)\circ\iota_{B}(j)
for all
i\in[k_{A}]
and
j\in[k_{B}]
. Thus
(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})\circ\pi(\alpha)\geq(A+B)(\alpha)
for all
\alpha\in[k_{A}+k_{B}]
. By Lemma
2.1
, this implies that
A+B\prec A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}
.
∎

The converse of Lemma
4.3
is not always true. For example, both
1278
and
3456
are not smaller than
2468
but

1278+3456\leq 2468+2468.

As we will see in Lemma
4.4
, uniform asummability of a shifted matroid
M=\langle T\rangle
measures to what extent the converse of Lemma
4.3
is true for the non-bases of
M
.

If
M
is a shifted matroid, we can use the component-wise partial order to classify uniform asummability. This is the formulation which will be most useful for us. We will use this to classify which shifted matroids are
not
threhsold; see Theorem
2
.

Lemma 4.4
.

A rank
k
, shifted matroid
M=\langle T\rangle
is
\ell
-uniform assumable if and only if for any
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{\ell}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus M
,

D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}\nprec T+\ldots+T

where the right-hand side is
\ell
copies of
T
.

Proof.

We prove both directions by contrapositive. First suppose that
M
is not
\ell
-uniform assumable. Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{\ell}\in M
and
D_{1},\ldots,D_{\ell}\not\in M
be witnesses of this failure so that,

B_{1}+\ldots+B_{\ell}=D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}.

By construction, each
B_{i}\prec T
and Lemma
4.3
ensures that

D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}=B_{1}+\ldots+B_{\ell}\prec T+\ldots+T.

Now for the other direction, assume that
D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{\ell}\in\binom{[n]}{k}\setminus M
but

D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}\prec T+\ldots+T.

Let
T=t_{1}t_{2}\cdots t_{k}
and

T+\ldots+T=t_{1,1}t_{1,2}\cdots t_{1,\ell}t_{2,1}\cdots t_{k,\ell}

where
t_{i,j}=t_{i,j^{\prime}}=t_{i}
for any
i
and any
j,j^{\prime}
. Also let
D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}=b_{1}\cdots b_{k\cdot\ell}
. By our assumption,
b_{\ell(i-1)+j}\leq t_{i,j}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
and
1\leq j\leq\ell
. Now let

B_{j}=\{b_{\ell(i-1)+j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}1\leq i\leq k\}

for all
1\leq j\leq\ell
. Because
t_{i}=t_{i,j}\geq b_{\ell(i-1)+j}
, we have that
B_{j}\prec T
. Further, by definition of the
B_{j}
s,

B_{1}+\ldots+B_{\ell}=D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}.

Now the only thing left to check is that each
B_{j}
is an honest set, i.e., each
B_{j}
does not contain any repeated elements. Suppose that some
B_{j}
has a repeated element. This means that there is some index
1\leq i<k
so that
b_{\ell(i-1)+j}=b_{\ell i+j}
. But because the
b_{i}
s are arranged in weakly increasing order, this implies

b_{\ell(i-1)+j}=b_{\ell(i-1)+j+1}=\ldots=b_{\ell(i-1)+j+\ell}.

Hence
D_{1}+\ldots+D_{\ell}
contains at least
\ell+1
repeated elements. Since each
D_{i}
is a set, this would violate the pigeonhole principle and lead to a contradiction.
∎

5.
Proof of the main theorem

Here we prove our characterization of threshold matroids. This characterization determines whether a shifted matroid is threshold using only the data of its defining basis. We will explore consequences of this characterization in Section
6
.

Theorem
2
.

Suppose
M
is a shifted matroid and let

Then:

(1)

If

is not threshold.

(2)

If

is threshold.

(3)

If

is threshold.

(b)

Otherwise,

is not threshold.

By Lemma

We now prove the last remaining cases of thresholdness. We will prove one part of the case explicitly and then use the fact that thresholdness is closed under duality to prove the other.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
M=\langle T\rangle
be a coloopless shifted matroid of rank
k
. If
T
has three blocks and the second block of

has size one then

is threshold.

Proof.

Suppose the first block of
T
ends at

and has size
\lambda_{1}
, the second block of

consists solely of the element
t_{2}
and the third block of

ends at

and has size

. Let

be defined by

w(j)=\begin{cases}2\lambda_{3}^{3}+6\lambda_{3}^{2}+4\lambda_{3}+1&\text{ if }j\leq t_{1}\\
(1-2\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}^{2}+(1-3\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}&\text{ if }t_{1}<j\leq t_{2}\\
-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}^{2}-(4\lambda_{1}+1)\lambda_{3}-(\lambda_{1}+1)&\text{ if }t_{2}<j\leq t_{3}\\
-\infty&\text{ if }t_{3}<j\end{cases}.

We will prove that

is a weight function for

and hence that

is threshold. To do so, we first show that

is a weakly monotonically decreasing function. This reduces the task of checking that

is non-negative on all bases of

, to just checking that

is non-negative on

. We then use our characterization of circuits, provided by Lemma

, to determine the structure of the non-bases of

. We use this structure and the fact that

is decreasing to verify that

is negative on all non-bases of

.

To see that

is weakly monotonically decreasing, observe that

\left(2\lambda_{3}^{3}+6\lambda_{3}^{2}+4\lambda_{3}+1\right)-\left((1-2\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}^{2}+(1-3\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}\right)=2\lambda_{3}^{3}+(2\lambda_{1}+5)\lambda_{3}^{2}+(3\lambda_{1}+3)\lambda_{3}+1>0

and

\left((1-2\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}^{2}+(1-3\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}\right)-\left(-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}^{2}-(4\lambda_{1}+1)\lambda_{3}-(\lambda_{1}+1)\right)=\lambda_{3}^{2}+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{1}+1>0.

We can now compute

to check that the weight of

is positive:

\displaystyle w(T)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\left(2\lambda_{3}^{3}+6\lambda_{3}^{2}+4\lambda_{3}+1\right)+1\left((1-2\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}^{2}+(1-3\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}\right)+\lambda_{3}\left(-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}^{2}-(4\lambda_{1}+1)\lambda_{3}-(\lambda_{1}+1)\right)

\displaystyle=(2\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}^{3}+(6\lambda_{1}+1-2\lambda_{1}-4\lambda_{1}-1)\lambda_{3}^{2}+(4\lambda_{1}+1-3\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}-1)\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle>0.

By Lemma
2.2
, the non-loop circuits of

with size less than

are of the form

C=c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}\ldots c_{\lambda_{3}}

and

D=d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}\ldots d_{\lambda_{3}+2}

where:

•

and

is smaller than the third block of

.

•

,

is smaller than
t_{2}
and

is smaller than the third block of

.

Since any non-basis must contain a circuit, a non-basis must contain a loop or a circuit of the form

or

. If a non-basis contains a loop then it contains some element larger than

and hence has a negative weight. We now consider non-bases that don’t contain a loop. First suppose that

. Then

many elements greater than

\displaystyle w(A)

\displaystyle\leq(k-\lambda_{3}-1)w(1)+(\lambda_{3}+1)w(t_{2}+1)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\left(2\lambda_{3}^{3}+6\lambda_{3}^{2}+4\lambda_{3}+1\right)+(\lambda_{3}+1)\left(-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}^{2}-(4\lambda_{1}+1)\lambda_{3}-(\lambda_{1}+1)\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{3}^{2}-(\lambda_{1}+2)\lambda_{3}-1

\displaystyle<0.

Similarly, suppose

is a size

\displaystyle w(A^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq(k-\lambda_{3}-2)w(1)+(\lambda_{3}+2)w(t_{1}+1)

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{1}-1)\left(2\lambda_{3}^{3}+6\lambda_{3}^{2}+4\lambda_{3}+1\right)+(\lambda_{3}+2)\left((1-2\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}^{2}+(1-3\lambda_{1})\lambda_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{3}^{3}-(\lambda_{1}+3)\lambda_{3}^{2}-(\lambda_{1}+2)\lambda_{3}-(\lambda_{3}-1)\lambda_{1}-1

We have now shown that

Since thresholdness is preserved under duality, we obtain the last case of the main theorem.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let

be a coloopless shifted matroid of rank

. If

The dual matroid

of

has no loops so that

will be a shifted matroid with three blocks whose second block has size one. By Proposition

Now suppose

has coloops. By Lemma

, we can contract the coloops from

Combining the previous lemma and corollary, we obtain the last part of our main theorem.

6.
Consequences of Theorem
2

We can use our characterization of threshold matroids to give an explicit enumeration of the number of isomorphism classes of threshold matroids on a ground set of size
n
. To do so, we first observe that a subset
T\subset[n]
determines a unique shifted matroid isomorphism class.

Lemma 6.1
.

Each shifted matroid isomorphism class contains a unique shifted matroid
M=\langle T\rangle
whose bases are the elements of the principal order ideal generated by
T\in\binom{[n]}{k}
in the component-wise partial order.

Proof.

By definition, every shifted matroid isomorphism class contains at least one representative
M=\langle T\rangle
. Thus we need to show that, for two different size
k
subsets
T,T^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{k}
,
M=\langle T\rangle
and
M^{\prime}=\langle T^{\prime}\rangle
are not isomorphic. Let
T=T_{1}\cdots T_{\ell}
and
T^{\prime}=T_{1}^{\prime}\cdots T_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\prime}
be the block decompositions of
T
and
T^{\prime}
. Let
i
be the smallest index such that
T_{\ell-i}\neq T^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}-i}
. Then either
|T_{\ell-i}|\neq|T^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}-i}|
or the last element
t
of
T_{\ell-i}
is not equal to the last element
t^{\prime}
of
T^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}-i}
. Assume that either
|T_{\ell-i}|<|T^{\prime}_{\ell^{\prime}-i}|
or
t<t^{\prime}
. In the first case, Lemma
2.2
implies that
M
has a circuit of size
|T_{\ell-i}|+|T_{\ell-i+1}|+\ldots+|T_{\ell}|+1
and
M^{\prime}
does not. Therefore
M\not\simeq M^{\prime}
. In the second case, Lemma
2.2
implies that
M
has
t^{\prime}-t
more circuits of size
|T_{\ell-i}|+\ldots+|T_{\ell}|+1=|T_{\ell^{\prime}-i}^{\prime}|+\ldots+|T_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\prime}|+1
. Therefore
M\not\simeq M^{\prime}
.
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let
F(n)
be the number of isomorphism classes of threshold matroids on a ground set of size
n
. Then

F(n)=\binom{n+1}{2}+\binom{n+1}{4}+\binom{n+1}{6}+\binom{n-1}{6}

where we take
\binom{n}{k}=0
whenever
n<k
.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, an isomorphism class of a threshold matroid
M=\langle T\rangle
is determined by a choice of subset
T\subset[n]
with block structure as determined by Theorem
2
. We break into cases and enumerate all subsets of
[n]
which follow the block structure determined by Theorem
2
. As we allow
M
to have coloops and
T
to start with a
1
, there are quite a few cases to enumerate. A subset
T\subset[n]
determines a threshold matroid if and only if it satisfys one of the following mutually exclusive cases:

(1)

T
has one block.

(2)

T
has two blocks.

(3)

T
has three blocks and

(a)

1\in T
.

(b)

1\not\in T
and the second block of
T
has size one.

(c)

1\not\in T
, the second block of
T
has at least two elements and the second gap of
T
has size one.

(4)

T
has four blocks,
1\in T
and

(a)

the third block of
T
has size one.

(b)

the third block of
T
has at least two elements and the second gap of
T
has size one.

By picking the starts and ends of the blocks, there are
\binom{n+1}{2}
and
\binom{n+1}{4}
subsets of
[n]
with one and two blocks, respectively. Similarly, by choosing the end of the block containing
1
and the starts and ends of the other blocks, there are
\binom{n}{5}
subsets of
[n]
which have three blocks and contain
1
.

We can count the other cases with similar, slightly more intricate, counting arguments. We give such an argument for Case
3b
and leave the others to the reader. Given a subset
T\subset[n]
obeying the conditions of Case
3b
, we construct a five element subset
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4},s_{5}\}\subset\{2,3,\ldots,n+1\}
determining
T
such that
s_{3}+1<s_{4}
. Let
s_{1}
be the start of the first block of
T
,
s_{2}
be the start of the second gap of
T
,
s_{3}
be the second block of
T
,
s_{4}
be the start of the third block of
T
and
s_{5}
be the start of the fourth gap of
T
(where
s_{5}=n+1
if
T
does not have a fourth gap). Because the third gap has size at least one,
s_{3}+1<s_{4}
. This process is reversible as our recorded information completely determines
T
. As an example, the defining basis
T=3578\subset[8]
corresponds to the set
S=34579\subset\{2,3,\ldots,9\}
. As there are
\binom{n-1}{5}
five element subsets of
\{2,3,\ldots,n+1\}
with
s_{3}+1<s_{4}
, there are also
\binom{n-1}{5}
subsets of
[n]
with three blocks, not containing
1
and whose second block has size one.

There are
\binom{n-2}{5}
,
\binom{n-1}{6}
and
\binom{n-2}{6}
subsets of
[n]
satisfying Cases
3c
,
4a
and
4b
, respectively. Therefore

\displaystyle F(n)

\displaystyle=\binom{n+1}{2}+\binom{n+1}{4}+\binom{n}{5}+\binom{n-1}{5}+\binom{n-2}{5}+\binom{n-1}{6}+\binom{n-2}{6}

\displaystyle=\binom{n+1}{2}+\binom{n+1}{4}+\binom{n+1}{6}+\binom{n-1}{6}

∎

With this formula for the number of isomorphism classes of threshold matroids, a proof of Theorem
1
quickly follows.

Theorem
1
.

Almost all shifted matroids are
not
threshold. That is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\text{$\#$ of isomorphism classes of threshold matroids on a groundset of size $n$}}{\text{$\#$ of isomorphism classes of shifted matroids on a groundset of size $n$}}=0.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.1
, the number of isomorphism classes of shifted matroids on a groundset of size
n
is equal to
2^{n}
. While by Theorem
6
, the number of isomorphism classes of shifted matroids on a groundset of size
n
is equal to a sum of binomial coefficients. As binomial coefficients grow polynomially in
n
, our claim follows.
∎

v_{1}<v_{2}
contained in the complement of
T
and which are smaller than at least two elements of
T
. Let
T_{i_{1}}
and
T_{i_{2}}
be the first blocks of
T
after
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, respectively. Using Lemma
2.2
, one can check that

\displaystyle C_{1}=v_{1}T_{i_{1}}T_{i_{1}+1}\cdots T_{\ell}

\displaystyle C_{2}=v_{2}T_{i_{2}}T_{i_{2}+1}\cdots T_{\ell}

are circuits of
M
. Finally, using that
v_{2}
is smaller than at least two elements of
T
and is the largest element of the symmetric difference of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, one can check that the symmetric difference of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
is independent. This computation will prove that
M
is not binary.
∎

Proposition 6.6

(Theorem
5.4
of
[
DO23
]
)
.

Let
M=\langle T\rangle
be a binary shifted matroid. Then

is threshold.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.5
, after contracting all coloops of
M
,
T
can have at most two blocks. By Theorem
2
, this means that
M
is threshold.
∎

We conclude by giving a polynomial time algorithm to check matroid if a matroid

is threshold. Suppose that
M
is a rank
k
matroid on a ground set
E
of size

with
m
bases and

is presented by a list of its bases. In Section

[

time, to determine if

is shifted. They also give an algorithm, which is polynomial in
\binom{n}{k}
, to determine if
M
is threshold. In practice,
\binom{n}{k}
can be much larger than

so it is desirable to find an algorithm, polynomial in

,
m
, and

, to check if

is threshold. The below theorem exhibits such an algorithm.

Theorem 8
.

Suppose
M
is a rank
k
matroid on a ground set
E
of size
n
with
m
bases and
M
is presented by its list of bases. There is an algorithm, which terminates in
O(m^{2}k^{2}+n\log(n))
time, to decide if
M
is a threshold matroid.

If
M
were presented to us as a principal order ideal generated by some
T\in\binom{[n]}{k}
, then, by using Theorem
2
and checking the block structure of
T
, we could easily check if
M
if threshold in
O(k)
time. Thus the main content of Theorem

is the construction of an algorithm to find an isomorphism between
M
, an arbitrary shifted matroid, and
\tilde{M}=\left\langle T\right\rangle
, a principal order in the component-wise partial order of
\binom{[n]}{k}
. We will do so by utilizing a result of Klivans
[

]
which lets us totally order
E
to induce such an isomorphism. To state Klivan’s result, we recall the definition of the
vicinal preorder
.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a rank
k
matroid
M
on a ground set
E
and
i\in E
, the
open and closed neighborhoods
of
i
in
M
are the following two subcollections of
\binom{E}{k-1}
:

\displaystyle N_{M}(i)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{B\setminus i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\text{ $i\in B$ and $B$ a basis of $M$}\right\}

\displaystyle N_{M}[i]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{B\setminus j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\text{ $i\in B$, $j\in B$ and $B$ a basis of $M$}\right\}.

Note that
N_{M}(i)\subset N_{M}[i]
. The
vicinal preorder
of
M
on
E
declares
i\prec_{M}j
whenever
N_{M}[i]\supseteq N_{M}(j)
.

Theorem 9

(Theorem

[

]
)
.

The vicinal preorder of
M
on

is total if and only if
M
shifted. If

is shifted, any bijection between
E
and
[n]
which preserves the vicinal preorder induces an isomorphism between
M
and a shifted matroid
\tilde{M}=\left\langle T\right\rangle

.

Combining Theorems
9
and
2
, we can compute if
M
is threshold by first checking if

is threshold, computing the vicinal preorder of

on

to determine an isomorphism from

Proof of Theorem
8
.
As remarked, Deza and Onn
[
DO23
, Section
8
]
provide an algorithm which checks if
M
is shifted in
O(m^{2}k^{2})
time. Thus we can now assume that
M
is a shifted matroid. With this in mind, we will now compute the total vicinal ordering of
E
. We can calculate all of the sets

and

and

B\setminus i
to the sets

and

whenever

.
