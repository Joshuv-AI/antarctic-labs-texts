Community Detection in the Hypergraph SBM: Exact Recovery Given the Similarity Matrix † † footnotetext: Accepted for presentation at the Conference on Learning Theory (COLT) 2023.
Julia Gaudio, Nirmit Joshi

Abstract
Community detection is a fundamental problem in network science. In this paper, we consider community detection in hypergraphs drawn from the hypergraph stochastic block model (HSBM), with a focus on exact community recovery. We study the performance of polynomial-time algorithms which operate on the similarity matrix W W , where W i ​ j W_{ij} reports the number of hyperedges containing both i i and j j . Under this information model, while the precise information-theoretic limit is unknown, Kim, Bandeira, and Goemans derived a sharp threshold up to which the natural min-bisection estimator on W W succeeds. As min-bisection is NP-hard in the worst case, they additionally proposed a semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxation and conjectured that it achieves the same recovery threshold as the min-bisection estimator. In this paper, we confirm this conjecture. We also design a simple and highly efficient spectral algorithm with nearly linear runtime and show that it achieves the min-bisection threshold. Moreover, the spectral algorithm also succeeds in denser regimes and is considerably more efficient than previous approaches, establishing it as the method of choice. Our analysis of the spectral algorithm crucially relies on strong entrywise bounds on the eigenvectors of W W . Our bounds are inspired by the work of Abbe, Fan, Wang, and Zhong, who developed entrywise bounds for eigenvectors of symmetric matrices with independent entries. Despite the complex dependency structure in similarity matrices, we prove similar entrywise guarantees.

.

There is a sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
in

\max\{p_{e}:e\in\mathcal{E}\}\leq\mu_{n},\textnormal{ and}\hskip 5.69054ptn\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}\geq c_{0}\log n,

.

Let

and

Analyzing the SDP.

We use a dual certificate strategy as in
[
KBG18
]
. The dual of (

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\text{trace}(D)

(4)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }

\displaystyle D\text{ is }n\times n\text{ diagonal matrix, }\nu\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle D+\nu\mathbf{11}^{\top}-W\succeq 0.

The form of the dual motivates the following sufficient condition, whose proof we include for completeness (see Appendix

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose there is a diagonal matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
such that the following holds. Letting
S\triangleq D+\nu\mathbf{11}^{\top}-W
, the matrix
S
is positive semidefinite, its second-smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{n-1}(S)
is strictly positive, and
S\sigma^{*}=0
. Then
X^{*}:=\sigma^{*}{\sigma^{*}}^{\top}
is the unique optimal solution to (

).

To apply Lemma
3.1
, we let
D
be the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are specified by

D_{ii}=\sum_{j\in[n]}W_{ij}\sigma^{*}(i)\sigma^{*}(j).

(5)

Setting
\nu=1
, write
S=D+\mathbf{11}^{\top}-W
. By construction, we have
S\sigma^{*}=0
. It remains to show

\mathbb{P}\left(\inf_{x\perp\sigma^{*}:\|x\|_{2}=1}x^{\top}Sx>0\right)=1-o(1).

(6)

This is where our proof diverges from
[
KBG18
]
; rather than showing (
6
),
[
KBG18
]
proceed through a different sufficient condition.
Using steps similar to the proof of
[
HWX16a
, Theorem 2]
, we show that for all
x\perp\sigma^{*}
such that
\|x\|_{2}=1
,

x^{\top}Sx\geq\min_{i\in[n]}D_{ii}-\|W-W^{*}\|_{2},

(7)

where
W^{*}
is the expected value of
W
, conditioned on
\sigma^{*}
. It remains to (1) lower-bound
D_{ii}
for each
i\in[n]
and (2) upper-bound
\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}
.

To lower-bound

, we condition on
\sigma^{*}
and establish concentration of

around its mean. To see why

should be positive and bounded away from zero, it helps to rewrite (

D_{ii}=\sum_{j\in[n]:\sigma^{*}(i)=\sigma^{*}(j)}W_{ij}-\sum_{j\in[n]:\sigma^{*}(i)\neq\sigma^{*}(j)}W_{ij}.

While the values
\{W_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{n}
are dependent, they are functions of
independent
random variables (namely, the hyperedge random variables). After re-expressing
D_{ii}
in terms of the underlying hyperedge random variables, the proof proceeds by a Chernoff-style argument (Lemma

, we establish the existence of
\epsilon>0
such that for each

with probability

. A union bound then implies
\min_{i\in[n]}D_{ii}\geq\epsilon\log n
with high probability. Next, we need a tail bound on
\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}
. While sharp concentration results are known
[

]

Theorem 5

(
Spectral norm expectation
)
.

Let
d\in\{2,3,\dots\}
be fixed. Let
p\in[0,1]^{\binom{[n]}{d}}
, where
\max_{e}p_{e}\leq\nicefrac{{c_{0}\log n}}{{\binom{n-1}{d-1}}}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
. Let
G\sim H(d,n,p)
, and
W=\mathcal{S}(G)
whose expectation is
W^{*}
. Then there exists a constant
c\coloneqq c(d,c_{0})>0
such that

\Exp{[\left\lVert W-W^{*}\right\rVert_{2}]}\leq c\sqrt{\log n}.

Markov’s inequality immediately implies the desired tail bound. Returning to (
7
), we see that
x^{\top}Sx>0
simultaneously for all
x
satisfying
x\perp\sigma^{*},\|x\|_{2}=1
, with high probability, concluding (
6
).

Spectral norm concentration.

We highlight our proof technique for bounding
\mathbb{E}\left[\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}\right]
(Theorem
5
). Similar bounds are well-known for the spectral norm
\|A-\mathbb{E}[A]\|_{2}
in the case where
A
is a symmetric matrix with independent, bounded entries and suitably bounded expectation
[
FO05
,
LR15
,
HWX16a
]
.
The first step in our proof is inspired by the symmetrization argument of
[
HWX16a
]
. Let
R
be a symmetric tensor of order
d
and dimension
n
with independent Rademacher entries. Let
G\circ R
be the hypergraph where each hyperedge is independently assigned to a
+1
or
-1
label. Let
\mathcal{S}(G\circ R)
denote the corresponding similarity matrix, where the
(i,j)
entry is the sum of
\pm 1
-weighted hyperedges containing
i,j
. We show that

\mathbb{E}\left[\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G\circ R)\|_{2}\right].

A coupling argument then allows us to replace
G
by
G^{(1)}\sim\text{HSBM}(d,n,p_{\text{max}},p_{\text{max}})
, where
p_{\max}=\max_{e}{p_{e}}
. Unlike the matrix
W-W^{*}
, the matrix
S(G^{(1)}\circ R)
has entries with identical distributions. However, the entries are dependent.

Our next goal is to create independence. We invoke Jensen’s inequality, establishing

\mathbb{E}\left[\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})\right\|_{2}\right],

where each
G^{(m)}
is an independent copy of
G^{(1)}
, each
R^{(m)}
is an independent copy of
R
, and
K=d^{2}-d
. Note that
\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})
is a sum of
independent
matrices with
dependent
entries. For any
m\in[K]
, observe that a given hyperedge random variable affects exactly
2\times\binom{d}{2}=K
entries of
\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})
. By adding
K
independent copies, we can rearrange the underlying hyperedge random variables to achieve
\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}C^{(k)},
where
\sum_{k=1}^{K}C^{(k)}
is a sum of
dependent
matrices with
independent
entries. Then

\mathbb{E}[\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}C^{(k)}\right\|_{2}\right]\leq 2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\|C^{(k)}\|_{2}].

The final summation is then straightforwardly bounded using
[
HWX16a
, Theorem 5]
; see Appendix
D
for a complete proof.

4
Analysis of the Spectral Algorithm

Correctness of the spectral algorithm.

Recall that
u_{2}
denotes the second eigenvector of
W=\mathcal{S}(G)
. Since our algorithm determines communities based on the signs of
u_{2}
, we need precise bounds on each entry of
u_{2}
. A natural strategy would be to compare
u_{2}
to
u_{2}^{*}
, since
u_{2}^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sigma^{*}
due to the block structure of
W^{*}
. Unfortunately,
\|u_{2}-u_{2}^{*}\|_{\infty}
is too large for our purposes, but still
u_{2}
recovers communities by sign. To gain intuition for this behavior, write

u_{2}-u_{2}^{*}=\left(\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}-u_{2}^{*}\right)+\left(u_{2}-\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\right).

Figure 2
:
Consider
\textnormal{HSBM}(d,n,\alpha f_{n},\beta f_{n})
for
d=4
,
n=1000
,
\alpha=50
,
\beta=10
and
f_{n}=\log n/\binom{n-1}{d-1}
. The boxplots show three different errors over 100 realizations: (1)
\sqrt{n}\left\lVert u_{2}-u_{2}^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, (2)
\sqrt{n}\|u_{2}^{*}-Wu_{2}^{*}/\lambda_{2}^{*}\|_{\infty}
, and (3)
\sqrt{n}\|u_{2}-Wu_{2}^{*}/\lambda_{2}^{*}\|_{\infty}
.

The first term on the right-hand side is the main term, while the second represents a smaller-order term (see Figure
2
). Such behavior was also observed in the SBM setting by
[
AFWZ20
]
.

Our first step is to apply Theorem
4
, showing that

\min_{s^{*}\in\{\pm 1\}}\left\|u_{2}-s^{*}\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\right\|_{\infty}=o(1/\sqrt{n})

(8)

(see Corollary
C.1
). Therefore, if we can show that the vector
\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}
has the same signs as
\sigma^{*}
(up to a global sign flip), then the same is true for
u_{2}
. Our goal is then to show that
\sigma^{*}
and
Wu_{2}^{*}/\lambda_{2}^{*}
have the same signs, i.e.

\min_{i\in[n]}\sigma^{*}(i)\left(\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\right)_{i}>0.

Fixing the orientation
u_{2}^{*}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sigma^{*}
, we obtain that for
i\in[n]
,

\sigma^{*}(i)\left(\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}\right)_{i}=\frac{\sigma^{*}(i)\sum_{j\in[n]}W_{ij}\sigma^{*}(j)}{\lambda_{2}^{*}\sqrt{n}}=\frac{D_{ii}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}\sqrt{n}},

where
D_{ii}
is defined in (
5
).

In the logarithmic degree regime, we have that
\lambda_{2}^{*}=\Theta(\log n)
. Moreover, as in the SDP analysis, we note that for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small,
\min_{i\in[n]}D_{ii}\geq\epsilon\log n
with high probability, whenever
I(d,\alpha,\beta)>1
. Therefore, the vector
\frac{Wu_{2}^{*}}{\lambda_{2}^{*}}
has the same signs as
\sigma^{*}
. Moreover, the entries are of order
1/\sqrt{n}
; in turn, (
8
) implies that
u_{2}
also has the same signs as
\sigma^{*}
, up to a global sign flip, implying Theorem
2
. Similarly, when
f_{n}=\omega(\log n/n^{d-1})
, the we have that
\lambda_{2}^{*}=\Theta(n^{d-1}f_{n})
. In this case, using a Chernoff-style bound, we establish the existence of
\epsilon>0
such that
\min_{i\in[n]}D_{ii}\geq\epsilon\,n^{d-1}f_{n}
with high probability for all
\alpha>\beta>0
, giving us Theorem
3
.

Runtime analysis.

Observe that Step 2 of Algorithm
2
requires only
O(n)
time. The bottleneck is the time required for Step 1 (to compute the second eigenvector of
W
). In order to compute the eigenpairs, one can use the power method on the matrix
W
, which computes the top eigenpair
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
first. The method converges in
O(\log(n)/\delta)
iterations (see
[
GHJ+16
]
), where
\delta=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{\lambda_{1}}
is the relative eigengap. In our setting, both
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{1}
are
\Theta(n^{d-1}f_{n})
. This is because the eigengap
\Delta_{1}^{*}=\lambda_{1}^{*}-\lambda_{2}^{*}
of
W^{*}
is
\Theta(n^{d-1}f_{n})
and
\left\lVert W-W^{*}\right\rVert_{2}=o(n^{d-1}f_{n})
with high probability using
[
LKC20
]
. We conclude that
\delta=\Theta(1)
and the power method converges in
O(\log n)
iterations. In each iteration, it needs to multiply a vector with
W
. The cost of this operation depends on the sparsity of
W
.

In the logarithmic degree regime, we have
O(n\log n)
edges in the hypergraph, and thus,
W
has at most
O(n\log n)
non-zero entries, which is the effective cost of a matrix-vector multiplication. Therefore, the total time to compute the first eigenpair
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
of
W
is
O(n\log^{2}n)
. To obtain the second eigenvector, we can deflate
W
by subtracting
\lambda_{1}u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}
. The new relative eigengap is
\frac{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}}{\lambda_{2}}=\Theta(1)
since the other eigenvalues of
W
are close to 0. We emphasize that, even though
W-\lambda_{1}u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}
may have
n^{2}
non-zero entries, a matrix-vector multiplication can still be done in
O(n\log n)
time by multiplying the vector with
W
and
\lambda_{1}u_{1}u_{1}^{\top}
separately and then taking the difference. Thus, the power method requires
O(n\log^{2}n)
time to obtain the second eigenvector.

In super-logarithmic degree regimes, a matrix-vector multiplication may take up to
O(n^{2})
time. Therefore, the total runtime is
O(n^{2}\log n)
in the worst-case.

Entrwise eigenvector analysis.

[
AFWZ20
]
introduced a powerful entrywise eigenvector bound, which has been used to show the optimality of spectral algorithms without the need of a clean-up stage
[
AFWZ20
,
DGMS22c
,
DGMS22b
,
DGMS22a
]
. Unfortunately, the entrywise bound
[
AFWZ20
, Theorem 2.1]
does not apply to
W=\mathcal{S}(G)
, since
W
violates a certain independence assumption. The independence assumption is critically used in a
leave-one-out
argument; we therefore carefully adapt this step. It also remains to prove a certain
row concentration
property of the matrix
W
.

For simplicity, let
\lambda=\lambda_{k}
,
\lambda^{*}=\lambda^{*}_{k}
and
\Delta_{k}^{*}=\Delta^{*}
. For clarity of presentation, we assume the orientation
\langle u,u^{*}\rangle\geq 0
, and make similar simplifying assumptions throughout the outline. Our goal is then to show

\left\lVert u-\frac{W{u^{*}}}{\lambda^{*}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim\hskip 5.69054pt\frac{\left\lVert u^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\log\log n}.

We first relate
\lambda
to
\lambda^{*}
. By Weyl’s inequality,
|\lambda-\lambda^{*}|\leq\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}
. In turn,
[
LKC20
, Theorem 4]
implies
\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}\leq\gamma\lambda^{*}
with high probability, for certain
\gamma=\gamma_{n}=o(1)
. It then follows that
|\lambda^{-1}-{\lambda^{*}}^{-1}|\lesssim\gamma|\lambda^{*}|^{-1}
. Using this observation along with the triangle inequality, we can show

\displaystyle\left\lVert u-\frac{Wu^{*}}{\lambda^{*}}\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{Wu}{\lambda}-\frac{Wu^{*}}{\lambda}+\frac{Wu^{*}}{\lambda}-\frac{Wu^{*}}{\lambda^{*}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\hskip-5.69054pt\leq\left|\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{*}}\right|\left\lVert Wu^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\frac{1}{|\lambda|}\left\lVert W(u-u^{*})\right\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{|\lambda^{*}|}\left(\gamma\left\lVert Wu^{*}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\left\lVert W(u-u^{*})\right\rVert_{\infty}\right).

(9)

Note that
u^{*}
is a deterministic vector. Therefore, in order to bound the term
\|Wu^{*}\|_{\infty}
, we derive a row concentration result (Lemma
C.5
). For a fixed vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, our row concentration result controls
\|Wv\|_{\infty}
in terms of both
\|v\|_{\infty}
and
\|v\|_{2}
.

Since
u
depends on
W
, the second term in (
9
) requires a different strategy than the first. We therefore apply the leave-one-out technique, motivated by other works using a similar strategy
[
BBKY13
,
JMRT16
,
ZB18
,
AFWZ20
]
. Bounding
\|W(u-u^{*})\|_{\infty}
reduces to bounding
\left|\left[W(u-u^{*})\right]_{m}\right|
for each
m\in[n]
. To this end, we fix
m\in[n]
and define a random matrix
W^{(m)}
which is independent of the
m
-th row and column of
W
. Let
G^{(m)}
be the hypergraph formed from
G
by deleting all hyperedges containing
m
, and let
W^{(m)}=\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)})
be its similarity matrix. Let
u^{(m)}
be the
k
-th eigenvector of
W^{(m)}
.
Applying the triangle and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\left[W(u-u^{*})\right]_{m}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|W_{m\cdot}(u-u^{(m)})\right|+\left|W_{m\cdot}(u^{(m)}-u^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow\infty}\|u-u^{(m)}\|_{2}+|W_{m\cdot}(u^{(m)}-u^{*})|.

(10)

Observe that
W_{m\cdot}
and
u^{(m)}-u^{*}
are independent by the leave-one-out construction. Therefore, we can bound the second term in (
10
) using our row concentration result. In order to bound
\|u-u^{(m)}\|_{2}
, we apply a version of the Davis–Kahan
\sin(\theta)
theorem
[
DK70
]
, which yields

\|u-u^{(m)}\|_{2}\lesssim\frac{\|(W-W^{(m)})u\|_{2}}{\Delta^{*}}.

Our analysis so far is almost identical to that of
[
AFWZ20
]
; the main difference arises here. Note that the
m
-th row and column
W-W^{(m)}
are the same as those of
W
. Since a hyperedge containing the vertex
m
contributes to other entries of
W
, there will be additional non-zero entries outside of the
m
-th row and column. Thus it is harder to find tight bounds on the quantities that essentially are of main interest:
\|W-W^{(m)}\|_{2}
and
\|(W-W^{(m)})u\|_{2}
. This complication arises whenever
d\geq 3
, and is absent from the analysis of
[
AFWZ20
]
, due to their independence assumption. However, we are still able to prove similar probabilistic bounds for these quantities via a series of careful (and non-trivial) lemmas that uses structural properties of the similarity matrix along with the spectral norm concentration of
[
LKC20
]
to yield the final sharp bounds.

5
Discussion and future work

This paper contributes to a line of work which studies random graph inference problems under restricted information. This paper considers
aggregated
information through the similarity matrix transformation. Other recent works consider
noisy
or
censored

[
ABBS14
,
HWX16b
,
DGMS22c
,
DGMS22b
,
DGMS22a
]
] information models. While the similarity matrix is a lossy representation of the full hypergraph information, it retains most of the information about the
latent
community structure. That is, the similarity matrix is sufficient for exact recovery in all denser regimes, and even in the logarithmic degree regime, at least up to the threshold
I(d,\alpha,\beta)=1
which is slightly worse than the threshold given the full information (see Figure
1
). In Appendix
A
, we further investigate the recovery problem given the adjacency matrix
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
, where
A_{ij}=1
if
i,j
appear together in some hyperedge. In the logarithmic degree regime, our results suggest that the adjacency matrix preserves much of the latent community structure, while in higher degree regimes the adjacency matrix becomes uninformative.

Our work shows that, in the logarithmic degree regime, the spectral algorithm matches the performance of min-bisection estimator, i.e. it succeeds whenever
I(d,\alpha,\beta)>1
. It is not clear whether the exact recovery given only the similarity matrix is possible when
I(d,\alpha,\beta)<1
but
I_{\text{\small full}}(d,\alpha,\beta)>1
. Deriving a precise information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery given the similarity matrix remains an important direction for future work. We believe that
I(d,\alpha,\beta)
is also the information-theoretic limit, which would make the spectral and the SDP algorithms optimal.

Finally, the spectral algorithm is advantageous even when the full information is given, due to its computational efficiency compared to existing approaches. To our knowledge, this constitutes the first algorithm with a nearly linear runtime for exact recovery in the HSBM.

We include some directions for future work.

1.

What is the sharp information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery given only the similarity matrix in the logarithmic degree regime?

2.

We show that the SDP is robust to a monotone adversary who operates on the similarity matrix. What can we say about an adversary who instead operates on the hypergraph directly, by adding intra-community edges and removing inter-community edges?

3.

Can we find a simple proof for the sharp concentration
\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}
, by leveraging our bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\|W-W^{*}\|_{2}\right]
and using a concentration argument? While Talagrand’s inequality leads to a sharp bound in the
d=2
case
[
HWX16a
]
, it becomes vacuous in the
d\geq 3
case.

4.

What is the precise recovery threshold given the adjacency matrix
A
, in the logarithmic degree regime? Does the same spectral strategy (of recovering communities based on the signs of the entries of the second eigenvector of
A
) achieve the information-theoretic limit?

From a memory and runtime standpoint, recovery using

Denser regimes.

Fix

and
\alpha>\beta>0

and

. For each pair

grows with

.

Let
d\in\{2,3,\dots\}

\alpha>\beta>0
, such that
I(d,\alpha,\beta)>1
. Let
f_{n}=\log n/\binom{n-1}{d-1}

W=\mathcal{S}(G)
where
G\sim\textnormal{HSBM}(d,n,\alpha f_{n},\beta f_{n})
. Then there exists a constant
\epsilon:=\epsilon(d,\alpha,\beta)>0

i\in[n]

1-o(n^{-1})

\sum_{j\in[n]}W_{ij}\sigma^{*}(i)\sigma^{*}(j)\geq\epsilon\log n.

Proof.

Fix
i\in[n]
, and let
X\triangleq\sum_{j\in[n]}W_{ij}\sigma^{*}(i)\sigma^{*}(j)
. Let
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\binom{[n]}{d}
be the set of possible edges. For each
e\in\mathcal{E}

A_{e}
be the indicator that edge

is present. Let
\mathcal{E}^{(i)}:=\{e\in\mathcal{E}:i\in e\}

For any edge
e\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}
, let
n_{i}(e):=|\{j\in e\setminus\{i\}:\sigma^{*}(i)\neq\sigma^{*}(j)\}|
be the number of vertices in
e
that belong to the opposite community as
i
. We can rewrite

as follows

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}}\sum_{j\in e,j\neq i}\sigma^{*}(i)\sigma^{*}(j)A_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}}\left((d-1-n_{i}(e))-n_{i}(e)\right)A_{e}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}}\left(d-1-2n_{i}(e)\right)A_{e}.

Next, observe that for
r\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}

\{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}:n_{i}(e)=r\}
has cardinality

Let
\{Y_{r}\}_{r=0}^{d-1}
be independent random variables, where
Y_{r}\sim\text{Bin}(N_{r},q_{r})
, with

q_{r}=\begin{cases}\nicefrac{{\alpha\log n}}{{\binom{n-1}{d-1}}},&\text{if }r=0;\\
\nicefrac{{\beta\log n}}{{\binom{n-1}{d-1}}},&\text{if }1\leq r\leq d-1.\end{cases}

We then further rewrite
X
as follows:

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(i)}}\mathbbm{1}\{n_{i}(e)=r\}\left(d-1-2n_{i}(e)\right)A_{e},

so that
X
is equal to
\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}(d-1-2r)Y_{r}
in distribution.

Let
h_{r}=d-1-2r
. Fix
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
and

\displaystyle\Prob\left(X\leq\epsilon\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Prob\left(e^{-tX}\geq e^{-t\epsilon\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Exp{[e^{-tX}]}}{e^{-t\epsilon\log n}}

\displaystyle=e^{t\epsilon\log n}\Exp{[e^{-t\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}h_{r}Y_{r}}]}

\displaystyle=e^{t\epsilon\log n}\prod_{r=0}^{d-1}\Exp{[e^{-th_{r}Y_{r}}]}

\displaystyle=e^{t\epsilon\log n}\prod_{r=0}^{d-1}\left(1-q_{r}(1-e^{-th_{r}})\right)^{N_{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\text{exp}\left(t\epsilon\log n-\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}N_{r}q_{r}(1-e^{-h_{r}t})\right).

(11)

Here, the second equality is due to independence of the

random variables, and the final step uses
1-x\leq e^{-x}
. Next,

\displaystyle N_{r}

\displaystyle=\binom{n/2}{r}\binom{n/2-1}{d-1-r}=\frac{\binom{n/2}{r}\binom{n/2-1}{d-1-r}}{\binom{n-1}{d-1}}\binom{n-1}{d-1}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\frac{(n/2)^{r}}{r\,!}\cdot\frac{(n/2)^{d-1-r}}{(d-1-r)\,!}\cdot\frac{(d-1)!}{n^{d-1}}\cdot\binom{n-1}{d-1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\leq\epsilon\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(t\epsilon\log n-\frac{(1+o(1))}{2^{d-1}}\bigg(\alpha(1-e^{-(d-1)t})+\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\beta\binom{d-1}{r}(1-e^{-(d-1-2r)t})\bigg)\log n\right).

Let
t=t^{*}(d,\alpha,\beta)

t^{*}(d,\alpha,\beta)=\arg\max_{t\geq 0}\hskip 5.69054pt\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}\left(\alpha(1-e^{-(d-1)t})+\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\beta\binom{d-1}{r}(1-e^{-(d-1-2r)t})\right):=\arg\max_{t\geq 0}\psi(t).

We then obtain

\displaystyle\Prob\left(X\leq\epsilon\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\text{exp}\big(t^{*}\epsilon\log n-I(d,\alpha,\beta)\log n+o(\log n)\big)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-I(d,\alpha,\beta)+t^{*}\epsilon+o(1)}.

Note that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\psi^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2^{d-1}}\left(\alpha(d-1)+\beta\sum_{r=1}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{r}(d-1-2r)\right)=(\alpha-\beta)(d-1)/2^{d-1}>0.

Furthermore, since
I(d,\alpha,\beta)>1
, one can choose

sufficiently small such that

\Prob\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}W_{ij}\sigma^{*}(i)\sigma^{*}(j)\leq\epsilon\log n\right)=o(n^{-1}).\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 15.00002pt\penalty\vrule height=7.5pt,width=5.0pt,depth=2.5pt

Finally, we make some important observations about the structure of
W^{*}
. Observe that
W^{*}
has a
block
structure (up to the diagonal entries). In particular,
W^{*}
is a zero diagonal symmetric matrix whose non-diagonal entries are given by

W^{*}_{ij}=\begin{cases}p^{\prime}\triangleq\binom{n/2-2}{d-2}\alpha f_{n}+\left(\binom{n-2}{d-2}-\binom{n/2-2}{d-2}\right)\beta f_{n},&\text{if }\sigma^{*}(i)=\sigma^{*}(j);\\
q^{\prime}\triangleq\binom{n-2}{d-2}\beta f_{n},&\text{if }\sigma^{*}(i)\neq\sigma^{*}(j).\end{cases}

(12)

Observe that
W^{*}
can be decomposed as

W^{*}=\left(\frac{p^{\prime}+q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\mathbf{11}^{\top}+\left(\frac{p^{\prime}-q^{\prime}}{2}\right)\sigma^{*}{\sigma^{*}}^{\top}-p^{\prime}\mathbf{I}.

(13)

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The proof uses ideas from the proof of

Let

satisfies the conditions mentioned in Lemma

. Therefore, using the fact that

Therefore, it remains to show that

We additionally show that the SDP is robust to a monotone adversary (Lemma

for any
(i,j)
in the same community, and decrease the value of
W_{ij}
for any
(i,j)
in opposite communities. The robustness of SDPs to monotone adversaries is well-known (see e.g.

). While the monotone adversary appears to provide helpful information, spectral algorithms generally fail under such a semirandom model.

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

Let
X
be a feasible solution to the modified SDP, and let
X^{*}
be the unique optimal solution to (
3
), which is guaranteed by Theorem

. Due to uniqueness of

, we have

\displaystyle X=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\lambda_{l}v_{l}v_{l}^{\top}

\lambda_{l}\geq 0
for all

. Then by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle X_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\lambda_{l}v_{l,i}v_{l,j}\right)^{2}

One could also consider another natural monotone adversary that operates directly on the underlying hypergraph instead of on its similarity matrix. More specifically, the adversary is allowed to add intra-community edges and delete cross-community edges. It is not immediately clear whether an SDP algorithm would be robust to such an adversary.

\displaystyle\Prob\left(X\leq\epsilon\,n^{d-1}f_{n}\right)

Proof of Theorem

Observation C.3

\left\lVert W^{*}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow\infty}\leq\sqrt{n}\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}.

Proof.

\max_{e}p_{e}\leq\mu_{n}

\left\lVert W^{*}\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow\infty}=\max_{i}\left\lVert W^{*}_{i\cdot}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\max{|W^{*}_{ij}|}\leq\sqrt{n}\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}.\penalty\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 15.00002pt\penalty\vrule height=7.5pt,width=5.0pt,depth=2.5pt

Define the function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

\varphi(x)=\frac{2+8d/c_{0}}{\left(1\vee\log(1/x)\right)},

(19)

where

Observation C.4

The next result provides a probabilistic upper bound on the inner product of a row of
W-W^{*}
and a fixed vector

\varphi(\cdot)
. Intuitively, one can think that the rate of growth of

essentially controls the strength of the concentration bound. Formally,

Lemma C.5

(
Row concentration
)
.

For any
m\in[n]
and any fixed non-zero

\Prob\left(|(W-W^{*})_{m\cdot}v|\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}\varphi\left(\frac{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}}\right)n\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}\right)\geq 1-O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right).

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{E}^{(m)}=\{e\in\mathcal{E}:m\in e\}
. Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a fixed vector. Let
X\triangleq(W-W^{*})_{m\cdot}v
. It is convenient to rewrite
X
as a sum of random variables
\{A_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(m)}}
, where
A_{e}
is the indicator random variable associated with the hyperedge
e

X=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(m)}}\left(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}\right)(A_{e}-\Exp[A_{e}]).

Without loss of generality, assume that
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}\hskip-2.84526pt=\frac{1}{d}
(otherwise,
v
may be scaled). By Markov’s inequality, for any
\delta,t>0
,

\Prob\left(X\geq\delta\right)\leq\Prob\left(e^{tX}\geq e^{t\delta}\right)\leq e^{-t\delta}\prod_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(m)}}\Exp\left(e^{t(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j})(A_{e}-\Exp[A_{e}])}\right).

(20)

For
e\in\mathcal{E}^{(m)}
, we can bound the logarithm of the moment generating function as follows:

\displaystyle\log\left(\Exp\left[e^{t(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j})(A_{e}-\Exp[A_{e}])}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\Exp\left[e^{t(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j})A_{e}}\right]\right)-t\left(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}\right)\mathbb{E}[A_{e}]

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-p_{e}+p_{e}e^{t\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}}\right)-tp_{e}\left(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p_{e}\left(e^{t\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}}-1\right)-tp_{e}\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j},

(21)

where we have used the fact that
\log(1+x)\leq x
for
x>1
in the last step. Next, we use the fact that
e^{x}\leq 1+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}e^{r}
for
|x|\leq r
to further upper-bound (
21
) by

\displaystyle p_{e}\left(1+t\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}+\frac{e^{td\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}}}{2}\cdot t^{2}\left(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}\right)^{2}-1\right)-tp_{e}\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}

\displaystyle=p_{e}\frac{e^{t}}{2}\cdot t^{2}\left(\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}v_{j}\right)^{2}\leq\hskip 2.84526pt\frac{e^{t}p_{\max}t^{2}}{2}\cdot d\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}{v_{j}}^{2},

where
\max_{e}p_{e}\triangleq p_{\max}
.

Substituting our bounds on the log of moment generating functions into (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle\log(\Prob\left(X\geq\delta\right))

\displaystyle\leq-t\delta+\frac{e^{t}p_{\max}d}{2}t^{2}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{(m)}}\sum_{j\in e\setminus{\{m\}}}{v_{j}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-t\delta+\frac{e^{t}p_{\max}d}{2}\cdot t^{2}\binom{n-2}{d-2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where the last step follows from the fact that each
j\neq m
appears in
\binom{n-2}{d-2}
potential hyperedges in
\mathcal{E}^{(m)}
. Let
t=1\bigvee\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\right)
. Using the fact that
(1\lor\log x)^{2}\leq x
for
x\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\log(\Prob\left(X\geq\delta\right))

\displaystyle\leq-t\delta+\frac{e^{t}p_{\max}d}{2}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\binom{n-2}{d-2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Observe that
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}=\sqrt{n}/d
, so that
\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\|v\|_{2}}\right)\geq 0
. Therefore,
e^{t}=e^{1\vee\log(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\|v\|_{2}})}\leq e^{1+\log(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\|v\|_{2}})}\leq\frac{e\sqrt{n}}{d\|v\|_{2}}.
Hence

\log(\Prob\left(X\geq\delta\right))\leq-t\delta+\frac{ep_{\max}d}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\binom{n-2}{d-2}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=-t\delta+\frac{ep_{\max}n\binom{n-2}{d-2}}{2d}.

Let
a=8d/c_{0}
and set
\delta=~t^{-1}d^{-1}(2+a)p_{\max}n\binom{n-2}{d-2}.

We then obtain the bound

\log(\Prob\left(X\geq\delta\right))\leq-\frac{(2+a)p_{\max}n}{d}\binom{n-2}{d-2}+\frac{ep_{\max}n\binom{n-2}{d-2}}{2d}\leq-\frac{ap_{\max}n\binom{n-2}{d-2}}{d}.

By replacing
v
with
-v
, we obtain a similar bound for the lower tail. The union bound gives

\Prob\left(|X|\geq\delta\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ap_{\max}n\binom{n-2}{d-2}}{d}\right).

Substituting in the value of
t
and using Assumption
1
that

,

\Prob\left(|(W-W^{*})_{m\cdot}v|\geq\frac{(2+a)n\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}}{d\left(1\bigvee\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\right)\right)}\right)\leq 2e^{-\frac{a{c_{0}}\log n}{d}}.

Finally, substituting the value of
a

\Prob\left(|(W-W^{*})_{m\cdot}v|\leq\frac{(2+8d/c_{0})n\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}}{d\left(1\bigvee\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\right)\right)}\right)\geq 1-2{n}^{-4}.

Recalling that
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}=1/d
yields:

\Prob\left(|(W-W^{*})_{m\cdot}v|\leq\frac{(2+\frac{8d}{c_{0}})\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}n\binom{n-2}{d-2}\mu_{n}}{1\bigvee\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\right)}\right)\geq 1-2{n}^{-4}.

Since
\varphi\left(\frac{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{n}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}}\right)=(2+8d/c_{0})/(1\vee\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{\infty}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}}\right)
, the lemma follows.

Lemma C.6

\left\lVert W-W^{(m)}\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\hskip 2.84526pt\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{2\rightarrow\infty}

Proof.

\displaystyle\|W-W^{(m)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|W-W^{(m)}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[n]}\left\lVert(W-W^{(m)})_{i\cdot}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}

Proposition C.10

Appendix D
Spectral norm concentration for similarity matrices

In order to prove Theorem
5
, we require the following result.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
A
be a random matrix with independent entries, where
A_{ij}\in[a,b]
for two constants
a<b
. Suppose
\Exp{[|A_{ij}|]}\leq q
for all
i,j
, where
\frac{c_{2}\log n}{n}\leq q\leq 1-c_{3}
for arbitrary constants
c_{2},c_{3}>0
. Then, there exists a constant
c^{\prime}\coloneqq c^{\prime}(c_{2},c_{3},a,b)>0
such that

\Exp{[\left\lVert A-\Exp{[A}]\right\rVert_{2}]}\leq c^{\prime}\sqrt{nq}.

Proof.

We use ideas from
[
DGMS22a
, Lemma 4.5]
, who showed a similar result for zero-diagonal, symmetric matrices with independent entries. We first construct a symmetric matrix
B
using
A
to reduce it to the symmetric case.
Let

B=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
A^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Fix a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}=1
, and consider the vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
such that
y=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}\\
x\end{bmatrix}
. Observe that
\left\lVert y\right\rVert_{2}=1.
Moreover,

(B-\Exp[B])y=\begin{bmatrix}(A-\Exp[A])x\\
\mathbf{0}_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,

\left\lVert(B-\Exp[B])y\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert(A-\Exp[A])x\right\rVert_{2},

so that

\|(A-\mathbb{E}[A])x\|_{2}=\|(B-\mathbb{E}[B])y\|_{2}\leq\|B-\mathbb{E}[B]\|_{2}.

Since
x
was arbitrary, we have shown
\left\lVert A-\Exp{[A]}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert B-\Exp{[B]}\right\rVert_{2}
, and therefore

\Exp[\left\lVert A-\Exp{[A]}\right\rVert_{2}]\leq\Exp[\left\lVert B-\Exp{[B]}\right\rVert_{2}].

(33)

Thus, it suffices to bound
\Exp[\left\lVert B-\Exp{[B]}\right\rVert_{2}]
. Let
B^{+}=\max\{B,0\}
, where the maximum is taken entrywise. Similarly, let
B^{-}=-\min\{B,0\}
. Then we can write
B=B^{+}-B^{-}
. By the triangle inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|B-\mathbb{E}[B]\|_{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|B^{+}-\mathbb{E}[B^{+}]\|_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|B^{-}-\mathbb{E}[B^{-}]\|_{2}\right].

(34)

Observe that
B^{+}
and
B^{-}
are nonnegative, zero-diagonal, symmetric matrices with independent entries. Also, for all
i,j
,

\max\{\mathbb{E}[B^{+}_{ij}],\mathbb{E}[B^{-}_{ij}]\}\leq\Exp[|B_{ij}|]\leq\max\Exp[|A_{ij}|]\leq q.

If
b\leq 0
, then
\left\lVert B^{+}-\Exp[B^{+}]\right\rVert_{2}=0
. Otherwise it follows from
[
HWX16a
, Theorem 5]
that there exists
c^{+}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{b}(\|B^{+}-\mathbb{E}[B^{+}]\|_{2})\right]\leq c^{+}\sqrt{\frac{nq}{b}}.

Similarly, if
a\geq 0
, then
\left\lVert B^{-}-\Exp[B^{-}]\right\rVert_{2}=0
. Otherwise, we again use
[
HWX16a
, Theorem 5]
to conclude that there exists a constant
c^{-}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|a|}(\|B^{-}-\mathbb{E}[B^{-}]\|_{2})\right]\leq c^{-}\sqrt{\frac{nq}{|a|}}.

Combining these with (
33
) and (
34
) we get

\Exp[\left\lVert A-\Exp{[A]}\right\rVert_{2}]\leq c^{\prime}\sqrt{nq},

where
c^{\prime}=c^{+}\sqrt{\max\{b,0\}}+c^{-}\sqrt{|\min\{a,0\}|}
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

The symbols
\lesssim
and
\asymp
hide constants in
d,c_{0}
throughout the proof. Our goal is to bound
\Exp{\left[\left\lVert\mathcal{S}(G)-\Exp{[\mathcal{S}(G)]}\right\rVert_{2}\right]}
. Let
G^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
G
. Observe that for a fixed matrix
X
, the function
f(Y)=\|X-Y\|_{2}
is convex. By Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}(G)]\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}(G^{\prime})]\|_{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G)-\mathcal{S}(G^{\prime})\|_{2}\right].

We can extend the definition of
\mathcal{S}
so that
\mathcal{S}(G-G^{\prime})=\mathcal{S}(G)-\mathcal{S}(G^{\prime})
; i.e.
G-G^{\prime}
is a “hypergraph” with edges labeled by
\{1,0,-1\}
.

Let
R
be a symmetric tensor of order
d
and dimension
n
with independent Rademacher entries; i.e. the entries
\{R(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d}):i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{d}\}
are mutually independent. Let
\circ
denote the edge-wise product. Since
G-G^{\prime}
has the same distribution as
(G-G^{\prime})\circ R
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G)-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}(G)]\|_{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G-G^{\prime})\|_{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}((G-G^{\prime})\circ R)\|_{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G\circ R)\|_{2}\right],

(35)

where the last inequality follows from the triangle inequality. Let
p_{\max}\triangleq c_{0}\log n/\binom{n-1}{d-1}
for simplicity. Consider the hypergraph
G_{+}
that is coupled to
G
as follows. The hypergraph
G_{+}
does not contain any edge that appears in
G
. Each edge
e
that is not present in

G_{+}
with probability
\frac{p_{\max}-p_{e}}{1-p_{e}}
(independently across edges). Letting

, we see that
(G+G_{+})\circ R
has the same distribution as
G^{(1)}\circ R
. Also,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{S}(G_{+}\circ R)~|~G\right]=0
. Using these observations along with Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G\circ R)\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G\circ R)+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{S}(G_{+}\circ R)~|~G\right]\|_{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G\circ R)+\mathcal{S}(G_{+}\circ R)\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}((G+G_{+})\circ R)\|_{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G^{(1)}\circ R)\|_{2}\right].

Substituting this into (
35
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathcal{S}(G)-\Exp{[\mathcal{S}(G)]}\|_{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\|S(G^{(1)}\circ R)\|_{2}\right].

(36)

Note that, even though
\mathcal{S}(G^{(1)}\circ R)
has identically distributed entries, they are still dependent; we want a matrix with independent entries instead to apply Lemma
D.1
. To achieve this, we use a somewhat involved symmetrization argument. For simplicity, consider the case when
d=2
(which reduces to the adjacency matrix), where we have independent entries up to the symmetry. In other words, each independent edge random variable appears exactly twice in the matrix. In this situation,
[
HWX16a
, Theorem 5]
uses the standard symmetrization technique by adding another independent copy of
\mathcal{S}(G^{(1)}\circ R)
and rearranging random variables to create independence. However, for a general
d
, this is not sufficient. But observe that the random variable (label) associated with any fixed edge
e
is added to exactly
K:=2\cdot\binom{d}{2}=d^{2}-d

K

More formally, let
G^{(m)}
and
R^{(m)}
be independent copies of
G^{(1)}
and
R

. Note that
\Exp{[\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})]}

\displaystyle\Exp{[\|\mathcal{S}(G^{(1)}\circ R)\|_{2}]}

\displaystyle=\Exp{\left[\left\lVert\mathcal{S}(G^{(1)}\circ R^{(1)})+\sum_{m=2}^{K}\Exp{[\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})]}\right\rVert_{2}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\Exp\left[\left\lVert\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})\right\rVert_{2}\right].

Observe that
\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})

independent

dependent

dependent

L(e)

e

(i,j):i,j\in e

For

and

\displaystyle X_{ij}^{(e,m,\ell)}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}A^{(m)}_{e}\circ R^{(m)}_{e}&(i,j)=\mathsf{op}^{(\ell)}_{e}\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
A_{e}^{(m)}
denotes the indicator random variable associated with the edge

in

. By construction,

\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)})=\sum_{m=1}^{K}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}X^{(e,m,\ell)}

Here

represents the standard modulo operation, except

is

instead of

. Intuitively,

is the matrix for which the

. Observe that all the entries of

are independent copies of

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}C^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}X^{(e,(\ell+k-1)\%K,\ell)}+(-1)^{k+1}D

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}X^{(e,(\ell+k-1)\%K,\ell)}+(-1)^{k+1}D

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sum_{m=1}^{K}X^{(e,m,\ell)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{K}\mathcal{S}(G^{(m)}\circ R^{(m)}),

(39)

where the last step follows from (
38
). Therefore, combining (
36
), (
37
), and (
39
)

\Exp{[\left\lVert W-W^{*}\right\rVert_{2}]}\leq 2\Exp{\left[\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{K}C^{(k)}\right\rVert_{2}\right]}\leq 2K\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}]},

(40)

where
C
has the same distribution as
C^{(1)}
. Each entry of
C
is an independent copy of
Y
. Let
F
be the event that all the entries of
C
are in the range
[-4d,4d]
. By a union bound,

\displaystyle\Prob\left(F^{\textnormal{c}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\,n^{2}\cdot\Prob\left(|Y|\geq 4d\right)\leq\,n^{2}\cdot\Prob\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\binom{n-2}{d-2}}{X_{i}}\geq 4d\right).

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{\binom{n-2}{d-2}}{X_{i}}\sim\textnormal{Bin}\left(\binom{n-2}{d-2},p_{\max}\right)
, by
[
MU17
, Theorem 4.4, Equation 4.1]

\Prob\left(F^{\textnormal{c}}\right)\leq n^{2}\cdot\Prob\left(\text{Bin}\left(\binom{n-2}{d-2},\frac{c_{0}\log n}{\binom{n-1}{d-1}}\right)\geq 4d\right)\leq n^{2}\frac{e^{4d}}{\Theta(n/\log n)^{4d}}=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4d-3}}\right).

(41)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}]}

\displaystyle=\Prob\left(F\right)\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}+\Prob\left(F^{\textnormal{c}}\right)\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F^{\textnormal{c}}]}

\displaystyle\leq\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4d-3}}\right)\Exp[\|C\|_{F}\mid F^{\textnormal{c}}]

\displaystyle\leq\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{4d-3}}\right)n\binom{n-2}{d-2}

\displaystyle=\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}+O\left(n^{3-4d+1+d-2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}+o(1),

(42)

where the second inequality uses the fact that each entry of
C
is at most
\binom{n-2}{d-2}
.
Thus, it is only left to show
\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}=O(\sqrt{\log n})
. Note that the entries of
C
are independent even after conditioning on
F
. Moreover, the entries are bounded in
[-4d,4d]
. Thus

\displaystyle\Exp{[|C_{ij}|\mid F]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{4d}k\cdot\Prob\left(|C_{ij}|=k\mid F\right)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{4d}k\cdot\frac{\Prob\left(F\cap|C_{ij}|=k\right)}{\Prob\left(F\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{4d}k\cdot\frac{\Prob\left(|C_{ij}|=k\right)}{1-o(1)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{4d}k\cdot\Prob\left(|C_{ij}|=k\right)\leq\Exp{[|C_{ij}|]}\leq\binom{n-2}{d-2}p_{\max}=\frac{\binom{n-2}{d-2}c_{0}\log n}{\binom{n-1}{d-1}}\lesssim\frac{\log n}{n}.

Therefore, by Lemma
D.1
and the fact that
\Exp{[C\mid F]}
is the zero matrix, we obtain

\Exp{[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]}=\Exp[\left\lVert C-\Exp{[C\mid F]}\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]\leq c^{\prime}\sqrt{n\frac{\log n}{n}}\leq c^{\prime}\sqrt{\log n}.

Substituting this back in (
40
) and using (
42
),

\Exp{[\left\lVert W-W^{*}\right\rVert_{2}]}\leq 2K\Exp[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}]\leq 2K\Exp[\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}\mid F]+o(1)\leq 2Kc^{\prime}\sqrt{\log n}+o(1)\leq c\sqrt{\log n},

for some
c
that depends on
d
and
c_{0}
, concluding the proof.
