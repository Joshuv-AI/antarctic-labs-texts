Introduction

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer,
2m>n
, and
0<\alpha\leq(n+2m)/(2m-n)
. In this work, we consider the following equation

\displaystyle\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v)=\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}(\varepsilon v+v^{-\alpha})\quad\text{in }\mathbb S^{n}.

(
1.1
)
ε

Here
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}
is the well-known GJMS operator on
\mathbb S^{n}
equipped with the standard metric
g_{\mathbb S^{n}}
, which is given as follows

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}:=\prodop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(-\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}-(i+\frac{n}{2})(i-\frac{n}{2}+1)\right),

see
[
GJMS92
]
, and

Q_{n}^{2m}:=\frac{2}{n-2m}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(1)=\frac{2}{n-2m}\frac{\Gamma(n/2+m)}{\Gamma(n/2-m)}

is a non-zero constant representing the so-called
Q
-curvature of
(\mathbb S^{n},g_{\mathbb S^{n}})
, namely

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}=(-\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}})^{m}+\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k\leq m-1}c_{k}(-\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}})^{k}+\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}

for suitable constants
c_{k}
with
1\leq k\leq m-1
. A special case of the operator
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}
, which has often been studied over the last two decades, is the well-known Paneitz operator, which is of fourth order. This example of a higher-order conformal operator gains interest because of its role in conformal geometry; see
[
CGY02
,
HY16
]
. On
(\mathbb S^{3},g_{\mathbb S^{3}})
, the Paneitz operator is given by

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}=\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{3}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{3}}}-\frac{15}{16},

and therefore
Q_{3}^{4}=-2\Gamma(7/2)/\Gamma(-1/2)=15/8
. Using the above recursive formula for
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}
we can compute higher dimensional cases, for example

\mathbf{P}_{3}^{6}=-\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}^{3}-\frac{23}{4}\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}^{2}-\frac{27}{16}\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}+\frac{315}{64}\quad\text{on }(\mathbb S^{3},g_{\mathbb S^{3}})

with
Q_{3}^{6}=-105/32
and

\mathbf{P}_{5}^{6}=-\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}^{3}+\frac{13}{4}\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}^{2}+\frac{93}{16}\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}-\frac{945}{64}\quad\text{on }(\mathbb S^{5},g_{\mathbb S^{5}})

with
Q_{5}^{6}=945/32
. One should pay attention on the sign difference of
Q_{3}^{6}
and
Q_{5}^{6}
.

Our motivation of working on the equation (
1.1
)
ε
traces back to a recent conjecture by F. Hang and P. Yang in
[
HY20
]
that we are going to describe now. This conjecture concerns the following sharp critical Sobolev inequality on
\mathbb S^{3}

\|\phi^{-1}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb S^{3})}^{2}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\Big[(\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{3}}}\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{g_{\mathbb S^{3}}}\phi|^{2}-\frac{15}{16}\phi^{2}\Big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}\geq-\frac{15}{16}|\mathbb S^{3}|^{4/3}

(1.2)

for any
\phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb S^{3})
with
\phi>0
, which was already proved in
[
YZ04
]
by symmetrization argument and in
[
HY04
]
by variational argument. Apparently, the inequality (
1.2
) can be rewritten as follows

\|\phi^{-1}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb S^{3})}^{2}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}\geq-\frac{15}{16}|\mathbb S^{3}|^{4/3}

(1.3)

for any
0<\phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb S^{3})
, because the integral in (
1.2
) is nothing but
\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}
. In (
1.3
) and what follows,
|\mathbb S^{n}|
denotes the surface area of
\mathbb S^{n}
. Besides, by Morrey’s theorem, functions in
H^{2}(\mathbb S^{3})
are continuous and therefore the condition
\phi>0
is understood in pointwise sense. By direct calculation, one can easily verify that equality in (
1.3
) occurs if
\phi
is any positive constant. This tells us that the Paneitz operator
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}
on the standard sphere
\mathbb S^{3}
is no longer positive; see
[
XY02
]
for the assumption on the positivity of the Paneitz operator on closed
3
-manifolds.

In an effort to provide a new proof for (
1.3
) with the sharp constant, the authors in
[
HY20
]
propose a new way to prove the above Sobolev inequality by considering the following minimizing problem

\inf_{0<\phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb S^{3})}\|\phi^{-1}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb S^{3})}^{2}\Big[\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}+\varepsilon\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\phi^{2}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}\Big]

(1.4)

for small
\varepsilon>0
. Thanks to the small perturbation
\varepsilon\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb S^{3})}^{2}
, it is standard and straightforward to verify that the extremal problem (
1.4
) has a minimizer. Such a minimizer, denoted by
v_{\varepsilon}
, eventually solves

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(v_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon v_{\varepsilon}=-v_{\varepsilon}^{-7}

on
\mathbb S^{3}
, up to a constant. Here is the key observation: if the above equation only admits constant solution for small
\varepsilon>0
, namely
v_{\varepsilon}\equiv\text{const.}
, then one immediately has

\|\phi^{-1}\|_{L^{6}(\mathbb S^{3})}^{2}\Big[\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}+\varepsilon\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\phi^{2}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}\Big]\geq|\mathbb S^{3}|^{1/3}\Big[\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{3}}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(1)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{3}}+\varepsilon|\mathbb S^{3}|\Big]

for any
0<\phi\in H^{2}(\mathbb S^{3})
. Having this and as
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(1)=-(1/2)Q_{3}^{4}=-15/16
, letting
\varepsilon\searrow 0
yields (
1.3
). The novelty of this new approach is that it automatically implies the sharp form of (
1.3
) with the precise sharp constant.

The above observation leads Hang and Yang to propose the following conjecture.

The Hang–Yang conjecture
(
[
HY20
, page 3299]
).
Let
\varepsilon>0
be a small number. If
v
is a positive smooth solution to

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(v)+\varepsilon v=-v^{-7}

on
\mathbb S^{3}
, then
v
must be a constant function.

In a recent work Zhang
[
Zha21
]
provides an affirmative answer to the above conjecture. The idea behind Zhang’s proof is first to transfer the differential equation on
\mathbb S^{3}
to some differential equation on
\mathbf{R}^{3}
and then to classify solutions to that equation on
\mathbf{R}^{3}
. More precisely, let
\pi_{N}:\mathbb S^{3}\to\mathbf{R}^{3}
be the stereographic projection from the north pole
N
; see subsection
2.1
below. The pullback
(\pi_{N}^{-1})^{*}
enjoys

(\pi_{N}^{-1})^{*}(g_{\mathbb S^{3}})=\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2}dx^{2}

and for any smooth solution
v
on
\mathbb S^{3}
there holds

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(v)\circ\pi_{N}^{-1}=\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-7/2}\Delta^{2}\Big(\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-1/2}v\circ\pi_{N}^{-1}\Big).

(Here and in the sequel,
\Delta
is the usual Laplacian on Euclidean spaces.) Setting

u(x):=\big(\frac{1+|x|^{2}}{2}\big)^{1/2}\big(v\circ\pi_{N}^{-1}\big)(x),

(1.5)

we see that if
v
solves
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{4}_{3}(v)+\varepsilon v=-v^{-7}
in
\mathbb S^{3}
, then
u
solves

\Delta^{2}u(x)+\varepsilon\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{4}u(x)=-u^{-7}(x)\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{3}.

Via a dedicated argument based on the method of moving planes and techniques from potential theory, which are rather involved, it is proved that
u
is radially symmetric. Finally, with the help of a Kazdan–Warner type identity, the function
v
must be constant.

Inspired by the work of Zhang described above, we are interested in Hang–Yang’s conjecture in higher dimensional cases, namely we want to seek for a suitable Liouville type result for positive, smooth solution to equations involving GJMS operators. This leads us to investigate solutions to (
1.1
)
ε
. Very similar to situation studied by Hang and Yang, our motivation to study the equation (
1.1
)
ε
comes from the higher-order sharp critical Sobolev inequality; see Theorem
1.2
below. Using the perturbation approach introduced in
[
HY20
]
, we are able to establish a Liouville type result for solutions to (
1.1
)
ε
.

Toward a suitable Liouville type result, let us first describe some preliminary results on (
1.1
)
ε
. Our first observation concerns the admissible range for
\varepsilon
. As the perturbation approach is being used, we require the condition
\varepsilon\geq 0
; see the proof of Lemma
5.1
. Now, by integrating both sides of (
1.1
)
ε
over
\mathbb S^{n}
and as
2m-n>0
and
Q_{n}^{2m}\neq 0
we conclude that

(1-\varepsilon)\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}vd\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v^{-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}.

This immediately tells us that
\varepsilon<1
. Thus, the admissible range for
\varepsilon
is
0\leq\varepsilon<1
. Having this, let us now state the main result of this paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
be odd and
m>n/2
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{*}\in(0,1)
such that under one of the following conditions

(1)

either
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{*})
and
0<\alpha\leq(n+2m)/(2m-n)

(2)

or
\varepsilon=0
and
0<\alpha<(n+2m)/(2m-n)

any positive, smooth solution to (
1.1
)
ε
must be constant.

We have the following remarks:

•

The above result again confirms the Hang–Yang conjecture for the Paneitz operator on
\mathbb S^{3}
, and generalizes the result of Zhang in the critical setting in higher dimensional cases.

•

Theorem
1.1
can be compared with the Liouville type results obtained by Bidaut-Véron and Véron in
[
BVV91
, Theorem 6.1]
for the Emden equation, see also the work of Gidas and Spruck in
[
GS81
]
. Note that the condition
\alpha<(n+2m)/(2m-n)
is sharp for
\varepsilon=0
as the result does not hold if
\alpha=(n+2m)/(2m-n)
. This is because in this limiting case the equation (
1.1
)
0
is conformally invariant; see section
3
.

•

The threshold
\varepsilon_{*}
is given in Lemma
4.3
.

•

Although for any
0\leq\varepsilon<1
, equation (
1.1
)
ε
always admits the trivial solution
v_{\varepsilon}\equiv(1-\varepsilon)^{-1/(\alpha+1)}
, but it is not clear whether or not the above Liouville type result still holds for
\varepsilon\in[\varepsilon_{*},1)
. This seems to be an interesting open question.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we adopt the strategy used by Zhang. Such strategy can be formulated as the following two main steps: first to transfer (
1.1
)
ε
in
\mathbb S^{n}
to the equation (
1.8
)
ε
and the corresponding integral equation in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then to study symmetry properties of solutions to these equations for small
\varepsilon>0
. However, to be able to handle higher-order cases, our approach is significantly different from Zhang. One major reason is that less results is known for the higher-order cases compared to the case
m=2
. For example, we do not know if the preliminary results of Hang and Yang mentioned in
[
Zha21
, section 2]
are available for
m\geq 3
. Because of this difficulty, instead of the differential equation (
1.8
)
ε
, we mainly work on the corresponding integral equation on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, and directly prove compactness results and symmetry properties of solutions. As pays off, our analysis is much simpler, and could handle higher-order cases efficiently.

As the operator
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}
is conformally covariant, for any smooth function
\varphi
on
\mathbb S^{n}
we have the following identity (
\pi
denotes the stereographic projection from
\mathbb S^{n}
to
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with respect to either the north or the south pole)

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\varphi)\circ\pi^{-1}=\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-\frac{n+2m}{2}}(-\Delta)^{m}\Big(\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{\frac{n-2m}{2}}\varphi\circ\pi^{-1}\Big);

see e.g.
[
Han07
, Section 2]
. Then, similar to (
1.5
), by setting

\displaystyle u(x):=\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{\frac{n-2m}{2}}\big(v\circ\pi^{-1}\big)

(1.6)

and

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon,u}(x):=\varepsilon\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}u(x)+\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{\frac{n+2m}{2}+\alpha\frac{n-2m}{2}}u(x)^{-\alpha}

(1.7)

we see that
u
satisfies

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{m}u=\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}F_{\varepsilon,u}\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n}.

(
1.8
)
ε

In view of (
1.6
), we know that the function
u
on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
has exact growth
|x|^{2m-n}
at infinity. This additional information allows us to transfer the differential equation (
1.8
)
ε
into the following integral equation

u(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u}(y)dy\quad\text{on }\mathbf{R}^{n}

for some constant
\gamma_{2m,n}>0
; see Theorem
2.2
below. Notice that in general there might be more solutions to (
1.8
)
ε
than the above integral equation, see e.g.
[
HW19
]
and
[
DN22
]
.

Let us emphasize that transferring to an equivalent integral equation on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
also appears in the work of Zhang, but the proof provided in
[
Zha21
]
does not seem to work in our case. Similar integral representation in the fractional setting also appears in
[
FKT22
]
. In our work, by exploiting some nice structures on
\mathbb S^{n}
as well as some intriguing properties of the stereographic projection, we offer a completely new argument, which is surprisingly simpler; see section

.

Having the above integral equation in hand, we use a variant of the method of moving planes in the integral form to show that any positive smooth solution
u
to the above integral equation with exact growth
|x|^{2m-n}
at infinity must be radially symmetric. The symmetry of solutions to the integral equation helps us to conclude that the corresponding function
v
, appeared as in (
1.6
), must be constant. The strategy we just describe seems to be very simple and straightforward at the first glance, but there are two major difficulties that we want to highlight. First, it is worth emphasizing that the method of moving planes and its variants work well in the case of equations with positive exponents; unfortunately, our equations, both differential and integral forms, have a negative exponent. Second, by analyzing the form of
F_{\varepsilon,u}
in (
1.7
), one immediately notices that because of our special choice of perturbation, there are two powers of
u
, whose exponents have opposite sign. Unless
\varepsilon=0
, otherwise to run the method of moving planes, one needs to establish certain compactness result for solutions to (
1.1
)
ε
for suitable small
\varepsilon
, which costs us some energy.

Concerning classification of solutions to (
1.8
)
ε
with
\varepsilon=0
and with the RHS depending only on

, that is equation of the form

we refer to
[

,
Ngo18

Li04
]
and the references therein.

Finally, to illustrate our finding on a Liouville type result for solutions to (

)
ε
, we revisit the sharp critical Sobolev inequality for

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer and
m=(n+1)/2
. Then, for any
\phi\in H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})
with
\phi>0
and any
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2n+1]

\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(n/2+m)}{\Gamma(n/2-m)}|\mathbb S^{n}|^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha-1}}.

(1.9)

Moreover, the equality occurs if
\phi
is any positive constant.

Let us have some comments on Theorem
1.1
above.

Remark 1.3
.

•

Although the condition

is not required in Theorem
1.1
, but in our proof of (

being positive. In general, the inequality (

[

•

Apparently, by chosing
\alpha=(n+2m)/(2m-n)=2n+1
, our inequality (

\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi^{-\frac{2n}{2m-n}}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2m-n}{n}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(n/2+m)}{\Gamma(n/2-m)}|\mathbb S^{n}|^{\frac{2m}{n}},

which was already proved in
[

]

[

[

•

The case

\alpha=1

1.11

•

Our last comment concerns the order of the inequality (
1.9
) as
\alpha
varies. It turns out that the subcritical case

can be obtained from the critical case
\alpha=2n+1

Note that our inequality (

\Big(\fintop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\fintop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(n/2+m)}{\Gamma(n/2-m)},

where
\fintop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}:=|\mathbb S^{n}|^{-1}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}
denotes the average. Using this new form one can easily compute the limit as
\alpha\searrow 1
to obtain an inequality in the limiting case as shown in the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
n\geq 3
be an odd integer and
m=(n+1)/2
. Then, for any
\phi\in H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})
with
\phi>0
, we have the following sharp Sobolev inequality

\exp\Big(-2\fintop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\log\phi d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)\fintop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(n/2+m)}{\Gamma(n/2-m)}.

(1.11)

Moreover, the equality occurs if
\phi
is any positive constant.

It turns out that without using any limit process, one can still obtain (
1.11
) directly from (
1.9
); see Proposition
5.2
. As such, we omit the proof of (
1.11
). Without using averages, (

\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{|\mathbb S^{n}|}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\log\phi d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\phi)d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq\frac{\Gamma(n/2+m)}{\Gamma(n/2-m)}|\mathbb S^{n}|.

To the best of our knowledge, the above inequality (or the inequality (
1.11
)) seems to be new.

Our final comment concerns a possible generalization to the fractional setting. Indeed, it seems that part of our argument can be quickly extended to the case of fractional operators of order
2s>n
instead of GJMS operators of integer order
2m>n
. However, to maintain our work in a reasonable length, we leave this future research.

\pi_{S}
.

We leave the details for interested readers; also see Figure
1
above.
∎

2.2.
From differential equations to integral equations

Let
v
be a positive, smooth solution to (
1.1
)
ε
. Recall from (
1.8
)
ε
that the projected function
u
, defined by (
1.6
), solves

(-\Delta)^{m}u=\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}F_{\varepsilon,u}\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n}.

The main result of this subsection is to show that
u
actually solves the corresponding integral equation (
2.1
). To achieve this goal, we need certain preparation including the introduction of a uniform constant that we are going to describe now.

Since
n
is an odd integer, for some dimensional constant
c_{2m,n}\neq 0
we have

(-\Delta)^{m}\big(c_{2m,n}|x|^{2m-n}\big)=\delta_{0},

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac measure at the origin. For convenience, we also set

\gamma_{2m,n}:=c_{2m,n}\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}.

For simplicity, throughout the paper, we often denote by
C
a generic constant whose value could vary from estimate to estimate. We now state our main result in this subsection.

Theorem 2.2
.

We have

\gamma_{2m,n}>0

and

\displaystyle u(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u}(y)dy

(2.1)

where
F_{\varepsilon,u}
is given by (
1.7
).

Notice that the integral in (
2.1
) is well-defined everywhere in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Indeed, as
v
is positive everywhere on
\mathbb S^{n}
, we have from (
1.6
) that
u(x)\approx|x|^{2m-n}
for
|x|\gg 1
, and hence

\displaystyle(1+|x|^{2m-n})F_{\varepsilon,u}(x)\leq\frac{C}{1+|x|^{2n}}.

(2.2)

In order to prove the above theorem we define the following functions associated with the projections
\pi_{N}
and
\pi_{S}
:

u_{N}(x):=\big(\frac{1+|x|^{2}}{2}\big)^{\frac{2m-n}{2}}(v\circ\pi_{N}^{-1})(x)

and

u_{S}(x):=\big(\frac{1+|x|^{2}}{2}\big)^{\frac{2m-n}{2}}(v\circ\pi_{S}^{-1})(x)

in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. In view of the integral equation (
2.1
), we denote

\widetilde{u}_{N}(x):=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u_{N}}(y)dy

and

\widetilde{u}_{S}(x):=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u_{S}}(y)dy

in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Our aim is to show that
u_{N}\equiv\widetilde{u}_{N}
and that
\gamma_{2m,n}>0
. This will be done through several steps. Our first observation is as follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

We have

u_{S}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}u_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big),\quad u_{N}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}u_{S}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)

in
\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

This is elementary. Indeed, let us compute
u_{S}
. Clearly, with the help of Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle u_{S}(x)

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{1+|x|^{2}}{2}\big)^{\frac{2m-n}{2}}v\big(\pi_{S}^{-1}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{1+|x|^{2}}{2}\big)^{\frac{2m-n}{2}}v\big(\pi_{N}^{-1}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=|x|^{2m-n}\big(\frac{1+|x/|x|^{2}|^{2}}{2}\big)^{\frac{2m-n}{2}}v\big(\pi_{N}^{-1}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\big)

\displaystyle=|x|^{2m-n}u_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big),

which gives the desired formula for
u_{S}
. The identity for
u_{N}
can be verified similarly.
∎

Our next observation is similar to that in Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 2.4
.

We have

\widetilde{u}_{S}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}\widetilde{u}_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big),\quad\widetilde{u}_{N}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}\widetilde{u}_{S}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)

in
\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

This is also elementary but rather involved. Indeed, let us verify the first identity. With a change of variable
y=z/|z|^{2}
and by Lemma
2.3
we easily get

\displaystyle|x|^{2m-n}\widetilde{u}_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2m,n}|x|^{2m-n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Big|\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}-y\Big|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u_{N}}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2m,n}|x|^{2m-n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\Big|\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{z}{|z|^{2}}\Big|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u_{N}}(\frac{z}{|z|^{2}})\frac{dz}{|z|^{2n}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-z|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon,u_{S}}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\widetilde{u}_{S}(x),

where in the second last equality we have used the following facts:

\left|\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}-\frac{z}{|z|^{2}}\right|=\frac{|x-z|}{|x||z|},\quad F_{\varepsilon,u_{N}}(\frac{z}{|z|^{2}})=|z|^{2m+n}F_{\varepsilon,u_{S}}(z).

The second identity can be verified similarly.
∎

Now we are able to examine
u_{N}-\widetilde{u}_{N}
and
u_{S}-\widetilde{u}_{S}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

The following functions

P_{N}:=u_{N}-\widetilde{u}_{N},\quad P_{S}:=u_{S}-\widetilde{u}_{S}

are polynomials in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
of degree at most
2m-n
.

Proof.

Before proving, we see that both
P_{N}
and
P_{S}
are well-defined everywhere in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Now it follows from (
2.2
) that the function
\widetilde{u}_{N}
satisfies

\widetilde{u}_{N}(x)\leq C(1+|x|^{2m-n})\quad\text{for }x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}.

This together with the growth of
u_{N}
implies that
|P_{N}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2m-n})
. Since

\Delta^{m}P_{N}=\Delta^{m}u_{N}-\Delta^{m}\widetilde{u}_{N}=0,

we conclude that
P_{N}
is a polynomial in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
of degree at most
2m-n
; see
[
Mar09
, Theorem 5]
. A similar argument applies to
P_{S}
yielding the same conclusion for
P_{S}
.
∎

Finally, we are in a position to prove Theorem
2.2
, which simply follows from the next two lemmas.

Lemma 2.6
.

There hold
u_{N}\equiv\widetilde{u}_{N}
and
u_{S}\equiv\widetilde{u}_{S}
everywhere.

Proof.

As

u_{S}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}u_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big),\quad\widetilde{u}_{S}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}\widetilde{u}_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)

we obtain

\displaystyle P_{S}(x)=|x|^{2m-n}P_{N}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big),

which is a polynomial (of degree at most
2m-n
). Surely, as
n
is odd, this is impossible because
|x|^{2m-n}
cannot be a polynomial unless
P_{N}\equiv P_{S}\equiv 0
, which implies that
u_{N}\equiv\widetilde{u}_{N}
and
u_{S}\equiv\widetilde{u}_{S}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

There hold
\gamma_{2m,n}>0
.

Proof.

The claim
\gamma_{2m,n}>0
follows trivially by seeing its definition

\gamma_{2m,n}=c_{2m,n}Q_{n}^{2m}.

Note that
Q_{n}^{2m}>0
and that
c_{2m,n}>0
because
n<2m
and
n
is odd. However, the claim can also be seen from the fact that
v\equiv 1
is a solution to (
1.1
)
0
. More precisely, making use of
v\equiv 1
and (
1.6
) one has the following identity

\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{\frac{n-2m}{2}}=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}\big(\frac{2}{1+|y|^{2}}\big)^{\frac{n+2m}{2}}dy

everywhere in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
∎

We conclude this subsection by noting that our approach to prove Theorem
2.2
can be used for the case of equations with positive exponent. For example, without using any super polyharmonic property, as in
[
CLS22
]
, our new approach offers a very simple and straightforward proof to convert differential equations on
\mathbb S^{n}
to the corresponding integral equations on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, detail will appear elsewhere.

2.3.
Pohozaev-type identity

Our last auxiliary result is a Pohozaev-type identity, which shall be used in the proof of a compactness type result; see section
3
below. For simplicity, we let

c_{\alpha}:=\alpha\frac{2m-n}{2}-\frac{2m+n}{2}\leq 0.

(2.3)

For future usage, let us state our Pohozaev-type identity in a more general framework.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
Q\in C^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
be such that

|Q(x)|\lesssim(1+|x|)^{-n+(\alpha-1)(2m-n)-\delta},

for some
\delta>0
. Let
u
be a positive, regular solution to

\displaystyle u(x)=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}Q(y)u^{-\alpha}(y)dy,

(2.4)

where
u
satisfies

u\gtrsim(1+|x|)^{2m-n}\quad\text{if }\alpha>1

and that

u\lesssim(1+|x|)^{2m-n}\quad\text{if }0<\alpha<1.

Then, for
\alpha\neq 1
, there holds

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}(x\cdot\nabla Q)u^{1-\alpha}dx=c_{\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}Qu^{1-\alpha}dx,

provided
(x\cdot\nabla Q)u^{1-\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

The proof given below is more or less standard. As
x=(1/2)(x+y+x-y)
and

\nabla_{x}(|x-y|^{2m-n})=(2m-n)|x-y|^{2m-n-2}(x-y),

by differentiating under the integral sign in (
2.4
), we obtain

x\cdot\nabla u(x)=\frac{2m-n}{2}u(x)+\frac{2m-n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|x|^{2}-|y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2-2m}}Q(y)u^{-\alpha}(y)dy.

Multiplying the above identity by
Q(x)u^{-\alpha}(x)
, and then integrating the resultant on
B_{R}
we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}Q

\displaystyle(x\cdot\nabla u^{1-\alpha})dx=\frac{2m-n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}Qu^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle+\frac{2m-n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}Q(x)u^{-\alpha}(x)\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|x|^{2}-|y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2-2m}}Q(y)u^{-\alpha}(y)dy\Big)dx.

Integration by parts leads to

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}Q(x\cdot\nabla u^{1-\alpha})dx=

\displaystyle-\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}(x\cdot\nabla Q)u^{1-\alpha}dx-n\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}Qu^{1-\alpha}dx

\displaystyle+R\intop\nolimits_{\partial B_{R}}Qu^{1-\alpha}dx.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{R}{1-\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\partial B_{R}}

\displaystyle Qu^{1-\alpha}d\sigma-\frac{2m-n}{2}\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|x|^{2}-|y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2-2m}}Q(y)u^{-\alpha}(y)Q(x)u^{-\alpha}(x)dydx

(2.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\Big[\frac{(2m+n)-\alpha(2m-n)}{2}\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}Qu^{1-\alpha}dx+\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}(x\cdot\nabla Q)u^{1-\alpha}dx\Big].

Thanks to the decay assumption on
Q
and the growth of
u
, we easily get

\lim_{R\to\infty}\Big(R\intop\nolimits_{\partial B_{R}}Qu^{1-\alpha}d\sigma\Big)=0,

and clearly

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|x|^{2}-|y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2-2m}}Q(y)u^{-\alpha}(y)Q(x)u^{-\alpha}(x)dydx=0

due to the antisymmetry of the integrand. Furthermore, under the assumptions on
Q
and on
u
, there holds
Qu^{1-\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
. Hence, by sending
R\nearrow+\infty
, we conclude that the LHS of (
2.5
) vanishes, giving the desired identity. This completes the proof.
∎

Let us now discuss how to use our Pohozaev-type identity in the current setting. Recall that the solution
v
to (
1.1
)
ε
is positive and smooth on
\mathbb S^{n}
. Thanks to (
1.6
) we deduce that
u
enjoys the upper and lower growths as in Lemma
2.8
. Hence, we have a Pohozaev-type identity for
u
whenever
\alpha\neq 1
. We shall use this identity in the proof of Lemma
3.2
below.

3.
Compactness results

This section is devoted to a compactness type result for solutions to (
1.1
)
ε
, which is of interest itself; see Theorem
3.1
below. Heuristically, one should study the compactness result for fixed
\varepsilon
and
\alpha
. However, to derive useful estimates for our analysis, one needs certain compactness result which is independent of
\varepsilon
; see the proof of Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon^{*}\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,(2m+n)/(2m-n)]
be arbitrary but fixed. Assume that
v_{k}=v_{\varepsilon_{k}}
is a sequence of positive regular solutions to (
1.1
)
{}_{\varepsilon_{k}}
for some
\varepsilon_{k}\in(0,\varepsilon^{*})
. Then there exists
C=C(\varepsilon^{*})>0
such that

\frac{1}{C}\leq v_{k}\leq C\quad\text{in }\mathbb S^{n}

for all
k
. The same conclusion holds true for
\varepsilon_{k}\in[0,\varepsilon^{*})
if
\alpha\in(0,(2m+n)/(2m-n))
.

It is worth noting that the above compactness fails for solutions to (
1.1
)
0
in the case
\alpha=(n+2m)/(2m-n)
due to the conformally invariant property of the underlying equation. More specifically, fixing any solution
v
to

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v)=\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v^{\frac{n+2m}{2m-n}}\quad\text{in }\mathbb S^{n}

and let

v_{\phi}=(v\circ\phi)|\det(d\phi)|^{-\frac{1}{2n}},

where
\phi
is any conformal transformation on
\mathbb S^{n}
. Then, it is well-known that
v_{\phi}
solves the same equation in
\mathbb S^{n}
. Hence, if one choose a sequence of
\phi
in such a way that
|\det(d\phi)|\searrow 0
, then the sequence
v_{\phi}
is unbounded in
\mathbb S^{n}
.

In order to prove the above theorem we first need to rule out the possibility that the sequence
v_{k}
will eventually touch zero. This in particular implies the lower estimate in the theorem.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
3.1
we have

\inf_{k\geq 1}\min_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}>0.

Proof.

We assume by contradiction that the lemma is false. Then, up to a subsequence, we assume that

\min_{\mathbb S^{3}}v_{k}\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Without loss of generality we can further assume that the minimum of
v_{k}
is attained at the south pole. Let
u_{k}
be defined by (
1.6
) using
\pi_{N}
, and let
F_{k}:=F_{\varepsilon_{k},u_{k}}
as in (
1.7
). In view of (
1.6
) and
2m>n
, the function
u_{k}
achieves its minimum at
0
. By Theorem
2.2
, the function
u_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle u_{k}(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy.

(3.1)

To show that this is also not the case, we use the Pohozaev-type identity of Lemma
2.8
and the role played by
\varepsilon_{k}
and
\alpha
. Indeed, as
F_{k}>0
we first obtain

u_{k}(0)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|y|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy=o(1)_{k\to\infty}.

(3.2)

Using this one can show that

\lim_{k\to\infty}u_{k}(x)=\infty\quad\text{for each }x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

(3.3)

Indeed, by way of contradiction suppose that there is some
x_{0}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
u_{k}(x_{0})=O(1)_{k\to\infty}
. As

\displaystyle\frac{u_{k}(x_{0})}{\gamma_{2m,n}}

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x_{0}-y|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-2m+n+1}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x_{0}|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy-\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|y|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy

we obtain

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}F_{k}(y)dy=O(1)_{k\to\infty},

thanks to
u_{k}(0)=O(1)_{k\to\infty}
. Hence

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}(1+|y|^{2m-n})F_{k}(y)dy=O(1)_{k\to\infty}.

(3.4)

Consequently, for any
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, one can estimate

\displaystyle\frac{u_{k}(x)}{\gamma_{2m,n}}

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy\leq 2^{2m-n-1}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}(|x|^{2m-n}+|y|^{2m-n})F_{k}(y)dy,

which leads to

u_{k}(x)\leq C(1+|x|^{2m-n})\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n}

for some constant
C>0
. Having this, one can bound
F_{k}
from below near the origin. For example, for any
x\in B_{2}
,
we easily get

\displaystyle F_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}u_{k}(x)^{-\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{C^{\alpha}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{\frac{n+2m}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{C^{\alpha}}\Big(\frac{2}{5}\Big)^{\frac{n+2m}{2}},

thanks to
u_{k}(x)\leq C(1+|x|^{2})^{(2m-n)/2}
in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. However, this violates the fact that
u_{k}(0)=o(1)_{k\to\infty}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{u_{k}(0)}{\gamma_{2m,n}}

\displaystyle\geq\intop\nolimits_{B_{2}\setminus B_{1}}|y|^{2m-n}F_{k}(y)dy\geq\frac{1}{C^{\alpha}}\Big(\frac{2}{5}\Big)^{\frac{n+2m}{2}}\intop\nolimits_{B_{2}\setminus B_{1}}|y|^{2m-n}dy>0

for all
k
. Thus, no such a point
x_{0}
could exist, and hence (
3.3
) must hold. Notice that the above proof also reveals the fact that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}F_{k}(y)dy=\infty,

(3.5)

otherwise by (
3.2
) one would again have (
3.4
) and again this leads to a contradiction. Now we normalize
u_{k}
and
F_{k}
as follows

\widetilde{u}_{k}:=\frac{u_{k}}{\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}F_{k}dy},\quad\widetilde{F}_{k}:=\frac{F_{k}}{\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}F_{k}dy}.

Then

\widetilde{u}_{k}(x)=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}\widetilde{F}_{k}(y)dy,\quad\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\widetilde{F}_{k}dy=1.

Having (
3.5
), it is clear that
\widetilde{u}_{k}(0)\to 0
and

|\nabla\widetilde{u}_{k}(x)|\leq(2m-n)\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n-1}\widetilde{F}_{k}(y)dy\leq C(1+|x|^{2m-n-1})\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n}.

Notice that because of (
3.5
) for large
k
there holds
\widetilde{F}_{k}(x)\leq F_{k}(x)
everywhere. This and (
3.2
) now implies the following

\lim_{k\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\delta}}\widetilde{F}_{k}(y)dy\to 0\quad\text{for any fixed }\delta>0.

Once we have the above limit in hand and seeing
\widetilde{u}
as a convolution, by standard argument, we get that

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{k}\to\widetilde{u}:=|x|^{2m-n}\quad\text{in }C^{0}_{\rm loc}(\mathbf{R}^{n})

(3.6)

and at the same time

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}|x|^{2m-n}\leq\widetilde{u}_{k}\leq C|x|^{2m-n}\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}

(3.7)

for some
C>0
. Notice that we can write
F_{k}
as

F_{k}=\left(\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m}u_{k}^{1+\alpha}+f^{-c_{\alpha}}\right)u_{k}^{-\alpha}=:Q_{k}u_{k}^{-\alpha},

where we denote

f(x):=\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}.

By the Pohozaev-type identity in Lemma
2.8
, we get

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}(x\cdot\nabla Q_{k})u_{k}^{1-\alpha}dx=c_{\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}Q_{k}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}dx.

(3.8)

(Here, the multiplicative constant
\gamma_{2m,n}\neq 0
cancels out from the both sides, thanks to Theorem
2.2
.) Let us first compute

\nabla\big(\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m}u_{k}^{1+\alpha}\big)=2m\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m-1}u_{k}^{1+\alpha}\nabla f+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m}u_{k}^{\alpha-1}\nabla u_{k}^{2}

and

\nabla(f^{-c_{\alpha}})=-c_{\alpha}f^{-c_{\alpha}-1}\nabla f,

leading us to

x\cdot\nabla Q_{k}=\Big[\left(2m\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m-1}u_{k}^{2}-c_{\alpha}f^{-c_{\alpha}-1}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}\right)(x\cdot\nabla f)+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m}(x\cdot\nabla u_{k}^{2})\Big]u_{k}^{\alpha-1}.

Therefore, from (
3.8
) we get

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\big[\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m}u_{k}^{2}+f^{-c_{\alpha}}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}\big]dx

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\big[2m\varepsilon_{k}f^{2m-1}u_{k}^{2}-c_{\alpha}f^{-c_{\alpha}-1}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}\big](x\cdot\nabla f)dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\varepsilon_{k}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{2m}(x\cdot\nabla u_{k}^{2})dx

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}m\varepsilon_{k}(1-\alpha)f^{2m-1}u_{k}^{2}(x\cdot\nabla f)dx

\displaystyle\quad+\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\varepsilon_{k}\frac{1+\alpha}{2}u_{k}^{2}(x\cdot\nabla f^{2m})dx

\displaystyle\quad-c_{\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{-c_{\alpha}-1}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}(x\cdot\nabla f)dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\varepsilon_{k}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{2m}(x\cdot\nabla u_{k}^{2})dx.

By integration by parts, we note that

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\big[u_{k}^{2}(x\cdot\nabla f^{2m})

\displaystyle+f^{2m}(x\cdot\nabla u_{k}^{2})\big]dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}\big[-u_{k}^{2}f^{2m}\big]dx+\frac{1}{R}\intop\nolimits_{\partial B_{R}}x_{i}^{2}f^{2m}u_{k}^{2}d\sigma\Big]

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\Big[-n\intop\nolimits_{B_{R}}u_{k}^{2}f^{2m}dx+R\intop\nolimits_{\partial B_{R}}f^{2m}u_{k}^{2}d\sigma\Big]

\displaystyle=-n\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{2m}u_{k}^{2}dx.

Putting the above estimates together we arrive at

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{2m-1}u_{k}^{2}\Big[m(1-\alpha)(x\cdot\nabla f)

\displaystyle-\Big(\frac{n(1+\alpha)}{2}+c_{\alpha}\Big)f\Big]dx

(3.9)

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{-c_{\alpha}-1}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}\left(x\cdot\nabla f+f\right)dx.

Since

x\cdot\nabla f+f=f\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}},

and

m(1-\alpha)+n\frac{1+\alpha}{2}+c_{\alpha}=0,

the identity (
3.9
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}m(1-\alpha)\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{2m}u_{k}^{2}\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}dx=c_{\alpha}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{-c_{\alpha}}u_{k}^{1-\alpha}\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}dx.

(3.10)

Our next step is to show that for large
k
, the two integrals in (
3.10
) are non-zero with different sign.

Estimate of the LHS of (
3.10
)
.
Concerning the integral on the LHS of (
3.10
), a simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{k}^{2}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{2m}(x)

\displaystyle u_{k}^{2}(x)\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}(x)\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Big(\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}(x)-|x|^{4m-2n}\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\Big)dx,

here we have converted the integral on
\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
into
B_{1}
using Kelvin’s transformation. In
B_{1}\setminus\{0\}
, it follows from (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) that

\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}(x)-|x|^{4m-2n}\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\to|x|^{4m-2n}-1\leq 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Notice that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}

\displaystyle\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Big(\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}(x)-|x|^{4m-2n}\widetilde{u}_{k}^{2}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\Big)dx

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\left(|x|^{4m-2n}-1\right)dx<0.

This and
\varepsilon_{k}>0
imply that the LHS of (
3.10
) is strictly negative for large
k
.

Estimate of the RHS of (
3.10
)
. Reasoning as in the previous step we should have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{k}^{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}f^{-c_{\alpha}}(x)u_{k}^{1-\alpha}(x)\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}dx

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Big(\widetilde{u}_{k}^{1-\alpha}(x)-|x|^{-2c_{\alpha}-2n}\widetilde{u}_{k}^{1-\alpha}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\Big)dx.

In
B_{1}
, it follows from (
3.6
) that

\widetilde{u}_{k}^{1-\alpha}(x)-|x|^{-2c_{\alpha}-2n}\widetilde{u}_{k}^{1-\alpha}\big(\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}\big)\to|x|^{(2m-n)(1-\alpha)}-1\geq 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Now observe that for
\alpha>1

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\frac{1-|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\big(|x|^{(2m-n)(1-\alpha)}-1\big)dx>0,

which imply that the the RHS of (
3.10
) is strictly positive for large
k
(for certain
\alpha>1
, the preceding integral could be infinity). Now going back to (
3.10
), we easily obtain a contradiction for
\alpha>1
. Indeed, we have two possible cases. First, if
\varepsilon_{k}>0
for large
k
, then as
\varepsilon_{k}m(1-\alpha)<0
, the LHS of (
3.10
) becomes strictly positive. However, as
c_{\alpha}\leq 0
, the RHS of (
3.10
) becomes non-positive. This is a contradiction. In contrary, we have
\varepsilon_{k}=0
for a sequence of
k
. However, under
\varepsilon_{k}=0
the LHS of (
3.10
) vanishes but as
c_{\alpha}<0
the RHS of (
3.10
) becomes strictly negative. This is again a contradiction. And this completes our proof of the compactness for
\alpha>1
.

Finally we consider the case
0<\alpha\leq 1
. We set

\eta_{k}(x):=\frac{u_{k}(r_{k}x)}{u_{k}(0)},\quad r_{k}:=u_{k}(0)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2m}}\to 0.

Then
\eta_{k}
satisfies
\eta_{k}\geq\eta_{k}(0)=1
, and

\displaystyle\eta_{k}(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2m-n}\left(\varepsilon_{k}r_{k}^{2m}f^{2m}(r_{k}y)\eta_{k}(y)+\frac{f^{-c_{\alpha}}(r_{k}y)}{\eta_{k}^{\alpha}(y)}\right)dy.

(3.11)

Then it follows that

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|y|^{2m-n}\left(\varepsilon_{k}r_{k}^{2m}f^{2m}(r_{k}y)\eta_{k}(y)+\frac{f^{-c_{\alpha}}(r_{k}y)}{\eta_{k}^{\alpha}(y)}\right)dy=\frac{\eta_{k}(0)}{\gamma_{2m,n}}\leq C,

(3.12)

and together with
\eta_{k}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left(1+|y|^{2m-n}\right)\frac{f^{-c_{\alpha}}(r_{k}y)}{\eta_{k}^{\alpha}(y)}dy\leq C.

(3.13)

Therefore,

\eta_{k}(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\varepsilon_{k}r_{k}^{2m}\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}|x-y|^{2m-n}f^{2m}(r_{k}y)\eta_{k}(y)dy+O(1)\quad\text{for }x\in B_{1}.

Integrating the above identity with respect to
x
in
B_{1}
, and using that
f(r_{k}y)=2+o(1)
on
B_{1}
, we obtain

\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\eta_{k}(x)dx=o(1)\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\eta_{k}(y)dy+O(1),

and hence

\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}\eta_{k}dx\leq C.

Combining the above estimates

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\left(1+|y|^{2m-n}\right)\left(\varepsilon_{k}r_{k}^{2m}f^{2m}(r_{k}y)\eta_{k}(y)+\frac{f^{-c_{\alpha}}(r_{k}y)}{\eta_{k}^{\alpha}(y)}\right)dy\leq C.

(3.14)

This yields

\displaystyle|\nabla\eta_{k}(x)|\leq C(1+|x|^{2m-n-1}),\quad\frac{1}{C}\left(1+|x|^{2m-n}\right)\leq\eta_{k}(x)\leq C\left(1+|x|^{2m-n}\right).

(3.15)

Hence, up to a subsequence,

\eta_{k}\to\eta\quad\text{in }C^{0}_{loc}(\mathbf{R}^{n}).

From Fatou’s lemma, we get that

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|y|^{2m-n}}{\eta^{\alpha}(y)}dy<\infty,

thanks to (
3.15
). Since
\eta
satisfies the second estimate in (
3.15
), we necessarily have that

\displaystyle(\alpha-1)(2m-n)>n,

a contradiction to
0<\alpha\leq 1
.
∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Since
\varepsilon_{k}\in[0,\varepsilon^{*})
and
0<\varepsilon^{*}<1
, integrating (
1.1
) on
\mathbb S^{n}
we get that

0\leq\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon^{*}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}^{-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}=O(1)_{k\to\infty},

thanks to Lemma
3.2
. Therefore, we arrive at

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{k})-\varepsilon_{k}\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{k}=O(1)_{k\to\infty}\quad\text{in }\mathbb S^{n}

with
\|v_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb S^{n})}=O(1)_{k\to\infty}
. The theorem follows from standard elliptic estimates.
∎

Throughout this section we let

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x):=u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}.

To start moving planes, the following lemma is often required.

Lemma 4.1
.

There hold

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\big[|x-y|^{2m-n}-|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}\big]F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

(4.1)

and

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x)=\gamma_{2m,n}\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}\big[|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}-|x-y|^{2m-n}\big][F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy

(4.2)

for any
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}
.

Proof.

The first identity is obvious from the definition of
w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}
. The second identity follows from variable changes. Indeed, one can write

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}+\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus\Sigma_{\lambda}}\Big)|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy+\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}|x-y^{\lambda}|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})dy

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy+\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})dy.

Similarly, one has

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy+\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}|x-y|^{2m-n}F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})dy.

By putting the above identities together we arrive at the second identity.
∎

Our next step is to show that the method of moving planes can start from a very large
\lambda_{0}>0
, where
\lambda_{0}
is independent of
\varepsilon
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\varepsilon^{*}\in(0,1)
be fixed. Then
there exists
\lambda_{0}\gg 1
such that for every
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon^{*}]
we have

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x)\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda}

for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
.

Proof.

We start the proof by observing the existence of some constant
C>0
such that for each
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon^{*}]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\frac{1}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}\leq F_{\varepsilon}(y)\leq C\frac{1}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}\quad\text{in }\mathbf{R}^{n};

(4.3)

see (
2.2
) for a similar estimate. In the case
\varepsilon>0
, this simply follows from the uniform bound for
v_{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon^{*}]
as given by Theorem
3.1
. In the case
\varepsilon=0
, the above estimate is trivial because
u(x)\approx|x|^{2m-n}
for
|x|\gg 1
. By a simple algebraic computations we have

\displaystyle|x-y|^{2m-n}-|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}=\frac{|x-y|^{2}-|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2m-n}+|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}}\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}(x,y),

where the function
\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}
is given by

\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}(x,y):=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{2m-n-1}|x-y|^{2(2m-n-1-k)}|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2k}.

(It is clear that
\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}\equiv 1
if
2m-n=1
.) Using (
4.1
) and

|x-y|^{2}-|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2}=4(x_{1}-\lambda)(\lambda-y_{1})

we can write

|x|^{2+n-2m}\frac{w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x)}{x_{1}-\lambda}=\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}(\lambda-y_{1})P_{\lambda}(x,y)F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy=:U_{\varepsilon}(x),

where

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x,y):=4\gamma_{2m,n}\frac{|x|^{2+n-2m}}{|x-y|^{2m-n}+|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}}\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}(x,y).

(4.4)

For later use, we note that for
x,y\in\Sigma_{\lambda}
there holds

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{2+n-2m}\frac{|x-y|^{2(2m-n-1)}+|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2(2m-n-1)}}{|x-y|^{2m-n}+|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}&&\text{for }2m-n=1\\
&1+|x|^{2+n-2m}|y|^{2m-n-2}&&\text{for }2m-n\geq 3\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}&&\text{for }2m-n=1\\
&|y|^{2m-n-2}&&\text{for }2m-n\geq 3.\end{aligned}\right.

To conclude the lemma, it suffices to show the existence of
\lambda_{0}\gg 1
such that

U_{\varepsilon}(x)>0\quad\text{for any }x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\cup T_{\lambda}

and for every
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
. With the help of (
4.3
) we can roughly estimate

\displaystyle U_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}(\lambda-y_{1})P_{\lambda}(x,y)F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy+\intop\nolimits_{\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}(\lambda-y_{1})P_{\lambda}(x,y)F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C}\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}(\lambda-y_{1})P_{\lambda}(x,y)dy+\intop\nolimits_{y_{1}>\lambda}(\lambda-y_{1})P_{\lambda}(x,y)F_{\varepsilon}(y)dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C}\intop\nolimits_{B_{1}}(\lambda-y_{1})P_{\lambda}(x,y)dy-C\intop\nolimits_{y_{1}>\lambda}\frac{P_{\lambda}(x,y)}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}dy

\displaystyle=:I_{1}(x)-I_{2}(x).

Here to get the term
I_{2}
we have used the estimates
0\leq y_{1}-\lambda\leq y_{1}\leq|y|
in the region
\{y\in\mathbf{R}^{n}:y_{1}>\lambda\}
and

\frac{|y|}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}\leq\frac{2}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}\quad\text{for all }y.

Next, we estimate
I_{1}
from below and
I_{2}
from above. For
I_{1}
, we note that

P_{\lambda}(x,y)\geq\frac{1}{C}\quad\text{for }y\in B_{1},\,x\in\Sigma_{\lambda},\,\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}\gg 1.

From this we deduce

I_{1}(x)\geq\frac{\lambda}{C}.

We now estimate
I_{2}
. For
2m-n\geq 3
and as

\frac{|y|^{2m-n-2}}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}\leq\frac{2}{1+|y|^{2n+1}}\quad\text{for all }y

and
|y|\geq y_{1}>\lambda
we can estimate

\displaystyle I_{2}(x)\leq C\intop\nolimits_{y_{1}>\lambda}\frac{|y|^{2m-n-2}dy}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}\leq C\intop\nolimits_{y_{1}>\lambda}\frac{dy}{1+|y|^{2n+1}}\leq\frac{C}{\lambda^{n+1}}\leq C.

For
2m-n=1
, we split
\{y_{1}>\lambda\}
as follows

\{y_{1}>\lambda\}\subset A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3}

where

A_{1}:=\big\{y:\lambda<|y|\leq|x|/2\big\},\quad A_{2}:=B_{2|x|}\setminus B_{|x|/2},\quad A_{3}:=\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2|x|}.

(Although
|x|>\lambda
as
x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}
, the set
A_{1}
could be empty if
|x|<2\lambda
, but it is not important.) Since
|x-y|\geq|x|/2
on
A_{1}\cup A_{3}
and again
|y|\geq y_{1}>\lambda
, we can estimate

\intop\nolimits_{A_{1}\cup A_{3}}\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}\frac{dy}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}\leq\frac{C}{\lambda^{2m-1}}.

On the remaining set
A_{2}
as
|x|/2\leq|y|\leq 2|x|
we easily get

\intop\nolimits_{A_{2}}\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}\frac{dy}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{2m+n-2}}\intop\nolimits_{A_{2}}\frac{dy}{|x-y|}\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{2m-1}}\leq\frac{C}{\lambda^{2m-1}}\leq C.

Putting the above estimate together, we arrive at

U_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq I_{1}(x)-I_{2}(x)\geq\frac{\lambda}{C}-C

for some constant
C>0
. Thus, the lemma follows by letting
\lambda_{0}
large enough.
∎

In Lemma
4.2
, we have compared
u_{\varepsilon}(x)
and
u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})
, via
w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x)
, in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. As there was no restriction on
\varepsilon^{*}\in(0,1)
, our comparison requires large

F_{\varepsilon}(x)

in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
. As there will be no restriction on

, our comparison now requires small
\varepsilon>0
, and this is the place where the constant
\varepsilon_{*}
appears. Due to the form of
F_{\varepsilon}
to achieve the goal we need the compactness result established earlier; see section

.

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{*}\in(0,\varepsilon^{*})
small enough such that for arbitrary
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}]
but fixed, the conclusion if

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda},

(4.6)

then

F_{\varepsilon}(x)-F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})\leq 0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda}

(4.7)

holds for each
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{*})
. In addition, if the inequality (
4.6
) is strict, then so is the inequality (
4.7
).

Proof.

Let us first be interested in the existence of
\varepsilon_{*}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{*})
. As
|x^{\lambda}|<|x|
for
\lambda>0
and
x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x)-F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}u_{\varepsilon}(x)-\varepsilon\big(\frac{2}{1+|x^{\lambda}|^{2}}\big)^{2m}u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)}-\big(\frac{2}{1+|x^{\lambda}|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda}))

\displaystyle\quad+\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\Big(\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)}-\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}\Big),

where the constant
c_{\alpha}\leq 0
is already given in (
2.3
). Hence, to prove (
4.7
) in
\Sigma_{\lambda}
, it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}{u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})}\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}\geq\varepsilon\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{(2m-n)\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda},

(4.8)

where we have used that

2m+c_{\alpha}=(2m-n)\frac{1+\alpha}{2}.

To this end, for some
R\gg 1
to be specified later, we first split
\Sigma_{\lambda}
into two parts as follows:

\Sigma_{\lambda}=\big[\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}\big]\cup\big[\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R}\big].

In the region
\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R}
, there exists some
\varepsilon_{1}>0
such that (
4.8
) holds. To see this we need to use uniform bounds with respect to
\varepsilon>0
, see Theorem
3.1
, to obtain

\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}{u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})}\geq\varepsilon_{1}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{(2m-n)\frac{1-\alpha}{2}}

and

\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}\geq\varepsilon_{1}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{\alpha}

for some small
\varepsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
. This is mainly because when
R
is large enough, we have
|x|\approx|x^{\lambda}|
for
|x|>R
and
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0}]
. In the region
\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R}
, by the smoothness of
u_{\varepsilon}
, there exists some small
\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}{u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\lambda})}\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}\geq\varepsilon_{2}\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{(2m-n)\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}

(4.9)

for any
x\in B_{R}
. Hence, combining (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) yields the desired estimate (
4.7
) with

\varepsilon_{*}=\frac{1}{2}\min\{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\}.

Now we consider the remaining case
\varepsilon=0
. However, this case is trivial because

\displaystyle F_{0}(x)-F_{0}(x^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\Big(\frac{1}{u_{0}^{\alpha}(x)}-\frac{1}{u_{0}^{\alpha}(x^{\lambda})}\Big)\leq 0

whenever
w_{0,\lambda}(x)=u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(x^{\lambda})\geq 0
. Finally, from the above calculation, it is clear that if the inequality (
4.6
) is strict, then the inequality (
4.7
) is also strict. Hence, the lemma is proved.
∎

Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, for each
\varepsilon>0
we can set

\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\big\{\lambda>0:w_{\varepsilon,\mu}\geq 0\;\text{ in }\;\Sigma_{\mu}\;\text{ for every }\;\mu\geq\lambda\big\}.

Then, still by Lemma
4.2
, we necessarily have

0\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}\leq\lambda_{0}.

Our goal is to show that
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}=0
. This can be done through two steps. First we show that if
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
, then we must have
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\equiv 0
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
; see Lemma
4.5
. Finally, we show that
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}=0
; see Lemma
4.6
.

Our next lemma is of importance to achieve the first step as it allows us to move
\lambda
to the left.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{*})
and
\bar{\lambda}\in(0,\lambda_{0}]
be such that

0\nequiv w_{\varepsilon,\bar{\lambda}}\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\bar{\lambda}}.

Then, there exist
R\gg 1
and
\delta>0
small, both may depend on
w_{\varepsilon,\bar{\lambda}}
, such that for every
\lambda\in(\bar{\lambda}-\delta,\bar{\lambda})
we have

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}>0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R}.

Proof.

Using the representation (
4.2
), and as in the first part of the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(x)\frac{|x|^{2+n-2m}}{x_{1}-\lambda}

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(y_{1}-\lambda)P_{\lambda}(x,y)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy,

(4.10)

where
P_{\lambda}
is given by (
4.4
). In view of (
4.10
), it suffices to show that its RHS is positive in
\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R}
for suitable
R>0
. For convenience, we recall the following formula for
P_{\lambda}

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x,y)=4\gamma_{2m,n}\frac{|x|^{2+n-2m}}{|x-y|^{2m-n}+|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2m-n}}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{2m-n-1}|x-y|^{2(2m-n-1-k)}|x^{\lambda}-y|^{2k}.

Hence, there exists some
\theta>0
such that for every
R_{1}>0
fixed

\displaystyle P_{\lambda}(x,y)\rightrightarrows\theta\quad\text{ uniformly in }y\in B_{R_{1}}

(4.11)

as
|x|\to\infty
. This is because
|x|\approx|x-y|\approx|x^{\lambda}-y|
for large
|x|
. From (
4.7
) we know that

0\nequiv F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\bar{\lambda}})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)\geq 0\quad\text{for }y\in\Sigma_{\bar{\lambda}},

which implies

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\bar{\lambda}}}(y_{1}-\bar{\lambda})[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\bar{\lambda}})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\geq 2c_{0}>0,

for some small constant
c_{0}>0
. Thus, by the dominated convergence theorem, we can find some
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}(y_{1}-\lambda)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\geq c_{0}>0,

(4.12)

for every
|\lambda-\bar{\lambda}|<\delta
. To obtain the positivity of the right hand side of (
4.10
), we split the integral
\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}
into two parts as follows

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}}=\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}+\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R_{2}}}

for some
R_{2}>0
to be determined later and estimate these integrals term by term; see the two estimates (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) below. Our aim is to show that the integral
\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}
is negligible.

We assume for a moment that such a constant
R_{2}
exists. We now estimate the integral
\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}
. First we choose a
R_{0}\gg 1
in such a way that
|y_{1}-\lambda|<2|y|
for all
|y|\geq R_{0}
. Then we find some
R_{1}\gg R_{0}
such that in
\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{1}}
we have

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{1}}}\frac{dy}{1+|y|^{2m+n-1}}\leq\frac{\theta c_{0}}{16C}

(4.13)

and

F_{\varepsilon}(y)+F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})\leq\frac{C}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}

for some
C>0
because
|y|\approx|y^{\lambda}|
. By the estimate (
4
) for
P_{\lambda}
, we now claim that there are some
R_{3}\gg 1
and
R_{2}\gg R_{1}
such that

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}(y_{1}-\lambda)P_{\lambda}(x,y)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})+F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\leq\frac{\theta c_{0}}{4}

(4.14)

for
|x|\geq R_{3}
. To see this, for clarity, we consider the two cases
2m-n=1
and
2m-n\geq 3
separately.

Case 1
. Suppose
2m-n=1
. In this case our estimate for
P_{\lambda}
becomes
P_{\lambda}(x,y)\leq C|x|/|x-y|
. Consequently, there holds

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}(y_{1}-\lambda)P_{\lambda}(x,y)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})+F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\leq C\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}\frac{|y|}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}dy.

For
|x|\geq R_{3}\gg 2R_{2}
to be determined later, we now split
\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}
as follows

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}=\intop\nolimits_{[\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}]\cap[B_{|x|/2}\cup(\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2|x|})]}+\intop\nolimits_{[\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}]\setminus[B_{|x|/2}\cup(\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2|x|})]}.

Thanks to (
4.13
), we get

C\intop\nolimits_{[\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}]\cap[B_{|x|/2}\cup(\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2|x|})]}\frac{|x|}{|x-y|}\frac{|y|}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}dy<\frac{\theta c_{0}}{8}.

For the remaining integral on
[\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}]\setminus[B_{|x|/2}\cup(\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2|x|})]
which is a subset of
B_{2|x|}\setminus B_{|x|/2}
because
|x|\geq 2R_{2}
, we estimate as follows

C\intop\nolimits_{[\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}]\setminus[B_{|x|/2}\cup(\mathbf{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2|x|})]}\leq\frac{C|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2m+n}}\intop\nolimits_{B_{2|x|}\setminus B_{|x|/2}}\frac{dy}{|x-y|}.

Since the last integral is of order
|x|^{n}
and
m\geq 2
we can find some
R_{3}\gg 1
such that

\frac{C|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2m+n}}\intop\nolimits_{B_{2|x|}\setminus B_{|x|/2}}\frac{dy}{|x-y|}\leq\frac{\theta c_{0}}{8}

for all
x\in\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R_{3}}
. Combining the two estimates above gives (
4.14
). This completes the first case.

Case 2
. Suppose
2m-n\geq 3
. This case is easy to handle. Recall that our estimate for
P_{\lambda}
becomes
P_{\lambda}(x,y)\leq C|y|^{2m-n-2}
. Consequently, there holds

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}(y_{1}-\lambda)P_{\lambda}(x,y)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})+F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\leq C\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R_{2}}}\frac{|y|^{2m-n-1}}{1+|y|^{2m+n}}dy.

Seeing (
4.13
) or as in the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we easily obtain the desired estimate.

Hence, up to this point, we have already shown that there are some
R_{2}\gg 1
and
R_{3}\gg 1
such that the estimate (
4.14
) holds for
|x|\geq R_{3}
. Now we estimate the integral
\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R_{2}}}
. Keep using the constant
R_{2}
. By the uniform convergence in (
4.11
), we can choose
R_{4}\gg R_{2}
such that

P_{\lambda}(x,y)\geq\frac{1}{2}\theta\quad\text{for }|x|\geq R_{4}\text{ and }|y|\leq R_{2}.

This and (
4.12
) imply that

\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\lambda}\cap B_{R_{2}}}(y_{1}-\lambda)P_{\lambda}(x,y)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\lambda})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\geq\frac{\theta c_{0}}{2}

(4.15)

for
|x|\geq R_{4}
. We conclude the lemma by combing the two estimates (
4.14
) and (
4.15
) and choosing
R=\max\{R_{3},R_{4}\}
.
∎

We are now in a position to complete the first step, namely, to show that
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}=0
. To this purpose, we must rule out the case
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
and this is the content of the next two lemmas. First, we characterize the function
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
in case
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
for some
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{*})
, then
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\equiv 0
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
. In other words, the function
u_{\varepsilon}
is symmetric with respect to the hyperplane
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}:x_{1}=\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}\}
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
for some
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{*})
and assume by contradiction that
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\nequiv 0
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
.
This and the definition of
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}
imply that

0\nequiv w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}.

By Lemma
4.4
, there exist
R\gg 1
and
\delta>0
small enough such that

w_{\varepsilon,\lambda}>0\quad\text{in }\Sigma_{\lambda}\setminus B_{R}\quad\text{for every }\lambda\in(\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}-\delta,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}).

Then there exists a sequence
\mu_{k}\nearrow\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}
such that
w_{\varepsilon,\mu_{k}}
is negative somewhere in
\Sigma_{\mu_{k}}
. Since outside
B_{R}
, the function
w_{\varepsilon,\mu_{k}}
is strictly positive, for each
k
there is some
x_{k}\in\Sigma_{\mu_{k}}\cap\overline{B_{R}}
such that

w_{\varepsilon,\mu_{k}}(x_{k})=\min_{\Sigma_{\mu_{k}}}w_{\varepsilon,\mu_{k}}<0.

In particular, there holds

\frac{w_{\varepsilon,\mu_{k}}(x_{k})}{(x_{k})_{1}-\mu_{k}}<0.

Obviously, the sequence
(x_{k})
is bounded as
x_{k}\in\overline{B_{R}}
. Also note that
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\subset\Sigma_{\mu_{k}}
and
\Sigma_{\mu_{k}}\searrow\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
as
k\nearrow+\infty
. Therefore, up to a subsequence, we have

\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\cup T_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\ni x_{\infty}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}.

In particular, by passing to the limit as
k\to\infty
, there holds
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\infty})\leq 0
. This and (
4.10
) implies that

\displaystyle 0\geq w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\infty})\frac{|x_{\infty}|^{2+n-2m}}{(x_{\infty})_{1}-\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}}(y_{1}-\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon})P_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}(x_{\infty},y)[F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy\geq 0,

thanks to
|x_{\infty}|>0
and
F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}})\geq F_{\varepsilon}(y)
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
by Lemma
4.3
.
Thus, we must have

F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\bar{\lambda}\varepsilon})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)=0\quad\text{for any }y\in\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}},

which, by (
4.10
), now yields
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\equiv 0
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
. However, this is a contradiction. Once we have
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}\equiv 0
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
, the symmetry of
u_{\varepsilon}
follows from the definition of
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
. The proof is complete.
∎

From the characterization of
w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
in the case
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
and the role of the size of
\varepsilon
and
\alpha
, we are able to show that in fact the case
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
cannot happen.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{*})
. There holds
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}=0
. In particular, the function
u_{\varepsilon}
is symmetric with respect to the hyperplane
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}:x_{1}=0\}
.

Proof.

By way of contradiction, assume that
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}>0
. In view of Lemma
4.5
, we must have

0=w_{\varepsilon,\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}(x)=u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}})

in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
. This and (
4.2
) tell us that

\displaystyle\intop\nolimits_{\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}}\big[|x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}-y|^{2m-n}-|x-y|^{2m-n}\big][F_{\varepsilon}(y^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}})-F_{\varepsilon}(y)]dy=0

for any
x\in\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
, thanks to
\gamma_{2m,n}\neq 0
, see Theorem
2.2
. But this cannot happen because
|x-y|\leq|x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}-y|
for any
x,y\in\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
and

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x)-F_{\varepsilon}(x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\Big[\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{2m}-\big(\frac{2}{1+|x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}}\big)^{2m}\Big]u_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big[\big(\frac{2}{1+|x|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}-\big(\frac{2}{1+|x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}|^{2}}\big)^{-c_{\alpha}}\Big]\frac{1}{u_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}(x)}

\displaystyle<0,

everywhere in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
, thanks to the estimates
u_{\varepsilon}>0
,
-c_{\alpha}\geq 0
, and
|x|\leq|x^{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}|
in
\Sigma_{\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}}
. (Here we also use the fact that if
\varepsilon=0
, then
\alpha<(n+2m)/(2m-n)
in order to guarantee
-c_{\alpha}>0
.) Thus, we must have
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}=0
. In particular, we have from the definition of
\overline{\lambda}_{\varepsilon}
the following

u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\geq u_{\varepsilon}(-x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}).

We now apply the method of moving planes in the opposite direction, namely
\lambda<0
, to get

u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\leq u_{\varepsilon}(-x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}).

Hence

u_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})=u_{\varepsilon}(-x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}).

This establishes the symmetry of
u_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the hyperplane
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}:x_{1}=0\}
. The proof is now complete.
∎

We now have a quick note. In the proof of Lemma
4.6
above, we crucially use the hypothesis that either
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\leq(n+2m)/(n-2m)
or
\varepsilon=0
and
\alpha<(n+2m)/(n-2m)
. For the latter case, if
\varepsilon=0
and
\alpha=(n+2m)/(n-2m)
, then we cannot claim that
\overline{\lambda}_{0}=0
. Therefore, we could only claim that
u_{0}
is radially symmetric with respect to some point not necessarily the origin. This leads to explicit form of non-trivial solutions to (
1.1
)
0
in the conformaly invariant case.

As a consequence of Lemma
4.6
above, we obtain a Liouville type result for positive, smooth solution to (
1.1
)
ε
for small
\varepsilon>0
, hence proving Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Any positive, smooth solution
v_{\varepsilon}
to (
1.1
)
ε
for small
\varepsilon
must be constant.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{*})
be arbitrary. From Lemma
4.6
we know that the corresponding solution
u_{\varepsilon}
is symmetric with respect to the hyperplane
\{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}:x_{1}=0\}
. This together with the relation

u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\Big(\frac{1+|x|^{2}}{2}\Big)^{\frac{2m-n}{2}}\big(v_{\varepsilon}\circ\pi_{N}^{-1}\big)(x)

tells us that
v_{\varepsilon}
depends only on the last coordinate
x_{n+1}
. However, as the
x_{n+1}
-axis is freely chosen, we conclude that
v_{\varepsilon}
must be constant. This completes the proof.
∎

Before closing this section, we have a remark. To obtain the symmetry of solutions to (
1.1
)
ε
for small
\varepsilon
, our approach is based on the method of moving planes in the integral form. A natural question is weather or not one can use the method of moving spheres; see
[
LZ95
,
Li04
]
. Due to the presence of the weight
2/(1+|x|^{2})
in (
1.7
), it is natural to ask whether or not the method of moving spheres can still be used. Toward a possible answer to this question, we refer the reader to the work
[
JLX08
]
.

5.
Application to the sharp Sobolev inequality

This section is devoted to a proof of Theorem
1.2
which concerns a sharp (critical or subcritical) Sobolev inequality. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and inspired by (
1.4
) consider the following variational problem

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}=\inf_{0<\phi\in H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})}\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\phi^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[\phi\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(\phi)-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}\phi^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}

(5.1)

with
m=(n+1)/2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2n+1]
. We note that although the constant
(n-2m)/2
becomes
-1/2
in the present case, we intent to keep it in various calculation below for convenience. Similar convention also applies for
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}
instead of
\mathbf{P}_{n}^{n+1}
, etc. Now as

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(1)-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}\neq 0

by testing (
5.1
) with constant functions we conclude from (
5.1
) that

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}|\mathbb S^{n}|^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha-1}}<0,

however,
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
could be
-\infty
. Next we show that
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
is finite and is achieved by some smooth positive function.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
m=(n+1)/2
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2n+1]
. Then, the constant
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
in (
5.1
) is finite and there exists some
v_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb S^{n})
such that
v_{\varepsilon}>0
and

\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[v_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}=\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}.

In particular,
v_{\varepsilon}
solves

\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{\varepsilon}=S_{\varepsilon}v_{\varepsilon}^{-\alpha}

in
\mathbb S^{n}
with

S_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}}{\|v_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\|_{L^{\alpha-1}(\mathbb S^{n})}^{\alpha+1}}.

Proof.

Let
(v_{k})_{k}
be a positive, smooth minimizing sequence in
H^{2m}(\mathbb S^{n})
, that is

\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[v_{k}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{k})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{k}^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\searrow\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}

as
k\to\infty
. By the scaling invariant we can assume
\max_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}=1
which then yields

\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb S^{n})}^{2}\leq|\mathbb S^{n}|.

As
\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}
is a monic polynomial of
-\Delta_{g_{\mathbb S^{n}}}
, the coefficient of the highest degree is equal to
1
, it is easy to get that

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{k})d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq c_{1}\|v_{k}\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})}^{2}-c_{2}\|v_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb S^{n})}^{2}\geq c_{1}\|v_{k}\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})}^{2}-c_{2}|\mathbb S^{n}|

for some
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}<0
and
Q^{2m}_{n}>0
would imply

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}v_{k}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}<0.

Therefore, the previous estimate leads to

c_{1}\|v_{k}\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})}^{2}\leq c_{2}|\mathbb S^{n}|,

giving the boundedness of the sequence
(v_{k})
in
H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})
. Hence, after passing to a subsequence if necessary, there exists some
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n})
such that

v_{k}\to v_{\varepsilon}\geq 0\text{ uniformly in }C(\mathbb S^{n})

by Morrey’s inequality and the Arzelà–Ascoli lemma, and

v_{k}\rightharpoonup v_{\varepsilon}\text{ weakly in }H^{m}(\mathbb S^{n}).

In particular, there holds
\max_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}=1
. As
v_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, there are two possibilities. First, let us assume that
v_{\varepsilon}
vanishes somewhere on
\mathbb S^{n}
. By assuming this we shall obtain a contradiction, therefore we must have
v_{\varepsilon}>0
. Indeed, as
n=2m-1
, we can make use of
[
Han07
, Corollary 3.1]
to conclude that

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{\varepsilon})d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\geq 0.

This together with
\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}<0
and
\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}>0
help us to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[v_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\nearrow+\infty}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[v_{k}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{k})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{k}^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}.

This is a contradiction to
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}<0
. Thus,
v_{\varepsilon}>0
everywhere. Then, this allows us to gain

v_{k}^{-1}\to v_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\quad\text{uniformly in }C(\mathbb S^{n})

and consequently

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\to\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}.

Putting these facts together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[v_{\varepsilon}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{\varepsilon})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}

(5.2)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\nearrow+\infty}\Big[\Big(\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{k}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{\alpha-1}}\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}\big[v_{k}\,{\mathbf{P}}^{2m}_{n}(v_{k})-\varepsilon\frac{n-2m}{2}Q_{n}^{2m}v_{k}^{2}\big]d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}.

Hence, on one hand implies that
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
must be finite, on the other hand, yields that
v_{\varepsilon}
is a minimizer for (
5.1
). Rest of the proof follows immediately.
∎

Now we are in a position to give a proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2n+1]
. By Lemma
5.1
, there is some positive, smooth function
v_{\varepsilon}
satisfying

\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb S^{n}}v_{\varepsilon}^{1-\alpha}d\mu_{\mathbb S^{n}}=1

and
