\dosserif Domenico Orlando ★✩ , Susanne Reffert ✩ and Tim Schmidt ✩ \authorBlock ★ \dosserif INFN sezione di Torino, via Pietro Giuria 1, 10125 Turin, Italy \authorBlock ✩ \dosserif Albert Einstein Center for Fundamental Physics Institute for Theoretical Physics, University of Bern, Sidlerstrasse 5, CH-3012 Bern, Switzerland

Abstract
We discuss the O ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) O(2N) vector model in three dimensions. While this model flows to the Wilson–Fisher fixed point when fine tuned, working in a double-scaling limit of large N and large charge allows us to study the model away from the critical point and even to follow the RG flow from the UV to the IR. The crucial observation is that the effective potential — at leading order in N but exact to all orders in perturbation theory — is the Legendre transform of the grand potential at fixed charge. This allows us to write an effective action and the free energy for generic values of the coupling in a very simple fashion and without evaluating any Feynman diagrams.

Since in a

We will study it in more detail in the following using the state-operator correspondence and computing also the leading non-perturbative corrections in the large-

Qualitatively, the effective potential

At the minimum,

It is natural to wonder what is the actual vacuum of the theory, given that the effective potential is constant for

and there seems to be no energy argument to prefer any choice in this range.
Moreover, it is very unlikely that this degeneracy can be lifted by higher-order corrections in

Consider now a linear superposition

(3.23)

The

3.2
The free energy density on flat space

The grand potential
\omega(m)
was originally introduced in
[
8
]
to compute the free energy density (at fixed
U(1)
charge) at the critical point.
Since we have now the expression for
\omega(m)
in the general case, we can repeat the computation, which amounts to a Legendre transform of
\omega(m)
, this time with respect to the variable
m
:

f(\rho)=\omega^{*}(\rho)=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}(m\rho-\omega(m)).

(3.27)

Note that there is no convexity constraint on
\omega(m)
and in fact,

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime\prime}(m_{\text{fl}})

\displaystyle=0,

for
m_{\text{fl}}=\frac{u}{12\pi}\pqty{\sqrt{1+ \frac{48 \pi^2 r}{u^2} } - 1}
.

(3.28)

For
r\leq 0
,
i.e.
in the broken phase and at the critical point,
\omega
is convex, while there is a flex for positive values of
m
in the massive
r>0
case.

The maximization condition
\rho=\omega^{\prime}(m)
admits a solution for any real positive value of
\rho
, independently of the sign of
r
.
What changes is that in the massive case, the region
m<m_{\text{fl}}
does not contribute to the Legendre transform.
In other words, if we look at the inverse Legendre transform, there are regions of
m
that cannot be reached starting from a fixed-charge description.
This is not surprising: in general we expect the fixed-chemical potential and fixed-charge regimes to be different (see
e.g.
the discussion in the appendix of
[
1
]
).

Explicitly, we can solve the maximization in terms of trigonometric functions:

m(\rho)=\frac{u}{12\pi}\pqty{ 2 \sqrt{1 + \frac{48 \pi^2 r}{u^2} } \cos(\frac{\theta}{3} ) - 1},

(3.29)

where

\cos(\theta)=\pqty{1 + \frac{48 \pi^2 r}{u^2}}^{-3/2}\pqty{ \frac{432 \pi^3 \rho}{N u^2} - \pqty{1 + \frac{72\pi^2 r}{u^2} }}.

(3.30)

Note
that the function is real and positive for any value of
\rho
since the function
\cos(\frac{1}{3}\arccos(\theta) )
can be continued analytically for
\theta>1
.
The resulting free energy density is

f(\rho)=\frac{Nu^{3}}{2^{9}3^{4}\pi^{4}}\Bigg[4\pqty{ 1 + \frac{144 \pi^2 r}{u^2} }+3\pqty{1+ \frac{48 \pi^2 r}{u^2} }^{2}\bqty{-1 + 4 \cos(\frac{2 \theta}{3} ) + 2 \cos(\frac{4 \theta}{3} )}\\
-8\pqty{1 + \frac{48 \pi^2 r}{u^2} }^{3/2}\cos(\theta)\Bigg].

(3.31)

To understand the actual physics it is convenient to expand the free energy density for small values of the charge, using the identities

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=-\omega(m_{\text{min}}),

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=m_{\text{min}},

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega^{\prime\prime}(m_{\text{min}})}.

(3.32)

In this way we can identify typical behaviors that characterize the possible phases.

Broken phase.

In the broken phase (
r<0
), we find

f_{\text{br}}(\rho)=-\frac{Nr^{2}}{2u}+\frac{u}{4N\absolutevalue{r}}\rho^{2}+\frac{u^{3}}{48N^{2}\pi r^{3}}\rho^{3}-\frac{u^{3}}{32N^{3}r^{4}}\pqty{1 + \frac{u^2}{8 \pi^2 r}}\rho^{4}+\order{\rho^5}.

(3.33)

Apart from the unphysical constant, at leading order the energy depends on the
square of the charge density
, as in the simple mechanics example of the rigid rotor.

Unbroken phase.

In the unbroken phase (
r>0
) we find

f_{\text{unb}}(\rho)=\frac{Nu^{3}}{3\times 2^{10}\pi^{4}}\left(1+\frac{96\pi^{2}r}{u^{2}}-\left(1+\frac{64\pi^{2}r}{u^{2}}\right)^{3/2}\right)+\frac{u}{8\pi}\pqty{\sqrt{1+\frac{64 \pi^2 r}{u^2} } - 1}\rho\\
+\frac{16\pi^{2}}{Nu}\frac{1}{1+\frac{64\pi^{2}r}{u^{2}}-\sqrt{1+\frac{64\pi^{2}r}{u^{2}}}}\rho^{2}+\order{\rho^3},

(3.34)

which at lowest order is
linear in the charge density
and consistent with a particle of mass

m^{2}=r\pqty{1 + \frac{u^2}{32\pi^2 r}\pqty{1 - \sqrt{1+\frac{64\pi^2r}{u^2}}}},

(3.35)

which is the coupling of
\varphi^{2}
in the effective potential in Eq. (
3.14
).

Critical phase.

For
r=0
we find

f_{\text{crit}}(\rho)=\frac{2\sqrt{2\pi}\rho^{3/2}}{3\sqrt{N}}-\frac{2\pi^{2}\rho^{2}}{Nu}+\frac{8\sqrt{2}\pi^{7/2}\rho^{5/2}}{N^{3/2}u^{2}}-\frac{256\pi^{5}\rho^{3}}{3\left(N^{2}u^{3}\right)}+\frac{528\sqrt{2}\pi^{13/2}\rho^{7/2}}{N^{5/2}u^{4}}+\order{ \frac{\rho^4}{u^5}}.

(3.36)

In particular, at the critical point
u\to\infty
the free energy is proportional to
\rho^{3/2}
, consistently with the predictions of the large-charge expansion in
[
2
,
23
]
.
In this case the system has an intermediate behavior, midway between the rotor (
\propto\rho^{2}
) and the massive particle (
\propto\rho
).
The simplest explanation is that in the critical limit scale invariance fixes the relationship between the energy density (dimension three) and the charge density (dimension two).

3.3
Nonperturbative finite size corrections on flat space

Up to this point we have neglected non-perturbative corrections. On the torus, we can however do better as the zeta function is known exactly. The exponential terms in the zeta function can be understood as exponentially suppressed finite-size effects contributing to the effective potential.

The expression that we have used for the zeta function on the torus receives exponentially suppressed corrections in
m
.
They have been computed
e.g.
in
[
9
]
and we can use them to write down the exact expression of the grand potential
\omega(m)
at leading order in
N
.
For a torus of side
L
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\omega(m)}{2N}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\zeta(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}|T^{2},m)+\frac{\pqty{m^2 - r}^{2}}{4u}

(3.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{3}}{12\pi}\pqty{ 1 + \sideset{}{'}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{e^{-\norm{\mathbf{k}} m L }}{\norm{\mathbf{k}}^2 m^2 L^2 } \pqty{1 + \frac{1}{\norm{\mathbf{k}} m L} } }+\frac{\pqty{m^2 - r}^{2}}{4u},

where the sum runs over a square lattice of length
1
minus the origin:
\mathbf{k}=\set{(k_{1},k_{2})|k_{1}=0,1,\dots;k_{2}=0,1,\dots;(k_{1},k_{2})\neq(0,0)}
.
The exponential terms have a geometric interpretation and can be understood in terms of massive particles propagating along the cycles of the torus
[
9
]
.

One can in principle take into account all the exponential corrections, but for ease of exposition we will keep only the first one:

\frac{\omega(m)}{2N}=\frac{m^{3}}{12\pi}\pqty{ 1 + 2 \frac{e^{- m L }}{m^2 L^2 } \pqty{1 + \frac{1}{ m L} } + \order{e^{-\sqrt{2}m L}} }+\frac{\pqty{m^2 - r}^{2}}{4u}.

(3.38)

It is convenient to think of this this expression as a trans-series and compute the Legendre transform of
\varpi(y)
separately for the exponentially suppressed term.

The final result is that the leading finite-size correction to the
\order{1}
potential that we have computed in Section
3.1
is given by

V(\varphi)=\hat{V}(\varphi)-\frac{N}{3\pi L^{3}}e^{-\frac{uL}{8\pi}(1+\sqrt{1+\eta})}\pqty{1+\frac{4u \left(3+\eta+3 \sqrt{1+\eta}\right) L + u^2 \left(2+\eta+2 \sqrt{1+\eta}\right) L
^2 }{32 \pi^2 \left(2+\sqrt{1+\eta}\right)}},

(3.39)

where again

\eta=64\pi^{2}\pqty{\frac{\abs{\varphi}^2}{Nu} + \frac{r}{u^2}}.

(3.40)

This correction preserves the convexity of the potential.

4
The effective potential on the sphere

We can use the same approach as before for constructing an effective action in curved space. We specialize in this section to the sphere as this allows us to invoke the state-operator correspondence at the conformal point and directly read off the scaling dimension of the lowest operator of a given charge
Q
from the free energy
F(Q)
.

In this case the dimensionful parameter is the curvature of the sphere.
There is an important technical difference
w.r.t.
flat space since the large-charge
nlsm
is only known perturbatively as an expansion in
R/\rho
.
Also, the effective potential can only be written perturbatively and it is not clear whether it is possible to resum it in terms of elementary functions.
The
nlsm
Lagrangian at the critical point
r=1/4
,
u\to\infty
on a sphere of radius
r_{0}
is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLSM}}=\frac{1}{3}\absolutevalue{\del_\mu\chi\del^\mu\chi}^{3/2}-\frac{1}{6r_{0}^{2}}\absolutevalue{\del_\mu\chi\del^\mu\chi}^{1/2}+\frac{1}{60r_{0}^{4}}\absolutevalue{\del_\mu\chi\del^\mu\chi}^{-1/2}+\frac{17}{15360r_{0}^{6}}\absolutevalue{\del_\mu\chi\del^\mu\chi}^{-3/2}+\dots

(4.1)

We can think of it as a perturbative expansion in
1/r_{0}^{2}
, or equivalently in terms of the curvature
R=2/r_{0}^{2}
. Similarly, we can think of the effective potential as an expansion

V(\varphi)=(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i})^{3}\sum_{l=0}\gamma_{l}y^{l}

(4.2)

in terms of the
dimensionless parameter

y=\frac{RN^{2}}{96\pi^{2}(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i})^{2}}.

(4.3)

By performing the Legendre transform term by term,

V(\varphi)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLSM}}^{*}(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i}),

(4.4)

we find

\displaystyle V(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{16\pi^{2}(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i})^{3}}{3N^{2}}\bqty{1 + 3 y - \frac{3}{20} y^2 + \frac{53}{2\,560}y^3 + \dots}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{16\pi^{2}}{3N^{2}}(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i})^{3}+\frac{R}{6}\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i}-\frac{N^{2}R^{2}}{11\,520\pi^{2}}(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i})^{-1}+\frac{53N^{4}R^{3}}{424\,673\,280\pi^{4}}(\varphi_{i}^{*}\varphi_{i})^{-3}+\dots

The leading term is the same as in flat space since it is curvature-independent. The linear coupling to the curvature may be surprising as it is not the usual conformal coupling of
R/8
. This is, however, the value found when expanding around the Gaussian fixed point. Here, we expand around a strongly coupled fixed point. Since the value of this coupling is not protected by any symmetry, there is no reason to suppose that the value does not change when expanding around different points.

The free energy, which at the critical point directly corresponds to the conformal dimension of the lowest operator of a given charge, was computed using the large-
N
techniques in
[
8
]
.

5
Conclusions

In this work we have computed the large-N effective potential of the
O(2N)
vector model in three dimensions in the presence of quadratic and quartic interactions.
To do so, we have taken advantage of the double-scaling limit
N\to\infty
,
Q\to\infty
,
Q/N
fixed, in which we can compute the grand potential, which is related via a Legendre transform to the effective potential to leading order in N.
This potential is independent of the charge fixing and describes the full theory and not just a subsector.
It is equivalent to the resummation of an infinite number of Feynman diagrams.
Depending on the sign of the coefficient of the quadratic term in the
uv
action, the model can be in different phases –unbroken, critical point, broken – but the effective potential always remains convex thanks to the properties of the Legendre transform.

Our results can be naturally extended to a general number of dimensions.
This is done in the companion paper
[
14
]
, where the convexity
\omega
serves as a criterion to identify non-unitary theories.

One limitation of our analysis is its reliance on the double scaling limit
N\to\infty
,
Q\to\infty
with
Q/N
fixed.
It would be very interesting to generalize it to finite values of N, possibly starting from a
nlsm
whose form is dictated by the symmetries of the problem and justifying a semiclassical analysis using the large-charge limit.
In this sense, the grand potential that we have derived here could be the starting point for the exploration of the phase space of more general systems.
