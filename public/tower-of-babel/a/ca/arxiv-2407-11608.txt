Characters of diagonal products and Hilbert–Schmidt stability Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary: 20F69, 20C32, 20E26, 43A35. Secondary: 20E22, 22D25.
Alon Dogon, Arie Levit and Itamar Vigdorovich

Abstract
. We initiate a quantitative study of Hilbert–Schmidt stability for infinitely presented groups through the novel notion of stability radius growth. We exhibit an uncountable family of Hilbert–Schmidt stable amenable groups with arbitrarily large such growth. In particular, this answers a question of Lubotzky. Our approach is based on the character-theoretic stability criterion of Hadwin and Shulman. We classify the characters of alternating and elementary enrichments as well as diagonal products, including the classical family of B.H. Neumann groups.



1.
Introduction

The study of stability for groups originated with Ulam
[
Ula60
]
, who asked the following general question: is a map between groups which is close to being a homomorphism, necessarily close to a
true
homomorphism? We focus on the notion of Hilbert–Schmidt stability, specializing this question to unitary representations. We will be specifically interested in certain quantitative aspects of this theory.

We now formally define what it means for a map to be close to a unitary representation. Consider the finite-dimensional unitary groups
U(d)
with the
normalized Hilbert–Schmidt distance

d_{\mathrm{HS}}(A,B)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left((A-B)^{*}(A-B)\right)}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall A,B\in U(d).

Let
G
be a group with a finite generating set
S
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
denote by
B_{S}(n)
the ball of radius
n
at the identity element of
G
with respect to the word metric.

Definition 1.1
.

Given some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
, an
(n,\delta)
-almost representation
of the group
G
is a map
\varphi:B_{S}(n)\to U(d)
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

d_{\mathrm{HS}}(\varphi(gh),\varphi(g)\varphi(h))\leq\delta

for all pairs of elements
g,h\in B_{S}(n)
with
gh\in B_{S}(n)
.

Hilbert–Schmidt stability
[
HS18
]
means that almost representations necessary arise as small deformations of honest representations, in the following precise sense.

Definition 1.2
.

The group
G
is called
Hilbert–Schmidt stable
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exist
\delta>0
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that the following holds: for every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and every
(n,\delta)
-almost representation
\varphi:B_{S}(n)\to U(d)
there exists an honest unitary representation
\pi:G\to U(d)
satisfying

d_{\mathrm{HS}}(\varphi(s),\pi(s))<\varepsilon\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\in S.

The notion of Hilbert–Schmidt stability is independent of the choice of the particular generating set
S
, see
[
HS18
,
BLT19
,
Dog21
]
for more background.

We turn our attention to quantifying Hilbert–Schmidt stability. One natural way to do so is to quantify
\delta
in terms of
\varepsilon
.
This approach leads to the notion of
stability rate

[
Gle10
,
BM21
]
, which was defined only for finitely presented groups. For infinitely presented groups, one needs to take into account the fact that the number of different group relations that need to be tested might grow as
\varepsilon
becomes smaller.
We capture this behaviour by means of the following novel notion.

Definition 1.3
.

The
Hilbert–Schmidt stability radius growth
of the group
G
with respect to its finite generating set
S
is the function

{\texttt{SR}}^{S}_{G}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

where for each
M>0
we let
{\texttt{SR}}_{G}^{S}(M)
be the smallest integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that there exists a
\delta>0
for which the pair
(n,\delta)
satisfies the conclusion of Definition
1.2
with respect to the parameter
\varepsilon=1/M
. If no such
n
exists, set
{\texttt{SR}}_{G}^{S}(M)=\infty
.

The asymptotic growth type of the function

{\texttt{SR}}_{G}^{S}
does not depend on the particular generating set
S
. As such, this growth type is a group invariant, to be denoted
{\texttt{SR}}_{G}
.
It follows from the definitions that the finitely generated group
G
is Hilbert–Schmidt stable if and only if
{\texttt{SR}}_{G}(M)<\infty
for all
M>0
.

It is important to note that the stability radius growth
{\texttt{SR}}_{G}
is an interesting invariant only in the infinitely presented case. In fact, if the group
G
is finitely presented and Hilbert–Schmidt stable then
{\texttt{SR}}_{G}
is bounded. For all this see §
12
.

Our first main result shows that the Hilbert–Schmidt stability radius of infinitely presented amenable groups can grow arbitrarily fast (see our standing notations on page
1
for the asymptotic comparison symbol
\preceq
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary monotone non-decreasing growth function. Then there exists an
2
-generated Hilbert–Schmidt stable amenable group
G_{f}
with
f\preceq{\texttt{SR}}_{G_{f}}
.

As an immediate corollary of Theorem
1.4
, we obtain the existence of uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic
2
-generated Hilbert–Schmidt stable groups. This answers a question of Alex Lubotzky, which was raised during the workshop ”Geometric Aspects of Flexible Stability” at the Erdős Center in 2022.

Diagonal products

A large supply of infinitely presented Hilbert–Schmidt stable groups can be provided by using
diagonal products

[
KP13
,
BZ21
]
.
This is a flexible construction which takes as input a given
LEF (locally embeddable into finite) group

[
VG97
]
. A group
G
is called LEF if the multiplication table of every finite subset of it can be represented in some finite group.

More precisely, the construction of a diagonal product depends not only on the given LEF group
G
, but also on a choice of a generating set
S
as well as a particular LEF approximation, i.e. a sequence of maps
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
into some finite groups
G_{n}
which are eventually multiplicative and injective (see Definition
2.1
). The diagonal product associated to this data is the subgroup of the direct product
\prod G_{n}
generated by the elements
(\theta_{n}(s))
for each
s\in S
(see Definition
2.2
). It is denoted
1
1

1

We have preferred to keep the (crucial) dependence of the diagonal product
\bigotimes G_{n}
on the generating set and on the LEF approximation implicit in our notation.

\bigotimes G_{n}
.

Our second main result shows that under a mild technical hypothesis (i.e. that the LEF approximation is essential and by simple groups, see Definition
2.3
), Hilbert–Schmidt stability passes to diagonal products.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be a LEF group with a fixed finite generating set
S
and a LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
. Assume that the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}
is essential and by simple groups. If the group
G
is amenable and Hilbert–Schmidt stable then so is the associated diagonal product
\bigotimes G_{n}
.

Once Theorem
1.5
on stability is established, Theorem
1.4
follows by working with the lamplighter-based diagonal products introduced by Bradford
[
Bra24a
]
. Another ingredient towards Theorem
1.4
is a quantitative relation of stability radius growth to the invariants of LEF growth and MAP growth; see §
12
for further details.

In addition, using a slightly more sophisticated technique, we are able to show that the following classical family of diagonal products is Hilbert–Schmidt stable.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{d_{n}\}
be any strictly increasing sequence of integers.

(1)

Assume that
d_{n}
is odd for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
d_{1}\geq 5
. The classical B.H. Neumann group
\bigotimes\Alt(d_{n})
is Hilbert–Schmidt stable; see Example
4.5
.

(2)

Let
F
be a finite field. Assume that
d_{1}\geq 2
. The diagonal product
\bigotimes\mathrm{SL}_{d_{n}}(F)
is Hilbert–Schmidt stable; see Example
4.8
.

We remark that the classical B.H. Neumann groups
[
Neu37
]
were shown to be stable in permutations in
[
LL22b
]
. This family is uncountable.

Character theory

The key to Theorem
1.5
is a study of
characters
of diagonal products. Indeed, in the setting of amenable groups, Hilbert–Schmidt stability and character theory are related by a fundamental result of Hadwin and Shulman
[
HS18
, Theorem 4]
. The result says that Hilbert–Schmidt stability can be reformulated as an approximation property for characters.

The character theory of infinite groups was first studied by Thoma
[
Tho64b
,
Tho64a
]
. Here are the key notions of the theory.
A
trace
on a countable group
G
is a conjugation-invariant and positive-definite function
\varphi:G\to\mathbb{C}
normalized so that
\varphi(e_{G})=1
. The extreme points of the space of all traces on the group
G
are called
characters
.
The space of characters of
G
is called the
Thoma dual
and is denoted
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
.
Thoma singled out
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
as an important object in the harmonic analysis on
G
, for it captures the representation theory of
G
into tracial von Neumann algebras.
A fundamental problem in the theory is to fully describe the space
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
, i.e.
character classification
. We refer to §
5
for more details.

The following result is a character classification for diagonal products.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
G
be a LEF group with a fixed finite generating set
S
and a LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
. Assume that the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}
is essential and by simple groups. Denote by
\Gamma=\bigotimes G_{n}
the associated diagonal product. The space of characters of
\Gamma
is

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Gamma\right)\cong\{(\varphi,\psi)\in\prod_{n}\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{n}\right)\times\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)\>:\>\text{$\psi$ is a compatible character for $\varphi$}\}.

The intricate notion of
compatible characters
is spelled out in detail in §
9
. In any case, the direct sum
\bigoplus G_{n}
is a normal subgroup of the diagonal product
\bigotimes G_{n}
in the setting of Theorem
1.7
. Furthermore
\prod_{n}\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{n}\right)\cong\mathrm{Ch}\left(\bigoplus_{n}G_{n}\right)
by Proposition
5.6
. Hence
the theorem shows that the space
\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Gamma\right)
is
“fibered”
over the character space of the direct sum of finite groups
\bigoplus_{n}G_{n}
.
The fibers themselves can be quite mysterious, see for instance the discussion in Example
9.6
.

There are two additional classes of groups we study in the context of character theory, namely the so called
alternating and elementary enrichments

[
Bra22a
]
.
These are close cousins of the lamplighter groups, falling under the more general framework of halo products introduced in
[
GT24
]
. The alternating enrichment of a group
G
is the semidirect product
\mathscr{A}(G)=G\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
, where
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
is the group of finitely supported even permutations on the underlying set of the group
G
, and the conjugation comes from the left action of
G
on this set. The elementary enrichment is
\mathscr{E}(G)=G\ltimes\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
, where
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
is the group of unimodular finite rank linear transformations on the vector space
V_{G}
over a finite field and with basis indexed by the underlying set of the group
G
. See Definitions
4.1
and
4.2
for details.

Here is our character classification result for these enrichments. Recall that a group is called ICC if every non-trivial conjugacy class is infinite.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be either the alternating enrichment
\mathscr{A}(G)
or the elementary enrichment
\mathscr{E}(G)
of a countably infinite group
G
. Let the normal subgroup
N\lhd\Gamma
be either
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
or
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
respectively. If the group
G
is ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Gamma\right)\cong\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\sqcup\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)\right)/\left(\delta_{e}^{G}\sim 1_{N}\right).

If the group
G
is not ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Gamma\right)\cong\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\sqcup\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)\backslash\{1_{N}\}\right).

Here
\delta_{e}^{G}
denotes the Dirac function at the identity on
G
, and
1_{N}
denotes the trivial character of
N
.
Note that the character space of both groups
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(\infty\right)
and
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(\infty\right)
is well-understood, see
[
Tho64a
]
and
[
Sku76
]
respectively.

Theorem
1.8
allows us to prove that classical B.H. Neumann groups
[
Neu37
]
are Hilbert–Schmidt stable (Theorem
1.6
), even though they do not fall under the framework of Theorem
1.5
.
This is done as part of a more general setting of
alternating and elementary diagonal products
, which we introduce in §
4
. See Theorem
11.3
for the full-fledged stability result in that context.

Local Hilbert–Schmidt stability

An interesting local variant of Hilbert–Schmidt stability was introduced quite recently by Fournier-Facio, Gerasimova and Spaas
[
FFGS25
]
. It extends the study of stability to potentially non-residually finite LEF groups.
Here is the relevant definition:

Definition 1.9
.

A group
G
with a finite generating set
S
is
locally Hilbert–Schmidt stable
if for every
\varepsilon>0
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist
\delta>0
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that the following holds:
for every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and every
(n,\delta)
-almost representation
\varphi:B_{S}(n)\to U(d)
there exists a map
f:B_{S}(r)\to U(d)
satisfying

d_{\mathrm{HS}}(\varphi(s),f(s))<\varepsilon\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\in S

as well as
f(gh)=f(g)f(h)
for every pair of elements
g,h\in B_{S}(r)
with
gh\in B_{S}(r)
.

The notion of local Hilbert–Schmidt stability is of course independent of the choice of the finite generating set
S
.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated amenable group. Then
G
is locally Hilbert–Schmidt stable if and only if its alternating enrichment
\mathscr{A}(G)
is locally Hilbert–Schmidt stable. The same statement holds for the elementary enrichment
\mathscr{E}(G)
.

This answers the Hilbert–Schmidt version of a question of Bradford for amenable groups
[
Bra24b
, Question 6.3]
. It can be seen using Theorems
1.6
and
1.10
that alternating enrichments of classical B. H. Neumann groups are locally Hilbert–Schmidt stable. Since alternating enrichments of infinite groups are never residually finite, we obtain an uncountable family of
4
-generated amenable Hilbert–Schmidt groups that are not Hilbert–Schmidt stable (see Corollary
11.8
). This reproves the result
[
FFGS25
, Theorem E]
using a different family of groups.

An application to characters of alternating groups

As an aside to the main flow of ideas, we present another interesting and unexpected application of Theorem
1.7
. It is an asymptotic result in the classical character theory of alternating groups.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
\sigma
be an even cycle structure with
|\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)|=q
. It represents a single conjugacy class in the alternating group
\Alt(n)
for all
n\geq q+2
. Let
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
be a strictly increasing sequence of integers with
n_{1}\geq q+2
and
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Alt(n_{i})\right)
be arbitrary irreducible normalized characters. Then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(\sigma)
exists.

For instance,
let
z_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}
be values from the normalized character tables of the alternating groups
\Alt(n_{i})
all corresponding to some fixed cycle structure. If
z_{i}\to z
for some value
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
|z|=1
then we obtain
z=1
. In other words, the complex number
1
is the only accumulation point of such normalized character values on the unit circle.
An analogous conclusion is true also in the context of the special linear groups
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
where
F
is a finite field. However, in that case this already follows from existing character bounds (at least for
|F|
sufficiently large, see §
8
).

Standing notations

We set up some notations to be used throughout this work.

•

We let
\left[n\right]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we will denote by
\Alt(n)=\Alt([n])
the alternating group on
[n]
.

•

Let
g,h\in G
be a pair of group elements. We use the notation
g^{h}=hgh^{-1}
for the conjugate of the element
g
by the element
h
.

•

Let
f,g:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be a pair of monotone non-decreasing functions.

–

We write
f\preceq g
if there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that
f(x)\leq Cg(Cx)
for all
x>0
.

–

We write
f\approx g
if
f\preceq g
and
g\preceq f
. In that case
f
and
g
are said to be
equivalent
.

–

We use the same terminology to compare functions defined only on natural numbers.

Part A: Group theory — diagonal products and enrichments

This part is purely group-theoretical. We introduce the notions of diagonal products, alternating and elementary enrichments as well as classical and generalized B.H Neumann groups.

2.
Diagonal products

Let
G
be a countable group.

Definition 2.1
.

A
partial homomorphism
is a sequence of maps
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
into some sequence of groups
G_{n}
such that every pair of elements
g,h\in G
satisfies
\theta_{n}(gh)=\theta_{n}(g)\theta_{n}(g)
for all
n
sufficiently large.

A
LEF approximation
is a partial homomorphism such that the target groups
G_{n}
are all
finite
and such that
every non-trivial element
g\in G
satisfies
\theta_{n}(g)\neq e
for all
n
sufficiently large.
The group
G
is called
locally embeddable into finite groups (LEF)
if it admits a LEF approximation.

For example, every residually finite group is LEF.

Assume that the group
G
is finitely generated and let
S
be a fixed generating set.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
be a LEF approximation of the group
G
. The
diagonal product

\bigotimes_{n}G_{n}
associated to this data is the subgroup of the direct product
\prod_{n}G_{n}
generated by the elements
\gamma_{s}=(\theta_{n}(s))_{n}
for each
s\in S
.

Note that the diagonal product construction crucially depends on the fixed LEF approximation. This dependence is kept implicit in our notation, in the hope that should not cause any confusion. For more information on diagonal products we refer to
[
KP13
,
Mim19
]
.

In what follows, it will be very convenient to work with LEF approximations satisfying following additional conditions.

Definition 2.3
.

A given LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
is called an
essential LEF approximation by simple groups
if

•

the finite groups
G_{n}
are simple and pairwise non-isomorphic,

•

the restriction of
\theta_{n}
to the generating set
S
does not extend to a homomorphism from
G
to
G_{n}
, and

•

the subset
\theta_{n}(S)
generates the group
G_{n}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The first two assumptions in Definition
2.3
are quite standard, and are closely related to the splitting assumption in
[
KP13
]
. Note that when all maps
\theta_{n}
are group homomorphisms, namely when the LEF approximation arises via residual finiteness, then the diagonal product
\bigotimes G_{n}
is isomorphic to the group
G
itself
[
Mim19
, §4.4]
. The third assumption can be made without a loss of generality by restricting the codomains of the functions
\theta_{n}
. All LEF approximations we will consider in this work will be essential and by simple groups.
This assumption comes in handy in light of the following well-known group theory fact, see e.g.
[
Mim19
, Lemma 4.4]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G_{i}
be a family of pairwise non-isomorphic simple groups for
i\in I
. Then any normal subgroup of the direct sum

\bigoplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
is of the form
\bigoplus_{i\in J}G_{i}
for some subset
J\subset I
.

The tail map

Let
G
be a group with a finite generating set
S
and an
essential
LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
by
simple
groups. Consider the associated diagonal product
\Gamma=\bigotimes G_{n}
. The diagonal product
\Gamma
comes equipped with a natural surjective homomorphism
t:\Gamma\to G
called the
tail map
.

The tail map
t
is defined on generators simply by
\gamma_{s}\mapsto s
for each
s\in S
. To see that
t
is a well-defined homomorphism, consider a word
w
in the generators
\gamma_{s}
such that
w(\gamma_{s})=e
in the group
\Gamma
. In particular
w(\theta_{n}(s))=e
in the finite group
G_{n}
for all
n
sufficiently large. This implies that
w(s)=e
in the group
G
, as required.
The tail map
t
is clearly surjective, as
S
is a generating set for
G
.

Lemma 2.5
.

The direct sum
\bigoplus_{n}G_{n}
is a subgroup of the diagonal product
\Gamma
.
The kernel of the tail map satisfies

\ker t=\bigoplus_{n}G_{n}
.

Proof.

The fact that
\ker t=\Gamma\cap\bigoplus G_{n}
follows readily from the definitions and holds true for arbitrary diagonal products. It remains to show that the diagonal product
\Gamma
contains the entire direct sum
\bigoplus G_{n}
. In light of Lemma
2.4
, it will be enough to show that the subgroup
\ker t
surjects onto each simple factor
G_{n}
.

Consider the quotient map
\pi_{n}:\Gamma\to G_{n}
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The simplicity of the group
G_{n}
implies that
\pi_{n}(\ker t)
is either equal to
G_{n}
or is trivial. Assume by contradiction that
\pi_{n}(\ker t)=\{e\}
. Then by
[
KP13
, Lemma 4.7]
there exists a group homomorphism
p_{n}:G\to G_{n}
such that
\pi_{n}=p_{n}\circ t
. It follows that
p_{n}
agrees with
\theta_{n}
on each generator
s\in S
, namely

p_{n}(s)=p_{n}(t(\gamma_{s}))=\pi_{n}(\gamma_{s})=\theta_{n}(s).

This contradicts the assumption that the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}
is essential.
∎

In what follows we will use the notation
U=\ker t
. The following short exact sequence holds true

1\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow\Gamma\xlongrightarrow{t}G\longrightarrow 1.

In particular, the diagonal product
\Gamma
is an extension of the LEF group
G
by the locally finite group
U
.
Consequently, if the LEF group
G
is amenable, then so is the diagonal product
\Gamma
.

In the following lemma, we analyze finite index normal subgroups of
U
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
V\lhd U
be a normal subgroup with
[U:V]<\infty
. Then
V\lhd\Gamma
and there is a finite subset
L\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\Gamma/V\cong G\times\prod_{n\in L}G_{n}.

Proof.

Recall that the diagonal product
\Gamma
is by construction a subgroup of the direct product
\prod_{n}G_{n}
. It follows from the elementary group theoretical Lemma
2.4
that there is some finite subset
L\subset{\mathbb{N}}
such that

V=\bigoplus_{n\notin L}G_{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptU/V\cong\bigoplus_{n\in L}G_{n}.

This means that the subgroup
V
is normal in the direct product
\prod_{n}G_{n}
, and particularly in the diagonal product
\Gamma
.

Let
p
be the quotient map

p:\Gamma\to U/V
obtained by identifying the quotient group
U/V
with the product of the finitely many coordinates of
\prod_{n}G_{n}
belonging to
L
.
Recall that the tail map
t
is the quotient map
t:\Gamma\to G
with
\ker t=U
. The Cartesian product of these two quotient maps
p
and
t
is the homomorphism

t\times p:\Gamma\to G\times U/V.

It is routine to verify that the product homomorphism
t\times p
is surjective and has
\ker(t\times p)=V
. This sets up the desired group isomorphism.
∎

The subgroups
U_{n}
and
\Gamma_{n}

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
denote

U_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}

so that
U_{n}
is a finite subgroup of
U
.
Further, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\Gamma_{n}
be the subgroup of the direct product
\prod_{i}G_{i}
generated by the elements

\gamma_{s,n}\in\prod_{i}G_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma_{s,n}(i)=\begin{cases}\theta_{i}(s)&i\geq n\\
e&1\leq i<n\end{cases}

for all
s\in S
.
In particular
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma
. The fact that
U_{n}\leq\Gamma
implies that
\Gamma_{n+1}\leq\Gamma
as well. The subgroups
U_{n}
and
\Gamma_{n}
are all normal in
\Gamma
and satisfy

\Gamma=U_{n}\times\Gamma_{n+1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{n}=G_{n}\times\Gamma_{n+1}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lastly, there is a sequence of
“asymptotic sections”
to the tail map, namely, maps

\Phi_{n}:G\to\Gamma_{n}

such that
t\circ\Phi_{n}(g)=g
holds true for each element
g\in G
and all
n
sufficiently large. Indeed, for each pair of indices
n\leq m
, the
m
-th coordinate of the map
\Phi_{n}
is simply the map
\theta_{m}:G\to G_{m}
coming from the LEF approximation. It follows that the maps
\Phi_{n}
are partial homomorphisms, in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Locally inner actions and subgroups

Definition 2.7
.

An automorphism
\sigma
of a group
G
is
locally inner
if for any finitely generated subgroup
F\leq G
there is some element
h\in G
such that
\sigma(g)=g^{h}
for all
g\in F
.
An action of a group
H
on a group
G
by
automorphisms is
locally inner
if each element of
H
is acting via a locally inner automorphism. A normal subgroup
N\lhd G
is called a
locally inner subgroup
if the conjugation action of the group
G
on
N
is locally inner.

To clarify the terminology of a locally inner subgroup; the conjugation action of
G
on its normal subgroup
N
is required to be locally inner from the
“point of view”
of
N
. Further, note that being normal is part of the definition of a locally inner subgroup.
Here are some useful examples to keep in mind.

Example 2.8
.

The finitary groups
\SymZ
and
\AltZ
are locally inner subgroups of the group
\Sym({\mathbb{Z}})
consisting of
all
permutations of the set
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
G_{i}
be a countable family of groups. The direct sum
\bigoplus_{i}G_{i}
is a locally inner normal subgroup of the direct product
\prod_{i}G_{i}
.

Certainly, if some action of the group
G
is locally inner, then the same is true for the restricted action of any subgroup of
G
.

We explore another interesting example of locally inner subgroups that arises in the context of diagonal products.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
G
be a group with a finite generating set
S
and an essential LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
by simple groups. Let
\Gamma=\bigotimes_{n}G_{n}
be the associated diagonal product with tail map
t
. If the subgroup
N\lhd G
is locally inner then the subgroup
\Delta=t^{-1}(N)\lhd\Gamma
is also locally inner.

Proof.

Consider a pair of elements
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
\delta\in\Delta
. The assumption that
N
is locally inner in
G
allows us to find some element
h\in N
such that
t(\delta^{\gamma})=t(\delta)^{h}
. Let
\delta_{0}\in\Delta
be an arbitrary element with
t(\delta_{0})=h
. Then
t(\delta^{\gamma})=t(\delta^{\delta_{0}})
which means
\delta^{\gamma}=\delta^{\delta_{0}}u
for some element
u\in U
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be some sufficiently large index such that
u\in U_{n}
. This means that there is some element
v\in U_{n}
such that the element
\delta_{1}=\delta_{0}v\in\Delta
has the same projection as the element
\gamma
onto the first
n
-many coordinates
\prod_{i=1}^{n}G_{n}
. We conclude that
\delta^{\delta_{1}}=\delta^{\gamma}
as required.
∎

In particular, the subgroup
U=\ker t\lhd\Gamma
is locally inner. This fact also follows from Example
2.9
discussed above.

3.
Classical and generalised B. H. Neumann groups

We discuss the classical groups of B.H. Neumann
[
Neu37
]
as well as their generalizations introduced by Bradford in
[
Bra24a
, §2.4]
.
Bradford’s groups are quite elementary yet interesting examples of diagonal products. They are closely related to the groups of fast residual finiteness growth introduced in
[
BRS16
]
.
We will show that Bradford’s groups are Hilbert–Schmidt stable and have arbitrarily large stability radius growth (Theorem
1.4
).

Definition 3.1

(Bradford
[
Bra24a
]
)
.

Let
d,r:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a pair functions, such that the function
d
is strictly increasing with
d(1)\geq 5
and
d(n)
odd for all
n
and such that the function
r
satisfies
2r(n)\leq d(n)-1
for all
n
. Consider the two elements

\alpha,\beta\in\prod_{n}\Alt(d(n))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha=(\alpha_{n}),\beta=(\beta_{n})

given for each
n
by

\alpha_{n}=(1\;2\;\dots\;d(n))\in\Alt(d(n))\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\beta_{n}=(1\;1+r(n)\;1+2r(n))\in\Alt(d(n)).

The
(d,r)
-generalized B. H. Neumann group
is the subgroup the direct product
\prod_{n}\Alt(d(n))
generated by the two elements
S=\{\alpha,\beta\}
. It will be denoted
B(d,r)
.

We will deal with generalised B. H. Neumann groups in two regimes.

•

r
is the constant function
1
, so that
\beta_{n}=(1\;2\;3)
for all
n
.
The group
B(d,1)
is a classical B. H. Neumann group
[
Neu37
]
. Two such groups
B(d,1)
and
B(d^{\prime},1)
are isomorphic if and only if
d=d^{\prime}
. This yields an uncountable family of
2
-generated groups
[
Neu37
]
.
The group
B(d,1)
is isomorphic to a diagonal product associated with a LEF approximation of the alternating enrichment
{\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left({\mathbb{Z}}\right)
(this will be elaborated in Example
4.5
below).

•

The function
r
is unbounded.
In this case, we will always be assuming that
d(n)
is prime for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and that

\lim_{n}r(n)=\lim_{n}d(n)-2r(n)=\infty.

Under these assumptions, Bradford
[
Bra24a
]
shows that the generalized B.H. Neumann group
B(d,r)
is isomorphic to a diagonal product associated with a LEF approximation of the wreath product
({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let us provide a bit more information about the generalized B.H. Neumann groups in the second regime on the above list.

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider the wreath product
G=({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
with the two generators
S=\{\alpha_{\infty},\beta_{\infty}\}

where
\alpha_{\infty}
is a generator of the cyclic subgroup
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\beta_{\infty}
is a generator of the direct summand
{\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}}
indexed by
0
of
\bigoplus_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Let
d:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a strictly increasing sequence of primes with
d(1)\geq 5
. Let
r:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a function such that

\lim_{n}r(n)=\lim_{n}d(n)-2r(n)=\infty.

Then there is an essential LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to\Alt(d(n))
by simple groups satisfying

\theta_{n}(\alpha_{\infty})=\alpha_{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\theta_{n}(\beta_{\infty})=\beta_{n}

where the permutations
\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}\in\Alt(d(n))
are as in Definition
3.1
, and

B(d,r)\cong\bigotimes_{n}\Alt(d(n))

where the diagonal product is taken with respect to the generating set
S
and the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}
.

Proof.

This is essentially a reformulation of
[
Bra24a
, Lemma 2.10 and Proposition 2.12]
. These results establish that the Cayley graphs of
\Alt(d(m))
with respect to the generators
\{\alpha_{m},\beta_{m}\}
converge in the Chabauty topology on the space of marked groups to the Cayley graph of
G
with respect to the generators
\{\alpha_{\infty},\beta_{\infty}\}
.
This guarantees the existence of a LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to\Alt(d(n))
, by the well-known connection between LEF approximations and limits of finite marked groups
[
FFGS25
, Theorem 2.16]
.
Hence
B(d,r)
is the diagonal product to this LEF approximation.
Further, the restrictions of
\theta_{n}
to the generating pairs
{\{\alpha_{\infty},\beta_{\infty}\}}
do not extend to homomorphisms from
G
to
\Alt(d(n))
, since the group
G
is metabelian, while the groups
\Alt(d(n))
are simple and non-abelian.
∎

4.
Alternating and elementary enrichments and diagonal products

Let
G
be a countable group. We study two particular extensions of the group
G
, namely the alternating and the elementary enrichments. We also describe a two-step procedure we call
alternating (or elementary) diagonal products
. It is inspired by Mimura’s work
[
Mim19
]
. Applying this procedure to the infinite cyclic group
{\mathbb{Z}}
give a (somewhat roundabout) construction of the classical B.H. Neumann groups.

Enrichments

Let
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
denote the group of all finitely-supported even permutations of the underlying set of the group
G
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
alternating enrichment
of a group
G
is the semidirect product
\mathscr{A}(G)=G\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
. The action of the group
G
by automorphisms on the normal subgroup
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
arises from conjugation by the left action of
G
on its underlying set.

Fix a finite field
F=\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
for some prime
p
and some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Given a set
X
(for us, this will always be the underlying set of a group) we will denote by
V_{X}
be the
F
-vector space with basis indexed by
X
. Let
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
be the group of all unimodular finite rank
F
-linear transformations of the vector space
V_{G}
.

Definition 4.2
.

The
elementary enrichment
of
G
is the semidirect product
\mathscr{E}(G)=G\ltimes\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
. The action of the group
G
by automorphisms on
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
arises from conjugation by the left-regular representation.

Alternating enrichments of LEF groups

Assume that the countable group
G
has a fixed finite generating set
S
and a LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
such that the orders
|G_{n}|
are pairwise distinct (see Definition
2.1
).

In addition, we make a technical parity assumption. Namely, we assume that the regular permutation action of the element
\theta_{n}(s)
on the underlying set of the group
G_{n}
is even for each
s\in S
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This assumption is equivalent to requiring that either the order of
\theta_{n}(s)
is odd or
\left[G_{n}:\left<\theta_{n}(s)\right>\right]
is even.

Lemma 4.3

(Encoding into alternating groups)
.

The alternating enrichment
{\mathscr{A}(G)}
is finitely generated and admits an essential LEF approximation
\Theta_{n}:\mathscr{A}(G)\to\Alt(G_{n})
by simple groups.

Proof.

Consider the alternating enrichment
{\mathscr{A}(G)}=G\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
of the group
G
.
The group
{\mathscr{A}(G)}
is finitely generated. Indeed, for each generator
s\in S
consider the
3
-cycle
t_{s}\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
given by the
3
-cycle
t_{s}=(s^{-1},e,s)
. Denote
T=\{t_{s}\>:\>s\in S\}
. It is not hard to verify that
S\amalg T
is a finite generating set for
{\mathscr{A}(G)}
. One may rely on the fact that
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
is generated by all
3
-cycles of the form
(g_{1},g_{2},h)
where
g_{1},g_{2}\in G
are fixed and
h\in G\setminus\{g_{1},g_{2}\}
is arbitrary.

The group
{\mathscr{A}(G)}
is LEF. It admits a LEF approximation
\Theta_{n}:\mathscr{A}(G)\to\Alt(G_{n})
called
“encoding into alternating groups”
and described in
[
Mim19
, §4]
. It is obtained by letting the element
\Theta_{n}(s)
be the permutation given by left multiplication by
\theta_{n}(s)
, and the element
\Theta_{n}(t_{s})
be the
3
-cycle
(\theta_{n}(s^{-1}),\theta_{n}(e),\theta_{n}(s))
for all
n
.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

The
alternating diagonal product
associated to the group
G
with its finite generating set
S
and its LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
is the diagonal product
\Gamma=\bigotimes\Alt(G_{n})
associated to the alternating enrichment
{\mathscr{A}(G)}
with its generating set
S\amalg T
and its LEF approximation
\Theta_{n}:\mathscr{A}(G)\to\Alt(G_{n})
defined by
“encoding into alternating groups”
.

We are ready to present the classical groups of B.H. Neumann
[
Neu37
]
.

Example 4.5

(Classical B. H. Neumann groups)
.

Consider the infinite cyclic group
{\mathbb{Z}}
with its generating set
S=\{1\}
. The corresponding alternating enrichment is the group
{\mathscr{A}(G)}={\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left({\mathbb{Z}}\right)
.
Let
\mathcal{P}=(d_{n})
be some strictly increasing sequence of odd integers with

. Consider the sequence of finite cyclic groups

and the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}/d_{n}{\mathbb{Z}}
given simply by the residue class modulo

. Note that
\Theta_{n}(1)
is the full
d_{n}
-cycle and
\Theta(t_{1})
is the

-cycle

for all

[
LPS96
]
or
[

B.H. Neumann groups are revisited in §

are denoted

.

Part B: Character theory

5.
Characters of discrete groups

Let
G
be a countable group.

Definition 5.1
.

A
trace
on the group
G
is a function
\varphi:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
the following properties:

(1)

\varphi
is
positive-definite
: for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any choice of group elements
g_{1},...,g_{n}\in G
and
scalars
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\overline{\alpha}_{j}\varphi(g_{j}^{-1}g_{i})\geq 0
.

(2)

\varphi
is
normalized
:
\varphi(e)=1
.

(3)

\varphi
is
conjugation-invariant
:
\varphi(g^{h})=\varphi(g)

for any pair of elements
g,h\in G
.

The space of all traces on the group
G
is denoted by
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
. We endow
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
with the topology of pointwise convergence. Any trace
\varphi
on
G
is bounded, and in fact

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}=\varphi(e)=1
.
It follows that
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is a convex and compact subset of the unit ball in
\ell^{\infty}(G)
endowed with the pointwise convergence topology. As the group
G
is countable, the space
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is metrizable.

The extreme points of the convex set
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
are called
characters
. That is, characters are traces that cannot be written
as a proper convex combination of traces.
We denote by
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
the space of characters of the group
G
.

Given a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
its
barycenter is the trace
\varphi
given by

\varphi=\int_{\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)}\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mu(\psi).

The convexity and compactness of the set
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
ensures that
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)

[
Phe01
, §1]
. Any trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
arises as the barycenter of a unique Borel probability measure
\mu_{\varphi}
on
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
satisfying
\mu_{\varphi}(\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right))=1
, see
[
Tho64b
]
.

Extending the representation theory of finite groups, finite-dimensional unitary representations give rise to traces. Indeed, let
\pi
be a finite-dimensional unitary representation of the group

G
. Then the function
\varphi_{\pi}=\frac{1}{\dim\pi}\tr\circ\pi(g)
is a trace on
G
. We call such traces
finite-dimensional
.
See
[
LV23
, §9]
for more information on finite-dimensional traces.

Associated to any trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is the
Gelfand–Naimark–Segal (GNS) construction
. It consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
, a unitary representation
\pi_{\varphi}:G\to\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi})
and a cyclic unit vector
v_{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
such that
\varphi(g)=\left<\pi_{\varphi}(g)v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi}\right>
for all elements
g\in G
. The GNS data consisting of the tuple
(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\pi_{\varphi},v_{\varphi})
is uniquely determined up to an isomorphism. The Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
is finite-dimensional if and only if the trace
\varphi
is finite-dimensional (in the above sense).

Definition 5.2
.

The
kernel
of the trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is

\ker\varphi=\left\{g\in G\>:\>\varphi(g)=1\right\}.

The trace
\varphi
is called
faithful
if
\ker\varphi=\{e\}
.
\varphi
is called
trivial
if
\ker\varphi=G
so that
\varphi\equiv 1
.

The kernel of any trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is a normal subgroup and
\varphi
factorizes to a faithful trace on the quotient group
G/\ker\varphi
. For this see e.g.
[
LV23
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
be a trace. If
|\varphi(g)|=1
for all
g\in G
then
\varphi:G\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
is a multiplicative character.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\pi_{\varphi},v_{\varphi})
be the GNS data associated to the trace
\varphi
. Note that
v_{\varphi}
is a unit vector, namely
\|v_{\varphi}\|=1
.
Note that
|\varphi(g)|=1
for some element
g\in G
if and only if
|\left<\pi_{\varphi}(g)v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi}\right>|=1
. In that case, the equality condition in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies that
\pi_{\varphi}(g)v_{\varphi}=\lambda_{g}v_{\varphi}
for some scalar
\lambda_{\varphi}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda_{g}|=1
. As the vector
v_{\varphi}
is cyclic, if
|\varphi(g)|=1
holds for all elements
g\in G
then
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}v_{\varphi}
and
\pi_{\varphi}(g)=\lambda_{g}
for all
g\in G
. The map
g\mapsto\lambda_{g}
is a multiplicative homomorphism.
∎

The following is well known, and we skip it’s proof.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
f:G\to H
be a quotient map of groups. The pullback map
f^{*}:\mathrm{Tr}\left(H\right)\to\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)

defined by
f^{*}(\psi)=\psi\circ f
is injective, continuous and
affine. The image of
f^{*}
is a face of
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.
In particular, a trace
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(H\right)
is a character of
H
if and only if
f^{*}(\psi)

is a character of
G
.

Recall that a
face
of a convex set
C
is a convex subset
F\subset C
such that for every pair of points
x,y\in C
, if
(1-\lambda)x+\lambda y\in F
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
then
x,y\in F
.

Restriction and induction of traces

Consider a normal subgroup
N
of the group
G
.
Denote

\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}=\{\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)\>:\>\psi(n^{g})=\psi(n)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall n\in N,g\in G\}.

For any trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
its restriction
\varphi_{\mid N}
belongs to
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)^{N}
. Namely, restriction gives rise to a continuous affine map
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)\to\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}
.

Let
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
be any trace. The
trivial extension
of
\psi
to the group
G
is given by

\widetilde{\psi}:G\to{\mathbb{C}},\hskip 10.00002ptg\mapsto\begin{cases}\psi(g)&\forall g\in N\\
0&\forall g\notin N\end{cases}.

It is easy to see that if
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}
then its trivial extension satisfies
\widetilde{\varphi}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
. We get a continuous affine injective
map
\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}\to\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.
In terms of the GNS construction, the trivial extension corresponds to induction of unitary representations from the subgroup
N
to the group
G
. This discussion shows that the restriction map
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)\to\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}
is surjective.

For example, the trivial extension
\widetilde{1}_{N}
of the trivial character
1_{N}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
is the characteristic function of the normal subgroup
N
. In particular, the Dirac function at the identity element
\delta_{e}
is always a trace.
The trivial extension
\widetilde{1}_{N}
is a character of the group
G
if and only if the quotient group
Q=G/N
has the
ICC property
, i.e every non-trivial conjugacy class of
Q
is infinite.

In general, restrictions of characters to normal subgroups as well as trivial extensions of
characters do not result in characters. Nevertheless, in many instances considered in this paper, the resulting trace turns out to be a character. See §
6
.

Characters of direct sums

Let
I
be a countable index set. Let
G_{i}
be a countable group for each index
i\in I
. Consider the direct sum group
G=\bigoplus_{i\in I}G_{i}
.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G_{i}\right)
be a trace for each index
i\in I
. The
tensor product
of the
\varphi_{i}
’s is given by

\varphi=\otimes_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}:G\to{\mathbb{C}},\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi:(g_{i})\mapsto\prod_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}(g_{i})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall(g_{i})_{i\in I}\in G.

This infinite product is well defined since all but finitely many multiplicands are equal to
1
for each given element of
G
.
It is not hard to see that the tensor product
\varphi=\otimes_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}
is indeed a trace on the group
G
.

A description of the characters of the group
G
in terms of tensor products of characters of the direct summands
G_{i}
was obtained by Thoma
[
Tho64b
, Satz 5]
.

Proposition 5.6

(Thoma)
.

Tensor product of traces sets up a bijection
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\cong\prod_{i\in I}\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)
.

For the sake of completeness, we provide a short proof of Thoma’s result, which is based on the special case of products of finitely many groups covered in
[
BF22
, Theorem 2.12]
.

Proof of Proposition
5.6
.

For each subset
J\subseteq I
it will be convenient to denote
G_{J}=\bigoplus_{i\in J}G_{i}
.
Consider the tensor product map

F:\prod_{i\in I}\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)\to\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right),\hskip 10.00002ptF((\varphi_{i})_{i\in I})=\otimes_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}.

The map
F
is clearly injective, for the restriction of the trace
F((\varphi_{i})_{i\in I})
to each summand
G_{i}
recovers the trace
\varphi_{i}
.
Let us verify that the image of the map
F
is
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
. Consider an arbitrary character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
. The restriction
\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{\mid G_{i}}
belongs to
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)
, see e.g. Proposition
6.1
below. By
[
BF22
, Theorem 2.12]
it is the case that the restriction of
\varphi
to the subgroup
\bigoplus_{i\in J}G_{i}
is equal to
\otimes_{i\in J}\varphi_{i}
for each finite collection of indices
J\subset I
. Therefore
F((\varphi_{i})_{i\in I})=\varphi
.

It remains to show that the tensor product of characters is a character. Let
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)
be an arbitrary character for each index
i\in I
. The case of finitely many summands considered in
[
BF22
, Theorem 2.12]
implies that the restriction of the trace
F((\varphi_{i})_{i\in I})
to the subgroup
\bigoplus_{i\in J}G_{i}
is a character for each finite collection of indices
J\subset I
. It follows that
F((\varphi_{i})_{i\in I})\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
as required.
∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
be a direct product. Let
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
be a trace with restrictions
\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{\mid G_{i}}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G_{i}\right)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. If
\varphi_{1}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{1}\right)
then
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{\varphi}
be the unique Borel probability measure on
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
such that
\varphi=\int\psi\;\mathrm{d}\mu_{\varphi}(\psi)
.
We know that
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)=\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{1}\right)\times\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{2}\right)
by Proposition
5.6
. Since
\varphi_{1}
is a character, the push-forward of the probability measure
\mu_{\varphi}
to the factor
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{1}\right)
is the Dirac point mass
\delta_{\varphi_{1}}
. The push-forward of
\mu_{\varphi}
to the other factor
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{2}\right)
is some Borel probability measure
\mu_{2}
satisfying
\varphi_{2}=\int\psi^{\prime}\;\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}(\psi^{\prime})
. By measure-theoretical reasons, it must be the case that
\mu=\delta_{\varphi_{1}}\otimes\mu_{2}
. This is equivalent to the desired conclusion.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

If
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
then
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
. Further, if the two traces
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are finite-dimensional, then so is the trace
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}
.

Proof.

The function
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}
is conjugation-invariant and satisfies
(\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2})(e)=1
. Further, the function
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}
is positive-definite, see e.g.
[
BdlHV14
, Proposition C.1.6]
. It follows that
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}
is a trace on the group
G
.

Now, assume that both traces
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are finite-dimensional, namely
\varphi_{i}=\frac{1}{\dim\pi_{i}}\mathrm{tr}\circ\pi_{i}
for some pair of finite-dimensional unitary representations
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
of
G
. Then
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}
is also finite-dimensional, for
\varphi_{1}\cdot\varphi_{2}=\frac{1}{\dim\pi}\mathrm{tr}\circ\pi
where
\pi=\pi_{1}\otimes\pi_{2}
is the tensor product representation.
∎

Bekka’s vanishing lemma for asymptotically orthogonal sequences

A very useful tool in the study of characters is a vanishing lemma of Bekka
[
Bek07
, Lemma 16]
; see
[
LL23
, Lemma 4.14]
for a relative version.
It is used to show that a character which restricts to the Dirac character on a large subgroup must vanish outside of that subgroup.
Here we present a generalization dealing with characters that
asymptotically
vanish.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{H}
be a sequence of unit vectors. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle\xi_{n},\xi_{m}\rangle=0
for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
then the sequence
\xi_{n}
weakly converges to
0
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary vector
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
. We are required to show that
\lim_{n}\langle\xi_{n},\eta\rangle=0
. We may assume without loss of generality that

\eta
is in the closure of the span of the vectors
\xi_{n}
by replacing
\eta
with its orthogonal projection to that subspace.
For each
\varepsilon>0
there is some
N\in\mathbb{N}
and values
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\xi_{i}-\eta\|<\varepsilon
. This means that

\limsup_{n}|\langle\xi_{n},\eta\rangle|\leq\limsup_{n}|\langle\xi_{n},\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\xi_{i}\rangle|+\varepsilon=\varepsilon.

The desired conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 5.10

(Generalised Bekka lemma)
.

Consider a trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
. Fix an element
g\in G
. If there is a sequence of elements
x_{n}\in G
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi([g,x_{n}]^{-1}[g,x_{m}])=0

for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
then
\varphi(g)=0
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi},\pi_{\varphi},v_{\varphi})
be the GNS data associated to the trace
\varphi
. Namely
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
is a Hilbert space,
\pi_{\varphi}:G\to\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{\varphi})
is a unitary representation and
v_{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
is a cyclic vector satisfying

\varphi(g)=\langle\pi_{\varphi}(g)v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi}\rangle\hskip 10.00002pt\forall g\in G.

It follows that the vectors
\xi_{n}=\pi_{\varphi}(\left[g,x_{n}\right])v_{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
satisfy

\langle\xi_{n},\xi_{m}\rangle=\langle\pi([g,x_{m}])\xi,\pi([g,x_{n}])\xi\rangle=\varphi([g,x_{n}]^{-1}[g,x_{m}])\hskip 10.00002pt\forall n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}.

The assumption of this lemma together with Lemma
5.9
imply that the sequence
\xi_{n}
converges to
0
in the weak topology. Consequently

\displaystyle\varphi(g)=\lim_{n}\varphi(x_{n}^{-1}gx_{n})=\lim_{n}\varphi(g[g,x_{n}])

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}\langle\pi_{\varphi}(g[g,x_{n}])v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}\langle\xi_{n},\pi_{\varphi}(g^{-1})v_{\varphi}\rangle=0.

∎

We will typically apply Lemma
5.10
in the situation where the elements
x_{n}
belong to some normal subgroup
N\lhd G
. In that case, the product of commutators
[g,x_{n}]^{-1}[g,x_{m}]
will belong to the subgroup
N
as well. Thus Lemma
5.10
recovers Bekka’s vanishing lemma in the special case where the restriction of the trace
\varphi
to the normal subgroup
N
is Dirac.

6.
Locally inner and inducing subgroups

We have introduced locally inner actions and subgroups in §
2
. These actions are
“degenerate”
from the point of view of character theory and make induction and restriction of traces easier to study. Here is why.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
G
be a countable group and
N
a locally inner subgroup of
G
. Then

(1)

\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
.

(2)

If
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
then
\varphi_{\mid N}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)
.

(3)

If
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
then
\widetilde{\psi}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.

Proof.

The conjugation action on the group
G
on its normal subgroup
N
induces an affine action of the group
G
on the compact convex set
\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
. We claim that this action is trivial. Indeed, consider some trace
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
and some element
g\in G
. The automorphism of
N
corresponding to conjugation by
g
is locally inner. So for every element
n\in N
there is some element
m\in N
such that
n^{g}=n^{m}
. Hence
\varphi^{g}(n)=\varphi(n^{g})=\varphi(n^{m})=\varphi(n)
. We get
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}
. Item (1) follows.

To prove
(2)
consider a character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
. This means that the restriction
\varphi_{\mid N}
is an extreme point of the compact convex set
\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}
of relative traces
[
BF22
, Theorem 2.11]
. However
\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
by (1). Therefore
\varphi_{\mid N}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)
as required.

We turn to proving
(3)
. Consider a trace
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)
. Its trivial extension
\widetilde{\psi}
is normalized and positive definite; see
[
BdlH20
, Proposition 1.F.9]
. From the fact that
\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(N\right)^{G}
, it is immediate that
\widetilde{\psi}
is conjugation invariant as a function on
G
. So
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.
∎

Inducing subgroups

The following notion captures a vanishing phenomenon which will play a role throughout our analysis.

Definition 6.2
.

A normal subgroup
N\lhd G
is called
inducing
(respectively
weakly inducing
) if every character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
such that
N\cap\ker\varphi\lneq N
(respectively
\left[N:N\cap\ker\varphi\right]=\infty
) satisfies
\varphi(g)=0
for all
g\in G\setminus N
.

Note that being inducing is a relative notion, which depends on the normal subgroup
N
as well as on the encompassing group
G
. It is clear that every inducing subgroup is weakly inducing. A simple weakly inducing subgroup is inducing.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
G
be a countable group and
N
a locally inner subgroup of
G
. Let
\psi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)
be a character. Assume either that

(1)

N
is inducing and
\psi
is non-trivial, or

(2)

N
is weakly inducing and
\left[N:\ker\psi\right]=\infty
.

Then the trivial extension of
\psi
satisfies
\widetilde{\psi}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition under the first assumption, namely that the subgroup
N
is inducing and the character
\psi
is non-trivial. We know from Item (3) of Proposition
6.1
that
\widetilde{\psi}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.
By Choquet’s theorem, there exists a unique Borel probability measure

\mu
on the space
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
such that

\widetilde{\psi}(g)=\int_{\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)}\chi(g)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(\chi)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall g\in G.

Consider the restriction map

r:\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\to\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right),\hskip 10.00002ptr:\varphi\mapsto\varphi_{\mid N}.

This map is well defined by Item (1) of Proposition
6.1
. It is clearly continuous. Applying restriction we get

\psi(g)=\int_{\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)}r(\chi)(g)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(\chi)=\int_{\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)}\chi^{\prime}(g)\;\mathrm{d}\,r_{*}\mu(\chi^{\prime})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall g\in N.

where
r_{*}\mu
is the pushforward measure on
\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)
.
Since
\psi
is a character of the group
N
, the measure
r_{*}\mu
must be the Dirac measure supported on
\psi
. This means that
\mu
-almost every character of
G
restricts to the particular non-trivial character
\psi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)
. Let
\chi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
be any such character. As the subgroup
N
is inducing we have
\chi(g)=0
for all
g\in G\setminus N
. Therefore
\chi=\widetilde{\psi}
as required.
We omit the proof in the weakly inducing case, as it follows similarly.
∎

We conclude §
6
with a general character classification result for any countable group admitting a locally inner inducing subgroup.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
N\lhd G
be a locally inner inducing subgroup with quotient group
Q=G/N
.
If the quotient group
Q
is ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\cong\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(Q\right)\sqcup\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)\right)/\left(\delta_{e}^{Q}\sim 1_{N}\right).

If the quotient group
Q
is not ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\cong\mathrm{Ch}\left(Q\right)\sqcup\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)\backslash\{1_{N}\}\right).

These identifications are understood in the sense of topological spaces.

Proof.

Let
p:G\to Q
denote the quotient map. Then the map

f_{1}:\mathrm{Ch}\left(Q\right)\to\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right),\hskip 10.00002ptf_{1}:\varphi\mapsto\varphi\circ p

is well-defined by Proposition
5.4
. Furthermore, it follows from the assumptions together with Proposition
6.3
that the map

f_{2}:\mathrm{Ch}\left(N\right)\setminus\{1_{N}\}\to\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right),\hskip 10.00002ptf_{2}:\psi\mapsto\widetilde{\psi}

is also well-defined. The two maps
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are injective and satisfy
\mathrm{Im}(f_{1})\cap\mathrm{Im}(f_{2})=\emptyset
. It follows from Item (2) of Proposition
6.1
that
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)=\mathrm{Im}(f_{1})\cup\mathrm{Im}(f_{2})
. A direct verification shows that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are both homeomorphisms onto their respective images.

To conclude the proof it remains the compute the closures of
\mathrm{Im}(f_{1})
and
\mathrm{Im}(f_{2})
. Note that a character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
belongs to
\mathrm{Im}(f_{1})
if and only if
\varphi(g)=1
for every
g\in N
. Therefore
\mathrm{Im}(f_{1})
is a closed subset of
\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
.
Likewise, any trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
belonging to the closure
\overline{\mathrm{Im}(f_{2})}
satisfies
\varphi(g)=0
for all
g\in G\setminus N
. This means that any character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Im}(f_{1})\cap\overline{\mathrm{Im}(f_{2})}
must coincide with the characteristic function of the subgroup
N
. This function is a character of the group
G
if and only if the quotient
Q
is ICC
[
BdlH20
, Proposition 7.A.1]
.
∎

7.
Characters of finitary alternating groups and alternating enrichments

The topic of the current section is the character theory of finite as well as finitary alternating groups. We also consider alternating enrichments.
Recall that in this work characters (in general, and of finite groups in particular) are always
irreducible
and
normalized
so that
\varphi(e)=1
.

Finite alternating groups

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\Alt(\left[n\right])
and
\Sym(\left[n\right])
respectively denote the alternating and the symmetric permutation groups on the finite set
\left[n\right]
. For each permutation
\sigma\in\Sym(\left[n\right])
we consider its
support
, namely

(1)

\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)=\{i\in\left[n\right]\>:\>\sigma(i)\neq i\}.

We begin with the following asymptotic result derived from the character bounds of Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\sigma_{n}\in\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])
be a sequence of even permutations satisfying

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log(|\supp(\sigma_{n})|)}{\log(n)}=1.

Then
\lim_{n}\varphi_{n}(\sigma_{n})=0
for all sequences of non-trivial characters
\varphi_{n}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Alt\left(\left[n\right]\right)\right)
.

Prior to proving Lemma
7.1
, we recall the relationship between characters of the symmetric group
\mathrm{Sym}(\left[n\right])
and the alternating group
\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])
.
If
\chi
is a character of
\mathrm{Sym}(\left[n\right])
then either its restriction
\chi|_{\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])}
is
a character, or there exist two distinct characters
\chi_{+},\chi_{-}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])\right)
such that
\chi=\frac{1}{2}\chi^{+}+\frac{1}{2}\chi^{-}
.
It is known that all characters of the alternating group
\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])
are obtained in one of these two ways
[
JK81
, Theorem 2.5.7]
.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

By a Theorem of Larsen and Shalev
[
LS08
, Theorem 1.3]
there exist
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n>N
every normalized character
\chi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Sym}(\left[n\right])\right)
and every element
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\left[n\right])
having at most
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
fixed points satisfy

|\chi(\sigma)|\leq\dim(\chi)^{-1/5}.

Here
\dim(\chi)
is the dimension of the corresponding irreducible representation. It is well known that except for the trivial and sign representations, we have

\dim(\chi)\geq n-1
. In that case the above bound reads

|\chi(\sigma)|\leq(n-1)^{-1/5}.

It remains to transfer this bound to the alternating group
\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])
.
Take some
n>N
. Let
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])\right)
be a non-trivial character and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Alt}(\left[n\right])
be a permutation having at most
n^{\frac{1}{2}}
fixed points.
By the discussion in the paragraph preceding this lemma, either the character
\varphi
is the restriction of some character
\chi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Sym}(\left[n\right])\right)
or
\varphi
is one of
\chi_{+},\chi_{-}
associated to some character
\chi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Sym}(\left[n\right])\right)
. In the former case, the desired conclusion follows from the above-mentioned bound of Larsen and Shalev for the symmetric group. In the latter case, the Young diagram associated to the character
\chi
must be self-conjugate. As such, its first row cannot contain more than
\frac{n}{2}
cells. In that situation
|\varphi(\sigma)|\leq q^{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}
for some constant
0<q<1
, see
[
Roi96
, Theorem 5.4]
.
∎

Finitary alternating groups

Let
Y
be an infinite countable set. We let
\mathrm{Sym}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
denote the group of all finitely supported permutations of the set
Y
. Likewise, the group
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
consists of all even such permutations.

Proposition 7.2
.

Fix a non-trivial character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)\right)
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup\{|\varphi(g)|\>:\>g\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right),|\mathrm{supp}(g)|=n\}=0.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary sequence of elements
g_{n}\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
such that the sequence
a_{n}=|\mathrm{supp}(g_{n})|
is strictly monotone increasing. To establish the lemma, it will suffice to show that
\lim\varphi(g_{n})=0
. Up to conjugating the elements
g_{n}
appropriately, we may find a nested sequence of subsets
Z_{n}\subset Y
with
|Z_{n}|=a_{n}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(g_{n})=Z_{n}
. In other words, each element
g_{n}
belongs to the finite subgroup
\Alt(Z_{n})
of
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
. Up to a further conjugation, we may assume that
\bigcup Z_{n}=Y
.
Write the restriction
\psi_{n}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Alt(Z_{n})\right)
of the character
\psi
to the subgroup
\Alt(Z_{n})
as
\psi_{n}=\alpha_{n}+(1-\alpha_{n})\psi^{\prime}_{n}
, where
0\leq\alpha_{n}\leq 1
and
\psi^{\prime}_{n}
is a trace which does not dominate
2

2

[
BdlH20
, 11.C]
, see also
[
LV23
, §2]
.
the trivial trace. Note that certainly
\lim\psi^{\prime}_{n}(g_{n})=0
by Lemma
7.1
and its proof. To conclude the proof we argue that
\lim\alpha_{n}=0
. Assume towards contradiction (and up to passing to a subsequence) that
\alpha=\lim\alpha_{n}>0
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large so that
\alpha_{n}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
for all
n>N
. Then the non-trivial character
\varphi
dominates the trivial character
\frac{\alpha}{2}1
, which is a contradiction to Proposition 2.2 and Corollary 2.3 of
[
LV23
]
.
∎

Thoma
[
Tho64a
]
has obtained a celebrated character classification for the two groups
\mathrm{Sym}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Z\right)
and
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Z\right)
. It is possible to obtain a proof of Proposition
7.2
directly from that classification. We refer the reader also to
[
Tho22
]
.

Permutations with infinite support

Let
Y
be an infinite countable set.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
r\in\Sym(Y)
be an infinitely supported permutation.
Then there exists a sequence of finitely supported permutations
x_{k}\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
such that for every fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}

\lim_{k\to\infty}|\mathrm{supp}(\left[r,x_{k}\right]^{-1}\left[r,x_{m}\right])|=\infty.

Proof.

The proof will depend on whether the permutations
r
admits an infinite orbit or not.

Assume first that
r
does admit an infinite orbit. Restricting attention to this orbit, we may and will assume without loss of generality that
Y={\mathbb{Z}}
and
r(i)=i+1
for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Denote
s_{i}=(i,i+1)
so that each
s_{i}
is a transposition. For each
odd
value
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
consider the permutation
x_{k}\in\AltZ
given by

x_{k}=\prod_{i=0}^{k}s_{2(i+k^{2})}=s_{2k^{2}}\cdot s_{2+2k^{2}}\cdots s_{2k+2k^{2}}.

It is easy to verify directly that the conjugation of
x_{k}
by
r
is the permutation

x_{k}^{r}=\prod_{i=0}^{k}s_{1+2i+2k^{2}}=s_{1+2k^{2}}\cdot s_{3+2k^{2}}\cdots s_{1+2k+2k^{2}}.

It follows that the commutator element
y_{k}=[r,x_{k}]=x_{k}^{-1}x_{k}^{r}
has support

\supp(y_{k})=\left\{j\ \>:\>2k^{2}\leq j\leq 2k^{2}+2k+2\right\}.

The size of these sets is unbounded and they are pairwise disjoint. The desired conclusion follows.

We pause to make a useful observation. Consider a permutation
z\in\Sym(Y)
and suppose that it has admits at least two orbits
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Let
t=(x_{1},x_{2})
be an arbitrary transposition for some pair of points
x_{1}\in S_{1}
and
x_{2}\in S_{2}
. Then the commutator
[z,t]
is the product of two transpositions supported inside
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
.
More generally, suppose that
z
admits
2k
orbits
S_{1},S_{2},...,S_{2k}
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Choose arbitrary points
x_{i}\in S_{i}
. For each
i=1,...,k
consider the transposition
t_{i}=(x_{2i-1},x_{2i})
and write
t=t_{1}\cdots t_{k}
. Then the commutator
[z,t]
splits as a product
[z,t]=[z,t_{1}]\cdots[z,t_{k}]
. Thus
[z,t]
is the product of
2k
transpositions supported in
S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{2k}
.
This observation will help us deal with the following situation.

Assume next that the permutation
r
is infinitely supported but has no infinite orbits. As such
r
has infinitely many finite orbits. Enumerate and denote these orbits by
S_{i}
where
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Choose arbitrary points
x_{i}\in S_{i}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Consider the transposition
t_{i}=(x_{2i-1},x_{2i})
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
We set

x_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}t_{i+k^{2}}=t_{1+k^{2}}\cdots t_{k+k^{2}}.

Using the observation above we see that the commutator
[r,x_{k}]
is the product of
2k
-many transpositions whose supports are contained
in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{2k}S_{i+2k^{2}}
. In particular, the supports of
[r,x_{k}]
are pairwise disjoint and of unbounded size, as desired.
∎

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
H
be a countable group acting on an infinite countable set
Y
.
Consider the semidirect product
G=H\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
corresponding to the resulting action of
H
on
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
by automorphisms.
If every non-trivial element of
H
has infinite support in its action on
Y
then the normal subgroup
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
of
G
is inducing.

Proof.

Fix an element
g\in G
and write
g=hx
for some pair of elements
h\in H
and
x\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
. Assume that
g\notin\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
, or what is equivalent
h
is non-trivial. As the action of the element
h
on the set
Y
admits an infinite
orbit, whereas the permutation
x
is finitely supported, their product
g
admits an infinite
orbit in its action on
Y
as well. By Lemma
7.3
there exists a sequence
of elements
x_{k}\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
such that the commutators
y_{n}=\left[g,x_{n}\right]\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
satisfy
\lim_{n}|\mathrm{supp}(y_{n}^{-1}y_{m})|=\infty
for each fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Note that
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
is a locally inner subgroup of
G
, see Example
2.8
and the remarks following it. Let
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
be any character such that the restriction of
\varphi
to the normal subgroup
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
is not-trivial. We know that this restriction is a character by Proposition
6.1
. It follows from Proposition
7.2
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi(y_{n}^{-1}y_{m})=0

for each fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The desired conclusion follows from our vanishing result for asymptotically orthonormal sequences, see Lemma
5.10
.
∎

It is worth noting that every automorphism of the group
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Y\right)
arises from conjugation by some element of
\Sym(Y)

[
DM96
, Theorem 8.2A]
.

Characters of alternating enrichments

Recall that the construction of alternating enrichments was introduced in §
4
.

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
G
be an infinite group with alternating enrichment
\mathscr{A}(G)
. If the group
G
is ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathscr{A}(G)\right)\cong\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\sqcup\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)\right)\right)/\left(\delta_{e}^{G}\sim 1_{\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)}\right).

If the group
G
is not ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathscr{A}(G)\right)\cong\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\sqcup\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)\right)\backslash\{1_{\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)}\}\right).

Proof.

This corollary follows immediately from Theorem
6.4
by taking into account the information that the normal subgroup
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(G\right)
is locally inner (see Example
2.8
) as well as inducing (by Proposition
7.4
).
∎

Corollary 7.6
.

Consider some semidirect product
G={\mathbb{Z}}\ltimes\AltZ
. If the automorphism of the group
\AltZ
corresponding to the generator of the group
{\mathbb{Z}}
arises from a permutation with infinite orbit of the underlying set of
{\mathbb{Z}}
then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)=S^{1}\sqcup\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(\AltZ\right)\backslash\{1_{\AltZ}\}\right).

Proof.

The abelian group
{\mathbb{Z}}
is certainly not ICC. Its character space coincides with its Pontryagin dual, namely
\mathrm{Ch}\left({\mathbb{Z}}\right)=\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cong S^{1}
.
∎

The above two statements deal with the finitary alternating group
\AltZ
. Alternating groups are better suited for our purposes. In any case, these two statements hold true verbatim (and with the same proofs) if the group
\AltZ
is replaced by the finitary symmetric group
\SymZ
.

8.
Characters of finitary special linear groups and elementary enrichments

Let
p
be a prime. Fix the finite field

F=\mathbb{F}_{q}
for some
q=p^{k}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\overline{F}
denote the algebraic closure of the field
F
. We consider the character theory of the finite as well as finitary special linear groups over the field
F
.
Furthermore, we study characters of elementary enrichments. We remind the reader that for us, characters are always extremal as a point in
\mathrm{Tr}(G)
and normalized so that
\varphi(e)=1
.

Finite special linear groups

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we consider the finite special linear group
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
.

Definition 8.1
.

The support
\mathrm{supp}(g)
of an element
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
is the codimension of the largest eigenspace in its canonical action on the vector space
\overline{F}^{n}\cong F^{n}\otimes\overline{F}
.

Note that the support of a central element is zero. The support of a permutation matrix in the sense of Definition
8.1
is related to the support of the underlying permutation.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
be a permutation matrix associated to the even permutation
\sigma\in\Alt(n)
. If the permutation
\sigma
has an orbit of size
d
then
\mathrm{supp}(g)\geq d-1
.

Proof.

Assume that
\sigma
has an orbit of size
d
. Let
V\leq\overline{F}^{n}
be the
d
-dimensional subspace corresponding to this orbit. The restriction of the matrix
g
to the invariant subspace
V
is diagonizable with
d
-many distinct eigenvalues
1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}
. Here
\omega\in\overline{F}
is a
d
-th root of unity. This certainly implies that
\mathrm{supp}(g)\geq d-1
.
∎

The size of the support (in the sense of Definition
8.1
) can be used to upper-bound values of normalized characters of finite special linear groups.

Theorem 8.3

(Theorem 1.2.1 of
[
LST11
]
)
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
be any non-trivial element and
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)\right)
be any non-trivial normalized character. Then

|\varphi(g)|\leq q^{-\sqrt{\mathrm{supp}(g)}/481}.

Unlike the case of finite alternating groups, normalized characters of finite special linear groups admit a universal upper bound.

Theorem 8.4

(Theorem 2.2 of
[
Glu97
]
)
.

Assume
q>10
. There is some constant
c<1
such that
|\varphi(g)|<c
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, all non-trivial characters
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)\right)
and all non-central elements
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
.

Finitary special linear groups

Let
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
denote the direct limit of the special linear groups
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(F)
as
n
tends to infinity.
Alternatively, we may view the group
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
as consisting of finitary matrices over a countably infinite index set. This means that the entries
g_{i,j}
of each matrix
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
differ from
\delta_{i,j}
only for finitely many pairs of indices
i
and
j
.

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)\right)
be any non-trivial character. Then

\lim_{n}\sup\{|\varphi(g)|\>:\>g\in\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right),\;\mathrm{rank}(g-\mathrm{I})=n\}=0.

Proof.

Let
g_{n}\in\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
be any sequence of elements with
a_{n}=\mathrm{rank}(g_{n}-\mathrm{I})
strictly monotone increasing. To establish the proposition it will suffice to show that
\lim\varphi(g_{n})=0
.

Up to conjugating each element by a suitable permutation matrix, we may and will assume that
g_{n}\in G_{n}
where the subgroup
G_{n}=\mathrm{SL}_{2a_{n}}(F)
is identified with an
“upper-left corner”
of the group
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
. In this way
1\in\overline{F}
will certainly be the largest eigenvalue of
g_{n}
regarded as an element of
G_{n}
acting of the finite-dimensional subspace
\overline{F}^{2a_{n}}
. Therefore
\mathrm{supp}(g_{n})=a_{n}
, where the notion of support is as in Definition
8.1
and
g_{n}
is regarded as an element of the finite group
G_{n}
.

Write the restriction
\psi_{n}
of the character
\varphi
to the subgroup
G_{n}
as a convex combination
\psi_{n}=\alpha_{n}\cdot 1+(1-\alpha_{n})\psi^{\prime}_{n}
where
\psi^{\prime}_{n}
is a trace which does not dominate the trivial character
1\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{n}\right)
. We know that
\lim\psi^{\prime}_{n}(g)=0
by Theorem
8.3
. To conclude to proof it remains to show that
\lim\alpha_{n}=0
.
Assume towards contradiction (and up to passing to a subsequence) that
\alpha=\lim\alpha_{n}>0
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large so that
\alpha_{n}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
for all
n>N
. Then the non-trivial character
\varphi
dominates the trivial character
\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot 1
, which is a contradiction to Proposition 2.2 and Corollary 2.3 of
[
LV23
]
.
∎

Alternatively, Proposition
8.5
can be deduced quite directly from the character classification of the group
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
available in
[
Sku76
]
. Proposition
8.5
is the special linear groups analogue of Proposition
7.2
.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
be a permutation matrix associated to the even permutation
\sigma\in\AltZ
. Then

\mathrm{rank}(g-\mathrm{I})=|\supp(\sigma)|-l\geq\frac{1}{2}|\supp(\sigma)|

where
l
is the number of orbits of the permutation
\sigma
.

Lemma
8.6
is very similar to Lemma
8.2
.

Proof of Lemma
8.6
.

For each orbit of size
d
of the permutation
\sigma
, the matrix
g
has a
d
-dimensional invariant subspace with distinct eigenvalues
1,\omega,\ldots,\omega^{d-1}
where
\omega
is a
d
-th root of unity. In particular, all but one of these eigenvalues are distinct from
1\in\overline{F}
. The desired conclusion follows.
∎

Proposition 8.7
.

Let
\sigma
be the automorphism of the finitary special linear group
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
corresponding to conjugation by a permutation matrix admitting an infinite orbit. Then there is a sequence of elements
x_{n}\in\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(F\right)
such that

\lim_{n}\mathrm{rank}(\left[\sigma,x_{n}\right]^{-1}\left[\sigma,x_{m}\right]-\mathrm{I})=\infty

for each fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The desired conclusion follows by letting the
x_{n}
’s be the permutation matrices associated to the particular permutations constructed in Lemma
7.3
. Lemma
8.6
can then be used to estimate the rank of the resulting commutators and their products.
∎

Characters of elementary enrichments

The elementary enrichment construction was introduced in §
4
. We consider the character theory of these enrichments.

Corollary 8.8
.

Let
G
be an infinite group with elementary enrichment
{\mathscr{E}(G)}
. If the group
G
is ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathscr{E}(G)\right)\cong\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\sqcup\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)\right)\right)/\left(\delta_{e}^{G}\sim 1_{\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)}\right).

If the group
G
is not ICC then

\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathscr{E}(G)\right)\cong\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)\sqcup\left(\mathrm{Ch}\left(\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)\right)\backslash\{1_{\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)}\}\right).

Proof.

Note that the normal subgroup
\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
of the elementary enrichment
\mathscr{E}(G)=G\ltimes\mathrm{SL}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(V_{G}\right)
is locally inner. It is inducing, as can be seen by combining Proposition
8.7
with our vanishing result Lemma
5.10
. The desired result now follows as a special case of Theorem
6.4
.

9.
Characters of diagonal products

Let
G
be a group generated by a finite set
S
and equipped with a LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
(see Definition
2.1
). In addition, we assume that the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}
is essential and by simple groups (see Definition
2.3
).

This section is dedicated to analyzing the character theory of the diagonal product
\bigotimes_{n}G_{n}
. In particular, we will prove Theorem
1.7
. Throughout the current section, we will be using notations and terminology set up in §
2
.

The following general observation will be useful. It follows from the local nature of the definition of traces (we leave the straightforward proof to the reader).

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
f_{n}:G\to H_{n}
be a partial homomorphism and

\varphi_{n}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(H_{n}\right)
be a sequence of traces. Then any accumulation point in the space
\ell^{\infty}(G)
of the sequence
\varphi_{n}\circ f_{n}
belongs to
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.

Diagonal products and compatible traces

Let
\Gamma=\bigotimes G_{n}
be the diagonal product of the finite groups
G_{n}
associated to the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
and to the generating set
S
. Let
t:\Gamma\to G
be the associated tail map. Denote
U=\ker t
so that
U=\bigoplus G_{n}
by Lemma
2.5
. In addition, recall that the normal subgroup
U
is locally inner in
\Gamma
by Lemma
2.10
.

Let
\zeta\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Gamma\right)
be any character. The fact that the subgroup
U
is locally inner implies that the restriction
\varphi=\zeta_{|U}
is a character of
U
, see Proposition
6.1
. Recall that the character space of the direct sum
U
is given by
\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)\cong\prod_{i}\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)
, see Proposition
5.6
.
As such, the character
\varphi
can be written as
\varphi=\otimes\varphi_{i}
for some uniquely determined sequence of characters
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)
.

Definition 9.2
.

An element
\tau\in G
is
null
for a character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
if
every
element
g=(g_{i})\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
satisfies

(2)

\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_{i}(g_{i})|=0.

A trace
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is
compatible
with a character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
if
\psi(\tau)=0
for every element
\tau\in G
which is null for
\varphi
.

Every trace
\varphi
satisfies
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, so that the partial products appearing in Equation (
2
) are monotone non-increasing, hence the infinite product of absolute values always exists.
Note that the subset of
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
consisting of those traces compatible with each given character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
is closed.

Theorem 9.3
.

Fix a character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
given by
\varphi=\otimes\varphi_{i}
with
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G_{i}\right)
. Then

\{\zeta\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)\>:\>\zeta_{|U}=\varphi\}\cong\{\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)\>:\>\text{$\psi$ is compatible with $\varphi$}\}.

The formula for this correspondence is
\zeta=\widehat{\varphi}\cdot(\psi\circ t)
where
\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)
is a certain auxiliary trace.

Proof.

We will construct a pair of continuous affine maps
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
in both directions. These two maps will depend on a choice of an auxiliary object, as follows.

Recall that the pointwise product of two traces is a trace
[
BdlHV14
, Proposition C.1.6]
. In particular, the function
\widehat{\varphi}_{n}:g\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(g_{i})
is a trace on the group
\Gamma
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This trace depends only on the projection of each element to the subgroup
U_{n}
. Fix an arbitrary accumulation point
\widehat{\varphi}
of the sequence
\widehat{\varphi}_{n}
in the compact space of traces
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)
. Let
n_{k}
be some subsequence such that
\widehat{\varphi}_{n_{k}}\to\widehat{\varphi}
.

We construct the map
F_{1}
in one direction. Let
\zeta\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)
be a trace with
\zeta_{|U}=\varphi
.
Write
\Gamma=U_{n}\times\Gamma_{n+1}
for each
n
. The restriction of
\zeta
to the subgroup
U_{n}
is a character. Take any element
g\in\Gamma
and write
g=g_{1}\cdots g_{n}g^{\prime}
where
g^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{n+1}\leq\Gamma
and
g_{i}\in G_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Corollary
5.7
implies that

(3)

\zeta(g)=\varphi_{1}(g_{1})\cdots\varphi_{n}(g_{n})\zeta(g^{\prime}).

Having fixed the element
g\in\Gamma
and assuming that the index
n
is sufficiently large so that
g^{\prime}=\Phi_{n+1}\circ t(g)
we can reformulate Equation (
3
) as

(4)

\zeta(g)=\varphi_{1}(g_{1})\cdots\varphi_{n}(g_{n})\zeta(\Phi_{n+1}\circ t(g)).

For each
n
, set
\psi_{n}:G\to\mathbb{C}
by
\psi_{n}=\zeta\circ\Phi_{n+1}
.
Equation (
4
) can be rewritten as

(5)

\zeta=\varphi_{1}\cdots\varphi_{n}\cdot(\psi_{n}\circ t).

Equation (
5
) holds true for every element
g\in\Gamma
and for all
n
sufficiently large (depending on
g
).

Note that each element
g\in\Gamma_{n}
can we decomposed uniquely as
g=hg^{\prime}
with
h\in G_{n}
and
g^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{n+1}
. According to Corollary
5.7
, every such element
g
satisfies

(6)

\psi_{n-1}(t(g))=\varphi_{n}(h)\cdot\psi_{n}(t(g^{\prime})).

We claim that the limit
\lim_{k}\psi_{n_{k}}
exists in the space
\ell^{\infty}(G)
(pointwise convergence). For a given element
\tau\in G
, there are two cases to consider:

•

The element
\tau\in G
is null for
\varphi
. Then by taking the limit
n\to\infty
in Equation (
5
), we see that
\zeta(g)=0
for all elements
g\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
. There are two subcases:

–

Every element
g\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
admits infinitely many
n
’s where
\varphi_{n}(g_{n})=0
. In this case, repeated application of Equation (
6
) shows that
\psi_{n}(\tau)=0
for all
n
sufficiently large.

–

There is some element
g=(g_{n})\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
such that
\varphi_{n}(g_{n})\neq 0
for all
n
sufficiently large. In this case, we may change finitely many coordinates of the element
g
to be the identity and assume without loss of generality that
\varphi_{n}(g_{n})\neq 0
for all
n
. It now follows from Equation (
5
) that
\psi_{n}(\tau)=0
for all
n
sufficiently large.

In either situation, we conclude that
\psi_{n}(\tau)=0
for all
n
sufficiently large. In particular
\lim_{n}\psi_{n}(\tau)=0
(and this limit exists even without passing to a subsequence).

•

The element
\tau\in G
is not null for
\varphi
. By definition, there is some element
g=(g_{i})\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
so that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}|\varphi_{i}(g_{i})|>0.

Note that the limit
\widehat{\varphi}_{n_{k}}(g)
exists along the subsequence
n_{k}
and is different from
0
. Therefore the limit as
k\to\infty
of the expressions

(7)

\psi_{n_{k}}(\tau)=\frac{\zeta(g)}{\widehat{\varphi}_{n_{k}}(g)}

exists as well. Observe that the value of the limit is independent of the particular choice of the element
g
, since the left-hand side
\psi_{n_{k}}(\tau)
depends only on
\tau
.

Denote
\psi=\lim_{k}\psi_{n_{k}}\in\ell^{\infty}(G)
. We now claim that
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
. This follows from the fact that
\Phi_{n}
are partial homomorphisms, see Lemma
9.1
.
This defines an affine map
F_{1}:\zeta\mapsto\psi
. The continuity of the map
F_{1}
follows from Equation (
7
). Further, the trace
\psi
is compatible with the character
\varphi
, as follows from the discussion in the first bullet above.

We now construct the map
F_{2}
in the converse direction. Let
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
be any trace compatible with the fixed character
\varphi
. We define a trace
\zeta\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)
as follows

\zeta(g)=\widehat{\varphi}(g)\cdot(\psi\circ t)(g)\hskip 10.00002pt\forall g\in\Gamma.

This is a trace, being a pointwise product of the two traces
\widehat{\varphi}
and
\psi\circ t
on
\Gamma
, see Proposition
5.8
. Note that
\zeta_{|U}=\varphi
as required. This defines a second continuous affine map
F_{2}:\psi\mapsto\zeta
.

It remains to verify that the composition
F_{2}\circ F_{1}
is the identity on the set
\{\zeta\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)\>:\>\zeta_{|U}=\varphi\}
and that the composition
F_{1}\circ F_{2}
is the identity on the set
\{\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)\>:\>\text{$\psi$ is compatible with $\varphi$}\}
.

•

For
F_{1}\circ F_{2}
: let
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
be any trace compatible with the character
\varphi
. Then the trace
\psi^{\prime}=(F_{1}\circ F_{2})(\psi)\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
is also compatible with
\varphi
by construction. Therefore it suffices to check that
\psi(\tau)=\psi^{\prime}(\tau)
only for elements
\tau\in G
that are not null for
\varphi
.
Denote
\zeta=F_{2}(\psi)=\widehat{\varphi}\cdot(\psi\circ t)
. Consider a non-null element
\tau\in G
. Take any element
g\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
for which
\ \prod_{i=1}^{\infty}|\varphi_{i}(g_{i})|>0
. We have

\psi^{\prime}(\tau)=\lim_{k}\psi^{\prime}_{n_{k}}(\tau)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\zeta(g)}{\widehat{\varphi}_{n_{k}}(g)}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\widehat{\varphi}(g)\cdot\psi(\tau)}{\widehat{\varphi}_{n_{k}}(g)}=\psi(\tau).

•

For
F_{2}\circ F_{1}
: let
\zeta\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Gamma\right)
be any trace with
\zeta_{|U}=\varphi
. Denote
\psi=F_{1}(\zeta)
. Then for any element
g\in\Gamma
we have

\displaystyle(F_{2}\circ F_{1})(\zeta)(g)=\widehat{\varphi}(g)\cdot\psi(t(g))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\varphi}_{n_{k}}(g)\cdot\psi_{n_{k}}(t(g))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\zeta(g)=\zeta(g).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 9.4
.

While the spaces on both sides of the above theorem are well-defined, the isomorphism between them is not canonical as it depends on a choice of a certain accumulation point
\widehat{\varphi}
(and subsequence

).

Compatible characters

The set of traces of
G
which are compatible with a fixed trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(U\right)
form a compact and convex subset of
\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
. We are interested in the extreme points of this space.

Definition 9.5
.

A trace
\psi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
compatible with a given fixed character
\varphi\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
is called a

if
\psi
cannot be written as a non-trivial convex combination of two traces on the group

both compatible with
\varphi
.

As such, the proof of Theorem
1.5
follows immediately from Definition
9.5
, and the affine correspondence established in Theorem
9.3
.
We spell out Definition
9.5
in two extreme cases:

•

If every non-trivial element

is null for the character
\varphi
then the only compatible trace (which is also a compatible character) is
\delta_{e}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
.

•

If no element
g\in G
is null for
\varphi
then all traces are compatible, and compatible characters are just characters of
G
.

Example 9.6
.

In this example
\Gamma
is the alternating diagonal product associated to the group

. This is the classical B.H. Neumann group,

below for more details. Specifically, the subgroup

with

.

•

Assume that the Young diagrams associated to the characters

are balanced, i.e. there is some constant

rows and columns. Then every non-trivial element

is null for

, see the main result of

]
.

•

Assume that

is the character of the standard

-dimensional representation on

for all

if null for

if and only if

Inducing subgroups of diagonal products

Let

be some normal subgroup. Denote

.

.

Let

be a character so that

where

. Assume that

•

\left[U:\ker\varphi\right]=\infty
, and

•

.

By definition, an element

for every element

Proposition 9.8
.

Let
\zeta\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Gamma\right)
be a character. Assume that
\left[U:U\cap\ker\zeta\right]<\infty
and

. If the subgroup

of

.

Proof.

Denote

factors trough this direct product. As such

.

Let

be the diagonal product associated to the generating set

by simple groups. Let

(1)

Consider any character

and assume that

An application to characters of simple groups

The proof of Theorem
9.3
has a shortcoming in that it depends on the choice of the auxiliary trace
\widehat{\varphi}
. Interestingly, if the LEF group
G
is simple (or admits a simple normal subgroup
N
) then we can show a posteriori that this trace
\widehat{\varphi}
is uniquely determined.

Corollary 9.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a diagonal product with tail map
t:\Gamma\to G
.
Assume that
N\lhd G
is a non-abelian simple locally inner normal subgroup. Denote
\Delta=t^{-1}(N)
. Fix an arbitrary character
\varphi=\otimes\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
. Then the sequence

\widehat{\varphi}_{n}:g\mapsto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(g_{i})

is convergent in
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta\right)
and its limit is a character of
\Delta
.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{\varphi}
and
\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}
be any two accumulation points of the sequence
\widehat{\varphi}_{n}
in the (compact) space of traces
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta\right)
. Both accumulation points satisfy
\widehat{\varphi}_{|U}=\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}_{|U}=\varphi
. Abusing notation, we let
\widehat{\varphi}
and
\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}
denote the trivial extensions from
\Delta
to
\Gamma
. As the subgroup
\Delta\leq\Gamma
is locally inner by Lemma
2.10
, these trivial extensions are traces on
\Gamma
, see Proposition
6.1
.
According to Theorem
9.3
there is a pair of compatible traces
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)
such that

\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}=\widehat{\varphi}\cdot(\psi\circ t)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{\varphi}=\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}\cdot(\psi^{\prime}\circ t).

Substituting one equation into the other gives

\widehat{\varphi}(g)=\widehat{\varphi}(g)\cdot(\psi\cdot\psi^{\prime})(t(g))\hskip 10.00002pt\forall g\in\Gamma.

We claim that
|\psi(\tau)|=1
for any element
\tau\in G
which is not null for
\varphi
. Indeed, for every such element
\tau
there is by definition some
g\in\Gamma
with
t(g)=\tau
and
\widehat{\varphi}(g)\neq 0
. Recall that
\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. The claim follows.

There are now two separate cases to consider:

•

All non-trivial elements
\tau\in N
are null for the character
\varphi
. This implies by compatibility that
\psi=\delta_{e}=\psi^{\prime}
on
N
.

•

There is some non-trivial element
\tau\in N
which is not null for the character
\varphi
. Recall that
|\psi|^{2}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(G\right)

[
LL23
, Proposition 4.2]
. Therefore the normal subgroup
\ker|\psi|^{2}\lhd G
is non-trivial by the above claim. Since the group
N
of
G
is simple we get
N\leq\ker|\psi|^{2}
so that
|\psi|=1
on
N
. Therefore the trace
\psi
is multiplicative on
N
, see Lemma
5.3
. The non-abelian simple group
N
is perfect. We get
N\leq\ker\psi
. In particular, no element of
N
is null for
\varphi
.

In either case
\widehat{\varphi}=\widehat{\varphi}^{\prime}
on
\Delta
so that the sequence
\widehat{\varphi}_{n}
considered in the proof of Theorem
9.3
must be convergent in
\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta\right)
. In the first case
\widehat{\varphi}
is the trivial extension of the character
\varphi
from the subgroup
U
to the subgroup
\Delta
.
Since this is the only possible such extension, we conclude that
\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Delta\right)
- see the proof of Proposition
6.3
. In the second case, the accumulation point
\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Delta\right)
corresponds to the trivial character
1\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(G\right)
via the bijective affine correspondence established in Theorem
9.3
. As such
\widehat{\varphi}
is a character of
\Delta
.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
1.11
from the introduction.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

Let
x
be a fixed even permutation with
q=|\mathrm{\supp}(x)|
. Let
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
be a strictly increasing sequence of integers with
n_{1}\geq q
and
\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(\Alt(n_{i})\right)
irreducible normalized characters. We first consider the following two special cases.

•

All
n_{i}
’s are odd
.
Let
\Gamma=\bigotimes_{i}\Alt(n_{i})
be the classical B.H. Neumann group. It is constructed in detail in Example
4.5
. This group admits a tail map
t:\Gamma\to\mathscr{A}(\mathbb{Z})
. Consider the normal subgroups
N=\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\lhd\mathscr{A}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\Delta=t^{-1}(N)\lhd\Gamma
. We have
U=\bigoplus\Alt(n_{i})\lhd\Delta
.
Consider the character
\varphi=\otimes_{i}\varphi_{i}\in\mathrm{Ch}\left(U\right)
. With these objects at hand, Theorem
1.11
follows immediately from Corollary
9.10
.

•

All
n_{i}
’s are even
. To deal with the even case, the above proof needs to be modified, since a full cycle is now an odd permutation. Consider the peculiar group
G=\mathbb{Z}\ltimes\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
where the the action of the generator
\sigma_{0}
of the infinite cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}
on the normal subgroup
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
arises from the permutation
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}\in\Alt(\mathbb{Z})
given by

\widehat{\sigma}_{0}(n)=\begin{cases}n+1&n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0\},\\
1&n=-1,\\
0&n=0.\end{cases}

In other words
\widehat{\sigma}_{0}
is a full shift on the integers
“skipping over”
the point
0\in\mathbb{Z}
. Further, consider
\tau=(-1,0,1)\in\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(Z\right)
. The pair
\{\sigma_{0},\tau\}
generates the group
G
. There is a LEF approximation
\theta_{i}:G\to\Alt(n_{i})
in which
\theta_{i}(\sigma_{0})
is a
(n_{i}-1)
-cycle and
\theta_{i}(\tau)
is a
3
-cycle for all

. From this point onwards, we may consider the corresponding diagonal product
\Gamma=\bigotimes_{i}\Alt(n_{i})
and proceed exactly as in previous case. Note that Corollary
7.6
applies so that
\mathrm{Alt}_{\mathrm{fin}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
is an inducing subgroup of
G
.

In the general case, if all but finite many

’s are of the same parity, then the above argument still applies, for the conclusion of the theorem is not effected by ignoring finitely many
n_{i}
’s. If there infinitely many odd
n_{i}
’s as well as infinitely many even
n_{i}
’s, then the existence of the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(x)
in question follows by knowing it exists separately along odd and even
n_{i}
’s.
∎

10.
Approximating characters of alternating and elementary enrichments and diagonal products

In this section we take up the groups and constructions introduced in §
4
and prove several technical approximation results, in anticipation for the study of Hilbert–Schmidt stability of these groups.

Let
G
be a group with a finite generating set
S
and a fixed LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
by groups with pairwise distinct orders and satisfying the parity condition.

Let
\Gamma_{0}=G\ltimes N
be either the alternating enrichment
{\mathscr{A}(G)}
or the elementary enrichment
{\mathscr{E}(G)}
of the group
G
with its fixed set of generators

(see Lemmas
4.3
and
4.6
, respectively). Here the normal subgroup

is either

or

N
is normally generated by the set
T
.

Let
\Gamma=\bigotimes H_{n}
denote the associated alternating (or elementary) diagonal product arising from the LEF approximation
\Theta_{n}
of the enrichment
\Gamma_{0}
into the sequence of finite groups
H_{n}=\Alt(G_{n})
or
H_{n}=\mathrm{SL}(V_{G_{n}})
(see Definitions
4.4
and
4.7

It will be useful to denote

\Delta=t^{-1}(N)\leq\Gamma

where

is the tail map.

We may assume for the remainder of §
10
that the group

is infinite, for otherwise its enrichment
\Gamma_{0}
and the associated diagonal product
\Gamma
are both finite. In particular
|G_{n}|\to\infty
as

. We may rearrange the order of terms in the LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
without changing the isomorphism type of the diagonal product
\Gamma
and assume without loss of generality that
|G_{n}|\leq|G_{n+1}|
holds true for all

.

Choose an arbitrary sequence of injective set-theoretical maps
f_{n}:G_{n}\to G_{n+1}
subject to the condition that
\theta_{n+1}(g)=f_{n}\circ\theta_{n}(g)
for each fixed element

and for all

sufficiently large (depending on

). Let
h_{n}:H_{n}\to H_{n+1}
be the sequence of group monomorphisms obtained by using the sequence of maps

to identify the underlying set of the group

as a subset of the underlying set of the group
G_{n+1}
in the alternating case, and the vector space

as a subspace of the vector space

in the elementary case.

Observe that the LEF approximation
\Theta_{n}:\Gamma_{0}\to H_{n}
given by
“encoding into alternating/elementary groups”
satisfies
\Theta_{n+1}(x)=h_{n}\circ\Theta_{n}(x)
for each element

and all

sufficiently large.

Proposition 10.1
.

For each trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta\right)
there is a sequence of traces
\varphi_{k}\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta\right)
with
\left[\Delta:\ker\varphi_{k}\right]<\infty
for all

such that
\varphi_{k}\to\varphi
pointwise on
\Delta
.

Proof.

It will suffice to construct a sequence of group endomorphisms
\pi_{k}:\Delta\to\Delta
with the following two properties:

(1)

\left[\Delta:\ker\pi_{k}\right]<\infty
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and

(2)

For each element
x\in\Delta
we have
\pi_{k}(x)=x
for all but finitely many
k
’s.

Indeed, given such a sequence of endomorphisms
\pi_{k}
, for every trace
\varphi\in\mathrm{Tr}\left(\Delta\right)
on the tail group
\Delta
the sequence of traces
\varphi_{k}=\varphi\circ\pi_{k}
is as required.

We now construct a sequence of endomorphisms
\pi_{k}
satisfying the above two properties. Consider the quotient map
p_{k}:\Delta\to\prod_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
as well as the embedding

(8)

\iota_{k}:\prod_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\to\Delta,\hskip 10.00002pt\iota_{k}:(g_{1},...,g_{k})\mapsto(g_{1},..,g_{k},h_{k}(g_{k}),(h_{k+1}\circ h_{k})(g_{k}),...)

for each

.
The desired endomorphisms are given by
\pi_{k}=\iota_{k}\circ p_{k}:\Delta\to\Delta
. These satisfy

so that
\left[\Delta:\ker\pi_{k}\right]<\infty
for all

. We get property (1). Finally, property (2) follows from the fact that
h_{n}\circ\Theta_{n}(x)=\Theta_{n+1}(x)
for each fixed element

and for all
n
sufficiently large.
∎

Building upon the above argument, we obtain the following approximation result for characters of enrichments.

.

\Gamma_{0}=G\ltimes N

We use the tail map

to pullback the character

of the group

Part C: Hilbert–Schmidt stability

11.
Stability of diagonal products

In this section we study Hilbert–Schmidt stability as well as local Hilbert–Schmidt stability for families of groups that were considered in previous sections, including diagonal products and enrichments. In particular, we prove Theorems
1.4
,
1.5
,
1.10
from the introduction.

We recall the elegant and useful Hadwin–Shulman criterion for Hilbert–Schmidt stability of amenable groups, which forms the basis of all our stability results.

Theorem 11.1

(Hadwin-Shulman
[
HS18
]
)
.

An amenable countable group
G
is Hilbert–Schmidt stable if and only if
any trace on
G
is a pointwise limit of finite-dimensional traces.

To verify the Hadwin–Shulman criterion it suffices to consider characters of the group
G
(rather than all traces on
G
), see
[
HS18
, Theorem 4]
.

Stability of diagonal products

We now prove Theorem
1.5
of the introduction, which says for amenable groups, that diagonal products preserve Hilbert–Schmidt stability (given some technical assumptions on the LEF approximation in questioon). Actually, the proof follows quite easily and directly from our character classification result for diagonal products, i.e. Theorem
9.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
G
be an amenable Hilbert–Schmidt stable group with a fixed finite generating set
S
and an essential LEF approximation
\theta_{n}:G\to G_{n}
by simple groups (see Definitions
2.1
and

Stability radius growth

In this section we study some basic properties of the stability radius growth function (Definition
1.3
).
Of particular interest is the relation between stability radius growth and the LEF and MAP growth functions of a group (Proposition

We begin by showing that stability radius growth is an uninteresting notion for finitely presented groups.

Lemma 12.1
.

If the group
\Gamma
is finitely presented and Hilbert–Schmidt stable then the function
{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}
is bounded.

Proof.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}_{S}
be the free group with generators labelled by
S

\Gamma
is finitely presented, then the kernel

is normally generated by a finite set of relations

.

The Hilbert–Schmidt stability of the group
\Gamma
is equivalent to the following
[
HS18
]
: For every
M>0
there is a
\delta>0
such that for every

and every homomorphism
\varphi:{\mathbb{F}}_{S}\to U(d)
with

\pi:\mathbb{F}_{S}\to U(d)
factoring through

and satisfying

for all generators

.
Consequently, if we let

be the maximal word length of any relation

, then
{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}(M)\leq l
for all

.
∎

Remark 12.2
.

It is natural to ask whether the converse direction of Lemma

is true. Namely, if
{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}
is bounded, does it follow that
\Gamma
is finitely presented?

For example, this implication would hold if all groups were hyperlinear, as we now explain. Let
\Gamma
be a group with a finite generating set

and

be the corresponding quotient map.
Assume that
{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}
is bounded, namely
{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}\leq l
for some

. Consider the quotient group

of the free group

presented by the subset of all relations of length at most

belonging to

be the corresponding quotient map.
We claim that the two groups

and

\Gamma
is finitely presented.

Assume by contradiction that there is a word

with

Let

be the parameter provided by Definition

with respect to the value
{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}(|w_{0}|)

Since the group

is hyperlinear (by our standing assumption), there exists a map

Recall the notion of equivalence between monotone functions which was a part of our standing notations on p.
1
.

Lemma 12.3
.

If

and

is a pair of finite generating sets for the group

Quantitative approximation properties

We recall various notions of quantification for group approximation properties, following

Let

The

is the smallest order

of a finite group

•

{\texttt{MAP}}_{\Gamma}^{S}:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}

Clearly

or

if and only if the group

Upon passing from one finite generating set to the other,

We now relate the stability radius, the MAP and the LEF growth. This can be thought of as a quantitative version of the following folklore observation: a Hilbert–Schmidt stable hyperlinear group is MAP (see

Proposition 12.6

Let
\Gamma

{\texttt{MAP}}_{\Gamma}^{S}(n)\leq{\texttt{LEF}}_{\Gamma}^{S}(\max\{{\texttt{SR}}_{\Gamma}^{S}(n),n\}).

Fix

. Denote

\varphi:B_{S}(m)\to F
into some finite group

By composing

with the left-regular representation of the group

The proof of this statement given below is a straightforward adaptation of the proof of the analogous statement on the residual finiteness growth of these groups, which was first established in

.

Proposition 12.7
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be any monotone non-decreasing function. There are two functions
d,r:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
as above, such that the corresponding
(d,r)
-generalized B.H. Neumann group
B=B(d,r)
satisfies
f\preceq{\texttt{MAP}}_{B}
.

Proof.

Let an arbitrary monotone non-decreasing function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be given. By making use of
[
Bra24a
, Proposition 2.8]
together with Bernard’s postulate, one can find a strictly increasing sequence
d(n)
of (odd) primes with
d(1)\geq 5
, and a function
r:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
, with the following properties:

(a)

The two inequalities
n\leq r(n)\leq\min\{18n,d(n)/3\}
and
f(n)\leq d(n)-1
hold true for all
n
.

(b)

r(l)
is distinct modulo
d(m)
from
\pm r(m)
as well as from
\pm 2r(m)
, for all
m
and for all
l
distinct from
m
.

Consider the corresponding generalized B.N. Neumann group
B=B(d,r)
with its generators
S=\{\alpha,\beta\}
. By (a) above, the assumptions of Proposition
3.2
are satisfied. Therefore
B
is isomorphic to the diagonal product
\bigotimes G_{n}
where
G_{n}=\Alt(d(n))
for all
n
. Note that
\bigoplus_{n}G_{n}\leq B
by Lemma
2.5
.
Both items (a) and (b) above are used in
[
Bra24a
, Lemma 3.1 and Lemma 3.2]
to show that for every
n
, there exists a non-trivial word
w_{n}\in B
with

w_{n}\in B_{S}(4+4r(n))\cap G_{n}

where
G_{n}
is viewed as a subgroup of the direct sum
\bigoplus G_{n}
inside
B
.
Consequently, if
\pi:B\to U(\mathcal{H})
is any finite-dimensional representation such that
\pi(w_{n})\neq 1
, then the restriction of the representation
\pi
to the subgroup
G_{n}\leq B
is a non-trivial unitary representation of
G_{n}
.
It is well known that the minimal non-trivial representation of
G_{n}\cong\Alt(d(n))
is of dimension
d(n)-1
(achieved by the standard representation). Therefore
\dim\mathcal{H}\geq d(n)-1
. We conclude that

{\texttt{MAP}}_{B}^{S}(18n)\geq{\texttt{MAP}}_{B}^{S}(4+4r(n))\geq d(n)-1\geq f(n).

This implies
f\preceq{\texttt{MAP}}_{B}
, as required.
∎

Another crucial ingredient towards Theorem
1.4
is a universal upper bound on the LEF growth of all
2
-generated (or, more generally, all
k
-generated) groups.

Theorem 12.8

(
[
Bra22b
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. There exists an increasing function
\Xi_{k}:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that any
k
-generated LEF group
\Gamma
satisfies
{\texttt{LEF}}_{\Gamma}\preceq\Xi_{k}
.

We can now conclude our construction of amenable groups with arbitrary large stability radius growth function.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a given monotone non-decreasing function. Assume without loss of generality that
n\preceq f(n)
. Recall that there is a universal function
\Xi_{2}
such that every
2
-generated group
\Gamma
has
{\texttt{LEF}}_{\Gamma}\preceq\Xi_{2}
, see Theorem
12.8
.
Define
g=\Xi_{2}\circ f
. Let
B=B(d,r)
be some generalised B. H. Neumann group associated with the two functions
d(n),r(n)
obtained by applying Proposition
12.7
to the function
g
.

The group
B
is
2
-generated and amenable. It is a diagonal product based on the wreath product
({\mathbb{Z}}/3{\mathbb{Z}})\wr{\mathbb{Z}}
, see Proposition
3.2
. Recall that such wreath products are Hilbert–Schmidt stable by
[
LV23
, Corollary D]
. Since the diagonal product construction preserves Hilbert–Schmidt stability by our Theorem
11.3
, we conclude that the group
B
is also Hilbert–Schmidt stable.

Putting together the two Propositions
12.6
and
12.7
we obtain

g(n)\preceq{\texttt{MAP}}_{B}(n)\preceq{\texttt{LEF}}_{B}(\max\{{\texttt{SR}}_{B}(n),n\})\preceq\Xi_{2}(\max\{{\texttt{SR}}_{B}(n),n\})

for all
n
.
This gives
f(n)\preceq\max\{{\texttt{SR}}_{B}(n),n\}
. Since
n\preceq f(n)
, we get
f\preceq{\texttt{SR}}_{B}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 12.9
.

There is a doubly exponential upper bound on the LEF growth of the groups
B(d,r)

[
Bra22b
, Theorem 4.9]
. So, one can in fact use
\exp\circ\exp
instead of
\Xi_{2}
.

Question 12.10
.

Can one find upper bounds on the stability radius growth of some class of infinitely presented groups? For instance, is there an explicit (e.g. computable) upper bound on the stability radius growth of the lamplighter group?
