Graphical Proof Theory I: Sequent Systems on Undirected Graphs
Matteo Acclavio

Abstract
In this paper we explore the design of sequent calculi operating on graphs. For this purpose, we introduce a set of logical connectives extending the well-known correspondence between classical propositional formulas and cographs, and we define sequent systems operating on formulas over these connectives. We prove, using an analyticity argument based on cut-elimination, that our systems provide conservative extensions of multiplicative linear logic (without and with mix) and classical propositional logics. We conclude by showing that one of our systems captures graph isomorphism as logical equivalence, and that this system is also sound and complete for the graphical logic 𝖦𝖲 \mathsf{GS} .

Introduction

In theoretical computer science,
formulas
play a crucial role in describing complex abstract objects.
At the syntactical level, the formulas of a logic describe complex structures by means of unary and binary operators, usually thought of as
connectives
and
modalities
respectively.
On the other hand, graph-based syntaxes are often favored in formal representation, as they provide an intuitive and canonical description of properties, relations and systems.
By means of example, consider the two graphs below:

\va 1\qquad\vb 1\qquad\vc 1\qquad\vd 1\Dedges{b1/a1,b1/c1,d1/c1}\qquad\quad\mbox{or}\quad\qquad\va 1\qquad\vb 1\qquad\vc 1\qquad\vd 1\Gedges{b1/a1,b1/c1,d1/c1}

It follows from results in
[
Valdes1979
,
cographs
]
that describing any of the above graphs by means of formulas only employing binary connectives would require repeating at least one vertex.
As a consequence, formulas describing complex graphs are usually long and convoluted, and a specific
encodings
are needed to standardize such formulas.

Since graphs are ubiquitous in theoretical computer science and its applications,
a natural question to ask is whether it is possible to define formalisms having graphs, instead of formulas, as first-class terms of the syntax.
Such a paradigm shift would allow to design efficient automated tools free from the bureaucracy introduced to handle the encoding required to represent graphs.
At the same time, a graphical syntax would provide a useful tool for investigations such as the ones in
[
learningPomset
]
or
[
Fu2004
,
Denielou2010
]
, where the authors restrain their framework to sequential-parallel orders, as these can be represented by means of formulas with at most binary connectives.

Two recent lines of works have generalized proof theoretical methodologies to graphs, extending the correspondence between classical propositional formulas and cographs.
In these works, systems operating on graphs are defined via local and context-free rewriting rules, similarly as what done in
deep inference
systems
[
gug:SIS
,
gug:gun:par:2010
,
tub:str:esslli19
]
.
The first line of research, carried out by Calk, Das, Rice and Waring in various works
[
CDW:ext-bool
,
calk:graph
,
waring:master
,
das:19
,
das:rice:FSCD2021
]
, explores the use of maximal stable sets/cliques-preserving homomorphisms to define notions of entailment

A similar approach was proposed in

pratt1986modeling
]
for studying pomsets.

Here, the choice of the using of a deep inference formalism is natural, since the rules of the calculus are local rewritings.
The second line of research, investigated by the author, Horne, Mauw and Straßburger in several contributions
[

The main goal of this line of research, partially achieved with the system

\mathsf{BV}

The logic

For this reason the aforementioned line of work focused on deep inference systems, and the question about the existence of a cut-free sequent calculus for

Main contributions

In this paper we focus on the definition of sequent calculi for
graphical logics
, and we positively answer the above question by providing, among other results, a cut-free sound and complete sequent calculus for
\mathsf{GS}
.
By using standard techniques in sequent calculus, we thus obtain a proof of analiticity for this logic which is simpler and more concise with respect to the one in
[
acc:LMCS
]
.

To achieve these results, we introduce
graphical connectives
, which are operators that can be naturally interpreted as graphs.
We then define the sequent calculi
\mathsf{MGL}
,
\mathsf{MGL}^{\circ}
and
\mathsf{GLK}
, containing rules to handle these connectives.
After showing that cut-elimination holds for these systems,
we prove that
\mathsf{MGL}
,
\mathsf{MGL}^{\circ}
and
\mathsf{GLK}
define conservative extensions of
multiplicative linear logic
,
multiplicative linear logic with mix
and
classical propositional logic
respectively.
We then prove that formulas interpreted as the same graph are logically equivalent, thus justifying the fact that we consider these systems as operating on graphs rather than formulas.
We conclude by showing that
\mathsf{MGL}^{\circ}
is sound and complete with respect to the logic
\mathsf{GS}
, thus providing a simple sequent calculus for the logic.

{obs}

The problem of graph isomorphism is a standard
\mathbf{NP}
-problem (to be more precise, its complexity is quasi-polynomial
[
bab:quasi
]
).
That is, verify that a given bijection between the sets of vertices of two graphs is an isomorphism can be checked in polynomial time,
while there is no known polynomial time algorithm to find such an isomorphism.
For this reason, whenever we say that two graphs are the same, either we assume they share the same set of vertices, therefore implicitly assuming the isomorphism
f
to be defined by the identity function over the set of vertices, or we assume an isomorphism to be given.
This allows us to verify whether two graphs are the same in polynomial time.

In order to use proof theoretical methodologies on graphs, we need a suitable notion of subgraphs to be used in the same way sub-formulas are used in proof systems, that is, to state properties of the calculus or to define the behavior of rules.
For this purpose, we use for a notion of
module
to identify subgraph allowing us to decompose a graph using abstract syntax trees similar to the ones underlying formulas
[
gallai:67
,
james1972graph
,
hab:paul:survey
,
lovasz2009matching
,
mcc:ros:spi:linear
,
Ehrenfeucht1999
]
.
A module is a subset of vertices of a graph having the same edge-relation with any vertex outside the subset.
This definition generalizes the interaction we usually be observed in formulas, where, in the formula tree, any literal in a subformula has the same relation (the one given by the least common ancestor) with a given literal not occurring in the subformula itself.

Classical Propositional Formulas and Cographs

The set of

Definition 26

\mathcal{A}

\phi,\psi\coloneqq a\mid\phi\lor\psi\mid\phi\land\psi\mid\phi^{\lbot}\qquad\mbox{with $a\in\mathcal{A}$.}

(3)

We denote by
\equiv
the equivalence relation over formulas generated by the following laws:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{\defn{Equivalence laws}}&\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{\;\feq\;}l@{\hskip 4em}r@{\;\feq\;}l}\phi\lor\psi\;\equiv&\psi\lor\phi\hfil\hskip 40.88867pt&\phi\lor(\psi\lor\chi)\;\equiv&(\phi\lor\psi)\lor\chi\\
\phi\land\psi\;\equiv&\psi\land\phi\hfil\hskip 40.88867pt&\phi\land(\psi\land\chi)\;\equiv&(\phi\land\psi)\land\chi\end{array}\right.\\[8.0pt]
\mbox{\defn{De-Morgan laws}}&\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{r@{\;\feq\;}l@{\hskip 6.8em}r@{\;\feq\;}l}\!(\phi^\lbot)^\lbot\;\equiv&\phi\hfil\hskip 69.51077pt&(\phi\land\psi)^\lbot\;\equiv&\phi^\lbot\lor\psi^\lbot\end{array}\right.
\end{equation} We define a map from literals to single-vertex graphs, which extends to formulas via the composition-via
the unlabeled
two-vertices stable set $\mathsf{S}_{2}$
and
two-vertices clique $\mathsf{K}_{2}$.
\begin{definition}Let $\phi$ be a classical formula, then $\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]$ is the graph inductively defined as follows:
$$\left[\!\left[a\right]\!\right]=a\qquad\left[\!\left[\phi^{\lbot}\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]^{\lbot}\qquad\left[\!\left[\phi\lor\psi\right]\!\right]=\mathsf{S}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}\!\begin{vmatrix}\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right],\left[\!\left[\psi\right]\!\right]\end{vmatrix}\!\end{pmatrix}\qquad\left[\!\left[\phi\land\psi\right]\!\right]=\mathsf{K}_{2}\begin{pmatrix}\!\begin{vmatrix}\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right],\left[\!\left[\psi\right]\!\right]\end{vmatrix}\!\end{pmatrix}$$where $\mathsf{S}_{2}$ and $\mathsf{K}_{2}$ are respectively a stable set and a clique with $2$ vertices,
and
where we denote by $a$ the single-vertex graph, whose vertex is labeled by $a$.
\end{definition}\par\par We can easily observe that the map $\left[\!\left[\cdot\right]\!\right]$ well-behaves with respect to the equivalence over formulas $\equiv$, that is, equivalent formulas are mapped to the symmetric graphs.
\begin{proposition}Let $\phi$ and $\psi$ be classical formulas.
Then $\phi\equiv\psi$ iff $\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[\psi\right]\!\right]$.
\end{proposition}\par\par\par\par\par\par We finally recall the definition of \emph{cographs}, and the theorem establishing the relation between cographs and classical formulas, i.e., providing an alternative definition of cographs as graphs generated by single-vertex graphs using the composition-via a two-vertices no-edge graph and a two-vertices one-edge graph.
\par\par\begin{definition}A \defn{cograph} is a graph $G$ such that
there are no four vertices $v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}$ in $G$
such that the induced subgraph $G|_{\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\}}$ is similar to the graph $\langle\{a,b,c,d\},\varnothing,\{ab,bc,cd\}\rangle=\va 1\quad\vb 1\quad\vc 1\quad\vd 1\Gedges{a1/b1,b1/c1,c1/d1}$ .
\end{definition}\begin{theorem}A graph $G$ is a cograph iff there is a formula $\phi$ such that $G\sim\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]$.
\end{theorem}\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Modular Decomposition of Graphs}
\par We recall the notion of \emph{prime graph},
allowing us to provide canonical representatives of graphs via modular decomposition.
(see e.g., \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{gallai:67,james1972graph,hab:paul:survey,lovasz2009matching,mcc:ros:spi:linear,Ehrenfeucht1999}{}{}]}).
\begin{definition}A graph $G$ is \defn{prime} if $|V_{G}|>1$ and all its modules are trivial.
\end{definition}We recall the following standard result from the literature.
\begin{theorem}Let $G$ be a graph with at least two vertices.
Then there are non-empty modules $M_{1},\dots,M_{n}$ of $G$ and a prime graph $P$ such that $G=P\llparenthesis M_{1},\dots,M_{n}\rrparenthesis$.
\end{theorem}\par This result implies the possibility of describing graphs using single-vertex graphs and the operation of composition-via prime graphs.
More precisely, we can define the notion of \emph{modular decomposition} of a graph composition-via prime graphs to provide a more canonical representation.
\begin{definition}Let $G$ be a non-empty graph.
A \defn{modular decomposition} of $G$ is a way to write $G$ using single-vertex graphs and the operation of composition-via prime graphs:
\begin{itemize}\par\itemize@item@if $G$ is a graph with a single vertex $x$ labeled by $a$, then $G=a$
(i.e., $G=\langle\{x\},\ell(x)=a,\varnothing\rangle$);
\par\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@if $H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}$ are maximal modules of $G$ such that $V_{G}=\biguplus_{i=1}^{n}V_{H_{i}}$,
then there is a unique prime graph $P$ such that $G=P\llparenthesis H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}\rrparenthesis$.
\end{itemize}\end{definition}\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\begin{remark}There are various reasons why modular decomposition is not unique.
\par The first is due to the possible presence of cliques and stable sets.
By means of example, consider a clique with three vertices $u$, $v$ and $w$
can be represented as $(u\ltens v)\ltens w$ or $u\ltens(v\ltens w)$.
\par We already observed the second reason in \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{rem:compVia},
since graph symmetries allow us to represent the same graph by different decompositions,
as shown in top-most modular decomposition below on the left.
$$\begin{array}[]{cc}P\llparenthesis u,v,w,t\rrparenthesis=\vu 1\quad\vv 1\quad\vw 1\quad\vt 1\Gedges{u1/v1,v1/w1,w1/t1}=P\llparenthesis t,w,v,u\rrparenthesis\\
P\llparenthesis u,v,w,t\rrparenthesis=\vu 1\quad\vv 1\quad\vw 1\quad\vt 1\Gedges{u1/v1,v1/w1,w1/t1}=P^{\prime}\llparenthesis u,w,v,t\rrparenthesis\end{array}\quad\mbox{where}\quad P=\va 1\quad\vb 2\quad\vc 3\quad\vd 4\Gedges{a1/b2,b2/c3,c3/d4}\quad\mbox{and}\quad P^{\prime}=\va 1\quad\vc 3\quad\vb 2\quad\vd 4\Gedges{a1/c3,b2/c3,b2/d4}\;.$$Finally, two symmetric prime graphs could provide distinct modular decompositions of the same graph,
as shown above with symmetric prime graphs $P$ and $P^{\prime}$.
\end{remark}\par The first problem could be addressed by considering in the modular decomposition not only prime graphs, but also cliques and stable sets, that is, including $n$-ary versions of the operations $\lpar$ and $\ltens$.
We show later in this paper that this problem is irrelevant due to the associativity of $\lpar$ and $\ltens$.
The second problem cannot be addressed without enforcing a cumbersome order over graphs taking into account vertex labels and factor positions.
However, we can address the latter source of ambiguity by introducing the notion of \emph{base} of \emph{graphical connectives},
allowing us to provide a single canonical prime graph for each class of symmetric prime graphs.
\par\begin{definition}A \defn{graphical connective}
$C=\langle V_{\mathcal{C}},\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frown}}}}\mkern 1.0mu\rangle$
(with \defn{arity} $n=|V_{\mathcal{C}}|$)
is given by
a finite list of vertices $V_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\rangle$
and
a non-reflexive symmetric edge relation $\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frown}}}}\mkern 1.0mu$ over the set of vertices occurring in $V_{\mathcal{C}}$.
We denote by $G_{C}$ the graph corresponding to $C$,
that is, the graph $G_{C}=\langle\{v\mid v\mbox{ in }V_{\mathcal{C}}\},\varnothing,\mkern 1.0mu\mathord{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{C}}}{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frown}}}}\mkern 1.0mu\rangle$.
The \defn{composition-via} a graphical connective
is defined as the composition-via the graph $G_{C}$.
\par A graphical connective is \defn{prime}
if $G_{C}$ is a prime graph. A set $\mathcal{P}$ of prime graphical connectives is a \defn{base}
if for each prime graph $P$
there is a unique connective $C\in\mathcal{P}$
such that $P\sim G_{C}$.
\par Given an $n$-ary connective $C$,
we define the following sets of permutations over the set $\{1,\ldots,n\}$:
\begin{equation}\begin{array}[]{l@{\;:\;}c@{\quad\coloneqq\quad}l}\mbox{the \defn{group
of symmetries} of $C$}&\mathfrak{S}(C)\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt\coloneqq\hskip 10.22217pt&\left\{\;\sigma\mid C\llparenthesis a_1,\ldots, a_n\rrparenthesis=C\llparenthesis a_{\sigma(1)},\ldots, a_{\sigma(n)}\rrparenthesis\right\}\\

\right\}
\end{equation}
for any $a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}$ single-vertex graphs.
\end{definition}

\par\par\par\par\par We can reformulate the standard result on modular decomposition as follows.
\begin{theorem}Let $G$ be a non-empty graph and $\mathcal{P}$ a base.
Then then there is a unique way (up to symmetries of graphical connectives and associativity of $\lpar$ and $\ltens$)
to write $G$ using single-vertex graphs and the graphical connectives in $\mathcal{P}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{corollary}Two graphs are isomorphic iff they admit a same modular decomposition.
\end{corollary}
\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Graphs as Formulas}
\par\par In order to represent graphs as formulas,
we define new connectives beyond conjunction and disjunction to represent graphical connectives in a base $\mathcal{P}$.
From now on, we assume bases $\mathcal{P}$ containing the graphical connectives in \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:gCon} to be fixed.
\par\begin{definition}The set of \defn{formulas} is generated by
the set of propositional atoms $\mathcal{A}$,
a \defn{unit} $\circ$,
using the following syntax:
\begin{equation}\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\coloneqq\circ\mid a\mid a^{\lbot}\mid\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n_{P}}\rrparenthesis\qquad\mbox{with $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $P\in\mathcal{P}$}\end{equation}We simply denote $\lpar$ (resp. $\ltens$)
the binary connective $\kappa_{\lpar}$ (resp. $\kappa_{\ltens}$)
and
we write
$\phi\lpar\psi$ instead of $\kappa_{\lpar}\llparenthesis\phi,\psi\rrparenthesis$
(resp. $\phi\ltens\psi$ instead of $\kappa_{\ltens}\llparenthesis\phi,\psi\rrparenthesis$).
The \defn{arity} of the connective $\kappa_{P}$ is the arity $n_{P}$ of $P$.
\par A \defn{literal} is a formula of the form $a$ or $a^{\lbot}$ for an atom $a\in\mathcal{A}$.
The set of literals is denoted $\mathcal{L}$.
A \defn{$\kappa$-formula} is a formula with \defn{main connective} $\kappa$, that is, a formula of the form $\kappa\llparenthesis\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\rrparenthesis$.
A formula is \defn{unit-free} if it contains no occurrences of $\circ$ and
\defn{vacuous} if it contains no atoms.
A formula is \defn{pure} if non-vacuous and such that its vacuous subformulas are $\circ$.
A \defn{$\mathsf{MLL}$-formula} is a formula containing only occurrences of $\lpar$ and $\ltens$ connectives.
\par\par\par\par A \defn{context formula} (or simply \defn{context}) $\zeta[\lbox]$ is a formula containing an \defn{hole} $\lbox$ taking the place of an atom.
Given a context $\zeta[\lbox]$, the formula $\zeta[\phi]$ is defined by simply replacing the atom $\lbox$ with the formula $\phi$.
For example, if $\zeta[\lbox]=\psi\lpar(\lbox\ltens\chi)$, then $\zeta[\phi]=\psi\lpar(\phi\ltens\chi)$.
\par For each $\phi$ formula (or context), the graph $\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]$ is defined as follows:
\begin{equation}\left[\!\left[\lbox\right]\!\right]=\lbox\qquad\left[\!\left[\circ\right]\!\right]=\varnothing\qquad\left[\!\left[a\right]\!\right]=a\qquad\left[\!\left[\phi^{\lbot}\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]^{\lbot}\qquad\left[\!\left[\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\rrparenthesis\right]\!\right]=P\begin{pmatrix}\!\begin{vmatrix}\left[\!\left[\phi_{1}\right]\!\right],\ldots,\left[\!\left[\phi_{n}\right]\!\right]\end{vmatrix}\!\end{pmatrix}\end{equation}\end{definition}\par\nota We could consider
a formula $\phi$ over the set of occurrences of literals $\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}$
as a \defn{synthetic connective}.
That is,
we may denote by $\phi\llparenthesis\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\rrparenthesis$
the formula obtained by replacing each literal $x_{i}$ with a corresponding $\psi_{i}$ for all $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$.
The set of \defn{symmetries} of $\phi$ (denoted $\mathfrak{S}(\phi)$) is the set of permutations $\sigma$ over $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ such that $\left[\!\left[\phi\llparenthesis x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rrparenthesis\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[\phi\llparenthesis x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)}\rrparenthesis\right]\!\right]$.
\par\par\par\par\par\begin{definition}The equivalence relation $\equiv$ over formulas
is generated by the
following equations:
\begin{equation*}\begin{array}[]{l@{\;}l}\mbox{\defn{Equivalence laws}}&\left\{\begin{array}[]{l@{\quad}r@{\;\feq\;}l@{\quad}l}\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt&\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_1, \ldots,\phi_{|P|}\rrparenthesis\;\equiv&\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\phi_{\sigma(|V_{P}|)}\rrparenthesis\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt&\mbox{ for each }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(P)\\
\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt&\phi\ltens(\psi\ltens\chi)\;\equiv&(\phi\ltens\psi)\ltens\chi\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt\\
\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt&\phi\lpar(\psi\lpar\chi)\;\equiv&(\phi\lpar\psi)\lpar\chi\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt\end{array}\right.\\
\\
\mbox{\defn{De-Morgan laws}}&\left\{\begin{array}[]{l@{\quad}r@{\;\feq\;}l@{\;}l}\lx@intercol\hfil
\circ^\lbot\;\equiv\;
\circ\hskip 20.44434pt\hskip 20.44434pt\phi^{\lbot\lbot}\;\equiv\;
\phi\hfil\lx@intercol
\\
\mbox{only if }\mathfrak{S}^\lbot(P)=\varnothing:\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt&\left(\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_1,\ldots\phi_{n_P}\rrparenthesis\right)^\lbot\;\equiv&\kappa_{P^\lbot}\llparenthesis\phi_{\sigma(1)}^\lbot,\ldots,\phi_{\sigma(n_P)}^\lbot\rrparenthesis&\\
\mbox{only if }\mathfrak{S}^\lbot(P)\neq\varnothing:\hfil\hskip 10.22217pt&\left(\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_1,\ldots\phi_{n_P}\rrparenthesis\right)^\lbot\;\equiv&\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_{\rho(1)}^\lbot,\ldots,\phi_{\rho(n_P)}^\lbot\rrparenthesis&\mbox{ for each }\rho\in\mathfrak{S}^\lbot(P)\end{array}\right.
\end{equation*}
for each $P\in\mathcal{P}$ (with arity $n_{P}$).
\par The \defn{(linear) negation} over formulas is defined by letting
$$\circ^{\lbot}=\circ\qquad\phi^{\lbot\lbot}=\phi\qquad\left(\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\rrparenthesis\right)^{\lbot}=\kappa_{Q}\llparenthesis\phi^{\lbot}_{\sigma_{P}(1)},\ldots,\phi^{\lbot}_{\sigma_{P}(n)}\rrparenthesis\quad$$where $Q$ is the unique graphical connective in $\mathcal{P}$ such that $\left[\!\left[\kappa_{P}\begin{pmatrix}\!\begin{vmatrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\end{vmatrix}\!\end{pmatrix}\right]\!\right]=Q\begin{pmatrix}\!\begin{vmatrix}a^{\lbot}_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,a^{\lbot}_{\sigma_{n}}\end{vmatrix}\!\end{pmatrix}$
for any single-vertex graphs $a^{\lbot}_{1},\ldots,a^{\lbot}_{n}$ (with vertex labeled by $a^{\lbot}_{1},\ldots,a^{\lbot}_{n}$ respectively)
and
a permutation $\sigma_{P}$ over the set $\{1,\ldots,n\}$.
\par The \defn{linear implication} $\phi\limp\psi$ is defined as $\phi^{\lbot}\lpar\psi$,
while
the \defn{logical equivalence} $\phi\limpeq\psi$ is defined as $(\phi\limp\psi)\ltens(\psi\limp\phi)$.
\end{definition}\par\begin{remark}As explained in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{acc:LMCS}{}{}]} (Section 9), the graphical connectives we discuss in this paper are \emph{multiplicative connectives} (in the sense of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{dan:reg:89,girard2000meaning,mai:19,acc:mai:20}{}{}]}) but they are not the same as the \emph{connectives-as-partitions} discussed in these works.
In fact,
there is a unique $4$-ary graphical connective $\mathsf{P_{4}}$ has symmetry group $\{\mathsf{id},(1,4)(2,3)\}$,
while,
as shown in
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{mai:19,acc:mai:20}{}{}]},
there is a unique pair of dual ``primitive'' $4$-ary multiplicative connectives-as-partitions $\mathsf{G}_{4}$ and $\mathsf{G}_{4}^{\lbot}$,
and
$\mathfrak{S}(\mathsf{P_{4}})\subsetneq\mathfrak{S}(\mathsf{G}_{4})=\mathfrak{S}(\mathsf{G}_{4}^{\lbot})$.
\end{remark}\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par\par The following result is consequence of \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{thm:modDec}.
\begin{proposition}Let $\phi$ and $\psi$ be formulas.
If $\phi\equiv\psi$, then $\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[\psi\right]\!\right]$.
Moreover, if $\phi$ and $\psi$ are unit-free,
then $\phi\equiv\psi$ iff $\left[\!\left[\phi\right]\!\right]=\left[\!\left[\psi\right]\!\right]$.
\end{proposition}Note that the the stronger result does not hold in presence of units.
For an example consider the formulas $\circ\ltens\circ$ and $\circ\lpar\circ$.
\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Sequent calculi over operating on graphs-as-formulas}
\par We assume the reader to be familiar with the definition of sequent calculus derivations as trees of sequents (see, e.g., \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{troelstra:schwichtenberg:00}{}{}]}) but we recall here some definitions.
\par\begin{definition}We define a \defn{sequent} is a set of occurrences of formulas.
A \defn{sequent system} $\mathsf{S}$ is a set of \defn{sequent rules} as the ones in \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{fig:rules}.
In a sequent rule $\rho$, we say that a formula is \defn{active} if
it occurs in one of its premises but not in its conclusion, and
\defn{principal} if it occurs in its conclusion but in none of its premises.\par\par A \defn{proof} of a sequent $\Gamma$ is a derivation with no open premises, denoted $\vlderivation{\vlpr{\pi}{\mathsf{S}}{\Gamma}}$.
We denote by
$\vlsmash{\vlderivation{\vlde{\pi^{\prime}}{\mathsf{S}}{\Gamma}{\vlhy{\Gamma^{\prime}}}}}$
an \defn{(open) derivation} of $\Gamma$ from $\Gamma^{\prime}$,
that is,
is a proof tree having exactly one open premise $\Gamma^{\prime}$.
\par A rule is \defn{admissible} in $\mathsf{S}$
if there is a derivation of the conclusion of the rule
whenever all premises of the rule are derivable.
A rule is \defn{derivable} in $\mathsf{S}$,
if there is a derivation in $\mathsf{S}$ from the premises to the conclusion of the rule.
\end{definition}\par\par\nota In this paper we use the same notation to denote a sequent system $\mathsf{S}$
and
the set of formulas admitting a proof in $\mathsf{S}$.

\begin{definition}Let $P$ and $Q$ be prime graphs.

$P\llparenthesis\circ,\ldots,\circ,v_{i_{1}},\circ,\ldots,\circ,v_{i_{k}},\circ,\ldots,\circ\rrparenthesis\sim Q\llparenthesis v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\rrparenthesis$

and
for some distinct $i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$,
then we may write
$\kappa_{P|_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}}=\kappa_{Q}$
and we say that
the connective $\kappa_{Q}$ is a \defn{sub-connective} of if $\kappa_{P}$.
\par\par A \defn{quasi-subformula} of a formula
$\phi=\kappa_{P}\llparenthesis\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}\rrparenthesis$

$\kappa_{P^{\prime}|_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}}\llparenthesis\psi^{\prime}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\psi^{\prime}_{i_{k}}\rrparenthesis$
with
$\psi^{\prime}_{i_{j}}$ a quasi-subformula of $\psi_{i_{j}}$ for all $i_{j}\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}$.
\end{definition}\begin{corollary}Let $\Gamma$ be a sequent.
