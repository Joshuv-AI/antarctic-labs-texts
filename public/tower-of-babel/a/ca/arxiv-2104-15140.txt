Exact detection thresholds and minimax optimality of Chatterjee’s correlation coefficient
Arnab Auddy, ??,??, Nabarun Deb, ??,??, Sagnik Nandy ??,??

Abstract
Recently, Chatterjee (2021) introduced a new rank-based correlation coefficient which can be used to measure the strength of dependence between two random variables. This coefficient has already attracted much attention as it converges to the Dette-Siburg-Stoimenov measure (see Dette et al. (2013)), which equals 0 0 if and only if the variables are independent and 1 1 if and only if one variable is a function of the other. Further, Chatterjee’s coefficient is computable in (near) linear time, which makes it appropriate for large-scale applications. In this paper, we expand the theoretical understanding of Chatterjee’s coefficient in two directions: (a) First we consider the problem of testing for independence using Chatterjee’s correlation. We obtain its asymptotic distribution under any changing sequence of alternatives converging to the null hypothesis (of independence). We further obtain a general result that gives exact detection thresholds and limiting power for Chatterjee’s test of independence under natural nonparametric alternatives converging to the null. As applications of this general result, we prove a n − 1 / 4 n^{-1/4} detection boundary for this test and compute explicitly the limiting local power on the detection boundary for popularly studied alternatives in the literature. (b) We then construct a test for non-trivial levels of dependence using Chatterjee’s coefficient. In contrast to testing for independence, we prove that, in this case, Chatterjee’s coefficient indeed yields a minimax optimal procedure with a n − 1 / 2 n^{-1/2} detection boundary. Our proof techniques rely on Stein’s method of exchangeable pairs, a non-asymptotic projection result, and information theoretic lower bounds.

We arrange the data as
(X_{(1)},Y_{(1)}),\ldots,(X_{(n)},Y_{(n)})

Now consider the statistic given by

\xi_{n}:=1-\frac{3}{n^{2}-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\left|R_{n,(i+1)}-R_{n,(i)}\right|.

Here

’s. For a more general definition of

, with marginal densities

and

. In various bivariate copula based models,

turns out to be a monotonic function of the natural dependence parameter (see examples 1.(a) - (d) in

; also see

X_{1}
and
Y_{1}
.

1.1
Problem setup and summary of main contributions

We will use the standard framework of local power analysis taken from
[
41
,
42
]
which is popularly used in independence testing procedures, see e.g.,
[
7
,
44
]
. Towards this direction, consider a
triangular array
of i.i.d. random variables
(X_{n,1}\mathpunct{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{,}}Y_{n,1}),\ldots,(X_{n,n}\mathpunct{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{,}}Y_{n,n})
from a bivariate density
f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot,\cdot)
with marginals
f_{X}^{(n)}(\cdot)
,
f_{Y}^{(n)}(\cdot)
. Note that the joint distribution is no longer fixed, but changes with the sample size. We analyze the testing problem

\mathrm{H}_{0,n}:\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\xi_{0}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad\mathrm{H}_{1,n}:\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\xi_{0}+c_{n},\quad\xi_{0}\in[0,1)

(1.5)

where
\xi(\cdot)
is as defined in (
1.4
) and

c_{n}\downarrow 0\quad\implies\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\downarrow\xi_{0}\quad\mbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

(1.6)

Clearly distinguishing between
\mathrm{H}_{0,n}
and
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}
becomes harder as
c_{n}
converges to
0
faster. Also note that by
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
and
[
21
, Theorem 2]
, testing
\xi_{0}=0
exactly corresponds to the test of independence as in (
1.1
). As
\xi_{n}-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
converges almost surely to
0
under mild assumptions (see
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
), a natural level
\alpha
test function for (
1.5
) is given by

\phi_{n}:=\mathbbm{1}(\xi_{n}-\xi_{0}\geq z_{n,\alpha}),

(1.7)

where
z_{n,\alpha}
is chosen appropriately so as to satisfy the level
\alpha
condition. Define the power function of
\phi_{n}
as

\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)}):=\mathbb{P}(\phi_{n}=1).

(1.8)

Our goal is to investigate the following:

“What is the fastest decaying
c_{n}\downarrow 0
such that
\phi_{n}
can distinguish between the null and the alternative, i.e.,
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
under
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}
? Further, is
\phi_{n}
optimal for the testing problem (
1.5
)?”

In this paper, we provide an exact answer to this question. We find a dichotomy based on whether or not the limiting Dette-Siburg-Stoimenov measure
\xi_{0}
is zero or positive. We make a two-fold contribution in both of these regimes. Note that the case where
\xi_{0}=0
in (
1.5
) corresponds to the important problem of
independence
testing. On the other hand, when
\xi_{0}>0
, we refer to the null hypothesis in (
1.5
) as the problem of testing for degree of association between
X
and
Y
.

Critical detection boundary of
\phi_{n}
for independence testing
(\xi_{0}=0)
:
For this case, in
Theorem

2.2
, we show that the power of
\phi_{n}
converges to
\alpha
, or
1
, or a number in
(\alpha,1)
depending on whether
\sqrt{n}c_{n}
converges to
0
, or
\infty
, or some number in
(0,\infty)
, respectively. This indicates that the best choice of
c_{n}
that ensure
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 1
is
c_{n}=O(n^{-1/2})
.

This is however a suboptimal threshold in terms of detecting dependence. To see this, consider the case where
(X_{1},Y_{1})
is a bivariate normal distribution with correlation
\rho_{n}
. Theorem
2.2
implies that
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
converges to
\alpha
, or
1
, or a number in
(\alpha,1)
depending on whether
n^{1/4}\rho_{n}
converges to
0
, or
\infty
, or some number in
(0,\infty)
, respectively. This implies that
\phi_{n}
has a detection boundary of
O(n^{-1/4})
in terms of
\rho_{n}
. However, it is well known from Le Cam’s theory of local asymptotic normality that the optimal detection threshold for
\rho_{n}
is of the order
n^{-1/2}
and not
n^{-1/4}
. In Section
3
, we give concrete examples of this detection boundary in some other local parametric alternatives, viz. simple mixtures, and noisy nonparametric regression.

Minimax optimality of
\phi_{n}
for testing degree of association
(\xi_{0}>0)
:
For this case, in
Theorem

4.1
, part 1, we show that
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
converges to
1
provided
\sqrt{n}c_{n}\to\infty
. In contrast to the
\xi_{0}=0
case, we prove in
Theorem

4.1
, part 2, that
c_{n}=O(n^{-1/2})
is indeed the optimal threshold when
\xi_{0}>0
in a local asymptotic minimax sense (see (
4.4
)). In other words, if
\sqrt{n}c_{n}\to(0,\infty)
, then no test can uniformly have power converging to
1
. Therefore
c_{n}=O(n^{-1/2})
is the correct detection boundary and this highlights the minimax optimality of Chatterjee’s correlation coefficient for the testing problem (
1.5
) when
\xi_{0}>0
; see Section
4
for more details.

Additionally, as a technical device for the results above, we develop a central limit theorem for
\xi_{n}
.

Central limit theorem for shrinking alternatives:
In Theorem
2.1
, we show that for any sequence of alternatives specified by
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 0
,
\xi_{n}
is asymptotically normal. Further, we characterize the limiting mean and the limiting variance explicitly. This is a wide generalization of the asymptotic normality results in

which are stated under independence, or the fast shrinking alternatives

weakens this assumption only to

and might be of independent interest. This CLT is obtained using Stein’s method-based technique (see
[
14
]
). After the first draft of our paper, a number of other interesting limit theorems for
\xi_{n}
or modified versions of
\xi_{n}
have been established that highlight the interest in Chatterjee’s correlation; see e.g.
[
36
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
1
,
70
]
to name a few. In the following section, we will summarize the other relevant contributions to the problem considered here.

1.2
Comparison with existing literature

Prior to the first version of our paper, the theoretical analysis of
\xi_{n}
had been carried out in three papers. In Chatterjee’s paper
[
15
]
, the asymptotic distribution of
\xi_{n}
was derived under
\mathrm{H}_{0}
as in (
1.1
) and its consistency against fixed (not changing with
n
) alternatives was proved. Two other papers
[
58
]
and
[
12
]
have analyzed
\xi_{n}
under smooth contiguous alternatives (see
[
65
, Chapter 6]
). For example, under the mixture type alternatives in Section
3.1
, their results show that
\phi_{n}
is powerless along “contiguous” alternatives, i.e., in cases where the mixing probability shrinks to zero at a
n^{-1/2}
rate. The proofs in
[
12
,
58
]
use Le Cam’s third lemma (see
[
65
, Example 6.7]
) which requires analyzing
\xi_{n}
and the likelihood ratio jointly but
only under the null
, (that is, when
X_{n,1}
and
Y_{n,1}
are independent) followed by a change of measure formula that only holds under contiguous scales and not beyond. In contrast, the focus of our paper is characterizing the exact detection boundary of
\xi_{n}
, which as we shall see, happens to be in the non-contiguous regime. We therefore adopt a proof strategy using Stein’s method-based technique of local dependence (see
[
14
]
) and non-asymptotic projection results. While the focus of the paper is in the bivariate setting, it should be noted that multivariate versions of
\xi_{n}
have been studied in the literature (see
[
4
,
19
,
1
]
) and asymptotic distributions under independence have been obtained in
[
19
,
59
,
1
]
.

After the first draft of our paper, a number of other results of interest have further solidified the understanding of
\xi_{n}
or modified versions thereof. In
[
48
]
, the authors modified
\xi_{n}
by incorporating more “right nearest neighbors” in its definition. They then proved that in the bivariate Gaussian independence testing problem (see
Section

3
), as the number of right nearest neighbors grow, the detection boundary moves from
O(n^{-1/4})
to nearly
O(n^{-1/2})
. On the other hand, in
[
49
]
, the authors show that
\xi_{n}
is asymptotically normal even when
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\xi_{0}>0
. The limiting variance, in that case, is no longer universal and depends on the data distribution. The authors in
[
49
]
obtain a consistent, analytic estimator of this limiting variance. In the follow-up paper
[
50
]
, the authors show that a natural bootstrap estimator of this limiting variance is not consistent under independence. On the other hand,
[
70
]
proved the asymptotic normality of a symmetrized version of
\xi_{n}
. We also refer the reader to the recent review paper
[
16
]
which provides a comprehensive overview of dependence/association measures that are based on
\xi_{n}
.

2
Critical detection boundary of
\phi_{n}
for independence testing

In this section, we first show that under a wide class of bivariate distributions satisfying (
1.6
),
\xi_{n}
, appropriately centered by its mean and scaled by its standard deviation, converges to a standard normal distribution. We provide a precise characterization of the limiting bias and standard deviation of
\xi_{n}
. We then use these findings to obtain the detection threshold and asymptotic power of the test
\phi_{n}
as described in (
1.7
).

Recall the setting from the Introduction. We consider
(X_{n,k},Y_{n,k})_{1\leq k\leq n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a triangular array of i.i.d. random variables drawn from a bivariate density
f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot,\cdot)
. All the probabilities and expectations taken in the sequel are with respect to the measure induced by
f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Notice that from (
1.3
), with the identity
\underset{i\neq j}{\sum\sum}|i-j|=n(n^{2}-1)/3
, one has

\xi_{n}=\dfrac{3}{n^{2}-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\underset{i\neq j}{\sum\sum}\left|i-j\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|R_{n,(i+1)}-R_{n,(i)}\right|\right).

We will need the following definitions.

Definition 2.1

(Kantorovic-Wasserstein distance)
.

Given any two probability measure
\mu
and
\nu
on the real line, the Kantorovic-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

\mathcal{W}(\mu,\nu):=\sup\left\{\bigg|\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.7}{$\displaystyle\int$}}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\textstyle\int$}}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\scriptstyle\int$}}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int$}}h\,d\mu-\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.7}{$\displaystyle\int$}}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\textstyle\int$}}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\scriptstyle\int$}}{\scalebox{0.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\int$}}h\,d\nu\bigg|:\ h(\cdot)\ \mathrm{is\ Lipschitz},\ \lVert h\rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1\right\}.

In our applications, we will fix
\nu
as the
standard Gaussian distribution
, which leads to the following natural notion of “distance to Gaussianity” based on
2.1
.

Definition 2.2

(Distance to Gaussianity)
.

Let
\nu
be the standard Gaussian law and
T
be a random variable with the law
\mu
. Then the distance between
T
and the standard Gaussian is defined as

\mathfrak{D}(T):=\mathcal{W}(\mu,\nu),

where
\nu
is the standard normal law.

We first state our assumptions.

Assumption (A1)
.

There exist functions
L_{i}^{(n)}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
for
i=1,2
and numerical constants
\kappa_{1}>0
,
\eta\in(0,1]
, and
\theta\in(1,\infty]
such that
\forall\,y,x_{1},x_{2},

\,\,\left|\mathbb{P}(Y_{n,1}\geq y|X_{n,1}=x_{1})-\mathbb{P}(Y_{n,1}\geq y|X_{n,1}=x_{2})\right|\leq(1+L_{1}^{(n)}(x_{1},y)+L_{2}^{(n)}(x_{2},y))\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|^{\eta},

(2.1)

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\int(L_{i}^{(n)}(x,y))^{\theta}f_{X}^{(n)}(x)f_{Y}^{(n)}(y)\,dx\,dy\leq\kappa_{1},

(2.2)

where
f_{X}^{(n)}(\cdot)
and
f_{Y}^{(n)}(\cdot)
are the marginal densities of
X_{n,1}
and
Y_{n,1}
under the joint density
f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Assumption (A2)
.

There exist numerical constants
\gamma>1
and
\kappa_{2}>0
such that

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}|X_{n,1}|^{\gamma}\leq\kappa_{2}.

Note that
(A3)
is weaker than the standard
\eta
Hölder condition, in that the Hölder constants are allowed to depend on
y,x_{1},x_{2}
and also
n
. In this sense, it is weaker than the assumptions in
[
4
]
and related papers.
(A4)
is a standard moment assumption to control the tail of the distribution of
X_{n,i}
’s. This tail behavior crucially affects the distance between
X_{n,(i)}
and
X_{n,(i+1)}
, see e.g.,
[
8
, Section 2.2]
.

We are now in position to state our main result.
In the following theorem (see Section
A.1
for a proof), we show that
\xi_{n}
, appropriately centered and scaled, has a limiting normal distribution in the asymptotic regime
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 0
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any bivariate density
f^{(n)}_{X,Y}(x,y)
satisfying Assumptions
(A3)
and
(A4)
, there is a numerical constant
C(\eta,\theta,\gamma,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})>0
, i.e., depending only on the parameters
\eta,\theta,\gamma,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
from Assumptions
(A3)
and
(A4)
, such that:

(i)

For all
n\geq 1,
we have

\sqrt{n}\left|\mathbb{E}(\xi_{n})-\xi(f^{(n)}_{X,Y})\right|\leq Cn^{-1/2}+C\sqrt{n}b_{n}\mathbbm{1}(\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})>0).

(ii)

Moreover,

\mathfrak{D}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{n}\left(\xi_{n}-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\right)}{\sqrt{2/5}}\right)\leq Cn^{-1/2}+C\left(\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})+\sqrt{n\log n}\,b_{n}\right)^{1/2}\mathbbm{1}(\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})>0),

where
\mathfrak{D}
is the Wasserstein distance to normality defined in
2.2
, and
b_{n}
is defined as

b_{n}:=n^{-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\log{n})^{2}+\left(\dfrac{(\log{n})^{2}}{n}\right)^{\left(\frac{\gamma(\theta-1)}{\theta(\gamma+1)}\wedge\frac{\eta\gamma}{\gamma+1}\right)}.

(2.3)

In particular, part (ii) of
Theorem

2.1
shows that if
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 0
and
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
, then

\sqrt{n}(\xi_{n}-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)}))\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,2/5).

(2.4)

Therefore, in the entire asymptotic regime
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 0
, we see that
\xi_{n}
has the same limiting variance, which matches the case where the
X
’s and
Y
’s are mutually independent (also see
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
). A couple of remarks on the assumptions needed in
Theorem

2.1
are now in order.

Remark 2.1
.

To understand the condition
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
, let us first focus on a simple case. Assume that the conditional probability in (
3.14
) is uniformly Lipschitz in
y,x
. In that case,
\eta=1
and
L_{1}^{(n)}(\cdot)
,
L_{2}^{(n)}(\cdot)
are both uniformly bounded. In view of (
3.15
), this implies
\theta=\infty
. Therefore
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta=1
. Recall from
(A4)
that
\gamma>1
denotes the number of finite moments of
X_{n,1}
. Therefore,
\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}<1
. As a result, the condition
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
holds in this case.

More generally speaking, the condition
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
can be rewritten as the combination of the following conditions:

\frac{1}{\theta}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}<1,\quad\frac{1}{\gamma}<2\eta-1.

Therefore, if the Hölder exponent
\eta
in (
3.14
) is greater than
\frac{1}{2}
, then the condition
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
holds whenever
L_{1}^{(n)}(X_{n,1},Y_{n,2})
,
L_{2}^{(n)}(X_{n,1},Y_{n,2})
, and
X_{n,1}
have sufficiently light tails.

Remark 2.2
.

In this paper, we have restricted ourselves to the case where the Hölder exponent
\eta\leq 1
instead of expanding to higher-order Hölder regularity. This is because parts (i) and (ii) of
Theorem

2.1
show that the order of the bias
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{n})-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
is
o(1/\sqrt{n})
which is already of a smaller order than its fluctuation
(\xi_{n}-\mathbb{E}\xi_{n})=O_{p}(1/\sqrt{n})
. As a result, imposing stronger regularity leads to no further gains here. The situation would be different for the multivariate version(s) of Chatterjee’s correlation (see
[
4
,
19
]
) where the bias would reflect a curse of dimensionality and be of a higher order than the fluctuations. While this is an interesting question, it is currently beyond the scope of this paper.

Note that
Theorem

2.1
greatly generalizes the asymptotic normality theorems of
[
12
,
15
,
58
]
that are only valid under the null hypothesis of independence, or for contiguous parametric alternatives and cannot be used to analyze
\xi_{n}
along non-contiguous and nonparametric alternatives. Therefore we believe
Theorem

2.1
is of independent interest and hence we have presented it here as a separate result. Since Theorem
2.1
aims to provide asymptotic normality for any alternative with
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 0
, we can no longer use traditional instruments such as Le Cam’s third lemma, which is the main tool in
[
12
,
58
]
. Note that
Theorem

2.1
holds for a large nonparametric class of distributions and comes with finite sample guarantees. In order to prove
Theorem

2.1
, we use Stein’s method of normal approximation for locally dependent structures
[
14
]
and some explicit bias and variance computations. To elaborate briefly, we observe that
\xi_{n}
can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\xi_{n}=\dfrac{3n}{n^{2}-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\underset{i\neq j}{\sum\sum}\left|\widehat{F}_{n}(Y_{n,i})-\widehat{F}_{n}(Y_{n,j})\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\widehat{F}_{n}(Y_{n,(i+1)})-\widehat{F}_{n}(Y_{n,(i)})\right|\right),\end{split}

(2.5)

where
\widehat{F}_{n}(\cdot)
is the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
Y_{n,1},\ldots,Y_{n,n}
. Let
F^{(n)}_{Y}(\cdot)
denote the population CDF of
Y_{n,1}
. Recall that
X_{n,(1)}\leq\ldots\leq X_{n,(n)}
and
Y_{n,(i)}
is the
Y
value concomitant to
X_{n,(i)}
. The main idea is to show that we can replace
\widehat{F}_{n}(\cdot)
by
F^{(n)}_{Y}(\cdot)
asymptotically. In other words, we show that
\xi_{n}
is close (with quantitative error bounds) to
\xi_{n}^{*}
, where

\xi_{n}^{*}:=\dfrac{3n}{n^{2}-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{n}\underset{i\neq j}{\sum\sum}\left|F_{Y}^{(n)}(Y_{n,i})-F_{Y}^{(n)}(Y_{n,j})\right|-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|F^{(n)}_{Y}(Y_{n,(i+1)})-F^{(n)}_{Y}(Y_{n,(i)})\right|\right).

(2.6)

Next, we quantify the proximity of the distribution of
\xi_{n}^{*}
(appropriately centered and scaled) to a standard normal distribution, using
[
14
, Theorem 3.4]
, to establish
Theorem

2.1
.

Now, we characterize the asymptotic behavior of
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
defined in (
1.8
). First, suppose that
\sqrt{n}\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to\infty
. As
\sqrt{n}\left(\xi_{n}-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\right)=O_{p}(1)
, by (
2.4
), it is clear that

\sqrt{n}\xi_{n}=\underbrace{\sqrt{n}\left(\xi_{n}-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\right)}_{O_{p}(1)}+\sqrt{n}\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}\infty.

Therefore, the last equality in (
1.8
) coupled with the above display implies that whenever
\sqrt{n}\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to\infty
, we have
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 1
. By using a similar sequence of arguments, we get the complete picture of the limiting behavior of
\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
, formalized in the theorem below.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to 0
and Assumptions
(A3)
,
(A4)
are satisfied with
\eta,\,\theta,\,\gamma
such that
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
. Let
z_{\alpha}
be the upper
\alpha
quantile of the standard normal distribution. Then the test
\phi_{n}
, defined in (
1.7
), with
z_{n,\alpha}=n^{-1/2}z_{\alpha}\sqrt{2/5}
has a power function satisfying

\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{n}\xi_{n}\geq z_{\alpha}\sqrt{2/5})=1-\Phi\left(z_{\alpha}-\sqrt{n}\xi(f^{(n)}_{X,Y})/\sqrt{2/5}\right)+o(1).

On the other hand, when
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\to c>0
,
\phi_{n}
has asymptotic power equal to one. In particular, we have the following explicit characterization of the asymptotic power of
\phi_{n}
:

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }\sqrt{n}\xi(f^{(n)}_{X,Y})\to 0\\
1-\Phi\left(z_{\alpha}-c_{0}/\sqrt{2/5}\right)&\text{ if }\sqrt{n}\xi(f^{(n)}_{X,Y})\to c_{0}\in(0,\infty)\\
1&\text{ if }\sqrt{n}\xi(f^{(n)}_{X,Y})\to\infty.\end{cases}

(2.7)

Theorem
2.2
reduces the power calculation to calculating the population measure of association under the alternative. Once again we emphasize that these results do not depend on any specific form of the alternative distribution. In
Section

3
we consider the applications of
Theorem

2.2
to certain parametric classes of alternatives, previously considered in the literature. In doing so, we discover that for smooth parametric alternatives, the detection threshold is seen on a non-standard scale of
n^{-1/4}
. This is much larger than the optimal detection threshold of
n^{-1/2}
in parametric problems, thereby leading to the suboptimality of
\xi_{n}
for testing independence (also see
[
58
,
12
]
). We provide a detailed account of this in the following section.

Throughout this section, we will use the test
\phi_{n}
in (

) with
z_{n,\alpha}=n^{-1/2}z_{\alpha}\sqrt{2/5}
, where
z_{\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
quantile of the standard normal distribution. To interpret the detection boundary from
Theorem

2.2
in terms of its rate of decay with respect to
n
, it is crucial to note that
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
(see
(P1)
) is the integrated
squared
distance between a conditional and a marginal distribution function. For example, consider the case where
f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the standard bivariate density
Gaussian
with correlation
\rho_{n}
. Then
Y|X=x\sim\mathcal{N}(\rho_{n}x,1-\rho_{n}^{2})
. Let
\phi(\cdot)
and
\Phi(\cdot)
be the standard normal density and distribution functions, respectively. By first-order Taylor approximations, we then get

\displaystyle\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})

\displaystyle=6\int\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi\left(\frac{t-\rho_{n}X}{\sqrt{1-\rho_{n}^{2}}}\right)-\Phi(t)\right]^{2}\phi(t)\,dt=\rho_{n}^{2}\sqrt{3}/\pi+o(\rho_{n}^{2}).

This implies
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})/\rho_{n}^{2}\to\sqrt{3}/\pi
, that is,
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
scales like
\rho_{n}^{2}
instead of
\rho_{n}
. This is the result of
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
being an integrated
squared
distance. Using this observation in (
2.7
), we get that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }n^{1/4}\rho_{n}\to 0\\
1-\Phi\left(z_{\alpha}-c_{1}\right)&\text{ if }n^{1/4}\rho_{n}\to c_{0}\in(0,\infty)\\
1&\text{ if }n^{1/4}\rho_{n}\to\infty.\end{cases}

(3.1)

This shows that a
n^{-1/4}
detection boundary emerges naturally out of Theorem
2.2
in the bivariate Gaussian setting.

3.1
Simple mixture model

Consider the bivariate density of
(X,Y)
defined by

f_{X,Y,r}(x,y):=(1-r)f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)+rg_{X,Y}(x,y)\quad\forall\ x,y\in\mathbb{R},

(3.2)

where
g_{X,Y}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a bivariate density function that does not factor into the product of its marginals,
f_{X}(\cdot)
,
f_{Y}(\cdot)
are univariate densities, and
r\in[0,1]
. We also note that if
r=0
, then
X
is independent of
Y
. Therefore it suffices to test if
r=0
or otherwise. Suppose that
g_{X,Y}(\cdot,\cdot)
has marginals
f_{X}(\cdot)
and
f_{Y}(\cdot)
, i.e.,

\int_{x}g_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx=f_{Y}(y),\quad\int_{y}g_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy=f_{X}(x).

(3.3)

Note that (
3.3
) implies that the marginals do not change under the alternative. Consequently, we cannot use marginal-based tests (e.g., goodness-of-fit on marginal distributions) to distinguish between the null and alternative. Instead, we will require an independence test as demonstrated above.
Furthermore, we also assume that there exist
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
,
\eta\in[0,1]
, and
\theta,\gamma>1
such that

\,\,\int_{y}^{\infty}\left|g_{Y|X=x_{1}}(t)-g_{Y|X=x_{2}}(t)\right|\,dt\leq(1+L_{1}(x_{1},y)+L_{2}(x_{2},y))\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|^{\eta},

(3.4)

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\int(L_{i}(x,y))^{\theta}f_{X}(x)f_{Y}(y)\,dx\,dy\leq\kappa_{1},

(3.5)

and

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\int|x|^{\gamma}f_{X}(x)\,dx<\kappa_{2}.

(3.6)

Let us observe that (
3.4
) is a mild regularity assumption on the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
when their joint distribution has density
g(\cdot,\cdot)
. Many common bivariate density functions, like bivariate normal with finite mean and variance, satisfy this assumption.

To perform local power analysis under model (
3.2
), we adopt the same framework as in
[
58
,
26
,
62
]
. Towards this direction, fix a sequence
\{r_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
with
r_{n}\in[0,1]
for all
n\geq 1
and consider the family of bivariate densities
f_{X,Y,r_{n}}(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
3.2
). It is easy to check that the condition (
1.6
), i.e.,
\xi(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})\to 0
holds if
r_{n}\to 0
. In the same vein as in (
1.5
), we consider the following testing problem:

\mathrm{H}_{0}:r_{n}=0\quad\mbox{vs.}\quad\mathrm{H}_{1,n}:r_{n}>0.

(3.7)

In view of (
1.6
) we focus on the shrinking alternative
r_{n}\to 0
.
Our object of interest is the limiting power function, i.e.,

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y,r_{n}}),\quad\mbox{where}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=0.

Crucially,
Theorem

2.2
reduces the above problem to characterizing the asymptotic behavior of
\xi(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})
. This is the subject of the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let us consider
\xi(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})
defined in (
1.4
) for a sequence
\{r_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that
r_{n}\in[0,1]
and
r_{n}\to 0
. Then we have

\xi(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})=r^{2}_{n}\;\xi(g_{X,Y}).

From
3.1
it is evident that
\sqrt{n}\xi(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})\to 0
or
\infty
accordingly as
n^{1/4}r_{n}\to 0
or
\infty
. Further,
\xi(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})
also increases with
\xi(g_{X,Y})
. Recall from
(P1)
that
\xi(g_{X,Y})
is a measure of association between
X,Y
jointly sampled according to
g_{X,Y}(\cdot,\cdot)
. Therefore,
3.1
shows that the power of
\phi_{n}
is governed by the strength of association between
X,Y
when they are jointly sampled from
g_{X,Y}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

We now present the complete characterization of the asymptotic power of
\phi_{n}
for the problem (
3.7
), which follows immediately from
3.1
coupled with

2.2
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Consider the problem of testing
\mathrm{H}_{0}
versus the sequence of alternatives
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}
defined by (
3.7
), for a sequence
\{r_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that
r_{n}\in[0,1]
and
r_{n}\to 0
. Suppose that (
3.3
) holds and Assumptions (
3.4
), (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) are satisfied with
\frac{\theta-1}{\theta}\wedge\eta>\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}
. Then the asymptotic power is given by

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y,r_{n}})=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }n^{1/4}r_{n}\to 0\\
1-\Phi(z_{\alpha}-c_{0}^{2}\xi(g_{X,Y})/\sqrt{2/5})&\text{ if }n^{1/4}r_{n}\to c_{0}\in(0,\infty)\\
1&\text{ if }n^{1/4}r_{n}\to\infty.\end{cases}

(3.8)

3.2
Regression model

In our main motivating paper
[
15
]
, the author considered the following model in numerical experiments:

Y=g(X)+\sigma Z,

(3.9)

where
\sigma\geq 1
,
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{E}[|g(X)|^{3}]<\infty
,
Z\sim N(0,1)
and
X,Z
are independent. We also assume that
X
has a finite
\gamma
-th moment for some
\gamma>3
and
g
satisfies

|g(x_{1})-g(x_{2})|\leq(1+|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|)|x_{2}-x_{1}|.

This is the classical noisy nonparametric regression model. Note that, as
\sigma\rightarrow\infty
, the “noise” part of the model (
3.9
) dominates the “signal” part given by
g(X)
. Therefore, as
\sigma\to\infty
, the independence between the “noise” and the “signal” makes it harder and harder for the independence testing procedures to have a high power. This makes it interesting to study the performance of
\phi_{n}
in the context of model (
3.9
) and to obtain detection thresholds in terms of
\sigma
.

Therefore, we consider the natural parametric model with
f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}}(\cdot,\cdot)
being the joint density of
(X,Y)
drawn according to the model (
3.9
) with
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{n}
. It is easy to check that
\xi(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}})\to 0
as
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
. Consequently, in the same spirit as in the previous section, we are interested in the following limiting power function:

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}}),\quad\mbox{where}\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}=\infty.

(3.10)

As in
Section

3.1
, we first state a proposition characterizing the asymptotics of
\xi(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider the model (
3.9
) with
\sigma\equiv\sigma_{n}
. We then have the following identity:

\xi(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}})={\sqrt{3}\over\pi}\sigma_{n}^{-2}\mathrm{Var}(g(X))+o(\sigma_{n}^{-2}).

3.2
shows that
\sqrt{n}\xi(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}})\to 0
or
\infty
accordingly as
n^{-1/4}\sigma_{n}\to\infty
or
0
. Also
\xi(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}})
increases with
\mbox{Var}(g(X))
. This is indeed very intuitive. Note that if
\mbox{Var}(g(X))=0
then
g(\cdot)
is a constant function which means
X,Y
are independent according to model (
3.9
). Also note that
\mbox{Var}(\mathbb{E}(Y|X))=\mbox{Var}(g(X))
and
\mathbb{E}\mbox{Var}(Y|X)=\sigma^{2}_{n}
, i.e.,
\mbox{Var}(g(X))
measures the proportion of the total variance of
Y
which is explained by
X
. Therefore, it is only natural that the larger the value of
\mbox{Var}(g(X))
, the larger is the power of
\phi_{n}
.

We now present the complete answer to problem (
3.10
), which follows immediately from
3.2
coupled with
Theorem

2.2
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider the problem in (
3.9
). Then the asymptotic power is given by

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\phi_{n}}(f_{X,Y,\sigma_{n}})=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{ if }n^{-1/4}\sigma_{n}\to\infty\\
1-\Phi(z_{\alpha}-c_{0}^{2}\sqrt{3}/\pi\,\mathrm{Var}(g(X))/\sqrt{2/5})&\text{ if }n^{-1/4}\sigma_{n}\to c_{0}^{-1}\in(0,\infty)\\
1&\text{ if }n^{-1/4}\sigma_{n}\to 0.\end{cases}

(3.11)

Assumption (A3)

There exist functions
L_{i}^{(n)}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)

\kappa_{1}>0

4
Minimax optimality of
\phi_{n}
for testing degree of association

Recall our basic setting, that is,
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}f_{X,Y}^{(n)}
. In the earlier sections, we focused on the case where
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
, the Dette-Siburg-Stoimenov measure,
converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
. On the contrary, the focus of this section is on the other regime.

\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})\downarrow\xi_{0}>0\qquad\mbox{as}\ n\to\infty.

(4.1)

This regime is of particular importance because the value of
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
provably encodes how noisy the functional relationship between
X
and
Y
is. In particular, in
[
21
, Theorem 2]
and
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
, the authors show that
\xi_{0}=1
implies
Y
is a noiseless function of
X
. Further, in
[
21
, Theorems 1 and 2]
, the authors also show that
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
can be used to define a natural notion of
dependence ordering
based on how well
Y
can be predicted from
X
. Through some explicit computations, in
[
21
, Section 4, Examples 1.(a) — (d)]
, the authors further prove that in multiple copula based dependence models,
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
is a
strictly monotonic
function of the natural dependence parameters. Moving to
[
15
]
, the author uses
extensive numerical experiments
to show that in a number of other examples, including variants of noisy nonparametric regression,
[
15
]
also shows through extensive simulations, that
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
decreases monotonically with increasing noise levels. Overall, these results show that through invertible transformations, one can directly convert
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
to natural dependence parameters in a variety of models for
(X_{1},Y_{1})
. Therefore, drawing inference about
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
immediately leads to interpretable inference about the nature of dependence in a large collection of models. We expand this collection of models further in the following proposition by showing that
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
is a monotonic function of natural dependence parameters in all examples from
Section

3
.

Proposition 4.1

(Monotonicity of
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
)
.

Recall the definition of
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
from (
1.4
).

(1)

Suppose

(X_{1},Y_{1})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}&\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rho\\
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rho&\sigma_{2}^{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),

for
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}>0
,
|\rho|\leq 1
.
Then
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
is free of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
and a strictly increasing function of
|\rho|
.

(2)

Suppose that
(X_{1},Y_{1})
is distributed according to the mixture model in (
3.2
) with
g_{X,Y}(\cdot)
further satisfying (
3.3
). Then
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
is a strictly increasing function of
r
.

(3)

Suppose
(X_{1},Y_{1})
is distributed according to the regression model (
3.9
), for
\sigma>0
. Then
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
is a strictly decreasing function of
\sigma
.

Motivated by the observations made above, we focus on the following natural hypothesis testing problem in this section:

\mathrm{H}_{0}:\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\xi_{0}\qquad\mbox{versus}\qquad\mathrm{H}_{1,n}:|\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})-\xi_{0}|\geq c_{n},

(4.2)

for some
positive
sequence
\{c_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
. Clearly if
c_{n}=O(1)
, then from
[
15
, Theorem 1.1]
, Chatterjee’s correlation
\xi_{n}
can be used to consistently separate
\mathrm{H}_{0}
from
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}
. On the other hand, our focus here is when
c_{n}=o(1)
which leads to (
4.1
). When
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
, (
4.2
) can be viewed as a slightly general (two-sided) version of (
1.5
). The goal of this section is to address the following pair of subtle
questions:

•

What is the fastest decaying sequence
c_{n}
such that Chatterjee’s correlation coefficient
\xi_{n}
can still separate
\mathrm{H}_{0}
from
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}
?

•

Conversely, what is the slowest decaying sequence
c_{n}
such that no test can separate
\mathrm{H}_{0}
from
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}
in the local asymptotic minimax sense?

We will prove in this section that, for Chatterjee’s correlation, the detection boundary occurs at
c_{n}\gg n^{-1/2}
. However, in sharp contrast to the
\xi_{0}=0
case,
c_{n}\approx n^{-1/2}
is indeed the minimax optimal threshold, in that no test can consistently separate the two hypotheses when
c_{n}\approx n^{-1/2}
. This indicates that Chatterjee’s correlation based test is indeed
minimax optimal
for testing the degree of association when the two variables are not exactly independent. In the sequel, we will formalize these two notions.

We begin with some notation. Let
\Gamma:=(C,\eta,\theta,\gamma)
where the aforementioned constants are taken from Assumptions
(A3)
and
(A4)
. Consider the following family of distributions:

\small\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma):=\left\{f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot):|\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})-\xi_{0}|\geq c_{n},\ f_{X,Y}^{(n)}\ \mbox{satisfies\ \ref{as:Lipschitz},\ \ref{as:nn_dists}\ with\ constants}\ \Gamma=(C,\eta,\theta,\gamma)\right\}.

(4.3)

In other words, the family
\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma)
consists of the family of joint distributions which admit
\xi(\cdot)
values at a
\geq c_{n}
distance away from the null value
\xi_{0}
, and also satisfy Assumptions
(A3)
and
(A4)
. Next, given a test function
\Phi_{n}\equiv\Phi_{n}((X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n}))
, consider its power function

\beta_{\Phi_{n}}(\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma)):=\inf_{f_{X,Y}^{(n)}(\cdot,\cdot)\in\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma)}\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{n}\ \mbox{rejects}\ \mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma)).

(4.4)

In terms of the definitions in (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), the goal of a good test
\Phi_{n}
would be to ensure that
\beta_{\Phi_{n}}(\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma))\to 1
as
n\to\infty
for “small” values of
c_{n}
. Given a level parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
, We define our candidate good test as follows:

\phi_{n}:=\mathbbm{1}\left(|\xi_{n}-\xi_{0}|\geq\frac{1+5\sqrt{\log{(2/\alpha)}}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(4.5)

The following theorem provides matching upper and lower bounds for
\beta_{(\cdot)}(\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma))
.

Theorem 4.1

(Testing for degree of association)
.

(1)

(Upper bound for
\xi_{n}
)
. Fix an arbitrary
\alpha\in(0,1)
and consider the test
\phi_{n}
from (
4.5
). Then it is an asymptotically level
\alpha
under
\mathrm{H}_{0}
and

\beta_{\phi_{n}}(\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma))\to 1

provided
\sqrt{n}c_{n}\to\infty
.

(2)

(Minimax lower bound)
. There exists some
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that for any level
\alpha
test
\Phi_{n}
based on
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
, the following holds:

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\beta_{\Phi_{n}}(\mathrm{H}_{1,n}^{\mathrm{loc}}(c_{n};\Gamma))<1

whenever
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\sqrt{n}c_{n}<\infty
.

The two parts of
Theorem

4.1
suggest that Chatterjee’s correlation based test is particularly suitable for testing the strength of association between two random variables and is able to detect small departures from a fixed
non-zero
value of
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})
(i.e.,
\xi_{0}
) at the optimal rate. It is worth noting that the lower bound in
Theorem

4.1
heavily relies on the fact that
0<\xi_{0}<1
.

Remark 4.1
.

In
[
49
]
, the authors show that

\frac{\sqrt{n}(\xi_{n}-\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)}))}{\widehat{\sigma}_{n}}\overset{w}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
is a function of the data, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}=O_{p}(1)
. Therefore, a natural alternative to
\phi_{n}
from (
4.5
) would be

\phi_{n}=\mathbbm{1}(\sqrt{n}|\xi_{n}-\xi_{0}|\geq z_{\alpha/2}),

where
z_{\alpha/2}
is the upper
\alpha/2
standard Gaussian quantile and
\xi(f_{X,Y}^{(n)})=\xi_{0}
. This test was proposed in
[
49
, Remark 1.4]
and it has asymptotic size exactly equal to
\alpha
. On the flip side, from the definition of
\widehat{\sigma}_{n}
(see
[
49
, Theorem 1.1]
), it seems that it has
O(n^{2})
time complexity which may be a greater computational burden depending on the application at hand.

Remark 4.2
.

In
[
48
]
, the authors show that if
(X,Y)
have a bivariate Gaussian distribution, then the detection boundary for
\xi_{n}
when
\xi_{0}=0
can be improved to near parametric rates by incorporating more “right nearest neighbors”. We would conjecture that the test in
[
48
]
achieves
O(n^{-1/2})
detection boundary when
\xi_{0}\in(0,1)
and
(X,Y)
are non-Gaussian. In view of
Theorem

4.1
, this would imply that the test in
[
48
]
attains (near) parametric efficiency whenever
\xi_{0}\in[0,1)
(at the expense of greater computational complexity). A conclusive answer on this and an inspection of the relevant applications might be of independent interest.
