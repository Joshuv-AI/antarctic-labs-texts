Weisfeiler–Leman and Graph Spectra A conference version [ 51 ] of this article appeared at SODA 2023.
Gaurav Rattan RWTH Aachen University rattan@cs.rwth-aachen.de, Tim Seppelt RWTH Aachen University seppelt@cs.rwth-aachen.de

Abstract
Two simple undirected graphs are cospectral if their respective adjacency matrices have the same multiset of eigenvalues. Cospectrality yields an equivalence relation on the family of graphs which is provably weaker than isomorphism. In this paper, we study cospectrality in relation to another well-studied relaxation of isomorphism, namely k k -dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman ( k k - WL ) indistinguishability. Cospectrality with respect to standard graph matrices such as the adjacency or the Laplacian matrix yields a strictly finer equivalence relation than 2 2 - WL indistinguishability. We show that individualising one vertex plus running 1 1 - WL already subsumes cospectrality with respect to all such graph matrices. Building on this result, we resolve an open problem of Fürer (2010) about spectral invariants. Looking beyond 2 2 - WL , we devise a hierarchy of graph matrices generalising the adjacency matrix such that k k - WL indistinguishability after a fixed number of iterations can be captured as a spectral condition on these matrices. Precisely, we provide a spectral characterisation of k k - WL indistinguishability after d d iterations, for k , d ∈ ℕ k,d\in\mathbb{N} . Our results can be viewed as characterisations of homomorphism indistinguishability over certain graph classes in terms of matrix equations. The study of homomorphism indistinguishability is an emerging field, to which we contribute by extending the algebraic framework of Mančinska and Roberson (2020) and Grohe et al. (2022).

Introduction

The algebraic properties of the adjacency matrix of a graph can reveal a great deal of information about the structure of the graph. In particular, the spectra of certain matrices associated with a graph, such as the adjacency matrix or the Laplacian matrix, can already determine several important graph properties such as connectedness and bipartiteness
[
9
]
. Moreover, they can be used to approximate several important graph parameters such as graph expansion
[
11
]
and chromatic number
[
24
]
. A well-known result of this kind is Kirchhoff’s Matrix Tree Theorem which asserts that the number of spanning trees of a connected graph is equal to the product of its non-zero Laplacian eigenvalues, cf.
[
26
]
. Apart from the adjacency matrix and the Laplacian matrix, several other graph matrices such as the signless or normalised Laplacian, the Seidel matrix, etc. have been utilised to obtain further insights into several graph properties and parameters
[
56
,
14
,
55
]
. In algebraic graph theory, the spectra of such graph matrices are collectively referred to as spectral graph invariants.

Two simple undirected graphs are said to be
cospectral
if their respective adjacency matrices have the same multiset of eigenvalues. Whereas isomorphic graphs are necessarily cospectral, the converse does not hold in general. Nevertheless, cospectrality is an important equivalence relation on graphs which continues to be extensively studied in algebraic graph theory. The notion of cospectrality can be easily extended to graph matrices other than the adjacency matrix: each such graph matrix induces a corresponding equivalence relation on graphs
[
55
]
. The main objective of this paper is to analyse cospectrality in relation to the well-studied relaxations of isomorphism arising from algebra, combinatorics, and logic, see e.g.
[
35
,
7
,
18
]
. In particular, we focus on the equivalence relations arising from the Weisfeiler–Leman framework
[
57
]
. Formally, the
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman (
k
-
WL
) is a combinatorial procedure which iteratively partitions the set of
k
-tuples of vertices into finer equivalence classes based on their local neighbourhoods, cf.
[
31
]
. In the parlance of
k
-
WL
, this yields a so-called stable colouring function which associates a colour to every
k
-tuple of vertices. Two graphs are
k
-
WL
indistinguishable
if the
k
-
WL
procedure generates the same multiset of stable vertex-tuple colours for the two graphs. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the
k
-
WL
procedure thus yields an equivalence relation on the domain of graphs, namely
k
-
WL
indistinguishability
, and moreover, letting
k\in\mathbb{N}
vary, an infinite hierarchy of strictly finer equivalence relations
[
10
]
. Hence, there is no fixed dimension
k
such that
k
-
WL
indistinguishability coincides with isomorphism. Importantly, the
WL
framework has been shown to admit equivalent formalisms in descriptive complexity
[
35
]
, convex optimisation
[
7
]
, and substructure counting
[
18
]
. The fact that
k
-WL is established as a powerful and expressive framework which captures a wide variety of equivalence relations on the domain of graphs justifies our interest in using the
k
-
WL
framework as a yardstick for analysing cospectrality. It may be noted that for most natural graph classes, there exists a fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
k
-
WL
indistinguishability coincides with the isomorphism relation on the given graph class. The prominent examples include trees
[
5
,
35
]
, planar graphs
[
27
,
37
]
, and graphs with excluded minors
[
28
]
.

Our line of investigation has a concrete practical motivation in the context of the recent work in the field of machine learning with graph data. In the emerging field of graph representation learning, the design of graph neural networks (GNNs) for achieving robust inference on graphs has received much attention
[
34
]
. There has been considerable recent work on measuring the expressive power GNNs, i.e. whether a GNN can generate distinct representations for non-isomorphic graphs. It turns out that the expressive power of the most commonly used GNNs, namely
message-passing
GNNs, is equal to
1
-
WL
indistinguishability
[
59
,
47
]
, i.e. a message-passing GNN can generate distinct representations for two graphs if and only if they are
1
-
WL
distinguishable. On the other hand,
spectral
GNNs work in a very different manner to message-passing networks: they learn a composition of spectral transformations on the adjacency matrix of the graph. Moreover, several machine learning models directly work with the eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix, arising from transformations such as principal component analysis, matrix factorisation, etc.
[
40
]
. We believe that our work will serve as a contribution towards studying the expressive power of combination of spectral invariants or representations with combinatorial algorithms and message passing GNNs.

Our Results: Spectral characterisation of
k
-
WL

The set of results described above leads to the following question: do there exist graph matrices such that their spectral properties yields a finer equivalence relation than
2
-
WL

We provide an affirmative answer to this question by characterising indistinguishability after

Firstly, it turns out that we need stronger spectral conditions than just the cospectrality of certain graph matrices. Nevertheless, our stated conditions on the devised graph matrices will be later presented as a spectral characterisation in the sense of representation theory, cf.
[

]
. Secondly, our results stipulate the number of

-
WL

[

]
that two graphs are indistinguishable after

-

-variable quantifier-depth-

fragment of first order logic with counting quantifiers, the notion is of much theoretical interest

,

,
36
]
.
On the practical side, it was recently shown
[

]
that two graphs are indistinguishable by a message passing GNN (resp.

iterations of

-

-

Moreover, our results serve as a possible pathway for incorporating spectral tools in the design and analysis of more powerful variants of the

Let

For proving

Each step is exemplified by the arguments used to derive a characterisation of homomorphism indistinguishability over trees in

1.

Define a family of labelled graphs and their homomorphism vectors

Labelled graphs are tuples
\boldsymbol{F}=(F,u)
where
F
is a graph and

. For every graph
G
, a labelled graph
\boldsymbol{F}
gives rise to a

\boldsymbol{F}_{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}
where
\boldsymbol{F}_{G}(v)
for

is defined as the number of

h\colon F\to G
such that

, i.e. mappings
h\colon V(F)\to V(G)
such that

for every

.

For the example, let
\mathcal{T}
denote the family of labelled trees
(T,u)
, i.e. of trees

with an arbitrary labelled vertex

.

2.

Define suitable combinatorial and algebraic operations

It was observed in
[

]
that there is a close correspondence between combinatorial operations on labelled graphs and algebraic operations on homomorphism vectors. An instance is the
gluing product
of
\boldsymbol{F}=(F,u)
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}=(F^{\prime},u^{\prime})
which results in the labelled graph
\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}
obtained by taking the disjoint union of

and

to yield the new labelled vertex. It can be shown that the entry-wise product
\boldsymbol{F}_{G}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}_{G}
of the homomorphism vectors
\boldsymbol{F}_{G}
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}_{G}
yields the homomorphism vector
(\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime})_{G}
of the gluing product
\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}
.

For the example, we consider gluing and the operation of attaching a fresh edge

to the labelled vertex

of

and moving the label to

.

3.

Prove finite generation

Aiming at a matrix equation with finitely many constraints, it is typically proven that the family of labelled graphs in step
1
is finitely generated by certain

under the operation in step

\mathcal{T}
is generated under gluing and attaching edges by the single-vertex labelled graph without any edges.

4.

Recover a matrix equation using algebraic and representation-theoretic techniques.

For trees, one may use the techniques in
[
33
]
to discover the fractional isomorphism matrix equation.

The main difficulty arising when applying this strategy to pebble forest covers is that there is no obvious way to define labelling and operations such that the resulting class of labelled graphs is closed under these. We overcome this obstacle by promoting the labels from mere distinguished vertices to objects encoding the role the selected vertices play in the associated pebble forest cover. Subsequently, the gluing operation can be restricted to pairs of labelled graphs whose labels play the same role. While this resolves the combinatorial problems in steps

–

Although this approach might appear to be tailored to the graph class relevant for

[

,

,

]

Their framework of comonads on the category of relational structures has given rise to a categorical language for capturing natural graph classes and decompositions leading moreover to results in homomorphism indistinguishability

,

For proving

We believe that our approach may facilitate the study of matrix equations for homomorphism indistinguishability over graph classes which are more intricate with respect to labellings, operations, and generation.

is a finite chain.
The
depth
of a forest
\mathcal{F}
is the length of a maximal chain in
\mathcal{F}
.
A

is a forest
(F,\leq)
with a
root
element

such that
x\leq y
for all

.
A

of a tree

is an element
x
of

such the set

of successors of
x
is
\{x\}
.
The
depth of a leaf
x
of a tree

is the length of the unique maximal chain from the root element to the leaf element.

A

of a graph

is a forest
\mathcal{F}=(F,\leq)
with
F=V(G)

such that for every edge
uv\in E(G)
, it must hold that either
u\leq v
or
v\leq u
.
A

of a graph

is a tree
\mathcal{T}
which is a forest cover of

.

Let
\mathcal{F}=(F,\leq)
be a forest cover for a graph

.
A
k
-pebbling function
for the pair
(G,\mathcal{F})
is a mapping

satisfying that for all vertices

such that
u\leq v
and

, it holds that

for every

such that

.
A

-pebble forest cover
of

is a pair
(\mathcal{F},p)
such that

is a forest cover for

and

is a

-pebbling function for

. It can be shown

that a graph

.

Definition 2.1

(Pebble Forest Covers for Labelled Graphs)
.

Let

.
A

-pebble forest cover of an
\ell
-labelled graph

is a

-pebble forest cover

of

1.

the labelled vertices

form a chain, i.e. are mutually comparable in

all other vertices lie below the labelled vertices, i.e. for all

it holds that

,

the pebbling function

is injective on

then
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=\boldsymbol{u}_{j}

.

The

of such an

is the length of the longest chain in

.
Write

for the class of

-labelled graphs admitting a

Write
\mathcal{PFC}_{k}^{d}

.

2.3
Weisfeiler–Leman

Let
k\geq 1
.
Given a graph
G
, the
k

(
k
-
WL
) colours
k
-tuples of its vertices in the following iterative manner.
Let
\atp
denote a function which maps a graph

and
\boldsymbol{x}\in V(G)^{\ell}
to some value such that
\atp(G,\boldsymbol{x})=\atp(H,\boldsymbol{y})
if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\mapsto\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
,

is a partial isomorphism, cf.
[

, Section IV]
.
Define
\textup{{WL}}_{k}^{0}(G,\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\atp(G,\boldsymbol{v})
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in V(G)
and for

\textup{{WL}}_{k}^{d+1}(G,\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\left(\textup{{WL}}_{k}^{d}(G,\boldsymbol{v}),\left\{\!\!\left\{\left(\atp(G,\boldsymbol{v}w),\textup{{WL}}_{k}^{d}(G,\boldsymbol{v}[1/w]),\dots,\textup{{WL}}_{k}^{d}(G,\boldsymbol{v}[k/w])\right)\ \middle|\ w\in V(G)\right\}\!\!\right\}\right).

Here,

for

denotes the
k
-tuple obtained from

by replacing its

-th entry by

.
The procedure terminates when the colouring is stable, i.e. once the partition of

into colour classes prescribed by this colouring is not refined by an additional iteration of

-
WL
.
We write
\textup{{WL}}_{k}^{\infty}
for this stable colouring.
For further details, see e.g.
[
10
,
31
]
.

and

are

-

for

and of the

for

are the same.
This equivalence relation is a well-studied notion which foremost admits a logical characterisation.
Let
\mathsf{C}_{k}
denote the
k
-variable fragment of first-order logic with counting quantifiers. Let
\mathsf{C}_{k}^{d}
denote the quantifier rank-

fragment of

.
The equivalence of

3
and

in the following theorem characterising

-

indistinguishability is implicit in
[

,

]
. The equivalence of

. For completeness, we give a self-contained proof in

.

Theorem 2.2

(
[

, Section IV.B]
,

, Theorem 18]
,

Let

A variation of the Specht–Wiegmann Theorem
[
53
,
58
]
has proven useful for deriving matrix equations for homomorphism indistinguishability in
[

]
. It also serves as the representation-theoretic core of the proof of

.
In order to state it, let

denote the finite alphabet

for some

. Let

denote the set of all finite words over

.
Given a family of complex-valued matrices

, a word

can be represented as a matrix

by sending

to

,

to

, the conjugate-transpose of

, and interpreting concatenation as matrix product.
Let

denote the sum of all entries of a complex matrix

.
Recall that a matrix is pseudo-stochastic if its row and column sums are one.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
33
, Theorem 20]
)
.

Let
I
and
J
be finite index sets.
Let
\mathsf{A}=(A_{1},\dots,A_{m})
and
\mathsf{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{m})
be two sequences of matrices such that

and

for

. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

such that

and

for all

.

2.

For every word

,
\soe(w_{\mathsf{A}})=\soe(w_{\mathsf{B}})
.

3.1
Spectral Characterisation of
(1,1)
-
WL
Indistinguishable Graphs

Let
G
be a graph.
Let
\mathcal{C}
denote a
vertex colouring
, i.e. a map from
V(G)
to a (unspecified) set of colours.
Abusing notation, we sometimes treat
\mathcal{C}
as a partition of
V(G)
into colour classes
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
Define
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{C}\leq\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}
to be the vector space spanned by colour class indicator vectors
\boldsymbol{1}_{C}
,
C\in\mathcal{C}
.
The projection onto
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{C}
, typically denoted by
M
, is called the
averaging matrix
of
\mathcal{C}
as
M=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\left|C\right|^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{C}\boldsymbol{1}_{C}^{T}
acts by averaging vectors over colour classes.
For
v\in V(G)
,
let
\mathcal{C}_{G}^{v}
denote the vertex colouring obtained by running
1
-
WL
on the vertex-individualised graph
G_{v}
, i.e. the coloured graph obtained from
G
by colouring
v
orange and all other vertices grey.

Definition 3.1
.

A matrix map
\varphi
which associates with every graph
G
a matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{V(G)\times V(G)}
is an
equitable matrix map
if for all
(1,1)
-
WL
indistinguishable graphs
G
and
H
and all colour-preserving bijections
\pi\colon V(G)\to V(H)
it holds that

(E1)

for all
v\in V(G)
,
M^{v}\varphi(G)=\varphi(G)M^{v}
for
M^{v}
the averaging matrix of
\mathcal{C}_{G}^{v}
,

(E2)

for all
v\in V(G)
and permutation transformations
P\colon\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}\to\mathbb{C}^{V(H)}
such that for all
x\in V(G)
there exists
y\in V(H)
such that
Pe_{x}=e_{y}
and
\mathcal{C}_{G}^{v}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{H}^{\pi(v)}(y)
it holds that
P\varphi(G)M^{v}=\varphi(H)PM^{v}
.

Write
\mathfrak{E}
for the set of all equitable matrix maps.

Assertion
(E1)
guarantees that equitable matrix maps respect the subspaces
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{C}^{v}_{G}\leq\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}
induced by
(1,1)
-
WL
. That a similar statement holds for the adjacency matrix and
1
-
WL
was first observed in
[
22
, Lemmata 3.1, 3.2]
.
(E2)
relates the entries of equitable matrix maps evaluated at
(1,1)
-
WL
indistinguishable graphs turning them essentially into functions of the
(1,1)
-
WL
colours.

Lemma 3.2
.

The adjacency matrix map
A
and the degree matrix map
D
are equitable matrix maps.

Proof.

For
(E1)
, let
G
be a graph with equitable vertex colouring
\mathcal{C}
whose averaging matrix is
M
.
For sets
S,T\subseteq V(G)
write
E_{G}(S,T)
for the number of edges connecting vertices in
S
with vertices in
T
.
Then for vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, letting
\mathcal{C}(v)\subseteq V(G)
denote the
\mathcal{C}
-colour class to which
v
belongs,

(MA(G))(u,v)=\frac{E_{G}(\{v\},\mathcal{C}(u))}{\left|\mathcal{C}(u)\right|}=\frac{E_{G}(\mathcal{C}(v),\mathcal{C}(u))}{\left|\mathcal{C}(u)\right|\left|\mathcal{C}(v)\right|}=\frac{E_{G}(\{u\},\mathcal{C}(v))}{\left|\mathcal{C}(v)\right|}=(A(G)M)(u,v).

For
(E2)
, let
H
be a second graph such that
G
and
H
are
(1,1)
-
WL
indistinguishable. Let
\pi\colon V(G)\to V(H)
be colour-preserving,
v\in V(G)
, and
M^{v}
and
P
as in
(E2)
. Let
C\in\mathcal{C}_{G}^{v}
be a colour class. Since
G
and
H
are
(1,1)
-
WL
indistinguishable, there is a corresponding colour class
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{H}^{\pi(v)}
. Then

PA(G)\boldsymbol{1}_{C}=\sum_{D\in\mathcal{C}_{G}^{v}}E_{G}(D,C)P\boldsymbol{1}_{D}=\sum_{D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{H}^{\pi(v)}}E_{H}(D^{\prime},C^{\prime})\boldsymbol{1}_{D^{\prime}}=A(H)P\boldsymbol{1}_{C}

since
P\boldsymbol{1}_{C}=\boldsymbol{1}_{C^{\prime}}
. This implies
(E2)
.
Similar arguments show that
D
is an equitable matrix map.
∎

The following
Lemma

3.3
asserts that
\mathfrak{E}
has enjoyable algebraic properties.

Lemma 3.3
.

\mathfrak{E}
is closed under linear combinations, matrix products, and transposes.

Proof.

Since
(E1)
and
(E2)
are linear conditions, the first claim is immediate.

For closure under matrix products, let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathfrak{E}
. Let
G
be any graph and
M^{v}
be as in
(E1)
. Then

M^{v}(\varphi\cdot\psi)(G)=M^{v}\varphi(G)\psi(G)=\varphi(G)M^{v}\psi(G)=\varphi(G)\psi(G)M^{v}=(\varphi\cdot\psi)(G)M^{v}
by
(E1)
.
Hence,
\varphi\cdot\psi
satisfies
(E1)
.
For
(E2)
, let
H
and
P
be as in
Definition

3.1
. Then

\displaystyle P(\varphi\cdot\psi)(G)M^{v}

\displaystyle=P\varphi(G)\psi(G)M^{v}\overset{\text{\ref{l1}}}{=}P\varphi(G)M^{v}\psi(G)\overset{\text{\ref{l2}}}{=}\varphi(H)PM^{v}\psi(G)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\ref{l1}}}{=}\varphi(H)P\psi(G)M^{v}\overset{\text{\ref{l2}}}{=}(\varphi\cdot\psi)(H)PM^{v}.

Hence,
(E2)
holds.

For closure under transposes, let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{E}
.
Observe that the
M^{v}
are symmetric matrices. Hence,
\varphi^{T}
satisfies
(E1)
.
Furthermore, if
P\colon\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}\to\mathbb{C}^{V(H)}
is a permutation transformation such that for all
x\in V(G)
there exists
y\in V(H)
such that
Pe_{x}=e_{y}
and
\mathcal{C}_{G}^{v}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{H}^{\pi(v)}(y)
then
P^{T}\colon\mathbb{C}^{V(H)}\to\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}
is such that for all
y\in V(H)
there exists
x\in V(G)
such that
Pe_{y}=e_{x}
and
\mathcal{C}_{G}^{\pi^{-1}(v)}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{H}^{v}(y)
where
\pi^{-1}\colon V(H)\to V(G)
is a colour-preserving bijection. It follows that
P^{T}\varphi(H)M^{v}=\varphi(G)P^{T}M^{v}
. Hence,
M^{v}\varphi^{T}(H)P=M^{v}P\varphi^{T}(G)
.
It remains to observe that
M^{v}P=PM^{\pi^{-1}(v)}
and that
(E2)
holds.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathfrak{E}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. For a graph

\varphi_{\lambda}(G)
to be the projection onto the space

spanned by the eigenvectors of

with eigenvalue

. Then
\varphi_{\lambda}\in\mathfrak{E}
.

Proof.

By
(E1)
,
\varphi(G)
preserves the direct sum decomposition
\mathbb{C}^{V(G)}=\mathbb{C}\mathcal{C}^{v}_{G}\oplus(\mathbb{C}\mathcal{C}^{v}_{G})^{\perp}
for the individualised vertex colouring of any

.
Furthermore,

preserves the direct sum decomposition

.
Indeed, if

, so

.
This implies
(E1)
.

For

, observe that if

then
\lambda PM^{v}x=P\varphi(G)M^{v}x=\varphi(H)PM^{v}x=\varphi(H)PM^{v}x
by

, so

is in the eigenspace of
\varphi(H)
with eigenvalue

. Hence,
P\varphi_{\lambda}(G)M^{v}x=PM^{v}x=\varphi_{\lambda}(H)PM^{v}x
. If
x\in(E_{\lambda})^{\perp}
then

. This implies
(E2)
.
∎

.

Lemma

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
G
be a graph. Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}=(\boldsymbol{F},p_{\textup{in}},p_{\textup{out}}),\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime},p^{\prime}_{\textup{in}},p^{\prime}_{\textup{out}})\in\mathcal{WL}_{k}^{d}
.

1.

Unlabelling corresponds to sum-of-entries, i.e.
\hom(\soe\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}},G)=\soe\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{G}
.

2.

Reversal corresponds to taking adjoints, i.e.
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}^{*})_{G}=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{G})^{*}
.

3.

Series composition corresponds to matrix product, i.e.
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\prime})_{G}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{G}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\prime}_{G}

Proof.

First consider the correspondence of unlabelling and sum-of-entries. Recalling the analogous argument for the homomorphism representation from
[
44
,
33
]
,

\displaystyle\hom(\soe\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}},G)

\displaystyle=\hom(\soe\boldsymbol{F},G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in V(G)^{k+d}}\boldsymbol{F}_{G}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in V(G)^{d},\\
p,p^{\prime}\colon[k+d]\to[k+1]\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{F}_{G}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})e_{p}^{T}e_{p_{\textup{in}}}e_{p_{\textup{out}}}^{T}e_{p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\soe\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{G}

since
e_{p}^{T}e_{p_{\textup{in}}}e_{p_{\textup{out}}}^{T}e_{p^{\prime}}=1
iff

and
p^{\prime}=p_{\textup{out}}
and zero otherwise for all
p,p^{\prime}\colon[k+d]\to[k+1]
.

The correspondence between reversal and transposition is purely syntactical.

Finally, consider series composition and matrix product. If one of the factors is
\bot
, which is mapped to the zero matrix under the augmented homomorphism representation, the statement is readily verified. Otherwise, let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in V(G)^{k+d}
and

be arbitrary.

\displaystyle(\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}_{G}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\prime}_{G})(\boldsymbol{u},p;\boldsymbol{u},p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in V(G)^{k+d},\\

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in V(G)^{k+d},\\
q\colon[k+d]\to[k+1]\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{F}_{G}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}_{G}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\delta_{p=p_{\textup{in}}}\delta_{p_{\textup{out}}=q=p^{\prime}_{\textup{in}}}\delta_{p^{\prime}_{\textup{out}}=p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in V(G)^{k+d}}\boldsymbol{F}_{G}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}_{G}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\delta_{p=p_{\textup{in}}}\delta_{p_{\textup{out}}=p^{\prime}_{\textup{in}}}\delta_{p^{\prime}_{\textup{out}}=p^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(\boldsymbol{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime})_{G}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\delta_{p=p_{\textup{in}}}\delta_{p^{\prime}_{\textup{out}}=p^{\prime}},&\text{if }p_{\textup{out}}=p^{\prime}_{\textup{in}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\prime})_{G}(\boldsymbol{u},p;\boldsymbol{u},p^{\prime}).

The last equality holds because of the correspondence of series composition and matrix product in the non-augmented case
[

,
33
]
.
∎

4.4
Matrix Equations

We complete the proof of
Theorem

1.5
by appealing to the sum-of-entries version of the Specht–Wiegmann theorem, stated as
Theorem

2.4
.

Proof.

By
Corollary

2.3
, it suffices to show that the second assertion in
Theorem

1.5
is equivalent to homomorphism indistinguishability of
G
and
H
over
\mathcal{PFC}_{k}^{d}
.
First suppose that the latter condition holds.
Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{1},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{m}
be an enumeration of
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{d}
.
Let
\mathsf{A}\coloneqq(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{1}_{G},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{m}_{G})
and
\mathsf{B}\coloneqq(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{1}_{H},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{m}_{H})
be the matrix sequences obtained by applying the augmented homomorphism representations with respect to
G
and
H
.
By
Theorem

2.4
, it suffices to show that
\soe(w_{\mathsf{A}})=\soe(w_{\mathsf{B}})
for every word
w\in\Gamma_{2m}
.

Since
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{d}
is closed under the reversal operation, there exist

\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{i_{1}},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}^{i_{r}}\in\mathcal{A}_{k}^{d}
such that

and

. If the product evaluates to

then

\soe(w_{\mathsf{A}})=0=\soe(w_{\mathsf{B}})
by

4.5
.
Otherwise, by

4.4
and
4.5
, the graph underlying the product is in

.
Hence,
\soe(w_{\mathsf{A}})=\hom(F,G)=\hom(F,H)=\soe(w_{\mathsf{B}})
, by assumption and
Lemma

4.5
.

Now suppose that the second assertion of
Theorem

1.5
holds, i.e. there exists a pseudo-stochastic matrix

satisfying the stipulated equations. Since
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{d}
contains the identity graph

,

By padding

for some

. Indeed, the disjoint union

of two graphs

,

and

and

have the same number of vertices.

Applying

4.7
to the graph
\widehat{\boldsymbol{F}}
, we obtain a sequence

.
∎

5
Conclusion

We mapped out the landscape of spectral graph invariants between
1
-
WL
and
2
-
WL
by introducing
(1,1)
-
WL
indistinguishability which subsumes many such invariants. This resolves an open question of
[
23
]
and strengthens a result of
[
25
]
.
We furthermore devised a hierarchy of spectral invariants matching the hierarchy of combinatorial invariants yielded by the Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm.
The design of graph neural networks based on these higher-order spectral invariants forms an interesting direction of future work.

Our results contribute to the study of homomorphism indistinguishability. For characterising homomorphism indistinguishability over graphs admitting certain
k
-pebble forest covers of depth
d
, we devised a representation of labelled graphs in terms of matrices encoding information about the structure of the labels such that it affords a correspondence between combinatorial and algebraic operations. We believe that this strategy may prove useful for studying other graph classes in the future.

Definition A.3
.

Let
k\geq\ell\geq 1
and
d\geq 0
.
An
\ell
-labelled graph

-pebble forest cover of depth

-pebble forest cover of depth

such that
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\leq\dots\leq\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}
and

.

The relevant property of such regular pebble forest covers is that if
\boldsymbol{F}
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}
both admit
k
-pebble forest cover of depth

then so does their gluing product
\boldsymbol{F}\odot\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}
. Indeed, the labelled vertices lie on a path of the cover in the same order and the pebbling functions of the covers of
\boldsymbol{F}
and
\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}

Subject to the next lemmas are relational structures
F
over the signature
\{E,I\}
comprising the binary relation symbols

and

. A

over
\{E,I\}
is a tuple
(U,R^{E},R^{I})

is a finite set, the

, and

are always symmetric relations. A

such that for all

and

. See
[
29
]

A

-labelled

-structure
is a tuple

where
\boldsymbol{u}\in U^{k}
. The notion of a homomorphism extends to
k
-labelled structures. The homomorphism count function
\hom(-,-)
can be defined analogously as for graphs.

The relation symbol

will be understood as the binary edge relation of the graphs which are of our ultimate interest.

is an additional binary relation.
A graph

can be turned into an
\{E,I\}
-structure by interpreting

as equality, i.e.

A (labelled)

-structure admits a (regular)

-pebble forest cover of depth

-relation in place of the

-relation, mutatis mutandis. In the language of
[

]
, this cover is a cover for the relational structure.

We recall the following notions from
[

]

A

-labelled quantum relational structure
is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear combination of
k
-labelled relational structures called its

.
For

and a
k
-labelled

-labelled relational structure over

\varphi

\boldsymbol{1}

If

and

•

\boldsymbol{1}-t_{1}
models
\varphi
.

•

If
\varphi=\exists^{\geq m}x_{i}.\ \psi
then let

denote the quantum graph modelling
\psi
.
Let

denote the
k
-labelled quantum graph obtained from

by applying the following operations to each of its constituents
\boldsymbol{F}
. Let

denote the regular
k
-pebble forest cover of depth
d
of
\boldsymbol{F}
.

1.

Drop the

-th label from the vertex

Since

is regular,

2.

Introduce a fresh vertex

carrying label

.

3.

Update

to

by inserting

at the original position of

succeeds all labelled vertices and precedes all other unlabelled vertices.

4.

by

for all

and

.

It has to be verified that the resulting pebble forest covered labelled graph satisfies all axioms. Critical is only the validity of the pebbling function.
Let

be such that

or

Distinguish cases:

If

and

then

(

)

On

It remains to define a representation which also incorporates information about the structure of the labels, i.e. their coalgebras.

Definition C.8

Let

augmented homomorphism representation
associates with every element of

\bot

Note that we do not succeed to parallel the notation of

.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}=\left((L,\lambda)\to(A,\alpha)\leftarrow(K,\kappa)\right)

\boldsymbol{B}=\left((K,\kappa)\to(B,\beta)\leftarrow(M,\mu)\right)
.

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}})_{G}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{G}\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}_{G}

With series composition and reversal,

Lemma C.13
.

For every graph
G
and every

,

\hom_{\mathsf{Graph}}(\soe\boldsymbol{A},G)=\soe\boldsymbol{A}_{G}=\soe\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{G}.

,

C.4
Finite Generation

For the case of the pebbling comonad, finite generation was proven in
Proposition

4.7
after devising a suitable set of generators. Proving finite generation for the involution semigroup of bilabelled objects over arbitrary comonads on
\mathsf{Graph}
, remains a problem for further investigations.

Definition C.14
.

A subset
\Gamma\subseteq\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
is a
set of generators
for
\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
if for every
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
there exist finitely many
\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{r}\in\Gamma
such that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{1}\cdot\dots\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{r}
.

C.5
Matrix Equations

It remains to deduce matrix equations characterising homomorphism indistinguishability over the set of graphs underlying
\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
.
Note that finite generation is not among the assumptions of the following theorem. It is only needed to obtain a finite system of matrix equations.

Theorem C.15
.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
be a comonad on
\mathsf{Graph}
. Let
L\in\operatorname{im}U^{\mathfrak{C}}
be a graph such that
\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
admits a set of generators
\Gamma
, which is closed under reversal. Let
G
and
H
be graphs. Then the following are equivalent:

1.

G
and
H
are homomorphism indistinguishable over the graphs
\soe\boldsymbol{A}
for
\boldsymbol{A}\in\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
.

2.

There exists a pseudo-stochastic matrix
X
such that
X\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{G}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{H}X
for all
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

The backward direction is easy to see, cf. proof of
Theorem

1.5
.
The forward direction follows from
Theorem

2.4
observing that
\soe\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}_{G}=\soe\boldsymbol{A}_{G}
for all
\boldsymbol{A}
and
G
.
∎

C.6
The Strategy

We conclude by summarising the comonadic strategy to derive homomorphism indistinguishability characterisations in terms of matrix equations.
This approach is exemplified with less notational overhead in
Section

4
for the case of the pebbling comonad
\mathfrak{P}_{k,d}
.

1.

Define a family of labelled graphs and their homomorphism matrices

Fix a graph
L
and define
\Lambda(\mathfrak{C},L)
as in
Section

C.2
as the family of bilabelled graphs of interest. They are of the form
(L,\lambda)\to(A,\alpha)\leftarrow(L,\lambda^{\prime})
where
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
are coalgebras of
L
and
A
is a graph of interest. The representation in terms of matrices is defined in
Definition

C.8
. It encodes the homomorphism counts and information about the labelling, i.e. the coalgebras
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
.

For the pebbling comonad,
Section

4.1
features the set
\mathcal{WL}_{k}^{d}
. It comprises tuples
(\boldsymbol{F},p_{\textup{in}},p_{\textup{out}})
where
\boldsymbol{F}
is a bilabelled graph possessing a certain pebble forest cover. This resembles the above definition. The functions
p_{\textup{in}}
and
p_{\textup{out}}
play the role of
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
. The object
L
is the graph with
d
isolated vertices placed on a path in its forest cover.

2.

Define suitable combinatorial and algebraic operations

Series composition and reversal are the relevant combinatorial operations, cf.
Section

C.3
. They correspond under the augmented homomorphism representation to matrix product and transposition.
In the coalgebraic case, closure under these operations follows essentially from basic properties of the Eilenberg–Moore category
\EM(\mathfrak{C})
. Remembering the coalgebras
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
also on the algebraic side ensures that no homomorphism matrices of graphs outside the class are produced.

For the pebbling comonad, the relevant lemmata were established in
Section

4.2
.

3.

Prove finite generation

Finite generation as defined in
Section

C.4
is necessary to obtain a finite system of matrix equations in the subsequent step. This has been proven in
Section

4.3
for the pebbling comonad. Proving finite generation for arbitrary comonads on
\mathsf{Graph}
remains an open problem.

4.

Recover a matrix equation using algebraic and representation-theoretic techniques.

The desired matrix equations can be obtained using the representation-theoretic framework of
[
33
]
, cf.
Theorem

2.4
. This resulted in
Theorem

C.15
in the general case and
Theorem

1.5
in the case of the pebbling comonad.

C.7
Outlook

We propose to apply this strategy to other comonads and hence to other graph classes in the future.
In
[
3
]
, it was recently shown that for every graph class
\mathcal{F}
which is closed under isomorphism, finite coproducts, and summands, cf. ibidem, there exists a comonad
\mathfrak{C}
such that
F\in\mathcal{F}
if and only if
F
admits a
\mathfrak{C}
-coalgebra.
Thus, it may be argued that all graph classes of interest can be characterised using a comonad.
However, the comonads constructed in
[
3
]
are only suitable for counting homomorphisms and fail to represent the graph decompositions, which were a conceptual ingredient for proving finite generation for
Theorem

1.5
.

For the future, we propose to eradicate issues with finiteness in
Theorem

C.15
to derive a general theorem characterising homomorphism indistinguishability over graph classes characterised by a comonad in terms of matrix equations.
