Conjugate Bayesian analysis of compound-symmetric Gaussian models
Zachary M. Pisano

Abstract
We discuss Bayesian inference for a known-mean Gaussian population with a compound symmetric variance-covariance matrix. Since the space of such matrices is a linear subspace of that of positive definite matrices, we utilize the methods of [ 28 ] to decompose the usual Wishart conjugate prior and derive a closed-form, bivariate conjugate prior distribution for the distinct entries of the compound-symmetric half-precision matrix. One samples from this density by transforming independent gamma random variables into the cone of possible entries of such matrices. We also demonstrate how the prior may be utilized to naturally test for the positivity of a common within-class correlation in a random-intercept model using two data-driven examples.

Introduction

In parametric Bayesian inference conjugate prior distributions are comprised of equal parts practical utility and mathematical beauty: the former, in so far as such distributions’ structure may greatly mitigate issues related to posterior computation and model selection; the latter, in so far as derivations demonstrating the conjugacy of individual priors may be satisfyingly and succinctly described via a simple rule by which the likelihood updates the hyperparameters
[
29
]
. Conjugate priors are guaranteed when the likelihood belongs to a full-rank exponential family in canonical form
[
10
]
, but a few non-exponential family examples (e.g., the Uniform-Pareto model,
[
11
]
, Sec. 2.5) have been catalogued as well. Due to the relatively limited number of likelihood settings which permit conjugate priors, Bayesian practitioners often implement priors which necessitate the use of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to approximate corresponding posteriors. While a few conjugate prior settings are ubiquitous and well-studied — e.g., the Bayesian Gaussian model is the subject of an entire chapter in
[
14
]
— unless a likelihood possesses an obvious conjugate prior, MCMC methods are the norm, not the exception.

In this article we illustrate how defaulting to inherently computational methods — thereby bypassing mathematical and analytical methods — may result in a conjugate prior’s being overlooked. In particular, we demonstrate how the distribution of a simple linear transformation of two independent gamma variables is conjugate for the unique entries a Gaussian model’s precision matrix when this matrix is assumed to be compound symmetric. Throughout the article we assume

\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}_{d}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\mu},\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}\bigg),\ \ i=1,\dots,n

2.1
A Conjugate Prior for Compound-Symmetric Precision

We observe that

2.2
A Conjugate Prior for Compound-Symmetric Variance-Covariance

Typically, the parameter of interest for Gaussian settings is the variance-covariance matrix

24

The prior constructed in Theorem

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{1}+(d-2)\eta_{2}}{2(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})(\eta_{1}+(d-1)\eta_{2})}

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta_{2}}{2(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})(\eta_{1}+(d-1)\eta_{2})}.

In applying this change-of-variables to the conjugate prior for

It turns out that we can derive a prior for

\displaystyle L(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=(2\pi)^{-\frac{nd}{2}}

\displaystyle\theta_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\theta_{1}+\frac{n(d-1)}{2}

\displaystyle\theta_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto\theta_{2}+\frac{n}{2}

\displaystyle\theta_{3}

\displaystyle\mapsto\theta_{3}+\frac{\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})-\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}}{d}}{2}

In the previous section we derived the conjugate prior for the half-precision of a compound symmetric Gaussian model using an appropriate transformation of independent gamma random variables. This is to say nothing as to how we determined which transformation, or which random variables to transform, or even whether a conjugate prior could be derived at all. Due to the partial fraction decomposition needed to derive the functional form (

in the previous subsection. In determining the normalization constant we shall see that we only care about the trace and sum of the off-diagonals of
B
as far as hyperparameterization may be concerned.

Using Lemma
B.4
and the properties of the matrix trace, we observe that (
7
) may be rewritten as

(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})^{\frac{m(d-1)}{2}}(\eta_{1}+(d-1)\eta_{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}\exp\{-\eta_{1}\beta_{1}-\eta_{2}\beta_{2}\}

where
\beta_{1}:=\text{tr}(\textbf{B})
and
\beta_{2}:=\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}-\text{tr}(\textbf{B})
; Lemma
B.1
implies
-\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<(d-1)\beta_{1}
and, subsequently,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<(d-1)\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}.

Theorem
2.1
next tells us that the exact functional form of the prior is

\rho(\boldsymbol{\eta})=Z_{d}(m,\beta_{1},\beta_{2})(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})^{\frac{m(d-1)}{2}}(\eta_{1}+(d-1)\eta_{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}\exp\{-\beta_{1}\eta_{1}-\beta_{2}\eta_{2}\}

with the normalization constant

Z_{d}(m,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}):=\frac{(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})^{\frac{m+2}{2}}((d-1)\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})^{\frac{m(d-1)+2}{2}}}{d^{\frac{md+2}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m(d-1)+2}{2})\Gamma(\frac{m+2}{2})}.

(8)

We can sample from
\rho(\boldsymbol{\eta})
by first sampling the independent variates
Y_{1}\sim\Gamma(\frac{m+2}{2},\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})
and
Y_{2}\sim\Gamma(\frac{m(d-1)+2}{2},\beta_{1}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{d-1})
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{>0}
and transforming them into
\mathcal{C}_{d}
via
\textbf{C}_{d}
.

The hyerparameter updates are

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\beta_{1}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto\beta_{2}+\big(\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}-\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})\big)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\mapsto m+n.

We see that
\beta_{1}
conveys information about the diagonal entries of prior data’s variance-covariance matrix, and
\beta_{2}
may be interpreted in terms of the off-diagonal entries; both of these values are based on
m
observations in our sample.

3.3
Marginal and Conditional Priors for the Entries of the Half-Precision

Theorem
2.1
indeed gives us the constant (
8
), but we still have not addressed how we determined the relevance of the transformation
\textbf{C}_{d}
and the gamma variates to be transformed. We outline our reasoning here, choosing to relegate the finer details to Appendix
C
. If we did not have access to Theorem
2.1
, one means of determining the normalizing constant of (
7
) would be to directly compute

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})^{\frac{m(d-1)}{2}}(\eta_{1}+(d-1)\eta_{2})^{\frac{m}{2}}\exp\{-\eta_{1}\beta_{1}-\eta_{2}\beta_{2}\}\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}.

Alternatively, we might instead attempt to write the non-normalized prior as the product of a non-normalized marginal prior for
\eta_{1}
and a non-normalized conditional prior for
\eta_{2}\ |\ \eta_{1}
, either of which might be more easily identifiable.

The latter approach first reveals the conditional prior of
\eta_{2}\ |\ \eta_{1}
as a Kummer-Beta distribution
[
27
,
26
]
shifted and scaled to a support of
(-\frac{\eta_{1}}{d-1},\eta_{1})
. Next, the marginal prior for
\eta_{1}
is revealed to be a convolved-gamma distribution
[
31
]
, i.e., that of the sum of independent
Y_{1}\sim\Gamma(\frac{m+2}{2},\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})
and
Y_{2}\sim\Gamma(\frac{m(d-1)+2}{2},\beta_{1}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{d-1})
, which should be unsurprising in light of Theorem
2.1
. With the exact form of the conjugate prior for
\boldsymbol{\eta}
now in hand one can show that this quantity is exactly equal in distribution to
\textbf{C}_{d}\boldsymbol{Y}
(Theorem
C.2
).

This effectively concludes our analysis when the model’s mean is known. In the next section we extend our conjugate prior framework to include models whose mean is unknown.

4
Conjugate Bayesian Analysis in the Unknown Mean Case

So far we have considered the setting (
1
) in which the true population mean is known. Suppose instead that

\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}_{d}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\mu},\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}\bigg),\ i=1,\dots,n

(9)

in which
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
is the unknown population mean and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
once again denotes the unknown
d\times d
positive definite precision matrix. Without any further constraints on either of these parameters, the Normal-Wishart distribution (
[
14
]
, Chapter 7) serves as a natural conjugate prior, with hierarchical structure

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\sim W_{d}(\alpha,\textbf{B})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nu},\frac{1}{2\lambda}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}\bigg)

where
\alpha>\frac{d+1}{2}
,
\textbf{B}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d\times d}
is positive definite,
\lambda>0
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. As in Section
3
, the first two hyperparameters convey prior information about
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
; meanwhile
\boldsymbol{\nu}
functions as the portion of
\boldsymbol{\tau}
corresponding to a prior estimate of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
from
\lambda
observations. The conjugate prior for (
9
) when
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
is assumed to be conic compound-symmetric (i.e., supported on
\mathcal{C}_{d}
) is the subject of the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},m_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}>0,\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d},\beta_{1}>0,
and
\beta_{2}\in(-\beta_{1},(d-1)\beta_{1})
. Define independent gamma variates

\displaystyle Y_{1}

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma\bigg(\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}+2}{2},\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle Y_{2}

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma\bigg(\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}(d-1)+2}{2},\beta_{1}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{d-1}\bigg)

and the linear transformation matrix
\textbf{C}_{d}
as in (
3
).

The hierarchical density of

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle=\textbf{C}_{d}\boldsymbol{Y}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}=(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})\textbf{I}_{d}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nu},\frac{1}{2m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{-1}\bigg)

forms a conjugate prior for (
9
) with CS precision, with posterior hyperparameters defined by

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}

\displaystyle\mapsto m_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}+n

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\mapsto m_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}}+n

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\boldsymbol{\nu}+n\boldsymbol{\overline{x}}}{m_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}+n}

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\beta_{1}+\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})+\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}n}{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\overline{x}_{j}-\nu_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto\beta_{2}+\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}-\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})+\bigg(\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}n}{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+n}\bigg)\sum_{j\neq k}(\overline{x}_{j}-\nu_{j})(\overline{x}_{k}-\nu_{k}),

where
\boldsymbol{\overline{x}}=[\overline{x}_{1}\cdots\overline{x}_{d}]^{\top}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}{n}
and
\boldsymbol{s}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\overline{x}})(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\overline{x}})^{\top}
.

For a given collection of hyperparameters
m_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
we can also define a conjugate prior for
(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, and thereby extend Theorem
2.2
to the unknown mean case as well.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
m_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}>0,
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{d})^{\top}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
. Define independent inverse-gamma variates

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma^{-1}\bigg(\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+2}{2},\lambda_{1}\bigg)

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma^{-1}\bigg(\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(d-1)+2}{2},\lambda_{2}\bigg)

and
\textbf{C}_{d}
as above.

The hierarchical density of

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\textbf{C}_{d}\boldsymbol{Z}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}=(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\textbf{I}_{d}+\sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nu},\frac{1}{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\bigg)

forms a conjugate prior for (
9
) parameterized with a compound symmetric variance-covariance matrix, with posterior hyperparameters defined by

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle\mapsto m_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}+n

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda_{1}+\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}}{2d^{2}}+\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}n}{2d^{2}(m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+n)}\big(\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\overline{x}}-\boldsymbol{\nu})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\mapsto\lambda_{2}+\frac{(d-1)(d\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{s}_{n})-\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}_{n}\boldsymbol{1})}{2d^{2}}+\bigg(\frac{(d-1)m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}n}{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+n}\bigg)\frac{d\|\boldsymbol{\overline{x}}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|^{2}-\big(\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\overline{x}}-\boldsymbol{\nu})\big)^{2}}{2d^{2}}

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}

\displaystyle\mapsto m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+n

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\boldsymbol{\nu}+n\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}}{m_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+n}.

The proofs of Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
have been relegated to Appendix
A
. The latter result permits us to straightforwardly conduct Bayesian hypothesis tests as to whether
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
falls in a particular region of
\mathcal{C}_{d}
; we give an example of such a test in the following section.

5
Test for the Positivity of a Common Within-Class Correlation in a Random-Intercept Model

Suppose
D
univariate observations
X_{ij},\ j=1,\dots,J,\ i=1,\dots,d_{j},\ \sum_{j=1}^{J}d_{j}=D
arise from a random-intercept model (e.g.,
[
30
]
, Chapter 5):

\displaystyle X_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mu+\mu_{0j}+\epsilon_{ij}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ij}

\displaystyle\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\mu_{0j}

\displaystyle\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mu})

where the
\mu_{0j}
and
\epsilon_{ij}
are independent of each other. In this two-level model each observation deviates from the global mean
\mu
by a random quantity
\mu_{0j}
particular to the group to which it belongs in addition to an error term
\epsilon_{ij}
particular only to that observation. This model has been frequently implemented in educational research to model the nesting of children in classes or schools (e.g.,
[
24
,
25
]
). One can show, marginally, that the observations within a particular group share a common pairwise correlation
\frac{\sigma_{\mu}}{\sigma_{\mu}+\sigma_{\epsilon}}
. Expressing the model in the above conditional paradigm permits convenient partitioning of the total variation within and between classes.

However, when the primary research focus concerns the structure of within-group dependency and not necessarily how the total variation may be partitioned, one may treat the
\mu_{0j}
as nuisance parameters and instead consider the marginal model

\boldsymbol{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot 1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot J}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu\boldsymbol{1}_{D},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*})

(10)

in which

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{J}\big(\sigma_{\epsilon}\textbf{I}_{d_{j}}+\sigma_{\mu}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}^{\top}\big),

where
\bigoplus
denotes a direct sum. Note that the matrices on the block diagonal of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}
all possess compound symmetric structure. Under the conditional model it must necessarily be the case that
\sigma_{\mu}>0
so that the distribution of the
\mu_{0j}
can be defined, but one can define a marginal model with
\sigma_{\mu}\in(-\frac{\sigma_{\epsilon}}{d_{\text{max}}},0]
. The marginal formulation therefore describes a greater class of models. In this section we are concerned with developing a hypothesis test to determine whether the more restrictive conditional formulation may be permitted; i.e.,

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:\ \sigma_{\mu}\leq 0

\displaystyle\text{versus}\ H_{a}

\displaystyle:\ \sigma_{\mu}>0.

If we conclude
H_{a}
we effectively determine that the data may be suitably modeled by a random-intercept model. Given the observed data vector
\boldsymbol{x}
a naïve Bayesian hypothesis test consists of evaluating the posterior probability of
H_{0}
versus that of
H_{a}
; if
P(H_{0}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})>\frac{1}{2}
, then we conclude
H_{0}
. These hypotheses were considered by
[
24
]
, who used MCMC methods to obtain posterior samples.

In line with our earlier work, the marginal model (
10
) may be re-expressed in terms of its half-precision; if we rewrite
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}
in terms of
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{\epsilon}+\sigma_{\mu}
and
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{\mu}
, we have that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{*})^{-1}
where

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{J}\bigg((\eta_{1j}-\eta_{2j})\textbf{I}_{d_{j}}+\eta_{2j}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}_{d_{j}}\bigg),

where the
\eta_{1j}
and
\eta_{2j}
are given by Lemma
B.5
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\eta_{1j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{1}+(d_{j}-2)\sigma_{2}}{(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{1}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2}))}

\displaystyle\eta_{2j}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\sigma_{2}}{(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{1}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2}))}.

When the
d_{j}
are all equal, it is clear that the
\eta_{1j}
and
\eta_{2j}
are as well, and so the space of such
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}^{*}
forms a two-dimensional linear subspace of the
D\times D
positive definite cone. In this case it is straightforward to sample from the posterior density of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
if we equip
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
with an inverse-Gamma-type conjugate prior.

However, when the
d_{j}
are not all equal the inverse-Wishart-type prior on
\mathcal{C}_{d_{\text{max}}}
is not conjugate for
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. In this case we propose a Gibbs sampling procedure
[
5
]
to sample from the posterior of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
based around the EM algorithm
[
9
]
. The natural complete-data formulation of the marginal model (
10
) consists of extending the vectors
\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}
each to a full length of
d_{\text{max}}
with
d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}
latent
Y
random variates:

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}\\
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\end{bmatrix}\ |\ \mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}_{d_{\text{max}}}(\mu\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}},(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\textbf{I}_{d_{\text{max}}}+\sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}}^{\top}).

Should we observe the latent random variables, the posterior for
(\mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma})\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{y}
may be easily derived.
We also have that the latent variables, conditioned on
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x},\mu
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, are normally distributed, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\ |\ \mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}\sim\mathcal{N}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{*},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}^{*})

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}+\sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}^{\top}\bigg[\frac{(\sigma_{1}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2})\textbf{I}_{d_{j}}-\sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}^{\top}}{(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{1}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2})}\bigg](\boldsymbol{x}_{\cdot j}-\mu\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}})

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\textbf{I}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}+\sigma_{2}\bigg(\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}-2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}(d_{j}-1)}{(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{1}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2})}\bigg)\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}^{\top}.

The full conditional distributions of the
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot,j},\mu,
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
are available to us, hence we propose the following Gibbs sampling procedure:

1.

Initialize
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(0)},\mu=\mu^{(0)},
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}=\boldsymbol{y}_{\cdot j}^{(0)}
. Define
s=0
.

2.

Set
(\mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mapsto(\mu^{(s+1)},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(s+1)})
sampled from their posterior given
(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{(s)})
.

3.

Set
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\mapsto\boldsymbol{y}_{\cdot j}^{(s+1)}
sampled from their normal density given
(\boldsymbol{X},\mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma})=(\boldsymbol{x},\mu^{(s+1)},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{(s+1)})
. Set
s\mapsto s+1
.

4.

Repeat steps 2 and 3 until a desired sample size has been achieved.

We propose two means of initializing the parameters and latent variables. One method is to first generate the parameters from their joint prior, and then the latent variables from their full conditional. Another is to carry out the EM algorithm induced by the complete data-formulation and initialize the parameters at their resulting MAP estimates, and then either initialize the latent random variables at their conditional expectations or at values randomly generated from their full conditionals. The former initialization does not require wrestling with the EM algorithm, but may require longer burn-in depending on the sample from the prior or may not even be possible if we use an uninformative or improper prior; whereas the latter may require less burn-in, but still requires an iterative scheme which must also be properly initialized. However, in computing the MAP estimates we uncover more information about the posterior; hence we recommend the EM algorithm, which we have relegated to Appendix
D
.

Regardless of whether the
d_{j}
are all equal or not, we can generate a sample from the posterior of
(\mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma})
. The desired posterior probability of
H_{0}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
can then be approximated as such:

\displaystyle P(H_{0}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{\sigma_{2}<0}\rho(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ |\ \boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{\sigma}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathbbm{1}(\sigma_{2}^{(s)}<0)}{S}

where
S
is the size of our posterior sample (excluding burn-in, if the
d_{j}
are unequal). Again, if this approximation is greater than
\frac{1}{2}
, we fail to reject
H_{0}
in favor of the alternative that
\sigma_{2}
is non-negative.

6
Discussion

We have described conjugate prior distributions for two characterizations of a Gaussian model with a compound symmetric variance-covariance matrix. The derivation of these priors depended upon the expression of the desired model as a full-rank linear submodel of an exponential family in canonical form, a fact which allowed us to initially propose the existence of these priors before deriving them in terms of the canonical Wishart prior for the Gaussian half-precision. Although the exact densities which comprise the priors are nonstandard, simple changes-of-variables demonstrate that sampling from them is no more complicated than sampling from gamma or inverse-gamma distributions.

We suspect that similar approaches may yield conjugate priors for other linear submodels of the multivariate Gaussian model, e.g., Toeplitz
[
4
]
and block-compound symmetric variance-covariance
[
6
]
. In particular, we suspect that Wishart/inverse-Wishart generalizations of the priors described in Sections
2
-
3
will arise as conjugate priors for the latter model. We plan to demonstrate this in future work.

The hypothesis test in Section
5
provides an example of the priors’ utility beyond the mathematical convenience they exude.
[
24
,
25
,
12
]
considered related tests concerning the ordering and equality of the groups’ respective interclass correlations. Our own conjugate priors are unsuitable for these tests, since the alternative models are not linear submodels of the canonical formulation of the multivariate Gaussian model. If, however, the research question on hand concerns the ordering of interclass co-precisions then the Wishart-type conjugate prior on
\mathcal{C}_{d}
may be extended to yield balanced Bayes factors across all possible orderings when the groups are of equal size.

The conjugate prior for the entries of a compound symmetric matrix can also be used to perform model selection to determine whether the variance-covariance matrix Gaussian data may be assumed to possess either arbitrarily positive definite structure or be restricted to a specific linear subset, e.g., diagonal, constant diagonal, or compound symmetric. Approximate model selection criteria such as BIC
[
32
]
are not necessary for this task; since conjugate priors are available for all of these structures, each model’s evidence may be computed in closed form. Moreover, the linearly nested relationship of these models amongst each other permits one to “match” their hyperparameters so as to mitigate the possibility of prior regularization being to used to arbitrarily select the final model
a priori
(
[
28
]
, Chapter 3; and
[
18
]
). The conjugate prior can also be used to carry out an exact Bayesian hypothesis test for repeated measures ANOVA
[
21
]
; in this case we can simultaneously verify the sphericity assumption and carry out the ANOVA test itself by computing the evidences of the corresponding models.

Such considerations effectively proffer the priors we derived as the default Bayesian regularization for Gaussian models exhibiting compound symmetry. In all of these proposed applications, the usual decision criteria for statistical tests — i.e., posterior probabilities of hypotheses and marginal likelihoods of the data — can be obtained either in analytical form or after minimal Monte Carlo simulation. We contrast the implementation of these conjugate priors with those constructed in previous work, which only permit posterior inference after carrying out computationally intensive MCMC methods.

Proofs of Theorems

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

B
Positive Definite and Compound Symmetric Matrices

It is known that the set of
d\times d
positive semidefinite matrices forms a cone in
\mathbbm{R}^{\frac{d(d+1)}{2}}
, where the dimension is the number of potentially unique entries of such matrices
[
13
]
. If one considers only the interior of this cone (itself a convex cone), we instead have the space of
d\times d
positive definite matrices. In this article we are largely concerned with the two-dimensional subset of the latter cone associated with compound symmetric matrices; we call this the
(
d
-th) compound symmetric cone
and denote its projection onto
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
as

\mathcal{C}_{d}:=\{(x,y)\ :\ x>0,\frac{-x}{d-1}<y<x\}.

One immediately observes that
(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
iff the matrix
(x-y)\textbf{I}_{d}+y\boldsymbol{1}_{d}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}
is compound symmetric. We also consider an open two-dimensional rectangle (which we call the
(
d
-th) compound symmetric rectangle
) isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}_{d}
:

\mathcal{R}_{d}:=\{(x,y)\ :\ x>0,\frac{-1}{d-1}<y<1\}.

We also see that
(x,y)\in\mathcal{R}_{d}
iff
x\big((1-y)\textbf{I}_{d}+y\boldsymbol{1}_{d}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}\big)
is compound symmetric. That
\mathcal{R}_{d}
and
\mathcal{C}_{d}
are isomorphic is obvious, since one can easily define
(x,y)\mapsto(x,\frac{y}{x})
, with inverse
(x,y)\mapsto(x,xy)
, which is a bijection between the two sets.

Key to our derivation of the marginal and conditional priors of
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}\ |\ \eta_{1}
is the following lemma, which bounds the average off-diagonal entry of a positive definite matrix in terms of the average diagonal entry.

Lemma B.1
.

If
\textbf{B}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d\times d}
(
d\geq 2
) is positive definite, then

-\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{B})}{d(d-1)}<\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}-\text{tr}(\textbf{B})}{d(d-1)}<\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{B})}{d}.

Proof.

From the definition of positive definiteness, we have that
0<\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}
. Subtracting
\text{tr}(\textbf{B})
from both sides and dividing through by
d(d-1)
yields the lower bound.

To obtain the upper bound, we use the Spectral Theorem to write
\textbf{B}=\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\textbf{B}}\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}^{\top}
in which the rows of
\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}
are unit eigenvectors of
B
and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\textbf{B}}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d})
is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
B
, subsequently giving us

\displaystyle\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}}{d}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg)^{\top}\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\textbf{B}}\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}^{\top}\bigg(\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}^{\top}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\textbf{B}}\bigg(\textbf{U}_{\textbf{B}}^{\top}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}\bigg(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{\top}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg)^{2}.

Note that
\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}
and the
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
are all unit vectors, hence the squared dot product in each summand is

\text{cos}^{2}(\theta(\boldsymbol{u}_{j},\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}))\leq 1,

(13)

where
\theta(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
is the measure of the angle between vectors
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
, with equality only occurring when
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{\top}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}=\pm 1
. On the unit sphere in
\mathbbm{R}^{d}
this only occurs when
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}=\pm\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}
, and if this is true for two or more of the
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
then those eigenvectors are not linearly independent, which would contradict our first assumption that
B
be positive definite, i.e., necessarily full-rank with linearly independent eigenvectors. Therefore the inequality (
13
) is strict for at least one of the
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}
.

We now have

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\textbf{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle>\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}\bigg(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}^{\top}\frac{\boldsymbol{1}}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}}{d}.

Subtracting
\frac{\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{B})}{d}
from both sides and dividing by
d-1
yields the desired upper bound.
∎

Two immediate consequences follow. The first is that

-\text{tr}(\textbf{B})<\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}-\text{tr}(\textbf{B})<(d-1)\text{tr}(\textbf{B}).

The second is that a compound symmetric matrix may be naturally constructed from any positive definite matrix. When we construct the conjugate prior for the compound-symmetric half-precision on
\mathcal{C}_{d}
in the following section as a linear submodel of the unconstrained (i.e., arbitrarily positive definite) case, this result permits us to begin with any positive definite rate matrix
B
in the original Wishart prior; said matrix need not be compound symmetric itself.

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose
\textbf{B}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d\times d}
(
d\geq 2
) is positive definite, and define
\hat{b}_{1}=\frac{\text{tr}(\textbf{B})}{d}
and
\hat{b}_{2}=\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}-\text{tr}(\textbf{B})}{d(d-1)}
. The matrix

\textbf{\^{B}}:=(\hat{b}_{1}-\hat{b}_{2})\textbf{I}_{d}+\hat{b}_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}

solves the optimization problem

\displaystyle\underset{\textbf{B}^{\prime}}{\text{min}}\

\displaystyle\|\textbf{B}-\textbf{B}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{F}

s.t.

\displaystyle\textbf{B}^{\prime}\ \text{is compound symmetric}

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
denotes the usual Frobenius norm.

Proof.

We consider a relaxed version of the optimization problem and show that its solution is in fact compound symmetric; let us solve instead

\displaystyle\underset{b_{1},b_{2}}{\text{min}}\

\displaystyle\|\textbf{B}-(b_{1}-b_{2})\textbf{I}_{d}-b_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}

s.t.

\displaystyle b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbbm{R}.

Let
\mathcal{O}(b_{1},b_{2})
denote the objective function; from the definition of the Frobenius norm, we have

\mathcal{O}(b_{1},b_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(b_{ii}-b_{1})^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}(b_{ij}-b_{2})^{2},

which is clearly convex in
(b_{1},b_{2})
, with partial derivatives proportional to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{O}(b_{1},b_{2})}{\partial b_{1}}

\displaystyle\propto\text{tr}(\textbf{B})-db_{1}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{O}(b_{1},b_{2})}{\partial b_{2}}

\displaystyle\propto\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}_{d}-\text{tr}(\textbf{B})-d(d-1)b_{2}.

Setting these equal to 0 and solving for
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
gives the
\hat{b}_{1}
and
\hat{b}_{2}
in the statement of the lemma. Lemma
B.1
yields the fact that
(\hat{b}_{1},\hat{b}_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
, which is true iff the above
B̂
is compound symmetric.
∎

Heuristically, the nearest compound-symmetric matrix to a positive definite matrix (in Frobenius norm) is that defined by the latter’s average diagonal entry and average off-diagonal entry.

The following result assists our derivation of the conjugate prior for the parameters of (
1
) when both
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
are unknown.

Lemma B.3
.

If
\textbf{B},\textbf{\^{B}}
are as in Lemma
B.2
and
A
is
d\times d
compound symmetric, then
\text{tr}(\textbf{AB})=\text{tr}(\textbf{A\^{B}})
.

Proof.

Let
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
denote the entries of
A
. Note that
\text{tr}(\textbf{B})=\text{tr}(\textbf{\^{B}})
and
\text{tr}(\textbf{B11}^{\top})=\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\textbf{\^{B}}\boldsymbol{1}=\text{tr}(\textbf{\^{B}11}^{\top})
. We have

\displaystyle\text{tr}(\textbf{AB})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\bigg(\big((a_{1}-a_{2})\textbf{I}+a_{2}\boldsymbol{11}^{\top}\big)\textbf{B}\bigg)

\displaystyle=(a_{1}-a_{2})\text{tr}(\textbf{B})+a_{2}\text{tr}(\textbf{B}\boldsymbol{11}^{\top})

\displaystyle=(a_{1}-a_{2})\text{tr}(\textbf{\^{B}})+a_{2}\text{tr}(\textbf{\^{B}}\boldsymbol{11}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\textbf{A\^{B}})

as desired.
∎

We also invoke the Matrix Determinant Lemma (ex. 1.3.24 of
[
15
]
) to obtain the determinant of a positive definite compound symmetric matrix.

Lemma B.4
.

If
\textbf{B}=(b_{1}-b_{2})\textbf{I}+b_{2}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}
is
d\times d
positive definite compound symmetric, then

|\textbf{B}|=(b_{1}-b_{2})^{d-1}(b_{1}+(d-1)b_{2})

Finally, since we are interested in inference for a compound symmetric variance covariance matrix of a Gaussian model
vis-à-vis
inference for the half precision, we also give the inverse of such matrices, easily computed via the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury formula (sec. 0.7.4 of
[
15
]
) for the inverse of a rank-1 updated matrix.

Lemma B.5
.

If
\textbf{B}=(b_{1}-b_{2})\textbf{I}+b_{2}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}
is
d\times d
compound symmetric, then
\textbf{B}^{-1}=(b_{1}^{\prime}-b_{2}^{\prime})\textbf{I}+b_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}
is compound symmetric as well, where

\displaystyle b_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{1}+(d-2)b_{2}}{(b_{1}-b_{2})(b_{1}+(d-1)b_{2})}

\displaystyle b_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{b_{2}}{(b_{1}-b_{2})(b_{1}+(d-1)b_{2})}.

One observes that if the off-diagonal entry of compound symmetric
B
is positive (negative), the off-diagonal of
\textbf{B}^{-1}
is negative (positive).

C.1
Notation and Terminology

We frequently refer to the usual gamma function
\Gamma(\alpha):=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-x\}dx
and beta function
\text{B}(\alpha,\beta):=\int_{0}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}dx=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}
for
\alpha,\beta>0
. Our analyses require knowledge of Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric function
[
19
]
, denoted by

{}_{1}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\lambda):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{(\beta)_{n}}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}

for
\alpha,\beta>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbbm{R}
, as well as Gauss’ generalized hypergeometric function

{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\lambda):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}(\gamma)_{n}}{(\beta)_{n}}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}

for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
and
|\lambda|<1
. Here,
(\alpha)_{n}
is the rising factorial or Pochhammer symbol, known to be equivalent to

(\alpha)_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

While much has been written about both
{}_{1}F_{1}
and
{}_{2}F_{1}
, we only require knowledge of a few of their properties, in particular that

{}_{1}F_{1}(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,\lambda)=\exp\{\lambda\}{}_{1}F_{1}(\beta,\alpha+\beta,-\lambda),

(14)

a result known as Kummer’s transformation (eq. 13.1.27 in
[
1
]
), and that

{}_{2}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\beta,\lambda)=\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}}

(15)

(eq. 15.1.8 in
[
1
]
).

C.2
Kummer-Beta Distribution

The Kummer-beta distribution
[
27
,
26
]
generalizes the usual beta distribution to include an exponential term in the random variable which offers greater weight to one end of the unit interval to permit greater probability thereto
[
7
]
. For
\alpha,\beta>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbbm{R}
one writes
X\sim KB(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)
to indicate that the random variable
X
has such a distribution, with density function

f_{X}(x;\alpha,\beta,\lambda)=\begin{cases}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}\exp\{-\lambda x\}}{Z(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)},\ \ x\in(0,1)\\
0,\ \ \text{otherwise}\end{cases},

where the normalization constant is

Z(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)=\text{B}(\alpha,\beta){}_{1}F_{1}(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,-\lambda).

(16)

Note that when
\lambda=0
then
X\sim\text{B}(\alpha,\beta)
as a special case. We generalize this density by considering, for
a>0
and
b\in\mathbbm{R}
, the random variable
Y=aX+b
, which we call a shifted/scaled Kummer-beta random variable (denoted as
Y\sim KB_{a,b}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
). The density function of
Y
, which can be easily gleaned from the usual change of variables, is the subject of the following lemma.

Lemma C.1
.

If
X\sim KB(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)
,
a>0
, and
b\in\mathbbm{R}
, then the density of
Y=aX+b
is

f_{Y}(y;\alpha,\beta,\lambda,a,b)=\begin{cases}\frac{\exp\{\lambda\frac{b}{a}\}}{a^{\alpha+\beta-1}Z(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)}(y-b)^{\alpha-1}(a+b-y)^{\beta-1}\exp\{-\frac{\lambda}{a}y\},\ \ y\in(b,a+b)\\
0,\ \ \text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Proof:

The linear relationship may be rearranged as
X=\frac{Y-b}{a}
, with Jacobian
\frac{dX}{dY}=\frac{1}{a}
. Moreover it’s clear the said relationship is a bijection from
(0,1)
to
(b,a+b)
, hence
Y
has strictly positive support on the latter interval. Using change-of-variables we have

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y;\alpha,\beta,\lambda,a,b)

\displaystyle=f_{X}\bigg(\frac{y-b}{a};\alpha,\beta,\lambda\bigg)\frac{1}{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{aZ(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)}\bigg(\frac{y-b}{a}\bigg)^{\alpha-1}\bigg(1-\frac{y-b}{a}\bigg)^{\beta-1}\exp\bigg\{-\lambda\bigg(\frac{y-b}{a}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\{\lambda\frac{b}{a}\}}{a^{\alpha+\beta-1}Z(\alpha,\beta,\lambda)}(y-b)^{\alpha-1}(a+b-y)^{\beta-1}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\lambda}{a}y\bigg\}

on
(b,a+b)
, and 0 otherwise.∎

That
a
be strictly positive is hardly necessary to arrive at such a generalization. Indeed, we enforce such a restriction to avoid absolute values in the final density. If one is interested in generalizing for
a<0
, first note that Kummer’s transformation (
14
) gives
X^{\prime}:=1-X\sim KB(\beta,\alpha,-\gamma)
; when
a
is negative we can see that
Y
is a transformation of
X^{\prime}
as described above.

The marginal prior we derive for the diagonal entry of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{H}}
takes the form of a gamma density, albeit one generalized with Kummer’s hypergeometric function. One writes
X\sim C\Gamma(\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta)

X

\alpha,\beta,\lambda>0
and
-\infty<\delta<\beta

f_{X}(x;\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta)=\begin{cases}\frac{\beta^{\alpha}(\beta-\delta)^{\lambda}}{\Gamma(\alpha+\lambda)}x^{\alpha+\lambda-1}\exp\{-\beta x\}{}_{1}F_{1}(\lambda,\alpha+\lambda,\delta x),&\text{ if }x>0\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

[
31
]
obtained this density for the sum of independent
\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)
and
\Gamma(\lambda,\beta-\delta)
random variables, and has since been used to model the time-activity of a thyroidal imaging agent

D
EM Algorithm for the Marginal Formulation of the Random Intercept Model

We suggested above that one may potentially initialize the Gibbs sampler for the test of positivity of a common within-class correlation at the terminal values of an expecation-maximization (EM) algorithm. While such methods for the conditional formulation of the random-intercept model have been thoroughly discussed in the literature (e.g,
[
17
,
16
,
23
]
), we could not find any for the marginal formulation (
10
). For the sake of thoroughness we detail here how such a method may be derived.

Suppose we are in the marginal characterization of the random-intercept model, i.e., with vectors
\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j},\ j=1,\dots,J
of varying lengths
d_{j}
(with sum
D
and maximum length
d_{\text{max}}
) arranged into

\boldsymbol{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot 1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot J}\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu\boldsymbol{1}_{D},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*})

in which

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{J}\big((\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\textbf{I}_{d_{j}}+\sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{j}}^{\top}\big)

with
\mu\in\mathbbm{R}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}_{d_{\text{max}}}
. We shall take a frequentist point of view and assume that no prior has been placed on the parameters.

Any EM algorithm begins with a complete-data extension of the model which, when completely observed, would render usual maximum likelihood estimation straightforward. Following initialization of the parameter estimate, the algorithm alternates between an E-step, which computes the expectation under the current parameter of the complete-data log-likelihood conditional on the observed data, and an M-step, in which the parameters are optimized by maximizing the resulting expression. For the particular case in which the complete-data model is a full-rank exponential family, the E-step only involves computing conditional expectations of the complete-data sufficient statistics; and the M-step performs maximum likelihood estimation supposing the said conditional expectation had been observed (
[
9
]
, Section 2).

Since maximum likelihood estimation of
(\mu,\boldsymbol{\sigma})
can be performed when the group sizes are all equal, we propose extending each group observation in the marginal model with additional
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}
of length
d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}\\
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\end{bmatrix}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),\ j=1,\dots,J

(19)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\textbf{I}_{d_{\text{max}}}+\sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}}^{\top}
. This is a fully rank-3 exponential family, with sufficient statistics

\displaystyle T_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d_{j}}X_{ij}+\sum_{i=d_{j}+1}^{d_{\text{max}}}Y_{ij}\bigg)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}^{\top}\\
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}^{\top}&\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=(\mu^{*},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*})
be the most recent iterate of the parameters; since each
(\boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j})
is jointly normally distributed under
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
, conditional on
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}
, the
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}
are independent of each other and respectively normally distributed in
\mathbbm{R}^{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}
(
[
2
]
, Theorem B.6.5); we eventually obtain

\boldsymbol{Y}_{\cdot j}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\cdot j}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*}_{j}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{*}_{j})

(20)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu^{*}(\sigma_{1}^{*}-\sigma_{2}^{*})+\sigma_{2}^{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d_{j}}x_{ij}}{\sigma_{1}^{*}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2}^{*}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\sigma_{1}^{*}-\sigma_{2}^{*})\textbf{I}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}+\frac{\sigma_{2}^{*}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}_{d_{\text{max}}-d_{j}}}{\sigma_{1}^{*}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2}^{*}}.

To compute

\displaystyle T_{\mu}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}[T_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}]

(21)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}[\boldsymbol{T}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}]

(22)

for the E-step, we find that we must only compute at most three conditional expectations for each group of size smaller than
d_{\text{max}}
, those of
Y_{ij}
,
Y_{ij}^{2}
, and
Y_{ij}Y_{kj}
with

. Each of these terms may be easily gleaned from

and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{j}^{*}
above:

\displaystyle Y_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\mathbbm{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}[Y_{ij}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\cdot j}]=\mu_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle(Y_{j}^{2})^{*}

\displaystyle:=\mathbbm{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}[Y_{ij}^{2}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\cdot j}]=\frac{(\sigma_{1}^{*}-\sigma_{2}^{*})^{2}+(d_{j}\sigma_{1}^{*}+1)\sigma_{2}^{*}}{\sigma_{1}^{*}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2}^{*}}+(\mu_{j}^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle(Y_{j}Y_{j}^{\prime})^{*}

\displaystyle:=\mathbbm{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}[Y_{ij}Y_{kj}\ |\ \boldsymbol{X}_{\cdot j}=\boldsymbol{x}_{\cdot j}]=\frac{\sigma_{2}^{*}}{\sigma_{1}^{*}+(d_{j}-1)\sigma_{2}^{*}}+(\mu_{j}^{*})^{2}.

The M-step only consists of performing maximum likelihood estimation having observed the sufficient statistics

and

\displaystyle=\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}_{d_{\text{max}}}\boldsymbol{T}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*}\boldsymbol{1}_{d_{\text{max}}}-\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{T}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{*})-d_{\text{max}}(d_{\text{max}}-1)J\hat{\mu}^{2}}{d_{\text{max}}(d_{\text{max}}-1)(J-1)}.

We alternate between the E- and M-steps until some convergence criterion has been achieved, e.g., the increases to the log-likelihood fall beneath a specified threshold. We propose a simple initialization for this procedure. Simply take
