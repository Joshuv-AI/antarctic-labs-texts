Algebraic Morse theory via Homological Perturbation Lemma
Jun Chen, Yuming Liu, Guodong Zhou

Abstract
. As a generalization of the classical killing-contractible-complexes lemma, we present algebraic Morse theory via homological perturbation lemma, in a form more general than existing presentations in the literature. Two-sided Anick resolutions due to E. Sköldberg are generalised to algebras given by quivers with relations and a minimality criterion is provided as well. Two applications of algebraic Morse theory are presented. It is shown that the Chinese algebra of rank n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 is homologically smooth and of global dimension n ⁡ ( n + 1 ) 2 \frac{n(n+1)}{2} , and the minimal two-sided projective resolution of a Koszul algebra is constructed.

Introduction

Discrete Morse theory has its origin in the work of K. S. Brown, R. Geoghegan
[
7
]
. In that paper, one encountered a cell complex with one
vertex and infinitely many cells in each positive dimension. The authors, using ad hoc method,
collapsed this cell complex to a quotient complex with only two cells in each
positive dimension. K. S. Brown formalized and
applied the collapsing method scheme to groups with a rewriting system
[
6
]
. Motivated by differential topology, R. Forman
[
17
]
rediscovered and developed this theory as a discrete version of the usual smooth Morse theory, hence the name “discrete Morse theory”.
Since then, this subject has received much attention in combinatorial and computational topology; see
[
18
,
27
,
28
,
37
]
etc.

An algebraic version of discrete Morse theory has been developed by E. Sköldberg
[
39
]
, D. Kozlov
[
26
]
, M. Jöllenbeck and V. Welker
[
25
]
. It has many applications in algebra, such as
combinatorial commutative algebra
[
25
]
, cohomology of Lie algebras
[
40
,
30
,
32
,
33
]
, Hochschild cohomology
[
35
,
31
]
, and operad theory
[
14
]
.

E. Sköldberg
[
41
]
studied algebraic Morse theory from the viewpoint of the Homological Perturbation Lemma
[
16
,
38
,
5
,
23
,
12
]
.
This paper pushes this idea further, generalising and complementing
[
39
,
41
]
as well as
[
26
,
25
]
and presents applications to Chinese algebras and a Koszul algebra.

The layout of this paper is as follows.

The first section contains an introduction to the Homological Perturbation Lemma (HPL) by providing some new proofs and observations which seem to be of independent interest.
In the second section we use HPL to generalise the classical killing-contractible-complexes lemma in homological algebra.

Algebraic Morse theory is presented in the third section and our definition of the key concept “Morse matching” is more general than those in

[

,

,

.

One of the most important advantages of algebraic Morse theory is that it provides a method to construct two-sided Anick resolutions, compared with the original one-sided version in

In the fourth section, using the corresponding Gröbner-Shirshov basis theory

A similar resolution with a different construction of the differentials is provided by S. Chouhy and A. Solotar

The fifth section contains a criterion which gives a sufficient condition when the two-sided Anick resolution is minimal; see Theorem

The sixth section contains an application of algebraic Morse theory to Chinese algebras

In last section, we consider the quadratic algebra

Homological Perturbation Lemma

In this section, we give an introduction to the Homological Perturbation Lemma (HPL).

We first introduce some relevant notions which appear to be well known, although to our knowledge, some of them do not appear in the literature.

Remark 1.3

In a heuristic manner,

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}A&=&(1-\delta h)^{-1}\delta&=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\delta h)^{n}\delta=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta(h\delta)^{n},\\
i_{\infty}&=&i+hAi&=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(h\delta)^{n}i,\\
p_{\infty}&=&p+pAh&=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(\delta h)^{n},\\
h_{\infty}&=&h+hAh&=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}h(\delta h)^{n},\\
b_{\infty}&=&b+pAi&=&b+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p(\delta h)^{n}\delta i=b+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p\delta(h\delta)^{n}i.\end{array}

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right):(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathrm{Ker}(g)\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \ (\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime}):\mathrm{Cok}(f)\rightarrow(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})

are isomorphisms of complexes with inverse maps

\begin{array}[]{cclcl}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)^{-1}&=&(0\ \ \lambda)&:&\mathrm{Ker}(g)\rightarrow(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}),\\
(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})^{-1}&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\overline{\lambda}\end{array}\right)&:&(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathrm{Cok}(f)\end{array}

respectively,
where
\overline{\lambda}=1-\sigma\lambda\eta^{\prime\prime}
is the inverse of
1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime}

In fact,

g\circ\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)=\beta\sigma-\beta\sigma\lambda(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)=0,

so

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right):(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\rightarrow Ker(g)

is well-defined.
We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}d\circ\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\eta\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)\\
&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\beta\sigma+\beta(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)\\
\gamma\beta\sigma+\eta(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\beta\eta\sigma+\beta(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)\\
-\eta^{2}\sigma+\eta(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)\end{array}\right)\\
&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma-\eta\sigma)\\
\eta(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma-\eta\sigma)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\eta^{\prime}\\
\eta\sigma\eta^{\prime}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\eta^{\prime}\\
\eta^{\prime}+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\eta^{\prime}\end{array}\right)\\
&=&\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)\eta^{\prime}.\end{array}

Hence it is a chain map.
Now

(0\ \ \lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)=\lambda(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)=1;

for the other equality, observe that an arbitrary element of
\mathrm{Ker}(g)
has the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\lambda(y)\\
y\end{array}\right)
with
y\in C^{\prime}_{*}
and so

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)(0\ \ \lambda)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\lambda(y)\\
y\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\lambda(y)\\
(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma)\lambda(y)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\lambda(y)\\
y\end{array}\right).

We have shown that
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\sigma\\
1+\eta^{\prime\prime}\sigma\end{array}\right)
is an isomorphism of chain complexes.

For the statements about
(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime}):\mathrm{Cok}(f)\rightarrow(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
,
we have

(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-\sigma\lambda\gamma\end{array}\right)=\sigma\gamma-(1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\sigma\lambda\gamma=\sigma\gamma-\sigma\lambda\gamma-\sigma(1-\lambda)\gamma\\
=0.

Hence
(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime}):\mathrm{Cok}(f)\rightarrow(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
is well-defined.

As

(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\eta\end{array}\right)=(\sigma\gamma\alpha+(1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\gamma\ \ \ \sigma\gamma\beta+(1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\eta)=(\eta^{\prime}\sigma\gamma\ \ \eta^{\prime}\sigma\eta)

and

\eta^{\prime}(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})=(\eta^{\prime}\sigma\gamma\ \ \eta^{\prime}+\eta^{\prime}\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime}),

together
with the fact that

\eta^{\prime}\sigma\eta=\eta-\sigma\eta^{\prime}\eta=\eta-\sigma\eta^{\prime}\eta^{\prime\prime}=\eta^{\prime}+(1-\sigma\eta^{\prime})\eta^{\prime\prime}=\eta^{\prime}+\eta^{\prime}\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime},

we see that
(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})
is a chain map.

We have

(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\overline{\lambda}\end{array}\right)=(1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\overline{\lambda}=1

and for
x\in C_{*},y\in C_{*}^{\prime}
,

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\overline{\lambda}\end{array}\right)(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime})\overline{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right)}=\overline{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\overline{\lambda}\sigma\gamma(x)+y\end{array}\right)}=\overline{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right)}

where
\overline{\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right)}\in Cok(f)
.
Then
(\sigma\gamma\ \ 1+\sigma\eta^{\prime\prime}):\mathrm{Cok}(f)\rightarrow(C_{*}^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
is an isomorphism of complexes.

\Box

.

In Theorem

, if

The following corollary (the so-called

) is well-known in homological algebra and we cite it from the textbook

. Note that this result is stated over a commutative ring

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
R
be an associative ring, and let

\cdots\longrightarrow C_{n}\oplus C_{n}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\

3.
Algebraic Morse theory

In this section, we present a version of
algebraic Morse theory which is more general than existing ones in the literature.

Let
R
be a ring, all modules will be (left)
R
-modules.

Let
X_{*}
be the following complex of
R
-modules:

\cdots\to X_{n+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{n+1}}}{{\to}}X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d_{n}}}{{\to}}X_{n-1}\to\cdots

Suppose that for each
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there exists a decomposition into direct sums of submodules

X_{n}=\oplus_{i\in I_{n}}X_{n,i}.

So
d_{n}:X_{n}\to X_{n-1}
has a matrix presentation
d_{n}=(d_{n,ji})
with
i\in I_{n},j\in I_{n-1}
and
where
d_{n,ji}:X_{n,i}\to X_{n-1,j}
is a homomorphism of modules.

We shall construct a weighted quiver
Q=Q_{X_{*}}
as follows:

(Q1)

The vertices are the pairs
(n,i)
with
n\in{\mathbb{Z}},i\in I_{n}
;

(Q2)

if a map
d_{n,ji}
with
i\in I_{n},j\in I_{n-1}
does not vanish, then draw an arrow from
(n,i)
to
(n-1,j)
;

(Q3)

for an arrow in (Q2), its weight is just the map
d_{n,ji}
.

A
partial matching
is a full subquiver
{\mathcal{M}}
of
Q
such that

(M1)

each vertex in
Q
belongs to at most one arrow of
{\mathcal{M}}
;

(M2)

each arrow in
{\mathcal{M}}
has its weight invertible as a homomorphism of modules.

Given a partial matching
{\mathcal{M}}
, we can construct a new weighted quiver
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
with additional dotted arrows as follows:

(QM1)

Keep everything for all arrows which are not in
{\mathcal{M}}
(they will be called
thick arrows
);

(QM2)

For an arrow in
{\mathcal{M}}
, replace it by a new
dotted arrow
in the reverse direction and the weight of this new arrow is the negative inverse of the weight of the original arrow.

Note that both
Q
and
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
have no multiple arrows.

We need to fix some notations:
For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, denote
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}=\{(n,i):i\in I_{n}\}
. Call a vertex of
Q
a
critical vertex
(with respect to
{\mathcal{M}}
), if it is not incident to any arrow in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Denote by
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
the set of critical vertices in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
. Denote by
{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
the set of vertices which appear as the starting vertex of an arrow in
{\mathcal{M}}
, by
{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
the set of vertices which appear as the ending vertex of an arrow in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Clearly, we have
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}={\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}\bigcup{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\bigcup{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
(disjoint union).
For a path
p
in
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
, we can define a composition which is the composition of all maps appearing as the weights of all arrows in
p
, denote this composition as
\varphi_{p}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

A path in
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
is called zigzag if dotted arrows and thick arrows appear alternately. For any two vertices
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
and
(m,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{m}
, denote by
{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(m,j))
the set of all zigzag paths from
(n,i)
to
(m,j)
in
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
; notice that in this case, we have necessarily
m=n,n-1
or
n+1
.
For any two vertices
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
and
(n,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
, denote by
{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{1}((n,i),(n,j))
the set of all zigzag paths from
(n,i)
to
(n,j)
in
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
which begin with a dotted arrow (and which necessarily end with a thick arrow). Similarly, we denote by
{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{2}((n,i),(n,j))
the set of all zigzag paths from
(n,i)
to
(n,j)
in
Q^{\mathcal{M}}
which begin with a thick arrow (and which necessarily end with a dotted arrow).

We impose a
local finiteness hypothesis
(LFH) which is more general than conditions imposed previously; for the comparison between this condition with those of
[
26
,
39
,
25
]
, see Remarks
3.5
,
3.6
and
3.7
. A
Morse matching
is a partial matching which satisfies the following local finiteness hypothesis:

(LFH)

Given an arbitrary vertex
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
, for each vertex
(n,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
and
for each element
x\in X_{n,i}
, the sum

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{1}((n,i),(n,j))}\varphi_{p}^{\mathcal{M}}(x)

exists (for instance, it may be a finite sum or it is convergent in a certain norm); moreover,
the number of vertices
(n,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
such that

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{1}((n,i),(n,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x)\neq 0

is finite.

Remark 3.1
.

Since for each element
x\in X_{n,i}
the value
d_{n}(x)
is a finite sum in
X_{n-1}=\oplus_{j\in I_{n-1}}X_{n-1,j}
, it is easy to see that the condition (LFH) will imply the following three conditions:

(LFH1)

Given an arbitrary vertex
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
, for each vertex
(n-1,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n-1}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
for each element
x\in X_{n,i}
, the sum

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(n-1,j))}\varphi_{p}^{\mathcal{M}}(x)

exists; moreover,
the number of vertices
(n-1,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n-1}^{\mathcal{M}}
such that

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(n-1,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x)\neq 0

is finite.

(LFH2)

Given an arbitrary vertex
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
, for each vertex
(n+1,j)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n+1}
and
for each element
x\in X_{n,i}
, the sum

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(n+1,j))}\varphi_{p}^{\mathcal{M}}(x)

exists; moreover,
the number of vertices
(n+1,j)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n+1}
such that

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(n+1,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x)\neq 0

is finite.

(LFH3)

Given an arbitrary vertex
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}
, for each vertex
(n,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
, and
for each element
x\in X_{n,i}
, the sum

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{2}((n,i),(n,j))}\varphi_{p}^{\mathcal{M}}(x)

exists; moreover,
the number of vertices
(n,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
such that

\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{2}((n,i),(n,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x)\neq 0

is finite.

The following proposition is more readily applicable to check Morse matching, which is a reformulation of Sköldberg’s sufficient condition in Remark
3.6
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be a partial matching of
Q
. If any zigzag path from
(n,i)
is of finite length for each vertex
(n,i)
in
Q^{{\mathcal{M}}},
then
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Morse matching.

Let
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
. We first prove that the set of all zigzag paths from
(n,i)
which begin with a dotted arrow is a finite set. Assume that there are infinite such zigzag paths in
Q^{{\mathcal{M}}}
. The condition (M1) of the partial matching
{\mathcal{M}}
guarantees that the first dotted arrows of these zigzag paths all coincide with an arrow of the form
(n,i)\dashrightarrow(n+1,j)
.
Since each term
X_{n}
in the complex
X_{*}
is a direct sum, there are only finitely many thick arrows leaving from
(n+1,j)
. So there exists at least one thick arrow
(n+1,j)\to(n,k)
such that there are infinite zigzag paths which begin with

(n,i)\dashrightarrow(n+1,j)\to(n,k).

Repeating the above process for the zigzag paths from
(n,k)
and by induction, we obtain a zigzag path from
(n,i)
of infinite length, a contradiction. Hence the set
of all zigzag paths from
(n,i)
which begin with a dotted arrow is a finite set. In particular, for each element
x\in X_{n,i}
, the set

\{p\mid p\text{ is a zigzag path which begins with a dotted arrow from (n,i)},\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x)\neq 0\}

is a finite set. Therefore the condition (LFH) follows.

\Box

Given a Morse matching
{\mathcal{M}}
, we can construct a new complex
({X}_{*}^{\mathcal{M}},d^{\mathcal{M}})
as follows:

The complex
{X}_{*}^{\mathcal{M}}
has its
n
-th component
X_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}=\oplus_{(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}X_{n,i}
and the differential

d_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}:X_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}\to X_{n-1}^{\mathcal{M}}
has the matrix presentation

d_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}=(d_{n,ji}^{\mathcal{M}})
with
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}},(n-1,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n-1}^{\mathcal{M}}
and where

d_{n,ji}^{\mathcal{M}}:X_{n,i}\to X_{n-1,j}
is defined to be

d_{n,ji}^{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(n-1,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}.

Note that
d^{\mathcal{M}}
exists by (LFH1).

Now we can state the main result of algebraic Morse theory, which contains as special cases and refines all versions appearing in the literature
[
26
,
40
,
25
]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

(a)

Within the above setup,

({X}_{*}^{\mathcal{M}},d^{\mathcal{M}})
is a complex.

(b)

Define maps

\displaystyle f_{n}:X_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow X_{n}

\displaystyle x\in X_{n,i}

\displaystyle\mapsto f_{n}(x):=x+\sum_{(n,j)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}}\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{2}((n,i),(n,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x),

and

\displaystyle g_{n}:X_{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow X^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}

\displaystyle x\in X_{n,i}

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{n}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{(n,j)\in{\mathcal{V}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{n}}\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{1}((n,i),(n,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x),&(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\\
x,&(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}\\
0&(n,i)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n}\end{array}\right.

Then
f_{*}:{X}_{*}^{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow X_{*}
and
g_{*}:X_{*}\rightarrow{X}_{*}^{\mathcal{M}}
are chain maps which are homotopy equivalent:

gf={\rm Id}_{{X}_{*}^{\mathcal{M}}}
and
fg\sim{\rm Id}_{X_{*}}
via the homotopy

\displaystyle{\theta_{n}}:X_{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow X_{n+1}

\displaystyle x\in X_{n,i}

\displaystyle\mapsto{\theta_{n}}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{(n+1,j)\in{\mathcal{U}}_{n+1}}\sum_{p\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\mathcal{M}}((n,i),(n+1,j))}\varphi^{\mathcal{M}}_{p}(x),&(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\\
0&otherwise\end{array}\right.

(c)

We have a decomposition

(X_{*},d)\cong(X_{*}^{\mathcal{M}},d^{\mathcal{M}})\oplus(Y_{*},d^{Y})

where
(Y_{*},d^{Y})
is a null homotopic complex.

We will use Theorem
2.1
to prove the result.

Construct a chain complex of
R
-modules
({C_{n}\oplus C_{n}^{\prime}},d)
,
d=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\eta\end{array}\right)
,
as follows:

let

{C_{n}}=\oplus_{(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}X_{n,i},{C_{n}^{\prime}}=\oplus_{(n,i)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}\backslash{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}}X_{n,i},

\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\eta
be the maps defined by corresponding arrows in
Q
respectively,
\eta^{\prime}
be the map defined by arrows in
{\mathcal{M}}
,
\eta^{\prime\prime}=\eta-\eta^{\prime}
be the weight of the arrows which are not in
{\mathcal{M}}
;
for an arrow
d_{n,ji}:X_{n,i}\to X_{n-1,j}
lying in
{\mathcal{M}}
, then
{\sigma}=d_{n,ji}^{-1}:X_{n-1,j}\rightarrow X_{n,i}
is the homotopy for the “piece”

, so the weight of the dotted arrow is
-{\sigma}
.
Note that we have always
{\sigma}^{2}=0
.

It’s obvious that
({C_{n}\oplus C_{n}^{\prime}},d)
is just the original chain complex
X_{*}
; the fact that
\eta^{\prime 2}=0
and that
{\sigma}
is contracting homotopy of

follows from the definition of Morse matching; it is interesting to see that powers of

are zigzag paths in

, so for each

with
(n,i)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
,

\lambda(x):=(1+\eta^{\prime\prime}{\sigma})^{-1}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}(\eta^{\prime\prime}(-{\sigma}))^{i}(x)

Note that the existence of

,

, and that the homotopy

{h_{\infty}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\

Remark 3.5
.

In the paper
[

]
, D. N. Kozlov defined a Morse matching to be a partial matching satisfying the following conditions:

(K1)

Each

is finite;

(K2)

for each vertex

, i.e. there is no zigzag path, which begins with a dotted arrow and ends with a thick arrow, from

In fact, D. N. Kozlov also asked that

is commutative ring and that

for

It is obvious that the first condition is unnecessary and the latter one is superfluous by

It is easy to see that whenever the conditions (K1)(K2) hold, the condition in Proposition

On the other hand, the condition of Proposition

, together with the finiteness of each

Remark 3.6
.

In the article
[

4.
Two-sided Anick resolutions

In this section, we generalise the construction of two-sided Anick resolutions of E. Sköldberg in
[
39
]
from one vertex algebras to algebras given by quotients of path algebras of quivers.

Let
k
be a fixed field. Let
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
be a finite quiver with vertex set
Q_{0}
and arrow set
Q_{1}
, where
s:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
(resp.
t:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
) gives the starting vertex (resp. the target vertex) of an arrow.
We will write paths from left to right, that is, the notation
p=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{r}
means that for
1\leq i\leq r-1
,
s(\alpha_{i+1})=t(\alpha_{i})
. The length
l(p)
of the above path
p
is defined to be
r
and the vertices are viewed as paths of length
0
. For
n\geq 0
,
Q_{n}
denotes the set of all paths of length
n
and
Q_{\geq n}
is the set of all paths with length at least
n
. Denote by
kQ
its path algebra, that is the space generated by all paths of finite length and endowed with
the multiplication given by concatenation of paths.

Let us first briefly recall the Gröbner-Shirshov basis theory for a path algebra
kQ
following E. Green’s paper
[
20
]
. Let
\mathcal{B}:=Q_{\geq 0}
denote the set of all finite (directed) paths in
Q
. Then
\mathcal{B}
is a multiplicative
k
-basis of
kQ
. Write
\mathcal{B}_{+}=\mathcal{B}\setminus Q_{0}
. Fix an admissible well-order
\prec
on
\mathcal{B}
, that is, a well-order on
\mathcal{B}
which is compatible with multiplication. For instance, we can take a left length-lexicographic order extending some total order on the arrows. For a linear combination
r
of paths, its tip
\mathrm{Tip}(r)
is by definition the maximal monomial appearing with nonzero coefficients in
r
. For a nonempty subset
X
of
kQ
, put

\mathrm{Tip}(X)=\{\mathrm{Tip}(r)\ |\ r\in X,r\neq 0\}
.

Let
I
be a two-sided ideal in
kQ
contained in
kQ_{\geq 2}
.
Write
{\mathrm{NonTip}}(I)
for the complement set of
{{\mathrm{Tip}}}(I)
in
\mathcal{B}
. Then there exists a decomposition of vector spaces

kQ=I\oplus\text{Span}_{k}({\mathrm{NonTip}}(I)).

So
{\mathrm{NonTip}}(I)
is a basis of the quotient algebra
A=kQ/I
. Recall that a Gröbner-Shirshov basis of
I
with respect to the admissible order
\prec
is a subset
\mathcal{G}\subseteq I
such that
W:={\mathrm{Tip}}(\mathcal{G})
generates the initial ideal
\langle{{\mathrm{Tip}}}(I)\rangle
. Note that in this case
I=\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle
. A Gröbner-Shirshov basis
\mathcal{G}
for the ideal
I
is reduced if the following three conditions are satisfied:

(R1)

For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the coefficient of
{\mathrm{Tip}}(g)
is
1
;

(R2)

For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
g-{\mathrm{Tip}}(g)\in\text{Span}_{k}({\mathrm{NonTip}}(I))
;

(R3)

No element in
W={\mathrm{Tip}}(\mathcal{G})
is a factor of another element in
W
.

It is easy to see that under the given admissible order,
I
has a unique reduced Gröbner-Shirshov basis, and in this case
W
is a minimal generator set of
\langle{{\mathrm{Tip}}}(I)\rangle
; moreover,
b\in\mathcal{B}
lies in
{\mathrm{NonTip}}(I)
if and only if
b
is not divided by any element of
W
. In the following, we always assume that
W={\mathrm{Tip}}(\mathcal{G})
for a reduced Gröbner-Shirshov basis
\mathcal{G}
of
I
.

Similar as in
[
39
]
, we define a new quiver
Q_{W}=(V,E)
(with respect to
W
), called the Ufnarovskiĭ graph
[
42
]
, with vertex set
V
and arrow set
E
as follows:

V=Q_{0}\cup Q_{1}\cup\{u\in\mathcal{B}~|~u\text{ is a proper right factor of some }v\in W\},

and
E
is the union of

\{e\to x\ |\ e\in Q_{0},x=ex\in Q_{1}\}

with

\{u\to v\ |\ uv\in\mathcal{B},uv\in\langle{\mathrm{Tip}}(I)\rangle,w\notin\langle{\mathrm{Tip}}(I)\rangle\text{ for all proper left factors }w\text{ of }uv\}.

Note that the above condition for the arrow
u\to v
is equivalent to the following:
uv\in\mathcal{B}
and
uv
has a unique factor
w\in W
which is a right factor of
uv
. Clearly the above condition implies the following: if
u\to v
is an arrow in
Q_{W}
, then
u\to v_{1}
can not be an arrow in
Q_{W}
for any proper left factor
v_{1}
of
v
. The set of

(also called Anick chains) consists of all sequences

in

such that

is a path in

, where

and

; by convention

. For

is called the degree of

and

is called the weight of

. In order to have an intuition, let us consider a concrete example of the Ufnarovskiĭ graph.

5.
Minimality criterion for two-sided Anick resolutions

In this section we will consider in which case a two-sided Anick resolution is minimal.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
A=kQ/I
be an algebra as in Section
4
,
E=\oplus_{e\in Q_{0}}ke
be its semisimple subalgebra. Then
A
is an augmented
E
-algebra with augmented ideal
A_{+}
.

A projective resolution
(P_{*},d_{*})
of a left
A
-module
M
is minimal if the induced map

1\otimes d_{*}:E\otimes_{A}P_{*}\to E\otimes_{A}P_{*-1}

is zero. A projective resolution
(P^{\prime}_{*},d^{\prime}_{*})
of an
A
-bimodule
M^{\prime}
is minimal if the induced map

1\otimes d^{\prime}_{*}\otimes 1:E\otimes_{A}P^{\prime}_{*}\otimes_{A}E\to E\otimes_{A}P^{\prime}_{*-1}\otimes_{A}E

is zero.

Our definition of minimality is consistent with the usual one in the literature, see, for example
[
3
, Page 325]
.

We keep the notations in Section
4
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
. Define a reduction step from
w
to
w^{\prime}
with coefficient
\lambda\in k^{*}
, denoted by
w\Longrightarrow_{\lambda}w^{\prime}
, if there exist

and

such that

(a)

{\mathrm{Tip}}(f)\in W
;

(b)

w={\mathrm{Tip}}(ufv)=u{\mathrm{Tip}}(f)v;

(c)

-\lambda w^{\prime}=upv
where
p\neq{\mathrm{Tip}}(f)
is a monomial appearing in

.

We say

converges to

if there is a sequence of reduction steps

with

.

Remark 5.3
.

For a thick arrow

(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\overset{d_{n}^{i}}{\to}(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n-1})

with
1\leq i\leq n-1
, we have either
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\Longrightarrow_{\lambda}v=v_{1}\cdots v_{n-1}
or

. Similarly,
for a dotted arrow

\textstyle{(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n-1})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{d_{n}^{-i}}
\textstyle{(u_{1},\cdots,u_{i-1},u_{i}^{\prime},u_{i}^{\prime\prime},u_{i+1},\cdots,u_{n-1})}

with

-chain cannot converge to an

Theorem 5.4

(Minimality criterion)
.

With the notations in Section
4
. For an arbitrary
(n-1)
-chain
(w_{1},\cdots,w_{n})
with
n\geq 1
, if
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{n}
cannot converge to
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n-1}
for any

, then the two-sided Anick resolution
(B(A,A)^{\mathcal{M}},d^{{\mathcal{M}}})
is minimal.

It is clear that the two-sided Anick resolution
(B(A,A)^{\mathcal{M}},d^{{\mathcal{M}}})
is minimal if and only if

{\rm Im}(d_{n}^{{\mathcal{M}}})\subseteq(A^{e})_{+}\otimes_{E^{e}}B(A,A)_{n}^{\mathcal{M}}

So it suffices to prove that each zigzag path from

or

(A^{e})_{+}.

As the weight of
p
is the product of the weight of all arrows appearing in
p.

Hence the weight of
p
lies in
(A^{e})_{+}
.

Remark 6.4

Recall that an algebra is homologically smooth, if it admits a finite length resolution by finitely generated projective bimodules
