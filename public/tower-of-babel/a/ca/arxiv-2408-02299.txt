Abstract

This book studies ultrafilters on connectivity systems, that is, on pairs
(X,f)
where
X
is a finite set and
f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is a symmetric submodular function. Ultrafilters, which play a fundamental role in topology and set theory, are considered here in this broader setting, with particular emphasis on their connections to graph width parameters and to the structural analysis of graph complexity.

We develop several results on ultrafilters on connectivity systems and examine related notions such as prefilters, ultra-prefilters, and filter subbases. We also discuss additional width-, length-, and depth-type parameters that naturally arise in this framework, thereby broadening the perspective from which graph structure may be studied. In addition, the book compares a wide range of graph width parameters and related concepts, with the aim of providing a unified viewpoint and a useful point of departure for further research in graph theory and computational complexity.

More broadly, the book highlights connections with several neighboring areas of mathematics, including set theory, lattice theory, and matroid theory. It also contains survey-style material intended to clarify the current landscape of graph width theory and to stimulate further developments in the subject.

Keywords:
Filter, Ultrafilter, TreeWidth

Introduction

1.1  Filters and Graph Width Parameters

Filters are fundamental objects in set theory and topology.
They are families of sets that are closed under finite intersections and upward closed under inclusion.
Intuitively, a filter selects those subsets that are regarded as “large” or “relevant” in a given context.
An ultrafilter is a maximal filter, and it plays an important role in many areas of mathematics, including topology, set theory, model theory, Boolean algebra, combinatorics, and logic.
Because of their maximality and decisiveness properties, ultrafilters provide a useful framework for studying convergence, compactness, extensions, and various duality phenomena

[
1
,
2
]
.

Graph theory studies combinatorial structures formed by vertices and edges, together with their paths, cuts, decompositions, and connectivity properties
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Within graph theory,
graph width parameters
are numerical invariants designed to measure how far a graph is from a simple tree-like structure.
Such parameters are central in structural graph theory and algorithm design, since many computationally difficult problems become more tractable on graph classes of bounded width.
A large number of width parameters have been introduced and studied in the literature, each capturing a different structural aspect of graphs and related combinatorial objects
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
For broader background, see Appendix A, “Various Width Parameters,” and Appendix C, “Comparing Various Graph Parameters.”

Among the most important graph width parameters are
tree-width
and
branch-width
.
Tree-width is defined via tree decompositions, in which the vertices of a graph are organized into overlapping bags indexed by the nodes of a tree; the tree-width is the size of the largest bag minus one
[
8
,
9
]
.
Branch-width is defined via branch decompositions, which encode how the edge set, or more generally an underlying connectivity structure, is recursively separated
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
These two parameters are closely related.
For every graph
G
with branch-width at least
2
, one has

\operatorname{bw}(G)\leq\operatorname{tw}(G)+1\leq\frac{3}{2}\operatorname{bw}(G),

so bounded tree-width and bounded branch-width are equivalent up to constant factors
[
12
]
.
This close relationship is one reason why connectivity systems and separation-based formalisms are useful in the study of graph width.

Graph width parameters are important for at least the following reasons:

1.

Structural significance.

Width parameters play a central role in structural graph theory, especially in the Graph Minors framework developed by Robertson and Seymour
[
10
,
11
]
.
They provide a systematic way to describe graph complexity from the viewpoint of decomposition theory.

2.

Algorithmic usefulness.

Many problems that are intractable in general admit efficient or fixed-parameter algorithms on graph classes of bounded width
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
Consequently, width parameters are indispensable in parameterized complexity and algorithmic graph theory.

3.

Applicability to real-world networks.

Graphs arising in applications such as software structure, road networks, and organizational or communication systems often exhibit sparse or decomposable structure, which makes width-based methods practically relevant
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
.

A
connectivity system
is a pair
(X,f)
, where
X
is a finite set and
f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is a symmetric submodular function
[
10
]
.
This abstraction provides a common language for studying separations, decompositions, and width parameters beyond ordinary graphs, and it is particularly natural in the theory of branch-width and related min–max results
[
10
,
11
,
6
]
.
In this setting, a duality theorem typically asserts that the absence of a decomposition of small width is equivalent to the existence of a certain obstruction object
[
19
]
.
Within the framework considered in this book, ultrafilters on connectivity systems are studied as obstruction-type objects related to width phenomena, especially branch-width
[

]
.

The main contributions and themes of this book are as follows:

These notions are useful for generating and analyzing filter-like structures, and their behavior in the connectivity-system setting is studied in detail.

•

Chapter 5.

We extend the discussion from finite connectivity systems to infinite and countable settings, and we analyze how ultrafilter-type properties behave in these broader contexts.

•

Chapter 6.

We discuss several additional directions related to width-, length-, and depth-type parameters in the present framework.

[
20
,
21
,

Appendices.

We provide a comparative overview of various graph width parameters and related notions, with the aim of clarifying the landscape of decomposition-based graph invariants and encouraging further research in this direction.

Overall, this book aims to contribute to the study of graph width parameters and graph algorithms by interpreting decomposition-based concepts—such as tree decompositions, branch decompositions, tangles, and related obstruction objects—through the perspectives of several neighboring areas of mathematics.
These include set theory (ultrafilters and prefilters), lattice theory (chains and antichains), model theory (ultraproducts), hypergraph theory (for example, clutters), matroid theory, topology, fuzzy theory, first-order logic, social choice, game theory, and Sperner theory.
By placing these ideas within a common framework, we hope to clarify structural parallels and to suggest further directions for the study of connectivity systems and graph width.

2.1  Symmetric Submodular Functions and Connectivity Systems

We begin with the definition of a symmetric submodular function, a concept that is widely used in the literature

,
24
,
25
]
.
Although symmetric submodular functions are often defined with values in the real numbers, in this book we restrict attention to functions taking values in the natural numbers.
Such a function is also commonly called a

[
10
]
.
Related notions include submodular partition functions
[

,
27
]
, as well as
k
-submodular functions
[

,

,
30
]
, two-dimensional submodular functions
[

]
, monotone submodular functions
[

]
, and maximum submodular functions
[

]
.

Definition 2.1.1
.

Let
X
be a finite set. A function
f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is called
symmetric submodular
if it satisfies the following conditions:

•

For every
A\subseteq X
, one has
f(A)=f(X\setminus A)
.

•

For all
A,B\subseteq X
, one has

f(A)+f(B)\geq f(A\cap B)+f(A\cup B).

In this book, a pair
(X,f)
, where
X
is a finite set and
f
is a symmetric submodular function, is called a
connectivity system
.
This notion is frequently used in the study of graph width parameters, such as branch-width and tree-width, in order to analyze graph structure from the viewpoint of separations and decompositions (see, for example,
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
10
]
).

The following examples illustrate the notion of a symmetric submodular function.

Example 2.1.2
.

Consider a simple undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the vertex set and
E
is the edge set.
Let
V=\{1,2,3,4\}
and

E=\{(1,2),(2,3),(3,4),(4,1)\},

so that
G
is a cycle.
Define a function
f:2^{V}\to\mathbb{N}
by

f(A)=|E(A,V\setminus A)|,

where
E(A,V\setminus A)
denotes the set of edges having one endpoint in
A
and the other in
V\setminus A
.
Then
f
is a symmetric submodular function.

Example 2.1.3
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be random variables, and let

h(I):=H(X_{I})\qquad(I\subseteq[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}),

where
X_{I}=(X_{i})_{i\in I}
and
H(X_{I})
denotes the joint entropy of the subfamily indexed by
I
.

Then
h
is a submodular function on
2^{[n]}
, namely,

h(I)+h(J)\geq h(I\cap J)+h(I\cup J)\qquad(I,J\subseteq[n]).

However,
h
is generally not symmetric, since in general

h(I)\neq h([n]\setminus I).

Hence entropy is a standard example of a submodular function, but not in general of a symmetric submodular one.
Related quantities, such as mutual information, satisfy a symmetry property in an appropriate sense.
Entropy measures uncertainty, or equivalently, the average information produced by a stochastic source
(cf.
[
37
,
38
]
).

Example 2.1.4
.

In network flow theory, the cut-value function
f(S)
of a subset
S\subseteq V
, where
V
is the vertex set, is defined as the total capacity of the edges crossing from
S
to
V\setminus S
.
This provides a standard example of a symmetric submodular function.

Example 2.1.5
.

Let
\Omega=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
be the vertex set of a directed graph.
For each subset
S\subseteq\Omega
, define
f(S)
to be the number of directed edges
e=(u,v)
such that
u\in S
and
v\in\Omega\setminus S
.
In other words,
f(S)
counts the number of edges leaving
S
.

This function is submodular. Indeed, for any
A\subseteq B\subseteq\Omega
and any
x\in\Omega\setminus B
, one has

f(A\cup\{x\})-f(A)\geq f(B\cup\{x\})-f(B).

However,
f
is generally not symmetric, because the number of edges leaving
S
need not equal the number of edges leaving
\Omega\setminus S
.
This asymmetry arises from the orientation of the edges.

More generally, one may assign nonnegative weights to the directed edges.
In that case,
f(S)
becomes the total weight of the edges leaving
S
, which remains submodular but is still not necessarily symmetric.

It is well known that every symmetric submodular function satisfies the following useful properties.

Lemma 2.1.6
.

[
10
]

Let
X
be a finite set, and let
f
be a symmetric submodular function on
X
. Then:

1.

For every
A\subseteq X
,

f(A)\geq f(\emptyset)=f(X)=0.

2.

For all
A,B\subseteq X
,

f(A)+f(B)\geq f(A\setminus B)+f(B\setminus A).

Proof.

1.

By submodularity applied to
A
and
A
, we obtain

f(A)+f(A)\geq f(A\cup A)+f(A\cap A)=f(A)+f(A),

and by symmetry,

f(X)=f(\emptyset).

Standard properties of symmetric submodular functions imply

f(\emptyset)=f(X)=0\quad\text{and}\quad f(A)\geq 0.

2.

By submodularity,

f(A)+f(B)\geq f(A\cup B)+f(A\cap B).

Using symmetry,

f(A\cup B)=f(X\setminus(A\cup B))=f((X\setminus A)\cap(X\setminus B)),

and one obtains the stated inequality in the standard form

f(A)+f(B)\geq f(A\setminus B)+f(B\setminus A).

This completes the proof.
∎

2.2  Ultrafilters in Set Theory

We now turn to ultrafilters on connectivity systems.
Before introducing that notion, we first recall the classical definition of an ultrafilter in set theory.

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
X
be a set. A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
is called a
filter
on
X

(BF1)

If
A,B\in\mathcal{F}

A\cap B\in\mathcal{F}
.

(BF2)

If
A\in\mathcal{F}
and

B\in\mathcal{F}
.

(BF3)

\emptyset\notin\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 2.2.2
.

A

ultrafilter
.
Equivalently, an ultrafilter satisfies the following additional condition:

(BT1)

For every subset
A\subseteq X
, either
A\in\mathcal{F}
or
X\setminus A\in\mathcal{F}
, but not both.

Definition 2.2.3
.

(cf.

A subset

.

Let

•

\mathcal{F}

every superset of such a set again contains a tail interval,

and the empty set does not contain any interval of the form

Example 2.2.8

Definition 2.2.14

(Finite intersection property)
.

(cf.
[
61
]
)
Let
\mathcal{C}
be a family of subsets of
X
.
We say that
\mathcal{C}
has the

(FIP) if for every finite choice
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
, one has

D_{1}\cap\cdots\cap D_{n}\neq\emptyset.

Theorem 2.2.15
.

Let
X
be a finite set, let
\mathcal{F}
be a filter on
X
, and let
A\subseteq X
.
Then
\mathcal{F}\cup\{A\}
has the finite intersection property if and only if
X\setminus A\notin\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Assume first that
\mathcal{F}\cup\{A\}
has the finite intersection property.
Suppose, for contradiction, that
X\setminus A\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then both
A
and
X\setminus A
belong to
\mathcal{F}\cup\{A\}
, but

A\cap(X\setminus A)=\emptyset,

contradicting the finite intersection property.
Hence
X\setminus A\notin\mathcal{F}
.

Conversely, assume that
X\setminus A\notin\mathcal{F}
.
Take any finite family
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}\in\mathcal{F}\cup\{A\}
.
If none of the
D_{i}
is equal to
A
, then all
D_{i}
belong to
\mathcal{F}
, and their intersection is nonempty since every filter has the finite intersection property.

Now suppose that one of the sets is
A
, say
D_{1}=A
, while
D_{2},\dots,D_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Since
\mathcal{F}
is closed under finite intersections,

B:=D_{2}\cap\cdots\cap D_{n}\in\mathcal{F}.

If
A\cap B=\emptyset
, then
B\subseteq X\setminus A
, and by upward closure of
\mathcal{F}
, this would imply
X\setminus A\in\mathcal{F}
, a contradiction.
Therefore
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
, and hence

D_{1}\cap\cdots\cap D_{n}\neq\emptyset.

Thus
\mathcal{F}\cup\{A\}
has the finite intersection property.
∎

Definition 2.2.16

(Uniform ultrafilter)
.

(cf.
[
62
]
)
An ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
on

|A|=|Y|

Theorem 2.2.17
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an ultrafilter on
Y
, and suppose that
Z\in\mathcal{U}
has minimal cardinality among the members of
\mathcal{U}
.
Then the induced ultrafilter on
Z
is uniform.

Proof.

Consider the family

\mathcal{U}\!\upharpoonright Z:=\{A\subseteq Z\mid A\in\mathcal{U}\}.

Since
Z\in\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
Y
, it follows that
\mathcal{U}\!\upharpoonright Z
is an ultrafilter on
Z
.

Now let
A\in\mathcal{U}\!\upharpoonright Z
.
Then
A\subseteq Z
and
A\in\mathcal{U}
.
By the minimality of
|Z|
among the cardinalities of members of
\mathcal{U}
, one must have
|A|\geq|Z|
.
Since
A\subseteq Z
, also
|A|\leq|Z|
.
Therefore
|A|=|Z|
.
Hence every member of
\mathcal{U}\!\upharpoonright Z
has the same cardinality as

, so
\mathcal{U}\!\upharpoonright Z
is uniform.
∎

Theorem 2.2.18

(Ultrafilter on
A
induced by
\mathcal{U}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be an ultrafilter on
X
, and let
A\in\mathcal{U}
.
Define

A\cap\mathcal{U}:=\{A\cap B\mid B\in\mathcal{U}\}.

Then
A\cap\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
A
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{U}
is closed under finite intersections,
A\cap\mathcal{U}
is closed under finite intersections.
Indeed, if
A\cap B_{1}
and
A\cap B_{2}
belong to
A\cap\mathcal{U}
, then

(A\cap B_{1})\cap(A\cap B_{2})=A\cap(B_{1}\cap B_{2})\in A\cap\mathcal{U}.

It is also upward closed inside
A
.
Finally, let
C\subseteq A
.
Since
\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
X
, either
C\in\mathcal{U}
or
X\setminus C\in\mathcal{U}
.
In the first case,

A\cap C=C\in A\cap\mathcal{U}.

In the second case,

A\cap(X\setminus C)=A\setminus C\in A\cap\mathcal{U}.

Thus, for every
C\subseteq A
, either
C\in A\cap\mathcal{U}
or
A\setminus C\in A\cap\mathcal{U}
.
Therefore
A\cap\mathcal{U}
is an ultrafilter on
A
.
∎

2.3  Ultrafilters on Connectivity Systems

We introduce several properties of ultrafilters on a connectivity system

, viewed as an extension of ultrafilters on Boolean algebras.

We begin with ultrafilters on graphs. This notion extends the set-theoretic concept of an ultrafilter to graphs and has a dual relationship with tree-width

Definition 2.3.1
.

[
65
]

Let
G
be a graph. A
G
-ultrafilter of order

is a family
\mathcal{F}
of separations of

(FG0)

Every separation
(A,B)\in\mathcal{F}
has order less than
k
.

(FG1)

For every separation
(A,B)
of
G
of order less than
k

(A,B)\in\mathcal{F}
or
(B,A)\in\mathcal{F}
.

(FG2)

If
(A_{1},B_{1})\in\mathcal{F}
,
A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}
, and
(A_{2},B_{2})
is a separation of
G
of order less than

2.4.1  Relation between Tangles and Ultrafilters

A notion closely related to ultrafilters on a connectivity system is that of a

Tangles are dual obstruction-type objects for decomposition parameters such as tree-decompositions and branch-decompositions, and they play a central role in the theory of graph width parameters
[
89
,
90
]
.
They also appear in several algorithmic contexts, including graph minors, width parameters, and graph isomorphism problems.
For graphs, tangles and ultrafilter-type orientations of separations are closely related
[

]
.

For reference, we first recall the graph-theoretic definition.

Definition 2.4.1
.

[
91
]

A
tangle
in a graph
G
of order
k
is a set
T
of separations of
G
, each of order less than
k
, such that:

(T1)

For every separation
(A,B)
of

of order less than

, exactly one of

and
(B,A)
lies in
T
.

(T2)

If
(A_{1},B_{1}),(A_{2},B_{2}),(A_{3},B_{3})\in T
, then

A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3}\neq G.

(T3)

If
(A,B)\in T
, then
V(A)\neq V(G)
.

Several variants of tangles have also been studied, including directed tangles, hypertangles, connected tangles, distance tangles, edge tangles, matching tangles, and linear tangles
[

,
93
,
94
,
95
,
96
,
97
,

]
.

The notion of a tangle extends naturally from graphs to matroids and connectivity systems
[

]
.
The corresponding definition for connectivity systems is as follows.

Definition 2.4.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
X
be a finite set, and let

be a symmetric submodular function.
A family

is called a
tangle of order
k+1
on the connectivity system
(X,f)
if it satisfies:

(T1)

For every
A\in T
, one has
f(A)\leq k
.

(T2)

For every
A\subseteq X
with
f(A)\leq k
, either
A\in T
or
X\setminus A\in T
.

(T3)

If

A\cup B\cup C\neq X
.

(T4)

For every

, one has
X\setminus\{e\}\notin T
.

It is standard that a tangle of larger order induces tangles of smaller order by restriction.

Proposition 2.4.3
.

Let
T
be a tangle of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
, and let

be an integer with
0\leq j\leq k
.
Define

T^{\leq j}:=\{A\in T\mid f(A)\leq j\}.

Then
T^{\leq j}
is a tangle of order

on
(X,f)
.

Proof.

Condition (T1) is immediate from the definition of
T^{\leq j}
.
For (T2), let
A\subseteq X
satisfy
f(A)\leq j
. Since
j\leq k
, the tangle axiom (T2) for
T
implies that either
A\in T
or
X\setminus A\in T
. Because
f(X\setminus A)=f(A)\leq j
, whichever of these belongs to

also belongs to

.
Condition (T3) is inherited from

, and (T4) is also inherited since

and the restriction does not create new members.
Hence

is a tangle of order

.
∎

The same restriction principle holds for ultrafilters on connectivity systems.

Proposition 2.4.4
.

Let

be an ultrafilter of order

on a connectivity system
(X,f)
, and let

0\leq j\leq k

Define

Then
F^{\leq j}
is an ultrafilter of order

on
(X,f)
.

Proof.

Axiom (Q0) is immediate from the definition of
F^{\leq j}
.
For (Q1), if
A,B\in F^{\leq j}
and
f(A\cap B)\leq j
, then
A,B\in F
, so
A\cap B\in F
by (Q1) for

. Since
f(A\cap B)\leq j
, we have
A\cap B\in F^{\leq j}
.
For (Q2), if
A\in F^{\leq j}
,
A\subseteq B\subseteq X
, and
f(B)\leq j
, then
f(B)\leq k
, so

by (Q2) for

Axiom (Q3) is inherited from

For (Q4), let

with
f(A)\leq j
. Since
f(A)\leq k
, axiom (Q4) for

implies that either
A\in F
or

.

In many settings, tangles and ultrafilters are related by taking complements of the oriented sides.
More precisely, if
T
is a tangle, one may consider the complementary family

F_{T}:=\{X\setminus A\mid A\in T\}.

This family satisfies several filter-like intersection properties.
An ultrafilter on a connectivity system can therefore be viewed as a strengthened, highly coherent co-tangle-type object.

Analogously, linear tangles give rise to single-filter-type axioms, where one of the admissible deletions is restricted to a singleton.
We do not pursue the full abstract equivalence here, but this viewpoint is useful for understanding the role of axioms such as
(QS1)
,
(QSD1)

Definition 2.4.7
.

This is immediate from axiom (Q1).

Definition 2.4.9
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.

(L1)

.

(L2)

.

(L3)

Example 2.4.10
.

Let

\{1\}\in P

Corollary 2.4.11
.

A filter of order

on a connectivity system need not be a
\lambda
-system of order
k+1
.

Definition 2.4.12

(Superfilter on a Connectivity System)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and fix

.
A nonempty family
S\subseteq 2^{X}
is called a
superfilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
if:

(SUF1)

For every

f(A)\leq k
.

(SUF2)

If
A\in S
,
A\subseteq B\subseteq X
, and
f(B)\leq k
, then
B\in S
.

(SUF3)

If

and

, then
A\in S
or
B\in S
.

Theorem 2.4.13
.

Every ultrafilter of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
is a superfilter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

Let
F
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

Condition (SUF1) is exactly axiom (Q0), and (SUF2) is exactly axiom (Q2).
It remains to verify (SUF3).

Assume that
A\cup B\in F
, with
f(A)\leq k
and
f(B)\leq k
.
Suppose, towards a contradiction, that
A\notin F
and
B\notin F
.
By axiom (Q4), this implies

and
X\setminus B\in F
.

Since

(X\setminus A)\cap(X\setminus B)=X\setminus(A\cup B),

and

f\bigl(X\setminus(A\cup B)\bigr)=f(A\cup B)\leq k

by symmetry and (Q0), axiom (Q1) yields

But now both
A\cup B\in F
and
X\setminus(A\cup B)\in F
.
Because

(A\cup B)\cap\bigl(X\setminus(A\cup B)\bigr)=\emptyset

and
f(\emptyset)=0\leq k
, another application of (Q1) would give

Therefore at least one of

or

∎

The language of independence systems is also relevant.

Definition 2.4.17

(SIF2)

Proof.

2.4.7  Ultrafilters and Majority Systems on Connectivity Systems

We next consider majority systems on a connectivity system.
A

Definition 2.4.26

(Majority system)
.

2.5  Branch-Width on Connectivity Systems

In this section, we discuss branch-width for connectivity systems.

2.5.1  Branch-Width

Branch-width is a fundamental width parameter in graph theory.
It is defined via branch decompositions, in which the leaves of a tree correspond
to the edges of the graph.
This notion extends naturally to connectivity systems through symmetric submodular functions

[
10
,
11
,
6
,
155
]
.

We begin with the classical graph-theoretic definition.

Definition 2.5.1
.

[
156
]

Let
G
be a graph.
A
branch decomposition
of
G
is a pair
(T,\tau)
, where

T
is a tree whose vertices all have degree
1
or
3
, and

\tau
is a bijection from the set
L(T)
of leaves of
T
onto the edge set
E(G)
.

For an edge
e\in E(T)
, the deletion of
e
separates
T
into two components.
These components induce a partition
(E_{1},E_{2})
of
E(G)
via the bijection
\tau
.
The
order
of
e
is the number of vertices
v\in V(G)
that are incident with
at least one edge in
E_{1}
and at least one edge in
E_{2}
.

The
width
of
(T,\tau)
is the maximum order of its edges, and
the
branch-width
of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{bw}(G)
,
is the minimum width over all branch decompositions of
G
.

By convention, if
|E(G)|=1
, then
\operatorname{bw}(G)=0
.

Tree-width and branch-width are closely related, but they arise from different kinds
of decompositions.
Tree-width is based on tree decompositions of the vertex set,
whereas branch-width is based on recursive partitions of the edge set.
Thus both parameters measure structural complexity, but from different viewpoints.

The corresponding notion for connectivity systems is as follows.

Definition 2.5.2

(Branch decomposition of a connectivity system)
.

[
11
]

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, where
X
is a finite set and

f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is symmetric and submodular.

A
branch decomposition
of
(X,f)
is a pair
(T,\mu)
, where

T
is a tree all of whose internal vertices have degree
3
,
and
\mu:L(T)\to X
is a bijection from the leaf set
L(T)
of
T
onto
X
.

For each edge
e\in E(T)
, the graph
T-e
has exactly two components,
say
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.
Let

X_{1}(e):=\{\mu(\ell)\mid\ell\in L(T_{1})\},\qquad X_{2}(e):=\{\mu(\ell)\mid\ell\in L(T_{2})\}.

Then
(X_{1}(e),X_{2}(e))
is a partition of
X
, and by symmetry of
f
,

f\bigl(X_{1}(e)\bigr)=f\bigl(X_{2}(e)\bigr).

The
order
of
e
is defined by

\operatorname{ord}_{(T,\mu)}(e):=f\bigl(X_{1}(e)\bigr).

The
width
of the branch decomposition
(T,\mu)
is

\operatorname{width}(T,\mu):=\max_{e\in E(T)}\operatorname{ord}_{(T,\mu)}(e).

The
branch-width
of the connectivity system
(X,f)
, denoted by

\operatorname{bw}(X,f)
, is the minimum width over all branch decompositions of
(X,f)
.

Example 2.5.3
.

Let
X=\{a,b,c,d\}
, and define

f(S):=\min\{|S|,\ |X\setminus S|\}\qquad(S\subseteq X).

Then
f
is a symmetric submodular function on
X
.

Consider a tree
T
with four leaves
v_{a},v_{b},v_{c},v_{d}
, and let

\mu(v_{a})=a,\qquad\mu(v_{b})=b,\qquad\mu(v_{c})=c,\qquad\mu(v_{d})=d.

Suppose that one edge
e\in E(T)
separates the leaves into

\{v_{a},v_{b}\}
and
\{v_{c},v_{d}\}
.
Then

X_{1}(e)=\{a,b\},\qquad X_{2}(e)=\{c,d\},

and hence

\operatorname{ord}_{(T,\mu)}(e)=f(\{a,b\})=\min\{2,2\}=2.

For every edge of a branch decomposition on four leaves, the corresponding side has
size
1
or
2
, so the order is always at most
2
.
Moreover, some edge necessarily induces a
2
-
2
partition, so the width is at least
2
.
Therefore

\operatorname{width}(T,\mu)=2\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{bw}(X,f)=2.

In the theory of graph and connectivity width parameters,
duality theorems connect decompositions with obstruction objects such as tangles,
filters, and ultrafilters
[

,

,

For branch-width on connectivity systems, the following duality theorem with ultrafilters is known.

.

[

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
Then

\operatorname{bw}(X,f)\leq k

if and only if there does not exist a non-principal ultrafilter of order

on
(X,f)

A corresponding duality theorem with tangles is also classical.

Theorem 2.5.5

[

Let

\operatorname{bw}(X,f)\leq k

2.5.2  Linear Branch-Width

We next introduce
linear branch decompositions
, which are the linear counterparts of branch decompositions.
Like branch-width itself, linear branch-width has been studied extensively in the literature
[
158
,
98
,
159
]
.
Restricting the decomposition tree to a linear shape often leads to useful refinements of general width parameters and their associated dual objects.

Definition 2.5.7

(Linear branch decomposition)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, where
X
is a finite set and

f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is a symmetric submodular function.

A
linear branch decomposition
of
(X,f)
is a branch decomposition
(T,\mu)
of
(X,f)
in which the tree
T
is a caterpillar; that is, every vertex of
T
has degree
1
or
3
, and the subgraph obtained from
T
by deleting all leaves is a path.

Equivalently, a linear branch decomposition may be described by an ordering

x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}

of the elements of
X
, where
n=|X|
.
For each
i=1,2,\dots,n-1
, let

X_{i}:=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{i}\}.

The
width
of this linear branch decomposition is defined by

\max_{1\leq i\leq n-1}f(X_{i}).

The
linear branch-width
of
(X,f)
, denoted by

\operatorname{lbw}(X,f)
, is the minimum of these widths over all orderings of
X
.

Remark 2.5.8
.

The above ordering-based description is equivalent to the caterpillar-tree description.
Indeed, every caterpillar branch decomposition determines a linear ordering of the leaves from one end of the spine to the other, and conversely every linear ordering of
X
yields a caterpillar branch decomposition.

Example 2.5.9
.

Let

X=\{a,b,c,d\},

and define

f(S):=\min\{|S|,\ |X\setminus S|\}\qquad(S\subseteq X).

Then
f
is a symmetric submodular function, so
(X,f)
is a connectivity system.

Consider the ordering

a,b,c,d.

Then

X_{1}=\{a\},\qquad X_{2}=\{a,b\},\qquad X_{3}=\{a,b,c\}.

Hence

f(X_{1})=f(\{a\})=1,\qquad f(X_{2})=f(\{a,b\})=2,\qquad f(X_{3})=f(\{a,b,c\})=1.

Therefore the width of this linear branch decomposition is

\max\{1,2,1\}=2.

Since every ordering of a
4
-element set has a middle cut of size
2
, it follows that

\operatorname{lbw}(X,f)=2.

A single filter on a connectivity system is known to be dual to linear branch-width
[

]
.
Likewise, linear tangles
[

]
and linear loose tangles
[
35
]
are also known to exhibit corresponding duality phenomena.

Theorem 2.5.10

(
[
36
]
)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system. Then the linear branch-width of
(X,f)
is at most
k
if and only if there does not exist a non-principal single ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

Chapter 3
Maximal Filters, Chains, and Antichains on Connectivity Systems

In this chapter, we study several order-theoretic aspects of connectivity systems.
Since the ground set is finite, many arguments can be formulated in terms of finite partially ordered sets, and no choice principle is needed.

3.1  Chains and Antichains on the
k
-Truncation

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, where
X
is a finite set and

f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is a symmetric submodular function.
For a fixed integer
k\geq 0
, define

\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f):=\{A\subseteq X\mid f(A)\leq k\}.

We regard
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
as a finite poset under inclusion.

Definition 3.1.1

(Chain)
.

A
chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a finite family

\mathcal{C}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)

such that, after relabeling if necessary,

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{C}
is a chain in the poset
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f),\subseteq)
.

Definition 3.1.2

(Antichain)
.

An
antichain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a finite family

\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)

such that for all distinct
i,j
,

A_{i}\nsubseteq A_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{j}\nsubseteq A_{i}.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{A}
is an antichain in the poset
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f),\subseteq)
.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
is a finite poset, standard order-theoretic results apply directly.

Theorem 3.1.3

(Dilworth’s theorem on
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and let

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f).

Then the maximum size of an antichain contained in
\mathcal{A}
is equal to the minimum number of chains needed to cover
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate application of the classical Dilworth theorem to the finite poset
(\mathcal{A},\subseteq)
.
∎

3.2  A Finite Tukey-Type Observation for Filters

We now turn to filters on a connectivity system.
Recall that a filter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a family

F\subseteq 2^{X}
satisfying axioms
(Q0)
–
(Q3)
, and an ultrafilter is a filter satisfying the additional decisiveness axiom
(Q4)
.

Proposition 3.2.1

(Existence of maximal extensions)
.

Let
F_{0}
be a filter of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
.
Consider the family

\mathfrak{F}(F_{0}):=\{F\subseteq 2^{X}\mid F\text{ is a filter of order }k+1\text{ and }F_{0}\subseteq F\},

partially ordered by inclusion.
Then
\mathfrak{F}(F_{0})
has a maximal element.

Proof.

Since
X
is finite, the power set
2^{X}
is finite, and therefore the set of all families
F\subseteq 2^{X}
is finite as well.
Hence
\mathfrak{F}(F_{0})
is a finite partially ordered set.
Every finite partially ordered set has a maximal element.
∎

Remark 3.2.2
.

Proposition
3.2.1
is the finite analogue of the usual Tukey/Zorn type maximality argument.
In the present finite setting, no appeal to Tukey’s Lemma or Zorn’s Lemma is necessary.

The following implication is always valid.

Proposition 3.2.3
.

Every ultrafilter of order
k+1
on a connectivity system is maximal among filters of order
k+1
under inclusion.

Proof.

Let
U
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
, and suppose that
F
is a filter of order
k+1
with

U\subsetneq F.

Choose
A\in F\setminus U
.
By axiom
(Q0)
for
F
, we have
f(A)\leq k
.
Since
U
is an ultrafilter and
A\notin U
, axiom
(Q4)
implies that

X\setminus A\in U\subseteq F.

Now

A\cap(X\setminus A)=\emptyset,

and

f(\emptyset)=0\leq k.

Hence axiom
(Q1)
for
F
yields
\emptyset\in F
, contradicting axiom
(Q3)
.
Therefore no proper filter extension of
U
exists, and
U
is maximal.
∎

Remark 3.2.4
.

The converse implication, namely that every maximal filter is an ultrafilter, is not automatic from the definitions and should not be assumed without a separate proof.
For this reason it is better to distinguish carefully between
maximal filters
and
ultrafilters
.

3.3  Interaction between Filters and Chains

We next record a basic monotonicity property.

Lemma 3.3.1
.

Let
F
be a nonempty filter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
Then
X\in F
.

Proof.

Choose
A\in F
.
Since
A\subseteq X
and

f(X)=f(\emptyset)=0\leq k,

axiom
(Q2)
implies that
X\in F
.
∎

This yields the following simple description of how a filter meets a chain.

Proposition 3.3.2
.

Let
F
be a nonempty filter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
, and let

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}

be a chain in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
Then the set of indices

I_{F}:=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\mid A_{i}\in F\}

is either empty or a final interval of
\{1,\dots,m\}
.
In other words, if
A_{i}\in F
and
i\leq j\leq m
, then
A_{j}\in F
.

Proof.

If
A_{i}\in F
and
A_{i}\subseteq A_{j}
, then
f(A_{j})\leq k
because
A_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
Hence axiom
(Q2)
implies
A_{j}\in F
.
∎

Corollary 3.3.3
.

Let
U
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
, and let

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}=X

be a chain in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
Then there exists an index
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
such that

U\cap\{A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\}=\{A_{j},A_{j+1},\dots,A_{m}\}.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.3.1
,
X\in U
, so
I_{U}\neq\emptyset
.
Now apply Proposition
3.3.2
.
∎

Remark 3.3.4
.

An ultrafilter does
not
in general contain exactly one set from a chain.
Rather, along a chain ending at
X
, its intersection with the chain is typically a final segment, as described in Corollary
3.3.3
.

3.4  Sequence Chains and Linear Branch-Width

The sequence concept appearing naturally in the linear theory is the following.

Definition 3.4.1

(Sequence chain)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and write
n:=|X|
.
A
sequence chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a sequence

\emptyset=A_{0}\subsetneq A_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{n}=X

such that

|A_{i}|=i\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(A_{i})\leq k\quad\text{for all }i=0,1,\dots,n.

Equivalently, there exists an ordering

x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}

of the elements of
X
such that

A_{i}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\}\qquad(0\leq i\leq n).

This notion is exactly the ordering formulation of linear branch-width.

Theorem 3.4.2
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
Then the following are equivalent:

1.

\operatorname{lbw}(X,f)\leq k
.

2.

There exists a sequence chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

Proof.

By the ordering form of a linear branch decomposition,
\operatorname{lbw}(X,f)\leq k
means that there exists an ordering

x_{1},\dots,x_{n}

of
X
such that

f(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\})\leq k\qquad\text{for all }i=0,\dots,n.

Defining

A_{i}:=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\},

we obtain a sequence chain of order
k+1
.

Conversely, if

\emptyset=A_{0}\subsetneq A_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{n}=X

is a sequence chain with
|A_{i}|=i
, then for each
i
there is a unique element

x_{i}\in A_{i}\setminus A_{i-1}.

Thus

A_{i}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\},

and the ordering
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
defines a linear branch decomposition of width at most
k
.
Hence
\operatorname{lbw}(X,f)\leq k
.
∎

Combining this with the duality theorem for linear branch-width yields the following consequence.

Corollary 3.4.3
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
If there exists a sequence chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
, then there does not exist a non-principal single ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4.2
, the existence of a sequence chain of order
k+1
implies that

\operatorname{lbw}(X,f)\leq k.

Now apply the linear branch-width duality theorem.
∎

Remark 3.4.4
.

The existence of a sequence chain does
not
by itself rule out the existence of antichains in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
Chains and antichains may coexist in the same finite poset.
Thus any statement asserting that a sequence chain alone forbids all antichains is too strong in general.

The main order-theoretic conclusions obtained in this chapter are as follows:

•

Chains and antichains of order
k+1
are naturally studied in the finite poset
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f),\subseteq)
.

•

Dilworth’s theorem applies directly to this poset.

•

Every ultrafilter is maximal among filters, although the converse is not automatic.

•

The intersection of a filter with a chain is always a final segment.

•

Sequence chains are precisely the ordering-theoretic form of linear branch decompositions.

3.5  Other Chain Concepts on Connectivity Systems

3.5.1  Separation Chains on a Connectivity System

In graph theory, separation chains are frequently used in the study of width parameters such as path-width and cut-width

,

,

]
.
For connectivity systems, the corresponding notion can be formulated in terms of nested bipartitions of the ground set.

Definition 3.5.1

(Separation chain)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, where
X
is a finite set and

f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is a symmetric submodular function.
A
separation chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a finite sequence

(A_{1},B_{1}),\,(A_{2},B_{2}),\,\dots,\,(A_{m},B_{m})

such that, for each
i
,

A_{i}\cup B_{i}=X,\qquad A_{i}\cap B_{i}=\emptyset,\qquad B_{i}=X\setminus A_{i},

and the following conditions hold:

1.

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad B_{1}\supseteq B_{2}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq B_{m}.

2.

For every
i
,

f(A_{i})=f(B_{i})\leq k.

The
width
of the separation chain is defined by

\max_{1\leq i\leq m}f(A_{i}).

Definition 3.5.2

(Separation sequence chain)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A
separation sequence chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a separation chain

(A_{0},B_{0}),\,(A_{1},B_{1}),\,\dots,\,(A_{m},B_{m})

such that

A_{0}=\emptyset,\quad B_{0}=X,\quad A_{m}=X,\quad B_{m}=\emptyset,

and

A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}.

Its width is defined by

\max_{0\leq i\leq m}f(A_{i}).

Remark 3.5.3
.

Under the correspondence

A_{i}\longleftrightarrow(A_{i},X\setminus A_{i}),

chains and separation chains are equivalent descriptions of the same underlying nested structure.
Likewise, sequence chains and separation sequence chains are equivalent.

3.5.2  Sperner Systems and Traces on a Connectivity System

Concepts closely related to chains and antichains include Sperner systems and traces.
These notions are naturally formulated on the truncated set system

\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f):=\{A\subseteq X\mid f(A)\leq k\}.

Definition 3.5.4

(Sperner system of order
k+1
)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
is called a
Sperner system of order
k+1
if

A,B\in\mathcal{F}\ \text{and}\ A\subsetneq B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{impossible}.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{F}
is an antichain in the poset
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f),\subseteq)
.

Definition 3.5.5

(
l
-chain of order
k+1
)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
An
l
-chain of order
k+1
is a family

A_{1}\subsetneq A_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{l}

with each
A_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.

Definition 3.5.6

(
l
-Sperner system of order
k+1
)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
is called an
l
-Sperner system of order
k+1
if it contains no
l
-chain of order
k+1
.
It is called
saturated
if, for every

S\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)\setminus\mathcal{F},

the enlarged family
\mathcal{F}\cup\{S\}
contains an
l
-chain of order
k+1
.

Definition 3.5.7

(Trace on a connectivity system)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
, and let
Y\subseteq X
.
The
trace
(or
restriction
) of
\mathcal{F}
on
Y
of order
k+1
is

\mathcal{F}\!\restriction_{Y}:=\{F\cap Y\mid F\in\mathcal{F},\ f(F\cap Y)\leq k\}.

Thus
\mathcal{F}\!\restriction_{Y}
is a family of subsets of
Y
obtained by intersecting
Y
with members of
\mathcal{F}
and retaining only those intersections whose
f
-value is at most
k
.

Definition 3.5.8

(Strong trace)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
, and let
Y\subseteq X
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}

strongly traces

Y
of order
k+1
if there exists a set

B\subseteq X\setminus Y

such that for every
Z\subseteq Y
,

f(B\cup Z)\leq k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad B\cup Z\in\mathcal{F}.

Such a set
B
is called a
support
of
Y
in
\mathcal{F}
, and the set of all supports is denoted by
S_{\mathcal{F}}(Y)
.

Remark 3.5.9
.

A clutter is a family of subsets in which no member contains another.
Hence, in the present setting, a clutter contained in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
is precisely a Sperner system of order
k+1
.

3.5.3  Chain Decompositions on a Connectivity System

In the theory of partially ordered sets, chain decompositions, antichain decompositions, and symmetric chain decompositions are classical notions.
The corresponding concepts for connectivity systems are obtained by working inside the poset
(\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f),\subseteq)
.

Definition 3.5.10

(Chain decomposition)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
A
chain decomposition
of
\mathcal{G}
is a partition of
\mathcal{G}
into pairwise disjoint chains.

Definition 3.5.11

(Symmetric chain)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A chain

A_{1}\subsetneq A_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{m}

in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
is called
symmetric
if

|A_{i+1}|=|A_{i}|+1\qquad(1\leq i<m)

and

|A_{1}|+|A_{m}|=|X|.

Definition 3.5.12

(Symmetric chain decomposition)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
A
symmetric chain decomposition
of
\mathcal{G}
is a partition of
\mathcal{G}
into pairwise disjoint symmetric chains.

Definition 3.5.13

(Antichain decomposition)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
An
antichain decomposition
of
\mathcal{G}
is a partition of
\mathcal{G}
into pairwise disjoint antichains.

Question 3.5.14
.

How do symmetric chain decompositions of
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
reflect the structure of the underlying connectivity function
f
?

3.5.4  Operations and Single-Element Chains on a Connectivity System

We next record some elementary operations on chains, together with the notion of a single-element chain.

Definition 3.5.15

(Extension and deletion of a chain)
.

Let

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{m}

be a chain of order
k+1
in
(X,f)
.

1.

An
extension
of the chain is a chain obtained by adjoining a set
A_{m+1}
such that

A_{m}\subseteq A_{m+1}\subseteq X\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(A_{m+1})\leq k.

2.

A
deletion
from the chain is the removal of one member
A_{i}
, provided that the remaining family is still a chain in
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.

Definition 3.5.16

(Single-element chain)
.

A
single-element chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a chain

A_{1}\subsetneq A_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{m}

such that

f(A_{i})\leq k\qquad\text{for all }i,

and

|A_{i}\setminus A_{i-1}|=1\qquad(2\leq i\leq m).

Equivalently, consecutive members differ by exactly one newly added element.

Definition 3.5.17

(Single-element sequence chain)
.

A
single-element sequence chain of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a sequence

\emptyset=A_{0}\subsetneq A_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{m}=X

such that

f(A_{i})\leq k\qquad(0\leq i\leq m),

and

|A_{i}\setminus A_{i-1}|=1\qquad(1\leq i\leq m).

Remark 3.5.18
.

A single-element sequence chain is precisely an ordering

x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{|X|}

of the elements of
X
, together with the initial segments

A_{i}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}\},

subject to the condition
f(A_{i})\leq k
for all
i
.
Hence this notion is the ordering-theoretic form of linear branch-width.

Definition 3.5.19

(Single-element extension)
.

Let

A_{1}\subsetneq A_{2}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq A_{m}

be a single-element chain of order
k+1
.
A
single-element extension
is obtained by adjoining a set

A_{m+1}=A_{m}\cup\{e\},\qquad e\in X\setminus A_{m},

such that

f(A_{m+1})\leq k.

Remark 3.5.20
.

For arbitrary connectivity systems, there is no single universally accepted notion of
single-element coextension
analogous to the graph- or matroid-theoretic one.
Accordingly, extension- and coextension-type operations should be introduced with care, depending on the ambient category under consideration.

Chapter 4
Prefilters and Filter Subbases on a Connectivity System

In this chapter, we study prefilters and filter subbases on a connectivity system
(X,f)
.
These notions are closely related to filters and ultrafilters, and they play an important role in topology, set theory, and order theory.
Here we adapt them to the setting of connectivity systems by imposing the admissibility condition
f(A)\leq k
.

4.1  Prefilters on a Connectivity System

We begin with prefilters and ultra-prefilters.

Definition 4.1.1

(Prefilter)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, where
X
is a finite set and

f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is a symmetric submodular function.
A family
P\subseteq 2^{X}
is called a
prefilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
if:

(P1)

P\neq\emptyset
and
\emptyset\notin P
;

(P2)

for every
A\in P
, one has
f(A)\leq k
;

(P3)

for all
B,C\in P
, there exists
A\in P
such that

A\subseteq B\cap C.

Thus a prefilter is a nonempty proper family of
k
-admissible sets that is downward directed under inclusion.

Definition 4.1.2

(Ultra-prefilter)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A prefilter
P
of order
k+1
is called an
ultra-prefilter of order
k+1
if it satisfies the following additional condition:

(P4)

For every
A\subseteq X
with
f(A)\leq k
, there exists
B\in P
such that

B\subseteq A\qquad\text{or}\qquad B\subseteq X\setminus A.

The following observation is immediate.

Proposition 4.1.3
.

Every filter of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
is a prefilter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be a filter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

By axiom
(Q3)
, we have
\emptyset\notin Q
, and since a filter is assumed to be nonempty, condition (P1) holds.
Condition (P2) is exactly axiom
(Q0)
.

For (P3), let
B,C\in Q
.
If
f(B\cap C)\leq k
, then
B\cap C\in Q
by axiom
(Q1)
, so we may take
A:=B\cap C
.

Thus
Q
is a prefilter of order
k+1
.
∎

Likewise, every ultrafilter induces an ultra-prefilter.

Proposition 4.1.4
.

Every ultrafilter of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
is an ultra-prefilter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
By Proposition
4.1.3
,
Q
is already a prefilter.

Let
A\subseteq X
satisfy
f(A)\leq k
.
By axiom
(Q4)
, either
A\in Q
or
X\setminus A\in Q
.
In the first case, choose
B:=A
; in the second case, choose
B:=X\setminus A
.
Then
B\in Q
and
B\subseteq A
or
B\subseteq X\setminus A
, as required.

Hence
Q
is an ultra-prefilter.
∎

4.2  Filter Subbases on a Connectivity System

We next introduce filter subbases adapted to the connectivity-system setting.

Definition 4.2.1

(Filter subbase)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A family
S\subseteq 2^{X}
is called a
filter subbase of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
if:

(SB1)

S\neq\emptyset
;

(SB2)

\emptyset\notin S
;

(SB3)

for every
A\in S
, one has
f(A)\leq k
;

(SB4)

for every finite subfamily
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in S
, one has

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\neq\emptyset\qquad\text{and}\qquad f\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right)\leq k.

The family of finite intersections of members of a filter subbase forms a prefilter.

Definition 4.2.2
.

Let
S
be a filter subbase of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
Define

\operatorname{Pf}(S):=\left\{A\subseteq X\,\middle|\,\exists n\geq 1,\ \exists A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in S\text{ such that }A=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}\right\}.

We call
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
the
prefilter generated by
S
.

Proposition 4.2.3
.

Let
S
be a filter subbase of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
.
Then
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is a prefilter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

By definition,
S\neq\emptyset
, so
\operatorname{Pf}(S)\neq\emptyset
.
Moreover, by (SB4), every finite intersection of members of
S
is nonempty and has
f
-value at most
k
.
Hence
\emptyset\notin\operatorname{Pf}(S)
, and every member of
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is
k
-admissible.
Thus (P1) and (P2) hold.

To prove (P3), let

B=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}

be two members of
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
, where all
B_{i},C_{j}\in S
.
Then

B\cap C=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}B_{i}\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}

is again a finite intersection of members of
S
, so
B\cap C\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)
.
Taking
A:=B\cap C
, we obtain
A\subseteq B\cap C
, which proves (P3).

Therefore
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is a prefilter of order
k+1
.
∎

From a prefilter one obtains a filter by upward closure inside the
k
-admissible part.

Definition 4.2.4
.

Let
S
be a filter subbase of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
Define

\operatorname{Fil}(S):=\left\{B\subseteq X\,\middle|\,\exists A\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)\text{ such that }A\subseteq B\text{ and }f(B)\leq k\right\}.

We call
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
the
filter generated by
S
.

Theorem 4.2.5
.

Let
S
be a filter subbase of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
.
Then
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
is a filter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

We verify axioms
(Q0)
–
(Q3)
.

(Q0)
holds by definition of
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
.

To show nonemptiness, choose
A\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)
.
Then
A\subseteq A
and
f(A)\leq k
, so
A\in\operatorname{Fil}(S)
.
Thus
\operatorname{Fil}(S)\neq\emptyset
.

(Q3)
:
Since every
A\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is nonempty by (SB4), the empty set cannot contain any member of
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
.
Hence
\emptyset\notin\operatorname{Fil}(S)
.

(Q2)
:
Let
B\in\operatorname{Fil}(S)
, and suppose
B\subseteq C\subseteq X
with
f(C)\leq k
.
Choose
A\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)
such that
A\subseteq B
.
Then
A\subseteq C
, so
C\in\operatorname{Fil}(S)
.

(Q1)
:
Let
B,C\in\operatorname{Fil}(S)
, and assume
f(B\cap C)\leq k
.
Choose
A_{B},A_{C}\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)
such that

A_{B}\subseteq B,\qquad A_{C}\subseteq C.

Since
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is a prefilter by Proposition
4.2.3
,
there exists
A\in\operatorname{Pf}(S)
such that

A\subseteq A_{B}\cap A_{C}\subseteq B\cap C.

Because
f(B\cap C)\leq k
, it follows that
B\cap C\in\operatorname{Fil}(S)
.

Therefore
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
is a filter of order
k+1
.
∎

4.3  Ultrafilter-Oriented Subbases

A subbase does not, in general, determine an ultrafilter uniquely.
However, one can impose an orientation condition ensuring that the generated prefilter behaves like an ultra-prefilter.

Definition 4.3.1

(Ultrafilter-oriented subbase)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system.
A filter subbase
S
of order
k+1
is called an
ultrafilter-oriented subbase of order
k+1
if, in addition,

(USB)

for every
A\subseteq X
with
f(A)\leq k
, there exists
B\in S
such that

B\subseteq A\qquad\text{or}\qquad B\subseteq X\setminus A.

Theorem 4.3.2
.

Let
S
be an ultrafilter-oriented subbase of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
Then
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is an ultra-prefilter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.2.3
,
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is a prefilter.

Let
A\subseteq X
satisfy
f(A)\leq k
.
By (USB), there exists
B\in S
such that

B\subseteq A\qquad\text{or}\qquad B\subseteq X\setminus A.

Since
B\in S\subseteq\operatorname{Pf}(S)
, condition (P4) holds for
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
.
Hence
\operatorname{Pf}(S)
is an ultra-prefilter.
∎

Corollary 4.3.3
.

Let
S
be an ultrafilter-oriented subbase of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
Then the filter
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
extends to a maximal filter of order
k+1
.
If a maximal-extension theorem identifying such maximal filters with ultrafilters is available in the surrounding framework, then
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
extends to an ultrafilter of order
k+1
.

Proof.

Apply the finite maximal-extension argument to the filter
\operatorname{Fil}(S)
.
∎

4.4  Ultrafilter Base Number

In classical set theory, several cardinal characteristics measure the size of objects generating ultrafilters or related structures.
For connectivity systems, the most natural finite analogue is the minimum size of an ultra-prefilter base generating a non-principal ultrafilter.

Definition 4.4.1

(Ultrafilter base number on a connectivity system)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a finite connectivity system.
The
ultrafilter base number
of
(X,f)
, denoted by

u(X,f),

is defined by

u(X,f):=\min\left\{|\mathcal{B}|\;\middle|\;\mathcal{B}\subseteq 2^{X}\text{ is an ultra-prefilter and }\operatorname{Fil}(\mathcal{B})\text{ is a non-principal ultrafilter on }(X,f)\right\},

provided that a non-principal ultrafilter exists.

Question 4.4.2
.

How does the invariant
u(X,f)
relate to the order parameter
k
, to branch-width, and to the existence of non-principal ultrafilters on
(X,f)
?

Chapter 5
Ultrafilters on Infinite and Countable Connectivity Systems
(Exploratory Chapter)

5.1  Infinite Connectivity Systems

So far, we have considered ultrafilters on finite connectivity systems.
We now turn to the infinite case.

Definition 5.1.1

(Infinite connectivity system)
.

Let
X
be an infinite set, and let

f:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

be a set function.
We call
(X,f)
an
infinite connectivity system
if
f
satisfies:

1.

Symmetry:

f(A)=f(X\setminus A)\qquad\text{for all }A\subseteq X.

2.

Submodularity:

f(A)+f(B)\geq f(A\cup B)+f(A\cap B)\qquad\text{for all }A,B\subseteq X.

For several infinite arguments, it is convenient to impose an additional closure property.

Definition 5.1.2

(
k
-union-closed on chains)
.

Let
(X,f)
be an infinite connectivity system, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that
(X,f)
is
k
-union-closed on chains
if for every increasing chain

A_{0}\subseteq A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq\cdots

of subsets of
X
satisfying
f(A_{i})\leq k
for all
i
, one has

f\!\left(\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}A_{i}\right)\leq k.

Remark 5.1.3
.

In concrete examples arising from infinite matroids or graph-theoretic connectivity functions, additional continuity properties are often available.
When needed, such properties should be stated explicitly rather than built into the basic definition.

Definition 5.1.4

(Filters and ultrafilters on an infinite connectivity system)
.

Let
(X,f)
be an infinite connectivity system, and let
k\geq 0
.

A family
F\subseteq 2^{X}
is called a
filter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
if:

(Q0)

for every
A\in F
, one has
f(A)\leq k
;

(Q1)

if
A,B\in F
and
f(A\cap B)\leq k
, then
A\cap B\in F
;

(Q2)

if
A\in F
,
A\subseteq B\subseteq X
, and
f(B)\leq k
, then
B\in F
;

(Q3)

\emptyset\notin F
.

An
ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a filter of order
k+1
satisfying:

(Q4)

for every
A\subseteq X
with
f(A)\leq k
, either
A\in F
or
X\setminus A\in F
.

Definition 5.1.5

(Principal and non-principal)
.

Let
F
be a filter of order
k+1
on an infinite connectivity system
(X,f)
.
We say that
F
is:

1.

principal
if

\{x\}\in F\qquad\text{for some }x\in X\text{ with }f(\{x\})\leq k;

2.

non-principal
if

\{x\}\notin F\qquad\text{for every }x\in X.

Remark 5.1.6
.

The formulation above is the direct infinite analogue of the finite definitions used earlier.
In the infinite setting, existence theorems for ultrafilters generally require a separate maximality argument, typically via Zorn’s lemma or an equivalent principle.

As in the finite case, one may pass from higher order to lower order by restriction.

Proposition 5.1.7
.

Let
F
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on an infinite connectivity system
(X,f)
, and let
0\leq j\leq k
.
Define

F^{\leq j}:=\{A\in F\mid f(A)\leq j\}.

Then
F^{\leq j}
is an ultrafilter of order
j+1
on
(X,f)
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the finite case.
Axioms
(Q0)
–
(Q3)
are inherited by restriction, and
(Q4)
follows because symmetry gives

f(X\setminus A)=f(A)\leq j

whenever
f(A)\leq j
.
∎

Definition 5.1.8

(Fréchet-type filter)
.

Let
(X,f)
be an infinite connectivity system, and let
k\geq 0
.
Define

\mathrm{Fr}_{k}(X,f):=\{A\subseteq X\mid X\setminus A\text{ is finite and }f(A)\leq k\}.

When this family satisfies the filter axioms, we call it the
Fréchet filter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

Remark 5.1.9
.

In contrast with the classical set-theoretic case,
\mathrm{Fr}_{k}(X,f)
need not always be a filter.
Its behavior depends on how the connectivity bound
f(A)\leq k
interacts with cofinite subsets.

5.1.1  Selective-Type Notions

The following definitions are natural analogues of classical notions from the theory of ultrafilters on
\omega
.

Definition 5.1.10

(Selective ultrafilter)
.

Let
(X,f)
be an infinite connectivity system, and let
F
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.
We call
F

selective
if for every function
g:X\to X
, there exists
A\in F
such that the restriction
g|_{A}
is either constant or injective.

Remark 5.1.11
.

If
X
is equipped with a fixed well-order or is identified with
\mathbb{N}
, one may also introduce quasi-selective variants analogous to the classical theory.
Such variants depend on the ambient order structure on
X
, not only on the connectivity system itself.

5.1.2  P-Points and Q-Points

We now state the corresponding point-like notions in terms of the ultrafilter
F
.

Definition 5.1.12

(P-point and Q-point)
.

Let
(X,f)
be an infinite connectivity system, let
k\geq 0
, and let
F
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

1.

We call
F
a
P-point of order
k+1
if for every partition

X=\bigsqcup_{n<\omega}X_{n}

with
X_{n}\notin F
and
f(X_{n})\leq k
for all
n<\omega
, there exists
A\in F
such that

|A\cap X_{n}|<\omega\qquad\text{for all }n<\omega.

2.

We call
F
a
Q-point of order
k+1
if for every partition

X=\bigsqcup_{n<\omega}X_{n}

into finite sets with
X_{n}\notin F
and
f(X_{n})\leq k
for all
n<\omega
, there exists
A\in F
such that

|A\cap X_{n}|\leq 1\qquad\text{for all }n<\omega.

Question 5.1.13
.

Under what additional assumptions on
(X,f)
does the classical equivalence

\text{selective}\Longleftrightarrow\text{P-point}+\text{Q-point}

admit a meaningful analogue for ultrafilters of order
k+1
?

5.2  Countable Connectivity Systems and Games

We next consider the countable case, where
X
is countably infinite.

Definition 5.2.1

(Countable connectivity system)
.

A
countable connectivity system
is an infinite connectivity system
(X,f)
such that
X
is countably infinite.

5.2.1  An Ultrafilter Game on a Countable Connectivity System

The following game is a natural countable analogue of the ultrafilter game.

Game 5.2.2

(Ultrafilter game on a countable connectivity system)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a countable connectivity system, and let
D\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Two players, Player I and Player II, alternately choose elements of
X
, thereby producing a sequence

a=(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\in X^{\mathbb{N}}.

We write

\operatorname{supp}(a):=\{a_{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq X.

Player I wins if

a\in D\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(\operatorname{supp}(a))\leq k.

Otherwise Player II wins.

The game is said to be
determined
if one of the two players has a winning strategy.

Remark 5.2.3
.

The interaction between determinacy of Game
5.2.2
and the existence of ultrafilters of order
k+1
appears to be an interesting open direction.
At present, it is best formulated as a programmatic question rather than as an established theorem.

5.2.2  Elimination Games with an Invisible Robber

When a countable connectivity system is induced by a graph or digraph, one may also consider pursuit-evasion games constrained by the connectivity function.

Definition 5.2.4

(Elimination game with an invisible robber)
.

Let
G
be a countable graph or digraph with an associated connectivity function
f
, and let
k\geq 0
.
An
elimination game with an invisible robber
on
G
consists of a sequence of cop positions

C_{0},C_{1},C_{2},\dots\subseteq V(G)

satisfying

f(C_{i})\leq k\qquad\text{for all }i,

while the robber moves invisibly inside the components of
G-C_{i}
that remain available.
The cops win if, after finitely many rounds, every possible robber position is eliminated.
Otherwise the robber wins.

Conjecture 5.2.5
.

For suitable graph-induced connectivity systems, robber-winning strategies in elimination games are closely related to non-principal single ultrafilters.

5.3  Ultrafilters on Maximum-Connectivity Systems

We finally record a related class of connectivity-type functions.

Definition 5.3.1

(Maximum-submodular function)
.

Let
X
be a finite set.
A set function
\kappa:2^{X}\to\mathbb{N}
is called
maximum-submodular
if for all
A,B\subseteq X
,

\max\{\kappa(A),\kappa(B)\}\geq\max\{\kappa(A\cap B),\kappa(A\cup B)\}.

A normalized, symmetric, maximum-submodular function may be viewed as a
maximum-submodular connectivity function
, and the pair
(X,\kappa)
may be called a
maximum-connectivity system
.

Question 5.3.2
.

Can one define a satisfactory notion of ultrafilter on a maximum-connectivity system, and if so, how does it interact with tangle-type obstruction objects?

Chapter 6
Future Tasks

This chapter collects several directions for future research related to the themes of the present work.
Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the statements in this chapter are intended as proposed definitions, heuristic viewpoints, or open questions rather than as established results.

6.1  New Width, Length, and Depth Parameters

In the present work, ultrafilters arise as obstruction-type objects for branch-width and linear branch-width.
It is therefore natural to ask whether analogous ultrafilter-type objects can also be associated with other width, length, and depth parameters, including directed and linear variants.

6.1.1  A Possible Branch-Distance Decomposition

One natural direction is to enrich branch decompositions by incorporating a distance term.

Definition 6.1.1

(Proposed branch-distance decomposition)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph.
A
branch-distance decomposition
of
G
is a triple

(T,\mu,r),

where
(T,\mu)
is a branch decomposition of
G
and
r\in V(T)
is a distinguished root.

For each edge
e\in E(T)
, let
\operatorname{mid}_{T}(e)
denote the usual middle set associated with
e
.
Moreover, let

\operatorname{dist}_{T}(r,e)

denote the distance in
T
from the root
r
to the edge
e
, for example the minimum of the distances from
r
to the two endpoints of
e
.

The
branch-distance width
of
(T,\mu,r)
is defined by

\operatorname{bdw}(T,\mu,r):=\max_{e\in E(T)}\Bigl(|\operatorname{mid}_{T}(e)|+\operatorname{dist}_{T}(r,e)\Bigr).

The
branch-distance width
of
G
, denoted by
\operatorname{bdw}(G)
, is the minimum of
\operatorname{bdw}(T,\mu,r)
over all branch-distance decompositions of
G
.

Remark 6.1.2
.

This is only a proposed definition.
Its significance depends on whether it admits meaningful structural, extremal, or algorithmic characterizations, and whether it relates naturally to existing parameters such as tree-distance-width.

Question 6.1.3
.

How does
\operatorname{bdw}(G)
compare with tree-distance-width, path-distance-width, branch-width, and linear-width?

6.1.2  Directed Tree-Distance and Path-Distance Decompositions

For directed graphs, distance-based decompositions may be formulated in the following way.

Definition 6.1.4

(Proposed directed tree-distance decomposition)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a digraph.
A
directed tree-distance decomposition
of
G
is a triple

(R,\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(R)},r),

satisfying the following conditions:

1.

R
is an arborescence rooted at
r
;

2.

\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(R)}
is a partition of
V(G)
;

3.

for every
t\in V(R)
and every
v\in X_{t}
,

d_{G}(X_{r},v)=d_{R}(r,t);

4.

for every directed edge
(u,v)\in E(G)
, if
u\in X_{s}
and
v\in X_{t}
, then either
s=t
or
t
is an out-neighbour of
s
in
R
.

The
width
of the decomposition is

\max_{t\in V(R)}|X_{t}|.

The minimum such width is called the
directed tree-distance width
of
G
.

Definition 6.1.5

(Proposed directed path-distance decomposition)
.

A
directed path-distance decomposition
is a directed tree-distance decomposition in which the underlying arborescence
R
is a directed path.
The corresponding minimum width is called the
directed path-distance width
.

Question 6.1.6
.

Do these directed distance-width parameters admit obstruction-type duals analogous to tangles, filters, or ultrafilters?

6.1.3  Further Linear Width Parameters

Many width parameters admit linear variants obtained by restricting the underlying decomposition structure to a path.
Possible examples deserving systematic study include:

•

linear amalgam width,

•

linear modular-decomposition width,

•

linear tree-cut width,

•

directed linear branch-width.

Remark 6.1.7
.

For many width parameters, the linear variant is expected to dominate the unrestricted one:

\text{general width}\leq\text{linear width}.

A systematic treatment of such inequalities in the setting of connectivity systems remains open.

Question 6.1.8
.

Which of these linear width parameters admit natural dual objects, and which of them can be described in terms of ultrafilter-type structures?

6.1.4  Matroid Length Parameters

We next discuss possible length-type parameters for matroids.

For graphs, one of the most familiar length parameters is
tree-length
, which measures the maximum graph distance between two vertices that occur together in a bag of a tree-decomposition, minimized over all such decompositions
[
164
,
165
,
166
]
.
For matroids, however, there is no canonical intrinsic distance between arbitrary ground elements.
Accordingly, any matroid analogue of tree-length or branch-length must depend on an additional choice of distance-like structure.
The framework below should therefore be regarded as proposed rather than canonical.

Definition 6.1.9

(Proposed distance-labeled matroid)
.

A
distance-labeled matroid
is a pair
(M,d)
, where
M
is a matroid on ground set
E(M)
and

d:E(M)\times E(M)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is a symmetric function satisfying

d(e,e)=0\qquad\text{for all }e\in E(M).

Definition 6.1.10

(Proposed tree-length of a distance-labeled matroid)
.

Let
(M,d)
be a distance-labeled matroid.
A
tree-decomposition
of
M
is a pair
(T,\tau)
, where
T
is a tree and

\tau:E(M)\to V(T)

is a mapping.

For each node
t\in V(T)
, define the corresponding bag by

X_{t}:=\tau^{-1}(t)\subseteq E(M).

The
d
-length
of the bag
X_{t}
is

\lambda_{d}(X_{t}):=\max\{d(e,e^{\prime})\mid e,e^{\prime}\in X_{t}\}.

The
tree-length
of the decomposition
(T,\tau)
is

\operatorname{tl}_{d}(T,\tau):=\max_{t\in V(T)}\lambda_{d}(X_{t}).

The
tree-length
of
(M,d)
is

\operatorname{tl}_{d}(M):=\min_{(T,\tau)}\operatorname{tl}_{d}(T,\tau).

Definition 6.1.11

(Proposed branch-length of a distance-labeled matroid)
.

Let
(M,d)
be a distance-labeled matroid.
A
branch-decomposition
of
M
is a pair
(T,\mu)
, where
T
is a subcubic tree and

\mu:E(M)\to L(T)

is a bijection onto the leaf set of
T
.

For an edge
e\in E(T)
, let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be the two components of
T-e
, and define

E_{1}:=\mu^{-1}(L(T_{1})),\qquad E_{2}:=\mu^{-1}(L(T_{2})).

Assume further that to each cut edge
e\in E(T)
there is assigned a designated separator

\operatorname{mid}(e)\subseteq E(M).

Then the
branch-length
of
(T,\mu)
is defined by

\operatorname{bl}_{d}(T,\mu):=\max_{e\in E(T)}\max\{d(x,y)\mid x,y\in\operatorname{mid}(e)\},

with the convention that the inner maximum is
0
whenever
|\operatorname{mid}(e)|\leq 1
.
The
branch-length
of
(M,d)
is the minimum of
\operatorname{bl}_{d}(T,\mu)
over all branch-decompositions.

Remark 6.1.12
.

A useful future direction would be to identify natural choices of
d
and
\operatorname{mid}(e)
arising from representable matroids, lattice-path matroids, or matroids associated with graphs or hypergraphs.

6.1.5  Branch-Breadth and Linear-Breadth

We next consider breadth-type analogues of branch-width and linear-width for graphs.

Recall that tree-breadth measures how well each bag of a tree-decomposition can be covered by a single graph ball of small radius, and path-breadth is the corresponding path analogue
[
167
,
168
]
.
The following definitions are natural branch- and linear-ordering counterparts.

Definition 6.1.13

(Proposed branch-breadth)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, and let
(T,\mu)
be a branch-decomposition of
G
, where

\mu:E(G)\to L(T)

is a bijection.

For each edge
e\in E(T)
, let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be the two components of
T-e
, and define

E_{i}:=\mu^{-1}(L(T_{i}))\qquad(i=1,2).

Let

V_{i}:=\{v\in V(G)\mid v\text{ is incident with some edge of }E_{i}\},

and define the corresponding middle set by

\operatorname{mid}(e):=V_{1}\cap V_{2}.

The
breadth
of
e
is

\operatorname{br}(e):=\min_{c\in V(G)}\max_{x\in\operatorname{mid}(e)}d_{G}(c,x).

The
branch-breadth
of the decomposition
(T,\mu)
is

\operatorname{bb}(T,\mu):=\max_{e\in E(T)}\operatorname{br}(e),

and the
branch-breadth
of
G
is

\operatorname{bb}(G):=\min_{(T,\mu)}\operatorname{bb}(T,\mu).

Definition 6.1.14

(Proposed linear-breadth)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph, and let

(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{m})

be a linear ordering of
E(G)
.

For each
1\leq i\leq m-1
, define

E_{1}^{i}:=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\},\qquad E_{2}^{i}:=\{e_{i+1},\dots,e_{m}\},

and let
V_{1}^{i}
and
V_{2}^{i}
be the sets of vertices incident with edges in
E_{1}^{i}
and
E_{2}^{i}
, respectively.
Set

\operatorname{mid}(i):=V_{1}^{i}\cap V_{2}^{i}.

The
breadth
of the ordering is

\operatorname{lb}(e_{1},\dots,e_{m}):=\max_{1\leq i\leq m-1}\min_{c\in V(G)}\max_{x\in\operatorname{mid}(i)}d_{G}(c,x).

The
linear-breadth
of
G
is

\operatorname{lb}(G):=\min_{(e_{1},\dots,e_{m})}\operatorname{lb}(e_{1},\dots,e_{m}).

Remark 6.1.15
.

These definitions parallel the classical transitions

\text{tree-width}\leadsto\text{branch-width},\qquad\text{path-width}\leadsto\text{linear-width},

with bag cardinality replaced by the radius of a ball covering the relevant middle set.

6.1.6  Directed Proper-Path-Width

We next formulate a directed analogue of proper-path-width.
The following definition mirrors the usual path-decomposition framework, with the edge-covering condition adapted to the directed setting.

Definition 6.1.16

(Directed path-decomposition)
.

Let
D=(V,E)
be a digraph.
A sequence

\mathcal{P}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{r})

of subsets of
V
is called a
directed path-decomposition
of
D
if:

1.

\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}X_{i}=V;

2.

for every directed edge
(u,v)\in E
, there exist indices
i\leq j
such that

u\in X_{i},\qquad v\in X_{j};

3.

for all
1\leq i<j<\ell\leq r
,

X_{i}\cap X_{\ell}\subseteq X_{j}.

Its
width
is

\max_{1\leq i\leq r}|X_{i}|-1.

The
directed path-width
of
D
, denoted by
\operatorname{dpw}(D)
, is the minimum width over all directed path-decompositions of
D
.

Definition 6.1.17

(Directed proper-path-decomposition)
.

A directed path-decomposition

\mathcal{P}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{r})

is called a
directed proper-path-decomposition
if

X_{i}\not\subseteq X_{j}\qquad\text{for all }i<j.

Equivalently, no bag is contained in a later bag.
The
directed proper-path-width
of
D
, denoted by
\operatorname{dppw}(D)
, is the minimum width over all directed proper-path-decompositions of
D
.

Remark 6.1.18
.

The additional properness condition above is the directed counterpart of the usual strictness condition for proper path-decompositions.
It is cleaner to formulate this condition directly by containment exclusion.

6.1.7  Weighted Width Parameters

For weighted graphs, the cleanest approach is often to replace cardinalities in the classical objective functions by appropriate total weights.

Definition 6.1.19

(Weighted bandwidth)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a vertex-weighted graph, where

w:V\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

For a linear layout

\varphi:V\to\{1,\dots,|V|\},

define

\operatorname{bw}_{w}(\varphi):=\max_{\{u,v\}\in E}\bigl(w(u)+w(v)\bigr)\,|\varphi(u)-\varphi(v)|.

The
weighted bandwidth
of
G
is

\operatorname{bw}_{w}(G):=\min_{\varphi}\operatorname{bw}_{w}(\varphi).

Definition 6.1.20

(Weighted path-distance width)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a vertex-weighted graph.
A path-distance decomposition

(P,\{X_{t}\}_{t\in V(P)},r)

has
weighted width

\max_{t\in V(P)}\sum_{v\in X_{t}}w(v).

The minimum such value is called the
weighted path-distance width
of
G
, denoted by

\operatorname{wpdw}(G).

Definition 6.1.21

(Weighted carving-width)
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be an edge-weighted graph, where

w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

For a carving tree
T
, each edge
e\in E(T)
induces a partition of
V(G)
, and the
congestion
of
e
is defined as the total weight of the graph edges crossing that partition.
The minimum possible value of the maximum congestion is called the
weighted carving-width
of
G
, denoted by

\operatorname{wcvw}(G).

Remark 6.1.22
.

Weighted clique-width and weighted linear clique-width are best formulated within a weighted graph-expression formalism.
Since several inequivalent weighted variants appear in the literature, it is preferable to define such parameters only after fixing the allowed label operations and the way in which weights interact with them.

6.1.8  Directed Linear Width

We next state an ordering-based version of directed linear width.

Definition 6.1.23

(Proposed directed linear width)
.

Let
D=(V,E)
be a digraph, and let

(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{m})

be a linear ordering of
E
.

For each
1\leq i\leq m-1
, set

E_{1}^{i}:=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{i}\},\qquad E_{2}^{i}:=E\setminus E_{1}^{i}.

Let

S_{V}(E_{1}^{i},E_{2}^{i})

denote the chosen directed separator associated with the cut
(E_{1}^{i},E_{2}^{i})
.
The
width
of the ordering is

\max_{1\leq i\leq m-1}|S_{V}(E_{1}^{i},E_{2}^{i})|.

The
directed linear width
of
D
is the minimum of this quantity over all linear orderings of
E(D)
.

Remark 6.1.24
.

To obtain a fully rigorous parameter, the separator operator
S_{V}(\cdot,\cdot)
must be specified in advance.
Different choices may lead to genuinely different directed width notions.

6.1.9  Directed Connectivity Systems

The notions of directed subsets and directed submodular functions suggest a possible directed analogue of connectivity systems.

Definition 6.1.25

(Directed subset)
.

[

]

Let
S
be a finite set.
A
directed subset
of
S
is a function

X:S\to\{-1,0,1\}.

For
e\in S
, we say that:

•

e
is
forward
in

if
X(e)=1
,

•

e
is
backward
in

if
X(e)=-1
,

•

X(e)=0
.

For directed subsets
X
and

X\cap Y
and

coordinatewise in the usual way.

Definition 6.1.26

(Directed submodular function)
.

[
169

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a family of directed subsets of a finite set

A function

f:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}

is called
directed submodular

f(X)+f(Y)\geq f(X\cap Y)+f(X\cup Y).

Question 6.1.27

Can one define a useful notion of

6.1.10  DAG Parameters

For directed acyclic graphs, ordering-based width parameters can be defined naturally in terms of topological orderings.

Definition 6.1.28

(DAG cut-width)
.

Let
D=(V,E)
be a directed acyclic graph.
Its
DAG cut-width
is

\operatorname{dcutw}(D):=\min_{\varphi}\max_{1\leq i\leq|V|}\left|\{(u,v)\in E\mid\varphi(u)\leq i<\varphi(v)\}\right|,

where the minimum is taken over all topological orderings
\varphi
of
D
.

Definition 6.1.29

(DAG bandwidth)
.

Let
D=(V,E)
be a directed acyclic graph.
Its
DAG bandwidth
is

\operatorname{dbw}(D):=\min_{\varphi}\max_{(u,v)\in E}|\varphi(v)-\varphi(u)|,

where the minimum is taken over all topological orderings
\varphi
of

.

Question 6.1.30
.

Which obstruction objects naturally correspond to DAG-path-width, DAG cut-width, and DAG bandwidth?

6.2  Further Properties of Ultrafilters on a Connectivity System

In this section we discuss several additional aspects of ultrafilters on a connectivity system.
Some are immediate consequences of the axioms, whereas others should be regarded as open-ended research directions.

6.2.1  Simple Games on a Connectivity System

Simple games are standard mathematical models of voting systems and collective decision-making.
They are closely related to filters and ultrafilters through the interpretation of winning coalitions.

Definition 6.2.1

(Simple game on a connectivity system)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and let
k\geq 0
.
A family

W\subseteq\{A\subseteq X\mid f(A)\leq k\}

is called a
simple game of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
if:

(SG1)

\emptyset\notin W
,

(SG2)

X\in W
,

(SG3)

whenever
A\in W
,
A\subseteq B\subseteq X
, and
f(B)\leq k
, one has
B\in W
.

The members of
W
are called
winning coalitions
.

Definition 6.2.2
.

A simple game
W
of order
k+1
is called:

1.

proper
if

A\in W\text{ and }f(A)\leq k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X\setminus A\notin W;

2.

strong
if

A\notin W\text{ and }f(A)\leq k\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X\setminus A\in W.

Proposition 6.2.3
.

Let
U
be an ultrafilter of order
k+1
on a connectivity system
(X,f)
.
Then
U
is a proper strong simple game of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
.

Proof.

By axiom
(Q3)
, one has
\emptyset\notin U
.
Since
U\neq\emptyset
, axiom
(Q2)
implies
X\in U
, because

f(X)=f(\emptyset)=0\leq k.

Hence
(SG1)
and
(SG2)
hold.
Moreover,
(SG3)
is exactly axiom
(Q2)
.

To prove properness, suppose that
A\in U
and
X\setminus A\in U
.
Then

A\cap(X\setminus A)=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad f(\emptyset)=0\leq k.

Therefore axiom
(Q1)
yields
\emptyset\in U
, contradicting
(Q3)
.
Thus
U
is proper.

To prove strength, let
A\subseteq X
satisfy
f(A)\leq k
, and assume that
A\notin U
.
Then axiom
(Q4)
implies
X\setminus A\in U
.
Hence
U
is strong.
∎

6.2.2  Choice Functions Compatible with a Connectivity Constraint

The ordinary Axiom of Choice concerns arbitrary families of nonempty sets.
In the setting of connectivity systems, one may ask whether a choice can be made while preserving some
f
-bounded structure.

Definition 6.2.4

(Admissible choice function)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a connectivity system, and let

\{S_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}

be a family of nonempty subsets of
X
such that

f(S_{\alpha})\leq k\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in A.

A
choice function
for this family is a map

c:A\to X

such that

c(\alpha)\in S_{\alpha}\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in A.

Question 6.2.5
.

Under what additional assumptions on
(X,f)
, or on the family
\{S_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in A}
, can one choose
c
so that the image

c[A]=\{c(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in A\}

also satisfies a meaningful connectivity bound, for example

f(c[A])\leq k\qquad\text{or}\qquad f(c[A])\leq g(k)?

Remark 6.2.6
.

This question may be especially interesting in the infinite setting, where selective and Ramsey-type ultrafilters could interact with admissible choice principles.

6.2.3  Ultraproducts and Ultrapowers: A Programmatic Viewpoint

Ultraproducts and ultrapowers are fundamental constructions in model theory.
A satisfactory extension of these notions to ultrafilters on connectivity systems appears to require additional compatibility assumptions, because the ultrafilter axioms on
(I,f)
only decide those subsets of the index set
I
that are
f

6.2.4  A Brute-Force Algorithm for Constructing an Ultrafilter

For finite connectivity systems, one may search exhaustively for an ultrafilter.

Algorithm 1

Exhaustive search for an ultrafilter on a finite connectivity system

•
3

Input:
A finite connectivity system
(X,f)
and an integer
k\geq 0
.

•
4

Output:
An ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
, if one exists.

1.
5

Compute the
k
-admissible family

\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f):=\{A\subseteq X\mid f(A)\leq k\}.

2.
6

Enumerate all subfamilies
F\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.

3.
7

For each such
F
, test whether
F
satisfies axioms
(Q1)
–
(Q4)
and whether
\emptyset\notin F
.

4.
8

Return the first family that passes all tests.

5.
9

If no such family exists, report that no ultrafilter of order
k+1
exists.

Proposition 6.2.9
.

The above algorithm is correct.

Proof.

By definition, every ultrafilter of order
k+1
on
(X,f)
is a subfamily of
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
Hence exhaustive enumeration of all subfamilies of
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
includes every possible candidate.
The algorithm returns exactly those families satisfying the ultrafilter axioms.
Therefore, if an ultrafilter exists, the algorithm finds one, and if none exists, it correctly reports failure.
∎

Proposition 6.2.10
.

Let

m:=\bigl|\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)\bigr|.

Then the brute-force algorithm runs in time

O(2^{m}m^{2}).

Moreover, if one measures space only at the level of enumerating candidate subfamilies of
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
, the algorithm uses

O(m)

storage locations.
A more precise bit-complexity bound depends on the chosen representation of subsets of
X
.
Since

m\leq 2^{|X|},

the running time is doubly exponential in
|X|
in the worst case.

Proof.

There are exactly
2^{m}
candidate families

F\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f).

For each candidate
F
, axioms
(Q1)
and
(Q2)
can be verified by checking pairs of members of
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
, which requires
O(m^{2})
time.
The remaining conditions, namely
(Q3)
and
(Q4)
, require at most
O(m)
additional time.
Therefore, the total running time is

O(2^{m}m^{2}).

For the space bound, one only needs to keep in memory the admissible family
\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
together with one current candidate family
F\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{k}(X,f)
.
Thus, at the level of family enumeration, the required storage is
O(m)
.
The corresponding bit-complexity depends on how the subsets of
X
are encoded.
∎

Remark 6.2.11
.

This algorithm is not intended to be efficient.
Its purpose is simply to provide a mathematically correct finite decision procedure.
It would be interesting to develop substantially faster algorithms for special classes of graphs or for special types of connectivity functions.

6.2.5  Ultrafilter Width and Expansion-Type Hypotheses

We next isolate a natural width-type invariant associated with ultrafilters.

Definition 6.2.12

(Ultrafilter width)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a finite connectivity system.
The
ultrafilter width
of
(X,f)
, denoted by

\operatorname{ufw}(X,f),

is defined by

\operatorname{ufw}(X,f):=\max\{k\geq 0\mid\text{there exists a non-principal ultrafilter of order }k+1\text{ on }(X,f)\},

provided that at least one such non-principal ultrafilter exists.

Remark 6.2.13
.

Whenever the branch-width duality theorem applies,
\operatorname{ufw}(X,f)
is closely related to branch-width.
Indeed, in such settings the existence of a non-principal ultrafilter of order
k+1
is equivalent to the failure of branch-width at most
k
.

To formulate an expansion-type hypothesis for connectivity systems, it is natural to imitate conductance-type quantities.

Definition 6.2.14

(Connectivity conductance)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a finite connectivity system.
For
S\subseteq X
, define the
f
-volume of
S
by

\operatorname{vol}_{f}(S):=\sum_{x\in S}f(\{x\}).

Whenever the denominator is positive, define the
connectivity conductance
of
S
by

\Phi_{f}(S):=\frac{f(S)}{\min\{\operatorname{vol}_{f}(S),\operatorname{vol}_{f}(X\setminus S)\}}.

Definition 6.2.15

(Small set connectivity expansion problem)
.

Let
(X,f)
be a finite connectivity system and let
0<\delta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The
small set connectivity expansion problem
asks for a subset
S\subseteq X
satisfying

0<\operatorname{vol}_{f}(S)\leq\delta\,\operatorname{vol}_{f}(X)

that minimizes
\Phi_{f}(S)
.

Conjecture 6.2.16

(Connectivity-system SSE-type hardness)
.

Under a suitable Small Set Connectivity Expansion Hypothesis for connectivity systems, it is NP-hard to approximate
\operatorname{ufw}(X,f)
within a constant factor.

Question 6.2.17
.

Can one formulate a natural class of connectivity systems for which the approximation hardness of ultrafilter width follows from the classical Small Set Expansion Hypothesis?

6.2.6  Further Variants of Ultrafilters

Many variants of ultrafilters have been studied in set theory, topology, logic, fuzzy theory, and related areas.
It is therefore natural to ask whether analogous notions can be defined for graphs, directed graphs, infinite graphs, connectivity systems, and infinite connectivity systems.

Question 6.2.18

Can one define and systematically study connectivity-system analogues of partition ultrafilters, uniform ultrafilters, normal ultrafilters, regular ultrafilters, good ultrafilters, fuzzy ultrafilters, selective ultrafilters, Ramsey ultrafilters, and related variants?

Remark 6.2.19

A particularly appealing goal would be to determine which of these variants correspond to known width parameters, game-theoretic obstructions, or decomposition phenomena.

Definition A.1.1

(Tree-decomposition)

Definition A.13.1

(Matroid via rank function)
.

A

[

,
362
]

Definition A.13.2

(Rank-width)
.

[

For each subset

A

(T,L)

T

to the leaves of

.

Each edge

induces a bipartition

of the leaves of

of

The

of

of the rank-decomposition

[

(Tree-cut decomposition and tree-cut width)

Definition A.26.1

(Twin-width)

(cf.
[

,

,
445
]

•

and

•

if and only if both

and

•

otherwise.

-trigraph
if every vertex is incident with at most

-contraction
if both the original trigraph and the contracted trigraph are
d
-trigraphs.

of

Appendix B
Various Length Parameters

This chapter surveys several graph parameters of
length type
.
Unlike width parameters, which typically measure the size of separators, bags, or interfaces, length parameters usually measure metric spread inside the parts of a decomposition.
Among them, tree-length is one of the most fundamental examples.

B.1  Tree-length, Tree-breadth, and Branch-length

We begin with tree-length and some closely related parameters.
Tree-length measures the maximum distance between two vertices that appear together in a bag of a tree-decomposition, minimized over all tree-decompositions of the graph
[
164
,
165
,
166
]
.

Definition B.1.1

(Tree-length)
.

[
164
,
165
,
166
]

Let

be a tree-decomposition of a graph

.

of

is defined by

\lambda(\mathcal{T}):=\max_{i\in I}\ \max_{u,v\in X_{i}}d_{G}(u,v),

where
d_{G}(u,v)
denotes the distance between
u
and
v
in
G
.

The
tree-length
of
G
, denoted by
\tl(G)
, is the minimum value of
\lambda(\mathcal{T})
over all tree-decompositions
\mathcal{T}
of
G
.

Chordal graphs have tree-length
1
.
A closely related parameter is

, which measures how well each bag can be covered by a disk of small radius in the original graph
[
167
,
168
]
.

Definition B.1.2

(Tree-breadth)

[
167
,
168

is the smallest integer

such that, for every

centered at
v_{i}
.

tree-breadth
of
G
, denoted by
\tb(G)

G
.

]

B.2  Possible Future Directions for Length Parameters

We next list several possible length-type parameters that may be worth studying in the future.
The following notions are intended only as preliminary directions rather than fully established definitions.
In particular, for some decomposition frameworks, the phrase “diameter of a bag” is natural, whereas for others a more appropriate formulation may involve separators, middle sets, or induced boundary structures.

•

Directed tree-length.

A possible directed analogue of tree-length based on directed tree-decompositions and directed distance.

•

Directed path-length.

A linear analogue of directed tree-length based on directed path-decompositions.

•

Rank-length.

A length-type parameter associated with rank-decompositions, possibly defined through metric properties of the vertex parts induced by decomposition edges.

•

Linear rank-length.

A linear version of rank-length.

•

Boolean-length.

A possible length-type refinement of Boolean decompositions.

•

Linear Boolean-length.

A linear restriction of Boolean-length.

•

Arc-length.

A length-type parameter derived from arc-based decompositions or circular representations.

•

Star-length.

A length analogue of star decompositions, defined via the largest diameter among the star parts.

•

Hypertree-length.

A length-type version of hypertree decompositions for hypergraphs.

•

Carving-length.

A possible length parameter associated with carving decompositions.

•

Cut-length.

A length parameter based on cut decompositions.

•

Tree-cut-length.

A length-type refinement of tree-cut decompositions.

•

Tree-distance-length.

A length version of tree-distance decompositions.

•

Path-distance-length.

A linear version of tree-distance-length.

•

Clique-length.

A possible length-type refinement of clique-width decompositions.

•

Linear clique-length.

A linear restriction of clique-length.

•

NLC-length.

A length-type parameter corresponding to NLC decompositions.

•

Linear NLC-length.

A linear restriction of NLC-length.

•

Modular-length.

A length-type parameter associated with modular decompositions.

A systematic study of such parameters may help clarify how metric information interacts with decomposition theory beyond width-type invariants.

\mathcal{P}_{x}=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{m}\}

of

\lambda(\mathcal{P}_{x}):=\max\Bigl\{\lambda\!\Bigl(\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i}\Bigr)\;\Big|\;\emptyset\neq I\subsetneq\{1,\dots,m\}\Bigr\}.

branch-depth
of

(k,k)
-decomposition.

When

is a graph, its
branch-depth
is defined as the branch-depth of the standard connectivity function associated with

[
558
]
.

Appendix E
Graph Games Related to Width Parameters

As discussed throughout this book, many graph width parameters admit natural game-theoretic interpretations.
Such interpretations are often useful for developing intuition about width parameters, their obstructions, and their algorithmic significance.
In this chapter, we briefly review several well-known games that are closely connected with graph width theory.

E.1  Cops and Robbers (Pursuit–Evasion Games)

Cops and Robbers is a classical pursuit–evasion game played on a graph, in which a team of cops attempts to capture a robber.
Many variants have been studied, including versions with different visibility assumptions, movement rules, and monotonicity restrictions
[
6
,
179
,
566
]
.
One standard example is the following jump-searching version.

Definition E.1.1

(Helicopter Cops and Robber Game)
.

[
19
]

Let
G
be a graph, and let
X\subseteq V(G)
.
An
X
-flap
is the vertex set of a connected component of
G-X
.

Two vertex sets
X,Y\subseteq V(G)
are said to
touch
if

N(X)\cap Y\neq\emptyset.

A
position
in the game is a pair
(X,R)
, where
X\subseteq V(G)
represents the set of vertices occupied by the cops, and
R
is an
X
-flap representing the current location of the robber.
Since the robber may move arbitrarily fast inside
G-X
, only the component containing the robber is relevant.

The game proceeds as follows.

•

Initially, the cops choose a set
X_{0}\subseteq V(G)
with
|X_{0}|\leq k
, where
k
is the number of cops.

•

The robber then chooses an
X_{0}
-flap
R_{0}
.

•

At round
i\geq 1
, if the current position is
(X_{i-1},R_{i-1})
, then the cops choose a new set
X_{i}\subseteq V(G)
with
|X_{i}|\leq k
.

•

After seeing
X_{i}
, the robber chooses an
X_{i}
-flap
R_{i}
that touches
R_{i-1}
.

If at some stage the robber has no legal choice of
R_{i}
, then the cops win.
Otherwise, if the robber can continue indefinitely, then the robber wins.

E.2  Cops and Invisible Robbers

The
Cops and Invisible Robber
game is a pursuit–evasion game in which the cops attempt to capture a robber whose location is hidden from them
[
567
,
568
,
569
,
570
]
.
Variants of this game are closely related to parameters such as path-width and search numbers.

Definition E.2.1

(Zero-Visibility Cops and Robber Game)
.

[
567
]

The zero-visibility cops and robber game is played on a connected graph
G
by two players: the
cop player
and the
robber player
.
The cop player controls
k
cops, while the robber player controls one robber.

The game is played according to the following rules.

•

Setup.
The cops are first placed on vertices of
G
. The robber then chooses a starting vertex, which is not revealed to the cops.

•

Moves.
The players move alternately, with the cops moving first. On each turn, the cop player may move one or more cops along edges of
G
, and the robber may move along an edge to an adjacent vertex.

•

Visibility.
The robber sees all cop moves, but the cops do not know the robber’s position.

•

Winning condition.
The cops win if at some point a cop occupies the same vertex as the robber. The robber wins if capture never occurs.

A
strategy
for the cops specifies how the cops move at each stage of the game.
The
zero-visibility cop number
of
G
, denoted by
c_{0}(G)
, is the minimum number of cops required to guarantee capture.

E.3  Parity Games

A parity game is a two-player infinite-duration game played on a directed graph.
Such games have been studied extensively, especially in connection with directed width parameters and model checking
[
477
,
571
,
572
]
.

Definition E.3.1

(Parity Game)
.

[
477
]

A
parity game
is a tuple

P=(V,V_{0},E,\Omega),

where

•

V
is a finite set of vertices,

•

V_{0}\subseteq V
is the set of vertices controlled by player
Even
,

•

V\setminus V_{0}
is the set of vertices controlled by player
Odd
,

•

E\subseteq V\times V
is the set of directed edges,

•

\Omega:V\to\omega
is a priority function.

A
play
of the game is an infinite sequence

\pi=(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2},\dots)

such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in E
for all
i\geq 0
.

The winner is determined by the priorities appearing infinitely often along the play:

•

\pi
is winning for
Even
if the minimum priority appearing infinitely often is even;

•

otherwise,
\pi
is winning for
Odd
.

A
strategy
for a player specifies the next move whenever the token is at a vertex controlled by that player.
A strategy is called
memoryless
if it depends only on the current vertex.
Parity games are determined, and moreover both players admit memoryless optimal strategies.

E.4  Spanning-Tree Games

A spanning tree of a graph is a connected acyclic spanning subgraph.
Games based on spanning trees have been studied from combinatorial, algorithmic, and game-theoretic viewpoints
[
573
,
574
,
575
]
.
These games are also related to graph structure, since width parameters often influence the complexity of constructing or optimizing spanning trees.

Definition E.4.1

(Spanning-Tree Game)
.

[
575
]

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be a weighted graph, where
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a nonnegative edge-weight function.

A
configuration
of the game is a forest
F\subseteq E
.
A move is
legal
from
F
if an edge
e\in E\setminus F
is added such that

(V,F\cup\{e\})

is still acyclic.
Thus the set of legal moves is

M(F):=\{e\in E\setminus F\mid(V,F\cup\{e\})\text{ is a forest}\}.

There are two players,
Max
and
Min
, who alternate turns, with Max moving first.

•

Max aims to maximize the total weight of the final spanning tree.

•

Min aims to minimize that total weight.

A
strategy
for a player is a rule that selects a legal move from every configuration.
If both players follow strategies
\pi_{\max}
and
\pi_{\min}
, the resulting spanning tree is denoted by

T(\pi_{\max},\pi_{\min}),

and its total weight is

w(\pi_{\max},\pi_{\min})=\sum_{e\in T(\pi_{\max},\pi_{\min})}w(e).

E.5  Mixed Search Games

Mixed search games model the process of clearing a contaminated graph by placing and moving searchers
[
576
,
577
,
578
,
579
,
580
]
.
They are closely related to search numbers and several width parameters.
Related games include the edge search game and the node search game
[
581
]
.

Definition E.5.1

(Mixed Search Game)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
Initially, every edge of
G
is considered
contaminated
.
The goal is to clear all edges using searchers.

An edge
e\in E
becomes
cleared
if either

1.

searchers occupy both endpoints of
e
, or

2.

a searcher slides along
e
.

A cleared edge becomes
recontaminated
if there exists a path from a contaminated edge to that edge such that no vertex or edge on the path is occupied by a searcher.

A
mixed search strategy
is a sequence of operations chosen from the following types:

1.

placing a searcher on a vertex,

2.

removing a searcher from a vertex,

3.

sliding a searcher along an edge to an adjacent vertex,

4.

sliding a searcher along an edge in such a way that the edge is cleared during the move.

The objective is to clear all edges of
G
while preventing recontamination.
The main optimization problem is to minimize the number of searchers required.
