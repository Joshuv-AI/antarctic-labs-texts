Convergence Analysis for A Stochastic Maximum Principle Based Data Driven Feedback Control Algorithm
Siming Liang, Hui Sun, Richard Archibald, Feng Bao

Abstract
This paper presents convergence analysis of a novel data-driven feedback control algorithm designed for generating online controls based on partial noisy observational data. The algorithm comprises a particle filter-enabled state estimation component, estimating the controlled system’s state via indirect observations, alongside an efficient stochastic maximum principle type optimal control solver. By integrating weak convergence techniques for the particle filter with convergence analysis for the stochastic maximum principle control solver, we derive a weak convergence result for the optimization procedure in search of optimal data-driven feedback control. Numerical experiments are performed to validate the theoretical findings.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we carry out numerical analysis demonstrating the convergence of a data-driven feedback control algorithm designed for generating online control based on partial noisy observational data.

Our focus lies in the stochastic feedback control problem, which aims to determine optimal controls. These control actions are used to guide a controlled state dynamical system towards meeting certain optimality conditions, leveraging feedbacks from the system’s current state. There are two practical challenges in solving the feedback control problem. First, when the control problem’s dimension is high, the computational cost for searching the optimal control escalates exponentially. This is known as the “curse of dimensionality”. Second, in numerous scenarios, the state of the controlled system is not directly observable. Therefore, state estimation techniques become necessary to estimate the current state for designing optimal control, with observations gathered to aid in estimating the hidden state.

To address the aforementioned challenges, a novel online data-driven feedback control algorithm has been developed
[
6
,
7
]
. This algorithm introduces a stochastic gradient descent optimal control solver within the stochastic maximum principle framework to combat the high dimensionality issue in optimal control problems. Traditionally, stochastic optimal control problems are solved using dynamical programming or the stochastic maximum principle, both requiring numerical simulations for large differential systems
[
15
,
17
,
24
]
. However, the stochastic maximum principle stands out for its capability to handle random coefficients in the state model and finite-dimensional terminal state constraints
[
28
]
. In the stochastic maximum principle approach, a system of backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs) are derived as the adjoint equation of the controlled state process.
Then, the solution of the adjoint BSDE is utilized to formulate the gradient of the cost function with respect to the control process
[
18
,
27
]
. However, solving BSDEs numerically entails significant computational costs, especially in high-dimensional problems, demanding a large number of random samples
[
30
,
32
]
. To bolster efficiency, a sample-wise optimal control solver method has been devised
[
3
]
, where the solution of the adjoint BSDE is represented using only one realization or a small batch of samples. This approach justifies the application of stochastic approximation in the optimization procedure
[
25
,
26
]
, and it shifts the computational cost from solving BSDEs to searching for the optimal control, thereby enhancing overall efficiency
[
5
]
.

In data-driven feedback control, optimal filtering methods also play a pivotal role in dynamically estimating the state of the controlled system. Two prominent approaches for
nonlinear
optimal filtering are the Zakai filter and the particle filter. While the Zakai filter aims to compute the conditional probability density function (pdf) for the target dynamical system using a parabolic type stochastic partial differential equation known as the Zakai equation
[
29
]
. The particle filter, also known as a sequential Monte Carlo method, approximates the desired conditional pdf using the empirical distribution of a set of random samples (particles)
[
19
]
. Although the Zakai filter theoretically offers more accurate approximations for conditional distributions, the particle filter is favored in more practical applications due to the high efficiency of the Monte Carlo method in approximating high-dimensional distributions
[
23
]
.

The aim of this study is to examine the convergence of the data-driven feedback control algorithm proposed in
[
7
]
, providing mathematical validation for its performance. While convergence in particle filter methods has been well studied
[
1
,
16
,
21
]
, this work adopts the analysis technique outlined in
[
16
]
to establish weak convergence results for the particle filter regarding the number of particles. Analysis techniques for BSDEs alongside classical convergence results for stochastic gradient descent
[
5
,
8
]
are crucial for achieving convergence in the stochastic gradient descent optimal control solver. The theoretical framework of this analysis merges the examination of particle filters with the analysis of optimal control, and the overarching objective of this paper is to derive a comprehensive weak convergence result for the optimal data-driven feedback control.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the data driven feedback control algorithm. Convergence analysis will be presented in Section 3, and in Section 4 we conduct multiple numerical experiments to validate our theoretical findings.

3.2
The convergence theorem for the data driven feedback control algorithm

Our algorithm combines the particle filter method and the stochastic gradient descent method. Lemma
3.1
(combine Lemma 4.7-4.9 from the book
[
22
]
) provides the convergence result for the particle filter method alone. It shows that each prediction and updating step is guaranteed to be convergent.

Recall that
\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{N}}
is the sampling operator where we sample
\mathcal{N}
particles.
P^{N}_{n}
denotes the transition operator (the prediction step) under the SGD-particle filter framework.
P_{n}
denotes the deterministic transition operator assuming that SGD gives the exact control.
L_{n}
denotes the updating step in the particle filter method.

Lemma 3.1
.

We assume that there exists
\kappa\in(0,1]
. The following is true:

\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mu)}d(S^{\mathcal{N}}\mu,\mu)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathcal{N}}}}

\displaystyle d(P^{N}_{n}\mu,P^{N}_{n}\nu)

\displaystyle\leq d(\mu,\nu)

(32)

\displaystyle d(P_{n}\mu,P_{n}\nu)

\displaystyle\leq d(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle d(L_{n}\nu,L_{n}\mu)

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa^{-2}d(\nu,\mu)

(33)

Given Lemma
3.1
, Theorem 4.5 in
[
22
]
tells us the particle filter framework is convergent. Then, following Lemma
3.1
, we can have the distance between the true distribution of the state and the estimated distribution through the SGD-particle filter framework.

\displaystyle d(\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n+1},\mu_{n+1})

\displaystyle\equiv d(L_{n}S^{\mathcal{N}}P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},L_{n}P_{n}\mu_{n})

(34)

\displaystyle\leq d(L_{n}S^{\mathcal{N}}P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},L_{n}S^{\mathcal{N}}P_{n}\mu_{n})+d(L_{n}S^{\mathcal{N}}P\mu_{n},L_{n}P_{n}\mu_{n})

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa^{-2}\Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}+d(P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},P_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n})+d(S^{\mathcal{N}}P_{n}\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},P_{n}\mu_{n})\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa^{-2}\Big(\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}+d(P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},P_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n})+d(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})\Big)

(35)

where in the above inequalities, we have used triangle inequalities and lemma
3.1
.

Hence, if we can show that the inequality of the following form holds

\displaystyle d(P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},P_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n})\leq C_{n}d(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})+\epsilon_{n}

(36)

for some constant

and
\epsilon_{n}
that we can tune,
then by recursion, we can show that by using (
35
) the convergence holds true.

Remark
.

We point out that the difficulty lies in showing (
36
).
Recall that the distance between two random measures is defined in (
27
) which involves testing the overall measurable function bounded by
1
. However, we will see later that it is more desirable to test against Lipschitz functions. Hence, since the underlying measure is a finite Borel probability measure, we want to identify the function first with a continuous function on a compact set (Lusin). Then we approximate this continuous function uniformly by a Lipschitz function since now the domain is compact. This way, we can roughly show that a form close to (
36
) is true.

Remark
.

Lemma

Next, we want to show that by moving forward in one step, the distance between the true distribution of the state and the estimated distribution through the SGD-particle filter framework is bounded by the distance of the previous step with some constants in Lemma

.

Lemma 3.4
.

For each
n=0,1,...,N-1
, there exist
M_{n},L_{n},\delta_{n},K_{n}
such that the following inequality holds

\displaystyle d(\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n+1},\mu_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa^{-2}\Big((1+C\Delta tL_{n}M_{n})d(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})+\frac{C}{M_{n}}+\frac{CM_{n}}{K_{n}}+2\delta_{n}+\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}})

(73)

Proof.

The key step is to estimate the quantity
d^{2}(P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},P_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n})
in (
35
).
WLOG, we assume that the sup is realized by the function
f
with
||f||_{\infty}\leq 1
, then we have

\displaystyle d^{2}(P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n},P_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n})=\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[|P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n}f-P_{n}\mu^{N,\cdot}_{n}f|^{2}]

(74)

Notice that
P^{N}_{n}
is the prediction operator that uses the control
u_{n}
which carries the randomness from SGD, and
P_{n}
uses the control
u^{*}_{n}
. Then
P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\omega}_{N}
is a random measure. And we comment that both
u^{*}_{n}
and
\mu_{n}
are deterministic.

Without loss of generality, we use
u_{n}^{\omega}
and
\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}
to denote the random control and the random measure. (Even though the randomness can be different, we can concatenate
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):=\omega
to define them as
\omega
in general.)

We have for the fixed randomness
\omega
, and by Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle|P^{N}_{n}\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}f-P_{n}\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}f|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbb{E}[\underbrace{f(X_{n}+b(X_{n},u^{\omega}_{n})\Delta t+\sigma(X_{n})\Delta W_{n})}_{f^{\omega}_{1}}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbb{E}[\underbrace{f(X_{n}+b(X_{n},u^{*}_{n})\Delta t+\sigma(X_{n})\Delta W_{n})}_{f_{2}}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]\bigg|^{2}

(75)

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbb{E}[f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]\bigg|^{2}}_{{A_{1}}}+\underbrace{\bigg|\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{c}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]\bigg|^{2}}_{{A_{2}}}

(76)

where the inner conditional expectation is taken with respect to
\Delta W_{n}
.

Now, since we can pick
\mathcal{M}_{n}
to be a large compact set containing the origin, with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sup_{n,U_{0}}|X_{n}|\geq\textit{diam}(\mathcal{M}_{n}))\leq\frac{C}{M_{n}^{2}}

(77)

To deal with
A_{1},A_{2}
, we see that it is desirable that the function

. However, it is only in general measurable. The strategy to overcome this difficulty is to first use the

to find a continuous identification
\tilde{f}
with

\tilde{f}
uniformly by a Lipchitz function.

We see that

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}|^{2}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]

(78)

Then, by taking expectation on both side over all the randomness in this quantity, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[A_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}|^{2}\big]\Big]

(79)

We know that there exists a big compact
\mathcal{K}_{n}
(so a large

) containing the origin such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sup_{n,U_{0}}|X_{n}|\geq\textit{diam}(\mathcal{K}_{n}))\leq\frac{C}{M_{n}^{2}}

(80)

and a continuous
\tilde{f}^{n}

.

And so we know that
\tilde{f}^{n}|_{\mathcal{K}_{n}\cap\mathcal{M}_{n}}=f|_{\mathcal{K}_{n}\cap\mathcal{M}_{n}}
.
And we also have the following inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}|^{2}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\cap\mathcal{K}_{n}}+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\cap\mathcal{K}^{c}_{n}})\mathbb{E}[|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}|^{2}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}_{n}\cap\mathcal{K}_{n}}+\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}^{c}_{n}})\mathbb{E}[|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}|^{2}\big]\Big]

Also, since both

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\Big[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{X\in\mathcal{K}^{c}_{n}\}}]\Big]\leq\frac{C}{M^{2}_{n}}

Hence, we have that

(
82

\displaystyle\leq E^{\omega}\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}|^{2}\big]\Big]+\frac{C}{M_{n}^{2}}

To deal with

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{M}^{c}_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\big|f^{\omega}_{1}-f_{2}\big|^{2}\big|X_{n}=x]\Big]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[A_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}^{c}_{n}}]]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{M^{2}_{n}}

(85)

by Lemma
3.2
.

To deal with
A_{1}
, we have by the density of the Lipchitz function there exists

||f^{n}-\tilde{f}^{n}||_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{n},\infty}\leq\delta_{n}
with Lipschitz constant

. We point out that
L_{n}
may depend on
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{n}
,
\delta_{n}
and the function

Now by taking the expectation on both sides and using the Lipchitz property, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[A_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq(C\Delta tL_{n})^{2}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}^{\omega}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{n}}|u^{\omega}_{n}-u^{*}_{n}|^{2}\big|X_{n}=x\Big]\Bigg]}_{{\ast}}+\frac{C}{M^{2}_{n}}+4\delta^{2}_{n}

We realize that
\ast

\mathbb{E}^{\mu^{N,\omega}_{n}}[\cdot]
mean that given the initial condition

, with

, one wants to find the difference in expectation between

Now, by using (

, absorbing N in the constant C, we obtain the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[A_{1}]\leq(C\Delta tL_{n})^{2}NM_{n}^{2}\ \sup_{||q||\leq 1}\mathbb{E}^{\omega}[|\ \mu^{N,\omega}_{n}q-\mu_{n}q\ |^{2}]+\frac{CM_{n}^{2}}{K_{n}}+\frac{C}{M_{n}^{2}}+4\delta_{n}^{2}

By definition of the distance between two random measures, we have that :

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq(C\Delta tL_{n})^{2}NM_{n}^{2}d^{2}(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})+\frac{CM_{n}^{2}}{K_{n}}+\frac{C}{M_{n}^{2}}+4\delta_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[A_{1}]}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta tL_{n}M_{n}d(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})+\frac{CM_{n}}{\sqrt{K_{n}}}+\frac{C}{M_{n}}+2\delta_{n}

Since

, we have that

(
35
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa^{-2}\Big(\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}+C\Delta tL_{n}M_{n}d(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})+\frac{CM_{n}}{\sqrt{K_{n}}}+\frac{C}{M_{n}}+2\delta_{n}+\frac{2}{M_{n}}+d(\mu_{n}^{N,\cdot},\mu_{n})\Big)

into

Remark
.

Lusin’s theorem requires the underlying measure to be finite Borel regular, and in this case we are looking at the measure
\tilde{\mu}
defined as follows: for
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\tilde{\mu}(A)=\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\Big|\ there\;exist\;n,U_{0}

such\;that\;X_{n}(\omega)\in A\})
.
\tilde{\mu}
is clearly a probability measure induced on the Polish space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and so it is tight by the inverse implication of the Prokhorov’s theorem (or we can use the fact that all finite Borel measures defined on a complete metric space is tight). And so it is inner regular; since now
\tilde{\mu}
is also clearly locally finite, it also implies the outer regularity.

Finally, we can use Lemma
3.4
repeatedly to show the convergence result:

Theorem 3.5
.

By taking
\mu^{N}_{0}=\mu^{0}
, there exist
\{M_{n}|M_{n}\in\mathbb{R},n=0,1,..N-1\}
,
\{L_{n}|L_{n}\in\mathbb{R},n=0,1,..N-1\}
and
\{\delta_{n}|\delta_{n}\in\mathbb{R},n=0,1,..N-1\}
such that

\displaystyle d(\mu^{N,\cdot}_{N},\mu_{N})

\displaystyle\leq{\sum}^{N-1}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}\bigg(\frac{C}{M_{N-i}}+2\delta_{N-i}+\frac{CM_{N-i}}{\sqrt{K_{N-i}}}+\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}\bigg)

(91)

where
C_{j}:=1+C\Delta tL_{j}M_{j}
.

Then, for any
M>0
, we have by picking
\{M_{n}\}
,
\{K_{n}\}
,
\mathcal{N}
large enough and
\{\delta_{n}\}
small enough, then the following hold

\displaystyle d(\mu^{N,\cdot}_{N},\mu_{N})\leq\frac{C}{M}

(92)

for some fixed constant
C
which depend only on
\kappa
.

Proof.

With
C_{n}
defined as

\displaystyle C_{n}:=1+C\Delta tL_{n}M_{n}

(93)

And by using (
90
) repeatedly, we obtain the following result:

\displaystyle d(\mu^{N,\cdot}_{N},\mu_{N})

\displaystyle\leq{\sum}^{N-1}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}(\frac{C}{M_{N-i}}+\frac{CM_{N-i}}{K_{N-i}}+2\delta_{N-i}+\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}})+(2\kappa^{-2})^{N}\prod^{N-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}d(\mu^{N}_{0},\mu_{0})

(94)

\displaystyle\leq{\sum}^{N-1}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}(\frac{C}{M_{N-i}}+\frac{CM_{N-i}}{K_{N-i}}+2\delta_{N-i}+\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}})

(95)

Since we know that
d(\mu^{N}_{0},\mu_{0})=0
.
Now, we just need to show that (
95
) vanishes when
K_{l},N
gets large and
\delta_{i}
gets small,
i\in\{0,1,...,N\}
.
Notice that
M_{l}
comes from the domain truncation for each time step and
\delta_{l}
comes from the uniform approximation which are free to choose. The choice of
\delta_{l}
will potentially determine the value of
L_{n}
.

We fix
M_{N}:=NM,\delta_{N}:=\frac{1}{NM}
where
N
is the number of time discretization and
M
is potentially a large number.

Then, we define
\delta_{l}
,
M_{l}
through the following:

\displaystyle(2\kappa^{-2})^{i+1}\prod^{i}_{j=0}C_{N-j}2\delta_{N-i-1}

\displaystyle=(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}2\delta_{N-i}

(96)

\displaystyle(2\kappa^{-2})^{i+1}\prod^{i}_{j=0}C_{N-j}\frac{C}{M_{N-i-1}}

\displaystyle=(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}\frac{C}{M_{N-i}}

(97)

Here we define
C_{N+1}\equiv 1
.

Notice that should iterate (
96
) and (
97
) iteratively, since defining
\delta_{i}
will lead to the lipschitz constant
L_{i}
at stage
i
, which is needed for the definition for
C_{i}
.

Then we have that

\displaystyle{\sum}^{N-1}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}\frac{C}{M_{n-i}}

\displaystyle\leq N\frac{C}{NM}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{M}

(98)

And we also have

\displaystyle{\sum}^{N-1}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}2\delta_{n-i}

\displaystyle\leq N\frac{1}{NM}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}

(99)

By picking
K_{N-i}
to be large, we then can have

\displaystyle{\sum}^{N-1}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j}\frac{CM_{N-i}}{K_{N-i}}

\displaystyle\leq N\frac{1}{NM}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}

(100)

Last but not least, by taking
\mathcal{N}
so large such that

\displaystyle({\sum}^{N-i}_{i=0}(2\kappa^{-2})^{i}\prod^{i-1}_{j=0}C_{N-j})\frac{3}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}}}\leq\frac{1}{M}

(101)

we can see that (
95
) converges to 0 by taking
M
to be very large.
∎

Remark
.

Notice that in Theorem

4
Numerical example

In this section, we carry out two numerical examples. In the first example, we consider a classic linear quadratic optimal control problem, in which the optimal control can be derived analytically. We use this example as a benchmark example to show the baseline performance and the convergence trend of our algorithm. In the second example, we solve a more practical Dubins vehicle maneuvering problem, and we design control actions based on bearing angels to let the target vehicle follow a pre-designed path.

4.1
Example 1. Linear quadratic control problem with nonlinear observations

Assume B, K are symmetric, positive definite.
The forward process
Y
and the observation process
M
is given by

\displaystyle dY(t)

\displaystyle=A(u(t)-r(t))dt+\sigma Bu(t)dW_{t}

(102)

\displaystyle dM(t)

\displaystyle=\sin(Y(t))dt+dB_{t}

The cost functional is given by

J[u]=\frac{1}{2}E\left[\int_{0}^{T}\langle R(Y_{t}-Y^{\ast}_{t}),(Y_{t}-Y^{\ast}_{t})\rangle dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\langle Ku_{t},u_{t}\rangle dt+\frac{1}{2}\langle QY_{T},Y_{T}\rangle\right]

(103)

we want to find
J(u^{\ast})=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{ad}[0,T]}J(u)
.

4.1.1
Experimental design

An interesting fact of such example is that one can construct a time deterministic exact solution which depend only on
x_{0}
.

By simplifying (
103
), we have

J[u]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(RE[Y_{t}^{T}Y_{t}]-2RY^{\ast T}_{t}E[Y_{t}]+RY^{\ast T}Y^{\ast}+\langle Ku_{t},u_{t}\rangle dt)+\frac{1}{2}E[\langle QY_{T},Y_{T}\rangle]

(104)

Then, we define:

\displaystyle X_{t}

\displaystyle=E[Y_{t}]=E[Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}A(u(s)-r(s))ds+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma Bu(s)dW_{s}]

(105)

\displaystyle=E[Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}A(u(s)-r(s)ds]

Hence, we see that

\displaystyle E[Y_{t}^{T}Y_{t}]

\displaystyle=E[(Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}A(u(s)-r(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma Bu(s)dW_{s})^{2}]

(106)

\displaystyle=E[Y_{0}^{T}Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(u(s)-r(s))^{T}A^{T}A(u(s)-r(s)ds+Y_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}A(u(s)-r(s))ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}(u(s)-r(s))^{T}A^{T}Y_{0}ds]+E[\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}u(s)^{T}B^{T}Bu(s)ds]

\displaystyle=X_{t}^{T}X_{t}+\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{t}u(s)^{T}B^{T}Bu(s)ds

And (
106
) is true because all the terms are deterministic in time given
x_{0}
. Also, we observe that

\displaystyle E[Y_{T}^{T}Y_{T}]

\displaystyle=E[(Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}A(u(s)-r(s))ds+\int_{0}^{T}\sigma Bu(s)dW_{s})^{2}]

(107)

\displaystyle=X_{T}^{T}X_{T}+\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{T}u(s)^{T}B^{T}Bu(s)ds

As a result, we see that now (
104
) takes the form:

\displaystyle J[u]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(X_{s}^{T}RX_{s}-2X_{s}^{T}RX_{s}^{\ast}+X_{s}^{\ast T}RX_{s}^{\ast}+u_{s}^{T}(\sigma^{2}B^{T}QB+K)u_{s})ds

(108)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}^{T}B^{T}RBu_{s}dsdt+\frac{1}{2}X_{T}^{T}QX_{T}

by doing a simple integration by part, we have

\displaystyle J[u]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(X_{s}^{T}RX_{s}-2X_{s}^{T}RX_{s}^{\ast}+X_{s}^{\ast T}RX_{s}^{\ast}+u_{s}^{T}(\sigma^{2}B^{T}QB+K)u_{s})ds

(109)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{T}(T-t)u_{s}^{T}B^{T}RBu_{s}ds+\frac{1}{2}X_{T}^{T}QX_{T}

As a result, we have the following standard deterministic control problem:

\displaystyle J[u]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\underbrace{(X_{s}^{T}RX_{s}-2X_{s}^{T}RX_{s}^{\ast}+X_{s}^{\ast T}RX_{s}^{\ast}+u_{s}^{T}(\sigma^{2}B^{T}QB+K)u_{s}+\sigma^{2}(T-t)u_{s}^{T}B^{T}RBu_{s})}_{2f}ds

(110)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}X_{T}^{T}QX_{T}

\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}=\underbrace{A(u(t)-r(t))}_{b},X_{t_{0}}=X_{0}

(111)

Then, one can form the following Hamiltonian

H(x,p,u)=bp+f

(112)

where
p
is
\nabla_{x}v
,
v
is the value function.

Then, to find the optimal control, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial u}H=0

(113)

which is

\displaystyle Ap+u(\sigma^{2}B^{T}RB(T-t)+(K+\sigma^{2}B^{T}QB))=0

(114)

So, we got

\displaystyle u_{t}=\frac{-Ap(t)}{\sigma^{2}B^{T}RB(T-t)+(K+\sigma^{2}B^{T}QB)}

(115)

Also, notice that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}p(t)=-R(X_{t}-X_{t}^{\ast}),\;\;\;\;p(T)=QX_{T}

(116)

then,

\displaystyle p(t)=QX_{T}+\int_{0}^{T}R(X_{s}-X_{s}^{\ast})ds

(117)

Combine (
111
),(
115
),(
117
) together, we can solve the control of the system.

Then, set

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.2&0.2&0.2\\
0.2&1&0.2&0.2\\
0.2&0.2&1&0.2\\
0.2&0.2&0.2&1\end{bmatrix}

(118)

Set B, R, K ,Q be identity matrices. With
X_{0}=0
, we have the following solution according to this setup.

To solve (
117
), let

\displaystyle X_{t}^{1}-X_{t}^{\ast 1}

\displaystyle:=t

\displaystyle X_{t}^{2}-X_{t}^{\ast 2}

\displaystyle:=cos(t)

\displaystyle X_{t}^{3}-X_{t}^{\ast 3}

\displaystyle:=t^{2}

\displaystyle X_{t}^{4}-X_{t}^{\ast 4}

\displaystyle:=-2\pi sin(2\pi t)

Then we have

\displaystyle p(t)=\begin{bmatrix}X_{t}^{1}\\
X_{t}^{2}\\
X_{t}^{3}\\
X_{t}^{4}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\frac{T^{2}}{2}-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\\
sin(T)-sin(t)\\
\frac{T^{3}}{3}-\frac{t^{3}}{3}\\
cos(2\pi T)-cos(2\pi t)\end{bmatrix}

(119)

Let
\hat{r}(t)
as

\hat{r}_{t}^{1}=\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\beta_{t}},\quad\hat{r}_{t}^{2}=\frac{-sin(t)}{\beta_{t}},\quad\hat{r}_{t}^{3}=\frac{-t^{3}}{3\beta_{t}},\quad\hat{r}_{t}^{4}=\frac{-cos(2\pi t)}{\beta_{t}}

where
\beta_{t}=(1+\sigma^{2})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)
.
then

r(t)=A\hat{r}(t)

Then, plug (
119
) into (
115
), then we solve (
111
)

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=A(u(t)-r(t))=A(-\frac{Ap(t)}{\beta_{t}}-A\hat{r}(t)))

(120)

we get

\displaystyle X_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{t}^{1}\\
X_{t}^{2}\\
X_{t}^{3}\\
X_{t}^{4}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\alpha_{t}A^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{T^{2}}{2}\\
sin(T)\\
\frac{T^{3}}{3}\\
cos(2\pi T)\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}X_{T}^{1}\\
X_{T}^{2}\\
X_{T}^{3}\\
X_{T}^{4}\end{bmatrix})

(121)

So, replace
X_{t}
with
X_{t}^{\ast}
, we get

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\ast}=\begin{bmatrix}X_{t}^{1,\ast}\\
X_{t}^{2,\ast}\\
X_{t}^{3,\ast}\\
X_{t}^{4,\ast}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}t\\
cos(t)\\
t^{2}\\
-2\pi sin(2\pi t)\end{bmatrix}+\frac{\alpha_{t}A^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{T^{2}}{2}\\
sin(T)\\
\frac{T^{3}}{3}\\
cos(2\pi T)\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}X_{T}^{1}\\
X_{T}^{2}\\
X_{T}^{3}\\
X_{T}^{4}\end{bmatrix})

(122)

where
X_{T}^{i}
can be obtained from the system (
121
) by letting
t=T
, and

\alpha_{t}=ln\frac{1+\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}T}{(1+\sigma^{2})+\sigma^{2}(T-t)}

Then, to find an the exact form by following the trajectory of
y_{t}
in this setup, one will have to solve the following Coupled forward-backward ODE.

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=A(u_{t}-r_{t})\ \ \ \ \ x_{n}=y_{t_{n}}

(123)

\displaystyle\frac{dp(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=X_{t}-X^{*}_{t},\ \ \ p_{T}=x^{t_{n},y_{t_{n}}}_{T}

(124)

with
u_{t}=-Ap_{t}\big/\big(\sigma^{2}(T-t)+(1+\sigma^{2})\big)
.
As a result, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=A(-Ap_{t}\big/\big(\sigma^{2}(T-t)+(1+\sigma^{2})\big)-r_{t})\ \ \ \ \ x_{n}=y_{t_{n}}

(125)

\displaystyle\frac{dp(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=X_{t}-X^{*}_{t},\ \ \ p_{T}=x^{t_{n},y_{t_{n}}}_{T}

(126)

That is, we need to solve the above coupled FBODE. Then, seeing that
p_{t}=x^{t_{n},y_{t_{n}}}_{T}+\int^{T}_{t_{n}}X_{s}-X^{*}_{s}ds
. Writing
a_{t}:=1/\big(\sigma^{2}(T-t)+(1+\sigma^{2})\big)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=A\big(-a_{t}AX_{T}-a_{t}A\int^{T}_{t_{n}}(X_{s}-X^{*}_{s})ds-r_{t}\big),\ \ \ \ \ x_{t_{n}}=y_{t_{n}}

(127)

To solve (
127
) numerically, we do a numerical discretization:

\displaystyle x_{t_{n+1}}-x_{t_{n}}

\displaystyle=-a_{t_{n}}A^{2}X_{T}\Delta t-a_{t_{n}}(\Delta t)^{2}A^{2}\sum^{N-1}_{i=n}(X_{t_{i}}-X^{*}_{t_{i}})-Ar_{t}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Ar_{t}-a_{t_{n}}(\Delta t)^{2}A^{2}\sum^{N-1}_{i=n}X^{*}_{t_{i}}

\displaystyle=X_{t_{n}}-X_{t_{n+1}}-a_{t_{n}}(\Delta t)^{2}A^{2}\sum^{N-1}_{i=n}X_{t_{i}}-a_{t_{n}}A^{2}X_{T},\ \ \ x_{t_{n}}=y_{t_{n}}

(128)

We can put (
128
) into a large linear system, and solve it numerically.

Example 2. 2D Dubins vehicle maneuvering problem

In this example, we solve a Dubins vehicle maneuvering problem. The controlled
process is described by the following nonlinear controlled dynamics:

\displaystyle dS_{t}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}dX_{t}\\
dY_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sin(\theta_{t})dt\\
\cos(\theta_{t})dt\end{bmatrix}+\sigma dW_{t}

(129)

\displaystyle d\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=u_{t}dt+\sigma^{2}dW_{t}

(130)

\displaystyle dM_{t}

\displaystyle=[\arctan(\frac{X_{t}+1}{Y_{t}-1}),\arctan(\frac{X_{t}-2}{Y_{t}-1})]^{T}+\eta_{t}

(131)

where the pair
(X,Y)
gives the position of a car-like robot moving in the 2D plane,
\theta
is
the steering angle that controls the moving direction of the robot, which is governed by the
control action
u_{t}
,
\sigma
is the noise that perturbs the motion and control actions. Assume that we do not have direct observations on the robot. Instead, we use two detectors
located on different observation platforms at
(-1,1)
and
(2,1)
to collect bearing angles of
the target robot as indirect observations. So, we have the observation process
M_{t}
Given the expected path
S^{\ast}
, the car should follow it and arrive the terminal position on time. The performance cost functional based on observational
data that we aim to minimize is defined as:

J[u]=E\left[\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\langle R(S_{t}-S^{\ast}_{t}),(S_{t}-S^{\ast}_{t})\rangle dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\langle Ku_{t},u_{t}\rangle dt+\langle Q(S_{T}-S^{\ast}_{T}),(S_{T}-S^{\ast}_{T})\rangle\right]

(132)

In our numerical experiments, we let the car start from
(X_{0},Y_{0})=(0,0)
to
(X_{T},Y_{T})=(1,1)
. The expected path
S^{\ast}_{t}
is
X_{t}^{2}+Y_{t}^{2}=1
. Other settings are
T=1
,
\Delta t=0.02
i.e.
N_{T}=50
,
\sigma=0.1
,
\eta_{t}\sim N(0,0.1)
,
L=1000
,
K=1
and the initial heading direction is
\pi/2
. To emphasize the importance of following the expected path and arriving at the target location at the terminal time, let
R=Q=20

Lemma 5.1
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[||j^{\prime x}(U_{n})||^{2}]\leq C|x|^{2}+C

Recall the definition of

\displaystyle||j^{\prime x}(U_{n})||^{2}

taking expectation on both side and use Lemma 3.3 in
[
4
]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[||j^{\prime x}(U_{n})||^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}[\sup_{j\in\{n,...,N\}}|Y_{j}^{x}|^{2}]+C

\displaystyle\leq C|x|^{2}+C

(135)

where (
5.1
) follows again from estimates
Theorem 4.2.1
and
Theorem 5.3.3
in

31
]
