A Tutorial on Statistical Models Based on Counting Processes
Elvis Han Cui elviscuihan@g.ucla.edu

Abstract
Since the famous paper written by Kaplan and Meier 1958 , survival analysis has become one of the most important fields in statistics. Nowadays it is one of the most important statistical tools in analyzing epidemiological and clinical data including COVID-19 pandemic ( Salinas-Escudero et al. 2020 ) . This article reviews some of the most celebrated and important results and methods, including consistency, asymptotic normality, bias and variance estimation, in survival analysis and the treatment is parallel to the monograph statistical models based on counting processes ( Andersen et al. 1993 ) . Other models and results such as semi-Markov models and the Turnbull’s estimator that jump out of the classical counting process martingale framework are also discussed.

Introduction

Survival analysis, a cornerstone of statistical methodology, has significantly evolved since its inception. Initially introduced through seminal works like the Kaplan-Meier estimator, this field has expanded its scope to address increasingly complex time-to-event data challenges. Applications span a wide range of disciplines, including medicine, epidemiology, engineering, and social sciences, underscoring its interdisciplinary importance.

At the heart of survival analysis lies the interplay between stochastic processes and inferential techniques. Modern approaches leverage the mathematical rigor of counting processes and martingale theory to derive powerful estimators, such as the Nelson-Aalen and Kaplan-Meier estimators. These methods provide robust tools for estimating survival probabilities, hazard rates, and transition intensities, even under random censorship. Moreover, extensions to multivariate and bivariate survival data have led to breakthroughs, including the Dabrowska estimator and Aalen-Johansen framework, broadening the applicability of survival analysis in longitudinal and competing risks studies.

This manuscript offers a detailed exploration of classical and contemporary techniques in survival analysis. We build upon the foundational concepts while incorporating modern advancements, such as simultaneous confidence bands, asymptotic normality, and the Brookmeyer-Crowley method for quantile estimation. Furthermore, the text provides historical insights into the development of these methods and their connections to broader statistical principles.

Concluding Remarks

In this manuscript, we have systematically reviewed key concepts in survival analysis, from univariate methods like the Nelson-Aalen and Kaplan-Meier estimators to multivariate extensions, including the Dabrowska and Aalen-Johansen frameworks. We highlighted their theoretical underpinnings, variance estimators, and applications in real-world scenarios. By synthesizing classical results with recent advancements, this work serves as a comprehensive resource for researchers and practitioners seeking to understand and apply survival analysis techniques in diverse fields. This paper bridges historical milestones and future directions, demonstrating the field’s enduring relevance and innovation.

Nonparametric Estimation

Nonparametric estimation is a critical aspect of survival analysis, as it enables the estimation of hazard rates and cumulative hazard functions without relying on restrictive parametric assumptions. Among the prominent nonparametric methods, the
Nelson-Aalen estimator
is a widely utilized technique for estimating the cumulative hazard function. This section provides an in-depth overview of the Nelson-Aalen estimator, including its derivation, variance estimation, bias, consistency, asymptotic normality, and simultaneous confidence bands.

The Nelson-Aalen estimator is based on modeling the counting process of events, leveraging the relationship between hazard functions and cumulative hazard measures. By employing martingale theory, this method allows for efficient and unbiased estimation, making it particularly useful in censored survival data scenarios often encountered in medical and biostatistical applications.

1.1
The Nelson-Aalen Estimator

Suppose within a small interval
[t,t+dt)
, there is a probability
\alpha(t)dt
of an event (e.g., death) occurring. Thus, the expected number of events in this interval is
Y(t)\alpha(t)dt
, where
Y(t)=\sum_{i}\mathbb{I}(T_{i}\geq t)
counts the number of individuals at risk at time
t
. This relationship suggests the decomposition of the counting process:

dN(t)=Y(t)\alpha(t)dt+dM(t),

where the first term is predictable, and the second term represents the increment of a martingale. Rearranging terms gives:

\frac{dN(t)}{Y(t)}-\frac{dM(t)}{Y(t)}=\alpha(t)dt.

This leads to the following estimator for
A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)ds
:

\widehat{A}(t)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}dN(s),

where
J(s)=\mathbb{I}(Y(s)>0)
ensures that
Y(s)>0
is accounted for, and
0/0=0
. This is the **Nelson-Aalen estimator** for the cumulative hazard function.

1.1.1
Variance Estimator

To estimate the variance of
\widehat{A}(t)
, let
A^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}J(s)\alpha(s)ds
and
A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)ds
. The difference
(\widehat{A}-A^{*})(t)
can be expressed as:

(\widehat{A}-A^{*})(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}\left(dN(s)-Y(s)\alpha(s)ds\right).

Since
dN(s)-Y(s)\alpha(s)ds=dM(s)
, where
M(\cdot)
is the counting process martingale, it follows that
\widehat{A}-A^{*}
is a mean-zero martingale. A natural variance estimator is obtained by plugging in the Nelson-Aalen estimator into the compensator representation:

\langle\widehat{A}-A^{*}\rangle(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}dA(s).

The corresponding variance estimator is:

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)^{2}}dN(s).

1.1.2
Bias

The expected value of the Nelson-Aalen estimator can be derived as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{A}(t)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}(Y(s)dA(s)+dM(s))\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[J(s)]dA(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(Y(s)>0)dA(s).

Thus, the bias of the Nelson-Aalen estimator is:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{A}(t)]-A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}(Y(s)=0)\alpha(s)ds.

1.1.3
Consistency

Theorem 1

(Consistency of the Nelson-Aalen Estimator)
.

Suppose
\inf_{s\in[0,t]}Y(s)\to_{p}\infty\text{ as }n\to\infty
, and assume that:

A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)ds<\infty,

then:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\widehat{A}(s)-A(s)|>\epsilon\right)=0,

where
\epsilon>0
and
\widehat{A}
is the Nelson-Aalen estimator.

Proof.

We have
(\widehat{A}-A^{*})(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}dM(s)
, which is a martingale. By Lenglart’s inequality:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\widehat{A}-A^{*}|(t)>\eta\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{\eta^{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left(\langle\widehat{A}-A^{*}\rangle(t)>\delta\right),

for any small positive
\eta
and
\delta
. The compensator
\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}dA(s)
converges to zero in probability by the dominated convergence theorem. Furthermore:

\sup_{s}|A^{*}(s)-A(s)|=\sup_{s}\left|\int_{0}^{s}(J(u)-1)\alpha(u)du\right|,

and since the integrand is bounded by
\alpha(u)
, Gill’s lemma applies.
∎

1.1.4
Asymptotic Normality

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic Normality of the Nelson-Aalen Estimator)
.

Suppose the cumulative distribution function
F(s)
of
T_{i}
satisfies
F(s)<1
for
s\leq t
, and:

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|\frac{1}{n}Y(s)-1+F(s)\right|\to_{p}0,

then:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{A}-A)\to_{d}U,

where
U
is a Gaussian martingale with
U(0)=0
and covariance structure:

\text{cov}(U(s),U(t))=\sigma(s\wedge t),

where:

\sigma^{2}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\alpha(u)}{1-F(u-)}du.

In the case of independent censoring, where both survival time
X_{i}
and censoring time
U_{i}
are absolutely continuous, the variance simplifies to:

\sigma^{2}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{p(u)}{S(u)^{2}G(u)}du,

where
p
is the density of
X
, and
S
and
G
are the survival functions of
X
and
U
, respectively.

1.1.5
Simultaneous Confidence Bands

Simultaneous confidence bands are essential tools for assessing the uncertainty of the Nelson-Aalen estimator over an interval. Let
q
be a continuous and non-negative function on
[s,t]
, where
0\leq s<t\leq\tau
. The following asymptotic result holds:

\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}(\widehat{A}-A)}{1+n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}\right)q\circ\left(\frac{n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}{1+n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\left(\frac{U}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)q\circ\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right),

on
\mathcal{D}[s,t]
, where
U
is a Gaussian martingale with predictable variation, and:

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)^{2}}dN(s),

is the predictable variation of the martingale
\widehat{A}-\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)J(s)ds
.

The processes
\left(\frac{U}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)q\circ\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)
and
(qW^{0})\circ\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)
share the same distribution, where
W^{0}
is a standard Brownian bridge. This connection enables the construction of simultaneous confidence bands.

The
100(1-\alpha)\%
confidence band for
A
on
[s,t]
is given by:

\widehat{A}(s)\pm K_{q,\alpha}(c_{1},c_{2})\frac{1+n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(s)}{\sqrt{n}q\left(\frac{n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(s)}{1+n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(s)}\right)},

where
K_{q,\alpha}(c_{1},c_{2})
represents the upper
\alpha
-percentile of the distribution:

\sup_{x\in[c_{1},c_{2}]}\left|q(x)W^{0}(x)\right|.

Common Choices
for
q(x)
:

•

Equal Precision (EP) Band
:

q(x)=x(1-x)
, which ensures equal precision across the range.

•

Hall-Wellner (HW) Band
:

q(x)=x
, which provides a more straightforward interpretation in some applications.

These bands allow for simultaneous inference about the cumulative hazard function
A(t)
over the interval of interest, accounting for variability and uncertainty in the estimation process.

1.2
The Kaplan-Meier (KM) Estimator

The Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimator is a widely used nonparametric estimator of the survival function in the presence of censoring. It provides an intuitive way to estimate the probability of survival beyond a given time while accommodating right-censored data. The Kaplan-Meier estimator is constructed as a product of conditional survival probabilities, which accounts for the number of individuals at risk and the observed events over time.

Definition 3

(Kaplan-Meier (KM) Estimator)
.

Let
\widehat{A}
denote the Nelson-Aalen estimator for the integrated hazard rate function. The Kaplan-Meier estimator for the survival function is defined as:

\widehat{S}(t)=\Prodi_{s\leq t}(1-d\widehat{A}(s)),

which can also be expressed as:

\widehat{S}(t)=\prod_{s\leq t}\left(1-\frac{N(\Delta s)}{Y(s)}\right),

where
N(\Delta s)
represents the number of events (e.g., deaths) at time
s
, and
Y(s)
denotes the number of individuals at risk at time
s
.

1.2.1
Variance Estimation: Greenwood’s Formula

Variance estimation for the Kaplan-Meier estimator is a critical step in constructing confidence intervals for the survival function. Using martingale theory, we can derive the variance of the Kaplan-Meier estimator. Let
J(t)=\mathbb{I}(Y(t)>0)
, and define
A^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}J(u)dA(u)
, where
A(u)
may exhibit jumps. The Kaplan-Meier estimator can be expressed as:

\widehat{S}(t)=\Prodi_{0\leq s\leq t}(1-dA^{*}(s)).

Using the Duhamel equation and Doob-Meyer decomposition for stochastic integration, we have the following mean-zero martingale:

\frac{\widehat{S}(t)}{S^{*}(t)}-1=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\widehat{S}(s-)}{S^{*}(s)}dM(s),

where
M(s)
is a martingale and
S^{*}(t)
is the deterministic part of the survival function. The compensator of this martingale is given by:

\left\langle\frac{\widehat{S}(t)}{S^{*}(t)}-1\right\rangle(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left\{\frac{\widehat{S}(s-)}{S^{*}(s)}\right\}^{2}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}(1-\Delta A(s))dA(s).

Plugging in
S^{*}(t)=\widehat{S}(t)
, the variance estimator simplifies to:

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{Y(s)(Y(s)-\Delta N(s))}dN(s).

Thus, the pointwise variance of the Kaplan-Meier estimator is:

\widehat{\text{Var}}(\widehat{S}(t))=(\widehat{S}(t))^{2}\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t).

This result is commonly referred to as
Greenwood’s formula
and is widely used in practical applications.

1.2.2
Bias

Under the assumption of random censorship, where
X
(survival time) and
U
(censoring time) are independent with distribution functions
1-S(t)
and
G(t)
, respectively, the bias of the Kaplan-Meier estimator can be bounded. Specifically, we have:

0\leq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{S}(t)]-S(t)\leq(1-S(t))\left\{1-S(t)(1-G(t))\right\}^{n},

where
n
is the sample size. If
G(t)
is continuous, then:

0\leq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{S}(t)]-S(t)\leq(1-S(t))\exp\{-\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\},

where
Y(t)
represents the risk process. This shows that the bias decreases as the sample size increases.

1.2.3
Consistency

Theorem 4

(Consistency of the KM Estimator)
.

Assume
\inf_{s\in[0,t]}Y(s)\to_{p}\infty
and
S(t)>0
. Then, as
n\to\infty
:

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\widehat{S}(s)-S(s)|\to_{p}0.

The proof follows similar arguments as for the Nelson-Aalen estimator, with additional applications of the continuity of product integrals and the continuous mapping theorem (see ABGK, p. 114).

1.2.4
Asymptotic Normality

We now consider the asymptotic behavior of the Kaplan-Meier estimator under a random censorship model, where the true survival distribution is
X\sim S(t)
and the censoring distribution is
U\sim G(t)
, with both
X
and
U
absolutely continuous.

Theorem 5

(Asymptotic Normality of the KM Estimator)
.

Let
A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)ds
, where
\alpha(s)=-\frac{dS(s)/ds}{S(s)}
, and define:

\sigma^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha(u)}{S(u)G(u)}du.

Assume:

•

A(t)<\infty
.

•

\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|\frac{1}{n}Y(s)-F(s)G(s)\right|\to_{p}0
, where
Y(s)
is the at-risk process.

Then:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{S}-S)\to_{d}-S\cdot U,

on
\mathcal{D}[0,t]
, where
U
is a Gaussian martingale with
U(0)=0
and covariance:

\text{Cov}(U(s),U(t))=\sigma^{2}(s\wedge t).

Furthermore,
n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(s)
is uniformly weakly consistent on
[0,t]
, where
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t)
is Greenwood’s variance estimator.

Proof.

The proof uses the functional delta method, leveraging the product-integral representation of the survival function:

S(t)=\Prodi_{s\in[0,t]}(1-dA(s)).

By the delta method, the asymptotic distribution of
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{S}-S)
follows from the Nelson-Aalen estimator’s asymptotic normality:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{A}-A)\to_{d}U,

where
U
is a Gaussian martingale. The uniform consistency of
n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(s)
follows from Rebolledo’s martingale central limit theorem.
∎

1.2.5
Simultaneuous Confidence Bands

The previous theorem on AN of KM can be applied to construct CIs for
S(t)
. That is,

\widehat{S}(t)\pm c_{\alpha/2}\widehat{S}(t)\widehat{\sigma}(t)

is a linear asymptotic
100(1-\alpha)\%
interval where
c_{\alpha/2}
is the upper
\alpha/2
quantile of
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. However, this interval is rarely used in practice and transformation is needed. One is the
Kalbfleisch-Prentice’s log-log transformation
, i.e.,
g(S(t))=\log(-\log(S(t)))
and the other is the
Thomas-Grunkemeier’s square root arcsin transformation, i.e.,
g(S(t))=\arcsin(\sqrt{S(t)})
. The usual delta method applies in this case and the pointwise CIs can be derived by reverting the transfomration. One can construct CB from the fact that
U
has the same distribution as
W\circ\sigma
where
W
is the Brownian motion on
[0,t]
. Similar to the CB for NA estimator, one can also use the fact that

\left(\frac{U}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)q\circ\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)=_{d}\left(qW^{0}\right)\circ\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{1+\sigma^{2}}\right)

where
q
is a continuous and non-negative function on
[s,t]
and
W^{0}
is the standard Brownian bridge. Hence, the
100(1-\alpha)\%

equal precision
(EP) band is

\widehat{S}(s)\pm d_{\alpha}(\widehat{c}_{1},\widehat{c}_{2})\widehat{S}(s)\widehat{\sigma}(s)

where for
i=1,2
,

\widehat{c}_{i}=\frac{n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t_{i})}{1+n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(t_{i})}

and
d_{\alpha}(\widehat{c}_{1},\widehat{c}_{2})
is the upper
\alpha
quantile of the distribution of

\sup_{c_{1}\leq x\leq c_{2}}|W^{0}(x)\sqrt{x(1-x)}|.

The
100(1-\alpha)\%

Hall-Wellner
(HW) band is

\widehat{S}(s)\pm\frac{1+n\widehat{\sigma}^{2}(s)}{\sqrt{n}}e_{\alpha}(\widehat{c}_{1},\widehat{c}_{2})\widehat{S}(s)

where
e_{\alpha}(\widehat{c}_{1},\widehat{c}_{2})
is the upper
\alpha
quantile in the distribution of

\sup_{c_{1}\leq x\leq c_{2}}|W^{0}(x)|.

1.2.6
Quantile Estimation: Brookmeyer-Crowley

Quantile estimation plays a vital role in survival analysis, as it provides interpretable summaries of the survival distribution, such as the median survival time or other percentiles. In this subsection, we outline the theoretical framework for quantile estimation based on the Kaplan-Meier estimator and describe the Brookmeyer-Crowley method for constructing confidence intervals.

•

Quantile Function and Asymptotics

Let
Q(p)
denote the quantile function, which is defined as a functional of the distribution function
F
:

Q(p)=\phi(F)=\phi(1-S)=\inf\{x:F(x)\geq p\},

where
S
is the survival function. By applying the **functional delta method**, the asymptotic behavior of the estimated quantile function
\widehat{Q}(p)
can be derived. Specifically, we have:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{Q}(p)-Q(p))\xrightarrow{d}-\frac{(1-p)U(Q(p))}{f(Q(p))},

where
\widehat{Q}(p)=\phi(1-\widehat{S})
,
f
is the density function of
F
, and
U(\cdot)
is a Gaussian martingale. Using the properties of
U(\cdot)
, the limiting distribution is:

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{Q}(p)-Q(p))\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{(1-p)^{2}\sigma^{2}(Q(p))}{f(Q(p))^{2}}\right),

where
\sigma^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha(u)}{S(u)G(u)}du
, as defined in Theorem
(see ABGK, pp. 275–277). This result provides a theoretical basis for constructing confidence intervals for quantiles.

•

Brookmeyer-Crowley Method for Confidence Intervals

In practice, constructing confidence intervals for
Q(p)
can be achieved using the
Brookmeyer-Crowley method
. This approach relies on transforming the Kaplan-Meier survival estimator and solving for the confidence interval bounds. The confidence interval is given by:

\left\{u:\frac{|g(\widehat{S}(u))-g(1-p)|}{|g^{\prime}(\widehat{S}(u))|\widehat{S}(u)\widehat{\sigma}(u)}\leq c_{\alpha/2}\right\},

where:

–

g(\cdot)
is a suitable transformation, such as
\log(-\log(\cdot))
or
\arcsin(\sqrt{\cdot})
, chosen to stabilize variance and improve numerical properties.

–

c_{\alpha/2}
is the
\alpha/2
-quantile of the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

This method ensures robust and interpretable confidence intervals for survival quantiles, even in the presence of censoring.

•

**Variance Estimation of
\widehat{Q}(p)
**

To estimate the variance of
\widehat{Q}(p)
, we need an estimate of the density
f(Q(p))
, which can be obtained by smoothing the Nelson-Aalen estimator. This requires fitting a smoothed survival function and differentiating it to estimate the density at
Q(p)
. See Definition
12
for details on smoothed Nelson-Aalen estimators.

1.3
The Aalen-Johansen (AJ) Estimator

The
Aalen-Johansen estimator
is a nonparametric estimator used to estimate the transition probabilities of a Markov process in survival analysis. It generalizes the Nelson-Aalen estimator to multi-state models, where the transitions between states are modeled using a transition intensity matrix. The AJ estimator is essential in multi-state survival analysis as it provides a robust tool for estimating the transition probabilities between states while accounting for censoring.

Definition 6

(
Aalen-Johansen (AJ) Estimator
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
denote the intensity measure of a Markov process
X
, and let
\mathbf{P}
represent the transition probability matrix. Define:

\widehat{A}_{hj}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J_{h}(s)}{Y_{h}(s)}dN_{hj}(s),

where
\widehat{A}_{hj}(t)
is the Nelson-Aalen estimator for the cumulative hazard
A_{hj}
for
h\neq j
, and:

\widehat{A}_{hh}(t)=\sum_{j\neq h}\widehat{A}_{hj}(t).

Then, the Aalen-Johansen estimator for the transition probability matrix
\mathbf{P}(s,t)
is given by:

\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t)=\Prodi_{(s,t]}\left(\mathbf{I}+d\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(u)\right),

where
\widehat{\mathbf{A}}=\{\widehat{A}_{hj}\}
is the matrix of estimated cumulative hazards, and
\Prodi
denotes the product integral.

1.3.1
Complete Observation

Under the assumption of complete observation (i.e., no censoring), the
Aalen-Johansen estimator
exhibits a simple relationship between the at-risk process and the transition probability matrix.

Lemma 7
.

Suppose no censoring occurs, and the observations are complete for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
. Let
\mathbf{Y}(t)
denote the row vector
(Y_{1}(t),\cdots,Y_{k}(t))
, where
Y_{h}(t)
is the number of individuals at risk in state
h
at time
t
. Then:

\mathbf{Y}(t+)=\mathbf{Y}(s+)\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t),

where
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t)
is the Aalen-Johansen estimator.

Proof.

For any state
h
, the at-risk process evolves as:

Y_{h}(t+)=Y_{h}(0+)+\sum_{j\neq h}N_{jh}(t)-\sum_{j\neq h}N_{hj}(t).

By the definition of the
Aalen-Johansen estimator
:

\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t)=\prod_{s<u\leq t}\left(\mathbf{I}+\Delta\widehat{\mathbf{A}}(u)\right).

Assuming
s=0
and substituting the product integral representation, the result follows from straightforward algebraic manipulation.
∎

1.3.2
Bias and Covariance Estimation

Recall that the cumulative hazard matrix
\mathbf{A}^{*}=\{A_{hj}^{*}\}
is defined as:

A_{hj}^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}J_{h}(u)\alpha_{hj}(u)du,

where
\alpha_{hj}(u)
is the transition intensity. Define the transition matrix:

\mathbf{P}^{*}(s,t)=\Prodi_{(s,t]}\left(\mathbf{I}+d\mathbf{A}^{*}(u)\right).

By the Duhamel equation and boundedness arguments, the following holds:

\mathbf{M}(s,t)=\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t)\mathbf{P}^{*}(s,t)^{-1}-\mathbf{I},

where
\mathbf{M}(s,t)
is a
k\times k
martingale. If
\mathbb{P}(Y_{h}(u)=0)
is small for
u\in(s,t]
, then the AJ estimator is nearly unbiased.

To estimate the covariance of
\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t)
, we can use:

\widehat{\text{Cov}}(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,t))=\int_{s}^{t}\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(u,t)^{T}\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,u)d[\widehat{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{A}^{*}](u)\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(u,t)\otimes\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,u)^{T},

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
represents the quadratic variation of a local square-integrable martingale (see ABGK, pp. 292–293).

1.3.3
Large Sample Properties

The large sample properties of the
Aalen-Johansen estimator
follow naturally from the properties of the Nelson-Aalen estimator.

•

Uniform Consistency:

The uniform consistency of the AJ estimator arises as a direct consequence of the uniform consistency of the Nelson-Aalen estimator and the continuity of the product integral. Specifically:

\sup_{u\in[s,t]}\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,u)-\mathbf{P}(s,u)\rVert\xrightarrow{p}0.

•

Asymptotic Normality:

Using the asymptotic normality of the Nelson-Aalen estimator, the Hadamard differentiability of the product integral, and the functional delta method, we obtain:

\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{P}}(s,\cdot)-\mathbf{P}(s,\cdot)\right)\xrightarrow{d}\int_{s}^{\cdot}\mathbf{P}(s,u)d\mathbf{U}(u)\mathbf{P}(u,\cdot),

where
\mathbf{U}=(U_{hj})
is a
k\times k
matrix-valued process. For
h\neq j
,
U_{hj}
are independent Gaussian martingales with
U_{hj}(0)=0
and:

\text{Cov}(U_{hj}(s),U_{hj}(t))=\sigma^{2}(s\wedge t),

while
U_{hh}=-\sum_{j\neq h}U_{hj}
.

1.4
The Dabrowska Estimator

The Dabrowska estimator is a seminal nonparametric method for estimating the joint survival function of bivariate survival times under censoring. Introduced in the 1980s, it generalizes the Kaplan-Meier estimator to handle the complexities of two-dimensional survival data. This estimator is essential in scenarios where dependencies between survival times need to be accounted for, such as in biomedical studies involving paired organ failure or recurrent events.

1.4.1
Bivariate Survival Times and Cumulative Hazards

Let
T=(T_{1},T_{2})
represent a pair of nonnegative random variables corresponding to two survival times. The joint survival function is defined as:

S(s,t)=\mathbb{P}(T_{1}>s,T_{2}>t),

which captures the probability that both survival times exceed given thresholds
s
and
t
.

Definition 8

(Bivariate Cumulative Hazard)
.

The bivariate cumulative hazard function is defined as:

\Lambda(s,t)=(\Lambda_{10}(s,t),\Lambda_{01}(s,t),\Lambda_{11}(s,t)),

where:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{11}(ds,dt)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\in ds,T_{2}\in dt)}{\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}\geq t)}=\frac{S(ds,dt)}{S(s-,t-)},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{10}(ds,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\in ds,T_{2}>t)}{\mathbb{P}(T_{1}\geq s,T_{2}>t)}=-\frac{S(ds,t)}{S(s-,t)},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{01}(s,dt)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(T_{1}>s,T_{2}\in dt)}{\mathbb{P}(T_{1}>s,T_{2}\geq t)}=-\frac{S(s,dt)}{S(s,t-)}.

These hazard components are subject to the initial conditions:

\Lambda_{10}(0,t)=\Lambda_{01}(s,0)=\Lambda_{11}(0,0)=0.

The bivariate cumulative hazard function extends the univariate Nelson-Aalen framework by modeling hazards for both marginal survival times and their joint dependence.

1.4.2
The Dabrowska Representation and Estimator

The Dabrowska representation provides a product-integral decomposition of the joint survival function based on the cumulative hazard components. This result serves as the theoretical foundation for constructing nonparametric estimators.

Theorem 9

(The Dabrowska Representation)
.

For
(s,t)
such that
S(s,t)>0
, the joint survival function can be expressed as:

S(s,t)=\Prodi_{u\leq s}(1-\Lambda_{10}(du,0))\Prodi_{v\leq t}(1-\Lambda_{01}(0,dv))\Prodi_{u\leq s,v\leq t}(1-L(du,dv)),

where:

L(du,dv)=\frac{\Lambda_{10}(du,v-)\Lambda_{01}(u-,dv)-\Lambda_{11}(du,dv)}{\left\{1-\Lambda_{10}(\Delta u,v-)\right\}\left\{1-\Lambda_{01}(u-,\Delta v)\right\}},

and the first two product-integral terms represent the marginal survival functions
S(s,0)
and
S(0,t)
, respectively. The bivariate product integral is taken as the limit when both
\max_{i}|u_{i}-u_{i-1}|
and
\max_{i}|v_{i}-v_{i-1}|
approach zero.

1.4.3
Observed Data and Counting Processes

Let
\delta_{j},j=1,2
be censoring indicators, and let
(T_{1i},T_{2i},\delta_{1i},\delta_{2i})
represent the observed data for subject
i
. Define the following counting processes and at-risk processes:

\displaystyle N_{11}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(T_{1i}\leq s,T_{2i}\leq t,\delta_{1i}=1,\delta_{2i}=1),

\displaystyle Y_{11}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(T_{1i}\geq s,T_{2i}\geq t),

\displaystyle N_{10}(s|t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(T_{1i}\leq s,T_{2i}\geq t,\delta_{1i}=1),\quad Y_{10}(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(T_{1i}\geq s),

\displaystyle N_{01}(t|s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(T_{1i}\geq s,T_{2i}\leq t,\delta_{2i}=1),\quad Y_{01}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}(T_{2i}\geq t).

1.4.4
The Dabrowska Estimator

Example 10

(The Dabrowska Estimator
(
Dabrowska 1988
)
)
.

Let
\widehat{S}(s,0)
and
\widehat{S}(0,t)
be the univariate Kaplan-Meier estimators for the marginal survival functions. Let
\widehat{\Lambda}_{10}(s,t)
and
\widehat{\Lambda}_{01}(s,t)
be the corresponding univariate Nelson-Aalen estimators. Define:

\widehat{\Lambda}(s,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dN_{11}(s,t)}{Y_{11}(s,t)},

as the bivariate Nelson-Aalen estimator (see ABGK, p. 702). Then, the Dabrowska estimator of the joint survival function is given by:

\widehat{S}(s,t)=\widehat{S}(s,0)\widehat{S}(0,t)\Prodi_{u\leq s,v\leq t}\left(1-\widehat{L}(du,dv)\right),

where
\widehat{L}(du,dv)
is obtained by plugging in the Nelson-Aalen estimators for the cumulative hazard components.

1.4.5
Historical Notes and Further Results

The Dabrowska estimator was introduced by Dorota Dabrowska in her influential 1988 paper
(
Dabrowska 1988
)
, where she extended the Kaplan-Meier framework to bivariate survival analysis. This work marked a significant advancement in the field, enabling the estimation of joint survival functions under censoring. Her contributions have since become a cornerstone in multi-dimensional survival analysis.

Subsequent research has explored the properties of the Dabrowska estimator, including its asymptotic behavior, consistency, and efficiency under various censoring mechanisms. For example, Dabrowska’s 1988 work demonstrated the uniform consistency of the estimator and provided asymptotic normality results for specific scenarios. Other researchers have since extended her methods to handle higher-dimensional survival data and competing risks frameworks.

The bivariate product integral used in the Dabrowska estimator has also found applications in areas such as reliability analysis and multivariate survival modeling. Extensions to incorporate covariate effects, copula-based modeling, and time-dependent covariates remain active areas of research, highlighting the ongoing influence of Dabrowska’s foundational work.

1.5
The Turnbull’s Estimator

1.5.1
Interval Censored Data

Interval Censored Data
Let
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots
be a sequence of i.i.d. survival times. In practice, we only observe the interval censored data:

\{(L_{i},R_{i}]:i=1,2,\cdots,n\}

where
0\leq L_{i}<R_{i}\leq\infty
are left- and right- endpoint of an observation. That is, we only know that
X_{i}\in(L_{i},R_{i}]
instead of the exact values.

•

The case I interval censoring
refers to either
L_{i}=0
or
R_{i}=\infty
and it is also called
the current status data
.

•

The case II interval censoring
refers to
L_{i}>0
and
R_{i}<\infty
.

Importantly, counting process approaches do not apply in this case because we do not have exact information to form a counting process.

Though counting process approaches do not apply, we can formulate the likelihood function of our observation:

\mathcal{L}(S)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(S(L_{i})-S(R_{i})\right)

where
S
is the survival function of
X
. If
L_{i}=R_{i}
for some
i
, we replace
S(L_{i})
with its the left limit
S(L_{i}-)
so that the likelihood assigns probability mass to
1-S(\Delta R_{i})
. The nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation (NPMLE) is defined as

\widehat{S}=\arg\max_{S}\log\mathcal{L}(S).

Following
Sun 2006
, let
\{s_{j}\}_{i=0}^{m}
be the unique ordered elements of
\{0,L_{i},R_{i}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
(see figure) and define

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(s_{j}\in(L_{i},R_{i}])

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=S(s_{j})-S(s_{j-1})

for
i=1,\cdots,n
and
j=1,\cdots,m
. If
L_{i}=R_{i}
for some
i
, i.e., an exact observation, then we replace
\alpha_{ij}
by
\alpha_{ij}=\mathbb{I}(s_{j}\in[L_{i}-,R_{i}])
so that
\alpha_{ij}=1
if
s_{j}=L_{i}
. Hence, the likelihood
\mathcal{L}(S)
can be written as

\mathcal{L}(S)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{ij}p_{j}\right)

and NPMLE refers to the probability vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{m})^{T}
.

1.5.2
The Turnbull’s Interval and Estimator

If
m
is large, then finding
\mathbf{p}
that maximizes
\mathcal{L}(S)
is computationally intractable or inefficient. However, if we know in advance that some (or many)
p_{j}
are
0
, then the computation will be reduced by a lot.

Lemma 11

(Turnbull’s intervals)
.

The
p_{j}
can be nonzero only if

s_{j-1}=L_{i},\ s_{j}=R_{k}
for some
i
and
k
.

•

The resulting intervals
(s_{j-1},s_{j}]
for nonzero
p_{j}
are referred as the
Turnbull’s intervals
.

•

The resulting estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}=(\widehat{p}_{1},\cdots,\widehat{p}_{m})^{T}
is termed as the
Turnbull’s estimator
.

The Turnbull’s estimator is not unique in general and should be solved by numerical algorithms such as EM-ICM
Anderson-Bergman 2017
and bat algorithm
Yang 2013
.

To determine if a candidate estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}
is the maximizer, one uses the Lagrange multiplier criterion, which is derived from graph theory
Gentleman and Geyer 1994
and general mixture maximum likelihood theory
HNING et al. 1996
. Specifically, define

d_{j}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{ij}}{\sum_{l=1}^{m}\alpha_{il}p_{l}}

for
j=1,\cdots,m
. Then
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}
is the Turnbull’s estimator or NPMLE if and only if
d_{j}(\widehat{\mathbf{p}})\leq n
for all
j
.

1.6.3
Excess Mortality Models

•

Suppose the intensity process of the individual counting process is

\lambda_{i}(t)=(\gamma(t)+\mu_{i}(t))Y_{i}(t)

\mu_{i}(t)
is a known deterministic function (say, male or female population hazard) and we are interested in estimating

\Gamma(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(s)ds

•

Define

•

By Doob-Meyer theorem, we have

\Prodi_{0}^{t}(1-d\widehat{\Gamma}(s))=\frac{\widehat{S}(t)}{\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{Y^{\mu}(s)}{Y(s)}ds\right)}

2.1
One-Sample Tests

Consider a sequence of univariate counting process
(N^{(n)}(t);t\in\mathcal{T})
with intensity process
\alpha(t)Y^{(n)}(t)
(I drop the superscript
(n)
in the following). We want to test

H_{0}:\alpha(s)=\alpha_{0}(s),s\in[0,t]

and this is equivalent to test

H_{0}:A(s)=A_{0}(s),s\in[0,t]

under the assumption
A(t)<\infty
for
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

Recall that under
H_{0}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{A}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}dN(s)

\displaystyle A_{0}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{0}(s)J(s)ds

\displaystyle\langle\widehat{A}-A_{0}^{*}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{J(s)}{Y(s)}\alpha_{0}(s)ds

The test statistic is based on the martingale (
45

Z(t)=\int_{0}^{t}K(s)d(\widehat{A}-A_{0}^{*})(s)

where
K
is any locally bounded predictable non-negative stochastic process satisfying

.

2.1.1
One Sample Log-rank Statistic

The test statistic is (
t

T(t)=\frac{Z(t)}{\sqrt{\langle Z\rangle(t)}}

where
\langle Z\rangle(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{K^{2}(s)}{Y(s)}\alpha_{0}(s)ds
. The special case

leads to the

, i.e.,

T(t)^{2}=\frac{(N(t)-E(t))^{2}}{E(t)}

where

. The ratio

is called the

(SMR) in epidemiology. Finally, Fleming and Harrington (1982) also suggested that

P_{01}(0,t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{02}(u)du\right)\left(1-\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{01}(u)du\right)\right).

•

We use the data on nephropathy (DN) and mortality among insulin-dependent diabetics. The total number of person-years at risk is 44,561 in state 0:

Regression Models

3.1
Cox’s Multiplicative Hazard Models / Anderson-Gill Models

3.1.1
Anderson-Gill Model

Assume the compensator of
\mathbf{N}=(N_{hi},h=1,\cdots,k;i=1,\cdots,n)
is

\Lambda_{hi}^{\theta}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{hi}^{\theta}(u)du

where
\theta^{T}=(\gamma,\beta^{T})
and

\lambda_{hi}^{\theta}(t)=Y_{hi}(t)\alpha_{h0}(t,\gamma)r(\mathbf{\beta}^{T}Z_{hi}(t))

and
Y_{hi}(t)
is predictable (risk process),
\alpha_{h0}(t,\gamma)
is the baseline hazard,
Z_{hi}
is a type-specific covariate vector (also predictable) and
r(\cdot)
is called the
relative risk function
. The most popular choice corresponds to
r(x)=\exp(x)
, which is known as the
Cox’s model
. We assume that
\alpha_{h0}(\cdot)
are non-negative and integrable over
t\in\mathcal{T}
.

3.1.2
Estimation and Partial Likelihood

Due to Jacod’s formula, the partial likelihood is proportional to

\Prodi_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{h,i}(dA_{h0}(t)r(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t)))^{\Delta N_{hi}(t)}\exp\left[-\sum_{h=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{T}}S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,u)dA_{h0}(u)\right],

where
A_{h0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{h0}(u)du
and

S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t))Y_{hi}(t).

Fix
\beta
, maximization w.r.t.
\Delta A_{h0}(t)
leads to

\Delta\widehat{A}_{h0}(t,\beta)=\frac{\Delta N_{h}(t)}{S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)}

where
N_{h}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta N_{hi}(t)
. Substitute this into the likelihood, we have the partial likelihood for
\beta
as

L(\beta)=\prod_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{h,i}\left(\frac{r(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t))}{S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)}\right)^{\Delta N_{hi}(t)}

so that
\widehat{\beta}
is obtained by maximizing the
log Cox partial likelihood process

C_{\tau}(\beta)=\log L(\beta)=\sum_{h=1}^{k}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}\left(\log r(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t))-\log S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)\right)dN_{hi}(t)\right].

Then we estimate
A_{h0}(t)
by
\widehat{A}_{h0}(t,\widehat{\beta})
, which is known as the
Breslow’s estimator
. Finally, the hazard rate
\alpha_{h0}(t)
can be estimated via smoothing (
K_{h}(\cdot)
is a kernel function and
b_{n}
is the bandwidth):

\widehat{\alpha}_{h0}(t)=\frac{1}{b_{n}}\int_{0}^{\tau}K_{h}\left(\frac{t-u}{b_{n}}\right)d\widehat{A}_{h0}(u,\widehat{\beta}).

3.1.3
Large Sample Properties

We assume
r(\cdot)=\exp(\cdot)
. Define the following quantities:

•

S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t))Y_{hi}(t)
,

•

\mathbf{S}_{h}^{(1)}(\beta,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{hi}(t)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t))Y_{hi}(t)
,

•

\mathbf{S}^{(2)}_{h}(\beta,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{hi}^{\otimes 2}(t)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{hi}(t))Y_{hi}(t)
,

•

\mathbf{E}_{h}(\beta,t)=\frac{\mathbf{S}_{h}^{(1)}(\beta,t)}{S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)}
,

•

\mathbf{V}_{h}(\beta,t)=\frac{\mathbf{S}_{h}^{(2)}(\beta,t)}{S_{h}^{(0)}(\beta,t)}-\mathbf{E}_{h}(\beta,t)^{\otimes 2}
,

where
h=1,\cdots,k
are states (components of the multivariate counting process). Further, we define the score process (
N_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{hi}
)

\mathbf{U}_{\tau}(\beta)=\sum_{h=1}^{k}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}Z_{hi}(t)dN_{hi}(t)-\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbf{E}_{h}(\beta,t)dN_{h}(t)\right].

Theorem 15

(Consistency of
\widehat{\beta}
)
.

Under some regularity conditions, the estimating equation
\mathbf{U}_{\tau}(\beta)=0
has a unique solution
\widehat{\beta}
with probability tending to 1 and
\widehat{\beta}\rightarrow_{p}\beta_{0}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
where
\beta_{0}
is the true parameter.

The key idea is to show that the process
X(\beta,t)=\frac{1}{n}(C_{t}(\beta)-C_{t}(\beta))
and its compensator converge to the same probability limit where
C_{t}(\beta)
is the log Cox partial likelihood process defined earlier and then apply a lemma in convex analysis. For details, see p497-498 in ABGK or p297-298 in FH.

Theorem 16

(AN of
\widehat{\beta}
)
.

Under some regularity conditions, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})\rightarrow_{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1})

where

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=1}^{k}\int_{0}^{\tau}v_{h}(\beta_{0},t)s_{h}^{(0)}(\beta_{0},t)\alpha_{h0}(t)dt

\displaystyle v_{h}

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{h}^{(2)}}{s_{h}^{(0)}}-e_{h}^{\otimes 2},\ e_{h}=\frac{s_{h}^{(1)}}{s_{h}^{(0)}}

and
s^{(m)}_{h}
and
v_{h}
are probability limits of
\mathbf{S}_{h}^{(m)}
and
\mathbf{V}_{h}
defined earlier.

In the special case that
k=1
and for
i=1,\cdots,n
,
X_{i}
are independent survival times given
Z_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are i.i.d.
p
-dimensional covariates, we have

\Sigma_{\tau}=\int_{0}^{\tau}v(\beta_{0},t)s^{(0)}(\beta_{0},t)\alpha_{0}(t)dt.

Theorem 17

(Joint asymptotic distribution)
.

Under some regularity conditions,
\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})
and the processes

W_{h}(\cdot)=\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{A}_{h0}(\cdot,\widehat{\beta})-A_{h0}(\cdot)\right)+\sqrt{n}(\widehat{\beta}-\beta_{0})^{T}\int_{0}^{\cdot}e_{h}(\beta_{0},u)\alpha_{h0}(u)du

h=1,\cdots,k
, are asymptotically independent. The limiting distribution of
W_{h}
is that of a mean zero Gaussian martingale with variance function

\omega_{h}^{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\alpha_{h0}(u)}{s_{h}^{(0)}(\beta_{0},u)}du.

3.1.4
Goodness-of-Fit and Model Diagnostics

Goodness-of-Fit and Model Diagnostics
This subsection is mainly based on chapter 11 in
Klein and Moeschberger 2003
and section 7.3 in
Andersen et al. 1993
. Some common methods for checking the goodness-of-fit in Cox’s models are:

•

Graphical methods;

•

Tests for log-linearity;

•

Tests for proportional hazards assumption;

•

Martingale residuals;

•

Deviance Residuals;

•

Influence Checking.

Example 18

(Graphical Methods: The Andersen Plot)
.

The full covariate vector is
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{T})^{T}
where
\mathbf{Z}_{2}
is the vector of the remaining
p-1
covariates and
Z_{1}
is the covariate that we want to test the proportional hazards assumption of. We first stratify the covariate
Z_{1}
into
2
(or
K
, in general) strata (this is automatically fulfilled if
Z_{1}
is discrete). We then fit a Cox model stratified on the discrete values of
Z_{1}
and we let
\widehat{H}_{g0}(t),g=1,2
be the estimated cumulative baseline hazard in the
g^{th}
stratum. If the proportional hazards assumption holds, then

\frac{H_{10}(t)}{H_{20}(t)}=\exp(\beta_{1}\Delta Z_{1})

where
\Delta Z_{1}=Z_{11}-Z_{12}
. Hence, Andersen’s approach is to plot
\widehat{H}_{10}(t)
against
\widehat{H}_{20}(t)
and if the null hypothesis holds, this should be a straight line across the origin. An alternative is to plot
\log\widehat{H}_{10}(t)
against
\log\widehat{H}_{20}(t)
which, under
H_{0}
, should be a horizontal line.

Example 19

(Graphical Methods: The Dabrowska-Doksum-Song Method)
.

Dabrowska et al. 1989
studied a general testing procedure in the special two-sample case based on the statistic

\widehat{\theta}(\tau)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\tau}L(s)d\widehat{H}_{1}(s)}{\int_{0}^{\tau}L(s)d\widehat{H}_{2}(s)}

where
L(s)
is a predictable weighting process. Note that
L(s)=1
reduces to the case in the Andersen plot. They also derived confidence bands for the relative trend function
H_{2}\circ H_{1}^{-1}
and for the relative change function
(H_{2}-H_{1})/H_{1}
which is constant under the proportional hazards assumption.

Example 20

(Martingale Residuals: The Arjas Plot)
.

Another graphical method is based on the so-called
martingale residuals
. We define

p_{i}(u,\beta)=\frac{Y_{i}(u)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{i})}{S^{(0)}(\beta,u)}

and
S^{(0)}
is defined as before. Under
H_{0}
, the differences

\text{Arjas}(h,t)=N_{h}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{h(i)=h}p_{i}(u,\beta)dN_{\cdot}(u),\ h=1,\cdots,k,

where
N_{\cdot}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}(u)
,
N_{h}(t)=\sum_{h(i)=h}N_{i}(t)
and
\beta
is the true parameter vector, are local martingales. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\text{Arjas}(h,dt)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(dN_{h}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})-\sum_{h(i)=h}p_{i}(t,\beta)\mathbb{E}(dN_{\cdot}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})

\displaystyle=\sum_{h(i)=h}Y_{i}(t)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{i})dt-

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \sum_{h(i)=h}\frac{Y_{i}(t)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{i})}{S^{(0)}(\beta,t)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(t)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{i})\right)dt

\displaystyle=0.

Therefore, plots of

\int_{0}^{X_{m}^{h}}\sum_{h(i)=h}p_{i}(u,\widehat{\beta})dN_{\cdot}(u),\ m=1,2,\cdots,N_{h}(\tau),h=1,\cdots,k

against
m
, where
X_{m}^{h},m=1,\cdots,N_{h}(\tau)
are the ordered jump times in stratum
h
should be approximated a straight line with unit slope. Such a plot is also referred as the
Total Time on Test plot
(TOT)
(
Klein and Moeschberger 2003
)
.

Example 21

(Martingale Residuals: Functional Form)
.

For right-censored data, define the martingale residuals

\widehat{M}_{i}(t)=N_{i}(t)-\int_{0}^{t}Y_{i}(u)\exp(\widehat{\beta}^{T}Z_{i})d\widehat{A}(u)

where
\widehat{\beta}
and
\widehat{A}(u)
are Cox’s partial likelihood estimator and Breslow’s estimator, respectively.
Fleming and Harrington 1991
(pp165-168) showed that under mild conditions, we have

\mathbb{E}(\widehat{M}(\infty)|Z)\approx cf(Z)

where
c=(\text{total number of events})/(\text{total number of subjects})
. If the proportional hazards assumption holds, i.e.,
\alpha(t|Z)=\alpha_{0}(t)\exp(\beta f(Z))=\alpha_{0}(t)\exp(\beta Z)
, then we shall have
f(Z)=Z
. In other words, it should be a straight line if we plot
\widehat{M}_{i}(\infty)
against
Z_{i}
based on iid observations.

Example 22

(The Cox-Snell Plot)
.

Let
(T_{i},D_{i},Z_{i}),i=1,2,\cdots,n
be the iid observations based on a right-censored counting process, and define

\widehat{r}_{i}=\widehat{A}(T_{i}|Z_{i})=\widehat{A}_{0}(T_{i})\exp(\widehat{\beta}^{T}Z_{i}).

The Cox-Snell’s plot is the Nelson-Aalen estimate based on the sample
(\widehat{r}_{i},D_{i}),i=1,2,\cdots,n
where
D_{i}
are right-censoring indicators. Under
H_{0}
, the NA estimate should be a line across the origin with unit slope. This is due to the fact that the true cumulative hazard evaluated at
T_{i}
should be iid standard exponential variables:

r_{i}=H(T_{i}|Z_{i})=-\log S(X_{i}|Z_{i})\sim\mathcal{E}(1).

3.2
Additive Hazard Models

3.2.1
Generalized Nelson-Aalen’s (GNA) Estimator

Definition 23

(Aalen’s additive hazard model)
.

Let
\mathbf{N}(t)=(N_{i}(t):i=1,\cdots,n)
be a multivariate counting process with intensity
\lambda_{i}(t)=\alpha_{i}(t;Z_{i}(t))Y_{i}(t)
. The
additive hazard model
assumes

\alpha_{i}(t;Z_{i}(t))=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}(t)Z_{i1}(t)+\cdots+\beta_{p}(t)Z_{ip}

where
\beta_{j}(t)
are all locally integrable for
t\in\mathcal{T}
. More compactly, we have

\mathbf{N}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{Y}(s)d\mathbf{B}(s)+\mathbf{M}(t)

where
B_{j}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\beta_{j}(s)ds,j=0,\cdots,p
are the parameters of primary interst and
\mathbf{Y}(s)
is an
n\times(p+1)
matrix with rows

Y_{i}(t)(1,Z_{i1}(t),Z_{i2}(t),\cdots,Z_{ip}(t))

and
\mathbf{M}(t)
is a vector of counting process martingales.

Heuristically, we have

d\mathbf{N}(t)=\mathbf{Y}(t)d\mathbf{B}(t)+\mathbf{M}(dt)

and this suggests the
GNA
estimator:

\widehat{\mathbf{B}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}J(s)\mathbf{Y}(s)^{-}d\mathbf{N}(s)

where
J(s)=\mathbb{I}(\text{rank}(\mathbf{Y}(s)=p+1))
and
\mathbf{Y}(s)^{-}
is any predictable generalized inverse of
\mathbf{Y}(s)
. The variance estimator can be

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(t)=\left[\widehat{\mathbf{B}}-\mathbf{B}^{*}\right](t)=\int_{0}^{t}J(s)\mathbf{Y}(s)^{-}\left(\text{diag }d\mathbf{N}(s)\right)(\mathbf{Y}(s)^{-})^{T}

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the quadratic variation and
\mathbf{B}^{*}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}J(u)d\mathbf{B}(t)
.

In the special case that
\alpha_{i}(t;Z_{i}(t))=\beta_{0}
, we have

\widehat{B}_{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}J(s)\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(s)dN_{i}(s)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(s)^{2}},

which reduces to the classical NA estimator if
Y_{i}(s)
are indicators. Further, we apply smoothed NA estimator technique to estimate
\beta_{j}(t)
and
\alpha_{i}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}_{j}=\frac{1}{b_{j}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}K\left(\frac{t-s}{b_{j}}\right)d\widehat{B}_{j}(s),

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}_{i}(t,Z_{i}(t))=\widehat{\beta}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\widehat{\beta}_{j}(t)Z_{ij}(t).

For large sample properties, see ABGK, pp575-578. We shall explore the additive hazards model in depth within the causal inference framework later.

3.3.1

The relation
S(t)=e^{-A(t)}
suggests that
A(X)\sim\mathcal{E}(1)

\log A_{0}(X)=-\beta^{T}Z+\epsilon

where
\mathbb{P}(\epsilon>t)=e^{-e^{t}}
. This leads to the following definition.

Example 24

(Transformation models)
.

Let
g
be an unknown monotone function, the
transformation model
is

g(X)=\beta^{T}Z+\epsilon

where
X
is the (possibly left-, right- and interval-censored) time-to-event outcome and
\epsilon
has some known distribution.

The
accelerated failure time (AFT) model
corresponds to
g(X)=\log(X)
.

3.3.2
Buckley-James Estimator

•

Let
V_{i}=\log X_{i}
where
X_{i}
is possibly right-censored, the likelihood function is

\mathcal{L}(\beta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})^{D_{i}}S(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})^{1-D_{i}}

where
D_{i}
are censoring indicators,
f
and
S
are density and survival function of
\epsilon_{i}
. Hence, the score equation for
\beta
is

\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\left\{D_{i}\frac{f^{\prime}(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})}{f(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})}-(1-D_{i})\frac{f(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})}{S(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})}\right\}=\mathbf{0}.

If
f
is known (a parametric distribution such as Gumbel type I), then
\beta
can be solved by numerical methods.

•

If
f
is unknown, then we replace
-\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}
with a suitable known function
s
, say,
a(t)=t
, which corresponds to
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The equation now becomes

\Psi(\beta,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\left\{D_{i}a(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i})-(1-D_{i})\frac{\int_{V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z_{i}}^{\infty}a(t)dS(t)}{S(V_{i}-\beta^{T}Z)}\right\}=\mathbf{0}.

Note that
a(t)=t
corresponds to the ordinary least squares (OLS) equation if no censoring occurs.

Lemma 25

(Buckley-James
Buckley and James 1979
)
.

We assume
V_{i}^{o}=\log X_{i}\wedge\log C_{i}
is the observed time-to-event and
C_{i}
is the independent censoring time. If
a(t)=t
, the equation
\Psi(\beta,a)=\mathbf{0}
becomes

\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\left\{D_{i}r_{i}+(1-D_{i})\frac{\int_{r_{i}}^{\infty}S(t)dt}{S(r_{i})}\right\}=\mathbf{0}

where
r_{i}=V_{i}^{o}-\beta^{T}Z_{i}
. Hence, it corresponds to the OLS of the
synthetic variable

V_{i}^{*}=D_{i}V_{i}^{o}+(1-D_{i})\mathbb{E}(V_{i}|V_{i}\geq V_{i}^{o},Z_{i})

The resulting estimator is called
Buckley-James
. Further,
V_{i}^{*}
has the property

\mathbb{E}(V_{i}^{*}|Z_{i})=\mathbb{E}(V_{i}|Z_{i}).

Proof.

Apparently,

\mathbb{E}(V_{i}^{*}|Z_{i})=\mathbb{E}(V_{i}|Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left\{D_{i}(V_{i}^{o}-\mathbb{E}(V_{i}|V_{i}\geq V_{i}^{o},Z_{i}))|Z_{i}\right\}.

But the second term is

\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{I}(D_{i}=1)(V_{i}^{o}-V_{i}^{o})+\mathbb{I}(D_{i}=0)*0|Z_{i})

which can only be
0
.
∎

•

Define

W_{F}(a(t))=a(t)-\frac{\int_{t}^{\infty}a(s)dF(s)}{S(t)}

where
F
has density
f
, then we have

W_{F}(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log f_{\theta})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\alpha_{\theta}

where
\alpha_{\theta}=f_{\theta}/S_{\theta}
is the hazard function.

Definition 26

(Semi-Markov processes and Markov renewal processes)
.

•

The stochastic process
(X,T)=\{X_{n},T_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is said to be a
Markov renewal process
if
T_{0}=0
a.s. and

–

the sequence
(J_{0},J_{1},\cdots)
forms a discrete time Markov chain;

–

For any integer

W_{n}=T_{n}-T_{n-1}
and we have

\mathbb{P}(W_{1}\leq t_{1},\cdots,W_{n}\leq t_{n}|X_{j},j\geq 0)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(W_{k}\leq t_{k}|X_{k-1},X_{k}).

•

3.4.2
Some Theory

Definition 27

(Semi-Markov kernel)
.

Let
X_{n}
take values in
\{1,\cdots,r\}
. The basic parameter in a semi-Markov (or Markov renewal) process is the semi-Markov kernel

\mathbf{Q}(w)=\left[Q_{ij}(w)\right]

where
p_{ij}=\mathbb{P}(X_{n+1}=i|X_{n}=j),F_{ij}(w)=\mathbb{P}(W_{n+1}\leq w|X_{n+1}=j,X_{n}=i),

\displaystyle Q_{ij}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{n+1}\leq w,X_{n+1}=j|X_{n}=i)=p_{ij}F_{ij}(w).

Set

\mathbf{G}(x,y)=\text{Diag}\left[\frac{S_{i}(y)}{S_{i}(x)}\right]

where
S_{i}(w)=\sum_{j=1,j\not=i}^{r}p_{ij}(1-F_{ij}(w))
so that on the diagonal, we have

\mathbb{P}(W_{n+1}>y|W_{n+1}>x,X_{n}=i).

Example 28

(Fowards recurrence time)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\mathcal{F}_{0}\vee\sigma(X(s):s\leq t)
and
\gamma_{t}=T_{N(t)+1}-t
be the forwards recurrence time of the process given
\mathcal{F}_{t-}
, then we have

\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{t}>w|\mathcal{F}_{t-})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{I}(X(t)=i)G_{ii}(\delta_{t},\delta_{t}+w)

where
\delta_{t}=t-T_{N(t)}
so that it is fully specified by
\mathbf{G}(x,y)
defined earlier. Indeed, let
\beta_{t}:=T_{N(t)+1}-T_{N(t)}=W_{N(t)+1}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{t}>w|\mathcal{F}_{t-})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T_{N(t)+1}-t>w|\mathcal{F}_{t-})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\beta_{t}>\delta_{t}+w|\delta_{t},X_{N(t)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{I}(X_{N(t)}=i)G_{ii}(\delta_{t},\delta_{t}+w).

Remark

3.5.1
Parametric Regression Models

Example 33

(Parametric regression)
.

Suppose the intensity process

3.5.2
Beran’s Nonparametric Regression

Example 34

.

Remark
: tests for the hypothesis

3.5.3
Dabrowska’s Smoothed Cox Regression

Dabrowska
Dabrowska 1997
studied the so-called

, where the intensity is of the form

\Lambda(dt)=\mathbb{E}(N(dt)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})=Y(t)\alpha(t,X)\exp(Z^{T}\beta)dt,

where

is a 0-1 predictable process and
X
and
Z
are covariates. Based on i.i.d. observations, Dr. Dabrowska defined the estimator as the solution to the smoothed partial likelihood score equation

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\tau}\left\{Z_{i}-\frac{S_{1}(X_{i},s,\beta)}{S_{0}(X_{i},s,\beta)}\right\}dN_{i}(s)=0,

where

S_{k}(x,s,\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{b}(x-X_{i})Y_{i}(t)\exp(\beta^{T}Z_{i}(t))Z_{i}^{\otimes k}

K_{b}(x-X_{i})=\frac{1}{b}K(\frac{x-X_{i}}{b})
is the kernel estimator as that in Beran’s nonparametric regression. Analogously to ABGK

\widehat{A}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{K_{b}(x-X_{i})}{S_{0}(x,s,\widehat{\beta})}dN_{i}(s).

In the absense of covariates this reduces to Beran’s estimator.

3.5.4
Cox-Aalen Hazards Model

As a special and more applicable case of Dabrowska’s smoothed Cox regression,

5.1
The Counting Process Framework

5.1.1
Counting Processes

Definition 36

(Counting processes)
.

A
multivariate counting process

\mathbf{N}=(N_{1},\cdots,N_{k})
is a vector of
k
adapted cadlag stochastic process defined on a quadruple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
where
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t},t\in\mathcal{T})
, zero at time zero, with piecewise constant paths and nondecreasing, having jumps of size +1, no two components jumping at the same time.

Example 37

(Right-censoring model)
.

Let
T
be a nonnegative random variable, then
X(t)=\mathbb{I}(T\leq t)
is a one jump counting process. Let
C
be another nonegative random variable independent of
T
, then
\widetilde{X}(t)=\mathbb{I}(T\wedge C\leq t)
is the right-censored counting process.

5.1.2
Martingales and Localization

Definition 38

(Martingales)
.

A cádlág process
\{M_{t}:t\geq 0\}
defined on a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
is called a
continuous time martingale
if

•

M_{t}
is adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

•

\mathbb{E}|M_{t}|<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
.

•

\mathbb{E}(M_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s})=M_{s}
a.s. for
s<t
.

The third property is referred as the martingale property.

Definition 39

(Local martingales)
.

A continuous time process
\{M_{t}:t\geq 0\}
is a
local martingale
if there exist stopping times
T_{n}\rightarrow\infty
such that
M_{t}^{n}=M_{t\wedge T_{n}}
is a uniformly integrable martingale for each
n
.

Definition 40

(Intensity of a counting process)
.

We say that
N_{h}
has intensity process
\lambda_{h}
if
\lambda_{h}
is predictable and

\Lambda_{h}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{h}(s)ds\ \forall t

where
\Lambda_{h}
is the compensator of
N_{h}
. The intensity process exists if and only if the time-to-event variable
T
has a density w.r.t. Lebesgue measure.

Example 41

(Time-to-event outcome)
.

Suppose
T
is a non-negative random variable with absolutely continuous distribution function
F
and density
f
, survival function
S=1-F
. Define
N(t)=\mathbb{I}(T\leq t)
as the one jump counting process, then
N(t)
has compensator

\Lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}Y(s)\alpha(s)ds

where
Y(s)=\mathbb{I}(T\geq t)
,

, and hence,

has intensity process
\lambda(t)=Y(t)\alpha(t)
.

5.1.3

Theorem 42

(Doob-Meyer for counting processes)
.

1.

Each component of
\mathbf{N}
can be uniquely decomposed as

where
\Lambda_{h}
is predictable and non-decreasing (called the compensator of

) and

is a mean
0

2.

Further, the compensator (predictable covariation) and the quadratic covariation of

are

\displaystyle\langle M_{h},M_{h^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{hh^{\prime}}\Lambda_{h}-\int\Delta\Lambda_{h}d\Lambda_{h^{\prime}}

\displaystyle[M_{h}]

\displaystyle=N_{h}-2\int\Delta\Lambda_{h}dN_{h}+\int\Delta\Lambda_{h}d\Lambda_{h}

\displaystyle[M_{h},M_{h^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=-\int\Delta\Lambda_{h}dM_{h^{\prime}}-\int\Delta\Lambda_{h^{\prime}}dM_{h}+\int\Delta\Lambda_{h}d\Lambda_{h^{\prime}}

where

Lemma 43

(Integration-by-parts)

Suppose that

and

where the sum extends over the jumps of

.

Example 44

(Quadratic variation)

If

Proof.

We prove the second part. Drop the subscript

, we have

\displaystyle(M(\Delta s))^{2}

\displaystyle=(N(\Delta s)-\Lambda(\Delta s))^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-2\Lambda(\Delta s))M(\Delta s)+(1-\Lambda(\Delta s))\Lambda(\Delta s)

Hence, by integration-by-parts

M^{2}(t)=Z_{t}+[M]_{t}

where
Z_{t}=2\int_{(0,t]}M(u-)dM(u)
and

[M]_{t}=\int_{(0,t]}(1-2\Lambda(\Delta s))M(ds)+\int_{(0,t]}(1-\Lambda(\Delta s))\Lambda(ds).

The first term of
[M]_{t}
is a mean zero martingale:

\mathbb{E}\left((1-2\Lambda(\Delta t))M(dt)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}\right)=(1-2\Lambda(\Delta t))\mathbb{E}(M(dt)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})=0

Hence, by the uniqueness of Doob-Meyer, the compensator (predictable variation) of
M^{2}
is

\langle M\rangle(t)=\int_{(0,t]}(1-\Lambda(\Delta s))\Lambda(ds)

The rest can also be verified by the property

[M_{j},M_{k}]=\frac{1}{4}\left([M_{j}+M_{k}](t)-[M_{j}-M_{k}](t)\right).

∎

Definition 48

(Independent censoring)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}\subset\mathcal{G}_{t},t\in[0,\tau]
be two nested filtrations and
\{N(t):t\in[0,\tau]\}
be a univariate counting process adapted to both filtrations. We assume that

\mathbb{E}(dN(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})=\lambda(t)dt,

i.e., the compensator of
N(t)
w.r.t. the smaller filtration is
\int\lambda
. Then

assumes that

\mathbb{E}(dN(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})=\mathbb{E}(dN(t)|\mathcal{G}_{t-})=\lambda(t)dt.

The existence of the above equality is ensured by the innovation theorem.

Example 49

(Right-censored counting processes)
.

Let
X
be a nonnegative random variable with hazard rate
\lambda
. Define

•

the complete counting process
N(t)=\mathbb{I}(t\geq X)
;

•

the risk process
Y(t)=\mathbb{I}(t\leq X)
so that
Y(t)\lambda(t)
is the compensator of
N(t)
w.r.t. the self-exciting filtration;

•

the observed counting process

N^{c}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}C(s)dN(s)

where
C(s)=\mathbb{I}(s\leq U)\in\mathcal{G}_{t}
and
U
is the (right-)censoring variable;

•

the self-exciting filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(N(s),s\leq t)
, the observed (or right-censored) filtration
\mathcal{F}^{c}_{t}=\sigma(N^{c}(s),s\leq t)
and the complete (or joint) filtration
\mathcal{G}_{t}=\sigma(N(s),C(s),s\leq t)
.

Under the independent censoring assumption, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(dN^{c}(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(C(t)dN(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}(C(t)dN(t)|\mathcal{G}_{t-})|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(C(t)\mathbb{E}(dN(t)|\mathcal{G}_{t-})|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(C(t)\mathbb{E}(dN(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-})|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(C(t)Y(t)\lambda(t)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{I}(t\leq X\wedge U)|\mathcal{F}_{t-}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda(t)\mathbb{I}(t\leq X\wedge U)

\displaystyle=C(t)Y(t)\lambda(t)

where the third, fourth equalities are due to the left-continuity of
C(t)
and the independent censoring assumption.

.

Let

5.2.4
The Functional Delta Method

Lemma 53

(The functional
\Delta
-method)
.

Let
T_{n}
be a sequence of random elements of a Banach space
B
,
a_{n}\rightarrow\infty
a real sequence such that
a_{n}(T_{n}-\theta)\rightarrow_{d}Z
for some fixed
\theta\in B
and a random element
Z\in B
. Suppose
\phi:B\rightarrow B^{\prime}
is Hadamard differentiable at
\theta
. Then

a_{n}(\phi(T_{n})-\phi(\theta))\rightarrow_{d}d\phi(\theta)\cdot Z

where
d\phi(\theta)
is the derivative of
\phi
at
\theta
.

Proof.

By Dudley-Skorohod-Wichura’s theorem, we can replace
d
with
a.s.
. That is, the set
A=\{\omega:\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n}(T_{n}(\omega)-\theta)=Z(\omega)\}
has probability 1. By Hadamard differentiability, we have

a_{n}(\phi(\theta+a_{n}^{-1}h_{n}(\omega))-\phi(\theta))\rightarrow d\phi(\theta)Z

for any
\omega\in A
where
h_{n}=a_{n}(T_{n}-\theta)
.
∎

Definition 55

A Key Theorem

Theorem 60

(Relation among PI, MP and CP)
.

•

Let the matrix function

•

Given that

.

•

Let

\displaystyle Y_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}(X(t-)=h)

\displaystyle N_{hj}(t)

\displaystyle=\#\{s\leq t:X(s-)=h,X(s)=j\},\ h\not=j
