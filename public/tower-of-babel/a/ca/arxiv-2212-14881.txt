A Computational Approach to Polynomial Conservation Laws
Aurélien Desoeuvres, Alexandru Iosif, Christoph Lüders, Ovidiu Radulescu, Hamid Rahkooy, Matthias Seiß, Thomas Sturm

Abstract
For polynomial ODE models, we introduce and discuss the concepts of exact and approximate conservation laws, which are the first integrals of the full and truncated sets of ODEs. For fast-slow systems , truncated ODEs describe the fast dynamics. We define compatibility classes as subsets of the state space, obtained by equating the conservation laws to constants. A set of conservation laws is complete when the corresponding compatibility classes contain a finite number of steady states. Complete sets of conservation laws can be used for model order reduction and for studying the multistationarity of the model . We provide algorithmic methods for computing linear, monomial, and polynomial conservation laws of polynomial ODE models and for testing their completeness. The resulting conservation laws and their completeness are either independent or dependent on the parameters. In the latter case, we provide parametric case distinctions. In particular, we propose a new method to compute polynomial conservation laws by comprehensive Gröbner systems and syzygies. Keywords : First integrals, chemical reaction networks, polynomial conservation laws, syzygies, comprehensive Gröbner systems.



1
Introduction

Several important models in physics, chemistry, biology,
ecology, economics, and engineering can be written as systems of polynomial ODEs.

Conservation laws
are first integrals
of such models
, i.e. quantities that are constant on any solution of the ODEs. When they exist, they can be used for
model order

reduction
[
11
]
.
A reduced model can be more easily simulated and analysed. For example,
ODEs with
n
variables and
n-1
independent conservation laws are solvable by quadratures
[
3
]
.

The use of first integrals for eliminating variables is an exact model reduction method
[
11
]
. However, many model reduction methods are approximate.
For instance, the quasi-steady state approximation is an approximate model
reduction method based on the elimination of fast variables

[
71
,
30
,
32
,
57
,
45
,
21
]
.

This reduction method fails when the fast dynamics has first integrals.

We introduce here the
approximate conservation laws,
a key concept
for model reduction by variable pooling
used when the fast dynamics has first integrals

[
58
,
5
,
21
]
.

Conservation laws are
also used for
studying
the steady states of
ODE models
,
for instance in applications
that deal with
multistationarity
[
29
]
. In the case
of linear conservation laws, the set of steady states can be partitioned
into stoichiometric compatibility classes

[
28
]

that are characterized by fixed, different values of the linear conservation
laws. Here we extend this definition
to polynomial
conservation laws.
Compatibility classes are invariant sets of
the ODE flow
and, under some
conditions that we call
completeness
, contain a finite number of steady states.

The completeness of approximate conservation laws is also important for model reduction
applications
[
21
]
.

A lot of effort has been put into computing linear conservation laws of
ODE models

[
60
,
65
]
.
Non-linear conservation laws
have been addressed using Darboux polynomials
[
56
,
51
,
16
,
50
]
.

Monomial first integrals were discussed in
[
33
,
50
]
. For a thorough introduction to first integrals and related computational methods, refer to Zhang’s book
[
75
]
.

Polynomial first integrals were used for formal proofs
of hybrid systems safety in
[
64
]
,
where they were computed using the method of undetermined coefficients.

In this paper, we propose a new method for computing polynomial conservation laws
using syzygies, which are relations among the polynomial r.h.s of
the ODEs.

Furthermore, we use Comprehensive Gröbner Bases techniques for partitioning the parameter space into branches such that the syzygies (and hence, conservation laws) are invariant under evaluation in each branch.

We also propose algorithmic solutions for testing the independence
and the completeness of the computed conservation laws.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In the next section, we introduce the polynomial ODE models.
We show that any polynomial ODE model can be written as a chemical reaction network (CRN)
model. In Section 3 we discuss exact and approximate conservation laws, that are important
in model reduction applications. In Section 4 we provide methods to test the independence
and completeness of the conservation laws. Section 5 exposes extant methods for computing
linear conservation laws. The Section 6.1 is dedicated to monomial conservation laws.
In Section 6.2 we present a new method to compute polynomial conservation laws using syzygies.

3
Exact Versus Approximate Conservation Laws

We start this section with a motivating example.

Example 5
.

Let us consider the irreversible
Michaelis-Menten mechanism,
used as a model of
enzymatic reactions. We choose rate constants corresponding to the so-called quasi-equilibrium, as done by Michaelis and Menten
[
52
]
.
The reaction network for this model is

S+E\underset{k_{2}}{\overset{k_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}ES\overset{k_{3}\delta}{\longrightarrow}E+P,

where
S
is a substrate,
E
is an enzyme,
ES
is an enzyme-substrate complex and
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
,
k_{3}

are rate constants of the same order of magnitude. Here
0<\delta<1
is a small positive scaling parameter, indicating that the third rate constant is
small.

According to mass-action kinetics, the concentrations

x_{1}=[S]
,
x_{2}=[SE]
and
x_{3}=[E]
satisfy the system of
ODEs

\displaystyle\dot{x_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}+k_{2}x_{2},

(3a)

\displaystyle\dot{x_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{2}x_{2}-\delta k_{3}x_{2},

(3b)

\displaystyle\dot{x_{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}+k_{2}x_{2}+\delta k_{3}x_{2}.

(3c)

We consider the truncated system of ODEs

\displaystyle\dot{x_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}+k_{2}x_{2},

(4a)

\displaystyle\dot{x_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{2}x_{2},

(4b)

\displaystyle\dot{x_{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}+k_{2}x_{2},

(4c)

which is obtained by setting
\delta=0
in (
3
).
If
k_{i},1\leq i\leq 3
are
of order
{O}(\delta^{0})
, then the truncated system (
4
) describes the
dynamics of the model on fast timescales of order
{O}(\delta^{0})
.

The steady state of the fast dynamics is obtained by equating to zero the r.h.s. of (
4
). The resulting
condition is called
quasi-equilibrium (QE)
,
because it means that the complex
formation rate
k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}
is equal
to the complex dissociation rate
k_{2}x_{2}
.
In other words, the reversible reaction

S+E\underset{k_{2}}{\overset{k_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}ES

is at
equilibrium. The QE condition is reached only at the end of the
fast dynamics and is satisfied
with a precision of order
{O}(\delta)
during
the slow dynamics
[
34
]
.

We introduce the linear combinations of
variables
x_{4}=x_{1}+x_{2}
and
x_{5}=x_{2}+x_{3}
, corresponding
to the total substrate and total enzyme concentrations, respectively.
Addition of equation (
3b
) and (
3c
) leads to

\dot{x_{5}}=0
which means that for solutions of the full system,
x_{5}
is constant for all times.
We will call such a quantity an
exact conservation law
.
The addition of equation (
4a
)
and (
4b
)
leads to
\dot{x_{4}}=0
.This means that
x_{4}
is constant for
solutions of the truncated dynamics, valid at short times
t={O}(\delta^{0})
.

However, addition of (
3a
) and (
3b
)
leads to

\dot{x_{4}}=-\delta k_{3}x_{2},

meaning that
x_{4}
evolves slowly under the full dynamics.

x_{4}
is not constant at large times
t={O}(\delta^{-1})
.

We call such a quantity an
approximate conservation law
.
The quantity
x_{5}
is both an exact and approximate conservation law, because is conserved by both (
4
) and (
3
) and therefore it
is constant at all times.

Remark 6
.

The exact and approximate conservation laws can be used as a new parameter
and as a slow variable of the model, respectively. Two extant variables can be then eliminated as such

x_{2}=x_{4}-x_{1},x_{3}=k_{4}+x_{1}-x_{4}
where
k_{4}=x_{5}
is the new parameter.
In the remaining variables
x_{1},x_{4}
the full system reads

\displaystyle\dot{x_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-k_{1}x_{1}(k_{4}+x_{1}-x_{4})+k_{2}(x_{4}-x_{1}),

(5a)

\displaystyle\dot{x_{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\delta k_{3}(x_{4}-x_{1}).

(5b)

The transformed model (
5

x_{1}
the fast and
x_{4}
the slow variable
[
71
,
30
]
and can be further reduced using the quasi-steady state approximation.
In the transformed model the fast dynamics (

dynamics (
4

However, the foliations defined by hyperplanes on which the two conservation laws are constant intersect transversally the degenerate state state manifold, resulting into non-degenerate steady states. This property, called completeness, will be detailed in the Section
4

Depending on the application, the truncated system in

Definition 8
.

(a)

A function

(b)

A function
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is a
parametric approximate
conservation law
if there exists a non-empty semi-algebraic set

V_{\boldsymbol{k}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r}_{+}
such that for all
\boldsymbol{k}\in V_{\boldsymbol{k}}
,

\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is a first integral of the truncated system in
(

), i.e. if

\forall\boldsymbol{k}\in V_{\boldsymbol{k}}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\quad(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})f_{i}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=0).

(c)

A parametric exact (approximate) conservation law of the form

\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=c_{1}(\boldsymbol{k})x_{1}+\dots+c_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})x_{n}

with
c_{i}(\boldsymbol{k})\in\mathbb{R}[\boldsymbol{k}]
is called an
exact (approximate)
linear conservation law
. If

1\leq i\leq n
,
the linear conservation law is called

.

(d)

A parametric exact (approximate) conservation law of the form

\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=a(\boldsymbol{k})x_{1}^{m_{1}}

\cdots x_{n}^{m_{n}}
with

m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

exact
(approximate) rational monomial conservation law
. If

1\leq i\leq n
, the conservation law is called

(e)

A parametric exact (approximate) conservation law of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{s}a_{i}(\boldsymbol{k})x_{1}^{m_{1i}}\cdots x_{n}^{m_{ni}}

with
m_{ji}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and

exact (approximate) polynomial conservation law
.

Remark 9

In some applications we are interested in conservation laws

4
Independence and Completeness

Some variables

may not appear in a conservation law

may vanish. If

.

Definition 11
.

An exact or approximate conservation law depending on

r
variables
is called

if it can’t be split
into the sum or the product of two conservation laws
such that at least one of them depends on
a number of variables

with
1\leq r^{\prime}<r
.

Definition 12
.

For
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{+}
a steady state
\boldsymbol{x}
is a positive solution of
\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=0
.
We denote

or

Degeneracy of steady states implies that

Theorem 13

Assume that for
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}_{+}

There is a neighborhood

of zero and a smooth function

\alpha\mapsto\boldsymbol{x}(\alpha)
with
\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}

By differentiating

∎

-k_{1}x_{3}&k_{2}&-k_{2}x_{1}\\
1&1&0\\
0&1&1\end{pmatrix}

and compute in line 1 the parametric rank

from which we select

\gamma=\forall\,\boldsymbol{k}\,\forall\,\boldsymbol{x}\,(\boldsymbol{k}>0\,\land\,\boldsymbol{x}>0\,\land\,k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{2}x_{2}=0\longrightarrow k_{1}x_{1}+k_{1}x_{3}+k_{2}\neq 0),

where some redundant equations are automatically removed from

via the standard simplifier described in

[
24
]
. Real quantifier elimination on
\gamma
in line 5
equivalently yields “
\operatorname{true}
”, which confirms completeness, and we return
“yes” in line 6.

Similarly, Algorithm
5

\operatorname{IsIndependent}
computes

0&1&1\end{pmatrix},

R=\{(\operatorname{true},2)\}
,

and
\varrho_{2}=\operatorname{true}
.
This yields

\iota=\forall\,\boldsymbol{k}\,\forall\,\boldsymbol{x}\,(\boldsymbol{k}>0\,\land\,\boldsymbol{x}>0\,\land\,k_{1}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{2}x_{2}=0\longrightarrow\operatorname{true}),

which by quantifier elimination is equivalent to “
\operatorname{true}
”, and we
return “yes”. Recall that any implication with “
\operatorname{true}

. In our
framework here, this corresponds to the observation that completeness and
independence hold whenever we encounter full rank of the corresponding
Jacobian for all choices of parameters.
The automatic computations described here take less than 0.01 sec altogether.

The next example shows that
there are models with degenerate steady states and no complete set of conservation laws.

Example 22
.

One checks easily that the system

\dot{x}_{1}=1-x_{1}-x_{2},\quad\dot{x}_{2}=x_{1}+x_{2}-1

\Phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=x_{1}+x_{2}

. Trivially the Jacobian has everywhere rank

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-c_{0})t+c_{1},

\displaystyle x_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(c_{0}-1)t+c_{0}-c_{1}.

Thus, all invariant curves are of the

. We conclude that there are no further first integrals and so the system has no

We can also note that the intersection of the

In the absence of the parameters
\boldsymbol{k}

5
Computing Linear Conservation Laws

Throughout this section we consider that the polynomial ODE system (
1
) has the structure given by (
2
). If the model is a CRN this is automatically true, otherwise we can always bring the system to this form by using the Algorithms
1
,
2
.

By definition a vector
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
defines an exact linear conservation law

\Phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}

of the system in (
1
)
with velocity field satisfying (
2
)
, if

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{S})_{j}k_{j}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}}=0

for all
\boldsymbol{k}
,
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Exact linear conservation laws of the system in (
1
) can be obtained from the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
as the following theorem shows.

Theorem 23
.

Assume that all monomial reaction rates
with the same multi-index have different
rate constants, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j^{\prime}}
with
j\neq j^{\prime}

implies
k_{j}\neq k_{j^{\prime}}
. This condition is always fulfilled in our setting (see the Remark
2
).
Let
r_{\boldsymbol{S}}
be the rank of the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
.
Then there is
a full rank matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
with
n-r_{\boldsymbol{S}}
rows such that
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{S}=0
. Furthermore,

\boldsymbol{C}
can be chosen such that its rows
form a set of independent, simple, exact linear conservation laws unconditional on
\boldsymbol{k}
.
Finally, all
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
defining unconditional, exact
linear conservation laws are linear combinations
of the rows of
\boldsymbol{C}
.

Proof.

As a direct consequence of the rank-nullity theorem, there is a matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
with

n-r_{\boldsymbol{S}}
independent rows such that
\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{S}=0
. If
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})

defines an exact, unconditional linear conservation law, we then have
that

\sum_{j=1}^{r}(\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{S})_{j}k_{j}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}}=0

for all
\boldsymbol{k}
,

\boldsymbol{x}
. If
i\neq j
one has
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}\neq\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}
or
k_{i}\neq k_{j}
.
It follows that
k_{j}\boldsymbol{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]

and
\boldsymbol{c}\boldsymbol{S}=0
. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{c}
is a linear combination of rows of
\boldsymbol{C}
.
Those rows of
\boldsymbol{C}
that are not
simple
can be decomposed as a sum of simple
linear conservation laws. The resulting conservation laws form a generating set for the vector space of linear conservation laws
and therefore this set contains a basis.
The
n-r_{\boldsymbol{S}}
elements of this basis can be chosen as the simple rows of
\boldsymbol{C}
.
Here, the unconditionality of
\boldsymbol{c}

results from the fact that
\boldsymbol{S}
and therefore

\boldsymbol{c}
do not depend on
\boldsymbol{k}
.
∎

Remark 24
.

Since
S_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the coefficients
c_{i}
can be chosen to be integers.
For conservative CRNs linear conservation laws can be chosen semi-positive, that is all
c_{i}\geq 0
(see
[
60
]
). Semi-positive linear
conservation laws are important, because their existence implies that concentrations of
some species are bounded at all times. Also, semi-positive conservation laws allow easy interpretation
in terms of pools of chemical species. An algorithm to compute
semi-positive linear conservation laws
can be found in
[
60
]
. We must emphasize that it is not always
possible to transform complete systems of linear conservation laws into semi-positive
linear conservation laws.
As an example, one may consider
the formation of a heterodimer described by

\dot{x}_{1}=-k_{1}x_{1}x_{2}+k_{2}x_{3},\,\dot{x}_{2}=-k_{1}x_{1}x_{2}+k_{2}x_{3},\,\dot{x}_{3}=k_{1}x_{1}x_{2}-k_{2}x_{3}
. This system
has the complete system of
linear conservation laws

\boldsymbol{\Phi}=(x_{1}-x_{2},x_{1}+x_{2}-2x_{3})

that can not be transformed into a semi-positive
system.
Of course, semi-positiveness is not relevant
for non-chemical application, except for population
dynamics models used in ecology.

.

In Example

we have seen that

6
Computing Nonlinear Conservation Laws

Nonlinear conservation laws, i.e. first integrals, of polynomial systems of ODEs have been approached by means of a variety
of methods. For example, simple conservation laws,
i.e. expressions composed of
polynomials, logarithms and exponentials, can be obtained by
Darboux polynomials

[
56
,
16
]
.
The bottleneck of this method is the computation of all Darboux polynomials
of the ODE system. This problem has been approached in computational algebra by
using Gröbner bases
[
51
]
.
For particular models,
for instance
Lotka-Volterra models, Darboux polynomials
and conservation laws of specific kind can be
obtained with the methods described in
[
50
]
.

In this section we restrict ourselves to polynomial
conservation laws.
We show that polynomial conservation laws are obtained
from syzygies, that are easier to compute than
Darboux polynomials. Our methods

are more general and apply to models not covered
in
[
50
]
. However,
we do not cope with parametric exponential
conservation laws existing
in Lotka-Volterra models.

We also discuss monomial and
rational monomial conservation laws that can be
effectively computed without syzygies or Darboux polynomials.
We show that the computation of monomial conservation laws can be reduced to the
computation of linear conservation laws.
Finally we use the
databases
Biomodels

[
15
]
and
ODEbase

[
48
]
, to study
the scalability of our methods.

6.1
Computing Monomial Conservation Laws

The following proposition establishes a link between
linear and monomial conservation laws
(see Theorem 1 of
[
33
]
for a similar result)
.

Proposition 28
.

Let
E
be a system of ODEs given by

\dot{x_{1}}=f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\,\dots,\,\dot{x_{n}}=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})

and let
E^{\prime}
be the system of ODEs given by

\dot{x_{1}}=\frac{f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{x_{1}},\,\dots,\,\dot{x_{n}}=\frac{f_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{x_{n}}.

Then
E
admits a monomial conservation law if and only if
E^{\prime}
admits a linear conservation law with
integer coefficients. Moreover, if the linear conservation law for
E^{\prime}
is
m_{1}x_{1}+\dots+m_{n}x_{n}
then the monomial conservation law for
E
is
x_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{m_{n}}
.
The linear conservation laws of
E^{\prime}
are
simple
if and only if the
corresponding monomial conservation laws of
E
are
simple
.

Proof.

Let
M=x_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{m_{n}}
be a monomial. Then the derivative of
M
is

\dot{M}=\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\frac{\dot{x_{i}}}{x_{i}}\big)M.

The equivalences

\dot{M}=0\Longleftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\frac{\dot{x_{i}}}{x_{i}}=0\Longleftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\frac{f_{i}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{x_{i}}=0

prove the first part of the proposition.

Let
\phi=m_{1}x_{1}+\dots+m_{n}x_{n}
be the linear conservation law of
E^{\prime}
corresponding
to the monomial conservation law
M
of
E
.
Assume that
M
is
not simple
, say
M=M_{1}M_{2}
with

M_{k}=x_{1}^{m_{k1}}\cdots x_{n}^{m_{kn}}

and
\dot{M}_{k}=0
for
k\in\{1,2\}
.
Then by the first part

\dot{M}_{k}=0\Longleftrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{ki}\frac{\dot{x_{i}}}{x_{i}}=0

for
k\in\{1,2\}
.
Thus,
\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
, where
\phi_{k}=m_{k1}x_{1}+\dots+m_{kn}x_{n}
are linear conservation laws of
E^{\prime}
for

k\in\{1,2\}
.
This means that the linear conservation for
E^{\prime}
is
not simple
. The reverse follows from the same reasoning.
∎

We consider that
E^{\prime}
has the structure given by (
2
)
with a stoichiometric matrix
\boldsymbol{S}^{\prime}
.
This is always possible, eventually after using the Algorithm
1
.

6.2
Computing Polynomial Conservation Laws from Syzygies

In this section, we are going to compute parametric polynomial conservation laws. To this end, we will use results and techniques from computational algebra, that is, from Gröbner bases theory.
Our algorithm will be based on the computation of parametric syzygies for the polynomial right-hand sides in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k}]
of (
1
). Since these right-hand sides depend on the parameters
\boldsymbol{k}
, we will compute a comprehensive Gröbner system and compute for each branch the parametric syzygies. The comprehensive Gröbner system will give us a decomposition of the parameter space into semi-algebraic sets such that evaluating the parameters
\boldsymbol{k}
at every point of such a semi-algebraic set, the syzygies of the respective Gröbner basis will still be syzygies but with entries in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
.
Vector calculus will then tell us which syzygies are the gradients of polynomial functions in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
defining parametric conservation laws.

In the first subsection, we introduce the concept of syzygies for a polynomial system, and in the second subsection, we explain comprehensive Gröbner systems. The next subsection is devoted to establishing a connection between syzygies, conservative vector fields, and conservation laws. We also explain there why we need to consider the set of syzygies as a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space instead of a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k}]
-module. Having this connection, in the next subsection, we develop our algorithm. Finally, in the last subsection, we present the results and discuss our benchmark.

6.2.1
The
\mathbb{Q}
-Vector Space of Syzygies

In this section
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
denotes a polynomial ring in the indeterminates

\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
over the field of rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
. For
m
polynomials

F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})

of
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
we consider the ideal
I
of
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
generated by the polynomials in
F
.

Definition 32
.

A syzygy of
I
with respect to the basis
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
is an
element
\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\in\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
such that

g_{1}f_{1}+\ldots+g_{m}f_{m}=0.

A syzygy of the form

\boldsymbol{g}=(0,\dots,0,f_{j},0,\dots,0,-f_{i},0,\dots,0),

where
f_{j}
is the
i-
th and
-f_{i}
is the
j-
th entry of
\boldsymbol{g}
, is called a trivial syzygy. The set of all syzygies for
F
is denoted by
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
.

In the definition of a syzygy, some authors require

F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
to be a minimal generating set of the ideal

I
(for example
[
26
]
). However, for the purpose of this paper, we do not require such
a condition on the generators.
Moreover, it is well known that
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
form a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-submodule of
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
.

Remark 33
.

In this section, for computational purposes, we consider only
polynomials over the rational numbers, that is polynomials in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
. Note that since
\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
all computations are also valid over
\mathbb{R}
and so all results can be considered as polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[\boldsymbol{x}]
making them applicable to CRNs.

Example 34
.

Consider the ideal
I=\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},x_{2}]
. Since

(-x_{2})x_{1}+(x_{1})x_{2}=0,

the vector
\boldsymbol{g}=(-x_{2},x_{1})\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},x_{2}]^{2}
is a trivial syzygy of the ideal
I

with respect to
F=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
. One can easily see that the
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},x_{2}]-
module
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
is generated by
\boldsymbol{g}
, that is, every syzygy is a multiplication of
\boldsymbol{g}
by a polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

Syzygies have been extensively studied in commutative algebra and
algebraic geometry. Buchberger’s algorithm for computing Gröbner
bases
[
14
,
10
]
leads to an algorithm which
computes a basis
of
a syzygy module (see Schreyer’s results in

[
9
,
59
]
). One can also obtain a basis
of the syzygy module of
I
with respect to a generating set
F
as a bi-product of computing a Gröbner basis of
I
via
signature-based algorithms
[
31
,
72
,
37
]
. Many
computer algebra systems such as Singular, CoCoA, etc. include
implementations for syzygy computations. For more on syzygies and in
particular on algorithms, we refer to the books
of Weispfenning
[
8
]
and Cox, Little &
O’Shea
[
17
, Chapter 5.3]
.

Above we mentioned that
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
is a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-module. It is clear that
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space. We will need to consider here the structure of
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
as a vector space instead of a module since for the computation of conservation laws we need to restrict to syzygies with a specific property which is only respected by the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space structure and not by the
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-module structure as we will see in the subsections
6.2.3
and
6.2.4
.

Lemma 35
.

Let
G
be a Gröbner basis for the
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-module
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
and denote the set of monomials in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
by
[\boldsymbol{x}]
. Consider
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
as a vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then

\mathrm{Gen}(\operatorname{Syz}(F)):=\{u\boldsymbol{g}\mid\boldsymbol{g}\in G,\ u\in[\boldsymbol{x}]\}

is a generating set for the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{s}\in\operatorname{Syz}(F)
be a syzygy. Since
G=\{\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{g}_{r}\}
is a Gröbner basis for the
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
-module
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
, there
are
h_{1},\dots,h_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]
such that

\boldsymbol{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}.

For each
h_{i}
there are monomials
u_{ij}
with
j=1,\dots,r_{i}
with some
r_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and constants
c_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that

h_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}c_{ij}u_{ij}.

We conclude that

\boldsymbol{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}h_{i}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{i}}c_{ij}u_{ij}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}.

is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of elements of
\{u\boldsymbol{g}\mid\boldsymbol{g}\in G,\ u\in[\boldsymbol{x}]\}
.
∎

Remark 36
.

If we choose a non-negative integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
, then the syzygies

\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}:=\{\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})\in\operatorname{Syz}(F)\mid deg(g_{i})\leq d,\quad 1\leq i\leq m\},

of degree at most
d
form a finite dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector subspace of
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
. By the proof of Proposition 4, Chap. 9, Section 3 in
[
18
]
,
if
G
is a Gröbner basis with respect to a degree ordering
, then the set

\mathrm{Gen}(\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}):=\{u\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathrm{Gen}(\operatorname{Syz}(F))\mid deg(ug_{i})\leq d,\ 1\leq i\leq m\}

is a finite generating set for
\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}
. Furthermore, those elements of
\mathrm{Gen}(\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d})
whose leading terms are different form a basis for the vector space
\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}
.
Alternatively, one can use Gaussian elimination to determine a basis of
\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}
from
\mathrm{Gen}(\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d})
.

6.2.2
Comprehensive Gröbner Systems

To compute parametric conservation laws from syzygies for CRNs we need to handle polynomial equations whose coefficients are again polynomials in the parameters, that is polynomials

F=(f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,f_{m}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}))

in the ring
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k}][\boldsymbol{x}]
in the indeterminates
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
over the coefficient ring
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k}]
in the indeterminates
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})
. The respective tool from computational algebra is the notion of a comprehensive Gröbner system.

A specialisation of

\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k}]
is a homomorphism
\sigma:\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k}]\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
. Every
element
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Q}^{r}
defines a specialisation

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
by evaluating
\boldsymbol{k}
at

\boldsymbol{\alpha}

and it can be canonically extended to a
specialisation

\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k}][\boldsymbol{x}]\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}].

Definition 37
.

Let
F
and
G_{1},\dots,G_{l}
be finite subsets of

\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k}][\boldsymbol{x}]
. Further let
A_{1},\dots,A_{l}
be semi-algebraic
sets of
\mathbb{Q}^{r}
and let
S\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{r}
such that

S\subset A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{l}
. A finite set

\mathcal{G}=\{(A_{1},G_{1}),\dots,(A_{l},G_{l})\}

is called a

comprehensive Gröbner system (CGS)
on
S
for
F
if for all

i=1,\dots,l
the set
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(G_{i})
is a
Gröbner basis for the ideal

\langle\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(F)\rangle\subset\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]

for every
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in A_{i}
. Each
(A_{i},G_{i})
is called
a branch of
\cal{G}
. The generic branch corresponds to the
semi-algebraic set that assumes all leading coefficients are
non-zero and ignores the other cases.
If
S=\mathbb{Q}^{r}
, then

\cal{G}
is called a comprehensive Gröbner system for
F
.

Comprehensive Gröbner systems were introduced by Weispfenning in
1992 in
[
74
]
. Independent of Weispfenning, Kapur
introduced the same concept in
[
40
]
, calling it parametric
Gröbner bases. Today in the literature there are many publications
dealing with the theory and algorithmics of CGSs, for example

[
70
,
53
,
19
,
46
,
42
,
41
,
36
]
only mentioning a few.
For a survey paper on this topic see
[
47
]
.
Moreover, several computer algebra systems provide implementations of
algorithms to compute comprehensive Gröbner systems, such as
Reduce

[
38
,
39
,
23
,
67
]
and
Singular

[
20
]
.

Lemma 38

(cf. Lemma 3.7, Chapter 5.3,
[
17
]
)
.

Let
\{(A_{1},G_{1}),\dots,(A_{l},G_{l})\}
be a CGS of the ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
generated by

F=(f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,f_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})).

For every
i
,
1\leq I\leq l
, there exists a linear map that converts a basis of the syzygy modules
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
to a basis of the syzygy module
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
. Also, there exists a map that converts a basis of the syzygy module
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
into a basis of the parametric syzygies for
F
only valid on the semi-algebraic sets
A_{i}
.

Proof.

Consider a branch
(A_{i},G_{i})
of the given CGS. In the following, we mean by
G_{i}
the vector of elements of the Gröbner basis
G_{i}
instead of the set
G_{i}
. One can use the algorithms described in
[
17
, Chapter 5.3]
to compute a basis of the
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
-module
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
of the ideal generated by the elements of
G_{i}
.
If
\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
, then for every
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in A_{i}
, the specialisation
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{g})
will be a syzygy of the ideal generated by
\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(G_{i})
. Following
[
17
, Chapter 5.3, Lemma 3.7]
, there exist matrices
B^{1,i}
and
B^{2,i}
such that

G_{i}B^{1,i}=F^{T}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad FB^{2,i}=G_{i}^{T}.

We obtain that for every
\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
the product
(B^{2,i}\boldsymbol{g}^{T})^{T}
is a syzygy of
F
. On the other hand for every
\boldsymbol{f}\in\operatorname{Syz}(F)
we have

(B^{1,i}\boldsymbol{f}^{T})^{T}\in\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
. The linear map
B^{1,i}
between the syzygy modules
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
and
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
is not necessarily one-to-one, and one
may
not obtain a basis for
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
by converting a basis of
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
via
B^{2,i}
. However, the kernel of
B^{1,i}
will give us the missing elements of a basis of
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
. Moreover, we have

G_{i}\boldsymbol{g}^{T}=(B^{2,i})^{T}F^{T}\boldsymbol{g}^{T}=(B^{2,i})^{T}F^{T}B^{1,i}\boldsymbol{f}^{T}=F^{T}\boldsymbol{f},

since
F^{T}=G_{i}B^{1,i}=(B^{2,i})^{T}F^{T}B^{1,i}
. Applying
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
to the equation we obtain

0=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(G_{i})\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{g})=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(F)\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{f}),

and therefore,

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{f})
is a syzygy of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(F)
for every
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in A_{i}
. Hence, having computed a CGS of the ideal generated by
F
with branches
(A_{i},G_{i})
and bases of the syzygy modules
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
, we obtain bases of the parametric syzygies for
F
only valid on the semi-algebraic sets
A_{i}
by applying the conversion matrices described above to the bases elements of
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
.
∎

Similar to the above lemma, one can use
[
17
, Proposition 3.8, Chapter 5.3]
in order to construct a matrix that converts Gröbner bases for
\operatorname{Syz}(G_{i})
into a Gröbner basis for
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
, respecting the relevant specialisations.

Example 39
.

We consider here the ideal generated by the polynomials

F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=(-k_{1}x_{1},-k_{2}x_{2},(k_{1}+k_{2})x_{1}x_{2}).

Computing a CGS of the ideal generated by
F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
we obtain the four branches

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(\langle 0\rangle)\setminus V(\langle k_{1}k_{2}\rangle),\ G_{1}=(k_{2}x_{2},\ k_{1}x_{1}),

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(\langle k_{2}\rangle)\setminus V(\langle k_{1}\rangle),\ G_{2}=(k_{1}x_{1}),

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{V(\langle k_{2},k_{1}\rangle)\setminus V(\langle 1\rangle)\},\ G_{3}=(0),

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(\langle k_{1}\rangle)\setminus V(\langle k_{2}\rangle),\ G_{4}=(k_{2}x_{2}).

We only look at the first branch which is the generic branch. We compute the conversion matrices

B^{1,1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&x_{1}(k_{1}+k_{2})/k_{2}\\
-1&0&0\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{and}\ B^{2,1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
-1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

which satisfy
G_{1}B^{1,1}=F^{T}
and
FB^{2,1}=G_{1}^{T}
.
The Syzygy module of
G_{1}
is generated by
\boldsymbol{g}=(-k_{1}x_{1},k_{2}x_{2})
. Computing
(B^{2,1}\boldsymbol{g}^{T})^{T}
, one obtains the syzygy
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=(-k_{2}x_{2},k_{1}x_{1},0)
of
F
.
The kernel of the matrix
B^{1,1}
gives us the additional syzygy
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}=(0,k_{1}x_{1}+k_{2}x_{1},k_{2})
of
F
. The syzygy module of
F
is generated by
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}
.
Alternatively, one can find the whole generating set of
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
using
[
17
, Proposition 3.8]

\operatorname{Syz}(F)=\langle As_{ij},\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{g}_{m}\rangle.

Example 40
.

We consider the system

F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})(x_{2}+x_{1},x_{2}+x_{1}+1,k_{1}x_{2})

This example shows how the system and its syzygies change in the different branches. We have here two branches.
In the second branch, we have
k_{1}=0
and so we only find the trivial syzygy
\boldsymbol{f}=(-f_{2},f_{1},0)
. In the generic branch, the parameter has to satisfy
k_{1}\neq 0
. Here we find more and non-trivial syzygies, namely

\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=(-k_{1}x_{1}-k_{1},k_{1}x_{1},1)\ \mathrm{and}\ \boldsymbol{f}_{2}=(-k_{1}x_{2},k_{1}x_{2},-1).

Example 41
.

We consider the system

F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=(x_{1}+k_{1}x_{2}+k_{2},k_{1}x_{1}+x_{2}+k_{2}).

As in the example above we want to see how the system and its syzygies change in the different branches.
In the first branch, that is the generic one, the parameters have to satisfy
k_{1}\neq 0
,
k_{2}\neq 0
and we find that the syzygy module is generated by the trivial syzygy
\boldsymbol{g}=(k_{1}x_{1}+x_{2}+k_{2},-x_{1}-k_{1}x_{2}-k_{2})
. In the second branch the parameters are
k_{1}=-1
,
k_{2}\neq 0
and so the system reduces to

F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=(x_{1}-x_{2}+k_{2},-x_{1}+x_{2}+k_{2}).

Again we only find the nonzero trivial syzygy here. In the third branch, the parameters have values

k_{1}=1
,
k_{2}=0
which reduces our system to

F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{1}+x_{2}).

Here the syzygy module will be generated by
\boldsymbol{f}=(1,-1)
.

6.2.3
The Connection between Syzygies and Conservation Laws

In this section, we consider system (
1
), that is the system of ODEs of the form

\dot{x}_{1}=f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\,\ldots,\,\dot{x}_{n}=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{Z}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}].

We will explain how a parametric conservation law of this ODE system leads to a syzygy of the ideal generated by
F=(f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\,\ldots,\,f_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}))
and how one can obtain parametric conservation laws from specific elements of
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
.

We remind that the curl of the vector field
\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,g_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}))
is defined as the anti-symmetric tensor field
\curl\boldsymbol{g}
with entries

\frac{\partial g_{i}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial g_{j}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\quad\mathrm{with}\ 1\leq i,j\leq n.

Definition 42
.

1.

For the dynamical system
1
and its right hand side
F
we define the set of conservative syzygies as

\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F):=\{\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}(F)\mid\curl\boldsymbol{g}=0\}.

2.

For a positive integer
d\in\mathbb{N}
we define the set of conservative syzygies of degree at most
d
as

\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)_{d}:=\{\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}\mid\curl\boldsymbol{g}=0\}.

3.

We define
\mathcal{C}(F)\subset\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
to be the set of polynomial conservation laws of
F
.

Proposition 43
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between conservation laws and conservative syzygies:

\varphi_{1}:\mathcal{C}(F)\rightarrow\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F),\quad\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\mapsto\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\quad and\quad\varphi_{2}:\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}(F),\quad\boldsymbol{g}\mapsto\int\boldsymbol{g}

with
\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}=id_{\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)}
and
\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}=id_{\mathcal{C}(F)}
, where
\int\boldsymbol{g}
denotes the potential of a conservative vector field
\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
.

Proof.

First, we show that
\varphi_{1}
is well-defined. Let
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{C}(F)
, that is, let
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
be a parametric exact polynomial conservation law for (
1
) with semi-algebraic set
V_{\boldsymbol{k}}
for the parameters
\boldsymbol{k}
. We fix this semialgebraic set throughout the proof and ignore
k
. By Definition
8
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})f_{i}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{k}\in V_{\boldsymbol{k}},\ \forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

which implies that

\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{1}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\,\dots,\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{n}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\right)\in\operatorname{Syz}(F).

In other words, this means that the gradient

\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{1}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\,\dots,\,\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{n}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\right)

of a parametric polynomial conservation law
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is a syzygy of the ideal generated by
F
when
\boldsymbol{k}\in V_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Moreover, by well-known identities of gradient and curl, we have that

\curl(\grad\phi)=0

and so
\gradient\phi\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
. This shows that
\varphi_{1}
is well-defined.

Second, we show that
\varphi_{2}
is well-defined. Assume that
\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
is a parametric syzygy of the ideal generated by
F
with semi-algebraic set
V_{\boldsymbol{k}}
obtained from the comprehensive Gröbner system described at the end of subsection
6.2.2
. Therefore,

f_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})g_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})+\dots+f_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})g_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=0

for points
\boldsymbol{k}\in V_{\boldsymbol{k}}
.

It is well-known in vector calculus that a potential function
\phi
for
\boldsymbol{g}
as a vector field, is well-defined in a simply connected domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
if only if
\curl\boldsymbol{g}=0
everywhere in
\mathcal{D}
, i.e., only when
\boldsymbol{g}
defines a conservative (or irrotational) vector field in
\mathcal{D}
(see
[
66
]
). In this case,
\phi
satisfies

\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{g}.

By well-known vector calculus method, one can integrate
\boldsymbol{g}
along a suitable path to obtain the corresponding potential function. For the sake of self-containedness, we explain one possible integration below. Consider the differential form
d\boldsymbol{g}=g_{1}dx_{1}+\dots+g_{n}dx_{n}
and take a piecewise smooth curve connecting the origin with
\boldsymbol{x}
, for instance

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\{(sx_{1},0,\dots,0)\mid s\in[0,1]\}\cup

\displaystyle\{(x_{1},sx_{2},0,\dots,0)\mid s\in[0,1]\}\cup\dots\cup\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},sx_{n})\mid s\in[0,1]\}.

Integrating the differential form
d\boldsymbol{g}
along the curve

\mathcal{C}
yields

\int_{0}^{x_{1}}g_{1}(\cdot,0,...,0)dx_{1}+\int_{0}^{x_{2}}g_{2}(x_{1},\cdot,0,...,0)dx_{2}+\dots+\int_{0}^{x_{n}}g_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{n-1},\cdot)dx_{n},

(8)

where the constant of the integration is assumed to be zero. We denote this integration (with constant being zero) by
\int\boldsymbol{g}
.
Denote that if
\curl\boldsymbol{g}\neq 0
,
the result of the integration depends on the path
\mathcal{C}
and
there is no function
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
satisfying

\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{g}
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Therefore, if
\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
, then integration implies that
\int\boldsymbol{g}
is an element of
\mathcal{C}(F)
as we showed above. This shows that the definition of the map
\varphi_{2}
is correct.

Having shown that both
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are well-defined, we just need to show that
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are inverse to each other.
The identity
\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{1}=id_{\mathcal{C}(F)}
follows from
\int\gradient\phi=\phi
. Moreover,
since
\gradient\int\boldsymbol{g}=\boldsymbol{g}
, we have
\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}=id_{\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)}
.
∎

Remark 44
.

In vector calculus, there are other standard methods to compute from a conservative syzygy

\boldsymbol{g}=(g_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}),\dots,g_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}))
, the corresponding conservation law
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
. One can first start with integrating
g_{n}
with respect to
x_{n}
, obtaining

\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{x_{n}}g_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})dx_{n}+c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}),

where the integration constant
c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
is in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}]
. Let
p_{n}:=\int_{x_{n}}g_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})dx_{n}
where we choose the integration constant to be zero.
Then in order to compute
c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
, one can use the fact that
\frac{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{n-1}}=g_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
and obtain an equation for
\frac{\partial c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})}{\partial x_{n-1}}
, namely

\frac{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{n-1}}=\frac{\partial p_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{n-1}}+\frac{\partial c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})}{\partial x_{n-1}}.

Replacing
\frac{\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{n-1}}
with
g_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
one obtains

g_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\partial p_{n}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_{n-1}}+\frac{\partial c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})}{\partial x_{n-1}}.

As we know the polynomials
g_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
and
\frac{\partial p_{n}}{\partial x_{n-1}}
, one can obtain
equations for the coefficients of
\frac{\partial c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})}{\partial x_{n-1}}
. Continuing the above procedure by taking partial derivatives of
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
with respect to the variables
x_{n-2}
, replacing it with
g_{n-2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
, one can obtain more equations for the coefficients of the integration constant
c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
. As
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is a conservative syzygy, the existence of a conservation law
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
such that
\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is guaranteed, which implies that the equations obtained for the coefficients of
c_{n-1}(\boldsymbol{k},x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
yields a unique solution.

An alternative method for integration is considering the following integration

\phi=\int g_{1}dx_{1}+\dots\int g_{n}dx_{n}+C,

(9)

and then removing the duplicated terms that show up in the integrals.

Example 45
.

Consider the trivial syzygy
\boldsymbol{g}=(-x_{2},x_{1})
for the ideal in
\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},x_{2}]
generated by

arising from this particular syzygy is not irrotational, i.e.,

with the trivial syzygy

\boldsymbol{g}=(x_{2},x_{1})

is a constant.

Example 46

(Linear Conservation Laws)
.

Assume that
F
admits a degree zero syzygy
\boldsymbol{g}
. As the entries of a degree

\curl\boldsymbol{g}=0
and so a degree zero syzygy is conservative. This means that
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)_{0}=\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{0}

Conservation laws corresponding to degree zero syzygies are the well-known linear conservation laws.

A syzygy of the form

Remark 48
.

1.

First we want to point out that
\mathcal{C}(F)
is not necessarily an ideal in the ring
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
. Indeed, let
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{C}(F)
,
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\neq 0
and

h\in\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
. Then by the chain rule, we have

\gradient(h \phi)=h\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})+\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient h.

Since
\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is a conservation law, that is
\gradient\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\cdot F=0
, we obtain that

\gradient(h \phi(\vk,\vx))\cdot F=(h\gradient\phi+\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient h)\cdot F=\phi(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})(\gradient h\cdot F)

and
\gradient h\cdot F
is in general not zero as the following example shows. In Example
45
, for the generating set
F=(x_{1},-x_{2})
, we found the conservation law
\phi(\boldsymbol{x})=x_{1}x_{2}
. One easily checks that the product
x_{1}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
is not a conservation law.

2.

The set
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
is not necessarily a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
-submodule of the
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
-module

\operatorname{Syz}(F)
. Indeed, for
h\in\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
and
\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
we obtain that

\curl(h \vg)=h(\curl\boldsymbol{g})+\gradient h\times\boldsymbol{g},

where we mean by
\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{v}
for two vectors
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
the vector with entries

u_{i}v_{j}-u_{j}v_{i},\quad\mathrm{with}\ 1\leq i,j\leq n,\ i\neq j.

As
\boldsymbol{g}\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
, we have that
\curl\boldsymbol{g}=0
, however, not
necessarily
\gradient h\times\boldsymbol{g}=0
as the example at the end shows.
Therefore,
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
is not
necessarily closed under multiplication by the elements of

\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
, which implies that
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
is not necessarily a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
-submodule of

\operatorname{Syz}(F)
.

As a counter-example, consider again the generating set

F=(x_{1},-x_{2})
in Example
45
. It yields the conservative syzygy
\boldsymbol{g}=(x_{2},x_{1})
. However, one easily checks that the product
x_{1}\boldsymbol{g}
is not a conservative syzygy, that is
\curl(x_1\vg)\neq 0
.

Remark 49
.

We want to point out that by Remark
48
,
2
it is not sufficient to check only the elements of a basis of the syzygies module
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
to be conservative to obtain from the ones which are conservative all of
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
, since
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
has not the structure of a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
-module.

Proposition 50
.

The set
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space.

Proof.

It is easy to check that the operator
\curl(\cdot)
is
\mathbb{Q}
-linear, that is for
c\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]^{n}
we have

\curl(c \vg_1 + \vg_2)=c\curl\boldsymbol{g}_{1}+\curl\boldsymbol{g}_{2}.

Thus, if
\boldsymbol{g}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{2}\in\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
, then
\curl(c \vg_1 + \vg_2)=0
and so
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space.
∎

We will use the
\mathbb{Q}-
vector space structure of
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
for computing conservation laws. Apart from this, the conservation laws form an algebra. Although this is well-known in the literature on first integrals (e.g., see Arnold’s book
[
4
, §10.3]
), we present it here and detail the proof, not only for the sake of self-containedness, but also because we will refer to the algebra structure of conservation laws and discuss its generators when we present our benchmark results.

Proposition 51
.

The set
\mathcal{C}(F)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-subalgebra of
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
.

Proof.

Let
\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
,
\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{C}(F)
be two conservation laws. The chain rule implies
that

\gradient(\phi_1(\vk,\vx) \phi_2(\vk,\vx) )=\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})+\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}).

Moreover, since
\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
,
\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
are conservation laws, we have that

\gradient\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\cdot F=\gradient\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\cdot F=0
and so we obtain

\displaystyle\gradient(\phi_1(\vk,\vx)\phi_2(\vk,\vx))\cdot F

\displaystyle=\left(\phi_{2}\gradient\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})+\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\right)\cdot F

\displaystyle=\left(\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\cdot F\right)+\left(\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\gradient\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\cdot F\right)

\displaystyle=0+0.

So the product of two conservation laws
is a conservation law is again a conservation law. Since
\gradient
is additive,
\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})+\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is again a conservation law. Finally, for
c\in\mathbb{Q}
we have

\gradient(c \phi_1(\vk,\vx))\cdot F=c(\gradient\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})\cdot F)=0,

that is
c\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is again a conservation law.
This implies that
\mathcal{C}(F)
is a

\mathbb{Q}
-subalgebra of
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
.
∎

6.2.4
Computing Conservation Laws from Syzygies

As pointed out in Remark
49
, conservative syzygies do not form a
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
-module, and hence, conservative syzygies cannot be generated by the conservative elements of a basis for the syzygy module. However, we have seen in Proposition
50
that conservative syzygies form a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space. Restricting to conservative syzygies up to degree
d
, one can compute a finite basis for the vector space
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)_{d}
via a basis of the syzygy module
\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}
by the
\mathbb{Q}
-linearity of

\curl(\cdot)
.

Proposition 52
.

1.

The set

V_{\gradient}(F)=\{\curl\boldsymbol{f}\mid\boldsymbol{f}\in\operatorname{Syz}(F)\}

is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space.

2.

The curl induces a surjective
\mathbb{Q}
-linear map

\curl(\cdot):\operatorname{Syz}(F)\rightarrow V_{\gradient}(F),\ \boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\curl\boldsymbol{f}

with

\mathrm{ker}(\curl(\cdot))=\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F).

Proof.

1.

Since
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space and
\curl(\cdot)
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-linear operator, its image is a
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space.

2.

The map
\curl(\cdot)
is clearly
\mathbb{Q}
-linear and surjective. Obviously every element of
\mathrm{ker}(\curl(\cdot))
is an element of
\operatorname{Syz}_{\gradient}(F)
and every syzygy
\boldsymbol{g}
with
\curl\boldsymbol{g}=0
lies in the kernel.

∎

We remind that a basis for the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of syzygies up to a given degree bound
d\in\mathbb{N}
can be computed as in Remark (
36
). Then one can apply the curl operator and compute its kernel in order to obtain a basis for the conservative syzygies up to degree
d
.

Remark 54

Remark 55
.

By Definition
8
parametric conservation laws
depend on the rate constants
\boldsymbol{k}
, whereas unconditional conservation
laws are independent of
\boldsymbol{k}
. If
\phi
is an unconditional
conservation law, then it can be seen as a parametric conservation law as well,
hence,
\phi
must be obtained from a syzygy that does not contain
variables
\boldsymbol{k}
. Therefore, in order to compute unconditional conservation
laws, we need to find those syzygies of the ideal generated by
F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
that only depend on the variables
\boldsymbol{x}
and not on
\boldsymbol{k}
. To this end, we compute directly a basis of the syzygy module of
F(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
without the detour of the CGS using the elimination property of Gröbner bases. We fix a block term order on the variables
\boldsymbol{k}
,
\boldsymbol{x}
with
\boldsymbol{k}>\boldsymbol{x}
. If
G
is a Gröbner basis for
\operatorname{Syz}(F)
with respect to the block order, then

G\cap\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
is a Gröbner basis for
\operatorname{Syz}(F)\cap\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
, see
[
44
, Thm 3.4.5]
.
This means that in order to compute unconditional conservation laws,
one can modify Algorithm
7
by adding a computation
of
G\cap\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
. Also in order to compute a generating set for
\operatorname{Syz}(F)_{d}\cap\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
one needs to multiply the elements of the Gröbner basis
G\cap\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x}]^{m}
with the monomials only in
\boldsymbol{x}
.

Example 57

For the system

x_{2}

x_{3}

\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent syzygies. We compute for these syzygies the images under the map
\curl(\cdot)
and then the
\mathbb{Q}
-linear dependencies between the results. We obtain that the kernel of the linear map
\curl(\cdot)
has a basis formed by the elements

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=(0,x_{2},-1),\ \boldsymbol{f}_{2}=(0,x_{2}^{3}-2x_{2}x_{3},-x_{2}^{2}+2x_{3}),\ \boldsymbol{f}_{3}=(x_{1},-x_{2},0),

Note that
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}

\boldsymbol{f}_{3}

\phi_{2}

and
\phi_{2}

(x_{2}^{2}-2x_{3})\ \boldsymbol{f}_{1}=2\phi_{1}\boldsymbol{f}_{2}

\boldsymbol{f}_{5}

.

\phi_{1}=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}.

The other conservative syzygy

(x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+x_{2}x_{3},x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{3},x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3})

leads to the degree three conservation law

\phi_{2}=\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+\frac{1}{2}x_{3}^{2}.

One can easily see that
\phi_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\phi_{1}^{2}
, hence the two conservation laws computed are algebraically dependent.
One can check that the second element of the module basis is not conservative, because

\curl(-k_2x_2,k_1x_1,0)=(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}(-k_{2}x_{2})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}(k_{1}x_{1})=-(k_{1}+k_{2}),0,0)\neq 0.

Note that

\{((k_{1}+k_{2})x_{2},0,k_{1}),(-k_{2}x_{2},k_{1}x_{1},0)\}

is another basis of the syzygy module of the generic branch, in which both elements have non-zero curl, but the linear combination of the basis elements

((k_{1}+k_{2})x_{2},0,k_{1})+(-k_{2}x_{2},k_{1}x_{1},0)=k_{1}(x_{2},x_{1},1)

has zero curl and it is the only one
of degree two and up to multiplication.

Computations for all branches of the dynamical system in this example were carried out using our Singular library
polconslaw.lib
. The reader can use
example6_30.sg
, available in our Github and Zenodo Repositories, in order to carry on the computations automatically.

\displaystyle-k_{2}x_{1}x_{3}+k_{3}x_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{x_{2}}=

\displaystyle k_{2}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{3}x_{2}-k_{4}x_{2}x_{4}+k_{5}x_{5},

\displaystyle\dot{x_{3}}=

\displaystyle-k_{2}x_{1}x_{3}+k_{3}x_{2},

\displaystyle\dot{x_{4}}=

\displaystyle-k_{4}x_{2}x_{4}+k_{5}x_{5},

Computations for generic branch using our Singular library
polconslaw.lib
can be found in the file gbm629.txt, and computations for all branches using the Singular library
genpolconslaw.lib
can be carried on automatically via the file

, which is available in our Github and Zenodo Repositories.

A comprehensive Gröbner system for the steady state ideal consists
of 10 branches. We consider first the generic branch and after that
one nongeneric branch.
One easily checks that the semi-algebraic set of the generic branch of the comprehensive
Gröbner basis consists of
\{k_{2}\neq 0\ ,k_{4}\neq 0\}
, and the corresponding polynomial set consists of the five polynomials in the right-hand sides of the ODE system. The syzygy module has a basis

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=(-1,0,1,0,0),\ \boldsymbol{f}_{2}=(0,-1,-1,1,0),\ \boldsymbol{f}_{3}=(0,0,0,1,1),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{4}=(k_{2}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{4}x_{2}x_{4}-k_{3}x_{2}+k_{5}x_{5},k_{2}x_{1}x_{3}-k_{3}x_{2},0,0,0)\}.

Obviously,
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{f}_{3}
are conservative, and integrating them leads to the linearly independent unconditional conservation laws

\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=-x_{1}+x_{3},\ \phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=-x_{2}-x_{3}+x_{4},\ \phi_{3}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})=x_{4}+x_{5}.

The syzygy
\boldsymbol{f}_{4}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
is not conservative
as
\curl\boldsymbol{f}_{4}\neq 0
. Multiplying
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}
,
\dots
,
\boldsymbol{f}_{4}
with all monomials such that the degree of the products is at most two, we obtain an
18
dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vectors space of syzygies and find nine conservation laws, where the six new found degree two conservation laws can be expressed as linear combinations of
\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
,
\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
and
\phi_{3}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{k}]
.
Enlarging the degree by one gives us a
64
dimensional
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space of syzygies. Our algorithm finds here
10
new conservation conservation laws of degree three. Again these conservation laws are in the algebra generated by
\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
,
\phi_{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
and
\phi_{3}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x})
. One may assume that it is enough to check the generators of the syzygy module to be conservative and that all further conservation laws can be obtained as a linear combination of the generators that are conservative over
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
. But the last paragraph of Example
58
shows that this is not true.

As discussed earlier in Remark
55
one
can compute unconditional conservation laws from the syzygy module by
an additional Gröbner basis computation.
On the other hand, unconditional conservation laws up to a given degree bound can be computed via ansatz, i.e., guessing the conservation laws with undetermined coefficients, and then obtaining coefficients by imposing the definition of first integrability. This method has been discussed in
[
35
]
for computing polynomial first integrals of dynamical systems and it has been implemented in the automated prover tool Pegasus
[
64
]
.

Computations based on an ansatz avoid Gröbner basis (and syzygy) computations in the ring
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
.
However, computing polynomial conservation laws via ansatz would not necessarily lead to a basis in
\mathbb{Q}[\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{x}]
for the syzygy module, but rather to a basis for the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space of conservative syzygies up to a given degree.
Moreover, the ansatz method can not be used to compute branches of parametric conservation laws.

Finally, using syzygies brings an algebraic structure to the problem of first integral computation.
Since this paper is the first step in this particular research direction, we are confident that there exists ample room for exploration regarding the algebraic characteristics of the conservation laws. In particular, we could anticipate the
possibility of finding a
basis for the algebra of conservation laws con be found directly, rather than
computing a
\mathbb{Q}-
vector space basis for syzygies and subsequently filtering them via curl operator.

\mathbb{Q}

Furthermore, the generators other than those coming from a basis of the syzygy module are rare. This subject will be further developed elsewhere.

2.

Secondly, we did a full implementation of our algorithm, in order to compute a basis for the

Below is an interpretation of the four tables produced out of our experiments.

•

Table
3
shows the result of computations of the generic branch, performed using our Singular library
genpolconslaw.lib
, only computing the basis for the syzygy module and not considering the vector space of syzygies up to a given degree. This results in computing some conservation laws that correspond to the conservative elements of the syzygy module basis.
The computations either finished within a few seconds or did not finish after one hour. This is due to the EXPSPACE complexity of the syzygy computations (which is the same as Gröbner bases basis computation complexity). Another interesting observation concerning Table
3
is the large proportion of irrotational syzygies. For example, for Biomodel 270, there are 956 syzygies (computed just within a second), out of which only 10 syzygies are irrotational.
Furthermore,

polconslaw.lib
. As explained in Section
6.2
, parametric polynomial conservation laws result from non-generic branches. Interestingly, most of the parametric conservation laws that are computed within one hour from the non-generic branches are polynomial. Again, we must clarify that we only considered a basis of the syzygy module, but not a basis for the
Q
-vector space of syzygies up to a given degree. This results in computing only those conservation laws that arise

x_{1}=const.
of the Biomodel 6.

computations in the generic branch are finished within one hour,
but that is not the case for parametric polynomial conservation law
computations. This is because when the number of branches of CGS is
high, computing syzygies for all of those branches can be expensive.

We note that our implemented libraries are prototype implementations of our algorithm and there is a lot of room for improvement in terms of speed and memory. For instance, one can ask the algorithm to first compute the conservative syzygies obtained from a basis of the syzygy module and then continue with computing the
Q

r
independent linear conservation
laws generate the linearly independent polynomial
conservation laws
\phi_{1}^{n_{1}}\phi_{2}^{n_{2}}\ldots\phi_{r}^{n_{r}}
of degree at most
d
,
where
n_{1},\ldots,n_{r}
satisfy

1\leq n_{1}+n_{2}+\ldots+n_{r}\leq d,n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},1\leq i\leq r.

(10)

The inequalities (
10
) have

\binom{r+d}{d}-1
distinct solutions.
It follows that

r
linearly independent linear conservation laws generate

\binom{r+d}{d}-1
linearly independent polynomial conservation laws.

There are
5,20,55
such generated polynomial conservation laws for
d=5
and
r=1,2,3
linear conservation laws, respectively. Comparing these numbers with the dimension of the linear space of polynomial conservation laws, given in Tables
5
