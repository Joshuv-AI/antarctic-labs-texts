Massive scalar field in de Sitter spacetime: a two-loop calculation and a comparison with the stochastic approach Journal: Eur. Phys. J. C
Alexander Yu. Kamenshchik e1,addr1,addr2, Alexei A. Starobinsky e2,addr2,addr3, Tereza Vardanyan e3,addr4

Abstract
We examine long-wavelength correlation functions of massive scalar fields in de Sitter spacetime. For the theory with a quartic self-interaction, the two-point function is calculated up to two loops. Comparing our results with the Hartree-Fock approximation and with the stochastic approach shows that the former resums only the cactus type diagrams, whereas the latter contains the sunset diagram as well and produces the correct result. We also demonstrate that the long-wavelength expectation value of the commutator of two fields is equal to zero both for spacelike and timelike separated points.

Introduction

Quantum field theory in expanding spacetimes is crucial for exploring the origin and the evolution of the universe. Scalar fields in de Sitter spacetime are of particular importance for understanding the period of inflation and the growth of quantum fluctuations during this period.

Free scalar fields on the de Sitter background—both massive and massless—have been studied extensively (see, e.g.,
ChT
;
Tagirov
;
BD
;
Linde:1982uu
;
Starobinsky:1982ee
;
Vilenkin:1982wt
;
Allen:1985ux
;
Allen:1987tz
). It has been shown that for massive fields there exists a one-parameter family of de Sitter-invariant states; this includes the Bunch-Davies vacuum, whose modes in the Poincare patch match with the adiabatic in-vacuum modes in the remote past. From this family, only the Bunch-Davies vacuum has finite renormalized values of
\langle\phi^{2}\rangle
and the energy-momentum tensor of the scalar field.

The massless minimally coupled scalar field also has the Bunch-Davies state, but it is no longer de Sitter invariant. It is, however, invariant under the
\rm E(3)
subgroup, which is the group of translations and rotations in the flat spatial sections of de Sitter spacetime. One of the manifestations of the breakdown of de Sitter invariance is the presence of an infrared divergence in the theory. Hence, to evaluate correlation functions, an infrared cutoff should be introduced in momentum space. As a result, expectation values of products of fields are explicitly time-dependent; for example,

\langle\phi^{2}(\vec{x},t)\rangle_{\rm ren}={H^{3}t\over 4\pi^{2}}+{\rm Constant}\,,

where
H
is the constant Hubble parameter in the Poincare patch of de Sitter space (see (
3
) below) and
t=0
denotes the beginning of de Sitter stage
Linde:1982uu
;
Starobinsky:1982ee
;
Vilenkin:1982wt
(see also
Allen:1985ux
), e.g., arising from a previous generic anisotropic and inhomogeneous stage with curvature much exceeding
H^{2}
that does not require fine tuning of pre-inflationary initial conditions. At late times, the first term on the right side dominates. This time-dependent term arises solely from long-wavelength modes, that is, modes with physical wavelengths much greater than the Hubble scale.

A similar result is obtained for small gauge-invariant metric tensor perturbations— gravitational waves (GW)—of the de Sitter background
Starobinsky:1979ty
:

\langle h_{ij}h^{ij}\rangle_{\rm ren}=\frac{16GH^{3}t}{\pi}+{\rm Constant}\,,

where

is Newton’s gravitational constant and
i,j=1,2,3
(in
Starobinsky:1979ty

are gauge invariant). In the context of the inflationary scenario where the duration of de Sitter (inflationary) stage is finite, i.e. our past light cone is finite, this formula just corresponds to the standard expression for the primordial power spectrum of tensor perturbations generated during inflation, summed over polarizations:
P_{g}=\frac{16GH^{2}}{\pi}
, where slowly varying
H
is estimated at the moment of the first Hubble radius crossing,
k/a(t)=H(t)
, for each mode with a comoving momentum k. In
Starobinsky:1979ty
, these results were explicitly presented in another equivalent form—as the energy density of primordial GW after the second Hubble radius crossing at the subsequent radiation dominated stage.

In the massive case there is no infrared divergence, but if
0<m^{2}\ll H^{2}
, the leading contribution to
\langle\phi^{2}\rangle_{\rm ren}
, which is given by

;
Starobinsky:1982ee
;

\langle\phi^{2}(\vec{x},t)\rangle_{\rm ren}={3H^{4}\over 8\pi^{2}m^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(m^{2}/H^{2}\right)^{0}\right),

still derives entirely from the long-wavelength modes.

When there is a self-interaction, each successive term in the weak coupling perturbative expansion contains higher and higher powers of
Ht
or
H^{2}/m^{2}

overwhelms the smallness of the coupling constant

;
Prokopec:2007ak
;

The emergence of the Fokker-Planck equation from the full quantum evolution of the theory was presented in more recent works

This was done in the one-loop (Hartree-Fock, or Gaussian) approximation in
Finelli:2008zg

Finelli:2010sh

A number of two-loop calculations for scalar fields with quartic self-interaction in de Sitter spacetime were made using the stochastic approach and different quantum field theoretical methods

In our previous paper
we

\langle\phi^{2}(\vec{x},t)\rangle
and
\langle\phi^{4}(\vec{x},t)\rangle
to second order in the coupling constant and developed a method for taking the late-time limit of these perturbative series. This method was inspired by the renormalization group and is based on the assumption that expectation values of products of fields at coinciding spacetime points satisfy autonomous first-order differential equations. We then compared our results with the results of the stochastic approach and found them to be in good agreement.

In this paper we turn to the study of the massive case. In Sec. 2 we review the quantization of a free massive scalar field in de Sitter space. Section 3 presents a perturbative calculation of the long-wavelength part of the two-point function. Section 3.1 contains the zeroth-order computation and a comparison with the untruncated two-point function of the free theory. In Sec. 3.2 we outline the “in-in” formalism and use it to calculate the one- and two-loop corrections in Sections 3.3 and 3.4. For coinciding spacetime points our perturbative expression agrees with those obtained in

We compare our results with the Hartree-Fock approximation in Sec. 4 and with the stochastic approach in Sec. 5. In Sec. 6 we argue that the perturbative expression for the two-point function can be reorganized into a sum of exponential functions that depend on the two given points in a de Sitter invariant way.

2
Scalar field in de Sitter spacetime

We will study a massive scalar field with a quartic self-interaction,

S=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi-{1\over 2}{m^{2}}\phi^{2}-{\lambda\over 4}\phi^{4}\right)\;,

(1)

propagating in the de Sitter background represented as an expanding spatially flat Friedmann universe (the Poincare patch)
with the metric

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-a^{2}(t)\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}\;,

(2)

where the scale factor
a(t)
is

a(t)=e^{Ht}\;.

(3)

Here
t
is a cosmic time coordinate and
H
is the Hubble constant or the inverse of the de Sitter radius. The cosmic time in an expanding de Sitter universe runs in the interval
-\infty<t<\infty
. It will also be convenient to use a conformal time coordinate
\eta
, which is related to the cosmic time
t
by the condition
dt=a(\eta)d\eta
. Expressed in terms of the conformal time, the metric is

ds^{2}=a^{2}(\eta)(d\eta^{2}-\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j})\;,

(4)

where

a(\eta)=-\frac{1}{H\eta}\;,

(5)

and
\eta
runs from
-\infty
to
0
.

We will assume that the coupling constant and the mass are small:
\lambda\ll 1
,
m^{2}\ll H^{2}
. However, as we will see from the perturbative loop expansion, it has to be assumed additionally that the mass is not too small:

\sqrt{\lambda}H^{2}\ll m^{2}\ll H^{2}
. Note that there is no need in the last condition for the stochastic approach, but we have to impose it since we want to compare the stochastic approach with the results of perturbation theory.

When
\lambda=0
, the equation of motion for the rescaled field
\chi\equiv a(\eta)\phi
is

\chi^{\prime\prime}-\nabla^{2}\chi-{1\over\eta^{2}}\left(2-{m^{2}\over H^{2}}\right)\chi=0\;,

where primes denote derivatives with respect to the conformal time and
\nabla^{2}
is the three-dimensional Laplacian.

To quantize the field
\phi(\vec{x},t)
, we expand it in terms of creation and annihilation operators

\phi(\vec{x},t)=\int{d^{3}\vec{k}\over(2\pi)^{3}}\left\{\phi_{k}(\eta)e^{i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}a_{\vec{k}}+\phi_{k}^{*}(\eta)e^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{x}}a_{\vec{k}}^{\dagger}\right\}\;,

where
a_{\vec{k}}^{\dagger}
and
a_{\vec{k}}
satisfy the usual commutation relations. The mode functions
\chi_{k}\equiv a(\eta)\phi_{k}
then obey the differential equation

\displaystyle\chi_{k}^{\prime\prime}+k^{2}\left[1-{1\over k^{2}\eta^{2}}\left(2-{m^{2}\over H^{2}}\right)\right]\chi_{k}=0\;,

where
k=|\vec{k}\rvert
.
The general solution of this equation can be expressed as a linear combination of Hankel functions:

\chi_{k}(\eta)=\sqrt{-k\eta}\left[A_{k}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}^{(1)}(-k\eta)+B_{k}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}^{(2)}(-k\eta)\right]\;,

where the index
\nu
is

\nu=\sqrt{{9\over 4}-\frac{m^{2}}{H^{2}}}\;.

(6)

The choice of the coefficients
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
defines a vacuum state
|0\rangle
annihilated by
a_{\vec{k}}
:

\displaystyle a_{\vec{k}}|0\rangle=0\;\;\text{for any $\vec{k}$}\;.

If one wants to have a vacuum that in the remote past
\eta\to-\infty
(or, equivalently, for modes with very short physical wavelengths,
-kH\eta\gg H
) behaves like the vacuum in Minkowski spacetime,

\displaystyle\chi_{k}(\eta)\to{e^{-ik\eta}\over\sqrt{2k}}\;,

one should choose

\chi_{k}(\eta)=-{\sqrt{\pi}\over 2}\sqrt{-\eta}\,{\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}^{(1)}\left(-k\eta\right)\;.

Such a choice is called the Bunch-Davies vacuum
BD
. The mode functions of the original field,
\phi_{k}=a^{-1}\chi_{k}
, are then given by

\phi_{k}(\eta)=-{\sqrt{\pi}\over 2}H(-\eta)^{3/2}{\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}^{(1)}(-k\eta)\;.

(7)

As long as
m>0
, this state is de Sitter invariant
Allen:1985ux
.

Throughout this paper we will be assuming that
m^{2}\ll H^{2}
, in which case the order (
6
) of the Hankel function becomes

\nu\approx{3\over 2}-u,\text{ with $u\equiv{m^{2}\over 3H^{2}}\ll 1$}\;.

(8)

3
Perturbative calculation of the two-point correlation function

In this section we present a perturbative calculation of the long-wavelength part of the two-point function. There are two reasons why it is meaningful to consider exclusively the long-wavelength modes.

The first reason is physical. The fluctuations relevant for the formation of the observed large-scale structure of the universe are those whose wavelength, by the end of inflation, has been stretched to a size much larger than the Hubble horizon.

The second reason is mathematical. Calculations are much simpler if instead of the modes (
7
), one uses their long-wavelength limit. At the same time, in many cases the results can reflect the behavior of the untruncated theory. In the small mass limit, the long-wavelength two-point function matches with the untruncated one for large separations and for coinciding spacetime points. For the free theory, this is explicitly shown at the end of Sec. 3.1. Perturbative corrections (which we calculate in Sections 3.3 and 3.4) consist of products of Wightman functions and retarded Green’s functions, integrated over momenta and intermediate time coordinates. Here too the leading contribution in small mass derives entirely from the long-wavelength limit of these functions.

3.1
The free theory

At the level of the free theory, the two-point function in a vacuum state
|0\rangle
defined by a set of modes
\phi_{k}(\eta)
is given by

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle_{\lambda^{0}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}{\vec{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\phi_{k}(\eta_{1})\phi_{k}^{*}(\eta_{2})e^{i\vec{k}\cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\;k^{2}{\sin{kr}\over kr}\phi_{k}(\eta_{1})\phi_{k}^{*}(\eta_{2})\,,

where on the last line the integration over the angles was performed, and
r\equiv|\vec{x}-\vec{y}\rvert
.

The long-wavelength part of (
) consists of modes with physical momenta much less than
H
:

\displaystyle{k\over a(\eta_{1})H}=-k\eta_{1}<\epsilon\;,\;\;{k\over a(\eta_{2})H}=-k\eta_{2}<\epsilon\;,

where
\epsilon
is a small constant parameter,
\epsilon\ll 1
. Expanding the mode functions (
7
) in this limit yields

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(\eta)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle{iH\over\sqrt{2}}(-\eta)^{3/2}(-k\eta)^{-\nu}={iH\over\sqrt{2k^{3}}}(-k\eta)^{u}.

(10)

The long-wavelength part of the two-point function is then given by

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~={H^{2}(\eta_{1}\eta_{2})^{u}\over 4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{-\epsilon/\eta_{\rm m}}{dk\over k}{k^{2u}\sin(kr)\over kr}\;,

(11)

where
\eta_{\rm m}
is the earliest time that accompanies the momentum
\vec{k}
:
\eta_{\rm m}\equiv{\rm min}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
, and the subscript
L
stands for “long-wavelength”.

In the case of coinciding spacetime points, Eq. (
11
) gives

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{2}(\vec{x},t)\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}={H^{2}(-\eta)^{2u}\over 4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{-\epsilon/\eta}{dk\over k^{1-2u}}={H^{2}\over 8\pi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2u}\over u}.

(12)

By expanding
\epsilon^{2u}
for small values of
u

\epsilon^{2u}=1+2u\ln\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(u^{2}\ln^{2}\epsilon),

we see that if we choose
\epsilon
in such a way that it satisfies the following conditions

\exp\left(-u^{-1}\right)\ll\epsilon\ll 1,

(13)

then
\epsilon^{2u}
in Eq. (
12
) may be replaced by 1:

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{2}(\vec{x},t)\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}={H^{2}\over 8\pi^{2}u}={3H^{4}\over 8\pi^{2}m^{2}}\;.

(14)

It is very important that
\langle\phi^{2}\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}
appears to be anomalously large—much larger than
H^{2}
. This makes
\langle\phi^{2}\rangle_{L}
a very good approximation for the total renormalized value of

\langle\phi^{2}\rangle_{\rm ren}
, both here and at higher loops, since small-scale and renormalization contributions to this quantity are of the order of
H^{2}
(possibly up to a logarithmic multiplier).

To consider a more general case, it is convenient to introduce a new integration variable
w\equiv kr
and rewrite Eq. (
11
) as

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}={H^{2}\over 4\pi^{2}}\left({\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\over r^{2}}\right)^{u}\int_{0}^{-\epsilon r/\eta_{\rm m}}{\sin w\over w^{2-2u}}{dw}\;.

(15)

In order to analyze this expression, let us look at the following quantity: given two points in de Sitter spacetime, there is a de Sitter invariant function associated with them
Allen:1985ux
;
Allen:1987tz
:

Z(X,Y)=-H^{2}\eta_{\mu\nu}X^{\mu}Y^{\nu},

(16)

where
X
and
Y
represent coordinates in five-dimensional Minkowski embedding spacetime with the metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}={\rm diag}(1,-1,-1,-1,-1)
. We describe this function in more details in Appendix A. The explicit expression for (
16
) depends on the coordinate system. In spatially flat coordinates (
4
) that we use throughout this paper it has the form:

\displaystyle Z(\vec{x}_{1},\eta_{1};\vec{y},\eta_{2})={\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}-|\vec{x}-\vec{y}\rvert^{2}\over 2\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}\;.

(17)

The important property of this quantity is that it allows us to distinguish between timelike and spacelike related points. If points are timelike separated, then
Z>1
; if points are lightlike separated, then
Z=1
, and if points are spacelike separated, then
Z<1
.

If
(\vec{x},t_{1})
and
(\vec{y},t_{2})
are related in such a way that

Z>1-{1\over 2\epsilon^{2}}\;,

(18)

then it can be deduced from

\displaystyle{\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}-|\vec{x}-\vec{y}\rvert^{2}\over 2\eta_{1}\eta_{2}}>1-{1\over 2\epsilon^{2}}\;

that

{-r/\eta_{\rm m}}<{1/\epsilon}\;,

where
\eta_{\rm m}\equiv{\rm min}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
and
r\equiv|\vec{x}-\vec{y}\rvert
. This means that the upper limit of the integral in (
15

1
, so we can use the approximation
\sin w\approx w
and obtain

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{H^{2}\over 4\pi^{2}}\left({\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\over r^{2}}\right)^{u}\int_{0}^{-\epsilon r/\eta_{\rm m}}{dw\over w^{1-2u}}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{H^{2}\over 8\pi^{2}}{\epsilon^{2u}\over u}\bigg({\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\over\eta_{\rm m}^{2}}\bigg)^{u}\;.

As explained earlier,
\epsilon^{2u}
can be replaced by

13

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{H^{2}\over 8\pi^{2}u}e^{-uH|t_{1}-t_{2}\rvert}\;.

(20)

There are two important subcases of (
18

(\vec{x},t_{1})
and
(\vec{y},t_{2})
are timelike or lightlike separated; the other is when these points have coinciding time coordinates and the physical spatial distance between them satisfies

Turning to the case where the upper limit of the integral in (
15

1

(-r/\eta_{\rm m})>1/\epsilon\;,

(21)

it can be shown by using (

therefore, this corresponds to the regime of large spacelike separation between points. Let us split the integral in (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{-\epsilon r/\eta_{\rm m}}{\sin w\over w^{2-2u}}{dw}=\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}{\sin w\over w^{2-2u}}{dw}-\int_{-\epsilon r/\eta_{\rm m}}^{\infty}{\sin w\over w^{2-2u}}{dw}.

(22)

The first integral can be calculated exactly

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\sin w\over w^{2-2u}}{dw}=-\cos(\pi u)\Gamma(2u-1)={1\over 2u}\bigg(1+\mathcal{O}(u)\bigg)\;,

while the second integral can be shown to be smaller than

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-\epsilon r/\eta_{\rm m}}^{\infty}{\sin w\over w^{2-2u}}{dw}\right\rvert<\int_{-\epsilon r/\eta_{\rm m}}^{\infty}{w^{2u-2}}{dw}<{1\over 1-2u}\;,

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{H^{2}\over 8\pi^{2}u}\left({\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\over r^{2}}\right)^{u}

\displaystyle{H^{2}\over 8\pi^{2}u}e^{-uH(t_{1}+t_{2})}(rH)^{-2u}\;.

As we can see, in this regime the equal-time two-point function,

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t)\phi(\vec{y},t)\rangle_{\lambda^{0},L}={3H^{4}\over 8\pi^{2}m^{2}}(RH)^{-{2m^{2}\over 3H^{2}}}\;,

(24)

On the other hand, it follows from Eqs. (

3.2
The “in-in” formalism

To calculate perturbative corrections to the two-point function, we will work in the interaction picture and use the “in-in” formalism
Schwinger
;
Keldysh
;
Bakshi:1962dv
;
Jordan
;
Weinberg:2005vy
. In this formalism the two-point function can be written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad=\bigl\langle 0\big|U^{\dagger}(t_{1},-\infty)\phi_{I}(\vec{x},t_{1})U(t_{1},-\infty)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad~~~\times U^{\dagger}(t_{2},-\infty)\phi_{I}(\vec{y},t_{2})U(t_{2},-\infty)\big|0\bigr\rangle\;.

(30)

Here,
|0\rangle
is the vacuum state of the free theory and
U(t,-\infty)
is the unitary time-evolution operator:

\displaystyle U(t,-\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Te^{-i\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,H_{I}(t^{\prime})}\;,

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}(t,-\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{T}e^{i\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,H_{I}(t^{\prime})}\;,

where
T
stands for time-ordering,
\overline{T}
— for anti-time-ordering. Just as in “in-out” formalism,
\phi_{I}
-s are interaction picture fields with time evolution governed by the free theory Hamiltonian and
H_{I}
is the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture. In what follows we suppress the subscript
I
of the interaction picture fields.

Unlike in “in-out” formalism, conditions on fields and states are not imposed at both very early and very late times, but only at very early times: both the vacuum state
|0\rangle
and its Hermitian conjugate are defined at
-\infty
.

For practical calculations it will be convenient to rewrite Eq. (
30
) in a slightly different form. Let us represent the time-evolution operator and its Hermitian conjugate as

\displaystyle U(t_{1},-\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}(\infty,t_{1})U(\infty,-\infty)\;,

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}(t_{2},-\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}(\infty,-\infty)U(\infty,t_{2})\;.

Using the right-hand side of these equalities to replace the product
U(t_{1},-\infty)U^{\dagger}(t_{2},-\infty)
in (
30
) gives

\displaystyle\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl\langle 0\big|U^{\dagger}(t_{1},-\infty)\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})U^{\dagger}(\infty,t_{1})

\displaystyle~~~\times U(\infty,t_{2})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})U(t_{2},-\infty)\big|0\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl\langle 0\big|\overline{T}\bigl(\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})e^{i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,H_{I}(t^{\prime})}\bigr)

\displaystyle~~~\times T\bigl(\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})e^{-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\,H_{I}(t^{\prime})}\bigr)\big|0\bigr\rangle\,.

The second equality is obtained by noticing that on the right-hand side of the first equality the first three operators are anti-time-ordered and the last three operators are time-ordered.

The right-hand side of (
) can be interpreted in the following way: we start with the initial state at
-\infty
, evolve forward to
t_{2}
where the operator
\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})
is inserted, then continue to evolve forward to
\infty
; afterwards, we evolve backward to
t_{1}
where the operator
\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})
occurs, then continue to evolve back to
-\infty
. This is why the “in-in” formalism is also called “closed-time-path” formalism. This interpretation makes it possible to write (
) in terms of a single time-ordered expression
Jordan
;
Collins:2011mz
: label the fields on the forward-flowing part of the path with a “
+
” superscript, and the fields on the backward-flowing part of the path with a “
-
” superscript; thereby, (
) can be written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\langle\phi(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi(\vec{y},t_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle 0\big|T\bigl(\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t_{1})\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t_{2})e^{-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\;[H_{I}^{+}(t^{\prime})-H_{I}^{-}(t^{\prime})]}\bigr)\big|0\bigr\rangle\,,

(32)

where
H_{I}^{\pm}(t)\equiv H_{I}[\phi^{\pm}(t,\vec{x})]
, and the time-ordering operation is extended in the following way: two “
+
” fields are ordered as usual,

\displaystyle T(\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(t-t^{\prime})\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime})\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t)\;,

“
-
” fields always occur after “
+
” fields,

\displaystyle T(\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime})\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t)\;,

\displaystyle T(\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime})\;,

and two “
-
” fields are ordered in the opposite of the usual sense,

\displaystyle T(\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\theta(t-t^{\prime})\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime})\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\;.

We can use Wick’s theorem to express the time-ordered products in (
32
) in terms of field contractions, but unlike in “in-out” formalism, there are four types of Wick contractions (and hence, four propagators)

\displaystyle\langle 0|T(\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))|0\rangle

\displaystyle~~=\theta(t-t^{\prime})G^{>}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})+\theta(t^{\prime}-t)G^{<}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})\;,

\displaystyle\langle 0|T(\phi^{+}(\vec{x},t^{\prime})\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))|0\rangle=G^{<}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})\;,

\displaystyle\langle 0|T(\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{+}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))|0\rangle=G^{>}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})\;,

\displaystyle\langle 0|T(\phi^{-}(\vec{x},t)\phi^{-}(\vec{y},t^{\prime}))|0\rangle

\displaystyle~~=\theta(t^{\prime}-t)G^{>}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})+\theta(t-t^{\prime})G^{<}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})\;,

where
G^{>}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})
and
G^{<}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})
are the Wightman functions

\displaystyle G^{>}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi(\vec{x},t)\phi(\vec{y},t^{\prime})|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;e^{i{\vec{k}(\vec{x}-\vec{y})}}G_{k}^{>}(t,t^{\prime})\;,

\displaystyle G^{<}(\vec{x},t;\vec{y},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi(\vec{y},t^{\prime})\phi(\vec{x},t)|0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\;e^{i{\vec{k}(\vec{x}-\vec{y})}}G_{k}^{<}(t,t^{\prime})\;.

In terms of the conformal time, the momentum representations of the Wightman functions are given by

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!G_{k}^{>}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\phi_{k}(\eta)\phi_{k}^{*}(\eta^{\prime})

\displaystyle={\pi H^{2}\over 4}(-\eta)^{3/2}(-\eta^{\prime})^{3/2}{\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}^{(1)}(-k\eta){\mathcal{H}}_{\nu}^{(2)}(-k\eta^{\prime})\;,

(33)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{<}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\phi_{k}^{*}(\eta)\phi_{k}(\eta^{\prime})=G_{k}^{>}(\eta^{\prime},\eta)\;,

(34)

where we have used the mode functions (
7
) associated with the Bunch-Davies state.

Comparison with the Hartree-Fock approximation

Let us follow paper

1

\displaystyle\Box\phi=-m^{2}\phi-\lambda\phi^{3}\;,

Multiplying both sides of this equation by
\phi

\displaystyle\phi\Box\phi={1\over 2}\Box{\phi^{2}}-\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi\;,

and taking expectation values of the field operators results in the following equation

\frac{1}{2}\Box\langle\phi^{2}\rangle-\langle\partial_{\mu}\phi\,\partial^{\mu}\phi\rangle=-m^{2}\langle\phi^{2}\rangle-\lambda\langle\phi^{4}\rangle\;.

If we use the Hartree-Fock (Gaussian) approximation,
\langle\phi^{4}\rangle=3\langle\phi^{2}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{2}(\vec{x},t)\rangle_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{-\epsilon/\eta}\frac{d^{3}{\vec{k}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\phi_{k}(\eta)\phi_{k}^{*}(\eta)={H^{2}\over 4\pi^{2}}\int_{\kappa}^{-\epsilon/\eta}{dk\over k}

(64)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{H^{2}\over 4\pi^{2}}\ln\left({-\kappa\eta\over\epsilon}\right)\;,

where Eq. (
10

7
Conclusions

In Minkowski spacetime, quantum fluctuations decay quickly with scale and are significant only at short distances. The situation is different in de Sitter spacetime. Here the exponential expansion stretches short-wavelength modes to super-Hubble scales without decreasing its amplitude too much. On super-Hubble scales, the amplitude of fluctuations is scale-independent for the massless minimally coupled scalar field, and only weakly decays with scale when
0<m^{2}\ll H^{2}
.

In this article we have calculated—up to two loops—the long-wavelength two-point function for a scalar theory with a small mass and a quartic interaction. It has been shown that it is de Sitter invariant for coinciding points as well as at large spacelike and large timelike separations. We have also demonstrated that the commutator of the long-wavelength part of the field is equal to zero both at the free theory level and with the perturbative corrections.

Our results are in agreement with Starobinsky’s stochastic approach in which the coarse-grained quantum field is equivalent to a classical stochastic quantity.

Appendix A
Geodesics in de Sitter spacetime

De Sitter spacetime can be represented as the four-dimensional hyperboloid

(X^{1})^{2}+(X^{2})^{2}+(X^{3})^{2}+(X^{4})^{2}-(X^{0})^{2}=H^{-2}

(93)

embedded in five-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. In this appendix we set
H=1
.

For two points in de Sitter spacetime, the only invariant quantity associated with these points is the function

Z(X,Y)\equiv X^{1}Y^{1}+X^{2}Y^{2}+X^{3}Y^{3}+X^{4}Y^{4}-X^{0}Y^{0}\;.

(94)

Let us consider the spacetime interval between these points:

\displaystyle s^{2}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(X^{0}-Y^{0})^{2}-(X^{1}-Y^{1})^{2}-(X^{2}-Y^{2})^{2}

(95)

\displaystyle-(X^{3}-Y^{3})^{2}-(X^{4}-Y^{4})^{2}.

Using the hyperboloid equation (
93
) (with
H=1
) and the definition of
Z
from Eq. (
94
), we obtain

Z(X,Y)=1+{1\over 2}s^{2}(X,Y).

(96)

Thus, if the interval between
X
and
Y
is timelike, then
Z(X,Y)>1
; if the interval is spacelike, then
Z(X,Y)<1
, and if the interval is lightlike, then
Z(X,Y)=1
. Note the if
\overline{Y}
is the antipodal point of
Y
(that is,
\overline{Y}=-Y
), then
Z(X,\overline{Y})=-Z(X,Y)
; hence, if
Z(X,Y)<-1
, the interval between
X
and
\overline{Y}
is timelike.

Let us show that the geodesics in de Sitter spacetime are intersections of the hyperboloid with hyperplanes passing through the origin of the ambient spacetime just like the geodesics on the sphere are the great circles. To simplify notations, we will consider two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime embedded into 3-dimensional flat spacetime. In terms of the spherical coordinates

\displaystyle X^{0}=\sinh t,

\displaystyle X^{1}=\cosh t\cos\phi,

\displaystyle X^{2}=\cosh t\sin\phi\;,

(97)

which cover all of the de Sitter hyperboloid, the metric has the form

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-\cosh^{2}td\phi^{2}\;.

(98)

The Christoffel symbols for this metric are

\Gamma_{\phi t}^{\phi}=\tanh t,\ \ \Gamma_{\phi\phi}^{t}=\sinh t\cosh t\;.

(99)

The equations for the geodesics are

\frac{d^{2}\phi}{d\tau^{2}}+2\Gamma_{\phi t}^{\phi}\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}\frac{dt}{d\tau}=0\;,

(100)

\frac{d^{2}t}{d\tau^{2}}+\Gamma_{\phi\phi}^{t}\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}\right)^{2}=0\;.

(101)

Upon substituting the expressions for the Christoffel symbols (
99
) into Eqs. (
100
)-(
101
) , we obtain

\frac{d^{2}\phi}{d\tau^{2}}+2\tanh t\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}\frac{dt}{d\tau}=0\;,

(102)

\frac{d^{2}t}{d\tau^{2}}+\sinh t\cosh t\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}\right)^{2}=0\;.

(103)

Equation (
102
) gives

\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}=\frac{A}{\cosh^{2}t}\;,

(104)

where
A
is an integration constant. Substituting the expression (
104
) into Eq. (
103
) yields

\frac{d^{2}t}{d\tau^{2}}=-\frac{A^{2}\sinh t}{\cosh^{3}t}\;.

(105)

Multiplying Eq. (
105
) by
2(dt/d\tau)
produces

\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)^{2}=A^{2}\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}t}\right).

(106)

After integrating Eq. (
106
), we obtain

\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{A^{2}}{\cosh^{2}t}+B},

(107)

where
B
is an integration constant. Comparing Eqs. (
104
) and (
107
) gives the expression for the geodesics in terms of the variables
\phi
and
t
:

\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\frac{A}{\cosh t\sqrt{A^{2}+B\cosh^{2}t}}\;.

(108)

The equation for a plane passing through the origin of the ambient spacetime can be written as

\alpha X_{0}-\beta X_{1}-\gamma X_{2}\;.

(109)

To obtain the expression for the intersection of (
109
) with the de Sitter hyperboloid, one should substitute the expressions (
97
) into Eq. (
109
):

\alpha\sinh t-\beta\cosh t\cos\phi-\gamma\cosh t\sin\phi=0.

(110)

We can rewrite Eq. (
110
) as follows:

\alpha\tanh t=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}\sin(\phi+\phi_{0})\;.

(111)

Eq. (
111
) gives the differential equation

\frac{\alpha}{\cosh^{2}t}dt=\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}\cos(\phi+\phi_{0})d\phi\;.

(112)

From Eq. (
111
) we find that

\cos(\phi+\phi_{0})=\sqrt{1-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\tanh^{2}t}\;,

(113)

and using Eqs. (
112
) and (
113
), we obtain

\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\frac{\alpha}{\cosh t\sqrt{(\beta^{2}+\gamma^{2}-\alpha^{2})\cosh^{2}t+\alpha^{2}}}\;.

(114)

The equation (
114
) coincides with Eq. (
108
), and hence we have shown that intersections of planes through the origin with the de Sitter hyperboloid are geodesics.

This result should not depend on the choice of the coordinate system. However, for completeness we will reproduce it in the flat coordinates

\displaystyle X^{0}=\sinh t+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}e^{t},

\displaystyle X^{1}=xe^{t},

\displaystyle X^{2}=\cosh t-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}e^{t}\;.

(115)

In these coordinates the metric is

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-e^{2t}dx^{2}\;,

(116)

and the equations for the geodesics are

\frac{d^{2}x}{d\tau^{2}}+2\frac{dx}{d\tau}\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}=0\;,

(117)

\frac{d^{2}t}{d\tau^{2}}+e^{2t}\left(\frac{dx}{d\tau}\right)^{2}\;.

(118)

Integrating Eq. (
117
) gives

\frac{dx}{d\tau}=Ce^{-2t}\;,

(119)

where
C
is an integration constant.
Substituting the expression (
119
) into Eq. (
118
) and proceeding in a manner similar to that used for the spherical coordinates yields

\frac{dt}{d\tau}=\sqrt{C^{2}e^{-2t}+D}\;.

(120)

Then,

\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac{Ce^{-2t}}{\sqrt{C^{2}e^{-2t}+D}}\;.

(121)

This equation can be integrated explicitly:

x(t)=F-\frac{1}{C}\sqrt{D+C^{2}e^{-2t}}\;.

(122)

In terms of the coordinates (
115
), intersections of planes through the origin of the ambient spacetime with the de Sitter hyperboloid are given by

\displaystyle\delta\left(\sinh t+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}e^{t}\right)+\varepsilon xe^{t}+\eta\left(\cosh t-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}e^{t}\right)=0\;.

(123)

Solving this quadratic equation with respect to
x
, we find

x(t)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta-\eta}+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}-\delta^{2}+\eta^{2}}{(\delta-\eta)^{2}}+e^{-2t}}.

(124)

The form of Eq. (
124
) coincides with Eq. (
122
).

For any two points in Minkowski spacetime, there always exist a geodesic that connects these points. This geodesics can be timelike, lightlike or spacelike, but it always exists. In de Sitter spacetime the situation is different. Let us take two arbitrary points
X
and
Y
on the de Sitter hyperboloid. When is it possible to connect them by a geodesic?

To answer this question, consider the plane that passes through these two points and the origin of the coordinates. Note that if some point
U
belongs to the intersection of this plane with the de Sitter hyperboloid, then its antipodal point

\overline{U}=-U
also belongs to this intersection. If the angle between this plane and the plane
X^{0}=0
is less than
\pi/4
, its intersection with the hyperboloid is an ellipse. In this case, all vectors tangent to this curve are spacelike, and we can call this geodesic spacelike. Therefore,
Z(X,Y)<1
for any two points on the ellipse, and since their antipodal points are also on this ellipse,
Z(X,{\overline{Y}})<1
as well. Hence, any two points that can be connected by an elliptical geodesic should satisfy the condition that
-1<Z(X,Y)<1
.

If the angle between a plane through a pair of points and the origin and the plane
X^{0}=0
is
\geq\pi/4
, its intersection with the hyperboloid gives a hyperbola with two branches. Any two points on the same branch are timelike related (or lightlike related if the angle is equal to
\pi/4
), which means that
Z(X,Y)\geq 1
. The antipodal point of any point on a given branch is located on the other branch, and hence for any two points lying on different branches
Z(X,Y)\leq-1
. The latter points, obviously, cannot be not connected by any geodesic.
