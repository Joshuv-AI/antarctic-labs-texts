On the number of universal algebraic geometries Thanks: Supported by the Austrian Science Fund (FWF): P33878.
Erhard Aichinger, Bernardo Rossi

Abstract
. The algebraic geometry of a universal algebra 𝐀 \mathbf{A} is defined as the collection of solution sets of systems of term equations. Two algebras 𝐀 1 \mathbf{A}_{1} and 𝐀 2 \mathbf{A}_{2} are called algebraically equivalent if they have the same algebraic geometry. We prove that on a finite set A A with | A | > 3 \lvert A\rvert>3 there are countably many algebraically inequivalent Mal’cev algebras and that on a finite set A A with | A | > 2 \lvert A\rvert>2 there are continuously many algebraically inequivalent algebras.

1.
Introduction

Universal algebraic geometry, introduced in
[
18
,
10
]
, is based on the notion of
algebraic sets
. Given a universal algebra
{\mathbf{A}}=(A;(f_{i})_{i\in I})
, a subset
B
of
A^{n}
is
algebraic
if it is the solution set of a system of (possibly infinitely many) term equations in the language of
{\mathbf{A}}
. Following
[
17
]
, we denote the collection of the algebraic subsets of
A^{n}
by
\Alg_{n}{\mathbf{A}}
and we define the
universal algebraic geometry
of
{\mathbf{A}}
by
\Alg{\mathbf{A}}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Alg_{n}{\mathbf{A}}
. Clearly, for a universal algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
,
\Alg{\mathbf{A}}
is completely determined by its clone of term functions (cf.
[
8
,
19
]
). We define the universal algebraic geometry of a clone
\mathcal{C}
on a set
A
by
\Alg\mathcal{C}:=\Alg(A;\mathcal{C})
. Following
[
17
]
, we say that two clones
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
on the same set
A
are algebraically equivalent,
\mathcal{C}\algeq\mathcal{D}
, if
\Alg\mathcal{C}=\Alg\mathcal{D}
.
In
[
17
,
21
]
one finds examples of different clones that are algebraically equivalent; in fact Tóth and Waldhauser
[
21
]
proved that on the two element set there are only finitely many algebraically inequivalent clones. Pinus showed that on each finite set there are only finitely many universal algebraic geometries closed under taking unions
[
17
,
6
]
.
The aim of this note is to provide infinite families of algebraically inequivalent clones on a finite set.

Analysing the construction of
2^{\aleph_{0}}
clones on
\{0,1,2\}
in
[
13
]
, we obtain:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 3
. Then there are
2^{\aleph_{0}}
algebraically inequivalent clones on
A
.

The proof will be given in Section
5
.

A clone
\mathcal{C}
is called a
Mal’cev clone
if it contains a ternary function such that for all
a,b\in A
,

d(a,b,b)=d(b,b,a)=a,

and it is called
constantive
if every unary constant function on
A
is in
\mathcal{C}
.
Using Idziak’s construction from
[
12
]
, we prove:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 4
. Then there are exactly
\aleph_{0}
algebraically inequivalent constantive Mal’cev clones on
A
.

The proof will be given in Section
4
. It relies on the following fact:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
p
be a prime, and let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there are exactly
\aleph_{0}
algebraically inequivalent clones that contain
\POL(\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}},+)
.

The proof will be given in Section
3
.

2.
Notation

We write
\mathbb{N}
for the set of positive integers and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
[{n}]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
For a set
A
the
i
-th component of
\mathbfsl{a}\in A^{n}
is denoted by
a_{i}
and
\mathbfsl{a}(i)
.
We will use the notions
clone
,
\POL_{n}({\mathbf{A}})
and
\Clo_{n}({\mathbf{A}})
as they are commonly used in universal algebra
[
8
,
16
]
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq n
, we define the
k
-th
n
-ary projection
\pi_{k}^{(n)}:A^{n}\to A
by
\pi_{k}^{(n)}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=a_{k}
for all
\mathbfsl{a}\in A^{n}
. We set
\mathcal{J}_{A}:=\{\pi_{k}^{(n)}\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }k\leq n\}
.
For a clone
\mathcal{C}
on
A
,
\mathcal{C}^{[n]}
is the set of
n
-ary functions in
\mathcal{C}
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, for
\sigma\colon[{k}]\to[{n}]
and for
f\in\mathcal{C}^{[k]}
, we define
f_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{C}^{[n]}
by
f_{\sigma}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}):=f(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(k)})
for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in A
, and we call
f_{\sigma}
a
minor
of
f
.
If
t
is a
k
-ary term in the language of a universal algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
, we write
t_{\sigma}
for the term
t(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(k)})
. Observe that in this case
(t_{\sigma})^{\mathbf{A}}=(t^{\mathbf{A}})_{\sigma}
.

Let
A
be a set and let
\mathcal{C}
be a clone on
A
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for
X\subseteq A^{n}
, we define
\text{V}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
to be the intersection of all the elements of
\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
that contain
X
as a subset. Since the intersection of a collection of elements of
\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
is an element of
\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
, we infer that
\text{V}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)\in\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
.
The following lemma will be used to assess whether a set
X
is algebraic with respect to a clone
\mathcal{C}
, which is equivalent to
X=\text{V}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
A
be a set, let
\mathcal{C}
be a clone on
A
, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X\subseteq A^{n}
, and let
\mathbfsl{a}\in A^{n}
. Then we have

\mathbfsl{a}\in\text{V}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)\Longleftrightarrow\Bigl(\forall f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{[n]}:f|_{X}=g|_{X}\Rightarrow f(\mathbfsl{a})=g(\mathbfsl{a})\Bigr).

Proof.

“
\Leftarrow
”:
We assume that for all
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{[n]}
with
f|_{X}=g|_{X}
, we have
f(\mathbfsl{a})=g(\mathbfsl{a})
. We show that
\mathbfsl{a}\in\text{V}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
. To this end, we show that for each
B\in\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
with

X\subseteq B
, we have

\mathbfsl{a}\in B
. Since
B
is algebraic with respect to
\mathcal{C}
, there exists a system of
\mathcal{C}
-equations
\{p_{i}\approx q_{i}\mid i\in I\}
such that
B=\{\mathbfsl{x}\in A^{n}\mid\forall i\in I\colon p_{i}(\mathbfsl{x})=q_{i}(\mathbfsl{x})\}
. Since
X\subseteq B
,
we have
p_{i}|_{X}=q_{i}|_{X}

for all
i\in I
.
Therefore, the assumption yields
p_{i}(\mathbfsl{a})=q_{i}(\mathbfsl{a})
for all
i\in I
,
and thus
\mathbfsl{a}\in B
.

“
\Rightarrow
”:
We assume that there exist
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{[n]}
such that
f|_{X}=g|_{X}
and
f(\mathbfsl{a})\neq g(\mathbfsl{a})
. Then
B:=\{\mathbfsl{x}\in A^{n}\mid f(\mathbfsl{x})=g(\mathbfsl{x})\}
satisfies
B\in\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
,
X\subseteq B
, and
\mathbfsl{a}\notin B
. Thus,
\mathbfsl{a}\notin\text{V}_{\mathcal{C}}(X)
.
∎

3.
Countably many algebraically inequivalent constantive expansions of
\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}

Let
{\mathbf{A}}=(A;+,-,0,(f_{i})_{i\in I})
be an expanded group. Following
[
3
]
, a function
f\colon A^{n}\to A
is
absorbing
if
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=0
for all
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
with
0\in\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be a prime, let
n,d,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
d\geq 2
and
k\geq d+1
, let
{\mathbf{A}}_{d}
be the algebra
(\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}};+,-,0,f_{d})
, where
f_{d}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}
is defined by

f_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=np\prod_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}\text{ for }x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}},

and let
g\in\POL_{k}({\mathbf{A}}_{d})
. If
g
is absorbing, then

g
is the constant zero function.

Lemma
3.1
states that the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}_{d}
is
d
-supernilpotent (cf.
[
5
]
). We provide a proof that makes no use of the notion of supernilpotency.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{d}
be the set of all operations on
\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}
that are induced by a sum of monomials of the form
ax_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}\dots x_{i_{r}}^{n_{r}}
, where
n_{1},\dots,n_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{r}}
are pairwise distinct variables,
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}
, and the following property is satisfied:

(3.1)

(r=0)\text{ or }(r=n_{1}=1)\text{ or }(2\leq n_{1}+\dots+n_{r}\leq d\text{ and }np\text{ divides }a).

It is clear that
\{+,-,0,f_{d}\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{d}
, that the projection clone satisfies
\mathcal{J}_{\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{d}
, and that
\mathcal{C}_{d}
contains the constant functions. Furthermore, it is straightforward to verify that for
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{d}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
we have that
t_{1}+t_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
,
t_{1}-t_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
and
f_{d}(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
.
Hence
\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{d})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{d}
. We assume that
g\in\POL_{k}({\mathbf{A}}_{d})
is absorbing, and consider the representation of
g
as a sum of monomials, each one satisfying (
3.1
). Since
g
is absorbing,
g(0,x_{2},\dots,x_{k})
is the constant
0
-function. Thus, the sum of those monomials of
g
that do not involve
x_{1}
induces the constant zero function.
Therefore, there exists a representation of
g
as a sum of monomials of the above form, each of them involving the variable
x_{1}
.
A similar argument applies to all the other variables
x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
. Therefore, there exists a (possibly empty) sum of monomials that induces the same polynomial function as
g
such that each monomial involves all the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
and satisfies (
3.1
).
If
x_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}\dots x_{i_{r}}^{n_{r}}
is such a monomial, then
r\geq k
,
and therefore
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}\geq k
. Since
k\geq 3
,
(
3.1
) implies

d\geq n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}\geq k
, contradicting the assumption
k\geq d+1
.
Hence
g
is induced by the empty sum of monomials, and therefore it is the constant
0
-function.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

For each
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, let
{\mathbf{A}}_{d}
and
f_{d}
be as in the statement of Lemma
3.1
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and for
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}^{m}
, we use
\text{V}_{d}(X)
as a shorthand for
\text{V}_{\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{d})}(X)
.
Let
l,i\in\mathbb{N}
with
l>i\geq 2
. We claim that
\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{i})\not\sim_{\text{alg}}\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{l})
.
For proving this, we let

Q:=\{\mathbfsl{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}^{l}\mid\exists s\in[{l}]:\mathbfsl{a}(s)=0\}

and show that
\text{V}_{i}(Q)\neq\text{V}_{l}(Q)
; as a consequence
\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{i})\not\sim_{\text{alg}}\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{l})
.

First we prove that
(1,\dots,1)\notin\text{V}_{l}(Q)
. To this end, we consider the term equation
f_{l}(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\approx 0
. We have that
f_{l}(1,\dots,1)=np
and
f_{l}|_{Q}=0
. Hence Lemma
2.1
yields
(1,\dots,1)\not\in\text{V}_{l}(Q)
.

We now prove that
\text{V}_{i}(Q)=\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}^{l}
.
To this end, let us consider an equation of the form
t_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\approx t_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})
with
t_{1},t_{2}\in\POL_{l}({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
. This equation is equivalent to
(t_{1}-t_{2})\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\approx 0
. Hence it suffices to consider equations of the form
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\approx 0
.
If
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\approx 0
is satisfied by all elements of
Q
, then the function
t
is absorbing and has arity greater than
i
. Lemma
3.1
implies that
t
is the constant zero function. Therefore, the equation
t(x_{1},\dots,x_{l})\approx 0
is satisfied by all elements in
\mathbb{Z}_{np^{2}}^{l}
. Hence Lemma
2.1
yields
(1,\dots 1)\in\text{V}_{i}(Q)
and therefore
\text{V}_{i}(Q)\neq\text{V}_{l}(Q)
.

We conclude that
(\POL({\mathbf{A}}_{i}))_{i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}}
is an infinite family of algebraically inequivalent clones.
∎

4.
Countably many algebraically inequivalent constantive Mal’cev clones on finite sets with at least 4 elements

We report a construction from
[
12
, Section 3]
: Let
A
be a finite set such that
\lvert A\rvert\geq 4
and let
\mathcal{C}
be a constantive clone on
A
with a Mal’cev term
p
.
The construction picks an element
1\in A
and an element
0\notin A
and constructs a new clone
\mathcal{C}^{\oplus}
on the set
A^{\oplus}:=A\cup\{0\}
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and for all
f\in\mathcal{C}
, the operation
f^{\oplus}\colon(A^{\oplus})^{k}\to A^{\oplus}
is defined by

f^{\oplus}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}):=\begin{cases}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})&\text{ if }(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in A^{k},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

A binary operation
\cdot
on
A^{\oplus}
is defined by

x\cdot y:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }(x,y)\in A^{2},\\
1&\text{ if }x=y=0,\\
x&\text{ if }x\in A\text{ and }y=0,\\
y&\text{ if }x=0\text{ and }y\in A.\end{cases}

Finally,
\mathcal{C}^{\oplus}
is defined as the clone on
A^{\oplus}
generated by
\{f^{\oplus}\mid f\in\mathcal{C}\}\cup\{\cdot\}
and all unary constant operations.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 4
, let
\mathcal{C}
be a constantive clone on
A
and let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\oplus}
. Then

(4.1)

f|_{A^{k}}=0\text{ or there exists }\hat{f}\in\mathcal{C}\text{ such that }f|_{A^{k}}=\hat{f}.

Proof.

Let
K
be the set of all unary constant functions on
A^{\oplus}
.
We will show that all projections on
A^{\oplus}
satisfy (
4.1
), and that for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
n
-ary

s\in\{f^{\oplus}\mid f\in\mathcal{C}\}\cup\{\cdot\}\cup K

and for all
k
-ary
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
that satisfy (
4.1
), also
s(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
satisfies (
4.1
). From this, we conclude that every function in
\mathcal{C}^{\oplus}
satisfies (
4.1
).

Clearly, all the projections satisfy (
4.1
).

Let
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
be
k
-ary functions on
A^{\oplus}
that satisfy (
4.1
), and let
s
be an
n
-ary function on
A^{\oplus}
from

\{f^{\oplus}\mid f\in\mathcal{C}\}\cup\{\cdot\}\cup K
.
We will show that
s(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
satisfies (
4.1
).

We first consider the case that
s=f^{\oplus}
with
f\in\mathcal{C}^{[n]}
.
If there exists
i\in[{n}]
such that
t_{i}|_{A^{k}}=0
, then by the definition
of
f^{\oplus}
,
f^{\oplus}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})|_{A^{k}}=0
, and hence
s(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})

satisfies (
4.1
).
If for all
i\in[{n}]
, there exists
\hat{t_{i}}\in\mathcal{C}^{[k]}
such that
t_{i}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{t_{i}}
, then by the definition of
f^{\oplus}
we have

s(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})|_{A^{k}}=f^{\oplus}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})|_{A^{k}}=f(\hat{g_{1}},\dots,\hat{g_{n}})\in\mathcal{C}.

Therefore
s(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
satisfies (
4.1
).

Now we consider the case that
s
is the function
\cdot
(and
n=2
).
Let
t_{1},t_{2}
be
k
-ary functions on
A^{\oplus}
satisfying (
4.1
).
We show that
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}
satisfies (
4.1
).
Since
t_{1},t_{2}
satisfy (
4.1
), we have to consider the following cases.

Case 1
:
t_{1}|_{A^{k}}=t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=0
: In this case, the definition of
\cdot
yields

t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=1
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is constantive,
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}\in\mathcal{C}
, and thus
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}
satisfies (
4.1
).

Case 2
:
t_{1}|_{A^{k}}=0
and there exists
\hat{t_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}^{[k]}
such that
t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{t_{2}}
: In this case, the definition of
\cdot
yields

t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{t_{2}}
. This implies that
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}\in\mathcal{C}
, and thus
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}
satisfies (
4.1
).

Case 3
:
t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=0
and there exists
\hat{t_{1}}\in\mathcal{C}^{[k]}
such that
t_{1}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{t_{1}}
: In this case, the definition of
\cdot
yields

t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{t_{1}}
. This implies that
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}\in\mathcal{C}
, and thus
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}
satisfies (
4.1
).

Case 4
:
There exist
\hat{g_{1}},\hat{g_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}^{[k]}
such that
g_{1}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{g_{1}}
and
g_{2}|_{A^{k}}=\hat{g_{2}}
: In this case, the definition of
\cdot
yields

t_{1}\cdot t_{2}|_{A^{k}}=0
, thus
t_{1}\cdot t_{2}
satisfies (
4.1
).

Finally, let us consider the case
s\in K
(and
n=1
). Let
a\in A^{\oplus}
be the unique function value of
s
, and let
t
be a
k
-ary function on
A^{\oplus}
that satisfies (
4.1
). Clearly,
s(t)(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=a
for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in A^{\oplus}
. If
a\in A
, since
\mathcal{C}
is constantive, we have that
s(t)
satisfies (
4.1
). If
a=0
, then
s(t)(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0
for all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in A^{k}
. Thus,
s(t)
satisfies (
4.1
).
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 4
, let
\mathcal{C}
be a constantive clone on
A
, let
f\in(\mathcal{C}^{\oplus})^{[k]}
, and let
\mathbfsl{a},\mathbfsl{b}\in A^{k}
. Then
f(\mathbfsl{a})=0
if and only if
f(\mathbfsl{b})=0
.

Proof.

If
f(\mathbfsl{a})\neq 0
, then
f|_{A^{k}}\neq 0
. Thus by Lemma
4.1
, there exists
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
f|_{A^{k}}=\hat{f}
, and therefore
f(\mathbfsl{b})=\hat{f}(\mathbfsl{b})\in A
, which implies
f(\mathbfsl{b})\neq 0
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

[
12
, Section 3]

Let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 4
and let
\mathcal{C}
be a constantive Mal’cev clone on
A
. Then
\mathcal{C}^{\oplus}
is a constantive Mal’cev clone on a set of cardinality
\lvert A\rvert+1
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 4
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
be two constantive Mal’cev clones on
A
. If
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are not algebraically equivalent, then
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\oplus}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\oplus}
are not algebraically equivalent.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that there exists
B\subseteq A^{k}
that is algebraic with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{1}
but not with respect to
\mathcal{C}_{2}

Case 7

f_{1}
and
f_{2}
both depend on at least two of their arguments
: In this case Lemma
5.3
(
3
) yields
f_{1}(1,\dots,1)=0=f_{2}(1,\dots,1)
.

Thus, we have proved that one among
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
is a projection, while the other depends on at least two of its arguments.
Without loss of generality, let us assume that
f_{2}
is a projection and that
f_{1}
depends on at least two of its arguments.
Let

T:=\{\mathbfsl{a}\in Z^{i+1}\mid a_{i+1}=1\text{ and }(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})\in D_{i}\}.

Note that
T\subseteq g_{i}^{\bullet}
.
Let
j\in\{1,\dots,i,i+1\}
be such that
f_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i+1})=x_{j}
for
all
\mathbfsl{x}\in Z^{i+1}
. We claim
j=i+1
. Seeking a contradiction, let us suppose that
j\leq i
. Let
\mathbfsl{x}\in T
be such that
\mathbfsl{x}(j)=2
. Then, since
f_{1}|_{T}=f_{2}|_{T}
, we have
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{x})=2
. On the other hand, Lemma
5.3
(
3
) implies that
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{x})\neq 2
. Thus we get the desired contradiction and deduce that
f_{2}
is the
(i+1)
-th projection.
Next, we prove that
f_{1}
does not depend on its
(i+1)
-th argument. Seeking a contradiction, let us suppose that
f_{1}
depends on its
(i+1)
-th argument. Then, since
f_{1}
depends on at least two of its arguments, there exists
j\in[{i}]
such that
f_{1}
depends also on its
j
-th argument. Let
\mathbfsl{a}\in T
be such that
\mathbfsl{a}(j)=1
. Since
f_{1}|_{T}=f_{2}|_{T}
, we have that
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{a})=f_{2}(\mathbfsl{a})=a_{i+1}=1
. On the other hand, Lemma
5.3
implies that
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{a})=0
because
a_{j}=a_{i+1}=1
. From this
contradiction, we conclude that
f_{1}
does not depend on its
(i+1)
-th argument.

Let
f\colon Z^{i}\to Z
be defined by
f(x_{1},\dots x_{i}):=f_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{i},x_{1})
.
Since
f_{1}
does not depend on its
(i+1)
-th argument, for each
\mathbfsl{a}\in g_{i}^{\bullet}
, we have

(5.3)

f(a_{1},\dots,a_{i})=f_{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{i},a_{1})=f_{1}(a_{1},\dots,a_{i+1})=f_{2}(a_{1},\dots,a_{i+1})=a_{i+1}.

We show that
g_{i}=f
. To this end, let
\mathbfsl{a}\in Z^{i}
. Then
(a_{1},\dots,a_{i},g_{i}(\mathbfsl{a}))\in g_{i}^{\bullet}
, and thus (
5.3
) yields
f(\mathbfsl{a})=g_{i}(\mathbfsl{a})
.
Since
f\in\Clo_{i}({\mathbf{Z}}_{I})
, we deduce that
g_{i}\in\Clo_{i}({\mathbf{Z}}_{I})
. We now apply
[
19
, Theorem 3.1.4]
and deduce that
i\in I
.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

On the set
Z=\{0,1,2\}
there are exactly
2^{\aleph_{0}}
algebraically inequivalent clones.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{Z}
be the set of all clones on
Z
and
let
\psi\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\})\to\mathcal{L}_{Z}/{\algeq}

be defined by
I\mapsto\Clo({\mathbf{Z}}_{I})/{\algeq}
. Proposition
5.4
yields that for all
I\neq J\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\})
we have
\Clo({\mathbf{Z}}_{I})\algeq\Clo({\mathbf{Z}}_{J})
if and only if
I=J
. Therefore,
\psi
is injective. Hence there are at least
2^{\aleph_{0}}
algebraically inequivalent clones on
Z
. Since by
[
19
, Theorem 3.1.4]
there are at most
2^{\aleph_{0}}
clones on
Z
, we can conclude that there are exactly
2^{\aleph_{0}}
algebraically inequivalent clones on
Z
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We first show that for a set
A
and an element
u\notin A
,
A\cup\{u\}
has at least as many algebraically inequivalent clones as
A
. To this end, let
A
be a finite set with
\lvert A\rvert\geq 3
, let
\mathcal{L}_{A}
be the set of all clones on
A
, let
u\notin A
, let
A^{\oplus}=A\cup\{u\}
, let
\mathcal{L}_{A^{\oplus}}
be the set of all clones on
A^{\oplus}
, and let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{L}_{A}\to\mathcal{L}_{A^{\oplus}}
be defined by

\Phi(\mathcal{C})=\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\bigcup}\{f:(A^{\oplus})^{n}\to A^{\oplus}\,\mid\,f|_{A^{n}}\in\mathcal{C}\}.

We first prove that for all
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
,
\Phi(\mathcal{C})
is a clone on
A^{\oplus}
.
Clearly, all the projections belong to
\Phi(\mathcal{C})
.
Let
f\in\Phi(\mathcal{C})^{[n]}
, let
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in\Phi(\mathcal{C})^{[k]}
. Then

f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})|_{A^{k}}=f(t_{1}|_{A^{k}},\dots,t_{n}|_{A^{k}})=f|_{A^{n}}(t_{1}|_{A^{k}},\dots,t_{n}|_{A^{k}}).

The definition of
\Phi
yields
f|_{A^{n}},t_{1}|_{A^{k}},\dots,t_{n}|_{A^{k}}\in\mathcal{C}
. Thus,
f|_{A^{n}}(t_{1}|_{A^{k}},\dots,t_{n}|_{A^{k}})
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
and
f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\Phi(\mathcal{C})
.
Therefore,
\Phi(\mathcal{C})
is a clone on
A^{\oplus}
.

Next, we prove that
\Phi
satisfies

(5.4)

\forall\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{L}_{A},\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\forall B\subseteq(A^{\oplus})^{n}\colon B\in\Alg\Phi(\mathcal{C})\Leftrightarrow B\cap A^{n}\in\Alg\mathcal{C}.

For proving (
5.4
), let
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
B\subseteq(A^{\oplus})^{n}
and let us assume that
B\in\Alg_{n}\Phi(\mathcal{C})
. We prove that
B\cap A^{n}\in\Alg_{n}\mathcal{C}
.
To this end, let
\mathbfsl{b}\in A^{n}\setminus B
. By Lemma
2.1
it suffices to prove that there are
\hat{f_{1}},\hat{f_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\hat{f_{1}}|_{A^{n}\cap B}=\hat{f_{2}}|_{A^{n}\cap B}
and
\hat{f_{1}}(\mathbfsl{b})\neq\hat{f_{2}}(\mathbfsl{b})
. Since
B\in\Alg_{n}\Phi(\mathcal{C})
and
\mathbfsl{b}\notin B
, Lemma
2.1
yields that there exist
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Phi(\mathcal{C})
such that
f_{1}|_{B}=f_{2}|_{B}
and
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{b})\neq f_{2}(\mathbfsl{b})
. Let
\hat{f_{1}}=f_{1}|_{A^{n}}
and let
\hat{f_{2}}=f_{2}|_{A^{n}}
. Then clearly
\hat{f_{1}},\hat{f_{2}}\in\mathcal{C}
,
\hat{f_{1}}|_{A^{n}\cap B}=\hat{f_{2}}|_{A^{n}\cap B}
and
\hat{f_{1}}(\mathbfsl{b})\neq\hat{f_{2}}(\mathbfsl{b})
. Thus,
B\cap A^{n}\in\Alg\mathcal{C}
.

Let us now assume that
B\cap A^{n}\in\Alg\mathcal{C}
. We prove that
B\in\Alg_{n}\Phi(\mathcal{C})
. Let
\mathbfsl{a}\in(A^{\oplus})^{n}\setminus B
. By Lemma
2.1
it suffices to show that there are
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Phi(\mathcal{C})
such that
f_{1}|_{B}=f_{2}|_{B}
and
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{a})\neq f_{2}(\mathbfsl{a})
.
We split the proof into two cases.

Case 1
:
\mathbfsl{a}\in A^{n}
: In this case Lemma
2.1
implies that there are
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
g_{1}|_{B\cap A^{n}}=g_{2}|_{B\cap A^{n}}
and
g_{1}(\mathbfsl{a})\neq g_{2}(\mathbfsl{a})
. Then for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we set

f_{i}(\mathbfsl{x})=\begin{cases}g_{i}(\mathbfsl{x})&\text{ if }\mathbfsl{x}\in A^{n},\\
u&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

By the definition of
\Phi(\mathcal{C})
, both
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
belong to
\Phi(\mathcal{C})
,
f_{1}|_{B}=f_{2}|_{B}
and, since
\mathbfsl{a}\in A^{n}
,
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{a})\neq f_{2}(\mathbfsl{a})
.

Case 2
:
\mathbfsl{a}\notin A^{n}
: Let
v_{1},v_{2}\in A
with
v_{1}\neq v_{2}
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, we define

f_{i}(\mathbfsl{x})=\begin{cases}u&\text{ if }\mathbfsl{x}\in(A^{\oplus})^{n}\setminus(A^{n}\cup\{\mathbfsl{a}\}),\\
x_{1}&\text{ if }\mathbfsl{x}\in A^{n},\\
v_{i}&\text{ if }\mathbfsl{x}\in\{\mathbfsl{a}\}.\end{cases}

By construction, we have that
f_{1}|_{A^{n}},f_{2}|_{A^{n}}\in\mathcal{C}
. Hence
f_{1},f_{2}\in\Phi(\mathcal{C})
. Moreover,
f_{1}|_{B}=f_{2}|_{B}
and
f_{1}(\mathbfsl{a})\neq f_{2}(\mathbfsl{a})
.
Thus, we can conclude that

.
Equation (
5.4
) implies that for all

, if

Since by Corollary

algebraically inequivalent clones.
∎
