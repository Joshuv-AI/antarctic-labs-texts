On Measuring Model Complexity in Heteroscedastic Linear Regression
Bo Luan, Yoonkyung Lee, Yunzhang Zhu

Abstract
Heteroscedasticity is common in real world applications and is often handled by incorporating case weights into a modeling procedure. Intuitively, models fitted with different weight schemes would have a different level of complexity depending on how well the weights match the inverse of error variances. However, existing statistical theories on model complexity, also known as model degrees of freedom, were primarily established under the assumption of equal error variances. In this work, we focus on linear regression procedures and seek to extend the existing measures to a heteroscedastic setting. Our analysis of the weighted least squares method reveals some interesting properties of the extended measures. In particular, we find that they depend on both the weights used for model fitting and those for model evaluation. Moreover, modeling heteroscedastic data with optimal weights generally results in fewer degrees of freedom than with equal weights, and the size of reduction depends on the unevenness of error variance. This provides additional insights into weighted modeling procedures that are useful in risk estimation and model selection. Keywords : Heteroscedasticity, Model complexity, Model degrees of freedom, Weighted least squares



1
Introduction

Heteroscedasticity is common in real world data and needs careful consideration for modeling. Mishandling it may lead to inefficient estimates for model parameters and unreliable predictions. To deal with heteroscedasticity, a commonly used strategy is to incorporate case weights into a modeling procedure. If the error variances associated with cases were known, ideally we would specify the weights inversely proportional to the error variances according to the normal likelihood. However, such case-specific variability is usually unknown a priori, and weights need to be estimated. This generally results in various weight schemes depending on how they are estimated. For models with different weight schemes, we are interested in whether and how their complexity measures vary. A deeper understanding of this issue will offer insights into the impact of weight schemes on model performance in relation to the true error variance and further provide guidance on weight selection and ensuing model selection.

In statistics, the term “model degrees of freedom” is used as a measure of the complexity or flexibility of a model
(
Tibshirani and Hastie, 1987
;
Ye, 1998
;
Efron, 2004
)
. For least squares linear regression as an example, the model degrees of freedom is defined as the number of free parameters in the model, which coincides with the number of predictors. More generally, the degrees of freedom of a model can be defined through the expected downward bias of the training error as an estimate of the prediction error. This expected bias is also known as the
expected optimism

(
Efron, 1986
)
. Intuitively, models fitted with different weight schemes would differ in complexity, since both training and prediction errors are affected by case weights. However, to the best of our knowledge, no complexity measure has been formally established for weighted modeling procedures.

In this paper, we examine the expected optimism for in-sample prediction as well as out-of-sample prediction with case weights. The classical optimism theory
(
Efron, 1986
)
takes in-sample prediction error as the primary target. For a standard regression setting with constant error variance, the model degrees of freedom is defined through the covariances between observed responses and their fitted values. A shortcoming of this notion of model degrees of freedom is that it fails to account for the flexibility of a model at new feature values, which could be a major issue with high dimensional data. To overcome this issue,
Luan et al., 2021
proposed a different version of model degrees of freedom, called the
predictive model degrees of freedom
, based on the out-of-sample prediction error. Both versions of model degrees of freedom have been derived under the assumption of equal error variance for regression models, and they do not directly apply to modeling procedures with case weights.

We aim to extend the optimism theory and the two notions of model degrees of freedom to a more general setting with heteroscedastic data for linear regression procedures. First, we assume that the error variance is a function of covariates rather than constant. This extension has also been considered in
Anscombe, 1961
,
Bickel, 1978
,
Box and Hill, 1974
,
Carroll and Ruppert, 1981
, for example. Second, unlike the existing theories that evaluate a model with squared loss, we use a weighted squared loss as an evaluation metric for a given weight scheme. Using these
weights for evaluation
, we define analogous expected optimism and model degrees of freedom.

As an example of linear regression procedures, we examine the two extended measures of model degrees of freedom for the weighted least squares method in two scenarios. In the first scenario of subset regression, we study their monotonicity as the subset size increases. According to these two measures, the weighted least squares model has smaller degrees of freedom than the ordinary least squares model of the same size, and the size of reduction in the degrees of freedom depends on the unevenness of the error variances. In the second scenario, we fix the set of covariates as well as weights for evaluation and examine the impact of the weight scheme for fitting a weighted least squares model to data, which we call
weights for model fitting
. From sensitivity analyses of degrees of freedom around the optimal weights, we gain some useful insights into the relationships between the two model complexity measures and case weights for model fitting.

Using the two extended measures of model degrees of freedom, we propose estimators for both in-sample and out-of-sample prediction errors in a heteroscedastic setting. For the former, our estimator is a direct extension of Mallows’s
C_{p}

(
Mallows, 1973
)
, whereas for the latter, the proposed estimator is an adaptation of the risk estimator developed in
Luan et al., 2021
to heteroscedastic data. In practice, the predictive model degrees of freedom is usually unknown due to its dependency on the underlying data distribution. We thereby propose methods that can estimate the predictive model degrees of freedom efficiently and use them for estimation of the out-of-sample prediction error.

These proposed estimators are then assessed through a series of simulation studies and real data analysis. First, we evaluate our out-of-sample risk estimator in terms of prediction error estimation and model selection. We find that it has smaller variance than the leave-one-out and five-fold cross validation strategies. In addition, it tends to favor slightly simpler models without compromising the predictive performance too much. Second, we evaluate the impact of weight misspecification on model selection for both in-sample and out-of-sample predictions. In general, ignoring heteroscedasticity may lead to misidenfication of the model size and inflation in prediction risk.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we first review the two notions of model degrees of freedom defined under the assumption of equal error variance. We then propose their extended versions in a heteroscedastic setting in Section
3
. We examine properties of the extended measures for the weighted least squares method in Section
4
, and propose approaches to estimation of the predictive model degrees of freedom and out-of-sample prediction error in Section
5
. In Section
6
, we evaluate the proposed estimators through simulation studies and real data analysis. We then conclude the paper in Section
7
. For conciseness, all proofs will be deferred to Appendix.

2
Review of Model Complexity Theories

In this section, we review the existing theories for the complexity of regression models. To start with, we first introduce the notation that will be used in this work. Consider a regression setting with a continuous response
Y\in\mathbb{R}
. For a given set of covariates
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
, assume that
Y=\mu(X;\bm{\beta})+\varepsilon
, where
\mu
is the mean regression function with unknown parameter
\bm{\beta}
, and
\varepsilon
is some random error such that
\mathrm{E}(\varepsilon|X)=0
and
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon|X)=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}>0
. Here, we allow
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
to be either numerical or categorical.

Assume that a dataset of size
n
is used for model fitting. Let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}
be the vector of responses and
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be the full design matrix that includes all
d
covariates. We encode a categorical variable with
L
levels by
L-1
binary columns in
\mathbf{X}
. Thus,
m>d
if there is at least one categorical variable with more than two levels.

For brevity, we abbreviate
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta})
by
\mu_{i}
and write
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})^{\top}
. For a given modeling procedure, define
\hat{\mu}_{i}
to be the fitted value of
y_{i}
and write
\hat{\bm{\mu}}=(\hat{\mu}_{i},\ldots,\hat{\mu}_{n})^{\top}
. With the training data
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
,
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
is obtained by minimizing the (unweighted) training error

\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}.

2.1
Classical Model Degrees of Freedom

We first review the classical optimism theory presented in
Efron, 1986
;
Efron, 2004
that focuses on in-sample prediction error. Let
\tilde{y}_{i}
be an independent copy of
y_{i}
given
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. The (unweighted) in-sample prediction error is defined as

\mathrm{ErrF}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\|^{2}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}),

where the expectation is with respect to
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\tilde{y}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{y}_{n})^{\top}
.

The expected optimism, denoted by
\mathrm{OptF}_{\mathbf{X}}
, is the expected difference between the prediction error
\mathrm{ErrF}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}
and training error
\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}
. The expectation is taken with respect to
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{X}
.
Efron, 1986
showed that

\mathrm{OptF}_{\mathbf{X}}\coloneqq\mathrm{E}(\mathrm{ErrF}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}-\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{X})=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X}).

(2.1)

Following this covariance representation of the expected optimism,
Efron, 2004
then formally defined the model degrees of freedom of a fitted model
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
as

\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}.

(2.2)

In particular, for a linear modeling procedure
\hat{\bm{\mu}}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{y}
, where
\mathbf{H}=(h_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
depends only on
\mathbf{X}
, it is easy to show that
\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}h_{ii}
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}={\rm tr}(\mathbf{H})
. Here,
\mathbf{H}
is usually called the
hat matrix
.

When the input space is large, the in-sample prediction error may be limited in measuring how well a modeling procedure will generalize at new feature values that arise in the future as in many prediction-oriented applications. As a result, the classical model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
needs some adjustment when prediction beyond the training data is concerned. This motivates the use of out-of-sample prediction error for an alternative definition of model degrees of freedom, which we will discuss next.

2.2
Predictive Model Degrees of Freedom

In this section, we review the model complexity theory for out-of-sample prediction proposed by
Luan et al., 2021
. Let
(\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\varepsilon_{\ast})
be an independent copy of
(\mathbf{x}_{i},\varepsilon_{i})
and define
\mu_{\ast}=\mu(\mathbf{x}_{\ast};\bm{\beta})
and
y_{\ast}=\mu_{\ast}+\varepsilon_{\ast}
. Let
\hat{\mu}_{\ast}
be a prediction of
y_{\ast}
. The (unweighted) out-of-sample prediction error is defined as

\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\mathrm{E}[(y_{\ast}-\hat{\mu}_{\ast})^{2}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}],

where the expectation is taken with respect to
(\mathbf{x}_{\ast},y_{\ast})
. Following the framework for in-sample prediction, we define the expected optimism as
\mathrm{OptR}_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}-\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}
, where
\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}
are the expectations of
\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}
and
\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}
with respect to
\mathbf{y}
respectively.

Consider a linear procedure with hat matrix
\mathbf{H}
. For any new covariate vector
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
, the procedure predicts the response at
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
using a linear combination of
y_{i}
’s, denoted by
\hat{\mu}_{\ast}=\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
, where
\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
depends only on
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
and
\mathbf{X}
. This coefficient vector
\mathbf{h}_{\ast}
is called the
hat vector
at
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
reminiscent of the hat matrix. Then, we can write the bias-variance decomposition for
\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\mathrm{E}[(\mu_{\ast}-\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}|\mathbf{X}]+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{E}(\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|^{2}|\mathbf{X}),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{ErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{\mu}-\mathbf{H}\bm{\mu}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}-2\mathbf{H}),

(2.4)

where the three terms on the right hand side are the irreducible error, (squared) bias and variance respectively. The expected optimism
\mathrm{OptR}_{\mathbf{X}}
can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{OptR}_{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}+\frac{2}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left[\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|^{2}|\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top})\right)\right],

(2.5)

where
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathrm{E}[(\mu_{\ast}-\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}|\mathbf{X}]-\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{\mu}-\mathbf{H}\bm{\mu}\|^{2}
is the
excess bias
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
is the classical model degrees of freedom defined in (
2.2
). The second term in the right hand side of (
2.5
) is the
excess variance
, and it doesn’t depend on the underlying true model
\mu
. Further, the quantity in the square brackets depends only on the modeling procedure through the hat matrix
\mathbf{H}
and hat vector
\mathbf{h}_{\ast}
. Since model complexity is an intrinsic property of a modeling procedure,
Luan et al., 2021
defined this quantity as a model complexity measure under the out-of-sample prediction setting. Denoting it by
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
:

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}=\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|^{2}|\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H})\right),

(2.6)

they call
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
the
predictive model degrees of freedom
to differentiate it from the classical model degrees of freedom and emphasize its use in an out-of-sample prediction setting.

3
Model Complexity in a Heteroscedastic Setting

In this section, we extend the existing theories on model complexity to a heteroscedastic setting. Our extensions include two aspects. First, we allow heteroscedasticity in error
\varepsilon
. In particular, we assume that
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon|X)=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\tau(X)
, where
\tau
is a function satisfying
\tau(\cdot)>0
and
\mathrm{E}[\tau(X)]=1
. We write
\tau_{i}=\tau(\mathbf{x}_{i})
for simplicity. Second, for model evaluation, we consider a general weighted squared error loss. Let
w\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a deterministic function and write
w_{i}=w(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. We define the weighted training error given
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
and
w
as

\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(y_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2},

where
\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{diag}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
and
\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{a}
for
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, if we assume
y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\tau_{i})
given
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and take
w_{i}=1/\tau_{i}
, then minimizing the weighted training error is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood of
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{X}
. For in-sample and out-of-sample prediction errors, we incorporate case weights in a similar way and define

\mathrm{wErrF}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{E}(\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})\text{ and }\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}(y_{\ast}-\hat{\mu}_{\ast})^{2}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}],

where
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
is an independent copy of
\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{X}
and
w_{\ast}=w(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})
.

3.1
Classical Model Degrees of Freedom

We first look at the classical model degrees of freedom. With the weighted training error and in-sample prediction error, it is easy to adjust the expected optimism (
2.1
) with weights as

\mathrm{wOptF}_{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X}).

Based on the expected optimism, we define the extended model degrees of freedom as

\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{w_{i}}{\bar{w}}\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}},

(3.1)

where
\bar{w}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}/n
. The use of
w_{i}/\bar{w}
as a normalized weight for each covariance summand guarantees that
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
is invariant to scaling of the weight. In addition, the normalizing constant
\bar{w}
ensures that the normalized weights sum up to the sample size
n
. In particular, when
w_{1}=\cdots=w_{n}
, the extended definition just reduces to the model degrees of freedom (
2.2
) for equal error variances.

Using the extended model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
, we can immediately get an unbiased estimator of
\mathrm{wErrF}_{\mathbf{X}}
, which is given by

\widehat{\mathrm{wErrF}}=\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}+\frac{2}{n}\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}.

For a linear procedure with hat matrix
\mathbf{H}
, we have
\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})=h_{ii}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\tau_{i}
. Then (
3.1
) is simplified to

\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{w_{i}}{\bar{w}}h_{ii}\tau_{i}=\frac{1}{\bar{w}}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}),

(3.2)

where
\mathbf{T}=\mathrm{diag}(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})
. When
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{I}_{n}
, this further reduces to
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}={\rm tr}(\mathbf{H})
in the homoscedastic setting. The following remarks demonstrate some interesting properties of this extension (
3.2
).

Remark 3.1

(Optimal weights)
.

Assume the optimal weights are used for model evaluation, i.e.,
w(\cdot)\propto 1/\tau(\cdot)
. Then we have

\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\zeta_{\tau}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}),

(3.3)

where
\zeta_{\tau}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
is the harmonic mean of
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}
. Since
\tau_{i}
’s are all positive, their harmonic mean is always no greater than the arithmetic mean
\bar{\tau}
, and the equality holds if and only if all
\tau_{i}
’s are equal. This implies that

\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\zeta_{\tau}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H})\leq\bar{\tau}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H})\approx{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}),

where the approximation in the last step is due to
\bar{\tau}\approx\mathrm{E}[\tau(\mathbf{x})]=1
. Note that
{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H})
is the classical model degrees of freedom of a linear procedure under the homoscedastic setting. This suggests that the unevenness in error variances leads to a deflation in model complexity.

Remark 3.2

(Equal error variances)
.

When error variances are all equal, i.e.,
\tau_{1}=\cdots=\tau_{n}=1
, (
3.2
) becomes
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(w_{i}/\bar{w})h_{ii}
, which is a weighted average of the leverages
h_{ii}
. Assume
h_{ii}>0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then, for any
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}>0
, we have
n\min_{i}h_{ii}\leq\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}\leq n\max_{i}h_{ii}
.
In other words,
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
is maximized (minimized) when all weights are assigned to the case with the largest (smallest) leverage. Since leverages are directly related to the impact of observations on the model, putting more weights on more (less) influential observations will make the model more (less) flexible. The extended model degrees of freedom properly reflects this relationship.

Remark 3.3

(Duplicate data)
.

Assume
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}
are
k
distinct covariate vectors in the training data with
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
replicates respectively (
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}
). For each
i=1,\ldots,k
, let
y_{i,1},\ldots y_{i,n_{i}}
be i.i.d. observations given
\mathbf{x}_{i}
with constant variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
, and define
\bar{y}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}y_{i,j}/n_{i}
. Note that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(y_{i,j}-\hat{\mu}_{i})^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}(y_{i,j}-\bar{y}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}(\bar{y}_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{i})^{2},

and the first term on the right hand side of the above equation doesn’t depend on the model. Thus, a weighted least squares model based on the collapsed data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},\bar{y}_{i})|i=1,\ldots,k\}
with weights
w_{i}=n_{i}
is identical to the ordinary least squares model based on the full data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i,j})|i=1,\ldots,k;\,j=1,\ldots,n_{i}\}
. This dual representation of the model brings a question about its degrees of freedom. If the collapsed data were to be viewed as a collection of genuine
k
independent observations with unequal weights, Remark
3.1
appears to suggest that the weighted model with a linear procedure has fewer degrees of freedom than the unweighted model fit to the same
k
observations. However, this difference in model complexity comes from the scale of weights tied to the sample size we assume. If the weights are scaled to match the original sample size
n
rather than the distinct number of observations
k
, the dual representation of the model fit to duplicate data leads to the same model degrees of freedom.

3.2
Predictive Model Degrees of Freedom

We now extend the notion of model complexity for the heteroscedastic setting to the out-of-sample prediction framework for all linear procedures. Let
w_{\ast}=w(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})
and
\tau_{\ast}=\tau(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})
, and define the weighted out-of-sample prediction error as

\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}=\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}(y_{\ast}-\hat{\mu}_{\ast})^{2}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}],

where the expectation is with respect to
(\mathbf{x}_{\ast},y_{\ast})
. Consider a linear procedure with hat matrix
\mathbf{H}
and hat vector
\mathbf{h}_{\ast}
at
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
. Similar to (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) in the homoscedastic setting, we have the following bias-variance decompositions for
\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})+\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}(\mu_{\ast}-\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}|\mathbf{X}]+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X}),

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}=\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}+\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{\mu}-\mathbf{H}\bm{\mu}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}-2\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}),

(3.5)

where
\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}
is the expectation of
\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}
with respect to
\mathbf{y}
. See Appendix
A.1
for detailed derivation of (
3.4
) and (
3.5
). As a consequence, the expected optimism
\mathrm{wOptR}_{\mathbf{X}}\coloneqq\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}-\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}
is of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{wOptR}_{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle+\left(\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}(\mu_{\ast}-\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}|\mathbf{X}]-\frac{1}{n}\|\bm{\mu}-\mathbf{H}\bm{\mu}\|_{\mathbf{W}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n}\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left[\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2\bar{w}}\left(\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{H}^{\top})\right)\right],

where
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}={\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W})/\bar{w}
is the model degrees of freedom for a linear procedure defined in (
3.2
). We call the first term on the right the
excess irreducible error
, which equals
0
in the homoscedastic setting due to
\tau
and
w
both being constant. In general, this term should be negligible as long as the sample size is large. The second and third terms are respectively the excess bias and excess variance adjusted by the weight function
w
. Following
Luan et al., 2021
, we still use
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}
to denote the excess bias. We then propose the extended predictive model degrees of freedom based on the excess variance. Formally, we define it as

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}=\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2\bar{w}}\left(\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{H}^{\top})\right).

(3.7)

In particular, when
w(\cdot)
is constant, we have

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}=\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{H}^{\top})\right).

If further
\tau_{i}
’s are all equal, this reduces to (
2.6
).

The expression in (
3.7
) suggests that the predictive model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
adjusts the classical model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
with an extra term that accounts for out-of-sample prediction. In fact, this adjustment can be expressed and interpreted in two different ways, which we summarize in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

For a linear procedure, the adjustment
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}-\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
can be expressed as

(i)

Covariance penalty representation

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}-\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\frac{n}{2\bar{w}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\left[\mathrm{E}\left(w_{\ast}\frac{\mathrm{Cov}^{2}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})}{(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\tau_{i})^{2}}\Bigg|\mathbf{X}\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\frac{\mathrm{Cov}^{2}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{j}|\mathbf{X})}{(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\tau_{i})^{2}}\right];

(ii)

Generalized degrees of freedom (GDF) representation

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}-\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\frac{n}{2\bar{w}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\left[\mathrm{E}\left(w_{\ast}\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{E}(\hat{\mu}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right)^{2}\Bigg|\mathbf{X}\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{E}(\hat{\mu}_{j}|\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mu_{i}}\right)^{2}\right].

The covariance penalty representation unifies the two measures of model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
by quantifying the level of dependency of the predicted values on the observed values. More specifically, the adjustment
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}-\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
compares the overall level of such dependency for out-of-sample predictions against that for in-sample ones. The GDF representation gets its name from the generalized degrees of freedom (GDF) proposed by
Ye, 1998
. In his work, he defined
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\mathrm{E}(\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})}{\partial\mu_{i}}
as the GDF for a modeling procedure
\hat{\bm{\mu}}
. The GDF representation of
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}-\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
expresses this adjustment in terms of the rate of change of the predicted values on the test data against that on the training data with respect to the true mean components
\mu_{i}
’s.

4
Weighted Least Squares

We study the two proposed measures of model degrees of freedom for the weighted least squares method in this section. Assume
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
has full column rank. Let
q\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a known weight function, and define
q_{i}=q(\mathbf{x}_{i})
and
\mathbf{Q}=\mathrm{diag}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
. For the weighted least squares problem of
\min_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\|_{\mathbf{Q}}^{2}
, the solution has the explicit form of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{y}
. The corresponding hat matrix and hat vector are then given by
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\ast}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
.

Consider evaluating the model using a weighted squared error loss with weight function
w
. Let
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\mathbf{x}_{\ast}\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{\top})/\bar{w}
. Then it is easy to show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{w}}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}],

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}\left[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\left(\bm{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{n\bar{w}}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}\right)\right].

(4.2)

4.1
Subset Regression

We first study
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
of the weighted least squares model in the subset regression setting. For simplicity, we assume the weight scheme used for modeling fitting is the same as that for model evaluation, i.e.,
q=w
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{1}\subset\cdots\subset\mathcal{S}_{d}\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,d\}
be a sequence of nested subsets of variables such that
|\mathcal{S}_{p}|=p
for each
p=1,\ldots,d
. Let
\mathbf{X}_{p}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{p}
be the submatrices of
\mathbf{X}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
corresponding to
\mathcal{S}_{p}
respectively. Consider regressing
\mathbf{y}
on
\mathbf{X}_{p}
using the weighted least squares method with weight function
w
. Then (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) are simplified to

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{w}}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p}],

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)+\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p}(\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}].

For each
j=1,\ldots,d
, let
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}
be the column(s) in
\mathbf{X}
associated with variable
j
. If the
j
th variable is continuous or binary,
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If the variable is categorical with
k\geq 3
levels,
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(k-1)}
. For the sake of convenience and conciseness, we assume
\mathbf{x}_{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the rest of this section, though all results can be tailored to categorical variables with more than two levels accordingly.

We examine the size of change in the model degrees of freedom as a covariate is added. Normalizing observations with weights, let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}=\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}_{(p+1)}/\sqrt{\bar{w}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}=\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{X}_{p}/\sqrt{\bar{w}}
. Similarly, let
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{y}/\sqrt{\bar{w}}
and note that
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{X})=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Define
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{\top}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{p+1}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\tilde{\mathbf{H}})\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}
. Here,
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}
is the residual vector when the normalized new variable vector
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}
is regressed on the normalized design matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
. The following theorem describes the increment in
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
in terms of
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}
as
p
increases by 1.

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume
p<d<n
. Then for the weighted least squares model with weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
and design matrix
\mathbf{X}_{p}
,
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
increases in
p
, and

\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)\coloneqq\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p+1)-\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)=\frac{\|\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}\|_{\mathbf{U}}^{2}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}\|^{2}},

where
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}/\bar{w}
.

Theorem
4.1
indicates that the increment in
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
generally depends on the amount of collinearity between the normalized new variable and existing variables. A special case occurs when
w\propto 1/\tau
. In this scenario, we have
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}=\zeta_{\tau}p
and
\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)=\zeta_{\tau}
(the harmonic mean of
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}
). Hence,
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
is a linear function of
p
. Its increment
\zeta_{\tau}
is constant for all
p
and depends only on the unevenness of the true error variances.

For the predictive model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
, assume that
\bm{\Sigma}
is positive definite. For
p=1,\ldots,d-1
, write

\bm{\Sigma}_{p+1}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}&\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}\\
\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}^{\top}&\sigma_{p+1}^{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad p=1,\ldots,d-1.

Let
a_{p,p+1}=\sigma_{p+1}^{2}-\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}
. Note that
a_{p,p+1}>0
when
\bm{\Sigma}
is positive definite. We derive the increment
\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)=\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p+1)-\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)
in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume
p<d<n
. Then for the weighted least squares model with weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
and design matrix
\mathbf{X}_{p}
,

\displaystyle\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{2}\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(p)\frac{(\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}\|_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}+a_{p,p+1})}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}\|^{2}}

(4.3)

\displaystyle-n\frac{\langle\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}\rangle_{\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}\|^{2}}

where
\mathbf{M}=\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{\top}
, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{A}}
for a matrix
\mathbf{A}
denotes the inner product with respect to
\mathbf{A}
.

The component
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}\bm{\Sigma}_{p}^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{p,p+1}
can be seen as the residual vector
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}_{(p+1)}
at the population level, in which we simply replace
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{p}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{(p+1)}
with their population versions
n\bar{w}\Sigma_{p}
and
n\bar{w}\phi_{p,p+1}
respectively. In general,
\Delta\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)
is not necessarily positive due to the third term in the right hand side of (
4.3
). However, when
w\propto 1/\tau
, this term vanishes due to the fact that
(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\tilde{\mathbf{H}})\mathbf{U}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{O}_{n\times n}
. In this case, we have

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)=\frac{\zeta_{\tau}}{2}(p+n\bar{w}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{p})]),

(4.4)

which is strictly increasing in
p
. The following remark gives an approximation of (
4.4
) under the assumption that
\mathbf{x}_{i}
’s are multivariate normal.

Remark 4.1
.

Assume
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. We slightly modify the setting by assuming
\tau
is a function of a random variable
\mathbf{u}
that is independent of
\mathbf{x}
. Further, assume that
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
is multivariate normal with
\mathrm{E}(\mathbf{x}_{\ast})=\mathbf{0}
. Then it can be shown that

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)\approx\frac{p}{2}\zeta_{\tau}\left(1+\frac{n_{\tau}}{n_{\tau}-p-1}\right),

(4.5)

where
n_{\tau}
is the nearest integer to
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}1/\tau_{i}\right)^{2}/\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1/\tau_{i})^{2}
. For the derivation of (
4.5
), see Appendix
A.5
.

Figure
1
shows the approximation (
4.5
) under the assumption that
\tau(u_{i})=(1+|u_{i}|)^{\lambda}
(
\lambda\geq 0
) and
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i})=\sqrt{10/d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{ij}
, where
x_{ij}
’s and
u_{i}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We set
n=100
and
d=50
and consider
\lambda=0.5,1,2\text{ and }3
. The distribution of
\tau(u)
becomes more skewed to the right as
\lambda
increases. Consequently, the rate of increase in both
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
drops as the skewness level
\lambda
increases. The approximation (
4.5
) works well when the distribution of
\tau
is less skewed, but tends to overestimate the true predictive model degrees of freedom with more uneven
\tau_{i}
’s at a large
p
due to the quickly shrinking denominator
n_{\tau}-p-1
.

In fact, the size of
\zeta_{\tau}
and
n_{\tau}
are both directly related to the unevenness of
\tau_{i}
’s. When
w=1/\tau=1
, we have
\zeta_{\tau}=1
and
n_{\tau}=n
. In this case, (
4.5
) becomes

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)\approx\frac{p}{2}\left(1+\frac{n}{n-p-1}\right),

(4.6)

which is the approximation of
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
for subset regression given by
Luan et al., 2021
under the homoscedastic setting. On the other hand, when
\tau_{i}
’s are not all equal,
\zeta_{\tau}<1
and
n_{\tau}\leq n
. In particular, Figure

suggests that the more uneven
\tau_{i}
’s are, the smaller
\zeta_{\tau}
and
n_{\tau}
will be. Since
n_{\tau}
in (
4.5
) plays a similar role as
n
in (
4.6
), we call it the
effective sample size
. When
\tau_{i}
’s are highly uneven, observations with large variance will receive little weight. The resulting effect is as if the sample size was reduced.

4.2
Effect of Weight Schemes

Since the optimal weight scheme is usually unknown a priori, misspecification of weights could happen in practice. A natural question is then how the model complexity is affected by the choice of weights. In this section, we try to answer this question by fixing the subset of variables for regression and varying the weight scheme
q
for the weighted least squares model. Since these models are trained under different weight schemes, it is important to assess their complexity using a common criterion. Here, we set
w=1/\tau
and define model degrees of freedoms for all weighted least squares models using this weight.

Assume that
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the design matrix. To avoid confusion, we use
\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}(q)
,
\mathrm{wErrF}_{\mathbf{X}}(q)
and
\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}(q)
in this section to denote the training and prediction errors of the weighted least squares model
\hat{\bm{\mu}}(q)
with weight scheme
q
. Similarly, we use
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(q)
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(q)
here to denote the corresponding measures of model degrees of freedom. By replacing
\mathbf{W}
with
\mathbf{T}^{-1}
in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), we get
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(q)=\zeta_{\tau}p
and

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(q)=\zeta_{\tau}p+\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}],

(4.7)

where
\mathbf{R}=\bm{\Sigma}-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{X}/n
.

For the classical model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
, we find that
\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}
is constant with respect to the weight scheme
q
. In other words, the average covariance between the fitted values
\hat{\mu}_{i}
and the observations
y_{i}
is the same for all weighted least squares models. Since
\mathrm{wErrF}_{\mathbf{X}}(q)=\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}(q)+(2/n)\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}(q)
, the best weighted least squares model should be the one that minimizes the expected training error
\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}(q)
. Apparently, this minimizer is the model with the optimal weight scheme
q=1/\tau
, since it is specifically determined to minimize the training error
\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}(q)=\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\bm{\mu}}(q)\|_{\mathbf{T}^{-1}}^{2}/n
for each
\mathbf{y}
.

For the predictive model degrees of freedom (
4.7
), the following theorem gives its gradient with respect to
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})^{\top}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

The gradient of the predictive model degrees of freedom
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
with respect to the weight vector
\mathbf{q}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{Q})
is given by

\frac{\partial\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}=n\,\mathrm{diag}\left((\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right),

where
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}
.

For a general weight scheme
q
, it is usually not easy to see directly from
\partial\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}/\partial\mathbf{q}
how
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
behaves when
q
is perturbed. However, when
q=1/\tau
, we have
(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{O}_{n\times p}
, which further implies that
(\partial\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}/\partial\mathbf{q})|_{q=1/\tau}=\mathbf{0}_{n}
. This indicates that
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
, as a function of the weight scheme
q
, is relatively flat around the optimal weight
1/\tau
. In practice, we usually need to estimate
\tau
with an estimator
\hat{\tau}
and use
\hat{q}=1/\hat{\tau}
as a weight function for model fitting. A good estimate of
\tau
guarantees that
\hat{q}
is close to the optimal weight
1/\tau
. Therefore, it is desirable for
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
to be flat around the optimal weight in the sense that the discrepancy between the optimal weight scheme
1/\tau
and a reasonably good estimate of it would make little change in
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
.

A more quantitative study of
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
is given as follows. Assume that
q
is of the form

q(\alpha;\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\alpha+(1-\alpha)\tau(\mathbf{x})},\text{ for }0\leq\alpha\leq 1.

(4.8)

Then
\mathrm{E}[1/{q(\alpha;\mathbf{x})}]=1=\mathrm{E}[\tau(\mathbf{x})]
for any
\alpha
. Note that
q=1/\tau
when
\alpha=0
and
q=1
when
\alpha=1
. Thus, (
4.8
) constructs a path from the optimal to the equal weight scheme. To examine
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
as a function of
\alpha
more closely, we consider a simulation study below. We set
n=100
and
d=20
, and assume that
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
. Also assume that
\tau(\mathbf{x})\propto(1+|\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}|)^{2}
with
\beta_{j}\propto(1-j/d)^{5}
and
\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}=10
. We generate 100 replicates of
\mathbf{X}
. Figure
3
demonstrates the predictive model degrees of freedom of models with the first
p
variables for
p=1,\,5,\,10
and
20
. We can see that
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
can be either increasing or decreasing in
\alpha
. However, as the number of variables increases,
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
is more likely to be an increasing function of
\alpha
. Note that, as more relevant variables are added, the weighted prediction errors
(y_{\ast}-\hat{\mu}_{\ast})/\sqrt{\tau_{\ast}}
of the model with the optimal weight scheme are generally homoscedastic, while those of the other models are not. Consequently, the variance of the predicted values produced by the model with optimal weights is the lowest on average. Since the predictive model degrees of freedom is defined through the excess variance, the optimal weight scheme generally leads to the simplest model.

:

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
of the weighted least squares method as a function of the coefficient
\alpha
when
\mathbf{Q}=(\alpha\mathbf{I}_{n}+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{T})^{-1}
(
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
). We assume that
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
,

,

and

with

and

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
based on 100 different realizations of
\mathbf{X}
, whereas the red curve is the average over these replicates.

On the other hand, though the weight scheme
q
for model fitting affects the predictive model degrees of freedom, our results in Figure
3
suggest that its impact may not be big enough to produce appreciable range of difference. When the model size is not too large, which is usually the case in many real applications, the difference in
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
due to the weight scheme
q
for model fitting is fairly small or even trivial, as we can see from the figure. In addition, the expected optimism (
3.6
) implies that

\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}\right)+\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}+\frac{2}{n}\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}.

(4.9)

Since
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
contributes to the prediction error
\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}
through
(2/n)\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
, the impact of the difference in
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
for different weighted least squares models will be reduced when the sample size
n
is large. By contrast, the choice of
q
may have a much larger impact on the training error
\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X}}
or excess bias
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}
. In Section
5
, we will take these terms into account and provide a risk estimator for model evaluation and comparison.

5
Prediction Error Estimation

In this section, we aim to construct an estimator for the out-of-sample prediction error based on (
4.9
). For the excess irreducible error, the law of large numbers guarantees that
\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}/n
is generally negligible when
n
is large. The excess bias
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}
depends on the unknown true mean function
\mu(\cdot;\bm{\beta})
, and thus needs to be estimated. The predictive model degrees of freedom depends on the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
through
\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})
. In practice, this expectation also needs to be estimated.

For simplicity, we assume that
\tau
is either known a priori or can be estimated by
\hat{\tau}
using some independent data (e.g., historical data) throughout this section. Further, we assume that
q=w\propto 1/\tau
(or
1/\hat{\tau}
).

5.1
Estimation of
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}

We first consider estimating the excess bias
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}
. Here, we adjust with weights the method proposed in
Luan et al., 2021
that uses the leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) trick. Let
\mathbf{X}^{-i}
,
\mathbf{y}^{-i}
and
\bm{\mu}^{-i}
be the corresponding terms with the
i
th case deleted. Write
\mathbf{H}=(\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n})^{\top}
and define
\mathbf{h}^{-i}_{i}
to be the hat vector at
\mathbf{x}_{i}
based on
\mathbf{X}^{-i}
. Conceptually, when
n
is large, we have

\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}(\mu_{\ast}-\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}|\mathbf{X}]\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu_{i}-(\mathbf{h}^{-i}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{-i})^{2}.

For linear procedures, the right hand side of the above equation can be evaluated efficiently due to the LOOCV identity
y_{i}-(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{-i})^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{-i}=(y_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{y})/(1-h_{ii})

(
Craven and Wahba, 1978
)
. Since
\mathbf{h}_{i}
doesn’t depend on either
\bm{\mu}
or
\mathbf{y}
, the identity still holds if we replace
y_{i}
with
\mu_{i}
and
\mathbf{y}^{-i}
with
\bm{\mu}^{-i}
respectively. Then, we have

\displaystyle\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}[(\mu_{i}-(\mathbf{h}^{-i}_{i})^{\top}\bm{\mu}^{-i})^{2}-(\mu_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\left[\frac{(\mu_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}}{(1-h_{ii})^{2}}-(\mu_{i}-\mathbf{h}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\mu})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}-\frac{2}{n}\bm{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{\varepsilon},

where
\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})
and
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}\left(w_{i}/(1-h_{ii})^{2}-w_{i}\right)
. Since
\mathrm{E}(\bm{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{\varepsilon}|\mathbf{X})=0
and
\mathrm{E}(\bm{\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bm{\varepsilon}|\mathbf{X})=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T})
, we can estimate
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}
by

\hat{\delta}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}).

Assume
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
is known. Then (
4.9
) implies that an estimator of
\mathrm{wErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}
is given by

\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}=\mathrm{wErrT}_{\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}}+\hat{\delta}+\frac{2}{n}\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}=\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}_{\rm loocv}+\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{X}},

(5.1)

where
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}_{\rm loocv}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(y_{i}-(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{-i})^{\top}\mathbf{y}^{-i})^{2}/n
is the LOOCV error and
\xi_{\mathbf{X}}=2\bar{w}\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}-{\rm tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T})
. Thus,
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
is an adjusted version of the LOOCV error. In fact, this adjustment has a close connection with the variance of prediction errors in the full model and leave-one-out models, which we summarize in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

The adjustment term
\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{X}}
in (
5.1
) can be expressed as

\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})|\mathbf{X}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}^{-i}_{i}|\mathbf{X})-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}\right),

where
\hat{\varepsilon}_{\ast}
is the prediction error of the full model at
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
, and
\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}^{-i}
is that of the leave-the-
i
th-out model at
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

Note that the third term on the right hand side of the expression above is the excess irreducible error, which is approximately 0 when
n
is large. Therefore,
\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}
can be approximated by

\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{X}}\approx\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})|\mathbf{X}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}^{-i}_{i}|\mathbf{X}).

This indicates that the adjustment term
\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{X}}
is a measure of the difference between the full model and leave-one-out models in the variance of prediction errors weighted by the case weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
. In addition, as shown in
Luan et al., 2021
, the adjustment term is generally negligible when
n
is large and
p/n
is relatively small. But when
p
is close to
n
,
{\rm tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T})
could be very large and variable due to the leverages
h_{ii}
approaching 1. This may make the adjustment negative and large in magnitude, and further cause
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
to be negative. Since the term
{\rm tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T})
in
\xi_{\mathbf{X}}
comes from the excess bias estimator
\hat{\delta}
, to circumvent this issue, they suggested to use
\hat{\delta}_{+}=\max(\hat{\delta},0)
in place of
\hat{\delta}
in (
5.1
). We will follow this adjustment in the rest of the paper.

5.2
Estimation of
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}

In practice, the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}
is usually unknown, which makes
\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})
and
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
not directly available. To estimate
\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})
, one can again use the LOOCV trick and approximate the expectation by
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\|\mathbf{h}_{i}^{-i}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}/n
. Unfortunately, unlike estimating the excess bias
\Delta B_{\mathbf{X}}
, this approach inevitably requires fitting
n
leave-one-out models, which could be highly inefficient when
n
is large.

Another approach is to estimate the expectation
\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}|\mathbf{X})
using some synthetic data generated based on the training data
\mathbf{X}
that might mimic genuine random samples from the joint distribution of
\mathbf{x}
. Assume that
B
synthetic examples are generated, denoted by
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{(B)}
. Then we can estimate the predictive model degrees of freedom by

\widehat{\mathrm{df}}_{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{df}_{\rm F}+\frac{n}{2\bar{w}}\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}w_{\ast}^{(b)}\|\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{(b)}\|_{\mathbf{T}}^{2}-\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{H}^{\top})\right),

where
w_{\ast}^{(b)}=w(\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{(b)})
and
\mathbf{h}_{\ast}^{(b)}
is the hat vector at
\mathbf{x}_{\ast}^{(b)}
(
b=1,\ldots,B
).

In this work, we consider two different procedures to generate synthetic data: the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) data synthesis method
(
Reiter, 2005
)
and the Naive Bayes Estimation (NBE) learning algorithm
(
Lowd and Domingos, 2005
)
. One big advantage of these two methods is that they are efficient and readily available without any extra model tuning. For a given set of variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, the former seeks to estimate the full conditional distributions
p(x_{j}|x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},x_{d})
using CART
(
Breiman et al., 2017
)
and sample sequentially from these distributions. The NBE algorithm, on the other hand, takes a parametric approach by assuming conditional independence among
X_{j}
’s given a latent variable
Z
and estimating the conditional joint distribution
p(\mathbf{x}|z)
by a mixture of models (Gaussian for continuous variables and multinomial for categorical ones) using the EM algorithm
(
Dempster et al., 1977
)
. In Section
6
, we compare the performance of these two methods in estimation of
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
through some simulation studies.

Here, one question remains to be answered: Can we synthesize
\{(\mathbf{x}_{\ast},y_{\ast})\}_{b=1,\ldots,B}
directly and evaluate out-of-sample prediction error based on the synthetic data? To study its feasibility, we consider a simple example below. Assume
y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{5}x_{ij}+\varepsilon_{i}
, where
x_{ij}
’s and
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We set
n=200
and
B=\text{1,000}
, and generate 500 synthetic datasets based on a fixed training dataset
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
using both the NBE and CART algorithms. We apply the ordinary least squares method with all variables included to fit a linear model. It is easy to show that the true out-of-sample prediction error is given by
\mathrm{ErrR}_{\mathbf{X}}=1+{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}]\approx 1.027
. However, we find that both data synthesizing methods overestimate the true prediction error, with an average of 3.872 (SD = 0.185) for the CART method and 5.878 (SD = 0.270) for the NBE algorithm. For each method, a scatterplot of the synthesized
y_{\ast}
’s against
x_{\ast}
’s is given in Figure
4
. While the linear pattern between
x_{\ast}
and
y_{\ast}
still remains as expected,
y_{\ast}
clearly has variance greater than 1. This explains the inflated prediction error estimates. Our numerical results indicate that such over-dispersion generally becomes more notable as the signal-to-noise ratio increases. Structurally, the full conditional distributions estimated using CART and the joint distribution estimated through the NBE algorithm under the assumption of conditional independence may not provide a good approximation of the true joint distribution of
(\mathbf{x},y)
. Therefore, it is not recommended to estimate the prediction error based on synthetic data directly.

Figure 4
:
Response
y
against its mean
\mu=\mu(\mathbf{x})
from synthetic data generated based on a fixed training dataset of size 200. The true data generating mechanism is
y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{5}x_{ij}+\varepsilon_{i}
, where
x_{ij}
’s and
\varepsilon_{i}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. 1,000 synthetic examples are generated for each method.

On the other hand, this result doesn’t invalidate our approach to estimating
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
using synthetic data. In fact, our numerical results in Section
6.1
demonstrate great robustness of
\widehat{\mathrm{df}}_{\rm R}
to synthetic data even when all features are moderately correlated. Also, note that the estimated predictive model degrees of freedom
\widehat{\mathrm{df}}_{\rm R}
contributes to the prediction error estimate
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
through the term
(2/n)\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\widehat{\mathrm{df}}_{\rm R}
. When
n
is large, the factor
2/n
in front can effectively alleviate the impact of discrepancy between the true and estimated joint covariate distribution. Besides, we can further reduce this impact by wisely selecting variables for data synthesis. For example, when multiple functions of a certain variable
X_{j}
are used as predictors in a model (e.g.,
X_{j},\ldots,X_{j}^{k}
in polynomial regression), it is good practice to synthesize
X_{j}
first and propagate it to other transformations.

6
Numerical Studies

In this section, we evaluate the performance of the estimators developed in Section
5
through numerical studies.

6.1
Simulations

Throughout our experiments, we set
n=60
and generate 20 continuous variables and 20 categorical variables in total (
d=40
). Let
\mathbf{V}=(V_{1},\ldots,V_{20})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{20}
and
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{20})^{\top}\in\{-1,+1\}^{20}
be the sets of continuous and binary categorical variables respectively. To specify relationship among features with different level of complexity, we consider the following two settings.

•

Single component. Assume that
\mathbf{V}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}})
is independent of
\mathbf{C}
, and
C_{1},\ldots,C_{20}
are i.i.d. with
\mathrm{P}(C_{1}=+1)=\mathrm{P}(C_{1}=-1)=1/2
.

•

Mixture of components. Let
Z\in\{-1,+1\}
be a latent random variable such that
\mathrm{P}(Z=+1)=\mathrm{P}(Z=-1)=1/2
. Assume that
\mathbf{V}|Z\sim\mathcal{N}(Z(\log 1,\ldots,\log 20)^{\top},\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}})
,
\mathrm{P}(C_{1}=+1)=1/2
and
\mathrm{P}(C_{j}=+1|C_{j-1},Z)=1/2+ZC_{j-1}/(4+\log j)
for
j=2,\ldots,20
.

For the variance-covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}
, we consider three different cases: 1) I.I.D.:
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{20}
; 2) Equicorrelation:
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}_{20}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{20}\mathbf{1}_{20}^{\top}
; and 3) Autoregressive correlation:
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}=(2^{-|i-j|})_{i,j=1,\ldots,20}
. Let
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}
be the design matrix. We assume that the true data generating mechanism
\mu(\cdot;\bm{\beta})
is linear, i.e.,
\mu(\mathbf{x}_{i};\bm{\beta})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\beta_{j}x_{ij}
. For convenience, we use
\beta^{\rm v}_{j}
and
\beta^{\rm c}_{j}
to denote the coefficient
\beta_{j}
’s for the corresponding continuous and categorical variables respectively. We set
\beta^{\rm v}_{j}=\beta^{\rm c}_{j}/2=a\left(1-j/20\right)^{5}
and choose
a
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{20}(\beta^{\rm v}_{j})^{2}=5
. In subset regression, we add continuous and categorical variables alternatingly in descending order of their coefficients, i.e., in the order
V_{1},C_{1},\ldots,V_{20},C_{20}
.

For the error variances, we assume
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=1
and
\tau(\mathbf{x})\propto(1+|\mu(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})|)^{2}
. We assume
\tau(\mathbf{x})
can be obtained for each
\mathbf{x}
without knowing the mean function
\mu
. We use
w=1/\tau
as the weight function when fitting weighted least squares models.

6.1.1
Estimation of
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}

We consider the CART method and the NBE learning algorithm described in Section
5.2
to generate synthetic data. Figure

illustrates and compares the results using these two procedures based on a random realization of
\mathbf{X}
.

Each method is applied 100 times to generate different synthetic datasets used for estimation of

p

.

pointed out that the naive Bayes mixture model is a special case of Bayesian networks, which are commonly used in general high-dimensional probability representation and estimation. In addition, the naive Bayes mixture model is readily available and orders of magnitude faster than Bayesian networks for sampling and inference. This enables us to estimate the predictive model degrees of freedom in an accurate and efficient fashion.

p
is small, but becomes slightly biased upward as

\hat{\delta}

when

.

6.1.3
WLS vs. OLS

So far in our numerical studies, we have assumed that the the weight function is correctly specified. In practice, however, since error variances are usually unknown, case weights need to be estimated and could be misspecified, which may consequently affect statistical inference and decision. In this section, we examine the effect of weight misspecification for the least squares method. In particular, we focus on model selection and compare two extreme weight schemes: the optimal weight and equal weight schemes under both in-sample and out-of-sample prediction settings. For simplicity, we still assume that the data are generated under the mixture components setting with
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{20}
, and that
\tau(\mathbf{x})\propto(1+|\mu(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})|)^{\lambda}
with
\lambda=2
.

We use
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrF}}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
as a model selection criterion in the in-sample and out-of-sample prediction settings respectively. As shown in Figure
9
, the ordinary least squares model tends to select fewer variables than the weighted least squares model with the optimal weight scheme in both scenarios. This difference in the size of the selected model is generally the same for in-sample and out-of-sample predictions. In terms of the variance of the estimated model size
\hat{p}_{\ast}
, the two methods are quite close, with the weighted least squares model being slightly more variable under the out-of-sample prediction setting.

Figure 9
:
Comparison of the selected model size
\hat{p}_{\ast}
between the ordinary least squares model and the weighted least squares model with the optimal weight scheme based on 500 replicates of
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
generated under the mixture components setting with
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{20}
and
\tau(\mathbf{x})\propto(1+|\mu(\mathbf{x};\bm{\beta})|)^{2}
.
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrF}}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
are used as the model selection criterion in the in-sample and out-of-sample prediction settings respectively.

Since
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
tends to favor a slightly more parsimonious model as we have shown in Section
6.1.2
, misspecification of weights could lead to an oversimplified model. This could further result in a nontrivial increase in the prediction error if the true risk curve to the left of the optimal model size is steep. In our example, the in-sample and out-of-sample prediction errors could be inflated by 12% and 50% respectively on average due to weight misspecification. This issue will become more substantial with more uneven error variances.

After selecting 12 initial predictors, we apply appropriate transformations. Further, using the same data, we estimate the variance function
\tau

.

For the modeling part, we randomly select a subset of size
n=200
to fit a set of WLS models using the estimated variance function

along the variable sequence. We repeat the procedure 500 times. For comparison, we also fit an OLS model with the same set of variables for each subset size

. This suggests that the training error and excess bias in this example may have larger influence on model selection. What is consistent with the simulation is that, when the difference does show up, it is most likely that the WLS model selects more variables than the OLS model. In a few extreme cases, this difference in the size of the selected model can reach up to 6 variables.

For a close-up examination of the above findings, we decompose
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrF}}
and
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
into the training error, excess bias and excess variance, and study the median percentages of these components of the selected models. A figure of the decomposition is provided in Appendix

. We find that the training error is the dominant component in the risk estimate under both in-sample and out-of-sample prediction settings, even when all variables are included. As a result, model selection is greatly impacted by the training error, especially in the in-sample scenario where the term explains more than 90% of the risk. Since the training error is decreasing in the subset size, both WLS and OLS models tend to favor the full model in this scenario. On the other hand, for out-of-sample prediction, the excess variance plays a relatively more important role in model selection (around 20-25% of the risk when all variables are included against 9-12% in the in-sample prediction setting), which leads to a reduction in the size of the selected model. Comparing the WLS and OLS models, the former has a constantly higher percentage of training error and lower percentage of excess variance than the latter. This explains why the WLS model has a slight tendency of selecting a few more variables. In addition, we also see a lower percentage of the excess bias in
\widehat{\mathrm{wErrR}}
of the WLS model. Since the overall percentage of this component is quite small in this example, we expect its impact on model selection would be small as well.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we extend the existing theories on model complexity to a heteroscedastic setting for linear regression procedures. In particular, we generalize the classical model degrees of freedom and predictive model degrees of freedom so that models that incorporate different case weights can be distinguished appropriately. As a typical example, we explore the two extended measures of model degrees of freedom of the weighted least squares method in depth. Interestingly, we find that, in the subset regression setting, the two measures are a shrunken version of those of the ordinary least squares method, and the amount of shrinkage depends on how uneven the error variances are. Furthermore, we set up connections between the two measures of model degrees of freedom and weight schemes both for model fitting and model evaluation. Our results provide valuable insights on how model complexity is affected by case weights, and further provide useful guidance in risk estimation and model selection.

For future study in model complexity, it will be interesting to extend the current framework to a generalized linear model setting. Note that for exponential family distributions other than normal, variance usually shares the same set of parameters with the mean and varies from sample to sample. As a result, our work on heteroscedastic linear regression models holds promise for the possibility of the extension and thus deserves further investigation.

\mathbf{I}_{p}

\mathcal{W}(1,\mathbf{I}_{p})

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)=\frac{\zeta_{\tau}}{2}(p+n\bar{w}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}_{p}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}_{p})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{p})])

When

is large,
Pivaro et al., 2017
suggest to approximate
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{w_{i}}{n\bar{w}}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}
by

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{w_{i}}{n\bar{w}}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{\approx}}\frac{\mathbf{V}}{n_{\tau}},\quad n_{\tau}=\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{w_{i}}{n\bar{w}}\right)^{2}}\right]=\left[\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}1/\tau_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1/\tau_{i})^{2}}\right],

where
\mathbf{V}\sim\mathcal{W}(n_{\tau},\mathbf{I}_{p})
. Since
\frac{\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast})}{\bar{w}}\approx 1
, we then have

n\bar{w}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{\approx}}n_{\tau}\mathbf{V}^{-1}.

Assume

. Note that
\mathbf{V}^{-1}
follows an inverse-Wishart distribution with degrees of freedom
n_{\tau}
and scale matrix
\mathbf{I}_{p}
. It then follows that
\mathrm{E}[{\rm tr}(\mathbf{V}^{-1})]=\frac{p}{n_{\tau}-p-1}

(
Mardia et al., 1979
)
. Using
\frac{n_{\tau}p}{n_{\tau}-p-1}
as an approximation of
n\bar{w}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{p})]
, we then have

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}(p)\approx\frac{p}{2}\zeta_{\tau}\left(1+\frac{n_{\tau}}{n_{\tau}-p-1}\right).

∎

A.6
Proof of Theorem
4.3

Proof.

We first assume that
\mathbf{Q}
is an arbitrary
n\times n
matrix such that
(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}
exists. Define
\bm{\Pi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
as

\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}.

Then
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
can be written as

\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}=\zeta_{\tau}p+\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}[\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{T}\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\mathbf{R}].

Note that

\displaystyle\partial\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=-(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}+(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H}).

Then we have

\displaystyle\partial(\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{T}\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=(\partial\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q}))\mathbf{T}\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\mathbf{R}+\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{T}(\partial\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q}))^{\top}\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle+(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}.

By the chain rule, it follows that

\displaystyle\partial\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\partial{\rm tr}[\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{T}\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\mathbf{R}]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}[\partial(\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{T}\bm{\Pi}(\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\mathbf{R})]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}]

\displaystyle+\frac{n}{2}{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}(\partial\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}]

\displaystyle=n{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\partial\mathbf{Q}].

Thus,

\frac{\partial\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}}{\partial\mathbf{Q}}=n(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}.

Since
\mathbf{Q}
is a diagonal matrix, we further have

\frac{\partial\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}}{\partial\mathbf{q}}=n\,\mathrm{diag}\left((\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right),

where
\mathbf{q}=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{Q})
.
∎

A.7
Proof of Theorem
5.1

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{H}
and
\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}=(\hat{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{n})^{\top}
be the hat matrix and residual vector of the full model respectively. Write
\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\rm loocv}=(\hat{\varepsilon}_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\hat{\varepsilon}_{n}^{-n})^{\top}
. Note that

\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}
,
\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\rm loocv}=\mathrm{diag}((1-h_{ii})^{-1})(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{X})=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathbf{T}
. Then, we have

\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}|\mathbf{X})=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}},

and

\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\rm loocv}|\mathbf{X})=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{\sqrt{w_{i}}}{1-h_{ii}}\right)(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{\sqrt{w_{i}}}{1-h_{ii}}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle{\rm tr}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T})

\displaystyle={\rm tr}[(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(w_{i}(1-h_{ii})^{-2})(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}]-{\rm tr}[(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})^{\top}\mathrm{diag}(w_{i})(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{H})\mathbf{T}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}({\rm tr}[\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{\rm loocv}|\mathbf{X})]-{\rm tr}[\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{W}^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}|\mathbf{X})])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}[\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}^{-i}_{i}|\mathbf{X})-\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|\mathbf{X})].

On the other hand, since
\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}
is defined through the excess variance of the predictions on the test data against those on the training data, it can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathrm{df}_{\rm R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\left(n\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})|\mathbf{X}]-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}[\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})-2\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i},\hat{\mu}_{i}|\mathbf{X})]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\bar{w}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\left(n\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})|\mathbf{X}]-\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{i}|\mathbf{X})-n\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}\right).

Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\xi_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathrm{E}[w_{\ast}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{\ast}|\mathbf{x}_{\ast},\mathbf{X})|\mathbf{X}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\varepsilon}^{-i}_{i}|\mathbf{X})-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\mathrm{E}(w_{\ast}\tau_{\ast})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\tau_{i}\right).

∎

To estimate the variance function

The residuals are then bucketed by 100 percentiles of the fitted values, and their variances are calculated and plotted against the median fitted values for each bucket, as shown in Figure 10 in the paper. \tau(\mathbf{x})=a(1+b|\mu|)^{2},\quad a>0,\,b\neq 0.
