One-Bit Compressed Sensing via One-Shot Hard Thresholding
Jie Shen

Abstract
This paper concerns the problem of 1-bit compressed sensing, where the goal is to estimate a sparse signal from a few of its binary measurements. We study a non-convex sparsity-constrained program and present a novel and concise analysis that moves away from the widely used notion of Gaussian width. We show that with high probability a simple algorithm is guaranteed to produce an accurate approximation to the normalized signal of interest under the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -metric. On top of that, we establish an ensemble of new results that address norm estimation, support recovery, and model misspecification. On the computational side, it is shown that the non-convex program can be solved via one-step hard thresholding which is dramatically efficient in terms of time complexity and memory footprint. On the statistical side, it is shown that our estimator enjoys a near-optimal error rate under standard conditions. The theoretical results are substantiated by numerical experiments.

Introduction

The last two decades have witnessed a large demand of learning from high-dimensional data where the number of attributes is of the same order of, or even greater than the number of observations. Consider, for example, the Lou Gehrig’s disease: there are millions of possible factors to evaluate but the scientists have a very restricted number of samples for research (each year 2 out of 100,000 individuals are affected by it). It thus turns out to be indispensable to investigate and to resolve two fundamental problems in this scenario:
a)
when is it possible to learn a useful model from the small amount of data; and
b)
how can we construct an accurate estimator with mild computational overhead. Answering these two questions has become a central theme in the field of high-dimensional statistics, and there have been a fruitful literature in a variety of applications such as linear regression
[
12
,
34
]
, classification
[
17
]
, principal component analysis
[

,

]
, precision matrix estimation
[

]
, to name just a few.

As a special instance of linear regression, compressed sensing (CS) has attracted increasing attention owing to its ease of implementation and the success in practical problems
[
16
]
. To be more detailed, CS consists of two phases: data acquisition and signal recovery. Suppose that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the signal of interest. During the first phase, the goal is to efficiently sample it and store the obtained measurements in a device. In light of the fact that such a device may not be computationally powerful, a common paradigm is to adopt linear measurements which mitigate hardware implementation and accelerate data collection. That is, we specify the number of measurements
n
and the sensing vectors
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and record in the device the measurements
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
given by

y_{i}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle,\ \forall\ i=1,\dots,n.

(1.1)

For the purpose of efficient sampling, it is required that
n\ll d
, hence the name compressed sensing.

During the second phase, one has access to
\{(\boldsymbol{a}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the primary concern is to recover the underlying signal
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
on a possibly powerful machine. While (
1.1
) is an underdetermined linear system that might have infinite number of feasible solutions, it has been well-understood that with a careful design of the sensing vectors, a broad range of algorithms are capable of producing accurate, or even exact reconstruction of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
in polynomial time provided that it exhibits certain low-dimensional structure. See, for example,
[
11
,
35
,

,
8
,

]
.

Though elegant in theory and compelling in practice,
[
9
]
pointed out that it is not always realistic to collect the measurements as in (
1.1
) since they entail infinite-bit precision for the hardware. Alternatively, the measurements are often quantized into finite bits and in the extreme case, only the sign patterns are retained:

y_{i}=\operatorname{sign}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle\right),\ \forall\ i=1,\dots,n.

(1.2)

The problem of recovering
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
from its binary measurements is referred to as 1-bit compressed sensing, and it bears the potential of savings of physical storage as long as accurate estimation in the 1-bit setting does not require significantly more measurements

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}

(a)

(b)

Sparse Approximation.

Perhaps surprisingly, it was provably shown in
[
25
]
that by seeking a global optimum of
\ell_{1}
-norm constrained programs, it is possible to reliably recover the underlying normalized signal with as many observations as in the standard model (

[
24

. In other words, one has to run the algorithm for a considerable number of iterations in order to attain the optimum. Therefore, a large body of works were dedicated to investigating alternative programs that are endowed with closed-form solutions, which naturally rules out the trouble of slow convergence. For instance,
[
47
]
considered optimizing an
\ell_{1}
-norm penalized function which is exactly the Lagrangian function of the program in
[
25
]
. Interestingly, with the slight modification, it was shown that a simple element-wise thresholding on a certain vector gives even better error rate. Yet, for the sake of accurate recovery, one has to search for a proper regularization parameter which itself could be expensive. In particular, specifying a large value for it will result in trivial statistical guarantee whereas a small value will lead to a trivial solution (i.e. a zero vector).
[
20
]
illustrated that one can derive closed-form solutions for other sparsity-regularized programs such as smoothly clipped absolute deviation penalty
[
18
]
, minimax concave penalty
[
46
]
, and
\ell_{0}
-norm (that counts the number of non-zeros of a vector). Unfortunately, no statistical guarantee was established in that work.

Norm Estimation.

While it seems implausible to evaluate the norm of

Support Recovery.

Parallel to the line of estimating the direction of the signal, a plethora of works examined the problem of recovering the support set. In statistics, it is also known as variable selection or feature selection

Note that support recovery sheds light on sparse approximation in that once the support is identified, we can safely eliminate the irrelevant features and apply standard tools from regression theory (that has a rich literature). Unfortunately, it is typically more challenging to establish theoretical guarantee in this space even under the standard CS model

1.1
Our Contributions

We propose to study a sparsity-constrained non-convex program for the 1-bit CS problem. We first demonstrate that the global optimum can be computed by a simple hard thresholding operator (to be defined) which is computationally efficient. More importantly, the solution is proved to have a near-optimal approximation error rate to the direction of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. We develop novel analysis showing that the approximation error is controlled by two factors: one is independent of the signal structure while the other is entirely determined by it. On top of that, we provide theoretical justification that our estimator recovers the support set of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, can be tailored to offer an accurate estimation of the magnitude of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and is resilient to model misspecification. Namely, even when we feed an inappropriate parameter to the program, our estimate still exhibits favorable performance.

Preliminaries

Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
s
-sparse signal of interest. For now, we presume without loss of generality that it has unit

-norm. While our concentration is on the 1-bit CS problem (

\mathbb{E}[y_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{a}_{i}]=\theta_{i}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle\right),\ \forall\ i=1,\dots,n,

(2.1)

where the mapping functions
\theta_{i}(\cdot)\in[-1,+1]
. Note that under the observation model (
1.2
),
\theta_{i}(\cdot)
is exactly the sign function. It is worth mentioning that our model (

) is more general than the one considered in prior works
[

,
22
,
26
]
, since we allow different mapping function for different sample, i.e.
\theta_{i}
can be mutually distinct.

2.1
The Non-Convex Estimator

In order to recover
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we consider the estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
that is a global optimum of the following program:

\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x},\quad\operatorname{s.t.}\ \left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\right\rVert_{0}\leq k,\ \left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\right\rVert=1,

(2.2)

where
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{A}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{n})^{\top}
. The two constraints accommodate our prior knowledge on the signal
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and the objective function seeks for a maximum correlation between the input and output of
\theta_{i}(\cdot)
. We recall that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is
s
-sparse. The integer
k>0
serves as the only parameter in our estimator, and we need to tune it in practice. Ideally,
k
plays as an upper bound on the true sparsity
s
, which indicates that our estimator is unbiased, i.e. the true signal is contained in the feasible set. Once the condition is violated, we say the model is misspecified which needs a particular treatment (see Section
4.3
).

The optimal solution of the program (
2.2
) can be obtained by simple algebraic calculation. Define the hard thresholding operator as follows:

\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{z}\right):=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}\right\rVert_{0}\leq k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert.

(2.3)

The following computational result is due to
[
5
]
. We present the proof in Appendix
A.1
for completeness.

Proposition 2.1
.

The global optimum
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
of the program (
2.2
) is given by

\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)}{\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)\right\rVert},\ \text{where}\ \boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}.

Observe that the time complexity of calculating
\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}
is
O(dn)
, and applying the hard thresholding costs
O(k\log d)
since the
k
-sparse vector
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{z}\right)
can be efficiently computed by first sorting the elements of
\boldsymbol{z}
in their magnitude, and then retaining the top
k
of them. Therefore, the total running time is
O(dn)
given that
n
is always greater than
k\log d
in compressed sensing.

In light of

While our assumption on

3.1
Noiseless 1-Bit CS

Consider the problem (
1.2

\theta_{i}(g)=\operatorname{sign}\left(g\right)

\lambda_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim N(0,1)}[g\operatorname{sign}\left(g\right)]=\mathbb{E}[\left\lvert g\right\rvert]=\sqrt{2/\pi}.

Thus, we obtain the following corollary regarding the sample complexity of our estimator.

Corollary 3.3

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem

\Pr\left(\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon\right)\geq 1-d^{-10}

3.2
Noisy 1-Bit CS

In real-world applications, the observations are not only highly quantized, but are also grossly corrupted. In fact, owing to possible systematic errors or human mistakes, the sign may be flipped with some probability. The noisy model can thus be formulated as

y_{i}=\xi_{i}\operatorname{sign}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{a}_{i},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle\right),\quad\forall\ i=1,\ \dots,\ n,

(3.2)

where
\xi_{i}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}

\Pr(\xi_{i}=1)=1-p_{i},\quad\Pr(\xi_{i}=-1)=p_{i}

for some

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(g)=\operatorname{sign}\left(g\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}]=(1-2p_{i})\operatorname{sign}\left(g\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\sqrt{2/\pi}(1-2p_{i}).

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\ \left\langle\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle

3.4
Comparison to Prior Works

We first compare with two of the most important works on 1-bit CS
[
25
,
26
]
. Both of them proposed to recover a signal with a generic structure
\mathcal{K}
. Regarding theoretical guarantee,
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
implies that to obtain
\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert\leq\epsilon
, the sample size
n=O\left(\epsilon^{-4}k\log(d/k)\right)
which is worse than what we derived in Theorem
3.1
in terms of the dependence on
\epsilon
. Though
[
26
, Theorem 2.1]
improved the sample complexity to
n=O\left(\epsilon^{-2}k\log(d/k)\right)
, their proof is technically involved (e.g. they applied high-dimensional geometric arguments) even specifying
\mathcal{K}
as the sparsity constraint. In contrast, we depart from their theoretical analysis and reach the same guarantee with fundamental facts in probability theory. Our analysis (Remark
3.7
) shows the decomposition of estimation error which is useful to develop new results for different sensing schemes and signal structures.

In the seminal work of
[
21
]
, a lower bound on the statistical error of sparse approximation was established, and a non-convex program was proposed to achieve the lower bound up to some logarithmic factor. Notably, their guarantee is uniform, meaning that a single draw of the sensing vectors ensures recovery of all sparse signals, whereas our result is specified to a particular signal. Their sample complexity is
n=O(\epsilon^{-1}k\log d)
, which has a better dependence on
\epsilon
than this work. However, due to the non-convex nature, it is not clear how to solve their program in polynomial time. As a matter of fact, an iterative algorithm was devised with compelling performance in practical problems, but it lacks theoretical backend on the convergence behavior.

It is worth noting that
[
48
, Theorem 4.3]
claimed a sample complexity bound of
O(s)
for certain type of
\mathcal{K}
, which is a stronger result than what we obtained in the paper. However, this may not be the true sample complexity since in order to fulfill their conditions (i.e.
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}
is sufficiently large), one needs
O(s\log d)
samples. For the recovery of general sparse signals, they showed a sample complexity bound similar to ours through different non-convex estimators. In this case, our primary improvement falls into the modular analysis and computational efficiency.

Extensions

Theorem

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}

4.1
Support Recovery

We first describe when our estimator reliably detects the support of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. We would like point out that in some applications such as medical diagnosis, it is of crucial importance to discover the determinants of a disease (i.e. support recovery). Intuitively, a factor can be identified only when it has “sufficient” impact on the disease. This notion of significance is characterized by the following mathematical quantity:

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}:=\min_{i\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\left\lvert\bar{x}_{i}\right\rvert.

(4.1)

Throughout the paper, we consider
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}\neq 0
, i.e. the signal of interest is non-zero. We utilize a well-known fact to derive the guarantee of support set of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For a given signal
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, if

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert<\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min},

then it holds that
\operatorname{supp}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\subset\operatorname{supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
.

The lemma can be proved by algebra (see Appendix
A.5
), and has been widely used in the literature
[
38
]
. In allusion to Theorem
3.1
(that is, let the right-hand side therein be less than
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}
), we show that the support set of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is contained in that of our estimate.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
3.1
. Then
\operatorname{supp}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\subset\operatorname{supp}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
provided that

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}>\frac{\mathrm{{C}}}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k\log d}{n}}.

In particular, if we know exactly the sparsity of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we have
\operatorname{supp}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)=\operatorname{supp}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
.

Remark 4.3
.

In the special case that
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a binary vector, the above theorem indicates exact signal recovery under near-optimal sample complexity.

Remark 4.4
.

The proof of Lemma
4.1
essentially suggests that the minimum condition for support recovery is
\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}<\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}
. Yet we conjecture that even with such condition Theorem
4.2
may not be significantly improved. Suppose that
N
samples suffice for support recovery of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Consider a two-step scheme of sparse approximation: 1) recover support; 2) linear regression restricted on the obtained support set. Since the second step needs
k
samples, the total sample size is

. As lower bound of sparse approximation is

we must have

.

4.3
Model Misspecification

In our previous discussion, we always assume that the sparsity
k
is equal to, or greater than the true sparsity
s
, i.e. the true signal is contained in the feasible set. However, sometimes we may choose
k<s
in that we are not aware of
s
. As a result, recovery of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is impossible but we can still hope to approximate its
k
largest (i.e. most important) components to a high precision.

We now elaborate the new results under the misspecified model. One notable fact is that even when
k<s
, the normalized sparse vector
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
is feasible to the non-convex program (
2.2
). Therefore, for sparse approximation we may apply the same induction and obtain the following.

Theorem 4.7
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
3.1
but
k<s
. With probability at least
1-d^{-10}
,

\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\mathrm{{C}}}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k\log d}{n}}+\sqrt{2k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty},

where
\boldsymbol{z}:=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}{\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\right\rVert}
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
A.4
. The second term on the right-hand side is the price we pay for model misspecification, and it vanishes as soon as
k\geq s
. It is worth mentioning that it depends exclusively on the nature of the signal
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
rather than on the data acquisition procedure. If the
s
-sparse signal
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
has a light tail, i.e. the first
k
components dominate the magnitude, then
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
behaves as a good estimate. To see this, let us write
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{s},0,\dots,0)
in descending order (according to the magnitude of the elements). In this way
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)=(\bar{x}_{1},\dots,\bar{x}_{k},0,\dots,0)
. Let us denote
\alpha=\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\right\rVert
for now. It follows that

\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\max\bigg\{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)\left\lvert\bar{x}_{1}\right\rvert,\ \left\lvert\bar{x}_{k+1}\right\rvert\bigg\}.

(4.5)

If the
k
leading components dominate the remaining, then
\alpha\approx 1
and
\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k+1}\right\rvert\approx 0
hold simultaneously. As a consequence,
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
is close to zero, under which Theorem
4.7
implies that we can accurately identify the principal direction of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
given sufficient measurements.

Now we move on to discuss the support recovery of the top
k
elements. In light of Lemma
4.1
, the support of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
is consistent with that of
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
as soon as the sample size
n=O\left((\lambda\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k}\right\rvert)^{-2}k\log d\right)
and

\sqrt{2k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}<\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k}\right\rvert.

Consider in (
4.5
) that the infinite norm is given by
(1/\alpha-1)\left\lvert\bar{x}_{1}\right\rvert
. It follows that a sufficient condition for support recovery is

\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k+1}\right\rvert\leq\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}-1\right)\left\lvert\bar{x}_{1}\right\rvert<\frac{\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k}\right\rvert}{\sqrt{2k}}.

The first inequality, which upper bounds
\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k+1}\right\rvert
, indicates that for our purpose, the elements outside of the support of interest cannot be too large. The second inequality, which lower bounds
\left\lvert\bar{x}_{k}\right\rvert
, tells that those inside of the support need to have sufficient magnitude.

Lastly, as we discussed in Section
4.2
, a good approximation to the direction of
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
implies a good estimation of the norm. We remark that this observation holds for a misspecified model as well.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied an efficient estimator for recovering a sparse signal from its binary measurements. On the computational side, the estimate can be obtained by a one-step hard thresholding operator which enjoys economic computational and memory cost. On the statistical side, we have shown that the estimation error matches the information-theoretic lower bound up to some logarithmic factor. We have also extended our results to support recovery and norm estimation, and have proved near-optimal error rate in these scenarios. For the estimation of all the three facets of a sparse signal, we have offered rigorous theoretical evidence that our estimator is robust to model misspecification. Finally, we have demonstrated through a comprehensive set of experiments that the practical performance of our estimator matches perfectly our analysis.

\displaystyle\lVert v-\mathbb{E}v\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq\lVert v\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\lVert\mathbb{E}v\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\lVert v\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\left\lvert\mathbb{E}v\right\rvert\leq\lVert v\rVert_{\psi_{2}}+\mathbb{E}\left\lvert v\right\rvert\leq 2\lVert v\rVert_{\psi_{2}}.

Thus
\lVert a_{i}^{(j)}y_{i}-\lambda_{i}\bar{x}^{(j)}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq 2\mathrm{{C}}_{0}
. Now by Proposition 5.10 in
[
37
]
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[a_{i}^{(j)}y_{i}-\lambda_{i}\bar{x}^{(j)}\Big]\right\rvert\geq t\right)\leq e\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{{C}}_{1}t^{2}}{(2\mathrm{{C}}_{0})^{2}/n}\right).

Picking
t=\mathrm{{C}}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}
for some constant
\mathrm{{C}}
we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[a_{i}^{(j)}y_{i}-\lambda_{i}\bar{x}^{(j)}\Big]\right\rvert\geq\mathrm{{C}}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-11\log d\right)=d^{-11}.

By union bound over the coordinates
j
, we show that

\Pr\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\geq\mathrm{{C}}\sqrt{\frac{\log d}{n}}\right)\leq d^{-10}

which completes the proof.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
4.7

The proof is very modular and thus facilitates the extension to other types of signals and sensing matrices, as we discussed in Remark
3.7
.

Proof.

Consider the program (
2.2
). Note that
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
is optimal and
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)/\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\right\rVert
is feasible. It is worth mentioning that when
k\geq s
,
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)/\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\right\rVert=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
. For simplicity, we write
\boldsymbol{z}={\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}/{\left\lVert\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\right\rVert}
. It immediately follows that

\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\geq\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{z}.

With some re-arrangement, we have

\left\langle-\lambda\boldsymbol{z},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\boldsymbol{z},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle.

Note that as
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert=1
, the left-hand side can be written as follows:

\left\langle-\lambda\boldsymbol{z},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert^{2}.

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\langle\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\boldsymbol{z},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\cdot\sqrt{2k}\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert,

where the second inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality, the third inequality follows from the facts that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert_{0}}\cdot\left\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\right\rVert
for all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and that
\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert_{0}\leq 2k
. Dividing both sides by
\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert
gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\sqrt{2k}}{\lambda}\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\boldsymbol{z}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq{\frac{\sqrt{2k}}{\lambda}\left\lVert\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}-\lambda\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}+{\sqrt{2k}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert_{\infty}}.

The first term on the right-hand side can be bounded from the above by Lemma
3.6
.
∎

A.5
Proof of Lemma
4.1

This is a well recognized result, and we include the proof for completeness.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
\operatorname{supp}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
is not contained in
\operatorname{supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
. That is, there exists an index
j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, such that
\bar{x}_{j}\neq 0,\quad x_{j}=0
. Therefore, we have
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert\geq\left\lvert\bar{x}_{j}\right\rvert\geq\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\min}
, which leads to a contradiction.
∎

A.6
Proof of Theorem
4.5

Proof.

Consider the program (
4.3
). Let the global optimum
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\ t_{0})}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+t_{0}^{2}}}
where
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}
is feasible, we may apply Theorem
3.1
and obtain that with probability at least
1-d^{-10}
,

\left\lVert\frac{(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}};\ R)}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert^{2}+R^{2}}}-\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{0};\ t_{0})}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+t_{0}^{2}}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\mathrm{{C}}}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k\log d}{n}}=:{\delta}.

(A.3)

For the case
t_{0}\neq 0
, the proof of Theorem 4 in
[
22
]
immediately implies that

\left\lVert\frac{R}{t_{0}}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\mathrm{{C}}_{1}\cdot R}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k\log d}{n}}.

For the case
t_{0}=0
, it follows from (
A.3
)

\left\lVert\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{1};\ 1)}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-{(\boldsymbol{x}_{2};\ 0)}\right\rVert\leq\delta,

(A.4)

where we write
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}/R
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}/\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\rVert
.

On the other side, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\zeta_{1}}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{2}\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\zeta_{2}}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{2}\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\rVert+\sqrt{2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{2}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\zeta_{3}}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{2}\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\rVert+\sqrt{2}\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\zeta_{4}}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{2}\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\rVert+\sqrt{2}\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\zeta_{5}}}{{\leq}}2\left(\left\lVert\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{1}}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lvert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=2\left\lVert\frac{(\boldsymbol{x}_{1};\ 1)}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}-(\boldsymbol{x}_{2};\ 0)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta.

In the above, the inequality
\zeta_{1}
holds since
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert\leq 1
,
\zeta_{2}
follows from the triangle inequality,
\zeta_{3}
uses the fact
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\right\rVert\leq 1
. To see why
\zeta_{4}
holds, note that
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert\leq 1
implies

\left\lvert 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}\right\rvert\leq 1-1/\sqrt{2}<1/\sqrt{2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\right\rVert^{2}+1}}.

The last inequality
\zeta_{5}
applies
a+b\leq\sqrt{2}\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
for any two scalars
a
and
b
. Therefore, we obtain that

\left\lVert\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}{R}-\frac{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\rVert}\right\rVert\leq\frac{2\mathrm{{C}}}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k\log d}{n}},

namely

\left\lVert\frac{R}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right\rVert}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{2\mathrm{{C}}\cdot R}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k\log d}{n}}

which completes the proof.
∎
