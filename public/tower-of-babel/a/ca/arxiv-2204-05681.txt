Abstract
This work presents the dual benefit of integrating imitation learning techniques, based on the dynamical systems formalism, with the visual servoing paradigm. On the one hand, dynamical systems allow to program additional skills without explicitly coding them in the visual servoing law, but leveraging few demonstrations of the full desired behavior. On the other, visual servoing allows to consider exteroception into the dynamical system architecture and be able to adapt to unexpected environment changes. The beneficial combination of the two concepts is proven by applying three existing dynamical systems methods to the visual servoing case. Simulations validate and compare the methods; experiments with a robot manipulator show the validity of the approach in a real-world scenario.

Preliminaries

II-A

Visual servoing

VS allows to control robots using a visual feedback with
the form of features
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{f}
.
The aim is the matching with desired values
\bm{s}^{\ast}
to zero the error
\bm{e}=\bm{s}-\bm{s}^{\ast}
.
In image-based VS, considered in this work, the feedback is directly defined on the image. With constant
\bm{s}^{\ast}
and a fixed target, the classical VS computes the 6D camera velocity commands as

\bm{v}=-\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}^{+}\bm{e}

(1)

where
\lambda
is a positive control gain and
\hat{\bm{L}}
is an approximation (e.g., due to the unknown features depth) of the interaction matrix
[
15
]
. A possibility is to use its value at the target, known in advance
[
14
]
.
To fully control the camera in the 3D space, avoid singularities and poses ambiguity, the feedback dimension
f
has to square the system, at least
[
14
]
. In image-based VS, the stability is ensured only close to the target, if the interaction matrix is full rank and well approximated. In this case, (
1
) realizes converging trajectories of the features to their desired values. For example, using the true interaction matrix and point features, such a convergence generates
a linear motion of
s
towards
{\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}^{\ast}
.
For complex trajectories, like that in Fig.
1
, the features should deviate from this
expected motion, e.g., by adding constraints or tasks to the VS law.

II-B

Skills representation using dynamical systems

In
ds
-based
il
, it is common to assume that a robotic skill is encoded into a control affine system:

\dot{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}={\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})+{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}},t),

(2)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state (e.g., robot position),
\dot{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the time derivative of the state,
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the time, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}(\cdot)
a control input. Both
{\mbox{\boldmath$f$}}(\cdot)
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}(\cdot)
are assumed to be smooth.
In order to use
ds
to generate discrete and converging-to-a-target trajectories, it is important to guarantee the stability of (
2
), e.g., using Lyapunov arguments
[
35
]
.
The work in
[
6
,
7
]
assume that
\bm{f}
is stable and exploits the explicit time dependency of
u
to ensure
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}\rightarrow\bm{0}
for
t\rightarrow+\infty
, guaranteeing the convergence to a given target.
Other approaches
[
9
,
10
,
36
]
further restrict the
ds
in (
2
) to be autonomous (without explicit time dependencies). More in detail,
[
9
,
36
]
assume
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}=\bm{0}
and focus on learning a stable
f
;
[
10
]
computes a stabilizing control input
u
from a learned Lyapunov function.

II-C

Problem definition

We propose to modify the standard VS law (
1
) using the DS rationale (
2
) to augment the skills of the vision-based robotic behavior.
In this way, additional tasks do not need to be explicitly coded. Instead, they can be programmed from demonstrations of the full desired behavior, which are manually executed and stored offline.
The objective is to online infer from data the modification to be applied to the basic original behavior without damaging its stability.

Image-based VS uses a feedback directly defined on the image to move the camera to a desired pose.
Indeed, a 3D camera trajectory corresponds a visual features trajectory on the image plane, see Fig.
1
.
In practice, the VS skill cannot be directly expressed as in (
2
), where the variable of interest
\bm{x}
and its time derivative are defined in the same state space.

In fact, in the case of robot kinematic control,
\bm{x}
is often associated to configuration vectors or end-effector poses
[
9
,
10
,
7
,
11
]
and actually treated as an error
1
1

1

Without loss of generality, it is commonly assumed that the system has to converge to zero, i.e., the target is assumed constant and at the origin.
(in the joint or task space). Thus, DS is used to generate a stable behavior zeroing this error.
Joint and task space quantities are also taken into account in the same DS
[
37
]
by first projecting the task space error in the joint space with the robot Jacobian transpose. The projected error is then pre-multiplied by a learned matrix forcing the trajectory to follow the demonstrations.
Also in the VS case, DS has to move between two spaces: the image and the Cartesian world.
Namely, the DS-based VS goal is to describe the desired camera behavior, through its velocity
\bm{v}
, from visual measurements
\bm{e}
.
Thus, it is convenient to define

\bm{\varepsilon}\triangleq\hat{\bm{L}}^{+}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}

(3)

relating the visual error to a Cartesian quantity and allowing to write the DS-based VS as
\bm{v}=\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})+\bm{u}(\bm{\varepsilon},t)
or
\bm{v}=\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})
depending on the DS choice. Note that the DS-based formulation differs from an end-to-end approach in that the control keeps its structure.
DS methods learns
\bm{f}
(and/or
\bm{u}
, according with the chosen method) respecting
the original controller stability.
We further highlight that
\hat{\bm{L}}
, expressing the image-Cartesian space mapping, is not learned but its analytical structure is
maintained in our data, see Sec.
III-A
. In principle,
\bm{\varepsilon}
could be measured from images—like in position-based
vs
schemes—and classical DSs could be directly used. However, pose estimation requires a major perception effort; we prefer to use image-based
vs
, for which an adaptation of existing
ds
is required (see Sec.
III
).

Solving complex VS tasks is formulated as learning DSs such that they (i)
stay stable as the original VS; (ii) reproduce the demonstrations; (iii) online adapt to environment changes using visual feedback.
To this end, we apply three existing DSs to VS, analysing and comparing their performance.

III-A

Demonstrations dataset

Let us assume to have
D

{\cal D}=\left\{\bm{e}_{n}^{d},\,\bm{v}_{n}^{d}\right\}_{n=1,d=1}^{N,D}

(4)

where

is the camera velocity needed to achieve the desired VS task at the
n

\bm{e}_{n}^{d}
is the related visual error.
We make the following assumptions:

1.

are fixed and known in advance; thus for a given set of observed visual features
\bm{s}
, the error
\bm{e}
is directly and uniquely defined;

2.

a sufficient number of visual features, enough to uniquely identify the pose of the camera, is used;

3.

\lambda

4.

the interaction matrix is approximated with its known value at the target
\forall\,n,d
, i.e.,
\hat{\bm{L}}_{n}^{d}=\bm{L}_{N}^{D}
.

Under assumptions

.
Assumptions

\bm{\varepsilon}
(
3
) and the standard VS law (

)
\forall\,n,d
.
The demonstrations (
4
) are the base for building the training datasets for the considered
ds
as detailed in what follows.

III-B

rds
for
vs

The RDS idea
[
7
]
is to reshape a stable base dynamics with an additive term following few demonstrations.
Convergence is ensured forcing the reshaping to vanish after a certain time.

Using the the rationale behind
rds
, we propose to handle complex
vs
tasks reshaping the base dynamics (
1
) as follows

\bm{v}=-\lambda\bm{\varepsilon}+h{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{\text{RDS}}(\bm{\varepsilon}),

(5)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{\text{RDS}}(\bm{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a continuous and continuously differentiable function, acting as reshaping term of the original
vs
law.
It is used to enable, besides classical
vs
tasks, additional skills that are difficult to carry out with (
1
), leveraging the information stored in demonstrations of the full desired task.
The activation of the reshaping term is triggered by the clock variable
h
that vanishes after a user-defined time
[
7
]
.

Our goal is to estimate
\bm{u}_{\text{RDS}}
such that the camera velocity computed from (
5
) accurately reproduces the demonstrations (
4
).
Thus, we first need to reconstruct
\bm{u}_{\text{RDS},n}^{d}
from
\cal D
. Assuming
h\!=\!1
in (
5
), it is
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{\text{RDS}}(\bm{\varepsilon})=\bm{v}+\lambda\bm{\varepsilon}
. The dataset
\cal D
can thus be translated in the actual training dataset

{\cal T}_{\text{RDS}}=\left\{\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{d},\,\bm{u}_{\text{RDS},n}^{d}\right\}_{n=1,d=1}^{N,D}.

(6)

Setting
h=1\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ n,d
implies only minor approximation errors on the overall reproduction accuracy, as remarked in
[
7
]
and empirically observed also in this work, see Sec.
IV
.

Given
{\cal T}_{\text{RDS}}
and the assumptions in Sec.
III-A
, a reshaping term can be uniquely computed from a set of observed visual features.
Thus, any regression technique can learn and online retrieve a reshaping term from the training dataset for a novel set of visual feature, i.e. we can estimate the reshaping term as
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{RDS}}(\bm{\varepsilon})\simeq\bm{r}(\bm{s}|{\cal T}_{\text{RDS}})
, being
\bm{r}
a regression function. Among the others, we use
gmr

[
38
]
because: its estimates are smooth and respect the assumption on
\bm{u}_{\text{RDS}}
;
it is fast to compute, thus, suitable to online control loops;
the only hyper-parameter (the number of Gaussians) is easy to tune.

III-C

clfdm
for
vs

clfdm
stabilizes at run-time possibly unstable dynamics learned from demonstration
[
10
]
.
The stabilization is achieved with a control input computed from a
clf
solving a convex optimization problem.
Also CLF, denoted with
V(\bm{\varepsilon})
, is learned from the demonstrations with the objective of minimizing the number of points for which
\dot{V}(\bm{\varepsilon})\geq 0
.

With
clfdm
, complex
vs
tasks are handled by the law

\bm{v}=\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})+\bm{u}_{\text{CLF}}(\bm{\varepsilon}),

(7)

where
\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})
is a possibly unstable dynamics and
\bm{u}_{\text{CLF}}(\bm{\varepsilon})
is a stabilizing controller computed from
V(\bm{\varepsilon})
.

In
clfdm
, the VS skill is encoded in
\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})
, while
\bm{u}_{\text{CLF}}(\bm{\varepsilon})
is only responsible to correct the dynamics of
\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})
to preserve stability. Therefore, the learning in
clfdm
is performed in two steps. First, the camera velocity
\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})
is estimated
such that it accurately reproduces the demonstrations.
Thus, we compute
\bm{f}_{n}^{d}
from
\cal D
as
\bm{f}_{n}^{d}=\bm{v}_{n}^{d}
, and the demonstrations are translated into the actual training dataset

{\cal T}_{\text{CLF}}=\left\{\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{d},\,\bm{f}_{n}^{d}\right\}_{n=1,d=1}^{N,D}.

(8)

As for
rds
, any regression technique can learn and online retrieve
\bm{f}(\bm{\varepsilon})
from
{\cal T}_{\text{CLF}}
for a novel set of visual feature, i.e.
\hat{\bm{f}}(\bm{\varepsilon})\simeq{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}|{\cal T}_{\text{CLF}})
.
Also here,
r
is implemented as a
gmr
.

In the second learning step, an estimate
\hat{V}
of the CLF is learned solving an optimization problem minimizing the number of points for which
\dot{\hat{V}}=\nabla\hat{V}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{d})\bm{f}_{n}^{d}\geq 0
. The expression of
\dot{\hat{V}}
depends on
\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{d}
and
\bm{f}_{n}^{d}
, which are already contained into the training dataset
{\cal T}_{\text{CLF}}
in (
8
). After the learning, we can retrieve
\hat{V}
and
\nabla\hat{V}
for the observed
\bm{\varepsilon}
and compute the stabilizing control input
\bm{u}_{\text{CLF}}(\bm{\varepsilon})
at run time as

{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{\text{CLF}}(\bm{\varepsilon})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\bm{0}&\forall\penalty\ \bm{\varepsilon}\neq\bm{0}:\dot{\hat{V}}\leq-\rho\\
-\hat{\bm{f}}&\text{if}\penalty\ \bm{\varepsilon}=\bm{0}\\
\frac{\nabla\hat{V}\hat{\bm{f}}+\rho}{\nabla\hat{V}\nabla\hat{V}^{\top}}\nabla\hat{V}^{\top}&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(9)

being
\rho(\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|)\!>\!0
a class
\mathcal{K}
function imposing the minimum change rate of
\hat{V}
; the dependence on
\bm{\varepsilon}
is omitted for brevity.
The details about the derivation of (
9
) can be found in
[
10
]
.

III-D

fdm
for
vs

fdm
is used to compute a diffeomorphism, a continuous and differentiable function
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}(\cdot)
with continuous and differentiable inverse, mapping linear trajectories into the demonstrated motions. Convergence to a target is guaranteed by the diffeomorphism properties (see
[
11
]
for the details).

According to
fdm
, complex
vs
tasks can be handled as:

{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}=-\gamma(\bm{\varepsilon}){\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{\psi}^{-1}(\bm{\varepsilon})\bm{\varepsilon},

(10)

being
{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}_{\psi}
the Jacobian of the diffeomorphism
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}(\bm{\varepsilon})
;
\gamma(\bm{\varepsilon})
is a variable gain used to track the demonstrated velocity.
Both
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}(\bm{\varepsilon})
and
\gamma(\bm{\varepsilon})
are estimated from data as described next.

As already mentioned,
\psi
maps linear error trajectories
\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{l}
into the demonstrated errors
\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{d}
, i.e.,
\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{d}={\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}(\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{l})
. The linear trajectory is obtained as
\{\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{l}=\left[(N-n)/N\right]\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}\}_{n=0}^{N}
, where
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}
is the average of the
D
initial errors computed from (
4
).
It is easy to verify that
\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{l}
contains a set of straight lines connecting each component of
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}
to the origin. Note that the
fdm
training algorithm does not handle multiple demonstrations.
A solution
[
11
]
is to average the
D
demonstrations—assuming they have the same length—obtaining the average errors
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{n}
. This solution is simple, but it generates deviations between reproduced and demonstrated motions (see Sec.
IV
).
With these considerations, we translate
\cal D
into

{\cal T}_{\text{FDM}}=\left\{\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{n},\,\bm{\varepsilon}_{n}^{l}\right\}_{n=1}^{N}

(11)

and use it to estimate
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}(\bm{\varepsilon})\simeq{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$s$}}|{\cal T}_{\text{FDM}})
using the fast algorithm proposed in
[
11
]
.
The gain variable
\gamma(\bm{\varepsilon})
is defined such that the error trajectory, governed by
\dot{\bm{\varepsilon}}=-\gamma(\bm{\varepsilon})\bm{\varepsilon}
, starts from
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}
, passes through the points
\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{n}
and finally converges to zero. Following the formulation in
[
11
]
, it is:

\gamma(\bm{\varepsilon})=\begin{cases}\|\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}\|/(NT\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|)&\text{if}\penalty\ \|\bm{\varepsilon}\|\geq\|\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}\|/N\\
\|\tilde{\bm{\varepsilon}}_{1}\|/N&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(12)

where
T
is the sampling time.

rds
and

V
Conclusions and Future Work

We presented the application of three DS-based IL methods to the VS case, for the achievement of complex visual tasks without the need of explicit coding.
By leveraging the information of few demonstrated trajectories, it has been possible to augment the basic VS behavior with additional tasks, such as collision avoidance.
At the same time, using VS within DS techniques has allowed to adapt to environmental changes.
Simulations and experiments validated the approach, showing the beneficial integration of DS and VS.

Future work will be devoted to reduce even more the user’s programming burden.
For example, direct VS approaches, which avoid to explicitly extract visual features from images, could be integrated in the DS-based paradigm.
Furthermore, the proposed system could be used to ease the deployment of VS strategies on complex platforms, such as humanoids.
