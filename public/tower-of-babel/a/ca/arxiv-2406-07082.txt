Independence of the Diophantine exponents associated with linear subspaces
Gaétan GUILLOT

Abstract
We elaborate on a problem raised by Schmidt in 1967 which generalizes the theory of classical Diophantine approximation to subspaces of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . We consider Diophantine exponents for linear subspaces of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} which generalize the irrationality measure for real numbers. We prove here that we have no smooth relations among some functions associated to these exponents. To establish this result, we construct subspaces for which we are able to compute the exponents.

Introduction

In classical Diophantine approximation, one studies how well real numbers (or points of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) can be approximated by rational numbers (or rational points). In 1967, Schmidt
[
14
]
stated a generalisation of this problem in which one studies how well can subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be approximated by rational subspaces. We define briefly the necessary notions for the study of this problem. We use the definitions and notation from
[
14
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
8
]
. Additional details regarding the results of this article can be found in
[
5
]
.

We say that a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is rational if it has a basis of vectors with rational coordinates. We denote by
\mathcal{R}_{n}(e)
the set of all rational subspaces of dimension
e
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To such a rational subspace
B\in\mathcal{R}_{n}(e)
we can associate a point
\eta=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{N})\in\mathbb{P}^{N}(\mathbb{R})
with
N=\binom{n}{e}
called the Grassmann (or Plücker) coordinates of
B
. We can choose a representative vector
\eta
with coprime integer coordinates and we define:

H(B)=\|\eta\|

where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let us notice that if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{e}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
B\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then
H(B)=\|X_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge X_{e}\|
. More results on the height can be found in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
.

Let us fix
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
. For
d,e\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket^{2}
we set
g(d,e,n)=\max(0,d+e-n)
. For
j\in\llbracket 1,\min(d,e)-g(d,e,n)\rrbracket
we say that a subspace
A
of dimension
d
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
(e,j)\text{-}
irrational if

\forall\,B\in\mathcal{R}_{n}(e),\quad\dim(A\cap B)<j+g(d,e,n).

We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{j}
the set of all
(e,j)\text{-}
irrational subspaces
A
of dimension
d
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We define now the notion of proximity between two subspaces. For
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\},
set

\omega(X,Y)=\frac{\|X\wedge Y\|}{\|X\|\cdot\|Y\|}

where
X\wedge Y
is the exterior product of
X
and
Y
. Geometrically,
\omega(X,Y)
is the absolute value of the sine of the angle between
X
and
Y
. Let
A
and
B
be two subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of respective dimensions
d
and
e
. We construct by induction
t=\min(d,e)
angles between
A
and
B
. Let us define

\omega_{1}(A,B)=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in A\setminus\{0\}\\
Y\in B\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\omega(X,Y)

and
(X_{1},Y_{1})\in A\times B
such that
\omega(X_{1},Y_{1})=\omega_{1}(A,B)
. Let us assume that
\omega_{1}(A,B),\ldots,\omega_{j}(A,B)
and
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{j},Y_{j})
has been constructed for
j\in\llbracket 1,t-1\rrbracket
. Let
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
be respectively the orthogonal complements of
\text{Span}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{j})
in
A
and
\text{Span}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{j})
in
B
. We define

\omega_{j+1}(A,B)=\min\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\in A_{j}\setminus\{0\}\\
Y\in B_{j}\setminus\{0\}\end{subarray}}\omega(X,Y)

and
(X_{j+1},Y_{j+1})\in A\times B
such that
\omega(X_{j+1},Y_{j+1})=\omega_{j+1}(A,B)
. We can notice that if
g(d,e,n)>0
then
\omega_{1}(A,B)=\ldots=\omega_{g(d,e,n)}(A,B)=0
, so we introduce for
j\in\llbracket 1,\min(d,e)-g(d,e,n)\rrbracket
:

\psi_{j}(A,B)=\omega_{j+g(d,e,n)}(A,B).

We now have all the tools to define the Diophantine exponents studied in this paper.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(d,e)\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket^{2}
,
j\in\llbracket 1,\min(d,e)-g(d,e,n)\rrbracket
and
A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{j}
. We define
\mu_{n}(A|e)_{j}
as the supremum of the set of all
\mu>0
such that there exist infinitely many
B\in\mathcal{R}_{n}(e)
such that

\psi_{j}(A,B)\leq H(B)^{-\mu}.

The goal here is to study some joint spectrum of these exponents, that is to say determine the values taken by functions of the form

\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bigcap\limits_{(e,j)\in U}\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{j}&\longrightarrow&(\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\})^{U}\\
A&\longmapsto&(\mu_{n}(A|e)_{j})_{(e,j)\in U}\end{array}\right.

where
d
is fixed and
U
is a subset of
V_{d,n}=\{(e,j)\mid e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket 1,\min(d,e)-g(d,e,n)\rrbracket\}
.

In the case
d=1
the joint spectrum
\left(\mu_{n}(\cdot|1)_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}(\cdot|n-1)_{1}\right)
has been fully described by Roy
[
13
]
using parametric geometry of numbers and the previous results of Schmidt
[
14
]
and Laurent
[
10
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Roy 2016)
.

For any
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n-1}\in[1,+\infty]
satisfying
\mu_{1}~\geq~\frac{n}{n-1}
and

\displaystyle\forall\,e\in\llbracket 2,n-1\rrbracket,\quad\frac{e\mu_{e}}{\mu_{e}+e-1}\leq\mu_{e-1}\leq\frac{(n-e)\mu_{e}}{n-e+1},

there exists
A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(1,n-1)_{1}
such that

\forall\,e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket,\quad\mu_{n}(A|e)_{1}=\mu_{e}.

A corollary of this theorem is that are there no smooth relations between these exponents. For
W
a set and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
functions from
W
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we say that
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}
are smoothly independent on
W
if there is no submersion
h:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
w\in W

(f_{1}(w),\ldots,f_{k}(w))\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h(f_{1}(w),\ldots,f_{k}(w))=0.

In particular, if such a relation were to exist, the image of
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
intersected with
\mathbb{R}^{k}
would be contained in a hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

The functions
\mu_{n}(\cdot|1)_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}(\cdot|n-1)_{1}
are smoothly independent on
\mathcal{I}_{n}(1,n-1)_{1}
.

By Theorem
1.2
, the image of the joint spectrum is indeed a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with non-empty interior ; it is not contained in any hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. The goal of this paper is to generalize the result of Corollary
1.3
with
d\geq 1
. We will show the following theorems. In Theorems
1.4
,
1.5
and
1.6
, we consider not only lines but subspaces of any dimension
d
. The first one deals with the first angle while the other two treat the case of the last angle.

Theorem 1.4
.

Given
d\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\},
the functions
\mu_{n}(\cdot|1)_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}(\cdot|n-d)_{1}
are smoothly independent on
\bigcap\limits_{e=1}^{n-d}\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{1}=\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,n-d)_{1}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Assume that
d
divides
n
. The functions
\mu_{n}(\cdot|1)_{\min(d,1)},\ldots,\mu_{n}(\cdot|n-d)_{\min(d,n-d)}
are smoothly independent on
\bigcap\limits_{e=1}^{n-d}\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{\min(d,e)}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume that
d
divides
n
. The functions
\mu_{n}(\cdot|d)_{d},\ldots,\mu_{n}(\cdot|n-1)_{d}
are smoothly independent on
\bigcap\limits_{e=d}^{n-1}\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{d}
.

In order to prove these theorems, we construct subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Diophantine exponents that can be computed. With these constructions, we can prove that the image of
(\mu_{n}(\cdot|e)_{j})_{(e,j)\in U}
contains a subset with non-empty interior which gives Theorems
1.4
,
1.5
and
1.6
. We will herein show the following theorems.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
d\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
. There exists an explicit constant
C_{d}>0
, such that for any
(\gamma_{1}\ldots,\gamma_{n-d})\in[C_{d},+\infty[^{n-d}
, there exists
A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,n-d)_{1}
satisfying

\displaystyle\forall\,e\in\llbracket 1,n-d\rrbracket,\quad\mu_{n}(A|e)_{1}=\max\limits_{i\in\llbracket 0,n-d-e\rrbracket}\gamma_{i+1}\ldots\gamma_{i+e}.

Theorem
1.7
focuses on constructing a subspace whose Diophantine exponents corresponding to the first angle are known in the case
d+e\leq n
. Theorem
1.8
is more general, as it provides a subspace for which we compute many exponents including in the case
d+e>n
.

2
Tools

This section is devoted to presenting various tools, some more technical than others, that will be used in computing the Diophantine exponents or constructing
(e,j)\text{-}
irrational subspaces. First we state a few results on
(e,j)
-irrationality.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with dimension
d
, and
e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
. For any
j\in\llbracket 1,\min(d,e)-g(d,e,n)\rrbracket
, we have:

\displaystyle A\text{ is }(e,j)\text{-irrational}\Longleftrightarrow A^{\perp}\text{ is }(n-e,j)\text{-irrational}

where
A^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal complement of
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

We recall that
g(d,e,n)=\max(0,d+e-n)
, and we observe that
g(n-d,n-e,n)=g(d,e,n)+n-d-e
. On the other hand,
\dim(A^{\perp}\cap B^{\perp})=n-\dim(A+B)=n-d-e+\dim(A\cap B)
for every rational subspace
B
of dimension
e
. This gives:

\displaystyle\dim(A\cap B)<j+g(d,e,n)\Longleftrightarrow\dim(A^{\perp}\cap B^{\perp})<j+g(n-d,n-e,n).

Proposition
2.1
directly follows from this equivalence since
(B^{\perp})^{\perp}=B
and
\dim(B^{\perp})=n-e
.

∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A
be a vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with dimension
d
. Then for any
j\in\llbracket 1,\min(d,n-d)\rrbracket
:

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,n-d)_{j}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\forall\,e\in\llbracket j,n-j\rrbracket,\quad A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{j}.

Proof.

Let
A
be
(n-d,j)
-irrational of dimension
d
.

•
First case:
Let
e\in\llbracket j,n-d\rrbracket
.
Let
B
be a rational subspace of dimension
e
, then there exists
C
a rational subspace of dimension
n-d
such that
B\subset C
.
We have
\dim(A\cap B)\leq\dim(A\cap C)<j+g(d,n-d,n)=j+g(d,e,n).

Thus, if
A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,n-d)_{j}
then
A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{j}
for all
e\in\llbracket j,n-d\rrbracket
.

•
Second case:
Let
e\in\llbracket n-d+1,n-j\rrbracket
. According to Proposition
2.1
,
A^{\perp}
is
(n-d,j)
-irrational.
We then use the first part of the proof and obtain
A^{\perp}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(n-d,f)_{j}
for any
f\in\llbracket j,n-(n-d)\rrbracket
. In particular, since
n-e\in\llbracket j,d\rrbracket
, we have:

A^{\perp}\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(n-d,n-e)_{j}.
By applying again Proposition
2.1
, we have
A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{j}.

∎

Now we can state a result enabling us to show that certain specific subspaces are
(e,j)
-irrational.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
1\leq d\leq n-1
. Let
M=\begin{pmatrix}G\\
\Sigma\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,d}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy:

(i)

G\in\text{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\Sigma\in\mathcal{M}_{n-d,d}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

The entries of
\Sigma
form an algebraically independent set over
\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{F})
, where
\mathcal{F}
is the set of entries of
G
.

Then, for all
e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, the subspace spanned by the columns of
M
is
(e,1)
-irrational.

Proof.

We denote by
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d}
the columns of the matrix
M
and
A=\text{Span}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})
the space spanned by these columns. We only show that
A
is
(n-d,1)
-irrational. By Proposition
2.2
, we then have that
A
is
(e,1)
-irrational for all
e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
.

Let
B
be a rational space of dimension
n-d
. Suppose by contradiction that
A\cap B\neq\{0\}
. Denoting by
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n-d}
a rational basis of
B
, we have
Y_{1}\wedge\ldots Y_{d}\wedge Z_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge Z_{n-d}=0.
In the following, we denote by
Q=(Z_{1}|\cdots|Z_{n-d})
the matrix whose columns are the
Z_{i}
. The equality
Y_{1}\wedge\ldots Y_{d}\wedge Z_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge Z_{n-d}=0
implies the nullity of the determinant of the following matrix
\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}G\\
\Sigma\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{n-d}\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\mathbb{R}).

This determinant is a polynomial with rational coefficients in the coefficients of
M
, as the
Z_{i}
are rational.
We can also view this determinant as a polynomial in
\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{F})[X_{1},\ldots,X_{d(n-d)}]
evaluated at the
d(n-d)
coefficients of
\Sigma
. Since, by
(ii)
, these coefficients form an algebraically independent family over
\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{F})
, then this polynomial is identically zero.
Thus, we can replace the coefficients of
\Sigma
by any real family, and the determinant will be zero. Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\forall\,\Sigma\in\mathcal{M}_{n-d,d}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\det\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}G\\
\Sigma\end{matrix}&\begin{matrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{n-d}\end{matrix}\end{pmatrix}=0.

(2.1)

For
\Delta
a minor of size

of

, we denote by
\text{Ind}(\Delta)
the set of indices

of the rows from which
\Delta
is extracted. We also denote by
\text{Mat}({\Delta})
the submatrix of

of size

from which
\Delta
is obtained.
We prove by strong induction on

the property:

"For any minor
\Delta
of size
n-d
of

such that
\#(\text{Ind}(\Delta)\cap\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket)=r
, we have
\Delta=0
."

\bullet

If
r=0
, then
\Delta
is the minor extracted from the last
n-d
rows of

. Taking
\Sigma=(0)
in

\displaystyle 0=\det\begin{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}G\\

\bullet

Let

Using Laplace expansion (Theorem 1.8 of

\displaystyle 0=\sum\limits_{I\in P(d,n)}(-1)^{\ell(I)+\ell(J)}\Delta_{I,J}(M)\Delta_{\bar{I},\bar{J}}(M)

We then study the minors of

\bullet

If

\bullet

Otherwise

\diamond

Proposition

is a result concerning the height of a space by "decomposing" it over the image and kernel of a particular orthogonal projection.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

to

[

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\llbracket e-t+1,e\rrbracket,\,\widetilde{X_{i}}=X_{i}-p(X_{i})\in\ker(p)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}

(2.3)

since

Finally we prove a lemma that is used throughout this article to show that an integer vector belongs to a given rational subspace.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
Y\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
B
be a rational subspace of dimension
e
. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{e}
be a basis of
B
with
X_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,e\rrbracket
. If
\|Y\wedge X_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge X_{e}\|<1
, then
Y\in B
.

Proof.

Denoting by
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
the supremum norm, we have
\|Y\wedge X_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge X_{e}\|_{\infty}\leq\|Y\wedge X_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge X_{e}\|<1.

However,
Y\wedge X_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge X_{e}
has integer coordinates since the vectors under consideration do. Therefore, its infinity norm is zero, and thus
Y\wedge X_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge X_{e}=0.

This implies that there is a linear dependency among the vectors
Y,X_{1},\ldots,X_{e}
, and since the family of
X_{i}
forms a basis of
B
, we have:

\displaystyle Y\in\text{Span}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{e})=B.

∎

\displaystyle H(B_{N,e})\geq\frac{c_{\ref{5cons_v_non_col}}}{2}\theta^{\lfloor\alpha_{N}\rfloor}.

Let

.
Then, for all

and the lower bound of the lemma is proved.

In this section we estimate the first angle between the line

Lemma 4.4
.

One has

with

independent of

.

The difficult part is bounding the angle from below. To prove this, we study the minors of size

of a specific matrix and show that at least one of them is not too small.

Proof.

Let

. Since

and

, we have
\psi_{1}(A,B_{N,e})\leq\omega(Y,X_{N+e-1})\leq c_{\ref{5cons_omega_XN_Y}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}

Using (

We express

This assumption gives, in particular,

with

independent of

e\in\llbracket 1,n-d\rrbracket
.

Define
M
as the matrix which columns are
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d}
. We can write
M=\begin{pmatrix}G\\
\Sigma\end{pmatrix}
with:

\displaystyle G=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&\cdots&1\\
0&&&0&\tau_{0,d}\\
\vdots&\cdots&0&\tau_{0,d-1}&\tau_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\reflectbox{$\ddots$}&&&\vdots\\
0&\tau_{0,2}&\cdots&\cdots&\tau_{d-2,d}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{0}&\tau_{1,2}&\cdots&\tau_{d-1,d}\\
&&\vdots&\\
\sigma_{n-d-1}&\tau_{n-d,2}&\cdots&\tau_{n-2,d}\end{pmatrix}.

We have
\Sigma\in\mathcal{M}_{n-d,d}(\mathbb{R})
and
G\in\text{GL}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
because
\det(G)=\pm\prod\limits_{i=2}^{d}\tau_{0,i}\neq 0
by expanding with respect to the first column. Moreover, by construction, the family
\{\sigma_{k}\}\cup\{\tau_{i,j}\}
is algebraically independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. In particular, we notice that the coefficients of
\Sigma
form a set algebraically independent over
\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{F})
where
\mathcal{F}
is the set of coefficients of
G
. So the space
A
spanned by the vectors
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d}
is
(n-d,1)
-irrational by Lemma
2.3
.

We now fix
e\in\llbracket 1,n-d\rrbracket
and compute
\mu_{n}(A|e)_{1}
. Using
(\ref{6exposant_Y1})
, we have
\mu_{n}(\text{Span}(Y_{1})|e)_{1}=K_{e}.

We note that
\text{Span}(Y_{1})\subset A
and thus obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(A|e)_{1}\geq\mu_{n}(\text{Span}(Y_{1})|e)_{1}=K_{e}.

(5.5)

It remains to bound from above the exponent
\mu_{n}(A|e)_{1}
by
K_{e}
, and this is the subject of the remainder of the section.

5.2
First angle

We want to show that the "best" rational subspaces approaching
A
are those that best approximate the line
\text{Span}(Y_{1})
. As in section
4
we call them
B_{N,e}
for
N\in\mathbb{N}
, namely
B_{N,e}=\text{Span}(X_{N},X_{N+1},\ldots,X_{N+e-1})
with

\displaystyle X_{N}

\displaystyle=\theta^{\lfloor\alpha_{N}\rfloor}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&\sigma_{0,N}&\cdots&\sigma_{n-d-1,N}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

where we have defined
\sigma_{j,N}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\frac{u^{j}_{k}}{\theta^{\lfloor\alpha_{k}\rfloor}}\in\frac{1}{\theta^{\lfloor\alpha_{N}\rfloor}}\mathbb{Z}
for
j\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket
. The subspace
B_{N,e}
satisfies Claim
4.2
and Lemma
4.3
. In this section, we first bound from below the angle
\psi_{1}(A,B_{N,e})
.

Lemma 5.1
.

There exists a constant
c_{22}>0
independent of
N
such that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(A,B_{N,e})\geq c_{\ref{6cons_min_angle_1}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}.

Proof.

Let
X\in B_{N,e}
and
Y\in A
be nonzero vectors such that
\omega(X,Y)=\psi_{1}(A,B_{N,e}).

According to Claim
4.2
, we have
B_{N,e}=\text{Span}(X_{N},v_{N+1},\ldots,v_{N+e-1})
where
v_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&0&\rho_{0,j}&\cdots&\rho_{n-d-1,j}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}
with
\rho_{i,j}=\dfrac{1}{u^{\phi(j)}_{j}}u^{i}_{j}\in\{0,1\}
for
i\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket
.
We then define
a,a_{1},\ldots,a_{e-1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
X=a\theta^{-\left\lfloor\alpha_{N}\right\rfloor}X_{N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}v_{N+i}
, so
X
is of the form

\displaystyle X=\begin{pmatrix}a&0&\cdots&0&a\sigma_{0,N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}\rho_{0,N+i}&\cdots&a\sigma_{n-d-1,N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}\rho_{n-d-1,N+i}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}.

Similarly, we define
b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
Y=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}Y_{j}
, so
Y
is of the form

\displaystyle Y=\begin{pmatrix}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}&b_{d}\tau_{0,d}&\cdots&\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-2,j}&b_{1}\sigma_{0}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-1,j}&\cdots&b_{1}\sigma_{n-d-1}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j+n-d-2,j}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}.

Assume, without loss of generality, that we have
a^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}^{2}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}^{2}=1.
In particular, this assumption yields

\|X\|\cdot\|Y\|\leq c_{\ref{6cons_maj_prodXY}}

with
c_{23}>0
a constant independent of
N
.

We then seek to bound
\|X\wedge Y\|
from below. Here, we use the fact that the coordinates of the vector
X\wedge Y
are the minors of size
2
of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}_{n,2}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular,
\|X\wedge Y\|
is bounded below by the absolute value of each of these minors.
We define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\sigma=\max(1,\max\limits_{i\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket}(\sigma_{i})),

\displaystyle\quad\tau=\min\limits_{j\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket}\min\limits_{i\in\llbracket 1,n-1-d+j\rrbracket}(\tau_{i,j}),

\displaystyle T=\max\limits_{j\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket}\max\limits_{i\in\llbracket 1,n-1-d+j\rrbracket}(\tau_{i,j}),

\displaystyle\quad s=\dfrac{1}{\theta^{\left\lfloor\alpha_{n-d-1}\right\rfloor}}.

We then assume
N\geq n-d-1
, thus for all
i\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket
there exists
k\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\phi(k)=i
, hence
u^{i}_{k}\in\{1,2\}
and

s\leq\sigma_{i,N}.

Finally, we define the following quantity:

\displaystyle M=\dfrac{\tau}{4(d-1)(T+\sigma)(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}}>0.

(5.6)

We will show that

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|\geq c_{\ref{6cons_minor_XwedgeY}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}

(5.7)

with
c_{24}>0
independent of
N
, by considering different cases based on the values taken by the
b_{j}
.

•
First case:
If there exists
k\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket
such that
\left|\sum\limits_{j=k}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-k,j}\right|\geq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}
, we study the minor of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\end{pmatrix}
corresponding to rows
d-k+2
and
d+1+\ell
with
\ell\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket
. We then have:

\displaystyle\forall\,\ell\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket,\quad\|X\wedge Y\|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}0&\sum\limits_{j=k}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-k,j}\\
a\sigma_{\ell,N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}\rho_{\ell,N+i}&b_{1}\sigma_{\ell}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j+\ell-1,j}\end{pmatrix}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum\limits_{j=k}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-k,j}\right|\cdot\left|a\sigma_{\ell,N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}\rho_{\ell,N+i}\right|

\displaystyle\geq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left|a\sigma_{\ell,N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}\rho_{\ell,N+i}\right|.

(5.8)

We then distinguish between two cases:
|a|\geq\dfrac{1}{2\sigma\sqrt{e}}
and
|a|<\dfrac{1}{2\sigma\sqrt{e}}
. In the first case, we choose
\ell=\phi(N+e)
. We have in particular
\rho_{\ell,N+1}=\ldots=\rho_{\ell,N+e-1}=0

and thus according to
(\ref{6minor_cas_1_bminor})
and using the fact that
s\leq\sigma_{i,N}:

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|\geq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left|a\sigma_{\ell,N}\right|\geq\dfrac{M\sigma_{\ell,N}}{2\sigma\sqrt{e}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\geq\dfrac{Ms}{2\sigma\sqrt{e}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}

which yields
(\ref{6minor_voulue_XYwedge})
.
In the second case, we have
|a|<\dfrac{1}{2\sigma\sqrt{e}}
and thus
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}^{2}=1-a^{2}\geq 1-\dfrac{1}{4\sigma^{2}e}\geq\dfrac{e-1}{e}
since
\sigma\geq 1
. In particular, there exists
i\in\llbracket 1,e-1\rrbracket
such that
|a_{i}|\geq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{e}}
. We then choose
\ell=\phi(N+i)
. In particular
\rho_{\ell,N+i}=1\text{ and }\rho_{\ell,N+1}=\ldots=\rho_{\ell,N+i-1}=\rho_{\ell,N+i+1}=\ldots=\rho_{\ell,N+e-1}=0.

According to
(\ref{6minor_cas_1_bminor})
we have:

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|

\displaystyle\geq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left|a\sigma_{\ell,N}+a_{i}\right|\geq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left(|a_{i}|-|a|\sigma\right)\geq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{e}}-\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{e}}\right)\geq\dfrac{M}{2\sqrt{e}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}.

•
Second case:

\forall\,k\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket,\quad\left|\sum\limits_{j=k}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-k,j}\right|<M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}.

We will show later (in Lemma
5.2
) that we then have:

\displaystyle\forall\,j\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket,\quad|b_{j}|\leq\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau},\quad\left|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\right|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\text{ and }|b_{1}|\geq\dfrac{3}{4}

(5.9)

because
M=\dfrac{\tau}{4(d-1)(T+\sigma)(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}}\leq\dfrac{\tau}{4(d-1)(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}
. We currently accept this result. We consider the minor of the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\end{pmatrix}
corresponding to rows
1
and
d+1+\ell
with
\ell\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket
. We then have for any
\ell\in\llbracket 0,n-d-1\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|

\displaystyle\geq\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}a&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\\
a\sigma_{\ell,N}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}\rho_{\ell,N+i}&b_{1}\sigma_{\ell}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j+\ell-1,j}\end{pmatrix}\right|.

(5.10)

We then distinguish between two cases:
|a|\geq K
and
|a|<K
with
K=\dfrac{1}{4(\sigma+(T+\sigma)(d-1))\sqrt{e}}
. In the first case, we choose
\ell=\phi(N+e)
. We have in particular
\rho_{\ell,N+e}=1\text{ et }\rho_{\ell,N+1}=\ldots=\rho_{\ell,N+e-1}=0
and
\sigma_{\ell,N}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\dfrac{u^{\ell}_{k}}{\theta^{\left\lfloor\alpha_{k}\right\rfloor}}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N+e-1}\dfrac{u^{\ell}_{k}}{\theta^{\left\lfloor\alpha_{k}\right\rfloor}}
since then
u^{\ell}_{N+1}=\ldots=u^{\ell}_{N+e-1}=0
. We deduce that

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell,N}|=\sum\limits_{k=N+e}^{+\infty}\dfrac{u^{\ell}_{k}}{\theta^{\left\lfloor\alpha_{k}\right\rfloor}}\geq\dfrac{u^{\ell}_{N+e}}{\theta^{\left\lfloor\alpha_{N+e}\right\rfloor}}\geq\dfrac{1}{\theta^{{\alpha_{N+e}}}}.

Inequality
(\ref{6minor_cas_2_bminor})
then gives:

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|

\displaystyle\geq\left|a\left(b_{1}\sigma_{\ell}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j+\ell-1,j}\right)-a\sigma_{\ell,N}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\right|\geq|a|\left|b_{1}(\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell,N})-\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}(\sigma_{\ell,N}-\tau_{j+\ell-1,j})\right|

and thus by recalling that
\sigma
and
T
bound from above respectively the
\sigma_{\ell}
and the
\tau_{\ell,j}
we have

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|

\displaystyle\geq|a|\left(|b_{1}|\dfrac{1}{\theta^{{\alpha_{N+e}}}}-(\sigma+T)\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}|b_{j}|\right)\geq|a|\left(\dfrac{3}{4\theta^{{\alpha_{N+e}}}}-(\sigma+T)(d-1)\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}}{\tau\theta^{\alpha_{N+e}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{|a|}{\theta^{\alpha_{N+e}}}\left(\dfrac{3}{4}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)\geq\dfrac{K}{2\theta^{\alpha_{N+e}}}

using
(\ref{6resultat_sur_familleb_admis})
and by definition of
M
in
(\ref{6defM})
.

In the second case, we have
|a|<K=\dfrac{1}{4(\sigma+(T+\sigma)(d-1))\sqrt{e}}
and thus:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{e-1}a_{i}^{2}=1-a^{2}\geq 1-\dfrac{1}{16(\sigma+(T+\sigma)(d-1))^{2}e}\geq\dfrac{e-1}{e}

since
\sigma\geq 1
. In particular, there exists
i\in\llbracket 1,e-1\rrbracket
such that
|a_{i}|\geq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{e}}
. We then choose
\ell=\phi(N+i)
. Specifically we have

\rho_{\ell,N+i}=1\text{ and }\rho_{\ell,N+1}=\ldots\rho_{\ell,N+i-1}=\rho_{\ell,N+i+1}=\ldots=\rho_{\ell,N+e-1}=0.

According to
(\ref{6minor_cas_2_bminor})
and
(\ref{6resultat_sur_familleb_admis})
, we have:

\displaystyle\|X\wedge Y\|

\displaystyle\geq\left|a\bigg(b_{1}\sigma_{\ell}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j+\ell-1,j}\bigg)-(a\sigma_{\ell,N}+a_{i})\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\left|a_{i}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\right|-\left|ab_{1}(\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell,N})\right|-\left|a\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}(\tau_{j+\ell-1,j}-\sigma_{\ell,N})\right|

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{|a_{i}|}{2}-|a|(\sigma+(T+\sigma)\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}|b_{j}|)\geq\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{e}}-|a|\bigg(\sigma+(T+\sigma)(d-1)\bigg)\geq\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{e}}

by bounding
\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}|b_{j}|
by
d-1
since
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}^{2}=1
.
Consequently,
\|X\wedge Y\|\geq\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}
if
N
is big enough. In all cases, we find that, for all
N\geq n-d-1
,
\|X\wedge Y\|\geq c_{\ref{6cons_minor_XwedgeY}}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}

with
c_{\ref{6cons_minor_XwedgeY}}>0
independent of
N
. By decreasing
c_{\ref{6cons_minor_XwedgeY}}
if necessary, since
\psi_{1}(A,B_{N,e})>0
due to the
(e,1)
-irrationality of
A
, we can assume this inequality holds for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Using the fact that
\|X\|\cdot\|Y\|\leq c_{\ref{6cons_maj_prodXY}}
, we therefore obtain:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(A,B_{N,e})=\omega(X,Y)=\dfrac{\|X\wedge Y\|}{\|X\|\cdot\|Y\|}\geq c_{\ref{6cons_minor_XwedgeY}}c_{\ref{6cons_maj_prodXY}}^{-1}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}.

Thus, the lemma is proven with
c_{\ref{6cons_min_angle_1}}=c_{\ref{6cons_minor_XwedgeY}}c_{\ref{6cons_maj_prodXY}}^{-1}
.

∎

We will now prove the result used in the previous proof.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}
satisfy
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}^{2}=1
. Suppose there exists
0<M\leq\dfrac{\tau}{4(d-1)(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}
such that:

\displaystyle\forall\,k\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket,\quad\left|\sum\limits_{j=k}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-k,j}\right|<M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}.

(5.11)

Then

\displaystyle|b_{1}|\geq\dfrac{3}{4},\quad\forall\,j\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket,\quad|b_{j}|\leq\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau}\text{ and }\left|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\right|\geq\dfrac{1}{2}

where
\tau
and
T
are respectively the minimum and maximum of the family
\{\tau_{i,j}\}
.

Proof.

We proceed by descending induction on
i\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket
. We prove a more refined result:

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket,\quad|b_{i}|\leq\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-i}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau}.

(5.12)

If
i=d
then hypothesis
(\ref{6lem_maj_b_hyp})
with
k=d
gives
|b_{d}|\leq\dfrac{M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau_{n-1,d}}\leq\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-d}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau}.

Let
i\in\llbracket 2,d-1\rrbracket
. Suppose that for all
i^{\prime}>i
inequality
(\ref{6lem_maj_b_hyp_rec})
holds. Then we apply hypothesis
(\ref{6lem_maj_b_hyp})
with
k=i
and have
\left|\sum\limits_{j=i}^{d}b_{j}\tau_{j-i,j}\right|<M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}.

In particular, we have
|b_{i}\tau_{j-i,i}|\leq M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}+\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{d}\left|b_{j}\tau_{j-i,j}\right|
, and using the induction hypothesis:

\displaystyle|b_{i}|\leq\dfrac{1}{\tau_{j-i,i}}\left(M\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}+\sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{d}\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-j}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau}T\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{M}{\tau}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left(1+\dfrac{T}{\tau}\left(\dfrac{1-(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-i}}{-\frac{T}{\tau}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{M}{\tau}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}\left(1+\frac{T}{\tau}\right)^{d-i}

which proves
(\ref{6lem_maj_b_hyp_rec})
for all
i\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket
and thus the second inequality of the lemma. In particular, we have:

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\llbracket 2,d\rrbracket,\quad|b_{i}|\leq\dfrac{M(1+\frac{T}{\tau})^{d-2}\theta^{-\alpha_{N+e}}}{\tau}\leq\dfrac{1}{4(d-1)}.

We use the fact that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}^{2}=1
to obtain
|b_{1}|^{2}=1-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{d}b_{i}^{2}\geq 1-(d-1)\dfrac{1}{16(d-1)^{2}}\geq\dfrac{3}{4}
,
and moreover

\left|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}\right|\geq|b_{1}|-\left|\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}b_{j}\right|\geq\dfrac{3}{4}-(d-1)\dfrac{1}{4(d-1)}=\dfrac{1}{2}.

∎

5.3
Rational subspaces of best approximation and conclusion

We show here that the subspaces

are the ones that achieve the best approximations of
A
in the first angle. Recall that the integer

is fixed.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
B
be a rational subspace of dimension

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(A,B)\leq H(B)^{-K_{e}-\varepsilon}.

Then if

Let

Claim 5.4

Then, if

is large enough depending on

and

, we have:

\displaystyle\forall\,\delta\in\left(0,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right),\quad\|Y_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge Y_{d}\wedge Z_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge Z_{e}\|

\displaystyle\leq c_{\ref{6cons_major_Y_Z}}\psi_{1}(A,B)H(B)^{1+\lambda_{e}+\delta}

\displaystyle\omega(\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j},p_{B}(Y))-\omega(Y,p_{B}(Y))\leq\omega(Y,\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j})

coming from the triangle inequality (see equation (3) of

]

\displaystyle\omega(Y,\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j})=\dfrac{\left\|\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j}\wedge\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j}\right\|}{\left\|\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j}\right\|}=\dfrac{\left\|a_{1}Y_{1}\wedge\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j}\right\|}{\left\|\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j}\right\|}\leq\left\|a_{1}Y_{1}\right\|.

\omega(\sum\limits_{j=2}^{d}a_{j}Y_{j},p_{B}(Y))\geq\psi_{1}(A^{\prime},B)\geq c_{\ref{6cons_Aprime}}H(B)^{-\mu_{n}(A^{\prime}|e)_{1}-\delta}=c_{\ref{6cons_Aprime}}H(B)^{-\lambda_{e}-\delta}

and
\delta
. Revisiting
(\ref{6relation_a_1_X})
and

\displaystyle c_{\ref{6cons_Aprime}}H(B)^{-\lambda_{e}-\delta}-\psi_{1}(A,B)\leq|a_{1}|\|Y_{1}\|.

Recalling that

and by assumption

is large enough, leading to

U
a subset of
V_{d,n}=\{(e,k)\mid e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket,k\in\llbracket 1+g(d,e,n),\min(d,e)\rrbracket\}.
In this section, we always work with
d
and
n
fixed. The functions under consideration, denoted by
\mu_{n}(\cdot|e)_{k-g(d,e,n)}
, are thus defined on
\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{k-g(d,e,n)}
, the set of
(e,k-g(d,e,n))
-irrational subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
d
. For
U\subset V_{d,n}
, we define the set
\mathcal{I}_{U}=\bigcap\limits_{(e,k)\in U}\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{k-g(d,e,n)}
and the mapping

\displaystyle M_{U}:\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{I}_{U}&\longrightarrow&(\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\})^{U}\\
A&\longmapsto&(\mu_{n}(A|e)_{k-g(d,e,n)})_{(e,k)\in U}\end{array}\right..

If the image of
M_{U}
contains a non-empty open set in
\mathbb{R}^{U}
, then the family
(\mu_{n}(\cdot|e)_{k-g(d,e,n)})_{(e,k)\in U}
is smoothly independent on
\mathcal{I}_{U}
. Indeed, otherwise, there would exist a submersion
\mathbb{R}^{U}\to\mathbb{R}
that vanishes on these functions, and the image of
M_{U}
would be contained in an differential hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{U}
that has an empty interior. Subsequently, the proofs focus on showin that the images of the considered functions contain a non-empty open set.

Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
present examples of subfamilies
U
of
V_{d,n}
for which
(\mu_{n}(\cdot|e)_{k-g(d,e,n)})_{(e,k)\in U}
is smoothly independent on
\mathcal{I}_{U}
. It is worth noting that, with Theorem
1.8
, we can also establish smooth independence for other families. For instance, such a result holds for
U=\{(e,k)\mid k\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,e\in\llbracket k,km\rrbracket,k|e\}
, as shown in
[
5
]
, chapter 8, section 8.4.

7.1
Sufficient condition

First we develop two technical lemmas that provide sufficient conditions on
U\subset V_{d,n}
for
M_{U}(\mathcal{I}_{U})
to contain a non-empty open set and therefore for the associated family of exponents to be smoothly independent on
\mathcal{I}_{U}
. We write
n=d(m+1)
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
U\subset V_{d,n}
with
\#U\leq dm
satisfying for all
(e,k)\in U
,
e<k(m+1).

For
(e,k)\in U
and
\beta=(\beta_{i,\ell})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
, we define

\displaystyle\Omega_{(e,k)}(\beta)=\sum\limits_{q=1+f(e,mk)}^{k}\beta_{q+d-k,1}\ldots\beta_{q+d-k,v_{q}(e,k)}=\sum\limits_{q=1+f(e,mk)+d-k}^{d}\beta_{q,1}\ldots\beta_{q,v_{q+k-d}(e,k)},

which defines a function
\Omega:(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}\to\mathbb{R}^{U}
,
\beta\mapsto(\Omega_{(e,k)}(\beta))_{(e,k)\in U}
. Suppose that the Jacobian matrix of
\Omega
at
\beta
,
J_{\Omega}(\beta)\in\mathcal{M}_{\#U,dm}(\mathbb{R})
, has rank
\#U
for all
\beta\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
. Then the image of the function
M_{U}
contains a non-empty open set in
\mathbb{R}^{U}
.

Proof.

Let us define the following function

\displaystyle\Omega^{\prime}:\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{U}\\
(\beta_{i,\ell})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}&\mapsto&\left(\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{q=1+f(e,mk)}^{k}(\beta_{q+d-k,1}\ldots\beta_{q+d-k,v_{q}(e,k)})^{-1}}\right)_{(e,k)\in U}\end{array}\right..

We will apply Theorem
1.8
to construct subspaces
A
of dimension
d
whose family of exponents
(\mu_{n}(A|e)_{k})_{(e,k)\in U}
range through an open subset of the image of
\Omega^{\prime}
. For this, we define
H^{\prime}\subset(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
as the set of
(\beta_{i,\ell})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}
satisfying the hypotheses

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\quad\beta_{i,1}>\ldots>\beta_{i,m},

(7.1)

\min\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{1,\ell})>{(3d)^{\frac{c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2}}}{c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2}}-1}}}
,
\min\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{1,\ell})^{c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2c1}}}>\max\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{1,\ell})^{c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2}}}
and for all
i\in\llbracket 1,d-1\rrbracket
:

\min\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{i,\ell})^{c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2c1}}}>\max\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{i+1,\ell})
and
\min\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{i+1,\ell})>\max\limits_{\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}(\beta_{i,\ell})^{c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2}}}

with
c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2c1}},c_{\ref{7cons_petite_hyp_theoc2}}
the constants defined in Theorem
1.8
. The set
H^{\prime}
thus defined is a non-empty open set in
(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
.

For
\beta\in H^{\prime}
, Theorem
1.8
yields a space
A
of dimension
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for all
e\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
and
k\in\llbracket 1+g(d,e,n),\min(d,e)\rrbracket
satisfying
e<k(m+1)
, we have

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(d,e)_{k-g(d,e,n)}\text{ and }\mu_{n}(A|e)_{k-g(d,e,n)}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\limits_{q=1+f(e,mk)}^{k}\frac{1}{K_{q+d-k,v_{q}(e,k)}}\right)^{-1}

where, due to hypothesis (
7.1
):
\forall\,i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\forall\,v\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket,\quad K_{i,v}=\beta_{i,1}\ldots\beta_{i,v}.

This result then gives
\Omega^{\prime}(H^{\prime})\subset M_{U}(\mathcal{I}_{U})
. To prove Lemma
7.1
, we will show that
\Omega^{\prime}(H^{\prime})
contains a non-empty open set. By composing
\Omega^{\prime}
on both ends with the application that inverts each coordinate, it suffices to show that the smooth function
\Omega
, defined on
H=\{(\beta_{i,\ell})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket},(\frac{1}{\beta_{i,\ell}})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\in H^{\prime}\}
by

\displaystyle\Omega:\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}H&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{U}\\
(\beta_{i,\ell})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}&\mapsto&\left(\sum\limits_{q=1+f(e,mk)}^{k}\beta_{q+d-k,1}\ldots\beta_{q+d-k,v_{q}(e,k)}\right)_{(e,k)\in U}\end{array}\right.

has a non-empty open set in its image. Since by assumption
J_{\Omega}(\beta)
has rank
\#U
for all
\beta\in H\subset(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
, the open mapping theorem ensures that
\Omega(H)
contains a non-empty open set, which concludes the proof of the lemma.

∎

We set the notation
P(\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket)
for the set of subsets of
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
, and we define

\displaystyle\chi:\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}V_{d,n}&\longrightarrow&P(\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket)\\
(e,k)&\longmapsto&(\llbracket 1+f+d-k,u+d-k\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v+1\rrbracket)\cup(\llbracket u+1+d-k,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v\rrbracket)\end{array}\right.

where
v
and
u
are respectively the quotient and remainder of the Euclidean division of
e
by
k
, and
f=f(e,mk)=\max(0,e-mk)
. Furthermore, we recall a definition made in section
6
: we associate to
(e,k)\in V_{d,n}
the quantities
v_{1}(e,k),\ldots,v_{k}(e,k)
defined by:

\displaystyle v_{q}(e,k)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}v+1&\text{ if }q\in\llbracket 1,u\rrbracket\\
v&\text{ if }q\in\llbracket u+1,k\rrbracket\end{array}\right..

A pair
(q,\ell)\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
belongs to
\chi(e,k)
if and only if we have

\displaystyle q\geq 1+f+d-k\text{ and }\ell\leq v_{q+k-d}(e,k).

(7.4)

We state Lemma
7.2
which is a consequence of the Lemma
7.1
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
U\subset V_{d,n}
with
\#U\leq dm
satisfying for all
(e,k)\in U
,
e<k(m+1)
.
Suppose furthermore that there exists an order
<_{U}
on
U
such that

\displaystyle\forall\,(e,k)\in U,\quad\chi(e,k)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})<_{U}(e,k)}\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})\neq\emptyset.

(7.5)

Then the image of the function
M_{U}
contains a non-empty open set in
\mathbb{R}^{U}
.

Proof.

Each
\Omega_{(e,k)}
, defined in the assumptions of Lemma
7.1
, is a polynomial in the variables
\beta_{i,\ell}
. We note using (
7.4
) that
\chi(e,k)
is in fact the set of indices of the
\beta_{i,\ell}
"appearing" in
\Omega_{(e,k)}(\beta)
. This implies in particular that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega_{(e,k)}}{\partial\beta_{q,\ell}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\prod\limits_{\underset{p\neq\ell}{1\leq p\leq v_{q+k-d}(e,k)}}\beta_{q,p}&\text{ if }(q,\ell)\in\chi(e,k)\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right..

According to hypothesis (
7.5
), we can order
U=\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{\#U}\}
in such a way that these quantities respect the order
<_{U}
:

\forall\,i<j,\quad\theta_{i}<_{U}\theta_{j}

and so that, for every
j\in\llbracket 2,\#U\rrbracket
, there exists
(q_{j},\ell_{j})\in\chi(\theta_{j})\setminus\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{j-1}\chi(\theta_{i})
.
In particular, using (
7.1
), for any
\beta\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}

\displaystyle\forall\,i<j,\quad\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{i}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{j},\ell_{j}}}=0\text{ and }\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{j}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{j},\ell_{j}}}=\prod\limits_{\underset{p\neq\ell_{j}}{1\leq p\leq v_{q_{j}+k-d}(\theta_{j})}}\beta_{q_{j},p}\neq 0.

(7.8)

Let
J_{\Omega}(\beta)=\left(\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{i}}}{\partial\beta_{j,\ell}}\right)_{i\in\llbracket 1,\#U\rrbracket,(j,\ell)\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\in\mathcal{M}_{\#U,dm}(\mathbb{R})
be the Jacobian matrix of
\Omega
at
\beta\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
. Here the index
i
corresponds to the rows of the matrix and
(j,\ell)
to the columns, ordering
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
according to the usual lexicographic order. We will show that
J_{\Omega}(\beta)
has maximal rank
\#U
, and then apply Lemma
7.1
.
We extract from
J_{\Omega}(\beta)
(by possibly interchanging columns) the matrix

\displaystyle G_{U}=\left(\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{i}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{j},\ell_{j}}}\right)_{i\in\llbracket 1,\#U\rrbracket,j\in\llbracket 1,\#U\rrbracket}\in\mathcal{M}_{\#U}(\mathbb{R}).

According to (
7.8
), we have

\displaystyle G_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{1}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{1},\ell_{1}}}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{2}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{1},\ell_{1}}}&\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{2}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{2},\ell_{2}}}&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{\#U-1}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{1},\ell_{1}}}&\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{\#U-1}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{2},\ell_{2}}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{\#U-1}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{\#U-1},\ell_{\#U-1}}}&0\\
\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{\#U}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{1},\ell_{1}}}&\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{\#U}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{2},\ell_{2}}}&&\cdots&\frac{\partial\Omega_{\theta_{\#U}}}{\partial\beta_{q_{\#U},\ell_{\#U}}}\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix
G_{U}
is thus lower triangular, and its diagonal coefficients are non-zero according to (
7.8
), thus it is invertible.
Therefore, we have shown that
J_{\Omega}(\beta)
has rank
\#U
for all
\beta\in H
. Lemma
7.1
then implies that the image of the function
M_{U}
contains a non-empty open set in
\mathbb{R}^{U}
, which concludes the proof of Lemma
7.2
.

∎

7.2
Results for the Last Angle

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
using the results from the previous section. We write
n=d(m+1)
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We set
U=\{(e,\min(d,e))\mid e\in\llbracket 1,n-d\rrbracket\}.

We will show that
U
satisfies the hypothesis (
7.5
) of Lemma
7.2
. First, note that for all
(e,k)\in U
, we have
g(d,e,n)=0
and
f=f(e,mk)=0
because
e\in\llbracket 1,dm\rrbracket
and
dm=n-d
. We define the following order on
U
:

\displaystyle(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})<_{U}(e,k)\Longleftrightarrow e^{\prime}<e.

This indeed defines an order since the first coordinates of the elements of
U
are pairwise distinct. Let
(e,k)\in U
. If
e\leq d
then
k=e
and

\chi(e,k)=\llbracket 1+d-e,d\rrbracket\times\{1\}.

For any
(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})<_{U}(e,k)
, we have
\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})=\llbracket 1+d-e^{\prime},d\rrbracket\times\{1\}
and thus
(1+d-e,1)\in\chi(e,k)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})<_{U}(e,k)}\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
.

If
e>d
, then
k=d
and we write
e=dv+u
for the Euclidean division of
e
by
d
. We have

\chi(e,k)=\llbracket 1,u\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v+1\rrbracket\cup\llbracket u+1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v\rrbracket.

For
(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})<_{U}(e,k)
, we write
e^{\prime}=k^{\prime}v^{\prime}+u^{\prime}
for the Euclidean division of
e^{\prime}
by
k^{\prime}
. We distinguish vetween two cases based on whether
u=0
or
u\neq 0
.

•
If
u=0
.
We will show that
(d,v)\notin\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
.
Since
e>d
and
u=0
, we necessarily have
v>1
. If
k^{\prime}=e^{\prime}
then
v^{\prime}=1
and
u^{\prime}=0
, so
\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})=\llbracket 1+d-e^{\prime},d\rrbracket\times\{1\}
and
(d,v)\notin\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
because
v>1
.

Otherwise
k^{\prime}=d
and then
1\leq v^{\prime}<v
since
e^{\prime}<e
. We have

\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})=\llbracket 1,u^{\prime}\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v^{\prime}+1\rrbracket\cup\llbracket u^{\prime}+1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
Then
(d,v)\notin\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
because
u^{\prime}\leq d-1
and
v^{\prime}<v
.

•
If
u>0
.
We will show that
(u,v+1)\notin\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. If
k^{\prime}=e^{\prime}
then
v^{\prime}=1
,
u^{\prime}=0
, and
\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})=\llbracket 1+d-e^{\prime},d\rrbracket\times\{1\}
. Since
v+1>1
, we have
(u,v+1)\notin\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
in this case.

Otherwise
k^{\prime}=d
and
v^{\prime}\leq v
because
e^{\prime}<e
. We have

\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})=\llbracket 1,u^{\prime}\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v^{\prime}+1\rrbracket\cup\llbracket u^{\prime}+1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,v^{\prime}\rrbracket
.
Therefore,

\left((u,v+1)\in\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow[v^{\prime}=v\text{ and }u^{\prime}\geq u]\right).

Since
e^{\prime}<e
, this property is not satisfied and thus
(u,v+1)\notin\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})
.

We have shown that for all
(e,k)\in U
, there exists
(q,\ell)
such that
(q,\ell)\in\chi(e,k)\setminus\bigcup\limits_{(e^{\prime},k^{\prime})<_{U}(e,k)}\chi(e^{\prime},k^{\prime}).

Thus, the hypotheses of Lemma
7.2
are satisfied:
M_{U}(\mathcal{I}_{U})
contains a non-empty open set, hence the functions
(\mu_{n}(\cdot|e)_{k})_{(e,k)\in U}
are smoothly independent on
\mathcal{I}_{U}
.

∎

Lemma
7.2
is not enough to show Theorem
1.6
. We will use Lemma
7.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We define
U=\llbracket d,n-1\rrbracket\times\{d\}.

We will show the invertibility for all
\beta\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*})^{dm}
of the Jacobian matrix
J_{\Omega}(\beta)\in\mathcal{M}_{dm,dm}(\mathbb{R})
where

\Omega_{(e,d)}(\beta)=\sum\limits_{q=1+f(e,md)}^{d}\beta_{q,1}\ldots\beta_{q,v_{q}(e,d)}
for
(e,d)\in U
with
v_{1}(e,d),\ldots,v_{d}(e,d)
defined in
(\ref{9eq_def_vj})
and
f(e,md)=\max(0,e-md)
. We then have
J_{\Omega}(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1}&\cdots&D_{d}\end{pmatrix}

with for
i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket
:

\displaystyle D_{i}=\left(\dfrac{\partial\Omega_{(e,d)}}{\partial\beta_{i,\ell}}\right)_{e\in\llbracket d,n-1\rrbracket,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}\in\mathcal{M}_{dm,m}(\mathbb{R}).

Given the form of
\Omega_{(e,d)}
, for
(i,\ell)\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
one has

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\Omega_{(e,d)}}{\partial\beta_{i,\ell}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}0\prod\limits_{\underset{p\neq\ell}{1\leq p\leq v_{i}(e,d)}}\beta_{i,p}&\text{ if }\ell\leq v_{i}(e,d)\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{array}\right..

In particular, for
\ell\in\llbracket 1,v_{i}(e,d)-1\rrbracket
we have

\dfrac{\beta_{i,\ell+1}}{\beta_{i,\ell}}\dfrac{\partial\Omega_{(e,d)}}{\partial\beta_{i,\ell+1}}=\dfrac{\partial\Omega_{(e,d)}}{\partial\beta_{i,\ell}}.

Let
D_{i,1},\ldots,D_{i,m}
be the columns of
D_{i}
. We successively perform the following elementary operations between the columns of
J_{\Omega}(\beta)
:

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\quad\forall\,\ell\in\llbracket 1,m-1\rrbracket,\quad D_{i,\ell}\leftarrow\left(D_{i,\ell}-\dfrac{\beta_{i,\ell+1}}{\beta_{i,\ell}}D_{i,\ell+1}\right),

and denote by
G
the resulting matrix. Thus,
\det(J_{\Omega}(\beta))=\det(G)
where
G=\left(g_{e,(i,\ell)}\right)_{e\in\llbracket d,n-1\rrbracket,(i,\ell)\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket}
and

\displaystyle g_{e,(i,\ell)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\neq 0&\text{ if }v_{i}(e,d)=\ell\\
=0&\text{ otherwise}\end{array}\right..

We then study
G
and for that, we reorder the columns of
G
, setting the following order on
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
:

\left((i,\ell)<(j,\ell^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow[\ell<\ell^{\prime}\text{ or }(\ell=\ell^{\prime}\text{ and }i<j)]\right)

which is the lexicographic order on
\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\times\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
with priority comparison of the second element of the pair. We denote by
G^{\prime}
the resulting matrix and we have
\det(G)=\pm\det(G^{\prime})
. With this chosen order for the columns, the diagonal coefficients of
G^{\prime}
are those of the form
g_{e,(u+1,v)}
with
e=dv+u
the Euclidean division of
e
by
d
for
e\in\llbracket d,n-1\rrbracket
.
We will show that the matrix
G^{\prime}
is upper triangular with non-zero diagonal coefficients.

Let
e\in\llbracket d,n-1\rrbracket
. We set
e=dv+u
the Euclidean division of
e
by
d
. According to the definition of
v_{i}(e,d)
in
(\ref{9eq_def_vj})
we have
v_{u+1}(e,d)=v
. The construction of
G
in
(\ref{9def_matrice_G})
then gives

g_{e,(u+1,v)}\neq 0
. Now consider
e^{\prime}>e
. We will show that
g_{e^{\prime},(u+1,v)}=0
. We set
e^{\prime}=dv^{\prime}+u^{\prime}
the Euclidean division of
e^{\prime}
by
d
and we then have
v^{\prime}\geq v
.

•
If
v^{\prime}>v
then since
v_{u+1}(e^{\prime},d)=v^{\prime}\text{ or }v^{\prime}+1
we have
v_{u+1}(e^{\prime},d)\neq v
and thus

g_{e^{\prime},(u+1,v)}=0

according to
(\ref{9def_matrice_G})
.

•
If
v^{\prime}=v
then
u^{\prime}>u
and
u+1\in\llbracket 1,u^{\prime}\rrbracket
. In particular, according to the definition of
v_{i}(e^{\prime},d)
in
(\ref{9eq_def_vj})
, we have
v_{u+1}(e^{\prime},d)=v^{\prime}+1\neq v
and thus

g_{e^{\prime},(u+1,v)}=0

according to
(\ref{9def_matrice_G})
.

We have thus shown that all diagonal coefficients of
G^{\prime}
are non-zero and that
G^{\prime}
is upper triangular.
It follows that
G
is invertible since
\det(G)=\pm\det(G^{\prime})
and thus
J_{\Omega}(\beta)
is also invertible. Lemma
7.1
then allows us to conclude that
M_{U}(\mathcal{I}_{U})
contains a non-empty open set and hence the functions
(\mu_{n}(\cdot|e)_{d})_{e\in\llbracket dm,d(m+1)-1\rrbracket}
are smoothly independent on
\mathcal{I}_{U}
.

∎
