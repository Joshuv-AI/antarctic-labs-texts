Finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated associative and Hopf algebras
O. Yu. Aristov

Abstract
. We introduce finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated algebras, which can be treated as ‘algebras of functions’ on non-commutative C ∞ C^{\infty} -differentiable spaces. Our approach uses the category of projective limits of real Banach algebras of polynomial growth. We prove the existence of some universal constructions in this and some similar categories. By analogy with holomorphically finitely generated algebras of Pirkovskii, a finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated algebra is defined as a quotient of a finite-rank algebra of ‘free C ∞ C^{\infty} -functions’. The latter notion was introduced by the author in a previous article, where a structure theorem for algebras of ‘free C ∞ C^{\infty} -functions’ was announced and proved in dimension at most 2 2 . Here this theorem is proved in full generality. The central result asserts that the projective tensor product of a finite tuple of finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated algebras is finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated. In particular, this makes it natural to consider finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated topological Hopf algebras. Furthermore, a construction called ‘envelope’ provides a functor from the category of affine real Hopf algebras to the category of finitely C ∞ C^{\infty} -generated Hopf algebras.



Introduction

We consider (in general, non-commutative) algebras that can be treated as the algebras of ’functions of class
C^{\infty}
’ on non-commutative spaces and generalize not only algebras of
C^{\infty}
functions on manifolds but also
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebras. Algebras of free
C^{\infty}
-functions (aka
C^{\infty}
-tensor algebras) were introduced by the author as envelopes of free algebras with respect to the class of Banach algebras of polynomial growth
[
10
]
. Here we discuss
finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras
, i.e., quotients of algebras of free
C^{\infty}
-functions of finite rank. It is shown in
[
10
]
that every
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra (i.e., a projective limit of real Banach algebras of polynomial growth) is a quotient of a
C^{\infty}
-tensor algebra but the additional imposition of the finiteness condition makes the class of quotients best behaved in many ways, e.g., it is well compatible with the projective tensor product. For example, it is not clear whether it makes sense to consider topological Hopf algebras with general underlying
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra as a separate class, but in §
6
we show that this is justified for finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras. Moreover, in the affine case the envelope inherits the Hopf algebra structure. In particular, this holds for finitely generated universal enveloping algebras and the quantum
SL(2)
.

Our approach is similar to that in non-commutative generalization of algebras of holomorphic functions given by Pirkovskii who introduced
holomorphically finitely generated algebras
— non-commutative generalizations of Stein algebras — as quotients of finitely generated algebras of free holomorphic functions
[
46
,
47
]
. Thus we follow the same route but in the case of
C^{\infty}
functions.

Finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras are a non-commutative generalization of
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebras (Proposition
4.3
). Recall that the spectrum of a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra is an affine
C^{\infty}
-differentiable space in the sense of Spallek whose theory is one of possible extensions of differential geometry to spaces possibly admitting singularities
[
42
]
. Thereby
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras can be treated as an object of study in non-commutative geometry. However, it is not yet clear how they relate to NC differential geometry à la Connes but there is at least one point in common: both theories use
C^{\infty}
functional calculus.

Since the envelopes of finitely generated commutative real algebras are
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebras, which have a geometric interpretation as mentioned above, we expect the envelope functor to be an avatar of a geometric functor similar to the analytization. But the corresponding theory in the
C^{\infty}
-case is still not well developed. Only a few hints can be found in the unpublished notes
[
26
, § 3.10]
and the preprint
[
27
, § 2.6]
. Surely this issue deserves a separate study.

Algebras of polynomial growth

Operators (and elements of Banach algebras) of polynomial growth is a classical topic
[
20
,
38
]
. Banach algebras of polynomial growth (i.e., such that all the elements are of polynomial growth) are introduced in
[
5
]
. Note that this assumption is quite restrictive, e.g., the ground field must be
\mathbb{R}
. It is also proved in
[
5
, Theorem 2.8 and Proposition 2.9]
that every Banach algebra of polynomial growth is commutative modulo Jacobson radical and the radical is nilpotent. Nevertheless, as the author shows in
[
5
,
6
,
10
]
, the corresponding theory is rich enough to become an interesting field of research.

The next step is to move on to algebras that can be approximated by Banach algebras of polynomial growth (
\mathsf{PGL}
algebras). The prototypical example of a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra is
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The most important feature of a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra is that every its element admits a
C^{\infty}
-functional calculus; see
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Envelopes

The most direct way to obtain a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra is to apply an enveloping functor.
The notion of an
envelope with respect to a given class of Banach algebras
was introduced in
[
10
]
but, in an implicit form, it appeared earlier in
[
5
]
for the class of Banach algebras of polynomial growth. Our study is based on envelopes with respect to this class. It is also noted in
[
7
]
and
[
10
]
that there are other interesting classes. Here is an extended list:

— all Banach algebras;

— Banach algebras of polynomial growth (notation is
\mathsf{PG}
);

— strictly real Banach algebras in the sense of
[
32
]
;

— Banach algebras satisfying a polynomial identity
[
7
]
;

— Dedekind-finite Banach algebras (see Remark
1.7
);

— operator algebras (possibly non-selfadjoint closed subalgebras of the algebra of bounded operators in a Hilbert space).

(Note that the envelope with respect to the class of operator algebras is not the same as the enveloping operator algebra of a Banach algebra as described, e.g., in
[
14
, 2.4.6]
).

If a class
\mathsf{C}
of Banach algebras satisfies some natural conditions, the corresponding category
\mathsf{CL}
of projective limits admits a tensor algebra construction; see
[
10
, § 1]
. Here we also consider other universal constructions in
\mathsf{CL}
: symmetric algebras, coproducts, colimits and tensor products. In particular, we may put
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{PG}
.

Finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras

In
[
10
]
, algebras of free
C^{\infty}
-functions are introduced and a structure theorem in the case of finite number (say,
k
) of generators is announced for general

and proved when

. The proof in the case of general finite

-generated algebras, which form a special subclass of

We give some general theory of finitely

-generated algebras in §

. Here we also consider a new example, a

-version of a deformation of the two-dimensional non-abelian real Lie algebra

\mathbb{C}
or

) is a monoidal category with respect to the bifunctor

of complete projective tensor product. A Hopf algebra in this category is called a

\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra
. This notion is well defined for the same reason as in the algebraic case: the complete projective tensor product of two associative
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebras is again an associative
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra. Various aspects of the theory of Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebras are discussed in
[
17
,
19
,
39
,
44
,
48
,
56
]
and also in the author’s papers
[

,
7
,
8
,
9
,
11
]
.

If we restrict ourselves to the subclass consisting of Arens–Michael algebras (projective limits of Banach algebras) and want to endow some Arens–Michael algebra
H
with a structure of topological Hopf algebra (in a certain sense), then it is natural to assume that the codomain of the comultiplication is the Arens–Michael tensor product
H\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{AM}}\!}H
. Since this algebra is isomorphic to
H\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}H
(see Remark
3.15
(B)), there is no need to introduce a new concept and go beyond Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebras. This implies that the Arens–Michael enveloping functor preserves Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra structure; see
[
44
, Proposition 6.7]
. Thus, it seems natural to study the subcategory of Arens–Michael Hopf algebras.

If we further narrow the class of Arens–Michael algebras and consider an HFG (stands for ‘holomorphically finitely generated’) algebra
H
in the sense of Pirkovskii
[
46
,
47
]
as underlying for a Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra, then we can use the fact that the codomain of the comultiplication,
H\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}H
is also HFG. So we get another natural subclass, HFG Hopf algebras. They are studied in detail in
[
9
]
.

Considering
\mathsf{PGL}
algebras instead of Arens–Michael and HFG algebras, we see that the situation is more subtle. The author does not know whether the enveloping functor with respect to
\mathsf{PG}
preserves Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra structure. This is because that we are only able to prove stability under
(-)\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}(-)
for the possibly smaller class
\mathsf{QDTS}
but not for the whole
\mathsf{PGL}
; see Remark
2.23
and Theorem
2.19
. Of course, one can formally define
\mathsf{PGL}
Hopf algebras, but at the moment this definition does not seem natural. On the other hand, Theorem
4.10
asserts that the smaller class of finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras is nevertheless stable under
(-)\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}(-)
. Thus it is taken for granted to introduce a notion parallel to that of HFG Hopf algebra. We say that a real Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated as a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra is a
C^{\infty}
-finitely generated Hopf algebra
. We discuss this topic in §
6
.

1.
Envelopes

In this section we discuss envelopes, a concept that formalizes the procedure of approximating a topological algebra by Banach algebras of a certain class.

Note that we consider topological algebras with separately continuous multiplication. Also, all algebras and homomorphisms are assumed to be unital.
The category of topological algebras is denoted by
\mathsf{TA}
.

Definition 1.1
.

[
10
, Definition 1.1]

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a class of Banach algebras (over
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
). We denote by
\mathsf{CL}
the class of topological algebras isomorphic to a projective limit of algebras contained in
\mathsf{C}
. We also use notation
\mathsf{CL}
for the corresponding full subcategory of
\mathsf{TA}
. If
A\in\mathsf{CL}
we say that
A
is
locally in
\mathsf{C}
or a
\mathsf{CL}
algebra
.

Definition 1.2
.

[
10
, Definition 1.2]

(A) Let
\mathsf{C}
be a class of Banach algebras,
\mathsf{CL}
defined as in Definition
1.1
and
F\!:\mathsf{CL}\to\mathsf{TA}
the corresponding forgetful functor. If
F
admits a left adjoint functor
L
, we call
L
the
enveloping functor with respect to
\mathsf{C}
.

(B) Denote by
\iota_{A}
the component of the identity adjunction (the functor morphism
\mathop{\mathrm{id}}\nolimits_{\mathsf{TA}}\Rightarrow F\circ L
) corresponding to a topological algebra
A
and put
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}\!:=(F\circ L)(A)
. We say that the pair
(\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}},\iota_{A})
is the
envelope of
A
with respect to
\mathsf{C}
.

In the case when
\mathsf{C}
is the class of all Banach algebras we get the standard definition of the Arens–Michael envelope; see
[
29
]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

[
10
, Proposition 1.4]

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a class of Banach algebras stable under passing to finite products and closed subalgebras. Then the enveloping functor with respect to
\mathsf{C}
exists.

For efficient computation of envelopes, an additional stability condition is useful.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a class of Banach algebras stable under passing to closed subalgebras and quotients (over closed ideals). If an algebra is locally in
\mathsf{C}
, then so is the completion of each of its quotient over a closed ideal.

Note that in this text ‘ideal’ stands for ’two-sided ideal’.

For the proof of Proposition
1.4
we need a lemma. Recall first that the quotient of a complete locally convex space over a closed subspace is complete only under extra assumptions, e.g., in the metrizable case. The completion of a topological algebra
A
is denoted by
\widetilde{A}
or
A^{~}
.

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
A
be a projective limit of a system
(A_{\nu})
of Banach algebras,
(\tau_{\nu}\!:A\to A_{\nu})
the corresponding projective cone, and
I
a closed ideal in
A
. If the range of
\tau_{\nu}
is dense in
A_{\nu}
for every
\nu
, then
(A/I)^{~}
is the projective limit of the naturally defined directed system
(A_{\nu}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}\,)
of Banach algebras.

Proof.

The density implies that, for every
\nu
,
\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}
is a closed ideal in
A_{\nu}
and so
A_{\nu}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}
is a Banach algebra. When
\nu^{\prime}\succeq\nu
, the linking homomorphism maps
\overline{\tau_{\nu}^{\prime}(I)}
into
\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}
and thus there is a continuous homomorphism
(A_{\nu^{\prime}}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu^{\prime}}(I)}\,)\to(A_{\nu}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}\,)
. It is clear that we obtain a projective system
(A_{\nu}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)})
of Banach algebras.

The density also implies that the projective limit provides an Arens–Michael decomposition of
A
. Then we can use
[
45
, Lemma 7.1]
, which immediately implies that
(A/I)^{~}
is a projective limit of
(A_{\nu}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}\,)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

Let
A
be a projective limit of a system
(A_{\nu})
such that
A_{\nu}\in\mathsf{C}
for every
\nu
and
I
a closed ideal in
A
. Since
\mathsf{C}
is stable under passing to closed subalgebras, we can assume that every homomorphism
A\to A_{\nu}
in the corresponding cone has dense range. By Lemma
1.5
, the completion of
A/I
is the projective limit of the system
(A_{\nu}/\,\overline{\tau_{\nu}(I)}\,)
. Since
\mathsf{C}
is stable under passing to quotients, all the algebras in this system are in
\mathsf{C}
. Thus
(A/I)^{~}
is locally in
\mathsf{C}
.
∎

In view of Propositions
1.3
and
1.4
, the following result is deduced in exactly the same way as in
[
44
, Proposition 6.1]
and so we omit the proof.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a class of Banach algebras stable under passing to finite products, closed subalgebras and quotients. Suppose that
A
is a topological algebra and
I
is a (possibly non-closed) ideal in
A
. Denote the closure of the image of
I
in
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}
by
J
. Then
J
is an ideal in
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}
and the homomorphism
A/I\to\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}/J
generated by
\iota_{A}\!:A\to\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}
extends to an isomorphism

\widehat{A/I}^{\,\mathsf{C}}\cong(\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}/J)^{~}

of topological algebras. In particular, if
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}
is a Fréchet algebra, then
\widehat{A/I}^{\,\mathsf{C}}\cong\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{C}}/J
.

Remark 1.7
.

All the classes of Banach algebras listed in the introduction are stable under passing to finite products and closed subalgebras. They are also stable under passing to quotients except for Dedekind-finite Banach algebras. So we cannot apply Proposition
1.6
to algebras in the last class.

Indeed, recall that a ring is said to be
Dedekind-finite
, if
ab=1
implies
ba=1
. In the context of
C^{*}
-algebras, a Dedekind-finite algebra is simply called ’finite’; see, e.g.,
[
50
, Exercise 5.1 and Lemma 5.1.2]
. It immediately follows from the definitions that the class of Dedekind-finite Banach algebras is stable under passing to finite products and closed subalgebras and so the corresponding envelope exists by Proposition
1.3
.

On the other hand, to show that the class of Dedekind-finite Banach algebras is not stable under passing to quotients it suffices to show that the class of finite
C^{*}
-algebras is not stable. In the case of the ground field
\mathbb{C}
, this fact is well known and furthermore there are many
C^{*}
-algebras that are quotients of finite but not themselves finite. For example, every nuclearly imbeddable unital separable
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is a quotient of a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of the CAR-algebra
[
34
, Corollary 1.4, (ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(v)]
; see also
[
55
, Theorem 2]
. Since the CAR-algebra is an AF-algebra, it is finite
[
22
, Theorem IV.2.3]
. It follows from the equivalent definition of finite
C^{*}
-subalgebra in terms of projections
[
50
, Definition 5.1.1]
that each of its
C^{*}
-subalgebras is finite. But can additionally suppose that
A
is infinite and thus the quotient property does not hold.

Banach algebra of polynomial growth

The following definition is from
[
5
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

(A) An element
b
of a Banach algebra
B
is said to be of
polynomial growth
if there are
K>0
and
\alpha\geqslant 0
such that

\|e^{isb}\|\leqslant K(1+|s|)^{\alpha}\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

(In the case of ground field
\mathbb{R}
we extends the norm to the complexification of
B
.)

(B) A real Banach algebra is said to be of
polynomial growth
if all its elements are of polynomial growth.

For
p\in\mathbb{N}
denote by
\mathrm{T}_{p}
the algebra of upper triangular real matrices of order
p
. When considering a finite-dimensional associative algebra (e.g.,
\mathrm{T}_{p}
), we always assume that some submultiplicative norm is fixed and so we get a Banach algebra.

Proposition 2.2
.

A finite-dimensional Banach algebra is of polynomial growth if and only if it is isomorphic to a subalgebra
of
\mathrm{T}_{p}
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

To prove the necessity suppose that
A
is a finite-dimensional Banach algebra of polynomial growth.
Denote
A
with the Lie algebra structure given by
[a,b]\!:=ab-ba
by
\mathrm{Lie}(A)
.
Since
A
is of polynomial growth and finite dimensional,
\mathrm{Lie}(A)
is triangular by
[
5
, Proposition 4.1]
. It follows from a generalization of Lie’s theorem
[
43
, § 1.2, p. 10, Theorem 1.2]
that every finite-dimensional real representation of
\mathrm{Lie}(A)
is triangular, i.e., there is a complete flag of invariant subspaces. Taking a faithful representation of
A
(e.g., the regular one), considering it as representation of
\mathrm{Lie}(A)
and choosing a basis compatible with a complete flag, we have that
A
is isomorphic to a subalgebra of
\mathrm{T}_{p}
, where
p
is the dimension of the representation.

On the other hand, it is not hard to see that
\mathrm{T}_{p}
is of polynomial growth for every
p
(for example, one can apply
[
5
, Theorem 2.14]
) and so are all its subalgebras.
∎

Recall that a
quiver
is a directed graph, possibly with loops and multiple
arrows. Denote by
\mathbb{R}Q
the path algebra (over
\mathbb{R}
) of a quiver
Q
; for details see, e.g.
[
13
, § 4.1]
. The following proposition is used in the proof of Theorem
A.1
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
Q
be a quiver with finitely many vertices and arrows, and
has no oriented cycle. Then
\mathbb{R}Q
is finite dimensional algebra of polynomial growth.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\mathbb{R}Q
is finite dimensional.

To show that
\mathbb{R}Q
has polynomial growth denote by
A
the linear span of all idempotents in
\mathbb{R}Q
corresponding to the paths of length
0
(i.e., the vertices) and by
I
the linear span of the paths of non-zero length. It is easy to see that
A
is a subalgebra and
I
is a nilpotent ideal. Note that
\mathbb{R}Q=A+I
and so we obtain a split nilpotent extension
0\leftarrow A\leftarrow\mathbb{R}Q\leftarrow I\leftarrow 0
. Since
A
is of polynomial growth, it follows from
[
5
, Theorem 2.14]
that so is
\mathbb{R}Q
.
∎

Now we discuss tensor products.
Recall that the complete projective tensor product
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
of Banach algebras
A
and
B
is also a Banach algebra with respect to naturally defined multiplication.

Remark 2.4
.

Generally speaking, Banach algebras of polynomial growth are poorly compatible with the projective tensor product.
For example, although
C(\mathbb{T})
is of polynomial growth,
C(\mathbb{T})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}C(\mathbb{T})
is not. Indeed, by Varopoulos’s theorem, the projective tensor product
C(\mathbb{T})_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}C(\mathbb{T})_{\mathbb{C}}
of the complexifications contains the algebra
A(\mathbb{T})
of absolutely
convergent Fourier series as a closed subalgebra; see
[
33
, Chapter VIII, § 3]
. Denote by
A(\mathbb{T})_{\mathbb{R}}
the subalgebra of real-valued functions in
A(\mathbb{T})
. Then we can treat it as a closed subalgebra of
C(\mathbb{T})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}C(\mathbb{T})
.

If
C(\mathbb{T})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}C(\mathbb{T})
had polynomial growth, so would
A(\mathbb{T})_{\mathbb{R}}
. But this contradicts a theorem of Katznelson
[
33
, Chapter VI, § 6]
. For details see
[
5
, Remark 2.7]
.

The following result is a strengthening of Theorem 2.12 in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
A
and
B
be Banach algebras of polynomial growth. If one of them is finite dimensional, then

A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
is of polynomial growth.

Proof.

The argument is essentially the same as in the case when
A=\mathrm{T}_{p}
and
B
is commutative; see
[
5
, Theorem 2.12]
. It follows from
[
5
, Theorem 2.14]
that it suffices to show that
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
is a split nilpotent extension of an algebra of polynomial growth.

Denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
the Jacobson radical of
A
and consider the extension

(2.1)

0\leftarrow A/\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A\leftarrow A\leftarrow\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A\leftarrow 0.

Since
\mathbb{R}
is a perfect field and
A
is finite dimensional, we can apply the Wedderburn factor theorem
[
25
, § 13, p. 471–472, 13.18 and 13.19]
, which implies that (
2.1
) splits and
A/\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
is classically semisimple. It follows from the Wedderburn decomposition theorem
[
36
, Theorem 2.2]
that a classically semisimple real algebra is isomorphic to a finite product of full matrix algebras over division algebras over
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, by
the Frobenius theorem
[
36
, Theorem 2.50]
, the only finite-dimensional division
\mathbb{R}
-algebras are
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
and quaternions. Thus, since
A/\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
is commutative by
[
5
, Theorem 2.8]
, it is a finite product of copies of
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
.

It follows from
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
that, being a quotient of an algebra of polynomial growth,
A/\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
is also of polynomial growth and hence strictly real (the spectrum of each element contains in
\mathbb{R}
) by
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
. Therefore
A/\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
contains no copy of
\mathbb{C}
and so is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Further,
\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
is nilpotent because
A
is finite dimensional; see, e.g.,
[
30
, Proposition I.3.59]
. Since (
2.1
) is splits and nilpotent, so is the extension

0\leftarrow B^{k}\leftarrow A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B\leftarrow(\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A)\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B\leftarrow 0

obtained by tensoring with
B
. Since
B
is of polynomial growth, so is
B^{k}

[
5
, Proposition 2.13]
. An application of Theorem 2.14 in
[
5
]
mentioned above completes the proof.

∎

in
\mathbb{N}
and

with dense interior. Denote by

Corollary 2.6

Suppose that

,

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
be split nilpotent extensions of Banach algebras

and
A^{\prime}
, respectively.

(A)
Then

(B)
If, in addition,

Proof.

(A) Let

(2.2)

By

The class

The following corollary is a partial case of Proposition 2.8
in

Corollary 2.10

Let

We can assume that

This result extends to a larger class of algebras. Namely, following

,
we say that a

-differentiable algebra

Corollary 2.11
.

Every

-differentiable algebra is in
\mathsf{PGL}
.

The following result is an immediate consequence of Proposition
1.6
in the case when
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{PG}
. It shows that the notion of envelope with respect to
\mathsf{PG}
has a natural interpretation in the context of differential geometry and its extension to spaces with singularities.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
C
be a finitely generated commutative real algebra. Then
\widehat{C}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra.

Remark 2.13
.

In view of Corollary
2.12
, we can treat the envelope with respect to
\mathsf{PG}
of a general commutative real algebra as the algebra of
C^{\infty}
-functions (modulo nilpotent elements) on some commutative space. For example,
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is the envelope of
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]

[
10
, Proposition 3.3]
. Moreover, following the standard paradigm of non-commutative geometry, we can say that the envelope of a general non-commutative algebra is the algebra of
C^{\infty}
-functions on a non-commutative space. See an extension of this approach in §
4
.

We now give two examples of applying of Proposition
1.6
to finding the explicit form of the envelope.

Example 2.14
.

Let
A=\mathbb{R}[x,y]/(xy)
, where
(xy)
is the ideal generated by
xy
. Our purpose is to find
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
.

We first claim that the homomorphism

\rho\!:\mathbb{R}[x,y]\to\mathbb{R}[t]\times\mathbb{R}[t]\!:\rho(h)\!:=(x\mapsto h(x,0),\,y\mapsto h(0,y))

induced an isomorphism

(2.3)

\rho^{\prime}\!:A\to\mathcal{R}(\Omega),

where
\mathcal{R}(\Omega)\!:=\{(f,g)\in\mathbb{R}[t]\times\mathbb{R}[t]\!:\,f(0)=g(0)\}
. (Here
\Omega
stands for the set of characters, which coincides with the union of the coordinate axes but we do not need this fact here.)

Indeed,
\rho
maps
(xy)
to
0
and so
\rho^{\prime}
is well defined. If
\rho(h)=0
, then
h
is divisible by
xy
and so it is in
(xy)
. Thus
\rho^{\prime}
is injective. On the other hand, if
f,g\in\mathbb{R}[t]
and
f(0)=g(0)
, then we have
\rho(h)=(f,g)
when
h(x,y)=f(x)+g(y)-f(0)
. It follows that
\rho^{\prime}
is surjective and hence bijective.

We now claim that
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is given by the same restriction, i.e., the continuous extension of
\rho
to a homomorphism
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\times C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
induces a topological isomorphism
\rho^{\prime}\!:\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\to C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, where

C^{\infty}(\Omega):=\{(f,g)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\times C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\!:\,f(0)=g(0)\}.

(In fact,
\rho^{\prime}
is an extension of the map in (
2.3
).)

Denote by
J
the ideal in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
generated by the polynomial
xy
.
Since
J
is closed (see
[
10
, Proposition 3.9]
), it coincides with the closure of
(xy)
. Proposition
1.6
(in the case when
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{PG}
) implies that
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\cong C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})/J
.
Since
xy
is in the kernel of
\rho
, we have that
\rho^{\prime}
is well defined.

To prove that
\rho^{\prime}
is injective, we check that
xy
satisfies the
zero property
, i.e., all functions in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
vanishing on
\Omega
belong to
J
. Indeed, it follows from Hadamard’s lemma that the functions
x
and
y
satisfy the zero property. Since the origin is a single critical point of the function
xy
, the set of regular points of
\Omega
is dense. So by
[
15
, Corollaire 3]
the function
xy
also satisfies the zero property. The argument for the surjectivity of
\rho^{\prime}
is
the same as above.

Thus
\rho^{\prime}
is bijective. Since we deal with Fréchet spaces, the inverse mapping theorem implies that
\rho^{\prime}
is a topological isomorphism.

Example 2.15
.

Let
A=\mathbb{R}[x,y]/(x^{2}-y^{3})
. Our purpose is to find
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
in this case.

Denote also by
J
the ideal in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
generated by
x^{2}-y^{3}
. Using the parametrization
x=t^{3}
,
y=t^{2}
, we can identify
A
with the subalgebra in
\mathbb{R}[t]
consisting of polynomials with zero coefficient at the term of first degree, i.e., with trivial first derivative at
0
. We now show that the same holds for the envelope, i.e.,
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\cong C^{\infty}_{1}
, where

C^{\infty}_{1}\!:=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\!:\,f^{\prime}(0)=0\}.

In this case, denote by
\Omega
the cuspidal cubic determined by the equation
x^{2}=y^{3}
. Being finitely generated,
J
is closed; see
[
10
, Proposition 3.9]
. By Proposition
1.6
, we have that
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\cong C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})/J
.

Consider the continuous homomorphism

\rho\!:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\!:g\mapsto(t\mapsto g(t^{3},t^{2})).

It is clear that the range of
\rho
is contained in
C^{\infty}_{1}
and
\rho(J)=0
. So we have the continuous homomorphism

\rho^{\prime}\!:\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\to C^{\infty}_{1}.

We need to show that
\rho^{\prime}
is a topological isomorphism.

(1) To prove that
\rho^{\prime}
is injective it suffices to show that
p(x,y)\!:=x^{2}-y^{3}
satisfies the zero property in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
The following argument is from the slides
[
37
]
of Kondo. Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and vanish on
\Omega
. Since

p(0,0)=\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}(0,0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x^{2}}(0,0)\neq 0,

it follows from the Malgrange preparation theorem
[
31
, Theorem 7.5.6]
that there are
q\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
h_{0},h_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\psi(x,y)=q(x,y)p(x,y)+h_{1}(y)x+h_{0}(y)\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb{R}).

If
y\geqslant 0
, then
(y^{3/2},y)
and
(-y^{3/2},y)
belong to
\Omega
. Since
\psi
and
p
vanish on
\Omega
, we have that

h_{1}(y)y^{3/2}+h_{0}(y)=-h_{1}(y)y^{3/2}+h_{0}(y)=0

and so
h_{0}(y)=h_{1}(y)=0
. Hence the function
h(x,y)\!:=h_{1}(y)x+h_{0}(y)
vanishes on the set
\{(x,y)\!:\,y\geqslant 0\}
and therefore all the partial derivatives vanish on this set and on its subset
\Omega
.

We claim that
h\in J
. Indeed, since
J
is closed (see above), by Whitney’s spectral theorem
[
40
, Chapter II]
, it suffices to show that for every point
(x_{0},y_{0})
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
there is a function in
J
whose Taylor expansion at
(x_{0},y_{0})
coincides with that of
h
. Since
h
vanishes in upper semiplane, this is obvious when
y_{0}\geqslant 0
. Otherwise, when
y_{0}<0
, we can take a function
q_{1}
that equals
h(x,y)p(x,y)^{-1}
in a small enough neighbourhood of
(x_{0},y_{0})
and
0
when
y\geqslant 0
. Then the Taylor expansion of
q_{1}p
at
(x_{0},y_{0})
coincides with that of
h
. Thus
h_{1}(y)x+h_{0}(y)=q_{1}(x,y)p(x,y)
, where
q_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Hence
\psi\in J
, which means that
x^{2}-y^{3}
has the zero property. Thus
\rho^{\prime}
is injective.

(2) To prove that
\rho^{\prime}
is surjective take
f\in C^{\infty}_{1}
and write
f=f_{+}+f_{-}
, where
f_{+}
is even,
f_{-}
is odd and both functions are in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. It is clear that
f^{\prime}_{-}(0)=0
.

We claim first that if
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and even, then the function
g(y)\!:=f(y^{1/2})
is in

C^{\infty}[0,+\infty)
. Indeed, it is obvious that
g
is of class
C^{\infty}
for
y>0
.
In the case when
y=0
, take
n\in\mathbb{N}
and write
f(t)=P_{2n}(t)+o(t^{2n})
as
t\to 0
, where
P_{k}
denotes the Taylor polynomial of degree
k
at
t=0
. Since
P_{2n}(y^{1/2})
is a polynomial in
y
and
g(y)-P_{2n}(y^{1/2})=o(y^{n})
as
y\to 0+
, we have that
g
is
n
times differentiable at
0
. Since
n
is arbitrary,
g\in C^{\infty}[0,+\infty)
.

We claim second that if
f
is an odd function in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
f^{\prime}(0)=0
, then
the function
y\mapsto f(y^{1/2})/y^{3/2}
extends to a function
\widetilde{f}\in C^{\infty}[0,+\infty)
. Indeed, since the Taylor’s coefficients at degrees
0
,
1
,
2
are trivial, the function
t\mapsto f(t)/t^{3}
extends to a function in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and so we can apply the first claim.

Now for
f\in C^{\infty}_{1}
put
g(x,y)\!:=f_{+}(y^{1/2})+x\widetilde{f}_{-}(y^{1/2})
. It follows from the claims above that
g
is of class
C^{\infty}
in the upper semiplane. So it can be extended to a function in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. It is obvious that
\rho(g)=f
. Thus
\rho^{\prime}
is surjective and hence a topological isomorphism. Thus
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\cong C^{\infty}_{1}
.

We now discuss projective tensor products of
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra. Our considerations for the Banach algebra case can be extended to a more general class, which is (possibly strictly) smaller than
\mathsf{PGL}
. Note first that if
A
and
B
are Arens–Michael algebras (i.e., projective limits of Banach algebras), then so is
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
with respect to a naturally defined multiplication. Moreover,
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
satisfies a natural universal property; see Remark
3.15
(B).

The following result is a strengthening of Theorem
2.5
.

Proposition 2.16
.

If
A
is a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra and
B
is a finite-dimensional algebra of polynomial growth, then
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
is also in
\mathsf{PGL}
.

Proof.

By
[
10
, Proposition 2.6]
, an Arens–Michael
\mathbb{R}
-algebra is in
\mathsf{PGL}
if only if it is isomorphic to a closed subalgebra of a product of Banach algebras of polynomial growth. So we can assume that
A\subset\prod_{i}A_{i}
, where each of
A_{i}
is in
\mathsf{PG}
. Since the complete projective tensor product commutes with products of complete locally convex spaces (see, e.g.,
[
29
, Chapter II, p. 127, Theorem 5.19]
) and hence with products of complete locally convex algebras,
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
is isomorphic to a closed subalgebra of
\prod_{i}(A_{i}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B)
. By Theorem
2.5
, each of
A_{i}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
is in
\mathsf{PG}
. Applying
[
10
, Proposition 2.6]
in the reverse direction, we conclude that
A\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B
is in
\mathsf{PGL}
.
∎

\mathsf{DTS}
and
\mathsf{QDTS}
algebras

We introduce two more classes of algebras and show that they are contained in
\mathsf{PGL}
and stable under tensor products, which is used in §
4
.

Definition 2.17
.

We denote by
\mathsf{DTS}
the class of closed subalgebras of products of algebras of the form
C\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathrm{T}_{p}
, where
C
is a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra and
p\in\mathbb{N}
. (Here ‘D’ stands for ‘differentiable’, ‘T’ for ‘triangular’ and ‘S’ for ‘subalgebra’.)

By Proposition
2.2
, we can take arbitrary finite-dimensional algebras of polynomial growth instead of
\mathrm{T}_{p}
.

Note also that this definition is a bit redundant for our purposes since in fact we only use closed subalgebras of products of algebras of the form
C^{\infty}(M)\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathrm{T}_{p}
, where
M
is a manifold (see §
5
and Appendix
A
). But it seems that the class
\mathsf{DTS}
is of independent interest.

Proposition 2.18
.

Every algebra in
\mathsf{DTS}
belongs to
\mathsf{PGL}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.11
, every
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra is in
\mathsf{PGL}
. Then by Proposition
2.16
, every algebra of the form
C\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathrm{T}_{p}
, where
C
is a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra, is in
\mathsf{PGL}
. Moreover, a closed subalgebra of a product of algebras that are in
\mathsf{PGL}
is also in
\mathsf{PGL}

[
10
, Theorem 2.7]
. It follows from the definition that
\mathsf{DTS}\subset\mathsf{PGL}
.
∎

We now turn to tensor products of
\mathsf{DTS}
algebras.
The following result is used in §
4
.

Proposition 2.19
.

If
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are in
\mathsf{DTS}
, then so is
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
.

Proof.

(1) Consider first the case when
A_{i}
has the form
C_{i}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B_{i}
, where
C_{i}
is a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra and
B_{i}
is a finite-dimensional algebra of polynomial growth (
i=1,2
). By
[
42
, Theorem 6.13]
,
C_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}C_{2}
is a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra and, by Theorem
2.5
,
B_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B_{2}
is a finite-dimensional algebra of polynomial growth. Thus
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
also has the same form.

(2) Suppose now that
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are products of algebras of the form considered in Case (1). Since the complete projective tensor product commutes with products of complete locally convex algebras (cf. the proof of proposition
2.16
), it follows from Case (1) that
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
is also a product of algebras of the above form.

(3) Finally, consider the general case. Let
A_{1}\subset B_{1}
and
A_{2}\subset B_{2}
, where
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are algebras of the form considered in Case (2). Note that
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebras and finite-dimensional algebras are nuclear spaces. Moreover, the class of nuclear spaces is stable under products
[
51
, Chapter 3, § 7.4]
and passing to closed subspaces. Hence all the space under consideration are nuclear. Therefore the natural homomorphism
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}\to B_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B_{2}
is topologically injective; see, e.g.,
[
24
, p. 322, Theorem A1.6]
. Thus
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
is a closed subalgebra of an algebra of the form considered in Case (2), i.e.,
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
is in
\mathsf{DTS}
.
∎

Definition 2.20
.

We denote by
\mathsf{QDTS}
the class of completions of quotients of
\mathsf{DTS}
algebras.

We need to complete quotients because there are non-metrizable
\mathsf{DTS}
algebras.

The next result follows immediately from Propositions
2.18
and
2.9
.

Proposition 2.21
.

Every algebra in
\mathsf{QDTS}
belongs to
\mathsf{PGL}
.

The following proposition is similar to Proposition
2.19
.

Proposition 2.22
.

If
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are in
\mathsf{QDTS}
, then so is
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that an Arens–Michael algebra is in
\mathsf{QDTS}
if and only if it contains a dense image of a
\mathsf{DTS}
algebra under a continuous homomorphism whose corestriction to this image is open.
Fix for
i=1,2
such a homomorphism
\varphi_{i}\!:B_{i}\to A_{i}
.

It is well known that the above property of linear maps is preserved by the projective tensor product; see, e.g.,
[
53
, Proposition 43.9 and Definition 4.1]
. In our case this means that the corestriction of
\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2}
to the image of
B_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B_{2}
in
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
is open and the image is dense. Since
B_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}B_{2}
is in
\mathsf{DTS}
by Propositions
2.19
and
\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2}

Remark 2.23

The author does not know whether

Commutativity modulo radical

The next result, which is a strengthening of
[
5
, Theorem 2.8]
, is also used in §
4
.

Proposition 2.24
.

Every
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra is commutative modulo the radical.

For the proof we need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.25
.

Suppose that a real or complex Arens–Michael algebra
A
is a projective limit of Banach algebras commutative modulo the radical. Then
A
is also commutative modulo the radical.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in A
. We need to show that
[a,b]\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A
, i.e.,
1-c[a,b]
is invertible for every
c\in A
.

Let
A
be the projective limit of a system
(A_{\nu})
of Banach algebras, each of which is commutative modulo the radical. For every
\nu
we denote by
\tau_{\nu}
the corresponding homomorphism
A\to A_{\nu}
. Then
\tau_{\nu}([a,b])=[\tau_{\nu}(a),\tau_{\nu}(b)]\in\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits A_{\nu}
. Hence
1-x[\tau_{\nu}(a),\tau_{\nu}(b)]
is invertible for every
x\in A_{\nu}
. In particular,
\tau_{\nu}(1-c[a,b])
is invertible for every
c\in A
. However, an element
d\in A
is invertible when all
\tau_{\nu}(d)
are invertible; see e.g.,
[
30
, p. 280, Proposition V.2.11]
. This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.24
.

It follows from
[
5
, Theorem 2.8]
that every Banach algebra of polynomial growth is commutative modulo the radical. Since an algebra in
\mathsf{PGL}
is, by definition, the projective limit of Banach algebras of polynomial growth, we conclude from Lemma
2.25
that it is also commutative modulo the radical.
∎

Remark 2.26
.

Proposition 13 in
[
28
]
asserts that a projective limit of strictly real Banach algebras is commutative modulo the radical. Since the spectrum of every element of polynomial growth is contained in
\mathbb{R}

[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
, in the real case we can use the cited result instead of Lemma
2.25
. In fact, the proof in
[
28
]
is essentially the same as for Lemma
2.25
but with a few typos.

Coproducts, colimits and tensor products

Now we consider coproducts, which are also called free products in categories of associative algebras, and related constructions.

Proposition 3.10
.

Coproducts exist in
\mathsf{CL}
.

Proof.

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a family of
\mathsf{CL}
algebras. Note that coproducts exist in the category
\mathsf{AM}
of Arens–Michael algebras; see
[
47
, § 4]
. By Proposition
1.3
, the forgetful functor from
\mathsf{CL}
to
\mathsf{TA}
admits a left adjoint functor and so does the forgetful functor from
\mathsf{CL}
to
\mathsf{AM}
since the latter is a subcategory of

. Therefore, taking first the coproduct of all

in
\mathsf{AM}
and then its envelope with respect to
\mathsf{CL}

\mathsf{CL}
.
∎

We denote the coproduct of a family
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
\mathsf{CL}
algebras by
{\ast^{\mathsf{C}}_{i\in I}}A_{i}
and the coproduct of a finite family

by
A_{1}\mathbin{\ast^{\mathsf{C}}\!}\cdots\mathbin{\ast^{\mathsf{C}}\!}A_{n}
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
X
and
Y
be sets. Then
\mathscr{F}^{\mathsf{C}}\{X\}\mathbin{\ast^{\mathsf{C}}\!}\mathscr{F}^{\mathsf{C}}\{Y\}\cong\mathscr{F}^{\mathsf{C}}\{X\coprod Y\}
.

The proof is straightforward.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose, in addition, that
\mathsf{C}
is closed under quotients.
Then the category
\mathsf{CL}
is cocomplete, i.e., colimits exist.

Proof.

It is well known that it suffices to show that coproducts and coequalizers of parallel pairs exist; see, e.g.,
[
18
, p. 60, Theorem 2.8.1]
. The first property is established in Proposition
3.10
. The second property can be proved in a standard way. Indeed, let
\alpha,\beta\!:A\to B
be homomorphisms in
\mathsf{CL}
. If
I
is the closed ideal of
B
generated by elements of the form
\beta(a)-\alpha(a)
, where

, then
(B/I)^{~}
is in
\mathsf{CL}

and

is a coequalizer.
∎

.

Suppose, in addition, that
\mathsf{C}
is stable under passing to quotients.

Let

4.
Finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras

In this section we introduce our main subject of study — a finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated (associative) algebra. This notion is on the one hand a non-commutative version of the notion of
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra, but on the other hand it is a
C^{\infty}
version of the notion of holomorphically finitely generated algebra.

Recall that every
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra is a quotient of some
C^{\infty}
-tensor algebra; see Proposition
3.5
. The similar result holds in the holomorphic case; namely, every Arens–Michael algebra is a quotient of some analytic tensor algebra. In
[
46
,
47
]
Pirkovskii introduced holomorphically finitely generated algebras as quotients of analytic tensor algebras of finite-dimensional spaces. We now consider a similar subclass in
\mathsf{PGL}
. Also, every
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra (i.e., a quotient of some
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
) is a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra; see Corollary
2.11
. Here we replace the free commutative algebra
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
by the free
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
(eq., the
C^{\infty}
-tensor algebra with
k
-dimensional generating space).

Definition 4.1
.

A real Arens–Michael algebra is said to be
finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated
if it is isomorphic to a quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We have two reasons for introducing this class: our main examples are finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated and the good behaviour of this class with respect to the projective tensor product.

Proposition 4.2
.

Every finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra is a nuclear Fréchet space.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Theorem
3.18
,
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is a nuclear Fréchet space and so are all its quotients.
∎

Finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras form a full subcategory in
\mathsf{PGL}
. We also consider the smaller subcategory of commutative finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras.

Proposition 4.3
.

A commutative real Arens–Michael algebra is finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated if and only if it is a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra.

Proof.

Take
k\in\mathbb{N}
and note that
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is the quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
by the closed ideal generated by commutators. So every
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra, being a quotient of some
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, is also a quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
.

On the other hand, every commutative quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is a quotient of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
because the quotient map factors on
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
.
∎

Recall that the
real spectrum
of an
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra
A
is the locally ringed
\mathbb{R}
-space
(\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}_{r}A,\,\widetilde{A})
, where
\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}_{r}A
is the set of homomorphisms to
A
to
\mathbb{R}
and
\widetilde{A}
is a naturally defined sheaf of
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra; for details see
[
42
, § 3]
.
Also, a locally ringed
\mathbb{R}
-space
(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})
is said to be an
affine
C^{\infty}
-differentiable space
(of finite type) if it is isomorphic to the real spectrum of some
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra
[
42
, p. 44]
.

The functors
(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
and
A\rightsquigarrow(\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}_{r}A,\,\widetilde{A})
define an anti-equivalence of the category of affine
C^{\infty}
-differentiable spaces with the category of
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebras
[
42
, p. 47, Theorem 3.20]
. In view of the fact, that every homomorphism of
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebras is automatically continuous
[
42
, p. 33, Corollary 2.22]
, we obtain the following corollary of Proposition
4.3
.

Corollary 4.4
.

The functors
(X,\,\mathcal{O}_{X})\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}(X)
and
A\rightsquigarrow(\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}_{r}A,\,\widetilde{A})
define an anti-equivalence of the category of affine
C^{\infty}
-differentiable spaces with the category of commutative finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras.

This result is an analogue of the Forster–Pirkovskii theorem, which states that the global sections functor is an anti-equivalence between the category of Stein spaces of finite embedding dimension and the category of commutative holomorphically finitely generated algebras
[
47
, Theorem 3.23]
.

Proposition 4.5
.

A finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra

is commutative modulo radical and

-differentiable algebra.

Proof.

Since

is in
\mathsf{PGL}
, the first assertion is implied by Proposition
2.24
.
The second assertion follows from Proposition
4.3
.
∎

If
A
is a finite-dimensional algebra of polynomial growth, then it is a
Banach algebra of polynomial growth and then
\widehat{A}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}=A
.
Since a finite-dimensional algebra is finitely generated, we can apply Proposition
4.6
.
∎

The following two results is related with the discussion on Hopf algebras in §
6
.

Proposition 4.9
.

If
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
are finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras, then

A_{1}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}A_{n}\cong A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\cdots\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{n}.

Proof.

Note that every finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra is in
\mathsf{QDTS}
since it is a quotient of an algebra of the form
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
, which is in
\mathsf{DTS}
by Theorem
3.18
. So we can apply Proposition
3.17
.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

If
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
are finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebras, then so is
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\cdots\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{n}
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case when
n=2
. By the definition,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are quotients of
\mathscr{F}_{k_{1}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
and
\mathscr{F}_{k_{2}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
, respectively, for some
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Since we are dealing with Fréchet algebras, a standard property of projective tensor products implies that
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
is a quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{k_{1}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathscr{F}_{k_{2}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
, which is isomorphic to
\mathscr{F}_{k_{1}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}\mathscr{F}_{k_{2}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
by Proposition
4.9
. Moreover, it follows from Proposition
3.14
that
\mathscr{F}_{k_{1}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}\mathscr{F}_{k_{2}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is a quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{k_{1}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}{\ast^{\mathsf{PG}}}\mathscr{F}_{k_{2}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
, which in turn is isomorphic to
\mathscr{F}_{k_{1}+k_{2}}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
by Proposition
3.11
.
Thus
A_{1}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}A_{2}
is a quotient of the last algebra and so is finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated.
∎

5.
Example of a finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra

In this section we construct a finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra that is not obtained as an envelope of a finitely generated algebra, but directly as a quotient of a
C^{\infty}
-free algebra. This example is a deformation of the envelope of
U(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1})
, where
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}
is the non-abelian solvable
2
-dimensional Lie algebra. The first form of this deformation was introduced in
[
2
]
at the physical level of rigour; for a holomorphic version see
[
9
]
.

Recall that
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}
is generated by elements
e_{1},e_{2}
subject to the relation
[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{2}
.
Now let
\hbar\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and denote by
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
the universal finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra with generators
e_{1},e_{2}
subject to the relation

(5.1)

[e_{1},e_{2}]=\frac{\sinh(\hbar e_{2})}{\sinh\hbar}.

This means that
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
is the quotient of
\mathscr{F}_{2}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
over the closed ideal generated by the element
[e_{1},e_{2}]\sinh\hbar-\sinh(\hbar e_{2})
, which is well defined because each element of a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra admits a
C^{\infty}
-functional calculus; see, e.g.,
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
.

Here we describe the structure of
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra on
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
. For the Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra structure see Proposition
6.9
in the next section. Of course, if we were not interested in such a structure, we could replace the function on the right-hand side by another one that has similar properties in the neighborhood of zero.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\hbar\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

(A)
The Fréchet space
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathbb{R}[[e_{2}]]
is a
\mathsf{DTS}
algebra with respect to a multiplication such that (
5.1
) holds.

(B)
With respect to this multiplication,
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathbb{R}[[e_{2}]]
is a universal finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra, i.e., it is topologically isomorphic to
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
.

Thus the underlying Fréchet space of
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
is the same as in the undeformed case of
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}}
; see
[
5
, Example 4.7]
. But, of course, the multiplication is different.

We need auxiliary results. The following theorem is a strengthening of a well-known result on ordered
C^{\infty}
-functional calculus; see a discussion in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

[
5
, Theorem 3.3]

Let
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}
be elements of an Arens–Michael
\mathbb{R}
-algebra
B
. Suppose that
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}

(
k\leqslant m
)
are of polynomial growth and
b_{k+1},\dots,b_{m}
are nilpotent. Then the linear map
\mathbb{R}[\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]\to B
taking the (commutative) monomial
\lambda_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{m}^{\beta_{m}}
to the (non-commutative) monomial
b_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots b_{m}^{\beta_{m}}
extends to a continuous linear map

C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathbb{R}[[\lambda_{k+1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]]\to B.

Now we use this theorem in our situation.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
x
and
z
are elements of a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra
B
such that
[x,z]=\sinh(\hbar z)/\sinh\hbar
,
then the linear map
\mathbb{R}[e_{1},e_{2}]\to B\!:e_{1}\mapsto x,\,e_{2}\mapsto z
extends to a continuous linear map defined on
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathbb{R}[[e_{2}]]
.

Proof.

We can assume that
B
is a Banach algebra of polynomial growth. The relation in the hypothesis implies that
\sinh(\hbar z)
belongs to the commutant and therefore belongs to
\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits B
by Proposition
2.24
. On the other hand,
\mathop{\mathrm{Rad}}\nolimits B
is nilpotent by
[
5
, Theorem 2.9]
. Thus
\sinh(\hbar z)
is nilpotent.

We claim that
z
is also nilpotent. Indeed, it follows from the composition property of holomorphic functional calculus
[
3
, p. 218, Theorem 4.95]
that
a=\arcsinh(\sinh a)
when
a
is an element of a Banach algebra such that the spectrum of
\sinh a
is sufficiently small. In particular, this holds when
\sinh a
is nilpotent. Hence
z=(\arcsinh y)/\hbar
, where
y=\sinh(\hbar z)
. Since
y
is nilpotent,
z
is a polynomial in
y
. Moreover, the free term of this polynomial is
0
and thus
z
is also nilpotent. The claim is proved.

Since
x
is of polynomial growth and
z
is nilpotent, it follows from Theorem
5.2
that there is a continuous linear map
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathbb{R}[[e_{2}]]\to B
taking
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
to
x
and
z
, respectively.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

(A) We construct a sequence
(\pi_{p})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
of triangular representations of (

). Let

X_{p}:=\begin{pmatrix}p&&&&\\
&p-1&&&\\
&&\ddots&&\\
&&&1&\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad E_{p}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&&&\\
&0&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&0&1\\
&&&&0\end{pmatrix}

be matrices in
\mathrm{T}_{p+1}
.

To find matrices satisfying (
5.1
) we first consider a simplified relation and assume that

(5.2)

[X_{p},Z_{p}]=\frac{\sinh(\hbar Z_{p})}{\hbar},

where

Z_{p}=E_{p}+\alpha_{2}E_{p}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}E_{p}^{p}

for some
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{p}\in\mathbb{R}

[X_{p},E_{p}^{j}]=jE_{p}^{j}
for every

E_{p}+2\alpha_{2}E_{p}^{2}+\cdots+p\alpha_{p}E_{p}^{p}=(E_{p}+\alpha_{2}E_{p}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}E_{p}^{p})+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{3!}(E_{p}+\alpha_{2}E_{p}^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}E_{p}^{p})^{3}+\cdots.

This relation gives a system of linear equations with
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{p}
as unknowns, which is finite because
E^{p+1}=0
. Note that
\alpha_{j}=0
for even
j
since Taylor’s expression for the hyperbolic sine contains only odd degrees. For odd

we have the system

\begin{cases}3\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{3}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{3!}\\
5\alpha_{5}=\alpha_{5}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2!}\alpha_{3}+\frac{\hbar^{4}}{5!}\\
\cdots\end{cases}.

It is not hard to see that all
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
in this case. Also, they are obviously independent of

Now put

and define
\pi_{p}(e_{1})=\varkappa X_{p}
and
\pi_{p}(e_{2})=Z_{p}

For small values of

\pi_{0}(e_{1})=0,\qquad\pi_{0}(e_{2})=0;

\pi_{1}(e_{1})=\varkappa\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

&0\end{pmatrix};

\pi_{2}(e_{1})=\varkappa\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\
&1&0\\
&&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\pi_{2}(e_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
&0&1\\
&&0\end{pmatrix};

&&1&0\\
&&&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\pi_{3}(e_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0&\frac{\hbar^{2}}{12}\\
&0&1&0\\
&&0&1\\
&&&0\end{pmatrix}.

Thus for

the representation

has the same form (up to scalar) as in the undeformed case in

but it is different for

For every

the formulas

also define elements of

satisfying (

). Treating

as a variable, we get elements of

5.3

We identify
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\times})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\mathbb{R}[[e_{2}]]
with
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and take the map

\rho\!:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\,\rightarrow\,\prod_{p}\mathrm{T}_{p}(C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}))

induced by the sequence
(\widetilde{\pi}_{p})
. To complete the proof of Part (A) it suffices to show that
\rho
is topologically injective.

Let
a=(f_{j})\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
or, in the power series form,
a=\sum_{j}f_{j}(e_{1})e_{2}^{j}
.
Then

\widetilde{\pi}_{0}(a)=f_{0}(\lambda);\qquad\widetilde{\pi}_{1}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{0}(\lambda+\varkappa)&f_{1}(\lambda+\varkappa)\\

\widetilde{\pi}_{2}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{0}(\lambda+2\varkappa)&f_{1}(\lambda+2\varkappa)&f_{2}(\lambda+2\varkappa)\\

\widetilde{\pi}_{3}(a)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{0}(\lambda+3\varkappa)&f_{1}(\lambda+3\varkappa)&f_{2}(\lambda+3\varkappa)&\frac{\hbar^{2}}{12}\,f_{1}(\lambda+3\varkappa)+f_{3}(\lambda+3\varkappa)\\

is nilpotent, the functions

f_{0},\ldots,f_{p}

\rho^{\prime}
the continuous linear map
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}

10

where
\tau
takes a sequence to the set of truncations and
\rho^{\prime\prime}
is the product of the maps
\rho^{\prime\prime}_{p}\!:C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})^{p+1}\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

is topologically injective.

Note that each of

is injective.

It follows from the inverse mapping theorem for Fréchet space that, being injective with closed range,

Part (B) follows from Part (A) and Lemma

.
∎

6.
Hopf algebras

General considerations leading to the following definition are given in Introduction.

Definition 6.1
.

A real Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra that is a finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra is called a
C^{\infty}
-finitely generated Hopf algebra
.

Enveloping functor

The following proposition provides a sufficient condition for the envelope with respect to
\mathsf{PG}
to preserve the Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra structure.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
H
be a real Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra. If the natural homomorphism

\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\to\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}

is a topological isomorphism, then there is a unique real Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra structure on
\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
such that
H\to\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
becomes a homomorphism of Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebras.

Proof.

Note that
\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}={(\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}})^{^}}{\,\,}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
; cf. Remarks
3.15
. So we can use the same argument as in
[
44
, Proposition 6.7]
applying this formula instead of
[
44
, Proposition 6.4]
. (It is essential here that the comutiplication
\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\to\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is obtained by an application of the universal property since the latter algebra is in
\mathsf{PGL}
.)
∎

The following result shows that under a finiteness condition the envelope is in fact a functor between categories of Hopf algebras.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
H
be a real Hopf algebra that is affine (i.e., finitely generated as an associative algebra).

(A)
Then there is a unique real Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra structure on
\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
such that
H\to\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
becomes a homomorphism of Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebras.

(B)
The correspondence
H\mapsto\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
extends to a functor from the category of affine real Hopf algebras to the category of
C^{\infty}
-finitely generated Hopf algebras.

Proof.

(A) Proposition
4.6
implies that
\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated. So
\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\to\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}\widehat{H}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
is a topological isomorphism by Proposition
4.9
.

On the other hand, being finitely generated,
H
has a countable basis.
Every real associative algebra with countable basis is a locally convex
algebra with respect to the strongest locally convex topology. (The proof is the same as for a complex associative algebra; see, e.g.,
[
21
, Proposition 2.1]
.) Since the topology is strongest,
H\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}H\cong H\otimes H
as a vector space and hence
H
is a Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra. So we can apply Proposition
6.2
, which completes the proof.

The argument for Part (B) is the same as for
[
44
, Proposition 6.7]
.
∎

is not well defined.

On the other hand,
a\otimes a+b\otimes c
is invertible in
\widehat{A}_{q}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
. This is implied by Proposition
6.7
but can also be shown directly. It suffices to show this for elements
a,b,c
of a Banach algebra of polynomial growth such that the first three relations in the definition of
\mathcal{R}(\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits_{q}(2,\mathbb{R}))
hold and
a
is invertible.
Indeed, since
a\otimes a+b\otimes c=(a\otimes a)(1\otimes 1+a^{-1}b\otimes a^{-1}c)
, we need to show that
1\otimes 1+a^{-1}b\otimes a^{-1}c
is invertible. Note that
ab=q(q-1)^{-1}[a,b]
is nilpotent and so is
a^{-1}b
, as well as
a^{-1}b\otimes a^{-1}c
. Then

\sum(-1)^{n}(a^{-1}b)^{n}\otimes(a^{-1}c)^{n}
converges and hence the limit is the inverse element of
1\otimes 1+a^{-1}b\otimes a^{-1}c
.

Finally, we consider the algebra
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
in Theorem
5.1
; cf. the holomorphic case in
[
9
, Proposition 5.6]
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\hbar\in\mathbb{C}
and
\sinh\hbar\neq 0
. Then
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
is a
C^{\infty}
-finitely generated Hopf algebra with respect to the comultiplication
\Delta
, counit
\varepsilon
and
antipode

determined by

\displaystyle\Delta\!

\displaystyle:e_{1}\mapsto e_{1}\otimes e^{-\hbar e_{2}}+e^{\hbar e_{2}}\otimes e_{1},

\displaystyle e_{2}\mapsto 1\otimes e_{2}+e_{2}\otimes 1;

\displaystyle S\!

\displaystyle:e_{1}\mapsto-e_{1}-\frac{\hbar}{\sinh\hbar}\,\sinh(\hbar e_{2}),

\displaystyle e_{2}\mapsto-e_{2};

\displaystyle\varepsilon\!

\displaystyle:e_{1},e_{2}\mapsto 0\,,

respectively, Moreover,

is invertible.

Proof.

The argument is the same as for
[
9
, Proposition 5.6]
, where the case of the universal complex Arens–Michael algebra determined by the same relation is considered. The only difference is that we use the fact that
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
is a finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra universal in the class
\mathsf{PGL}
, which implies that
(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}\mathbin{\otimes^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\!}(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}\cong(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}(C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}})_{\hbar}
by Proposition
4.9
.
∎

Example 6.10
.

Consider the Taft algebras

. Note that over
\mathbb{R}
the definition of these algebras makes sense only when

. We take

, which is the

-dimensional Hopf algebra generated as an associative algebra by

and

subject to the relations

,

and

; see, e.g.,
[

is a Hopf
\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}
-algebra.

For given

consider the following representation of

Appendix A
Envelopes of free algebras

Here we discuss the structure of
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
for general
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It is convenient to use the fact that the free algebra
\mathbb{R}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\rangle
is isomorphic to the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})
, where
\mathfrak{f}_{k}
denotes a free real Lie algebra with
k
generators. This section contains an explicit construction of
\widehat{U}(\mathfrak{f}_{k})^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
, which is topologically isomorphic
\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
. First recall some notation from
[
10
]
.

Notation and definitions from
[
10
]

To describe
\widehat{U}(\mathfrak{f}_{k})^{\,\mathsf{PG}}
we consider homomorphisms to Banach algebras of polynomial growth. Since the commutant of such an algebra is nilpotent
[
5
, Theorem 2.8 and Proposition 2.9]
, we need a description of
U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})
that uses
[\mathfrak{f}_{k},\mathfrak{f}_{k}]
. It is based on polynomials in the operators
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits e_{i}
, where
\{e_{1},\ldots e_{k}\}
is the set of generators of
\mathfrak{f}_{k}
. Denote by
\Delta
the set of all pairs
(l,j)
of positive integers such that
k\geqslant l>j\geqslant 1
.
For
\delta\in\Delta
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{l}
put

(A.1)

g_{\delta\beta}\!:=(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits{e_{1}})^{\beta_{1}}\cdots(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits{e_{l-1}})^{\beta_{l-1}}(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits{e_{l}})^{\beta_{l}+1}(e_{j}),

or, in an explicit form,

g_{\delta\beta}=[e_{1},[\ldots[e_{2},[\cdots[e_{l},e_{j}]\cdots]],

where each of
e_{i}
repeats
\beta_{i}
times except
e_{l}
which repeats
\beta_{l}+1
times.

It is well known that the commutant
[\mathfrak{f}_{k},\mathfrak{f}_{k}]
is a free Lie algebra with the algebraic basis

(A.2)

\{g_{\delta\beta}\!:\,\delta\in\Delta,\,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{l}\};

for a proof see, e.g.,
[
12
, p. 64, § 2.4.2, Corollary 2.16(ii)]
or
[
16
, p. 72, Lemma 2.11.16]
.
The PBW theorem implies that there is a linear isomorphism

(A.3)

U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\cong\mathbb{R}[e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}]\otimes T(V),

where
V
is the linear span of the set in (
A.2
) and
T(V)
denotes the tensor algebra of
V
.

Furthermore, each element of
V
has the form

(A.4)

\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}\Psi_{\delta}(f_{\delta})([e_{l},e_{j}]),

where
f_{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}[\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{l}]
and

(A.5)

\Psi_{\delta}\!:\mu_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mu_{l}^{\beta_{l}}\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits e_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits e_{l}^{\beta_{l}}

is a linear map from
\mathbb{R}[\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{l}]
to the space of linear endomorphisms of
[\mathfrak{f}_{k},\mathfrak{f}_{k}]
. Therefore,

(A.6)

V\cong\bigoplus_{\delta\in\Delta}\mathbb{R}[\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{l}].

Put
X\!:=\bigsqcup_{\delta\in\Delta}X_{\delta}
, where
X_{\delta}\!:=\mathbb{R}^{l}
and
\delta=(l,j)
. We also put
X=\emptyset
when
k=1
.
Let

(A.7)

C^{\infty}(X)\!:=\bigoplus_{\delta\in\Delta}C^{\infty}(X_{\delta}).

The natural maps
\mathbb{R}[\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{l}]\to C^{\infty}(X_{\delta})
,
\delta\in\Delta
, induce an embedding
V\to C^{\infty}(X)
. Denote by
[T](C^{\infty}(X))
the formal tensor algebra associated with the Fréchet space
C^{\infty}(X)
, that is,

[T](C^{\infty}(X))=\prod_{m=0}^{\infty}C^{\infty}(X)^{\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}m}

with the concatenation as the multiplication; see the
[
10
, Definition 4.1]
. It is also convenient to write

(A.8)

[T](C^{\infty}(X))=\prod_{m=0}^{\infty}C^{\infty}(X^{m}),

where
X^{m}
is the disjoint union of all possible
X_{\delta_{1}}\times\cdots\times X_{\delta_{m}}
.

The following definition is introduced in
[
10
, eq. (4.5)]
:

(A.9)

C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}\!:=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}[T](C^{\infty}(X))\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

The next two theorems were announced in
[
10
]
and proved there in the case when
k\leqslant 2
. A proof for arbitrary
k
was postponed due to many technical details. Our purpose here is to give a complete argument in the general case.

Theorem A.1
.

[
10
, Theorem 4.6]

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(A)
The multiplication on
U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})
extends to a continuous multiplication on
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
.

(B)

C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
is in
\mathsf{DTS}
(see Definition
2.17
) and is therefore a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra.

The conclusion that
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
belongs to
\mathsf{DTS}
is omitted in the statement of
[
10
, Theorem 4.6]
, but in fact the proof given there also provides this result for
k\leqslant 2
.

Theorem A.2
.

[
10
, Theorem 4.7]

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the algebra
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
together with the embedding
U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\to C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
is the envelope with respect to
\mathsf{PG}
, i.e.,
\widehat{U}(\mathfrak{f}_{k})^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\cong\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\,\mathsf{PG}}\cong C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
.

Theorem
A.2
easily follows from Theorem
A.1
and Theorem 4.4
in
[
10
]
, which asserts that a tuple
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})
of elements of a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra
B
determines a linear continuous map
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}\to B
which extends
the homomorphism
\theta\!:U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\to B
that maps
e_{j}
to
b_{j}
for each
j
.

So the main purpose is to prove Theorem
A.1
for general
k
.
The central idea of the proof is to construct a homomorphism from
U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})
to
a
\mathsf{DTS}
algebra admitting a linear extension to
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
that is a topological isomorphism. In other words, we need a family of triangular representations of
\mathfrak{f}_{k}
labelled by points of a union of manifolds and chosen so as to guarantee the topological injectivity. Because
\mathfrak{f}_{k}
is free, there is a huge selection of representations but our goal is to choose a family that is not too large, if possible. To do this we first generalize some constructions in
[
10
]
.

The argument for the case
k=2
, as presented in
[
10
]
, makes significant use of the upper right entry of the representation matrix; cf. the proof of Theorem
5.1
. For
k=2
, a single entry is sufficient since the set
\Delta
consists of a single element. However, in general, we require an algebra with many different elements similar to the matrix unit in the upper right corner. In the next section, we consider a specific path algebra containing such elements.

A family of triangular representations well behaved on the commutant

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
Q_{m}
be the quiver (which is actually just a directed graph) whose vertices are labelled by
(\delta,p)
with
\delta\in\Delta
and
p=1,\ldots,m+1
and
such that there is a unique arrow with the source
(\delta,p)
and the target
(\delta^{\prime},p+1)
for each

\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in\Delta
and
p=1,\ldots,m
. Since
Q_{m}
has finitely many vertices and arrows and no oriented cycles, the path algebra
\mathbb{R}Q_{m}
is of polynomial growth by Proposition
2.3
and we can use it for our purposes.

Denote the idempotent corresponding to
(\delta,p)
and the element corresponding to the arrow connecting
(\delta,p)
and
(\delta^{\prime},p+1)
by
q_{\delta p}
and
v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}
, respectively. Put also
v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}m+2}=0
.
We need the standard relations, which follow from the definition of
\mathbb{R}Q_{m}
:

(A.10)

q_{\delta p}v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}=v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}q_{\delta^{\prime},p+1}=v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\qquad(p=1,\ldots,m).

(Note that, unlike
[
13
]
, we multiply the paths in direct order.)

Put
Z_{m}\!:=\mathbb{R}^{|\Delta|(m+1)k}
and denote by
v
be the sum of all
v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}
and consider the family of Lie algebra homomorphisms
\{\theta_{\mathbf{t}}\!:\mathfrak{f}_{k}\to\mathbb{R}Q_{m};\,\mathbf{t}=(t_{\delta pi})\in Z_{m}\}

defined by

(A.11)

\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(e_{i})\!:=\sum_{\delta,p}t_{\delta pi}q_{\delta p}+v\qquad(i=1,\ldots,k).

The following two lemmas describe the action of
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}
on
V
and
T(V)
.
Let
I
be the two-sided ideal in
\mathbb{R}Q_{m}
generated by all
v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}
. Put also

(A.12)

s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pi}\!:=t_{\delta pi}-t_{\delta^{\prime},p+1,i}

and

(A.13)

y_{\delta_{0}\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\!:=s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pl_{0}}-s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pj_{0}}\qquad(\delta_{0}=(l_{0},j_{0})).

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\delta_{0}=(l_{0},j_{0})\in\Delta

and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{l_{0}})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{l_{0}}
. Then

(A.14)

\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\delta_{0}\beta})\in I\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\delta_{0}\beta})-\sum_{\delta,\delta^{\prime},p}y_{\delta_{0}\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\,s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pl_{0}}^{\beta_{l_{0}}}\,v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\in I^{2}

for every
\mathbf{t}
.

Proof.

We start by taking a look at the first commutators. Since all
q_{\delta p}
mutually commute, (
A.10
) and(
A.11
) imply that

(A.15)

\theta_{\mathbf{t}}([e_{l_{0}},e_{j_{0}}])=\sum y_{\delta_{0}\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\,v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}.

In particular,
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}([e_{l_{0}},e_{j_{0}}])\in I
.

To obtain (
A.14
) for the higher commutators note that, for given
i
,
\delta
,
\delta^{\prime}
and
p
,

(A.16)

[\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(e_{i}),v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}]-s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pi}v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\in I^{2}.

Then for arbitrary tuple
(r_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pi})
of real numbers, we have

(A.17)

[\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(e_{i}),\sum_{\delta,\delta^{\prime},p}r_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}]-\sum_{\delta,\delta^{\prime},p}s_{\delta\delta^{\prime}pi}r_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}v_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}\in I^{2}.

Since
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}([e_{l_{0}},e_{j_{0}}])\in I
, it follows from (
A.1
) that
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\delta_{0}\beta})\in I
.
Using now (
A.17
) first with
r_{\delta\delta^{\prime}p}=y_{\delta_{0}\delta\delta^{\prime}p}
and next making iterations, we conclude that the second relation in (
A.14
) is implied by (
A.15
).
∎

Put also

v_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\!:=v_{\delta_{1}\delta_{2}1}\cdots v_{\delta_{m}\delta_{m+1}m}\qquad(\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})\in\Delta^{m}).

Now, knowing how
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}
acts on the basis of
V
we can easily find the action on the basis of
T(V)
, i.e., on the products of the iterated commutators in (
A.2
).

Lemma A.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbf{t}=(t_{\delta pi})\in Z_{m}
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{m})\in\Delta^{m}
with
\delta_{p}=(l_{p},j_{p})
and
\beta^{p}=(\beta^{p}_{1},\ldots,\beta^{p}_{l_{p}})
for each
p=1,\ldots,m
.
Then

(A.18)

\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\beta^{1}\delta_{1}}\cdots g_{\beta^{m}\delta_{m}})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{m}}\prod_{p=1}^{m}y_{\delta_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p+1}p}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{l_{p}}s_{\delta^{\prime}_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p+1}pi}^{\beta_{i}^{p}}\right)\,v_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}},

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}=(\delta^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\delta^{\prime}_{m})
.

Proof.

It is obvious that

\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\beta^{1}\delta_{1}}\cdots g_{\beta^{m}\delta_{m}})=\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\beta^{1}\delta_{1}})\cdots\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\beta^{m}\delta_{m}}).

Now we apply Lemma
A.3
for each
\delta_{p}
.
Since
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(g_{\delta_{p}\beta})\in I
, the product on the right-hand side is equal modulo
I^{2}
to the product of
m
sums of the form given in (
A.14
).

Multiplying the sums, we have that only coefficients of terms of the form

(which we denote by

) do not vanish. Thus (
A.14
) implies (

).
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

(A)
Suppose that the hypotheses of Lemma

\widetilde{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{t}}(\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(h))=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{m}}\left(\prod_{p=1}^{m}y_{\delta_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p+1}p}\right)h(\lambda_{1}+t_{\delta^{\prime}_{1}11},\ldots,\lambda_{k}+t_{\delta^{\prime}_{1}1k},\,\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\delta^{\prime}\delta}})v_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}}.

\widetilde{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\Phi_{\emptyset}(h))=h(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})

(A) Suppose first that

and

.

The argument for Part (B) is straightforward and so is omitted.

Lemma A.6
.

\breve{m}\in\mathbb{N}

\breve{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\Delta^{\breve{m}}

It follows from (

\Phi_{\breve{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}(h)
is a sum of products of
\breve{m}+1
factors, where all factors except first are of the form
g_{\beta\delta}
. It follows from (
A.14
) that after applying of
\widetilde{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{t}}
each of the summands contains a product of the form
v_{\delta_{1}\delta_{2}1}\cdots v_{\delta_{\breve{m}}\delta_{\breve{m}+1}\breve{m}}
, which vanishes since
\breve{m}>m
.
∎

Proof of the structural theorem

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

We prove Parts (1) and (2) simultaneously. Since
\mathsf{DTS}
is stable under passing to closed subalgebras, it suffices to construct a homomorphism from
U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})
to a
\mathsf{DTS}
algebra admitting a linear extension to
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
that is a topological isomorphism.

For given
m\in\mathbb{N}
put

B_{m}\!:=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times Z_{m},\mathbb{R}Q_{m}),

where
Z_{m}\!:=\mathbb{R}^{|\Delta|(m+1)k}
. We also add the case of
m=0
, where
Z_{0}
is a single-point set and
\mathbb{R}Q_{0}=\mathbb{R}
by definition.
Consider the family

\{\widetilde{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{t}}\!:U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\to\mathbb{R}Q_{m}\!:\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\,\mathbf{t}\in Z_{m}\}

of homomorphisms defined in (
A.19
). Similarly, for
m=0
consider the family

\{\widetilde{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\!:U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\!:\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\},

where
e_{i}\mapsto\lambda_{i}
as above.
Varying
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\mathbf{t}
we have the homomorphism

\pi_{m}\!:U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\to B_{m}\!:\,\pi_{m}(a)(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{t})\!:=\widetilde{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{t}}(a)\qquad(a\in U(\mathfrak{f}_{k}))

for
m\geqslant 1
and similarly for
m=0
.

Consider also the homomorphism

\rho\!:U(\mathfrak{f}_{k})\to\prod_{m=0}^{\infty}B_{m}\!:a\mapsto(\pi_{m}(a)).

Since
\prod B_{m}
is a
\mathsf{PGL}
algebra, it follows from
[
10
, Theorem 4.4]
that
\rho
extends to a continuous linear map from
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
to
\prod B_{m}
.
By Definition
2.17
, every closed subalgebra of
\prod B_{m}
belongs to
\mathsf{DTS}
.
Thus to complete the proof we need to show that the extension of
\rho
is topologically injective.

By the definition (see (
A.9
) and (
A.7
)),
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k})\mathbin{\widehat{\otimes}}[T](C^{\infty}(X))
as a locally convex space, where
X=\coprod X_{\delta}
.
It follows from the explicit description of the formal tensor algebra that
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}=\prod_{m=0}^{\infty}C_{m}
, where
C_{m}\!:=C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times X^{m})
; see (
A.8
). Then
\rho
can be written as
\prod C_{m}\to\prod B_{m}
. It follows from Lemma
A.6
that
\rho
is determined by a lower triangular infinite matrix and so, by
[
10
, Lemma 4.12]
, it is sufficient to check that
C_{m}\to B_{m}
is topologically injective for every
m
.

Note first that
C_{0}\to B_{0}
is a topological isomorphism.
Assume next that
m\geqslant 1
. We treat
B_{m}
as a free
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times Z_{m})
-module and denote by
Y_{m}
the submodule
\sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}\in\Delta^{m}}B_{m}v_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}}
. It is obvious that
Y_{m}
is a direct factor in
B_{m}
. So it suffices to show that the composition of
\pi_{m}
and the projection of the whole
C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}
onto
Y_{m}
is topologically injective; see, e.g., Part (B) of
[
10
, Lemma 3.10]
. We denote this composition by
\pi^{\prime}_{m}
.

Lemma
A.5
asserts that (
A.22
) holds for every
\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\Delta^{m}
and every
h_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}[\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}]
. The existence of an extension to an ordered
C^{\infty}
-calculus given by
[
10
, Theorem 4.4]
implies that (
A.22
) holds for every
h_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times X_{\delta_{1}}\times\cdots\times X_{\delta_{m}})
. Note that the cardinality of
\Delta^{m}
equals
m|\Delta|
and identify
Y_{m}
with
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times Z_{m})^{m|\Delta|}
.
Identifying also
X^{m}
with
\coprod_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\Delta^{m}}(X_{\delta_{1}}\times\cdots\times X_{\delta_{m}})
and
C_{m}
with
\prod_{\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\Delta^{m}}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times X_{\delta_{1}}\times\cdots\times X_{\delta_{m}})
, we conclude that
\pi^{\prime}_{m}
has the form

(A.23)

(h_{\boldsymbol{\delta}})\mapsto\left((\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{t})\mapsto\sum_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\left(\prod_{p=1}^{m}y_{\delta_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p+1}p}\right)h_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\lambda_{1}+t_{\delta^{\prime}_{1}11},\ldots,\lambda_{k}+t_{\delta^{\prime}_{1}1k},\,\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\delta^{\prime}\delta}})\right)_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}},

where

y_{\delta_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p+1}p}=t_{\delta^{\prime}_{p},p,l_{\delta_{p}}}-t_{\delta^{\prime}_{p+1},p+1,l_{\delta_{p}}}-t_{\delta^{\prime}_{p},p,j_{\delta_{p}}}+t_{\delta^{\prime}_{p+1},p+1,j_{\delta_{p}}}\qquad(\delta_{p}=(l_{p},j_{p}))

by (
A.12
) and (
A.13
), and

\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\delta^{\prime}\delta}}=(t_{\delta pi}-t_{\delta^{\prime},p+1,i})\qquad(p=1,\ldots,m,\,i=1,\ldots,l_{p})

by (
A.21
).
The map in (
A.23
) is the composition of the map
C_{m}\to Y_{m}
induced by a finite-dimensional linear operator and the multiplication by the matrix
F\!:=\left(\prod_{p=1}^{m}y_{\delta_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p}\delta^{\prime}_{p+1}p}\right)_{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\delta}}
. (Here we fix a linear order on the set
\Delta^{m}
.)

The linear operator that induces the first map has the form

((\lambda_{i^{\prime}}),(t_{\delta pi}))\mapsto((\lambda_{i^{\prime}}+t_{\delta^{\prime}_{1}1{i^{\prime}}}),(t_{\delta^{\prime}_{p}pi}-t_{\delta^{\prime}_{p+1},p+1,i})),

where
i^{\prime}=1,\ldots,k
,
p=1,\ldots,m
and
i=1,\ldots,l_{p}
. It is easy to see that this operator is surjective. Therefore the first map is topologically injective.

The multiplication by
F
is injective since every tuple of functions that it takes to
0
obviously vanishes at the points, where
\det F\neq 0
, and also vanishes at the remaining points by continuity (since
\det F
is a polynomial). Also, the range of this map is a submodule of a finitely generated free
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\times Z_{m})
-module and, moreover, this submodule generated by a finite tuple of polynomials. It is well known that each such submodule is closed; see, e.g.,
[
52
, Chapitre VI, p. 119, Corollaire 1.5]
. Thus, we have a topological isomorphism onto the range by the inverse mapping theorem for Fréchet spaces. Being a composition of two topologically injective maps,
\pi^{\prime}_{m}\!:C_{m}\to Y_{m}
is topologically injective and so is
C_{m}\to B_{m}
.

Thus
C_{m}\to B_{m}
is topologically injective for every
m
. As noted above, this implies that
\rho\!:C^{\infty}_{\mathfrak{f}_{k}}\to\prod B_{m}
is also topologically injective and this completes the proof.
∎

Remark A.7
.

In
[
10
]
the author proved Theorem
A.1
for
k\leqslant 2
. Look at the case
k=2
.
Compared to the proof in the general case given here,
[
10
]
contains the following simplifications. First, it is easy to see that
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}Q_{m}\cong\mathrm{T}_{m+1}
. The second simplification is a technical one. Note that both
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(e_{1})
and
\theta_{\mathbf{t}}(e_{2})
defined in (
A.11
) can be written as sums of a diagonal matrix and a Jordan block with zero eigenvalue. But in
[
10
, eq. (4.9)]
a shorter formula was used: the image of
e_{1}
is assumed to be diagonal. The rest of the argument is very similar to that contained here.

Remark A.8
.

Recall that the
Whitney ideal

W_{Y/\mathbb{R}^{m}}
corresponding to a closed subset
Y
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the set of all functions in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
that are flat on
Y
, i.e., for each function in
W_{Y/\mathbb{R}^{m}}
there is an open neighbourhood of
Y
such that all partial derivatives vanish at every point. Elements of the
C^{\infty}
-differentiable algebra
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})/W_{Y/\mathbb{R}^{m}}
can be treated as ‘functions’ on a
C^{\infty}
-differentiable space, which is denoted by
\mathbf{W}_{Y/\mathbb{R}^{m}}
; see, e.g.,
[
42
, p. 60, Corollary 5.10]
. We denote this algebra by
C^{\infty}(\mathbf{W}_{Y/\mathbb{R}^{m}})
.

In at least four cases we can naturally identify the underlying locally convex space of a finitely
C^{\infty}
-generated algebra with
C^{\infty}(\mathbf{W}_{\Omega/\mathbb{R}^{m}})
for some
m
and
\Omega
: the quantum plane, the quantum group
\mathop{\mathrm{SL}}\nolimits_{q}(2,\mathbb{R})
and a triangular finite-dimensional Lie algebra (see Theorems 4.3, 4.10 and 4.12, in
[
10
]
) and also a deformation of the quantum group
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{f}_{1}
; see Theorem
5.1
. On the other hand, in the free algebra case the underlying space is more complicated (Theorem
A.2
). Thus it is interesting to know under which conditions the underlying space has the form
C^{\infty}(\mathbf{W}_{\Omega/\mathbb{R}^{m}})
. For example, is the finiteness of the Gelfand–Kirillov dimension is necessary?
