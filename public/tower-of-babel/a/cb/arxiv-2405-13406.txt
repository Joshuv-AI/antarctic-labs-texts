Smirnov-decompositions of vector fields
Alberto Rodríguez-Arenas, Jochen Wengenroth

Abstract
. We give streamlined proofs of theorems of S. Smirnov about the decomposition of vector fields of measures into curves.



1.
Introduction

An
n
-dimensional charge is a
\sigma
-additive function
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
from the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathbb{B}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
the components of such a vector measure are thus real-valued signed measures. Every charge
\mu
acts on
test fields

\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})
of bounded measurable functions as

\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{k}d\mu_{k}.

The aim of this note is to prove theorems of Smirnov
[
Smi93
]
characterizing charges which decompose as

\mu=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)

for a positive finite measure
\nu
on the set
K_{\ell}
of
1
-Lipschitz curves
\gamma:[0,\ell]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
[\gamma]
is
the charge acting on test fields as the work done by the field along
\gamma
,

\left\langle[\gamma],\varphi\right\rangle=\int_{[0,\ell]}\left\langle\varphi(\gamma(t)),\dot{\gamma}(t)\right\rangle d\lambda^{1}(t).

Such decompositions are crucial in geometric measure theory (charges are the
1-currents
of order
0
)
and in the theory of optimal transport
(where
\nu
is a
transport plan
)
but combined with the Hahn-Banach theorem they also have direct applications in approximation theory. As an illustration we give a very simple proof
of a result of Havin and Smirnov that for a compact set
M
like the Koch curve in which every rectifiable curve is constant and all

f,f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C(M)

one can approximate the function
f
uniformly on
M
by some
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\partial_{k}\psi
approximate
f_{k}
.

During the first 10 years since its publication Smirnov’s theorems did not find much attention. This changed when
[
Smi93
]
was mentioned by
Brezis and Bourgain in
[
BB04
]
and later on when the relevance for the theory of optimal transport was realized, see, e.g., Santambrogio’s book

[
San15
]

(Smirnov’s scientific laurels like the fields medal probably also boosted the interest in his early work).

Meanwhile, there are variants of Smirnov’s theorems
[
PS12
,
PS13
]
in the context of metric currents in the sense of Ambrosio and Kirchheim
[
AK00
]

but the classical approach of Smirnov is still interesting. However, at least the authors of the present note had a very hard time
to understand the details of
[
Smi93
]
and we do not know of a streamlined presentation. In view of the importance of the results for
several mathematical fields we will thus try to give detailed comprehensible proofs in Smirnov’s spirit.

The main ingredients are a version of Liouville’s theorem that flows of divergence free vector fields are volume preserving, approximation and
smoothing by convolution together with the Arzelá-Ascoli theorem, and a dimension trick to reduce to the divergence free case.

2.
Charges and curves

We recall basic facts about charges which can be found, e.g., in Schechter’s
Handbook of Analysis and its Foundations

[
Sch97
, §29]

(although the term
charge
is used in a different sense there)
or for the case
n=2
in Rudin’s classic
Real and Complex Analysis

[
Rud87
, chapter 6]
.

The variation measure of an
n
-dimensional charge
\mu
is defined for Borel sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)=\sup\left\{\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}|\mu(E_{k})|:E_{k}\subseteq E\text{ are pairwise disjoint Borel sets}\right\},

the similarity of the symbols
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\cdot\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
and the euclidean norm
|\cdot|
should not cause confusion, Schechter denotes the variation measure
as
/\mu/
whereas Smirnov writes
\|\mu\|
.
This is indeed a finite positive measure and
\Var(\mu)=\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is called the
total variation
of
\mu
.

The Radon-Nikodym theorem yields a measurable density
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|f(x)|=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu=f\cdot\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
, i.e.,

\mu(E)=\int_{E}fd\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}

for all
E\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
where the integral is defined component-wise. This
polar decomposition
of
\mu
suggests to define
\mu
-integrals as

\int gd\mu=\int gfd\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
and

\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle=\int\left\langle f,\varphi\right\rangle d\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}

for test fields
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})
with bounded measurable components (again, we use the same symbol for the action of
\mu
and for the euclidean scalar product).

The regularity of finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
yields that
\mu
is already determined by its action on fields
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
of (classical) test functions and that
for open sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)=\sup\{\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle:\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(E)^{n}\text{ with }|\varphi|\leq 1\}.

The components
\mu_{k}
of a charge are distributions (of order
0
) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and thus have distributional partial derivatives
\partial_{j}\mu_{k}
defined
by
\left\langle\partial_{j}\mu_{k},\psi\right\rangle=-\left\langle\mu_{k},\partial_{j}\psi\right\rangle
for
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
The divergence of
\mu
is
\Div(\mu)=\partial_{1}\mu_{1}+\cdots+\partial_{n}\mu_{n}

so that for
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\left\langle\Div(\mu),\psi\right\rangle=-\left\langle\mu,\nabla\psi\right\rangle

for the gradient
\nabla\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
.

For the interpretation of charges as elements of the dual of
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
it is enough that
\mu
is a
local charge
, i.e.,
\mu
is a
\sigma
-additive

\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued function on the ring of all bounded Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The same definition of
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
as for charges then yields
a locally finite variation measure. In particular, for every locally finite positive Borel measure
\rho
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every field

\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n})
with locally
\rho
-integrable components
\phi\cdot\rho
is a local charge. By the polar decomposition,
every local charge is of this form.

Every
1
-Lipschitz curve
\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
|\gamma(s)-\gamma(t)|\leq|s-t|
for all
s,t\in[a,b]
, yields a charge
[\gamma]
which acts on
test fields as a curve integral

\left\langle[\gamma],\varphi\right\rangle=\int_{[a,b]}\left\langle\varphi\circ\gamma,\dot{\gamma}\right\rangle d\lambda^{1}

where
\lambda^{1}
is the Lebesgue measure and
\dot{\gamma}
denotes the derivative of
\gamma
which exists
\lambda^{1}
-almost everywhere
by Rademacher’s theorem. The components of
[\gamma]
considered as a vector measure are the image measures or push-forwards of

\dot{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\lambda^{1}
under
\gamma
, i.e.,

\gamma_{\#}(\dot{\gamma}_{k}\cdot\lambda^{1})(A)=\int_{\{\gamma\in A\}}\dot{\gamma}_{k}d\lambda^{1}.

The mapping
\gamma\mapsto[\gamma]
fails to be injective for two different reasons: On the one hand, the curve integral is invariant under
reparametrizations, i.e.,
[\gamma]=[\gamma\circ h]
for increasing differentiable bijections
h:[\alpha,\beta]\to[a,b]
. This non-uniqueness could be
remedied by considering suitable equivalence classes of curves. On the other hand, parts of the curve integrals could cancel if
\gamma

goes back and forth
along some part of the curve, for example, the curve defined by
\gamma(t)=t
for
t\in[0,1]
and
\gamma(t)=2-t
for

t\in[1,2]
yields the
1
-dimensional charge
[\gamma]=0
. This phenomenon makes it difficult to determine the variation measure

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
in general. For open sets
E
we get from the description of
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)
as the supremum of
\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle

with
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(E)^{n}
with
|\varphi|\leq 1
that

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)\leq\int_{\{\gamma\in E\}}|\dot{\gamma}|d\lambda^{1}\text{ and }\Var([\gamma])\leq L(\gamma)

where
L(\gamma)=\int_{[a,b]}|\dot{\gamma}|d\lambda^{1}
is the length of
\gamma
.
Outer regularity of finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
yields the same inequality for all Borel sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover,
if

\Var([\gamma])=L(\gamma)
we can pass to complements and we thus get

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Var([\gamma])=L(\gamma)\text{ if and only if }\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(A)=\int_{\{\gamma\in A\}}|\dot{\gamma}|d\lambda^{1}\text{ for all $A\in\mathbb{B}^{n}$.}

One can easily calculate the divergence of
[\gamma]
:
Since Lipschitz curves are absolutely continuous, i.e., they satisfy the fundamental theorem of calculus, we have

\Div([\gamma])(\psi)=-\int_{[a,b]}\left\langle\nabla\psi\circ\gamma,\dot{\gamma}\right\rangle d\lambda^{1}=-(\psi\circ\gamma)\big|_{a}^{b}=(\delta_{\gamma(a)}-\delta_{\gamma(b)})(\psi).

For fixed
\ell>0
(in most situations, the case
\ell=1
is enough) we denote

K_{\ell}=\{\gamma:[0,\ell]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}:\gamma\text{ is Lipschitz with }|\dot{\gamma}|\leq 1\text{ $\lambda^{1}$-a.e.}\}.

Every rectifiable curve of length
\ell
can be reparametrized to become an element of
K_{\ell}
even with
|\dot{\gamma}|=1

\lambda^{1}
-a.e.,
but we do not need this arclength parametrization.
To give a precise meaning to the Smirnov decomposition
\mu=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)
we consider the uniform
norm
\|\gamma\|=\sup\{|\gamma(t)|:t\in[0,\ell]\}
on
K_{\ell}\subseteq C([0,\ell],\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The Arzelà-Ascoli theorem implies that

\{\gamma\in K_{\ell}:|\gamma(0)|\leq m\}
are compact in
C([0,\ell],\mathbb{R}^{n})
so that
K_{\ell}
is
\sigma
-compact. It is convenient to
compactify the set of curves: We consider a one-point compactification
i:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\cup\{\infty\}
, e.g., the inverse stereographic
projection
i:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to S^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:|x|=1\}
with the north pole
\infty
, and
I:C([0,\ell],\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C([0,\ell],\hat{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,

\gamma\mapsto i\circ\gamma
where the range has again the topology of uniform convergence.
From
|\gamma(t)|\leq|\gamma(0)|+\ell
for
\gamma\in K_{\ell}
it easily follows that every
\gamma\in\hat{K}_{\ell}=\overline{I(K_{\ell})}
is either constant
with value
\infty
or belongs to
I(K_{\ell})
. Again, the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem implies the compactness of

\hat{K}_{\ell}
which is convenient to get finite Borel measures
\nu
on
K_{\ell}
from Alaoglu’s theorem combined with
the Riesz-Kakutani representation theorem for the dual of
C(\hat{K}_{\ell})
.

Integrals
\mu=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)
are meant in a weak sense, i.e., for continuous fields
\varphi=(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})

with compact support we have

\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle=\int_{K_{\ell}}\left\langle[\gamma],\varphi\right\rangle d\nu(\gamma).

For this integral to be well-defined we need the Borel measurability of
\gamma\mapsto\left\langle[\gamma],\varphi\right\rangle
. The continuity of
\varphi
yields
that the curve integrals defining
\left\langle[\gamma],\varphi\right\rangle
are limits of Riemann-Stieltjes sums

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell m}\left\langle\varphi(\gamma(k/m)),\gamma(k/m)-\gamma((k-1)/m)\right\rangle

for
m\to\infty
, and for fixed
m
, these sums depend
continuously on
\gamma\in K_{\ell}
with respect to the uniform norm. Since indicator functions of open sets are increasing limits of continuous
functions a monotone class argument yields the measurability of
\gamma\mapsto\left\langle[\gamma],\varphi\right\rangle
for
all
\varphi
with bounded measurable components and that the
displayed equality above extends to such vector fields.

We can now state the main theorems from
[
Smi93
]
(the first one together with proposition
3.2
below is Smirnov’s Theorem A and the
second one is a version of his Theorem C):

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
\ell>0
and every
n
-dimensional charge
\mu
with
\Div(\mu)=0
there is a finite positive measure
\nu

on
K_{\ell}
with

\mu=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma),\;\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int_{K_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu(\gamma),

and
\nu
-almost every
\gamma\in K_{\ell}
has values in
\supp(\mu)
and satisfies
\Var([\gamma])=L(\gamma)=\ell
.

Theorem 2.2
.

An
n
-dimensional charge
\mu
has a decomposition
\mu=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)
for some (or, equivalently, all)
\ell>0

and a finite positive measure
\nu
on
K_{\ell}
if and only if
\Div(\mu)
is a real signed measure. Then there is a decomposition such that

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int_{K_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu(\gamma)

and
\nu
-almost every
\gamma\in K_{\ell}
has values in
\supp(\mu)
and satisfies
\Var([\gamma])=L(\gamma)
.

The difference to the divergence free case is thus that the length of the representing curves is only bounded by
\ell
.
Before turning to the proofs of these theorems let us show the application mentioned in the introduction (where
\|\cdot\|_{M}

denotes the uniform norm on
M
).

Theorem 2.3

(Havin-Smirnov)
.

Let
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set such that all rectifiable curves in
M
are constant. Then, for any
f,f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in C(M)
and any
\varepsilon>0
,
there is
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\|f-\psi\|_{M}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|f_{j}-\partial_{j}\psi\|_{M}<\varepsilon.

The somewhat surprising aspect of this theorem about
free approximation
is that although
M
can be quite big, e.g., in terms of its Hausdorff dimension,
knowledge of
\partial_{j}\psi|_{M}
does not yield any estimates for
\psi
on
M
which one would get in presence of rectifiable curves
in
M
: If
\gamma
is a curve in
M
joining
x,y
, the mean value inequality allows to estimate
|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|
by
the norms of
\partial_{j}\psi
times the length of
\gamma
(this argument shows the necessity of the absence of
rectifiable curves in
M
for the free approximation). A similar application to the density of restrictions

\{\psi|_{M}:\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}
in a suitably defined space
C^{1}(M)
of continuously differentiable functions can be seen in

[
FLW20
]
which was the main motivation for the present authors to get a better understanding of Smirnov’s results.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

We show with the aid of the Hahn-Banach theorem that
I:\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\longrightarrow C(M)^{n+1}
defined by

\psi\mapsto(\psi|_{M},\partial_{1}\psi|_{M}\ldots\partial_{n}\psi|_{M})
has dense range.
For
\Phi
in the dual of
C(M)^{n+1}
with
\Phi\circ I=0
we thus have to show
\Phi=0
.
By the Riesz-Kakutani representation theorem there are (signed) Borel measures
\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{n}
on
M
with

\Phi(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\int_{M}f_{j}d\mu_{j}

for all
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\in C(M)
.
For the charge
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

0=\Phi(I(\psi))=\int_{M}\psi d\mu_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{M}\partial_{j}\psi d\mu_{j}=\left\langle\mu_{0}-\Div(\mu),\psi\right\rangle.

Thus,
\Div(\mu)=\mu_{0}
so that Theorem
2.2
(for fixed
\ell>0
) yields a decomposition

\mu=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)

where
\nu
-almost all curves have values in
M
. Since by assumption, rectifiable curves in
M
are constant
and hence satisfy
[\gamma]=0
,
we obtain

\mu=0
,
\mu_{0}=\Div(\mu)=0
and thus
\Phi=0
.
∎

3.
Proof for divergence free charges

A main ingredient in this case is a version of Liouville’s theorem that flows of divergence free smooth vector fields are volume preserving.
Let us recall that a linearly bounded vector field
\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n})\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
, i.e.,
|\phi(x)|\leq c(1+|x|)
for some constant

c>0
and all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, has a globally defined flow
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, i.e.,
u(\cdot,x)
is the unique solution of the initial
value problem
\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\phi(u(t,x))
and
u(0,x)=x
. Depending on cultural context, this theorem is called after
some subset of
\{
Cauchy, Lipschitz, Picard, Lindelöf
\}
. For a proof which deduces existence as well as the differentiability of
u

from the inverse function theorem in Banach spaces, we refer to the book of Duistermaat and Kolk
[
DK00
, appendix B]
.
The flow equation
u(t+s,x)=u(t,u(s,x))
implies that all
u(t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are diffeomorphisms with inverse
u(-t,\cdot)
.

Every locally finite positive Borel measure
\varrho
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then yields a local charge
\phi\cdot\varrho
which has a distributional divergence.
For
\varrho=\lambda^{n}
we have
\Div(\phi\cdot\lambda^{n})=\Div(\phi)\cdot\lambda^{n}
where
\Div(\phi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\partial_{k}\phi_{k}
is the classical
divergence of
\phi
.

Proposition 3.1

(Liouville’s theorem)
.

Let
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
be a linearly bounded vector field with corresponding flow
u
. Then every locally finite positive
Borel measure
\varrho
with
\Div(\phi\cdot\varrho)=0
is invariant under
u
, i.e.,
\varrho_{t}=u(t,\cdot)_{\#}(\varrho)
is independent of
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

For
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it is enough to show that

\int\varphi d\varrho_{t}=\int\varphi(u(t,x))d\varrho(x)

is independent of
t
which we will show by differentiation of this parameter integral for
\psi(t,x)=\varphi(u(t,x))
.
The flow equation yields
\psi(t,u(-t,x))=\varphi(x)
, and hence

0=\partial_{t}\left(\psi(t,u(-t,x))\right)=\partial_{t}\psi(t,u(-t,x))-\left\langle\nabla_{x}\psi(t,u(-t,x)),\phi(u(-t,x))\right\rangle.

The surjectivity of
u(-t,\cdot)
thus implies for all
(t,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\partial_{t}\psi(t,y)=\left\langle\nabla_{y}\psi(t,y),\phi(y)\right\rangle.

To justify the differentiability under the integral we fix a compact interval
J=[-m,m]
and show that
\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)
is dominated by some

\rho
-integrable function
g(x)
which is independent of
t\in J
.
For
t\in J
, we have

\supp(\psi(t,\cdot))\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:u(t,x)\in\supp(\varphi)\}=u(-t,\supp(\varphi))\subseteq u(J\times\supp(\varphi)),

where this last set
K
is compact by the continuity of
u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The continuity of
\partial_{t}\psi
thus implies that

|\partial\psi_{t}(t,\cdot)|
is bounded by a constant (independent of
t
) times the indicator function of
K
which is

\varrho
-integrable.

We can thus differentiate with respect to
t\in J
under the integral and get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int\psi(t,x)d\varrho(x)

\displaystyle=\int\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)d\varrho(x)=\int\left\langle\nabla_{x}\psi(t,x),\phi(x)\right\rangle d\varrho(x)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\Div(\phi\cdot\varrho),\psi(t,\cdot)\right\rangle=0.\qed

Proof of theorem
2.1
.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\neq 0
be a divergence free charge and
\ell>0
. We take a smooth and strictly positive function
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

with integral
1
, e.g., a Gaussian kernel, and define the convolution
\tilde{\mu}=\mu*k=(\mu_{1}*k,\ldots,\mu_{n}*k)
which is a smooth vector field
with
|\mu*k|\leq\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}*k
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
is a positive measure and
k
is strictly positive also
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}*k
is strictly positive
and we can define
\phi=\mu*k/(\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}*k)
. This is a smooth vector field which is even bounded and hence linearly bounded
so that the proposition applies to the flow
u
of
\phi
and the measure
\varrho=(\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}*k)\cdot\lambda^{n}
(which is finite as the convolution of

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
with the probability measure
k\cdot\lambda^{n}
so that
\varrho(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\Var(\mu)
) because

\Div(\phi\cdot\varrho)=\Div((\mu*k)\cdot\lambda^{n})=(\Div(\mu)*k)\cdot\lambda^{n}=0.

We will show now that
\tilde{\mu}=\mu*k
decomposes into the curves
\gamma_{x}:[0,\ell]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t\mapsto u(t,x)
.
Indeed, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
, the invariance of
\varrho
under
u(t,\cdot)
yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}[\gamma_{x}](\varphi)d\varrho(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\ell}\left\langle\varphi(u(t,x)),\partial_{t}u(t,x)\right\rangle dtd\varrho(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\langle\varphi(u(t,x)),\phi(u(t,x))\right\rangle d\varrho(x)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\ell}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\langle\varphi,\phi\right\rangle d\varrho dt=\ell\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\langle\varphi,\phi\right\rangle(\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}*k)d\lambda^{n}

\displaystyle=\ell\left\langle\tilde{\mu},\varphi\right\rangle.

Form
|\phi|\leq 1
we get
\gamma_{x}\in K_{\ell}
so that the image measure
\nu
of
\varrho/\ell
under the map

\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to K_{\ell}
,
x\mapsto\gamma_{x}

satisfies
\nu(K_{\ell})=\Var(\mu)/\ell
and

\tilde{\mu}=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma).

For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we set
k_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{n}}k(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
and obtain measures
\nu_{\varepsilon}
on
K_{\ell}
with

\nu_{\varepsilon}(K_{\ell})=\Var(\mu)/\ell
and

\mu*k_{\varepsilon}=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu_{\varepsilon}(\gamma).

For
\varepsilon\to 0
we have
\mu*k_{\varepsilon}\to\mu
in the sense of distributions, i.e.,
\left\langle\mu*k_{\varepsilon},\varphi\right\rangle\to\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle
for all
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
.
On the other hand we consider
\nu_{\varepsilon}
as measures on
\hat{K}_{\ell}
with

and a positive measure

\int fd\nu_{\varepsilon_{k}}\to\int fd\nu
for all

For each

, we will show

\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma](\varphi)d\nu(\gamma).

This follows from the weak
∗

\gamma\mapsto[\gamma](\varphi)=\int_{[0,\ell]}\left\langle\varphi\circ\gamma,\dot{\gamma}\right\rangle d\lambda^{1}

(which is
0
for the curve with constant value
\infty

\varphi
has compact support).

For a uniformly convergent sequence
\gamma_{j}\to\gamma
it is enough to find a subsequence
j(i)
with
[\gamma_{j(i)}](\varphi)\to[\gamma](\varphi)
.
The case of the curve with constant value
\infty
is clear since then
\varphi\circ\gamma_{j}=0
for

big enough. Otherwise,
the sequence
\gamma_{j}
is uniformly bounded. Since
|\dot{\gamma}_{j}|\leq 1
the sequence
\dot{\gamma}_{j}
is bounded in the
Hilbert space

and has thus a weakly convergent subsequence
\dot{\gamma}_{j(i)}\to g
for some
g\in L_{2}([0,\ell])^{n}
.
Since
\gamma_{j}
are absolutely continuous we obtain for

\gamma(t)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\gamma_{j(i)}(0)+\int_{[0,t]}\dot{\gamma}_{j(i)}d\lambda^{1}=\gamma(0)+\int_{[0,t]}gd\lambda^{1},

which implies
\dot{\gamma}=g

\lambda^{1}
-a.e. We therefore obtain

[\gamma_{j(i)}](\varphi)=\int_{[0,\ell]}\left\langle\varphi\circ\gamma_{j(i)}-\varphi\circ\gamma,\dot{\gamma}_{j(i)}\right\rangle d\lambda^{1}+\int_{[0,\ell]}\left\langle\varphi\circ\gamma,\dot{\gamma}_{j(i)}\right\rangle d\lambda^{1}\to[\gamma](\varphi)

because the first integrand converges uniformly to

and the second integral converges because of the weak convergence in

of

\dot{\gamma}_{j(i)}\to\dot{\gamma}
.

We will next show that
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu(\gamma)
and that
\nu
-almost all curves have length
\ell

(till know we only know that their length is
\leq\ell
). For an open set

and

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)\leq\int_{K_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)d\nu(\gamma)

so that none of these inequalities can be strict. This implies
\Var{[\gamma]}=L(\gamma)=\ell
for
\nu
-almost all
\gamma\in K_{\ell}
,
\nu(K_{\ell})=\Var(\mu)/\ell
,
and also that
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu(\gamma)
by the same argument as in (
2.1
) (two positive

for all open sets and

To show finally that
\nu
-almost every curve has values in the support
S
of
\mu
we first recall that

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)\leq\int_{\{\gamma\in E\}}|\dot{\gamma}|d\lambda^{1}.

For
\gamma\in K_{\ell}
with
\Var([\gamma])=L(\gamma)=\ell
we thus get

|\dot{\gamma}|=1

\lambda^{1}
-a.e. and equality in the previous line. For

we obtain

0=\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E)=\int_{K_{\ell}}\lambda^{1}(\{\gamma\in E\})d\nu(\gamma)

which implies
\lambda^{1}(\{\gamma\in E\})=0
for
\nu
-almost all
\gamma
. The
open
sets
\{\gamma\in E\}
are thus empty
for
\nu
-almost all
\gamma
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The decomposition in theorem
2.1
also satisfies

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\ell\int_{K_{\ell}}\delta_{\gamma(0)}d\nu(\gamma)=\ell\int_{K_{\ell}}\delta_{\gamma(\ell)}d\nu(\gamma).

Proof.

We exploit the construction of
\nu
as a weak limit of
\nu_{\varepsilon}
defined as the push forward
\Gamma_{\#}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}/\ell)
for

\varrho_{\varepsilon}=(\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}*k_{\varepsilon})\cdot\lambda^{n}

under the map
\Gamma(x)=u(\cdot,x)
. For the evaluation

,
\gamma\mapsto\gamma(0)
we thus have

.

For an open set
E
and
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(E)^{n}
with

we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu,\varphi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle\mu_{\varepsilon_{k}},\varphi\right\rangle\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\int\left|\varphi(x)\right|d\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu_{\varepsilon_{k}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(x)\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\int\left|\varphi(x)\right|d\varrho_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x)

4.
Charges with the divergence being a measure

In this section we prove theorem
2.2
following Smirnov’s arguments more closely than in the previous section.
The necessity part follows from
\Div(\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma))=\int_{K_{\ell}}(\delta_{\gamma(0)}-\delta_{\gamma(\ell)})d\nu(\gamma)
.

Let now
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
be an

n
-dimensional charge whose divergence is a signed measure. We define an
(n+1)
-dimensional charge

\mu^{+}=\big(\mu\otimes(\delta_{0}-\delta_{\ell}),-\Div(\mu)\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{[0,\ell]}\big).

The product of a charge and a signed measure is meant component-wise and the product of two signed measures can be defined
using the polar decomposition: For
\varrho=r\cdot\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\varrho\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
and
\sigma=s\cdot\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\sigma\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
the product is

\varrho\otimes\sigma=(r\otimes s)\cdot(\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\varrho\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\otimes\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\sigma\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|})
with
r\otimes s(x,t)=r(x)s(t)
.

The restriction of
\mu^{+}
to the plane
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
gives back
\mu
, i.e., for
A\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and the projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto the first
n
components we have

\pi\circ\mu^{+}(A\times\{0\})=\mu(A).

Denoting the points of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as
(x,t)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
we write

\nabla\psi=(\nabla_{x}\psi,\partial_{t}\psi)
. It is easy to see that
\mu^{+}
is divergence free: For
\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
we have

\displaystyle-\left\langle\Div(\mu^{+}),\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu^{+},\nabla\psi\right\rangle=\left\langle\mu\otimes(\delta_{0}-\delta_{\ell}),\nabla_{x}\psi\right\rangle-\left\langle\Div(\mu)\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{[0,\ell]},\partial_{t}\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu,\nabla_{x}\psi(\cdot,0)-\nabla_{x}\psi(\cdot,\ell)\right\rangle-\left\langle\Div(\mu),\int_{[0,\ell]}\partial_{t}\psi(\cdot,s)d\lambda_{1}(s)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Div(\mu),\psi(\cdot,\ell)-\psi(\cdot,0)\right\rangle-\left\langle\Div(\mu),\psi(\cdot,\ell)-\psi(\cdot,0)\right\rangle=0.

Theorem
2.1
yields a finite positive measure
\nu^{+}
on
K_{\ell}^{+}
(the analogue of
K_{\ell}
for curves in

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
) with

\mu^{+}=\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}}[\gamma]d\nu^{+}(\gamma),\quad\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu^{+}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\gamma\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu^{+}(\gamma),

and
\nu^{+}
-almost all
\gamma\in K_{\ell}^{+}
satisfy
\Var([\gamma])=L(\gamma)=\ell
.

In order to get a representation of
\mu
we will show for
\nu^{+}
-almost all curves
\gamma
that
\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}\}

is an interval
[\alpha,\beta]
and that the curves
\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\gamma(\max\{\alpha,\min\{t,\beta\}\})
(which rest at
\gamma(\alpha)

for
t\in[0,\alpha]
, then follow
\gamma
till they stay at
\gamma(\beta)
)
represent
\mu
.
We first analyse the behaviour of the curves in the open
strip

S=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\ell)
:
We have

\mu^{+}|_{S}=(0,-\Div(\mu)\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)})\text{ and }\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu^{+}|_{S}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\Div(\mu)\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)}

so that the polar decomposition is
\mu^{+}|_{S}=f\cdot\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\Div(\mu)\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)}
where
f(x,t)=(0,g(x))
with

-\Div(\mu)=g\cdot\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\Div(\mu)\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}
. We set

E_{\pm}=\{x\in\supp(\Div(\mu)):g(x)=\pm 1\}

so that
\supp(\Div(\mu))=E_{+}\cup E_{-}
because
|g(x)|=1
.

We claim for
\nu^{+}
-almost all curves and
\lambda^{1}
-almost all
t\in[0,\ell]

that
\gamma(t)\in E_{\pm}
implies
\dot{\gamma}_{n+1}(t)=\pm 1
.

Let
\chi_{+}
be the indicator function of the set
E_{+}\times(0,\ell)
. Since the last component of
\dot{\gamma}
satisfies

\dot{\gamma}_{n+1}\in[-1,1]
for
\gamma\in K_{\ell}^{+}
we have

\displaystyle\nu^{+}\otimes

\displaystyle\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)}(\{(\gamma,t):\gamma(t)\in E_{+}\times(0,\ell)\})=\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}\times(0,\ell)}\chi_{+}(\gamma(t))d\nu^{+}\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)}(\gamma,t)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}}\int_{[0,\ell]}(\chi_{+}\circ\gamma)(t)\dot{\gamma}_{n+1}(t)d\lambda^{1}(t)d\nu^{+}(\gamma)=\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}}\left\langle[\gamma],(0,\ldots,0,\chi_{+})\right\rangle d\nu^{+}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mu^{+},(0,\ldots,0,\chi_{+})\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\chi_{+}\ d(-\Div(\mu)\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\chi_{+}gd(\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\Div(\mu)\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)})=\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu^{+}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E_{+}\times(0,\ell))

\displaystyle=\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(E_{+}\times(0,\ell))d\nu^{+}(\gamma)=\int_{K_{\ell}^{+}}\lambda^{1}(\gamma^{-1}(E_{+}\times(0,\ell)))d\nu^{+}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\nu^{+}\otimes\lambda^{1}|_{(0,\ell)}(\{(\gamma,t):\gamma(t)\in E_{+}\times(0,\ell)\}),

where the next-to-last equality comes from (

).
It follows that the only inequality above is also an equality and this implies that, for
\nu^{+}
-almost all
\gamma
, we have

\chi_{+}(\gamma(t))\dot{\gamma}_{n+1}(t)=\chi_{+}(\gamma(t))
for
\lambda^{1}
-almost all

.
The same arguments show the analogous statement for

so that we have proved the claim.

Since curves in

are absolutely continuous with
|\dot{\gamma}|=1

\lambda^{1}
-a.e., we obtain that
\nu^{+}
-almost all curves are

when they pass through

\gamma(t)\in E_{\pm}\times(0,\ell)

th unit vector

in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

and all

is the maximal interval containing

.

We next define

For

almost every curve

and

\dot{\gamma}(t)=(0,\ldots,0,\pm 1)
for

For

, we thus get

, and since

-almost all curves in

do not meet the plane

we obtain

\displaystyle(\mu,0)(A\times\{0\})

\displaystyle=\mu^{+}|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}}(A\times\{0\})=\int_{D}\left\langle[\gamma],\chi_{A\times\{0\}}\right\rangle d\nu^{+}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\left\langle([\pi_{H}\circ\gamma],\chi_{A}\right\rangle,0)d\nu^{+}(\gamma).

Taking the first

\mu=\int_{D}[\pi_{H}\circ\gamma]d\nu^{+}(\gamma)=\int_{K_{\ell}}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)

where

is the push forward of

under the mapping

defined by

\tilde{\gamma}(t)=\pi(\gamma(\max\{\alpha(\gamma),\min\{t,\beta(\gamma)\}\})
. Then

-almost all curves in

have values in

and
satisfy

-almost all curves

have this property. Finally, we have
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int_{K_{\ell}}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\mu(\gamma)

because of the corresponding property for
\mu^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(A)=\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu^{+}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}(A\times\{0\})
.

5.
The role of the length of the curves

In applications like theorem
2.3
or in
[
FLW20
]
it is enough to have theorem
2.2
for a fixed
\ell
.
In
[
Smi93
]
, curves of length
\ell\to\infty
are used in two situations. In Theorem B of
[
Smi93
]
Smirnov shows
a decomposition of divergence free charges into
elementary solenoids
which are Lipschitz curves
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

such that the limits

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{1}{2s}\int_{-s}^{s}\left\langle\varphi(\gamma(s)),\dot{\gamma}(s)\right\rangle ds

exist for all
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{n}
. In contrast to theorem
2.1
(where the curves of fixed length
\ell
are hardly ever
closed and thus have a non-vanishing divergence
\delta_{\gamma(0)}-\delta_{\gamma(\ell)}
) these elementary solenoids are divergence free.
Smirnov gives examples of even smooth three-dimensional charges which cannot be decomposed into closed curves.

The other situation where curves of arbitrary length are used is Smirnov’s Theorem C which is a slightly different version of
theorem
2.2
. Using the methods of the previous section he proves that every charge
\mu
can be decomposed
\mu=\sigma+\tau

with a divergence free part
\sigma
to which theorem
2.1
applies and a charge
\tau
which decomposes as

\tau=\int_{K}[\gamma]d\nu(\gamma)
with
K=\bigcup_{\ell>0}K_{\ell}
such that

\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\mu\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\sigma\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}+\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\tau\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|},\,\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\tau\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int_{K}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}[\gamma]\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu(\gamma),\text{ and }\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\Div(\mu)\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}=\int_{K}\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}\Div([\gamma])\boldsymbol{|}\!\!\boldsymbol{|}d\nu.

This decomposition is achieved by a recursive approximation process which starts as
\mu=\mu_{1}+\tau_{1}
where –
in the situation of the proof of theorem
2.2
– one can take

\tau_{1}=\int_{\tilde{D}}[\pi\circ\gamma]d\nu^{+}(\gamma)

with the set
\tilde{D}=\{\gamma\in D:\gamma(\ell)\in E_{+}\times(0,\ell)\}
and
\mu_{1}=\mu-\tau_{1}
. In the next step, this
procedure is applied to get
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}+\tau_{2}
. Smirnov shows that one can choose curve lengths
\ell_{k}

in such a way that the charges
\mu_{k}
converge (in the total variation norm) to a divergence free charge
\sigma

and that also the series
\tau=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}
converges.
