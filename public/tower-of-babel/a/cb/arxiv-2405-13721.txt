Connectivity Shapes Implicit Regularization in Matrix Factorization Models for Matrix Completion
Zhiwei Bai, Jiajie Zhao, Yaoyu Zhang

Abstract
Matrix factorization models have been extensively studied as a valuable test-bed for understanding the implicit biases of overparameterized models. Although both low nuclear norm and low rank regularization have been studied for these models, a unified understanding of when, how, and why they achieve different implicit regularization effects remains elusive. In this work, we systematically investigate the implicit regularization of matrix factorization for solving matrix completion problems. We empirically discover that the connectivity of observed data plays a crucial role in the implicit bias, with a transition from low nuclear norm to low rank as data shifts from disconnected to connected with increased observations. We identify a hierarchy of intrinsic invariant manifolds in the loss landscape that guide the training trajectory to evolve from low-rank to higher-rank solutions. Based on this finding, we theoretically characterize the training trajectory as following the hierarchical invariant manifold traversal process, generalizing the characterization of Li et al. (2020) to include the disconnected case. Furthermore, we establish conditions that guarantee minimum nuclear norm, closely aligning with our experimental findings, and we provide a dynamics characterization condition for ensuring minimum rank. Our work reveals the intricate interplay between data connectivity, training dynamics, and implicit regularization in matrix factorization models.

Introduction

Overparameterized models have the capacity to easily fit data with random labels
(
Zhang et al., 2017
;
Zhang et al., 2021
)
. However, in real-world applications, models with more parameters than training samples still generalize well. This has led researchers to hypothesize that overparameterized models undergo implicit regularization, favoring certain functions as outputs. Overparameterized matrix factorization models,
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
with
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B}),\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, have served as a simplified test-bed for studying this implicit regularization. In the context of matrix completion problems like the Netflix challenge, these models aim to find a low-rank completion of a partially observed matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Prior works have offered seemingly conflicting perspectives on the implicit regularization at play, with some claiming it promotes low nuclear norm
(
Gunasekar et al., 2017
)
and others arguing for low rank
(
Arora et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2020
;
Razin and Cohen, 2020
)
. However, a unified understanding of when, how, and why they achieve different implicit regularization effects remains elusive.

Unlike previous works that focus on either low rank or low nuclear norm regularization,, we systematically investigate the training dynamics and implicit regularization of matrix factorization for matrix completion. Through extensive experiments, we found that a certain connectivity property of observed data plays a key role in the implicit regularization effects. Data connectivity, in the context of this paper, refers to the way observed entries in the matrix are linked through shared rows or columns. A set of observations is considered connected if there’s a path between any two observed entries via other observed entries in the same rows or columns. This concept plays a crucial role in determining the behavior of matrix factorization models, as we will demonstrate throughout this paper. As shown in Fig.
1
, we sample observations randomly from a ground truth matrix
\bm{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{M}^{*})<d
and train models
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{A}\bm{B},\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
from small random initialization without any rank constraints. For each observation set, we calculate the solutions with the minimum nuclear norm and minimum rank, which serve as the ground truth benchmarks. These are then compared with the completion matrix obtained by the model. From Fig.
1
, we observe that:

(i)
Low rank bias in connected case:
When the observed entries are connected, the model consistently learns the lowest-rank solution.

(ii)
Low nuclear norm bias in certain disconnected case:
When the observed entries are disconnected, the model generally does not find the minimum nuclear norm or lowest-rank solution. However, in the special case where each connected component is a complete bipartite subgraph, the model consistently finds the minimum nuclear norm solution.

To understand how data connectivity modulates the implicit bias, we analyze the loss landscape and optimization dynamics. We find a hierarchy of intrinsic invariant manifolds
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
of different ranks in the loss landscape. These manifolds constrain the optimization trajectory, causing the model to learn by incrementally ascending through higher ranks. In the disconnected case, additional sub-
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifolds emerge within the
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold, preventing the model from reaching the global lowest-rank solution. However, we prove that the minimum nuclear norm solution is guaranteed in the disconnected with complete bipartite subgraph case.

The contributions of our work are summarized as follows:

(i) We systematically investigate the influence of data connectivity on the implicit regularization. Our empirical findings indicate that the connectivity of observed data plays a key role in the implicit bias, leading to a transition from favoring solutions with a low nuclear norm to those with a low rank as the data becomes more connected with an increase in observations (refer to Sec.
4
).

(ii) We characterize the training dynamics of matrix factorization theoretically, showing that the optimization trajectory follows a
Hierarchical Invariant Manifold Traversal (HIMT)
process. This generalizes the characterization of
Li et al. (2020)
, whose proposed
Greedy Low-Rank Learning(GLRL)
algorithm equivalence only corresponding to the connected case (refer to Sec.
5
and Sec.
6.1
).

(iii) Regarding the minimum nuclear norm regularization, we establish conditions that provide guarantees closely aligned with our empirical findings, which complement the results of
Gunasekar et al. (2017)
. For the minimum rank regularization, we present a dynamic characterization condition that assures the attainment of the minimum rank solution (refer to Sec.
6.2
).

Related works

Norm minimization and rank minimization.

Extensive research has been conducted on the implicit regularization of matrix factorization models, focusing on norm minimization and rank minimization. For norm minimization,
Gunasekar et al. (2017)
proved that gradient flow with infinitesimal initialization converges to the minimum nuclear norm solution in the special case of commutative observations.
Ji and Telgarsky (2019)
;
Gunasekar et al. (2018)
studied norm minimization regularization in deep linear networks. For rank minimization, numerous works have shown that matrix factorization models favor low-rank solutions.
Arora et al. (2019)
;
Gidel et al. (2019)
;
Gissin et al. (2019)
;
Razin and Cohen (2020)
;
Jiang et al. (2023)
;
Belabbas (2020)
investigated how infinitesimal initialization of gradient flow encourages low rank in specific settings.
Li et al. (2020)
showed that under certain assumptions, matrix factorization dynamics are equivalent to a greedy low-rank learning heuristic.
Li et al. (2018)
;
Stöger and Soltanolkotabi (2021)
;
Jin et al. (2023)
established low-rank recovery guarantees for matrix sensing problems under the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) condition.
Zhang et al. (2022)
;
Zhang et al. (2023)
studied a broader class of model rank minimization for nonlinear models, of which the matrix factorization model is a special case.

Nonlinear dynamics.

The initialization scale can significantly influence the implicit regularization of neural networks. Large initialization typically leads to linear dynamics
(
Jacot et al., 2018
)
and poor generalization
(
Chizat et al., 2019
)
, while small initialization induces nonlinear dynamics
(
Luo et al., 2021
)
. In this work, we focus on the case of infinitesimal initialization, which corresponds to highly nonlinear dynamics. An important characteristic of nonlinear neural network dynamics is the phenomenon of condensation
(
Luo et al., 2021
;
Zhou et al., 2022
)
, where the network’s effective complexity is small. The low-rank
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifolds we propose are essentially a manifestation of condensation.
Zhang et al. (2021)
;
Zhang et al. (2022)
;
Bai et al. (2022)
;
Fukumizu et al. (2019)
;
Simsek et al. (2021)
established the embedding principle of the loss landscape of neural networks and empirically demonstrated that the training process traverses critical points embedded from smaller subnetworks.
Jacot et al. (2021)
conjectured a saddle to saddle dynamics for deep linear networks, which is conceptually analogous to the dynamics characterization in this work.

3
Preliminaries

Matrix completion problem.

This study focuses on the matrix completion problem, which involves estimating missing entries within a partially observed matrix. Given an incomplete matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the goal is to predict the entirety of
\bm{M}
based on its observed elements. The set of observed entries is represented as
S=\{(i_{k},j_{k}),\bm{M}_{i_{k},j_{k}}\}_{k=1}^{n}
, where
(i_{k},j_{k})
indicates the row and column indices, and
\bm{M}_{i_{k},j_{k}}
is the corresponding value assumed non-zero in the matrix. The set of observed indices is defined as
S_{\bm{x}}=\{(i_{k},j_{k})\}_{k=1}^{n}
. Entries that are not observed, denoted by
\star
, are considered missing or unknown. The positions of observed elements in the matrix
\bm{M}
are defined by a binary observation matrix
\bm{P}
, where
\bm{P}_{ij}=1
indicates that
\bm{M}_{ij}
is observed, and
\bm{P}_{ij}=0
indicates that
\bm{M}_{ij}
is unobserved.

Matrix factorization model.

Matrix factorization is a prevalent approach for addressing the matrix completion problem. It reconstructs the matrix
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
through the product
\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
, where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}
. This work studies the overparameterized scenario with
r=d
, aiming to understand the implicit regularization effect in the absence of explicit rank restrictions, paralleling prior research
(
Gunasekar et al., 2017
;
Arora et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2020
;
Jin et al., 2023
)
. In this work, we focus on the asymmetric factorization, which can be represented as a parametric model:

\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{A}\bm{B},\quad\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

(1)

The matrix factorization model parameters are denoted by
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})
, identified with its vectorized form
\mathrm{vec}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
. The augmented matrix is
\bm{W}_{\mathrm{aug}}^{\top}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{A}^{\top}&\bm{B}\end{matrix}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times 2d}
, and
\textnormal{row}(\bm{A})
and
\textnormal{col}(\bm{B})
denote the row and column spaces of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
, respectively. The augmented matrix
\bm{W}_{\text{aug}}
plays a crucial role in our subsequent analysis, particularly in characterizing the intrinsic invariant manifolds
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
of the optimization process. Specifically, it allows us to establish the relationship
\text{rank}(\bm{A})=\text{rank}(\bm{B}^{\top})=\text{rank}(\bm{W}_{\text{aug}})
, which is important to understanding the invariance property under gradient flow.

Loss function.

The learning process for the parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})
involves minimizing a loss function that measures the difference between observed and estimated entries. In this work, we focus on the mean squared error, and the empirical risk is thus formulated as

R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\|(\bm{A}\bm{B}-\bm{M})_{S_{\bm{x}}}\|_{F}^{2}:=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}(\bm{a}_{i_{k}}\cdot\bm{b}_{\cdot,j_{k}}-\bm{M}_{i_{k},j_{k}})^{2},

(2)

where
\bm{a}_{i}
and
\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}
represent the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of matrix
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
, respectively. The residual matrix
\delta\bm{M}=(\bm{A}\bm{B}-\bm{M})_{S_{\bm{x}}}
has elements
\delta\bm{M}_{ij}=(\bm{A}\bm{B})_{ij}-\bm{M}_{ij}
for
(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}
and
\delta\bm{M}_{ij}=0
for
(i,j)\notin S_{\bm{x}}
. The training dynamics follow the gradient flow of
R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
:

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}),\quad\bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0}.

(3)

In all experiments,
\bm{\theta}_{0}\sim N(0,\sigma^{2})
is initialized from a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and small variance
\sigma^{2}
. We use gradient descent with a small learning rate to approximate the gradient flow dynamics (Please refer to Appendix
B.1
for the detailed experiment setup).

4
Connectivity affects implicit regularization

In this section, we define connectivity and present experimental results on implicit regularization for connected and disconnected observational data.

Definition 1

(
Associated Observation Graph
)
.

Given a incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed and its observation matrix
\bm{P}
, the associated observation graph
G_{\bm{M}}
is the bipartite graph with adjacency matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{P}^{\top}\\
\bm{P}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}
, with isolated vertices removed.

Definition 2

(
Connectivity
)
.

Given a incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed, it is considered connected if its associated observation graph
G_{\bm{M}}
is connected; otherwise, it is disconnected. The connected components of
\bm{M}
are defined as the connected components of
G_{\bm{M}}
.

The connectivity of the graph, as defined above, reflects the connectivity of the observed data. Appendix
A
Sec.
A.2
provides a detailed discussion on the equivalent definition of connectivity.

In the case of disconnectivity, there is a special case where each connected component has full observations, characterized by disconnectivity with complete bipartite components.

Definition 3

(
Disconnectivity with Complete Bipartite Components
)
.

A incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
is considered disconnected with complete bipartite components if its associated observation graph
G_{\bm{M}}
is disconnected and each connected component forms a complete bipartite subgraph.

\bm{M}_{1}
(disconnected),
\bm{M}_{2}
(disconnected with complete bipartite components), and
\bm{M}_{3}
(connected). Fig.

of Appendix

illustrates the associated graphs
G_{\bm{M}}
.

6
Theoretical analysis of training dynamics and implicit regularization

6.1
Characterization of training dynamics

Matrix factorization models exhibit a distinctive adaptive learning behavior, progressively evolving from low rank to high rank. Understanding this phenomenon is rooted in grasping the global dynamics of matrix factorization models, where the role of intrinsic invariant manifolds becomes critical.

Proposition 1

(
Hierarchical Intrinsic Invariant Manifold (HIIM)
)
.

(see Appendix
A
Prop.
A.1
for Proof)
Let
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
be a matrix factorization model and
\{\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k}\}
be
k
linearly independent vectors. Define the manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
as
\bm{\Omega}_{k}:=\bm{\Omega}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k})=\{\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})\mid\textnormal{row}(\bm{A})=\textnormal{col}(\bm{B})=\textnormal{span}\{\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k}\}\}
. The manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
possesses the following properties:

(i)
Invariance under Gradient Flow:
Given data
S
and the gradient flow dynamics
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, if the initial point
\bm{\theta}_{0}\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}
, then
\bm{\theta}(t)\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}
for all
t\geq 0
.

(ii)
Intrinsic Property:

\bm{\Omega}_{k}
is a data-independent invariant manifold, meaning that for any data
S
,
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
remains invariant under the gradient flow dynamics.

(iii)
Hierarchical Structure:
The manifolds
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
form a hierarchy:
\bm{\Omega}_{0}\subsetneqq\bm{\Omega}_{1}\subsetneqq\cdots\subsetneqq\bm{\Omega}_{k-1}\subsetneqq\bm{\Omega}_{k}
.

Figs.
3
(f-h) and Figs.
4
(b-d) show that the training process with small initialization consistently satisfies
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A})=\textnormal{rank}(\bm{B}^{\top})=\textnormal{rank}(\bm{W}_{\mathrm{aug}})
, aligning with the
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold. Since a non-zero initialization in practice, the training trajectory is close to the
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold, approaches a critical point, and transitions to the next level invariant manifold without getting trapped.

In both connected and disconnected scenarios, we observe a step-by-step hierarchical
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold traversal. In the connected case, at each level we observe that the model reaches an optimal solution (Fig.
3
). However, in the disconnected case, we can prove that each connected component induces a sub-
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold, leading to the experimentally observed sub-optimal solution (see Fig.
4
).

Proposition 2

(
Intrinsic Sub-
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
Invariant Manifold
)
.

(see Appendix
A
Prop.
A.2
for Proof)
Let
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
be a matrix factorization model,
\bm{M}
be an incomplete matrix and
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
be an invariant manifold defined in Prop.
1

Conclusion and future work

This study presents a comprehensive experimental and theoretical investigation of matrix factorization models. The primary objective was to develop a cohesive framework for understanding the conditions, mechanisms, and reasons behind the diverse implicit regularization effects exhibited by matrix factorization models. A key finding of this research is the pivotal role of the connectivity of observed data in shaping the implicit regularization behavior. To elucidate this phenomenon, we identified the significance of hierarchical invariant manifold traversal within the training dynamics.

Our experiments (Figs.
1
,
2
,
3
) provide strong evidence that connected observed data leads to minimum-rank solutions, as the model learns the optimal of the
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold. However, further investigation is needed to uncover the underlying mechanisms by which connectivity facilitates optimal attainment across different
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifolds. Additionally, the trade-offs between initialization scale and training efficiency warrant further research, as certain cases may require extremely small initialization, potentially impacting training speed (see Appendix
B
Sec.
B.4
).

Generalizing the insights gained from matrix factorization models to other architectures is also an important avenue for future work. Our preliminary experiments indicate that the learning phenomenon from low rank to high rank persists in deep multi-layer matrix factorization and the query-key factorization model in Transformer attention mechanisms (see Appendix
B
Figs.
B9
,
B11
). These findings suggest that the hierarchical invariant manifold traversal process uncovered in our study may have broader implications and merit further exploration.

\bm{P}

G_{\bm{M}}
is the bipartite graph with adjacency matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{P}^{\top}\\
\bm{P}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}
, with isolated vertices removed.

Definition A.2

(
Connectivity
)
.

A matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed is considered connected if its associated observation graph
G_{\bm{M}}
is connected, otherwise, we call it disconnected. The connected components of
\bm{M}
are defined as the connected components of this graph.

Definition A.3

(
Disconnectivity with Complete Bipartite Components
)
.

A matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed is considered disconnected with complete bipartite components if its associated observation graph
G_{\bm{M}}
is disconnected and each connected component forms a complete bipartite subgraph.

Remark
.

In the bipartite graph representation of the observed data, isolated vertices correspond to entire rows or columns of the matrix
\bm{M}
that are not observed. These rows or columns do not contribute to the loss calculation and have no influence on the dynamics of the matrix factorization under infinitesimal initialization. Consequently, when analyzing the connectivity of the observed data and its impact on the learning dynamics, these isolated vertices can be safely disregarded.

Remark
.

The disconnectivity of the bipartite graph representing the observed data is equivalent to the reducibility of the adjacency matrix
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{P}^{\top}\\
\bm{P}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\bm{P}
is the binary observation matrix indicating the positions of the observed entries in
\bm{M}
.

In the context of matrix completion problems, such as the Netflix problem, connectivity has a practical interpretation. Connected components in the bipartite graph indicate groups of users and movies that are linked by the users’ viewing history. Users within the same connected component are related through the movies they have watched in common. Due to this practical significance, we prefer to use the term “connectivity” instead of “reducibility” when discussing the structure of the observed data in matrix completion problems.

Definition A.4

(
Connectivity of Observed Data
)
.

Given a matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed, an undirected simple graph
G
can be induced from it: the nodes of the graph are the observed elements in the matrix, and two nodes are adjacent if and only if they are in the same row or column of the matrix
\bm{M}
. A matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed is considered connected if its induced graph
G
is connected, otherwise, we call it disconnected.

Lemma A.1
.

For any simple graph
G
, if we remove all isolated vertices from
G
to obtain a new graph
G^{\prime}
, then
G^{\prime}
is connected if and only if the line graph of
G^{\prime}
, denoted as
L(G^{\prime})
, is connected.

Proof.

\implies
Assume
G^{\prime}
is connected. Consider any two nodes in
L(G^{\prime})
, which correspond to two edges in
G^{\prime}
, say
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
. Since
G^{\prime}
is connected, there exists a path connecting the endpoints of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
. This path corresponds to a sequence of edges in
G^{\prime}
, which in turn corresponds to a path connecting the nodes representing
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
in
L(G^{\prime})
. Therefore,
L(G^{\prime})
is connected.

\impliedby
Conversely, assume
L(G^{\prime})
is connected. Consider any two vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
G^{\prime}
. Since
G^{\prime}
has no isolated vertices, each of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
is incident to at least one edge. Let these edges be
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, respectively. Since
L(G^{\prime})
is connected, there exists a path connecting the nodes representing
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
in
L(G^{\prime})
. This path corresponds to a sequence of edges in
G^{\prime}
, which in turn corresponds to a path connecting
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
G^{\prime}
. Therefore,
G^{\prime}
is connected.

In conclusion, we have proven that for any simple graph
G
, if we remove all isolated vertices from
G
to obtain a new graph
G^{\prime}
, then
G^{\prime}
is connected if and only if the line graph of
G^{\prime}
, denoted as
L(G^{\prime})
, is connected.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Given a incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
, the connectivity of
\bm{M}
defined in Def.
A.2
and Def.
A.4
is equivalent.

Proof.

By definition, each edge of a bipartite graph corresponds to an observed data item, and two edges in a bipartite graph are adjacent if and only if the two corresponding observed data items are in the same row or column. Therefore, the connectivity of the observed data is equivalent to the connectivity of the edges of the bipartite graph, which is, in turn, equivalent to the connectivity of the line graph of the bipartite graph.

According to Lem.
A.1
, for any graph
G
, if we remove all isolated vertices from
G
to obtain a new graph
G^{\prime}
, then
G^{\prime}
is connected if and only if the line graph of
G^{\prime}
, denoted as
L(G^{\prime})
, is connected.

In the context of the bipartite graph representation of the observed data, removing isolated vertices corresponds to removing rows and columns that contain no observed entries. Thus, the connectivity of the bipartite graph after removing isolated vertices is equivalent to the connectivity of the observed data as defined in Def.
A.4
.

Consequently, the connectivity of the observed data as defined in Def.
A.2
(based on the line graph of the bipartite graph) is equivalent to the connectivity of the observed data as defined in Def.
A.4
(based on the connectivity of observed data).
∎

Definition A.5

(
Decoupling of Dynamics
)
.

Given an incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
, consider the gradient flow dynamics of matrix factorization models,
\forall 1\leq i,j\leq d
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\bm{a}}_{i}&=-\dfrac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in I_{i}}(\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}-\bm{M}_{ij})\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}^{\top},\\
\dot{\bm{b}}_{\cdot,j}&=-\dfrac{2}{n}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}(\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}-\bm{M}_{ij})\bm{a}_{i}^{\top}.\end{aligned}\right.

(10)

The dynamics are said to be decoupled if there exist disjoint subsets of indices
R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{k}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,d\}
for the rows of
\bm{A}
and
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,d\}
for the columns of
\bm{B}
, such that for each
l\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, the dynamics of
\{\bm{a}_{i}:i\in R_{l}\}
and
\{\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}:j\in C_{l}\}
form an independent system of equations. In other words, the dynamics can be divided into
k(k>1)
independent subsystems, each involving a subset of rows of
\bm{A}
and a subset of columns of
\bm{B}
. If such a division is not possible, the dynamics are said to be coupled.

Proposition A.4
.

Given an incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
, if it is disconnected as defined by Def.
A.2
, then the dynamics are decoupled as defined by Def.
A.5
; if it is connected as defined by Def.
A.2
, then the dynamics are coupled as defined by Def.
A.5
.

Proof.

Consider a matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed, with its associated observation graph comprising
m
connected components, denoted as
L_{1},L_{2},\cdots,L_{m}
. Let
S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}\subseteq S_{\bm{x}}
represent the subset of observed indices corresponding to the connected component
L_{p}
, where
1\leq p\leq m
and
S_{\bm{x}}
denotes the complete set of observed indices. If the incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
is disconnected, then for each connected component
L_{p}
, the subset
S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}
can be partitioned into two subsets
R_{p}
and
C_{p}
,
1\leq p\leq m
, such that

R_{p}=\{i|\exists j:(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}\},\quad C_{p}=\{j|\exists i:(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}\}.

(11)

In other words,
R_{p}
contains the row indices and
C_{p}
contains the column indices of the observed entries in the connected component
L_{p}
.

It can be easily verified that the dynamics are decoupled in this case, as the subsets
\{R_{p},C_{p}\}_{p=1}^{m}
satisfy the conditions in Def.
A.5
. Each connected component
L_{p}
corresponds to an independent subsystem involving the rows of
\bm{A}
indexed by
R_{p}
and the columns of
\bm{B}
indexed by
C_{p}
.

If
\bm{M}
is connected, then its associated observation graph consists of a single connected component, and the entire dynamics are coupled.
∎

A.3
Loss Landscape

In this paper, we focus on the problem of asymmetric matrix factorization. Previous literature
(
Gunasekar et al., 2017
;
Li et al., 2018
;
Li et al., 2020
;
Jin et al., 2023
)
has predominantly concentrated on symmetric matrix factorization problems. Although asymmetric matrix factorization models can be transformed into symmetric cases, studying symmetric matrix factorization does not necessarily cover all aspects of the asymmetric scenarios.

Generally, an asymmetric matrix factorization model
\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
can be transformed into a symmetric situation by setting

\bm{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}\\
\bm{B}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times d}.

We then consider the model
\bm{W}^{\prime}=\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}
, which corresponds to the following matrix completion problem:

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}&\bm{A}\bm{B}\\
\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{A}^{\top}&\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{B}\end{bmatrix}.

We define the loss as

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}&\bm{B}\\
\bm{C}&\bm{D}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{B})+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{C}^{\top}).

Li et al. (2020)
established the following results:

Theorem A.1

(Theorem 5.10 in
Li et al. (2020)
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth function. (1). All stationary points of
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L}(\bm{U})=\frac{1}{2}f\left(\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}\right)
are either strict saddles or global minimizers; (2). For any random initialization,
GF(1)
converges to strict saddles of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{U})
with probability 0.

The proof of this theorem relies heavily on Theorem
A.2
of
Du and Lee (2018)
, which requires the parameter matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
to satisfy the condition that
k\geq d
. In the case of symmetric matrix factorization, where
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, this condition is naturally met. However, for asymmetric matrix factorization, where
\bm{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}\\
\bm{B}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times d}
, this condition is not satisfied, and thus the proof of Theorem
A.1
is only applicable to the symmetric case of matrix factorization.

Theorem A.2

(Theorem 3.1 in
Du and Lee (2018)
))
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
convex function. Then
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\mathcal{L}(\bm{U})=f\left(\bm{U}\bm{U}^{\top}\right),k\geq d
satisfies that (1). Every local minimizer of
\mathcal{L}
is also a global minimizer; (2). All saddles are strict. Here saddles denote those stationary points whose hessian are not positive semi-definite (thus including local maximizers).

Below we give a direct proof of the loss landscape of an asymmetric matrix factorization model.

Theorem A.3

(
Loss Landscape
)
.

For any data
S
, the critical points of
R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
are either strict saddle points or global minima.

Proof.

We start by recalling the definition of the loss function:

R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{A},\bm{B})=\dfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{A}\bm{B}-\bm{M}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}}((\bm{A}\bm{B})_{ij}-\bm{M}_{ij})^{2}.

Let
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})
denote a critical point. We define a new matrix,
\delta\bm{M}
, as the difference between the product of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
and the matrix
\bm{M}
, with this difference being computed only over the indices in the set
S_{\bm{x}}
. More formally,
\delta\bm{M}=(\bm{A}\bm{B}-\bm{M})_{S_{\bm{x}}}
, where the elements of
\delta\bm{M}
are given by:

•

For
(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}
, we have
\delta\bm{M}_{ij}=(\bm{A}\bm{B})_{ij}-\bm{M}_{ij}
.

•

For
(i,j)\notin S_{\bm{x}}
, we have
\delta\bm{M}_{ij}=0
.

This definition of
\delta\bm{M}
ensures that we only consider the differences in the entries that belong to the set
S_{\bm{x}}
, while all other entries are set to zero.

Consider the function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{A}+\bm{\bm{\varepsilon}},\bm{B}+\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\|\delta\bm{M}+\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{\eta}+\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{\eta}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\delta\bm{M}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}+\langle\delta\bm{M},\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{\eta}\rangle_{S_{\bm{x}}}+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{\eta}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\langle\delta\bm{M},\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{\eta}\rangle_{S_{\bm{x}}}+o(\|\bm{\varepsilon}\|^{2},\|\bm{\eta}\|^{2}),

where the inner product of two matrices
\bm{A},\bm{B}
is defined as
\left<\bm{A},\bm{B}\right>:=\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{A}\bm{B}^{\top})
.

At the critical point, the first order term
\langle\delta\bm{M},\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{\eta}\rangle_{S_{\bm{x}}}
equals 0. The Hessian operator, representing the second order term, is given by:

h_{\bm{A},\bm{B}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\eta})=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{\eta}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}+\langle\delta\bm{M},\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{\eta}\rangle_{S_{\bm{x}}}.

Our goal is to demonstrate that if
\delta\bm{M}\neq\bm{0}
, there always exists
\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\eta}
such that
h_{\bm{A},\bm{B}}(\bm{\varepsilon},\bm{\eta})<0
. To this end, we consider the ranks of matrices
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
in two cases:

(i)
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A})<d
or
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{B})<d
:

Without loss of generality, we assume
\delta\bm{M}_{ij}:=\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\neq 0
for some
(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}
, and
\textnormal{rank}\bm{A}<d
. Under these conditions, there exists a non-zero vector
\bm{v}
such that
\bm{A}\bm{v}=0
.

We set
\bm{\eta}^{*}_{,j}=\bm{v}
and
\bm{\eta}^{*}_{,s}=0
for
s\neq j
, where
\bm{\eta}^{*}_{,j}
denotes the
j
-th column of the matrix
\bm{\eta}
. Let
\bm{\varepsilon}_{i}^{*}=\bm{w}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{s}=0
for
s\neq i
, where
\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of the matrix
\bm{\varepsilon}
.

We then have:

\displaystyle h_{\bm{A},\bm{B}}(\bm{\varepsilon}^{*},\bm{\eta}^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\varepsilon}^{*}\bm{B}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}+\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{B}\|_{F}^{2}+\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{w}+\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}.

We define
g(\bm{w},\bm{v})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{w}+\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{v}
and consider:

\displaystyle g(-\alpha\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{v},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\delta\bm{M}_{ij}^{2}\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{v}-\alpha\delta\bm{M}_{ij}^{2}\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\delta\bm{M}_{ij}^{2}(\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{v}-2\frac{1}{\alpha}\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{v}).

For
0<\alpha<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\bm{B},\max}}
, where
\lambda_{\bm{B},\max}
represents the top eigenvalue of
\bm{B}\bm{B}^{\top}
, we find
g(-\alpha\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{v},\bm{v})<0
.

Therefore, when
\bm{w}=-\alpha\delta\bm{M}_{ij}\bm{v}
, we obtain
h_{\bm{A},\bm{B}}(\bm{\varepsilon}^{*},\bm{\eta}^{*})<0
. This immediately implies the critical point
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})
is a strict saddle point.

(ii)
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{A})=\textnormal{rank}(\bm{B})=d
:

Let
\bm{\varepsilon}=\alpha\delta\bm{M}\bm{B}^{-1}
and
\bm{\eta}=0
. In this scenario, the first order term
\langle\delta\bm{M},\bm{\varepsilon}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{\eta}\rangle_{S_{\bm{x}}}
in Eq. (
15
) simplifies to
\alpha\|\delta\bm{M}\|_{S_{\bm{x}}}^{2}
. At a critical point, this quantity equals zero, it implies that
\delta\bm{M}=0
. This in turn implies that the critical point
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})
is a global minimum.

This concludes the proof, establishing that the critical points of
R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
are either strict saddle points or global minima.
∎

A.4
Escaping from Top Eigendirection

In this section, we focus on the dynamics of escaping from a critical point. According to Prop.
A.3
, the loss landscape consists solely of strict saddle points and a global minimum. Consequently, gradient-based methods can readily escape from a critical point that is not a global minimum.

In the following, we will demonstrate that the escaping dynamics near a critical point can be approximated by a linearized version of these dynamics. For this, consider the following:

\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta}).

(16)

Assume
\bm{\theta}_{c}
is a saddle point for which
\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})=0
. We can apply a first-order Taylor expansion to the right-hand side of Eq. (
16
), yielding:

-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c})+\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c}\|^{2}),

(17)

where
\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
represents the Hessian matrix. Given that
\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})=0
, the gradient flow dynamics around
\bm{\theta}_{c}
can be approximated as:

\dot{\bm{\theta}}=\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c}),

(18)

where
\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
. Eq. (
18
) is a classic linear ordinary differential equation, with the solution:

\bm{\theta}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c})+\bm{\theta}_{c}.

(19)

The dynamics near a critical point can be approximated by a linearized version. Hence, in the vicinity of a critical point, we can analyze the linearized dynamics to understand the escape mechanism. In the following, we will show that during this escape process, the dynamics follow a pattern referred to as
dominant eigenvalue dynamics
.

Eq. (
19
) elucidates that the dynamics near the critical point
\bm{\theta}_{c}
are predominantly dictated by the properties of
\bm{H}
, a real symmetric matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}\times 2d^{2}}
. Its eigendecomposition is given by:

\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})=\bm{Q}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{Q}^{\top},

(20)

where
\bm{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix and
\bm{Q}
is an orthogonal matrix. Let
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
denote the eigenvalues of
\bm{H}
, and let
\bm{q}_{i1},\bm{q}_{i2},\cdots,\bm{q}_{il_{i}}
represent the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
.

Given that
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
, the ratio
e^{\lambda_{1}t}/e^{\lambda_{i}t}
for
i>1
grows exponentially fast. Consequently, near
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, the evolution of the system is primarily driven by the eigenvectors
\bm{q}_{11},\bm{q}_{12},\cdots,\bm{q}_{1l_{1}}
associated with the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
.

This following proposition formalizes the intuitive idea that in the vicinity of a saddle point, the dynamics primarily follow the direction associated with the largest eigenvalue. This leading eigendirection becomes increasingly dominant as time evolves, allowing for an escape from the saddle point and facilitating a specific structured transition.

Let’s consider
\bm{\theta}_{c}
to be a saddle point. Consider:

\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c}),\quad\bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0}.

(21)

Here,
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and
\bm{\theta}_{c}
are in close enough proximity for the linearized dynamics to be valid over a sufficiently long period. We can then establish the following proposition:

Proposition A.5

(
Escape from Saddle Points Following a Dominant Eigenvalue Dynamics
)
.

Consider the linearized dynamics given by
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}_{c})
, we denote
\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
. Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{H}
, with corresponding eigenvectors
\bm{q}_{11},\bm{q}_{12},\cdots,\bm{q}_{1l_{1}}
. Denote
c_{j}=\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle,\forall 1\leq j\leq l_{1}
. Assume there exists
j
such that
c_{j}\neq 0
, then, given any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
t_{0}>0
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
, the following holds:

\left\|\dfrac{\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}_{c}}{\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}c_{j}\bm{q}_{1j}}-\bm{1}\right\|<\varepsilon.

(22)

This means that, as time progresses, the direction of the parameter evolution increasingly aligns with the dominant eigenvectors of
\bm{H}
.

This proposition is a consequence of the fact that the solution of the differential equation is given by
\bm{\theta}(t)=e^{\bm{H}t}\bm{\theta}(0)
, and as
t
tends to infinity, the term corresponding to the dominant eigenvalue in the matrix exponential
e^{\bm{H}t}
becomes dominant. Therefore, near the saddle point, we have
\bm{\theta}(t)\approx\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}t}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}c_{j}\bm{q}_{1j}+\bm{\theta}_{c}
.

Proof.

The solution of the ordinary differential equation (
21
) is
\bm{\theta}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{t\bm{H}}(\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c})+\bm{\theta}_{c}
. Here,
\bm{H}=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
is a real symmetric matrix, which can be diagonalized. Let
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{s}
be the eigenvalues of
\bm{H}
, and let
\bm{q}_{i1},\bm{q}_{i2},\cdots,\bm{q}_{il_{i}}
be the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
. We can then express
\bm{\theta}(t)
as:

\bm{\theta}(t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{i}t}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{ij}\rangle\bm{q}_{ij}+\bm{\theta}_{c}.

(23)

Next, we analyze the norm of the relative difference between
\bm{\theta}(t)
and a term dominating its growth:

\displaystyle\left\|\dfrac{\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}_{c}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}t}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle\bm{q}_{1j}}-\bm{1}\right\|=\left\|\dfrac{\sum\limits_{i=2}^{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{i}t}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{ij}\rangle\bm{q}_{ij}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}t}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle\bm{q}_{1j}}\right\|

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=2}^{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{i}}\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{i})t}\left\|\dfrac{\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{ij}\rangle\bm{q}_{ij}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle\bm{q}_{1j}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})t}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{i}}\left\|\dfrac{\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{ij}\rangle\bm{q}_{ij}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle\bm{q}_{1j}}\right\|.

We define
C=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{s}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{i}}\left\|\dfrac{\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{ij}\rangle\bm{q}_{ij}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle\bm{q}_{1j}}\right\|
. By choosing
t_{0}=\dfrac{\log\frac{C}{\varepsilon}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}
, we ensure that for all
t>t_{0}
, the following condition is met:

\left\|\dfrac{\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}_{c}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{1}t}\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle\bm{q}_{1j}}-\bm{1}\right\|<\varepsilon.

(25)

∎

Prop.
A.5
describes that under the linearized dynamics, the parameters will escape from the saddle point along a specific direction. However, when considering the original nonlinear dynamics
\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, we encounter a trade-off: we should choose
t_{0}
sufficiently large so that the trajectory can align well with the dominant eigendirection while escaping the saddle point, but if
t_{0}
is too large, the linearization approximation will fail as
\bm{\theta}(t_{0})
moves away from
\bm{\theta}_{c}
.
Li et al. (2020)
(Theorem 5.3) proved a general dynamical result through careful analysis and error control: assuming the eigenvector corresponding to the maximum eigenvalue is unique and the initialization is sufficiently close to the saddle point, there always exists a suitable
t_{0}
such that the linear dynamics can align with the dominant eigendirection before the linearization breaks down. We can generalize this result to the case where the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue is not unique:

Proposition A.6
.

Consider the dynamics given by
\dot{\bm{\theta}}(t)=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, we use
\varphi(\bm{\theta}_{0},t)
to denote the value of
\bm{\theta}(t)
in the case of
\bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0}
.
At a critical point
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, we denote the negative Hessian as
\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
. Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{H}
, with corresponding eigenvectors
\bm{q}_{11},\bm{q}_{12},\cdots,\bm{q}_{1l_{1}}
. Denote
c_{j}=\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle,\forall 1\leq j\leq l_{1}
, and
\bm{v}_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{l_{1}}c_{j}\bm{q}_{1j}
. Assume there exists
j
such that
c_{j}\neq 0
.
Let
\bm{z}_{\alpha}(t):=\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{v}_{1},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)
for every
\alpha>0
, then
\bm{z}(t):=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\bm{z}_{\alpha}(t)
exists and is also a solution of the given dynamics, i.e.,
\bm{z}(t)=\varphi(\bm{z}(0),t)
.
Furthermore,
\forall t\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left\|\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{\theta}_{0},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)-\bm{z}(t)\right\|_{2}\leq C\alpha^{\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}}

for every sufficiently small
\alpha
, where
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>0
is the eigenvalue gap.

Proof.

By Theorem 5.3 in Section 5.1 of
Li et al. (2020)
, we know that if the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
is one-dimensional, i.e.,
l_{1}=1
, then the escaping direction will be the top eigenvector direction, and the convergence rate is
O(\alpha^{\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}})
. Therefore, Proposition
A.6
holds in this case.

Now, if the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
is not one-dimensional, we denote
c_{j}=\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle,\forall 1\leq j\leq l_{1}
, and
\bm{v}_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{l_{1}}c_{j}\bm{q}_{1j}
will be the escaping direction. Following the same technique as in
Li et al. (2020)
, we can easily verify that the convergence rate remains
O(\alpha^{\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}})
. Therefore, Proposition
A.6
holds in this case as well.

∎

A.5
Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of Hessian

Suppose the dominant directions fulfill specific conditions, such as any combination
c_{1}\bm{q}_{11}+c_{2}\bm{q}_{12}+\cdots+c_{l_{1}}\bm{q}_{1l_{1}}
, leading to rank 1 model parameters
(\bm{A},\bm{B})
. In such scenarios, we may observe a phenomenon where the rank of the matrix increases incrementally.

Firstly, we analyze the eigenvector structure of the Hessian matrix at the critical point
\bm{\theta}_{c}=(\bm{A}_{c},\bm{B}_{c})
to understand why the parameter will enter the rank-1 invariant manifold.

Computation of the Hessian Matrix at a Critical Point.

To compute the Hessian matrix, we first consider the gradient:

\displaystyle R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\ell\left(f(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),f^{*}(\bm{x})\right),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{S}\nabla\ell\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x}),

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i},

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{f}^{*})_{i}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i},

where
\partial_{i}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)
is the
i
-th element of
\nabla\ell\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)
, and
\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}
is the
i
-th element of the vectorization of
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
.

For the Hessian matrix
\bm{H}_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{H}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}R_{S}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\partial_{i}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{ij}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{j}\right)^{\top}+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\partial_{i}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\right),

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{j}\right)^{\top}+\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}(\bm{f_{\theta}}-\bm{f^{*}})_{i}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\right),

where
\partial_{ij}\ell\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{f}^{*}\right)
is the
(i,j)
-th element of
\nabla\nabla\ell\left(\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta}),\bm{f}^{*}(\bm{x})\right)
.

We define matrices
\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta})
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{j}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}(\bm{f_{\theta}}-\bm{f^{*}})_{i}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\right),

We further denote that
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}):=\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta})
.

For matrix factorization model, the eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
has a special structure, as characterized by Lem.
A.2
.

Lemma A.2

(
Data-Independent Interleaved Structure of Eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
)
.

Let
\bm{\theta}_{c}=(\bm{A}_{c},\bm{B}_{c})
be any critical point of the matrix factorization model. If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}\times 2d^{2}}
, then there exist at least
d
eigenvectors associated with
\lambda
. These
d
eigenvectors take the form
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1},\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
, where
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a vector to be determined and
\bm{e}_{i}
is the unit vector representing the
i
-th column of the identity matrix
\bm{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Proof.

Let’s denote the residual matrix at the critical point as
\delta\bm{M}=(\bm{A}_{c}\bm{B}_{c}-\bm{M})_{S_{\bm{x}}}
, where
(\bm{A}_{c},\bm{B}_{c})
is a critical point. For the vectorized parameter
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, by direct calculation the matrix
\bm{H}^{(2)}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
can be formulated as a block matrix, with the diagonal blocks being
0
. The specific format is as follows:

\bm{H}^{(2)}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{0}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}&\bm{0}\end{matrix}\right].

(26)

Next, we compute the eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
. Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
. We need to verify that
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1},\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
are the eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
corresponding to
\lambda
, for a particular
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
yet to be determined. That is, we need to ensure that for all
1\leq i\leq d
, the equation
(\bm{H}^{(2)}-\lambda\bm{I}_{2d^{2}})(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})=\bm{0}
has a non-zero solution for
\bm{v}
. Notice that

\displaystyle(\bm{H}^{(2)}-\lambda\bm{I}_{2d^{2}})(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}\otimes\bm{I}_{d}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}&-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\end{matrix}\right](\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})

(27)

\displaystyle=\left(\left[\begin{matrix}-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}&-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}\end{matrix}\right]\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\right)(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})

\displaystyle=\left(\left[\begin{matrix}-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}&-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}\end{matrix}\right]\bm{v}\right)\otimes\bm{e}_{i}.

Since
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
, the determinant of the matrix
\bm{H}^{(2)}-\lambda\bm{I}_{2d^{2}}
equals zero. Hence

\textnormal{det}\left(\left[\begin{matrix}-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}&-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}\end{matrix}\right]\right)^{2d}=\textnormal{det}\left(\left[\begin{matrix}-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}&-\lambda\bm{I}_{d}\end{matrix}\right]\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\right)=0.

(28)

Consequently, from Eq. (
27
), we conclude that there always exists a non-zero vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
such that
(\bm{H}^{(2)}-\lambda\bm{I}_{2d^{2}})(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})=0
. Since
\bm{v}\neq\bm{0}
, it is evident that
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1},\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
are linearly independent, and thus they represent
d
eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda
.
∎

Proposition A.7

(
Eigenvectors Structure of
\bm{H}
at the Origin
)
.

Consider the dynamics given by Eq. (
21
), where we denote
\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{0})
. If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}\times 2d^{2}}
, then there exist at least
d
eigenvectors associated with
\lambda
in
\bm{H}
. These
d
eigenvectors take the form
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1},\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2},\cdots,\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
, where
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a vector to be determined and
\bm{e}_{i}
is the unit vector representing the
i
-th column of the identity matrix
\bm{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Proof.

In the matrix factorization model, at the origin the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)=\bm{0}
and thus
\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{0})=0
and the Hessian matrix reduces to
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{0})
, making
\bm{H}=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{0})=-\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{0})
.

Let’s denote the residual matrix at the origin as
\delta\bm{M}=(\bm{A}_{c}\bm{B}_{c}-\bm{M})_{S_{\bm{x}}}
, where at the origin
(\bm{A}_{c},\bm{B}_{c})=(\bm{0},\bm{0})
. For the vectorized parameter
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, the matrix
\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{0})
can be formulated as a block matrix, with the diagonal blocks being
0
. The specific format is as follows:

\bm{H}=\bm{H}^{(2)}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{0}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}&\bm{0}\end{matrix}\right].

(29)

By Lem.
A.2
, the proof is completed.
∎

Lemma A.3

(
Eigenvectors Structure of
\bm{H}
at Second-order Stationary Point
)
.

Let
\bm{\Omega}
denote an
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold or sub-
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold defined in Prop.
A.1
and
A.2
, and consider a second-order stationary point
\bm{\theta}_{c}
within
\bm{\Omega}
, i.e.,
\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})=0
and
\bm{\theta}^{\top}\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})\bm{\theta}\geq 0
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Omega}
. Then, the eigenvectors corresponding to the negative eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
are contained within the span of the eigenvectors corresponding to the negative eigenvalues of
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
.

Proof.

Recall the definitions of
\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta})
given by:

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{j}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{f}^{*}\right)_{i}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}\right)_{i}\right].

The Hessian matrix
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta})
at
\bm{\theta}
is
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}):=\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta})+\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta})
.

The manifold
\bm{\Omega}
is an affine subspace with orthogonal complement denoted by
\bm{\Omega}^{\perp}
. Let
\bm{H}
have the following block representation in the bases of
\bm{\Omega}
and
\bm{\Omega}^{\perp}
:

\bm{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}_{11}&\bm{H}_{12}\\
\bm{H}_{21}&\bm{H}_{22}\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

Since
\bm{\Omega}
is an invariant subspace under the gradient flow, we have
\bm{H}\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Omega}
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Omega}
, which implies that
\bm{H}_{12}=\bm{0}
. Since
\bm{H}
is symmetry, we have
\bm{H}_{21}=\bm{0}
.

Let
\lambda<0
be a negative eigenvalue of
\bm{H}:=\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
with
\bm{v}
as the corresponding eigenvector. Since
\bm{H}_{11}
is positive semi-definite,
\bm{v}
must lie in
\bm{\Omega}^{\perp}
.

At a critical point
\bm{\theta}_{c}=(\bm{A}_{c},\bm{B}_{c})
, by direct calculation, the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}
can be structured as:

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\bm{B}_{c}&&&\\
&\bm{B}_{c}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
a_{11}\bm{I}&a_{21}\bm{I}&\cdots&a_{d1}\bm{I}\\
a_{12}\bm{I}&a_{22}\bm{I}&\cdots&a_{d2}\bm{I}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{1d}\bm{I}&a_{2d}\bm{I}&\cdots&a_{dd}\bm{I}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{I}\otimes\bm{B}_{c}\\
\bm{A}_{c}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I}\end{array}\right]_{2d^{2}\times d^{2}},

(31)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

Note that
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{*}}\right)_{j}
is the
j
-th column of matrix
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}
and it falls precisely within the defined
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold or sub-
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}
. Therefore, we have:

\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\right)_{j}\right)^{\top}\bm{v}=0\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq d^{2},

(32)

which implies that
\bm{v}
is orthogonal to the image of
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\right)
, placing it in the null space of
\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
.

As a result, we have:

\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c})\bm{v}=\left(\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})+\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})\right)\bm{v}=\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}.

Thus, the eigenvector
\bm{v}
of the Hessian
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
, corresponding to the negative eigenvalue
\lambda
, is also an eigenvector of
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
, confirming that
\bm{v}
is within the span of the eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
.
∎

A.6
Transition to the Next Rank-level Invariant Manifold

Proposition A.8
.

The linear combination of the eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
:
c_{1}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1})+c_{2}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2})+\cdots+c_{d}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d})
falls within the invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{1}(\bm{c})
, where
\bm{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{d})^{\top}
.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle c_{1}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1})+c_{2}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2})+\cdots+c_{d}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d})

(33)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}\otimes[c_{1}\bm{e}_{1}+c_{2}\bm{e}_{2}+\cdots+c_{d}\bm{e}_{d}]

\displaystyle=\bm{v}\otimes\bm{c}.

By Definition
A.1
, the data-independent invariant manifold generated by
\bm{c}
is
\bm{\Omega}_{1}(\bm{c})=\{(\bm{A},\bm{B})|\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j}\in\mathrm{span}\{\bm{c}\},\forall 1\leq i,j\leq d\}
. If
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})\in\bm{\Omega}_{1}(\bm{c})
, then
\bm{A},\bm{B}
must take the form

\bm{A}=\left[\begin{matrix}\beta_{1}\bm{c}\\
\beta_{2}\bm{c}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{d}\bm{c}\end{matrix}\right],\quad\bm{B}=\left[\begin{matrix}\beta_{d+1}\bm{c}\\
\beta_{d+2}\bm{c}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{2d}\bm{c}\end{matrix}\right],

(34)

for some
\bm{\beta}=[\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{2d}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, and the vectorized parameter
\bm{\theta}=\mathrm{vec}((\bm{A},\bm{B}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
takes the form
\bm{\beta}\otimes\bm{c}
. Let
\bm{\beta}=\bm{v}
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Suppose
\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are linearly independent, the data-independent invariant manifold exhibits the property
\bm{\Omega}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k})+\bm{\Omega}_{1}(\bm{\alpha}_{k+1})=\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k+1})
.

Proof.

Assume that
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})\in\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k+1})
. Then
\bm{A},\bm{B}
should adopt the form:

A=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\bm{\beta}_{i}\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}+\bm{\beta}_{k+1}\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}^{\top},B=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\bm{\gamma}_{i}\bm{\alpha}_{i}^{\top}+\bm{\gamma}_{k+1}\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}^{\top}.

(35)

Denote

\bm{c}_{i}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{\beta}_{i}\\
\bm{\gamma}_{i}\end{matrix}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, then the vectorized parameters
\bm{\theta}:=\textnormal{vec}(\bm{\theta})
can be expressed as:

\bm{\theta}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\bm{c}_{i}\otimes\bm{\alpha}_{i}+\bm{c}_{k+1}\otimes\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}.

(36)

Since we know that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\bm{c}_{i}\otimes\bm{\alpha}_{i}\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k})
and
\bm{c}_{k+1}\otimes\bm{\alpha}_{k+1}\in\bm{\Omega}_{1}(\bm{\alpha})
, it is straightforward to validate that
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k+1})=\bm{\Omega}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k})+\bm{\Omega}_{1}(\bm{\alpha}_{k+1})
.
∎

Theorem A.4

(
Transition to the Next Rank-level Invariant Manifold
)
.

Consider the dynamics
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
. Let
\varphi(\bm{\theta}_{0},t)
denote the value of
\bm{\theta}(t)
when
\bm{\theta}(0)=\bm{\theta}_{0}
.
Let
\bm{\Omega}
be a
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold or sub-
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
invariant manifold. Let
\bm{\theta}_{c}\in\bm{\Omega}
be a critical point satisfying Assump.
A.1
and
A.2
. Then, for randomly selected
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, with probability 1 with respect to
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, the limit

\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{\theta}_{c},t):=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{\theta}_{0},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)

(37)

exists and falls into an invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}
. Here
\lambda_{1}
is the top eigenvalue of negative Hessian
-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
.

Proof.

At the critical point
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, we denote the negative Hessian as
\bm{H}:=-\nabla^{2}R_{S}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
. Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{H}
, with corresponding eigenvectors
\bm{q}_{11},\bm{q}_{12},\cdots,\bm{q}_{1l_{1}}
.

Denote
c_{j}=\langle\bm{\theta}_{0}-\bm{\theta}_{c},\bm{q}_{1j}\rangle,\forall 1\leq j\leq l_{1}
, and
\bm{v}_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{l_{1}}c_{j}\bm{q}_{1j}
. For a randomly selected
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, with probability 1, there exists at least one
j
such that
c_{j}\neq 0
.

Consider the path
\bm{z}_{\alpha}(t):=\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{v}_{1},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)
for every
\alpha>0
. By Prop.
A.6
, the limit
\bm{z}(t):=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\bm{z}_{\alpha}(t)
exists and satisfies the dynamics
\bm{z}(t)=\varphi(\bm{z}(0),t)
.

Furthermore,
\forall t\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left\|\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{\theta}_{0},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)-\bm{z}(t)\right\|_{2}\leq C\alpha^{\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}}

for every sufficiently small
\alpha
, where
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}>0
is the eigenvalue gap.

This implies that the limit
\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{\theta}_{0},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)
exists and

\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{\theta}_{c},t):=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{\theta}_{0},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)=\lim_{\alpha\to 0}\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{v}_{1},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right).

Assuming
\bm{\theta}_{c}\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}
and satisfies Assumps.
A.1
and
A.2
, we aim to show the existence of a rank-
(k+1)
invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}
containing
\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{v}_{1}
.

Define the following matrices:

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d^{2}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\right)_{i}\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\right)_{j}\right)^{\top},

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{S}\left[\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}-\bm{f}^{*}\right)_{i}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}^{2}\left(\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}_{c}}\right)_{i}\right].

The Hessian matrix
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
at
\bm{\theta}_{c}
can be expressed as
\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c}):=\bm{H}^{(1)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})+\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
, and we have
\bm{H}=-\bm{H}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
.

Assump.
A.1
and Lem.
A.2
imply that there exist exactly
d
eigenvectors associated with the top eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
-\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})
. These eigenvectors are of the form
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,d
, where
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a vector to be determined and
\bm{e}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Assump.
A.2
and Lem.
A.3
, the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
of
\bm{H}
are contained within the span of the eigenvectors associated with the negative eigenvalues of
\bm{H}^{(2)}(\bm{\theta}_{c})

Prop.
A.8
ensures that the escaping direction
\bm{v}_{1}
lies within a rank-1 invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{1}
. Lem.
A.4
then guarantees the existence of an invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}
that includes
\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{v}_{1}
. Since
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}
is invariant under the gradient flow, the trajectory
\varphi\left(\bm{\theta}_{c}+\alpha\bm{v}_{1},t+\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\log\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)
remains within
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}
.

Finally, since
\bm{\Omega}_{k+1}
is a closed subspace, the limit
\tilde{\varphi}(\bm{\theta}_{c},t)
lies in
\bar{\bm{\Omega}}_{k+1}
, concluding the proof.
∎

A.8
Minimum Rank

Theorem A.5

(
Minimum Rank
)
.

Let
\bm{\Omega}
denote an invariant as defined previously. Assume
\bm{W}_{t}
achieves a global minimum within each invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
. If the limit
\widehat{\bm{W}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\bm{W}_{\infty}(\alpha I)
exists and is a global optimum with
\widehat{\bm{W}}(i,j)=\bm{M}(i,j)
for all
(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}
, then

\widehat{\bm{W}}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{\bm{W}}\operatorname{rank}(\bm{W})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{W}(i,j)=\bm{M}(i,j),\forall(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}.

(38)

Proof.

Consider the invariant manifold
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
, which is defined as follows:

\bm{\Omega}_{k}:=\bm{\Omega}_{k}(\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k})=\{(\bm{A},\bm{B})|\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}\in\mathrm{span}\{\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k}\},\forall 1\leq i,j\leq d\},

where
\bm{a}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of
\bm{A}
,
\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}
denotes the
j
-th column of
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\alpha}_{k}
are independent vectors that span the invariant subspace associated with
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
.

According to Thm.
A.4
, the training trajectory adheres to a hierarchical traversal across invariant manifolds. For any matrix
\bm{C}
with
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{C})\leq k
, we will show that there always exists
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}
such that
\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{C}
. Therefore,
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
contains all matrices of rank
k
.

In fact, Since
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{C})\leq k
, we can express
\bm{C}
as a sum of
k
rank-one matrices:

\bm{C}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}

where
\bm{u}_{i}
and
\bm{v}_{i}
are column vectors.
By the definition of
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
, for any
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}
, each row of
\bm{A}
and each column of
\bm{B}
can be expressed as a linear combination of
\{\bm{\alpha}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\alpha}_{k}\}
:

\bm{a}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{ij}\bm{\alpha}_{j}

\bm{b}_{\cdot,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{ij}\bm{\alpha}_{i}

where
c_{ij}
and
d_{ij}
are scalars. We can write:

\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{1j}\bm{\alpha}_{j}\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{dj}\bm{\alpha}_{j}\end{bmatrix},\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i1}\bm{\alpha}_{i}&\cdots&\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{id}\bm{\alpha}_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Now, we can express the product
\bm{A}\bm{B}
as:

\bm{A}\bm{B}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{d}c_{li}d_{jl}\right)\bm{\alpha}_{i}\bm{\alpha}_{j}^{\top}

By choosing appropriate values for
c_{ij}
and
d_{ij}
, we can make
\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{C}
. This is possible because the outer products
\bm{\alpha}_{i}\bm{\alpha}_{j}^{\top}
span the same subspace as the rank-one matrices
\bm{u}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
in the expression of
\bm{C}
.

Therefore, for any matrix
\bm{C}
with
\textnormal{rank}(\bm{C})\leq k
, there always exists
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{A},\bm{B})\in\bm{\Omega}_{k}
such that
\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{C}
.

If the output matrix
\bm{W}_{t}
attains optimums within each
\bm{\Omega}_{k}
, it suggests that the optimization process is selecting the best approximation from the set of all possible rank-
k
matrices. Provided that each step in the optimization is optimal, the resulting solution will naturally be the matrix with the lowest feasible rank that satisfies the matrix completion criteria, thereby completing the proof.
∎

A.9
Minimum Nuclear Norm Guarantee

Lemma A.5

(
Minimal Nuclear Norm Computation
)
.

Given a matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed with observed diagonal entries, i.e.,
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{M})=\bm{v}
, the minimal nuclear norm solution among all possible completions is
\|\bm{v}\|_{1}
.

Proof.

The nuclear norm of a matrix is the dual of the spectral norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, defined as:

\|\bm{A}\|_{*}=\max_{\|\bm{X}\|_{2}\leq 1}\langle\bm{A},\bm{X}\rangle.

Given that
\|\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{sign}(\bm{v}))\|_{2}\leq 1
, for any matrix
\bm{A}
with
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{A})=\bm{v}
, it follows that:

\|\bm{A}\|_{*}\geq\langle\bm{A},\operatorname{diag}(\operatorname{sign}(\bm{v}))\rangle=\langle\bm{v},\operatorname{sign}(\bm{v})\rangle=\|\bm{v}\|_{1}.

Specifically, the nuclear norm of the diagonal matrix with
\bm{v}
on its diagonal is
\|\operatorname{diag}(\bm{v})\|_{*}=\|\bm{v}\|_{1}
, which establishes that the diagonal matrix with
\bm{v}
is indeed a minimizer for the nuclear norm.
∎

Diagonal Observations

Proposition A.9

(
Minimum Nuclear Norm Guarantee in Diagonal Case
)
.

Consider the dynamics
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, where
\bm{\theta}(t)=(\bm{A}(t),\bm{B}(t))
and denote
\bm{W}_{t}=\bm{A}(t)\bm{B}(t)
. If the observation data is diagonal, and if for a full rank initialization
\bm{W}_{0}
, the limit
\widehat{\bm{W}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\bm{W}_{\infty}(\alpha\bm{W}_{0})
exists and is a global optimum with
\widehat{\bm{W}}_{ij}=\bm{M}_{ij}
for all
(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}
, then

\widehat{\bm{W}}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{\bm{W}}\|\bm{W}\|_{*}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{W}_{ij}=\bm{M}_{ij},\forall(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}.

(39)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\bm{M}
is a diagonal matrix given by:

\bm{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}&&&\\
&\mu_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mu_{d}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

By Lem.
A.5
, the minimal nuclear norm among all possible completions is
|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
. When the matrix to be completed is diagonal, the evolution of the
i
-th row of
\bm{A}
is influenced only by the
i
-th column of
\bm{B}
. Hence, the dynamics decouple into
d
independent parts, each equivalent to learning a scalar
\mu_{i}
. The learning process thus unfolds in
d
stages, with each stage passing through a critical point to learn a respective
\mu_{i}
.

By Lem.
A.2
, the second term of the Hessian matrix can be expressed as:

\bm{H}^{(2)}=\left[\begin{matrix}\bm{0}&-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}\\
-\bm{\delta\bm{M}}^{\top}\otimes\bm{I_{d}}&\bm{0}\end{matrix}\right].

According to Lem.
A.2
, the
d
eigenvectors of
\bm{H}^{(2)}
take the form
\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{1},\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
, where
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a vector to be determined and
\bm{e}_{i}
is the unit vector corresponding to the
i
-th column of the identity matrix
\bm{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

(i) Suppose
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}>\cdots>\mu_{d}>0
.

With a infinitesimal initialization, the training dynamics first focus on the element with the largest singular value, then proceed sequentially to blocks with smaller singular values. This pattern of learning is consistent with the concept of “sequential learning” as reported in the literature
(
Gidel et al., 2019
;
Gissin et al., 2019
;
Jiang et al., 2023
)
.

For a diagonal observation matrix, the residual matrix
\delta\bm{M}
at any critical point remains a diagonal matrix. While starting to learn
\mu_{i}
from a critical point, direct calculation confirms that vector
\bm{v}=[\bm{e}_{i},\bm{e}_{i}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Escaping from each saddle point
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, the trajectory
\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}_{c}
approximates
\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})
, which satisfies
\bm{B}_{,i}=(\bm{A}^{\top})_{,i}
. Thus, learning a diagonal matrix
\bm{M}
using the asymmetric model
\bm{A}\bm{B}
is equivalent to using a symmetric model
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
. The final outcome ensures that
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{A}\bm{B})=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{M})
.

The nuclear norm of
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
equals the sum of its eigenvalues, which is precisely the trace of the matrix, and
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{M})=\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\cdots+\mu_{d}
. Therefore, the nuclear norm of the learned matrix
\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
remains
|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
.

(ii) If some
\mu_{i}<0
, assume without loss of generality that
|\mu_{1}|>|\mu_{2}|>\cdots>|\mu_{n}|>0
.

While starting to learn
\mu_{i}
from a critical point, direct calculation confirms that
\bm{v}=[\bm{e}_{i},\operatorname{sign}(\mu_{i})\bm{e}_{i}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Escaping from each saddle point
\bm{\theta}_{c}
, the trajectory
\bm{\theta}(t)-\bm{\theta}_{c}
approximates
\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(\bm{v}\otimes\bm{e}_{i})
, satisfying
\bm{B}_{,i}=\operatorname{sign}(\mu_{i})(\bm{A}^{\top})_{,i}
. Hence,
\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Q}
, where
\bm{Q}
is an orthogonal matrix given by:

\bm{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{sign}(\mu_{1})&&&\\
&\operatorname{sign}(\mu_{2})&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\operatorname{sign}(\mu_{d})\\
\end{bmatrix}.

The final result ensures that
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{A}\bm{B})=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Q})=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{M})
, meaning
\mathrm{diag}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{Q}\bm{M})
. The nuclear norm of
\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
equals the sum of its eigenvalues, which is the trace of the matrix, and
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{Q}\bm{M})=|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
.

Since an orthogonal transformation does not change the nuclear norm of a matrix, the nuclear norm of the final learned matrix
\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Q}
is still
|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
.
∎

Disconnected with Complete Bipartite Components

Theorem A.6

(
Minimum Nuclear Norm Guarantee
)
.

Consider the dynamics
\dot{\bm{\theta}}=-\nabla R_{S}(\bm{\theta})
, where
\bm{\theta}(t)=(\bm{A}(t),\bm{B}(t))
, and let
\bm{W}_{t}=\bm{A}(t)\bm{B}(t)
. If the observation graph associated with the matrix
\bm{M}
to be completed is disconnected with complete bipartite components, and if for a full rank initialization
\bm{W}_{0}
, the limit
\widehat{\bm{W}}=\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\bm{W}_{\infty}(\alpha\bm{W}_{0})
exists and is a global optimum with
\widehat{\bm{W}}_{ij}=\bm{M}_{ij}
for all
(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}
, then

\widehat{\bm{W}}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{\bm{W}}\|\bm{W}\|_{*}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{W}_{ij}=\bm{M}_{ij},\forall(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}.

(40)

Proof.

Consider a matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
composed of
m
connected components, with each component forming a complete bipartite subgraph. Since
\bm{M}
is disconnected, it can be represented in a block diagonal form without loss of generality:

\bm{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{M}_{1}&&&\\
&\bm{M}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\bm{M}_{m}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where each block
\bm{M}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times d_{i}^{\prime}}
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}=d,\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{i}^{\prime}=d
, representing the sum of the dimensions of the blocks.

Each block
\bm{M}_{i}
corresponds some singular values of the corresponding Hessian matrix at a critical point. With a infinitesimal initialization, the training dynamics first focus on the block with the largest singular value, then proceed sequentially to blocks with smaller singular values. This pattern of learning is consistent with the concept of “sequential learning” as reported in the literature
(
Gidel et al., 2019
;
Gissin et al., 2019
;
Jiang et al., 2023
)
.

Since each connected component of
\bm{M}
forms a complete bipartite subgraph, the block
\bm{M}_{i}
is fully observed. We can do singular value decomposition (SVD) on each sub-block
\bm{M}_{i}
as
\bm{M}_{i}=\bm{U}_{i}\Sigma_{i}\bm{V}_{i}^{\top}
, where
\bm{U}_{i}
and
\bm{V}_{i}
are orthogonal matrices, and
\Sigma_{i}
is a diagonal matrix with the singular values of
\bm{M}_{i}
.

Construct block diagonal matrices
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
as follows:

\bm{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{U}_{1}&&&\\
&\bm{U}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\bm{U}_{m}\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{V}_{1}&&&\\
&\bm{V}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\bm{V}_{m}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

This leads to the diagonal matrix:

\bm{U}\bm{M}\bm{V}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}&&&\\
&\bm{\Sigma}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\bm{\Sigma}_{m}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1}&&&\\
&\mu_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mu_{d}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Orthogonal transformations preserve the nuclear norm, so by Lem.
A.5
, the minimal nuclear norm among all possible completions is the sum of the absolute values of the diagonal entries, i.e.,
|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
.

Consider an incomplete matrix
\bm{M}
whose associated observational graph is divided into
m
connected components, denoted by
L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{m}
. For each component
L_{p}
, we define
S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}
as the subset of observed indices within
L_{p}
, where
1\leq p\leq m
and
S_{\bm{x}}
is the set of all observed indices.

For each
L_{p}
, we can identify row indices
R_{p}
and column indices
C_{p}
corresponding to the observed entries in
L_{p}
as follows:

R_{p}=\{i|\exists j:(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}\},\quad C_{p}=\{j|\exists i:(i,j)\in S_{\bm{x}}^{L_{p}}\}.

Here,
R_{p}
includes the row indices and
C_{p}
includes the column indices of the entries observed in
L_{p}
.

Define
\bm{A}^{L_{p}}
and
\bm{B}^{L_{p}}
as the submatrices of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
corresponding to
R_{p}
and
C_{p}
, respectively. The evolution of
\bm{A}^{L_{p}}
is influenced only by
\bm{B}^{L_{p}}
. Thus, the dynamics decouple into
m
independent parts, each equivalent to learning a fully observed matrix
\bm{M}_{i}
.

Accordingly, we partition
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
into
m
blocks:

\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{A}_{m}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{B}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{B}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{A}_{i}=\bm{A}^{L_{i}}
and
\bm{B}_{j}=\bm{B}^{L_{j}}
.

Denote
L_{i}=\|\bm{A}_{i}\bm{B}_{i}-\bm{M}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
. The overall loss function
L=\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}
can be decomposed into
m
independent parts. Performing orthogonal transformations
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{i}=\bm{U}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}_{i}
and
\tilde{\bm{B}}_{i}=\bm{B}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}
, we obtain a diagonal loss
\tilde{L}_{i}=\|\tilde{\bm{A}}_{i}\tilde{\bm{B}}_{i}-\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
for
1\leq i\leq C
.

Since gradient descent is the steepest descent in the
l_{2}
norm and orthogonal transformations preserve this norm, the dynamics of optimizing
\tilde{L}_{i}
are equivalent to those of optimizing
L_{i}
.

Without loss of generality, assume
\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}>\cdots>\mu_{d}>0
. Otherwise, as with Prop.
39
, a sign orthogonal transformation
\bm{Q}
can be applied without changing the nuclear norm.

By Prop.
39
, the learning result for a diagonal matrix implies
\tilde{\bm{B}}_{i}=\tilde{\bm{A}}_{i}^{\top}
, which means
\bm{B}_{i}\bm{V}_{i}=\bm{U}_{i}^{\top}\bm{A}_{i}^{\top}
.

The final learning result

\tilde{\bm{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{A}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\bm{A}}_{m}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{\bm{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{B}}_{1}&\cdots&\tilde{\bm{B}}_{m}\end{bmatrix},

satisfies
\tilde{\bm{B}}=\tilde{\bm{A}}^{\top}
.

The result ensures that
\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\bm{A}}\tilde{\bm{B}})=\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{\bm{A}}\tilde{\bm{A}}^{\top})=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{\Sigma})
. The nuclear norm of
\tilde{\bm{A}}\tilde{\bm{A}}^{\top}
equals the sum of its eigenvalues, which is the trace of the matrix, and
\textnormal{Tr}(\tilde{\bm{A}}\tilde{\bm{A}}^{\top})=\textnormal{Tr}(\Sigma)=|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
.

Since orthogonal transformations do not alter the nuclear norm of a matrix, the nuclear norm of
\bm{W}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
is also
|\mu_{1}|+|\mu_{2}|+\cdots+|\mu_{d}|
, concluding the proof.
∎

Experimental Setup

For all our experiments, we employ gradient descent with a carefully chosen small learning rate. Because of the small size of the experiment, the experiment can be completed on a single CPU.

B.4
Initialization Scale Analysis

Our experimental findings indicate that when the observational data is connected, matrix factorization models often learn the lowest-rank solution starting from a small initialization. However, the required scale of initialization is not constant across different instances. We empirically observed that if the magnitude of the numerical values in the matrix to be completed varies significantly, an extremely small initialization is necessary, which, in some cases, can exceed machine precision.

Consider the following two simple
2\times 2
matrix completion problems, with the only difference being that the number 3 in the first row is replaced by 20. When training begins from a small initialization, for
\bm{M}_{4}
, the fourth element only needs to learn the value 6 to be a rank-1 solution. However, for
\bm{M}_{5}
, the fourth element needs to learn the value 40 to achieve rank-1.

\bm{M}_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\
3&\times\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{M}_{5}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\
20&\times\end{bmatrix}.

Fig.
B6
illustrates the difficulty in learning these two examples. For
\bm{M}_{4}
, an initialization variance of approximately
10^{-7}
is sufficient to learn the lowest-rank solution. In contrast, for
\bm{M}_{5}
, an extremely small initialization variance is required, making it challenging to learn a rank-1 solution. Yet, with an exceedingly small initialization variance of
10^{-83}
, we can still observe the second singular value plummeting to zero. The origin is a saddle point. The smaller the initialization, the longer it will stay at the origin. If initialization continues to decrease, training will stagnate. Therefore, if the magnitude difference is even greater, such as replacing 20 with 100 in
\bm{M}_{5}
, then with the small initialization allowed by machine precision, it is nearly impossible to learn the value 200 completely.

For the matrix factorization model
\bm{f}_{\theta}=\bm{A}\bm{B}
, the Hessian matrix at 0 has strictly negative eigenvalues, making the origin a strict saddle point. Under small random initialization, gradient descent escapes this saddle at an exponential rate. Our Theorem 1 ensures that only strict saddle points and global minima exist as critical points in the loss landscape. Subsequent saddle points on invariant manifolds are also strict, with exponential escape speeds, maintaining acceptable optimization process.

Reaching the lowest rank solution requires parameters to escape the saddle point along the unique top eigen-direction. Fig.
B6
illustrates the relationship between required initialization scale and observation magnitude differences. For lowest possible rank with large numerical magnitude differences in observations, extremely small initialization is necessary. However, for approximately low rank solutions, a relatively small initialization suffices.

If the authors answer NA or No, they should explain why their work has no societal impact or why the paper does not address societal impact.

•

Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

•

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

•

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
