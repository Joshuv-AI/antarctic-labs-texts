On the sum of digits of 1 / M 1/M in 𝔽 q ​ [ x ] \mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
Zeév Rudnick

Abstract
. For certain primes p p , the average digit in the expansion of 1 / p 1/p was found to have a deviation from random behaviour related to the class number of the imaginary quadratic field ℚ ⁡ ( − p ) {\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p}) (Girstmair 1994). In this short note, we observe that for the corresponding problem when we replace the integers by polynomials over a finite field, there is never any bias. The argument is elementary.



1.
Integers

Let
b\geq 2
be an integer. It has long been known (see e.g.
[
1
, Chapter VI]
) that for primes
p
such that
\gcd(p,b)=1
, the base
b
expansion of
1/p
is purely periodic

\frac{1}{p}=0.\overline{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{T}}

where
a_{j}=a_{j}(p)\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}

and the period
T=\operatorname{ord}(b,p)
is the order of
b
in the multiplicative group of residues modulo
p
, and thus divides
p-1
. If
b\neq 1\bmod p
then the period is at least
2
.

Define the average digit by

A(p)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T}a_{j}=\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p-1}a_{j}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}

In many cases, e.g. if
T
is even (which happens for instance if
T=p-1
is maximal, that is, if
b
is a primitive root modulo
p
) then it is easy to see that the average is that of a random string, that is

(1)

A(p)=\frac{b-1}{2},\quad\operatorname{ord}(b,p)=p-1.

But in several interesting cases there is a bias. For instance, Girstmair
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
showed that if
\operatorname{ord}(b,p)=(p-1)/2
and
p=3\bmod 4
then

A(p)=\frac{b-1}{2}-\frac{(b-1)}{p-1}h_{p}

where
h_{p}
is the class number of the imaginary quadratic field
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-p})
. In particular, in this case we obtain
A(p)<(b-1)/2
. See also
[
7
,
6
]
.

2.
Polynomials

In this short note, stimulated by a lecture
[
2
]
of Frank Calegari in the Number Theory Web seminar on August 5, 2021, I investigate the corresponding problem when we replace the integers by polynomials over a finite field, and find that there is never any bias. The argument is elementary.

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field of
q
elements, and
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
the ring of polynomials with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For nonzero
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
we define the norm

|f|:=\#\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(f)=q^{\deg f}

and the Euler totient function
\Phi(f)=\#(\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(f))^{*}
, the size of the multiplicative group modulo
f
. For
P
irreducible, we have
\Phi(P)=|P|-1
.

Let
B(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
be a polynomial of positive degree. Then for any rational function
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q}(x)
we have the base
B
expansion

f(x)=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{J}a_{-j}(x)B(x)^{j}

with
a_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
,
\deg a_{j}<\deg B
(or possibly
a_{j}(x)=0
). The
a_{j}(x)
are the “base
B
digits of
f
” and we write

f(x)=a_{-J}a_{-(J-1)}\dots a_{0}\;.\;a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\dots

Now let
M\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
be a polynomial of positive degree, coprime to
B
.
As in the case of the integers, one sees that the base
B
expansion of
1/M(x)
is periodic:

\frac{1}{M}=0.\overline{a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{T}}

and the minimal period
T
is the order of
B
in the multiplicative group
(\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]/(M))^{*}
, hence divides
\Phi(M)
.
If
B\neq 1\bmod M
then the period
T
is at least
2
, see examples in tables
1
and
2
.

3.
Sum of digits

We define the sum of digits function by

S_{B}(M)(x)=a_{1}(x)+a_{2}(x)+\dots+a_{T}(x)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]

which is a polynomial of degree less than
\deg B
. If
M=P
is irreducible, then
T
divides
\Phi(P)=|P|-1=q^{\deg P}-1
, which is an integer coprime to the characteristic of the finite field, and so by analogy with the integers, we can define the average digit by

A_{B}(P)(x)=\frac{1}{T}S_{B}(P)(x)=\frac{1}{|P|-1}\sum_{j=1}^{|P|-1}a_{j}(x).

If
B=1\bmod M
, that is
M
divides
B-1
, then writing
B-1=KM
gives an expansion with period one:

\frac{1}{M}=0.\overline{K}

and
S_{B}(M)=K
.
It turns out that in all other cases, the sum of digits (hence the average digit
A_{B}(P)
for
P
prime) always vanishes:

Theorem 1
.

If
M
is coprime to
B(B-1)
then
S_{B}(M)(x)=0
.

Proof.

The proof is elementary and straightforward. We write

B(x)^{T}-1=M(x)K(x)

and use the base
B
expansion of
K
:

K(x)=k_{0}(x)+k_{1}(x)B+\dots+k_{T-1}(x)B(x)^{T-1}

where
\deg k_{j}<\deg B
.
Then the “digits” of
1/M
are simply

a_{1}=k_{T-1},\;a_{2}=k_{T-2},\dots,a_{T}=k_{0}

and

(2)

S(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}k_{j}(x).

This is because

\begin{split}\frac{1}{M}&=\frac{K}{B^{T}-1}=\frac{K}{B^{T}}\left(1+\frac{1}{B^{T}}+\frac{1}{B^{2T}}+\dots\right)\\
&=\frac{k_{0}+k_{1}B+\dots+k_{T-1}B^{T-1}}{B^{T}}\left(1+\frac{1}{B^{T}}+\frac{1}{B^{2T}}+\dots\right)\\
&=\frac{k_{T-1}}{B}+\frac{k_{T-2}}{B^{2}}+\dots=0.\overline{k_{T-1}k_{T-2}\dots k_{0}}.\end{split}

Therefore we find

S(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}k_{j}(x).

Next, note that
K=S\bmod B-1
, because
B=1\;\operatorname{mod}B-1
and so

K=\sum_{j}k_{j}B^{j}=\sum k_{j}\bmod B-1

which equals
S
by (
2
).

On the other hand,

KM=B^{T}-1=(B-1)(B^{T-1}+\dots+1)=0\bmod B-1

and so
B-1\mid KM
. But by assumption,
\gcd(M,B-1)=1
and so
B-1\mid K
, and therefore

S=K=0\bmod B-1.

But
\deg S<\deg B=\deg(B-1)
while
B-1\mid S
which implies
S(x)=0
.
∎
