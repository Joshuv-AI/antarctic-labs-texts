Quantum radiation from a classical point source
J. Dimock

Abstract
We study the radiation of photons from a classical charged particle. We particularly consider a situation where the particle has a constant velocity in the distant past, then is accelerated, and then has a constant velocity in the distant future. Starting with no photons in the distant past we seek to characterize the quantum state of the photon field in the distant future. Working in the Coulomb gauge and in a C* algebra formulation, we give sharp conditions on whether this state is or is not in Fock space.



1
Introduction

We study the radiation of the quantum electromagnetic field in the presence of a classical source.
With a specified four-current consisting of functions
(j^{0},j)
satisfying
\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}=0
, one seeks associated quantum field operators
A=(A^{0},A)
. In the Lorentz gauge
\partial_{\mu}A^{\mu}=0
these are connected by the wave equation with source

\square A^{\mu}=j^{\mu}
. A textbook treatment of this problem can be found in Itzykson-Zuber
[
4
]
whose sign conventions we follow. A mathematical treatment is due
to Naudts-DeRoeck
[
6
]
. A characteristic phenomena is that
if one starts in a state with no photons in the distant past, the vacuum in Fock space, it may evolve into a state in the distant future with
infinitely many photons radiated and not in Fock space. This is sometimes known as the infrared catastrophe.

In this paper we are interested in characterizing exactly when this happens. We particularly study the case of a point source
where the four-current has the form

\begin{split}j^{0}(x,t)=&\delta(x-x(t))\\
j(x,t)=&x^{\prime}(t)\delta(x-x(t))\\
\end{split}

(1)

and
x(t)
is the trajectory of the charged particle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We suppose that
the particle has a velocity
v_{in}
in the distant past, then accelerates for a finite amount of time, and then continues with a velocity
v_{out}
in the distant future. We start with the Fock vacuum in the distant past considered as a state
\omega_{in}
on
the
C^{*}
-algebra generated by the fields. It evolves to
state
\omega_{out}
in the distant future. We establish the following results

•

If
v_{out}=v_{in}=0
and the trajectory
x(t)
is smooth then
\omega_{out}
is a Fock state.

•

If
v_{out}=v_{in}=0
and the velocity
x^{\prime}(t)
has a discontinuity then
\omega_{out}
is not a Fock state.

•

In general for a smooth trajectory
\omega_{out}
is a Fock state if and only if
v_{in}=v_{out}
.

We work
in the Coulomb gauge rather than the Lorentz gauge. The advantage is that we avoid working with indefinite metric Hilbert spaces.
The disadvantage is that relativistic properties like Lorentz invariance and locality are not manifest, although they are still there. (See
Skagerstam, Erikson, Rekdal
[
7
]
for a discussion of locality in the Coulomb gauge.)

The Coulomb gauge is defined by the condition
\nabla\cdot A=0
on the magnetic potential.
In this gauge Maxwells equations become

\begin{split}-\Delta A^{0}=&j^{0}\\
\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial t^{2}}-\Delta A+\nabla\frac{\partial}{\partial t}A^{0}=&j\\
\end{split}

(2)

The first equation for the electrostatic potential
A^{0}
has the solution

A^{0}(x,t)=\int V(x-y)j^{0}(t,y)dy

(3)

where
V(x)=(4\pi)^{-1}|x|^{-1}
is the Coulomb potential.
In the second equation we call the term
\nabla(\partial A^{0}/\partial t)
the longitudinal current. Since
\partial j^{0}/\partial t+\nabla\cdot j=0

this can be expressed as

j_{L}(x,t)=\nabla(\partial A^{0}/\partial t)(x,t)=-\nabla\int V(x-y)(\nabla\cdot j)(y,t)dy

(4)

It satisfies
\nabla\cdot j_{L}=\nabla\cdot j
.
The transverse current is defined by
j_{T}=j-j_{L}
and it satisfies
\nabla\cdot j_{T}=0
.
The second equation in (
2
) and the constraint can now be written

\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial t^{2}}-\Delta A=j_{T}\hskip 28.45274pt\nabla\cdot A=0

(5)

or just
\square A=j_{T}
with
\square=\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta
.

The electrostatic potential
A^{0}
is given by (
3
) and is not quantized. We seek quantum field operators
A

satisfying (
5
).

2
Preliminaries

2.1
fundamental solutions

We review some facts about solutions of the wave equation with source. At first this is just classical so
we seek functions
A
satisfying
\square A=j
. We are interested in solutions of the form
A=G_{\pm}*j
where
G_{\pm}
are retarded and advanced fundamental solutions which we define shortly. We want to allow
j
to be certain distributions so
G_{\pm}*j
is defined as

<G_{\pm}*j,\phi>=<j,G_{\mp}*\phi>
for
\phi
in (vector-valued)
{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
, the Schwartz space of smooth rapidly decreasing functions.
However we also want to work at sharp time
so the test functions
\phi
will be replaced by
f\otimes\delta_{t}
with
f
in (vector-valued)
{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The upshot is
that we want to define functions
G_{\pm}*(f\otimes\delta_{t})
, or without the convolution
G_{\pm}(f\otimes\delta_{t})\equiv G_{\pm}(f,t)
.

The
G_{\pm}
are given by

G_{\pm}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{p_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\pm i\epsilon}e^{i(-p_{0}t+px)}\frac{1}{p_{0}^{2}-|p|^{2}}dp_{0}dp

(6)

For
f\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
this is to be understood as
for
G_{\pm}(f,t)=\int f(x)G_{\pm}(x,t)dx
given by

G_{\pm}(f,t)=-(2\pi)^{-\frac{5}{2}}\int_{p_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\pm i\epsilon}\frac{1}{p_{0}^{2}-|p|^{2}}e^{-ip_{0}t}\tilde{f}(-p)\ dp_{0}dp

(7)

where
\tilde{f}(p)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int e^{-ipx}f(x)dx
is the Fourier transform of
f
.

Consider
G_{+}(f,t)
. We have
|e^{-ip_{0}t}|=e^{\textrm{Im}(p_{0})t}
so
if
t<0
we can close the
p_{0}
-contour in the upper half plane and get zero. On the other hand If
t>0
we we close the contour in the lower half plane, picking up
poles at
p_{0}=\pm\omega
where
\omega(p)=|p|
. Similarly for
G_{-}(f,t)
. We obtain

G_{\pm}(f,t)=\pm(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\theta(\pm t)i\int\Big(e^{-i\omega t}-e^{i\omega t}\Big)\tilde{f}(-p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}

(8)

where
\theta
is the Heaviside function.

The convolution is defined by

(G_{\pm}*(f\otimes\delta_{t_{0}}))(x,t)=G_{\pm}\Big(f(x-\cdot),\delta_{t_{0}}(t-\cdot)\Big)
.
But the Fourier transform of
f(x-\cdot)
at
-p
is
e^{ipx}\tilde{f}(p)
and
\delta_{t_{0}}(t-\cdot)=\delta_{t-t_{0}}
.
Thus we have

(G_{\pm}*(f\otimes\delta_{t_{0}}))(x,t)=\pm(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\theta(\pm(t-t_{0}))i\int\Big(e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}-e^{i\omega(t-t_{0})}\Big)e^{ipx}\tilde{f}(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}

(9)

We also will want to consider the propagator
G=G_{+}-G_{-}
. In this expression the Heaviside functions
\theta
disappear
and we have

(G*(f\otimes\delta_{t_{0}}))(x,t)=u_{f}(x,t-t_{0})

(10)

where

u_{f}(x,t)=(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}i\int\Big(e^{-i\omega t}-e^{i\omega t}\Big)e^{ipx}\tilde{f}(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}

(11)

is a smooth solution of the wave equation with data at
t=0

u_{f}(x,0)=0\hskip 28.45274pt\frac{\partial u_{f}}{\partial t}(x,0)=f(x)

(12)

2.2
free quantum field

We review the mathematics of the quantized free field in the Coulomb gauge.
In this case we can take
A^{0}=0
, and study the field equation

\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial t^{2}}-\Delta A=0

(13)

We want to define a quantum field operator satisfying the equation,
the constraint
\nabla\cdot A=0
, and the canonical commutation relations.

It is convenient to use the relativistic Hilbert space which is (vector valued)

{\cal H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},(2\omega)^{-1}dp)

(14)

again with
\omega(p)=|p|
. On this space define
a projection operator.

(Ph)_{j}(p)=\sum^{3}_{k=1}\Big(\delta_{jk}-\frac{p_{j}p_{k}}{|p|^{2}}\Big)h_{k}(p)

(15)

which is the orthogonal projection onto the space of transverse functions
satisfying
p\cdot h(p)=0
.
The one photon Hilbert space is
{\cal H}_{T}=P{\cal H}
,
the n-photon Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{T}^{n}
is the n-fold symmetric tensor product of
{\cal H}_{T}
with itself, and the Fock space over
{\cal H}_{T}
is

{\cal F}({\cal H}_{T})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\cal H}_{T}^{n}

(16)

For
h\in{\cal H}_{T}
let

a^{*}(h)
and
a(h)
be the creation and annihilation operators
on the Fock space, with
a(h)
anti-linear in
h
and

a^{*}(h)
linear in
h
. These operators (and all operators in this section) are defined on the dense subspace
{\cal D}
consisting
elements of Fock space with a finite number of particles. They satisfy
[a(h_{1}),a^{*}(h_{2})]=(h_{1},h_{2})
. We also define momentum space fields
A_{\circ}(h)
and
\Pi_{\circ}(h)=A_{\circ}(ih)

by

\begin{split}A_{\circ}(h)=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(a^{*}(h)+a(h)\Big)\\
\Pi_{\circ}(h)=&\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(a^{*}(h)-a(h)\Big)\\
\end{split}

(17)

These are real linear in
h
, symmetric, and satisfy
[A_{\circ}(h_{1}),A_{\circ}(h_{2})]=i\textrm{Im}(h_{1},h_{2})
.

The field operator is defined as a distribution. For a real test function
f\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

define
A(f,t)
(formally
\int f(x)A(x,t)dx
) by

A(f,t)=\sqrt{2}A_{\circ}\Big(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T}\Big)=a^{*}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})+a(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})

(18)

where
\tilde{f}_{T}\equiv P\tilde{f}
. This satisfies the field equation

\frac{\partial^{2}A}{\partial t^{2}}(f,t)-A(\Delta f,t)=0

(19)

It also satisfies
\nabla\cdot A=0
since for scalar
g\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we have
(\nabla\cdot A)(g,t)\equiv-A(\nabla g,t)
and

(\nabla g)_{T}^{\sim}(p)=iP(p)p\tilde{g}(p)=0
.
We have the commutation relations

[A(f_{1},t),A(f_{2},t)]=2\textrm{Im}(\tilde{f}_{1,T},\tilde{f}_{2,T})=0

(20)

The vanishing follows since for
f
real
\overline{\tilde{f}(p)}=\tilde{f}(-p)
so
(\tilde{f}_{1,T},\tilde{f}_{2,T})
is real.

The field has a conjugate momentum
\Pi(h,t)=\partial/\partial t\ A(h,t)
given by

\Pi(f,t)=\sqrt{2}\Pi_{\circ}\Big(e^{i\omega t}\omega\tilde{f}_{T}\Big)=i\Big(a^{*}(e^{i\omega t}\omega\tilde{f}_{T})-a(e^{i\omega t}\omega\tilde{f}_{T})\Big)

(21)

The
\Pi(f,t)
commute with each other and satisfy
the canonical commutation relations (CCR)

[A(f_{1},t),\Pi(f_{2},t)]=i(\tilde{f}_{1,T},2\omega\tilde{f}_{2,T})_{{\cal H}}=i(\tilde{f}_{1,T},\tilde{f}_{2,T})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3},dp)}

(22)

2.3
the
C^{*}
algebra and coherent states

We want to allow states for this system which are not Fock states, and for this we focus on the algebra of the fields.
See for example Bratteli-Robinson
[
1
]
for more details on this standard material. We consider operators
A_{\circ}(h)
for
h
in a dense subspace

{\cal S}\subset{\cal H}_{T}
. The operator is essentially self-adjoint on the domain
{\cal D}\subset{\cal F}({\cal H}_{T})
, and so
we can define

W_{\circ}(h)=e^{iA_{\circ}(h)}\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pth\in{\cal S}

(23)

This satisfies

W_{\circ}(h_{1})W_{\circ}(h_{2})=e^{-\frac{i}{2}\textrm{Im}(h_{1},h_{2})}W_{\circ}(h_{1}+h_{2})

(24)

This is the Weyl form of the CCR.
The operators
W_{\circ}(h)
generate a
C^{*}
algebra, denoted
\EuFrak{A}
, which is the closure of the set of operators which are finite sums
\sum_{n}c_{n}W_{\circ}(h_{n})

in the Banach space of bounded operators on
{\cal F}({\cal H}_{T})
. Different choices of the dense subspace
{\cal S}
give different algebras, and
we will make a specific choice later.

A state
\omega
on the algebra is a positive linear functional of norm one. Positivity is the condition that
\omega(B^{*}B)\geq 0
for any
B\in\EuFrak{A}
. Any normalized state in Fock space defines a state on the algebra.
In particular the Fock vacuum
\Omega_{0}
defines a state by
\omega_{0}(\ \cdot\ )=(\Omega_{0},[\ \cdot\ ]\Omega_{0})
.
It satisfies
a(h)\Omega_{0}=0
and is characterized by

\omega_{0}(W_{\circ}(h))=(\Omega_{0},e^{iA_{0}(h)}\Omega_{0})=(\Omega_{0},e^{ia^{*}(h)/\sqrt{2}}e^{ia(h)/\sqrt{2}}\Omega_{0})e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|h\|^{2}}=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|h\|^{2}}

(25)

Another class of states are the coherent states. Given
J\in{\cal H}_{T}
and the Fock vacuum
\Omega_{0}
we define the coherent state

\Omega_{J}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|J\|^{2}}e^{a^{*}(J)}\Omega_{0}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|J\|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}(a^{*}(J))^{n}\Omega_{0}

(26)

The series converges in Fock space since
\|(a^{*}(J))^{n}\Omega_{0}\|=\sqrt{n!}\|J\|^{n}
. It is normalized to
\|\Omega_{J}\|=1

and is an eigenstate of the annihilation operator:

a(h)\Omega_{J}=(h,J)\Omega_{J}

(27)

As a state on the algebra
\omega_{h}=(\Omega_{h},[\ \cdot\ ]\Omega_{h})

it satisfies

\begin{split}\omega_{J}(W_{\circ}(h))=&(\Omega_{J},e^{iA_{0}(h)}\Omega_{J})\\
=&(\Omega_{J},e^{ia^{*}(h)/\sqrt{2}}e^{ia(h)/\sqrt{2}}\Omega_{J})e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|h\|^{2}}\\
=&e^{i\overline{(h,J)}/\sqrt{2}}e^{i(h,J)/\sqrt{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|h\|^{2}}\\
=&e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|h\|^{2}+i\sqrt{2}\textrm{Re}(J,h)}\\
\end{split}

(28)

More generally we define a coherent state on
\EuFrak{A}
to be a state satisfying

\omega(W_{\circ}(h))=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|h\|^{2}+i\sqrt{2}\textrm{Re}L(h)}

(29)

for some linear function
L
on the dense domain
{\cal S}
. It can also be written

\omega(W_{\circ}(h))=\omega_{0}(W_{\circ}(h))e^{i\sqrt{2}\textrm{Re}L(h)}

(30)

The positivity condition is satisfied since for any finite sequence of complex numbers
c_{n}
and
h_{n}\in{\cal S}
and
B=\sum_{n}c_{n}W_{\circ}(h_{n})

we have

\begin{split}\omega(B^{*}B)=&\sum_{n,m}\bar{c}_{m}c_{n}\ \omega\Big(W_{\circ}(h_{n}-h_{m})\Big)e^{\frac{i}{2}\textrm{Im}(h_{n},h_{m})}\\
=&\sum_{n,m}\Big(\bar{c}_{m}e^{-i\sqrt{2}\textrm{Re}L(f_{m})}\Big)\Big(c_{n}e^{i\sqrt{2}\textrm{Re}L(f_{n})}\Big)\ \omega_{0}\Big(W_{\circ}(h_{n}-h_{m})\Big)e^{\frac{i}{2}\textrm{Im}(h_{n},h_{m})}\\
=&\omega_{0}(B_{0}^{*}B_{0})\geq 0\\
\end{split}

(31)

where

B_{0}=\sum_{n}c_{n}e^{i\sqrt{2}\textrm{Re}L(f_{n})}W_{\circ}(h_{n})

(32)

The coherent state
\omega
defined by
L
is known to be a pure state (Honegger-Rapp
[
3
]
).
Furthermore
\omega
is a Fock
state if and only if
L
is continuous on the pre-Hilbert space
{\cal S}
(Honegger-Rieckers
[
2
]
). This is the criterion we will use in the following.

3
Regular source

To warm up we first consider our problem with a regular source.
We take
j\in{\cal C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
and seek quantum field operators which solve

\square A=j_{T}
and
\nabla\cdot A=0
.
Note that partial Fourier transform
\tilde{j}(p,t)
is bounded and rapidly decreasing in
p
as is

\tilde{j}_{T}(p,t)=P(p)\tilde{j}(p,t)

The inverse Fourier transform
j_{T}(x,t)
then has compact support in
t
, and for each
t
is bounded and continuous in
x
(since
\tilde{j}(p,t)

is integrable in
p
) and square integrable in
x
(since
\tilde{j}(p,t)
is square integrable in
p
). The localization of
j_{T}(x,t)
in
x
is
considerably weaker
than that of
j(x,t)
.

We want a solution which is a free field in the distant past and the distant future. Therefore
we take
A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
to be a free field defined from creation and annihilation
operators
a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h),a_{\textrm{in}}(h)
as in (
18
).
Then for real
f\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we define

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)+(G_{+}*j_{T})(f,t)

(33)

The expression
G_{+}*j_{T}
can be analyzed pointwise. Indeed we have explicitly

(G_{\pm}*j_{T})(x,t)=\pm(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}i\int\theta(\pm(t-s))i\Big(e^{-i\omega(t-s)}-e^{i\omega(t-s)t}\Big)e^{ipx}\tilde{j}_{T}(p,s)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\ ds

(34)

Then
(G_{+}*j_{T})(x,t)
vanishes in the distant past since
j_{T}(p,s)
does, and hence

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
in the distant past.
We have
\square(G*j_{T})=j_{T}
, also in the sense of distributions, and hence
\square A=j_{T}
. Furthermore
\nabla\cdot(G*j_{T})
, also in the sense of distributions, and hence
\nabla\cdot A=0

Next we define
A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)
by

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)+(G_{-}*j_{T})(f,t)

(35)

Then
A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)
in the distant future since
(G_{-}*j_{T})(f,t)
vanishes in the distant future.
Combining (
33
) and (
35
) and identifying
G=G_{+}-G_{-}
gives

A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)+(G*j_{T})(f,t)

(36)

where now

\begin{split}(G*j_{T})(x,t)&=(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}i\int\Big(e^{-i\omega(t-s)}-e^{i\omega(t-s)}\Big)e^{ipx}\tilde{j}_{T}(p,s)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\ ds\\
&=(2\pi)^{-1}\ i\int\Big(e^{-i\omega t}\hat{j}_{T}(\omega,p)-e^{i\omega t}\hat{j}_{T}(-\omega,p)\Big)e^{ipx}\frac{dp}{2\omega}\\
\end{split}

(37)

where
\hat{j}
is the four dimensional Fourier transform with Lorentz inner product:

\hat{j}(p_{0},p)=(2\pi)^{-2}\int e^{i(p_{0}t-px)}j(x,t)\ dxdt=(2\pi)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int e^{ip_{0}t}\tilde{j}(p,t)dt

(38)

Then
G*j_{T}
satisfies
\square(G*j_{T})=0
and
\nabla\cdot(G*j_{T})=0
, and hence so does
A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)

Furthermore
together with

\Pi_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=\partial A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)/\partial t

it satisfies the CCR. Thus
A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)
is a free field.

In the second term in (
37
) make the change of variable
p\to-p
. Since
j
is real we have

\overline{\hat{j}_{T}(\omega,p)}=\hat{j}_{T}(-\omega,-p)
and
the second term is identified as the complex conjugate of the first term.
Thus if we define

J(p)=-\sqrt{2\pi}\ i\ \hat{j}(\omega,p)

(39)

and
J_{T}(p)=P(p)J(p)

then we have

(G*j_{T})(x,t)=-(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}2\ \textrm{Re}\ \int e^{-i(\omega t-px)}J_{T}(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}

(40)

This gives

(G*j_{T})(f,t)=-2\ \textrm{Re}\ \int\tilde{f}(-p)e^{-i\omega t}J_{T}(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}=-2\ \textrm{Re}\ (e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T},J_{T})

(41)

Now we have

A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)-2\ \textrm{Re}\ (e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T},J_{T})

(42)

We define creation and annihilation operators by taking what amounts to the positive and negative frequency parts of the last expression.
We define for
h\in{\cal H}_{T}

\begin{split}a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(h)=&a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h)-(J_{T},h)\\
a_{\textrm{out}}(h)=&a_{\textrm{in}}(h)-(h,J_{T})\\
\end{split}

(43)

These satisfy the standard
[a_{\textrm{out}}(h_{2}),a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(h_{2})]=(h_{1},h_{2})
.
Then

\begin{split}a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})=&a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})-(J_{T},e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})\\
a_{\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})=&a_{\textrm{in}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})-(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T},J_{T})\\
\end{split}

(44)

If we add these we get
A_{in}(f,t)-2\ \textrm{Re}\ (e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T},J_{T})=A_{out}(f,t)
on the right. Thus we must get the
same on the left which says

A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})+a_{\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})

(45)

just as for
A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
.

The out vacuum in Fock space should satisfy
a_{\textrm{out}}(h)\Omega_{\textrm{out}}=0
.
Note that
j\in{\cal C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
implies that
\hat{j}
is bounded and rapidly decreasing, hence
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
.
Therefore we can construct the coherent state

\Omega_{\textrm{out}}=\Omega_{J_{T}}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|J_{T}\|^{2}}\exp(a_{in}^{*}(J_{T}))\Omega_{\textrm{in}}

(46)

This gives the the desired

a_{\textrm{out}}(h)\Omega_{\textrm{out}}=\Big(a_{\textrm{in}}(h)-(h,J_{T})\Big)\Omega_{\textrm{out}}=0

(47)

We summarize:

Proposition 1
.

Let
j\in{\cal C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
. Then
J_{T}(p)=-\sqrt{2\pi}\ i\ \hat{j}_{T}(\omega,p)
is in
{\cal H}_{T}
and the out
vacuum
\Omega_{out}
exists in Fock space.

Remarks
.

1.

To obtain this result can certainly relax the condition that
j\in{\cal C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. The key condition is is
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
which follows
from
J\in{\cal H}
which says

\int|\hat{j}(\omega,p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}<\infty

(48)

This is similar to a condition that Naudts - De Roeck
[
6
]
found in the Lorentz gauge.

2.

We sketch some further developments of the scattering theory associated with a regular source.
As in (
17
) define
\Pi_{\circ,\textrm{in}}(h)=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(a_{\textrm{in}}^{*}(h)-a_{\textrm{in}}(h)\Big)
.
This is essentially self-adjoint on
{\cal D}
and we define a unitary operator by

S=\exp\Big(i\sqrt{2}\Pi_{\circ,\textrm{in}}(J_{T})\Big)=\exp\Big(-a_{\textrm{in}}^{*}(J_{T})+a_{\textrm{in}}(J_{T})\Big)

(49)

We have

\begin{split}S^{-1}\Omega_{\textrm{in}}=&\exp\Big(a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(J_{T})-a_{\textrm{in}}(J_{T})\Big)\Omega_{\textrm{in}}\\
=&e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|J_{T}\|^{2}}\exp\Big(a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(J_{T})\Big)\exp\Big(-a_{\textrm{in}}(J_{T})\Big)\Omega_{\textrm{in}}\\
=&e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|J_{T}\|^{2}}\exp\Big(a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(J_{T})\Big)\Omega_{\textrm{in}}=\Omega_{\textrm{out}}\\
\end{split}

(50)

and

S^{-1}a_{\textrm{in}}(h)S=a_{\textrm{in}}(h)+[a_{\textrm{in}}^{*}(J_{T}),a_{\textrm{in}}(h)]=a_{\textrm{in}}(h)-(h,J_{T})=a_{\textrm{out}}(h)

(51)

So the in and out fields are unitarily equivalent.

One can form other asymptotic states by applying creation operators
a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(h)
to
\Omega_{\textrm{out}}
and then form scattering amplitudes
like

\Big(a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(h^{\prime}_{1})\cdots a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(h^{\prime}_{m})\Omega_{\textrm{out}},a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h_{1})\cdots a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h_{n})\Omega_{\textrm{in}}\Big)

(52)

This can also be written

\Big(a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h^{\prime}_{1})\cdots a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h^{\prime}_{m})\Omega_{in},S\ a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h_{1})\cdots a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h_{n})\Omega_{\textrm{in}}\Big)

(53)

and
S
is revealed as a scattering operator.

4
Point source - I

Again we study the equations
\square A=j_{T}
and
\nabla\cdot A=0
and look for solutions which are free in the distant past
and future.
We now specialize to a point source with current
j(t,x)=x^{\prime}(t)\delta(x-x(t))
. We assume in this section that
x^{\prime}(t)
has compact support.
So the particle is at rest in the distant past, accelerates for finite amount of time, and then stops in the distant future.

Again
we take
A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
to be a free field as defined in (
18
) for real
f\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
Then we define

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)+(G_{+}*j_{T})(f,t)

(54)

Now we do not define
G_{+}*j_{T}
pointwise, but treat it as a distribution and proceed by throwing
everything onto the test function
f
.
Since

G_{+}(x,t)=G_{-}(-x,-t)
we interpret
(G_{+}*j_{T})(f,t)
as

\Big\langle G_{+}*j_{T},f\otimes\delta_{t}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle j_{T},G_{-}*(f\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle

(55)

Then formally we can transfer the transverse projection
from
j
and to
f
and get

\Big\langle j_{T},G_{-}*(f\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle=\Big\langle j,G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle

(56)

The latter is well-defined for our current since
G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})
as given by (
9
)
is a bounded function continuous in the spatial variable.
With this interpretation (
54
) becomes

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)+\Big\langle j,G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle

(57)

Since
j
has compact support and
G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})
vanishes to the future of
t
, we have that
<j,G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})>
vanishes
for
t
sufficiently negative and so

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
in the distant past. Since
A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
is free and since
G_{-}
is a fundamental solution
we have

\begin{split}\square A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=&\frac{\partial^{2}A_{\textrm{out}}}{\partial t^{2}}(f,t)-A_{\textrm{out}}(\Delta f,t)\\
=&\Big\langle j,\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})-G_{-}*((\Delta f)_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle\\
=&\Big\langle j,\square(G_{-}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t}))\Big\rangle=\Big\langle j,f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t}\Big\rangle\equiv\Big\langle j_{T},f\otimes\delta_{t}\Big\rangle\\
\end{split}

(58)

So
A
satisfies
\square A=j_{T}
in the sense of distributions. Similarly
\nabla\cdot A=0
.

Next we define
A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)
by

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)+G_{-}*j_{T}(f,t)

(59)

which is interpreted as

A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)+\Big\langle j,G_{+}*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle

(60)

Then
A(f,t)=A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)
in the distant future.

Combining (
57
) and (
60
) gives

A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)-\Big\langle j,G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle

(61)

Then
A_{\textrm{out}}
satisfies
\square A_{\textrm{out}}=0
and
\nabla\cdot A_{\textrm{out}}=0
in the sense of distributions
and is a free field.

Now take the expression
(G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t}))(x,s)=u_{f_{T}}(x,s-t)
from (
10
)
and find

\begin{split}\Big\langle j,G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle=&\int x^{\prime}(s)\delta(x-x(s))u_{f_{T}}(x,s-t)dxds\\
=&\int x^{\prime}(s)u_{f_{T}}(x(s),s-t)ds\\
=&(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}i\int x^{\prime}(s)\Big(e^{-i\omega(s-t)}-e^{i\omega(s-t)}\Big)e^{ipx(s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\ ds\\
=&(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int x^{\prime}(s)\Big[ie^{-i\omega(s-t)+ipx(s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(p)-ie^{i\omega(s-t)-ipx(s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\Big]\frac{dp}{2\omega}\ ds\\
=&(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}2\ \textrm{Re}\ \int x^{\prime}(s)\Big[-ie^{i\omega(s-t)-ipx(s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\Big]\ \frac{dp}{2\omega}ds\\
\end{split}

(62)

Now change the order of integration (easily justified), and define (new definition)

J(p)=-i(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\int x^{\prime}(s)e^{i\omega s-ipx(s)}ds

(63)

Then

\Big\langle j,G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle=2\ \textrm{Re}\ \int e^{-i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)J(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}=2\ \textrm{Re}\ (e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T},J_{T})

(64)

Then integral converges since
J_{T}(p)
is bounded and
\tilde{f}_{T}(p)
is bounded and rapidly decreasing. We have written
it as an inner product in
{\cal H}
but are not asserting that
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
, at least not yet.
Now we have as before

A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)-2\ \textrm{Re}\ (J_{T},e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})

(65)

We consider the linear function
h\to(J_{T},h)
for

h
in a dense domain
{\cal S}\subset{\cal H}_{T}
containing the functions
e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T}
for
f\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
It is the domain of of rapidly decreasing functions:

{\cal S}=\{h\in{\cal H}_{T}:\textrm{ for }k\geq 0\textrm{ there is }C\textrm{ so }|h((p)|\leq C(1+|p|^{2})^{-k}\}

(66)

For
h\in{\cal S}
we define

\begin{split}a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(h)=&a^{*}_{\textrm{in}}(h)-(J_{T},h)\\
a_{\textrm{out}}(h)=&a_{\textrm{in}}(h)-(h,J_{T})\\
\end{split}

(67)

and then as in (
43
) - (
45
)

A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=a^{*}_{\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})+a_{\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})

(68)

just as for
A_{\textrm{in}}(f,t)
.

Now we can write
A_{\textrm{in}/\textrm{out}}(f,t)=\sqrt{2}A_{\circ,\textrm{in}/\textrm{out}}(e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T})
where the momentum space fields are

A_{\circ,\textrm{in}/\textrm{out}}(h)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_{\textrm{in}/\textrm{out}}^{*}(h)+a_{\textrm{in}/\textrm{out}}(h))
. In terms of these fields
the defining relation (
67
) can be written

A_{\circ,\textrm{out}}(h)=A_{\circ,\textrm{in}}(h)-\sqrt{2}\ \textrm{Re}\ (J_{T},h)

(69)

With this identity we pass to the
C^{*}
algebra. Define

W_{\textrm{out}}(h)=e^{iA_{\circ,\textrm{out}}(h)}\hskip 28.45274ptW_{\textrm{in}}(h)=e^{iA_{\circ,\textrm{in}}(h)}

(70)

and then

W_{\textrm{out}}(h)=W_{\textrm{in}}(h)e^{-i\sqrt{2}\ \textrm{Re}\ (J_{T},h)}

(71)

These are elements of
C^{*}
algebra
\EuFrak{A}
generated by
W_{\textrm{in}}(h)
,
h\in{\cal S}
.
In the Fock vacuum
\omega_{in}=(\Omega_{0,in},[\ \cdot.\ ]\Omega_{0,in})
we have

\omega_{in}(W_{\textrm{out}}(h))=\omega_{\textrm{in}}(W_{\textrm{in}}(h))e^{-i\sqrt{2}\ \textrm{Re}(\bar{J}_{T},h)}

(72)

But
W_{\textrm{out}}(h)
is again a representation of the CCR and generates the same
C^{*}
algebra
\EuFrak{A}
as
W_{\textrm{in}}(h)
.
Then there is a unique
*
-automorphism
\alpha
on
\EuFrak{A}
such that
\alpha(W_{\textrm{out}}(h))=W_{\textrm{in}}(h)
(see for example
[
1
]
).
This satisfies
\alpha(W_{\textrm{in}}(h))=W_{\textrm{in}}(h)e^{i\sqrt{2}\ \textrm{Re}(\bar{J}_{T},h)}
.
If we define a
new state by
\omega_{\textrm{out}}\equiv\omega_{\textrm{in}}\circ\alpha
then

\omega_{\textrm{out}}(W_{\textrm{in}}(h))=\omega_{\textrm{in}}(W_{\textrm{in}}(h))e^{i\sqrt{2}\ \textrm{Re}(J_{T},h)}

(73)

Instead of changing operators we have changed states. The form of the equation shows that
\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is a coherent state by
our
{\cal C}^{*}
algebra definition.

The question is now whether
\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is a Fock state.
Before answering the question we sharpen our criterion for when this is the case.

Proposition 2
.

\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is a Fock state if and only if
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
.

Proof
. We have already noted that
\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is a Fock state if and only if the linear function

h\to(J_{T},h)
is continuous on the pre-Hilbert space
{\cal S}
. We now claim this is equivalent to
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
.
If
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
then continuity is immediate. If the continuity holds then there is
J^{\prime}\in{\cal H}_{T}
such that

(J_{T},h)=(J^{\prime},h)
for
h\in{\cal S}
. If
h
rapidly decreasing then
h_{T}\in{\cal S}
and
(J_{T}-J^{\prime},h)=(J_{T}-J^{\prime},h_{T})=0
. Then

(J_{T}-J^{\prime},h)=0
for
h\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Hence
(J_{T}-J^{\prime})/2\omega
as distributions and hence almost everywhere as functions.
Thus
J_{T}=J^{\prime}
almost everywhere and so
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
. This completes the proof.

In the following we assume that the velocity of the charged
particle is less than the speed of light which is here one.

Proposition 3
.

Let
x(t)
be

{\cal C}^{3}
with
|x^{\prime}(t)|\leq v_{0}<1
, and suppose
x^{\prime}(t)
has compact support.
Then
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
and
\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is the Fock state defined by
\Omega_{\textrm{out}}=\Omega_{J_{T}}
.

Proof
. It suffices to show
J\in{\cal H}
. Ignoring the multiplicative constants in (
63
) we might as well assume that

J(p)=\int x^{\prime}(t)e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt\hskip 28.45274pt\hskip 28.45274pt\psi(p,t)=\omega t-p\cdot x(t)

(74)

Since this is a bounded continuous function of
p
,
to decide whether it is in
{\cal H}
we need to study asymptotics as
p\to\infty
. Our first thought might be the stationary phase method. So
we look for points where

\psi_{t}(p,t)=\omega-p\cdot x^{\prime}(t)=0

(75)

However
|p\cdot x^{\prime}(t)|<|p|v_{0}=\omega v_{0}
and

\omega(1-v_{0})\leq|\psi_{t}(p,t)|\leq\omega(1+v_{0})

(76)

There is no point of stationary phase for
p\neq 0
.
Instead we can integrate by parts and write with
\psi_{tt}=-p\cdot x^{\prime\prime}(t)

\begin{split}J(p)=&\int x^{\prime}(t)\frac{1}{i\psi_{t}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt\\
=&\frac{1}{i}\int\left[\frac{\psi_{tt}}{\psi^{2}_{t}}x^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{\psi_{t}}x^{\prime\prime}(t)\right]e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt\\
\end{split}

(77)

Now
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
are bounded and
\psi_{t}^{-1}
and
\psi_{tt}/\psi^{2}_{t}
are
{\cal O}(\omega^{-1})
for
|p|\geq 1
. Hence
J(p)={\cal O}(\omega^{-1})
. This is not quite enough for convergence,
but we can repeat the last step and get

\begin{split}J(p)=&\frac{1}{i}\int\left[\frac{\psi_{tt}}{\psi^{2}_{t}}x^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{\psi_{t}}x^{\prime\prime}(t)\right]\frac{1}{i\psi_{t}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt\\
=&\int\left[\Big(\frac{\psi_{ttt}}{\psi^{3}_{t}}-\frac{3\psi_{tt}^{2}}{\psi_{t}^{4}}\Big)x^{\prime}(t)+\frac{3\psi_{tt}}{\psi^{3}_{t}}x^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{\psi_{t}^{2}}x^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\right]e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt\end{split}

(78)

Now
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime}
are all bounded and the coefficients are all
{\cal O}(\omega^{-2})
for
|p|\geq 1
. Thus
J(p)={\cal O}(\omega^{-2})
and we have

\int_{|p|\geq 1}|J(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}\leq\textrm{const}\int_{|p|\geq 1}\omega^{-5}dp<\infty

(79)

Hence
J\in{\cal H}
and the proof is complete.

Remark
. We can allow jump discontinuities in
x^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)
without changing the result.
Suppose we allow a jump discontinuity in
x^{\prime\prime}(t)
, say at
t=0
.
Then in the integration by parts in (
78
) we get a boundary term which is

\left[\frac{p\cdot(x^{\prime\prime}(0^{-})-x^{\prime\prime}(0^{+}))x^{\prime}(0)}{\psi^{3}_{t}(p,0)}+\frac{x^{\prime\prime}(0^{-})-x^{\prime\prime}(0^{+})}{\psi^{2}_{t}(p,0)}\right]e^{i\psi(p,0)}

(80)

This is still
{\cal O}(\omega^{-2})
and our results still hold. However a discontinuity in the velocity
x^{\prime}(t)

is more serious as we now show.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose
x(t)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and
{\cal C}^{3}
on
(-\infty,0]
and
[0,\infty)
with

x^{\prime}(0^{-})-x^{\prime}(0^{+})\neq 0

(81)

Suppose also
x^{\prime}(t)
has compact support and
|x^{\prime}(t)|\leq v_{0}<1
.
Then
J_{T}\notin{\cal H}_{T}
and
\omega_{out}
is not
a Fock state

Proof
. Since
J(p)
is still bounded, it again suffices to consider asymptotics as
p\to\infty
. In the integration by parts in (
77
) there is now a boundary term and we have

\begin{split}J(p)=&\int x^{\prime}(t)\frac{1}{i\psi_{t}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt\\
=&\frac{1}{i}\int\left[\frac{\psi_{tt}}{\psi^{2}_{t}}x^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{\psi_{t}}x^{\prime\prime}(t)\right]e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt+\frac{1}{i}\Delta(p)e^{i\psi(p,0)}\\
\end{split}

(82)

where

\Delta(p)=\frac{x^{\prime}(0^{-})}{\omega-p\cdot x^{\prime}(0^{-})}-\frac{x^{\prime}(0^{+})}{\omega-p\cdot x^{\prime}(0^{+})}

(83)

The first term in (
82
) is analyzed as in the previous proposition, unaffected by possible discontinuities in
x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime}
, and the transverse part is
in
{\cal H}_{T}
. Thus to prove our result it suffices to show that
\Delta_{T}
is not in
{\cal H}_{T}
.

Let
v^{-}=x^{\prime}(0^{-})
and
v^{+}=x^{\prime}(0^{+})
. Then this can be written

\Delta(p)=\frac{\omega(v^{-}-v^{+})+(v^{+}p\cdot v^{-}-v^{-}p\cdot v^{+})}{(\omega-p\cdot v^{-})(\omega-p\cdot v^{+})}

(84)

Now
v^{+}p\cdot v^{-}-v^{-}p\cdot v^{+}=p\times(v^{+}\times v^{-})
. If we define
\delta v=v^{-}-v^{+}\neq 0
and

n_{p}=p/\omega=p/|p|
then
\Delta(p)=\Delta_{1}(p)+\Delta_{2}(p)
where

\begin{split}\Delta_{1}(p)=&\frac{1}{\omega}\left[\frac{\delta v}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{-})(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{+})}\right]\\
\Delta_{2}(p)=&\frac{1}{\omega}\left[\frac{n_{p}\times(v^{+}\times v^{-})}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{-})(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{+})}\right]\\
\end{split}

(85)

Note that the denominators here are bounded below since
|v^{\pm}|<1
.
Now
\Delta_{2}(p)
is already transverse, and the transverse part of
\Delta_{1}(p)
is with
n_{\delta v}=\delta v/|\delta v|

\Delta_{1,T}(p)=\frac{1}{\omega}\left[\frac{\delta v-n_{p}(n_{p}\cdot\delta v)}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{-})(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{+})}\right]=\frac{|\delta v|}{\omega}\left[\frac{n_{\delta v}-n_{p}(n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v})}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{-})(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{+})}\right]

(86)

We have now
\Delta_{T}(p)=\Delta_{1,T}(p)+\Delta_{2}(p)
.

Now we divide into two cases. Either
v^{\pm}
are colinear or not. If they are colinear then

\Delta_{2}(p)=0
, and we compute

\begin{split}\int_{|p|\geq 1}|\Delta_{T}(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}&\geq\int_{|p|\geq 1,|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\leq\frac{1}{2}}|\Delta_{1,T}(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}\\
&\geq\textrm{const}|\delta v|^{2}\int_{|p|\geq 1,|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\omega^{3}}dp=\infty\\
\end{split}

(87)

If
v^{\pm}
are not colinear then
v_{\times}\equiv v^{+}\times v^{-}\neq 0
. We look at the component of
\Delta_{T}(p)
along
v_{\times}
.
Again
\Delta_{2}(p)
does not contribute, nor does the
n_{\delta v}
term in
\Delta_{1,T}(p)
. We have

n_{v_{\times}}\cdot\Delta_{T}(p)=\frac{|\delta v|}{\omega}\left[\frac{-(n_{p}\cdot n_{v_{\times}})(n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v})}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{-})(1-n_{p}\cdot v^{+})}\right]

(88)

Then

\begin{split}\int_{|p|\geq 1}|\Delta_{T}(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}&\geq\int_{|p|\geq 1,|n_{p}\cdot n_{v_{\times}}|\geq\frac{1}{2},|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\geq\frac{1}{2}}|n_{v_{\times}}\cdot\Delta_{T}(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}\\
&\geq\textrm{const}|\delta v|^{2}\int_{|p|\geq 1,|n_{p}\cdot n_{v_{\times}}|\geq\frac{1}{2},|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\geq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\omega^{3}}\ dp=\infty\\
\end{split}

(89)

The last step follows since
\delta v
orthogonal to
v_{\times}
implies the cones
|n_{v_{\times}}\cdot n_{p}|\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\geq\frac{1}{2}
have a non-empty intersection. This completes the proof.

5
Point source - II

Now we consider a trajectory

which has constant velocity
v_{\textrm{in}}
in the distant past, and constant velocity

in the distant future, and

There is now a problem with the convergence of integrals like (

j_{R}(x,t)=\begin{cases}x^{\prime}(t)\delta(x-x(t))&\hskip 28.45274pt|t|\leq R\\
0&\hskip 28.45274pt\textrm{ otherwise }\\

(90)

.
The analysis proceeds as in the the previous section and we have
A_{\textrm{out}}(f,t)=A_{in}(f,t)-\Big\langle j_{R},G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle

where

\begin{split}\Big\langle j_{R},G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle=&(2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}2\ \textrm{Re}\ \int_{-R}^{R}x^{\prime}(s)\int\Big[-ie^{i\omega(s-t)-ipx(s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\Big]\frac{dp}{2\omega}\ ds\\
\end{split}

(91)

Proposition 5
.

Let
x(t)
be

{\cal C}^{3}
with
|x^{\prime}(t)|\leq v_{0}<1
, and suppose
x^{\prime\prime}(t)
has compact support.
Then the limit
R\to\infty
exists on the right side of (

R=\infty
. We have as in (

\Big\langle j,G*(f_{T}\otimes\delta_{t})\Big\rangle=2\ \mathrm{Re}\ \int e^{-i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)J(p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}=2\ \mathrm{Re}\ (e^{i\omega t}\tilde{f}_{T},J_{T})

(92)

where up to a multiplicative constant

J(p)=\frac{1}{i}\int\left[\frac{\psi_{tt}}{\psi^{2}_{t}}x^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{\psi_{t}}x^{\prime\prime}(t)\right]e^{i\psi(p,t)}dt

(93)

as in (
77
), and the representation (
78
) for

holds as well.

Proof
. Note that since
\psi_{tt}=-p\cdot x^{\prime\prime}(t)
the integrand on the right side of (

and that integral exists.
Furthermore
J(p)
is
{\cal O}(\omega^{-1})
everywhere and
\tilde{f}_{T}(p)
is bounded and rapidly decreasing so the integral (
92
) converges. Once
(
93
) is established (

) holds
by a further integration by parts.

To derive (
92
) it suffices to consider the case
t=0
. Then in (
91
) with
\psi(p,s)=\omega s-p\cdot x(s)
we need to find an
R\to\infty
limit for

\begin{split}&\int_{-R}^{R}x^{\prime}(s)\left[\int e^{i\psi(p,s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\right]ds\\
=&\int_{-R}^{R}x^{\prime}(s)\frac{d}{ds}\left[\int\frac{1}{i\psi_{s}}e^{i\psi(p,s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\right]ds+\int_{-R}^{R}x^{\prime}(s)\left[\int\frac{\psi_{ss}}{i\psi^{2}_{s}}e^{i\psi(p,s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\right]ds\\
=&\frac{1}{i}\int_{-R}^{R}\left[\int\left(\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi^{2}_{s}}x^{\prime}(s)-\frac{1}{\psi_{s}}x^{\prime\prime}(s)\right)e^{i\psi(p,s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\right]ds\\
&+\left[x^{\prime}(s)\int\frac{1}{i\psi_{s}}e^{i\psi(p,s)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)\frac{dp}{2\omega}\right]_{s=-R}^{s=R}\\
\end{split}

(94)

In the double integral the
R\to\infty
limit exists by the support of
x^{\prime\prime}(t)
. The integrand is
{\cal O}(\omega^{-2})
as
|p|\to 0
and rapidly decreasing as

the integral is is absolutely convergent. Changing the order of integration gives the result (

For the endpoint term we need to show

\int\psi_{t}^{-1}e^{i\psi(t,p)}\tilde{f}_{T}(-p)dp/2\omega

so it suffices to

goes to zero as

. Let

and then

with

as the

\omega+p\cdot v_{\textrm{out}}=\omega(1+\cos\theta\ v_{\textrm{out}})

and
(1+\cos\theta\ v_{\textrm{out}})
is bounded above and below. The integral is now

\int\frac{1}{2(1+\cos\theta\ v_{\textrm{out}})}\Big[\int e^{i|p|(1+\cos\theta\ v_{\textrm{out}})t}\tilde{f}_{T}(|p|,\theta,\phi)\ d|p|\Big]\sin\theta d\theta d\phi

Now the interior integral goes to zero by the Riemann- Lebesgue lemma, and the overall integral goes to zero by dominated convergence. The limit

Proposition 6
.

Let
x(t)
be
{\cal C}^{3}
with
|x^{\prime}(t)|\leq v_{0}<1
, and suppose
x^{\prime}(t)=v_{\textrm{in}}
in the distant past and
x^{\prime}(t)=v_{\textrm{out}}
in the distant future so
x^{\prime\prime}(t)
has compact support.
Then
\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is a Fock state if and only if
v_{in}=v_{\textrm{out}}
.

Proof
. We must show that
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
if and only if
v_{in}=v_{\textrm{out}}
.
We can use the representation (
78
) as before
to get convergence for
|p|\geq 1
. So the issue is whether the
the integral for
|p|\leq 1
converges. (In proposition
3
convergence for
|p|\leq 1
was easy.) Our general bound is that

J(p)
is
{\cal O}(\omega^{-1})
, as in
J_{T}(p)
.
We show that in case
v_{in}\neq v_{\textrm{out}}
this is actually sharp and gives a logarithmic divergence.

In the representation (
93
) for
|p|\leq 1
we write

e^{i\psi(p,t)}=1+{\cal O}(\omega)
and correspondingly
J(p)=F(p)+{\cal O}(1)
. The
{\cal O}(1)
term is in
{\cal H}
and the transverse part in in
{\cal H}_{T}
,
so it suffices to consider
F(p)
and show that
F_{T}(p)
either is or is not square integrable for
|p|\leq 1
with respect to
(2\omega)^{-1}dp
.

We have up to a multiplicative constant

\begin{split}F(p)=&\int\left[\frac{x^{\prime}(p\cdot x^{\prime\prime})}{\omega-p\cdot x^{\prime}(t))^{2}}+\frac{x^{\prime\prime}}{(\omega-p\cdot x^{\prime}(t))}\right]dt\\
=&\int\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{x^{\prime}(t)}{(\omega-p\cdot x^{\prime}(t))}\right]dt\\
=&\frac{v_{\textrm{out}}}{(\omega-p\cdot v_{\textrm{out}})}-\frac{v_{\textrm{in}}}{(\omega-p\cdot v_{\textrm{in}})}\\
=&\frac{\omega(v_{\textrm{out}}-v_{\textrm{in}})+(v_{\textrm{in}}\ p\cdot v_{\textrm{out}}-v_{\textrm{out}}\ p\cdot v_{\textrm{in}})}{(\omega-p\cdot v_{\textrm{out}})(\omega-p\cdot v_{\textrm{in}})}\\
\end{split}

(98)

where
\delta v=v_{\textrm{out}}-v_{\textrm{in}}
.
We see that if
v_{\textrm{in}}=v_{\textrm{out}}
then
F=0
and
F_{T}=0
and hence
J\in{\cal H}
and
J_{T}\in{\cal H}_{T}
and
\omega_{\textrm{out}}
is Fock.

We proceed with the case
v_{\textrm{out}}\neq v_{\textrm{in}}
. We are now almost exactly in the situation that occurred in the proof of Proposition
4
. The
main difference is that we consider
|p|\leq 1
rather than
|p|\geq 1
. We write
F_{T}(p)=F_{1,T}(p)+F_{2}(p)

where with
\delta v\equiv v_{\textrm{out}}-v_{\textrm{in}}
and
v_{\times}=v_{\textrm{in}}\times v_{\textrm{out}}

\begin{split}F_{1,T}(p)=&\frac{|\delta v|}{\omega}\left[\frac{n_{\delta v}-n_{p}(n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v})}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v_{\textrm{out}})(1-n_{p}\cdot v_{\textrm{in}})}\right]\\
F_{2}(p)=&\frac{1}{\omega}\left[\frac{n_{p}\times n_{v_{\times}}}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v_{\textrm{out}})(1-n_{p}\cdot v_{\textrm{in}}))}\right]\\
\end{split}

(99)

If
v_{\textrm{in}}
and
v_{\textrm{out}}
are colinear (which includes the case where one of them is zero), then only
F_{1,T}
contributes and we have
as in (
87
)

\int_{|p|\leq 1}|F_{T}(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}\geq\textrm{const}|\delta v|^{2}\int_{|p|\leq 1,|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\omega^{3}}dp=\infty

(100)

If
v_{\textrm{in}}
and
v_{\textrm{out}}
are not colinear we look at the component along

and find as in (

n_{v_{\times}}\cdot F_{T}(p)=\frac{|\delta v|}{\omega}\left[\frac{-(n_{p}\cdot n_{v_{\times}})(n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v})}{(1-n_{p}\cdot v_{\textrm{out}})(1-n_{p}\cdot v_{\textrm{in}})}\right]

(101)

Then as in (

)

\begin{split}\int_{|p|\leq 1}|F_{T}(p)|^{2}\frac{dp}{2\omega}&\geq\textrm{const}|\delta v|^{2}\int_{|p|\leq 1,|n_{p}\cdot n_{v_{\times}}|\geq\frac{1}{2},|n_{p}\cdot n_{\delta v}|\geq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\omega^{3}}\ dp=\infty\\
\end{split}

Thus the
F_{T}

\omega_{\textrm{out}}

Remarks
.

1.

Note that if the particle has constant velocity then
x^{\prime\prime}(t)=0
, hence

, and
\omega_{\textrm{out}}=\omega_{\textrm{in}}
. No photons
are radiated.

2.

The result does not depend on the complete history of the trajectory, only on the initial and final states.
Except in a the special circumstance
v_{\textrm{in}}=v_{\textrm{out}}
we always leave Fock space.

3.

It is noteworthy that the departure from Fock space comes from a UV divergence in the case of of discontinuities in the velocity
(proposition
4
), and from an IR divergence in the case

(proposition
6
).

4.

It might be of interest to obtain results of this kind for the radiation of gravitons by a classical source in linearized quantum gravity.
See Skagerstam, Eriksson, Rekdal
[
8
]
, for a formulation of this model in an analog of the Coulomb gauge. In this connection we also mention the work of Kegeles, Oriti, Tomlin
[
5
]
who suggest non-Fock coherent states
of the type used here as appropriate for a group field theory model of quantum gravity.
