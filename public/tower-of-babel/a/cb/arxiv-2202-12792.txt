Separable symmetric tensors and separable anti-symmetric tensors
Changqing Xu

Abstract
In this paper, we first introduce the invertibility of even-order tensors and the separable tensors, including separable symmetry tensors and separable anti-symmetry tensors, defined respectively as the sum and the algebraic sum of rank-1 tensors generated by the tensor product of some vectors, say, v 1 , v 2 , … , v m \textbf{v}_{1},\textbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\textbf{v}_{m} . We show that the m ! m! sumrands, each in form v σ ⁡ ( 1 ) × v σ ⁡ ( 2 ) × … × v σ ⁡ ( m ) \textbf{v}_{\sigma(1)}\times\textbf{v}_{\sigma(2)}\times\ldots\times\textbf{v}_{\sigma(m)} , are linearly independent if v 1 , v 2 , … , v m \textbf{v}_{1},\textbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\textbf{v}_{m} are linearly independent, where σ \sigma is any permutation on { 1 , 2 , … , m } \left\{1,2,\ldots,m\right\} . We offer a class of tensors to achieve the upper bound for rank ​ ( 𝒜 ) ≤ 6 \texttt{rank}(\mathcal{A})\leq 6 for all 𝒜 ∈ ℝ 3 × 3 × 3 \mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3\times 3} ( see e.g. [ 13 ] ). We also show that each 3 × 3 × 3 3\times 3\times 3 anti-symmetric tensor is separable.



keywords:
S-product; invertible tensor; separable symmetric tensor; separable anti-symmetric tensor.

A tensor is a multi-array or hyper-matrix with multiple index, which can be applied in many fields related to multi-dimensional dimensional data (MDD) such as theory of relativity

A tensor can be regarded as a natural extension of a matrix which is fundamental in many fields. Some basic terminology e.g. rank, determinant, the eigenvalues and

For example, there are several different kind of definitions for the rank of a tensor, say, the marginal rank, meaning the maximal rank of the matricizations in different modes. There are also several kind of eigenvalues (and related eigenvectors) such as the H-eigenvalues, the Z-eigenvalues and the E-eigenvalues (see e.g.

The rank of a tensor was proposed in 1927 by Hitchcock

Another interesting tensor product is the t-product defined between two third order tensors. Let

Let
S_{1}=\left\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{p}\right\},S_{2}=\left\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{q}\right\}

(\mathcal{A}\boxtimes_{S}\mathcal{B})_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{r}}A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{p}}B_{j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{q}}

where

The S-product can be contractive or extensive. In fact, there are two cases for the S-product:

(a)

is a tensor of order

(b)

and

is a tensor of order

If

, then

,
\mathcal{A}\boxtimes_{S}\mathcal{B}
is a contractive product along mode-

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{A}\boxtimes_{S}\mathcal{B}

A special case is the inner product

or equivalently

. On the other hand, the S-product of tensors in case (b) is exactly the outer (extensive) product of tensors.

The S-product can be recursively employed to yield a higher order or a lower order tensor, depending on what we need. An extreme case is a rank-1 symmetric tensor

\textbf{x}^{m}
generated from the power of a vector

Invertibility of a hypercubic tensor with an even-order

We first present some basic properties on the identity tensors. Recall that the identity matrix
I_{n}
in matrix space
\mathcal{M}_{n}
obeys the following rule:

I_{n}A=AI_{n}=A,\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}.

(2.1)

This is also valid for the identity tensor in tensor space
\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any even number
m=2k
, the identity tensor
\mathcal{I}
defined by (
1.4
) in tensor space
\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
obeys the following rule:

\mathcal{I}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{A},\quad\forall\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}.

(2.2)

Proof.

For any
\sigma\equiv(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m})\in S(m,n)
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{I})_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{k}}A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{k}j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{k}}\delta_{j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{k}i_{k+1}i_{k+2}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{k}}A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{k}j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{k}}\delta_{j_{1}i_{k+1}}\delta_{j_{2}i_{k+2}}\ldots\delta_{j_{k}i_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{k}i_{k+1}i_{k+2}\ldots i_{m}}

Thus
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{A}
. Similarly we can prove
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}
.
∎

The identity tensor
\mathcal{I}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
can be regarded as the S-power of the identity matrix
I_{n}
in the sense of the outer-product:

\mathcal{I}=I_{n}\boxtimes_{S_{2}}I_{n}\boxtimes_{S_{3}}\ldots\boxtimes_{S_{k}}I_{n}

where
S_{i}\equiv\left\{i,k+i\right\}
(
i=1,2,\ldots,k
). Therefore we can also write
\mathcal{I}=I_{n}^{[k]}
.

An even-order tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
is said to be

if there exists a tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
such that

\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{I}.

(2.3)

\mathcal{B}
is called the inverse of
\mathcal{A}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
.

The invertibility of an even-order tensor can be transferred to that of a square matrix by tensor
matricization
, which is usually called

.
An unfolding of a tensor
\mathcal{A}
is defined as a process through which the elements of
\mathcal{A}
is rearranged into a matrix. For more detail concerning unfolding of a general

[
12

Given an even-order tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
with
m=2k

into an

is the process which yields a matrix

whose entries are defined as
a_{ij}=A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{k}i_{k+1}i_{k+2}\ldots i_{2k}}

i=1+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{k}(i_{r}-1)n^{k-r},\quad j=1+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{k}(i_{k+r}-1)n^{k-r}.

(2.4)

We call matrix

obtained by the normal unfolding a

or
NS

\mathcal{A}
. We have

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
where
m=2k
is an even number, and
A,B
are respectively the NS matrices of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is invertible if and only if

A
is invertible. Furthermore,
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}
if and only if
B=A^{-1}
.

Proof.

We take
m=4
for our convenience in notations. The argument in general case (i.e.
m=2k
) follows the same route. Write
A=(a_{ij}),B=(b_{ij})
.
Then our result follows by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B})_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j_{1},j_{2}}A_{i_{1}i_{2}j_{1}j_{2}}B_{j_{1}j_{2}i_{3}i_{4}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j_{1},j_{2}}a_{i_{2}+(i_{1}-1)n,j_{2}+(j_{1}-1)n}b_{j_{2}+(j_{1}-1)n,i_{4}+(i_{3}-1)n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{s=1}^{n^{2}}a_{is}b_{sj}=(AB)_{ij}

where
\sigma:=(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},i_{4})\in S(4;n)
and
i=i_{2}+(i_{1}-1)n,j=i_{4}+(i_{3}-1)n
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}

m=2k

\mathcal{A}
is invertible if and only if

.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}

3
Symmetric tensors and anti-symmetric tensors

Let
n>1
be a positive integer and
m=2k>0
be an even number. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{m}
the set of all permutations on set
[m]
. For any tensor

\mathcal{A}=(A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}})\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
and any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
, we define
\sigma(\mathcal{A})
as the tensor
\mathcal{A}^{(\sigma)}=(A^{(\sigma)}_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}})
where

A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}^{(\sigma)}=A_{i_{\sigma(1)}i_{\sigma(2)}\ldots i_{\sigma(m)}},\quad\forall\sigma:=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m})\in S(m;n).

(3.1)

We call
\mathcal{A}

\sigma
-symmetric
if it satisfies
\sigma(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}
.
\mathcal{A}
is called
\sigma
-sign symmetric
if
\sigma(\mathcal{A})=(-1)^{\tau(\sigma)}\mathcal{A}
where
\tau(\sigma)
is the inverse number of
\sigma
. A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
is called
anti-symmetric
if
\mathcal{A}
it is
\sigma
-sign symmetric for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
. A symmetric tensor is
\sigma
-symmetric
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
. Now we denote

\mathcal{S}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m!}}\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\sigma

(3.2)

then
\mathcal{S}\colon\mathcal{T}_{m;n}\to\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
is a linear operator sending each tensor in
\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
into
\mathcal{S}_{m;n}

[
5
]
. Note that

\sigma\circ\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}\circ\sigma=\mathcal{S},\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}.

(3.3)

A tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
is symmetric if and only if
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A})
. Given a tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
. The
symmetrization
of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as tensor
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A})
.
Note that the polynomial associated with
\mathcal{A}
is the same as that with
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A})
, which makes reasonable for us to assume the symmetry of tensors. For our convenience,
we denote the set of
m
th order
n
-dimensional symmetric tensors by
\mathcal{S}_{m;n}
.

A symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{m;n}
is called
separable
if

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}(\textbf{u}_{1}\times\textbf{u}_{2}\times\ldots\times\textbf{u}_{m})

(3.4)

for some vectors
\textbf{u}_{1},\textbf{u}_{2},\ldots,\textbf{u}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Some natural questions are: when is a symmetric tensor separable? can a symmetric tensor be decomposed into the sum of some separable tensors? what is the rank of a separable tensor?

Now we denote

Let
\textbf{v}_{1},\textbf{v}_{2},\cdots,\textbf{v}_{m}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
where each
\textbf{v}_{j}
is a nonzero vector, and write

\mathcal{L}:==\frac{1}{\sqrt{m!}}\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}(-1)^{\tau(\sigma)}\sigma.

Then
\mathcal{L}
is a linear operator on
\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
. We denote

\textbf{v}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textbf{v}_{m}=\mathcal{L}(\textbf{v}_{1}\times\textbf{v}_{2}\times\cdots\times\textbf{v}_{m})

(3.5)

and

\textbf{v}_{1}\vee\textbf{v}_{2}\vee\cdots\vee\textbf{v}_{m}=\mathcal{S}(\textbf{v}_{1}\times\textbf{v}_{2}\times\cdots\times\textbf{v}_{m})

(3.6)

For
m=2
, the operator
\wedge
produces an
n\times n
anti-symmetric matrix of rank 2 when
\textbf{v}_{1},\textbf{v}_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
are linearly independent (
n\geq 2
).
In the following we will show that tensor
\textbf{v}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\textbf{v}_{m}
must be an anti-symmetric tensor in general case.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\textbf{u}_{j},\textbf{v}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},j\in[m]
and
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
with
a_{ij}=\left<\textbf{u}_{i},\textbf{v}_{j}\right>
for all
i,j\in[n]
. Denote

\mathcal{A}_{l}=\mathcal{L}(\textbf{u}_{1}\times\cdots\times\textbf{u}_{m}),\mathcal{B}_{l}=\mathcal{L}(\textbf{v}_{1}\times\cdots\times\textbf{v}_{m})
and
\mathcal{A}_{s}=\mathcal{S}(\textbf{u}_{1}\times\cdots\times\textbf{u}_{m}),\mathcal{B}_{s}=\mathcal{S}(\textbf{v}_{1}\times\cdots\times\textbf{v}_{m})
. Then we have

\left<\mathcal{A}_{l},\mathcal{B}_{l}\right>=\det(A)

(3.7)

and

\left<\mathcal{A}_{s},\mathcal{B}_{s}\right>=\texttt{perm}(A)

(3.8)

where
\texttt{perm}(A)
denotes the permanent of matrix
A
.

Proof.

For convenience, we denote
\textbf{U}:=\textbf{u}_{1}\times\cdots\times\textbf{u}_{m},\textbf{V}:=\textbf{v}_{1}\times\cdots\times\textbf{v}_{m}
. We come to prove (

4
The invertibility of separable tensors

We denote by

for the spectrum of a tensor
\mathcal{A}
. It is known that a real symmetric matrix

is diagonalizable with all eigenvalues
being real numbers, and a real anti-symmetric matrix has zero as its unique real eigenvalue. We conjecture that this phenomenon is also true in the tensor case. In the following we first consider the spectrum of separable symmetric tensors and the spectrum of a separable anti-symmetric tensor. We show that

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
.
\mathcal{A}
is called a
separable symmetric
tensor if there exist some vectors
\textbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\textbf{v}_{m}
such that

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}(\textbf{V})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m!}}\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\textbf{v}_{\sigma(1)}\times\textbf{v}_{\sigma(2)}\times\cdots\times\textbf{v}_{\sigma(m)}.

(4.1)

where
\textbf{V}=\textbf{v}_{1}\times\textbf{v}_{2}\times\ldots\times\textbf{v}_{m}
. A separable tensor is either a separable symmetric tensor or a separable anti-symmetric tensor. In the following
we write
\textbf{v}_{1}\diamondsuit\textbf{v}_{2}\diamondsuit\ldots\diamondsuit\textbf{v}_{m}
for
\mathcal{L}(\textbf{V})
or
\mathcal{S}(\textbf{V})
. Now suppose that
1\leq m<n
and
\mathcal{A}
is a separable tensor, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}=\textbf{v}_{1}\diamondsuit\textbf{v}_{2}\diamondsuit\cdots\diamondsuit\textbf{v}_{m}.

(4.2)

A natural question is: Is
\mathcal{A}
invertible or not? The next result tells us that
\mathcal{A}
is singular (not invertible) if

.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
1\leq m<n
,
m=2k
is an even number, and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}

If

, there is a nonzero vector
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to
\lambda=0
, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}^{m-1}=0.

(4.3)

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is invertible, then there exists a tensor

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{I}

\textbf{x}^{[m-1]}=(\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A})\textbf{x}^{m-1}=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{A}\textbf{x}^{m-1})=0.

It follows that

\displaystyle A_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{m}}K_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m},j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{m}}u_{j_{1}1}u_{j_{2}2}\cdots u_{j_{m}m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{i_{1}\sigma(1)}u_{i_{2}\sigma(2)}\cdots u_{i_{m}\sigma(m)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\textbf{u}_{\sigma(1)}\times\textbf{u}_{\sigma(2)}\times\ldots\times\textbf{u}_{\sigma(m)})_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m}}

Thus
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
. The proof is completed.
∎

Now we are ready to prove

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
1\leq m\leq n
). Then the
m!
vectors in set

\left\{\alpha_{\sigma(1)}\times\alpha_{\sigma(2)}\times\ldots\times\alpha_{\sigma(m)}:\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\right\}

(5.5)

are linearly independent if and only if vectors
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

For sufficiency, we assume that the vectors in set (
5.5
) are linear independent, and we want to prove that vectors
\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
are
linear independent. Suppose, to the contrary, that vectors
\left\{\alpha_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{m}
are linear dependent. Then by Corollary
3.2
we have

\alpha_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\alpha_{m}=\mathcal{O}
, which implies that the vectors in set (
5.5
) are linear dependent, a contradiction to our hypothesis.

Now we show the necessity. We assume that vectors
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
are linearly independent. To show the linear dependency of vectors in set
(
5.5
), we let

\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\lambda_{\sigma}\alpha_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\alpha_{\sigma(2)}\otimes\ldots\otimes\alpha_{\sigma(m)}=\mathcal{O}

(5.6)

where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the zero tensor in
\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
and
\lambda_{\sigma}\in\textbf{R}
is a scalar. It suffices to show that
\lambda_{\sigma}=0
for each
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
.
By Proposition
5.2
, (
5.6
) is equivalent to

\mathcal{O}=(\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\lambda_{\sigma}\mathcal{K}^{(\sigma)})\alpha_{1}\otimes\alpha_{2}\otimes\ldots\otimes\alpha_{m}

(5.7)

which implies that

\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\lambda_{\sigma}\mathcal{K}^{(\sigma)}=\mathcal{O}

(5.8)

It is easy to see that the tensors in the set
S:=\left\{\mathcal{K}^{(\sigma)}\colon\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}\right\}
are linearly independent. In fact, this assertion can be easily confirmed if we consider
the set of matrices
A^{\sigma}
where each
A^{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{m}\times n^{m}}
is obtained by matricization of tensor
\mathcal{K}^{\sigma}
in set
S
, i.e.,

A^{\sigma}=(A_{ij})
with
A_{ij}=K_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{m},j_{1}j_{2}\ldots j_{m}}
where

i=i_{m}+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{m-1}(i_{r}-1)n^{k-r},\quad j=j_{m}+\sum\limits_{r=1}^{m-1}(j_{r}-1)n^{k-r}.

(5.9)

Thus
\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\lambda_{\sigma}\mathcal{K}^{(\sigma)}=\mathcal{O}
implies that
\lambda_{\sigma}=0
for each
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}
. Thus the proof is completed.
∎

We note that Theorem
5.3
is also true if the tensor-products in set (
5.5
) are replaced by the Kronecker products. From Theorem
5.3
,
we immediately get

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathcal{A}=\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}\wedge\ldots\wedge\alpha_{m}
. Then
\texttt{rank}(\mathcal{A})\leq m!
. Furthermore,

\texttt{rank}(\mathcal{A})=m!
if the vectors
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
are linearly independent.

Corollary
5.4
shows that the rank of a tensor can be very large even though its dimension
n
is small, a fact which is not conformal to the case when
m=2
.
We know that the rank of an
n\times n
matrix
A
satisfies
\texttt{rank}(A)\leq n
. But an 4-order 3-dimensional tensor
\mathcal{A}
can have rank
4!=24
, which is much bigger than
3, if we choose
\mathcal{A}
to be a nonzero separable tensor. Our result can be used to enhance the consequences appeared in
[
12
]
.

Now we let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{m;n}
. We want to know when a symmetric tensor
\mathcal{A}
can be separable. In the case
m=2
, we see that
A=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\beta^{\top}+\beta\alpha^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a rank-2 symmetric matrix when
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are linearly independent. Furthermore, we can show that

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq 2
) be linearly independent, and
A=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\beta^{\top}+\beta\alpha^{\top})
. Then
A
is neither positive semidefinite nor negative
semidefinite.

Proof.

We may assume w.l.g. that
\parallel\!\alpha\!\parallel=\parallel\!\beta\!\parallel=1
where the norm
\parallel\!\cdot\!\parallel
denote the Euclidean norm. Denote
a=\left<\alpha,\beta\right>
. Then we have

\left|a\right|<1
since
\left<\alpha,\beta\right>^{2}\leq\left<\alpha,\alpha\right>\left<\beta,\beta\right>=1
and the equality holds only if
\alpha=\pm\beta
, which contradicts with the linear
independency of
\alpha,\beta
. We suppose that
0<a<1
, and denote
\eta=\frac{1-a}{a}
. Take
\lambda=-a(1+2\eta)
and
\textbf{u}=\alpha-a(1+2\eta)\beta
. Then we can
easily check that
\textbf{u}^{\top}A\textbf{u}<0
. When
-1<a<0
, we can also find a vector
v
such that
\textbf{v}^{\top}A\textbf{v}<0
. Thus
A
is not positive semidefinite.
Similar argument also applies to show that
A
is not negative semidefinite.
∎

We end the paper by conjecturing that the conclusion in Lemma
5.5
is also valid for a separable symmetric tensors of order
m\geq 2
.

Conjecture 5.6
.

Let
\textbf{v}_{1},\textbf{v}_{2},\ldots,\textbf{v}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n\geq m
) be linearly independent vectors, and
\mathcal{A}=\textbf{v}_{1}\vee\textbf{v}_{2}\vee\ldots\vee\textbf{v}_{m}
. Then
\mathcal{A}

is neither a positive semidefinite tensor nor a negative semidefinite tensor.
