Optimal Transport-based Coverage Control for Swarm Robot Systems: Generalization of the Voronoi Tessellation-based Method
Daisuke Inoue, Yuji Ito, Hiroaki Yoshida

Abstract
Swarm robot systems, which consist of many cooperating mobile robots, have attracted attention for their environmental adaptability and fault tolerance advantages. One of the most important tasks for such systems is coverage control, in which robots autonomously deploy to approximate a given spatial distribution. In this study, we formulate a coverage control paradigm using the concept of optimal transport and propose a novel control technique, which we have termed the optimal transport-based coverage control (OTCC) method. The proposed OTCC, derived via the gradient flow of the cost function in the Kantorovich dual problem, is shown to covers a widely used existing control method as a special case. We also perform a Lyapunov stability analysis of the controlled system, and provide numerical calculations to show that the OTCC reproduces target distributions with better performance than the existing control method.



I
Introduction

Swarm robot systems, in which many mobile robots work cooperatively to perform given tasks, are expected to have strong environmental adaptability and high fault tolerance in comparison with single-robot systems
Barca2013Swarm
;
Brambilla2013Swarm
;
Rubenstein2009Scalablea
.
One of the most fundamental and important challenges of such systems is
coverage control
, in which robots move and reposition themselves autonomously so that their placement approaches a predetermined spatial distribution.
The application ranges from the optimal placement of sensor networks to efficient rescue of human life in the event of a disaster
Choset2001Coverage
.
From the early 2000s to the present, various methods have been proposed, such as potential-function-based control
Howard2002Mobile
, probability-based control
Izumi2014Coveragea
;
Inoue2019Stochastic
, and broadcast-based control
Azuma2013Broadcast
;
Ito2020Pseudoperturbationbased
, in order to solve the coverage tasks.
A detailed review of these methods is provided in
Huang2017Review
.

Among them, the
Voronoi tessellation-based coverage control (VTCC)
method proposed by Cortes et al.
Cortes2004Coverage
is a seminal work and widely used.
In this method, the coverage area is divided into subspaces referred to as Voronoi regions, each of which is assigned to a robot, and the robot is moved toward the center of gravity of its assigned Voronoi region.
The cost function defined for the entire robot swarm is shown to decrease over time, meaning that eventually the robots are appropriately scattered throughout the coverage area.
The VTCC has been commonly used in practice for its mathematical guarantee of stability, as well as the simplicity and the scalability of the algorithm
Shibata2019Development
.

By regarding a robot swarm as an abstract group of points in Euclidean space, the coverage control is interpreted as the problem of transporting a given discrete distribution to approximate a target continuous distribution. This is commonly referred to as the
optimal transport
problem, and its mathematical properties and numerical solutions have been widely investigated
Villani2003Topics
;
Santambrogio2010Introduction
;
peyre2019computational
. Recent areas of interest concerning the optimal transport problem extend to applications such as machine learning
Santambrogio2015Optimal
;
Courty2017Optimal
;
Arjovsky2017Wasserstein
, image processing
Bonneel2016Wasserstein
;
deGoes2012Blue
, and natural language processing
Grave2019Unsupervised
.

In this study, we formulate coverage control as an optimal transport problem in order to propose a novel control technique, which we call the
optimal transport-based coverage control (OTCC)
method.
Multi-agent control methods using optimal transport have been proposed in
Bandyopadhyay2014Probabilistic
;
Krishnan2019Distributed
.
However, the relation between the control laws proposed in these references and the VTCC has not been investigated.
Our control method differs from existing methods in that it considers gradient flows for the cost function of the optimal transport problem,
which allows us to compare the structure and performance of the proposed OTCC with that of the VTCC.
The contributions of the present coverage control formulation is summarized as follows:

•

The cost function for the VTCC is shown to be a special case of the cost function for the Kantorovich dual problem.

•

The new control law is derived as the gradient flows of the cost function for the Kantorovich dual problem.

•

A sufficient condition for the Lyapunov stability is provided for the controlled system, followed by a more specific condition in one-dimensional case.

•

Numerical analysis is conducted to show that the proposed method reaches a closer state to the global optimum than can be achieved via the VTCC.

II
Review of Optimal Transport

This section provides a brief overview of optimal transport in order to assist in formulating the optimal transport-based coverage control. We begin by considering two continuous density functions
\rho_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\rho_{T}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
space, where
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the space dimension.

Definition 1

(Kantorovich problem)
.

The problem of finding a simultaneous probability density function
p(x,y)
that minimizes the following cost function
C_{\text{K}}(p)
is called the
Kantorovich problem
:

\displaystyle C_{\text{K}}(p)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}p(x,y)\differential{x}\differential{y},

(1)

where
p(x,y)
satisfies
the following conditions:

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p(x,y)\differential{y}

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}(x),

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p(x,y)\differential{x}

\displaystyle=\rho_{T}(y).

(3)

We denote the solution of the Kantorovich problem as
W(\rho_{0},\rho_{T})\coloneqq\inf_{p}C_{\text{K}}(p)
and call it the
Wasserstein metric
.
In fact,
W
is a function that measures the distance between the two distributions, and it is known that
W
actually satisfies the axiom of distance
Santambrogio2010Introduction
.

Definition 2

(Kantorovich dual problem)
.

The problem of finding integrable functions
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that maximize the following cost functions
C_{\text{KD}}(\phi,\psi)
is called the
Kantorovich dual problem
:

\displaystyle C_{\text{KD}}(\phi,\psi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi(x)\rho_{0}(x)\differential{x}+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi(y)\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y},

(4)

where
\phi
and
\psi
satisfy the following condition:

\displaystyle\phi(x)+\psi(y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

(5)

Strong duality is known to hold for the Kantorovich problem and its dual problem.

Proposition 1

(
(
Villani2003Topics
, Theorem 1.3)
)
.

For the Kantorovich problem solution
p^{*}(x,y)
and the dual problem solutions
\phi^{*}(x)
and
\psi^{*}(y)
, the following equation holds:

\displaystyle C_{\text{K}}(p^{*})=C_{\text{KD}}(\phi^{*},\psi^{*}).

(6)

Thus, the problem of finding the simultaneous distribution of
p
in (
1
) is replaced by the problem of finding the functions
\phi
and
\psi
in (
4
).
In fact, it is known that we only need to find one of the two functions.

Proposition 2

(
(
Villani2003Topics
, Remark 1.12)
)
.

In the Kantorovich dual problem, the following equality holds for the pair of functions
\phi^{*}
and
\psi^{*}
that maximize the cost function
C_{\text{KD}}(\phi,\psi)
.

\displaystyle\psi^{*}(y)=\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}-\phi^{*}(x)\right\}.

(7)

In the next section we focus on the optimal transport problem where the distribution is restricted to a particular family.

III
Proposed Control Method

This section provides the optimal transport-based coverage control with the aid of the idea in the previous section.
We begin by considering
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
mobile robots located on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
space. Let the position of the
i
-th robot at time
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be
x_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and let its dynamics be given as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}(t)=u_{i}(t),

(8)

where
u_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the input that determines the speed of the robot.
Next, we define a distribution formed by robots as

\displaystyle\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\delta(x-x_{i}(t)),

(9)

where we define
\delta(\cdot)
as a Dirac’s delta function.
We focus on the problem of minimizing the distance (measured by
W
) between the target distribution
\rho_{T}
and the distribution
\rho_{0}(x)=\rho(x,t)
at each time:

\displaystyle\min_{{\small\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}W(\rho(x,t),\rho_{T}(x)),

(10)

where we define
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\coloneqq[x_{1}^{\top},\ldots,x_{n}^{\top}]^{\top}
as a position vector of robots.
Our goal is to design inputs
u_{i}(t)\ (\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,n\})
that achieves (
10
) at each time for the system of (
8
).

The results provided in the previous section yield the following expression for the problem of (
10
).

Proposition 3
.

The following equation holds:

\displaystyle W(\rho(x,t),\rho_{T}(x))=\max_{{\small\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}}F(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}),

(11)

where we define a real vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}=[\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}]^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and we define a function
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{dn}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\displaystyle F(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\left\{\frac{1}{n}-\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}\right\}\phi_{i}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)}\frac{1}{2}\|x_{i}-y\|^{2}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}\right].

(12)

In addition, the set
{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)
is called
Laguerre regions
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)\coloneqq

(13)

\displaystyle\left\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\frac{1}{2}\|x_{i}-y\|^{2}-\phi_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|x_{j}-y\|^{2}-\phi_{j}\quad\forall j\neq i\right\}.

Proof.

We show that the function
W
is transformed into (
12
) by using the Kantorovich duality.
The first term in (
4
) is calculated as

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi(x)\rho(x,t)\differential{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\phi(x)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\delta(x-x_{i})\differential{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}^{n}\phi_{i}.

(14)

Using (
7
), the second term in (
4
) is rearranged as

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\psi(y)\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|x_{i}-y\|^{2}-\phi_{i}\right\}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|x_{i}-y\|^{2}-\phi_{i}\right\}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}.

(15)

Finally, (
6
) ensures that (
11
) holds.

\blacksquare

Thus, the optimization problem to be solved by the robot swarm is rearranged as the following min-max problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\small\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}\max_{{\small\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}}F(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}).

(16)

Accordingly, we propose the following controller as a solution to the problem of (
16
).

Definition 3
.

For the system in (
8
), the
optimal transport-based coverage control (OTCC)
is defined as

\displaystyle u_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-k\left(x_{i}(t)-\tilde{b}_{i}(t)\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=k^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{n}-\tilde{a}_{i}(t)\right),

(18)

where
k\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
k^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
are design parameters that provide the feedback gain, and
\tilde{a}_{i}(t)
and
\tilde{b}_{i}(t)
are defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y},

(19)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tilde{a}_{i}(t)}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)}y\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}.

(20)

We define and consider the following time derivative of the function
F
in (
12
) along the trajectories
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(t)
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}(t)
of the system controlled via the OTCC:

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(t))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\partialderivative{F(\bmx(t),\bmphi(t))}{x_i}\right\}^{\top}\dot{x}_{i}(t),

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{x}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}(t))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partialderivative{F(\bmx(t),\bmphi(t))}{\phi_i}\dot{\phi}_{i}(t).

(22)

The following proposition then justifies the conclusion that the OTCC provides a solution to the problem in (
16
).

Proposition 4
.

The following hold for any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
:

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(t))\leq 0,\ \dot{F}_{x}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}(t))\geq 0.

(23)

Proof.

We calculate the gradients of the function
F
as

\displaystyle\partialderivative{F}{\phi_i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}-\tilde{a}_{i}(t),

(24)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{F}{x_i}

\displaystyle=\tilde{a}_{i}(t)\left(x_{i}(t)-\tilde{b}_{i}(t)\right),

(25)

where we used Reynolds’ transport theorem
Cortes2005Spatiallydistributed
to differentiate the functions including variables in the integration domain.
The time derivative of
F
along the trajectories
x
and
\phi
are evaluated as

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{\phi}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}(t))

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{{\tilde{a}}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\partialderivative{F}{x_i}\right\|^{2}\leq 0,

(26)

\displaystyle\dot{F}_{x}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}(t))

\displaystyle=k^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\partialderivative{F}{\phi_i}\right)^{2}\geq 0,

(27)

which ends the proof.

\blacksquare

Remark 1
.

We show that the Voronoi tessellation-based coverage control (VTCC) method
Cortes2004Coverage
is regarded as a special case of the proposed OTCC.
In
Cortes2004Coverage
, the following cost function is introduced:

\displaystyle J(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{V}_{i}(x)}\frac{1}{2}\left\|y-x_{i}\right\|^{2}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y},

(28)

where the set
{\mathcal{V}}_{i}(x)
is called the
Voronoi region
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{i}(x)=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid\|x_{i}-y\|\leq\|x_{j}-y\|\quad\forall j\neq i\}.

(29)

In their study, they proposed the following control law to minimize the cost function of (
28
):

\displaystyle u_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-k\left(x_{i}(t)-b_{i}(t)\right),

(30)

\displaystyle b_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{a_{i}(x(t))}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}(t)}y\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y},

(31)

\displaystyle a_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{{\mathcal{V}}_{i}(x(t))}\rho_{T}(y)\differential{y}.

(32)

The OTCC in (
17
) and (
18
) agree with the VTCC of (
30
)
when the variable
\phi
is set to
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}(t)\equiv 0
.
Under this condition, the cost function for both methods ((
12
) and (
28
)) coincide.
Therefore, the VTCC is one of the gradient flows that realize the transport from
\rho(x,t)
to
\rho_{T}(x)
.
However, the VTCC is not optimal from the aspect of optimal transport because the cost function is not maximized for
\phi
at each time. In contrast, the proposed OTCC overcomes this problem and is expected to provide better control performance.

Remark 2
.

We discuss the difference between the Voronoi region in (
29
) and the Laguerre region in (
13
).
In both sets, the boundary is perpendicular to the line between neighboring robots
i
and
j
.
While the boundary in the Voronoi region is a bisector, it is not a bisector in the Laguerre region if
\phi
is not zero.
Specifically, when
\phi_{i}
is larger than
\phi_{j}
, the boundary moves towards the robot
j
, and consequently robot
i
acquires more area.

Remark 3
.

Under suitable conditions, the proposed algorithm is scalable in the sense that each robot only needs the information of neighboring robots.
Indeed, the controllers in (
17
) and (
18
) are computed using
x_{j}
and
\phi_{j}
of the robots
j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{i}^{\phi}
, where
{\mathcal{N}}^{\phi}_{i}\coloneqq\{j\mid{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{i}\cap{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{j}\neq\emptyset\}
is the set of adjacent robots in the Laguerre sense.
Hence, we focus on clarifying the condition that each robot is capable of obtaining the information of the neighboring robots.
Suppose that each robot
i
has a measurement range
R_{i}
and obtains the information of robots
j
satisfying
\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|\leq R_{i}
.
Then, the proposed algorithm is feasible if
R_{i}
satisfies
\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|\leq R_{i}
for any
i
and
j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{i}^{\phi}
.
Such a range
R_{i}
does not increase in a common situation where many robots are distributed on the workspace and
|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}|
(for any
i,j
) is sufficiently smaller than the size of the workspace.

IV
Stability Analysis

In this section, we prove the Lyapunov stability of the system’s equilibrium when using our proposed OTCC.
Suppose that the dynamical system represented by (
8
) are controlled with the OTCC in (
17
) and (
18
).
The system equilibrium
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*})
is then characterized as a point that satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}x_{i}^{*}&={\tilde{b}}_{i},\\
\phi_{i}^{*}&\in\left\{\phi\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\tilde{a}}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\right\},\end{cases}&\quad\text{for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\end{split}

(33)

where we assume that
x_{i}^{*}\neq x_{j}^{*}
holds for all
i\neq j
.

Theorem 1
.

The equilibrium point
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*})
is Lyapunov stable if there exist neighborhoods
{\mathcal{U}}_{x^{*}}
around
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}
and for any
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{x^{*}}
,

\displaystyle H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*})\text{ is positive definite,}

(34)

where
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nd\times nd}
is defined as

\displaystyle\left(H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\right)_{ij}\ (\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d})=

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\|x_{j}-x_{i}\|}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{ij}}(x_{i}-s)(x_{j}-s)^{\top}\rho_{T}(s)\differential{s},\quad\mbox{$j\neq i$,}\quad\\
{\tilde{a}}_{i}I_{d}-\sum_{\ell\neq i}\frac{1}{\|x_{\ell}-x_{i}\|}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{i\ell}}(x_{i}-s)(x_{\ell}-s)^{\top}\rho_{T}(s)\differential{s},\\
\hfill\mbox{$j=i$.}\quad\end{cases}

Here,
\left(H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\right)_{ij}
denotes
(i,j)
-block matrix element of
H(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})
, and
I_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
denotes
d
-dimensional identity matrix.
We define
\partial{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{ij}\coloneqq{\mathcal{V}}_{i}^{\phi}(x)\cap{\mathcal{V}}_{j}^{\phi}(x)
as the Laguerre region boundary of
i
and
j
, and the integral in (
35
) is
0
when
\partial{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{ij}
is an empty set.

We prove Theorem
1
by utilizing following proposition.

Proposition 5

(
Cherukuri2017Saddlepoint
)
.

For a continuous and differentiable function
G:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, consider a system with the following dynamics:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{p}(t)&=-\nabla_{p}G(p,q),\\
\dot{q}(t)&=\nabla_{q}G(p,q).\end{split}

(36)

If
G
is convex-concave around the saddle point
(p^{*},q^{*})
, then the system of (
36
) is Lyapunov stable at
(p^{*},q^{*})
.

Here, the convex-concave function and the saddle point are defined below.

Definition 4

(Convex-concave function)
.

A function
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
convex
around
\bar{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
if a neighborhood
{\mathcal{U}}_{\bar{z}}
exists and the inequality of
\lambda g(x)+(1-\lambda)g(y)\geq g(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)
holds for any
x,y\in{\mathcal{U}}_{\bar{z}}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
The function
g
is
concave
around
\bar{z}
if the inverse inequality holds. Furthermore, the function
G:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
convex-concave
around
(\bar{p},\bar{q})
if
p\mapsto G(p,\bar{q})
is convex around
\bar{p}
and
q\mapsto G(\bar{p},q)
is concave around
\bar{q}
.

Definition 5

(Saddle point)
.

For a continuous and differentiable function
G:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, a point
(p^{*},q^{*})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a

if there exists neighbors
{\mathcal{U}}_{p^{*}}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{q^{*}}
, and the following holds for any
p\in{\mathcal{U}}_{p^{*}}
and
q\in{\mathcal{U}}_{q^{*}}

\displaystyle G(p^{*},q)\leq G(p^{*},q^{*})\leq G(p,q^{*}).

(37)

Proposition

ensures that it is sufficient to show that the function
F
in (
12
) is convex-concave around the equilibrium
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*})
of (
33
), and that
(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*})
is the saddle point of

. The former is guaranteed with the aid of the following two propositions:

Proposition 6
.

The function
\phi\mapsto F(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})
with
x
fixed to
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}
is concave around
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*}
.

Proof.

We denote the above function as
F_{x^{*}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})
. In order to use the second-order sufficient condition for the concave function

, we evaluate
\nabla^{2}_{\phi}F_{x^{*}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})
, the second-order derivative of
F_{x^{*}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})
for
\phi
. Using Reynolds’ transport theorem, it is shown that

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}_{\phi}F_{x^{*}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\right)_{ij}

(38)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\|x_{j}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}\|}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{ij}}\rho_{T}(s)\differential{s},&\mbox{for $j\neq i$,}\\
-\sum_{\ell\neq i}\frac{1}{\|x_{\ell}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}\|}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{V}}^{\phi}_{i\ell}}\rho_{T}(s)\differential{s},&\mbox{for $j=i$.}\end{cases}

Equation (

) is a weighted graph Laplacian multiplied by
-1
. Thus,
\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}^{\top}\nabla^{2}_{\phi}F_{x^{*}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\leq 0
holds for any
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
which means that

is negative semidefinite at any point
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

\blacksquare

Proposition 7
.

If the condition of (
34
) holds, the function
x\mapsto F(\mbox{\boldmath$x$},\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*})
with
\phi
fixed to
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{*}
is convex around
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{*}
.

Proof.

We denote the above function as
F_{\phi^{*}}(\mbox{\boldmath$x$})
.
Using Reynolds’ transport theorem, the second-order derivative of

Remark 4
.

We discuss the reasons of higher performance of the OTCC.
This is because that the equilibrium condition in the OTCC is stricter than that in the VTCC, which makes the robot less likely to be trapped in the stationary point.
The equilibrium condition in the VTCC is that the robot is placed at the center of gravity of each region
(x_{i}=b_{i})
, and once this condition is satisfied, each robot does not move thereafter.
In the OTCC, however, there is an additional condition that the weighted area of each region is equal (
{\tilde{a}}_{i}=1/n
in (
33
)).
Thus, even if the former condition is satisfied, robots continue to move unless the latter condition is satisfied, which is why robots are less likely to be trapped at the stationary point.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose the optimal transport-based coverage control (OTCC) method as an improvement to the Voronoi tessellation-based coverage control (VTCC) method.
Our proposed method, which is derived via the Kantorovich dual problem, is consistent with the VTCC with setting
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}(t)\equiv 0
.
This correspondence successfully reconsiders the VTCC in the optimal transport framework.
We also derive the conditions of Lyapunov stability for the controlled system in Theorem
1
and
2
, showing that once the robot swarm reaches the equilibrium point, they remain in the neighborhood. Numerical calculations clearly show that our proposed method is more capable of escaping the local optimum point and achieving better control than the VTCC.
As one of our next research topics, we plan to extend the applicability of the OTCC to more general class of systems.
