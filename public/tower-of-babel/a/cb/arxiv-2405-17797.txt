A note on the second neighborhood problem for k k -anti-transitive and m m -free digraphs
Dania Mezher, Moussa Daamouch

Abstract
Seymour’s Second Neighborhood Conjecture (SSNC) asserts that every finite oriented graph has a vertex whose second out-neighborhood is at least as large as its first outneighborhood. Such a vertex is called a Seymour vertex. A digraph D = ( V , E ) D=(V,E) is k k -anti-transitive if for every pair of vertices u , v ∈ V u,v\in V , the existence of a directed path of length k k from u u to v v implies that ( u , v ) ∉ E (u,v)\notin E . An m m -free digraph is digraph having no directed cycles with length at most m m . In this paper, we prove that if D D is k k -anti-transitive and ( k − 4 ) (k-4) -free digraph, then D D has a Seymour vertex. As a consequence, a special case of Caccetta-Haggkvist Conjecture holds on 7-anti-transitive oriented graphs. This work extends recently known results.

Terminology and introduction

In this paper, A directed graph (or a digraph)
D
is a pair of two disjoint sets
(V,E)
, where

is non-empty and

. The set

. While

, denoted by

, is called the arc set of

. Each pair

signifies an arc from a vertex

to a vertex

. All the directed graphs discussed in this paper are finite oriented graph, meaning that

is finite, there are no self-loops
(u,u)\notin E
, and for any pair of vertices

and

in
D
, there is at most one arc connecting them. We may write

meaning that
(u,v)\in E(D)
. We denote by

a directed

-path (

) and we may write
x_{0}\rightarrow x_{1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow x_{k}
. A directed
k
-cycle (

) is denoted by
x_{0}\cdots x_{k-1}x_{0}
, and we may write
x_{0}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow x_{k-1}\rightarrow x_{0}
. The length of a directed path (or cycle) is the number of its arcs. The distance
\text{$dist$}(v,u)
from a vertex

to

. The out-neighborhood

. The second out-neighborhood

, that is
N_{G}^{++}(v)=\{w\in V\colon\text{dist}(v,w)=2\}
. The second out-degree of a vertex

. Given a subset

In 1990, Paul Seymour proposed the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.1

(Seymour’s Second Neighborhood Conjecture)
.

For any oriented simple graph
D
, there is a vertex
v\in V
such that
d^{+}(v)\leq d^{++}(v)
.

A vertex
v
satisfying
d^{+}(v)\leq d^{++}(v)
is referred to as a
Seymour vertex
.

In the study of graph theory, when researchers encounter a challenging problem, it is a common strategy to limit the problem to a specific family of graphs or digraphs. Seymour’s conjecture, as an instance, has been proven exclusively for a few selected classes of digraphs. Notably, in 1996, Fisher
[
12
]
took a significant step by proving SSNC for a family of digraphs known as tournaments. Progress continued into the new millennium: in 2000, Havet and Thomassé
[
19
]
offered a streamlined proof of SSNC for tournaments, and in 2001, Kaneko and Locke
[
21
]
established the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
21
]
:
Let
D
be an oriented graph, and let
\delta^{+}
be its minimum out-degree. If
\delta^{+}\leq 6
, then
D
has a Seymour vertex.

In 2007, Fidler and Yuster
[
11
]
substantiated the case of tournaments missing a matching. Moving forward, Ghazal
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, during the years 2012, 2013, and 2015, affirmed that the conjecture stands true for several classes of directed graphs, including tournaments missing an
n
-generalized stars and other categories of oriented graphs. In the same manner, some attempts were made to settle SSNC for tournaments missing a specific graph (see
[
1
,
9
,
10
]
).

Another approach to SSNC is to determine the maximum value
\lambda
such that every oriented graphs has a vertex
v
and
d^{++}(v)\geq\lambda d^{+}(v)
. In 2003, Chen, Shen, and Yuster
[

]
proved that in every oriented graph, there exists a vertex

such that

where
\lambda=0.657\cdots
is the unique real root in the interval
(0,1)
of
2x^{3}+x^{2}-1=0
. In 2017, Liang and Xu
[
22
]
improved this result for the class of digraphs having no directed cycles with length at most
m
, called
m
-free digraphs, by showing that for any
m
-free digraph

, there exists
v\in V(D)
and a real number
\lambda_{m}\in(0,1)
such that
d^{++}(v)\geq\lambda_{m}d^{+}(v)
, and
\lambda_{m}\rightarrow 1
while
m\rightarrow\infty
. Note that the class of
m
-free digraphs is a subclass of digraphs with forbidden

, and SSNC for both still remains open.

In a separate development in 2012, Galena-Sánchez and Hernández-Cruz
[
20
]
introduced the class of

-transitive digraphs and the class of

-quasi-transitive digraphs. A digraph

is

-transitive if, for any vertices

in

, the existence of a directed path from

to

implies that there is a arc from

to

in

. A digraph

is

in

, the existence of a directed path from

to

of length

implies that there is a an arc from

to

or from

to

. In terms of forbidden (not necessarily induced) subdigraphs,

-transitive oriented graphs are oriented graphs with forbidden

and

, and

-quasi-transitive oriented graphs are oriented graphs with forbidden

. In 2017, García-Vásquez and Hernández-Cruz
[
13
]
demonstrated that every 4-transitive oriented graph has a Seymour vertex. Additionally, Gutin and Li
[
17
]
proved SSNC for quasi-transitive oriented graphs, specifically those that are 2-quasi-transitive. Moving ahead, recent works, as seen in
[
6
,
8
]
, validated SSNC for 3-quasi-transitive oriented graphs as well as for some instances of
k
-transitive oriented graphs, specifically for
k\leq\delta^{+}+4
and consequently for

as well as for

-transitive digraphs that are
(\frac{k}{2}-1)
-free.

The vertices
x
,
y
, and
z
are said to form a transitive triangle if we have
x\rightarrow y\rightarrow z
and also
x\rightarrow z
. It is easily seen that a transitive triangle is obtained from a directed triangle
C_{3}
by reversing the direction of exactly one arc. We denote by
C(n,1)
an oriented cycle on
n+1
vertices obtained from a directed cycle
C_{n+1}
by reversing the direction of exactly one arc. A digraph
D
is
k
-anti-transitive
if for any
u,v\in V(D)
, the existence of a directed
uv
-path of length
k
implies
(u,v)\notin E(D)
. In other words, the class of
k
-anti-transitive digraphs are that of digraphs with forbidden

. In 2009, Brantner, Brockman, Kay, and Snively
[
2
]
proved that every minimum out-degree vertex in 2-anti-transitive digraphs (digraphs without transitive triangles) is a Seymour vertex. In 2020, this result is improved and extended in
[
5
,
7
]
to
k
-anti-transitive digraphs for

. later, in 2021, Hassan et al.
[
18
]
proved SSNC for 6-anti-transitive digraphs. It is seen that the difficulty of SSNC for
k
-anti-transitive digraphs is increasing with respect to
k
, especially with the lack of structural description of these digraphs. Note that every digraph
D
is
k
-anti-transitive for

greater than the length of a longest directed path in
D
. So if a property holds for
k
-anti-transitive digraphs with arbitrary

, then this property holds for all digraphs. As it is difficult to settle SSNC for the class of
k
-anti-transitive digraphs when

is an arbitrary positive integer, it is convenient to combine this class of digraphs with another. For instance, in
[
7
]
, the SSNC is proven for digraphs that are both
k
-anti-transitive and
(k-3)
-free. In this paper, we prove SSNC for digraphs that are
k
-anti-transitive and
(k-4)
-free. As a consequence, we show that a special case of Caccetta-Haggkvist Conjecture holds on 7-anti-transitive oriented graphs.

2
Some requisite results

Lemma 2.1
.

[
7
]

Let
D
be an oriented graph, and let
\delta^{+}
be its minimum out-degree. Let
v\in V(D)
such that
d^{+}(v)=\delta^{+}
. If
D[N^{+}(v)]
has a sink vertex
x
, i.e.,
d^{+}_{N^{+}(v)}(x)=0
, then
v
is a Seymour vertex.

Proof.

We have
|N^{++}(v)|\geq|N^{+}(x)\setminus N^{+}(v)|=|N^{+}(x)|\geq\delta^{+}=|N^{+}(v)|
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

[
5
]

Let
D
be a
k
-anti-transitive oriented graph with
k\geq 2
, and let
v\in V(D)
. If there exists a directed path of length
d
in
D[N^{+}(v)]
, then
d\leq k-2
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
d>k-2
. Then we can find a directed path
x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{k-1}
of length
k-1
in
D[N^{+}(v)]
. Hence,
vx_{0}\cdots x_{k-1}
is a directed path of length
k
, but
D
is
k
-anti-transitive, so
v\nrightarrow x_{k-1}
, which contradicts to
x_{k-1}\in N^{+}(v)
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

[
18
]

Let
v
be a vertex of
D
with the minimum out-degree, i.e.,
d^{+}(v)=\delta^{+}
, and
R=N^{+}(v)
. Let
d^{++}(v)\leq\delta^{+}-1
. For distinct
r_{i},r_{j},r_{k}\in R
, we have

\displaystyle|N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{i},r_{j})|

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{+}+1-d^{+}_{R}(r_{i})-d^{+}_{R}(r_{j})

\displaystyle|N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{i},r_{j},r_{k})|

\displaystyle\geq\delta^{+}+2-d^{+}_{R}(r_{i})-d^{+}_{R}(r_{j})-d^{+}_{R}(r_{k})

Proposition 2.1
.

[
7
]

Let
D
be an
m
-free digraph. If
m\geq\delta^{+}-1
, then
D
has a Seymour vertex.

Remark:
Let
D
be an oriented simple graph. In view of Theorem
1.1
, when proving that
D
has a Seymour vertex, it is sufficient to assume that
\delta^{+}\geq 7
.

It is well known that SSNC implies the following special case of Caccetta-Haggkvist Conjecture.

Conjecture 2.1
.

[
3
]

Every digraph on
n
vertices with minimum in-degree and minimum out-degree both at least
\frac{n}{3}
has a directed triangle

.

More precisely, we have the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph on
n
vertices such that its minimum in-degree and its minimum out-degree are both at least
\frac{n}{3}
. If
D
has a Seymour vertex, then it has a directed triangle.

As SSNC holds on
k
-anti-transitive digraphs for
2\leq k\leq 6
, the special case of Caccetta-Haggkvist conjecture also holds on
k
-anti-transitive digraphs for
2\leq k\leq 6
.

3
Main result

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
D
be an oriented graph. If
D
is both
k
-anti-transitive and
(k-4)
-free, then
D
has a Seymour vertex.

Proof.

Notice that the validity of this theorem has been confirmed for
k=6
, denoting
6
-anti-transitivity and
2
-freeness
[
18
]
. Subsequently, our focus shifts to cases with
k\geq 7
, ensuring that the digraph is at least
3
-free. Hence
m\geq 3
. In addition, in view of Theorem
1.1
, it is sufficient to assume that
\delta^{+}\geq 7
.

Let
v\in V(D)
such that
d^{+}(v)=\delta^{+}
. Set
R=N^{+}(v)=\{r_{0},r_{1},\ldots,r_{\delta^{+}-1}\}
and let
m=k-4
.
If
m\geq\delta^{+}-1
, then by Proposition
2.1
,
D
has a Seymour vertex. Due to this, we can presume that
m<\delta^{+}-1
.
If there exists
r\in R
such that
d^{+}_{R}(r)=0
(this vertex is referred to as a sink vertex), then by Lemma
2.1
,
v
is a Seymour vertex in
D
. Presume now that we have
d^{+}_{R}(r)\geq 1
for all
r\in R
. Let
l
be the length of a longest directed path in
D[R]
. Given that
D
is an
m
-free digraph, we have
D[R]
is also an
m
-free digraph, and as
d^{+}_{R}(r)\geq 1
for all
r\in R
, it follows that
D[R]
has a directed path of length at least
m
. On the other hand, Lemma
2.2
implies that
l\leq k-2=(m+4)-2=m+2
. So we have three cases:
l=m
,
l=m+1
and
l=m+2
.

Observe that we may assume that
d^{++}(v)\leq\delta^{+}-1
, since otherwise
v
is a Seymour vertex.

Case 1
.

\ell=m
.

Let
r_{0}r_{1}\cdots r_{m}
be a longest path in
D[R]
of length
m
. Set
B=\{r_{i}\in R\colon i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\}
. As
r_{0}r_{1}\cdots r_{m}
is a longest path, we must have
N^{+}_{R}(r_{m})\subseteq B
. If
r_{m}\rightarrow r_{i}
for some
i\neq 0
, then
r_{m}r_{i}\cdots r_{m}
would be a directed cycle of length at most
m
, which contradicts the fact that
D
is an
m
-free digraph. It follows that
N^{+}_{R}(r_{m})=\{r_{0}\}
as
d^{+}_{R}(r_{m})\geq 1
, and hence
d^{+}_{R}(r_{m})=1
. Similarly, since
D
is an
m
-free digraph and
\ell=m
, we must have
N^{+}_{R}(r_{i})=\{r_{i+1}\}
and for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
. Therefore
d^{+}_{R}(r_{i})=1
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
.
If
d^{+}(r_{i})\geq\delta^{+}+1
for some
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
, then
d^{++}(v)\geq|N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{i})|\geq d^{+}(r_{i})-d^{+}_{R}(r_{i})\geq\delta^{+}+1-1=\delta^{+}
, which contradicts our assumption that
d^{++}(v)\leq\delta^{+}-1
. Thus
d^{+}(r_{i})=\delta^{+}
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
. It follows that
d^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{i})=\delta^{+}-1
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
. We can easily see that
N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{i})=N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{j})
for all
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
. Otherwise, there exists a vertex
z\in\overline{R}
such that
z\in N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{i})\setminus N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{j})
. Thus,
|N^{++}(v)|\geq|N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{j})|+|\{z\}|\geq\delta^{+}-1+1=\delta^{+}
, which contradicts the fact that
d^{++}(v)\leq\delta^{+}-1
. Now let
X=N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{m})=N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{0})
. We will show that there exists a vertex
z\in X
such that
d^{+}_{X}(z)=0
, and
r_{m}
will be a Seymour vertex. On the contrary, assume that for each vertex
z\in X
, we have
d^{+}_{X}(z)\geq 1
. Hence we can find
z_{1},z_{2},
and
z_{3}\in X
such that
z_{1}\rightarrow z_{2}\rightarrow z_{3}
. As
m\geq 3
, we have
z_{3}\nrightarrow z_{1}
. Furthermore
z_{3}\nrightarrow z_{4}
for any
z_{4}\in X
. Because otherwise
r_{0}r_{1}\cdots r_{m}z_{1}z_{2}z_{3}z_{4}
would be a directed path of length

, giving
z_{4}\notin N^{+}(r_{0})
, which is a contradiction as
z_{4}\in N^{+}(r_{0})
. Thus, there exists

such that

, and hence

. Therefore
d^{++}(r_{m})\geq d^{+}_{\overline{X}}(z)\geq\delta^{+}=d^{+}(r_{m})
, and

is a Seymour vertex.

Case 2
.

\ell=m+1
.

Let
r_{0}r_{1}\cdots r_{m+1}
be a longest directed path in

of length

, and consider

. In fact, suppose

. Hence

would be a directed cycle of length at most

, which contradicts the fact that

is an

for all

, otherwise

, which is a contradiction. We examine two cases:

and
r_{m+1}\nrightarrow r_{0}
.

Sub-Case 2.1
.

r_{m+1}\rightarrow r_{0}
.

In this case

. Note that for all

, we have

, since otherwise

, which is a contradiction. In the other hand, since

is an
m
-free digraph, we must have
N^{+}_{R}(r_{i})\subseteq\{r_{i+1},r_{i+2}\}

r_{i}\in B

. Hence, we have
d^{+}_{R}(r_{i})\leq 2
for all
r_{i}\in B
. Observe that there must exist a vertex
r_{i}\in B

d^{+}_{B}(r_{i})=1
. Otherwise,
N^{+}_{B}(r_{i})=\{r_{i+1},r_{i+2}\}

, and hence
r_{m+1}r_{0}r_{2}r_{4}r_{6}r_{7}\cdots r_{m}r_{m+1}
would be a directed cycle of length at most

, which contradicts the fact that

is an
m
-free digraph. We can presume that

. It follows that
N^{+}_{R}(r_{m+1})=\{r_{0}\}
and

. Furthermore, we must have

, for otherwise we get

, which is a contradiction. Now, Lemma

. Let

.

If

, then

. Observe that

and

. It follows that

, and hence

is a Seymour vertex.

d^{+}_{X}(z_{1})\geq 2
, then there must exist a vertex
z_{2}\in N^{+}_{\overline{R}}(r_{m+1})
such that
z_{1}\rightarrow z_{2}
. Note that
z_{2}\nrightarrow z_{3}

, since otherwise

would be a directed path of length

, such that

which is a contradiction. Additionally,
z_{2}\nrightarrow r_{0}
since otherwise
vr_{m+1}z_{1}z_{2}r_{0}\cdots r_{m}
would be a directed path of length
k=m+4
, giving
r_{m}\notin N^{+}(v)
, which is a contradiction.
Hence,
d^{+}_{X}(z_{2})=0
and
d^{+}_{\overline{X}}(z_{2})\geq\delta^{+}
.
Therefore
d^{++}(r_{m+1})\geq d^{+}_{\overline{X}}(z_{2})\geq\delta^{+}=d^{+}(r_{m+1})
, and
r_{m+1}
is a Seymour vertex.

.

r_{m+1}\nrightarrow r_{0}
.

In this case we have
N^{+}_{R}(r_{m+1})=\{r_{1}\}
and

, since otherwise

, which is a contradiction. In addition, we may assume that

, since otherwise

, which is a contradiction.

. Clearly,

, as otherwise

. Let

Let

2) Finally, suppose that
