The Sierpinski Object in the Scott Realizability Topos Thanks: Corresponding author: Jaap van Oosten
Tom de Jong, Jaap van Oosten

Abstract
. We study the Sierpinski object Σ \Sigma in the realizability topos based on Scott’s graph model of the λ \lambda -calculus. Our starting observation is that the object of realizers in this topos is the exponential Σ N \Sigma^{N} , where N N is the natural numbers object. We define order-discrete objects by orthogonality to Σ \Sigma . We show that the order-discrete objects form a reflective subcategory of the topos, and that many fundamental objects in higher-type arithmetic are order-discrete. Building on work by Lietz, we give some new results regarding the internal logic of the topos. Then we consider Σ \Sigma as a dominance; we explicitly construct the lift functor and characterize Σ \Sigma -subobjects. Contrary to our expectations the dominance Σ \Sigma is not closed under unions. In the last section we build a model for homotopy theory, where the order-discrete objects are exactly those objects which only have constant paths.

Introduction

In this paper, we aim to revive interest in what we call the
Sierpinski object
in the
Scott realizability topos
. We show that it is of fundamental importance in studying the subcategory of
order-discrete
objects (section 3), arithmetic in the topos (section 4), the Sierpinski object as a dominance (section 5) and a notion of homotopy based on it (section 6). Section 2 deals with preliminaries and establishes notation.

2.
Preliminaries

Independently observed by G. Plotkin and D.S. Scott (
[
11
,
12
,
10
]
), there is a combinatory algebra structure (in fact, a
\lambda
-model structure) on the power set
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
of the natural numbers.

Identifying
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
with
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{0,1\}
and giving
\{0,1\}
the Sierpinski topology (with
\{1\}
the only nontrivial open set), we endow
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
with the product topology. Concretely, basic opens of
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
are of the form

{\mathcal{U}}_{p}\;=\;\{U\subseteq\mathbb{N}\,|\,p\subseteq U\}

for
p
a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}
. A function
F:{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})\to{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
is continuous for this topology if and only if it has the property that

n\in F(U)\text{ if and only if for some finite $p\subseteq U$, }n\in F(p).

Note, that every continuous function automatically preserves the inclusion
\subseteq
.

Such continuous maps are encoded by elements of
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
in the following way. Consider the following coding of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
:

e_{n}=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}\}\text{ if and only if }n=2^{k_{1}}+\cdots+2^{k_{m}}.

By convention,
e_{0}=\emptyset
. So:
e_{1}=\{0\}
,
e_{2}=\{1\}
,
e_{3}=\{0,1\}
,
e_{2^{n}}=\{n\}
, etc.

Consider also a bijection
\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\rangle:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, which we don’t specify.

Now we define a binary operation (written as juxtaposition) on
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
:

UV=\{n\,|\,\text{ for some }m,e_{m}\subseteq V\text{ and }\langle m,n\rangle\in U\}

For every function
F:{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})\to{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
we have

{\sf graph}(F)\;=\;\{\langle m,n\rangle\,|\,n\in F(e_{m})\}

so that, when
F
is continuous,
{\sf graph}(F)V=F(V)
for all
V\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
. Note that the operation
U,V\mapsto UV
is a continuous map of two variables.

We think of the operation
U,V\mapsto UV
as an
application operation
: apply the continuous function coded by
U
to the argument
V
.

The structure
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
together with this application function is denoted
\mathcal{S}
. The fact that
\mathcal{S}
is a
\lambda
-model is worked out in detail in
[
12
]
. We shall use
\lambda
-terms to denote elements of
\mathcal{S}
according to this interpretation. We use repeated juxtaposition and associate to the left when discussing iterated application:
VU_{1}\cdots U_{n}
means
(\cdots((VU_{1})U_{2})\cdots)U_{n}
. For continuous functions of several variables
F:{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})^{n}\to{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
we let

{\sf graph}(F)\,=\,\{\langle m_{1},\langle m_{2},\langle\cdots m_{n},k\rangle\cdots\rangle\rangle\,|\,k\in F(e_{m_{1}},\ldots,,e_{m_{n}})\}

So with these conventions,
{\sf graph}(F)U_{1}\cdots U_{n}=F(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
.

In
\mathcal{S}
, special combinators for important operations are available:

Pairing and unpairing combinators:

{\sf p},{\sf p}_{0},{\sf p}_{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}
satisfying
{\sf p}_{0}({\sf p}UV)=U
,
{\sf p}_{1}({\sf p}UV)=V
. In fact we can take

\{\langle 2^{n},\langle 0,2n\rangle\rangle\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\langle 0,\langle 2^{n},2n+1\rangle\rangle\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}

for
\sf p
, so that

{\sf p}U\;=\;\{\langle 0,2n\rangle\,|\,n\in U\}\cup\{\langle 2^{n},2n+1\rangle\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}

and

{\sf p}UV\;=\;\{2n\,|\,n\in U\}\cup\{2n+1\,|\,n\in V\}.

Booleans:

{\sf t}=\{1\}
,
{\sf f}=\{0\}
. We have an “if … then …, else…” operator
\sf q
, satisfying
{\sf qt}UV=U
and
{\sf qf}UV=V
.

Natural numbers:

we write
\overline{n}
for the element
\{n\}
.

Coding of tuples:

we write
[U_{0},\ldots,U_{n}]
for a standard coding of
n+1
-tuples in
\mathcal{S}
. This coding has the usual properties as given in
[
15
]
: there are elements of
\mathcal{S}
which give the length of a coded tuple, the
i
’th element (for appropriate
i
), and the code of the concatenation of two tuples.

As usual we have a
category of assemblies
over
\mathcal{S}
, denoted
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
and the
realizability topos

{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
, which we call the
Scott realizability topos
(unfortunately, the term “Scott topos” is already in use). We briefly introduce these categories.

An assembly over
\mathcal{S}
is a pair
X=(|X|,E_{X})
where
|X|
is a set and
E_{X}
gives, for each
x\in|X|
, a nonempty subset
E_{X}(x)
of
\mathcal{S}
. A morphism of assemblies
(|X|,E_{X})\to(|Y|,E_{Y})
is a function
f:|X|\to|Y|
for which there is some element
U
of
\mathcal{S}
which
tracks

f
, meaning that for all
x\in|X|
and all
V\in E_{X}(x)
we have
UV\in E_{Y}(f(x))
.

The category
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
is a quasitopos. By way of example, let us construct exponentials explicitly: the exponent
(Y,E_{Y})^{(X,E_{X})}
can be given as
(Z,E_{Z})
where
Z
is the set of morphisms from
(X,E_{X})
to
(Y,E_{Y})
and
E_{Z}(f)
is the set of those elements of
\mathcal{S}
which track the morphism
f
.

Actually,
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
has countable products: given a countable family
((X_{n}),E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of assemblies over
\mathcal{S}
, one has the assembly
(\prod_{n}X_{n},E)
where

E((x_{n})_{n})\;=\;\{U\,|\,U\overline{n}\in E_{n}(x_{n})\text{ for all }n\}.

The category
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
has several subcategories of interest. First we recall that there is an adjunction

{\rm Set}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\nabla}}{{\to}}{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}});\;\;\;{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Gamma}}{{\to}}{\rm Set};\;\;\;\Gamma\dashv\nabla

where
\Gamma(|X|,E_{X})=|X|
,
\nabla(Y)=(Y,E)
with
E(y)={\mathcal{S}}
for all
y
.

An assembly
X
is
partitioned
if
E_{X}(x)
is always a singleton. It is not hard to see that the object
\nabla(Y)
is isomorphic to
(Y,E)
where
E(y)=\{\emptyset\}
for all
y
, so all objects
\nabla(Y)
are isomorphic to partitioned assemblies. Up to isomorphism, the partitioned assemblies are exactly the regular projective objects (the objects for which the representable functor into Sets preserves regular epimorphisms), which, in the topos
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
, coincide with the internally projective objects (the objects
X
for which the endofuctor
(-)^{X}
preserves epimorphisms); see
2
below.

An assembly
X
is
modest
if
E_{X}(x)\cap E_{X}(y)=\emptyset
whenever
x\neq y
. The modest assemblies are, up to isomorphism, the ones for which the diagonal map
X\to X^{\nabla(2)}
is an isomorphism (here, 2 denotes any two-element set); such objects are called
discrete
in
[
4
]
.

A special object of
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
is the
object of realizers

S=({\mathcal{S}},E)
where
E(U)=\{U\}
; one sees that
S
is both partitioned and modest. Every object which is both partitioned and modest is a regular subobject of
S
: that is, isomorphic to an object
({\mathcal{A}},E)
where
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
and
E
is the restriction to
\mathcal{A}
of the map defining
S
.

Another partitioned and modest object is the
natural numbers object

N=(\mathbb{N},E_{N})
, where
E_{N}(n)=\{\overline{n}\}
.

As Andrej Bauer observed in his thesis (
[
1
]
), one can embed the category
T_{0}^{\rm cb}
of countably based
T_{0}
-spaces into the subcategory of partitioned and modest assemblies over
\mathcal{S}
. To be precise, by “countably based
T_{0}
-space” we mean a
T_{0}
-space together with a chosen enumeration of a subbasis. If
(X,\{S_{n}\,|\,n\in\mathbb{N}\})
is such, then the map
e:X\to{\mathcal{S}}
given by

e(x)=\{n\,|\,x\in S_{n}\}

defines an embedding of
X
into
\mathcal{S}
. Moreover, given countably based spaces
X
and
Y
with associated embeddings
e:X\to{\mathcal{S}},d:Y\to{\mathcal{S}}
, there is for every continuous map
f:X\to Y
a continuous extension
F_{f}:{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{S}}
satisfying
F_{f}{\circ}e=d{\circ}f
. This means that if we see the space
X
with embedding
e:X\to{\mathcal{S}}
as assembly
(X,E_{X})
where
E_{X}(x)=\{e(x)\}
, then every continuous map
f:X\to Y
is tracked (as a morphism of assemblies
(X,E_{X})\to(Y,E_{Y})
) by
{\sf graph}(F_{f})
.

Finally, we have the
Scott realizability topos
, the realizability topos over
\mathcal{S}
, denoted
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
. Its objects are pairs
(X,\sim)
where
X
is a set and for
x,y\in X
,
[x\sim y]
is a subset of
\mathcal{S}
(so
\sim
is a
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{S}})
-valued relation on
X
), which satisfies certain conditions (we refer to
[
15
]
for details). Every assembly
(X,E)
is an object of
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
if we define

[x\sim y]\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}E(x)&\text{if }x=y\\
\emptyset&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

As subcategory of
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
, the category
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
is equivalent to the category of
\neg\neg
-stable objects of
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
, that is: the objects
(X,\sim)
satisfying
\forall x,x^{\prime}:X.\neg\neg(x\sim x^{\prime})\to x\sim x^{\prime}
.
We shall not rehearse the structure of
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
or its internal logic, based on realizability over
\mathcal{S}
, any further, as for this theory now standard texts are available. We only recall the following facts from
[
15
]
, pp.135–7 (the proofs generalize to arbitrary pcas):

Lemma 1
.

If
P
is a partitioned assembly and
X
is any object, then the exponential

X^{P}
is isomorphic to the object
(|X|^{|P|},\approx)
where

f\approx g=[\forall p(E_{P}(p)\to f(p)\sim_{X}g(p))].

Lemma 2
.

An object is internally projective in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
if and only if it is isomorphic to a
partitioned assembly.

The following small proposition will be of use later on. Given an assembly
(X,E)
let
{\int}(X,E)
be
(X^{\prime},E^{\prime})
where
X^{\prime}=\{(x,U)\,|\,U\in E(x)\}
and
E^{\prime}(x,U)=\{U\}
. Consider also
{\int\!\!\!\int}(X,E)=(X^{\prime\prime},E^{\prime\prime})
where
X^{\prime\prime}=\{(x,U,V)\,|\,U,V\in E(x)\}
and
E^{\prime\prime}(x,U,V)=\{{\sf p}UV\}
. Clearly, we have a projection
{\int}(X,E)\to(X,E)
and two projections
{\int\!\!\!\int}(X,E)\to{\int}(X,E)
, and these maps form a coequalizer diagram in
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
(Y,\sim)
be an arbitrary object of
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
and let
(X,E)
be an assembly over
\mathcal{S}
. The exponential
(Y,\sim)^{(X,E)}
can be presented as follows: its underlying set is the set of functions from
\{(x,U)\,|\,U\in E(x)\}
to
Y
. Given two such functions
f,g
, the set
[f\approx g]
consists of those coded triples
[P,Q,R]
(coded in the sense of
\mathcal{S}
) of elements of
\mathcal{S}
, which satisfy the following three conditions:

(1)

for
U\in E(x)
,
PU\in[f(x,U)\sim f(x,U)]
;

(2)

for
U,U^{\prime}\in E(x)
,
QUU^{\prime}\in[f(x,U)\sim f(x,U^{\prime})]
;

(3)

for
U\in E(x)
,
RU\in[f(x,U)\sim g(x,U)]
.

Proof 2.1
.

Observe that the objects
{\int}(X,E)
and
{\int\!\!\!\int}(X,E)
are partitioned assemblies, hence projective objects in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
. Also note that the contravariant functor
(Y,\sim)^{(-)}
sends coequalizers to equalizers. Finally, use Lemma
1
.

3.
The Sierpiński object and order-discrete objects

{defi}

The
Sierpiński object

\Sigma
is the image of Sierpiński space
under the embedding
T_{0}^{\text{cb}}\to\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
. Concretely,

\Sigma
is the modest set
(\{0,1\},E_{\Sigma})
with

E_{\Sigma}(0)=\{\emptyset\}\text{ and }E(1)=\{\{1\}\}.

{defi}

The
object of realizers

S
is the modest set
(\mathcal{S},\{-\})
.

The two objects above are related, as follows.

Proposition 4
.

The object of realizers is isomorphic to the exponential
\Sigma^{N}
, where

N
is the natural numbers object of
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof 3.1
.

We prove that the underlying set of
\Sigma^{N}
is
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Since any morphism from
N
to
\Sigma
is in particular a function
from
\mathbb{N}
to
\{0,1\}
, one inclusion is clear. Conversely, if
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\{0,1\}
, then
f
is tracked by
\textup{graph}(F)
, where
F\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
is the continuous function defined by:

\overline{n}\mapsto\begin{cases}\overline{1}&\text{if }f(n)=1;\\
\emptyset&\text{if }f(n)=0.\end{cases}

Thus,
\Sigma^{N}
is the assembly
(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},E)
where
E(f)\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is the set of trackers of the function
f
(note, that this set is always nonempty).

We have a canonical bijection

\chi\colon\mathcal{S}\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},\quad U\mapsto\chi_{U}

(where
\chi_{U}
is the characteristic function of
U
) with inverse

\chi^{-1}\colon\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathcal{S},\quad f\mapsto f^{-1}(\{1\}).

It remains to prove that these functions are tracked. For
\chi
, consider
the continuous function
F\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
given by

U\mapsto\{\langle 2^{n},1\rangle\mid n\in U\}.

Then
\chi
is tracked by graph(
F
). Indeed, if
U\in\mathcal{S}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\textup{graph}(F)U\overline{n}=F(U)\overline{n}
and this is
\overline{1}

if
n\in U
and
\emptyset
otherwise.

For the inverse of
\chi
, we define
G\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
continuous by

U\mapsto\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid U\overline{n}=\overline{1}\}
. (This is
continuous, because the application of
\mathcal{S}
is continuous.) We claim that

\chi^{-1}
is tracked by
\textup{graph}(G)
.
Indeed, for
f\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
U\in E(f)
, we have
n\in G(U)
if
and only if
f(n)=1
, since
U
tracks
f
.

3.1.
Order-discrete objects

{defi}

An object
X
of
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
is
order-discrete
if it is
orthogonal to
\Sigma
, viz. the diagonal
X\xrightarrow{\delta_{X}}X^{\Sigma}

is an isomorphism.

We wish to characterize the order-discrete objects in terms of their realizers.
To this end, we first present some useful lemmas.

Lemma 5
.

An object
X
is order-discrete if and only if the diagonal
\delta_{X}
is an
epimorphism.

Proof 3.2
.

Of course,
\delta_{X}
is epic if
X
is order-discrete. Conversely, suppose

\delta_{X}
is an epi. Clearly, the unique morphism
!_{\Sigma}\colon\Sigma\to 1
is an epimorphism. Hence, the diagonal
\delta_{X}=X^{!_{\Sigma}}
is
monic. Thus,
\delta_{X}
is an iso, as desired.

Proposition 6
.

An object
X
is order-discrete if and only if there is
A\in\mathcal{S}
such that
for any
x,x^{\prime}\in|X|
: if
U\in[x\sim_{X}x]
and
V\in[x^{\prime}\sim_{X}x^{\prime}]
with

U\subseteq V
, then
AUV\in[x\sim_{X}x^{\prime}]
.

Proof 3.3
.

Suppose first that
X
is order-discrete. We construct the desired element

A\in\mathcal{S}
. Assume we have
x,x^{\prime}\in|X|
and
U\in[x\sim_{X}x],V\in[x^{\prime}\sim_{X}x^{\prime}]
with
U\subseteq V
.
By Lemma
1
we have
X^{\Sigma}\cong(|X|^{\{0,1\}},\approx)
. Define
f\colon\{0,1\}\to|X|
by
f(0)=x

and
f(1)=x^{\prime}
. The map
H\colon\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{S}
defined as

(W,W^{\prime})\mapsto\{\langle 0,n\rangle\mid n\in W\}\cup\{\langle 2,n\rangle\mid n\in W^{\prime}\}

is easily seen to be continuous. Write
G=\textup{graph}(H)
. We claim that
GUV\in[f\approx f]
. Indeed,
GUV=\{\langle 0,n\rangle\mid n\in U\}\cup\{\langle 2,n\rangle\mid n\in V\}
, so that
GUV\emptyset=U\in[f(0)\sim_{X}f(0)]
. Moreover,
GUV\overline{1}=U\cup V=V\in[f(1)\sim_{X}f(1)]
, since

e_{0}=\emptyset
and
e_{2}=\overline{1}
and
U\subseteq V
. Thus,
GUV\in[f\approx f]
. Now let
R\in\mathcal{S}
be a realizer of the fact that

\delta
is epic. Then
R(GUV)
is an element of
[\forall p(E_{\Sigma}(p)\to x_{0}\sim_{X}f(p))]
for some
x_{0}\in|X|
. Thus,
R(GUV)\emptyset\in[x_{0}\sim_{X}x]
and
R(GUV)\overline{1}\in[x_{0}\sim_{X}x^{\prime}]
. Finally, let

t,s\in\mathcal{S}
respectively realize transitivity and symmetry of
\sim_{X}
.
Then, we see that

\lambda{uv}.t(\mathsf{p}(s(R(Guv)\emptyset))(R(Guv)\overline{1}))

is the desired element
A
.

Conversely, suppose we have an
A\in\mathcal{S}
as in the proposition. Let
f\colon\{0,1\}\to|X|
be arbitrary. By Lemma
5
, it suffices to show that
from an element of
[f\approx f]
, we can continuously find an
x\in|X|

and an element of
[\forall p(E_{\Sigma}(p)\to x\sim_{X}f(p))]
. Let
F\in[f\approx f]
. Then
F\emptyset\in[f(0)\sim_{X}f(0)]
,
F\overline{1}\in[f(1)\sim_{X}f(1)]
and
F\emptyset\subseteq F\overline{1}
, so

A(F\emptyset)(F\overline{1})\in[f(0)\sim_{X}f(1)]
and

A(F\emptyset)(F\emptyset)\in[f(0)\sim_{X}f(0)]
. Hence, if we set
x=f(0)
,
then the graph of the continuous function

\emptyset\mapsto A(F\emptyset)(F\emptyset),\quad W\neq\emptyset\mapsto A(F\emptyset)(F\overline{1})

is the desired element.

Corollary 7
.

An assembly
X
is order-discrete if and only if the existence of realizers

U\in E_{X}(x)
and
V\in E_{X}(y)
with
U\subseteq V
implies that
x
and
y

are equal.

Proof 3.4
.

Immediate.

{exa}

The natural numbers object
N
(c.f. the proof of

4
) is easily seen to be order-discrete using the
corollary above.

Recall from the section on preliminaries that an object is called

discrete
if it is orthogonal to
\nabla(2)
. For an assembly
X
, this
means that the realizing sets are disjoint. Thus, Corollary
7
shows that any
order-discrete assembly is also discrete (alternatively, one might observe that the map from
\Sigma
to
\nabla(2)
is an epi in
{\sf Ass}({\mathcal{S}})
, and therefore the map
X^{\nabla(2)}\to X^{\Sigma}
is monic. From this one also easily deduces that order-discrete implies discrete, for assemblies). In fact, this holds for all objects,
not just assemblies.

Proposition 8
.

Any order-discrete object is discrete.

Proof 3.5
.

This follows from Proposition
6
and
[
15
]
, Corollary 3.2.20. The statement there carries over
to arbitrary realizability toposes.

Remark 9
.

One might expect the availability of a simpler proof of the proposition
above, i.e. a proof that does not rely on the characterization of the
(order-)discrete objects. However, note that although we have a morphism

\Sigma\to\nabla(2)
, it is not a regular epimorphism, so one cannot apply

[
4
]
, Lemma 2.2.

In
[
4
]
, it it shown that the class of objects orthogonal to an
object
A
satisfies some nice properties if
A
is well-supported and
internally projective. By Lemma
2
, the object
\Sigma
is internally projective. It is clearly well-supported, which gives us the following proposition:

Proposition 10
.

The order-discrete objects are closed under all existing limits, subobjects
and quotients in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
. Moreover, they form an exponential
ideal. Finally, they form a reflective subcategory of
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof 3.6
.

The first statement is true, because any collection of orthogonal objects is
closed under all existing limits (see
[
4
]
, p. 1). Similarly,
any collection of orthogonal objects forms an exponential ideal.
The other closure properties follow from Lemmas 2.3 and 2.8 of
[
4
]
, and Lemma
2
above. The final claim is proven as a
corollary right after Lemma 2.3 of
[
4
]
.

Remark 11
.

For comparison with the notion of homotopy to be defined below, we give a concrete representation of the order-discrete reflection of an arbitrary object
(X,\sim)
. It has the same underlying set
X
, and we let
[x\approx y]
consist of those coded (in the sense of
\mathcal{S}
)
(3n+1)
-tuples

4.
Arithmetic in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})

The order-discrete modest sets already make a disguised appearance in

[
5
]
. Herein, Lietz singles out a class of so-called
well-behaved modest sets. It is not hard to show that a modest set is
well-behaved if and only if it is order-discrete and moreover, it has the
join-property, which we define now.

{defi}

An assembly has the
join-property
if it is isomorphic to an
assembly
X
whose realizing sets are closed under binary joins, viz. if

U\in E_{X}(x)
and
V\in E_{X}(x)
, then
U\cup V\in E_{X}(x)
for all
x\in|X|
.

{exa}

The natural numbers object
N
has the join-property.

The following proposition is a very slight generalization of
[
5
]
, Theorem
2.3.

Proposition 12
.

Let
X
and
Y
be modest sets. If
X
has the join-property and
Y
is
order-discrete, then the following form of the Axiom of Choice holds in

\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
:

\forall x{:}X\exists y{:}Y\phi(x,y)\to\exists f{:}Y^{X}\forall x{:}X\phi(x,f(x)).

Proof 4.1
.

See the proof of
[
5
]
, Theorem 2.3.

We have already seen that the order-discrete objects form an exponential ideal
and are closed under binary products. In
[
5
]
, Theorem 2.2, it is
shown that this also holds for the modest objects that have the join-property.
Since
N
is order-discrete and has the join-property, it follows that the Axiom
of Choice holds for all finite types. Consequently, the following two principles

\displaystyle\forall f{:}N^{N}\exists x{:}N\phi(f,x)\to\forall f{:}N^{N}\exists xy{:}N\forall g{:}N^{N}(\overline{f}y=\overline{g}y\to\phi(g,x)),

where
\overline{f}y=\overline{g}y
is short for
\forall z{:}N(z<y\to f(z)=g(z))

(
Weak Continuity for Numbers
)

\displaystyle\forall F{:}N^{(N^{N})}\forall f{:}N^{N}\exists x{:}N\forall g{:}N^{N}(\overline{f}x=\overline{g}x\to F(f)=F(g))

(
Brouwer’s Principle
)

are equivalent (and false) in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
; c.f.
[
5
]
, Section 2.3.

4.1.
Second-order arithmetic in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})

We close this section by some brief considerations on second-order arithmetic in
realizability toposes and
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
in particular.

Lemma 13
.

Let
A
be any partial combinatory algebra. Write
\overline{n}
for the

n
-th Curry numeral in
A
and let
{\mathsf{p}}
be a pairing combinator in

A
with projections
{\mathsf{p}}_{0}
and
{\mathsf{p}}_{1}

The power object
{\mathcal{P}}(N)
of
the natural numbers object

in
{\sf RT}(A)
can be given as the object
(P,\approx)

where

is the set

P=\{\phi\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathcal{P}(A)\mid\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, if $a\in\phi(n)$, then ${\mathsf{p}}_{1}a=\overline{n}$}\}

\psi\approx\psi=[\forall n(\phi(n)\leftrightarrow\psi(n))].

Proof 4.2
.

Straightforward.

We now turn our attention to
\lnot\lnot
-stable subsets of
N
, viz. subsets

X
such that

\forall x(\lnot\lnot(x\in X)\to x\in X)

is true in

\mathsf{RT}(A)
. We will abbreviate this formula by

We formulate the following triviality as a proposition for comparison with

below, in the case of

.

Proposition 14
.

If

is nontrivial, then not every subset is
\lnot\lnot
-stable, i.e. the sentence
\forall X\textup{Stab}(X)
is not valid in
{\sf RT}(A)
.

Proof 4.3
.

Note that
\forall X\textup{Stab}(X)
implies
\forall p.\neg\neg p\to p
(where

is a variable of type
\Omega
), which fails in
{\sf RT}(A)
as soon as

has two disjoint nonempty subsets. If

is trivial then
{\sf RT}(A)={\rm Set}
, and
\forall X\textup{Stab}(X)
obviously holds.

By contrast, in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
we have the following result (substituting
\mathcal{S}
for
A
in the definition of
P
in Lemma
13
):

Proposition 15
.

For any
\alpha\in P
, we have a realizer of
\textup{Stab}(\alpha)
.
Hence, the sentence
\forall X\lnot\lnot\textup{Stab}(X)
is true in

\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})

Proof 4.4
.

Let
\alpha\in P
. Note that there is a function

such that

f_{\alpha}(n)\in\begin{cases}[n\in\alpha]&\text{if }[n\in\alpha]\neq\emptyset;\\
\{\emptyset\}&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Moreover, there is an element
F_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{S}
such that

F_{\alpha}\overline{n}=f_{\alpha}(n)
. Now note that
\lambda{xy}.F_{\alpha}x
realizes
\textup{Stab}(\alpha)
.

Corollary 16
.

First-order arithmetic in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
is the same as first-order arithmetic in Set.

Proof 4.5
.

Given a true first-order arithmetical sentence
\phi
, we have, by Proposition
15
, a realizer for
\forall x(\psi(x)\vee\neg\psi(x))
, for every subformula
\psi
of
\phi
. From this one constructs a realizer for
\phi
.

Just as for ordinary natural numbers, we write
\langle-,\!-\rangle
for a
definable coding of
N^{2}
to

in
{\sf RT}(A)
. The sentence
\forall X\exists Y(\textup{Stab}(Y)\land\forall x(x\in X\leftrightarrow\exists y\langle{y,x}\rangle\in Y))
is known as

. It holds in the Effective Topos (
[

]
, p. 127). Internally, it says that every

is covered by a stable subset of

.

Proposition 17
.

If
|A|>|\mathbb{N}|
, then Shanin’s Principle does not hold in

{\sf RT}(A)
.

Proof 4.6
.

Assume for the sake of contradiction that it does. Then we have a realizer

R\in\bigcap_{\alpha\in P}\bigcup_{\beta\in P}\llbracket\textup{Stab}(\beta)\land\forall x(x\in\alpha\leftrightarrow\exists y\langle y,x\rangle\in\beta)\rrbracket.

Let us write
R_{0}=\mathsf{p_{0}}R
and
R_{1}=\mathsf{p_{1}}R
.
For each
a\in A
, define the element
\alpha_{a}\in P
by:

\alpha_{a}(0)=\{\mathsf{p}a\overline{0}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{a}(n+1)=\emptyset\quad\text{for any }n\in\mathbb{N}.

For each
a\in A
, pick some
\beta_{a}\in P
such that
R\in\llbracket\textup{Stab}(\beta_{a})\land\forall x(x\in\alpha_{a}\leftrightarrow\exists y\langle y,x\rangle\in\beta_{a})\rrbracket
.

From
R_{1}
, we effectively obtain

such that for every
a\in A
, we have:

R^{\prime}(\mathsf{p}a\overline{0})\in[\langle m,0\rangle\in\beta_{a}]

for some

.
As
|A|>|\mathbb{N}|
, there must be two different

such that

R^{\prime}(\mathsf{p}a\overline{0})\in[\langle m,0\rangle\in\beta_{a}]\text{ and }R^{\prime}(\mathsf{p}a^{\prime}\overline{0})\in[\langle m,0\rangle\in\beta_{a^{\prime}}]

(
\ast
)

for the same

.

From

, we effectively obtain

witnessing the stability of

and

to get a

Finally, using

and

are different, so that
\alpha_{a}
and

are disjoint.

Corollary 18

Shanin’s Principle does not hold in
\mathsf{RT}(\mathcal{S})
and

\mathsf{RT}({\mathcal{K}}_{2})
(where

6.
A notion of homotopy for the order-discrete objects

In
[
14
]
, a notion of homotopy for the Effective Topos is given. This notion is tied up with the subcategory of discrete objects, in the following way: internally, for each object
X
, the “discrete reflection” of
X
is the set of path components of
X
. It is just natural to wonder whether this would also work for the order-discrete objects in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
.

Another interpretation of homotopy for the Effective Topos is in the paper
[
3
]
by Dan Frumin and Benno van den Berg. They build a very elegant framework on purely category-theoretic notions. The basic ingredients are: an interval object
\mathbb{I}
with two distinct points
\partial_{0},\partial_{1}:1\to\mathbb{I}
, binary maps
\wedge,\vee:I\times I\to I
satisfying some axioms, and a dominance
\Sigma
which is closed under finite unions and satisfies the condition that the inclusion
[\partial_{0},\partial_{1}]:1+1\to\mathbb{I}
is a
\Sigma
-subobject. From these notions, a subcategory of “fibrant objects” is defined, and there is a Quillen model structure on this subcategory. When applying to the Effective Topos they take the trivial dominance:
\Omega
itself.

In
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
we have an interesting dominance: the Sierpiński object. However, the Frumin-van den Berg framework is not applicable for this dominance, as the following proposition shows.

Proposition 27
.

The Sierpiński object, as subobject of
\Omega
, is not closed under finite unions.

Proof 6.1
.

We apply the characterization
22
. Let the variables
X,Y,Z
range over the object of realizers
S
(which in turn is seen as subobject of
{\mathcal{P}}(N)
). We prove that the statement

\forall XY\exists Z(1\in Z\leftrightarrow 1\in X\vee 1\in Y)

(
\ast
)

is not valid in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
. Indeed, working out the realizability of
(\ast)
, we see that we need continuous functions
F
and
G
on
\mathcal{S}
such that for all
U,V\in{\mathcal{S}}
we have:

•

If
1\in U
or
1\in V
then
1\in F(U,V)
.

•

If
1\in F(U,V)
then either
G(F(U,V))=\overline{0}
and
1\in U
, or
G(F(U,V))=\overline{1}
and
1\in V
.

However, this is impossible: consider
G(F(\{1\},\{1\}))
. If this is equal to
\overline{0}
then by continuity we cannot have
G(F(\emptyset,\{1\}))=\overline{1}
; the other case is similar.

Caveat: note that if we interpret Escardó’s “synthetic topology” in
{\sf RT}({\mathcal{S}})
, we see from Proposition

that this topology will not be standard, but “substandard” in the terminology of
[
2

In view of Proposition

(which essentially is an iterated pushout of copies of
\nabla(2)

{defi}

\begin{array}[]{lcl}E_{n,\sigma}(0)&=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{{\sf p}\overline{0}\,\overline{1},{\sf p}\overline{1}\emptyset\}&\text{if }\sigma_{0}=0;\\
