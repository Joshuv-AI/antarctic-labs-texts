Disorder Chaos in Short-Range, Diluted, and Lévy Spin Glasses
Wei-Kuo Chen, Heejune Kim, Arnab Sen

Abstract
In a recent breakthrough [ 12 ] , Chatterjee proved site disorder chaos in the Edwards-Anderson (EA) short-range spin glass model [ 29 ] utilizing the Hermite spectral method. In this paper, we demonstrate the further usefulness of this Hermite spectral approach by extending the validity of site disorder chaos in three related spin glass models. The first, called the mixed even p p -spin short-range model, is a generalization of the EA model where the underlying graph is a deterministic bounded degree hypergraph consisting of hyperedges with even number of vertices. The second model is the diluted mixed p p -spin model, which is allowed to have hyperedges with both odd and even number of vertices. For both models, our results hold under general symmetric disorder distributions. The main novelty of our argument is played by an elementary algebraic equation for the Fourier-Hermite series coefficients for the two-spin correlation functions. It allows us to deduce necessary geometric conditions to determine the contributing coefficients in the overlap function, which in spirit is the same as the crucial Lemma 1 in [ 12 ] . Finally, we also establish disorder chaos in the Lévy model with stable index α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in(1,2) .



1
Introduction and Main Results

The study of chaos in spin glasses began from
[
7
,
32
]
, which is concerned with the sensitivity of the systems subject to a small change in some external parameters, such as the temperature, disorder, or external field.
One typical way to capture chaotic phenomenon is to measure the site overlap between two configurations independently drawn from an unperturbed and a perturbed system, respectively. The expectation is that with the perturbation, the site overlap is concentrated near zero if the model is symmetric with respect to the sign change of the spin configuration and is concentrated near a non-zero value if the symmetry is broken, for example, due to the presence of the external field.
This is in sharp contrast to the “lack of self-averaging” behavior of the site overlap in the low temperature phase of many models if the two spin configurations are independently sampled from the same system (see, e.g., Example 1 in
[
41
]
). For a modern survey of chaos in spin glasses in the physics literature, see
[
44
]
.

Establishing disorder chaos in the Edwards-Anderson (EA) short-range spin glass model
[
29
]
has received great attention recently.
In
[
8
]
, Chatterjee established a weak form of disorder chaos for the site overlap in the sense that it is concentrated around its Gibbs average; an alternative proof was given in Chen-Panchenko
[
22
]
. In addition to the site overlap, which aims to capture chaos through spin interactions on vertices, the bond overlap that measures chaos through the spin interactions on edges is another quantity of interest and it was shown in
[
8
]
that the expectation of the bond overlap is strictly positive. In a related direction, Arguin-Newman-Stein
[
3
]
made a connection between bond disorder chaos and incongruent ground states, and Arguin-Hanson
[
2
]
reproved positivity of the expectation of the bond overlap in
[
8
]
in three different perspectives.

In a recent breakthrough, chaos in disorder for the site overlap at zero temperature in the EA model was settled by Chatterjee
[
12
]
through a beautiful adaptation of the Hermite spectral method. The main ingredient of his proof is a quantitative version,
[
12
, Theorem 2]
, of a statement regarding an expected glassy behavior: “relative orientations of spins with large separation are sensitive to small changes in the bond strengths”, quoted from Bray-Moore
[
7
]
. Following the validity of the site disorder chaos, a number of long-standing conjectured spin glass behaviors were answered quantitatively.

In the present paper, we aim to investigate chaos in disorder in three spin glass models. The first, called the mixed
p
-spin short-range model, is a generalization of the EA model, whose Hamiltonian is defined on a mixture of hypergraphs of varying lengths
p
and the spin interactions are placed along the hyperedges. The second model called the diluted mixed
p
-spin model is a randomization of the first model, where each
p
-hyperedge is chosen independently with the same success probability. We assume that the disorder distribution is symmetric around zero. Therefore, it can always be written as
\rho(J)
for an odd measurable function
\rho
and
J\sim N(0,1)
. For any
t>0,
we perturb the disorder in the original systems in continuous and discrete manners, namely,

\rho(e^{-t}J+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}J^{\prime})

and

B\rho(J)+(1-B)\rho(J^{\prime})=\rho(BJ+(1-B)J^{\prime}),

where
J^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
J
and
B
is Bernoulli with head probability
e^{-t}
independent of
J,J^{\prime}.
Under these two perturbations, we establish chaos in disorder for the site overlap in the mixed
even

p
-spin short-range model that generalizes the earlier result
[
12
]
in the EA model. Additionally, we show that the same holds in the diluted mixed
p
-spin model,
which was one of the major open problems in the class of mean-field models and to the best of our knowledge, it was previously known only for the diluted even
p
-spin model with large connectivity, see Chen-Panchenko
[
24
]
. Our main result in this direction holds without this constraint and most importantly, it works for any mixture including odd
p
.

Our approach to establishing site disorder chaos in the two aforementioned models is based on the Hermite spectral method; the key ingredient is played by an elementary algebraic equation stated in Lemma
2.2
for the Fourier-Hermite series coefficients associated with the two-point spin correlation function, which allows us to deduce certain geometric conditions, see Propositions
2.4
and
2.5
, to identify the nonzero (contributing) Fourier coefficients. These propositions are similar in spirit to
[
12
, Lemma 1]
. It was derived in the EA model through flipping the signs of the spins as well as the disorders in the Hamiltonian depending on the graph structure associated with the given two vertices heavily relying on the fact that the EA model in the absence of an external field is symmetric. However, our algebraic Lemma
2.2
does not depend too much on the geometry of the underlying graph. As such, it substantially broadens the scope of applicability of the Hermite spectral method in deducing disorder chaos in a wider range of spin glass models including diluted mixed
p
-spin models.

The third model of interest is the Lévy spin glass initially introduced by Cizeau-Bouchaud
[
26
,
27
]
. Its Hamiltonian is essentially the same as the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model except that its disorder possesses a power-law density of order
1<\alpha<2
so that it only has
\alpha^{\prime}
-moment for any
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha.
Due to this formulation, it is expected that only the edges with heavy weights will be responsible for the behavior of the system and as a result, the Lévy model should behave like diluted models in many aspects, see
[
26
]
for physicists’ predictions. Indeed, it is the case that one can relate the Lévy model to the diluted
2
-spin model to understand its free energy and fluctuation, see Chen-Kim-Sen
[
18
]
. It is then an intriguing question to ask whether chaos in disorder holds in the Lévy model under the same mechanism as the diluted model or not. To this end, we let the disorder be an arbitrary symmetric heavy-tailed random variable and write the disorder as
\rho(J)
for some odd function
\rho
and again
J\sim N(0,1)
such that
\rho(J)
belongs to the domain of attraction of the symmetric stable distribution of index
\alpha
. Under the continuous perturbation as before, we show that the Lévy model exhibits disorder chaos. Somewhat unexpectedly, while one can understand the free energy in the Lévy model via the diluted model, it is by no means clear if one can use our chaos result for the diluted model to understand the same objective in the Lévy model as the Gibbs measures are much more sensitive subject to small perturbation. As we shall see, our proof makes use of the Hermite spectral method again, but instead of counting the nonzero Fourier-Hermite coefficients under the same geometric conditions as before, it is in fact more useful to control the overlap through the treatment in the SK model, see
[
10
,
19
]
.

1.1
Mixed Even
p
-spin short-range Model

Let
\Delta\geq 2
. For
2\leq p\leq\Delta,
denote by
{[N]\choose p}
the collection of all choices of
p
distinct elements from
[N]
. Let
G=([N],E)
be a hypergraph with a mixture of hyperedges

E=\bigcup_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}E_{p},

(1.1)

for some
E_{p}\subseteq{[N]\choose p}
.
An ordinary graph would correspond to
\Delta=2
, which is the case of
the EA model.
A
ball of radius
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
centered at
v\in[N]
is defined in the usual way as
B_{r}(v)=\{v^{\prime}\in[N]:d(v,v^{\prime})\leq r\}
, where
d(v,v^{\prime})
is the hypergraph distance between the vertices
v
and
v^{\prime}
defined in Section
2.1
.
We assume one of the following growth assumptions on
G
.

Assumption 1.1

(Volume growth assumptions on
G
)
.

(i)

(Polynomial growth) For some positive constants
A
and
\theta
independent of
N
,

\max_{i\in[N]}|B_{r}(i)|\leq Ar^{\theta},\ \ r\in\mathbb{N}.

(ii)

(Exponential growth) For some constants
A
and
\gamma>1
independent of
N
,

\max_{i\in[N]}|B_{r}(i)|\leq A\gamma^{r},\ \ r\in\mathbb{N}.

For each hyperedge
e\in E
, let
J_{e}
be an i.i.d. copy from the standard normal distribution.
For each
2\leq p\leq\Delta
,
let
\rho_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be an odd function.
With the notation

\sigma_{e}=\prod_{i\in V(e)}\sigma_{i},\quad\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},

(1.2)

we consider the Hamiltonian

H_{J}(\sigma)=\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}H_{J}^{(p)}(\sigma),\quad\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},

(1.3)

where

H_{J}^{(p)}(\sigma):=\sum_{e\in E_{p}}\rho_{p}(J_{e})\sigma_{e}

for
2\leq p\leq\Delta
.
Note that we can represent any symmetric disorder as
\rho(J)
.
The
Gibbs measure
associated to (any) Hamiltonian
H_{J}
at inverse temperature
\beta>0
is given by

G_{J,\beta}(\sigma)=\frac{e^{\beta H_{J}(\sigma)}}{Z_{N}},\quad\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},

(1.4)

where
Z_{N}=Z_{N}(J,\beta)=\sum_{\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}}\exp(\beta H_{J}(\sigma))
is the normalizing factor called the
partition function
. Denote by
(\sigma^{l})_{l\geq 1}
i.i.d. samples (replicas) from
G_{J,\beta}^{\infty}
and the expectation with respect to these randomness by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{J,\beta}.

Note that, when
\beta=\infty
,
G_{J,\beta}
is a uniform measure supported on the set of
ground states
, which are spin configurations achieving the maximum value of
H_{J}
given the disorder
J
. In this case, we shall understand
(\sigma^{l})_{l\geq 1}
as i.i.d. copies that are sampled uniformly at random from the set of ground states.
We often omit the notation for
J
and
\beta
to enhance brevity.
Given two spin configurations
\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, we define their overlap to be

R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}\sigma^{1}_{i}\sigma^{2}_{i}.

Throughout the article, we consider either one of the following perturbations of the disorder
\rho(J)
.

Definition 1.2

(Two types of perturbations)
.

(i)

For
t\geq 0
,
J(t)=e^{-t}J+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}J^{\prime}
, where
J^{\prime}
is an indepdent copy of
J
.

(ii)

For
t\geq 0
,
J(t)=BJ+(1-B)J^{\prime}
where
B\sim\mathrm{Ber}(e^{-t})
,
J^{\prime}\sim J
, and all the random variables are independent.

We call the perturbation in (i) as a
continuous perturbation
and the one in (ii) as a
discrete perturbation
.

In both types of perturbation, larger
t\geq 0
corresponds to more perturbation.
The terminology is obvious because, in the first type, we are continuously deforming the disorder, which contrasts with the discrete replacement of the disorder with an i.i.d. copy in the second type.
The continuous perturbation is natural when considering Gaussian disorders in view of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, see (
2.2
).
The discrete one generally applies to any disorders, and it is the one often considered in the noise-sensitivity literature (see, e.g.,
[
36
]
). We also comment that we may naturally associate the discrete perturbation with a Markov process called the independent flip process, a term coined by Chatterjee, see
[
10
, Chapter 7]
.

As mentioned in the introduction, our focus lies in understanding the sensitivity of our models subject to the perturbation under Definition
1.2
.
To make this notion precise, we consider the overlap
R(\sigma,\tau)
where
(\sigma,\tau)
is sampled from the product measure
G_{J}\otimes G_{J(t)}
.
We always sample
(\sigma^{l},\tau^{l})_{l\geq 1}
from the product measure
(G_{J}\otimes G_{J(t)})^{\otimes\infty}
.
The Gibbs average in this context will be denoted by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t}
to emphasize that
\tau
is drawn from the perturbed Gibbs measure
G_{J(t)}
.
Note that these are only defined for the positive temperature case (
\beta<\infty
), and we understand the zero temperature case (
\beta=\infty
) as
\sigma
and
\tau
being independently sampled uniformly at random from the set of ground states of the Hamiltonian
H_{J}
and
H_{J(t)}
, respectively.

Our first main result focuses on the mixed even
p
-spin short-range model, i.e., all
p
’s are restricted to even values in (

).

Theorem 1.3
.

Consider the mixed even

-spin short-range model with the Hamiltonian (

). Let

and

. Then, for both types of perturbations and for any

, we have

\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\leq\frac{1}{N}\max_{i\in[N]}|B_{r}(i)|+e^{-tr}.

(1.5)

In particular, we can specialize into the following cases.

1.

Under Assumption
1.1
(i), for any
t>0
and
\beta\in[0,\infty]
,

\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\leq\frac{1}{N}+\frac{C}{Nt^{\theta}}

for some constant

and

.

2.

Under Assumption

(ii), for any

Remark 1.4
.

1.

It is an easy fact (see, e.g., Lemma
2.1
) that
t\mapsto\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}
is non-increasing. Hence, the overlap bounds given in
Theorem
1.3
are more interesting for small
t
, that is, when the amount to perturbations to disorders is small. In the polynomial growth case, our result says that
\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\to 0
as soon as
t\gg N^{-1/\theta}
(independent of
\beta)
. For
\beta=\infty
, this matches with the result obtained in
[
12
]
for the ground state of the Edwards-Anderson model on finite boxes of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. On the other hand, for the exponential growth case, our result implies that
\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\to 0
as soon as
t\gg(\log N)^{-1},
again independent of
\beta
.

2.

It is possible to establish crude lower bounds on the overlap when

is small enough.
Under the discrete perturbation, Equation (

) implies
\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\geq c\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})^{2}\rangle
for some constant
c>0
, independent of
\beta\in[0,\infty]
, whenever
t\leq|E|^{-1}.

This holds without any restrictions on the hyperedge set
E
, and, in particular, for dense graphs as well.
Under the continuous perturbation, we can show that
t\leq|E|^{-5/2-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
guarantees a similar lower bound with further regularity assumptions on the functions
(\rho_{p})_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}
, though this bound appears to be non-optimal.
Specializing into the canonical case of Gaussian disorders, we can find an alternative bound
t\leq 12^{-2}\beta^{-1}|E|^{-3/2}(\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})^{2}\rangle)^{2}
which has a better exponent in

but depends on
\beta
, implies a similar lower bound on the perturbed overlap.
We defer the precise statements and proofs of these inequalities to the appendix.

1.2
Diluted Mixed
p
-spin Model

Fix
\Delta\geq 2
. For each
2\leq p\leq\Delta
, each hyperedge from
{[N]\choose p}
is selected independently with probability

\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}

for some
\alpha_{p}>0
, and let
E_{p}
be the collection of chosen hyperedges. Consider the random hypergraph
([N],E)
for
E:=\bigcup_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}E_{p}
.
The Hamiltonian is the same as before, namely (
1.3
), where the disorder is independent of the randomness of the edge set.
While we considered deterministic graphs in the mixed even
p
-spin short-range model described in Theorem
1.3
, the randomness of the graphs in the diluted mixed
p
-spin model aids in removing the restriction on the edges being even, as will be apparent in Proposition
2.5
and Lemma
4.4
below.

Let us fix the notation

\lambda\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}p(p-1)\alpha_{p}.

Theorem 1.5
.

Consider the diluted mixed
p
-spin model. Let
t\geq 0
and
\beta\in[0,\infty]
. Assume
\lambda>1
. For both types of perturbations, there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
(\alpha_{p})_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}
such that

\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\leq\frac{C}{N^{t/(t+2\log\lambda)}}.

Remark 1.6
.

1.

Site disorder chaos for a version of the diluted model was studied earlier in Chen-Panchenko
[
24
]
, where the result was limited only to the pure even
p
spin model with very large
\alpha_{p}
so that it is close to the mean-field model. Our result here holds without these restrictions.

2.

The parameter
\lambda
is the average vertex degree of the underlying random hypergraph. It is known
[
45
]
that the critical threshold is
\lambda=1
. The case
\lambda>1
corresponds to the supercritical regime when there is a giant (linear-sized) component with high probability. On the other hand, if
\lambda<1
, the hypergraph is subcritical, that is, all connected components have at most logarithmic size with high probability, which renders the diluted model less interesting in this regime.

3.

For the diluted pure
2
-spin model with Gaussian disorder, for any
\beta>0
, as
N\to\infty
, the unperturbed overlap
R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})
has a trivial distribution at zero in the annealed region
\alpha_{2}\leq 1/2
(see
[
37
]
). On the other hand, if
\alpha_{2}>1/2
, it is expected that there exists some critical temperature such that below the criticality, the limiting overlap has a non-trivial distribution. In sharp contrast to this nontriviality, the result in Theorem
1.5
indicates that the overlap is trivialized at any temperature as long as the perturbation is in force.

4.

We can view
\lambda
as the bound on the (average) growth rate for the underlying random hypergraph of the diluted model (see Lemma
4.2
). However, let us emphasize that Theorem
1.5
does not assume that hyperedges contain an even number of vertices. We circumvent the issue of the odd hyperedges by exploiting the fact that random hypergraphs are locally tree-like. However, in the implementation of the argument, we lose a multiplicative factor of
2
in front of
\log\lambda
compared to the bound (
1.6
) obtained in the deterministic case with no odd hyperedges.

1.3
Lévy Model

Let
1<\alpha<2.

Let
X
be a symmetric random variable with tail probability

\mathbb{P}(|X|>x)=\frac{L(x)}{x^{\alpha}},\ \forall x>0,

where
L:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a slowly varying function at
\infty
.
It is well-known that for any
p<\alpha
,

\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}<\infty.

(1.7)

Potter’s bound says that for any
\eta>0
, there exists
y_{0}>0
such that for all
y\geq y_{0}
and
s\geq 1
,

\displaystyle(1-\eta)s^{-\eta}<\frac{L(sy)}{L(y)}<(1+\eta)s^{\eta}.

(1.8)

In particular, (
1.8
) dictates the behavior of a slowly varying function as
s\to\infty
.
Suppose that
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-decreasing function such that

\rho(J)
has the same distribution as
X
for
J\sim N(0,1).

Let
(J_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
be i.i.d. copies of
J.

Define the Hamiltonian of the Lévy model as

H_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{a_{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\rho(J_{ij})\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},\quad\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},

where
a_{N}=\inf\{x>0:\mathbb{P}(|X|>x)\leq 1/N\}.

It is a standard result, e.g.,
[
6
]
, in the study of heavy-tailed random variables that

\displaystyle a_{N}=N^{1/\alpha}\ell(N)

(1.9)

for some slowly varying function
\ell
at
\infty
.

The Gibbs measure for the Lévy model is defined in the same way as (
1.4
) with the above Hamiltonian.

Theorem 1.7
.

Consider the Lévy model with
1<\alpha<2
and the continuous perturbation.
For any
0\leq\beta<\infty
and
0<\varepsilon<2/\alpha-1
, there exist two constants
c=c(\beta)>0
and
K=K(\beta,\varepsilon)>0
such that for any
t\geq 0
and
N\geq 1,

\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}\leq\frac{K}{N^{(\frac{2}{\alpha}-1-\varepsilon)\min(1,ct)}}.

Remark 1.8
.

As in the third item of Remark
1.6
, the nearly zero behavior of the overlap in Theorem
1.7
is a sharp transition as opposed to the unperturbed overlap
R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})
in the Lévy model, for which it is expected to have a nontrivial distribution as long as
\beta
is large enough, see

[
26
,
27
]
.

1.4
Related Results and Applications

In addition to the short-range and graphical models, there has been vast mathematical progress on chaos in mean-field models in recent years. First of all, chaos in disorder in the SK model as well as its variant, the mixed even
p
-spin model, was pioneered by Chatterjee
[
9
]
(among many other results) in the absence of external field. Later on, disorder chaos was extended by Chen
[
13
,
15
]
in the presence of the external field and was pushed forward to the zero temperature setting by Chen-Handschy-Lerman
[
16
]
.
Some efforts (e.g.
[
15
,
21
]
) were made to go beyond the mixed even
p
-spin model and allow odd interactions, which culminated in Eldan’s
[
31
]
proof in the general mixed
p
-spin model.
On the other hand, for the same class of models, if it was assumed that the mixture of the
p
-spin Hamiltonians is
generic
, a form of chaos in temperature was achieved by Chen-Panchenko
[
21
]
and Chen
[
14
]
and the canonical case was settled by Panchenko
[
42
]
. Interestingly, in the spherical setting, while chaos in disorder or external field remains valid, see, Chen et al.
[
17
]
, Chen-Sen
[
25
]
, and Panchenko-Talagrand
[
43
]
, the validity of chaos in temperature depends on the mixture of the
p
-spin Hamiltonians; when the model consisted of a pure
p
-spin Hamiltonian, Subag
[
46
]
showed that chaos in temperature is absent, whereas if one couples the pure
p
-spin model with a perturbative
q
-spin Hamiltonian for
q\neq p
, Chen-Panchenko
[
23
]
and Ben Arous-Subag-Zeitouni
[
4
]
showed that temperature chaos indeed holds. Notably, the results mentioned here all assumed that the disorders are Gaussian. The works, Auffinger-Chen
[
5
]
and Chen-Lam
[
20
]
, addressed the universality of chaos for general settings of the disorders under matching Gaussian moment assumptions.

While the aforementioned works (and the current paper) measure chaos in disorder by the chaotic rearrangement of the overlap, it is also possible to formalize chaos in disorder in terms of other distances between the perturbed and unperturbed Gibbs measures. For example, El Alaoui-Montanari-Sellke
[
30
]
proposed using the (normalized) 2-Wasserstein distance as a stronger notion than the overlap-based approach and showed that there is disorder chaos for the SK model in the low-temperature regime with the Wasserstein distance, which poses a barrier to stable sampling algorithms. In a subsequent work
[
1
]
, the same authors demonstrated the shattering phenomenon in pure
p
-spherical spin glasses for large enough
p
and high temperatures, which again implies disorder chaos in the Wasserstein distance. On the other hand, shattering and the overlap gap property in the Ising pure
p
-spin case in the high-temperature regime were investigated by Gamarnik-Jagannath-Kızıldağ
[
34
]
, again for large enough
p
by making a connection to the random energy model.

Finally, we close this subsection by mentioning that the overlap gap and disorder chaos properties have been shown to be the main features that result in computational barriers for stable algorithms to achieve near-ground states in a variety of random optimization problems, including the approximate message passing, low-degree polynomial, and Lipschitz algorithms (see
[
33
,
35
,
38
,
39
]
and references therein).

1.5
Open Problems

1.

For any deterministic sequence of 3-uniform hypergraphs with either a polynomial or exponential growing number of vertices, does site disorder chaos hold in the corresponding short-range model?

2.

While the definition of discrete perturbation is non-ambiguous for general disorder distribution, there is more than one way to introduce continuous perturbations to disorders. For example,
let us consider the case when the disorders have a density
e^{-V(x)}
for some nice (say, continuously differentiable and convex) function
V
. Let
X
be drawn from this distribution and let
X(t)
be the solution to the stochastic differential equation,

X(t)=-V^{\prime}(X(t))dt+\sqrt{2}{dB(t)},\ \ X(0)=X,

where
(B(t))_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Brownian motion independent of
X
.
If
p(t,\cdot)
is the density of

, then

satisfies the associated Fokker-Planck equation

\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigl(V^{\prime}(x)p(t,x)+\frac{\partial p(t,x)}{\partial x}\Bigr),\ \ p(0,x)=e^{-V(x)}.

Note that
X(0)=X,X(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X
for all

is independent of

. Therefore, we can take

as another candidate for continuous perturbation of

. In the case

is known as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process and it can be solved as

Consequently,
(X,X(t))
corresponds to our continuous perturbation
(J,J(t)).
It would be interesting to see if chaos in disorder can be studied beyond this case by Fourier analysis involving orthonormal polynomials in
L^{2}(e^{-V(x)}dx)
.

3.

For stable index
\alpha>1

establishes disorder chaos in the Lévy model under the continuous perturbation. Prove disorder chaos for the discrete perturbation in the same regime of

2.1
Hypergraphs

For
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, a
Berge path of length
\ell
between
v_{1}
and
v_{\ell+1}
is a sequence
(v_{1},e_{1},v_{2},e_{2},\dots,v_{\ell},e_{\ell},v_{\ell+1})
of distinct
\ell+1
vertices and distinct
\ell
hyperedges such that
\{v_{k},v_{k+1}\}\subseteq V(e_{k})
for
k=1,\dots,\ell
.
We define the
distance

d:[N]\times[N]\to\mathbb{R}
between two vertices as the minimum length among all possible Berge paths connecting those vertices, provided they are distinct; the distance is defined as
0
if the vertices are identical.
We assign
\infty
if there are no paths connecting two distinct vertices.
A (closed)
ball of radius
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
centered at
v\in[N]
is defined in the usual way as
B_{r}(v)=\{v^{\prime}\in[N]:d(v,v^{\prime})\leq r\}
.
Two vertices are considered
connected
if there is a Berge path between them, and this establishes a partition of
[N]
into connected components.
A
Berge cycle of length
\ell
is a sequence
(v_{1},e_{1},v_{2},e_{2},\dots,v_{\ell},e_{\ell},v_{\ell+1})
where
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
are distinct vertices,
e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell}
are distinct edges, and
\{v_{k},v_{k+1}\}\subseteq V(e_{k})
for
k=1,\dots,\ell
with
v_{\ell+1}=v_{1}
.
A
hypertree
is a connected hypergraph without Berge cycles.
For brevity, a path and a cycle will always mean a Berge path and a Berge cycle, respectively.

For a subset
B\subseteq[N]
, we define its interior and the edge boundary as

\mathring{B}=\{e\in E:V(e)\subseteq B\}\text{ and }\partial B=\{e\in E:0<|V(e)\cap B|<|V(e)|\}.

Clearly, the edge set has the partition
E=\mathring{B}\cup\partial B\cup\{e\in E:|V(e)\cap B|=0\}
.

Denote
\mathbb{Z}_{+}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
For
n=(n_{e})_{e\in E}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}
, we define

E(n)=\{e\in E:n_{e}>0\}\text{ and }V(n)=\{v\in[N]:v\in V(e)\text{ for some }e\in E(n)\}.

Let
G(n)=(V(n),E(n))
be the associated sub-hypergraph of
G
.
Denote the connected component of
v\in V(n)
in
G(n)
as
C(v,n)
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}
.

2.2
Hermite Polynomial Expansion

Let
(h_{m})_{m\geq 0}
be the orthonormal basis of normalized Hermite polynomials for
L^{2}(\mu)
where
\mu
is the standard Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}
and
h_{0}\equiv 1
. Then, products of the form
h_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\prod_{e\in E}h_{n_{e}}(x_{e})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
is a basis for
L^{2}(\mu^{\otimes E})
. For any
\phi\in L^{2}(\mu^{\otimes E})
, we have the Hermite polynomial expansion

\phi(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}\hat{\phi}(n)h_{n}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{E},

(2.1)

where

\hat{\phi}(n)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\mathbb{E}(\phi(J)h_{n}(J)).

We use the notation
|n|\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sum_{e\in E}n_{e}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
t\geq 0
. For any
\phi\in L^{2}(\mu^{\otimes E})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\phi(J)\phi(J(t))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}e^{-|n|t}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2},

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl(\phi(J)\phi(J(t))\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}e^{-|E(n)|t}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}

(2.3)

for the continuous and discrete perturbations, respectively.

Proof.

Our proof relies on the following two equations:
for the continuous perturbation,
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E},
we have

\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(J(t))|J)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}e^{-|n|t}h_{n}(J),

(2.4)

whereas for the discrete perturbation,

\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(J(t))|J)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}e^{-|E(n)|t}h_{n}(J).

(2.5)

Equation (
2.4
) is a standard result of the OU process (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
).
Equation (
2.5
) can be obtained by taking expectation in the Bernoulli randomness.

Now, let
t\geq 0
and consider the continuous perturbation.
From (
2.1
) and (
2.4
), it holds that

\mathbb{E}(\phi(J(t))|J)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}\hat{\phi}(n)e^{-|n|t}h_{n}(J).

Since
\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(J)h_{m}(J))=\mathbbm{1}_{n=m}
for
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}
, we have

\mathbb{E}(\phi(J)\phi(J(t)))=\mathbb{E}(\phi(J)\mathbb{E}(\phi(J(t))|J))=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}e^{-|n|t}.

The case of discrete perturbation can similarly be treated using (
2.5
).

∎

Recall the notation
\eqref{eq: product notation}.

For any
a,b\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, we define the tensors
{\bf a}=(a_{e})_{e\in E}
,
{\bf b}=(b_{e})_{e\in E}
, and define their Hadamard product
{\bf a}\circ{\bf b}=(a_{e}b_{e})_{e\in E}

We denote the Gibbs average of the disorder
(a_{e}J_{e})_{e\in E}\mathrel{{=}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}}{\bf a}\circ J

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the generalized short-range model with Hamiltonian in (
1.3
) with
\beta\in[0,\infty)
.
For any
a\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
,

, and

, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\bigl(h_{n}(J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle\bigr)=I_{n}(a)\mathbb{E}\bigl(h_{n}(J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle\bigr),

where

Remark 2.3
.

It is immediate from the lemma that
\mathbb{E}(h_{n}(J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle)=0
if
I_{n}(a)=-1
for some choice of
a\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
. However, the following example demonstrates that the converse is not true, even for a connected graph (
p=2
).

Let
G=(V,E)=([4],\{\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,4\}\})
. Let
\phi=\langle\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle
. Then one can check that
\phi
does not depend on
J_{\{1,3\}},J_{\{2,4\}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\sigma}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,2\}})\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,3\}})\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3})(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{2,4\}})\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4})}{\sum_{\sigma}(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,2\}})\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,3\}})\sigma_{1}\sigma_{3})(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{2,4\}})\sigma_{2}\sigma_{4})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,2\}})\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})}{\sum_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}(1+\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,2\}})\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2})}=\tanh(\beta J_{\{1,2\}}).

Now let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}
with
n_{\{1,2\}}=1,

n_{\{1,3\}}=2,
and
n_{\{2,4\}}=0
.
It follows that

\hat{\phi}(n)=\mathbb{E}[\phi h_{1}(J_{\{1,2\}})h_{2}(J_{\{1,3\}})]=\mathbb{E}[\phi h_{1}(J_{\{1,2\}})]\mathbb{E}[h_{2}(J_{\{1,3\}})]=0,

since
\mathbb{E}[h_{2}(J_{\{1,3\}})]=0
. On the other hand,

I_{n}(a)=a_{1}a_{2}(a_{1}a_{2})(a_{1}a_{3})^{2}=1\ \text{ for all }a\in\{\pm 1\}^{4}.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Note that for any deterministic
a\in\{-1,1\}^{N},
since
\rho_{p}
’s are odd functions, we can write

H_{J}({\bf a}\circ\sigma)=\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}\sum_{e\in E_{p}}\rho_{p}(J_{e})a_{e}\sigma_{e}=\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}\sum_{e\in E_{p}}\rho_{p}(a_{e}J_{e})\sigma_{e}=H_{{\bf a}\circ J}(\sigma)

so that
\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{J}=a_{i}a_{j}\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{{\bf a}\circ J}.

From this and the fact that
{\bf a}\circ{\bf a}\circ J=J
,

\mathbb{E}[h_{n}(J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{J}]=a_{i}a_{j}\mathbb{E}[h_{n}({\bf a}\circ{\bf a}\circ J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{{\bf a}\circ J})]=a_{i}a_{j}\mathbb{E}[h_{n}({\bf a}\circ J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{J}],

where the second equality used the obvious fact that
{\bf a}\circ J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}J
.
Finally, using
h_{n}({\bf a}\circ J)=\prod_{e\in E}h_{n_{e}}(a_{e}J_{e})=\prod_{e\in E}a_{e}^{n_{e}}h_{n_{e}}(J_{e})=\prod_{e\in E}a_{e}^{n_{e}}\cdot h_{n}(J)=\prod_{e\in E(n)}a_{e}^{n_{e}}\cdot h_{n}(J)
, we have

\mathbb{E}h_{n}(J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{J}=a_{i}a_{j}\prod_{e\in E(n)}a_{e}^{n_{e}}\cdot\mathbb{E}h_{n}(J)\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle_{J}.

∎

Now, for any
i,j\in[N]
, define

which is a bounded measurable function of the disorder
J

The remainder of the proof follows as in Theorem

4.1
Exploration Process

We first describe the exploration process on the random hypergraph which starts from a single vertex and conducts a breadth-first search on its connected component. Fix a vertex
i\in[N]
.
We define the
exploration process

(R_{t},I_{t},S_{t},E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\in[N]^{3}\times E
inductively as follows.
Put
R_{0}=\emptyset
,
I_{0}=\{i\}
,
S_{0}=[N]\setminus\{i\}
, and
E_{0}=\emptyset
.
Given
\mathcal{F}_{t}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\sigma(R_{s},I_{s},S_{s},E_{s})_{0\leq s\leq t}
, we define

\displaystyle R_{t+1}

\displaystyle=R_{t}\cup I_{t},

\displaystyle E_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\{e\in E:V(e)\cap I_{t}\neq\emptyset,\ V(e)\cap R_{t}=\emptyset\},

\displaystyle I_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{e\in E_{t+1}}V(e)\cap S_{t},

\displaystyle S_{t+1}

\displaystyle=S_{t}\setminus I_{t+1}.

Heuristically, at step
t
,
R_{t}
represents the removed (or recovered) vertices,
I_{t}
represents the newly infected (or explored) vertices,
S_{t}
represents the susceptible (or unexplored) vertices.
E_{t}
represents the set of hyperedges that we ‘reveal’ during the step
t
of the algorithm. It consists of hyperedges with the property that they contain at least one vertex from
I_{t-1}
and all of their vertices belong to
I_{t-1}\cup S_{t-1}
.

Below we state some simple properties of the exploration process without a proof.
Here
\dot{\cup}
denotes the disjoint union.

Proposition 4.1

(Properties of exploration processes)
.

(i)

R_{t}=\dot{\bigcup}_{s<t}I_{s}
for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(ii)

R_{t}\dot{\cup}I_{t}\dot{\cup}S_{t}=[N]
for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

(iii)

V(e)\subseteq I_{t}\cup I_{t+1}
for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
e\in E_{t+1}
.

(iv)

\bigcup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}R_{t}=V(C)
and
\dot{\bigcup}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}E_{t}=E(C)
where
C
is the connected component of
i
.

(v)

B_{t}=R_{t+1}=\dot{\bigcup}_{s\leq t}I_{s}
for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where
B_{t}=B_{t}(i)
is the ball of radius
t
centered at
i
.

Recall
\lambda=\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}p(p-1)\alpha_{p}
. Let us also define
\lambda^{\prime}=\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}p(p-1)(p-2)\alpha_{p}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{t},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=t}^{2t}\lambda^{k}+\lambda^{\prime}\sum_{k=t-1}^{2(t-1)}\lambda^{k}.

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.
Observe that
v\in I_{t+1}
only if there is a
p
-hyperedge in
E_{t+1}
connecting
v
to some vertex in
I_{t}
for some
2\leq p\leq\Delta
, and similarly, for
v\neq v^{\prime}\in S_{t}
,
\{v,v^{\prime}\}\subseteq I_{t+1}
only if either there is a
p
-hyperedge in
E_{t+1}
connecting
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
to some vertex in
I_{t}
for some
2\leq p\leq\Delta
or there is a pair
(e_{1},e_{2})
of a
p_{1}
-hyperedge
e_{1}\in E_{t+1}
and a
p_{2}
-hyperedge
e_{2}\in E_{t+1}
for some
2\leq p_{1},p_{2}\leq\Delta
such that
v\in V(e_{1})\setminus V(e_{2})
and
v^{\prime}\in V(e_{2})\setminus V(e_{1})
.

Figure 2:
(Left panel):
v,v^{\prime}
belonging to the same hyperedge. (Right panel):
v,v^{\prime}
belonging to different hyperedges.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v\in I_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq q_{t},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(v,v^{\prime}\in I_{t+1}|\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq q^{\prime}_{t},

(4.1)

where

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1-\prod_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}\Bigl(1-\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}\Bigr)^{{N-2\choose p-2}|I_{t}|}

\displaystyle q^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}1-\prod_{3\leq p\leq\Delta}\Bigl(1-\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}\Bigr)^{{N-3\choose p-3}|I_{t}|}+\Bigl(1-\prod_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}\Bigl(1-\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}\Bigr)^{{N-3\choose p-2}|I_{t}|}\Bigr)^{2}.

Using the elementary bound
1-\prod_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}(1-x_{p})^{n_{p}}\leq\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}n_{p}x_{p}
for
n_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
x_{p}\in(0,1),
which follows from the convexity of the function
t\mapsto\prod_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}(1-tx_{p})^{n_{p}}
on the interval
(0,1)
,

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}{N-2\choose p-2}|I_{t}|=\frac{\lambda|I_{t}|}{N-1},

\displaystyle q^{\prime}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{3\leq p\leq\Delta}\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}{N-3\choose p-3}|I_{t}|+\Bigl(\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}{N-3\choose p-2}|I_{t}|\Bigr)^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda^{\prime}|I_{t}|}{(N-1)(N-2)}+\frac{\lambda^{2}|I_{t}|^{2}}{(N-1)^{2}}.

From the previous display and the trivial bound
|S_{t}|\leq N-1
, we have

\displaystyle q_{t}|S_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq\lambda|I_{t}|,

\displaystyle q_{t}^{\prime}|S_{t}|(|S_{t}|-1)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{\prime}|I_{t}|+\lambda^{2}|I_{t}|^{2}.

Now, since
|I_{t+1}|=\sum_{v\in S_{t}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{v\in I_{t+1}\}}
, (
4.1
) implies the recursions

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|I_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\leq q_{t}\mathbb{E}|S_{t}|\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|I_{t+1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq q_{t}\mathbb{E}|S_{t}|+q^{\prime}_{t}\mathbb{E}|S_{t}|(|S_{t}|-1)\leq(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime})\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|^{2}.

(4.3)

Equation (
4.2
) and the initial condition
|I_{0}|=1
readily implies
\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|\leq\lambda^{t}
.
Plugging this into (
4.3
), we have

\mathbb{E}|I_{t+1}|^{2}\leq(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime})\lambda^{t}+\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|^{2},

and an induction easily yields

\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|^{2}\leq(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime})\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\lambda^{t-1+k}+\lambda^{2t}.

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
\lambda>1
. Then there exists a constant

depending only on
(\alpha_{p})_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}
such that

Proof.

By Proposition
4.1
(v),
|B_{t}(i)|=\sum_{s=0}^{t}|I_{s}|
. The lemma now follows from the estimate of

in Lemma

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
\lambda>1
. Let
0<\varepsilon<1
and
\delta=(2\log\lambda)^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)
. We have

\mathbb{P}(\text{$B_{\lfloor\delta\log N\rfloor}(i)$ contains a Berge cycle})\leq CN^{-\varepsilon}

for some constant
C>0
depending only on
(\alpha_{p})_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}
.

Proof.

We run the exploration process
(R_{t},I_{t},S_{t},E_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
starting at the vertex
i
. Suppose there is no cycle until step
t-1
.
Because of (iii) of Proposition
4.1
, there are only two ways to create a potential cycle at step
t\geq 1

Case 1. A hyperedge
e\in E_{t}
contains two distinct vertices
v\neq v^{\prime}
in
I_{t-1}
.

Case 2. Two distinct hyperedges
e_{1}\neq e_{2}
in
E_{t}
have a common vertex
v
in
I_{t}
.

Figure 3:
Left panel depicts Case 1 and right panel depicts Case 2.

Let
A_{t}
and
D_{t}
denote the number of hyperedges corresponding to Case 1 and Case 2 respectively.
Given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
,
A_{t}
is stochastically dominated by
\sum_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}Y_{p}
, where
(Y_{p})_{2\leq p\leq\Delta}
are independent binomial random variables with distribution

Y_{p}\sim\text{Bin}\Biggl(|I_{t-1}|^{2}{N-2\choose p-2},\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}\Biggr),

so by linearity of expectation, we have

\mathbb{E}(A_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\sum_{p=2}^{\Delta}\mathbb{E}(Y_{p}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\sum_{p=2}^{\Delta}|I_{t-1}|^{2}{N-2\choose p-2}\frac{\alpha_{p}N}{{N\choose p}}=\frac{\lambda|I_{t-1}|^{2}}{N-1}.

(4.4)

Let us write

D_{t}=\sum_{2\leq p_{1},p_{2}\leq\Delta}\sum_{|V(e_{l})|=p_{\ell},\ \ell=1,2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{(e_{1},e_{2})\text{ satisfies Case 2}\}},

and note that the indicators in the previous display may be dependent if
p_{1}=p_{2}
.
Nonetheless, the number of all such pairs in Case 2 is at most

\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}=2}^{\Delta}N|I_{t-1}|^{2}{N-2\choose p_{1}-2}{N-2\choose p_{2}-2},

so we have

\mathbb{E}(D_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\sum_{p_{1},p_{2}=2}^{\Delta}N|I_{t-1}|^{2}{N-2\choose p_{1}-2}{N-2\choose p_{2}-2}\frac{\alpha_{p_{1}}N}{{N\choose p_{1}}}\frac{\alpha_{p_{2}}N}{{N\choose p_{2}}}=\frac{\lambda^{2}N|I_{t-1}|^{2}}{(N-1)^{2}}.

(4.5)

By Markov’s inequality, (
4.4
), and (
4.5
), we have

\mathbb{P}(A_{t}\geq 1\text{ or }D_{t}\geq 1|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\mathbb{E}(A_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})+\mathbb{E}(D_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})\leq\frac{\lambda|I_{t-1}|^{2}}{N-1}+\frac{\lambda^{2}N|I_{t-1}|^{2}}{(N-1)^{2}}.

Taking another expectation and summing over

, we have that for sufficiently large

and
C>0
depending only on
\lambda

\mathbb{P}(\text{$B_{\lfloor\delta\log N\rfloor}(i)$ contains a cycle})\leq\sum_{1\leq t\leq 1+\delta\log N}\mathbb{P}(A_{t}\geq 1\text{ or }D_{t}\geq 1)\leq\frac{C}{N}\sum_{0\leq t\leq\delta\log N}\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|^{2}.

(4.6)

On the other hand, from Lemma

,
there is another constant

depending only on
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
such that

\sum_{0\leq t\leq\delta\log N}\mathbb{E}|I_{t}|^{2}\leq CN^{2\delta\log\lambda}.

Together with (

Our proof of Theorem

We begin our proof by the following lemma that is essentially taken from Chatterjee
[

]

Let

be a fixed integer.
Let
J
be a
k
-dimensional standard normal random vector and
{J}^{1}
and
{J}^{2}
i.i.d. copies of
J.

For the sake of symmetry, we set
J^{1}(t)=e^{-t/2}{J}+\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}{J}^{1}
and
J^{2}(t)=e^{-t/2}{J}+\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}{J}^{2}
for
t\geq 0
. Notice that the Gaussian vector
(J^{1}(t),J^{2}(t))
has the same distribution as
(J,J(t))
in Definition
1.2
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable functions. Assume that

\max_{1\leq r\leq m}\mathbb{E}|L_{r}({J})|^{2}<\infty.

Let
\phi(t):=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}L_{r}(J^{1}(t))L_{r}(J^{2}(t))
. For any
0\leq s\leq t
, we have

(i)

0\leq\phi(t)\leq\phi(s),

(ii)

\phi(s)\leq\phi(t)^{\frac{s}{t}}\phi(0)^{1-\frac{s}{t}}.

Proof.

It is immediate to see that Lemma
2.1
implies
(i)
.
Moreover,
(ii)
follows from Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\phi(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{k}}e^{-s|n|}\widehat{L}_{r}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{k}}e^{-s|n|\frac{t}{s}}\widehat{L}_{r}(n)^{2}\Bigr)^{\frac{s}{t}}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{k}}1\cdot\widehat{L}_{r}(n)^{2}\Bigr)^{1-\frac{s}{t}}=\phi(t)^{\frac{s}{t}}\phi(0)^{1-\frac{s}{t}}.

∎

Define

H_{N,t}^{k}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{a_{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\rho(J_{ij}^{k}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},\quad\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{N},\,\,k=1,2.

For any
t\geq 0
and
\lambda>0,
consider the coupled free energy

\displaystyle F_{N}(t,\lambda)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln\sum_{\sigma,\tau}\exp\bigl(\beta H_{N,t}^{1}(\sigma)+\beta H_{N,t}^{2}(\tau)+\lambda NR(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\bigr).

Let
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t,\lambda}
be the Gibbs expectation associated to this free energy.
We use the following consequence of the Gaussian hypercontractive inequality (see, e.g., page 45 in
[
10
]
): for any function
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
f\in L^{1+e^{-t}}(J)
,

\mathbb{E}f(J^{1}(t))f(J^{2}(t))\leq(\mathbb{E}f(J)^{1+e^{-t}})^{2/(1+e^{-t})}.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
\lambda>0,

t>-\ln(\alpha-1)
, and
1<b<\alpha/(1+e^{-t})
, we have that

\displaystyle F_{N}(t,\lambda)-F_{N}(\infty,\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N\beta^{2}}{a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-t}}\bigl(\mathbb{E}|J|^{b/(b-1)}\bigr)^{2(b-1)/b}\bigl(\mathbb{E}|\rho|^{b(1+e^{-t})}(J)\bigr)^{2/((1+e^{-t})b)}.

Proof.

Case 1: Smooth
\rho
.
For any
R>0,
let
\zeta_{R}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
be a smooth function satisfying that
\zeta_{R}\equiv 1
on
[-R,R]
and
\zeta_{R}\equiv 0
on
[-(R+1),R+1]^{c}
. We further assume that
\sup_{R>0}\|\zeta_{R}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<\infty.
Let
\rho_{R}=\rho\zeta_{R}
. Let
F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda)
be the same as
F_{N}(t,\lambda)
with the replacement of
\rho
by
\rho_{R}
and let
G_{N,t,\lambda}^{R}
be the Gibbs measure associated to
F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda).
Denote by
(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2}),(\sigma^{1},\tau^{1}),(\sigma^{2},\tau^{2})
the i.i.d. samples of
G_{N,t,\lambda}^{R}
and by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}
the corresponding Gibbs expectation. Note that
\rho_{R}
is twice differentiable and its derivatives are uniformly bounded. A direct computation gives that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta}{2Na_{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Bigl\langle\bigl(-e^{-t/2}J_{ij}+e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}J_{ij}^{1}\bigr)\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\bigl(-e^{-t/2}J_{ij}+e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}J_{ij}^{2}\bigr)\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{2}(t))\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\Big\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}.

Here, using the Gaussian integration by parts yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}J_{ij}\bigl\langle\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-t/2}\beta}{a_{N}}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\bigl\langle\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}(\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}-\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2})\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{-t/2}\beta}{a_{N}}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{2}(t))\bigl\langle\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}(\tau_{i}^{1}\tau_{j}^{1}-\tau_{i}^{2}\tau_{j}^{2})\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}

\displaystyle+e^{-t/2}\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle\rho_{R}^{\prime\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}.

(5.1)

and

\displaystyle(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}J_{ij}^{1}\bigl\langle\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle\rho_{R}^{\prime\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta}{a_{N}}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\bigl\langle\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}(\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}-\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2})\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}.

(5.2)

Adding these together leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl\langle\bigl(-e^{-t/2}J_{ij}+e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}J_{ij}^{1}\bigr)\rho^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\Bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{-e^{-t}\beta}{a_{N}}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{2}(t))\bigl\langle\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}(\tau_{i}^{1}\tau_{j}^{1}-\tau_{i}^{2}\tau_{j}^{2})\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}.

By symmetry, we then have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{-e^{-t}\beta^{2}}{Na_{N}^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{1}(t))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J_{ij}^{2}(t))\bigl\langle\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{j}^{1}(\tau_{i}^{1}\tau_{j}^{1}-\tau_{i}^{2}\tau_{j}^{2})\bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R},

(5.3)

which implies that for
b>1
such that
b(1+e^{-t})<\alpha
and its conjugate exponent

,

\displaystyle F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda)-F_{N}^{R}(\infty,\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t}^{\infty}\partial_{s}F_{N}^{R}(s,\lambda)ds\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{2Ne^{-s}\beta^{2}}{a_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J^{1}(s))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J^{2}(s))ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N\beta^{2}}{a_{N}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J^{1}(t))\rho_{R}^{\prime}(J^{2}(t))=\frac{2N\beta^{2}}{a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-t}}\mathbb{E}J^{1}J^{2}\rho_{R}(J^{1}(t))\rho_{R}(J^{2}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N\beta^{2}}{a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-t}}(\mathbb{E}|J|^{a})^{2/a}(\mathbb{E}|\rho_{R}|^{b}(J^{1}(t))|\rho_{R}|^{b}(J^{2}(t)))^{1/b}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2N\beta^{2}}{a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{1-e^{-t}}(\mathbb{E}|J|^{a})^{2/a}(\mathbb{E}|\rho_{R}|^{b(1+e^{-t})}(J))^{2/((1+e^{-t})b)},

(5.4)

where the inequalities used Lemma
5.1
(i),
Gaussian integration by parts, Hölder’s inequality, and Gaussian hypercontractive inequality.

Finally, we argue that our assertion follows by taking

To see this, note that

\displaystyle\bigl|F_{N}(t,\lambda)-F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\ln\Bigl\langle\exp\frac{\beta}{a_{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\Bigl(\bigl(\rho(J^{1}_{ij}(t))-\rho_{R}(J^{1}_{ij}(t))\bigr)\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\bigl(\rho(J^{2}_{ij}(t))-\rho_{R}(J^{2}_{ij}(t))\bigr)\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\Bigr)\Bigr\rangle_{t,\lambda}^{R}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta}{Na_{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\bigl(\bigl|\rho(J^{1}_{ij}(t))-\rho_{R}(J^{1}_{ij}(t))\bigr|+\bigl|\rho(J^{2}_{ij}(t))-\rho_{R}(J^{2}_{ij}(t))\bigr|\bigr)

\displaystyle=2\beta Na_{N}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\bigl|\rho(J)-\rho_{R}(J)\bigr|,

(5.5)

where the inequality used the Jensen inequality. Since

implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl|\rho(J)-\rho_{R}(J)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[|\rho(J)|;|J|\geq R\bigr]\to 0,\,\,\mbox{as $R\to\infty$,}

we have
\lim_{R\to\infty}F_{N}^{R}(t,\lambda)=F_{N}(t,\lambda)
for any

Additionally, from (
1.7
), we have

\mathbb{E}|\rho|^{b(1+e^{-t})}(J)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl(|\rho|^{b(1+e^{-t})}(J)-|\rho_{R}|^{b(1+e^{-t})}(J)\bigr)=0,

hence the right-hand side of (

) converges as well.

Case 2: General
\rho
.

for

∎

Proof of Theorem

\displaystyle\leq F_{N}(t_{0},\lambda_{0})-F_{N}(t_{0},0)

\displaystyle\leq F_{N}(\infty,\lambda_{0})-F_{N}(\infty,0)+\frac{KN}{a_{N}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\ln\bigl\langle\exp\lambda_{0}NR(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\bigr\rangle_{\infty}+\frac{KN}{a_{N}^{2}}

Putting these together, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}

Lemma A.4

Let

\displaystyle\leq g(\gamma t)+\frac{1}{((\gamma m)^{-1}-1)(\gamma^{-1}-2)}\sum_{a\in E}\mathbb{E}\bigl\|\nabla\partial_{x_{a}}^{2}\phi(J)\bigr\|^{2}+e^{-tm}.

Let
0<\gamma<1.
Write

\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|E(n)|\geq\gamma|n|}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}+\sum_{|E(n)|<\gamma|n|}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|E(n)|\geq\gamma|n|}e^{-\gamma t|n|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}+\sum_{|E(n)|<\gamma|n|}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq g(\gamma t)+\sum_{|E(n)|<\gamma|n|}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}.

Here, for any
m\geq 1,

\displaystyle\sum_{|E(n)|<\gamma|n|}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{|E(n)|<\gamma|n|,|E(n)|\leq m}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq|E(n)|<\gamma|n|,|E(n)|\leq m}e^{-t|E(n)|}\hat{\phi}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\mathbb{E}\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\tau_{i}\tau_{j}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{E}}e^{-t|n|}\hat{\phi}_{ij}(n)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\bigl(e^{-1}\mathbb{E}\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle^{2}-o(1)\bigr)=c\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})^{2}\rangle

∎

Proof of Proposition
A.3
.

For the sake of symmetry,
we set
J^{1}(t)=e^{-t/2}{J}+\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}{J}^{1}
and
J^{2}(t)=e^{-t/2}{J}+\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}{J}^{2}
for
t\geq 0
, where
J,J^{1},J^{2}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables.
Notice that the Hamiltonians satisfy

\partial_{t}H^{i}_{t}=-2^{-1}e^{-t/2}H_{J}+2^{-1}e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}H_{J^{i}},\quad i=1,2.

We can compute the derivative

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle R(\sigma^{1},\tau^{1})^{2}\bigl(\partial_{t}H^{1}_{t}(\sigma^{1})+\partial_{t}H^{2}_{t}(\tau^{1})-\partial_{t}H^{1}_{t}(\sigma^{2})-\partial_{t}H^{2}_{t}(\tau^{2})\bigr)\bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle R(\sigma^{1},\tau^{1})^{2}\bigl(\partial_{t}H^{1}_{t}(\sigma^{1})-\partial_{t}H^{1}_{t}(\sigma^{2})+\partial_{t}H^{2}_{t}(\tau^{1})-\partial_{t}H^{2}_{t}(\tau^{2})\bigr)\bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle R(\sigma^{1},\tau^{1})^{2}\bigl(-2^{-1}e^{-t}(H_{J}(\sigma^{1})-H_{J}(\sigma^{2}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2^{-1}e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}(H_{J^{1}}(\sigma^{1})-H_{J^{1}}(\sigma^{2}))\bigr)\bigr\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle R(\sigma^{1},\tau^{1})^{2}\bigl(-2^{-1}e^{-t}(H_{J}(\tau^{1})-H_{J}(\tau^{2}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+2^{-1}e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2}(H_{J^{2}}(\tau^{1})-H_{J^{2}}(\tau^{2}))\bigr)\bigr\rangle_{t}.

Theorem 4 in
[
22
]
implies that

\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle|H_{J}(\sigma^{1})-H_{J}(\sigma^{2})|^{2}\bigr\rangle_{t}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\cdot|E|^{3/2}}{\beta},

and similar results for
J
replaced with
J^{1}
and
J^{2}
, or
\sigma
replaced with
\tau.

Therefore, taking the absolute value of the derivative and using Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}(1+(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2})\bigl(\mathbb{E}\bigl\langle|H_{J}(\sigma^{1})-H_{J}(\sigma^{2})|^{2}\bigr\rangle_{t}\bigr)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{3/4}e^{-t}(1+(1-e^{-t})^{-1/2})\frac{|E|^{3/4}}{\sqrt{\beta}}.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}2^{3/4}e^{-u}(1+(1-e^{-u})^{-1/2})\sqrt{\beta}|E|^{3/4}du

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{0}-2^{3/4}\bigl(1-e^{-t}+2\sqrt{1-e^{-t}}\bigr)\sqrt{\beta}|E|^{3/4}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{0}-6\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\beta}|E|^{3/4}.

This implies that if
t
is small enough such that the second term is less than or equal to
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{0}
, i.e.,

t\leq 12^{-2}\beta^{-1}|E|^{-3/2}(\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})^{2}\rangle)^{2},

then
\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{t}>2^{-1}\mathbb{E}\langle R(\sigma,\tau)^{2}\rangle_{0}.

∎
