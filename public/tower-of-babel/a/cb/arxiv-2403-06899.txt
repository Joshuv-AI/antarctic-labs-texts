Abstract
In many multiobject tracking applications, including radar and sonar tracking, after prefiltering the received signal, measurement data is typically structured in cells. The cells, e.g., represent different range and bearing values. However, conventional multiobject tracking methods use so-called point measurements. Point measurements are provided by a preprocessing stage that applies a threshold or detector and breaks up the cell’s structure by converting cell indexes into, e.g., range and bearing measurements. We here propose a Bayesian multiobject tracking method that processes measurements that have been thresholded but are still cell-structured. We first derive a likelihood function that systematically incorporates an adjustable detection threshold which makes it possible to control the number of cell measurements. We then propose a Poisson Multi-Bernoulli (PMB) filter based on the likelihood function for cell measurements. Furthermore, we establish a link to the conventional point measurement model by deriving the likelihood function for point measurements with amplitude information (AM) and discuss the PMB filter that uses point measurements with AM. Our numerical results demonstrate the advantages of the proposed PMB filter for thresholded cell measurements compared to the conventional PMB filter for point measurements with and without AM.

In this paper, we introduce a measurement model for thresholded cell measurements and propose a PMB filter based on this new model.
The novel method can be interpreted as a tradeoff between the already existing DTT PMB and TBD PMB filters.
In particular, as in DTT filtering, a threshold controls the number of considered measurements, but, as in TBD filtering, measurements still possess the cell structure of the original, unthresholded, measurements.
In addition, this cell structure makes it possible to avoid the Poisson clutter approximation used by conventional DTT approaches. We also establish a link to the conventional likelihood function for point measurements with amplitude information (AM) and discuss the approximations applied in conventional point measurement models. Our numerical results indicate an improved tracking performance compared to the DTT PMB filter with and without AM in both tracking accuracy and runtime.

II
Measurement Model

The measurement model describes the statistics of the measurements
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}
conditioned on the multiobject state
{\cal{X}}_{k}
and can be characterized by the likelihood function
f(\bm{z}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
.
We here model the multiobject state at time
k
by the RFS
\mathsfbr{X}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=\{\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}^{(1)}\hskip-0.85358pt,\ldots,\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}^{(n)}\}
and the measurements by the random vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}=[\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(1)}\ldots\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(M)}]^{\text{T}}
.
The single-object state vectors
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}
are defined as
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}\!=\hskip-0.85358pt[\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k}^{\text{T}}\,\,\sgamma_{k}]^{\text{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt
, where
\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k}=[\mathsfbr{p}_{k,1}\,\,\mathsfbr{p}_{k,2}\,\,\dot{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k,1}\,\,\dot{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k,2}]^{\text{T}}
is a two-dimensional (2D) position and velocity vector and
\sgamma_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt
is the object’s intensity.
The modeling of
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}
will be described in the following.

II-A

Detector-Based Association Model

Our modeling of
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}
is based on
M
data cells characterized by the random vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{c}}_{k}=[\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(1)}\ldots\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(M)}]^{\text{T}}
.
Here, each
\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
,
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathcal{M}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt\{1,\ldots,M\}
represents the scalar intensity or magnitude of data cell
m
, and the cell index
m
itself represents the corresponding positional information. We consider point objects, where the influence of one of those objects with state
\bm{x}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}
on cell
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
is determined by an indicator function given

by

\delta^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})=\begin{cases}1,&\bm{x}_{k}\hskip 2.84526pt\text{is in bin}\hskip 2.84526ptm\\[2.84526pt]
0,&\bm{x}_{k}\hskip 2.84526pt\text{is not in bin}\hskip 2.84526ptm\hskip 0.85358pt.\end{cases}\vskip 1.42262pt

(1)

In particular, object
\bm{x}_{k}
contributes to cell
\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(m)}
only if
\bm{x}_{k}
is located in that cell, i.e., by knowing
\bm{x}_{k}
, we also know the index of the influenced cell.
If cell
\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(m)}
is influenced by
\bm{x}_{k}
, it is distributed according to
f_{1}\big(\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)
.
If cell
\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(m)}
is not influenced by any object, it is due to clutter and distributed according to
f_{0}\big(\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}\big)
.

Since
\mathsfbr{X}_{k}
is random, it is unknown which object contributes to which cell.
We therefore introduce the random object-cell association vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\big[\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(1)}\ldots\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(n)}\big]^{\text{T}}
with
n\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt|{\cal{X}}_{k}|
and entries
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathcal{M}
. Here,
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathcal{M}
indicates that object
\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
contributes to cell
\mathsfbr{c}_{k}^{(m)}
.
We assume that an object contributes to at most one cell and, conversely, that a cell is at most influenced by one object.
These assumptions result in a set of valid association vectors
\bm{a}_{k}
collected in the set
{\cal{A}}_{k}
.

We apply now a detector with threshold
\eta
to each cell
\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt
,
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathcal{M}
resulting in “thresholded” measurements
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=[\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(1)}\ldots\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(M)}]^{\text{T}}
. In particular, we set
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}
if
\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt>\eta
and
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\eta
if
\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\leqslant\eta
.
In what follows, we refer to
\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt>\hskip-0.85358pt\eta
as detections.
Following classical detection theory
[
23
]
, we directly obtain a statistical model for
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}
.
In particular, each detection, i.e.,
\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt>\hskip-0.85358pt\eta
, is either generated by an object or is a false alarm/clutter.
In the former case, it is distributed according to

f_{1,\eta}\big(z^{(m)}_{k}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)=\frac{f_{1}\big(z^{(m)}_{k}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)}{p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})}\vskip 1.42262pt

(2)

and, in the latter case, according to

f_{0,\eta}\big(z^{(m)}_{k}\big)=\frac{f_{0}\big(z^{(m)}_{k}\big)}{p^{(m)}_{\text{FA}}}.

(3)

Here, the normalizations
p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})
and
p^{(m)}_{\text{FA}}
are referred to as detection probability and false alarm probability, respectively, and can be computed as

\displaystyle p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta}^{\infty}f_{1}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\mathrm{d}z_{k}^{(m)}

\displaystyle p^{(m)}_{\text{FA}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta}^{\infty}f_{0}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt\text{d}z_{k}^{(m)}.

II-B

The Joint Likelihood Function

The likelihood function
f(\bm{z}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})

is obtained by first computing the conditional pdf
f(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
and then marginalizing out the association variable
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}_{k}
, i.e.,

f(\bm{z}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\sum_{\bm{a}_{k}\in\hskip 0.85358pt\mathcal{A}_{k}}f(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt.

(4)

Based on the aforementioned assumptions, the conditional pdf
f(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
is found

as

\displaystyle f(\bm{z}_{k},\bm{a}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\prod_{i\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{I}}_{k}}\delta^{(a_{k}^{(i)})}\big(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)\hskip 0.85358ptf\big(z_{k}^{(a_{k}^{(i)})}|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle\hskip 2.84526pt\times\prod_{m\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}}f\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt

(5)

where we introduced
{\cal{I}}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\mathcal{M}\setminus\big\{a_{k}^{(1)},\dots,a_{k}^{(n)}\big\}
.
The set
\bar{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{a}_{k}}
comprises all measurement indices that are not associated to any object by association
\bm{a}_{k}
.
In (
5
), the distribution
f(z_{k}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)})
is given by

\hskip-7.11317ptf\big(z_{k}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)=\begin{cases}1-p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}\big(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big),&\hskip-2.84526pt\hskip-0.85358ptz_{k}^{(m)}=\eta\\[7.11317pt]
p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}\big(\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big)\hskip 0.85358ptf_{1,\eta}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}^{(i)}\big),&\hskip-2.84526pt\hskip-0.85358ptz_{k}^{(m)}>\eta\end{cases}

(6)

Furthermore, the distribution
f\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big)
reads

f(z_{k}^{(m)})=\begin{cases}1-p_{\text{FA}}^{(m)},&z_{k}^{(m)}=\eta\\[7.11317pt]
p_{\text{FA}}^{(m)}\hskip 0.85358ptf_{0,\eta}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big),&z_{k}^{(m)}>\eta.\\[7.11317pt]
\end{cases}

(7)

Our measurement model excludes the case where multiple objects are in the same cell.
Apart from the fact that this event is rather unlikely in many applications anyway, our proposed method can still handle scenarios where some objects are in the same cell for a limited amount of time.
This is confirmed by the very good object detection and tracking performance of our proposed method, as reported in Section
V
.

III
Multiobject Tracking with Thresholded Cell Measurements

In the following, we present our PMB filtering method based on the thresholded cell measurement model introduced in the previous section.

III-A

Sequential Bayesian Estimation

To derive our PMB filter, we use the sequential Bayesian estimation framework for RFS-based multiobject state models.
Here, the statistics of the multiobject state
\mathsfbr{X}_{k}
at time
k
, conditioned on all obtained measurements
\bm{z}_{1:k}\hskip-0.85358pt\triangleq\hskip-0.85358pt[\bm{z}_{1}^{\text{T}}\ldots\bm{z}_{k}^{\text{T}}]^{\text{T}}
up to time
k
, are described by the posterior pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k})
.
The estimation framework consists of a prediction and an update step.
The prediction step transforms the posterior pdf at time
k-1
, i.e.,
f({\cal{X}}_{k-1}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})
, into the predicted posterior pdf at time
k
, i.e.,
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})
using the state-transition pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k-1})
.
Analogously, the update step transforms the predicted posterior pdf into the updated posterior pdf at time
k
, i.e.,
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k})
, using the likelihood function
f(\bm{z}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k})
presented in Section
II-B
and the current measurements
\bm{z}_{k}
.

Following the PMB MOT paradigm
[
10
,
13
,
11
,
17
]
, we here model the multiobject state
{\cal{X}}_{k-1}
at time
k\hskip-0.85358pt-\hskip-0.85358pt1
as the union of a Poisson RFS and an MB RFS, i.e., the posterior pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k-1}|{\cal{X}}_{k-1})
is of PMB form.
Using standard model assumptions for
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|{\cal{X}}_{k-1})

[
10
,
13
]
, it was shown in, e.g.,
[
10
,
13
]
, that, after applying the prediction step, the predicted posterior pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})
is again of PMB form.
In fact,
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})
is given according to

\hskip-2.84526ptf({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k-1})\hskip 0.85358pt=\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(1)}\uplus\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(2)}=\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}}\hskip-8.53581ptf_{k|k-1}^{\text{P}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}^{(1)}\big)\hskip 0.85358ptf_{k|k-1}^{\text{MB}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}^{(2)}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt.\vskip-1.42262pt

(8)

Here,
\sum_{{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(1)}\uplus\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(2)}=\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}}

denotes the sum over all disjoint decompositions of
{\cal{X}}_{k}
into
{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(1)}
and
{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(2)}
.
Furthermore,
f_{k|k-1}^{\text{P}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
is a Poisson pdf
[
7
]
, which is fully parametrized by its probability hypothesis density (PHD)
\lambda_{k|k-1}(\bm{x}_{k})
, and
f_{k|k-1}^{\text{MB}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
is an MB pdf
[
7
]
consisting of
J_{k-1}
Bernoulli pdfs, where each Bernoulli pdf is itself parametrized by an existence probability
r_{k|k-1}^{(j)}
and a spatial pdf
f_{k|k-1}^{(j)}(\bm{x})
,
j\hskip-0.85358pt\in{\cal{J}}_{k-1}\triangleq\{1,\ldots,J_{k-1}\}
.
See, e.g.,
[
7
]
, for more information on RFSs and their statistical description.
In the following two subsections, we present the exact and approximate update steps.

III-B

Exact Update Step

Note that the current measurements
\bm{z}_{k}
have already been observed and are therefore known.
It is therefore also known which data cells
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
have an intensity above threshold
\eta
and which do not.
We here introduce the index set
{\cal{M}}_{k}^{\text{D}}\subseteq{\cal{M}}
of all detections, i.e., all cell indexes with
z_{k}^{(m)}>\eta
and the index set
{\cal{M}}_{k}^{\text{M}}={\cal{M}}\setminus{\cal{M}}_{k}^{\text{D}}
of all missed detections, i.e., all cell indexes with
z_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\eta
.

After applying the update step, as we show in Appendix
A
, the posterior pdf
f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k})
is no longer a PMB pdf but a Poisson/MB mixture (PMBM) pdf,
which is given by

f({\cal{X}}_{k}|\bm{z}_{1:k})\hskip 0.85358pt=\!\sum_{{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(1)}\uplus\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}^{(2)}=\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{X}}_{k}}\hskip-8.53581ptf^{\text{P}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}^{(1)}\big)\hskip 0.85358ptf^{\text{MBM}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}^{(2)}\big)\hskip 0.85358pt.\vskip-1.42262pt

(9)

Here,
f^{\text{P}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
is a Poisson pdf and
f^{\text{MBM}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
is a multi-Bernoulli mixture (MBM) pdf; expressions of both will be presented now.

The Poisson pdf
f^{\text{P}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
in (
9
) is fully characterized by the posterior PHD
\lambda(\bm{x}_{k})
, which is given by

\displaystyle\lambda(\bm{x}_{k})=\bigg(\sum_{m\hskip 0.85358pt\in\hskip 0.85358pt{\cal{M}}^{\text{M}}_{k}}\delta^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt\frac{1-p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})}{1-p_{\text{FA}}^{(m)}}\hskip 0.85358pt\bigg)\lambda_{k|k-1}(\bm{x}_{k})\hskip 0.85358pt.

Here,
p_{\text{D}}^{(m)}(\bm{x}_{k})
and
p_{\text{FA}}^{(m)}
are the detection probability and false alarm probability, respectively, defined in Section
II-A
and
\lambda_{k|k-1}(\bm{x}_{k})
is the predicted posterior PHD characterizing
f_{k|k-1}^{\text{P}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
in (
8
).

In order to describe
f^{\text{MBM}}\big({\cal{X}}_{k}\big)
in (
9
),

we first introduce the random object-cell association vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}^{\prime}_{k}\triangleq[\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(1)}\ldots\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(J_{k})}]^{\text{T}}
where
J_{k}=J_{k-1}+M^{\text{D}}_{k}
and
M_{k}^{\text{D}}\triangleq|{\cal{M}}_{k}^{\text{D}}|
.
Whereas the association vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}_{k}
defined in Section
II-A
describes the association between objects and data cells, the vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}^{\prime}_{k}
describes now the association of potential objects, i.e., Bernoulli components, with data cells.
More precisely,
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}^{\prime}_{k}
has entries
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(j)}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\{0\}\hskip-0.85358pt\cup\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}
for

and

.
Here, for

,
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(j)}\hskip-0.85358pt=0
indicates that the object
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}
modeled by Bernoulli component

\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(j)}\hskip-0.85358pt\in{\cal{M}}^{\text{M}}_{k}

Furthermore, for

\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(j)}\hskip-0.85358pt=1

was generated either by an object
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}
modeled by the Poisson pdf or by clutter and, conversely,
\mathsfbr{a}_{k}^{\prime(j)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt0
that detection
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}_{k}^{\text{D}}
was neither generated by an object modeled by the Poisson RFS nor by clutter.
Here, the mapping between
j\in{\cal{J}}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt\setminus\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{J}}_{k-1}
and
m\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt{\cal{M}}_{k}^{\text{D}}
is arbitrary but unique.
Note that there is exactly one

.
All valid association vectors
\bm{a}^{\prime}_{k}
form the set
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{k}

Using
\bm{\mathsfbr{a}}^{\prime}_{k}
, we can now express

according to

Approximate Update Step

We now approximate the MBM pdf in (

IV
Multiobject Tracking with Point Measurements

In the following, we derive a likelihood function for point measurements that also uses AM.
Contrary to our cell measurement model where the measurements are described by the random vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{z}}_{k}=[\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(1)}\ldots\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(M)}]^{\text{T}}
and where the measurement index represents the positional information, point measurements are typically modeled by an unordered set
\mathsfbr{Z}_{k}=\{\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathsfbr{z}_{k}^{(\mathsfbr{M}_{k})}\}
. Here, positional information is modeled explicitly by position-related measurements and not by the measurement index.

IV-A

Explicit Location Information and Clutter Cardinality

Let
\big[\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k,1}\,\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k,2}\big]^{\text{T}}

\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k}

\bm{\mathsfbr{y}}_{k}^{(m)}=\big[\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k}\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k,1}\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\hskip 0.85358pt\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k,2}\big]^{\text{T}}
be the corresponding joint measurement vector.
Position-related measurements can be obtained by breaking up the cell structure and converting cell indices to position-related measurements.
A joint measurement vector
\bm{\mathsfbr{y}}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt

\displaystyle f_{1}(\bm{y}_{k}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=f_{1,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})\hskip 0.85358ptf_{1,\eta}(z_{k}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=f_{0,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)})\hskip 0.85358ptf_{0,\eta}(z_{k}^{(m)}).

We start by noting that in the likelihood function for thresholded cell measurements the information about the number of clutter measurements

\mathsfbr{b}_{k}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt1

Next, we note that conventional point measurement models allow any possible object-measurement association, theoretically, with non-zero probability.

V
Simulation Study

In the following, we perform a simulation study in which we compare the performance of our proposed PMB filter with thresholded pixel measurements (PMB-CM) with that of a PMB filter that uses point measurements with (PMB-AM) and without (PMB) AM. But first, we start with a short description of the underlying simulation setup.

V-A

Simulation Setup

We consider a 2D simulation scenario with a region of interest (ROI) of
[\text{0}\text{m},\text{32}\text{m}]\times[\text{0}\text{m},\text{32}\text{m}]
.
We simulated 10 objects during 200 time steps.
Recap that the single-object state vectors are defined as
\bm{\mathsfbr{x}}_{k}\!=\hskip-0.85358pt[\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k}^{\text{T}}\,\,\sgamma_{k}]^{\text{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt
, where
\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k}=[\mathsfbr{p}_{k,1}\,\,\mathsfbr{p}_{k,2}\,\,\dot{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k,1}\,\,\dot{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k,2}]^{\text{T}}
is the object’s 2D position and 2D velocity and
\sgamma_{k}
the object’s intensity (cf. Section
II
).
The objects appear at various times before time step 30 at randomly chosen positions in the ROI, and they disappear at various times after time step 170 or when they leave the ROI.
The objects’ initial velocities are drawn from
f_{\text{v}}(\dot{p}_{k,1},\,\dot{p}_{k,2})={\cal{N}}(\dot{p}_{k,1},\,\dot{p}_{k,2};\bm{0}_{2},\sigma^{2}_{\text{v}}\bm{I}_{2})
with
\sigma^{2}_{\text{v}}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt10^{-2}
, and the objects’ initial intensities are set to
10
.

The object states’ evolution is modeled as follows:
The kinematic part
\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k}
evolves according to the nearly constant velocity motion model, which is given by
\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt=\bm{A}\hskip 0.85358pt\bm{\mathsfbr{p}}_{k-1}+\bm{W}\hskip 0.85358pt\bm{\mathsfbr{w}}_{k}
with
\bm{A}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
and
\bm{W}\hskip-0.85358pt\in\hskip-0.85358pt\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}
chosen as in
[
13
]
. The driving noise
\bm{\mathsfbr{w}}_{k}
is distributed according to
{\cal{N}}(\bm{w}_{k};\bm{0}_{2},10^{-3}\bm{I}_{2})
. The object intensities remain constant over time.

The measurement consists of
32\times 32
data cells of square size, each of which has a side length of
1
m. The data is generated by modeling the cell intensities as Rayleigh distributed, i.e.,
f_{1}\big(c_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)={\cal{R}}(c^{(m)}_{k};\sqrt{\gamma_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{\text{n}}}\hskip 0.85358pt)\vskip-1.42262pt
and
f_{0}(y_{k}^{(m)})={\cal{R}}\big(y^{(m)}_{k};\sigma_{\text{n}}\big)
and drawing samples from them. In radar applications, this model is referred to as Swerling 1
[
25
]
.
We set
\sigma^{2}_{\text{n}}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt1
leading to a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of
10
.
If there are several objects in the same cell, lets say
\bm{x}^{(m_{1})}_{k}\hskip-0.85358pt,\dots,\bm{x}^{(m_{I})}_{k}
, then

the cell intensity
\mathsfbr{c}^{(m)}_{k}
is sampled from
{\cal{R}}\Big(c^{(m)}_{k};\sqrt{\gamma^{(m_{1})}_{k}+\dots+\gamma^{(m_{I})}_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{\text{n}}}\Big)
.

In PMB-CM, we set

f_{1}\big(c_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)={\cal{R}}(c^{(m)}_{k};\sqrt{\gamma_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{\text{n}}}\hskip 0.85358pt)

and
f_{0}(y_{k}^{(m)})={\cal{R}}\big(y^{(m)}_{k};\sigma_{\text{n}}\big)
.
As discussed in Section
II-A
,

for a given threshold
\eta
, this directly induces
f_{1,\eta}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big|\bm{x}_{k}\big)
and
f_{0,\eta}\big(z_{k}^{(m)}\big)
.

For PMB and PMB-AM, position-related measurement information is represented explicitly according to
\big[\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k,1}\,\mathsfbr{z}^{(m)}_{k,2}\big]^{\text{T}}
, which necessitates the further specification of
f_{1,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})
in (
26
) and
f_{0,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)})
in (
27
).
We follow the frequently used convention for point measurement filtering and model
f_{1,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})
as a Gaussian pdf
{\cal{N}}\big(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)};[p_{k,1}\hskip 0.85358ptp_{k,2}]^{\mathrm{T}}\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt\hskip-0.85358pt,\sigma^{2}_{\text{p}}\bm{I}_{2}\big)
.
Here, the variance
\sigma^{2}_{\text{p}}
is obtained via moment matching by setting
\sigma^{2}_{\text{p}}
to the variance of a uniform distribution defined by the side length of a data cell given by
1
m, i.e.,
\sigma^{2}_{\text{p}}=1/12
.
In PMB and PMB-AM,
f_{1,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})
is evaluated by setting
[z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip 0.85358ptz_{k,2}^{(m)}]^{\text{T}}
equal to the 2D cell center point. Furthermore, we model
f_{0,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)})
as uniformly distributed over the ROI. It has been observed, that modeling the likelihood function
f_{1,\text{p}}(z_{k,1}^{(m)}\hskip-0.85358pt,z_{k,2}^{(m)}|\bm{x}_{k})
constant on the area of the corresponding data cell and zero otherwise leads to almost identical performance compared to the aforementioned Gaussian model.

V-B

Filter Settings and Simulation Results

We employ particle implementations of PMB-CM, PMB-AM, and PMB. We represent the spatial pdf of each Bernoulli component by
3,\!000
particles, the posterior PHD by
50,\!000
particles, and the birth PHD by another
50,\!000
; the resulting
100,\!000
particles are reduced to
50,\!000
again after the update step.
More precisely, the birth PHD is given by
\lambda_{\text{B}}(\bm{x}_{k})\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt\mu_{\text{B}}\hskip 0.85358ptf_{\text{B}}(\bm{x}_{k})
with
\mu_{\text{B}}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt5/32^{2}
and
f_{\text{B}}(\bm{x}_{k})\hskip-0.85358pt=f(p_{k,1},p_{k,2})\hskip 0.85358ptf_{\text{v}}\big(\dot{p}_{k,1},\dot{p}_{k,2}\big)\hskip 0.85358ptf_{\text{I}}(\gamma_{k})
where
f(p_{k,1},p_{k,2})
is uniform over the ROI,
f_{\text{v}}\big(\dot{p}_{k,1},\dot{p}_{k,2}\big)
was defined in the previous subsection, and
f_{\text{I}}(\gamma_{k})
is uniform from
0
to
30
.
We set the survival probability to
p_{\text{S}}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt0.999
and the recycling threshold (cf. Section
III-C
) to
\eta_{\text{R}}\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt0.1
.

The tracking performance is evaluated by means of the generalized optimal subpattern assignment (GOSPA) metric
[
26
]
with parameters
p\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt1
,
c\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt20
, and
\beta\hskip-0.85358pt=\hskip-0.85358pt2
for different thresholds
\eta\hskip-2.84526pt\in\hskip-2.84526pt\{2,4,6\}
.
We show the GOSPA errors for PMB and PMB-AM in Fig.​​
1
. It can be seen that the use of AM improves tracking performance.
In particular, as Fig.​
1
(c) indicates, surprisingly, the detection performance of newborn objects of PMB decreases for small
\eta
, which in turn also increases the total GOSPA error. This can be attributed to the fact that for low
\eta
not only the possibility of object detection but also the number of clutter measurements increases. Here, AM helps the tracker to distinguish between object-generated measurements and clutter measurements.

Next, in Fig.

As can be seen there, PMB-CM outperforms PMB-AM for all three values of

.
This can be attributed to the applied point measurement approximation described in Section

The table shows that PMB-AM is slightly slower than PMB due to the additional processing caused by AM.
While the runtime of PMB-CM increases only slightly for lower values of
\eta

We can thus conclude that the proposed PMB filter using thresholded cell measurements achieves a very good accuracy/complexity tradeoff.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed a Poisson multi-Bernoulli (PMB) filter that uses thresholded cell measurements.
In a simulation experiment, we demonstrated the advantages of our proposed PMB filter using thresholded cell measurements compared to the conventional PMB filter with and without amplitude information.
Future work includes advanced measurement models that account for multiple objects being in the same cell and objects contributing to multiple cells.
Further promising future research venues are the combination of probabilistic focalization with neural networks
[
27
]
and particle flow techniques
[
28
]
