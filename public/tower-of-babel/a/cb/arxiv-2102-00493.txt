Hausdorff’s forgotten proof that almost all numbers are normal
Edmund Weitz

Abstract
In 1914, Felix Hausdorff published an elegant proof that almost all numbers are simply normal in base 2. We generalize this proof to show that almost all numbers are normal. The result is arguably the most elementary proof for this theorem so far and should be accessible to undergraduates in their first year. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 11K16 Keywords: normal numbers

“Almost all”

The idea of a
measure
is to assign non-negative numbers to sets (of
real numbers) in such a way that these numbers can intuitively be interpreted
as the sizes of the sets. Two obviously meaningful requirements for a measure
are that the empty set is assigned the measure zero and that the measure is

additive
: the measure of the union of two (or finitely many) sets
which are mutually disjoint must be the sum of their measures. In order to be
useful in analysis, measures are actually required to be

\sigma
-additive
: the above must also hold for countably many sets
(in which case the sum of the measures becomes a series).

The most important measure, and the one to be used in this article, is the

Lebesgue measure
which we’ll denote with the letter
\lambda
. The
Lebesgue measure of an interval of real numbers is its length, e.g.,

\lambda([1,5])=5-1=4
. And the open interval
(1,5)
has the same measure.
This implies that the endpoints
“don’t count:”
finite sets like
\{1,5\}
are

null sets
, their measure is zero. Generally, a set is a null set if,
for every
\varepsilon>0
, one can find countably many intervals such that the
set is a subset of the union of these intervals and the sum of the measures of
the intervals is at most
\varepsilon
. An important example of a null set is
the set
\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers. More generally:

Lemma 2.1
.

Every countable set is a null set.

Proof.

Let
A
be a countable set and let
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an enumeration
of
A
.
2
2

2

The enumeration doesn’t have to be one-to-one, so the proof
also applies to finite sets. Also note that in this article
0\in\mathbb{N}
and
we write
\mathbb{N}^{+}
for the set of
positive
integers.
Furthermore,
let
\varepsilon
be an arbitrary positive number. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

I_{n}
be an interval of length
\varepsilon/2^{n+1}
which includes

.
A

is then covered by the intervals

and we have:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda(I_{n})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n+1}}=\varepsilon\qed

Lemma 2.2
.

The countable union of null sets is again a null set.

Proof.

The main idea is that, for a given

Lemma 3.1
.

If

is simply normal in base
r^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and all

m<n
, then
\alpha
is normal in base
r
.

Proof.

Let
n
be fixed and use
r^{n}
as the base. For
m\geq n
we find non-negative integers
c
and
k

with
c<n
and

, and we have:

r^{m}\alpha=r^{c}\cdot(r^{n})^{k}\alpha

The digits after the radix point of

(r^{n})^{k}\alpha
are the same as those of
\alpha
beginning at the
k

r^{m}\alpha
are the

r^{c}\alpha
beginning at the
k
-the digit.

The reason for this is that the digits in base
r^{n}
are obtained by

digits in base
r

Multiplication with

thus has the same effect on both sequences of

.

If

viewed as a sequence of ones and zeros. If
\alpha

your book will appear infinitely often in the binary representation

\alpha

One should think that such

“normal”

The main lemma

The proof that almost all numbers are normal relies on a technical lemma which
generalizes a computation from
[
5
, p. 420 f]
:

Lemma 4.1
.

If
r
is an integer greater than 1, then there’s a positive constant
D
such that
the following inequality holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
:

\sum_{p=0}^{n}\binom{n}{p}\frac{(r-1)^{n-p}}{r^{n}}\biggl(\frac{p}{n}-\frac{1}{r}\biggr)^{\!4}\leq\frac{D}{n^{2}}

Proof.

We fix positive integers
s
and
n
and define some functions recursively:

\displaystyle f_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{n}\binom{n}{p}x^{sp}y^{n-p}

\displaystyle f_{k+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=x\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{k}(x,y)-y\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{k}(x,y)\quad\quad\quad(k\in\mathbb{N})

By working with individual summands, it is easy to check that

\displaystyle f_{k}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{n}\binom{n}{p}\bigl((s+1)p-n\bigr)^{k}x^{sp}y^{n-p}

(3)

holds for all
k
.

By the binomial theorem, we know that
f_{0}
can also be written like this:

f_{0}(x,y)=(x^{s}+y)^{n}

It is a tedious—but completely elementary—exercise to compute
f_{4}
based on this representation.
8
8

8

As we don’t live in Hausdorff’s times anymore, we can use a computer algebra system.
We get:

\displaystyle f_{4}(x,y)

\displaystyle=n(x^{s}+y)^{n-4}Q

\displaystyle\text{with }Q

\displaystyle=6n^{2}(s+1)^{2}x^{s}y(sx^{s}-y)^{2}+n^{3}(sx^{s}-y)^{4}+{}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}(s+1)^{4}x^{s}y(x^{2s}-4x^{s}y+y^{2})+{}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\phantom{=}}n(s+1)^{3}x^{s}y(7(s+1)x^{s}y-4sx^{2s}-4y^{2})

We now set
r=s+1
,
x=1/\sqrt[s]{r}
, and
y=(r-1)/r
.
This implies
sx^{s}-y=0
and two of the four summands in
Q
vanish.
The remaining terms simplify to this:

\displaystyle f_{4}(x,y)

\displaystyle=3(r-1)^{2}n^{2}+(r^{3}-7r^{2}+12r-6)n

That’s a second degree polynomial in
n
and we thus know that

\displaystyle f_{4}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2}

for some constant

independent of
n
.

If we now replace

with the term from (

\sum_{p=0}^{n}\binom{n}{p}\frac{(r-1)^{n-p}}{r^{n}}(rp-n)^{4}\leq Cn^{2}

Dividing by
(rn)^{4}
yields the inequality we’re after with
D=C/r^{4}
.

5
Almost all numbers are normal.

For the rest of this text, we will concentrate on numbers in the interval

[0,1)
. We fix some base
r\geq 2
. If we look at a specific sequence of
n

digits, then the set of numbers starting with this sequence is an interval
with Lebesgue measure
1/r^{n}
. For example, in base 10, the set of numbers
starting with the sequence
141
is the interval
[0.141,0.142)
which
includes numbers like
\pi-3
. Its measure is
1/1000
.

We now also fix a specific digit
b
of
\Sigma_{r}
. We want to know the
measure of the set of numbers that have exactly
p
occurrences of this digit
among their first
n
digits. That’s also easy to compute: There are

\binom{n}{p}
ways to pick
p
of the available
n
positions. For the
remaining
n-p
positions we can pick any of the other
r-1
digits and there
are
(r-1)^{n-p}
ways to do that. And each of the sequences thus created
results in an interval of length
1/r^{n}
disjoint from all other intervals of
the same type. The measure therefore is:

\binom{n}{p}\cdot(r-1)^{n-p}\cdot\frac{1}{r^{n}}

(4)

For a positive real number
\varepsilon
we now look at the set of all numbers
where the relative frequency of
b
’s among the first
n
digits deviates from
the
“expected”
value
1/r
by at least
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle M_{b}(n,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in[0,1):|p_{b,n,\alpha,r}/n-1/r|\geq\varepsilon\}

With (
4
), we can compute the measure of this set:

\displaystyle\lambda(M_{b}(n,\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=0\\
|p/n-1/r|\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{n}{p}\frac{(r-1)^{n-p}}{r^{n}}

Using the constant
D
from lemma
4.1
we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}\cdot\lambda(M_{b}(n,\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=0\\
|p/n-1/r|\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{n}{p}\frac{(r-1)^{n-p}}{r^{n}}\cdot\varepsilon^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=0\\
|p/n-1/r|\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}^{n}\binom{n}{p}\frac{(r-1)^{n-p}}{r^{n}}\biggl(\frac{p}{n}-\frac{1}{r}\biggr)^{4}\leq\frac{D}{n^{2}}

and thus:

\displaystyle\lambda(M_{b}(n,\varepsilon))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{\varepsilon^{4}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}

(5)

Let
M_{b}(\varepsilon)
be the set of numbers
\alpha\in[0,1)
where the relative
frequency
p_{b,n,\alpha,r}/n
deviates from
1/r
by at least
\varepsilon

for
infinitely many

n
. In other words,
\alpha\in M_{b}(\varepsilon)
iff for each

m\in\mathbb{N}
there’s an
n\geq m
such that
\alpha\in M_{b}(n,\varepsilon)
:

\displaystyle M_{b}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}S_{b}(m,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle S_{b}(m,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{n=m}^{\infty}M_{b}(n,\varepsilon)

By (
5
), we have

\lambda(S_{b}(m,\varepsilon))\leq\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\lambda(M_{b}(n,\varepsilon))\leq\frac{D}{\varepsilon^{4}}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}

and because the
series on the right converges, the measure of
S_{b}(m,\varepsilon)
will become
arbitrarily small if
m
is just big enough.
M_{b}(\varepsilon)
must therefore
be a null set as it is contained in all
S_{b}(m,\varepsilon)
.

Finally, let
M_{b}
be the set of all numbers in
[0,1)
that are not simply

because condition (

the digit

will look like this

M_{b}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}M_{b}(1/k)

. The set of the

which are not simply normal in base

is then also a
null set as it is the union of the
r

M_{0}
to
M_{r-1}
. We just proved:

Theorem 5.1
.

If

is an arbitrary base, then almost all numbers are simply normal
in this base.

If
