Abstract




Anisotropic homogenized composite mediums arising from truncated spheres, spheroids, and ellipsoids

Abstract

Closed-form expressions were recently derived for depolarization dyadics for
truncated spheres and truncated spheroids, and the formalism was extended to truncated ellipsoids. These results were exploited to develop an implementation of the
Maxwell Garnett homogenization formalism for the relative permittivity parameters of homogenized composite mediums (HCMs) arising from an isotropic host medium impregnated with isotropic
inclusions that are truncated spheres, spheroids, and ellipsoids. In so doing, the anisotropy of the HCM was related to the geometry of the inclusions: in general, the more the shape of the inclusions deviated from spherical, the greater was the degree of anisotropy exhibited by the HCM.

Keywords
: Maxwell Garnett homogenization formalism; depolarization dyadics; anisotropy; truncated spheres; truncated spheroids; truncated ellipsoids

1
Introduction

A host medium impregnated with particulate inclusions may be regarded as a homogenized composite medium (HCM) provided that the inclusions are much smaller than the wavelengths involved
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Current interest in HCMs is intense
due to the burgeoning development of nanocomposite materials for optical applications
[
5
]
. In particular, HCMs can exhibit properties not exhibited by the component mediums from which they arise
[
6
]
. For example, anisotropic HCMs can rise from isotropic component mediums if the geometries of the inclusions exhibit certain symmetries
[
7
]
.

Homogenization formalisms are used to estimate the constitutive parameters
of HCMs
[
1
]
. The Maxwell Garnett formalism
[
8
]
is perhaps the most widely used of all homogenization formalisms.
Most homogenization formalisms (including the Maxwell Garnett formalism) use
depolarization dyadics
to represent the scattering responses of the inclusions
[
4
]
, with the depolarization dyadic being the integrated singularity of the corresponding dyadic Green function
[
9
,
10
]
.
Generally, homogenization formalisms are used for inclusions with simple shapes, e.g., spherical, spheroidal, and ellipsoidal. This is because closed-form expressions
for depolarization dyadics
are only available
for a limited range of inclusion shapes
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
, with more complex inclusion shapes requiring numerical evaluation
[
18
,
19
]
.

Recently, the
range
of inclusion shapes that can be accommodated in homogenization formalisms was extended considerably by the derivation of closed-form expressions for depolarization dyadics for truncated spheres and truncated spheroids. Furthermore, the methodology was extended to truncated ellipsoids
[
20
]
.
In this paper, these
recently derived expressions for depolarization dyadics are used in an implementation of the
Maxwell Garnett formalism to estimate
the relative permittivity parameters of HCMs arising from inclusions shaped as truncated spheres and spheroids. The implementation is extended to
the case of
inclusions with truncated ellipsoidal shapes, with the corresponding depolarization dyadics being evaluated numerically. As regards notational matters: vectors are boldface; dyadics
[
4
,
10
]
are double underlined;
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}
is the free-space permittivity;

\underline{\underline{I}}=\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}+\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}+\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}
is the identity dyadic with
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}
,
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}
, and
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}
being the unit vectors aligned with the coordinate axes of the Cartesian coordinate
system
(x,y,z)
.

2
Homogenization – theoretical matters

Consider a composite medium
comprising a
random distribution of identical, similarly oriented, electromagnetically small inclusions surrounded by a host medium.
The inclusion medium has relative permittivity scalar
{\varepsilon_{\rm in}}
, and the host medium

{\varepsilon_{\rm h}}
.
The volume fraction of the composite material occupied by the inclusions is denoted by
f_{\rm in}
.

The Maxwell Garnett formalism delivers the following estimate of the relative permittivity dyadic of homogenized composite material:

\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{MG}={\varepsilon_{\rm h}}\,\underline{\underline{I}}+{\frac{f_{\rm in}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}}\,\underline{\underline{a}}\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }\left(\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\varepsilon_{\rm h}}f_{\rm in}\,\underline{\underline{a}}\right)^{-1}.

(1)

Herein the polarizability density dyadic

\underline{\underline{a}}=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\left(\varepsilon_{\rm in}-\varepsilon_{\rm h}\right)\left[\underline{\underline{I}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\rm in}-\varepsilon_{\rm h}}{\varepsilon_{\rm h}}\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})\right]^{-1};

(2)

and the depolarization dyadic

\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{S_{\rm in}}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm n}({\bf r}^{\prime})\frac{{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{\rm o}}{|{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{\rm o}|^{3}}\,d^{2}{\bf r}^{\prime}

(3)

is an integral over the
surface
S_{\rm in}
of an inclusion, with
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm n}
being the unit outward normal vector to
S_{\rm in}

and
{\bf r}_{\rm o}
being the position vector of a point inside the inclusion. Details leading to Eq. (
1
) are provided in the

Supplementary Material
.

3
Numerical results and discussion

We used Eq. (
1
) to calculate the results presented in this section. Since
\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})
is diagonalizable,
both
\underline{\underline{a}}
and
\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{MG}
also are. In addition, all three dyadics have the same set of eigenvectors. This fact
can be used to obtain simple expressions for the three eigenvalues of
\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{MG}
, if desired.

The inclusion shapes were taken to be that of truncated spheres, spheroids, and ellipsoids. For the cases of truncated spheres and spheroids,
closed-form expressions for
\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})
were recently derived
[
20
]
;
these cases are
represented schematically in Fig.
1
. For each type of inclusion shown in this figure,

\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})
was derived with

{\bf r}_{\rm o}
specifying the center of the largest sphere inscribed inside the inclusion.

Without loss of generality, the rotational symmetry axis of the truncated spheres, spheroids, and ellipsoids was fixed as the
z
coordinate axis; i.e., all truncation planes were parallel to the
xy
plane.
The three eigenvectors of
\underline{\underline{L}}\equiv\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})
are
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}
,
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}
, and
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}
for all inclusions due to the chosen shapes. Accordingly,

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}\underline{\underline{L}}={L_{\rm x}}\,\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}+{L_{\rm y}}\,\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}+{L_{\rm z}}\,\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}\\
\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{MG}=\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm x}\,\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm x}+\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm y}\,\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm y}+\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\,\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}\end{array}\right\};

(4)

with
{L_{\rm x}}={L_{\rm y}}\equiv L_{\rm t}
and
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm x}=\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm y}\equiv\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}
for truncated spheres and truncated spheroids.

For the purposes of numerical illustration, the representative values
{\varepsilon_{\rm h}}=3
,
{\varepsilon_{\rm in}}=2+0.5i
, and
f_{\rm in}=0.3
were selected,
with higher values of
f_{\rm in}
being incompatible with the Maxwell Garnett formalism
[
4
,
1
]
. All inclusions are taken to be aligned in parallel, but orientational statistics can be easily incorporated as needed
[
21
]
.

3.1
Inclusions based on spherical geometry

The inclusion shape is specified relative to the unit sphere, centered at the coordinate origin.

3.1.1
Truncated sphere

The inclusion shape is that of the upper part of the sphere which is
bounded below by the plane
z=1-2\kappa
, where
0<\kappa<1
, as schematically illustrated in Fig.
1(a)
.
Thus,
\kappa
represents the radius of the largest sphere that can be inscribed inside the truncated sphere.
In this case,
{L_{\rm z}}=1-2L_{\rm t}
and

L_{\rm t}=\frac{\kappa}{6\left(1-\kappa\right)^{3}\tau}\,\left\{6-\tau\left(3-3\kappa+\kappa^{2}\right)-\kappa\left[11-3\kappa\left(3-\kappa\right)\right]\right\}\,,

(5)

where

\tau=\sqrt{\left(4-3\kappa\right)\kappa}\,.

(6)

Plots of the
real and imaginary parts of the
relative permittivity parameters
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}
and
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}
of the HCM versus
\kappa
are provided in Fig.
2
. As
\kappa
increases,
the values of
\mbox{Re}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}
and

\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\right\}

decrease monotonically whereas the values of

\mbox{Re}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\right\}
and

\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}

increase monotonically. For all
\kappa<1
,
\mbox{Re}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}>\mbox{Re}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\right\}
and
\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\right\}>\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}
.
The differences between
\mbox{Re}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}
and

\mbox{Re}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\right\}
and between

\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}
and

\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}\right\}

decrease monotonically as
\kappa
increases, and these differences vanish in the limit
\kappa\to 1
. That is, the degree of anisotropy that the HCM exhibits decreases monotonically as
\kappa
increases and the HCM becomes isotropic
in the limit
\kappa\to 1
.

Double-truncated sphere

In this case,

In Fig.

real and imaginary parts of the

\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}
and
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}

As is the case for the truncated sphere presented in §
3.1.1

The plots of
the real and imaginary parts of
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}
and
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}

for the double-truncated sphere
in Fig.
3
are similar to the corresponding plots in Fig.
2
for the truncated sphere. Differences are
most conspicuous in the regime of small values of
\kappa

wherein
the real and imaginary parts of
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}
and
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}

change less rapidly as
\kappa
increases
for the double-truncated sphere.

Inclusions based on spheroidal geometry

The inclusion shape is specified relative to the spheroid

\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}+z^{2}\leq 1,

(8)

\alpha>0

Hemispheroid

The inclusion shape is that of the upper half of the spheroid; this shape lies between

z=0

The radius of the largest sphere that can be inscribed inside the hemispheroid

For

\displaystyle L_{\rm t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\nu\gamma^{4}}\left\{1+\nu+\alpha^{2}\left[\alpha\left(\alpha+\sqrt{2-\alpha^{2}}\right)-\nu-3\right]\right.

(9)

\displaystyle\left.-\left(\alpha\gamma\right)^{2}\sqrt{2-\alpha^{2}}\log\displaystyle{\frac{\left(1-\alpha^{2}+\gamma\right)\left(1-\alpha\right)}{\alpha\left(\nu-\gamma\right)}}\right\},

\gamma=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\end{array}\right\};

(10)

\displaystyle L_{\rm t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\frac{3\left(1+\sqrt{1+4\alpha^{2}}\right)+\alpha^{2}\left(10-8\sqrt{1+4\alpha^{2}}\right)-8\alpha^{4}}{4\gamma^{2}\left(3+8\alpha^{2}-16\alpha^{4}\right)}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle{+\frac{\alpha^{2}\log\displaystyle{\frac{1-2\alpha^{2}+\gamma}{\sqrt{1+3\alpha^{2}-4\alpha^{4}}-1}}}{4\gamma^{3}}}\,.

For all
\alpha

Plots of
the
real and imaginary parts of the

\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}
and
\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm z}
of the HCM

\alpha

As

\mbox{Im}\left\{\varepsilon^{MG}_{\rm t}\right\}

4
Closing remarks

In this paper, the Maxwell Garnett formalism was implemented to estimate the relative permittivity parameters of HCMs arising from
an isotropic host medium impregnated with isotropic
inclusions of relatively complex shapes. The inclusion shapes considered
were truncated spheres, spheroids, and ellipsoids. In so doing, the anisotropy of the HCM was related to the geometry of the inclusions: in general, the more the shape of the inclusions deviates from spherical, the greater is the degree of anisotropy exhibited by the HCM.
The availability of closed-form expressions for depolarization dyadics for inclusions shaped as truncated spheres and spheroids
[
20
]
increases considerably the reach of the Maxwell Garnett formalism, allowing more complex HCMs to be theoretically analyzed. Additionally, this formalism can be used for more realistic modeling of poultry dust
[
22
]
, house dust
[
23
,
24
]
, cements
[
25
]
,
volcanic ash
[
26
,
27
]
, mineral dust
[
28
,
29
]
, and interstellar dust
[
30
]
,
which are presently modeled very simply as agglomerations of complete ellipsoids
[
31
,
32
]
. Although we considered all inclusions in a composite medium to be identical and aligned in parallel, both of those restrictions can be easily surmounted
as shown elsewhere
[
21
,
33
]
.

The Maxwell Garnett formalism is usually implemented for cases involving spherical, spheroidal, and ellipsoidal inclusions. In such cases, the derivations of the corresponding polarizability density dyadics rest upon the assumption that electromagnetic field within each inclusion is spatially uniform. This assumption is physically reasonable provided that the inclusions are electrically small. In the preceding sections, this assumption was extended to truncated spheres, truncated spheroids, and truncated ellipsoids. For such truncated inclusions, this assumption is also physically reasonable provided that the inclusions are sufficiently small and that their shapes do not deviate too much from non-truncated spheres, spheroids, and ellipsoids.

Finally, all homogenization formalisms – including the Maxwell Garnett formalism – involve various assumptions and approximations. The acid test of any homogenization formalism can only come from comparison with suitable experimental data.

Introduction

In this document, the derivations of the polarizability density dyadic and the
Maxwell Garnett formalism are outlined, no novelty being claimed for their derivations.
The electrodynamic provenance of these theoretical constructions is emphasized.

, and
\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm z}
are the unit vectors aligned with the coordinate axes of the Cartesian coordinate
system
(x,y,z)
.

2
Polarizability density dyadic

A crucial ingredient in homogenization formalisms for particulate composite mediums is the polarizability density dyadic
of a solitary inclusion immersed in a host medium. Sometime in 1848, Mossotti
[
3
]

conceptualized the existence of the polarizability
density scalar of an electrically small, isotropic-dielectric sphere in free space. In this section, we present a clear derivation
of the polarizability density dyadic of an electrically small, isotropic-dielectric inclusion of quite general shape embedded in an isotropic
dielectric host medium. No novelty is implied for this derivation.

Let the closed surface
S_{\rm in}
separate the electrically small region
V_{\rm in}
from the region
V_{\rm h}
that extends to infinity in all directions. The shape
of
V_{\rm in}
must be such that
the unit outward normal

\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm n}({\bf r})
can be unambiguously identified at every point
{\bf r}\in S_{\rm in}
;
if necessary, wedges and vertexes on
S_{\rm in}
can be rounded off slightly to overcome this restriction.

Whereas
V_{\rm in}
is occupied by a homogeneous dielectric medium of relative permittivity
\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)
,
V_{\rm h}
is occupied by a homogeneous
dielectric medium of relative permittivity
\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)=n_{\rm h}^{2}(\omega)
.

Suppose that the region
V_{\rm in}
is irradiated by a monochromatic electromagnetic field phasor
{\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r},\omega)
whose source is confined
to the bounded region
V_{\rm s}\subset V_{\rm h}
far from
V_{\rm in}
. Then,
the actual electric field phasor
at any location
{\bf r}\notin V_{\rm s}
is given exactly by
[
4
,
5
,
6
]

{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r},\omega)+k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\iiint_{V_{\rm in}}\left[\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\right]\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,,\quad{\bf r}\notin V_{\rm s}\,,

(1)

where

\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)=\left[\underline{\underline{I}}+\frac{\nabla\nabla}{k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}\right]\frac{\exp\left[ik_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}n_{\rm h}(\omega)|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|\right]}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|}\,.

(2)

Setting
{\bf r}={\bf r}_{\rm o}
in Eq. (
1
), where

{\bf r}_{\rm o}\in V_{\rm in}
but
{\bf r}_{\rm o}\notin S_{\rm in}
, we get

{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)+k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\iiint_{V_{\rm in}}\left[\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\right]\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,,\quad{\bf r}_{\rm o}\in V_{\rm in}\,.

(3)

The integral on the right side of Eq. (
3
) is improper due to the appearance of the term
|{\bf r}_{\rm o}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{-3}
in the integrand

[
7
]
. In order to overcome this difficulty in a “unified and consistent” manner
[
8
]
, the term
\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm P}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
is added to and subtracted
from that integrand to obtain
[
9
]

\displaystyle{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)+k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\iiint_{V_{\rm in}}\left[\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf{\bf r}^{\prime}},\omega)-\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm P}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\right]\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad+k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\left[\iiint_{V_{\rm in}}\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm P}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\right]\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\,,\quad{\bf r}_{\rm o}\in V_{\rm in}\,,

(4)

where

\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm P}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}\nabla\nabla\frac{1}{4\pi|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|}\,.

(5)

In order to treat the second integral on the right side of Eq. (
4
), first the identity
\nabla\nabla\left(|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{-1}\right)=-\nabla\nabla^{\prime}\left(|{\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime}|^{-1}\right)
is invoked and then the Gauss theorem is used to obtain
[
8
,
4
]

{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)+k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\left[{\bf M}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)-\frac{1}{k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\right]\,,\quad{\bf r}_{\rm o}\in V_{\rm in}\,,

(6)

where

{\bf M}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)=\iiint_{V_{\rm in}}\left[\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf{\bf r}^{\prime}},\omega)-\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm P}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\right]\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,,

(7)

and

\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\iint_{S_{\rm in}}\hat{{\bf u}}_{\rm n}({\bf r}^{\prime})\frac{{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{\rm o}}{|{\bf r}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{\rm o}|^{3}}\,d^{2}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,.

(8)

Note that
\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})
is a symmetric dyadic whose trace equals unity
[
10
]
.

In the limit
{\bf r}^{\prime}\to{\bf r}_{\rm o}
, the singularity of
\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)
is offset by the singularity of
\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm P}({\bf r}_{\rm o},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)

in the integrand
of the volume integral
{\bf M}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
. Furthermore, this integral is not improper if it is assumed that
the electric field satisfies the Hölder
continuity condition in
V_{\rm in}

[
9
,
8
]
: real constants
\bar{\gamma}>0
and
\bar{\nu}>0
exist such that

|\hat{{\bf u}}_{j}\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }\left[{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)-{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\right]|\leq\bar{\gamma}|{\bf r}-{\bf r}_{\rm o}|^{\bar{\nu}}
for all
{\bf r}\in V_{\rm in}
and
j\in\left\{x,y,z\right\}
.

Provided that
V_{\rm in}
is sufficiently small in relation to the wavelengths in both mediums, we can set
{\bf E}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\simeq{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)

for all
{\bf r}^{\prime}\in V_{\rm in}
, as is commonly done
[
8
,
9
,
4
]
following Maxwell
[
11
]
and
has been computationally verified
[
8
]
. Then,
{\bf M}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
can be factored as

\underline{\underline{M}}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
and
Eq. (
6
) simplifies to

{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\simeq{\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)+k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\left[\underline{\underline{M}}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)-\frac{1}{k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})\right]\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\,,\quad{\bf r}_{\rm o}\in V_{\rm in}\,.

(9)

The concept of
\underline{\underline{M}}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
must be credited to Lorenz
[
12
,
13
]
.

Even more commonly, with good justification for really small
V_{\rm in}

[
4
,
14
]
,

\underline{\underline{M}}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
is ignored in favor
of
\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})/k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}

[
4
,
10
,
15
]
to get

{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\approx\left[\underline{\underline{I}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})\right]^{-1}\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}_{s}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\,,\quad{\bf r}_{\rm o}\in V_{\rm in}\,.

(10)

The electrically small region
V_{\rm in}
can be said to have acquired an electric dipole moment
[
4
]

{\bf p}(\omega)={v_{\rm in}}\,\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]{\bf E}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\,

(11)

located at
{\bf r}_{\rm o}

as the source of the field scattered into
V_{\rm h}
, with

v_{\rm in}
denoting the volume of
V_{\rm in}
. Substituting Eq. (
10
) in Eq. (
11
), we get

{\bf p}(\omega)={v_{\rm in}}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)\,,

(12)

where the polarizability density dyadic

\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\approx\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\left[\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)\right]\left[\underline{\underline{I}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{\rm in}(\omega)-\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}{\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}\underline{\underline{L}}({\bf r}_{\rm o})\right]^{-1}\,.

(13)

The right side of Eq. (
13
)
is definitely an approximation of
\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)
, even for spheres, for two reasons. The first reason is the removal of
\underline{\underline{M}}({\bf r}_{\rm o},\omega)
even though

V_{\rm in}
cannot be infinitesimally small. The second reason is that the Hölder continuity condition can best be applied inside the largest ellipsoid

[
11
]
centered at
{\bf r}_{\rm o}
and inscribed in
V_{\rm in}
. The electric field phasor in the portion of
V_{\rm in}
outside that inscribed ellipsoid having only been approximately accounted
for,
{\bf r}_{\rm o}
must be carefully chosen
[
16
]
. Regardless, Eq. (
13
) is implicitly used for cubes
[
17
,
18
]
, rectangular parallelopipeds
[
19
]
, and circular cylinders
of finite height
[
10
]
. Furthermore, the analog of Eq. (
13
) exists for quasi-two-dimensional problems
[
10
]
.

3
Maxwell Garnett homogenization formalism

Let us now briefly go through the Maxwell Garnett formalism in this section, no novelty being implied for the derivation.
Suppose that all space
V_{\rm all}
be occupied by a composite medium comprising a
random distribution of identical, similarly oriented, electromagnetically small inclusions surrounded by a host medium.
The inclusion medium has relative permittivity scalar
{\varepsilon_{\rm in}}(\omega)
, and the host medium

{\varepsilon_{\rm h}}(\omega)
. The volume fraction of the composite material occupied by the inclusions is denoted by
f_{\rm in}
. With
N_{\rm in}
denoting the number density of inclusions,
f_{\rm in}/N_{\rm in}
is the volume of
each inclusion.

The electric field in
V_{\rm all}
satisfies

\nabla\times\left[\nabla\times{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)\right]-k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega){\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)=i\omega\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}{\bf J}({\bf r},\omega)\,,\quad{\bf r}\in V_{\rm all}\,,

(14)

where the electric current density phasor
{\bf J}({\bf r},\omega)={\bf 0}
if
{\bf r}
lies in a region occupied by the host medium
but
{\bf J}({\bf r},\omega)\neq{\bf 0}
if
{\bf r}
lies inside an inclusion
[
4
,
20
]
.
Clearly, Eq. (
14
) represents a grainy visualization of the electromagnetic field in the composite medium. If this medium were
to be homogenized, then the HCM will have

{\bf D}({\bf r},\omega)=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega){\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)+{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r},\omega)=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{\rm eff}(\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)\,,

(15)

as its constitutive relation, with
{\bf D}({\bf r},\omega)
as the electric displacement phasor,
{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r},\omega)

as the excess polarization representing the
homogenized
distribution of the inclusion medium inside the HCM,
and
\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{\rm eff}(\omega)
as the effective relative permittivity dyadic of the HCM.

In the Maxwell Garnett homogenization formalism, Eq. (
14
) is replaced by

\nabla\times\left[\nabla\times{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)\right]-k_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega){\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)=i\omega\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}{\bf J}_{\rm avg}({\bf r},\omega)\,,\quad{\bf r}\in V_{\rm all}\,,

(16)

where
{\bf J}_{\rm avg}({\bf r},\omega)=-i\omega{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r},\omega)
is the spatial average of
{\bf J}({\bf r},\omega)
in some small neighborhood
of
{\bf r}
called the Lorentzian cavity
[
21
]
. Accordingly, the
electric field phasor in the HCM
is given by

{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm cf}({\bf r},\omega)+\omega^{2}\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\iiint_{V_{\rm all}}\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,,\quad{\bf r}\in V_{\rm all}\,,

(17)

where
{\bf E}_{\rm cf}({\bf r},\omega)
is the complementary function.

Let the electrically small spherical region
V_{\ell}
centered at
{\bf r}
be identified as the Lorentzian cavity. Then,
we can define

{\bf E}_{\rm loc}({\bf r},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm cf}({\bf r},\omega)+\omega^{2}\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\iiint_{V_{\rm all}-V_{\ell}}\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf P}_{xs}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}

(18)

as the electric field phasor present at
{\bf r}

if
the excess polarization were to be null-valued in
V_{\ell}
.
Equations (
17
) and (
18
) together yield

{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)={\bf E}_{\rm loc}({\bf r},\omega)+\omega^{2}\mu_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\iiint_{V_{\ell}}\underline{\underline{G}}_{\rm h}({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime};\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r}^{\prime},\omega)\,d^{3}{\bf r}^{\prime}\,.

(19)

The conventional assumption is that the excess polarization is uniform inside a Lorentzian cavity, so that
[
21
]

{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)\simeq{\bf E}_{\rm loc}({\bf r},\omega)-\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r},\omega)\,

(20)

emerges from Eq. (
19
).

In the Maxwell Garnett formalism, we set
{\bf P}_{\rm xs}=N_{\rm in}{\bf p}
and use Eq. (
12
) with
{\bf E}_{\rm loc}({\bf r},\omega)

fulfilling the role of
{\bf E}_{\rm s}({\bf r},\omega)
; accordingly,

{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r},\omega)=f_{\rm in}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}_{\rm loc}({\bf r},\omega)\,.

(21)

We then get

{\bf E}_{\rm loc}({\bf r},\omega)\simeq\left[\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}f_{\rm in}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\right]^{-1}\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)

(22)

from Eq. (
20
), followed by

{\bf P}_{\rm xs}({\bf r},\omega)\simeq f_{\rm in}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }\left[\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}f_{\rm in}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\right]^{-1}\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }{\bf E}({\bf r},\omega)

(23)

from Eq. (
21
).
The Maxwell Garnett estimate

\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{MG}(\omega)={\varepsilon_{\rm h}}(\omega)\,\underline{\underline{I}}+{\frac{f_{\rm in}}{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}}}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\mbox{ \tiny{${}^{\bullet}$} }\left[\underline{\underline{I}}-\frac{1}{3\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle 0}\varepsilon_{\rm h}(\omega)}f_{\rm in}\,\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)\right]^{-1}

(24)

of the relative permittivity dyadic
\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{eff}(\omega)
then emerges from Eqs. (
15
) and (
23
).

Note that the Maxwell Garnett formalism does not really depend on any explicit formula for
\underline{\underline{a}}(\omega)
, although
more than a century ago, Maxwell Garnett
himself
[
22
]
and others
[
23
]
had
used the Mossotti–Clausius expression
[
13
]
for the polarizability scalar of an electrically small, isotropic, dielectric sphere. Parenthetically,
even for that simple inclusion, other expressions have also been used
[
13
,
12
,
24
,
25
]
.
Finally, Eq. (
24
) for
\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{MG}
readily generalizes to cases involving inclusions of more than one type as well as to cases involving anisotropic inclusion materials
[
1
,
26
]
.
