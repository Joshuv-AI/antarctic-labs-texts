Expressions and characterizations for the Moore–Penrose inverse of operators and matrices
Patricia Mariela Morillas

Abstract
Under certain conditions, we prove that the Moore–Penrose inverse of a sum of operators is the sum of the Moore–Penrose inverses. From this, we derive expressions and characterizations for the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator that are useful for its computation. We give formulations of them for finite matrices and study the Moore–Penrose inverse of circulant matrices and of distance matrices of certain graphs.



or

keywords

Hilbert space, Moore–Penrose inverse, Circulant matrix, Distance
matrix, Weighted tree, Wheel graph.

Introduction

A generalization of the concept of inverse for matrices was first
introduced by Moore
[
1
,
2
]
. Then,
this generalized inverse was independently reintroduced and studied
by Bjerhammer
[
3
]
and Penrose
[
4
]
. The now commonly called
Moore–Penrose inverse
has
been also defined and studied for operators in Hilbert spaces (see
e.g.,
[
5
,
6
]
and the references
therein). It has numerous applications to physics, statistics,
optimization theory, solution of differential and integral
equations, prediction theory, control system analysis, etc. For more
details see, e.g.,
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces over

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. Let

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
denotes the set of
bounded linear operators from
\mathcal{H}_{1}
to

\mathcal{H}_{2}
. The set of elements in

\mathcal{B}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
with closed
range will be denoted with
\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
. Given
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
, we denote the adjoint, the null space, and the
range of
A
by
A^{*}
,
{\rm N}(A)
and
{\rm R}(A)
,
respectively. If
A\in\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
, then
\mathcal{H}_{1}={\rm N}\!\left(A\right)\oplus{\rm R}\!\left(A^{*}\right)
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}={\rm R}\!\left(A\right)\oplus{\rm N}\!\left(A^{*}\right)
, where
\oplus
denotes orthogonal
sum. If
\mathcal{W}
is a closed subspace of a Hilbert space, then

P_{\mathcal{W}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto

\mathcal{W}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
. The unique solution
X\in\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1}\right)
of the
system of operator equations

(1)\,AXA=A,\,\,(2)\,XAX=X,\,\,(3)\,\left(AX\right)^{*}=AX\text{ and }\,\,(4)\,\left(XA\right)^{*}=XA,

is called the
Moore–Penrose inverse
of
A
and is denoted by

A^{{\dagger}}
. Any
X\in\mathcal{B}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1}\right)
that satisfies (1), (3) and (4) is known as a

\{1,3,4\}
-inverse
of
A
.

One of the most important properties used in the applications of the
Moore–Penrose inverse is that the
minimal norm least squares
problem

\displaystyle{\min\left\|x\right\|}
subject to
\displaystyle{\left\|Ax-g\right\|=\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{1}}\left\|Af-g\right\|}
,

has the unique solution
x=A^{{\dagger}}g
.

We note that if
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
, then
A^{{\dagger}}
exists if and only if
{\rm R}\!\left(A\right)
is closed, and
{\rm R}\!\left(A\right)
is closed if and
only if
{\rm R}\!\left(A^{*}\right)
is closed. Let
A\in\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
. Then

\left(A^{{\dagger}}\right)^{{\dagger}}=A
and

\left(A^{*}\right)^{{\dagger}}=\left(A^{{\dagger}}\right)^{*}
. If
A
is invertible,
then
A^{{\dagger}}=A^{-1}
. The Moore–Penrose inverse is characterized
in the following theorem (see
[
5
, Theorem]
or

[
7
, Theorem 9.3]
):

Theorem 2
.

If
A\in\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
,
then
A^{{\dagger}}\in\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{1}\right)
is the unique solution of anyone of the following
equivalent systems:

(i)

AX=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A\right)}
,
{\rm N}\!\left(X^{*}\right)={\rm N}\!\left(A\right)
.

(ii)

AX=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A\right)}
,
XA=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A^{*}\right)}
,
XAX=X
.

(iii)

XAA^{*}=A^{*}
,
XX^{*}A^{*}=X
.

(iv)

XAP_{{\rm R}\!\left(A^{*}\right)}=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A^{*}\right)}
,
XP_{{\rm N}\!\left(A^{*}\right)}=0
.

(v)

XA=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A^{*}\right)}
,
{\rm N}\!\left(X\right)={\rm N}\!\left(A^{*}\right)
.

(vi)

AX=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A\right)}
,
XA=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(X\right)}
.

2
Moore–Penrose inverses of sums of operators

The following theorem gives conditions under which the
Moore–Penrose inverse of a sum is the sum of the Moore–Penrose
inverses.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
. Assume that

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\right)
and

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for
each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
. Then:

(i)

A_{k}A_{k^{\prime}}^{{\dagger}}=0
and
A_{k}^{{\dagger}}A_{k^{\prime}}=0
for each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
.

(ii)

{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
and
{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)
.

(iii)

{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
and
{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)
.

(iv)

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}
exists and
\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{{\dagger}}
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows from the inclusions
{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{{\dagger}}\right)={\rm R}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
and

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)={\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}^{{\dagger}}\right)
for each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
.

Since
{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\right)

for each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
, we get
{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\perp{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
. Then, we have the orthogonal sum

\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
. Clearly,
{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)\subseteq\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
and
\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)
.

Let
g\in\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
. Then there
exist
f_{1},\ldots,f_{K}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
such that

g=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}f_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)}f_{k}
. Let
f=\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)}f_{k}
. Using that
{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,

k\neq k^{\prime}
, we get

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)f=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)}f_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)}f_{k}=g.

Then
g\in{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)
. This
sows that
\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)
. Hence,
{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
. The equality
{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)
can be proved similarly. Therefore, (ii)
holds.

Let
k_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
. We have
f\in{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)
if and only if

A_{k_{0}}f=-\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)f\in{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)\cap{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)\cap{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)=\left\{0\right\}.

Thus,
f\in{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)
for each
k_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
. Therefore,
\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}\right)={\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)
. In a
similar way, we prove that
{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)
. This shows (iii).

By (ii),
{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)
is closed. Thus,

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}
exists. Using part now (i),
the rest of (iv) follows from Definition
1
.
∎

From the definition of
\{1,3,4\}
-inverse and Theorem
4
(i)(iv) we obtain the following result:

Corollary 5
.

Let
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
. Assume that

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\right)
and

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for
each
k,k^{\prime}=1,\ldots,K
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
. Then

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}
is a
\{1,3,4\}
-inverse of

A_{k_{0}}
for each
k_{0}=1,\ldots,K
.

From Theorem
4
, we now derive expressions
and characterizations for the Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator.
They are useful for its computation and part of them can be viewed
as an extension of Proposition
3
to a sum
of operators with closed range. Before we enunciate the results, we
note that, under the hypothesis of Theorem
4
, we always have
{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)
and
{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)
for each
k_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}\subset\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)
. Assume that

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\right)
and

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for
each
k,k^{\prime}=1,2,\ldots
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
. Let
k_{0}\in\left\{1,\ldots,K\right\}
. The following assertions hold:

(i)

A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)-\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}^{{\dagger}}
.

(ii)

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=A_{k_{0}}^{*}
and
A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}A_{k_{0}}^{*}
.

(iii)

{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)=\left\{0\right\}
if and only if

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)={\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)
.

(iv)

If
{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)=\left\{0\right\}
, then

\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}
is invertible.

Proof.

(i): From Theorem
4
(iv) and
Proposition
3
(i), we obtain

\displaystyle A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}-\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)-\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)-\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}^{{\dagger}}.

(ii): Using Theorem
4
(i) we get,

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=A_{k_{0}}^{*}A_{k_{0}}A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=A_{k_{0}}^{*}P_{{\rm R}\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)}=A_{k_{0}}^{*}.

From this equality,

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}A_{k_{0}}^{*}.

(2.1)

Since

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)=P_{{\rm R}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)}

and, by Theorem
4
(ii),

{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)={\rm R}\!\left(\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)\right)={\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}\right)=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{K}{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)\supseteq{\rm R}\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)={\rm R}\left(A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}\right),

equality (
2.1
) becomes

A_{k_{0}}^{{\dagger}}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}^{*}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}A_{k_{0}}^{*}.

(iii): Assume that
{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\right)=\left\{0\right\}
.
Let
f\in{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}\right)
. Since

f=P_{{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)}f+P_{{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)}f
and
{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k}\right)
for
k\neq k_{0}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}P_{{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)}f=\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}P_{{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)}f=\sum_{k=1,k\neq k_{0}}^{K}A_{k}f=0.

Consequently,
P_{{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}\right)}f=0
and
f\in{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k_{0}}^{*}\right)
. This shows that

Proposition 11
.

Let
\left\{A_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{BC}\!\left(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}\right)

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\right)

{\rm R}\!\left(A_{k}^{*}\right)\subseteq{\rm N}\!\left(A_{k^{\prime}}\right)
for

,

{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\right)=\left\{0\right\}

exists
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
A_{k}=0
for each
k>K_{0}
.

Proof.

Assume that
{\rm N}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\right)=\left\{0\right\}
and

{\rm R}\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\right)
is closed. Then

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\right)^{{\dagger}}
exists.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
. We have

\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}-\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}A_{k}.

(2.2)

. There exists

such that

This shows that

{\rm R}\left(\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}A_{k}\right)\subseteq{\rm R}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\right),

(2.3)

for each

Let

4
Moore–Penrose inverse of circulant matrices

In this section, we consider circulant matrices of order
n\geq 2
,

C={\rm circ}\!\left(c\right)
where
c\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
(see

[
22
,
23
,
24
]
). For example,
if
n=2,3
we have,

C=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}c(1)&c(2)\\
c(2)&c(1)\\
\end{array}\right),\,\,\,\,\,C=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}c(1)&c(2)&c(3)\\
c(3)&c(1)&c(2)\\
c(2)&c(3)&c(1)\\
\end{array}\right).

If
\Pi={\rm circ}\!\left(0,1,0,\ldots,0\right)
then

\Pi^{-1}=\Pi^{n-1}=\Pi^{t}={\rm circ}\!\left(0,\ldots,0,1\right)
and

C={\rm circ}\!\left(c\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c(k+1)\Pi^{k}
. If
\rho:\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
is given by

\left(\rho(c)\right)\!(1)=c(1)
and
\left(\rho(c)\right)(k)=c(n-k+2)
for

k=2,\ldots,n
, then
C^{t}={\rm circ}\!\left(\rho(c)\right)
.

If
{\rm circ}\!\left(c\right)={\rm circ}\!\left(a\right){\rm circ}\!\left(b\right)
, then
{\rm circ}\!\left(c\right)={\rm circ}\!\left(b\right){\rm circ}\!\left(a\right)
and

c(l)=\sum_{k=1}^{l}a(k)b(l-k+1)+\sum_{k=l+1}^{n}a(k)b(n+l-k+1)

(4.7)

for
l=1,\ldots,n
, or equivalently,
c={\rm circ}\!\left(\rho(a)\right)b
.

Remark 19
.

If
0\leq l\leq n-1
and
C
is a circulant matrix, then
{\rm N}\!\left(\Pi^{l}C\right)={\rm N}\!\left(C\right)
and, by the reverse-order
law for the Moore–Penrose inverse (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Chapter 4, Ex.
22]
),

\left(\Pi^{l}C\right)^{{\dagger}}=C^{{\dagger}}\Pi^{n-l}
.

In
[
29
,
30
]
, the coefficients of the
inverse and the group inverse of a circulant matrix depending on up
to four complex parameters, i.e.
{\rm circ}\!\left(a,b,c,d,\ldots,d\right)
, are expressed in terms of
functions
k_{j}(a,b,c,d)
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. In particular, in the
case of four parameters these functions involves Chebyshev
polynomials. The group inverse of a circulant matrix coincides with
its Moore–Penrose inverse. The techniques used in these papers are
related with the solution of boundary value problems associated to
second-order linear difference equations. Here we use results of the
previous sections to obtain properties and explicit expressions of
the Moore–Penrose inverse of circulant matrices.

Consider circulant matrices of the form

C=\alpha\Pi^{k-1}+\beta\Pi^{k}

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
and

. Then

C=\Pi^{k-1}\left(\alpha I+\beta\Pi\right)
. Similarly,
C=\alpha I+\beta\Pi^{n-1}=\Pi^{n-1}\left(\beta I+\alpha\Pi\right)
. Hence, by
Remark
19
, in both cases in order to study
C^{{\dagger}}

it is sufficient to consider circulant matrices of the form

. Part (ii) of the following lema can be
obtained from
[

2.3]
with parameters

,
b=0
and

. We include here a direct brief proof.

Lemma 20
.

The following assertions hold:

(i)

If
n

C_{1,1}={\rm circ}\!\left(1,1,0,\ldots,0\right)

\left(C_{1,1}+v*v^{t}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}{\rm circ}\!\left(w\right).

(ii)

If

Proof.

(i) Let
{\rm circ}\!\left(a\right):=\left(C_{1,1}+v*v^{t}\right){\rm circ}\!\left(w\right)

obtain,

\displaystyle a(1)

\displaystyle=2\left(n^{2}-n+2\right)+\sum_{k=2}^{n-1}\left(-1\right)^{n-k+3}\left(-1\right)^{k+1}\left(n^{2}-\left(2k-1\right)n+2\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(n^{2}-n+2\right)+\left(n-2\right)\left(n^{2}+n+2\right)-2n\left(\frac{(n-1)n}{2}-1\right)=2n^{2},

and for

\displaystyle a(l)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{l-2}\left(-1\right)^{l-k+2}\left(-1\right)^{k+1}\left(n^{2}-\left(2k-1\right)n+2\right)+2\left(-1\right)^{l+1}\left(n^{2}-\left(2l-1\right)n+2\right)+

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=l+1}^{n}\left(-1\right)^{n+l-k+2}\left(-1\right)^{k+1}\left(n^{2}-\left(2k-1\right)n+2\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-1\right)^{l+1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(n^{2}-\left(2k-1\right)n+2\right)+\left(n^{2}-\left(2l-1\right)n+2\right)-\left(n^{2}-\left(2\left(l-1\right)-1\right)n+2\right)\right)

This shows that
\left(C_{1,1}+v*v^{t}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}{\rm circ}\!\left(w\right)
.

(ii) Let
B=-\frac{1}{2n}{\rm circ}\!\left(1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1\right)+\frac{n^{2}+1}{2n^{2}}ee^{t}
.
Using that

\displaystyle\left(C_{1,-1}+ee^{t}\right)B

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2n}C_{1,-1}{\rm circ}\!\left(1,3,5,\ldots,2n-1\right)-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(2k-1\right)ee^{t}+\frac{n^{2}+2}{2n}ee^{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2n}{\rm circ}\!\left(2-2n,2,2,\ldots,2\right)+\frac{1}{n}ee^{t}={\rm circ}\!\left(1,0,\ldots,0\right)=I.

Hence

.
∎

The Fourier matrix of order

C=\overline{F}\Lambda F,\,\,\,\,C^{{\dagger}}=\overline{F}\Lambda^{{\dagger}}F,

\lambda=\sqrt{n}\overline{F}c\text{ and }c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}F\lambda.

(4.8)

where

is the diagonal of

4

support

Theorem 22
.

Let
\left\{c_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{K}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

\lambda_{k}=\sqrt{n}\overline{F}c_{k}

\widetilde{\lambda_{k}}=\sqrt{n}\overline{F}\rho\!\left(c_{k}\right)

Example 23
.

Let
n
be even. Let

.

. We

Proposition 24

Let

(i)

Part (ii) of the following proposition can be used to compute

.

Let

and

(i)

(ii)

Hence,

Taking into account that

5.2

Let

of

e&{\rm circ}(u)\\
\end{array}\right).

The following proposition is about
{\rm null}(D)
and
D^{{\dagger}}
.

Proposition 27
.

Let
n\geq 5
odd. Let

be the distance matrix of the wheel
graph with
n

and

D^{{\dagger}}=\left(D+aa^{t}\right)^{-1}-\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}aa^{t}.

(5.15)

Proof.

Clearly,
{\rm span}\{a\}\subseteq{\rm null}(D)
. Let
x\in{\rm null}(D)
. Since
x^{t}D(1,:)=0
,
\sum_{j=2}^{n}x(j)=0
. We have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}D(i,:)=(n-1,3+2(n-3),\ldots,3+2(n-3))
. Since

x^{t}D(i,:)=0
for
i=2,\ldots,n
,

x(2)=-\frac{x(n)+x(3)}{2},

(5.16)

x(j)=-\frac{x(j-1)+x(j+1)}{2},\text{ for $j=3,\ldots,n-1$},

(5.17)

x(n)=-\frac{x(n-1)+x(2)}{2},

(5.18)

and

0=x^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}D(i,:)\right)=\left(n-1\right)x(1)+\left(3+2(n-3)\right)\sum_{j=2}^{n}x(j)=\left(n-1\right)x(1)
.

From the last equality,
x(1)=0
, and from (
5.16
)-(
5.17
),

x(j)=\left(-1\right)^{j}\left(\left(j-1\right)x(2)+\left(j-2\right)x(n)\right)
for

j=3,\ldots,n
. In particular,

x(n)=-\left(\left(n-1\right)x(2)+\left(n-2\right)x(n)\right)
. Therefore,

x(n)=-x(2)
and, more generally,
x(j)=\left(-1\right)^{j}x(2)
for

j=3,\ldots,n
. This shows that
{\rm null}(D)={\rm span}\{a\}
.
Now, from Corollary
13
(iii), we obtain
(
5.15
).
∎
