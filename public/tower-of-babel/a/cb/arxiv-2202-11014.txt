Global Solutions to Nonconvex Problems by Evolution of Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs
Howard Heaton Samy Wu Fung Stanley Osher Typal Research Dept. of Applied Mathematics and Statistics Dept. of Mathematics Typal LLC Colorado School of Mines UCLA

Abstract
Computing tasks may often be posed as optimization problems. The objective functions for real-world scenarios are often nonconvex and/or nondifferentiable. State-of-the-art methods for solving these problems typically only guarantee convergence to local minima. This work presents Hamilton-Jacobi-based Moreau Adaptive Descent (HJ-MAD), a zero-order algorithm with guaranteed convergence to global minima, assuming continuity of the objective function. The core idea is to compute gradients of the Moreau envelope of the objective (which is “piece-wise convex”) with adaptive smoothing parameters. Gradients of the Moreau envelope ( i.e. proximal operators) are approximated via the Hopf-Lax formula for the viscous Hamilton-Jacobi equation. Our numerical examples illustrate global convergence.

Standard data-oriented tasks such as neural network training, parameter estimation in physical models, and phase recovery may be cast as optimization problems.
These problems are often highly non-convex, and practical schemes to solve for global minimizers are scarce.
State-of-the-art methods are often either impractical
[
39
]
, heuristic
[
36
,
44
]
, or only guarantee convergence to local minima
[
10
,
25
]
.

In this work, we introduce a zero-order algorithm with guaranteed convergence to global minima.
Our approach minimizes Moreau envelopes of objective functions (
e.g.
see Figure
1
).
To compute gradients of the Moreau envelope, we leverage a connection to Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) partial differential equations (PDEs) via the Hopf-Lax formula. This yields an explicit solution to the HJ equation from the Cole-Hopf formula, giving the name Hamilton-Jacobi-based Moreau Adaptive Descent (HJ-MAD). These gradients take the form of expectation formulas that can be estimated via sampling.

Connections to Hamilton-Jacobi PDEs

When the envelope
u
is differentiable at
x^{k}
, its gradient is precisely
g^{k}

(see Lemma
A.3
)
,
i.e.

\displaystyle g^{k}=\dfrac{x^{k}-\hat{x}^{k}}{t_{k}}=\nabla u(x^{k},t_{k}),\ \ \ \mbox{where}\ \ \ \hat{x}^{k}=\mathrm{prox}_{t_{k}f}(x^{k}).

(5)

Although the idea to use gradients of
u(x^{k},t_{k})
is simple, computing
\hat{x}^{k}
and
g^{k}
can be as difficult as the original problem (
1
).
This difficulty can be (approximately) circumvented by using a PDE formulation.
The envelope
u
is a special case of the Hopf-Lax formula
[
18
]
for PDEs. It can be shown (
e.g.
see
[
19
, Theorem 3.2]
) that
u
is a viscous solution to Burgers’ Hamilton-Jacobi equation
2
2

2

We call (
6
)
Burgers’
since, if
\lim\limits_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|u|=0
, the original Burgers’ PDE is obtained via integration by parts.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}u_{t}+\dfrac{1}{2}\|Du\|^{2}&=0&\mbox{in\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,T]$}\\
u&=f&\mbox{on\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\{t=0\}$}.\end{array}\right.

(6)

The key step in obtaining an explicit expression for each subgradient is to approximate the solution
u
to (
6
) by adding a small amount of viscosity via a Laplacian term. Namely, fixing
\delta>0
, we approximate solutions to (
6
) via the solution
u^{\delta}
of the associated viscous Burgers’ equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}u_{t}^{\delta}+\frac{1}{2}\|Du^{\delta}\|^{2}&=\frac{\delta}{2}\Delta u^{\delta}&\mbox{in\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,T]$}\\[5.0pt]
u^{\delta}&=f&\mbox{on\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\{t=0\}$}.\end{array}\right.

(7)

A key result justifying this approximation is that of Crandall and Lions
[
12
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\delta,T\in(0,\infty)
and
f
is bounded and Lipschitz, then there is
C\in(0,\infty)
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left|u(x,t)-u^{\delta}(x,t)\right|\leq C\sqrt{\delta}.

(8)

This establishes uniform convergence
u^{\delta}\rightarrow u
as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
Although
f
is not necessarily bounded and Lipschitz continuous on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the sequence
\{x^{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
is bounded (see Lemma
A.7
).
Thus, the above result still applies since
f
is bounded and Lipschitz continuous on a compact domain containing
\{x^{k}\}
.
Consequently, for
\delta>0
sufficiently small, one is, for all practical purposes, justified in using Algorithm
2
to estimate solutions to (
1
).

Using the transformation
v^{\delta}\triangleq\exp(-u^{\delta}/\delta)
, originally attributed to Cole and Hopf
[
18
,
11
]
, it follows that
v^{\delta}
solves the heat equation,
i.e.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}v^{\delta}_{t}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{\delta}{2}}\Delta v^{\delta}&=0&\mbox{in\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,T]$}\\
v^{\delta}&=\exp(-f/\delta)&\mbox{on\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\{t=0\}$}.\end{array}\right.

(9)

This transformation to
v^{\delta}
is of particular interest since
v^{\delta}
can be expressed via the convolution formula (
e.g.
see
[
18
]
for a derivation)

v^{\delta}(x,t)=\Big(\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}*\exp(-f/\delta)\Big)(x),

(10)

where
\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta t}}
is the fundamental solution to the heat equation in (
9
),
i.e.

\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}(x)\triangleq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}\pi{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t)^{-n/2}\cdot\exp\Large(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}\Large)&\mbox{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,\infty)$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(11)

Thus, using algebraic manipulations, we recover the viscous Burgers’ solution

u^{\delta}(x,t)=-\delta\ln\Big(\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}*\exp(-f/\delta)\Big)(x)\ \ \mbox{in\ ${\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times(0,T]$}.

(12)

See Appendix
C
for an intuitive, informal argument that
u^{\delta}
expressed by (
12
) converges pointwise to the envelope
u
as
\delta\to 0
. Moreover, for
\delta
sufficiently small,
if
u
is diffierentiable at
(x,t)
, then

\nabla u(x,t)\approx\nabla u^{\delta}(x,t)=-\delta\cdot\nabla\left[\ln\left(v^{\delta}(x,t)\right)\right]=-\delta\cdot\dfrac{\nabla v^{\delta}(x,t)}{v^{\delta}(x,t)}.

(13)

Loosely speaking, (
13
) shows we can approximate
g^{k}
in Algorithm
1
using convolutions with the heat kernel
\Phi_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}
.
A more practical formula for the convolutions in (
13
) is given in Section

4
Time Evolution

To prevent the sequence
\{x^{k}\}
from converging to local minima that are not globally optimal, the HJ equation is evolved in time. The time stepping method we propose is similar in spirit to trust region methods.
If the gradient
\nabla u
is small, then time is increased to get out of non-global local minima. If the gradient is relatively large, then time is reduced to utilize the local landscape. In mathematical terms,
for
0<\eta_{-}<1<\eta_{+}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0<\theta_{1}\leq\theta_{2}<1}
,
and
\varepsilon>0
,
the time stepping rule is defined by

\mbox{TimeStep}(t,p,q;\tau,T)\triangleq\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\big(\eta_{+}t,T\big)&\mbox{if}\ \|p\|\leq\theta_{1}\|q\|+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon}\\[7.0pt]
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mbox{else if}\ \|p\|\leq\theta_{2}\|q\|+\varepsilon}\\[7.0pt]
\max\big(\eta_{-}t,\tau\big)&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\end{cases}

(14)

Algorithms
1
and
2
evolve time using the above time stepping rule with
t=t_{k}
,
p=g^{k}
and
q=g^{k-1}
.

Remark 4.1
.

Other updates can be used to generate
\{t_{k}\}
. We restrict our presentation to that above for simplicity. Future work may investigate convergence improvements with other time step rules.

Gradient Estimation

Expectation Formulation

An essential property of any optimization algorithm is it scales well with dimension
n
.
At first glance, the expression for
\nabla u^{\delta}
in (
13
) consists of two convolutions, which require evaluating
f
over all of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
This is an intractable task; however, the heat kernel coincides with the probability density of a Gaussian distribution with mean
x
and standard deviation
\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}
.
This enables the convolutions to be written as expectations, which can be approximated via sampling (
e.g.
see
[
20
]
for an overview of sampling methods).
Namely, setting
{\mathbb{P}}_{x,t}\triangleq{\cal N}(x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t)
yields
3
3

3

For completeness, we include a brief derivation of this connection in Appendix
B
.

\nabla u^{\delta}(x,t)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dfrac{1}{t}\cdot}\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}x}-\dfrac{{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}}\left[{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y\cdot}\exp\left(-\delta^{-1}{f}(y)\right)\right]}{{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim{\mathbb{P}}_{x,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta}t}}\left[\exp\left(-\delta^{-1}{f}(y)\right)\right]}\right).

(15)

When
t
is small, a few samples are needed to adequately estimate the expectation in (
15
) since the heat kernel is concentrated for small time.

When
t
is large, many samples are
needed
. However, it is worth noting no grids are required when solving the HJ equation
[
37
,
38
,
32
]
.

Exponentially Weighted Moving Average

To reduce the number of samples needed to adequately estimate gradients, especially when
n
and/or
t
are large, moving averages of gradients can be used.
This allows samples from previous steps to estimate the gradient at the current step.
This is similar to ADAM’s
[
25
]
variance reduction, which estimates first moments via a moving average of gradients.

Limitations

Some limitations of HJ-MAD require careful consideration.
First, since HJ-MAD relies on smoothing the objective function, it is expected to not perform as well on functions that are very flat and smooth. Second, while the method is guaranteed to converge to the global minimizer, it does not necessarily outperform some of the state-of-the-art methods.
We simply present a method with theoretical guarantees, whereas several current methods are mostly heuristic.
Finally, as in most algorithms, the choice of parameters (in this case, time update parameters) is problem-dependent.

Related Works

Global Optimization Algorithms

Random Search Methods
[
39
]
are derivative-free methods that iteratively move to better positions in the search-space.
Pure Random Search (PRS)
[
6
]
samples points from the domain independently and set the point with lowest function value to be the next iterate.
Differential Evolution (DE)
[
44
]
uses a population/batch of candidate solutions that are moved around using a simple formula. If the new position of an agent is an improvement then it is accepted and forms part of the population, otherwise the new position is simply discarded. The method is iterative and heuristic. It is similar to PRS and requires many function evaluations. Moreover, there are no convergence guarantees. Basin-hopping (BH)
[
46
,
36
]
is a two-phase method that iterates by performing random perturbation of coordinates, performing local optimization, and accepting or rejecting new coordinates based on a minimized function value. There are no convergence guarantees.
Simulated Annealing
[
26
]
is a stochastic, heuristic method that starts at a randomized point
x
in
the parameter space, and then evaluates a neighboring point
x^{\prime}
, usually chosen at random. In its simplest form, if the value of the objective
function is lesser at the new point, the new point is accepted, and the process is repeated.
If the value at the new point is greater, the point is chosen with some acceptance probability
P
; this allows the currently best point considered by the algorithm to zero in on optima in
f
, but to escape local optima.

A method with comparable convergence guarantees to our work is the DIRECT (DIviding RECTangles)
[
21
]
method, which partitions a bound-constrained domain into
2n+1
hyper-rectangles with an evaluated point at the centre of each. Each hyper-rectangle is scored via a combination of the length of its longest side and the function value at its centre. This scoring favors hyper-rectangles exhibiting both long sides and small function values; the best-scoring hyper-rectangles are further divided.

Moreau Envelope Minimization

Our work relates to entropy gradient descent (EGD)
[
10
,
11
]
, which fixes a time
T
for which to compute the Moreau envelope.
There gradients of the Moreau envelope are approximated using a subroutine involving (stochastic) gradient descent. A key difference is HJ-MAD is a zero-order method. Moreover, HJ-MAD does not require a subroutine and instead leverages the Cole-Hopf
[

]

15

[
41
]
(albeit without the HJ PDE). Finally, many works also consider minimization of the Moreau envelope, either for weakly convex functions
[
14
,
15
]
, or escaping saddle points of the Moreau envelope
[
13
]
.

Other zero-order methods include derivative-free quasi-Newton methods

8
Conclusion

We propose a Hamilton-Jacobi-based Moreau Adaptive Descent (HJ-MAD) method for finding global solutions to optimization problems.
HJ-MAD is a zero-order algorithm that does not require
differentiable objective functions
.
Our approach is based on two key ideas.
First, we use the Moreau envelope with sufficiently large time
t
such that its minimizers are global minimizers of the objective function.
Second, we leverage connections with Hamilton-Jacobi equations to obtain analytic expressions for the Moreau envelope and its gradient. In particular, we use the Cole-Hopf and Hopf-Lax formulas to
express
the gradient of the Moreau envelope as an expectation.
To make the sampling of these expectations more efficient, we include an adaptive time-stepping scheme.

Our work also provides a way to estimate proximal operators, in general, using connections to HJ equations.

In our experiments, HJ-MAD is the most efficient algorithm and
always manages to converge to the global minimizer. Future work may improve the efficiency of HJ-MAD.

\|z-x\|^{2}\leq 2t(f(x)-f(z)+1)\leq 2t(f(x)-f(x^{\star})+1),

(18)

and so
{\cal T}\subseteq B(x,2t[f(x)-f(x^{\star})+1])
,
i.e.

{\cal T}
is bounded.
Moreover, since
x\in{\cal T}
,
{\cal T}
is nonempty, and
{\cal T}
is closed because
f
is continuous.
Due to the fact the minimal value of
f
is a lower bound,
i.e.

f(z)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z-x\|^{2}\geq f(x^{\star})\ \ \ \mbox{for all $z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$,}

(19)

the set

\left\{f(z)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z-x\|^{2}:z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\right\}

(20)

has an infimum.
Since
x\in{\cal T}
, this infimum is less than or equal to
f(x)
.
Thus, there exists a sequence
\{z^{k}\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f(z^{k})+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z^{k}-x\|^{2}=\inf_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(z)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z-x\|^{2}.

(21)

By definition of this limit and the fact the infimum does not exceed
f(x)
, there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

f(z^{k})+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z^{k}-x\|^{2}\leq\left(\inf_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(z)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z-x\|^{2}\right)+1\leq f(x)+1,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $k\geq N$,}

(22)

and so
z^{k}\in{\cal T}
for
k\geq N
. This implies the sequence
\{z^{k}\}
is bounded, whereby the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem may be applied to deduce existence of a convergent subsequence
\{z^{n_{k}}\}\subseteq\{z^{k}\}
with limit
\hat{x}\in{\cal T}
(
n.b.

{\cal T}
is closed).
Then observe

f(\hat{x})+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f(z^{n_{k}})+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z^{n_{k}}-x\|^{2}=\inf_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(z)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z-x\|^{2},

(23)

which implies
\hat{x}\in\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
, and the proof is complete.
∎

This is a minor extension of a well-known result in convex analysis originating from Moreau
[
35
]
(
e.g.
see Proposition 12.30 in
[
2
]
, Theorem 6.60 in
[

]

Lemma A.3
.

Given a time
t\in(0,\infty)
, if Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold for
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{{\mathbb{R}}}
and if the envelope
u(\cdot,t)
of
f
is differentiable at
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then

\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
is a singleton set and

\nabla u(x,t)=\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},\ \ \ \mbox{where}\ \ \ \hat{x}\triangleq\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)=\argmin_{z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(z)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|z-x\|^{2}.

(26)

Proof.

By Lemma
A.1
, there exists
\hat{x}\in\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
. Fix any
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. By definition of the gradient,

\displaystyle\left<\nabla u(x,t),h\right>

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{u(x,t)-u(x-\lambda h,t)}{\lambda}

(27a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{f(\hat{x})+\frac{1}{2t}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}-u(x-\lambda h,t)}{\lambda}

(27b)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{f(\hat{x})+\frac{1}{2t}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}-f(\hat{x})-\frac{1}{2t}\|\hat{x}-(x-\lambda h)\|^{2}}{\lambda}

(27c)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{\frac{1}{t}\left<\hat{x}-x,-\lambda h\right>-\frac{1}{2t}\|\lambda h\|^{2}}{\lambda}

(27d)

\displaystyle=\left<\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},h\right>,

(27e)

where the equality (
27b
) holds because
\hat{x}\in\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
and the subsequent inequality (
27c
) follows from the fact
u(x-\lambda h,t)
is the infimum of
f(z)+\frac{1}{2t}\|z-(x-\lambda h)\|^{2}
among all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Since (
27
) holds for arbitrarily chosen
h
, this limit inequality holds over
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Consequently,

\left<\nabla u(x,t)-\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},z\right>\geq 0,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.$}

(28)

By way of contradiction, suppose the gradient formula in (
26
) does not hold,
i.e.

\nabla u(x,t)-\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t}\neq 0.

(29)

In such a case, taking

z=\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t}-\nabla u(x,t)

(30)

yields

0\leq\left<\nabla u(x,t)-\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},z\right>=-\left\|\nabla u(x,t)-\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t}\right\|^{2}<0,

(31)

which implies
0<0
, a contradiction. Thus, (
29
) must be false, from which the result (
26
) follows.

All that remains is to verify
\hat{x}
is the unique element of
\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
.
To this end, fix any
\hat{y}\in\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
and observe, repeating the same argument as for the gradient formula above,

\dfrac{x-\hat{y}}{t}=\nabla u(x,t)=\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t}\ \ \ \iff\ \ \ \hat{x}=\hat{y}.

(32)

That is,
\hat{y}\in\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
if and only if
\hat{y}=\hat{x}
, completing the proof.
∎

The following lemma is again a generalization of well-known results in convex analysis; here again the proof adapts arguments from

Lemma A.4
.

Given a time
t\in(0,\infty)
, if Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
holds for
f\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{{\mathbb{R}}}
and if
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a local minimizer of the envelope
u(\cdot,t)
of
f
, then
x
is a local minimizer of
f
,
u(x,t)=f(x)
,
x=\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x)
,
u(\cdot,t)
is differentiable at
x
, and

\nabla u(x,t)=0.

(33)

Proof.

Suppose
x
is a local minimizer of
u(\cdot,t)
. By Lemma
A.1
, there exists
\hat{x}\in\mathrm{prox}_{tf}(x).
We first show
x=\hat{x}
(Step 1), which implies
u(x,t)=f(x)
. This is used to verify
\nabla u(x,t)=0
(Step 2), and we conclude by showing
x
is a local minimizer of
f
(Step 3).

Step 1.

Fix any
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Since
x
is a local minimizer of
u
, there is
\overline{\lambda}>0
such that, for
\lambda\in(-\overline{\lambda},\overline{\lambda})
,

\displaystyle u(x-\lambda h,t)

\displaystyle\leq f(\hat{x})+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|\hat{x}-(x-\lambda h)\|^{2}

(34a)

\displaystyle=f(\hat{x})+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|\hat{x}-x\|^{2}+\left<\dfrac{\hat{x}-x}{t},\lambda h\right>+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2t}\|h\|^{2}

(34b)

\displaystyle=u(x,t)+\left<\dfrac{\hat{x}-x}{t},\lambda h\right>+\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2t}\|h\|^{2}.

(34c)

Upon rearrangement, we deduce

\left<\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},\lambda h\right>-\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2t}\|h\|^{2}\leq u(x,t)-u(x-\lambda h,t)\leq 0,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\lambda\in(-\overline{\lambda},\overline{\lambda})$.}

(35)

This implies

0\geq\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\dfrac{u(x,t)-u(x-\lambda h,t)}{\lambda}\geq\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left<\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},h\right>-\dfrac{\lambda}{2t}\|h\|^{2}=\left<\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t},h\right>.

(36)

By the arbitrariness of
h
, we may choose

h=\dfrac{\hat{x}-x}{t}

(37)

and apply (
36
) to find

\left\|\dfrac{x-\hat{x}}{t}\right\|^{2}\leq 0\ \ \ \implies\ \ \ x=\hat{x}.

(38)

Step 2.
Plugging
x=\hat{x}
back into (
36
) furthermore reveals, by the squeeze lemma,

\left<\nabla u(x,t),h\right>=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{u(x,t)-u(x-\lambda h,t)}{\lambda}=0,\ \ \ \mbox{for all $h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$.}

(39)

Since
h
was arbitrarily chosen, it follows that
\nabla u(x,t)=0
.

Step 3.
By way of contradiction, suppose
x
is not a local minimizer of
f
. This would imply existence of nonzero
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\hat{\lambda}\in(0,\infty)
such that

f(x+\lambda h)<f(x)\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\lambda\in(0,\hat{\lambda})$.}

(40)

However, our above results together with the truth of (
40
) would imply

\displaystyle u(x+\lambda h,t)

\displaystyle\leq f(x+\lambda h)+\dfrac{1}{2t}\|(x+\lambda h)-(x+\lambda h)\|^{2}

(41a)

\displaystyle=f(x+\lambda h,t)

(41b)

\displaystyle<f(x)

(41c)

\displaystyle=u(x,t),\ \ \ \mbox{for all $\lambda\in(0,\hat{\lambda})$,}

(41d)

contradicting the fact
x
is a local minimizer of
u(\cdot,t)
. Thus,
x
is a local minimizer of
f
.
∎
