Wiener densities for the Airy line ensemble
Duncan Dauvergne

Abstract
The parabolic Airy line ensemble 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} is a central limit object in the KPZ universality class and related areas. On any compact set K = { 1 , … , k } × [ a , a + t ] K=\{1,\dots,k\}\times[a,a+t] , the law of the recentered ensemble 𝔄 − 𝔄 ⁡ ( a ) \mathfrak{A}-\mathfrak{A}(a) has a density X K X_{K} with respect to the law of k k independent Brownian motions. We show that X K ​ ( f ) = exp ⁡ ( − S ​ ( f ) + o ⁡ ( S ​ ( f ) ) ) X_{K}(f)=\exp\left(-\textsf{S}(f)+o(\textsf{S}(f))\right) where S is an explicit, tractable, non-negative function of f f . We use this formula to show that X K X_{K} is bounded above by a K K -dependent constant, give a sharp estimate on the size of the set where X K < ϵ X_{K}<\epsilon as ϵ → 0 \epsilon\to 0 , and prove a large deviation principle for 𝔄 \mathfrak{A} . We also give density estimates that take into account the relative positions of the Airy lines, and prove sharp two-point tail bounds that are stronger than those for Brownian motion. These estimates are a key input in the classification of geodesic networks in the directed landscape [ 18 ] . The paper is essentially self-contained, requiring only tail bounds on the Airy point process and the Brownian Gibbs property as inputs.

Introduction

The Airy line ensemble is a stationary random sequence of functions
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathcal{A}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},i\in\mathbb{N}\}
satisfying
\mathcal{A}_{1}>\mathcal{A}_{2}>\dots
. It was first introduced by Prähofer and Spohn
[
41
]
via a determinantal formula, see (
9
) below. Prähofer and Spohn showed that the top line
\mathcal{A}_{1}
–known as the Airy (or Airy
2
) process–describes the scaling limit at a fixed time for a certain one-dimensional random growth model in the KPZ (Kardar-Parisi-Zhang) universality class started from a point. The remaining lines describe the scaling limit of a richer multi-layer random growth model. Since this work, the Airy process and the Airy line ensemble have been shown to be universal limit objects in the KPZ universality class and other related branches of probability. Among other results, we now know:

(i)

The Airy process and the Airy line ensemble appear as one-parameter scaling limits of classical solvable random metric and random growth models in the KPZ universality class (e.g. zero temperature models including exponential/geometric/Poisson/Brownian last passage percolation and tasep, see
[
37
,
41
,
15
,
19
]
; positive temperature models including the KPZ equation and the KPZ line ensemble, see
[
16
,
43
,
46
,
47
]
). See
[
27
,
42
,
14
,
44
,
11
]
for background on the KPZ universality class and related areas.

(ii)

The richest scaling limit in the KPZ universality class–the directed landscape–can be described via a last passage problem across the Airy line ensemble, see
[
20
]
. Moreover, for last passage models, convergence to the Airy line ensemble is the only integrable input required for proving convergence to the directed landscape, see
[
23
]
.

(iii)

The Airy line ensemble is the limit as
n\to\infty
at the edge of a system of
n

[

,
15
]
. This implies that it is the edge limit for the eigenvalue process in Hermitian-matrix valued Brownian motion (Dyson’s Brownian motion with
\beta=2

(iv)

The Airy line ensemble appears as the limit at the boundary between the frozen and liquid regions in general classes of random lozenge tilings, see
[
5

[

25

39

While the determinantal representation of the Airy line ensemble is useful for the definition, it can be difficult to work with in practice and does not give good intuition for the probabilistic structure of Airy line ensemble. It is more useful to view
\mathcal{A}
as a system of infinitely many non-intersecting Brownian motions (i.e. a limit of (iii) above).

This idea was made rigorous by Corwin and Hammond
[
15
]
by identifying a
Brownian Gibbs property
for the Airy line ensemble. To describe this property, we work with the
parabolic Airy line ensemble

\mathfrak{A}_{1}>\mathfrak{A}_{2}>\dots
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{A}_{i}(t)-t^{2}
. The Brownian Gibbs property states that inside any region
K=\{1,\dots k\}\times[a,b]
, conditionally on all values
\mathfrak{A}_{i}(t)
for
(i,t)\notin K
, the parabolic Airy line ensemble on

is simply given by a sequence of
k
independent Brownian bridges of variance
2

(a,\mathfrak{A}_{i}(a))

(b,\mathfrak{A}_{i}(b))

1
1

1

This property is extremely useful for understanding the Airy line ensemble. For example, the Brownian Gibbs property is necessary to rigorously show that the determinantal formula of Prähofer and Spohn defines a system of non-intersecting paths. The Brownian Gibbs property also implies that that recentered Airy lines
\mathfrak{A}_{i}-\mathfrak{A}_{i}(0)
are locally absolutely continuous with respect to Wiener measure of variance
2

\mathfrak{A}

Given that recentered Airy lines are locally absolutely continuous with respect to Wiener measure, the next natural question is to ask exactly how this occurs. More precisely, we would like to explicitly describe the density (i.e. the Radon-Nikodym derivative) of Airy lines against Wiener measure. This is the goal of the present paper.

1.1
More motivation and applications

The Brownian Gibbs property and the local Brownian structure of the Airy line ensemble have been repeatedly exploited to prove results about the Airy process, the Airy line ensemble, and more general limit objects in the KPZ universality class, e.g. see
[
15
,
34
]
for some early examples and
[
30
]
for a survey with more recent applications. In particular, the construction of the directed landscape
[
20
]
relies heavily on Brownian Gibbs analysis from
[
22
]
.

While it is quite useful to know that the Airy line ensemble is locally absolutely continuous with respect to Brownian motion, there are many situations when a more quantitative comparison is required. The search for a strong quantitative comparison was first undertaken by Hammond
[
35
]
and then continued by Calvert, Hegde, and Hammond
[
12
]
. The culmination of these two papers is the following estimate from
[
12
]
. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t>0
, and any Borel set
A\subset\mathscr{C}^{1}_{0}([0,t])
we have

\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{k}(\cdot)-\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)\in A)}{\mu_{t}(A)}\leq 2\exp(d_{k,t}\log^{5/6}[\mu_{t}(A)^{-1}]),

(3)

where
d_{k,t}
is a
k,t
-dependent constant and
\mu_{t}=\mu_{1,t}
is Wiener measure of variance
2
on
\mathscr{C}^{1}_{0}([0,t])
.

While (
3
) looks like a fairly technical statement, it has proven to be extremely useful. Indeed, since the growth in the right-hand side of (
3
) is slower than any power of
\mu_{t}(A)
, it shows that events for Brownian motion that occur with probability
\epsilon
occur only with probability
\epsilon^{1-o(1)}
for the
k
th Airy line. This is a key estimate for ruling out pathological behaviour in the Airy line ensemble, and has been used repeatedly for understanding limiting random geometry in the KPZ universality class, e.g. see
[
7
,
28
,
32
,
6
,
34
,
36
,
33
,
29
,
10
,
24
,
21
]
.

Theorem
1.1
strengthens the estimate (
3
). In fact, it implies something more surprising: namely, that the left-hand side of (
3
) is uniformly bounded over all
A
!

Corollary 1.3
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N},t\geq 1
, we have that
X_{k,t}(f)\leq e^{O_{k}(t^{3})}
for
\mu_{k,t}
-a.e.
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}_{0}([0,t])
. In other words,
\nu_{k,t}(A)\leq e^{O_{k}(t^{3})}\mu_{k,t}(A)
for any Borel set
A\subset\mathscr{C}_{0}^{k}([0,t])
.

It is worth noting that our proof of Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.3
is shorter than the proof of (
3
) from
[
35
,
12
]
, and hence gives an easier route to this cornerstone result and its consequences.

Corollary
1.3
bounds how
large

\nu_{k,t}(A)
can be compared to
\mu_{k,t}(A)
. We can also use Theorem
1.1
to get a sharp estimate on how
small

\nu_{k,t}(A)
can be compared to
\mu_{k,t}(A)
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Define the function
\Theta:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Theta(\mathbf{x})=\frac{2}{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}|\right)^{3/2}+\frac{4}{3}\sum_{j=2}^{k}\left(\sum_{i=j}^{k}|x_{i}|\right)^{3/2},

and let
\alpha_{k}
be the maximum of
\Theta
on the sphere
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1
.
Then

\inf\Bigg\{\frac{\nu_{k,t}(A)}{\mu_{k,t}(A)}:\mu_{k,t}(A)\geq\epsilon\Bigg\}=\exp\left(-\alpha_{k}[4t\log(\epsilon^{-1})]^{3/4}+O_{k}(t^{3}+t\log^{5/8}(\epsilon^{-1}))\right).

(4)

Note that the decay on the right-hand side of (
4
) is slower than any power of
\epsilon
. Because of this, we expect that Corollary
1.4
could be useful for showing that rare behaviour for Brownian motion also occurs in the Airy line ensemble and the directed landscape, just as (
3
) has been used previously for ruling out certain behaviour in these objects.

For general
k
, the minimum value of
\Theta
does not have a nice closed form. However, when
k=1
, then the minimum value is
2/3
(i.e.
\Theta(x)=2/3
when
x=\pm 1
). The proof of Corollary
1.4
also gives a sense of what types of sets will come close to achieving the infimum in (
4
).

Remark 1.5
.

To give some sense of the fragility and strength of the estimate in Theorem
1.1
, it is interesting to note that Corollary
1.3
fails if we replace
\mathfrak{A}
by the stationary Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{A}(t)=\mathfrak{A}(t)+t^{2}

As another application, we can quickly deduce a large deviation principle for
\mathfrak{A}
by using Corollaries

X_{k,t},X_{k,t}^{-1}\in L^{p}(\mu_{k,t})

Corollary 1.6
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, let
\nu_{k,t}^{\epsilon}
denote the pushforward on
\mathscr{C}_{0}^{k}([0,t])
of
\nu_{k,t}
under the map
f\mapsto\epsilon f
. Also, for
f\in\mathscr{C}_{0}^{k}([0,t])

I(f)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|f_{i}^{\prime}(s)|^{2}ds,

1.2
A tool for Airy analysis

While the Brownian Gibbs property is a powerful tool for analyzing the Airy line ensemble, it can nonetheless be difficult to analyze a collection of non-intersecting Brownian bridges on
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t]
conditioned to stay above a possibly arbitrary and unknown boundary. Indeed, at this point there is a fairly substantial bag of tricks designed precisely to handle these issues (e.g. monotonicity ideas
[
15
]
, the bridge representation
[
22
]
, the Wiener candidate and jump ensemble methods
[
35
,
12
]
, tangent methods
[
31
]
).

One of the main contributions of this paper is to construct a line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
that is equal to the Airy line ensemble under a certain non-intersection conditioning, but is given by
independent Brownian bridges
on the region
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t]
. The existence of the line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
implies Wiener density bounds that are similar to (
3
) and (
1.3
), but crucially
take into account the locations of the Airy endpoints
.

As our main theorem about the line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
has a somewhat technical statement, we first give an example of its use that only concerns a single Airy line.

Example 1.7
.

Fix
t>0,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the law of process
\mathfrak{A}_{k}|_{[0,t]}
has a bounded density against the law of a process
B+L
, where:

•

B:[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}
is a Brownian bridge of variance
2
with
B(0)=B(t)=0
.

•

L:[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}
is an affine function, independent of
B
, and there are
t,k
-dependent constants
c,d>0
such that for all
m>0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L(0)\vee L(t)>m)\leq e^{-\frac{4}{3}m^{3/2}+cm^{5/4}},\qquad\mathbb{P}(L(0)\wedge L(t)<-m)\leq 2e^{-dm^{3}},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|L(0)-L(t)|>m)\leq e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{4t}-\frac{4k-2}{3}m^{3/2}+cm^{5/4}}.

(6)

The one-point bounds on
L(0),L(t)
in Example
1.7
give the same stretched exponential tail behaviour as for the Tracy-Widom random variable
\mathfrak{A}(0)
. Moreover, the two-point bound on
L(0)-L(t)
is actually stronger than the same bound for a variance
2
Brownian motion! We now state the main theorem about the line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Fix
t\geq 1,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a random sequence of continuous functions
\mathfrak{L}=\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}=\{\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},i\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that the following points hold:

1.

Almost surely,
\mathfrak{L}
satisfies
\mathfrak{L}_{i}(r)>\mathfrak{L}_{i+1}(r)
for all pairs
(i,r)\notin\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times(0,t)
.

2.

The ensemble
\mathfrak{L}
has the following Gibbs property. For any set
S=\llbracket 1,\ell\rrbracket\times[a,b]
, conditional on the values of
\mathfrak{L}_{i}(r)
for
(i,r)\notin S
, the distribution of
\mathfrak{L}_{i}(r),(i,r)\in S
is given by
\ell
independent Brownian bridges
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
from
(a,\mathfrak{L}_{i}(a))
to
(b,\mathfrak{L}_{i}(b))
for
i\in\llbracket 1,\ell\rrbracket
, conditioned on the event
B_{i}(r)>B_{i+1}(r)
whenever
(i,r)\notin\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times(0,t)
.

In particular, the law of
\mathfrak{L}
on
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t]
is simply given by
k
independent Brownian bridges connecting
(0,\mathfrak{L}_{i}(0))
to
(t,\mathfrak{L}_{i}(t))
.

3.

We have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}_{1}>\mathfrak{L}_{2}>\dots)\geq e^{-O_{k}(t^{3})},

and conditional on the event
\{\mathfrak{L}_{1}>\mathfrak{L}_{2}>\dots\}
, the ensemble
\mathfrak{L}
is equal in law to the parabolic Airy line ensemble
\mathfrak{A}
.

The process
L+B
in Example
1.7
is given by
\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}_{k}|_{[0,t]}
. The strong one- and two-point tail bounds in that example follow from more general tail bounds on

\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
proven in Sections
4
and
5
. These tail bounds make the line ensemble

\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
useful for analysis in practice.

Remark 1.9
.

Theorem
1.8
is a key input in the classification of geodesic networks in the directed landscape
[
18
]
. In that work, we use Theorem
1.8
to find an upper bound on the Hausdorff dimension of geodesic

Remark 1.10
.

Theorem
1.8
allows us to relax the Brownian Gibbs property for
\mathfrak{A}
by removing the non-intersection condition on the patch
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t]
. We can also give a more general version of Theorem
1.8
that removes the non-intersection condition on
\ell
disjoint patches
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[a_{1},a_{1}+t],\dots,\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[a_{\ell},a_{\ell}+t]
for a vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})
. If we call the new line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}^{\mathbf{a}}
, then

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}^{\mathbf{a}}_{1}>\mathfrak{L}^{\mathbf{a}}_{2}>\dots)\geq\exp(-e^{O_{k}(1+\ell\log\ell)}t^{3}).

Crucially, this bound does not depend on
\mathbf{a}
. Moreover, the patches
\mathfrak{L}^{\mathbf{a}}|_{\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[a_{i},a_{i}+t]}
become asymptotically independent as

. The line ensembles
\mathfrak{L}^{\mathbf{a}}
are useful for studying the Airy process and the Airy line ensemble on large spatial scales. See Theorem
3.8
for details.

1.3
A heuristic for (
2
): Brownian motion avoiding a parabola

Before giving a brief overview of the proof in Section
1.4
, we give a heuristic for formula (
2
). For simplicity, we will only consider the case when
k=1
.
A good starting point for trying to understand the behaviour of the top curve
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
is the well-known pair of tail estimates

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{1}(s)+s^{2}>m)\sim e^{-(\frac{4}{3}+o(1))m^{3/2}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{2}(s)+s^{2}<-m)\sim e^{-cm^{3}}.

(7)

Similar estimates hold for all Airy lines, see Lemmas
2.1
,
2.2
.
The intuition from these estimates suggests first that
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
typically adheres to the parabola
-s^{2}
, and secondly that it is much easier for
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
to move up away from this parabola than it is for
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
to push
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
down below the parabola. Because of this, a toy model for
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
can be given as follows. Consider a sequence of Brownian bridges
B_{n}
from
(-n,-n^{2})
to
(n,-n^{2})
conditioned to stay above the parabola
f(s)=-s^{2}
, and take
n
to
\infty
to get a distributional limit
\mathcal{B}
. The process
\mathcal{B}
has been studied in
[
26
]
and can actually be described as a diffusion with an explicit drift involving Airy functions. However, for this discussion we will stick to softer intuition regarding
\mathcal{B}
.

Figure 2
:
The easiest way for
\mathcal{B}(r)-\mathcal{B}(0),r\in[0,t]
to equal a function
f\in\mathscr{C}^{1}_{0}([0,t])
. The cost to lift
\mathcal{B}
far enough above of the parabola to achieve the function

is the difference between the Dirichlet energies of the solid blue and purple curves. This energy difference is
\textsf{S}(f)
plus a lower order term in

.

One way to check the reasonability of this toy model is to check the probability that
\mathcal{B}(0)\sim m
. The best way for
\mathcal{B}(0)
to equal
m
should be if
\mathcal{B}
only deviates from the parabola by following straight lines from
(-\lambda,-\lambda^{2})
to
(0,m)
and back to
(\lambda,-\lambda^{2})
for some
\lambda>0
. Computing the Dirichlet energy of this path against the Dirichlet energy of the parabola, and optimizing over
\lambda
yields that our best guess
for
\mathbb{P}(B(0)\sim m)
should be
e^{-(\frac{4}{3}+o(1))m^{3/2}}
, which agrees with the Tracy-Widom upper tail on
\mathfrak{A}_{1}(0)
. The optimal value is
\lambda=\sqrt{m}
; with this choice the straight lines from
(-\lambda,-\lambda^{2})
and
(\lambda,-\lambda^{2})
to
(0,m)
are
tangent
to the parabola.

Next, for
\mathcal{B}(r)-\mathcal{B}(0),r\in[0,t]
to equal a function
f\in\mathscr{C}^{1}_{0}([0,t])
, we must have
\mathcal{B}(0)+f(r)\geq-r^{2}
for all
r\in[0,t]
. From the definition of the tetris map
T
, we can observe that

\mathcal{B}(0)\geq\textsf{T}f(0)\quad\implies\quad\mathcal{B}(0)+f(r)\geq-r^{2}\;\forall r\in[0,t]\quad\implies\mathcal{B}(0)\geq\textsf{T}f(0)-t^{2}.

Since it is costlier to raise
\mathcal{B}
up further away from the parabola, this implies that the optimal way to have
\mathcal{B}(r)-\mathcal{B}(0),r\in[0,t]
equal to
f
is to let
\mathcal{B}(0)
sit in the interval
[\textsf{T}f(0)-t^{2},\textsf{T}f(0)]
and similarly let
\mathcal{B}(t)
sit in the interval
[\textsf{T}f(t)-t^{2},\textsf{T}f(t)]
. The cost of this, relative to the cost of simply having a Brownian motion equal to
f
can again be computed by comparing the Dirichlet energy of straight lines against a parabola. Assuming
t^{2}
is very small relative to the size of
\textsf{T}f
, this amounts to computing the Dirichlet energy of the straight line from
(-\sqrt{\textsf{T}f(0)},-\textsf{T}f(0))
to
(0,\textsf{T}f(0))
plus the Dirichlet energy of the straight line from
(t,\textsf{T}f(t))
to
(\sqrt{\textsf{T}f(t)},-\textsf{T}f(t))
minus the Dirichlet energy of the parabola from
(-\sqrt{\textsf{T}f(0)},-\textsf{T}f(0))
to
(\sqrt{\textsf{T}f(t)},-\textsf{T}f(t))
. This is

\frac{2}{3}([\textsf{T}f(0)]^{3/2}+[\textsf{T}f(t)]^{3/2})+O(t\textsf{T}f(t)),

which is
\textsf{S}(f)+O(t\textsf{T}f(t))
. The assumption that
t^{2}
is small relative to the size of
\textsf{T}f
guarantees that the error term here is lower order. See Figure
2
for an illustration. The intuition behind the expression (
2
) for more than one line is similar, except now we need to stack the
k
functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
on top of each other,
and
on top of the parabola in order to achieve the ordering
\mathfrak{A}_{1}>\mathfrak{A}_{2}>\dots
.

Understanding probabilistic objects built from non-crossing paths via a tangent picture is not a new idea. Indeed, tangent methods were proposed by Colomo and Sportiello
[
13
]
for studying limit shapes of the six-vertex model, and made rigorous by Aggarwal
[
4
]
. In the context of the KPZ and Airy line ensembles, tangent methods were used by Ganguly and Hegde
[
31
]
to pinpoint the upper tail one-point asymptotics in (
7
) and related upper tail two-point asymptotics using only minimal assumptions.

1.4
Outline of the proof and the paper

A priori, it is not clear why the hydrodynamic tangent heuristic should give fine control over the Wiener density.
As a result, our proof of the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
does not proceed by directly trying to prove this heuristic.
Rather, we first prove Theorem
1.8
, which implies that the only contribution to the Wiener density
X_{k,t}(f)
comes from the joint distribution of the endpoints
\mathfrak{L}(0),\mathfrak{L}(t)
(here
\mathfrak{L}
is as in Theorem
1.8
). To prove Theorem
1.8
, by the Brownian Gibbs property we can abstractly define a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu_{\mathfrak{L}}
on the space
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
of sequences of continuous functions
f=\{f_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},i\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that if
\mu_{\mathfrak{L}}(\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}))
were finite, then after scaling it to a probability measure, it would give the law of a line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}
satisfying Theorem
1.8
. The key difficulty is in showing that
\mu_{\mathfrak{L}}(\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}))<\infty
: here we use a refinement of some ideas from
[
22
]
and
[
35
]
, along with new insights about how the Brownian Gibbs property on different domains controls
\mu_{\mathfrak{L}}(\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}))
. The proof of Theorem
1.8
is contained in Section
3
.

In Section
4
, we analyze the joint distribution of
\mathfrak{L}(0),\mathfrak{L}(t)
by again appealing to one-point bounds, using monotonicity arguments, and applying an induction argument to pass from control of
(\mathfrak{L}_{i}(0),\mathfrak{L}_{i}(t))
for any fixed
i
to joint control of
(\mathfrak{L}_{i}(0),\mathfrak{L}_{i}(t)),i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. It is only because the upper tail one-point bound in (
7
) sharply matches the bound coming from the tangent heuristic that the tangent picture emerges. In Section
5
, we combine these tail bounds with Theorem
1.8
to establish that LHS
\leq
RHS in (
2
).

The lower bound LHS
\geq
RHS is easier, since we only need to work on a part of the probability space where the
(k+1)
st line does not deviate too far from the parabola. It closely resembles a rigorous version of the heuristic discussed in Section
1.3
, and is shown in Section
6
. A final section (Section
7
) proves the corollaries.

We have striven to keep the paper self-contained, relying only on standard tail bounds on the Airy points
\mathfrak{A}_{1}(0),\mathfrak{A}_{2}(0),\dots
, the Brownian Gibbs property, and basic monotonicity properties for non-intersecting Brownian bridges as inputs.

Conventions throughout the paper

2
,

and

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce a few basic preliminaries about the Airy line ensemble and other non-intersecting collections of Brownian motions.

Let
I
be a closed interval in
\mathbb{R}
, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
\mathscr{C}^{k}(I)
denote the space of
k
-tuples of continuous functions
f=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})
from
I
to
\mathbb{R}
with the
compact topology

f_{n}\to f\quad\text{ if }\quad\sup_{x\in K}|f_{n,i}(x)-f_{i}(x)|\to 0\quad\text{ for all compact sets }K\subset I,i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket.

We also let
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(I)
be the space of all sequences of continuous functions
f=(f_{1},f_{2},\dots)
from
I
to
\mathbb{R}
, again equipped the with compact topology (we replace
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
with
\mathbb{N}
in the definition above), and we write
\mathscr{C}(I)=\mathscr{C}^{1}(I)
.

We can also think of
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}(I)
as a function
f:\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times I\to\mathbb{R}
with the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets of
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times I
, and with this in mind, we will often write
f|_{U}
for the restriction of
f
to a subset of
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times I
. For
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}(I)
with
\ell\leq k
we write
f^{\ell}=(f_{1},\dots,f_{\ell})
for the first
\ell
coordinates of
f
.

The
parabolic Airy line ensemble

\mathfrak{A}
is a random element of
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
(a.k.a. a line ensemble) satisfying

\mathfrak{A}_{1}>\mathfrak{A}_{2}>\dots

almost surely. The process
\mathcal{A}_{i}(t)=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(t)+t^{2}
is the usual
Airy line ensemble
and is stationary under shifts and time reversal. For any
r\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{A}(\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(-\;\cdot)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{A}(\cdot+r),

(8)

where the equality in distribution is as random element of
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Throughout the paper we will typically use the parabolic version
\mathfrak{A}
, which has a more natural Gibbs resampling property. The Airy line ensemble
\mathcal{A}
is uniquely characterized by a determinantal formula. Indeed, for every finite set of times
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
, the set of locations set
\{(\mathcal{A}_{i}(t_{j}),t_{j}):i\in\mathbb{N},j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\}
is a determinantal point process on
\mathbb{R}\times\{t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\}
with with kernel

K((x,s);(y,r))=\begin{cases}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\lambda e^{-\lambda(s-r)}\Ai(x+\lambda)\Ai(y+\lambda),\qquad&\;\text{if}\;s\geq t,\\
-\int_{-\infty}^{0}d\lambda e^{-\lambda(s-r)}\Ai(x+\lambda)\Ai(y+\lambda),\qquad&\;\text{if}\;s<r,\end{cases}

(9)

where
\Ai(\cdot)
is the Airy function.

2.1
Tail bounds

The Airy points
\mathfrak{A}_{i}(0),i\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy well-known tail bounds. A fairly sharp upper tail bound is a quick consequence of the determinantal formula.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
c_{k}>0
such that for
\mathbf{m}\in[0,\infty)^{k}_{\geq}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{i}(0)>m_{i}\quad\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket)\leq c_{k}\exp\Big(-\frac{4}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}\Big).

Proof.

This is a simple calculation involving the Airy kernel at a single time. Let
\Lambda=\{\mathfrak{A}_{i}(0),i\in\mathbb{N}\}.
Then for
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}
and
\epsilon>0
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{i}(0)\in[m_{i}-\epsilon,m_{i}+\epsilon]\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\#(\Lambda\cap[m_{i}-\epsilon,m_{i}+\epsilon])\right).

As long as
\epsilon>0
is small enough, the right hand side above equals

\int_{m_{k}-\epsilon}^{m_{k}+\epsilon}\cdots\int_{m_{1}-\epsilon}^{m_{1}+\epsilon}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}K(x_{i},0;x_{j},0)dx_{1}\dots dx_{k},

where
K
is the Airy kernel. Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we see that
(\mathfrak{A}_{1}(0),\dots,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0))
has a Lebesgue density that at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\geq}
is bounded above by

\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}K(x_{i},0;x_{j},0).

Using the formula (
9
) and the standard bound
\operatorname{Ai}(x)\leq c\exp(-\tfrac{2}{3}x^{3/2})
for a universal constant
c
(see formula 10.4.59 in
[
2
]
), we have that

K((x,0),(y,0))\leq c^{\prime}\exp(-\tfrac{2}{3}(x^{3/2}+y^{3/2})).

and so

\det_{1\leq i,j\leq n}K(x_{i},0;x_{j},0)\leq k!c^{\prime}\exp(-\tfrac{4}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{3/2}).

Integrating this over the set of all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}
with
x_{i}\geq m_{i}
for all
i
yields the result.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
d_{k}>0
such that for all
m>0
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{k}(0)<-m)\leq 2\exp(-d_{k}m^{3}).

(10)

Proof.

For
k=1
this is well-known tail bound goes back to
[
45
]
. For
k>1
, this is a limiting version of Theorem 3.1 of
[
22
]
, equation (5). More precisely, that theorem states that for a sequence of random variables
\mathcal{A}^{n}_{k}(0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{A}_{k}(0)
as
n\to\infty
, that (
10
) holds for all large enough
n
.
∎

Later on in the paper, we will need a quick way to move between two-point tail bounds and a maximum bound on a stochastic process. This is accomplished through the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
t>0
,
W\in\mathscr{C}([0,t])
and suppose that there exist constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that for all
m>0
and all
s,s+r\in[0,t]
, we have the estimate

\mathbb{P}(|W(s)-W(s+r)|>m\sqrt{r})\leq\alpha\exp(-\beta m^{2}).

Then for
m>20\sqrt{\beta}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\sup_{s,s+r\in[0,t]}|W(s)-W(s+r)|\geq m\sqrt{t})\leq 2\alpha\exp(-\beta m^{2}/64).

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

The proof is a standard chaining argument. We may assume
W(0)=0
, and focus on bounding
2\|W\|_{\infty}
.
For every
k=0,1,\dots
, let
G_{k}
be the function with
G_{k}(s)=W(s)
for
s=it/(2^{k})
for some
i\in\llbracket 0,2^{k}\rrbracket
, and such that
G_{k}
is linear on each of the intervals
[it/(2^{k}),(i+1)t/(2^{k})]
. For
k=0,1,\dots
, let
H_{k}=G_{k}-G_{k-1}
, where
G_{-1}:=0
. We can write
W=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}H_{k}
. Also,

\|H_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq\sup_{i\in\llbracket 1,2^{k}\rrbracket}|W(it2^{-k})-W((i-1)t2^{-k})|.

and hence we have the union bound

\mathbb{P}(\|H_{k}\|_{\infty}>m\sqrt{t}2^{-k/2})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2^{k}}\mathbb{P}(|W(it2^{-k})-W((i-1)t2^{-k})|>m\sqrt{t}2^{-k/2})\leq 2^{k}\alpha\exp(-\beta m^{2}).

Therefore letting

M=\max_{k\geq 0}(t^{-1/2}2^{k/2}\|H_{k}\|_{\infty}-\sqrt{k/\beta}),

we have

\mathbb{P}(M>m)\leq 2\alpha\exp(-\beta m^{2}).

Next,

\displaystyle 2\|W\|_{\infty}\leq 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\|H_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\sqrt{t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(M+\sqrt{k/\beta})2^{-k/2}\leq\sqrt{t}(4M+10/\sqrt{\beta}),

which yields the result after converting the tail bound on
M
.
∎

2.2
Gibbs resampling

As discussed in the introduction, the Airy line ensemble satisfies a resampling property called the Brownian Gibbs property. Here we introduce notation that will help us work with the Brownian Gibbs property, and state a few useful lemmas.

First, for an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, an interval
J\subset I
and an
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
-valued function
g
whose domain contains
J
, let

\operatorname{NI}(g,J)=\{f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}(I):f_{1}(s)>f_{2}(s)>\dots>f_{k}(s)>g(s)\qquad\text{ for all }s\in J\}.

Note that the value of
k
and the interval
I
is not explicit in the notation
\operatorname{NI}(g,J)
. These will always be clear from context (i.e. by specifying the initial space
\mathscr{C}^{k}(I)
). We will use the shorthand
\operatorname{NI}(g)=\operatorname{NI}(g,I)
.
Next, we say that a
k
-tuple of
independent Brownian bridges

B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([s,t])
goes from
(s,\mathbf{x})
to
(t,\mathbf{y})
for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
if each of the
B_{i}
is a Brownian bridge with
B_{i}(s)=x,B_{i}(t)=y
. Note that our bridges will always have variance
2
. We write

\mathbb{P}_{s,t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},f,J)

for the probability that a
k
-tuple of independent Brownian bridges from
(s,\mathbf{x})
to
(t,\mathbf{y})
is in the non-intersection set
\operatorname{NI}(f,J)
. Again, we omit
J
from all notation if
J=[s,t]
. We say that the
k
-tuple of Brownian bridges is
non-intersecting
if it is conditioned on the event
\operatorname{NI}(-\infty)
.

The Brownian Gibbs property for
\mathfrak{A}
can now be more formally stated as follows:

For a set
S=\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[a,b]
, let
\mathcal{F}_{k,a,b}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{\mathfrak{A}_{i}(x):(i,x)\notin S\}
. Then the conditional distribution of
\mathfrak{A}|_{S}
given
\mathcal{F}_{k,a,b}
is equal to the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(a,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(a))
to
(b,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(b))
conditioned on the event
\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})
.

In the statement above, recall that
\mathfrak{A}^{k}(a)=(\mathfrak{A}_{1}(a),\dots,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(a))
and note that when applying the Gibbs property, the bridges are always drawn independently of the endpoint vectors
\mathfrak{A}^{k}(a),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(b)
and of the lower boundary
\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}
.

We record two key lemmas that will allow us to work with the Brownian Gibbs property. The first is a crucial monotonicity property.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
[s,t],J
be closed intervals in
\mathbb{R}
with
J\subset[s,t]
, let
\mathbf{x}^{1}\leq\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{y}^{1}\leq\mathbf{y}^{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}
where
\leq
is the coordinate-wise partial order, and let
g_{1}\leq g_{2}
be bounded Borel measurable functions from
[s,t]\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. For
i=1,2
be
B^{i}
be a
k
-tuple of Brownian bridges from
(s,\mathbf{x}^{i})
to
(t,\mathbf{y}^{i})
, conditioned on the event
\operatorname{NI}(g_{i},J)
. Then there exists a coupling such that
B^{1}_{j}(r)\leq B^{2}_{j}(r)
for all
r\in[s,t],j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
.

Lemma
2.4
is proven as Lemmas 2.6 and 2.7 in
[
15
]
in the case when
[s,t]=J
. The proof goes through verbatim in the slight extension when
J
is a strict subset of
[s,t]
.

The next lemma is a lower bound on the probability of Brownian bridges being non-intersecting. This bound will allow us to compare regular Brownian bridge probabilities with non-intersecting bridge probabilities.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}
be vectors with

\min_{i\in\llbracket 1,k-1\rrbracket}(x_{i}-x_{i+1})\wedge(y_{i}-y_{i+1})\geq\alpha

for some
\alpha>0
. Let
t>0
. Then setting
\epsilon=\alpha^{2}/t
we have

\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},-\infty)\geq e^{-k^{3}\epsilon/6}(\epsilon/2)^{k(k-1)/2}.

This lemma is fairly standard and follows from the Karlin-McGregor formula. For example, it is very similar to Proposition 3.1.1 in
[
35
]
, and we follow the same steps used in the proof of that proposition.

Proof.

First,
\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},-\infty)\geq\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\alpha\iota,\alpha\iota,-\infty)
where
\iota=(k,k-1,\dots,1)
. Therefore letting
\mathbf{x}=\alpha\iota
, by the Karlin-McGregor formula, after simplification we can write

\displaystyle P_{0,t}(\alpha\iota,\alpha\iota,-\infty)

\displaystyle=\exp(-\frac{1}{2t}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{2})\det(e^{x_{i}x_{j}/(2t)})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}.

See the middle of p. 48 in
[
35
]
for this version of the Karlin-McGregor formula. The determinant is a Vandermonde, and satisfies

\det(e^{x_{i}x_{j}t^{-1}})_{1\leq i,j\leq k}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(e^{\alpha^{2}j(2t)^{-1}}-e^{\alpha^{2}i(2t)^{-1}})\geq\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\alpha^{2}(2t)^{-1}(j-i)\geq(\alpha^{2}(2t)^{-1})^{k(k-1)/2}.

Finally

\exp(-(2t)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{2})\geq\exp(-k^{3}\alpha^{2}t^{-1}/6),

yielding the result.
∎

3
Constructing
\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}

In this section, we construct the line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}=\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
and prove Theorem
1.8
. Throughout the section, we fix
t\geq 1,k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be the parabolic Airy line ensemble. We will define a
\sigma
-finite measure
\eta=\eta^{t,k}
on
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
according to the following procedure.

1.

First, define a line ensemble
\mathfrak{B}
by letting
\mathfrak{B}_{i}(r)=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(r)
for
(i,r)\notin\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t]
, and let
\mathfrak{B}^{k}|_{[0,t]}
be given by
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(0,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0))
to
(t,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t))
. These bridges are independent of each other and of
\mathfrak{A}
. Let
\tilde{\eta}
denote the law of
\mathfrak{B}
.

2.

Let
\eta
be the measure which is absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\eta}
with density given by

\frac{d\eta}{d\tilde{\eta}}(f)=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(f^{k}(0),f^{k}(t),f_{k+1})}

(11)

for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
. This density exists for
\tilde{\eta}
-a.e.
f
since
\mathfrak{B}^{k}(0)=\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0)
,
\mathfrak{B}^{k}(t)=\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t)
,
\mathfrak{B}_{k+1}=\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}
and almost surely,
\mathfrak{A}_{1}(0)>\dots>\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(0)
and
\mathfrak{A}_{1}(t)>\dots>\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(t)
.

We would like to say that
\eta
is a finite measure, and hence can be normalized to a probability measure. This normalized measure will be the law of the line ensemble
\mathfrak{L}=\mathfrak{L}^{t,k}
. Once we know this, Theorem
1.8
will follow easily. However, it is highly non-trivial to show that
\eta
is finite. The bulk of this section is devoted to proving this. Indeed, by (
11
) we can observe that

\eta(\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}))=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}\right)

(12)

and so concretely we just need to show that the
inverse acceptance probability

[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]^{-1}

has finite expectation.
In order to do this, we will study alternate constructions of the measure
\eta
. Fix
s>t
, and define a measure
\eta_{s}=\eta_{s}^{t,k}
by the following procedure:

1.

Define a line ensemble
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
by letting
\mathfrak{B}_{s,i}(r)=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(r)
for
(i,r)\notin\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[-s,s]
, and let
\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}|_{[-s,s]}
have the conditional law given
\mathfrak{A}
outside of
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[-s,s]
of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(-s,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s))
to
(s,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s))
conditioned on the event

\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s]).

Let
\tilde{\eta}_{s}
denote the law of
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
.

2.

Let
\eta_{s}
be the measure which is absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\eta}_{s}
with density given by

\frac{d\eta_{s}}{d\tilde{\eta}_{s}}(f)=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(f^{k}(-s),f^{k}(s),f_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])}{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(f^{k}(-s),f^{k}(s),f_{k+1})}

(13)

for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every
s>t
, we have
\eta=\eta_{s}
, and letting
\mathcal{F}_{s}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the Airy line ensemble
\mathfrak{A}
outside of the set
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[-s,s]
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]}\right).

(14)

Equation (
14
) exchanges the expectation of an inverse for the inverse of an expectation, which is much easier to bound.

The idea that the inverse acceptance probability
[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]^{-1}
can be controlled by an inverse of a conditional expectation by ‘stepping back’ onto the interval
[-s,s]
and applying the Brownian Gibbs property on
[-s,s]
is inspired by the Wiener candidate method of Brownian Gibbs resampling introduced by Hammond
[
35
]
, see also
[
12
]
.

Proof.

We first compute the density of
\mathfrak{B}
against
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
(more precisely, the density of
\tilde{\eta}_{s}
against
\tilde{\eta}
). First, letting
U=\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[-s,s]
we have that
\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{B}_{s}=\mathfrak{A}
outside of
U
. Therefore the density of
\mathfrak{B}
against
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
is the same as the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
against
\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{U}
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, all of
\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{U},\mathfrak{B}|_{U},
and
\mathfrak{A}|_{U}
have densities with respect to the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(-s,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s))
to
(s,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s))
. The density of
\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{U}
is given by

\frac{\mathbf{1}(f\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s]))}{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(f(-s),f(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])}

(15)

for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([-s,s])
. The density of
\mathfrak{A}|_{U}
is

\frac{\mathbf{1}(f\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,s]))}{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(f(-s),f(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}

(16)

for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([-s,s])
. To find the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
, first observe that this density only depends on
f|_{[-s,0]\cup[t,s]}
, since conditional on
\mathfrak{B}^{k}|_{[-s,0]\cup[t,s]}
, the law of
\mathfrak{B}^{k}|_{[0,t]}
is that of
k
independent Brownian bridges. We obtain
\mathfrak{B}
from
\mathfrak{A}
by replacing
\mathfrak{A}
with independent Brownian bridges on
[0,t]
from
(0,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0))
to
(t,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t))
, and so the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
is zero unless
f\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
. Moreover, given any such
f
we can find
\tilde{f}\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,s])
with
f|_{[-s,0]\cup[t,s]}=\tilde{f}|_{[-s,0]\cup[t,s]}
. The density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
at
f
equals the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
at
\tilde{f}
.

To find the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
at
\tilde{f}
, observe that the density of
\mathfrak{A}
on
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t]
with respect to independent Brownian bridges between these points is given (again by the Brownian Gibbs property) by

\frac{\mathbf{1}(g\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[0,t]))}{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(g(0),g(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}

(17)

for
g\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([0,t])
. Therefore the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
at
\tilde{f}
(and hence
f
) is simply the product of (
16
) with the inverse of (
17
) at
g=\tilde{f}|_{[0,t]}
, which is non-zero since
\tilde{f}\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,s])
. Putting everything together, we get that the density of
\mathfrak{B}
at a general
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([-s,s])
equals

\frac{\mathbf{1}(f\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(f(0),f(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(f(-s),f(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}.

(18)

The ratio of (
18
) and (
15
) gives the density of
\mathfrak{B}|_{U}
against
\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{U}
, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
:

\left(\frac{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])}{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}\right)\times\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(f(0),f(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}),

(19)

for
f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([-s,s])
.
Using this, and the definitions (
11
), (
13
) of
\eta,\eta_{s}
from
\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\eta}_{s}
gives that
\eta=\eta_{s}
. For the equality (
14
), using that the first factor in (
19
) is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})}{\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])},

which equals
1/\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]
.
∎

The goal of the next few lemmas is to estimate the right-hand side of (
14
). The first lemma gives a bound on the right-hand side of (
14
) in terms of a few simple
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable random variables.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
s\geq 2t
and define
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable random variables

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=D(k,t)=\sqrt{t}+\max_{r,r^{\prime}\in[0,t]}|\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r^{\prime})|,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=M(k,s)=\sqrt{s}+\max_{r,r^{\prime}\in[-s,s]}|\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r^{\prime})|+\max_{i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}|\mathfrak{A}_{i}(s)-\mathfrak{A}_{i}(-s)|.

Then with
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
as in the previous lemma, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]\geq\exp\left(-\frac{ck^{3}(D^{2}+MD)}{s}-cks\right).

We separate out one of the main calculations for Lemma
3.2
, as it will also be required later in the paper.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
s\geq 2t
, and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}
. Let
g\in\mathscr{C}([-s,s])
be such that
g(-s)<x_{k},g(s)<y_{k}
.

Let
B
be a
k
-tuple on independent Brownian bridges from
(-s,\mathbf{x})
to
(s,\mathbf{y})
, conditioned on the event

\operatorname{NI}(g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s]).

Define
\iota=(k,\dots,1),{\bf 1}=(1,1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and for
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
define
f^{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathscr{C}^{k}([-s,s])
by letting

f^{\alpha,\beta}(-s)=0,\quad f^{\alpha,\beta}(0)=f^{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\alpha\iota+\beta{\bf 1},\quad f^{\alpha,\beta}(s)=0,

and so that
f^{\alpha,\beta}
is linear on each of the pieces
[-s,0],[0,t],[t,s]
.

Then for
f\in\operatorname{NI}(g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
we have that

\frac{\mathbb{P}(B=f+f^{\alpha,\beta})}{\mathbb{P}(B=f)}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{k^{3}(\alpha+\beta)^{2}}{s}-\frac{k(\alpha+\beta)\sum_{i=1}^{k}[(f_{i}(0)-x_{i})^{+}+(f_{i}(t)-y_{i})^{+}]}{s}\right).

(20)

The more formal way to interpret (
20
) is as a bound on the density of the law
\mu_{B}^{\prime}
of
B-f^{\alpha,\beta}
against the law
\mu_{B}
of
B
, on the set
\operatorname{NI}(g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
where
\mu_{B}^{\prime}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu_{B}
.

Proof.

Let
\nu
be the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(-s,\mathbf{x})
to
(s,\mathbf{y})
. Observe that
\mu_{B},\mu_{B}^{\prime}
are absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
with densities

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{B}}{d\nu}(f)=\frac{1}{Z}\mathbf{1}(f\in\operatorname{NI}(g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s])),

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{B}^{\prime}}{d\nu}(f)=\frac{1}{Z}\mathbf{1}(f+f^{\alpha,\beta}\in\operatorname{NI}(g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s]))

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(-\frac{2(f(0)-\mathbf{x})\cdot f^{\alpha,\beta}(0)+\|f^{\alpha,\beta}(0)\|^{2}}{4s}-\frac{2(f(t)-\mathbf{y})\cdot f^{\alpha,\beta}(t)+\|f^{\alpha,\beta}(t)\|^{2}}{4(s-t)}\right).

(21)

where
Z=\mathbb{P}_{-s,s}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
is a normalizing factor. Now, if
f
is in the set
\operatorname{NI}(g,[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
, then so is
f+f^{\alpha,\beta}
. Hence the right-hand side of (
20
) is bounded below by the exponential factor (
21
). We can bound (
21
) below by using that
s-t\geq s/2
, and that
0\leq f^{\alpha,\beta}\leq k(\alpha+\beta){\bf 1}
, which yields the desired bound.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

All statements in the proof are conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{s}
. Define the
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable vector

\mathbf{z}=(D+k,D+(k-1),\dots,D+1)

and the
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable set

O=\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}:x_{k}>\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(0),y_{k}>\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(t)\}.

(22)

By the definition of
D
, for
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in O

\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z},\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq r\leq t}|B(r)|\leq 1/2\Big)^{k},

(23)

where
B
is a Brownian bridge from
(0,0)
to
(0,t)
. By a standard bound, the right hand side above is bounded below by
e^{-ckt}
. Therefore letting
\mu_{\mathfrak{B}}
denote the conditional law of
(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t))
given
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, to complete the proof it suffices to find a set
A\subset O
such that

\mu_{\mathfrak{B}}(A+(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}))

is large. Fix
\Delta>0
and let
A_{\Delta}
be the
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable subset of

x_{i}\leq\mathfrak{A}_{i}(-s)+\Delta,\qquad\qquad y_{i}\leq\mathfrak{A}_{i}(s)+\Delta

for all

. Then by Lemma
3.3
with
\alpha=1,\beta=D
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathfrak{B}}(A_{\Delta}+(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}))

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{\mathfrak{B}}(A_{\Delta})\sup_{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in A}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{3}(\sqrt{t}+D)^{2}}{s}-\frac{k(\sqrt{t}+D)\sum_{i=1}^{k}((x_{i}-\mathfrak{A}_{i}(-s))^{+}+(y_{i}-\mathfrak{A}_{i}(-s))^{+})}{s}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mu_{\mathfrak{B}}(A_{\Delta})\exp\left(-\frac{4k^{3}D^{2}}{s}-\frac{4k^{2}D\Delta}{s}\right).

In the final line we have used that
\sqrt{t}+D\leq 2D
.

\Delta
where

is large.
Define vectors
\mathbf{w}^{s},\mathbf{w}^{-s}

w^{\pm s}_{i}=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(\pm s)+M+k+1-i.

By Lemma
2.4
, on the interval

, the
k

k

B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
from

It remains to prove tails bounds on
M,D
. The key is a two-point estimate for the Airy line ensemble. In Section
4.2
will prove a highly refined two-point estimate directly for
\mathfrak{L}
, whose proof goes through verbatim for
\mathfrak{A}
. However, the full power of such an estimate is not necessary and we make do with the following lemma, whose proof is much less involved.

Lemma 3.4
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
c_{k}>0
such that for all
s\in\mathbb{R},r\in(0,\infty),a>k^{2}r^{2}
we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(|\mathfrak{A}_{k}(s)+s^{2}-&\mathfrak{A}_{k}(s+r)-(s+r)^{2}|>a)\\
\leq&\;c_{k}\exp\Big(-\frac{a^{2}}{4r}-\frac{ka^{3/2}}{8}+3a^{3/2}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{r}}+2k^{2}|\log(\sqrt{a}/r)|\Big).\end{split}

(25)

The proof for Lemma
3.4
is similar to the proof of Lemma 6.1 in
[
22
]
, except here we keep closer track of the error terms. Somewhat remarkably, this already gives a stronger tail bound than for Brownian motion when
k
is large enough. We will need the more nuanced method in Section
4.2
to give a stronger tail bound than Brownian motion for all
k
.

Proof.

By stationarity, it suffices to prove the lemma when
s=0
. Moreover, since
\mathfrak{A}(\cdot)=\mathfrak{A}(-\;\cdot)
, it suffices to prove the bound with the absolute value removed. For
r>0
, define

L_{r}=\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)-\mathfrak{A}_{k}(r)-r^{2}.

Let
\lambda>r
be a parameter that we will optimize over. Let
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{A}
outside of the set
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,\lambda]
. Let
\mathbf{v}=\sqrt{r}(1/k,2/k\dots,1)
, and let
B
be a
k
-tuple of non-intersecting Brownian bridges from
(0,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)-\mathbf{v})
to
(\lambda,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(\lambda)-\mathbf{v})
. By Lemma
2.4
, given
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
, the Airy lines
\mathfrak{A}|_{\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,\lambda]}
stochastically dominate
B
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{r}>a\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)-B_{k}(r)>r^{2}+a\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})=\mathbb{P}(B_{k}(0)-B_{k}(r)>r^{2}-\sqrt{r}+a\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}).

Now let
\tilde{B}
be a collection of

k
-tuple of independent Brownian bridges from
(0,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)-\mathbf{v})
to
(\lambda,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(\lambda)-\mathbf{v})
without a non-intersection condition. By Lemma
2.5
with
t=\lambda,\alpha=\sqrt{r}/k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{k}

\displaystyle(0)-B_{k}(r)>r^{2}-\sqrt{r}+a\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{kr}{6\lambda}+\frac{k(k-1)\log(k^{2}\lambda/(2r))}{2}\right)\mathbb{P}(\tilde{B}_{k}(0)-\tilde{B}_{k}(r)>r^{2}-\sqrt{r}+a\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(k^{2}\log(k\lambda/r)\right)\mathbb{P}(\tilde{B}_{k}(0)-\tilde{B}_{k}(r)>a-\sqrt{r}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}).

(26)

Given
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}
, the increment
\tilde{B}_{k}(0)-\tilde{B}_{k}(r)
is normal with variance
2r(1-r/\lambda)
and mean
rL_{\lambda}/\lambda+r\lambda
.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{B}_{k}(0)-\tilde{B}_{k}(r)>a-\sqrt{r}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq c\exp\left(-\frac{(a-\sqrt{r}-rL_{\lambda}/\lambda-r\lambda)^{2}}{4(1-r/\lambda)r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(a^{2}-2a(\sqrt{r}+rL_{\lambda}/\lambda+r\lambda))(1+r/\lambda)}{4r}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4r}-\frac{a^{2}}{4\lambda}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{r}}+a(L_{\lambda})^{+}/\lambda+a\lambda\right).

(27)

In the final inequality, we have used that
1+r/\lambda\leq 2
.
Now, by Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, for all
m>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L_{\lambda}>m)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)>m-m^{2/3})+\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{k}(0)<-m^{2/3})

\displaystyle\leq c_{k}e^{-\frac{4k}{3}(m-m^{2/3})^{3/2}}+2e^{-d_{k}m^{2}}.

Therefore there exists
c_{k}^{\prime}>0
such that
L_{\lambda}
is stochastically dominated by a random variable with Lebesgue density bounded above by
c_{k}^{\prime}e^{-\frac{4k-1}{3}m^{3/2}}
and so

\mathbb{E}e^{aL_{\lambda}/\lambda}\leq c_{k}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{am/\lambda-\frac{4k-1}{3}m^{3/2}}dm.

For any
\alpha,\beta>0
, if
f(x)=\alpha x-\beta x^{3/2}
and
u=4\alpha^{2}/(9\beta^{2})
is the argmax of
f
, then

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{f(x)}dx\leq e^{f(u)}(2+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-|f^{\prime}(u+1)\wedge f^{\prime}(u-1)|x}dx)\leq\frac{4e^{f(u)}}{|f^{\prime}(u+1)\wedge f^{\prime}(u-1)|}\leq\frac{4e^{f(u)}}{\alpha}.

Using this inequality and simplifying the result gives that

\mathbb{E}e^{aL_{\lambda}/\lambda}\leq c_{k}^{\prime\prime}(\lambda/a)e^{4a^{3}\lambda^{-3}/(3(4k-1)^{2})},

and so the expectation of (
27
) is bounded above by

c_{k}^{\prime\prime}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4r}-\frac{a^{2}}{4\lambda}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{r}}+\frac{4a^{3}}{3\lambda^{3}(4k-1)^{2}}+a\lambda+\log(\lambda/a)\right).

(28)

A quick inspection reveals that the minimum value of (
28
) is taken when
\lambda=O(k^{-1}\sqrt{a})
. Plugging in
\lambda=\sqrt{a}k^{-1}
(which is bounded below by
r
since
a>k^{2}r^{2}
) and simplifying gives that (
28
) is bounded above by

c_{k}^{\prime\prime}\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4r}-\frac{ka^{3/2}}{8}+3a^{3/2}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{r}}\right).

Combining this with (
26
) yields the result.
∎

We can deduce tail bounds on
M
and

immediately from Lemma

.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
c_{k}>0
such that the following bounds hold for all
t\geq 1,s\geq 2t
. Letting
D=D(k,t)
and
M=M(k,s)
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D>a)\leq\exp\Big(c_{k}t^{3}-\frac{a^{2}}{320t}\Big),\qquad\mathbb{P}(M>a)\leq\exp\Big(c_{k}s^{3}-\frac{a^{2}}{320s}\Big).

Proof.

To prove the bound on
D
, we apply Lemma
2.3
to the process
\mathfrak{A}_{k}(r),r\in[0,t]
. Set
\mathcal{A}_{i}(s)=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(s)+s^{2}
. For
r<r+\epsilon\in[0,t]
and
a>c_{k}^{\prime}t^{3/2}
, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathfrak{A}_{k}(r)-\mathfrak{A}_{k}(r+\epsilon)|>a\sqrt{\epsilon})\leq\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{A}_{k}(r)-\mathcal{A}_{k}(r+\epsilon)|>a\sqrt{\epsilon}+t\epsilon)\leq c_{k}\exp(-a^{2}/5).

Here we use Lemma
3.4
in the second bound, along with the lower bound on
a
to simplify the result. This estimate ensures that
\mathfrak{A}
on
[0,t]
satisfies Lemma
2.3
with
\beta=1/5
and

; the large value of
\alpha
deals with the case when

. Appealing to that lemma yields the result. The proof of the bound on

is essentially the same.
∎

We can finally give a concrete bound on
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{s})]
, which in turn shows that the measure
\eta
is finite.

Corollary 3.6
.

There exists
d_{k}\in(0,1)
such that for all
s\geq 2t,\epsilon>0
we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]\leq\epsilon

(29)

with probability at most

e^{O_{k}(s^{3})}\exp\left(-d_{k}\alpha\sqrt{s/t}\right),

where
\alpha=\log(\epsilon^{-1})

(30)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume

for some large constant

, since otherwise the bound holds trivially. Let

and write

3.5

Moreover, by Lemma
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(0),\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-c_{k}^{\prime}(X^{2}\sqrt{t/s}+s\sqrt{t}X+st^{2})\right).

Therefore the probability of the event (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(c_{k}^{\prime}(X^{2}\sqrt{t/s}+s\sqrt{t}X+st^{2})\geq\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(X(X+s^{3/2})\geq c_{k}^{\prime-1}\sqrt{s/t}\alpha/2)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(X^{2}\geq c_{k}^{-1}\sqrt{s/t}\alpha/4)+\mathbb{P}(X\geq c_{k}^{-1}\alpha/(4s\sqrt{t}))

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-d_{k}(\alpha\sqrt{s/t})\wedge(\alpha^{2}/(s^{2}t))\right)

to achieve the bound.
The equality in (

Lemma 3.10

Fix

\mathfrak{A}(r)=\mathfrak{B}_{s}(r)
when
r\geq s
.
The case when
|r|<s
is more involved.
Let

S=\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[-s,s],\qquad S_{1}=\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times([-s,0]\cup[t,s]),\qquad S_{2}=\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t].

Define a line ensemble
\mathfrak{M}_{s}
by letting
\mathfrak{M}_{s,i}(r)=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(r)
for
(i,r)\notin S
, and let
\mathfrak{M}_{s}|_{S}
have the conditional law given
\mathfrak{A}|_{S^{c}}
of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(-s,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(-s))
to
(s,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(s))
conditioned on the event

\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])\cap\operatorname{NI}(-\infty,[0,t]).

In words,
\mathfrak{M}_{s}
is defined similarly to
\mathfrak{B}_{s}
, except with the extra condition that
\mathfrak{M}_{s,1}>\dots>\mathfrak{M}_{s,k}
on the interval
[0,t]
. Then by Lemma
2.4
,
\mathfrak{M}_{s}
is stochastically dominated by
\mathfrak{A}
, so by Lemma
2.1
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{M}_{i}(r)+r^{2}>m_{i}\quad\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket)\leq c_{k}\exp\Big(-\frac{4}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}\Big)

(41)

for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{m}\in[0,\infty)_{>}^{k}
. Our goal is to effectively compare
\mathfrak{M}^{k}_{s}|_{S_{2}}
to
\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}|_{S_{2}}
conditionally on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{s}:=\sigma(\mathfrak{A}|_{(\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[-s,s])^{c}})
in order to apply this bound. By Lemma
3.3
(and with notation as in that lemma), for any
g\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
we have

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{S_{2}}=[g+f^{\epsilon,0}]|_{S_{2}})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{S_{2}}=g|_{S_{2}})}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{k^{3}\epsilon^{2}}{s}-\frac{k^{2}\epsilon(g_{1}(0)\vee g_{1}(t)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(-s)\wedge\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(s))^{+}}{s}\right).

On the other hand, the conditional laws of
\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{S_{2}}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{s}|_{S_{2}}
given
\mathcal{F}_{s}
are mutually absolutely continuous with density

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{M}_{s}|_{S_{2}}=g)}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{S_{2}}=g)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(g(0),g(t),-\infty)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[-s,s]}\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s]))}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[-s,s]}\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])\cap\operatorname{NI}(-\infty,[0,t]))}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(g(0),g(t),-\infty).

In particular, if
g_{i}(r)\geq g_{i+1}(r)+\epsilon
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,k-1\rrbracket,r\in\{0,t\}
for some
\epsilon<1/2
, then by Lemma
2.5
, this is bounded below by

e^{-c_{k}\log(t/\epsilon)}.

Putting this together with the previous bound implies that for
g\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
, setting

Y_{g}=2s+(g_{1}(0)\vee g_{1}(t)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(-s)\wedge\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(s))^{+},

and
\epsilon=s/Y_{g}
, we have

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{M}_{s}|_{S_{2}}=[g+f^{\epsilon,0}]|_{S_{2}})}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{S_{2}}=g|_{S_{2}})}\geq\exp\left(-c_{k}\log Y_{g}\right).

(42)

Therefore for every
\mathbf{m}\in[0,\infty)^{k}_{\geq},r\in[-s,0]\cup[t,s],
and
\lambda>0
we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))\leq e^{c_{k}\log\lambda}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(\mathfrak{M}_{s}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(Y_{\mathfrak{B}}>\lambda).

Upon taking expectations of both sides, we get that

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))\leq e^{c_{k}\log\lambda}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{M}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))+\mathbb{P}(Y_{\mathfrak{B}}>\lambda).

It remains to bound
\mathbb{P}(Y_{\mathfrak{B}}>\lambda)
and then optimize over
\lambda
. Define vectors
\mathbf{w}^{s},\mathbf{w}^{-s}
where

w^{\pm s}_{i}=\mathfrak{A}_{i}(\pm s)+\sup_{r\in[-s,s]}|\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(s)|+(k+1-i)s.

By Lemma
2.4
, on the interval
[-s,s]

\mathfrak{B}^{k}_{s}
is stochastically dominated by
k
independent Brownian bridges
B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
from
(-s,\mathbf{w}^{-s})
to
(s,\mathbf{w}^{s})
conditioned on the event

\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s]).

Now, by the construction of
\mathbf{w}^{\pm s}
, the probability that
k
independent Brownian bridges
B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
from
(-s,\mathbf{w}^{-s})
to
(s,\mathbf{w}^{s})
satisfy
B\in\operatorname{NI}(\mathfrak{A}_{k+1},[-s,0]\cup[t,s])
is bounded below by
e^{-c_{k}}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{\mathfrak{B}}>\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(Y_{B}>\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{1}(s)\vee\mathfrak{A}_{1}(-s)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(-s)\wedge\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(s)+\sup_{r\in[-s,s]}|\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r)-\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(s)|\geq\lambda/2-3ks)

\displaystyle+e^{c_{k}}\mathbb{P}(\sup_{r\in[-s,s]}W(s)>\lambda/2),

where
W
is an independent Brownian bridge from
(-s,0)
to
(-s,0)
. We can bound the first of these terms with Lemma
2.1
, Lemma
2.2
, and Lemma
3.5
. The second of these terms can be bounded with a standard Brownian bridge bound. Altogether, we get that both of terms are of size
e^{-d_{k}\lambda^{3/2}}
as long as
\lambda>c_{k}s^{3}
. Therefore setting
\lambda=c_{k}(s^{3}+\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2})
and applying the bound (
41
), we get

e^{c_{k}\log\lambda}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{M}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))+\mathbb{P}(M_{\mathfrak{B}}>\lambda)\leq\exp\Big(c_{k}\log(\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}+s)-\frac{4}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}\Big).

which gives the desired result.
∎

We can translate Lemma
4.1
into a one-time bound on
\mathfrak{L}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
r\notin[0,t]
and
\mathbf{m}\in[0,\infty)^{k}_{\geq}
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{4}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}+c_{k}\sqrt{t}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}\Big)^{5/6}\Big).

Proof.

First, we may assume
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}>c_{k}^{\prime}t^{3}
for a large
c_{k}^{\prime}
since otherwise the bound is trivial. We can choose
c_{k}^{\prime}
arbitrarily large to wash out other constants in the proof; this only affects the constant
c_{k}
in the lemma statement.
We use the notation introduced prior to Lemma
3.1
. For every
s>t
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))=\frac{\eta_{s}(A(\mathbf{m},r))}{\eta(\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}))}=\frac{1}{\eta(\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R}))}\int_{A(\mathbf{m},r)}\frac{d\eta_{s}}{d\tilde{\eta}_{s}}(f)d\tilde{\eta}_{s}(f).

(43)

Now, the density
\frac{d\eta_{s}}{d\tilde{\eta}_{s}}
can be thought of as an
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variable whose domain is the Borel space
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
with probability measure
\tilde{\eta}_{s}
. When thought of this way, it is equal in distribution to

X:=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}[\mathbb{P}_{0,t}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{B}_{s}^{k}(t),\mathfrak{A}_{k+1})]}.

We use this along with Corollary
3.6
to bound (
43
). Let
S(y)=\mathbb{P}(X_{s}>y)
be the survival function for
X
, set
S^{-1}(x)=\inf\{y\in\mathbb{R}:S(y)<x\}
, and let
\beta=\eta_{s}(A(\mathbf{m},r))
. Let
\tilde{S}(y)=\exp(c_{k}s^{3}-d_{k}\log y\sqrt{s/t})
be the upper bound for
S
from Corollary
3.6
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{A(\mathbf{m},r)}\frac{d\eta_{s}}{d\tilde{\eta}_{s}}(f)d\tilde{\eta}_{s}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{S^{-1}(\beta)}^{\infty}S(y)dy\leq\int_{\tilde{S}^{-1}(\beta)}^{\infty}\tilde{S}(y)dy

\displaystyle=\exp(c_{k}d_{k}^{-1}s^{5/2}t^{1/2})\frac{\beta^{1-d_{k}^{-1}\sqrt{t/s}}}{1-d_{k}\sqrt{s/t}}

Note that the final equality is an explicit computation, i.e. the constants
c_{k},d_{k}
that appear are those in the definition of
\tilde{S}
.
Therefore by the bound on
\beta
in Lemma
4.1
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}\in A(\mathbf{m},r))\leq\exp\Bigg(c_{k}d_{k}^{-1}s^{5/2}\sqrt{t}-(1-d_{k}^{-1}\sqrt{t/s})\Bigg[\frac{4}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2}-c_{k}\log(\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}+s)\Bigg]\Bigg).

We can now minimize over
s
. This occurs roughly when
s=(\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}^{3/2})^{1/3}
(which is greater than
t
by our assumption), which gives the desired bound after simplification, assuming
c_{k}^{\prime}
was chosen sufficiently large.
∎

4.2
The two-time bound

The proof of a sharp two-time bound is a much more refined version of the argument used Lemma
3.4
that takes into account the parabola. The argument is also applied to the ensemble
\mathfrak{L}
rather than directly to
\mathfrak{A}
. The first step is to achieve a lower bound on the probability that
\mathfrak{L}_{k+1}
is small. This is achieved via the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S=\llbracket k+1,\infty\rrbracket\times\mathbb{R}
. Then the law of
\mathfrak{L}|_{S}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of
\mathfrak{A}|_{S}
and for any Borel set
A
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}|_{S}\in A)=\epsilon>0
have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}|_{S}\in A)\leq\epsilon e^{c_{k}\sqrt{t}(\log^{5/6}(\epsilon^{-1})))}

Proof.

First, we may assume
\log^{1/3}(\epsilon^{-1})>c_{k}t
for a large constant
c_{k}
, since otherwise the bound is trivial.
Letting
\mathfrak{B}=\mathfrak{B}_{s}
for some
s>c_{k}t
we have
\mathfrak{B}_{s}|_{S}=\mathfrak{A}|_{S}
. Moreover, as in the proof of Lemma
4.2
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}|_{S}\in A)\leq[\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}\in A)]^{1-c_{k}^{\prime}\sqrt{t/s}}e^{c_{k}s^{5/2}\sqrt{t}},

and minimizing at
s=c_{k}(\log\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{s}\in A)^{-1})^{1/3}
(which is greater than
c_{k}t
by our lower bound on
\epsilon
), gives the result.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Fix an interval
[-\lambda,\lambda]
for
\lambda\geq 2
. Then for
m>c_{k}t
we have

\mathbb{P}(\min_{r\in[-\lambda,\lambda]}\mathfrak{L}_{k+1}(r)+r^{2}\leq-m)\leq\lambda e^{-d_{k}m^{3}}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}(r)=\mathfrak{A}(r)+r^{2}
. First, by Lemma
2.2
and a union bound we have that

\mathbb{P}(\min_{r\in[-\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda],r\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}_{k+1}(r)\leq-m+1)\leq 2\lambda\epsilon^{-1}e^{-d_{k}m^{3}},

and by Lemma
3.4
, Lemma
2.3
, and a union bound

\mathbb{P}(\min_{r\in[-\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda],r\in\epsilon\mathbb{Z},\delta\in[0,\epsilon]}|\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}_{k+1}(r+\delta)-\tilde{\mathfrak{A}}_{k+1}(r)|\leq 1)\leq c_{k}\lambda\epsilon^{-1}e^{-d_{k}/\epsilon}.

Combining these bounds with
\epsilon=m^{-3}
gives that

\mathbb{P}(\min_{r\in[-\lambda,\lambda]}\mathfrak{A}_{k+1}(r)+r^{2}\leq-m)\leq c_{k}\lambda m^{6}e^{-d_{k}m^{3}}\leq\lambda e^{-d_{k}^{\prime}m^{3}}.

Applying Lemma
4.3
with
\epsilon=\lambda e^{-d_{k}^{\prime}m^{3}}
and simplifying yields the result.
∎

Lemma
4.2
and Corollary
4.4
give the two bounds in (
5
).

One of the main technical ingredients we need to achieve a two-point bound is a probability estimate for a Brownian bridge avoiding a parabola.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
\lambda\geq 1
, and for
x,y\geq 0
with
\sqrt{x}<\lambda/2,\sqrt{y}<\lambda/2
, define

\displaystyle E(x,y,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}+(\lambda-\sqrt{y})^{2}\sqrt{y}+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda-\sqrt{y})^{3},\quad\;\text{and}\;

\displaystyle J(x,y,\lambda)

\displaystyle=E(x,y,\lambda)-\frac{(x-y+\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4\lambda}.

Next let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
f(s)=-s^{2}+\alpha s
.
Then we have the following bounds.

1.

For
\lambda>1
, any
x,y>0
with
\sqrt{x}<\lambda/2,\sqrt{y}<\lambda/2
, and any
t\in[0,\sqrt{x}+1]
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{0,\lambda}(x,y+f(\lambda),f,[t,\lambda])\leq\exp(-J(x,y,\lambda)+c\lambda\log(1+\lambda)).

2.

Let
\lambda>1,x,y>0
with
\sqrt{x}<\lambda/2,\sqrt{y}<\lambda/2
, and let
t\in(0,\sqrt{x}+1],a\in(0,x]
. If
B
is a Brownian bridge from
(0,x)
to
(\lambda,f(\lambda)+y)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B(s)>f(s)\text{ for all }s\in[t,\lambda],B(t)\leq x-a)\leq

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-J(x,y,\lambda)-\frac{a^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}-\frac{2}{3}(x-a)^{3/2}+c(t^{3}+tx+\lambda\log(1+\lambda))\Big).

3.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in[1,\infty)^{k}_{>}
with
x_{i}-x_{i+1}\geq 1
and
y_{i}-y_{i+1}\geq 1
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,k-1\rrbracket
, and suppose that
\sqrt{x_{1}}<\lambda/2,\sqrt{y_{1}}<\lambda/2
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{0,\lambda}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}+f(\lambda),f)\geq\exp\Big(-\sum_{i=1}^{k}J(x_{i},y_{i},\lambda)-ck^{2}\lambda\Big).

The proof of Lemma
4.5
is essentially a standard Dirichlet energy computation. We sketch the idea here, and leave the details to the appendix.

First, we can set
\alpha=0
, as Brownian bridge law commutes with affine shifts. This simplifies the computations. Next, the cost for a Brownian bridge
B
from
(0,x)
to
(\lambda,-\lambda^{2}+y)
to roughly follow a path
g:[0,\lambda]\to\mathbb{R}
with
g(0)=x,g(\lambda)=-\lambda^{2}+y
is the exponentiated Dirichlet energy

\exp\left(-E(g)+\frac{(x-y+\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4\lambda}\right),\qquad\text{ where }\quad E(g)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\lambda}|g^{\prime}(s)|^{2}dt.

This follows, for example, from the Cameron-Martin theorem.
The factor of
1/4
here (as opposed to the usual
1/2
) is due to the fact that we are working with variance
2
bridges, and the added factor of
(x-y+\lambda^{2})^{2}/(4\lambda)
comes from conditioning a Brownian motion to end at the point
y-\lambda^{2}
to give rise to a Brownian bridge. Hence we should look for a path
g
of minimal Dirichlet energy that stays above the function
f(s)=-s^{2}
. This is the minimal concave function
g=g_{x,y}
from
(0,x)
to
(\lambda,-\lambda^{2}+y)
that dominates
f
. Since
f
is just a parabola, the function
g
is easily computed, and is determined by the following constraints:

•

g(0)=0,g(\lambda)=-\lambda^{2}+y
, and there are values
0\leq a<b\leq\lambda
such that
g
is linear on the intervals
[0,a]
and
[b,\lambda]
.

•

g(z)=f(z)
for all
z\in(a,b)
.

•

g
is differentiable at
a
and
b
. This follows from forcing
g
to be both concave and to dominate
f
.

We can compute that
a=\sqrt{x},b=\lambda-\sqrt{y}
, and that
E(g)=E(x,y,\lambda)
. This gives rise to the bound in Lemma
4.5
.1, and the bound in part
3
; the cost of having the
k
bridges stay ordered is a lower order term. The bound in part
2
follows similarly to part
1
, by computing the path of minimal Dirichlet energy from
(0,x)
to
(\lambda,-\lambda^{2}+y)
that dips below
x-a
at time
t
.

We can now state the first version of the two-time bound for
\mathfrak{L}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{\geq}
, let
\mathbf{a}\in[0,\infty)^{k}
and let
\sigma\in\{0,t\}^{k}
. Define
\hat{\sigma}\in\{0,t\}^{k}
to be the unique vector with
\hat{\sigma}_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}
for all
i
. Next, define

A(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)=\{f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}):f(\sigma_{i})\geq m_{i},\quad f_{i}(\sigma_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{\sigma}_{i})\geq a_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\}.

Then

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}^{k}\in A(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma))\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+\frac{2}{3}[(m_{i}-a_{i})^{+}]^{3/2}\right)+E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)\right),

(44)

where

E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)=O_{k}(t^{3}+\sqrt{t}\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{5/4}+t\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}+t^{-1/6}\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2/3}_{2}\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{1/2}).

The proof of Proposition
4.6
is the most technical proof in the paper, and it is based on a backwards induction on
\ell\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. The next lemma gives the key inductive step.

Lemma 4.7
.

In the setup of Proposition
4.6
, for
\ell\in\llbracket 1,k+1\rrbracket
define the sets

\displaystyle A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)=\{f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}):f(\sigma_{i})\geq m_{i},\quad f_{i}(\sigma_{i})-f_{i}(\hat{\sigma}_{i})\geq a_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket\ell,k\rrbracket\}.

and for
r=0,t
define

\displaystyle D^{r}_{\ell}(\mathbf{m})

\displaystyle=\{f\in\mathscr{C}^{k}(\mathbb{R}):f_{i}(r)\geq m_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,\ell\rrbracket\}.

Note that with these definitions,
A_{k+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma),D^{0}_{0}(\mathbf{m}),
and
D^{t}_{0}(\mathbf{m})
are the whole space.
Then for all
\ell\in\llbracket 1,k+1\rrbracket
and
r=0,t
we have

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}^{k}\in A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{r}(\mathbf{m}))\\
&\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{i=\ell}^{k}\left(\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+\frac{2}{3}[(m_{i}-a_{i})^{+}]^{3/2}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{4}{3}(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)\right).\end{split}

(45)

Proposition
4.6
is immediate from Lemma
4.7
since

A_{1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{0}^{r}(\mathbf{m})=A(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma).

Proof.

The proof is based on a downwards induction on
\ell
. In the base case when
\ell=k+1
, since
A_{k+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)
is the whole space, the bound is equivalent to the claim that

\mathbb{P}(D^{r}_{k}(\mathbf{m}))\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{4}{3}(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)\right)

for
r=0,t
. This follows from Lemma
4.2
. Now assume
\ell\leq k
, and that the lemma holds for
\ell+1
for any choice of
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},\sigma,r
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sigma_{\ell}=0
; the case when
\sigma_{\ell}=t
follows from the distributional equality

\mathfrak{L}(r)+r^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathfrak{L}(t-r)+(t-r)^{2}

as functions in
\mathscr{C}^{\mathbb{N}}(\mathbb{R})
. This equality follows from the same result for
\mathfrak{A}
. There are two cases here, depending on whether
r=0
or
r=t
. We start with the simpler case when
r=0
.

Step 1: Setting up a Gibbs resampling.
Let
\beta_{k}>0
be a large
k
-dependent constant and set

\gamma=\gamma(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)=\beta_{k}(t^{3/2}+t^{-1/2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{m}\|^{3/4}_{2}).

How large we need to take
\beta_{k}
will be made clear in the proof.
Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{L}
outside of the set
S=\llbracket 1,\ell\rrbracket\times[0,\gamma^{2/3}]
. The basic idea of the induction is a Gibbs resampling on the set
S
. To see how the structure will work, observe that

\displaystyle A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m})=A_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m})\cap\{\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell}\}.

The set

A_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m})

is
\mathcal{F}
-measurable, so for any set
E
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(A_{\ell}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}))

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}(A_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m}))\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}(A_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m})\cap E)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})+\mathbf{1}(E^{c}).

(46)

Now, for every
n=0,1,\dots
, let

\mathbf{m}_{n}=(m_{1}\vee(m_{\ell}+n),\dots,m_{\ell-1}\vee(m_{\ell}+n),m_{\ell}+n,m_{\ell+1},\dots,m_{k}),

and let
D_{n}=D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m}_{n-1})\setminus D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m}_{n})
so that
D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m})=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}
.
We can write

\begin{split}&\mathbf{1}(A_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell}^{0}(\mathbf{m})\cap E)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})\\
&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}(A_{\ell+1}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{n})\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})\mathbf{1}(E\cap D_{n})\|_{\infty},\end{split}

Taking expectations in (
46
) and applying the inductive hypothesis we get

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}))\leq\mathbb{P}(E^{c})+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})\mathbf{1}(E\cap D_{n})\|_{\infty}\times\\
&\times\exp\left(-\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{k}\left(\frac{a_{i}^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+\frac{2}{3}[(m_{i}-a_{i})^{+}]^{3/2}\right)-\frac{4}{3}[(m_{\ell}+n-1)^{+}]^{3/2}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{4}{3}(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m}_{n},t)\right).\end{split}

(47)

To complete the proof we will find a set
E
such that
\mathbb{P}(E^{c})
is bounded above by the right-hand side of (
45
), such that

\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})\mathbf{1}(E\cap D_{n})\|_{\infty}=0,\qquad\text{for}\quad n\notin[a_{\ell}-m_{\ell}-1-\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},\gamma^{4/3}],

(48)

and

\begin{split}\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}&(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})\mathbf{1}(E\cap D_{n})\|_{\infty}\\
&\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{a_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}[(m_{\ell}+n-1)^{+}]^{3/2}-\frac{2}{3}[(m_{\ell}-a_{\ell})^{+}]^{3/2}+E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m}_{n},t)\Big).\end{split}

(49)

To see why these bounds complete the proof, observe that by (
48
), the sum in (
47
) has at most
\gamma^{4/3}
non-zero terms, so it suffices to bound each of these terms by the right-hand side of (
45
) since
\log\gamma^{4/3}\leq E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)
. Second, if either
n\leq c_{k}m_{\ell}
or
n\leq c_{k}t^{2}
, then
E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m}_{n},t)\leq E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)
and so by (
49
) the
n
th summand in (
47
) is bounded by the right-hand side of (
45
). If both
n\geq c_{k}|m_{\ell}|
and
n\geq c_{k}t^{2}
, then by the lower bound on
n
in (
48
) we also have that
n\geq d_{k}a_{\ell}
for some small constant
d_{k}
. These constraints together imply that
E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m}_{n},t)=E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)+O_{k}(\sqrt{t}n^{5/4})
. On the other hand, using again that
n\geq c_{k}|m_{\ell}|
, we have that

-\frac{2}{3}[(m_{\ell}+n-1)^{+}]^{3/2}\leq-\frac{2}{3}(m_{\ell}^{+})^{3/2}-\frac{1}{2}n^{3/2}.

Since
O_{k}(\sqrt{t}n^{5/4})-\tfrac{1}{2}n^{3/2}\leq c_{k}^{\prime}t^{3}\leq E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m},t)
, the
n
th summand in (
47
) is bounded by the right-hand side of (
45
) in this case as well.

Step 2: Defining
E
and comparing to a simpler ensemble.
Let
E_{r}
be the set where:

•

For all
s\in[-\gamma^{2/3},\gamma^{2/3}]
, we have

\mathfrak{L}_{\ell+1}(s)+s^{2}>-\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}.

•

We have the inequalities

\displaystyle-0.9\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(r)+r^{2}\leq\mathfrak{L}_{1}(r)+r^{2}\leq\frac{1}{10}\gamma^{4/3}\qquad\text{ and }

\displaystyle-0.9\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}

\displaystyle\leq\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(\pm\gamma^{2/3})+\gamma^{4/3}\leq\mathfrak{L}_{1}(\pm\gamma^{-2/3})+\gamma^{4/3}\leq\frac{1}{10}\gamma^{4/3},

and set
E=E_{0}
(in the final step of the proof we will also use the set
E_{t}
).
By Lemma
4.2
, Corollary
4.4
and a union bound, as long as the constant
\beta_{k}
is large enough we have that

\mathbb{P}(E^{c})\leq e^{-\gamma^{2}},

(50)

which is bounded above by the right-hand side of (
45
) for
\beta_{k}
large enough. Moreover, (
48
) follows from the constraints imposed on
\mathfrak{L}_{\ell+1},\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}
on
E
.

Now let
\bar{\iota}=(0,1,\dots,\ell-1)
, and let

B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{\ell})
be a
k
-tuple of independent Brownian bridges from
(0,\mathfrak{L}^{\ell}(0)-\bar{\iota})
to
(\gamma^{2/3},\mathfrak{L}^{\ell}(\gamma^{2/3})-\bar{\iota})
. Let
f:[0,\gamma^{2/3}]\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
f(s)=-s^{2}-\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}
, and let
\tilde{B}
denote
B
conditioned on the non-intersection event
\operatorname{NI}(f;[t,\gamma^{2/3}])
.

By Lemma
2.4
and the Gibbs property for
\mathfrak{L}
on the set
S
, on the event
E
the bottom line
\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}|_{[0,\gamma^{2/3}]}
stochastically dominates
\tilde{B}_{\ell}
. Therefore on
E
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-\tilde{B}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{B}_{\ell}(0)-\tilde{B}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell}-(\ell-1)).

Now,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(\tilde{B}_{\ell}(0)-\tilde{B}_{\ell}(t)>a_{\ell}-(\ell-1))=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(B\in\operatorname{NI}(f;[t,\gamma^{2/3}]),B_{\ell}(t)<B_{\ell}(0)-a_{\ell}+(\ell-1))}{\mathbb{P}_{0,\gamma^{2/3}}(B(0),B(\gamma^{2/3}),f;[t,\gamma^{2/3}])},

(51)

and we can bound the numerator above and denominator below on the event
E_{0}
using Lemma
4.5
.

Step 3: Bounding (
51
).
Starting with the denominator in (
51
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0,\gamma^{2/3}}(B(0),B(\gamma^{2/3}),f;[t,\gamma^{2/3}])

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{0,\gamma^{2/3}}(B(0),B(\gamma^{2/3}),f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0,\gamma^{2/3}}(B(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},B(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},g),

where
g(s)=-s^{2}
.
Moreover, on
E
we have that

\max(\mathfrak{L}_{1}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},\mathfrak{L}_{1}(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}-g(\gamma^{2/3}))\leq\frac{1}{5}\gamma^{4/3}

as long as
\beta_{k}
was chosen large enough. Similarly,

\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}\geq 2k,\quad\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}-g(\gamma^{2/3}))\geq 2k.

Therefore we are in the setting of Lemma
4.5
.3, and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0,\gamma^{2/3}}(B(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},B(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},g)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\Big(-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}J(B_{i}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},B_{i}(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}+\gamma^{4/3},\gamma^{2/3})-c\ell^{2}\gamma^{2/3}\Big).

Turning to the numerator, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(B\in\operatorname{NI}(f;[t,\gamma^{2/3}]),

\displaystyle B_{\ell}(t)<B_{\ell}(0)-a_{\ell}+(\ell-1))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}(B_{\ell}\in\operatorname{NI}(f;[t,\gamma^{2/3}]),B_{\ell}(t)<B_{\ell}(0)-a_{\ell}+(\ell-1))

\displaystyle\times\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\mathbb{P}_{0,\gamma^{2/3}}(B_{i}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},B_{i}(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},g;[t,\gamma^{2/3}])

The product of
\ell-1
terms above can be bounded above by Lemma
4.5
.1. Note that
t\leq 0.1\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}\leq B_{i}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}
by the definition of
\gamma
as long as
\beta_{k}
is large enough. The remaining
\ell
-term can be bounded using Lemma
4.5
.2. Together we get that the right-hand side above is bounded above by

\displaystyle\exp\Big(-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}J(B_{i}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3},B_{i}(\gamma^{2/3})+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}+\gamma^{4/3},\gamma^{2/3})+c_{k}(t\gamma^{2/3}\log(\gamma)+t^{3}+t|B_{\ell}(0)|)

\displaystyle-\frac{(a_{\ell}-\ell+1)^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}(B_{\ell}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3})^{3/2}-\frac{2}{3}(B_{\ell}(0)+\sqrt{\beta_{k}}\gamma^{2/3}-a_{\ell}+\ell-1)^{3/2}\Big).

Putting together the two bounds in this section, and using that
B_{\ell}(0)=\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-(\ell-1)
and that
\gamma\gg k
gives that (
51
) is bounded above by

\begin{split}\exp\Big(-\frac{a_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}&+\frac{2}{3}[\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)^{+}]^{3/2}-\frac{2}{3}[(\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)-a_{\ell})^{+}]^{3/2}\\
+O_{k}&(t\gamma^{2/3}\log(\gamma)+t^{3}+t|\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)|+\frac{a_{\ell}}{t}+\gamma^{2/3}\sqrt{|\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)|}+\gamma)\Big).\end{split}

(52)

Now, on the set
E\cap D_{n}
, we know that
\mathfrak{L}_{\ell}(0)\in[m_{\ell}+n-1,m_{\ell}+n]
. Applying this, and simplifying the error, we get that (
52
) is bounded above by

\displaystyle\exp

\displaystyle\Big(-\frac{a_{\ell}^{2}}{4t}+\frac{2}{3}[(m_{\ell}+n-1)^{+}]^{3/2}-\frac{2}{3}[(m_{\ell}+n-a_{\ell})^{+}]^{3/2}+E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{m}_{n},t)\Big),

implying the desired bound in (
49
).

Step 4: Completing the proof when
\sigma_{\ell}=0,r=t
.

First, with
E_{t}
as defined in Step 2 above, by noting that
\mathbb{P}(E_{t}^{c})
satisfies the same bound as
\mathbb{P}(E^{c})
in (
50
), it is enough to bound

\mathbb{P}(A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{t}(\mathbf{m})\cap E_{t}).

Our strategy here is to bootstrap from the previous case, by using that the Gibbs property implies that it is unlikely for the lines in
\mathfrak{L}
to be large at time
t
without being large at time
0
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathfrak{L}
outside of the set
\llbracket 1,\ell-1\rrbracket\times[-\gamma^{2/3},t]
, and suppose that we can find
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{m}
with
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}}(D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime}))\geq 1/2
almost surely on the set
D_{\ell-1}^{t}(\mathbf{m})\cap E_{t}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{t}(\mathbf{m})\cap E_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}}(D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime}))\mathbf{1}(A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap D_{\ell-1}^{t}(\mathbf{m})\cap E_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{G}}(D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime}))\mathbf{1}(A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime},\mathbf{a},\sigma)]

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{P}(D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime})\cap A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime},\mathbf{a},\sigma)).

Here the second inequality uses that
A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\subset A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime},\mathbf{a},\sigma)
since
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{m}
, and the final equality uses that
A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m}^{\prime},\mathbf{a},\sigma)
is
\mathcal{G}
-measurable.

Now, on
D_{\ell-1}^{t}(\mathbf{m})\cap E_{t}
and conditional on
\mathcal{G}
, by Lemma
2.4
the vector
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0)
stochastically dominates an
(\ell-1)
-tuple of Brownian bridges
B
from
(-2\gamma^{4/3}-1,\dots,-2\gamma^{4/3}-\ell)
at time

to
(m_{1}-1,\dots,m_{\ell}-\ell)
at time

, conditioned not to intersect each other. The probability of non-intersection is bounded below by

by Lemma

,
so

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0)\geq\mathbf{m}^{\prime})\geq 1/2

for any

for which
\mathbb{P}(B(0)\geq\mathbf{m}^{\prime})\geq 1-e^{-ck\log\gamma}/2.

This holds with
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}=\mathbf{m}-c_{k}\sqrt{t}\gamma^{2/3}
, and so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{\ell-1}^{t}(t)\cap A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{a},\sigma)\cap E_{t})\leq 2\mathbb{P}(D_{\ell-1}^{0}(\mathbf{m}-c_{k}\sqrt{t}\gamma^{2/3})\cap A_{\ell}(\mathbf{m}-c_{k}\sqrt{t}\gamma^{2/3},\mathbf{a},\sigma)),

which satisfies the same upper bound as

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\mathbf{a}\in[0,\infty)^{k}

\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}\geq c_{k}t^{2}
and let
\sigma\in\{0,t\}^{k}
. Let
\hat{\sigma}\in\{0,t\}^{k}
be the unique vector with
\hat{\sigma}_{i}\neq\sigma_{i}
for all

. Next, let
f(\mathbf{a},\sigma)\in\mathscr{C}^{k}_{0}([0,t])
be the unique
k

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}_{i}(\sigma_{i})-\mathfrak{L}_{i}(\hat{\sigma}_{i})\geq a_{i}\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket)\\

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume

\mathfrak{L}_{k}(t)\geq-\alpha\qquad\implies\qquad\mathfrak{L}(0)\geq\textsf{T}f(0)-\alpha,\qquad\mathfrak{L}(t)\geq\textsf{T}f(t)-\alpha,

since the vectors
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t)
are ordered.
Therefore for any

, with notation as in Proposition
4.6

\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}_{i}(\sigma_{i})-\mathfrak{L}_{i}(\hat{\sigma}_{i})\geq a_{i}\forall i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}\in A(\textsf{T}f(0)\vee\textsf{T}f(t)-\alpha,\mathbf{a},\sigma))+\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}_{k}(t)\leq-\alpha).

Setting

, by Corollary
4.4
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{L}_{k}(t)\leq-\alpha)
is bounded above by the right-hand side of (

Tf_{1}\leq c_{k}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}
and that

. For this choice of

5
The upper bound in Theorem
1.1

In this section we prove the upper bound in Theorem
1.1
. Throughout the section we fix
k\in\mathbb{N},t\geq 1
. For this, it is enough to prove that the upper bound holds for
\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]}-\mathfrak{A}^{k}(1)
, since in law
\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]}-\mathfrak{A}^{k}(1)
and
\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[0,t]}-\mathfrak{A}^{k}(0)
only differ by a linear shift which can be wrapped into the error. Moreover, we can work with
\mathfrak{L}=\mathfrak{L}^{t+2,k}
rather than
\mathfrak{A}
, since the conditional probability that
\mathfrak{L}
is non-intersecting–
e^{-O_{k}(t^{3})}
–can again be wrapped into the error term in Theorem
1.1
. The key step is to convert Proposition
4.6
into a density estimate.

For this lemma, we define a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
on
X=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathscr{C}^{k}([1,t+1])
as follows. First let
\mu^{\prime}
denote the product measure on
X
given by Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
and the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(1,0)
to
(t+1,0)
on
\mathscr{C}^{k}([1,t+1])
. Let
\mu
denote the pushforward of
\mu^{\prime}
under the map

(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},f)\mapsto(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},f+L_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}),

where
L_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is the function which is linear in each coordinate and satisfies
L(1)=\mathbf{x},L(t+1)=\mathbf{y})
. Then the law of
(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t+1),\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]})
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
. The next lemma bounds its density.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
Y=Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)
denote the density on
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathscr{C}^{k}([1,t+1])
of
(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t+1),\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]})
against the measure
\mu
. Then we have the following bounds:

1.

For all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathscr{C}^{k}([1,t+1])
we have

Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)\leq\exp(-d_{k}|\inf g_{k}|^{3}\mathbf{1}(\inf g_{k}\leq-c_{k}t^{2})+c_{k}t^{3}).

2.

For all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathscr{C}^{k}([1,t+1])
we have

Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)\leq\exp\left(-\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}_{2}}{4t}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{2}{3}[(x_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+(y_{i}^{+})^{3/2}]\right)+O_{k}(t^{3}+\sqrt{t}(\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{5/4}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{5/4}))\right).

Proof.

Throughout, we work with
\mathfrak{L}
conditional on the event
\mathfrak{L}_{1}>\dots>\mathfrak{L}_{k}>\mathfrak{L}_{k+1}
, which is equal in law to
\mathfrak{A}
. Note that this event has probability
e^{-O_{k}(t^{3})}
.

Bound 1:
First, since
\mathfrak{L}^{k}|_{[0,t+2]}
given
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+2)
is simply given by
k
independent Brownian bridges, the Lebesgue density of
(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
is bounded above by a constant
c_{k}>0
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{L}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]}
has the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(1,\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1))
to
(t+1,\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
given
(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
. Therefore the density of
(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]})
against
\mu
is uniformly bounded by a constant
c_{k}
, and so
Y
is a.s. by
e^{O_{k}(t^{3})}
.

Note that this density bound also holds a.s. if we condition on any information about
\mathfrak{L}
outside of
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket\times[0,t+2]
. In particular, if we let
Y_{\alpha}
be the conditional density of
(\mathfrak{A}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{A}^{k}(t+1),\mathfrak{A}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]})
against
\mu
on the event
A_{\alpha}=\{\inf\mathfrak{L}_{k+1}|_{[0,t+2]}\leq-\alpha\}
and
Z_{\alpha}
the conditional density on
A_{\alpha}^{c}
we have

\displaystyle Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)

\displaystyle=Y_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha})+Z_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)\mathbb{P}(A^{c}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq e^{c_{k}t^{3}}\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha})+Z_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g).

If
-\alpha>\inf g_{k}
, then
Z_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)=0
by the ordering of the lines in
\mathfrak{A}
. Moreover,
\mathbb{P}(A_{\alpha})\leq(t+2)e^{-d_{k}\alpha^{3}}
by Corollary
4.4
for
\alpha\geq c_{k}t^{2}
, which yields the desired bound when
\inf g_{k}\leq-c_{k}t^{2}
.

Bound 2:
Let
f
be the Lebesgue density of
(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
. It is enough to prove the second bound with
Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},g)
replaced by
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, since the
e^{O(t^{3})}
cost of conditioning
\mathfrak{L}
to be non-intersecting folds into the error, and
\mathfrak{L}^{k}|_{[1,t+1]}
has the law of
k
independent Brownian bridges from
(1,\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1))
to
(t+1,\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
given
(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
.

For
\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq}
, let
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
be the conditional Lebesgue density of
(\mathfrak{L}^{k}(1),\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+1))
at
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
on the event

C(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})=\{{\left\lfloor\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0)\right\rfloor}=\mathbf{m},{\left\lfloor\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+2)\right\rfloor}=\mathbf{n}\}.

Since
\mathfrak{L}^{k}|_{[0,t+2]}
is simply a
k
-tuple of Brownian bridges from
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0)
to
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t+2)
, we have that
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})
is bounded above by

c_{k}\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}_{2}}{4t}-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}\|^{2}_{2}}{4}-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{n}\|^{2}_{2}}{4}+\frac{\|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{n}\|^{2}_{2}}{4(t+2)}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}-m_{i}|+|y_{i}-n_{i}|+|n_{i}-m_{i}|}{4}\right).

The final error term comes from the fact that
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(0)\in[\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m}+1]
, and
\mathfrak{L}^{k}(t)\in[\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}+1]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(C(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}))\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{n}\|^{2}_{2}}{4(t+2)}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{2}{3}[(m_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+(n_{i}^{+})^{3/2}]\right)+O_{k}(\sqrt{t}(\|\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{5/4}+\|\mathbf{n}\|_{2}^{5/4})+t^{3})\right)

for

.
Therefore for
\alpha>c_{k}^{\prime}t^{2}

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq c_{k}\exp(-d_{k}\alpha^{3})+

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq},\\
n_{k}\wedge m_{k}\geq-\alpha\end{subarray}}\exp(-D(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}))

(54)

\displaystyle D_{z}(\ell)=\frac{(z-\ell)^{2}}{4}+\frac{2}{3}(\ell^{+})^{3/2}-c_{k}\sqrt{t}|\ell|^{5/4}.

(55)

Now,

\displaystyle-D(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})\leq-\frac{1}{3}(m_{1}\vee n_{1})^{3/2}-100D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

(56)

m_{1}\vee n_{1}>(\|\mathbf{x}|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+2)^{c_{k}^{\prime}}
. All but
(2t^{2}+\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2})^{c_{k}^{\prime}}
terms in the sum in (

) satisfy this bound. Therefore by (
54
), (
55
), and (
56
) we have

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-100D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+c_{k}^{\prime}\log(\|\mathbf{x}|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+2)\right)

\displaystyle+\max_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq},n_{k}\wedge m_{k}\geq-\alpha}\exp(-D(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})+c_{k}^{\prime}\log(\|\mathbf{x}|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+2))

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq},n_{k}\wedge m_{k}\geq-\alpha}\exp(-D(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})+O_{k}(\log(\|\mathbf{x}|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}+2)).

below. Indeed, the bound in the lemma will follow if we can show that for every

whenever

. First, for

D_{z}(\ell)\geq\frac{(z^{+})^{2}}{4}+c_{k}\sqrt{t}|\alpha|^{5/4}=\frac{(z^{+})^{2}}{4}+O_{k}(t^{3}+\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}),

(58)

which is bounded below by the right-hand side of (
57

.
Next, for
\ell\geq 0

\displaystyle D_{z}^{\prime}(\ell)=\frac{2(z-\ell)}{t}-\sqrt{\ell}+\frac{5}{4}c_{k}\sqrt{t}\ell^{1/4}

which completes the proof of (

∎

We are now ready to prove the upper bound in Theorem

.

Corollary 5.2
.

With notation as in Theorem
1.1
,
for
\mu_{k,t}
-a.e.

we have that

(59)

Proof.

With notation as in Lemma
5.1
we can write

X_{k,t}(f)=\int Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}+f(t),f)\sqrt{4\pi t}\exp\left(\frac{\|f(t)\|_{2}^{2}}{4t}\right)d\mathbf{x}.

(60)

Observe that
Y(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}+f(t),f)=0
unless
f_{1}+x_{1}>f_{2}+x_{2}>\dots>f_{k}+x_{k}
. Equivalently, we require

\mathbf{x}-x_{k}{\bf 1}\geq\textsf{T}f(0)-\textsf{T}f_{k}(0){\bf 1}

(61)

where

. here the functional

is the Tetris map from Theorem
1.1
.
Next, by Lemma
5.1
.2 we can see that the integrand in (

\exp\left(-\frac{2}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}[(x_{i}^{+})^{3/2}+([x_{i}+f_{i}(t)]^{+})^{3/2}]+O_{k}(t^{3}+\sqrt{t}(\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{5/4}+\|f(t)\|_{2}^{5/4}))\right).

(62)

Also, by Lemma
5.1
.1, for
x_{k}-\textsf{T}f_{k}(0)\leq-c_{k}t^{2}

\exp\Big(-d_{k}|x_{k}-\textsf{T}f_{k}(0)|^{3}+\|f(t)\|_{2}^{2}+c_{k}t^{3}\Big),

Together with (
62
), this bounds implies that the contribution to the integral from the region where either
x_{1}\geq c_{k}(t^{2}+\textsf{S}(f)^{2/3})
or

is at most

which is bounded above by the right-hand side of (

The size of the remaining region is
\exp(O_{k}(\log(\textsf{S}(f)+2))
, which folds in to the error, so it enough to simply maximize (
62
) subject to the constraint (
61
) on the set where
x_{1}\geq c_{k}(t^{2}+\textsf{S}(f)^{2/3})
or

so it suffices to understand the main term. As the main term

In this section we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1

The proof of the lower bound for Theorem

With these definitions, we have the separation

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
A_{m}
be the event where

|\mathfrak{A}_{i}(y)+y^{2}|\leq m\log^{2/3}(3kE_{t,f})\quad\text{ for all }\quad y\in[-3kE_{t,f},3kE_{t,f}],i\in\llbracket 1,k+1\rrbracket.

Also, for each

, let

be the set where

\mathbb{P}_{x_{i}^{-},x_{i}^{-}+1}(\mathfrak{A}_{i}(x_{i}^{-}),\mathfrak{A}_{i}(x_{i}^{-}+1),\mathfrak{A}_{i+1})\geq\epsilon.

In words,

is the set where the probability that an independent Brownian bridge

from
(x_{i}^{-},\mathfrak{A}_{i}(x_{i}^{-}))

k
-dependent constant

such that for

we have

\mathbb{P}(D_{m})\geq 1/2,\qquad\text{ where }\qquad D_{m}=A_{m}\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}C_{1/m}^{i,-}\cap C_{1/m}^{i,+}

Note the implicit dependence on
t,f
in the events in the lemma; the claim is that we can take

independent of

.

Proof.

By a union bound, it is enough to show that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\inf_{t\geq 1,f}\mathbb{P}A_{m}=1,\qquad\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\inf_{t\geq 1,f}\mathbb{P}C_{\epsilon}^{i,*}=1\quad\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket,*\in\{+,-\}.

By Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
and a union bound, we have

|\mathfrak{A}_{j}(i)+i^{2}|\leq m\log^{2/3}(3kE_{t,f})

for all integers
i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-3kE_{t,f}-1,3kE_{t,f}+1]
and all
j\in\llbracket 1,k+1\rrbracket
with probability tending to
1
with
m
, uniformly in
t
and
f
.
Next, for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-3kE_{t,f}-1,3kE_{t,f}]
and

, by Lemma
3.4
each of the processes

satisfies the conditions of the modulus of continuity Lemma
2.3
with common
\beta=1/5
and
\alpha=c_{k}
. Therefore by another union bound,
\mathbb{P}A_{m}\to 1
as
m\to\infty
uniformly in
t
and
f
.

Next, since

is stationary, the probabilities

, and hence they do not depend on

. Moreover, the strict ordering of the Airy line ensemble implies that for every

\mathbb{P}C^{i,*}_{\epsilon}\to 1
as

, as desired.
∎

One of the main difficulties in applying a Gibbs resampling to make rigorous the picture in Figure

Setting some notation, let

Lemma 6.2
.

Define
\mathfrak{A}^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\pm})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}_{>}

\mathfrak{A}^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\pm})=(\mathfrak{A}_{1}(x_{1}^{\pm}),\dots,\mathfrak{A}_{k}(x_{k}^{\pm})),

(64)

\mathbb{P}_{t,\mathbf{x}^{+}}(\textsf{T}f(t)+2\zeta_{m}\iota,\mathfrak{A}^{k}(\mathbf{x}^{+}),\mathfrak{A}|_{U^{c}})\geq e^{-c_{k}m^{2}\sqrt{E_{t,f}}}.

Step 1: Defining events for the proof of (

Step 2: Bounding

Proposition 6.3
.

We have

X_{k,t}(f)\geq\exp(-\textsf{S}(f)+O_{k}(tE_{t,f}))=\exp(-\textsf{S}(f)+O_{k}(t^{3}+t\textsf{S}(f)^{2/3})).

Proof.

Step 1: A conditional density formula.

\mathcal{F}
be the

We need to work harder to prove Corollary

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
B\in\mathscr{C}_{0}([0,t])
be a Brownian motion, and let

S=S(B)=\sup_{s\in[0,t]}B(s),\qquad I=I(B)=-\inf_{s\in[0,t]}B(s).

Then
(S,I,B(t))
has a Lebesgue density on the set
\{(a,b,x)\in[0,\infty)^{2}\times\mathbb{R}:-b\leq x\leq a\}
given by
h(a,b,x)+h(a,b,-x)
, where

h(a,b,x)=\left[\frac{(2a+2b-x)^{2}}{8\sqrt{\pi}t^{5/2}}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}t^{3/2}}\right]\exp\left(-\frac{(2a+2b-x)^{2}}{4t}\right)

Proof.

This follows from a reflection principle argument. Fix
a\geq 0,b\geq 0
and let

\tau_{a}=\inf\{s\in[0,t]:B(s)=a\},\qquad\tau_{b}=\inf\{s\in[0,t]:B(s)=-b\},

and let
\tau_{1}=\tau_{a}\wedge\tau_{b},\tau_{2}=\tau_{a}\vee\tau_{b}
. Let
\tilde{B}
be the unique continuous process satisfying

\tilde{B}|_{[0,\tau_{1}]}=B|_{[0,\tau_{1}]},\quad\tilde{B}|_{[\tau_{1},\tau_{2}]}-\tilde{B}(\tau_{1})=-B|_{[\tau_{1},\tau_{2}]}+B(\tau_{1}),\quad B|_{[\tau_{2},t]}-\tilde{B}(\tau_{2})=B|_{[\tau_{2},t]}-B(\tau_{2}).

Then by the strong Markov property,
\tilde{B}
is another Brownian motion, and for
x\in[-b,a]
we have

\{S\geq a,I\geq b,B(t)=x\}=\{\tilde{B}(t)=2a+2b+x\}\cup\{-\tilde{B}(t)=2a+2b-x\}.

Therefore

\mathbb{P}(S\geq a,I\geq b,B(t)\in dx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(2a+2b-x)^{2}}{4t}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{(2a+2b+x)^{2}}{4t}\right)\right]dx.

Differentiating in both
a
and
b
gives the joint density.
∎

We also require a straightforward optimization lemma.

Lemma 7.2

Define

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(Y_{i}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})^{3/2}+(Y_{i}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})+x_{i})^{3/2}\right),\qquad Y_{i}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})=\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}a_{j}+\sum_{j=i}^{k}b_{j}.

First, observe that

\displaystyle\beta^{3/2}\max\{\Theta(\mathbf{a}):\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\mathbf{a}\|^{2}=1\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\Theta(\mathbf{a}):\mathbf{a}\in[0,\infty)^{k},\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\beta\}

\displaystyle=\max\{g(\mathbf{a},0,\mathbf{a}):\mathbf{a}\in[0,\infty)^{k},\|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}^{2}=\beta\}

\displaystyle=\max\{g(\mathbf{a},0,\mathbf{a}):(\mathbf{a},0,\mathbf{a})\in D_{\beta}\},

so it suffices to prove that the maximum of
g
is obtained at a point of the form
(\mathbf{a},0,\mathbf{a})
.
For

, observe that the map from

sending

(a_{i},b_{i},x_{i})\mapsto(a_{i}-x_{i},b_{i}+x_{i},-x_{i})

[2\partial_{a_{i}}-\partial_{b_{i}}+2\partial_{x_{i}}]g\geq 0.

(80)

\mu_{1,1}(D_{n})\to 0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\in D_{n})\geq\mu_{1,1}(D_{n})e^{c\log^{1/2}(\mu_{1,1}(D_{n})^{-1})}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

It is not difficult to use Theorem
1.1
to find the optimal value of
c
and show that the
\log^{1/2}
-decay in the exponent is sharp, but we do not pursue this as it is a rather peripheral point.

Proof.

Let
\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}(r)=\mathfrak{A}_{1}(r)-\mathfrak{A}_{1}(0)=\mathfrak{A}_{1}(r)-r^{2}-\mathcal{A}_{1}(0)
and let
B:[0,t]\to\mathbb{R}
be a Brownian motion. Fix
m>0
and consider the class of functions
A_{m,s}\subset\mathscr{C}_{0}([0,1])
such that

f(0)=0,\qquad f(1)\in[-1,s],\qquad\inf f\geq-1,\qquad f(1/2)\geq m.

Equation (
2
) implies that
X_{1,1}(f)
is bounded below by an absolute constant on
A_{m,1}
, and
\hat{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}\in A_{m,1}
implies
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\in A_{m+1/4,2}
. Combining this observation with standard estimates on Brownian motion, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{1}\in A_{m+1/4,2})

\displaystyle\geq c\mathbb{P}(B\in A_{m,1})\geq c^{\prime}e^{m/4}\mathbb{P}(B\in A_{m+1/4,2}).

where
c,c^{\prime},
are absolute constants. Since
\mathbb{P}(B\in A_{m+1/4,2})\geq de^{-c^{\prime\prime}m^{2}}
, taking
D_{n}=A_{n+1/4,2}
gives the result.
∎

Appendix: Proof of Lemma
4.5

As discussed after the statement of Lemma
4.5
, we may assume that
\alpha=0
, since Brownian bridges commute with linear shifts.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.1.

First, the bound is trivial for small
\lambda
if
c>0
is large enough. Therefore throughout the proof we may assume
\lambda>1000
.

Step 1: A Brownian bridge estimate.
Let
B
be a Brownian bridge from
(0,x)
to
(\lambda,y-\lambda^{2})
. Now, let
\zeta=\lambda/{\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor},m={\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor}
and define the mesh

\Pi=\{\pi_{0},\dots,\pi_{m}\}:=\{0,i\zeta,\dots,{\left\lfloor\lambda\right\rfloor}\zeta\}.

Since
t\leq\sqrt{x}+1
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0,\lambda}(x,y+f(\lambda),f,[t,\lambda])\leq\mathbb{P}(B(n)>f(n)\text{ for all }n\in\Pi,n\geq\sqrt{x}+1).

(81)

Now, letting
\pi_{j}
be the smallest element of
\Pi\cap[\sqrt{x}+1,\lambda]
we have that

\mathbb{P}(B(n)>f(n)\quad\text{ for all }n\in\Pi,n<\sqrt{x}+1\mid B(\pi_{j})>f(\pi_{j}))\geq e^{-c\lambda}

for an absolute constant
c>0
. To see this, observe that if
L:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the linear function satisfying
L(0)=x
and
L(\pi_{j})=B(\pi_{j})
for some
B(\pi_{j})>f(\pi_{j})
then
L(s)+c^{\prime}>-s^{2}
for an absolute constant
c^{\prime}>0
, and the probability that
B(n)\geq L(n)+c^{\prime}
for
n\in\Pi,0<n<\sqrt{x}+1
is easily bounded below by
e^{-c\lambda}
. Noting also that
B(0)=x>f(0)=0
yields the above display. Therefore (
81
) is bounded above by

e^{c\lambda}\mathbb{P}(B(n)>f(n)\text{ for all }n\in\Pi).

(82)

Step 2: Converting to a Dirichlet energy bound.
Consider the vector
\Delta B\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by

\Delta B_{i}=B(\pi_{i})-B(\pi_{i-1}),

and let
E
be the minimal value of
\frac{1}{4\zeta}\|\Delta B\|_{2}^{2}
among all possible choices of
B
with
B(n)>f(n)
for all
n\in\Pi
. The vector
\Delta B=\sqrt{2\zeta}N
, where
N
is a standard normal vector on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
conditioned on the event
\sum_{i=1}^{m}N_{i}=(y-\lambda^{2}-x)/\sqrt{2\zeta}
, and so (
82
) is bounded above by

e^{c\lambda}\mathbb{P}(\|N\|^{2}_{2}\geq 2E).

Next, by rotational invariance of the standard normal distribution,
\|N\|_{2}^{2}
has the same distribution as if we conditioned
N
to lie in the hyperplane
\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:x_{1}=(y-\lambda^{2}-x)/\sqrt{2m\zeta}\}
. Therefore

\|N\|_{2}^{2}=(y-\lambda^{2}-x)^{2}/(2m\zeta)+\chi_{m-1},

where
\chi_{m-1}
is a
\chi
-squared random variable with
m-1
degrees of freedom. Therefore letting
I=2E-(y-\lambda^{2}-x)^{2}/(2m\zeta)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\|N\|^{2}_{2}>2E)=\mathbb{P}(\chi_{m-1}\geq I)=\frac{1}{2^{(m-1)/2}\Gamma((m-1)/2)}\int_{I}^{\infty}x^{(m-1)/2-1}e^{-x/2}dx.

Making the substitution
y=x/2
, the right hand side equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma((m-1)/2)}\int_{I/2}^{\infty}y^{(m-1)/2-1}e^{-y}dy.

If
I\geq 2
, then this is bounded above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma((m-1)/2)}\int_{I/2}^{\infty}y^{{\left\lceil(m-1)/2\right\rceil}-1}e^{-y}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{({\left\lceil(m-1)/2\right\rceil}-1)!}{\Gamma((m-1)/2)}e^{-I/2}\sum_{k=0}^{{\left\lceil(m-1)/2\right\rceil}-1}\frac{(I/2)^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-I/2}(I/2+9)^{m/2}.

If
I\leq 2
, then
\mathbb{P}(\chi_{n-1}\geq I)\leq e^{-I/2}(I/2+9)^{m/2}
trivially. Putting everything together and using that
m\zeta=\lambda
we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0,\lambda}(x,y+f(\lambda),f,[t,\lambda])\leq\exp\left(c\lambda-E+\frac{(y-\lambda^{2}-x)^{2}}{4\lambda}+c\lambda\log(E+9)\right).

(83)

Step 2: Estimating the Dirichlet energy
E
.
At this point, the problem is purely deterministic. Let
f_{\Pi}:[0,\lambda]\to\mathbb{R}
be the function given by linearly interpolating between the values of
f
on the mesh
\Pi
. We can then equivalently write
E
as the minimum value of the Dirichlet energy

E(g)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\lambda}|g^{\prime}(s)|^{2}ds

among all functions
g:[0,\lambda]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
g\geq f_{\Pi}
and
g(0)=x,g(\lambda)=y-\lambda^{2}
. The Dirichlet energy minimizer
g_{\Pi}
is the smallest concave function with these properties. Since
f_{\Pi}
itself is concave by the concavity of
f
,
g_{\Pi}
has the following form:

•

There are values
a\leq b\in\Pi
such that
g_{\Pi}
is linear on
[0,a]
and
[b,\lambda]
.

•

g_{\Pi}(z)=f(z)
for all
z\in(a,b)
.

The fact that
g_{\Pi}
dominates
f_{\Pi}
forces
a\leq\sqrt{x}
. Also, the fact that
g_{\Pi}
is concave forces

\frac{(-a^{2}-x)}{a}\geq\frac{-(a+\zeta)^{2}+a^{2}}{\zeta},

which implies that
a>\sqrt{x}-\zeta
. Hence
a={\left\lfloor\sqrt{x}\right\rfloor}_{\Pi}
, the largest element of
\Pi
that is less than or equal to
\sqrt{x}
. Similarly
b={\left\lceil\lambda-\sqrt{y}\right\rceil}_{\Pi}
, the smallest element of
\Pi
that is greater than or equal to
\lambda-\sqrt{y}
. The function
g_{\Pi}
can be thought of as a discretized version of the function
g
introduced after the statement of Lemma
4.5
whose energy equals
E(x,y,\lambda)
. Moreover, a computation shows that
|E(x,y,\lambda)-E(g_{\Pi})|\leq c\lambda
. Finally, since
\sqrt{x}<\lambda/2,\sqrt{y}<\lambda/2
, we have that
E(x,y,\lambda)\leq 2\lambda^{3}
, so (
83
) is bounded above by

\exp\Big(c\lambda\log(1+\lambda)-E(x,y,\lambda)+\frac{(y-\lambda^{2}-x)^{2}}{4\lambda}\Big),

as desired.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.2.

First, the bound holds trivially for small
\lambda
, so we may assume
\lambda>1000
. Also, to simplify notation we write
u=x-a
in the proof. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(

\displaystyle B(s)>f(s)\text{ for all }s\in[t,\lambda],B(t)\leq u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(B(s)>f(s)\text{ for all }s\in[t,\lambda]\mid B(t)\leq u)\mathbb{P}(B(t)\leq u)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{t,\lambda}(u,f(\lambda)+y,f)\mathbb{P}(B(t)\leq u)

\displaystyle\leq c\mathbb{P}_{t,\lambda}(u,f(\lambda)+y,f)\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4t}-\frac{(u-y-\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4(\lambda-t)}+\frac{(x-y-\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4\lambda}\right).

(84)

Now, since Brownian bridge law commutes with affine shifts,

\mathbb{P}_{t,\lambda}(u,f(\lambda)+y,f)=\mathbb{P}_{0,\lambda-t}(u+t^{2},f(\lambda-t)+y,f).

Bounding this with Lemma
4.5
.1, we get that (
84
) is bounded above by

\exp\left(-\frac{a^{2}}{4t}-E(u+t^{2},y,\lambda-t)+\frac{(u+t^{2}-y-(\lambda-t)^{2})^{2}}{4(\lambda-t)}-\frac{(u-y-\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4(\lambda-t)}+\frac{(x-y-\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4\lambda}+c\lambda\log(1+\lambda)\right).

Now,

\frac{(u+t^{2}-y-(\lambda-t)^{2})^{2}}{4(\lambda-t)}-\frac{(u-y-\lambda^{2})^{2}}{4(\lambda-t)}\leq-\lambda^{2}t+O(t^{3}+tx),

where we have bounded the error term by using that
t\leq\sqrt{x}+1\leq 2\lambda/3
and
\sqrt{x},\sqrt{y}\leq\lambda/2
. We also have that

E(x,y,\lambda)-E(u+t^{2},y,\lambda-t)\leq\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}-\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}+\lambda^{2}t+\frac{t^{3}}{3}

which combined with the previous computations gives the desired result.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.3.

For
x,y>0
with
\sqrt{x}<\lambda/2,\sqrt{y}<\lambda/2
, let
g_{x,y}
be the function defined immediately after Lemma
4.5
, and for every
i
, define
h_{i}:[0,\lambda]\to\mathbb{R}
by

h_{i}(s)=g_{x_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,y_{i}-(k+1-i)/2}(s)+(k+1-i)/2.

Then
h_{i}(0)=x_{i},h_{i}(\lambda)=y_{i}-\lambda^{2}
. Moreover, since
g_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}\geq g_{x,y}\geq f
for any
x^{\prime}\geq x
and
y^{\prime}\geq y
and

x_{k},y_{k}\geq 1,\quad x_{i}-x_{i+1}\geq 1,\quad y_{i}-y_{i+1}\leq 1,

we have that
h_{k}-f\geq 1/2
and
h_{i}-h_{i+1}\geq 1/2
for all
i\leq k-1
. Therefore letting
B
denote a
k
-tuple of independent Brownian bridges from
(0,\mathbf{x})
to
(\lambda,\mathbf{y}-\lambda^{2})
, we have that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}_{0,\lambda}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}+f(\lambda),f)&\geq\mathbb{P}(\|B_{i}-h_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/4\text{ for all }i\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket)\\
&=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}(\|B_{i}-h_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/4).\end{split}

(85)

It just remains to bound
\mathbb{P}(\|B_{i}-h_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/4)
below for each
i
. We use the Cameron-Martin theorem for Brownian bridges. Indeed, letting
\mu_{i}
denote the law of
B_{i}
and
\nu_{i}
denote the law of
B_{i}+h_{i}
, the Cameron-Martin theorem yields that

\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\nu_{i}}(W)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{\lambda}[h_{i}^{\prime}(s)]^{2}ds-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\lambda}h_{i}^{\prime}dW+\frac{(x_{i}+\lambda^{2}-y_{i})^{2}}{4\lambda}\right).

Note that the first term in the exponent above equals
E(x_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,y_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,\lambda)
.
Now, standard estimates on the maximum absolute value of a Brownian bridge imply that

\nu_{i}(w\in\mathscr{C}([0,\lambda]):\|w-h_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/4)\geq e^{-c\lambda}.

On the other hand, for a Brownian sample path
W
with
W(0)=h_{i}(0),W(\lambda)=h_{i}(\lambda)
, by integration by parts and stochastic integration by parts we have that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\lambda}h_{i}^{\prime}dW-\int_{0}^{\lambda}(h_{i}^{\prime}(s))^{2}ds\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{\lambda}h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(s)W(s)ds-\int_{0}^{\lambda}h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(s)h_{i}(s)ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\lambda}|h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(s)||W(s)-h_{i}(s)|ds

This is bounded above by
\lambda/2
when
\|W-h_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/4
since
|h_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\leq 2
. Therefore
\mathbb{P}(\|B_{i}-h_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/4)
is bounded below by

\exp\left(-c\lambda-E(x_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,y_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,\lambda)+\frac{(x_{i}+\lambda^{2}-y_{i})^{2}}{4\lambda}\right).

We can complete the proof by appealing to (
85
) and recognizing that

E(x_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,y_{i}-(k+1-i)/2,\lambda)\leq E(x_{i},y_{i},\lambda)+ck(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y})\leq E(x_{i},y_{i},\lambda)+ck\lambda.\qed
