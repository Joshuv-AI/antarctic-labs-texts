1.
Introduction

Let
C
be a smooth projective curve over a field
k
. The
k
-gonality of
C
, denoted by
\textup{gon}_{k}C
, is the least degree of a non-constant
k
-rational morphism
f:C\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Morphisms from modular curves have been extensively studied. Zograf
[
33
]
gave a lower bound for the
\mathbb{C}
-gonality for any modular curve, linear in terms of the index of the corresponding congruence subgroup. Later, Abramovich
[
1
]
and Kim and Sarnak
[
22
, Appendix 2]
improved the constant in that bound. That lower bound is
3.15
of this paper.

Regarding the curve
X_{0}(N)
, Ogg
[
28
]
determined all hyperelliptic, Bars
[
3
]
determined all bielliptic, Hasegawa and Shimura
[
13
]
determined all trigonal curves
X_{0}(N)
over
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{Q}
, and Jeon and Park
[
19
]
determined all tetragonal curves
X_{0}(N)
over
\mathbb{C}
. Recently, Najman and Orlić
[
25
]
determined all curves
X_{0}(N)
with
\mathbb{Q}
-gonality equal to
4,5,
or
6
, and for many of those curves also determined their
\mathbb{C}
-gonality.

Much progress has been made in studying the
\mathbb{Q}
-gonality of the modular quotients of
X_{0}(N)
as well. Furumoto and Hasegawa
[
10
]
determined all hyperelliptic quotients of
X_{0}(N)
, Hasegawa and Shimura
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
determined all trigonal quotients of
X_{0}(N)
over
\mathbb{C}
and partially determined the
\mathbb{Q}
-trigonal quotients (more precisely, they solved all cases when the genus is not equal to
4
). Furthermore, Bars, Gonzalez and Kamel
[
4
]
determined all bielliptic quotients of
X_{0}(N)
for squarefree levels, Jeon
[
17
]
determined all bielliptic curves
X_{0}^{+}(N)
, and Bars, Kamel and Schweizer
[
5
]
determined all bielliptic quotients of
X_{0}(N)
for non-squarefree levels.

The next logical step is determining all tetragonal quotients of
X_{0}(N)
. In this paper we will study the
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{C}
-gonality of the quotient curve
X_{0}^{+}(N)=X_{0}(N)/w_{N}
(
w_{N}
being the Atkin-Lehner involution).

One of the reasons we study the gonality of
X_{0}^{+}(N)
, apart from being an interesting question in itself, is that it can help us to determine the gonality of
X_{0}(N)
. The reason for that is, of course, that we have a natural rational quotient map
X_{0}(N)\to X_{0}^{+}(N)
of degree
2
. Therefore, any map
X_{0}^{+}(N)\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
d
induces a map
X_{0}(N)\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
2d
defined over the same base field.

Also, the existence of rational degree
d
maps to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is closely linked to the problem of determining whether that curve has infinitely many points of degree
d
, as can for example be seen in
[
2
,
12
]
, and, more recently,
[
20
, Theorem 1.4]
.

For a field
K
, a
K
-curve is an elliptic curve defined over some finite separable extension of
K
which is isogenous over
\overline{K}
to all its
\textup{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)
conjugates
[
9
, Page 81]
. The
\mathbb{Q}
-curves are most studied of the
K
-curves. There is a number of papers which use Frey
\mathbb{Q}
-curves defined over quadratic fields to solve Diophantine equations. Some of the more recent ones are
[
23
,
29
]
.

Non-cuspidal rational points on the curve
X_{0}^{+}(N)
correspond to a pair of
\mathbb{Q}
-curves of degree
N
defined over a quadratic field
[
26
, Section 2]
. Similarly, for a number field
K
,
K
-rational points on the curve
X_{0}^{+}(N)
correspond to a pair of
K
-curves of degree
N
defined over a quadratic extension of
K
. Therefore, determining the
\mathbb{Q}
-gonality of curves
X_{0}^{+}(N)
could be useful in determining whether there are infinitely many
K
-curves of a certain degree defined over a quadratic extension of
K
.

Let

3.2.
Castelnuovo-Severi inequality

This is a very useful tool for producing a lower bound on the gonality (see
[
32
, Theorem 3.11.3]
for a proof).

Proposition 3.7

(Castelnuovo-Severi inequality)
.

Let
k
be a perfect field, and let
X,\ Y,\ Z
be curves over
k
with genera
g(X),g(Y),g(Z)
. Let non-constant morphisms
\pi_{Y}:X\rightarrow Y
and
\pi_{Z}:X\rightarrow Z
over
k
be given, and let their degrees be
m
and
n
, respectively. Assume that there is no morphism
X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
of degree
>1
through which both
\pi_{Y}
and
\pi_{Z}
factor. Then the following inequality holds:

(2)

g(X)\leq m\cdot g(Y)+n\cdot g(Z)+(m-1)(n-1).

Since
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are both perfect fields, we can use Castelnuovo-Severi inequality to get lower bounds on both
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{Q}
-gonalities.

As we can see, the assumption of Castelnuovo-Severi inequality is that there is no morphism
X\to X^{\prime}
defined over
\overline{k}
through which both
\pi_{Y}
and
\pi_{Z}
factor. However, recently Khawaja and Siksek have recently shown
[
21
, Theorem 14]
that we can weaken this assumption. Namely, that there is no morphism
X\to X^{\prime}
defined over
k
through which both
\pi_{Y}
and
\pi_{Z}
factor.

Proposition 3.12
.

There exists a degree
4
rational map from

to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
for

N\in\{160,192,224,229,241,257,281\}.

Proof.

In these cases we were not able to find a degree
4

We searched for quadratic points by intersecting the curve

where

[

\mathbb{Q}

b_{0}x_{0}+b_{1}x_{1}+b_{2}x_{2}=0.

In all of these cases we found a function of degree

lying in the Riemann-Roch space of a divisor of the form
P_{1}+P_{2}+Q+\sigma(Q)

is one of the quadratic points we found.
∎

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
X
be a curve of genus
g\geq 5
with
\beta_{2,2}=0
. Suppose that
X
is neither hyperelliptic nor trigonal. Then
\textup{gon}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)\geq 5
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\textup{gon}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)=4
. Then there is a degree
4
morphism
f:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We have
\textup{div}(f)=P-Q
, where
P
is a zero divisor and
Q
is a polar divisor. This means that
\ell(Q)\geq 2
.

Suppose that
\ell(Q)=2
. Since
d=\deg Q=4
, this implies the existence of
g_{4}^{1}
. However, from the assumptions we have
\beta_{2,2}=0
and by
3.19
(by plugging in
d=4
,
r=1
,
p=2
) this is impossible. Therefore, we must have
\ell(Q)\geq 3
and there exists another morphism
g:X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
such that
\textup{div}(g)=R-Q
and
1,f,g
are linearly independent.

Let us fix
P_{0}\in X(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
. Then the morphisms
f^{\prime}:=f-f(P_{0})
and
g^{\prime}:=g-g(P_{0})
are in
L(D)
and have a common zero
P_{0}
. We now have

\textup{div}(f^{\prime})=P_{0}+P^{\prime}-Q,\ \textup{div}(g^{\prime})=P_{0}+R^{\prime}-Q

for some effective degree
3

P^{\prime},R^{\prime}
. Also,
P^{\prime}\neq R^{\prime}
since the morphisms

are linearly independent. Therefore,
\textup{div}(f^{\prime}/g^{\prime})=P^{\prime}-R^{\prime}
and
f^{\prime}/g^{\prime}
is a non-constant morphism from
X
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
of degree
\leq 3
, a contradiction.
∎

To prove the next result, we need to introduce the Clifford index and Clifford dimension.

Definition 3.22
.

For a curve
X
, let
D
be a divisor on
X
and
K
a canonical divisor on
X
. The Clifford index of
D
is the integer

\textup{Cliff}(D):=\deg D-2(\ell(D)-1),

and the Clifford index of
X
is

\textup{Cliff}(X):=\min\{\textup{Cliff}(D)\mid\ell(D)\geq 2,\ell(K-D)\geq 2\}.

The Clifford dimension of
X
is defined to be

\textup{CD}(X):=\min\{\ell(D)-1\mid\ell(D)\geq 2,\ell(K-D)\geq 2,\textup{Cliff}(D)=\textup{Cliff}(X)\}.

For every such divisor
D
that achieves the minimum we say that it computes the Clifford dimension.

The Clifford index gives bounds for the
\mathbb{C}
-gonality of
X

[
8
]
:

\textup{Cliff}(X)+2\leq\textup{gon}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)\leq\textup{Cliff}(X)+3.

We can see that
\textup{CD}(X)\geq 1
immediately from the definition. In most cases we have
\textup{CD}(X)=1
. It is a classical result that
\textup{CD}(X)=2
if and only if
X
is a smoooth plane curve of degree
\geq 5

[
19
, Page 309]
. Martens
[
24
]
proved that
\textup{CD}(X)=3
if and only if
X
is a complete intersection of two irreducible cubic surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, and hence its genus is
10
.

Proposition 3.23
.

The curve
X_{0}^{+}(243)
is tetragonal over
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

We compute the genus
g(X_{0}^{+}(243))=7
and the Betti table. In particular, we get
\beta_{2,2}=9
. By
[
31
, Table 1.]
, this implies the existence of
g_{6}^{2}
. Now we use a similar argument as in
[
19
, Page 310, Case 3]
.

Since there exists a
g_{6}^{2}
, there is a divisor
D
such that
\deg D=6
and
\ell(D)=3
. By definition, for this
D
we have
\textup{Cliff}(D)=2
. We now apply Riemann-Roch theorem to get

3-\ell(K-D)=\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=6-7+1=0.

Therefore,
\ell(K-D)=3
and we have proven that
\textup{Cliff}(X_{0}^{+}(243))\leq 2
.

The homogenous ideal of the canonical embedding of this curve is generated by quadrics (
Magma
function X0NQuotient(243,[243]) gives the canonical embedding for example). By
[
19
, Theorem 1.1]
, this implies that
\textup{Cliff}(X_{0}^{+}(243))\geq 2
and we conclude that
\textup{Cliff}(X_{0}^{+}(243))=2
.

Since
\textup{Cliff}(X_{0}^{+}(243))=\textup{Cliff}(D)=2
, from the definition of the Clifford dimension
\textup{CD}(X)
we get that
\textup{CD}(X_{0}^{+}(243))\leq 2
. Since
g(X_{0}^{+}(243))=7
, it is not a smooth plane curve of degree
d\geq 5
(a smooth plane curve of dimension
d
has genus
(d-1)(d-2)/2
) and we have that
\textup{CD}(X_{0}^{+}(243))=1
.

Let us now take a divisor
D^{\prime}
that computed the Clifford dimension. We have
\ell(D^{\prime})=2
and
\textup{Cliff}(D^{\prime})=\textup{Cliff}(X_{0}^{+}(243))=2
. Therefore, from the definition of
\textup{Cliff}(D^{\prime})
we compute that
\deg D^{\prime}=4
. We may assume that
D^{\prime}>0
, otherwise we can take an effective divisor linearly equivalent to
D^{\prime}
(which exists because
\ell(D^{\prime})\geq 1
) and it will also compute the Clifford dimension of
X_{0}^{+}(243)
.

There exists a non-constant morphism
f\in L(D^{\prime})
and its degree is at most
4
, otherwise
\textup{div}(f)+D^{\prime}\ngeq 0
(here we used that
D^{\prime}
is effective). Since
\textup{gon}_{\mathbb{C}}(X_{0}^{+}(243))\geq 4
by
[
14
]
,
f
is the desired degree
4
morphism from
X_{0}^{+}(243)
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
∎
