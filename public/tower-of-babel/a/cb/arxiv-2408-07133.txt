1.
Introduction

The following question was raised by Müller
[
Mül12
]
: Is every finite group isomorphic to a normalizer quotient of a symmetric group, that is a group of the form
N_{S_{n}}(H)/H
for some
n
and
H\leq S_{n}
? In the present paper we give a positive answer.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a group
T
is a
normalizer quotient
of another group
S
, if there exists a subgroup
H\leq S
such that
N_{S}(H)/H\cong T
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
T
be a finite group. There exists a natural number
n
such that
T
is a normalizer quotient of
S_{n}
.

The question was motivated by a variation of the Inverse Galois Problem, called the Weak Inverse Galois Problem in
[
DL21
]
and studied also in
[
FK78
,
Fri80
,
Tak80
,
Gey83
,
LP18
]
, in which given a base field
F
and finite group
T
one looks for (not necessarily Galois) finite extensions
K/F
with
{\mathrm{Aut}}(K/F)\cong T
. Applications of Theorem
1
(and its corollary below) to this problem appear in a separate paper by the first author
[
Ent24
]
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
T
be a finite group. For all large enough
n\geq n_{0}(T)
, the group
T
is a normalizer quotient of
S_{n}
and of
A_{n}
.

A key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1
is the determination of the normalizer of the inner holomorph of a centerless indecomposable group, which may be of independent interest. First we recall a few definitions.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
denote the group of all permutations of the set
G
. The
left regular representation
of
G
is defined as the map

\lambda:G\to{\mathrm{Sym}}(G):\quad g\mapsto(x\mapsto gx).

Similarly, the
right regular representation
of
G
is defined as the map

\rho:G\to{\mathrm{Sym}}(G):\quad g\mapsto(x\mapsto xg^{-1}).

The
(permutational) holomorph
of
G
is defined to be

{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)=\lambda(G)\rtimes{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)=\rho(G)\rtimes{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)\leq{\mathrm{Sym}}(G).

Alternatively, it is not difficult (see
[
Chi00
, Proposition 7.2]
) to show that

{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)=N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(\lambda(G))=N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(\rho(G)).

In view of this, the
multiple holomorph
of
G
is defined to be

\mathrm{NHol}(G)=N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{Hol}}(G))=N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(\lambda(G)))=N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(\rho(G))).

It is well-known that the quotient
\mathrm{NHol}(G)/{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
acts regularly on the set of regular normal subgroups of
{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
that are isomorphic to
G
(see
[
Koh15
, §1]
for instance) and its structure has been studied for various families of groups
G

[
Koh15
,
CV17
,
CV18
,
CT23
,
Tsa20
]
. Here we will restrict to the group
{\mathrm{Inn}}(G)
of inner automorphisms of
G
and consider the normalizer of the
inner holomorph
of
G
, the latter defined to be

{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)=\lambda(G)\rho(G)=\lambda(G)\rtimes{\mathrm{Inn}}(G)=\rho(G)\rtimes{\mathrm{Inn}}(G)\leq{\mathrm{Sym}}(G).

Clearly
{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
normalizes
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
. The inversion map
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\in{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
defined by
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}(g)=g^{-1}
for any
g\in G
also normalizes
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
because it swaps
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
. Since
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
centralizes
{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)
, it follows that
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
normalizes
{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
and we have

N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))\geq\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle={\mathrm{Hol}}(G)\cup{\mathrm{Hol}}(G){\mathrm{inv}}_{G}.

We note that
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\in{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
if and only if
G
is abelian. In the case that
G
is centerless (that is
Z(G)=1
), we will give a complete characterization of when equality holds, i.e. when
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))
is as small as possible.

Theorem 2
.

Let
G
be a finite centerless group. We have
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
if and only if
G
is indecomposable (that is, cannot be written as a direct product of proper normal subgroups).

Here is a brief sketch of the proof
of Theorem
1
. Let
T
be a finite group of order
|T|>2
and let
p>|T|
be a prime. The
Cornulier-Sambale construction (see §
2
)
produces a finite group
G={\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)

that has outer automorphism group

{\mathrm{Out}}(G)\cong T
. Furthermore we show
that
G
is centerless and
indecomposable when
p>|T|+1
, so Theorem
2
applies and we have
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
. Using this and some
further properties of
G
to be established in

\lx@sectionsign\ref{sec: cs}
, we will show that

H=\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
has the same
normalizer
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(H)=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
and then deduce that
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(H)/H\cong{\mathrm{Out}}(G)\cong T
.

The paper is organized as follows: In
\lx@sectionsign\ref{sec: cs}
we review the Cornulier-Sambale construction and prove some useful properties of it. In
\lx@sectionsign\ref{sec: inhol}
we give the proof of Theorem
2
. Finally, the proofs of Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.2
will be given in §
4
.

2.
The Cornulier-Sambale construction

In the present section we summarize a construction due to Cornulier
[
Cor20
]
and Sambale
[
Sam24
]
, which given a finite group
T
produces a finite group
G
with
{\mathrm{Out}}(G)\cong T
. We will also prove some properties of this construction which will be important for our application.
Cornulier’s original construction was cast in the language of Lie algebras using the Lazar-Mal’cev correspondence
[
Khu98
, §10]
. Sambale described a similar construction in a purely group-theoretic language. Here we follow Sambale
[
Sam24
, §3]
.

In what follows let
p
be a prime and
n
a natural number. Let
F_{n}
be the free group of rank
n
and consider the group
F
with presentation

F=\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\,|\,w(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})^{p}=1\,:\,w\in F_{n}\rangle.

This is the free exponent
p
group on generators
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
. Consider its lower central series

F^{[1]}=F,\quad F^{[l+1]}=[F,F^{[l]}].

For any
k
, the group
F/F^{[k+1]}
is the universal exponent
p
group of nilpotency class
k
on
n
generators, in the sense that if
P_{k}
is a nilpotent group of exponent
p
and nilpotency class
\leq k
, then for any elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in P_{k}
there exists a unique homomorphism
F/F^{[k+1]}\to P_{k}
taking
t_{i}\bmod F^{[k+1]}
to
x_{i}

[
Sam24
, p. 6]
. Moreover
F/F^{[k+1]}
is a finite
p
-group because each
F^{[l]}/F^{[l+1]}
is a finite elementary abelian
p
-group
[
Sam24
, p. 6]
. We define
l
-fold commutators recursively by setting

[g]=g,\quad[g_{1},\ldots,g_{l}]=[g_{1},[g_{2},\ldots,g_{l}]].

Let us also denote
Q=({\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{\times})^{n}\simeq C_{p-1}^{n}
.

The group
Q=({\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
acts by automorphisms on
F

[
Sam24
, p. 8]
. We use a right exponential notation for this action. The action of
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in Q
on the generators is given by
t_{i}^{a}=t_{i}^{a_{i}}
(raising to the
a_{i}
-th power). This action descends to
F/F^{[k+1]}
and satisfies

(2.1)

[t_{i_{1}},\ldots,t_{i_{k}}]^{a}\equiv[t_{i_{1}},\ldots,t_{i_{k}}]^{a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}}\pmod{F^{[k+1]}}

by
[
Sam24
, Lemma 6]
.
Consequently, the action of
Q
also descends to the quotient group
F/N_{k}F^{[k+1]}
where
N_{k}\leq F^{[k]}
is any subgroup generated by elements of the form
[t_{i_{1}},\ldots,t_{i_{k}}]
(it follows from Lemma
2.2
(i) below that
N_{k}F^{[k+1]}\trianglelefteq F^{[k]}
and hence
N_{k}F^{[k+1]}\trianglelefteq F
because
F^{[k]}
is characteristic in
F
).

Now assume that the set of generators
T=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}
is equipped with a group structure with the underlying binary operation denoted by
*
. The following construction appears in
[
Sam24
, §3]
.

Definition 2.1
.

The
Cornulier-Sambale group
of
T
with respect to the prime
p
is defined as follows: first set
P=F/NF^{[n+1]}
, where

N=\langle[t*t_{1},t*t_{2},\ldots,t*t_{n-1},t*t_{1}]\,:\,t\in T\rangle.

As noted above
Q=({\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{\times})^{n}
acts on
P
and we define
{\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)=P\rtimes Q
with respect to this action.

The main property of
{\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)
is the following:

Theorem 3

(Cornulier-Sambale
[
Sam24
, Theorem 8]
)
.

{\mathrm{Out}}({\mathrm{CS}}(T,p))\cong T
whenever
p>|T|>2
.

Next we want to show that whenever
p>|T|+1>2
, the group
G={\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)
is centerless (i.e.
Z(G)=1
) and indecomposable (i.e. one cannot write
G=H\times K,\,1\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\lneq$\cr\raise 0.94722pt\hbox{$\lhd$}\cr}}}H,K\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\lneq$\cr\raise 0.94722pt\hbox{$\lhd$}\cr}}}G
). This will be needed in order to apply Theorem
2
later. First we need a couple of lemmas.

Lemma 2.2
.

The following statements hold.

(i)

F^{[k]}/F^{[k+1]}
is a finite elementary abelian
p
-group, with a basis consisting of (not necessarily all) elements of the form
[t_{i_{1}},\ldots,t_{i_{k}}]F^{[k+1]}
. If
k=1
the basis is precisely
\{t_{i}F^{[2]}:\,1\leq i\leq n
}
.

(ii)

The group
Q
acts diagonally with respect to this basis.

(iii)

If
p>k+1
no basis element is fixed by
Q
.

Proof.

For (i) see
[
Sam24
, p. 6]
(the second assertion of (i) is easily seen by applying the universality of
F/F^{[2]}
as an abelian group of exponent
p
to the group
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
). Part (ii) follows from (
2.1
). For (iii) use (
2.1
) to conclude that the action of
Q
on a basis element
[t_{i_{1}},\ldots,t_{i_{k}}]F^{[k+1]}
is trivial if and only if each generator is repeated a multiple of
p-1
times in the above commutator, which is impossible if
p>k+1
.∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
M=A\rtimes B
be an internal semidirect product of groups. Then

Z(M)=\left\{(a,b):\,b\in Z(B),\,a^{B}=\{a\},\,{\mathrm{conj}}(ab)|_{A}=\mathrm{id}_{A}\right\}.

Here
{\mathrm{conj}}(ab)\in{\mathrm{Inn}}(A\rtimes B)
denotes conjugation by
ab
and
a^{B}
is the orbit of
a
under conjugation by
B
.

Proof.

Every element in
M
can be written uniquely as
ab
for
a\in A,b\in B
. The condition
ab\in Z(M)
is equivalent to
aba^{\prime}=a^{\prime}ab,\,abb^{\prime}=b^{\prime}ab
for all
a^{\prime}\in A,b^{\prime}\in B
. These can be rewritten as

a(ba^{\prime}b^{-1})b=a^{\prime}ab,\,abb^{\prime}=(b^{\prime}ab^{\prime-1})b^{\prime}b.

Since
A\cap B=1
, the above are equivalent to

a^{\prime}=(ab)^{-1}a^{\prime}(ab),\,a=b^{\prime}ab^{\prime-1},\,bb^{\prime}=b^{\prime}b.

The validity of these conditions for all
a^{\prime}\in A,\,b^{\prime}\in B
precisely means that
{\mathrm{conj}}(ab)|_{A}=\mathrm{id}_{A},\,a^{B}=\{a\}
, and
b\in Z(B)
, as desired. ∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Z({\mathrm{CS}}(T,p))=1
whenever
p>|T|+1>2
.

Proof.

Write
G={\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)=P\rtimes Q
as in Definition
2.1
. By Lemma
2.3
it is enough to show that
Q
has no nontrivial fixed elements in
P
and that it acts faithfully on
P
. We have a normal series

(2.2)

1\trianglelefteq F^{[n]}/NF^{[n+1]}\trianglelefteq F^{[n-1]}/NF^{[n+1]}\trianglelefteq\ldots\trianglelefteq F^{[1]}/NF^{[n+1]}=P.

The action of
Q
descends to the quotients of this series, which are

F^{[n]}/NF^{[n+1]},\,F^{[n-1]}/F^{[n]},\,\ldots,F^{[1]}/F^{[2]},

and if
Q
has a nontrivial fixed element in
P
then it has a nontrivial fixed element in one of these quotients (if
1\neq x\in P
is fixed by
Q
look at the first subgroup in (
2.2
) containing
x
and the image of
x
in the corresponding quotient). Moreover, if the action of
Q
on one of these quotients is faithful, then so is its action on
P
. By Lemma
2.2
and the condition
p>|T|+1=n+1
, each of the above quotients is an elementary abelian
p
-group having a basis consisting of elements not fixed by
Q
, and
Q
acts diagonally with respect to this basis. This implies that no nontrivial element of these quotients is fixed by
Q
and that the action of
Q
on
F^{[1]}/F^{[2]}
is faithful, which concludes the proof.∎

Lemma 2.5
.

{\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)
is indecomposable whenever
p>|T|>2
.

Proof.

We use the notation of Definition
2.1
. Assume by way of contradiction that
{\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)=P\rtimes Q=H\times K
for some proper normal subgroups
H,K
. Denote
H^{\prime}=H\cap P,\,K^{\prime}=K\cap P
. Since
P
is the unique
p
-Sylow subgroup of
P\rtimes Q
, we have that
H^{\prime},K^{\prime}
are the unique
p
-Sylow subgroups of
H,K
respectively and
P=H^{\prime}K^{\prime}
.
In what follows we identify
P/(F^{[2]}/NF^{[n+1]})
with
F/F^{[2]}
and
P/(F^{[3]}/NF^{[n+1]})
with
F/F^{[3]}
in the natural way (recall (
2.2
) and the assumption
n=|T|>2
).
The congruences we write down below are well-defined in light of these identifications.

Let
x\in H^{\prime}
and write (as one may by the case
k=1
of Lemma
2.2
(i))
x\equiv t_{i_{1}}^{\lambda_{1}}t_{i_{2}}^{\lambda_{2}}\cdots t_{i_{r}}^{\lambda_{r}}\pmod{F^{[2]}}
with
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
distinct and
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{\times}
. Assume that
yq\in K
for some
y\in P,\,q\in Q
. Since
K
centralizes
H
we have
xyq=yqx=y(qxq^{-1})q
and therefore
y^{-1}xy=qxq^{-1}=x^{q^{-1}}
. It follows that

\prod_{j=1}^{r}t_{i_{j}}^{\lambda_{j}}\equiv x\equiv y^{-1}xy\equiv x^{q^{-1}}\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{r}t_{i_{j}}^{\lambda_{j}/a_{i_{j}}}\pmod{F^{[2]}},

where
q=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
.
Using the case
k=1
of Lemma
2.2
(i) we conclude that
a_{i_{j}}=1,\,1\leq j\leq r
. Thus for any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\pi(K)
(
\pi:P\rtimes Q\to Q
is the projection to the second factor) we have
a_{l}=1
for any index
l
for which there exists an element
x\in H^{\prime}
such that the basis element
t_{l}\bmod F^{[2]}
occurs (with a nonzero coefficient) in the expansion of
x\bmod F^{[2]}
in the basis
t_{1}\bmod F^{[2]},\,\ldots\,,t_{n}\bmod F^{[2]}
. Denote the set of such indices
l
by
L
.

Similarly, for any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\pi(H)
we have
a_{m}=1
for any index
m
for which there exists an element
y\in K^{\prime}
such that the basis element
t_{m}\bmod F^{[2]}
occurs (with a nonzero coefficient) in the expansion of
y\bmod F^{[2]}
in the basis
t_{1}\bmod F^{[2]},\,\ldots\,,t_{n}\bmod F^{[2]}
. Denote the set of such indices
m
by
M
.

Since
\pi(HK)=Q
and
H^{\prime}K^{\prime}=P
, it follows that
L,M
are disjoint (if
r\in L\cap M
were to exist then the
r
-th coordinate of any
q\in\pi(HK)
would always be
1
),
\{1,\ldots,n\}=L\cup M
(if
r\not\in L\cup M
then the natural surjection
P\rightarrow F/F^{[2]}
would not contain
t_{r}\bmod F^{[2]}
in its image), and

(2.3)

\pi(H)=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):\,a_{m}=1\,\,\forall m\in M\},\quad\pi(K)=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):\,a_{l}=1\,\,\forall l\in L\},

(2.4)

H^{\prime}\bmod F^{[2]}=\left\{\prod_{l\in L}t_{l}^{a_{l}}\bmod F^{[2]}:\,a_{l}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}\right\},\quad K^{\prime}\bmod F^{[2]}=\left\{\prod_{m\in M}t_{m}^{a_{m}}\bmod F^{[2]}:\,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{p}\right\}.

First assume that
L,M\neq\emptyset
. Let
l\in L,m\in M
and let
x\in H^{\prime},y\in K^{\prime}
be such that
x\equiv t_{l}\pmod{F^{[2]}},\,y\equiv t_{m}\pmod{F^{[2]}}.
By
[
Sam24
, Lemma 6]
we have
[x,y]\equiv[t_{l},t_{m}]\not\equiv 1\pmod{F^{[3]}}
(the last incongruence follows from the universality of
F/F^{[3]}
as an exponent
p
group of nilpotency class
2
on the generators
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
and the fact that there is a nonabelian group of exponent
p
and nilpotency class
2
, e.g. the group of unipotent

upper triangular matrices over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
). But
H^{\prime},K^{\prime}
centralize each other, so
[x,y]=1
, a contradiction.

Next assume
L=\emptyset
, in which case
M=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. From (
2.3
),(
2.4
) we have
\pi(K)=Q
and the projection
K^{\prime}\to F/F^{[2]}
is surjective. Denote by
\overline{K}^{\prime}
the preimage of
K^{\prime}
under the quotient map
F\to F/NF^{[n+1]}=P
. The projection
\overline{K}^{\prime}\to F/F^{[2]}
is surjective and it follows by induction on
k
using
[
Sam24
, Lemma 6]
(which implies that
F^{[k-1]}/F^{[k]}
is generated by the classes of
(k-1)
-fold commutators of elements from

) that the projection
\overline{K}^{\prime}\to F/F^{[k]}
is surjective for every

. In particular the projection
\overline{K}^{\prime}\to F/F^{[n+1]}
is surjective, and a fortiori the projection
\overline{K}^{\prime}\to P
is surjective, i.e.

. Since

The case

is handled similarly, so we obtain a contradiction in all cases, establishing the assertion of the lemma.

To apply Theorem

to prove Theorem

, we will need one more property of the group

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
M=(A\times A)\rtimes C_{2}
, where
A
is a finite abelian group with
|A|>2
and the action of the nontrivial element of
C_{2}
on
A\times A
is by swapping the coordinates. Then
A\times A
is a characteristic subgroup of
M
.

Proof.

We regard
A\times A
and
C_{2}
as subgroups of
M
and let us write elements of
M
in the form
(a_{1},a_{2})c
for
a_{1},a_{2}\in A,\,c\in C_{2}
. We will show that
A\times A
is the unique abelian subgroup of
M
of index 2, which implies the assertion of the lemma.

Let
B\leq M
be an abelian subgroup of index 2. Assume by way of contradiction that
B\neq A\times A
. Then
D=B\cap(A\times A)
is a subgroup of
A\times A
of index
2
. Let
b\in B\setminus A\times A
. Then
b
centralizes
D
(since
B
is abelian). Write
b=(a_{1},a_{2})i
for
a_{1},a_{2}\in A,\,1\neq i\in C_{2}
.

Since
A\times A
is abelian, for any
(d_{1},d_{2})\in D
we have

(d_{1},d_{2})=b^{-1}(d_{1},d_{2})b=i^{-1}(a_{1},a_{2})^{-1}(d_{1},d_{2})(a_{1},a_{2})i=i^{-1}(d_{1},d_{2})i=(d_{2},d_{1}),

so
d_{1}=d_{2}
. Thus
D
is contained in the diagonal
\Delta_{A}\leq A\times A
, which has index
|A|>2
in
A\times A
. Since
[A\times A:D]=2
, we have arrived at a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
G={\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)
. Then
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
is a characteristic subgroup of
\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
whenever
p>|T|+1>2
.

Proof.

Since the group
G
is centerless by Lemma
2.4
, we have
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)=\lambda(G)\times\rho(G)
. Now, conjugation by
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
swaps
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
: more precisely, we have
\lambda(g){\mathrm{inv}}_{G}={\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rho(g)
for any
g\in G
. This means that
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
normalizes
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
and therefore
\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle={\mathrm{InHol}}(G)\rtimes\langle{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
. Since
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
is an involution, in particular
[\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle:{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)]=2
.

We may write
G=P\rtimes Q
where
P
is the unique (since it is normal)
p
-Sylow subgroup of
G
and
Q\cong C_{p-1}^{|T|}
. Then
\lambda(P)\rho(P)
is the unique
p
-Sylow subgroup of
\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
(it is normal because conjugation by
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
swaps
\lambda(P)
and
\rho(P)
) and is therefore a characteristic subgroup of
\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
. Thus it remains to show that
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)/\lambda(P)\rho(P)
is characteristic in
\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle/\lambda(P)\rho(P){\xrightarrow{\sim}}(Q\times Q)\rtimes C_{2}
(the action of
1\neq i\in C_{2}
is by swapping the coordinates). But under the above isomorphism
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)/\lambda(P)\rho(P)
corresponds to
Q\times Q
, which is characteristic in
(Q\times Q)\rtimes C_{2}
by Lemma
2.6
. This completes the proof.∎

3.
Normalizer of the inner holomorph

In what follows let
G
be a finite group. A subgroup
R\leq{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
is said to be
regular
if its natural action on
G
is regular, or equivalently, if the map

R\to G:\quad\sigma\mapsto\sigma(1_{G})

is bijective. Regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
come in pairs in some sense because if
R
is a regular subgroup of
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
, then so is its centralizer
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R)
. Moreover
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R)\cong R
and
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R))=R
. Of course, if
R
is abelian, then
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R)=R
and we do not have a genuine pair of regular subgroups. All of these facts are easy to verify or one can see
[
Koh07
, §3]
. For example,
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
are regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
isomorphic to
G
, and they are centralizers of each other. It is well-known that isomorphic regular subgroups are conjugates in
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
(a proof can be found in
[
Tsa19
, Lemma 2.1]
). In particular, the regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
that are isomorphic to
G
are exactly the conjugates of
\lambda(G)
. For example,
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
are conjugates via the inversion map
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
.

With the above observations, we can give a characterization of when the equality
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
holds in terms of regular subgroups, as follows.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
if and only if
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
are the only regular subgroups
R\leq{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
isomorphic to
G
for which
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R)\leq{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)=\lambda(G)\rho(G)
, for any
\pi\in{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
it is clear that

\displaystyle\pi\in N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))

\displaystyle\iff\pi^{-1}\lambda(G)\pi,\pi^{-1}\rho(G)\pi\leq{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)

\displaystyle\iff\pi^{-1}\lambda(G)\pi,C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(\pi^{-1}\lambda(G)\pi)\leq{\mathrm{InHol}}(G).

Moreover, since
{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)=N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(\lambda(G))
and
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
swaps
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}^{-1}\lambda(G){\mathrm{inv}}_{G}=\rho(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}\lambda(G)\pi=\lambda(G)

\displaystyle\iff\pi\in{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}\lambda(G)\pi=\rho(G)

\displaystyle\iff\pi\in{\mathrm{Hol}}(G){\mathrm{inv}}_{G}.

We now deduce the assertion of the lemma because the regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)
isomorphic to
G
are precisely the conjugates of
\lambda(G)
.
∎

In the case that
G
is centerless, the product

{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)=\lambda(G)\rho(G)=\lambda(G)\times\rho(G)

is direct, and the regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
(not necessarily isomorphic to
G
) may be parametrized in terms of these so-called fixed point free pairs of homomorphisms. We will not need it but let us remark that there is an extension of this result to all regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
in
[
Tsa23
, Proposition 2.5]
. Below let
N
denote a finite group of the same order as
G
.

Definition 3.2
.

A pair
f,g:N\to G
of homomorphisms is said to be
fixed point free
if
f(x)=g(x)
has no solutions other than
x=1_{N}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any fixed point free pair
f,g:N\to G
of homomorphisms, the set

R_{(f,g)}=\{\lambda(f(x))\rho(g(x)):x\in N\}

is a regular subgroup of
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
isomorphic to
N
. In the case that
G
is centerless, every regular subgroup of
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
isomorphic to
N
arises in this way.

Proof.

See
[
BC12
, §2 and Proposition 6]
.
∎

Here we are interested in the regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
whose centralizer also lies inside
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f,g:N\to G
and
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}:N\to G
be two fixed point free pairs of homomorphisms. In the case that
G
is centerless, we have
R_{(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})}=C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R_{(f,g)})
if and only if
[f(N),f^{\prime}(N)]=[g(N),g^{\prime}(N)]=1
.

Proof.

For any
x,y\in N
, note that

\displaystyle\lambda(f(x))\rho(g(x))\cdot\lambda(f^{\prime}(y))\rho(g^{\prime}(y))

\displaystyle=\lambda(f(x)f^{\prime}(y))\rho(g(x)g^{\prime}(y))

\displaystyle\lambda(f^{\prime}(y))\rho(g^{\prime}(y))\cdot\lambda(f(x))\rho(g(x))

\displaystyle=\lambda(f^{\prime}(y)f(x))\rho(g^{\prime}(y)g(x))

because
\lambda(G)
and
\rho(G)
centralize each other. Since
G
is centerless, we have
\lambda(G)\cap\rho(G)=1
, and the above elements are equal if and only if
f(x)f^{\prime}(y)=f^{\prime}(y)f(x),\,g(x)g^{\prime}(y)=g^{\prime}(y)g(x)
. It follows that
R_{(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})}
and
R_{(f,g)}
are centralizers of each other if and only if
[f(N),f^{\prime}(N)]=[g(N),g^{\prime}(N)]=1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

First suppose that
G
is decomposable. Then
G=H\times K
for some proper nontrivial normal subgroups
H
and
K
. Clearly
f,g:G\to G
and symmetrically
g,f:G\to G
defined by

f(hk)=h,\quad g(hk)=k\quad(h\in H,k\in K)

are fixed point free pairs of homomorphisms. Then
R_{(f,g)}
and
R_{(g,f)}
are regular subgroups of
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
that are isomorphic to
G
by Lemma
3.3
, and they are neither
\lambda(G)
nor
\rho(G)
by the assumption on
H,K
. Since
f(G)=H
and
g(G)=K
centralize each other, we have
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R_{(f,g)})=R_{(g,f)}\leq{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
by Lemma
3.4
, and so
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))
strictly contains
\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
by Lemma
3.1
.

Next suppose that
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))
strictly contains
\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
. Then
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
contains a regular subgroup
R
isomorphic to
G
that is neither
\lambda(G)
nor
\rho(G)
for which
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R)
also lies in
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
, by Lemma
3.1
. We also know from Lemma
3.3
that
R=R_{(f,g)}
and
C_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(R)=R_{(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})}
for some fixed point free pairs
f,g:G\to G
and
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}:G\to G
of homomorphisms. Moreover, we have

(3.1)

[f(G),f^{\prime}(G)]=[g(G),g^{\prime}(G)]=1

by Lemma
3.4
. It is a consequence of fixed point free-ness that

\ker(f)\cap\ker(g)=\ker(f^{\prime})\cap\ker(g^{\prime})=1,

and as shown in
[
BC12
, Proposition 1]
, that

G=f(G)g(G)=f^{\prime}(G)g^{\prime}(G).

Since
G
is centerless, the above and (
3.1
) yield that

f(G)\cap g(G)=f^{\prime}(G)\cap g^{\prime}(G)=1.

But then we get the decomposition
G=\ker(f)\times\ker(g)
because

\displaystyle|\ker(f)\ker(g)|

\displaystyle=|\ker(f)||\ker(g)|

\displaystyle=|G|/|f(G)|\cdot|G|/|g(G)|

\displaystyle=|G|^{2}/|f(G)g(G)|

\displaystyle=|G|.

The assumption
R\neq\lambda(G),\rho(G)
implies that
f
and
g
are both nontrivial, so
\ker(f)
and
\ker(g)
are proper normal subgroups of
G
. It follows that
G=\ker(f)\times\ker(g)
is decomposable.
∎

4.
Derivation of Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.2

Proof of Theorem
1
.

If
T=1
we may take
n=1,\,H=S_{1}
, and if
T\cong C_{2}
we may take
n=2,\,H=1
. Hence we may assume
|T|>2
. Pick a prime
p>|T|+1
and denote
G={\mathrm{CS}}(T,p)
. By Theorem
3
we have
{\mathrm{Out}}(G)\cong T
. By Lemmas
2.4
and
2.5
the group
G
is centerless and indecomposable, so Theorem
2
applies and we have
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
. Take

H=\langle{\mathrm{InHol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle\leq{\mathrm{Sym}}(G).

We will show that
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(H)/H\cong T
, from which the assertion of Theorem
1
follows immediately.

Since
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
is a characteristic subgroup of
H
by Lemma
2.7
, we have

N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(H)\leq N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}({\mathrm{InHol}}(G))=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle.

The reverse inclusion is obvious, so we have equality
N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(H)=\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle
. Moreover, since
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}
normalizes
{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
and
{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)
we have

\langle{\mathrm{Hol}}(G),{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle={\mathrm{Hol}}(G)\rtimes\langle{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle,\quad H={\mathrm{InHol}}(G)\rtimes\langle{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle

(here
{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\not\in{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)
because
G
is nonabelian) and therefore

N_{{\mathrm{Sym}}(G)}(H)/H\cong\frac{{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)\rtimes\langle{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle}{{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)\rtimes\langle{\mathrm{inv}}_{G}\rangle}\cong{\mathrm{Hol}}(G)/{\mathrm{InHol}}(G)\cong\frac{\lambda(G)\rtimes{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)}{\lambda(G)\rtimes{\mathrm{Inn}}(G)}\cong{\mathrm{Aut}}(G)/{\mathrm{Inn}}(G)\\
={\mathrm{Out}}(G)\cong T.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume that
T
is a normalizer quotient of
S_{n}
. Then it is also a normalizer quotient of
S_{m}
and of
A_{m}
for any
m\geq 2n+1
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
n>1
, otherwise the assertion is obvious. We naturally view
S_{n}\times S_{m-n}
as a subgroup of
S_{m}
, letting
S_{n}
act on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
S_{m-n}
on
\{n+1,\ldots,m\}
. Let
H\leq S_{n}
be such that
N_{S_{n}}(H)/H\cong T
. Define

H_{1}=H\times S_{m-n}
and
H_{2}=(H\times S_{m-n})\cap A_{m}
. We claim that
N_{S_{m}}(H_{1})/H_{1}\cong N_{A_{m}}(H_{2})/H_{2}\cong T
, which would establish the assertion of the lemma.

Since
m-n>n
,
H_{i}\leq S_{n}\times S_{m-n}
, and
H_{i}
acts transitively on
\{n+1,\ldots,m\}
(it contains
A_{m-n}
and
m-n\geq 3
because
n>1
), the only invariant subset
X\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
of size
m-n
for
H_{i}
is
\{n+1,\ldots,m\}
. Therefore if
g\in S_{m}
normalizes
H_{i}
, then
g
preserves
\{n+1,\ldots,m\}
and so
g\in S_{n}\times S_{m-n}
. We conclude that
N_{S_{m}}(H_{i})\leq S_{n}\times S_{m-n}
and in particular
N_{A_{m}}(H_{2})\leq N_{S_{m}}(H_{2})\leq S_{n}\times S_{m-n}
.

In the case of
H_{1}=H\times S_{m-n}
it follows that

N_{S_{m}}(H_{1})=N_{S_{n}}(H)\times N_{S_{m-n}}(S_{m-n})=N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n}

and therefore
N_{S_{m}}(H_{1})/H_{1}\cong N_{S_{n}}(H)/H\cong T
. It remains to show the same for
H_{2}=H_{1}\cap A_{m}
.

Clearly
N_{S_{m}}(H_{1})\leq N_{S_{m}}(H_{2})
because
A_{m}\trianglelefteq S_{m}
, so in particular
(N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n})\cap A_{m}\leq N_{A_{m}}(H_{2})
. We will show the reverse inclusion. Let
g=(a,b)\in N_{A_{m}}(H_{2})
, where
a\in S_{n},b\in S_{m-n}
. For any
h\in H
we can pick
c\in S_{m-n}
such that
(h,c)\in H_{2}
. Since
(a,b)
normalizes
H_{2}
we have
a^{-1}ha\in H
and therefore
a\in N_{S_{n}}(H)
(since
h\in H
can be arbitrary). Thus
g=(a,b)\in(N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n})\cap A_{m}
. We conclude that
N_{A_{m}}(H_{2})=(N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n})\cap A_{m}
.

Since
H\times S_{m-n}
is not contained in
A_{m}
, we see that

[\Gamma:\Gamma\cap A_{m}]=[\Gamma A_{m}:A_{m}]=[S_{m}:A_{m}]=2

for both
\Gamma=N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n},\,H\times S_{m-n}
, and so the injection

N_{A_{m}}(H_{2})/H_{2}=\frac{(N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n})\cap A_{m}}{(H\times S_{m-n})\cap A_{m}}\hookrightarrow\frac{N_{S_{n}}(H)\times S_{m-n}}{H\times S_{m-n}}\cong T

is an isomorphism (since both quotients have the same size).
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Immediate from Theorem
1
and Lemma
4.1
(take
n_{0}(T)=2n+1
where
T
is a normalizer quotient of
S_{n}
).∎
