1.
Introduction

Let
\displaystyle K
be a number field of degree
\displaystyle n
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}
its ring of integers, and
\displaystyle d_{K}
its absolute discriminant. It is well known that
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}
is a free abelian group of rank
\displaystyle n
and by the The fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups,
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
for every primitive element
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}
of
\displaystyle K
. Let
\displaystyle ind(\theta)=(\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
. Then
\displaystyle ind(\theta)
is called the index of
\displaystyle\theta
. The index of the number field
\displaystyle K
is defined by
\displaystyle i(K)=GCD((\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])\mid K=\mathbb{Q}(\theta)\mbox{ and }\theta\in\mathbb{Z}_{K})
. A rational prime integer
\displaystyle p
dividing
\displaystyle i(K)
is called a prime common index divisor of
\displaystyle K
.
The number field
\displaystyle K
is called monogenic if it admits a
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
basis of type
\displaystyle(1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^{n-1})
for some
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}
. Remark that if
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}
has a power integral basis, then
\displaystyle i(K)=1
. Therefore a field having a prime common index divisor is not monogenic.
Monogenity of number fields is a classical problem of algebraic number theory, going back to Dedekind, Hasse and Hensel, see for instance
[
21
,
22
]
and
[
17
]
for the present state of this area.
It is called a problem of Hasse to give an arithmetic characterization of those number fields which are monogenic
[
21
,
22
,
19
]
.
For any primitive element
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}
of
\displaystyle K
,
we denote by

ind(\theta)=(\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])

the index of
\displaystyle\theta
, that is
the index of the
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
-module
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
in the free-
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}
-module
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}
of rank
\displaystyle n
.
As it is known
[
17
]
, we have

|\triangle(\theta)|=ind(\theta)^{2}\cdot|d_{K}|

where
\displaystyle\triangle(\theta)
is the discriminant of the minimal polynomial of
\displaystyle\theta
over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
.

Clearly,
\displaystyle ind(\theta)=1
for some primitive element
\displaystyle\theta\in\mathbb{Z}_{K}
of
\displaystyle K
if and only if
\displaystyle(1,\theta,\ldots,\theta^{n-1})
is a power integral basis
of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}
.

The problem of testing the monogenity of number fields and constructing power integral bases have been intensively studied during the last four decades mainly by Gaál, Györy, Nakahara, Pohst and their collaborators (see for instance

In 1871, Dedekind was the first who gave an example of a number field with non trivial index, he considered the cubic field
\displaystyle K
generated by a root of
\displaystyle x^{3}-x^{2}-2x-8

\displaystyle 2
splits completely in
\displaystyle K

[

[

\displaystyle K
is monogenic, then we would be able to find a cubic polynomial defining
\displaystyle K
, that splits completely into distinct polynomials of degree
\displaystyle 1
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]
. Since there is only two distinct polynomials of degree
\displaystyle 1
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]

\displaystyle 1930

\displaystyle 8

[
11

\displaystyle K
of degree
\displaystyle n\leq 7

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(i(K))
is explicitly determined by the factorization of
\displaystyle p\mathbb{Z}_{K}
into powers of prime ideals of
\displaystyle K
for every positive rational prime integer
\displaystyle p\leq n
. This motivated Narkiewicz to ask a very important question, stated as problem 22 in Narkiewicz’s book (
[
34

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(i(K))
for a given rational prime
\displaystyle p
dividing
\displaystyle i(K)
. In
[
33

, Nakahara studied the index of non-cyclic but abelian biquadratic number fields. He showed that the field index of such fields is in the set
\displaystyle\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}
. In
[
15
]
Gaál et al. characterized the field indices of biquadratic number fields having Galois group
\displaystyle V_{4}
and they proved that
\displaystyle i(K)\in\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}
.
Recently, many authors are interested on monogenity of number fields defined by trinomials.

3.
A short introduction to prime ideal factorization based on Newton polygons

In 1894, Hensel
developed a powerful approach by showing that for every prime integer
\displaystyle p
, the prime ideals of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}

lying above
\displaystyle p
are in one–one correspondence with
monic irreducible factors of
\displaystyle F(x)
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}[x]
. For every prime ideal
corresponding to any irreducible factor in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}[x]
, the
ramification index and the residue degree together are the same as
those of the local field defined by the associated irreducible factor

[
24
]
. Since then, to factorize
\displaystyle p\mathbb{Z}_{K}
, we need to factorize
\displaystyle F(x)
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{p}[x]
. Newton’s polygon techniques can be used to refine the factorization. This is a standard method which is rather technical but
very efficient to apply. We have introduced the corresponding concepts in several former papers.
Here we only give a brief introduction which makes our proofs understandable.
For a detailed description, we refer to Ore’s Paper
[
37
]
and Guardia, Montes and Nart’s paper
[

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{2}
, we conclude that
\displaystyle\omega(\theta_{i})=1/7
for every
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,7
. Thus
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(s_{7})=\omega(\theta_{1}\cdots\theta_{7})=7\times 1/7=1
and
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(s_{i})\geq i/7
for every
\displaystyle i=1,\dots,6
, which means that
\displaystyle g(x)
is a
\displaystyle 2
-Eisenstein polynomial. Hence
\displaystyle 2
does not divide the index
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
. As
\displaystyle 2
is the unique positive prime integer candidate to divide
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
, we conclude that for every prime integer
\displaystyle p
,
\displaystyle p
does not divide
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\theta])
, which means that
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}=\mathbb{Z}[\theta]
.

5.
Examples

Let
\displaystyle F=x^{7}+ax+b\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a monic irreducible polynomial and
\displaystyle K
a number field generated by a root
\displaystyle\alpha
of
\displaystyle F(x)
. In the following examples, we calculate the index of the field
\displaystyle K
. First based on Theorem
2.5
,
\displaystyle\nu_{p}(i(K))=0
for every prime integer
\displaystyle p\geq 5
. Thus we need only to calculate
\displaystyle\nu_{p}(i(K))
for
\displaystyle p=2,3
.

(1)

For
\displaystyle a=6
and
\displaystyle b=6
, since
\displaystyle{F(x)}
is
\displaystyle p
-Eisenstein for every
\displaystyle p=2,3
, we conclude that
\displaystyle F(x)
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
,
\displaystyle 2
(resp.
\displaystyle 3
) does not divide
\displaystyle(\mathbb{Z}_{K}:\mathbb{Z}[\alpha])
. Thus
\displaystyle 2
(resp.
\displaystyle 3
) does not divide
\displaystyle i(K))
, and so
\displaystyle i(K)=1
.

(2)

For
\displaystyle a=28
and
\displaystyle b=32
, since
\displaystyle\overline{F(x)}
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{5}
,
\displaystyle F(x)
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. By the first item of Theorem
2.3
, we have
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(i(K))=1
. By Theorem
2.4
,
\displaystyle\nu_{3}(i(K))=0
. Thus
\displaystyle i(K)=2
.

(3)

For
\displaystyle a=3
and
\displaystyle b=8
,
\displaystyle\overline{F(x)}
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{5}
,
\displaystyle F(x)
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. Again since
\displaystyle a\equiv 3\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{4})
and
\displaystyle b\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{8})
, by Theorem
2.3
,
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(i(K))=3
. By Theorem
2.4
,
\displaystyle\nu_{3}(i(K))=0
. Thus
\displaystyle i(K)=8
.

(4)

For
\displaystyle a=-1
and
\displaystyle b=9
, since
\displaystyle\overline{F(x)}
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{2}
,
\displaystyle F(x)
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. Since
\displaystyle 2\mathbb{Z}_{K}
is a prime ideal of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{K}
,
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(i(K))=0
. Also since
\displaystyle a\equiv 8\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{9})
and
\displaystyle b\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{9})
, by Theorem
2.4
,
\displaystyle\nu_{3}(i(K))=2
. Thus
\displaystyle i(K)=9
.

(5)

For
\displaystyle a=803
and
\displaystyle b=2112
, since
\displaystyle\overline{F(x)}
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{5}
,
\displaystyle F(x)
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. Since
\displaystyle a\equiv 3\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{4})
and
\displaystyle b\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{8})
, by Theorem
2.3
,
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(i(K))=3
. Similarly since
\displaystyle a\equiv 5\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{9})
and
\displaystyle b\equiv 6\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{9})
, by Theorem
2.4
,
\displaystyle\nu_{3}(i(K))=1
. Thus
\displaystyle i(K)=24
.

(6)

For
\displaystyle a=35
and
\displaystyle b=72
, since
\displaystyle\overline{F(x)}
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{11}
,
\displaystyle F(x)
is irreducible over
\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}
. Since
\displaystyle a\equiv 3\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{4})
and
\displaystyle b\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{8})

2.3
,
\displaystyle\nu_{2}(i(K))=3

\displaystyle a\equiv 8\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{9})

\displaystyle b\equiv 0\ \mbox{\rm(mod }{9})

2.4
