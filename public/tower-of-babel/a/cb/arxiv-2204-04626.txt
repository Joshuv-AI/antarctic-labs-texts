Plucker Formulas for Plane Algebraic Curves with a Given Newton Polygon Thanks: Keywords: Plucker formulas, Newton polytope, toric variety, tropical fan
Aliaksandr Yuran

Abstract
Let C C be a generic complex plane curve with a given Newton polygon P P . We compute the number of its inflection points and bitangents (equivalently, the number of singularities of the projectively dual curve C ∨ C^{\vee} ). We also prove that C ∨ C^{\vee} has no singularities other than nodes and cusps for large enough polygons P P .



1
Introduction

Let
C
be a generic complex plane projective curve of degree
d>1
. The classical Plücker formulas (see e.g.
[
GH
]
) compute the number of inflection points and bitangents of
C
. They are

\displaystyle\#(\mathrm{infl})=3d(d-2);

\displaystyle\#(\mathrm{bitang})={1\over 2}d(d+3)(d-3)(d-2).

We prove their analogue for a generic curve
C\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
with given Newton polygon
P
. There is a standard embedding
i\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}
given by

i\colon(a,b)\mapsto(a:b:1).

(1)

The closure of the image
\overline{i(C)}
is a projective curve whose singular locus is contained in the set
\{(1:0:0),(0:1:0),(0:0:1)\}
. We induce the notions of bitangents and inflection from
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. A bitangent is a line that is tangent to
i(C)
at two distinct points that lie in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
. A point of
i(C)
is an inflection point if the tangent at it intersects the curve with multiplicity 3. (In fact, we could allow tangency at any nonsingular point of the closure
\overline{i(C)}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
, but for some polygons
P
we would obtain a different answer.)

The papers
[
BL
]
,
[
LM
]
,
[
CM
]
and
[
BBM
]
allow to study valuations of the flexes and bitangents that we count, under a tropical degeneration of the plane curve. Ultimately, it would be interesting to combinatorially interpret the answers that we obtain as the (weighted) numbers of flexes and bitangent lines of a generic tropical curve with a given Newton polygon.

In particular,
[
BL
, Theorem 6.1]
gives a bound on the number of flexes from above. We prove that it is sharp for most polygons.

There are other possible notions of inflection, e.g. log-inflection points that are considered in
[
MR
]
. These are the points where a
logarithmic line
intersects
C
with multiplicity 3 rather than a usual line.

1.1
Main definitions and results

Definition 1.1
.

Let
f=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}a_{k}\mathbf{z}^{k}
be a Laurent polynomial of
n
complex variables where
\mathbf{z}^{k}=z_{1}^{k_{1}}z_{2}^{k_{2}}\dots z_{n}^{k_{n}}
. The Newton diagram of
f
is the set
\mathcal{P}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mid a_{k}\neq 0\}
. The Newton polytope
\newt(f)
is the convex hull of
\mathcal{P}
.

The Newton polytope of a hypersurface in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}
is the Newton polytope of its defining equation. It is defined up to a lattice translation.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be any subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{P}}
denote the space of all Laurent polynomials that can be expressed as
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap\mathcal{P}}a_{k}\mathbf{z}^{k}
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{(}\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}
be a linear function and
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded set. The support set
P^{\gamma}
is the subset of
P
on which
\gamma|_{P}
attains its
maximal
value.

We denote the linear functions
(x,y)\mapsto-y
,
(x,y)\mapsto x+y
,
(x,y)\mapsto-x
by
{\downarrow}
,
{\nearrow}
,
{\leftarrow}
respectively. Set also
{\uparrow}=-{\downarrow}
,
{\swarrow}=-{\nearrow}
,
{\rightarrow}=-{\leftarrow}
.

By
\vol(P)
denote the area of a polygon
P
. By
\len(AB)
denote the lattice length of the segment
AB
(i.e.
\#(AB\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n})-1
). In particular,
\len(\text{point})=0
.

Now we are ready to formulate the main results of the paper.

By
\Delta
denote the standard triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(1,0)
,
(0,1)
. A line is the zero locus of a polynomial from
\mathbb{C}^{\Delta}
.

Let
C\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
be a curve and
l\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
be a line. We say that
l
is a simple tangent if
l
is tangent to
C
at exactly one point
p
such that multiplicity of intersection of
C
and
l
at
p
is
C\circ_{p}l=2
. The line
l
is a simple inflection tangent if
l
is tangent to
C
at exactly one point
p
with
C\circ_{p}l=3
. In this case
p
is an inflection point. The line
l
is a simple bitangent if
l
is tangent to
C
at exactly two points
p
,
q
with
C\circ_{p}l=C\circ_{q}l=2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume that the polygon
P
contains the dilated standard triangle
5\Delta
. Consider a generic curve
C
with Newton polygon
P
. Then:

1) Any line is either transversal to
C
, or is a simple tangent, or is a simple inflection tangent, or is a simple bitangent.

2) The number of inflection points of
C
is

6\vol(P)-2\sum_{\gamma\in{\downarrow},{\nearrow},{\leftarrow}}\len(P^{\gamma})-\sum_{\gamma\in{\uparrow},{\swarrow},{\rightarrow}}\len(P^{\gamma}).

(2)

3) The number of bitangents is

-10\vol(P)+\vol(P^{\vee})+3\sum_{\gamma\in{\downarrow},{\nearrow},{\leftarrow}}\len(P^{\gamma})+\sum_{\gamma\in{\uparrow},{\swarrow},{\rightarrow}}\len(P^{\gamma}).

(3)

Here
P^{\vee}
is a certain polygon that we describe below. Its area is explicitly computed in Proposition
3.6

In the following subsections we formulate more detailed Theorems
1.14
–
1.16
that cover the three parts of Theorem
1.3
. We prove these theorems in the main part of the text.

Throughout the paper
P
is a lattice polygon on the plane. Suppose that
f
is a generic polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{P}
. By
C
we denote the curve
\{(x,y)\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}\mid f(x,y)=0\}
.

Just like in the classical Plücker problem, it is useful to consider the dual curve. Its nodes correspond to the bitangent lines to
C
and cusps correspond to the inflection points of
C
.

Definition 1.4
.

The closure of the set
\{(a,b)\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}\mid\text{the line }ax+yb+1=0\text{ is tangent to }C\}
is called the dual curve and denoted by
C^{\vee}
.

By
P^{\vee}
denote the Newton polygon of
C^{\vee}
.

The polygon
P^{\vee}
was described in
[
IL19
]
. We also give a self-contained proof of the following description in Definition
1.11
and Theorem
3.4
. We compute
\vol(P^{\vee})
in Proposition
3.6
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{*}
be a covector whose coordinates are coprime integers. Then

\len(P^{\vee})^{\gamma}=\begin{cases}2\vol(P)-\len P^{\gamma}+\len P^{-\gamma}\text{, $\gamma\in\{{\downarrow},{\nearrow},{\leftarrow}\}$;}\\
0\text{, $\gamma\in\{{\uparrow},{\swarrow},{\rightarrow}\}$;}\\
\len P^{-\gamma}\text{, otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

Here we specialize our formulas for the number of cusps and bitangents to some important Newton polygons.

Example 1.6
.

Suppose that the faces
P^{\downarrow}
,
P^{\uparrow}
,
P^{\nearrow}
,
P^{\swarrow}
,
P^{\leftarrow}
and
P^{\rightarrow}
are vertices and a generic curve supported at
P
has only simple inflections and bitangents. Then the number of them is as follows:

\displaystyle\#\mathrm{(infl)}=6\vol(P);

\displaystyle\#\mathrm{(bitang)}=\vol(P)(2\vol(P)+2\vol(\Delta,-P)-9);

\displaystyle P^{\vee}=2\vol(P)\Delta+(-P).

Here
-P
denotes the polygon centrally symmetric to
P
and the last addition is in the sense of Minkowski. The expression
\vol(\Delta,-P)
stands for the mixed volume
\vol(\Delta+(-P))-\vol(\Delta)-\vol(P)
.

Example 1.7
.

Suppose that
f
is a generic bihomogeneous polynomial of bidegree
(c,d)
(i.e. its Newton polygon
P
is the rectangle with edges
c
and
d
).

Then the curve
\{f=0\}
has

\displaystyle\#\mathrm{(infl)}=6cd-3c-3d;

\displaystyle\#\mathrm{(bitang)}=2c^{2}d^{2}-10cd+4c+4d;

\displaystyle P^{\vee}=2cd\Delta.

Example 1.8
.

Suppose that
f
is a generic quasihomogeneous polynomial of degree
(c,d)
with
c\neq d
(i.e. its Newton polygon
P
is the triangle with vertices
(0,0)
,
(c,0)
and
(0,d)
).

Then the curve
\{f=0\}
has

\displaystyle\#\mathrm{(infl)}=3cd-2c-2d;

\displaystyle\#\mathrm{(bitang)}={1\over 2}(c^{2}d^{2}-11cd+6c+6d);

\displaystyle P^{\vee}=\conv\{(c,0),(0,d),(0,cd),(cd,0)\}.

Theorem
1.3
asserts that the equalities (
2
) and (
3
) hold for the diagrams in the last two examples if
c\geq 5
and
d\geq 5
. It is not hard to use the technique of Section
4
to show that they hold for all
c
and
d
(see Example
4.13
).

1.2
Toric varieties and tropical fans

In this subsection we introduce the notation regarding toric varieties.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a lattice polytope and
\{p_{0},\dots,p_{k}\}
be the set of its lattice points.

Definition 1.9
.

The monomial map
m_{P}\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{CP}^{k}
is defined by
\mathbf{z}\mapsto(\mathbf{z}^{p_{0}}\colon\dots\colon\mathbf{z}^{p_{k}})
. The toric variety
X_{P}
is the closure of the image of
m_{P}
. By definition, put
\mathcal{O}_{X_{P}}(P)=m_{P}^{*}(\mathcal{O}(1))
.

Each polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}\mathbf{z}^{p_{i}}\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
corresponds to a section of
\mathcal{O}_{X_{P}}(P)
. Indeed, it can be viewed as the pullback
m_{P}^{*}(\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_{i}z_{i})
of a homogenous linear polynomial on
\mathbb{CP}^{k}
.

Each face
Q\subsetneq P
corresponds to an orbit of
X_{P}
. We denote it by
X_{Q}
.

Definition 1.10
.

A 1-dimensional fan is a finite set of rational 1-dimensional cones (i.e. rays starting at the origin).

Suppose the rays
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
form a fan. Assign a nonzero integer weight
a_{i}
to each of the rays. Each ray contains a unique lattice vector
v_{i}
of unit lattice length. The set of all pairs
(r_{i},a_{i})
is called a tropical fan if
\sum_{i}a_{i}v_{i}=0
.

Definition 1.11
.

Let
C\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
be a closed curve. Suppose that
P
is a lattice polygon and that the compactification
\overline{C}\subset X_{P}
does not intersect the
0
-dimensional orbits. Then the tropical fan of the curve
\trop C
consists of the rays
r_{Q}=\{\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{*}\mid P^{\gamma}=Q\}
where
Q
runs over the set of edges of
P
. The weight of
r_{Q}
is the index of intersection of
\overline{C}
and the orbit
X_{Q}
.

It is known (see e.g.
[
MS
, Chapter 6]
) that
\trop C
does not depend on the choice of polygon
P
. In particular, if
P
is the Newton polygon of
C
, then the tropical fan coincides with the normal fan of
P
. The weights of the rays equal the lattice lengths of the corresponding edges of
P
.

1.3
The assumptions

Throughout the paper we impose several assumptions on the polygon
P
and prove formulas (
2
) and (
3
) of Theorem
1.3
under them.

Assumption 1
.

The curve
C^{\vee}
has only nodes (i.e. singularities of type
A_{1}
) and cusps (of type
A_{2}
) as singularities for the given
P
.

Consider the toric variety
X_{P}
and the compactification
\overline{C}\subset X_{P}
.

Definition 1.12
.

An inflection point of the curve
C
at infinity is a point
p\in\overline{C}\cap X_{P^{\gamma}}
where
\gamma\in\{{\downarrow},{\leftarrow},{\nearrow}\}
for which there exists a line
l\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
such that the multiplicity of intersection
\overline{C}\circ_{p}\overline{l}\geq 3
.

Assumption 2
.

The curve
C
does not have inflection points at infinity.

Definition 1.13
.

Suppose that
l\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
is a vertical line.
Then the curve
C
tends asymptotically to
l
as
y\rightarrow\infty
if
\overline{C}
and
\overline{l}
have a common point on the orbit
X_{P^{\uparrow}}
. Suppose that
l
is horizontal. Then the definition of an asymptote is the same, with
X_{P^{\uparrow}}
is replaced by
X_{P^{\rightarrow}}
.

Suppose that
l
passes through
(0,0)
. Then
l
tends to
C
as
x\rightarrow 0
if
\overline{l}
and
\overline{C}
have a common point on
X_{P^{\swarrow}}
.

Assumption 3
.

Suppose that a vertical line
l
is tangent to a generic curve
C
with given Newton polygon
P
. Then
l
is neither a bitangent, nor a tangent at an inflection point, nor asymptotically tends to
C
as
y\rightarrow\infty
. That is also the case for horizontal tangents (the asymptotes tend to
C
as
x\rightarrow\infty
) and tangents that pass through the point
(0,0)
(the asymptotes tend to
C
as
x\rightarrow 0
).

So, the statements that we prove in Sections 2 and 3 are formulated as follows.

Theorem 1.14
.

Suppose the Assumptions 1 and 2 hold. Then the number of inflection points of the curve
C
is given by formula (
2
) of Theorem
1.3
.

Theorem 1.15
.

Suppose the Assumptions 1, 2 and 3 hold. Then the number of bitangents to the curve
C
is given by formula (
3
) of Theorem
1.3
.

Finally, in Section 4 we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.16
.

If
P\supset 5\Delta
, then the three assumptions hold.

There exist Newton diagrams
P\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for which the Assumptions do not hold: the trivial
P=\Delta
for which
C^{\vee}
is a point gives an example. We know that Assumption 2 fails for some other polygons. We classify them in Section
4.2
. This classification describes the polygons for which formula (
2
) hold if the inflection points are counted with multiplicities.

1.4
Classical results on Newton polytopes

The following classical results are particular cases of the general BKK principle that we use in the paper.

Theorem 1.17

(Kouchnirenko, Bernstein
[
B
]
)
.

Let
f
and
g
be any square-free Laurent polynomials with
\newt(f)=P
,
\newt(g)=Q
. They define curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in the torus. Then the index of intersection of their closures in the toric variety
X_{P}
equals the mixed volume

\overline{C}_{1}\circ\overline{C}_{2}=\vol(P,Q)=V(P+Q)-V(P)-V(Q).

Assume that
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
have no common components. Then the number of intersection points of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
(counted with multiplicities) is
\vol(P,Q)-\sum_{p\in\overline{C}_{1}\setminus C_{1}}\overline{C}_{1}\circ_{p}\overline{C}_{2}
where
\circ_{p}
denotes the intersection number of the curves at point
p
.

Remark 1.18
.

One may replace the toric variety
X_{P}
by any
X_{R}
with the following property: if
P^{\gamma}
and
Q^{\gamma}
are both edges, then
R^{\gamma}
is an edge as well.

Corollary 1.19
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
1.17
, assume that for each nonzero
\gamma\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{*}
the system
f^{\gamma}=g^{\gamma}=0
does not have solutions in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
. Then the number of common zeroes of
f
and
g
(counted with multiplicities) in
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
equals
V(P,Q)
.

Here
f^{\gamma}
denotes the sum of all the monomials of
f
corresponding to the points of
P^{\gamma}
.

Theorem 1.20

(Kouchnirenko, Bernstein, Khovanskii
[
Kh
]
)
.

Let
P
be a lattice polygon and let
f
be a generic polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{P}
. Then the genus of the curve
\{z\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}\mid f(z)=0\}
equals the number of lattice points that lie strictly inside
P
.

Equivalently, the closure of this curve in the toric variety
X_{P}
has Euler characteristic
-2V(P)+\sum_{Q}\len(Q)
where
Q
in the sum runs over all edges of
P
.

Inflection points

In this section we prove Theorem
1.14
. From now on let
P
be a lattice polygon and
f
be a generic Laurent polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{P}
. By
C
we denote the curve
\{(x,y)\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}\mid f(x,y)=0\}
.

The inflection points of
C
are its intersection points with the hessian curve
H=\{\hess f=0\}\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
where

\hess f=\begin{vmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}&f_{x}\\
f_{xy}&f_{yy}&f_{y}\\
f_{x}&f_{y}&0\end{vmatrix}

(4)

.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that the vertices of
P
have large positive coordinates. Then
\newt(x^{2}y^{2}\hess f)=3P
.

Proof.

It is enough to verify this fact for the monomials corresponding to the vertices of
P
. It is easy to see that the hessian of a monomial
ax^{\alpha}y^{\beta}
is
a^{3}\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)x^{3\alpha-2}y^{3\beta-2}
.
∎

In the rest of this section we still assume with no loss in generality that the vertices of
P
have large positive coordinates.

Consider the toric variety
X_{P}
. In order to compute the number of intersection points of the curves
C
and
H
we do the following:

•

Compute the intersection number of the toric compactifications
\overline{C}
and
\overline{H}
of these curves in
X_{P}
. (Proposition
2.2
)

•

Compute the intersection multiplicity of
\overline{C}
and
\overline{H}
at each point of the 1-dimensional orbits of
X_{P}
. (Propositions
2.3
,
2.4
and
2.6
)

•

Use Theorem
1.17
.

Proposition 2.2
.

The intersection number of the curves
\overline{C}
and
\overline{H}
in
X_{P}
is
\overline{C}\circ\overline{H}=6\vol(P)
.

Proof.

The statement follows directly from the Kouchnirenko-Bernstein theorem:

\overline{C}\circ\overline{H}=\vol(\newt(f),\newt(\hess f))=\vol(P,3P)=6\vol(P).

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that a covector
\gamma
is not parallel to the covectors denoted by the arrows. Then the curves
\overline{C}
and
\overline{H}
do not intersect on the orbit
X_{P^{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

For generic
f
the curve
\overline{C}
does not pass through the 0-dimensional orbits of
X_{P}
. So now let
\gamma
be a covector for which the edge
P^{\gamma}
satisfies the conditions of the proposition.

It is easy to see that
(\hess f)^{\gamma}=\hess(f^{\gamma})
since these two polynomials consist of the same monomials. So, by Corollary
1.19
, our problem is reduced to the fact that the system of equations
f^{\gamma}=\hess(f^{\gamma})=0
does not have solutions. Suppose it does, i.e.
\{f^{\gamma}=0\}
has inflection points. Note that the Newton polygons of the polynomials in the system are parallel segments, so their mixed volume is 0 and the system has no isolated solutions. This means that
\{f^{\gamma}=0\}
has a nonisolated inflection point, hence,
\{f^{\gamma}=0\}
contains a line and thus
\newt(f)^{\gamma}
contains a Newton polygon of a line. It can only happen if
\newt(f)^{\gamma}
is parallel to
\{x=0\}
,
\{y=0\}
or
\{x+y=0\}
i.e. if
\gamma
is parallel to a covector denoted by an arrow.

∎

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}^{P}
be a generic polynomial.

Suppose that Assumption 2 holds for the given
P
. Then for any
p\in\overline{C}\cap X_{P^{\downarrow}}
the local intersection number of
\overline{C}
and
\overline{H}
equals
\overline{C}\circ_{p}\overline{H}=2
.

In general, for any
P
, the inequality
\overline{C}\circ_{p}\overline{H}\geq 2
holds.

Proof.

First let us introduce some notation. By definition, for any complex
\alpha,\beta
and natural
\gamma,\delta
put

\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{x^{\gamma}y^{\delta}}f=x^{\gamma-\alpha}y^{\delta-\beta}{\partial^{\gamma+\delta}\over\partial x^{\gamma}\partial y^{\delta}}(x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}f)

The idea to consider such operators in a similar context first appeared in
[
E03
]
.

It is easy to see that
\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{x^{\gamma}y^{\delta}}f
is also a Laurent polynomial with Newton polygon contained in
P
, for instance,
\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{xx}f=\alpha(\alpha-1)f+2\alpha xf_{x}+x^{2}f_{xx}
. Now denote

\hess^{\alpha\beta}(f)=\begin{vmatrix}\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{xx}f&\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{xy}f&\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{x}f\\
\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{yx}f&\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{yy}f&\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{y}f\\
\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{x}f&\partial^{\alpha\beta}_{y}f&0\end{vmatrix}.

It is easy to see that the Newton polygons of all the entries of the matrix
\hess^{\alpha\beta}(f)
are contained in
P
. Thus we may say that the polynomial
\hess^{\alpha\beta}(f)
is a section of the bundle
\mathcal{O}_{X_{P}}(P)^{\otimes 3}=\mathcal{O}_{X_{P}}(3P)
. By
H^{\alpha\beta}
denote the divisor of
X_{P}
defined by this section. Note that
H^{\alpha\beta}
in general non-trivially depends on
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Now, the proof proceeds in the following four steps.

(I) The intersection of
H^{\alpha\beta}
with
\overline{C}
is contained in the finite set
\mathrm{(infl)\cup(\overline{C}\setminus C)}
consisting of the inflections of
C
and the intersection points of
\overline{C}
and the 1-dimensional orbits of
X_{P}
. This set does not depend on
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Consequently, neither does the index
i=H^{\alpha\beta}\circ_{p}\overline{C}
.

(II) The number
H^{0,0}\circ_{p}\overline{C}
obviously equals the sought one.

(III) For any lattice point
(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\in P^{\downarrow}
,

H^{-\alpha_{0},-\beta_{0}}=2X_{P^{\downarrow}}+[\hess(x^{-\alpha_{0}}y^{-\beta_{0}}f)=0].

Thus, the desired index
i
equals

2+[\hess(x^{-\alpha_{0}}y^{-\beta_{0}}f)=0]\circ_{p}\overline{C}.

(5)

(IV) The latter intersection number is clearly nonnegative. It vanishes under Assumption 2.

Proof of (I).

It can easily be checked that

\hess^{\alpha\beta}(f)=x^{2-3\alpha}y^{2-3\beta}\hess(x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}f)

(6)

for any integers
\alpha
and
\beta
. Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be any complex numbers. Then both sides of (
6
) can be defined on a neighborhood of any point of
(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
, and (
6
) still holds.

It now follows that the intersection
\overline{C}\cap H^{\alpha\beta}
is contained in
\mathrm{(infl)}\cup(\overline{C}\setminus C)
. Indeed, each intersection point of
C\subset(\mathbb{C}\setminus 0)^{2}
and
H^{\alpha\beta}
must be an inflection point of
\{x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}f=0\}=C
.

The global index of intersection of
\overline{C}
and
H^{\alpha\beta}
does not depend on
\alpha
and
\beta
since
H^{\alpha\beta}

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
R
be any lattice parallelogram of area 1. Assume that
P
contains
5R
. Then a generic curve in
\mathbb{C}^{P}
has no tritangents.

Consider a nondegenerate line

and 3 points

,
p_{2}
and

on it such that for any 6-point

supported at

tangent to

at the points. We have shown the existence of such a line while proving Theorem
4.1
.

We will construct several (namely, eight) such

It follows that for any 6-point

the number of common points of

and
C_{Q_{2}}
in

counted with multiplicities is

First, we need several lemmas.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
S
be any lattice segment with
\len(S)=1
. Consider two curves supported at (i.e. having Newton diagram)
S
. Then they have no common tangent lines.

Proof.

This is trivial if the curves are lines.

Otherwise, it is easy to compute that the dual curves are also distinct binomial curves supported at
S
, so have no common points.
∎

Lemma 6.3

Proof.

First, using an argument similar to Lemma

Hence,
