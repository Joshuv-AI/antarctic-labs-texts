Rowmotion on rooted trees
Pranjal Dangwal, Jamie Kimble, Jinting Liang, Jianzhi Lou, Bruce E. Sagan, Zach Stewart

Abstract
. A rooted tree T T is a poset whose Hasse diagram is a graph-theoretic tree having a unique minimal element. We study rowmotion on antichains and lower order ideals of T T . Recently Elizalde, Roby, Plante and Sagan considered rowmotion on fences which are posets whose Hasse diagram is a path (but permitting any number of minimal elements). They showed that in this case, the orbits could be described in terms of tilings of a cylinder. They also defined a new notion called homometry which means that a statistic takes a constant value on all orbits of the same size. This is a weaker condition than the well-studied concept of homomesy which requires a constant value for the average of the statistic over all orbits. Rowmotion on fences is often homometric for certain statistics, but not homomesic. We introduce a tiling model for rowmotion on rooted trees. We use it to study various specific types of trees and show that they exhibit homometry, although not homomesy, for certain statistics.

Introduction

Let
S
be a set with
\#S
finite where the hash symbol denotes cardinality. A
statistic
on
S
is a function
\st:S\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
where
{\mathbb{Z}}
is the integers. We extend
\st
to subsets
R\subseteq S
by letting

\st R=\sum_{r\in R}\st r.

Now suppose that
G
is a finite group acting on
S
. Statistic
\st
is said to be

homomesic
if, for any orbit
{\mathcal{O}}
of
G
, we have

\frac{\st{\mathcal{O}}}{\#{\mathcal{O}}}=c

for some constant
c
. To be more specific, we say in this case that this statistic is
c
-mesic
. Homomesy is a well-studied property; see the survey articles of Roby
[
Rob16
]
or Striker
[
Str18
]
.
Recently Elizalde, Roby, Plante, and Sagan
[
EPRS21
]
introduced a weaker notion which is exhibited by certain actions and statistics.
We say that a statistic
\st
is
homometric
if
for any two orbits
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{2}
of the same cardinality we have
\st{\mathcal{O}}_{1}=\st{\mathcal{O}}_{2}
. We will see numerous examples of statistics which are homometric but not homomesic in the present work.

Now consider a finite partially ordered set, often abbreviated to
poset
,
(P,\leq)
. An
antichain
of
P
is a
A\subseteq P
such that no two elements of
A
are comparable. We denote the set of all antichains as

{\mathcal{A}}(P)=\{A\subseteq P\mid\text{$A$ is an antichain}\}.

A
lower order ideal of
P
is
L\subseteq P
such that if
y\in L
and
x\leq y
then
x\in L
. We will use the notation

{\mathcal{L}}(P)=\{L\subseteq P\mid\text{$L$ is a lower order ideal}\}.

The lower order ideal
generated
by any
Q\subseteq P
is

Q\downarrow\ =\{x\in P\mid\text{$x\leq y$ for some $y\in Q$}\}.

We also let
\min Q
and
\max Q
be the sets of minimal and maximal elements of
Q
, respectively.
We now define
rowmotion on antichains
to be the action generated by
\rho:{\mathcal{A}}(P)\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}(P)
where

\rho(A)=\min\{x\not\in(A\downarrow)\}.

Similarly,
rowmotion on ideals
has generator
\hat{\rho}:{\mathcal{L}}(P)\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(P)
with

\hat{\rho}(L)=\rho(\max L)\downarrow.

We will usually use a hat to distinguish a notation on ideals from the corresponding one on antichains. More information about rowmotion can be found in the aforementioned survey articles.

The paper of Elizalde et al. was devoted to the study of rowmotion on fences. A fence is a poset whose Hasse diagram is a path. They showed that the antichain orbits can be modeled using certain tilings of a cylinder. This tool permitted them to prove a number of homometries which were not homomesies. In the present work we will consider rowmotion on rooted trees. A poset
T
is a
rooted tree
if its Hasse diagram is a tree in the graph theory sense of the term, and it has a unique minimal element called the
root
and denoted
\hat{0}
. Note that these posets are more general than fences in that the tree need not be a path, but also more restricted in that fences can have any number of minimal elements. We will assume all our trees our rooted.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. In the next section we will show that rowmotion on antichains of a rooted tree can also be viewed in terms of certain cylindrical tilings. The following three sections will apply this tiling model to three different families of trees: stars, trees with three leaves, and finally combs and zippers. We end with a section with comments and open questions.

We wish to characterize the possible
\tau({\mathcal{O}})
. In the definition below, an
I\times b
tile is a tile which covers the rows indexed by
I
and

has a partition using intervals in

since all singletons are intervals. And property (I2) guarantees that among all such partitions of

there is a maximal one.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a rooted tree
T
, an
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-tiling
is a tiling of a cylinder using

I\times\beta_{I}
black tiles and
I\times 1
yellow tiles if
\#I=1
, satisfying the following two properties.

(t1)

An
I\times\beta_{I}
black tile is followed by a yellow tile if
\#I=1
, or by black tiles corresponding to the intervals in a maximal proper
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-partition of
I
if
\#I\geq 2
.

(t2)

If
J
is a maximal interval of yellow tiles in a column, then they are followed by black tiles corresponding to the intervals in a maximal
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-partition of
J
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Given a rooted tree,
T
, the map
{\mathcal{O}}\mapsto\tau({\mathcal{O}})
is a bijection between the antichain rowmotion orbits of
T
and the possible
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-tilings.

Proof.

We must first show that this map is well defined in that
\tau=\tau({\mathcal{O}})
has tiles satisfying (t1) and (t2) and of the correct shape. We will do this by studying how rowmotion affects various elements of
T
.

Consider
A\in{\mathcal{O}}
and any
x\in A
which is not maximal in its branch and let
I
be the associated interval.
Then there is a unique element
y
which covers
x
and it is in the same branch.
Furthermore
y\in\rho(A)
. Since
x
and
y
correspond to the same interval
I
, it follows that the tile covering those rows in the column for
A

extends into the column for
\rho(A)
. By induction, this tile extends into a column for an antichain containing the maximal element on the branch.

Now suppose that
x\in A
is maximal in its branch. If
\#I=1
then
v
is maximal in
T
. So in
\rho(A)
the branch will be empty and the algorithm will place a yellow tile in the corresponding row and column. This proves the first case in (t1).
On the other hand, if
\#I\geq 2
then
x
is covered by at least two elements

y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
. So the column for
\rho(A)
will contain tiles in the corresponding intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
which is a proper
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-partition of
I
since
k\geq 2
. And it is maximal since if there is some
J\in{\mathcal{I}}(T)
containing two or more of the
I_{i}
then there would have to be at least one element between
x
and the corresponding
y_{i}
’s. This completes the proof of (t1).

For (t2), we will assume for simplicity that
1,n\not\in J
where
n
is the number of leaves of
T
. The cases when
J
contains one or both of these special values is similar. Say
J=[m,n]
. Then by our assumption, there are black tiles covering rows
m-1
and
n+1
in the column for
J
. Let
x
and
y
be the corresponding elements of
A
. Removing the
\hat{0}
–
x
and
\hat{0}
–
y
paths from
T
breaks the lower order ideal generated by the leaves in
J
into rooted subtrees. Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}
be their roots with corresponding intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}
. Then
\rho(A)
contains these

and so its column contains tiles for the intervals
I_{i}
which form a partition of
J
. Maximality is obtained by the same argument as in the previous paragraph.

To complete showing that
\tau
is well defined, we must check the shape of the tiles. Yellow tiles are of the correct shape by definition of the algorithm. As far as the black tiles, they cover rows indexed by intervals in
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
by definition. So it suffices to show that a tile in the rows indexed by
I
has the correct length. From the previous two paragraphs we see that the tiles in the partitions following the maximal element of a black tile or following an interval of yellow tiles all begin with the minimal elements of their respective branches. And by the second paragraph, such a tile will extend to the maximal element on its branch. So the tile will have length
\beta_{I}
, the length of the branch.

To show that this map is a bijection, we construct its inverse. So given an
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-tiling
\tau
, we must construct a corresponding orbit
{\mathcal{O}}
. For each column of
\tau
we form an antichain

as follows. For each interval
I
covered by a black tile, suppose the give column is the
i
th in that tile. Then add the
i
th smallest element on the branch for
I
to
A
.
Now arrange the antichains in the same order as the columns of the tiling to get an orbit.
The demonstration that this map is well defined is similar to the one just given. And the two functions are inverses since the algorithms described are step-by-step reversals. This completes the proof.
∎

We will often call the tiles of shape
I\times\beta_{I}
simply

-tiles

As a first application of the tiling model, we will use it to compute various statistics on rowmotion orbits. It will also give us a simple proof of our first homomesy. Given
x\in T
we have the statistic on antichains
A\in{\mathcal{A}}(T)
given by

\chi_{x}(A)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $x\in A$,}\\
0&\mbox{if $x\not\in A$.}\end{array}\right.

If we want to count the size of antichains we use the statistic

\chi(A)=\sum_{x\in T}\chi_{x}(A)=\#A.

The corresponding statistics for ideals are denoted

\hat{\chi}_{x}
and
\hat{\chi}
. Given a
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-tiling
\tau
we will use the notation

m_{I}=m_{I}(\tau)=\text{number of $I$-tiles in $\tau$.}

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
T
be a rooted tree and
\tau
be a
{\mathcal{I}}(T)
-tiling corresponding to a rowmotion orbit
{\mathcal{O}}
on
T
. The following hold.

(a)

If
x\in T
has interval
I
then

\chi_{x}({\mathcal{O}})=m_{I}.

(b)

We have

\chi({\mathcal{O}})=\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}(T)}\beta_{I}m_{I}.

(c)

If
x=x_{I,j}
then

\hat{\chi}_{x}({\mathcal{O}})=j\cdot m_{I}+c_{I}

where
c_{I}
is the number of columns of
\tau
intersecting a
J
-tile for
J\subset I
.

(d)

We have

\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}})=\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}(T)}\left[\binom{\beta_{I}+1}{2}m_{I}+\beta_{I}c_{I}\right]

(e)

If
x,y
are in the same branch then
\chi_{x}-\chi_{y}
is
0
-mesic.

Proof.

(a) This follows from the fact that
v
is represented by a single column in each
I
-tile of
\tau
.

(b) Since
I
-tiles have length
\beta_{I}=\#B_{I}
we get by summing (a)

\displaystyle\chi({\mathcal{O}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in T}\chi_{x}({\mathcal{O}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}(T)}\sum_{x\in B_{I}}m_{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{I\in{\mathcal{I}}(T)}\beta_{I}m_{I}.

(c) For a lower order ideal
L
we have that
x\in L
if and only if
x\leq y
for some
y\in A
where
A=\max L
. Note also that if
y
has interval
J
then
y\geq x
implies
J\subseteq I
.
If
J=I
then there are
j

and so

counts the total number of columns containing such an element. And

accounts for the columns intersection some
J
-tile where

J\subset I
.

(d) This result follows from (c) in much the same way that (b) followed from (a). So the proof is left to the reader.

(e) Let the common branch be
B_{I}
. Using (a) one last time we get

\chi_{x}({\mathcal{O}})-\chi_{y}({\mathcal{O}})=m_{I}-m_{I}=0

which implies the homomesy.
∎

We end this section with a recursive formula for the number of antichains in a rooted tree

which will be useful in the sequel.
We use

for the forest of rooted trees obtained by removing

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

be a rooted tree. If
\#T=1
then

. If

then let

be the rooted tree components of

. In this case

\#{\mathcal{A}}(T)=1+\prod_{i=1}^{k}\#{\mathcal{A}}(T_{i}).

Proof.

If
\#T=1
then

has antichains

.
When

, let

be an antichain of

. Either

, corresponding to the

is the sum, or

. In the latter case the restriction

of

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the star

(b)

(d)

\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}})=l+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{l}{\alpha_{i}}\binom{\alpha_{i}}{2}.

is homometric but not homomesic.

(a) Consider the tiling
\tau=\tau({\mathcal{O}})
. For all

Now consider the case when

Now let

(b) We will consider the case

(c) Using part (b) and Corollary

(e) It suffices to calculate the terms in the sum of Corollary

The special case
n=3
of
3.4
gives information about the rowmotion orbits on trees that have three leaves whose branches have minimal elements covering a single vertex of the tree. Up to isomorphism, there is only one other arrangement of branches in a tree with three leaves and this section is devoted to studying this case. First, we will prove a result about removing the branch containing
\hat{0}
from a certain type of tree.

3.3
describes the tilings of a tree
T
whose
\hat{0}
is covered by a single element. We will determine what happens when it is covered by two elements or, more generally, when removing the branch of
\hat{0}
leaves exactly two rooted trees remaining. It is possible to derive a similar result for any number of rooted subtrees, but the notation becomes cumbersome and we will only need the case of two subtrees in the sequel.

In order to state our result we will need some notation.
Let
T
be a rooted tree such that
T\setminus B=T^{\prime}\uplus T^{\prime\prime}
where
B
is the branch of
\hat{0}
and
T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}
are rooted trees. Suppose that
T^{\prime}
has
n^{\prime}
leaves and tilings
\tau_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\tau_{s}^{\prime}
where
\tau_{1}^{\prime}
is corresponds to the orbit containing
\hat{0}^{\prime}
, the minimal element of
T^{\prime}
. Further, let
c_{i}^{\prime}
be the number of columns of
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
for
i\in[s]
. Notation used previously for
T
will be given a single prime when applied to
T^{\prime}
. Similarly, let
T^{\prime\prime}
have
n^{\prime\prime}
leaves and tilings
\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\tau_{t}^{\prime\prime}
with the same conventions about the tilings and other notation except with a double prime. An example of this construction can be found in
Figure

5
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T
be a rooted tree with
T\setminus B=T^{\prime}\uplus T^{\prime\prime}
as above.

(a)

The tilings of
T
can be described as follows. For all
(i,j)\in[n^{\prime}]\times[n^{\prime\prime}]
there are tilings
\tau^{i,j}_{m}
for
1\leq m\leq g_{i,j}:=\gcd(c_{i}^{\prime},c_{j}^{\prime\prime})
. Unless
i=j=m=1
, we have that
\tau^{i,j}_{m}
consists of consecutive copies of
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
in the first
n^{\prime}
rows, consecutive copies of
\tau_{j}^{\prime\prime}
in the last
n^{\prime\prime}
rows, and has
l_{i,j}:=lcm(c_{i}^{\prime},c_{j}^{\prime\prime})
columns.
Tiling
\tau^{1,1}_{1}
is as in the previous sentence except that one copy of
\tau_{1}^{\prime}
and one of
\tau_{1}^{\prime\prime}
align so that their columns of all yellow tiles coincide, and an
[n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}]\times b
black tile is inserted directly after that column to make the total length of the orbit
l_{1,1}+b
where
b=\#B
.

(b)

Let
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{O}}_{j}^{\prime\prime}
, and
{\mathcal{O}}^{i,j}_{m}
be the orbits corresponding to tilings
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
,
\tau_{j}^{\prime\prime}
, and
\tau^{i,j}_{m}
, respectively. For any
x\in T

\chi_{x}({\mathcal{O}}^{i,j}_{m})=\begin{cases}l_{i,j}\chi_{x}({\mathcal{O}}_{i}^{\prime})/c_{i}^{\prime}&\text{if $x\in T^{\prime}$,}\\
l_{i,j}\chi_{x}({\mathcal{O}}_{j}^{\prime\prime})/c_{j}^{\prime\prime}&\text{if $x\in T^{\prime\prime}$,}\\
\delta&\text{if $x\in B$.}\end{cases}

(c)

We have

\chi({\mathcal{O}}^{i,j}_{m})=\delta b+l_{i,j}\chi({\mathcal{O}}_{i}^{\prime})/c_{i}^{\prime}+l_{i,j}\chi({\mathcal{O}}_{j}^{\prime\prime})/c_{j}^{\prime\prime}.

(d)

For any
x\in T

\hat{\chi}_{x}({\mathcal{O}}^{i,j}_{m})=\begin{cases}l_{i,j}\hat{\chi}_{x}({\mathcal{O}}_{i}^{\prime})/c_{i}^{\prime}&\text{if $x\in T^{\prime}$,}\\
l_{i,j}\hat{\chi}_{x}({\mathcal{O}}_{j}^{\prime\prime})/c_{j}^{\prime\prime}&\text{if $x\in T^{\prime\prime}$,}\\
l_{i,j}+\delta(j-1)&\text{if $x=x_{[n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}],j}$.}\end{cases}

(e)

We have

\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}}^{i,j}_{m})=l_{i,j}b+\delta\binom{b}{2}+l_{i,j}\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}}_{i}^{\prime})/c_{i}^{\prime}+l_{i,j}\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}}_{j}^{\prime\prime})/c_{j}^{\prime\prime}.

Proof.

We will only prove (a), as once this is established then the other parts of the theorem follow from straight-forward computations similar to those already seen in
3.1
. Let
{\mathcal{O}}
be an antichain orbit of
T
. Pick an antichain
A
in
{\mathcal{O}}
which does not contain an element of
B
, so that it can be written as
A=A^{\prime}\uplus A^{\prime\prime}
where
A^{\prime}=A\cap T^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}=A\cap T^{\prime\prime}
. Let
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
be the orbits of
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
in
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
, respectively.

First consider the case when (at least) one of
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
does not contain the empty antichain. It follows that as
\rho
is applied to
A
, the antichains
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
will describe their respective orbits
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
in
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
.
If
c^{\prime}=\#{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime\prime}=\#{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
then, in order for both orbits to return to
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
at the same time, we must have
\#{\mathcal{O}}=\lcm(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})
. And since there are
c^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}
ways to pair an antichain in
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
with one in
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
, the total number of orbits obtained from such pairs is
c^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}/\lcm(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})=\gcd(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})
.
This description matches the one given for the tilings
\tau^{i,j}_{k}
for as long as we do not have
i=j=1
.

In the case when both
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
contain the empty antichain, the argument of the previous paragraph goes through with one exception. Suppose the elements of
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
are repeated in
{\mathcal{O}}
in such a way that at some point the empty antichain of
T
is reached. Then
\emptyset
will be followed by the elements of
B
in increasing order. This, in turn, will be followed by the antichain
\{\hat{0}^{\prime},\hat{0}^{\prime\prime}\}
which will cause the orbits
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime\prime}
to continue. This orbit corresponds to the tiling
\tau^{1,1}_{1}
and completes our description of the orbits and their tilings.

Now consider a tree

with three leaves which is not an extended star. It follows that, using a suitable embedding, we will have

{\mathcal{I}}(T)=\{([3],a),\ ([2],b),\ ([1,1],c),\ ([2,2],d),\ ([3,3],e)\}

for

. Although we can use the previous theorem to calculate the orbits and their statistic values for arbitrary

the resulting formulas are not very enlightening. So we will concentrate on a specific tree of this type. Define the three-leaf tree
T_{k}
to be the one with

{\mathcal{I}}(T)=\{([3],k),\ ([2],k),\ ([1,1],k-1),\ ([2,2],k-1),\ ([3,3],k-1)\}.

The tree in
Figure

is
T_{3}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

The orbits of rowmotion on
T_{k}
can be partitioned by length into three sets
{\mathcal{S}}
(for small),
{\mathcal{M}}
(for medium), and
{\mathcal{L}}
(for large) with the following properties.

(a)

We have

\#{\mathcal{S}}=k(k-1),\quad\#{\mathcal{M}}=k-1,\quad\#{\mathcal{L}}=1,

and

\#{\mathcal{O}}=\begin{cases}k&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{S}}$},\\
2k&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{M}}$},\\
3k&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{L}}$}.\end{cases}

(b)

We have

\chi({\mathcal{O}})=\begin{cases}3k-3&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{S}}$},\\
5k-4&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{M}}$},\\
6k-4&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{L}}$}.\end{cases}

Thus
\chi
is homometric but not homomesic.

(c)

We have

\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}})=\begin{cases}\frac{7}{2}k^{2}-\frac{3}{2}k&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{S}}$},\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{11}{2}k^{2}-\frac{5}{2}k&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{M}}$},\\[4.30554pt]
6k^{2}-3k&\text{if ${\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathcal{L}}$}.\end{cases}

Thus
\hat{\chi}
is homometric but not homomesic.

Proof.

(a) Let
B=B_{[3]}
and
b=\#B=k
. Then
T_{k}\setminus B=S_{k}(k,k)\uplus S(k)
is a disjoint union of two (extended) stars. Clearly
T^{\prime\prime}=S(k)
has only one orbit which contains
\hat{0}
.
By
3.4
,
T^{\prime}=S_{k}(k,k)
has orbits of size
\lcm(k,k)=k
and the total number of orbits is
(k\cdot k)/k=k
. So one of these orbits contains
\hat{0}
and the other
k-1
do not, and they will have lengths given by
k+\delta k
.
It follows that the latter will be of length
k
while the former is of length
2k
.
Applying
4.1
,
T_{k}
will have
k(k-1)
orbits
{\mathcal{O}}^{i,1}_{m}
with
i\neq 1
and these will have length
\lcm(k,k)=k
. These are the orbits in
{\mathcal{S}}
. There will also be the orbits
{\mathcal{O}}^{1,1}_{m}
for
m\in[2,k]
which gives
k-1
possible values for
m
. Here the length is
\lcm(2k,k)=2k
. These are the orbits in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Finally, the unique orbit
{\mathcal{O}}^{1,1}_{1}
is of length
2k+b=2k+k=3k
and this describes
{\mathcal{L}}
.

(b) We will do the case of
{\mathcal{O}}^{1,1}_{1}
, the unique element of
{\mathcal{L}}
, as the others are similar. Applying
3.4
(c) to orbit
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}^{\prime}
of

6.1.
Other trees

The trees considered in the previous section had such nice homometry properties that one might ask if the same is true for other binary trees. In particular, one could consider the complete binary trees which are those all of whose leaves are at the same rank. Such a tree is displayed in Figure
9
. Unfortunately, homometry fails for this example tree. Consider the orbit
{\mathcal{O}}
which contains the antichain
\{x,y\}
as well as the one
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
which contains
\{z\}
. Then it is easy to verify that
\#{\mathcal{O}}=\#{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}=4
. But

\chi({\mathcal{O}})=15\neq 14=\chi({\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})
and
\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}})=35\neq 26=\hat{\chi}({\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})
.

As mentioned in the introduction, Elizalde et al.
[
EPRS21
]
considered fences whose Hasse diagrams are paths with any number of minimal elements. Here we have concentrated on arbitrary trees, but insisted that there be a unique minimal
element. It would be interesting to study trees which are not paths and also not rooted.

6.2.
Piecewise-linear and birational rowmotion

There are two generalizations of rowmotion which have also been studied and which have consequences for trees. We first need to describe rowmotion in terms of toggles. In the discussion which follows we will just write ideal for lower order ideal.

If
(P,\leq_{P})
is a finite poset and
x\in P
then the corresponding
toggle map
is

t_{x}:{\mathcal{L}}(P)\rightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(P)
defined by

t_{x}(L)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}L\triangle\{x\}&\mbox{if $L\triangle\{x\}\in{\mathcal{L}}(P)$, }\\
L&\mbox{else}\end{array}\right.

where
\triangle
denotes symmetric difference of sets.
A
linear extension
of
P
is a listing of
P
’s elements
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p}
such that
x_{i}\leq_{P}x_{j}
implies
x_{i}
is weakly left of
x_{j}
in the sequence, that is,
i\leq j
. Cameron and Fon-Der-Flaass showed that rowmotion on ideals can be broken into a sequence of toggles. In what follows we compose functions right to left.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
CFDF95
]
)
.

For any finite poset
P
and any linear extension

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p}
of
P
we have

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=t_{x_{1}}t_{x_{2}}\cdots t_{x_{p}}.}\hfill\qed

Stanley
[
Sta86
]
introduced the order polytope as a way to use geometry to study posets.
Poset
P=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{p}\}
has
order polytope

\Pi(P)=\{(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{p}))\in[0,1]^{p}\mid\text{$x_{i}\leq_{P}x_{j}$ implies $f(x)\leq f(y)$}\}.

So
\Pi(P)
is a subpolytope of the
p
-dimensional unit cube. Also note that every ideal
L
of
P
has a corresponding point of
\Pi(P)
defined by the function

f(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if $x\not\in P$,}\\
1&\mbox{if $x\in P$.}\end{array}\right.

Einstein and Propp
[
EP14
]
extended rowmotion to
\Pi(P)
.
Write
x\lessdot y
if
x
is covered by
y
in
P
, that is
x<_{P}y
and there is no
z
with

x<_{P}z<_{P}y
.
If
f\in\Pi(P)
and
x\in P
then define the
piecewise-linear toggle

\sigma_{x}
of
f
at
x
to be
g=\sigma_{x}f\in\Pi(P)
where

(2)

g(v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}M+m-f(x)&\mbox{if $v=x$,}\\
f(v)&\mbox{if $v\neq x$}\end{array}\right.

using the notation

(3)

\text{$M=\max_{y\lessdot x}f(y)$ and $m=\min_{z\gtrdot x}f(z)$}.

It is not hard to verify from the definitions that
g\in\Pi(P)
. One can also show that
\sigma_{x}
is an involution just like
t_{x}
, and
\sigma_{x}
is also piecewise-linear as a function.
Finally, one defines
piecewise-linear rowmotion
,

\rho_{\PL}:\Pi(P)\rightarrow\Pi(P)
, by

\rho_{\PL}=\sigma_{x_{1}}\sigma_{x_{2}}\cdots\sigma_{x_{p}}

where
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p}
is a linear extension of
P
. It is true, but not obvious from the equation just given, that
\rho_{\PL}
is well defined in that it does not depend on the chosen linear extension. Since
\Pi(P)
has an infinite number of points, it is very possible for orbits of
\rho_{\PL}
to be infinite.
However, in certain cases the orbits are nice. Take, for example, the poset
[p]\times[q]
which is the poset product of a
p
-element chain and a
q
-element chain.

Theorem 6.2

(
[
EP14
]
)
.

The order of
\rho_{\PL}
on

is
p+q
. ∎
