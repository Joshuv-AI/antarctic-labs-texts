SGD with Clipping is Secretly Estimating the Median Gradient
Fabian Schaipp, Guillaume Garrigos, Umut Şimşekli, Robert M. Gower

Abstract
There are several applications of stochastic optimization where one can benefit from a robust estimate of the gradient. For example, domains such as distributed learning with corrupted nodes, the presence of large outliers in the training data, learning under privacy constraints, or even heavy-tailed noise due to the dynamics of the algorithm itself. Here we study SGD with robust gradient estimators based on estimating the median. We first consider computing the median gradient across samples , and show that the resulting method can converge even under heavy-tailed, state-dependent noise. We then derive iterative methods based on the stochastic proximal point method for computing the geometric median and generalizations thereof. Finally we propose an algorithm estimating the median gradient across iterations , and find that several well known methods – in particular different forms of clipping – are particular cases of this framework.



1
Introduction

Many problems in machine learning can be represented by

\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell(\bm{w}),

an optimization problem
where
\bm{w}
is a vector of parameters and
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a loss function.
To tackle this problem, gradient-based optimization algorithms have been the de facto choice in many application domains
(
Nemirovsky & Yudin 1983
;
Bottou 2010
;
Bottou et al. 2018
)
, where we are only given access to a
noisy oracle
of the gradient, i.e.,

\bm{g}_{t}=\nabla\ell(\bm{w}_{t})+\bm{\xi}_{t}(\bm{w}_{t}).

Here
t
denotes the iterations and
\bm{\xi}_{t}(\bm{w}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is noise vector that is sampled from a distribution that depends on the parameter
\bm{w}_{t}
.
With this noise oracle, stochastic gradient descent (
SGD
) with learning rate
\eta_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{t+1}=\bm{w}_{t}-\eta_{t}\bm{g}_{t}.

(
SGD
)

While
SGD
has proven useful in numerous applications, its performance heavily relies on the ‘quality’ of the noisy oracles
\bm{g}_{t}
, and can tragically degrade when the noise
\bm{\xi}_{t}
has significant outliers, exhibits heavy tails (i.e.,
\|\bm{\xi}_{t}\|
can be very large with non-negligible probability), or has been corrupted in an adversarial way. We provide two examples.

(i)

Heavy-tailed gradients.
Recent studies have provided theoretical and empirical evidence that
heavy tails can naturally arise in stochastic optimization. From a theoretical perspective
(
Gurbuzbalaban et al. 2021
;
Hodgkinson & Mahoney 2021
;
Pavasovic et al. 2023
)
showed that the parameter-dependent nature of
\bm{\xi}_{t}
can result in heavy tails, even in very basic settings such as linear regression with Gaussian data. On the other hand, it has been empirically observed that the gradients for training transformer architectures on language tasks are more heavy-tailed compared to, for example, convolutional models for image data
(
Zhang et al. 2020b
;
Kunstner et al. 2023
)
. While the presence of heavy-tails in gradients might be beneficial in certain settings
(
Simsekli et al. 2020
)
, from an optimization perspective, it mostly introduces non-trivial challenges, which might even make the algorithm diverge unless additional care is taken
(
Zhang et al. 2020b
;
Gorbunov et al. 2020
)
.

(ii)

Corrupted nodes.
A well-known problem in distributed learning is
when some computation nodes are malicious and can communicate adversarial updates, which results in inaccurate gradient oracles that can significantly misguide the optimization procedure.
Similar to the heavy-tailed setting, depending on
how much malicious are the nodes,

SGD
can be impractical unless certain modifications are made
(
Mhamdi et al. 2018
)
.
We refer to
(
Karimireddy et al. 2021
)
and references therein for an overview of more robust aggregation techniques.

In order to make the optimization algorithms more robust to such noisy oracles, many techniques have been developed for different domains under various conditions. In the context of heavy-tailed gradients, clipping is often employed
(
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Puchkin et al. 2023
;
Koloskova et al. 2023
)
. In distributed learning robust aggregation rules are needed to protect against corrupted or malicious nodes.

(
Data et al. 2018
;
Karimireddy et al. 2021
;
Khirirat et al. 2023
)
. This line of research is also closely related to error feedback in federated learning
(
Seide et al. 2014
;
Karimireddy et al. 2019
;
Richtárik et al. 2021
)
and learning under differential privacy constraints
(
Khirirat et al. 2023
)
.

Robustness of median.

While attracting increasing attention in stochastic optimization thanks to modern machine learning applications, taming the impact of strong noise has already been considered in various other domains. Indeed, estimation under heavy-tails and corruptions has been a long-standing topic in robust statistics
(
Huber 1981
)
. Being a vast research field that has produced numerous algorithms designed for different tasks, one of the recurring themes in robust statistics is the use of the
sample median
and its variations as opposed to the
sample mean

(
Minsker 2015
)
, whenever an aggregation of random variables is needed. For instance, in the presence of heavy tails, the sample median has been shown to be a significantly more robust estimator of the true mean, whereas the sample mean can be vulnerable to strong noise
(
Lugosi & Mendelson 2019
)
.

Sample median
SGD
.

Inspired by tools from robust statistics, some notions of median have been utilized in stochastic optimization as well
(
Acharya et al. 2022
;
Yin et al. 2018
)
. One
such approach is to compute a sample median over a sample of gradients. That is, let

\bm{g}_{t}^{(i)}=\nabla\ell(\bm{w}_{t})+\bm{\xi}^{(i)}_{t}(\bm{w}_{t})\quad\mbox{for}\quad i=1,\dots,n,

where
\{\bm{\xi}^{(i)}_{t}(\bm{w}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a set of i.i.d. noise vectors.

Given these sampled gradients, stochastic optimization algorithms typically aggregate them by computing their sample mean, or average
\bm{g}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{g}_{t}^{(i)}
, and then use this
\bm{g}_{t}
in (
SGD
). Alternatively, to improve robustness, one can aggregate these gradients

\{\bm{g}_{t}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
using a multidimensional variant of the median.
While this approach has paved the way for powerful optimization algorithms in terms of robustness, it often introduces a significant computational burden, since a multivariate median needs to be computed at every iteration
(
Weiszfeld & Plastria 2009
;
Vardi & Zhang 2000
)
.

Motivation.
Even though the clipping-based approaches developed in optimization theory and the median-based approaches inspired from robust statistics aim at solving similar tasks, they where designed in different mathematical frameworks under different conditions. In this study, our main goal is to bridge the gap between these two seemingly different branches and to bring a unified theoretical perspective that can shed further light on both directions by leveraging tools from both optimization and robust statistics.

Contributions.

We first revisit median gradient-based optimization and consider a generic notion of median which include several multivariate medians that have been extensively used in practice. By exploiting the statistical properties of the sample median estimator, we show that
SGD
with (approximately computed) median gradients indeed yields a very powerful algorithm, which converges with
1/\sqrt{T}
rate (
T
being the number of iterations) even when the gradient oracles are heavy-tailed with diverging second-order moments. We further illustrate that, in addition to having infinite variance, the noise vectors
\bm{\xi}_{t}(\bm{w})
can even have a multiplicative dependence on
\bm{w}
, a scenario which is highly challenging and cannot be directly covered by existing theoretical results, see e.g.,
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2020b
;
Puchkin et al. 2023
)
.

Encouraged by this result, we then focus on the practical computation of the medians. To this end, we solve the
optimization-based formulation of the median using
Stochastic Proximal Point (
SPP
)
(
Asi & Duchi 2019
;
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
;
Toulis et al. 2020
)
. As an alternative, we also consider iterative solvers based on the stochastic subgradient method.

We then depart from previous work
by approximately computing the median gradient
across
iterations. That is, the median over
\{\bm{g}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}.
We use this online median gradient within the
SGD
framework.
When using the Stochastic Proximal Point method for computing the median across iterations, we recover several known clipping-based optimization algorithms as special cases. In particular, we recover different forms of clipping
(
Gorbunov et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020a
)
, such as
Clip21
for distributed learning
(
Khirirat et al. 2023
)
, and signed gradient methods
(
Riedmiller & Braun 1993
;
Bernstein et al. 2018
)
. We also show that (non-clipped) heavy-ball momentum
(
Polyak 1964
)
is in fact an online mean estimator.

Our proposed framework allows to unify the different motivations for previously developed techniques, and illustrates that clipping-based optimization algorithms essentially runs a “hidden” estimation of the median across iterations. In particular, the observation that
centered clipping

(
Karimireddy et al. 2021
)
and
Clip21
are an iterative estimator of a geometric median appears to be new, and might have consequences for its application in distributed learning.

We finally illustrate our theory on experiments where we compare the effectiveness of sample median, sample mean, and several online median estimators. Our results underline that for heavy-tailed noise, using the sample median is highly effective in contrast to the sample mean which is unstable and often does not converge.
Our experiments also show that our online median estimates are robust, while being less expensive to compute.
We further compare different clipping techniques for training transformer architectures on language modeling tasks, and show that they improve upon the performance of
SGD
with momentum.

\|\cdot\|_{p}
denotes the

-norm, given by
\|\bm{z}\|_{p}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\bm{z}|_{i}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

.

, we denote its Frobenius norm by
\|\bm{A}\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}\bm{A}_{ij}^{2}}
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Heavy-tailed Random Variables

A random variable
X
is said to be
heavy-tailed
if
the tails of its distribution decay slower
than the tails of an exponential distribution.
One important example, is the family of symmetric
\alpha
-stable distributions
(
Nolan 2020
, Def. 1.4)
, denoted
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha,\sigma}
: it is parameterized by a stability parameter
\alpha\in(0,2]
and a scale parameter
\sigma>0
. For
\alpha=1
, it is the Cauchy distribution, while for
\alpha=2
it is a Gaussian.
The parameter
\sigma
controls the spread of the distribution. When
\sigma=1
, we call the distribution
standard
and denote it by
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha,1}
.
The parameter
\alpha
on the other hand controls the heaviness of the tails: for
\alpha<2
and
X\sim\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
has infinite variance, whereas for
\alpha\leq 1
even the mean of
X
is infinite. However, for any
\alpha
, the median is equal to
0
.

2.2
Mean Estimation and (Sample) Median

Estimating the mean of a distribution, given a finite number of samples, is one of the core problems studied in statistics.
When the distribution in question has heavy-tails, or has significant outliers, the sample mean turns out to be a poor estimator. We refer to
(
Lugosi & Mendelson 2019
)
for a survey of alternative techniques. In this section, we will introduce basic definitions for median.

In this section we denote by
z
a real-value random value, and with
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
a random vector. We always denote the expectation with respect to the distribution of
z
(or
\bm{z}
respectively) by
\mathbb{E}
.

Multivariate median.

The one-dimensional median is defined by

\displaystyle\texttt{median}{}(z)\in\underset{m\in\mathbb{R}}{\argmininn}\;\mathbb{E}\left[|m-z|\right].

(1)

In contrast to the mean, the median is always defined (though it may not be unique)
(
Cramér 2016
)
.

Since we are interested in approximating the median gradient,
we need a notion of median that generalizes to random vectors
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For this, we can define the
\ell_{p}
-median:

\texttt{median}_{\ell_{p}}(\bm{z})\in\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{m}-\bm{z}\|_{p}\right].

(2)

This recovers several existing notions of a multivariate median: for
p=1
we obtain a
componentwise median
(or
\ell_{1}
-median) and for
p=2
we obtain the
geometric median
(or
\ell_{2}
-median)
(
Fréchet 1948
;
Weiszfeld & Plastria 2009
;
Minsker 2015
;
Cohen et al. 2016
)
. Clearly, for
d=1
all notions conincide with the definition in (
1
).

Sample median.

Given
n
realizations
\bm{z}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{z}^{(n)}
of
\bm{z}
, the sample median is defined as

\texttt{median}_{\ell_{p},i\in[n]}(\bm{z}^{(i)})\in\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{m}-\bm{z}^{(i)}\|_{p}.

(3)

We remark that (
3
) is a special case of (
2
), by setting the distribution of
\bm{z}
as a sum of Dirac measures
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{z}_{i}}
.

It is generally accepted that the sample median is a more robust estimator than the sample mean
(
Huber 1981
;
Hampel et al. 2005
)
.
This is nicely illustrated by the notion of a breakdown point
(
Donoho & Huber 1983
;
Lopuhaa & Rousseeuw 1991
)
, which is the smallest fraction of a sample that, if arbitrarily corrupted, can arbitrarily change the value of the estimator. The breakdown point of the sample median is roughly
\tfrac{1}{2}
, meaning that at least half of the samples need to be outliers for the median to diverge. The sample mean however can be arbitrarily corrupted by a single outlier, thus its breakdown point is one over
\tfrac{1}{n}
.

If the samples
\bm{z}^{(i)}
are obtained by taking block-wise means over observations, (
3
) is called the median-of-means estimator
(
Nemirovsky & Yudin 1983
;
Lugosi & Mendelson 2019
)
, which is known to be a robust mean estimator
(
Lugosi & Mendelson 2019
)
.

Relation to M-estimation.

More generally, for a function
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we will later consider the problem

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{z})\right].

(4)

We assume that
\mathcal{D}
is a closed, proper, convex function and that (
4
) admits a solution (see Appendix
B
for a discussion).
For
\mathcal{D}=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
, the solution to (
4
) is clearly the mean
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right]
, and for
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
, the solution is the geometric median as defined in (
2
).
Problem (
4
) can also be seen as a special case of
M-estimators

(
Huber 1981
, Sec. 3.2)
.

The choice of
\mathcal{D}
determines properties of the associated estimator:
if the function
\mathcal{D}
heavily penalizes large values, like the quadratic
\mathcal{D}=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
, then the solution to (
4
) will be sensitive to outliers. In contrast
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{1}
grow only linearly, and thus (
4
) will be less sensitive to outliers. We return to problem (
4
) later in
Section

4
.

For
SGD
, its update direction is a sampled gradient
\bm{g}_{t}
which aims to approximate the full gradient
\nabla\ell(\bm{w}_{t})
.
This section motivates that in the presence of large outliers it might be necessary to use more robust gradient estimators as update direction. A natural alternative is to use the sample median together with
SGD

(
Alistarh et al. 2018
;
Acharya et al. 2022
)
, as we do next.

Warm-up: Sample Median Gradient

In this section, we showcase that

Consider the following setup: at every iteration

4
Iterative Methods for Median Estimation

We now focus on the following gradient estimation problem:
let
\mathcal{G}
denote the (fixed) distribution over noisy gradients
\bm{g}
, and consider the problem of approximating

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{G}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{g})\right].

(8)

In the previous section, we used the sample median. It follows that solving (
8
) with
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{p}
, and with

being the empirical distribution over a sample
(\bm{g}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{g}^{(n)})
, the solution to (
8
) is the sample median (

).

For solving (
8

. The most commonly used such method is

(
Cardot et al. 2011
)
. But here we advocate for a different method: The stochastic proximal point method (
SPP
)
(
Asi & Duchi 2019
;
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
)
.
In cases where
SPP
has a closed form update, it is often preferred to
SGD
since is easier to tune
(
Asi & Duchi 2019
;
Milzarek et al. 2022
)
.
Fortunately, as we show next, the
SPP
method enjoys closed-form updates when
\mathcal{D}\in\{\|\cdot\|_{1},\|\cdot\|_{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}\}
.

The update of
SPP
with stepsize
\tau>0
applied to (
8
) can be written as (see details in Appendix
B.3
)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}\;=\;\bm{g}_{t}+\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}}(\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}),

(9)

where we denote by
\mathrm{prox}_{\mathcal{D}}
the proximal operator of
\mathcal{D}
,

\mathrm{prox}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{m}):=\underset{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathcal{D}(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{y}-\bm{m}\|^{2}.

Because we are interested in the
\ell_{p}
-median,
we first characterize the
SPP
update (
9
) for when
\mathcal{D}
is the
\ell_{p}
-norm, and show it simply require to project the sampled gradient onto a certain ball of radius
\tau
centered at
\bm{m}_{t}
.

4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{p}
be the
\ell_{p}
-norm for
p\in[1,\infty].
Let
q\in[1,\infty]
such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
, and let

\mathbb{B}_{q}(\bm{m},\tau)\;:=\;\left\{\bm{y}\;:\;\|\bm{m}-\bm{y}\|_{q}\leq\tau\right\}.

The
SPP
update (
9
) is given by

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{m}_{t}\;+\;\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbb{B}_{q}(\bm{0},\tau)}\big(\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t}\big)

(10)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbb{B}_{q}(\bm{m}_{t},\tau)}\big(\bm{g}_{t}\big).

(11)

Figure 2
:
One
SPP
update (
11
) for
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{1}
(blue) or
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
(green) amounts to project onto a ball of the dual norm, centered at the current iterate
\bm{m}_{t}
and of radius
\tau>0
. The thick lines represent the projections of
\bm{g}_{t}
for all possible values of
\tau
.

Using
Proposition

4.1
we can now develop closed form updates of the
SPP
method for when
\mathcal{D}
is the
\ell_{1}
- or the
\ell_{2}
-norm, with both methods being related to clipping.

Vectorwise clipping.

If we choose
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
,
SPP
performs projections onto the
\ell^{2}
-ball
which is also known as clipping
(
Khirirat et al. 2023
;
Koloskova et al. 2023
)
.

4.2
.

For
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
update (
10
) is given by

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{m}_{t}+\mathrm{clip}_{\tau,2}(\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t}),

(12)

where
\mathrm{clip}_{\tau,2}(\bm{v}):=\frac{\tau}{\max\{\tau,\|\bm{v}\|_{2}\}}\bm{v}
.

This corollary shows that the
SPP
method for computing the
\ell_{2}
-median can be written as

\bm{m}_{t+1}=\beta_{t}\bm{m}_{t}+(1-\beta_{t})\bm{g}_{t}
,
with
\beta_{t}:=1-\tfrac{\tau}{\max\{\tau,\|\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t}\|_{2}\}}
. In this sense, (
12
) is momentum where the momentum coefficient depends on how
far

\bm{g}_{t}
is from the previous estimate
\bm{m}_{t}
.
This allows the iterates to be more robust to outliers, as samples which are too far from
\bm{m}_{t}
will
be down weighted
(see also
Fig.

2
).

Componentwise clipping.

If we choose
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{1}
,
SPP
performs projections onto the
\ell^{\infty}
-ball, also known as componentwise clipping.
Here as well, clipping protects against large individual entries by shrinking them independently.

4.3
.

For
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{1}
update (
10
) is given by

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}=\bm{m}_{t}+\mathrm{clip}_{\tau,1}(\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t}),

(13)

where
\mathrm{clip}_{\tau,1}(\bm{v}):=\left(\min\{\max\{\bm{v}_{i},-\tau\},\tau\}\right)_{i=1}^{d}
.

SPP
and momentum.

It is easy to see that the solution to problem (
8
) with
\mathcal{D}=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
is the mean
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}\right]
.
Computing (
9
) for this choice of
\mathcal{D}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}=(1-\tfrac{\tau}{1+\tau})\bm{m}_{t}+\tfrac{\tau}{1+\tau}\bm{g}_{t}.

(14)

Interestingly, this is the (heavy-ball) momentum gradient estimator
(
Polyak 1964
;
Sutskever et al. 2013
)
, where the momentum coefficient is given by
\beta=1/(1+\tau)\in[0,1).

Rates for
SPP

Now that we have presented how to compute the iterates (
9
) in practice, let us state its convergence properties. Since all these methods are instantiates of the
SPP
method, we can use standard results such as
(
Davis & Drusvyatskiy 2019
, Thm. 4.4)
.

4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a norm, let
\varphi(\bm{m}):=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{G}}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{g})\right]
, and let
\bm{m}_{*}\in\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\varphi(\bm{m})
.
If
\bm{m}_{t}
is generated by (
9
)
then for
\bar{\bm{m}}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{m}_{t}
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(\bar{\bm{m}}_{T})-\varphi(\bm{m}_{*})\right]=O\left(\frac{\|\bm{m}_{0}-\bm{m}_{*}\|^{2}}{\tau T}+\tau\right).

In Appendix
B
we give further examples for
\mathcal{D}
, such as the Huber loss.
We also compare
SPP
with
SGD
for solving (
8
), showing that sign-
SGD
is related to
\ell_{1}
-median estimation.

5
Gradient Estimator in the Wild

Our final objective is to use robust gradient estimators within a
SGD
-type method. Because at each iteration the weights
\bm{w}_{t}
are updated, the distribution of the gradients (denoted by
\mathcal{G}
in the previous section) also changes at each iteration of
SGD
.
Our strategy is to interweave updates in the weights
\bm{w}_{t}
with
SPP
updates in the gradient estimators
\bm{m}_{t}.

That is, we sample
one
stochastic gradient
\bm{g}_{t}
per iteration and set

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{llr}\bm{m}_{t+1}&=\quad\bm{g}_{t}+\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}}(\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}),\\
\bm{w}_{t+1}&=\quad\bm{w}_{t}-\eta_{t}\bm{m}_{t+1}.\\
\end{array}

Formally,
\bm{m}_{t}
is now an estimate of the median of the gradients
across
iterations,
which is in contrast to (
5
), where

\bm{m}_{t}
was an approximation of the sample median at fixed
\bm{w}_{t}
.
With the results from
Section

4
, we immediately obtain the following special cases (here
p\in\{1,2\}
):

(i)  if
\mathcal{D}=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
:
\begin{cases}\bm{m}_{t+1}=(1-\tfrac{\tau}{1+\tau})\bm{m}_{t}+\tfrac{\tau}{1+\tau}\bm{g}_{t}\\
\bm{w}_{t+1}=\bm{w}_{t}-\eta_{t}\bm{m}_{t+1},\end{cases}

(ii)  if
\mathcal{D}=\|\cdot\|_{p}
:
\begin{cases}\bm{m}_{t+1}=\bm{m}_{t}+{\rm clip}_{p,\tau}(\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t})\\
\bm{w}_{t+1}=\bm{w}_{t}-\eta_{t}\bm{m}_{t+1}.\end{cases}

Equation (i) is
SGD
with momentum (
14
); notice how the
SPP
step size
\tau
defines the momentum coefficient.
Instead, (ii) corresponds to clipping, and here the
SPP
step size determines the clipping threshold.
In other words,
SGD
with momentum is to online mean estimation what clipping is to online median estimation.

A slightly different approach would be to restart
\bm{m}_{t}=\bm{0}
in every iteration (
cold start
).
In this case, (i) reduces to
SGD
with no momentum, while in (ii) the clipping operator is applied to
\bm{g}_{t}
, instead of
\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t}
, which is also a common technique in practice
(
Zhang et al. 2020a
)
. We discuss this relation below in more detail.

Connection to Error Feedback.

Methods such as (
5
) are also used in the distributed learning setting to improve communication and protect against corrupted nodes
(
Karimireddy et al. 2021
)
. These methods are often called Error Feedback (
EF
) methods
(
Seide et al. 2014
;
Richtárik et al. 2021
)

and generally involve an update of the form

\bm{m}_{t+1}=\bm{m}_{t}+\mathcal{C}_{t}(\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t})

where
\mathcal{C}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a compression operator.
Projections onto balls can be seen as a compression, since one can encode the resulting vector in fewer bits
(
Safaryan et al. 2021
)
.
In particular, centered clipping from
(
Karimireddy et al. 2021
)
, and the
Clip21
method
(
Khirirat et al. 2023
)
are using update (
12
) in the distributed setting, and are thus secretly estimating the geometric median of the gradients returned across all nodes.

SGD
with standard clipping.

Even in the non-distributed setting, a standard technique to avoid training instabilities is to directly clip the gradient
\bm{g}_{t}

(
Zhang et al. 2020a
)
. For momentum coefficient
\beta\in[0,1)
and
c>0
, let

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{m}_{t+1}&=\beta\bm{m}_{t}+(1-\beta)\min\{1,\tfrac{c}{\|\bm{g}_{t}\|}\}\bm{g}_{t},\\
\bm{w}_{t+1}&=\bm{w}_{t}-\eta_{t}\bm{m}_{t+1}.\end{split}

(17)

We will refer to (
17
) as
clipped-SGD
.
The method has been studied extensively, often for the case
\beta=0
, see
(
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Gorbunov et al. 2020
;
Koloskova et al. 2023
;
Puchkin et al. 2023
)
and references therein.

We present two motivations for using
clipped-SGD
.
If
\beta=0
then (
17
) is the same as (
12
) with cold start (as explained above).
When
\beta\neq 0
,
clipped-SGD
can also be viewed as a
SPP
method for computing a robust mean estimator given by

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{G}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{m}-\min\{1,\tfrac{c}{\|\bm{g}\|}\}\bm{g}\|^{2}\right].

(18)

The solution to (
18
) is
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{G}}\left[\min\{1,\tfrac{c}{\|\bm{g}\|}\}\bm{g}\right]
, which is known as the
Catoni-Giulini mean estimator

(
Catoni & Giulini 2018
;
Lugosi & Mendelson 2019
)
.
One step of
SPP
(
9
) applied to (
18
) gives (
17
) with
\beta=\tfrac{1}{1+\tau}
.

7
Conclusion

We provide an analysis of
SGD
using the median of sampled gradients, and showed convergence guarantees under potentially heavy-tailed and state-dependent noise. In contrast, under the same assumption,
SGD
with the sample mean does not converge.
We further provide a new approach to compute medians using the stochastic proximal point method. Our final algorithm proposes to use online estimates of the median gradient, requiring only one sample per iteration. We observe that this general framework intersects with many existing works on robust stochastic optimization, where the connection with median estimation was not known previously.

A.1
On the Assumptions on the Sample Median

We first state a simple fact about Lipschitz functions:

A.1
.

If Assumption
3.1
holds, and if
\ell
is Lipschitz continuous, then without loss of generality we can assume that
\delta_{2}=\sigma_{2}=0
.

Proof.

If
\ell
is
G
-Lipschitz, then for every subgradient
\bm{u}\in\partial\ell(\bm{w})
we have
\|\bm{u}\|\leq G
, so one can replace
\delta_{1}^{2}
with
\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2}G^{2}
and
\sigma_{1}^{2}
with
\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}G^{2}
.
∎

In the following proof of
Proposition

3.3
we will need an equivalent but more compact formulation of Assumption
3.1
.

A.2
.

Let
\bm{m}
be a random variable in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and consider the following property:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{m}-\nabla\ell(\bm{w})\|^{2}\right]\leq\nu_{1}^{2}+\nu_{2}\|\nabla\ell(\bm{w})\|^{2}.

(19)

If (
6
) holds then (
19
) holds with
\nu_{1}^{2}=\delta_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\nu_{2}=\delta_{2}+\sigma_{2}
.
If (
19
) holds, then (
6
) holds with
\delta_{1}=\sigma_{1}=\nu_{1}
and
\delta_{2}=\sigma_{2}=\nu_{2}
.

Proof.

Expanding the squares, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{m}-\nabla\ell(\bm{w})\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{m}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{m}\right]\|^{2}\right]+\|\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{m}\right]-\nabla\ell(\bm{w})\|^{2},

where we used that
\mathbb{E}\left[\langle\bm{m}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{m}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{m}\right]-\nabla\ell(\bm{w})\rangle\right]=0
.
Now, the first implication is trivial, and the second follows from the positivity of the involved terms.
∎

We now verify that Assumption
3.1
holds true for the
\ell_{1}
-sample median, provided that the noise on the subgradient is linearly depending on an
\alpha
-stable distribution, and that the linear coefficients grow with the norm of the subgradient.

A.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be odd and
n\geq 3
. Suppose that the subgradient oracles
\bm{g}^{(i)}=\nabla\ell(\bm{w})+\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{w})\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}
verify Assumption
3.5
, for all
i=1,\dots,n
and set

\bm{m}=\texttt{median}_{\ell_{1},i\in[n]}\left(\bm{g}^{(i)}(\bm{w})\right).

Then, Assumption
3.1
holds with
\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=0
, and
\sigma_{1}^{2}=C_{n}c_{1}
,
\sigma_{2}=C_{n}c_{2}
, for some constant
C_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

Proof.

Let us denote the noise vector by
\bm{\xi}^{(i)}=\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{w})\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}
. By the stability property of
\alpha
-stable distributions
(
Nolan 2020
, Proposition 1.3)
, we have that

\displaystyle{\xi}_{k}^{(i)}:=\bm{\xi}_{k}^{(i)}(\bm{w})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}[\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{w})]_{kj}\bm{\zeta}^{(i)}_{j}=^{\mathrm{d}}\|\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{w})_{k}\|_{\alpha}z,

(20)

where
=^{\mathrm{d}}

A.3
Rates for Biased SGD for Convex Lipschitz Functions

In this section we prove another result about the complexity of biased
SGD
, that is
SGD
with biased estimators of the subgradient.
As far as we know, biased
SGD
was only studied in the case of strongly convex functions or smooth nonconvex functions
(
Demidovich et al. 2023
)
, or in the particular case of random projections.
In that regard, the next result is a new contribution to the analysis of biased
SGD
.

Before stating our result, we recall a few standard definitions given that we are working with a nonsmooth convex function.
Given a convex function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we say that
\bm{u}
is a subgradient of
\ell
at
\bm{w}
if

(\forall\bm{w}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\ell(\bm{w}^{\prime})-\ell(\bm{w})-\langle\bm{u},\bm{w}^{\prime}-\bm{w}\rangle\geq 0.

We note
\partial\ell(\bm{w})
the set of all subgradients of
\ell
at
\bm{w}
, that is called the subdifferential of
\ell
at
\bm{w}
.

A.4
.

Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex
G
-Lipschitz function such that
{\rm{argmin}}~\ell\neq\emptyset
.
Let
\eta>0
, and note
D:=\|\bm{w}_{0}-\bm{w}_{*}\|
for some
\bm{w}_{*}\in{\rm{argmin}}~\ell
.
Consider the iterates generated by a biased Stochastic Subgradient Descent method

\bm{w}_{t+1}=\bm{w}_{t}-\eta\bm{g}_{t},

and assume that the estimator
\bm{g}_{t}
has a uniformly finite bias and variance (we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{t}
the filtration induced by
\bm{w}_{0},\dots,\bm{w}_{t}
):

\|\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\bm{u}_{t}\|\leq\delta,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{g}_{t}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\|^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},

where
\bm{u}_{t}
is any subgradient of
\ell
at
\bm{w}_{t}
.
Then, for every
T\geq 1

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bar{\bm{w}}_{T})-\inf\ell\right]\leq\frac{2D^{2}}{\eta T}+\eta\frac{\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2}}{2}+\eta\frac{\delta^{2}T}{4}

where
\bar{\bm{w}}_{T}
is an average of the first
T
iterates defined by
\bar{\bm{w}}_{T}=\frac{1}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\theta^{t}}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\theta^{t}\bm{w}_{t}
,
\theta=\frac{T}{T+2}
.
In particular, if we take a constant step size equal to

\eta=\frac{2\sqrt{2}D}{\sqrt{2(\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2})T+\delta^{2}T^{2}}}\quad\text{(resp. $\eta=\frac{1}{\delta T+\sqrt{T}}$ )}

then we can guarantee that

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bar{\bm{w}}_{T})-\inf\ell\right]\leq 2D\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2}}{T}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}}\quad\text{(resp. $\frac{2D^{2}+\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}+G^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}+(2D^{2}+1)\delta$ ).}

Proof.

Let
w_{*}\in{\rm{argmin}}~\ell
and
T\geq 1
be fixed.
Develop the squares and use the definition of the algorithm to write

\displaystyle\|\bm{w}_{t+1}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}+2\langle\bm{w}_{t+1}-\bm{w}_{t},\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\rangle+\|\bm{w}_{t+1}-\bm{w}_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle\bm{g}_{t},\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\rangle+\eta^{2}\|\bm{g}_{t}\|^{2}.

Let
\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}
be the projection of
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]
onto
\partial\ell(\bm{w}_{t})
, the subdifferential of
\ell
at
\bm{w}_{t}
.
This means that from our assumption on the bias of
\bm{g}_{t}
we will have

\|\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}\|\leq\|\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{g}_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\bm{u}_{t}\|\leq\delta.

Then, after taking conditional expectation (denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\cdot\right]
instead of
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]
) we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\|\bm{w}_{t+1}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\bm{g}_{t}\right],\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\rangle+\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\|\bm{g}_{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}-2\eta\langle\bar{\bm{g}}_{t},\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\rangle+2\eta\langle\bar{\bm{g}}_{t}-\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\bm{g}_{t}\right],\bm{w}_{t}-\bm{w}_{*}\rangle+\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\|\bm{g}_{t}\|^{2}\right]

Now define

\eta

We will focus now on the geometric sum, and start by using the definition of
\rho_{t}

On the one hand, we see that from the definition of
\theta

\eta\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta\eta}\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{1+\eta\eta}}=\frac{\eta}{\eta\eta}=\eta^{-1}.

On the other hand, we can guarantee that

Now, observe that

.

, which is equivalent to
T\geq 1
, which is true.

Combining all those bounds together with our new definition for
\eta
, we now have

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bar{\bm{w}}_{T})-\inf\ell\right]\leq\frac{2\|\bm{w}_{0}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}}{\eta T}+\eta\frac{\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2}}{2}+\frac{\eta\delta^{2}T}{4}.

To simplify the last stage of our analysis, let us note
a=2\|\bm{w}_{0}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}
,
b=\frac{\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2}}{2}
and
c=\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}
, so that our bound writes as

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bar{\bm{w}}_{T})-\inf\ell\right]\leq\frac{a}{\eta T}+\eta b+c\eta T.

(23)

Minimizing the right-hand side is equivalent to solve
\frac{a}{\eta T}=\eta b+c\eta T
, where

\frac{a}{\eta T}=\eta b+c\eta T\Leftrightarrow\eta^{2}=\frac{a}{T(b+cT)}\Leftrightarrow\eta=\sqrt{\frac{a}{bT+cT^{2}}}\Leftrightarrow\eta=2\sqrt{\frac{2\|\bm{w}_{0}-\bm{w}_{*}\|^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2})T+\delta^{2}T^{2}}}

With such choice of stepsize, our bound becomes

\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bar{\bm{w}}_{T})-\inf\ell\right]\leq\frac{2a}{\eta T}=\frac{2a}{T}\sqrt{\frac{bT+cT^{2}}{a}}=2\sqrt{a}\sqrt{\frac{b}{T}+c}=2\|\bm{w}_{0}-\bm{w}_{*}\|\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+(\delta+G)^{2}}{T}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}}

and this gives us our first bound.
For the second bound, let us take simply
\eta=\frac{1}{\delta T+\sqrt{T}}
and inject it into (
23
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\bar{\bm{w}}_{T})-\inf\ell\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{a\delta T+a\sqrt{T}}{T}+\frac{b}{\delta T+\sqrt{T}}+\frac{cT}{\delta T+\sqrt{T}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a\delta+\frac{a}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{b}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{c}{\delta}=\frac{a+b}{\sqrt{T}}+a\delta+\frac{c}{\delta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2D^{2}+\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}+G^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}+(2D^{2}+1)\delta,

where in the last inequality we simplified some numerical constants.
∎

Proof.

Appendix B
Approximate Computation of Median-like Estimators

B.1
Median-like Estimators : General Considerations

Given a function
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
we consider the problem (
4
), which writes as

\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{z})\right].

In what follows, we will mostly consider functions
\mathcal{D}
satisfying the following assumption.

B.1
.

The function
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex,
{\rm{argmin}}~\mathcal{D}=\{0\}
and the problem (
4
) admits a solution.

Note that assuming
\mathcal{D}
to be convex and finite implies that it is continuous, therefore measurable, which means that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{z})\right]
is well defined.
The assumption that
{\rm{argmin}}~\mathcal{D}=\{0\}
guarantees that in the trivial case when the distribution is a Dirac
\delta_{\bm{m}_{0}}
, then the solution to (
4
) is exactly
\bm{m}_{0}
.
The assumption that a solution to (
4
) exists is easily verified in practice, under the assumption that
\mathcal{D}
is coercive:

B.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex.
Assume that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right])\right]<+\infty
.
If
\mathcal{D}
is coercive, i.e.
\lim\limits_{\|m\|\to\infty}\mathcal{D}(\bm{m})=+\infty
, then (
4
) admits a solution.

Proof.

Let
\phi(\bm{m}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{z})\right]
.
Since we assume that
\phi(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right])=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right])\right]<+\infty
then
\phi
is proper.
Since
\mathcal{D}
is convex then
\phi
is convex, as an expectation of convex functions.
Moreover
\mathcal{D}
is convex and finite, which means that
\mathcal{D}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see e.g. Corollary 8.39 in
(
Bauschke & Combettes 2017
)
.
Therefore
\phi
is an expectation of lower semicontinuous functions, which implies that
\phi
is lower semicontinuous.

Since
\mathcal{D}
is coercive, convex and continuous, we know that there exists
a\in(0,+\infty)
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
(see Proposition 14.16 in
(
Bauschke & Combettes 2017
)
) such that

\mathcal{D}(\cdot)\geq a\|\cdot\|+b
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\phi(\bm{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{m}-\bm{z})\right]\geq a\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{m}-\bm{z}\|\right]+b

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle a\|\bm{m}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right]\|-a\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right]\|\right]+b

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle a\|\bm{m}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right]\|-a\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right])\right]\underset{\|\bm{m}\|\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}+\infty,

where in the inequalities we used the fact that
\|\cdot\|
is
1
-Lipschitz, and that
a\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right]\|\right]\leq a\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{D}(\bm{z}-\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{z}\right])\right]-b<+\infty
.
So
\phi
is coercive on top of being proper convex and lower semicontinuous. We can then conclude that
\phi
has a minimizer, with for instance Proposition 11.15 from
(
Bauschke & Combettes 2017
)
.
∎

(25)

Here,

\bm{u}_{t}
is a (convex) subgradient. Since
\mathcal{D}
could be a non-differentiable function such as the

-norm, we use subgradients instead of gradients.

Squared
\ell_{2}
-norm.

\ell_{1}

If we choose

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}=\bm{m}_{t}+\tau\sgn(\bm{g}_{t}-\bm{m}_{t}),

\ell_{2}

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}=\bm{m}_{t}-\tau\frac{\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}}{\|\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}\|}=\big(1-\frac{\tau}{\|\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}\|}\big)\bm{m}_{t}+\frac{\tau}{\|\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}\|}\bm{g}_{t}.

B.3

\mathcal{D}

Given a proper, closed convex function

\begin{cases}\text{Sample $\bm{g}_{t}$ i.i.d. from $\mathcal{G}$}\\

(27)

As we see next, those iterations can be reformulated to simply involve the proximal operator of
\mathcal{D}

B.3

Let
\mathcal{D}
verify Assumption
B.1

The

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{t}+\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}}(\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}^{*}(\frac{\bm{m}_{t}-\cdot}{\tau})}(\bm{g}_{t}),

(29)

Proof.

Here we make use of proximal calculus rules, which can found in most textbooks on convex analysis, such as chapter 6 in
(
Beck 2017

Starting from (

\bm{y}=\bar{\bm{y}}+\bm{g}_{t}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\bm{m}_{t+1}=\underset{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\argmininn}\;\mathcal{D}(\bm{y}-\bm{g}_{t})+\tfrac{1}{2\tau}\|\bm{y}-\bm{m}_{t}\|^{2}\\

This proves (
28

29

(
Beck 2017

Beck 2017

\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}}(\bm{m})=\bm{m}-\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}^{*}(\frac{\cdot}{\tau})}(\bm{m})

in (

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{t}+\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}}(\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t})

\displaystyle=\bm{g}_{t}+\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t}-\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}^{*}(\frac{\cdot}{\tau})}(\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t})

\displaystyle=\bm{m}_{t}-\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}^{*}(\frac{\cdot}{\tau})}(\bm{m}_{t}-\bm{g}_{t})

(30)

Finally, using that for any function

\underset{\bm{y}}{\argmininn}\;f(\bm{y})=\bm{m}+\underset{\bar{\bm{y}}}{\argmininn}\;f(\bar{\bm{y}}+\bm{m})

in (

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{m}_{t}-\left(\bm{m}_{t}+\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}^{*}(\frac{\cdot+\bm{m}_{t}}{\tau})}(-\bm{g}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{D}^{*}(\frac{\bm{m}_{t}\cdot-}{\tau})}(\bm{g}_{t})

, or error bound
(
Lewis & Pang 1998
)
.
is true if and only if the following inequality is satisfied (see Theorem 5.1 in
(
Cornejo et al. 1997
)
or Proposition 3.1 in
(
Lemaire 1998
)
)

(\forall\bm{m}\in\dom\partial\varphi)\quad\|\partial\varphi(\bm{m})\|_{-}\geq\mu.

Our goal now is to prove the above inequality, from which the conclusion will follow.
Let us consider
\bm{m}\notin{\rm{argmin}}~\varphi
, and use standard calculus to write

\partial\varphi(\bm{m})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sgn(\bm{m}-{\bm{g}}_{i})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\sgn(\bm{m}_{j}-({\bm{g}}_{i})_{j})\big)_{j=1,\ldots,d}=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sgn(\bm{m}_{j}-({\bm{g}}_{i})_{j})\Big)_{j=1,\ldots,d}.

Our goal is to show that
\|\partial\varphi(\bm{m})\|_{-}\geq\frac{1}{n}
, which is equivalent to show that
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sgn(\bm{m}-{\bm{g}}_{i})\|_{-}\geq 1
.
Because
\bm{m}\notin{\rm{argmin}}~\varphi
, we know that
0\notin\partial\varphi(\bm{m})
so there must exist a coordinate
j
such that the
j
-th coordinate of
\partial\varphi(\bm{m})
does not contain zero, in other words such that
0\notin\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sgn(\bm{m}_{j}-({\bm{g}}_{i})_{j})
.
Using the fact that
\|\cdot\|_{2}\geq\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, we can therefore lower bound
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sgn(\bm{m}-{\bm{g}}_{i})\|_{-}\geq|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sgn(\bm{m}_{j}-({\bm{g}}_{i})_{j})|_{-}
.
Let us denote
s_{i}:=\sgn(\bm{m}_{j}-({\bm{g}}_{i})_{j})
, so that we want to prove that
|\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}|_{-}\geq 1
.
Let us now consider a few cases.

•

If
s_{i}=\{+1\}
or
\{-1\}
for every
i
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
is a singleton. Furthermore it is a sum of relative numbers, so
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. But we also know that
0\notin\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
, so we conclude that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}
, and so that
|\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}|_{-}\geq 1
.

•

If
s_{i}=[-1,+1]
for every
i
, then we immediately see that
0\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
which is a contradiction.

•

Otherwise, the
s_{i}
are combinations of singletons and intervals. Let us note
I=\{i:s_{i}=[-1,+1]\}
and
I^{\prime}=\{i:s_{i}=\{\pm 1\}\}
, which are not empty by assumption.
Let us also note
k\geq 1
the cardinality of
I
, so that
\sum_{i\in I}s_{i}=[-k,+k]
.
We also introduce
s:=\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}}s_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Now we can write

|\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}|_{-}=\inf\limits_{t\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}}|t|=\inf\limits_{t\in[-k,+k]}|t+s|=d(s;[-k,+k]),

where the latter is the distance from the number
s
to the interval
[-k,+k]
.
Now remember that
0\notin\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}
, so this distance cannot be zero, which means that
|s|>k
.
Moreover both
s
and
k
are rational numbers, so this distance must be greater or equal to
1
. This concludes the proof.

∎
