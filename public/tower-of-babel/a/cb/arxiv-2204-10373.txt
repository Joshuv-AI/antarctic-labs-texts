Distributed Nonparametric Estimation under Communication Constraints
Azeem Zaman azaman@g.harvard.edu, Botond Szabó botond.szabo@unibocconi.it

Abstract
In the era of big data, it is necessary to split extremely large data sets across multiple computing nodes and construct estimators using the distributed data. When designing distributed estimators, it is desirable to minimize the amount of communication across the network because transmission between computers is slow in comparison to computations in a single computer. Our work provides a general framework for understanding the behavior of distributed estimation under communication constraints for nonparametric problems. We provide results for a broad class of models, moving beyond the Gaussian framework that dominates the literature. As concrete examples we derive minimax lower and matching upper bounds in the distributed regression, density estimation, classification, Poisson regression and volatility estimation models under communication constraints. To assist with this, we provide sufficient conditions that can be easily verified in all of our examples.

1
Distributed Estimation

As a consequence of the ever increasing amount of information being collected there has been substantial growth in distributed methods to handle data that is too large to store and analyze on a single machine. Distributed methods also arise naturally due to privacy considerations or scenarios when data are collected and processed at multiple locations before being summarized centrally.

To statistically model a distributed estimation problem, we assume that we have
m
machines or servers observing independent, identically distributed (iid) data. We will perform calculations on the local machines and transmit the results to a central machine or server for aggregation. In theory any type of information transmission is allowed, but in practice we typically transmit a local estimate (based on the local data). The local estimates are converted to a binary string and transmitted to a central machine that may aggregate these estimates in any manner. In this paper we focus on procedures that use only a single round of communication: the local machines transmit to the central machine only once and a final estimate is constructed based only on this single set of transmissions.

Communicating data across a network is orders of magnitude slower than working with the same data on a machine, forming the computational bottleneck of the problem. This means that methods communicating large amounts of data may be too slow to be practically useful. In addition, excess communication can cause congestion on the computing network and inconvenience other users. Therefore we introduce communication constraints in our network to minimize the run time of the algorithm. In the absence of such communication constraints, one may transfer the whole data set to the central machine and the distributed framework imposes no meaningful restriction. We assume that each machine from
k=1,\ldots,m
has a communication budget
B^{(k)}
, which is a limit on the length of the binary string that can be transmitted to the central machine.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{\text{dist}}(\boldsymbol{B})
, where
\boldsymbol{B}=(B^{(1)},\ldots,B^{(m)})
, denote set of estimators that can be constructed with a single round of communication subject to the budget constraint. Our goal is to determine the minimax rate for estimators in
\mathcal{F}_{\text{dist}}(\boldsymbol{B})
in an abstract statistical framework and apply the derived results on various specific models.

The theoretical study of statistical estimation and testing under communication constraints has emerged only recently, with most of the work focusing on parametric models, see for instance
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Duchi et al. 2014
;
Shamir 2014
;
Braverman et al. 2016
;
Xu and Raginsky 2016
;
Fan et al. 2019
;
Han et al. 2020
;
Cai and Wei 2020
;
Cai and Wei 2021b
for estimation and
Acharya et al. 2020b
;
Acharya et al. 2020a
;
Szabo et al. 2020
;
Szabo et al. 2022
for testing. Nonparametric models, where the parameter of interest is infinite dimensional, have been studied in just the last few years in context of specific models, focusing mainly on Gaussian likelihood functions. Distributed minimax estimation rates were derived in context of the Gaussian white noise
Zhu and Lafferty 2018
, and nonparametric regression models
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
. Adaptation to the unknown regularity parameter was investigated in
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
;
Szabo and van Zanten 2020a
;
Cai and Wei 2021a
. Outside of the Gaussian framework only the closely related density estimation problem
Barnes et al. 2019
has been investigated so far. Thus up to now there is a lack of a general, abstract understanding of the limitations and guarantees of distributed techniques under communication constraints in nonparametric settings.

In this paper we take on this task and derive minimax lower and matching upper bounds in abstract settings going beyond the Gaussian framework, requiring, roughly speaking, only appropriate tail bounds on the likelihood ratio over an appropriately selected finite sieve. We demonstrate the applicability of our results over a range of nonparametric models, including nonparametric regression, density estimation, binary and Poisson regressions and volatility estimation.

We organize the paper as follows: In Section
2
we formally introduce distributed methods and our abstract modeling assumptions under which minimax lower and matching upper bounds are derived in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, respectively. In Section
2.3
we provide simple sufficient conditions that we can be used to apply our general theorems to the examples presented in Section
3
. We summarize our results and discuss further research questions in Section
4
. The proofs for the minimax lower and upper bounds are presented in Section
5
, while in Section
6
we demonstrate the validity of our sufficient conditions. The proofs of the examples are given in Section
7
, while auxiliary lemmas and their proofs are deferred to the Supplement.

2
Main results

The goal of this paper is to derive theoretical limitations and guarantees for distributed methods under communication constraints in a general, abstract setting. We assume that there are
m
machines and in each machine
k=1,...,m
we observe a sample
X^{(k)}=\{X_{i}^{(k)}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
from some unknown distribution
p_{f}
indexed by an infinite dimensional functional parameter
f\in L_{2}([0,1])
of interest. The total (global) sample size is denoted by
N=nm
. We assume, that the function
f
belongs to a Besov (Sobolev) ball
f\in B_{2,2}^{r}(L)
for some known
r,L>0
, see Section
10.1
in the Supplement for a brief summary of Besov spaces and wavelets. As an example consider the distributed nonparametric regression model with observations
(T_{i}^{(k)},X_{i}^{(k)})
,
i=1,...,n
,
k=1,...,m
, satisfying that
T_{i}^{(k)}\sim^{iid}U(0,1)
and
X_{i}^{(k)}|T_{i}^{(k)}\sim^{iid}\phi(x-f(T_{i}^{(k)}))
, studied also in
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
.

We consider estimation procedures where each machine can transmit a binary message
Y^{(k)}
with almost sure length constraint
l(Y^{(k)})\leq B^{(k)}
to the central machine where the information is aggregated to form a global estimator
\hat{f}_{n,m}=\hat{f}_{n,m}(Y^{(1)},...,Y^{(m)})
depending only on the transmitted information; see display (
1
) below for a graphical representation.

\begin{matrix}X^{(1)}&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\qquad Y^{(1)}\quad&\put(-10.0,4.0){\vector{3}{-1}{20.0}}\\
\vdots&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\qquad\vdots\quad&\put(-10.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}\\
X^{(m)}&\put(0.0,5.0){\vector{1}{0}{20.0}}&\qquad Y^{(m)}\quad&\put(-10.0,5.0){\vector{3}{1}{20.0}}\end{matrix}\qquad\hat{f}_{n,m}.

(1)

We derive minimax lower bounds under abstract conditions on the likelihood ratio in an appropriately selected sieve and show the sharpness of the lower bounds by providing matching upper bounds (up to
\log n
factor) under non-restrictive conditions. Before proceeding to the lower bounds we quantify the information content in the communication-restricted, distributed setting for recovering the functional parameter of interest
f
. For this end we define the
bit adjusted sample size
(BASS)
N_{\text{bass}}=N_{\text{bass}}(B^{(1)},\ldots,B^{(m)})
for estimating
f\in B_{2,2}^{r}
as the solution to the following equation:

\displaystyle N_{\text{bass}}

\displaystyle=\max\Big(n\log{N},n\sum_{k=1}^{m}\big(\frac{B^{(k)}\log{N}}{N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}}\wedge 1\big)\Big).

(2)

The exact form of
N_{\text{bass}}
will be verified below. The equation in (
2
) does not have a closed form solution in general, but the BASS is always defined since the left-hand side of (
2
) is increasing in
N_{\text{bass}}
and the right-hand side is decreasing in
N_{\text{bass}}
. The expression can be greatly and easily simplified if we assume that
B^{(1)}=B^{(2)}=\dots=B^{(m)}=B
, which is presented below.

Proposition 1

(
N_{\text{bass}}
in symmetric case)
.

If
B^{(k)}=B
for all
k=1,\ldots,m
, then

\displaystyle N_{\text{bass}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}N,&\text{if}\quad B\geq N^{1/(1+2r)}/\log{N},\\
\left(NB\log{N}\right)^{\frac{1+2r}{2+2r}},&\text{if}\quad\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m\leq B<N^{1/(1+2r)}/\log{N},\\
n\log{N},&\text{if}\quad B<\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m.\end{cases}

(3)

We briefly discuss the above proposition. Based on (
3
), we see that in the symmetric budget case the BASS is a continuous function of the budget
B
. Furthermore, note that it depends on the smoothness
r
of the function
f
. For more regular functions communication constraints results less information loss. Also note that if the communication budget is large enough, then
N_{\text{bass}}=N
and there is no information loss due to the distributed architecture. However, if the communication budget is very small (i.e.
B<\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m
) then there are no benefits in communicating between machines and it is sufficient or even advantageous to use only the information available on a single machine, assuming that the central machine also receives a local data set of size
n
. In the intermediate case,
N_{\text{bass}}
depends on the communication budget
B
, the smoothness level
r
, and the overall sample size
N
.

2.1
Lower bounds

In this section we present lower bounds for the distributed minimax estimation rate in a general setting. To prove lower bounds one typically considers an appropriately chosen finite sieve
\mathcal{F}_{0}\subset\mathcal{F}
of the function class
\mathcal{F}=B_{2,2}^{r}(L)
of interest, i.e. note that

\displaystyle\adjustlimits{\inf}_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{dist}}(\boldsymbol{B})}{\sup}_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{f}d(f,\hat{f})\geq\adjustlimits{\inf}_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{dist}}(\boldsymbol{B})}{\sup}_{f\in{\mathcal{F}_{0}}}\mathbb{E}_{f}d(f,\hat{f}),

for arbitrary loss function
d:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\mapsto[0,\infty)
. For Besov regularity classes it is natural to define the finite sieve with the help of the wavelet basis functions. For resolution level
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{0}(j,C_{0},\epsilon)=\Big\{{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(t)+C_{0}:\,{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(t)=\epsilon 2^{-j(r+1/2)}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}\beta_{h}\psi_{jh}(t)\,:\,\beta\in\{-1,1\}^{2^{j}}\Big\},

for parameters
C_{0},\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
. The parameter
C_{0}
shifts the function to ensure that the modeling assumptions are met; certain models, such as density estimation (where
C_{0}+{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
is the density) or Poisson regression (where
C_{0}+{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
is the mean of a Poisson), require
C_{0}+{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(t)\geq 0
. In our analysis we consider compactly supported wavelets (e.g. boundary corrected Daubechies wavelets with
S>r
vanishing moments), see Section
10.1
for a brief review. For convenience we consider a subset of the basis functions at resolution level
j
, such that they have pair-wise disjoint supports. Therefore, we divide the interval
[0,1]
into a partition of
d=c2^{j}
(for some constant
0<c<1
depending on
r
) disjoint intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{d}
, such that each interval
I_{k}
contains the full support of a wavelet basis function
\psi_{j,h}
for
h\in\{0,\ldots 2^{j}-1\}
. For Daubechies wavelets with
S
vanishing moments, this is possible for
c\leq 1/(2S+2)
. By slightly abusing our notations we re-index the basis functions so that the subset of size
d
of wavelets with disjoint support are indexed by
h=1,\ldots,d
. Hence we consider finite sieves of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}(d,C_{0},\epsilon)=\Big\{{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(t)+C_{0}:\,{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(t)=\epsilon d^{-(r+1/2)}\sum_{h=1}^{d}\beta_{h}\psi_{jh}(t)\,:\,\beta\in\{-1,1\}^{d}\Big\},

(4)

where
\psi_{jh}
and
\psi_{jh^{\prime}}
have disjoint support for any
1\leq h<h^{\prime}\leq d
. For a fixed
d
, we will take wavelets at resolution
j
, where
j
is the smallest resolution level that allows for the construction of
d
disjoint wavelets.

We give our assumption in terms of the log-likelihood-ratios in the sieve
\mathcal{F}_{0}(d,C_{0},\epsilon)
. Let us denote the log-likelihood ratio between two elements
{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(t)+C_{0},f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}(t)+C_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}(d,C_{0},\epsilon)
for a single observation
X_{i}^{(k)}
by

\displaystyle W_{d}^{(k)}(X_{i}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p_{{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}}(X_{i}^{(k)})}{p_{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}}(X_{i}^{(k)})}.

We assume below that the log-likelihood-ratio for the observations is sufficiently small, both in expectation and with high-probability, and that it satisfies a nonrestrictive decomposition structure. This is formalized in the following assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Expectation control)
.

Assume that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}[W_{d}^{(k)}(X_{i}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})]\leq C\epsilon^{2}d^{-2r}.

Assumption 2

(Decomposable structure)
.

Assume that the sample space can be partitioned into events
\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{d}
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\chi_{h})=1/d
for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
, and for all
i=1,...,n
,
k=1,...,m
and
h=1,...,d
it holds that the distribution
X_{i}^{(k)}|(X_{i}^{(k)}\in\chi_{h})
only depends on
\beta_{h}
and not on
\beta_{h^{\prime}}
for
h^{\prime}\neq h
.

Assumption 3

(Tail control)
.

Assume that for
a_{n}=C\epsilon d^{-1/2-r}\sqrt{n\log(N)}
the event

E_{h}^{(k)}(a)=\left\{X^{(k)}\,:\,\max_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\sum_{X_{i}^{(k)}\in\chi_{h}}W_{d}^{(k)}\left(X_{i}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\right)\leq a\right\}

satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{h}}\left(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})\right)=1+o(m^{-2}d^{-2})\qquad\forall\,h\in\{1,...,d\},\,\,k\in\{1,...,m\},\,\,\beta_{h}\in\{-1,1\}.

Remark 1
.

In Assumption
2
one can consider arbitrary fixed distribution over the partition events
\chi_{h}
,
h=1,...,d
, which for simplicity we take to be uniform. Our results can be easily generalised to other distributions over the partition.

We provide a brief discussion of the above assumptions. Assumption
1
is equivalent to bounding the KL divergence between
p_{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
and
p_{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}}
, which is a standard tool used for deriving minimax lower bounds in various nonparametric models, see for instance Theorem 6.3.2 of
Giné and Nickl 2015
. Assumption
2
means that the dependence on the functional parameter must be spatially localized, which is true for many common models. When working with a wavelet based testing sieve, we can select the partition based on the supports of the wavelets used in
{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
. Finally, in Assumption
3
we require that the maximum of the likelihood ratio over the sieve
\mathcal{F}_{0}(d,C_{0},\epsilon)
evaluated at the data belonging to bin
I_{h}
is bounded (by
a_{n}
) with high probability. While the first assumption controls the mass (expected value) of the likelihood ratio, this assumption controls the tail behaviour. Based on these assumptions we can formulate our general lower bound theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold. The distributed minimax rate with communication constraints
\boldsymbol{B}=(B^{(1)},\ldots,B^{(m)})
for recovering
f\in B_{2,2}^{r}(L)
is bounded from below by

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{dist}}(\boldsymbol{B})}\sup_{f\in B_{2,2}^{r}(L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}-f\|_{2}^{2}\gtrsim N_{\text{bass}}^{-\frac{2r}{1+2r}}.

The proof is deferred to Section
5.1
, but we provide a few comments here. The proof is an extension of the common technique for deriving minimax bounds using Fano’s inequality. The general technique follows the ideas of
Zhang et al. 2013
, which studied parametric models. These techniques were extended to the nonparametric regression model in
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
. In non-distributed models the main step is to control the expected value of the log-likelihood-ratio of two distributions in the testing sieve (the quantity
\cramped{W_{d}^{(k)}}
discussed above). The difficulty of extending to distributed architectures with communication constraints is that we must control not just the expectation of the likelihood ratio (Assumption
1
), but also the tail of the distribution of the ratio (Assumption
3
).

2.2
Upper bounds

Here we introduce conditions that ensure that the general lower bounds derived in Theorem
1
are tight by providing matching upper bounds (up to log-factors). We focus on the case where the communication budget across machines are all equal,
B=B^{(1)}=\dots=B^{(m)}
. We also assume that the central machine receives a local sample of size
n
. Then if the communication budget is too small (i.e.
B<\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m
), then a standard local estimator (based solely on the local data available at the central machine) achieves the minimax lower bounds
n^{-r/(1+2r)}
. In this case distributed methods are not adventageous compared to methods considering only local information hence our focus lies on the more interesting large enough budget case (i.e.
B\geq\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m
).

We will estimate the wavelet coefficients
f_{jh}
of
f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}f_{jh}\psi_{jh}(x)
using local estimators
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}
on all machines
k=1,\ldots,m
. The estimator
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}
should be thought of as the local version of the standard estimator of the wavelet coefficient
f_{jh}
that would be used in a non-distributed setting. We then compress
\cramped{\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}}
of
f_{jh}
into a
\log_{2}{N}
long binary string
\cramped{Y_{jh}^{(k)}}
following Algorithm 1 from
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
, which is reproduced as Algorithm
2
in the Supplement for reference. When
|\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}|>\sqrt{N}
, we transmit
Y_{jh}^{(k)}=0
to reduce communication costs. We denote the binary approximation of the estimators
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}
by
\tilde{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}=\tilde{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}(Y_{jh}^{(k)})
. We provide below general assumptions on the local estimators to ensure that upper bounds match (up to a logarithmic factor) the lower bounds derived in the preceding section.

Assumption 4
.

There exist
\sigma^{2}>0
such that the local wavelet coefficient estimators
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}
,
j\leq\log(N)/(1+2r)
,
h=0,...,2^{j}-1
satisfy

1.

\mathbb{E}_{f}[\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}]=f_{jh}
,
\text{var}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)})\leq\sigma^{2}/n
,

2.

\mathbb{P}_{f}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}>\sqrt{N})\leq o(m^{-1}N^{-(3+4r)/(1+2r)})
.

We briefly discuss the assumptions. The condition on the mean and variance of the non-zero estimators are standard. The tail condition is necessitated by the communication constraint. In order to ensure that our estimation procedure satisfies the communication constraint, we do not transmit estimators larger than
\sqrt{N}
(see Algorithm
1
in the Supplement) as this would require too many bits. Since we truncate large estimators before transmitting them, we need to control the probability that an estimator is truncated in order to control the error introduced by this truncation. In all examples considered in this paper, the local estimator
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}
is an average which converges almost surely to
f_{jh}
by the law of large numbers. In light of this, the tail condition is mild. Section
2.3.2
gives sufficient conditions for the tail condition in terms of existence of higher order moments.

When the communication budget is too small, we must select a subset of wavelet coefficients for each machine to estimate. Our choice will extend the construction given in
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
in context of the nonparametric regression model to our abstract setting. We divide the machines into groups of size
\kappa
, where

\displaystyle\kappa=\max\left(1,\left\lfloor\left(\frac{B}{\log_{2}{N}}m\right)^{\frac{1+2r}{2+2r}}n^{-\frac{1}{2+2r}}\right\rfloor\wedge m\right).

(5)

Machines in different blocks will transmit the binary strings
Y_{jh}^{(k)}
corresponding to different wavelet coefficients. Machines in the
\ell
th block, i.e. machines
k\in\{(\ell-1)\kappa+1,...,\ell\kappa\}
with
\ell\in\{1,...,\lceil m/\kappa\rceil\}
, will transmit the coefficients in the
\ell
th block, i.e. transmit
Y_{jh}
with indices satisfying
2^{j}+h=(\ell-1)\lfloor B/\log_{2}{N}\rfloor
to
\ell\lfloor B/\log_{2}{N}\rfloor
. For all other values of
j
and
h
we set
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}=0
and transmit nothing. See Figure
1
for graphical representation.

The aggregated estimator for the
(j,h)
th wavelet coefficient with
2^{j}+h=(\ell-1)\lfloor B/\log_{2}{N}\rfloor
to
\ell\lfloor B/\log_{2}{N}\rfloor
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{jh}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{k=(\ell-1)\kappa+1}^{\ell\kappa}\tilde{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}

and the corresponding estimator of the functional parameter is

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{n,m}(\cdot)=\sum_{j=0}^{j_{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}\hat{f}_{jh}\psi_{jh}(\cdot),

(6)

with
j_{n}=\lfloor\log_{2}{(n\kappa)}/(1+2r)\rfloor
being the maximum resolution level we will estimate with the given budget. The theorem below states that this estimator achieves the minimax distributed rate up to a logarithmic factor.

Theorem 2

(Upper bound on minimax rate)
.

Under Assumption

\hat{f}_{n,m}\in\mathcal{F}_{distr}(\textbf{B})

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in B_{2,2}^{r}(L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim(N_{\text{bass}}/\log N)^{-\frac{2r}{1+2r}}.

.

The
\log N
factor in the upper bound is not optimized and in some regimes our estimator performs better, for instance for large enough budget (i.e.
N^{1/(1+2r)}\log{N}\leq B
) it can be completely removed.

Assumption
4
can be easily verified for many models using the sufficient conditions given in Section

.

2.3
Sufficient conditions for regression type problems

In this section we consider “regression type” models and provide sufficient conditions for upper and lower bounds for them. We assume that there are
m
machines and in each machine
k=1,...,m
we observe a sample
(T^{(k)},X^{(k)})=\{(T_{i}^{(k)},X_{i}^{(k)})\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
whose density
p_{f}(x,t)
factors as

\displaystyle X_{i}^{(k)}\mid T_{i}^{(k)}\sim p_{X\mid T}(x\mid f(T_{i}^{(k)})),\quad T_{i}^{(k)}\sim p_{T}(t),\quad i=1,...,n,\quad k=1,...,m,

(7)

for densities
p_{T}(t)
and
p(x\mid f(t))
. For simplicity we take
p_{T}(t)
to be the uniform density on
[0,1]
, but it can be easily extended to bounded, positive densities in compact intervals. In this more structured model it is possible to present sufficient conditions that are easier to verify in many examples.

2.3.1
Sufficient conditions for lower bounds

In this section we collect sufficient conditions for Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
when we are considering models of the form (
7
). These conditions consider a more restrictive class of models, but are typically easier to verify in a range of our examples. Without these results, verifying Assumption
3
requires us to establish a complicated tail bound on the likelihood-ratio (rather than the random variable itself). First let us introduce some short hand notations for the log-likelihood-ratio, which decomposes into a sum under (
7
):

\displaystyle W_{d}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{d,i}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{d}^{(k)}(X_{i}^{(k)},T_{i}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta^{\prime}}).

First we show that in the model (
7
) Assumption
2
automatically holds. Consider the partition
\chi_{h}=I_{h}\times\mathbb{R}
for
h=1,\ldots,d
and note that the event
(T_{i}^{(k)},X_{i}^{(k)})\in\chi_{h}
holds with probability
1/d
. Furthermore, conditional on the preceding event, the random design point
T_{i}^{(k)}
is uniformly distributed over
I_{h}
, while
X_{i}^{(k)}|T_{i}^{(k)}\sim p_{X|T}\big(\cdot|f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(T_{i}^{(k)})\big)

Examples

We apply our abstract theorem over various nonparametric models, including nonparametric regression, density estimation, binary regression, Poisson regression and heteroscedastic regression. We consider the case where each machine has the same budget

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n,m,B}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}N^{-\frac{r}{1+2r}},&B\geq N^{1/(1+2r)}/\log{N},\\
\left(NB\log{N}\right)^{-\frac{r}{2+2r}},&\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m\leq B<N^{1/(1+2r)}/\log{N},\\
(n\log{N})^{-\frac{r}{1+2r}},&B<\left(n\log{N}\right)^{1/(1+2r)}/m.\end{cases}

4
Concluding remarks and future work

In this paper we have studied the problem of nonparametric estimation in a distributed setting under communication constraints. We introduced the bit adjusted sample size (BASS), which characterizes the efficient information content in a distributed setting taking the communication constraint into account. We have identified sufficient conditions on the model for deriving distributed minimax lower bounds and a sufficient condition on a block distributed estimator achieving the theoretical lower bounds (up to a logarithmic factor). The abstract results were applied in a variety of nonparametric models including nonparametric regression, density estimation, classification, Poisson regression and volatility estimation.

Extending our results to high-dimensional models with sparse structure is a natural and interesting direction, which however, requires new ideas due to different underlying model structure. A possible future direction is to derive adaptive distributed procedures for the unknown regularity of the underlying functional parameter of interest achieving the minimax lower bounds. Such kind of estimators were considered in context of the nonparametric regression model based on the distributed Lepskii’s method in
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
. Another potential direction is to derive adaptive estimators which minimize communication between machines while achieving the non-distributed minimax rate; see
Szabo and van Zanten 2020a
;
Cai and Wei 2021a
for such approaches in context of the nonparametric regression and the Gaussian white noise models.

5
Proofs of the minimax distributed lower and upper bounds

5.1
Proof of Theorem
1

The proof of the theorem can be viewed as the extension of Theorem 2.1 of
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
to a more general, abstract framework. Here, however, we do not carry out explicit computations tailored to the regression model, but keep the setting more general, allowing to apply our results to a variety of models.

We first put a prior on our testing sieve; let
F\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
be a uniform random element on the discrete hypercube independent of
T
. This indexes a uniform distribution over the testing sieve by the bijection
F=\boldsymbol{\beta}\leftrightarrow{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
. First we give an upper bound for the mutual information
I(F;Y)
between the prior on the sieve
F
and the transmitted information
Y
, which will be needed to apply Theorem
17
(a variant of Fano’s inequality).

Lemma 3
.

Under Assumption
1
, we have
I(F;Y^{(k)})\leq C\epsilon^{2}nd^{-2r}
for all
k=1,\ldots,m
.

Proof.

Applying the data processing inequality to the Markov chain
F\to X^{(k)}\to Y^{(k)}
we get that
I(F;Y^{(k)})\leq I(F;X^{(k)})
. From the definition of the mutual information, the convexity of the KL-divergence, the independence of the observations and finally the definition of
W_{d}^{(k)}
together with Assumption
1
we get that

\displaystyle I(F;Y^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\frac{1}{2^{d}}KL\left(p_{f}(X^{(k)}\mid F=\boldsymbol{\beta}),\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}p(X^{(k)}|F=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}KL\left(p(X^{(k)}\mid F=\boldsymbol{\beta}),p(X^{(k)}\mid F=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}KL\left(p(X_{i}^{(k)}\mid F=\boldsymbol{\beta}),p(X_{i}^{(k)}\mid F=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}W_{d}^{(k)}(X_{i}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime})\leq C\epsilon^{2}nd^{-2r}.

∎

The next bound on the mutual information following from Assumption
3
relates the communication constraint to the entropy of the binary string
H(Y^{(k)})
, which can be related to the length of the code
l(Y^{(k)})
and therefore to the communication budget via a modified version of Shannon’s source coding theorem.

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
3
with
d\asymp N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}
we have for some
C>0
that

\displaystyle I(F;Y^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq C\epsilon^{2}d^{-1-2r}n\log(N)H(Y^{(k)})+o(m^{-1}).

Proof.

Let us start by defining the latent variables
U_{i}^{(k)}
,
i=1,...,n
,
k=1,...,m,
such that
U_{i}^{(k)}=h
if
X_{i}^{(k)}\in\chi_{h}
. Note that
U_{i}^{(k)}
s are independent, discrete uniformly distributed on the set
[d]=\{1,...,d\}
, and independent from
F
. Hence, in view of the chain rule and the nonnegativity of the mutual information we get that

\displaystyle I(F;Y^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq I(F;Y^{(k)})+I(F;U^{(k)}|Y^{(k)})=I\big(F;(Y^{(k)},U^{(k)})\big)

\displaystyle=I(F;Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)})+I(F;U^{(k)})=I(F;Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in[d]^{n}}I(F;Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{u})P(U^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{u}).

Furthermore, in view of the assumption that
X^{(k)}_{i}|(U^{(k)}_{i}=u_{i},F_{u_{i}})
is independent of
F_{u_{i}^{\prime}}
,
u_{i}^{\prime}\neq u_{i}
, we can apply Theorem A.13 of
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
, implying that

\displaystyle I(F;Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{d}\Big[(\log{2})\sqrt{\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{h}}(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})^{\complement}|U^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{u})}

\displaystyle\quad+(\log{|\mathcal{F}_{0}(r,\epsilon)|})\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{h}}(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})^{\complement}|U^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{u})\Big]

\displaystyle\quad+2e^{2a_{n}}(e^{a_{n}}-1)^{2}I(X^{(k)};Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)}=\boldsymbol{u}),

(14)

where the event
E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})
was defined in Assumption
3
.

Noting that
\log{|\mathcal{F}_{0}(d,C_{0},\epsilon)|}\leq d\log 2
, we have in view of Assumption
3
, Jensen’s inequality and that the mutual information with
Y^{(k)}
is always bounded by the entropy
I(X^{(k)};Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)})\leq H(Y^{(k)})
,

\displaystyle I(F;Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq\log(2)\sum_{h=1}^{d}\Big[\sqrt{\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{h}}(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})^{\complement})}+d\mathbb{P}_{\beta_{h}}(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})^{\complement})\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad+2e^{2a_{n}}(e^{a_{n}}-1)^{2}I(X^{(k)};Y^{(k)}|U^{(k)})

\displaystyle=o(m^{-1})+2e^{2a_{n}}(e^{a_{n}}-1)^{2}H(Y^{(k)}).

Since
d\asymp N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}
, by Proposition
1
we get that
d^{2r+1}\geq cn\log{N}
and thus

\displaystyle a_{n}^{2}=C^{2}\epsilon^{2}d^{-1-2r}n\log{N}\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon^{2},

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
. Furthermore, by the convexity of the function
x\mapsto e^{x}
, there exists a constant
C
such that
e^{x}\leq 1+Cx
for all
x\in[0,C^{\prime}]
. Putting everything together we get that

\displaystyle I(F;Y^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq Ca_{n}^{2}H(Y^{(k)})+o(m^{-1})=C^{\prime}\epsilon^{2}d^{-1-2r}n\log(N)H(Y^{(k)})+o(m^{-1}),

completing the proof.
∎

Finally, we note that one can adapt assertion (3.4) in
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
to the present setting. For completeness we provide the proof of the lemma in the supplement.

Lemma 5
.

For
N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}\geq 20
we get that

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{f}_{n,m}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{dist}}(\boldsymbol{B})}\sup_{f\in B_{2,2}^{r}(L)}\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim N_{\text{bass}}^{-2r/(1+2r)}\left(1-\frac{I(F;Y)+\log{2}}{N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}/6}\right).

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1
. Based on Lemma
5
, it is sufficient to show that
I(F,Y)<N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}/7
. In view of
d\asymp N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}
, and Lemma
3
and
4
,

\displaystyle I(F;Y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}I(F;Y^{(k)})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\min\left(Cn\epsilon^{2}N_{\text{bass}}^{-2r/(1+2r)},C^{\prime}N_{\text{bass}}^{-1}\epsilon^{2}n\log(N)H(Y^{(k)})+o(m^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{2}nN_{\text{bass}}^{-2r/(1+2r)}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\min\left(1,C^{\prime}\frac{\log{N}}{N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}}H(Y^{(k)})\right)+o(1).

Furthermore, from Lemma 5.3 of
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
we know that

H(Y^{(k)})\leq 2\mathbb{E}[l(Y^{(k)})]+1\leq 2B^{(k)}+1,

so

\displaystyle n\sum_{k=1}^{m}\min\Big(1,C^{\prime}\frac{\log{N}}{N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}}H(Y^{(k)})\Big)\lesssim n\sum_{k=1}^{m}\min\Big(1,\frac{\log{N}}{N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}}B^{(k)}\Big)\leq N_{\text{bass}},

which in turn implies that
I(F;Y)\lesssim\epsilon^{2}N_{\text{bass}}^{1/(1+2r)}
. Picking
\epsilon>0
small enough concludes the proof.

5.2
Proof of Theorem
2

First we show that
\hat{f}_{n,m}\in\mathcal{F}_{dist}(B)
. On machine
k
, consider
l(Y_{jh}^{(k)})
, which is one if
|\hat{f}_{jh,n}^{(k)}|\geq\sqrt{N}
and
\log_{2}{|\hat{f}_{jh,n}^{(k)}|}+(1/2)\log_{2}{N}\leq\log_{2}{N}
if
\hat{f}_{jh,n}^{(k)}<\sqrt{N}
. Therefore
l(Y_{jh}^{(k)})\leq\log_{2}{N}
almost surely. The number of estimated coefficients is
\lfloor B/\log_{2}{N}\rfloor
, so the number of transmitted bits is bounded above by
\log_{2}{N}\times\lfloor B/\log_{2}{N}\rfloor\leq B
bits on each machine. Thus the communication budget is always satisfied.

Next we show that the estimator achieves the minimax distributed rate (up to a logarithmic factor). Let
R_{n}
denote the event that none of the transmitted strings
Y_{jh}^{(k)}
, for
j\in\{0,...,j_{n}\},h\in\{0,...,2^{j}-1\}
and
k\in\{1,...,m\}
, is equal to zero. Then

\displaystyle f_{jh}-\hat{f}_{jh}=f_{jh}-|U_{jh}|^{-1}\sum_{k\in U_{jh}}\tilde{f}_{jh}^{(k)}=Z_{jh}+\epsilon_{jh},

(15)

where

\epsilon_{jh}=|U_{jh}|^{-1}\sum_{k\in U_{jh}}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}-\tilde{f}_{jh}^{(k)})
,
Z_{jh}=|U_{jh}|^{-1}\sum_{k\in U_{jh}}(f_{jh}-\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)})
and
U_{jh}=\{(\ell-1)\kappa+1,....,\ell\kappa\}
. Note that by Assumption
4
on the event
R_{n}
we have
\epsilon_{jh}\leq|U_{jh}|^{-1}\sum_{k\in U_{jh}}1/\sqrt{N}\leq 1/\sqrt{N}
. Furthermore, by the same assumption,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{jh}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[Z_{jh}^{2}]=\text{var}(Z_{jh})=|U_{jh}|^{-2}\sum_{k\in U_{jh}}\text{var}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)})\leq\sigma^{2}/(\kappa n)
.

Next we decompose the mean integrated squared error into two terms

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\|f-\hat{f}_{n,m}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}I_{R_{n}}+\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}I_{R_{n}^{\complement}}

and deal with them separately. First note that in view of (
15
), and the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}I_{R_{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}\mathbb{E}_{f}(f_{jh}-\hat{f}_{jh})^{2}I_{R_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j=0}^{j_{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}\big(\mathbb{E}_{f}(\epsilon_{jh}^{2}I_{R_{n}})+\mathbb{E}_{f}(Z_{jh}^{2})\big)+\sum_{j>j_{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}f_{jh}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{j_{n}+1}\big(1/N+\sigma^{2}/(n\kappa)\big)+2^{-2rj_{n}}L\lesssim N_{\text{bass}}^{-\frac{2r}{1+2r}},

since
N_{\text{bass}}/2<n\kappa\leq N_{\text{bass}}\leq N
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}I_{R_{n}^{\complement}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\|\hat{f}_{n,m}\|_{2}^{2}I_{R_{n}^{\complement}}\right]+2\|f\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{\complement}).

Then note that
\|\hat{f}_{n,m}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{j_{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}\hat{f}_{jh}^{2}{\leq 2^{j_{n}+1}N}\leq 2N^{\frac{2+2r}{1+2r}}
and by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{\complement})\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{j_{n}}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{j}-1}\mathbb{P}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}>\sqrt{N})\lesssim m2^{j_{n}}o(m^{-1}N^{-\frac{3+4r}{1+2r}})=o(N^{-2}).

By combining the above bounds we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f}\left[\|\hat{f}_{n,m}-f\|_{2}^{2}I_{R_{n}^{\complement}}\right]

Proof of Lemma

Using iterated expectations we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1}^{(k)})\mid T_{1}^{(k)}]\psi_{jh}(T_{1}^{(k)})]\right]=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\psi_{jh}(t)\,dt=f_{jh}.

\displaystyle n\text{var}\left(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\psi_{jh}(T_{1}^{(k)})^{2}\text{var}(h(X_{1}^{(k)})\mid T_{1}^{(k)})\right]+\text{var}\left(f(T_{1}^{(k)})\psi_{jh}(T_{1}^{(k)})\right)\leq 2C,

across all

Next consider the asymptotics of the tail bound. In view of Markov’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}>\sqrt{N})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}|^{q}>N^{q/2}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}|^{q}]}{N^{q/2}}.

Then by Minkowski’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}|^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[|h(X_{1}^{(k)})\psi_{jh}(T_{1}^{(k)})|^{q}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}[|h(X_{1}^{(k)})|^{q}\mid T_{1}^{(k)}]\psi_{jh}^{2}(T_{1}^{(k)})|\psi_{jh}(T_{1}^{(k)})|^{q-2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C2^{(q-2)j/2}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{jh}^{2}(T_{1}^{(k)})]\leq CN_{\text{bass}}^{\frac{(q-2)/2}{1+2r}}.

for a constant
C_{r}
that only depends on
r
.

Now we consider Assumption
3
. The event
E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})
only depends on
\beta_{h}
and
\beta_{h}^{\prime}
. If
\beta_{h}=\beta_{h}^{\prime}
, then the probability of the event is zero because the log-likelihood ratio is zero. So consider
\beta_{h}\neq\beta_{h}^{\prime}
. This means the bounds derived above a fortiori apply for
X_{i}^{(k)}\in I_{h}=\chi_{h}
.

Let us introduce the latent variable
U_{i}^{(k)}
such that
U_{i}^{(k)}=h
if
X_{i}^{(k)}\in I_{h}
and let
U^{(k)}=(U_{i}^{(k)})_{i=1,...,n}
. Define the event

\displaystyle D_{h}^{(k)}=\left[\frac{n}{2d}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{U_{i}^{(k)}=h}\leq\frac{2n}{d}\right],

which requires the number of observations in
I_{h}
to be close to the expected value of
n/d
. In view of Chernoff’s bound (see for instance Lemma 5.2 of
Szabo and van Zanten 2020b
for its application to the present setting), we know that
\mathbb{P}(D_{h}^{(k)})=1+o(m^{-2}d^{-2})
. Using the law of total probability we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n}))\leq\mathbb{P}(E_{h}^{(k)}(a_{n})\mid D_{h}^{(k)})+o(m^{-2}d^{-2}),

so it suffices to bound the conditional probability to be
o(m^{-2}d^{-2})
. We now want to apply Lemma
11
to bound the probability. The sequence
X_{i}^{(k)}
is iid for
i=1,\ldots,n
and we take the function
h(X_{i}^{(k)})=W_{d}^{(k)}(X_{i}^{(k)},\beta,\beta_{h})
. The sum
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(X_{i}^{(k)})\mathbbm{1}_{X_{i}^{(k)}\in I_{h}}
is exactly the quantity for which we seek to establish a tail bound. Let
\chi_{n}=I_{h}
be the set of interest. The length of the support of
W_{d,i}^{(k)}=h(X_{i}^{(k)})
conditional on
X_{i}^{(k)}\in I_{h}
is bounded by
C_{r}^{\prime}\epsilon^{2}d^{-2r}
for a constant
C_{r}^{\prime}
that only depends on
r
. From
\mathbb{P}(X_{i}^{(k)}\in I_{h})=1/d
and the above calculation we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{d,i}^{(k)}\mid X_{i}^{(k)}\in I_{h}]=\int_{I_{h}}W_{d}^{(k)}(x,\beta_{h},\beta_{h}^{\prime})d(1+\psi_{j_{n}h}(x))\,dx\leq C_{r}\epsilon^{2}d^{-2r}.

If we let
\delta_{n}=d^{-r}
, then Lemma
11
holds with
r_{n}=n/d
,
I=\{i:U_{i}^{(k)}=h\}
, and
\alpha=2
. The lemma gives a bound of
\exp(-C^{\prime}d^{2r}\log{N})=o(m^{-2}d^{-2})
for each of the finitely many combinations of
\beta_{h}
and
\beta_{h}^{\prime}
. This establishes that Assumption
3
holds.

Upper bound:
We verify Assumption
4
directly. Consider the local estimator
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{jh}(X_{i}^{(k)})
. We see that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}]=\int\psi_{jh}(x)f(x)=f_{jh}
. As the target density
f
is continuous on a compact interval, we can pick a
C
such that
f(x)\leq C
for all
x\in[0,1]
. The variance of
\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}
is bounded by the second moment, which satisfies
\text{var}(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)})\leq\frac{1}{n}\int\psi_{jh}^{2}f\leq C/n
. Now we can establish the tail bounds using Hoeffding’s inequality. As the wavelet
\psi_{jh}
is bounded by
|\psi_{jh}|\leq CN^{1/(1+2r)}
, we see that the estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{f}_{n,jh}^{(k)}>\sqrt{N}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2n^{2}\left(\sqrt{N}-f_{jh}\right)^{2}}{2nCN^{1/(1+2r)}}\right)\leq e^{-Cn}=o(m^{-1}N^{(3+2r)/(1+2r)})

as long as
m
is polynomial in
n
. Therefore all parts conditions of Assumption
4
are satisfied and Theorem
2
holds.

Auxiliary lemmas

In this section we collect technical lemmas used in the proof of our main results.

8.1
Sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential random variables

In this paper we make use of sub-Gaussian and sub-exponential random variables for their tail properties. We briefly review the features of these classes of random variables that are most relevant to our work.

Definition 1
.

A random variable
X
is
sub-Gaussian
if there exists a
\sigma^{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}[X])}\right]\leq e^{\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2}/2},\quad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}

in which case we write
X\sim\text{SG}(\sigma^{2})
. We say that
X
is
sub-exponential
if there exist
\nu^{2}>0
and
b\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(X-\mathbb{E}[X])}\right]\leq e^{\nu^{2}\lambda^{2}/2}\quad\text{for }|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{b},

in which case we write
X\sim\text{SE}(\nu^{2},b)
.

Note that these random variables can posses non-zero means, only the above tail bound is required on the centered random variables. We allow
b=0
in the definition of sub-exponential random variables (taking
1/0=\infty
, implying that the bound holds for all
\lambda
) so that
\text{SE}(\nu^{2},0)=\text{SG}(\nu^{2})
. Recall that bounded random variables
X\in[a,b]
are sub-Gaussian with
\sigma^{2}=(b-a)^{2}/4
.

Next we recall the Hoeffding’s tail bound for sub-exponential random variables, see Proposition 2.9 of
Wainwright 2019
for a proof.

Lemma 8

(Sub-exponential tail bound)
.

Suppose that
X\sim\text{SE}(\nu^{2},b)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq\mathbb{E}[X]+t\right)\leq\begin{cases}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\nu^{2}}},&0\leq t\leq\nu^{2}/b,\\
e^{-\frac{t}{2b}},&t>\nu^{2}/b.\end{cases}

Note that a similar bound for
\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq t)
holds by including a multiplicative factor of two on the right hand side. In the case when
b=0
, we see that the bound for sub-Gaussian random variables is
\exp(-t^{2}/(2\nu^{2}))
.

Lemma 9

(Properties of sub-exponential random variables)
.

If
X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\text{SE}(\tau_{i}^{2},b_{i})
then

\displaystyle aX_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\text{SE}(a^{2}\tau_{i}^{2},ab_{i}),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\text{SE}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}^{2},\max_{i}b_{i}\Big).

Proof.

The second result is proved on page 29 of
Wainwright 2019
. The first result is straight-forward but not widely available. We have for the moment generating function of the centered random variable that

\displaystyle M_{aX_{i}}(\lambda)=M_{X_{i}}(a\lambda)\leq\exp\Big(\frac{\lambda^{2}a^{2}\tau_{i}^{2}}{2}\Big),\qquad\text{for}\,|a\lambda|\leq 1/b_{i},

so the MGF is bounded for
|\lambda|\leq(ab_{i})^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 10

(Poisson is sub-exponential)
.

Suppose
X\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda)
, then
(X-\lambda)\sim SE(2\lambda,1)
.

Proof.

Recall that
e^{x}\leq 1+x+x^{2}
for
|x|\leq 1
. The moment generating function of
X-\lambda
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{t(X-\lambda)}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-t\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tX}\right]=e^{-t\lambda}e^{\lambda(e^{t}-1)}\leq e^{-t\lambda}e^{\lambda t+\lambda t^{2}}=e^{t^{2}\tau^{2}/2}

for
|t|\leq 1
and
\tau^{2}=2\lambda
.
∎

8.2

Lemma 11

(General sum bound)
.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are iid random variables. Let
\chi_{n}
be a subset of the support of these random variables and define the corresponding random index set
I_{n}=\{i:X_{i}\in\chi_{n}\}
. Assume that for a measurable function
h
there exists a sequence
\delta_{n}=o(1)
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\chi_{n}}h(x)-\inf_{x\in\chi_{n}}h(x)\leq c_{1}\delta_{n}^{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[h(X_{i})\mid X_{i}\in\chi_{n}]\leq c_{2}\delta_{n}^{2}

for some
\alpha\geq 1
and positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. Define

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(X_{i})\mathbbm{1}_{X_{i}\in\chi_{n}}

to be the sum over
I_{n}
. Then the following hold:

1.

For any realization of
I_{n}
such that
|I_{n}|\leq c_{3}r_{n}
, with
\delta_{n}^{2}r_{n}<\log{N}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid I_{n}]<c_{2}c_{3}\delta_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}\log{N}}.

2.

For
0\leq c_{4}<c_{3}
,
C>c_{2}c_{3}
and
\delta_{n}^{2}r_{n}<\log{N}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{n}>C\delta_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}\log{N}}\mid c_{4}r_{n}\leq|I_{n}|\leq c_{3}r_{n})\leq e^{-C^{\prime}\delta_{n}^{2(1-\alpha)}\log{N}},

(21)

where
C^{\prime}>0
can be made arbitrarily large by taking
C
large enough.

Proof.

Using iterated expectations by conditioning on the realization of the set
I_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid I_{n}]=|I_{n}|\,\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1})\mid X_{1}\in\chi_{n}]\leq c_{2}c_{3}\delta_{n}^{2}r_{n}<c_{2}c_{3}\delta_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}\log{N}}.

Next note that
S_{n}|I_{n}
can be written as a sum of
|I_{n}|
iid terms, whose support length is bounded by
c_{1}\delta_{n}^{\alpha}
. Then by Hoeffding’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{n}>C\delta_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}\log{N}}\mid I_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid I_{n}]>C\delta_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}\log{N}}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid I_{n}]\mid I_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|S_{n}-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mid I_{n}]\right|>(C-c_{2}c_{3})\delta_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}\log{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(C-c_{2}c_{3})^{2}\delta_{n}^{2}r_{n}\log{N}}{2|I_{n}|c_{1}^{2}\delta_{n}^{2\alpha}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-C^{\prime}\delta_{n}^{2(1-\alpha)}\log{N}\right),

with
C^{\prime}=(C-c_{2}c_{3})^{2}/(2c_{3}c_{1}^{2})
, which can be made arbitrarily large by increasing
C
. Equation (
21
) then follows from the law of total probability by averaging over the distribution of
I_{n}\mid(c_{4}r_{n}\leq|I_{n}|\leq c_{3}r_{n})
.
∎

We now provide a number of simple inequalities that allow us to relate the log-likelihood ratio in our examples to polynomials in the testing sieve functions
{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
. These results
do not
have optimized constants or the largest possible range of validity in
x
.

Lemma 12
.

For any
-1\leq x\leq 1/2
the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle 1+2x+x^{2}\leq\frac{1+x}{1-x}\leq 1+2x+4x^{2},

\displaystyle x^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}-1\right)^{2}\leq 16x^{2}.

Proof.

By elementary algebra one can restate the first line as

\displaystyle 0\geq-x^{2}(1+x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\leq 2x^{2}(1-2x),

and the second line as

\displaystyle(x-3)(x+1)\leq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad(x-1/2)(x-3/2)\geq 0,

which all hold for
x\in[-1,1/2]
.
∎

Lemma 13
.

For
|x|\leq 1/2
,

\displaystyle-x-x^{2}\leq\log(1-x).

Proof.

Define the function

\displaystyle h(x)=\log(1-x)+x+x^{2}.

It is sufficient to show that
h(x)\geq 0
for
|x|\leq 1/2
. The derivative of
h
is

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=x(1-2x)(1-x)^{-1}.

Since
h^{\prime}(x)
is negative for
-1/2\leq x<0
and non-negative for
0\leq x\leq 1/2
, we see that the minimum is achieved at
x=0
. As
h(0)=0
, we have
h(x)\geq 0
, which proves the inequality.
∎

We now combine the previous two lemmas to create a bound on
\log((1+{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})/(1-{f_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}))
.

Lemma 14

(Log ratio bound)
.

For
|x|\leq 1/2
we have

\displaystyle 2x-4x^{2}\leq\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\leq 2x+4x^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq x\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\leq 4x^{2}.

Proof.

From Lemma
12
and the inequality
\log(1+x)\leq x
we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\leq\log(1+2x+4x^{2})\leq 2x+4x^{2}.

For the lower bound, we use Lemma
12
and
13
:

\displaystyle\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\geq\log(1+2x+x^{2})\geq\log(1+2x)\geq 2x-4x^{2}

when
|x|\leq 1/2
, which establishes the first result. To establish the second result, first consider
0\leq x\leq 1/2
. In this case, the result just established gives

\displaystyle x\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\leq x(2x+4x^{2})\leq 4x^{2}

for
x\in[0,1/2]
. When
x\in[-1/2,0)
, we use the lower bound just established to get

\displaystyle x\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\leq x(2x-4x^{2})\leq 4x^{2},

which establishes the upper bound for all
|x|\leq 1/2
. The product is non-negative because
x
and
\log((1+x)/(1-x))
always have the same sign.
∎

Lemma 15

(Bound on log squared)
.

For
0<x<\sqrt{2}
we have

\displaystyle\left(\log{x}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

First consider
0<x<1
. Then it suffices to establish that
0>(x-1)/\sqrt{2}>\log{x}
, which follows from
x-1>\log{x}
(by convexity) for all
x>0
and
0>(x-1)/\sqrt{2}>x-1
for
x<1
. Now consider the case
1\leq x<\sqrt{2}
, when it suffices to establish that
0\leq(x-1)/\sqrt{2}\leq\log{x}
. Define the function
h(x)=\log{x}-(x-1)/\sqrt{2}
. The statement follows by noting that
h(1)=0
and
h^{\prime}(x)=1/x-1/\sqrt{2}>0
for
x<\sqrt{2}
.

∎

Lemma 16

(Log ratio squared bound)
.

When
-1<x<(\sqrt{2}-1)/(\sqrt{2}+1)
we have

\displaystyle\left(\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{x^{2}}{2}.

Proof.

When
x<(\sqrt{2}-1)/(\sqrt{2}+1)
then
(1+x)/(1-x)<\sqrt{2}
so we can apply Lemma
15
to get

\displaystyle\left(\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}-1\right)^{2}.

As
0<(\sqrt{2}-1)/(\sqrt{2}+1)<1/2
, we can apply Lemma
12
to get the desired result.
∎
