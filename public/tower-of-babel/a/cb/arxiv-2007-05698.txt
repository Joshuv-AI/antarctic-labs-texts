From Heun Class Equations to Painlevé Equations
Jan DEREZIŃSKI a, Artur ISHKHANYAN bc and Adam LATOSIŃSKI a

Abstract
In the first part of our paper we discuss linear 2nd order differential equations in the complex domain, especially Heun class equations, that is, the Heun equation and its confluent cases. The second part of our paper is devoted to Painlevé I–VI equations. Our philosophy is to treat these families of equations in a unified way. This philosophy works especially well for Heun class equations. We discuss its classification into 5 supertypes, subdivided into 10 types (not counting trivial cases). We also introduce in a unified way deformed Heun class equations, which contain an additional nonlogarithmic singularity. We show that there is a direct relationship between deformed Heun class equations and all Painlevé equations. In particular, Painlevé equations can be also divided into 5 supertypes, and subdivided into 10 types. This relationship is not so easy to describe in a completely unified way, because the choice of the “time variable” may depend on the type. We describe unified treatments for several possible “time variables”.

Introduction

There are many types of differential equations and special functions. Typically, within a given class there is one generic type and many confluent types.
This is the case of Riemann (hypergeometric) class equations, Heun class equations, as well as Painlevé equations. For instance, the generic type of Riemann
class equations can always be reduced to the Gauss hypergeometric equation, but there are also confluent types such as Kummer’s confluent equation, the
F_{1}
equation, and the Hermite equation, see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
17
,
24
,
25
]
.

One can try to understand the process of confluence by considering
equations depending holomorphically on parameters.
Some properties of the whole class can be described in a uniform way,
without splitting the class into types.
For instance, one can identify various transformations (“symmetries”) that leave the class invariant.

In order to study the equations
in more detail, one needs to split the class into types.
Within a given type one can simplify the equation by symmetries and convert it to a
normal form
,
thereby reducing the number of parameters. This has to be done case by case.

In the case of hypergeometric class equations, this idea was successfully applied in the book by Nikiforov–Uvarov
[
18
]
. It works especially well for hypergeometric polynomials, that is Jacobi, Laguerre, Bessel and Hermite polynomials, which can be elegantly treated in a unified way.

In this paper, we try to apply this idea to the derivation of Painlevé equations from Heun class equations by the method of isomonodromic deformation.
We will see that each type of Heun class equation corresponds to a
(properly understood) type of Painlevé equation. The passage from
Heun class to Painlevé can be accomplished in a fairly uniform way, although one has to consider
several similar but distinct cases.

We start our paper by Section
2
containing basic theory of
singularities of 2nd order scalar ordinary differential equations.
This is a classic subject with several well-known textbooks, such as
[
9
,
23
]
.
We follow to a large extent the
treatment
described in the monograph by Slavyanov–Lay
[
25
]
and the
appendix to
[
24
]
, written by Slavyanov. Sometimes we
introduce new notation and terminology to
make precise some concepts which in
[
25
]
are implicit.

The central concept of the theory of linear differential equations in the complex domain is the
rank of a singularity
. In our paper we introduce several kinds of the rank.
In particular, we distinguish between the usual rank and the
absolute rank

(the infimum over the ranks of all possible transformed forms of a given equation).
The rank can be an integer or a half-integer. We also introduce the
rounded rank
, which has always an integer value.
Thus if the rank is
m
or
m-\frac{1}{2}
, where
m
is an integer, then
we say that its rounded rank is
m
. In particular, the rounded rank
is
1
if the singularity is
Fuchsian
(also called
regular
).
We believe that all these concepts clarify the theory of ordinary differential equations. We also discuss formal power series solutions of these equations (the so-called
Thomé solutions
). We introduce the concept of
indices

of a singular point. This is of course well-known for Fuchsian singularities. For non-Fuchsian singularities this concept is not so well known, although it is implicit in
[
25
]
.

In Section
3
we discuss equations with rational coefficients.
Such equations have a finite number of singularities on the Riemann sphere.
Following
[
25
]
, the class of equations with
n
singularities in

{\mathbb{C}}
and a singularity at infinity, and their confluent cases are
called
M_{n}
class
equations. red It is easy to see that these equations can be
written as

\displaystyle\big(\sigma(z)\partial_{z}^{2}+\tau(z)\partial_{z}+\eta(z)\big)u(z)=0,

(1.1)

\sigma
is a polynomial of degree
\leq n

\tau

\leq n-1

\sigma\eta

\leq 2n-2
.
We introduce also a closely related

[
25

such equations are called

not appropriate for this meaning.) They can be written as (
1.1

with the same conditions on
\sigma

\tau

\eta

The best known classes of equations with rational coefficients are the

The grounded

One of the central objects of our paper is the

\sigma

\leq 3

The main type within the Heun class is the standard

In the literature the name Heun class equations is employed in two meanings: the meaning that we have just described is used in

The full Heun class, beside the above five types has five more types, which we call
degenerate confluent, degenerate biconfluent, degenerate doubly confluent, doubly degenerate doubly confluent

[
25

Sometimes a singularity of an equation does not lead to a singularity
of its solutions. We call such points
non-logarithmic

Non-logarithmic singularities play an important role in the
following construction.

Let us start with an
equation (
1.1
). We introduce the
deformed form of (
1.1
)

to be the equation

\displaystyle\bigg(\sigma(z)\partial_{z}^{2}+\bigg(\tau(z)-\frac{\sigma(z)}{z-\lambda}\bigg)\partial_{z}+\eta(z)-\eta(\lambda)-\mu^{2}\sigma(\lambda)-\mu\big(\tau(\lambda)-\sigma^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+\frac{\mu\sigma(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}\times v(z)=0.

(1.2)

Note that all finite singularities of (

) are the same as
in (
1.1

,
2
.
\lambda

\mu=\frac{v^{\prime}}{v}(\lambda)

If (

]

Let us note that non-logarithmic singularities of an

, that is by replacing
the unknown

This procedure applied to grounded Heun class equations produces the so-called

[

that in deformed Riemann class equations one can
remove all non-logarithmic singularities by a change of gauge. We do not know if the same is
possible for all kinds of deformed Heun class equations.

The main topic of our paper, described in Section
4
,
is a derivation of
Painlevé equations from
Heun class equations.
The first step of this derivation is a choice of a family of Heun
class equations
depending on a
parameter denoted
t
and called the
time
.
Then we consider then the corresponding deformed Heun class equation, (
1.2
), which depends on two additional variables:
\lambda
,
\mu
.
The conditions for a constant monodromy lead to a set of nonlinear
differential equation for
\lambda
,
\mu
in terms of
t
. These
equations can be interpreted as Hamilton equations generated by

H(t,\lambda,\mu)
, which we
will call
Painlevé Hamiltonians
.

The most difficult ingredient of the passage from Heun class to
Painlevé is the choice of the time variable and of the so-called

compatibility functions

a,b
, which control the isomonodromic
deformations. The main idea of our paper is to present this passage in
a unified way. Our first attempt in this direction is described in Theorem
4.1
. The description of compatibility
functions and the corresponding Hamiltonian contained in this theorem
is unified, however it is not very transparent.
Theorem
4.2
, which can be viewed as the first part of the main
result of this paper, is more satisfactory. The method of
isomodronomic deformation is here divided into two slightly different
ansatzes, called Cases A and B. The main condition on
\sigma
,
\tau
,
\eta
,
t
for the applicability of Ansatz A is the existence of a zero of

\sigma
, so that we can write
\sigma(z)=(z-s)\rho(z)
, where
\rho
is a
polynomial of degree
\leq 2
. Under these conditions
there exists a function
t\mapsto m(t)
such that

\displaystyle H(t,\lambda,\mu)=m\big(\sigma(\lambda)\mu^{2}+(\tau(\lambda)-(\lambda-s)\rho^{\prime}(\lambda))\mu+\eta(\lambda)\big)

(1.3)

is a Painlevé Hamiltonian.

Among the conditions on

\sigma
,
\tau
,
\eta
,
t
needed for
Ansatz B the most important is
\deg\sigma\leq 2
.
Then we can show that
there exists
t\mapsto m(t)
such that

\displaystyle H(t,\lambda,\mu)=m\big(\sigma(\lambda)\mu^{2}+(\tau(\lambda)-\sigma^{\prime}(\lambda))\mu+\eta(\lambda)\big)

(1.4)

is a Painlevé Hamiltonian.

It seems impossible to implement Theorem
4.2
in a
fully unified way, and one has to subdivide Cases A and B into
several subcases. Case A splits into Subcases A1, Ap,
Aq and Case B splits into Subcases Bp and Bq.
We describe these subcases in Theorem
4.3
. The main difference
between the subcases is the choice of the time variable
t
:

In Subcase A1 the variable
t
is the position of one of Fuchsian
singularities.

In Subcases Ap and Bp the variable
t
is contained in
\tau
.

In Subcases Aq and Bq the variable
t
is contained in
\eta
.

In the following list we informally describe when we can apply various subcases.

A1.

\sigma(t)=0
,
\sigma^{\prime}(t)\neq 0
,
\deg(z-t)\eta\leq 2
.

Ap.

\sigma(s)=\sigma^{\prime}(s)=0
,
\tau(s)\neq 0
,
\sigma\eta(s)=(\sigma\eta)^{\prime}(s)=0
.

Aq.

\sigma(s)=\sigma^{\prime}(s)=0
,
\tau(s)=0
,
\sigma\eta(s)=0
,
(\sigma\eta)^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
.

Bp.

\deg\sigma\leq 2
,
\deg\tau=2
,
\deg\sigma\eta\leq 2
.

Bq.

\deg\sigma\leq 2
,
\deg\tau\leq 1
,
\deg\sigma\eta=3
.

Note that Subcase A1 works in the
standard Heun
type
(\underline{1}\underline{1}\underline{1}\underline{1})
, leading to Painlevé VI. Hence it works in
under generic conditions.
However, it does not works for some confluent types. For instance, for most degenerate
types one needs to use either Subcase Aq or Subcase Bq.
Altogether, Theorem
4.3
works for certain normal forms of all
types of Heun class equations. This
allows us to derive all types of Painlevé equations.

The derivation of the
Painlevé VI equation from the Heun equation can be traced back to a paper by Fuchs
[
6
]
from the early 20th century (written by the son of Fuchs from whose name the adjective “Fuchsian” comes). The approach was generalized to other Painlevé equations by Okamoto
[
20
]
and refined by Ohyama–Okumura
[
19
]
. A discussion of the relationship between the biconfluent Heun type and the Painlevé IV equation can be also found in
[
1
]
.
Thus derivations described in Sections
4.5
–

are known. They are in particular
described by Ohyama–Okumura
[
19
]
, where they were checked case by case.
Our approach allows us to automatize these derivations and view them as implementations of a unified algorithm.
In fact, our paper can be to some extent viewed as an explanation of the principles that underly
the results of
[
19
]
.

Slavyanov and Lay devote in
[
25
]
a whole chapter to the Heun class — Painlevé
correspondence. In particular, they stress that Painlevé
Hamiltonians can be viewed as “dequantizations” of the corresponding
Heun class equations. In fact, the symbol of Heun class operators,
that is the expression obtained from (
1.1
) by
replacing
\partial_{z}
with
\mu
and
z
with
\lambda
, is very
similar to (
1.3
) and (
1.4
). (The difference is a
“lower order term”, which can be interpreted as a result of an “ordering prescription”.) Nevertheless, to our understanding,

[
25
]
contains only a sketch of a program. The details of this
program are quite involved and
[
25
]
does not
provide its full description.

Clearly, every 2nd order scalar equation can be rewritten as a system
of two 1st order equations. For instance, we can rewrite (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle\sigma(z)\partial_{z}\begin{bmatrix}u(z)\\
v(z)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-\sigma(z)\eta(z)&-\tau(z)+\sigma^{\prime}(z)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u(z)\\
v(z)\end{bmatrix}

(or in other ways). In particular, Heun class equations are essentially equivalent to
certain systems of 1st
order equations called sometimes
Heun connections
. This suggests
a different approach to deriving Painlevé equations
starting from Heun connections.
This alternative approach was studied, e.g., by Jimbo and Miwa

[
13
,
14
]
. A recent exposition of this approach can be found in

[
11
]
.

Note that in the above references each type of Painlevé equations
were treated separately. Besides, degenerate cases
were usually left out.
It would be interesting to investigate to what extent a derivation of
all types
Painlevé equations from Heun connections can be treated in a uniform way, in the spirit of Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
.
Anyway, in this paper we do not consider this question and we stick to
(scalar 2nd order)
deformed Heun class equations.

2
Second order linear differential equations

in the complex domain

2.1
Differential equation and operator

Let us recall basic concepts of ordinary 2nd order linear
differential equations in the complex domain with holomorphic
coefficients. They have the form (
1.1
),
where
\sigma
,
\tau
and
\eta
are holomorphic functions.
We will often describe the equation (
1.1
) by specifying the corresponding operator

\displaystyle\sigma(z)\partial_{z}^{2}+\tau(z)\partial_{z}+\eta(z).

(2.1)

Clearly, by
multiplying an equation
from the left by an arbitrary nonzero holomorphic function we obtain an equivalent equation. However, we change the corresponding operator.
Speaking of operators instead of equations, which we will often do, has two advantages. First it saves a little space, since we do not need to write the function
u
.
Besides, an operator contains more information than the corresponding equation, therefore sometimes allows for making more precise statements.

Divide (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) by
\sigma(z)
and set

\displaystyle p(z):=\frac{\tau(z)}{\sigma(z)},\qquad q(z):=\frac{\eta(z)}{\sigma(z)}.

This leads to the so-called
principal form of the equation

and the corresponding
principal operator
:

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z)\big)u(z)=0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle A:=\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z).

(2.3)

The principal form is often used as the standard form.
However, we will often prefer different forms.

2.2
Singularities of functions

Let
p
be a function holomorphic on an open subset of the Riemann
sphere
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Let
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
be its singularity, so that

\displaystyle p(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}p_{z_{0},k}(z-z_{0})^{k},\qquad|z-z_{0}|<r\qquad\text{for some}\quad r>0,\quad\text{if}\quad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle p(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}p_{\infty,k}z^{k},\qquad|z|>R\qquad\text{for some}\quad R\geq 0,\quad\text{if}\quad z_{0}=\infty.

We define the
degree of the singularity
of
p
at
z_{0}
by

\displaystyle\deg(p,z_{0}):=-\min\{k\mid p_{z_{0},k}\neq 0\},\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\deg(p,\infty):=\max\{k\mid p_{\infty,k}\neq 0\},\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

(If
p
is a polynomial, then
\deg(p,\infty)=\deg(p)
is its usual degree.)

Note that if
\phi
is a biholomorphic transformation of a neighborhood of
z_{0}
onto a neighborhood of
\phi(z_{0})
, then

\displaystyle\deg(p,z_{0})=\deg\big(p\circ\phi^{-1},\phi(z_{0})\big).

2.3
Singularities of equations

Consider now the equation (
2.2
) (in the principal form) with holomorphic coefficients represented by the operator (
2.3
), which we denote by
A
.
We say that
z_{0}
is a
regular point
of
A
if

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\deg(p,z_{0})\leq 0,\qquad\deg(q,z_{0})\leq 0,\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},\\
&\deg\bigg(p-\frac{2}{z},\infty\bigg)\leq-2,\qquad\deg(q,\infty)\leq-4,\qquad z_{0}=\infty.\end{split}

Otherwise we say that it is a
singular point
of
A
.

We say that the singular point
z_{0}
is
regular
or
Fuchsian
if

\displaystyle\deg(p,z_{0})\leq 1,\qquad\deg(q,z_{0})\leq 2,\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\deg(p,\infty)\leq-1,\qquad\deg(q,\infty)\leq-2,\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

It is standard to introduce two
indices
of a Fuchsian singular point:

\displaystyle\text{the roots of}\quad\lambda(\lambda-1)+p_{z_{0},-1}\lambda+q_{z_{0},-2}\quad\text{are called\quad{indices of $z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}$},}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\text{the roots of}\quad\lambda(\lambda+1)-p_{\infty,-1}\lambda+q_{\infty,-2}\quad\text{are called\quad{indices of $z_{0}=\infty$}}.

(2.5)

The
rank of
A
at
z_{0}
is defined as follows. If
z_{0}
is a regular point, we set
{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})=0
. The case of rank equal to
\frac{1}{2}
is somewhat special:

\displaystyle{\rm rk}(A,z_{0}):=\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{if}\quad\deg\bigg(p-\frac{1}{2(z-z_{0})},z_{0}\bigg)\leq 0,\quad\deg(q,z_{0})\leq 1,\quad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle{\rm rk}(A,\infty):=\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{if}\quad\deg\bigg(p-\frac{3}{2z},\infty\bigg)\leq-2,\quad\deg(q,\infty)\leq-3,\quad z_{0}=\infty.

If
{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})\neq 0,\frac{1}{2}
, then we set

\displaystyle{\rm rk}(A,z_{0}):=\max\bigg\{\deg(p,z_{0}),\,\frac{1}{2}\deg(q,z_{0}),\,1\bigg\},\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle{\rm rk}(A,\infty):=\max\bigg\{\deg(p,\infty)+2,\,\frac{1}{2}\deg(q,\infty)+2,\,1\bigg\},\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

The rank and indices of a singularity are invariants of biholomorphic transformations. For instance,
this is the case of homographies, that is
w=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}
, or
z=\frac{dw-b}{-cw+a}
,
where
ad-bc=1
. We obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z)=(-cw+a)^{4}\partial_{w}^{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\partial_z^2+p(z)\partial_z+q(z)}{}+\!\bigg(\!{-}2c(-cw+a)^{3}\!+p\bigg(\frac{dw-b}{-cw\!+a}\bigg)(-cw\!+a)^{2}\bigg)\partial_{w}+q\bigg(\frac{dw-b}{-cw\!+a}\bigg).\!\!\!\!

(2.6)

In order to obtain the principal form we need to divide (
2.3
)
by
(-cw+a)^{4}
.

Note that the rank is always an integer or a half-integer.
A singularity of rank
m-\frac{1}{2}
with
m\in\{1,2,\dots\}
can be often treated
as a degeneration of a singularity of rank
m
. This motivates us to
introduce the
rounded rank
, denoted

\lceil{\rm rk}\rceil
:

\displaystyle\lceil{\rm rk}\rceil(A,z_{0}):=\begin{cases}{\rm rk}(A,z_{0}),&\text{if}\quad{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})\in\{0,1,2,\dots\},\\[2.15277pt]
{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})+\frac{1}{2},&\text{if}\quad{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})\in\big\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2},\dots\big\}.\end{cases}

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\lceil{\rm rk}\rceil(A,z_{0}):=\bigg\lceil\max\bigg\{\deg(p,z_{0}),\,\frac{1}{2}\deg(q,z_{0}),\,0\bigg\}\bigg\rceil,\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\lceil{\rm rk}\rceil(A,\infty):=\bigg\lceil\max\bigg\{\deg(p,\infty)+2,\,\frac{1}{2}\deg(q,\infty)+2,\,0\bigg\}\bigg\rceil,\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

Here
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is the
ceiling function
, that is

\displaystyle\lceil x\rceil:=\inf\{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\mid x\leq n\}.

The singularity
z_{0}
is Fuchsian if its rank is
\frac{1}{2}
or
1
.
Thus
z_{0}
is a Fuchsian singularity iff its rounded rank is
1
.

According to our definition, a Fuchsian singularity has rank
\frac{1}{2}
if its indices are
0
,
\frac{1}{2}
.
The splitting of the Fuchsian case into two subcases, the rank
\frac{1}{2}
and
1
, is quite useful, even if its definition is not obvious. Nevertheless, in our paper we will not make much use of this splitting and both will be usually treated as one case, denoted
\underline{1}
.

2.4
Sandwiching with functions

The family of equations (
2.2
) is preserved by several kinds of transformations of the form

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-r(z)}\big(\partial_{z}^{2}\!+p(z)\partial_{z}\!+q(z)\big){\rm e}^{r(z)}=\partial_{z}^{2}\!+\big(p(z)\!+2r^{\prime}(z)\big)\partial_{z}\!+q(z)\!+p(z)r^{\prime}(z)\!+r^{\prime}(z)^{2}\!+r^{\prime\prime}(z)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\rm e}^{-r(z)}\big( \partial_z^2\!+p(z)\partial_z\!+q(z)\big){\rm e}^{r(z)}}{}=:\partial_{z}^{2}+\tilde{p}(z)\partial_{z}+\tilde{q}(z)=\tilde{A}.

(2.7)

Sandwiching with powers.

For
r(z)=\kappa\log(z)

\displaystyle(z-z_{0})^{-\kappa}\big(\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z)\big)(z-z_{0})^{\kappa}=\partial_{z}^{2}+\big(p(z)+2\kappa(z-z_{0})^{-1}\big)\partial_{z}+q(z)

\displaystyle\hphantom{(z-z_0)^{-\kappa} \big(\partial_z^2+p(z)\partial_z+q(z)\big) (z-z_0)^{\kappa} =}{}+p(z)\kappa(z-z_{0})^{-1}+(\kappa^{2}-\kappa)(z-z_{0})^{-2}.

(2.8)

If
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
is a singularity of (
2.2
) and
{\rm rk}(z_{0})>1
, then the
transformation (
2.8
) preserves its rank.
If
z_{0}
is a Fuchsian singularity, then after the transformation it is also Fuchsian.

If
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
are the indices of (
2.2
) at
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, then

\rho_{1}+\kappa
,
\rho_{2}+\kappa
are the indices of (
2.8
) at
z_{0}
.
The same is true for
\infty
, except that the indices
of (
2.8
) at
\infty
are
\rho_{1}-\kappa
,
\rho_{2}-\kappa
.

Sandwiching with exponentials.

Let
k=2,3,\dots
. We have

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(\frac{\kappa(z-z_{0})^{-k+1}}{k-1}\bigg)\big(\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z)\big)\exp\bigg({-}\frac{\kappa(z-z_{0})^{-k+1}}{k-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\partial_{z}^{2}+\big(p(z)+2\kappa(z-z_{0})^{-k}\big)\partial_{z}+q(z)+p(z)\kappa(z-z_{0})^{-k}

\displaystyle\qquad\phantom{=}{}+\kappa^{2}(z-z_{0})^{-2k}-\kappa k(z-z_{0})^{-k-1}.

(2.9)

Hence this transformation preserves
{\rm rk}(z_{0})
if it is
>k
,
and preserves or decreases
{\rm rk}(z_{0})
if it is
=k
. The transformation does not change the coefficients
p_{z_{0},-1}
,
q_{z_{0},-1}
,
q_{z_{0},-2}
. Therefore, it also does not change the indices of
z_{0}
.

The same is true for
\infty
under the transformation

\displaystyle\exp\bigg({-}\frac{\kappa z^{k+1}}{k+1}\bigg)\big(\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z)\big)\exp\bigg(\frac{\kappa z^{k+1}}{k+1}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\partial_{z}^{2}+\big(p(z)+2\kappa z^{k}\big)\partial_{z}+q(z)+p(z)\kappa z^{k}+\kappa^{2}z^{2k}+\kappa kz^{k-1}.

(2.10)

More generally, we have transformations of the form (
2.7
),
where

\displaystyle r(z)=\sum_{k=-m+1}^{-1}\frac{w_{k}}{k}(z-z_{0})^{k}+w_{0}\log(z-z_{0}),

(2.11)

\displaystyle\text{so that}\quad r^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{k=-m+1}^{0}w_{k}(z-z_{0})^{k-1}\qquad\text{if}\quad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}};

\displaystyle r(z)=-\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\frac{w_{k}}{k}z^{k}-w_{0}\log(z),

(2.12)

\displaystyle\text{so that}\quad r^{\prime}(z)=-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}w_{k}z^{k-1}\qquad\text{if}\quad z_{0}=\infty.

We define the
absolute rank
of
A
at
z_{0}
as

\displaystyle{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0}):=\inf\big\{{\rm rk}(\tilde{A},z_{0})\big\},

where
\tilde{A}
are all possible transforms of
A
of the form (
2.7
) with
r
as in (
2.11
) or (
2.12
).

2.5
Half-integer rank

In this subsection we discuss singular points with a half-integer rank. They are in a sense exceptional and have special properties.

Suppose that the equation (
2.2
) has a singular point at
z_{0}
and
{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})=m+\frac{1}{2}
, where
m=0,1,\dots
. It is easy to see that this implies

\displaystyle{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})={\rm rk}(A,z_{0}).

Without loss of generality we can assume that
the singularity is at
0
. This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\deg(p,0)\leq m,\qquad\deg(q,0)=2m+1,\qquad m\geq 1,

\displaystyle\deg\bigg(p-\frac{1}{2z},0\bigg)\leq 0,\qquad\deg(q,0)\leq 1,\qquad m=0.

Let us make the substitution

\displaystyle z=y^{2},\qquad y=\sqrt{z}.

(2.13)

Using
\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2y}\partial_{y}
we transform (
2.3
) into

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4y^{2}}\partial_{y}^{2}-\frac{1}{4y^{3}}\partial_{y}+\frac{p(y^{2})}{2y}\partial_{y}+q\big(y^{2}\big).

(2.14)

Multiplying (
2.14
) by
4y^{2}
we obtain
an equation in the principal form

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{2}+2y\bigg(p\big(y^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2y^{2}}\bigg)\partial_{y}+4y^{2}q\big(y^{2}\big).

(2.15)

Now

\displaystyle\deg\bigg(2y\bigg(p\big(y^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2y^{2}}\bigg),0\bigg)\leq 2m-1,

\displaystyle\deg\big(4y^{2}q\big(y^{2}\big),0\big)=2(2m+1)-2=4m,\qquad m\geq 1,

\displaystyle\deg\big(4y^{2}q\big(y^{2}\big),0\big)\leq 0,\qquad m=0.

Thus the rank of (
2.15
) at zero is
2m
.

Thus we have shown that by a quadratic substitution
we can reduce a singularity of a half-integer rank to a singularity of integer rank. The resulting equation (
2.15
) is in addition invariant with respect to the substitution
y\to-y
and the rank of the singularity is even.

Note that our definition of rank
\frac{1}{2}
has been chosen so that
the above quadratic reduction works for all half-integer ranks.

2.6
Simplifying the equation

Suppose that the equation (
2.2
) has a singular point at
z_{0}
.
Obviously, we have 3 exclusive possibilities:

(0)

{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})\leq 1
;

(1)

{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
is an integer and
\geq 2
;

(2)

{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
is a half-integer and
\geq\frac{3}{2}
.

We would like to simplify the equation around this singularity by
sandwiching with
{\rm e}^{r}
, where
r
is given by (
2.11
) or (
2.12
). The transformed operator will be, as usual, denoted
\tilde{A}
.
We will see that the simplification will be quite different
depending on Case (1) and (2). (Case (0) is simple enough, therefore we do not discuss it in the following proposition).

Proposition 2.1
.

(1)

If
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
is an integer and
\geq 2
, then there exist exactly two transformations
such that

\displaystyle{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})={\rm rk}(\tilde{A},z_{0})=\deg(\tilde{p},z_{0})\geq\deg(\tilde{q},z_{0}),\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle{\rm Rk}(A,\infty)={\rm rk}(\tilde{A},\infty)=\deg(\tilde{p},\infty)+2\geq\deg(\tilde{q},\infty)+4,\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

(2.16)

(2)

If
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
is a half-integer and
\geq\frac{3}{2}
, then there exists a unique transformation such that

\displaystyle\deg(\tilde{p},z_{0})\leq 0\quad\ \text{and}\quad\ {\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})={\rm rk}(\tilde{A},z_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\deg(\tilde{q},z_{0}),\quad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\deg(\tilde{p},\infty)\leq-2\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm Rk}(A,\infty)={\rm rk}(\tilde{A},\infty)=\frac{1}{2}\deg(\tilde{q},\infty)+2,\quad z_{0}=\infty.

(2.17)

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
the singularity is at
0
.
We will use the identity

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(z)=\!\!\sum_{n=-2m}^{\infty}z^{n}\Bigg(\sum_{k=\max(-m,n+1)}^{\min(-1,n+m)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!w_{k+1}w_{n-k+1}+(n+1)w_{n+2}+\!\!\!\sum_{k=-m}^{\min(-1,n+m)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!w_{k+1}p_{n-k}+q_{n}\Bigg).

(2.18)

We will apply one of the following three transformations, denoted I, II and III.

Transformation I.
Suppose that the rank of the initial equation is
m-\frac{1}{2}
,
m=2,3,\dots
.
Then
\deg(q,0)=2m-1
and

\displaystyle p(z)=\sum_{j=-m+1}^{\infty}p_{j}z^{j}.\vskip-4.30554pt

We choose
r(z)
such that

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=-m+1}^{-1}p_{j}z^{j}.\vskip-4.30554pt

Then
\deg(\tilde{p},0)\leq 0
and
\deg(\tilde{q},0)=\deg(q,0)
.
The transformed equation satisfies (
2.17
).

For transformations II and III we suppose that the
rank of the initial equation is
m=2,3,\dots
.

Transformation II.
Assume that

\displaystyle p_{-m}^{2}\neq 4q_{-2m}.

Let
w_{-m+1}
be one of two solutions of

\displaystyle 0=w_{-m+1}^{2}+w_{-m+1}p_{-m}+q_{-2m}.

(2.19)

Then
\tilde{q}_{-2m}=0
.
Equating
\tilde{q}_{-m+j}=0
for
j=-m+1,\dots,-1
we obtain from (
2.18
)
the recurrence relations

\displaystyle 0=w_{j+1}(2w_{-m+1}+p_{-m})

\displaystyle\hphantom{0=}{}+\sum_{k=-m+1}^{j-1}w_{k+1}w_{-m+j-k+1}+(-m+j+1)w_{-m+j+2}+\sum_{k=-m}^{j-1}w_{k+1}p_{-m+j-k}+q_{-m+j}.

Using

\displaystyle 2w_{-m+1}+p_{-m}=\sqrt{p_{-m}^{2}-4q_{-2m}}\neq 0

we can solve the recurrence relations. The transformed equation
satisfies (
2.16
).

Transformation III.
If

\displaystyle p_{-m}^{2}=4q_{-2m},

then we sandwich with
{\rm e}^{r}
, where
r(z)=\frac{p_{-m}z^{-m+1}}{2(-m+1)}
. The transformed operator
\tilde{A}
has
\deg(\tilde{p},0)\leq m-1
and
\deg(\tilde{q},0)\leq 2m-1
. Thus after the transformation

{\rm rk}\big(0,\tilde{A}\big)\leq m-\frac{1}{2}
. If the resulting rank is half-integer, then we apply I and stop. If the resulting rank is an integer, we apply II and stop, or III and we iterate.

We have thus two possibilities:

Case
(1)

First a finite number of III, and then II. At the end we obtain equation satisfying (
2.16
). Steps III are uniquely determined and II has two possibilities.

Case
(2)

First a finite number of III, and then I obtaining (
2.17
). All steps are uniquely determined.
∎

We will say that the operator
A
has a
grounded form at
z_{0}

if
\deg(p,z_{0})\geq\deg(q,z_{0})
.
If
z_{0}
is Fuchsian, then this is equivalent to one of the indices being
0
.

It follows from Proposition
2.1
that if
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
is an integer or
\frac{1}{2}
, then
the equation can be brought to a grounded form at
z_{0}
.

2.7
Solutions in terms of formal power series

We consider the equation (
2.2
) and try to solve it
in terms of a nontrivial, not necessarily convergent power series

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{k}z^{k}.

As we will see, this is not always possible.

Proposition 2.2
.

Set
m:=\deg(p,0)
and
l:=\deg(q,0)
.

1.

If
m\leq 0
and
l\leq 0
, then for any
v_{0}
,
v_{1}
there exists a power series solution.

2.

If
m\leq 1
and
l\leq 2
, then there are no power series solutions unless for some
n=1,2,\dots
we have

\displaystyle n(n-1)+np_{-1}+q_{-2}=0.

(2.20)

3.

If
m\geq 2
and
l\leq m
, then for any
v_{0}
there is a unique power series solution.

4.

If
m\geq 2
and
l=m+1
, then there is no power series solution unless for some
n=1,2,\dots

\displaystyle p_{-m}n+q_{-m-1}=0.

5.

If
m\geq 1
and
l\geq m+2
, then there are no power series solutions.

Proof.

By equating
the terms at
z^{n}
to zero in

\displaystyle\bigg(\partial_{z}^{2}+\sum_{j=-m}^{\infty}p_{j}z^{j}\partial_{z}+\sum_{j=-l}^{\infty}q_{j}z^{j}\bigg)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v_{k}z^{k}=0

we obtain the following equations:

\displaystyle(n+2)(n+1)v_{n+2}+\sum_{k=1}^{n+1+m}p_{n+1-k}kv_{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n+l}q_{n-k}v_{k}=0,\qquad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Let us prove (5). For
j:=n+l=0,\dots,l-m-1
we obtain the equations

\displaystyle 0=q_{-l}v_{0},

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots

\displaystyle 0=q_{-l}v_{j}+\cdots+q_{-l+j}v_{0},

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots

\displaystyle 0=q_{-l}v_{l-m-1}+\cdots+q_{-m-1}v_{0}.

Thus
0=v_{0}=\cdots=v_{l-m-1}
.

If
j\geq l-m
, there are additional terms coming from the 1st order derivative.
v_{k}
with the highest
k
contained in such a term has
k=m-l+j+1
. But because of
l\geq m+2
we have
k\leq j-1
. Thus
v_{k}=0
by one of previous recursion steps.

If
j\geq l
, there is an additional term coming from the 2nd order derivative, involving
v_{k}
with
k=-l+j+2
. But
l\geq 3
implies
k\leq j-1
.
Again, this
v_{k}=0
by one of previous recursion steps.

Let us prove (4). We have recursion relations

\displaystyle 0=q_{-l}v_{0},

\displaystyle 0=(p_{-l+1}+q_{-l})v_{1}+q_{-l+1}v_{0},

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots

\displaystyle 0=(jp_{-l+1}+q_{-l})v_{j}+\cdots,

where dots denote terms depending on
v_{j-1},\dots,v_{0}
.
If (
2.20
) has no solutions, then we can solve the recurrence obtaining

0=v_{0}=v_{1}=\cdots
.

Let us prove (3). We have the recursion relations

\displaystyle 0=p_{-m}v_{1}+q_{-l}v_{0},

\displaystyle 0=p_{-m}2v_{2}+p_{-m+1}v_{1}+q_{-m}v_{1}+q_{-m+1}v_{0},

(2.21)

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots

\displaystyle 0=jp_{-m}v_{j}+\cdots,

where
\cdots
involve
v_{0},\dots,v_{j-1}
.
If
m=2
, there is an additional term
2v_{0}
in (
2.21
).
Note that
p_{-m}\neq 0
.
Hence, for any
v_{0}
we can solve the recurrence obtaining
v_{1},v_{2},\dots
.

(2) follows immediately from the well-known theory of solutions around a Fuchsian singular point.

(1) is the well-known fact about the Cauchy problem in the regular case. ∎

2.8
Thomé solutions

By the so-called Frobenius method, if
z_{0}
is a Fuchsian singularity
and
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are its indices such that

\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\not\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then solutions of the equation (
2.2
)
are spanned by two convergent power series indexed by
i=1,2
with
v_{i,0}\neq 0
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{i,j}(z-z_{0})^{j+\rho_{i}},\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-\infty}^{0}v_{i,j}z^{j-\rho_{i}},\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

If
\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
this is not always true. One can then assume that
\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\geq 0
. There exists one solution as above with
i=1

and the second has the form

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{2,j}(z-z_{0})^{\rho_{2}+j}+\log z\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{1,j}(z-z_{0})^{\rho_{1}+j},\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\sum_{j=-\infty}^{0}v_{2,j}z^{-\rho_{2}+j}+\log z\sum_{j=-\infty}^{0}v_{1,j}z^{-\rho_{1}+j},\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

If the singular point is not Fuchsian, then we can also look for solutions in a similar form, however the resulting power series are usually no longer convergent. One obtains the so-called
Thomé solutions
. Note that in some way the situation is simpler, because we do not have the logarithmic case. On the other hand, half-integer ranks need to be treated separately and lead to power series in
\sqrt{z}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
z_{0}
be a singular point of
A
with
{\rm rk}(A,z_{0})=n
.
Then there exist two formal solutions of
A
, indexed by
i=1,2
.

1.

If
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
is an integer
\geq 2
,
they have the form

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg(\sum_{k=-n+1}^{-1}\frac{(z-z_{0})^{k}}{k}w_{i,k}\Bigg)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{i,j}(z-z_{0})^{w_{i,0}+j},\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg({-}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{z^{k}}{k}w_{i,k}\Bigg)\sum_{j=-\infty}^{0}v_{i,j}z^{-w_{i,0}+j},\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

2.

Let
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})
be a half integer
\geq\frac{3}{2}
.
The two formal solutions have the form

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg(\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{k=-n+1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{(z-z_{0})^{k}}{k}w_{i,k}\Bigg)\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{j=0}^{\infty}v_{i,j}(z-z_{0})^{w_{i,0}+j},\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg({-}\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{k=\frac{1}{2}}^{n-1}\frac{z^{k}}{k}w_{i,k}\Bigg)\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{j=-\infty}^{0}v_{i,j}z^{-w_{i,0}+j},\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

Here
\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}
denotes the sum where the index
k
within its range runs over
both integers and half-integers. We have

w_{0,k}=(-1)^{2k}w_{1,k},\qquad v_{0,k}=(-1)^{2k}v_{1,k}.

(2.22)

Proof.

For simplicity, assume that
z_{0}=0
.

Suppose that
{\rm Rk}(A,0)
is an integer
\geq 2
.
By Proposition
2.1
(1) we can transform the equation to
a grounded form in two distinct ways. By Proposition
2.2
(3), the grounded form has a solution in terms of the power series.

If
{\rm Rk}(A,0)=m+\frac{1}{2}
is a half-integer
\geq\frac{3}{2}
, first we reduce the equation to
the form with a half-integer rank, see Proposition
2.1
(2). Then we apply the quadratic transformation, as described in Section
2.5
.
We obtain
an even equation in
\sqrt{z}
of the rank
2m
, with
\tilde{p}_{-2m}=0
and
\tilde{q}_{-4m}\neq 0
.
We already know that it has a solution of the form described in 1:

\displaystyle\exp\Bigg(\sum_{k=-2m+1}^{-1}\frac{\big(\sqrt{z}\big)^{k}}{k}\tilde{w}_{i,k}\Bigg)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\tilde{v}_{j}\big(\sqrt{z}\big)^{\tilde{w}_{i,0}+j}.

(2.23)

Using
2m\geq 2
and (
2.19
) we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{-2m+1}=\sqrt{-\tilde{q}_{-4m}}\neq 0.

(2.24)

Clearly, (
2.23
) with
\sqrt{z}
replaced by
-\sqrt{z}
is also a solution. By (
2.24
) both solutions are not proportional to one another. This proves (
2.3
).
∎

Note that Proposition
2.3
is also true in the Fuchsian case,
except that for
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})=1
, one has to make an obvious modification
in the logarithmic case, and for
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})=\frac{1}{2}
(
2.3
) does not have to be true.

In the Fuchsian case
w_{i,0}
,
i=1,2
, coincide with the indices of
z_{0}
.
In what follows, the numbers
w_{i,0}
,
i=1,2
, will be called
indices of
z_{0}
in the general case as well. We also introduce the alternative notation

\displaystyle\rho_{z_{0},i}:=w_{i,0},\qquad i=1,2.

(2.25)

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
z_{0}
be a singularity of
A
.

1.

We have

\displaystyle\rho_{z_{0},1}+\rho_{z_{0},2}=-p_{z_{0},-1}+{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0}),\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

(2.26)

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty,1}+\rho_{\infty,2}=p_{\infty,-1}-2+{\rm Rk}(A,\infty),\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

(2.27)

2.

If
{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})\in\big\{\frac{3}{2},\frac{5}{2},\dots\big\}
, then

\displaystyle\rho_{z_{0},1}=\rho_{z_{0},2}=\frac{1}{2}\big({-}p_{z_{0},-1}+{\rm Rk}(A,z_{0})\big),\qquad z_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}},

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty,1}=\rho_{\infty,2}=\frac{1}{2}\big(p_{\infty,-1}-2+{\rm Rk}(A,\infty)\big),\qquad z_{0}=\infty.

3.

If

2.9
Nonlogarithmic singularities

Let

be a singular point of the equation (

is a nonlogarithmic Fuchsian singular point, then both its indices are integers.

The following proposition shows how to deform a given equation
so that one obtains an additional nonlogarithmic singularity with indices
0,2
.
This deformation depends on two parameters
\lambda

\mu

additional singularity is located at
\lambda
,
and solutions of the deformed equation satisfy
\mu v(\lambda)=v^{\prime}(\lambda)
.
It will play the central role in the derivation of Painlevé equations from Heun class equations in Section
4
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\sigma
,
\tau
,
\eta
be analytic at
\lambda
and
\sigma(\lambda)\neq 0
. Let
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
Then all solution of the equation given by

\displaystyle\sigma(z)\partial_{z}^{2}+\bigg(\tau(z)-\frac{\sigma(z)}{z-\lambda}\bigg)\partial_{z}+\eta(z)-\eta(\lambda)-\mu^{2}\sigma(\lambda)-\mu\big(\tau(\lambda)-\sigma^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)+\frac{\mu\sigma(\lambda)}{z-\lambda}

\lambda
. Thus the equation given by (

)
has a nonlogarithmic singularity at

. The singularity is Fuchsian with indices

,
2
.

Proof.

We look for a solution analytic around
\lambda
:

\displaystyle v(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}v_{n}(z-\lambda)^{n}.

We obtain

\displaystyle\big({-}\sigma(\lambda)v_{1}+\mu\sigma(\lambda)v_{0}\big)(z-\lambda)^{0}+\big(\sigma(\lambda)2v_{2}+\big(\tau(\lambda)-\sigma^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)v_{1}-\sigma(\lambda)2v_{2}-\big(\mu\tau(\lambda)

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\mu\sigma^{\prime}(\lambda)+\mu^{2}\sigma(\lambda)\big)v_{0}+\mu\sigma(\lambda)v_{1}\big)(z-\lambda)^{1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\big(\sigma(\lambda)n(n-2)v_{n}+\cdots\big)(z-\lambda)^{n-1}=0.

We have

3
Equations with rational coefficients

3.1

\boldsymbol{M_{n}}

\displaystyle A:=\partial_{z}^{2}+p(z)\partial_{z}+q(z),

Each finite singularity has at least one index equal

Thus such equations are given by operators

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{a_{j}}{z-z_{j}}\partial_{z}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{b_{j}}{z-z_{j}}\qquad\text{with}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}=0.

type

Proposition 3.1
.

By sandwiching with powers, as in (

type equation into a grounded
M_{n}
type equation.

We say that a differential equation belongs to the

class

We say that a differential equation belongs to the

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{2}+\frac{\tau(z)}{\sigma(z)}\partial_{z}+\frac{\eta(z)}{\sigma(z)},

1.

3.2
Generalized Fuchs relation

Recall that for any singular point
z_{0}
of an equation
A
we defined
its two indices
\rho_{z_{0},1}
and
\rho_{z_{0},2}
. For Fuchsian singularities they were defined in (

), (
2.5
) and for non-Fuchsian singularities in (

). If all singularities are regular then the well-known Fuchs relation says that the sum of all indices equals the number of singularities minus

. In the following proposition we describe its generalization which is valid if some of
the singularities are non-Fuchsian.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{k}
be the singular points of an equation
A
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\rho_{z_{j},1}+\rho_{z_{j},2})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\rm Rk}(A,z_{j})-2.

(3.11)

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that

.
We have

\displaystyle\rho_{z_{i},1}+\rho_{z_{i},2}=-p_{z_{j},-1}+{\rm Rk}(A,z_{j}),\qquad j=1,\dots,k-1,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty,1}+\rho_{\infty,2}=p_{\infty,-1}-2+{\rm Rk}(A,\infty),

(3.13)

\displaystyle p_{\infty,-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}p_{z_{j},-1},

where (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) follows from Proposition
2.4
.
Summing up the above three relations we obtain (
3.11
).
∎

3.4

type equations were studied by Heun in

]

.

.

Our terminology is consistent with

]

is used to denote what we call the

We will represent Heun class equations by Heun class operators. More precisely,

\displaystyle\sigma(z)\partial_{z}^{2}+\tau(z)\partial_{z}+\eta(z)

\displaystyle\sigma\neq 0,\qquad\deg\sigma\leq 3,\qquad\deg\tau\leq 2,\qquad\deg\xi\leq 4.

,

,

\displaystyle\sigma\neq 0,\qquad\deg\sigma\leq 3,\qquad\deg\tau\leq 2,\qquad\deg\eta\leq 1.

The Heun class is also preserved by sandwiching with powers and exponentials, see
(

), (

) and (

\displaystyle\tilde{\tau}(w):=w^{2}\big(2\rho\big(w^{-1}\big)-\tau\big(w^{-1}\big)\big),

(3.17b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}(w):=\eta\big(w^{-1}\big)

1.

4.1
Method of isomonodromic deformations

Let us review the theory of isomonodromic deformations of linear second order differential equations following
[
12
,
19
,
20
]
. We shall use a notation similar to
[

Let

,

be rational functions of

Among these parameters we single out a parameter

We will write

for

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v

Consider a family of linear second order differential equations of the form

\displaystyle v^{\prime\prime}(z)+p(z)v^{\prime}(z)+q(z)v(z)=0.

(4.1)

We assume that when we deform the equation (
4.1
), we can also deform its certain solution

so that the following condition is satisfied:

\displaystyle\dot{v}=a(z,t)v^{\prime}+b(z,t)v.

(4.2)

This essentially means that when we deform the equation, its solutions
“live” on the same Riemann surface. In particular, if there are singularities, then one should expect that the monodromy of solutions stays the same.

The compatibility of (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) imposes a strong condition on the deformation. Indeed, differentiating (
4.2
) in
z
we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{v}^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}-ap+b)v^{\prime}+(-aq+b^{\prime})v.

Differentiating (
4.1
) once in

and (
4.2
) twice in

\displaystyle\dot{v}^{\prime\prime}=\big({-}\dot{p}-pa^{\prime}+ap^{2}-pb-qa\big)v^{\prime}+\big(paq-pb^{\prime}-\dot{q}-qb)v,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\dot{v}^{\prime\prime}=\big({-}2qa^{\prime}-aq^{\prime}+b^{\prime\prime}+apq-bq\big)v+\big(a^{\prime\prime}-2pa^{\prime}-ap^{\prime}+2b^{\prime}-aq+ap^{2}-bp)v^{\prime}.

(4.4)

Equating (
4.4
) and (
4.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{p}-ap^{\prime}+2b^{\prime}-pa^{\prime}+a^{\prime\prime}=0,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\dot{q}+pb^{\prime}-2qa^{\prime}-aq^{\prime}+b^{\prime\prime}=0.

(4.6)

When applying this method to a concrete family of equations one needs to divide its parameters into two categories. The first category should contain all parameters responsible for the monodromy around singular points. For example, the coefficients
p_{-1}
and
q_{-2}
of the Laurent series of
p
, resp.

around singular points.
In the second category we have parameters that do not influence the monodromy, typically denoted
\mu
,
\lambda
,
t

t
is called the “time variable”.

In what follows we discuss this classification.
We describe the minimal set of parameters that can be used in a given type and various equivalences. In our discussion we
try to include the Hamiltonian aspect, whenever it is possible.

The above classification of Painlevé equation was pointed out by Ohyama–Okumura, see the beginning of Section 2 of
[
19

(In that reference the authors use the word “type” both for what we call “supertype” and “type”.)
The discussion in
[
19
]

the
Hamiltonian form of the five supertypes.

The first supertype is Painlevé VI or
(\underline{1}\underline{1}\underline{1}\underline{1})
, which contains only one type. All the four remaining supertypes contain at least two types. We discuss them in the following subsections.

In each of the following subsections we start with a general form of
the given supertype of the Painlevé equation. It will be
indicated by
\diamond
. It always has the form

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\lambda}{{\rm d}t^{2}}=F\bigg(t,\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}t},\lambda\bigg).

The corresponding differential (nonlinear) operator will be denoted

\displaystyle P(t,\lambda):=\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\lambda}{{\rm d}t^{2}}-F\bigg(t,\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}t},\lambda\bigg).

We also introduce the corresponding Hamiltonian
H(t,\lambda,\mu).

Both
P(t,\lambda)
and
H(t,\lambda,\mu)
depend on several parameters, put as subscripts.
Next we give the scaling properties of the equation and the Hamiltonian.

Then we list various nontrivial types that belong to a given
supertype, marking them with
\ast
.

Finally, we discuss the relationship between various types. In particular, show how to reduce the number of parameters using scaling.

Each supertype contains one generic type, which we call non-degenerate.
Besides, it may contain one or more degenerate types. The Hamiltonian that covers the non-degenerate type, does not always allow us to describe all
types that belong to a given supertype.
This can be viewed as a drawback of the Hamiltonian approach.

Appendix B
Hamilton equations

B.1
From Hamilton equations to second order equations

We devote this appendix
to a few remarks about Hamilton equations.

Suppose that
H(t,\lambda,\mu)
is a time-dependent Hamiltonian. The equations

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mu}(t,\lambda,\mu),

(B.1)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\mu}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}(t,\lambda,\mu),

(B.2)

are called the Hamilton equations generated by
H
.

All Painlevé Hamiltonians have the form

\displaystyle H(t,\lambda,\mu)=f(t,\lambda)\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}+\mu g(t,\lambda)+h(t,\lambda).

For such Hamiltonians it is easy to eliminate

\mu
from the Hamilton equations. One obtains a second order differential equation for
\lambda
of the form

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\lambda}{{\rm d}t^{2}}=A(t,\lambda)\bigg(\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}t}\bigg)^{2}+B(t,\lambda)\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}t}+C(t,\lambda),

\displaystyle A:=\frac{1}{2f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda},\qquad B:=\frac{1}{f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t},\qquad C:=-\frac{g^{2}}{2f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda}-\frac{g}{f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+g\frac{\partial g}{\partial\lambda}+\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}-f\frac{\partial h}{\partial\lambda}.

B.2
Invariance of Hamilton equations

The Hamilton equations are invariant with respect to various transformations.

•

The equations generated by
\epsilon H(\epsilon t,\lambda,\mu)
are equivalent to (
B.1
) and (
B.2
).

•

Let
(\lambda,\mu)\mapsto\big(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mu}\big)
be a (time-independent) canonical transformation, that means

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{\lambda}}{\partial\lambda}\frac{\partial\tilde{\mu}}{\partial\mu}-\frac{\partial\tilde{\lambda}}{\partial\mu}\frac{\partial\tilde{\mu}}{\partial\lambda}=1.

Then the Hamilton equations in the new variables

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\tilde{\lambda}}{{\rm d}t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\tilde{\mu}},\qquad\frac{{\rm d}\tilde{\mu}}{{\rm d}t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\tilde{\lambda}},

are equivalent to (
B.2
).

•

The Hamilton equations are invariant with respect to the following time-dependent transformation:

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}=t^{-1}\lambda,\qquad\tilde{\mu}=t\mu,\qquad\tilde{H}=H-t^{-1}\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{\mu}.

B.3
Hamiltonian solvable in quadratures

Let us now consider a Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(t,\lambda,\mu)=m(t)\bigg(f(\lambda)\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}+\mu g(\lambda)+h(\lambda)\bigg).

(B.3)

We will show that it is solvable in quadratures.

First we change the time from
t
to
s
, by solving

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}s}{{\rm d}t}=m(t).

Using the time
s
we can replace (
B.3
) by the time-independent Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H(\lambda,\mu)=f(\lambda)\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}+\mu g(\lambda)+h(\lambda).

Now

\displaystyle E:=f(\lambda)\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}+\mu g(\lambda)+h(\lambda)

is a constant of motion, hence we can express
\mu
in terms of
\lambda
:

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{-g(\lambda)\pm\sqrt{g(\lambda)^{2}-2f(\lambda)(h(\lambda)-E)}}{f(\lambda)}.

We insert this into the first Hamilton equation

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}s}=\mu f(\lambda)+g(\lambda)

obtaining

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\lambda}{{\rm d}s}=\pm\sqrt{g(\lambda)^{2}-2f(\lambda)(h(\lambda)-E)}.

This is clearly solvable in quadratures.
