From Control to Mathematics–Part I: Controllability-Based Design for Iterative Methods in Solving Linear Equations
Deyuan Meng, Senior Member, IEEE, Yuxin Wu

Abstract
In the interaction between control and mathematics, mathematical tools are fundamental for all the control methods, but it is unclear how control impacts mathematics. This is the first part of our paper that attempts to give an answer with focus on solving linear algebraic equations (LAEs) from the perspective of systems and control, where it mainly introduces the controllability-based design results. By proposing an iterative method that integrates a learning control mechanism, a class of tracking problems for iterative learning control (ILC) is explored for the problem solving of LAEs. A trackability property of ILC is newly developed, by which analysis and synthesis results are established to disclose the equivalence between the solvability of LAEs and the controllability of discrete control systems. Hence, LAEs can be solved by equivalently achieving the perfect tracking tasks of resulting ILC systems via the classic state feedback-based design and analysis methods. It is shown that the solutions for any solvable LAE can all be calculated with different selections of the initial input. Moreover, the presented ILC method is applicable to determining all the least squares solutions of any unsolvable LAE. In particular, a deadbeat design is incorporated to ILC such that the solving of LAEs can be completed within finite iteration steps. The trackability property is also generalized to conventional two-dimensional ILC systems, which creates feedback-based methods, instead of the common used contraction mapping-based methods, for the design and convergence analysis of ILC.



I
Introduction

Solving linear algebraic equations (LAEs) has been one of the most fundamentally significant problems in science and engineering
[
1
,
2
]
. It has caught attention in lots of areas since many practical and complex problems can ultimately boil down to the solving of LAEs, such as forecast, estimation, and approximation of nonlinear systems and modelling of physical systems. In general, there are two basic categories of methods for solving LAEs, that is, direct methods and iterative methods. The direct methods are traditionally adopted for solving LAEs, which leverage the Gaussian elimination and its enhancements to directly calculate the exact solutions of LAEs with a process of finite steps. Though the direct methods can lead to the exact solutions of LAEs, it is lack of robustness and requires a large amount of computation and storage, especially for LAEs with higher dimensions. The iterative methods mainly make use of the successive iterations to update the approximate solutions of LAEs such that the exact solutions of them can be determined when the convergence of iterative process is achieved. Thanks to that the iterative methods only need to perform the addition and multiplication operations over a few matrices and vectors, they are generally preferred in practical applications
[
4
,
3
]
.

The classic iterative methods for solving LAEs make certain use of the feedback mechanism. However, they ignore the use of “control design” in feedback, due to which the convergence rate, or even the convergence, for them may not be guaranteed. What will emerge if we can incorporate the idea of control into the iterative methods, and can this make them more effective in solving LAEs? If the answers to them are affirmative, how can we reasonably integrate control into the iterative methods? For this key problem, a promising control-theoretic design method is explored in
[
5
,
6
]
, which not only presents continuous-time and discrete-time algorithms but also is effective, regardless of linear or nonlinear LAEs. In
[

]
, the design methods from optimal control and robust control are successfully introduced to solve LAEs, which yield solution algorithms with the global convergence or tunable convergence properties. Recently, there have been reported insightful consensus-based network control methods for solving LAEs, together with providing distributed algorithms based on local information exchange (see, e.g.,
[

,

). It has been revealed in

However, the use of control methods to better performances of iterative methods is less developed especially in comparison with the popular application of iterative methods in the control area (see also
[

It is required to further study whether and how the control design ideas can be leveraged to determine all solutions (or least squares solutions) for solvable (or unsolvable) LAEs. More fundamentally, are there inherent relationships between the solvability or unsolvability problems for LAEs and the basic properties for control systems, such as controllability, reachability, or stabilizability? Furthermore, in case of a positive answer to this question, how can the inherent relationships be reasonably disclosed? To our knowledge, these problems still remain open in the interaction between iterative methods for solving LAEs and control design methods, which will be addressed in this paper. Our approach is to incorporate the strategies of iterative learning control (ILC) in establishing effective solving methods for LAEs, in which a salient idea of learning from experience can be leveraged to connect iterative methods with control systems.

As a class of intelligent control methods, ILC contributes to realizing the “perfect output tracking” of any desired reference, which is accomplished by learning information from previous iterations and iteratively updating the control input signal (see, e.g.,
[
14

15

Once the convergence for the iterative process is ensured, together with decreasing the tracking error to zero, the output tracking objective for any specified ILC system can be achieved perfectly over a fixed interval from the beginning to the end. It has been reported in the literature (see, e.g.,
[

Simultaneously, the update of the control input for ILC arrives at the approximation to the desired input for the desired reference, which is actually consistent with the exploration of iterative methods in realizing the approximation to the solution for an LAE. This consistency result brings the possibility to solve LAEs from the perspective of control analysis and synthesis based on constructing certain ILC systems, which however has not been investigated in the literature to the best of our knowledge.

With appropriate design of iterative learning controllers, the convergence performance of iterative processes may be greatly enhanced. The design and analysis of ILC mainly resorts to the contraction mapping method, rendering ILC distinct especially from the popular feedback-based control methods and separate from them simultaneously, e.g., see

It is known that the controllability is considered as a fundamentally significant property in most popular control methods, whereas it is seldom used in ILC, and instead, the system relative degree is regarded as one of the essential conditions in ILC. Are basic properties, such as reachability, controllability, or stabilizability, of control systems really not significant for ILC? The answer is negative. For the design and analysis of ILC, a realizability hypothesis is usually imposed, which embeds the controllability information of ILC systems (see, e.g.,
[
28

In this paper, we provide a viewpoint of systems and control to solve LAEs by constructing a framework of ILC, for which a fundamental trackability property of ILC is newly introduced by requiring only the existence of some desired inputs for ILC systems in generating the desired reference. It helps to develop the equivalent relation between the solvability of LAEs and the controllability of control systems, as shown in Fig.

In addition, we explore the trackability property for traditional two-dimensional (2-D) ILC systems, and thus can benefit from the controllability and state feedback-based design methods to implement the perfect tracking tasks of ILC. Specifically, three main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

1.

We can incorporate the state feedback-based control into the solving of LAEs. It is thanks to the transformation of this problem to a tracking problem of ILC, which can be further disclosed with a connection to the controllability property. Thus, we can give an algorithm to determine all solutions (respectively, all least squares solutions) of any solvable (respectively, unsolvable) LAE by the selections of different initial inputs. By comparison, we may extend the existing control-theoretic iterative methods given in, e.g.,

2.

We propose a design method to improve the convergence rate of the iterative processes that result from the solving of LAEs. By integrating the idea of deadbeat control into the design of ILC, we can enable the convergence of the iterative processes to be accomplished in finite iteration steps. This not only overcomes the shortcoming of slow convergence in the classic iterative methods for solving LAEs, but also preserves their advantages of robustness and simple calculation. It thus renders iterative methods more practical for determining the solutions to LAEs.

We disclose that the realizability hypothesis, required in many ILC literature (see, e.g.,

We also extend the trackability property to the traditional 2-D ILC systems, and hence the state feedback-based design and analysis methods are effective in implementing the perfect output tracking tasks for them, which narrows the gap between ILC and the classic feedback-based control methods.

II
Problem-Solving of LAEs Via ILC Tracking

Let any desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be specified. The problem of interest in this paper is to solve an LAE expressed by

\bm{Y}_{d}=\bm{G}\bm{U}_{d}

(1)

to obtain the solution
\bm{U}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, where
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
is the transfer or mapping matrix. If the LAE (
1
) has solutions, then it is said to be
solvable
. Generally, there exist multiple solutions for the LAE (
1
) when it is solvable. We thus need to not only calculate a certain solution but also determine an analytical formulation of the multiple solutions for the LAE (
1
).

We target at addressing the solving problem of the LAE (
1
) from the perspective of ILC. Toward this end, we duplicate the LAE (
1
) in an iterative manner to arrive at a process, described with respect to the iteration index
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, as

\bm{Y}_{k}=\bm{G}\bm{U}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(2)

where we call
\bm{Y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\bm{U}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
the output and the input of (
2
), respectively. Since (
2
) can be employed for the description of an ILC system implicitly involving an independent discrete-time dynamics (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
15
,
34
]
for more discussions), it is also directly called a system for clarity, in accordance with which
\bm{U}_{d}
is called the desired input for generating the desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}
.

With the construction of the system (
2
), the solving problem of the LAE (
1
) can be interpreted as an ILC tracking problem.

Problem Statement:
For the problem-solving of the LAE (
1
), our objective is to design an input sequence
\left\{\bm{U}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\right\}
for the system (
2
), with any initial input
\bm{U}_{0}
that can be arbitrarily selected, such that the resulting output sequence
\left\{\bm{Y}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\right\}
can approach the desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}
as
k\to\infty
, namely,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{Y}_{k}=\bm{Y}_{d}

(3)

and we can also learn a desired input
\bm{U}_{d}
through the design of
\bm{U}_{k}
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and identify the unified properties of all possible desired inputs simultaneously after the ILC process.

To address the abovementioned tracking problem in ILC, we denote the tracking error as
\bm{E}_{k}=\bm{Y}_{d}-\bm{Y}_{k}
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Obviously, (
3
) holds if and only if
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{E}_{k}=0
. Let the output deviation and input deviation between two sequential iterations for (
2
) be denoted as
\Delta\bm{Y}_{k}=\bm{Y}_{k+1}-\bm{Y}_{k}
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\Delta\bm{U}_{k}=\bm{U}_{k+1}-\bm{U}_{k}
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, respectively. Then the use of (
2
) yields

\bm{E}_{k+1}=\bm{E}_{k}-\Delta\bm{Y}_{k}=\bm{E}_{k}+\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(4)

which describes a dynamic system evolving along the iteration axis
k
and having a control input given by

\bm{\Psi}_{k}=-\Delta\bm{U}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(5)

For distinction from the notations adopted in the system (
2
), we present the following definition to introduce some notations of the linear discrete control system (
4
).

Definition 1

For the system (
4
),
\bm{E}_{k}
and
\bm{\Psi}_{k}
are called the
k
-state and the
k
-input, respectively. This system, controlled with some certain
k
-state feedbacks, is said to be
k
-stable if, for any initial
k
-state condition,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{E}_{k}=0
can be accomplished.

Based on Definition
1
, the tracking objective (
3
) is achieved for the system (
2
) if and only if the
k
-stability is accomplished for the system (
4
). As a consequence of this equivalence result, the design of the input
\bm{U}_{k}
for the system (
2
) to get the tracking objective (
3
) can be transformed into designing the
k
-input
\bm{\Psi}_{k}
for the system (
4
) to realize the
k
-stability. It can be easily seen that the
k
-state is accessible for the feedback controller design of the system (
4
). This renders the classic state feedback-based design tools in the Kalman state-space framework available for the ILC design of the system (
2
) in the presence of the output tracking task (
3
), and consequently available for the problem-solving of the LAE (
1
). Motivated by such an observation, we will further develop a
trackability
property for the system (
2
), which matches exactly with the
solvability
of the LAE (
1
), and disclose the close relation between them and the
controllability
of the system (
4
) (see also Fig.
1
) such that the solutions of the LAE (
1
) can be obtained by a
k
-state feedback-based method.

III
Trackability in ILC

In this section, we propose a fundamental class of trackability problems for the desired references in realizing the tracking tasks of ILC. We introduce the basic trackability properties and criteria, and disclose tight relations of them with the controlled systems of ILC at the same time.

III-A

Trackability and Trackability Subspace

Typically, existing ILC results aim at how to design updating laws of inputs to achieve the output tracking objectives and at how to analyze the convergence performances of their resulting ILC processes. However, it is lack of investigations for ILC to explore whether the desired references are trackable with some certain inputs. To address this crucial problem, the realizability assumption is often made directly for the desired references in the ILC literature, but its reasonability has not been developed. For the sake of our discussions on these fundamental problems of ILC, we introduce the following concepts of trackability and realizability for the desired references in ILC, and also present the related properties of the corresponding ILC systems.

Definition 2

A desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}
is said to be trackable (respectively, realizable) for the system (
2
) if there exists some (respectively, a unique) desired input
\bm{U}_{d}
fulfilling the LAE (
1
).

Definition 3

The system (
2
) is said to have the trackability property (respectively, the realizability property) if any desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is trackable (respectively, realizable) for the system (
2
).

With Definitions
2
and
3
, we connect the solvability problem of the LAE (
1
) tightly to two basic problems of the trackability and the realizability in ILC for the system (
2
).

Remark 1

From Definition
2
, it is clear that the trackability and realizability represent different concepts for characterizing the output tracking tasks of ILC systems. The realizability of a desired reference requires the uniqueness of the corresponding desired input for the controlled system, but by comparison, the trackability does not. In the literature of ILC, the realizability is usually adopted as a basic assumption, especially for under-actuated systems with less number of the inputs than that of the outputs (see, e.g.,
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
). Despite this fact, the trackability provides a more general property than the realizability for ILC by removing the uniqueness requirement, through which more fundamentally important problems in ILC may be explored.

Remark 2

From Definition
3
, we know that an ILC system has the trackability (respectively, realizability) property if any desired reference is trackable (respectively, realizable). We can clearly see that these two properties for an ILC system are two inherent characteristics independent from the selections of the desired references. When the ILC system dose not possess the trackability property, only a portion of the desired references may be trackable, for which the corresponding desired inputs are available; and otherwise, it is impossible to determine any desired input by iterations. Thus, it is a crucial issue to identify whether the given desired reference is trackable before seeking control inputs for the system (
2
).

It is worth highlighting, however, that few results of ILC are devoted to exploring whether any desired reference of interest is trackable for the system (
2
). If not, what properties do the trackable desired references have, and how do they connect to the characteristics of the system (
2
)? Furthermore, whether and how could some easy-to-check criteria be developed to validate the trackability of the desired references of interest? To address these fundamental problems, we focus on exploiting the output trackability of ILC for the system (
2
) during accomplishing the tracking objective (
3
) in the presence of any desired reference, and on characterizing the roles of the trackability of the desired references in performing the tracking tasks of ILC.

In Definition
2
, the satisfaction of the LAE (
1
) can guarantee that the linear combination of two trackable desired references for the system (
2
) is also trackable. This evidently hints that all trackable desired references for the system (
2
) span a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let us denote this subspace as
\mathcal{Y}_{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
that is directly called the
trackability subspace
of the system (
2
) for simplicity and adopt
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
to denote any trackable
\bm{Y}_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. It clearly follows from Definition
3
that
\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
holds if and only if the system (
2
) has the trackability property. However, by contrast, similar properties can not be analogously shown for the system (
2
) when it is concerned with the realizable desired references owing to the requirement of uniqueness. This problem depends heavily upon the column independence of the matrix
\bm{G}
, which will be addressed with details in the following subsection.

III-B

Trackability Criteria

Let
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
be the space that is spanned by the columns of
\bm{G}
. Let us also denote
\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)=m
. Then we represent a set of basis vectors for
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
, defined by
\left\{h_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:i=1,2,\cdots,m\right\}
, with which we denote
\bm{H}_{1}=\left[h_{1},h_{2},\cdots,h_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
. Moreover, we choose some matrix
\bm{H}_{2}=\left[h_{m+1},h_{m+2},\cdots,h_{p}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(p-m)}
such that
\bm{H}=\left[\bm{H}_{1}~\bm{H}_{2}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is nonsingular. Correspondingly, we denote
\bm{H}^{-1}=\bm{F}
, and let
\bm{F}
be in a structured form of

\bm{F}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}~\hbox{with}~\bm{F}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m},\bm{F}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(p-m)}.

Clearly,
\bm{H}_{1}
,
\bm{H}_{2}
,
\bm{F}_{1}
, and
\bm{F}_{2}
are full-column rank matrices. These denotations also help develop a useful lemma as follows.

Lemma 1

For any matrix
\bm{G}
, the following statements hold:

1.

\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}
is such that
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\oplus\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
;

2.

\left(\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}=\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\left(\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}=\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}
are idempotent matrices such that
\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}+\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}=I
.

Proof:

“1):” From
\bm{F}\bm{H}=I
,
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{H}_{1}=0
follows. Since the columns of
\bm{H}_{1}
form a set of basis vectors for
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
, we know
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}=\mathrm{span}\bm{H}_{1}
. From these two facts, we can obtain

\bm{\vartheta}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\theta}=0,\quad\forall\bm{\theta}\in\mathrm{span}\bm{G},\forall\bm{\vartheta}\in\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}.

(6)

As a consequence of (
6
), we can arrive at
\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}
, and then
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\oplus\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
by considering
\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)=m
and
\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}\right)=p-m
.

“2):” From
\bm{F}\bm{H}=I
, it is direct to get
\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{H}_{1}=I
and
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{H}_{2}=I
. This leads to that
\left(\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}=\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}
and
\left(\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{2}=\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}
are idempotent. Moreover,
\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}+\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}=I
is a straightforward consequence of
\bm{H}\bm{F}=I
.
∎

To proceed with Lemma
1
, we establish a trackability result of any desired reference in ILC.

Lemma 2

For any desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
of the system (
2
), the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
;

2.

\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
;

3.

\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=0
;

4.

\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=\bm{Y}_{d}
.

Proof:

We prove the equivalences among four statements in a circular manner.

“1)
\Rightarrow
2):” If
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
, then the LAE (
1
) holds for some
\bm{U}_{d}
from Definition
2
. Clearly, (
1
) implies
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
.

“2)
\Rightarrow
3):” With
\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}\bm{F}_{2}
in Lemma
1
,
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=0
is an immediate result of
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
.

“3)
\Rightarrow
4):” By
\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}+\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}=I
in Lemma
1
together with
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=0
, we can deduce

\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=\left(I-\bm{H}_{2}\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\bm{Y}_{d}=\bm{Y}_{d}.

“4)
\Rightarrow
1):” Owing to
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}=\mathrm{span}\bm{H}_{1}
,
\bm{H}_{1}=\bm{G}\bm{H}_{3}
holds for some
\bm{H}_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}

\bm{U}_{d}=\bm{H}_{3}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}

\bm{Y}_{d}=\bm{H}_{1}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=\bm{G}\left(\bm{H}_{3}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}\right)=\bm{G}\bm{U}_{d}

namely, the LAE (
1
) holds. According to Definition
2
,
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
follows immediately.
∎

By moving Lemma
2
a bit further, we present a realizability result of any desired reference in ILC.

Lemma 3

Consider the system (
2
) with any
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
. Then
\bm{Y}_{d}
is realizable if and only if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=q
.

Proof:

For
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
, let
\bm{U}_{d}^{1}
and
\bm{U}_{d}^{2}
be two desired inputs that both fulfill the LAE (
1
), namely,
\bm{Y}_{d}=\bm{G}\bm{U}_{d}^{1}=\bm{G}\bm{U}_{d}^{2}
. This obviously leads to
\bm{G}\left(\bm{U}_{d}^{1}-\bm{U}_{d}^{2}\right)=0
. Since
\bm{U}_{d}^{1}
and
\bm{U}_{d}^{2}
denote any two desired inputs satisfying (
1
), we can validate

\bm{U}_{d}^{1}-\bm{U}_{d}^{2}=0~\Leftrightarrow~\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=q

which, together with Definition
2
, implies that
\bm{Y}_{d}
is realizable if and only if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=q
.
∎

With Lemma
3
, we reveal that the realizability of the desired references in ILC of the system (
2
) requires the matrix
\bm{G}
being of full-column rank. Under the satisfaction of this requirement, we can obtain that the linear combination of any two realizable desired references for the system (
2
) is also realizable. Thus, it follows that all realizable desired references for the system (
2
) span a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, denoted by
\mathcal{Y}_{R}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We directly call
\mathcal{Y}_{R}
the
realizability subspace
of the system (
2
) for convenience and use
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{R}
to denote any realizable
\bm{Y}_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. By Lemma
3
, we particularly have
\mathcal{Y}_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

Based on Lemmas
2
and
3
, the following theorem introduces basic trackability and realizability properties in ILC.

Theorem 1

For the system (
2
), the following two properties can be developed.

1.

Trackability:
\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
holds, and in particular,
\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p
.

2.

Realizability: Either
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\mathcal{Y}_{T}
or
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\{0\}
holds, where
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\mathcal{Y}_{T}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=q
.

Proof:

“1):” From the equivalent results between 1) and 2) in Lemma
2
,
\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
follows directly. We, in particular, can arrive at

\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathbb{R}^{p}\Leftrightarrow~\mathrm{span}\bm{G}=\mathbb{R}^{p}\Leftrightarrow~\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)=p\Leftrightarrow~\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p.

Thus, the trackability result 1) is obtained.

“2):” With Lemma
3
, we can verify that
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\mathcal{Y}_{T}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=q
. Otherwise, let
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)\neq q
. Then
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=m<q
holds, and for the null space
\mathrm{null}\bm{G}
of
\bm{G}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{null}\bm{G}=\left\{\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}\big|\bm{G}\bm{\phi}=0\right\}

we have
\mathrm{null}\bm{G}=\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{\bot}
, and as a consequence,

\dim\left(\mathrm{null}\bm{G}\right)=q-\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)=q-m>0.

(7)

With (
7
), there exist more than one desired inputs that generate every nonzero trackable desired reference based on Theorem 2 of
[
35
, Subchapter 3.10]
. Namely, for every nonzero
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
, we have
\bm{Y}_{d}\not\in\mathcal{Y}_{R}
, and thus
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\{0\}
holds owing to
\mathcal{Y}_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}_{T}
. The realizability result 2) is developed.
∎

Remark 3

By
\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
, it discloses that the trackability subspace of the system (
2
) is exactly the spanning space for the columns of
\bm{G}
. This, however, is generally no longer applicable for the realizability subspace
\mathcal{Y}_{R}
of the system (
2
), which only works (namely,
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
) if and only if
\bm{G}
is of full-column rank. Otherwise,
\mathcal{Y}_{R}=\{0\}
emerges, namely, there do not exist any nonzero realizable desired references. Therefore, we reveal with Theorem
1
that the trackability for ILC systems is a much more available property than the realizability. Simultaneously, for the system (
2
), the trackability and realizability properties can also be determined according to Theorem
1
. To be specific, the system (
2
) has the trackability (respectively, realizability) property if and only if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p
holds (respectively,
\bm{G}
is an invertible square matrix).

IV
Controllability in ILC

In this section, we introduce the fundamental controllability property and its related problems into the tracking tasks of ILC, which helps to bring a viewpoint of addressing the tracking problems of ILC, and thus of dealing with the problem-solving of LAEs, from the classic theories and methods of systems and control. We develop the ideas for controllability, controllability subspace, controllability criteria, and controllability decomposition of the linear discrete systems (see, e.g.,
[
36
, Chapter 3]
) to ILC systems.

IV-A

Controllability and Controllability Subspace

Next, we turn to explore the tracking error system (
4
) that is formulated in the state-space form of a linear discrete system. To this end, let us introduce two concepts for the controllability of the linear discrete systems (see, e.g.,
[
36
, Chapter 3]
).

Definition 4

For the system (
4
), a nonzero
k
-state
\bm{\beta}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to be controllable if, for some finite
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists some
k
-input sequence
\bm{\Psi}_{k}
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{l-1}
that transfers the
k
-state from
\bm{\beta}
to the origin at the
l
th iteration.

Definition 5

For the system (
4
), if all, some but not all, and none of the nonzero
k
-states in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
are controllable, respectively, then (
4
) is said to be completely controllable, incompletely controllable, and completely uncontrollable, respectively.

With Definition
4
, we can validate that all the controllable
k
-states of the system (
4
), together with the null vector, constitute a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We call it the
controllability subspace
of the system (
4
), which is denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{C}
. It follows from Definition
5
that
\mathcal{C}_{C}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
holds if and only if the system (
4
) is completely controllable. For clarity, let
\mathcal{C}_{NC}=\mathcal{C}_{C}^{\bot}
be the
uncontrollability subspace
of the system (
4
). We thus have
\mathcal{C}_{C}\oplus\mathcal{C}_{NC}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and can develop specific properties of both
\mathcal{C}_{C}
and
\mathcal{C}_{NC}
as follows.

Theorem 2

For the system (
4
), the following controllability properties hold:

1.

\mathcal{C}_{NC}=\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
;

2.

\mathcal{C}_{C}=\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
.

Proof:

“1):” For any controllable
k
-state
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}_{C}
, there exist some
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and the corresponding input
\left\{\bm{\Psi}_{k}:\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{l-1}\right\}
such that the solution of the system (
4
) starting with
\bm{\beta}
arrives at the origin after
l
iterations, namely,

0=\bm{E}_{l}=\bm{E}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k}=\bm{\beta}+\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k}.

This implies that
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}_{C}
is a controllable
k
-state for the system (
4
) if and only if it can be described in the form of

\bm{\beta}=-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k},\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}_{C}.

(8)

“
\mathcal{C}_{NC}\subseteq\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
:” For any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}_{NC}
, we can obtain
\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}=0
,
\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}_{C}
. By specifically considering the controllable
k
-state generated by
\bm{\Psi}_{k}=-l^{-1}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\alpha}
, we can resort to (
8
) to derive

0=\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}=-\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k}=\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\alpha}

which leads to
\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\alpha}=0
, i.e.,
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
. This clearly implies
\mathcal{C}_{NC}\subseteq\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
.

“
\mathcal{C}_{NC}\supseteq\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
:” For any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
,
\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}=0
holds, and thus we can leverage (
8
) to arrive at

\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}=-\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}\left(\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\right)\bm{\Psi}_{k}=0,\quad\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}_{C}

which ensures
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}_{NC}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{C}_{NC}\supseteq\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
holds.

By combining
\mathcal{C}_{NC}\subseteq\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
with
\mathcal{C}_{NC}\supseteq\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
, the result 1) can be immediately obtained.

“2):” For any
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}_{NC}
, the use of
\mathcal{C}_{NC}=\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
in 1) yields
\bm{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}=0
,
\forall\bm{\beta}\in\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
. This implies
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{C}
. Again using
\mathcal{C}_{NC}=\mathrm{null}\bm{G}^{\mathrm{T}}
, we can derive

\dim\left(\mathcal{C}_{NC}\right)+\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)=p.

(9)

From
\mathcal{C}_{C}\oplus\mathcal{C}_{NC}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it is clear that
\dim\left(\mathcal{C}_{NC}\right)+\dim\left(\mathcal{C}_{C}\right)=p
, which, together with (
9
), yields

\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)=\dim\left(\mathcal{C}_{C}\right)=p-\dim\left(\mathcal{C}_{NC}\right).

Thanks to
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{C}
, we actually have
\mathcal{C}_{C}=\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
. Namely, the result 2) holds.
∎

We can see from Theorem
2
that the controllability of any
k
-state depends only on the matrix
\bm{G}
. The good property benefits from the specific structure of the system (
4
) that is established from the tracking task (
3
) of ILC for the system (
2
). Obviously, we can use Theorem
2
and Definition
5
to, respectively, obtain
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\{0\}\subset\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\bm{G}=0
when (
4
) is completely controllable, incompletely controllable, and completely uncontrollable, respectively. Next, the complete controllability of the system (
4
) is directly called controllability for simplicity.

IV-B

Controllability Criteria

With the property of the controllable
k
-states in Theorem
2
, we can present the controllability criteria for the system (
4
).

Theorem 3

For the system (
4
), it is controllable if and only if any of the following conditions holds:

1.

(
4
) is
k
-stabilizable;

2.

\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p
.

Proof:

If the system (
4
) is controllable, then it naturally is
k
-stabilizable, and as a consequence, there exists some
k
-state feedback with a gain matrix
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
in the form of

\bm{\Psi}_{k}=-\bm{K}\bm{E}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(10)

to stabilize the resulting closed-loop system, given by

\bm{E}_{k+1}=\left(I-\bm{G}\bm{K}\right)\bm{E}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(11)

Clearly, the
k
-stability of the system (
11
) holds if and only if

\rho\left(I-\bm{G}\bm{K}\right)<1

(12)

which implies the nonsingularity of
\bm{G}\bm{K}
. Hence,
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p
is immediate.

On the contrary, if
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p
, then (
12
) holds for some
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
, under which (
11
) is a
k
-stable system. That is, the system (
4
) is
k
-stabilizable under some
k
-state feedback controller (
10
). Moreover, we incorporate the result of Theorem
2
and can deduce

\dim\left(\mathcal{C}_{C}\right)=\dim\left(\mathrm{span}\bm{G}\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=p

and hence,
\mathcal{C}_{C}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
. That is, the controllability of the system (
4
) is obtained.
∎

As a counterpart result of Theorem
3
, the following theorem is introduced in the presence of the incomplete controllability of the system (
4
).

Theorem 4

If the system (
4
) is incompletely controllable, then there exists some nonsingular linear transformation
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}=\bm{F}\bm{E}_{k}
such that
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}=\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{C}=\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}+\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(13)

and
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-m}
satisfies

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=\widehat{\bm{E}}_{0}^{NC},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(14)

Moreover, the subsystem (
13
) is controllable.

Proof:

From (
6
), we have
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}=0
. We thus apply
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}=\bm{F}\bm{E}_{k}=\left[\left(\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}~\left(\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}
to the system (
4
) and can derive

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{C}\\
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{NC}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}\\
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}\bm{G}\bm{\Psi}_{k}

(15)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}\\
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\bm{\Psi}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

from which (
13
) is straightforward. Also, (
15
) implies

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{NC}=\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

which is equivalent to (
14
).

For the subsystem (
13
), we can further obtain
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\right)=m
because the use of the nonsingularity of
\bm{F}
and
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=m
leads to

\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{G}\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{F}\bm{G}\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right)\\
=\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\right).

(16)

Hence, (
13
) is a controllable system by considering Theorem
3
for this subsystem.
∎

Remark 4

By Theorems
2
,
3
and
4
, we obtain fundamental controllability properties for the tracking error systems of ILC. They actually provide criteria on the design of
k
-state feedback controllers to achieve the
k
-stability. Of particular note is the close relation between these results and rank conditions of the matrix
\bm{G}
. The full-row rank of
\bm{G}
is a necessary and sufficient condition for the controllability of the system (
4
). Otherwise, there exist uncontrollable
k
-states for the system (
4
), of which a standard controllability decomposition is provided in (
15
). In particular, it is interesting to derive (
14
) by this decomposition, which reveals that all the uncontrollable components of the
k
-states are not dynamic but fixed along the iteration axis.

V
Controllability-Based Design of ILC

In this section, we connect the trackability problems for ILC systems to the fundamental controllability problems, based on which we explore the design and analysis of ILC in performing output tracking tasks. For this purpose, next we present a result to reveal how to verify the trackability of the desired references in ILC from the perspective of the controllability.

Theorem 5

For the system (
2
) and the associated tracking error system (
4
), it always holds:

\mathcal{Y}_{T}=\mathcal{C}_{C}.

(17)

Further, for any
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
if and only if

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{0}=0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(18)

and otherwise,
\bm{Y}_{d}\not\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
if and only if

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{0}\neq 0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(19)

Proof:

From Theorems
1
and
2
, we can directly gain (
17
) because two subspaces are both identical with
\mathrm{span}\bm{G}
. Further, we leverage (
2
) and
\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}=0
by Lemma
1
to validate that for any
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{Y}_{d}-\bm{G}\bm{U}_{k}\right)=0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}

where we also incorporate the equivalent results of Lemma
2
. For the same reason, we can deduce

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}\neq 0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{Y}_{d}-\bm{G}\bm{U}_{k}\right)\neq 0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{d}\neq 0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow~\bm{Y}_{d}\not\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}.

Then all results of this theorem can be derived with (
14
).
∎

Based on Theorem
5
, we can study the trackability problems of ILC systems by instead treating the controllability problems of their resulting tracking error systems. This makes it feasible to address the trackability analysis and the updating law design of ILC by resorting only to its tracking error system. Further, it is worth noting that all trackable desired references correspond to
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=0
,
\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, namely, the uncontrollable components of their relevant
k
-states are always equal to zero.

To proceed with the development of Theorem
5
, we develop a fundamental tracking result of ILC in the following theorem, regardless of the system (
2
) in the presence of any plant model.

Theorem 6

Let any desired reference
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be specified. Then the system (
2
) can realize the tracking objective (
3
) under some updating law of ILC in the form of

\bm{U}_{k+1}=\bm{U}_{k}+\bm{K}\bm{E}_{k},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(20)

if and only if
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
. Otherwise, the system (
2
) can no longer achieve the tracking objective (
3
), regardless of the application of any input sequence
\left\{\bm{U}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\right\}
.

Proof:

From (
5
), we can validate the equivalence between the updating law (
20
) and the
k
-state feedback (
10
). Note that
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\right)=m
and
\mathrm{rank}\left(\bm{H}_{1}\right)=m
, namely,
\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}
and
\bm{H}_{1}
are of full-row rank and of full-column rank, respectively. Hence, there always exists some gain matrix
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
such that

\rho\left(I-\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\bm{K}\bm{H}_{1}\right)<1

(21)

and some gain matrix
\widehat{\bm{K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times m}
such that

\rho\left(I-\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\widehat{\bm{K}}\right)<1.

(22)

By these preparations, next we show that the tracking objective (
3
) holds for the system (
2
) under the updating law (
20
) if and only if
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
.

Necessity:
If (
3
) holds for (
2
) under (
20
), then
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{E}_{k}=0
holds for (
11
) (namely, the closed-loop system resulting from (
4
) and (
10
)). With Lemma
1
and from (
11
), we can leverage
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}=\bm{F}\bm{E}_{k}
,
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}=\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{k}
, and
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{k}
to obtain (
14
) and

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{C}=\left(I-\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\bm{K}\bm{H}_{1}\right)\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}-\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\bm{K}\bm{H}_{2}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(23)

Based on
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{E}_{k}=0
and with
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{NC}=\bm{F}_{2}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{k}
, the use of (
14
) leads to (
18
). It hence follows from Theorem
5
that
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
. In addition, the substitution of (
18
) into (
23
) yields

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{C}=\left(I-\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\bm{K}\bm{H}_{1}\right)\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(24)

which is
k
-stable, and hence
\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}=0
, under the spectral radius condition (
21
). Clearly, this coincides with the two facts of
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{E}_{k}=0
and of
\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}=\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{E}_{k}
.

Sufficiency:
Let us consider the controllable subsystem (
13
), and there always exists some
k
-state feedback
\bm{\Psi}_{k}=-\widehat{\bm{K}}\bm{E}_{k}^{C}
to generate the closed-loop system given by

\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k+1}^{C}=\left(I-\bm{F}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{G}\widehat{\bm{K}}\right)\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(25)

of which the
k
-stability can be ensured with the spectral radius condition (
22
). This implies
\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}^{C}=0
. If
\bm{Y}_{d}\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
, then (
18
) holds based on Theorem
5
. Hence, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}=0
which, together with
\bm{E}_{k}=\bm{H}\widehat{\bm{E}}_{k}
, yields
\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{E}_{k}=0
. Owing to

IX
Conclusions

In this paper, we have discussed and answered the questions about whether and how we can achieve the objective of solving LAEs from the perspective of systems and control, concerning which we have developed an iterative method integrated with a learning control mechanism. By introducing a new trackability problem and connecting it to typical tracking problems of ILC, we have revealed an equivalent relation between the solvability of LAEs and the controllability of discrete control systems. In view of this equivalence relation, we have leveraged the classic state feedback-based methods to solve LAEs, which is realized by equivalently accomplishing the perfect tracking tasks of the resulting ILC systems. It has been shown that the solutions for any solvable LAE are linearly dependent upon the initial inputs and thus all its solutions can be derived by different selections of the initial inputs. This property also applies to obtaining all the least squares solutions for any unsolvable LAE. Moreover, we have incorporated the design idea of deadbeat control into ILC such that the solving of LAEs can be realized within finite iteration steps. In addition, we have generalized the trackability property to conventional 2-D ILC systems, and have developed a state feedback-based method for the analysis and synthesis of ILC, instead of resorting to the generally employed contraction mapping-based methods for ILC. This brings new insights into ILC and narrows the gap between it and the popular feedback-based control methods. Our analysis and synthesis results may also provide a way to promote the interaction between control and mathematics.
