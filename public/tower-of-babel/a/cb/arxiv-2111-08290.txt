On the inverse gamma subordinator
Fausto Colantoni, Mirko D’Ovidio

Abstract
In this paper we deal with some open problems concerned with gamma subordinators. In particular, we provide a representation for the moments of the inverse gamma subordinator. Then, we focus on λ \lambda -potentials and we study the governing equations associated with gamma subordinators and inverse processes. Such representations are given in terms of higher transcendental functions , also known as Volterra functions



Keywords—

Gamma subordinators, inverse gamma processes, higher transcendental functions, Volterra functions, fractional calculus

1
Introduction

Gamma subordinator is a well-known subordinator which has been considered in many fields of Applied Sciences. In Mathematical Finance for instance, a well-known process is the Variance Gamma Process (or
Laplace motion
) which can be obtained by considering a Brownian motion with a random time given by a Gamma subordinator (
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
25
]
). We recall that a subordinator is a Lévy process with non-negative and non-decreasing paths. The Gamma subordinator is a special case in which the associated Lévy measure on
(0,\infty)
is infinite, then the paths are increasing (
[
29
]
).

In the connection between non-local analysis and Probability the Gamma subordinator plays a relevant role, many authors have investigated such a connection and the related properties, we list only few references throughout the work. Recently, in
[
4
]
some new operators associated with Gamma subordinators appear whereas, in
[
10
]
the connection between parabolic and elliptic problems in case of Gamma (and inverse Gamma) time change is considered.

Despite the fact that Gamma subordinators are well-known objects, deeply investigated in the past years, there is a lack in the theory concerned with inverse Gamma subordinators. At the current stage there are some results on
the moments of the inverse gamma subordinators only concerned with their
asymptotic behavior (
[
23
]
,
[
10
]
) and their Laplace transforms (
[
23
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
31
]
).

Our main contributions, in order to close such a gap, are stated in Section
4
. We obtain a new representation for the moments of any real (positive) order. Moreover, we obtain explicitly, in a closed form, the potentials and the Sonine kernels concerned with our case study, that is for Gamma subordinators and their inverses. We also provide a detailed discussion on densities and governing equations in order to have a clear picture about the processes we deal with. Indeed, the Gamma subordinator belongs to a special class of time-dependent continuous functions.

In our analysis a central role has been played by the functions
\nu
and
\mu
, two
higher transcendental functions
(see for example the book
[
15
]
), also known as
Volterra functions
(
[
2
]
,
[
17
]
). The function
\nu
has been introduced by Volterra (1916) in his theory of convolution-logarithms (
[
32
]
), hence the name. The importance of these functions does not surprise, indeed they seem to be the analogue of the Mittag-Leffler function in case of stable subordinator and the corresponding inverses. The interest readers can consult the book
[
18
]
for the Mittag-Leffler function.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Gamma subordinators

We introduce the Bernstein function
\Phi:(0,\infty)\mapsto(0,\infty)
which is uniquely defined by the so-called Bernstein representation

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda z})\Pi(dz),\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\geq 0

where
\Pi
on
(0,\infty)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge z)\Pi(dz)<\infty
is the associated Lévy measure. We also recall that

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda z}\overline{\Pi}(z)dz,

(1)

where
\overline{\Pi}(z)=\Pi((z,\infty))
is termed
tail of the Lévy measure
(see
[
8
]
Section 1.2 for details).

From now on we consider the symbol

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=a\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)=a\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-e^{-\lambda y}\right)\frac{e^{-by}}{y}\,dy,\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pta>0,\;b>0

(2)

and the associated gamma subordinator
H=\{H_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
for which

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=e^{-t\Phi(\lambda)},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\geq 0.

(3)

Since
\Pi(0,\infty)=\infty
, then from Theorem 21.3 of
[
29
]
, we have that
H_{t}
has increasing sample path with jumps. The inverse process

\displaystyle L_{t}:=\inf\{s>0\,:\,H_{s}\notin(0,t)\},\hskip 10.00002ptt>0

can be regarded as an exit time for
H
. In particular, the process
L=\{L_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
turns out to be non-decreasing with continuous paths and it can be associated with some delaying or rushing effects (see
[
10
]
). By definition of inverse process, we can also write

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}<s)=\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{s}>t),\ \ \ s,t>0

(4)

where
\mathbf{P}_{x}
denote the probability measure for a process started from
x
at time
t=0
and
\mathbf{E}_{x}
the mean value with respect to
\mathbf{P}_{x}
. Here we have that
L_{0}=0
and
H_{0}=0
. We also use the notation

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}\in dx)=h(t,x)\,dx,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{t}\in dx)=l(t,x)\,dx.

It is well known that,
\forall\,t>0
,

\displaystyle h(t,x)=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{b^{at}}{\Gamma(at)}x^{at-1}e^{-bx},&x>0\\
\displaystyle 0,&x\leq 0\end{array}\right.

verifies

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}<0)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}\geq 0)=1\hskip 10.00002pt\forall t\geq 0

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}h(t,x)\,dx=\frac{b^{at}}{(\lambda+b)^{at}}=e^{-t\,a\,\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda>0,\ t>0

(7)

which agrees with formula (
3
). However, we immediately see that

\displaystyle h(t,x)\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as}\hskip 10.00002ptx\downarrow 0\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{only if}\hskip 10.00002ptat>1.

The property for a Lévy process to be continuous and derivable depending on the time variable is known as
time dependent property
(see
[
29
, Chapter 23]
). Such a property turns out to be quite demanding from the analytical point of view. We conclude the discussion about
H
by recalling that, from the representation (
2.1
), we are able to evaluate the moments

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[(H_{t})^{q}]=\frac{1}{b^{q}}\frac{\Gamma(at+q)}{\Gamma(at)},\hskip 10.00002ptq\geq 1,\;t>0.

(8)

In
[
23
, formula (17)]
, the authors provide the following representation for the density of
L
,

\displaystyle l(t,x)=\frac{ae^{-t}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y^{-ax}e^{-yt}}{1+y}\left(\pi\cos(a\pi x)-\ln(y)\sin(a\pi x)\right)\ dy,\ ax\notin\mathbb{N}

which is written in their work for
b=1
(our notation).

Concerning the analogue of formulas (
7
) and (
8
), the explicit representation for the process
L
is an open problem.

2.2
Special functions

Let us introduce the incomplete gamma function

\displaystyle\gamma(a,z)=\int_{0}^{z}e^{-y}y^{a-1}dy,\hskip 10.00002pta>0,\;z\geq 0

(9)

and the digamma function

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{z-1}\ln y\ e^{-y}dy,\hskip 10.00002ptz\geq 0.

(10)

Figure 1:
\psi_{0}(x)

We notice that the incomplete gamma function
\gamma(a,z)
is a Bernstein function and therefore we can use (
9
) in order to define a new symbol for a subordinator (see
[
6
]
).
We now present some special functions which have been introduced in
[
15
, Section 18.3]
and which will be useful further on. Let
\alpha\in(-\infty,+\infty),\beta\in(-1,+\infty)
, we define

\displaystyle\mu(x,\beta,\alpha):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha+y}y^{\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta+1)\Gamma(\alpha+y+1)}dy,\hskip 10.00002ptx>0.

(11)

The integral in (
11
), focusing on complex variables, defines an analytical function in
x
, with branch-points in
x=0
and
x=\infty
. It is an entire function of
\alpha
. For our convenience we also introduce the function

\displaystyle\nu(x,\alpha):=\mu(x,0,\alpha).

(12)

An further definition can be given as follows (
[
2
, formula (33.3)]
)

\displaystyle\nu(x,\alpha)=e^{x}\int_{\alpha}^{\alpha+1}\frac{\gamma(y,x)}{\Gamma(y)}dy.

(13)

The Laplace transforms of these special functions can be easily obtained, in particular

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\nu(x,\alpha)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}\ln\lambda}

(14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\mu(x,\beta,\alpha)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}(\ln\lambda)^{\beta+1}},

(15)

with
\lambda>1
.
The functions
\mu
and
\nu
belongs to the class of higher transcendental functions as well as the famous Mittag-Leffler function. Such functions have been introduced in
[
15
]
together with a detailed discussion. However, in our view there is a misprint in
[
15
, page 221, formula (14)]
. Thus, for the reader’s convenience, we provide the following revised result concerning that formula.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. The following holds true

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha\vartheta}\nu(xe^{\vartheta},\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\vartheta^{k}\mu(x,k,\alpha),\hskip 10.00002ptx>0,\;\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}.

(16)

Proof.

Since

\displaystyle\displaystyle\nu(xe^{\vartheta},\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha+t}(e^{\vartheta})^{\alpha+t}}{\Gamma(\alpha+t+1)}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha+t}e^{\vartheta\alpha}e^{\vartheta t}}{\Gamma(\alpha+t+1)}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha\vartheta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha+t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\vartheta t)^{k}}{\Gamma(k+1)}}{\Gamma(\alpha+t+1)}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha\vartheta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\vartheta^{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{\alpha+t}t^{k}}{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(\alpha+t+1)}dt

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha\vartheta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\vartheta^{k}\ \mu(x,k,\alpha)

we get the claimed result. A further check can be given by considering the Laplace transforms (
14
) and (
15
) in order to achieve the equality (
16
).
∎

3
Governing equations

Let us consider a continuous function
\varphi
on
\mathbb{R}
extended with zero on the negative part of the real line, that is
\varphi(x)=0
if
x\leq 0
. From the Bochner subordination rule or in general, from the Phillips’ representation (
[
27
]
) we are able to obtain new operators through subordination. By following the same spirit we obtain a Marchaud (type) operator given by

\displaystyle-\Phi(-\partial_{x})\varphi(x)=a\int_{0}^{\infty}(\varphi(x)-\varphi(x-y))\frac{e^{-by}}{y}dy.

(17)

Indeed, by taking into account (
2
), we can immediately check that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\Phi(-\partial_{x})\varphi(x)\ dx=-\Phi(\xi)\tilde{\varphi}(\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\xi>0

where
\widetilde{\varphi}
denote the Laplace transform of
\varphi
. We notice that, if
\varphi
is
\alpha-
Hölder, that is
|\varphi(x)-\varphi(x-y)|\leq M\,|y|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>0
for some
M>0
, then

\displaystyle|\Phi(-\partial_{x})\varphi(x)|\leq a\,M\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{b^{\alpha}}.

Notice that the Marchaud operator (of order
\alpha
) is well defined for (essentially) bounded and
\gamma
-Hölder functions with
\gamma>\alpha
. The readers can consult the interesting work
[
16
]
for further details. The operator (
17
) can be also written as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\Phi}_{x}\varphi(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{x}\varphi(x-y)\,\overline{\Pi}(y)\,dy

(18)

which can be regarded as a Riemann-Liouville definition. From (
1
) and the fact that
\varphi(0)=0
, we still get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\mathcal{D}_{x}^{\Phi}\varphi(x)\ dx=\xi\tilde{\varphi}(\xi)\frac{\Phi(\xi)}{\xi}=\Phi(\xi)\tilde{\varphi}(\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\xi>0.

Since
\varphi(0)=0
, the Riemann-Liouville representation (
18
) is also equivalent to the Caputo-Dzherbashian (type) non-local operator

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{x}\varphi(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\varphi^{\prime}(x-y)\,\overline{\Pi}(y)\,dy

(19)

where
\varphi^{\prime}=d\varphi/dx
. In the literature, the Caputo-Dzherbashian fractional derivative is well-known. The operator (
19
) is similarly defined except for the convolution kernel. Caputo introduced his derivative in
[
11
,
12
]
whereas, the second author actively investigated this operator starting from the papers
[
14
,
13
]
. The operator (
19
) is well-defined for

\displaystyle\varphi\in L^{1}(0,\infty)\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{such that}\hskip 10.00002pt\varphi^{\prime}(x-y)\overline{\Pi}(y)\in L^{1}(0,x),\;\forall x.

From the regularity of
\overline{\Pi}
, it turns out that (
19
) is well-defined as
\varphi\in AC(0,\infty)
, that is the set of continuous functions on
(0,\infty)
with
\varphi^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Moreover, this is confirmed from the Young’s inequality for convolution from which

\displaystyle\|\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{x}\varphi\|_{1}\leq\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{1}\,\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}

(20)

where, as a quick check shows,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}a\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)\to\frac{a}{b}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{as}\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\to 0.

(21)

As usual we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{1}
the
L^{1}
-norm. Sometimes, for the sake of clarity, we write
L^{1}(dx)
or
L^{1}(dt)
in place of
L^{1}(0,\infty)
if some confusion may arise.

In view of (
20
) we now introduce the spaces

\displaystyle W^{1,1}(0,\infty)=\{\psi\in L^{1}(0,\infty):\;\psi^{\prime}\in L^{1}(0,\infty)\}

and

\displaystyle W^{1,1}_{0}(0,\infty)=\{\psi\in W^{1,1}(0,\infty):\,\psi(0)=0\}.

Observe that
AC(I)
coincides with
W^{1,1}(I)
if
I
is bounded.

Before proceeding, let us remember that the Laplace transform is well defined for piecewise continuous functions of exponential order. In particular, let us consider
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
on
(0,\infty)
with Laplace transforms
\widetilde{\varphi}_{1},\widetilde{\varphi}_{2}
. We recall that (Lerch’s theorem) if
\widetilde{\varphi}_{1}=\widetilde{\varphi}_{2}
, then
\varphi_{1}=\varphi_{2}
up to isolated points where at least one of the two functions is not continuous. Let
\mathcal{M}_{\eta}
be the set of piecewise continuous functions of order
\eta\geq 0
, that is
|\varphi(z)|\leq C\,e^{\eta z}
with
\widetilde{\varphi}
defined on
(\eta,\infty)
. Further on we focus on the set of functions
\mathcal{M}_{0}\cap C(0,\infty)
.

We introduce the next result by first noticing that, from the representation (
2.1
),
h\nin W^{1,1}_{0}(0,\infty)
. This is because of the time dependent property introduced above in Section
2.1
. Thus, the problem to address the right PDE for
h
must be taken with some care. We now present the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
v(t,x)\in C^{1,1}((0,\infty),W^{1,1}_{0}(0,\infty);[0,\infty))
be the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v(t,x)=-\mathcal{D}_{x}^{\Phi}v(t,x),&t>0,\,x\in(0,\infty),\\
\displaystyle v(0,x)=f(x),&f\in W_{0}^{1,1}(0,\infty),\\
\displaystyle v(t,x)=0,&t>0,\,x\in(-\infty,0].\end{array}\right.

(22)

Then,

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\,h(t,y)\,dy=\mathbf{E}_{0}[f(x-H_{t}),t<L_{x}].

(23)

Proof.

Since
v(t,\cdot)\in W^{1,1}_{0}(0,\infty)
for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}\mathcal{D}^{\Phi}_{x}v(t,x)\,dx=\Phi(\xi)\,\widetilde{v}(t,\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\xi>0

where
\widetilde{v}(t,\xi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi x}v(t,x)\,dx
,
\xi>0
. Let us write
\widetilde{v}(\lambda,x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}v(t,x)\,dt
,
\lambda>0
and denote by
\widetilde{v}(\lambda,\xi)
the double Laplace transform. The problem (
22
) takes the form

\displaystyle\lambda\tilde{v}(\lambda,\xi)-\tilde{f}(\xi)=-\Phi(\xi)\tilde{v}(\lambda,\xi)

where, with obvious notation,
\widetilde{f}(\xi)
is the Laplace transform of
f
. Thus,

\displaystyle\tilde{v}(\lambda,\xi)=\tilde{f}(\xi)\frac{1}{\lambda+\Phi(\xi)}=

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\xi)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}e^{-t\Phi(\xi)}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\xi)\mathbf{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}e^{-\xi H_{t}}dt\right]

where in the last step we have used (
3
). From this,

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}(t,\xi)=\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\xi H_{t}}]

and

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\,h(t,y)\,dy.

Uniqueness follows from the Laplace transform technique (Lerch’s theorem). Indeed, we are looking for a continuous solution, in particular
v\in C^{1,1}
. Thus,
\psi_{t}(\xi)=\widetilde{v}(t,\xi)
,
t>0
and
\psi_{x}(\lambda)=\widetilde{v}(\lambda,x)
,
x>0
are two Laplace transforms with unique inverses. We observe that

\displaystyle\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{1}\leq\|f\|_{1}\|h(t,\cdot)\|_{1}

for any
t>0
, thus
e^{-\xi x}v(t,x)
is obviously an element of
L^{1}(dx)
. Furthermore, we have that
\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\|h(t,\cdot)\|_{1}
for any
t>0
, thus
v(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{M}_{0}\cap C(0,\infty)
as required. Indeed,
W^{1,1}_{0}(0,\infty)
embeds into
L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
and
f
is essentially bounded. This holds only in the one dimensional case (for more details see section 11.2 of
[
24
]
). Similar arguments applies w.r. to the time variable by considering
\widetilde{h}(\lambda,x)
. In particular,

\displaystyle\|v(\cdot,x)\|_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{x}|f(x-y)|\kappa(y)\,dy=(J|f|)(x)

where
J
is a non-local (sometimes termed fractional) integral. This integral can be compared with the solution of (
40
) below. Here, the function
t\mapsto v(t,x)
is continuous and integrable for any
x
and this allows us to proceed with the Laplace technique w.r. to the time variable. We can also observe that
t\to h(t,x)
is continuous and, since
\Gamma(at)
is asymptotically faster than
b^{at}x^{at-1}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}h(t,x)=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and }\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t,x)=0,

from which we conclude that
h(\cdot,x)
is bounded for any
x>0
. Proceeding as before, we obtain
\|v(\cdot,x)\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{1}\|h(\cdot,x)\|_{\infty}
, thus
v(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{M}_{0}\cap C(0,\infty)
as required.

We obtain the probabilistic representation from the fact that

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)\,h(t,y)\,dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x-y)\,h(t,y)\,\mathbf{1}_{(y<x)}dy,

that is

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{0}[f(x-H_{t})\mathbf{1}_{(H_{t}<x)}].

From (
4
), we have that
\mathbf{1}_{(H_{t}<x)}
is equivalent to
\mathbf{1}_{(t<L_{x})}
under
\mathbf{E}_{0}
.

-) We check that
v(t,\cdot)\in C^{1}(0,\infty)
for any fixed
t>0
. First we observe that
v(t,\cdot)\in C(0,\infty)
for any
t>0
. Moreover,
\forall\,t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)h(t,y)dy

\displaystyle=f(0)h(t,x)+\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(x-y)h(t,y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(x-y)h(t,y)dy.

(24)

Since
f\in W_{0}^{1,1}(0,\infty)
and
h(t,\cdot)\in L^{1}(dx)

\forall\,t>0
, we conclude that
\frac{d}{dx}v(t,\cdot)\in C(0,\infty)
for any
t>0
. From the Young’s inequality, we notice that the convolution in (
3
) is in
L^{1}(dx)
and therefore
v(t,\cdot)\in W_{0}^{1,1}(0,\infty),\ t>0
.

-) We check that
v(\cdot,x)\in C^{1}(0,\infty)
for any fixed
x>0
. The derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}v(t,x)=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{x}f(x-y)h(t,y)dy

can be written in terms of

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}h(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{b^{at}x^{at-1}}{\Gamma(at)}e^{-bx}\right]=\frac{ab^{at}x^{at-1}}{\Gamma(at)}\left[\ln x+\ln b-\psi_{0}(at)\right]e^{-bx}

\displaystyle=ah(t,x)\left[\ln x+\ln b-\psi_{0}(at)\right].

(25)

The idea is to proof that
\frac{d}{dt}h(t,x)
is continuous and bounded in
t
for each
x>0
, then it is integrable. First we show that

\displaystyle h(t,x)\psi_{0}(at)=-\frac{e^{-bx}}{x}\hskip 10.00002ptas\ t\to 0,\hskip 10.00002pth(t,x)\psi_{0}(at)=0\ \hskip 10.00002ptas\ t\to\infty\ .

(26)

Let us recall (
[
21
, formula (13.1.5.1)]
)

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!n^{z}}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}.

(27)

Since
\psi_{0}(z)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\Gamma(z)}
, then

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\log n-\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{1}{z+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{z+n}\right).

(28)

From (
27
) and (
28
) we write

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{0}(z)}{\Gamma(z)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log n-\left(\frac{1}{z}+\frac{1}{z+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{z+n}\right)}{\frac{n!n^{z}}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{z\log n-\left(1+\frac{z}{z+1}+\cdots+\frac{z}{z+n}\right)}{\frac{n!n^{z}}{(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}}.

As
z\to 0
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{\psi_{0}(0)}{\Gamma(0)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{0-\left(1+0+\cdots+0\right)}{\frac{n!}{n!}}=-1

(29)

whereas, due to the asymptotic contribution of
n^{z}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{\psi_{0}(z)}{\Gamma(z)}=0.

(30)

From (
29
) and (
30
) we respectively obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}h(t,x)\psi_{0}(at)=\lim_{t\to 0}{b^{at}x^{at-1}e^{-bx}}\frac{\psi_{0}(at)}{\Gamma(at)}=-\frac{e^{-bx}}{x},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t,x)\psi_{0}(at)=\lim_{t\to\infty}{b^{at}x^{at-1}e^{-bx}}\frac{\psi_{0}(at)}{\Gamma(at)}=0,

which prove (
26
).
Summing up, for each fixed
x>0
, from (
3
),
h(t,x)(\ln x+\ln b)
is continuous in
t
and, from (
26
), we have that
h(t,x)\psi_{0}(at)
is continuous
\forall x\neq 0
, hence
\frac{d}{dt}h
is continuous and bounded, so it is in
L^{1}(dt)
. This guarantees that
\frac{d}{dt}v(\cdot,x)
is
C(0,\infty)
for any
x>0

and concludes the proof.
∎

Notice that

in (

) can be regarded as the stopping time depending on the threshold

. Indeed,

.

Let us consider once again the representation (

) of

. The profile of (

4.1
Densities and kernels

As for

through the integral representation

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(a,z):=\frac{1}{\gamma(a,z)}\int_{0}^{z}y^{a-1}e^{-y}\ln y\ dy\hskip 10.00002pta>0,z\geq 0.

We observe that

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}\Psi_{0}(a,z)=\psi_{0}(a).

We provide the following representation result.

Theorem 6
.

For

the following representation holds

\displaystyle l(t,x)=\frac{a\ \gamma(ax,bt)}{\Gamma(ax)}\left(\psi_{0}(ax)-\Psi_{0}(ax,bt)\right)\hskip 10.00002ptt>0,\;x>0.

(31)

Proof.

For positive

[

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{t}>x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{x}<t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{b^{ax}}{\Gamma(ax)}y^{ax-1}e^{-by}dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{bt}\frac{b^{ax}}{\Gamma(ax)}\left(\frac{z}{b}\right)^{ax-1}e^{-z}\frac{dz}{b}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{bt}\frac{1}{\Gamma(ax)}z^{ax-1}e^{-z}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(ax,bt)}{\Gamma(ax)}

(32)

where the incomplete gamma function (
9

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\gamma(ax,bt)=\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}dy=\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}\ \ln(y)\ dy,

and from (formula 6.3.1 in
[

]

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\Gamma(x)=\psi_{0}(x)\Gamma(x),

(33)

we get

\displaystyle l(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{dx}\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{t}>x)=-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\gamma(ax,bt)}{\Gamma(ax)}

\displaystyle=\frac{a\ \gamma(ax,bt)\ \Gamma(ax)\ \psi_{0}(ax)-\Gamma(ax)\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}dy}{\Gamma(ax)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a\ \gamma(ax,bt)\ \psi_{0}(ax)-a\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}\ \ln(y)\ dy}{\Gamma(ax)}.

From (
33

10

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}\ \ln(y)\ dy=\Psi_{0}(ax,bt)\gamma(ax,bt)

and therefore, the claim follows.

We now proceed by considering the Laplace technique. We observe that (

26

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}l(t,x)dt=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda>0

is verified by the representation (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\gamma(ax,bt)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}dy\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda\frac{y}{b}}e^{-y}y^{ax-1}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Gamma(ax)\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)^{-ax}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(ax)}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}a\frac{\gamma(ax,bt)}{\Gamma(ax)}\psi_{0}(ax)dt=a\psi_{0}(ax)\frac{1}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}\ln y\ dy\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{b}y}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}\ln y\ dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)^{ax}}\Gamma(ax)\left[\psi_{0}(ax)-\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Gamma(ax)\left[\psi_{0}(ax)-\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)\right]e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)},

where the second-last equality comes from (

and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\frac{a}{\Gamma(ax)}\int_{0}^{bt}y^{ax-1}e^{-y}\ln y\ dy\ dt

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{\lambda}\left[\psi_{0}(ax)-\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)\right]e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[a\psi_{0}(ax)-\Phi(\lambda)\right]e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}.

By collecting all together the previous Laplace transforms,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}l(t,x)dt=

\displaystyle a\psi_{0}(ax)\frac{1}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[a\psi_{0}(ax)-\Phi(\lambda)\right]e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda>0.

This concludes the proof.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}\varphi=\Delta\varphi.

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[(B_{H_{t}})^{q}]=\mathbf{E}_{0}[\mathbf{E}_{x}[(B_{H_{t}})^{q}|H_{t}]]=\mathbf{E}_{x}[(B_{1})^{q}]\,\mathbf{E}_{0}[(H_{t})^{q/2}]

4.3
Potentials

To the best of our knowledge, for the Laplace transforms

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cx}l(t,x)dx\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}h(t,x)dt

(53)

with
c,\lambda>0
, there are no explicit representations. On the other hand, the relation between potentials (
[
9
, formula 5.5]
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}f(L_{t})dt\right]=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda H_{t}}f(t)dt\right]

can be explicitly verified for any symbol
\Phi
. For a general subordinator and the associated inverse process, the problem to obtain the representations (
53
) is still open. The only case in which we have a closed form for such objects is the case of
\alpha
-stable subordinator and its inverse process.
That is, we respectively have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(-ct^{\alpha})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptx^{\alpha-1}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})\ ,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,\beta}(-z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z)^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}

is the Mittag-Leffler function for which
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(z)=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,1}(z)
.

In this section we direct our efforts in order to obtain explicit representations in (
53
) and we begin our discussion by introducing the following result.

Theorem 12
.

Let
u
be the unique continuous solution on
[0,+\infty)
to

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{t}^{\Phi}u(t)

\displaystyle=-c\ u(t),\hskip 10.00002ptu(0)=1,\hskip 10.00002ptc>0\ .

(54)

Let
L_{t}
be the inverse to a gamma subordinator. Then, we have that:

(i)

u(t)=\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]
;

(ii)

u(t)
has the following representation

\displaystyle u(t)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{c}{a}e^{-bt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{e^{\frac{c}{a}n}}\nu(bte^{-\frac{c}{a}},n)

(55)

\displaystyle=1-b\frac{c}{a}e^{-\frac{c}{a}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}\nu(bse^{-\frac{c}{a}},-1)ds

(56)

Proof.

(i)

By applying the
\lambda-
Laplace transform w.r.t.
t
in (
54
) and by using

u(0)=1

we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)+c\ \tilde{u}(\lambda)=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}.

Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\Phi(\lambda)+c}=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cw}e^{-\Phi(\lambda)w}dw

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cw}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}l(t,w)dt\right)dw,

from which

\displaystyle u(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cw}l(t,w)\ dw=\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]\ .

From Laplace machinery we have uniqueness. Indeed
u\in\mathcal{M}_{0}\cap C[0,\infty)
.

(ii)

By applying the Laplace transform in the left-hand-side of (
55
), we write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-cx}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\frac{1}{c+\Phi(\lambda)},\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda>0.

On the other hand

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\left[1-\frac{c}{a}e^{-bt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{e^{\frac{c}{a}n}}\nu(bte^{-\frac{c}{a}},n)\right]dt=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{c}{a}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{e^{\frac{c}{a}n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\lambda+b)t}\nu(bte^{-\frac{c}{a}},n)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{c}{a}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{e^{\frac{c}{a}n}}\frac{e^{\frac{c}{a}}}{b}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{\lambda+b}{b}e^{\frac{c}{a}}t}\nu(t,n)dt,

by taking into account the formula (
14
), we get that

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{c}{a}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b}{e^{\frac{c}{a}(n+1)}}\frac{e^{-\frac{c}{a}(n+1)}}{\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)^{n+1}}\frac{1}{\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)+\frac{c}{a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{c}{a}\frac{a}{a\ln\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{b}\right)+c}\frac{1}{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\frac{1}{\Phi(\lambda)+c}\ ,

which is the claim for (
55
).

Now we prove (
56
). Since
L_{t}\geq 0
, for
c>0
the mean
\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]
is well-defined. From the Taylor expansion of the exponential we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-c)^{k}}{k!}\mathbf{E}_{0}[L_{t}^{k}].

From (
42
) we get the new representation

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-c)^{k}}{a^{k}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-bt}\mu(bt,k-1,n)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{c}{a}e^{-bt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{c}{a}\right)^{k}\mu(bt,k,n)

\displaystyle=[{\text{by using \eqref{serienumu}}}]

\displaystyle=1-\frac{c}{a}e^{-bt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{e^{\frac{c}{a}n}}\nu(bte^{-\frac{c}{a}},n)

which has been obtained in (
55
).

By considering (
43
) instead of (
42
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-cL_{t}}]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-c)^{k}}{k!}\mathbf{E}_{0}[L_{t}^{k}]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{b}{a^{k}}{(-c)^{k}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}\mu(bs,k-1,-1)ds,

\displaystyle=[{\text{by using \eqref{serienumu}}}]

\displaystyle=1-b\frac{c}{a}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}e^{-\frac{c}{a}}\nu(bse^{-\frac{c}{a}},-1)ds

\displaystyle=1-b\frac{c}{a}e^{-\frac{c}{a}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-bs}\nu(bse^{-\frac{c}{a}},-1)ds.

Using the same argument for the convergence of (
43
), we have that for any finite
t>0
also the integral in (
56
) converges.

∎

Remark 13
.

(Convergence) From the convergence of (
56
), and from the equivalence between (
55
) and (
56
) we have also that the series in (
55
) converges absolutely on
(0,\infty)
.

Now we move to the next result.

Theorem 14
.

For
x\in(0,\infty)
, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}h(t,x)dt=e^{-bx}\ \frac{b}{a}\ e^{-\frac{\lambda}{a}}\ \nu(b\ x\ e^{-\frac{\lambda}{a}},-1),\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda>0.

(57)

Proof.

Let us write
\tilde{h}(\lambda,x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}h(t,x)dt
, for positive
x
. Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(\lambda,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\frac{b^{at}}{\Gamma(at)}x^{at-1}e^{-bx}dt=\frac{e^{-bx}}{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t(\lambda-a\ln b-a\ln x)}}{\Gamma(at)}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-bx}}{ax}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{z}{a}(\lambda-a\ln b-a\ln x)}}{\Gamma(z)}dz.

By using (
[
19
, formula 6.423]
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x}\frac{dx}{\Gamma(x+\beta+1)}=e^{\beta\alpha}\nu(e^{-\alpha},\beta),

we have

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-bx}}{ax}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{z}{a}(\lambda-a\ln b-a\ln x)}}{\Gamma(z)}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-bx}}{ax}e^{-\frac{1}{a}(\lambda-a\ln b-a\ln x)}\ \nu(e^{-\frac{1}{a}(\lambda-a\ln b-a\ln x)},-1)

\displaystyle=e^{-bx}\ \frac{b}{a}\ e^{-\frac{\lambda}{a}}\ \nu(b\ x\ e^{-\frac{\lambda}{a}},-1).

(58)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Alternative proof concerning Theorem
7
.

From Theorem
14
we are able to write

\displaystyle\kappa(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t,x)dt=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}h(t,x)dt=

\displaystyle=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}e^{-bx}\ \frac{b}{a}\ e^{-\frac{\lambda}{a}}\ \nu(b\ x\ e^{-\frac{\lambda}{a}},-1)=e^{-bx}\ \frac{b}{a}\nu(bx,-1)

which gives an alternative proof of the result.
